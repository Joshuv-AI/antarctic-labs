 Executive Summary
The proposed Direct Feed High Level Waste (DFHLW) approach for processing high-level tank
waste at Hanford is intended to reduce processing time by bypassing the Pretreatment Facility
and transferring waste directly from the tank farm to the WTP HLW vitrification facility. This
processing strategy could reduce or eliminate the washing and leaching steps that would have
occurred in the Pretreatment facility. Operation of the vitrification facility is subject to chemical
and radiological limits protecting safety (e.g. Waste Acceptance Criteria, or WACs) and process
quality (e.g. Process Control Limits, or PCLs). Without washing and leaching, there is a greater
risk of exceeding the WACs and PCLs. Hanford process engineers have devised blending
strategies based on known chemical and radiological composition, volumes, and solids loadings
of individual layers within each waste tank. These blending campaigns succeed in predicting a
processing strategy that does not exceed the WACs and PCLs. However, the calculations do not
ascribe uncertainties to the tank analysis data, quantities of material taken from the tanks to make
the blend, or potential for mixing of layers within tanks. In order to confirm that a process strategy
is working, it would be advantageous to have inline or at-line analytical instrumentation installed
in the processing facilities that deliver measurement results in real time.
To support the development of such real-time analytical instrumentation, we have undertaken a
meta-analysis of the layer-specific waste tank data and proposed blending campaigns. We have
applied Monte Carlo methods to incorporate uncertainties into the compositions of the individual
layers specified in the campaign recipes. The resulting variations in the compositions of the
blended materials allow us to estimate the risks of exceeding WACs and PCLs for each campaign.
We have identified a critical subset of WACs/PCLs that are especially at risk of being exceeded
in multiple proposed campaigns, and which should be focused on during instrument development:

 • NO2- and NO3-, the sum of which are accounted as a total “NOx” concentration;
 • Na and K, the sum of which are accounted as a “NaK” concentration defined as [Na] +
 0.59*[K];
 • Al, Fe, and Zr, the sum of which are accounted as a total “AlFeZr” concentration; and
 • S, which is accounted individually.
We have extracted the expected solid/supernate distribution for these analytes as well as the
different chemical forms in which the analytes appear. These factors are significant for
development of real-time, inline instrumentation such as FTIR, Raman, and LIBS spectroscopies,
for which the signals for elements and molecules can be affected by chemical form and phase.

 • NO2- and NO3- are present almost entirely in the supernate;
 • Na is present more in the liquid than the solid phase but generally will be present in both
 phases, as the ion in the liquid phase and as several molecular forms in the solid phase;
 • K may be present in either phase; its concentration is usually much lower than Na and it
 usually does not contribute substantially to the NaK limit;
 • Al is present mostly (>80%) in the solid phase, in the form of gibbsite, boehmite, or
 undifferentiated solid aluminum;
 • Fe and Zr are present almost entirely (>90%) in the solid phase, in the form of oxides; and
 • S is present only in the liquid phase (as SO42-) for half of the blending campaigns, and is
 in both phases for the other campaigns, with the solid phase consisting of kogarkoite
       (Na3FSO4).

 vi

This analysis is conducted for one processing scenario with specific targets for supernatant
density (1.35 g/mL), solids density (1.20 g/mL), and solids loading (20 weight percent). It also
assumes a total uncertainty of 20% for the elements associated with the calculations of the tank
blends, including the composition of the waste layers, the anticipated waste stream heterogeneity
(e.g. representativeness of sampling layers from tanks, especially where partial sampling is
specified), and possible imprecision in waste transfer volumes. This processing scenario was
considered to be potentially one likely to approach WAC/PCL criteria. Future work repeating
these calculations for other processing scenarios and/or including more specific determinations
for the uncertainties can be done quickly. This analysis serves as a guideline for future analyses.

1.0 Introduction

One of the consequences of the aggressive production of plutonium in the United States during
the Cold War era was the generation of millions of gallons of radioactive and chemical wastes.1
The extended effort to vitrify these wastes, i.e. turn them into a glass, is to create a more viable
form for long-term storage than the current option of storage in single- or double-shell
underground tanks. The enormous volume of waste at Department of Energy (DOE) facilities –
mainly Hanford and Savannah River – and its chemical and physical complexity ensures that
completion of the processing will require many decades. Any improvements to the rate of waste
processing will accrue over this time to produce substantial time and cost savings to the overall
effort.
One way to improve processing efficiency is to conduct real-time monitoring (RTM) of the
chemical and physical composition of the slurries and solutions being processed. The benefits
are analogous to those that have been realized with the implementation into other industrial-scale
production facilities in the oil, food, or pharmaceutical industries. Variations in feed or additive
properties, process conditions, and product properties can be observed in real time. Deviations
can be corrected, minimizing generation of product with inferior properties and optimizing the
amount of time and energy required to make the product.2 Recent efforts to determine the
suitability of analytical technologies, including fiber-based Raman and attenuated total reflectance
– Fourier transform infrared (ATR-FTIR) probes and laser-induced breakdown spectroscopy
(LIBS), demonstrate the recognition of the potential benefits of RTM for improving nuclear waste
processing.3-6
Successful development and demonstration of RTM technology necessitate an understanding of
several factors. A fundamental requirement is an understanding of the process chemistry and
engineering, which leads to knowledge of how the information obtained from the measurements
can be used to improve the process. Also important is knowledge of the likely composition and
properties of the process streams. These requirements are apt for High-Level Waste (HLW)
processing at Hanford, particularly the direct-feed HLW (DFHLW) approach under consideration
as an alternative to inclusion of pretreatment steps.7 Where pretreatment was planned to reduce
the concentrations of certain chemical components, DFHLW processing deals directly with the
waste material. The contents of HLW storage tanks vary substantially due to the different
processing campaigns over the history of the Hanford site.8 Also, within a given tank, as the
contents have not been routinely agitated, material has separated into distinct layers with different
chemical and physical properties. These layers are in the form of slurries or sludges, with liquid
(supernate) and solid (insoluble and supersaturated) components. Introduction of the material to
the vitrification facility is limited by waste acceptance criteria (WAC) to protect safety and process
control limits (PCL) to ensure waste glass quality. A full list of these criteria, which include
elemental and molecular concentrations and radiological and physical properties, can be found in
Appendix A.
Because many of the individual layers within the waste tanks have properties that do not meet
WAC and PCL requirements, blending of the layers is required to proceed with processing. Prior
work7 has generated estimates of a series of campaigns that can be constructed to process all of
the waste in the tanks under these requirements. One of the proposed blending strategies is
shown in Appendix B. Individual campaigns (columns of Table B-1) would involve the blending of
3 to 6 layers each. While some tank layers (rows of Table B-1) are proposed to be used in their

 1

entirety in a single campaign, other layers will be split between multiple campaigns. The strategies
are based on the results of elemental, molecular, and radiochemical analyses of both the solid
and supernatant phases in the slurries sampled from each layer.
The calculations have generated blending recipes such that each campaign would avoid
exceeding the WACs and PCLs. However, for some campaigns those limits are closely
approached. Importantly, the calculations do not account for any uncertainties, and thus some
campaigns may be at risk of exceeding the limits if the actual tank layer concentrations are not
as expected. Several credible sources for uncertainties include the analytical measurements of
retrieved tank waste samples, the representativeness of the retrieved samples (i.e. homogeneity
of the layers), and the quantity of the layers being retrieved. The role of RTM of sampled tank
layers and/or materials that have been introduced to a blending tank in helping assure that WAC
and PCLs are being met is apparent.
In this report, we demonstrate that the calculational analysis of Britton et al.7 can be enhanced by
the quantitative inclusion of uncertainties into the components listed in each campaign. This
approach allows us to identify, on a campaign-specific basis, which WACs and PCLs are at risk
of being exceeded and by which analytes. Taken as a whole, we have generated a prioritized
analyte short-list that can be a focus of subsequent instrument development. Relatedly, the
expected concentrations of the analytes can be established, informing the construction of test
simulants as well as providing appropriate targets for method sensitivities. It is important to note
that the WACs and PCLs for elemental composition are typically specified as total values
irrespective of the chemical form or phase in which the element appears. The identity of these
forms and phases and their distribution between the solid and supernatant phases of the slurry
are also tracked. As the sensitivities of spectroscopy-based chemical analysis methods are often
dependent on the form of the analyte and its environment9, 10, an understanding of the expected
phases of the material will drive appropriate method development and validation.
This report is organized as follows. Section 3.0 describes the data sources and calculational
approaches for the analysis. Section 4.0 provides analysis results, specifically the identification
of at-risk analytes and, for each of those analytes, an analysis of their concentrations, elemental
and molecular composition, and distribution between solid and supernatant phases in the slurry.
Section 5.0 summarizes these results, describes further information that can be gleaned from this
data set, and generalizes our approach and findings to other waste processing scenarios.

3.0 Data and Analysis Strategies

3.1 Input Data

The Tank Waste Inventory Network System (TWINS) is comprised of a set of databases and other
tools that house data and support characterization of Hanford Site tank waste.11 Within this
network is the Best Basis Inventory Maintenance (BBIM) database in which the individual tank
inventory data is kept, including analyte concentrations, densities, waste volumes, and other
physical, chemical, and radiological properties. These Best Basis Inventories (BBIs) are the
source of the waste data explored in this work. This data is described in Appendices D (“Layer-
by-Layer Feed Vectors and Analysis”) and E (“Blended Feed Vectors and Analysis”) of Britton et
al.7 The electronic files containing the data were provided to the authors in February 2025 † by
one of the authors of Britton et al.7
In the anticipated blending operations, tank waste layers will be retrieved using a supernatant and
secondary liquid effluent to achieve a target liquid density and solids loading in the retrieved waste
stream. The supernatants used in these operations are to be derived from those available in the
tank farm; the supernatant liquid densities and secondary effluent compositions are described in
Britton et al.7 A number of scenarios using the various available supernatants and that target a
range of liquid densities and solids loadings were generated; the calculated retrieved waste
stream compositions (RWSC) by layer ‡ are subsequently fed into a blending algorithm to generate
campaigns of blended layers §. The scenarios contained within the database are differentiated by
the supernatant density used to retrieve the solids, target liquid density, and target weight percent
solids loading. The ranges of these properties are: supernatant density, 1.20 - 1.40 g/mL; liquid
density, 1.04 - 1.20 g/mL; solids loading, 5 – 20%. Herein, we have performed analysis on the
scenario for supernatant density of 1.35 g/mL, liquid density of 1.2 g/mL and 20% solids loading,
labeled “135.120.200”. ** While each scenario results in a different number of campaigns, for the
scenario explored in this work, it was calculated that optimal blending could be achieved over the
course of seventeen blending campaigns.7 We chose to perform our analysis assuming this
processing scenario because it has the highest concentration of solids in the melter and is one of
the more aggressive scenarios with respect to the potential to exceed the WACs and PCLs. Thus,
it should indicate the largest number of analytes that potentially need to be monitored.

3.2 Identification of At-Risk Analytes Via Monte Carlo Simulation

To identify at-risk analytes in the selected feed vector scenario, Monte Carlo (MC) simulations
were applied to explore the effects of uncertainty that exist in the reported analyte concentrations,

which may result from several sources. The analytical measurement techniques or modeling used
to derive the reported concentrations values naturally contribute to uncertainty, though the
magnitude of this effect for each analyte is not currently known to the authors. Sample aliquots of
each layer analyzed may not be fully representative of the entire layer due to potential
heterogeneity. For example, deposition of the solid waste can create contouring in the layer.7
Similarly, the waste layers represented in this data are non-discrete, meaning that any mixing that
may occur at layer interfaces is not represented in the reported analyte concentrations. Finally,
when considering the transfer of waste for blending, there is likely some error in the total amount
of waste being blended from each layer, leading to further uncertainty in the final concentrations
reported for potential blended feed vectors. The authors of Ref. 7 acknowledge the existence of
these and other unknowns and specify the assumptions that must be made to estimate the
compositions of the proposed blends.
To better understand which analytes are at risk of exceeding the WAC and PCLs, a random
relative error of ± 20% representing the 95% confidence interval was incorporated into each
analyte concentration at the waste layer level and propagated via MC simulation to generate
concentration ranges for each blending campaign (as defined in Appendix B) to compare against
the WAC and PCL. This blanket relative error level was selected to account for not only a lack of
access to any reported uncertainties for each analyte concentration but also the other sources of
error described in the above paragraph that have not been characterized for their relative error
contributions. MC simulations were run for 1000 iterations. Following the logic of Britton et al.7,
concentration ranges were also compared against an 80% threshold of the WAC or PCL, serving
as an indicator of “borderline at-risk” analytes or groups. Certain parameters (e.g. unit dose,
equivalent Cs-137 concentration, etc.) that would not be addressed using the RTM methods (i.e.,
LIBS, Raman, ATR-FTIR) under consideration for this project were not included in the evaluation
of at-risk analytes. Additionally, parameters such as solids loading, bulk density, and liquid density
parameters were also excluded from evaluation given that these are pre-defined values of the
feed vector scenario selected (i.e. 135.120.200) and are therefore predetermined to exceed at
least the 80% margin. Analyte or analyte group distributions were evaluated based on Box and
Whisker plot analysis. Distributions whose top whisker, indicating the maximum value in the
distribution not exceeding 1.5 times the interquartile range (IQR), exceeding the WAC/PCL and/or
80% margin were categorized as at-risk analytes or analyte groups.

3.3 Concentration Levels and Distribution of At-Risk Analytes

For those analytes (or groups of analytes) that were identified as at-risk and that are most
compatible with the RTM methods under investigation, the average concentration across waste
layers and campaigns was investigated further. This information is useful for comparison against
the analytical detection limits of these methods as well as to support development of realistic
simulants for method development. Additionally, the distribution of the at-risk analytes between
the solid and supernatant phases and the dominant chemical forms were investigated to better
understand how to tailor the analytical techniques to the anticipated waste forms.

potassium concentrations (NaK = [Na] + 0.59[K]), (3) the sum of aluminum, iron, and zirconium
concentrations (AlFeZr), (4) the sulfur concentration (S), and (5) the thorium concentration (Th).

4.2 Concentration Levels of At-Risk Analytes

The efficacy of the blending towards reducing instances of exceeding the WACs and PCLs can
be observed through the narrower distribution of concentrations across campaigns compared to
the individual layers. This is demonstrated in Figure 3, which shows the distribution of
concentrations across individual layers (Figure 3A) and in blends (Figure 3B). The distribution of
concentrations in the blends is narrower (as evidenced by the widths of the quartile boxes), and
the maximum extremes of concentrations observed in the layers are suppressed in the blends.

4.2.1 NOx-

Total Concentration. NOx- is defined as the sum of the nitrite (NO2-) and nitrate (NO3-) ion
concentrations, where [NOx-] = [NO2-] + [NO3-]. The WAC defines the limit for the molar
concentration of this NOx- analyte group to be ≤ 2.7 mol/L in the liquid waste (Appendix A). The
values of [NO2-] and [NO3-] in individual tank waste layers and campaigns are presented in Figure
4 and Figure 5, respectively. The concentrations shown represent those calculated directly from
RWSC values for waste layers and campaigns, without the effects of uncertainties incorporated
as described above. The WAC is represented by a solid red line. In Figure 5, asterisks (*) and
plus signs (+) denote campaigns where MC simulations (Section 4.1) determined the NOx- analyte
group was at risk of exceeding the WAC and 80% margin, respectively. The total NOx-
concentration ranges between ~0.7 mol/L and ~3 mol/L across waste layers; blending cuts this
concentration range in half, generating total NOx- concentrations between 1.2 and 2.3 mol/L
across campaigns. Both NO2- and NO3- are found essentially only in the liquid phase. A small
portion of the total NO3- concentration comes from solid bound NO3-; however, the WAC is defined
in terms of the liquid waste and therefore this solid contribution does not count towards the
evaluation of the NOX- criterion. The solid and liquid concentrations of NO3- in the slurry can be
found in Appendix D for reference.

4.2.2.1 Sodium

Total Concentration. As the primary contributor to the “at-risk” NaK process control parameter,
Na will be a key analyte to monitor via RTM. While there is no individual PCL defined for Na, the
effective limit for Na – termed “Na Only PCL” in this report – is 6.96 mol/L, which is calculated as
a conversion of the NaK mass concentration limit (160,000 mg/L) to molar concentration under
an assumption of no contribution from the K analyte. As exhibited in Figure 3A above, the median
Na concentration across waste layers is 5.03 mol/L, with extremes sitting at 1.2 mol/L and 6.5
mol/L. After blending (Figure 3B), this median Na concentration becomes 4.79 mol/L, with all the

campaigns falling between 3.5 mol/L and 6.2 mol/L. While the range of Na concentrations shrinks
substantially with blending, the maximum concentration decreases minimally.
Solid / Liquid Distribution. Figure 9 shows the relative molar concentrations of Na in the liquid
(yellow) and solid (purple) phases for each waste layer. The solid red line represents the “Na Only
PCL” as described above. Substantial variation in the Na phase across the individual is observed,
where Na fluctuates from almost completely in the liquid phase to having a nearly equal
distribution between solid and liquid phases. The layers with proportionally large Na distributions
in the solid phase tend to have the highest total Na concentrations and approach the NaK PCL
irrespective of any K contributions. This observation is not surprising since high Na concentrations
would promote saturation resulting in the formation of solid species.

does not change dramatically after blending – both layers and blends result in an average Na

Predominant Species. The species that make up the liquid Na concentrations include Na+ and

The proposed blending strategies are based on calculations that predict that none of the WACs
or PCLs will be exceeded, under the assumption that the values used to make the calculations
are exactly correct. The definition of “at-risk” analytes used here reflects the potential importance
of several sources of uncertainty that could lead to actual concentrations varying from the
calculated values. These sources include uncertainties of the composition of the waste layers,
anticipated waste stream heterogeneity (e.g. representativeness of sampling layers from tanks,

 29

especially where partial sampling is specified), and possible imprecision in waste transfer
volumes.
The analyte concentration data reported in the BBI have been derived in various manners; some
represent sample-based data, while others are calculated from other sample data, based on
process knowledge, derived from templates, or modeled.16, 17 Each mode of establishing the BBI
value carries a different level of uncertainty, with sample-based data generally providing the
highest level of confidence. Only inventory values derived from sample-based data (including
sample-based templates) have an uncertainty reported along with them.16 The available sample-
based uncertainties were not included in the data used for this work. Future work can include
more accurate uncertainty estimates where available and assign different uncertainties for
different sources (in contrast to simply assigning an estimated 20% relative uncertainty across all
constituents). Further, incorporating methods similar to those reported by Brown and Kosson18,
where uncertainties were also estimated and propagated for non-sample-based inventory values,
may provide a better understanding of uncertainty in the blended compositions.
Ultimately, the assignment of WACs/PCLs and campaigns as being “at-risk” will be driven by the
uncertainties of the RTM methods used to analyze the waste streams. However, this practical
approach will not remove the value of the predictive analysis outlined here. It may be determined
from experimental work that a measurement of a specific analyte can be biased due to the
presence of another specific species in the slurry. For example, Fe is known to suppress the LIBS
signal of solids and powders due to alteration of the plasma temperatures. Also, LIBS can be
susceptible to overlap of emission lines for samples with many elements present.9 Analysis of the
BBI data can anticipate when these scenarios may occur, leading to the development of
campaign-specific calibrations and/or data fusion approaches to compensate for these effects.
All the analytes discussed here, except S, are present with concentrations that are anticipated to
be well above the detection limits of the methods being tested, even with the added complication
of the two-phase composition of the slurries. S concentrations are near the demonstrated
detection limits for ATR-FTIR measurements of the supernate components of slurries.6 The
detection limits for S in either the liquid or solid phase of slurries for LIBS measurements have yet
to be determined. One approach that can be considered if those detection limits are not sufficiently
low for these streams is to determine if there are higher concentration “marker species” with
concentrations that correlate with [S]. For blends of multiple layers, the concentration ratio
between the marker species and the target species in the individual layers should be consistent,
so that the ratio in the blend can be reasonably expected to be the same as in the individual
layers. For a species like S that for some campaigns will appear in both the solid and supernate
phases of the slurry, tracking should be independent for each phase, and the specific marker
species may be different for each phase. Furthermore, the varying properties of the layers and
campaigns indicate that marker and target pairs identified for one campaign may not be
appropriate for another campaign. Future work will examine the potential for using marker species
to supplement or replace direct sulfur analysis for slurries in this campaign scenario. As noted
above regarding interferences, data fusion approaches involving multiple measurements may
prove to be more effective in estimating the concentrations of low-concentration species than the
measurement of a single marker species.
