Authors: Wes Baumgartner, Ute Bach, James Baily, Christine Ruehl-Fehlert, Stacey Fossey, Satoshi Furukawa, Yuki Kato, Victoria Laast, Emily Meseck, Heike Schmidt-Posthaus, Helmut Segner, Shiva Kumar Shanmukhappa, Jan Spitsbergen, Jeffrey Wolf

Introduction

From a physiological perspective, there is a high degree of functional similarity between piscine and mammalian endocrine systems. Accordingly, hormonal signaling molecules, hormone receptors, and stimulatory and inhibitory feedback loops are all surprisingly well-conserved among these distant vertebrate taxa. Conversely, the structural composition and anatomic distribution of endocrine tissues vary markedly between mammals and fish, and to a somewhat lesser degree among fish species; examples of this variability will be highlighted in the discussion to follow. Histopathologic evidence of spontaneous lesions or induced effects involving fish endocrine glands is relatively uncommon in toxicological studies, aside from a few notable exceptions in selected species (e.g., ultimobranchial gland tumors in zebrafish Danio rerio).

Major endocrine tissues in fish include: chromaffin and interrenal cells, the corpuscles of Stannius, islets of the endocrine pancreas, pineal gland, pituitary gland, thyroid follicles, ultimobranchial gland, and the urophysis. Excluded from this category, but discussed elsewhere in this issue, are additional tissues that are capable of producing endocrine or paracrine signals, such as the gastrointestinal tract, gonads, heart, kidney, leukocytes, and liver.

Necropsy and Trimming

Because fish endocrine organs are small and often buried within other tissues, they are rarely excised specifically for routine examination in toxicological studies, and this is true even for investigations that involve larger fish species. Exceptions include: interrenal and chromaffin tissues, which may appear in histologic sections if certain areas of anterior kidney are sampled; the corpuscles of Stannius, which may be collected intentionally or unintentionally with segments of posterior kidney; islets of endocrine pancreas, which may be present in areas of mesenteric pancreas collected with gut specimens; and the pituitary gland which may remain attached to the brain if it is sampled. Additionally, special effort can be made to excise certain tissues, e.g., pharyngeal tissue that contains thyroid follicles, if they are suspected to be toxicologic targets. More commonly, endocrine organs are visualized in whole-body sections of small fish species, or in sections of larvae or juveniles of large fishes. However, due to their diminutive size, endocrine tissues can be difficult to recover consistently in whole-body sections, and thus the acquisition of multiple step sections at and near the sagittal midline (where many endocrine organs are located) may be required.

Chromaffin and interrenal cells are the piscine analogues of adrenal medullary and cortical tissue, respectively. Unlike the encapsulated adrenal glands of mammals, in teleost fish these tissues are typically located as linear or patchy cords of cells in the anterior kidney, often immediately adjacent to or near the posterior cardinal veins. The interrenal and chromaffin cells are intermingled in some species and separated in others1, and if there is a difference between the cell types that can be appreciated in H&E sections, it is that interrenal cell cytoplasm typically stains more intensely pink compared to the paler chromaffin cells.

Chromaffin cells produce catecholamines that include epinephrine, norepinephrine, and lesser amounts of dopamine. In addition to serving as a component of the fish stress response, catecholemines have roles in regulating gill blood flow and oxygen uptake. The major hormone produced by interrenal cells is cortisol, followed by lesser amounts of corticosterone, deoxycorticosterone, and aldosterone.

Interestingly, cortisol also functions as a mineralocorticoid in fish.

Chromaffin and Interrenal Cells – Non-Proliferative

Comments

Spontaneous and induced lesions involving the interrenal or chromaffin cells are uncommon. Because of their location within the anterior kidney they may be inadevertently affected by renal inflammation or other kidney diseases.

Atrophy, chromaffin or interrenal cells

Other Terms

• Chromaffin cells, reduced; Interrenal cells, reduced

Pathogenesis

• A reduction of cellular material such that a reduction in cell size is clearly evident, possibly due to reduced stimulus

Diagnostic Features

• Chromaffin or interrenal cells are smaller than expected but typically display normal cytologic features

Differential Diagnoses

• Autolysis

Comments

Atrophy of chromaffin or interrenal tissue has not been reported as a spontaneous finding and appears to be rare as an induced effect. Interrenal cell atrophy, associated with low blood cortisol levels, was reported in northern pike Esox lucius and yellow perch Perca flavescens that were exposed to bleached kraft mill effluent2.

A diagnosis of atrophy should be based on evidence of decreased cell size and not merely a decrease in the amount of tissue present, as the amount of chromaffin/interrenal tissue visualized in a single histologic section is highly variable.

Stereological methods may be useful for confirming decreases in chromaffin or interrenal cell size.

Nomenclature recommendation

• Interrenal or chromaffin cells can alternatively be referred to as interrenal or chromaffin tissue

Hypertrophy, chromaffin or interrenal cells

Other Terms

• Chromaffin cells, enlarged

• Interrenal cells, enlarged

Pathogenesis

• An increase of cellular material such that an enlargement in cell size is clearly evident, possibly due to increased stimulus

Diagnostic Features

• Chromaffin or interrenal cells are enlarged and may have enlarged nuclei

Differential Diagnoses

• Vacuolation

• Autolysis

Comments

Interrenal cell hypertrophy was reported in fish collected from an urban stream3 and from water contaminated with metals such as cadmium and zinc4. It has been proposed that hypertrophy of these cell types may be a response to chronic physiological stress. It is unknown whether chromaffin or interrenal cell hyperplasia is associated with increased catecholamine or corticosteroid secretion, respectively.

Comments

The example of chromaffin cell hyperplasia illustrated in this paper occurred spontaneously in a single guppy Poecilia reticulata. Interrenal cell hyperplasia (and hypertrophy) was reported in brown trout Salmo trutta collected from water contaminated with metals such as cadmium and zinc4, and both chromaffin and interrenal cell hyperplasia were observed in paddlefish Polyodon spathula from water contaminated with chlordane and PCBs5.

Credible changes in cell numbers can be difficult to confirm, because the amount of chromaffin/interrenal tissue in a histologic section is highly dependent on the precise locations available for viewing, and the plane of section acquired.As such, morphometric (e.g., stereological) tools are used to quantify these tissues in serial or step sections. It is important to recognize that the perception of increased nuclear density can be a function of increased cell number, decreased cell size, or both. Additionally, it is not always easy to distinguish chromaffin from interrenal cells, especially for species where cell types are mixed, as well as in cases of suboptimal histologic staining. It is unknown whether hyperplastic proliferation of these cell types is associated with increased catecholamine or corticosteroid secretion.

Chromaffin and Interrenal Cells – Neoplastic

Neoplasms involving piscine chromaffin or interrenal tissues are not typically observed in toxicological studies.

These discrete, bilaterally paired, finely-encapsulated ovoid structures are typically embedded in the posterior kidney, ventrolaterally along the caudal aspect. They can be visualized macroscopically in some larger species. The anterior-posterior location of these glands varies according to species, and the right and left gland may be offset to some degree. The glands themselves consist of cells with basilar nuclei that are arranged in tightly folded cords or packets. The apical margins of the cells appose capillaries that are embedded within connective tissue septa. From a pathological perspective, it is imperative not to misdiagnose these normal structures as renal tubular adenomas.

Blood calcium levels in fish are regulated by hormones produced by the corpuscles of Stannius, the ultimobranchial body, and the pituitary gland. The corpuscles of Stannius secrete stanniocalcin, which reduces plasma calcium by decreasing its absorption via the gills and intestine. Conversely, calcitonin, which is produced by the ultimobranchial body (fish lack thyroid gland C-cells), is thought to reduce calcium via increased renal excretion and increased bone sequestration. Parathyroid hormone, and more notably in fish, parathyroid hormone-related protein (PTHrP), counteract these effects by increasing plasma calcium. Fish lack parathyroid glands, and production of PTHrP is thought to occur in the pituitary and possibly other peripheral sites6. Additional substances that may play a role in piscine calcium homeostasis include cortisol, isotocin, hydrogen sulfide, and insulin-like growth factor7.

Spontaneous and induced lesions involving the corpuscles of Stannius are rare. Because of their intimate anatomic association with the posterior kidney they may be affected inadvertently by renal inflammation or other kidney diseases.

Piscine islets consist of alpha, beta and delta cells; the proportions and distributions vary depending on the species8, 9. As in mammals, the alpha cells produce glucagon-like peptide which promotes hyperglycemia and gluconeogenesis, the beta cells produce insulin which promotes hypoglycemia and transport of glucose into tissues, and delta cells are the source of somatostatin which inhibits the release of insulin and glucagon. Studies have demonstrated that amino acids may actually have a greater capacity than glucose to stimulate insulin and glucagon-like peptide release10.

Endocrine pancreas (islets) may be affected as bystanders in peritoneal inflammatory diseases or intraperitoneal vaccine reactions that affect the abdominal mesentery and exocrine pancreas. In some cases, the endocrine pancreas is relatively spared, as in alphavirus infections (“pancreas disease”) of Atlantic salmon Salmo salar. There is little evidence to suggest that the endocrine pancreas is a common target of toxicants. The few published reports to date include: vacuolation and degranulation of beta cells in Clarias batrachus as a response to glibenclamide (an anti-diabetic therapeutic agent) exposure11; similar effects in the alpha cells of Channa punctatus following cobalt salts exposure12; islet cell hyperplasia in a cutthroat trout Salmo clarki exposed to the insecticide endrin13; and disrupted islet organogenesis in zebrafish Danio rerio embryos exposed to perfluorooctanesulfonic acid14. Initial observations of islet cell hypertrophy or atypia, or islet gland hypertrophy, should be interpreted cautiously; such changes may be evident in untreated controls of some species, such as Japanese medaka Oryzias latipes.

Diagnostic Features

Comments

Islet cell hyperplasia has not generally been observed as a spontaneous finding, although there has been some association made between islet mass and spawning in salmonids16. One of the most convincing reports of islet cell hyperplasia involved cutthroat trout Salmo clarki, in which greater than one third of male and female fish developed this condition following year-long experimental exposure to the insecticide endrin13. Both extra-islet proliferation or hyperplasia of islets cells may be observed as spontaneous or induced findings. Observations and the literature also support a role for metaplastic transformation of duct cells to budding islet endocrine cells, reported as nesidioblastosis17. Given the variation in presentation and definition, the descriptive terms of hyperplasia or metaplasia are recommended rather than nesidioblastosis.

Many fish species have large primary islets (Brockman bodies), and it is imperative not to misdiagnose these normal anatomic structures as hyperplastic or neoplastic islets.

Located along the axial midline, the piscine pituitary is a plump or elongated ovoid structure which in most laboratory teleosts is suspended ventral to the diencephalon by a short narrow infundibular stalk (leptobasic type) (Figs. 4.32, 4.33 and 4.34Fig. 4.32.Medaka. The pituitary gland may be partially encased in a bony sella turcica (e.g., zebrafish) or not surrounded at all by bone (e.g., Japanese medaka Oryzias latipes). As in mammals, the primary components of the pituitary are the adenohypophysis and the neurohypophysis, which interdigitate extensively. The adenohypophysis consists of the pars distalis and the posteriorly located pars intermedia. Based on morphologic and functional characteristics, the pars distalis can be further divided into rostral and caudal portions. Located in the rostral pars distalis are the eosinophilic-staining prolactin cells, adrenocorticotrophic hormone (ACTH)-secreting cells, and the non-granular chromophobe cells. The caudal pars distalis contains somatotropic, gonadotropic, and thyrotropic cells. Cells that produce melanocyte stimulating hormone (MSH) are the chief component of the pars intermedia. The vascularized pars nervosa is comprised of neurosecretory fibers and glial pituicytes. The precise arrangement and proportional representation of the various hypophyseal subdivisions differ markedly among fish species. For example, in zebrafish there is a sizeable rostral extension of the neurohypophysis that is designated as the anterior pars nervosa21. There is no hypothalamic-hypophyseal portal vascular system in most teleosts; instead, the pituitary receives input from the hypothalamus via neurons.

As in mammals, the piscine pituitary produces a variety of hormones that modulate functions in a number of other endocrine glands and organ systems. These include adrenocorticotropic hormone, gonadotropic hormones (e.g., follicle stimulating and luteinizing hormones), growth hormone, prolactin, somatolactin, and thyroid stimulating hormone. In most instances, the function of these pituitary messengers is analogous to their mammalian counterparts, but exceptions exist. For example, rather than stimulation of milk production and release, the predominant role of prolactin in fish is osmoregulatory22.

Although many studies have demonstrated putative disruption of pituitary function in fishes as a result of experimental hormonal perturbation, there are few reliable accounts of toxicologically-induced morphologic changes in the piscine pituitary. For example, Kirubagaran and Joy report that degeneration, degranulation, hypertrophy, and hyperplasia of pituitary ACTH-producing cells occurred following exposure to mercuric chloride; however, based on the included figure photomicrographs, those findings have little credibility due to substandard quality of the histologic preparation23. Additionally, caution should be exercised when comparing the numbers and proportions of various adenohypophyseal cell types among animals and treatment groups, because cellular components can vary markedly depending on the precise plane of histologic sectioning in each animal. Furthermore, there is evidence that the relative sizes and numbers of different hormone-secreting cells may differ according to developmental stage, age, endogenous and exogenous hormonal signals, and reproductive status in fish that spawn seasonally24,25,26.

Physiological aspects involving the thyroid follicles and hypothalamic-pituitary-thyroid axis are highly conserved among vertebrates and fish are no exception. Thyroid stimulating hormone (TSH) secreted by the pituitary drives synthesis of L-thyroxine (T4) in the thyroid follicle, and peripheral conversion of T4 to the active metabolite triiodothyronine (T3) occurs via enzymatic monodeiodination. Thyroid hormone (TH) exerts effects by binding to nuclear receptors that function as DNA-binding transcription factors, and hormone inactivation in the kidney, brain, liver, and muscle is mediated by iodothyronine- deiodinases. Thyroid hormones have roles in growth, metabolism, development, reproduction, pigment deposition, and are thought to be instrumental for the metamorphic transformation of larval to juvenile stages34.

Aside from involvement in systemic or regional inflammation, it is rare to observe non-proliferative lesions, whether spontaneous or treatment-induced, in the thyroid tissue of teleosts used in toxicological studies. To some extent, that may be from failure to consistently include the lower jaw as a tissue designated for examination. Proliferative changes affecting the thyroid are relatively more common, however, and include both hyperplastic and neoplastic lesions. The frequent finding of follicular tumors within areas of follicular hyperplasia suggests that progression of hyperplasia to benign or malignant neoplasia occurs along a continuum35. By necessity, criteria used to determine malignancy differ from those used for mammals. In mammals the predominant indicators of malignancy are local invasion and distant metastasis. However, fish have cells with the potential to form thyroid tissue distributed along multiple midline areas throughout their bodies, either in the form of overt follicles or embryonic cellular rests. Thus, when pituitary production of TSH increases (e.g., following exposure to a goitrogenic substance or as a result of low iodine levels), both the pharyngeal and ectopic thyroid tissue may become hyperplastic or even neoplastic. Consequently, hyperplasia of ectopic thyroid glands, with or without evidence of hyperplasia in primary thyroid locations, must be differentiated from the extremely rare metastasis of thyroid adenocarcinoma; the latter can be distinguished by aberrant growth pattern (lack of follicles lined by single epithelial layer) and/or the presence of cellular atypia.

• Follicles are moderately well-differentiated (still recognizable as thyroid gland) and lined by a single layer of follicular epithelial cells

• Cells are usually hypertrophic, and papillary infolding may be prominent

• Adenomas are internally homogeneous, but the growth pattern and/or coloration typically differs from the surrounding hyperplastic thyroid tissue (if present)

• The amount of colloid is usually variable to markedly reduced, in which case the lesion may have a nearly solid appearance

• Mitotic figures are rare

Differential Diagnoses

• Follicular cell hyperplasia may be focal or multifocal, can mimic invasiveness

• Follicular cell carcinoma – prominent pleomorphism with an anaplastic growth pattern and decreased or absent colloid

Comments

Adenomas differ from hyperplasia because the former are discrete expansile masses that tend to be less well-differentiated compared to hyperplastic lesions. Carcinomas do not retain the basic follicular architecture in which follicles are lined by a single cell layer, are generally not discrete lesions, and exhibit cellular atypia and increased numbers of mitoses35.

Other Terms

• Malignant follicular cell tumor

Pathogenesis

Because they often appear within areas of follicular cell hyperplasia, it has been speculated that follicular cell neoplasms may tend to arise from hyperplastic lesions35; this is presumably due to the increased opportunity for transcription errors in a cell population characterized by increased cell division. Carcinogenic chemical induction of thyroid follicular cell tumors may occur in addition to, or in lieu of, hyperplasia41, 42

Modifiers (qualifiers or secondary descriptors)

• Well differentiated

• Poorly differentiated

Diagnostic Features

• Small to very large, poorly circumscribed, ovoid or irregular mass

• Often, but not always, found within areas of follicular cell hyperplasia

• Generally, see piling up of cells rather than follicles lined by a single cell layer

• Even relatively “well-differentiated” tumors do not usually display standard thyroid follicular architecture

• In poorly differentiated carcinomas, evidence of follicle formation may be minimal or not at all evident

• Frequently, minimal if any colloid formation

• Common to see marked cellular atypia and numerous mitotic figures

Differential Diagnoses

• Follicular cell hyperplasia may be focal or multifocal, can mimic invasiveness

• Follicular cell adenoma is a well circumscribed space occupying mass with or without compression

Comments

Although local invasion and distant metastasis are the hallmarks of malignant thyroid tumor identification in mammals, these attributes are not generally useful for fish thyroid neoplasms because of the widespread distribution of ectopic thyroid progenitor tissue in fish. Instead, diagnoses of malignancy are based primarily on aberrant growth patterns and cytologic atypia. Additionally, there is evidence in both fish and rodents that even true thyroid neoplasms may regress following correction of the stimulus for cell proliferation (e.g., low iodine)43, 44. One proposed theory to explain this is that tumors generated during prolonged periods of elevated TSH production may remain TSH-dependent due to the lack of selection pressure to become TSH-independent44.

7. Ultimobranchial gland

The ultimobranchial gland (body) is a single midline organ located dorsal to the heart within the transverse membrane that separates the pericardial cavity from the coelomic cavity. This finely- encapsulated angular organ consists of deeply basophilic cells that have basally oriented nuclei and are arranged in pseudorosette patterns. Small and often difficult to capture in histologic sections, the ultimobranchial body is readily visualized in zebrafish which, coincidentally, is also the species in which neoplasms of this gland are most likely to be found45. Fish do not possess thyroid gland C-cells, and instead calcitonin is secreted primarily by the ultimobranchial gland.

Major hormones synthesized and secreted by the urophysis are urotensin I, which is structurally similar to corticotropin-releasing hormone, and urotensin II, which shares structural similarity with somatostatin54. Urotensins are thought to influence stress responses and osmoregulation and may function as brain neurotransmitters. Urotensin II, which is also present in mammals, has vasoactive effects when administered experimentally54.

Spontaneous or induced lesions of the piscine urophysis have not been reported.
