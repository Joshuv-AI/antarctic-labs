Efficient Temporal Datalog Materialisation for Composite Event Recognition
Periklis Mantenoglou \affiliations Örebro University, Sweden \emails periklis.mantenoglou@oru.se

Abstract
Several applications demand the timely detection of critical situations, such as threats to safety and transparency, over high-velocity streams of symbolic events. This demand has motivated the development of (i) event specification languages , which define composite events via temporal patterns over simpler events, and (ii) stream reasoning frameworks , evaluating patterns expressed in these languages. However, event specification languages are typically studied in isolation, complicating their comparison in terms of expressivity and obscuring the scope of their associated stream reasoners. To mitigate this issue, we map practical fragments of prominent event specification languages into Temporal Datalog → ⊝ \it{}^{\rightarrow}_{\circleddash} , a temporal Datalog with stratified negation and no future dependencies. To support efficient stream reasoning over Temporal Datalog → ⊝ \it{}^{\rightarrow}_{\circleddash} , we propose Streaming Trigger Graphs, an extension of a state-of-the-art technique for Datalog materialisation. Our approach yields a uniform composite event recognition mechanism that has the potential to generalise across a wide range of practical event specification languages.

Background

2.1
Logic Programming

We investigate ESLs that are based on logic programming.
Logic programs include variables, constants, predicates, and functors.
Variables start with an upper-case letter, whereas predicates, functors and constants start with a lower-case letter.
A term is either a variable, a constant, or an expression
\it f(\boldsymbol{X})
, where
\it f
is a functor and
\it\boldsymbol{X}
is a term tuple.
An expression
\it p(\boldsymbol{X})
, where
\it p
is a predicate and
\it\boldsymbol{X}
is a term tuple, is called an atom.
A fact is a variable-free atom.
A literal is an atom optionally preceded by “
not
”, expressing negation-by-failure
[
15
]
.
Arity means number of arguments.

A logic program is a set of rules
\it{h\leftarrow b_{1},\dots,b_{n}}
, where
\it\leftarrow
denotes implication, atom
\it h
is the head of the rule and its body
\it b_{1},\dots,b_{n}
is a conjunction of conditions.
All variables in a rule are implicitly universally quantified.
In definite (resp. normal) logic programs, all body conditions are atoms (literals).
An expression is called ground if it contains no variables.
A substitution is a mapping of the terms in an expression to constants.
A predicate is called intensional if it appears in the head of a rule with non-empty body, and extensional otherwise.
A database
\it D
is a set of facts with extensional predicates.
A Datalog program is a definite logic program without functors.
Every Datalog program can be rewritten into an equivalent program partitioned into extensional rules (with only extensional body predicates) and intensional rules (with only intensional body predicates).

An interpretation of a logic program
\it P
is a set of facts from
\it P
.
To define the semantics of
\it P
, we identify its models, i.e., interpretations satisfying all rules in
\it P
.
Definite logic programs have a unique minimal model, while normal logic programs (which include negation) may have multiple minimal models, complicating their semantic characterisation.
To interpret such programs, the closed world assumption (CWA) allows us to infer “
\it\text{not}\ a
” if
\it a
cannot be finitely proven
[
40
]
.
Thus, for a program with rule “
\it a\leftarrow b,\text{not}\ c
” and fact
\it b
, model
\it\{b,a\}
is preferred over model
\it\{b,c\}
, because, since it is impossible to prove
\it c
, the CWA infers “
\it\text{not}\ c
”, leading to the derivation of
\it a
via the rule.
When the program does not include cyclic dependencies via
not
, i.e., it is
stratified
, we can derive an intended interpretation for it by applying the CWA to negated atoms following rule dependencies bottom-up
[
39
]
.

Definition 1

(Global Stratification and Local Stratification)
.

Consider a normal logic program
\it P
with the predicates in set
\it\Sigma
and the ground atoms in set
\it B
.
A rule
\it r
in
\it P
is:

\it\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{l}h(\boldsymbol{X_{h}})\leftarrow b_{1}(\boldsymbol{X_{b_{1}}}),\dots,b_{m}(\boldsymbol{X_{b_{m}}}),\\
\hskip 20.44434pt\hskip 20.44434pt\;\text{not}\ c_{1}(\boldsymbol{X_{c_{1}}}),\dots,\text{not}\ c_{k}(\boldsymbol{X_{c_{k}}}).\end{array}

\it P
is globally stratified if
\it\Sigma
can be decomposed into disjoint sets (strata)
\it\Sigma_{1}
, …,
\it\Sigma_{n}
, so that, for every
\it r
in
\it P
, we have that, if
\it h\in\Sigma_{i}
, where
\it i\in[n]
, then: (i)
\it b_{t}\in\Sigma_{j}
, where
\it t\in[m]
and
\it j\leq i
, and (ii)
\it c_{t}\in\Sigma_{j}
, where
\it t\in[k]
and
\it j<i
.
Such a decomposition is a global stratification of
\it P
.

\it P
is locally stratified if
\it B
can be split into (possibly infinite) strata
\it B_{1},B_{2},\dots
, so that, for every ground instance of a rule
\it r
in
\it P
, if
\it h(\boldsymbol{x_{h}})\in B_{i}
then: (i)
\it b_{t}(\boldsymbol{x_{b_{t}}})\in B_{j}
, where
\it t{\in}[m]
and
\it j\leq i
, and (ii)
\it c_{t}(\boldsymbol{x_{c_{t}}})\in B_{j}
, where
\it t{\in}[k]
and
\it j<i
.
Such a decomposition is a local stratification of
\it P
.

Based on Definition
1
, every globally stratified program is also locally stratified.
Local stratification, however, is undecidable in the general case, while global stratification can be decided in polynomial time
[
38
]
.
A stratified program admits a unique
perfect model

[
39
]
, derived by (i) computing a stratification, and (ii) evaluating its rules in a bottom-up order based on the stratum of their head while applying the CWA to negative conditions.

2.2
Temporal Datalog

A temporal program is a logic program where constants are split into objects and time-points, variables are split into object variables and time variables, and time-points are positive integers.
An object term is an object or an object variable.
A temporal fact is a fact including exactly one time-point.
The model of a temporal program is evaluated over a stream, i.e., an unbounded database of temporal facts.

Datalog programs have been studied extensively
[
53
,
45
]
, and several temporal extensions have been proposed
[
60
,
58
,
10
]
.
Such an extension is Datalog
1S
where each predicate is augmented with an additional argument featuring a
time term

[
14
]
.
A time term is
\it T\minus k
, where
\it T
is a time variable and
\it k
is an integer called the time offset.
Integer addition is silently applied when
\it T
is grounded. We focus on Temporal Datalog, i.e., a fragment of Datalog
1S
where each rule may include at most one time variable
[
42
]
.

To formalise Temporal Datalog
\it{}^{\rightarrow}_{\circleddash}
, i.e., the Temporal Datalog variant we use for embedding ESLs, we make two modifications on Temporal Datalog: (i) we introduce a restricted form of negation, and (ii) we disallow derivations towards the past.
A Temporal Datalog
\it{}^{\rightarrow}_{\circleddash}
rule has the following form:

\it\begin{array}[]{l}h(\boldsymbol{X_{h}},T)\leftarrow\\
\hskip 10.22217ptb_{1}(\boldsymbol{X_{b_{1}}},T\minus k_{b_{1}}),\dots,b_{m}(\boldsymbol{X_{b_{m}}},T\minus k_{b_{m}}),\\[1.0pt]
\hskip 10.22217pt\text{not}\ c_{1}(\boldsymbol{X_{c_{1}}},T\minus k_{c_{1}}),\dots,\text{not}\ c_{k}(\boldsymbol{X_{c_{k}}},T\minus k_{c_{k}}).\end{array}

(3)

We restrict negation in Temporal Datalog
\it{}^{\rightarrow}_{\circleddash}
by requiring programs to be temporally stratified
[
37
]
.

Definition 2

(Temporal Stratification)
.

Consider a program
\it P
with rules (
3
), and the program
\it P^{\prime}
produced by removing all object terms from
\it P
and reducing predicate arity to
\it 1
.

\it P
is called temporally stratified if
\it P^{\prime}
is locally stratified.

For a local stratification
\it B^{\prime}_{1},B^{\prime}_{2},\dots
of
\it P^{\prime}
, the sequence
\it B_{1},B_{2},\dots
, where
\it(p,t)\in B_{i}
iff
\it p(t)\in B^{\prime}_{i}
, is a temporal stratification of
\it P
.

According to Definition
2
, if, in a temporally stratified program
\it P
,
\it p(\boldsymbol{X},T)
depends on
\it p(\boldsymbol{X^{\prime}},T^{\prime})
through negation, then
\it{T^{\prime}\nval T}
.
Thus, such dependencies are not cyclic in the grounded program, and can be resolved by unfolding (i.e., grounding) time.
As a result, all temporally stratified programs are locally stratified, and thus have a unique perfect model.
Temporal stratification can be decided in polynomial time, while local stratification is co-NP hard on Temporal Datalog with negation
[
37
]
.

To formalise our second modification on Temporal Datalog, we define a forward-propagating program.

Definition 3

(Forward-Propagating Program)
.

A program
\it P
with rules of the form (
3
) is forward-propagating if, for every time term
\it T\minus k
in the body of a rule in
\it P
,
\it k
is non-negative.

Definition 4

(Temporal Datalog
\it{}^{\rightarrow}_{\circleddash}
Program)
.

A Temporal Datalog
\it{}^{\rightarrow}_{\circleddash}
program
\it P
is a set of forward-propagating rules of the form (
3
), such that
\it P
is temporally stratified.

Example 1

(Temporal Datalog
\it{}^{\rightarrow}_{\circleddash}
Program)
.

The following program
\it P
evaluates the safety status of a device
\it X
over a stream of
\it repair
and
\it warning
temporal facts.

\it\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{l}safe(X,T)\leftarrow repair(X,T\minus 1).\end{array}

\it\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{l}safe(X,T)\leftarrow\\
\hskip 10.22217ptsafe(X,T\minus 1),\text{not}\ warning(X,T\minus 1).\end{array}

\it\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{l}trusted(X,T)\leftarrow\\
\hskip 10.22217ptsafe(X,T),safe(X,T\minus 1),safe(X,T\minus 2).\end{array}

Rules (
1
)–(
1

\it X
is considered
\it safe
at the current time-point
\it T

\it repair
fact with time-stamp
\it T\minus 1

\it safe
at
\it T\minus 1
and no
\it warning

\it T\minus 1

Rule (

\it X

\it P

2.3
Trigger Graphs

Trigger Graphs (TGs) were proposed as a technique to steer Datalog materialisation away from redundant computations
[
47
]
, complementing classical semi-naive evaluation and the chase-based approaches
[
36

54
,
11
]

Towards defining TGs, we start with Execution Graphs (EGs).

Definition 5

(Execution Graph)
.

An Execution Graph (EG) for a Datalog program
\it P

\it G\val(V,E,\mathrm{rule},l)

\it V
and
\it E

\it\mathrm{rule}
and
\it l

\it\mathrm{rule}

\it V

\it P

There can be a labeled edge of the form
\it u\rightarrow_{j}v
, i.e.,
\it(u,v)\in E
and
\it l((u,v))\val j

\it j
-th predicate in the body of
\it\mathrm{rule}(v)
equals the head predicate of
\it\mathrm{rule}(u)
.
For each non-leaf node
\it v
, the EG includes exactly one edge
\it u\rightarrow_{j}v
for each body atom of
\it\mathrm{rule}(v)
.

Each node
\it v
of an EG denotes a plan for executing
\it\mathrm{rule}(v)
by specifying the node
\it u
that will be used for the evaluation of its
\it i
-th body condition through an edge
\it u\rightarrow_{i}v
.
To enable plan execution, each node
\it v
is populated with facts produced by applying
\it\mathrm{rule}(v)
on the facts in the nodes specified by its incoming edges.

•

Event Specification Languages

We focus on two prominent logical ESLs based on LARS and the Event Calculus.
A thorough review of ESLs with informative comparisons is provided in Section

.

3.1
LARS

LARS extends ASP with window operators and temporal modalities for stream reasoning
[
8
]
.
A window operator
\it\mathbin{\scalebox{0.8}{$\it\boxplus$}}^{w}\mskip-2.0mua
over formula
\it a
uses window function
\it w
to restrict its evaluation on a particular subset of a stream
\it S
.
For example, time windows
\it\mathbin{\scalebox{0.8}{$\it\boxplus$}}^{n}\mskip-2.0mu
restrict attention to the events that took place over the last
\it n
time-points. Temporal modalities express the temporal validity of a formula
\it a
.

\it\mathord{\scalebox{0.8}{$\it\Diamond$}}\mskip 2.0mu{a}
(resp.
\it\mathord{\scalebox{0.7}{$\it\Box$}}\mskip 2.0mu{a}
) requires that
\it a
holds at some (every) time-point in
\it S
.

\it\mathop{\scalebox{0.9}{@}}\nolimits_{T^{\prime}}\!a
requires that
\it a
holds when the evaluation time is shifted to
\it T^{\prime}
, granted that
\it T^{\prime}\in S
.
In practice, it is used to express that
\it a
holds at some time-point in
\it S
(i.e., same as
\it\mathord{\scalebox{0.8}{$\it\Diamond$}}\mskip 2.0mu
), but also to bind
\it T^{\prime}
to such a time-point.

The main LARS-based stream reasoners are Ticker
[
9
]
and Laser
[
6
]
. Both are based on the “plain LARS” fragment, whose core building blocks are the so-called
extended atoms
.

Definition 9

(Extended atom)
.

Let
\it a
be an atom
\it p(\boldsymbol{X})
, where
\it p
is a predicate and
\it\boldsymbol{X}
is a sequence of object terms,
\it T^{\prime}
a time variable and
\it d
a positive integer.
An extended atom
\it e
is defined by the following grammar:

\it\displaystyle e::=a\ |\ \mathop{\scalebox{0.9}{@}}\nolimits_{T^{\prime}}\!a\ |\ \mathbin{\scalebox{0.8}{$\it\boxplus$}}^{d}\mskip-2.0mu\mathop{\scalebox{0.9}{@}}\nolimits_{T^{\prime}}\!a\ |\ \mathbin{\scalebox{0.8}{$\it\boxplus$}}^{d}\mskip-2.0mu\mathord{\scalebox{0.8}{$\it\Diamond$}}\mskip 2.0mua\ |\ \mathbin{\scalebox{0.8}{$\it\boxplus$}}^{d}\mskip-2.0mu\mathord{\scalebox{0.7}{$\it\Box$}}\mskip 2.0mua

We focus on time windows and rules without
\it\mathop{\scalebox{0.9}{@}}\nolimits_{T^{\prime}}
operators in the head, leading to forward-propagating programs.

Definition 10

(Plain LARS)
.

A plain LARS program is a set of rules of the form “
\it h\leftarrow b_{1},\dots,b_{m}.
”, where
\it h
is an atom,
\it b_{i}
,
\it i\in[m]
, is a (possibly negated) extended atom, and all window operators are time windows.

In plain LARS, if a rule
\it r
includes condition
\it\mathop{\scalebox{0.9}{@}}\nolimits_{T^{\prime}}\!a
, then
\it T^{\prime}
must be grounded earlier in the body of

3.2
Event Calculus with Delayed Effects

The Event Calculus (EC) is a temporal logic programming formalism where the functors are classified as event types and
fluent
types, expressing time-varying properties, and a constant sort is reserved for the possible values of each fluent type
[
29
]
.
An event
\it e(\boldsymbol{X})
(resp. fluent
\it f(\boldsymbol{X})
) is a term where
\it e
(
\it f
) is an event (fluent) type and
\it\boldsymbol{X}
contains only objects and variables.
Expression
\it f(\boldsymbol{X})\val v
states that fluent
\it f(\boldsymbol{X})
has value
\it v
and is often used in CER to denote composite events
[
3
,
24
]
.
We focus on an EC variant where events may have
d
elayed
e
ffects on fluent values—which we term EC
DE
—as it is employed in a state-of-the-art CER framework
[
34
]
.
The predicates in EC
DE
and their meaning are as follows.

•

\it\text{happensAt}(e(\boldsymbol{X}),T)
: event
\it e(\boldsymbol{X})
occurs at time
\it T
.

•

\it\text{holdsAt}(f(\boldsymbol{X})\val v,T)
: fluent
\it f(\boldsymbol{X})
has value
\it v
at
\it T
.

•

\it\text{initiatedAt}(f(\boldsymbol{X})\val v,T)
/
\it\text{terminatedAt}(f(\boldsymbol{X})\val v,T)
: a time period where
\it f(\boldsymbol{X})\val v
is initiated/terminated at
\it T
.

•

\it\text{fi}(f(\boldsymbol{X})\val v,f(\boldsymbol{X})\val v^{\prime},d)
: an initiation of
\it f(\boldsymbol{X})\val v
leads to a
f
uture
i
nitiation of
\it f(\boldsymbol{X})\val v^{\prime}
after
\it d
time-points, unless
\it f(\boldsymbol{X})\val v
is terminated in the meantime.

•

\it\text{p}(f(\boldsymbol{X})\val v)
: the above future initiation of
\it f(\boldsymbol{X})\val v^{\prime}
is
p
ostponed by intermediate initiations of
\it f(\boldsymbol{X})\val v
.

In EC
DE
, fluents are classified as
simple
or
statically determined
.
The values of a simple fluent change based on the satisfaction of initiation and termination conditions and persist via the law of inertia.
A statically determined fluent is defined as a Boolean combination of other fluents.

Simple fluent definitions.
The rules defining
\it f(\boldsymbol{X_{f}})\val v
based on immediate effects of events have this form:

\it\begin{array}[]{l}\text{initiatedAt}\mathit{(f(\boldsymbol{X_{f}})\val v,\ T)}\leftarrow\\
\hskip 10.22217pt\text{happensAt}(e_{1}(\boldsymbol{X_{e_{1}}}),\ T)[,\\
\hskip 10.22217pt[\text{not}]\ \text{happensAt}(e_{2}(\boldsymbol{X_{e_{2}}}),\ T),\dots\\
\hskip 10.22217pt[\text{not}]\ \text{happensAt}(e_{n}(\boldsymbol{X_{e_{n}}}),\ T),\\
\hskip 10.22217pt[\text{not}]\ \text{holdsAt}(f_{1}(\boldsymbol{X_{f_{1}}})\val v_{1},\ T),\ \dots,\\
\hskip 10.22217pt[\text{not}]\ \text{holdsAt}(f_{m}(\boldsymbol{X_{f_{m}}})\val v_{m},\ T)].\\
\end{array}

(15)

We use “
\it[\ ]
” to denote optional parts of the rule, i.e., only the first positive
happensAt
atom is necessary.
Rules with head
\it\text{terminatedAt}(f(\boldsymbol{X_{f}})\val v,T)
have the same form.

fi
and
p
facts express future initiations as follows:

\it\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{l}\text{initiatedAt}\mathit{(F\val V^{\prime},T{+}d)}\leftarrow\\
\hskip 10.22217pt\text{fi}(F\val V,F\val V^{\prime},d),\text{initiatedAt}(F\val V,T),\\
\hskip 10.22217pt\text{not}\ \textsf{cancelled}(F\val V,T,T{+}d).\end{array}

\it\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{l}\mathit{\textsf{cancelled}(F\val V,T,T\plus d)}\leftarrow\\
\hskip 10.22217pt\mathit{\textsf{termIn}(F\val V,T,T\plus d).}\end{array}

\it\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{l}\mathit{\textsf{cancelled}(F\val V,T,T\plus d)}\leftarrow\\
\hskip 10.22217pt\text{p}(F\val V),\textsf{initIn}(F\val V,T,T\plus d).\end{array}

\it\textsf{initIn}(F\val V{,}T{,}T\plus d)
and
\it\textsf{termIn}(F\val V{,}T{,}T\plus d)
express that
\it F\val V
is initiated or terminated, respectively, at some time-point strictly between
\it T
and
\it T\plus d
.
In rules (
3.2
)–(
3.2
),
\it F
is a variable spanning over fluents, while
\it V
and
\it V^{\prime}
span over its possible values.
This abstraction emphasises that these rules are included regardless of the domain being modelled.
However, they may only fire if
fi
—and possibly
p
—facts are included, expressing the dynamics of delayed fluent value changes in the domain.

EC
DE
also uses the following rules regardless of domain.

\it\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{l}\text{holdsAt}(F\val V,T)\leftarrow\text{initiatedAt}(F\val V,T\minus 1).\end{array}

\it\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{l}\text{holdsAt}(F\val V,T)\leftarrow\\
\hskip 10.22217pt\text{holdsAt}(F\val V,T\minus 1),\\
\hskip 10.22217pt\text{not}\ \text{terminatedAt}(F\val V,T\minus 1).\end{array}

\it\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{l}\text{terminatedAt}(F\val V,T)\leftarrow\\
\hskip 10.22217pt\text{initiatedAt}(F\val V^{\prime},T),V^{\prime}\nval V.\end{array}

Rules (
3.2
)–(
3.2
) express the law of inertia, i.e.,
\it F\val V
holds at
\it T
if it was initiated at the previous time-point
\it T\minus 1
or held at
\it T\minus 1
and it was not terminated at
\it T\minus 1
.
Rule (
3.2
) terminates
\it F\val V
when
\it F
is initiated with a value other than
\it V
, restricting fluents to at most one value at a time.

Statically determined fluent definitions.
Such a definition for
\it f(\boldsymbol{X_{f}})\val v
contains
\it m
rules where the
\it i
-th rule is:

\it\begin{array}[]{l}\text{holdsAt}\mathit{(f(\boldsymbol{X_{f}})\val v,\ T)}\leftarrow\\
\hskip 10.22217pt[\text{not}]\ \text{holdsAt}(f_{i1}(\boldsymbol{X_{f_{i1}}})\val v_{i1},\ T)[,\\
\hskip 10.22217pt[\text{not}]\ \text{holdsAt}(f_{i2}(\boldsymbol{X_{f_{i2}}})\val v_{i2},\ T),\ \dots,\\
\hskip 10.22217pt[\text{not}]\ \text{holdsAt}(f_{in}(\boldsymbol{X_{f_{in}}})\val v_{in},\ T)].\end{array}

(29)

Together, these
\it m
rules define
\it f(\boldsymbol{X_{f}})\val v
as a DNF formula on the values held by the fluents in their bodies.
This definition has proven to be equivalent to the interval-based analog used for practical CER
[
31
]
.

EC
DE
programs are locally stratified, and thus admit a unique perfect model
[
34
]
.
The reasoning task in EC
DE
is to compute
holdsAt

5
Streaming Trigger Graphs

We cannot use TGs to materialise Temporal Datalog
\it{}^{\rightarrow}_{\circleddash}
programs because (i) they do not support negation and (ii) they do not support variables with countably infinite groundings, such as time variables.
The presence of such variables makes the order of rule evaluation crucial, as materialisation can become trapped in an infinite chain of derivations for one predicate, thereby preventing the derivation of facts for other predicates.
Tackling this issue by generating a TG
\it G^{t}
that materialises the program up to the latest time point
\it t
is problematic because incrementally updating
\it G^{t}
in the presence of new data may lead to incorrect results, while recomputing a TG from scratch at every time step is prohibitively expensive in the streaming setting.
To address these issues, we propose Streaming Trigger Graphs (STGs), which constitute an optimised computational model for Temporal Datalog
\it{}^{\rightarrow}_{\circleddash}
.

We start with Streaming Execution Graphs (SEGs), which extend EGs with negation and time.
Mirroring the relationship between EGs and TGs, we then define STGs as SEGs that materialise Temporal Datalog
\it{}^{\rightarrow}_{\circleddash}
programs correctly.

(Streaming Execution Graph)

Optimisations.
TGs reduce redundant computations via two optimisations
[
47
]
:
(i) after each round
\it k
, nodes constructed at
\it k
whose query is subsumed by another node, based on rewritings recursively replacing intensional predicates with extensional ones following TG dependencies, are removed; and
(ii) joins during node materialisation are restricted to tuples that are not in the previous interpretation.
Both optimisations extend to STGs.
For (i), n-edges stem from nodes in previous strata, so their facts can be treated as extensional in query rewritings (line
24
).
Optimisation (ii) applies unchanged (line
25
).
We may further optimise STGs by
forgetting
nodes whose facts can no longer contribute to future derivations based on their time-stamps.

Summary, Related and Further Work

We proposed mappings from two prominent event specification languages (ESLs) for composite event recognition (CER), which are fragments of LARS and EC, to Temporal Datalog
\it{}^{\rightarrow}_{\circleddash}
, i.e., a temporal Datalog with stratified negation and no future dependencies, and proved their correctness.
We also introduced Streaming Trigger Graphs as an optimised evaluation mechanism for Temporal Datalog
\it{}^{\rightarrow}_{\circleddash}
.

Numerous ESLs have been proposed for CER
[
18
,
5
,
1
]
.
DatalogMTL, e.g., combines Datalog with quantitative temporal modalities in a streaming setting
[
59
]
.
Contrary to LARS and EC, DatalogMTL is defined over a rational time model and uses intervals to quantify temporal offsets.
DatalogMTL variants with forward-propagating rules
[
57
]
, negation
[
17
]
and an integer time model
[
56
]
have also been studied.
We conjecture that such a restricted variant can be mapped to Temporal Datalog
\it{}^{\rightarrow}_{\circleddash}
in a similar way to our plain LARS translation, due to similarities between their temporal operators.
Another prominent CER system is CORE
[
26
,
12
]
.
Contrary to Temporal Datalog
\it{}^{\rightarrow}_{\circleddash}
, CORE is restricted to unary predicates, and thus cannot express relational composite events.

A LARS-to-ASP translation has been proposed for an incremental evaluation of LARS queries
[
9
]
, but introduces multiple time variables per rule, producing programs outside Temporal Datalog
\it{}^{\rightarrow}_{\circleddash}
.
Furthermore, the stratification in
[
7
]
prohibits recursion via window operators in LARS, facilitating efficient reasoning.
Our approach does not make this restriction.

Our work is motivated by earlier attempts to avoid “blocking queries”, requiring arbitrary wait times on new streaming data
[
4
]
.
Temporal Datalog
\it{}^{\rightarrow}_{\circleddash}
prohibits such queries by construction.
Streamlog is also a temporal Datalog without blocking queries. Unlike our work, it allows equality constraints on time terms and reasons only over time-points appearing in the input
[
21
]
.

Several large-scale Datalog engines exist
[
45
,
44
,
27
]
, and incremental evaluation has been studied for streaming data
[
13
]
, but these approaches do not model temporal modalities.
Temporal Vadalog supports temporal knowledge graph reasoning over existential rules
[
10
]
.
Such rules have also been studied in the context of LARS
[
55
]
, DatalogMTL
[
30
]
, and trigger graphs
[
47
]
, thus constituting a natural future extension for STGs.
Our work assumes ordered input, as is common in stream reasoning
[
60
]
.
Handling out-of-order streams
[
49
,
16
]
via temporal delays in reasoning
[
42
]
is left for future work.
Finally, we aim to leverage a probabilistic extension of trigger graphs
[
48
]
to support CER under uncertainty
[
2
,
46
,
51
]
.
