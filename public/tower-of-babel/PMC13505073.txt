Association of prolonged pre-intubation high-flow nasal cannula duration with survival and successful V-V ECMO liberation in severe ARDS: a retrospective multicenter study

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIn patients with severe acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS), prolonged invasive mechanical ventilation (IMV) prior to veno-venous extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (V-V ECMO) is a known risk factor for poor outcomes. However, the clinical impact of high-flow nasal cannula (HFNC) duration before intubation remains poorly defined. We examined the association between pre-intubation HFNC duration and clinical outcomes, including survival and ECMO liberation.MethodsThis retrospective multicenter study using the J-CARVE registry analyzed 209 adults with severe ARDS who received HFNC before V-V ECMO (2012–2022). The continuous risk of prolonged HFNC for 60-day in-hospital mortality was evaluated using multivariable logistic regression. Subsequently, patients were stratified into Short (< 3 days) and Long (≥ 3 days) HFNC groups using an exploratory ROC-derived cutoff. Clinical outcomes (60-day in-hospital mortality and successful ECMO liberation) were further analyzed using multivariable Cox and Fine–Gray models.ResultsMultivariable logistic regression identified prolonged HFNC duration (analyzed continuously) as an independent risk factor for 60-day in-hospital mortality (adjusted odds ratio 1.22 per 1-day increase; 95% CI 1.07–1.39; P = 0.0035). Sixty-nine patients (33.0%) were in the Long HFNC group. After adjustment, prolonged HFNC (≥ 3 days) was independently associated with a twofold increase in 60-day in-hospital mortality (adjusted Hazard Ratio 2.27; 95% CI 1.24–4.15; P = 0.008). Furthermore, successful ECMO liberation was significantly lower in the Long HFNC group (adjusted Subdistribution Hazard Ratio 0.68; 95% CI 0.50–0.94; P = 0.018).ConclusionsExtended pre-intubation HFNC duration was associated with increased mortality and impaired lung recovery in severe ARDS. Rather than a direct cause of injury, prolonged HFNC likely serves as a complex prognostic marker reflecting disease trajectory and delayed escalation. Clinicians should recognize prolonged HFNC as a continuous trajectory of accumulating risk, rather than relying solely on IMV duration to guide ECMO initiation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40560-026-00915-1.

Background

Acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) remains a life-threatening condition associated with high mortality, often necessitating veno-venous extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (V-V ECMO) as a rescue therapy for patients with refractory hypoxemia. Historically, the timing of ECMO initiation has been considered a crucial prognostic factor. Specifically, the duration of invasive mechanical ventilation (IMV) prior to cannulation is a well-established predictor of survival, incorporated into major prognostic scores, such as the RESP score [1, 2]. Prolonged IMV is thought to reflect the progression of ventilator-induced lung injury (VILI) and irreversible lung parenchymal damage. Accordingly, major randomized controlled trials and international guidelines generally recommend ECMO initiation within 7 days of IMV [2–4].

However, the landscape of respiratory management for severe ARDS has evolved significantly in recent years, particularly with the widespread adoption of high-flow nasal cannula (HFNC) oxygen therapy [5]. This shift has introduced a "pre-intubation" period, where patients may receive high-concentration oxygen support for days or even weeks while maintaining spontaneous breathing. A growing body of evidence suggests that high respiratory drive during spontaneous breathing can trigger patient self-inflicted lung injury (P-SILI). Crucially, HFNC may clinically mask the severity of this injury by improving oxygenation without mitigating the excessive transpulmonary pressure swings, thereby accumulating irreversible damage before intubation [6–8]. While this concern became particularly visible during the COVID-19 pandemic due to the extensive use of HFNC [9], HFNC has become a commonly recommended first-line non-invasive respiratory support strategy for acute hypoxemic respiratory failure of diverse etiologies [10, 11]. Therefore, understanding the safety boundaries of prolonged HFNC use is no longer merely a pandemic-related issue, but a universally important challenge in the management of patients with non-COVID ARDS. Relying solely on "IMV duration" may underestimate the true duration of injurious respiratory failure. To address this, the concept of "Advanced Respiratory Support (ARS) duration"—the cumulative duration of HFNC, non-invasive ventilation, and IMV—has been proposed as a more comprehensive marker of pre-ECMO disease burden [12]. While recent studies have suggested the prognostic importance of pre-ECMO HFNC or ARS duration [12, 13], these findings were derived from single-center experiences with limited sample sizes. Consequently, the impact of prolonged pre-intubation HFNC duration on survival and successful ECMO liberation remains unclear in a broad population of patients with severe ARDS.

To address this knowledge gap, this study investigated the association between the duration of HFNC therapy prior to V-V ECMO and clinical outcomes, utilizing data from the Japan Chest CT for ARDS Requiring V-V ECMO (J-CARVE) registry, a large multicenter database in Japan [14, 15]. We hypothesized that prolonged pre-intubation HFNC duration is associated with worse clinical outcomes—specifically increased mortality and reduced likelihood of successful ECMO liberation—in patients with severe ARDS. Furthermore, given the potential heterogeneity in pathophysiology and management strategies between viral pandemics and conventional ARDS [16], we sought to determine whether this accumulated risk associated with delayed escalation is universal or etiology-specific. Therefore, we examined these associations stratified by the primary etiology of ARDS (COVID-19 vs. non-COVID), evaluating pre-intubation HFNC duration as our primary focus, along with total ARS duration as a supportive analysis.

Methods

Study design and setting

This study was conducted as a secondary analysis of the Japan Chest CT for ARDS Requiring V-V ECMO Registry (J-CARVE registry). The J-CARVE registry is a retrospective multicenter database including adult patients with severe ARDS who underwent V-V ECMO at 24 intensive care units in Japan between January 2012 and December 2022. Detailed methodologies of the registry have been described previously [14].

The registry protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of Hiroshima University Hospital (approval number: E-2768) and registered with the UMIN Clinical Trials Registry (UMIN000048709). For the present study, the protocol was approved by the IRB of Sapporo Medical University Hospital (approval number: 352-217) on March 8, 2024. The requirement for informed consent was waived due to the retrospective nature of the study and the use of anonymized data, in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and national guidelines. As this is a retrospective observational study, there were no standardized treatment protocols across the participating centers. Decisions regarding the initiation and duration of HFNC therapy, the timing of endotracheal intubation, and the initiation and liberation of V-V ECMO were left entirely to the clinical discretion of the treating physicians at each institution.

Participants

The study included adult patients (age ≥ 18 years) with severe ARDS, defined according to the Berlin criteria (PaO2/FIO2 ratio [P/F ratio] ≤ 100 mmHg), who were initiated on V-V ECMO support during the study period. For this specific analysis, we included patients who received HFNC oxygen therapy prior to intubation.

Patients were excluded if they met the following criteria: (1) use of non-invasive ventilation; (2) HFNC duration of 14 days or more, to minimize the influence of extreme outliers; or (3) missing data on essential covariates, including prone positioning, P/F ratio, or Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) score.

Eligible patients were categorized into two groups based on the primary etiology of ARDS: the COVID-19 ARDS group and the non-COVID ARDS group.

Outcomes

The clinical outcomes evaluated in this study were 60-day in-hospital mortality and successful liberation from V-V ECMO. The observation period for all outcomes began at the initiation of V-V ECMO support (day 1). The duration of observation was calculated as (Date of event − Date of ECMO initiation) + 1. Sixty-day in-hospital mortality was defined as death from any cause occurring within 60 days after the initiation of V-V ECMO support. Successful liberation from V-V ECMO was defined as separation from the ECMO circuit without the need for re-cannulation within 48 h, consistent with the definition used in the primary analysis of the J-CARVE registry [14]. The follow-up period for successful ECMO liberation was limited to 60 days. Patients who remained alive on ECMO at day 60, or those who were transferred to another hospital while still on ECMO before day 60, were right-censored at that time. For the analysis of successful ECMO liberation, death occurring before liberation was defined as a competing risk event.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were presented as means with standard deviations. Categorical variables were presented as numbers and percentages.

For our primary evaluation of the association between HFNC duration and clinical outcomes, we analyzed pre-ECMO HFNC duration as a continuous variable using a multivariable logistic regression model.

Subsequently, as an exploratory analysis to evaluate continuous risk and determine the optimal cutoff value of HFNC duration for predicting 60-day in-hospital mortality, we performed receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analyses. The Youden index was used to identify the threshold that maximized the sum of sensitivity and specificity [17]. Based on these cutoffs, patients were categorized into two groups: the Long HFNC group and the Short HFNC group.

All multivariable models were adjusted for clinically relevant confounders, including age, sex, body mass index (BMI), Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) score, P/F ratio at the time of ECMO initiation, and pre-ECMO prone positioning. The models for the overall cohort additionally adjusted for the primary etiology of ARDS (COVID-19 vs. non-COVID). Covariates for the multivariable models were selected a priori based on clinical relevance and prior literature [1, 18]. To prevent overfitting and multicollinearity, the total number of covariates was strictly limited in accordance with the number of observed events, precluding the inclusion of highly correlated treatment variables, such as corticosteroid use. These analyses were initially performed in the overall cohort, and subsequently stratified by the primary etiology of ARDS (COVID-19 vs. non-COVID) to assess the heterogeneity of the treatment effect. To formally evaluate whether the impact of HFNC duration on outcomes differed significantly between the two etiologies, we tested for effect modification by including a multiplicative interaction term (HFNC duration × Etiology) in the multivariable models. Finally, the proportional hazards assumption for the Cox regression models was verified using Schoenfeld residuals [19].

All statistical tests were two-sided, and a P value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All analyses were performed using R statistical software (version 4.3.2; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria), utilizing the survival, tidycmprsk, and cmprsk packages.

Sensitivity analyses

To account for temporal shifts in ARDS management and the concentration of COVID-19 cases, we conducted a sensitivity analysis stratifying the cohort into the pre-COVID era (before 2020) and the COVID era (2020 and later). Using a multivariable logistic regression model with HFNC duration as a continuous variable (days), we incorporated an interaction term (HFNC duration × treatment era) to assess effect modification. We also constructed an era-adjusted multivariable model to evaluate whether the association between HFNC duration and mortality was independent of the treatment era. To avoid severe multicollinearity, the ARDS etiology variable (COVID-19 vs. non-COVID) was excluded from these models.

Furthermore, to assess the robustness of our primary findings regarding the impact of pre-ECMO support duration, we conducted a sensitivity analysis using a composite exposure variable. We defined "ARS duration" as the sum of the duration of HFNC therapy and the duration of IMV prior to ECMO initiation. We repeated multivariable analyses using ARS duration as the continuous exposure variable instead of HFNC duration alone. Consistent with the primary analysis, we used multivariable Cox proportional hazards regression models for 60-day in-hospital mortality and fine–gray subdistribution hazard models for successful liberation from V-V ECMO. These models were adjusted for the same covariates (age, sex, BMI, SOFA score, P/F ratio, and pre-ECMO prone positioning) and stratified by the primary etiology of ARDS.

Results

Study population and continuous HFNC duration analysis

A total of 697 patients with severe ARDS requiring V-V ECMO were screened during the study period. Among them, 260 patients received high-flow nasal cannula (HFNC) oxygen therapy prior to intubation and V-V ECMO insertion. After excluding 51 patients (28 who used non-invasive positive pressure ventilation, 13 with missing SOFA score, 5 with missing P/F ratio, 1 with missing data on prone positioning, and 4 with an HFNC duration of ≥ 14 days), a total of 209 patients were included in the final analysis (Fig. 1).Fig. 1Flow diagram of the study patients. In our primary analysis, in the overall cohort, multivariable logistic regression analysis identified prolonged pre-ECMO HFNC duration (analyzed as a continuous variable) as a significant independent risk factor for 60-day in-hospital mortality (Table 1). The adjusted odds ratio (aOR) was 1.22 (95% CI 1.07–1.39, P = 0.0035) for every 1-day increase in HFNC duration, even after adjusting for age, sex, BMI, SOFA score, P/F ratio, pre-ECMO prone positioning, and etiology (COVID-19 vs. non-COVID). Although the etiology of COVID-19 itself was not significantly associated with mortality in this model (aOR 0.51, P = 0.13), adjusting for it confirms that the risk associated with prolonged HFNC duration is robust regardless of the cause of ARDS. ROC curve analysis indicated an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.59. Based on the Youden index, the optimal cutoff value for HFNC duration was identified as 2.5 days, with a sensitivity of 46.8% and a specificity of 71.0% (Fig. 2). Consequently, we categorized patients into two groups using a cutoff of 3 days for subsequent analyses. Baseline characteristics by HFNC duration

Based on the optimal cutoff value derived from the ROC analysis, 140 patients (67.0%) were classified into the Short HFNC group (< 3 days) and 69 patients (33.0%) into the Long HFNC group (≥ 3 days). The baseline characteristics of the study population are summarized in Table 2. The mean age was 55.1 (SD 13.5) years in the Short HFNC group and 57.7 (SD 10.9) years in the Long HFNC group. Disease severity scores at ECMO initiation were similar between the groups, with a mean SOFA score of 10.0 (SD 3.7) vs. 9.7 (SD 3.0) and a mean P/F ratio of 90.9 (SD 37.7) mmHg vs. 97.1 (SD 37.2) mmHg, respectively. Notably, there were substantial differences in etiology and pre-ECMO management. The proportion of patients with COVID-19 was markedly higher in the Long HFNC group (73.9%) compared to the Short HFNC group (58.6%). Furthermore, patients in the Long HFNC group more frequently received corticosteroids (88.4% vs. 65.7%) and prone positioning (37.7% vs. 24.3%) prior to V-V ECMO support. Sensitivity analysis: impact of treatment era

We conducted a sensitivity analysis categorizing the cohort into the pre-COVID era (before 2020) and the COVID era (2020 and later). A multivariable logistic regression model incorporating an interaction term (continuous HFNC duration × treatment era) revealed a significant interaction for 60-day in-hospital mortality (P = 0.011), indicating that the treatment era modified the impact of prolonged HFNC. Nevertheless, in the multivariable model adjusted directly for the treatment era, prolonged HFNC duration (per 1-day increase) remained an independent predictor of 60-day in-hospital mortality (adjusted OR 1.21; 95% CI 1.07–1.38; P = 0.0037) (Supplementary Table 1).

Sensitivity analysis: association between pre-ECMO ARS duration and 60-day in-hospital mortality

To assess the robustness of our findings, we evaluated the impact of the total duration of ARS—defined as the sum of HFNC and invasive mechanical ventilation duration prior to ECMO—on 60-day in-hospital mortality. In the univariable logistic regression analysis, longer ARS duration (analyzed as a continuous variable) was significantly associated with increased 60-day in-hospital mortality (OR 1.08 per day; 95% CI 1.01–1.14; P = 0.018). This association remained significant in the multivariable model. Every 1-day increase in pre-ECMO ARS duration was independently associated with an approximately 10% increase in the odds of 60-day in-hospital mortality (adjusted OR 1.09; 95% CI 1.02–1.17; P = 0.0076). The ROC curve analysis for ARS duration yielded an AUC of 0.671, with an optimal cutoff value of 6.5 days (Supplementary Fig. 1). Subsequently, based on the identified cutoff, patients were categorized into Long (≥ 7 days) and Short (< 7 days) ARS duration groups. Baseline characteristics of patients stratified by ARS duration are presented in Supplementary Table 2. Kaplan–Meier survival analysis demonstrated significantly lower survival probability in the Long ARS group compared to the Short ARS group (log-rank P = 0.0028; Supplementary Fig. 2). In the multivariable Cox proportional hazards model, prolonged ARS duration (≥ 7 days) was independently associated with an approximately threefold increase in the risk of 60-day in-hospital mortality (adjusted HR 3.00; 95% CI 1.56–5.76; P = 0.0010). The proportional hazards assumption was satisfied for this model (Global P = 0.87). Finally, we stratified the sensitivity analysis by ARDS etiology to examine whether the impact of ARS duration differed between subgroups (Supplementary Fig. 3 and Supplementary Table 3). In the non-COVID cohort, prolonged ARS duration (≥ 7 days) was strongly associated with an increased risk of 60-day in-hospital mortality (adjusted HR 6.29; 95% CI 2.32–17.07; P < 0.001). Conversely, in the COVID-19 cohort, the association between ARS duration and mortality did not reach statistical significance (adjusted HR 1.61; 95% CI 0.68–3.81; P = 0.28). The proportional hazards assumption was verified and satisfied for both stratified models (Global P > 0.05).

Sensitivity analysis: association between pre-ECMO ARS duration and successful liberation from V-V ECMO

Finally, regarding successful liberation from V-V ECMO, the sensitivity analysis using ARS duration yielded consistent and critical findings. The cumulative incidence of liberation was significantly lower in the Long ARS group compared to the Short ARS group in the overall, non-COVID, and COVID-19 cohorts (Gray's test, P < 0.001 for all; Supplementary Figs. 4 and 5). This adverse association remained significant in the multivariable fine–gray model: prolonged ARS duration (≥ 7 days) was independently associated with a significantly lower probability of liberation in the overall cohort (adjusted SHR 0.35; 95% CI 0.24–0.52; P < 0.001). Notably, unlike the mortality analysis, where the risk was pronounced only in non-COVID patients, prolonged ARS duration significantly reduced the likelihood of liberation in both the non-COVID (adjusted SHR 0.18; 95% CI 0.08–0.42; P < 0.001) and COVID-19 cohorts (adjusted SHR 0.46; 95% CI 0.28–0.74; P = 0.0016) (Supplementary Table 4).

Discussion

The principal finding of this study was that prolonged duration of HFNC therapy prior to V-V ECMO initiation, evaluated continuously, was independently associated with adverse clinical outcomes. In our primary analysis, the risk of mortality increased progressively with each additional day of HFNC (Table 2). Subsequently, using an exploratory 3-day cutoff, longer HFNC duration (≥ 3 days) was also shown to be an independent risk factor for increased 60-day in-hospital mortality and reduced successful liberation from V-V ECMO. Notably, stratified analyses revealed that these detrimental associations were particularly pronounced in the non-COVID cohort. Collectively, these findings from this multicenter registry elucidate the substantial accumulated risks incurred during the prolonged use of HFNC prior to invasive mechanical ventilation and subsequent V-V ECMO initiation. Importantly, while we utilized a 3-day cutoff for pre-intubation HFNC duration based on the Youden index, the relatively modest AUC (0.59) mandates cautious interpretation. This 3-day mark should not be viewed as a deterministic or clinically prescriptive threshold. Rather, it serves as a pragmatic and exploratory marker to illustrate the escalating risk. As demonstrated in our continuous variable analysis (Table 2), the risk of mortality increases progressively with each additional day of HFNC. Therefore, clinicians should view prolonged pre-intubation HFNC as a continuous trajectory of accumulating risk, rather than a dichotomous safe-or-dangerous timeframe.

The observed association between prolonged pre-intubation HFNC duration and poor outcomes suggests that the "clock" for irreversible lung injury starts ticking well before the initiation of invasive mechanical ventilation. Historically, prolonged IMV duration (e.g., > 7 days) has been a well-established relative contraindication for ECMO due to the accumulation of VILI. Our data extends this concept, indicating that the period of high-intensity non-invasive support is not benign. However, given the observational nature of this registry study, this relationship must be interpreted as an association rather than direct causation. Prolonged HFNC duration is likely a composite proxy marker reflecting a multitude of clinical and systemic factors. For instance, it may serve as a surrogate for delayed escalation to invasive mechanical ventilation, variations in institutional culture or physician-level decision-making regarding intubation thresholds, and resource or pandemic-related constraints.

Mechanistically, this accumulated risk may also be partially attributable to P-SILI [7, 8]. This observation is consistent with the previous finding by Kang et al., who demonstrated that delayed intubation following HFNC failure (> 48 h) was associated with significantly increased ICU mortality [20]. Although HFNC improves oxygenation and comfort, it does not necessarily dampen the excessive respiratory drive characteristic of severe ARDS [21, 22]. Vigorous spontaneous breathing during this phase can generate large transpulmonary pressure swings and Pendelluft phenomena, exacerbating lung inflammation and edema. Consequently, the "masked" period of HFNC usage may represent a window of unchecked P-SILI progression, operating alongside the systemic factors to accumulate irreversible parenchymal damage even before the patient is intubated. This progressive biotrauma explains the delayed divergence of our survival curves observed approximately 20 days after ECMO initiation. While baseline parameters such as the P/F ratio accurately reflect acute gas-exchange impairment, they may not fully capture the underlying histopathologic injury, regional stress/strain, or early fibroproliferative changes that can accompany sustained vigorous spontaneous breathing [23–25]. Although ECMO can rapidly stabilize acute gas-exchange failure through extracorporeal oxygenation and CO₂ removal, and may improve short-term outcomes in selected patients with severe ARDS [26], the accumulated structural lung injury associated with prolonged HFNC may still compromise subsequent lung recovery, resulting in ECMO dependency and delayed fatal complications.

The discrepancy in the prognostic implications of prolonged HFNC duration on mortality between the two cohorts may be explained by the distinct pathophysiology of COVID-19-associated ARDS. Previous studies have indicated that, in the early phase, patients with COVID-19 often present with relatively preserved respiratory system compliance despite profound hypoxemia—a phenomenon attributed to endothelial dysfunction and impaired hypoxic pulmonary vasoconstriction rather than extensive alveolar collapse [16, 27]. In the COVID-19 cohort, therefore, HFNC may have been initiated primarily to treat hypoxemia driven by vascular dysregulation rather than reduced lung compliance. In this context, the duration of HFNC might not linearly reflect the accumulation of irreversible parenchymal damage due to P-SILI to the same extent as in non-COVID ARDS, where hypoxemia is typically tightly coupled with consolidation and low compliance. Because of this inherently low compliance, patients with classical non-COVID ARDS often need to generate high inspiratory effort to maintain ventilation [28]. The resulting large transpulmonary pressure swings make the injured lung particularly susceptible to rapid and severe P-SILI when spontaneous breathing is prolonged under HFNC [21]. This physiological dissociation likely blunted the association between HFNC duration and immediate mortality in COVID-19.

However, when extending the observation window to include the total duration of ARS as a supportive analysis, a universal pattern emerged. We observed that prolonged ARS duration (≥ 7 days) was consistently associated with reduced successful ECMO liberation in both the non-COVID and COVID-19 cohorts. This finding suggests a "phenotypic convergence" during the prolonged course of respiratory management. While COVID-19 ARDS often begins with preserved compliance, it can progress over time to a phenotype characterized by low compliance and extensive consolidation, resembling classical non-COVID ARDS [27, 29]. Crucially, this evolution is likely driven by the distinct pathophysiology of COVID-19, which is uniquely characterized by severe endothelial injury and a high propensity for fibroproliferative evolution [30, 31]. Thus, as the duration of support extends, the cumulative lung injury likely homogenizes across etiologies, resulting in irreversible fibrosis and impaired recovery. Regarding the mortality outcomes, the lack of statistical significance in the COVID-19 cohort might be attributable to limited statistical power; however, the clear signal for reduced liberation highlights that the detrimental consequences in this population manifests primarily as morbidity. Therefore, regardless of the mortality trends, excessive persistence with ARS should be avoided to preserve the potential for weaning.

This study has several limitations inherent to its retrospective, multicenter observational design. First, the 10-year study period encompasses significant shifts in ARDS management and ECMO technology. Our sensitivity analysis demonstrated a significant interaction (P = 0.011) between the treatment era and the impact of prolonged HFNC. During the pandemic, resource constraints and the influx of COVID-19 patients altered treatment thresholds, leading to more extensive and prolonged HFNC use. Consequently, HFNC duration serves partly as a complex surrogate marker influenced by clinical practice trends, introducing era bias. Nevertheless, our era-adjusted model confirmed that each additional day of HFNC remained an independent risk factor for mortality, suggesting a persistent physiological detriment alongside changing practice patterns. Second, due to the retrospective observational design, we cannot definitively establish a causal relationship between prolonged HFNC and adverse outcomes. Although we adjusted for multiple confounders, including disease severity scores and etiology, the potential for unmeasured residual confounding remains. Specific pre-ECMO treatments, such as corticosteroid administration, were heavily influenced by COVID-19 protocols and were not included in the multivariable models to avoid multicollinearity and overfitting. Decisions regarding the initiation of HFNC, the choice and intensity of treatments for the underlying cause of ARDS, and the subsequent escalation to intubation and ECMO were left to the discretion of the treating physicians at each participating center. This variation in practice may have introduced indication bias, particularly regarding the timing of ECMO initiation. Therefore, prolonged HFNC duration must be interpreted strictly as a composite indicator reflecting disease trajectory and clinical decision-making, including intubation thresholds, treatment strategy, era effects, and variables, such as steroid use, rather than a direct and sole cause of poor outcomes. Third, while we hypothesized that P-SILI contributes to the poor outcomes associated with prolonged ARS duration, our registry lacked granular physiological data during the pre-ECMO period, such as serial measurements of respiratory drive, esophageal pressure [32–34], dynamic changes in HFNC settings, or the ROX index [35, 36]. Therefore, we could not directly quantify esophageal pressure swings or respiratory drive, which are unmeasured confounders that might influence the clinical trajectory. Nonetheless, the clear association between HFNC duration and reduced weaning potential suggests that the pre-intubation period is a critical phase of lung injury accumulation. Fourth, the dichotomization of HFNC duration at 3 days was based on an ROC analysis with a modest AUC (0.59). While useful as an exploratory threshold to illustrate risk, this cutoff lacks the discriminatory power to serve as a strict clinical rule, and the true risk of prolonged HFNC is fundamentally continuous. Fifth, the sample size in the stratified analysis, particularly for the COVID-19 subgroup, may have been insufficient to detect a statistically significant difference in 60-day in-hospital mortality (Type II error), despite the observed hazard ratio suggesting a potential risk. The discrepancy between mortality and liberation outcomes in this subgroup should be interpreted with caution and warrants validation in larger cohorts. Finally, this study was conducted in Japan, where the healthcare system and ECMO management strategies may differ from those in other registries [37, 38]. Consequently, our findings may not be fully generalizable to populations with different practice patterns or resource availability.

Conclusions

Extended pre-intubation HFNC duration was associated with increased mortality and impaired lung recovery in severe ARDS. Rather than a direct cause of injury, prolonged HFNC likely serves as a complex prognostic marker reflecting disease trajectory and delayed escalation. Clinicians should recognize prolonged HFNC as a continuous trajectory of accumulating risk, rather than relying solely on invasive mechanical ventilation duration to guide ECMO initiation. Furthermore, total ARS duration can serve as a supportive indicator of this pre-intubation risk.
