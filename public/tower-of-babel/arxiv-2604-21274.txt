Random Access Codes: Explicit Constructions, Optimality, and Classical–Quantum Gaps
Ruho Kondo, Yuki Sato, Hiroshi Yano, Yota Maeda, Kosuke Ito, Naoki Yamamoto

Abstract
A random access code (RAC) encodes an L L -bit string into a k k -bit ( L > k ) (L>k) message from which any designated source bit can be recovered with high probability. Its quantum counterpart, a quantum random access code (QRAC), replaces the k k -bit message with k k qubits. While upper bounds on the decoding success probability have long been studied in both classical and quantum settings, explicit constructions of optimal codes are known only in special cases, even for classical RACs. In this paper, we develop a constructive framework for classical ( L , k ) (L,k) -RACs under both average- and worst-case criteria. We show that optimal code design reduces to selecting 2 k 2^{k} points in { 0 , 1 } L \{0,1\}^{L} and [ 0 , 1 ] L [0,1]^{L} for the average- and worst-case criteria, respectively, so as to minimize a distance-like objective. This characterization yields explicit constructions for general ( L , k ) (L,k) . For k = L − 1 k=L-1 , we further obtain closed-form optimal encoders and decoders for both criteria, and show that the resulting classical ( L , L − 1 ) (L,L-1) -RACs attain the corresponding proved upper bounds. We also show that these optimal classical codes induce ( L , L − 1 ) (L,L-1) -QRACs that attain a conjectured upper bound on the decoding success probability. Numerical optimization suggests little difference between RACs and QRACs in the average-case setting, but a potentially large classical–quantum gap in the worst-case nonasymptotic regime.



I
Introduction

A random access code (RAC) is a protocol that encodes multiple bits of information into a message shorter than the original information, such that a receiver can probabilistically recover any designated bit position from the message.
When the message is encoded into a quantum state, the protocol is called a quantum random access code (QRAC).
The idea dates back to Wiesner’s conjugate coding
[
24
]
and was later rediscovered and formulated explicitly as a random access code by Ambainis
et al.

[
3
]
.
By convention, a protocol that encodes
L
bits into a
k
-(quantum)bit message is referred to as an
(L,k)
-(Q)RAC.
In
[
3
]
,
(2,1)
-QRACs and
(3,1)
-QRACs were proposed, and it is known that they achieve higher decoding success probabilities than the corresponding classical
(2,1)
-RAC and
(3,1)
-RAC.
Leveraging their compressibility, QRACs have also been applied to accelerating combinatorial optimization problems
[
7
,
22
,
20
,
13
,
18
,
10
,
16
]
.
In this way, it is known that QRACs can outperform RACs in terms of decoding success probability for certain specific choices of
L
and
k
.
However, for general
L
and
k
, the extent to which a quantum advantage can be achieved remains unclear.

We next summarize several theoretical results known for RACs and QRACs.
First, it has been shown that there exists no
({L},k)
-RAC for
L\geq 2^{k}
whose worst-case decoding success probability exceeds
\tfrac{1}{2}
, whereas an
(2^{k}-1,k)
-RAC does exist
[
12
, Corollary 5.3]
.
Similarly, there exists no
({L},k)
-QRAC for
L\geq 4^{k}
, while a
(4^{k}-1,k)
-QRAC exists.
Note that there exists
(L,k)
-(Q)RAC for arbitrary
L,k\in\mathbb{N}
whose average decoding success probability exceeds
\tfrac{1}{2}
.
For the worst-case decoding success probability of an
(L,k)
-RAC,
P^{*}_{\rm C}(L,k)
, the following upper bound is known
[
3
, Theorem 2.1]
:

P^{*}_{\rm C}(L,k)\leq\mathcal{H}_{2}^{-1}\left(1-\frac{k}{L}\right)<\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\frac{(2\ln 2)k}{L}}.

(1)

Here
\mathcal{H}_{2}(p)=-p\log_{2}p-(1-p)\log_{2}(1-p)
denotes the binary entropy function.
Moreover, the worst-case decoding success probability of an
(L,k)
-QRAC,
P^{*}_{\rm Q}(L,k)
, is known to satisfy the same upper bound
[
17
, Theorem 2.3]
:

P^{*}_{\rm Q}(L,k)\leq\mathcal{H}_{2}^{-1}\left(1-\frac{k}{L}\right)<\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\frac{(2\ln 2)k}{L}}.

(2)

That is, the worst-case decoding success probabilities of RACs and QRACs are known to satisfy the same upper bound.
However, the tightness of this bound has not been evaluated, and the extent of the gap between the actual supremum of the worst-case decoding success probabilities of RACs and QRACs remains unknown.
More recently, a new upper bound on the average decoding success probability of an
(L,k)
-QRAC,
\overline{P}_{\rm Q}(L,k)
, was established in
[
15
, Theorem 10]
:

\overline{P}_{\rm Q}(L,k)\leq\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\frac{2^{k-1}}{L}}.

(3)

Since the optimal worst-case success probability is upper bounded by the optimal average success probability, this also yields an upper bound on the worst-case success probability of
(L,k)
-QRACs.
In addition, the following conjectured upper bound on the worst-case decoding success probability has been proposed
[
15
]
:

P^{*}_{\rm Q}(L,k)\leq\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\frac{k}{L}}\quad(\text{conjecture}).

(4)

Upper bounds for general
d
-level
(L,k)
-QRACs were proposed in
[
6
, Corollary 1]
; however, for the qubit case (
d=2
), the bound in
[
15
]
is tighter.

Although it is known that replacing classical message bits by qubits does not provide an asymptotic advantage in the sense that one cannot substantially reduce the message length
k

[
3
, Theorem 1.2]
, quantum advantage can be observed when focusing on the average success probability in the case
k=1

[
1
]
.
The average decoding success probabilities of the
(2,1)
-RAC and
(3,1)
-RAC have also been discussed in the literature (see, e.g.,
[
21
]
).
In
[
1
]
, an average-optimal
(L,1)
-RAC was constructed, and the following upper bound on the average decoding success probability was shown:

\overline{P}_{\rm C}(L,1)\leq\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{2^{L}}\binom{L-1}{\lfloor\frac{L}{2}\rfloor}.

(5)

The above results summarize the current theoretical landscape on upper bounds for
(L,k)
-RACs and
(L,k)
-QRACs.

On the other hand, explicit methods to build encoders and decoders for (Q)RACs have also been studied.
In
[
11
]
,
(L,2)
-QRACs are constructed via a seesaw semidefinite programming (SDP) algorithm, i.e., an alternating SDP optimization over the quantum states and the POVMs.
The same work also proposes an optimal
(3,2)
-QRAC in terms of both the average and worst-case decoding success probability, and further discussion of its structure is given in
[
23
]
.
A general construction method for
(L<2^{k},k)
-RACs and
(L<4^{k},k)
-QRACs is discussed in
[
14
]
, which proposes the following form of encoder:

\rho({\bf b})=\frac{1}{2^{k}}\mathbb{I}^{\otimes k}+\frac{1}{2^{k}\sqrt{(2^{k}-1)L}}\sum_{i\in[L]}(-1)^{b_{i}}\xi_{i}.

(6)

Here
\xi_{i}
are Pauli operators; for RACs one uses
\xi_{i}\in\{\mathbb{I},\texttt{Z}\}^{\otimes k}\setminus\{\mathbb{I}^{\otimes k}\}
, whereas for QRACs one uses
\xi_{i}\in\{\mathbb{I},\texttt{X},\texttt{Y},\texttt{Z}\}^{\otimes k}\setminus\{\mathbb{I}^{\otimes k}\}
, where
X
,
Y
, and
Z
are the Pauli matrices.
The corresponding positive operator-valued measure (POVM) is proposed as

E^{b_{i}}_{i}=\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{I}^{\otimes k}+\frac{(-1)^{b_{i}}}{2}\xi_{i}.

(7)

For the above (Q)RAC
\big\{\{\rho({\bf b})\},\{E^{0}_{i},E^{1}_{i}\}\big\}
, the decoding success probabilities satisfy

\displaystyle{P^{*}_{\rm C}(L,k)=\overline{P}_{\rm C}(L,k)=P^{*}_{\rm Q}(L,k)=\overline{P}_{\rm Q}(L,k)}

=\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\frac{1}{(2^{k}-1)L}}.

(8)

While this value is significantly below the known upper bounds, at present no alternative constructive method is known that yields
(L,k)
-(Q)RACs for arbitrary
L
and
k
beyond the above approach.

Very recently, a construction of an
(L,L-1)
-QRAC achieving the conjectured upper bound in Eq. (
4
) was proposed in
[
19
]
.
However, for general
(L,k)
, an optimal construction of QRACs remains unknown, and it is also still open whether the conjectured upper bound in Eq. (
4
) holds in full generality.

In summary, the current open questions on (Q)RACs can be stated as follows:

1.

The actual gap between RACs and QRACs in the non-asymptotic regime with respect to both the average and worst-case decoding success probabilities.

2.

Constructive methods to build optimal
(L,k)
-(Q)RACs for arbitrary
L
and
k
.

In this work, we provide several answers to these questions.
Our main contributions are summarized as follows:

1.

For arbitrary
L
and

-RAC can be formulated as the problem of minimizing a directed Chamfer dissimilarity over subsets of

(Theorem

2.

For arbitrary

and

We provide explicit constructions of optimal

4.

For small

An explicit construction of an optimal

In addition, although a constructive method for building a conjectured optimal

Throughout this work we restrict attention to two-level systems (

II
Definition

In this section, we first introduce the definition of quantum random access codes (QRACs).
We then define their classical counterpart, random access codes (RACs).
Next, we formalize two performance measures for (Q)RACs: the average decoding success probability and the worst-case decoding success probability.
Finally, we present the definition of the distance measure used in this paper.

II-A

Definitions of (Q)RACs

Definition 1

(Quantum Random Access Codes)
.

Let
L,k\in\mathbb{N}
satisfy
1\leq k\leq L
, and let
{\bf b}=(b_{0},\dots,b_{L-1})\in\{0,1\}^{L}
be a binary string of length
L
.
For each
{\bf b}\in\{0,1\}^{L}
, we associate a quantum state
\rho({\bf b})
on
k
qubits, i.e., a density operator
\rho({\bf b})\in\mathbb{C}^{2^{k}\times 2^{k}}
satisfying

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\rho({\bf b})\in\mathbb{C}^{2^{k}\times 2^{k}},\\
\rho({\bf b})^{\dagger}=\rho({\bf b}),\\
\rho({\bf b})\succeq 0,\\
{\rm tr}\big(\rho({\bf b})\big)=1.\end{array}\right.

(9)

The mapping
{\bf b}\mapsto\rho({\bf b})
is referred to as the encoding of
{\bf b}
into
k
qubits.

Next, we consider the decoding procedure that aims to recover a bit
b_{i}
from the encoded state
\rho({\bf b})
via quantum measurement.
For each position
i\in\{0,\dots,L-1\}=:[L]
, we fix a two-outcome POVM
\{E_{i}^{0},E_{i}^{1}\}
.
Let
B_{i}\in\{0,1\}
denote the classical random variable corresponding to the measurement outcome when
\rho({\bf b})
is measured with
\{E_{i}^{0},E_{i}^{1}\}
.
Its distribution is given by

\Pr(B_{i}=x)={\rm tr}\big(E^{x}_{i}\,\rho({\bf b})\big),\qquad x\in\{0,1\}.

(10)

Here, the measurement operators satisfy, for all
x\in\{0,1\}
and
i\in[L]
,

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}E^{x}_{i}\in\mathbb{C}^{2^{k}\times 2^{k}},\\
\big(E^{x}_{i}\big)^{\dagger}=E^{x}_{i},\\
E^{x}_{i}\succeq 0,\\
\sum_{x}E^{x}_{i}=\mathbb{I}^{\otimes k},\end{array}\right.

(11)

where
\mathbb{I}
denotes the identity operator on a single qubit.
The mapping
\rho({\bf b})\mapsto B_{i}
is referred to as the decoding of
i
-th bit from
k
qubits.

A protocol consisting of such encoding and decoding is called a quantum random access code (QRAC).
In particular, we refer to it as an
(L,k)
-QRAC to emphasize the length
L
of the original bit string and the number of message qubits
k
used for encoding.

Definition 2

(Random Access Codes)
.

A random access code (RAC) is defined as a special case of a QRAC in which both the encoding states
\{\rho({\bf b})\}
and the decoding POVMs
\{E_{i}^{0},E_{i}^{1}\}
are restricted to be diagonal in the computational basis.
In particular, we call it an
(L,k)
-RAC to emphasize the length
L
of the original bit string and the number of message (classical) bits
k
used for the encoding.

Since
\rho({\bf b})\succeq 0
and
{\rm tr}(\rho({\bf b}))=1
, if
\rho({\bf b})
is restricted to be diagonal in the computational basis, then its diagonal entries admit a natural probabilistic interpretation.
More precisely, letting
(\rho({\bf b}))_{mm}
denote the
m
-th diagonal element, we may regard it as the classical conditional probability that an encoded message
M={\bf m}\in\{0,1\}^{k}
is produced given the input string
B={\bf b}\in\{0,1\}^{L}
:

\displaystyle{P_{\rm E}(M={\bf m}\mid B={\bf b})=\big(\rho({\bf b})\big)_{mm},}

\sum_{{\bf m}\in\{0,1\}^{k}}P_{\rm E}(M={\bf m}\mid B={\bf b})=1.

(12)

Here
{\bf m}\in\{0,1\}^{k}
is the
k
-bit binary representation of
m\in[2^{k}]
.
Thus, in RACs, the encoding procedure corresponds to stochastically generating a
k
-bit message from an
L
-bit input string.

Similarly, if the POVM
\{E^{0}_{i},E^{1}_{i}\}
is restricted to be diagonal in the computational basis, then its
m
-th diagonal element can be interpreted as the classical conditional probability of outputting a bit value
B^{\prime}_{i}=b^{\prime}_{i}\in\{0,1\}
given the message
M={\bf m}
:

\displaystyle{P_{\rm D}(B^{\prime}_{i}=b^{\prime}_{i}\mid M={\bf m})=\big(E^{b^{\prime}_{i}}_{i}\big)_{mm},}

\sum_{b^{\prime}_{i}\in\{0,1\}}P_{\rm D}(B^{\prime}_{i}=b^{\prime}_{i}\mid M={\bf m})=1.

(13)

Indeed, computing
{\rm tr}\big(E^{b^{\prime}_{i}}_{i}\rho({\bf b})\big)
yields

{\rm tr}\big(E^{b^{\prime}_{i}}_{i}\rho({\bf b})\big)=\sum_{m\in[2^{k}]}\big(E^{b^{\prime}_{i}}_{i}\big)_{mm}\big(\rho({\bf b})\big)_{mm}

=\sum_{{\bf m}\in\{0,1\}^{k}}P_{\rm D}(B^{\prime}_{i}=b^{\prime}_{i}\mid M={\bf m})\,P_{\rm E}(M={\bf m}\mid B={\bf b}),

=\Pr(B^{\prime}_{i}=b^{\prime}_{i}\mid B={\bf b}),

(14)

which is precisely the probability that the decoding procedure applied to
{\bf b}\in\{0,1\}^{L}
outputs the bit value
b^{\prime}_{i}\in\{0,1\}
.

In what follows, whenever it is clear from the context, we write
P_{\rm E}(M={\bf m}\mid B={\bf b})
as
P_{\rm E}({\bf m}\mid{\bf b})
and
P_{\rm D}(B^{\prime}_{i}=b^{\prime}_{i}\mid M={\bf m})
as
P_{\rm D}(b^{\prime}_{i}\mid{\bf m})
for simplicity.

II-B

Average and worst-case decoding success probabilities

In what follows, we say that decoding is successful whenever
b^{\prime}_{i}=b_{i}
holds.

Definition 3

(Average and worst-case decoding success probabilities)
.

The average decoding success probability of an
(L,k)
-QRAC is defined as

\overline{P}_{\rm Q}(L,k)=\frac{1}{L\cdot 2^{L}}\sum_{i\in[L],{\bf b}\in\{0,1\}^{L}}{\rm tr}\big(E^{b_{i}}_{i}\rho({\bf b})\big),

(15)

whereas the worst-case decoding success probability of an
(L,k)
-QRAC is defined as

P^{*}_{\rm Q}(L,k)=\min_{i\in[L],\,{\bf b}\in\{0,1\}^{L}}{\rm tr}\big(E^{b_{i}}_{i}\rho({\bf b})\big).

(16)

Likewise, the average decoding success probability of an
(L,k)
-RAC is defined as

\overline{P}_{\rm C}(L,k)=\frac{1}{L\cdot 2^{L}}\sum_{i\in[L],{\bf b}\in\{0,1\}^{L}}\Pr(b_{i}\mid{\bf b}),

(17)

whereas the worst-case decoding success probability of an
(L,k)
-RAC is defined as

P^{*}_{\rm C}(L,k)=\min_{i\in[L],\,{\bf b}\in\{0,1\}^{L}}\Pr(b_{i}\mid{\bf b}).

(18)

II-C

Distances

We introduce below the fundamental distance measures used in this paper.

Definition 4
.

The Hamming distance
d_{\rm H}
, the relative Hamming distance
d_{{\rm H}/L}
, and the Chebyshev distance
d_{\infty}
between
{\bf a},{\bf b}\in\{0,1\}^{L}
are defined as follows:

d_{\rm H}({\bf a},{\bf b}):=\sum_{i\in[L]}{\bf 1}_{a_{i}\neq b_{i}}

(19)

d_{{\rm H}/L}({\bf a},{\bf b}):=\frac{1}{L}d_{\rm H}({\bf a},{\bf b})

(20)

d_{\infty}({\bf a},{\bf b}):=\max_{i\in[L]}|a_{i}-b_{i}|

(21)

III
Optimal random access codes

In this section, we describe explicit methods for constructing
(L,k)
-RACs that are optimal with respect to the average decoding success probability and the worst-case decoding success probability, respectively, defined in the previous section.
For the average success probability, we also derive a closed-form upper bound that is tighter than the previously known one.
Moreover, we show that the problem of obtaining a worst-case optimal
(L,k)
-RAC can be simplified by assuming a certain conjecture.
Under this conjecture, we further prove that when
k=L-1
, an RAC that is optimal for both the average and worst-case success probabilities admits a closed-form construction.
Finally, for
k=L-1
, we show how to construct a QRAC whose decoding success probability saturates a conjectured upper bound, constructed from the corresponding optimal RAC.

III-A

Average-Optimal
(L,k)
-RAC

main-pratenddefaultcategory.tex

Theorem 5
.

Let
k,L\in\mathbb{N}
satisfy
1\leq k\leq L
.
The maximum achievable average decoding success probability of an
(L,k)
-RAC is

1-\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}S\subset\{0,1\}^{L}\\
|S|=2^{k}\end{subarray}}d_{\mathrm{Cham}}^{\rightarrow}\big(\{0,1\}^{L},S;\,d_{{\rm H}/L}\big)

(22)

where

d_{\mathrm{Cham}}^{\rightarrow}(A,B;d):=\frac{1}{|A|}\sum_{{\bf a}\in A}\inf_{{\bf b}\in B}d({\bf a},{\bf b}).

(23)

is the directed Chamfer dissimilarity
[
4
]
, also known as the directed modified Hausdorff distance
[
5
]
.

Proof.

By definition, the average decoding error probability can be written as

\displaystyle\epsilon_{\rm avg}(P_{\rm E},P_{\rm D})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{L\cdot 2^{L}}\sum_{{\bf b},i}\Pr(B^{\prime}_{i}\neq b_{i}\mid{\bf b})

(24)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2^{L}}\sum_{{\bf b},{\bf m},i}\frac{1}{L}P_{\rm D}(B^{\prime}_{i}\neq b_{i}\mid{\bf m})\,P_{\rm E}({\bf m}\mid{\bf b})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2^{L}}\sum_{{\bf b},{\bf b}^{\prime},{\bf m}}d_{{\rm H}/L}({\bf b},{\bf b}^{\prime})\,P_{\rm D}({\bf b}^{\prime}\mid{\bf m})\,P_{\rm E}({\bf m}\mid{\bf b})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2^{L}}\sum_{{\bf b}\in\{0,1\}^{L}}\Delta({\bf b},P_{\rm E},P_{\rm D}).

Here we used the identity

\displaystyle\frac{1}{L}\sum_{i\in[L]}P_{\rm D}(B^{\prime}_{i}\neq b_{i}\mid{\bf m})

(25)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{L}\sum_{i\in[L]}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}{\bf b}^{\prime}\in\{0,1\}^{L}\\
b^{\prime}_{i}\neq b_{i}\end{subarray}}P_{\rm D}({\bf b}^{\prime}\mid{\bf m})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{L}\sum_{i,{\bf b}^{\prime}}{\bf 1}_{b^{\prime}_{i}\neq b_{i}}\,P_{\rm D}({\bf b}^{\prime}\mid{\bf m})

\displaystyle=\sum_{{\bf b}^{\prime}}d_{{\rm H}/L}({\bf b},{\bf b}^{\prime})\,P_{\rm D}({\bf b}^{\prime}\mid{\bf m}),

and defined

\Delta({\bf b},P_{\rm E},P_{\rm D}):=\sum_{{\bf m},{\bf b}^{\prime}}d_{{\rm H}/L}({\bf b},{\bf b}^{\prime})\,P_{\rm D}({\bf b}^{\prime}\mid{\bf m})\,P_{\rm E}({\bf m}\mid{\bf b}).

(26)

Define

\mathcal{F}_{\rm D}({\bf b},{\bf m};P_{\rm D}):=\sum_{{\bf b}^{\prime}}d_{{\rm H}/L}({\bf b},{\bf b}^{\prime})\,P_{\rm D}({\bf b}^{\prime}\mid{\bf m}),

(27)

and

\mathcal{F}_{\rm E}({\bf b}^{\prime},{\bf m};P_{\rm E}):=\sum_{{\bf b}}d_{{\rm H}/L}({\bf b},{\bf b}^{\prime})\,P_{\rm E}({\bf m}\mid{\bf b}).

(28)

Then
\epsilon_{\rm avg}
can be expressed as

\epsilon_{\rm avg}(P_{\rm E},P_{\rm D})=\frac{1}{2^{L}}\sum_{{\bf m},{\bf b}}\mathcal{F}_{\rm D}({\bf b},{\bf m};P_{\rm D})\,P_{\rm E}({\bf m}\mid{\bf b}),

(29)

and also as

\epsilon_{\rm avg}(P_{\rm E},P_{\rm D})=\frac{1}{2^{L}}\sum_{{\bf m},{\bf b}^{\prime}}\mathcal{F}_{\rm E}({\bf b}^{\prime},{\bf m};P_{\rm E})\,P_{\rm D}({\bf b}^{\prime}\mid{\bf m}).

(30)

Since the right-hand side of (
29
) (resp. (
30
)) is a convex combination of
\mathcal{F}_{\rm D}({\bf b},{\bf m};P_{\rm D})
(resp.
\mathcal{F}_{\rm E}({\bf b}^{\prime},{\bf m};P_{\rm E})
), it suffices to consider deterministic encoders and deterministic decoders.

We now show the following two facts, which allow us to rewrite the minimization over
(P_{\rm E},P_{\rm D})
as a minimization over
(S,f)
:

•

For any deterministic encoder–decoder pair
(P_{\rm E}^{\rm det},P_{\rm D}^{\rm det})
, there exist a set
S\subset\{0,1\}^{L}
with
|S|\leq 2^{k}
and a map
f:\{0,1\}^{L}\rightarrow S
such that

\Delta({\bf b},P^{\rm det}_{\rm E},P^{\rm det}_{\rm D})=d_{{\rm H}/L}\big({\bf b},f({\bf b})\big)
.

•

Conversely, for any
S\subset\{0,1\}^{L}
with
|S|\leq 2^{k}
and any map
f:\{0,1\}^{L}\rightarrow S
, there exists a deterministic encoder–decoder pair
(P_{\rm E}^{\rm det},P_{\rm D}^{\rm det})
such that

d_{{\rm H}/L}\big({\bf b},f({\bf b})\big)=\Delta({\bf b},P^{\rm det}_{\rm E},P^{\rm det}_{\rm D})
.

First, fix an arbitrary deterministic decoder
P_{\rm D}^{\rm det}
and define, for each message
{\bf m}\in\{0,1\}^{k}
, the decoded string by

{\bf b}^{({\bf m})}(P^{\rm det}_{\rm D}):=\sum_{{\bf b}^{\prime}\in\{0,1\}^{L}}{\bf b}^{\prime}\,P^{\rm det}_{\rm D}({\bf b}^{\prime}\mid{\bf m}).

(31)

Since
P^{\rm det}_{\rm D}
is deterministic and
{\bf b}^{\prime}\in\{0,1\}^{L}
, we have
{\bf b}^{({\bf m})}(P^{\rm det}_{\rm D})\in\{0,1\}^{L}
.
Collecting these decoded strings over all messages yields

S(P_{\rm D}^{\rm det}):=\{{\bf b}^{({\bf m})}(P^{\rm det}_{\rm D})\mid{\bf m}\in\{0,1\}^{k}\}\subset\{0,1\}^{L}.

(32)

Because
|\{0,1\}^{k}|=2^{k}
and duplicates are allowed, we have
|S(P_{\rm D}^{\rm det})|\leq 2^{k}
.

Now suppose a deterministic encoder maps each
{\bf b}\in\{0,1\}^{L}
to a unique message
{\bf m}^{({\bf b})}\in\{0,1\}^{k}
, i.e.,

P^{\rm det}_{\rm E}({\bf m}\mid{\bf b})=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}1&{\bf m}={\bf m}^{({\bf b})},\\
0&\text{otherwise}.\end{array}\right.

(33)

Then

\displaystyle\Delta({\bf b},P^{\rm det}_{\rm E},P^{\rm det}_{\rm D})

\displaystyle=\sum_{{\bf m}\in\{0,1\}^{k}}d_{{\rm H}/L}({\bf b},{\bf b}^{({\bf m})})\,P^{\rm det}_{\rm E}({\bf m}\mid{\bf b})

(34)

\displaystyle=d_{{\rm H}/L}\big({\bf b},f({\bf b})\big),

where we defined

f({\bf b}):={\bf b}^{({\bf m}^{({\bf b})})}.

(35)

By (
32
),
{\bf b}^{({\bf m})}\in S(P_{\rm D}^{\rm det})
for all
{\bf m}
, and hence
f({\bf b})\in S(P_{\rm D}^{\rm det})
. This proves the first item.

Next, suppose we are given a set

S=\{{\bf b}^{(1)},{\bf b}^{(2)},\dots\}\subset\{0,1\}^{L},\quad|S|\leq 2^{k},

(36)

and a map

f:\{0,1\}^{L}\rightarrow S.

(37)

We construct a deterministic decoder by assigning, for each message
{\bf m}\in\{0,1\}^{k}
, a fixed output
{\bf b}^{({\bf m})}\in S
:

P_{\rm D}^{\rm det}({\bf b}^{\prime}\mid{\bf m})=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}1&{\bf b}^{\prime}={\bf b}^{({\bf m})},\\
0&\text{otherwise}.\end{array}\right.

(38)

Then

\sum_{{\bf b}^{\prime}\in\{0,1\}^{L}}d_{{\rm H}/L}({\bf b},{\bf b}^{\prime})\,P_{\rm D}^{\rm det}({\bf b}^{\prime}\mid{\bf m})=d_{{\rm H}/L}({\bf b},{\bf b}^{({\bf m})}).

(39)

Since
f({\bf b})\in S
, we can choose the indexing so that for each
{\bf b}
we have

f({\bf b})={\bf b}^{({\bf m}^{({\bf b})})}

(40)

for some message
{\bf m}^{({\bf b})}
. Define a deterministic encoder by

P_{\rm E}^{\rm det}({\bf m}\mid{\bf b})=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}1&{\bf m}={\bf m}^{({\bf b})},\\
0&\text{otherwise}.\end{array}\right.

(41)

Then

\displaystyle\Delta({\bf b},P_{\rm E}^{\rm det},P_{\rm D}^{\rm det})

\displaystyle=\sum_{{\bf m}\in\{0,1\}^{k}}d_{{\rm H}/L}({\bf b},{\bf b}^{({\bf m})})\,P_{\rm E}^{\rm det}({\bf m}\mid{\bf b})

(42)

\displaystyle=d_{{\rm H}/L}\big({\bf b},f({\bf b})\big),

which establishes the second item.

Combining the two items, minimizing over deterministic encoder–decoder pairs is equivalent to minimizing over
(S,f)
:

\displaystyle{\min_{P_{\rm E},P_{\rm D}}\sum_{{\bf b}\in\{0,1\}^{L}}\Delta({\bf b},P_{\rm E},P_{\rm D})}

=\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}S\subset\{0,1\}^{L}\\
|S|\leq 2^{k}\end{subarray}}\sum_{{\bf b}\in\{0,1\}^{L}}\min_{{\bf s}\in S}d_{{\rm H}/L}\big({\bf b},{\bf s}\big).

(43)

Therefore,

\displaystyle{\min_{P_{\rm E},P_{\rm D}}\epsilon_{\rm avg}(P_{\rm E},P_{\rm D})=\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}S\subset\{0,1\}^{L}\\
|S|\leq 2^{k}\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{2^{L}}\sum_{{\bf b}\in\{0,1\}^{L}}\min_{{\bf s}\in S}d_{{\rm H}/L}({\bf b},{\bf s}).}

=\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}S\subset\{0,1\}^{L}\\
|S|\leq 2^{k}\end{subarray}}d_{\mathrm{Cham}}^{\rightarrow}\big(\{0,1\}^{L},S;\,d_{{\rm H}/L}\big)

(44)

where

d_{\mathrm{Cham}}^{\rightarrow}(A,B;d):=\frac{1}{|A|}\sum_{{\bf a}\in A}\inf_{{\bf b}\in B}d({\bf a},{\bf b}).

(45)

is a directed Chamfer dissimilarity.
Moreover, if
|S|<2^{k}
, we can add arbitrary points to
S
without increasing the objective value, and hence we may restrict to
|S|=2^{k}
:

\min_{P_{\rm E},P_{\rm D}}\epsilon_{\rm avg}(P_{\rm E},P_{\rm D})=\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}S\subset\{0,1\}^{L}\\
|S|=2^{k}\end{subarray}}d_{\mathrm{Cham}}^{\rightarrow}\big(\{0,1\}^{L},S;\,d_{{\rm H}/L}\big).

(46)

Thus the maximum average decoding success probability of an
(L,k)
-RAC is

\displaystyle 1-\min_{P_{\rm E},P_{\rm D}}\epsilon_{\rm avg}(P_{\rm E},P_{\rm D})

(47)

\displaystyle=1-\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}S\subset\{0,1\}^{L}\\
|S|=2^{k}\end{subarray}}d_{\mathrm{Cham}}^{\rightarrow}\big(\{0,1\}^{L},S;\,d_{{\rm H}/L}\big).

∎

III-A
1
Constructing average-optimal
(L,k)
-RAC

To construct an average-optimal
(L,k)
-RAC, one needs to solve the optimization problem

\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}S\subset\{0,1\}^{L}\\
|S|=2^{k}\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{2^{L}}\sum_{{\bf b}\in\{0,1\}^{L}}\min_{{\bf s}\in S}d_{{\rm H}/L}({\bf b},{\bf s})

(48)

This problem can be formulated as a mixed-integer linear program (MILP) as follows.

Introduce binary variables
{z_{\bf a}}\in\{0,1\}
indicating whether
{{\bf a}}\in\{0,1\}^{L}
is selected into
S
(i.e.,
z_{\bf a}=1
if
{\bf a}\in S
, and
z_{\bf a}=0
otherwise).
Also introduce binary assignment variables
\lambda_{{\bf a}}({\bf b})\in\{0,1\}
indicating whether
{\bf b}\in\{0,1\}^{L}
is assigned to
{\bf a}\in\{0,1\}^{L}
(i.e.,
\lambda_{\bf a}({\bf b})=1
if
{\bf a}
is chosen as the representative for
{\bf b}
, and
\lambda_{\bf a}({\bf b})=0
otherwise).
The objective is to minimize the total (relative) Hamming distance between each
{\bf b}
and its assigned representative
{\bf a}
.
After optimization, for each
{\bf b}
the variable
\lambda_{\bf a}({\bf b})
identifies a nearest representative in the selected set
S
.
The resulting MILP is

\displaystyle\text{minimize}

\displaystyle\sum_{{\bf b}\in\{0,1\}^{L}}\sum_{{\bf a}\in\{0,1\}^{L}}d_{\rm H}({\bf b},{\bf a})\,\lambda_{\bf a}({\bf b})

(49)

\displaystyle\text{subject to}

\displaystyle\sum_{{\bf a}\in\{0,1\}^{L}}z_{\bf a}=2^{k},

\displaystyle\sum_{{\bf a}\in\{0,1\}^{L}}\lambda_{\bf s}({\bf b})=1

\displaystyle\forall{\bf b}\in\{0,1\}^{L},

\displaystyle z_{\bf a}\in\{0,1\}

\displaystyle\forall{\bf a}\in\{0,1\}^{L},

\displaystyle\lambda_{\bf a}({\bf b})\in\{0,1\}

\displaystyle\forall{\bf a},{\bf b}\in\{0,1\}^{L},

\displaystyle\lambda_{\bf a}({\bf b})\leq z_{\bf a}

\displaystyle\forall{\bf a},{\bf b}\in\{0,1\}^{L}.

After solving (
49
), one can define an encoder and a decoder as follows.
Fix a bijection between the selected representatives in
S
and the message set
\{0,1\}^{k}
, and denote the corresponding message for
{\bf s}^{(j)}\in S
by
{\bf m}^{(j)}
.
Then define the encoder by assigning
{\bf b}
to the message corresponding to its selected representative:

P_{\rm E}({\bf m}^{(j)}\mid{\bf b})={\lambda_{{\bf s}^{(j)}}({\bf b})}.

(50)

Define the decoder deterministically by

P_{\rm D}({\bf b}^{\prime}\mid{\bf m}^{(j)})=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}1&{\bf b}^{\prime}={\bf s}^{(j)},\\
0&\text{otherwise}.\end{array}\right.

(51)

In this formulation, for each
{\bf b}
we allow only a single nearest representative in
S
; however, if there are multiple representatives at the same minimum Hamming distance, any tie-breaking rule yields the same average success probability.

Alternatively, one may directly construct an encoder and a decoder from a given set
S
.
Although finding an
S
that maximizes the average success probability takes an exponentially large time in general, if an optimal
S
is obtained by some method, then one can build an average-optimal
(L,k)
-RAC as follows.
First, define the decoder by assigning each message
{\bf m}^{(j)}
to a distinct
{\bf b}^{*(j)}\in S
:

P_{\rm D}({\bf b}^{*(j)}\mid{\bf m}^{(j)})=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}1&D({\bf m}^{(j)})={\bf b}^{*(j)},\\
0&\text{otherwise}.\end{array}\right.

(52)

That is, the
j
-th message
{\bf m}^{(j)}
is decoded to the
j
-th element
{\bf b}^{*(j)}
of
S
.
Next, define the encoder by choosing a message that decodes to the nearest codeword in
S
:

\displaystyle{P_{\rm E}({\bf m}^{(j)}\mid{\bf b})}

=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}>0&{\bf m}^{(j)}\in\big\{D^{-1}(\{{\bf b}^{\prime}\mid d_{\rm H}({\bf b},{\bf b}^{\prime})=d_{\rm H}^{*}({\bf b})\})\big\},\\
0&\text{otherwise},\end{array}\right.

(53)

where

d_{\rm H}^{*}({\bf b})=\min_{{\bf b}^{\prime}\in S}d_{\rm H}({\bf b},{\bf b}^{\prime}).

(54)

In other words, the encoder selects a message whose decoded
{\bf b}^{\prime}\in S
is at minimum Hamming distance from
{\bf b}
.
If there are multiple nearest bit strings in
S
, one may either distribute probability among the corresponding messages, or select any one of them deterministically; both choices yield the same average success probability.

III-A
2
Closed-form upper bound of average decoding success probability

Theorem
5
shows that constructing an average-optimal
(L,k)
-RAC amounts to solving an MILP; however, solving this problem in general requires exponential time.
Motivated by this, we derive a new closed-form upper bound on the average decoding success probability of
(L,k)
-RACs that is tighter than the previously known bound in Eq. (
1
).

Theorem 6
.

Let

H=\min\Bigg\{h\in\mathbb{N}\ \Bigg|\ \sum^{h}_{l=0}\binom{L}{l}\geq 2^{L-k}\Bigg\}.

(55)

Then the average decoding success probability of an
(L,k)
-RAC is upper bounded by

\displaystyle{\overline{P}_{\rm C}(L,k)\leq 1-\frac{1}{L\cdot 2^{L-k}}\Bigg(\sum^{H-1}_{h=0}h\binom{L}{h}}

+H\Bigg(2^{L-k}-\sum^{H-1}_{h^{\prime}=0}\binom{L}{h^{\prime}}\Bigg)\Bigg)=:\overline{P}_{\rm C}^{\rm close}(L,k).

(56)

Proof.

For a fixed
{\bf b}^{*(i)}\in S
, the number of bit strings at Hamming distance exactly
l
from
{\bf b}^{*(i)}
is at most
\binom{L}{l}
.
Hence, the total number of bit strings within Hamming distance at most
h
from the
2^{k}
elements in
S
is at most

N(h)=2^{k}\sum^{h}_{l=0}\binom{L}{l}.

(57)

Choose the smallest
h\in\mathbb{N}
such that
N(h)\geq 2^{L}
, and denote it by
H
. Equivalently,

H=\min\Bigg(h\in\mathbb{N}\ \Bigg|\ \sum^{h}_{l=0}\binom{L}{l}\geq 2^{L-k}\Bigg).

(58)

Let
S^{(h)}
denote the set of bit strings at minimum Hamming distance
h
from
S
, i.e.,

S^{(h)}=\Big\{{\bf b}\ \Big|\ \min_{{\bf b}^{*}\in S}d_{\rm H}({\bf b},{\bf b}^{*})=h\Big\}.

(59)

We have
|S^{(h)}|\leq 2^{k}\binom{L}{h}
, and in the ideal (non-overlapping) case one may think of
|S^{(h)}|=2^{k}\binom{L}{h}
.
To maximize the average success probability, we would like to make the distances
\min_{{\bf s}\in S}d_{{\rm H}/L}({\bf b},{\bf s})
as small as possible for as many
{\bf b}\in\{0,1\}^{L}
as possible.
If we assume that the layers
S^{(h)}
can be arranged without overlaps up to radius
H
, that is, there is no
{\bf b}\in\{0,1\}^{L}
such that
d_{\rm H}({\bf b},{\bf b}^{*})=d_{\rm H}({\bf b},{{\bf b}^{*}}^{\prime})\leq H
for
{\bf b}^{*},{{\bf b}^{*}}^{\prime}\in S
and
{\bf b}^{*}\neq{{\bf b}^{*}}^{\prime}
, then the sum of normalized distances is lower bounded by

\frac{1}{L}\Bigg(\sum^{H-1}_{h=0}h\cdot\big|S^{(h)}\big|+H\cdot\Bigg(2^{L}-\sum^{H-1}_{h=0}\big|S^{(h)}\big|\Bigg)\Bigg).

(60)

Since such an ideal partition may not exist in general, this yields only a lower bound on the average error probability. Concretely,

\displaystyle{\min_{P_{\rm E},P_{\rm D}}\epsilon_{\rm avg}(P_{\rm E},P_{\rm D})}

\geq\frac{1}{L\cdot 2^{L-k}}\Bigg(\sum^{H-1}_{h=0}h\binom{L}{h}+H\Bigg(2^{L-k}-\sum^{H-1}_{h^{\prime}=0}\binom{L}{h^{\prime}}\Bigg)\Bigg).

(61)

Therefore, the average decoding success probability satisfies

\displaystyle{1-\epsilon_{\rm avg}}

\leq 1-\frac{1}{L\cdot 2^{L-k}}\Bigg(\sum^{H-1}_{h=0}h\binom{L}{h}+H\Bigg(2^{L-k}-\sum^{H-1}_{h^{\prime}=0}\binom{L}{h^{\prime}}\Bigg)\Bigg),

(62)

which proves (
56
).∎

Using this closed-form formulation, The upper bound of the average decoding success probability of
(L,1)
-RAC and
(L,L-1)
-RAC can be obtained straightforwardly as follows:

Corollary 7

(
[
2
]
)
.

The maximum average decoding success probability of an
(L,1)
-RAC is

\displaystyle{\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{2^{L}}\binom{L-1}{\lfloor\frac{L}{2}\rfloor}}
.

Proof.

By the binomial theorem and the symmetry of binomial coefficients, it can be shown that

H=\left\lfloor\frac{L}{2}\right\rfloor

when
k=1
, regardless of whether
L
is even (
L=2n
with
n\in\mathbb{N}
) or odd
(L=2n+1)
.
Moreover, using the identity

h\binom{L}{h}=L\binom{L-1}{h-1},

(63)

together with the change of variables
j=h-1
, we obtain

\sum_{h=0}^{n-1}h\binom{L}{h}=L\sum_{j=0}^{n-2}\binom{L-1}{j}.

(64)

Applying this identity, when
L=2n+1
, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{h=0}^{n-1}h\binom{2n+1}{h}+n\left(2^{2n}-\sum_{h=0}^{n-1}\binom{2n+1}{h}\right)

(65)

\displaystyle=(2n+1)\sum_{j=0}^{n-2}\binom{2n}{j}+n\binom{2n+1}{n}

\displaystyle=(2n+1)\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\binom{2n}{j}

\displaystyle=(2n+1)\left(2^{2n-1}-\frac{1}{2}\binom{2n}{n}\right)

\displaystyle=L\cdot 2^{L-1}\left(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{2^{L}}\binom{L-1}{\left\lfloor\frac{L}{2}\right\rfloor}\right),

whereas, when
L=2n
, we obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{h=0}^{n-1}h\binom{2n}{h}+n\left(2^{2n-1}-\sum_{h=0}^{n-1}\binom{2n}{h}\right)

(66)

\displaystyle=2n\sum_{j=0}^{n-2}\binom{2n-1}{j}+n\cdot\frac{1}{2}\binom{2n}{n}

\displaystyle=2n\left(\sum_{j=0}^{n-2}\binom{2n-1}{j}+\frac{1}{2}\binom{2n-1}{n-1}\right)

\displaystyle=2n\left(2^{2n-2}-\frac{1}{2}\binom{2n-1}{n-1}\right)

\displaystyle=L\cdot 2^{L-1}\left(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{2^{L}}\binom{L-1}{\frac{L}{2}-1}\right)

\displaystyle=L\cdot 2^{L-1}\left(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{2^{L}}\binom{L-1}{\frac{L}{2}}\right).

Therefore,

\overline{P}_{\rm C}(L,1)\leq\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{2^{L}}\binom{L-1}{\left\lfloor\frac{L}{2}\right\rfloor}.

(67)

Moreover, this upper bound is attained by
S=\{0^{L},1^{L}\}
.∎

III-B

Worst-Case Optimal
(L,k)
-RAC

Theorem 9
.

Let
k,L\in\mathbb{N}
satisfy
1\leq k\leq L
.
The maximum achievable worst-case decoding success probability of an
(L,k)
-RAC is

1-\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}S\subset[0,1]^{L}\\
|S|=2^{k}\end{subarray}}d_{\mathrm{Haus}}^{\rightarrow}\big(\{0,1\}^{L},{\rm conv}(S);\,d_{\infty}\big)

(68)

where

d_{\mathrm{Haus}}^{\rightarrow}(A,B;d):=\sup_{{\bf a}\in A}\operatorname*{inf\vphantom{p}}_{{\bf b}\in B}d({\bf a},{\bf b})

(69)

is a directed Hausdorff distance
[
9
]
.

Proof.

We write the worst-case decoding success probability in terms of the corresponding worst-case error probability
\epsilon_{\rm worst}(P_{\rm E},P_{\rm D})
:

\min_{i\in[L]}\min_{{\bf b}\in\{0,1\}^{L}}\Pr(b_{i}\mid{\bf b})=1-\epsilon_{\rm worst}(P_{\rm E},P_{\rm D}),

(70)

where

\epsilon_{\rm worst}(P_{\rm E},P_{\rm D})=\max_{i\in[L]}\max_{{\bf b}\in\{0,1\}^{L}}\Pr(B^{\prime}_{i}\neq b_{i}\mid B={\bf b}).

(71)

We aim to lower bound
\min_{P_{\rm E},P_{\rm D}}\epsilon_{\rm worst}(P_{\rm E},P_{\rm D})
.

For a given encoder–decoder pair
(P_{\rm E},P_{\rm D})
, define the expected decoded bit value by

\overline{c}_{i}({\bf b},P_{\rm E},P_{\rm D})=\sum_{b^{\prime}_{i}\in\{0,1\}}b^{\prime}_{i}\;\Pr(b^{\prime}_{i}\mid{\bf b})\in[0,1].

(72)

Since
b_{i}\in\{0,1\}
, and
\overline{c}_{i}({\bf b},P_{\rm E},P_{\rm D})=\Pr(B^{\prime}_{i}=1\mid B={\bf b})
, we have

\Pr(B^{\prime}_{i}\neq b_{i}\mid B={\bf b})=\big|b_{i}-\overline{c}_{i}({\bf b},P_{\rm E},P_{\rm D})\big|.

(73)

Therefore,

\displaystyle\epsilon_{\rm worst}(P_{\rm E},P_{\rm D})

\displaystyle=\max_{i\in[L]}\max_{{\bf b}\in\{0,1\}^{L}}\big|b_{i}-\overline{c}_{i}({\bf b},P_{\rm E},P_{\rm D})\big|

(74)

\displaystyle=\max_{{\bf b}\in\{0,1\}^{L}}d_{\infty}\big({\bf b},\overline{{\bf c}}({\bf b},P_{\rm E},P_{\rm D})\big),

where
\overline{{\bf c}}({\bf b},P_{\rm E},P_{\rm D})=(\overline{c}_{0},\dots,\overline{c}_{L-1})\in[0,1]^{L}
.

We now rewrite the minimization over
(P_{\rm E},P_{\rm D})
as the problem of selecting a set
S\subset[0,1]^{L}
and a mapping
f:\{0,1\}^{L}\to{\rm conv}(S)
.
To this end, we show the following two statements:

•

For any
(P_{\rm E},P_{\rm D})
, there exist a set
S\subset[0,1]^{L}
with
|S|\leq 2^{k}
and a map

f:\{0,1\}^{L}\rightarrow{\rm conv}(S)
such that
\overline{{\bf c}}({\bf b},P_{\rm E},P_{\rm D})=f({\bf b})
for all
{\bf b}\in\{0,1\}^{L}
.

•

Conversely, for any
S\subset[0,1]^{L}
with
|S|\leq 2^{k}
and any map
f:\{0,1\}^{L}\rightarrow{\rm conv}(S)
, there exist
(P_{\rm E},P_{\rm D})
such that

f({\bf b})=\overline{{\bf c}}({\bf b},P_{\rm E},P_{\rm D})
for all
{\bf b}\in\{0,1\}^{L}
.

First, fix a decoder
P_{\rm D}
and define, for each message
{\bf m}\in\{0,1\}^{k}
,

{\bf c}^{(m)}(P_{\rm D}):=\sum_{{\bf b}^{\prime}\in\{0,1\}^{L}}{\bf b}^{\prime}\,P_{\rm D}({\bf b}^{\prime}\mid{\bf m})\in[0,1]^{L}.

(75)

Collect these points into

S(P_{\rm D}):=\{{\bf c}^{(m)}(P_{\rm D})\mid{\bf m}\in\{0,1\}^{k}\},

(76)

which satisfies
|S(P_{\rm D})|\leq 2^{k}
.
Using this notation, the expected decoded string can be rewritten as

\displaystyle\overline{{\bf c}}({\bf b},P_{\rm E},P_{\rm D})

\displaystyle=\sum_{{\bf b}^{\prime}\in\{0,1\}^{L}}{\bf b}^{\prime}\sum_{{\bf m}\in\{0,1\}^{k}}P_{\rm D}({\bf b}^{\prime}\mid{\bf m})P_{\rm E}({\bf m}\mid{\bf b})

(77)

\displaystyle=\sum_{{\bf m}\in\{0,1\}^{k}}{\bf c}^{(m)}(P_{\rm D})\,P_{\rm E}({\bf m}\mid{\bf b})

\displaystyle=:f({\bf b}).

Since the coefficients
P_{\rm E}({\bf m}\mid{\bf b})
form a probability distribution,
f({\bf b})
is a convex combination of points in
S(P_{\rm D})
, and hence
f({\bf b})\in{\rm conv}(S(P_{\rm D}))
. This proves the first statement.

For the converse direction, suppose
S=\{{\bf c}^{(1)},{\bf c}^{(2)},\dots\}\subset[0,1]^{L}
with
|S|\leq 2^{k}
, and let
f:\{0,1\}^{L}\rightarrow{\rm conv}(S)
.
For each message
{\bf m}
, associate it with a point
{\bf c}^{(m)}\in S
, and define the decoder componentwise by

P_{\rm D}(b^{\prime}_{i}\mid{\bf m})=(c^{(m)}_{i})^{b^{\prime}_{i}}\,(1-c^{(m)}_{i})^{1-b^{\prime}_{i}}.

(78)

Then
\sum_{b^{\prime}_{i}\in\{0,1\}}b^{\prime}_{i}\,P_{\rm D}(b^{\prime}_{i}\mid{\bf m})=c^{(m)}_{i}
, and hence
\sum_{{\bf b}^{\prime}\in\{0,1\}^{L}}{\bf b}^{\prime}\,P_{\rm D}({\bf b}^{\prime}\mid{\bf m})={\bf c}^{(m)}
.
Since
f({\bf b})\in{\rm conv}(S)
, there exist coefficients
\lambda_{\bf m}({\bf b})\geq 0
with
\sum_{{\bf m}\in\{0,1\}^{k}}\lambda_{\bf m}({\bf b})=1
such that

f({\bf b})=\sum_{{\bf m}\in\{0,1\}^{k}}\lambda_{\bf m}({\bf b})\,{\bf c}^{(m)}.

(79)

Define the encoder by

P_{\rm E}({\bf m}\mid{\bf b})=\lambda_{\bf m}({\bf b}).

(80)

Then the resulting expectation satisfies
\overline{{\bf c}}({\bf b},P_{\rm E},P_{\rm D})=f({\bf b})
, proving the second statement.

Combining the above, we obtain

\displaystyle{\min_{P_{\rm E},P_{\rm D}}\max_{{\bf b}\in\{0,1\}^{L}}\big\|{\bf b}-\overline{{\bf c}}({\bf b},P_{\rm E},P_{\rm D})\big\|_{\infty}}

\displaystyle=\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}S\subset[0,1]^{L}\\
|S|=2^{k}\end{subarray}}\max_{{\bf b}\in\{0,1\}^{L}}\min_{{\bf x}\in{\rm conv}(S)}d_{\infty}({\bf b},{\bf x})

(81)

\displaystyle=\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}S\subset[0,1]^{L}\\
|S|=2^{k}\end{subarray}}d_{\mathrm{Haus}}^{\rightarrow}\big(\{0,1\}^{L},{\rm conv}(S);\,d_{\infty}\big)

where

d_{\mathrm{Haus}}^{\rightarrow}(A,B;d):=\sup_{{\bf a}\in A}\operatorname*{inf\vphantom{p}}_{{\bf b}\in B}d({\bf a},{\bf b})

(82)

is a directed Hausdorff distance.
Thus the maximum worst-case decoding success probability of an
(L,k)
-RAC is

\displaystyle 1-\min_{P_{\rm E},P_{\rm D}}\epsilon_{\rm worst}(P_{\rm E},P_{\rm D})

(83)

\displaystyle=1-\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}S\subset[0,1]^{L}\\
|S|=2^{k}\end{subarray}}d_{\mathrm{Haus}}^{\rightarrow}\big(\{0,1\}^{L},{\rm conv}(S);\,d_{\infty}\big).

∎

Hence, the set
S(P_{\rm D})=\{{\bf c}^{(m)}(P_{\rm D})\mid{\bf m}\in\{0,1\}^{k}\}
is a subset of
\{0,1\}^{L}
.
Therefore, assuming
S\subset\{0,1\}^{L}
is equivalent to assuming a deterministic decoder.∎

If Conjecture
III-B
is true, the problem of finding the optimal
(L,k)
-RAC maximizing the worst-case decoding success probability can be written in MILP.
If not, the solution of such an MILP gives an achievable
(L,k)
-RAC maximizing the worst-case decoding success probability because the feasible region with
S\subset\{0,1\}^{L}
is narrower than that with
S\subset[0,1]^{L}
.

Corollary 12
.

Assuming Conjecture
III-B
holds, the worst-case decoding success probability of an
(L,L-1)
-RAC is upper bounded by
1-\frac{1}{L}
.

Proof.

It suffices to show that

\displaystyle{\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}S\in\{0,1\}^{L}\\
|S|=2^{L-1}\end{subarray}}d_{\mathrm{Haus}}^{\rightarrow}\big(\{0,1\}^{L},{\rm conv}(S);\,d_{\infty}\big)=\frac{1}{L}.}

When
k=L-1
, we can choose exactly half of the vertices in
\{0,1\}^{L}
as
S
.
Let
S
be the set of even-parity vertices:

S=\Bigg\{{\bf b}\in\{0,1\}^{L}\ \Bigg|\ \sum_{i\in[L]}b_{i}\equiv 0\pmod{2}\Bigg\}.

(85)

(Choosing odd parity would yield the same conclusion.)

If
{\bf b}\in S
, then clearly
\min_{{\bf x}\in{\rm conv}(S)}d_{\infty}({\bf b},{\bf x})=0
.
Thus, consider
{\bf b}\in\{0,1\}^{L}\setminus S
, i.e., an odd-parity vertex.
There exist exactly
L
vertices
{\bf v}^{(i)}
at Hamming distance
1
from
{\bf b}
, obtained by flipping the
i
-th bit of
{\bf b}
.
Each
{\bf v}^{(i)}
has even parity and hence belongs to
S
.
Therefore, it suffices to consider the convex hull of these
L
neighbors.

Write

{\bf v}^{(i)}={\bf b}+(1-2b_{i}){\bf e}_{i},\quad i\in[L],

(86)

where
{\bf e}_{i}
is the
i
-th standard basis vector.
An arbitrary point in
{\rm conv}(\{{\bf v}^{(i)}\}_{i\in[L]})
can be written as

{\bf y}=\sum_{i\in[L]}\lambda_{i}{\bf v}^{(i)},\qquad\lambda_{i}\geq 0,\qquad\sum_{i\in[L]}\lambda_{i}=1,

(87)

whose
i
-th coordinate is

y_{i}=b_{i}+\lambda_{i}(1-2b_{i}).

(88)

Hence the Chebyshev distance from
{\bf b}
to
{\bf y}
is

d_{\infty}({\bf b},{\bf y})=\max_{i\in[L]}|\lambda_{i}(1-2b_{i})|=\max_{i\in[L]}\lambda_{i}.

(89)

Therefore,

\displaystyle\min_{{\bf y}\in{\rm conv}(\{{\bf v}^{(i)}\}_{i})}d_{\infty}({\bf b},{\bf y})

\displaystyle=\min_{\lambda_{i}\geq 0,\ \sum_{i}\lambda_{i}=1}\max_{i\in[L]}\lambda_{i}

(90)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{L},

where the minimum is achieved by
\lambda_{i}=\frac{1}{L}
for all
i
.
Since the same argument applies to every
{\bf b}\in\{0,1\}^{L}\setminus S
, we obtain

\displaystyle d_{\mathrm{Haus}}^{\rightarrow}\big(\{0,1\}^{L},{\rm conv}(S);\,d_{\infty}\big)

\displaystyle=\max_{{\bf b}\in\{0,1\}^{L}}\min_{{\bf x}\in{\rm conv}(S)}d_{\infty}({\bf b},{\bf x})

(91)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{L}.

Finally, if
S^{\prime}
is not a parity class, then there exists
{\bf s}\in S^{\prime}
whose Hamming distance to
{\bf b}
is at least
2
for some
{\bf b}\in\{0,1\}^{L}\setminus S^{\prime}
.
For such a vertex, the above neighborhood-based construction fails, and the resulting Chebyshev distance from
{\bf b}
to
{\rm conv}(S^{\prime})
is strictly larger than
\frac{1}{L}
.
Thus the parity choice is optimal and the minimum of
d_{\mathrm{Haus}}^{\rightarrow}
is
\frac{1}{L}
.
Consequently, the worst-case decoding success probability of an
(L,L-1)
-RAC is at most
1-\frac{1}{L}
.∎

III-B
1
Constructing worst-case achievable
(L,k)
-RAC

To obtain a worst-case optimal
(L,k)
-RAC, one needs to solve a bilinear programming problem (
68
).
Nevertheless, when Conjecture
III-B
is accepted, an achievable solution can be obtained by solving an MILP.
Specifically, consider

\displaystyle\text{minimize}

\displaystyle h

(92)

\displaystyle\text{subject to}

\displaystyle\sum_{{\bf a}\in\{0,1\}^{L}}z_{\bf a}=2^{k},

\displaystyle\sum_{{\bf a}\in\{0,1\}^{L}}\lambda_{\bf a}({\bf b})=1

\displaystyle\forall{\bf b}\in\{0,1\}^{L},

\displaystyle-h\leq b_{i}-\sum_{{\bf a}\in\{0,1\}^{L}}\lambda_{\bf a}({\bf b})a_{i}\leq h

\displaystyle\forall{\bf b}\in\{0,1\}^{L},\;\forall i\in[L],

\displaystyle z_{\bf a}\in\{0,1\}

\displaystyle\forall{\bf a}\in\{0,1\}^{L},

\displaystyle 0\leq\lambda_{\bf a}({\bf b})\leq z_{\bf a}

\displaystyle\forall{\bf a}\in\{0,1\}^{L},\;\forall{\bf b}\in\{0,1\}^{L}.

After solving (
92
), one can define an encoder and a decoder by setting

P_{\rm E}({\bf m}^{(j)}\mid{\bf b})={\lambda_{{\bf s}^{(j)}}({\bf b})},

(93)

and

P_{\rm D}({\bf b}^{\prime}\mid{\bf m}^{(j)})=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}1&{\bf b}^{\prime}={\bf s}^{(j)},\\
0&\text{otherwise}.\end{array}\right.

(94)

One may also construct a worst-case achievable
(L,k)
-RAC directly from a set
S
.
Although finding an
S
that maximizes the worst-case success probability takes an exponentially large time in general, if an optimal
S
is available, then one can proceed as follows.
Let
{\bf b}^{*(j)}
denote the
j
-th element of
S
, and let
{\bf m}^{(j)}
denote the
j
-th element of
\{0,1\}^{k}
.
Define the deterministic decoder by

P_{\rm D}(B={\bf b}^{*(j)}\mid M={\bf m}^{(j)})=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}1&D({\bf m}^{(j)})={\bf b}^{*(j)},\\
0&\text{otherwise},\end{array}\right.

(95)

where
D:\{0,1\}^{k}\rightarrow\{0,1\}^{L}
is a map that assigns
{\bf b}^{*(j)}
to
{\bf m}^{(j)}
.
That is, the
j
-th message
{\bf m}^{(j)}
is decoded to
{\bf b}^{*(j)}
.
For each input
{\bf b}\in\{0,1\}^{L}
, define the encoder by

P_{\rm E}({\bf m}^{(j)}\mid{\bf b})=\lambda^{*}_{j}({\bf b}),

(96)

where
{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}^{*}({\bf b})\in\mathbb{R}^{2^{k}}_{+}
is an optimal solution to

\displaystyle\min_{{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}}\ \max_{i\in[L]}\Bigg|\sum_{j\in[2^{k}]}\lambda_{j}b^{*(j)}_{i}-b_{i}\Bigg|

(97)

\displaystyle\text{subject to}\quad\sum_{j\in[2^{k}]}\lambda_{j}=1,\qquad\lambda_{j}\geq 0\ \ (j\in[2^{k}]).

This can be converted into the following linear program:

\displaystyle\min_{t,\{\lambda_{j}\}}

\displaystyle t

(98)

\displaystyle\text{subject to}

\displaystyle\sum_{j\in[2^{k}]}\lambda_{j}b^{*(j)}_{i}-b_{i}\leq t

\displaystyle\forall i\in[L],

\displaystyle b_{i}-\sum_{j\in[2^{k}]}\lambda_{j}b^{*(j)}_{i}\leq t

\displaystyle\forall i\in[L],

\displaystyle\sum_{j\in[2^{k}]}\lambda_{j}=1,

\displaystyle\lambda_{j}\geq 0

\displaystyle\forall j\in[2^{k}].

Since this is a linear program,
{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}^{*}({\bf b})
can be computed efficiently.

(Conjecturally) optimal encoder and decoder of
(L,L-1)
-(Q)RAC

In this section, we provide explicit closed-form expressions for the encoder and decoder of the (conjectured) optimal
(L,L-1)
-RAC and
(L,L-1)
-QRAC.
We first present the optimal
(L,L-1)
-RAC, and then construct the optimal
(L,L-1)
-QRAC based on it.

Theorem 13
.

There exists an
(L,L-1)
-RAC with average decoding success probability
1-\frac{1}{2L}
and worst-case decoding success probability
1-\frac{1}{L}
, which attain the (conjectured) upper bound.

Proof.

Let

p({\bf b}):=\bigoplus_{i}b_{i}

(99)

be a parity of bit string
{\bf b}
.
The claimed performance can be achieved by the state in (
100
) and the POVMs in (
102
) below:

\displaystyle{\operatorname{diag}(\rho_{\rm C}({\bf b}))}

=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\displaystyle{|{\bf c}({\bf b})\rangle}&\displaystyle{\text{for}\quad p({\bf b})=0},\\
\displaystyle{\frac{1}{\sqrt{L}}\Bigg(\mathbb{I}^{\otimes L-1}+\sum_{j\in[L-1]}\texttt{X}_{j}\Bigg)|{\bf c}({\bf b})}\rangle&\displaystyle{\text{for}\quad p({\bf b})=1},\end{array}\right.

(100)

where

{\bf c}({\bf b}):=b_{L-2}\,b_{L-3}\dots b_{0}

(101)

is the lower
L-1
bits of
{\bf b}
, and the POVM elements are

E^{0}_{i}=\frac{1}{2}\big(\mathbb{I}^{\otimes L-1}+\zeta_{i}\big),\quad E^{1}_{i}=\frac{1}{2}\big(\mathbb{I}^{\otimes L-1}-\zeta_{i}\big),

(102)

with

\zeta_{i}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\texttt{Z}_{i}&\text{for}\quad i<L-1,\\
\texttt{Z}^{\otimes L-1}&\text{for}\quad i=L-1.\end{array}\right.

(103)

Expressed directly in terms of classical conditional distributions, the above encoder–decoder can be written as follows:

P_{\rm E}({\bf m}\mid{\bf b})=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}1&\text{for}\quad p({\bf b})=0\ \ \text{and}\ \ {\bf m}={\bf c}({\bf b}),\\[5.69054pt]
\frac{1}{L}&\text{for}\quad p({\bf b})=1\ \ \text{and}\ \ d_{\rm H}({\bf m},{\bf c}({\bf b}))\leq 1,\\[5.69054pt]
0&\text{otherwise},\end{array}\right.

(104)

and

P_{\rm D}(b^{\prime}_{i}\mid{\bf m})=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}1&\text{for}\quad b^{\prime}_{i}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}m_{i}&i<L-1\\
p({\bf m})&i=L-1\end{array}\right.,\\[14.22636pt]
0&\text{otherwise}.\end{array}\right.

(105)

This protocol can be described as follows.
Given
{\bf b}
, if
p({\bf b})=1
, flip exactly one bit of
{\bf b}
uniformly at random; otherwise, do not flip any bit. Let
{\bf m}
be the resulting lower
k=L-1
bits.
For decoding, output
b^{\prime}_{i}=m_{i}
for
i<L-1
, and output the parity
b^{\prime}_{L-1}=p({\bf m})
to estimate
b_{L-1}
.
Under this protocol, for each
i
the event
b^{\prime}_{i}\neq b_{i}
occurs with probability
\frac{1}{L}
, while all other positions
j\neq i
are decoded correctly with probability
1
.
Hence the average decoding success probability equals
\frac{1}{L}\Big(1\cdot\frac{1}{2}+(L-1)\cdot 1\Big)=1-\frac{1}{2L}
, and the worst-case decoding success probability equals
1-\frac{1}{L}
, achieving both upper bounds if Conjecture
III-B
is accepted.∎

Theorem 14
.

There exists an
(L,L\!-\!1)
-QRAC whose average and worst-case decoding success probabilities are both

\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\frac{L-1}{L}},

(106)

which attains the conjectured upper bound (Eq. (
4
)).

Proof.

For the optimal
(L,L-1)
-QRAC, the encoding state is given by

\rho_{\rm Q}({\bf b})=|\psi({\bf b})\rangle\langle\psi({\bf b})|,

where

|\psi({\bf b})\rangle=\begin{cases}|{\bf c}({\bf b})\rangle,&p({\bf b})=0,\\[4.30554pt]
U\,|{\bf c}({\bf b})\rangle,&p({\bf b})=1,\end{cases}

(107)

and

U:=\frac{1}{\sqrt{L}}\left(\mathbb{I}^{\otimes(L-1)}+\sum_{j\in[L-1]}\texttt{X}_{j}\texttt{Z}_{0}\cdots\texttt{Z}_{j}\right).

(108)

This construction coincides with
\operatorname{diag}(\rho_{\rm C}({\bf b}))
(see Eq. (
100
)) except when
p({\bf b})=1
, where the states are modified to ensure mutual orthogonality.
The optimal POVMs are given by

E^{0}_{i}=\frac{1}{2}\big(\mathbb{I}^{\otimes(L-1)}+\xi_{i}\big),\qquad E^{1}_{i}=\frac{1}{2}\big(\mathbb{I}^{\otimes(L-1)}-\xi_{i}\big),

(109)

where

\xi_{i}=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\frac{L}{L-1}}\big(\texttt{Z}_{i}+U\texttt{Z}_{i}U^{\dagger}\big),&i<L-1,\\[8.61108pt]
\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\frac{L}{L-1}}\big(\texttt{Z}^{\otimes(L-1)}-U\texttt{Z}^{\otimes(L-1)}U^{\dagger}\big),&i=L-1.\end{cases}

(110)

Using these states and POVMs, the decoding success probability is

\operatorname{tr}\big(E^{b_{i}}_{i}\rho_{\rm Q}({\bf b})\big)=\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\frac{L-1}{L}},\quad\forall\,i\in[L],\ {\bf b}\in\{0,1\}^{L}.

(111)

Therefore, both the average and worst-case decoding success probabilities are

\overline{P}_{\rm Q}(L,L-1)=P^{*}_{\rm Q}(L,L-1)=\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\frac{L-1}{L}},

(112)

which attains the conjectured upper bound (Eq. (
4
)).
∎

V
Conclusion

In this paper, we formulated the problem of finding an optimal RAC in order to clarify the gap between RACs and QRACs in the non-asymptotic regime.
We showed that both an average-optimal and a worst-case optimal RAC can be obtained by selecting an appropriate subset of
\{0,1\}^{L}
of size
2^{k}
that minimizes a distance-like objective.
Comparing achievable QRAC solutions with optimal RACs, we found little difference in the average decoding success probability, but a substantial gap in the worst-case decoding success probability.
This suggests that the main advantage of QRACs over RACs may emerge in the worst-case decoding regime.
