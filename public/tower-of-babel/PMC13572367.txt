Interplay of electron–phonon coupling, dissipative phonon bath, and electron–electron interaction in a triangular quantum-dot trimer
Authors: Hemant Kumar Sharma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractNonequilibrium charge transport through a trimer molecular transistor (TMT) composed of three quantum dots arranged in a triangular geometry has been studied in the presence of electron–electron and electron–phonon interactions. The system is described by an extended Anderson–Holstein–Caldeira–Leggett Hamiltonian, where phonon damping arises from coupling to a substrate phonon bath. The electron–phonon interaction is treated nonperturbatively using the Lang–Firsov transformation, while the Coulomb interaction is included at the mean-field level. Using the Keldysh nonequilibrium Green’s function (NEGF) formalism, we compute the spectral function, current, and differential conductance. Our results reveal how Coulomb repulsion, polaronic renormalization, and dissipation jointly influence transport through the triangular molecular transistor.Subject terms: Materials science, Nanoscience and technology, Physics

Introduction

Semiconductor quantum dots (QDs) provide a universal platform for analyzing quantum transport, strong correlations, and vibronic effects at the nanoscale1–3. Improvements in nanofabrication have made it easier to make coupled-dot architectures like double and triple quantum dots, where electron coherence, interference, and Coulomb interactions create a lot of interesting transport phenomena12,17,18.

Among these, triangular triple quantum-dot or trimer molecular configurations have attracted particular attention due to their inherent geometric frustration and multiple tunneling pathways19,20,44. The closed-loop geometry allows for direct and indirect tunneling channels, leading to quantum interference and symmetry-protected degeneracies that strongly influence conductance and level hybridization.

When such nanoscale systems are embedded on or coupled to insulating substrates, the interaction between electrons and local vibrational modes becomes unavoidable. The electron–phonon (e–ph) coupling locally modulates the dot potential and gives rise to phonon-assisted tunneling, vibronic sidebands, and polaron formation7,8,23,24. The canonical Lang–Firsov transformation (LFT) provides a nonperturbative route to account for these effects, yielding renormalized onsite energies and tunneling amplitudes that depend exponentially on the e–ph coupling strength23,26. Experimental evidence of such polaronic phenomena has been observed in suspended carbon-nanotube QDs and molecular junctions7,25,27, where phonon sidebands and phonon blockade appear in current and conductance spectra.

In realistic environments, however, the phonon modes of QDs are not isolated. They couple to substrate or surrounding lattice vibrations, which act as a dissipative phonon reservoir. The Caldeira–Leggett model6,9,28 captures this coupling by introducing a spectral density that governs frequency renormalization and damping of the local phonon mode. The resulting phonon softening and energy relaxation profoundly modify charge transport by broadening sidebands and suppressing coherence29,31–33. The competition between electron–phonon interaction and bath-induced damping thus determines whether transport is coherent, phonon-assisted, or dissipative.

Electron–electron (e–e) interactions introduce an additional layer of complexity. The onsite Coulomb repulsion U leads to Coulomb blockade and spin or charge correlations that can suppress or shift resonant transport windows2,34. In multi-dot geometries, mean-field treatments of U yield effective level shifts that modify interdot coherence and current–voltage response35,36,44. The simultaneous inclusion of e–ph coupling, dissipation, and Coulomb interaction in a triangular geometry, however, has not been extensively explored.

In this work, we investigate nonequilibrium charge transport through a triangular trimer molecular transistor (TMT)–a closed-loop triple quantum-dot system–coupled to a dissipative phonon environment. Unlike earlier studies on linear or side-coupled geometries, the triangular configuration enables multiple tunneling pathways and quantum interference controlled by an Aharonov–Bohm phase (ϕ). The electron–phonon interaction is treated nonperturbatively via the Lang–Firsov transformation, while the substrate phonons are incorporated through a Caldeira–Leggett bath leading to phonon frequency renormalization and effective dissipation. The on-site Coulomb interaction is included at the mean-field level, allowing for a self-consistent evaluation of electronic occupation and transport.

A key novelty of our work lies in the interplay between quantum interference (ϕ) and polaronic effects (λ) in a dissipative environment. By computing the current as a function of both ϕ and λ, we obtain two-dimensional transport phase diagrams, revealing how interference patterns evolve under strong electron–phonon coupling and damping. This enables us to identify regimes of constructive and destructive interference, phonon-induced suppression of coherence, and interaction-driven nonlinearities in transport.

Our results demonstrate that the competition between interference, dissipation, and Coulomb interaction leads to rich transport behavior, including modulation of current amplitude, suppression of coherence, and emergence of nontrivial transport regimes not accessible in simpler geometries.

While the present framework captures the essential interplay between interference, electron–phonon coupling, and dissipation, it is based on a mean-field treatment of Coulomb interaction. This approximation is expected to be valid in the weak to intermediate interaction regime, where charge fluctuations dominate and strong many-body correlations are suppressed. In particular, the polaronic renormalization reduces the effective interaction strength, further supporting the applicability of the mean-field approach in the parameter regime considered here. However, strong correlation effects such as Kondo physics and higher-order many-body coherence are beyond the scope of the present work.

In the non-interacting case (U=0) [panels (a) and (b)], the current–voltage characteristics exhibit a smooth, dependence for λ =0, reflecting coherent resonant transport through the system. As the electron-phonon coupling is increased to λ =0.5 and λ =1.0, the current is progressively suppressed and develops visible step-like features, indicating the onset of polaronic effects that renormalize the effective tunneling amplitudes and open additional inelastic transport channels. This behavior is further corroborated by the differential conductance in panel (b), where the sharp and well-defined resonance peaks at λ =0 evolve into multiple smaller peaks and oscillatory structures for finite λ, corresponding to phonon-assisted sidebands arising from multi-phonon processes. In contrast, in the interacting regime (U ≠ 0) [panels (c) and (d)], the current exhibits pronounced staircase-like features even at λ =0, which are signatures of Coulomb blockade, where transport occurs via discrete charging states of the system. With increasing λ, these steps become more structured and split, reflecting the combined effect of Coulomb interaction and phonon dressing of electronic states. The corresponding conductance in panel (d) shows sharp, well-separated peaks for λ =0, associated with resonant tunneling through interaction-split energy levels, while for λ =0.5 and λ =1.0 these peaks broaden, split, and multiply, indicating the emergence of phonon-assisted tunneling channels superimposed on Coulomb-induced resonances. Notably, the overall suppression of conductance near zero bias and the modulation of peak amplitudes across all curves can be attributed to destructive quantum interference arising from the finite Aharonov–Bohm phase (), which alters the effective transmission pathways. Altogether, the evolution of both current and conductance across different λ and U highlights the intricate competition between coherent transport, Coulomb blockade, and polaronic effects, leading to rich nonlinear transport behavior, which also give rise to Negative differential resistance(NDR). Also the enhancement of differential conductance peaks in the interacting regime (U ≠ 0) can be understood from the modification of the underlying spectral properties and transport mechanisms. In the absence of interaction (U=0), transport is governed by a single, relatively broad resonance, leading to a smooth variation of the current with bias and consequently moderate conductance peaks. However, for finite U, the system enters the Coulomb blockade regime, where the electronic spectrum splits into distinct many-body states separated by the interaction energy. This results in the emergence of sharp, well-defined resonances in the spectral function corresponding to different charge configurations. As the bias voltage aligns the chemical potentials with these discrete levels, the current exhibits step-like increases, reflecting the opening of new transport channels. Since the differential conductance is given by the derivative of the current with respect to bias, these abrupt changes translate into pronounced and enhanced conductance peaks. Furthermore, the effective narrowing of resonances due to interaction-induced localization, combined with phonon-assisted sidebands at finite electron-phonon coupling, leads to a concentration of spectral weight at specific energies, further amplifying the peak heights. Thus, the larger conductance peaks observed for finite U arise from the interplay of Coulomb-induced level splitting, sharper resonant transport, and the step-like evolution of current under bias.

Summary

In this work, we have theoretically investigated nonequilibrium charge transport through a triangular trimer molecular transistor in the presence of electron–phonon coupling, a dissipative phonon bath, and on-site Coulomb interaction. Using the Lang–Firsov transformation combined with the nonequilibrium Green’s function formalism, we consistently incorporated polaronic renormalization, phonon sidebands, and bath-induced phonon softening within a unified framework.

Our results show that electron–phonon coupling λ generates phonon-assisted sidebands and renormalizes both the onsite energy and tunneling amplitudes, leading to suppression and restructuring of transport channels. The coupling to a dissipative phonon bath, characterized by γ, modifies the effective phonon frequency, thereby altering the energy-level alignment and spectral distribution. As a result,

Within the present approximation, the bath-induced effects are dominated by phonon-frequency renormalization rather than strong dissipative broadening.

A key feature of the triangular geometry is the presence of Aharonov–Bohm (AB) interference, which leads to pronounced oscillations of the current as a function of the phase ϕ, with destructive interference occurring near . We find that electron–phonon coupling leads to a monotonic suppression of interference visibility due to phonon-induced inelastic scattering and redistribution of spectral weight. In contrast, dissipation produces a non-monotonic dependence of visibility on γ, arising from the renormalization of the effective phonon frequency and the resulting modification of energy-level alignment. This demonstrates that environmental effects can both suppress and enhance quantum interference depending on the regime.

The inclusion of Coulomb interaction U further leads to the emergence of Coulomb blockade, splitting the energy spectrum into interaction-driven resonances and shifting the transport thresholds to higher bias. The combined interplay of interference, polaron formation, dissipation, and electron correlation results in rich transport behavior, including phonon-assisted tunneling, enhanced conductance peaks, and negative differential resistance.

Overall, our study provides a comprehensive understanding of how quantum interference, vibrational effects, and dissipation jointly govern transport in triangular quantum-dot systems, offering new insights for controlling coherence and current in nanoscale molecular devices. Finally, we note that the present analysis is based on a mean-field treatment of Coulomb interaction, which is valid in the weak to intermediate interaction regime explored here. While this approach successfully captures the interplay between quantum interference, electron–phonon coupling, and dissipation, it does not include strong correlation effects such as Kondo physics or many-body entanglement. A more complete treatment of such effects in closed-loop geometries remains an interesting direction for future work.
