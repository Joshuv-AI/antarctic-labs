MYDGF promotes pathological and physiological retinal angiogenesis via the Gαi1/3-Gab1-Akt-mTOR signaling
Authors: Ke-ran Li, Ping-ping Fu, Wen Bai, Chao-wen Bai, Yitian Yang, Le Liu, Jin-long Chai, Yujia Yao, Dan Huang, Zhi-qing Zhang, Qin Jiang, Zhou-rui Ma, Yu Feng, Jin Yao, Cong Cao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractRetinal neovascularization (RNV) is a potential vision-threatening process characterized by the abnormal growth of retinal vessels. Despite its clinical prevalence, the precise mechanisms governing RNV initiation and progression remain incompletely defined. Here we identify myeloid-derived growth factor (MYDGF) as a critical regulator of retinal vascular dynamics. Using single-cell RNA sequencing and human patient validation, we show that MYDGF is upregulated in retinal endothelial cells during proliferative diabetic retinopathy and mouse models of pathological neovascularization. In vitro, MYDGF promotes retinal endothelial cell proliferation, migration, and sprouting. In vivo, endothelial-specific MYDGF depletion inhibits both normal vascular development and pathological neovascularization in neonatal mice, and disrupting adult vascular homeostasis in male mice. MYDGF drives angiogenesis by activating the Akt-mTOR cascade through the Gαi1/3-Gab1 signaling complex; genetic depleting or mutation these components suppress MYDGF-induced Akt-mTOR activation and angiogenic responses. Together, MYDGF promotes retinal angiogenesis and maintains vascular homeostasis via the Gαi1/3-Gab1-Akt-mTOR signaling axis.Subject terms: Growth factor signalling, Retinopathy of prematurity

Introduction

Retinal neovascularization (RNV)1,2 represents a definitive pathological characteristic in several prevalent, vision-threatening retinal vascular diseases, including diabetic retinopathy (DR)3, oxygen-induced retinopathy (OIR)4, and retinopathy of prematurity (ROP)5. This condition is typified by the anomalous proliferation of new blood vessels, primarily initiated by retinal ischemia and hypoxia1,2. The newly formed vasculature is often inherently fragile, prone to leakage, and can induce fibrotic changes, leading to severe sequelae such as vitreous hemorrhage, tractional retinal detachment, and ultimately irreversible vision loss1,2,6–10. The etiological mechanisms driving RNV are multifaceted, encompassing the intricate interplay of metabolic dysregulation, inflammatory processes, and hypoxia-induced signaling pathways1,2.

Angiogenesis constitutes the fundamental and highly regulated process of new blood vessel formation11, which is central to RNV. The process commences with angiogenic or inflammatory stimuli inducing the activation of resident endothelial cells11. Subsequently, these activated cells degrade the underlying basement membrane, facilitating their proliferation and directional migration toward the angiogenic cue. This migratory phase culminates in the establishment of new lumen structures11. The sequence concludes with vessel maturation, during which the nascent vasculature gains structural integrity11. A central mediator in this pathological cascade is vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF). Although the advent of anti-VEGF therapies has considerably advanced the management of retinal neovascularization diseases, notable challenges persist12–15. A subset of patients exhibit suboptimal or incomplete therapeutic responses, and the recurrence of neovascularization remains a frequent clinical observation13–15. Furthermore, the necessity for long-term administration of anti-VEGF agents can be associated with potential adverse effects13–15, thereby underscoring the ongoing imperative for identifying more efficacious and sustainable therapeutic targets and modalities13–15.

Myeloid-derived growth factor (MYDGF) is a recently characterized protein, initially identified for its potent anti-apoptotic and tissue-repairing properties16–18. Discovered in bone marrow-derived monocytes and macrophages16,19, where it demonstrated roles in cardioprotection and myocardial infarction repair16,17, MYDGF’s functional scope has since expanded significantly17,18. It is broadly expressed across nearly 140 human tissues and cells, with notable abundance in oral epithelium and skin, and is secreted into the blood plasma17,18. This widespread distribution underscores its fundamental involvement in cellular homeostasis and stress responses, encompassing the regulation of cell apoptosis and proliferation, tissue repair and regeneration, anti-inflammation, and modulation of glycolipid metabolism17,18. Its potential role in retinal neovascularization has not been studied thus far. This study delineates the expression, functional role, and underlying mechanisms of MYDGF in retinal angiogenesis and vascular homeostasis.

Emerging research suggests a possible role for MYDGF in promoting angiogenesis. MYDGF is an important regulator of cardiac angiogenesis, acting via upregulating FGF1 to promote endothelial cell proliferation and neovascularization after myocardial infarction, with its deficiency leading to exacerbated cardiac dysfunction20. A double-cross-linked hydrogel loaded with MYDGF significantly promoted full-thickness skin wound healing in mice by accelerating angiogenesis, likely via enhanced endothelial cell migration21. Considering the persistent challenges with anti-VEGF therapies13–15 and MYDGF’s known roles in tissue repair and cell homeostasis, we hypothesize that MYDGF represents a regulatory factor in RNV that could be targeted to improve outcomes in conditions like DR and ROP. In this study, we demonstrate that MYDGF promotes both physiological and pathological retinal angiogenesis and maintains vascular homeostasis by activating the Gαi1/3 (G protein subunit alpha i 1 and 3)-Gab1-Akt-mTOR signaling pathway.

Results

Increased expression of MYDGF in fibrovascular membrane tissue of PDR patients

To determine whether MYDGF expression is dysregulated in proliferative diabetic retinopathy (PDR), we initiated our investigation by analyzing single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) data derived from fibrovascular membranes (FVMs) obtained from PDR patients (GEO database accession: GSE16578422). This analysis identified ten distinct cellular subpopulations within the FVMs, including microglia, monocytes/macrophages, pericytes, cycling microglia, endothelial cells, dendritic cells, fibroblasts, astrocytes, and B cells (Fig. 1A). Subsequent expression percentage and density analysis of the scRNA-seq data revealed a notable enrichment of MYDGF transcripts predominantly within the endothelial cells of FVMs (Fig. 1B). Further quantitative assessment using a dot plot (Fig. 1B, the right panel) showed that the endothelial cell cluster exhibited the highest average expression (Ave. Exp.) of MYDGF compared to all other identified cell types, including various immune and stromal cell populations (e.g., microglia, fibroblasts, pericytes). Furthermore, a substantial percentage of endothelial cells (Percent Exp.) expressed MYDGF (Fig. 1B, the right panel), indicating that the MYDGF is broadly present throughout this population.

Using CD31-based magnetic bead sorting, we isolated primary retinal endothelial cells from human clinical samples. Control endothelial cells (Ctrl) were harvested from adult retinal tissues following traumatic globe enucleation under informed consent, while pathological endothelial cells were isolated from fibrovascular membranes (FVMs) harvested from PDR patients. Due to the limited cellular yield from individual surgical specimens, cells from three patients were combined into a single biological pool for analysis. A total of six patients per group were utilized to generate two independent biological pools (Pool 1# and Pool 2#). Western blot analysis was performed using 12 μg of total protein per lane to compare MYDGF protein levels. As shown, MYDGF protein expression was markedly elevated in primary human endothelial cells derived from FVMs compared to those from control retinal tissues (Fig. 1C). A substantial increase in MYDGF protein abundance in both FVM pools (relative expression values of 0.44 and 0.56) compared to control pools (0.04 and 0.06) was noticed (Fig. 1C). qRT-PCR analysis further demonstrated a significant 4- to 5-fold increase in MYDGF mRNA transcripts in FVM-derived endothelial cells (Fig. 1C). These results demonstrate that MYDGF is highly expressed in the vascular endothelial cells of PDR-associated fibrovascular membranes.

To further validate these findings, the immunofluorescence staining, using a specific vascular marker CD31 (platelet endothelial cell adhesion molecule-1), revealed significant co-localization of MYDGF (green) with vascular endothelial cells (CD31 positive) within the FVMs of PDR patients (Fig. 1D), indicating that MYDGF is primarily expressed within the vascular endothelium within these membranes. To quantify this relationship, a Pearson correlation coefficient was calculated: the correlation between MYDGF and CD31 is high (approximately 0.7), suggesting a robust spatial overlap with the vasculature. In contrast, the correlation between MYDGF and IBA1 (an established marker for microglia/macrophages) is significantly lower (less than 0.1) (Fig. 1D). While these data demonstrate that MYDGF is highly enriched within the vascular endothelial cells of PDR-associated membranes, we acknowledge that a possible contribution from infiltrating immune cells (microglia/macrophages) cannot be entirely excluded. These results provide evidence consistent with the scRNA-seq data and further support the possible involvement of MYDGF in retinal neovascularization.

MYDGF-induced pro-angiogenic activity in cultured endothelial cells is mediated by Akt-mTOR activation

To elucidate the functional impact of MYDGF on endothelial cell angiogenic capacity, human retinal microvascular endothelial cells (hRMECs) were treated with recombinant MYDGF at varying concentrations (2.5–25 ng/mL). Prior to treatment, hRMEC were subjected to a 30-min incubation with warm PBS. This step is a necessary procedure for eliminating all potential nutrition, growth factors, and other activating components from the cell surface and culture environment. Western blotting analysis demonstrated that MYDGF treatment induced a concentration-dependent increase in the phosphorylation levels of Akt (p-Akt Ser473), its downstream effectors, ribosomal protein S6 kinase (p-S6K, Thr-389) and ribosomal protein S6 (p-S6, Ser-235/236) (Fig. 1E). Expression of total Akt1, S6K and S6 was however not significantly changed (Fig. 1E). Furthermore, MYDGF, at 10 and 25 ng/mL, significantly enhanced several key angiogenic processes in hRMECs: cell proliferation, as quantified by nuclear EdU incorporation assays (Fig. 1F); cellular migration, assessed via Transwell migration assays (Fig. 1G); in vitro tube formation on Matrigel (Fig. 1H); and in vitro sprouting from endothelial spheroids (Fig. 1I). The most robust pro-angiogenic effects across all assays were consistently observed at a MYDGF concentration of 25 ng/mL (Fig. 1F–I). To confirm the mechanistic role of Akt-mTOR activation, MYDGF-treated hRMECs were co-treated with the phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase (PI3K)-Akt-mTOR inhibitor LY294002 (LY, 10 µM) or wortmannin (WT, 10 µM)23. The functional assay results show that the two inhibitors almost completely abrogated MYDGF-induced cell proliferation (Fig. S1A), migration (Fig. S1B), tube formation (Fig. S1C), and sprouting (Fig. S1D) in hRMECs. Thus, the observed pro-angiogenic activity of MYDGF in cultured endothelial cells is critically dependent upon the activation of the PI3K-Akt-mTOR signaling pathway.

To further examine the interplay between MYDGF and activated Akt-mTOR signaling in angiogenesis, hRMECs were transfected with constitutively-active Akt1 (caAkt1, S473D24,25). As demonstrated in Fig. S1E, caAkt1 significantly increased Akt phosphorylation (p-Akt-S473) and phosphorylation of S6K (p-S6K), compared to vector controls (“Vec”). The addition of MYDGF (25 ng/mL, 15 min) to caAkt1-expressing hRMECs did not elicit any further statistically significant increase in either p-Akt-S473 or p-S6K levels (Fig. S1E). Furthermore, caAkt1 significantly promoted multiple pro-angiogenic functions in hRMECs. Specifically, functional assay results show that caAkt1 enhanced cell proliferation (EdU incorporation, Fig. S1F), increased cell migration (Fig. S1G), augmented tube formation (Fig. S1H), and stimulated vessel sprouting (Fig. S1I), all significantly compared to vector controls. Importantly, MYDGF supplementation (25 ng/mL) in caAkt1-expressing cells failed to induce any additional statistically significant increases in proliferation (Fig. S1F), migration (Fig. S1G), tube formation (Fig. S1H), or sprouting (Fig. S1I) in hRMECs. These data collectively indicate that while caAkt1 is sufficient to markedly increase Akt-mTOR activation and induce robust pro-angiogenic effects in hRMECs, exogenous MYDGF does not provide additional stimulatory effects under conditions of already maximally engaged Akt signaling. This further supports that the Akt-mTOR cascade is vital for MYDGF-induced pro-angiogenesis response.

To determine if conditions associated with diabetic vascular disease could induce similar cellular phenotypes to MYDGF stimulation, hRMECs were treated with high-glucose medium (25 mM). Western blot analysis revealed a significant, time-dependent increase in intracellular MYDGF protein levels following exposure to high-glucose for 24 and 48 h (Fig. S2A). Similarly, an enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) of the cell culture supernatant demonstrated that high-glucose markedly stimulated the secretion of MYDGF into the extracellular environment (Fig. S2B). High-glucose significantly increased cell proliferation (EdU assays) and migration (“Transwell” assays); however, these effects were partly inhibited by MYDGF knockdown using targeted shRNA (Fig. S2C, D). In tube formation and 3D spheroid assays, the marked increase in capillary-like structures and sprout growth triggered by high-glucose was also suppressed after MYDGF shRNA treatment in hRMECs (Fig. S2E, F).

To determine if secreted MYDGF is functionally responsible for the observed angiogenic changes, we performed a neutralization assay using a specific anti-MYDGF antibody. hRMECs were cultured in conditioned media collected from cells exposed to either basic or high-glucose conditions (Fig. S2G). Exposure to high-glucose conditioned media (HG cond. media) stimulated hRMEC proliferation and migration compared to basal conditioned media (Fig. S2H, I). However, the addition of the anti-MYDGF neutralizing antibody effectively inhibited these effects (Fig. S2H, I). In contrast, treatment with a non-specific IgG control had no such inhibitory effect (Fig. S2H, I). Similarly, the enhanced spheroid sprouting-induced by HG conditioned media, measured by both sprout number and total sprout length, was suppressed following the neutralization of extracellular MYDGF (Fig. S2J). These findings identify MYDGF as an important downstream mediator of high-glucose-induced pathological angiogenesis in hRMECs.

Endothelial knockdown of MYDGF inhibits pathological angiogenesis in oxygen-induced retinopathy (OIR) mice

The OIR mouse model is a well-established and highly valuable tool for investigating retinal neovascularization due to its ability to consistently mimic the ischemic and subsequent proliferative stages characteristic of various human retinopathies. To delineate the cellular landscape and investigate the role of MYDGF in OIR, scRNA-seq data from OIR and normoxic control retinas were obtained from the GEO database (GSE15070326). Unsupervised clustering and UMAP (uniform manifold approximation and projection) visualization identified 12 distinct cell subpopulations based on unique transcriptional profiles (Fig. 2A, B). UMAP density plots revealed an enrichment of MYDGF-positive cells within the endothelial cell cluster in OIR retinas (Fig. 2B–D). Quantitative analysis by dot plot further demonstrated a significant upregulation of MYDGF expression and percentage of expressing cells specifically within the retinal endothelial cell population derived from OIR mice (Fig. 2D). Subsequent scRNA-seq analysis focused on resolving distinct endothelial cell subtypes, including Phalanx cells, Stalk cells and tip cells (Fig. 2E–G). Stalk cells proliferate and extend the emerging vessel, maintaining its structure and lumen formation while coordinating with tip cells to ensure proper elongation and stabilization during angiogenesis27,28. Tip cells lead the sprouting process during angiogenesis by extending filopodia to navigate and sense guidance cues, thus directing the growth and direction of new vessel sprouts27,28. scRNA analysis shows that elevated MYDGF expression is substantially expressed in Tip cells and Stalk cells (Fig. 2E–G), thereby suggesting a specific linkage between MYDGF and retinal endothelial cell activation during pathological angiogenesis.

To validate these transcriptomic observations at the protein level, Western blotting analysis was performed, confirming a significant upregulation of MYDGF protein expression in the primary murine retinal microvascular endothelial cells (mRMECs) of OIR model mice at P17, as compared to age-matched normoxic controls (Fig. 2H). To experimentally determine the functional contribution of endothelial MYDGF to retinal neovascularization, we administered AAVENT-ICAM2p-shMYDGF via retro-orbital injection to P3 mice for endothelial-specific knockdown of MYDGF (MYDGF-eKD). Control groups received the same AAV vector but carrying scramble control shRNA (shC). Following induction of the OIR model, quantitative assessment of retinal vascular pathology at P17 via IB4 immunofluorescence staining demonstrated that MYDGF-eKD significantly attenuated the pathological vascular tuft area (Fig. 2I). In contrast, no significant difference was observed in the avascular area between the MYDGF-eKD and shC groups (Fig. 2I). Western blotting analysis of mRMECs of the P17 mice confirmed that MYDGF-eKD led to a significant reduction in MYDGF protein expression as well as the phosphorylation levels of both Akt and S6K, indicating inhibition of the Akt-mTOR signaling pathway (Fig. 2J). These data collectively establish that MYDGF is significantly upregulated in activated retinal endothelial cells during OIR and is important in promoting pathological angiogenesis and the activation of the Akt/mTOR signaling cascade. Next, rescue experiments were carried out. In OIR mice, MYDGF-eKD significantly reduced the area of pathological neovascular tufts compared to the shC control (Fig. S3A). Critically, the inhibitory effect of MYDGF-eKD was fully reversed when recombinant MYDGF was subsequently injected (+MYDGF), restoring the neovascular tuft area (Fig. S3A). MYDGF knockdown, or cotreatment with recombinant MYDGF, did not significantly alter the avascular area across any group (Fig. S3A). The ELISA assay results show that MYDGF-eKD, alone or combined with MYDGF rescue treatment, did not alter the concentration of VEGF in the vitreous humor of P17 OIR mice (Fig. S3B).

qRT-PCR analysis of P17-OIR mouse retinal tissues showed that while VEGFA mRNA levels remained unchanged, the expression of VEGFR2 mRNA was downregulated in the MYDGF-eKD group compared to the control (shC) group (Fig. S3C). Notably, intravitreal administration of recombinant MYDGF effectively restored VEGFR2 mRNA transcripts to near-baseline levels (Fig. S3C). Western blot analysis confirmed that MYDGF-eKD led to a substantial reduction in VEGFR2 protein abundance in the retinal tissues, while VEGFA protein levels were unaffected (Fig. S3D). The supplementation of recombinant MYDGF successfully rescued VEGFR2 protein expression (Fig. S3D). In addition, MYDGF-eKD treatment also led to a marked reduction in VEGFR2 protein expression in ex vivo cultured mRMECs of P17 OIR mice, an effect that was completely reversed by rMYDGF treatment, which restored expression to baseline levels (Fig. S3E). These findings suggest that MYDGF is a positive regulator of VEGFR2 expression in the retinal vasculature under pathological conditions, independent of VEGFA ligand availability.

Importantly, the immunofluorescence staining of P17 OIR retinal flat mounts revealed that MYDGF (green) localized primarily to the IB4-positive endothelial cells within the vitreous-extending neovascular tufts (Fig. 2K). Although the scRNA results showed that MYDGF expression was also increased (compared to lower basal expression) in both microglia and Müller glia of P17 OIR mice (Fig. 2D), neither cell type targeted knockdown of MYDGF affected key pathological outcomes. Specifically, microglial-specific knockdown of MYDGF (MYDGF-mKD) did not reduce the OIR-induced increase in the pathological vascular tuft area (Fig. 2L). MYDGF-mKD reduced MYDGF protein expression in ex vivo cultured microglia, (Fig. 2M), but failed to alter MYDGF expression or the activation of the Akt-mTOR cascade in mRMECs (Fig. 2N). Similarly, Müller glial knockdown of MYDGF (MYDGF-MüKD) did not mitigate OIR-induced RNV (Fig. S3F). It reduced MYDGF protein expression in ex vivo cultured Müller glial cells (Fig. S3G), but had no effect on MYDGF expression or Akt-mTOR cascade activation in mRMECs (Fig. S3H).

To confirm the role of endothelial Akt1 in vivo, we administered AAVENT-ICAM2p-shAkt1 via retro-orbital injection at P3 to achieve endothelial-specific knockdown of Akt1 (Akt1-eKD). Assessment of retinal vascular pathology at P17 via IB4 immunofluorescence demonstrated that Akt1-eKD significantly attenuated the pathological vascular tuft area, without significantly affecting the avascular area (Fig. 2O). Western blot analysis of mRMECs at P17 confirmed that Akt1-eKD successfully led to a significant reduction in total Akt1 expression and decreased phosphorylation levels of both Akt and its downstream effector, S6K (Fig. 2P).

Endothelial knockdown of MYDGF inhibits pathological angiogenesis in choroidal neovascularization (CNV) mice

To investigate MYDGF’s role in CNV, we analyzed scRNA-seq data from the Murine ECTax database29. This revealed 18 distinct endothelial cell (EC) subpopulations, with MYDGF significantly enriched in proliferating ECs and tip cells, and overall upregulated in CNV-challenged choroids (Fig. S4A–D). Western blot analysis of primary mouse choroidal vascular ECs (mCVECs) confirmed elevated MYDGF protein levels in CNV mice compared to controls (Fig. S4E). Functionally, endothelial-specific MYDGF knockdown (MYDGF-eKD) reduced the laser-induced CNV lesion area (Fig. S4F). MYDGF-eKD also decreased MYDGF expression and the phosphorylation of Akt and S6K in mCVECs (Fig. S4G). Finally, the reduction in CNV area caused by MYDGF-eKD was reversed by intravitreal injection of recombinant MYDGF (Fig. S4H).

Endothelial knockdown of MYDGF inhibits neonatal mouse retinal angiogenesis

The neonatal mouse retinal angiogenesis model serves as a valuable tool for detailed investigations into the physiological processes of angiogenesis in a controlled setting24,30. This model enables the elucidation of sequential stages, including vessel sprouting, proliferation, migration, and maturation24,30. The scRNA-seq data from mouse retinas at postnatal days P6 and P10 were obtained from the GEO database (GSE17589531). Unsupervised clustering and UMAP visualization identified 12 distinct cell subpopulations (Fig. 3A, B). Expression density plots and subsequent dot plot analysis revealed that MYDGF expression was predominantly localized to endothelial cells and pericytes during retinal development, exhibiting minimal expression in other retinal cell types (Fig. 3C, D). Further sub-clustering of the endothelial cell compartment indicated that MYDGF expression was particularly enriched in actively angiogenic populations, specifically Tip and proliferative cells (Fig. 3E–G).

The temporal expression pattern of MYDGF during retinal vascular development was further validated by qRT-PCR of mRMECs collected at postnatal days P1, P3, P5, and P7. A progressive and significant increase in MYDGF mRNA expression in mRMECs was observed from P1 to P7, correlating with the active phase of retinal vascularization (Fig. 3H, top panel). Western blotting analysis demonstrated a similar upward trend in MYDGF protein levels in mRMECs during this developmental period (Fig. 3H, bottom panel). To investigate the functional significance of MYDGF in normal retinal vascular development, neonatal mice at P1 received retro-orbital injection of either the above AAVENT-ICAM2p-shMYDGF (MYDGF-eKD) or scrambled shRNA (shC). Retinas were collected at P5 and P7, and vascular development was assessed by IB4 immunofluorescence staining. As shown, MYDGF-eKD resulted in a significant reduction in both the retinal vascular area and the number of tip cells at both P5 and P7 (Fig. 3I, top two rows of images and graphs). The number of filopodia was also markedly reduced at P5, indicating impaired angiogenic sprouting (Fig. 3I, bottom row of images and graph). Furthermore, Western blotting analysis of mRMECs from neonatal mice (P7) confirmed that MYDGF-eKD led to a significant decrease in MYDGF protein expression as well as the phosphorylation levels of Akt and S6K (Fig. 3J). These findings collectively establish MYDGF as a crucial factor for physiological retinal vascular development.

Endothelial cell conditional knockout of MYDGF disrupts retinal vascular homeostasis in adult mice

We next established endothelial cell conditional knockout of MYDGF (MYDGF-eCKO) in adult mice. This was achieved by intravitreous injection of AAV5-FLEX-sgMYDGF into 4-week-old TIE1-Cre mice (as reported previously24). Two distinct sgRNA sequences were employed for gene targeting, generating MYDGF-eCKO#1 and MYDGF-eCKO#2 cohorts. Subsequent analysis of primary mRMECs isolated from these animals at week 8 confirmed a substantial reduction in MYDGF protein expression (Fig. 4A). In addition, levels of p-Akt (Ser-473) and p-S6K (Thr-389) were largely decreased, supporting Akt-mTOR inactivation in MYDGF-eCKO endothelial cells (Fig. 4B). Comprehensive retinal vascular imaging was conducted on MYDGF-eCKO mice. As illustrated in Fig. 4C, a distinct inhibition of vascular branch formation was observed in MYDGF-eCKO retinas compared to sgC control retinas. Quantitative analysis showed a statistically significant reduction in the number of vascular branches per view in MYDGF-eCKO mice compared to sgC controls (Fig. 4C). Further detailed histological examination, utilizing trypsin digestion coupled with PAS (Periodic Acid-Schiff) staining (Fig. 4D), revealed a significant elevation in the number of acellular capillaries in MYDGF-eCKO mice compared to sgC controls. Evans Blue (EB) assay results demonstrated a statistically significant increase in retinal vascular permeability in MYDGF-eCKO mice (Fig. 4E). These combined vascular abnormalities represent a disruption of the retinal vascular system after MYDGF-eCKO.

Given the established link between vascular compromise and neuronal injury, the condition of retinal ganglion cells (RGCs) was subsequently evaluated. Our findings consistently demonstrated a significant reduction in RGC numbers in MYDGF-eCKO mice. Staining of retinal flat mounts with Tubb3 (Tubulinß 3 Class III) and NeuN (Neuronal Nuclei) revealed a substantial decrease in positive RGCs per view (Fig. 4F). Similarly, immunohistochemical analysis of retinal sections using RBPMS (RNA binding protein with multiple splicing), a specific marker for RGCs, indicated a significant reduction in RGCs after MYDGF-eCKO (Fig. 4G). Collectively, these results unequivocally demonstrate that MYDGF-eCKO in adult mice leads to a severe disruption of the retinal vasculature and loss of RGCs.

Endothelial cell conditional knockout of MYDGF inhibits pathological angiogenesis in OIR mice and CNV mice

To further elucidate the specific role of MYDGF in pathological retinal angiogenesis, we intravitreously injected the same AAV5-FLEX-sgMYDGF into TIE1-Cre mice at postnatal day 3 (P3). Two distinct sgRNA sequences were again employed for gene targeting, generating MYDGF-eCKO#1 and MYDGF-eCKO#2 cohorts. Subsequently, these mice and the control sgC mice were subjected to OIR treatment. Western blotting validation, performed on primary mRMECs isolated from these OIR mice at P17, confirmed a significant downregulation of MYDGF protein levels in the endothelial cells of the MYDGF-eCKO mice (Fig. 4H). Moreover, Akt-mTOR activation, assessed by levels of p-Akt and p-S6K, was reduced (Fig. 4I). Investigation of the OIR model revealed that MYDGF-eCKO significantly alleviated abnormal neovascularization (Fig. 4J). The quantitative analysis demonstrated a substantial reduction in the percentage of tufts area relative to the total vessel area in MYDGF-eCKO mice (Fig. 4J).

Gαi1 and Gαi3 are essential for MYDGF-induced Akt-mTOR cascade activation in mouse embryonic fibroblasts

Gαi1 (G protein subunit alpha i1) and Gαi3 (G protein subunit alpha i3) have been established as pivotal signaling intermediates, orchestrating the activation of the Akt-mTOR cascade downstream of a spectrum of both RTKs and non-RTK receptors25,28,32–35. In addition, our studies establish that Gαi1/3 proteins function as essential scaffold proteins for pro-angiogenic signaling, integrating inputs from various pro-angiogenic stimuli (VEGF33, RSPO325, Netrin-135) to activate the pivotal PI3K-Akt pathway. Given that MYDGF also engaged the same core PI3K-Akt signaling cascade (see Figs. 1 and S1), we therefore investigated whether Gαi1 and Gαi3 are important for the Akt-mTOR signaling initiated by MYDGF.

Wild-type mouse embryonic fibroblasts (WT-MEFs), Gαi1 single knockout MEFs (Gαi1 KO-MEFs), Gαi3 single knockout MEFs (Gαi3 KO-MEFs), and Gαi1/3 double knockout MEFs (Gαi1/3 DKO-MEFs) were reported previously25,28,32–35. Upon stimulation with 25 ng/mL MYDGF for 10 or 20 min, WT-MEFs exhibited a significant increase in the phosphorylation of Akt (p-Akt, Ser-473), Gsk3α/β (p-GSK3α/β, Ser21/9), S6 kinase (p-S6K, Thr-389), and ribosomal protein S6 (p-S6, Ser-235/6) (Fig. 5A). This robust phosphorylation response, indicative of Akt-mTOR cascade activation, was inhibited in Gαi1-KO MEFs and Gαi3-KO MEFs, and was nearly abolished in Gαi1/3 DKO-MEFs (Fig. 5A). Importantly, total protein levels of Akt, GSK3β, S6K, and S6 remained comparable across all MEF genotypes (Fig. 5A). These findings strongly suggest that both Gαi1 and Gαi3 are individually important, and their combined presence is essential for MYDGF-induced Akt-mTOR cascade activation.

Further validating these observations, Gαi1/3 double knockdown MEFs (Gαi1/3 shRNA-MEFs), generated using lentiviral shRNA vectors (see our previous studies28,33,35), also exhibited a similar blockade of MYDGF-induced phosphorylation of Akt, Gsk3α/β, S6K, and S6 (Fig. 5B), despite comparable total protein levels. Next, Gαi1/3 DKO-MEFs were reconstituted by transducing a lentiviral construct carrying either Gαi1 (OE-Gαi1) or Gαi3 (OE-Gαi3) (see our previous studies28,33,35). Re-expression of either Gαi1 or Gαi3 in Gαi1/3 DKO-MEFs partially restored the phosphorylation of S6K and S6 in response to MYDGF stimulation (Fig. 5C). This demonstrates that reintroduction of individual Gαi subunits can partially rescue the MYDGF-induced signaling deficit. Furthermore, WT-MEFs co-transduced with both OE-Gαi1 and OE-Gαi3 (OE-Gαi1/3-MEFs28,33,35) exhibited an amplified phosphorylation response of Akt, Gsk3α/β, S6K, and S6 upon MYDGF stimulation (Fig. 5D).

The involvement of VEGF receptor 2 (VEGFR2) in MYDGF signal transduction was investigated through shRNA-mediated silencing. While successful knockdown was achieved in hRMECs, resulting in pronounced suppression of total VEGFR2 protein by two distinct shRNAs (shVEGFR2-s1 and shVEGFR2-s2), MYDGF stimulation did not induce detectable phosphorylation of VEGFR2 itself (Fig. 5E). More importantly, subsequent analysis revealed that the MYDGF (25 ng/mL, 20 min)-induced phosphorylation of both Akt and S6K was not inhibited by VEGFR2 silencing (Fig. 5E). In addition, hRMECs were stimulated with MYDGF (25 ng/mL, 20 min), VEGF (25 ng/mL, 20 min), or a combination of both. Both ligands independently induced a significant increase in the phosphorylation of Akt and S6K relative to vehicle-treated hRMECs (Fig. 5F). Notably, co-administration of both ligands (“Combine”) resulted in a significantly augmented phosphorylation of p-Akt and p-S6K compared to stimulation with either ligand alone (Fig. 5F). These findings, therefore, demonstrate that MYDGF-mediated activation of the downstream Akt/S6K signaling cascade is independent of VEGFR2. MYDGF and VEGF can cooperate to amplify downstream pro-angiogenic signaling in endothelial cells.

The functional relevance of these pathways was assessed in vivo utilizing the OIR mouse model. We compared the effects of endothelial-specific MYDGF knockdown (MYDGF-eKD, see Fig. 2), anti-VEGF treatment, and a combination of both on OIR-induced pathological neovascularization (tuft formation). Relative to the shRNA control (shC) group, which exhibited extensive pathological tufts, monotherapy with either anti-VEGF or MYDGF-eKD demonstrated a comparable inhibitory effect on pathological neovascularization, as no statistically significant difference was detected when comparing the two groups. Notably, the combined treatment (MYDGF-eKD + anti-VEGF) yielded a significantly greater reduction in tuft area than either treatment alone (Fig. 5G). Conversely, no significant differences were observed in the closure of the central avascular area among the treatment groups (Fig. 5G). These in vivo data demonstrate that dual targeting of the MYDGF and VEGF pathways provide superior efficacy in reducing pathological retinal neovascularization.

Gαi1 and Gαi3 are essential for MYDGF-induced signaling and pro-angiogenic responses

To establish the role of Gαi1/3 proteins in MYDGF-activated signaling and angiogenesis within retinal endothelial cells, hRMECs were engineered for stable knockdown of Gαi1/3 using lentiviral shRNA vectors. The lentiviral human Gαi1 shRNA construct and the lentiviral human Gαi3 shRNA construct, reported previously25,28,33,35, were both transduced into hRMECs and stable cells were formed after selection (shGαi1/3-hRMECs). The control hRMECs were generated by transfection with a scramble-shRNA lentivirus (shC-hRMECs). Western blotting analysis confirmed successful and substantial knockdown of Gαi1 and Gαi3 protein expression in shGαi1/3-hRMECs (Fig. 6A). MYDGF-induced phosphorylation of Akt and S6K was almost entirely abrogated in shGαi1/3-hRMECs, while total protein levels of Akt and S6K remained unchanged (Fig. 6A). Functional assays demonstrated that MYDGF significantly enhanced cell proliferation (EdU-positive cell), cell migration, and tube formation (Fig. 6B) in shC-hRMECs. Crucially, these pro-angiogenic effects of MYDGF were markedly suppressed in shGαi1/3-hRMECs (Fig. 6B).

To further validate these findings with an alternative genetic approach, a lentiviral CRISPR/Cas9 Gαi1 knockout construct36 and a lentiviral CRISPR/Cas9 Gαi3 knockout construct37,38 were employed to genetically knockout Gαi1/3 in hRMECs (koGαi1/3-hRMECs), and “−1” and “−2” stands for two stable cell selections. The knockout control cells were transduced with the scramble sgRNA-expressing lentiviral CRISPR/Cas9 construct (koC-hRMECs). Western blotting analysis confirmed successful depletion of Gαi1 and Gαi3 protein levels (Fig. 6C). Gαi1/3 knockout significantly inhibited MYDGF-induced phosphorylation of Akt and S6K (Fig. 6C). The functional assay results showed that Gαi1/3 knockout effectively inhibited MYDGF-induced enhancement of cell proliferation (tested via nuclear EdU assays, Fig. S5A), migration (tested via “Transwell” assays, Fig. S5A), and tube formation (Fig. S5C) in hRMECs. In addition, hRMECs were co-transduced with the lentiviral human Gαi1-overexpressing construct and the lentiviral human Gαi3-overexpressing construct (also reported earlier25,28), generating stable Gαi1/3-overexpressing hRMECs (oeGαi1/3-hRMECs) (Fig. 6D). Notably, overexpression of Gαi1/3 significantly enhanced the MYDGF-induced phosphorylation of Akt and S6K in response to MYDGF treatment (Fig. 6D). This amplification of signaling translated into a further promotion of MYDGF-induced cell proliferation, migration, and tube formation (Fig. 6E). These comprehensive in vitro results support the critical role of Gαi1 and Gαi3 in mediating MYDGF-induced pro-angiogenic Akt-mTOR signaling and functional responses in cultured retinal endothelial cells. It should be noted that the protein expression of Gαi1 and Gαi3 in hRMECs remained unchanged following MYDGF (25 ng/mL) treatment over the full time course evaluated (15 min to 24 h) (Fig. 6F).

Endothelial knockdown of Gαi1/3 inhibits MYDGF-induced retinal angiogenesis in vivo

To extend our in vitro findings to an in vivo context, we investigated the role of Gαi1/3 in MYDGF-mediated pro-angiogenic functions in the OIR model. Postnatal day 3 (P3) mice were intravitreally injected with either scrambled shRNA-AAV (shC) or the endothelial-specific murine Gαi1 shRNA AAV plus the endothelial-specific murine Gαi3 shRNA AAV (Gαi1/3-eKD), both were reported previously25,28,35. The shRNA or the Gαi1/3-eKD mice were subjected to OIR and were then intravitreally injected with 50 pg of recombinant MYDGF (MYDGF+shC and MYDGF+Gαi1/3-eKD) at P12. The retinal flat mounts were harvested at P17 and stained with IB4 to assess areas of retinal neovascular tufts and non-perfused zones. MYDGF treatment significantly promoted OIR-induced formation of neovascular tufts in shC-AAV injected retinas (Fig. 6G). Crucially, this pro-angiogenic effect of MYDGF was significantly inhibited in retinas subjected to Gαi1/3-eKD (Fig. 6G), demonstrating that Gαi1/3 are indispensable for MYDGF-induced pathological angiogenesis in vivo.

Furthermore, we examined the role of Gαi1/3 and MYDGF in physiological retinal vascular development. Postnatal day 1 (P1) mice were intravitreally injected with either scrambled shRNA-AAV (shC) or the endothelial-specific murine Gαi1 shRNA AAV plus the endothelial specific murine Gαi3 shRNA AAV (Gαi1/3-eKD), with the two groups concurrently receiving 50 pg of recombinant MYDGF (MYDGF+shC and MYDGF+Gαi1/3-eKD). At P5, MYDGF treatment significantly promoted retinal neovascularization, evidenced by increased vascular area, a higher number of tip cells, and an elevated number of filopodia (Fig. 6H). However, these pro-endothelial cell developmental effects of recombinant MYDGF were significantly suppressed in retinas with Gαi1/3-eKD (Fig. 6H). Consistent with our in vitro findings, Western blotting analysis of primary mRMECs from the above P5 mice demonstrated a significant blockade of MYDGF-induced phosphorylation of Akt and S6, with Gαi1/3 protein expression significantly inhibited (Fig. 6I). These collective in vivo findings strongly corroborate our in vitro results, confirming the essential role of Gαi1/3 in mediating MYDGF-induced angiogenic responses in both pathological (OIR) and physiological retinal vascular contexts.

Gab1 acts downstream of Gαi1/3 to mediate MYDGF-induced Akt/mTOR activation and angiogenesis

Previous studies from our group have indicated an interaction between the adapter protein Gab1 and the Gαi1/3 complex32,35,39, prompting further investigation into its potential role in MYDGF-mediated signaling activation. Western blotting analysis revealed that MYDGF-induced Gab1 phosphorylation (Tyr-627) was completely abolished in Gαi1/3 double knockout (DKO) MEFs, and significantly reduced in Gαi1-KO or Gαi3-KO MEFs (Fig. 7A). This observation was further supported by findings in Gαi1/3 shRNA-silenced MEFs (Fig. 7B), where MYDGF-induced Gab1 phosphorylation (Tyr-627) was also significantly inhibited. Reintroduction of either Gαi1 or Gαi3 into Gαi1/3 DKO MEFs partially restored Gab1 (Tyr-627) phosphorylation (Fig. 7C), while the co-overexpression of both Gαi1 and Gαi3 further amplified the Gab1 Tyr-627 phosphorylation response (Fig. 7D), indicating that Gαi1/3 are critical upstream regulators of Gab1 phosphorylation in MYDGF signaling.

To confirm Gab1’s downstream role in MYDGF signaling, Gab1 KO-MEFs (as reported previously39) were utilized. Similar to the Gαi1/3 DKO phenotype, Gab1 knockout significantly inhibited MYDGF-induced phosphorylation of Akt, Gsk3α/β, S6K, and S6 (Fig. 7E), underscoring Gab1’s essential role in transmitting MYDGF signal. Furthermore, in hRMECs, MYDGF treatment not only enhanced the phosphorylation of Gab1 but also promoted the formation of a Gαi1/3-Gab1 signaling complex, as demonstrated by co-immunoprecipitation experiments (Fig. 7F). This interaction further supports the critical role of this complex in mediating MYDGF-induced signaling activation.

To definitively confirm Gab1’s role in MYDGF-induced in vitro angiogenesis, stable Gab1 knockdown (shGab1-hRMECs, using the lentiviral shRNA construct) or knockout (koGab1-hRMECs, using the CRISPR/Cas9 method) hRMECs were generated. Both shGab1 and koGab1 hRMECs exhibited a complete abrogation of MYDGF-induced phosphorylation of Akt and S6K (Fig. 7G), mirroring the effects observed in shGαi1/3 and koGαi1/3 hRMECs (see Fig. 6). Gαi1/3 protein expression was unchanged in these Gab1-depleted hRMECs (Fig. 7G). Consistent with its pivotal role in signaling, the functional results showed that both shGab1 and koGab1 hRMECs displayed a significant suppression of MYDGF-induced cell proliferation (tested via nuclear EdU incorporation), cell migration (tested via “Transwell” assays), and tube formation (Fig. S6A).

The phosphorylation of three specific tyrosine residues on the scaffolding protein Gab1—Y447, Y472, and Y589—is essential for activating the PI3K/Akt pathway40–42. When phosphorylated, these sites form pYxxM motifs that directly bind the p85 regulatory subunit of PI3K, leading to the activation of Akt40–42. Co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) experiments revealed that stimulation with MYDGF (25 ng/mL) in hRMECs markedly increased the tyrosine phosphorylation of Gab1 (p-Tyr) and enhanced its association with Gαi3 and the p85 regulatory subunit of PI3K (Fig. S6B), which was abolished in shGαi1/3 and koGαi1/3 hRMECs (Fig. S6C). We next tested the necessity of the three canonical p85-binding sites on Gab1 by expressing a mutant (Gab1-3F) in which these sites (Y447/472/589) were mutated to phenylalanine (Y447F/Y472F/Y589F). In koGab1 hRMECs (see Fig. 5), while wild-type Gab1 (wt-Gab1) rescued MYDGF (25 ng/mL, 5 min)-induced p85 binding (Fig. S6D), the Gab1-3F mutant failed to do so (Fig. S6D). Consistent with this, re-expression of wild-type Gab1, but not the Gab1-3F mutant, restored MYDGF-induced phosphorylation of Akt (S473) and its downstream target S6K in koGab1 cells (Fig. S6E). This demonstrates that MYDGF-dependent Akt activation is mediated through the specific p85-binding sites on Gab1 (Y447/472/589). Note that the Gab1-3F mutant did not alter Gαi1 and Gαi3 protein expression (Fig. S6E).

We then determined if downstream Akt activation was sufficient to rescue the functional defects caused by Gab1 loss. Expression of a constitutively active Akt1 (caAkt1, see Fig. S1) in shGab1 hRMECs restored Akt and S6K phosphorylation without altering Gab1 protein expression (Fig. S6F). Importantly, this downstream activation completely rescued the impairments in proliferation (EdU incorporation), cell migration, and tube formation that were observed in shGab1 hRMECs (Fig. S6G). Collectively, these data establish that the primary pro-angiogenic function of Gab1 in response to MYDGF is to serve as a scaffold for the activation of the PI3K/Akt pathway.

To further investigate the role of Gab1 in MYDGF-mediated responses, hRMECs were engineered to overexpress wild-type Gab1 (oeGab1) via a lentiviral construct, with vector-transduced cells (Vec) serving as a control. Western blotting analysis confirmed successful overexpression of Gab1 and showed increased phosphorylation of Gab1 in oeGab1 hRMECs upon MYDGF stimulation (Fig. 7H). MYDGF-induced phosphorylation of Akt and S6K was also significantly augmented, whereas Gαi1 and Gαi3 protein expression levels were unchanged (Fig. 7H). Functionally, increasing Gab1 expression significantly amplified MYDGF-induced cell proliferation, as measured by nuclear EdU incorporation (Fig. 7I). Similarly, cell migration, assessed by Transwell assay (Fig. 7J), and in vitro tube formation (Fig. 7K) were markedly enhanced in oeGab1 hRMECs in response to MYDGF. Collectively, these data suggest that Gab1 acts downstream of Gαi1/3 to mediate MYDGF-induced Akt/mTOR activation and angiogenesis.

Discussion

MYDGF is a pleiotropic protein with significant paracrine functions in tissue repair, metabolism, and inflammation17–19. It has been established as a pro-angiogenic factor, critically contributing to tissue repair and regeneration, particularly following ischemic injury17,18. Its pro-angiogenic activity is primarily mediated by directly stimulating endothelial cell proliferation and regulating their survival16–18. Mechanistically, MYDGF activates key intracellular signaling cascades43 which are essential for endothelial cell function17,18. Furthermore, MYDGF functions as a sophisticated mechanically-induced angiocrine signal; for instance, in the context of liver regeneration, mechanical stretch on endothelial cells in hepatic vasculature triggers MYDGF release, which then stimulates the proliferation and survival of adjacent hepatocytes43. This multifaceted role highlights MYDGF’s pivotal contribution to adaptive angiogenesis, facilitating robust tissue recovery and repair across various physiological and pathological conditions17,18.

This study provides evidence that MYDGF plays a pivotal role in retinal neovascularization and vascular homeostasis, highlighting its potential as a therapeutic target. scRNA-seq and validation in PDR patient fibrovascular membranes (FVM) reveal that MYDGF expression is significantly upregulated and enriched in endothelial cells in PDR, emphasizing its possible involvement in pathological angiogenesis. In vitro experiments demonstrated that recombinant MYDGF activated the Akt-mTOR signaling pathway in hRMECs, leading to enhanced proliferation, migration, tube formation, and sprouting—key processes in neovascularization. The activation of this pathway was confirmed to be essential, as inhibition of Akt-mTOR signaling effectively abolished MYDGF-induced pro-angiogenic behaviors in hRMECs (see proposed signaling model cartoon in Fig. 8).

Moreover, bioinformatic analyses combined with experimental validation revealed that MYDGF levels were elevated in activated endothelial cells during OIR and CNV in mice, further supporting its role in pathological retinal neovascularization. Functionally, endothelial-specific knockdown of MYDGF significantly suppressed abnormal neovascularization in both OIR and CNV models, demonstrating its necessity in retinal neovascularization. MYDGF-eKD also impeded normal retinal vascular development in neonatal mice. Additionally, endothelial cell conditional knockout of MYDGF disrupted vascular integrity in adult mice, resulting in impaired vascular branching, increased formation of acellular capillaries, blood-retinal barrier leakage, and RGC loss. These findings underscore MYDGF’s dual role in promoting pathological angiogenesis and maintaining physiological vascular homeostasis, revealing its importance in retinal vascular biology. Therefore, MYDGF acts as a central regulator of retinal angiogenesis, providing an additional avenue for therapeutic intervention beyond current anti-VEGF strategies.

Our findings support a primarily pro-angiogenic role for MYDGF in endothelial cells. scRNA-seq analysis identified endothelial cells as the predominant source of MYDGF, which we confirmed via co-localization with endothelial cell markers in both FVMs of PDR patients and mouse OIR neovascular tufts. While MYDGF was also upregulated in microglia and Müller glia during OIR, this contribution did not appear essential for pathology. Critically, targeted MYDGF knockdown in either microglia or Müller glia failed to reduce retinal neovascularization in OIR mice or inhibit downstream Akt-mTOR signaling within mRMECs.

Despite its established extracellular biological activities in angiogenesis, tissue repair, metabolism, and inflammation17,18, the specific plasma membrane receptor(s) mediating these effects remain largely unknown. MYDGF possesses a unique 10-stranded β-sandwich structure that shares no sequence or structural homology with any previously characterized growth factor or cytokine families, making it difficult to infer potential receptor classes44.

G protein subunit alpha inhibitory (Gαi) proteins constitute a family of heterotrimeric G proteins, comprising three primary isoforms: Gαi1, Gαi2, and Gαi345,46. Gαi proteins are recognized for their role in coupling with G protein-coupled receptors (GPCRs) to inhibit adenylyl cyclase activity, leading to reduced cyclic AMP (cAMP) levels45,46. Gαi1/3 are also indispensable mediators of pro-angiogenic signaling originating from several RTKs and other receptors, including VEGFR2, c-Kit, CD146 (netrin-1 receptor), and LGR4 (leucine-rich repeat-containing G protein-coupled receptor 4, RSPO3 receptor)25,28,33,35. Gαi1/3 participates in receptor endocytosis and complex formation with key adapter proteins, thereby orchestrating the robust activation of crucial intracellular signaling cascades, prominently the PI3K-Akt-mTOR and Erk-MAPK pathways25,28,33,35. This functional integration of Gαi1/3 in receptor trafficking and downstream signal transduction is essential for endothelial cell proliferation, migration, invasion, and tube formation, ultimately promoting angiogenesis25,28,33,35.

Here, we found that the ablation of Gαi1/3 significantly suppressed MYDGF-induced activation of the Akt-mTOR signaling cascade and associated pro-angiogenic activities in both in vitro models (hRMECs) and in vivo during retinal pathological and physiological angiogenesis. Importantly, the adapter protein Gab1 functions as a vital downstream component within this signaling cascade; MYDGF facilitates the formation of the Gαi1/3-Gab1 complex, leading to Gab1 phosphorylation, which is necessary for subsequent Akt-mTOR activation. Knockdown or knockout of Gab1 effectively abolished MYDGF-induced signaling and pro-angiogenic effects, whereas its overexpression potentiated these responses.

Our findings demonstrate that MYDGF triggers the Gαi1/3–Gab1–Akt–mTOR signaling to drive pro-angiogenic activities in retinal endothelial cells. While the genetic ablation of Gαi1/3 or Gab1 effectively abolishes MYDGF-induced signaling, it is important to acknowledge that the precise upstream receptor initiating this cascade remains unidentified. The specific plasma membrane component—whether a classical G-protein-coupled receptor (GPCR), an integrin-associated complex, or a non-canonical scaffold—that bridges extracellular MYDGF to intracellular Gαi1/3 is yet to be determined.

Gαi1/3 and its subsequent recruitment of the Gab1 adapter protein represent a critical signal transduction node, yet the mechanism of Gαi1/3 activation itself could be either receptor-mediated or facilitated through a cytoplasmic scaffold. Given that MYDGF lacks structural homology to traditional growth factors, its interaction with the cell surface may involve a novel recognition mechanism. Therefore, while we have mapped a robust and functionally essential downstream signaling axis (Gαi1/3-Gab1-Akt), this pathway should not be viewed as mechanistically exhaustive. Identifying the “cognate receptor” or the immediate upstream activator of Gαi1/3 remains a pivotal next step to fully resolve the MYDGF signaling landscape. Nevertheless, this detailed pathway elucidation offers a strategic advantage for therapeutic development; rather than solely targeting an elusive receptor, we have now pinpointed multiple, druggable components (Gαi1/3, Gab1, Akt/mTOR) within a confirmed pro-angiogenic cascade (see proposed signaling model cartoon in Fig. 8).

Thus, this study highlights MYDGF as a key regulator of retinal angiogenesis and vascular stability, exerting its effects primarily through the activation of the Gαi1/3-Gab1-Akt-mTOR signaling pathway. Elevated MYDGF levels in pathological retinal conditions underscore its potential as a novel therapeutic target for retinal neovascular diseases, especially given the limitations of current anti-VEGF treatments. Endothelial MYDGF demonstrates a critical duality of function; it acts as a mediator of pathological neovascularization while concurrently serving as an essential factor for both physiological vascular development and the maintenance of adult vascular homeostasis. Consequently, any therapeutic strategy targeting MYDGF, such as a small molecule inhibitor or a monoclonal antibody, must be designed with considerable caution. A complete or systemic blockade of MYDGF signaling risks inadvertently disrupting vascular homeostasis in healthy retinal regions, presenting a challenge analogous to the known limitations of anti-VEGF therapies. Therefore, future therapeutic interventions may require precise dosing, localized delivery mechanisms, or sophisticated designs capable of selectively inhibiting the pathological hyper-activation of the MYDGF pathway in hypoxic/inflammatory settings while preserving its essential homeostatic functions.

Methods

Human tissues and ethics

Research involving human tissues was performed in accordance with all relevant guidelines and regulations, and all protocols adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki principles. The study was approved by the Institutional Ethics Board of the Affiliated Hospital of Nanjing Medical University. This study enrolled 8 patients diagnosed with proliferative diabetic retinopathy who underwent lensectomy combined with vitrectomy surgery (4 males and 4 females, mean age of 58.5 years). An equal number of age-matched and sex-matched control patients undergoing traumatic retinectomy were included as the control group. The primary human vascular endothelial cells used in this study were isolated directly from the surgical tissues of these same recruited patient and control cohorts. For molecular analyses, cells were pooled from donors of both sexes. Importantly, separate written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to enrollment, which explicitly authorized both study participation and the isolation and generation of primary cells from their donated tissues. Patient groups were balanced for age and sex to minimize potential confounding factors. The study complied with the Guidance of the Ministry of Science and Technology (MOST) for the Review and Approval of Human Genetic Resources. No participant compensation was provided for the donation of tissue samples during the necessary surgical procedures.

Mice and ethics

All animal experiments were conducted in strict accordance with the guidelines stipulated in the Association for Research in Vision and Ophthalmology (ARVO) Statement for the Use of Animals in Ophthalmic and Vision Research. The studies were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee at the Affiliated Hospital of Nanjing Medical University. C57BL/6J mice were procured from Nanjing Junke Biological Co., Ltd (Nanjing, China). TIE1-Cre C57BL/6J mice were procured from Genechem Co., Ltd (Shanghai, China). Mice were maintained in a specific pathogen-free facility at 22–24 °C and 50–60% humidity. The environment followed a standard 12-h light and 12-h dark cycle, with animals receiving ad libitum access to water and food. For neonatal experiments, due to the subtle morphological differences in neonates and to minimize maternal stress and potential cannibalism, sex was not identified during the neonatal period (P1-P17). However, sex-based biological bias was mitigated by ensuring a randomized distribution of littermates across all experimental groups. For adult studies, including CNV mice, only male mice were used to avoid confounding effects from the female estrous cycle. Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) and the Ethics Committee of Nanjing Medical University Eye Hospital, adhering to international standards for ethical treatment of animals in research.

Reagents

Chemicals, including Polybrene, puromycin, LY294002, and wortmannin, were procured from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO). All cell culture components, such as Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM), antibiotics, and serum, were obtained from Gibco-BRL (Suzhou, China). For a comprehensive list of all antibodies utilized, please refer to Table S1.

Single-cell RNA sequencing data analysis

Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) data were processed and analyzed using R software (version 4.2.2). This included data from proliferative diabetic retinopathy (PDR) patient fibrovascular membranes (FVM) (GSE165784), oxygen-induced retinopathy (OIR) mouse retinas (GSE150703), postnatal day 6 (P6) and P10 mouse retinas (GSE175895), and choroidal endothelial cells from CNV mice (Murine ECTax, https://endotheliomics.shinyapps.io/murine_ectax/). Raw count data underwent normalization using the ‘Seurat’ package (version 4.3.0), followed by highly variable gene identification. Dimensionality reduction was performed via principal component analysis (PCA), and UMAP was applied for visualization and cellular relationship mapping. Cells were clustered using “FindClusters” based on shared gene expression, and UMAP plots were generated for cluster distribution and relationship illustration.

Primary human vascular endothelial cell isolation

FVM specimens from three PDR patients were pooled as one biological sample, while individual normal retinas served as controls. Tissues were rapidly thawed at 37 °C, washed in ice-cold PBS (2% P/S), and minced into 1 mm fragments. Enzymatic digestion was performed in DMEM containing 1 mg/mL collagenase type II and 20 μg/mL DNase I at 37 °C for 45 min with periodic trituration. After termination with 10% FBS and filtration through a 70 μm mesh, cells were collected by centrifugation (300 × g, 5 min). For purification, cells were incubated with human CD31 MicroBeads (Miltenyi Biotec, #130-091-935) in MACS buffer at 4 °C for 15 min, followed by magnetic separation using an MS column. The eluted CD31+ fraction was immediately processed for Western blot and qRT-PCR analyses.

Laser-induced choroidal neovascularization (CNV) model and ex vivo choroidal explant culture

Following AAV injection, C57BL/6J mice were anesthetized, and their pupils were dilated with 1% tropicamide (Alcon, USA). Laser photocoagulation (577 nm, Quantel Medical; 50 µm beam, 100 ms pulse, 100 mW) was applied at four peripapillary positions using a slit-lamp delivery system. Subretinal bubble formation confirmed Bruch’s membrane disruption; mice without bubbles were excluded. Mice were then humanely euthanized and their eyes were immediately enucleated and submerged in ice-cold Endothelial Basal Medium (EBM) (Lonza, Basel, Switzerland) for dissection. The choroid was carefully isolated and sectioned into 1 ×1 mm² explants. These explants were then embedded in a mixture of growth factor-reduced Matrigel (Corning, Corning, New York) and Endothelial Growth Medium-2 (EGM-2) (Lonza) supplemented with 10% serum. Each embedded explant was placed into a 24-well plate and incubated at 37 °C for 30 min to allow the Matrigel to solidify. Subsequently, 500 μL of fresh EGM-2 medium was added to each well, and the cultures were maintained at 37 °C. Vascular growth was assessed by capturing images of the explants between 5 days post-culture, with the sprouting area quantified by measuring the extent of vascular outgrowth.

Oxygen-induced retinopathy (OIR)

C57BL/6J mice at postnatal day 7 (P7) were exposed to 75 ± 2% oxygen (P7-P12) with nursing mothers, then returned to normoxic room air (21% oxygen) until P17. To ensure adequate sample size and minimize potential litter-to-litter variation, we confirm that the five neonatal mice used in each experimental group were sourced from a minimum of three distinct litters and mouse body weights at the experimental endpoint (P17 OIR) were not significantly different between the treatment groups (P > 0.05).

Immunofluorescence staining

For flat-mount preparation, mice were first anesthetized with a mixture of ketamine hydrochloride (80 mg/kg) and xylazine (10 mg/kg), followed by euthanasia via cervical dislocation. Eyes were enucleated and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) for 60 min. The retina was then carefully dissected into four quadrants, while the choroid was sectioned into eight quadrants. Tissue samples were permeabilized in PBS containing 0.1% Triton X-100 and 5% BSA (240GR100, Biofroxx, Germany) for 60 min. Following permeabilization, samples were incubated with primary antibodies at 4 °C for 12 h. After washing, samples were incubated with appropriate secondary antibodies at room temperature for 2 h. For vascular staining, samples were incubated with Griffonia simplicifolia isolectin GS-IB4 (IB4, Thermo Fisher Scientific, I21413, 1:50). Images were subsequently captured using a fluorescence microscope (Zeiss). For RGC staining, samples were subjected to either NeuN/Tubb3 double staining in flat-mounted retinas or RBPMS staining in retinal cryosections. The antibodies utilized for the immunofluorescence assays were listed in Table S1.

Cells

Mouse embryonic fibroblasts (MEFs, cat no. CL-0006) and Human retinal microvascular endothelial cells (hRMECs, cat no. CP-H130) were procured from the Procell (Shanghai, China) and were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% antibiotic-antimycotic solution (penicillin/streptomycin), and maintained at 37 °C with 5% CO₂ in a humidified incubator. Primary retinal and choroidal endothelial cells were isolated via enzymatic digestion. Retinas were minced and digested with 1 mg/mL collagenase I (Sigma) for 45 min at 37 °C. Digests were then filtered (45 µm strainer, Beyotime) and centrifuged (300 × g, 5 min) before resuspension in endothelial basal medium. For choroidal cells, the RPE (retinal pigment epithelium)-choroid-sclera complex was initially treated with 0.05% trypsin (Thermo Fisher) for 15 min at 37 °C to detach the RPE. The remaining choroid-sclera complex was then minced and digested with 2 mg/mL collagenase II (BioFroxx) and 30 U/mL DNase I (Beyotime) in serum-free medium for 60 min at 37 °C, with pipetting to facilitate dissociation. Post-filtration and centrifugation, choroidal cells were washed and resuspended in endothelial basal medium. All cell suspensions were purified using Dynabeads™ CD31 magnetic beads (Invitrogen) and cultured on 0.2% w/v gelatin-coated 6-well plates in endothelial basal medium for subsequent analysis. For primary culture of murine retinal microglia, eyes from P17 OIR mice are rapidly enucleated and the retinas are carefully dissected free of the cornea, lens, iris, and pigmented epithelium. The tissue undergoes brief enzymatic digestion followed by gentle trituration to yield a single-cell suspension, which is then filtered. High-purity microglia are subsequently isolated using Magnetic-Activated Cell Sorting (MACS) by targeting the cell-surface marker CD11b with magnetic beads. The purified microglia are plated onto poly-L-lysine-coated dishes and maintained in high-glucose DMEM/F-12 medium supplemented with 10% FBS, 1% penicillin-streptomycin, and 10 ng/mL M-CSF, all within a humidified 37 °C incubator with 5% CO2. The CD11b-negative flow-through was used for subsequent isolation of Müller cells, which were isolated using an Anti-GLAST MicroBead Kit (Miltenyi Biotec).

Genetic modifications in vitro and in vivo

Genetic modifications of Gαi1/3 in vitro were performed using lentiviral constructs, including shRNA (short hairpin RNA), overexpression, and knockout (KO) vectors. Genetic modifications of Gab1 (shRNA or overexpression) in vitro were similarly carried out using lentiviral GV492 constructs (shRNA, overexpression, and KO) obtained from Shanghai Genechem Co. (Shanghai, China). The Gab1 triple mutant (Y447F/Y472F/Y589F) was generated by site-directed mutagenesis of the PI3K binding sites using a Quick-Change II kit (Stratagene) with the oligonucleotides. Lentivirus was generated by transfecting HEK-293 cells with the constructs and essential viral packaging components (Genechem Co.). Cells, seeded at 50-60% confluence, were then infected with lentivirus at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 10-11 in complete medium supplemented with polybrene. Following a 24 h incubation, stably transduced cells were selected by culturing them in medium containing 3.0 μg/mL puromycin for 5–6 passages. For in vivo studies, adeno-associated viruses (AAV) from Shanghai Genechem Co. were engineered to express specific shRNA sequences. The MYDGF shRNA sequence (shMYDGF) was incorporated into an endothelial-specific promoter, intercellular adhesion molecule 2 (ICAM2), in conjunction with a high-transduction serotype (AAVENT-ICAM2p), thereby facilitating endothelial knockdown of MYDGF in the retina. To generate Müller glia-specific knockdown of MYDGF, the MYDGF shRNA sequence (shMYDGF) was incorporated into a Müller glia-specific GFAP (glial fibrillary acidic protein) promoter, in conjunction with a high-transduction serotype (AAV-ShH10-pGFAP). To achieve microglial-specific knockdown of MYDGF, the MYDGF shRNA sequence was inserted into a high-transduction adeno-associated virus (AAV-MG1.1) construct. AAV5-TIE1-shGαi1/3 was employed to establish endothelial-specific Gαi1/3 knockdown (Gαi1/3-eKD). To generate MYDGF endothelial cell conditional knockout (MYDGF-eCKO) mice, AAV5-FLEX-sgMYDGF construct was engineered and administered via intravitreal injection into TIE1-Cre C57BL/6 mice. Gene expression modifications were confirmed through qRT-PCR and western blotting assays. The targeted sequences utilized were listed in Table S2.

Anesthesia and AAV administration

C57BL/6 adult mice were anesthetized using a combination of ketamine hydrochloride (80 mg/kg) and xylazine (10 mg/kg). For injections, oxybuprocaine hydrochloride provided topical anesthesia at the injection site. Pups, however, were manually restrained without anesthesia for this procedure. AAV solution, ranging from 0.1 μL (postnatal mice) to 0.25 μL (adult mice) depending on the mouse’s age, was administered via intravitreal or retro-orbital routes. For retro-orbital injections in mice, a 31-gauge needle attached to a 0.3-mL BD Ultra-Fine II syringe (8 mm needle, Shanghai, China) was used following anesthesia. The needle was carefully inserted into the eye socket at the “three o’clock” position with the bevel facing downward and at approximately a 30° angle. Successful entry into the sinus was confirmed by aspirating the syringe plunger to observe blood flow. Intravitreal injections were conducted under a dissecting microscope using a fine glass micropipette connected to a 10 μL Hamilton syringe (Hamilton Co., Reno, NV). The micropipette was precisely inserted through a corneal incision and guided between the iris and lens into the posterior chamber. To ensure optimal data quality, mice exhibiting post-injection lens injury or vitreous hemorrhage were excluded from the study. Following injection, gentle compression was applied to the injection site to prevent reflux of the AAV solution.

5-ethynyl-2′-deoxyuridine (EdU) staining

EdU incorporation assays were performed using the EdU assay kit (Beyotime, China). Cells were incubated with 10 μM EdU (37 °C, 2 h), fixed, washed, and stained with Hoechst 33342 (30 min). Fluorescent images were captured using a Zeiss microscope.

Cell migration

hRMECs (5 × 10⁴ cells/chamber) were seeded into Transwell inserts (Corning). Migration was induced by 10% FBS in the lower chamber (16 h, 37 °C, 5% CO₂). Non-migrated cells were removed, and migrated cells were fixed with 3% paraformaldehyde (15 min), then stained with 0.5% crystal violet. Images were captured using a Zeiss microscope.

Tube formation

The basement membrane matrix (BD Biosciences) was polymerized in 24-well plates (30 min, 37 °C). hRMECs (5 × 10⁴ cells/well) were seeded and cultured (37 °C, 5% CO₂). Tube formation was observed and imaged after 8 h with a Zeiss microscope, and quantified to assess angiogenic activity.

Sprouting assay

hRMECs (8 × 10³) were mixed with 4 mL of cell culture medium and 1 mL of methocel reserve solution containing 12 mg of methylcellulose (Aladdin). Twenty-five microliters of the cell solution were placed onto the lid of a 100 mm cell culture dish, which was then inverted and incubated for 24 h in a humidified incubator. The hanging drops were washed with PBS. The cells were then resuspended in 2 mL of methocel solution containing FBS, PBS, and rat collagen I. The resulting spheroid-collagen solution was added to a 24-well plate and incubated for 24 h. Spheroid sprouting was captured using a light microscope.

Periodic acid-Schiff (PAS) staining

PAS staining was employed for the quantification of acellular capillaries. Freshly enucleated eyes were fixed in 10% neutral formaldehyde for 24 h, after which the retinas were excised and incubated in 3% trypsin at 37 °C until non-vascular tissue dissociated. The isolated vascular network was then gently washed, mounted on glass slides, dried, and stained with periodic acid-Schiff-hematoxylin for examination under light microscopy and subsequent quantification of acellular capillaries from captured images.

Evans blue (EB) permeability assay

Adult mice received intravenous EB dye (30 mg/kg). After 1 h, mice were euthanized, eyes fixed (4% PFA, 30 minutes), and retinas dissected. Portions were used for fluorescence microscopy to visualize EB leakage, while others were incubated overnight in dimethylformamide (70 °C) for dye extraction. Extracted dye concentration was determined spectrophotometrically (A620nm, background corrected at A740nm), and leakage was calculated based on the standard curve of EB.

In vivo retinal ganglion cells (RGCs) assessment

RGCs were quantified using two distinct methods. In flat-mounted retinas, RGCs were identified by NeuN (neuronal nuclear) and Tubb3 (tubulin beta 3 class III) double staining. Alternatively, in retinal cryosections, RGCs were assessed via RBPMS (RNA-binding protein with multiple splicing) staining. Detailed immunofluorescence procedures for both approaches are outlined in the immunofluorescence section.

Retinal vascular development

The retinal vascular development was evaluated in neonatal mice. Pups were euthanized via cervical dislocation, followed by prompt dissection and staining of their retinas for analysis. For studies involving genetic manipulation, endothelial-targeting AAV (0.1 µL) was injected on postnatal day 1 (P1), and eyes were subsequently harvested at predetermined postnatal days. Stained retinal flat mounts allowed for the quantification of several key parameters: radial length (measured from the optic nerve head to the outermost extent of the vascular plexus), vascular density (calculated as the ratio of vascularized area to total retinal area), branching nodes (manually enumerated), tip cells (manually counted at the leading edge of vascular sprouts), and filopodia (number and length, measured from base to tip, assessed from captured images).

Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR)

Total cellular and tissue RNA was extracted using Trizol Reagents (Thermo Fisher Scientific). cDNA synthesis utilized the Prime Script RT Reagent Kit (Takara). qRT-PCR was performed on an ABI Prism 7900 system (Applied Biosystems) with 2 μL cDNA, 2 μL primers, and 10 μL 2× SYBR Green PCR Mix (Takara). Specificity was confirmed by melting curve analysis. Gene expression was calculated using the comparative Ct method (2−ΔΔCt), with GAPDH as the reference. All mRNA primers were from Genechem, listed in Table S3.

Western blotting

Proteins were extracted from cells or fresh tissues using RIPA buffer with 1X protease and phosphatase inhibitors. Supernatants were collected, mixed with sample buffer, and boiled (95 °C, 3–5 min). Proteins were separated by 8-15% Tris-glycine gradient SDS-PAGE (Bio-Rad) and transferred to a PVDF blot. Membranes were blocked with 5% BSA in TBST, incubated with primary antibody (4 °C, overnight), and then with secondary antibody (room temperature, 1 h). The X-ray film, capturing the ECL signal from the PVDF membrane, was mechanically fed into a developing machine using internal rollers to transport the film. The non-stained (non-visible under X-ray film) but colorful protein molecular weight markers were used in our lab. The PVDF membrane was cut post-transfer based on the predicted molecular weight of the target band, leaving approximately a window (15–20 kD above and below the target). This portion was marked on the X-ray film for alignment. The primary image was captured using X-ray film. The target band and its surrounding area were then cropped to a rectangular outline for presentation. This rectangular image was imported into Adobe Photoshop to crop the final band to the figure’s desired width. Full, uncropped rectangular blotting images, including molecular markers (their marked alignment) are provided in the Source Data File. All antibodies used in this study are listed in Table S1.

Quantification of MYDGF and VEGF by ELISA

The R&D Systems Quantikine VEGF ELISA Kit (Cat. No. MMV00) and Cusabio MYDGF ELISA Kit (Cat. No. CSB-EL003433HU) were used to quantify VEGF and MYDGF in the supernatant, strictly following the manufacturer’s sandwich ELISA protocol. After adding samples, standards, detection antibodies, and Streptavidin-HRP conjugate, TMB substrate was used for color development and stopped with stop solution. Absorbance was read at 450 nm (with 540/570 nm correction). The concentration (pg/mL) was calculated against a logistic standard curve.

MYDGF neutralization antibody

Conditioned media collected from hRMECs after 48 h of high-glucose treatment were pre-incubated with a mouse MYDGF neutralizing antibody (R&D Systems, MAB1104) or a rat IgG isotype control (MAB006) for 1 h at 37 °C to block endogenous MYDGF. These pre-treated media were then applied to recipient hRMECs for subsequent functional evaluations.

Statistical analysis

Data were normally distributed and are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical analyses were performed using Student’s t-test for comparisons between two groups and one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s multiple comparison test for comparisons among three or more groups. A P-value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant.
