Adoption of Telemedicine in African Health Policy in Pre and Post COVID‐19 Eras: Innovations, Limitations, Challenges, and Lessons Learned
Authors: Areeba Shahid, Temidire Adesiji, Joseph Conteh, Abdullahi Zainab Zubairu, Rida Arif, Mohamed Hassan Salad, Amna Tariq, Emmanuel Sabo, Melynda Dumbuya, Chijioke Vitalus Iloke, Malik Olatunde Oduoye
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundTelemedicine in Africa has evolved from small‐scale pilots to an essential tool for enhancing healthcare access, efficiency, and equity. Early efforts faced challenges due to limited infrastructure, high costs, and weak policies. The COVID‐19 pandemic accelerated telemedicine adoption, exposing the need for stronger digital health strategies. Despite interest, many countries still struggle with regulation, coordination, and scaling sustainable implementations. This review examines the development and policy adoption of telemedicine across Africa before and after the pandemic, identifying key innovations, ongoing challenges, and lessons to guide future digital health planning.MethodsA narrative review was conducted, with study selection guided by PRISMA transparency standards. A structured search was conducted across PubMed, Google Scholar, Web of Science, Cochrane Library, Scopus, and policy databases from the WHO, the African Union, and UNECA. The search included publications from 2000 to 2025, focusing on English‐language studies addressing telemedicine policies or implementation in African countries.ResultsThe COVID‐19 pandemic accelerated adoption, driving rapid innovation and policy shifts. In Nigeria, platforms such as MobiHealth, Tremendoc, and Dokilink expanded access via phone and WhatsApp, maintaining care during lockdowns. In South Africa, regulatory reforms enabled consultations without prior doctor‐patient relationships, allowing 97% of patients to be managed virtually and reducing hospital burden. Nurse‐led teleconsultations improved access, while a remote diabetes program reduced mortality by 20% among high‐risk patients. Telepsychiatry expanded mental health services, and SMS‐based interventions remained the most widely used due to affordability and accessibility. Despite progress, policy responses were uneven. South Africa's rapid reforms contrasted with Nigeria's fragmented frameworks, which lacked enforceable telemedicine provisions. Persistent challenges include weak data governance, limited interoperability, inadequate funding, and “pilotitis,” where projects fail to scale.ConclusionTelemedicine adoption in Africa has grown markedly since COVID‐19 but remains constrained by infrastructural and regulatory weaknesses, and variable acceptance. Strengthening legal frameworks, improving infrastructure, and increasing awareness among providers and communities are vital to sustain progress, integrate telehealth into routine care, foster innovation, and enhance equitable healthcare across the continent.

Introduction

Telemedicine refers to the delivery of medical care at a distance using digital technologies. Initially developed to improve access for underserved populations, it has become an essential component of modern healthcare systems worldwide. The World Health Organization recognizes telemedicine as a key element of eHealth, supporting affordability, accessibility, and equity in care delivery [1, 2, 3].

In Africa, telemedicine contributes to universal health coverage by enabling chronic disease management, health education, diagnostics, and remote consultations [4, 5, 6]. It reduces travel burdens, alleviates hospital overcrowding, and lowers operational costs, making healthcare more patient‐centered and efficient.

Before COVID‐19, telemedicine adoption across Africa was limited to small‐scale pilots, often donor‐funded and constrained by poor infrastructure, unreliable internet, high costs, and weak regulatory frameworks [7, 8, 9, 10]. These early initiatives, such as teleconsultations in South Africa, Egypt, and Kenya, laid important groundwork but lacked sustainability. African health systems have long struggled with inadequate funding, weak coordination, and inequitable access. Policy frameworks were slow to adapt, leaving gaps in data protection, payment systems, and cross‐border regulation [11, 12, 13]. As a result, most countries were unprepared for large‐scale telemedicine deployment when the pandemic struck.

Strong policy frameworks are essential for telemedicine integration. Clear rules on licensing, ethics, interoperability, and reimbursement build trust and facilitate adoption [12, 14]. Regional collaboration further enhances consistency and knowledge sharing [15]. The COVID‐19 pandemic highlighted both the potential and the vulnerabilities of telemedicine, underscoring the need for robust strategies to embed digital health into routine care [14]. The pandemic acted as a stress test for African health systems, accelerating telemedicine adoption while exposing long‐standing infrastructural and regulatory weaknesses. Reviewing developments before and after COVID‐19 provides critical insights into what worked, where gaps remain, and how digital health can be strengthened for the future. This review examines the evolution and policy adoption of telemedicine across Africa in the pre‐ and post‐COVID‐19 eras, identifying key innovations, persistent challenges, and lessons to guide future digital health planning and integration into resilient healthcare systems.

Methodology

Study Design

This review takes a narrative approach to explore how telemedicine has been adopted into health policies across Africa, both before and after the COVID‐19 pandemic. The goal was to provide a broad understanding of how policies have evolved, how technology has been used, and how health systems have responded. While this is not a systematic review, the process followed PRISMA guidelines where applicable (e.g., the flow of study selection) to ensure clarity and transparency in how studies were chosen and reported. To maintain the narrative nature of this work, a formal quality appraisal of the included studies was not conducted; however, papers were selected based on their relevance to the policy and implementation themes of the review.

Search Strategy

To gather relevant material, a structured search was carried out in August 2025 using specific keywords and Boolean operators. The search was limited to publications from 2000 to 2025 to capture the full evolution of digital health policy in Africa from early pilot programs to the post‐pandemic landscape. Databases and Sources Searched

The search included major academic databases such as PubMed, Google Scholar, Web of Science, Cochrane Library, and Scopus. Additional reports and documents were sourced from organizations like the World Health Organization (WHO), the African Union (AU), the United Nations Economic Commission for Africa (UNECA), and various national health ministry websites. Only English‐language publications from 2000 to 2025 were included.

Study Selection

All search results were imported into reference management software, and duplicates were removed. To ensure scientific rigor and minimize selection bias, two authors (Areeba Shahid and Abdullahi Zainab Zubairu) independently screened titles and abstracts for relevance, followed by a full‐text review. Any conflicts or disagreements during the screening process were resolved by a third author (Rida Arif). Data were synthesized using a thematic analysis approach, identifying and grouping recurring themes across national policies and technological implementation. Information extracted from each study included the country of focus, the specific telemedicine technology used (e.g., mHealth, video consultation), the policy or regulatory framework discussed, and the key outcomes or barriers identified.

Final selections were guided by predefined inclusion criteria, prioritizing literature that provided meaningful insights into telemedicine adoption, policy development, implementation barriers, and health system implications across Africa. A PRISMA flow diagram was used to transparently document the literature identification, screening, eligibility assessment, and final selection process; however, the review was conducted as a narrative synthesis rather than a systematic review. Although 83 studies were included in the final synthesis, the reference list was not restricted exclusively to these studies. Additional relevant literature, including methodological papers, policy documents, regulatory frameworks, and contextual evidence, was incorporated where necessary to support the interpretation of findings and the discussion of broader implications. This approach is consistent with the narrative review design, which aims to synthesize evidence while providing contextual understanding rather than applying systematic review limitations.

Results

Evolution of Telemedicine in Africa: Pre‐COVID‐19 Eras

Before the COVID‐19 pandemic, telemedicine development in Africa was limited by significant infrastructural and non‐infrastructural challenges. Poor telecommunications, unreliable electricity, and low internet penetration restricted the scale and sustainability of telemedicine initiatives [7, 16, 17]. Ethical and policy frameworks addressing patient privacy and data security were often inadequate or absent. As detailed in Table 1, pre‐pandemic initiatives in Mali, Sudan, and Nigeria were characterized by a heavy reliance on donor funding and a lack of long‐term sustainability frameworks [7, 18, 19].

Adoption varied markedly by region. South Africa gradually implemented telemedicine through real‐time and store‐and‐forward modalities, though regulatory restrictions confined its use to established doctor‐patient relationships [20, 21, 22, 23]. Other nations prioritized specific structural enablers; for instance, Kenya leveraged digital identification systems to facilitate care [24], while Rwanda and Uganda focused on workforce mitigation and foundational e‐health infrastructure, respectively [7]. Despite these regional advancements, countries like Nigeria continued to struggle with low digital literacy and funding gaps. On a broader scale, regional collaborations (e.g., FOMTA, RAFT) and public‐private partnerships (e.g., Telemedicine Africa) sought to bridge the urban‐rural divide through knowledge sharing and mobile web‐based platforms. Telepathology also emerged as a promising specialty contingent on increased training in digital skills [7].

Innovations and Acceleration of Telemedicine in African Health Policy During the COVID‐19 Pandemic

The COVID‐19 pandemic accelerated telemedicine uptake by exposing weaknesses in traditional healthcare delivery and highlighting telemedicine's capacity to maintain care continuity under constrained conditions. In Nigeria, telemedicine services primarily relied on telephone and WhatsApp, reflecting infrastructural limitations yet expanding healthcare access during lockdowns [25]. Platforms such as MobiHealth, Tremendoc, and Dokilink offered remote consultations, electronic prescriptions, and follow‐up care, mitigating physician shortages and overcoming geographic barriers [8].

In South Africa, a temporary regulatory shift facilitated the rapid adoption of telemedicine as a primary triage tool. This resulted in approximately 97% of patients being managed virtually, which significantly reduced the burden on physical healthcare facilities [26]. Innovative applications included nurses utilizing teleconsultations from pharmacies and the implementation of a specialized remote service for high‐risk diabetic COVID‐19 patients, which reduced mortality by 20% through early intervention and consistent remote follow‐up [27]. While these advancements demonstrated high clinical efficacy, the lack of a permanent framework has led to ongoing difficulties with service standardization [25]. Rapid Policy Adaptations

Regulatory responses across Africa have been uneven and often reactive. South Africa's rapid guideline revision enabled expanded telemedicine access by allowing the Health Professions Council of South Africa (HPCSA) to waive the requirement for pre‐existing doctor‐patient relationships [28, 29], while Nigeria's regulatory environment remains fragmented. Existing laws and frameworks, such as Nigeria's 2019 Data Protection Regulation (NDPR), lack telemedicine‐specific provisions on cross‐border licensure, informed consent, liability, and data governance [30, 31].

The Federal Ministry of Health's National Health ICT Strategic Framework (2015–2020) recognized digital health's potential but fell short of enforceable regulations or sustainable funding. COVID‐19 spurred the creation of government digital tools for self‐assessment and contact tracing, yet these lacked legislative permanence and ceased post‐pandemic.

Apart from privacy issues, other legal loopholes that need to be addressed include issues of licensing across borders and professional liability [9]. The current regulation does not provide the appropriate jurisdiction when it comes to the responsibility of medical professionals working in one area for patients in another area, regarding their duty and standard of care in the case of negligence [31]. Additionally, there is no standardized method of obtaining the consent of the patient online in virtual consultation sessions [22]. Table 3 summarizes rapid policy adaptations.

Technological Advancements

Technological innovation remains central to telemedicine expansion. Mobile health applications widely facilitate health education, remote consultations, and medication management, making care accessible in rural and low‐resource settings. Communication technologies, SMS, interactive voice responses, and GPS‐enabled platforms enhance data exchange between providers and users [33]. E‐health systems incorporating electronic health records and telemedicine portals support coordinated care and provider collaboration [15].

Emerging integration of artificial intelligence (AI) enhances remote monitoring and diagnostics. Wearable devices and AI‐powered sensors provide continuous surveillance of vital signs, enabling early detection of anomalies and improving management of chronic diseases [34]. Nigerian institutions have developed AI applications such as breast cancer diagnostic algorithms at Lagos University Teaching Hospital and the ADVISER vaccination intervention optimizer in Oyo State [35]. Startups like Wellvis and Pronov offer AI‐driven telemedicine platforms with clinical decision support, diagnostics, prescriptions, and chronic care management. These efforts expand healthcare reach, especially in underserved communities [34].

However, widespread adoption of AI and related technologies faces challenges including weak data protection enforcement, insecure networks, and infrastructural deficits—especially in rural contexts. Additionally, gaps in regulatory clarity related to data governance, algorithmic fairness, and interoperability restrict technology integration at scale.

Combined, these developments reflect a growing ecosystem of telemedicine across Africa, evolving from fragmented pilot projects toward more integrated, technology‐driven healthcare delivery systems. Table 4 summarizes technological advancements.

Expansion of Use Cases

During the pandemic, several African countries used telemedicine to support primary healthcare, mental health services, and chronic disease management. These efforts relied on tools like mobile platforms, basic teleconsultations, and remote monitoring. According to one article, eleven studies from sub‐Saharan Africa highlighted different types of telemedicine interventions, including wearable devices, online training programs, mobile apps, text messaging, video calls, and phone consultations. Among these, phone calls were the most commonly used method [10].

A review of how hypertension is diagnosed and managed across Africa found that text messaging (SMS) remains the most widely used tool for mobile health initiatives. This trend aligns with findings from individual country case studies and is largely because SMS is both affordable and easy for most people to access [36]. The regulatory aspect of chronic care management can also be seen from pilot data on specialized conditions [25]. For instance, it was found by Nittari et al. (2023) that although telemedicine proves to be an excellent approach to handling diabetic ulcers, it requires intensive scrutiny into the medico‐legal issues, such as defining liability and creating a unique form of governance of remote wound treatment [37].

Tanzania reported successful teleconsultations, including pediatric cases, overcoming distance barriers. Telepsychiatry in Nigeria improved access to mental health services and reduced consultation backlogs [38]. The development of mental health interventions using digital technology indicates that the importance of both physical and psychological effects of digital health care cannot be overlooked, despite the emphasis on technological implementation alone [10]. According to findings presented by Scuri et al. (2022), the transition from conventional to online healthcare due to social distancing measures significantly affected the psycho‐physical well‐being of users [39]. While telephones and SMS also support care for children with sickle cell anemia, making it a useful tool in providing counseling to such patients [40]. In Kenya, online training enhanced psychological first aid capacity among scarce mental health professionals, thus helping them counsel their patients more effectively [41]. A randomized controlled trial in South Africa showed improved medication adherence in diabetics [42], while teleconsultations supported toxicology management efficiently [43] and virtual family visits maintained patient dignity and made him feel loved during quarantine [44]. These examples highlight that while high‐tech solutions such as video calls and wearables are emerging, low‐cost tools like SMS and telephone remain the backbone of telemedicine in Africa due to their wide accessibility. Stakeholder Engagement

For digital health programs to take root and expand in developing countries, active involvement from all key stakeholders is crucial. The literature characterizes the stagnation of digital health initiatives as ‘pilotitis,’ where projects fail to transition from the testing phase to full‐scale implementation. This has slowed progress for several innovative efforts aimed at improving healthcare delivery and disease management [45].

This problem often stems from weak coordination among stakeholders, a lack of clear systems for monitoring and evaluation, and poorly defined ways for organizations to work together. When government bodies, NGOs, private tech companies, and healthcare professionals don't coordinate effectively, it can lead to duplicated efforts, wasted resources, and stalled progress. On top of that, limited national digital infrastructure makes it even harder for stakeholders to sustain these advancements [45].

The reviewed studies indicate that stakeholder involvement across lawmakers, healthcare workers, and patients is a primary driver of goal alignment and service delivery. Strengthening public‐private partnerships (PPPs) within digital health systems can further boost funding, scalability, and accountability. In healthcare, PPPs are agreements between public and private sectors that bring together their resources and expertise to build a system that's more responsive, efficient, and effective.

Initiatives such as Hello Mama in Nigeria and Project Kopano in South Africa highlight how poor coordination and limited collaboration among stakeholders can cause projects to stall. When key players aren't fully engaged, it becomes harder to expand health innovations fairly and effectively, slowing progress toward achieving Sustainable Development Goal 3, Good Health and Well‐Being [46]. Discussion

Lessons From the Pandemic and Policy Implications

The COVID‐19 pandemic led to a rapid, global acceleration of telemedicine, with significant innovations in Africa. This review critically examined the adoption of telemedicine in Africa during the pre‐ and post‐COVID‐19 eras. The findings reveal that telemedicine adoption in Africa before COVID‐19 was gradual and constrained by multiple challenges, including inadequate infrastructure, fragmented policies, and limited regulatory frameworks. The pandemic acted as a stress test for digital health ecosystems, exposing critical gaps in health system readiness. One major success was the rapid deployment and adoption of remote consultations, which made healthcare more accessible to people across different income levels, regions, and backgrounds [48]. Health systems that delivered consistent training and support to clinicians and patients saw higher acceptance and effectiveness of telehealth services [49].

Conversely, significant barriers included fragmented systems with poor interoperability, inconsistent internet connectivity, particularly in rural regions, and the lack of robust regulatory frameworks for privacy, reimbursement, and quality assurance [50]. Sustainability of emergency policies is a core challenge; many of the regulatory relaxations, reimbursement flexibilities, and ad hoc telehealth platforms were instituted under duress and lack long‐term institutionalization. Without embedding these emergency measures into formal health policy, their gains risk being reversed [51]. To transition from a reactive to a proactive telehealth strategy, countries must shift focus from crisis response to integrating telehealth into routine care models, extending its use to chronic disease management, preventive services, and follow‐ups [52].

Finally, embedding telemedicine into national health systems requires alignment with health policy, regulatory standards, and financing mechanisms. As telehealth use stabilizes, health systems must invest in infrastructure, interoperability standards, clinician training, equity in access (especially for underserved populations), and oversight mechanisms to ensure quality and patient safety [53]. Only by embedding telemedicine within the national health architecture, rather than treating it as a separate emergency tool, can digital health realize its full potential for sustainable, equitable health care.

This integration is particularly urgent given that current technical limitations raise concerns about patient confidentiality and cybersecurity. Therefore, strengthening legal frameworks, institutional capacity, and ICT infrastructure remains essential to harness the full potential of AI‐enhanced telemedicine [31, 34]. Ultimately, continued investment in infrastructure, policy refinement, ethical standards, and workforce training will be pivotal to sustaining and expanding telemedicine's contributions to health equity across the continent [7, 15].

Tiered Maturity of African Telemedicine

To understand the diverse nature of the continent, the current research has outlined the three different tiers of telemedicine implementation that depend on regulatory development and infrastructure [12]. First tier (Advanced Adopters), which includes South Africa and Rwanda, was successful in transitioning from emergency protocols to a national telemedicine strategy framework [9]. Second‐tier countries (Policy‐Transitional), such as Nigeria and Kenya, have relatively high mobile penetration rates along with numerous pilot projects, yet lack enforceable laws and telemedicine regulation at the national level [31]. Third‐tier (Infrastructure Limited) regions continue to experience issues related to poor network coverage and “pilotitis” when it comes to digital health care [45]. Comparative Global Perspectives

As noted in the African context, this pre‐pandemic stagnation was mirrored globally, with the majority of countries across Asia, Europe, and America, many of which lacked robust regulatory frameworks for the authorization, integration, and reimbursement of telemedicine services. For example, in Europe, only a few countries (e.g., France) had established reimbursement for telemedicine before the pandemic; in Italy, telemedicine was not included in the essential levels of care, and only a limited number of services were reimbursed.

In the US, telemedicine policies differ widely by state, with uneven reimbursement and licensing requirements, creating substantial barriers to extensive adoption. In Asia, several countries had only experimental programs or limited frameworks, often without national‐level integration or clear reimbursement pathways [14, 54, 55, 56, 57, 58]. These similarities across continents signify a lack of clear policy on telemedicine practice; limited technology before the pandemic; absence of need for virtual care; preference for physical consultations; poor internet connectivity; and low digital literacy, especially in developing countries like Africa and parts of Asia.

Reflecting these tiered disparities, the reliance on low‐barrier communication tools in Africa's lower tiers contrasts with the integrated virtual hospitals seen in Asia, where China initiated AI‐powered triage and remote monitoring, leveraging government‐supported integrated applications to power adoption. AI and IoT were used for risk assessment and patient tracking [25, 56, 59, 60].

In Europe, commercially available messaging and video‐conferencing tools were widely used for consultations and education [25, 56, 60]. Telemedicine was rapidly integrated into clinical workflows, with major Western health systems using AI‐driven screening and virtual visits in America. Social media and ubiquitous video conferencing software enabled both patient care and professional collaboration [59, 60, 61, 62, 63]. The global reliance on these ubiquitous digital tools can be attributed to the familiarity and easy access to both patients and care providers, reducing the need for any formal training at the time of crisis. Plus, the platforms enable real‐time messaging, image and video sharing, serving as a temporary but effective substitute for the system, especially in countries without dedicated telemedicine infrastructure at the onset of the pandemic.

Consistent with the rapid regulatory shifts seen in South Africa, the pandemic sparked swift government action in parts of Asia, Europe, and the US, leading to permanent or temporary policy changes that enabled far‐reaching telemedicine usage. Conversely, the fragmentation observed in Nigeria mirrored policy gaps in Italy, where the absence of unified frameworks hindered integration. In Asia, countries like China, Thailand, and India leveraged high mobile penetration to scale telemedicine, though rural‐urban inconsistencies persisted. Like the successful pivots in Tier‐1 African nations, France and the UK rapidly expanded telemedicine reimbursement and access to ensure service continuity [14, 54, 56, 58, 64, 65, 66, 67].

Building on the technological advances seen in Africa, the COVID‐19 pandemic prompted rapid technological innovation across continents, fundamentally reshaping healthcare, logistics, and communication. This led to the extensive global use of AI for contact tracing, outbreak prediction, and rapid diagnosis. In Asia (notably China and India), as well as the US and Europe, telemedicine platforms integrated with AI‐powered imaging and remote monitoring were rapidly adopted to support COVID‐19 triage, chronic disease follow‐up, and mental health services [14, 25, 59, 61, 64, 66, 67, 68, 69, 70, 71]. These innovations improved diagnosis speed while reducing healthcare worker exposure [59, 72, 73, 74]. Robots and drones were reported to be used for disinfection, delivery, crowd monitoring, and even patient care, with significant deployments in Asia (notably China and South Korea), and growing adoption in Europe and the US [59, 75, 76]. Mobile apps and big data analytics enabled real‐time tracking of cases and contacts, with Asia (China, South Korea, Singapore) leading large‐scale implementations, while Europe and the US balanced privacy concerns with public health needs [74, 75, 77, 78, 79]. Corresponding to Africa, the advancement of these technologies came with challenges mainly from data delays, data fragmentation, privacy security, and data security vulnerabilities [74].

As evidenced by the stagnation of African projects like Hello Mama and Project Kopano [46], the coordination challenges and ‘pilotitis’ identified in this review were not unique to the continent; in Asia and the US, limited collaboration among stakeholders was also noted before the COVID‐19 era [56, 80]. In Europe, Engagement was hampered by inconsistent regulations, data privacy concerns, and digital literacy gaps [54, 64, 80]. During the COVID‐19 era, governments, tech companies, and healthcare providers collaborated to expand telemedicine, especially in response to urgent needs. Public‐private partnerships and regional cooperation increased, though access disparities persisted in Asia [56]. A similar collaborative momentum was observed in the US and Europe, where Stakeholder engagement intensified, with policymakers, clinicians, and patient groups working together to accelerate telemedicine adoption. Regulatory changes and reimbursement reforms were implemented, but in Europe, post‐pandemic use declined in some countries due to cultural and systemic barriers [54, 57, 64, 80, 81].

Policy Implementation and Sustainable Strategies

To move past the coordination challenges observed in both the African context and global settings [45, 46, 56], African nations must transition small‐scale pilot projects into scalable, sustainable programs. This transition requires a shift from reactive emergency measures to institutionalized routine care by strengthening governance and establishing unified systems to monitor progress. Our review suggests that this requires a three‐pronged strategy.

First is the development of unified national telehealth frameworks [50] that include permanent reimbursement models [51] and data privacy and quality standards [50] to replace temporary pandemic‐era relaxations. Second, investment must prioritize rural digital infrastructure and internet access [50] to ensure equitable access beyond urban centers. Finally, to move past ‘pilotitis,’ governments should establish interoperability standards [53] and formal training programs [49, 53] that embed telemedicine directly into everyday clinical workflows [52]. Robust legal and regulatory frameworks are essential to ensure long‐term viability, alongside scaling successful pilots to meet growing demand. Comprehensive legal frameworks are critical to secure patient trust, ensure professional accountability, and promote telemedicine scale‐up [31, 32]. Addressing these legal complexities is essential to move from reactive, emergency‐use policies to a permanent, legally sound telehealth ecosystem [25]. Investment in reliable broadband connectivity and digital platforms, particularly real‐time communication technologies, remains a priority.

Realism in policy suggests that legal endorsement must go hand‐in‐hand with sustainable models for reimbursement [51]. While telemedicine is legally recognized in many African jurisdictions, there may be gaps in terms of national insurance coverage for teleconsultations in countries like Nigeria through its NHIS scheme [31]. Failure to integrate telehealth services into formal reimbursement frameworks makes any policy “superficial” since it does not take care of the economic feasibility for service providers [14].

Indeed, the lack of clinical protocols, as seen from the findings on chronic wounds [37] implies a clear professional malpractice risk. The lack of benchmarking standards for tele‐monitoring diabetic ulcers puts medical professionals at risk for negligence in case of any complications that could arise as a consequence of the technological constraints involved in assessing the patient remotely. Such circumstances could imply the creation of a ‘standard of care’ dilemma, making professionals accountable for potential misdiagnoses caused by the technical aspects of telecommunication [37, 82]. Furthermore, the absence of standardized protocols for obtaining informed consent in digital care remains a critical loophole. Policies must explicitly define how consent is documented and stored in virtual environments to protect both the patient's autonomy and the provider's legal standing during teleconsultations [22, 37]. The findings suggest that the success of these technological interventions in chronic care is closely linked to the resolution of underlying legal and governance challenges [31]. Moreover, reimbursement and liability continue to be significant barriers. When teleconsultation is not considered reimbursable under national health schemes such as the NHIS, professionals will be at risk of facing legal challenges concerning the ‘commercial validity’ of their medical advice [83]. Hence, linking the legal and economic aspects of the medical profession is important not only economically but also in preventing liability risks.

Financial barriers should be mitigated through sustainable funding models and expanded insurance coverage, while targeted interventions are needed to address workforce shortages, staff turnover, and language diversity. Developing context‐appropriate telehealth solutions tailored to local needs will further enhance effectiveness.

Consequently, the literature identifies the integration of psychological and socio‐behavioral factors into telemedicine frameworks as a key component of patient‐oriented intervention strategies [14, 39]. Strengthening digital readiness will depend on partnerships with telecommunications providers to leverage widespread mobile phone use and expand service reach. Governments should promote the dissemination of health information through digital channels and implement clear governance structures for data protection, performance monitoring, and strategic planning. Regulatory oversight must ensure privacy, confidentiality, equity, and accountability, while subsidies or other support mechanisms can improve affordability for users and providers. Sustained progress also requires multisectoral collaboration.

Governments play a central role in policy, regulation, and financing; private sector partners drive technological innovation and infrastructure development; academic institutions generate evidence and train the workforce; and civil society and international organizations support awareness, capacity building, and resource mobilization. These collaborations can drive innovation, improve infrastructure, and support the sustainable expansion of digital health nationwide [47]. Together, these measures can enhance the sustainability, equity, and resilience of telehealth systems, positioning digital health as a core component of stronger and more crisis‐responsive health systems across Africa.

Study Limitations

This review has several limitations. Much of the available evidence on telehealth in Africa is based on short‐term pilot projects, limiting insights into long‐term sustainability. Variability in study design, data quality, and the lack of standardized outcome measures also affect the comparability and reliability of findings, making it difficult to assess effectiveness and cost‐efficiency. The literature is geographically concentrated in a few countries, particularly South Africa, Kenya, and Nigeria, reducing generalizability to settings with weaker digital infrastructure. In addition, the rapid evolution of telehealth policies and technologies means some evidence may quickly become outdated. Limited attention to sociocultural and language factors, along with reliance on published and secondary sources, may have resulted in the omission of relevant gray or unpublished data. Despite these constraints, the review provides a useful synthesis of key structural and policy issues shaping telehealth adoption across Africa.

Conclusion

Telemedicine in Africa has evolved from fragmented and often donor‐supported pilot initiatives into an increasingly important component of healthcare delivery systems. Before COVID‐19, adoption was constrained by persistent infrastructural, financial, and regulatory barriers, limiting scalability despite promising early initiatives. The pandemic served as a catalyst, accelerating the uptake of mobile health platforms, digital tools, and more flexible regulatory approaches, and demonstrating telemedicine's value in maintaining service continuity during crises. Despite this progress, challenges, including weak digital infrastructure, fragmented governance, data privacy concerns, and reliance on short‐term funding, continue to hinder sustainable integration. Moving forward, institutionalizing telemedicine within national health strategies will be essential. This includes strengthening regulatory and reimbursement frameworks, investing in connectivity and workforce capacity, and promoting regional collaboration to reduce disparities. Ultimately, telemedicine represents not only a technological innovation but a pathway toward more equitable, resilient, and accessible healthcare systems across Africa. Sustaining the momentum generated during the pandemic will be critical to translating short‐term gains into lasting health system transformation.
