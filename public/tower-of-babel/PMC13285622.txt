Bronchoscopy guided resection of endobronchial mucormycosis in a renal transplant patient with chronic graft rejection: A non-surgical approach using bronchoscopy guided intervention as salvage therapy
Authors: Sazia Afrin, Fazle Rabbi Mohammed, Mashrura Haq
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPulmonary mucormycosis is a rare but rapidly progressive fungal infection commonly associated with diabetes mellitus, hematologic malignancies, and solid-organ transplants. Prompt diagnosis is essential given the infection's invasive nature and the likelihood of dissemination. The standard treatment typically involves surgical resection and systemic antifungal therapy, specifically liposomal amphotericin B, along with correction of the underlying risk factors.We present a 53-year-old male with diabetes, hypertension, end-stage renal disease (ESRD) on maintenance hemodialysis (MHD) and chronic graft rejection following renal transplantation who presented with progressive respiratory symptoms. Initially treated as community-acquired pneumonia, his clinical condition progressively worsened despite appropriate antimicrobial therapy. Subsequent fibre-optic bronchoscopy (FOB) revealed a friable endobronchial mass with a spongiform appearance completely occluding the left mainstem bronchus and extending into the segmental bronchi, including upper, middle, and lower lobes. Complete resection of the endobronchial lesion was achieved using bronchoscopy-guided techniques, including cryoablation, argon plasma coagulation, and snare-forceps excision, thereby avoiding high-risk surgical intervention. Histopathological examination was consistent with invasive pulmonary mucormycosis. Following bronchoscopy, treatment with liposomal amphotericin B and oral posaconazole for parenchymal disease resulted in significant clinical and radiological improvement.This case highlights the promising role of advanced bronchoscopy-guided interventions as an adjunct to both diagnostic and therapeutic modalities in high-risk cases of pulmonary mucormycosis with an endobronchial component, particularly when conventional surgical approaches are not feasible due to life-threatening post-surgical complications and comorbidities.Keywords: Chronic graft rejection, Renal transplantation, Fibre-optic bronchoscopy, Cryoablation, Argon plasma coagulation, Snare forceps

Introduction

Mucormycosis is an uncommon but highly aggressive fungal infection characterized by its tendency to invade blood vessels and potential for rapid progression [1]. The risk of developing mucormycosis is significantly higher in individuals with poorly controlled diabetes and in those with impaired immune function, including patients who have undergone solid organ transplantation, those with hematological cancers, autoimmune conditions, and individuals affected by COVID-19 [2,3] (see Fig. 1, Fig. 2, Fig. 3, Fig. 4, Fig. 5, Fig. 6, Fig. 7, Fig. 8, Fig. 9).

Among individuals who have undergone renal transplantation, mucormycosis is a rare complication, occurring in approximately 0.4–0.5 per 1000 patients, with pulmonary involvement representing the second most frequent site of infection after the transplanted organ [4,5].

Inhalation of spores into the bronchioles and alveoli leads to the development of pulmonary mucormycosis (PM), which presents with nonspecific clinical features that are not easily distinguished from those of other pulmonary infections. It is generally classified into two clinical forms-parenchymal and endobronchial [6]. The more prevalent parenchymal disease has a rapidly progressive clinical course, and only a few cases of endobronchial mucormycosis have been reported, mainly involving the large airways, with stenosis, erythematous mucosa, and airway obstruction on bronchoscopy [6].

Treatment involves a combination of systemic antifungal therapy-most commonly intravenous liposomal amphotericin B, along with surgical removal of necrotic tissue and optimization of underlying predisposing factors [7]. Minimally invasive interventions like fibre-optic bronchoscopy guided cryoablation, and argon plasma coagulation have been increasingly playing a promising role in the management of endobronchial mucormycosis.

This report presents a unique case of a 53-year-old male with diabetes and hypertension who had undergone renal transplantation and later developed chronic graft rejection. He was diagnosed with invasive pulmonary mucormycosis after being presented with dry cough and dyspnea. The endobronchial component of mucor was successfully resected using a bronchoscopy-guided approach (cryoablation, argon plasma coagulation, and snare-forceps excision) as an alternative to surgery, while managing the parenchymal disease with systemic antifungal therapy. The unique non-surgical management led to an uncomplicated recovery, allowing the patient to resume daily activities quickly. This approach is a step up from the conventional complex surgical approach.

Case presentation

A 53-year-old hypertensive, diabetic man was admitted to our hospital with the complaints of shortness of breath and dry cough for about a month. He also has anorexia followed by vomiting for the same duration. He is a known case of ESRD on maintenance hemodialysis (MHD), chronic graft rejection of transplanted kidney, type 2 diabetes mellitus, essential hypertension, ischemic heart disease, dyslipidemia, and asthma. The physical examination was unrevealing, and relevant investigations were conducted.

His initial full blood count revealed hemoglobin: 11.4 g/dl, white blood count: 15.3 K/μL, platelet count: 93 k/ul, and renal function test revealed sodium: 125 mmol/L, potassium: 5.5 mmol/L, urea: 257mg/dl, and creatinine: 5.4 mg/dl. His initial C-reactive protein, N-terminal pro-B-type natriuretic peptide (NT-proBNP), and troponin-I were 222.5 mg/L, 16462 pg/ml, and 0.276 ng/ml, respectively. High-resolution computed tomography (HRCT) scan of the chest revealed multiple cavitating consolidations in the right upper and both lower lobes, larger ones in the lower lobes, and centrilobular nodules in the right middle, left upper, and both lower lobes, suggesting endobronchial spread. Chest x-ray revealed diffuse dense opacities throughout the left lung.

His echocardiogram was normal except for elevated pulmonary artery systolic pressure (PASP)- 55 mmHg. Serological analysis showed negative results for both perinuclear antineutrophil cytoplasmic antibody (p-ANCA) and cytoplasmic antineutrophil cytoplasmic antibody (c-ANCA). Phosphorus (Po4), albumin, calcium, uric acid, and magnesium levels were 3.8 mg/dl, 2.1 g/dl, 6.5 mg/dl, 5.8 mg/dl, and 1.6 mg/dl, respectively.

Urine culture and sensitivity showed Enterococcus faecalis, sputum culture and sensitivity showed Acinetobacter spp. Moderate growth, and blood culture and sensitivity showed no growth. He was diagnosed with urinary tract infection, community-acquired pneumonia, electrolyte imbalance, and metabolic acidosis. He was managed conservatively with intravenous antibiotics according to culture and sensitivity.

Despite intravenous diuretics and strict fluid management, he developed vasodilatory shock and acute kidney injury requiring vasopressors, and hemodialysis via arteriovenous (AV) fistula with multiple transfusions of packed red blood cells. He was transferred to critical care due to a rapid fall in blood pressure (70/40 mmHg) and increased oxygen demand (SpO2 90% with 15L/min oxygen via non-rebreather mask). Repeat sputum culture and sensitivity revealed coagulase-negative staphylococcus spp., profuse growth, and candida spp. Profuse growth, and urine culture and sensitivity showed no growth. Mantoux (Tuberculin) test was negative.

In the following days he developed hemoptysis followed by cough and persistent dyspnea. Repeated HRCT chest revealed multiple irregular cavitating lesions in both lungs (right and left) - lung abscesses, volume reduction of left hemithorax with hyperinflated right lung, centrilobular nodules in right middle, lower lobes, suggesting endobronchial spread, and left sided mild pleural effusion with atelectasis.

When oxygen demand decreased to 2-3 L/min, flexible bronchoscopy (FOB) was performed using a mouth guard under local anesthesia (Lidocaine Hydrochloride) with light sedation (titrated dose of short acting benzodiazepine to ensure patient comfort, reduce anxiety and suppress cough reflex) to further explore the underlying cause behind his worsening symptoms. Ventilation was not required in the procedure, allowing the intervention to be performed using argon plasma coagulation (APC). On advancing the bronchoscope into the airway, an obstructing endobronchial lesion was visualized at the lower end of the trachea, involving the left mainstem bronchus with extension into the left upper, middle, and lower lobe bronchus. Fibre-optic bronchoscopy guided argon plasma coagulation, and cryoablation were used to remove the obstructing lesion. Argon plasma coagulation was initially performed, but later, cryoablation was used alongside it to clear the obstructing lesion from the lower end of the trachea to the left mainstem bronchus. Finally, snare forceps was used to scoop out the remainder of the lesion from the lobar bronchus.

During argon plasma coagulation (APC), supplemental oxygen was withheld to minimize the risk of endobronchial (airway) fire. Intermittent oxygen supplementation at 2–3 L/min via nasal cannula was administered between APC applications for brief periods to maintain adequate oxygen saturation. During cryoablation and snare forceps resection, supplemental oxygen was continuously administered via nasal cannula to maintain satisfactory oxygen saturation throughout the procedure.

A biopsy was taken during the procedure, and the report was consistent with invasive fungal infection- Mucormycosis. Post-intervention, 100% luminal patency of the left mainstem bronchus and left lobar bronchus was successfully re-established. Following bronchoscopy-guided resection of the lesion, he was treated with a total of 5 doses of intravenous liposomal amphotericin B (5mg/kg) post-dialysis and one month of oral posaconazole 200mg twice daily to treat parenchymal disease. Post-treatment chest x-ray showed complete resolution of the left lung, and he had stable vital signs with no oxygen demand and normal lab parameters.

Although surgery is a fundamental treatment for pulmonary mucormycosis, for this patient, surgical management was high-risk owing to immunosuppression, multiple co-morbidities, and dialysis dependency. With the help of advanced bronchoscopy-guided intervention, the endobronchial component of mucormycosis was resected without any complex surgical intervention, and systemic anti-fungal therapy was given to manage the parenchymal involvement of mucormycosis.

Discussion

Early identification of pulmonary mucormycosis might be challenging due to non-specific clinical signs and symptoms, similarity with prevalent bacterial infections, rapid progression, rarity, and virulent nature of the infection. Many patients have a secondary bacterial infection [4] as pulmonary mucormycosis is associated with bacterial pneumonia in some cases. As a result, mortality increases owing to late diagnosis and treatment [8].

Sputum samples are inconclusive for diagnosis [9], and radiographic manifestations are often nonspecific. However, in comparison to a standard chest radiograph, a high-resolution computed tomography scan of the chest can determine the extent of mucormycosis infection earlier [8]. The gold standard for diagnosis is histological examination of a tissue specimen via bronchoscopy [9] when feasible. The reason bronchoscopy is the preferred diagnostic method is that it is relatively safe and minimally invasive [10].

In short, pulmonary mucormycosis is treated using effective antifungal therapy (Intravenous liposomal amphotericin B is the current gold standard), surgical resection of the involved areas of the lung, along with modification of risk factors [9]. Despite the nephrotoxic effects of Liposomal Amphotericin B (5-10 mg/kg/day) [11], it is the medication of choice for treating Mucorales spp. Furthermore, all currently available azole antifungal agents have less effect against mucormycosis; nevertheless, in patients with preexisting renal compromise, itraconazole, isavuconazole, and oral posaconazole are used as an alternative [11] (isavuconazonium has been linked with significantly lower incidence of renal injury) [4].

Our case is comparable to the findings of Zeqin Fan et al., who highlighted the emerging role of minimally invasive bronchoscopic interventions in patients with tracheobronchial or endobronchial mucormycosis in high-risk surgical candidates. Immunosuppression is a recognized predisposing factor for invasive mucormycosis. Initial presentation of tracheobronchial or endobronchial mucormycosis may mimic severe bacterial pneumonia, leading to delayed diagnosis despite appropriate antimicrobial therapy, as demonstrated in both our case and the cohort study. Despite appropriate antimicrobial coverage, a bronchoscopic evaluation was prompted due to persistent respiratory deterioration, hemoptysis, and progressive airway compromise of our patient, which ultimately established the diagnosis through histopathological confirmation.

While surgical intervention is frequently considered a cornerstone in the management of pulmonary mucormycosis, surgical management was high-risk for our patient owing to dialysis dependency, severe renal dysfunction, chronic graft rejection, anemia requiring repeated transfusions, and critical illness requiring intensive care support.

Fibre-optic bronchoscopy-guided interventions (cryoablation, argon plasma coagulation, and snare forceps resection) were used for both diagnostic and therapeutic purposes in the retrospective cohort study and in our patient, enabling the successful removal of the obstructive endobronchial lesion without the need for thoracic surgery, with acceptable procedural safety. Additionally, systemic antifungal therapy was administered to manage the parenchymal involvement of mucormycosis.

The favorable clinical and radiological response observed in our patient further highlights the potential utility of interventional bronchoscopy as an adjunct in the management of invasive endobronchial fungal disease in carefully selected patients who are poor surgical candidates.

Conclusions

In this case, endobronchial mucormycosis was treated with fibre-optic bronchoscopy-directed interventions, including cryoablation, argon plasma coagulation, and debridement using snare forceps. At the same time, parenchymal involvement was managed with systemic anti-fungal therapy.

Fibre-optic bronchoscopy is an advanced method used in the management of pulmonary mucormycosis due to its minimally invasive nature, which allows for early diagnosis, targeted sampling, and, in selected cases like this, therapeutic intervention such as removal of the obstructive endobronchial lesion. This case is a unique example of how bronchoscopy-guided interventions can serve as an adjunct in the management of pulmonary mucormycosis without the need for extensive thoracic surgery. Further studies are required to define its long-term clinical outcomes.
