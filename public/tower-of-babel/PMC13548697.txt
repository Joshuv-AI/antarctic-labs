Preference-based measures in people with dementia: a systematic review of psychometric properties
Authors: Anouk Lamé, Jan Heijdra Suasnabar, Esmee Adam, Ron Handels, Hanneke J. A. Smaling, Miriam L. Haaksma, M. Elske van den Akker-van Marle
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeAccurate measurement of health and well-being outcomes is essential for economic evaluations of dementia care interventions. However, the suitability of existing preference-based measures (PBMs) for people with dementia remains uncertain. This review aims to provide guidance in measurement choice by assessing the psychometric properties of PBMs in people with dementia.MethodsA systematic review was conducted of studies indexed in PubMed, Embase, PsycINFO and Web of Science up to July 2025. Studies were eligible if they evaluated psychometric properties of PBMs in people with dementia. Title and abstract screening were performed using a validated AI-powered tool, ASReview. The quality of the included studies and psychometric properties of the PBMs were assessed using the COSMIN methodology.ResultsA total of 21,347 records were identified, of which 32 studies covering fourteen different PBMs were included. The EQ-5D-3L was used in most studies, and studies focused mainly on construct validity and reliability. Study quality was predominantly high, while most psychometric evidence was inconsistent or insufficient. The ICECAP-O and HUI2 demonstrated the most appropriate psychometric evidence.ConclusionEvidence of psychometric properties of PBMs is insufficient for some measures and limited for other measures. Therefore, none can yet be recommended as fully appropriate for use in people with dementia. This underscores the need for further validation studies to improve preference-based outcome measurement, and to strengthen economic evaluations of dementia care interventions.Supplementary informationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12955-026-02563-3.

Introduction

Dementia is a global health challenge, with substantial economic burden and impact on the quality of life (QoL), both for people living with the disease and their caregivers [1–3]. This impact on QoL, combined with high care costs and shortage of trained staff, places strain on healthcare systems [4]. Economic evaluations that incorporate QoL can inform resource allocation decisions in dementia care [5].

In economic evaluations, intervention effectiveness is traditionally reflected by health-related quality of life (HR-QoL). HR-QoL is a multidimensional concept that encompasses an individual’s physical, social and psychological health and well-being [6]. Measures of HR-QoL can be used to convert health states into a single index value between 0 (equal to death) and 1 (equal to perfect health), or values below 0 reflecting a state considered worse than death [7]. These values are derived from the general population’s preferences, and are therefore also known as preference-based measures (PBMs). Lately, more PBMs on constructs related to HR-QoL were developed for use in economic evaluations, such as capability, well-being, and Social Care related QoL [8–10]. PBMs can be completed through self- or proxy-report. In the context of dementia, proxy-reports from caregivers are often used, particularly as cognitive decline progresses and self-report becomes less feasible [11].

A wide range of PBMs is available for economic evaluations of dementia care interventions, including different generic, population-specific, and disease-specific measures [12]. The choice of measure is crucial, as it may influence reported outcomes and, consequently, decision-making [12–15]. An important element in measurement choice is evaluating the psychometric properties (reliability, validity and responsiveness) of the measures [16]. However, previous reviews comparing PBMs in dementia reported inconsistent findings on their psychometric properties, and no single PBM performed best across different contexts or dementia severity levels [17–20]. There is yet no consensus on the most suitable measure(s) for people with dementia, which highlights the need for guidance on PBMs that are appropriate to use in dementia populations.

This systematic review aims to assess the psychometric properties of PBMs in people with dementia. The review’s outcomes can help researchers and policymakers in choosing appropriate PBMs for economic evaluations of dementia care interventions.

Methods

Search strategy

An electronic database search was conducted in PubMed, EMBASE, PsycINFO and Web of Science in July 2025. The search strategy was developed in consultation with a professional librarian, using keywords and MeSH terms focused on dementia, QoL, PBMs, and psychometric properties. The full search strategy for each database can be found in more detail [see Additional File 1 and 2]. Articles were included if they described: 1) adults living with dementia in the community and/or in residential care, 2) preference-based measures (self- and/or proxy-reported), and 3) one or more psychometric properties of PBMs. Reviews, commentaries, protocols, editorials, dissertations, abstracts, and conference proceedings were excluded. This systematic review followed a protocol registered in PROSPERO (Record ID: CRD42024608651; 14 November 2024). It was conducted largely in accordance with the protocol, with one minor deviation: forward and backward citation searching was not conducted as originally planned.

Title and abstract screening with ASReview

The searches in multiple databases were combined in one dataset that was used for screening the records. The AI-powered tool ASReview was used for title and abstract screening (version 1.6.2) [21]. Deduplication of records was also performed with ASReview, prior to screening. A slightly adapted version of the SAFE procedure (Screen random set, Active learning, Find more with deep learning, Evaluate quality) was followed for title and abstract screening by using one relevant key article and one random irrelevant article for training ASReview [22, 23].

One researcher (AL) executed title and abstract screening, with predetermined stopping rules based on previous studies [22, 23]: 1) all (five) predetermined key papers have been marked as relevant, 2) a minimum of 7.7% of the total dataset has been screened, and 3) no relevant records have been identified in the last 50 records. Additionally, to evaluate screening quality, all irrelevant records were checked by a researcher (MEAM) using ASReview to identify manually excluded but possibly relevant records, until the stopping rule was met [23]. Unseen records were screened with a neural network model to check for possibly relevant records that were missed by ASReview.

Full text screening and data extraction

Two researchers (AL and EA) manually determined studies for final inclusion through full text screening using a predetermined eligibility form in Microsoft Excel. The first ten articles were independently screened by the two researchers. During a consensus meeting, researchers agreed on eligibility of these articles. The remaining articles were divided equally between the researchers for screening. One researcher (AL) extracted study data on study design, setting, participant demographics, and measures used in each study.

COSMIN methodology for quality assessment and evaluation of psychometric properties

To evaluate the methodological quality of studies and psychometric properties, the Consensus-based Standards for the selection of Measurement Instruments (COSMIN) manual version 2.0 (Conducting a systematic review of Patient-Reported Outcome Measures) was used [16, 24]. This methodology is developed by an international expert-panel for evaluating the methodological quality of studies that examine psychometric properties of patient-reported outcome measures.

The methodological quality of the included studies was assessed with the COSMIN Risk of Bias checklist [24]. Two researchers (AL and JHS or EA) independently reviewed the quality of the included studies. Disagreements between them were presented to a third researcher (MEAM) for discussion. The quality of a study (very good, adequate, doubtful, inadequate) affected the grading of the evidence (high, moderate, low, very low).

The psychometric properties were evaluated with the COSMIN criteria for good measurement properties [24]. This concerns an evaluation of: content validity, structural validity, internal consistency, cross-cultural validity, reliability, measurement error, criterion validity, construct validity, and responsiveness. The definitions of these properties can be found in more detail [see Additional File 3]. For each property and measure, two researchers (AL and JHS or EA) rated study results as sufficient (+), insufficient (-), inconsistent (±), or indeterminate (?) based on COSMIN criteria [24]. For assessing construct validity and responsiveness, predefined hypotheses were formulated corresponding to the COSMIN guideline [see Additional File 4]. These hypotheses included expected high correlations between measures assessing similar constructs, and moderate correlations between measures assessing related but distinct constructs. For known-groups validity, we expected differences between clinically relevant subgroups.

The overall evidence for each psychometric property and measure was summarized by two researchers (AL and JHS or EA). The overall rating for each property was determined as sufficient (+), insufficient (-), inconsistent (±), or indeterminate (?) based on the direction and consistency of results across studies. The quality of the overall evidence was rated as high, moderate, low, or very low using the COSMIN-modified GRADE approach, taking into account risk of bias (methodological quality of the studies), inconsistency of results, and imprecision (sample size) [24].

Results

Screening process and inclusion of studies

The search and screening process is documented in a flow chart (Fig. 1). After removing duplicates, 21,347 records were identified for title and abstract screening, of which 1,642 (7.7%) were manually screened until stopping criteria were met. Remaining articles were excluded by ASReview, but the neural network model identified two possibly relevant records. Study characteristics are presented in Table 2. Most studies were conducted in Europe (n = 19), Northern America (n = 6), or Australia (n = 5). One study was conducted in both Europe and Northern America [50], and one in Mexico [59]. The majority included community-dwelling people with dementia (n = 20), with fewer focusing on nursing home residents (n = 10). Two studies included both populations [37, 43]. Most studies included mild and/or moderate dementia (n = 15), while fewer studies also included severe dementia (n = 13). Review of quality of studies and psychometric properties

The methodological quality of individual studies and ratings of psychometric properties can be found in the COSMIN worksheets in more detail [see Additional File 5 and 6]. The study quality was rated as high in more than half of the cases (59%). However, only 27% of all psychometric evidence was rated as sufficient based on COSMIN criteria [24]. Although most studies were methodologically well conducted, the psychometric evidence did not meet the predefined thresholds for sufficiency. EQ-5D-3L and EQ-5D-5L

Three studies evaluated the content validity (i.e., relevance, comprehensiveness, and comprehensibility) of the EQ-5D measures. One found that the EQ-5D-3L lacked on comprehensiveness, omitting key concepts in the context of dementia, such as cognition, emotional and physical well-being [63], which led to an insufficient (low-quality evidence) rating. Another study identified response issues for the EQ-5D-5L, particularly in capturing fluctuating health states and mobility aid use within the ‘today’ timeframe [51]. The EQ-5D-5L was rated as insufficient (high-quality evidence) on comprehensibility. For content validity of the EQ-5D-5L, one study reported that while it captures key concepts, it was perceived more as ‘an objective measure of health’ rather than a reflection of QoL [41]. Overall, the content validity of the EQ-5D-5L was determined inconsistent (moderate-quality evidence).

One study assessed the structural validity of the EQ-5D-3L and identified a two-factor solution, comprising a functional and subjective factor [40]. This was rated as indeterminate (moderate-quality evidence) due to evidence of a lack of unidimensionality. Internal consistency of the EQ-5D-5L differed between self- and proxy-rated versions (Cronbach’s α = 0.60–0.74) [36, 42, 64]. This was rated as indeterminate (moderate-quality evidence) due to limited reporting of dimensionality assessment. Similarly for the internal consistency of the EQ-5D-3L (Cronbach’s α = 0.64–0.98), this was rated as indeterminate (moderate-quality evidence) [40, 53, 59].

For the EQ-5D-3L, reliability was inconsistent (low-quality evidence), as the included studies reported both sufficient and insufficient inter-rater reliability [37, 39, 40, 49, 58], and test-retest reliability [37, 45, 46, 56]. Inter-rater reliability of the EQ-5D-5L was insufficient (low-quality evidence) when comparing self- and proxy-ratings [36, 42, 43].

Criterion validity of the EQ-5D-5L was insufficient (high-quality evidence) [12]. For both measures, construct validity was insufficient (high-quality evidence), with 35% of results supporting the hypotheses for the EQ-5D-5L (n = 81 hypotheses), and 50% for the EQ-5D-3L (n = 115) [12, 13, 36, 37, 40, 42, 45, 46, 49, 53–56, 59]. Responsiveness was also insufficient (high-quality evidence), with respectively 28% of results supporting hypotheses for the EQ-5D-5L (n = 80), and 30% for the EQ-5D-3L (n = 10) [43, 49].

HUI2 and HUI3

One study found that both the HUI2 and HUI3 lacked comprehensiveness and missed key concepts important to people with dementia, such as social/family well-being and cognition [63]. This resulted in an insufficient (low-quality evidence) rating of content validity. Test-retest reliability of HUI2 was insufficient (low-quality evidence) for self- and proxy-ratings [45, 46]. The HUI3 showed insufficient test-retest reliability for self-rated (ICC = 0.6), but sufficient for proxy-rated (ICC = 0.8) [56], which led to an overall inconsistent (moderate-quality evidence) rating of reliability.

The HUI2 showed sufficient (high-quality evidence) construct validity, with 80% of results confirming hypotheses (n = 15) [45–47], while the HUI3 was insufficient (high-quality evidence), with 25% of hypotheses confirmed (n = 36) [47, 56]. Similarly, responsiveness was sufficient (high-quality evidence) for HUI2 with 78% of results were in line with hypotheses (n = 9), while this was insufficient (high-quality evidence) for HUI3 with 67% of results consistent with hypotheses (n = 42) [47, 50].

QWB

The QWB captured some relevant aspects on physical and functional well-being, but omitted other aspects on cognition and emotional well-being [63], which resulted in an insufficient (low-quality evidence) rating of content validity. Test-retest reliability was sufficient (moderate-quality evidence) for both self- and proxy-rated versions (ICC = 0.7) [56]. Construct validity was insufficient (high-quality evidence), with 23% of results consistent with hypotheses (n = 35) [48, 56].

AQoL-8D

One study found that the AQoL-8D captured many aspects important to people with dementia, but included one irrelevant item (hurting yourself) and omitted other relevant domains, such as being bored [41]. This resulted in an inconsistent (moderate-quality evidence) rating of content validity.

QoL-ACC

Nursing home residents with dementia struggled with comprehension, recall and response mapping of the QoL-ACC [52]. Especially incorporating the use of mobility aids and fluctuating health states in the ‘today’ timeframe was seen as difficult. This resulted in an insufficient (low-quality evidence) rating of content validity. Inter-rater reliability was rated as insufficient, but it should be noted that this was based on very low quality evidence, because there appeared to be a systematic change between measurements [44].

ICECAP-O

One study reported that the ICECAP-O included relevant questions to people with dementia, but missed many relevant aspects such as emotions, and some issues with the response options were noted [41]. Therefore, content validity was rated as inconsistent (moderate-quality evidence). Structural validity showed good model fit (CFI = 0.96) for a one factor solution [57], but was rated as indeterminate (moderate-quality evidence) due to limited reporting of dimensionality assessment. A principal component analysis with five factors explaining 67% of the variance was reported one study [60]. However, we deemed the methods underreported and inadequate, and therefore disregarded this study. Internal consistency was also evaluated (Cronbach’s α = 0.81–0.82), and this was rated as indeterminate (moderate-quality evidence) due to limited reporting [60, 64].

Test-retest reliability was insufficient (moderate-quality evidence) [38]. Construct validity was sufficient (high-quality evidence), with 100% of the results consistent with hypotheses (n = 18) [15, 57, 60, 64]. Also, responsiveness was sufficient based on one high quality study evaluating one hypothesis, and the results were as expected [57].

ASCOT-SCT4 and ASCOT-Proxy

The ASCOT-SCT4 included irrelevant items according to people with dementia, and some response issues were noted, but no key aspects were perceived as missing [41]. This led to an inconsistent (moderate-quality evidence) rating of content validity. Structural validity of the ASCOT-Proxy showed a one or two factor solution, depending on the proxy perspective used [62]. However, limited reporting of dimensionality assessment led to an indeterminate (moderate-quality evidence) rating of structural validity. Similarly, internal consistency of ASCOT-Proxy was rated as indeterminate (moderate-quality evidence) due to limited reporting [62, 64]. However, construct validity of ASCOT-Proxy was sufficient (high-quality evidence), with 100% of results consistent with hypotheses (n = 18) [62, 64].

DEMQOL-U and DEMQOL-Proxy-U

The DEMQOL-U showed generally good relevance, with some doubts about the memory-related items [41]. Overall, the questions were seen as generic and lacked some key aspects important to people with dementia. This led to an inconsistent (moderate-quality evidence) rating of content validity for the DEMQOL-U. Criterion validity of DEMQOL-Proxy-U was insufficient (high-quality evidence) [12]. Construct validity of both measures was insufficient (high-quality evidence), with none of the results consistent with hypotheses (n = 6; n = 9) [12, 13].

AD-5D

The AD-5D included many key aspects of quality of life in people with dementia, and items were understood as intended [41]. However, it was unclear if all items were relevant and had appropriate response options. This led to an inconsistent (moderate-quality evidence) rating of content validity.

DQI

The DQI showed insufficient (moderate-quality evidence) inter-rater reliability, and insufficient (high-quality evidence) construct validity, with none of the results consistent with hypotheses (n = 2) [61].

Discussion

This systematic review assessed the psychometric properties of fourteen PBMs in people with dementia. Overall, the psychometric properties of PBMs in people with dementia have not been extensively studied. Studies examining these properties were predominantly of high quality, but frequently resulted in insufficient evidence when COSMIN criteria were applied. The ICECAP-O demonstrated sufficient evidence across the most psychometric domains, followed by the HUI2, particularly for construct validity and responsiveness. All included PBMs require more rigorous psychometric testing before recommendations for their use in people with dementia can be made.

Challenges with COSMIN criteria

We used the COSMIN guideline to ensure transparency and consistency in our assessment of methodological quality and psychometric properties. Alternative frameworks, such as the minimum standards for patient-reported outcome measures developed by the International Society for Quality of Life Research (ISOQOL) [65] or the checklist by Brazier and Deverill [66] for judging PBMs, can provide guidance but lack standard scoring criteria. Although COSMIN provides comprehensive and detailed criteria, applying it to PBMs in dementia populations presented several challenges.

First, some COSMIN criteria appeared to be strict or less appropriate given our study’s focus on dementia and PBMs. For instance, a low quality assessment was given for short test-retest intervals [37], even though this design choice may be appropriate in this population to minimize the influence of clinical progression [67]. Similarly, inter-rater agreement between self- and proxy-ratings was often rated as insufficient. However, we question to what extent this reflects poor measurement reliability or overly strict criteria when applied to dementia studies. Indeed, agreement between self- and proxy-ratings in dementia is often (and expectedly) below COSMIN’s 0.7 threshold due the vastly different perspectives of respondents [68–71].

A second challenge relates to the conceptual model of PBMs, which are often multi-dimensional and based on a formative model. In a formative model, items represent distinct components that together define the construct. For example, the EQ-5D is based on a formative model, because each item reflects a separate dimension of HR-QoL [26]. The COSMIN guideline explicitly recommends not assessing structural validity and internal consistency for multi-dimensional measures based on a formative model [24]. Despite this, we identified and included several studies on structural validity and internal consistency [36, 40, 42, 53, 57, 59, 60, 62, 64], which we had to assign indeterminate ratings according to COSMIN criteria. This exemplifies an important mismatch: COSMIN was primarily developed for reflective, unidimensional measures (with multiple items per dimension), while PBMs (i.e., formative and multi-dimensional, with usually one item per dimension), were not central in its development.

To avoid systematically undervaluing the properties of PBMs, future research should develop specific psychometric evaluation criteria of properties that are relevant for formative, multi-dimensional measures. For example, structural validity might be assessed by examining whether the dimensions of a PBM capture empirically distinct aspects of QoL, rather than reflecting a single underlying concept (i.e. unidimensional) [72]. Moreover, the methodological quality of the value set used to derive utility values might also influence psychometric performance. Therefore, future criteria for evaluating psychometric properties of PBMs should ideally include criteria for the descriptive system (dimension or level sum scores), criteria to assess the quality of value sets, and guidance on which psychometric properties are appropriately assessed using level sum scores versus utility values.

Choosing appropriate preference-based measures

The following considerations can support the selection of appropriate PBMs for economic evaluations of dementia care interventions: 1) determining the construct of interest, 2) choosing between generic and specific measures, and 3) using current psychometric evidence.

The first consideration is determining the construct of study to identify relevant measures. The measures included in this study capture different constructs: HR-QoL (e.g. EQ-5D), Social Care related QoL (ASCOT), QoL and well-being (QWB), and capability well-being (ICECAP-O). Although these measures can all be used in economic evaluations with index scores attached to each health or well-being state, their outcome is different. For example, the ICECAP-O scores range from no capability to full capability, and is used to calculate capability-adjusted life years [73]. Also, the measures produce different outcomes when completed by the same person [7]. This highlights that the construct of interest and the choice of PBM influence outcomes.

The second consideration is choosing between using generic or specific measures, or both. The generic measures were more often evaluated but showed insufficient psychometric properties. This is in line with studies suggesting that exclusive use of generic measures might fail to capture relevant aspects of QoL in people with dementia [74, 75]. The ICECAP-O, which targets older populations, appeared more suitable for use in people with dementia in this review, due to sufficient evidence across most psychometric properties.

However, generic measures such as the EQ-5D have shown acceptable psychometric properties in other populations, such as older adults and those with other chronic diseases [76–79]. Practical considerations may therefore justify the continued use of generic measures such as the EQ-5D in dementia, including feasibility, cross-cultural validity, its comparability across diseases, and widespread use in economic evaluations [17–20]. Furthermore, the addition of a cognition bolt-on has been proposed to improve the EQ-5D’s sensitivity to cognitive impairment, although the lack of value sets currently limits the applicability in economic evaluations [80]. The balance between relevance to the target population and comparability across diseases is a known challenge in health economic guidelines and policy-decisions [81, 82].

The third consideration for selecting appropriate PBMs for people with dementia is the assessment of current psychometric evidence. The quality and rating of evidence (Table 3 and 4) provide insight in whether these measures are appropriate for this population, and if further validation is warranted. For example, when psychometric evidence of a measure is either lacking or of low quality, this measure may require more rigorous validation prior to use in people with dementia. In contrast, high quality evidence for insufficient psychometric properties suggests that these measures are less appropriate in this population, and it is questionable whether further validation is necessary. This way, the current psychometric evidence synthesized in this review can guide the choice of PBMs, which demonstrated that the ICECAP-O had sufficient evidence across most psychometric domains, followed by the HUI2.

Strengths and limitations

A strength of this review is the use of an AI-tool for time-efficient title and abstract screening whilst attaining to the methodology and predetermined stopping rules of the SAFE procedure [23]. This supported a thorough and efficient screening of existing evidence. The use of a widely accepted and robust approach for evaluating psychometric properties and quality of studies (i.e., the COSMIN methodology) is a strength. This process was conducted independently by two researchers, with consensus on all decisions to minimize bias, which strengthens the reliability of our findings.

However, some limitations should be noted. Compared with previous reviews reporting 61 or more studies [19, 20, 83], this review identified fewer eligible studies (n = 32), mainly due to differences in eligibility criteria. This review applied stricter inclusion criteria, focusing on people with dementia rather than the general older population [83], and on psychometric evaluation rather than broader PBM use and outcomes [19, 20]. Although this narrower scope reduced the number of included studies, it enabled a more focused evaluation of psychometric evidence of PBMs in people with dementia.

Other limitations of this study are that only the first ten articles were double screened. Although the researchers agreed on the eligibility of this double screened set, this might have the limited the reliability of the full text screening process. Another limitation is that the generalizability of the evidence to severe dementia might be limited because most studies focused on people with mild to moderate dementia. Also, articles evaluating psychometric properties of non-preference-based QoL measures were excluded from this review. For example, articles on the AD-5D and the DEMQOL-U were included, while articles on their full versions (QOL-AD and DEMQOL) were excluded [33, 34]. Evidence on the preference-based versions cannot be assumed to apply to their full versions. Furthermore, the strict COSMIN criteria might have resulted in conservative evaluations, potentially underestimating current evidence. Finally, this review did not evaluate feasibility of these measures and interpretability of the measures’ scores, which can be important considerations in the selection and use of measures.

Aims for future research

High quality evidence remains limited for content validity and reliability of all measures, and population- and disease-specific measures were less studied. Future research should focus on addressing this gap and evaluate the psychometric properties of the PBMs with no or low quality evidence. Also, in-depth evaluations of differences in self- and proxy-rated outcomes may further support the validation of PBMs. Future research may explore how evidence from full versions could inform interpretation of their preference-based versions. Finally, the next step is to systematically review the evidence regarding feasibility and interpretability of the measures. All of these aspects are crucial for appropriate use of PBMs in practice and policy.

Conclusion

Research into the psychometric properties of PBMs in people with dementia remains limited and available evidence is insufficient. No single measure can be recommended for use in people with dementia, although the ICECAP-O and HUI2 showed sufficient evidence on most psychometric properties. Specific recommendations for choosing appropriate measures are to be considered: 1) the construct of interest, 2) the choice between generic and specific measures, and 3) the psychometric evidence synthesized in this review. This review can guide PBM selection and inform future validation studies in people with dementia. Ultimately, this will improve the quality and relevance of economic evaluations of dementia care interventions, benefiting people with dementia, their caregivers, and the health care system.
