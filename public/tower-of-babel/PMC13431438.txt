Circulating TFH2 Cell Expansion and GZMK+ TFH1 Cells are Associated with Allergic Rhinitis
Authors: Jiejun He, Yueqi Sun, Yang Chen, Yinyan Lai, Han Lei, Jianbo Shi, Wenxiang Gao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAllergic rhinitis (AR) is a prevalent condition characterized by an immunoglobulin E (IgE)-mediated immune response. T follicular helper (TFH) cells regulate B-cell differentiation and antibody production, and circulating TFH2 cells are closely associated with type 2 humoral immunity and IgE-related responses in AR. However, whether AR is marked by isolated TFH2 expansion or broader remodeling of circulating TFH (cTFH) subsets, including TFH1-related heterogeneity, remains unclear.MethodsA total of 56 participants were enrolled, including 39 patients with AR and 17 healthy controls (HC). Flow cytometry was performed in 36 patients with AR and 14 HC to quantify cTFH subsets and activation states. Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) was conducted in an independent exploratory cohort comprising 3 patients with AR and 3 HC to characterize TFH transcriptional heterogeneity, based on canonical transcriptional programs.ResultsFlow cytometry demonstrated that cTFH2 cell frequencies were significantly increased in patients with AR and positively correlated with disease severity. Activated CCR7^low PD-1^high cTFH cells were also elevated, indicating enhanced TFH activation. Further scRNA-seq analysis identified six transcriptionally distinct TFH clusters, with TFH2-like transcriptional signatures enriched in patients with AR, consistent with flow cytometric findings. Notably, a GZMK+ TFH1 subset, enriched in cytotoxic genes, including GZMK, CCL5, and GZMA, was more abundant in patients with AR, a result corroborated by flow cytometry.ConclusionThis study demonstrates the expansion of cTFH2 and GZMK+ TFH1 subsets in patients with AR and highlights the phenotypic and transcriptional heterogeneity of cTFH cells. These findings suggest that peripheral TFH profiling may aid in characterizing systemic immune alterations in allergic inflammation. The scRNA-seq results should be interpreted as exploratory due to the limited sample size.Keywords: allergic diseases, T follicular helper cells, GZMK, single-cell RNA sequencing

Introduction

Allergic rhinitis (AR) is a common global health concern primarily driven by an immunoglobulin E (IgE)-mediated hypersensitivity reaction.1 It significantly affects patients’ quality of life and imposes a substantial economic burden.2 Previous studies have reported that the global prevalence of AR ranges from 10% to 40%, impacting an estimated 400 million individuals worldwide.3

Although T helper 2 (Th2)-driven inflammation is central to AR,4 increasing evidence highlights a pivotal role for T follicular helper (TFH) cells in regulating IgE-mediated immune responses.5 Conventional Th2 cells act as primary effector cells in AR through the production of type 2 cytokines such as interleukin (IL)-4, IL-5, and IL-13. However, Th2 profiling alone does not fully represent the B-cell assistance required for antibody class switching, affinity maturation, and sustained allergen-specific IgE production. In this context, TFH cells provide complementary insights beyond those offered by conventional Th2 cells. TFH cells, a specialized subset of CD4+ T cells, are essential for B-cell differentiation and antibody generation.6,7 Among TFH subsets, TFH2 cells are particularly associated with type 2 humoral immunity because they promote IgE-related B-cell responses.8 Unlike Th2 cells, which primarily mediate type 2 effector inflammation, TFH2 cells lie at the intersection of T-cell and B-cell immunity, potentially accounting for interindividual variability in IgE-related humoral responses among patients with established AR. Clinically, AR diagnosis relies on typical symptoms and evidence of allergen sensitization, whereas TFH2 profiling may provide additional information on IgE-related humoral immune activity and severity-associated immune alterations in patients with established AR. Previous studies have shown that increased peripheral TFH2 cells in patients with AR positively correlate with elevated serum IgE levels, suggesting preferential polarization of TFH2 cells toward enhanced IgE production in these individuals.9

Despite evidence linking TFH cells to IgE regulation,10 knowledge regarding the functional subsets of TFH cells across different immune environments remains limited. In particular, differences in TFH cell subsets between patients with AR and healthy controls (HC) have not been thoroughly characterized. Within the TFH compartment, TFH1 and TFH2 cells represent distinct but related polarization states shaped by specific immune milieus. TFH2 cells are closely associated with type 2 humoral immunity and IgE-related responses, whereas TFH1 cells are generally characterized by CXCR3 expression and type 1-related transcriptional programs. Therefore, concurrent assessment of TFH1 and TFH2 cells may help determine whether AR is defined by isolated TFH2 polarization or broader remodeling of the circulating TFH (cTFH) compartment. Kumar et al demonstrated that Th1- and Th2-skewed immune responses preferentially drive TFH1 and TFH2 differentiation, respectively, contributing to humoral immunity within lymphoid tissues. Notably, their work also identified a cytotoxic granzyme K-positive (GZMK+) TFH1 population with distinct transcriptional features, underscoring additional functional heterogeneity within the TFH compartment.11 However, whether a similar GZMK⁺ TFH1 population is altered in patients with AR remains unclear.

Although TFH cells primarily exert their physiological functions within germinal centers of secondary lymphoid tissues, direct sampling of such tissues is not routinely feasible in patients with AR. Peripheral blood therefore serves as a practical and minimally invasive source for immune monitoring. Peripheral blood cTFH cells have been shown to share phenotypic and transcriptional similarities with lymphoid TFH cells,12 and exhibit clonotypic relationships with tonsillar TFH cells.13 Moreover, activated TFH-like cells can enter the circulation following mucosal immune stimulation.14 These findings support the use of peripheral cTFH profiling as an accessible approach to characterize systemic humoral immune alterations associated with mucosal allergic disease, while acknowledging its limitations in fully recapitulating for tissue-level dynamics.

To address these knowledge gaps, we employed flow cytometry combined with single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) to profile TFH cell characteristics in the peripheral blood of patients with AR and HC. This study aimed to provide a comprehensive characterization of cTFH cells in AR and to enhance our understanding of TFH subset heterogeneity associated with allergic inflammation.

Methods

Study Participants

All participants were older than 18 years. The study was conducted in accordance with institutional ethical approval and the Declaration of Helsinki. A total of 56 individuals were enrolled, including 39 patients with AR and 17 HC. Among them, 36 patients with AR and 14 HC were analyzed by flow cytometry, while the remaining 3 patients with AR and 3 HC formed an independent exploratory cohort for scRNA-seq. Given the exploratory nature of the study, the sample size was informed by previous TFH studies,15,16 participant availability, and technical feasibility.

Patients with AR were diagnosed according to the Allergic Rhinitis and its Impact on Asthma (ARIA) guidelines, based on typical nasal symptoms and evidence of dust mite sensitization.17 Dust mite sensitization was confirmed by serum specific IgE detection and skin prick testing (SPT). Serum total IgE and Dermatophagoides pteronyssinus (Der p)-specific IgE were measured using the AllergyScreen allergen detection system (Mediwiss Analytic GmbH, Moers, Germany). SPT was performed using an inhalant allergen skin prick test reagent kit (Zhejiang Wolwo Bio-Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd., Zhejiang, China), with 10 mg/mL histamine as the positive control and saline as the negative control. Wheal diameters were measured 15 minutes after application, and a wheal at least 3 mm larger than that of the negative control was considered positive.

Patients were classified into mild or moderate-to-severe groups according to the ARIA guidelines. Mild AR was defined as the presence of mild symptoms without significant impact on quality of life (including sleep, daily activities, work, or study), whereas moderate-to-severe AR was defined as symptoms that noticeably impaired quality of life. Patients were excluded if they exhibited positive SPT reactions to inhalant allergens other than dust mites; had physician-diagnosed asthma or other allergic diseases; concomitant upper respiratory tract infection or sinusitis; pregnancy or lactation; previous allergen immunotherapy (AIT); major cardiovascular, autoimmune, or malignant diseases; use of anti-allergic medications, such as antihistamines, leukotriene receptor antagonists, mast cell stabilizers, or intranasal corticosteroids within 1 week prior to enrollment; or use of oral corticosteroids within 1 month prior to enrollment. HC had no history of allergic disease, major cardiovascular disease, autoimmune disease, malignancy, chronic inflammatory disease, or recent upper respiratory or systemic infections, and showed no evidence of dust mite sensitization.

Isolation of Peripheral Blood Mononuclear Cells (PBMCs)

Peripheral blood (30 mL) was collected from all participants in heparinized tubes. Samples were diluted with an equal volume of phosphate-buffered saline and layered onto a Ficoll-Hypaque solution (Meijingbio, Guangzhou, China), followed by centrifugation at 400 × g for 25 min. The PBMC layer was collected and washed twice with Hanks’ balanced salt solution (HBSS) by centrifugation at 300 × g for 5 min. The final cell pellet was resuspended in HBSS for subsequent experiments.

Flow Cytometry and Cell Sorting of TFH Cells

Following PBMC isolation, cells were stained with fluorochrome-conjugated monoclonal antibodies for flow cytometric analysis and cell sorting. Detailed antibody information, including fluorochromes, clones, catalog numbers, and manufacturers, is provided in Supplementary Table S1. Singlet cells were selected, followed by gating on CD3+CD4+ T cells. Regulatory T cells were excluded as CD25+CD127− cells, and cTFH cells were defined as CD3+CD4+CD45RA−CXCR5+ cells. cTFH1, cTFH2, and cTFH17 cells were defined as CXCR3+CCR6−, CXCR3−CCR6−, CXCR3−CCR6+ subsets, respectively, within the cTFH population. Activated cTFH cells were identified as CCR7^lowPD-1^high cells. Surface expression of functional molecules, including inducible T-cell costimulator (ICOS), programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1), and OX40 (CD134), was analyzed. For intracellular detection of GZMK⁺ TFH cells, cells were fixed and permeabilized using Fixation Buffer and Intracellular Staining Perm/Wash Buffer (10×) (BioLegend, San Diego, CA, USA), followed by staining with PE-conjugated anti-human GZMK antibody. GZMK⁺ TFH1 cells were analyzed within the CXCR3+CCR6− TFH1 gate. Data acquisition was performed using an X-20 flow cytometer (BD Biosciences, San Jose, CA, USA), and TFH cell sorting was conducted using a BD FACSAria II cell sorter (BD Biosciences, San Jose, CA, USA). Compensation was performed with single-stained controls, and gating was defined using fluorescence minus one (FMO) controls. Data were analyzed using Flow Cytometry Standard (FCS) 3.0 and Kaluza software (Beckman Coulter, Brea, CA, USA).

Preparation of Single-Cell Suspension

Cells were immediately transferred to Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM) (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) after collection. The suspension was filtered through a 40-µm cell strainer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) to remove residual cell clumps and immediately placed on ice. After centrifugation at 300 × g for 6 min at 4°C, the supernatant was discarded and the cell pellet was resuspended in DMEM and kept on ice prior to staining for loading.

Sequencing Library Construction Using the 10x Chromium Platform

Approximately 15,000–20,000 cells were loaded onto the 10x Chromium Controller (10x Genomics, Pleasanton, CA, USA) to generate nanoliter-scale droplets, targeting the recovery of approximately 5,000 cells per sample. The resulting complementary DNA (cDNA) was tagged with a common 16-nucleotide cell barcode and a 10–12-nucleotide unique molecular identifier (UMI) during reverse transcription. Full-length derived cDNA from poly(A)–tailed mRNA transcripts was enzymatically fragmented and size-selected to optimize the cDNA amplicon length (~400 bp) for library construction (10x Genomics). The concentration of the single-cell libraries was quantified using quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) on the QuantStudioTM 3 Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) with the KAPA Library Quantification Kit (Kapa Biosystems, Wilmington, MA, USA), ensuring appropriate cluster densities for sequencing on the NovaSeq 6000 platform (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA). Sequence data consisting of 28–98 bp reads (3′v3 libraries) or 2–150 bp reads (5′ libraries and TotalSeq libraries) were generated targeting approximately 50,000 read pairs per cell for gene expression profiling, thereby providing digital expression data for each individual cell.

scRNA-Seq Data Preprocessing

Raw sequencing data were processed using Cell Ranger v3.1.0 (10x Genomics, Pleasanton, CA, USA) to generate a gene–cell expression matrix. Downstream analyses were conducted with Seurat v3.1.1 in R 3.6.3. Cells with detected gene or UMI counts outside the mean ± 2 standard deviations for each sample, or exhibiting >10% mitochondrial gene expression, were excluded. After quality control, over 10,000 high-quality cells were retained. Gene expression values were normalized using the LogNormalize method, and highly variable genes were identified. Dimensionality reduction was performed by principal component analysis (PCA). Batch effects among the six individual scRNA-seq samples were corrected using Harmony with parameters theta = 2 and lambda = 1. Graph-based clustering was carried out using 30 nearest neighbors and a resolution of 0.3, followed by uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP) visualization in Seurat. Cluster marker genes were identified using the FindMarkers function in Seurat. P values were adjusted for multiple testing using the default Bonferroni correction implemented in Seurat, and genes with adjusted P < 0.05 were considered statistically significant marker genes. Cell-type annotation was performed manually based on canonical marker gene expression. Cluster-specific statistically significant marker genes were used for Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analysis via the R package clusterProfiler. For GO and KEGG analyses, multiple-testing-adjusted P values were applied, and adjusted P <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Statistical Analysis

All statistical analyses for clinical and flow cytometry data were performed using GraphPad Prism 5.0 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). Data normality was assessed prior to group comparisons. Normally distributed continuous variables were assessed using the unpaired Student’s t-test or one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Tukey’s post hoc test, whereas non-normally distributed variables were analyzed using the Mann–Whitney U-test or Kruskal–Wallis test followed by Dunn’s test or Bonferroni-adjusted pairwise comparisons, as appropriate. For categorical data, the χ2 or Fisher’s exact tests were used. Data presentation is detailed in the corresponding tables and figure legends. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05. Effect size estimates for key flow cytometry comparisons are provided in Supplementary Table S2.

Circulating Activated TFH Cells Were Higher in Patients with AR

The cTFH compartment is functionally heterogeneous and can be further divided into activated (CCR7^low PD-1^high) and resting (CCR7^high PD-1^low) subsets based on differentiation status (Figure 2A).19 The CCR7^low PD-1^high subset represents a population actively undergoing TFH differentiation within lymphoid tissues and has been proposed as a peripheral biomarker indicative of ongoing humoral immune responses,19,20 whereas CCR7^high PD-1^low cells are generally regarded as a resting or memory-like population with recirculating potential. In this study, activated CCR7^low PD-1^high cTFH cells were significantly increased in patients with AR compared with HC (26.02% ± 1.28 vs 21.59% ± 1.43, P<0.05). Furthermore, these cells were more abundant in patients with moderate-to-severe AR than in those with mild disease (29.62% ± 2.33 vs 23.41% ± 1.63, P<0.05) (Figure 2B).

Heterogeneity of TFH Cells Revealed by scRNA-Seq

Building on the flow cytometry findings, we further investigated TFH heterogeneity at the transcriptional level. TFH cells from patients with AR and HC were analyzed using scRNA-seq. UMAP analysis identified six distinct clusters (C0–C5) (Figure 3A). The top cluster marker genes defined the molecular characteristics of these clusters and together with canonical TFH markers, facilitated the annotation of their transcriptional states (Figure 3B). C0 exhibited a transcriptional profile enriched for CCR7, IL7R, TCF7, and LEF1, consistent with a precursor or central memory-like TFH state possessing lymphoid homing potential, representing the pro TFH compartment.21 C1 displayed a type I interferon-driven program, characterized by ISG15, STAT1, IRF7, MX1, ISG20, IFITM1, and IFITM2 expression, indicative of antiviral activity.22 C2 showed transcriptional features of CCR6, KLRB1 (CD161),23 and RORA, consistent with a TFH17 phenotype. Notably, C3 exhibited a cytotoxicity-associated module, comprising GZMK, GZMA, CCL5, NKG7, and CST7, suggesting cytotoxic transcriptional characteristics.11 C4 was defined by the expression of DDX17,24 NEAT1,25 OGA,26 and HMGB1, consistent with an activated TFH state. Finally, C5 cells expressed cell cycle markers MKI67 and TOP2A (Figure 3C). Next, we compared the relative distributions of the TFH clusters under healthy and AR conditions. C0 was more prevalent in the healthy group, whereas C1 and C3 were relatively enriched under AR conditions (Figure 3D). For marker-informed reannotation, we used TFH1- and TFH2-associated gene patterns reported in previous studies, including CXCR3/STAT1/IFNG-related type 1 features and CCR4/GATA3-related type 2 features. Based on these patterns, C3 exhibited TFH1-like transcriptional features, whereas C1 and C4 aligned more closely with TFH2-like transcriptional programs (Figure 3E and F).11,27 Following this marker-based reannotation, TFH1-like and TFH2-like clusters were relatively enriched under AR conditions (Figure 3G). These findings suggest a potential shift in TFH subset composition between the AR and HC groups. The annotations were established based on canonical marker gene expression patterns.

GZMK+ TFH1-Like Cluster Exhibited Cytotoxicity- and Activation-Associated Transcriptional Features

Single-cell clustering analysis revealed a relative enrichment of a GZMK⁺ TFH1-like cluster in AR, prompting a detailed characterization of its transcriptional profile. This cluster expressed high levels of cytotoxic genes, including GZMK, GZMA, NKG7, CCL5, and CST7, and demonstrated a cytotoxic transcriptional signature (Figure 4A, B). Violin plots confirmed higher expression of cytotoxicity-associated genes in this subset compared with other TFH populations (Figure 4C). GZMK⁺ TFH1-like cells from patients with AR broadly upregulated early activation genes, including FOS, JUN, CD69, and NFKBIA (Figure 4D), indicating an activated transcriptional state, which was also observed in other TFH subsets (Supplementary Figure S2). GO and KEGG enrichment analyses revealed that GZMK⁺ TFH1-like cells were predominantly enriched in key pathways, including T-cell receptor (TCR) signaling, natural killer (NK) cell-mediated cytotoxicity, antigen processing and presentation, and Th1/Th2/Th17 differentiation (Figure 4E and F), whereas other TFH subsets were enriched in their respective subset-specific immune pathways (Supplementary Figure S3 and S4).

Increased Frequency of GZMK+ TFH1 Cells in Patients with AR Confirmed by Flow Cytometry

To validate the presence and enrichment of GZMK+ TFH1 cells identified by scRNA-seq, we conducted flow cytometry on peripheral blood samples from patients with AR and HC (Figure 5A). Representative flow cytometry plots demonstrated a higher proportion of GZMK+ TFH1 cells in patients with AR compared with HC (Figure 5B). Quantitative analysis confirmed that the frequency of GZMK+ TFH1 cells was significantly elevated in patients with AR relative to HC (Figure 5C). However, no significant differences were observed among AR patients with varying disease severities at this stage (Figure 5D). Collectively, these results confirm that GZMK+ cells are increased within the phenotypically defined TFH1 compartment in patients with AR.

Discussion

AR is a common chronic inflammatory disorder of the upper airway that adversely affects sleep, daily activities, and overall quality of life. Poorly controlled AR is clinically important because it often coexists with asthma and may contribute to suboptimal asthma control.1 Therefore, identifying peripheral immune characteristics associated with AR severity may enhance our understanding of systemic immune alterations in allergic airway diseases.

TFH cells are closely associated with IgE production and play a critical role in the pathogenesis of AR and allergic asthma.28 In our cohort, patients with AR exhibited positive SPT responses to Der p and significantly higher total IgE and Der p-specific IgE levels compared with HC, supporting the clinical phenotype of dust mite-sensitized IgE-mediated AR. In this context, the observed increase in cTFH2 cells is biologically consistent with elevated Der p-specific IgE levels, as TFH2 cells are tightly linked to type 2 humoral immunity and IgE-associated responses. In contrast, TFH1 cells are typically associated with CXCR3/STAT1/IFNG-related type 1 pathways and are not generally considered the dominant TFH subset driving IgE production. Consistently, total TFH1 frequencies were not significantly increased by flow cytometry. However, recent studies indicate that humoral immune diseases may harbor disease-specific TFH subsets beyond classical TFH2 cells, such as IL-13+TFH cells in Kimura disease—a type 2 immune disorder, and GZMK+ TFH cells in IgG4-related disease.29,30 Accordingly, the enrichment of a GZMK+ TFH1 population suggests that the TFH1 compartment may undergo activation- or cytotoxicity-associated remodeling in AR, distinct from the classical TFH2–IgE axis.

Flow cytometric analysis revealed a significant increase in cTFH2 cells in patients with AR, with their frequencies displaying a severity-related trend, consistent with previous findings linking TFH2-related immune features and allergen-specific IgE levels to AR severity.31 To our knowledge, this is the first study to report an association between peripheral blood cTFH2 cell frequencies and AR severity. We also observed a marked increase in CCR7^low PD-1^high activated cTFH cells in AR, which was further enriched in moderate-to-severe cases. Collectively, these findings suggest that AR is characterized by both TFH2-skewed humoral immunity and enhanced cTFH activation, particularly in patients with more severe disease. Given the cross-sectional nature of this study, these findings should be interpreted as immune features associated with AR severity and warrant validation in longitudinal studies.

Further scRNA-seq analysis revealed pronounced cTFH heterogeneity. In addition to TFH2-like transcriptional programs, we observed relative enrichment of a TFH1-like cluster under AR conditions. This finding suggests that AR may involve broad remodeling of the cTFH compartment rather than isolated TFH2 expansion alone. This interpretation is supported by recent scRNA-seq analyses of polarized humoral immune responses showing that type 2 immune conditions are dominated by TFH2 cells but can also contain a minor TFH1 population, indicating that type-specific humoral responses may involve heterogeneous TFH states rather than a single polarized subset.11 Because the TFH1-like and TFH2-like annotations were based on marker-informed transcriptional patterns, these populations should be interpreted as transcriptional-state assignments rather than definitive stable lineages. This approach is consistent with previous human cTFH classification studies and recent transcriptomic analyses of TFH polarization.11,27 The observed discrepancy between flow cytometry, which showed selective TFH2 expansion, and scRNA-seq, which suggested enrichment of both TFH2-like and TFH1-like transcriptional states, may reflect methodological differences between surface marker-based phenotyping and transcriptome-based clustering.32 Collectively, these findings underscore the importance of integrating surface-marker and transcriptomic approaches to comprehensively delineate TFH cell states.

A notable observation in this study was the identification of an increased GZMK+ population within the TFH1 compartment in patients with AR. This subset expressed cytotoxicity-associated genes, including GZMK, GZMA, NKG7, CCL5, and CST7, and exhibited activation-related transcriptional characteristics. Similar GZMK+ cytotoxic TFH populations have been reported in other immune-mediated disorders, supporting the concept that TFH cells can acquire diverse transcriptional states under inflammatory conditions.30 Pathway enrichment analysis further corroborated cytotoxicity- and immune activation-associated transcriptional programs in GZMK+ TFH1 cells. Although this observation expands the spectrum of TFH heterogeneity in AR, the biological role of GZMK+ TFH1 cells warrants further validation through functional and tissue-level investigations.

Patients who had previously undergone AIT were excluded from this study to minimize the potential impact of immunotherapy on cTFH profiles. Previous studies have shown that AIT can modulate allergen-specific TFH/follicular regulatory T cell (TFR) responses, including Der p-specific IL-4+ TFH cells, TFR cell activity, and cTFH1/cTFH2-related changes during AIT.33–35 Whether GZMK+ TFH1 cells are involved in heterogeneous immune responses to AIT remains unclear and requires further longitudinal investigation.

Several limitations should also be acknowledged. First, immune profiling was performed using peripheral blood rather than local airway tissue; thus, it may not fully capture immune responses occurring in the upper respiratory tract. Nevertheless, peripheral blood profiling provides valuable insights into systemic immune alterations associated with allergic inflammation. Second, although the scRNA-seq cohort yielded exploratory transcriptomic information, its relatively small sample size limits statistical power and increases susceptibility to donor variability. Third, the cross-sectional design precludes assessment of temporal dynamics, treatment responses, or causal relationships. Finally, functional experiments were not conducted, and the biological role of GZMK+ TFH1 cells remains to be confirmed.

In summary, our study identified expansion of cTFH2 cells, activated cTFH cells, and GZMK+ TFH1 cells in patients with AR. These findings reveal the phenotypic and transcriptional heterogeneity of cTFH cells in AR and suggest that peripheral TFH profiling may aid in characterizing systemic immune alterations associated with allergic inflammation.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our study delineated the phenotypic and transcriptional heterogeneity of cTFH cells in patients with AR. Flow cytometric analysis demonstrated an expansion of cTFH2 cells, an increased proportion of activated cTFH cells, and a higher frequency of GZMK+ TFH1 cells in patients with AR, with both cTFH2 and activated cTFH cells showing severity-dependent elevations. Subsequent scRNA-seq analysis identified transcriptionally distinct TFH clusters, including a GZMK+ TFH1-like subset characterized by cytotoxicity- and activation-associated transcriptional profiles. Collectively, these findings suggest that the expansion of cTFH2 and the presence of GZMK+ TFH1 cells are associated with AR, underscoring the heterogeneity of cTFH cells in allergic inflammation.
