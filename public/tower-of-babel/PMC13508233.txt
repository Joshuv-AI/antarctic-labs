Background

Hyperuricemia has been increasingly recognized as an independent risk factor for cardiovascular and cerebrovascular disease [1, 2]. A 2025 meta-analysis of 22 prospective studies involving over 770,532 adults found hyperuricemia to be significantly associated with both stroke incidence (pooled RR 1.42; 95% CI 1.31–1.53) and stroke mortality (pooled RR 1.53; 95% CI 1.18–1.99), with a modest but significant association for hemorrhagic stroke mortality (pooled RR 1.13; 95% CI 1.02–1.26) [3]. The proposed mechanisms include nitric oxide depletion, endothelial dysfunction, NLRP3 inflammasome activation, and promotion of oxidative stress [4, 5].

Chronic tophaceous gout, the most advanced stage of gout, represents a visible marker of sustained, inadequately treated hyperuricemia [6]. While the epidemiological link between hyperuricemia and stroke is established, clinical reports documenting the co-occurrence of extensive tophaceous disease and hemorrhagic stroke are sparse. Herein, we describe a case of fatal hypertensive hemorrhagic stroke in a 72-year-old Nepali male in whom advanced tophaceous gout was incidentally detected on physical examination.

Case presentation

A 72-year-old male was brought to the emergency department of Hospital in Chitwan, Nepal, following a witnessed fall at home with subsequent decrease in consciousness. His medical history included systemic hypertension diagnosed 15 years prior and progressive gouty arthritis for 12 years. He had been prescribed febuxostat 40 mg daily, colchicine 0.5 mg daily, and indomethacin, but reported inconsistent adherence throughout his illness. He consumed alcohol occasionally and followed a predominantly non-vegetarian, high-carbohydrate diet.

On arrival, blood pressure was 200/100 mmHg; other vital signs were within normal limits. Neurological assessment revealed a Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) score of 11/15 (E3V3M5), left-sided hemiparesis, and right-sided facial deviation. Initial laboratory investigations revealed marked hyperuricemia with serum uric acid of 10.2 mg/dL(normal range 3.5–7.2 mg/dL). Additional investigations revealed a serum creatinine of 1.2 mg/dL (rising to 1.8 mg/dL by day 3, consistent with acute-on-chronic renal impairment), blood urea nitrogen of 24.3 mg/dL, and serum sodium of 138 mEq/L. Coagulation profile showed a prothrombin time of 14.7 s and INR of 1.13, excluding a coagulopathic contribution to the hemorrhage. Complete blood count revealed a total platelet count of 172,000/μL and hemoglobin of 12.8 g/dL. Lipid profile showed total cholesterol 170 mg/dL, LDL cholesterol 103.2 mg/dL, HDL cholesterol 35 mg/dL, and triglycerides 159 mg/dL. HbA1c was 5.1% (32.6 mmol/mol), excluding concurrent diabetes mellitus. Thyroid function tests were within normal limits. C-reactive protein was mildly elevated at 6.5 mg/L. The patient had a history of systemic hypertension with documented inconsistent medication adherence; he had no prior stroke or transient ischemic attack. Cardiovascular and respiratory examinations were unremarkable. Echocardiography demonstrated moderate concentric left ventricular hypertrophy, grade I diastolic dysfunction, normal LV systolic function (LVEF 65%), mild tricuspid regurgitation, and mild pulmonary artery hypertension, consistent with long-standing hypertensive heart disease. Physical examination incidentally revealed extensive chronic tophaceous gout with multiple firm, non-tender subcutaneous tophi at olecranon regions, left second metacarpophalangeal joint, bilateral first metatarsophalangeal joints, right Achilles tendon, and bilateral knees (Figs. 1, 2and3). The tophi had been progressively enlarging over several years.

Non-contrast CT of the brain confirmed a right thalamic hemorrhage with intraventricular extension (IVH), consistent with a hypertensive bleed at this location. No midline shift or hydrocephalus was documented on initial imaging. MRI was not performed during the admission. The patient was admitted to the intensive care unit under neurosurgery and managed with intravenous mannitol and 3% hypertonic saline for cerebral edema, and aggressive blood pressure control with intravenous labetalol transitioning to oral amlodipine–losartan. Neurosurgical intervention was not pursued given the deep thalamic location and poor neurological baseline.

Over four days, neurological status deteriorated to GCS 8/15. Neurosurgical consultation recommended conservative management given the thalamic location. Despite counseling regarding the guarded prognosis, the family elected to discharge the patient against medical advice (AMA) on nasogastric tube feeding. The patient died five days after discharge (day 9 from presentation) (Table 1).

Discussion

The primary cause of stroke in this patient was almost certainly his hypertensive crisis in the context of long-standing, poorly controlled hypertension. Tophaceous gout was an incidental finding on physical examination, not the proximate cause of the hemorrhagic event. We report this case not to claim a causal relationship between tophaceous gout and hemorrhagic stroke, but to highlight two clinically important observations.

First, the presence of extensive tophi across seven anatomical sites served as a visible indicator of years of sustained hyperuricemia and medication non-adherence. While this patient’s stroke was driven by hypertension, epidemiological evidence increasingly supports hyperuricemia as an independent contributor to cerebrovascular risk. Jiang et al. (2025) demonstrated significantly increased stroke incidence and mortality in hyperuricemic individuals [3], and Mendelian randomization studies suggest this association may be partly causal, mediated through nitric oxide depletion and NLRP3 inflammasome activation [4, 5]. In patients with both hypertension and chronic hyperuricemia, these vascular insults may act additively, though establishing individual-level causality from a single case is not possible.

Second, this case illustrates the challenges of chronic disease management in resource-limited settings. Our patient’s inconsistent adherence to prescribed febuxostat and colchicine, a well-documented problem in gout, with non-adherence rates exceeding 50% within the first year of therapy [7] contributed directly to the progression of tophaceous disease over several years [6, 8]. In Nepal, where healthcare infrastructure presents challenges for chronic disease follow-up, advanced tophaceous disease persists at rates rarely seen in high-income countries.

A comparable case was reported by Cardoza and Hoque (2025), describing a 62-year-old male with advanced tophaceous gout discovered incidentally during admission for acute ischemic stroke [9]. Silva Veiga et al. (2024) described an 85-year-old with giant tophaceous gout and a history of prior hemorrhagic stroke [10]. Our case adds to this limited literature a fatal outcome and a South Asian context.

Limitations

This case has several limitations. First, the diagnosis of tophaceous gout was clinical and was not confirmed by polarized light microscopy or synovial fluid crystal analysis; while the clinical picture is highly consistent with tophaceous gout, histological confirmation was not performed. Second, as a single case report, no causal inference can be drawn regarding the relationship between hyperuricemia or tophaceous gout and hemorrhagic stroke; the association between these conditions is epidemiological, not mechanistically established at the individual level. Third, longitudinal cardiovascular and metabolic data were unavailable, including prior serum uric acid trends, duration of hyperuricemia, prior echocardiographic data, and adherence history beyond patient self-report. These gaps limit the completeness of the clinical picture and restrict broader generalizability of the findings.

Conclusion

This case documents a fatal hypertensive hemorrhagic stroke in a 72-year-old male with coexisting advanced chronic tophaceous gout detected incidentally on examination. While the stroke was attributable to hypertensive crisis rather than gout per se, the extensive tophaceous burden reflects the systemic consequences of sustained hyperuricemia and medication non-adherence. Clinicians encountering advanced tophaceous gout should recognize it as a marker of metabolic derangement warranting comprehensive cardiovascular risk assessment, strict blood pressure control, and sustained urate-lowering therapy targeting serum uric acid below 6.0 mg/dL.
