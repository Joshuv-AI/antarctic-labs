Medicine in the Seventeenth Century.?As is well known,
medicine in the Middle Ages was closely associated with the
church: most of the doctors were at the same time monks. In
1452 the cardinal d'Estouteville was charged by Pope Nicolas V.
to re-organise the University of Paris. The Faculty of Medicine
required least change; one of the reforms then introduced was
the removal of the ban of celibacy from the doctors. The
practice of surgery is as old as the practice of medicine, and
it is a mistake to regard the birth of surgery as dating from
the time of the barber-surgeons. In the writings of Hippocrates,
Galen, and Celsus the various maladies were treated by
medicine or by operation. When the practice of medicine
became a part of the church the monks were forbidden to draw
blood, and that was one reason why in the Middle Ages surgery
became separate from medicine. But there was another reason,
perhaps more important, that all manual work was considered
derogatory and unworthy of the dignity of the church. The
end of the sixteenth century was for French surgery a glorious
epoch, due to the influence of Ambroise Pare (1510-1590). At
the commencement of the reign of Louis XIV there were
three rival medical corporations?(1) The Faculty of Medicine,
" petrifiee dans son immobility," claiming a submission from the
others which it did not obtain; (2) the Surgeons of St. Come,
intermediate in their position and custom, wearing the robe

on ceremonial occasions only and made to pass examinations
and take degrees; (3) the Barber-Surgeons, who had neither
robe nor School, and lived at the expense of the other two.
The reasons which originally separated the three corporations
no longer existed, the Faculty was against both, and drove the
surgeons and the barber-surgeons to act together against a
common enemy. Anatomy was the subject around which
discussion arose. Bodies for dissection could only be got after
an execution, the doyen of the Faculty had the right of
distribution, but often after an execution the body was seized,
taken to some boutique, and there dissected in the presence
of the surgeons and the barber-surgeons. In 1665 the surgeons
and barber-surgeons, having a common interest, united and
were taught at the College of St. Come. A fervent minority
of the surgeons, however, objected to the union. The Faculty
also objected, and succeeded in getting the Government, in 1660,
to pass a law prohibiting the granting of degrees at St. Come
and the wearing of robe or bonnet. This law retarded the
progress of surgery until the next century, and eventually led
to the separation of the barbers and surgeons. The Faculty
lived on its traditions, and though it was not completely the
enemy of progress, it made it understood that all progress must
be made through it. Relatively few doctors were admitted
to the Faculty in the seventeenth century. Between 1640-1670,
four were added each year. " On concoit des lors Vimportance
a ce grade, et la solennite dont etait entourree une reception"
(Raynaud).4 There was no complete system of clinical teaching.

The three outstanding medical discoveries of the seventeenth
century were the circulation of the blood, the medicinal use of
antimony, and the medicinal use of quinine. The circulation
of the blood was discovered by Harvey in 1628, and was
proscribed by the Faculty, not onty because the discovery was
made in England, but because it relegated the liver?the seat
of the facultes naturelles?to a state of idleness, and, what was
worse, to some inferior service. But what was graver still was
to think that the chyle, "impure et indigeste," entered the veins
under the left clavicle, and from there passed to the heart?that
noble place of vital heat?and sent by the right ventricle to
infect the organs of respiration and by the left ventricle to
penetrate, by the carotid vessels, the brain, to invade the viscera
and thus compromise seriously the fabrication des esprits
animaux. " Bref, c'est la vie elle-meme, qui serait compromise
par le nouveau systeme-ergo, la chyle doit aller au foie comme par
le passe." The followers of Harvey were called " circulateurs,"
and circulator in Latin was charlatan. That was sufficient; a
circulator was a charlatan. All les bons esprits adopted the

At the commencement of the sixteenth century a benedictine
monk, Basile Valentin by name, discovered and isolated
antimony, but as the antimony contained a certain proportion
of arsenic the pigs and the peasants on which he first tried it
grew fat. Encouraged by first success he gave it to the monks
in the convent to which he was attached, but whether it had
been badly prepared or given in too large a dose the monks, as
a result, became very ill. He called the drug, then, antimoine,
though this derivation has been questioned. He tried it again,
however, and was very successful in the cure of certain con-
ditions, and thought in consequence that he had discovered the
universal panacea for all ailments.
In 1638 a Jesuit, passing through the village of Malacotes, in
Peru, had a violent attack of intermittent fever. An Indian
chief promised to restore him to health, and going out brought
in the bark of a tree which he boiled. The beverage cured the
Jesuit of his attack. Others relate that the Countess
d'El-Cinchon, wife of the Viceroy of Peru, was similarly cured.
The Jesuit brought the drug to Europe. Sydenham reports that
it was brought to England in 1660. In 1679 an empirique
anglais, named Talbot, cured the French King of severe inter-
mittent fever. The King bought his secret for 48,000 livres,
and made him a knight. Three years afterwards the cure was
made public and found to be tincture of quinine.

Blood-letting.?When the Faculty was accused of being the
enemy of progress they answered, " Have we not discovered the
advantage of frequent blood-letting ? " " Le sang, dans le corps
humain, est comme I'eau dans une bonne fontaine: plus on en
tire, et plus il s'en trouve" (Botal). Guy Patin bled his wife
twelve times for une fluxion de poitrine and his son twenty
times for a fever. Neither age nor sex would stop him. The
Faculty was divided, however, on the value of blood-letting.
Guy de Labrosse, a doctor, was dying, and his confreres
recommended blood-letting. He called them sanguinary pedants,
and said that he would prefer to die than to be bled. They replied
that the devil would bleed him in the next world, as befitted
an impostor like him. During the campaign of 1658 the King,
who was then 20 years of age, fell ill with what now would
be called enteric fever. After a great and solemn consultation
it was resolved to give him antimony. In the opinion of the
opponents of antimony, what saved the King was his innocence,
his strong and robust youth, nine good blood-lettings, and the
prayers des gens de bien comme nous.

Conclusion.?Moliere has immortalised the doctor of the
seventeenth century. The medical profession with its pedantry
and sophism lent itself to satire and to ridicule. Moliere was
not slow to see this. His doctors on the stage were easily
recognised as well-known representatives of the Faculty. Here
is his description of the doctor who treated Monsieur de
Pourceaugnac: " He is a man who knows medicine thoroughly,
and for all the gold in the world would not treat his patients
by other remedies than those prescribed by the Faculty. He
is not one of those doctors who prolongs his patient's complaints:
he is an expeditious man, who loves to despatch his cases
promptly, and, if you have to die, he is the man to help you to
do it quickly."13 Thanks to Moliere, we see the doctor as
he lived and worked three hundred years ago, wearing his long
magisterial robe, his red stockings, his bonnet, and his rabat.
The rabat I take to be somewhat similar to the " bands " worn
by ordained ministers of the Church of Scotland. It was
a visible sign of the doctorate. " Who would put their
confidence in a doctor who did not wear a rabat ?" (Pascal).
The beard also was a part of the profession. The mule as
a means of locomotion was giving place to the horse (see
" L'Amour Medicin)."
Moliere, says Raynaud, had, in common with his teacher,
Gassendi, a sincere horror and contempt for all pedants and for
the " tartufes " of science who pretend to teach of what they are
ignorant. These are the outstanding traits of the doctors
of Moliere, all are pedants or charlatans. All doctors, however,
