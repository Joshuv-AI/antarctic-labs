Micropropagation and ISSR-based genetic fidelity assessment of the medicinal legume Butea monosperma (Lam.) Taub
Authors: Rahul J Janephalkar, Narendrasingh Chavan, Rahul R Rodge, Wagh A M, Kiran Pawar, Sakshi Nagesh Sagade
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractButea monosperma (Lam.) Taub., a leguminous tree of high ecological, medicinal and socio-economic importance, suffers from poor seed viability, dormancy and unreliable vegetative propagation, which restricts its large-scale cultivation and conservation. In this study, an efficient, reproducible indirect organogenesis protocol, combined with genetic fidelity analysis based on ISSR markers, was established for mature leaf and internodal explants of B. monosperma, providing an integrated system for clonal multiplication and quality control. Explants cultured on Murashige and Skoog (MS) medium supplemented with 2,4-D exhibited a clear dose-dependent response, with 5.0 µM 2,4-D inducing maximum callus formation (88.89% in leaf and 77.78% in internodes) and producing profuse, compact, organogenic callus within 4 weeks, whereas hormone-free controls remained non-responsive. Subsequent transfer of this callus to MS media containing individual cytokinins identified 2.5 µM 6-benzyladenine (BA) and 2.5 µM meta-topolin (mT) as optimal, yielding 13.56 ± 0.10 and 13.30 ± 0.36 shoots per callus clump with mean shoot lengths of 4.45 ± 0.15 and 4.08 ± 0.02 cm, respectively, while higher cytokinin levels reduced shoot number and elongation. Combining 2.5 µM BA or mT with low auxin concentrations further enhanced shoot multiplication; BA + 0.5 µM NAA produced 18.42 ± 0.05 shoots (5.34 ± 0.02 cm), and mT + 0.5 µM NAA yielded 18.64 ± 0.07 shoots (5.38 ± 0.03 cm), highlighting a narrow optimum for cytokinin-auxin balance. For rooting, half-strength MS containing 1.0 µM IBA produced the most vigorous root systems (94.44% response, 12.56 ± 0.07 roots, 5.26 ± 0.08 cm). Acclimatized plantlets transferred to a sterilized garden soil: organic manure (3:1) mixture exhibited normal morphology, rapid growth and about 80% survival under greenhouse and field conditions, comparable to earlier reports in B. monosperma var. lutea. Using 20 primers, ISSR profiling of the donor and nine regenerants yielded 71 scorable bands, 69 of which were monomorphic (97.18%) and only 2 polymorphic, indicating very high clonal uniformity despite the callus phase. The optimized micropropagation protocol and ISSR-based fidelity assessment together provide a promising system for large-scale propagation. However, further field validation is required, as well as for germplasm preservation and future genetic improvement and biotechnological applications in B. monosperma.”Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-52788-x.Keywords: Butea monosperma, Indirect organogenesis, Meta-topolin, ISSR markers, Clonal fidelity, Rooting, IBASubject terms: Biotechnology, Plant sciences

Introduction

The "Flame of the Forest," Butea monosperma (Lam.) Taub., or palash, is a multipurpose leguminous tree with substantial ecological, medicinal, and commercial importance. It is a medium-sized deciduous species that is usually 8 to 15 m tall. It is a member of the Fabaceae family, which is one of the largest angiosperm families, comprising more than 650 genera and approximately 18,000 species1. The species is easily identified by its pinnately trifoliate leaves and characterized by bright orange to red inflorescences that appear on leafless branches during the dry season2. Geographically, B. monosperma is found in many tropical and subtropical areas of Southeast Asia and the Indian subcontinent, such as Bangladesh, Nepal, Pakistan, Sri Lanka, Myanmar, Thailand, Laos, Cambodia, Vietnam, Malaysia, and portions of western Indonesia2,3.

More than 40 bioactive components, including alkaloids like palasonin, triterpenoids like lupeol and β-sitosterol, and flavonoids like butrin and isobutrin, have been found in B. monosperma through phytochemical and pharmacological studies4,5. These chemicals’ diverse biological actions, which include hepatoprotective, antidiabetic, antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, antibacterial, and anticancer effects, support the species’ growing pharmacological significance4,6–8.

While the stem bark is rich in medicarpin, a phytoalexin with well-established wound healing and osteogenic potential9, seeds and seed oil show notable anthelmintic and antifertility activities10,11. Their ethnomedical usage against chronic inflammatory and parasite illnesses is supported by the antioxidant and antifilarial properties of leaves and other aerial parts12.

Significant propagation limits prevent B. monosperma from being cultivated and conserved on a wide scale, despite its considerable ecological and medicinal importance. According to Tandon et al.3 and Mahender et al.13, seeds exhibit low viability, pronounced dormancy, and post-harvest loss of germination, leading to poor, irregular natural regeneration. When applied to old trees, conventional vegetative techniques like stem cuttings and grafting are slow, sensitive to genotype, and typically ineffective, which makes them inappropriate for mass multiplication or genetic improvement initiatives14–16. Due to these constraints, there is now more focus on in vitro micropropagation to support ex situ conservation of forestry and medicinal plants, including B. monosperma, and to produce consistent, disease-free planting material.

For B. monosperma, a number of micropropagation techniques have been documented; these primarily employ nodal or cotyledonary node explants cultivated on BA-based media to accomplish direct shoot organogenesis13,17,18. Large-scale clonal planting carries the risk of undetected somaclonal variation because these studies show effective shoot proliferation and satisfactory acclimatization, but they typically concentrate on direct pathways and only occasionally assess genetic stability with molecular markers17,18. Indirect organogenesis via callus can provide higher multiplication rates, allow the use of diverse explant sources, and serve as a basis for somatic embryogenesis or transformation, even though callus-mediated regeneration is often associated with a higher incidence of somaclonal variation than strictly direct regeneration19,20.

Therefore, DNA markers must be used to evaluate callus-based regeneration systems meant for clonal proliferation. There is a lot of polymorphism in inter-simple sequence repeat (ISSR) markers, which require no prior sequence information, and have been widely used to confirm clonal uniformity in micropropagated medicinal and woody species like Stevia rebaudiana, Cassia alata, Solanum khasianum, orchids, and timber trees21–24. In B. monosperma var. lutea, ISSR and SCoT markers have already been employed to demonstrate genetic homogeneity across plantlets produced from nodal cultures, indicating their usefulness for monitoring genetic stability in this genus17–19. Therefore, the goals of the current study were to: (i) standardize an effective, repeatable protocol for indirect organogenesis in B. monosperma using mature leaf and internodal explants; (ii) optimize shoot regeneration and rooting responses through systematic variation in auxin-cytokinin combinations and MS salt strength; and (iii) Using ISSR markers, assess the genetic integrity of plants grown in vitro to make sure they are suitable for conservation, clonal propagation, and future genetic improvement initiatives. Although several studies have reported micropropagation of Butea monosperma using nodal explants and direct organogenesis pathways, these approaches primarily focus on shoot proliferation with limited evaluation of genetic stability. In contrast, the present study emphasizes callus-mediated indirect organogenesis, which offers higher multiplication potential but may involve somaclonal variation. Therefore, this study integrates an optimized regeneration protocol with ISSR-based genetic fidelity assessment, providing a more comprehensive system for large-scale clonal propagation and quality assurance.

Materials and methods

Plant material source

Seeds of Butea monosperma (Lam.) Taub. were collected from Saralgaon, Murbad, Thane District, Maharashtra, India, in April 2019. The plant material was taxonomically identified and authenticated by Dr. Santosh Vishnu Sawardekar (Professor and Incharge, Biotechnology Centre, Dr. Balasaheb Sawant Konkan Krishi Vidyapeeth, Dapoli, India). A voucher specimen (DrBSKKV.BM-2019–01) has been deposited at the same institute for future reference.

All necessary permissions for plant collection were obtained from the Maharashtra State Forest Department, Government of Maharashtra, India, and all procedures complied with relevant institutional, national, and international guidelines and regulations.

Seeds collected from a mature tree were germinated under controlled greenhouse conditions to produce a population of about 50 seedlings. Uniform and healthy seedlings (3–4 months old) were kept in earthen pots within the greenhouse. Fully expanded leaves and internodal segments from actively growing shoots were cut and used as explants for in vitro experiments. Explants were taken from multiple healthy plants within this seed-derived population to ensure good genetic diversity and to prevent bias from a single donor genotype. Including multiple donor plants helped reduce genotype-dependent variation in the in vitro response.

Surface sterilization of explants

Explants were initially washed under running tap water for 40 min to remove surface contaminants. They were then treated with 0.2% (w/v) Bavistin (systemic fungicide) solution for 30 min, followed by washing with 2% (v/v) Labolene (liquid detergent) for 8–10 min. Subsequently, explants were thoroughly rinsed with sterile distilled water to remove detergent residues.

Under aseptic conditions in a laminar airflow cabinet, explants were surface sterilized with 70% (v/v) ethanol for 30 s, followed by treatment with 0.1% (w/v) mercuric chloride (HgCl₂; HiMedia, Mumbai, India) for 4 min. After sterilization, explants were rinsed 4–5 times with sterile distilled water to remove any traces of HgCl₂ before inoculation onto culture media.

Culture medium and conditions

Murashige and Skoog (MS) basal medium25 was used for all in vitro experiments. The medium was supplemented with 3% (w/v) sucrose and solidified with 0.8% (w/v) agar. The pH of the medium was adjusted to 5.8 before autoclaving at 121 °C for 20 min.

Cultures were maintained under controlled environmental conditions at 25 ± 2 °C with a 16 h light/8 h dark photoperiod provided by cool white fluorescent lamps, with a light intensity of 40–50 µmol m⁻2 s⁻113,18.

Callus induction and indirect organogenesis

Leaf and internodal explants were cultured on Murashige and Skoog (MS) medium supplemented with 2,4-D at concentrations of 0.0, 1.0, 2.5, 5.0, 7.5, and 10.0 µM. The percentage of callus induction, degree of callus formation (based on visual scoring), and callus texture (compact or friable) were recorded after 4 weeks of incubation (Fig. 1). A hormone-free MS medium was used as a control to confirm the auxin-dependent nature of callus induction.

Similar concentrations of 2,4-D have been reported to be effective for inducing callus-mediated organogenesis in several leguminous and medicinal plant species16,26,27.

Callus-induced shoot regeneration: cytokinins alone

Actively growing organogenic callus obtained from the optimal 2,4-D treatment was excised and cut into approximately 0.5 cm diameter uniform clumps using a sterile scalpel. These callus clumps were cultured on MS medium supplemented with different cytokinins, namely 6-benzyladenine (BA), kinetin (Kin), and meta-topolin (mT), at concentrations of 0.0, 1.0, 2.5, 5.0, 7.5, and 10.0 µM.

After 8 weeks of culture, the number of shoots per callus clump and mean shoot length were recorded. The data were subjected to analysis of variance (ANOVA), and mean comparisons were performed using Duncan’s Multiple Range Test (DMRT) at P ≤ 0.05.

Effect of cytokinin-auxin combinations on shoot regeneration

To enhance shoot multiplication, the optimal cytokinin concentrations (2.5 µM BA, 2.5 µM Kin, and 2.5 µM mT) were combined with different auxins, including α-naphthaleneacetic acid (NAA), indole-3-acetic acid (IAA), and indole-3-butyric acid (IBA), at concentrations of 0.1, 0.5, 1.0, 1.5, and 2.0 µM.

Uniform-sized callus clumps (approximately 0.5 cm in diameter) were subcultured onto these media and incubated under standard culture conditions. After 8 weeks, the number of shoots per callus clump and mean shoot length were recorded. The experimental data were analyzed using ANOVA followed by DMRT at P ≤ 0.05 to determine significant differences among treatments.

Root induction and the effect of MS strength

Elongated microshoots (4–5 cm in length) with well-developed nodes were excised and transferred to Murashige and Skoog (MS) medium of varying strengths (¼, ⅓, ½, full-strength [1 ×], and double-strength [2 ×]) supplemented with 1.0 µM indole-3-butyric acid (IBA). Cultures were maintained under standard incubation conditions, and observations on rooting response, including percentage of rooting, number of roots per shoot, and root length, were recorded after 4 weeks.

Acclimatization (Hardening)

Well-rooted and morphologically normal plantlets were carefully removed from culture vessels and washed gently under running tap water to remove adhering medium. The plantlets were then transferred to clay pots containing a sterilized mixture of garden soil and organic manure (3:1).

For primary hardening, plantlets were maintained in a greenhouse under high humidity, diffused light, and regular watering. After 3–4 weeks, hardened plants were transferred to open field conditions for further growth and evaluation. Survival percentage, plant vigour, and morphological uniformity in comparison with the donor plants were recorded. The acclimatization procedure followed protocols reported earlier for Butea monosperma var. lutea17,18.

Genetic fidelity analysis using ISSR markers

Genomic DNA was extracted from young leaves of the donor plant and nine randomly selected in vitro regenerated plantlets using a modified CTAB method. DNA quality and concentration were assessed by agarose gel electrophoresis and spectrophotometric analysis.

A total of 20 ISSR primers (UBC series) were selected based on their reproducibility and ability to generate clear banding patterns. PCR amplification was carried out in a thermal cycler using primer-specific annealing temperatures ranging from 50 to 56 °C.

Amplified products were resolved on 1.5% agarose gel in 1 × TBE buffer, stained with ethidium bromide, and visualized under UV light using a gel documentation system. Full-length, uncropped gel images with visible boundaries corresponding to Figs. 2 and 3 have been provided in the Supplementary Information (Fig. S1– Fig. S2) to ensure compliance with image integrity guidelines. No lanes were spliced or rearranged, and any image adjustments were applied uniformly across the entire image. All experiments were performed in replicate; however, representative gel images are shown, and additional replicate gel images are not available due to limitations in image documentation. Only clear and reproducible bands were considered for analysis. The total number of bands and the number of monomorphic bands were recorded for each primer. The percentage of monomorphism was calculated as (number of monomorphic bands / total number of bands) × 100. Genetic fidelity was inferred based on the extent of monomorphic banding patterns observed between the donor plant and regenerated plantlets. The results were compared with recent ISSR and SCoT-based fidelity studies in woody and medicinal plants22,23,28. Genetic homogeneity was inferred using the total number of ISSR loci, monomorphic loci, and polymorphic loci.

Data collection and statistical analysis

All experiments were conducted using a completely randomized design (CRD) with three independent replicates, each consisting of twelve explants per treatment. Data on callus induction, shoot regeneration, and rooting responses were recorded and expressed as mean ± standard error (SE). Percentage data were arcsine transformed before analysis when necessary.

The data were subjected to one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) to determine the significance of treatment effects. Mean comparisons were performed using Duncan’s Multiple Range Test (DMRT) at a significance level of P ≤ 0.05. Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software (IBM SPSS Statistics, version 27).

Results and discussion

Effect of 2,4-D on callus induction

While control explants on hormone-free MS medium did not develop callus and did not alter morphologically over 4 weeks, leaf and internodal explants showed a significant response to exogenous 2,4-D. Internodal segments showed slightly lower but still high induction (77.78%) and generated compact callus of comparable texture, compared to leaf explants, which showed the maximum callus induction (88.89%) at 5.0 µM 2,4-D, creating a profuse, compact, cream-colored callus mass scored as degree + + + + + . Callus frequency dropped to roughly 59–72%, and callus development was moderate at lower 2,4-D concentrations (1.0–2.5 µM), while induction frequencies decreased and the callus became softer and less compact (degree + +) at higher concentrations (7.5–10.0 µM), suggesting a clear optimum near 5.0 µM for both explant types shown in Table 1.

This bell-shaped response implies that in order to initiate dedifferentiation while maintaining the explant tissues’ organogenic ability, an intermediate 2,4-D level is necessary. Pterocarpus marsupium, Clitoria ternatea, Acacia chundra, and other legumes have been shown to exhibit similar patterns: while excessive auxin promotes friable, non-organogenic proliferation that is less appropriate for shoot regeneration, moderate 2,4-D concentrations promote morphogenic callus16,26,27. For later shoot regeneration tests, the compact callus obtained from 5.0 µM 2,4-D was chosen as the initial material.

Shoot regeneration from callus using cytokinins alone

Shoot organogenesis from callus was significantly affected by cytokinin supplementation, with responses varying among the three cytokinins tested. No shoot regeneration occurred on cytokinin-free MS medium, confirming the vital role of cytokinins in shoot induction.

Among the cytokinins assessed, 6-benzyladenine (BA) showed the highest shoot induction response. At 1.0 µM BA, an average of 7.75 ± 0.05 shoots per callus clump and a mean shoot length of 3.21 ± 0.04 cm were recorded after 8 weeks of culture. The optimal response was observed at 2.5 µM BA, which yielded the maximum number of shoots (13.56 ± 0.10 per callus clump) along with the longest shoot length (4.45 ± 0.15 cm). Further increases in BA concentration led to a gradual decline in shoot regeneration, with 10.0 µM BA producing only 3.05 ± 0.24 shoots per callus clump and a reduced shoot length of 2.12 ± 0.16 cm (Table 2).

Kinetin (Kin) and meta-topolin (mT) also promoted shoot regeneration, though their responses were comparatively lower than BA. At 2.5 µM mT, callus cultures produced 13.30 ± 0.36 shoots per clump with a mean shoot length of 4.08 ± 0.02 cm, indicating mT as a potential alternative cytokinin for shoot induction in Butea monosperma. Kinetin showed comparatively lower efficiency, with a maximum of 6.89 ± 0.16 shoots per clump and a shoot length of 3.60 ± 0.18 cm at 1.0 µM.

The overall trend indicated that moderate cytokinin concentrations were optimal for shoot induction, while higher concentrations inhibited shoot proliferation and elongation. Similar responses have been reported in various woody and medicinal plant species, where supra-optimal cytokinin levels negatively affect meristematic activity29,30.

These findings align with previous reports suggesting that BA and mT are more effective than other cytokinins for shoot induction in woody and medicinal plants such as Tecoma stans, Coleus forskohlii, and Wedelia chinensis31–33.

Regeneration of shoots from callus: BA in combination with auxins

The combination of 6-benzyladenine (BA) with auxins significantly enhanced shoot regeneration compared to BA alone. Among the auxins tested, indole-3-acetic acid (IAA), indole-3-butyric acid (IBA), and α-naphthaleneacetic acid (NAA) exhibited a concentration-dependent effect on shoot proliferation and elongation.

In the BA + IAA series, the maximum response was observed at 0.5 µM IAA, which produced 14.61 ± 0.03 shoots per callus clump with a mean shoot length of 4.70 ± 0.06 cm after 8 weeks of culture (Table 3). Further increase in IAA concentration resulted in a gradual decline in both shoot number and shoot length, with 2.0 µM IAA yielding only 5.39 ± 0.07 shoots per clump and a shoot length of 2.61 ± 0.04 cm, indicating a narrow optimal range for BA-IAA interaction.

A similar trend was observed in the BA + IBA series, where 0.5 µM IBA in combination with 2.5 µM BA produced 15.03 ± 0.15 shoots per callus clump with a mean shoot length of 4.98 ± 0.05 cm. However, higher concentrations of IBA reduced both shoot proliferation and elongation.

Among the three auxins tested, NAA proved to be the most effective. The combination of 2.5 µM BA with 0.5 µM NAA resulted in the highest shoot regeneration (18.42 ± 0.05 shoots per callus clump) and maximum shoot length (5.34 ± 0.02 cm). Beyond this concentration, both shoot number and shoot length declined, suggesting that only low concentrations of NAA act synergistically with BA to promote shoot organogenesis.

These results indicate that an optimal auxin-cytokinin balance is critical for efficient shoot regeneration. Low concentrations of auxins are known to enhance cytokinin-mediated activation of axillary and adventitious meristems, whereas higher concentrations may inhibit shoot development by promoting callus proliferation or suppressing meristematic activity. Similar responses have been reported in woody and medicinal plants such as Pterocarpus marsupium, Bauhinia variegata, and Tecoma stans, where low levels of NAA in combination with BA significantly improved shoot regeneration efficiency27,31,34.

Regeneration of shoots from callus: kinetin and meta-topolin in combination with auxins

The interaction of auxins with kinetin (Kin) and meta-topolin (mT) significantly influenced shoot regeneration from callus cultures. In kinetin-based treatments, supplementation with low concentrations of auxins enhanced shoot proliferation compared to kinetin alone. Among the combinations tested, 0.5 µM NAA in combination with 2.5 µM kinetin resulted in the highest response, producing 13.39 ± 0.03 shoots per callus clump with a mean shoot length of 4.97 ± 0.01 cm (Table 4).

Combinations of mT with IAA and IBA at 0.5 µM also resulted in substantial shoot proliferation (approximately 14–15 shoots per callus clump) and good shoot elongation (4.6–4.9 cm). However, NAA remained the most effective auxin in promoting shoot regeneration across all cytokinin treatments.

These findings highlight the importance of an optimal auxin-cytokinin balance for efficient shoot organogenesis. Meta-topolin, in particular, has been reported to improve shoot proliferation and quality while minimizing physiological abnormalities in several woody and medicinal plant species28,31,35, supporting its effectiveness in the present study.

In vitro rooting of regenerated microshoots

Root induction in regenerated microshoots was significantly influenced by the strength of MS medium in the presence of 1.0 µM indole-3-butyric acid (IBA). Among the different treatments, half-strength MS (½ MS) medium exhibited the highest rooting response (94.44%), producing 12.56 ± 0.07 roots per shoot with a mean root length of 5.26 ± 0.08 cm after 4 weeks of culture (Table 6).

These results indicate that reduced salt strength of MS medium is favourable for root induction, possibly due to decreased osmotic stress and enhanced auxin sensitivity in regenerating shoots. Similar observations have been reported in several woody plant species, where half-strength MS medium significantly improved root induction and root quality15,16.

Acclimatization of plantlets

Well-rooted and morphologically normal plantlets were carefully removed from culture vessels, washed gently to remove adhering medium, and transferred to clay pots containing a sterilized mixture of garden soil and organic manure (3:1). The plantlets were maintained under greenhouse conditions with high humidity, diffused light, and regular watering for primary hardening.

After 3–4 weeks, hardened plants were transferred to open field conditions, where they exhibited normal morphology, active growth, and approximately 80% survival. This survival rate is comparable to earlier reports on Butea monosperma var. lutea, where similar acclimatization strategies yielded 70–80% survival under greenhouse conditions17,18.

These findings suggest that the use of porous, well-drained substrates, along with controlled humidity during initial transfer, is critical for minimizing transplant shock and enhancing ex vitro survival of regenerated plantlets.

Genetic fidelity analysis using ISSR markers

The genetic homogeneity of plants regenerated through the optimized indirect organogenesis protocol was assessed using inter-simple sequence repeat (ISSR) markers. A total of 20 ISSR primers (UBC series) were screened, and all primers produced clear and reproducible banding patterns across the donor plant and nine randomly selected regenerants, indicating good DNA quality and reliable PCR amplification.

In total, 71 scorable bands were generated, of which 69 were monomorphic and only 2 were polymorphic, resulting in 97.18% monomorphism and 2.82% polymorphism. Primers such as ISSR 886 and UBC 840 produced completely monomorphic banding profiles, with identical banding patterns observed between the donor plant and all regenerants (Figs. 2 and 3).

Minor variation was detected with primers UBC 811 and, to a lesser extent, UBC 812, where the absence of a few bands was observed in one or two regenerants. However, no consistent or significant polymorphism was detected across the analyzed samples.

The high level of monomorphism observed in this study indicates that the regenerated plants are genetically stable and true-to-type. These results are in agreement with previous ISSR-based studies in woody and medicinal plant species such as Cassia alata, Stevia rebaudiana, and Solanum khasianum, where high monomorphism (≥ 95%) has been reported as an indicator of clonal fidelity21–23,36.

Despite the involvement of a callus phase, which is often associated with somaclonal variation, the present protocol maintained a high degree of genetic uniformity. This suggests that the optimized auxin–cytokinin regime used in this study effectively minimized genetic variation during regeneration. Similar observations have been reported in recent studies employing meta-topolin-based micropropagation combined with ISSR and SCoT markers in medicinal and woody species28,35,37.

Overall, the high level of genetic uniformity (97.18% monomorphism) confirms that the developed protocol is suitable for the production of true-to-type plants of Butea monosperma, supporting its potential application in large-scale propagation and conservation programs.

Study limitations

Although the present study demonstrates an efficient indirect organogenesis protocol for Butea monosperma, certain limitations should be acknowledged. Genetic fidelity was assessed using ISSR markers; however, the inclusion of additional high-resolution marker systems such as SSRs or SNP-based analyses would provide a more comprehensive assessment of genetic stability.

Additionally, full sets of replicate gel images were not archived at the time of experimentation, which may limit complete visual documentation of reproducibility. While an approximately 80% survival rate was achieved during acclimatization, long-term performance and stability under diverse agro-climatic conditions remain to be evaluated.

Future studies focusing on multi-location trials, larger sample sizes, and advanced molecular characterization would enhance the protocol’s applicability to large-scale propagation and conservation programs.

Conclusion

The present study establishes an efficient and reproducible indirect organogenesis protocol for Butea monosperma using mature leaf and internodal explants. An intermediate concentration of 2,4-D (5.0 µM) was found to be optimal for inducing compact and organogenic callus. Among cytokinins, 6-benzyladenine (BA) and meta-topolin (mT) at 2.5 µM, particularly in combination with low concentrations of α-naphthaleneacetic acid (NAA; 0.5 µM), resulted in high-frequency shoot regeneration with enhanced shoot multiplication and elongation.

For in vitro rooting, indole-3-butyric acid (IBA) proved to be the most effective auxin, with half-strength MS medium supplemented with 1.0 µM IBA producing well-developed and branched root systems. The regenerated plantlets showed successful acclimatization with approximately 80% survival under greenhouse and field conditions.

Genetic fidelity analysis using ISSR markers revealed a high level of monomorphism (97.18%), confirming the genetic stability and true-to-type nature of the regenerated plants despite the involvement of a callus phase.

Overall, the developed protocol provides a reliable system for large-scale propagation and conservation of Butea monosperma, and may serve as a useful platform for future genetic improvement and biotechnological applications.
