Red blood cells stored in anticoagulant versus additive solution for large volume transfusions in pediatric patients undergoing cardiopulmonary bypass
Authors: Nabiha H Saifee, Meghan Delaney
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe safety of red blood cells (RBCs) in additive solutions (AS) for large‐volume transfusion (LVT) in pediatrics remains understudied. This study examines the laboratory and clinical impact of CPD‐stored products versus CP2D/AS‐3 products in pediatric patients receiving LVT during cardiac surgery.Study Design and MethodsA retrospective review compared patients undergoing Norwood or heart transplant procedures 28 months before and after a transition from CPD to CP2D/AS‐3 blood products. Laboratory values were compared pre‐surgery, immediately post‐surgery, and 1 day later, along with clinical outcomes.ResultsThis study included 89 patients: 46 in the CPD group and 43 in the AS‐3 group. Patient weight and transfused RBC, platelet, and cryoprecipitate units were similar between groups. Plasma usage was higher in the AS‐3 group, and 28% of the CPD group received whole blood. Sodium, glucose, and phosphorus were significantly higher in the AS‐3 group immediately post‐surgery, but not 1 day later. Lactate was significantly higher immediately post‐surgery in the AS‐3 group undergoing Norwood procedure, likely due to their significantly younger age. Conversely, lactate was significantly lower immediately post‐surgery in the AS‐3 group undergoing heart transplants and remained lower on day one. Mortality, total length of stay (LOS), and intensive care unit LOS were similar between groups.DiscussionThis analysis suggests that AS‐3 may transiently increase sodium, glucose, and phosphorus immediately after LVT during pediatric cardiac surgery. These changes are consistent with LVT exposure to CP2D/AS‐3 and fully resolved by 24 h, with no effect on patient mortality or LOS.Keywords: AS‐3 additive solution, Cardiopulmonary bypass, Citrate‐phosphate‐dextrose (CPD), Electrolyte abnormalities, Large‐volume transfusion, Pediatric cardiac surgery, Red blood cell additive solutions

BACKGROUND

The safety of donor red blood cells (RBCs) preserved in additive solutions (AS) has been demonstrated for small‐volume transfusions in pediatric patients in numerous clinical studies. 1 , 2 , 3 With limited availability of non‐AS RBCs, many hospitals utilize RBCs in additive solutions for large volume transfusions (LVTs) as well, despite a lack of comprehensive studies in this clinical setting. 4 , 5 Our pediatric hospital's primary blood supplier changed from RBC inventory including AS‐5 RBCs and citrate–phosphate‐dextrose (CPD) whole blood (WB) and RBC units to AS‐3 RBC units. This study examines the laboratory and clinical outcomes of WB and RBC units stored in CPD versus RBCs in citrate–phosphate‐double‐dextrose (CP2D)/AS‐3 additive solution and plasma given to neonatal and pediatric patients receiving LVT during cardiac surgery.

METHODS

At a single tertiary care pediatric hospital, a retrospective chart review was performed on patients undergoing either Norwood or heart transplant surgeries 28 months before or after switching from using CPD whole blood and RBCs to CP2D/AS‐3 RBC units and plasma. Patients receiving supernatant‐removed RBCs for ABO‐incompatible heart transplants were excluded. All patients included in the study underwent cardiopulmonary bypass and received massive transfusion at the time of surgery. Intraoperative blood recovery (transfusion of washed, autologous recovered red cells from CellSaver™) was not counted. Each whole blood unit was assumed to be 500 mL and each RBC unit 350 mL. Based on the number of units used during surgery, including those for priming the cardiopulmonary bypass pump, the dose in milliliters per kilogram of body weight (mL/kg) of RBCs was calculated. Patients were considered massively transfused in the OR if they received greater than 20 mL/kg whole blood or RBCs. All RBC and whole blood units were irradiated just prior to issue and were less than 7 days old for children less than 5 years in both groups.

Data were collected as part of the Transfusion Service Quality Improvement program, and this study was approved by Seattle Children's Institutional Review Board, which waived the requirement for patient informed consent. Data collected included age, sex, weight closest to time of surgery, admission date, length of stay (LOS), number of days in intensive care unit (ICU), primary surgical procedure during admission, survival during admission, number and type of transfused blood products (including whole blood, RBCs, plasma, platelets, and cryoprecipitate), and labs from blood gas measurement (pH, base excess, sodium, potassium, glucose, ionized calcium, and lactate), chemistry lab panel, and complete blood counts (CBCs). The pre‐transfusion labs included the first point‐of‐care blood gas testing in OR at or just before surgery start time and nearest pre‐procedure chemistry/CBC. Post‐transfusion labs included first post‐procedure blood gas, chemistry lab panel, and CBC and first complete blood gas, chemistry panel, and CBC on day one post‐procedure.

Laboratory values reflecting known constituents of blood product additives and red blood cell metabolism were compared pre‐surgery to (1) immediately post‐surgery and (2) day one post‐surgery. Statistical analyses were performed using R v4.2.1; continuous variables were analyzed using the Wilcoxon rank sum test and categorical variables using the Fisher exact test. A 2‐sided p≤ .05 was considered statistically significant.

RESULTS

Patient and transfusion characteristics

The study included 89 patients: 46 in the CPD group and 43 in the AS‐3 group (Table 1). The CPD group included 27 infants undergoing Norwood procedure (mean age 10.5 days) and 19 pediatric patients undergoing heart transplant (mean age 4.6 years). The AS‐3 group included 28 infants undergoing Norwood procedure (mean age 5 days) and 15 pediatric patients undergoing heart transplant (mean age 5.6 years). Among patients receiving heart transplant, age was not significantly different; however, patients receiving Norwood procedure were significantly younger in the AS‐3 group compared to the CPD group (p< .01).

Body weight and number of RBC, platelet, and cryoprecipitate units transfused were not significantly different between groups (Table 1). Patients in the CPD group weighed on average 9.8 ± 15.2 kg versus 7.9 ± 11.2 kg in the AS‐3 group (p = .13). Patients received on average 197 ± 170 mL/kg WB or RBCs in the CPD versus 190 ± 136 mL/kg RBCs in the AS‐3 group (p = .72). Patients were exposed to on average 2.2 ± 2.0 versus 2.7 ± 2.6 RBC units (p = .44), 1.2 ± 0.6 versus 1.3 ± 0.9 platelet units (p = 0.54), and 1.5 ± 0.8 versus 1.7 ± 0.8 cryoprecipitate units (p = .09) in the CPD versus AS‐3 groups, respectively. Plasma usage was higher with an average 1.7 ± 2.1 units in the AS‐3 group versus 1.1 ± 1.5 units in the CPD group (p< .01). About 28% of the CPD group received a WB unit for pump priming whereas the AS‐3 group received none.

Clinical and laboratory outcomes

There were no significant differences in clinical outcomes, including mortality, hospital LOS, and ICU LOS (Table 1). The AS‐3 group (Norwood and heart transplant combined) demonstrated significant increases in sodium, glucose, and phosphorus immediately post‐surgery, but these resolved by day 1 post‐surgery (Tables 2 and 3). Potassium levels showed a slight average increase in the CPD group 0.3 ± 0.8 mEq immediately post‐surgery compared to a slight decrease in the AS‐3 group −0.02 ± 0.5 mEq immediately post‐surgery (Tables 2 and 3). A smaller difference in pH was noted on day 1 post‐surgery in the AS‐3 group compared to CPD (0.03 ± 0.09 vs. 0.07 ± 0.09, p = .03). Lactate was similar when analyzing Norwood and heart transplant surgery patients together immediately post‐surgery (AS‐3: 1.1 ± 3.9 vs. CPD: 1.6 ± 1.7, p = .53) and on day one (AS‐3: −0.3 ± 3.4 vs. CPD: 1.0 ± 1.5, p = .68); however, a significantly higher lactate was noted immediately post‐surgery in the AS‐3 group undergoing the Norwood procedure (3.3 ± 2.0 vs. 2.0 ± 2.0, p< .01), but not on day one (1.7 ± 1.2 vs. 1.5 ± 1.7, p = .19). Of note, the AS‐3 group undergoing Norwood procedures were significantly younger compared to the CPD group undergoing Norwood procedure (Table 1). Conversely, lactate was significantly lower in AS‐3 heart transplant group immediately post‐surgery (−2.8 ± 3.5 vs. 1.2 ± 1.3, p< 0.01) and on day one (−4.1 ± 3.1 vs. 0.2 ± 0.3, p <.01). The changes in hematocrit immediately post‐surgery (AS‐3: −0.5 ± 8.9 vs. CPD: −1.8 ± 8.6, p = .48) or on day one (AS‐3: 1.2 ± 7.9 vs. CPD: 0.13 ± 8.8, p = .44), and platelet counts immediately post‐surgery (AS‐3: 46.7 ± 146.8 vs. CPD: −1.3 ± 135.2, p = .18) or on day one (AS‐3: 45.4 ± 164.0 vs. CPD: −9.7 ± 133.7, p = .12) were not significantly different between groups (Tables 2 and 3).

DISCUSSION

This retrospective study is one of few published evaluations of LVT in children comparing AS‐RBC units to anticoagulant‐only blood units. 6 , 7 Surveys of pediatric transfusion practice have found that hospitals lack a standard approach to the use of or concerns about AS‐containing RBCs. 4 , 5 , 8 This analysis provides an objective comparison of biochemical and clinical endpoints in patients receiving RBCs stored in AS.

Despite very large transfusion volumes (~190 mL/kg over 4–6 h), the laboratory changes observed immediately post‐surgery—specifically higher sodium, glucose, and phosphorus in the AS‐3 group—were transient and resolved within 24 h. These findings are consistent with the composition of CP2D/AS‐3, which contains higher amounts of sodium, dextrose, and phosphate compared to CPD. Potassium differences were not clinically significant. Overall, these biochemical changes appear transient and consistent with LVT exposure. Reassuringly, no significant differences were observed in mortality or LOS between the two transfusion groups.

We also observed differences in lactate levels. In the AS‐3 group, neonates undergoing Norwood procedures (mean age ~5 days) had higher immediate postoperative lactate levels, whereas pediatric patients (mean age ~5.6 years) undergoing heart transplant had lower lactate levels. These findings are likely driven by patient and procedural factors rather than the additive solution itself. The higher lactate in the very young infants undergoing cardiac surgery may be due to surgical factors such as cold ischemic time on pump combined with the reduced clearance capacity. In a cohort of 376 neonates undergoing cardiac surgery with a median age of 5 days (range 1–30 days), significant increases in lactate were observed from pre‐bypass to start of bypass and from start to end of bypass. 9 The primary predictors of lactate increase were related to surgical factors (duration of circulatory arrest, blood pressure management). Another study of infants undergoing cardiac surgery with cardiopulmonary bypass demonstrated that lactate clearance increases significantly with age. 10 Younger neonates have reduced hepatic metabolic capacity for lactate clearance compared to older infants. Consistent with our study, neonates admitted for cardiac surgery typically normalize lactate levels by 36 h. 11 The lower lactate observed in the transplant cohort likely reflects both improved metabolic clearance and small sample size variability.

Our study had several weaknesses due to its retrospective design. First, the patient ages were significantly different between the CPD and AS‐3 groups in the Norwood subgroup, and the heart transplant group had older and variable patient ages. Second, some patients in the CPD group also received a whole blood component for heart pump priming, while those in the AS‐3 group received an RBC and plasma unit for cardiopulmonary pump prime. With this, we also noted the AS‐3 group patients undergoing heart transplant also received more plasma compared to the CPD group. While the age of the whole blood and RBC unit in both groups was all <7 days per institutional policy, the differences in component exposure may have influenced laboratory findings. Another limitation is the focus on standard laboratory values and broad clinical outcomes such as mortality and LOS. More granular physiologic parameters, such as cerebral oxygen saturation or markers of microcirculatory perfusion, were not available. Strengths of the study include care delivered by a small group of highly experienced cardiac surgeons and a closely coordinated multidisciplinary team, which enhances consistency in clinical management.

As DEHP‐containing blood bags are phased out due to concerns regarding reproductive, endocrine, and environmental effects, new additive solutions and storage systems will be introduced. These new additives may include higher concentrations of adenine and mannitol, potentially raising theoretical concerns in neonates receiving LVTs. 12 , 13 Adenine is metabolized to 2,8‐dihydroxyadenine, which has limited solubility and could theoretically precipitate in renal tubules, particularly in neonates with immature glomerular filtration. 14 Mannitol, as an osmotic diuretic, raises specific concerns regarding fluid and electrolyte disturbances. 15 Given the limited physiologic reserve and immature renal function in neonates, careful evaluation of these newer products in LVT for neonatal and pediatric patients is essential. Our study provides a framework for assessing both biochemical and clinical outcomes in this vulnerable population.

CONCLUSION

Our study of very young neonates with congenital heart disease and pediatric heart transplant patients undergoing LVTs indicates that while AS‐3 LVT causes transient laboratory changes, there are no differences in clinical outcomes. This study, although small, provides careful assessment of using additive containing blood products in the smallest patients who have received very large doses and overall suggests that the products are tolerated and do not lead to negative clinical outcomes.
