Exploration of the radiation dose-toxicity relationship for multiple ophthalmological organs at risk for pediatric patients assessed in long-term head and neck rhabdomyosarcoma survivors

ABSTRACT

Introduction

Survivors of head and neck rhabdomyosarcoma (HNRMS) are known to develop a wide variety of late adverse effects following treatment. These frequently include ophthalmological adverse events (OAE). In a previous study by our group [1], we reported a prevalence of 74% for any grade OAE and 35% for at least one grade ≥ 3 OAE in a large cohort of 98 long term survivors of pediatric HNRMS. Ophthalmological OAE include cataract, globe displacement, dry eyes, epiphora, retinopathy and optic neuropathy. Radiation exposure is one of the most important risk factors for ocular complications in long-term childhood cancer survivors [2]. In order to minimize radiation induced OAE, continuous efforts have been made to limit radiation doses to ophthalmologic organs at risk (OAR). Despite these efforts, complete sparing of all OAR is often impossible without compromising oncologic treatment. A more detailed and nuanced understanding of OAR specific dose tolerance could help guide radiotherapy treatment planning. For adult patients, consensus treatment guidelines include specific threshold doses that are based on available literature describing specific dose-toxicity relationships: for the retina (Dmax < 45 Gy), optic nerves and optic chiasm (Dmean < 50–60 Gy), lacrimal glands (Dmean < 15–35 Gy) and lens (Dmean < 10 Gy) [3], [4], [5], [6], [7]. However, the radiosensitivity of normal healthy tissue is known to be different between adults and children [8]. Only a few studies were specifically conducted in children, describing dose-toxicity relationships between a mean lens dose of 2.5 Gy and associated risk of cataract development [9], [10], [11], [12]. Such relationships are not well-described for other ophthalmological OAR that are associated with the commonly observed OAE in HNRMS survivors.

The aim of the present study is to describe the relationship between radiation dose to ophthalmological OAR (orbit, lacrimal gland, lens, nasolacrimal duct, retina, and optic nerves) and the clinical presence of OAE at in long-term survivors of pediatric HNRMS.

Methods

Participants

Survivors at least two years after treatment completion were eligible for inclusion if they were treated between 1993 and 2017 for a HNRMS at an age between 0–18 years and did not develop a relapse or a secondary malignancy. Survivors were recruited at three participating centers, at which their original 3D radiotherapy planning CT had to be available. The participating centers were: Great Ormond Street Hospital, United Kingdom; University of Florida Proton Therapy Institute, United States of America; and Amsterdam University Medical Centers, The Netherlands, which transferred its pediatric oncological care to the Princess Máxima Center for pediatric oncology in 2018, The Netherlands.

Clinical data

Clinical data were derived from a previously published multicenter cross-sectional cohort study, investigating late adverse events (AEs) following local treatment for HNRMS in long-term survivors.[1] HNRMS survivors were systematically evaluated for the presence of AEs by a multidisciplinary team. This included assessment of a predefined list of ophthalmological AEs (OAE) (Supplement 2). OAE were graded according to the Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events v 4.0 system. [13] OAE were assessed for both eyes separately.

For these analyses, only OAEs likely caused by irradiation of OARs were included. Epiphora cases were considered only if no pre-radiotherapy nasolacrimal duct obstruction existed, and optic neuropathy cases only if the optic nerve was normal before treatment.

Dosimetry

Ocular OAR were delineated on the original treatment CT scans for both eyes separately by one of the researchers (MM) and checked by another researcher (MH) and a head and neck radiologist. Delineation of the lens, lacrimal gland, optic nerves, optic chiasm, and retina was performed according to consensus guidelines [14], [15]. The orbit was delineated as a conus shape from the inlet of the optic nerve, alongside the anatomical bony walls, thus including all orbital soft tissues and ocular structures. The nasolacrimal duct was delineated starting at the lacrimal crest, along the intra-osseous part of the duct, up until the inferior meatus [16]. For each OAR the maximum dose to 0.01 cm3 (Dmax), minimal dose received by 99% of the OAR volume (Dmin), and the mean dose (Dmean) were exported from the original treatment plans.

Radiotherapy was delivered as external beam radiation with photons (XRT), external beam radiation with protons (PBT), or brachytherapy (BT) as part of the AMORE strategy [17]. The AMORE strategy combines macroscopically radical surgery of the residual tumor after induction chemotherapy, with post-operative brachytherapy, followed by surgical reconstruction of the defect. Prescription doses ranged from 36 to 55.8 Gy, depending on the protocol and risk group. XRT and PBT were delivered with 1.8 Gy fractions. Through May 2002, BT was delivered using low-dose rate (LDR) and subsequently as pulsed dose rate (PDR) [18]. In order to compare all treatment strategies and fractionation schemes, delivered physical doses were recalculated into the equivalent dose in 2 Gy fractions (GyEQD2). The relative biological effectiveness (RBE) was set at 1.0 for XRT and BT and at 1.1 for PBT [19]. A half-time of recovery of 1.5 h was used to describe the biological response between pulses for BT (i.e. during LDR dose delivery and between pulses for PDR dose delivery) [20]. The α/β ratios used were: 1 Gy for the lens; 2 Gy for the optic nerve, optic chiasm and orbit; 3 Gy for the lacrimal gland, retina and nasolacrimal duct [5], [21].

Analyses

We hypothesized that each eye could be treated as an independent observation. To test this, we calculated intra-class correlation coefficients (ICCs) from multilevel logistic regression models for each OAE. ICC < 0.5 indicated minimal clustering, supporting independence. Consequently, both OAE assessment and delineation of ocular OAR were performed for both eyes separately.

OAE were dichotomized into absent/present (e.g., grade 0 versus grade ≥ 1). Differences in median dose (Dmin, Dmean and Dmax) to the different OAR between the survivors with- and without the associated OAE were tested with the Mann-Whitney U test.

Spearman’s rho was used to assess the correlation between OAE and OAR dose parameters (Dmax, Dmean, Dmin). The parameter with the strongest correlation was used in a univariable logistic regression to model OAE risk and generate dose-probability curves. Model fit was evaluated using ROC curves, with AUC ≥ 0.8 indicating good fit.

Selection of dosimetric parameters

The correlation between the different doses to the OAR and associated OAE was weak to moderate 0.1 – 0.6 (Table A.1). Since the strength of the correlation per OAE did not differ between the assessed doses (Dmax, Dmean, Dmin), we chose to follow the clinical custom and use Dmean for the OAE globe displacement, dry eyes, epiphora and cataract and Dmax for the OAE retinopathy and RION.

Evaluation of details

Because of the low number of events, we could not describe separate dose-toxicity curves for different grades of OAE. Fig. A.1 gives a visual presentation of the different grades within the dose-toxicity curve for dry eyes and cataract. Dry eyes grade 2 was not seen following doses below a Dmean of 14 GyEQD to the lacrimal gland. All but one (91%) of the eyes with cataract grade ≥ 3 had received a Dmean > 10 GyEQD2 to the lens and 45% a Dmean > 20 GyEQD2.

In two eyes, retinopathy was observed. The first eye belonged to a survivor treated at the age of 7.7y, and developed a grade 3 retinopathy following a Dmax 78 GyEQD (Dmean 23 GyEQD2) to the retina. The second eye belonged to a survivor who was treated at the age of 2.6y and developed a grade 1 retinopathy following a Dmax 41GyEQD (Dmean 39 GyEQD2) to the retina. Of N = 22 included eyes that received a Dmax > 40 GyEQD2 to the retina, 2 (9%) developed retinopathy.

In two other eyes, RION grade 4 was observed. The first eye belonged to a survivor who was treated at the age of 0.8y and received Dmax optic nerve 53GyEQD2 (Dmean 48GyEQD2, Dmax optic chiasm 49GyEQD2) to the optic nerve. The second survivor who had RION grade 4 in one eye, was treated at the age of 4.5y and received a Dmax of 59 GyEQD2 (Dmean of 57 GyEQD2, Dmax optic chiasm 53 GyEQD2) to the optic nerve. Of the N = 13 included eyes that received a Dmax > 50GyEQD2 to the optic nerve, 2 (15%) developed RION.

Discussion

We offer a detailed description of pediatric dose-toxicity probability for multiple ophthalmological OAR based on clinically assessed OAE in long-term HNRMS survivors. The dose-toxicity curves for the lens, lacrimal gland, and orbit show a gradual increase in probability of toxicity with increasing dose, without a clear threshold dose. Every dose reduction to these OAR could be relevant in decreasing the probability of developing cataract, dry eyes, and globe displacement. Based on a > 20% probability of clinical toxicity, we propose constraining the Dmean < 6, <10, <40 GyEQD2 to the lens, lacrimal gland, and orbit, respectively. Besides that, we have shown that none of the eyes that received a Dmax < 40 and < 50 GyEQD2 to the retina and optic nerve developed retinopathy or radiation-induced optic neuropathy respectively.

Previous studies report lens radiosensitivity, with cataracts developing after doses of 2.5–5 Gy [10], [12], [22], and increased risk at mean doses > 10–15 Gy. Although based on an estimated lens-dose, One Childhood Cancer Survivor Study even suggests cataract formation at doses as low as 0.5 Gy [22]. Our results align with these data. All cataract cases in our cohort occurred in survivors treated before age 10. However, the majority (81%) of survivors were treated before this age. Due to the low number of cases, we could not test if age at treatment is an independent risk factor for OAE development. In addition to radiation, alkylating agents have been identified as a cataract risk factor [10]. All our survivors received protocols including Ifosfamide or Cyclophosphamide. Two survivors received high lens doses (39 and 47 GyEQD2) but showed no cataracts at assessment at respectively 2 and 3 years post-treatment. As cataracts may develop after a median latency of 4 years, these individuals remain at risk [23], [24].

The dose-toxicity relationship for retinopathy has been described in adults, with current guidelines recommending a retinal Dmax < 45 Gy [5], [6], [7]. A recent PENTEC (Pediatric Normal Tissue Effects in the Clinic) ocular task force publication [25] generated normal tissue complication probability models from data of 5 pediatric studies, concluding that a dose of 42 Gy corresponds to a 5% risk of retinopathy. In addition to dose, irradiated retinal volume was found to influence toxicity in a small study using estimated retinal dose [26]. In our cohort, both eyes that developed retinopathy received retinal Dmax > 40 GyEQD2, with Dmean of 23 and 39 GyEQD2 and Dmin of 10 and 38 GyEQD2, suggesting substantial retinal volume exposure. Although full DVH curves were unavailable, four eyes received very high retinal Dmax (80–220 GyEQD2) without retinopathy. These were all brachytherapy cases under the AMORE strategy, where Dmin was low (<8 GyEQD2), indicating only a small retinal volume was exposed to high doses.

Current planning guidelines for adults recommend a Dmax < 50–60 Gy to the optic nerves and chiasm [3], [4], [5], [6] with RION incidence reported at 7–20% above 60 Gy [4]. The PENTEC review [25] as described here above, included three studies describing optic neuropathy and estimated a 5% risk of optic neuropathy at 57 Gy. In our cohort, among 13 eyes with Dmax ≥ 53 GyEQD2, two developed RION (14% prevalence), compared to 0% in eyes receiving < 53 GyEQD2.

Proposed dose constraints for the lacrimal gland vary across adult studies, with grade ≥ 2 dry eye risk increasing at doses > 15–35 Gy [27], [28], [29] and a 50% probability reported following Dmean 25 – 45 Gy. Radiotherapy treatment guidelines use different dose constraints ranging from Dmean 25 GyEQD2 [5] to Dmax < 40 Gy [7]. In our cohort most dry eye cases were low-grade (grade 1), based on patient reported symptoms. Objective tear production was not measured in our study. In our population, dry eyes may result from reduced tear production due to lacrimal gland damage, tear evaporation from eyelid abnormalities, or poor tear quality [1]. Furthermore, grade 1 dry eyes occur in 5–23% of healthy children/adolescents [30], possibly explaining why our dose-toxicity curve did not start at 0% at 0 GyEQD2. Because of the low number of dry eyes, we were not able to test for the higher grades of dry eyes separately. Due to the low number of cases, we could not analyze higher-grade dry eyes separately. Supplemental Fig. A.1 shows survivors with grade 2–3 dry eye received higher lacrimal gland doses than those with grade 1. OAEs were graded cross-sectionally, and many survivors were already using treatment for dry eyes at assessment, complicating interpretation of radiation-related toxicity.

A study by Kuno et al [31] studied the dose-toxicity relationship between nasolacrimal duct dose and obstruction causing epiphora in 28 adult HN-NSCC patients treated with carbon-ion RT. They identified V40 > 0.08 cm3 as a significant risk factor, with a 94% 5-year incidence of obstruction compared to 0% when V40 < 0.08 cm3. However, all patients received high doses (57.6–64.0 GyRBE), limiting dose constraint characterization. In our cohort, the small number of eyes with epiphora limited analysis. The cases with epiphora present received a Dmean between 9 and 22 GyEQD2 and Dmax between 18 and 30 GyEQD2 to the nasolacrimal gland.

Hol et al [32] reported that Dmax > 40 GyRBE to the orbital rim significantly reduces orbital volume in children. In our analysis, globe displacement, specifically enophthalmos, was included as a clinical OAE, mainly due to soft tissue hypoplasia and volume loss. We found a Dmean of 40 GyEQD2 to the orbit resulted in a probability of 20% for enophthalmus. A Dmean of 40 GyEQD2 to the orbit corresponded to a 20% probability of enophthalmos. One case showed globe displacement at Dmean 9 GyEQD2, but this patient also underwent resection of the temporal muscle and zygomatic bone, indicating non-radiation-related causes. Additionally, one outlier (marked with a star in Fig. 1) received Dmean 120 GyEQD2 due to brachytherapy for orbital RMS with bony involvement as part of the AMORE strategy; the high dose reflects a hotspot near the catheter.

Limitations

Our results are based on small patient numbers per OAE, limiting statistical power. While the AUC curves indicate good internal model fit, external generalizability requires validation in larger cohorts, as reflected by wide 95% CIs in the dose-toxicity curves. Notably, findings for retinopathy and RION are based on only two cases each, with most patients receiving relatively low OAR doses. However, the absence of these OAEs at lower doses remains clinically informative.

Small sample sizes also prevented multivariable analyses, which could have explored the impact of age at diagnosis and follow-up duration. Besides that, radiation volume parameters were not included, though these may influence dose-toxicity relationships—especially for larger OARs like the orbit, retina, and optic nerves [33].

Importantly, 43% of the cohort received brachytherapy as part of the AMORE strategy, which includes surgery. Surgical extent varied from tumor excision to major resections and reconstructions (n = 8). While many OAEs (e.g., cataract, retinopathy, RION) are likely radiation-induced, surgical factors may contribute to globe displacement and dry eyes—particularly in cases involving eyelid abnormalities.

Conclusion

We offer a detailed description of pediatric dose-toxicity probability for the orbit, lacrimal gland, lens, retina and optic nerve, based on clinically assessed OAE (globe displacement, dry eyes, cataract, retinopathy and radiation-induced optic neuropathy) in long-term HNRMS survivors. These data can be used to inform physicians and future patients and families about expected long-term toxicity, necessary surveillance, and potential follow-up care. Our results need validation in a larger cohort, but can then be used to quantify expected variation in clinical toxicity between different treatment techniques when making a treatment plan for an individual patient.
