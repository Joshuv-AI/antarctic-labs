Textured phase diagrams of featureless insulators
Sashank Singam, Nick G. Jones, Abhishodh Prakash

Abstract
We study phase diagrams of charge-conserving ‘class A’ non-interacting fermions, focusing on the trivial phase in various dimensions. Such phases are usually termed ‘featureless’ to distinguish them from those others with either symmetry-broken or topological order. We show that the presence of non-trivial topological families of states, including charge pumps and their generalizations, results in phase diagrams being endowed with non-trivial topological textures that can be visualized through Berry phases and their higher-dimensional generalizations. We show that for non-interacting fermion systems with translation invariance, these ‘higher’ Berry phases can be computed using integrals of non-abelian Chern-Simons forms of the Berry-Bloch connection over momentum and parameter spaces. Singularities in these textures correspond to gap-closing loci of ‘diabolical points’, which represent the obstruction to contracting topologically non-trivial families of states, and bulk-boundary correspondence results in a locus of robust boundary modes that terminate at the bulk diabolical points. In the presence of finite chemical potential, we argue that the edge modes are generically robust without any need for fine-tuning for two and higher dimensions, whereas in one dimension they are ‘estranged’ in the phase diagram, i.e. appearing at different parameter values for different edges. We demonstrate our results by constructing several microscopic models of non-interacting fermions. We argue stability to interactions and explore proximate phase diagrams by mapping to continuum field theories.

Introduction and overview of results

Over the past decades, topological states of matter

Any system with a ground state not satisfying this property is, by definition, non-trivial. Trivial phases are often also called ‘featureless’ due to the absence of long-range order of the kind resulting when symmetries are spontaneously broken and the absence of topological order. We can also obtain the vortex texture using field theory. The two masses in

For finite chemical potential, the first-quantized continuum Hamiltonian becomes

For

We now turn to generalizations of the Rice-Mele model in higher dimensions. To build such models, a recipe was outlined by Kitaev
32
;
33
and formalized as the so-called suspension construction in Ref.
71
. This recipe takes as input a non-trivial topological Hamiltonian family
\mathrm{H}^{[d]}(\vec{\lambda}_{q})
in
d
spatial dimensions parametrized over a
q

\mathcal{M}_{q}
with coordinates
\vec{\lambda}_{q}
. From this, it constructs a Hamiltonian family
\mathrm{H}^{[d+1]}(\vec{\lambda}_{q+1})
in
d+1
spatial dimensions over a
q+1
dimensional parameter space
\mathcal{M}_{q+1}\cong\Sigma\mathcal{M}_{q}
, called the

of

. We briefly review the details of this construction here.

Let
\mathrm{H}^{[d]}(\vec{\lambda})
be a non-trivial topological family with a unique ground state on any closed spatial manifold for all values of
\vec{\lambda}_{q}
. Such states are termed ‘invertible’

\mathrm{H}^{[d]}_{\text{inv}}(\vec{\lambda}_{q})

; that is, it can be adiabatically deformed to a Hamiltonian
\mathrm{H}_{0}

with a unique ground state that does not vary with
\vec{\lambda}
,

\displaystyle\mathrm{H}^{[d]}(\vec{\lambda}_{q})\oplus\mathrm{H}^{[d]}_{\text{inv}}(\vec{\lambda}_{q})\sim\mathrm{H}_{0}\ .

(31)

Given
\mathrm{H}^{[d]}(\vec{\lambda}_{q})
, to construct
\mathrm{H}^{[d+1]}(\vec{\lambda}_{q+1})
we alternatively stack
\mathrm{H}^{[d]}(\vec{\lambda}_{q})
and its inverse along a new spatial direction

8

\lambda_{q+1}
that couples the neighbouring layers and deforms them into the trivial family in two different ways, as schematically shown in
Fig.

8
. Given
\mathrm{H}^{[d]}(\vec{\lambda}_{q})
, this iterative procedure in principle produces models for us in all higher spatial dimensions
\mathrm{H}^{[d+1]}(\vec{\lambda}_{q+1}),\mathrm{H}^{[d+2]}(\vec{\lambda}_{q+2}),\dots
. We will call these the
ascendants
of
\mathrm{H}^{[d]}(\vec{\lambda}_{q})
.

III.3
Mathematical underpinnings

Here we comment on the mathematics that underpins the suspension construction. We will simply state the main results relevant to this work and direct the interested reader to Refs
32
;
33
;
34
;
35
;
15
;
73
for more details.

Consider the space of Hamiltonians with fixed attributes (i.e.
d
spatial dimensions, bosons or fermions, global symmetries) having a unique ground state on any spatial manifold without boundaries. Call
\mathscr{I}_{d}
the space of such
invertible
ground states.
\mathscr{I}_{d}
includes ground states of the trivial phase and also non-trivial invertible phases
15
;
73
such as topological insulators and the integer quantum Hall states. We are interested in topological properties of
\mathscr{I}_{d}
. Its disconnected components,
\pi_{0}(\mathscr{I}_{d})
, are nothing but the
d
-dimensional invertible phases. In this work, we are interested in
\pi_{1}(\mathscr{I}_{d}),\pi_{2}(\mathscr{I}_{d}),\ldots
. Kitaev proposed that the full topology of
\mathscr{I}_{d}
is classified by generalized cohomology
32
;
33
;
34
. In particular, the collection of spaces,
\{\mathscr{I}_{d}\}
,
d=0,1,\ldots
are conjectured to form a so-called
\Omega
-spectrum

15
;
71
;
13
;
39
. This means that the space of loops of
\mathscr{I}_{d}
, denoted
\Omega\mathscr{I}_{d}
has the same topology as that of
\mathscr{I}_{d-1}
. That is, there exist homotopy equivalences,

\displaystyle\mathscr{I}_{d-1}\overset{\sim}{\rightarrow}\Omega\mathscr{I}_{d}\ .

(38)

This allows us to determine the homotopy groups of
\mathscr{I}_{d}
as follows. Recall that
\pi_{n}(\mathscr{I}_{d})
are homotopy classes of based maps from
S^{n}
to
\mathscr{I}_{d}
, denoted by
[S^{n},\mathscr{I}_{d}]
. From
Eq.

38
, we have that

\displaystyle\pi_{n}(\mathscr{I}_{d})=[S^{n},\mathscr{I}_{d}]=[S^{n},\Omega\mathscr{I}_{d+1}]\ .

(39)

Using the Freudenthal suspension theorem
13
, we have

[S^{n},\Omega\mathscr{I}_{d+1}]=[\Sigma S^{n},\mathscr{I}_{d+1}]\ ,

(40)

where
\Sigma
is the so-called reduced suspension
21
;
71
which relates manifolds of different dimensions. In particular, it relates
n-
spheres as
\Sigma S^{n}\cong S^{n+1}
. Thus we have

\displaystyle\pi_{n}(\mathscr{I}_{d})=[S^{n+1},\mathscr{I}_{d+1}]=\pi_{n+1}(\mathscr{I}_{d+1})\ .

(41)

This tells us that non-trivial families of states in
d
spatial dimensions over
S^{n}
imply the existence of non-trivial families of states over
S^{n+1}
in
d+1
spatial dimensions and so on. The suspension construction of Ref.
71
reviewed above is a physical way of constructing models for the latter given the former.

IV.4
Stable edge modes

Figure 15:
Boundary conditions used to study edge modes

For the Rice-Mele model, we were able to show that the topological texture in the phase diagram was associated, through a bulk-boundary correspondence with edge modes of an unusual variety, which we termed ‘estranged’. We now study the edge modes present in the two-dimensional ascendant with open boundaries. We will consider the system on a finite cylinder, retaining periodic boundary conditions in one direction,
x_{1}
, but terminating in the
x_{2}
direction, as shown in
Fig.

15

\delta_{2}=1
. This decouples the Hilbert space that lives at the circular ends of the cylinder with a width of one lattice point. The effective Hamiltonian of the boundary Hilbert space is nothing but the one-dimensional Rice-Mele model
\mathrm{H}_{\text{RM}}
, with parameters
J,~\delta=\delta_{1},~\mu
.

Let us begin with
\mu=0
. We know that
\mathrm{H}_{\text{RM}}
is gapless when
J=\delta_{1}=0
, which tells us that its higher dimensional ascendant
\mathrm{H}^{[2]}_{\text{RM}}
has gapless boundary modes for
J=\delta_{1}=0
and
\delta_{2}=1
, described by a free massless Dirac fermion. Even as we move away from
\delta_{2}=1
, along
0<\delta_{2}<1
, we expect these boundary modes to persist on the line
J=\delta_{1}=0
over a Hilbert space that is localized on the edges of the cylinder, but with a larger width. The edge modes terminate on the bulk gapless point on the origin
J=\delta_{1}=\delta_{2}=0
. The resulting phase diagram is shown in
Fig.

10
(a). For the 1d Rice-Mele model, the edge modes were obtained by tuning one parameter, whereas for the 2d model, at
\mu=0
, we see that we need to fine-tune two parameters to access the edge modes. This picture changes significantly for
\mu\neq 0
.

Indeed for non-zero
\mu
, the chemical potential is raised for both the bulk
and boundary
. To see the consequence of this, let us again begin at
\delta_{2}=1
parametric plane, where we have the Rice-Mele model at finite chemical potential. This has an extended region of gapless states in the circular disc
\delta_{1}^{2}+J^{2}<\mu^{2}
described by a massless Dirac fermion. This disc is expected to extend along
0<\delta_{2}\leq 1
and terminates on the equator of the bulk metallic phase at the origin, as shown in
Fig.

10
(b). We see that unlike the Rice-Mele model in 1d, the edge modes of the 2d ascendant are parametrically stable for generic
\mu\neq 0
, requiring no fine-tuning whatsoever. This is reminiscent of strong topological free-fermion phases, such as the quantum spin Hall insulator

which are characterized by similarly stable edge modes. In fact, the edge modes of 2d QSH systems have the same boundary degrees of freedom i.e. a massless Dirac fermion! Unlike the QSH phase, whose
topologically protected
edge modes can only be eliminated by a bulk gap closure, the edge modes of the 2d Rice-Mele ascendant can disappear either by a bulk transition (when it meets the bulk metal near the origin of the phase diagram) or a boundary Lifshitz transition (which completely empties or fills up the boundary bands) as seen in
Fig.

10
(a).

IV.5
Stability to band deformations

Figure 16:
Adiabatic deformation to a flat-band limit.

We have shown that the free-fermion Hamiltonian family in
Eqs.

and
44

Eqs.

42
and
44

One may wonder if this degeneracy, and the accidental symmetries protecting it, are necessary to preserve the integer invariant and texture in the phase diagram. The answer is no. The only symmetry that needs to be preserved is the
\mathrm{U}(1)
particle conservation since this symmetry gives meaning to the charge from which all the textures ascend. All other symmetries can be safely broken
3
3

3

Lattice translations allow us to use band theory but even this can be broken weakly. An analysis of this case would require more sophisticated tools
36
;
60
. In particular, we can introduce the following perturbation on the single particle Hamiltonian,

44

\displaystyle\delta h=m_{s}\left(\sigma^{1}\otimes\sigma^{2}\right).

(55)

The phase diagram of this perturbed system can again be analyzed easily by studying its bands. We set
\mu=0
for simplicity. So long as we are sufficiently far from the origin,
J^{2}+\delta_{1}^{2}+\delta_{2}^{2}>m_{s}^{2}
, the many-body ground states are adiabatically connected to the original
m_{s}=0
system, except that the filled bands characterizing them are no longer degenerate. How then do we infer the topological nature of the ground state family? As explained in
53
, as we continuously deform away from the limit of exact degeneracy and, as long as there is no level crossing at the Fermi level, the topological nature of the ground states remains the same. In particular, to compute and analyze the non-abelian Berry connection in
Eq.

48
, we do not need the filled levels to be degenerate.

Conversely, consider a generic Bloch Hamiltonian family
\mathrm{h}(\vec{\lambda},k)
with eigenstates and eigenvalues
\{\mbox{$|\varepsilon_{a}(\vec{\lambda},\vec{k})\rangle$},\varepsilon_{a}(\vec{\lambda},\vec{k})\}
. So long as this represents a family of gapped states with no partially filled bands, we can always deform this system to one described by a spectrally flattened Bloch Hamiltonian,

\displaystyle\mathrm{q}(\vec{\lambda},\vec{k})=\sum_{a}\frac{\varepsilon_{a}(\vec{\lambda},\vec{k})}{|\varepsilon_{a}(\vec{\lambda},\vec{k})|}|\varepsilon_{a}(\vec{\lambda},\vec{k})\rangle\langle\varepsilon_{a}(\vec{\lambda},\vec{k})|\ ,

(56)

and perform the analysis as in the unperturbed, degenerate case.

Let us return to Berry’s original spin model

75

;

;

;

;
46
;
47
;

;
62
,

;

;

;
62

;

;
46
;
46
;

80

is characterized by the second Chern invariant
\mathsf{C}_{2}(S^{3}_{\text{par}}\times S^{1}_{\text{BZ}})\in{\mathbb{Z}}

\mathsf{C}_{2}(S^{3}_{\text{par}}\times S^{1}_{\text{BZ}})
and
\mathrm{DD}(S^{3}_{\text{par}})

We begin by identifying the cohomology groups which they are valued in,
\mathsf{C}_{2}(\mathcal{N})\in\mathrm{H}^{4}(\mathcal{N},{\mathbb{Z}})

43
and
\mathrm{DD}(\mathcal{M})\in\mathrm{H}^{3}(\mathcal{M},{\mathbb{Z}})

;

;
46
;
46
;
47

\mathcal{M}=\mathcal{M}_{3}
a three-manifold representing parameter-space and
\mathcal{N}=\mathcal{M}_{3}\times S^{1}
is a 4-manifold where
S^{1}
represents the one-dimensional Brillouin zone. We have so far been considering
\mathcal{M}_{3}=S^{3}
for concreteness but the present argument works for any general 3-manifold
\mathcal{M}_{3}
. We now invoke the the Künneth formula
21
,

\mathrm{H}^{\ell}(\mathcal{M}\times\mathcal{N},{\mathbb{Z}})\cong\bigoplus_{p+q=\ell}\mathrm{H}^{p}(\mathcal{M},{\mathbb{Z}})\otimes_{{\mathbb{Z}}}\mathrm{H}^{q}(\mathcal{N},{\mathbb{Z}})\ .

For

\mathcal{M}=\mathcal{M}_{3}

\mathcal{N}=S^{1}

105

21

\displaystyle\mathrm{H}^{1}(S^{1},{\mathbb{Z}})\cong{\mathbb{Z}},~\mathcal{A}\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}{\mathbb{Z}}\cong\mathcal{A}

(106)

we get

\displaystyle\mathrm{H}^{4}(\mathcal{M}_{3}\times S^{1},{\mathbb{Z}})\cong\mathrm{H}^{3}(\mathcal{M}_{3},{\mathbb{Z}})\ .

(107)

This tells us that the Chern invariant we compute for non-interacting systems is identical to the Dixmier-Douady invariant that is evaluated in the presence of interactions.

This can, in fact be generalized to higher dimensions. Recall that for
d
spatial dimensions, the topological invariant for our free-fermion ascendant is measured by the Chern number
\mathsf{C}_{d+1}\in\mathrm{H}^{2d+2}(\mathcal{M}_{d+2}\times T^{d},{\mathbb{Z}})

105
with

\mathcal{M}=\mathcal{M}_{d+2},

\mathcal{N}=T^{d}
, we get that only the

Eq.

105

Eq.

106
, we finally get

\displaystyle\mathrm{H}^{2d+2}(\mathcal{M}_{d+2}\times T^{d},{\mathbb{Z}})\cong\mathrm{H}^{d+2}(\mathcal{M}_{d+2},{\mathbb{Z}})\ .

(108)

\mathrm{H}^{d+2}(\mathcal{M}_{d+2},{\mathbb{Z}})
is a topological invariant that can be extracted from a higher Gerbe structure
52

\mathsf{C}_{d+1}\in\mathrm{H}^{2d+2}(\mathcal{M}_{d+2}\times T^{d},{\mathbb{Z}})
.

\pi_{0}(\mathscr{I}_{d})=\pi_{q}(\mathscr{I}_{d+q})\ .

. This gives rise to textures over
d+2
spheres in parameter space, in
d
spatial dimensions due to

\pi_{2}(\mathscr{I}_{0})=\pi_{d+2}(\mathscr{I}_{d}).

(110)

This tells us that non-trivial families need not be associated with any phases. Together, the conjectural classification of topological families of invertible states can be completely determined from the knowledge of (i) invertible phases in all spatial dimensions
\pi_{0}(\mathscr{I}_{d})
and (ii) all topological families for
d=0
spatial dimensions
\pi_{q}(\mathscr{I}_{0})
.

VII
The Qi-Wu-Zhang model, Chern insulator pump and ascendants

VII.1
The Qi-Wu-Zhang model

Figure 22:
Phase diagram of the Qi-Wu-Zhang model (
111
). This exhibits three phases with the filled band exhibiting Chern number
\mathsf{C}_{1}=0,\pm 1
in its occupied band.

The final series of textured phase diagrams we will consider in this work ascends from the Chern insulator phase in
d=2
. Let us begin with a review of the Qi-Wu-Zhang model
54
which hosts various Chern insulator phases. The Hamiltonian is as follows:

\mathrm{H}_{\text{QWZ}}=\sum_{\vec{x}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}}\sum_{\alpha,\beta\in\{\uparrow,\downarrow\}}\biggl[mc_{\alpha}^{\dagger}(\vec{x})\sigma^{3}_{\alpha\beta}c_{\beta}(\vec{x})\\
-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{a=1,2}c^{\dagger}(\vec{x})\left({\sigma^{3}_{\alpha\beta}+i\sigma^{a}_{\alpha\beta}}\right)c(\vec{x}+\hat{e}_{a})+h.c\biggr]\ .

(111)

The Bloch Hamiltonian corresponds to

\mathrm{h}(\vec{k})=\vec{g}(\vec{k})\cdot\vec{\sigma},~\text{where }g_{1}=\sin k_{1},\ g_{2}=\sin k_{2},\\
g_{3}=(m+\cos k_{1}+\cos k_{2})\ .~~~~

(112)

The system consists of two energy bands with dispersion
\varepsilon_{\pm}(\vec{k})=\pm|\vec{g}(\vec{k})|
which are separated for all values of
m
except at
m=0,\pm 2
. The phase corresponding to the many-body ground state can be determined by computing the Chern number for the abelian
\mathrm{U}(1)
connection,
A
, constructed for the single filled band and evaluated over the Brillouin zone
T^{2}_{\text{BZ}}

\displaystyle\mathsf{C}_{1}=\int_{T^{2}_{\text{BZ}}}\frac{F}{2\pi}\ .

(113)

The resulting phase diagram, shown in
Fig.

22
consists of two Chern insulators with
\mathsf{C}_{1}=\pm 1
(for
m\in(0,\pm 2)
respectively) and a trivial insulator with
\mathsf{C}_{1}=0
(for
|m|>2
).

VII.2
The 3d QWZ ascendant

Figure 23:
Single particle band dispersions for the

m,\delta,m

We will now study the ascendants of the QWZ model using the suspension construction. First, we need to identify the inverse of
\mathrm{H}_{\text{QWZ}}
for a fixed value of
m
. This turns out to be
-\mathrm{H}_{\text{QWZ}}
. Using the same arguments as in the Rice-Mele and Berry series, the
d=3
dimensional ascendant of the QWZ model can be written as

\mathrm{H}^{[3]}_{\text{QWZ}}=\sum_{x_{3}\in\mathbb{Z}}(-1)^{x_{3}}\mathrm{H}_{\text{QWZ},x_{3}}+\mu\sum_{\vec{x}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{3}}n(x)\\
+\sum_{\vec{x}\in\mathbb{Z}^{3}}\sum_{\alpha\in\{\uparrow,\downarrow\}}\left(\frac{1+(-1)^{x_{3}}\delta}{2}\right)(c^{\dagger}_{\alpha}(\vec{x})c_{\alpha}(\vec{x}+\hat{e}_{3})+h.c)\ .

(114)

Expanding
\mathrm{H}_{\text{QWZ}}
, we get,

\mathrm{H}^{[3]}_{\text{QWZ}}=\sum_{\vec{x}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{3}}\sum_{\alpha,\beta\in\{\uparrow,\downarrow\}}\sum_{a=1}^{3}t^{a}_{\alpha\beta}(\vec{x})(c^{\dagger}_{\alpha}(\vec{x})c_{\beta}(\vec{x}+\hat{e}_{a})+h.c)\\
+\sum_{\vec{x}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{3}}\sum_{\alpha,\beta\in\{\uparrow,\downarrow\}}\mu_{\alpha\beta}(\vec{x})c^{\dagger}_{\alpha}(\vec{x})c_{\beta}(\vec{x})\ ,

(115)

where

\displaystyle t^{1,2}_{\alpha\beta}

\displaystyle=(-1)^{1+x_{3}}\left(\frac{\sigma^{3}_{\alpha\beta}+i\sigma^{1,2}_{\alpha\beta}}{2}\right),

\displaystyle t^{3}_{\alpha\beta}

\displaystyle=\delta_{\alpha\beta}\left(\frac{1+(-1)^{x_{3}}\delta}{2}\right),

\displaystyle\mu_{\alpha\beta}

\displaystyle=\mu\delta_{\alpha\beta}+(-1)^{x_{3}}m~\sigma^{3}_{\alpha\beta}\ .

(116)

This has a two-site translation symmetry in the
x_{3}
direction and a single site translation symmetry in
x_{1},x_{2}
, as well as
\mathrm{U}(1)
particle conservation symmetry. Choosing a two-site fiducial unit cell,
Eq.

115
can be written in momentum space as a four-band model (including the spin indices) with the following Bloch Hamiltonian

\displaystyle\mathrm{h}(\vec{k})=\mu\mathbb{1}+\sum_{m=1}^{5}g_{m}(\vec{k})\Gamma^{m}\ ,

(117)

where, corresponding to a particular choice of unit cell, we have,

g_{1,2}=\sin k_{1,2},\ g_{3}=\left(\frac{1-\delta}{2}\right)+\left(\frac{1+\delta}{2}\right)\cos{k_{3}}\\
g_{4}=\left(\frac{1+\delta}{2}\right)\sin k_{3},\ g_{5}=m+\cos k_{1}+\cos k_{2}\ .

(118)

The
\{\Gamma_{m}\}
are elements of the Clifford algebra
\mathrm{Cl}(5)
with matrix representation,

\displaystyle\Gamma_{1}

\displaystyle=\sigma_{3}\otimes\sigma_{1},\ \Gamma_{2}=\sigma_{3}\otimes\sigma_{2},

\displaystyle\Gamma_{3}

\displaystyle=\sigma_{1}\otimes\mathbb{1},~~\Gamma_{4}=\sigma_{2}\otimes\mathbb{1},\ \Gamma_{5}=\sigma_{3}\otimes\sigma_{3}\ .

(119)

VII.3
Field theory and proximate phase diagrams

Figure 26:
The addition of singlet masses
m_{0},~m_{1}
shown in

2

1

(m,\delta)=\pm(m_{0},m_{1})

We now study the 3d QWZ ascendant using effective field theory. We will consider a low-energy continuum description in the vicinity of the diabolical points. Let us begin with
m\approx\pm 2,\delta\approx 0
where the continuum Lagrangian density in Euclidean space can be written as

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}=-\bar{\psi}\not{\partial}\psi-\delta\bar{\psi}\psi+i(m\pm 2)\bar{\psi}\gamma_{c}\psi\ .

(122)

Here,
\psi
is a four component spinor field and
Eq.

122
describes a relativistic Dirac fermion in 3+1 d. The couplings
\delta
and
m
correspond to the ordinary and chiral mass terms, which saturate the space of relevant operators. Thus, in the vicinity of
m\approx\pm 2,\delta\approx 0
, the phase diagram is qualitatively unchanged for arbitrary weak perturbations, including interactions.

Near
m\approx 0,~\delta\approx 0,
the continuum Lagrangian density can be written as

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}=-\sum_{a=1}^{2}\bar{\psi}_{a}\not{\partial}\psi_{a}-\sum_{a,b=1}^{2}\tau^{3}_{ab}\left(\delta\bar{\psi}_{a}\psi_{b}-im\bar{\psi}_{a}\gamma_{c}\psi_{b}\right).

(123)

Eq.

123
is a
N_{f}=2
component Dirac fermion where
\delta
and
m
couple to one of the components of the the ordinary and chiral triplet masses. However, the space of independent relevant perturbations for
N_{f}=2
Dirac fermions is three dimensional and the diabolical point is not stable. We can add, for instance, the singlet ordinary and chiral mass

\displaystyle\delta\mathcal{L}=\sum_{a=1}^{2}\left(m_{1}\bar{\psi}_{a}\psi_{a}-im_{0}\bar{\psi}_{a}\gamma_{c}\psi_{a}\right).

(124)

For the microscopic Bloch Hamiltonian in
Eq.

117
, this perturbation takes the form

\displaystyle\delta\mathrm{h}=\frac{1}{2}(\cos k_{1}-\cos k_{2})(m_{1}\Gamma_{3}-m_{0}\Gamma_{5}).

(125)

It is easy to check that this results in the
N_{f}=2
gapless point at
m=\delta=0
splitting into two
N_{f}=1
points at
(m,\delta)=\pm(m_{0},m_{1})
. For the overall microscopic phase diagram, this splits the core of the charge 2 vortex into two charge 1 vortices as shown in
Fig.

26
. We can also consider adding in other triplet mass components. These merely move the location of various diabolical points and add smooth changes to the texture.

VII.4
QWZ ascendants in arbitrary dimensions

The higher dimensional ascendants
\mathrm{H}_{\text{QWZ}}^{[d]}
for
d\geq 3
are constructed from the lower-dimensional model
\mathrm{H}_{\text{QWZ}}^{[d-1]}
using the suspension recipe,

\mathrm{H}^{[d]}_{\text{QZW}}=\mu\sum_{\vec{x}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}n(\vec{x})+\sum_{x_{d}\in\mathbb{Z}}(-1)^{x_{d}}\mathrm{H}^{[d-1]}_{\text{QWZ},x_{d}}|_{\mu=0}\\
+\sum_{x_{d}\in\mathbb{Z}}\left(\frac{1+(-1)^{x_{d}}\delta_{d-2}}{2}\right)(c^{\dagger}(\vec{x})c(\vec{x}+\hat{e}_{d})+h.c)\ .

(126)

An explicit form can be given by iteratively repeating the above, and expanding out the Hamiltonian to get

\mathrm{H}^{[d]}_{\text{QWZ}}=\sum_{\vec{x}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}}\sum_{\alpha,\beta\in\{\uparrow,\downarrow\}}\sum_{a=1}^{d}t^{a}_{\alpha\beta}(\vec{x})(c^{\dagger}_{\alpha}(\vec{x})c_{\beta}(\vec{x}+\hat{e}_{a})+h.c)\\
+\sum_{\vec{x}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}}\sum_{\alpha,\beta\in\{\uparrow,\downarrow\}}\mu_{\alpha\beta}(\vec{x})c^{\dagger}_{\alpha}(\vec{x})c_{\beta}(\vec{x})\ ,

(127)

where

\displaystyle t^{1,2}_{\alpha\beta}=(-1)^{1+\sum_{k=3}^{d}x_{k}}\left(\frac{\sigma^{3}_{\alpha\beta}+i\sigma^{1,2}_{\alpha\beta}}{2}\right),

\displaystyle t^{a}_{\alpha\beta}=\delta_{\alpha\beta}(-1)^{\sum_{k=a+3}^{d}x_{k}}\left(\frac{1+(-1)^{x_{a}}\delta_{a}}{2}\right),a=3,\ldots,d-1,

\displaystyle t^{d}_{\alpha\beta}=\delta_{\alpha\beta}\left(\frac{1+(-1)^{x_{d}}\delta_{d}}{2}\right),~

\displaystyle\mu_{\alpha\beta}=\mu\delta_{\alpha\beta}+(-1)^{\sum_{k=3}^{d}x_{k}}m~\sigma^{3}_{\alpha\beta}\ .

(128)

Choosing a
2^{d-2}
site unit cell, we get a
2^{d-1}
band model with the Bloch Hamiltonian as follows,

\displaystyle\mathrm{h}(\vec{k})=\mu\mathbb{1}+\sum_{m=1}^{2d-1}g_{m}\Gamma^{m}\ ,

(129)

where for a particular choice of the unit cell, we have

\displaystyle g_{1}

\displaystyle=\sin k_{1},\ g_{2}=\sin k_{2},

\displaystyle g_{2m-3}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{1-\delta_{m-2}}{2}\right)+\left(\frac{1+\delta_{m-2}}{2}\right)\sin k_{2m-1},

\displaystyle g_{2m-2}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{1+\delta_{m-2}}{2}\right)\cos k_{2m-1},\ m=3,\ldots,d

\displaystyle g_{2d-1}

\displaystyle=(-1)^{d+1}(m+\cos k_{1}+\cos k_{2})\ .

(130)

The
\{\Gamma^{m}\}
represent the
2^{d-1}
dimensional irreducible representation of the Clifford algebra
\mathrm{Cl}(2d-1)
satisfying
Eq.

46
whose matrix representation for the same choice of the unit cell is given by

\displaystyle\Gamma^{2m-3}=\underbrace{\sigma^{3}\otimes\sigma^{3}\cdots\otimes\sigma^{3}}_{\text{$d-m$ terms}}\otimes\ \sigma^{1}\otimes\underbrace{\mathbb{1}\otimes\mathbb{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes\mathbb{1}}_{\text{$m-2$ terms}},

\displaystyle\Gamma^{2m-2}=\underbrace{\sigma^{3}\otimes\sigma^{3}\cdots\otimes\sigma^{3}}_{\text{$d-m$ terms}}\otimes\ \sigma^{2}\otimes\underbrace{\mathbb{1}\otimes\mathbb{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes\mathbb{1}}_{\text{$m-2$ terms}}.

\displaystyle\Gamma^{2d-1}=\underbrace{\sigma^{3}\otimes\sigma^{3}\cdots\otimes\sigma^{3}}_{\text{$d-1$ terms}}\ m=2,\ldots,d\ .

(131)

The
d
-dimensional ascendant gives us a non-trivial topological family over a parametric
S^{d-2}_{\text{par}}
which can be detected by computing the Chern number
\mathsf{C}_{d-1}
evaluated over
\mathcal{M}_{2d-2}=S^{d-2}_{\text{par}}\times T^{d}_{\text{BZ}}
. A non-trivial texture in the phase digram can be extracted by evaluating the higher Berry phase
\check{\gamma}
defined over a parametric
S^{d-3}_{\text{par}}
as an integral over the Chern-Simons form
\mathsf{CS}_{2d-3}
over
\mathcal{M}_{2d-3}=S^{d-3}_{\text{par}}\times T^{d}_{\text{BZ}}
.

VIII
Conclusion and outlook

In this work, we have comprehensively analyzed the topological structures of charge conserving systems in Class A using free-fermion representatives. Using topological and geometric invariants, we show that phase diagrams can have topological textures and associated gap closing loci in the bulk and boundary. We have studied three series of textures using microscopic models in arbitrary dimensions constructed using suspension, and analyzed them using band theory and effective field theory. We also make connections between interacting invariants and our free-fermion invariants. Altogether, we show that trivial insulators are not featureless, but have interesting topological structures hidden in phase diagrams.

There are various potential future directions following this work. First, it would be nice to extend our analysis to other symmetry classes of insulators and superconductors and study how topological textures can arise in their phase diagrams. It would also be useful to consider disordered systems and understand how the topological textures can be extracted without access to band theory
36
;
60
, possibly through appropriate local observables. Related to this, it would be interesting to see how topological and geometric invariants that result in phase diagram textures can be measured experimentally. For the 1d Rice-Mele model, the answer to this question follows from the well-known connection between Berry’s phase and charge polarization
29
. For higher dimensions, Berry phases and Chern numbers are expected to be related to nonlinear response coefficients
31
;
30
which would be interesting to study in detail. The main focus of our work has been on invertible phases, particularly the trivial phase, where the conjectured generalized cohomology framework allows us to determine the structure of phase diagrams across dimensions from information about one particular dimension. This advantage is lost for non-invertible phases of matter such as symmetry breaking, gapless, critical and topologically ordered phases. It would be interesting to repeat our study how non-trivial families in such phases
59
;
44
leaves an imprint on phase diagrams. It would also be illuminating to know if textured phase diagrams can occur for classical systems
50
;
69
. We leave these and other questions for future work.
