1 Introduction
Reliable detection of aerosolized pathogens is difficult due to need to distinguish between the benign
bioaerosols such as dander and pollen and the thousands of pathogens capable of infecting people.
Accurate identification of airborne pathogens of concern in the past has required the collection of
aerosol samples in a filter, periodic collection of the samples, and processing and identification in
the laboratory. This process is labor intensive and expensive. Additionally, this method necessarily
has a time to detection window of hours to days depending on the collection frequency. Biolog-
ical pathogens have an incubation period before the onset of symptoms and severe health effects
and/or mortality, people will typically be exposed to them without realizing it. This has resulted
in a detect to treat strategy for protection against bio releases. While prophylactic measures can
still be effective over these time scales, reducing the time to detection will significantly improve
the effectiveness of these measures and subsequently reduce the consequences of a release. Various
attempts to reduce the time to detection and identification have been plagued by highly undesir-
able false-positives which degrade confidence in the system. Zeteotech, LLC has developed a mass
spectrometer based bioaerosol sensing system which is capable of autonomously identifying airborne
pathogens of concern and alert authorities within minutes instead of hours to days. They have de-
ployed these instruments to protect high-risk facilities by alerting authorities of public health events
and intentional bioterrorism events in near real time. This makes it possible to more accurately
identify the time and location of the release and minimize the number of people that are exposed
through prompt quarantining of affected areas.
 Zeteotech is currently relying on physical experiments to identify the "optimal" location of their
sensors. Unfortunately, this is a time and labor intensive process and it is difficult to adequately
explore the possible release scenarios to truly optimize the placement of the sensors.
 Los Alamos National Laboratory has unique expertise and world-class aerosol dispersion mod-
eling tools that could significantly help Zeteotech in optimizing the placement of their systems.
Development of the Quick Urban & Industrial Complex (QUIC) suite of rapid transport and dis-
persion models began in 1998 [7]. Models for transport and dispersion phenomena typically fall
under one of two categories: analytical models that have historically been used by the emergency
response community or high-fidelity computational fluid dynamics (CFD) codes. Analytical models,
e.g. Gaussian plume and/or puff for outdoor environments, are extremely fast but are too simpli-
fied to properly account for anything beyond highly idealized scenarios. CFD approaches model the
governing equations for fluid flow and mass transport directly and subsequently can more accurately
simulate complex phenomena, but at a much higher cost of computational time and resources. The
QUIC models operate under the axiom that it is often preferable to have an 80% correct answer
when you need it than a 100% correct answer when it is too late to be useful. While computational
resources and methods are ever improving, they have yet to progress to the point that makes it pos-
sible to use CFD for all applications. Every second counts in an emergency and other applications
which require large parametric analyses, such as optimal sensor placement, involve large batches
of simulations that would be impractical to perform with high-fidelity CFD. This represents an
important gap in modeling capabilities that QUIC models were designed to fill.
 In order to meet this need for models that are sufficiently sophisticated to simulate complex
transport and dispersion phenomena, while still minimizing the computational requirements, QUIC
models employ parameterizations based on CFD simulations, and physical observations combined
with mass conservation to replicate CFD results. Effectively, the QUIC models rely on subject
matter expertise to accelerate simulations rather than brute computational force to provide CFD-

 The computational core of QUIC Indoor traces its origins to the Emergency Dose program
developed by George Sayre[9] at the University of Texas at Austin in 2008. The original motiva-
tion stemmed from post-9/11 security concerns regarding radiological dispersion devices (RDDs) in
buildings and the need for rapid assessment tools that could predict contaminant transport with
reasonable accuracy. The original authors left LANL and the model was abandoned for years.
In 2020, the COVID-19 pandemic and mass quarantines that were imposed throughout the world
reignited interest in indoor air quality and the transport and dispersion of pathogens within the
indoor environment reignited interest in air quality and the transmission of diseases in enclosed
spaces. This is of particular interest when it was observed that indoor transmission was up to 18.7
times higher indoors than outdoors [2]. We recognized the potential of the Emergency Dose code to
fill the gap between well-mixed zonal models (e.g. MELCOR [5] and CONTAM [3])) that assume
uniform concentration within zones, providing fast results but no spatial resolution within rooms
and high-fidelity CFD (e.g., FLUENT [6], COMSOL [8]) solve full Navier-Stokes equations with
turbulence models, providing high fidelity but requiring days or weeks of computation time. This
prompted the continued development of the model and incorporation of Emergency Dose into the
QUIC suite of models as QUIC Indoor.
 LANL has used the original QUIC-URB/QUIC-PLUME dispersion modeling system has been
used to optimize biological aerosol detectors for decades [1]. The combination of these methods
and expertise with the QUIC Indoor model provides an ideal tool to satisfy Zeteotech’s need to
determine the optimal placement of a limited number of sensors by maximizing the probability of
detection and minimizing the time to detection.

2 QUIC Indoor Theory
2.0.1 Modified Potential Flow Theory
Sayre’s key innovation was the development of Modified Potential Flow Theory (MPFT),
which is the indoor analogue to QUIC-URB [7]. MPFT bridges the gap between well-mixed zonal
models [3, 5] and full CFD. Classical potential flow theory assumes that the flow is inviscid, irro-
tational, and incompressible. Potential flow does not include momentum, pressure gradients, shear
stress, or body forces (gravity) and it is unable to generate turbulence or boundary layers. Instead
the flow field is driven by volumetric sources and sinks. Standard potential flow is computationally
efficient but it lacks the ability to represent momentum from jets, eddies, or buoyancy-driven flows,
all of which are critical to accurately simulating indoor air flow and subsequently the dispersion of
airborne contaminants.
 Similar to the methods used to simulate building induced flow in QUIC-URB by injecting com-
plex flow structures around buildings using empirical parameterizations, MPFT extends classical
theory through two major enhancements:

  1. Turbulent Jet Approximation: Empirical parameterizations for turbulent jets were pro-
 duced by analyzing a series of k-ε simulations in COMSOL [8]. Jets are assumed to be
 axisymmetric and are divided into four regions following Yue [10].

  2. Natural Convection: Buoyancy-driven convective cells from heated surfaces (walls, people,
 equipment) are implemented using heat transfer correlations to produce a series of volumetric
 sources and sinks that direct the potential flow to produce the desired convective cell behavior.

2.0.2 Dual Transport Modules
QUIC Indoor includes both Eulerian and Lagrangian concentration transport and dispersion models:

 • Eulerian Module: Solves advection-diffusion equations throughout the entire simulation
 domain using high-order schemes (SMART [4])

 • Lagrangian Module: Tracks the movement of tracer particles through the velocity field,
 accounting for turbulent dispersion with random perturbations assuming a constant turbulence
 intensity

 Both methods have advantages and disadvantages. The Eulerian solver can be slower but its
dynamic range of concentrations it can resolve is only dependent on the floating point calculations.
The Lagrangian solver can be faster by using fewer tracer particles, but this will also result in higher
minimum concentrations that can be resolved.

2.0.3 Performance Validation
Sayre compared Emergency Dose with a well-mixed zonal model (MELCOR) and high-fidelity CFD
(FLUENT) demonstrating its intermediate fidelity.

 • vs. MELCOR [5]: Emergency Dose was capable of properly simulating the temporal and
 spatial distribution of the plume within a large room, while MELCOR underestimated the
 concentrations near the source and overestimated the concentrations on the other side of the
 room.

 • vs. FLUENT [6]: The largest discrepancy in the velocity field was due to the lack of viscous
 drag in MPFT, however it performed well for turbulent jets.

 • Computation Time: Simulations were performed in seconds to minutes depending on the
 size of the domain, which is significantly faster than CFD simulations.

2.0.4 Evolution to QUIC Indoor
Recent development has significantly improved Emergency Dose since 2008:

 • A Python GUI wrapper was added to assist the user in setting up the complex input files and
 visualizing results.

 • Parallelized batch processing and sensor placement optimization capability was added to the
 GUI.

 • A point cloud (PLY) file import capability was implemented in the GUI to automate the
 generation of geometry from LiDAR scans of building interiors. This significantly improves
 the process of defining the simulation domain, which can be tedious for complex geometries.

 • The Lagrangian solver has been restructured to match the architecture used in QUIC-PLUME
 as this is far more memory efficient than the architecture used in Emergency Dose.

 While these modifications have significantly improved the usability of the code, the core MPFT
solver in QUIC Indoor remains the same to Emergency Dose. The name change to QUIC Indoor
signifies its adoption into the QUIC suite of models following the standard naming conventions while
indicating the focus on the indoor environment.

3 Task Descriptions and Deliverables
The project was separated into five phases.

3.1 Phase I: Define the Project
The first phase focused on identifying the capability requirements. LANL and Zeteotech personnel
have met regularly throughout the project. Due to LANL’s existing capabilities and expertise in
transport and dispersion model development and optimized sensor siting analysis, we identified the
import of the building interior geometry as the largest technical challenge for the project. The
original plan was to develop importers for computer assisted drafting (CAD) files of the facility.
Zeteotech was tasked to inquire about the availability of these CAD files from their customers.
 We determined that the Python programming language would be the best platform for the tools
that would be developed in this project. LANL investigated the availability of Python software
packages with the ability to read CAD files so they could be translated into the QUIC Indoor input
file format.
 After several months of unsuccessful inquiries to Zeteotech’s customers for CAD files of their
facilities, it was determined that an alternate method for acquiring building interior geometry
was required. We identified portable LiDAR scanners as a viable alternative that could either
be purchased or possibly rented to capture the interior geometries of facilities of interest in the
form of point cloud files, i.e., collections of 3D coordinates of the surfaces that reflected the laser.
LANL investigated the availability of software packages for importing and processing point clouds
and identified several candidates and it was subsequently decided to pursue point clouds instead of
CAD files since this would remove external dependencies on the availability of the CAD files, since
the point cloud files can be produced on demand given access to a LiDAR scanner, and CAD files
may not be easily accessible or current. The point cloud PLY format was chosen as it was identified
as being common and well supported in several programming languages.
 The QUIC Indoor code and in particular the input file formats used by the code were identified
as being too opaque and complex for non expert users. They consist of uncommented ASCII text
lines filled with seemingly randomly determined lengths of numeric entries on each line that describe
complex interactions between the various elements of the model: rooms, doors, obstacles, HVAC
supplies and exhaust vents, tracer source locations. It was therefore decided that a graphical user
interface would be needed to make the QUIC Indoor code accessible to the average user without
requiring months of experience.

3.2.1 Point Cloud Importer
The conversion system processes raw LIDAR point cloud data (PLY format) to generate struc-
tured geometric representations compatible with QUIC-Indoor simulations. The primary objectives
include:

 • Automated extraction of building geometry from point cloud data

 • Generation of discretized wall definitions for interior building structures

 • Creation of multi-resolution geometric representations (combined and per-story)

 • Production of simulation-ready configuration files

 The rooms in the QUIC Indoor code are required to be simple rectangles. This is obviously
very limiting as rooms in the real world can be irregularly shaped both in the floor footprint and
less commonly with the vertical walls as well (see Fig. 3-1). While the original QUIC Indoor
architecture anticipated that the building interior would be divided into individual rooms, however
the fact that the rooms in QUIC indoor are simple rectangles and walls must be located along
grid cell boundaries, this makes it difficult to accurately reproduce complex room shapes with this
paradigm. We realized that it would be far easier to replicate complex interior geometries including
walls at oblique angles and curved walls if we employ a single room and discretize the surfaces with
smaller elements along the cell boundaries (see Fig.3-2). The user can specify the number of vertical
levels so it can either divide the geometry into multiple floors or approximate non vertical walls.
The importer defines the exterior perimeter of each floor with a convex hull and groups interior
points into the nearest grid cell boundaries to generate contiguous complex walls.

The development of the GUI began with the implementation of the read/write functions for the
QUIC Indoor input files as a basis for defining how the GUI would interact with the C++ executable.

3.2.5 Connectivity Tab
The Connectivity Tab (see Fig. 3-5) allows the user to specify how the rooms are connected and
interact with each other through the definition of doors, HVAC supply/exhaust vents, and leaky
regions. HVAC supply/exhaust vents can be turbulent jets, isotropic volumes, and/or isotropic
points. Jets include directional momentum along a principal axis, while the isotropic sources simply
add or remove volume in a particular grid cell at a specified rate and allow the MPFT solver to
calculate how this affects the velocity field.

3.3 Phase III: Assess and Refine Code
Previous to the development of the GUI the process of assessing the performance of the QUIC
Indoor was difficult and slow since the output had to be loaded manually into Paraview. The GUI
make the QUIC Indoor model far more user friendly and accelerates the assessment process since
it makes it possible to visualize what is occurring within the model immediately after a simulation
is run. This accelerates the iterative process of model development. Once this was possible, several
issues with the original code structure and performance became evident.
 The Eulerian and Lagrangian models each have their strengths and weaknesses. The Eulerian
model is necessarily more computationally intensive than the Lagrangian model since it relies on
solving the full advection/diffusion equation from CFD methods. While Eulerian models experience
numerical diffusion, which artificially diffuses the tracer throughout the domain due to the numerical
methods rather than physical processes, Eulerian models also have the advantage of a flexible
dynamic range of plume concentrations. The Lagrangian solver does not experience numerical

Los Alamos National Laboratory Page 3-11

diffusion but it also has a limited dynamic range of concentrations due to the discretization of
the mass in the tracer particles. The dynamic range of concentrations that can be simulated by
Lagrangian models scales with the number of tracers used in the simulation. In order to simulate the
low concentration regions at the edges of the plume, and subsequently fully simulating the detectable
plume, it may be necessary to use a lot of particles. This may result in eliminating the runtime
advantage that Lagrangian methods have over the Eulerian method. Running batches of Eulerian
simulations helped identify numerical instabilities in the Eulerian model which would occasionally
cause the simulation to blow up and produce non physical concentration values. This will need to
be addressed, but in the mean time it was decided to concentrate on using the Lagrangian model
for sensor siting purposes.
 The Lagrangian model was found to have its own issues. Rather than tracking the trajectories
of the tracer particles simultaneously as the simulation progresses in time. Instead the Lagrangian
solver cycled through the tracers and simulated the whole life span of each tracer. This structure
is not ideal for the purposes of simulating large facilities as it required the storage of the full 3D
concentration field for each time step throughout the simulation. This is very inefficient in memory,
so the code was restructured to use the architecture that has been used within the QUIC-PLUME
model for decades. It was also found that the Lagrangian dispersion model was employing log-
normal distributions in the random walk perturbations of the tracer trajectories. This resulted
in generally very small perturbations in the trajectories of the tracers with the occasional large
jump. This architecture resulted in Lagrangian tracers that essentially just followed the velocity
streamlines rather than properly simulating the diffusive processes. It was not evident that this
issue existed in the code when tracer sourcs are located at or near HVAC supply vents since these
are a regions of divergence in the velocity field. It was far more obvious that this was an issue
when simulating a passive tracer that was injected into the flow away from diverging flow. The
validation of the Lagrangian model by Sayre [9] focused on collocated HVAC and tracer sources.
The QUIC-PLUME model has successfully employed Gaussian normal distributions in the random
walk perturbation algorithm for decades and has demonstrated good agreement with field observa-
tions. We subsequently added the ability to switch between the two distributions in QUIC Indoor’s
Lagrangian transport solver. Through modifying the architecture of the Lagrangian model to match
the one that has successfully been used in the QUIC-PLUME model for decades, the Lagrangian
model has become much more computationally efficient and better replicates the expected diffusive
behavior.
 The sensor optimization algorithms that we have used in the past have almost exclusively focused
on dosage rather than concentration, primarily due to the nature of the detection process. The
Zeteotech mass-spectra based bioaerosol detection system is fundamentally different. Rather than
accumulating over time, the fact that this system can detect a release in near real time means that
it necessarily operates using collection periods on the order of minutes rather than hours to days.
It is desirable to optimize for the strengths of this sensor and the optimization algorithm in QUIC
Indoor has been modified to include three different optimization metrics based on time to detection:
1) Minimize the mean time to detection of all potential sources. 2) Minimize the maximum time
to detection in order to avoid the longest time to detect scenarios. And 3) Maximize the number
of early detections. This provides maximum flexibility for the user to define which metric is most
important for their application.

4.2 Sensor Siting Results
Once the batch of simulations has been run the user must specify the number of sensors that will
be used, the properties (detection threshold, concentration vs dosage based measurements, etc.)
that they use to detect the bioaerosols, and constraints about where the sensors can be placed. In
addition to detection based on a concentration threshold the GUI also allows the user to optimize
based on time to detection, but for this particular example, we are simply using dosage. The
optimization scheme determines the regions of the plume for each scenario in the batch that is
above the detection threshold. The probability of detection field is determined by summing all
of the scenarios that can be detected at a particular location by the total number of scenarios.
The greedy optimization method looks for the grid cell with the highest probability of detection
within the allowed candidates. Subsequent sensors remove scenarios that have already been detected
by previously placed sensors and are placed in the location that will detect the most undetected
scenarios. Figure 4-5 shows the locations within the ballroom for the first five optimized sensors.
The list with the precise locations for these optimized sensors are in Table 4-1. The optimization
results are physically plausible as the optimization method will tend to place sensors near the HVAC
return vents since this is where the flow and tracer concentration will accumulate regardless of the
tracer source location.

5 Conclusions
LANL developed a GUI wrapper for the QUIC Indoor transport and dispersion modeling system.
This GUI wrapper significantly improves the usability of the QUIC Indoor C++ code. The GUI
includes the basic capabilities to optimize sensor locations including: 1) the ability to import the
geometry of building interiors in the form of PLY point cloud files from LiDAR scans. 2) The
GUI also facilitates the definition and manipulation of all the parameters of the simulations, e.g.,
simulation grid, rooms, obstacles, HVAC vents, tracer source locations, etc. And 3) the GUI
includes a sensor siting mode that can run multiple simulations simultaneously and then optimize
the sensor placements based on the batch of simulations from an array of possible source locations
using a greedy algorithm. The example case demonstrated the physical plausibility of optimization
method as it identified the HVAC return vents as the preferred locations for the sensors due to the
accumulation of flow and concentration at these locations. While this is the intuitive solution, it is
not obvious how the vents should be prioritized for a limited number of sensors in a large facility
with complex HVAC systems.
