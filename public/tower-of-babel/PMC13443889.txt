Two-dimensional surface melting with an intermediate quasi-hexatic layer
Authors: Wei Li, Tianran Zhang, Zhisheng Wang, Yufeng Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMelting in two-dimensional (2D) systems exhibits physics absent in three dimensions, yet its mechanism has been debated for decades. The prevailing Kosterlitz-Thouless-Halperin-Nelson-Young (KTHNY) theory describes 2D melting as two continuous transitions in the bulk with an intermediate hexatic phase. While this framework can describe bulk behavior at the transition, it does not account for the influence of free surfaces. Here, using single-particle imaging of optically-driven 2D colloidal solids, we report that melting with free surfaces proceeds via a double wetting pathway, where an inhomogeneous layer with hexatic order forms between the bulk solid and an outer quasi-liquid layer. Prior to melting, the thicknesses of both wetting layers grow into the bulk following a power-law divergence. The quasi-hexatic layer penetrates the bulk ahead of the quasi-liquid layer, giving rise to separate solid-hexatic and hexatic-liquid transitions. Different from the KTHNY scenario, in which dislocations nucleate as bound pairs in the bulk, the free surface acts as a prolific source that continuously emits dislocations into the solid, driving the invasion of the wetting layers. These results present a surface-initiated pathway for 2D melting, offering insights into colloidal matter, 2D materials, and condensed matter physics.Subject terms: Phase transitions and critical phenomena, Colloids

Introduction

Melting represents one of the most fundamental phase transitions in condensed matter physics1. For two-dimensional (2D) systems, the mechanism of melting has been debated for decades, motivating extensive studies2–12. The dominant framework is the Kosterlitz-Thouless-Halperin-Nelson-Young (KTHNY) theory2, which predicts two continuous transitions from solid to liquid via an intermediate hexatic phase, characterized by topological defect unbinding. A critical assumption of the KTHNY theory is an idealized, infinite system with no boundary, thereby neglecting the effects of free solid surfaces.

However, melting in most solids begins at temperatures below the bulk melting point by forming a quasi-liquid surface layer1,13,14, i.e., a spatially inhomogeneous liquid layer that retains some crystalline order near the solid. Free surfaces break translational symmetry because distinct phases coexist across the interface. Atoms near the surface are weakly bound and susceptible to thermal fluctuations. As the temperature approaches the melting point, a progressive loss of crystalline order takes place at the surface forming a thin, quasi-liquid layer, a phenomenon known as premelting13. This surface layer eliminates the nucleation barrier, leading to the outside-in melting pathway. In the absence of premelting, bulk melting would require superheating, which has not been found for solids under typical conditions15.

Thermodynamically, surface premelting occurs when the solid–vapor interfacial energy (γsv) exceeds the combined energy of the solid–liquid (γsl) and liquid–vapor (γlv) interfaces, satisfying the so-called “wetting condition”, γsl + γlv − γsv≤0 1,13. While this condition is widely satisfied in 3D systems, its validity and implications in two dimensions remain unclear.

Colloidal particles exhibit phase behaviors analogous to those of atoms16,17, yet they can be directly visualized via optical microscopy. Serving as model systems, colloid-based experiments have provided significant insights into phase behaviors8,10,18–28. Previous studies have reported a grain-boundary-mediated melting with a short-ranged depletion attraction in 2D colloidal solids8, yet premelting is suppressed, and the hexatic phase is absent due to the low dislocation core energy4. An experimental observation of a complete surface premelting in 2D systems has not been reported.

Results

Light-tunable interactions

In this work, light-powered core-shell colloids are employed to probe surface melting in 2D solids. The particles are synthesized by coating 3-(trimethoxylsilyl)propyl methacrylate microspheres (2.1 μm in diameter) with a thin ( ≈ 50 nm) shell of titanium dioxide (TPM@TiO2), followed by adsorption of a photosensitizer, D5 dye, or 5-[4-(diphenylamino)phenyl]thiophene-2-cyanoacrylic acid on the shell29,30. When dispersed in an aqueous solution containing hydroquinone (HQ) and imidazole (IM), and upon illumination at 470 nm, the particles catalyze the redox reactions of HQ to generate H+ and OH−. The photochemical reaction establishes a steady-state chemical gradient within a second. This process produces local ion gradients that trigger diffusiophoretic/osmotic flows30,31 (Fig. 1a and Supplementary Fig. 9 and “Methods”). Flow-based interactions arise between particles, inducing an effective long-range attractive and short-range repulsive pair potential as illustrated in Fig. 1b. No obvious change in attraction is observed over 30 h continuous illumination at 30 mW cm−2. The attraction strength U(rm) scales linearly with light intensities I (Fig. 1b, inset). An effective temperature can be defined as T~=kBT/∣U(rm)∣8,32. This optical tunability enables precise control over interparticle interactions. At high light intensities (≥30 mW cm−2), the TPM@TiO2 particles self-assemble into centimeter-scale 2D solid (Supplementary Movie 1). By quasi-statically reducing light intensity, this solid system provides a robust platform for observing melting transitions.

Surface-initiated invasion of wetting layers

When the light intensity is gradually tuned close to the melting point, the initially well-ordered surface layer progressively disorders. The invasion of surface layers into the bulk is a signature of premelting. We examined the local structure order by the bond-orientational order parameter ψ6(r⃗j)=1/Nj∑k=1Njei6θjk, where θjk is the bond angle between particle j and its kth nearest neighbor, and k runs over all Nj neighbors (see Supplementary Note 1). As light intensity decreases, the disordered surface layer thickens, corresponding to an increase in effective temperature T~ (Fig. 2a–c and Supplementary Movie 2).

The profiles of ∣ψ6∣ (Fig. 2g) show that as light intensity nears the melting point (I = 18 mW cm−2), the disordered layer (low values of ∣ψ6∣) penetrates the bulk. At this point, ∣ψ6∣ becomes uniform and equal to the value of the isotropic disordered phase (see Supplementary Fig. 5), i.e., the dilute vapor-like (or fluid-like) phase, with ∣ψ6vapor∣≈0.36, allowing us to identify the positions of the liquid–vapor interface xlv during premelting (∣ψ6(x)∣≥∣ψ6vapor∣ for x≤xlv). Each particle’s ψ6 conveys two types of information: ∣ψ6∣, indicative of sixfold crystalline symmetry, and θ, representing local orientations. θ itself is not well-suited for distinguishing different phases (see Supplementary Note 9). Intriguingly, the surface profiles of the angular standard deviation σ(θ)=⟨θ2⟩−⟨θ⟩2 differ from those of ∣ψ6∣, as σ(θ(x)) saturates to a plateau deeper into the bulk. For instance, at I = 21 mW cm−2 (pink symbols in Fig. 2g, h), ∣ψ6∣ decays to a liquid-like value (equals to the value of black symbols) at x ≈ 200μm (Fig. 2g) whereas σ(θ(x)) reaches its plateau at x ≈ 80 μm (Fig. 2h). This implies an intermediate layer in which the crystalline order remains high, yet substantial orientational twisting develops. The twisting reflects an enhanced dislocation density that reduces true long-range orientational order to quasi-long-range order, suggesting the presence of an intermediate quasi-hexatic layer, i.e., a spatially inhomogeneous layer exhibiting hexatic order.

Dynamic order parameters are also crucial for characterizing melting behavior. The Lindemann parameter, defined as the ratio of the particles’ thermal vibration amplitude to the lattice constant1,19, is the classical melting criterion. However, due to long-wavelength fluctuations in 2D systems33–35, this parameter diverges even in the bulk solid (Supplementary Fig. 11). To remove these fluctuations, we measure the dynamic Lindemann parameter Ld, which is adjusted for local coordinates28,36 (Supplementary Note 3). Ld(t) reaches a plateau after 2 s, reflecting the cage effect in the crystalline region (Supplementary Fig. 11, inset). Thus, Ld is defined as Ld(t = 2 s). Fig. 2i shows that melting occurs when Ld in the bulk exceeds 0.125, i.e., satisfying the Lindemann melting criterion1,19. Notably, the long-time limit of Ld for liquid and hexatic particles diverges, meaning that Ld, defined within 2 s of vibration, for quasi-liquid particles in Fig. 2i only reflects a 2 s diffusion distance rather than the vibrational amplitude. The premelted layer does not fully resemble normal liquid, as particles within the layer still exhibit slow dynamics with Ld < 0.125 (Fig. 2d–f) and spatially stratified and temporally persistent orientational orders with ∣ψ6∣ > 0.8 (Fig. 2a–c), distinct from a homogeneous equilibrium liquid near coexistence.

Melting typically proceeds via the divergence of surface wetting layers. For systems with long-range interactions, e.g., van der Waals and Lennard-Jones potentials, the thickness of the surface wetting layer follows a power-law divergence, i.e., l ∝ ∣Tm − T∣m8,13,32,37. However, defining the boundary of this surface wetting layer is challenging because the surface profiles vary smoothly, lacking abrupt changes. A contrast in the order parameter between the surface and the bulk gives rise to a surface layer. Typically, the boundary of the surface layer xb is defined as extending from the surface to the position where an order parameter has decayed to 1/e of its bulk contrast32,38 (see Supplementary Note 4), i.e., ∣ξ(xb) − ξbulk∣ = (1/e)∣ξ(xsurface) − ξbulk∣, with l = xsurface − xb, where ξ denotes the relevant parameter. Accordingly, ∣ψ6∣, θ, and Ld in Fig. 2g–i help define the surface layers, as shown in Fig. 3b. Surface profiles of order parameters at different light intensities are self-similar and collapse onto master curves when x is rescaled by the corresponding surface layer thickness (see Supplementary Fig. 12 and Supplementary Note 5).

Both structure and dynamic parameters reveal that the surface wetting layers diverge as a power law: l∝(T~m−T~)−3.6 (Fig. 3b), a typical feature of surface premelting behavior13. The exponent appears to be highly system-dependent and sensitive to the detailed form of the interactions8,13,32,39. Interestingly, the surface layer defined by the dynamic parameter Ld, denoted as lLd, is thicker than that defined by the structure parameter ∣ψ6∣. This suggests the presence of an additional highly structured but dynamically active layer. The similarity in thickness between lLd and lθ, which is the surface layer defined by σ(θ), implies that fast dynamics in this intermediate layer are driven by variations in local orientations, typically resulting from an increased dislocation density. The surface density profile (Supplementary Fig. 4a) defines a surface layer of thickness that closely matches the thickness defined by the dynamic Lindemann parameter lLd (Supplementary Fig. 4c). This agreement is expected, since Ld reflects the vibrational amplitude of particles, which is directly linked to the local free volume set by the density.

The intermediate quasi-hexatic layer

The surface region can be divided into four distinct parts, each with characteristic structure and dynamics: I, crystal (solid); II, quasi-hexatic; III, quasi-liquid; IV, vapor. Spatial and temporal correlations of ψ6, g6(r) and g6(t), are key for differentiating these structures (Supplementary Note 2). Figure 3c, d shows behaviors predicted by the KTHNY theory: region I (crystal) with non-decaying correlations, region II (quasi-hexatic) with power-law decay, and region III (quasi-liquid) with exponential decay.

As is demonstrated in Fig. 2g, the surface layer has a clear gradient of order, with the inner part more crystal-like and the outer part more liquid-like, i.e., a typical premelting-like behavior. Thus, the intermediate quasi-hexatic layer in region II is intrinsically inhomogeneous, becoming more crystal-like toward region I and more liquid-like toward region III. To quantify this, we subdivide region II into five thinner sublayers along the surface normal, denoted as II1-II5 in Fig. 3a, within which the order is much more uniform. Although the reduced thickness of each subregion limits the correlation range for g6(r), the power-law decay remains clearly resolvable. From II1 to II5, both g6(r) and g6(t) progressively approach the KTHNY hexatic-liquid critical forms r−1/4 and t−1/8 from r0 and t0. This confirms a more crystal-like inner quasi-hexatic layer (g6(r), g6(t) ∝ r0) and a more liquid-like outer quasi-hexatic layer (g6(r) ∝ r−1/4, g6(t) ∝ r−1/8, see Supplementary Fig. 6c), and also, agrees with the bulk KTHNY prediction that the decay of g6(t) is two times slower than that of g6(r). The spatial and temporal exponents of g6(r) and g6(t) exhibit an inhomogeneous, approximately power law variation across the quasi-hexatic layer (Supplementary Fig. 6c). The first two sublayers (II1, II2) show very similar correlation functions because their exponents are both very close to zero and fall within our systematic uncertainty. We note that we cannot fully exclude a contribution from a slight mismatch between the crystal-quasi-hexatic boundary we define and the true microscopic interface.

The behavior of Ld(t) (Fig. 3e) further distinguishes these phases, indicating caged behavior in region I and a diffusive behavior in region III. In contrast, Ld(t) exhibits a transient caged behavior at intermediate times but slow diffusive dynamics at long times, indicating a quasi-hexatic phase in region II3,10,36.

For solid region I, elastic moduli are measured from the equilibrium strain fluctuations40–42 (see Supplementary Note 7). As the quasi-hexatic layer approaches region I at light intensity 21 mW cm−2, the Young’s modulus of region I is reduced to 16π at the solid–hexatic transition (Fig. 3f). Upon further decreasing the light intensity (increasing the effective temperature), the quasi-hexatic layer invades region I and the measured Young’s modulus rapidly drops from 16π to zero, suggesting a solid–hexatic transition41,43.

In addition, the 2D structure factor S(k) in Fig. 3g underscores the crystalline (region I), quasi-hexatic (region II), and quasi-liquid or vapor (regions III, IV) phases, showing distinct structural features. Region II shows a hexatic-like pattern with sixfold symmetry, but the intensities of the six maxima are noticeably asymmetric. This reflects the gradual variation of hexatic order across region II from the solid side to the quasi-liquid side. The angular dependences of 2D structure factor S(χ) further reveal these differences (Supplementary Fig. 13). For solid region I, S(χ) is well described by a simple Lorentzian. For a homogeneous hexatic phase, the first peak of S(χ) is expected to follow a square-root Lorentzian (SRL) form44, S(χ)∝[(χ−θ0)2+κ2]−1/2, where θ0 is the angular position of the first peak in S(k), χ is the in-plane angle, and κ is the SRL angular width. In the inhomogeneous quasi-hexatic region II, the local SRL width κ should increase from subregion II1 to II5. So the measured S(χ) is effectively an average over SRLs with different κ. As a result, one side of the peak is well fitted by a narrower SRL, while the other side is fitted by a broader SRL, consistent with the asymmetric sixfold pattern in Fig. 3g. Although S(χ) in region II can also be fitted by a simple Lorentzian form, the resulting reduced chi-square values are systematically larger than those obtained from the SRL fits (see Supplementary Fig. 13). This indicates that the SRL form provides a better description of the angular line shape in region II45.

The evidence indicates that 2D surface premelting involves double wetting layers, a quasi-liquid layer identified by l∣ψ6∣, and a thicker layer encompassing both the quasi-liquid and an additional quasi-hexatic layer, delineated by lLd. Notably, lLd and l∣ψ6∣ diverge with the same power-law, implying the quasi-hexatic layer also follows this divergence, i.e., lLd−l∣ψ6∣∝(T~m−T~)−3.6. Importantly, the inner quasi-hexatic layer propagates into the bulk ahead of the quasi-liquid layer.

The bulk behavior is consistent with the KTHNY theory, featuring a two-step melting process, while it incorporates the crucial effects of surface premelting. First, the solid transforms into a hexatic phase as the surface quasi-hexatic layer invades inward. Then, at the hexatic-liquid transition, the hexatic phase transforms into a liquid, marked by the divergence of the quasi-liquid layer.

The “wetting condition” for forming double wetting layers is γsh + γhl + γlv < γsv, where γsh and γhl are interfacial energies of solid–hexatic and hexatic-liquid interfaces. Formation of double wetting layers typically requires a sufficiently low interfacial energy between the bulk phase and the second wetting layer, e.g., coherent crystal-crystal interfaces46. γsh naturally fulfills this requirement, as the interfaces between the crystal and hexatic phase are coherent and visually indistinguishable, thus promoting the formation of double wetting layers.

Surface-mediated dislocation emission and propagation

The KTHNY theory predicts that the hexatic phase emerges via the creation of free dislocations and that the hexatic-liquid transition is driven by their unbinding. As they are thermally activated in the bulk, new dislocations must form as bound pairs with a 5-7-7-5 nearest-neighbor (nn) configuration, incurring a substantial energy barrier. Our experiments show that free surfaces dramatically lower this barrier, enabling efficient dislocation production.

Figure 4a, b demonstrates this surface-mediated process. The quasi-liquid layer at the solid–vapor interface accumulates a high density of dislocations that simultaneously form and unbind. This active region continuously ejects dislocations into the bulk, propagating the quasi-hexatic layer (Supplementary Movie 3). The large free volume at the free surface facilitates spontaneous dislocation (5–7 pairs) formation through thermal fluctuations (Fig. 4c–f), initiating the growth of a quasi-hexatic layer as these dislocations glide into the bulk.

Even dislocations created with Burgers vectors parallel to the surface, which cannot glide into the bulk, can penetrate the solid through a climbing mechanism. As shown in Fig. 4g–l, these dislocations climb into the bulk via out-of-plane motion of the 5-nn particle: the 5-nn particle is vertically displaced by neighboring particles, enabling the 5–7 pairs to advance by one lattice constant into the bulk (Supplementary Movie 3). It is important to consider whether out-of-plane motion alters the KTHNY scenario. Experiments on 2D atomical materials show that KTHNY-type melting and hexatic phases can persist despite vacancies and out-of-plane motions induced by electron-beam damage47,48. In this system, out-of-plane motions do not generate a stable, dense second layer or 3D stacking, and they do not change the in-plane defect topology (e.g., 5–7 pairs). We therefore expect that such out-of-plane effects do not qualitatively invalidate the KTHNY-type scenario applied here.

The observed results suggest that the free surface acts as a defect source that actively ejects dislocations into the bulk. While it may also act as a defect sink, where dislocations disintegrate into disclinations that form quasi-liquids. This naturally produces a dislocation density gradient that is highest at the surface and decreases toward the solid interior, so that hexatic order first appears near the surface. As the system approaches the melting point, the dislocation density at the surface increases, and dislocations progressively diffuse inward. This generates double surface wetting layers, in which an inner quasi-hexatic layer and an outer quasi-liquid layer both thicken and advance into the crystal. The inner quasi-hexatic layer invades the bulk more rapidly than the quasi-liquid layer. Viewed from the perspective of the bulk interior, the system therefore undergoes an effective two-step evolution: solid–hexatic–liquid, even though the primary defect generation is surface driven.

These results suggest a unified, surface-enabled route to the KTHNY theory, where barrierless creation of dislocations at the interface seeds and feeds the growth of a quasi-hexatic layer into the bulk, facilitating the two-step solid–hexatic–liquid transition. Although KTHNY is formulated for a homogeneous bulk system and classical premelting focuses on surface-initiated melting, our observations show that the two pictures are not contradictory. Surface premelting, via the sequential invasion of double wetting layers, sets the stage for the two-step solid–hexatic-liquid transition in the bulk described by KTHNY.

The previous study8 using short-ranged depletion attractions reported a melting scenario that deviates from the KTHNY framework, which is attributed to the low dislocation core energy, Ec = 1.82kBT. According to the KTHNY theory2, melting proceeds via the unbinding and proliferation of dislocations when the core energy exceeds EcKTHNY≈2.84kBT. In our system, we measure Ec = 3.82kBT (Supplementary Fig. 2), which is well above this threshold. This difference in dislocation core energy results in the distinct surface melting behaviors observed in ref. 8 and in the present work.

In conclusion, using optically responsive colloidal crystals, we resolve surface melting at the single-particle level. We reveal that 2D surface melting proceeds via the formation of double wetting layers. This observation bridges the KTHNY theory with the surface melting scenario, as the sequential invasion of surface quasi-hexatic and quasi-liquid layers support the predicted two-step melting pathway. However, the dislocation kinetics shows notable deviations from KTHNY theory. Free surfaces act as prolific sources, continuously ejecting dislocations into the bulk and promoting the invasion of wetting layers. These findings establish surfaces as active drivers for 2D melting, a mechanism that reshapes our understanding of melting dynamics in 2D.

Methods

Materials

Synthesis of TPM colloidal cores49

In total, 300 μL of 3-(trimethoxysilyl)propyl methacrylate (TPM monomer) (Sigma-Aldrich, 98%), 50 μL of 5.4 vol.% NH3 aqueous solution, and 10 mL of DI water were mixed and stirred for 2 h. The size of the TPM oil droplet was monitored using an optical microscope. When the particles’ size reaches 2 μm in diameter, a pinch of 2,2′-azobis(2-methylpropionitrile) (AIBN) (Sigma-Aldrich, 98%) was added into the reaction, followed by heating at 80 °C for 4 h. The solidified TPM particles were washed with ethanol three times and stored in ethanol for further use.

Coating of a TiO2 shell on TPM particle50

In total, 1 mL of ethanol solution containing ≈ 1 wt.% TPM particles was centrifuged to remove the ethanol. The remaining particles were redispersed in 5 mL of ethanol solution containing 0.05 g polyvinylpyrrolidone (PVP-K90, Mw 360 kg mol−1) (TCI), followed by the addition of 50 μL of 10 mM NaCl aqueous solution. The mixture was sonicated for 1 min, and then stirred at 1000 rpm. In total, 60 μL of titanium isopropoxide (J&K Scientific, 98%) was added to the mixture. After 1 min, the stirring rate was reduced from 1000 to 250 rpm and stirring was kept for 20 min. The particles were collected by repeated centrifugation/redispersion process in ethanol. The particles were stored in 1 mL of ethanol.

Sample preparation and experimental setup

TPM@TiO2 particles were washed three times with ethanol by the centrifugation/redispersion process (2000 × g, 1 min each), and then immersed in a 0.8 mM ethanolic solution containing D5 dye for 2 h to complete dye absorption. After that, the particles were washed twice with DI water by centrifugation/redispersion, and then resuspended in 1.0 mL of hydroquinone (HQ) and imidazole (IM) solution. The HQ/IM solution was prepared by mixing 1.00 mL of 4 mg mL−1 HQ with 10 μL of 50 mM IM solution.

The sample was loaded into a rectangular glass capillary (0.30 × 3.00 × 50.00 mm3, VitroTubesTM, Cat. No. 3530-050) and sealed with optical adhesive (NOA 65) with UV curing for microscopy (Supplementary Fig. 7). Imaging was performed on a Nikon Eclipse Ti2 inverted microscope with a ×40 objective, equipped with a Nikon N7000 camera.

Fabrication of cm-sized 2D crystals

Large single crystals are produced in two stages: (1) rapid condensation (Supplementary Fig. 8a–c) and (2) Ostwald ripening (Supplementary Fig. 8d–f). Initially, TPM@TiO2 particles were dispersed in a gas-like state. We illuminated the sample with a high intensity I = 90 mW cm−2, inducing strong attractions that drive rapid condensation into small 2D crystalline islands (Supplementary Fig. 8a, b), which subsequently merge into a polycrystal (Supplementary Fig. 8c, d). This condensation stage completes within ≤5 min. To enlarge crystalline domains, we then exploited Ostwald ripening, a thermodynamically driven process favoring growth of larger grains at the expense of smaller ones. To accelerate ripening, we periodically modulated the illumination between 90 and 30 mW cm−2 every 5 min. After ≈ 2 h of cycling, grain boundaries are largely annealed out, yielding large-domain crystals (Supplementary Fig. 8e, f).

It is important to note that the direct Boltzmann inversion method is only valid when the interparticle attraction is not too strong. In cases of strong attraction, particles may irreversibly cluster in the attractive well, leading to a vanishing probability of finding particles at intermediate separations. This results in artificially low values of g(r), which in turn can produce spurious repulsive peaks in U(r). As is shown in Fig. 1b, at I = 90 mW cm−2, the extracted U(r) exhibits a repulsive peak of approximately 0.3kBT (blue circles). This feature is an artifact of limited sampling and highlights the inaccuracy of direct Boltzmann inversion under strong attractive interactions at higher light intensities. However, such a strong attraction is not required for the purposes of this experiment, and accurate measurements of the pair potential can be obtained under moderate illumination conditions where the method remains accurate.
