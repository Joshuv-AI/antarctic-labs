Discrete Langevin-Inspired Posterior Sampling
Chaitanya Amballa, Sattwik Basu, Jorge Vančo Sampedro, Romit Roy Choudhury

Abstract
We study posterior sampling for inverse problems in discrete state spaces using discrete diffusion models as generative priors. While continuous diffusion models have become widely used for inverse problems, their discrete counterparts remain comparatively underexplored. Existing discrete posterior samplers often rely on continuous relaxations of discrete variables, Gibbs-style updates, or mechanisms specialized to particular corruption processes, which can limit scalability or generality. We propose Δ \Delta LPS , a Discrete Langevin-Inspired Posterior Sampler that uses gradient information to identify promising discrete moves without leaving the discrete state space. The resulting approach enables efficient parallel updates across all token dimensions and is agnostic to the training paradigm of the discrete diffusion prior, including masked and uniform-state diffusion. We evaluate our method on image restoration tasks across MNIST, CIFAR, and FFHQ, as well as spatial mapping, covering linear, nonlinear, and blind inverse problems. Across these settings, we improve over recent discrete diffusion posterior samplers and are competitive with strong continuous diffusion-based inverse solvers. Our results suggest that fully discrete, gradient-informed posterior samplers offer a scalable and general path toward solving inverse problems over discrete representations.

Introduction

Inverse problems are a class of methods aimed at recovering an unknown signal
\mathbf{x}
from indirect, sparse, and noisy measurements
\mathbf{y}
.
The signal and measurement are typically related through a forward model

\mathbf{y}=\mathcal{A}(\mathbf{x})+\mathbf{n}
,
where
\mathcal{A}(\cdot)
denotes the forward operator and
\mathbf{n}
denotes measurement noise.
Given only
\mathbf{y}
, the goal is to infer a plausible underlying signal
\mathbf{x}
sampled from the Bayesian posterior
p(\mathbf{x}|\mathbf{y})
.
The central challenge is that inverse problems are often ill-posed: many distinct signals may be consistent with the same measurement.
This ambiguity makes structural priors on
\mathbf{x}
essential.
Classical signal processing has developed domain-specific priors based on sparsity, smoothness, total variation, and related regularizers
5
.
These priors have enabled sophisticated optimization-based estimators, but they often underrepresent complex data and require careful tuning to balance measurement fidelity against regularization.

Recent progress has seen a paradigm shift towards using generative priors, particularly diffusion models, that have delivered substantial gains across domains
33
;
36
;
9
.
Most existing posterior sampling methods, however, are designed for continuous score-based diffusion models that are naturally compatible with gradient-driven likelihood guidance
36
;
9
;
50
;
4
;
35
.
In contrast, discrete diffusion models remain relatively underexplored for inverse problems, despite their growing role as priors over tokenized, symbolic, and quantized latent representations.
Recent work has begun to address posterior sampling in discrete state spaces
3
;
21
;
29
, but typically rely on one of three mechanisms: relaxing categorical variables into a continuous space, introducing augmented Gibbs-style
41
updates, or specializing the sampler to a particular corruption process.
For example, G2D2
21
enables likelihood guidance through a continuous relaxation, but this changes the underlying geometry on which inference is performed; SGDD
3
provides a principled Gibbs-based construction, but such updates can mix slowly in high-dimensional spaces; and APS
29
, while being a strong method, is specially designed around masked-diffusion models and anchoring.
These limitations motivate posterior samplers that remain fully discrete, and efficiently update all coordinates in parallel while remaining agnostic to training methodologies of the discrete diffusion prior.
This paper aims to meet these requirements.

Our proposed algorithm is centered around the following idea:
gradients can tell us where to “hop” in a discrete space, even if the sampler itself cannot move continuously.
Our key idea is to appropriately utilize this gradient information (from continuous space) to choose better discrete hops, without ever leaving the discrete state space.
We formalize this intuition by using gradients to parameterize a Langevin-inspired proposal between valid discrete states, rather than optimizing continuously over soft-latents and projecting them back to tokens.
As an outcome, we meet our desired requirements.
Specifically,
(1) we avoid a potential failure mode where a soft latent may fit the measurement well, but its projected discrete token may not decode to a similarly consistent sample,
(2) our Langevin-inspired proposal factorizes across coordinates, so transition probabilities for all dimensions can be computed and sampled in parallel, avoiding the sequential bottleneck of Gibbs-style samplers,
(3) finally, the sampler is not tied to a specific corruption process and can be used with masked, uniform-state, or other discrete diffusion priors.

Preliminaries and Related Work

Discrete diffusion models
have recently emerged as a powerful class of generative models for
discrete
data, including text
23
;
51
;
22
;
6
, code
14
;
10
, vector-quantized images
44
;
19
, audio
43
, protein, and molecular generation
42
;
38
;
20
;
46
.
These models use a forward Markov corruption process over categorical states
\mathbf{z}_{0}\in\{1,\dots,K\}^{L}
and learn a reverse denoising model.
In D3PM
(
1
)
, the forward process is specified by transition matrices

\mathbf{Q}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{K\times K}
, where
\mathbf{Q}_{t}^{ij}
denotes the probability of transitioning from token
i
to token
j
at time
t
.
For each coordinate
\ell
,

q(\mathbf{z}_{t}[\ell]=j|\mathbf{z}_{t-1}[\ell]=i)=\mathbf{Q}_{t}^{ij},

and the marginal corruption distribution is tractable:

q(\mathbf{z}_{t}|\mathbf{z}_{0})=\prod_{\ell=1}^{L}\operatorname{Cat}\left(\mathbf{z}_{t}[\ell];\mathbf{e}_{\mathbf{z}_{0}[\ell]}\bar{\mathbf{Q}}_{t}\right),\bar{\mathbf{Q}}_{t}=\mathbf{Q}_{1}\mathbf{Q}_{2}\cdots\mathbf{Q}_{t},

where
\mathbf{e}_{i}
denotes the categorical basis vector for token
i
.
The learned reverse model is typically parameterized through a denoiser

p_{\theta}(\mathbf{z}_{0};\mathbf{z}_{t})
, which predicts clean tokens from a noisy input i.e., approximates
p(\mathbf{z}_{0}|\mathbf{z}_{t})
.
This can then be combined with the analytic posterior

q(\mathbf{z}_{s}|\mathbf{z}_{t},\mathbf{z}_{0})
to define a reverse transition for
s<t
:

\displaystyle p_{\theta}(\mathbf{z}_{s}|\mathbf{z}_{t})=\sum_{\hat{\mathbf{z}}_{0}}q(\mathbf{z}_{s}|\mathbf{z}_{t},\hat{\mathbf{z}}_{0})p_{\theta}(\hat{\mathbf{z}}_{0};\mathbf{z}_{t}).

(1)

Masked diffusion models instantiate this framework using an absorbing mask state.
For a special token
[\mathrm{M}]
, the corruption process can be written as

q(\mathbf{z}_{t}[\ell]|\mathbf{z}_{0}[\ell])=\operatorname{Cat}\left(\mathbf{z}_{t}[\ell];\alpha_{t}\mathbf{e}_{\mathbf{z}_{0}[\ell]}+(1-\alpha_{t})\mathbf{e}_{[\mathrm{M}]}\right),

where
\alpha_{t}
decreases with
t
.
MDLM-style models
(
31
)
build on this absorbing process and train the denoiser with a weighted masked-token prediction objective.
A different line of work, including Duo-style uniform-state diffusion
(
32
)
, instead uses uniform corruption,

\displaystyle q(\mathbf{z}_{t}[\ell]|\mathbf{z}_{0}[\ell])=\operatorname{Cat}\left(\mathbf{z}_{t}[\ell];\alpha_{t}\mathbf{e}_{\mathbf{z}_{0}[\ell]}+(1-\alpha_{t})\mathbf{u}\right),\qquad\mathbf{u}=\frac{1}{K}\mathbf{1},

(2)

so corrupted variables are replaced by uniformly random tokens rather than a single absorbing mask.
Both masked and uniform-state models provide pretrained discrete priors of the form

p_{\theta}(\mathbf{z}_{0};\mathbf{z}_{t})
, but they induce different sampling behavior and different reverse-transition structure.

Diffusion posterior sampling.

Given measurements
\mathbf{y}
with likelihood
p(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{z}_{0})
, posterior sampling seeks samples from

p(\mathbf{z}_{0}|\mathbf{y})\propto p(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{z}_{0})p(\mathbf{z}_{0})
.
In continuous score-based diffusion models
36
, posterior samplers use the decomposition of the posterior score into a prior score and a likelihood score, often approximating the likelihood term through a one-step denoised estimate
(
4
;
35
)
.
For discrete diffusion models, an analogous Euclidean score is not directly available because
\mathbf{z}_{t}
takes values in a finite categorical space.
Recent work has therefore explored alternative mechanisms for discrete posterior sampling.
G2D2
(
21
)
addresses inverse problems with discrete diffusion priors by optimizing a variational categorical distribution using continuous relaxations, together with a star-shaped noising process designed to reduce irreversible token commitments.
SGDD
(
3
)
proposes a plug-and-play split Gibbs sampler that alternates between a likelihood-side sampling step and a diffusion-prior denoising step.
APS
(
29
)
focuses on masked diffusion foundation models and introduces quantized expectation for gradient-like guidance, together with anchored remasking to adaptively control which tokens are committed during inference.
These methods show the promise of discrete diffusion priors for inverse problems, but typically rely on continuous relaxations, Gibbs-style updates, or mechanisms specialized to a particular discrete prior.
In contrast, our goal is to construct a prior-agnostic discrete posterior sampler that uses the pretrained denoiser
p_{\theta}(\mathbf{z}_{0};\mathbf{z}_{t})
while enabling fully parallel updates across token coordinates.

Next, we first formalize the problem in a Bayesian setting and introduce our discrete sampler.

3
Method

Formulation.

We formulate the inverse problem directly in the discrete latent space of an encoder–decoder generative model, such as a VQ-VAE
40
.
Let
\mathbf{z}_{0}\in\{1,\ldots,K\}^{L}
denote a clean latent sequence of
L
discrete tokens, where
K
is the codebook size.
The decoder
\mathcal{D}
maps this discrete latent sequence to the signal domain, giving
\mathbf{x}=\mathcal{D}(\mathbf{z}_{0})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
The measurements are generated as
\mathbf{y}=\mathcal{A}(\mathcal{D}(\mathbf{z}_{0}))+\mathbf{n}
, where
\mathcal{A}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{m}
is the forward operator,
\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
, and
\mathbf{n}\sim\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{0},\sigma_{y}^{2}\mathbf{I}_{m})
.
For inverse problems in the signals native representation (e.g., pixel space for images),
\mathcal{D}
may be interpreted as an identity operator with
\mathbf{x}_{t}=\mathbf{z}_{t}
.

We assume access to a pretrained discrete diffusion prior
p_{\theta}(\mathbf{z}_{0};\mathbf{z}_{t})\approx p(\mathbf{z}_{0}|\mathbf{z}_{t})
, where
\mathbf{z}_{t}
denotes the current noisy latent state at diffusion time
t\in[0,T]
.
Let
s<t
denote the next lower-noise time.

Our goal
is to sample from the posterior transition
p(\mathbf{z}_{s}|\mathbf{z}_{t},\mathbf{y})
.
We introduce the clean latent state
\mathbf{z}_{0}
as an intermediate variable between
\mathbf{z}_{t}
and
\mathbf{z}_{s}
, following DAPS
47
:

\displaystyle p(\mathbf{z}_{s}|\mathbf{z}_{t},\mathbf{y})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathbf{z}_{0}}p(\mathbf{z}_{s}|\mathbf{z}_{t},\mathbf{y},\mathbf{z}_{0})\,p(\mathbf{z}_{0}|\mathbf{z}_{t},\mathbf{y})\approx\sum_{\mathbf{z}_{0}}p(\mathbf{z}_{s}|\mathbf{z}_{0})\,p(\mathbf{z}_{0}|\mathbf{z}_{t},\mathbf{y}).

(3)

This approximation uses
\mathbf{z}_{0}
as the clean intermediate state: we first infer a measurement-consistent clean latent and then renoise it to time
s
.
The clean-latent posterior can be simplified as:

\displaystyle p(\mathbf{z}_{0}|\mathbf{z}_{t},\mathbf{y})

\displaystyle=\frac{p(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{z}_{0},\mathbf{z}_{t})\,p(\mathbf{z}_{0}|\mathbf{z}_{t})}{p(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{z}_{t})}\propto p(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{z}_{0})\,p(\mathbf{z}_{0}|\mathbf{z}_{t}).

(4)

Here, we use the conditional independence assumption that, given the clean latent
\mathbf{z}_{0}
, the measurement
\mathbf{y}
is independent of the noisy latent
\mathbf{z}_{t}
.
This leads to the core sampling strategy.
(1) First, we sample a clean latent
\mathbf{z}_{0}
that is both likely under the discrete diffusion prior and consistent with the measurement:
\mathbf{z}_{0}\sim p(\mathbf{z}_{0}|\mathbf{z}_{t},\mathbf{y})\propto p(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{z}_{0})\,p(\mathbf{z}_{0}|\mathbf{z}_{t})
.
Unlike in continuous diffusion models, we can directly approximate
p(\mathbf{z}_{0}|\mathbf{z}_{t})
using the discrete diffusion prior
p(\mathbf{z}_{0};\mathbf{z}_{t})
.
(2) Then, we renoise this clean latent to the next diffusion time
s
, i.e.,
\mathbf{z}_{s}\sim p(\mathbf{z}_{s}|\mathbf{z}_{0})
.

Discrete Langevin-inspired clean latent sampling.

The key step is to sample the clean latent posterior

p(\mathbf{z}_{0}|\mathbf{z}_{t},\mathbf{y})
appearing in Eq.
4
.
To motivate our sampler, first consider how the clean-latent sampling problem would be solved if
\mathbf{z}_{0}
were continuous.
We define the log-posterior potential

\displaystyle U(\mathbf{z}_{0};\mathbf{z}_{t},\mathbf{y})=\log p(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{z}_{0})+\log p_{\theta}(\mathbf{z}_{0};\mathbf{z}_{t}),

(5)

whose high-probability regions correspond to clean latents that are both measurement-consistent and likely under the discrete diffusion prior.
A Langevin
26
;
28
update would move the current sample in the direction of increasing
U
, while injecting Gaussian noise to maintain stochasticity:

\displaystyle\mathbf{z}_{0}^{\prime}:=\mathbf{z}_{0}+\eta\nabla_{\mathbf{z}_{0}}U(\mathbf{z}_{0};\mathbf{z}_{t},\mathbf{y})+\sqrt{2\eta}\boldsymbol{\epsilon},\qquad\boldsymbol{\epsilon}\sim\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{0},\mathbf{I}),

(6)

where
\eta>0
is a step size.
Equivalently, this corresponds to sampling
\mathbf{z}_{0}^{\prime}
from the Gaussian proposal

\displaystyle q_{\mathrm{cont}}(\mathbf{z}_{0}^{\prime}|\mathbf{z}_{0};\mathbf{z}_{t},\mathbf{y})

\displaystyle=\mathcal{N}\left(\mathbf{z}_{0}^{\prime};\mathbf{z}_{0}+\eta\nabla_{\mathbf{z}_{0}}U(\mathbf{z}_{0};\mathbf{z}_{t},\mathbf{y}),2\eta\mathbf{I}\right)

\displaystyle\propto\exp\left(-\frac{1}{4\eta}\left\|\mathbf{z}_{0}^{\prime}-\mathbf{z}_{0}-\eta\nabla_{\mathbf{z}_{0}}U(\mathbf{z}_{0};\mathbf{z}_{t},\mathbf{y})\right\|_{2}^{2}\right).

(7)

Thus, the continuous proposal assigns high probability to states near the gradient-shifted point
\mathbf{z}_{0}+\eta\nabla_{\mathbf{z}_{0}}U(\mathbf{z}_{0};\mathbf{z}_{t},\mathbf{y})
.
With a Metropolis–Hastings
27
;
37
correction, this gives the standard Metropolis-Adjusted Langevin Algorithm
28
;
27
proposal; without the correction, it gives the usual unadjusted Langevin sampler.

In our setting,
\mathbf{z}_{0}
is a discrete token sequence, so we cannot directly add a gradient vector to it and obtain another valid latent.
However, by borrowing from the discrete MCMC literature
7
;
49
, we can preserve the same principle without leaving the discrete state space.
Instead of using the Langevin update to produce a continuous-valued proposal, we use the Langevin Gaussian score to assign probabilities to valid discrete candidates
\mathbf{z}_{0}^{\prime}\in\{1,\ldots,K\}^{L}
:

\displaystyle q(\mathbf{z}_{0}^{\prime}|\mathbf{z}_{0};\mathbf{z}_{t},\mathbf{y})

\displaystyle\propto\exp\left(-\frac{1}{4\eta}\left\|\mathbf{z}_{0}^{\prime}-\mathbf{z}_{0}-\eta\nabla_{\mathbf{z}_{0}}U(\mathbf{z}_{0};\mathbf{z}_{t},\mathbf{y})\right\|_{2}^{2}\right).

(8)

This is the key continuous-to-discrete conversion: the Gaussian proposal is not sampled and rounded.
Rather, this quantity is evaluated only on valid discrete token sequences and then normalized only over that specific finite set.
Expanding the exponent
leads to a final term that does not depend on the proposed state
\mathbf{z}_{0}^{\prime}
, so it is absorbed into the normalizing constant.
Therefore, the discrete proposal can be written as

\displaystyle q(\mathbf{z}_{0}^{\prime}|\mathbf{z}_{0};\mathbf{z}_{t},\mathbf{y})

\displaystyle\propto\exp\left(\frac{1}{2}\nabla_{\mathbf{z}_{0}}U(\mathbf{z}_{0};\mathbf{z}_{t},\mathbf{y})^{\top}(\mathbf{z}_{0}^{\prime}-\mathbf{z}_{0})-\frac{1}{4\eta}\|\mathbf{z}_{0}^{\prime}-\mathbf{z}_{0}\|_{2}^{2}\right).

(9)

This form has a useful energy-ratio interpretation.
Using a first-order Taylor approximation around the current state
\mathbf{z}_{0}
,

U(\mathbf{z}_{0}^{\prime};\mathbf{z}_{t},\mathbf{y})\approx U(\mathbf{z}_{0};\mathbf{z}_{t},\mathbf{y})+\nabla_{\mathbf{z}_{0}}U(\mathbf{z}_{0};\mathbf{z}_{t},\mathbf{y})^{\top}\left(\mathbf{z}_{0}^{\prime}-\mathbf{z}_{0}\right)
.
Substituting this approximation into the proposal, utilizing Eqn.
5
and simplifying the expression resultin, gives

\displaystyle q(\mathbf{z}_{0}^{\prime}|\mathbf{z}_{0};\mathbf{z}_{t},\mathbf{y})

\displaystyle\propto\exp\left(\frac{1}{2}\left[U(\mathbf{z}_{0}^{\prime};\mathbf{z}_{t},\mathbf{y})-U(\mathbf{z}_{0};\mathbf{z}_{t},\mathbf{y})\right]-\frac{1}{4\eta}\|\mathbf{z}_{0}^{\prime}-\mathbf{z}_{0}\|_{2}^{2}\right).

(10)

\displaystyle\propto\left[\frac{p(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{z}_{0}^{\prime})}{p(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{z}_{0})}\right]^{1/2}\left[\frac{p_{\theta}(\mathbf{z}_{0}^{\prime};\mathbf{z}_{t})}{p_{\theta}(\mathbf{z}_{0};\mathbf{z}_{t})}\right]^{1/2}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{4\eta}\|\mathbf{z}_{0}^{\prime}-\mathbf{z}_{0}\|_{2}^{2}\right).

(11)

Thus, the proposal
q(\mathbf{z}_{0}^{\prime}|\mathbf{z}_{0};\mathbf{z}_{t},\mathbf{y})
favors candidates that improve the measurement likelihood, improve the diffusion-prior probability, and remain close to the current clean latent.

The same expression also yields a factorized categorical (see App.
G
).
Since the exponent decomposes across token positions, the proposal factorizes as

q(\mathbf{z}_{0}^{\prime}|\mathbf{z}_{0},\mathbf{z}_{t},\mathbf{y})=\prod_{\ell=1}^{L}q_{\ell}(\mathbf{z}_{0}^{\prime}[\ell]|\mathbf{z}_{0},\mathbf{z}_{t},\mathbf{y})
.
Each coordinate-wise proposal is a categorical distribution over the
K
valid token values:

\displaystyle q_{\ell}(\mathbf{z}_{0}^{\prime}[\ell]|\mathbf{z}_{0};\mathbf{z}_{t},\mathbf{y})=\mathrm{Cat}\left[\mathbf{z}_{0}^{\prime}[\ell];\sigma\left(\frac{1}{2}\nabla_{\mathbf{z}_{0}[\ell]}U(\mathbf{z}_{0};\mathbf{z}_{t},\mathbf{y})\left(k-\mathbf{z}_{0}[\ell]\right)-\frac{\left(k-\mathbf{z}_{0}[\ell]\right)^{2}}{4\eta}\right)\right].

(12)

The Softmax
\sigma
normalizes these
K
candidate scores at each coordinate.
This gives a fully discrete Langevin-inspired transition.
The gradient
\nabla_{\mathbf{z}_{0}}U(\mathbf{z}_{0};\mathbf{z}_{t},\mathbf{y})
is computed from the full latent sequence, so each coordinate update is informed by the global measurement likelihood and the diffusion prior.
However, once the logits are formed, all
L
categorical distributions can be sampled independently and in parallel:

\mathbf{z}_{0}^{\prime}[\ell]\sim q_{\ell}(\cdot|\mathbf{z}_{0},\mathbf{z}_{t},\mathbf{y}),~~\ell=1,\ldots,L.

Therefore, the sampler uses posterior gradients to guide transitions toward high-probability clean latents, while ensuring that every proposed state remains a valid discrete token sequence.

3.1
Generalizing across Inverse Problems

The proposed sampler only requires access to the likelihood-gradient component of the posterior potential

U(\mathbf{z}_{0};\mathbf{z}_{t},\mathbf{y})=\log p(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{z}_{0})+\log p_{\theta}(\mathbf{z}_{0};\mathbf{z}_{t})
.
This makes the method applicable to a broad class of inverse problems.
For known linear or nonlinear forward operators under the Gaussian noise model, the likelihood is explicit, and its contribution to the sampler is obtained by differentiating the measurement residual:

\nabla_{\mathbf{z}_{0}}\log p(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{z}_{0})=-\frac{1}{2\sigma_{y}^{2}}\nabla_{\mathbf{z}_{0}}\left\|\mathbf{y}-\mathcal{A}(\mathcal{D}(\mathbf{z}_{0}))\right\|_{2}^{2}.

When
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{D}
are differentiable, this gradient can be computed directly by automatic differentiation.
This covers standard linear inverse problems such as inpainting, super-resolution, and deblurring, as well as nonlinear inverse problems with known differentiable forward operators.

For fully blind or non-differentiable inverse problems, the likelihood
p(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{z}_{0})
may be unavailable or difficult to differentiate.
In such settings, we draw on recent advances in blind posterior sampling
2
;
45
;
12
and replace the likelihood gradient with a learned contrastive
11
;
15
;
25
surrogate.
Specifically, we can leverage two encoders
f_{\varphi}
and
g_{\psi}
to map decoded clean latents and measurements into a shared smooth embedding space, where compatible pairs are close and mismatched pairs are separated.
The likelihood-gradient term is then approximated as

\nabla_{\mathbf{z}_{0}}\log p(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{z}_{0})\approx\frac{1}{\tau}\nabla_{\mathbf{z}_{0}}\left\langle f_{\varphi}(\mathcal{D}(\mathbf{z}_{0})),g_{\psi}(\mathbf{y})\right\rangle,

where
\tau>0
is a temperature parameter.
This surrogate provides a smooth guidance signal even when the true forward process is unknown, non-differentiable, or only implicitly specified.

Therefore, the same discrete Langevin-inspired sampler applies across known, nonlinear, and blind inverse problems.
Crucially, changing the inverse problem only changes the likelihood term used inside
U(\mathbf{z}_{0};\mathbf{z}_{t},\mathbf{y})
; the discrete posterior proposal and the parallel categorical update remain unchanged.

3.2
Adam Preconditioning and Renoising

In high-dimensional discrete latent spaces, the raw posterior gradient

\nabla_{\mathbf{z}_{0}}U(\mathbf{z}_{0};\mathbf{z}_{t},\mathbf{y})

can be poorly scaled across coordinates.
Some latent coordinates may receive large, noisy gradients, while others receive gradients that are consistently small but informative. Directly using the raw gradient in the discrete Langevin proposal can therefore lead to unstable jumps in some coordinates and slow progress in others.

To address this, we replace the raw gradient with an Adam
16
preconditioned direction.
Thus, Adam does not change the structure of the sampler; it only replaces the raw posterior gradient with an adaptively rescaled update direction.
Intuitively, Adam acts as a diagonal preconditioner that normalizes the gradient using its recent first- and second-moment statistics. This makes the proposal less sensitive to coordinate-wise differences in gradient scale. Large, noisy directions are damped, while consistently useful directions are preserved. This is especially helpful in VQ or discrete latent spaces, where different token coordinates can have very different effects on the decoded image and measurement loss. In practice, this improves mixing and convergence by producing more balanced discrete proposals across the latent grid.

Overall algorithm.
This gives a complete transition from
\mathbf{z}_{t}
to
\mathbf{z}_{s}
: the sampler refines a clean latent estimate
\mathbf{z}_{0}
using the discrete langevin-inspired proposal mechanics, then returns to the diffusion trajectory through the discrete renoising kernel.
This process is repeated for all diffusion time steps
t\rightarrow 0
.

Datasets.

5
Discussion

We introduced
\Delta
LPS
, a Langevin-inspired posterior sampler for inverse problems in discrete state spaces.
Rather than performing slow sequential Gibbs updates or relying on a continuous relaxation as the final representation,
\Delta
LPS
updates token coordinates in parallel through discrete proposals.
We improved the stability of these proposals by using Adam-preconditioned gradients, which adaptively rescale the likelihood and prior gradients across high-dimensional latent coordinates.

\blacksquare

Limitations.

A first limitation is that the method still requires informative gradients of the posterior potential.
For inverse problems with differentiable forward operators, these gradients are directly available, but for highly non-differentiable or black-box measurement processes, one may need surrogate losses, or guided diffusion models as priors.
The quality of the sampler can therefore depend on the fidelity of this gradient signal.
A second limitation is that our current proposal is factorized across token coordinates.
While this enables efficient fully-parallel updates, it may not capture strong dependencies between semantically coupled tokens, especially in highly structured discrete spaces.
Future work could address this through blockwise proposals or via autoregressive correction terms.
Combining
\Delta
LPS
with anchored remasking could help improve the generated posterior samples.

\blacksquare

Follow-up:
Beyond image latents, an important direction is to study inverse problems over naturally discrete data.
Many scientific and creative domains are inherently discrete, including biomolecular sequences, symbolic music representations, program tokens, circuit layouts, and structured design spaces.
These settings may benefit more directly from discrete posterior inference, since the target variables are not merely quantized continuous signals but genuinely combinatorial objects.

\blacksquare

Conclusion.

As discrete generative models continue to scale to longer sequences, larger vocabularies, and multimodal token spaces, principled inference over discrete representations will become increasingly important.
We view gradient-informed discrete samplers, such as
\Delta
LPS
, as a useful primitive for this broader class of future inverse problems.

Appendix D
Discrete diffusion models (trained priors)

D.1
Shared backbone architecture (DUO and MDLM)

Both trained priors use the same transformer denoiser: a DiT-style discrete diffusion transformer applied to flattened token sequences.
Each token is embedded into a hidden width of
256
.
The stack comprises eight transformer blocks, each with eight attention heads (head dimension
32
), bidirectional self-attention, and rotary positional encodings over the token axis.
Every block applies layer normalization, multi-head attention, dropout at rate
0.1
, and a position-wise MLP expanding the hidden width by a factor of four with GELU nonlinearities.
Noise-level information is passed through a
128
-dimensional embedding that drives AdaLN-style feature modulation.
Under DUO training this embedding tracks the instantaneous forward-process noise coordinate; under MDLM training the coordinate fed to the embedder is identically zeroed so the network is not explicitly conditioned on the current noise magnitude even though the forward process still follows the prescribed schedule.
The output layer applies the same modulation recipe before projecting hidden states to per-token logits.
Representations are additionally scaled by a noise-level-dependent gain (the training configuration enables sigma scaling in the forward pass).

D.2
DUO (uniform-state discrete diffusion)

DUO denotes the uniform-state, mean-parameterized discrete diffusion objective trained by ELBO minimization with full noise-level conditioning: the denoiser receives the correct noise coordinate and produces logits that are interpreted as categorical log-probabilities after a softmax.

D.3
MDLM (masked discrete diffusion)

MDLM denotes the absorbing-state formulation with a substitution-style parameterization for masked positions, likewise trained with an ELBO.
Conditioning on the noise magnitude is disabled as in the preceding subsection.
Ancestral updates use a cached implementation for computational efficiency.
During training, likelihood terms are structured so masked positions carry the learning signal while unmasked positions enforce the instantaneous mask pattern.

Appendix G
From Continuous Langevin Proposals to Discrete Categorical Proposals

Our goal is to sample a clean latent sequence
\mathbf{z}_{0}
from a posterior distribution proportional to

p(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{z}_{0})p_{\theta}(\mathbf{z}_{0};\mathbf{z}_{t})
.
Equivalently, we define the log-posterior objective

\displaystyle U(\mathbf{z}_{0};\mathbf{z}_{t},\mathbf{y})=\log p(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{z}_{0})+\log p_{\theta}(\mathbf{z}_{0};\mathbf{z}_{t}).

(13)

We restrict the Langevin-inspired proposal to valid discrete candidates and normalize over the finite state space:

\displaystyle q(\mathbf{z}_{0}^{\prime}|\mathbf{z}_{0},\mathbf{z}_{t},\mathbf{y})

\displaystyle=\frac{\exp\left(-\frac{1}{4\eta}\left\|\mathbf{z}_{0}^{\prime}-\mathbf{z}_{0}-\eta\nabla_{\mathbf{z}_{0}}U(\mathbf{z}_{0};\mathbf{z}_{t},\mathbf{y})\right\|_{2}^{2}\right)}{Z(\mathbf{z}_{0})},

(14)

where

Z(\mathbf{z}_{0})=\sum_{\widetilde{\mathbf{z}}_{0}\in\{1,\ldots,K\}^{L}}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{4\eta}\left\|\widetilde{\mathbf{z}}_{0}-\mathbf{z}_{0}-\eta\nabla_{\mathbf{z}_{0}}U(\mathbf{z}_{0};\mathbf{z}_{t},\mathbf{y})\right\|_{2}^{2}\right)
.

Expanding the exponent in Eqn.
14
gives

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{4\eta}\left\|\mathbf{z}_{0}^{\prime}-\mathbf{z}_{0}-\eta\nabla_{\mathbf{z}_{0}}U(\mathbf{z}_{0};\mathbf{z}_{t},\mathbf{y})\right\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{4\eta}\left\|(\mathbf{z}_{0}^{\prime}-\mathbf{z}_{0})-\eta\nabla_{\mathbf{z}_{0}}U(\mathbf{z}_{0};\mathbf{z}_{t},\mathbf{y})\right\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{4\eta}\left[\|\mathbf{z}_{0}^{\prime}-\mathbf{z}_{0}\|_{2}^{2}-2\eta\nabla_{\mathbf{z}_{0}}U(\mathbf{z}_{0};\mathbf{z}_{t},\mathbf{y})^{\top}(\mathbf{z}_{0}^{\prime}-\mathbf{z}_{0})+\eta^{2}\left\|\nabla_{\mathbf{z}_{0}}U(\mathbf{z}_{0};\mathbf{z}_{t},\mathbf{y})\right\|_{2}^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\nabla_{\mathbf{z}_{0}}U(\mathbf{z}_{0};\mathbf{z}_{t},\mathbf{y})^{\top}(\mathbf{z}_{0}^{\prime}-\mathbf{z}_{0})-\frac{1}{4\eta}\|\mathbf{z}_{0}^{\prime}-\mathbf{z}_{0}\|_{2}^{2}-\frac{\eta}{4}\left\|\nabla_{\mathbf{z}_{0}}U(\mathbf{z}_{0};\mathbf{z}_{t},\mathbf{y})\right\|_{2}^{2}.

(15)

The term
\left\|\nabla_{\mathbf{z}_{0}}U(\mathbf{z}_{0};\mathbf{z}_{t},\mathbf{y})\right\|_{2}^{2}
does not depend on the proposed state
\mathbf{z}_{0}^{\prime}
, so it cancels as the same term appears in the normalization constant. Therefore the overall unnormalized discrete Langevin-inspired proposal can be written as,

\displaystyle q(\mathbf{z}_{0}^{\prime}|\mathbf{z}_{0};\mathbf{z}_{t},\mathbf{y})

\displaystyle\propto\exp\left(\frac{1}{2}\nabla_{\mathbf{z}_{0}}U(\mathbf{z}_{0};\mathbf{z}_{t},\mathbf{y})^{\top}(\mathbf{z}_{0}^{\prime}-\mathbf{z}_{0})-\frac{1}{4\eta}\|\mathbf{z}_{0}^{\prime}-\mathbf{z}_{0}\|_{2}^{2}\right).

(16)

The exponent decomposes across token positions:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\nabla_{\mathbf{z}_{0}}U(\mathbf{z}_{0};\mathbf{z}_{t},\mathbf{y})^{\top}(\mathbf{z}_{0}^{\prime}-\mathbf{z}_{0})-\frac{1}{4\eta}\|\mathbf{z}_{0}^{\prime}-\mathbf{z}_{0}\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}\left[\nabla_{\mathbf{z}_{0}}U(\mathbf{z}_{0};\mathbf{z}_{t},\mathbf{y})\right]_{\ell}\left(\mathbf{z}_{0}^{\prime}[\ell]-\mathbf{z}_{0}[\ell]\right)

\displaystyle-\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}\left(\mathbf{z}_{0}^{\prime}[\ell]-\mathbf{z}_{0}[\ell]\right)^{2}.

(17)

Therefore,

q(\mathbf{z}_{0}^{\prime}|\mathbf{z}_{0};\mathbf{z}_{t},\mathbf{y})=\prod_{\ell=1}^{L}q_{\ell}(\mathbf{z}_{0}^{\prime}[\ell]|\mathbf{z}_{0},\mathbf{z}_{t},\mathbf{y}),

where each coordinate hass categorical logits

\displaystyle r_{\ell,k}=\frac{1}{2}\left[\nabla_{\mathbf{z}_{0}}U(\mathbf{z}_{0};\mathbf{z}_{t},\mathbf{y})\right]_{\ell}\left(k-\mathbf{z}_{0}[\ell]\right)-\frac{\left(k-\mathbf{z}_{0}[\ell]\right)^{2}}{4\eta}.

Thus,
q_{\ell}(\mathbf{z}_{0}^{\prime}[\ell]|\mathbf{z}_{0},\mathbf{z}_{t},\mathbf{y})=\mathrm{Cat}\left(\mathbf{z}_{0}^{\prime}[\ell];\sigma\left(r_{\ell,1},\ldots,r_{\ell,K}\right)\right).

Equivalently,

\displaystyle q(\mathbf{z}_{0}^{\prime}|\mathbf{z}_{0};\mathbf{z}_{t},\mathbf{y})=\prod_{\ell=1}^{L}\frac{\exp\left(r_{\ell,\mathbf{z}_{0}^{\prime}[\ell]}\right)}{\sum_{k=1}^{K}\exp\left(r_{\ell,k}\right)}.

(18)

G.1
Proposal in Codebook-embedding space

Now suppose the discrete tokens index a codebook
\mathcal{C}=\{\mathbf{c}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{c}_{K}\},\qquad\mathbf{c}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

The embedding sequence corresponding to
\mathbf{z}_{0}
is
\mathbf{c}(\mathbf{z}_{0})=\left[\mathbf{c}_{\mathbf{z}_{0}[1]},\ldots,\mathbf{c}_{\mathbf{z}_{0}[L]}\right]\in\mathbb{R}^{L\times d}.

Let

\mathbf{C}_{0}=\mathbf{c}(\mathbf{z}_{0}),\mathbf{C}_{0,\ell}=\mathbf{c}_{\mathbf{z}_{0}[\ell]}.

The posterior objective is

U(\mathbf{z}_{0};\mathbf{z}_{t},\mathbf{y})=\log p(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{z}_{0})+\log p_{\theta}(\mathbf{z}_{0};\mathbf{z}_{t}).

When the measurement likelihood is evaluated through a decoder,

\displaystyle\log p(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{z}_{0})=\log p\left(\mathbf{y}|\mathcal{A}(\mathcal{D}(\mathbf{c}(\mathbf{z}_{0})))\right),

(19)

we can, in this case, define the differentiable likelihood objective

\displaystyle U_{y}(\mathbf{C}_{0};\mathbf{y})=\log p\left(\mathbf{y}|\mathcal{A}(\mathcal{D}(\mathbf{C}_{0}))\right).

(20)

The diffusion-prior term is defined on discrete tokens:

\displaystyle U_{\theta}(\mathbf{z}_{0};\mathbf{z}_{t})=\log p_{\theta}(\mathbf{z}_{0}|\mathbf{z}_{t}).

(21)

Thus,

U(\mathbf{z}_{0};\mathbf{z}_{t},\mathbf{y})=U_{y}(\mathbf{c}(\mathbf{z}_{0});\mathbf{y})+U_{\theta}(\mathbf{z}_{0};\mathbf{z}_{t}).

If the full posterior objective were differentiable with respect to the continuous embedding variable
\mathbf{C}_{0}
, the Langevin-shaped proposal with variance
2\eta
would be

\displaystyle q(\mathbf{C}_{0}^{\prime}|\mathbf{C}_{0};\mathbf{z}_{t},\mathbf{y})

\displaystyle\propto\exp\left(-\frac{1}{4\eta}\left\|\mathbf{C}_{0}^{\prime}-\mathbf{C}_{0}-\eta\nabla_{\mathbf{C}_{0}}U(\mathbf{C}_{0};\mathbf{z}_{t},\mathbf{y})\right\|_{F}^{2}\right).

(22)

Here, the Frobenius norm may be used instead of the
L_{2}
norm. Expanding the exponent gives

\displaystyle q(\mathbf{C}_{0}^{\prime}|\mathbf{C}_{0},\mathbf{z}_{t},\mathbf{y})

\displaystyle\propto\exp\left(\frac{1}{2}\nabla_{\mathbf{C}_{0}}U(\mathbf{C}_{0};\mathbf{z}_{t},\mathbf{y})^{\top}\mathbf{C}_{0}^{\prime}-\mathbf{C}_{0}-\frac{1}{4\eta}\left\|\mathbf{C}_{0}^{\prime}-\mathbf{C}_{0}\right\|_{F}^{2}\right),

(23)

where we have dropped terms independent of the proposal
\mathbf{C}_{0}^{\prime}
.
In our discrete setting, candidate embeddings must come from codebook entries. Therefore, for a proposed token sequence
\mathbf{z}_{0}^{\prime}
, we set

\mathbf{C}_{0}^{\prime}=\mathbf{c}(\mathbf{z}_{0}^{\prime}).

The likelihood part of the directional derivative is available by backpropagation:

\displaystyle\left\langle\nabla_{\mathbf{C}_{0}}U_{y}(\mathbf{C}_{0};\mathbf{y}),\mathbf{c}(\mathbf{z}_{0}^{\prime})-\mathbf{c}(\mathbf{z}_{0})\right\rangle.

(24)

However, the prior term
U_{\theta}(\mathbf{z}_{0};\mathbf{z}_{t})
is defined only on discrete token sequences, so its gradient with respect to
\mathbf{C}_{0}
is not directly available. We approximate its directional contribution by a discrete finite difference over codebook candidates.

For coordinate
\ell
and candidate token
k
, define the sequence obtained by replacing only the
\ell
-th token of
\mathbf{z}_{0}
with
k
:

\displaystyle\mathbf{z}_{0}^{(\ell\rightarrow k)}[m]=\begin{cases}k,&m=\ell,\\
\mathbf{z}_{0}[m],&m\neq\ell.\end{cases}

(25)

The corresponding finite-difference approximation to the prior directional score is

\Delta_{\theta,\ell,k}=U_{\theta}(\mathbf{z}_{0}^{(\ell\rightarrow k)};\mathbf{z}_{t})-U_{\theta}(\mathbf{z}_{0};\mathbf{z}_{t}).

This approximates

\displaystyle\left[\nabla_{\mathbf{C}_{0,\ell}}U_{\theta}(\mathbf{z}_{0};\mathbf{z}_{t})\right]^{\top}\left(\mathbf{c}_{k}-\mathbf{c}_{\mathbf{z}_{0}[\ell]}\right)\approx\Delta_{\theta,\ell,k}.

(26)

Using this finite-difference approximation, the posterior directional score for a full proposal
\mathbf{z}_{0}^{\prime}
is approximated as

\displaystyle\left\langle\nabla_{\mathbf{C}_{0}}U(\mathbf{C}_{0};\mathbf{z}_{t},\mathbf{y}),\mathbf{c}(\mathbf{z}_{0}^{\prime})-\mathbf{c}(\mathbf{z}_{0})\right\rangle

\displaystyle\approx\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}\left[\nabla_{\mathbf{C}_{0,\ell}}U_{y}(\mathbf{C}_{0};\mathbf{y})\right]^{\top}\left(\mathbf{c}_{\mathbf{z}_{0}^{\prime}[\ell]}-\mathbf{c}_{\mathbf{z}_{0}[\ell]}\right)+\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}\Delta_{\theta,\ell,\mathbf{z}_{0}^{\prime}[\ell]}.

(27)

Therefore, the codebook-restricted Langevin proposal becomes

\displaystyle q(\mathbf{z}_{0}^{\prime}|\mathbf{z}_{0},\mathbf{z}_{t},\mathbf{y})

\displaystyle\propto\exp\left(\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}\frac{1}{2}\left[\nabla_{\mathbf{C}_{0,\ell}}U_{y}(\mathbf{C}_{0};\mathbf{y})\right]^{\top}\left(\mathbf{c}_{\mathbf{z}_{0}^{\prime}[\ell]}-\mathbf{c}_{\mathbf{z}_{0}[\ell]}\right)\right.

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\left.+\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}\frac{1}{2}\Delta_{\theta,\ell,\mathbf{z}_{0}^{\prime}[\ell]}-\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}\frac{1}{4\eta}\left\|\mathbf{c}_{\mathbf{z}_{0}^{\prime}[\ell]}-\mathbf{c}_{\mathbf{z}_{0}[\ell]}\right\|_{2}^{2}\right).

(28)

Since every term decomposes across token positions, the proposal factorizes:

\displaystyle q(\mathbf{z}_{0}^{\prime}|\mathbf{z}_{0},\mathbf{z}_{t},\mathbf{y})=\prod_{\ell=1}^{L}q_{\ell}(\mathbf{z}_{0}^{\prime}[\ell]|\mathbf{z}_{0},\mathbf{z}_{t},\mathbf{y}).

(29)

For candidate token
k
at position
\ell
, the local embedding-space proposal logit is

\displaystyle r_{\ell,k}^{\mathrm{emb}}=\frac{1}{2}\left[\nabla_{\mathbf{C}_{0,\ell}}U_{y}(\mathbf{C}_{0};\mathbf{y})\right]^{\top}\left(\mathbf{c}_{k}-\mathbf{c}_{\mathbf{z}_{0}[\ell]}\right)+\frac{1}{2}\Delta_{\theta,\ell,k}-\frac{1}{4\eta}\left\|\mathbf{c}_{k}-\mathbf{c}_{\mathbf{z}_{0}[\ell]}\right\|_{2}^{2}.

(30)

Therefore,

\displaystyle q_{\ell}(\mathbf{z}_{0}^{\prime}[\ell]=k|\mathbf{z}_{0},\mathbf{z}_{t},\mathbf{y})=\frac{\exp\left(r_{\ell,k}^{\mathrm{emb}}\right)}{\sum_{j=1}^{K}\exp\left(r_{\ell,j}^{\mathrm{emb}}\right)}.

(31)

If the diffusion prior factorizes across token positions as

\displaystyle U_{\theta}(\mathbf{z}_{0};\mathbf{z}_{t})=\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}\ell_{\theta,\ell,\mathbf{z}_{0}[\ell]}(\mathbf{z}_{t}),

(32)

then the prior finite difference simplifies to

\displaystyle\Delta_{\theta,\ell,k}=\ell_{\theta,\ell,k}(\mathbf{z}_{t})-\ell_{\theta,\ell,\mathbf{z}_{0}[\ell]}(\mathbf{z}_{t}).

(33)

Since
\ell_{\theta,\ell,\mathbf{z}_{0}[\ell]}(\mathbf{z}_{t})
is constant with respect to the candidate token
k
, it can be dropped inside the softmax normalization. Hence, an equivalent local logit is

\displaystyle r_{\ell,k}^{\mathrm{emb}}=\frac{1}{2}\left[\nabla_{\mathbf{C}_{0,\ell}}U_{y}(\mathbf{C}_{0};\mathbf{y})\right]^{\top}\left(\mathbf{c}_{k}-\mathbf{c}_{\mathbf{z}_{0}[\ell]}\right)+\frac{1}{2}\ell_{\theta,\ell,k}(\mathbf{z}_{t})-\frac{1}{4\eta}\left\|\mathbf{c}_{k}-\mathbf{c}_{\mathbf{z}_{0}[\ell]}\right\|_{2}^{2}.

(34)

This was the version that used we in our experiments The likelihood contribution is obtained by a continuous gradient through the decoder, while the prior contribution is approximated by discrete finite differences over candidate codebook embeddings.

G.2
One-Hot Form

It is also possible to implement the proposal using one-hot representations of the discrete tokens. Let
\mathbf{w}_{0}\in\{0,1\}^{L\times K}
denote the one-hot representation of
\mathbf{z}_{0}
, where
\mathbf{w}_{0,\ell,k}=\mathbf{1}\{\mathbf{z}_{0}[\ell]=k\}.

Equivalently, each row
\mathbf{w}_{0,\ell}
is a one-hot vector

\mathbf{w}_{0,\ell}=\mathbf{e}_{\mathbf{z}_{0}[\ell]}
,
where
\mathbf{e}_{k}
is the
k
-th standard basis vector in
\mathbb{R}^{K}
.
Let
\ell_{\theta,\ell,k}(\mathbf{z}_{t})
denote the diffusion-model logit or log-probability assigned to token
k
at position
\ell
given
\mathbf{z}_{t}
.
A continuous extension of the objective over one-hot variables is

\displaystyle U(\mathbf{w}_{0};\mathbf{z}_{t},\mathbf{y})=\log p(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{w}_{0})+\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\mathbf{w}_{0,\ell,k}\ell_{\theta,\ell,k}(\mathbf{z}_{t}).

(35)

Then the Langevin-shaped proposal over one-hot candidates is

\displaystyle q(\mathbf{w}_{0}^{\prime}|\mathbf{w}_{0},\mathbf{z}_{t},\mathbf{y})

\displaystyle\propto\exp\left(-\frac{1}{4\eta}\left\|\mathbf{w}_{0}^{\prime}-\mathbf{w}_{0}-\eta\nabla_{\mathbf{w}_{0}}U(\mathbf{w}_{0};\mathbf{z}_{t},\mathbf{y})\right\|_{F}^{2}\right).

(36)

Expanding and dropping terms independent of
\mathbf{w}_{0}^{\prime}
gives

\displaystyle q(\mathbf{w}_{0}^{\prime}|\mathbf{w}_{0},\mathbf{z}_{t},\mathbf{y})

\displaystyle\propto\exp\left(\frac{1}{2}\left\langle\nabla_{\mathbf{w}_{0}}U(\mathbf{w}_{0};\mathbf{z}_{t},\mathbf{y}),\mathbf{w}_{0}^{\prime}-\mathbf{w}_{0}\right\rangle-\frac{1}{4\eta}\|\mathbf{w}_{0}^{\prime}-\mathbf{w}_{0}\|_{F}^{2}\right).

Since both
\mathbf{w}_{0}
and
\mathbf{w}_{0}^{\prime}
are row-wise one-hot, this again factorizes across token positions:

\displaystyle q(\mathbf{w}_{0}^{\prime}|\mathbf{w}_{0},\mathbf{z}_{t},\mathbf{y})=\prod_{\ell=1}^{L}q_{\ell}(\mathbf{w}_{0,\ell}^{\prime}|\mathbf{w}_{0},\mathbf{z}_{t},\mathbf{y}).

For candidate token
k
,
\mathbf{w}_{0,\ell}^{\prime}=\mathbf{e}_{k}
, so the local logit is

\displaystyle r_{\ell,k}^{\mathrm{onehot}}=\frac{1}{2}\left[\nabla_{\mathbf{w}_{0,\ell}}U(\mathbf{w}_{0};\mathbf{z}_{t},\mathbf{y})\right]^{\top}\left(\mathbf{e}_{k}-\mathbf{w}_{0,\ell}\right)-\frac{1}{4\eta}\left\|\mathbf{e}_{k}-\mathbf{w}_{0,\ell}\right\|_{2}^{2}.

Therefore,

\displaystyle q_{\ell}(\mathbf{z}_{0}^{\prime}[\ell]=k|\mathbf{w}_{0},\mathbf{z}_{t},\mathbf{y})=\frac{\exp\left(r_{\ell,k}^{\mathrm{onehot}}\right)}{\sum_{j=1}^{K}\exp\left(r_{\ell,j}^{\mathrm{onehot}}\right)}.

Since
\mathbf{w}_{0,\ell}=\mathbf{e}_{\mathbf{z}_{0}[\ell]}
, we can also write

\displaystyle r_{\ell,k}^{\mathrm{onehot}}=\frac{1}{2}\left[\nabla_{\mathbf{w}_{0,\ell}}U(\mathbf{w}_{0};\mathbf{z}_{t},\mathbf{y})\right]^{\top}\left(\mathbf{e}_{k}-\mathbf{e}_{\mathbf{z}_{0}[\ell]}\right)-\frac{1}{4\eta}\left\|\mathbf{e}_{k}-\mathbf{e}_{\mathbf{z}_{0}[\ell]}\right\|_{2}^{2}.

(37)

Because

\displaystyle\left\|\mathbf{e}_{k}-\mathbf{e}_{\mathbf{z}_{0}[\ell]}\right\|_{2}^{2}=\begin{cases}0,&k=\mathbf{z}_{0}[\ell],\\
2,&k\neq\mathbf{z}_{0}[\ell],\end{cases}

(38)

the one-hot form applies a fixed Hamming-distance for changing the token. In particular,

\displaystyle r_{\ell,k}^{\mathrm{onehot}}=\begin{cases}0,&k=\mathbf{z}_{0}[\ell],\\
\frac{1}{2}\left[\nabla_{\mathbf{w}_{0,\ell}}U(\mathbf{w}_{0};\mathbf{z}_{t},\mathbf{y})\right]^{\top}\left(\mathbf{e}_{k}-\mathbf{e}_{\mathbf{z}_{0}[\ell]}\right)-\frac{1}{2\eta},&k\neq\mathbf{z}_{0}[\ell].\end{cases}

Appendix H
Proof for Contrastive Guidance as a valid surrogate:

For completeness, we include a proof from
[
2
]
to show the validity of the contrastive surrogate in blind settings. In situations where the likelihood is intractable,
\Delta
LPS
benefits from the replacement of the likelihood term with a contrastive similarity score from the InfoNCE formulation. This relationship was originally shown in Contrastive Predictive Coding
[
24
]
.
Consider a batch of size
N
with
\mathcal{\widetilde{X}}=\{\mathbf{x}_{j}\}_{j=1}^{N}
and measurements
\mathcal{\widetilde{Y}}=\{\mathbf{y}_{j}\}_{j=1}^{N}
.
For a given clean data sample
\mathbf{x}_{j}
, we shuffle the measurements
\mathcal{\widetilde{Y}}
, so that the corresponding positive measurement
\mathbf{y}_{j}
is placed at position
i\in\{1,\dots,N\}
.
The task is then to identify the correct index
i
. Let
I
be the random variable representing the index of the correct measurement for
\mathbf{x}_{j}
and
p(\mathbf{y})
be the marginal distribution of measurements.
Given a similarity general score function
s(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})
, the InfoNCE assumes a softmax distribution over indices and formulates a cross-entropy loss. So, the approximate posterior
q
:

\displaystyle q(I=i\mid\mathbf{x}_{j},\mathcal{\widetilde{Y}})=\frac{\exp\{s(\mathbf{x}_{j},\mathbf{y}_{i})\}}{\sum_{k=1}^{N}\exp\{s(\mathbf{x}_{j},\mathbf{y}_{k})\}}.

(40)

Specifically, in
\Delta
LPS
, the similarity score function assumes the form
s(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})=\langle f_{\varphi}(\mathbf{x}),\;g_{\psi}(\mathbf{y})\rangle\,/\,\tau

since we use encoders
f_{\varphi}
(for clean data samples), and
g_{\psi}
(for measurements).
Next, we calculate the true optimal posterior
p
from Bayes’ rule as :

\displaystyle p(I=i\mid\mathbf{x}_{j},\mathcal{\widetilde{Y}})

\displaystyle=\frac{p(\mathbf{x}_{j},\mathcal{\widetilde{Y}},I=i)}{\sum_{r=1}^{N}p(\mathbf{x}_{j},\mathcal{\widetilde{Y}},I=r)}

\displaystyle=\frac{\tfrac{1}{N}\,p(\mathbf{y}_{i}\mid\mathbf{x}_{j})\prod_{k\neq i}p(\mathbf{y}_{k})}{\sum_{r=1}^{N}\tfrac{1}{N}\,p(\mathbf{y}_{r}\mid x_{j})\prod_{k\neq r}p(\mathbf{y}_{k})}

\displaystyle=\frac{p(\mathbf{y}_{i}\mid\mathbf{x}_{j})\prod_{k\neq i}p(\mathbf{y}_{k})}{\sum_{r=1}^{N}p(\mathbf{y}_{r}\mid\mathbf{x}_{j})\prod_{k\neq r}p(\mathbf{y}_{k})}

\displaystyle=\frac{\dfrac{p(\mathbf{y}_{i}\mid\mathbf{x}_{j})}{p(\mathbf{y}_{i})}}{\sum_{r=1}^{N}\dfrac{p(\mathbf{y}_{r}\mid\mathbf{x}_{j})}{p(\mathbf{y}_{r})}}.

Matching
q
and
p
requires that the numerators differ only by a multiplicative constant (since softmax is invariant to shifts).
Thus at the InfoNCE optimum,

\displaystyle\exp\big(\langle f_{\varphi}(\mathbf{x}),g_{\psi}(\mathbf{y})\rangle\,/\,\tau\big)

\displaystyle\propto\frac{p(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{x})}{p(\mathbf{y})}

\displaystyle\implies\frac{1}{\tau}\langle f_{\varphi}(\mathbf{x}),g_{\psi}(\mathbf{y})\rangle

\displaystyle\propto\log\;p(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{x})-\log\;p(\mathbf{y})

\displaystyle\implies\frac{1}{\tau}\langle f_{\varphi}(\mathbf{x}),g_{\psi}(\mathbf{y})\rangle

\displaystyle=\log\;p(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{x})-\log\;p(\mathbf{y})+C

(41)

where
C
is independent of
\mathbf{x}
.
Taking gradients with respect to
\mathbf{x}
cancels both
\log\;p(\mathbf{y})
and
C
, yielding

\frac{1}{\tau}\nabla_{x}\langle f_{\varphi}(\mathbf{x}),g_{\psi}(\mathbf{y})\rangle=\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}\log\;p(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{x}).

Appendix I
Societal Impact

\Delta
LPS
is a general posterior sampling method for inverse problems in discrete state spaces.
Its potential positive impact lies in enabling more reliable reconstruction and inference for data that are naturally discrete or tokenized.
This could benefit scientific and engineering applications such as biomolecular design, protein sequence analysis, symbolic music restoration, code completion, circuit/layout design, and compressed media recovery.
More broadly, improved discrete posterior inference may make generative models more useful for solving constrained reconstruction problems where uncertainty quantification is important.

At the same time, the flexibility of
\Delta
LPS
introduces potential risks.
Improved inverse solvers can be misused to reconstruct sensitive information from incomplete, corrupted, or indirect measurements, raising privacy concerns in domains such as images, audio, text, biological data, or other tokenized representations.
Because the method is not tied to a specific prior or measurement operator, it could also be adapted to dual-use settings, including unsafe biological or code-generation objectives.
Finally, as with other generative-model-based inverse solvers, biases in the pretrained discrete diffusion prior may be preserved or amplified during posterior sampling.
These concerns motivate careful dataset curation, access controls for sensitive applications, misuse evaluation, and domain-specific safeguards when deploying such methods outside controlled research settings.
