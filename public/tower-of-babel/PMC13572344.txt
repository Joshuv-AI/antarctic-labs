Circ_0001313 promotes an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment through GM-CSF-associated granulocytic myeloid cell activation in colorectal cancer
Authors: Ya Liu, Longfei Liu, Yingping Quan, Li Wang, Jiaotao Xing, Jun Zhao, Hui Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCirc_0001313 is upregulated in colorectal cancer (CRC) and has been implicated in tumor progression, but its role in remodeling the immune microenvironment remains incompletely defined. This study investigated whether circ_0001313 contributes to CRC progression in association with granulocytic myeloid-derived suppressor cell (gMDSC)-like immunosuppression.MethodsCirc_0001313 expression was evaluated by qRT-PCR in CRC tissues and cell lines. Functional studies were performed using transient siRNA-mediated circ_0001313 knockdown in MC38 cells, a syngeneic murine CRC model suitable for immunocompetent C57BL/6J mice, followed by subcutaneous tumor growth and experimental lung metastasis assays. Tumor immune composition was analyzed by flow cytometry, Ly6G+ granulocytic myeloid cell-mediated suppression of CD8 + T-cell proliferation was assessed by coculture, and tumor explant supernatant (TES) assays, qPCR, ELISA, and GM-CSF neutralization were used to examine a GM-CSF-associated mechanism linked to gMDSC-like activation.ResultsCirc_0001313 was upregulated in CRC tissues and cell lines. Transient circ_0001313 knockdown suppressed primary tumor growth and prolonged survival in the lung metastasis model. Immune profiling showed increased intratumoral CD4 + and CD8 + T cells but reduced Ly6G+ granulocytic myeloid cell abundance after circ_0001313 silencing. Ly6G+ cells from circ_0001313-silenced tumors exhibited lower PD-L1 and arginase 1 expression and diminished suppression of CD8 + T-cell proliferation. Mechanistically, circ_0001313 depletion was associated with reduced tumor-derived GM-CSF expression, and GM-CSF neutralization attenuated TES-induced gMDSC-like activation.ConclusionCirc_0001313 promotes CRC progression, at least in part, by remodeling the tumor immune microenvironment through a GM-CSF-associated granulocytic suppressor program, supporting further evaluation of this axis as a potential therapeutic target in CRC.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s12672-026-05525-9.Keywords: circ_0001313, Colorectal cancer, Tumor microenvironment, Granulocytic myeloid cells, GM-CSF

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) is one of the most prevalent malignancies worldwide and remains a leading cause of cancer-related mortality [1, 2]. Despite advancements in early detection and treatment, the prognosis for patients with advanced CRC remains poor, primarily due to the development of an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment that fosters tumor progression and resistance to therapy [3]. Understanding the molecular mechanisms driving this immunosuppressive environment is critical for identifying novel therapeutic targets and improving patient outcomes.

Among the emerging regulators implicated in CRC progression, circular RNAs (circRNAs) have gained significant attention due to their unique structure and regulatory functions [4–6]. Recent CRC studies have continued to identify noncoding RNA and oncogenic signaling programs that contribute to proliferation, metastasis, matrix remodeling, metabolic adaptation, and disease progression [7–10]. Circ_0001313, in particular, has been identified as a key player in CRC, with its expression strongly associated with poor prognosis and aggressive tumor behavior [11–13]. CircRNAs are known to act as molecular sponges, interacting with microRNAs and proteins to regulate various cellular processes, including proliferation, apoptosis, and metastasis [14]. Recent studies have also highlighted their role in modulating the tumor immune microenvironment, including macrophage-directed and exosomal signaling programs, which is critical for tumor immune evasion and resistance to therapy [15–17].

Myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) are a heterogeneous population of immature myeloid cells that inhibit anti-tumor immunity and facilitate tumor progression [18–22]. Among them, granulocytic MDSCs (gMDSCs) overlap phenotypically with neutrophil-lineage cells and can suppress CD8 + T-cell activity through immune checkpoint molecules and metabolic enzymes [19, 20]. Nevertheless, whether circ_0001313 contributes to CRC progression by reprogramming granulocytic suppressor cells has not been defined.

Here, we investigated the role of circ_0001313 in CRC growth, metastatic colonization, and immune remodeling. Using patient tissues, syngeneic mouse models, immune phenotyping, functional coculture assays, and TES-based mechanistic studies, we tested the hypothesis that circ_0001313 promotes an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment through a GM-CSF-associated gMDSC-like program.

Materials and methods

Animal ethics statement

All animal experiments were reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee of Nanhua Hospital, University of South China, in accordance with institutional guidelines for the care and use of laboratory animals.

Cell line and culture conditions

Colon cancer cell lines (MC38, SW480, SW620, HCT116, HT-29) and the normal colon epithelial cell line NCM460 were provided by ATCC, USA. Cells were maintained as adherent monolayers in RPMI 1640 medium supplemented with 10% fetal calf serum (FCS; Hyclone), 2 mM L-glutamine, 100 U/mL penicillin, and 100 µg/mL streptomycin. Cells were cultured in a humidified incubator at 37 °C with 5% CO2 and subcultured every 3 days. MC38 was selected for in vivo experiments because it is a murine colon adenocarcinoma cell line compatible with immunocompetent C57BL/6 mice, allowing assessment of tumor-immune interactions in a syngeneic setting. The human CRC cell lines were used for expression screening but were not used for the immunocompetent murine in vivo experiments.

Animal studies

Female C57BL/6 mice (4–6 weeks old) were obtained from the Guangdong Experimental Animal Center (Guangzhou, China) and housed under specific pathogen-free conditions. For primary tumor growth studies, transiently transfected MC38 cells (1 × 106) suspended in 100 µL phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) were injected subcutaneously into the right axillary region of each mouse after in vitro confirmation of circ_0001313 knockdown. The final primary tumor growth analysis included 10 mice per group. Tumor dimensions were measured after the tumors became palpable, and tumor volumes were calculated using the formula: V (mm3) = [width2 (mm2) × length (mm)]/2.

For experimental metastasis models, transiently transfected MC38 cells (5 × 105) in 100 µL PBS were injected into the tail vein of recipient mice. Metastatic progression was tracked using bioluminescence imaging (Spectral Ami HT Advanced Molecular Imager). Prior to imaging, mice were intraperitoneally injected with D-luciferin, anesthetized with 2% isoflurane, and imaged using the Xenogen in vivo imaging system (IVIS; Xenogen, Alameda, CA). Bioluminescence signals were analyzed using Living Image 3.2 software, with data presented as pseudocolor heatmaps representing light intensity.

Knockdown of circ_0001313 using siRNA constructs

To silence circ_0001313 expression, small interfering RNA (siRNA) oligonucleotides were designed to target the back-splice junction of circ_0001313 (si-circ_0001313), with a control siRNA (si-NC). Sequences are provided in Supplementary Table S1. This junction-directed design was intended to reduce interference with the corresponding linear CCDC66 transcript. MC38 cells were transiently transfected with 50 nmol/L siRNA using Lipofectamine 2000 (Life Technologies), following the manufacturer’s protocol. RT-qPCR was used to validate circ_0001313 knockdown efficiency before functional assays and before implantation in the in vivo models. The corresponding linear CCDC66 transcript was also measured by RT-qPCR after siRNA transfection to assess junction-directed specificity.

Quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR)

Total RNA was extracted from cells and tissues using the RNeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen). RNA was reverse transcribed into cDNA using a reverse transcription kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Gene-specific primers (Supplementary Table S2) were used for qPCR, performed with SYBR Green reagents on an iCycler iQ detection system (Bio-Rad). Relative gene expression was normalized to GAPDH and calculated using the 2-ΔΔCt method. For the siRNA specificity assay, statistical comparison was performed on ΔCt values from three independent biological replicates.

Tissue collection and single-cell preparation

Tumors were harvested once their diameter reached approximately 1 cm. Fresh tumor tissues were finely minced and digested enzymatically in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) containing 10% FBS, 1 mg/mL collagenase (Sigma-Aldrich, #COLLD-RO), and 0.1 mg/mL DNase I (Sigma-Aldrich, #10104159001) at 37 °C for 1–2 h with gentle agitation. Following digestion, the cell suspension was centrifuged, and the pellet was resuspended in prewarmed Trypsin/EDTA solution and incubated for 5 min at 37 °C to ensure complete dissociation. The reaction was stopped by adding DMEM with 10% FBS. Cells were passed through a 70-µm cell strainer (BD Biosciences), washed, and resuspended in FACS buffer (PBS containing 2% FBS and 2 mM EDTA) for downstream applications.

Ly6G+ granulocytic myeloid cell isolation

Tumor-infiltrating Ly6G+ granulocytic myeloid cells were isolated from single-cell suspensions. Red blood cells were lysed using RBC lysis buffer, and Ly6G+ cells were enriched using the MojoSort Mouse Ly6G Selection Kit (BioLegend, #480124), following the manufacturer’s instructions.

Flow cytometry for immune profiling

Single-cell suspensions were stained for flow cytometry to profile immune cell populations. Fc receptors were blocked by incubating cells with anti-mouse CD16/CD32 (clone 2.4G2; Tonbo Biosciences, #70-0161-U500) for 10 min on ice. Cells were then stained with fluorochrome-conjugated antibodies, including PE-Cy7 anti-CD45 (clone 30-F11), APC anti-CD45 (clone 30-F11), PerCP-Cy5.5 anti-CD11b (clone M1/70), PE-Cy7 anti-Ly6G (clone 1A8), PE anti-CD4 (clone RM4-5), PerCP-Cy5.5 anti-CD8a (clone 53 − 6.7), APC-Cy7 anti-CD3 (clone 145-2C11), and FITC anti-PD-L1 (clone 10 F.9G2), all from Tonbo Biosciences unless otherwise indicated. DAPI (0.5 µg/mL; Sigma-Aldrich, #D9542) was used to exclude dead cells. Data were acquired on a CytoFLEX S flow cytometer (Beckman Coulter) and analyzed using FlowJo v10.8 software. The gating hierarchy was as follows: debris exclusion by forward- and side-scatter, singlet selection using FSC-A/FSC-H, exclusion of DAPI-positive dead cells, CD45 + leukocyte gating, and then lineage gating. T cells were quantified as CD45 + CD3+CD4 + or CD45 + CD3+CD8a+ cells. Granulocytic myeloid cells were quantified as CD45 + CD11b+Ly6G+ cells. Representative full gating strategies for Ly6G+ granulocytic myeloid cells, CD8 + T cells, and CD4 + T cells are provided in Supplementary Fig. S1. Because Ly6G+ tumor-infiltrating neutrophils and granulocytic MDSCs overlap phenotypically in mouse tumors, these cells are referred to as Ly6G+ granulocytic myeloid cells or gMDSC-like cells when the flow phenotype is interpreted together with PD-L1, arginase 1, and T-cell suppression assays.

Ly6G+ granulocytic myeloid cell and T cell coculture assay

Ly6G+ cells were isolated as described above. Splenic T cells were purified from C57BL/6 mice using the MojoSort Mouse CD3 Selection Kit (BioLegend, #480100) and labeled with CFSE (Thermo Scientific, #C34570) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Labeled T cells were activated with plate-bound anti-mouse CD3 (BioLegend, #100302; 5 µg/mL) and anti-CD28 (BioLegend, #102116; 2 µg/mL) antibodies. Ly6G+ cells were cocultured with activated T cells at specified ratios for 48 h. CFSE dilution in CD8 + T cells was assessed by flow cytometry to evaluate T cell proliferation.

Tumor explant supernatant (TES) preparation

Primary MC38 tumors were excised, rinsed in sterile PBS, cut into small fragments (< 3 mm), and incubated in RPMI 1640 medium supplemented with 10% FBS and penicillin-streptomycin at 37 °C for 16–18 h. Tumor fragments from each group were processed in parallel using the same incubation duration and a consistent tumor tissue-to-medium ratio to reduce preparation-related variation. The supernatant was collected, centrifuged to remove debris, and filtered through a 0.22-µm filter. TES was used as a medium supplement at the same final percentage across comparison groups (20% for BMDN differentiation unless otherwise stated) or stored at -80 °C for further experiments. GM-CSF concentrations in TES were measured using a mouse GM-CSF ELISA kit (BioLegend, #432201).

Bone marrow-derived Ly6G+ granulocytic cell differentiation

Bone marrow cells were harvested by flushing femurs and tibias of mice with PBS and passing the suspension through a 40-µm cell strainer. After RBC lysis, cells were cultured in 6-well plates with 20% TES for 3 days. For GM-CSF neutralization, anti-mouse GM-CSF antibody (BioLegend, #505401; 10 µg/mL) or isotype control was added to the culture. Ly6G+ granulocytic cells were subsequently isolated using the MojoSort Mouse Ly6G Selection Kit (BioLegend, #480124).

Clinical specimens

Thirty pairs of colon cancer tissues and non-tumor normal tissues were acquired from patients pathologically diagnosed with colon cancer who underwent surgery at Nanhua Hospital, University of South China between 2016 and 2023. The study involving human participants was approved by the Ethics Committee of Nanhua Hospital, University of South China, and was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent to participate and consent for publication of anonymized clinical data were obtained from all participants prior to the study. All samples were stored at -80 °C for subsequent analysis. Relevant clinical data are summarized in Supplementary Table S3.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were performed independently at least three times with technical duplicates when applicable. Statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism software. Results were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Comparisons between two groups were performed using Student’s t-test for normally distributed continuous variables or the Mann-Whitney U test when distributional assumptions were not met. Multiple-group comparisons were analyzed using one-way ANOVA followed by an appropriate post hoc test. Tumor growth curves were analyzed using two-way repeated-measures ANOVA. Survival curves were generated by the Kaplan-Meier method and compared using the log-rank test. Clinicopathologic categorical variables were compared using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. Statistical significance was denoted as p < 0.05 (*), p < 0.01 (**), p < 0.001 (***), and p < 0.0001 (****).

Results

circ_0001313 promotes colorectal cancer growth and metastatic colonization

Our analysis showed that circ_0001313 expression was higher in colon cancer tissues than in matched adjacent tissues and was also elevated across CRC cell lines relative to NCM460 cells (Fig. 1A, B). To determine its functional role in an immunocompetent model, MC38 cells were transiently transfected with siRNAs targeting circ_0001313, reducing circ_0001313 expression to approximately 23% of the si-NC level before implantation (Fig. 2A). In contrast, the corresponding linear CCDC66 transcript was not significantly altered by si-circ_0001313 (1.00 ± 0.07 vs. 0.98 ± 0.10; P = 0.77; Supplementary Fig. S2), supporting junction-directed specificity. Harvested tumor tissues were not directly reanalyzed for circ_0001313 expression in the available dataset, and this limitation is noted in the Discussion. In the subcutaneous model, circ_0001313 silencing significantly slowed tumor growth (Fig. 2B). In the experimental metastasis model, mice injected with circ_0001313-silenced cells showed prolonged survival relative to controls (Fig. 2C). Together, these data indicate that circ_0001313 supports CRC growth and metastatic progression in vivo.

circ_0001313 shapes an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment by expanding Ly6G+ granulocytic myeloid cells with gMDSC-like features

We next examined whether circ_0001313 affects the immune landscape of CRC tumors. Flow-cytometric profiling of primary tumors showed that circ_0001313 silencing increased the proportions of CD4 + and CD8 + T cells, whereas macrophage abundance was not substantially altered (Fig. 3A). In contrast, CD45 + CD11b+Ly6G+ granulocytic myeloid cells, which represented the dominant immune population in control tumors, were markedly reduced after circ_0001313 knockdown (Fig. 3A, B), suggesting that circ_0001313 preferentially supports an immunosuppressive myeloid compartment. Representative gating strategies and the antibody panel used for these analyses are provided in Supplementary Fig. S1 and Supplementary Table S4.

To further define the functional state of these Ly6G+ cells, we assessed PD-L1 and arginase 1 expression, two markers associated with gMDSC-like suppressive activity [19, 20]. Both molecules were significantly decreased in Ly6G+ granulocytic myeloid cells isolated from circ_0001313-silenced tumors (Fig. 3C), consistent with attenuated immunosuppressive polarization.

We then tested whether these phenotypic changes affected T-cell suppression. In coculture assays, Ly6G+ cells from control tumors strongly inhibited CD8 + T-cell proliferation, whereas Ly6G+ cells from circ_0001313-silenced tumors showed substantially weaker suppressive activity (Fig. 3D). These data indicate that circ_0001313 is associated with enhanced T-cell suppressive function of tumor-associated Ly6G+ granulocytic myeloid cells.

To evaluate the in vivo contribution of these cells, we depleted Ly6G+ cells in both primary tumor and experimental metastasis models. Ly6G+ cell depletion significantly reduced tumor growth and lung metastatic burden (Fig. 3E, F), supporting the conclusion that granulocytic myeloid cells are functionally involved in the tumor-promoting effects observed in this model.

Taken together, these findings support a model in which circ_0001313 fosters CRC progression by expanding and functionally activating Ly6G+ granulocytic myeloid cells with gMDSC-like suppressive features within the tumor microenvironment.

circ_0001313 knockdown is associated with reduced GM-CSF-dependent gMDSC-like induction

To explore the mechanism underlying gMDSC-like induction, we established an in vitro differentiation system using bone marrow cells cultured with tumor explant supernatant (TES) from control or circ_0001313-silenced tumors. TES from control tumors induced robust expression of PD-L1 and arginase 1, whereas TES from circ_0001313-silenced tumors had a substantially weaker effect (Fig. 4A). These results indicate that tumor-derived soluble factors associated with circ_0001313 expression promote gMDSC-like polarization.

We next profiled gMDSC-related cytokines and chemokines in tumor tissues to identify candidate mediators. Among the 11 genes examined, GM-CSF showed the largest decrease after circ_0001313 knockdown, with an approximately 75% reduction at the mRNA level (Fig. 4B). ELISA further confirmed reduced GM-CSF protein in TES derived from circ_0001313-silenced tumors (Fig. 4C).

To test whether GM-CSF contributes to this phenotype, we neutralized GM-CSF in TES from control tumors. GM-CSF blockade attenuated the induction of PD-L1 and arginase 1 in differentiating Ly6G+ granulocytic cells (Fig. 4D), indicating that GM-CSF participates in TES-induced gMDSC-like activation.

Overall, these data suggest that circ_0001313 enhances CRC-associated immune suppression, at least in part, through a GM-CSF-associated soluble program that facilitates gMDSC-like differentiation and activation.

Discussion

Our study identifies circ_0001313 as a potential regulator of the immune microenvironment in CRC. Beyond confirming its elevated expression and tumor-promoting role, we show that transient circ_0001313 knockdown reduces tumor growth, prolongs survival in an experimental metastasis model, increases intratumoral T-cell abundance, and weakens the suppressive phenotype of tumor-associated Ly6G+ granulocytic myeloid cells. This interpretation is consistent with broader evidence that organ microenvironments, including the lung niche, critically influence metastatic outgrowth [23, 24]. Mechanistically, the data support a working model in which circ_0001313 is associated with higher tumor-derived GM-CSF, thereby facilitating gMDSC-like differentiation and immune suppression.

These findings extend previous reports that circ_0001313 (circCCDC66) promotes CRC progression through cell-intrinsic oncogenic pathways [11, 12] by suggesting an additional immune-extrinsic mechanism. The observed reduction of PD-L1 and arginase 1, together with restoration of CD8 + T-cell proliferation after circ_0001313 silencing, supports the interpretation that circ_0001313 influences both the abundance and function of granulocytic suppressor cells. Because gMDSC-like granulocytic cells are increasingly recognized as barriers to effective anti-tumor immunity in CRC, linking circ_0001313 to this compartment provides a plausible explanation for its association with aggressive disease.

The mechanistic data point to GM-CSF as an important downstream soluble mediator. TES from control tumors promoted a gMDSC-like phenotype, whereas TES from circ_0001313-silenced tumors did so less efficiently, and neutralization of GM-CSF attenuated this effect. These results do not establish that circ_0001313 directly controls GM-CSF transcription. Instead, they support a circ_0001313-GM-CSF-gMDSC-like axis as a working model that requires further rescue and pathway-mapping experiments.

This study also has limitations. First, the human cohort was modest in size, limiting the strength of clinicopathological inference. Second, the mechanistic work relied primarily on murine models and ex vivo assays rather than genetic rescue experiments or direct mapping of the upstream transcriptional pathway controlling GM-CSF. Third, although the siRNA was designed against the back-splice junction and did not significantly alter the corresponding linear CCDC66 transcript by qPCR (Supplementary Table S5), RNase R resistance testing and back-splice junction sequencing were not available in the current dataset; therefore, the circRNA-specific validation remains incomplete. Fourth, circ_0001313 suppression was confirmed before implantation, but knockdown was not directly reassessed in harvested tumor tissues. Fifth, Ly6G+ tumor-infiltrating neutrophils and gMDSCs overlap phenotypically in mouse tumors; therefore, we describe the relevant population as Ly6G+ granulocytic myeloid cells with gMDSC-like suppressive features based on surface phenotype, PD-L1/arginase 1 expression, and T-cell suppression assays. Additional response-to-immunotherapy experiments would further strengthen translational relevance.

Conclusion

In summary, our data suggest that circ_0001313 contributes to CRC progression not only through tumor-intrinsic effects but also by remodeling the immune microenvironment. Targeting circ_0001313 or its downstream GM-CSF-associated granulocytic suppressor program may therefore represent a rational strategy for future combination immunotherapy studies in CRC [25].
