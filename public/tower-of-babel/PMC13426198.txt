Spectral cardiac CT in type 1 Kounis syndrome presenting as myocardial infarction with non-obstructive coronary arteries following anaphylactic shock
Authors: Houssem Hamrouni, Wissam Alam, Lamiae Ibne Brahim, Hatem Hamrouni, Nizar Boughanmi, Cyrus Moini, Ivelina Choneva
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Myocardial infarction with non-obstructive coronary arteries MINOCA poses a diagnostic challenge, particularly in complex clinical settings. We report the case of a 62-year-old woman who developed acute myocardial injury during severe anaphylactic shock following a Hymenoptera sting. Although she did not experience chest pain, electrocardiography revealed ST-segment elevation and cardiac biomarkers were markedly elevated. Coronary computed tomography angiography CCTA excluded obstructive coronary artery disease. Notably, delayed dual-energy spectral imaging demonstrated myocardial late iodine enhancement consistent with multifocal infarction, later confirmed by cardiac magnetic resonance imaging. In the context of anaphylaxis, these findings supported the diagnosis of type I KS. This case highlights the importance of systematic cardiac evaluation during severe allergic reactions and underscores the added diagnostic value of spectral CCTA in MINOCA presentations.

Introduction

Myocardial infarction with non-obstructive coronary arteries MINOCA describes a distinct clinical presentation where patients meet the diagnostic criteria for myocardial infarction despite having non-obstructive coronary arteries on coronary imaging. It is considered a working diagnosis that encompasses a heterogeneous spectrum of underlying mechanisms, including plaque disruption, coronary vasospasm, microvascular dysfunction, or supply–demand mismatch [1]. In some situations, due to the complexity of the clinical presentation such as concurrent anaphylaxis or hypersensitivity reactions the diagnosis of myocardial infarction can be particularly challenging. Conditions like Kounis syndrome (KS) illustrate this difficulty, as allergic or hypersensitivity-mediated processes can trigger coronary vasospasm, plaque destabilization, or thrombosis, leading to myocardial injury even in the absence of obstructive coronary artery disease.

Case report

A 62-year-old woman was admitted to the emergency department for acute dyspnea associated with oral mucosal angioedema shortly after a Hymenoptera sting to the neck. On initial evaluation, she appeared hemodynamically unstable, with a blood pressure of 70/40 mmHg and a heart rate of 120 beats/min. She was promptly transferred to the intensive care unit, where treatment for anaphylactic shock was initiated with continuous intravenous epinephrine infusion.

Notably, the patient did not report chest pain at any time. However, routine electrocardiography revealed ST-segment elevation in the apical and lateral leads, without pathologic Q waves. High-sensitivity cardiac troponin (hs-cTn) levels were significantly elevated at 1900 ng/L on admission, reaching a peak of 3900 ng/L 6 hours later, consistent with acute myocardial injury.

Transthoracic echocardiography demonstrated preserved global and regional left ventricular systolic function, without wall motion abnormalities.

Given the unexpected electrocardiographic and biological findings, emergency coronary computed tomography angiography CCTA (Fig. 1) was performed. This was followed by a 5-minute delayed dual-energy spectral acquisition. Cardiac magnetic resonance imaging performed later confirmed the presence of bifocal acute myocardial infarction, while left ventricular ejection fraction remained preserved.

Taken together, these findings, occurring in the setting of severe anaphylactic shock and in the absence of obstructive coronary artery disease, are consistent with MINOCA secondary to type I Kounis syndrome, characterized by allergic mediator-induced coronary vasospasm.

The patient was discharged after few days of observation. Treatment included calcium channel blockers, anti-allergic treatment, and strict allergen avoidance counseling, with beta-blockers avoided due to potential coronary vasospasm exacerbation. Follow-up echocardiography demonstrated no left ventricular dysfunction or dilatation. Cardiac MRI is planned for myocardial scar revaluation.

Discussion

Kounis syndrome (KS) is a rare condition where cardiovascular and immunological process converge after mast cell degranulation [2,3]. The hypersensitivity reaction triggers acute coronary syndrome by releasing vasoactive mediators such as histamine leukotrienes and platelet-activating factor. Three types of KS have been described according to coronary occlusion mechanism. Type I: allergic vasospastic angina in patients without coronary artery disease, it is the most prevalent subtype. Type II: acute myocardial infarction in patients with pre-existing atherosclerosis. Type III: stents thrombosis from hypersensitivity reactions to stent materials. In our case the patient has no cardiovascular risk factors and the CCTA revealed no significant coronary artery disease.

The most reported triggers of this type of KS are antibiotics, benzodiazepines and serotonin receptor antagonists (5-HT3) [4], the diagnosis is usually challenging regarding the complexity of the clinical presentation, acute coronary syndrome can be difficult to diagnose overwhelmed by allergy-related symptoms reaction, it is usually based on electrocardiographic repolarization abnormalities and elevated cardiac enzymes.

The management of KS must simultaneously treat the anaphylactic reaction and confirm or exclude coronary stenosis with imaging such as invasive coronary angiography or in our case a CCTA. According to the mechanism, it can be normal or shows stenosis due to vasospasm in case of type 1 KS, anti-allergic treatment such as corticosteroid and histamine receptors H1 and H2 is usually effective as treatment [5]. In type II, coronary stenosis/occlusion can be due to a preexisting plaque erosion, rupture and a spastic component is usually associated. The management includes treating coronary syndrome according to the standard guidelines along with the treatment allergic reaction. In Type III, stent occlusion requires thrombus aspiration.

Differential diagnosis should be considered in this complex clinical situation, especially when the anaphylactic shock requires intensive and continuous vasoactive drug administration such as epinephrine and norepinephrine that remain the cornerstone to manage various subtypes of hemodynamic instability and shock. By increasing heart rate, systemic blood pressure, and myocardial contractility, epinephrine significantly elevates myocardial oxygen demand [6]. This hemodynamic stress may exacerbate pre-existing coronary artery disease CAD or trigger coronary vasospasm, resulting in a supply-demand mismatch and acute ischemia. This often leads to myocardial infarction that may involve multiple coronary territories. Furthermore, high circulating epinephrine levels may exert direct myocardial toxicity through calcium overload and oxidative stress, manifesting as myocardial injury and necrosis. Takotsubo syndrome can also be considered as an alternative differential, imaging and specially MRI shows characteristic features such as mid and apical akinesia, T2 elevation with no significant late gadolinium enhancement.

Conclusion

Kounis syndrome remains a challenging and often underrecognized cause of acute myocardial infarction in the setting of hypersensitivity reactions. Cardiac involvement may be clinically silent and masked by the severity of anaphylactic manifestations. Early identification is essential, as management requires simultaneous treatment of both the allergic reaction and the myocardial injury.

In this context, CCTA played a decisive role by excluding obstructive coronary artery disease. Moreover, delayed dual-energy spectral acquisition with late iodine enhancement enabled early myocardial tissue characterization, supporting the diagnosis prior to cardiac magnetic resonance confirmation.
