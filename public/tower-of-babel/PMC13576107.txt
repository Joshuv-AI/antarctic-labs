Outcomes in patients undergoing benign prostatic hyperplasia surgical re-treatment with holmium laser enucleation of the prostate compared to treatment-naïve patients

Background

Benign prostatic hyperplasia (BPH) is a highly prevalent condition among aging men and remains a leading cause of lower urinary tract symptoms (LUTS), urinary retention, recurrent urinary tract infections, and reduced quality of life (QOL) [1–3]. Over the past decade, the treatment landscape for BPH has expanded rapidly, driven by increasing demand for less invasive therapies that tout symptom relief with reduced perioperative morbidity, faster recovery, and preservation of ejaculatory function [3]. As a result, a growing number of minimally invasive surgical therapies (MISTs) have been introduced and widely adopted, including prostatic urethral lift, convective water vapor therapy, and various other endoscopic technologies [4–7].

While these procedures have shown favorable short-term outcomes in select patients, concerns persist regarding their long-term durability, particularly in men with larger prostates or more advanced disease [4]. Multiple studies have reported variable re-treatment rates following minimally invasive surgeries, with some patients ultimately requiring additional surgical intervention due to persistent or recurrent symptoms, urinary retention, or complications [8–10]. As the utilization of these therapies continues to rise, urologists are increasingly encountering patients presenting for definitive surgical management after one or more prior BPH interventions [10–12].

Holmium laser enucleation of the prostate (HoLEP) has emerged in the past few decades as a size-independent, durable surgical option for the treatment of BPH, with long-term outcomes superior to traditional transurethral resection of the prostate (TURP) and open or robotic simple prostatectomy [13, 14]. Due to its ability to completely remove obstructing adenomatous tissue regardless of gland size, HoLEP is frequently considered the definitive or “salvage” procedure for patients with failed prior BPH treatments [15]. However, despite its growing role in this setting, data evaluating outcomes of HoLEP in patients undergoing BPH re-treatment, in particular after MIST procedures, remain limited.

The primary objective of this study was to compare perioperative, postoperative, and functional outcomes of patients undergoing HoLEP for BPH re-treatment with treatment-naïve HoLEP patients. We hypothesize that HoLEP performed in the re-treatment setting would show comparable operative safety and functional outcomes to primary treatment-naïve HoLEP.

Methods

Study design and patient population

A retrospective cohort study consisting of patients who underwent HoLEP for BPH at a single tertiary academic medical center between February 2021 and August 2025 was conducted. Institutional review board approval was obtained prior to study initiation. Two fellowship-trained endourologists were included in this study utilizing a bottom-up approach with early apical release using a high-power 120-W MOSES™ 2.0 laser (Boston Scientific Corporation, Marlborough, MA, USA) with pulse modulation. Settings for enucleation were 2 J and 50 Hz and for hemostasis were 2 J and 20 Hz. The Piranha morcellator (Richard Wolf GmbH, Knittlingen, Germany) was utilized for tissue morcellation. HoLEP followed a two-lobe technique when no median lobe was present and a three-lobe technique when present. The re-treatment cohort consisted of patients with a history of at least one prior surgical or minimally invasive BPH intervention before HoLEP. The treatment-naïve cohort consisted of patients undergoing primary HoLEP with no prior BPH procedures.

Prior BPH treatments

Prior BPH interventions were identified and included Aquablation®, Greenlight™ photoselective vaporation of the prostate (PVP), iTind™, microwave ablation, prostate artery embolization (PAE), Rezūm™, UroLift®, TURP, transurethral needle ablation (TUNA), prior HoLEP, robotic simple prostatectomy, and Optilume® BPH. Patients with multiple prior BPH procedures were included, and the total number of prior interventions was recorded.

Data collection

Demographic data, clinical history, preoperative, intraoperative variables, and postoperative outcomes were extracted from the electronic medical record. Functional outcomes were assessed using the American Urological Association Symptom Score (AUASS) and the Michigan Incontinence Symptom Index (M-ISI) [16, 17]. Patients followed a standardized postoperative protocol including a nurse telephone call at approximately 1 week after surgery and a provider visit at approximately 3 months. Postoperative PSA was typically obtained at the 3-month visit to establish a new post-HoLEP baseline. This protocol is consistent with previously published approaches to PSA monitoring and management after HoLEP, including evaluation of unexpected PSA elevation [18].

Propensity score matching and statistical analysis

Patients in the BPH re-treatment cohort were propensity-score matched 1:1 to treatment-naïve controls based on prostate specimen weight. Balance between groups was assessed following matching. Because matching was limited to specimen weight and residual imbalance persisted in age, urinary retention history, and UTI history, multivariable linear regression was performed as a sensitivity analysis for AUASS outcomes. Postoperative AUASS was modeled as the dependent variable, with re-treatment status as the primary exposure and adjustment for preoperative AUASS, age at surgery, history of UTI, history of urinary retention, preoperative prostate size, and specimen weight. Continuous variables were reported as means with standard deviations or medians with interquartile ranges, as appropriate, and compared using independent t-tests. Categorical variables were reported as frequencies and percentages and compared using Chi-square tests. All statistical tests were two-sided, and a p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Statistical analyses were performed using R statistical software (version 4.4.2, R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Results

A total of 418 patients undergoing HoLEP for BPH re-treatment were identified and propensity matched to 418 treatment-naïve HoLEP patients based on prostate specimen weight. Within the re-treatment cohort, 472 prior BPH procedures were recorded. The most common prior intervention was TURP (42%), followed by GreenLight™ PVP (20%), UroLift® (19%), and Rezūm™ (7.2%). Less common prior therapies included PAE, Aquablation®, microwave ablation, and other minimally invasive or surgical techniques (Fig. 1). The majority of patients (88%) had undergone a single prior BPH procedure, while 11% had two prior interventions and 1% had three prior procedures.

Patients undergoing re-treatment were slightly older than treatment-naïve controls (72 ± 8 vs. 70 ± 7 years, p < 0.001). Body mass index (BMI) and prostate volume were similar between cohorts. The re-treatment group had higher rates of preoperative urinary retention (60.0% vs. 41.8%, p = 0.006) and prior urinary tract infection (UTI) (26.3% vs. 22.0%, p < 0.001). Prostate specimen weight was identical between groups (71 g in both cohorts, p > 0.9), confirming adequate matching. American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) class distribution and anticoagulation use did not differ significantly (Table 1).

Mean procedure time, enucleation time, morcellation time, and laser energy usage were comparable between cohorts. Intraoperative complication rates were low and similar (3.1% vs. 1.9%, p = 0.4). UTI/infection accounted for 5/38 (13.2%) ED visits in the re-treatment cohort and 1/22 (4.5%) in the treatment-naïve cohort. Most 90-day complications were Clavien-Dindo grade I–II in both cohorts, while grade III–IV events were uncommon (1.2% vs. 1.4%).

Discussion

In this retrospective propensity-matched analysis, HoLEP performed in the BPH re-treatment setting demonstrated perioperative safety profiles and postoperative symptom improvements comparable to treatment-naïve HoLEP. Despite a heterogeneous mix of prior interventions, operative efficiency, complication rates, same-day trial-of-void success, and short-term functional outcomes were similar between cohorts, with no signal of excess long-term complications such as urethral stricture or bladder neck contracture. To our knowledge, this is one of the first and possibly largest study investigating HoLEP after MIST and other alternative BPH therapies. These findings support HoLEP as a safe, durable and effective option even after prior BPH surgery.

As the BPH treatment landscape continues to evolve, urologists are increasingly encountering men with persistent or recurrent LUTS following one or more prior interventions. The rapid expansion of MISTs has broadened treatment options but has also introduced variability and uncertainty in long-term durability, particularly across prostate sizes and follow-up horizons. Contemporary series have demonstrated significant re-treatment or ongoing medical therapy rates following commonly used modalities [7, 19, 20]. Five-year retreatment rates have been reported in the range of approximately 4–11% for Rezūm™ [8, 20], 22% for UroLift® [12], and 4–13% for Greenlight™ PVP [21, 22], with higher rates observed in larger glands and longer follow-up. Aquablation® has demonstrated lower early retreatment rates in selected populations, approximately 4–6% [8]. While randomized and prospective data support the short- to mid-term efficacy of several MISTs in appropriately selected patients with smaller glands, durability in larger prostates remains less well established, with higher rates of subsequent intervention or continued symptom management reported in real-world and meta-analytic studies [4, 23, 24]. In contrast, HoLEP has consistently demonstrated long-term retreatment rates typically below 2% at 5 years across multiple series, independent of prostate size [14, 25]. However, data evaluating perioperative and functional outcomes of HoLEP specifically in heterogeneous re-treatment populations spanning multiple prior modalities remain limited.

Our study cohort reflects real-world re-treatment pathways rather than a single prior intervention, with a comprehensive inclusion of prior therapies ranging from TURP and PVP to implant-based and thermal MISTs. Our findings align with existing literature suggesting that salvage HoLEP is feasible, safe, and effective following prior BPH procedures [15, 26]. A contemporary synthesis of salvage HoLEP studies demonstrated that prior intervention generally does not preclude identification of the enucleation plane and that outcomes remain favorable in appropriately selected patients [15]. At our institution, we similarly have found that while prior surgery can sometimes distort the planes, this has not led to significant differences in overall procedure time or enucleation time. We similarly found that the enucleation plane was identifiable in nearly all salvage cases, although prior Aquablation® and GreenLight™ PVP occasionally produced scarring or synechiae that required more deliberate dissection. In the hands of high-volume fellowship-trained endourologists, these findings did not translate into clinically meaningful differences in operative efficiency, but they remain important technical considerations for surgeons performing HoLEP after prior BPH intervention. Because prior BPH intervention may distort the enucleation plane and increase technical difficulty, surgeons early in the HoLEP learning curve should gain experience with treatment-naïve cases before attempting salvage HoLEP.

The most extensively studied salvage scenario remains HoLEP after TURP. A 2022 analysis demonstrated that prior TURP did not negatively impact HoLEP outcomes for symptomatic recurrence, reinforcing the concept that HoLEP can provide durable relief even when an initial transurethral procedure fails [27]. Beyond TURP, the literature describing HoLEP following implant-based or thermal minimally invasive surgical therapy failure is emerging. HoLEP after failed prostatic urethral lift has been reported as safe and effective, with considerations primarily related to morcellation and implant-associated challenges, including occasional morcellator blade issues [28, 29]. More recently, a large single-institution analysis evaluating HoLEP after prior prostatic urethral lift demonstrated comparable perioperative and postoperative outcomes despite higher baseline irritative symptoms in the re-treatment cohort, strengthening the role of HoLEP as an effective salvage strategy following MIST failure [30]. In our cohort, which included patients with multiple prior interventions, we found that operative efficiency and complication rates did not lead to clinically significant disadvantages, supporting the concept that HoLEP can function as a unifying salvage strategy across diverse prior BPH treatments. We did not encounter intraoperative difficulty from UroLift® implants; however, we routinely used a “catch-and-release” morcellation technique to avoid entrapping clips within the morcellator blades, which may reduce blade jamming, unnecessary blade exchanges, and associated intraoperative delays. This technique has been detailed in previous literature [29].

Stress urinary incontinence is commonly cited as a concern following HoLEP, particularly when the procedure is performed after prior BPH intervention [31]. In the present study, postoperative M-ISI severity scores were modestly lower in the re-treatment cohort compared with treatment-naïve patients, while the magnitude of improvement from baseline did not differ between groups. Given incomplete patient-reported outcome data, this postoperative difference should be interpreted cautiously. This pattern suggests that overall continence recovery trajectories are comparable, and that prior intervention does not confer an increased long-term incontinence burden following HoLEP. Although statistically significant, the absolute difference in postoperative M-ISI severity scores was modest and may not be clinically meaningful. One possible explanation is that re-treatment patients, having already undergone prior BPH intervention, may perceive greater relative improvement or satisfaction after definitive symptom relief with HoLEP. From a counseling standpoint, these findings are reassuring and support the use of HoLEP in the re-treatment setting without expectation of worse continence outcomes, while reinforcing the importance of individualized risk assessment in patients with preexisting LUTS.

In our study, we found that ED encounters within 90 days occurred more frequently among patients undergoing HoLEP in the re-treatment setting. This difference may reflect the more complex preoperative clinical profile of these patients, including higher rates of preoperative urinary retention and UTI, rather than procedure-related morbidity. Patients with prior BPH interventions may also be more likely to seek urgent evaluation for postoperative urinary symptoms based on previous experiences, heightened concern for recurrence, or lower tolerance for transient postoperative changes such as hematuria or voiding difficulty. Fortunately, this increase in ED utilization was not associated with higher readmission rates, perioperative complications, or inferior functional outcomes, suggesting that these encounters may represent precautionary care rather than clinically significant adverse events. Such findings represent an opportunity to increase perioperative counseling and early postoperative follow-up in the re-treatment population, particularly with respect to expected recovery, catheter management, and symptom thresholds that warrant urgent evaluation.

This study is not without limitations. Its retrospective, single-center design introduces the potential for selection bias and limits generalizability. Propensity score matching was performed using specimen weight as a proxy for adenoma burden, a major driver of HoLEP operative complexity. This approach has inherent limitations, particularly because matching was limited to a single variable. Propensity score matching can only balance measured variables included in the matching model and cannot account for unmeasured or incompletely captured differences between groups. Therefore, residual confounding may persist, including differences in baseline detrusor function, degree of obstruction, prostate configuration, medication exposure, surgeon-level factors, and socioeconomic drivers of postoperative utilization. However, matching was intentionally restricted to specimen weight to preserve the largest possible re-treatment cohort, as additional candidate variables had greater missingness and would have substantially reduced the matched sample size. The re-treatment cohort was also heterogeneous with respect to prior procedure type and number of interventions. The re-treatment cohort was analyzed as an aggregate salvage HoLEP population to address whether HoLEP remains safe and effective after prior BPH intervention in real-world practice. We recognize that prior therapies may create different technical challenges, including altered enucleation planes after resection or ablative procedures and morcellation issues after implant-based therapies. However, subgroup analyses by prior intervention type were limited by small sample sizes and low event rates, which would yield unstable estimates and risk overinterpretation. Finally, outcomes from a high-volume HoLEP center may not be directly generalizable to lower-volume settings, and results should be interpreted in the context of surgeon experience. Further studies will be necessary as MIST therapy uptake increases to delineate long-term re-treatment rates.

Conclusions

In this propensity-matched cohort, HoLEP performed after prior BPH intervention demonstrated comparable perioperative safety and postoperative symptom improvement to treatment-naïve HoLEP, supporting its role as a definitive and durable treatment option for recurrent or residual BPH symptoms as minimally invasive therapies with variable long-term durability continue to expand.
