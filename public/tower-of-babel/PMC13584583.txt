Revealing mandibular asymmetry in orthognathic surgery patients via Bonwill triangle morphology: a retrospective cross-sectional study
Authors: Shiuan-Hui Wang, Tzu-Yuan Weng, Hao Shih, Yen-Wen Shen, Lih-Jyh Fuh, Jui-Ting Hsu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe Bonwill triangle, defined by the mandibular incisor (MI) point and the center of right (CR) and left (CL) condyles, which provide a crucial reference for determining craniofacial symmetry and occlusion. Although three-dimensional imaging has enhanced the precision of triangle measurement, few studies have evaluated Bonwill triangle geometry in patients who have undergone orthognathic surgery (OGS). The present study assessed Bonwill triangle geometry in a Taiwanese population by comparing individuals who underwent OGS and those who did not and by analyzing the effects of sex and age on mandibular asymmetry.MethodsCone-beam computed tomography images from 109 adults (54 in the OGS group and 55 in the non-OGS group) were retrospectively analyzed. Three side lengths of the Bonwill triangle (mandibular incisor point to left condyle (MI-CL), mandibular incisor point to right condyle (MI-CR), and right condyle to left condyle (CR-CL)) were measured using Mimics software. Group comparisons and subgroup analyses by sex and age were conducted using independent and paired t tests and Pearson correlation analysis.ResultsThe OGS group exhibited greater asymmetry in the bilateral side lengths than the non-OGS group did (3.41 ± 2.35 mm vs. 1.69 ± 1.02 mm, p < 0.001), particularly the men in the group (p < 0.001). Additionally, only the men in the OGS group exhibited a negative correlation between age and bilateral side length (r = − 0.480, p = 0.034). CR–CL length did not differ significantly between the OGS and non-OGS groups.ConclusionsThe Bonwill triangle can support preoperative mandibular asymmetryassessments. Candidates for OGS, especially men, exhibit greater skeletal asymmetry than non-OGS candidates do, underscoring a need for individualized planning. Future studies evaluating surgical type and long-term outcomes can enhance the clinical applications of the Bonwill triangle in pre-OGS assessments.Trial registrationThis retrospective study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of China Medical University Hospital, Taichung, Taiwan (CMUH 114REC2019).

Introduction

The Bonwill triangle was described in 1885 by American dentists William Gibson and Arlington Bonwill, who analyzed approximately 6,000 cadaver skulls and 4,000 living individuals [1]. The Bonwill triangle is defined by three anatomical landmarks: the mandibular incisor point (MI) and the centers of the right condyle (CR) and left condyle (CL), with each side measuring approximately 100 mm (4 inches) in length [1]. This geometrically stable configuration frequently provides a reference for evaluating mandibular symmetry and occlusal balance [2, 3].

Bonwill triangle measurements were traditionally obtained using calipers on dry skull specimens [4]. However, the development of three-dimensional imaging has established cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT) as the standard modality for assessing craniofacial structures [5–7]. CBCT provides high-resolution, noninvasive imaging that enables accurate and objective Bonwill triangle measurements in clinical settings [3], enhancing its applicability in live populations and preoperative planning. Studies have validated the clinical relevance of CBCT Bonwill triangle measurements across diverse populations, including East Asian cohorts with varying skeletal patterns [8, 9].

In clinical practice, orthognathic surgery (OGS) is often indicated for patients with maxillomandibular skeletal discrepancies that cause malocclusion or facial asymmetry, such as skeletal protrusion, retrusion, or crossbite [10, 11]. Preoperative evaluation of OGS candidates requires an assessment of multiple craniofacial parameters to determine skeletal symmetry. However, no standardized geometric index is currently available to quantify these features, and no individualized surgical simulations have been developed. Most studies on the Bonwill triangle have examined ethnic or sex-specific differences [12, 13], which may reflect genetic, masticatory, or lifestyle influences. By contrast, few studies have investigated Bonwill triangle parameters in patients with skeletal mandibular asymmetry or mandibular developmental disorders, and the triangle’s application in OGS assessments remains underexplored [12, 13].

Some researchers have questioned the applicability of classical Bonwill triangle geometry for patients with skeletal deformities, particularly those requiring OGS [13]. Nevertheless, the stable three-point configuration reveals underlying craniofacial asymmetries from deviations in bilateral side lengths, providing clinically relevant information [14]. When integrated with CBCT-based three-dimensional analysis, the Bonwill triangle can serve as a useful indicator for identifying skeletal asymmetry in OGS candidates [3]. Nevertheless, few systematic investigations have examined Bonwill triangle morphology in individuals who have undergone OGS. If specific geometric deviations can be identified in such patients, the Bonwill triangle may provide a reference for individualized surgical evaluations and craniofacial structure analysis [15].

The present study investigated Bonwill triangle geometry in a Taiwanese population by using CBCT imaging. This study compared groups of patients who had and had not undergone OGS and conducted subgroup analyses stratified by sex and age to evaluate the correlations between triangle measurements and asymmetry and midline variation [8]. The results clarify the role of the Bonwill triangle in diagnosing mandibular asymmetry and assessing craniofacial symmetry, addressing gaps in the literature regarding skeletal anomalies. These findings also support the development of preoperative reference standards and individualized surgical planning tools.

Materials and methods

Patient selection and exclusion criteria

The dental CBCT images used in this study were acquired from the Department of Dentistry at China Medical University Hospital in Taichung, Taiwan, between 2018 and 2023. Eligible samples were selected from the dataset and analyzed using Mimics 14.0 (Materialise, Leuven, Belgium) medical imaging software. The CBCT scans were obtained using a ProMax 3D Max system (Planmeca, Helsinki, Finland) at a resolution of 400 μm, a tube voltage of 96 kV, a tube current of 5.6 mA, and an exposure time of 18 s. The analysis evaluated images from 109 individuals (48 men and 61 women). The Institutional Review Board of China Medical University Hospital approved this study (CMUH 114-REC2-019).

The general inclusion criteria for all participants were as follows: (1) age ≥ 18 years; and (2) availability of complete CBCT imaging data encompassing the entire region of interest suitable for geometric analysis. To minimize confounding factors, the general exclusion criteria applied to all subjects comprised: (1) history of acute craniofacial infection, significant trauma, or tumors at the time of imaging; (2) presence of supernumerary teeth within the measurement region; and (3) substantial metal or motion artifacts in the CBCT images that could impede accurate landmark identification.

For group allocation, eligible individuals were subsequently categorized into two groups based on their treatment history: (a) Non-OGS Group: Individuals who had not undergone orthognathic surgery. To ensure a stable baseline, patients who had a history of orthodontic treatment or those scheduled for future orthognathic surgery were excluded from this group. (b) OGS Group: Individuals who received orthognathic surgery at our institution. To ensure the consistency of surgical interventions, all procedures were independently performed by one of two experienced oral and maxillofacial surgeons. Furthermore, the pre-operative CBCT images for this group must have been acquired within six months prior to the surgery. Notably, to eliminate the risk of selection bias, this study strictly included only OGS patients who underwent orthognathic surgery followed by orthodontic treatment. Because the pre-operative CBCT images were acquired before any surgical or orthodontic interventions, all patients in this group were in a completely un-orthodontized, pristine state. This ensured that the mandibular incisor (MI) point remained natural and unaltered. Only pre-operative CBCT images obtained within six months prior to surgery were included in this study.

Bonwill triangle measurement

The CBCT images were imported in Digital Imaging and Communications in Medicine format into Mimics. The minimum threshold was set to 480 Hounsfield units to define the bone tissue, and the maximum threshold remained at its default value. The “Region Growing” function was used to identify the region of interest, and the “Calculate Part” function was employed to generate a three-dimensional cranial reconstruction (Fig. 1.) and localize the Bonwill triangle reference points as follows: (a) MI point–central point of the mandibular incisor edge, typically the central incisor edge. (b) CR–center point of the right condyle. (c) CL–center point of the left condyle. The processed CBCT images were used to measure the Bonwill triangle in Mimics. The “Measure Distance” function was used to calculate the lengths of the three line segments of the triangle: (a) MI–CR, (b) MI–CL, and (c) CR–CL. (Fig. 2.)

Statistical analysis and measurement reliability

Before parameters were evaluated, the accuracy of the measurements was verified. Intraclass correlation coefficients (ICCs) were calculated to assess intra- and inter-examiner reliability. Ten CBCT image sets were randomly selected for consistency analysis. Four professionals conducted the measurements: two experienced dentists, one senior faculty member in dentistry, and one dental radiologic technologist. These professionals evaluated Bonwill triangle reference points, angles, and segment lengths. Repeated measurements by all four raters were used to determine the inter-examiner error. ICC values for Bonwill triangle measurements across CBCT images ranged from 0.916 to 0.948. To assess intra-examiner reliability, one examiner repeated the measurements of nine parameters, yielding ICC values between 0.930 and 0.958. These values indicated negligible intra- and inter-examiner variability.

The sample size was determined separately for the male and female participants on the basis of the literature. A priori power analysis was conducted based on population-level Bonwill triangle measurements reported in previous studies [8, 14, 21]. Published differences of approximately 4–6 mm with standard deviations of 1.0–1.5 mm indicated that fewer than 20 participants per group would be required to achieve adequate power. Our final sample (54 OGS, 55 non-OGS) therefore exceeded the estimated requirement for detecting group differences. The Kolmogorov–Smirnov test was used to assess data normality, and the results indicated that all groups, including those with the smallest sample sizes, conformed to a normal distribution. Hence, parametric statistical methods were used for analysis. For each sex and age subgroup, descriptive statistics (minimum, maximum, mean, and standard deviation) were calculated for Bonwill triangle parameters, specifically, the intercondylar distance (CR–CL) and condyle-to-incisor distances (MI–CR and MI–CL). An additional derived variable, the average left–right side length, was defined as the mean of the two arms: (MI–CL + MI–CR) / 2.

The participants were initially categorized on the basis of whether they had undergone OGS, and independent t tests were conducted to compare the Bonwill triangle geometry between the OGS and non-OGS groups. Subgroup analyses were conducted as follows:

(1) Sex-based comparison

Independent t tests were used to compare Bonwill triangle side lengths between the men and the women.

(2) Left–right side length comparison:

A.Paired t tests were used to determine whether a significant difference existed between the left and right sides (MI–CL vs. MI–CR) within each group.B.To evaluate the true magnitude of asymmetry regardless of the deviation direction, the absolute difference between MI–CL and MI–CR (|MI–CL - MI–CR|) was calculated for each individual. Independent t tests were used to compare this asymmetry between the OGS and non-OGS groups.

A p value of < 0.05 was considered significant. Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (IBM Corporation, Armonk, NY, USA) was used for all statistical analyses.

Results

Overall measurements and sex-based statistical analysis

This study included CBCT data from 109 patients (age range: 18–65 years; mean age: 29.24 years), 54 in the OGS group (mean age: 30.18 years) and 55 in the non-OGS group (mean age: 28.21 years). In the OGS group, the mean left-side length (MI–CL) was 105.30 ± 6.38 mm, the mean right side length (MI–CR) was 105.59 ± 6.68 mm, and the mean central condylar length (CR–CL) was 102.59 ± 6.30 mm. By contrast, in the non-OGS group, the corresponding mean lengths were 101.73 ± 5.16 mm (MI–CL), 101.98 ± 4.93 mm (MI–CR), and 103.49 ± 5.52 mm (CR–CL). Between-group comparisons indicated that the OGS group had significantly longer MI–CL and MI–CR distances than the non-OGS group did (p = 0.002 for both). However, no significant difference was observed in CR–CL distance between the groups (p = 0.395). The results of a subgroup analysis stratified by sex that was conducted using independent t tests revealed significant differences in all three Bonwill triangle side lengths between the men and the women (MI–CL: p < 0.001; MI–CR: p < 0.001; CR–CL: p < 0.001). Correlation between bonwill triangle side lengths and age

This study analyzed the correlation between age and Bonwill triangle measurements, specifically, the average of the left and right side lengths and the central length (condyle-to-condyle distance), by using Pearson correlations. The patients were stratified into four subgroups according to receipt of OGS and sex: OGS men, OGS women, non-OGS men, and non-OGS women.

(1) Correlation between average left–right side length and age

A significant negative correlation was observed only in the OGS men subgroup, in which age was moderately associated with reduced average left–right side length (r = − 0.480, p = 0.034). No significant correlations were observed in the OGS women, non-OGS men, or non-OGS women groups (all p > 0.05), and the corresponding r values indicated weak or nonsignificant correlation (Fig. 3).

No significant correlations were identified between age and the condyle-to-condyle distance (CR–CL) in any of the four subgroups (all p > 0.05). Overall, age exerted minimal influence on the central length.

Discussion

The Bonwill triangle has long provided a geometric reference for evaluating mandibular symmetry. However, its clinical relevance in patients with skeletal deformities, such as those who have undergone OGS, remains unclear. The present study employed CBCT imaging to compare Bonwill triangle parameters between adults who had and had not undergone OGS and identified distinct geometric deviations in the OGS group. By contrast, the non-OGS group exhibited near-symmetric patterns consistent with normative craniofacial morphology. These results suggest that the Bonwill triangle may provide an auxiliary indicator for assessing mandibular asymmetry during preoperative planning.

It is important to note that the OGS group in this study consisted of patients with a history of orthognathic surgery but without preoperative–postoperative comparison. As such, the asymmetry observed in this group reflects the characteristics of patients who require orthognathic correction, rather than postoperative outcomes. This cross-sectional design does not permit conclusions about the effectiveness of surgery or postoperative symmetry.

To clarify the clinical implications of these findings, comparisons with population-based studies across ethnic cohorts were conducted. Studies on the Bonwill triangle have been conducted on North American [16, 17], African [18], European [13, 19], and Asian populations [8, 14, 20], progressively establishing region-specific geometric references (Table 3). For example, the mean CR–CL in Korean men is 108.3 mm [8], whereas that in Indian women is 102.6 mm [14], which reflects the influence of ethnicity and region on mandibular morphology. The results from the Taiwanese non-OGS group in the present study align with these regional patterns and suggest shared geometric traits within East Asian populations [21].

Bonwill triangle characteristics in patients who have undergone OGS remain understudied, and quantitative evaluations of mandibular asymmetry and skeletal abnormalities are rare [12]. Nevertheless, skeletal asymmetry is prevalent among patients who undergo OGS, and conventional Bonwill triangle parameters may inadequately estimate geometric deviations during occlusal plane simulations. Another study similarly reported substantial interindividual variation in the preoperative virtual hinge axis positions among patients who undergo OGS, underscoring the need for individualized mandibular structural assessments [13]. Addressing this gap, the present study systematically analyzed CBCT-derived Bonwill triangle parameters in a Taiwanese cohort, providing the first comparison between OGS and non-OGS groups. Additionally, this study conducted stratification by sex and age to explore the influence of Bonwill triangle parameters on craniofacial structural asymmetry.

The results reveal that the OGS group had a significantly greater absolute difference in side lengths (3.41 ± 2.35 mm) than that in the non-OGS group (1.69 ± 1.02 mm; p < 0.001), with the most pronounced discrepancy observed in the OGS men (3.69 ± 2.61 mm) subgroup. These findings align with clinical observations of skeletal mandibular asymmetry and facial disproportion commonly identified in OGS candidates [12, 13]. By contrast, the non-OGS group exhibited near-equal side lengths, consistent with the symmetry documented in healthy populations [8, 14, 19]. Although central length (CR–CL) did not differ significantly between the groups (p = 0.395), the greater variation within the OGS group suggests underlying skeletal instability. These results highlight the importance of incorporating individual asymmetry assessments into preoperative planning to enhance surgical simulation accuracy [3]. Beyond skeletal deformities, functional laterality—such as habitual chewing patterns and asymmetric masticatory muscle tension—may also contribute to variations in mandibular morphology. Differential mechanical loading on the condyles during growth and development can influence bone remodeling, potentially leading to the observed discrepancies in the Bonwill triangle dimensions. While the present study focused on static skeletal measurements, the interplay between functional habits and geometric asymmetry remains an important consideration in craniofacial analysis.

Further analysis of left–right side length differences across sex subgroups ranked the subgroups in descending order of asymmetry as follows: OGS men > OGS women > non-OGS men > non-OGS women. Pairwise comparisons revealed that the OGS men exhibited significantly greater asymmetry than the non-OGS men (p = 0.007) and non-OGS women (p < 0.001) did. The OGS women also exhibited significantly greater asymmetry than the non-OGS women (p = 0.007) did. These findings indicate that skeletal abnormalities affect mandibular asymmetry more profoundly than sex alone does, underscoring a clinical need for patient-specific assessments for OGS [22, 23].

In cross-population comparisons (Table 3), the Bonwill triangle dimensions in the Taiwanese non-OGS group were similar to those reported in Japanese and Korean populations, consistent with East Asian craniofacial traits. However, the geometric differences between the OGS and non-OGS individuals within the same population exceeded those observed across ethnic groups. For example, Yin et al. (2021) [9] identified significant geometric differences in vertical skeletal patterns in Han Chinese adults from Liaoning (p < 0.05), although the symmetry within each group remained high (p > 0.05). These results support the present study’s conclusion that skeletal abnormalities exert a greater effect on mandibular geometry than ethnic variation does [24], indicating that individualized evaluations should be prioritized over generalized population-based references during preoperative planning for OGS [13].

Studies on the application of the Bonwill triangle in patients with skeletal abnormalities are lacking, and the findings of the current study demonstrate that the precise geometric measurements obtained through CBCT effectively reflect variations in mandibular asymmetry. Hence, the Bonwill triangle may provide a useful auxiliary indicator for the early detection of skeletal mandibular asymmetry or developmental irregularities, supporting individualized evaluation and planning for OGS. Originally conceptualized as an equilateral triangle [1, 2], subsequent studies have documented considerable variation across populations and individuals [8, 19, 20], underscoring a need for patient-specific geometric assessments. With advances in three-dimensional imaging, integrating Bonwill triangle analysis into skeletal asymmetry evaluations and preoperative classifications may increase surgical planning precision. Future incorporation of three-dimensional occlusal simulations and soft-tissue correspondence analysis can support the development of personalized treatment protocols.

In summary, the Bonwill triangle side lengths observed in the Taiwanese population in the present study resembled those reported in other East Asian cohorts, reflecting region-specific anatomical features [8, 9, 20]. Significant asymmetry was identified in the OGS group, particularly among the men, highlighting the influence of skeletal abnormalities on mandibular geometry [12, 13]. Additionally, correlations noted between age and side length parameters in specific subgroups suggest that mandibular development and stability may be influenced by age. In this study, a moderate negative correlation was observed between age and the bilateral average length of the Bonwill triangle in male patients who had undergone orthognathic surgery (r = − 0.480) [25–27]. This variable was included to reflect overall mandibular length rather than bilateral symmetry. In patients with asymmetry, variations in mandibular size—such as retrusion or elongation—cannot be determined from either side alone, and the averaged value therefore serves as a general indicator of mandibular size. From a biological perspective, previous studies have demonstrated that mandibular length tends to decrease with age in males, likely due to reductions in bone mineral density and age-related declines in skeletal remodeling capacity. Clinically, this trend may also reflect a selection bias among surgical candidates: younger patients undergoing CBCT evaluation are often those with more severe skeletal deformities, who seek early intervention due to esthetic or functional concerns. In contrast, older patients may present with milder conditions or compensatory adaptations that delayed their need for treatment. Therefore, the observed inverse relationship between age and mandibular length in this subgroup may result from the combined effects of intrinsic skeletal remodeling and the clinical severity distribution across age groups [28–30].

This study employed a retrospective design, involving a sample of patients who either had undergone OGS or had not, stratified by sex and age. However, surgical procedures, skeletal classifications, and functional assessments were not included in the analyses. Furthermore, this study did not account for the potential influence of functional laterality, such as masticatory muscle tension or dominant chewing sides, as these records were unavailable in our retrospective data. Consequently, a stratified analysis regarding the impact of physical sides on mandibular morphology could not be performed. Additionally, although CBCT provides high-resolution imaging of bony structures [3, 5], it does not capture soft-tissue characteristics or postoperative stability, a notable limitation of the present study. Future research incorporating long-term preoperative and post-operative follow-up, clinical assessments of functional habits, and computer-assisted asymmetry analysis is required to clarify the clinical utility of the Bonwill triangle in OGS planning and outcome evaluations. Such efforts can enhance its value as a supplementary tool for preoperative risk assessment and post-operative monitoring.

Conclusion

This study systematically analyzed Bonwill triangle geometry in a Taiwanese population by using CBCT imaging. Given the cross-sectional design of the study, the greater asymmetry observed in the OGS group reflects the inherent characteristics of patients indicated for orthognathic surgery and should not be interpreted as a postoperative outcome. The results revealed substantial mandibular asymmetry in the patients who had undergone OGS, particularly the men, whereas those who had not undergone OGS exhibited symmetric craniofacial structures. These findings support the use of Bonwill triangle parameters as an auxiliary index in mandibular asymmetry assessments in preoperative evaluations. Future studies should explore the applicability of Bonwill triangle geometry across diverse surgical modalities and clinical settings.
