Device independent quantum key distribution with robust self-tests
Andreas Bluhm 1, Gereon Koßmann 2, René Schwonnek 3

Abstract
. Device-independent quantum key distribution (DIQKD) provides a model of quantum key distribution with minimal assumptions and highly abstract theoretical building blocks. Although DIQKD frees us from detailed discussions of specific device models and associated error parameters, it replaces them with fundamental assumptions about the validity of quantum experiments. In this work, we propose a way to lift a protocol based on DIQKD-style assumptions to a device-dependent QKD protocol by performing local self-tests in the laboratories of the two key-generating parties. In particular, we consider routed Bell-test setups as a means of self-testing the local parties in earnest and develop a rigorous mathematical framework showing that the underlying optimization problems can indeed be transferred to the device-dependent QKD setting. As an application, we illustrate many of the relevant techniques through the case study of a routed BB84 protocol.



1.
Introduction

Quantum key distribution offers an opportunity to extract a provably secure key between distant parties, which is certified by quantum theory
[
26
]
. A standard model of QKD considers two parties, Alice and Bob, both equipped with quantum devices characterized by quantum measurements and sharing bipartite quantum states
[
9
]
. Usual QKD protocols now expect the two parties to measure the shared states often enough to obtain meaningful statistics, which can subsequently be used to extract a secure key by the rules of the composable security framework (cf. e.g.
[
32
]
). In particular, since its very first developments
[
3
,
4
,
9
]
, it has been shown that actual security requires the consideration of, in principle, all imperfections of the underlying devices and mismatches between theory and experiment. In order to handle the huge number of potential security lacks, so-called device-independent quantum key distribution (DIQKD) emerged as the gold standard for QKD experiments, as it does not require any assumption on the underlying devices beyond that they are subject to the laws of quantum mechanics
[
21
]
. Moreover, in concrete bipartite scenarios, security proofs for DIQKD only require statistics made by the two partners, Alice and Bob, in order to estimate the amount of key that can be extracted
[
24
,
34
]
. Given these arguably weak assumptions on the physical modeling, it has been discovered that, instead of a concrete mathematical modeling of the devices, a different aspect of the experiment needs to be well understood. At the forefront of this aspect are the loopholes of Bell experiments, as they turn out to be the most important security risks in a DIQKD experiment. Generally, a loophole can be defined as any lack of coherence between experiment and physical modeling that is able to fake the observed statistics. The most prominent instance is the detection loophole, as its effect on the observed statistics equals the influence of long-distance experiments, which, by the natural Lambert-Beer decay effect, have vanishing event sizes.

In a recent sequence of works
[
18
,
8
,
19
,
17
]
, models of DIQKD have been developed in order to address the detection loophole, which have been named
routed Bell tests
. Routed Bell tests in
[
18
,
8
,
19
,
17
]
are tripartite experiments between Alice, Bob, and a third party named Fred in this work. It is assumed that Alice and Fred are close to each other such that one may assume that Alice and Fred are able to perform a close-to-perfect CHSH test, while Alice and Bob could be far apart and perform a key-generation protocol. The main purpose of routed Bell tests has been to show that the zero-key threshold, i.e., the amount of randomness needed in order to extract key, significantly improves depending on the detection efficiency. In comparison,
[
15
]
introduced a fourth party, called George, and investigated the key rate depending on the quantum bit-error rate and local Bell violation, where both Alice and Bob perform local tests with Fred and George, respectively. The key insight compared to its predecessors is a marginal constraint between the applied states for key generation between Alice and Bob and the Bell tests between Alice and Fred, respectively between Bob and George, as we will discuss in the next section.

Even though it has been observed in all proposals
[
18
,
8
,
19
,
17
,
15

that Fred and George are, at least in principle, local self-tests of Alice’s and Bob’s devices, a rigorous mathematical formulation is still missing. Self-testing is a technique in the realm of device-independent quantum information processing that allows one to infer directly from observed statistics a concrete mathematical model of the implemented states and measurements, up to isometric degrees of freedom

However, in proper device-independent models, the performance of perfect self-tests has only very limited applications, as one can arguably assume that a perfect test cannot be recovered in practice, at the very least because of finite-size effects in the observed statistics. For this purpose, a notion of robust self-testing has recently been introduced, eventually capturing the case of imperfect statistics
[
29
,
37

. We furthermore remark that the model

was written in the language of

with

In particular, for a fully device-independent modeling, it is important to make the assumptions of the setup explicit in order to connect to concrete experiments. 2.1.
Security assumptions and switches

DIQKD can arguably be understood as a framework in which the devices and any explicit classical description of the implemented measurements are abstracted away, and all assumptions are reduced to the fundamental ones: the correctness of quantum theory, access to local private randomness, and the confidentiality of any information within the agents’ local laboratories. If a more intricate, possibly multipartite, model of a cryptographic protocol aims to call itself
device-independent
, security under these assumptions provides the standard said protocol must rise to meet.

In the concrete setting of routed Bell tests, we are therefore asked to clearly articulate the assumptions under consideration. From the setup it is clear that all proposed experiments may, without doubt, assume the correctness of quantum theory and the availability of local private randomness for Alice and Bob. Moreover, the assumption of confidentiality of any information within the laboratories of the key-generating parties, Alice and Bob, can be adopted, since it does not differ from the one in any bipartite DIQKD protocol studied extensively over the last decade (cf., e.g.,
[

2.3.
Short-range quantum correlations in the multipartite setting

After debating models in earlier works, in
[
19
]
a proper definition for the set of short quantum correlations was given. Short range quantum correlations in the sense of
[
19
]
are the correlations in the model depicted in

. Based on the model considered in this work we are in a position to generalize this notion to situations in which Alice and Bob are assisted by arbitrarily many local partners, whereas the original definition in
[
19
]
did not allow for such a generalization. We will see that the previous definition will arise as a subcase.

For the following consider a setting with parties
A_{0}\dots A_{n}
on Alice’s side and parties
B_{0}\dots B_{n}
on Bobs side. Routing is controlled by switches with input random variables
T_{A}
and
T_{B}
with support
\{0,1,\dots,n\}
. Employing the perspective of a virtual global source, this corresponds to the creation of a state
\omega^{s}
for every input
(T_{A},T_{B})=s
.
The measurement of each party on Alice’s side has an input
x_{k}
and an output
a_{k}
for

. Inputs and outputs on Bob’s side are labeled by
b_{k}
and

, respectively.
The measurements themselves are described by POVM elements
M^{k}_{a_{k}|x_{k}}
and
N^{k}_{b_{k}|y_{k}}
. In a device-independent setting these can be assumed to be projective
[
27
]
. We will model the independence of the local measurements by assuming the typical commutator relations of a form

(12)

\displaystyle[M^{k}_{a_{k}|x_{k}},M^{j}_{a_{j}|x_{j}}]=[N^{k}_{b_{k}|y_{k}},N^{j}_{b_{j}|y_{j}}]=[M^{k}_{a_{k}|x_{k}},N^{j}_{b_{j}|y_{j}}]=0.

Formally, we can now treat this as a description of an universal
C^{*}
-algebra generated by partially commuting projections
[
6
]
. Intuitively, this basically corresponds to a proper abstract description of what it means to consider all measurements of all states in all feasible Hilbert spaces. In this language a state can be seen as a linear functional acting on a measurement which motivates the notation
\omega^{s}(M)
to describe the expectation of an observable
M
in a state
\omega^{s}
. The notation of
\tr(\rho_\omega M_\mathcal{H})
which is more common in the DIQKD community corresponds to a representation of this algebra on a concrete Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
. For the purposes of this work, these formulations can be assumed to be equivalent. The main advantage of the linear functional notation as used here is that measurements and observables can be kept as abstract symbols and that a mathematically delicate iteration over all possible realizations in all feasible Hilbert spaces can be avoided.
The measurement data in an experiment described in this model leads to conditional probability distributions, also known as behaviors,

(13)

\displaystyle p^{s}(\vec{a},\vec{b}|\vec{x},\vec{y})=\omega^{s}\left(M^{0}_{a_{0}|x_{0}}\dots N^{n}_{b_{n}|y_{n}}\right),

where we grouped indices as
\vec{a}=(a_{0}\dots a_{n})
and so on.

Let
\omega^{s}_{K}
denote the restriction of a state to a subsystem
K
, i.e. the marginal on
K
.
We express the independence of a local system of the switch settings by marginal constraints

(14)

\displaystyle\omega^{s}_{K}=\omega^{s^{\prime}}_{K}\quad\forall\;s,s^{\prime}\in\{0,\dots,n\}

Intuitively, this expresses that the setting of a switch can not have any influence recognizable by any experiment that only acts on the
K

We say that a state
\omega^{s}
is
A
–
B
separable if it can be decomposed as
\omega=\sum p_{i}w_{i,A_{0}\dots A_{n}}w_{i,B_{0}\dots B_{n}}
, i.e. as convex hull of product states.
We will use the convention that the switch setting
s=(0,0)
will be used for key generation. The key will be based on the measurement data obtained by the parties
A_{0}B_{0}
.
Based on this convention we can define correlations with a local model in the key generation round via

(15)

\displaystyle LQC_{A_{0}B_{0}}=\{p^{s}(\vec{a},\vec{b}|\vec{x},\vec{y})\ |\ \omega^{(0,0)}\text{is $A$--$B$ separable},\omega^{s}_{A_{0}}=\omega^{s^{\prime}}_{A_{0}},\omega^{s}_{B_{0}}=\omega^{s^{\prime}}_{B_{0}}\forall s,s^{\prime}\}

Note that, in a situation without further test parties
A_{1}\dots
and
B_{1},\dots
this definition recovers the well known set of correlations arising from local hidden variable models. Furthermore, in the case of Bob being characterized by a concrete Hilbert space, we get the set of behaviors measurable on an unsteerable state. In this sense this definition can be seen a natural extension of these concepts to a situation with partial information obtained by self-testing.

We can adapt this definition to the 2+1 party setting of Lobo et al.
[
19
]
(see
Figure 1
(a)). It is easy to see that our definition recovers the previous definition of short range quantum correlations from
[
19
]
. A formal proof is given in the appendix
App. B
.

We will briefly sketch the main mechanisms of this proof. Followig the nomenclature of
[
19
]
, we have two states
\omega^{long}
and
\omega^{short}
, where our party
A_{1}
is equivalent to the party
B_{S}
.
B_{L}
corresponds to
B_{0}
in our setting.
Here, our definition of A-B local correlations
Eq. 15
reduces to the set of correlations generated by a state
\omega^{long}
that is separable with respect to
B_{L}
and consistent with the statistics observed by
A-B_{S}
on a state
\omega^{short}
.
In contrast, and after applying the equivalence shown in the previous section, Lobo et al. (see
[
19
]
Eq. 15) define the set of short range correlations as all correlations that arise from a joint measurement by
B_{L}
on
\omega^{long}
. The equivalence of the two definitions reduces to the claim that in device-independent models joint measurability and separability are interchangeable concepts at the level of correlations.
In one direction it has to be shown that any behavior measured on a separable state can be obtained from a joint measurement. As every separable state admits a local hidden variable model, this joint measurement can be directly constructed from the classical hidden variable model (see for example
[
33
]
for constructions).

In the other direction, we have to convince ourselves that any behavior measured by a joint measurement can be obtained from a separable state. W.l.o.g. any fixed joint measurement can be represented by a projective parent measurement on a larger space by Naimark’s dilation Theorem. These projections generate an abelian subalgebra of
B_{long}
, which is large enough to describe the specific behavior. As abelian algebras cannot be entangled with any other system the corresponding state must be separable
[
33
]
.

3.
Robust lifts of DIQKD to device-dependent QKD

It has been recognized in earlier work regarding routed Bell setups that routed Bell tests are, in principle, able to lift device-independent models to device-dependent models in the setting of a perfect local test as part of a routed BB84 protocol
[
18
,
8
,
19
,
17

is achieved as shown in

]
. However, hitherto a concrete and general mathematical modeling taking into account an error due to imperfect local tests was still missing, as it would require a smooth interpolation between device-independent and device-dependent setups. These, however, are fundamentally different from the perspective of assumptions. This section is dedicated to a rigorous mathematical analysis of this observation using the recently introduced notion of
robust self-tests

[
37
]
. In order to introduce the language of robust self-tests, we will start by treating bipartite quantum experiments between Alice and Bob where both parties query their boxes with local private randomness. Each of them has a finite input set, called
X
for Alice and
Y^{\prime}
for Bob, and a finite output set denoted by
A
for Alice and
B
for Bob. We assume a setup in which Alice and Bob are spacelike separated. Hence, Alice and Bob will have in the asymptotic limit a conditional probability distribution

(16)

\displaystyle p(a,b|x,y),\qquad a\in A,\ b\in B,\ x\in X,\ y\in Y^{\prime},

which satisfies the usual non-signaling conditions imposed by the spacelike separation. A
self-test
of a conditional probability distribution
p(a,b|x,y)
is, in a sense, a unique quantum model defined by a tuple

(17)

\displaystyle\tilde{S}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\left(\mathcal{H}_{\tilde{A}},\mathcal{H}_{\tilde{B}},\left\{\tilde{M}_{a|x}\right\},\left\{\tilde{N}_{b|y}\right\},\ket{\varphi_{\tilde{A}\tilde{B}}}\right),

whereby we have fixed (finite-dimensional) Hilbert spaces, fixed measurement operators on these Hilbert spaces and, without loss of generality, a shared pure state. An application of Born’s rule yields

(18)

\displaystyle p(a,b|x,y)=\tr\left[\tilde{M}_{a|x}\otimes\tilde{N}_{b|y}\outerproduct{\varphi_{\tilde{A}\tilde{B}}}{\varphi_{\tilde{A}\tilde{B}}}\right]

and each other model of the form in
Eq. 17
with the same statistics is connected to
Eq. 17
via isometries and auxiliary states
[
23
]
. One then defines the unique model via the smallest model ordered by isometries mapping between them (in the finite-dimensional case, this is just the model with the smallest dimensions of
\mathcal{H}_{\tilde{A}}
and
\mathcal{H}_{\tilde{B}}
). Setups which allow for self-testing are exceptional, because usually completely different models can lead to the same statistics as in
Eq. 16
.

In DIQKD we are interested in the randomness we can extract purely from the statistics produced by a quantum experiment, without making any further assumptions on the devices. The experiment is described by a general but unknown quantum model

(19)

\displaystyle S\coloneqq\left(\rho_{AB},\{M_{a|x}\},\{N_{b|y}\},\mathcal{H}_{A},\mathcal{H}_{B}\right).

corresponding to the observed statistics via Born’s rule (see
Eq. 18
). Importantly,
\rho_{AB}
does not need to be pure.
Generally, given any statistic
p
, in DIQKD we are interested in an optimization over all compatible models

(20)

\displaystyle\inf

\displaystyle H(A|X=\tilde{x},E)

\displaystyle\operatorname{s.t.}

\displaystyle S\quad\text{quantum model}

\displaystyle S\quad\text{yields the statistics}\ p.

In general, the optimization problem in
Eq. 20
is very difficult and few general techniques exist, which rely on techniques from non-commutative optimization
[
7
,
16
]
. These techniques scale badly with the problem size. However, in the situation of an exact self-test, the conditional von Neumann entropy
H(A|X=\tilde{x},E)
for a specific measurement setting can be calculated from the unique model in
Eq. 17
as sketched in the following proposition. Thus, the optimization in
Eq. 20
simplifies drastically.

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
\left(\mathcal{H}_{A},\mathcal{H}_{B},\{M_{a|x}\},\{N_{b|y}\},\rho_{AB}\right)
be a realization of the statistics
p(a,b|x,y)
and assume that there exists an exact self-test for
p(a,b|x,y)
given by
\left(\mathcal{H}_{\tilde{A}},\mathcal{H}_{\tilde{B}},\{\tilde{M}_{a|x}\},\{\tilde{N}_{b|y}\},\ket{\psi_{\tilde{A}\tilde{B}}}\right)
. Then we have

(21)

\displaystyle H(A|X=\tilde{x},E)_{\rho}=H(A|X=\tilde{x})_{\ket{\psi_{\tilde{A}\tilde{B}}}}=H((q_{a})_{a\in A}),

where

(22)

\displaystyle q_{a}\coloneqq\tr\!\left[\outerproduct{\psi_{\tilde{A}\tilde{B}}}{\psi_{\tilde{A}\tilde{B}}}\,\tilde{M}_{a|\tilde{x}}\otimes\mathds{1}_{\tilde{B}}\right].

Proof.

By the exact self-test property, there exist local isometries

(23)

\displaystyle I_{A}:\mathcal{H}_{A}\to\mathcal{H}_{\tilde{A}}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{\operatorname{aux}}^{A}\quad\text{and}\quad I_{B}:\mathcal{H}_{B}\to\mathcal{H}_{\tilde{B}}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{\operatorname{aux}}^{B},

such that for a purification
\ket{\psi_{ABE}}
of
\rho_{AB}
we have

(24)

\displaystyle(I_{A}\otimes I_{B}\otimes\mathds{1}_{E})\ket{\psi_{ABE}}=\ket{\Phi_{\operatorname{aux}_A \operatorname{aux}_B E}}\otimes\ket{\psi_{\tilde{A}\tilde{B}}}.

Hence, after measuring
A
with setting
\tilde{x}
, the resulting classical-quantum state factorizes as

(25)

\displaystyle\rho_{AE}^{\tilde{x}}\;\cong\;\outerproduct{\Phi_{\operatorname{aux}_A \operatorname{aux}_B E}}{\Phi_{\operatorname{aux}_A \operatorname{aux}_B E}}_{\operatorname{aux}_{A}\operatorname{aux}_{B}E}\otimes\sum_{a}q_{a}\outerproduct{a}{a}_{A},

up to the above local isometries. Since conditional von Neumann entropy is invariant under local isometries on the conditioning system and unchanged by tensoring a product state on that system, it follows that

(26)

\displaystyle H(A|X=\tilde{x},E)_{\rho}=H(A|X=\tilde{x})_{\ket{\psi_{\tilde{A}\tilde{B}}}}.

Finally, on the ideal state the register
A
is classical with distribution
(q_{a})_{a}
, so

(27)

\displaystyle H(A|X=\tilde{x})_{\ket{\psi_{\tilde{A}\tilde{B}}}}=H((q_{a})_{a\in A}).

∎

The key difference between
3.1
and a local self-test in a routed setup is that the Alice-Bob correlations are used not only for key generation, but also for self-testing the devices. This leads to two natural questions.

First, what is the effect of noise in the observed statistics, and how does the conditional von Neumann entropy behave under such perturbations? In general, inferring the conditional von Neumann entropy (defined via a purification) directly from observed statistics is difficult, which is also reflected by the optimization over all compatible models being a hard problem. For self-tests, the notion of robustness introduced in
[
37
]
provides the appropriate framework, and one goal of this work is to apply it to the conditional von Neumann entropy. Generally, it is unclear how the conditional von Neumann entropy behaves for certain statistics if one varies the statistics slightly. This phenomenon has been observed in
[
10
]
and
[
36
,
11
]
, where for arbitrarily small non-locality, quantified in terms of Bell-violation, both statistics allowing for secret key extraction and not allowing for secret key extraction have been found. Here, it is obvious that knowing the statistics (or slightly more general Bell-violations) is not sufficient to infer something about the conditional von Neumann entropy.

Second, self-tests can also certify the devices themselves, i.e. the measurement operators. In routed Bell-test scenarios, noise between distant parties is most likely governed by the physical Lambert-Beer effect. This suggests that one may self-test the devices locally and interpret the Lambert–Beer effect as effectively reducing the bit-error rate in key-generating rounds.

To connect with the results in
[
31
,
18
,
8
,
19
,
17
]
, we proceed in two steps. First, we adapt the framework of robust self-tests to the BB84 setting, recovering the well-known conclusions of
[
31
,
18
]
. Second, we extend the analysis to the setting of general routed Bell tests with self-test.

3.1.
Routed BB84 protocol

The simplest model to investigate many of the interesting properties of a routed Bell setup is the routed BB84 protocol. The routed BB84 protocol is defined by the actions of three parties: Alice, Bob, and Fred. Each of them has two projections, and Alice and Bob measure noisy BB84 statistics, while Alice and Fred measure CHSH statistics. It is well known that the entropic uncertainty relation under side information
[
5
]
is tight for the BB84 protocol, which is also used in the semi-DI framework
[
35
]
. Semi-DI means that one party, usually Alice, is fully trusted and a concrete Hilbert space representation of her device is known. The step from semi-DI to routed BB84 is simply to allow noise in the local CHSH test, because, in fact, an optimal local CHSH test self-tests Alice’s device perfectly, such that we would be back in the semi-DI case
[
22
,
23
]
. This observation was made in
[
17
]
and verified numerically in
[
15
]
. Without yet stating what robustness in self-testing means, this already shows, in some sense, a robustness effect of the key rate depending on the quality of the local CHSH test. The key for an analysis of the more refined routed BB84 protocol with dependency on the local test is the adaptive uncertainty relation from
[
31
]
, which yields tight bounds in the case of an optimal CHSH test and can be reduced to the observation that, in that case, the whole analysis depends only on the overlap between the two projections in Alice’s device. Thus, from a theoretical perspective, the analysis of this setup can be reduced to the effective overlap of Alice’s two projections. This drastically simplifies the analysis and even yields quantitative bounds on the key rate. Nevertheless, we emphasize and show subsequently in
Theorem 3.5
that the theory of robust self-tests can already be applied, as the CHSH test is a robust self-test
[
37
, Thm. 9.5]
. The following definition is from
[
31
]
.

Definition 3.2

(Effective overlap)
.

Let
\rho_{A}\in\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{H}_{A})
and let

Z=\{M_{a\mid Z}\}_{a}
and
X=\{M_{b\mid X}\}_{b}
be POVMs on
A
. The
effective overlap
of
(\rho_{A},X,Z)
is defined as

(28)

\displaystyle c^{\star}(\rho_{A},X,Z)\coloneqq\inf_{\left(U,X^{\prime},Z^{\prime},K^{\prime}\right)}\sum_{k}\tr\left[P_{A^{\prime}}^{k}\,U\rho_{A}U^{\dagger}\right]\;\max_{b}\left\lVert\sum_{a}\left(P_{A^{\prime}}^{k}\Pi_{a\mid Z}P_{A^{\prime}}^{k}\right)\left(P_{A^{\prime}}^{k}\Pi_{b\mid X}P_{A^{\prime}}^{k}\right)\left(P_{A^{\prime}}^{k}\Pi_{a\mid Z}P_{A^{\prime}}^{k}\right)\right\rVert.

Here, the infimum ranges over all
isometries
U:\mathcal{H}_{A}\to\mathcal{H}_{A^{\prime}}
,
all POVMs
Z^{\prime}=\{\Pi_{a\mid Z}\}_{a}
and
X^{\prime}=\{\Pi_{b\mid X}\}_{b}
on
A^{\prime}
,
and all projective measurements
K^{\prime}=\{P_{A^{\prime}}^{k}\}_{k}
on
A^{\prime}
,
such that for all outcomes
a,b
,

(29)

\displaystyle\sum_{k}U^{\dagger}P_{A^{\prime}}^{k}\Pi_{a\mid Z}P_{A^{\prime}}^{k}U=M_{a\mid Z}\quad\text{and}\quad\sum_{k}U^{\dagger}P_{A^{\prime}}^{k}\Pi_{b\mid X}P_{A^{\prime}}^{k}U=M_{b\mid X}.

We note that the effective overlap is continuous in its input state.

Lemma 3.3

(Continuity of the effective overlap in the marginal state)
.

Fix Alice’s two measurements
X,Z
.
Then for any two density operators
\tau_{A},\tau^{\prime}_{A}
on
\mathcal{H}_{A}
,

(30)

\displaystyle\lvert c^{\star}(\tau_{A},X,Z)-c^{\star}(\tau^{\prime}_{A},X,Z)\rvert\ \leq\ \frac{1}{2}\lVert\tau_{A}-\tau^{\prime}_{A}\rVert_{1}.

Proof.

Denote by
\gamma\coloneqq\left(U,X^{\prime},Z^{\prime},K^{\prime}\right)
an admissible tuple. Then we define

(31)

\displaystyle f_{\gamma}\left(\tau_{A}\right)\coloneqq\sum_{k}\tr\left[P_{A^{\prime}}^{k,\gamma}\,U_{\gamma}\tau_{A}U^{\dagger}_{\gamma}\right]c_{k,\gamma}

with

(32)

\displaystyle c_{k,\gamma}\coloneqq\max_{b}\left\lVert\sum_{a}\left(P_{A^{\prime}}^{k,\gamma}\Pi_{a\mid Z}^{\gamma}P_{A^{\prime}}^{k,\gamma}\right)\left(P_{A^{\prime}}^{k,\gamma}\Pi_{b\mid X}^{\gamma}P_{A^{\prime}}^{k,\gamma}\right)\left(P_{A^{\prime}}^{k,\gamma}\Pi_{a\mid Z}^{\gamma}P_{A^{\prime}}^{k,\gamma}\right)\right\rVert.

Now we can write

(33)

\displaystyle\inf_{\gamma}f_{\gamma}\left(\tau_{A}\right)-\inf_{\gamma}f_{\gamma}\left(\tau_{A}^{\prime}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{\gamma}f_{\gamma}\left(\tau_{A}\right)-f_{\gamma}\left(\tau_{A}^{\prime}\right)

\displaystyle=\sup_{\gamma}\tr\left[\sum_{k}c_{k,\gamma}U_{\gamma}^{\dagger}P_{A^{\prime}}^{k,\gamma}U_{\gamma}\left(\tau_{A}-\tau_{A}^{\prime}\right)\right].

But we also have
0\leq\sum_{k}c_{k,\gamma}U_{\gamma}^{\dagger}P_{A^{\prime}}^{k,\gamma}U_{\gamma}\leq\mathds{1}_{A}
, implying the result with the variational characterization of the trace-distance.
∎

Adapting the setup from
[

18
]
to
[

]
and thus

\displaystyle c^{\star}(\sigma_{A},X,Z)\leq\frac{1}{2}+\frac{\omega\left(\operatorname{CHSH}\right)}{8}\sqrt{8-\omega\left(\operatorname{CHSH}\right)^{2}},

Then we have

\displaystyle c^{\star}(\rho_{A},X,Z)\leq\frac{1}{2}+\frac{\omega\left(\operatorname{CHSH}\right)}{8}\sqrt{8-\omega\left(\operatorname{CHSH}\right)^{2}}+\frac{\varepsilon}{2}.

Now we apply the uncertainty relation under side-information from

and
X_{A}=\{M_{a|Z}\}_{a}

\displaystyle H(Z_{A}|E)_{\rho}+H(X_{A}|B)_{\rho}\geq-\log c^{\star}(\rho_{A},X_{A},Z_{A}).

\displaystyle r_{\mathrm{key}}\ \geq\ 1\,-\,\log_{2}\left(1+\frac{\omega\left(\operatorname{CHSH}\right)}{4}\sqrt{8-\omega\left(\operatorname{CHSH}\right)^{2}}+\varepsilon\right)\;-\;h_{2}(Q_{Z})\;-\;h_{2}(Q_{X}).

This example shows many of the interesting properties of a routed Bell experiment. First of all, it shows that the value

can be incorporated into the key rate and thus demonstrates the smooth dependence between the CHSH value and the BB84 key rate.
However, comparing this result with the numerical key-rate results for the case of one-sided self-tests and full statistics from

In the comparison of

, and thus shows that key can still be extracted even when the marginal constraint is only approximate.

For the following investigations, it is worth mentioning that

does not include any information about a potential second self-testing partner on Bob’s side. Given that the extractable randomness is drawn from Alice’s device, it is also unclear how to include this directly in

3.2.
General robust self-testing and conditional von Neumann entropies

The ideas of
Sec. 3.1
show that the routed Bell-test framework is, at least in principle, able to derive smooth interpolation results between fully DI scenarios and device-dependent QKD. However, techniques based on uncertainty relations usually depend only on a one-sided analysis, as they concern the extractable randomness from Alice’s device. Moreover, uncertainty relations for more than two measurements are inherently hard, and they remain open even in the fully device-dependent case beyond qubits. In this subsection, we introduce the framework of robust self-testing from
[
37
]
and connect the results to the general theoretical building blocks. Our first goal is to reprove the results from
Sec. 3.1
and then to generalize them and to incorporate self-tests with both parties Alice and Bob. A robust self-test is defined as follows.

Definition 3.4

(Robust self-test)
.

Let
\varepsilon>0
and let two quantum models be given as

(38)

\displaystyle S

\displaystyle=\left(\mathcal{H}_{A},\mathcal{H}_{B},\{M_{a|x}\},\{N_{b|y}\},\ket{\psi}\right)\}

\displaystyle\tilde{S}

\displaystyle=\left(\mathcal{H}_{\tilde{A}},\tilde{\mathcal{H}}_{B},\{\tilde{M}_{a|x}\},\{\tilde{N}_{b|y}\},\ket{\tilde{\psi}}\right)\}.

We say
\tilde{S}
is a local
\varepsilon
-dilation of
S
, denoted as
S\succeq_{\varepsilon}\tilde{S}
, if there are isometries
I_{A}:\mathcal{H}_{A}\to\mathcal{H}_{\tilde{A}}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{A}^{\operatorname{aux}}
and
I_{B}:\mathcal{H}_{B}\to\tilde{\mathcal{H}}_{B}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{B}^{\operatorname{aux}}
and a vector state
\ket{\operatorname{aux}}\in\mathcal{H}_{A}^{\operatorname{aux}}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{B}^{\operatorname{aux}}
, such that

(39)

\displaystyle\|I_{A}\otimes I_{B}\left(M_{a|x}\otimes\mathds{1}_{B}\ket{\psi}\right)-\left(\tilde{M}_{a|x}\otimes\mathds{1}_{B}\ket{\tilde\psi}\right)\otimes\ket{\operatorname{aux}}\|_{2}\leq\varepsilon

\displaystyle\|I_{A}\otimes I_{B}\left(\mathds{1}_{A}\otimes N_{b|y}\ket{\psi}\right)-\left(\mathds{1}_{A}\otimes\tilde{N}_{b|y}\ket{\tilde\psi}\right)\otimes\ket{\operatorname{aux}}\|_{2}\leq\varepsilon

\displaystyle\|I_{A}\otimes I_{B}\ket{\psi}-\ket{\tilde\psi}\otimes\ket{\operatorname{aux}}\|_{2}\leq\varepsilon

The following theorem shows the result from
Eq. 37
as a qualitative result in the language of robust self-tests.

3.5
.

In the routed BB84 protocol with
\sigma_{AA_{F}}
a self-test for Alice’s device performing a CHSH game with winning probability
\omega(\mathrm{CHSH})-\varepsilon
and Alice and Bob using
\rho_{AB}
to run a BB84 protocol, the one-way communication, device-independent key-rate satisfies

(40)

\displaystyle r_{\mathrm{key}}\geq 1-h(Q_{Z})-h(Q_{X})-\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{\varepsilon})\,.

In particular, as
\varepsilon\to 0
, they attain the optimal BB84 key-rate
[
28
]
.

Proof.

We assume that the winning probability
\omega(\operatorname{CHSH})-\varepsilon
is achieved by Alice and Fred given by the (w.l.o.g.) PVM model

(41)

\displaystyle S\coloneqq(\mathcal{H}_{A},\mathcal{H}_{F},\{M_{a|x}\},\{N_{b|y}\},\ket{\psi})

and we call the reduced states of that model
\sigma_{A}
and
\sigma_{F}
. Moreover, we call the optimal CHSH model on a qubit

(42)

\displaystyle\tilde{S}\coloneqq(\mathcal{H}_{\tilde{A}},\tilde{\mathcal{H}}_{F},\{\tilde{M}_{a|x}\},\{\tilde{N}_{b|y}\},\ket{\tilde{\psi}})

with
\tilde{M}_{a|X}
the optimal Pauli-

[

is centrally supported and

(43)

\displaystyle S\succeq_{\delta}\tilde{S}

via local isometries

and an auxiliary state

.

Let

(44)

\displaystyle\ket{\Psi'}\coloneqq(I_{A}\otimes I_{F})\ket{\psi},\qquad\ket{\phi}\coloneqq\ket{\tilde\psi}\otimes\ket{\operatorname{aux}},\qquad\zeta\coloneqq\|\ket{\Psi'}-\ket{\phi}\|_{2}=O(\delta).

Define Alice’s reflections in
S
by

(45)

\displaystyle X\coloneqq M_{0|X}-M_{1|X},\qquad Z\coloneqq M_{0|Z}-M_{1|Z}.

Fix the isometry
I_{A}
from the self-test and consider the associated dilated projective measurements
\{\Pi_{a\mid x}\}
, so that we can set the (dilated) reflections

(46)

\displaystyle X^{\prime}\coloneqq\Pi_{0|X}-\Pi_{1|X},\qquad Z^{\prime}\coloneqq\Pi_{0|Z}-\Pi_{1|Z},\qquad\sigma_{A}^{\prime}\coloneqq I_{A}\sigma_{A}I_{A}^{\dagger}.

Let

From

(47)

\displaystyle\eta_{X}\coloneqq\big\|(X^{\prime}-\tilde{X}\otimes\mathds{1})\ket{\phi}\big\|_{2}=O(\delta),\qquad\eta_{Z}\coloneqq\big\|(Z^{\prime}-\tilde{Z}\otimes\mathds{1})\ket{\phi}\big\|_{2}=O(\delta).

We also need control of the

Since

(49)

\displaystyle\{X^{\prime},Z^{\prime}\}\ket{\phi}=\bigl(X^{\prime}Z^{\prime}-\tilde{X}\tilde{Z}\otimes\mathds{1}\bigr)\ket{\phi}+\bigl(Z^{\prime}X^{\prime}-\tilde{Z}\tilde{X}\otimes\mathds{1}\bigr)\ket{\phi},

and therefore, by the triangle inequality and

\displaystyle\big\|\{X^{\prime},Z^{\prime}\}\ket{\phi}\big\|_{2}\leq\kappa_{XZ}+\kappa_{ZX}=O(\delta).

\displaystyle\big\|\{X^{\prime},Z^{\prime}\}\ket{\Psi'}\big\|_{2}\leq\big\|\{X^{\prime},Z^{\prime}\}\ket{\phi}\big\|_{2}+\|\{X^{\prime},Z^{\prime}\}\|\,\zeta\leq O(\delta)+2\,O(\delta)=O(\delta).

\displaystyle\tr[\sigma_{A}^{\prime}\{X^{\prime},Z^{\prime}\}^{2}]=\langle\Psi^{\prime}|\{X^{\prime},Z^{\prime}\}^{2}|\Psi^{\prime}\rangle=\|\{X^{\prime},Z^{\prime}\}\ket{\Psi'}\|_{2}^{2},

[

(56)

\displaystyle H(X_{A}|B)\leq H(X_{A}|X_{B})\leq h(Q_{X}),\qquad H(Z_{A}|Z_{B})\leq h(Q_{Z}).

\displaystyle r_{\operatorname{key}}\geq H(Z_{A}|E)-H(Z_{A}|Z_{B}).

The following lemma shows that, given a local
\varepsilon_{A}
-robust self-test in Alice’s lab, the conditional entropy can be transferred from the fully device-independent setting to a device-dependent one, up to an error controlled by the continuity properties of the conditional entropy. In general, this is a very strong statement, and one should not expect it to be efficient outside a small neighborhood of the self-test. In essence, it shows that the conditional entropy remains well behaved even when viewed solely as a function of the underlying statistics. By contrast, within the convex set of quantum-realizable statistics, for example those arising in a quantum game, many different strategies can produce the same observed statistics. In such situations, it is therefore unlikely that the conditional entropy can, in general, be reduced to an effectively device-dependent problem.

3.4.
Error bounds for robust self-tests

In
3.6
, we started with
\varepsilon_{A}
- and
\varepsilon_{B}
-dilations. One might therefore rightfully ask how to obtain the parameters
\varepsilon_{A}
,
\varepsilon_{B}
which played a crucial role in our analysis. In this subsection, we will make explicit how they can be obtained if we use the CHSH inequality as our Bell inequality. In
[
37
]
, it was proven that for an
\varepsilon
-optimal strategy (meaning that the value of the game is at most
\varepsilon
-far from the optimal value, or in other words that the observed violation of the CHSH inequality is at most
\varepsilon
far from the maximal quantum violation),
\varepsilon_{A}
and
\varepsilon_{B}
can be chosen as
\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{\varepsilon})
.
We will sketch the arguments in Section 5.2 of
[
38
]
that yield a concrete bound on the constant in front of
\sqrt{\epsilon}
.

For the CHSH game, its associated game polynomial is given by

(132)

\Phi_{\mathrm{CHSH}}:=\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{8}[a_{0}\otimes b_{0}+a_{0}\otimes b_{1}+a_{1}\otimes b_{0}-a_{1}\otimes b_{1}]

Here,
a_{0}
,
a_{1}
are the observables Alice measures and
b_{0}
,
b_{1}
the observables Bob measures. Since the optimal quantum value of the CHSH game is
\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{2\sqrt{2}}\approx 0.85
, we can find a sum-of-squares decomposition of

(133)

\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{2\sqrt{2}}-\Phi_{\mathrm{CHSH}}=\frac{1}{8\sqrt{2}}\left[\left(a_{0}\otimes\mathds{1}-\mathds{1}\otimes\frac{b_{0}+b_{1}}{\sqrt{2}}\right)^{2}+\left(a_{1}\otimes\mathds{1}-\mathds{1}\otimes\frac{b_{0}-b_{1}}{\sqrt{2}}\right)^{2}\right]\,.

That is, for an
\varepsilon
-optimal strategy, with observables
A_{0}
,
A_{1}
,
B_{0}
,
B_{1}
and state
\ket{\psi}
,
we have that

(134)

\displaystyle\left\|A_{0}\otimes\mathds{1}\ket{\psi}-\mathds{1}\otimes\frac{B_{0}+B_{1}}{\sqrt{2}}\ket{\psi}\right\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt[4]{128}\sqrt{\varepsilon}

(135)

\displaystyle\left\|A_{1}\otimes\mathds{1}\ket{\psi}-\mathds{1}\otimes\frac{B_{0}-B_{1}}{\sqrt{2}}\ket{\psi}\right\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt[4]{128}\sqrt{\varepsilon}

Thus, we obtain, applying the triangle inequality and using
A_{0}^{2}=\mathds{1}=A_{1}^{2}
and
\|\frac{B_{0}+B_{1}}{\sqrt{2}}\|\leq\sqrt{2}
,

(136)

\displaystyle\left\|\ket{\psi}-\left(\frac{B_{0}+B_{1}}{\sqrt{2}}\right)^{2}\ket{\psi}\right\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq(1+\sqrt{2})\sqrt[4]{128}\sqrt{\varepsilon}

(137)

\displaystyle\left\|\ket{\psi}-\left(\frac{B_{0}-B_{1}}{\sqrt{2}}\right)^{2}\ket{\psi}\right\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq(1+\sqrt{2})\sqrt[4]{128}\sqrt{\varepsilon}

Noting that we can write
\mathds{1}\otimes(B_{0}B_{1}+B_{1}B_{0})=\left(\frac{B_{0}+B_{1}}{\sqrt{2}}\right)^{2}-\left(\frac{B_{0}-B_{1}}{\sqrt{2}}\right)^{2}

we can estimate

(138)

\displaystyle\|\mathds{1}\otimes(B_{0}B_{1}+B_{1}B_{0})\ket{\psi}\|_{2}\leq 2(1+\sqrt{2})\sqrt[4]{128}\sqrt{\varepsilon}.

Using Gowers-Hatami theorem, this implies that there exists an isometry
I_{B}
such that

(139)

\left\|\mathds{1}\otimes B_{y}\ket{\psi}-\mathds{1}\otimes I_{B}^{\dagger}\left(\tilde{B}_{y}\right)I_{B}\ket{\psi}\right\|_{2}\leq 2(1+\sqrt{2})\sqrt[4]{128}\sqrt{\varepsilon},

where

I_{A}

(140)

(141)

\displaystyle\left\|M_{a|x}\otimes\mathds{1}\ket{\psi}-I_{A}^{\dagger}\left(\tilde{M}_{a|x}\right)I_{A}\ket{\psi}\right\|_{2}

(142)

\displaystyle\left\|\mathds{1}\otimes N_{b|y}\ket{\psi}-\mathds{1}\otimes I_{B}^{\dagger}\left(\tilde{N}_{b|y}\right)I_{B}\ket{\psi}\right\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq(1+\sqrt{2})\sqrt[4]{128}\sqrt{\varepsilon}

Next, choosing representations

\pi_{B}

(143)

\displaystyle\pi_{A}(a_{0})=X,

\displaystyle\pi_{A}(a_{1})=Z

5.
Outlook

In this work, we developed and investigated a framework for the description of device independent QKD in a routed Bell-test setting (cf.
Sec. 2.3
). In the first part of this work we compared the models described in earlier proposals
[
18
,
17
,
30
]
, for three parties with the model in
[
15
]
and showed in particular that the short-range-quantum correlations are equivalent to special cases of this model. Moreover, we gave a detailed description of the assumptions on the switch, including in which sense it is device-independent and that the marginal constraint introduced in
[
15
]
is on one hand unavoidable and on the other robust in the sense that a small deviation in trace-distance does not lead to a general lack of security (cf.
3.3
).

The analysis in the second part of this work makes precise how the intuition of local self-tests can be used to lift a routed device-independent description to an effectively device-dependent one, provided the relevant marginal constraints are satisfied (cf.
3.7
and
3.8
). In the ideal limit, this recovers the familiar device-dependent picture and the corresponding optimal BB84 behavior. For the non-ideal case, we show that this lift is indeed robust. This means that we can recover an effective device-dependent picture with an error term expressing the performance of the local self-tests (cf.
Theorem 3.9
). This also extends in particular to situations in which the marginal constraints are only fulfilled approximately (cf.
Eq. 90
and
Eq. 82
).
Taken together, this makes this ansatz a practical tool for the analysis of experiments. We showed exemplary how this effective device-dependent description can be used in order to lift a state of the art finite size analysis to device independent settings. However, this is a direction we have only begun to explore.

A central next step will be the application of our tools to predict the performance of realistic future experiments.
Here we especially have to compute and optimize the error thresholds on the local self-tests for a successful finite size key generation. An important step for this is to improve techniques for estimating the errors
\epsilon_{A}
and
\epsilon_{B}
(cf.
[
2
,
14
]
). So far, theses estimates are based on the score of a non-local game. We however expect a much better performance when this estimate is done based on the full measurement statistics.

If these remaining ingredients can be brought under quantitative control, QKD based on routed Bell-tests may offer a realistic route towards a next generation of quantum key distribution technology.
