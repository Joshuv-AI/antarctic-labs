Toward a public health and prevention‐oriented approach to stimulant‐induced psychosis
Authors: Anne Bouthillier, Heidar Sharafi, Adam Bisaga, Didier Jutras‐Aswad
Published: 2026

The international convergence regarding the burden and clinical complexity of stimulant‐induced psychosis underscores the need for prevention‐oriented and integrated approaches that extend beyond acute psychiatric care to include public health strategies, coordinated surveillance and management of the broader psychiatric, medical and social complexity associated with stimulant‐induced psychosis.

We thank the authors of the commentaries on our Opinion and Debate [1] for their thoughtful contributions to the discussion, which collectively reinforce the growing recognition of stimulant‐induced psychosis (StIP) as an increasingly important psychiatric and public health challenge [2, 3, 4]. One striking observation emerging from these international commentaries is the remarkable convergence across jurisdictions regarding the burden and clinical complexity of StIP. Despite differences in healthcare systems, diagnostic traditions and local stimulant use patterns, authors identify similar challenges: fragmented care pathways, limited evidence‐based guidance, high morbidity and urgent need for integrated longitudinal approaches. These perspectives also highlight the importance of coordinated surveillance and rapid knowledge‐sharing mechanisms capable of responding to evolving stimulant‐related harms. Regional variations in stimulant markets and the emergence of synthetic cathinones and other novel psychoactive substances may outpace current clinical guidance and health system adaptation [5]. International collaborative efforts, such as #ScaleUp, a joint initiative by the United Nations Office on Drugs and Crime, the World Health Organization and the European Union Drugs Agency, may help accelerate the development and dissemination of scalable psychosocial and pharmacological interventions for stimulant use disorder (StUD) and StIP across diverse healthcare settings [6].

Although our original article primarily focused on diagnostic and management evidence gaps, the present discussion also underscores the importance of moving beyond a narrow clinical perspective toward a broader public health approach. One important implication raised by these commentaries is the need to transition away from reactive models focused on acute psychosis management in individuals with StUD. Currently, clinical interventions only occur after severe psychosis emerges, typically in emergency or psychiatric inpatient settings. By that point, opportunities for early intervention may already have been missed. A broader prevention‐oriented framework may, therefore, be warranted, spanning individuals with psychotic disorders who are beginning to use stimulants, stimulant users who have not yet developed psychotic symptoms and individuals with broader vulnerability factors for both stimulant use and psychosis. Primary prevention efforts should include addressing upstream social and structural determinants associated with stimulant use and psychiatric vulnerability, including homelessness, trauma, marginalization and limited access to care [7]. Secondary prevention should focus on identifying individuals with transient psychotic symptoms or other early markers of psychosis risk. Tertiary prevention should aim to reduce relapse, rehospitalization, overdose, suicide risk and progression toward chronic psychotic disorders through integrated longitudinal care [8, 9]. The challenge, therefore, is not only to treat StIP when it appears, but to build systems capable of preventing it, detecting it early, and supporting long‐term recovery.

Finally, these commentaries underscore that StIP rarely occurs in isolation and should not be understood solely through the dual lens of psychosis and StUD. Comorbid conditions, such as other substance use and mental health disorders, infectious diseases and social instability, substantially influence prognosis, treatment needs and the capacity to engage in care [2, 7, 10, 11, 12]. Recognizing this multi‐dimensional burden will be essential for developing more integrated and patient‐centered models of care for StIP. Ultimately, how healthcare systems respond to StIP may become a marker of their broader capacity to deliver integrated, prevention‐oriented psychiatric, addiction and public healthcare.
