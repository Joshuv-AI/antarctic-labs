Operator-Algebraic Methods for Asymptotic-Preserving Quantum Simulation of Open Systems
M.W. AlMasri

Abstract
We develop a mathematically rigorous framework for simulating multiscale physical systems using quantum computational resources, by translating the language of asymptotic-preserving (AP) schemes into the formalism of quantum channels and Lindbladian dynamics. For stiff open quantum systems governed by singularly perturbed generators ℒ ε = ε − 1 ​ ℒ fast + ℒ slow \mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon}=\varepsilon^{-1}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{fast}}+\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{slow}} with ε → 0 \varepsilon\to 0 , we prove that layered quantum protocols, which implement fast-scale relaxation via native analog evolution or analytic manifold projection, converge uniformly in the diamond norm to consistent discretizations of the limiting slow dynamics, with explicit error bound 𝒪 ⁡ ( ε ​ Δ ​ t + Δ ​ t 2 ) \mathcal{O}(\varepsilon\Delta t+\Delta t^{2}) independent of stiffness. We establish precise resource-complexity bounds showing that superlinear gate-count savings Ω ⁡ ( κ ⋅ ( d tot / d slow ) c ) \Omega(\kappa\cdot(d_{\mathrm{tot}}/d_{\mathrm{slow}})^{c}) arise if and only if fast dynamics are resolved via (i) hardware-native analog evolution, or (ii) analytic adiabatic elimination reducing effective Hilbert space dimension. The framework is illustrated through cavity QED in the bad-cavity limit and a quantum-inspired AP discretization of kinetic equations converging to fluid limits, with quantified error propagation in trace and diamond norms. This work provides a principled mathematical bridge between classical multiscale numerical analysis and quantum simulation algorithms.

The simulation of multiscale physical systems, spanning disparate temporal, spatial, or energetic scales, represents a grand challenge across computational physics, chemistry, and engineering. Classical numerical analysis addresses such problems through asymptotic-preserving (AP) schemes

In this work, we bridge these domains by formulating AP quantum simulation as a rigorous operator-theoretic framework for multiscale physical systems. We consider stiff open quantum systems whose Lindbladian generators admit singular perturbations:

\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon}=\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{fast}}+\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{slow}},\qquad 0<\varepsilon\ll 1,

(1)

where
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{fast}}
generates rapid dissipative relaxation on time scale
\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon)
and
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{slow}}

[

]
require time steps

to resolve the fast scale, leading to gate complexity

even when observables of interest evolve slowly. This stiffness penalty can render simulations infeasible on near-term hardware.

Our central insight is that the language of AP schemes, originally developed for classical kinetic and fluid equations, can be faithfully translated into quantum information theory by:

1.

Replacing function-space norms with the diamond norm to bound worst-case distinguishability of quantum channels,

2.

Interpreting asymptotic consistency as convergence of quantum instruments in the limit
\varepsilon\to 0

3.

Encoding scale separation via spectral gaps of Lindbladian generators and centering conditions,

4.

Accounting for quantum resource costs (gate counts, coherence times, measurement overhead) in complexity bounds.

Mathematical Preliminaries

We establish the operator-theoretic framework required for rigorous analysis of asymptotic-preserving quantum simulation. Throughout,
\mathcal{H}
denotes a finite-dimensional complex Hilbert space with
\dim\mathcal{H}=d<\infty
.

2.1
Operator Spaces and Quantum Channels

Let
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
denote the Banach space of bounded linear operators on
\mathcal{H}
equipped with the operator norm

\norm{X}:=\sup_{\norm{\psi}=1}\norm{X\psi},\qquad X\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}).

(2)

Let
\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{H})\subset\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
denote the subspace of trace-class operators equipped with the trace norm

\norm{X}_{1}:=\tr\sqrt{X^{\dagger}X},\qquad X\in\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{H}).

(3)

The set of density operators is
\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{H}):=\{\rho\in\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{H}):\rho\geq 0,\ \tr\rho=1\}
.

Definition 2.1

(Completely Positive Trace-Preserving Map)
.

A linear map
\Phi:\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{H})\to\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{H})
is completely positive trace-preserving (CPTP) if:

1.

\Phi
is trace-preserving:
\tr[\Phi(X)]=\tr[X]
for all
X\in\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{H})
,

2.

\Phi
is completely positive: for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
, the map
\Phi\otimes\mathbb{I}_{n}:\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{H}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{n})\to\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{H}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{n})
is positive, where
\mathbb{I}_{n}
is the identity map on
\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{C}^{n})
.

By the Stinespring dilation theorem
[
11
]
, any CPTP map
\Phi
admits a representation

\Phi(X)=\tr_{\mathcal{E}}[VXV^{\dagger}],\qquad X\in\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{H}),

(4)

where
\mathcal{E}
is an auxiliary Hilbert space and
V:\mathcal{H}\to\mathcal{H}\otimes\mathcal{E}
is an isometry.

Definition 2.2

(Diamond Norm)
.

The
diamond norm
of a linear map
\Phi:\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{H})\to\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{H})
is

\left\|\Phi\right\|_{\diamond}:=\sup_{n\geq 1}\sup_{\norm{X}_{1}\leq 1}\norm{(\Phi\otimes\id_n)(X)}_{1},

(5)

where the supremum is over all
X\in\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{H}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{n})
.

The diamond norm satisfies the following properties
[
12
,
13
]
:

1.

Submultiplicativity:
\left\|\Phi\circ\Psi\right\|_{\diamond}\leq\left\|\Phi\right\|_{\diamond}\cdot\left\|\Psi\right\|_{\diamond}
,

2.

Stability:
\left\|\Phi\otimes\mathbb{I}_{n}\right\|_{\diamond}=\left\|\Phi\right\|_{\diamond}
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
,

3.

Distinguishability: For CPTP maps
\Phi,\Psi
,
\left\|\Phi-\Psi\right\|_{\diamond}
equals the maximum probability of distinguishing
\Phi
from
\Psi
using any input state (including entangled ancillas) and any measurement.

Remark 2.3

(Norm relations)
.

For any linear map
\Phi:\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{H})\to\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{H})
, we have the norm inequalities

\norm{\Phi}\leq\left\|\Phi\right\|_{\diamond}\leq d\cdot\norm{\Phi},

(6)

where
\norm{\Phi}
is the operator norm induced by the trace norm on
\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{H})
. For primitive CPTP maps with unique steady state, exponential convergence in operator norm implies exponential convergence in diamond norm with the same rate.

2.2
Lindbladian Semigroups and Generators

Definition 2.4

(CPTP Semigroup)
.

A family
\{\Phi_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
of CPTP maps on
\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{H})
is a
CPTP semigroup
if:

1.

\Phi_{0}=\mathbb{I}
and
\Phi_{t+s}=\Phi_{t}\circ\Phi_{s}
for all
t,s\geq 0
,

2.

\lim_{t\to 0^{+}}\norm{\Phi_t(\rho) - \rho}_{1}=0
for all
\rho\in\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{H})
(strong continuity).

By the Gorini–Kossakowski–Sudarshan–Lindblad (GKSL) theorem
[
14
,
15
]
, the generator
\mathcal{L}
of a CPTP semigroup on finite-dimensional
\mathcal{H}
admits the representation

\mathcal{L}(\rho)=-i[H,\rho]+\sum_{k=1}^{m}\left(L_{k}\rho L_{k}^{\dagger}-\frac{1}{2}\anticommutator{L_k^\dagger L_k}{\rho}\right),

(7)

where
H=H^{\dagger}\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
is the Hamiltonian and
\{L_{k}\}_{k=1}^{m}\subset\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
are Lindblad operators. The semigroup is given by
\Phi_{t}=\exp(t\cL)
, where the exponential is defined via the power series in the Banach algebra of bounded linear maps on
\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{H})
.

Lemma 2.5

(Spectral properties of Lindbladians)
.

Let
\mathcal{L}
be a Lindbladian generator on finite-dimensional
\mathcal{H}
. Then:

1.

\operatorname{spec}(\mathcal{L})\subset\{z\in\mathbb{C}:\operatorname{Re}z\leq 0\}
,

2.

0\in\operatorname{spec}(\mathcal{L})
with eigenvector any steady state
\rho_{\star}
satisfying
\mathcal{L}(\rho_{\star})=0
,

3.

If
\mathcal{L}
has a unique steady state
\rho_{\star}
, then the spectral projection
\mathcal{P}
onto
\ker\mathcal{L}
is given by
\mathcal{P}(X)=\tr[X]\rho_{\star}
.

Proof.

Part (1) follows from the dissipativity of Lindbladians: for any
\rho\in\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{H})
,
\frac{d}{dt}\tr[\rho(t)^{2}]\leq 0
along solutions of
\dot{\rho}=\mathcal{L}(\rho)
, implying non-positive real parts of eigenvalues. Part (2) is immediate from trace preservation:
\tr[\mathcal{L}(\rho)]=0
for all
\rho
. Part (3) follows from the Perron–Frobenius theory for positive maps
[
16
]
: uniqueness of the steady state implies simplicity of the eigenvalue
0
, and the projection formula follows from the dual action on observables.
∎

2.3
Singular Perturbations of Lindbladians

We consider families of Lindbladian generators parameterized by
\varepsilon>0
:

\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon}=\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{fast}}+\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{slow}},

(8)

where
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{fast}}
and
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{slow}}
are themselves Lindbladian generators. The asymptotic analysis of
\exp(t\cL_\eps)
as
\varepsilon\to 0
relies on the spectral structure of
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{fast}}
.

Assumption 2.6

(Spectral gap, primitivity, and centering condition)
.

The fast generator
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{fast}}
satisfies:

1.

Unique steady state
: There exists a unique
\rho_{\mathrm{fast}}^{\star}\in\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{H})
such that
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{fast}}(\rho_{\mathrm{fast}}^{\star})=0
.

2.

Spectral gap
: The spectrum satisfies

\lambda_{\mathrm{gap}}:=\min\{|\operatorname{Re}\lambda|:\lambda\in\operatorname{spec}(\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{fast}})\setminus\{0\}\}>0.

(9)

3.

Primitivity
: The semigroup
\exp(t\cL_{\mathrm{fast}})
is primitive: there exists
t_{0}>0
such that
\exp(t_0\cL_{\mathrm{fast}})
is strictly positive (maps every nonzero
\rho\geq 0
to a full-rank state), implying a unique full-rank steady state.

4.

Centering Condition
: The slow generator satisfies the vanishing first-order coupling condition on the fast steady state manifold:

\mathcal{P}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{slow}}(\mathbb{I}-\mathcal{P})=0\quad\text{or equivalently}\quad\mathcal{P}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{slow}}=\mathcal{P}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{slow}}\mathcal{P}.

(10)

This ensures that the leading-order correction to the effective dynamics arises from the second-order Schur complement (
13
).
More precisely, for the standard adiabatic elimination to yield a valid Lindbladian at second order, we assume the
zero-mean condition
relative to the fast steady state:

\tr_{\mathrm{fast}}[\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{slow}}(\rho_{\mathrm{fast}}^{\star}\otimes\rho_{\mathrm{slow}})]=\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{eff}}^{(1)}(\rho_{\mathrm{slow}}),

(11)

where typically
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{eff}}^{(1)}=0
for interaction Hamiltonians that couple orthogonal subspaces (e.g., Jaynes-Cummings type). In the general case, the effective generator is determined by the Schur complement.

Remark 2.7

(Primitivity and diamond-norm convergence)
.

Assumption
2.6
(3) ensures exponential convergence in diamond norm: there exists
C>0
such that

\left\|\exp(t\cL_{\mathrm{fast}})-\mathcal{P}\right\|_{\diamond}\leq Ce^{-\lambda_{\mathrm{gap}}t},\qquad t\geq 0,

(12)

where
\lambda_{\mathrm{gap}}
is the spectral gap from (2). This follows from the spectral gap of the primitive map and the equivalence of norms on finite-dimensional spaces.

Under Assumption
2.6
, singular perturbation theory for Lindbladians
[
17
,
18
,
19
]
guarantees convergence of the solution
\rho^{\varepsilon}(t)=\exp(t\cL_\eps)(\rho_{0})
as
\varepsilon\to 0
to
\rho^{0}(t)=\exp(t\cL_{\mathrm{slow}}^{\mathrm{eff}})(\mathcal{P}\rho_{0})
, where
\mathcal{P}
is the spectral projection onto
\ker\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{fast}}
and the effective generator is given by the second-order Schur complement formula:

\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{slow}}^{\mathrm{eff}}=\mathcal{P}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{slow}}\mathcal{P}-\mathcal{P}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{slow}}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{fast}}^{+}(\mathbb{I}-\mathcal{P})\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{slow}}\mathcal{P},

(13)

with
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{fast}}^{+}
the Drazin inverse of
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{fast}}
on
\operatorname{ran}(\mathbb{I}-\mathcal{P})
. Note that for Lindbladian generators, the Schur complement structure preserves complete positivity to second order under additional coupling constraints; see
[
19
]
for sufficient conditions ensuring that
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{slow}}^{\mathrm{eff}}
generates a CPTP semigroup.

Lemma 2.8

(Resolvent estimate for
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{fast}}
)
.

Under Assumption
2.6
, the resolvent of
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{fast}}
satisfies

\norm{(\zeta- \cL_{\mathrm{fast}})^{-1}(\id- \cP)}\leq\frac{1}{\operatorname{dist}(\zeta,\operatorname{spec}(\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{fast}})\setminus\{0\})}\leq\frac{1}{\lambda_{\mathrm{gap}}-|\operatorname{Re}\zeta|}

(14)

for all
\zeta\in\mathbb{C}
with
\operatorname{Re}\zeta>-\lambda_{\mathrm{gap}}
.

Proof.

By the spectral mapping theorem for bounded operators, the resolvent norm is bounded by the inverse distance to the spectrum. The spectral gap condition ensures
\operatorname{dist}(\zeta,\operatorname{spec}(\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{fast}})\setminus\{0\})\geq\lambda_{\mathrm{gap}}-|\operatorname{Re}\zeta|
for
\operatorname{Re}\zeta>-\lambda_{\mathrm{gap}}
. The projection
\mathbb{I}-\mathcal{P}
restricts to the invariant subspace where
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{fast}}
is invertible, so the Drazin inverse
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{fast}}^{+}=-\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{t\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{fast}}}(\mathbb{I}-\mathcal{P})\,dt
is well-defined with norm
\norm{\cL_{\mathrm{fast}}^+}\leq 1/\lambda_{\mathrm{gap}}
.
∎

2.4
Trotter Error Analysis

Lemma 2.9

(Trotter error bound for Lindbladians)
.

Let
\mathcal{L}=\sum_{j=1}^{m}\mathcal{L}_{j}
be a decomposition of a Lindbladian generator into Lindbladian summands. The first-order Trotter product formula satisfies

\left\|\exp(\Delta t\cL)-\prod_{j=1}^{m}\exp(\Delta t\cL_j)\right\|_{\diamond}\leq\frac{(\Delta t)^{2}}{2}\sum_{1\leq j<k\leq m}\left\|\commutator{\cL_j}{\cL_k}\right\|_{\diamond}+\mathcal{O}((\Delta t)^{3}),

(15)

where
\commutator{\cL_j}{\cL_k}=\mathcal{L}_{j}\circ\mathcal{L}_{k}-\mathcal{L}_{k}\circ\mathcal{L}_{j}
is the commutator of superoperators.

Proof.

The proof follows from the Baker–Campbell–Hausdorff (BCH) formula for bounded operators on the Banach space
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{H}))
. For two generators
\mathcal{L}_{1},\mathcal{L}_{2}
, the BCH formula gives

\log(\exp(\Delta t\cL_1)\exp(\Delta t\cL_2))=\Delta t(\mathcal{L}_{1}+\mathcal{L}_{2})+\frac{(\Delta t)^{2}}{2}\commutator{\cL_1}{\cL_2}+\mathcal{O}((\Delta t)^{3}).

(16)

Exponentiating and using the submultiplicativity of the diamond norm
[
13
]
yields the two-term bound. The general case follows by induction on
m
, noting that the leading error term accumulates additively over pairwise commutators. The
\mathcal{O}((\Delta t)^{3})
remainder is uniform in the operators’ norms by analyticity of the exponential map.

Remark 2.10

(Exponential prefactor for CPTP maps)
.

In rigorous BCH-based derivations, the Trotter error bound typically includes a prefactor
e^{\Delta t\sum_{j}\norm{\cL_j}}
. However, for CPTP maps, each
\exp(\Delta t \cL_j)
is a contraction in the diamond norm:
\left\|\exp(\Delta t \cL_j)\right\|_{\diamond}\leq 1
. Consequently, the exponential prefactor equals 1, and the stated bound holds without additional multiplicative factors. This observation simplifies the error analysis for quantum channels while preserving mathematical rigor.

∎

For stiff generators of the form (
8
), the commutator of the scaled generators satisfies

\left\|\commutator{\frac{1}{\eps}\cL_{\mathrm{fast}}}{\cL_{\mathrm{slow}}}\right\|_{\diamond}=\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\left\|\commutator{\cL_{\mathrm{fast}}}{\cL_{\mathrm{slow}}}\right\|_{\diamond},

(17)

leading to the stiffness penalty
\Delta t=\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon)
for fixed error tolerance in standard Trotterization when
\left\|\commutator{\cL_{\mathrm{fast}}}{\cL_{\mathrm{slow}}}\right\|_{\diamond}=\Theta(1)
.

2.5
The Language of Asymptotic Preservation in Quantum Settings

The classical theory of AP schemes
[
2
]
is built upon a distinctive mathematical language: commutative diagrams expressing limiting procedures, uniform error bounds in appropriate function spaces, and resource estimates independent of scale-separation parameters. We now formalize how this language translates to quantum simulation.

Definition 2.11

(AP Language Components for Quantum Channels)
.

An asymptotic-preserving quantum simulation framework comprises:

1.

Limiting diagram
: A commutative diagram in the category of CPTP maps with diamond-norm morphisms:

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\exp(\Delta t\cL_\eps)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\Psi_{\Delta t}^{\varepsilon}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\exp(\Delta t\cL_{\mathrm{slow}}^{\mathrm{eff}})\circ\mathcal{P}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\Psi_{\Delta t}^{0}}
discretize
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\varepsilon\to 0}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\varepsilon\to 0}
discretize

(18)

2.

Uniform error metric
: Error bounds expressed in the diamond norm
\left\|\cdot\right\|_{\diamond}
, which controls worst-case distinguishability over all input states (including entangled ancillas) and is stable under tensoring with identity channels.

3.

Scale-invariant resource accounting
: Complexity bounds
\mathcal{R}(\Psi_{\Delta t}^{\varepsilon})
that remain bounded as
\varepsilon\to 0
for fixed
\Delta t
and target accuracy
\delta
, explicitly separating costs attributable to fast-scale resolution versus slow-scale dynamics.

4.

Centering Condition
: The algebraic constraint ensuring that first-order couplings vanish or are accounted for in the effective generator, allowing the second-order Schur complement to dominate the slow dynamics.

Remark 2.12

(Why diamond norm?)
.

The diamond norm is essential for quantum AP analysis because: (i) it is the operational metric for channel distinguishability in quantum information
[
13
]
, (ii) it satisfies submultiplicativity and stability properties required for compositional error analysis, and (iii) exponential convergence in diamond norm follows from spectral gaps for primitive Lindbladians. Classical
L^{p}
norms lack these properties for quantum channels.

Remark 2.13

(Commutative diagrams as design principle)
.

Diagram (
18
) is not merely illustrative: it encodes the requirement that discretization and asymptotic limiting commute. This guides protocol design: any quantum algorithm claiming AP properties must explicitly verify that its
\varepsilon\to 0
limit reproduces a consistent discretization of the effective slow dynamics.

Asymptotic-Preserving Quantum Simulation: Rigorous Formulation

We now formulate asymptotic-preserving quantum simulation with explicit error bounds uniform in the stiffness parameter
\varepsilon
.

3.1
Problem Statement and AP Requirements

Definition 3.1

(AP Quantum Simulation Protocol)
.

A discrete-time quantum simulation protocol
\{\Psi_{\Delta t}^{\varepsilon}\}_{\Delta t>0,\varepsilon>0}
, where each
\Psi_{\Delta t}^{\varepsilon}:\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{H})\to\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{H})
is a CPTP map, is
asymptotic-preserving
for the family
\{\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon}\}_{\varepsilon>0}
if there exist constants
C_{1},C_{2},p,q>0
independent of
\varepsilon
and
\Delta t
such that:

1.

Consistency
: For fixed
\varepsilon>0
,

\left\|\Psi_{\Delta t}^{\varepsilon}-\exp(\Delta t\cL_\eps)\right\|_{\diamond}\leq C_{1}(\Delta t)^{p+1}.

(19)

2.

Asymptotic consistency
: As
\varepsilon\to 0
with fixed
\Delta t
,

\left\|\Psi_{\Delta t}^{\varepsilon}-\exp(\Delta t\cL_{\mathrm{slow}}^{\mathrm{eff}})\circ\mathcal{P}\right\|_{\diamond}\leq C_{2}(\varepsilon(\Delta t)^{q}+(\Delta t)^{p+1}),

(20)

where
\mathcal{P}
is the spectral projection onto
\ker\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{fast}}
and
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{slow}}^{\mathrm{eff}}
is given by (
13
).

3.

Resource uniformity
: The resource cost
\mathcal{R}(\Psi_{\Delta t}^{\varepsilon})
(gate count, measurement overhead, classical control time) satisfies

\mathcal{R}(\Psi_{\Delta t}^{\varepsilon})\leq C_{3}\cdot\operatorname{poly}(d,1/\delta)\quad\text{for fixed }\Delta t,\delta>0,

(21)

where
d=\dim\mathcal{H}
and
\delta
is the target accuracy in trace norm.

Definition
3.1
formalizes the AP property with explicit error bounds. Equation (
19
) ensures the protocol accurately simulates the full stiff dynamics for fixed
\varepsilon
. Equation (
20
) is the key AP condition: as
\varepsilon\to 0
, the protocol converges to a consistent discretization of the limiting slow dynamics, with error vanishing linearly in
\varepsilon
for fixed
\Delta t
. Equation (
21
) ensures computational advantage by bounding resource costs independently of
\varepsilon
.

3.2
Commutative Diagram Theorem

The asymptotic-preserving property can be expressed through a commutative diagram in the category of CPTP maps with diamond-norm morphisms, as in (
18
).

Theorem 3.2

(Commutative Diagram for AP Quantum Simulation)
.

Let
\{\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon}\}_{\varepsilon>0}
satisfy Assumption
2.6
. Suppose the protocol
\Psi_{\Delta t}^{\varepsilon}
implements a layered strategy:

1.

Fast layer
: Apply
N=\lceil\Delta t/(\varepsilon\tau_{\mathrm{fast}})\rceil
substeps of a CPTP map
\Phi_{\mathrm{fast}}
satisfying

\left\|\Phi_{\mathrm{fast}}-\exp((\eps\tau_{\mathrm{fast}})\cL_{\mathrm{fast}})\right\|_{\diamond}\leq C_{\mathrm{fast}}(\varepsilon\tau_{\mathrm{fast}})^{r+1}.

(22)

2.

Slow layer
: Apply a CPTP map
\Phi_{\mathrm{slow}}
satisfying

\left\|\Phi_{\mathrm{slow}}-\exp(\Delta t\cL_{\mathrm{slow}})\right\|_{\diamond}\leq C_{\mathrm{slow}}(\Delta t)^{s+1}.

(23)

3.

Composition
:
\Psi_{\Delta t}^{\varepsilon}=\Phi_{\mathrm{slow}}\circ(\Phi_{\mathrm{fast}})^{N}
.

Then
\Psi_{\Delta t}^{\varepsilon}
satisfies the AP property (Definition
3.1
) with explicit constants:

\displaystyle C_{1}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}\!\left(C_{\mathrm{fast}}+C_{\mathrm{slow}}+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\left\|\commutator{\cL_{\mathrm{fast}}}{\cL_{\mathrm{slow}}}\right\|_{\diamond}\right),\quad p=\min(r,s),

(24)

\displaystyle C_{2}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}\!\left(\frac{C_{\mathrm{fast}}}{\lambda_{\mathrm{gap}}}+\left\|\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{slow}}^{\mathrm{eff}}-\mathcal{P}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{slow}}\mathcal{P}\right\|_{\diamond}\right),\quad q=1,

(25)

\displaystyle C_{3}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}\!\left(N\cdot\mathcal{R}(\Phi_{\mathrm{fast}})+\mathcal{R}(\Phi_{\mathrm{slow}})+\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{classical}}\right),

(26)

where
\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{classical}}
is the cost of computing effective parameters. Moreover, the diagram (
18
) commutes in the sense that

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\left\|\Psi_{\Delta t}^{\varepsilon}-\Psi_{\Delta t}^{0}\right\|_{\diamond}=0\quad\text{for fixed }\Delta t,

(27)

with
\Psi_{\Delta t}^{0}=\exp(\Delta t\cL_{\mathrm{slow}}^{\mathrm{eff}})\circ\mathcal{P}
.

Proof.

To establish the AP property, we first analyze the convergence of the fast layer. By Assumption
2.6
and Remark
2.7
, the semigroup
\exp(t\cL_{\mathrm{fast}})
converges exponentially to the spectral projection
\mathcal{P}
in diamond norm, specifically
\left\|\exp(t\cL_{\mathrm{fast}})-\mathcal{P}\right\|_{\diamond}\leq Ce^{-\lambda_{\mathrm{gap}}t}
. Since the approximate map
\Phi_{\mathrm{fast}}
approximates the exact evolution over a small time step
\varepsilon\tau_{\mathrm{fast}}
with error
\mathcal{O}((\varepsilon\tau_{\mathrm{fast}})^{r+1})
, iterating this
N=\lceil\Delta t/(\varepsilon\tau_{\mathrm{fast}})\rceil
times yields a total error bound for the fast layer composition. Using the submultiplicativity of the diamond norm and the fact that
N\varepsilon\tau_{\mathrm{fast}}\geq\Delta t
, the error decomposes into an accumulation of local approximation errors and the exponential decay toward the projection
\mathcal{P}
. For fixed
\Delta t>0
, the exponential term vanishes as
\varepsilon\to 0
, leaving an error scaling dominated by the local approximation order.

Next, we address the interaction between fast and slow dynamics using the Duhamel expansion for the exact evolution
\exp(\Delta t\cL_\eps)
. This integral representation allows us to isolate the contribution of the slow generator
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{slow}}
acting on the rapidly decaying fast semigroup. Due to the spectral gap
\lambda_{\mathrm{gap}}
, the integral term involving the fast semigroup acting on the range of
(\mathbb{I}-\mathcal{P})
is suppressed by a factor of
\varepsilon/\lambda_{\mathrm{gap}}
. This suppression ensures that the error contribution from the non-commutativity of
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{fast}}
and
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{slow}}
scales as
\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon\Delta t)
rather than the naive
\mathcal{O}(\Delta t^{2}/\varepsilon)
, provided the centering condition holds. This condition ensures that the leading-order term
\mathcal{P}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{slow}}\mathcal{P}
is correctly captured by the effective generator, preventing secular growth in the error.

Combining these results, we bound the total error of the layered protocol
\Psi_{\Delta t}^{\varepsilon}=\Phi_{\mathrm{slow}}\circ(\Phi_{\mathrm{fast}})^{N}
. By the triangle inequality and submultiplicativity, the total error is bounded by the sum of the slow layer error, the fast layer error propagated through the slow map, and the interaction error derived from the Duhamel expansion. This yields the consistency bound with constants
C_{1}
and exponent
p=\min(r,s)
. In the asymptotic limit
\varepsilon\to 0
with fixed
\Delta t
, the fast-layer error vanishes exponentially, and the residual error is dominated by the second-order Schur complement term, which scales as
\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon\Delta t)
. This establishes the asymptotic consistency bound with
C_{2}
and
q=1
. Finally, the resource bound follows directly from the construction: if the fast layer is implemented via analog evolution (zero gates) or analytic elimination, its cost is negligible, and the total complexity is dominated by the slow layer and classical coordination, remaining independent of
\varepsilon
.

4

We now establish precise resource-complexity bounds for AP quantum simulation, with explicit dependencies on Hilbert space dimension, error tolerance, and stiffness parameter.

Assumption 4.1

(Time-scale hierarchy, dimension scaling, and locality)

The target system admits a decomposition

d_{\mathrm{slow}}=\dim\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{slow}}

.

3.

Local gate complexity
: For
k

k

\delta

\mathcal{O}(d^{c}\cdot\operatorname{poly}(1/\delta))
gates for some

k

Theorem 4.2

(Rigorous Resource Complexity for AP Quantum Simulation)

Under Assumptions

4.1

\varepsilon=\tau_{1}

(28)

where

(29)

G_{\mathrm{std}}/G_{\mathrm{AP}}^{\mathrm{digital}}=\Theta(1)

3.

1

1

Proof.

For standard digital simulation, the Trotter error per step is determined by the commutator of the scaled generators, scaling as
\mathcal{O}((\Delta t)^{2}\varepsilon^{-1}\left\|\commutator{\cL_{\mathrm{fast}}}{\cL_{\mathrm{slow}}}\right\|_{\diamond})

\delta

\Delta t

\varepsilon

\Delta t=\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon\delta/T\cdot\left\|\commutator{\cL_{\mathrm{fast}}}{\cL_{\mathrm{slow}}}\right\|_{\diamond}^{-1})

\Omega(T/(\varepsilon\delta))

\Omega(T/\varepsilon\cdot d_{\mathrm{tot}}^{c}\cdot\operatorname{poly}(1/\delta))

\tau_{n}=\kappa\varepsilon
, reducing the number of slow-layer steps to
T/\tau_{n}
. Furthermore, because the fast degrees of freedom are slaved to the slow ones, the simulation effectively occurs on the reduced Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{slow}}

(d_{\mathrm{tot}}/d_{\mathrm{slow}})^{c}=d_{\mathrm{fast}}^{c}
. Combining the temporal and dimensional savings yields the superlinear speedup. Similarly, analytic adiabatic elimination allows for classical precomputation of the effective generator, shifting the computational burden from online quantum gates to offline classical processing. While the precomputation cost scales as
\mathcal{O}(d_{\mathrm{tot}}^{3})

T

\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{slow}}
with time steps determined by
\tau_{n}
, leading to the stated complexity bound. The necessity of these approaches is underscored by the tightness of the Trotter error bound, which dictates that any purely digital method must resolve the fastest timescale
\varepsilon

\Omega(1/\varepsilon)
penalty.
∎

Remark 4.3

(Necessity of analog or analytic resolution and limitations)
.

Theorem
4.2
establishes that superlinear savings
\Omega(\kappa\cdot d_{\mathrm{fast}}^{c})
are achievable
if and only if
fast dynamics are resolved via (i) native analog evolution, or (ii) analytic dimensionality reduction. Purely digital implementations cannot circumvent the
\Omega(1/\varepsilon)
stiffness penalty, as the Trotter error bound is tight for generic stiff generators
[
9
]

\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{fast}}
,
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{slow}}
for analytic elimination, and (c) negligible overhead from classical coordination. Relaxing these assumptions may reduce the achievable savings. In particular, the analysis assumes that analog implementation errors are
\ll\varepsilon
; incorporating realistic analog error models (calibration drift, decoherence, control imperfections) into the error budget is an important direction for practical applications.

5.1
Cavity QED in the Bad-Cavity Limit

Consider a two-level system (qubit) coupled to a single lossy bosonic mode (cavity), with joint Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{cav}}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{qubit}}
, governed by the Lindblad master equation

\frac{d}{dt}\rho=-i[H,\rho]+\kappa\mathcal{D}[a](\rho),

(33)

where
H=\omega_{q}\sigma^{z}/2+g(\sigma^{+}a+\sigma^{-}a^{\dagger})
and
\mathcal{D}[a](\rho)=a\rho a^{\dagger}-\frac{1}{2}\anticommutator{a^\dagger a}{\rho}

\kappa\gg g
, we define
\varepsilon=g/\kappa\ll 1

\tau_{\mathrm{fast}}=1/\kappa
for cavity decay and
\tau_{\mathrm{slow}}=1/g
for qubit dynamics.

Proposition 5.1

(Spectral gap and fixed-point algebra for cavity dissipator)
.

The fast generator
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{fast}}(\rho)=\kappa\mathcal{D}[a](\rho)
has:

1.

Unique steady state on the cavity:
\rho_{\mathrm{cav}}^{\star}=\outerproduct{0}{0}
,

2.

Spectral gap
\lambda_{\mathrm{gap}}=\kappa
,

3.

Fixed-point algebra
\ker\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{fast}}=\{\outerproduct{0}{0}\otimes\sigma_{q}:\sigma_{q}\in\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{qubit}})\}
,

4.

Spectral projection
\mathcal{P}(\rho)=\outerproduct{0}{0}\otimes\tr_{\mathrm{cav}}(\rho)
.

Proof.

The dissipator
\mathcal{D}[a]
acts only on the cavity mode and has eigenvalues
-\kappa n
for
n=0,1,2,\ldots
corresponding to photon number states, with
n=0
being the unique zero eigenvalue
[
20
]
. The spectral gap is therefore
\kappa
. The fixed-point algebra consists of all states with the cavity in vacuum, tensored with arbitrary qubit states. The projection formula follows from the partial trace over the cavity.
∎

The effective slow generator is derived via Eq. (
13
). Since
\mathcal{P}H_{int}\mathcal{P}=0
(centering condition), the first-order term vanishes. The second-order term yields
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{slow}}^{\mathrm{eff}}(\rho_{q})=-i[\omega_{q}\sigma^{z}/2,\rho_{q}]+\gamma\mathcal{D}[\sigma^{-}](\rho_{q})
with Purcell rate
\gamma=4g^{2}/\kappa

[
10
]
, acting on
\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{qubit}}\cong\mathbb{C}^{2}

(AP error bound for cavity QED)
.

Let
\Psi_{\Delta t}^{\varepsilon}
implement the layered protocol with:

•

Fast layer: Analog evolution
\Phi_{\mathrm{fast}}=\exp((\eps/\kappa)\cL_{\mathrm{fast}})

•

\Phi_{\mathrm{slow}}
of

Then for

(34)

with

G_{\mathrm{AP}}=\mathcal{O}(T/\tau_{n}\cdot\operatorname{poly}(1/\delta))

Proof.

Apply Theorem
3.2
with

(exact analog evolution),
s=1
(first-order Trotter). By Proposition

(35)

Since

(Classical-quantum interface)

Let

Remark 5.6

(Classical-quantum interface and multiscale limits)

Proposition

6
Discussion and Open Problems

While the framework provides rigorous error bounds for AP quantum simulation, several mathematical challenges remain:

1.

Non-Markovian extensions
: Assumption
2.6
requires Markovian fast dynamics. Extending to non-Markovian settings requires analysis of time-convolutionless generators
[
22
]
and their singular perturbations, involving memory kernels and integro-differential equations.

2.

Infinite-dimensional systems
: Our analysis assumes finite-dimensional
\mathcal{H}
. Extending to bosonic modes or field theories requires careful treatment of unbounded generators, domain issues, and strong vs. norm convergence of semigroups
[
17
]
.

3.

Measurement backaction
: The stochastic nature of quantum measurements in the slow layer introduces additional error terms not captured by diamond-norm bounds; a full analysis requires martingale techniques for quantum trajectories
[
23
]
.

4.

Optimality of bounds
: The constants
C_{1},C_{2}
in Definition
3.1
depend on commutator norms that may be loose for specific physical systems; tighter bounds via problem-specific analysis remain an open direction.

5.

Hardware imperfections
: The resource analysis assumes perfect analog evolution and exact knowledge of generators. Incorporating control errors, decoherence, and calibration uncertainty into the error bounds is an important direction for practical applications.

6.

Automatic AP protocol synthesis
: Can the commutative diagram condition (
18
) be used to algorithmically generate AP quantum circuits from high-scale specifications of multiscale physical systems?

Remark 6.1

(Comparison with classical AP theory)
.

Our quantum AP framework parallels classical theory
[
2
]
in structure but differs in key aspects: (i) diamond-norm vs.
L^{p}
error metrics, (ii) CPTP constraints vs. positivity preservation, (iii) measurement-induced stochasticity vs. deterministic projection. These differences necessitate new mathematical tools, particularly for handling the interplay between unitary evolution, dissipation, and measurement.

Remark 6.2

(Finite-dimensional assumption)
.

All diamond-norm error bounds in this work implicitly assume that the underlying Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
is finite-dimensional. This ensures that: (i) the diamond norm is well-defined and equivalent to other operator norms, (ii) spectral gaps imply exponential convergence with uniform constants, and (iii) the Drazin inverse
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{fast}}^{+}
is bounded. Extensions to infinite-dimensional systems (e.g., bosonic modes, quantum fields) require careful treatment of unbounded generators, domain considerations, and strong vs. norm convergence of semigroups
[
17
]
; such generalizations are an important direction for future work.

7
Conclusion

We have developed a mathematically rigorous framework for simulating
multiscale physical systems
using quantum computational resources, by systematically translating the
language of asymptotic-preserving schemes
into quantum channel formalism. The central contributions are: (i) a precise definition of AP quantum simulation with explicit diamond-norm error bounds uniform in stiffness (Definition
3.1
), (ii) a commutative diagram theorem (Theorem
3.2
) establishing uniform convergence under spectral conditions, with complete proof providing explicit constants, and (iii) rigorous resource-complexity bounds (Theorem
4.2
) clarifying necessary and sufficient conditions for superlinear savings.

Our analysis demonstrates that time-scale separation alone does not guarantee quantum advantage: purely digital implementations yield at most constant-factor improvement. Genuine superlinear savings
\Omega(\kappa\cdot d_{\mathrm{fast}}^{c})
arise if and only if fast dynamics are resolved via native analog evolution or analytic adiabatic elimination. This precise characterization resolves misconceptions and establishes clear hardware requirements for resource-efficient stiff quantum simulation.

By providing explicit error bounds and resource accounting with quantified dependencies on
\varepsilon
,
\Delta t
, and system dimension, this work enables rigorous comparison with alternative methodologies and guides the co-design of quantum hardware and multiscale algorithms. The framework’s implications extend to quantum simulation of open systems, quantum thermodynamics, kinetic theory, and multiscale quantum control. Future directions include extending the AP language to non-Markovian dynamics, infinite-dimensional systems, and measurement-based adaptive protocols, as well as experimental validation on near-term quantum hardware with engineered dissipation.

A.1
Stinespring Dilation Theorem and Quantum Channel Representations

The Stinespring dilation theorem provides a fundamental structural characterization of completely positive maps, which underlies our treatment of quantum channels and their compositions.

Theorem A.1

(Stinespring Dilation)
.

Let
\mathcal{H}
and
\mathcal{K}
be Hilbert spaces, and let
\Phi:\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})\to\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{K})
be a completely positive linear map. Then there exists a Hilbert space
\mathcal{E}
, a
*
-representation
\pi:\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})\to\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{E})
, and a bounded operator
V:\mathcal{K}\to\mathcal{E}
such that

\Phi(X)=V^{\dagger}\pi(X)V,\qquad\forall X\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}).

(38)

If
\Phi
is trace-preserving, then
V
is an isometry:
V^{\dagger}V=\mathbb{I}_{\mathcal{K}}
.

Remark A.2

(Interpretation for CPTP maps)
.

For a CPTP map
\Phi:\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{H})\to\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{H})
, Theorem
A.1
implies the existence of an auxiliary environment
\mathcal{E}
and an isometry
V:\mathcal{H}\to\mathcal{H}\otimes\mathcal{E}
such that

\Phi(\rho)=\tr_{\mathcal{E}}[V\rho V^{\dagger}],\qquad\rho\in\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{H}).

(39)

This representation is crucial for our analysis because:

1.

It justifies the physical interpretation of CPTP maps as unitary evolution on a larger system followed by partial trace.

2.

It enables the derivation of diamond-norm bounds via purification arguments: for any two CPTP maps
\Phi,\Psi
,

\left\|\Phi-\Psi\right\|_{\diamond}=\sup_{\ket{\psi}\in\mathcal{H}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{ref}}}\norm{(\Phi\otimes\id)(\ketbra{\psi}{\psi}) - (\Psi\otimes\id)(\ketbra{\psi}{\psi})}_{1},

(40)

where the supremum is achieved on pure states due to convexity of the trace norm.

3.

It facilitates the analysis of layered protocols: the composition
\Phi_{2}\circ\Phi_{1}
corresponds to sequential isometric embeddings
V_{2}\circ V_{1}
, with error accumulation controlled by submultiplicativity of the diamond norm.

Proof of Theorem
A.1
.

The construction proceeds via the GNS-like representation:

1.

Define a sesquilinear form on
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})\otimes\mathcal{K}
by
\langle X\otimes\psi,Y\otimes\phi\rangle_{\Phi}:=\langle\psi,\Phi(X^{\dagger}Y)\phi\rangle_{\mathcal{K}}
.

2.

Complete the quotient by null vectors to obtain a Hilbert space
\mathcal{E}
.

3.

Define
\pi(X)(Y\otimes\phi):=(XY)\otimes\phi
and
V\psi:=\mathbb{I}\otimes\psi
.

4.

Verify that
\Phi(X)=V^{\dagger}\pi(X)V
and that
\pi
is a
*
-representation.

For finite-dimensional
\mathcal{H}
, one may take
\dim\mathcal{E}\leq(\dim\mathcal{H})^{2}
; minimal dilations are unique up to unitary equivalence on
\mathcal{E}
.
∎

A.2
Spectral Theory of Lindbladian Generators

The analysis of stiff open quantum systems relies on detailed spectral properties of Lindbladian generators. We collect here the key results used in the main text.

Lemma A.3

(Spectral decomposition of primitive Lindbladians)
.

Let
\mathcal{L}
be a Lindbladian generator on finite-dimensional
\mathcal{H}
satisfying Assumption
2.6
. Then:

1.

The spectrum admits the decomposition

\operatorname{spec}(\mathcal{L})=\{0\}\cup\{\lambda\in\mathbb{C}:\operatorname{Re}\lambda\leq-\lambda_{\mathrm{gap}}\},

(41)

where
\lambda_{\mathrm{gap}}>0
is the spectral gap.

2.

The spectral projection
\mathcal{P}
onto
\ker\mathcal{L}
is given by the ergodic average

\mathcal{P}(X)=\lim_{T\to\infty}\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}e^{t\mathcal{L}}(X)\,dt=\tr[X]\rho_{\star},

(42)

where
\rho_{\star}
is the unique steady state.

3.

The Drazin inverse
\mathcal{L}^{+}
on
\operatorname{ran}(\mathbb{I}-\mathcal{P})
is well-defined and satisfies

\mathcal{L}^{+}=-\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{t\mathcal{L}}(\mathbb{I}-\mathcal{P})\,dt,\qquad\norm{\cL^+}\leq\frac{1}{\lambda_{\mathrm{gap}}}.

(43)

Proof.

Part (1) follows from the dissipativity of Lindbladians: for any
\rho\in\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{H})
, the function
t\mapsto\tr[\rho(t)^{2}]
is non-increasing along solutions of
\dot{\rho}=\mathcal{L}(\rho)
, implying
\operatorname{Re}\lambda\leq 0
for all eigenvalues. Primitivity ensures that
0
is a simple eigenvalue with strictly positive eigenvector
\rho_{\star}
, and all other eigenvalues have real parts bounded away from zero by
\lambda_{\mathrm{gap}}
.

Part (2) is a standard result in ergodic theory for Markov semigroups: the time average converges to the projection onto the fixed-point subspace, which for primitive Lindbladians is one-dimensional.

Part (3) follows from the Laplace transform representation of the resolvent: for
\operatorname{Re}z>-\lambda_{\mathrm{gap}}
,

(z-\mathcal{L})^{-1}=\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-zt}e^{t\mathcal{L}}\,dt,

(44)

and evaluating at
z=0
on
\operatorname{ran}(\mathbb{I}-\mathcal{P})
yields the Drazin inverse formula. The norm bound follows from exponential convergence:
\norm{e^{t\cL}(\id- \cP)}\leq Ce^{-\lambda_{\mathrm{gap}}t}
.
∎

A.3
Duhamel’s Principle for Operator Semigroups

Duhamel’s principle provides an integral representation for the difference between two semigroups, which is essential for analyzing the interaction between fast and slow dynamics.

Lemma A.4

(Duhamel expansion for perturbed generators)
.

Let
\mathcal{L}_{0}
and
\mathcal{L}_{1}
be bounded generators on a Banach space
\mathcal{X}
, and define
\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon}=\mathcal{L}_{0}+\varepsilon\mathcal{L}_{1}
. Then for any
t\geq 0
,

e^{t\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon}}=e^{t\mathcal{L}_{0}}+\varepsilon\int_{0}^{t}e^{(t-s)\mathcal{L}_{0}}\mathcal{L}_{1}e^{s\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon}}\,ds.

(45)

Iterating yields the Dyson series

e^{t\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon}}=e^{t\mathcal{L}_{0}}+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\varepsilon^{n}\int_{0\leq s_{1}\leq\cdots\leq s_{n}\leq t}e^{(t-s_{n})\mathcal{L}_{0}}\mathcal{L}_{1}e^{(s_{n}-s_{n-1})\mathcal{L}_{0}}\cdots\mathcal{L}_{1}e^{s_{1}\mathcal{L}_{0}}\,ds_{1}\cdots ds_{n}.

(46)

Proof.

Differentiate both sides of (
45
) with respect to
t
:

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\left[e^{t\mathcal{L}_{0}}+\varepsilon\int_{0}^{t}e^{(t-s)\mathcal{L}_{0}}\mathcal{L}_{1}e^{s\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon}}\,ds\right]

\displaystyle=\mathcal{L}_{0}e^{t\mathcal{L}_{0}}+\varepsilon\mathcal{L}_{1}e^{t\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon}}+\varepsilon\int_{0}^{t}\mathcal{L}_{0}e^{(t-s)\mathcal{L}_{0}}\mathcal{L}_{1}e^{s\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon}}\,ds

\displaystyle=(\mathcal{L}_{0}+\varepsilon\mathcal{L}_{1})\left[e^{t\mathcal{L}_{0}}+\varepsilon\int_{0}^{t}e^{(t-s)\mathcal{L}_{0}}\mathcal{L}_{1}e^{s\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon}}\,ds\right]

\displaystyle=\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon}\cdot(\text{RHS}),

and both sides agree at
t=0
. Uniqueness of solutions to the abstract Cauchy problem implies the identity. The Dyson series follows by iterating the integral equation.
∎

Remark A.5

(Application to stiff generators)
.

For the singularly perturbed generator
\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon}=\varepsilon^{-1}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{fast}}+\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{slow}}
, we apply Lemma
A.4
with
\mathcal{L}_{0}=\varepsilon^{-1}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{fast}}
and
\mathcal{L}_{1}=\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{slow}}
. The key observation is that
e^{t\mathcal{L}_{0}}=e^{(t/\varepsilon)\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{fast}}}
converges exponentially to
\mathcal{P}
as
t/\varepsilon\to\infty
. Thus, for fixed
\Delta t>0
,

\int_{0}^{\Delta t}e^{(\Delta t-s)\mathcal{L}_{0}}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{slow}}e^{s\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon}}\,ds=\int_{0}^{\Delta t}\mathcal{P}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{slow}}\mathcal{P}\,ds+\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon),

(47)

where the
\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon)
term arises from the exponential decay of
(\mathbb{I}-\mathcal{P})e^{t\mathcal{L}_{0}}
and the centering condition (
10
). This yields the uniform error bound
\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon\Delta t+(\Delta t)^{2})
in Theorem
3.2
.

A.4
Schur Complement and Adiabatic Elimination

The effective generator (
13
) arises from a Schur complement reduction, a standard technique in singular perturbation theory.

Lemma A.6

(Schur complement for block operators)
.

Let
\mathcal{X}=\mathcal{X}_{1}\oplus\mathcal{X}_{2}
be a direct sum decomposition, and let
\mathcal{L}:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{X}
have block matrix representation

\mathcal{L}=\begin{pmatrix}A&B\\
C&D\end{pmatrix},

(48)

where
D
is invertible on
\mathcal{X}_{2}
. Then the Schur complement of
D
in
\mathcal{L}
is

S=A-BD^{-1}C,

(49)

and satisfies: if
(x_{1},x_{2})^{\top}\in\ker\mathcal{L}
, then
x_{1}\in\ker S
. Conversely, if
x_{1}\in\ker S
, then
(x_{1},-D^{-1}Cx_{1})^{\top}\in\ker\mathcal{L}
.

Proof.

Direct computation:
\mathcal{L}\binom{x_{1}}{x_{2}}=0
implies
Ax_{1}+Bx_{2}=0
and
Cx_{1}+Dx_{2}=0
. Solving the second equation for
x_{2}=-D^{-1}Cx_{1}
and substituting into the first yields
Sx_{1}=0
.
∎

Corollary A.7

(Effective generator via Schur complement)
.

Under Assumption
2.6
, decompose
\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{H})=\ker\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{fast}}\oplus\operatorname{ran}(\mathbb{I}-\mathcal{P})
. The generator
\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon}=\varepsilon^{-1}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{fast}}+\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{slow}}
has block form

\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon}=\begin{pmatrix}\mathcal{P}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{slow}}\mathcal{P}&\mathcal{P}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{slow}}(\mathbb{I}-\mathcal{P})\\
(\mathbb{I}-\mathcal{P})\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{slow}}\mathcal{P}&\varepsilon^{-1}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{fast}}+(\mathbb{I}-\mathcal{P})\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{slow}}(\mathbb{I}-\mathcal{P})\end{pmatrix}.

(50)

The Schur complement of the lower-right block is

S_{\varepsilon}=\mathcal{P}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{slow}}\mathcal{P}-\varepsilon\,\mathcal{P}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{slow}}(\mathbb{I}-\mathcal{P})\left[\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{fast}}+\varepsilon(\mathbb{I}-\mathcal{P})\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{slow}}(\mathbb{I}-\mathcal{P})\right]^{-1}(\mathbb{I}-\mathcal{P})\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{slow}}\mathcal{P}.

(51)

Expanding the inverse in powers of
\varepsilon
and using
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{fast}}^{+}=-\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{t\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{fast}}}(\mathbb{I}-\mathcal{P})\,dt
yields

S_{\varepsilon}=\mathcal{P}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{slow}}\mathcal{P}-\varepsilon\,\mathcal{P}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{slow}}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{fast}}^{+}(\mathbb{I}-\mathcal{P})\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{slow}}\mathcal{P}+\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{2}),

(52)

which, upon rescaling time by
\varepsilon
, gives the effective generator (
13
).

Remark A.8

(Complete positivity of the effective generator)
.

While the Schur complement formula (
13
) is derived algebraically, ensuring that
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{slow}}^{\mathrm{eff}}
generates a CPTP semigroup requires additional structure. For Lindbladian generators, this holds to second order under the centering condition (
10
) and additional coupling constraints; see
[
19
]
for sufficient conditions ensuring complete positivity preservation under adiabatic elimination. The key is that the correction term
-\mathcal{P}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{slow}}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{fast}}^{+}(\mathbb{I}-\mathcal{P})\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{slow}}\mathcal{P}
can be written as a sum of dissipators when
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{fast}}
and
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{slow}}
arise from physical couplings satisfying appropriate commutation relations.

A.5
Diamond Norm: Properties and Estimation Techniques

The diamond norm is the operational metric for quantum channel distinguishability. We collect here the properties used in our error analysis.

Proposition A.9

(Key properties of the diamond norm)
.

Let
\Phi,\Psi:\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{H})\to\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{H})
be linear maps. Then:

1.

Stability
:
\left\|\Phi\otimes\mathbb{I}_{n}\right\|_{\diamond}=\left\|\Phi\right\|_{\diamond}
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

2.

Submultiplicativity
:
\left\|\Phi\circ\Psi\right\|_{\diamond}\leq\left\|\Phi\right\|_{\diamond}\cdot\left\|\Psi\right\|_{\diamond}
.

3.

Triangle inequality
:
\left\|\Phi+\Psi\right\|_{\diamond}\leq\left\|\Phi\right\|_{\diamond}+\left\|\Psi\right\|_{\diamond}
.

4.

Duality
:
\left\|\Phi\right\|_{\diamond}=\sup\{|\tr[Y^{\dagger}\Phi(X)]|:\left\|X\right\|_{\diamond}\leq 1,\left\|Y\right\|_{\diamond}\leq 1\}
, where the dual norm is also the diamond norm.

5.

Semidefinite characterization
: For Hermiticity-preserving
\Phi
,

\left\|\Phi\right\|_{\diamond}=\max\{\tr[J(\Phi)(W_{0}\otimes\mathbb{I})]:W_{0}\geq 0,\tr[W_{0}]=1\},

(53)

where
J(\Phi)=\sum_{i,j}\Phi(\outerproduct{i}{j})\otimes\outerproduct{i}{j}
is the Choi matrix.

6.

Exponential convergence
: If
\mathcal{L}
is primitive with spectral gap
\lambda_{\mathrm{gap}}
, then

\left\|e^{t\mathcal{L}}-\mathcal{P}\right\|_{\diamond}\leq Ce^{-\lambda_{\mathrm{gap}}t},\qquad t\geq 0,

(54)

for some
C>0
depending on the dimension and the condition number of the eigenbasis.

Proof of exponential convergence.

By the spectral gap assumption,
\mathcal{L}
admits a decomposition
\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{P}\mathcal{L}\mathcal{P}+(\mathbb{I}-\mathcal{P})\mathcal{L}(\mathbb{I}-\mathcal{P})
with
\operatorname{spec}((\mathbb{I}-\mathcal{P})\mathcal{L}(\mathbb{I}-\mathcal{P}))\subset\{z:\operatorname{Re}z\leq-\lambda_{\mathrm{gap}}\}
. The semigroup satisfies

e^{t\mathcal{L}}=\mathcal{P}+e^{t(\mathbb{I}-\mathcal{P})\mathcal{L}(\mathbb{I}-\mathcal{P})}(\mathbb{I}-\mathcal{P}).

(55)

The norm bound follows from the spectral mapping theorem and equivalence of norms on finite-dimensional spaces: there exists a basis in which
(\mathbb{I}-\mathcal{P})\mathcal{L}(\mathbb{I}-\mathcal{P})
is upper triangular with diagonal entries having real parts
\leq-\lambda_{\mathrm{gap}}
, yielding exponential decay with polynomial prefactor absorbed into
C
.
∎

Remark A.10

(Practical estimation)
.

For numerical verification of diamond-norm bounds, one may use:

•

Semidefinite programming via the Choi matrix characterization
[
13
]
.

•

Monte Carlo estimation using random pure states and ancillas.

•

Analytic bounds via commutator norms and Trotter error formulas (Lemma
2.9
).

In our analysis, we primarily rely on analytic bounds to maintain uniformity in
\varepsilon
.

A.6
Trotter–Suzuki Error Analysis for Lindbladians

The Trotter error bound in Lemma
2.9
is derived from the Baker–Campbell–Hausdorff (BCH) formula. We provide additional detail on the extension to Lindbladian generators.

Lemma A.11

(BCH formula for bounded superoperators)
.

Let
\mathcal{L}_{1},\mathcal{L}_{2}
be bounded linear maps on a Banach space. Then

\log(e^{\Delta t \cL_1} e^{\Delta t \cL_2})=\Delta t(\mathcal{L}_{1}+\mathcal{L}_{2})+\frac{(\Delta t)^{2}}{2}[\mathcal{L}_{1},\mathcal{L}_{2}]+\sum_{n=3}^{\infty}(\Delta t)^{n}Z_{n}(\mathcal{L}_{1},\mathcal{L}_{2}),

(56)

where
[\mathcal{L}_{1},\mathcal{L}_{2}]=\mathcal{L}_{1}\circ\mathcal{L}_{2}-\mathcal{L}_{2}\circ\mathcal{L}_{1}
and the remainder terms
Z_{n}
are homogeneous polynomials of degree
n
in
\mathcal{L}_{1},\mathcal{L}_{2}
with coefficients independent of the operators.

Proof.

The BCH formula holds in any Banach algebra. The space
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{H}))
of bounded linear maps on
\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{H})
is a Banach algebra under composition and the operator norm induced by the trace norm. The series converges absolutely for
\Delta t\norm{\cL_1}+\Delta t\norm{\cL_2}<\log 2
.
∎

Corollary A.12

(Trotter error for Lindbladians)
.

Under the hypotheses of Lemma
2.9
, the constant in the error bound satisfies

\sum_{j<k}\left\|[\mathcal{L}_{j},\mathcal{L}_{k}]\right\|_{\diamond}\leq 2\sum_{j<k}\norm{\cL_j}\cdot\norm{\cL_k}\cdot d,

(57)

where
d=\dim\mathcal{H}
and
\norm{\cdot}
is the operator norm induced by the trace norm.

Proof.

For any linear maps
A,B
on
\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{H})
, we have
\left\|[A,B]\right\|_{\diamond}\leq 2\norm{A}\cdot\norm{B}\cdot d
by the norm inequality
\left\|\cdot\right\|_{\diamond}\leq d\cdot\norm{\cdot}
and submultiplicativity of the operator norm. The factor of 2 accounts for the two terms in the commutator.
∎

Remark A.13

(Higher-order Trotter formulas)
.

While we use first-order Trotterization for simplicity, higher-order Suzuki formulas yield improved error scaling:

\left\|e^{\Delta t\mathcal{L}}-S_{2k}(\Delta t)\right\|_{\diamond}\leq C_{2k}(\Delta t)^{2k+1}\sum_{\text{nested commutators}}\left\|[\mathcal{L}_{i_{1}},[\mathcal{L}_{i_{2}},\cdots[\mathcal{L}_{i_{2k}},\mathcal{L}_{i_{2k+1}}]\cdots]]\right\|_{\diamond},

(58)

where
S_{2k}
is the
2k
-th order Suzuki product formula. For stiff generators, however, the commutator scaling
\left\|[\varepsilon^{-1}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{fast}},\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{slow}}]\right\|_{\diamond}=\varepsilon^{-1}\left\|[\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{fast}},\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{slow}}]\right\|_{\diamond}
still necessitates
\Delta t=\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon)
unless the AP framework is employed.

A.7
Primitive Maps and Exponential Convergence in Diamond Norm

The exponential convergence (
12
) is a cornerstone of our error analysis. We provide a self-contained proof adapted to the quantum setting.

Lemma A.14

(Exponential convergence for primitive CPTP maps)
.

Let
\Phi:\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{H})\to\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{H})
be a primitive CPTP map with unique steady state
\rho_{\star}
and spectral gap
\lambda_{\mathrm{gap}}>0
(i.e., all eigenvalues
\lambda\neq 1
satisfy
|\lambda|\leq e^{-\lambda_{\mathrm{gap}}}
). Then there exists
C>0
such that

\left\|\Phi^{N}-\mathcal{P}\right\|_{\diamond}\leq Ce^{-\lambda_{\mathrm{gap}}N},\qquad N\in\mathbb{N},

(59)

where
\mathcal{P}(X)=\tr[X]\rho_{\star}
.

Proof.

By primitivity,
\Phi
is diagonalizable over
\mathbb{C}
(or admits a Jordan decomposition with blocks of size 1 for the eigenvalue 1). Let
\{v_{i}\}
be a basis of generalized eigenvectors with
\Phi v_{i}=\lambda_{i}v_{i}
(or Jordan chains). Decompose any
X\in\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{H})
as
X=\tr[X]\rho_{\star}+\sum_{i\geq 2}c_{i}v_{i}
, where the sum excludes the steady state. Then

\Phi^{N}(X)-\mathcal{P}(X)=\sum_{i\geq 2}c_{i}\lambda_{i}^{N}v_{i}.

(60)

Taking the trace norm and using equivalence of norms on finite-dimensional spaces,

\norm{\Phi^N(X) - \cP(X)}_{1}\leq C^{\prime}\max_{i\geq 2}|\lambda_{i}|^{N}\cdot\norm{X}_{1}\leq C^{\prime}e^{-\lambda_{\mathrm{gap}}N}\norm{X}_{1}.

(61)

The diamond norm bound follows by tensoring with an ancilla and using stability: for any
n
and
X\in\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{H}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{n})
,

\norm{(\Phi^N \otimes\id_n)(X) - (\cP\otimes\id_n)(X)}_{1}\leq C^{\prime}e^{-\lambda_{\mathrm{gap}}N}\norm{X}_{1},

(62)

since
\Phi\otimes\mathbb{I}_{n}
inherits primitivity and the same spectral gap. Taking the supremum over
X
with
\norm{X}_{1}\leq 1
yields the result with
C=C^{\prime}
.
∎

Remark A.15

(Dependence of
C
on dimension)
.

The constant
C
in Lemma
A.14
typically scales polynomially with
d=\dim\mathcal{H}
, due to the condition number of the eigenbasis. For physically relevant Lindbladians (e.g., local dissipators),
C
can often be bounded independently of
d
via logarithmic Sobolev inequalities or hypercontractivity. Our resource bounds absorb such polynomial factors into the
\operatorname{poly}(d)
notation.

A.8
Summary of Error Propagation in Layered Protocols

We conclude by synthesizing the error analysis for the layered protocol
\Psi_{\Delta t}^{\varepsilon}=\Phi_{\mathrm{slow}}\circ(\Phi_{\mathrm{fast}})^{N}
.

Proposition A.16

(Composite error bound)
.

Under the hypotheses of Theorem
3.2
, the total error decomposes as

\displaystyle\left\|\Psi_{\Delta t}^{\varepsilon}-e^{\Delta t\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon}}\right\|_{\diamond}

\displaystyle\leq\underbrace{\left\|\Phi_{\mathrm{slow}}-e^{\Delta t\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{slow}}}\right\|_{\diamond}}_{\text{slow layer error}}

\displaystyle\quad+\underbrace{\left\|e^{\Delta t\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{slow}}}\right\|_{\diamond}\cdot\left\|(\Phi_{\mathrm{fast}})^{N}-e^{\Delta t\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{fast}}/\varepsilon}\right\|_{\diamond}}_{\text{fast layer error}}

\displaystyle\quad+\underbrace{\left\|e^{\Delta t\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{slow}}}\circ e^{\Delta t\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{fast}}/\varepsilon}-e^{\Delta t\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon}}\right\|_{\diamond}}_{\text{interaction error}}.

(63)

Each term is bounded as follows:

1.

Slow layer:
\mathcal{O}((\Delta t)^{s+1})
by assumption on
\Phi_{\mathrm{slow}}
.

2.

Fast layer:
\mathcal{O}(N\cdot(\varepsilon\tau_{\mathrm{fast}})^{r+1}+e^{-\lambda_{\mathrm{gap}}\Delta t/\varepsilon})=\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{r}\Delta t+e^{-\lambda_{\mathrm{gap}}\Delta t/\varepsilon})
.

3.

Interaction:
\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon\Delta t\cdot\left\|[\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{fast}},\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{slow}}]\right\|_{\diamond}/\lambda_{\mathrm{gap}})
via Duhamel expansion and centering condition.

For fixed
\Delta t>0
and
\varepsilon\to 0
, the exponential term vanishes, yielding the uniform bound
\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon\Delta t+(\Delta t)^{\min(r,s)+1})
.

Proof.

The decomposition (
63
) follows from the triangle inequality and submultiplicativity of the diamond norm. The slow layer bound is by assumption. For the fast layer, we use the telescoping sum

(\Phi_{\mathrm{fast}})^{N}-e^{N\varepsilon\tau_{\mathrm{fast}}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{fast}}}=\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}(\Phi_{\mathrm{fast}})^{N-1-k}\circ(\Phi_{\mathrm{fast}}-e^{\varepsilon\tau_{\mathrm{fast}}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{fast}}})\circ e^{k\varepsilon\tau_{\mathrm{fast}}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{fast}}},

(64)

and bound each term using
\left\|\Phi_{\mathrm{fast}}-e^{\varepsilon\tau_{\mathrm{fast}}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{fast}}}\right\|_{\diamond}=\mathcal{O}((\varepsilon\tau_{\mathrm{fast}})^{r+1})
and
\left\|e^{k\varepsilon\tau_{\mathrm{fast}}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{fast}}}\right\|_{\diamond}\leq 1
(CPTP maps are contractions in trace norm). The interaction error follows from Lemma
A.4
and the spectral gap estimate (
12
), with the centering condition ensuring cancellation of the leading
\mathcal{O}(\Delta t)
term.
∎

Remark A.17

(Uniformity in
\varepsilon
)
.

The key to asymptotic preservation is that the interaction error scales as
\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon\Delta t)
rather than
\mathcal{O}(\Delta t^{2}/\varepsilon)
, which would arise from naive Trotter analysis. This improvement is achieved by: (i) resolving the fast scale via analog evolution or sufficient substeps, (ii) exploiting exponential convergence to the slow manifold, and (iii) imposing the centering condition to eliminate secular terms. These ingredients collectively ensure that the error bound remains valid uniformly as
\varepsilon\to 0
.
