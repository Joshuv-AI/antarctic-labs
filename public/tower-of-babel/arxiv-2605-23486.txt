A high-order nodally bound-preserving and mass-conservative method for linear fourth-order elliptic problems and its applications to nonlinear parabolic equations
Jie Shen, Zuodong Wang

Abstract
We propose a high-order finite element method for linear fourth-order elliptic problems that is both nodally bound-preserving and mass-conservative, based on a variational inequality formulation. The method admits an equivalent strictly convex minimization structure, which ensures well-posedness and enables an optimal error estimate in the H 1 H^{1} -seminorm, under suitable regularity assumptions. This framework is further extended to nonlinear fourth-order parabolic problems through space–time high-order discretizations that combine variational inequalities, BDF schemes, and scalar auxiliary variable (SAV) techniques. The fully discrete schemes preserve nodal bounds and mass, and a modified energy stability result is established for the first-order temporal scheme. We also apply the same framework to nonlinear second-order parabolic problems by introducing a consistent fourth-order regularization, leading to space–time high-order schemes with the same bound-preserving and mass-conservative properties. Extensive numerical results, including challenging tests with singularities and low regularity, demonstrate the stability, efficiency, and high-order accuracy of the proposed methods. Keywords. Fourth-order problems; nonlinear parabolic equations; bound-preservation; mass-conservation; variational inequalities; finite element methods. MSC. 65M12, 65M60, 65N12

Linear fourth-order elliptic problems.

Nonlinear second-order parabolic problems.
We introduce a consistent fourth-order regularization strategy that allows second-order problems to be treated within the fourth-order parabolic framework, leading to schemes that preserve bounds and mass. A mass-conservative and bound-preserving scheme for linear fourth-order elliptic problems

In this section, we develop a high-order finite element discretization of (
1.1

2.1
Spatial discrete setting and numerical scheme

For the spatial discretization, we consider either a simplicial or a tensor-product conforming mesh
\{{\mathcal{T}}_{h}\}_{h>0}
of
D
, with mesh size parameter
h:=\max_{K\in{\mathcal{T}}_{h}}\text{diam}(K)
.
A generic mesh cell is denoted by
K\in{\mathcal{T}}_{h}
. We assume only that the mesh is shape-regular, rather than the
acute angle
assumption which guarantees the discrete maximum principle in the literature (see for example
[
5
]
). We focus on continuous Lagrange finite elements of order
p\geq 1
. Thus, each degree of freedom (dof)
i
corresponds to
the value at the mesh node
\bm{x}_{i}
for all
i\in{\mathcal{V}}:=\{1\hskip 0.50003pt{:}\hskip 0.50003ptI\}
, where
I
is the total number of mesh nodes. The global shape functions are
denoted by
\{\varphi_{i}\}_{i\in{\mathcal{V}}}
. The corresponding
H^{1}
-conforming FEM space is denoted by

V_{h}^{p}:=\text{span}\{\varphi_{i}\}_{i\in{\mathcal{V}}}.

(2.1)

We define
V_{h}^{p,+}\subset V_{h}^{p}
as the subset of bound-preserving functions (in the nodal sense) in
V_{h}^{p}
, i.e.,

V_{h}^{p,+}:=\Big\{v_{h}:=\sum_{i\in{\mathcal{V}}}V_{i}\varphi_{i}\in V_{h}^{p}\>|\>V_{i}\in[a,b],\ \forall i\in{\mathcal{V}}\Big\}.

(2.2)

We now construct a high-order, bound-preserving and mass-conservative numerical scheme for the problem (
1.1
). The key idea is to reformulate the PDE as a variational inequality, which naturally incorporates the bound constraint through a convex admissible set.
Following the argument in
[
23
, Chapter 3]
and
[
9
]
, the problem (
1.1
) with a sufficiently regular solution can be reformulated as finding
(u,w)\in H^{1}(D)\cap H^{2}(D;[a,b])\times H^{1}(D)
such that

\begin{cases}(u,v)+(M\nabla w,\nabla v)=(f_{1},v),\quad\forall v\in H^{1}(D),\\
(w,\xi-u)\leq(\kappa\nabla u,\nabla(\xi-u))+(f_{2},\xi-u),\quad\forall\xi\in H^{1}(D;[a,b]).\end{cases}

(2.3)

This inequality can be interpreted as enforcing the bound constraint via projection onto a convex admissible set, leading naturally to a variational inequality formulation.
We then discretize the continuous problem by replacing trial and test functions from
H^{1}(D)
and
H^{1}(D;[a,b])
to
V_{h}^{p}
and
V_{h}^{p,+}
, respectively, to get the numerical scheme: find
(u_{h},w_{h})\in V_{h}^{p,+}\times V_{h}^{p}
such that

\begin{cases}(u_{h},v_{h})+(M\nabla w_{h},\nabla v_{h})=(f_{1},v_{h}),\quad\forall v_{h}\in V_{h}^{p},\\
(w_{h},\xi_{h}-u_{h})\leq(\kappa\nabla u_{h},\nabla(\xi_{h}-u_{h}))+(f_{2},\xi_{h}-u_{h}),\quad\forall\xi_{h}\in V_{h}^{p,+}.\end{cases}

(2.4)

2.2
Fundamental properties

In this section, we establish the well-posedness, bound-preservation and mass-conservation of the proposed scheme (
2.4
), by considering its associated minimization problem posed in the
H^{-1}
-
H^{1}
-norms.

We start with notation which will be frequently used throughout this and the next section.
The weighted Poisson solver
(-\Delta^{M})^{-1}:L^{2}_{0}(D)\to H^{1}(D)
,
g\mapsto z:=z(g)
is given by

(M\nabla z,\nabla v)=(g,v),\qquad\int_{D}z=0,\qquad\forall v\in H^{1}(D).

(2.5)

Based on this operator, we have an integration by parts property in the
H^{-1}
-inner product. We denote
z_{v}:=(-\Delta^{M})^{-1}v
and
z_{w}:=(-\Delta^{M})^{-1}w
as the solution of (
2.5
) with source
v\in L^{2}_{0}(D)
and
w\in L^{2}_{0}(D)
, respectively. Then, the following identity holds true:

\displaystyle\quad(w,(-\Delta^{M})^{-1}v)=(M\nabla z_{w},\nabla(-\Delta^{M})^{-1}v)=(M\nabla(-\Delta^{M})^{-1}v,\nabla(-\Delta^{M})^{-1}w)

\displaystyle=(M\nabla z_{v},\nabla(-\Delta^{M})^{-1}w)=(v,(-\Delta^{M})^{-1}w).

(2.6)

Following the argument in the continuous setting above, the weighted discrete Poisson solver
(-\Delta_{h}^{M})^{-1}:L^{2}_{0}(D)\to V_{h}^{p}
,
g\mapsto z_{h}
is defined by

(M\nabla z_{h},\nabla v_{h})=(g,v_{h}),\qquad\int_{D}z_{h}=0,\qquad\forall v_{h}\in V_{h}^{p}.

(2.7)

We also have an integration by parts property in the discrete
H^{-1}
-inner product: By denoting
z_{h,v}:=(-\Delta_{h}^{M})^{-1}v
and
z_{h,w}:=(-\Delta_{h}^{M})^{-1}w
as the solution of (

) with source
v\in L^{2}_{0}(D)
and

\displaystyle(w,(-\Delta_{h}^{M})^{-1}v)=(M\nabla(-\Delta_{h}^{M})^{-1}v,\nabla(-\Delta_{h}^{M})^{-1}w)=(v,(-\Delta_{h}^{M})^{-1}w).

In addition, we introduce the weighted discrete

-seminorm for all

\lVert v\rVert_{H^{-1}_{h}}^{2}:=(M\nabla(-\Delta_{h}^{M})^{-1}v,\nabla(-\Delta_{h}^{M})^{-1}v).

(2.9)

We are now in a position to prove the well-posedness, mass-conservation and bound-preservation for the proposed scheme (

Lemma 2.1

(Well-posedness for the linear elliptic scheme (
2.4
))
.

The variational inequality (
2.4
) admits a solution
(u_{h},w_{h})
. The component

u_{h}
is unique, bound-preserving, and mass-conservative. Moreover,
u_{h}
is the unique minimizer of the following problem:

\min_{v\in Y}\Big\{\frac{1}{2}\lVert\sqrt{\kappa}\nabla v\rVert^{2}+(f_{2},v)+\frac{1}{2}\lVert v-f_{1}\rVert_{H^{-1}_{h}}^{2}\Big\},

(2.10)

where

Y=\{v\in V_{h}^{p,+}\ |\ \int_{D}v=\int_{D}f_{1}\}.

(2.11)

In addition, the solution
u_{h}
satisfies the following variational inequality for all
\xi_{h}\in Y
:

((-\Delta_{h}^{M})^{-1}(u_{h}-f_{1}),\xi_{h}-u_{h})+(\kappa\nabla u_{h},\nabla(\xi_{h}-u_{h}))+(f_{2},\xi_{h}-u_{h})\geq 0.

(2.12)

Remark 2.2

(
H^{-1}
-
H^{1}
pair)
.

The appearance of the discrete
H^{-1}
and
H^{1}
inner products in (
2.12
) and (
2.10
) allows the fourth-order problem to be reformulated as a strictly convex minimization problem without

. This reformulation avoids terms containing

in the later error analysis in Theorem

.

Proof.

We first establish the well-posedness of the strictly convex minimization problem (
2.10
). We then show that its associated KKT system is equivalent to the variational inequality (
2.4
), which yields the well-posedness of (
2.4
). Finally, we derive (
2.12
) as the Euler inequality associated with (
2.10
).

Step 1: well-posedness of the minimization problem.

We show first that the admissible set
Y
is nonempty, convex and closed.

Nonemptiness.
Since the mean value of
f_{1}
is well-defined and assumed to lie in
[a,b]
, the constant function
\frac{1}{|D|}\int_{D}f_{1}
belongs to
V_{h}^{p,+}
and satisfies the mass constraint, implying that
Y
is nonempty.

Convexity.
For any
\theta\in[0,1]
, and any pairs
v_{1}
and
v_{2}
in
Y
, we have
\theta v_{1}+(1-\theta)v_{2}\in V_{h}^{p,+}
and
\int_{D}\theta v_{1}+(1-\theta)v_{2}=\int_{D}f_{1}
. Hence all convex combinations of
v_{1}
and
v_{2}
are also in
Y
, which implies that
Y
is convex.

Closedness.
Since
V_{h}^{p}
is finite-dimensional, and constraints
V_{i}\in[a,b]
are closed in
{\mathbb{R}}^{I}
,
V_{h}^{p,+}
is closed.

Therefore, the minimization problem (
2.10
) is a strictly convex functional subject to a finite dimensional, nonempty, convex and closed feasible set. This implies existence and uniqueness of the minimizer for (
2.10
).

Step 2: well-posedness of the variational inequality (
2.4
).

Finally, we follow the argument in
[
10
]
to prove that (
2.4
) admits a unique solution.
The minimization problem (
2.10
) can be reformulated as the following minimization problem:

\displaystyle\min_{u_{h},v_{h}\in V_{h}^{p}}\Big\{\frac{1}{2}\lVert\sqrt{\kappa}\nabla u_{h}\rVert^{2}+(f_{2},u_{h})+\frac{1}{2}(M\nabla v_{h},\nabla v_{h})\Big\}

\displaystyle s.t.

\displaystyle\qquad(u_{h}-f_{1},\chi_{h})+(M\nabla v_{h},\nabla\chi_{h})=0,\quad\forall\chi\in V_{h}^{p},\quad\int_{D}v_{h}=0,

\displaystyle\qquad a\leq U_{i}\leq b,\quad\forall i\in{\mathcal{V}},\qquad u_{h}=\sum_{i\in{\mathcal{V}}}U_{i}\varphi_{i}.

The corresponding Lagrangian is given by

\frac{1}{2}\lVert\sqrt{\kappa}\nabla u_{h}\rVert^{2}+(f_{2},u_{h})+\frac{1}{2}(M\nabla v_{h},\nabla v_{h})-\Big((M\nabla v_{h},\nabla w_{h})+(u_{h}-f_{1},w_{h})\Big)-\kappa_{1}\int_{D}v_{h}-\langle\mu_{+},b-u_{h}\rangle-\langle\mu_{-},u_{h}-a\rangle,

where we introduced the Lagrange multipliers
w_{h}\in V_{h}^{p}
for the first FEM constraint,
\kappa_{1}\in{\mathbb{R}}
for the mean-free constraint of
v_{h}
, and
\mu_{+}=\sum_{i\in{\mathcal{V}}}\mu_{+,i}\varphi_{i}\in V_{h}^{p}
,
\mu_{-}=\sum_{i\in{\mathcal{V}}}\mu_{-,i}\varphi_{i}\in V_{h}^{p}
for the bound-preserving constraints on
u_{h}
, respectively. The inner product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
is defined as the Euclidean inner product for dofs.
The associated KKT-system is given by

\displaystyle(M\nabla(w_{h}-v_{h}),\nabla\chi_{h})=(\kappa_{1},\chi_{h}),\hskip 9.24994pt\forall\chi_{h}\in V_{h}^{p},\hskip 9.24994pt\int_{D}v_{h}=0,

\displaystyle(u_{h}-f_{1},\chi_{h})+(M\nabla v_{h},\nabla\chi_{h})=0,\hskip 9.24994pt\forall\chi_{h}\in V_{h}^{p},

\displaystyle(w_{h},\chi_{h})=(\kappa\nabla u_{h},\nabla\chi_{h})+(f_{2},\chi_{h})+\langle\mu_{+}-\mu_{-},\chi_{h}\rangle,\hskip 9.24994pt\forall\chi_{h}\in V_{h}^{p},

\displaystyle\mu_{+,i},\mu_{-,i}\geq 0,\mu_{+,i}(U_{i}-b)=0,\mu_{-,i}(U_{i}-a)=0,\hskip 9.24994pt\forall i\in{\mathcal{V}}.

Additionally, taking
\chi_{h}=1
in the first equation, we obtain
\kappa_{1}=0
. Thus this identity with the first and the second equations in the above KKT system implies that
v_{h}
and
w_{h}
only differ by a constant. Therefore, replacing
\nabla v_{h}
by
\nabla w_{h}
, we infer a simplified KKT-system

\displaystyle(u_{h}-f_{1},\chi_{h})+(M\nabla w_{h},\nabla\chi_{h})=0,\hskip 9.24994pt\forall\chi_{h}\in V_{h}^{p},

\displaystyle(w_{h},\chi_{h})=(\kappa\nabla u_{h},\nabla\chi_{h})+(f_{2},\chi_{h})+\langle\mu_{+}-\mu_{-},\chi_{h}\rangle,\hskip 9.24994pt\forall\chi_{h}\in V_{h}^{p},

\displaystyle\mu_{+,i},\mu_{-,i}\geq 0,\mu_{+,i}(U_{i}-b)=0,\mu_{-,i}(U_{i}-a)=0,\hskip 9.24994pt\forall i\in{\mathcal{V}}.

We now show that the second and the third line in the simplified KKT-system is equivalent to

(w_{h},\xi_{h}-u_{h})\leq(\kappa\nabla u_{h},\nabla\xi_{h}-u_{h})+(f_{2},\xi_{h}-u_{h})\hskip 9.24994pt\forall\xi_{h}\in V_{h}^{p,+}.

We prove first that the second and the third line in the simplified KKT-system implies the variational inequality above.
Indeed, for all
\xi_{h}:=\sum_{i\in{\mathcal{V}}}\xi_{i}\varphi_{i}\in V_{h}^{p,+}
, we have

(\mu_{+,i}-\mu_{-,i})(\xi_{i}-U_{i})\leq\mu_{+,i}(b-U_{i})-\mu_{-,i}(a-U_{i})=0,\hskip 18.49988pt\forall i\in{\mathcal{V}},

which implies
\langle\mu_{+}-\mu_{-},\xi_{h}-u_{h}\rangle\leq 0
. Setting
\chi_{h}=\xi_{h}-u_{h}
in the second line of the simplified KKT-system and using
\langle\mu_{+}-\mu_{-},\xi_{h}-u_{h}\rangle\leq 0
, we obtain the expected implication.
To establish the converse implication, we construct Lagrange multipliers
\mu_{+}
and
\mu_{-}
at the level of dofs. For each
i\in{\mathcal{V}}
, we consider admissible perturbations of the form
\xi_{h}=u_{h}+\delta\varphi_{i}\in V_{h}^{p,+}
, where the sign of
\delta
is restricted by the bound-preserving constraints and
|\delta|
is sufficiently small. By testing the variational inequality with such perturbations, we obtain the following dofwise definition:

\begin{cases}\mu_{+,i}=0,\hskip 9.24994pt\mu_{-,i}=(\kappa\nabla u_{h},\nabla\phi_{i})+(f_{2},\phi_{i})-(w_{h},\phi_{i}),&\text{if }U_{i}=a,\\[3.6999pt]
\mu_{-,i}=0,\hskip 9.24994pt\mu_{+,i}=(w_{h},\phi_{i})-(\kappa\nabla u_{h},\nabla\phi_{i})-(f_{2},\phi_{i}),&\text{if }U_{i}=b,\\[3.6999pt]
\mu_{+,i}=\mu_{-,i}=0,&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

This definition satisfies the complementarity conditions

\mu_{+,i},\mu_{-,i}\geq 0,\hskip 9.24994pt\mu_{+,i}(U_{i}-b)=0,\hskip 9.24994pt\mu_{-,i}(U_{i}-a)=0,

together with

(w_{h},\varphi_{i})=(\kappa\nabla u_{h},\nabla\varphi_{i})+(f_{2},\varphi_{i})+(\mu_{+,i}-\mu_{-,i}).

Summing over all dofs yields the expected representation

(w_{h},\chi_{h})=(\kappa\nabla u_{h},\nabla\chi_{h})+(f_{2},\chi_{h})+\langle\mu_{+}-\mu_{-},\chi_{h}\rangle,\hskip 9.24994pt\forall\chi_{h}\in V_{h}^{p},

and hence the equivalence between the variational inequality and the simplified KKT system. Therefore, the existence and uniqueness of the minimization problem leads to the well-posedness of (
2.4
).

We apply (
2.8
) to reformulate the minimization problem (

) as

\min_{v\in Y}{\mathcal{J}}(v),\hskip 18.49988pt{\mathcal{J}}(v)=\frac{1}{2}\lVert\sqrt{\kappa}\nabla v\rVert^{2}+(f_{2},v)+\frac{1}{2}((-\Delta_{h}^{M})^{-1}(v-f_{1}),v-f_{1}).

Its minimizer

satisfies the first-order optimality condition

(\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}v}{\mathcal{J}}(u_{h}),\xi_{h}-u_{h})\geq 0,\hskip 18.49988pt\forall\xi_{h}\in Y.

We now explore the explicit formula of
\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}v}{\mathcal{J}}(u_{h})
in all admissible directions. In a neighborhood of the minimizer
u_{h}\in Y
, we take any perturbation
u_{h}+\delta v_{h}\in Y
, with
\delta\in{\mathbb{R}}\setminus\{0\}
and
v_{h}\in V_{h}^{p}
. Since
\int_{D}u_{h}=\int_{D}u_{h}+\delta v_{h}=\int_{D}f_{1}
, we must have
\int_{D}v_{h}=0
. Therefore, this perturbation with (
2.8
) implies

\displaystyle{\mathcal{J}}(u_{h}+\delta v_{h})={\mathcal{J}}(u_{h})+\delta\Big(((-\Delta_{h}^{M})^{-1}(u_{h}-f_{1})+f_{2},v_{h})+(\kappa\nabla u_{h},\nabla v_{h})\Big)+O(\delta^{2}).

Since the perturbation is arbitrary, we find from the above calculation the expected variational inequality (
2.12
).
This concludes the proof.
∎

Remark 2.3

(Stampacchia’s theorem)
.

For the second-order elliptic variational inequalities, a standard way to establish the well-posedness of the variational inequality is to apply Stampacchia’s Theorem, as in
[
7
]
. However, due to the lack of coercivity of the associated operator, Stampacchia’s theorem cannot be directly applied in the present setting, and we bypass this problem by using the minimization problem (
2.10
).

2.3
Error estimate

In this section, we derive an optimal error estimate in the
H^{1}
-seminorm, for sufficiently smooth solutions.

In order to derive an optimal error bound of the proposed scheme, we require a mass-conservative and bound-preserving variant of the quasi-interpolation used in
[
19
]
.

Lemma 2.4

(A bound-preserving and mass-conservative map)
.

Let
v\in{\mathcal{C}}^{0}(\bar{D};[a,b])
. Assume that
h<h_{0}(v)
with
h_{0}(v)
a constant depending on
v
. Then, there is a bound-preserving and mass-conservative
\Pi_{h}v\in V_{h}^{p,+}
, satisfying the following estimate

\lVert\Pi_{h}v-v\rVert_{L^{\infty}}\leq C\inf_{\chi_{h}\in V_{h}^{p}}\lVert\chi_{h}-v\rVert_{L^{\infty}}.

(2.13)

In addition, for
v\in W^{p,\infty}(D)\cap H^{p+1}(D)
, we have

\lVert\nabla(\Pi_{h}v-v)\rVert\leq Ch^{p}.

(2.14)

Remark 2.5

(Regularity assumption on
v
)
.

In order to obtain the optimal convergence rate in the
H^{1}
-seminorm, we assumed
v\in W^{p,\infty}(D)
to control the error introduced by the mass-conservative and bound-preserving correction on the quasi-interpolation of
v
;
v\in H^{p+1}(D)
is used to control the standard
H^{1}
-approximation errors of
v
.

Proof.

We assume that
v
is not a constant, since the constant case is trivial.
We first consider the case
a=-b
, and then generalize it to the case
a<b
with
a\neq-b
.

Case
a=-b
.

We consider the quasi-interpolation
{\mathcal{I}}_{h}v
introduced in
[
19
]
, which is
L^{\infty}
- and
H^{1}
-stable, and satisfies the corresponding optimal convergence properties under our regularity assumption (see, for example,
[
20
, Theorem 22.6 and Corollary 22.8]
).
Then, we construct the map

\tilde{v}_{h}:={\mathcal{I}}_{h}v+c_{1}:=\sum_{i\in{\mathcal{V}}}\tilde{V}_{i}\varphi_{i},\qquad c_{1}:=\frac{1}{|D|}\int_{D}v-{\mathcal{I}}_{h}v.

It follows that
\tilde{v}_{h}
is mass-conservative and

|c_{1}|\leq\lVert{\mathcal{I}}_{h}v-v\rVert_{L^{\infty}}\leq C\inf_{\chi_{h}\in V_{h}^{p}}\lVert\chi_{h}-v\rVert_{L^{\infty}}.

(2.15)

Since
\lVert\tilde{v}_{h}-v\rVert_{L^{\infty}}\leq\lVert{\mathcal{I}}_{h}v-v\rVert_{L^{\infty}}+|c_{1}|\leq C\lVert{\mathcal{I}}_{h}v-v\rVert_{L^{\infty}}
and
\lVert\nabla(\tilde{v}_{h}-v)\rVert=\lVert\nabla({\mathcal{I}}_{h}v-v)\rVert
,
\tilde{v}_{h}
inherits the
L^{\infty}
- and
H^{1}
-approximation properties of
{\mathcal{I}}_{h}v
.

In the case
\lVert v\rVert_{L^{\infty}}<b
, we have

\lVert\tilde{v}_{h}\rVert_{L^{\infty}}\leq\lVert\tilde{v}_{h}-v\rVert_{L^{\infty}}+\lVert v\rVert_{L^{\infty}}\leq\lVert{\mathcal{I}}_{h}v-v\rVert_{L^{\infty}}+|c_{1}|+\lVert v\rVert_{L^{\infty}}\leq C\inf_{\chi_{h}\in V_{h}^{p}}\lVert\chi_{h}-v\rVert_{L^{\infty}}+\lVert v\rVert_{L^{\infty}}.

Therefore, there exists a constant
h_{0}(v)
such that for all
h<h_{0}(v)
, we have
\lVert\tilde{v}_{h}\rVert_{L^{\infty}}<b
. Hence it is sufficient to set
\Pi_{h}v=\tilde{v}_{h}
in the case
\lVert v\rVert_{L^{\infty}}<b
.

In the case
\lVert v\rVert_{L^{\infty}}=b
, owing to the mass shifting
c_{1}
,
\tilde{v}_{h}
is no longer nodally bound-preserving, hence we need one more step to correct it.
If
\tilde{V}_{i}\in[a,b]
for all
i\in{\mathcal{V}}
, we set
\Pi_{h}v:=\tilde{v}_{h}
. Otherwise, we denote
m=\frac{1}{|D|}\int_{D}v
as the average of
v
, and
c_{2}\in{\mathbb{R}}
a constant to be determined later. We construct
\Pi_{h}v
by

\Pi_{h}v=(1-c_{2})\tilde{v}_{h}+c_{2}m.

By the construction,
\int_{D}\Pi_{h}v-v=0
. Now, in order to make it bound-preserving at every nodal point, we need to carefully choose
c_{2}
such that

\lVert\Pi_{h}v\rVert_{L^{\infty}}\leq(1-c_{2})\lVert\tilde{v}_{h}\rVert_{L^{\infty}}+c_{2}|m|\leq b.

This can be achieved by choosing
c_{2}\in(0,1)
and
c_{2}\geq\frac{\lVert\tilde{v}_{h}\rVert_{L^{\infty}}-b}{\lVert\tilde{v}_{h}\rVert_{L^{\infty}}-|m|}
. Hence we set
c_{2}=\frac{\lVert\tilde{v}_{h}\rVert_{L^{\infty}}-b}{\lVert\tilde{v}_{h}\rVert_{L^{\infty}}-|m|}
. We claim that such
c_{2}
is well defined and takes value in
(0,1)
for sufficiently small
h
.
Indeed, we have that
\lVert v\rVert_{L^{\infty}}=b>|m|
(the case
b=|m|
implies
|v|=b
, which is already covered by the constant case). Noticing that
\lVert\tilde{v}_{h}\rVert_{L^{\infty}}\to\lVert v\rVert_{L^{\infty}}=b>|m|
as
h\to 0
, there exists a constant
h_{0}^{\prime}
depending on
v
such that for all
h<h_{0}^{\prime}
,
\lVert\tilde{v}_{h}\rVert_{L^{\infty}}-|m|\geq\frac{b-|m|}{2}
. Hence the
L^{\infty}
-stability and approximation properties of the quasi-interpolation implies that for all
h<h_{0}^{\prime}
,

|c_{2}|\leq C\Big|\lVert\tilde{v}_{h}\rVert_{L^{\infty}}-b\Big|\leq C\Big|\lVert\tilde{v}_{h}\rVert_{L^{\infty}}-\lVert v\rVert_{L^{\infty}}\Big|\leq C\lVert\tilde{v}_{h}-v\rVert_{L^{\infty}}\leq C\lVert{\mathcal{I}}_{h}v-v\rVert_{L^{\infty}}\leq C\inf_{\chi_{h}\in V_{h}^{p}}\lVert\chi_{h}-v\rVert_{L^{\infty}}.

(2.16)

Therefore, there exists another constant
h_{0}\leq h_{0}^{\prime}
such that
c_{2}\in(0,1)
for all
h<h_{0}
.
This implies that the nodal values satisfy
(\Pi_{h}v)(\bm{x}_{i})\in[a,b]
for all
i\in{\mathcal{V}}
, and hence
\Pi_{h}v\in V_{h}^{p,+}
.

Now, we apply the
L^{\infty}
-stability and approximation properties of the quasi-interpolation, (
2.15
) and (
2.16
) to find

\displaystyle\lVert v-\Pi_{h}v\rVert_{L^{\infty}}

\displaystyle\leq\lVert v-{\mathcal{I}}_{h}v\rVert_{L^{\infty}}+\lVert{\mathcal{I}}_{h}v-\Pi_{h}v\rVert_{L^{\infty}}\leq\lVert v-{\mathcal{I}}_{h}v\rVert_{L^{\infty}}+\lVert{\mathcal{I}}_{h}v-\tilde{v}_{h}\rVert_{L^{\infty}}+\lVert\tilde{v}_{h}-\Pi_{h}v\rVert_{L^{\infty}}

\displaystyle\leq\lVert v-{\mathcal{I}}_{h}v\rVert_{L^{\infty}}+|c_{1}|+|c_{2}|(\lVert\tilde{v}_{h}\rVert_{L^{\infty}}+|m|)\leq C\lVert v-{\mathcal{I}}_{h}v\rVert_{L^{\infty}}\leq C\inf_{\chi_{h}\in V_{h}^{p}}\lVert\chi_{h}-v\rVert_{L^{\infty}},

which is the expected convergence property in the
L^{\infty}
-norm.

For the
H^{1}
-seminorm, we use the
H^{1}
-stability, the optimal approximation property of the quasi-interpolation in the
H^{1}
-seminorm, the regularity assumption on
v
and (
2.16
) to find

\displaystyle\lVert\nabla(\Pi_{h}v-v)\rVert

\displaystyle\leq\lVert\nabla(\Pi_{h}v-\tilde{v}_{h})\rVert+\lVert\nabla(\tilde{v}_{h}-v)\rVert\leq|c_{2}|\lVert\nabla\tilde{v}_{h}\rVert+\lVert\nabla(\tilde{v}_{h}-v)\rVert

\displaystyle=|c_{2}|\lVert\nabla{\mathcal{I}}_{h}v\rVert+\lVert\nabla({\mathcal{I}}_{h}v-v)\rVert\leq C|c_{2}|\lVert\nabla v\rVert+\lVert\nabla({\mathcal{I}}_{h}v-v)\rVert\leq Ch^{p}.

General case
a<b
with
a\neq-b
.

The same construction applies for
\bar{v}=v-\frac{a+b}{2}
with lower and upper bound
-\bar{a}=\bar{b}=\frac{b-a}{2}
, which leads to the optimal construction
\Pi_{h}\bar{v}
. The proof is completed by setting
\Pi_{h}v:=\Pi_{h}\bar{v}+\frac{a+b}{2}
.
∎

We recall that, owing to the Poincaré inequality and that
M
is uniformly bounded from below and above, the following PDE stability estimate holds true with
g\in L^{2}_{0}(D)
:

\lVert(-\Delta^{M})^{-1}g\rVert\leq C\lVert\nabla(-\Delta^{M})^{-1}g\rVert\leq C\lVert g\rVert.

(2.17)

Similarly, owing to
V_{h}^{p}\subset H^{1}(D)
, the following stability estimate holds true for all
g\in L^{2}_{0}(D)
:

\lVert(-\Delta_{h}^{M})^{-1}g\rVert\leq C\lVert g\rVert.

(2.18)

Now, we are ready to prove that
u_{h}
in (
2.4
) has the optimal convergence rate in the
H^{1}
-seminorm by using the variational inequality (
2.12
) and the bound-preserving and mass-conservative map in Lemma
2.4
.

Theorem 2.6

(Error estimate)
.

Assume that the domain
D
has a sufficiently smooth boundary, data are sufficiently regular such that
u\in{\mathcal{C}}^{0}(\bar{D};[a,b])\cap W^{p,\infty}(D)\cap H^{p+1}(D)
and
w\in H^{p+1}(D)
. Then, for
h<h_{0}(u)
with
h_{0}(u)
determined in Lemma
2.4
, we have the following estimate for the numerical solution
u_{h}
delivered by (
2.4
):

\lVert\nabla(u-u_{h})\rVert\leq Ch^{p}.

(2.19)

Remark 2.7

(Assumptions in error analysis)
.

The regularity assumptions on
u,w\in H^{p+1}(D)
are used to derive an optimal error bound in the
H^{1}
-seminorm, whereas
u\in{\mathcal{C}}^{0}(\bar{D};[a,b])\cap W^{p,\infty}(D)
and
h<h_{0}(u)
is used to construct a bound-preserving and mass-conservative element in
Y
with optimal
H^{1}
-convergence rate.

Proof.

The proof follows a standard approach for variational inequalities, combining a suitable projection with stability and consistency estimates.
The whole proof is divided into four steps. In the first step, we introduce the necessary notation and properties of
u
in (
1.1
) and
u_{h}
in (
2.4
), and introduce the error decomposition. In the second step, we control the consistency error. In the third step, we use the stability of the numerical scheme to derive the bounds on the rest part of the error decomposition. In the final step, we combine the estimates in the previous steps to conclude the proof. We recall the
M
and
\kappa
are assumed to be uniformly bounded from below and above, hence there exists constants
M_{1},M_{2},\kappa_{1},\kappa_{2}
such that

0<M_{1}\leq M\leq M_{2}<\infty,\qquad 0<\kappa_{1}\leq\kappa\leq\kappa_{2}<\infty.

These constants will be hidden into generic constant
C
in this proof.

Step 1.

Following the argument in
[
10
]
, for sufficiently regular solutions, (
1.1
) can be reformulated as

((-\Delta^{M})^{-1}(u-f_{1}),v)+(\kappa\nabla u,\nabla v)+(f_{2},v)=0,\quad\forall v\in H^{1}(D),

(2.20)

with the weighted Poisson solver
(-\Delta^{M})^{-1}
defined in (
2.5
). Indeed, from the first equation in (
1.1
), we obtain
(-\Delta^{M})^{-1}(u-f_{1})=-w
up to an additive constant. Then, the second equation in (
1.1
) with
\int_{D}f_{2}=0
implies
\int_{D}w=0
. Hence we conclude that
(-\Delta^{M})^{-1}(u-f_{1})=-w
. Therefore, inserting
(-\Delta^{M})^{-1}(u-f_{1})=-w
in the second line of (
1.1
), we obtain (
2.20
).

The numerical solution
u_{h}\in V_{h}^{p,+}
of (
2.4
) satisfies the variational inequality (
2.12
):

((-\Delta_{h}^{M})^{-1}(u_{h}-f_{1}),\xi_{h}-u_{h})+(\kappa\nabla u_{h},\nabla(\xi_{h}-u_{h}))+(f_{2},\xi_{h}-u_{h})\geq 0,\quad\forall\xi_{h}\in Y,

with
Y=\{v_{h}\in V_{h}^{p,+}\ |\ \int_{D}v_{h}=\int_{D}f_{1}\}
.
By choosing a function
\Pi_{h}u\in Y
(since
\int_{D}u=\int_{D}f_{1}
) approximating
u
as in Lemma
2.4
, we decompose the error as

e:=u_{h}-u=(u_{h}-\Pi_{h}u)+(\Pi_{h}u-u):=\eta+\zeta.

(2.21)

In addition, we introduce a parameter
\epsilon>0
, to be specified later.

Step 2.

We now estimate the consistency error. The weak form of the PDE (
2.20
) yields

((-\Delta_{h}^{M})^{-1}(\Pi_{h}u-f_{1}),v_{h})+(\kappa\nabla\Pi_{h}u,\nabla v_{h})+(f_{2},v_{h})=-({\mathcal{R}},v_{h}),\quad\forall v_{h}\in V_{h}^{p},

(2.22)

where the consistency term is defined by

\displaystyle({\mathcal{R}},v_{h})

\displaystyle=\Big([(-\Delta^{M})^{-1}-(-\Delta_{h}^{M})^{-1}](u-f_{1}),v_{h}\Big)+\Big((-\Delta_{h}^{M})^{-1}(u-\Pi_{h}u),v_{h}\Big)+\Big(\kappa\nabla(u-\Pi_{h}u),\nabla v_{h}\Big)

\displaystyle:=({\mathcal{R}}_{1},v_{h})+({\mathcal{R}}_{2},v_{h})+({\mathcal{R}}_{3},v_{h}),

(2.23)

where
{\mathcal{R}}_{i}
,
i=1,...,3
are defined in the natural way.

We now estimate
{\mathcal{R}}_{i}
,
i=1,...,3
one by one.
For
{\mathcal{R}}_{1}
, we set
z_{h}:=(-\Delta_{h}^{M})^{-1}(f_{1}-u)
and
z:=(-\Delta^{M})^{-1}(f_{1}-u)=w
, as in (
2.7
) and (
2.5
), respectively, to get

({\mathcal{R}}_{1},v_{h})=(z-z_{h},v_{h})=(z-z_{h},v_{h}-\frac{1}{|D|}\int_{D}v_{h}),

where we used that
z-z_{h}
has zero mean and is therefore orthogonal to constants. Then, we use the Poincaré inequality, the optimal approximation properties of the elliptic projection (
2.7
) with assumed regularity on
M
(see, for example
[
32
]
), the Cauchy-Schwarz and Young’s inequalities to infer

\displaystyle(z-z_{h},v_{h}-\frac{1}{|D|}\int_{D}v_{h})

\displaystyle\leq C\lVert\nabla(z-z_{h})\rVert\lVert\nabla v_{h}\rVert\leq C\inf_{\chi_{h}\in V_{h}^{p}}\lVert\nabla(\chi_{h}-z)\rVert\lVert\nabla v_{h}\rVert

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C}{\epsilon}\inf_{\chi_{h}\in V_{h}^{p}}\lVert\nabla(\chi_{h}-z)\rVert^{2}+\frac{\epsilon}{3}\lVert\nabla v_{h}\rVert^{2}.

Moreover, invoking the regularity assumption in
w=z\in H^{p+1}(D)
, the approximation theory leads to

|({\mathcal{R}}_{1},v_{h})|\leq\frac{C}{\epsilon}h^{2p}+\frac{\epsilon}{3}\lVert\nabla v_{h}\rVert^{2}.

For
{\mathcal{R}}_{2}
, we recall that
(-\Delta_{h}^{M})^{-1}
maps mean-free functions to mean-free functions, use the stability (
2.18
), and apply the Poincaré, the Cauchy-Schwarz and Young’s inequalities to get

\displaystyle((-\Delta_{h}^{M})^{-1}(u-\Pi_{h}u),v_{h})

\displaystyle=((-\Delta_{h}^{M})^{-1}(u-\Pi_{h}u),v_{h}-\frac{1}{|D|}\int_{D}v_{h})

\displaystyle\leq C\lVert\nabla(u-\Pi_{h}u)\rVert\lVert\nabla v_{h}\rVert\leq\frac{C}{\epsilon}\lVert\nabla(u-\Pi_{h}u)\rVert^{2}+\frac{\epsilon}{3}\lVert\nabla v_{h}\rVert^{2}.

Then, we use the approximation properties of
\Pi_{h}u
in Lemma
2.4
and the regularity assumption on
u
to find

|({\mathcal{R}}_{2},v_{h})|\leq\frac{C}{\epsilon}h^{2p}+\frac{\epsilon}{3}\lVert\nabla v_{h}\rVert^{2}.

For
{\mathcal{R}}_{3}
, we apply the Cauchy-Schwarz and Young’s inequalities to find

(\kappa\nabla(u-\Pi_{h}u),\nabla v_{h})\leq\frac{C}{\epsilon}\lVert\nabla(u-\Pi_{h}u)\rVert^{2}+\frac{\epsilon}{3}\lVert\nabla v_{h}\rVert^{2}.

Hence the PDE regularity assumption and the approximation properties of
\Pi_{h}u
implies

|({\mathcal{R}}_{3},v_{h})|\leq\frac{C}{\epsilon}h^{2p}+\frac{\epsilon}{3}\lVert\nabla v_{h}\rVert^{2}.

Therefore, collecting the above estimates on
{\mathcal{R}}_{i}
,
i=1,2,3
, it follows

|({\mathcal{R}},v_{h})|\leq\frac{C}{\epsilon}h^{2p}+\epsilon\lVert\nabla v_{h}\rVert^{2}.

(2.24)

Step 3.

We now estimate
\eta
in the error decomposition (
2.21
) .
We subtract (
2.22
) (with
v_{h}=\xi_{h}-u_{h}\in V_{h}^{p}
) from (
2.12
) and obtain

\displaystyle((-\Delta_{h}^{M})^{-1}\eta,\xi_{h}-u_{h})+(\kappa\nabla\eta,\nabla(\xi_{h}-u_{h}))\geq({\mathcal{R}},\xi_{h}-u_{h}).

Picking
\xi_{h}=\Pi_{h}u\in Y
, invoking (
2.24
), the coercivity of operator
-\nabla{\cdot}(\kappa\nabla)
, and using the integration by parts property of the
H_{h}^{-1}
-norm in (
2.8
), we have

\displaystyle\lVert\eta\rVert_{H^{-1}_{h}}^{2}+C_{\kappa}\lVert\nabla\eta\rVert^{2}\leq\lVert\eta\rVert_{H^{-1}_{h}}^{2}+\lVert\sqrt{\kappa}\nabla\eta\rVert^{2}\leq({\mathcal{R}},\eta)\leq\frac{C}{\epsilon}h^{2p}+\epsilon\lVert\nabla\eta\rVert^{2},

where
C_{\kappa}>0
is a constant depending on
\kappa
.
Choosing
\epsilon=\frac{C_{\kappa}}{2}
, we obtain

\lVert\nabla\eta\rVert^{2}\leq Ch^{2p},

(2.25)

which leads to the expected bound on
\eta
.

Step 4.

We now apply Lemma
2.4
and the estimates in step 3 to find

\lVert\nabla(u-u_{h})\rVert\leq\lVert\nabla(u-\Pi_{h}u)\rVert+\lVert\nabla(\Pi_{h}u-u_{h})\rVert=\lVert\nabla\eta\rVert+\lVert\nabla\zeta\rVert\leq Ch^{p},

which completes the proof.

∎

2.4
Practical implementation

Variational inequalities with box constraints are typically solved using primal–dual active set methods; we refer to
[
24
,
10
]
and the references therein for details. In general, for a given fourth-order discretized variational inequality of the form

\begin{cases}(u_{h},v_{h})+(M\nabla w_{h},\nabla v_{h})=(f_{1},v_{h}),\qquad\forall v_{h}\in V_{h}^{p},\\
(w_{h},\xi_{h}-u_{h})\leq(\kappa\nabla u_{h},\nabla(\xi_{h}-u_{h}))+(f_{2},\xi_{h}-u_{h}),\qquad\forall\xi_{h}\in V_{h}^{p,+},\\
\end{cases}

with
f_{1},f_{2}\in L^{2}(D)
, the primal–dual active set method proceeds as follows:

1.

Set threshold parameter
c>0
, iteration index
j=0
, initial guess
u_{h,0}\in V_{h}^{p}
,
A_{0}=\{i\in{\mathcal{V}}\ |\ U_{i,0}<a\text{ or }U_{i,0}>b\}
,
I_{0}={\mathcal{V}}\setminus A_{0}
.

2.

Set
V_{h,j}^{p}:=\text{span}\{\varphi_{i}\}_{i\in I_{j}}
.

3.

Solve
(u_{h,j+1},w_{h,j+1})\in V_{h}^{p}\times V_{h}^{p}
via

\begin{cases}(u_{h,j+1},v_{h})+(M\nabla w_{h,j+1},\nabla v_{h})=(f_{1},v_{h}),\qquad\forall v_{h}\in V_{h}^{p},\\
(w_{h,j+1},\xi_{h})=(\kappa\nabla u_{h,j+1},\nabla\xi_{h})+(f_{2},\xi_{h}),\qquad\forall\xi_{h}\in V_{h,j}^{p},\\
U_{i,j+1}=\min(b,\max(a,U_{i,j})),\qquad\forall i\in A_{j}.\end{cases}

4.

Define
\mu_{h,j+1}\in V_{h}^{p}
via

\begin{cases}(\mu_{h,j+1},\xi_{h})=(w_{h,j+1},\xi_{h})-(\kappa\nabla u_{h,j+1},\nabla\xi_{h})-(f_{2},\xi_{h}),\qquad\forall\xi_{h}\in V_{h,j}^{p},\\
\mu_{i,j+1}=0,\qquad\forall i\in I_{j}.\end{cases}

5.

Set
A_{j+1}=\{i\in{\mathcal{V}}\ |\ U_{i,j+1}+\frac{\mu_{i,j+1}}{c}<a\text{ or }U_{i,j+1}+\frac{\mu_{i,j+1}}{c}>b\}
,
I_{j+1}={\mathcal{V}}\setminus A_{j+1}
.

6.

If
A_{j+1}=A_{j}
stop; otherwise set
j=j+1
, go to step 2.

In practice, we take
c=10^{-2}
, which is robust in our experiments. The initial guess is given by standard FEM approximation.

3
Application to parabolic problems

In this section, we extend the variational inequality framework to time-dependent problems. We construct bound-preserving and mass-conservative schemes for nonlinear fourth-order parabolic equations and propose a regularization-based extension to second-order problems. The former admits a rigorous analysis for the well-posedness, while for the latter we primarily provide numerical evidence.

We consider three classes of problems: 1. fourth-order problem (
1.5
) without a potential term; 2. fourth-order parabolic problem (
1.4
) with a general potential
f\neq 0
; 3. second-order parabolic problem (
1.7
). The corresponding schemes are presented first, followed by their fundamental properties.

The temporal discretization is defined on time nodes
\{t^{n}\}_{n=0}^{N}
with
t^{0}=0
and
t^{N}=T
. Let
{\tau}^{n}=t^{n+1}-t^{n}
for
n\in{\mathcal{N}}:=\{0\hskip 0.50003pt{:}\hskip 0.50003ptN-1\}
, and denote
{\mathcal{N}}_{k}:=\{k-1\hskip 0.50003pt{:}\hskip 0.50003ptN-1\}
for
k\in\{1,\ldots,5\}
. For simplicity, we assume a uniform time step
{\tau}
.

3.1
Numerical schemes

We now present three schemes corresponding to the above problem classes. For fourth-order parabolic problems without a potential term, we combine the variational inequality formulation in space with BDF temporal discretizations, leading to schemes that preserve bounds and mass. In addition, for first-order temporal discretization, the energy stability is also satisfied. For problems with a potential term, we incorporate the SAV technique to enhance the stability while maintaining bounds and mass. For second-order parabolic problems, we introduce a fourth-order regularization, allowing the same framework to be applied.

Fourth-order parabolic case without potential term.

Here we consider the model problem (
1.5
). Similar to the linear elliptic case, under suitable regularity assumptions
[
10
]
, the model problem can be reformulated as the following variational inequality: find
(u,w)\in H^{1}((0,T];H^{-1}(D))\cap L^{2}((0,T];H^{2}(D;[a,b]))\cap L^{\infty}((0,T];H^{1}(D))\times L^{2}((0,T];H^{1}(D))
such that for almost every
t\in(0,T]
,

\begin{cases}\langle\partial_{t}u,v\rangle_{H^{-1},H^{1}}+(M(u)\nabla w,\nabla v)=0,\quad\forall v\in H^{1}(D),\\
(w,\xi-u)\leq(\nabla u,\nabla(\xi-u)),\quad\forall\xi\in H^{1}(D;[a,b]).\end{cases}

(3.1)

The spatial discretization is obtained by replacing
H^{1}(D)
and
H^{1}(D;[a,b])
by
V_{h}^{p}
and
V_{h}^{p,+}
, respectively. For the temporal discretization, we consider the BDF-type discretization with
M
treated explicitly. The fully discrete problem then reads as follows: for all
n\in{\mathcal{N}}_{k}
, find
(u_{h}^{n+1},w_{h}^{n+1})\in V_{h}^{p,+}\times V_{h}^{p}
such that

(3.2)

(3.3)

(3.4)

Remark 3.1

(mass-conservation and BDF)

We observe that, the operator pair

in the above definition is mass-conservative, in the sense that if
\int_{D}u_{h}^{n}=\cdots=\int_{D}u_{h}^{n-k+1}

General fourth-order parabolic case.

When
f\neq 0
, introducing the auxiliary variable
r=\sqrt{E_{1}(u)}

to enhance the stability, the system with sufficiently smooth solution can be reformulated as

\begin{cases}\partial_{t}u=\nabla{\cdot}(M(u)\nabla w),\\
w=-\Delta u+\frac{r}{\sqrt{E_{1}(u)}}f(u),\\
\partial_{t}r=(\frac{f(u)}{2\sqrt{E_{1}(u)}},\partial_{t}u).\end{cases}

We discretize this system using BDF time stepping, explicit extrapolation for nonlinear terms, and the variational inequality constraint. The resulting fully discrete scheme reads: for
n\in{\mathcal{N}}_{k}
, find
(u_{h}^{n+1},r^{n+1},w_{h}^{n+1})\in V_{h}^{p,+}\times{\mathbb{R}}\times V_{h}^{p}
such that

\begin{cases}(\frac{\alpha_{k}u_{h}^{n+1}-A_{k}(u_{h}^{n})}{{\tau}},v_{h})+(M(\bar{u}_{h}^{n+1})\nabla w_{h}^{n+1},\nabla v_{h})=0,\qquad\forall v_{h}\in V_{h}^{p},\\
(w_{h}^{n+1},\xi_{h}-u_{h}^{n+1})\leq(\nabla u_{h}^{n+1},\nabla(\xi_{h}-u_{h}^{n+1}))+(\frac{r^{n+1}}{\sqrt{E_{1}(\bar{u}_{h}^{n+1})}}f(\bar{u}_{h}^{n+1}),\xi_{h}-u_{h}^{n+1}),\quad\forall\xi_{h}\in V_{h}^{p,+},\\
\alpha_{k}r^{n+1}-A_{k}(r^{n})=(\frac{f(\bar{u}_{h}^{n+1})}{2\sqrt{E_{1}(\bar{u}_{h}^{n+1})}},\alpha_{k}u_{h}^{n+1}-A_{k}(u_{h}^{n})),\end{cases}

(3.5)

where
\alpha_{k}
,
A_{k}
and

Second-order parabolic case.

For the model problem (
1.7
), we perturb it by adding a small fourth-order operator
\epsilon\Delta^{2}u
with
\epsilon>0
in the PDE. Formally, this modification introduces a modeling error of order
O(\epsilon)
. We illustrate this idea by considering the Poisson problem
-\Delta u=f_{1}
with periodic boundary condition in the torus
\mathbb{T}^{d}
. Through Fourier transform, the original PDE is equivalent to
\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}|k|^{2}\hat{u}(k)e^{ik\bm{x}}=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\hat{f_{1}}(k)e^{ik\bm{x}}
, with Fourier modes
\{\hat{u}(k)\}_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}
and
\{\hat{f_{1}}(k)\}_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}
. Owing to the orthogonality of Fourier basis, for each mode
k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}\setminus\{0\}
, we have
|k|^{2}\hat{u}(k)=\hat{f_{1}}(k)
. For the perturbed PDE
-\Delta u^{\epsilon}+\epsilon\Delta^{2}u^{\epsilon}=f_{1}
, the same argument leads to
(|k|^{2}+\epsilon|k|^{4})\hat{u}^{\epsilon}(k)=\hat{f_{1}}(k)
. Hence for each mode
k\neq 0
, the perturbation introduces an error of
O(\epsilon)
. This argument formally indicates that the perturbation introduces an
O(\epsilon)
consistency error at the continuous level.

At the discrete level, different placements of
\epsilon
lead to systems with varying conditioning. We consider a symmetric scaling to minimize the conditioning and motivate the following regularized model:

\begin{cases}\partial_{t}u=\nabla{\cdot}(K(u)\nabla u)+\sqrt{\epsilon}\Delta w,\\
w=-\sqrt{\epsilon}\Delta u,\end{cases}

with the initial data
u_{0}
and the homogeneous Neumann boundary conditions for
u
and
w
. It follows that an artificial boundary condition for
\Delta u
is imposed since the second-order parabolic problem does not have homogeneous Neumann boundary condition for
w:=-\sqrt{\epsilon}\Delta u
. However, based on our numerical observation on (
4.13
), the model error introduced by such perturbation appears negligible, and it does not influence the convergence rate for smooth solutions.
Then, we reformulate the above model problem as the fourth-order cases: find
(u,w)\in H^{1}((0,T];H^{-1}(D))\cap L^{2}((0,T];H^{2}(D;[a,b]))\cap L^{\infty}((0,T];H^{1}(D))\times L^{2}((0,T];H^{1}(D))
such that for almost every
t\in(0,T]
,

\begin{cases}\langle\partial_{t}u,v\rangle_{H^{-1},H^{1}}+\sqrt{\epsilon}(\nabla w,\nabla v)+(K(u)\nabla u,\nabla v)=0,\quad\forall v\in H^{1}(D),\\
(w,\xi-u)\leq\sqrt{\epsilon}(\nabla u,\nabla(\xi-u)),\quad\forall\xi\in H^{1}(D;[a,b]).\end{cases}

(3.6)

Then, we discretize it as in (
3.2
), with
\epsilon=h^{p+1}
to balance the perturbation error and conditioning, leading to the fully discretized problem: for all
n\in{\mathcal{N}}_{k}
, find
(u_{h}^{n+1},w_{h}^{n+1})\in V_{h}^{p,+}\times V_{h}^{p}
such that

\begin{cases}(\frac{\alpha_{k}u_{h}^{n+1}-A_{k}(u_{h}^{n})}{{\tau}},v_{h})+(K(\bar{u}_{h}^{n+1})\nabla u_{h}^{n+1},\nabla v_{h})+\sqrt{h^{p+1}}(\nabla w_{h}^{n+1},\nabla v_{h})=0,\qquad\forall v_{h}\in V_{h}^{p},\\
(w_{h}^{n+1},\xi_{h}-u_{h}^{n+1})\leq\sqrt{h^{p+1}}(\nabla u_{h}^{n+1},\nabla(\xi_{h}-u_{h}^{n+1})),\quad\forall\xi_{h}\in V_{h}^{p,+},\end{cases}

(3.7)

with
\alpha_{k}
,
A_{k}
the BDF operators, and
\bar{u}_{h}^{n+1}
the extrapolation approximation of
u_{h}^{n+1}
constructed as in (
3.2
).

Unlike the fourth-order case, the resulting scheme cannot be recast as a convex minimization problem. Therefore, its well-posedness is not covered by the present analytical framework. Nevertheless, the scheme exhibits stable behavior in all numerical experiments, which provides strong evidence of well-posedness.

Two components remain to complete the proposed schemes. The first concerns the construction of high-order, bound-preserving and mass-conservative initial data
(u_{h}^{0},...,u_{h}^{k-1})
; the other concerns the construction of initial SAVs. We answer them one by one in the rest of this part.

Initial data.

For BDF-type schemes, the initial data
u_{h}^{0}
and
(u_{h}^{1},...,u_{h}^{k-1})
are in general prepared by the
L^{2}
-projection and the Runge-Kutta method, respectively. We refer to
[
32
]
and
[
26
]
for details. The drawback of this initialization is the lack of the bound-preservation. Hence we propose two different strategies here.

In the case
k=1,2
and
u_{0}\in H^{2}(D)
, we can generate
u_{h}^{0}
by our stationary scheme (
2.4
), i.e., we use the stationary scheme (
2.4
) as a bound-preserving and mass-conservative alternative to the
L^{2}
-projection. More precisely, we define
u_{h}^{0}\in V_{h}^{p,+}
by solving

\begin{cases}(u_{h}^{0},v_{h})+(\nabla w_{h},\nabla v_{h})=(u_{0}+\Delta^{2}u_{0},v_{h}),\qquad\forall v_{h}\in V_{h}^{p},\\
(w_{h},\xi_{h}-u_{h}^{0})\leq(\nabla u_{h}^{0},\nabla(\xi_{h}-u_{h}^{0})),\qquad\forall\xi_{h}\in V_{h}^{p,+}.\end{cases}

By the construction,
u_{h}^{0}
is bound-preserving and mass-conservative. Then
u_{h}^{1}
can be prepared by (
3.2
), (
3.5
) or (
3.7
) with
k=1
.

For other cases, we consider a bound-preserving and mass-conservative postprocessing to prepare initial data. We first compute
\tilde{u}_{h}^{0}
and
(\tilde{u}_{h}^{1},...,\tilde{u}_{h}^{k-1})
by the
L^{2}
-projection and the Runge-Kutta method, respectively. Then, we apply the optimization-based postprocessing

u_{h}^{j}=\argmin_{v_{h}\in V_{h}^{p,+},\int_{D}v_{h}=\int_{D}\tilde{u}_{h}^{j}}\lVert v_{h}-\tilde{u}_{h}^{j}\rVert^{2},\qquad j\in\{0,...,k-1\}

to define the initial data. The above postprocessing is motivated by
[
29
]
, and it is numerically observed to achieve the optimal convergence rate.

Initialization of SAV.

Since at the continuous level,
r=\sqrt{E_{1}(u)}
, we set
r^{n}=\sqrt{E_{1}(u_{h}^{n})}
for
n\in\{0,...,k-1\}
. This choice maintains the optimal approximation properties of
r^{n}
, once
u_{h}^{n}
has such properties.

3.2
Fundamental properties

We first establish the well-posedness and structure-preserving properties of the scheme (
3.5
) for fourth-order parabolic problems with a potential term, as this formulation is the most general. The scheme (
3.2
) can be treated as a special case of (
3.5
).

For the regularized second-order scheme (
3.7
), the presence of the additional diffusion term prevents a direct reformulation as a convex minimization problem. Therefore, its well-posedness is not covered by the present analytical framework. Instead, we prove the bound-preservation and mass-conservation by assuming the existence of the numerical solution.

General fourth-order parabolic case.

The fundamental properties of the proposed scheme (
3.5
) can be derived following the same argument used for the stationary scheme (
2.4
).

The following lemma establishes the well-posedness of the variational inequality (
3.5
).

Lemma 3.2

(Properties of (
3.5
))
.

For all
n\in{\mathcal{N}}_{k}
, the variational inequality (
3.5
) admits a solution
(u_{h}^{n+1},r^{n+1},w_{h}^{n+1})
. Moreover,
u_{h}^{n+1}
is unique, bound-preserving, mass-conservative and the unique minimizer of the following problem:

\min_{(v,s)\in X^{n+1}}\Big\{\frac{\alpha_{k}}{2}\lVert\nabla v\rVert^{2}+\alpha_{k}s^{2}+\frac{1}{2{\tau}}\lVert\alpha_{k}v-A_{k}(u_{h}^{n})\rVert_{H^{-1,n+1}_{h}}^{2}\Big\},

(3.8)

where

X^{n+1}=\{(v,s)\in V_{h}^{p,+}\times{\mathbb{R}}\ |\ \int_{D}v=\int_{D}u_{h}^{n}\ ,\ \alpha_{k}s-A_{k}(r^{n})=(\frac{f(\bar{u}_{h}^{n+1})}{2\sqrt{E_{1}(\bar{u}_{h}^{n+1})}},\alpha_{k}v-A_{k}(u_{h}^{n}))\}.

(3.9)

In addition, for
k=1
, i.e., the first-order temporal discretization, we have the modified energy stability

\frac{1}{2}\lVert\nabla u_{h}^{n+1}\rVert^{2}+(r^{n+1})^{2}\leq\frac{1}{2}\lVert\nabla u_{h}^{n}\rVert^{2}+(r^{n})^{2}.

(3.10)

Remark 3.3

(SAV stabilization)
.

We note that the modified energy stability (
3.10
) holds true unconditionally, similar to its application on parabolic PDEs in
[
30
,
25
]
. Although the modified energy stability is not proved for high-order temporal discretizations, they are numerically observed to have an improved energy stability, even for a challenging Cahn–Hilliard test in (
4.11
).

Proof.

The proof follows the same variational framework as in Lemma
2.1
. For completeness, we outline the main steps, emphasizing the additional coupling introduced by the auxiliary variable
r
.

Step 1: well-posedness of the minimization problem.

We first verify that the admissible set
X^{n+1}
is nonempty, convex, and closed.

Nonemptiness.

We notice that
(u_{h}^{n},\frac{1}{\alpha_{k}}((\frac{f(\bar{u}_{h}^{n+1})}{2\sqrt{E_{1}(\bar{u}_{h}^{n+1})}},\alpha_{k}u_{h}^{n}-A_{k}(u_{h}^{n})))+A_{k}(r^{n}))
satisfies all constraints of
X^{n+1}
, hence
X^{n+1}
is nonempty.

Convexity.

Let
(v_{1},s_{1}),(v_{2},s_{2})\in X^{n+1}
and
\theta\in[0,1]
. Then
\theta v_{1}+(1-\theta)v_{2}\in V_{h}^{p,+}
and satisfies the mass constraint by linearity. Moreover, the linear constraint on
s
is preserved by convex combinations. Hence
X^{n+1}
is convex.

Closedness.

Since
V_{h}^{p,+}
is closed in
V_{h}^{p}
and the additional constraints are linear,
X^{n+1}
is closed.

Therefore, the minimization problem (
3.8
) consists of minimizing a strictly convex functional over a finite-dimensional, nonempty, closed, and convex set. It thus admits a unique minimizer
(u_{h}^{n+1},r^{n+1})
.

Step 2: equivalence with the variational inequality.

We show that the minimization problem (
3.8
) is equivalent to the variational formulation (
3.5
). For notational simplicity, we write

M=M(\bar{u}_{h}^{n+1}),\qquad f=f(\bar{u}_{h}^{n+1}),\qquad E_{1}=E_{1}(\bar{u}_{h}^{n+1}).

The minimization problem can be rewritten in the equivalent form

\displaystyle\min_{(u_{h},v_{h},s)\in V_{h}^{p}\times V_{h}^{p}\times\mathbb{R}}\;

\displaystyle\frac{\alpha_{k}}{2}\|\nabla u_{h}\|^{2}+\alpha_{k}s^{2}+\frac{{\tau}}{2}(M\nabla v_{h},\nabla v_{h})

s.t.

\displaystyle(\alpha_{k}u_{h}-A_{k}(u_{h}^{n}),\chi_{h})+{\tau}(M\nabla v_{h},\nabla\chi_{h})=0,\quad\forall\chi_{h}\in V_{h}^{p},\quad\int_{D}v_{h}=0,

\displaystyle a\leq U_{i}\leq b,\quad\forall i\in{\mathcal{V}},\quad u_{h}=\sum_{i\in{\mathcal{V}}}U_{i}\varphi_{i},

\displaystyle\alpha_{k}s-A_{k}(r^{n})=\Big(\frac{f}{2\sqrt{E_{1}}},\alpha_{k}u_{h}-A_{k}(u_{h}^{n})\Big).

By standard convex optimization theory with box and linear constraints, the minimizer satisfies the associated KKT system. Eliminating the Lagrange multipliers associated with the box constraints yields the variational inequality: find
(u_{h},w_{h})\in V_{h}^{p,+}\times V_{h}^{p}
such that

\displaystyle(\tfrac{\alpha_{k}u_{h}-A_{k}(u_{h}^{n})}{{\tau}},\chi_{h})+(M\nabla w_{h},\nabla\chi_{h})=0,\quad\forall\chi_{h}\in V_{h}^{p},

\displaystyle(w_{h},\xi_{h}-u_{h})\leq(\nabla u_{h},\nabla(\xi_{h}-u_{h}))+\Big(\frac{s}{\sqrt{E_{1}}}f,\xi_{h}-u_{h}\Big),\quad\forall\xi_{h}\in V_{h}^{p,+},

together with the scalar constraint

\alpha_{k}s-A_{k}(r^{n})=\Big(\frac{f}{2\sqrt{E_{1}}},\alpha_{k}u_{h}-A_{k}(u_{h}^{n})\Big).

This is precisely the scheme (
3.5
). Therefore, the minimization problem and the variational formulation are equivalent, and the well-posedness of (
3.5
) follows.

Step 3: structure-preservation.

For the structure-preserving properties, we notice that
(u_{h}^{n+1},r^{n+1})
is an element in
X^{n+1}
, hence
u_{h}^{n+1}
is mass-conservative and bound-preserving. Then, for
k=1
, we notice that
(u_{h}^{n+1},r^{n+1})
is a minimizer of the problem (
3.8
), and
(u_{h}^{n},r^{n})
is also in
X^{n+1}
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\lVert\nabla u_{h}^{n+1}\rVert^{2}

\displaystyle+(r^{n+1})^{2}+\frac{1}{2{\tau}}\lVert u_{h}^{n+1}-u_{h}^{n}\rVert_{H_{h}^{-1,n+1}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\min_{(v_{h},s)\in X^{n+1}}\Big(\frac{1}{2}\lVert\nabla v_{h}\rVert^{2}+s^{2}+\frac{1}{2{\tau}}\lVert v_{h}-u_{h}^{n}\rVert_{H_{h}^{-1,n+1}}^{2}\Big)\leq\frac{1}{2}\lVert\nabla u_{h}^{n}\rVert^{2}+(r^{n})^{2}.

It follows the expected modified energy stability (
3.10
), which concludes the proof.

∎

Second-order parabolic case.

We then discuss the properties of the proposed scheme (

Lemma 3.5

5
Concluding remarks

In this work, we developed a variational inequality-based framework for a broad class of fourth-order elliptic and parabolic problems. For linear fourth-order elliptic equations, the proposed approach yields finite element schemes that are bound-preserving and mass-conservative, and that admit an equivalent strictly convex minimization formulation. This structure allows us to establish well-posedness and derive an optimal error estimate in the
H^{1}
-seminorm for sufficiently smooth solutions. It also enables an efficient implementation via primal–dual active set methods.

We then extended the framework to nonlinear fourth-order parabolic problems by coupling the variational inequality formulation with BDF temporal discretizations and SAV stabilization. The resulting schemes are designed to achieve high-order accuracy in both space and time, while rigorously preserving bounds and mass. In addition, its first-order version is energy stable with a modified energy. Numerical experiments confirm that these schemes are robust and efficient on several challenging benchmark problems, including lubrication-type equations and Cahn–Hilliard models with nonlinear mobility and logarithmic potentials.

As a further application, we proposed a consistent fourth-order regularization strategy for nonlinear second-order parabolic problems and applied the same variational inequality–BDF framework. The resulting method enforces bound-preservation and mass-conservation. Extensive numerical tests, including to problems with low regularity and moving interfaces such as porous medium equations in Barenblatt form, indicated that they were highly stable and accurate in practice. However, a complete well-posedness theory for this regularized second-order setting remains an open problem.

Compared with existing approaches, the present framework addresses several gaps in the literature:

•

it provides high-order spatial schemes for fourth-order elliptic problems that are simultaneously nodally bound-preserving and mass-conservative, with a rigorous
H^{1}
-error analysis;

•

it yields space–time high-order, bound-preservation and mass-conservation for nonlinear fourth-order parabolic problems, and the energy stability is established for the first-order temporal discretization;

•

it offers a practical high-order strategy for nonlinear second-order parabolic problems that enforces bounds and mass-conservation.

Error analysis in the
L^{2}
-norm for the elliptic scheme (
2.4
), rigorous convergence analysis for parabolic schemes (
3.5
), (
3.2
) and (
3.7
), as well as extensions to more complex systems such as coupled multiphysics models and higher-order PDEs, will be considered in future work.
