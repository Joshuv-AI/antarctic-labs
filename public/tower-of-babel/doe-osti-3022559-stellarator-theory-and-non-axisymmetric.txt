Divertors are a critical concept for all magnetically confined fusion systems. The W7-X stellarator
has an island divertor as do many of the privately funded stellarator reactor designs, but non-
resonant divertors have many advantages, which are defined in the paper OSTI ID: 3021689,
“Stellarators with enhanced tritium confinement and edge radiation control” and discussed in
OSTI ID: 3021687, 3021688, 3021692, and 3021695. The three basic advantages are: (1)
Resilience, the location at which the diverted plasma reaches the walls is insensitive to plasma
conditions, unlike island divertors. (2) The width of the region at the plasma edge that flows into
the divertor has a controllable width. It can be made sufficiently broad to avoid neutrals
impending on the plasma from charge exchanging with high energy plasma particles. The
resulting high energy neutrals erode the walls. (3) The controllable width and confinement of
non-resonant divertors allow high-Z impurities to be added. The shortness of their confinement
time limits their diffusion into the plasma interior. High-Z impurity radiation at the plasma edge
seems the only way to sufficiently spread the outcoming power over the walls. The lack of
experiments is a major impediment to application of non-resonant divertors, although the small
experiment STAR_LITE is being built at Hampton University to study them.

Two additional papers developed the mathematics of chaos, turnstiles and cantori, which is
required to understand non-resonant divertors: OSTI ID: 3021693, “Eﬃcient analysis of magnetic
field line behavior in toroidal plasmas” was supported by this grant but the other

<https://arxiv.org/pdf/2510.25047> was not since it was written after the grant funding ended.
This review, “Magnetic Field Line Chaos, Cantori, and Turnstiles in Toroidal Plasmas,” explains
little-known mathematics, which is essential to understanding non-resonant divertors as well as
runaway electron losses in tokamak disruptions.

Although disruptions are associated with tokamaks, not stellarators, they will occur in stellarators
if there are large scale instabilities---ideal or resistive---that do no self-saturate. A non-ideal
effect such as resistivity is required to break magnetic surfaces. The P.I. has shown even an ideal
magnetic evolution can cause neighboring magnetic field lines to develop an arbitrarily large
number of e-folds of separation. This exponentiation is shown in “Magnetic field evolution and
reconnection in low resistivity plasmas,” OSTI ID: 3021685, to make the preservation of magnetic
surfaces exponentially sensitive to the resistive diffusion of magnetic field lines. OSTI ID:
3021694, “Magnetic reconnection and dynamos in the presence of plasma turbulence,” explains
why traditional reconnection theory, which ignores chaos, is applicable to systems that have only
two spatial coordinates, but not to systems that depend on all three spatial coordinates. Since
funding stopped, continued research by the P.I. showed that collisionless reconnection due to
electron inertia has a closely related divergence-free field that does not reconnect, Phys.
Plasmas 33, 022109 (2026). Even electron motion across the pressure surfaces due to the
reconnection are small compared to ordinary gradient-B drifts. The paper shows that the chaos
that naturally arises in a three-dimensional evolution of a magnetic field can cause rapid
reconnection unless the diffusive part of non-ideal evolution, such as that due to resistivity, is
absolutely zero.

The empirical scaling of the energy confinement time in tokamaks and stellarators is within a
multiplying factor given by the gyro-Bohm level of transport. OSTI ID: 3021684, “Required
toroidal confinement for fusion and omnigeneity” pointed out that the tolerable multiplicative
factor is highly dependent of the temperature at which a fusion power plant operates. By this
measure, fusion is easiest at approximately 9 keV and an order of magnitude more difficult at 30
keV. It also shows that electron heating, which the fusion heating is, is inconsistent with hot ions
when the energy confinement time of the ions is short compared to the electron-ion energy
equilibration time, which increases as Te3/2. When this effect was seen on W7-X, it was first
interpreted as a stellarator issue, but it is a constraint on the heating of all fusion systems to
ignition conditions.

Although funding from this grant ended on April 30, 2025, the P.I. has continued to do research,
primarily funded by a sabbatical. Unlike neoclassical transport in a stellarator, microtubulent
transport is generally thought to be intrinsically ambipolar. This was shown by the P.I. to have
an in principle important exception in the article “Non-Ambipolarity of Microturbulent
Transport,” https://arxiv.org/pdf/2601.15661 , which has been submitted for journal publication.
He has shown that so-called electrostatic microturbulence produces magnetic perturbations
when the plasma pressure is non-zero. Although chaotic magnetic fields need not be turbulent,
turbulent magnetic fields are always chaotic and naturally break the magnetic surfaces. It has
long been known that Ion Temperature Gradient (ITG) turbulence has associated magnetic fields,
which can cause enhanced energy transport by wiggling the magnetic surfaces, but this effect is

generally negligibly small. Of far greater potential importance is the breaking of the magnetic
surfaces due to the magnetic field chaos, which gives enhanced electron transport across the
pressure surfaces. Intrinsically non-ambipolar transport must be restored to ambipolarity by a
radial electric field, which has two effects: (1) It produces a particular plasma rotation, which
requires a torque. (2) It either pulls in or pushes out impurities. In non-ambipolar neoclassical
transport, the associated torques are balanced by forces on the coils. But, non-ambipolar
transport produced by microturbulence cannot transfer the required forces to the coils.
Nevertheless, it can sustain the forces by an effect that has the form of an electron viscosity---
despite the fact that viscosities are thought of as primarily an ion effect. Neoclassical transport
in stellarators often, but not always produces an electric field that pulls in impurities and this
electron-viscosity effect can reverse that. The critical parameter is how far the electrons can
move across the pressure surfaces before becoming decorrelated. The effect was derived in a
paper, “Electric field effects during disruptions,” Phys. Plasmas 31, 102506 (2024). This paper
was published by the P.I. during the period of this grant but supported by a different grant DE-
FG02-03ER54696. This other grant was terminated, after a twenty-three-month review of a
renewal proposal, due to the lack predictability of future results.

Freedom in the coils for all magnetic fusion devices has had only limited exploitation for
maximizing buildability, controllability of construction errors, accessibility of the plasma
chamber, and flexibility in the plasmas that can be produced. Mathematics shows that in
principle the full magnetic field can be produced by one toroidal field coil plus windowpane coils,
which are removable when parts of the plasma chamber are removed for maintenance.
Although the one toroidal field coil case is not optimal, it does illustrate the enormity of the space
available for optimization. The relevant papers are OSTI ID: 3021688, “Needed computations
and computational capabilities for stellarators,” OSTI ID: 3021690, “Current potential patches,”
which was part of Todd Elder’s thesis, and OSTI ID: 3021691, “Use of current-potential patches
to obtain fundamental improvements to the coils of magnetic fusion devices.”
