Effects of sparsity and superposition on loss in simple autoencoders
Mriganka Basu Roy Chowdhury, Eric McLaughlin Weiner

Abstract
One of the major difficulties in the mechanistic interpretability of neural networks is the occurrence of polysemanticity , which suggests that each neuron is typically responsible for multiple different tasks, impeding a clean interpretation of their function. The seminal paper of 6 argues that this occurs due to superposition , a phenomenon where the neural network represents distinct features as non-orthogonal directions in a lower-dimensional space, a strategy that allows much greater compression of the data without sacrificing fidelity due to the feature sparsity of input vectors. 6 empirically validates these hypotheses in a rather natural and simple autoencoder with sparse inputs. The contribution of the present work is to analyze the mathematical basis for the occurrence and optimality of superposition, while rigorously corroborating some of their findings. In particular, we provide upper and lower bounds for the L 2 L^{2} reconstruction loss, tight in the very sparse regime, for power activation functions. A short list of interesting open problems are also included at the end.

Introduction

Advances in the capabilities and impacts of large-scale neural networks have necessitated a deeper
understanding of their internal mechanisms, with the hope that these insights will lead to more
reliable and customizable models. The field of
mechanistic interpretability
(see, for instance,
1
) seeks to
uncover the detailed inner workings of trained neural networks, and although nascent, a vast array of
empirical findings have been reported. However, since neural networks learn directly from data,
without any bias towards human-interpretable features, it is often difficult to cleanly separate and
ascribe functionality to individual neurons or layers. A key challenge in this regard is the
presence of
polysemantic
neurons, which respond to multiple different features, thereby
obscuring their role. The recent work of
6
proposes that this phenomenon arises due to the
neural network attempting to pack more features into a limited number of neurons, a phenomenon they
refer to as
superposition
. A hypothesis for the effectiveness of this strategy is that
inputs are typically
feature-sparse
, that is, each input typically exhibits only a few of
the many possible learned features. Consequently, this “overloading” does not significantly impact
fidelity. Various aspects of this phenomenon have been studied extensively, for instance, recovering
superposed features in
3
;
14
, or understanding their occurrence in large language
models in
5
. The literature also follows a long line of research
on sparse coding, starting with
10
, which seeks to decompose a signal into a sparse
combination of basis elements; for connections to autoencoders see
11
.
We refer the reader to the references in
6
(also
7
;
3
) for a more
comprehensive overview of prior work.

6
proposes a simple one-layer autoencoder (with tied weights) to study the effects of
sparsity and superposition. The core purpose of the current paper is to attempt to rigorously understand
this simple model, and to provide theoretical guarantees on just how beneficial is superposition
in the presence of sparsity, and how nonlinearity aids the same. Without any further ado, we now
describe the model, which is almost identical to that of
6
, with some minor modifications
to aid analysis. We note that throughout this paper, we consider the “equal-importance” case as
the results are cleanest here; we strongly believe our techniques extend to the general case as
well.

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\left\langle v,M_{zy}^{T}M_{zz}^{-1}M_{zy}v\right\rangle\Big|y\right]

\displaystyle\leqslant\left\|M_{zy}^{T}M_{zz}^{-1}M_{zy}\right\|_{\mathrm{op}}

\displaystyle\overset{(a)}{\leqslant}\left\|M\right\|_{\mathrm{op}}\leqslant K,

where

\displaystyle M=\nabla^{2}V(y,z)=\begin{pmatrix}M_{yy}&M_{zy}^{T}\\
M_{zy}&M_{zz}\end{pmatrix},

(17)

and step
(a)
is via Lemma
A.2
. All this allows us
to conclude that
|f^{\prime}(0)|^{2}\leqslant K^{2}
, and since

and
v
were arbitrary unit
vectors, we have that

is

-Lipschitz in

. Then, if

is an
independent copy of

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left\|m(y)-m(y^{\prime})\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leqslant K^{2}\mathbb{E}\left\|y-y^{\prime}\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle=2K^{2}\mathbb{E}\left\|y\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle=2K^{3}d

using the Poincaré inequality again to derive

\mathbb{E}[y_{i}^{2}]\leqslant K
. Finally, a
simple computation shows that

\mathbb{E}\left\|m(y)-m(y^{\prime})\right\|^{2}=2\cdot\mathbb{E}\left\|m(y)\right\|^{2}

since

by symmetry of

.
∎

Lower bounds

Our proof of the lower bound will depend upon the construction of a
particular type of matrix exhibiting essentially optimal
closeness (as implied by the celebrated
Welch bound
, see

)
to a large identity matrix, despite being of much lower rank. This is described in the
following lemma. See Remark

below for a heuristic explanation of why such

Lemma 4.1
.

For all sufficiently large

(depending on

), there is a matrix

of the form

with
V\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d^{m}}
(so that

\mathop{\mathrm{rank}}M\leqslant d
)
satisfying

\displaystyle M_{ii}

\displaystyle=1,\quad\forall i,

\displaystyle\left|M_{ij}\right|

\displaystyle\leqslant C_{m}d^{-1/2}<1,\quad\forall i\neq j,

(18)

where
C_{m}
depends only on

.

Direct randomized constructions will yield
\lesssim\sqrt{\log d/d}
in (

Armed with this lemma, we now proceed to prove the lower bound in Theorem

,
encapsulated in the

Theorem 4.2
.

Under the usual assumptions we have

\sup_{W}\tilde{\mathcal{L}}(W)\gtrsim\min(pd^{m},dp^{1/m}).

Proof.

For some
k\leqslant d^{m}
to be optimized over later, choose

to be a matrix of the form

A=\begin{pmatrix}tM^{(k)}&0\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n},

where
M^{(k)}
is the top-left
k\times k
block of the matrix

in Lemma
4.1
(so
that
M^{(d^{m})}=M
), and

is a parameter to be chosen soon. With this choice (and
W
chosen

), we have

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathcal{L}}(W)=2t^{m}\cdot\mathbb{E}\left\langle x,(M^{(k)}x)^{\odot m}\right\rangle-t^{2m}\cdot\mathbb{E}\left\|(M^{(k)}x)\right\|_{2m}^{2m}.

(19)

This can be optimized over

to yield

\displaystyle\sup_{W}\tilde{\mathcal{L}}(W)

\displaystyle\geqslant\sup_{k\leqslant d^{m}}\frac{\left(\mathbb{E}\left\langle x,(M^{(k)}x)^{\odot m}\right\rangle\right)^{2}}{\left(\mathbb{E}\left\|(M^{(k)}x)\right\|_{2m}^{2m}\right)}.

The numerator is

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left\langle x,(M^{(k)}x)^{\odot m}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\mathbb{E}\left[x_{i}\cdot\left(\sum_{j=1}^{k}M_{ij}x_{j}\right)^{m}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\sum_{\alpha:|\alpha|=m}\binom{m}{\alpha}\prod_{j=1}^{k}M_{ij}^{\alpha_{j}}\cdot\mathbb{E}\left[x_{i}^{\alpha_{i}+1}\prod_{j\neq i}x_{j}^{\alpha_{j}}\right]

where we use the multinomial expansion with the terms indexed by multi-indices
\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{k}

(
\mathbb{N}=\{0,1,\ldots\}
) with
|\alpha|\coloneq\sum_{j=1}^{k}\alpha_{j}=m
, and
\binom{m}{\alpha}\coloneq\frac{m!}{\prod_{j}\alpha_{j}!}
. Due to the symmetry of
\mu
,
each
\alpha_{j}
must be even for
j\neq i
for the expectation to be nonzero, and
\alpha_{i}
must be
odd. This implies that for every
\alpha

can therefore lower bound this quantity by only considering the terms with

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left\langle x,(M^{(k)}x)^{\odot m}\right\rangle

\displaystyle\geqslant\sum_{i=1}^{k}M_{ii}^{m}\mathbb{E}[x_{i}^{m+1}]=p\mu_{m+1}\cdot k\gtrsim pk.

(21)

We now turn to the denominator, which may be upper bounded as follows:

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{k}\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\sum_{j=1}^{k}M_{ij}x_{j}\right)^{2m}\right]

\displaystyle\overset{(a)}{\lesssim}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\max\left(\sum_{j}\mathbb{E}\left|M_{ij}x_{j}\right|^{2m},\left(\sum_{j}\mathbb{E}\left|M_{ij}x_{j}\right|^{2}\right)^{m}\right)

\displaystyle\lesssim\max\left(p\cdot\sum_{i=1}^{k}\sum_{j=1}^{k}|M_{ij}|^{2m},\;p^{m}\cdot\sum_{i=1}^{k}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{k}M^{2}_{ij}\right)^{m}\right)

\displaystyle\lesssim\max\left(pk,\;p^{m}k\left(1+kd^{-1}\right)^{m}\right),

(22)

where step
(a)
is due to
Rosenthal’s inequality
, see
12
.
Combining (
21
) and (
4
) yields

\displaystyle\sup_{W}\tilde{\mathcal{L}}(W)

\displaystyle\gtrsim\sup_{k\leqslant d^{m}}\min\left(pk,\;p^{2-m}k\cdot(1+k/d)^{-m}\right)

\displaystyle\gtrsim\sup_{d\leqslant k\leqslant d^{m}}\min\left(pk,\;p^{2-m}k^{1-m}d^{m}\right)

where restricting to
k\geqslant d
allows the bound
(1+k/d)^{-m}\gtrsim(k/d)^{-m}
. If
p\leqslant d^{-m}
, the choice
k=d^{m}
yields
p^{2-m}k^{1-m}d^{m}=p\cdot p^{1-m}d^{2m-m^{2}}\geqslant pd^{m}
and thus

\sup_{W}\tilde{\mathcal{L}}(W)\gtrsim pd^{m}
. Otherwise, choose
6
6

6

The
integer effect can be ignored by noting that since this choice is
\geqslant d
, the nearest
integer is within a factor of 2, which can be subsumed into the constant.

k=\frac{d}{p}\cdot p^{1/m}
(which is a valid choice since

implies

), so that

∎

Remark 4.3
.

We quickly note a heuristic that was in fact the guiding principle for our construction above.
Let us begin by extracting the first-order in
p
from (
7
). To that
end, we expand

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathcal{L}}(W)

\displaystyle=2\cdot\mathbb{E}\left\langle x,\phi(W^{T}Wx)\right\rangle-\mathbb{E}\left\|\phi(W^{T}Wx)\right\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=p\cdot\left(2\mu_{m+1}\cdot\sum_{i=1}^{n}A_{ii}^{m}-\mu_{2m}\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}A_{ij}^{2m}\right)+\text{terms of order $p^{2}$},

where
A=W^{T}W
. The quantity inside the parenthesis may be simplified by setting
A=cB
where

c=\left(\mu_{m+1}/\mu_{2m}\right)^{1/m}
, yielding

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathcal{L}}(W)

\displaystyle=p\cdot\frac{\mu_{m+1}^{2}}{\mu_{2m}}\cdot\mathcal{F}(B)+\text{terms of
order $p^{2}$},

where the
\mu
-free objective
\mathcal{F}(B)
is defined as

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}(B)=2\cdot\sum_{i=1}^{n}B_{ii}^{m}-\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}B_{ij}^{2m}.

(23)

which must be maximized over positive semidefinite matrices
B\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
with
\mathop{\mathrm{rank}}B\leqslant d
. Since, at least for small enough
p
, the first order should be dominant, this is an important objective to optimize, and the answer to this problem should dictate the very sparse limit.
Define
\tilde{B}=B^{\odot m}
(pointwise power), so that

\mathcal{F}(B)=\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}(2\tilde{B}-\tilde{B}^{2})\leqslant\sum_{i=1}^{r}2\tilde{\lambda}_{i}-\tilde{\lambda}_{i}^{2}\leqslant r,

where
r=\mathop{\mathrm{rank}}\tilde{B}
. If
B=V^{T}V
for
V\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}
, then
\tilde{B}=\tilde{V}^{T}\tilde{V}
where
the columns of
\tilde{V}
are the
m
-fold tensor products of the columns of
V
, and thus
\tilde{V}\in\mathbb{R}^{d^{m}\times n}
. Consequently,
r\leqslant d^{m}
, and thus
\mathcal{F}(B)
can be at most
d^{m}
. One may easily check that the construction in
Lemma
4.1
(with an optimal scaling) achieves this upper bound up to constant factors.

5
Open problems and future directions

During the development of the work in this article, we encountered a selection of interesting
problems, some of which we list below.

1.

Perhaps the most interesting question is the true correct order of
\sup_{W}\tilde{\mathcal{L}}(W)
,
under natural conditions on
\mu
. Note that our
O(d)
upper bound, which is really the
only piece that we can expect to be loose, does not rely on the particular form of
\phi

considered here.

2.

We expect Theorem
3.2
, which is a direct
consequence of an
O(d)
upper bound on (
16
), to hold under
a wider range of conditions on
\mu
than strong log-concavity.

3.

We are also interested in a tight understanding of the first-order loss (
23
),
which dictates the loss behavior under very sparse inputs. This is particularly appealing
to us due to its self-contained nature as an optimization problem over low-rank positive
semidefinite matrices. It does not appear to yield easily to a direct spectral analysis since
pointwise powers, in general, behave rather poorly with respect to the spectrum.

4.

The reader will note that the construction in Lemma
4.1
, used in the lower
bound, is somewhat involved. Does the gradient descent on the loss truly converge to such
highly structured solutions? This does not seem likely, and therefore it remains interesting to
understand the true nature of solutions obtained via training.

Lemma A.2
.

Suppose

A_{21}&A_{22}\end{pmatrix},\quad A_{12}=A_{21}^{T},

Lemma A.3
.

Suppose
x\in\mathbb{R}^{q}
and
y\in\mathbb{R}^{r}
are random vectors with all finite moments. Then,

\sup_{C\in\mathbb{R}^{q\times r}}\left(2\cdot\mathbb{E}\left\langle x,Cy\right\rangle-\mathbb{E}\left\|Cy\right\|_{2}^{2}\right)=\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\left(M\Sigma^{\dagger}M^{T}\right),

where
\Sigma=\mathbb{E}[yy^{T}]
,
M=\mathbb{E}[xy^{T}]
, and
\Sigma^{\dagger}
is the
Moore-Penrose pseudoinverse of
\Sigma
.

Proof.

To begin, observe that the supremum is finite, since

2\left\langle x,Cy\right\rangle-\left\|Cy\right\|_{2}^{2}\leqslant\left\|x\right\|_{2}^{2},

and therefore the objective is bounded above by
\mathbb{E}\left\|x\right\|_{2}^{2}

assumption.
Let us begin by writing the objective as a deterministic function
of
C

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[2\left\langle x,Cy\right\rangle-\left\|Cy\right\|_{2}^{2}]

\displaystyle=2\cdot\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\left(C\cdot\mathbb{E}[yx^{T}]\right)-\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\left(C\cdot\mathbb{E}[yy^{T}]\cdot C^{T}\right)

\displaystyle=2\cdot\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}(CM^{T})-\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}(C\Sigma C^{T}),

where
\Sigma=\mathbb{E}[yy^{T}]
and
M=\mathbb{E}[xy^{T}]
. Note that
\Sigma\succeq 0
,
and therefore, the objective is a concave function of
C
.

Consider the first order optimality condition. Differentiating with respect to

C
and setting to zero, we obtain

\nabla_{C}\left(2\cdot\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}(CM^{T})-\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}(C\Sigma C^{T})\right)=2M-2C\Sigma=0,

a solution to which would exist if and only if each row
M_{i}
of

lies
in the row space of
\Sigma
. In that case, note that the choice of
C=M\Sigma^{\dagger}

is valid, since by hypothesis, there is some

, and therefore,

by properties of the pseudoinverse. Plugging this choice into the objective yields the value

2\cdot\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}(M\Sigma^{\dagger}M^{T})-\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}(M\Sigma^{\dagger}\Sigma\Sigma^{\dagger}M^{T})=\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}(M\Sigma^{\dagger}M^{T}),

as desired.

If, however, some row

does not lie in the row space of
\Sigma
, construct

such that every row of

is zero except the

By choosing

to be a multiple of a vector orthogonal to the row space of
\Sigma
but with

, the objective may be made arbitrarily large, contradicting
the finiteness of the supremum. Consequently this case must be impossible, concluding the proof.
