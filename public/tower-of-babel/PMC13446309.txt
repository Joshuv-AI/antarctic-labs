An explicit strategy facilitates adaptation to virtual surgeries by biasing the recruitment of existing synergies
Authors: Daniele Borzelli, Paolo De Pasquale, Sergio Gurgone, Maura Mezzetti, Angelica Quercia, Denise J. Berger, Lucas R. Dal’Bello, Andrea d’Avella
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryMuscle synergies simplify motor control by mapping movement goals onto a reduced set of neural commands. However, how this modular architecture adapts during motor learning and the role of explicit strategies remain unclear. Here, we investigated adaptation to “virtual surgeries,” perturbations of muscle pulling directions simulated in virtual reality, with and without explicit cues consisting of touching and naming the muscles most effective. Explicit cues increased reaction times and improved performance after both compatible surgeries, which could be compensated by recombining existing synergies, and incompatible surgeries, which required the exploration of new muscle patterns. After incompatible surgeries, however, performance gains were limited to conditions requiring only minor synergy reorganization. These findings suggest that explicit strategies primarily bias recombination toward existing synergies rather than forming new ones. Understanding the contributions of explicit processes can inform rehabilitation protocols and human-machine interfaces aimed at refining existing movements or acquiring new motor skills.

Introduction

Motor learning refines movements through explicit strategies and implicit processes.1 For example, an explicit strategy to counteract rotated visual feedback involves aiming at a counter-rotated target, instantly compensating for the perturbation. In contrast, implicit learning gradually and unconsciously reduces the error between the aimed target and observed hand location.2

Effective movement refinement often requires changes in muscle activation patterns, believed to arise from combinations of muscle synergies, i.e., modular elements representing the coordinated recruitment of muscle groups flexibly activated according to task conditions.3,4,5,6,7,8,9,10 However, how such changes occur in modular architecture remains unclear. Some studies suggested that adjusting synergy coefficients suffices to adapt to visuomotor rotations11,12 or cycling with delayed biofeedback,13 leaving the synergy structure unchanged. Other studies indicated that new synergies emerge when learning novel skills, such as one-legged cycling14 or walking on a narrow beam,15 or a complex bimanual task in virtual reality with feedback on the similarity of the participant’s muscle activation patterns to those of an expert16 or when adapting to force field perturbations.17 Muscle pattern changes may occur either to accommodate environmental changes (adaptation) or to acquire entirely new motor skills (de novo learning).1,18 In a synergy-based framework, motor adaptation to visuomotor transformations may fine-tune synergy recruitment without altering synergy structure, whereas de novo learning or dynamic perturbations may require novel synergies,19,20,21 and exploration beyond the synergy subspace.22 Whether this exploration is purely noise-driven or guided by explicit strategies remains unknown.

The virtual surgery paradigm allows investigation of motor learning in a modular control framework. Virtual surgeries during multi-directional isometric force generation exploit myoelectric control and virtual reality to simulate altered mappings from upper-limb muscle activations to forces. These virtual surgeries can be either compatible or incompatible with existing synergies.23 While force can be generated in all directions after both types of surgeries by adjusting the activation of individual muscles, compatible surgeries can be overcome by recombining existing synergies, whereas incompatible surgeries require the formation of new synergies. Recent evidence suggests that task space exploration facilitates synergy acquisition after incompatible virtual surgeries,19 yet the role of explicit cues in guiding synergy recruitment remains unclear.

To assess the role of explicit cues in adapting to virtual surgeries, we informed participants which muscles were most effective in overcoming the perturbation, using touch and verbal naming. This resembles providing instructions about perturbations in motor adaptation tasks.2 Since the muscle activity patterns recruited in unperturbed conditions were no longer effective in reaching all targets, cues were given at the muscle rather than the task level. A cue on a specific muscle may promote new coordination, either by enhancing its activity or by recombining synergies.

As voluntary motor commands rely on existing synergies (Figure 1A), we hypothesize that explicit cues influence only synergy recruitment, while learning new synergies requires noise-driven exploration and relies on implicit processes. Thus, after compatible surgeries, cues should enhance performance by optimizing synergy recombination (Figure 1B). After incompatible surgeries, cues may initially bias activation within the synergy subspace, around which noise-driven exploration and implicit processes may enable new synergy formation (Figure 1C). Explicit cues increased reaction times (RTs), indicating the use of an explicit strategy. Participants performed better after both surgery types when cues were provided; however, improvements in incompatible surgeries occurred only when the synergy adjustments were small. Notably, cued participants in incompatible surgeries relied more on original synergies, suggesting explicit information biases recruitment rather than promoting new synergy formation. These findings highlight the role of muscle synergies in motor learning and indicate that explicit strategies may enhance performance by biasing the recombination of existing synergies, although synergy recombination occurs in all groups regardless of cue presence.

Setup and protocol

(A) Experimental setup. Subjects sat in front of a desktop and placed their right forearm into an orthosis connected to a 6-axis force transducer.

A mirror blocked the view of their hand, reflecting a virtual scene projected by an LCD monitor.

The monitor displayed a stereoscopic view of a desktop that matched the real one, along with a blue spherical cursor positioned approximately at the center of the palm when the subject was relaxed.

The cursor’s spatial position was simulated using a mass-spring-damper system and depended on the force applied by the subject.

(B) The experimental protocol was composed of 24 blocks.

After two initial blocks, in which the cursor position was directly estimated based on the force collected by the force sensor (force control blocks), the cursor position was controlled by the EMG activity by evaluating the force generated by the subject as the product of an EMG-to-force matrix (unperturbed or perturbed) times the recorded EMG signal (EMG control blocks).

EMG control blocks were perturbed (the estimated EMG-to-force matrix was multiplied by a perturbation matrix, see Methods) or unperturbed (“baseline unperturbed,” before the perturbation, and “washout unperturbed” after the perturbation).

The first and the last blocks of baseline unperturbed and the last blocks of perturbed and washout unperturbed (blocks 3, 7, 20, 24) were hidden blocks, during which participants were not provided with visual feedback of the cursor position.

(C) Example of the cursor trajectory during one trial. The displacement of the cursor for the rest position is proportional to the estimated force.

(D) Example of the force components for the same trial as in (C).

(C and D) Events were identified with circular markers (C) or vertical lines (D) of different colors.

At the beginning of each trial, the participant was asked to relax to position the cursor at the rest position (rest phase, blue to cyan lines or markers).

Then a target appeared (target go event, cyan line or marker) and the participant was asked to reach it as quickly as possible (dynamic phase, cyan to red) and to keep the cursor within the boundaries for 0.2 s (static phase, light green to dark green).

The phase between the target-go event and the end of the static phase was called the execution phase (cyan to dark green).

Previous studies have demonstrated that participants can voluntarily modulate the activity of target muscles.24,25,26 In this study, an LMM (Equation 4 in the Methods section) on the normalized muscle activation of the hint-muscle during the first velocity peak of the first hint-block (block 9) and the block preceding it (block 8), confirmed that, on average, participants were able to voluntarily increase the activity of the hint-muscles (p = 0.02, standardized effect size (SES) = 0.1). No effect of the perturbation type was identified when considering blocks 8 and 9 (p = 0.66, SES <0.1), blocks 11 and 12 (p = 0.57, SES <0.1), and blocks 15 and 16 (p = 0.45, SES = 0.1). However, some participants failed to increase the hint-muscle activity (e.g., participants 2, 9, 12, 14, 15, and 17 in Figure 5). Thus, we verified whether this inter-participant variability would have affected the performance. The presence of an explicit cue increased the reaction time

As explicit strategies are expected to lead to higher RTs, we investigated the effect of the hint on RT. We initially assessed how the hint would affect the participants’ strategies in terms of RT measured in the block immediately preceding and the block immediately following each hint block using the LMM described in Equation 6. A statistically significant increase in RT (p < 0.01, SES <0.1, Figure 8) was observed in the block immediately following the first hint block compared with the block immediately preceding it (Block 10 vs. Block 8). In contrast, no significant differences were detected in the corresponding blocks of participants who were not provided with the hint (p = 0.18, SES <0.1). This pattern is consistent with an effect of the hint on the motor strategies of participants who received it. In contrast, no effect could be detected when the task required a large change in the structure of muscle synergies (i.e., for farther targets during incompatible surgeries). The presence of an explicit cue did not affect feedforward control

Feedforward control was investigated by assessing the initial direction error (IDE) between the target direction and the direction of the cursor displacement at the first velocity peak. Considering IDE as a continuous variable and considering perturbed blocks with visual feedback (Equation 15), we found that the perturbation effect was significant and strong (p < 0.01, SES = 5.8) with a higher IDE when participants practiced a compatible surgery than an incompatible surgery (Figure 9). IDE was significantly lower in blocks from 15 to 19 (p < 0.01, SES >1.8), and participants who practiced the compatible surgeries showed a faster decrease in IDE with respect to participants who practiced the incompatible surgeries in all blocks (interaction between block and perturbation type, p < 0.001 and SES = 4.3 for all blocks). Finally, no effect of the hint was detected (p = 0.88, SES = 0.1).

When only hidden perturbed blocks (Equation 16) were considered, no effect of the presence of the cue (p = 0.21, SES = 1.1) was identified, but participants who practiced the compatible surgery showed a higher IDE (p < 0.01, SES = 3.1).

Finally, when IDE was assessed during the washout blocks (Equation 17), it significantly decreased along blocks with a strong effect (p < 0.01 and SES >1.9 for both blocks 22 and 23), while the decrease was faster in participants who practiced compatible surgery (p = 0.03, SES = 1.7 and p = 0.002, SES = 2.4 interaction between block and perturbation type, for blocks 22 and 23 respectively). The hint increased IDE; however, this increase was not significant (p = 0.07, SES = 1.5).

The IDE measured during washout blocks was then stratified, and data collected from participants who were exposed to compatible or incompatible surgeries were investigated separately (Equation 18). Both datasets showed a strong effect of the block (p < 0.01, SES >1.9 for both incompatible and compatible datasets collected during blocks 22 and 23). No effect of the presence of the cue was detected in the dataset collected from participants exposed to the compatible (p = 0.63, SES = 0.5) or incompatible (p = 0.05, SES = 2.6) surgeries.

In conclusion, the presence of the hint did not affect the initial movement, driven by feedforward control, as assessed by the angular deviation at the first velocity peak.

Discussion

We investigated the effect of an explicit cue during adaptation to a perturbation that virtually altered upper limb muscle pulling directions, i.e., virtual surgeries23 in a multi-directional isometric force-reaching task. Two perturbations were applied: one compatible with baseline synergies, requiring their recombination, and one incompatible, necessitating novel muscle patterns. Incompatible surgeries aligned the pulling forces of all synergies along a single direction, defining two target sets: closer targets requiring only minor muscle pattern alterations relative to original synergies and farther targets requiring major changes. When participants were provided with a cue, which indicated which muscle’s perturbed pulling force would move the virtual cursor toward the target, the RT increased, as expected by a greater involvement of explicit strategies. The increase in RT block immediately following the first hint block compared with the block immediately preceding it, identified in participants who were provided with the cue but not in those who were not (Equation 6), and the main effect of the hint administration on the RT along blocks (Equation 8) that was systematically associated with the blocks in which the cue was provided showed a reproducible temporal pattern that suggests that the increase in RT reflects the engagement of explicit, deliberative control processes, rather than random variability. Nevertheless, even modest and transient RT increases are considered a reliable behavioral marker of explicit strategy deployment.2 Therefore, the present RT effects, although with a modest effect size, are functionally meaningful and confirm that participants temporarily relied on conscious control during cue presentation. In fact, in this study, explicit strategies require additional planning to selectively recruit the target muscle, resulting in longer RTs. Therefore, the observed RT increase is consistent with the engagement of the intended explicit strategy, in line with previous studies.19,30 The RT modulation is better interpreted as a signature of intentional strategy use rather than as an interference effect. In fact, an intentional strategy exploiting motor planning, i.e., increasing the activity of the hint muscle, positively affected the performance (Equation 5) at least when the hint was provided for the first time (block 8), while interfering or neutral cues would not have to the observed increase in the performance in participants provided with the explicit cue with respect to those who were not. Furthermore, while the administration of the cue may interfere with the task in the hint blocks, in those blocks where the hint was not expected by the participant, the higher RT likely reflects explicit processes rather than an interference due to the cue. Unlike a previous study,19 no RT increase was found for incompatible versus compatible surgeries, possibly due to protocol differences: In the previous study, participants had more time to reach the target (15 s vs. 4 s), providing less time pressure and allowing for greater exploration.

Learning new synergies requires motor exploration beyond the synergy subspace.22 Assuming that voluntary actions are accomplished by recruiting existing synergies, we hypothesize that this exploration is noise-driven rather than guided by explicit strategies, which instead bias muscle activation within the existing synergy domain toward the muscle pattern required to overcome the perturbation (Figure 1). Thus, after compatible virtual surgeries, where the required muscle patterns remain within the synergy subspace, we expected explicit cues to facilitate adaptation. In contrast, after incompatible surgeries, we expected explicit cues to initially bias muscle activation within the synergy domain, with implicit processes later learning new patterns from local exploration outside the synergy domain. Due to time constraints in the exploration, this bias would aid learning only when new patterns remain close to the synergy subspace.

This hypothesis is compatible with our results that cues increased the learning rate (measured through the interaction between the block and the presence of the hint effects in the linear mixed models of (Equation 8), (Equation 9)) in both surgeries, but only for closer targets after incompatible surgeries (Equation 11). It aligns with the higher reconstruction quality of EMG data with original synergies and the lower angle between the motor exploration and the synergy subspace after incompatible surgeries for participants who were given cues. Moreover, the Specificity Index, indicating new muscle pattern emergence, was similar for both surgeries with cues but lower for incompatible surgeries without cues. This is an indication that explicit strategies may have limited influence on creating new patterns, at least in the initial phases of motor learning.

It is worth noting that each trial had a fixed 4-s time limit, so longer RTs actually reduced the time available for motor exploration during movement execution. Therefore, the increase in performance observed when participants were provided with the explicit hint cannot be explained by additional exploration time, but rather by cue-related modulation of muscle recruitment and synergy recombination.

Thus, this study provides some evidence that explicit strategies alter multi-muscle coordination by recombining existing synergies. In line with the hypothesis that explicit strategies modulate existing synergies rather than directly forming new muscle patterns, we observed that they facilitate adaptation to virtual surgeries by biasing the muscle activation pattern toward the pattern in the synergy subspace that is closer to the pattern required to overcome the perturbation.

Explicit cues did not affect feedforward control

Goal-directed movement control relies on feedback and feedforward mechanisms. Feedback control uses real-time sensorimotor information for movement adjustments, while feedforward control integrates sensory data on targets and motor capabilities within internal models to predict and plan movements.31,32 Both mechanisms involve internal models for accurate adjustments and contribute to motor learning through distinct processes.33,34

We analyzed feedforward control by assessing the IDE of the cursor trajectory at the first velocity peak relative to the target direction.23 Results confirmed prior findings showing a reduction in initial angular error after both compatible and incompatible surgeries,23 with a greater reduction in the compatible condition. Additionally, we found no significant effect of explicit cues on IDE. Performance improvement in the presence of explicit cues, despite no reduction in direction error, suggests no update in the feedforward control policy, i.e., muscle synergies and mapping of targets onto synergy coefficients, but it likely affected feedback control. Thus, results on angular deviation confirm that, within a single session, explicit processes did not alter existing synergistic control.

Explicit strategies recombine existing synergies by constraining exploration to the synergy subspace

Motor learning often proceeds from being predominantly explicit to mostly implicit,35 and providing cues on how to overcome a perturbation increases the rate of initial learning36 but can negatively affect performance after extensive practice. During adaptation to visuomotor rotation, an explicit strategy consists of shifting the aiming direction, and an implicit learning process, minimizing the prediction error between the aimed direction and actual hand movement direction, may override the explicit strategy and increase the error of the cursor.2 Similarly, instructing participants to verbalize or monitor their actions, to encourage the use of explicit processes, boosts performance at the initial stages of training but worsens performance in the final stages, when learning is primarily implicit.37,38,39 Therefore, promoting explicit strategies may interfere with the development of implicit motor learning,30,40 suggesting a competitive interaction between the neural systems underlying explicit and implicit processes.41,42 A description of the neurophysiological processes underlying this competition is still unknown, but some of the underlying mechanisms can be described in terms of muscle synergies.

Previous studies already demonstrated that different tasks may recruit the same set of synergies,6,43,44,45,46 that synergy activation adjustments are sufficient for motor adaptation, e.g., when adapting to visuomotor rotations11,12 or delayed biofeedback.13 Furthermore, in this study, we showed that changes in muscle patterns driven by an explicit cue can also be achieved by recombining existing synergies. Thus, the formation of muscle patterns outside the synergy subspace, which cannot be driven by explicit strategies, should be attributed to implicit processes, which may not be fully effective in a single experimental session.19,23

In this view, when performing a “de novo” learning task, which requires the coordination of muscles in new patterns that cannot be generated by existing synergies, the intrinsic stochastic noise of the CNS,47,48,49 promotes exploration50,51,52,53,54,55,56,57 necessary to learn new muscle patterns,22 and thus possibly forms new muscle synergies. As our results demonstrate that explicit strategies promote the coordination of muscles within the synergy domain, we expect them to constrain exploration outside this space. Consequently, explicit strategies do not directly contribute to the formation of new synergies. However, their role in motor learning lies in directing the exploration of muscle patterns toward those patterns within the synergy domain that are “closer” to the desired solutions outside the synergy domain (see Figure 1). In this context, explicit strategies act as a bias within the synergy domain, around which implicit processes drive the formation of new coordination patterns, i.e., new muscle synergies.

Importantly, as explicit strategies were successfully elicited through the cue and constrained the motor exploration, which was required to form new synergies, in the muscle synergy domain, we may expect that the formation of new synergies, as measured by reconstruction quality and synergy analysis, would remain primarily driven by implicit processes. This would be in line with our conclusion that explicit strategies mainly facilitate the recombination of existing synergies, rather than the generation of novel synergies.

While our conclusions are primarily based on behavioral and electromyographic data, they are grounded in well-established neurophysiological observations linking muscle synergies to cortical and subcortical substrates.7,58,59,60,61

The analysis of the washout period provides additional insights into the persistence of explicit strategy effects and the dynamics of disengagement from learned motor patterns. Across all groups, RS progressively returned toward baseline levels, and IDE decreased over blocks, reflecting recovery of feedforward control.

Importantly, no main effect of the explicit cue on RS, IDE, or EMG reconstruction was observed during washout, indicating that explicit strategies facilitated performance during perturbed blocks without inducing stable, condition-independent changes in movement planning or muscle synergy structure. However, although washout blocks were characterized by higher RS compared with baseline, participants who received the hint exhibited RS values closer to baseline, indicating a reduced persistence of adaptation during washout. This suggests that disengagement from adapted motor patterns is primarily driven by implicit processes, while explicit cues modulate the short-term carry-over of adaptation without altering the long-term organization of muscle synergies. These results align with our hypothesis that explicit strategies primarily promote recombination of existing synergies rather than the formation of new ones, and highlight the transient nature of their influence on motor learning.

In conclusion, we showed that a conscious motor strategy, driven by the presence of an explicit cue, affects the muscle patterns by recombining existing muscle synergies. This is the first study that characterized the effect of an explicit strategy in a motor learning task in the context of muscle synergies, an approach that may lead to a better understanding of the physiological processes underlying motor learning. Furthermore, these results may improve the understanding of neural correlates underlying human-human cooperation,62 and it may shape the explicit cues provided to patients in novel rehabilitation therapies based on patient-therapist interactions.63

Limitations of the study

This study is subject to several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the results. Nevertheless, as detailed in the following paragraphs, these limitations are unlikely to affect the overall conclusions of the study, which are supported by converging evidence.

As expected in EMG-based paradigms, a large inter-individual variability was detected. However, differences in the magnitude or persistence of the effect across conditions are likely not due to this variability, or to an imbalance in cue timing or application, but rather to distinct underlying physiological mechanisms, such as those associated with motor adaptation versus de novo learning. These differences are not a confound but rather a key aspect of the phenomenon under investigation.

A statistically significant increase in the relative activation of the hint muscle was detected during the first block with hints, and all participants who received sensory cues reported, at the end of the experiment, their attempt to follow the experimenter’s indications. Participants were familiarized with the anatomical identity of each muscle during the EMG sensor attachment procedure, which required brief, experimenter-guided contractions to verify signal quality. During this phase, the name of the muscle was provided, and the corresponding EMG activity was visually displayed to the participant. Importantly, this procedure was intended to ensure muscle identification and signal validity, not to train or prescribe a specific motor execution strategy. Furthermore, post-experiment debriefing confirmed that participants consistently understood the cue as an invitation to consciously bias muscle recruitment, albeit implementing this strategy with individual variability. This variability is consistent with the notion that explicit strategies operate at the level of intention and planning rather than prescribing precise motor outputs. However, this strategy might not have been maintained throughout the entire experiment. Moreover, large inter-subject variability was found in the ability to voluntarily recruit the hint muscle. Despite the variability in the recruitment of the hint muscle across participants, the cue effectively increased activation of the target muscle in most participants, supporting the validity of this novel technique. Nonetheless, we acknowledge that not all participants consistently increased activation of the specific cued muscle, as shown in Figure 10. This heterogeneity does not necessarily indicate ineffective cue usage. Rather, it implies that the cue biased motor behavior to different extents in the population, with non-uniform muscle recruitment across individuals. Nevertheless, an increase in the activity of the hint muscle during the first hint block was associated with improved performance, consistent with the hypothesis that the cue would facilitate task execution. Nonetheless, our conclusion that explicit processes modulate existing synergies does not rely solely on recruiting the specific hint muscle. The changes in the muscle patterns before and after the hint block were significantly more aligned with the muscle synergy subspace when participants received the cue, as shown in Figure 11, especially during the second and third administration. Furthermore, a greater involvement in conscious control was also supported by the observed increase in RT and consistent performance improvements, which indicate that participants effectively utilized this explicit cue, despite variability at the level of individual muscles. Thus, we may conclude that this variability does not undermine the conclusion that participants effectively utilized the explicit cue.

Although the hint targeted a single muscle, we showed that participants recruited multiple muscles as combinations of the original synergies. We did not investigate the effect of extended training with a cue on a single muscle or multiple cues on multiple muscles. Implicit processes underlying the formation of new muscle synergies may require extended training or multiple sessions to fully develop.1 Therefore, the contribution of implicit strategies here may be hidden, as participants had a limited opportunity to establish novel synergistic patterns. Further studies with longitudinal designs are needed to assess whether explicit cues on one muscle can speed up learning across sessions and whether cues on multiple muscles can promote new synergy formation to clarify the balance between explicit guidance and implicit synergy formation over longer-term practice.

While our data show that explicit cues facilitate adaptation by recombining existing muscle synergies, their influence on the formation of entirely new synergies appears limited, particularly in the context of incompatible surgeries requiring substantial reorganization. This conclusion is primarily based on negative results, including the absence of cue effects on the Specificity Index and the limited improvement for farther targets after incompatible perturbations. We acknowledge that these findings do not definitively exclude a role for explicit strategies in promoting new synergy formation, as such effects may require extended training or multiple sessions to emerge. Therefore, the present study provides evidence for the biasing of existing synergies, but further longitudinal investigations are needed to clarify whether explicit guidance can contribute to de novo synergy formation over longer time scales.

Although the present study focused on behavioral and electromyographic data, muscle synergy analysis provides a valid model for inferring the modular organization of neural motor commands.7,58 Nevertheless, the proposed distinction between explicit and implicit processes as mechanisms modulating existing versus novel synergies remains inferential. Future studies would therefore combine this paradigm with neural recording techniques (e.g., EEG, fNIRS, or TMS) to verify whether explicit cues selectively modulate cortical patterns associated with synergy recruitment, and whether implicit learning drives neural reorganization underlying new synergy formation. Additionally, computational models of modular motor control64 could help formalize how explicit strategies bias exploration within the synergy subspace and predict conditions favoring the emergence of new synergies.

The interpretation of the Specificity Index requires some caution. This index was defined on the synergy structure (W in Equation 2), and is therefore not directly influenced by changes in the synergy activation coefficients (C in Equation 2). It is worth noting that in the context of muscle synergy analysis, each synergy is defined by a fixed vector of muscle weights. Therefore, any substantial change in the synergy structure, as captured by the Specificity Index, directly reflects the formation of a new synergy rather than merely a reweighting of existing ones. Variations in the Specificity Index thus reflect modifications in the synergy structure, which can be interpreted as the emergence of new synergies. Nevertheless, the Specificity Index may be indirectly affected by increased data variability, especially during motor exploration following task perturbation. However, the procedure for selecting the number of shared synergies, which is based on a bootstrap approach, accounts for variability due to noise, thereby reducing its impact on the determination of the number of extracted synergies and, consequently, on the Specificity Index. The convergence between results obtained on the Specificity Index, the EMG reconstruction based on the original synergies, and the angle between motor exploration and synergy domain suggested the robustness of our interpretation that explicit processes modulate existing synergies.

One limitation of the present study may be the difference in task difficulty between the compatible and incompatible surgery conditions for some of the targets, particularly in the farther targets with the incompatible surgeries. Although the imposed perturbations were quantitatively matched across conditions, as confirmed by a comparable difficulty index, the two types of surgeries engage different physiological processes. Specifically, compatible surgeries involve adaptation through the reorganization of existing motor synergies, while incompatible surgeries require de novo learning, which is inherently more demanding and slower. As a result, performance in the incompatible condition, especially for far targets, may have been limited by a floor effect. This could have reduced sensitivity to detect subtle improvements induced by cueing. However, such an effect aligns with our hypothesis that explicit cues are less effective when de novo learning is required, particularly in the absence of extended practice. For distant targets requiring de novo learning and substantial reorganization of muscle patterns, performance is inherently constrained by task difficulty and limited practice time, and explicit cues are expected to have a smaller impact. Therefore, while the floor effect may limit the detection of cue-related improvements for these targets, it does not contradict the main conclusion that explicit strategies primarily bias existing synergies rather than forming new ones, as suggested by other analyses on muscle coordination, such as those on the quality of reconstruction of muscle activations with the synergies and on the angle between the motor exploration and the synergy subspace.

A statistically significant baseline difference in the number of identified muscle synergies was observed between participants who received the cue and those who did not. While this difference represents a potential source of bias, it is unlikely to account for the main findings. Specifically, a lower initial number of synergies, as observed in the hint group, would be expected to increase the probability that voluntary actions or stochastic variability in muscle activation generate coordination patterns not well captured by the original synergy set, thereby reducing the quality of EMG reconstruction with baseline synergies and promoting exploration outside the synergy subspace. Contrary to this expectation, participants who received the cue showed improved EMG reconstruction using the original synergies and reduced exploration outside the synergy domain. These observations suggest that the reported effects cannot be explained by baseline differences in synergy number alone, but rather reflect changes in motor coordination strategies induced by the explicit cue. Nevertheless, the presence of a baseline difference should be acknowledged as a limitation, as it may contribute to inter-individual variability and warrants cautious interpretation of group-level comparisons.

Following the introduction of the cue, a significant increase in RT was observed. While RT can be influenced by various factors such as increased attentional load, task novelty, or caution in response to verbal instructions, the observed RT increase in those perturbed blocks in which the cue was not administered is most consistent with the engagement of explicit, deliberate strategies rather than nonspecific interference. In fact, attempting to recruit the hint muscle led to a beneficial effect on performance, resulting in a performance advantage rather than a disadvantage, as would be expected in the presence of interference. Thus, the elevation in RT, which persisted beyond the blocks in which the cue was administered, likely reflects a carry-over of strategy-driven planning rather than a disruptive effect of the cue itself. Supporting this interpretation, the RT increase was associated with improved task-relevant performance and higher activation of the targeted muscle, indicating functional benefit. Furthermore, post-experiment debriefing confirmed that participants consciously attempted to follow the cue. Overall, these observations, together with previous literature showing that explicit strategy deployment is generally accompanied by longer RTs during motor adaptation,19,36,65,66,67,68 support the conclusion that RT modulation reflects strategy engagement rather than cue interference. In contrast, the cue may represent an interference in those blocks in which it was administered, which were therefore excluded from statistical analyses.

While the observed effects were statistically reliable, we acknowledge that their magnitude and specificity warrant a cautious interpretation. In fact, some SES were small (<0.1), which does not necessarily imply negligible effects in this context. The linear mixed models with repeated measures across multiple blocks (nine blocks) explicitly model within-subject variability; therefore, the resulting SESs may appear smaller despite consistent underlying trends. Thus, the small SES values likely reflect that motor adaptation relies on subtle changes distributed across muscles and control processes, rather than on large, localized effects, and therefore should not be interpreted as a lack of practical relevance. Moreover, the variability inherent to motor behavior across repetitions and participants can further attenuate SES, despite consistent effects at the group level. Although the repeated-measures design and the resulting effective sample size support adequate statistical sensitivity, null effects, particularly those related to the formation of new muscle synergies, should be interpreted in light of the subtle and distributed nature of the underlying control processes rather than as definitive evidence for their absence. Overall, the present findings should be viewed as evidence consistent with the hypothesis that explicit processes recruit existing synergies, rather than as definitive proof of causal mechanisms.

Finally, the relatively modest sample size may have further limited statistical power to detect small or condition-specific effects, particularly interaction effects and changes related to the emergence of new muscle synergies. As a consequence, null results—especially in the incompatible surgery condition—should be interpreted with caution, and cannot be taken as conclusive evidence for the absence of such effects. Future studies with larger samples or extended training protocols will be required to strengthen causal inference and to assess whether explicit strategies can contribute to de novo synergy formation over longer timescales.

Experimental model and study participant details

Human participants

Thirty-six right-handed participants (mean age 25.7 years, SD 6.0, age range 19–43, 15 females, see Table 1 for participant demographics) were enrolled in the study after giving written informed consent. All participants had normal or corrected-to-normal vision and no history of neurological diseases or upper right limb injuries. All procedures were conducted in conformance with the Declaration of Helsinki and were approved by the Ethical Review Board of the University of Messina and the AOU ‘Gaetano Martino’ (Prot. 145-21 A-B, 30-11-2021, participants 1–18) and by the Ethical Review Board of the IRCCS Sicilia—Sezione Neurolesi ‘Bonino Pulejo’ (Prot.n.02/18, participants 19–36). Sex/gender was not included as an experimental factor in the present study, as the aim was to investigate general mechanisms of motor learning and muscle synergy recruitment rather than sex-related differences. Similarly, information regarding participants’ ancestry, race, and ethnicity was not collected. The study focused on fundamental sensorimotor learning mechanisms in healthy participants and was not designed to assess potential effects of ancestry, race, ethnicity, or sex/gender. Furthermore, the sample size was not powered to detect potential differences related to these factors.

Method details

Experimental setup

Participants sat on a gaming chair in front of a desktop. The hand and forearm were immobilized by an orthosis that was rigidly connected to a 6-axis force and torque transducer (Delta F/T Sensor; ATI Industrial Automation), fixed to the desktop (Figure 2A). The elbow was flexed at 90° and the and forearm was pronated. The chair height was adjusted such that the hand was at the height of the solar plexus when inserted in the orthosis. Car safety belts, attached to the chair, immobilized the participant’s torso and shoulders while they rested their forehead on an adjustable headrest.

Hand view was occluded by a black horizontal screen above the desktop over which a mirror was placed. Participants, who wore 3D shutter glasses (3D Vision P854, NVIDIA Corporation), viewed the tridimensional scene projected by a 21-inch LCD monitor through the mirror. The virtual scene was rendered by a 3D graphics card (Quadro Fx 4200, NVIDIA) on a PC workstation using custom software and updated at 120 Hz. Feedback of the amplitude and direction of the exerted force was provided in real time as the displacement of a spherical cursor, whose position was computed according to the instantaneous recorded force or EMG by a second dedicated data-acquisition PC workstation running a real-time operating system (MATLAB XPC) and transmitted to the first workstation through an Ethernet link using the UDP protocol. Tri-dimensional cursor motion was simulated in real time as a mass-spring-damper system, accelerated by the force applied by the participant on the orthosis, as previously described23 Briefly, the spring constant was set such that the force applied to maintain the cursor stationary at the target, distant 5 cm from the center of the palm, had a magnitude equal to 20% of the participant’s maximum voluntary force (MVF, see below). The mass was adjusted adaptively in the range 15–140 g as a sigmoidal function of the rate of change in the magnitude of the recorded force to maintain fast responses to changes in force while reducing the effect of the noise when the force was stationary. The damping constant was set to make the system critically damped.

Surface electromyographic (EMG) activity was recorded from fifteen muscles acting on the shoulder and elbow: brachioradialis (BracRad), biceps brachii long heads (BicLong) and short head (BicShort), pectoralis major (PectMaj), anterior deltoid (DeltA), middle deltoid (DeltM), posterior deltoid (DeltP), triceps brachii lateral head (TriLat) and long head (TriLong), infraspinatus (InfraSp), teres major (TerMaj), latissimus dorsi (LatDorsi), lower trapezius (TrapLow), middle trapezius (TrapMid), and upper trapezius (TrapSup). EMG activity was acquired at 1000 Hz with active bipolar surface electrodes (Delsys Trigno Wireless System, Boston, MA), bandpass filtered (20–450 Hz), and amplified with a gain of 1000. Participants’ skin was cleansed with alcohol and electrodes were placed based on recommendations from SENIAM69 and by palpating muscles during specific maneuvers70 to locate the muscle belly and orienting the electrodes along the main direction of the fibers. Force and EMG data were digitized at 1 kHz using an analog-to-digital PCI board (PCI-6229; National Instruments).

Data acquisition and analysis were made with custom software written in MATLAB 2009a and 2022a (MathWorks Inc., Natick, MA) and Java.

Experimental Protocol

After an initial familiarization with the isometric force generation task, participants performed two blocks of trials in force control, during which the cursor moved according to the force recorded by the force transducer (Figure 2B). For the remainder of the experiment, the cursor moved according to the force estimated, in real-time, from the EMG signals (EMG control).

In the first force control block, participants were asked to generate their maximum voluntary force (MVF) along one direction on the horizontal plane (-y, toward their chest). During this block, participants were encouraged to exert the maximum force with verbal reinforcements like ‘go’, or ‘more’.71

During the second force control block, participants were asked to move the virtual cursor accurately and quickly from a rest position to a target, appearing in one of twenty spatial locations, by applying isometric forces on the orthosis (Force Baseline Block in Figure 2B). At the beginning of each trial (rest phase), participants were asked to relax their right arm to maintain the cursor inside a transparent sphere at the center of the scene for 1 s. Successively, a transparent sphere appeared in one of the twenty target positions (target go event), placed at the vertices of a dodecahedron inscribed into a sphere, centered in the rest position, and whose radius was either 15% or 25% of the MVF. After a preview of the target position, which lasted 0.2 s in all blocks except those in which a cue was provided, where it lasted 1 s (see below), participants had 4 s to reach the target and stay within the target sphere, whose radius was 2% the MVF, for 0.2 s (static phase). The 4-s time, and the explicit instruction of participants to reach the target as accurately and as quickly as possible, created a sense of temporal pressure consistent with the measurement of reaction time (see below). Each target was repeated 3 times, for a total of 120 trials (20 targets × 2 radii × 3 repetitions). After this block, there was a 5 min pause to process the collected data and to construct the myoelectric controller implementing virtual surgeries. EMG and force data collected during the dynamic phase (i.e., the phase from the target go event until the first time the cursor gets inside the target, see Figures 2C and 2D) were used to calculate the matrix mapping EMG activations onto isometric force (EMG-to-force matrix), the synergy matrix, and the transformations defining the virtual surgeries (see below). The maximum value of each EMG signal collected during the execution phase (i.e., the phase from the target-go event to the end of the static phase), or the dynamic phase if the static phase was not completed, was used to normalize that signal for the whole experiment. All subsequent EMG control blocks consisted of 24 trials with targets at 20% MVF in random order within cycles of eight directions equally spaced in the horizontal plane (45° steps).

During the first EMG control block, the cursor position was not shown to the participant (EMG Hidden block, block 3). Since the suppression of the visual feedback prevented the participants from attempting to make corrections to compensate for any visual error, it allowed us to check the quality of the reconstruction of the endpoint force from the recorded EMG signals.

The following 21 EMG control blocks consisted of: 4 baseline blocks (Baseline unperturbed), one of which without cursor feedback; 13 virtual surgery blocks (Perturbed), one of which without cursor feedback; and 4 washout blocks (Washout unperturbed), one of which without cursor feedback. The blocks without cursor feedback (Hidden blocks in Figure 2B) were inserted to investigate the acquisition of a new feedforward control policy, as participants could not correct any error using visual feedback.

EMG-to-force mapping

Isometric generation of submaximal force allowed to approximate the relationship between the shoulder and elbow muscles activations and the force generated at the hand as linear23,72,73,74,75:(Equation 1)f=Hmwhere f is the tridimensional force vector generated by the participant, m is the 15-dimesional muscle activation vector (each dimension representing the activation of one of the 15 recorded muscles), and H is the EMG-to-force matrix that maps muscle activations onto generated forces (dimensions 3 × 15, see Figures 2A and 2C). The H matrix was estimated using multiple linear regressions of each applied force component, low-pass filtered (second-order Butterworth; 1 Hz cutoff), with EMG signals recorded during the dynamic phase of the force baseline block, low-pass filtered (second-order Butterworth; 1 Hz cutoff) and normalized to the maximum EMG activity recorded during the force baseline targets.

Muscle synergies

Muscle synergies were identified by decomposition of the EMG patterns recorded during the dynamic phase of the force baseline block:(Equation 2)m=Wcwhere m is the muscle activation vector, W is the matrix of spatial synergies, whose columns represent the individual synergies, and the vector c indicates the synergy activation coefficients. The NMF model assumes that muscle activation patterns can be expressed as a linear combination of a limited number of non-negative spatial modules (synergies), whose activation coefficients vary over time, under the constraint that both muscle weights and activation coefficients remain non-negative.

The non-negative matrix factorization (NMF)76 algorithm was applied to decompose the pre-processed EMG signals. The factorization was performed using an iterative multiplicative update algorithm initialized with random non-negative values and optimized to minimize the reconstruction error under non-negativity constraints. Each EMG signal was zero-centered to remove any signal offset, rectified, low-pass filtered (second-order Butterworth filter; 1 Hz cutoff frequency), baseline noise level subtracted, and normalized to the MVC. Processed EMG were also averaged within each trial to reduce computational time. Baseline noise was estimated during the rest phase at the beginning of the experiment and periodically throughout the experiment.

The algorithm for the synergy extraction was repeated 10 times for each number of synergies, from 1 to 15 (i.e., the number of recorded muscles), and the solution of the repetition with the best reconstruction quality (i.e., the highest R2) was retained. The number of synergies was selected according to two criteria8,23: 1) the smallest set that explained at least 90% of the data variation (R2 ≥ 0.9); 2) the number of synergies at which there was a change in the slope of the curve of the R2 value as a function of the number of synergies. If the two criteria identified different numbers of synergies, the set of synergies whose preferred directions were distributed more uniformly was retained. As the synergies represent a combination of the activities of muscles, the pulling force of each synergy (Figures 3B and 3D) is the combination of the pulling forces generated by each muscle, weighted by its contribution to the synergy (Figures 3E and 3F). Similar NMF-based muscle synergy extraction approaches have been adopted in recent studies to characterize neuromuscular coordination patterns and to inform data-driven modeling frameworks, including neuro-fuzzy inference systems and deep learning architectures for movement and fatigue prediction77,78

Post-processing validation was performed by applying the same algorithm to the processed EMG signals collected during the force baseline block, but without averaging within each trial. Poor reconstruction quality (R2 < 0.7), which may reflect larger within-trial variability or inconsistent muscle activation patterns, was used as an exclusion criterion. As a result, Participant 23 (Incompatible surgery without hint, R2 = 0.68) and Participant 31 (Compatible surgery without hint, R2 = 0.50) were excluded from further analyses (see Table 2). The remaining participants showed a robust reconstruction quality (mean ± SD R2 = 0.84 ± 0.05; range: 0.75–0.92).

The same algorithm was also implemented to extract the muscle synergies from data collected during the initial unperturbed EMG control blocks (blocks 4 to 6) and the last three perturbed blocks (blocks 17 to 19). These muscle synergies were then compared to detect specific changes in the muscle patterns occurring during adaptation to the perturbation (see below).

Virtual surgeries

Virtual surgeries simulate a rearrangement of the origins and insertions of the tendons of all the recorded muscles, providing visual feedback of the effect that such a tendon transfer surgery would have during the manipulation of an object. Virtual surgeries were performed by altering the EMG-to-force mapping as a rotation in the muscle space (H′ = HT, where T is an orthonormal rotation matrix). Therefore, virtual surgeries were defined based on subject-specific EMG-to-force mapping and synergies. Two types of virtual surgeries were constructed (Berger et al. 2013). Compatible surgeries were chosen as rotations in the subspace spanned by the synergies and not belonging to the null space of H, and incompatible surgeries as rotations that transformed a vector of the same synergy subspace into the null space of H outside to the synergy subspace. Therefore, despite both surgery types allowed the generation of any force along the 3D space with an appropriate muscle pattern (see Figures 3G and 3I), incompatible surgeries were constructed such that the muscle activation, obtained by combinations of synergies, could only generate forces along a single direction on the horizontal plane (HTW direction, Figure 3H), while compatible surgeries allowed the generation of force along all directions by recombining the existing synergies (Figure 3L). In practical terms, after compatible surgeries the pulling directions of the original muscle synergies still spanned the horizontal plane, allowing forces to be generated in all directions through recombination of the synergies, i.e., by selecting different synergy activation coefficients. Conversely, incompatible surgeries aligned the pulling forces of all synergies along a single direction on the horizontal plane, making it impossible to reach other target directions without generating new muscle patterns outside the original synergy domain, i.e., that could not be generated by any selection of synergy activation coefficients. Therefore, if the muscle patterns were generated by synergy combinations, participants who practiced an incompatible surgery were required not only to adapt the synergy coefficients (c in Equation 2), as it would be for participants who practiced a compatible surgery, but also to learn new synergies (W in Equation 2), which differed from the existing synergies, identified from the data collected during the baseline block, to move the cursor away from the HTW direction. Hence, overcoming compatible surgeries can be considered as a motor adaptation task, while overcoming incompatible surgeries requires de novo motor skill learning.19,20

Details on the approach used to define compatible and incompatible virtual surgeries are described elsewhere.23 Briefly, virtual surgeries were defined as orthonormal rotations in the M-dimensional muscle activation space, transforming EMG patterns according to a matrix T (with TTT = I) that preserves the pattern norm while altering the muscle–force mapping.

We identified three subspaces: the synergy subspace (the dimension was the number of synergies N), spanned by the columns of W, the force space (dimension D = 2), spanned by the columns of H corresponding to the horizontal plane, and the null space of H, denoted N(H) (dimension M − D). Because typically N > D, the synergy and the null spaces share a common component (dimension N − D), characterized by the singular value decomposition of WoN = USVT, where Wo and N are orthonormal bases of the synergy and null spaces, respectively. From this we derived Wc = Wo[u1, …,uN-D] and Nc = N[v1, …,vN-D], as orthonormal bases of the common subspace, and Wnc = Wo[uN-D+1, …,uN] and Nnc = Wo[vN-D+1, …,vN], as the synergy and null components outside this intersection. Compatible virtual surgeries (Tc) rotate vectors within the span of Wnc preserving force generation through the same synergies, whereas incompatible virtual surgeries (Ti) rotate vectors from Wnc into Nnc, preventing force production along those directions.

This procedure ensures that both Tc and Ti are true orthonormal norm-preserving transformations, differing only in whether they maintain or disrupt the synergy–force mapping. Unlike previous studies involving virtual surgeries,19,23 in which participants had visual feedback of the force generated only along the horizontal plane, in this study, feedback of the force along the vertical direction was also provided to participants. Therefore, the direction in muscle activation space generating an endpoint force in the vertical direction, which constituted a component of the null space of H in previous experiments,19,23 was not considered here for the construction of the perturbation matrix T. Thus, the surgeries were designed to only perturb the horizontal components of the H matrix, without affecting its vertical components.

Perturbation matrices were individualized based on each participant’s baseline EMG to ensure comparable difficulty across participants. Specifically, the rotation matrices T were constructed such that the changes in the individual muscles required to compensate for the surgery, similar for each subject. Therefore, the rotation angle of the compatible surgery was chosen such that a difficulty index (Idiff, see Table 2), defined as the average change across muscles and force targets in muscle activity required to perform the task after the surgery with a minimum-norm, non-negative muscle pattern,23 was similar across all participants regardless of the perturbation to which they were exposed. Thus, the task difficulty index was used to confirm that the perturbations were effectively matched across subjects, despite structural differences.

As each incompatible surgery defined a unique direction on the horizontal plane, along which the cursor could be displaced by recruiting muscles synergies (the HTW direction), targets were subdivided in two sets (Figure 3M): targets closer to the HTW direction, which required muscle patterns only slightly different from those that could be generated with the original muscle synergies, and targets farther from the HTW direction, which required muscle patterns significantly different from those that could be generated with original muscle synergies. This subdivision could not be made for compatible surgeries, and therefore the following analyses involving the separation into closer and farther targets were performed only on data collected from the participants who were exposed to incompatible surgeries.

Participants in both groups, either receiving a hint (participants 1–16, see below) or not (participants 17–32), were randomly assigned to one of two subgroups according to the type of virtual surgery to which they would be exposed, i.e., compatible or incompatible with the original muscle synergies.

Cue on the muscle most effective to overcome the virtual surgery

Half of the participants who were exposed to either a compatible or incompatible surgery were provided with a cue about the muscle, for each target, whose pulling direction, altered by a virtual surgery, was closest to the direction of the target, as an explicit instruction (“hint”) on how to modify their muscle patterns to overcome the perturbation. Thus, participants who received the hint were aware of the muscles most effective in compensating for the perturbation.

Participants who received the hint and those who did not receive it were enrolled in the study at different times, but they performed the same protocol (except for the blocks in which the hint was provided, see below).

After the definition of the perturbation T and consequently the identification of the perturbed pulling directions HT, the muscle whose pulling direction showed the highest projected component along the target direction, was selected as the cued muscle for that direction for participants in the incompatible with hint or in the compatible with hint groups (Figure 6). If the same muscle was to be selected for different targets, such a muscle was assigned only to the target for which it contributed the most, i.e., with the highest projected component (e.g., muscle 4 in Figure 6). That muscle and target were then removed, and the procedure was repeated for the remaining muscles and targets. This led to the definition of a unique set of eight muscles (‘hint-muscles’) each used to provide a cue of the most effective muscle required to overcome the perturbation for a specific target. The selection of muscles was not influenced by the structure of the muscle synergies, nor by their pulling directions or the type and structure of the perturbation to which the participant would be exposed, but solely by the pulling directions of the muscles. Therefore, any differences observed between participants exposed to compatible or incompatible surgeries can be attributed to the distinct physiological processes involved in adapting to the two types of surgeries.

The cue was first provided during the second block of exposure to the surgery, after approximately 2 min of practicing the perturbation. At this point, adaptation or learning processes had not yet been fully engaged, but these processes were subsequently initiated following the provision of the cue.

The hint on the muscle most effective to overcome the perturbation for each target was provided only during three of the 12 perturbation blocks, i.e., blocks 9, 12, and 16 (Hint blocks in Figure 3B). The first hint at block 9 was delivered after participants had experienced the perturbation for one block (block 8), allowing them to develop an initial baseline of uncued adaptation. Subsequent hints at blocks 12 and 16 were spaced to maintain the effect of explicit guidance without overly disrupting the adaptation process, and to provide participants with opportunities to respond to the cue across different stages of the exposure. Including blocks without a cue allows the effect of the cue on the adaptation process to be tested by comparing performance across groups under the same trial conditions. The selected timing thus balances providing sufficient explicit information to influence performance while preserving the ability to observe uncued adaptation and the differential effects of explicit cues.

However, in this study, the aim was not to instruct participants to selectively modulate a single muscle, but to elicit explicit strategies to cope with the perturbation. Participants were informed that the perturbation affected the force generated by each muscle and that activating certain muscles could facilitate task performance. While the instruction referred to individual muscles, recruitment of any single muscle was considered a consequence of engaging an explicit strategy rather than a primary target. The effectiveness of explicit strategies was assessed through reaction time and performance measures, rather than the precise increase of the target muscle’s activation.

At the beginning of each trial of the Hint blocks, participants were provided with a preview of the position of the target. This preview lasted 1 s, while it lasted only 0.2 s in the other blocks in which the cue was not provided or in all the blocks performed by participants in the incompatible and compatible without hint groups. During this interval, both the virtual spheres in the rest position and in the target were displayed, but if participants attempted to reach the target, moving the cursor outside the rest position sphere, the target disappeared. Thus, participants were asked to remain relaxed and not to exert any endpoint force when they were informed about the hint-muscle that would mostly help them to compensate for the surgery when reaching the visible target. The information was provided by an experimenter who simultaneously touched the hint-muscle with a small steel bar and pronounced the muscle’s Latin name. The bar touched the hint-muscle close to the electrode placed on the muscle, as it was previously verified that the steel bar did not introduce any artifacts in the EMG signal, but did not directly touch the electrode, to avoid noise due to movement, artifacts, or electrode-skin interface in the EMG signal. After providing the hint, the rest position sphere disappeared, and participants were asked to try to reach the target while increasing the activity of the target muscle. As the different preview times and the presence of the cue may be confounding factors that alter the natural behavior of participants, data collected during the blocks in which the hint was provided (blocks 9, 12, and 16) were excluded in all participants, with or without the cue, from the statistical analyses.

Task performance and reaction time

The task performance was quantified based on reaching success (modeled with a Bernoulli distribution) and angular deviation of the cursor movement direction at the first velocity peak with respect to the target direction, treated as a continuous variable. The first velocity peak was calculated as the first maximum in the cursor trajectory along the horizontal plane, detected after the target-go event. The cursor movement direction ϑcursor was computed as the direction of the regression line fitted to the cursor position in the [-50 + 50] ms around the first velocity peak, while the target direction ϑtarget was computed as the direction of the line connecting the cursor position at the first velocity peak and the target. The angular deviation was then computed as abs(ϑtarget-ϑcursor).

The reaction time was estimated as the time between the target-go signal and the cursor movement onset, which was computed as the time when the cursor velocity crossed a trial-specific threshold, set to five times the maximum cursor velocity observed during the time interval from 0.5 s before the target ‘go’ signal until the signal itself in each trial.19

Muscle synergy reconstruction of data from perturbed blocks

The changes of synergistic organization in muscle patterns were assessed by computing the quality of reconstruction (R2) of EMG data collected during the perturbed blocks as a combination of the synergies extracted during the initial force control blocks.

The muscle patterns for each block were reconstructed using the NMF algorithm, which was initialized with the synergies extracted from the EMG control blocks, and updated solely for the combination coefficients.79

The Specificity Index was computed to detect the recruitment of muscles in new synergies after practicing virtual surgeries

Overcoming incompatible virtual surgeries requires coordinating muscles in novel patterns that differ from those that can be generated as combinations of the original synergies. To detect the emergence of these new muscle patterns, we introduced a Specificity Index (Isyn). This index represents the ratio between the sum of the number of muscle synergies specific to the baseline unperturbed blocks (blocks 4 to 6) and number of muscle synergies specific to the last three perturbed blocks (blocks 17 to 19), and the total number of synergies extracted from these two sets of conditions, including shared synergies and condition-specific synergies. A larger Isyn indicates a larger number of synergies differing between the unperturbed blocks and the last three perturbed blocks. In this analysis, we focused on muscle synergies extracted from EMG-control baseline blocks rather than force-control blocks. This choice was made because the target positions in the EMG-controlled blocks were the same as during the perturbation, allowing for a more effective comparison of changes in the recruited synergies.

The number of shared dimensions was detected through a bootstrap procedure aimed at distinguishing whether differences between synergy sets, extracted from unperturbed baseline blocks or the last three perturbed blocks, were due to noise or structural differences. This procedure assesses the distributions of principal angles between the subspaces spanned by the synergies extracted from unperturbed baseline blocks and by the synergies extracted from the last three perturbed blocks, and accounts for noise to quantify the significant differences between these subspaces.80 Only the subspace dimensions with principal angles significantly larger than those expected from noise were considered non-shared. To estimate the noise distribution, bootstrap distributions of principal angles were generated by dividing each dataset into two subsets of trials, extracting synergies from each subset, and computing principal angles between the synergies extracted from the two subsets. The distributions of principal angles between synergies extracted from each dataset (baseline and perturbed blocks) were then combined into a single distribution. To do this, a unique reference subspace was generated, composed of orthogonal vectors equal in number to the identified synergies. Each dataset’s subspace was then simulated by randomly rotating this reference subspace according to the observed distribution of principal angles, and the principal angles between the resulting transformed subspaces were computed. The bootstrap process was repeated 500 times and the 95th percentile angle (ϑ95) was retained. Principal angles between the synergies extracted from unperturbed baseline blocks and the synergies extracted from the last three perturbed blocks were then compared to ϑ95. The number of shared synergy components Nsh was defined by counting the principal angles that were smaller than their corresponding ϑ95, while the number of specific synergy components (Nsp) of the unperturbed baseline block (Nsp,bl) or the last three perturbed blocks (Nsp,pt) was defined as the difference between the actual number of synergies N and Nsh: Nsp = N-Nsh.

The SI was then calculated as the ratio between the total number of specific synergies, both from the unperturbed baseline blocks and the last three perturbed blocks, and the total number of synergies:(Equation 3)Isyn=Nsp,bl+Nsp,ptNbl+NptWhere, in the denominator, Nbl and Npt are respectively the number of synergies extracted during the baseline or the perturbed blocks. Thus, Isyn = 0 if no specific synergies are detected neither in the baseline nor in the perturbed blocks, and Isyn = 1 if all the detected synergies in both the baseline and perturbed blocks are specific.

Synergy exploration

Muscle activation patterns were averaged over the execution phase, when the participant successfully reached the target, or the interval between the target ‘go’ phase and the trial end, otherwise, to obtain a representative muscle activation vector for each trial.

Motor exploration was quantified as the difference between consecutive activation vectors corresponding to consecutive trials to the same target. The angle between this difference vector and the subspace spanned by the muscle synergies extracted from the baseline (unperturbed) blocks was then computed to assess the synergy exploration. Finally, exploration values were averaged across all trials belonging to the same block.

Conceptually, this metric captures how participants modify their muscle activation patterns from one trial to the next, and whether such adjustments remain consistent with the coordination patterns observed at baseline. Small angles indicate that trial-to-trial changes predominantly lie within the subspace spanned by baseline muscle synergies, reflecting exploration through re-weighting or recombination of existing synergies. Larger angles, instead, indicate exploration outside the baseline synergy domain, suggesting the recruitment of muscle activation patterns that are poorly explained by pre-existing synergies.

To evaluate whether the sample size was sufficient given the repeated-measures design, we quantified the effective sample size by accounting for within-subject dependence. Specifically, we estimated the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) from a random-intercept linear mixed-effects model fitted to reaching success, and computed the corresponding design effect (DE):DE=1+(ntrials−1)·ICCWhere ntrials represents the number of trials.

The effective sample size was then defined as followsES=(nsubj·ntrials)DEWhere nsubj represents the number of participants in each condition. This approach accounts for the loss of independence induced by repeated observations while retaining the gain in precision provided by multiple trials per participant. Across conditions, the resulting ES substantially exceeded the nominal number of participants per group, supporting adequate statistical sensitivity.

To ensure the validity of our models, we tested for normality of the residual distributions using QQ plots (MATLAB function qqplot) and histograms (MATLAB function histogram), and confirmed through visual inspection that they did not show substantial deviations from normality, which supports the validity of the parametric assumptions underlying the linear mixed-effects models.
