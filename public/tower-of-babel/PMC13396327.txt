Descriptive Analysis of Retrospective Observational Real-World Evidence of a Monoclonal Anti-Amyloid Therapy Program in a Wide Catchment Specialty Memory Clinic
Authors: Tara C Carlisle, Samantha K Holden, Stefan Sillau, Jennifer R Unger, Marrisa Lafreniere, Brianne M Bettcher, Joie Molden, Muthiah Nachiappan, Jody Tanabe, Kavita V Nair, Victoria S Pelak
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionAnti-amyloid therapy (AAT) protocol complexity may widen specialty dementia care access gaps. The University of Colorado (CU) Advanced Therapy in Neurodegenerative Disorders (ATND) Clinic is within the CU-Memory Disorders Clinic (CU-MDC). The aim of this study is to report initial CU-ATND real-world evidence (RWE).MethodsPatients who completed CU-ATND Clinic intake paperwork were included (n = 123) and compared to CU-MDC (n = 1100) seen between April 2024 to June 2025. Demographic information was included for both clinics. Retrospective observational data on CU-ATND Clinic patients including work-up, decisions about pursing AAT, and adverse events at the time of data extraction was described.ResultsAAT status included patient declined (37.4%) or deferred (8.1%), clinically excluded (14.6%), undergoing work-up (8.1%), consented for infusions (26.0%), completed infusions (0.8%), and stopped infusions (4.1%). CU-ATND Clinic patients had a median age of 73 years and were 50.4% women with the majority white and urban-dwelling similar to the CU-MDC. AAT candidacy steps and decision duration varied. AAT-related adverse events were managed without extended inpatient admission or persistent effects.ConclusionMany care disparities were present prior to CU-ATND referral. The CU-ATND RWE highlights successes and provides insight into barriers impacting access.Keywords: Alzheimer disease, Amyloid, Amyloid related imaging abnormality, Access to health care, Systems of care, Care coordination, Real-world evidence

Key Summary Points

Why carry out this study?Alzheimer’s disease (AD) clinical trials including the landmark trials for anti-amyloid therapy (AAT) insufficiently represent the real-world diversity of patients with AD; care for patients with dementia including AD dementia has baseline disparate access for specific populations especially those with higher baseline risk.The relatively new availability of AAT with considerable complexity of care management has the potential for widening gaps already present in dementia care.The goal of this study was to better understand barriers to dementia care access including for complex AAT protocols and present preliminary real-world evidence of AAT adverse events.What was learned from the study?AAT-related adverse events were similar to those seen in the landmark clinical trials with management achieved without extended inpatient admission or persistent effects.Baseline referral bias to dementia-specialty clinics impact access to dementia care in general and therefore may impact access to AAT, therefore highlighting systems of care (SoC) vulnerabilities with the most potential for improvement.Further real-world evidence including a variety of AAT programs and experiences will facilitate refinement to SoC to ameliorate care gaps and improve processes for evidence-based and safety-minded care.

Introduction

Alzheimer’s disease (AD) clinical trials typically fail to recruit representative populations that mirror real-world individuals with AD, negatively affecting the generalizability of therapeutics investigated in AD trials for clinical use [1]. This major limitation includes the landmark trials for the current monoclonal antibodies directed at amyloid, or anti-amyloid therapy (AAT), lecanemab [2] and donanemab [3].

Despite appropriate use recommendations for the available AATs [4, 5], practice patterns regarding candidacy requirements [6] and program protocols including safety monitoring [7] are variable; the published literature is not fully representative as many programs have not reported their protocols or outcomes. Systems-of-care (SoC) challenges are unique to every site given resource and hurdle differences, therefore much can be learned from real-world evidence (RWE). For example, centers vary in amyloid related imaging abnormality (ARIA) risk tolerance including: (1) some allowing [8–10] and others excluding [11] patients with apolipoprotein (APOE) ε4 homozygosity, (2) many excluding patients on anticoagulation but not all [10], and (3) some excluding patients with magnetic resonance (MR)-compatible pacemakers given concerns about emergency MR imaging access but not others [12]. While not every dementia healthcare setting has the same resources, opportunities, obstacles, or complexities, individual AAT programs can benefit from RWE of different models [9, 13–17] for site-specific adaptations.

Thorough analyses of RWE during the AAT post-market surveillance period are essential [18] to optimize protocols over time. Multiple AAT programs have reported RWE [8–11], which communicate the integration and adaptation of AAT protocols across multiple settings. Compared to the landmark trials [2, 3], ARIA outcomes are lower [8, 11] or higher [9, 10] with patients with mild dementia showing more risk compared to those with mild cognitive impairment (MCI) [10]. RWE infusion-related reactions have been similar to those seen in the AAT trials [11] or higher [9, 10], but improved with pre-dosing protocol adjustments [9, 10]. However, these prior RWE studies highlight ongoing concerns for limited access, including populations that are overwhelmingly white [8–10, 19] and non-Hispanic [8, 10, 19], as well as highly educated [8, 9]. Female representation [8–11, 19] does not reflect the disproportionate AD prevalence burden for women [20]. United States claims data for patients prescribed AAT show similar disparities [21–24], but also highlight the vast majority of AAT recipients reside in urban settings [21–23] with higher than average incomes [24].

The goal of this study was to describe initial experiences in the University of Colorado (CU) Advanced Therapy in Neurodegenerative Disorders (CU-ATND) Clinic. The CU-ATND Clinic is housed in the academic subspecialty CU-Memory Disorders Clinic (CU-MDC), which is part of a greater quaternary care center with a nine-state catchment area in the United States Rocky Mountain region. This unique patient population and clinic model may help understand systems of care (SoC) barriers limiting access that can be applied to other settings. Ultimately, the goal of this RWE study is to contribute to a better understanding of the successes and challenges of various AAT protocols developed across multiple care delivery models to improve pathways in the future.

Methods

Study Population

Patients seen in the CU-ATND Clinic from the establishment date, April 26, 2024, until June 24, 2025 were included in the retrospective review. The CU-ATND Clinic is housed in the CU-MDC located in Denver, CO, staffed by CU School of Medicine Neurology Department clinical faculty in collaboration with the UCHealth healthcare system. The CU-MDC core providers include five Behavioral Neurology & Neuropsychiatry (BNNP) board-certified subspecialist neurologists, a dual-trained neurologist-psychiatrist, a Neurodevelopmental Disabilities specialist, two advanced practice providers, up to two BNNP fellows per year, and seven Neuropsychologists including two postdoctoral fellows. The CU-ATND Clinic had two AAT prescribers over the study period.

All patients seen in the CU-ATND Clinic are referred specifically for AAT consideration and were included in the analysis regardless of whether infusions were initiated. Only one patient seen in the CU-ATND Clinic was excluded in the analysis, as the work-up and therapy initiation was performed at a referring site prior to considering transferring care but they ultimately opted to receive care elsewhere. CU-MDC comparisons were extracted from the electronic medical record (EMR) using SlicerDicer during the same period (April 26, 2024–June 24, 2025) and included new patient encounters in the primary Denver-based CU-MDC and affiliated UCHealth satellite clinics in Boulder and Lone Tree. CU-MDC encounters with the Neurodevelopmental Disabilities specialist and within the Multidisciplinary Traumatic Brain Injury Clinic were excluded.

Demographics

Demographics for every CU-ATND patient are collected at the initial visit, including age at initial visit, early- versus late-onset AD, cognitive impairment stage per referring provider, self-reported race and ethnicity, urban–rural residency, insurance coverage, referral source, and dementia syndrome subtype. Urban–rural residency was determined via self-reported zip code to determine county and then county-level definitions of rurality available at the time of initial assessment from Colorado Department of Health Care Policy and Financing (https://hcpf.colorado.gov/) and Wyoming Department of Administration & Information Economic Analysis Division (http://eadiv.state.wy.us/).

Clinical Measures

Every CU-ATND Clinic patient completes clinical assessment tools with the assistance of collateral if present, including: Functional Activities Questionnaire (FAQ [25]), Quick Dementia Rating Scale (QDRS [26]), Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9 [27]), Generalized Anxiety Disorder-7 (GAD-7 [28]), and Quality of Life in AD (QoL-AD [29]). If not performed in the prior 6 months, Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA [30]) is performed unless a commercial payor requires an alternative screen. Activities of daily living (ADL) screen-defined cognitive staging is defined by initial visit scores on two measures (i.e., MCI for FAQ score < 9 [25]; moderate dementia for QDRS total score > 12.5 [26]).

Clinical Results

Standard-of-care work-up for AAT consideration in the CU-ATND Clinic includes AD biomarker confirmation [e.g., cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) and/or amyloid positron emission tomography (PET); serum-based AD biomarkers not accepted during study period], bleeding risk assessments [i.e., international normalized ratio (INR), platelet count], baseline ARIA MRI findings (e.g., microhemorrhages, superficial siderosis, Fazekas score [31]), and APOE ε4 status. Patients that meet criteria for early-onset AD (EOAD) are also generally referred to genetic counseling to consider testing for monogenic autosomal dominant AD (ADAD) and frontotemporal lobar dementia (FTLD) given increasing evidence for common pathways across neurodegenerative disorders [32]. The standard CSF assessment for the CU-ATND Clinic is the Roche Diagnostics Alzheimer Disease Evaluation (ADEVL) [33]. Some patients have alternative CSF testing performed at outside facilities prior to the initial CU-ATND visit. Amyloid PET positivity is determined by visual inspection and centiloid values (not reported in this study). Key dates in clinical assessment are also collected, including first CU-MDC visit, initial CU-ATND Clinic visit, Brain Board presentation, follow-up CU-ATND Clinic visit after work-up completion and possible consent, decision to not proceed with AAT, exclusion based on work-up, and first AAT infusion. Once AAT infusions are started, presence of infusion-related reactions are documented with each infusion and then entered into the database after the first three lecanemab and five donanemab infusions. Timing of ARIA outcomes is based on regularly scheduled ARIA MRI per monitoring protocols or any additional scans performed when concerning symptoms were reported. ARIA is further subdivided by type (e.g., ARIA-E for edema/effusion, ARIA-H for hemorrhage), severity, and whether symptoms are present.

Brain Board

The Brain Board was created with advent of the ATND Clinic. It is an interdisciplinary group created for the purpose of the AAT program that reviews candidacy and AAT appropriate use. Brain Board members include CU-MDC/ATND physicians and APPs, neuropsychology, and neuroradiology. The inclusion and exclusion criteria for AAT consideration for the ATND Clinic were determined by consensus within the BNNP Section (Table 1). The Brain Board reviews inclusion and exclusion criteria, AD subtype and severity, scores on ATND Clinic forms (i.e., MoCA, FAQ, QDRS, PHQ-9, GAD-7, QoL-AD), interpretation of neuropsychological testing results if performed, APOE ε4 status, and AD-related biomarker results including review of amyloid PET images and quantification if performed. During Brain Board, ARIA MRIs are reviewed in detail to assess for ARIA risk and vascular disease contribution. The Brain Board is a collaborative case conference and any member can express concerns that may impact eligibility or AAT care.

Descriptive Analysis and Statistics

Demographic and clinical data are presented as median with interquartile range (IQR) or number of patients and percentage. Descriptive analysis was performed on the entire study populations and specific subgroups within the CU-ATND Clinic per predetermined criteria. The baseline demographics include all patients seen in the CU-ATND Clinic with the CU-MDC as a comparison for the study period. The baseline screen and questionnaire scores, AD biomarker assessments, and bleeding/ARIA risk include all patients seen in the CU-ATND Clinic for the study period; additionally, descriptive analysis was also performed for patients who met criteria for EOAD. Durations to key work-up points and decisions for AAT includes all patients who received at least one infusion at the time of data extraction. As a result, descriptive analysis of the baseline clinical data has more patients who consented for treatment (n = 39) compared to work-up timelines that includes only patients who had at least one infusion performed at the time of data extraction (n = 33).

Differences between percentages were assessed using chi-square/Fisher’s exact association test. Differences between medians were tested with the Wilcoxon rank sum or Kruskal–Wallis tests. The p values for exact tests were estimated using simulation (n = 100,000). A Lexis plot was made to display the timing of key events for each patient. Kaplan–Meier plot for time to event outcomes was made for stopping therapy prematurely. Kaplan–Meier plots were also made for ARIA events (i.e., total ARIA, ARIA-E, ARIA-H, and ARIA-E/-H). For Kaplan–Meier plots, patients who were eligible for the specified event but never experienced the event were censored at the last date for which information existed. Statistical software packages included SAS 9.4, STATA 17, R 4.5.1 with the “EPR” package. Univariate, two-sided alpha was set to 0.05 unless otherwise stated.

Ethical Approval

Ethical approval was reviewed by the Colorado Multiple Institutional Review Board (COMIRB) that determined that this observational study investigating standard-of-care and associated outcomes was exempt (Protocol #24—2013); as a result, consent for participation and publication was not obtained for this retrospective observational study. This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Results

Baseline Demographics of CU-ATND Clinic

The CU-ATND Clinic population (n = 123) baseline demographics (Table 2) highlight that the patients are generally older but with over-representation of patients with EOAD based on published estimates (4–6% of AD) [34]. Women were under-represented based on the higher baseline AD prevalence in females [20]. The CU-ATND Clinic had a low representation from rural counties (4.9%), but with relative over-representation in the patient deferred status (20.0%). Most primary insurance coverage included Medicare fee-for-service (FFS) followed by Medicare Advantage plans. Referrals were mostly from the CU-MDC, with no referrals from the CU Seniors Clinic or outside systems advancing to initiation of infusion therapy during the period assessed. Most patients were at an MCI stage, but referrals for mild dementia were also common. Although some patients were referred with moderate dementia, communication with the referring physicians and APPs led to improving quality of potentially eligible individuals. Agreement between the referral stage and stage determined by functional screens was high. Most patients had amnestic syndromes followed by atypical/mixed and mixed amnestic with dysexecutive presentations.

In comparison, new patients seen in the CU-MDC (Table 2) over the same period (n = 1100) have a similar median age (74 years, range 41–100), similar female representation (53.9%), and largely from urban counties (96.5%). Self-reported race had slightly greater representation of underrepresented groups (i.e., Black: n = 52, 4.7%; Asian: n = 14, 1.2%; Native American: n = 2, 0.2%; Other: n = 25, 2.2%) and for self-reported ethnicity (n = 77, 6.8% Hispanic), but was still overwhelmingly white (n = 946, 83.6%). Primary insurance coverage was primarily Medicare FFS followed by Medicare Advantage, with lesser representation with commercial payers, Medicaid, and others (e.g., Tricare, self-pay, hospital discounted), showing generally more Medicare coverage for the CU-ATND Clinic.

The baseline demographics were further subdivided by AAT candidacy status (Table 2). Lecanemab was the AAT used for the Holding Infusions and Stopped Infusions statuses. For the Completed Infusions status, lecanemab was the initial agent followed by washout and then donanemab. The predominance of lecanemab versus donanemab was driven by Food and Drug Administration (FDA) and healthcare system-specific Pharmacy and Therapeutics approval timelines. There were various communicated reasons (sometimes multiple) for declining treatment, including reported perception that benefits did not outweigh risks in general (n = 6) as well as specifically for APOE ε4 homozygotes (n = 12), younger women (n = 1), and in the setting of suspected or biomarker-supported co-pathology or other contributions contributing to cognitive decline (e.g., n = 2 for Lewy body, n = 1 for suspected Limbic-predominant Age-associated TDP-43 Encephalopathy, n = 1 for alcohol-associated); other concerns included not wanting infusions (n = 1), treatment protocol burden (n = 8), impact on travel (n = 2), financial concerns (n = 5), unknown risk with vascular malformations (n = 2), declining atrial device placement as anticoagulation alternative (n = 1), and opting to continue anti-CD20 immunotherapy not accepted per protocol parameters (n = 1). Reasons for deferring or postponing treatment included concerns about side effects (n = 1), burden (n = 2), financial costs (n = 2), travel plans (n = 1), travel distance (n = 2), and recovering from other medical conditions (n = 2); some patients wanted more time to consider AAT including discussing further with family and waiting for more data to be available (n = 2). There were various reasons for patients not meeting clinical criteria for AAT including being at a moderate stage of dementia at the initial visit (n = 2) or that progressed during work-up (n = 1) and one patient suspected to be a moderate stage per clinical suspicion despite caregiver-reported functional screens. Some were clinically excluded based on presence of > 4 microhemorrhages (n = 3) or any superficial siderosis (n = 4), inability to tolerate MRIs (n = 1), and being started on anticoagulation (n = 1). Exclusion also occurred due to amyloid negativity (n = 2), absence of measurable cognitive impairment on formal neuropsychological testing (n = 1), and active metastatic cancer (n = 1).

Baseline Clinical Characteristics of CU-ATND Clinic

The CU-ATND Clinic baseline clinical characteristics via questionnaires, cognitive screens, and clinical results (Table 3) and ARIA risk assessment (Table 4) showed differences only in INR across the AAT candidacy status groups which did not have clinical significance. The presence of microhemorrhages and superficial siderosis will be biased higher in the clinically excluded population by nature of the subgroup definitions, therefore this analysis was not performed. Within the patients with EOAD, there were various monogenic testing statuses (Table 4) including one patient that was heterozygous for a presenilin 1 (PSEN1) c.275GC (p.Cys92Ser) mutation and another that was heterozygous for an annexin A11 (ANXA11) c.89C > G (p.Pro30Arg) variant.

Pathway to AAT Consideration in CU-ATND Clinic

Despite having a protocol for AAT consideration for the CU-ATND Clinic, the initial pathway to AAT consideration is variable (Fig. 1A) likely in part due to the regulatory approval of AAT leading to an initial catch-up period where multiple patients pursued AAT after long-established diagnoses of AD as well as different practice styles within the CU-MDC group prior to referral to the CU-ATND Clinic. The CU-ATND Clinic does not use hard cut-offs for cognitive screens, given that this approach would exclude patients with lower educational attainment and language-based or mixed presentations. However, many of the major commercial payors have specific cut-offs listed in the medical benefit drug policies. Although the CU-ATND Clinic uses cognitive screens in the work-up, greater importance was placed on functional questionnaires to guide discussions about current stage and therefore AAT candidacy. Additionally, not every patient had formal neuropsychological testing performed, but if the driving etiology was unclear or presence of measurable cognitive impairment was questioned, referrals were placed after the initial CU-ATND Clinic visit that would sometimes add to the work-up duration. Neuropsychological testing was deferred if there was a very clear clinical history, the neurobehavioral examination supported an AD syndrome, functional questionnaires supported MCI or mild dementia level of severity, and AD biomarker confirmation.

Most patients who started on AAT continued the therapy plan (Fig. 1B). There were multiple different reasons for stopping AAT (Fig. 1C), however, many were related to ARIA events (n = 3), which are discussed in the next section ("CU-ATND Infusion Characteristics and Side Effects").

Durations between key CU-ATND timepoints (e.g., first Memory Disorder Clinic visit, initial CU-ATND visit, Brain Board presentation, follow-up CU-ATND visit, first infusion) for patients who started infusions did not significantly differ by urban–rural location, insurance coverage (e.g., commercial vs. others), onset (i.e., early versus late), or clinical syndrome (Table 5). Several patients had not yet had the first infusion at the time of data extraction, but work-up was fully completed and consent performed after shared decision-making (n = 5 for lecanemab, n = 1 for donanemab; Tables 2, 3, and 4 vs. Table 5). Patients with EOAD trended towards longer duration from initial ATND visit to first infusion. Patients with EOAD were often referred to genetic counseling to consider monogenic causes of AD, therefore creating an additional work-up step that may have delayed infusion start times. Of note, commercial payors can have more burdensome requirements for AAT approval, but payor status did not have any differences in durations; additionally, processes quickly improved once major roadblocks were identified [i.e., Clinical Dementia Rating (CDR) Scale [35] requirement]. Durations between initial ATND clinic visits and the decision to decline AAT or being clinically excluded from AAT did not significantly differ based on urban–rural location, APOE ε4 status, onset, or syndrome (Table 5), but APOE ε4 homozygotes trended towards declining AAT faster. There was greater variability and a trend for longer durations for the non-amnestic presentations. A small number of patients seen in the CU-ATND Clinic lived in rural locations (n = 1 for infusions initiated, n = 2 for patient declined, and n = 1 for clinically excluded); notably, both patients who lived in rural locations that declined AAT did so at the time of the initial CU-ATND visit with provided reasons being the APOE ε4 homozygote status.

CU-ATND Infusion Characteristics and Side Effects

Infusion-related reactions were similar to those reported in prior RWE studies (Fig. 2A) [9–11]. The initial protocol for documenting infusion-related reactions in the CU-ATND Clinic was that the presence of reactions were documented with each infusion but only entered into the database after the first three lecanemab and five donanemab infusions as the highest risk infusions. As a result, the number of possible full documentation events are reported (Fig. 2A). One patient received brief emergency department care for a grade 3 reaction to the first donanemab infusion, but did not have persistent symptoms. This patient had transitioned off lecanemab to donanemab following a 5-week washout; donanemab was previously unavailable due to formulary approval delays and was preferred by the patient per convenience. One patient on lecanemab reported persistent cough documented as a delayed infusion-reaction that was treated with steroids by urgent care.

There were seven separate ARIA events involving five different patients who were mostly asymptomatic (n = 6), but with variable contribution from ARIA-E and/or ARIA-H and one mixed ARIA-E/-H case with additional associated ischemia or possible ARIA-I [36] (Fig. 2B). All ARIA events occurred in patients on lecanemab, which was the more predominant AAT at the time of data extraction. Probability of ARIA was highest early in the infusion protocol (Fig. 2C) with highest probability of ARIA-E earlier compared to ARIA-H (Fig. 2D–F). The seven ARIA events were identified on routine ARIA monitoring MRIs prior to the 5th (n = 2), 7th (n = 2), and 14th (n = 3) lecanemab infusions.

Of the five patients with ARIA, most were APOE e4 heterozygotes (n = 4) and one non-carrier. There were two ARIA events that were second episodes for two different patients; both patients with recurrent ARIA events were APOE e4 heterozygotes. One patient stopped infusions per initial ATND Clinic stoppage criteria after having two unique ARIA events, which were identified on routine ARIA monitoring MRIs prior to the 7th and 14th lecanemab infusions. The other patient with two unique ARIA events moved to another state and established care locally before full resolution of the second ARIA event; both events were identified on routine ARIA monitoring MRIs prior to the 5th and 7th lecanemab infusions.

The one patient with symptomatic ARIA was identified with a routine ARIA monitoring MRI prior to the 5th lecanemab infusion. She required outpatient high-dose intravenous solumedrol for 5 days for severe ARIA-E with moderate ARIA-H, but avoided inpatient admission. She had full resolution of the associated symptoms and MRI findings. The CU-ATND Clinic intake indicated MCI stage per the caregiver-reported functional screens, but she had been referred for mild dementia stage after being followed in the CU-MDC for about 3.5 years. Of note, this 68-year-old woman also had no microhemorrhages or superficial siderosis on the screening ARIA MRI. Additionally, this was prior to the FDA update to add an ARIA monitoring MRI before the 3rd lecanemab infusion [37], and therefore may have been caught earlier with the updated protocol.

There were no reported deaths in patients who were started on AAT. Overall, all ATND Clinic AAT-related adverse events were managed outpatient without extended ED treatment and no inpatient admission. There were no persistent effects from AAT documented during the study period.

Discussion

Evidence-based and safety-minded AAT program protocols are complex with unique strengths and barriers that are setting-dependent. Unfortunately, this complexity has downstream impacts to AAT access, which can potentially further widen divides already seen in dementia care [38, 39]. In this RWE analysis of the CU-ATND Clinic, many of the common disparities in patient population diversity were shared with the previously reported RWE AAT studies [8–11, 21–24]. Most patients were referred from the CU-MDC due to preferred referral pathways. Therefore, specialty dementia care access disparities in the CU-MDC already created bias in the largest pool of patients referred to the CU-ATND, therefore SoC troubleshooting needs to begin even prior to AAT consideration. Additionally, many outside referrals had non-AD etiological diagnoses, highlighting the need for improved direct referral pathways to the ATND Clinic in the future.

This is the second RWE study to describe a population of patients considering AAT who were clinically determined to not be candidates or opted to not start therapy [11], as the other site-specific RWE AAT studies focused on patients who started infusions [8–10]. In Bregman et al. [11], of the patients being evaluated for lecanemab consideration (n = 169), 9.5% (n = 16) declined treatment, 3.0% (n = 5) deferred treatment, 17.2% (n = 29) were clinically excluded, 7.7% (n = 13) had work-up ongoing, 10.7% (n = 18) were awaiting insurance approval or scheduled for future infusions, and 50.9% (n = 86) started infusions. In contrast, the CU-ATND Clinic had a lower rate of patients who consented for infusions (31.7%, n = 39) and higher rate that declined AAT (37.5%, n = 46). The CU-ATND Clinic provides individualized shared decision-making in addition to traditional written consent. The CU-ATND Clinic was designed to allow for in-depth education about AAT individualized risks, benefits, and other psychosocial considerations. Multiple patient and caregiver educational materials were created; these tools are routinely used by the CU-MDC team members to provide pre-education prior to scheduling in the CU-ATND Clinic. It is possible this creates a higher rate of patients declining after careful education and shared decision-making, however this is an area that needs to be studied further in future RWE endeavors.

There are some limitations to this single site retrospective RWE study. The patient numbers are relatively low especially in the CU-ATND Clinic; however, the depth of data offers insight into a unique AAT program model. Data extraction differed between the CU-ATND and CU-MDC, therefore direct comparisons cannot be made for all the variables. The baseline demographics still allow for a comparison of the clinic populations and show referral bias in both. Additionally, the CU-ATND requires in-depth clinical assessments that are not routinely used in the CU-MDC, therefore data depth discrepancies in part represent inherent differences. The CU-ATND Clinic protocols were not consistent throughout the study period due to adaptations driven by increasing experience and available data updates, such as the post-approval recommendations in ARIA safety monitoring from the FDA [37]; however, this mirrors real-world experience in a rapidly changing clinical setting. Infusion-related reactions were not fully documented until after all the initial highest risk infusions were completed (i.e., first three for lecanemab, first five for donanemab). This protocol accounts for differences in the reported numbers for patients who were consented for, holding, completed, or stopped infusions (n = 39; Table 2) and number of possible infusion-related reaction documentations (n = 29 for lecanemab, n = 1 for donanemab), therefore does not capture all the possible events. The protocol for documenting infusion-related reactions has now been altered to improve RWE analysis in the future.

The CDR [35] was not routinely used in the ATND Clinic, which therefore may limit comparison to other RWE AAT program studies. Instead, the FAQ and QDRS were used in combination for staging purposes. FAQ discriminates well between normal cognition and MCI or dementia [40]. QDRS is correlated with CDR but is a faster 10-item questionnaire [26], and therefore was used to screen for moderate dementia at which stage AAT is not indicated. A minority of patients in the ATND Clinic had CDR performed to meet commercial plan requirements, but these are not reported for this study. Of note, the CDR Sum of Boxes (CDR-SB) was the primary outcome for CLARITY-AD [2] and a secondary outcome for TRAILBLAZER-ALZ2 [3]. CDR administration requires participation from both the patient and an informant, additional training for raters, and extended time for interview-based data collection [35], which is not available in every patient context or clinical setting. AAT program RWE shows variability with some reporting CDR data [9, 10] and others not [8, 11]. CDR use will be biased towards larger centers and especially sites with associated Alzheimer’s Disease Research Centers. This variability may also be influenced by which database is preferred at each site to satisfy the Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services (CMS) National Coverage Determination (NCD) for AAT [41]. For example, for the CMS database (https://qualitynet.cms.gov/alzheimers-ced-registry/submission), the FAQ is required but CDR is optional. Therefore, despite variability of practices across AAT programs, the use of FAQ and QDRS in the ATND Clinic can still have wider applicability and allow for comparisons.

RWE analysis needs to be designed to address how SoC challenges may impact access and outcomes, such as comparing academic to private practice settings [8]. Many SoC challenges can be overcome with buy-in from administrators to provide necessary resources including hiring staff required for complex care coordination. The duration from AD diagnosis to starting AAT will improve with increasing experience [8, 11], but reaching underrepresented groups in the first place is a clear gap. Understanding the root causes of organizational, clinical, reimbursement, and cultural challenges can lead to refinement of protocols [9]. AAT access can grow significantly once protocols are in place [22], but data collection and reporting are required to inform future adjustments.

Known geographically-based inequities include limited rural access to dementia care [38], which is also highlighted in AAT access for this RWE study and others [21–23]. Although the CU-MDC has a very wide catchment area that includes urban and far-reaching rural areas, the rural population is not well represented in the CU-ATND Clinic. Of note, patients who were counseled about AAT by CU-MDC team members, which includes discussions about travel requirements, were not captured in this study if they declined AAT before being seen in the CU-ATND Clinic. The lack of rural representation may be due to the location constraints for infusions and ARIA MRI scans; this is further supported by patients who were seen in the CU-ATND citing travel burden as a reason to decline AAT. However, future studies could investigate the impact of distances or travel-time on decisions to pursue AAT. Multiple rural patients communicated that AAT was not available to them locally. Even if there is local neurology coverage, the ability to run a complex AAT program is not always feasible given differences in comfort with etiological diagnosis, access to reliable infusion centers and timely imaging, and ARIA interpretation by American Society of Neuroradiology (ASNR)-trained neuroradiology physicians [7].

Understanding sex differences in AAT in the real-world will help guide shared decision-making conversations about choosing the most appropriate agent for women versus men [42, 43] and groups that were underrepresented in the landmark trials [2, 3] including other than white backgrounds and Hispanic/Latino ethnicities; however, if AAT programs also do not recruit a greater diversity of patients, then the data will ultimately continue to not be available to help guide conversations going forwards.

A better understanding of RWE outcomes for AAT in patients with EOAD will also help guide shared decision-making conversations. Younger patients have more decline compared to older patients on AAT [11]; however, patients with EOAD also tend to have a more rapid progression and more atypical presentations limiting interpretation of cognitive screens [44]. Additionally, there are some ADAD mutations with a theoretically increased risk for ARIA given increased risk for cerebral amyloid angiopathy (CAA) [45]. RWE on ARIA outcomes for patients with EOAD will guide shared decision-making conversations including the outcomes from the Dominantly Inherited Alzheimer Network Trials Unit [46]. Patients with EOAD were over-represented in the CU-ATND Clinic RWE, suggesting that these patients may be highly motivated to start AAT despite greater psychosocial challenges [47]. Consideration of monogenic causes of AD was not standardized leading to multiple patients meeting criteria for EOAD not being referred for this testing, therefore shows an area that can be improved going forwards for the CU-ATND. Although site participation in post-market surveillance is part of the CMS announcement for AAT coverage [41], individuals with AD with commercial insurances may not be entered into a database if it is not required for coverage. Therefore, RWE that do not rely on databases biased by Medicare-heavy inclusion are also needed.

A significant concern about AAT has been the added costs to already vulnerable population of patients with AD, but out-of-pocket estimates have been lower than expected [8]. There is often a build-it-first approach where new clinical programs must prove financial feasibility, but the activation energy required to start AAT programs can make this difficult. Relationships between academic centers and their associated medical systems often have opaque communication regarding reimbursements and income generation, therefore estimating the financial benefit added by AAT programs can be difficult. However, when AAT programs are adequately supported with additional staff [10, 11], success without impacting other services areas is possible. Additionally, individuals with AD obtain information about AAT from a variety of sources and make decisions based on multiple factors [48], highlighting the importance of providing education and discussing individual factors that impact shared decision making.

Conclusion

AAT RWE will allow for further evaluation of factors impacting AAT access to better support patients from multiple backgrounds. The more thorough and appropriately tailored the information provided, the more confident patients and their caregivers will be making an informed decision. RWE on AAT has the potential to guide these important discussions and to promote authentic shared decision-making moving forward.
