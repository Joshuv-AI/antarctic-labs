A Kinematic Metric for Fine Manipulation Ability in Robotic Hands
Martin Peticco, Pulkit Agrawal

Abstract
Traditional robotic hand metrics focus on static properties such as workspace, manipulability, and grasp stability. However, these metrics do not directly measure dexterity under the standard definition in robotic manipulation: the ability to continuously change an object’s pose within the hand while maintaining contact from an initial grasp. We introduce Kinematic Rolling Manipulation Ability (KaRMA), a kinematic-only metric for fine manipulation that quantifies reachable in-hand translation and reorientation of a spherical test object within a two-finger precision pinch through feasible rolling motions. KaRMA enforces joint limits, collision constraints, rolling contact, and antipodal force feasibility, then explores reachable in-hand object poses via breadth-first search over translation and rotation primitives. KaRMA reports three scores: translational coverage (KaRMA-T), rotational coverage (KaRMA-R), and sensitivity to the initial grasp (KaRMA-S). We evaluate KaRMA on 16 widely used robotic hands and compare against static baselines, showing that KaRMA separates hands that rank identically under static proxies, reveals translation–rotation tradeoffs invisible to existing baselines, and is qualitatively consistent with selected published task benchmarks where Jacobian-based metrics can be misleading.



I
Introduction

A significant number of robotic hands and similar dexterous end-effectors have been presented in the past few years from both industry and academia, targeting fine manipulation and assembly tasks. Hand selection and design decisions are largely guided by simple proxies such as joint count and degrees of freedom (DOFs), which do not fully describe manipulation capability. For procurement and design iteration, it is useful to have an objective, model-based score that can be computed directly from a hand’s kinematics.

Robotic manipulation literature often describes dexterity as the ability to continuously change an object’s pose while maintaining contact from an initial grasp
[
1
,
13
,
2
]
. Some metrics attempt to capture this, such as thumb opposition, fingertip workspace, or local manipulability
[
5
,
25
,
26
]
. However, they do not directly quantify a hand’s ability to change object pose through a
continuous
sequence of in-hand motions while maintaining contact from an initial grasp.

One could argue that a robot can simply reorient its end effector using its arm, or set down the object being manipulated to regrasp it in a new pose. However, this means giving up the last-mile precision that a dexterous end-effector affords for precision tasks
[
14
]
. Regrasping also increases task horizon by introducing extra steps, and many objects are not feasible to pick up immediately in a desired pose without fixtures
[
10
]
. This matters in precision tasks such as assembly with small parts, fine reorientation during bin picking, or manipulating everyday objects in assistive settings. Thus, it is important to have a way to quantify a robot hand’s ability to perform fine object translation and reorientation.

This paper introduces
Kinematic Rolling Manipulation Ability
(KaRMA), a kinematic metric for measuring fine manipulation capability of a robotic hand based off its kinematic model. KaRMA measures the reachable set of object translations and orientations for a small spherical test object in a precision pinch under rolling contact without regrasping or finger gaiting. The metric reports (i) a translational score, (ii) a rotational score, and (iii) a score that summarizes sensitivity to the initial pinch grasp. KaRMA is intended as a kinematics-only comparison tool for hand procurement and kinematic design iteration, not as a general grasp planner or a task benchmark. The contributions of this paper are:

1.

A kinematics-only dexterity metric based on reachability of in-hand object pose changes under rolling contact in a two-finger pinch.

2.

A practical computation procedure that evaluates translational ability, rotational ability, and seed robustness from a hand kinematic model under standardized constraints, including coupled joints.

3.

An evaluation of KaRMA across a set of widely used robot hands with comparisons to common baselines.

II
Related Work

II-A

Robotic hand evaluation via kinematic proxies

People often use simple quantitative descriptors such as DOF count and joint count to compare robot hands. Beyond this, kinematics-based proxies exist as well, such as the Kapandji score, which measures touching the thumb to different points on the hand
[
11
]
. Another such proxy is the measure of workspace volume for individual fingers
[
12
]
, which was built upon in Mouri et al.’s paper on the design of the GIFU Hand III, optimizing its design based on the intersection volume between the fingertip and thumb workspace volumes
[
19
]
. You et al.’s “Interactivity of Fingers” extends this by measuring the intersection of multiple fingers
[
26
]
. Finally, local manipulability metrics such as the Yoshikawa index
[
25
]
are commonly used in grasp quality analysis. These measures are useful for individual grasps and local configurations, but they do not directly quantify how much an object can be repositioned and reoriented within a grasp, per the commonly accepted definition of dexterity.

II-B

Benchmarks and Task Suites

Some benchmarks exist to directly capture object-centric in-hand capabilities through prescribed tasks and success criteria, such as the Elliott–Connolly benchmark
[
6
]
, the Asterisk Test for two-finger translation
[
17
]
, and planar rotation benchmarks
[
18
]
. More broadly, other general manipulation benchmarks involve performing a suite of tasks, often with learning-based methods, such as the Adroit dexterous manipulation suite
[
20
]
. These benchmarks are useful for evaluating a full system, but their outcomes depend heavily on controllers, sensing, and training choices, making them less suitable for objectively comparing hand kinematics.

II-C

Rolling contact formulation for robotic manipulation

A natural way to isolate kinematics while still being object-based is to model in-hand manipulation through contact kinematics. Rolling-contact formulations exist that relate joint motion, contact motion, and object motion
[
16
]
, which have been extended for use in dexterous motion generation and analysis. This has included classic rolling-based manipulation formulations and “dexterous gripper” designs that exploit rolling effects
[
3
,
2
]
. More recent work prioritizes practical optimization or graph search methods for motion generation
[
24
,
7
]
. These formulations provide the modeling basis needed to define an objective, contact-constrained measure of in-hand object pose change from a kinematic hand model.

These works motivate a metric that goes beyond simple kinematic proxies, is less sensitive to system-level integration details, and measures realistic in-hand object pose manipulation via rolling contact constraints. KaRMA is proposed as a standardized kinematics-only score for thumb–index rolling-pinch manipulation, intended to complement rather than replace full task benchmarks.

III
KaRMA Metric Definition

This section defines what KaRMA measures and what it reports. Details about computation are in Section
IV
. A summary of the metric is seen in Fig.
1
.

III-A

Scope and Assumptions

KaRMA evaluates a two-finger precision pinch between the thumb and index finger on a small spherical test object under rolling contact and without regrasping or finger gaiting.

Two-finger precision pinch.

Thumb-index pinch is the most common grasp in fine manipulation and a lower-bound case for maintained-contact in-hand motion
[
8
,
2
,
17
]
. We use it as a standardized lower bound, and extension to other finger pairs or multi-finger grasps is future work.

Spherical test object.

A sphere removes object-specific edge and corner effects, making the comparison object-agnostic and focused on rolling dexterity
[
4
]
.

Rolling contact, no regrasp.

Maintained contact isolates kinematic in-hand capability and avoids dependence on controller design, gravity, or object geometry
[
1
,
2
]
.

Kinematics only.

KaRMA uses the hand kinematics only and is meant as a comparison tool, not to predict task success.

III-B

Standardized Evaluation via
L_{\mathrm{ref}}
Scaling

To make scores comparable across hand sizes and reproducible from a URDF, KaRMA non-dimensionalizes all length parameters (sphere radius, voxel size, tolerances) by scaling them with a characteristic length
L_{\mathrm{ref}}
. We compute
L_{\mathrm{ref}}
from the URDF as

L_{\mathrm{ref}}=\text{mxPr}+\text{mF}
,
where
mxPr
is the maximum pairwise distance between finger knuckle joint origins (the first non-mimic revolute joint in each finger chain), and
mF
is the median knuckle-to-tip chain length (sum of link lengths along each chain). The sum captures both palm spread and finger reach, the two geometric factors that bound the envelope of feasible pinch locations. Parameters are defined at nominal
L_{\mathrm{ref}}=200\,\mathrm{mm}
and then scaled by
L_{\mathrm{ref}}/200\,\mathrm{mm}
. Dimensionless parameters (friction coefficient, HEALPix resolution, BFS budget) are not scaled.

III-C

Twist-Invariant Formulation

In a two-finger pinch, rotation about the axis connecting the two contacts is uncontrollable (see Fig.
2
). We factor out this DOF by tracking the pinch-axis direction in the sphere’s body frame. Since flipping the axis gives the same physical pinch, opposite directions are equivalent, leaving a two-dimensional orientation representation.

We discretize this space using HEALPix
[
9
]
, which partitions
S^{2}
into equal-area bins. With
n_{\mathrm{side}}=6
and antipodal identification, this yields
N_{\mathrm{bin}}=6n_{\mathrm{side}}^{2}+2n_{\mathrm{side}}=228
bins at approximately
10^{\circ}
resolution. Equal-area bins make the coverage fraction less sensitive to discretization bias across orientation space.

III-D

Reported Scores

Given a kinematic model, KaRMA computes the reachable set of object translations and orientations from an initial feasible
seed
pinch configuration under rolling contact. Three scores are reported:

III-D
1
KaRMA-T (Translational Ability)

The reachable set of sphere center positions is discretized on a voxel grid aligned with the principal directions of the seed configuration’s manipulability ellipsoid. The translational score is the dimensionless ratio:

\text{KaRMA-T}=\frac{n_{\mathrm{voxels}}\cdot h^{3}}{L_{\mathrm{ref}}^{3}}

(1)

where
n_{\mathrm{voxels}}
is the number of reached voxels and
h
is the (scaled) voxel edge length. This can be interpreted as the fraction of an
L_{\mathrm{ref}}
-sided cube occupied by the reachable in-hand workspace. To reduce seed noise, the reported score is the mean of the top 3 seed configurations. Sensitivity to the seed is captured separately by KaRMA-S.

III-D
2
KaRMA-R (Rotational Ability)

For each reached voxel, the fraction of the 228 orientation bins visited is recorded. KaRMA-R is the mean rotation coverage of the five best voxels:

\text{KaRMA-R}=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{V}_{5}|}\sum_{v\,\in\,\mathcal{V}_{5}}\frac{|\mathcal{B}(v)|}{N_{\mathrm{bin}}}

(2)

where
\mathcal{V}_{5}
is the set of the five reached voxels with highest orientation coverage and
\mathcal{B}(v)
is the set of orientation bins reached at voxel
v
. We average over the top five rather than reporting a single-voxel maximum to reduce sensitivity to outlier voxels, while still focusing on the best region of the translational workspace. The score is averaged across the top 3 seeds, like KaRMA-T.

III-D
3
KaRMA-S (Initial Grasp Sensitivity)

KaRMA-S measures sensitivity to the initial grasp configuration. Each feasible seed is evaluated for its translational score, and KaRMA-S is the ratio of the median to the best:

\text{KaRMA-S}=\frac{\text{KaRMA-T}_{\mathrm{median}}}{\text{KaRMA-T}_{\mathrm{best}}}

(3)

Intuitively, a KaRMA-S near 1 means most seeds perform similarly to the best seed, and near 0 means high performance is concentrated in specific configurations. KaRMA-S is defined by translational scores rather than rotational scores because translational reachability is more sensitive to the initial grasp, as different seeds produce different frontier voxels, directly affecting KaRMA-T. In contrast, KaRMA-R averages the top-five voxels’ rotational coverage, and a seed that reaches even a few well-positioned voxels can still achieve high rotational coverage. Translational sensitivity is therefore a more conservative and informative robustness indicator. Note that KaRMA-S should always be interpreted jointly with KaRMA-T, as a high KaRMA-S with a low KaRMA-T means the hand uniformly lacks dexterity (as with very low-DOF hands).

IV
Full Metric Computation

This section describes how KaRMA is computed from a hand kinematic model. The pipeline has three stages: seed selection (
IV-D
), two-phase BFS (
IV-E
), and scoring (
IV-F
). All constraints are checked at every BFS step. See Fig.
2
for the setup.

Fig. 2:
Schematic of the pinch grasp setup, including finger links, sphere, contact normals, BFS primitives, and pinch axis. ARMS model shown.

IV-A

Contact Geometry

Each finger link is modeled as a capsule (cylinder with hemispherical caps) of fixed radius
r_{\ell}
, and the manipulated object is a sphere of radius
r_{s}
. Contact between a capsule and the sphere is checked using the distance from the sphere center
\bm{p}
to the closest point
\bm{c}
on the capsule axis segment:

g=\left\|\bm{p}-\bm{c}\right\|-r_{s}-r_{\ell}

(4)

where
g
is the signed contact gap. A contact is maintained when
|g|\leq\epsilon_{g}
, and the outward unit normal at contact is
\hat{\bm{n}}=(\bm{p}-\bm{c})/\left\|\bm{p}-\bm{c}\right\|
. At each BFS step, the contact pair is selected as the closest thumb link and closest index link to the sphere center, so the contacting link pair may change during BFS. For rolling constraints, we use the contact-point surface velocity Jacobian induced by joint motion and sphere rotation.

IV-B

Feasibility Constraints for Candidates

Contact gap:
both contacts
|g_{i}|\leq\epsilon_{g}
after projection.

Joint limits:
satisfy URDF bounds
q_{j}^{-}\leq q_{j}\leq q_{j}^{+}
.

Collision avoidance:
the sphere does not penetrate any non-contact link capsule (
d-r_{s}-r_{\ell}\geq\epsilon_{\mathrm{col}}
), and thumb–index self-collisions satisfy a segment–segment distance of at least
2r_{\ell}+\epsilon_{\mathrm{col}}
for all capsule pairs.

Antipodal grasp feasibility:
contact normals satisfy a friction-cone force-closure test. For friction coefficient
\mu
, feasibility requires
\cos(\angle(-\hat{\bm{n}}_{0},\hat{\bm{n}}_{1}))\geq\cos(2\arctan\mu)
.

IV-C

Rolling Contact QP

Each BFS primitive solves a quadratic program (QP) for a local joint displacement
\Delta\bm{q}
that approximately achieves either a target sphere translation
\Delta\bm{p}
or a target rotation
\Delta\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{target}}
while maintaining locally rolling-consistent contact motion.

For translation primitives, we optimize over
\bm{x}=[\Delta\bm{q}^{\top},\,\Delta\bm{\theta}^{\top}]^{\top}
and penalize deviation from ideal rolling (computed geometrically from
\Delta\bm{p}
) alongside regularization:

\min_{\bm{x}}\left\|\mathbf{M}\bm{x}-\bm{b}\right\|^{2}+\lambda_{\theta}\left\|\Delta\bm{\theta}-\Delta\bm{\theta}^{*}\right\|^{2}+\lambda_{r}\left\|\Delta\bm{q}\right\|^{2}

(5)

where
\mathbf{M}
encodes the linearized rolling constraints at both contacts and
\bm{b}
encodes the desired contact point motion induced by
\Delta\bm{p}
. Rotation primitives are solved similarly with
\Delta\bm{p}=0
, targeting
\Delta\bm{\theta}_{\mathrm{target}}
while maintaining rolling-consistent surface motion. Both QPs enforce joint step limits
|\Delta q_{j}|\leq\Delta q_{\mathrm{max}}
, joint position limits, and linearized gap constraints. Each voxel move is integrated using fixed sub-steps to maintain linearization accuracy, and a brief projection step re-closes residual contact gap. QPs are solved with OSQP
[
22
]
. KaRMA therefore approximates the reachable set under rolling contact through repeated local linearized steps rather than solving an exact global contact-kinematics problem in one shot.

IV-D

Seed Selection

The seed is the initial feasible thumb–index pinch configuration from which BFS starts. We deterministically generate candidate joint configurations using a fixed union of Sobol quasi-random samples, per-joint linspace sweeps, and uniform pseudo-random draws with a fixed seed. For each candidate, we propose a sphere center near the thumb–index contact midpoint (with small offsets) and project onto the two-contact manifold using a nonlinear least-squares IK solve with backtracking. Feasible seeds are then optionally polished by moving toward joint-range center in the nullspace of
\mathbf{J}_{\mathrm{obj}}=\frac{1}{2}(\mathbf{J}_{v,0}^{\mathrm{surf}}+\mathbf{J}_{v,1}^{\mathrm{surf}})
. All feasible seeds are evaluated with the full translational BFS.

The voxel grid is aligned with the seed configuration’s manipulability ellipsoid:
\mathbf{R}_{\mathrm{seed}}
is formed from the eigenvectors of
\mathbf{J}_{\mathrm{obj}}\mathbf{J}_{\mathrm{obj}}^{\top}
(ordered by descending eigenvalue), with a deterministic sign convention and right-handedness enforced.

IV-E

Two-Phase BFS

KaRMA uses a two-phase breadth-first search.

Phase 1 (translation):
starting from the seed, BFS expands over voxels using six translation primitives (
\pm
along each principal axis of the seed configuration). Each candidate step solves the rolling QP, applies projection, and checks all feasibility constraints. The search terminates when the frontier is exhausted or the state budget is reached.

Phase 2 (rotation):
for each voxel discovered in Phase 1, local rotational primitives about the two controllable axes are explored to record reached HEALPix bins. KaRMA-R is computed from the mean orientation coverage of the five best voxels.

IV-F

Scoring

After the BFS completes, KaRMA is computed as in Section
III-D
.

V
Experimental Setup

V-A

Hands Evaluated

We evaluate 16 hands: Ability, Allegro, D-Claw, Unitree Dex3, Unitree Dex5, Inspire, LEAP, ORCA, Sharpa, Shadow, Schunk SVH, Wuji, XHand1, XHand Lite, plus skeletal models from ARMS and DLR
[
15
,
23
]
. The set spans low to high DOF, compact to large designs, and includes non-anthropomorphic morphology.

V-B

Default Parameters

All evaluations use a sphere radius of
10\,\mathrm{mm}
(at nominal
L_{\mathrm{ref}}=200\,\mathrm{mm}
), voxel size
h=10\,\mathrm{mm}
, friction coefficient
\mu=0.6
, HEALPix
n_{\mathrm{side}}=6
(
228
orientation bins at
{\approx}10^{\circ}
resolution), and a BFS budget of
10{,}000
states. Contact, collision, and step-size tolerances are fixed across hands and scaled with
L_{\mathrm{ref}}/200\,\mathrm{mm}
as described in Section
III-B
; dimensionless parameters (friction, HEALPix resolution, BFS budget) are not scaled.

V-C

Baselines

We compare KaRMA against kinematic proxies computed on the thumb and index chains. Coupled / mimic joints are taken into account everywhere.

DOF count:
number of controllable joints.

Total joint range:
sum of joint ranges (upper minus lower limit, in radians) over active thumb–index joints.

Workspace volume:
convex hull volume of the fingertip workspace from 65,536 Sobol samples per finger, normalized by
L_{\mathrm{ref}}^{3}
. Fingers with
\leq 2
DOF produce planar workspaces with zero 3D volume, making this baseline uninformative.

Workspace intersection (opposability):
volume of intersection of the thumb and index fingertip convex hulls, normalized by
L_{\mathrm{ref}}^{3}

[
19
,
26
]
.

Yoshikawa manipulability:
mean manipulability of the combined thumb–index Jacobian over the same samples
[
25
]
.

Global conditioning index (GCI):
mean of
\sigma_{\mathrm{min}}/\sigma_{\mathrm{max}}
over the samples, excluding near-singular configurations.

V-D

Robustness and Sensitivity Experiments

We validate KaRMA on five representative hands across the observed score range: LEAP (8 DOF, highest T), Shadow (9 DOF, highest DOF), xHand1 (6 DOF, mid-range), Dex3 (5 DOF, low T and DOF), and Inspire (3 DOF, lowest T). All experiments use a reduced BFS budget of 1,000 states.

Invariance.
Test translation (four offsets), rotation (four rotations), and scale (
0.5\times
and
2\times
URDF scaling).

Parameter sensitivity.
Independently perturb link lengths (
\pm 1\%
,
\pm 2\%
), joint limits (
\pm 1\degree
,
\pm 3\degree
), sphere radius (
\pm 2\%
,
\pm 5\%
), and capsule radius (
\pm 2\%
,
\pm 5\%
), and report the maximum absolute and relative change in KaRMA-T and KaRMA-R.

Determinism.
Verify bit-for-bit reproducibility over three identical runs.

VI
Results

VI-A

KaRMA Scores

Table
I
reports KaRMA-T, KaRMA-R, and KaRMA-S for all 16 hands alongside kinematic baselines. KaRMA-T spans three orders of magnitude, from 0.098 (LEAP) to 0.0003 (Ability), providing substantially more dynamic range than DOF count (3–9) or GCI (0.08–0.38) for discriminating between hands. The top five hands all achieve KaRMA-T
>0.02
, whereas the bottom five are all below 0.003. Fig.
3
shows two interesting examples, with Allegro having more uniform rotation ability compared to Sharpa. This is a property that other metrics do not capture.

Fig. 3:
KaRMA on two robot hands: Allegro and Sharpa. Red denotes voxels with lowest rotation range, and green denotes highest. Note Sharpa’s lower rotation range at the edges and smaller overall workspace.

KaRMA-R ranges from 0.337 (Allegro) to 0.004 (Inspire, Ability), so the best hand reaches 33.7% of the 228 HEALPix bins, averaged over its five best voxels. Allegro ranks 2nd in T but 1st in R, while Wuji overtakes Sharpa in R despite ranking below it in T. KaRMA therefore separates translational reach from local reorientation coverage.

KaRMA-S measures initial grasp sensitivity. Allegro (0.53) and LEAP (0.44) are relatively stable across initial pinches, whereas D’Claw (0.12) and xHand1 (0.13) are much more seed-sensitive among hands with nontrivial KaRMA-T. Very low-S hands such as Dex3 and xHandLite (both 0.06) should be interpreted together with their small absolute workspaces, and manipulation with these hands can be more challenging than their T score would suggest.

KaRMA remains nonzero for the five hands with zero workspace intersection (
\dagger
in Table
I
) because BFS measures reachability along the lower-dimensional manifold those fingers can still traverse.

VI-B

Baseline Comparison

Among baselines, workspace intersection correlates most strongly with KaRMA-T (
\rho_{s}=0.92
,
p<0.001
), which is expected since fingertip workspace intersection limits where feasible pinches can form. Total joint range (
\rho_{s}=0.82
,
p<0.001
) and DOF count (
\rho_{s}=0.73
,
p=0.001
) also correlate, but neither is reliable on its own: Shadow and DLR (both 9 DOF) rank only 6th and 8th in KaRMA-T, while D’Claw (6 DOF) ranks 3rd. Jacobian-based metrics show no correlation at all—Yoshikawa manipulability (
\rho_{s}=-0.20
,
p=0.46
) and GCI (
\rho_{s}=-0.11
,
p=0.68
)—because low-DOF hands like Ability have trivially well-conditioned Jacobians, giving them the highest GCI despite ranking last in KaRMA-T.

The high
\rho_{s}
with opposability is partly inflated by five zero-intersection hands clustering at the bottom of both rankings. Fig.
4
plots both KaRMA scores against opposability; despite the strong trend, rank displacement analysis reveals that opposability misranks 5 of 16 hands (31%) by
\geq\!2
positions in KaRMA-T, with 8% of pairwise orderings inverted. The largest outliers (diamonds in Fig.
4
) are Allegro (opposability rank 5
\to
KaRMA-T rank 2), whose wide joint ranges sustain long rolling trajectories despite modest intersection, and Dex5 (rank 6
\to
9), where constraints reduce its moderate overlap to limited BFS reach. xHand1 (rank 9
\to
11) provides another example: joint limits eliminate 99% of its unconstrained workspace (Table
IV

VII
Discussion and Limitations

The results support two main points. First, KaRMA-T is broadly consistent with thumb–index overlap: hands with little fingertip overlap rarely translate the object far under maintained contact. At the same time, overlap alone is not sufficient. Workspace intersection ignores whether a continuous rolling trajectory exists under collisions, joint limits, and force-feasible antipodal contact. KaRMA filters overlap through these contact-consistency constraints, which is where many of the rank differences and most of the metric’s practical value arise. The constraint ablation (Table
IV
) confirms that joint limits alone reduce unconstrained workspace by 61–99% for hands with nontrivial unconstrained reach, and the full constraint stack removes additional states, separating KaRMA from raw workspace overlap. The same table shows that KaRMA-R is affected by each constraint layer but with less dynamic range, indicating that rotation coverage is shaped more by local joint mobility than by the global reachability constraints that dominate translation.

Second, KaRMA-R captures a manipulability axis that the baselines do not resolve. Allegro achieves the highest rotational coverage (0.337) despite ranking 2nd in translation, while Sharpa ranks 4th in translation but only 5th in rotation, displaced by Wuji. These T–R divergences suggest that hand selection should depend on the task: a design that repositions well is not necessarily the best at reorientation, and no single baseline predicts which way a hand will skew.

KaRMA-S complements T and R by indicating how concentrated performance is across feasible initial pinches. Low-S hands (e.g., D’Claw:
\text{S}=0.12
) require reliable seed selection to realize peak capability, whereas higher-S hands (e.g., Allegro:
\text{S}=0.53
) are more tolerant to initial-grasp variation, making execution easier.

The robustness results in Section
VI-C
suggest that accurate joint-limit specification matters more than sub-millimeter link geometry for KaRMA-T, since joint limits and sphere radius have the largest effect. At the same time, KaRMA should be interpreted as a standardized lower bound on thumb–index rolling-pinch dexterity, not as a universal dexterity score. It evaluates a spherical test object, maintained rolling contact, no regrasp or finger gaiting, and kinematics only; it also depends on local linearization and discretization choices. These assumptions are deliberate for comparability, but they limit direct prediction of task success on arbitrary objects.

VIII
Conclusion

This paper introduced KaRMA, a kinematics-only metric for evaluating a standardized lower bound on fine manipulation ability in robotic hands. KaRMA measures the reachable set of object translations and orientations from an initial two-finger precision pinch on a spherical test object under rolling contact, reporting three complementary scores: translational ability (KaRMA-T), rotational ability (KaRMA-R), and initial-grasp sensitivity (KaRMA-S). The metric is computed entirely from a URDF kinematic model, requires no controller or actuator specification, and is non-dimensionalized via a characteristic hand length so that scores are comparable across hand sizes.

We evaluated KaRMA on 16 robotic hands spanning a wide range of designs. KaRMA-T correlates strongly with workspace intersection (
\rho_{s}=0.92
), consistent with thumb-index overlap being the dominant factor in translation and providing a useful first-pass proxy, while KaRMA adds contact-consistency filtering that pure kinematic overlap cannot capture: the constraint ablation shows that joint limits, collisions, and antipodal feasibility together remove 82–99% of the unconstrained translational workspace for hands with nontrivial unconstrained reach and reduce rotational coverage by
1.8
–
5.1\times
, with rotation affected less dramatically because it depends more on local joint mobility than on global reach. KaRMA-R reveals a distinct axis of manipulation ability that no baseline predicts: the Allegro achieves the highest rotational coverage despite ranking only 2nd in translation, and the T–R divergences in the mid-tier hands show that translational and rotational ability do not track each other. Jacobian-based metrics (Yoshikawa manipulability, GCI) show no correlation with either KaRMA score.

Several limitations point to future work. KaRMA currently evaluates only two-finger thumb-index pinch grasps. Extending to multi-finger grasps and additional finger pairs would broaden its applicability. It could be useful in quantifying the advantages of having a robotic hand with certain biomimetic features, such as additional palm DOFs that give the pinkie and ring finger more dexterity. The spherical test object intentionally isolates kinematic rolling ability and omits geometry-dependent effects, but adapting the metric to non-spherical objects is a natural extension. Finally, incorporating dynamic feasibility (torque limits, friction uncertainty) would move the metric closer to predicting task success, though at the cost of requiring more than a kinematic model.
