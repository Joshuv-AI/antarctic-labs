Semi-inner branch relining for late AFX type Ia/IIIa endoleak
Authors: Giovanni Gagliardo di Carpinello, Simona Rubino, Martina Pacillo, Claudia Bittoni, Luca di Marzo, Wassim Mansour
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractLate failure of early-generation AFX endografts (Endologix Inc) remains a relevant challenge, sometimes presenting as combined type IA/IIIA endoleak many years after apparently successful endovascular aneurysm repair. We report an 85-year-old man who developed rapid sac enlargement 13 years after AFX-Strata implantation (Endologix Inc) due to modular disconnection and proximal cuff migration. He was treated with relining using a custom-made semi-inner branch endograft, with adjunctive proximal thoracic extension to seal a residual endoleak. Completion angiography and follow-up computed tomography angiography confirmed complete exclusion and branch patency, with uneventful recovery. This case highlights the need for lifelong surveillance after AFX implantation and supports semi-inner branch relining for high-risk patients.Key words: AFX, Type IIIA endoleak, Type IA endoleak, Semi-Inner branch, E-xtra Design, EVAR, iBEVAR

Case report

An 85-year-old man with hypertension and dyslipidemia was referred for a late complication after endovascular aneurysm repair (EVAR) performed 13 years earlier for a 55-mm saccular infrarenal abdominal aortic aneurysm using an AFX-Strata (Endologix Inc) unibody bifurcated endograft (25-60/I16-40) with a suprarenal aortic extension cuff (VELA A28-28/C75-O20V), achieving complete aneurysm exclusion. Surveillance included computed tomography angiography (CTA) and duplex ultrasound (DUS) guidance at 1 month and 1 year, then annual DUS guidance. For 12 years, imaging showed no endoleak and sac shrinkage to 40 mm. At 13 years, DUS guidance detected endoleak with rapid sac enlargement to 70 mm. CTA confirmed a type IIIA endoleak from modular disconnection and concomitant type IA endoleak from distal migration of the proximal cuff (Fig 1).

Preoperative computed tomography angiography (CTA). A, Axial image showing disconnection between the unibody endograft and the aortic cuff. B, Three-dimensional (3D) reconstruction. C, Volume-rendering.

Comparison between the preindex EVAR CTA and the last pre-semi-inner branch (SIB) CTA showed no relevant infrarenal neck remodeling, with substantially unchanged neck diameter, conicity, and beta angle. Thus, the combined type IA/IIIA endoleak was considered mainly device-related rather than secondary to progressive proximal neck degeneration, although the exact temporal sequence between seal loss and modular disconnection could not be established.

On admission, his body mass index was 23.3 kg/m2 (171 cm; 68 kg). Physical examination revealed a pulsatile, tense-elastic epimesogastric mass, with bilaterally palpable lower-limb pulses. Baseline creatinine was 0.94 mg/dL.

Because of rapid aneurysm expansion, inner-branched EVAR was performed using a custom-made SIB bifurcated endograft (Artivion E-xtra Design Multibranch Jotec, 38-13-160, Artivion Inc.) (Fig 2).

Schematic representation of the implanted semi-inner branch E-xtra Design device.

The SIB required 28 days for manufacturing; as the patient was asymptomatic without CTA signs of rupture/contained rupture, outpatient repair was scheduled once available.

Under general anesthesia and bilateral percutaneous femoral access, operative time was 194 minutes, fluoroscopy time 63 minutes (1089.5 mGy), and contrast volume 180 mL. Intravascular ultrasound (IVUS) ensured to avoid passage between graft layers. The device was introduced through a 24F Gore DrySeal (W.L. Gore and Associates) sheath and deployed from the descending thoracic aorta to the endograft above the iliac bifurcation, with two E-tegra (Artivion Inc) iliac limbs (16-135 mm), preserving bilateral hypogastric artery patency.

Complete relining excluded the modular disconnection responsible for the type IIIA endoleak, whereas the proximal SIB portion created a new sealing zone.

The SIBs were selectively catheterized using a steerable catheter, and target vessels were bridged with Gore VBX (W. L. Gore and Associates) covered stents to the celiac trunk (9 × 39 mm), superior mesenteric artery (9 × 29 mm), left renal artery (6 × 39 mm), and right renal artery (6 × 59 mm).

A persistent proximal endoleak, likely related to incomplete graft-wall apposition in a calcified/thrombotic sealing segment, was identified intraoperatively. Under angiographic and IVUS guidance, a more proximal landing zone was selected and sealed with a Terumo Relay Pro (Terumo Aortic) thoracic endograft (38 × 38 × 154 mm) overlapping the branched device. Final angiography showed complete exclusion, visceral and iliac patency, and no residual endoleak. Femoral hemostasis was achieved using prepositioned Perclose ProStyle (Abbott Vascular) devices: two for the right common femoral artery and one for the left.

The postoperative course was uneventful, with 1 day intensive care and discharge on postoperative day 6 on dual antiplatelet therapy. At discharge, lower-limb pulses were unchanged, and creatinine was 1.01 mg/dL.

CTA at 1 and 6 months confirmed absence of sac enlargement, visceral and iliac patency, and no recurrent endoleak (Fig 3).

The patient provided written informed consent for publication.

Discussion

The AFX platform is a bifurcated unibody endograft based on anatomical fixation at the native aortic bifurcation. It consists of an inner cobalt-chromium endoskeleton covered by expanded polytetrafluoroethylene fabric, attached only at the proximal and distal ends, according to the ActiveSeal concept. This design may offer advantages in selected anatomies, including narrow bifurcations, short renobifurcation distance, saccular aneurysms, or complex aortoiliac occlusive disease.1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6

However, late type III endoleak remains the Achilles' heel of this platform, either from modular separation (IIIA) or fabric/stent failure (IIIB), sometimes with distal migration and/or concomitant type IA endoleak. These failures may occur years after apparently successful implantation, even within instructions for use. Several mechanisms have been implicated, including large aneurysm diameter, hostile neck morphology, progressive aortic tortuosity, component overlap loss, multiple components, and mixed-device configurations.11,13,14,17, 18, 19

Therefore, patients with previous AFX implantation, especially early-generation or off-label/mixed-manufacturer constructs, require strict lifelong surveillance.15 Sac enlargement may be the first warning sign, and sideways displacement may precede frank type IIIA failure.7,10,12,16

Once diagnosed, type III endoleak requires prompt repair. Endovascular relining is generally first-line and may include complete graft relining, bridging aortic extensions, or, for selected late type IA failures, fenestrated or branched strategies.16 Open conversion remains necessary in device collapse, infection, or unsuitable anatomy for endovascular repair,20 but many patients present years after EVAR and are frequently elderly and at high surgical risk.21,22

SIB devices based on the E-xtra design platform are recent custom-made grafts developed in Europe for complex abdominal aortic aneurysms.23,24 They are inspired by the innerbranch concept but differ from the off-the-shelf E-nside (Artivion Inc), which was primarily designed for thoracoabdominal aneurysms and has also shown promising results in selected complex abdominal aortic aneurysms.25, 26, 27

However, E-nside requires relatively long aortic coverage, not always necessary in visceral-segment disease and may theoretically increase the risk of spinal cord ischemia. SIB devices were conceived as a hybrid between fenestrated EVAR (FEVAR) and inner-branched EVAR, preserving the innerbranch concept while using shorter, customizable branches, thus enabling the use of shorter-bridging stents. This allows tailoring branch position and aortic coverage, with the additional aim of improving target vessel stability by reducing the risk of fenestration-target vessel misalignment and unfavorable bridging-stent geometry.28 In the present case, several alternatives were considered. Standard infrarenal relining was unsuitable because it would not have provided an adequate proximal sealing zone between the failed AFX cuff and renal arteries. FEVAR was feasible and remains a valid option for similar cases; however, SIB was selected according to operator preference and case-specific planning, allowing proximal seal restoration and complete relining with short, customized branches. Chimney EVAR was not selected because a custom-made solution was available, and no emergent bailout strategy was required. Open conversion was reserved as bailout but considered excessively invasive in this elderly high-risk patient. Thus, SIB relining should not be interpreted as superior to FEVAR, but as a pragmatic alternative in selected complex relining scenarios. Although evidence remains limited, initial experiences appear promising.23,24

These devices currently require a large-profile delivery system, a potential limitation, especially in female patients with small iliofemoral access vessels.29,30 Technically, implantation after previous AFX may not substantially differ from native-aorta implantation; here, the renal free flow created no relevant difficulties. The main precaution is to ensure, ideally with IVUS that the guidewire does not pass within the AFX endoskeleton, avoiding entrapment of the new device or displacement of the previous graft.

Conclusions

Although no comparative conclusions can be drawn from a single case, this report illustrates a pragmatic endovascular solution for late AFX failure. It reinforces three messages: AFX implantation should be meticulous and anatomy-driven; patients treated with AFX require strict lifelong imaging surveillance; and SIB relining may offer a valuable alternative in the selected patients.
