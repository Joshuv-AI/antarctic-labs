Incremental diagnostic value of 24-hour Holter monitoring during training in elite athlete preparticipation screening
Authors: Ramon Pi-Rusiñol, Marga Calvo, Gonzalo Grazioli, Marian Adame, Carlos Eduardo Bolaños Gómez, Josep Brugada, Marta Sitges, Gil Rodas, Maria Sanz de la Garza
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPreparticipation cardiovascular evaluation (PPE) is widely recommended to reduce the risk of sudden cardiac events in athletes; however, the optimal screening protocol remains debated. Although resting ECG and echocardiography are commonly used, intermittent arrhythmias may remain undetected.ObjectiveTo assess whether the addition of 24-hour ambulatory ECG (Holter) monitoring, including during sleep and training, increases the detection of potentially pathological cardiovascular findings.Design and settingsThis observational cross-sectional study included athletes (aged 14–42 years) from a Spanish elite multisport professional club undergoing pre-season PPE during the 2023–2024. All athletes completed a standardised evaluation comprising medical history and physical examination, resting 12-lead ECG, transthoracic echocardiography and 24-hour wireless Holter monitoring performed during daily activities including at least one training session. Detection rates of potentially pathological findings were compared across screening test combinations.ParticipantsOf 343 athletes recruited, 299 (79.6% male; mean age 22 years) were included in the final analysis.ResultsPotentially pathological findings prompting further evaluation were identified in 34 athletes (11.3%). The addition of any single test to medical history and physical examination significantly increased detection rates. Holter monitoring identified more intermittent arrhythmic events, particularly during exercise or sleep, whereas resting ECG more frequently detected repolarisation abnormalities. Echocardiography primarily contributed to the identification of structural abnormalities. Following a comprehensive evaluation, only one athlete was disqualified from competition.ConclusionsIn elite athletes, integrating Holter monitoring into PPE improved the detection of intermittent arrhythmias that may be missed by a resting ECG alone, and providing complementary diagnostic information and representing a useful adjunct to echocardiography in comprehensive cardiovascular screening strategies, particularly in selected high-risk athletic populations. Future longitudinal studies are warranted to determine the clinical significance of these findings.Keywords: Athlete, Ultrasound, Cardiology prevention, Sports & exercise medicine

Introduction

Despite the low incidence of sudden cardiac death (SCD) in athletes, such events are devastating and challenge the well-established health benefits of regular physical activity.1 Intense physical exertion may trigger malignant arrhythmias in individuals with underlying, often silent cardiovascular disease.2 3

Preparticipation cardiovascular evaluation (PPE) enables early identification of athletes with such conditions, thereby mitigating the risk of exercise-induced cardiac events. While most scientific and sporting organisations recommend PPE, substantial debate persists regarding its optimal components, hindering the implementation of a standardised approach.4 The European model typically includes personal and family history, physical examination and resting 12-lead ECG.5 Some national protocols, particularly for elite or professional athletes, also incorporate transthoracic echocardiography because of its additional value in detecting structural heart disease.6

Nonetheless, certain arrhythmic disorders and/or conduction abnormalities may go undetected when relying solely on resting evaluations.7 Continuous rhythm assessment with 24-hour ECG (Holter) monitoring, particularly when it includes sleep and at least one training session, has been proposed as a potentially valuable adjunct to PPE.8 Although evidence supporting routine Holter monitoring in asymptomatic elite athletes remains limited, ambulatory ECG can reveal transient exercise-related or rest-related arrhythmia, conduction disorders and bradycardia, which are unlikely to be detected by short-duration, static recordings.9 Recent technological advances have enabled the acquisition of high-quality, evaluable ECG signals during exercise without the limitations of traditional wired Holter systems. These innovations facilitate ECG monitoring during field-based sports activities, providing a more realistic assessment of athletes’ cardiovascular responses under actual training or competition conditions. This represents a significant advantage over conventional cable-based Holter monitoring, which can be cumbersome and less representative of real sporting activity.10 This approach may improve the sensitivity of abnormality detection and, when combined with other tests, enhance diagnostic accuracy and clinical relevance.

At our elite Spanish multisport club, the standard preparticipation evaluation (PPE) includes medical history, physical examination, resting ECG and echocardiography. This study aimed to assess whether adding 24-hour Holter monitoring—including both sleep and exercise periods—provides incremental diagnostic value beyond the current screening protocol, by increasing the detection of findings that warrant further evaluation.

Methodology

Study design, setting and participants

This was a descriptive observational study, without a gold standard or longitudinal outcomes, conducted among athletes from a Spanish elite multisport club during the 2023–2024 preseason. Participating teams included:

Men’s and women’s soccer: first team, second team, under-18 (U18) and under-16 (U16).

All athletes underwent standardised medical screenings, including anamnesis and physical examination, in accordance with the Bethesda conference recommendations,11 baseline 12-lead ECG, transthoracic echocardiography and 24-hour Holter monitoring.12,14 Additional tests, such as X-ray or MRI, were performed as required. Data were recorded in the FCB COR system. Device specifications included GE Cardiosoft (ECG), Canon Aplio i900 (ultrasound Doppler) and NUUBO (24-hour Holter monitoring). All studies were performed by experienced cardiologists and digitally stored for offline analysis. In cases of disagreement, the entire cardiology team reviewed the data.

Each test has distinct capabilities in detecting conduction, morphological and haemodynamic abnormalities, which limits direct comparison between them.

Besides, in our real-world clinical setting, cardiologists had access to the full clinical and diagnostic information when interpreting results, and formal blinding between modalities was not performed.

Online supplemental appendix contains a complete and expanded explanation of pathological thresholds according to each of the methods used (online supplemental S1.1).

Electrocardiography

Pathological criteria on the ECG were defined according to the 2017 International Criteria for Electrocardiographic Interpretation in Athletes.15

Including: abnormal T wave inversion, ST depression, pathological Q waves, complete left bundle branch block, intraventricular conduction delay, epsilon waves, ventricular pre-excitation, prolonged QT, Type 1 Brugada pattern, marked sinus bradycardia, deep first degree and high-grade AV block, ventricular extrasystoles and premature ventricular complexes, atrial and ventricular arrhythmias.

Echocardiogram

Pathological criteria for transthoracic echocardiography used in this study were based on the European Association of Echocardiography Guidelines.14 Given the specific physiological cardiac adaptations observed in athletes, tailored echocardiographic thresholds were applied to define abnormal findings, ensuring differentiation between training-related remodelling and true pathology and included:

Left ventricular wall thickness, left ventricular dilation, right ventricular dilation or systolic dysfunction, left atrium volume index, regional wall motion abnormalities, left ventricular ejection fraction, biventricular remodelling, suspicion of cardiomyopathy, bicuspid aortic valve, mitral valve prolapse, valvular abnormalities, aortic dilation, pericardial effusion, congenital heart disease.

24-hour ECG monitoring

For 24-hour ECG monitoring, pathological criteria targeted clinically relevant arrhythmias and/or conduction abnormalities. As with imaging, adapted thresholds were applied to differentiate physiological adaptations from pathological findings in athletes. Criteria were based on the 2017 consensus of the International Society for Holter and Non-invasive Electrocardiology and the Heart Rhythm Society on ambulatory ECG monitoring, with specific modifications for athletes (figure 1) and include:

Atrial fibrillation or atrial flutter, supraventricular tachycardia and Wolf-Parkinson-White Syndrome, second and third degree AV Block, sinus pauses >3 s, resting bradycardia <30 bpm, frequent PVCs, sustained or non-sustained ventricular tachycardia, frequent supraventricular extrasystoles, sinus node dysfunction in symptomatic individuals, long QT syndrome.

Patient and public involvement

Patients or the public were not involved in the design, or conduct, or reporting, or dissemination plans of our research

Statistical analysis

This study aimed to analyse and compare the incremental detection of pathological findings using different screening tools—anamnesis, 24-hour Holter monitoring (Nuubo), transthoracic echocardiography and resting ECG—both individually and in combination. Descriptive and inferential statistics were used to assess whether the number and type of tests performed influenced detection rates. An important limitation has been that these analyses were exploratory in nature and were not adjusted for multiple testing. Besides, the statistical analyses were intended to explore differences between predefined screening combinations rather than to imply a confirmatory trial design.

To compare diagnostic performance across different test combinations, Cochran’s Q test and McNemar’s test were applied.

Baseline characteristics, including age, sex, height, weight, sports discipline and competitive level, were examined in relation to the final classification (healthy vs pathological). Continuous variables were analysed using the Student’s t-test or Mann-Whitney U test, depending on the normality of the distribution, while categorical variables were compared using the χ² test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate.

A p<0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Regarding sample size, this was a consecutive cohort of all elite athletes undergoing mandatory preseason screening within our institution during the 2023–2024 season. As such, no formal a priori power calculation was performed.

Results

Basal characteristics

The study findings highlight three key points: as expected, the greater the number of tests performed, the more pathological findings are identified; no combination of two additional tests proved clearly superior; and 24-hour Holter monitoring provides complementary differential detection without overshadowing the other tests. Only one athlete was disqualified, while most findings prompted further evaluation without imposing restrictions.

No significant differences were observed between athletes classified as healthy and those with findings requiring further evaluation in terms of baseline characteristics. Discussion

Principal findings

In this cohort of elite athletes undergoing comprehensive PPE, the addition of wireless 24-hour Holter monitoring increased the detection of potentially pathological findings, particularly intermittent arrhythmias occurring during exercise, recovery or sleep. While no single diagnostic modality proved superior when combined with medical history and physical examination, Holter monitoring provided complementary information to resting ECG and echocardiography, supporting a multimodal rather than a test-centred screening strategy.

Of the 34 recordings with detected abnormalities, only 3 (8.8%) occurred in female athletes, despite women comprising 20% of the cohort (n=299), suggesting a potential sex-related disparity that warrants further investigation. This imbalance limits the generalisability of the findings, particularly to female athletes across different levels of competition.

Incremental value of Holter monitoring

Holter monitoring captures arrhythmias and conduction abnormalities that are intermittent or context-dependent and are therefore unlikely to be detected on a resting ECG. In our cohort, more than one-third of the potentially pathological findings identified by Holter monitoring occurred during exercise, including uncommon premature ventricular complex responses and complex ventricular ectopy, which cannot be reliably characterised on resting recordings. While approximately one-third of events occurred during training, we could not reliably determine whether the remaining events took place during sleep or wakefulness. Notably, eight athletes had abnormalities detected solely by Holter monitoring, which, although not leading to disqualification, allowed for closer follow-up and targeted evaluation. These findings highlight the importance of prolonged rhythm assessment under real-world physiological conditions.

Conversely, resting 12-lead ECG proved more effective in detecting spatially localised abnormalities, such as T-wave inversion, which may be missed by limited-lead Holter configurations. Echocardiography, in turn, uniquely contributed to the identification of structural abnormalities. Collectively, these results demonstrate that Holter monitoring does not replace existing tests but rather complements them by identifying different categories of abnormalities.16 17

However, increased detection may also have potential drawbacks, including patient anxiety, labelling effects and the risk of unnecessary downstream testing or temporary restrictions from sports participation, particularly when findings are of uncertain clinical significance. In addition, this study did not evaluate the cost-effectiveness or resource implications of routine or semiroutine Holter monitoring across the entire cohort, which should be considered an important area for future research.

Field-based monitoring versus laboratory testing

A key strength of this study is the use of field-based ambulatory 24-hour ECG Holter monitoring during actual training sessions,18 allowing assessment of cardiac rhythm under realistic physiological and psychological stressors. The value of evaluating athletes in their natural environment is increasingly recognised, as laboratory-based19 exercise stress testing may not fully reproduce the intensity, variability and contextual demands of competitive sports. In contrast, wireless Holter devices enable continuous rhythm assessment during training, recovery and sleep, providing a more comprehensive evaluation of athletes’ cardiovascular responses in daily practice.

Comparison with previous studies

Previous studies using exercise testing have reported variable detection rates of ventricular arrhythmias in athletes.19 20 Although evidence on its usefulness in PPE is limited, Grazioli et al19 found that exercise-induced ventricular arrhythmias were markers of underlying heart diseases, with pathological findings in 1.7% of athletes, while Zorzi et al21 reported a prevalence of 5% supporting their diagnostic value. Continuous wireless Holter monitoring during real-world training may capture a broader spectrum of arrhythmic events including variable workloads, recovery phases and competition-related stress. Although it did not perform a direct comparison and cannot claim superiority over exercise testing, our results suggest that combining exercise stress testing with ambulatory Holter monitoring may enhance arrhythmia detection. This could be further enhanced by multi-lead Holter devices (12 leads), allowing a more comprehensive assessment of cardiac electrical activity and detection of abnormalities that may be missed by single-lead systems. (online supplemental Appendix S1.2).

Clinical implications and target use

Current guidelines do not recommend routine Holter monitoring in all asymptomatic athletes, largely due to limited evidence and concerns regarding feasibility and cost-effectiveness. Our findings support a selective, risk-based approach rather than universal application. Holter monitoring may be particularly valuable for athletes with borderline or suspicious findings on resting ECG or echocardiography, those reporting symptoms, individuals with a family history of arrhythmias or SCD, athletes engaged in high-intensity sports, and those with known cardiac conditions who continue to participate competitively.

Importantly, despite the increased detection of abnormalities, only one athlete in this cohort was ultimately disqualified from competition. This underscores that enhanced detection does not necessarily translate into excessive restriction but rather facilitates appropriate further evaluation and risk stratification.

Conclusions

In elite athletes, 24-hour Holter ECG monitoring may offer valuable complementary information to resting ECG and echocardiography by capturing intermittent arrhythmias under real-world conditions. Its selective use, particularly in high-risk or borderline cases, appears both feasible and clinically meaningful, potentially improving the detection of relevant abnormalities, while minimising unnecessary restrictions on sports participation.
