Soluble α-synuclein oligomers drive transient corticostriatal pathology and delayed nigral vulnerability in a mouse model of early α-synucleinopathy
Authors: Anna Wilkaniec, Grzegorz A Czapski, Magdalena Cieślik, Gabriela Olech-Kochańczyk, Elżbieta Gawinek, Ewelina Bielska, Eva D Ruiz-Ortega, Lidia Babiec, Marcin Strawski, Piotr Wójcik, Carsten Culmsee, Agata Adamczyk
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstractα-Synucleinopathies display pronounced heterogeneity in the spatial distribution of α-synuclein (αSyn) pathology and clinical progression. Although distinct αSyn assemblies—from monomers and soluble oligomers to fibrils—exert non-equivalent biological effects, in vivo studies have predominantly focused on preformed fibrils (PFFs), leaving the pathogenic potential of soluble oligomers insufficiently explored. Here, we investigated the spatiotemporal, molecular, and behavioral consequences of striatal delivery of structurally validated αSyn oligomers in adult mice. Three-month-old male C57BL/6 J mice received bilateral injections of αSyn oligomers into the dorsal caudate–putamen and were analyzed at 30, 90, and 180 days post-injection (dpi) using molecular, histological, and behavioral approaches. αSyn oligomers induced a highly dynamic and region-specific pathological cascade. At 30 dpi, widespread inclusions were evident in cortical and limbic regions projecting to the striatum, followed by a progressive redistribution of pathology toward the striatum at later stages, while inclusions were consistently absent from the substantia nigra pars compacta. In parallel, αSyn oligomers elicited distinct spatiotemporal patterns of inflammatory and oxidative responses across brain regions, characterized by an immediate pro-inflammatory cytokine surge in the striatum, early but transient oxidative response in the cortex and delayed, sustained oxidative stress in the midbrain. Despite modest nigrostriatal degeneration and preserved gross motor performance, sensitive behavioral measures revealed early and persistent motor weakness, suggesting synaptic and axonal dysfunction rather than neuronal loss. Collectively, our findings provide the first in vivo evidence that soluble αSyn oligomers act as potent yet transient drivers of a distributed and partially reversible neuropathological program fundamentally distinct from canonical PFF-based models. By uncovering an oligomer-specific mode of αSyn-induced neurodegeneration, this work demonstrates that disease-relevant pathology can arise from dynamic, regionally uncoupled processes rather than stable fibrillar inclusions, redefining early α-synucleinopathy as a state of selective circuit vulnerability and revealing a previously unrecognized therapeutic window for intervention.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40478-026-02340-9.Keywords: Soluble α-synuclein oligomers, α-synucleinopathies, Intrastriatal, Dopaminergic, Astrocyte, Microglia

Introduction

α-Synucleinopathies constitute a clinically and pathologically heterogeneous group of neurodegenerative disorders of major medical and societal relevance. Their defining feature is the misfolding and aggregation of alpha-synuclein (αSyn) in the nervous system, yet the anatomical distribution, cellular targets, and clinical manifestations differ substantially across disease entities. This spectrum includes Parkinson’s disease (PD), dementia with Lewy bodies (DLB), and pure autonomic failure (PAF), which are characterized predominantly by neuronal inclusions, as well as multiple system atrophy (MSA), where αSyn accumulates preferentially in oligodendrocytes [63, 65]. REM sleep behavior disorder (RBD), often considered a prodromal α-synucleinopathy, also shows widespread pathology involving brainstem and autonomic neurons [1]. Advances in biomarkers, neuroimaging, and ultrasensitive αSyn detection assays have recently improved early detection and differential diagnosis, highlighting the need to clarify how distinct αSyn conformers contribute to disease heterogeneity [64].

αSyn exists in multiple conformational states, ranging from soluble monomers to oligomers, protofibrils, and mature fibrils, each with distinct seeding capacity, neurotoxicity, and potential disease specificity. Early research focused predominantly on fibrillar aggregates, and most experimental paradigms have employed preformed fibrils (PFFs) or viral overexpression models. However, increasing evidence indicates that small, soluble oligomers represent highly neurotoxic species in PD and related disorders. These assemblies disrupt membranes, impair mitochondrial function, and trigger glial activation, thereby propagating oxidative and inflammatory stress [21, 25, 37, 74–76]. Their bidirectional interactions with glial cells further amplify neuroinflammation, which is increasingly recognized as a key driver of progression [30, 38, 39].

Despite these insights, the contribution of soluble αSyn oligomers to in vivo pathogenesis remains insufficiently explored. In particular, little is known about how striatal delivery of oligomeric assemblies affects molecular, cellular, and behavioral outcomes across interconnected brain regions. As a major hub of corticostriatal and nigrostriatal circuits, the striatum is strategically positioned to propagate pathogenic signals toward both cortical and midbrain targets. Synaptic dysfunction in this region, including altered dopamine release and impaired plasticity, is thought to precede dopaminergic cell loss and contribute to early motor symptoms in PD [31].

The present study

was designed to address this gap by examining the early molecular, cellular, and behavioral consequences of bilateral striatal inoculation of αSyn oligomers in mice. We specifically investigated whether this paradigm recapitulates key hallmarks of PD, including αSyn deposition, oxidative imbalance, neuroinflammatory responses, and dopaminergic neuron loss, as well as subtle motor and non-motor impairments. By mapping these processes in a region- and time-dependent manner, our aim was to elucidate the pathogenic role of oligomeric assemblies and to provide new mechanistic insights into the early, potentially reversible stages of α-synucleinopathy.

Experimental design

Three-month-old male C57BL/6 J mice were transferred from the Animal Facility to the animal housing and laboratory unit for 1 week of acclimatization prior to the operation. Animals received bilateral injections of the validated αSyn oligomers into the dorsal caudate–putamen (CPu). Control animals underwent identical surgical procedures but received bilateral intrastriatal injections of sterile PBS. Separate experimental cohorts were sacrificed at 3 h, 30, 90, and 180 days post-injection (dpi) for molecular and histological analyses. A distinct longitudinal cohort was sequentially evaluated in behavioral tests at 30, 90, and 180 dpi and sacrificed at the final 180-day time point. Animals designated for biochemical and genetic analysis were anesthetized by isoflurane inhalation and decapitated. Animals designated for immunohistochemical analysis were anesthetized by a mixture of ketamine and xylazine and perfused (Fig. 1A).

Preparation of α-synuclein oligomers

Lyophilized recombinant murine αSyn (rPeptide, Bogart, GA, USA) was dissolved in Dulbecco’s phosphate-buffered saline (Sigma-Aldrich, USA) to obtain a 2 mg/ml stock solution, which was stored at –20 °C in small aliquots. For the preparation of oligomeric species, aliquots were thawed on ice and incubated in a Thermomixer Compact (Eppendorf, Hamburg, Germany) at 37 °C under continuous vertical agitation (800 rpm) for 24 h, as described in the protocol of [52] with minor modifications. This procedure favors the formation of soluble, non-fibrillar αSyn oligomers while minimizing fibril assembly. After incubation, the preparations were immediately used.

Endotoxin levels in the αSyn preparations were assessed using the PyroGene™ Recombinant Factor C Endpoint Fluorescent Assay (Lonza, Basel, Switzerland) and the Endosafe® Nexgen-PTS™ system (Charles River Laboratories, Wilmington, MA, USA), according to the manufacturers’ instructions. The endotoxin concentration in non-purified oligomer preparations was approximately 10 EU/ml. To reduce endotoxin contamination in αSyn, Pierce™ High-Capacity Endotoxin Removal Resin (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) was used according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Following purification, endotoxin levels were reduced to below 0.1 EU/ml (Supplementary Fig. 1; S1).

Atomic force microscopy (AFM)

AFM analysis was performed as described previously [73]. Samples were prepared by applying a 10 µl drop of medium to freshly cleaved mica, V1 grade (NanoAndMore GmbH, Germany). After incubation for 10 min, the sample was rinsed with deionised water (Merck Millipore Inc., Burlington, MA, USA) and dried under a gentle argon stream. A Multimode 8 Nanoscope atomic force microscope (AFM, Bruker, Billerica, MA, USA) was used to image the surfaces of the mica substrate and the deposited Samples. High-resolution probes (Single HiRes150, BudgetSensors®, Innovative Solutions Bulgaria Ltd.) were used for imaging in PeakForce Tapping® mode.

Glutaraldehyde cross-linking and SDS-PAGE analysis of α-synuclein oligomers

For glutaraldehyde (GA) cross-linking, αSyn samples (50 μM) were incubated with 0.05% GA at room temperature for 45 min. The reaction was quenched by adding Tris–HCl, pH 8.0, to a final concentration of 10 mM. After an additional 10 min incubation at room temperature, the samples were stored overnight at 4 °C and analyzed the following day by SDS-PAGE using a 10% gel. Proteins were then visualized by standard Coomassie staining.

Intrastriatal administration of α-synuclein oligomers

The stereotaxic coordinates, injection parameters, and general surgical approach were based on the protocol described by [43] with modifications. Mice were anesthetized by intraperitoneal injection of a mixture of ketamine (70 mg/kg body weight (b.w.); Biowet sp. z o. o., Puławy, Poland) and medetomidine hydrochloride (Domitor, 1 mg/kg b.w.; Orion Corporation, Espoo, Finland). Prior to surgery, animals received subcutaneous injections of the analgesic butorphanol (Butomidor, 5 mg/kg b.w.; Richter Pharma AG, Wels, Austria) and the antibiotic enrofloxacin (Enrotron, 5 mg/kg b.w.; aniMedica GmBH, Senden, Germany). To prevent the corneal surface from drying, the eyes were covered with a moisturizing ointment. The absence of deep pain reflexes was verified by toe pinch before placing the animals in a stereotaxic apparatus (RWD Life Science Co., Ltd., Shenzhen, China). The scalp was disinfected with an iodine solution, and a midline incision was made to expose the skull. Small burr holes were drilled at the coordinates corresponding to the dorsal striatum: + 0.2 mm anterior to bregma, ± 2.0 mm lateral, and − 2.6 mm ventral from the skull surface. Using a 10 µl Hamilton microsyringe fitted with a 33-gauge stainless-steel needle (Hamilton Company, Reno, NV, USA) connected to an automated infusion pump (KD Scientific model 78–0315, Stoelting Co., Wood Dale, IL, USA), 5 µg of αSyn oligomers in 2.5 µl PBS were injected per hemisphere (total 10 µg/5 µl per animal) at a rate of 0.4 µl/min. Control animals underwent identical surgical procedures but received bilateral intrastriatal injections of sterile PBS (2 × 2.5 µl). After injection, the needle was left in place for an additional 5 min to minimize backflow before being slowly withdrawn. The scalp incision was closed with absorbable sutures (Novosyn 4.0; Braun Surgical SA, Barcelona, Spain), and lidocaine gel was applied topically. Anesthesia was reversed with an intraperitoneal injection of atipamezole hydrochloride (Revertor, 1 mg/kg b.w.; CP-Pharma Handelsges mbH, Burgdorf, Germany). Animals were kept on a heated pad (37–39 °C) until they had fully recovered, then housed individually for 2–3 days to allow wound healing. For postoperative care, enrofloxacin (5 mg/kg b.w.; aniMedica GmBH, Senden, Germany) and butorphanol (5 mg/kg b.w.; Richter Pharma AG, Wels, Austria) were administered subcutaneously once daily for two consecutive days.

Behavioral analysis

Behavioral tests were performed in a test room that was familiar to the animals by familiar operators. Animals’ behavior was recorded by a Basler acA1300-60 GigE camera (Bassler AG, Ahrensburg, Germany), and calculated using Ethovision XT 10 software version 10.1.856 (Noldus Information Technology, Wageningen, Netherlands) or analyzed by the blinded operator. The manual analysis was supported by using the BehaView version 0.0.21 (2025.05.25) software http://www.pmbogusz.net/?a=behaview (accessed 05.08.2021). Animals at 30, 90, and 180 dpi were tested. To avoid overtiring the animals during testing, different test sets were performed for each series of animals. Series I: open field and rotarod; Series II: open field, rotarod, odor test; Series III: pole test, novel object recognition (NOR), grip strength test; Series IV: pole test, NOR, grip strength test; Series V: open field, rotarod, NOR, odor test; Series VI: pole test, grip strength test, odor test. The mice were given a one-day break between consecutive tests. All tests were performed between 8 a.m. and 1 p.m.

Open field test

The open-field test was performed as described previously with modifications [16]. In this test, general locomotor activity, exploration of a novel environment, and anxiety-related behavior are measured. Animals were individually placed in the open field chamber (grey PCV box, 55 × 55 × 30 cm), and the total distance, exploration of the central zone (the central zone; square 33 × 33 cm), and the border zone were analyzed. The number, duration, and latency of grooming, rearing, and climbing events were counted by the blinded operator.

Novel object recognition test

The test was performed, as described previously, with modifications [16]. In this test, the natural tendency of rodents to explore novel objects is exploited to assess non-spatial memory. One day before testing, mice were allowed to freely explore the test chamber (a grey PCV box, 30 × 20 × 30 cm) for 5 min to habituate to the chamber. The experimental session comprised two 5-min trials, with the second trial (T2) occurring 120 min after the first (T1). In the T1 trial, two identical objects (O1 and O1) were placed in the chamber. During the second T2 trial, one of the O1 objects was replaced with the alternative object O2. As testing objects, we used a set of colorful plastic square bricks and a 75 cm2 cell-culture bottle filled with sand. The objects presented during sessions were free of olfactory cues, and their positions within the chamber were randomized to eliminate any potential spatial bias in the task. At the beginning of each trial, mice were placed in the center of the box, facing away from the objects. The primary measurement was the total time spent by mice exploring each object during T1 and T2 trials. Exploration of an object was defined as: directing the nose within 2 cm of the object and/or touching it with the nose. Climbing time was excluded from the analysis. The index of discrimination (ID) for each animal was calculated as a ratio: ID = time spent exploring the novel object (O2) / (time spent exploring the novel object (O2) + time spent exploring the known object (O1)).

Accelerating rotarod

The test was performed, as described previously, with modifications [50]. The rotarod test was used to assess motor coordination and balance in rodents. Mice were required to maintain balance on a rotating rod as its speed increased. The latency to fall from the rod rotating at an increasing speed (from 4 to 40 rpm over 5 min) was recorded. The test consisted of three trials with 15-min intervals between trials. The best result (longest time) and the average of the 3 results were used for calculations. The experiments were conducted using a Rota Rod Model 47,700 apparatus (Ugo Basile S.R.L., Comerio, Italy).

Pole test

The procedure was conducted following the previously established protocol [26]. Each mouse was gently placed on top of the vertical wooden pole with a rough surface (50 cm height, 1 cm diameter), and head-up. The time it took for the animal to turn and face downward and then descend to the bedding was measured. The animal underwent 5 test trials with a 5-min break between trials. The best result (shortest time) from the 5 trials for each mouse was selected, reflecting its maximum locomotor performance in this specific task.

Grip test

The procedure was conducted as described previously with modifications [6], using the grip strength meter from BioSeb (Vitrolles, France). Mice were allowed to grasp the metal wire mesh with their forelimbs, while the experimenter held the animal by the tail and pulled it upwards with increasing force until the animal released the grip. The maximum value recorded by the apparatus was noted as grip strength (N). Each mouse was tested three times with a 10-min break between trials. The best (highest) result from the 3 trials for a given animal was taken for analysis.

Analysis of potential forms of αSyn aggregation using sequential supernatant fractionation

For the analysis of potential forms of αSyn aggregates, protein extraction and solubility assay were performed as described previously [4]. First, the striatum and midbrain from C57BL/6 J mice (30, 90, and 180 dpi) were dissected, transferred to separate tubes, immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at − 80 °C until use. Then, the frozen tissue was homogenized in 10 volumes of buffer (50 mM Tris–HCl, pH 7.4, 150 mM NaCl, supplemented with 1 × protease and 1 × phosphatase inhibitor cocktail) using a mechanical homogenizer for 10 s, followed by incubation on ice for 2 min; this cycle was repeated three times. Samples were clarified by centrifugation at 1000 × g for 5 min at 4 °C to remove non-homogenized material, and the supernatant was retained as the crude fraction (unfractionated homogenate). The crude fraction was centrifuged at 100,000 × g for 1 h at 4 °C, and the supernatant (S1) was retained; S1 represents the TBS-soluble fraction. The pellet was washed in TBS and ultracentrifuged at 100,000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C to effectively remove soluble material. The supernatant was discarded, and the pellet was resuspended in 5 volumes of 1 × TBS-5%SDS (50 mM Tris–HCl pH 7.4, 150 mM NaCl, 5% SDS; with protease and phosphatase inhibitor cocktail) by sonicating the pellet for 10 s at 20 kHz. The sample was centrifuged at 100,000 × g for 30 min at 25 °C. The resulting supernatant (S2) was collected, representing the detergent (SDS)- soluble fraction (SDS-soluble). The pellet from this step was washed by resuspension in 5 volumes of 1 × TBS-5% SDS and centrifuged at 100,000 × g for 15 min at 25 °C. The final pellet was resuspended in 50 μl of 1 × TBS-5%SDS-8 M urea buffer (50 mM Tris–HCl, pH 7.4, 150 mM NaCl, 5% SDS, 8 M urea; with protease and phosphatase inhibitor cocktail) using a sonicator set at 20 kHz for 10 s; then shaken for 12–18 h at room temperature (RT) to attain complete solubilization. This fraction is termed S3 and represents the insoluble fraction. Protein was quantified using Pierce™ BCA Protein Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions, with BSA as a standard. All samples were diluted in 2 × Laemmli buffer and denatured for 5 min at 95 °C. About 10 µg of total protein from each fraction was loaded and run on standard 12% SDS-PAGE gels. After protein separation, proteins were transferred via the “wet” transfer method onto nitrocellulose membranes at 50 V for 2 h and analyzed using the Western blotting method. The membranes were washed with H2O following a 5-min wash in TBS-T buffer (100 mM Tris pH 7.6, 140 mM NaCl, and 0.1% Tween 20). Membranes were then blocked with 5% BSA in TBS-T buffer for 1 h at RT, following an overnight incubation at 4 °C with the primary antibody against αSyn (1:1000, 5% BSA in TBS-T). The next day, the membranes were incubated with goat anti-rabbit HRP-conjugated secondary antibody (1: 4000 in 5% non-fat milk in TBS-T) for 1 h at RT, after which the protein bands were visualized using the Clarity Western ECL Substrate (Bio-Rad, USA) under standard conditions. Images were acquired using the FUSION FX imaging system (Vilber Lourmat Deutschland GmbH, Eberhardzell, Germany) controlled by EvolutionCapt FX6 software. Densitometric analysis was performed using TotalLab4 software (NonLinear Dynamics Ltd., Newcastle upon Tyne, UK) using glyceraldehyde 3-phosphate dehydrogenase level as loading control and for data normalization (GAPDH, 1:8000, in 5% non-fat milk in TBS-T; secondary goat-anti-mouse antibody, 1:4000, in 5% non-fat milk in TBS-T). Detailed information on antibodies used for Western blot analysis is presented in Table 2.

Western immunoblotting

The immunochemical analysis was performed using Western blotting under standard conditions. Tissue samples were homogenized using a 2 mL syringe fitted with a 0.6 mm diameter needle in 2 × Laemmli buffer (125 mM Tris–HCl, pH 6.8, 2% SDS, 100 mM DTT, 0.2 mM bromophenol blue, and 50% (v/v) glycerol) to obtain 2.5% (w/v) homogenates. The samples were subsequently sonicated for 10 s and denatured at 95 °C for 5 min prior to electrophoresis. Samples were run in either 10 (for GFAP, PSD-95, SYP and P2Y12), 12 (for aggregates of αSyn), or 15% (IBA-1) SDS-PAGE. After protein separation, proteins were transferred via the “wet” transfer method onto nitrocellulose membranes at 50 V for 2 h, and then detected by immunodetection with specific antibodies. Membranes were washed for 5 min in TBS-T, and then non-specific binding was blocked by incubation with 5% non-fat milk in TBST for 1 h at RT. Membranes were probed with the primary antibodies (1:1000, in 5% non-fat milk in TBS-T; Table 2) and incubated overnight at 4 °C. Next day, membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibody (1: 4000 in 5% milk in TBS-T) for 1 h at RT, after which the protein bands were visualized using the Clarity Western ECL Substrate (Bio-Rad, USA) under standard conditions. Images were acquired using the FUSION FX imaging system) controlled by EvolutionCapt FX6 software. Densitometric analysis was performed using TotalLab4 software (NonLinear Dynamics Ltd., Newcastle upon Tyne, UK) with GAPDH as the loading control and for data normalization (1:8000 in 5% non-fat milk in TBS-T; secondary goat-anti-mouse antibody, 1:4000 in 5% non-fat milk in TBS-T). Proteins were detected using a stripping and re-probing procedure. Detailed information on antibodies used for Western blot analysis is presented in Table 2.

Quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR)

Total RNA was extracted using TRI Reagent (Sigma-Aldrich, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. RNA concentration and purity were measured using a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). To remove potential genomic DNA contamination, samples were treated with DNase I (Sigma-Aldrich, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Complementary DNA (cDNA) was synthesized from 1 µg of total RNA using a High-Capacity cDNA Reverse Transcription Kit with RNase Inhibitor (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) following the manufacturer’s protocol. Quantitative PCR was performed on an ABI 7500 Real-Time PCR System using TaqMan Fast Advanced Master Mix (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Reactions were carried out in triplicate in a final volume of 10 µl. The relative expression levels of target genes were calculated using the comparative ΔΔCt method, with Actb (β-actin) serving as the endogenous reference gene. The following TaqMan Gene Expression Assays (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) were used: Tnfα (Mm00443258_m1), Il1b (Mm00434228_m1), Il6 (Mm00446190_ m1), Ifng (Mm01168134_m1), Nos2 (Mm00440502_m1), Actb (Mm00607939_s1). The results are presented as fold change (FC).

Measurement of the reactive oxygen species (ROS) level

Measurement of the ROS level was carried out using fluorescent probe 2′,7′-dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate (DCFH-DA) (Cayman Chemical Company, Ann Arbour, MI, USA), as described previously [18]. Cellular esterases deacetylate DCFH-DA to 2′,7′-dichlorodihydrofluorescein (DCFH) and then may be oxidised to a highly fluorescent compound, 2′,7′-dichlorofluorescein (DCF). 50 µl of 2% brain tissue homogenates (in PBS), were incubated with 50 µl of 20 μM of DCFH-DA in the dark for 45 min at 37 °C. DCF fluorescence was measured using a microplate reader TECAN Infinite M1000PRO (Tecan US, Research Triangle Park, NC) at 488 nm excitation and 525 nm emission wavelengths. Each sample was analysed in triplicate. To confirm that deacetylation of probe was not a limiting factor of reaction, each sample was incubated additionally in the presence of 10 μM FeCl2 (positive control). The results of fluorescence measurements are presented as arbitrary units (AU).

Measurement of the NADPH oxidase (NOX) activity

NOX activity was determined by the initial rate of inhibitable ferricytochrome c reduction, and the measurements were performed based on the method described previously [62]. Tissue samples were homogenized in ice-cold PBS (pH 7.4) for 5% homogenates. The reaction buffer contained 100 mM K,Na-phosphate buffer, 1 mM SDS (Sigma-Aldrich, Saint Louis, MO, USA), 1 mM cytochrome c (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), and 1 mM FAD (Sigma-Aldrich, Saint Louis, MO, USA). Before being added to the reaction buffer, cytochrome c was reduced by incubation for 20 min in the dark with DTT (100 mM) (Sigma-Aldrich, Saint Louis, MO, USA). The enzyme activity in the homogenates was measured with and without the presence of the NADPH oxidase inhibitor (apocynin to final concentration 5 mM) (Sigma-Aldrich, Saint Louis, MO, USA). The reaction was initiated by the addition of NADPH (Sigma-Aldrich, Saint Louis, MO, USA) to final concentration 2 mM. Each sample was measured on a microplate reader, Multiscan GO (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), in duplicate at 550 nm in a kinetic loop for 30 min (kinetic interval 1 min). The enzyme activity was calculated as the difference between measurements with and without an inhibitor in one chosen time point (same for every sample) and presented as arbitrary units (AU).

The level of superoxide anion generation and redox status of cells.

The level of superoxide anion generation was determined using dihydroethidium (DHE) as a fluorogenic probe, as described previously [77]. DHE oxidation by superoxide anion leads to the formation of fluorescent 2-hydroethidium. However, DHE can also be oxidized by other reactive oxygen species to yield ethidium, which can be used as an indicator of the redox status of cells. For measure of both superoxide anion generation and redox status of cells 50 µl of 2% brain tissue homogenates (in PBS), were added into 50 µl of 20 μM of DHE (in 5 mM glucose) and fluorescence was immediately measured using a microplate reader TECAN Infinite M1000PRO (Tecan US, Research Triangle Park, NC) at 37 °C at 400 nm excitation and 590 nm emission wavelengths for fluorescent 2-hydroethidium (superoxide anion generation indicator) and at 528 nm excitation and 605 nm emission wavelengths for yield ethidium (redox status of cells indicator). Each sample was analyzed in triplicate. The results of fluorescence measurements are presented as arbitrary units (AU).

The morphometric analysis of microglia and astrocytes in the motor cortex and striatum was performed as previously described [3]. Semiquantitative analysis of microglia and astroglia density

The semiquantitative analysis of microglia and astroglia density within the cortex, midbrain and striatum was performed by manually counting Iba1-or GFAP-positive cells in six randomly selected fields (200 × 200 μm) per region per animal. Fields were randomly selected with three from the left and three from the right hemisphere. The procedure was performed by a blinded operator and followed the method described previously [3]. For immunohistochemical analyses, brain hemispheres were used as the unit of analysis. From each animal, both hemispheres were processed and analyzed separately, yielding two samples per animal. This approach was chosen to increase anatomical sampling and tissue utilization in region-specific IHC analyses.

Statistical analysis

The group size was planned using G*Power 3.1 software [24]. Standard assumptions adopted: test power = 0.95, α = 0.05, groups of equal size. Based on group size calculation, it was necessary to use a total of 145 animals in the control group and 145 in the αSyn group. No exclusion criteria were established a priori. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism version 8.3.0 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). Data distribution was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Outliers were identified using the Grubbs test and removed if P < 0.05. Data are presented as mean ± SD.

Depending on the experimental design, statistical analyses were performed using either a two-tailed Student’s t-test or two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Sidak’s post hoc test with correction for multiple comparisons. Repeated-measures statistical analysis was applied only to longitudinal datasets in which the same animals were assessed across multiple time points, namely, body weight measurements and behavioral analyses. For all other experiments, independent groups of animals were analyzed at each time point.

Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. Unless otherwise indicated, N refers to independent biological samples (individual animals). In Figs. 10, 11, 15, and 16, N refers to the analyzed hemispheres.

Morphological characterization of α-synuclein oligomers and animal condition

The morphology of αSyn assemblies was analyzed by AFM on mica surfaces, which provide atomically smooth, flat substrates for high-resolution height measurements. The image of the surface before protein deposition is shown in Fig. 1B. The statistical roughness, as measured by the root mean square (RMS) parameter, is 0.09 nm under the specified measurement conditions. Figure 1C shows the sample after monomer deposition. The surface is characterized by a granular structure, which we attribute to the presence of monomers. The diameter size in the case of such small objects is burdened with an error due to geometric reasons; therefore, the height of the objects was analyzed. The image is dominated by structures with a height of up to 0.5 nm, although larger grains are also visible. We assume that in the case of densely packed structures, local overlap of the objects of interest is expected during the deposition and drying processes of the sample. The RMS value for this sample is 0.26 nm. After deposition of oligomeric structures, we observed an increase in roughness; the RMS parameter is equal to 0.46 nm, which we interpret as an increase in the size of the base structures (Fig. 1D). Profile analysis indicates that the recorded objects have an average height of up to 1 nm. Again, the sample is not entirely free from the effect of local clumping, and the presence of larger grains can be observed. Nevertheless, these samples behave in the expected way and are observed in other works [21]. They are also distinct from the αSyn fibrils shown in Fig. 1E, characterized as fibrous structures with predominant heights of approximately 10 and 20 nm, as indicated by the profile line; these correspond to diameters determined by cryo-TEM, with the main fibril diameter reported as 10 nm [40]. Together, these AFM data confirm that the injected material consisted predominantly of oligomeric αSyn assemblies, clearly distinct from both monomeric and fibrillar forms.

Additional biochemical characterization of the αSyn preparations was performed using glutaraldehyde cross-linking followed by SDS-PAGE analysis (Fig. 1F). Cross-linking stabilized transient oligomeric assemblies and revealed the presence of multiple αSyn species with distinct molecular weights. In addition to the predominant monomeric band (~ 13.4 kDa), higher-molecular-weight species, including the band corresponding to oligomeric assemblies (~ 95.8 kDa), were detected in oligomer-containing samples. This pattern is consistent with the heterogeneous nature of αSyn oligomer populations and supports the presence of soluble multimeric assemblies in the preparation. Importantly, these biochemical findings are in agreement with the AFM analysis, which demonstrated increased particle size and surface roughness in oligomeric samples compared with monomeric αSyn, while remaining clearly distinct from mature fibrillar structures.

Endotoxin levels in the initial αSyn oligomer preparations were approximately 10 EU/ml, as determined using recombinant factor C–based and Endosafe assays. Additional purification using endotoxin-removal columns reduced endotoxin levels to below 0.1 EU/ml. Importantly, control experiments demonstrated that endotoxin depletion did not substantially alter the early induction of Tnfα and Il-1β expression following αSyn administration (Supp. Fig. S1). However, endotoxin-removal procedures were also associated with partial loss of protein material and changes in preparation yield, which could potentially affect the stability and composition of oligomeric assemblies. Therefore, all long-term in vivo experiments were performed using the original, non-depleted preparations characterized by AFM and biochemical analyses described above.

Although no quantitative clinical scoring was performed, daily systemic assessment did not reveal any noticeable alterations in the general condition of the animals following intrastriatal administration of αSyn oligomers compared with control animals. Animals did not display overt signs of distress, abnormal posture, or alterations in spontaneous activity, and no treatment-related morbidity or mortality was observed throughout the observation period. Consistently, body weight trajectories did not differ between experimental groups over the entire observation period (Fig. 1F). During the experiment, nine animals died prematurely. Eight deaths occurred during the surgical procedure (three during anesthesia before operation and five during operation or directly after operation, three animals from the control group, and two from the αSyn group). One animal from αSyn group died three months after operation.

Regional and biochemical characterization of αSyn pathology following striatal oligomer delivery

We mapped αSyn pathology using phospho-Ser129 αSyn (pSer129-αSyn) immunostaining. At 30 dpi, widespread inclusions were evident in cortical and limbic regions that project to the caudate–putamen (CPu). Staining manifested as intraneuronal somatic and neuritic accumulations, which included both diffuse Lewy neurite-like (LN-like) and compact Lewy body-like (LB-like) inclusions. Representative examples of somatic inclusions are indicated by red arrows, whereas neuritic inclusions are marked by blue arrows (Fig. 2A). An overview of the regional distribution of α Syn pathology at 30, 90, and 180 days after striatal injection of α Syn oligomers in the analyzed brain regions is provided in Supplementary Fig. 2 (Fig. S2). Quantitative region-specific analysis demonstrated that the highest inclusion burden was observed in the primary and secondary motor cortices (Fig. 2B; Fig. S2). An intermediate level of pathology was detected in the primary somatosensory cortex (Fig. 2B; Fig. S2), as well as in the temporal association cortex (TeA), dorsal and ventral secondary auditory cortices (AuD and AuV), and the perirhinal (PRh), ectorhinal (Ect), and lateral entorhinal (LEnt) cortices (Fig. 2C). In contrast, the lowest inclusion burden was found in the CPu, corresponding to the injection site, where pathology remained relatively sparse at 30 dpi (Fig. 2B; Fig. S2). Over time, cortical inclusion load progressively decreased but remained clearly detectable at moderate levels at 180 dpi, whereas pathology within the CPu showed a relative increase (Fig. 2B; Fig. S2). Summary of regional inclusion burden across time points is provided in Fig. 2D. Notably, no pSer129-positive inclusions were detected in the substantia nigra pars compacta (SNpc) at any analyzed time point.

In contrast, the midbrain exhibited a markedly different pattern of pSer129 immunoreactivity. Although classical Lewy body-like inclusions were not observed, diffuse and punctate pSer129-positive signals were detectable, particularly at 30 dpi. These signals did not exhibit the morphology of mature fibrillar inclusions and instead appeared as dispersed non-classical αSyn-positive aggregates. At later time points, the pSer129-positive signal in the midbrain became less prominent and remained detectable mainly as weak diffuse staining.

This analysis confirmed significant region- and time-dependent differences in pSer129 αSyn signal intensity, supporting the distinct temporal dynamics of αSyn pathology observed in the striatum, cortex, and midbrain.

Together, these observations indicate that αSyn pathology in the midbrain differs qualitatively from the overt inclusion pathology observed in cortical and striatal regions and is characterized predominantly by sparse diffuse or punctate pSer129-positive species rather than classical Lewy body-like aggregates.

This approach enables qualitative assessment of αSyn distribution across fractions with different biochemical solubility properties, corresponding to distinct aggregation states.

The TBS-soluble fraction, representing predominantly soluble αSyn species, was detectable under all experimental conditions. In the striatum of αSyn-injected mice (Fig. 5A), αSyn immunoreactivity was additionally detected in the SDS-soluble fraction, consistent with the presence of less soluble oligomeric assemblies, and in the urea/SDS-soluble fraction, corresponding to highly insoluble aggregated species, at 30 and 90 dpi. α-Synuclein oligomers elicit oxidative disturbances with distinct spatiotemporal signatures

Considering the established involvement of oxidative stress in αSyn-induced neurodegeneration, we next examined whether αSyn accumulation is associated with region-specific disruptions in redox homeostasis. To this end, we quantified general ROS production using DCF fluorescence, superoxide accumulation using DHE fluorescence measured at excitation/emission wavelengths selective for 2-hydroxyethidium formation, enzymatic sources of ROS through NADPH oxidase (NOX) activity, and the overall cellular redox state using alternative DHE fluorescence settings corresponding predominantly to ethidium formation (Fig. 6). We first assessed overall oxidative stress using DCF fluorescence at 30, 90, and 180 days post-injection (Fig. 6A). A significant increase in the midbrain was observed at 30 and 90 dpi, indicating an early and sustained elevation of ROS in this structure, which returned to baseline by 180 dpi. In the cortex, a statistically significant but transient increase was detected at 30 dpi. To specifically evaluate superoxide accumulation, DHE fluorescence was measured at 400 nm excitation and 590 nm emission wavelengths, corresponding predominantly to formation of 2-hydroxyethidium (Fig. 6B) This analysis revealed a robust elevation in the midbrain at both 30 and 90 dpi, whereas in the cortex, a significant increase was observed only at 30 dpi.

To further investigate enzymatic sources of ROS, we measured NOX activity (Fig. 6C). Interestingly, a significant increase was observed exclusively in the cortex at 30 dpi, suggesting early activation of NADPH oxidases as a potential contributor to cortical oxidative stress. Finally, to assess the overall cellular redox state, DHE fluorescence was additionally measured at 528 nm excitation and 605 nm emission wavelengths, corresponding predominantly to ethidium formation generated by oxidation of DHE by multiple reactive oxygen species (Fig. 6D). This revealed a pronounced shift toward a more oxidized environment in the midbrain at 30 and 90 dpi, accompanied by a parallel but transient redox shift in the cortex at 30 dpi that mirrored the pattern of changes in DCF and NOX. No significant alterations were observed in the striatum across any of the parameters and time points examined.

Taken together, these findings demonstrate that αSyn oligomers trigger distinct spatiotemporal patterns of oxidative disturbances. The midbrain exhibits sustained oxidative stress and superoxide accumulation, accompanied by a progressive redox imbalance, whereas the cortex mounts a rapid but short-lived response characterized by early ROS elevation, NOX activation, and transient redox alterations.

Characterization of astrocyte and microglia reactivity and proinflammatory cytokine expression following α-synuclein oligomer injection

Neuroinflammatory processes have increasingly been recognized as key contributors to the progression of α-synucleinopathies, and cytokines are among the earliest indicators of immune activation. To explore the potential involvement of neuroinflammation in αSyn oligomer-induced pathology, we assessed the expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines in multiple brain regions. We measured transcript levels of TNFα, Il-1β, Il-6, IFN-γ, and iNOS in the striatum, cortex, and midbrain at 3 h, 30 days, 90 days, and 180 days post-injection (Fig. 7).

Quantitative PCR analysis revealed a region- and time-dependent modulation of inflammatory gene expression following intrastriatal injection of αSyn oligomers. In the striatum, transient increases in Tnfα, Il-1β, and Il-6 mRNA expression were observed at 3 h post-injection (Fig. 7A). However, additional protein-level analyses performed by Western blot did not reveal detectable accumulation of TNFα or IL-1β protein at this time point (Supp. Figure S4), suggesting that these inflammatory changes are relatively subtle and tightly regulated. In the cortex, no significant early changes were detected, whereas delayed increases in Tnfα and Il-6 mRNA expression were observed at 180 days post-injection (Fig. 7C). In contrast to the striatum and cortex, the midbrain exhibited a distinct, highly selective pattern of inflammatory regulation (Fig. 7B). The expression of the major pro-inflammatory cytokines IL-1β, IL-6, and IFN-γ remained unchanged at all analyzed time points. TNFα expression in the midbrain was significantly reduced exclusively at 30 days post-injection. Moreover, iNOS expression exhibited a non-linear, time-dependent regulation in this region. A significant decrease in iNOS mRNA levels was detected as early as 3 h post-injection compared with control animals. At 30 days post-injection, iNOS expression returned to control values, whereas at 90 days a second significant reduction was observed. This decrease was followed by a subsequent normalization of iNOS expression to baseline levels at 180 days post-injection.

Together, these data indicate region-specific and temporally dynamic inflammatory alterations following αSyn oligomer administration. While transient changes in cytokine mRNA expression were detected in selected regions and time points, the absence of detectable cytokine protein accumulation suggests that these inflammatory responses are relatively modest and tightly regulated.

To investigate microglial alterations in response to intrastriatal injection of αSyn oligomers, we analysed protein levels of IBA-1—a marker of microglial abundance—and P2Y12, a purinergic receptor selectively expressed by homeostatic microglia and downregulated upon activation. Western blot analysis was performed on tissue from the striatum, cortex, and midbrain at 30, 90, and 180 dpi. In the striatum, IBA-1 levels remained unchanged across all time points (Fig. 8A), whereas P2Y12 protein levels decreased at 90 dpi (Fig. 8B). In the midbrain, IBA-1 expression showed a trend toward lower levels at 30 dpi (p = 0.06) compared to controls, while no differences were observed at 90 and 180 dpi (Fig. 8C). P2Y12 levels remained unchanged throughout all examined time points (Fig. 8D). In the cortex, IBA-1 expression remained comparable to control levels at all analysed time points (Fig. 8E). Similarly, cortical P2Y12 levels were stable throughout the analysed period (Fig. 8F).

To further examine the potential interaction between microglia and αSyn pathology, we performed double immunofluorescence staining for IBA1 and pSer129 αSyn (Fig. 12). In the striatum and cortex, where αSyn pathology was predominantly observed as compact Lewy body–like (LB-like) and diffuse Lewy neurite–like (LN-like) inclusions, no evident colocalization between pSer129 αSyn signal and IBA1-positive microglia was detected (Fig. 12A, C). However, because these observations are based on static imaging, the present data do not allow definitive conclusions regarding active phagocytosis or intracellular degradation of αSyn by microglia.

Intra-striatal administration of αSyn oligomers results in a limited decrease of dopaminergic neuron marker in the substantia nigra

Dopaminergic neurons of the substantia nigra pars compacta (SNpc) provide dense and topographically organized projections to both the dorsal and ventral striatum. This nigrostriatal connectivity has been comprehensively mapped and represents one of the principal dopaminergic pathways in the mammalian brain [70]. Based on this anatomical connectivity, we hypothesized that intrastriatal injection of αSyn oligomers would propagate pathology into the substantia nigra (SN). Accumulating experimental evidence indicates that pathological αSyn assemblies can exploit these dopaminergic projections for retrograde transport, allowing material deposited in the striatum to propagate back to dopaminergic cell bodies in the SN [43]. Accordingly, we hypothesized that intrastriatal delivery of αSyn assemblies would result in the retrograde spread of pathology along nigrostriatal axons, ultimately impacting nigral dopaminergic neurons; thus, we quantified tyrosine hydroxylase (TH)-positive neurons in the SN. A modest reduction in TH + neuron number was observed in the SNpc at 180 dpi, suggesting a delayed and possibly indirect impact on dopaminergic cell survival (Fig. 18A). In contrast, no significant changes were found in the ventral tegmental area (VTA), where TH⁺ neuron numbers remained comparable to controls at all examined time points (Fig. 18B).

Dicussion

α-Synucleinopathies display heterogeneous spatial patterns of αSyn pathology and clinical trajectories. Converging evidence indicates that distinct conformational states—monomers, soluble oligomers, protofibrils, and fibrils—exert non-equivalent biological effects. While many in vivo studies rely on preformed fibrils (PFFs) to model template-directed spread, soluble oligomers remain comparatively understudied despite mounting evidence for their early pathogenicity.

A central and previously unrecognized finding of this study is that oligomeric αSyn assemblies orchestrate a highly dynamic, region-specific pathological cascade, beginning with a rapid cortical inclusion burden and an early pro-inflammatory cytokine surge in the striatum, followed by delayed striatal αSyn aggregation, the emergence of oxidative stress in the midbrain, and culminating in only modest, late-onset degeneration of nigral dopaminergic neurons (Fig. 24).

This profile stands in sharp contrast to canonical PFF-based models, which typically produce stable inclusions in the SNpc and progressive degeneration of dopaminergic neurons. Our findings, therefore, highlight that soluble assemblies, long considered elusive intermediates, can leave a distinct and reversible footprint in the intact brain. Comparable findings have been reported by Bellingacci and colleagues, who found that striatal oligomer delivery triggered cortical αSyn accumulation and early behavioral changes in the absence of nigral inclusions, further supporting the idea that oligomers act as transient but disruptive species [5].

These results extend previous in vitro evidence that oligomers destabilize membranes, impair mitochondrial function, and induce oxidative stress [21, 25, 37, 74–76]. The present in vivo data demonstrate that such properties translate into selective metabolic disturbances and subtle functional deficits, even in the absence of stable inclusions. Particularly noteworthy is the dissociation between the timing of αSyn deposition and the onset of dopaminergic alterations: cortical and striatal deposits emerged early and later decreased, whereas the modest decrease in dopaminergic markers in the SNpc appeared with a clear delay. This interpretation is reinforced by complementary studies showing that oligomer inoculation into nigral or striatal regions produces redox alterations and metabolic reprogramming, even when inclusion burden is low or transient [48, 53]. This sequence suggests that soluble oligomers compromise neuronal integrity not only through local aggregation but also via sustained synaptic, inflammatory, and metabolic stress, underscoring their role as potent disruptors of neuronal resilience [27, 34].

Importantly, although classical Lewy body-like pSer129-positive inclusions were not detected in the SNpc using standard DAB-based histology, higher-resolution confocal imaging revealed sparse, diffuse, and punctate pSer129-positive signals in the midbrain. These structures did not exhibit the morphology of mature fibrillar inclusions and instead appeared as non-classical αSyn-positive assemblies, potentially corresponding to soluble or weakly aggregated oligomeric species. Together with the biochemical fractionation data, these observations suggest that biologically active αSyn species may persist in the midbrain in forms that are not readily detectable as overt inclusions.

The temporal and spatial trajectories we observed also refine current theories of disease progression. Early cortical pathology is best explained by transport along corticostriatal projections, as oligomers delivered to the striatum can be rapidly internalized at presynaptic terminals and retrogradely transported to projection neurons [5, 17, 28]. By contrast, striatal inclusions accumulated more slowly, perhaps reflecting delayed nucleation dynamics in medium spiny neurons or differences in proteostatic capacity. Regional variability in proteasomal activity and microglial function, documented across multiple species and disease models [33, 67, 68], provides a plausible substrate for these differences. In both regions, inclusions eventually disappeared, a pattern most plausibly explained by active clearance mechanisms. The transient nature of αSyn inclusions observed in both the cortex and striatum strongly suggests the engagement of active clearance mechanisms. Microglia are known to internalize and degrade αSyn assemblies, and the region-specific microglial activation detected in our model provides a plausible cellular substrate for this process. Importantly, the temporal dissociation between microglial morphological activation and overt neurodegeneration argues against a purely neurotoxic role of microglia, instead supporting a context-dependent function that may encompass both protective clearance and inflammatory stress. Additional double-immunofluorescence analyses revealed that interactions between microglia and αSyn pathology differed substantially between brain regions. In the cortex and striatum, where αSyn pathology was predominantly organized into compact LB-like and LN-like inclusions, no evident colocalization between pSer129 αSyn signal and IBA1-positive microglia was detected. In contrast, partial colocalization was observed in the midbrain, where αSyn appeared primarily as diffuse and punctate pSer129-positive structures rather than classical inclusion-like aggregates. These observations may suggest that microglial engagement preferentially occurs with diffuse or non-classical αSyn assemblies rather than with mature compact inclusions. However, because the present analyses were based on static imaging, they do not allow definitive conclusions regarding active phagocytosis or intracellular degradation of αSyn species by microglia. Electrophysiological studies further demonstrate that oligomers depress excitatory transmission and impair long-term synaptic plasticity in striatal circuits, providing a mechanistic context for how transient cortical pathology could translate into persistent circuit stress [13, 53]. Moreover, the temporal shift from predominantly LN-like to LB-like inclusions observed in the cortex may reflect a sequence of maturation and detoxification rather than progressive aggregation. Early LN-like structures, enriched in soluble oligomeric αSyn and associated with synaptic dysfunction, appear to evolve into more compact LB-like inclusions as neurons attempt to sequester toxic species [15, 44]. This interpretation is supported by the concurrent decline in overall cortical inclusion burden, suggesting that local proteostatic and glial clearance mechanisms ultimately succeed in removing stabilized aggregates once their conversion is complete. In contrast, the predominance of LB-like inclusions in the striatum from early stages may reflect region-specific aggregation and inclusion maturation dynamics, shaped by local cellular and proteostatic contexts, rather than differential neuronal vulnerability or impaired handling of oligomeric species.

The choice of the striatum as the injection site is not only technically justified but also conceptually central for understanding the mechanisms driving α-synucleinopathies such as PD. As the primary integrative hub of nigrostriatal dopaminergic signaling, the striatum represents a critical convergence point where dopaminergic modulation from the substantia nigra meets dense glutamatergic input from cortical layer V pyramidal neurons (Fig. 24). This unique anatomical and functional position places the striatum at the core of both corticostriatal and nigrostriatal loops, making it an ideal site for the initiation and propagation of αSyn–driven pathology [32]. Consequently, local delivery of αSyn to the striatum directly targets a key regulatory node of dopaminergic neurotransmission, providing a powerful and pathophysiologically relevant framework for dissecting early pathogenic events in α-synucleinopathies. Synaptic dysfunction in this region, including alterations in dopamine release, impaired presynaptic plasticity, and changes in long-term potentiation and depression, has been suggested to precede dopaminergic cell loss in the SNpc and may drive early motor disturbances [32, 66]. Direct delivery of αSyn oligomers into the striatum therefore provides a physiologically relevant entry point, mimicking the natural prion-like spread of αSyn along neuronal projections rather than inducing acute injury by direct nigral injection. Within this framework, the delayed nigral alterations observed in our study can be understood as a downstream consequence of striatal and corticostriatal dysfunction, consistent with the view that neuronal vulnerability may proceed through circuit-level mechanisms rather than solely through local fibrillar deposition. Importantly, although overt LB-like pathology was not detected in the SNpc using standard brightfield immunohistochemistry, higher-resolution confocal analyses revealed diffuse and punctate pSer129 αSyn signal within the midbrain region. Together with the transient appearance of insoluble αSyn species in biochemical fractionation assays and the early oxidative stress response observed in the midbrain, these findings support the interpretation that biologically active non-classical αSyn assemblies may contribute to delayed nigral vulnerability even in the absence of mature fibrillar inclusions. Recent work on intranigral oligomer models has similarly reported delayed degeneration and neuroinflammation despite a modest inclusion load, supporting the notion that oligomer-induced toxicity spreads through interconnected networks rather than direct fibrillar accumulation [48, 53].

Astrocytic responses were evident in our model, particularly in the striatum, where we observed GFAP upregulation, accompanied by hypertrophy and increased process complexity. In the cortex, a later reduction in dendritic area was detected at 180 dpi, suggesting that astrocytic remodeling is dynamic and may not follow a uniform trajectory across brain regions. These features point to a reactive transformation of astrocytes and a remodeling of their local homeostatic functions. Experimental evidence indicates that astrocytes can transition into a pro-degenerative, A1-like reactive state under the influence of microglia-derived cytokines [41], and astrocytic reactivity has been implicated in dopaminergic neuron vulnerability in experimental models of PD [8, 80]. Consistent with this view, studies in seeding models demonstrated that neurodegeneration and neuroinflammatory responses can progress independently of overt αSyn inclusions [30]. Within this framework, the astrocytic alterations we detected may contribute to the delayed dopaminergic vulnerability, reinforcing the interpretation that glial activation and soluble assemblies, rather than stable fibrillar aggregates, are critical drivers of neuronal vulnerability. Mechanistically, several processes may be involved, including impaired astrocytic support for synaptic transmission, reduced metabolic and antioxidant buffering, and the direct release of pro-inflammatory mediators—all of which could exacerbate dopaminergic vulnerability in the SNpc. While these interpretations remain plausible, they require further validation in models directly targeting astrocyte function.

This transient pathology does not imply benign consequences. On the contrary, the delayed decrease in dopaminergic markers observed at later time points suggests that early αSyn burden may initiate processes that compromise neuronal homeostasis over time. Increasing evidence indicates that soluble or submicroscopic oligomeric species, rather than mature fibrillar aggregates, may represent important drivers of sustained cellular stress. Immune activation, oxidative imbalance, diffuse non-classical αSyn accumulation in the midbrain, and altered synaptic organization have all been implicated as early components of αSyn-associated pathology [5, 7, 25, 48].

Given the growing recognition that synaptic pathology represents one of the earliest and most consistent consequences of αSyn accumulation [10, 55]. We additionally assessed the levels of the synaptic proteins synaptophysin and PSD-95. Synaptophysin is a major synaptic vesicle membrane protein and one of the most extensively validated presynaptic markers of synaptic density and presynaptic integrity in both human and experimental studies [46, 59]. Reductions in synaptophysin have repeatedly been associated with synaptic loss, impaired neurotransmission, and disease progression in Parkinson’s disease, dementia with Lewy bodies, and other neurodegenerative disorders [29, 35, 59]. PSD-95, in turn, is a key postsynaptic scaffolding protein essential for the organization and stabilization of glutamatergic synapses and synaptic plasticity. Alterations in PSD-95 expression are strongly associated with disturbances in postsynaptic signaling and synaptic organization [14, 79].

Importantly, numerous studies in both human tissue and experimental models of synucleinopathy demonstrate that reductions in synaptophysin and PSD-95 closely track αSyn-associated synaptic pathology and frequently precede overt neuronal degeneration [12, 49, 60]. Experimental αSyn models, including PFF- and overexpression-based paradigms, consistently report decreases in synaptic proteins in regions affected by αSyn pathology, supporting the concept that synaptopathy is an early and central component of disease progression [11, 56, 78].

In the present study, we observed region-specific alterations in synaptophysin and PSD-95 levels, particularly in the midbrain. Although biochemical measurements of synaptic proteins do not directly quantify synapse number or function and may additionally reflect compensatory or regulatory processes, they are widely regarded as biologically meaningful indicators of altered synaptic state. Therefore, the observed changes in synaptophysin and PSD-95 likely reflect early disturbances in synaptic organization and protein homeostasis occurring prior to overt neurodegeneration. Multiple experimental studies demonstrate that αSyn oligomers preferentially disrupt presynaptic function and axonal integrity, impairing neurotransmitter release and synaptic plasticity at nigrostriatal and corticostriatal terminals before dopaminergic neuron loss occurs [11, 25, 47, 54].

At the same time, because the present analyses were performed at the tissue level, without cell-type-specific or functional assessment, the precise neuronal populations and synaptic pathways involved remain unclear. Accordingly, our findings should be interpreted conservatively as evidence of altered synaptic protein homeostasis rather than definitive proof of functional synaptic impairment.

The behavioral profile observed in our study is consistent with this interpretation. Despite the presence of molecular and biochemical alterations, classical motor parameters such as spontaneous locomotion, rotarod performance, and pole test latencies remained largely preserved throughout the experimental period. In contrast, more sensitive behavioral measures revealed selective impairments, including an early reduction in climbing activity, decreased rearing behavior at later stages, and a persistent deficit in grip strength beginning at 90 dpi.

This dissociation between preserved gross motor coordination and subtle impairments in exploratory and motor-associated behaviors is noteworthy and aligns with observations from transgenic and PFF-based models of α-synucleinopathy, in which early functional abnormalities frequently precede overt neurodegeneration [20, 45]. Such findings support the concept that soluble αSyn assemblies may perturb neuronal and synaptic homeostasis long before extensive neuronal loss becomes detectable [9, 19, 42].

In this context, the observed alterations in synaptophysin and PSD-95 levels may provide a molecular correlate of these early functional disturbances. At the same time, the interpretation of these findings requires caution. The present analyses do not establish which neuronal populations or synaptic pathways are specifically affected, nor do they directly demonstrate impaired neurotransmission or synaptic function. Therefore, the observed alterations are best interpreted as evidence of disturbed synaptic protein homeostasis and altered synaptic state rather than definitive proof of functional synaptopathy.

Similarly, reduced object exploration and altered anxiety-related behavior may indicate subtle disturbances in network function rather than severe cognitive impairment. The preservation of the discrimination index in the NOR task suggests that motivational or attentional components may be more strongly affected than recognition memory itself [22, 23]. Likewise, the absence of the normal age-related reduction in thigmotaxis observed in control animals may reflect altered regulation of emotional or exploratory behavior rather than generalized anxiety [22, 58, 69].

Taken together, these findings support a model in which αSyn oligomers induce early molecular and synaptic alterations associated with subtle behavioral abnormalities that emerge prior to overt dopaminergic degeneration. While the present data do not permit mechanistic conclusions regarding specific neurotransmitter systems or neural circuits, they are consistent with the growing view that early synaptic and network-level disturbances represent an important component of oligomer-driven pathology [5, 53].

The model employed here offers important advantages. Direct oligomer injection enables precise control over dose, conformational state, and injection site, thereby avoiding artifacts associated with viral overexpression and facilitating side-by-side comparisons with other assemblies. By using ultrapure, endotoxin-free mouse αSyn, nonspecific immune responses to foreign protein were minimized. At the same time, limitations must be acknowledged. Oligomers are structurally unstable and heterogeneous, which may affect reproducibility. Nevertheless, our preparation was carefully characterized before administration, ensuring a well-defined conformational state at the time of injection. Previous studies indicate that αSyn oligomers delivered in vivo retain sufficient biological activity during the early post-injection period to trigger pathological and functional effects [5, 25, 36, 51], supporting their use as tools to study early pathogenic events. What happens to these assemblies at later stages inside the brain remains uncertain, but this very property makes them valuable for probing transient and reversible aspects of α-synucleinopathy.

Looking forward, several directions emerge. Future work should aim to elucidate the mechanisms of dopaminergic cell death in greater detail, distinguishing between apoptotic and necrotic pathways, and clarifying how inflammation, excitotoxicity, and redox stress converge. Parallel in vitro approaches using iPSC-derived neurons and organotypic cultures will help dissect cell-autonomous vulnerability. In vivo, longitudinal imaging of redox dynamics, assays of neuromuscular junction integrity, and high-sensitivity behavioral monitoring could capture early and subtle dysfunctions that escape conventional testing. Manipulating microglial activation or mitochondrial resilience in conjunction with oligomer delivery would further clarify causality in clearance and vulnerability mechanisms.

For the field, this implies that therapeutic strategies should focus on early, reversible processes—such as soluble assemblies, mitochondrial stabilization, and immune modulation—rather than solely on inclusions. By shifting the emphasis toward dynamic species and systemic responses, it may become possible to intercept disease processes at a stage when they remain reversible and most amenable to treatment.
