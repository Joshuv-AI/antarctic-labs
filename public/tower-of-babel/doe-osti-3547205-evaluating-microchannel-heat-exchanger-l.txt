ABSTRACT
This report presents the work and results of a Solar Energy Technology Office (SETO) funded project
to evaluate the lifetime of advanced microchannel etched and diffusion bonded heat exchangers
for use in Generation 3 concentrated solar power (CSP) plants. Such plants transfer heat from a
high temperature solid media to supercritical carbon dioxide (sCO2) via a primary heat exchanger
in order to drive advanced Brayton cycles with turbine inlet temperatures greater than 700 °C,
enabling greater cycle efficiency than current state of the art power cycles.
The project included three main tasks. Task 1 was to develop numerical models of microchannel
heat exchangers in order to asses damage accumulation during expected operational conditions.
We found that a simplified modeling approach enabled the reduction of CFD mesh sizes by an
order of magnitude, allowing for much larger physical components to be analyzed with reasonable
computational resources. We also found that, under the operating conditions considered, fatigue
damage accumulation is insignificant compared to creep damage accumulation.
Task 2 was to diffusion bond and test both stainless steel and a high nickel alloy in order to test
as-bonded material properties of both monolithic test blocks and etched heat exchanger samples.
We found that for both dual certified stainless steel 316/316L and Inconel alloy 617, the material
behavior under high temperature creep, high temperature low-cycle fatigue, and fatigue + creep
loading produced material behaviors within the specified bands for wrought material behavior. We
also subjected small representative etched and bonded heat exchangers to temperature and pressures
well in excess of their nameplate rating. The accelerated failure time matched up reasonably well
with predicted values and demonstrated the very conservative parameters used in the current design
of these types of heat exchangers.
Task 3 was to design, build, and operate a small thermal fatigue test loop, capable of rapid and
repeatable thermal cycling of small prototype heat exchangers. Capable of circulating sCO2 at
0.1 kg/s and a maximum temperature and pressure of 550 °C and 17 MPa, the test loop was able
to complete several hundred thermal cycles, with ramp rates on the order of 50 °C/s with no
control faults or mechanical failures, demonstrating its reliability and capability for simulating
representative operating conditions.

1. INTRODUCTION AND PROJECT OBJECTIVES

Microchannel heat exchanger technology is being pursued for next generation CSP concepts for
primary power cycle heat addition and power cycle heat recuperation due to the high heat transfer
coefficients and pressure containment advantages of small sCO2 channels. The economics of future
CSP plants as dictated by the SETO 2020 or 2030 targets depend on a heat exchanger with a 30-year
lifetime (resisting creep, fatigue, corrosion, erosion) and operational characteristics such as fast
ramping and the ability to withstand thermal shock. However, the lifetime and operational limits of
microchannel heat exchangers operating at high-temperatures, particularly those constructed from
high-nickel alloys, are not well known. This uncertainty has resulted in heat exchanger vendors
not being able to accurately forecast heat exchanger lifetime as required by customers, specify
operational limits as required by process engineers to prevent premature heat exchanger failure, or
overdesign heat exchanger which leads to higher cost than necessary.
The project goal is to evaluate heat exchanger lifetime and operational limits for the manufacturing
and prototype design for next-generation CSP heat exchanger technology through a combination of
collecting experimental data and modeling studies. The project includes three main tasks:

 1. Heat exchanger modeling to evaluate damage accumulated during design point operation,
 off design operation, startup, and thermal shock scenarios for pilot and commercial scale
 concepts
 2. High-temperature mechanical characterization of test block samples capturing manufacturing
 and operational requirements for Gen3 heat exchangers
 3. Development of a thermal fatigue test apparatus to evaluate subscale prototypes that are
 representative of geometries being considered for Gen3 projects

By performing these three tasks, we will develop the knowledge required to accurately forecast
the lifetime of microchannel heat exchangers for the application of primary heat addition and
heat recuperation in an sCO2 power cycle in a CSP plant. The three distinct outcomes are
modeling tools and studies for predicting lifetime and guiding the designer, material mechanical
properties after experiencing the diffusion bonding process, and experimental data for testing
subscale representative heat exchanger prototypes. Sandia partnered with VPE Thermal to provide
heat exchanger designs and diffusion bonded test samples, and EPRI to perform high temperature
mechanical testing and post-test analysis of the bonded samples.

2. HEAT EXCHANGER MODELING

The first project task was to develop thermal/mechanical models of microchannel heat exchangers
that could ultimately be used to estimate the expected lifetime of such a heat exchanger under
anticipated Gen3 CSP operating conditions. VPE provided designs for particle to sCO2 primary
heat exchangers and sCO2 recuperators based on their efforts to design and fabricate such heat
exchangers for the Gen3 program.

2.1. Sub-scale Model Development

The initial phase of this task focused on developing thermo-mechanical models of small scale heat
exchangers similar in physcial size that VPE and SNL have previously tested [1]. The primary
purpose of this effort is to develop and compare a numerical model where the microchannels
are fully meshed, and a reduced order model that uses advective bars instead of full geometric
channels [2]. Using this method, the microchannels are eliminated from the geometry entirely, and
thermal coupling is provided by Nusselt number correlations. While analyzing fully featured heat
exchanger geometries at these scales would be taxing but not out of reach of a moderately powerful
desktop computer or small local cluster, modeling a commercial-size microchannel heat exchanger
(with plate dimensions on the order of 1 m and hundreds of channels per plate) is expected to
be untenable without dedicated high power computing resources - something a heat exchanger
designer/fabricator is unlikely to have access to. Using this method decreased the mesh element
count by two orders of magnitude; an example of this is given in figure 2-1.

 Figure 2-1. Detail of mesh for fully featured microchannels versus using advective bars.

Both a particle/sCO2 heat exchanger and sCO2 recuperator design were developed, featuring
representative but non-proprietary channel layouts in order to allow for publication of results.

from VPE, four stress classification lines were located on the sCO2 outlet header port as this
would be the area of highest metal temperatures and thus lowest allowable stresses. The stress

 • The material has been modeling using a simple elastic stress model. This could potentially
 give rise to unrealistically high stress levels in certain areas of the heat exchanger, such as the
 corner of the microchannels. As constant failures at these locations are not seen in practice, it
 is expected that some amount of plastic deformation or high creep rate and subsequent stress
 relaxation occurs at these locations, thus permitting longer lifetimes than what a peak stress
 might predict.
 • The analysis has so far been done with wrought material properties, without taking any
 impact of diffusion bonding into account. However, it will be shown elsewhere that this may
 be acceptable for the diffusion bonded materials investigated in this project.

Part of the project milestones for this task were to develop a 90% confidence interval on the predicted
lifetime. In order to help develop this, a parametric sensitivity study was performed by coupling

SNL’s parametric anslysis tool Dakota [12] to the SIERRA solvers. Seven independent input
variables were selected for this study, as shown in table 2-5. The scalers represent a multiple applied
to the nominal value of sCO2 heat transfer coefficient and the temperature dependent effective bed
thermal conductivity. The gap conductance represents the additional near-wall thermal resistance
due to extra voids near the wall, and is a critical value in predicting heat tranfer from a moving
packed bed of particles to a surface. It is highly variable based on particle characteristics and
bed design [13]; this parameter range was set based on experimental measurements from [14]. A
nominal value of 211 MPa was used for the elastic modulus of IN617, and the range represents
a 25% uncertainty bound. It remains a temperature dependent property with the same relative
reductions in value at elevated temperatures. A relatively wide range of ramp rates was sampled
while limiting the maximum value to a reasonable level. Nfailure represents the number of cycles to
failure, which was calculated from the maximum strain ranges and IN617 data from [10]. As fatigue
damage was still expected to be minimal, the scaler represents 2 orders of magnitude. Finally, a0
is a term in the time-to-rupture equation for IN617 [10], which is a curve fit of experimental data.
This parameter was varied using a normal distribution using the mean and 95% bounds provided.

2.6. Summary

The important results from the heat exchanger modeling task are as follows:

 • The use of advective bars in place of physical microchannels can reduce the element count of a
 mesh used for an FEA analyis by up to an order of magnitude without significantly impacting
 the thermal or stress results, although it may be prudent to not completely eliminate the
 microchannels in specific areas of interest due to the impact of removing corners and/or
 adding material have on specific simulated behaviors. This puts the detailed design of
 channel layouts for current commercial sized plates of a PCHE within the reach without
 the need for extremely large high performance computing resources. However, it is likely
 still beyond the capability of all but the highest end dedicated workstations unless there is
 not significant schedule pressure. Smaller heat exchangers (likely in the range of tens to
 low-hundreds of kW thermal duty) could be designed and analyzed on a relatively powerful
 desktop computer.
 • Following procedures based on nuclear sections of the ASME BPVC and [7], accumulated
 creep and fatigue damaged were calculated for a sub-scale PHX. The results indicated that
 fatigue damage is insignificant compared to creep damage, which is not surprising. However,
 there may be a concern of creep failure before 10000 hours of operation for the 20 kW PHX
 design used in this study.
 • A commercial-scale PHX plate was similarly analyzed using the planned G3P3 thermal cycle.
 Once again, fatigue damage was almost non-existent. A sensitivity study was performed on
 several independent input variable to assess their relative importance in varying the predicted
 lifetime of the PHX. It was found that the only significant contributors to predicted lifetime
 are the elastic modulus and the uncertainty in the creep-rupture time for IN617. While the
 former is typically supplied as part of a material test report and could be accounted for if the
 designer knew exactly what material would be used for heat exchanger fabrication, the latter
 would like need a large effort dedicated to performing more creep-rupture tests in an attempt
 to reduce the uncertainty in the data.
 • An automated workflow was developed to perform both lifetime prediction calculations and
 parametric variable sweeps, enabling future investigations to happen much more rapidly.

3. MANUFACTURED MATERIAL TESTING

The second project task was to fabricate diffusion bonded specimens and subject them to high
temperature mechanical testing in order to compare their performance against data for wrought
material. The project sought to test at least two materials: stainless steel 316 (due to its widespread
use and ample material characterization data) and a high nickel alloy, such as IN617, alloy 740H,
Haynes® alloy 230, or other material suitable for high temperature and pressure operation in
advanced sCO2 power cycles that are being studied for Gen3 power applications.

3.1. Stainless Steel fabrication and testing

3.1.1. Room Temperature Testing

In order to characterize DB SS316, VPE produced a bonded test block made from dual certified
SS316/SS316L of approximately 8 in x 8 in x 5−1/4 in after removing sufficient material to perform
VPE internal quality checks. These checks consisted of room temperature tensile tests of three
bonded samples cut from the block, a piece of as-received sheet material, and a piece of sheet
material that went through the same thermal process as the DB block in order to asses the impact
of thermal cycling on material properties. These properties were compared to the relevant ASME
material spec for stainless steel sheet (SA-240). The results of this testing are shown in table 3-1.
VPE initially took 3 samples located near the edge of the block in order to test the as-bonded
material properties. In addition to the three required tensile specimens per code, VPE performed
testing on sheet material that experienced the thermal profile and environment of the diffusion
bonding process and collected an additional sample from the center of the block for comparison to
the three edge samples. The testing of the two sheet samples was primarily to compare the strength
of the as-received material to material that experienced the temperature and environment of the
diffusion bonding process. The results show that the as-received material meets the mechanical
requirements for 316L and 316, but there is an observed decrease in the tensile and yield of the sheet

of the bonded samples. In order to identify if the reduced strength is a function of sample location

3.1.3. Featured sample testing

One of the goals of this project was to compare tensile or creep data for typical dog bone test
specimens with similar data from a test specimen that was representative of an in-service PCHE. In
particular, the material testing performed in the early parts of this project applied a uniform stress
normal to a uniform cross section (technically LCF testing uses a non-uniform applied stress by
definition, but its direction was still always normal to the cross section). In an in-service PCHE,
not only is the stress applied is a very non-uniform manner due to pressure and thermal conditions,
but the specific layout of the microchannels causes highly curved stress field lines which could
cause deformation or failure in unexpected locations. This disparity has been previously noted
[25]; however, while featured test specimens were stressed to failure under uniaxial loading, none
of the pressurized samples experienced failure. Thus, it is difficult to use those results to develop
lifetime prediction models based on the complex stresses that are expected in PCHEs in service.
Additionally, that particular study didn’t use an alloy of interest for this study, making their results
not directly transferable.
In order to facilitate this type of testing, VPE fabricated several small representative 316 DB
PCHE and delivered them to EPRI. The provided PCHEs are VPE catalog items and thus use
commercially available materials, designs, and fabrication methods in their construction, giving
a very “real-world” basis for their subsequent performance data. They had a nameplate rating of
10 MPa at 550 °C; among other factors, SS was chosen specifically because it would have a lower
nameplate rating and thus be more suitable for accelerated creep testing.
In order to further facilitate accelerated creep testing, one set of channels was left open to atmo-
sphere, while the other set of channels was hooked up to a compressed gas bottle. In normal

 39

operation, these heat exchangers would be expected to experience pressures that were much closer
in value; thus this testing represents a worst-case scenario to develop strains within the bulk of the
material that are beyond the anticipated design life of the unit in actual service.
Prior to full thermal testing, 2 samples underwent Ar/He leak testing at 13.79 MPa as a quality
control measure to ensure no samples were leaking prior to testing, and another sample was sent
for XCT scanning to inspect the internal channel integrity. Width and perimeter measurement were
taken at several locations in order to quantify any gross changes in exterior dimension.
The samples had 3 thermocouples placed along and in contact with the body to measure metal
surface temperature. The samples were then placed inside a tube with a slot to maintain orientation
of the sample, and the ends were secured with caps affixed via threaded rods (see figure 3-13).
The space between the sample and the tube was filled with high temperature insulation to ensure
the thermocouples were not in contact with the secondary containment. Thus, while the tube
supports the sample against gravity and somewhat restrains rotation, the sample is otherwise free
to displace and grow as thermal conditions dictate. The entire assembly is inserted into a high
temperature furnace and temperature and pressure are continuously monitored. When pressure is
unable to be maintained, the sample is examined for failure. In order to decrease time to failure,
test temperatures and pressures were chosen that were far beyond the nameplate rating of the heat
exchangers; the first heat exchanger failed at 185 hours at 700 °C and 42.5 MPa.

3.3. Summary

The important results from the materials testing task are as follows:
 • When diffusion bonded properly, SS 316/316L appears to have similar strength, creep, and
 LCF performance to wrought material. If accepted as an ASME code case or other industry
 design practice, this could eliminate the need to apply any sort of strength reduction factor
 when using DB 316 in the design and fabrication of pressure parts. EPRI has submitted this
 test data to ASME to support the development of a code case for BPVC section III diffusion
 bonded heat exchangers.
 • High temperature creep testing of representative heat exchangers was performed, creating
 more representative stress fields in the material than a typical tensile or creep test. The small
 heat exchangers may experience failure of the channel membrane in a single plate before
 experiencing inter-layer failure. The failures identified in this project do not cross diffusion
 boundary lines, but do occur in places where the separation between channels may be on the
 order of a single grain. It should be noted that all testing of the featured samples was far
 beyond the nameplate rating of the samples; this hints at the conservatism with which these
 heat exchangers are designed in order to meet ASME criteria.

 53

• Diffusion bonding of 740H remains a challenge. While DB 740H has been demonstrated to
  have reasonable performance compared to wrought material, the material tested in this study
  had virtually no creep strength or ductility, indicating that very minor differences in specific
  material heat or thermal process could produce markedly different results in DB material
  performance.
• Diffusion bonding of IN617 using a direct bond process has show the capability to produce
  material that behaves nearly equivalently to wrought material. The use of a Ni interlayer
  caused poor grain growth across the bond line and generally poor creep performance. Re-
  moving the Ni interlayer allowed grain diffusion across the boundary and produced material
  with strength, creep, and LCF properties on par with wrought material. Differences in ther-
  mal processing leading to different material and bond line characteristics, particularly grain
  size and growth and bond line chemistry, highlighted the impact these parameters have on
  ultimate diffusion bonded material performance.

4. HEAT EXCHANGER THERMAL FATIGUE TESTING

The third project task was to design, build, and operate a thermal fatigue test loop at the SNL
Brayton Lab capable of subjecting a test article, such as a PCHE style recuperator, to thousands
of thermal cycles at elevated temperatures and pressures. This would allow for accelerated testing
of heat exchanger prototypes, and add to the overall capability of SNL to perform heat exchanger
research and development.

4.1. Design and commissioning

The basic operating principle of the fatigue loop involves using a series of valves to either direct the
flow of sCO2 through an electric heater or to bypass it. By alternating the open/closed position of
the valves, the “hot” inlet of the test section would see either the maximum system temperature or
a much lower temperature. The speed of the thermal shock would be determined by the travel time
of the valves between full open and close. An initial system schematic is shown in figure 4-1.

 Figure 4-1. Initial fatigue loop system layout.

In order to keep material costs reasonable, the system was designed to operate at 17 MPa and
up to 550 °C at the test article inlet. This allowed the used of stainless steel tubing and fittings
for the majority of the sCO2 loop; only the test article hot inlet line required the use of IN625
material. The major components of the loop were the heater, heater controller, Grayloc® hubs,
clamps, and seal rings, a circulation pump, a coriolis flow mater, a water-cooled chiller, control
valves, instrumentation and control hardware, piping and support, and the test article itself. Some

4.2. Thermal cycling

After commissioning demonstrated that the system operated as expected, thermal cycling began in
earnest. As thermal cycling was achieved by operating valves to change the flow path to bypass
the sCO2 heater, ramp rates on the order of 100 °C/min were achieved; this is far beyond what
would be expected in an industrial setting outside of trips or other abnormal operating conditions.
Heat was ultimately rejected via evaporative cooling; however, the cooling system did not have an
automatic water level control system installed, and thus thermal cycling was limited to daytime
operation while personnel who could monitor the water level were present. The system will be
switched to a closed loop chilled water system in the future to enable around-the-clock operation.
To date, several hundred thermal cycles have been performed with no noticeable changes in heat
exchanger or loop performance. Additionally, the first test article passed a helium leak check which
indicated no inter-layer failures. There are no noticeable changes in external dimensions, indicating
there are no membrane or other internal failures. Typical cycling data is shown in figure 4-7.

variations in a series of images caused by things like sensor noise, lighting variations, camera
vibrations, air turbulence, and other random variations in a series of images [39]. To estimate
noise floor, a series of images are taken of a test field which is known to be at steady state and
is not undergoing any dynamic strain. Thus, any displacements and strains calculated by DIC are
technically errors and a statistical analysis of these images can provide a noise floor for helping
understand subsequent data - in essence, any calculated displacements or strains below the noise
floor are technically indistinguishable from noise and should be used with caution. For the low
magnification cameras looking at the top of the test article, the noise floor displacement was
on the order of 8 to 25 𝜇m during high temperature imaging (see table 4-2, while the measured
displacements were in the range of several hundred 𝜇m; thus the measured displacements are easily
distinguishable from noise (this was also true of the high magnification set-up).

4.4. Summary

The important results from the fatigue testing are as follows:

 • SNL has designed, constructed, and operated a thermal fatigue loop capable of autonomous
 operation and extremely rapid thermal cycling. While designed for moderate temperature
 sCO2, it can operate with any fluid that is below the MAWP of the loop and is compatible with
 its materials of construction. SNL has already received interest from external commercial
 entities seeking to utilize this loop to test their components.
 • VPE fabricated three test articles for accelerated fatigue testing. Preliminary analysis, shown
 in chapter 2, has indicated that fatigue failure is much less of a concern than creep damage.
 Nevertheless, a test article has not experienced a fatigue failure at the time of this writing.
 • A small DIC set-up was deployed and captured images of a fatigue test article. The system
 was able to optically measure displacements on the outside surface of the test article that
 were above the noise floor of the system, and that were well correlated with the thermal state
 of the test article. Strains were also calculated, but these calculations generally had a much
 higher noise floor than displacements and would thus need more scrutiny to use.
 • The effort demonstrated the usefulness and unobtrusive nature of a DIC set-up, and SNL
 will likely look to utilize DIC in targeted areas of G3P3 and other future project where
 understanding strains are important.

5. CONCLUSIONS

The principle outcome of this project was to develop the knowledge required to accurately forecast
the lifetime of microchannel heat exchangers for the application of primary heat addition and
heat recuperation in an sCO2 power cycle in a CSP plant. This was to be accomplished with a
combination of numerical modeling, manufactured material testing, and accelerated lifetime testing
under representative conditions. The primary purpose of the second and third efforts was to supply
validation data for the numerical modeling effort. However, the course of the project revealed two
important results:

 • When properly done, diffusion bonded SS316/316L and IN617 have high temperature tensile,
 creep, LCF, and creep/fatigue properties that are within the experimental data for wrought
 material of the same grade. Thus, there is no need to apply any kind of strength reduction
 factor due to account for diffusion bonded material being weaker than wrought material.
 EPRI has submitted data for a ASME BPVC section III code case for the use of diffusion
 bonded SS316 heat exchangers in nuclear applications; this may be applicable to section VIII
 pressure vessels as well.
 • At the conditions considered likely for CSP primary heat exchangers and recuperators, a fail-
 ure due to fatigue is much less likely that a failure due to creep rupture due to the combination
 of high temperatures, high pressures, and the limited ramp rates so far investigated.

Ultimately, the project provided much better insight into the properties of diffusion bonded SS316
and IN617, and of the modeling and lifetime predictions of diffusion bonded heat exchangers and
recuperators.
While the modeling task was unable to be 100% completed, Sandia developed an automated
workflow to make lifetime predictions for printed circuit heat exchangers. The workflow starts
with a template FEA mesh, applies relevant boundary conditions, and post-processes data using
a procedure based on ASME BPVC secrion III and a similar procedure developed by Argonne
National Laboratory. While the workflow allows for a fairly wide parametric sweep of variables in
order to gauge their impact on predicted lifetime, the variables that have the had the largest impact
so far are the Young’s modulus of a given material, and the scatter in the experimental data used to
develop the LMP for IN617. The authors recommend the following steps for future work:
 • Complete a parametric sweep for the recuperator to provide lifetime estimates. While
 this study focused on SS316 recuperators that would operate at lower temperatures than
 IN617 recuperators or primary heat exchangers, SS316 also has lower allowable stress at
 all temperatures compared to IN617 and other high-temperature alloys. Additionally, a
 recuperator with sCO2 on both sides could potentially see much faster thermal transients due
 to the higher heat transfer rates of sCO2 compared to particles.

 73

 • Develop a model of the featured test article from task 2 for validation. This would require the
 use of non-linear elastic models to capture creep and plastic deformation, but as the physical
 test articles are relatively small the ability to model the entire sample is feasible. Special
 considerations may need to be taken if the element size is on the order of the average grain
 size.

 • This project demonstrated the use of advective bars in the place of physical microchannels
 to model the thermo-mechanical performance of printed circuit heat exchangers with a much
 smaller mesh. However, implementing the advective bars from a PCHE model or etching
 drawing is a relatively tedious manual process. While out of scope for this project, it may
 be possible to apply machine learning to “translate” from one kind of model or drawing
 into an FEA mesh complete with interior advective bar boundary conditions, thus saving a
 non-trivial amount of manual labor during problem set-up. It may also be possible to use
 machine learning to design a channel layout optimized for some variable of interest, but that
 is even further beyond the scope of this project.

The material testing task was an overall success. It demonstrated that the strength of diffusion
bonds can approach - if not equal - the strength of wrought material, greatly simplifying the process
of PCHE design. This was further demonstrated via high temperature pressure testing of small
commercially available diffusion bonded PCHE test articles; even under stresses far beyond their
nameplate ratings, the initial failures in the test articles have not been on a DB line. The authors
recommend the following steps for future work:

 • Additional development, fabrication, and testing of high temperature alloy diffusion bonds.
 This project generated enough data for DB SS316 to confidently suggest that properly bonded
 material has largely the same properties as wrought material. However, there is not enough
 data on high temperature alloys to reach the same conclusion. The results to-date for IN617
 appear promising enough to warrant further study to develop statistically significant data and
 provide confidence in this particular direct-bonded approach.

 • Continue testing and characterization of the featured test articles. This project exhausted
 funding before testing of the featured test articles was completed, but even the limited testing
 so far has produced some interesting findings. Perhaps most noteworthy is that none of the
 failed test articles to-date have experienced their initial failure along the bond line, but rather
 along the intra-channel membrane for a given fluid passage. While this failure would require
 the unit to be replaced, it did not constitute a channel-to-channel which could be a serious
 failure mode in an operating plant. These failures were first noticed by measuring changes
 in external dimensions of the test article.

The design, construction, and operation of a fatigue test loop was also successful. As of this
writing, a fatigue test article has undergone approximately 500 high-speed thermal cycles with no
indications of internal or external failure, which is consistent with the findings of the modeling
task about the relative importance of creep versus fatigue damage accumulation. Additionally, it
allowed Sandia to demonstrate the feasibility of a DIC system to optically monitor displacements
and strains in locations where a typical strain gauge may not be feasible. The authors recommend
the following steps for future work:

 74

• Additional funding to continue testing the current test article until failure. Operation of the
  loop is highly automated so this would not be a considerable investment. Even though failures
  due to fatigue are not anticipated to be frequent in high temperature sCO2 heat exchangers in
  CSP plants, analyzing the failure that do occur could provide inside into design or fabrication
  changes that could reduce these failure occurrences even further.
• Recuperators in an sCO2 power cycle will likely operate with a high-pressure and low-
  pressure side. Converting the current fatigue test loop to a similar two-pressure design could
  provide more realistic test conditions compared to the current single-pressure system, and
  also make internal failures easier to detect.
• Design a similar loop to operate with air, helium, or other gas compatible with advanced
  very high temperature power cycles. Such systems are typically operated at much lower
  pressures that sCO2 allowing for thinner walls and/or less exotic materials. However, this
  loss of wall thickness on pressure boundaries could increase the importance of fatigue damage
  accumulation relative to creep.
