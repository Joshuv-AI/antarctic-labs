 ABSTRACT
 A one-dimensional (1D) finite-element model of fission product transport in
the Advanced Gas Reactor (AGR)-3/4 experiment has been developed using the
Multiphysics Object Oriented Simulation Environment (MOOSE) framework and
implemented in the fuel performance code BISON. The model resolves capsule-
specific geometries, materials, and temperature histories to simulate radial
migration of fission products from the fuel compact through the inner ring, outer
ring, and into the sink ring. Model parameters governing diffusion and sorption
were estimated for cesium (Cs) by simultaneously fitting modeled isotopic
concentration profiles and total ring inventories to a post-irradiation experimental
measurement. These data include gamma scanning, radial deconsolidation leach-
burn-leach analysis, tomographic reconstructions, and destructive physical
sampling. A mortar-based interfacial sorption framework was implemented to
enforce physically consistent mass transfer and flux conservation across gas gaps.
Two classes of parameter sets were derived: a least-squares best-fit, and a safety-
oriented conservative-fit that applies strong penalties for underprediction of sink
inventories. Across all twelve capsules, the model successfully reproduces the
dominant radial transport trends for Cs, with decreasing concentrations from the
compact outward through successive rings. The results demonstrate that sink ring
weighted calibration provides conservative, safety relevant bounds on low
temperature fission product transport for cesium. Model insufficiency for other
isotopes highlights the need for additional physics, including fast-slow diffusion
model and incorporation of trapping mechanisms in the transport behavior.
Overall, this work establishes a robust, capsule-specific modeling framework for
AGR-3/4 fission product transport and provides a defensible basis for parameter
selection in source-term and fuel performance analyses for high temperature gas-
cooled reactors.

 1. OVERVIEW
 The report presents the latest developments in the modeling of fission product transport in the AGR-3/4
irradiation experiment, with a focus on one-dimensional (1D) finite-element simulations implemented in
BISON (Williamson et al. 2025) within the Multiphysics Object-Oriented Simulation Environment
(MOOSE) framework (Harbour et al. 2025). The current effort aims to optimize isotope-specific diffusivity
parameters by comparing model predictions against data collected from post irradiation examination (PIE).
Emphasis is placed on capturing the radial migration of key fission product – cesium (Cs) – from their
origin in the cylindrical fuel compact, to the surrounding carbonaceous rings. These include the inner ring
(IR), outer ring (OR), and the outermost ring, the sink ring (SR). The model used in this work builds upon
prior efforts (Riet 2022; Riet and Stempien 2022; Riet 2023) and has been revised and extended to
incorporate refined transport behavior, and detailed capsule-specific temperature histories. The present
analysis introduces new safety-driven constraints that ensure the fission product inventory in the outermost
portion of the experiment (i.e., the SR) is not underpredicted. Revised transport models are evaluated
against empirical results obtained through gamma scanning, leach-burn-leach (LBL) testing, tomographic
reconstructions, and destructive physical sampling. These simulations contribute to a broader programmatic
effort to validate and improve predictive models for TRISO fuel behavior under irradiation and accident
conditions.
 1.1 Overall Program Purpose
 The Advanced Gas Reactor (AGR) Fuel Development and Qualification program was established to
support the deployment of high-temperature-gas-cooled reactors (HTGRs) by performing research and
development on tristructural isotropic (TRISO)–coated particle fuel. Administered as part of the U.S.
Department of Energy’s (DOE) Advanced Fuel Campaign (AFC) program (previously under the auspices
of Advanced Reactor Technologies (ART) Program), the objective aims to provide a comprehensive
technical basis for licensing and commercializing HTGRs. To achieve this mission, the AGR program
encompasses a wide range of activities, including TRISO fuel fabrication, irradiation testing, PIE, accident
condition heating tests, fuel performance modeling, and fission product transport analysis. These are
designed to assess fuel integrity under operational and accident scenarios, improve fabrication methods,
and generate empirical datasets to support the validation of high-fidelity simulations tools.

 1.2 Purpose of AGR-3/4
 The AGR-1, AGR-2, and AGR-5/6/7 experiments primarily focused on evaluating the performance of
high quality TRISO-coated particle fuel under irradiation. PIE for those experiments emphasized
quantifying the probability and mechanisms of silicon carbide (SiC) and TRISO coating failures for fuel
irradiated under a range of conditions (e.g., temperature and burnup). In contrast, the AGR-3/4 experiment
was designed as a fission product transport experiment, with the primary objective of observing the
migration behavior of fission products through the fuel, the graphitic matrix material, and nuclear-grade
graphite components (Collin et al. 2018). The test configuration consisted of cylindrical fuel compacts
containing TRISO-coated driver particles – similar to AGR-1 baseline fuel (Collin 2015b; Hunn and
Lowden 2007; Hunn et al. 2014) – along with designed to fail (DTF) particles, which were engineered to
intentionally release fission products during irradiation. This allowed for investigation of radionuclide
migration radially through concentric cylindrical rings composed of matrix graphite and nuclear-grade
graphite. Following irradiation, PIE measurements were performed to quantify the total fission product
inventories and their spatial distributions (both axial and radial) within the AGR-3/4 carbonaceous rings
and fuel compacts (Stempien et al. 2018; Harp, Stempien, and Demkowicz 2020; Stempien 2021; Riet
2022; Riet and Stempien 2022; Riet 2023). These data are intended to support the development and
refinement of fission product transport models, as well as to inform source-term analyses for HTGR
designs.
 1.3 AGR-3/4 Fuel Description
 A defining feature of AGR-3/4 fuel – distinguishing it from AGR-1 and AGR-2 —was the
incorporation of exactly 20 DTF particles in each compact, in addition to the approximately 1,898 TRISO-
coated “driver” fuel particles. DTF fuel kernels were coated only with a thin (20-μm-thick) pyrocarbon
layer that was intentionally fabricated with high anisotropy, so it would be likely to fail during the
irradiation (Collin 2015a; Hunn and Miller 2009; Kercher et al. 2011), resulting in 20 exposed fuel kernels
per compact. As shown on the right in Figure 1, the DTF particles (highlighted in red) were aligned roughly
along the compact’s radial centerline. DTF particles provided a known source of fission products to migrate
radially outward in the compacts and into the surrounding concentric rings of graphite and matrix material.
It was expected that DTF particles would behave like TRISO particles with failed TRISO coatings (e.g.,
releasing both Cs and fission gases).
 The white particles in Figure 1 are the driver particles. AGR-3/4 driver particle fuel kernels were fully
TRISO-coated with a buffer layer, inner pyrolytic carbon (IPyC) layer, SiC layer, and outer pyrolytic carbon
(OPyC) layer with characteristics similar to the “baseline” variant from the AGR-1 experiment (Collin
2015b; Hunn and Lowden 2007). Both the AGR-3/4 driver and DTF fuel particles contain uranium
oxycarbide (UCO) fuel kernels (approximately 350 µm in diameter) manufactured at BWX Technologies
(BWXT) Nuclear Operations Group (Lynchburg, VA). The U-235 enrichment was 19.7 wt%. The DTF
pyrocarbon coating and the driver-fuel TRISO coatings were applied to the kernels at Oak Ridge National
Laboratory (ORNL). Driver particle and DTF particle properties are summarized in (Collin 2015a).
Complete kernel and particle characterization and fabrication data are compiled in several reports and
papers (Hunn and Kercher 2006; Hunn and Lowden 2007; Hunn and Miller 2009; Kercher et al. 2011).
Driver-fuel particles and DTF particles had average dimensions, as summarized in Table 1.
 AGR-3/4 driver and DTF particles were overcoated with a precursor to graphitic matrix material and
formed into cylindrical fuel compacts at ORNL. The compact graphitic matrix material is composed of
graphite and a carbonized phenolic resin. Compacts were nominally 12.3 mm in diameter and 12.5 mm
long (in contrast to the AGR-1 and AGR-2 compacts, which were approximately 12.3 mm in diameter and
25 mm long). A summary of AGR-3/4 fuel compact properties is provided in the AGR-3/4 Final As-Run
Report (Collin 2015b). Detailed characterization data of the as-fabricated compacts were compiled in an
ORNL report (Hunn, Trammell, and Montgomery 2011).

  1.4 AGR-3/4 Test Train, Irradiation, Graphite, and Graphitic Rings
 The AGR-3/4 irradiation test train consisted of 12 capsules, numbered 1 through 12 from the bottom to
the top of the ATR core (Collin et al. 2018). Each capsule contained four fuel compacts. Figure 2 shows an
axial cutaway diagram and corresponding radial schematic of an AGR-3/4 irradiation capsule. Within each
capsule, four fuel compacts were vertically stacked at the center of concentric rings composed of graphitic
matrix material and/or structural graphites IG-110 and PCEA. Toyo Tanso IG-110 is a fine grained,
isostatically molded graphite, while GrafTech PCEA is an extruded graphite. The matrix rings were
produced at ORNL using a recipe similar to the historical German A3-27 formulation (Hunn, Trammell,
and Montgomery 2011). These matrix rings were fabricated from a blend of natural and synthetic graphite
flakes, mixed with Hexion SD-1708 novolac resin. The graphite-resin blend was supplied to ORNL by
BWXT.

 2. METHODS
 2.1 Inventory Analysis
 To determine the fission product mass balance, each of the 12 irradiation capsules was disassembled,
and their component parts were analyzed via gamma spectrometry, mass spectrometry, and gas proportional
counting (Stempien 2018). The most commonly detected radionuclides were gamma- emitting Ag-110m,
Cs-134, Cs-137, Eu-154, Eu-155, and beta-emitting Sr-90. In standard capsules (Capsules 1, 3–5, 7–-8, 10,
and 12), IR, OR, SR, spacers, foils, felts, and through tubes were all analyzed. Small non-metallic hardware
(e.g., spacers, foils, and felts) and the large graphite SRs were gamma counted using high-purity germanium
detectors before leaching (of ceramic spacers) or burn-leaching (of carbonaceous samples like felts and sink
rings).
 The precision gamma scanner (PGS) was used to nondestructively measure the inventory of gamma-
emitting fission products in the compacts and IRs and ORs (Harp, Stempien, and Demkowicz 2020). Select
rings were also subjected to tomographic gamma scans on the PGS, and subsequent tomographic
reconstructions of those scans was used to produce 1D radial and two-dimensional (2D) representations of
the fission product spatial distributions in the rings (Riet 2022). In addition to non-destructive gamma
spectrometry of entire rings, portions of the IRs and ORs from Capsules 3, 4, 5, 7, 8, 10 and 12 were
destructively analyzed for gamma-emitting nuclides (Stempien 2021). FB Capsules 2, 6, 9, and 11 were
retained intact for future heating tests. While the compacts, smaller non-metallic items (e.g., spacers), and
IR and ORs from these capsules were not measured, the sink rings were measured for these and all other
AGR-3/4 capsules.
 Summing the fission product inventory measured on each capsule component made it clear that the
presence of 80 DTF particles in each capsule resulted in noticeably higher releases of cesium (Cs-134 and
Cs-137) from AGR-3/4 fuel when compared to AGR-1 and AGR-2 fuel, which did not have DTF particles.
Greater than 30 particles’ worth of cesium (of 80 in the four compacts associated with each capsule) was
measured outside of the fuel compacts in Capsules 3-5, 7, 8, and 10. In Capsule 11 (an intact FB for which

 5

the IRs and ORs were not measured), 26 particle equivalents of cesium were measured outside of the OR
(Riet 2023). Based on experience from AGR-1, it is very unlikely that any driver particles failed during the
irradiation (Demkowicz et al. 2015); therefore, this cesium is assumed to be overwhelmingly dominated by
release from DTF particles (Stempien 2018).
 2.2 Physical Sampling
 Physical sampling of the rings involved milling material from around the circumference of the rings,
collecting that material, and analyzing it for gamma-emitting fission products. The fines from milling each
step were collected in individual vials, and those were sent to Pacific Northwest National Laboratory
(PNNL) where they were gamma counted for key isotopes such as Ag-110m, Cs-134, Cs-137, Ce-144, Eu-
154, and Eu-155. After gamma counting the solid samples (i.e., the fines from milling), the samples were
transferred to fused silica beakers and oxidized in a muffle furnace at 750°C for 24 hours. The ash and
residue remaining in the beaker was dissolved with HNO3. In instances where solids did not dissolve using
only HNO3, small amounts of concentrated HF in HNO3 were used. PNNL reported that solutions with
residual solids were centrifuged, the supernate was decanted, and HF and HNO3were added. This process
was repeated until all solids were dissolved. All the solutions for a given sample were combined. Knowing
the thickness and location of material removed from the ring in each milling step and the fission product
inventory in the material collected from each milling step, the radial fission product concentration profiles
in the rings were constructed. Further details can be found in INL/EXT-21-62863 (Stempien 2021).
 2.3 Fuel Compact Radial Deconsolidation
 Selected fuel compacts have been subjected to electrolytic radial deconsolidation-leach-burn-leach (R-
DLBL). Several reports have been published on the radial deconsolidation of as-irradiated particles
(Stempien 2017, Hunn et al. 2020, Helmreich et al. 2021; Helmreich et al. 2022; Stempien and Cai 2024).
Radial deconsolidation sequentially removes radial segments of compacts from the outside in an inward
direction so that each segment can be analyzed separately for fission products and a fission product
concentration profile can be constructed without breaching the silicon carbide layer of driver particles. In
regions of the compact away from the DTF particles, this method gives the fission product inventory in the
fuel compact matrix and the OPyC of the driver- fuel particles. In the central region of the compact, where
the DTF particles reside, this method gives the fission product inventory retained in the DTF particles, plus
that in the fuel compact matrix, and the OPyC of driver-fuel particles surrounding the DTF particles.
 2.4 Finite-Element Method Model
 Starting with the work described in (Riet 2023), transport of fission products through the rings in each
capsule was initially modeled using the BISON code (Williamson et al. 2025) which is built on the
Multiphysics Object-Oriented Simulation Environment (MOOSE) finite-element framework (Harbour et
al. 2025). Capsule dimensions were obtained from post-irradiation metrology (Stempien et al. 2016) where
available. For most fuel-body capsules post-irradiation metrology was not carried out, and design
dimensions were used instead. The finite-element model simplifies the design of the experiment to a 1D
radially symmetric transport problem, as shown in Figure 3, with the center of the capsule on the left and
the outer surface of the graphite sink ring on the right.

the two surfaces should be equal. 𝑘 0 means the constraint is perfectly satisfied (𝑃 𝑃 ). The 𝜖𝜆 term
is a small numerical shift added to improve convergence and avoid singular Jacobians during numerical
iterations. The constraints are implemented through MassSorptionConstraint and
MassFluxConstraint classes in BISON, coupled with SorptionPartialPressure materials
for isotherm evaluation. Each interface uses one sorption constraint and two flux constraints (radial and
axial), ensuring strong enforcement of both conditions. This approach eliminates a mass imbalance

 8

observed in the earlier gas-gap model when time-varying temperature was used directly from the as-run
thermal report values (Hawkes 2016) and provides accurate (best-fit), and conservative (conservative-fit)
predictions of fission product migration under irradiation conditions. It also aligns with AGR-3/4 PIE data
and supports calibration of diffusivities and sorptivities for IG-110 and PCEA. The improved mass
conservation and implicit gas-transport model allow more robust simulation and allows for further model
improvements which may provide a positive impact on the physical fidelity of the transport model.
 The two distinct types of fit are here defined as “best” and “conservative.” The “best” fit is a
least-squares fit of the modeled elemental concentration profiles at the end of irradiation to experimentally
observed total ring inventories and concentration profiles using experimental data for each capsule
concurrently. A per-ring (including compacts as one ring group) weighting scheme was applied such that
for a typical initial guess value, the compacts, IRs, and ORs had a total least-squares residual on the order
of one, with SR total residual on the order of 100. These weights were chosen because it was expected that
underestimating transport at low temperatures would be especially hazardous, as it would imply inadequate
safety margins. The “conservative” fit was modeled starting with the optimal value of the “best” fit, and an
additional multiplied 106 penalty factor for any model residual where the model underpredicted transport
to the sink ring. The conservative fit used the same weights as the best fit, but the penalty factor strongly
affected the residuals. Simulations for each capsule were generated dynamically using the specific
geometry, temperature profile and material configuration, with the simplifying assumption that matrix
media and graphite have the same diffusion properties.
 Additional implementation details, including the full model input structure, parameter optimization
workflow, and supporting scripts, are provided in Appendix A, Optimization Code.

 3.1 Results Overview
 The two main gamma-emitting isotopes of cesium, Cs-134 and Cs-137, generally yield high-quality
measurements after a decay time of a few years. The Cs-134 isotope has a half-life of 2 years and is less
likely to be confounded with hot-cell contamination than Cs-137, which has a half-life of 30 years.
Measured concentrations of cesium generally decrease radially within the compact and IRs, though not
without exceptions. Very few cesium activity measurements fell below the minimum detectable activity
(MDA). There is some indication that literature parameters overestimate activation energy for cesium
transport compared to what is observed in this data set (Riet and Stempien 2022). We predict faster diffusion
at low temperatures using both the best and conservative fit than predicted with the International Atomic
Energy Agency (IAEA) Federal Republic of Germany (FRG) model below 1330℃, and faster diffusion for
the conservative fit than the IAEA FRG model below 1780℃, with predictions of slower diffusive transport
than the IAEA FRG model above 1780℃ (IAEA 1997).

the results of a simultaneous fit to all the recorded measurements across all of the capsules with a large

 4. DISCUSSION, FUTURE WORK AND CONCLUSIONS
 The 1D diffusion/sorption framework implemented in BISON/MOOSE often captures overall radial
trends observed in PIE measurements – i.e., decreasing concentration from compact to IR, OR, and SR –
for Cs across diverse capsule configurations. The predicted variation in total radionuclide inventory from
fuel compact to IR to OR to SR typically compares better to the measurements than the detailed radial
concentration profiles. The predicted fission product concentration profiles at ring surfaces or in the bulk
of the ring may vary notably from the measurements. The heterogeneous temperature histories, material
differences (matrix vs. IG-110 vs. PCEA), geometric variations (FB vs. standard), and measurement
modalities (R-DLBL, tomographic gamma scans, MDA-derived bounds, total ring inventories) complicated
matters. The absolute inventory agreement is mixed and isotope dependent. The prediction patterns reflect
a calibration trade‑off: the safety-driven weighting scheme (large penalties for SR underprediction)
systematically biases parameters toward higher apparent transport at low temperatures typical of SRs, often
at the cost of compact and intermediate ring accuracy. While this approach is aligned with conservative
safety margins, it reveals where additional physics and better constraints are needed to achieve balanced
performance.
 Building on the mortar-based MassSorptionConstraint and MassFluxConstraint already
implemented, stronger coupling strategies could enable explicit mass balance and partial pressure continuity
with rate-dependent interfacial kinetics (adsorption/desorption) and trap filling/emptying dynamics (e.g.,
reversible binding with temperature-dependent site energy distributions) on both gap-facing surfaces.
Moreover, it allows heterogeneous surface activity (e.g., different sorptivity for IG‑110 vs. PCEA vs.
matrix) and anisotropy in near-surface diffusion. These enhancements might reduce the sensitivity and
instability observed in optimizations, improve physical fidelity at interfaces, and reconcile SR/OR/IR
discrepancies.
 The texture and microstructure vary from graphitic matrix material to nuclear graphite. The diffusivity
and sorptivity may also vary among these carbonaceous materials. However, adding the additional four
parameters necessary to properly model the system with separate graphite and matrix material properties
increased the parameter uncertainty without noticeably improving the final agreement with experiments.
Attempts to model different grades of graphite separately encountered similar issues, though this is an area
where further investigation may be warranted.

 22

 The use of high SR weightings and penalties for SR underprediction is justified by source-term safety
considerations: it is more hazardous to underpredict low-temperature retention/release pathways than to
overpredict compact retention. Nonetheless, this calibration bias must be explicitly acknowledged in
uncertainty communication.
 Ongoing and future efforts will focus on developing advanced models for strontium, silver and
europium transport predictions including trapping and limited concentration fast-transport models.
Additionally, these in-pile release models could be paired with Fuel Accident Condition Simulator (FACS)
furnace test results to better bound total release and further constrain the total inventory remaining in
compacts at the beginning of the heating tests. This work advances a capsule-specific modeling framework
for fission product transport in AGR‑3/4 using a 1D diffusion–sorption approach implemented in BISON
using components from the MOOSE multiphysics environment. The model reproduced some of the overall
radial migration trends of cesium across diverse capsule configurations and graphite grades. Through SR-
weighted calibration, the framework provides conservative bounds that guard against underprediction in
sink rings, aligning with safety–driven priorities for source-term evaluations.
 Despite these achievements, systematic discrepancies remain. Compact inventories are frequently
underpredicted, while SR and intermediate ring inventories are often overestimated, highlighting the
limitations of a purely diffusion-driven model. These mismatches point to missing physics at critical
interfaces, including sorption kinetics, multi-site trapping, and precursor gas-phase transport. For silver,
preliminary evidence suggests that temperature-dependent trapping mechanisms must be incorporated; for
strontium, coupling to transient Kr-90 gas-phase migration is a possible refinement.
 Future improvements will expand physics beyond simple diffusion and potentially incorporate models
of safety and heating tests of compacts. Future work should also integrate uncertainty quantification,
sensitivity analysis, and multi-objective calibration strategies to balance SR safety margins with accuracy
in compacts and intermediate rings. These enhancements will enable robust, defensible parameter sets
suitable for modeling, supporting HTGR source-term analyses.
