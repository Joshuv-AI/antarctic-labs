Neutrophil extracellular traps in colorectal cancer: context-dependent roles from inflammatory carcinogenesis to liver metastasis and therapeutic targeting
Authors: Duogang Xu, Ruotian Wang, Jing Tan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Colorectal cancer (CRC) develops and metastasizes within a microenvironment shaped by chronic inflammation, microbial exposure, stromal remodeling, and treatment pressure. Among the innate immune processes implicated in this setting, neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs) have attracted increasing attention. Initially described as antimicrobial structures, NETs have been implicated in several aspects of CRC biology, including inflammatory carcinogenesis, epithelial plasticity at the primary tumor site, gut-liver crosstalk, metastatic niche formation, and immune regulation. Experimental and translational studies suggest that they may influence tumor cell migration, extracellular matrix remodeling, hepatic colonization, and T-cell dysfunction, while also interacting with microbial and stromal signals in the liver. The available literature, however, does not support a uniformly deleterious role. Their biologic effect appears to vary with disease phase, anatomical site, neutrophil state, microbial context, and treatment exposure. In some settings, therapy-induced or early trap-associated responses may accompany tumor control rather than progression. In this review, we summarize current evidence on NETs in sporadic CRC, colitis-associated cancer (CAC), and colorectal cancer liver metastasis (CRLM), with particular attention to the gut-liver axis, immune microenvironment, biomarker development, and therapeutic intervention. We also discuss the major limitations of the field, including inconsistent detection strategies, overreliance on static or reductionist models, and limited spatially resolved human data. Overall, NETs may be better viewed as context-dependent effectors generated by distinct neutrophil states across different phases of disease.

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) remains a major contributor to the global cancer burden and is still one of the leading causes of cancer-related death worldwide [1, 2]. Although screening, surgery, perioperative care, and systemic therapy have improved outcomes for selected patients, metastatic dissemination continues to account for most CRC-related mortality [2, 3]. The liver is the predominant site of distant spread, and the biology of colorectal cancer liver metastasis (CRLM) therefore remains central to both translational research and clinical management [2, 3]. In parallel, there is longstanding evidence that inflammation shapes colorectal tumorigenesis. This relationship is most clearly established in inflammatory bowel disease (IBD)-associated CRC, but inflammatory signaling networks also influence sporadic CRC across disease initiation, local progression, and metastatic outgrowth [4, 5].

Among innate immune cell populations, neutrophils occupy a somewhat paradoxical position in CRC biology. They are abundant at sites of mucosal injury and infection, yet in tumors their functions extend beyond canonical antimicrobial activity and may vary with local cytokine signaling, microbial exposure, stromal interactions, and treatment pressure [5]. Interest in neutrophil biology was further renewed after the description of neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs), extracellular structures composed of decondensed chromatin and granule-associated proteins that were originally identified as a mechanism of host defense against microbes [6, 7]. Subsequent work linked NETs to sterile inflammation, thrombosis, and cancer progression, raising the possibility that neutrophils influence malignancy not only through direct cell-cell interactions or soluble mediators, but also through the deposition of extracellular scaffolds with signaling, proteolytic, and matrix-remodeling properties [8, 9]. In CRC, the literature has gradually moved from descriptive studies of NET accumulation in tumors and circulation toward a more mechanistic view of how NETs may participate in progression, metastasis, prognosis, and treatment response [10–12].

Across recent CRC studies, several observations recur. In experimental CRLM models, an interleukin-8 (IL-8)-centered feed-forward loop has been linked to NET accumulation and hepatic metastasis [13], while coiled-coil domain-containing 25 (CCDC25) has been identified as a receptor through which cancer cells respond to NET-derived DNA [14]. Liver-directed delivery of deoxyribonuclease I (DNase I) likewise reduced metastatic burden and partly restored local innate and adaptive immune responses in preclinical models [15]. At the primary tumor site, NETs have been linked to epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT), enhanced migratory behavior, and extracellular signal-regulated kinase (ERK) activation driven by neutrophil elastase (NE) [16, 17]. Studies of the tumor microenvironment (TME) further suggest that NETs may contribute to T-cell dysfunction and reduced responsiveness to programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1)-based immunotherapy [18, 19].

Recent work has extended this picture to the gut-liver axis, stromal biology, and microbial ecology. Fusobacterium nucleatum has been reported to stimulate NET formation and facilitate colorectal tumor progression [20]. Stromal signaling may reinforce this process; for example, inflammatory cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) induced by fibroblast growth factor 19 (FGF19) can promote NET formation within the liver metastatic microenvironment [21]. Beyond individual mediators, experimental and review studies increasingly support a model in which barrier dysfunction, bacterial translocation, and hepatic conditioning cooperate during metastatic niche formation along the gut-liver axis [22–24]. Neutrophils in CRC are also heterogeneous. Studies have described neutrophil-dependent remodeling of DNA damage responses [25], tissue-specific angiogenic specialization [26], and distinct neutrophil subsets with divergent effects on CRLM [27]. More recent preclinical work has even suggested that Escherichia coli-driven NET formation may intersect with chromatin regulatory programs in metastatic CRC cells [28].

Still, the available evidence does not support an exclusively tumor-promoting view of NETs. Treatment context and disease stage appear to matter. Chemotherapy-induced NETs have been reported to inhibit tumor growth in selected murine CRC models [29], interferon-γ (IFN-γ) has been linked to NET-associated tumor-killing activity in microsatellite stable (MSS) CRC [30], and NETs may limit early metastatic seeding through effects on macrophage phagocytosis in some liver colonization models [31]. Conversely, local therapies such as cryoablation may amplify NET formation and immune escape [32].

Several reviews have summarized NET formation, NET-mediated tumor progression and metastasis, biomarker potential, and candidate therapeutic strategies in cancer, including CRC-focused reviews and broader discussions of neutrophil biology in CRC [8–12, 33–36]. These studies have established an important foundation for the field. However, most have been organized around individual mechanisms or clinical applications, have considered neutrophils more broadly rather than NETs specifically, or have focused on a restricted setting such as CRLM or single-cell analysis.

The rationale for the present review is that recent evidence has altered the conceptual model rather than simply added further pathways. NET-associated effects appear to differ across chronic intestinal inflammation, established primary tumors, perioperative liver injury, early hepatic seeding, overt CRLM, and treatment-exposed disease. In selected settings, NET formation may accompany tumor restraint rather than progression. We therefore organize NET biology along the CRC disease continuum, linking inflammatory carcinogenesis, primary-tumor remodeling, gut-liver signaling, hepatic niche formation, immune regulation, biomarkers, and therapeutic targeting (Fig. 1). Particular attention is given to disease stage, anatomical site, neutrophil state, microbial exposure, and treatment context, as well as to the distinction between neutrophil accumulation, NET-prone cellular states, and bona fide extracellular trap formation. This framework is intended to clarify not only what NETs do in CRC, but also when their measurement or therapeutic inhibition may be informative, misleading, or potentially counterproductive. Neutrophils and NETosis: a conceptual framework

In CRC, neutrophils should not be viewed as a uniform cell population. At the simplest level, three related but distinct questions need to be separated: how many neutrophils are present, what functional state they are in, and whether they actually release NETs [12, 35]. This distinction is especially relevant in CRC, where tumor-associated neutrophils (TANs) are exposed to stromal cues, cytokines, microbial products, hypoxia, and treatment-related stress, all of which can reshape their phenotype [12, 26, 35]. The traditional N1/N2 classification is still useful as a broad conceptual model, but it is increasingly clear that it does not fully capture neutrophil diversity in CRC [12]. Recent studies in primary CRC and CRLM have identified tissue-programmed angiogenic neutrophils as well as circulating high-density neutrophils and low-density neutrophils with distinct functional properties, arguing for a spectrum of neutrophil states rather than a single TAN phenotype [26, 27]. Findings from single-cell analyses point in the same direction and suggest that neutrophil heterogeneity needs to be built into any discussion of NET biology in CRC [27, 36].

NETs were originally described as extracellular chromatin fibers released by neutrophils and decorated with granular proteins capable of trapping and killing microorganisms [6]. Since then, they have been implicated not only in host defense, but also in sterile inflammation, thrombosis, and cancer [7, 37]. The term NETosis, however, has not been used consistently in the literature. In its original and narrower sense, it referred to a form of cell death culminating in extracellular trap release [38]. Later work showed that this framework was incomplete. Reviews by Yipp and Kubes and by Thiam et al. emphasized that NET release can occur through more than one route and that the kinetics and cellular consequences depend on the stimulus [39, 40]. Experimental studies also supported this broader view: rapid NET release has been reported after Staphylococcus aureus exposure without necessarily following the full sequence described in the classical phorbol 12-myristate 13-acetate (PMA) model, and viable neutrophils have been shown to release mitochondrial DNA under selected conditions [41, 42]. In the cancer literature, NETosis is commonly used in this broader operational sense, and that convention is followed here [7, 39, 40].

The best-characterized pathway remains the relatively slow, reactive oxygen species (ROS)-dependent model induced by PMA, in which nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide phosphate (NADPH) oxidase activation, chromatin decondensation, and translocation of NE and myeloperoxidase (MPO) contribute to extracellular trap formation [38, 40, 43]. Peptidyl arginine deiminase 4 (PAD4)-mediated histone citrullination is another recurrent feature and appears essential in some infectious settings [44, 45]. At the same time, no single pathway can be assumed to define all NET formation. Martinod et al. showed that NE-deficient mice can still form NETs in experimental thrombosis [46], and Kasperkiewicz et al. reported that NETosis may occur independently of neutrophil serine proteases under some conditions [47]. Holmes et al. further cautioned against treating histone citrullination as a universal proxy for all NET structures [48]. These distinctions are relevant in CRC, where mechanistic claims are sometimes carried too readily from one NET model to another.

A related issue in the CRC literature is that neutrophil infiltration is often discussed as though it were equivalent to NET formation, which it is not [12, 35]. Detection of intact neutrophils, or even strong staining for neutrophil granule proteins, does not by itself demonstrate the presence of extracellular traps. Stronger histologic evidence generally requires extracellular or decondensed DNA together with neutrophil-associated proteins such as MPO or NE, interpreted in the context of nuclear morphology and tissue localization [7, 48–51]. Citrullinated histone H3 (H3Cit) is widely used and is often informative, but it should be interpreted with some caution rather than as a stand-alone marker [48, 51]. Methodologic papers have repeatedly shown that how NETs are visualized matters: staining strategy, fixation conditions, image acquisition, and analytic approach all affect interpretation [49–51]. Image-based quantification can be made more reproducible by computational pipelines, including NETQUANT and related tools, but these approaches improve measurement rather than solve the underlying problem of marker specificity [52–54]. Accordingly, automated analysis should be used to standardize, rather than replace, the biological definition of NETs. Studies should prespecify extracellular-morphology and marker-colocalization criteria, tissue compartments, image-acquisition settings, and reproducibility measures.

Similar caution applies to blood-based assays. In plasma from patients, cell-free DNA (cfDNA), MPO-DNA complexes, NE-DNA complexes, H3Cit/H3Cit-bound DNA are best regarded as surrogate readouts of NET-associated activity rather than direct measurements of intact extracellular structures [51, 54, 55]. Over the last few years, assay performance has improved, including the validation of enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay methods for H3Cit and the development of improved assays for nucleosomal H3Cit/H3Cit-bound DNA [51, 54, 56]. Even so, not all plasma assays perform equally well. Hayden et al. showed that some MPO-DNA enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay formats are error-prone and provide only limited information on NETs formed in vivo [57]. For that reason, plasma-based measurements are usually more convincing when interpreted together with tissue imaging, functional studies, or both [51, 55].

Transcriptomic technologies are informative, but they can complicate interpretation when treated as direct evidence of NET formation. Single-cell RNA sequencing and spatial approaches are useful for identifying neutrophil subsets, inferring state transitions, and highlighting signaling circuits that may predispose neutrophils to NET formation [26, 27, 36]. What they do not show directly is extracellular chromatin release [12, 36]. This distinction is important because it helps explain why studies that measure neutrophil state, extracellular structures, and plasma surrogates do not always agree. In many cases, they are not actually addressing the same biological layer.

In CRC, it is helpful to keep three levels separate: neutrophils as a heterogeneous cellular compartment, NETs as extracellular structures released under selected conditions, and NET-related biomarkers as indirect surrogates whose meaning depends on method and context. This distinction makes the later literature easier to interpret and provides a clearer starting point for the discussion of chronic intestinal inflammation and colitis-associated cancer (CAC). To avoid confusing observations in patient samples with clinical validation, the following sections distinguish three levels of evidence: mechanistic causality in experimental systems; support from human tissue, blood, or ex vivo samples; and clinical implementation based on standardized assays or prospective intervention studies. Most NET-related findings in CRC currently fall within the first two levels. From inflammation to cancer: NETs in the IBD/CAC setting

IBD, particularly long-standing colonic disease with persistent inflammatory activity, is a recognized predisposing condition for colorectal neoplasia. In contrast to sporadic CRC, CAC develops on a background of recurrent epithelial injury, defective restitution, chronic cytokine exposure, altered microbial handling, and sustained oxidative stress. Within this setting, neutrophils merit particular attention because they are recruited early to inflamed mucosa and because their effector repertoire extends well beyond phagocytosis, including ROS, proteases, cytokines, and NETs [4, 68, 69]. Even so, the transition from chronic colitis to dysplasia is clearly multifactorial, and the contribution of NETs should be interpreted as part of a broader inflammatory program rather than as an isolated initiating event.

In human IBD, the evidence for NET formation is currently strongest in ulcerative colitis (UC). Proteomic analysis of colonic biopsies identified marked enrichment of NET-associated proteins in UC tissue, and later biopsy-based studies confirmed the presence of NET structures in pediatric IBD involving both the small bowel and colon [70, 71]. Mechanistic work further suggested that active UC, more than Crohn’s disease (CD), is characterized by NETs carrying bioactive interleukin-1 beta (IL-1β) and thrombogenic tissue factor, thereby linking neutrophil activation not only to mucosal inflammation but also to local thromboinflammation [72]. This picture is reinforced by experimental and translational work showing that NETs can aggravate intestinal injury and prothrombotic tendency in IBD and can impair epithelial barrier integrity, a process that may facilitate luminal bacterial translocation and further amplify inflammation [73, 74]. These studies indicate that NETs are present in inflamed intestinal tissue and are biologically active there, rather than serving only as a by-product of neutrophil influx.

Several experimental studies have further implicated PAD4-dependent NET formation in ongoing colitic injury. Early pharmacologic inhibition of peptidyl arginine deiminases with Cl-amidine attenuated murine colitis [75]. Subsequent work in trinitrobenzene sulfonic acid (TNBS)-induced colitis showed increased expression of H3Cit and PAD4 together with enhanced NET-forming capacity, whereas pharmacologic inhibition of PAD4 reduced disease severity and inflammatory cytokine imbalance [76]. In dextran sulfate sodium (DSS)-driven colitis, PAD4-dependent NET formation has likewise been associated with mucosal inflammation, and more recent work linked NET-mediated barrier dysfunction in UC to activation of the cyclic GMP-AMP synthase-stimulator of interferon genes (cGAS-STING) pathway [77, 78]. These findings are consistent with a model in which extracellular chromatin and neutrophil granular contents amplify epithelial damage and delay resolution of mucosal inflammation. That said, most of these data are derived from chemically induced colitis models, and caution is still needed when extrapolating from acute or subacute murine injury to the full spectrum of human IBD.

How this neutrophil-rich inflammatory environment intersects with carcinogenesis is less firmly established, but several observations are directly relevant to CAC. Chronic oxidative and proteolytic stress has long been implicated in inflammation-associated carcinogenesis of the colon [68, 69]. More recently, azoxymethane/dextran sulfate sodium (AOM/DSS) models have suggested that NET-related pathways may influence both tumor initiation and subsequent tumor progression. In one study, suppression of PAD4-dependent NETs by Huang Qin Decoction reduced the initiation of experimental CAC [79]. A later study using glycyrrhizic acid reached a broadly similar conclusion, reporting that inhibition of PAD4 activity and NET formation was accompanied by attenuation of CAC development [80]. In parallel, aryl hydrocarbon receptor nuclear translocator (ARNT) deficiency was shown to drive recruitment of more suppressive, NET-prone neutrophils during colitis-associated tumorigenesis in a gut microbiota-dependent manner [81]. Alpha-1 antitrypsin (AAT) has also been reported to restrain inflammatory neutrophil-activated serine proteases and to suppress CAC progression [82]. Although these observations do not yet establish NETs as autonomous initiators of neoplasia, they do support the idea that NET-associated inflammatory circuits can contribute to a mucosal milieu permissive for malignant transformation and tumor outgrowth.

The literature also does not support a simple model in which neutrophils are uniformly deleterious. CD177-positive neutrophils have been reported to negatively regulate intestinal inflammation in IBD [83], and the same group later showed that CD177-positive neutrophils suppress epithelial tumorigenesis in CAC and are associated with better outcome in CRC [84]. Likewise, PTK2B-dependent neutrophil programs appear to support mucosal protection in UC [85]. Even PAD4-dependent responses may not be exclusively harmful: in UC, PAD4-dependent immunothrombosis has been shown to limit rectal bleeding, suggesting that some neutrophil-driven extracellular responses serve hemostatic rather than tissue-destructive functions [86]. These observations are important for interpretation. They imply that the biological effect of neutrophils in chronic colitis depends not only on their abundance, but also on their activation state, localization, and the balance between antimicrobial, barrier-protective, and tissue-injurious functions.

Evidence from CD broadly reinforces this need for caution. Compared with UC, direct NET-focused studies in CD remain fewer, and much of the recent work has centered on fistulizing disease or intestinal fibrosis rather than carcinogenesis itself. Still, PAD4-dependent NETs have been shown to activate intestinal fibroblasts and amplify early fibrotic remodeling in CD, while IL-1-driven stromal-neutrophil programs define a therapy-refractory subset of IBD enriched for neutrophil infiltration, fibroblast activation, and vascular remodeling [87, 88]. These findings are not equivalent to a direct cancer mechanism, but they are relevant because chronic ulceration, matrix remodeling, and failed resolution are all part of the tissue context in which neoplastic change may emerge over time. Overall, the link between neutrophils and the inflammation-to-cancer continuum in the colon is supported by substantial evidence, whereas the case for NETs as independent initiators remains limited and largely preclinical. It is therefore more accurate to view NETs as amplifiers of colitic injury, barrier dysfunction, thromboinflammation, and tissue remodeling than as sole drivers of CAC. Human studies have established that NET-associated structures and proteins are present in inflamed UC and pediatric IBD mucosa, but they have not shown prospectively that NET activity predicts progression from colitis to dysplasia or CRC. The causal and therapeutic evidence in CAC therefore remains largely preclinical, and no NET-based assay or NET-directed intervention has been clinically validated in this setting [70]. Beyond this inflammatory initiation, however, understanding how NETs shape established lesions requires shifting focus to the primary tumor site, where tumor cells, stromal elements, microbial signals, and neutrophils interact more directly.

NETs at the primary tumor site: reshaping tumor cell behavior and the local microenvironment

At the primary tumor site, the evidence suggests that neutrophils and NET-associated material are not simply bystanders (Fig. 2). An early histopathological study in colon adenocarcinoma showed that NET deposition and neutrophil accumulation were detectable within the tumor and in metastatic draining lymph nodes, and that both declined with increasing distance from the tumor mass [67]. This observation supports the view that NET formation can be a local feature of the primary lesion rather than a purely systemic event. The same study also found that isolated NETs inhibited the growth of Caco-2 cells in vitro by inducing apoptosis and/or reducing proliferation [67]. Although this work predates the more mechanistic CRC studies discussed below, it remains relevant because it shows that NETs at the primary site do not exert a uniform effect across models.

From inflammatory carcinogenesis to primary-tumor remodeling. Chronic colitis provides a tissue context marked by epithelial injury, barrier dysfunction, thromboinflammation, and sustained neutrophil activation. Within this setting, NET-associated inflammatory programs may amplify conditions permissive for colitis-associated carcinogenesis. In established primary colorectal tumors, tumor-derived signals, including chemokine production, exosomes, and apoptotic cell-derived cues, promote neutrophil recruitment and NET formation. These processes are linked more consistently to epithelial-mesenchymal transition-like change, migration, stromal crosstalk, and vascular remodeling than to direct tumor-cell proliferation

Subsequent studies have begun to clarify how primary CRC lesions establish a neutrophil-rich local environment that may favor NET formation. Hwang et al. showed that CRC stem-like cell-derived exosomal RNAs prime neutrophils through a pattern recognition receptor/NF-κB/IL-1β axis, while CXCL1 and CXCL2 recruit these cells to support colon tumorigenesis [89]. Other tumor-derived programs converge on IL-8-dependent neutrophil recruitment: KRAS-mutant exosomes and apoptotic CRC cells both contributed to this response, whereas KRAS-mutant exosomes and SKAP1–NFATc1 signaling were additionally linked to NET formation [58, 59, 90]. Together, these findings indicate that oncogenic signaling, extracellular vesicles, and tumor-cell death can sustain a NET-prone neutrophil microenvironment.

Among the NET-specific studies performed in CRC, the clearest evidence at the primary site concerns changes in tumor cell plasticity and motility. Stehr et al. demonstrated that NETs induce an EMT-like process in human colon cancer cells, linking direct exposure to NETs with acquisition of a more migratory phenotype [16]. Okamoto et al. reached a related but slightly more restricted conclusion. In their study, NETs did not measurably increase CRC cell proliferation in vitro, but they did enhance migration through NE-dependent activation of ERK signaling; high intratumoral or circulating NET markers were also associated with shorter relapse-free survival [17]. Read together, these data suggest that in established CRC, NETs may be more consistently related to invasive behavior than to direct mitogenic stimulation.

The primary-tumor literature is broader than the NET-specific literature alone, and some of the best-supported observations at this stage concern TANs more generally. Bui et al. showed that tumor-infiltrating neutrophils can alter the DNA damage response of CRC cells in a stage-dependent manner [25]. In lower-grade tumors, neutrophils promoted homologous recombination deficiency through microRNA-155-dependent downregulation of RAD51, which constrained tumor growth and created sensitivity to poly(ADP-ribose) polymerase 1 (PARP1) inhibition; in more advanced tumors, persistent neutrophil-associated genotoxic stress favored non-homologous end joining and tumor survival [25]. In a later study, the same group identified an angiogenic TAN program in CRC characterized by secreted phosphoprotein 1 (SPP1, osteopontin) and matrix metalloproteinase 14 (MMP14), and linked this program to endothelial chemotaxis, vascular branching, and tumor vascularization [26]. Pharmacologic inhibition of TAN trafficking or MMP14 reduced vascular density and led to tumor regression in preclinical models [26]. These studies do not establish that NETs are the sole mediators of primary-site remodeling, but they do make clear that the neutrophil compartment in CRC is actively involved in genomic stress responses and local vascular niche formation.

Overall, current evidence suggests that NETs at the primary CRC site contribute to neutrophil recruitment loops, EMT-like plasticity, migratory behavior, stromal crosstalk, and vascular remodeling. Direct growth-promoting effects are less consistent, and some of the strongest mechanistic data at this stage come from neutrophil-centered rather than NET-exclusive studies. At the primary tumor site, human support consists mainly of NET detection in resected CRC and draining lymph nodes and associations between tissue or circulating NET-related markers and relapse [80, 90]. The EMT and NE–ERK mechanisms are supported chiefly by experimental models. Given the colorectal location and portal drainage, these local events also bear on the gut-liver axis.

Gut microbiota, NETs, and the gut-liver axis

The gut-liver axis provides a useful framework for understanding why microbial factors may have a disproportionate effect on CRLM. In CRC, the question is not only whether the gut microbiota is altered, but also whether epithelial and vascular barrier function is sufficiently disrupted to permit bacterial dissemination or sustained delivery of microbial products to the liver. This distinction is relevant for NETs, because a liver-directed NET response requires not only activated neutrophils, but also a route by which microbial and tumor-associated inflammatory signals can reach the hepatic microenvironment.

Bertocchi et al. addressed this issue at the level of the gut vascular barrier (GVB). Using preclinical CRC models, the authors showed that tumor-resident E. coli disrupted the GVB, facilitated bacterial dissemination, and promoted liver metastasis [22]. Although NET formation was not the main focus, this work provided an anatomical and biological basis for later NET-centered studies of CRLM.

Work on F. nucleatum has added a complementary layer to this model. Yin et al. reported that F. nucleatum promotes liver metastasis by regulating the hepatic immune niche and altering gut microbiota composition [59]. Clinical studies have likewise detected F. nucleatum in CRLM tissues and linked its presence to antitumor immune features and to differences in immune responses and metastatic organ diversity [58, 60]. These observations do not by themselves establish a NET-mediated pathway, but they do indicate that microbial signals relevant to CRC are not necessarily confined to the primary lesion and may remain biologically active within metastatic tissue.

Mechanistic studies provide a direct link between F. nucleatum and NET biology. Kong et al. showed that the bacterium increases NET abundance through TLR4–ROS and NOD1/2 signaling in neutrophils [20]. Casasanta et al. further found that F. nucleatum host-cell binding and invasion induce IL-8 and CXCL1 secretion, which in turn drive CRC cell migration [61]. More recently, F. nucleatum was also reported to promote CRLM through a miR-5692a/IL-8 axis coupled to epithelial-mesenchymal transition [62]. Together, these data suggest a sequence in which bacterial-tumor interactions generate a chemokine milieu favorable for neutrophil recruitment, activation, and, under permissive conditions, NET formation.

Once the liver has been conditioned, stromal amplification becomes more important. Li et al. showed that FGF19-induced inflammatory CAFs promote neutrophil infiltration and mediate NET formation in liver metastatic niches [21]. Although this was not a microbiota study, it helps explain how gut-derived inflammatory signals may be translated into a hepatic microenvironment that sustains neutrophil accumulation and extracellular trap formation after tumor cells arrive. In that sense, microbial priming and stromal reinforcement are better viewed as sequential rather than competing processes.

The clearest recent link between a gut-associated bacterium and NET-dependent prometastatic programming in CRC comes from Pan et al. In that study, E. coli promoted CRC metastasis by inducing NET formation and maintaining loops between enhancers and promoters in cancer cells [28]. These findings are still recent and will need broader validation, particularly in well-annotated human CRLM cohorts, but they suggest that the microbiota-NETs axis may shape not only inflammation and cell trafficking but also the transcriptional stability of metastatic programs.

Overall, current evidence supports a multistep model in which barrier dysfunction and intratumoral bacteria permit bacterial dissemination or sustained microbial signaling to the liver, microbial and tumor-derived mediators recruit and activate neutrophils, hepatic stromal circuits stabilize NET formation, and NET-associated products facilitate metastatic outgrowth. However, the complete microbial–NET–liver-metastasis sequence has not been demonstrated longitudinally in patients, and neither microbiome- nor NET-guided intervention is ready for clinical use. If microbial signaling and barrier dysfunction help condition the liver, the critical issue then becomes how this conditioned organ supports metastatic colonization. This leads naturally to the premetastatic and metastatic niche in CRLM, where NET-associated pathways appear to influence both hepatic remodeling and tumor-cell fitness.

CRLM and the premetastatic/metastatic niche

CRLM develops within a hepatic microenvironment that is already altered before overt metastatic nodules become evident (Fig. 3). In this setting, it is useful to distinguish, at least conceptually, between a premetastatic niche, in which the liver is conditioned by tumor- and host-derived signals, and a metastatic niche, in which disseminated tumor cells survive, adapt, and expand. The current literature on NETs in CRC spans both stages. It includes PAD4-dependent extracellular matrix remodeling, intravascular NET accumulation after systemic or perioperative inflammatory stimuli, direct sensing of NET-derived DNA by tumor cells, and multicellular circuits involving stromal cells and specialized neutrophil subsets. These mechanisms are not identical, but together they suggest that NET-associated pathways are integrated into the ecology of CRLM rather than being confined to a single late event [21, 22, 27, 28, 31, 63, 83, 85, 86, 91–94].

Yuzhalin et al. used proteomics to show that liver metastases harbor higher levels of citrullination and PAD4 than unaffected liver, primary CRC, or adjacent colonic mucosa [91]. Inhibition of citrullination substantially reduced metastatic burden in murine models, suggesting that PAD4-dependent extracellular remodeling is functionally relevant to CRLM growth [91]. This study centered on extracellular matrix citrullination rather than classical NET structures defined by modern colocalization criteria, and it should not be taken as direct proof that all PAD4 activity in CRLM is NET-derived. It remains informative, however, because it placed extracellular neutrophil-associated chemistry within the metastatic liver microenvironment rather than only in the circulation.

More direct NET-focused studies followed. Yang et al. combined analyses of patient samples with in vitro assays and murine models to describe a positive feedback loop between tumor-derived IL-8 and NET formation in CRLM [13]. Neutrophils isolated from patients with CRC, particularly those with liver metastases, showed an increased propensity to form NETs, and circulating and tissue NET-associated markers were associated with liver metastatic disease. However, reciprocal IL-8–NET signaling and the effects of DNase I or IL-8 blockade were established experimentally rather than prospectively validated in patients. The IL-8–NET axis should therefore be regarded as a mechanistically supported pathway with human observational evidence, but not as a clinically validated therapeutic target.

In a separate study, CCDC25 was identified as a tumor-cell receptor for NET-derived DNA that activates ILK–β-parvin signaling and promotes directional migration and distant metastasis [14]. Human data showed abundant NETs in liver metastases from colon cancer and an association between higher CCDC25 expression in primary colon tumors and poorer survival. However, receptor binding, downstream signaling, and the effects of CCDC25 depletion or blockade were established mainly in cell-based assays and mouse models. Moreover, the reported ability of circulating NET markers to predict liver metastasis was evaluated in an early-stage breast cancer cohort rather than in CRC. CCDC25 should therefore be considered a candidate target supported by human correlative evidence, not a clinically validated target in CRC.

Perioperative studies provide a separate line of human evidence. In patients undergoing curative-intent resection of CRLM, increased postoperative NET formation was associated with markedly shorter disease-free survival [63]. In the same study, local DNase I treatment or PAD inhibition attenuated surgery-associated metastatic progression in a murine liver ischemia–reperfusion model [63]. Other interventions, including AAV-mediated hepatic DNase I expression and NET-targeted DNase I delivery, reduced CRLM only in preclinical models [15, 94].

More recent work points to a more complex metastatic niche. Li et al. showed that FGF19-induced inflammatory CAFs promote neutrophil infiltration and mediate NET formation in liver metastatic niches [21]. Xu et al. described three neutrophil subsets in CRC—anti-tumor high-density neutrophils, pro-tumor mature low-density neutrophils, and immature low-density neutrophils—and proposed that tumor-derived granulocyte-macrophage colony-stimulating factor converts anti-tumor neutrophils into a pro-tumor state through the AKT/NF-κB/NFAT5 axis [27]. In a related study, Zhang et al. showed that hepatic stellate cell-derived interleukin-33 (IL-33) promotes diacylglycerol O-acyltransferase 1/2 (DGAT1/2)-dependent lipid droplet accumulation in mature low-density neutrophils, supporting their survival and immunosuppressive function and favoring reactivation of dormant CRC cells in the liver [92]. Li et al. further linked NET-associated signaling to metabolic adaptation in CRLM by reporting that NETs regulate lactate dehydrogenase A (LDHA) expression and promote liver metastasis [93]. These newer studies suggest that, in CRLM, NET biology is increasingly difficult to separate from stromal conditioning, neutrophil subset conversion, and metabolic rewiring.

Although most CRLM studies support a prometastatic role for NETs, the earliest stages of hepatic seeding may not behave in the same way as later phases of metastatic growth. Liu et al. reported that, in one CRLM model, NET-derived neutrophil elastase enhanced macrophage phagocytosis by inactivating protease-activated receptor 2 signaling and downregulating the phagocytic checkpoint CD24, thereby restraining metastatic seeding [31]. These data suggest that premetastatic conditioning, initial seeding, and overt metastatic expansion should not be treated as biologically identical phases. At present, the strongest evidence places NETs as contributors to hepatic niche maintenance and metastatic outgrowth, whereas their role in the very earliest seeding step appears more conditional. The available human evidence remains observational, and no NET-directed intervention has yet demonstrated clinical benefit in patients with CRLM [15, 62, 63, 94]. Beyond these stromal and seeding dynamics, however, successful metastatic expansion ultimately requires evading cytotoxic responses, directly linking NET biology to immune regulation and immunotherapy.

Immune regulation and immunotherapy: how NETs may shape “hot” and “cold” tumors

Although the “hot” versus “cold” distinction is an oversimplification, it remains a practical shorthand in CRC. Clinical benefit from immune checkpoint blockade has been observed mainly in mismatch repair-deficient (dMMR) or microsatellite instability-high (MSI-H) disease, whereas most proficient mismatch repair (pMMR) or MSS tumors derive limited benefit from PD-1-based monotherapy. Liver metastasis adds a further layer of immune restraint, and single-cell analyses of CRLM have described a highly heterogeneous but broadly suppressive immune microenvironment. Against this background, NET biology has become relevant not because it fully explains immune resistance, but because it offers one plausible mechanism by which inflammatory myeloid responses may be translated into T-cell dysfunction or immune exclusion in CRC [95–97].

CRC-specific evidence that NETs contribute to adaptive immune suppression came from Kaltenmeier et al. Using tumor models and human samples, the authors showed that NETs contain programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) and can induce both metabolic and functional exhaustion of CD4-positive and CD8-positive T cells. They also reported increased serum levels of soluble PD-L1 and MPO-DNA complexes after CRLM resection and found that postoperative neutrophils were primed to form NETs that impaired T-cell function ex vivo [18]. Teijeira et al. described a related mechanism by showing that tumor-derived agonists of C-X-C motif chemokine receptors 1 and 2 (CXCR1/2) drive NETosis and allow NETs to shield tumor cells from cytotoxic lymphocytes [65].

Support for this interpretation in CRC came from Zhang et al. who showed that DNase I-mediated NET disruption improved the efficacy of anti-PD-1 therapy in mouse colon cancer models. Mechanistically, DNase I reduced NET formation, increased CD8-positive T-cell infiltration and cytotoxicity, and partially reversed resistance to PD-1 blockade [19]. This study did not suggest that NETs are the only barrier to immunotherapy in CRC, but it did move the field beyond correlation by showing that dismantling extracellular traps can alter response to checkpoint inhibition in vivo.

The immunologic meaning of NETs in CRC is not uniformly negative. Teng et al. reported that IFN-γ was associated with higher NET formation and tumor-killing activity in MSS CRC, indicating that NET induction can, in some settings, accompany rather than oppose antitumor immunity [30]. Li et al. likewise found that a combination of CB-839 and 5-fluorouracil induced NETs in PIK3CA-mutant CRC models and that disrupting these NETs attenuated the antitumor effect of treatment. In their translational cohort, increased intratumoral NET markers after therapy were associated with longer progression-free survival [29]. These data argue against treating all NET formation as functionally equivalent.

Evidence from CRLM further illustrates this point. Tan et al. showed that cryoablation induced a sustained neutrophil-dominated inflammatory state, promoted a shift from N1- to N2-like neutrophils, increased intracellular calcium, and triggered PAD4-dependent NET formation. This was accompanied by stronger PD-L1–PD-1 interactions between neutrophils and CD8-positive T cells, consistent with enhanced immune evasion. In the same study, degradation of NETs or inhibition of the CXCL2-CXCR2-Ca2+-PAD4 axis restored antitumor immunity and improved response to PD-1 antibodies [32]. Along similar lines, a recent study in MSS CRC suggested that the FGF19/ERK/IL-8 axis contributes to NET formation and that blocking fibroblast growth factor receptor 4 (FGFR4) signaling enhances PD-1 efficacy [66]. While these findings are still preclinical, they strengthen the view that NET-related pathways may be one of the modifiable determinants of immunotherapy resistance in CRC, particularly in MSS disease and in the liver metastatic setting. Human samples support an association between PD-L1-bearing NETs, postoperative NET activity, and T-cell dysfunction after CRLM resection [18]. By contrast, reversal of checkpoint resistance by DNase I, CXCR2/PAD4-axis inhibition, or FGF19/FGFR4 blockade has been demonstrated mainly in mouse CRC models [19]. No NET-based threshold has been prospectively validated for selecting patients for immunotherapy combinations.

These data do not show that NETs alone determine whether a CRC is immunologically “hot” or “cold.” Rather, NET-associated programs may contribute to a colder microenvironment by promoting T-cell exhaustion, limiting effective cytotoxic contact, and sustaining neutrophil-dominant inflammatory circuits that weaken checkpoint responsiveness. Selected therapy-induced NET responses may nevertheless remain tumoricidal. The more relevant translational question is when NET dismantling, neutrophil reprogramming, or upstream pathway inhibition is most likely to improve immunotherapy efficacy in CRC [18, 19, 29, 30, 32, 65, 66]. This question is best addressed by examining the specific circumstances in which NETs appear neutral or even host-protective (Fig. 4).

Functional heterogeneity: when NETs are not uniformly deleterious

Most of the literature discussed above supports a tumor-promoting role for NETs in CRC, particularly in metastatic progression and immune suppression. That overall impression is justified, but it should not be turned into a general rule. The available evidence does not support the view that all NET formation is necessarily detrimental to the host. In CRC, at least three settings argue for a more cautious interpretation: direct growth inhibition in simplified experimental systems, therapy-induced tumoricidal NET responses, and restriction of very early metastatic seeding. These observations do not overturn the broader prometastatic literature; rather, they suggest that the functional meaning of NETs depends on when they arise, what induces them, and which neutrophil state gives rise to them.

An early indication of this complexity came from Arelaki et al. who detected NET deposition within colon tumors and metastatic lymph nodes, but also showed that in vitro-generated NETs impeded the growth of Caco-2 cells by inducing apoptosis and/or reducing proliferation [67]. That finding has not been reproduced uniformly across CRC models, and its physiological relevance remains uncertain because isolated NETs in a simplified coculture system are unlikely to capture the full stromal, microbial, and vascular context of an established tumor. Even so, the finding remains useful because it suggests that extracellular traps can, under some conditions, exert cytotoxic rather than trophic effects on colon cancer cells.

Evidence that NETs can contribute to tumor control in CRC comes mainly from treatment-induced models. Li et al. showed that the combination of the glutaminase inhibitor CB-839 and 5-fluorouracil inhibited the growth of PIK3CA-mutant CRC in xenograft, syngeneic, and genetically engineered mouse models partly through NETs [29]. In that study, DNase I treatment or neutrophil depletion attenuated the antitumor effect, indicating that extracellular traps were functionally involved rather than merely associated with treatment response. Mechanistically, the reported effector in this setting was not NE or NET-derived DNA, but cathepsin G (CTSG): chemotherapy-induced NETs released CTSG, which entered tumor cells through the receptor for advanced glycation end products (RAGE), cleaved 14-3-3ε, promoted BAX mitochondrial translocation, and induced apoptosis [29]. Importantly, in the accompanying clinical study, increased intratumoral NETs after treatment were associated with longer progression-free survival. These findings do not imply that NETs are broadly tumoricidal, but they do suggest that a NET-dependent response may contribute to therapeutic efficacy in a genotype- and regimen-specific setting.

Teng et al. reported a related finding in MSS CRC [30]. In that study, NETs were more abundant in MSI-H tumors, IFN-γ response genes were enriched, and coculture with MSI-H CRC cell lines induced greater NET formation and higher tumor-cell apoptosis than coculture with MSS lines [30]. When IFN-γ was added in MSS models, NET formation increased, tumor growth fell, and the tumor-killing activity of anti-PD-1 treatment was enhanced in subcutaneous and orthotopic CT26 models. Because IFN-γ affects multiple immune compartments, these results do not establish that NETs were the sole mediators of tumor control, but they do indicate that enhanced NET formation can coincide with a more effective antitumor response in selected immune contexts.

Another instructive exception concerns timing. Liu et al. examined the seeding phase of CRLM and found that NETs promoted macrophage phagocytosis both in vitro and in vivo [31]. In their model, NE derived from NETs inactivated canonical protease-activated receptor 2 (PAR2) signaling, downregulated the phagocytic checkpoint CD24, and favored macrophage recruitment and M1 polarization, thereby restraining early liver colonization [31]. This does not negate the larger body of work implicating NETs in hepatic niche formation and metastatic outgrowth. Instead, it suggests that the earliest interaction between circulating tumor cells, neutrophils, and hepatic innate immune cells may differ biologically from later phases of niche maintenance and expansion. In other words, the effect of NETs may vary across metastatic stages rather than remain constant throughout CRLM evolution.

These differences likely reflect heterogeneity at several levels. The phenotype of the neutrophil releasing the trap, the inducing stimulus, the molecular cargo retained on the chromatin scaffold, the tissue compartment, and the timing within the disease course may all shape the downstream effect. In CRLM, Xu et al. identified anti-tumor high-density neutrophils, pro-tumor mature low-density neutrophils, and immature low-density neutrophils, and showed that tumor-derived GM-CSF can drive neutrophil-state conversion before CXCR2-dependent NET release [27]. The field has not, however, established two fixed molecular classes of “pro-tumor” and “anti-tumor” NETs. These terms are better used as functional descriptions. NET-derived DNA and NE have been linked to migration and metastatic outgrowth in untreated disease [14, 17], whereas CTSG-containing NETs promoted tumor-cell apoptosis after CB-839 plus 5-fluorouracil treatment [29], and NET-derived NE enhanced macrophage phagocytosis during early hepatic seeding [31]. Thus, apparently similar extracellular structures may produce different outcomes according to their cellular source, cargo, anatomical site, disease stage, and treatment context.

Overall, current evidence argues against indiscriminate NET inhibition in CRC. NETs appear harmful in many untreated and metastatic settings, but they may be neutral or even beneficial when generated transiently during effective therapy or during the earliest phases of tumor-cell clearance. The practical question is therefore not whether NET-directed strategies should be abandoned, but when and in whom they are likely to help rather than interfere with host antitumor responses. Human evidence for tumor-restraining NET activity is limited to selected treatment-associated observations. Increased intratumoral NET markers after CB-839 plus 5-fluorouracil were associated with longer progression-free survival, whereas the causal tumoricidal and anti-seeding mechanisms were established in experimental models [29, 31]. These findings caution against indiscriminate NET suppression, but they are not yet actionable for treatment selection. Navigating these functional ambiguities in clinical practice will ultimately depend on developing biomarker readouts that are robust, interpretable, and highly context-specific.

Biomarkers: which readouts are closest to clinical use

From a clinical perspective, a NET-related biomarker in CRC needs to satisfy several conditions at once: it should be obtainable with minimal additional burden to the patient, reasonably reproducible across laboratories, and interpretable despite common confounders such as infection, surgery, thrombosis, and cytotoxic therapy. In practice, the current candidates fall into three broad categories: circulating surrogates, tissue-based measurements, and dynamic peri-treatment readouts. Most remain exploratory, but some formats appear more translatable than others.

Blood-based assays are the most accessible. An early study showed that neutrophils isolated from patients with CRC had a greater propensity to generate NETs in vitro than neutrophils from healthy controls, supporting the idea that systemic neutrophil priming is already present in this disease [98]. For routine use, however, ex vivo NETosis assays are unlikely to be practical. More feasible plasma readouts include MPO, NE, circulating cfDNA, and histone citrullination-based markers. In metastatic CRC, Pastor et al. measured MPO, NE, and circulating DNA in 219 patients and showed a close association between NET-related proteins and circulating DNA, suggesting that a relevant fraction of plasma DNA may derive from NET degradation rather than exclusively from tumor cells [99]. This observation is clinically relevant because total cfDNA is sometimes interpreted as a surrogate of tumor burden, whereas inflammatory NET formation may contribute materially to the signal. In a different clinical context, platelet-induced NET formation in CRC was linked to stronger procoagulant activity, indicating that some circulating NET readouts may reflect thromboinflammatory risk as much as tumor progression [100]. Across broader cancer cohorts, circulating H3Cit has shown associations with advanced disease, venous thromboembolism, and mortality [101–103]. Even so, the CRC-specific evidence base for H3Cit remains limited, and assay standardization is still evolving; notably, improved nucleosomal H3Cit assays have been developed, whereas commonly used MPO-DNA enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay formats have raised specificity concerns [56, 57].

Tissue-based assessment is more labor-intensive, but it preserves the spatial context that circulating assays necessarily lose. In colon cancer, NET deposition has been shown to form a gradient centered on the primary lesion and extending into metastatic lymph nodes [67]. In another CRC study, higher intratumoral and systemic NET readouts were associated with shorter relapse-free survival [17]. From a translational standpoint, one of the more immediately relevant studies is the work by Zhong et al. in locally advanced rectal cancer (LARC), where high tumor-infiltrating NET density was associated with poor postoperative survival, and pretreatment biopsy NET density was linked to a lower likelihood of a favorable response to neoadjuvant therapy [104]. Because diagnostic biopsy is already part of routine work-up in rectal cancer, this format is arguably closer to clinical implementation than specialized ex vivo assays. That said, tissue interpretation needs some discipline: MPO or NE staining alone is not sufficient to establish bona fide NET structures, and pathology-based scoring is more convincing when extracellular DNA, nuclear decondensation, and neutrophil-associated proteins are assessed together.

A third category consists of dynamic biomarkers measured around treatment. This may ultimately prove more informative than a single static measurement. In patients undergoing resection of CRLM, postoperative increases in MPO-DNA and soluble PD-L1 were observed, consistent with acute NET activation after surgery [18]. In a prospective perioperative study that included colon cancer, Kudriavtsev et al. found that circulating nuclear DNA, MPO, and NE were elevated before and after surgery and remained correlated in colon cancer, particularly in the postoperative period [64]. At minimum, these findings suggest that postoperative increases in plasma DNA cannot automatically be assumed to be tumor-derived. This point may matter when total cfDNA or non-tumor-informed circulating DNA assays are interpreted in the perioperative setting. Treatment-associated tissue changes may also carry information. In a selected therapeutic context, Li et al. found that increased intratumoral NET levels after CB-839 plus 5-fluorouracil treatment were associated with longer progression-free survival [29]. The practical implication is that longitudinal NET readouts may be useful, but only if the treatment context is taken into account.

Liquid-biopsy interpretation may be improved by pairing NET-associated readouts with tumor-informed circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA). Total cfDNA cannot distinguish tumor-derived DNA from DNA released during NET formation, a limitation that is particularly relevant after surgery or systemic treatment. Serial assessment of ctDNA together with analytically validated NET-associated markers could help separate changes in tumor burden from perioperative or treatment-related neutrophil activation. This approach remains investigational and will require harmonized sampling, pre-analytical standardization, and prospective evaluation against clinically relevant end points [56, 57, 102, 105].

Transcriptomic models have added another layer to biomarker development. Recent studies have proposed NET-related prognostic signatures for CRLM and for CRC more broadly, with reported associations with survival, immune infiltration, and treatment sensitivity [106, 107]. These models are best viewed as exploratory computational biomarkers rather than direct measurements of extracellular traps. What they capture, at least in their current form, is a NET-associated transcriptional program rather than extracellular chromatin structures in blood or tissue. For that reason, they are better viewed as exploratory computational biomarkers than as near-term clinical tests.

Overall, the biomarker formats currently closest to the clinic are probably pretreatment biopsy NET density in LARC and paired plasma panels that combine a NET-associated protein marker with a DNA-based readout in metastatic or perioperative CRC. Even these approaches still face substantial obstacles, including pre-analytical variability, lack of standardized cutoffs, and the difficulty of separating NET burden from nonspecific inflammation. These approaches remain investigational: neither tissue NET scoring nor circulating NET-associated panels have undergone sufficient analytical standardization and prospective clinical-utility validation for routine use. A reasonable near-term goal is therefore not to position NET biomarkers as stand-alone tests, but to evaluate whether they add useful information when interpreted alongside established clinicopathologic variables and tumor-informed ctDNA assays. Ultimately, since biomarkers are only clinically meaningful if they guide actionable interventions, the translational focus must naturally shift to therapeutics: whether NET-related pathways are best approached by dismantling extracellular traps, blocking their formation, or targeting the underlying neutrophil states.

Therapeutic targeting: from dismantling NETs to state-specific intervention

Work on therapeutic targeting has moved from documenting the presence of NETs to asking which part of the pathway can be targeted. Current approaches fall into four broad categories: dismantling preformed NETs, suppressing NET formation, interrupting tumor-cell recognition of NET-associated signals, and reprogramming the neutrophil states that give rise to pathogenic NETosis. Because CRC is biologically heterogeneous, the most useful point of intervention is likely to differ between the primary tumor, perioperative CRLM, and MSS disease undergoing immunotherapy [19, 32, 66, 86, 94, 105, 108–111] (Fig. 5). The most direct strategy remains degradation of the extracellular DNA scaffold. In preclinical CRLM models, liver-directed AAV-mediated DNase I expression reduced metastatic burden and partially restored local innate and adaptive immune responses [86]. In mouse colon cancer models, DNase I also improved the efficacy of PD-1 blockade [19]. More recently, a membrane-derived liposomal system carrying DNase I was designed to target NET-rich lesions and inhibit CRLM in vivo [94]. These studies are important because they show that dismantling already formed NETs can alter disease course even without fully preventing neutrophil recruitment. The main limitation of this approach is biological non-specificity: DNase I does not distinguish between prometastatic NETs and NET responses that may be transiently treatment-associated or contextually host-protective [19, 29, 31, 86, 94].

A second strategy is to prevent NETosis or weaken its downstream effectors. In CRC, PAD4 inhibition with GSK484 increased radiosensitivity and reduced NET formation in vitro and in vivo [108]. This is a relevant proof of concept, although PAD4 is not restricted to neutrophils in this disease context, so part of the observed antitumor effect may reflect tumor-cell-intrinsic biology rather than NET suppression alone. A complementary study identified a secreted protein acidic and rich in cysteine (SPARC)-receptor for activated C kinase 1 (RACK1)-nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide phosphate (NADPH) oxidase axis upstream of NET formation and integrin α5β1 as part of the tumor-side response to NETs; combined inhibition with diphenyleneiodonium chloride and ATN-161 suppressed metastasis in vivo [105]. This dual strategy is attractive because it targets both NET generation and the tumor response to NETs. By comparison, neutrophil elastase inhibition has a strong mechanistic rationale because of the link between NET-derived neutrophil elastase and ERK-dependent migration, but direct therapeutic testing in NET-defined CRC models remains less developed than approaches based on DNase I or PAD4 [17, 110].

A third approach is to intervene further upstream in the inflammatory circuits that drive NET-prone neutrophil responses. Building on the chemokine mechanisms described above, small-molecule CXCR1/2 antagonists reduced liver metastases generated by human colon cancer cells in mice [109]. In context-specific CRC models, CXCR2/PAD4 or FGFR4 pathway inhibition improved antitumor immunity and enhanced sensitivity to PD-1 blockade [32, 66]. FGF19/FGFR4 is of additional interest because it links stromal NET formation with a druggable tumor pathway [21, 111]. These upstream strategies are unlikely to be NET-specific, but that may also be their advantage in settings where neutrophil recruitment, stromal activation, and immune exclusion are tightly coupled.

The most recent literature points toward a fourth, more selective direction: targeting the neutrophil states that sustain pathogenic NETosis rather than suppressing all extracellular traps indiscriminately. In CRLM, tumor-derived GM-CSF can drive the conversion of anti-tumor neutrophils into pro-metastatic states [27], while IL-33-dependent lipid remodeling supports mature low-density neutrophils that favor hepatic colonization [92]. These studies do not yet provide ready-made therapies, but they shift the field toward state-specific intervention. This matters because blanket NET inhibition may not always be desirable. As discussed above, chemotherapy-induced NETs can contribute to tumor control in PIK3CA-mutant CRC [29], and NET-associated neutrophil elastase may even restrain the earliest phase of hepatic seeding in selected models [31]. Therapeutic selectivity may therefore matter more than intensity.

Overall, the therapeutic literature in CRC remains predominantly preclinical, but it no longer supports a single conceptual model. DNase I-based approaches provide the clearest proof that NET dismantling can alter metastatic behavior. PAD4, NADPH oxidase, integrin α5β1, CXCR2, and FGF19-related signaling now represent concrete intervention points upstream or downstream of NET formation. The key challenge is to determine when broad NET blockade is appropriate and when liver-directed, perioperative, or neutrophil-state-specific intervention is more likely to preserve host defense while limiting tumor progression [32, 66, 86, 94, 105, 108]. While this expanding therapeutic repertoire is encouraging, translating any of these approaches into true clinical utility ultimately requires a critical assessment of current literature limitations and a clear roadmap for future translational studies.

Outlook and limitations

Several constraints recur throughout the literature on NETs in CRC, and they are worth stating plainly. The field has progressed well beyond the stage of asking whether NETs are present in CRC. The more difficult question now is how NET-associated biology should be interpreted across different disease states. Primary tumors, premetastatic liver, early hepatic seeding, established CRLM, and treatment-exposed disease are often discussed within the same framework, even though recent studies suggest that these settings differ materially in neutrophil composition, spatial organization, and the consequence of extracellular trap release. This is likely one reason why apparently discordant findings continue to emerge. In some models, NETs support metastatic outgrowth and immune suppression; in others, they appear to limit very early seeding or participate in treatment-induced tumor killing. These differences are unlikely to be noise alone and probably reflect real biological context [27, 29, 31, 90, 112, 113].

One limitation is definitional and technical. Many CRC studies still infer NET burden from neutrophil abundance, myeloperoxidase staining, or a single plasma readout. H3Cit, myeloperoxidase-DNA complexes, neutrophil elastase-DNA complexes, and total circulating cell-free DNA are related measures, but they are not interchangeable. Methodologic work has improved the field, yet concerns about assay specificity remain, and perioperative increases in circulating DNA further complicate interpretation. Future CRC studies will need a higher evidentiary threshold, ideally combining tissue morphology with an orthogonal assay or a functional perturbation such as DNase I or PAD4 inhibition [30, 56, 57, 64].

Another limitation lies in model design. Much of the mechanistic evidence still comes from systems that compress the natural history of metastasis or rely on strong artificial stimuli of NET formation. These models have been useful for identifying candidate pathways, but they are less informative when the main question is when NETs matter most: during local invasion, liver conditioning, initial seeding, immune escape, or late metastatic expansion. This is where the newer orthotopic, single-cell, and spatial studies are particularly helpful. Recent work has begun to track the liver microenvironment from the premetastatic state to established metastasis and to identify neutrophil populations that expand before overt tumor colonization, rather than only after it. Such studies do not resolve every causality problem, but they do restore a temporal dimension that has often been missing from the CRC-NET literature. That temporal dimension will be essential if therapeutic timing is to become rational rather than empirical [27, 90, 113].

Third, spatial and multiomic approaches can move the field beyond a cell-inventory view of neutrophils. Spatial transcriptomics and spatial proteomics can define where neutrophil states sit in relation to tumor cells, fibroblasts, vessels, and cytotoxic lymphocytes, whereas multiomic profiling can link these neighborhoods to transcriptional, proteomic, and metabolic programs. These methods do not, however, demonstrate extracellular trap release. Their value will depend on co-registration with multiplex imaging and automated, morphology-aware NET quantification in the same specimen. Applied to matched primary tumors, blood, and liver metastases, preferably sampled longitudinally, this design could distinguish NET-prone neutrophil states from actual NET deposition and identify the disease stage and tissue context in which NETs are associated with immune exclusion, metastatic outgrowth, or treatment response [27, 89, 90, 97, 112, 113].

A further limitation is translational. Human data remain largely correlative and are usually obtained at a single time point. The readouts most often proposed as clinical biomarkers—circulating DNA, H3Cit, myeloperoxidase-DNA, and tissue NET density—are all influenced by infection, surgery, thrombosis, and systemic therapy. For that reason, NET-related biomarkers are unlikely to be most useful as stand-alone tests. A more realistic direction is to interpret NET-associated biomarkers longitudinally alongside treatment timing, metastatic site, pathologic response, thrombosis risk, and tumor-informed ctDNA, rather than as stand-alone measures of disease burden [56, 57, 64, 104].

Therapeutically, the main unanswered question is not whether NETs can be targeted, but how selective targeting should be. The coexistence of prometastatic NETs, therapy-induced tumoricidal NETs, and anti-seeding effects makes indiscriminate suppression difficult to justify. This argues for a more stratified strategy. In perioperative CRLM or in settings dominated by established metastatic outgrowth, dismantling extracellular traps may still be appropriate. In MSS disease with clear evidence of immune exclusion, upstream pathway inhibition may be more rational than broad neutrophil depletion. By contrast, in genotypes or treatment settings in which NETs may contribute to tumor killing, blanket suppression may be counterproductive. Another underexplored direction is endogenous NET clearance. The recent observation that DNASE1L3-expressing dendritic cells can degrade NETs and restore spatial access of CD8-positive T cells suggests that the field may need to think beyond how NETs are formed and devote more attention to how they are cleared, remodeled, and resolved within tumors [29, 31, 32, 66, 114].

Several priorities follow from these gaps. CRC studies would benefit from a minimum reporting framework for NET detection in tissue and blood, including pre-analytical handling and orthogonal validation. Longitudinal cohorts with matched primary tumor, blood, and liver samples are also needed to separate cause from consequence more convincingly. Intervention studies should be designed around biological scenarios rather than targets alone, and the most informative translational work will likely combine spatially resolved human tissue analysis with mechanism-aware pharmacodynamic endpoints. If these issues are addressed, NET biology may become less of a diffuse explanatory framework and more of a tractable component of precision medicine in CRC [27, 30, 32, 90, 112–114].

Conclusions

Current evidence supports a role for NETs across several stages of CRC, including inflammatory carcinogenesis, primary-tumor remodeling, gut-liver crosstalk, metastatic niche formation, immune regulation, and treatment response. However, the available data do not support a uniformly deleterious model. The biologic meaning of NETs appears to depend on disease phase, anatomical site, neutrophil state, and therapeutic context. Progress in this field will depend less on confirming the presence of NETs and more on defining when they are pathogenic, when they are incidental, and when they accompany effective host or treatment responses. Such a framework may be more useful for biomarker development and for the design of selective therapeutic strategies in CRC.
