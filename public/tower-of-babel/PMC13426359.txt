Exosomal miR-21 Derived from Paclitaxel-Resistant Breast Cancer Cells Promotes Chemoresistance in Breast Cancer via p53/PI3K/AKT/mTOR Signaling
Authors: Yanyan Wang, Qing Cai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo investigate whether exosomes derived from paclitaxel (PTX)-resistant breast cancer cells confer chemoresistance to parental cells and to explore the involvement of the p53/PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling pathway in this process.MethodsExosomes were isolated from PTX-sensitive and PTX-resistant breast cancer cells (MCF-7 and MDA-MB-231) and characterized by transmission electron microscopy, nanoparticle tracking analysis, and detection of exosomal markers. Bioinformatics analyses were performed to predict microRNAs potentially targeting TP53. Functional assays, including Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8), terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase dUTP nick end labeling (TUNEL), quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR), and Western blotting, were used to assess drug sensitivity, apoptosis, and pathway-related molecular changes in recipient cells. MicroRNA inhibition and p53 overexpression approaches were applied for mechanistic validation, and key experiments were conducted in multiple breast cancer cell models.ResultsExosomes derived from PTX-resistant cells significantly enhanced PTX resistance and attenuated apoptosis-related changes in recipient cells. Inhibition of exosomal miR-21 or restoration of p53 expression reversed these effects. Similar results were observed across different breast cancer subtypes.ConclusionThese findings indicate that exosomal miR-21 derived from PTX-resistant breast cancer cells may contribute to chemoresistance, at least in part, by suppressing p53 and modulating PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway activation.

Introduction

Breast cancer is the most commonly diagnosed malignancy among women worldwide, with over 2.3 million new cases and approximately 685,000 deaths reported annually, according to the Global Cancer Observatory (GLOBOCAN 2020).1,2 Despite improvements in early screening and treatment strategies, breast cancer stands as a leading cause of cancer-related deaths, particularly among individuals with late-stage or relapsed disease.3,4 Chemotherapy remains one of the main methods for treating tumors in clinical practice, and paclitaxel (PTX) is extensively applied as a first-line therapy against breast cancer due to its strong anti-mitotic and apoptosis-inducing properties.5,6 However, the long-term benefits of PTX are often undermined by emerging chemoresistance, which severely limits its therapeutic potential and is linked to unfavorable outcomes and high rates of disease relapse.7 Consequently, clarifying the molecular events driving resistance to PTX treatment in breast cancer is urgently required to uncover effective targets for intervention.

Emerging evidence suggests that tumor-derived exosomes, which are small extracellular vesicles measuring 30–150 nm, act as major contributors to intercellular signaling processes and chemoresistance.8,9 These nano-sized vesicles transport a complex cargo comprising proteins, RNAs, lipids, and metabolites capable of influencing recipient cell behavior.10 In breast cancer, exosomes originating from drug-resistant cells have been shown to contribute to promoting malignant phenotypes, including increased cellular proliferation, invasive capacity, and apoptotic resistance. Two molecular signaling axes are particularly relevant to this process: the tumor suppressor p53 pathway and the PI3K/AKT/mTOR cascade. p53 is a central stress-response regulator, but its biological output is not limited to apoptosis and is strongly influenced by stress intensity, signal duration, cellular context, and dynamic regulatory patterns.11,12 In response to DNA damage or chemotherapy-induced stress, p53 can induce distinct cellular outcomes, including cell-cycle arrest, DNA repair, senescence, metabolic adaptation, and apoptosis.13 Therefore, moderate or transient p53 activation may allow cancer cells to repair damage and survive treatment-induced stress, whereas stronger or sustained activation is more likely to promote apoptotic elimination. In parallel, PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling supports cancer cell proliferation, metabolism, survival, and therapeutic resistance.14 Importantly, the relationship between p53 and PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling is complex rather than strictly linear; p53 can regulate mTOR-related signaling through transcriptional and non-transcriptional mechanisms, while genotoxic stress may also induce p53-dependent nuclear AKT activation under specific biological conditions.15,16 However, whether paclitaxel-resistant breast cancer cell-derived exosomes reshape this p53-associated signaling balance and thereby contribute to PTX resistance remains insufficiently defined.

Although exosome-mediated mechanisms of chemoresistance have attracted growing attention in recent years, context-specific evidence linking p53 suppression to PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway activation in exosome-mediated PTX resistance remains relatively limited. Therefore, the current investigation sought to evaluate whether PTX-resistant MCF-7/PTX cell-derived exosomes confer resistance in parental MCF-7 cells by modulating the p53/PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling cascade. Furthermore, we evaluated whether restoring p53 expression could reverse exosome-induced resistance and downstream signaling activation. This study may provide mechanistic insight into exosome-driven chemoresistance and offer a potential basis for therapeutic intervention in breast cancer.

Materials and Methods

Experimental Cell Lines and Cultivation Protocols

Human breast cancer cell lines MCF-7 (HTB-22™) and MDA-MB-231 (HTB-26™) were acquired from the American Type Culture Collection. Paclitaxel-resistant sublines, designated MCF-7/PTX and MDA-MB-231/PTX, were established by stepwise exposure to increasing concentrations of PTX, as previously described, or were obtained from the Cell Research Institute, Chinese Academy of Sciences (Beijing, China).

Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM; Gibco, USA; Cat# 11965092) served as the culture medium for MCF-7 and MCF-7/PTX cells, while RPMI-1640 medium (Gibco; Cat# 11875093) was used for MDA-MB-231 and MDA-MB-231/PTX cells. All media were supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco; Cat# 10099141C) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin (Gibco; Cat# 15140122). Cells were maintained at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2. To maintain the resistant phenotype, MCF-7/PTX cells were routinely propagated in medium containing 100 nM PTX.

Plasmid Transfection and p53 Overexpression

Plated in 6-well plates (2 × 105 cells/well), MCF-7 cells were transfected at 70–80% confluence with a p53 overexpression plasmid (pcDNA3.1-p53) or the corresponding empty vector control (pcDNA3.1-empty) (GenePharma, Shanghai, China) using Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen, Cat# L3000008) following the supplier’s instructions. Plasmid DNA and Lipofectamine 3000 were individually prepared in Opti-MEM medium (Gibco, Cat# 31985070), subsequently combined and allowed to form complexes for 15 min at room temperature before application to the cultured cells. Six hours later, the transfection mixture was exchanged for complete culture medium.

In studies involving exosome exposure or combined interventions, cells were incubated for 24 h post-transfection to allow effective gene expression, followed by treatment with exosomes (100 μg/mL) derived from parental or PTX-resistant cells for an additional 24 h before subsequent analyses.

Exosome Isolation and Treatment

Exosomes were isolated from parental breast cancer cells and their PTX-resistant derivatives, including MCF-7, MCF-7/PTX, MDA-MB-231, and MDA-MB-231/PTX. Cells were cultured in medium supplemented with 10% exosome-depleted fetal bovine serum for 48 h, after which conditioned media were collected. Exosomes were purified by differential centrifugation and ultracentrifugation (100,000 × g, 70 min, 4°C), rinsed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), and stored at −80°C.

Exosome preparations were subjected to bicinchoninic acid (BCA)-based quantification and normalized by their protein content. Exosomes obtained from PTX-sensitive parental cells were defined as S/exo, whereas exosomes isolated from PTX-resistant cells were defined as P/exo. Recipient cells were exposed to either S/exo or P/exo at 100 μg/mL for a period of 24 h.17 In experiments involving drug exposure, cells were pretreated with exosomes prior to PTX administration. For combined treatments, exosomes were added 24 h after plasmid or miRNA inhibitor transfection to ensure effective gene modulation before downstream analyses. Particle-to-protein ratios were further calculated by combining NTA-derived particle concentrations with BCA-based protein concentrations to evaluate the comparability of exosome preparations.

Transmission Electron Microscopy (TEM)

The morphology of S/exo and P/exo particles was observed using TEM. Fixation was performed with 2% paraformaldehyde (Servicebio, Cat# G1101, China) at ambient temperature for 30 min. A 10-μL portion of exosomes was loaded onto carbon-coated copper grids (Beijing Zhongjingkeyi Technology Co., China) and incubated for 20 minutes. After removing excess liquid, the grids were washed with PBS followed by negative staining using 2% phosphotungstic acid (Solarbio, Cat# P8360, China) for 60s. Following air-drying, samples were observed on a JEM-1400 transmission electron microscope (JEOL, Japan) at 80 kV.

Nanoparticle Tracking Analysis (NTA)

The size distribution and particle concentration of S/exo and P/exo exosomes were measured using NTA.18 Exosome samples were diluted 1:1000 in sterile-filtered PBS (HyClone, Cat# SH30256.01, Cytiva, USA) to reach a particle range of 1–10 × 108 particles/mL. ZetaView PMX 110 (Particle Metrix, Germany) was used for measurement, with ZetaView 8.05.14 software applied for analysis. Three 60-s videos were captured for each sample, and the average particle diameter and particle concentration were computed from three independent runs.

Cell Viability Assay

Cell viability was assessed using the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) assay (Dojindo, Cat# CK04) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Breast cancer cells (MCF-7 or MDA-MB-231, as indicated) under different treatment conditions were seeded into 96-well plates (5 × 103 cells/well) and maintained overnight to allow attachment. Where indicated, cells were pretreated with exosomes (100 μg/mL) derived from parental or PTX-resistant cells during the 24 h preceding drug exposure.

Cells were then exposed to PTX (MedChemExpress, Cat# HY-B0015) at six indicated concentrations (0, 10, 50, 100, 500, and 1000 nM) for 2 d. Thereafter, each well received 10 μL of CCK-8 reagent prior to a 2-h incubation at 37 °C. The absorbance signal at 450 nm was quantified with a microplate reader (BioTek Epoch, USA). Cell survival rates were reported after normalization against control samples.

Apoptosis Detection

Apoptotic cells were identified by TUNEL staining (Beyotime, China) in accordance with the supplier’s recommendations, as previously described by Gavrieli et al19 After treatment, cells were subjected to 4% paraformaldehyde fixation and 0.1% Triton X-100 permeabilization, after which they were exposed to TUNEL mix for 1 h at 37°C in darkness. Following a 5-min 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI, Beyotime, Cat# C1002) nuclear counterstaining, fluorescent images were recorded through a Leica DMi8 microscope (Germany), and TUNEL-positive cell proportion was calculated from five randomly chosen fields/well via ImageJ (version 1.53, NIH, USA).

Quantitative Real-Time PCR (qRT-PCR)

RNAiso Plus reagent (Takara, Cat# 9108, Japan) was employed to obtain total RNA, which served as the template for cDNA synthesis using the PrimeScript RT reagent kit (Takara, Cat# RR037A). Subsequent real-time PCR reactions were run on a LightCycler 96 instrument (Roche, Switzerland) with TB Green Premix Ex Taq II (Takara, Cat# RR820A). Gene- and miRNA-specific primers were used for amplification, and the corresponding primer sequences are summarized in Supplementary Table 1. Relative transcript abundance was determined by the 2−ΔΔCt approach.20 GAPDH was used as the internal reference for mRNA quantification, whereas U6 was used for miRNA normalization.

Western Blotting

Cell lysates were prepared using Western and IP cell lysis buffer containing protease/phosphatase inhibitors, and protein concentration was determined by the BCA method. Identical amounts of protein were subjected to SDS-PAGE followed by transfer onto PVDF membranes. Following blocking with 5% non-fat milk, the membranes were exposed overnight at 4°C to primary antibodies against p53 (Abcam, Cat# ab33889, 1:5000), PI3K (Abcam, Cat# ab191606, 1:1000), p-PI3K (Affinity, Cat# AF3242, 1:1000), AKT (Abcam, Cat# ab8805, 1:2000), p-AKT (Abcam, Cat# ab38449, 1:1000), mTOR (Abcam, Cat# ab2732, 1:2000), p-mTOR (CST, Cat# 2971, 1:1000), Bax (Abcam, Cat# ab32503, 1:1000), Bcl-2 (Abcam, Cat# ab182858, 1:1000), β-actin (Proteintech, Cat# 81115-1-RR, 1:10,000), CD63 (Abcam, Cat# ab216130, 1:1500), CD81 (Abcam, Cat# ab79559, 1:1500), and TSG101 (Abcam, Cat# ab83, 1:1500). Washed membranes were subsequently incubated with HRP-labeled goat anti-rabbit IgG secondary antibody (BIOSS, Cat# bs-0295G-HRP, 1:3000) for 1 h at room temperature. Immunoreactive bands were detected by an enhanced chemiluminescence substrate (Thermo Fisher, Cat# 32106), and grayscale quantification was carried out with ImageJ (version 1.53, NIH, USA).

Immunofluorescence Detection

Exosomes isolated from parental and PTX-resistant breast cancer cells (S/exo and P/exo) were stained with the lipophilic fluorescent dye Dil (Invitrogen, Cat# V22885) following the supplier’s protocol. Following a 24-h exposure of recipient breast cancer cells (MCF-7 or MDA-MB-231, as appropriate) to Dil-labeled exosomes at 100 μg/mL, the cells were washed three times with PBS and then fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde. Once fixation was completed, DAPI (4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole; Beyotime, Cat# C1002) was used to counterstain nuclei, which enabled subsequent fluorescence imaging. Images were obtained with a Leica DMi8 fluorescence microscope (Leica Microsystems, Germany), while identical microscope settings were maintained throughout image acquisition.

Bioinformatics Analysis for microRNA Screening

Publicly available bioinformatics tools were used to predict microRNAs (miRNAs) potentially targeting TP53. miRWalk and miRDB were queried to identify miRNAs with predicted binding sites within the 3′ untranslated region (3′UTR) of TP53 mRNA, and only the overlapping miRNAs identified by both databases were retained for subsequent analyses. Candidate miRNAs were further prioritized using TCGA-BRCA miRNA expression profiles, published breast cancer literature, and relevance to chemoresistance. Differential expression in TCGA-BRCA was screened using normalized miRNA expression data, with tumor and non-tumor samples compared by standard statistical testing. A value of P < 0.05 was considered significant for candidate prioritization.

Dual-Luciferase Reporter Assay

The interaction between miR-21 and the TP53 3′UTR was examined through a dual-luciferase reporter assay. Wild-type (TP53-WT) and mutant (TP53-MUT) TP53 3′UTR luciferase reporter constructs were introduced into MCF-7 cells together with either miR-21 mimic or mimic negative control by Lipofectamine 3000-mediated transfection (Invitrogen, USA). After 48 h, luciferase activity was determined using the Dual-Glo® Luciferase Assay System (Promega, USA). Relative luciferase activity was calculated as Firefly luciferase activity normalized to Renilla luciferase activity.

Statistical Analysis

IBM SPSS Statistics 23.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) was used for statistical processing. Values are reported as mean ± standard deviation (SD) based on three independent biological experiments (n = 3). When multiple groups were analyzed, differences were assessed by one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) and subsequently subjected to Tukey’s multiple-comparison test, whereas two-group comparisons were examined using Student’s t-test. A value of P < 0.05 was considered indicative of statistical significance. R software (version 4.2.0) was employed for bioinformatics analyses. Differential expression and pathway enrichment analyses were evaluated using standard R packages. Graphs were generated using R and GraphPad Prism.

TEM revealed that both S/exo and P/exo exhibited a characteristic concave morphology with a diameter spanning 30 to 150 nm (Figure 1B). NTA subsequently indicated that the average particle size of S/exo and P/exo was approximately 100–110 nm, with similar distribution profiles and concentrations (Figure 1C). Immunoblotting showed robust levels of classical exosomal proteins CD63, CD81, as well as TSG101 in both exosome groups, confirming the identity and purity of the isolated vesicles (Figure 1D). Moreover, fluorescence imaging demonstrated that MCF-7 cells internalized Dil-labeled exosomes, and quantitative analysis showed the P/exo group exhibited substantially higher fluorescence intensity than the S/exo group (P < 0.001, Figure 1E).

Additionally, NTA-based particle quantification and BCA protein measurements showed that S/exo and P/exo, as well as S/exo-231 and P/exo-231, exhibited comparable particle concentrations, protein concentrations, and particle-to-protein ratios, indicating that exosome preparations used in subsequent functional experiments were normalized and comparable in dosage (Supplementary Figure S1).

PTX-Resistant Cell-Derived Exosomes Enhance Chemoresistance and Attenuate Apoptosis in MCF-7 Cells

To clarify the functional impact of exosomes on PTX sensitivity, MCF-7 cells were exposed to S/exo or P/exo. Compared with the MCF-7 control and S/exo groups, the P/exo-treated group exhibited a lower proportion of TUNEL-positive cells (P < 0.001, Figure 2B). Exosomal miR-21 Contributes to p53 Suppression and PTX Resistance

To identify exosomal microRNAs involved in p53 regulation, TP53-targeting miRNAs were predicted using the miRDB and miRWalk databases, yielding 44 overlapping candidates (Figure 4A). Based on bioinformatic ranking and TCGA-BRCA expression profiles, miR-125b-1, miR-125b-2, and miR-21 were selected for further validation. TCGA analysis showed that miR-125b-1 and miR-125b-2 were significantly downregulated, whereas miR-21 was markedly enhanced in breast cancer tissues (P < 0.001, Figure 4A). miR-221 was included in the initial expression screen but was not pursued further because the experimental exosome validation focused on the three candidates with the clearest relevance to the present model. Discussion

This work demonstrated that exosomes derived from PTX-resistant breast cancer cells can be efficiently internalized by drug-sensitive recipient cells, thereby promoting PTX resistance and attenuating apoptosis-related responses. These functional changes suggest that resistant-cell-derived exosomes may actively participate in reshaping drug responsiveness in recipient breast cancer cells, rather than merely reflecting the resistant phenotype of donor cells. These effects were consistently observed in both hormone receptor-positive (MCF-7) and triple-negative (MDA-MB-231) breast cancer cell models, indicating that the pro-resistance activity of PTX-resistant cell-derived exosomes is reproducible and not restricted to a single cellular context. Furthermore, modulation experiments targeting key regulatory factors demonstrated that the exosome-induced resistant phenotype is reversible under appropriate experimental interventions. Collectively, these observations establish a functional relationship between exosome-mediated intercellular communication and the acquisition of PTX resistance in breast cancer cells. These findings are consistent with the study objective and further support the emerging view that extracellular vesicle-mediated intercellular communication contributes to chemotherapy resistance in breast cancer.

The involvement of exosomes in mediating chemoresistance has gained increasing attention. It has been well established that exosomal cargos, including microRNAs, lncRNAs, and proteins, can reprogram the signaling networks of recipient cells and alter their response to therapy.21,22 Previous reports have shown that tumor-derived exosomes contribute to chemotherapy resistance by activating the PI3K/AKT signaling cascade, but few studies have investigated the upstream regulatory molecules involved in this process.23,24 Our study provides functional evidence that p53 suppression following P/exo exposure is associated with PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway activation and reduced PTX sensitivity in breast cancer cells. This suppression appears to be functionally significant, as the loss of p53 was associated with enhanced p-PI3K, p-AKT, and p-mTOR expression without changes in total protein expression. The findings align with previous studies demonstrating that p53 can inhibit PI3K/AKT signaling through transcriptional activation of PTEN and PHLDA3.25,26 In our model, the reintroduction of p53 effectively diminished phosphorylation levels of the pathway and re-sensitized cells to PTX. Taken together, these results support the involvement of an exosome–p53–PI3K/AKT/mTOR regulatory axis in breast cancer chemoresistance.

Importantly, the functional relevance of this exosome-mediated mechanism was further validated in an independent breast cancer model. The validation in MDA-MB-231 cells strengthens the reproducibility of this regulatory pattern across different breast cancer cell contexts, although it does not establish universality across all molecular subtypes. These findings indicate that the pro-resistance effect of resistant cell-derived exosomes is not restricted to one cell model and may represent a reproducible regulatory pattern across distinct breast cancer cellular contexts. Because MDA-MB-231 cells carry mutant TP53, the results obtained in this cell model should be interpreted as changes in total p53 protein abundance and associated signaling responses, rather than as direct evidence of altered wild-type p53 transcriptional activity.

A major strength of this study is the context-specific integration of exosomal miR-21, p53 suppression, and PI3K/AKT/mTOR activation in PTX-resistant breast cancer cell models. While earlier studies have explored either exosome-driven chemoresistance or PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway activation in isolation, our research bridges these concepts by identifying p53 as a critical intermediary.27 We observed that resistant cell-derived exosomes suppressed p53 expression, and this suppression was accompanied by increased phosphorylation of key PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling proteins. Moreover, by conducting both gain-of-function experiments and pathway analysis, we provide a biologically coherent framework suggesting that exosome-mediated intercellular communication may influence intracellular survival signaling in recipient breast cancer cells.

Our results expand our comprehension of the mechanisms underlying chemoresistance and highlight potential targets for therapeutic intervention.

Notably, further mechanistic interrogation revealed that exosomal miR-21 is an important functional component involved in p53 suppression. Bioinformatic screening combined with experimental validation demonstrated selective enrichment of miR-21 in PTX-resistant cell-derived exosomes, efficient transfer to recipient cells, and functional repression of p53 expression. Inhibition of miR-21 partially restored p53 levels and PTX sensitivity, supporting the functional involvement of miR-21 in the exosome-p53 regulatory axis.

Although this study did not identify the specific exosomal components responsible for p53 downregulation, previous studies have shown that exosomes can deliver oncogenic microRNAs or proteins that directly interfere with TP53 transcription or promote p53 degradation.28 Our findings place miR-21 as a potential upstream contributor within this context, while not excluding the potential contribution of additional exosomal RNAs or proteins. It is therefore reasonable to hypothesize that other p53-modulating miRNAs may act in concert with miR-21, warranting further investigation through transcriptomic or small RNA sequencing analyses of P/exo.

Despite the strengths of our study, certain limitations warrant consideration. To begin with, all experiments were carried out in vitro, and most quantitative analyses relied on three independent biological experiments per group, representing a relatively small sample size. Although MCF-7 and MDA-MB-231 cells were both included to improve reproducibility across distinct breast cancer contexts, it remains uncertain whether similar exosome-mediated regulatory mechanisms operate in additional breast cancer subtypes, animal models, or clinical samples. Therefore, caution is warranted when interpreting the translational significance of the present findings. Second, although miR-21 inhibition and p53 overexpression partially reversed exosome-induced chemoresistance, this study did not include pathway-specific rescue experiments using PI3K/AKT/mTOR inhibitors or related genetic interventions. Thus, PI3K/AKT/mTOR activation should be interpreted as being associated with, rather than definitively responsible for, the resistant phenotype. In addition, although miR-21 was validated as a direct regulator of TP53, other exosomal RNAs or proteins that may also contribute to p53 suppression were not exhaustively characterized. Future studies incorporating in vivo validation, clinical datasets, high-throughput exosomal profiling, and pathway-specific functional interventions are required to further confirm the mechanistic causality and translational significance of the exosomal miR-21/p53/PI3K/AKT/mTOR regulatory axis.

Conclusion

In summary, this study indicates that exosomes derived from PTX-resistant breast cancer cells contribute to reduced PTX sensitivity in recipient breast cancer cells, accompanied by attenuated apoptosis-related responses. This effect was observed in both MCF-7 and MDA-MB-231 breast cancer cell models. Mechanistically, resistant cell-derived exosomes were associated with p53 downregulation and PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway activation, with exosomal miR-21 acting as an important upstream contributor. Inhibition of miR-21 or restoration of p53 partially reversed the resistant phenotype, suggesting that the exosomal miR-21/p53/PI3K/AKT/mTOR regulatory axis may participate in PTX resistance and warrants further validation in in vivo models and clinical settings.
