Polycyclic groups and profinite isomorphism
Dan Segal

Abstract




1
Introduction

There is no algorithm to decide whether an arbitrary finitely presented group
has nontrivial profinite completion, or equivalently, a nontrivial finite
image
[
BW
]
. Within the universe of residually finite groups, however, the
opposite is true: given that the finitely presented group
G
is residually
finite, we note that
G
has a nontrivial finite image if and only if some
generator of
G
is not the identity; and this is decidable by the naive
routine of simultaneously listing (a) finite images and (b) consequences of
the relations. Thus we can decide whether or not the profinite completion

\widehat{G}
is the trivial group.

The
profinite isomorphism problem
asks: given two finitely presented
groups, do they have isomorphic profinite completions? This is of course more
demanding than the ‘profinite triviality problem’, and is undecidable even
within the class of residually finite groups
[
BW2
]
.

It has long been known that among finitely presented residually finite groups,
the polycyclic groups are particularly tractable. Around 40 years ago two
major results were established:

Theorem 1

[
S2
]

The isomorphism problem for virtually polycyclic
groups is decidable.

Theorem 2

[
GPS
]

The (relative) profinite genus of a virtually
polycyclic group is finite.

The former means that there exists a uniform algorithm that decides, given two
finite presentations of virtually polycyclic groups, whether the two groups
are isomorphic. (The fact that the presented groups are virtually polycyclic
has to be
given
: it is in principle undecidable, by the Adian-Rabin
Theorem,
[
LS
]
Chap. 4, §4.)

Theorem
2
asserts that the class of all virtually polycyclic groups
having a given profinite completion consists of finitely many isomorphism
classes. In fact we can remove the word “relative”:
the class of all finitely generated
residually finite groups having the same profinite completion as a given
virtually polycyclic group (consists of virtually polycyclic groups and)
contains only finitely many non-isomorphic groups
. This follows from the
theorem together with the main result of
[
SW
]
, §4.

The point of this note is to fill an obvious gap by establishing

Theorem 3

The profinite isomorphism problem for virtually polycyclic
groups is decidable.

That is, there exists a uniform algorithm that decides, given two finite
presentations of virtually polycyclic groups, whether the two groups have
isomorphic profinite completions.

Two groups have isomorphic profinite completions if and only if they have the
same finite images (‘isomorphism’ of profinite completions is supposed to be

topological
isomorphism; for virtually polycyclic groups this is the
same as abstract isomorphism, cf.
[
S1
]
Chap. 10, Prop. 4). Thus there is
a trivial procedure that will determine the
negation
of profinite
isomorphism: if
\widehat{G}\ncong\widehat{H}
we eventually find

m\in\mathbb{N}
such that
G/G^{m}\ncong H/H^{m}
. However to establish a
positive result is harder. This is the opposite of the situation with the
original isomorphism problem, where an isomorphism, if one exists, will
eventually be found, while non-isomorphism is much harder to establish.

Even given Theorems
1
and
3
, there is no known procedure
to determine the
class number
of a polycyclic group, that is the
cardinality of its profinite genus. This number is related, in a messy way, to
the class number of a certain algebraic group (cf.
[
S1
]
Chapters 9, 10
and
[
PR
]
Chapter 8, where the authors write “…
computation of class number is a difficult, even hopeless, problem in its most
general formulation” ). It is tempting to guess that at
least one of the three algorithmic problems – ‘isomorphism’, ‘profinite
isomorphism’, ‘class number’ – should be reducible to the conjunction of the
other two; but I don’t see it.

A guiding principle in number theory is that ‘local’ problems are supposed to
be easier than ‘global’ problems. To prove Theorem
3
, we use the
methodology of Theorems
1
and
2
to reduce the question to a

Diophantine problem
; the result then follows by the following very
general result of James Ax. Let us call a finite family of polynomial
equations in finitely many variables with coefficients in
\mathbb{Z}
a

Diophantine system
. The system is
locally solvable
if it is
solvable over
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
for every prime
p
.

Theorem 4

[
A
]

The local solvability of a given Diophantine system
is algorithmically decidable.

Ax remarks on the contrast between this fact and the (now known)

unsolvability
of Hilbert’s 10th problem. The possibility of proving
Theorem
1
rested on the reduction of that question not to any old
Diophantine problem, but to one of a very special kind: namely an orbit
problem for an arithmetic group (see
[
S1
]
Chapter 8, §D). From this
point of view, Theorem
3
should be seen as the ‘easy cousin’ of
Theorem
1
: apart from the group theory (and, to be fair, some
nontrivial number theory coming from the proof of Theorem
2
), it
only needs Theorem
4
.

The special case of all three theorems
1
-
3
relating to

\mathcal{T}
-groups – torsion-free finitely generated nilpotent groups – is
discussed in Section 1 of
[
GS3
]
; in that context, the group theory
question translates quickly to a corresponding arithmetical question via the
Lie algebra. The analogous reduction in the general polycyclic case is harder;
but much of the hard work has essentially been done, in the course of
establishing Theorems
1
and
2
.

Both of those theorems proceed by first embedding a virtually polycyclic group
into
\mathrm{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{Z})
in a particularly rigid way: the question
of isomorphism between abstract groups is thereby translated into one of
conjugacy between subgroups of a linear group. The same method could be
adapted to prove Theorem
3
, but the flexibility provided by
Theorem
4
allows us to specify an isomorphism more directly.

2
Strategy of the proof

In general, I use the conventions of
[
S2
]
: a group
G
is said to be

given
if a finite presentation of
G
is given; an element of
G
is
given if it is given as a word in the given generators, and a subgroup is
given if it is specified by finitely many generators, each of which is given.

For some frequently used notation see the end of this section.

Certain polycyclic groups are particularly easy to specify; they are called

splittable,
and defined in

[
S1
]
Chapter 7, §B. Details
will be recalled in §
4.2
. The splittable group

is called

lattice-splittable
if its Fitting subgroup
N=\mathrm{Fit}(G)
is a
lattice
\mathcal{T}
-group, that is, the subset
\log N
in the Lie algebra
associated to
N
is closed under addition (
cf.
[
S1
]
Chapter 10, §D).

The first result is a mild variation on material in
[
S1
]
Chapter 10, §E.

Proposition 2.1

There is an effective procedure that does the following:
given virtually polycyclic groups
G
and

, it finds

\widehat{G}\rightarrow\widehat{G^{{\dagger}}}

\overline{G_{0}}

(1)

\widehat{\mathbb{Z}}

\widehat{\mathbb{Z}}

\widehat{\mathbb{Z}}
.

Keeping the above notation, set
\theta_{1}=\Theta|_{\overline{N}}
, so

\theta_{1}:\overline{N}\rightarrow\overline{N^{{\dagger}}}
is an isomorphism (see
§
3
). Let
\theta_{2}:G/N\rightarrow G^{{\dagger}}/N^{{\dagger}}
be
induced from
\Theta
via

G/N\cong\overline{N}G/\overline{N}\rightarrow\overline{N^{{\dagger}}}G^{{\dagger}}/\overline{N^{{\dagger}}}\cong G^{{\dagger}}/N^{{\dagger}}

(noting that
G\cap\overline{N}=N
etc.). Let

be a finite presentation for
G,
so
R
is a finite
set of words

such that
w(g_{1},\ldots,g_{d})=1
. Say

Ng_{i}\theta_{2}=N^{{\dagger}}h_{i}~(i=1,\ldots,d)

(2)

where each
h_{i}\in G^{{\dagger}}
. For
w\in R
we then have

b_{w}:=w(h_{1},\ldots,h_{d})\in N^{{\dagger}}.

There exist
v_{i}\in\overline{N^{{\dagger}}}
such that

g_{i}\Theta=v_{i}h_{i}~(i=1,\ldots,d).

Now for any word

w(v_{1}h_{1},\ldots,v_{d}h_{d})=w_{\mathbf{h}}^{\prime}(v_{1},\ldots,v_{d}).w(h_{1},\ldots,h_{d}),

where the ‘derived word’
w_{\mathbf{h}}^{\prime}
is a ‘twisted word’, in
which each occurrence of
v_{i}
in

is replaced by a term of the form

v_{i}^{x}
where
x
is a product of some of the

(
[

]

§1.1). As
\Theta
is a homomorphism we see that

w\in R\Longrightarrow w_{\mathbf{h}}^{\prime}(v_{1},\ldots,v_{d})b_{w}=1.

(3)

Let
\{a_{1},\ldots,a_{s}\}
be a generating set for
N
. Then
a_{i}=u_{i}(\mathbf{g})
for certain words
u_{i}
. Set
c_{i}=u_{i}(h_{1},\ldots,h_{d})
, an element of
N^{{\dagger}}
, so we have
a_{i}\Theta=u_{i\mathbf{h}}^{\prime}(v_{1},\ldots,v_{d}).c_{i}
. Thus

a_{i}\theta_{1}=u_{i\mathbf{h}}^{\prime}(v_{1},\ldots,v_{d}).c_{i}~~~(i=1,\ldots,d).

(4)

Conversely, suppose we are given isomorphisms
\theta_{2}:G/N\rightarrow G^{{\dagger}}/N^{{\dagger}},

\theta_{1}:\overline{N}\rightarrow\overline{N^{{\dagger}}}
.
Choose the
h_{i}
to satisfy (
2
). Now
\left\langle g_{1},\ldots g_{d};~R\right\rangle
is a presentation for
\widehat{G}
in the category of

3
) holds then the mapping

extends to a homomorphism

\Theta:\widehat{G}\rightarrow\widehat{G^{{\dagger}}}
, which induces
\theta_{2}:G/N\rightarrow G^{{\dagger}}/N^{{\dagger}}
.

If in addition (
4
) holds, then
a_{i}\Theta=a_{i}\theta_{1}

for each

As

\overline{N}

\Theta
restricts to
\theta_{1}
on

\overline{N}
. As
\theta_{2}
and

We have established

Proposition 2.3

The following are equivalent:

(i)

\widehat{G}\cong\widehat{G^{{\dagger}}}

(ii)

There exist isomorphisms
\theta_{2}:G/N\rightarrow G^{{\dagger}}/N^{{\dagger}},

\theta_{1}:\overline{N}\rightarrow\overline{N^{{\dagger}}}
and
elements
v_{1},\ldots,v_{d}\in\overline{N^{{\dagger}}}
such that given
(
2
), both (
3
) and (
4
) are satisfied.

Now isomorphisms between
\mathcal{T}
-groups, or their profinite completions,
correspond to isomorphisms between their associated Lie algebras. This means
that condition
(ii)
can be expressed in terms of linear algebra, and
doing this in an explicit manner gives

Proposition 2.4

There is an algorithm that does the following: given

•

virtually polycyclic groups
G
,
~G^{{\dagger}}

•

\mathcal{T}
-subgroups
N\vartriangleleft G
,
N^{{\dagger}}\vartriangleleft G^{{\dagger}}
such that every isomorphism
\widehat{G}\rightarrow\widehat{G^{{\dagger}}}
sends
\overline{N}
onto
\overline{N^{{\dagger}}}

•

an isomorphism
\theta:G/N\rightarrow G^{{\dagger}}/N^{{\dagger}}

it constructs a Diophantine system
\mathcal{F(\theta})
that is locally
solvable if and only if condition
(ii)
is satisfied with
\theta_{2}=\theta
.

For details of the proof see §
5
.

Proof of Theorem
3

Given
G
and
G^{{\dagger}}
we have to decide whether or not
\widehat{G}\cong\widehat{G^{{\dagger}}}
. Two procedures are run simultaneously:

Procedure 1
: Enumerate the finite quotients
G/G^{m},~G^{{\dagger}}/G^{{\dagger}m}
(cf.
[
BCRS
]
, Proposition 2.10). For each
m
decide whether or not

G/G^{m}\cong G^{{\dagger}}/G^{{\dagger}m}
. When the answer is ‘no’, stop.

Procedure 2:
Enumerate isomorphisms
\theta:G/N\rightarrow G^{{\dagger}}/N^{{\dagger}}
(cf.
[
BCRS
]
, Proposition 2.2, Lemma 3.8). For each one,
construct the system
\mathcal{F(\theta})
by Proposition
2.4
and
decide (by Theorem
4
) whether the Diophantine system
\mathcal{F(\theta})
is locally solvable. When the answer is ‘yes’, stop.

The first procedure stops if and only if
\widehat{G}\ncong\widehat{G^{{\dagger}}};
the second stops if and only if
\widehat{G}\cong\widehat{G^{{\dagger}}}
. Thus
the profinite isomorphism of
G
and
G^{{\dagger}}
is decidable.

Technicalities

3.1
Profinite completions

The passage from a virtually polycyclic group
G
to its profinite completion

\widehat{G}
is very well behaved. Write
\overline{X}
to denote the closure
in
\widehat{G}
of a subset
X
. For a subgroup
A
and a normal subgroup
N

of
G
denote by
\pi:\widehat{G}\rightarrow\widehat{G/N}
and

the morphisms induced by the quotient map

G\rightarrow G/N
and the inclusion
A\hookrightarrow G
. Let
B\leq G
. Then

\displaystyle\widehat{G}/\overline{N}

\displaystyle\cong\widehat{G/N}\text{ induced by }\pi

\displaystyle\widehat{A}

\displaystyle\cong\overline{A}\text{ induced by }i

\displaystyle\overline{A}\cap G

\displaystyle=A

\displaystyle\overline{A}\cap\overline{B}

\displaystyle=\overline{A\cap B},~~\mathrm{C}_{\overline{B}}(\overline{A})=\overline{\mathrm{C}_{B}(A)},~~\mathrm{N}_{\overline{B}}(\overline{A})=\overline{\mathrm{N}_{B}(A)}~

(5)

\displaystyle\mathrm{Fit}(\widehat{G})

\displaystyle=\overline{\mathrm{Fit}(G)}

(6)

\displaystyle\widehat{G^{m}}

\displaystyle=\widehat{G}^{m}~~(m\in\mathbb{N})

(7)

\displaystyle\overline{A}\overset{\widehat{G}}{\sim}\overline{B}

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow A\overset{G}{\sim}B

where

means ‘is conjugate in
G

\mathrm{Fit}(G)

denotes the Fitting subgroup of

.

The first claim is a general fact. For the second claim see
[
S1
]

Chap 1, Ex. 11. (

) is
established in
[

]

) (Pickel’s theorem)
see

Recall that

\mathrm{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{Z)}<\mathrm{GL}_{n}^{\infty}=\mathrm{GL}_{n}(\widehat{\mathbb{Z}})=\prod\limits_{p}\mathrm{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{Z}_{p}).

\mathrm{GL}_{n}^{\infty}
is endowed with the congruence topology. If

G<\mathrm{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{Z)}
is virtually polycyclic, the embedding

G\rightarrow\overline{G}
induces an isomorphism
\widehat{G}\rightarrow\overline{G}
(
[
S1
]
Chap. 10, Prop. 5). This is a consequence of

[
S1
]

is
induced by the congruence topology on
\mathrm{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{Z)}
, and that

G
is closed in the latter topology (Formanek’s Theorem). As group
homomorphisms are continuous in the profinite topology, it follows that any

, extends uniquely to a continuous homomorphism
\overline{G}\rightarrow\overline{H}
.

We shall often use these facts without special mention. Many more or less
elementary algorithms for working with virtually polycyclic groups are
described in
[
BCRS
]
; these will also sometimes be used implicitly.

3.2
\mathcal{T}
-groups

Let
N
be a
\mathcal{T}
-group. The ‘canonical embedding’
\beta_{N}
of a

[

]
, identifies
N
with a subgroup of

\mathrm{U}_{n}(\mathbb{Z})
in such a way that every automorphism of

is
induced by conjugation with a matrix in
\mathrm{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{Z})
,

. Specifically,
\beta_{N}
gives the action of

for a certain characteristic ideal

of the group ring
\mathbb{Z}N
,
where

acts by right multiplication on
\mathbb{Z}N
. Here

is an

N,
in principle easily determined from the structure of
N

(
[
S1
]
Chapter 5 §B). We often identify
N
with
N\beta_{N}
.

We extend
\beta_{N}
to
\mathrm{Hol}(N)
by making automorphisms of
N

in the natural way on

.

Lemma 3.1

Let
\Gamma=\mathrm{Aut}(N)\beta_{N}
and write

\widetilde{\Gamma}
for the Zariski closure of
\Gamma
in
\mathrm{GL}_{n}
.
Then
\mathrm{C}_{\widetilde{\Gamma}}(N\beta_{N})=1
.

Proof.
The action of
\mathrm{Aut}(N)
on

fixes the element
1^{\sharp}=1+I_{N},
so
\widetilde{\Gamma}
is contained in the stabilizer of

1^{\sharp}
. The result follows since the
N

span

V(N)
.

There are mutually inverse polynomial mapppings

\displaystyle\log

\displaystyle:\mathrm{U}_{n}(\mathbb{Q})\rightarrow\mathfrak{u}_{n}(\mathbb{Q})

\displaystyle\exp

\displaystyle:\mathfrak{u}_{n}(\mathbb{Q})\rightarrow\mathrm{U}_{n}(\mathbb{Q}),

and

\mathcal{L}_{\mathbb{Q}}(N):=\mathbb{Q}\log N

\mathfrak{u}_{n}(\mathbb{Q})
. The Mal’cev completion

of

\exp\mathcal{L}_{\mathbb{Q}}(N)

\log

The subset

is not necessarily
closed under addition; if it happens to be so,
N
is said to be a

, and then
\log N
is indeed a full lattice in

\mathcal{L}_{\mathbb{Q}}(N)
.

An automorphism
\alpha
of
N
induces a Lie algebra automorphism, also
denoted
\alpha
, of
\mathcal{L}_{\mathbb{Q}}(N)
. This has a multiplicative
Jordan decomposition

\alpha=\alpha_{u}\cdot\alpha_{s}=\alpha_{s}\cdot\alpha_{u}

\alpha_{u}

\alpha_{s}

[

\alpha_{u}
and

; these may or may not

The automorphism
\alpha
of
N

\alpha=\alpha_{s}
.

Lemma 3.2

Let
\alpha
be an automorphism of
N
. Then

(\alpha\beta_{N})_{u}=\alpha_{u}\beta_{N},~(\alpha\beta_{N})_{s}=\alpha_{s}\beta_{N}.

Proof.
Write
\beta=\beta_{N}
and
L=\mathbb{Q}\log(N\beta)\subseteq\mathfrak{u}_{n}(\mathbb{Q})
. Let
\widetilde{\Gamma}
be as in Lemma
3.1
;
this is contained in the stabilizer of
L
in the conjugation action of

\mathrm{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{Q})
. Now
\alpha\beta\in\widetilde{\Gamma}
so also

(\alpha\beta)_{s},~(\alpha\beta)_{u}\in\widetilde{\Gamma}
(the Jordan

\alpha\beta
as an element of
\mathrm{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{Q})
).
As the conjugation action on
L
is rational, it follows that
(\alpha\beta)_{s}
and
(\alpha\beta)_{u}
act on
L
as they should, i.e.
semisimply, unipotently respectively (
[

]

the other hand,
\alpha_{s}\beta
and
\alpha_{u}\beta
act semisimply,

(\alpha\beta)_{s}(\alpha\beta)_{u}=\alpha\beta=(\alpha_{s}\beta)(\alpha_{u}\beta),

so
(\alpha_{s}\beta)^{-1}(\alpha\beta)_{s}=(\alpha_{u}\beta)(\alpha\beta)_{u}^{-1}

. The
result now follows by Lemma

.

4.4

Now we prove Proposition
