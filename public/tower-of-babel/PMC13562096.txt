Respiratory effects of recruitment maneuvers according to lung recruitability assessed by electrical impedance tomography in patients with acute respiratory distress syndrome
Authors: Dinh Van Trung, Bui Thi Huong Giang, Dang Quoc Tuan, Nguyen Cong Tan, Pham The Thach, Bui Van Cuong, Nguyen Ba Cuong, Trinh The Anh, Nguyen Dang Thu, Do Ngoc Son
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Recruitment maneuvers (RM) can improve oxygenation in patients with acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS), but their physiological effects depend on lung recruitability. This secondary analysis of a randomized controlled trial (RCT) evaluated oxygenation, respiratory mechanics, regional ventilation, and cardiorespiratory adverse events responses to a RM followed by electrical impedance tomography (EIT)-guided PEEP titration, using EIT to assess lung recruitability. In this study, fifty patients with moderate-to-severe ARDS underwent a stepwise RM followed by individualized PEEP titration guided by EIT. Lung recruitability was determined using the collapse index at PEEP 6 cmH₂O (CLPEEP6), defined as the proportion of collapsed lung at this PEEP level. Patients were classified into high- and low-recruitability groups based on median CLPEEP6 values. Oxygenation (PaO₂/FiO₂), static compliance (Cstat), driving pressure (Pdriv), regional ventilation distribution, and cardiorespiratory adverse events were compared before and after RM, during subsequent individualized EIT-guided PEEP titration. In patients with high recruitability (CLPEEP6 > 12.5), the PaO₂/FiO₂ ratio and Cstat increased significantly after RM (PaO₂/FiO₂: 100.8 ± 30.3 vs. 125.4 ± 38.3 mmHg, p < 0.05; Cstat: 21.5 ± 5.8 vs. 28.0 ± 7.0 mL/cmH₂O, p < 0.001), Pdriv decreased (19.1 ± 3.5 vs. 15.6 ± 3.2 cmH₂O, p < 0.001). EIT demonstrated a posterior redistribution of ventilation after RM. In contrast, patients with low recruitability (CLPEEP6 ≤ 12.5) showed no significant mechanical or oxygenation improvement and transient hypotension, arrhythmia, and desaturation appeared numerically more common in this group. No barotrauma or cardiac arrest occurred, and ICU mortality was similar between groups. A strategy combining a RM with subsequent individualized EIT-guided PEEP titration was associated with improved oxygenation and lung mechanics in patients with high lung recruitability, whereas patients with low recruitability showed limited physiological benefit, with cardiorespiratory adverse events appearing numerically more frequent. Introduction

Acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) is characterised by acute, diffuse inflammatory lung injury resulting from a variety of direct and indirect insults. The syndrome is characterized by increased alveolar–capillary permeability, pulmonary edema, loss of aerated lung units, severe hypoxemia, and reduced respiratory system compliance. Despite advances in critical care, ARDS remains a major contributor to morbidity and mortality in intensive care units worldwide [1]. The introduction of lung-protective ventilation, particularly low tidal volume strategies, together with the appropriate application of positive end-expiratory pressure (PEEP) and adjunctive therapies such as prone positioning and, in selected patients, neuromuscular blocker, has improved outcomes; however, mortality remains substantial [2–4].

Recruitment maneuvers (RMs) aim to transiently increase transpulmonary pressure in order to reopen collapsed alveoli, increase aerated lung volume, improve respiratory system compliance, and reduce intrapulmonary shunt. Higher PEEP strategies are intended to maintain alveolar stability and prevent derecruitment after lung units have been reopened. Current ARDS guidelines do not recommend routine use of recruitment maneuvers; higher PEEP strategies may be considered in patients with moderate to severe ARDS [5]. Responses to RMs, and to higher levels of PEEP more generally, are highly variable in patients with ARDS. While patients with a high potential for lung recruitability may derive physiological benefit, others may be exposed to alveolar overdistension and hemodynamic impairment. Consequently, the identification of patients who are likely to benefit from RMs remains challenging. Randomised trials and meta-analyses evaluating higher-PEEP strategies and routine recruitment manoeuvres have reported inconsistent results, highlighting the need for reliable bedside tools to guide individualised application of these interventions [6].

Electrical impedance tomography (EIT) is a non-invasive bedside imaging technique that allows continuous assessment of regional lung aeration. EIT has been used to estimate recruitable lung collapse and regional overdistension during PEEP titration. Early studies demonstrated the feasibility of quantifying alveolar recruitment and hyperdistension using EIT at the bedside [7]. More recently, the multicentre RECRUIT study proposed a standardized approach to lung recruitability, quantified by changes in lung collapse in response to increasing PEEP [8]. In the present study, we employed the collapse index at PEEP 6 cmH₂O (CLPEEP6) as a practical bedside indicator of lung recruitability.

The objective of this study was to determine whether CLPEEP6 can stratify ARDS patients according to oxygenation, respiratory mechanics, regional ventilation distribution, and cardiorespiratory adverse events in response to a strategy combining a RM with subsequent individualized EIT-guided PEEP titration.

Methods

This study is a secondary analysis of a single-center randomized trial conducted at Bach Mai Hospital, Vietnam, between 2024 and 2025, as described in detail in the primary publication [9]. The protocol was approved by the Bach Mai Hospital Ethics Committee (5532/BM-HDDD), and written informed consent was obtained from all patients. The trial adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki. It was registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT06733168; registered on December 13, 2024).

Patient population

Adult mechanically ventilated patients with moderate-to-severe ARDS (PaO₂/FiO₂ ≤ 200 mmHg) enrolled in the original randomized trial were included [9]. Exclusion criteria and patient selection followed those of the primary study, with no additional criteria applied for this secondary analysis.

Data collection

Baseline demographic and clinical data were collected, including age, sex, body mass index (BMI), and comorbidities. The primary outcome was the change in oxygenation (PaO₂/FiO₂) after the RM during subsequent individualized EIT-guided PEEP titration according to lung recruitability. Secondary outcomes included respiratory mechanics, including plateau pressure (Pplat), driving pressure (Pdriv), and static compliance (Cstat), and regional ventilation distribution assessed by electrical impedance tomography, including the proportion of tidal ventilation in the anterior (TVa) and posterior (TVp) lung regions, the anterior to posterior ventilation ratio (A/P ratio), and the lung recruitability index (CLPEEP6), as well as cardiorespiratory adverse events elated to the study procedure and clinical outcomes during ICU stay. Adverse pulmonary events included barotrauma (pneumothorax, pneumomediastinum, or subcutaneous emphysema) and desaturation (SpO₂ < 85%). Cardiovascular events included hypotension or hypertension defined as a change in mean arterial pressure (MAP) > 15 mmHg from baseline, any arrhythmia during monitoring, and cardiac arrest. These events were monitored before, during, and within one hour after the recruitment maneuver.

Definition of recruitability

Lung recruitability was assessed using the collapse index at PEEP 6 cmH₂O (CLPEEP6), derived from EIT after a RM and during the decremental PEEP trial, according to the method described by Costa et al. [7]. In this approach, compliance pixel is calculated at each PEEP step, and the highest value observed for each pixel throughout the entire titration is defined as its “best compliance.” Collapsepixel at a given PEEP level is then computed as the relative loss of compliance pixel compared with this reference. Accordingly, CLPEEP6 represents the cumulative relative loss of compliance pixel at the final low-PEEP step of the decremental trial. This approach is consistent with the recruitability concept proposed in the RECRUIT study [8]. Patients were stratified into low and high lung recruitability groups using the median CLPEEP6 value of the study population as the cut-off.

Recruitment maneuvers and individualized EIT-guided PEEP titration protocol:

Recruitment maneuvers and individualized EIT-guided PEEP titration were performed using a standardized, electrical impedance tomography–guided protocol identical to that described in the primary randomized trial [9]. Regional lung ventilation was continuously monitored using an EIT device (PulmoVista 500, Dräger Medical, Lübeck, Germany). Briefly, patients underwent a stepwise increase in PEEP under pressure-controlled ventilation, followed by a decremental PEEP trial to determine recommended PEEP based on EIT-derived indices of lung overdistension and collapse [7]. Once the recommended PEEP was identified, a final recruitment maneuver was performed, and PEEP was set at the recommended level. Recruitment maneuvers were discontinued in the presence of predefined safety criteria, including hemodynamic instability or severe hypoxemia. Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software (version 22.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Descriptive statistics are presented as counts with percentages for categorical variables. Continuous variables are summarized as means with standard deviations or as medians with interquartile ranges, as appropriate based on data distribution. Comparisons between subgroups were conducted using Student’s t-test for normally distributed variables and non-parametric methods for variables with skewed distributions. Changes in continuous variables before and after the recruitment maneuver and individualized EIT-guided PEEP titration were evaluated using paired statistical tests. Cardiorespiratory adverse events were reported descriptively because the study was not powered to detect differences in these infrequent events. All statistical tests were two-tailed, and a p-value of < 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance.

Discussion

We conducted a secondary analysis of a randomized controlled trial to evaluate oxygenation, lung mechanics, regional ventilation, and cardiorespiratory adverse events associated with a strategy combining RM and subsequent individualized EIT-guided PEEP titration according to lung recruitability in patients with ARDS. Our findings demonstrated that the PaO₂/FiO₂ ratio increased significantly after recruitment maneuver in patients with high lung recruitability, accompanied by a marked improvement in static compliance and a reduction in driving pressure, indicating effective alveolar recruitment and improved respiratory mechanics. In this group, plateau pressure remained relatively unchanged while driving pressure decreased and static compliance increased. This pattern is consistent with the mathematical relationship between these variables, as driving pressure is defined as the difference between plateau pressure and PEEP, and static compliance is calculated as tidal volume divided by driving pressure. The higher PEEP levels applied in patients with greater recruitability likely contributed to the reduction in driving pressure and the corresponding increase in static compliance. In contrast, patients with low lung recruitability showed minimal changes in both oxygenation and compliance, suggesting limited alveolar reopening and a potential risk of overdistension. Accordingly, the physiological effects observed within one hour should be interpreted as the combined effect of recruitment and subsequent individualized EIT-guided PEEP titration rather than the isolated effect of the recruitment maneuver alone.

Electrical impedance tomography provided valuable insight into the regional distribution of ventilation, demonstrating a posterior shift of tidal ventilation after RM in highly recruitable patients, consistent with reopening of dependent regions and improved aeration homogeneity. In contrast, no redistribution occurred in patients with low recruitability, reflecting poor response to elevated airway pressure. The EIT-guided optimal PEEP was significantly higher in the high-recruitability group (median 12 cmH₂O) compared with the low-recruitability group (median 8 cmH₂O), reinforcing the physiologic rationale for individualized PEEP titration based on recruitability indices. These findings are consistent with the mechanical “rationality” principle described by Amato [10], in which effective alveolar recruitment decreases driving pressure and reduces the strain on the so-called “baby lung”.

Our findings are consistent with previous physiological studies demonstrating that the effectiveness of recruitment maneuvers largely depends on the amount of recruitable lung tissue rather than on ARDS severity alone [11, 12]. In patients with high lung recruitability, a high CLPEEP6 reflects a greater proportion of potentially reopenable alveoli at low PEEP. These patients usually benefit from higher optimal PEEP levels that can maintain alveolar stability after recruitment, thereby preventing derecruitment during tidal ventilation. Adequately titrated PEEP keeps the newly opened alveoli aerated, improves ventilation–perfusion matching, and reduces intrapulmonary shunt, leading to a sustained increase in PaO₂/FiO₂ [13]. Mauri demonstrated that personalized PEEP guided by recruitability assessment can improve lung aeration distribution and oxygenation without inducing overdistension, highlighting the role of quantitative imaging and physiological indices in tailoring mechanical ventilation strategies [13]. Similarly, Cressoni and Constantin reported that recruitment potential varies widely among ARDS patients and that excessive airway pressures in poorly recruitable lungs provide minimal oxygenation benefit while increasing the risk of overdistension and hemodynamic compromise [14, 15]. Furthermore, in low-recruitability lungs, such pressure elevations may merely overdistended aerated regions without providing a substantial recruitment benefit, thereby predisposing to hemodynamic compromise and ventilator-induced lung injury [16].

The present analysis also showed that cardiorespiratory adverse events, particularly hypotension, arrhythmia, and desaturation, were numerically more frequent in patients with low recruitability. Because of the small number of events and the exploratory nature of this secondary analysis, no formal statistical comparison was performed and these findings should be considered descriptive. These adverse events reflect reduced cardiovascular tolerance to elevated intrathoracic pressure, as venous return and right ventricular preload decrease while right ventricular afterload rises, leading to transient reductions in cardiac output. This observation aligns with findings from the PHARLAP and ART trials, which reported higher rates of hypotension and vasopressor use during RM, particularly among patients with limited recruitable volume [17, 18]. Nevertheless, no barotrauma or cardiac arrest occurred in our study, and ICU mortality was comparable between groups, confirming that RM remains safe and feasible when guided by EIT and close hemodynamic monitoring.

The RECRUIT study by Jonkman provided an important framework for quantifying lung recruitability using EIT-derived collapse indices. In that trial, recruitability was defined as the absolute reduction in the percentage of collapse between PEEP 6 cmH₂O and 24 cmH₂O (ΔCollapse₂₄–₆), requiring a complete decremental PEEP trial [8]. EIT-derived collapse is calculated from relative changes in pixel-level compliance during a decremental PEEP trial, using the maximal compliance observed for each pixel during the whole titration as the reference. Within this framework, cumulative collapse increases progressively as PEEP is reduced and reaches its maximum at the final low-PEEP step. Because our protocol used the same post-recruitment decremental PEEP titration, with a highest PEEP of 25 cmH₂O rather than 24 cmH₂O, CLPEEP6 represents the final cumulative collapse derived from the same physiological and computational principle rather than a fundamentally different definition of recruitability. Our findings confirm that patients with higher CLPEEP6 values exhibited greater improvements in oxygenation and compliance after maneuver, consistent with Jonkman’s observations that ΔCollapse₂₄–₆ correlates strongly with recruited lung volume and improvement in oxygenation [8].

This integration of EIT-based assessment with mechanical response metrics (PaO₂/FiO₂, Pdriv, Cstat) provides a comprehensive physiological framework for stratifying RM responders at the bedside. The correlation between high CLPEEP6 and favorable RM response supports the potential usefulness of EIT as a practical, noninvasive bedside tool for physiological stratification and individualized ventilatory adjustment. Beyond its diagnostic role, EIT-guided PEEP titration allows continuous monitoring of regional overdistension and collapse, offering dynamic feedback for tailoring ventilatory support in real time [19, 20].

From a mechanistic and clinical standpoint, recruitment maneuvers in highly recruitable lungs not only enhance oxygenation but also promote more homogeneous ventilation, thereby reducing regional stress and strain heterogeneity—a central factor in ventilator-induced lung injury (VILI) [21, 22]. The observed decrease in the anterior-to-posterior ventilation ratio in our high-recruitability group suggests improved tidal volume distribution and a reduction in regional overdistension and cyclic alveolar collapse, consistent with prior evidence that homogeneous lung inflation mitigates epithelial injury and inflammatory responses [12, 23]. Conversely, in low-recruitability lungs, excessive airway pressures predominantly strain already aerated regions without meaningful recruitment, increasing mechanical power dissipation and microvascular damage [14, 24]. Clinically, the reduction of VILI through improved homogeneity has been associated with lower mortality, shorter duration of mechanical ventilation, more ventilator-free days, and reduced multiorgan dysfunction [10, 25], reinforcing the importance of assessing lung recruitability to guide individualized PEEP titration and optimize oxygenation.

Study limitations

This study has several limitations. First, being a secondary analysis, it was not originally designed to evaluate subgroup differences by recruitability, and residual confounding cannot be excluded. Second, the relatively small sample size limits external generalizability. Third, because physiological data were collected within one hour after the recruitment maneuver during subsequent individualized EIT-guided PEEP titration, the observed effects should be interpreted as reflecting the combined effects of recruitment and PEEP optimization rather than the isolated effect of the recruitment maneuver alone. Fourth, direct measurement of cardiac output or intrapulmonary shunt was not performed, and the proposed mechanisms remain physiologic inferences. Fifth, only one maneuver protocol was used; different pressure levels or durations could yield distinct effects on lung recruitment and hemodynamics. Finally, long-term outcomes such as ventilator-free days and survival beyond ICU discharge were not assessed.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this secondary analysis supports a stratified, physiology-guided approach to a ventilation strategy combining recruitment maneuver and individualized EIT-guided PEEP titration in ARDS. Patients with high lung recruitability, identified by CLPEEP6, showed greater improvement in oxygenation, respiratory mechanics, and ventilation distribution, whereas those with low recruitability exhibited limited physiological response and a numerically higher occurrence of cardiorespiratory adverse events. These findings suggest that EIT-derived CLPEEP6 may be useful for bedside physiological stratification and for tailoring ventilatory strategies, although confirmation in larger prospective studies is needed.
