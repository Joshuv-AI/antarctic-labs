Derivation of effective kinetic equations describing oscillations in viscoelasticity and in compressible Navier-Stokes
Athanasios E. Tzavaras

Abstract
. These lecture notes are devoted to solutions of hyperbolic-parabolic systems with persistent oscillations. We consider two examples both from mechanics: (i) The system of viscoelasticity of Kelvin-Voigt type with strain energies involving double well potentials, as employed in phase transitions. (ii) The compressible Navier-Stokes equations for a barotropic gas. For each system we construct solutions with persistent oscillations. In a later part we consider the nonlinear homogenization problem. For the systems of viscoelasticity in one-space dimension in Lagrangian coordinates, and for the compressible Navier-Stokes system for barotropic fluids we show how ideas from the kinetic formulation of conservation laws can be used to derive effective equations. The effective equation consists by a kinetic equation coupled with the macroscopic flow.



1.
Introduction

Solutions of Partial Differential Equations with oscillatory structures appear in several problems including
homogenization, turbulence and phase transitions. The origin and provenance of oscillations might well differ in distinct contexts
and is not at present well understood from a mathematical perspective even in the most prominent application of
turbulence. In this review we summarize certain mathematical results on the effects of regularizing mechanisms
on the persistence of oscillations and the related problem of homogenization. To a large extent our perspective is
focussed on problems of phase transitions, be that in solids or in liquids, and manifested through the lack of
convexity in the strain energy or the internal energy function.

The effectiveness of dissipation on smoothing of discontinuities was proposed by Dafermos
[
7
]
as
a way of classifying hyperbolic-parabolic systems of thermomechanics and was developed in a set
of unpublished Lecture Notes
[
8
]
. The situation ranges from the full absence of dissipative
mechanisms leading to formation of shocks, to full parabolic systems that instantaneously regularize solutions,
with hyperbolic-parabolic systems in the middle with some fields regularizing while others develop singularities.
The Green function for the linearized operator around a constant state was
demonstrated, in the penetrating studies of Liu and Zeng
[
29
,
30
]
, to be
an object capturing the efficacy of dissipation around shocks.

The situation is less understood regarding propagation of oscillations.
It is well-known that linear hyperbolic systems propagate
oscillations from the initial-data to solutions, and propagation and/or cancellations of oscillations results as
a consequence of nonlinear response captured by entropy inequalities.
On the other extreme, fully-parabolic systems instantaneously smoothen initial oscillations.
Less was known for the intermediate case of hyperbolic-parabolic systems.
Examples were recently produced in
[
39
]
and will be extensively reviewed here both for the linear case
but also nonlinear cases of hyperbolic-elliptic systems with partial dissipation.

The program will be developed using as paradigms two canonical systems.
The Cauchy problem for nonlinear viscoelastic materials of Kelvin-Voigt type

(1.1)

\displaystyle\partial_{tt}y-\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\Big(\frac{\partial W}{\partial F}(\nabla y)\Big)=\Delta\partial_{t}y\,,

\displaystyle y|_{t=0}=y_{0},\quad\partial_{t}y|_{t=0}=v_{0}\,.

Introducing the velocity
v=\partial_{t}y
and the deformation gradient
F=\nabla y
, the second order evolution (
1.1
)
is expressed as a hyperbolic-parabolic system

(1.2)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}v-\mathop{\mathrm{div}}(S(F))

\displaystyle=\Delta\,v

\displaystyle\partial_{t}F-\nabla\,v

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathrm{curl}}\,F

\displaystyle=0\,.

The second system of interest is the compressible Navier-Stokes system for a barotropic fluid in Eulerian coordinates,

(1.3)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\rho+\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\rho u

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\rho u+\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\rho u\otimes u+\nabla p(\rho)

\displaystyle=\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\Big(\mu(\nabla u+\nabla u^{T})+\lambda(\mathop{\mathrm{div}}u)\,I\,\Big)\,,

where

This system has analogies to viscoelasticity, both conceptually and in terms of mathematical properties.
In the one-dimensional case, the system of equations

\displaystyle w_{t}-v_{x}

\displaystyle=0\,,

\displaystyle v_{t}-\sigma(w)_{x}

\displaystyle=(\frac{\mu}{w}v_{x})_{x}\,,

describes both the equations for longitudinal motions of a (one-dimensional) viscoelastic bar
and the equations of a compressible viscous gas in Lagrangean coordinates.

2.
Existence theory of exemplar hyperbolic-parabolic systems

We review elements of the existence theory first for (
1.1
) and then for (
1.3
)
both viewed as important and representative examples of hyperbolic-parabolic systems with singular diffusion matrices.
Various reasons lead to conjecture that oscillations in the data partly propagate to solutions of these systems.

2.1.
The system of viscoelasticity of Kelvin-Voigt type

The system describing the equations of nonlinear viscoelasticity in Lagrangean coordinates is

(2.1)

\frac{\partial^{2}y}{\partial t^{2}}=\mathrm{div}\frac{\partial W}{\partial F}(F)+\mu\Delta\frac{\partial y}{\partial t}\,.

Here
y(x,t)
is a function describing the motion of a reference configuration,

v=\frac{\partial y}{\partial t}
is the velocity and
F=\nabla y
the deformation gradient; note that

\dot{F}=\nabla v
.
The system is obtained by combining the balance of momentum equation
\frac{\partial^{2}y}{\partial t^{2}}=\mathrm{div}T
,
where
T
describes the stress tensor, with the constitutive assumption of viscoelasticity of the rate type

(2.2)

T=\frac{\partial W}{\partial F}(F)+\mu\dot{F}

where
\mu>0
is the viscosity coefficient.
This leads to the equation (
2.1
) of viscoelasticity of Kelvin-Voigt type.
The constitutive hypothesis (
2.2
) is not frame indifferent
- which is considered a deficiency from a perspective of theoretical mechanics -
yet offers a good starting point to understand some basic mathematical features.

The limiting case
\mu=0
, when rate-dependence is ignored, leads to
T=\frac{\partial W}{\partial F}(F)
and is
called hyperelasticity. The elastic stresses are induced by a strain-energy function
W(F)
. The
system of hyperelasticity can be expressed as a first-order system of nonlinear partial differential equations,
written in coordinates as

(2.3)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\partial_{t}F_{i\alpha}=\partial_{\alpha}v_{i}\\
&\partial_{t}v_{i}=\partial_{\alpha}\tfrac{\partial W}{\partial F_{i\alpha}}(F)\end{aligned}

(2.4)

\displaystyle\partial_{\alpha}F_{i\beta}-\partial_{\beta}F_{i\alpha}=0\,,

where the summation convention over repeated indices is adopted.
The equation (
2.4
) is concisely expressed as
\mathop{\mathrm{curl}}F=0
and is a constraint expressing the requirement that
F
be a gradient

F=\nabla y
. The constraint is an involution along the evolution, that is it is propagated from the initial data to the solution
via the kinematic relation (
2.3
)
1
.

Analysis of hyperbolicity for the elasticity system (
2.3
) in dimension
d=3
indicates that hyperbolicity is equivalent to rank-1 convexity of the
strain energy function:

Hyperbolicity of (
2.3
)

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow\quad W(F)\quad\mbox{is rank-1 convex}

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow\frac{\partial^{2}W}{\partial F_{i\alpha}\partial F_{j\beta}}(F)\;\xi_{i}\xi_{j}\nu_{\alpha}\nu_{\beta}>0\quad\forall\xi\neq 0\,,\,\nu\in\mathcal{S}^{2}

There are twelve characteristic speeds of (
2.3
): the first six are

\lambda_{1}=\dots=\lambda_{6}=0
while the last six are the positive and negative square roots of the
eigenvalues of the acoustic tensor

Q_{ij}=\frac{\partial^{2}W}{\partial F_{i\alpha}\partial F_{j\beta}}\nu_{\alpha}\nu_{\beta}

The eigenvalues are real provided the stored energy function is rank-one convex.

2.1.1.
Existence Theory

Henceforth we set the viscosity
\mu=1
and consider (
2.1
) expressed as a system

(2.5)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}v

\displaystyle=\mathrm{div}\Big(\frac{\partial W}{\partial F}(F)\Big)+\Delta v

\displaystyle\partial_{t}F

\displaystyle=\nabla v

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathrm{curl}}F=0

The existence theory of (
2.5
) utilizes the (formal) energy identity

(2.6)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\left(\tfrac{1}{2}|v|^{2}+W(F)\right)-\mathrm{div}\left(v\cdot\Big(\frac{\partial W}{\partial F}(F)+\nabla v\Big)\right)+|\nabla v|^{2}=0

We place hypotheses on the stored energy function
W(F)
, designed to
allow for stored energies that exhibit double well potentials, associated with phase transitions. We assume that
W(F)
is
a
C^{2}
-function that satisfies

\displaystyle c|F|^{p}-\tilde{c}\leq W(F)

\displaystyle\leq C(1+|F|^{p})\qquad\mbox{for $p\geq 2$}

(H)

\displaystyle|DW(F)|

\displaystyle\leq C(1+|F|^{p-1})

\displaystyle W(F)\mbox{ is semiconvex }

\displaystyle\mbox{, i.e. $W(F)+\frac{K}{2}|F|^{2}$ is convex for some $K>0$}\,.

The hypothesis
p\geq 2
is for technical convenience, but plays an important role in technical aspects of the analysis.
More important, the smoothness hypothesis in (
H
) notably excludes potentials that blow up as
\det F\to 0
;
the inclusion of such potentials is a minimum requirement to avoid interpenetration of matter. How to include such potentials
and to avoid interpenetration of matter is an outstanding open problem for the dynamical theory.

The following existence theorems are available for (
2.5
), see
[
18
,
11
,
24
]
, stated avoiding technical details here
for solutions of the initial value problem on the torus

Theorem 2.1
.

(i)
[

If the initial data

which satisfies the energy inequality

\int\Big(\tfrac{1}{2}|v|^{2}+W(F)\Big)dx+\int_{0}^{t}\int|\nabla v|^{2}\leq\int\tfrac{1}{2}|v_{0}|^{2}+W(F_{0})\,.

(ii)
[
24

If

(almost) what is called a strong solution. In addition, the solution is unique for dimension

(2.8)

\int\tfrac{1}{2}|v|^{2}+W(F)dx+\int_{0}^{t}\int|\nabla v|^{2}=\int\tfrac{1}{2}|v_{0}|^{2}+W(F_{0})

under the restrictions

\begin{cases}2\leq p&d=2\\

The approach to the proof of Friesecke-Dolzmann
[
18
]
uses the natural energy bounds obtained from (
2.6
) and the hypotheses
(
H
) on

but with one important caveat outlined below. They construct solutions by a time-stepping algorithm, iteratively solving a minimization problem

Given
(v^{0},F^{0})
find the minimizer of

\min\int\tfrac{1}{2}|v-v^{0}|^{2}+\frac{1}{2h}|F-F^{0}|^{2}+W(F)dx

subject to the affine constraint

\frac{F-F^{0}}{h}=\nabla v

This problem is convex for

They then build approximate solutions

via piecewise affine
and piecewise linear interpolations, and in a last important step they show a propagation of
compactness result

\displaystyle\limsup_{h\to 0}\int_{0}^{t}\int\big|F^{h}(t,x)-F(t,x)\big|^{2}dxdt\leq\left(\limsup_{h\to 0}\int|F^{h}_{0}-F_{0}|^{2}dx\right)e^{Kt}

that allows to prove compactness, see
[
18
]
and
[
11
]
that develops
similar ideas in a more complex setup.

The approach of
[
24

is based on a transfer of dissipation identity that we outline at the level
of the system with variable viscosity
\mu

\displaystyle\partial_{t}v-\mathrm{div}S(F)-\mu\Delta v

\displaystyle=0\qquad\varepsilon>0

\displaystyle\partial_{t}F-\nabla v

\displaystyle=0

One then has the energy identity

(2.9)

\frac{d}{dt}\int\tfrac{1}{2}|v|^{2}+W(F)dx+\mu\int|\nabla v|^{2}=0

It is possible to obtain an additional estimate for periodic solutions. This is effected
by combining the compensated compactness type of bracket

(2.10)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\big(v\cdot\mathrm{div}F-\mu|\mathrm{div}F|^{2}\big)-\mathrm{div}(v\cdot\nabla v)=\mathrm{div}S(F)\cdot\mathrm{div}F-\nabla v:\nabla v

with a scaled version of the energy identity (

leading to the identity

(2.11)

\frac{d}{dt}\int\tfrac{1}{2}|v-\tfrac{\mu}{2}\mathrm{div}F|^{2}+\tfrac{\mu^{2}}{4}|\mathrm{div}F|^{2}+W(F)dx+{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\tfrac{\mu}{2}}\int D^{2}W:(\nabla F,\nabla F)+|\nabla v|^{2}=0\,.

The latter manifests transfer of dissipation from momentum to the strain under improved initial
regularity of the strain, see
[
24
]
. Clearly for
W(F)
convex equation (
2.11
) provides
a gradient estimate for the deformation gradient.

This estimate can also be achieved for semiconvex energies (
H
) by a small adaptation. Setting

\tilde{W}(F)=W(F)+\frac{K}{2}|F|^{2}

we note

is convex and use

\frac{d}{dt}\int|v-\mathop{\mathrm{div}}F|^{2}+|\nabla F|^{2}+2W(F)\,dx+\int\Big(D^{2}\tilde{W}:(\nabla F,\nabla F)+|\nabla v|^{2}\Big)dx\leq K\int|\nabla F|^{2}dx.

Using Grönwall’s lemma, (

.

Summarizing, there is an available existence theory for initial data

and a second theory
for data

The latter assumes

and obtains the same regularity

This raises the question: what happens if

2.1.2.
Relation between existence and strong convergence of the initial data

We show next an argument that illustrates the role of compactness of initial data. Let
y_{n}
be a family of periodic, approximate solutions generated
by the equations

(2.13)

y^{n}_{tt}=div_{x}\left(\frac{\partial W}{\partial F}(\nabla y^{n})+\nabla y^{n}_{t}\right)+f_{n}\,,\qquad(t,x)\in(0,T)\times\mathbb{T}^{d}\,,

where
f_{n}\to f
in
L^{2}_{t,x}
, and suppose the initial data are uniformly bounded in energy,

\int\tfrac{1}{2}|v_{0}^{n}|^{2}+W(F_{0}^{n})dx\leq C\,.

Assume that
W(F)
is strictly convex and that
p>2
.
The sequence
(v^{n},F^{n})
of approximate solutions are uniformly bounded in energy by (
2.7
) which together
with the Aubin-Lions lemma and the representation theorem via Young measures
[
3
]
implies that

satisfies

\displaystyle v^{n}\to v\;\mbox{ in $L^{2}$}

\displaystyle F^{n}\rightharpoonup F\;\;\mbox{wk-$\ast$ in $L^{\infty}(L^{p})$}

\displaystyle g(F^{n})\rightharpoonup\int g(\lambda_{F})\,d\nu_{t,x}(\lambda_{F})=:\overline{g(\lambda_{F})}

\displaystyle\mbox{ for $g$ continuous s.t. $\lim_{|\xi|\to\infty}\frac{g(\xi)}{1+|\xi|^{p}}=0$}

Here, we used the notation
\nu_{t,x}
for the
parametrized family of probability measures (called Young measures) that represent the weak convergence
properties of (a subsequence of)
\{F^{n}\}

We multiply the equation by
y^{n}
, integrate on the torus

and by parts to obtain

\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}(v^{n})^{2}-\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\left(\frac{\partial W}{\partial F}(\nabla y^{n})+\nabla y^{n}_{t}\right)\cdot\nabla y^{n}+\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}f^{n}y^{n}=\partial_{t}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}y^{n}v^{n}

which gives

(2.14)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}(v^{n})^{2}-\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\frac{\partial W}{\partial F}(F^{n})\cdot F^{n}+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}f^{n}y^{n}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\left[\tfrac{1}{2}|F^{n}|^{2}(x,t)+(y^{n}v^{n})(x,t)\right]dx-\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\left[\tfrac{1}{2}|F_{0}^{n}|^{2}(x)+(y_{0}^{n}v_{0}^{n})(x)\right]\,dx

We want to pass to the limit

. The difficulty arises that the function
\frac{\partial W}{\partial F}(F)\cdot F
, under the hypothesis
(
H
), has growth of order

and its weak limit cannot be represented via the usual Young measure theorem.

To bypass this difficulty we will use a variant of a representation result from
[

that we state and prove below.
Let
F(t,x)
be a given matrix valued function,
\lambda_{F}
a matrix and define

\phi(\lambda_{F},F)=\Big(\frac{\partial W}{\partial F}(\lambda_{F})-\frac{\partial W}{\partial F}(F)\Big):(\lambda_{F}-F)

Observe
\phi(\lambda_{F},F)\geq 0
and by (
H
) we have
|\phi(\lambda_{F},F)|\leq C\big(|\lambda_{F}|^{p}+|F|^{p}+1)
.
We prove the following representation theorem.

Lemma 2.2
.

There exists a positive measure
\mu(dxdt)\geq 0
so that

\phi(F^{n},F)\rightharpoonup\int\phi(\lambda_{F},F)\,d\nu_{t,x}(\lambda_{F})+d\mu(dxdt)

in the sense of measures, where the meaning of the integral is defined in the proof.

Proof.

First, we define
\int\phi(\lambda_{F},F)\,d\nu_{t,x}(\lambda_{F})
. Let
R>0
and consider the truncated functions

\phi_{R}(\lambda_{F},F)=\begin{cases}\phi(\lambda_{F},F)&\mbox{if}\;\;\phi(\lambda_{F},F)<R\\
R&\mbox{if}\;\;\phi(\lambda_{F},F)\geq R\\
\end{cases}

Then
0\leq\phi_{R}(\lambda_{F},F)\nearrow\phi(\lambda_{F},F)
a.e. on
Q_{T}:=(0,T)\times\mathbb{T}^{d}
and the monotone convergence theorem
implies

(2.15)

\int\phi(\lambda_{F},F)\,d\nu_{t,x}(\lambda_{F})=\lim_{R\nearrow\infty}\int\phi_{R}(\lambda_{F},F)d\nu_{t,x}(\lambda_{F})\quad\mbox{a.e. $(t,x)$}.

Observe that
0\leq\phi_{R}(F^{n},F)\leq R
and the representation of weak limits via Young measures implies
that along a subsequence
\{F^{n}\}
we have

\phi_{R}(F^{n},F)\rightharpoonup\int\phi(\lambda_{F},F)\,d\nu_{t,x}(\lambda_{F})\,,\quad\mbox{weak-$\ast$ in $L^{\infty}$}

Formula (
2.15
) serves as the definition of the representation of the weak limit.

Consider next the sequence
\Big\{\phi(F^{n},F)-\int\phi(\lambda_{F},F)\,d\nu_{t,x}(\lambda_{F})\Big\}
and observe that

\sup_{n}\int_{Q_{T}}\Big|\phi(F^{n},F)-\int\phi(\lambda_{F},F)\,d\nu_{t,x}(\lambda_{F})\Big|dtdx\leq C

Therefore, there exists a bounded measure
\mu(dxdt)
such that

\int_{Q_{T}}\Big(\phi(F^{n},F)-\int\phi(\lambda_{F},F)\,d\nu_{t,x}(\lambda_{F})\Big)\theta(t,x)dtdx\to\int_{Q_{T}}\theta(t,x)\mu(dtdx)

for all
\theta\in C(\overline{Q_{T}})
. Finally, for
\theta(t,x)\geq 0
, the chain of inequalities

\displaystyle\int_{Q_{T}}\Big(\int

\displaystyle\phi(\lambda_{F},F)\,d\nu_{t,x}(\lambda_{F})\Big)\theta(t,x)dtdx

\displaystyle=\sup_{R>0}\int_{Q_{T}}\Big(\int\phi_{R}(\lambda_{F},F)\,d\nu_{t,x}(\lambda_{F})\Big)\theta(t,x)dtdx

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{R>0}\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_{Q_{T}}\phi_{R}(F^{n},F)\theta(t,x)dtdx

\displaystyle\leq\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_{Q_{T}}\phi(F^{n},F)\theta(t,x)dtdx

implies that
\mu(dtdx)\geq 0
as asserted.
∎

Using the representation lemma
2.2
and letting
n\to\infty
in (
2.14
) gives

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}v^{2}-\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\Big(\overline{\big(\frac{\partial W}{\partial F}(\lambda_{F})-\frac{\partial W}{\partial F}(F)\big):(\lambda_{F}-F)}+\overline{\frac{\partial W}{\partial F}(\lambda_{F})}:F\Big)dxdt-\iint d\mu(dxdt)

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}fy=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\left(\overline{\tfrac{1}{2}|\lambda_{F}|^{2}}+(yv)\right)dx-\limsup_{n\to\infty}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\tfrac{1}{2}|F_{0}^{n}|^{2}\,dx-\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}y_{0}v_{0}dx

Consider next the weak form of the limiting equation to (
2.13
), this is what is often called measure-valued solution.
It has the form:

(2.16)

y_{tt}=\mathrm{div}_{x}\left(\overline{\frac{\partial W}{\partial F}(\lambda_{F})}+\nabla y_{t}\right)+f\,,\qquad(t,x)\in(0,T)\times\mathbb{T}^{d}\,.

We multiply by
y
and perform the analogous steps to obtain

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}v^{2}-\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\overline{\frac{\partial W}{\partial F}(\lambda_{F})}:Fdxdt+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}fy

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\left(\tfrac{1}{2}|F|^{2}(x,t)+(yv)(x,t)\right)dx-\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\left(\tfrac{1}{2}|F_{0}|^{2}+y_{0}v_{0}\right)dx

By comparing the identities obtained for the approximate family
(v^{n},F^{n})
and for the measure valued solution
\big((v,F);\nu_{(t,x)}\big)

one obtains after some cancellations that

(2.17)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\int\overline{\big(\frac{\partial W}{\partial F}(\lambda_{F})-\frac{\partial W}{\partial F}(F)\big):(\lambda_{F}-F)}\,dx\,dt

\displaystyle+\tfrac{1}{2}\int\overline{|\lambda_{F}-F|^{2}}dx+\iint\mu(dxdt)

\displaystyle=\limsup_{n\to\infty}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\tfrac{1}{2}|F_{0}^{n}-F_{0}|^{2}dx

If the initial data converge strongly
F_{0}^{n}\to F_{0}
in
L^{2}

then
\nu_{(0,x)}=\delta_{F_{0}(x)}
and (
2.17
) for
W(F)
strictly convex implies that
\nu_{(t,x)}=\delta_{F(t,x)}
for a.e.
(t,x)
.
The converse is also clear,
that is if
\nu_{(t,x)}=\delta_{F(t,x)}
and the concentration measure
\mu(dxdt)=0
then the initial data converge strongly in
L^{2}
.

In fact, something even stronger is proved in
[
24
]
:
Even when
W(F)
is semiconvex satisfying (
H
), if the initial data

converge strongly to
F_{0}
that
implies strong convergence of solutions.

2.2.
Finite-energy weak solutions for the compressible Navier-Stokes system

The compressible Navier-Stokes system

(2.18)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\rho+\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\rho u

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\rho u+\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\rho u\otimes u+\nabla p(\rho)

\displaystyle=\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\Big(\mu(\nabla u+\nabla u^{T})+\lambda(\mathop{\mathrm{div}}u)\,I\,\Big)

describes the evolution of
(\rho,u)
, where
\rho
and
u
are the density and velocity of the fluid,
of a compressible, barotropic fluid with pressure
p(\rho)
.
The pressure might be monotone, but in applications there is
interest in non-monotone pressures like in case of a Van der Waals type pressure.

The parameters
\mu,\lambda
are called shear and bulk viscosity. They satisfy conditions that ensure
the viscous stress which is isotropic is also dissipative. The viscous stress
S
may be expressed via the symmetric part of the strain rate

D=\tfrac{1}{2}(\nabla u+\nabla u^{T})
as

\displaystyle S

\displaystyle=\mu(\nabla u+\nabla u^{T})+\lambda(\mathrm{div}u)I

\displaystyle=2\mu D+\lambda(\mathrm{tr}D)I

Accordingly, the viscous dissipation reads

\displaystyle S:\nabla u=S:D

\displaystyle=2\mu|D|^{2}+\lambda(\mathrm{tr}D)^{2}

\displaystyle=2\mu\big(D-\tfrac{1}{3}(\mathrm{tr}D)I\big):\big(D-\tfrac{1}{3}(\mathrm{tr}D)I\big)+\big(\lambda+\tfrac{2\mu}{3}\big)(\mathrm{tr}D)^{2}\,.

Therefore, the viscous dissipation
S:D>0
for all
D\neq 0

if and only if
\mu>0
and
\lambda+\tfrac{2\mu}{3}>0
. (This condition is replaced by
\mu>0
and
\lambda+\tfrac{2\mu}{d}>0

for dimension
d
.)

The Cauchy problem is considered with either Dirichlet or periodic boundary conditions, and initial conditions for
x\in\Omega
,

(2.19)

\rho(0,x)=\rho_{0}(x)\,,\quad u(0,x)=u_{0}(x)\,.

The solution
(\rho,u)
is sought in
Q_{T}=(0,T)\times\Omega
. Local existence of classical solutions and
global existence of small perturbations of constant states are classical results.
A global existence result for weak solutions for initial densities close to a constant was proved in
[
21
]
.
Global weak solutions that dissipate the energy were constructed in the celebrated results of

[
26
]
,
[
16
]
.

The equations (
2.18
) are equipped with the (formal) energy estimate

\frac{d}{dt}E[\rho,\rho u](t)+\int_{\Omega}\mu|\nabla u|^{2}+(\lambda+\mu)(\mathrm{div}u)^{2}dx=0

where the energy functional is given by

E[\rho,\rho u](t)=\int_{\Omega}\rho|u|^{2}+h(\rho)dx\,.

The energy identity is obtained by multiplying respectively (
2.18
)
1
by
h^{\prime}(\rho)
and (
2.18
)
2
by
u
and adding the results.
The internal energy per unit volume
h(\rho)
is connected to the pressure
p(\rho)
by the thermodynamic relation

p(\rho)=\rho h^{\prime}(\rho)-h(\rho)\,.

The case of the isentropic gas law

(IG)

p(\rho)=\rho^{\gamma}\,,\quad h(\rho)=\tfrac{1}{\gamma-1}\rho^{\gamma}\,,\quad\mbox{ and $\gamma>1$}.

has provided a well studied example for which there exists an existence theory of global weak solutions, usually called
the Lions-Feireisl theory. The theory was first developed for various initial-boundary value problems in the book of
Lions
[
26
]
with significant improvements obtained by Feireisl-Novotný-Petzeltová
[
15
]
and Feireisl
[
16
]
.
Consider an isentropic gas law (
IG
) with Dirichlet boundary conditions

u=0
on
\partial\Omega
, or periodic boundary conditions
\Omega=\mathbb{T}^{d}
.

Definition 2.3
.

A pair
(\rho,u)
on
Q_{T}
is called a finite energy weak solution if

(i)

\rho\in L^{\infty}\big((0,T);L^{\gamma}(\Omega)\big)
,
u\in L^{2}\big((0,T);H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)\big)

(ii)

(\rho,u)
satisfies (
2.18
) in distributions

(iii)

\rho
is a renormalized solution of the transport equation, that is

\partial_{t}b(\rho)+\mathrm{div}_{x}(b(\rho)u)+\big(\rho b^{\prime}(\rho)-b(\rho)\big)(div_{x}u)=0\,,\quad\mbox{in $\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(Q_{T})$}

where
b\in C^{1}([0,\infty))
such that
b^{\prime}(\xi)=0
for
\xi
sufficiently large.

(iv)

E[\rho,\rho u](t)\in L^{1}_{loc}(0,T)
satisfies

\frac{d}{dt}E[\rho,\rho u](t)+\int_{\Omega}\mu|\nabla u|^{2}+(\lambda+\mu)(\mathrm{div}u)^{2}dx\leq 0\,,\quad\mbox{in $\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(0,T)$}.

The existence result in
[
26
,
15
,
16
]
is stated:

Theorem 2.4
.

Let
p(\rho)
satisfy (
IG
) with
\gamma>\frac{3}{2}
and assume
\rho^{0}\in L^{\gamma}(\Omega)
and
m^{0}

satisfy
\rho^{0}\geq 0

\displaystyle m^{0}(x)=0

\displaystyle\mbox{whenever}\;\;\rho^{0}(x)=0

\displaystyle\frac{|m^{0}|^{2}}{\rho^{0}}

\displaystyle\in L^{1}(\Omega)

Then there exists a global finite energy solution of (
2.18
), (
2.19
) with Dirichlet or periodic boundary conditions.

The details of the Lions-Feireisl theory are not within the scope of this work, and, besides the aforementioned references, the reader is referred
to the book by Novotný and Straškraba
[
31
]
. The work of Bresch-Jabin
[
5
]
contains a very nice and concise outline of the key aspects
of the Lions-Feireisl theory, and provides extensions and quantitative compactness estimates for general pressure laws

p=p(\rho)
including non-monotone pressures.

The Lions-Feireisl existence theory establishes compactness properties for finite energy approximate solutions

(\rho^{n},u^{n})
. A key ingredient is a propagation of compactness result, initially formulated by Lions, that indicates that if the initial data for the density
converge strongly
\rho^{n}(x,0)\to\rho(x,0)
then this
property transfers to solutions
\rho^{n}(\cdot,t)\to\rho(\cdot,t)
; see
[
26
, Sec 5.2]
.
It is also noted in Remark 5.9 of
[
26
, Sec 5.2]
that (for
p(\rho)=\rho^{\gamma}
in the whole space
\mathbb{R}^{d}
) if the weak limit
(\rho,u)
is a solution of compressible Navier-Stokes then necessarily
\rho_{0}^{n}
converges strongly to
\rho_{0}
.

Lions raised the issue of propagation of oscillations and homogenization
[
26
, Rmk 5.8]
. He constructs an exact periodic solution of the
problem

(2.20)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\rho+\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\rho u

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\Big(\mu(\nabla u+\nabla u^{T})+\lambda(\mathop{\mathrm{div}}u)\,I\,\Big)-\nabla\rho^{\gamma}

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\rho udx=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}m_{0}dx

in
\mathbb{R}^{d}\times[0,\infty)
with
m_{0}
given, and uses it to produce a sequence
(\rho^{n},u^{n})
of periodic solutions
with persistent oscillations of a forced variant of (
2.18
)
with
p(\rho)=\rho^{\gamma}
.

An example of a sequence
(\rho^{n},u^{n})
with persistent oscillations for (
2.18
) with non-monotone pressure
is constructed in
[
40
]
. The example consists of uniform shear flows that have continuous velocities
but discontinuous densities,
jumping between different phases across characteristic surfaces, see section
5.2
.

3.
Oscillations in Linear Hyperbolic-Parabolic Systems

In this section we study oscillations in linear hyperbolic-parabolic systems.
The form of solutions is motivated by examples that arise
in the theory of thermoviscoelasticity and relate to the mechanical interpretation of such models.

3.1.
Linear hyperbolic systems

Linear hyperbolic systems (in 1-d for simplicity)

\partial_{t}U+A\partial_{x}U=0

admit oscillatory solutions of the form

U(t,x)=\xi e^{in\big(x-\lambda t\big)}

where
\lambda
are the characteristic speeds, defined as the eigenvalues of the problem

(A-\lambda I)\xi=0\,.

Oscillations persist for linear hyperbolic systems, they are induced by the initial data, and they travel on characteristic directions.

3.2.
Linear hyperbolic-parabolic systems

By contrast, when the viscosity matrix
B>0
of a hyperbolic-parabolic system

(3.1)

\partial_{t}U+A\partial_{x}U=B\partial^{2}_{x}U

then oscillations are suppressed.

Consider now the question: What happens for the case of Hyperbolic-Parabolic Systems with
B
singular?
We focus on examples of systems with singular diffusion matrices
originating from thermomechanics.

3.2.1.
An illustrating example

The special case of linear viscoelasticity in one-space dimension is the equation

(3.2)

u_{tt}=\lambda u_{xx}+\mu u_{txx}\qquad x\in(-\pi,\pi)\,,t>0\,,

where
u:(0,T)\times\mathbb{T}^{1}\to\mathbb{R}
. This equation is closely related to the so called telegraph equation.

We explore an ansatz of the form

u(t,x)=\frac{1}{n}\alpha(t)e^{inx}

and examine conditions for existence of such solutions with high frequency
n
.
The amplitude
\alpha(t)
needs to satisfy

(3.3)

\frac{d^{2}\alpha}{dt^{2}}+\mu n^{2}\frac{d\alpha}{dt}+n^{2}\lambda\alpha=0\,,

where the associated characteristic polynomial
\rho^{2}+\mu n^{2}+\lambda n^{2}=0
has roots

\rho_{\pm}=\frac{\mu n^{2}}{2}\left(-1\pm\sqrt{1-\frac{4\lambda}{\mu^{2}n^{2}}}\right)\,.

These eigenvalues are real for
n
large, and enjoy the asymptotic expansions

\displaystyle\rho_{-}

\displaystyle=-\mu n^{2}+\frac{\lambda}{\mu}+\frac{\lambda^{2}}{\mu^{3}n^{2}}+O\big(\tfrac{1}{n^{4}}\big)

\displaystyle\rho_{+}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\lambda}{\mu}-\frac{\lambda^{2}}{\mu^{3}n^{2}}+O\big(\tfrac{1}{n^{4}}\big)

They give rise to solutions
\alpha_{\pm}=e^{\rho_{\pm}t}
for (
3.3
) and
u_{n,\pm}=\frac{1}{n}e^{inx+\rho_{\pm}t}
for (
3.2
).
Of interest here is the solution associated to
\rho_{+}
and we consider
(u_{n},v_{n})
where

(3.4)

u_{n}(t,x)=\frac{1}{n}\exp\left\{inx-\frac{\lambda}{\mu}t-\frac{\lambda^{2}}{\mu^{3}n^{2}}t+O\big(\tfrac{1}{n^{4}}\big)t\right\}

and
v_{n}=\frac{\partial u_{n}}{\partial t}
. Note that
u_{n}\,,v_{n}\to 0
as
n\to\infty
. By contrast,

(3.5)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial u_{n}}{\partial x}

\displaystyle=i\left(1+O\big(\frac{1}{n^{2}}\big)\right)\exp\left\{inx-\frac{\lambda}{\mu}t\right\}

\displaystyle\frac{\partial v_{n}}{\partial x}

\displaystyle=i\left(-\frac{\lambda}{\mu}+O\big(\frac{1}{n^{2}}\big)\right)\exp\left\{inx-\frac{\lambda}{\mu}t\right\}

exhibit persistent oscillations induced by the initial data.

To put this example into the perspective, note that (
3.2
) may be expressed as the hyperbolic-parabolic system,

(3.6)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial w}{\partial t}

\displaystyle=\;\frac{\partial v}{\partial x}

\displaystyle\frac{\partial v}{\partial t}

\displaystyle=\lambda\frac{\partial w}{\partial x}+\mu\frac{\partial^{2}v}{\partial x^{2}}

with
w=\frac{\partial u}{\partial x}
.
The oscillation in (
3.5
) reflects a balance between the terms
w
and
\frac{\partial v}{\partial x}

and is stationary in time.

It should be contrasted with the usual oscillatory solutions of hyperbolic systems. Recall, that the hyperbolic model

(3.7)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial w}{\partial t}

\displaystyle=\;\frac{\partial v}{\partial x}

\displaystyle\frac{\partial v}{\partial t}

\displaystyle=\lambda\frac{\partial w}{\partial x}

has oscillatory solutions propagating in the directions of the wave speeds

\begin{bmatrix}w\\[5.0pt]
v\end{bmatrix}(t,x)=\xi_{\pm}\exp\big\{in(x-\pm{\sqrt{\lambda}}t)\big\}

where
\xi_{\pm}
are the eigenvectors associated to the wave speeds
A\xi_{\pm}=\pm\sqrt{\lambda}\xi_{\pm}
. These propagate
with speeds
\pm\sqrt{\lambda}
and are of different nature than (
3.4
), (
3.5
) which are stationary in time.

3.2.2.
1-d linear thermoviscoelasticity

The results of the example of section
3.2.1
are now extended to the system of 1-d thermoviscoelasticity

(3.8)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}u}{\partial t^{2}}

\displaystyle=\lambda\frac{\partial^{2}u}{\partial x^{2}}+m\frac{\partial\theta}{\partial x}+\mu\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial x^{2}}\frac{\partial u}{\partial t}

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\theta}{\partial t}

\displaystyle=\kappa\frac{\partial^{2}\theta}{\partial x^{2}}+m\frac{\partial^{2}u}{\partial x\partial t}

Note that
(u(t,x),\theta(t,x))
describes a thermomechanical process consisting of the motion
u
and the temperature
\theta
,
while
\lambda>0
,
m\in\mathbb{R}
,
\mu>0
,
\kappa>0
are parameters describing the elasticity modulus, thermoelastic coupling,
viscosity and heat diffusivity, respectively. The system (
3.8
) is expressed as a hyperbolic-parabolic system by introducing
the velocity
v=\frac{\partial u}{\partial t}
and the elastic strain
w=\frac{\partial u}{\partial x}
and writing it as

(3.9)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\begin{pmatrix}w\\
v\\
\theta\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}0&1&0\\
\lambda&0&m\\
0&m&0\end{pmatrix}\partial_{x}\begin{pmatrix}w\\
v\\
\theta\end{pmatrix}+\begin{pmatrix}0&0&0\\
0&\mu&0\\
0&0&\kappa\end{pmatrix}\partial_{xx}\begin{pmatrix}w\\
v\\
\theta\end{pmatrix}

Solutions of (
3.8
) satisfy the energy identity

(3.10)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\left(\tfrac{1}{2}\Big|\frac{\partial u}{\partial t}\Big|^{2}+\tfrac{\lambda}{2}\Big|\frac{\partial u}{\partial x}\Big|^{2}+\tfrac{1}{2}|\theta|^{2}\right)+\kappa\Big|\frac{\partial\theta}{\partial x}\Big|^{2}+\mu\Big|\frac{\partial^{2}u}{\partial x\partial t}\Big|^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\left(\frac{\partial u}{\partial t}\big(\lambda\frac{\partial u}{\partial x}+m\theta+\mu\frac{\partial^{2}u}{\partial x\partial t}\big)\right)+\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\left(\kappa\theta\frac{\partial\theta}{\partial x}\right)

Under appropriate boundary conditions (either periodic or no mechanical work and adiabatic at the boundaries) we obtain the energy
dissipation identity

(3.11)

\frac{d}{dt}\int\Big(\tfrac{1}{2}\Big|\frac{\partial u}{\partial t}\Big|^{2}+\tfrac{\lambda}{2}\Big|\frac{\partial u}{\partial x}\Big|^{2}+\tfrac{1}{2}|\theta|^{2}\Big)\,dx+\int\kappa\Big|\frac{\partial\theta}{\partial x}\Big|^{2}+\mu\Big|\frac{\partial^{2}u}{\partial x\partial t}\Big|^{2}=0\,.

which illustrates that the dissipation is partial arising from a singular diffusion matrix.

Next, apply an
ansatz
of solutions of the form

(3.12)

\displaystyle u(t,x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}a(t)e^{inx}

\displaystyle\theta(t,x)

\displaystyle=-ib(t)e^{inx}

where
i
is the imaginary unit,
n
an integer, and
(a(t),-ib(t))
are complex amplitude functions.
Introducing (
3.12
) to (
3.8
) we deduce that

(a(t),b(t))
satisfies the system of ordinary differential equations

(3.13)

\displaystyle\ddot{a}

\displaystyle=-\lambda n^{2}a+mn^{2}b-\mu n^{2}\dot{a}

\displaystyle\dot{b}

\displaystyle=-\kappa n^{2}b-m\dot{a}

The amplitude
(a,b)
in (
3.13
) was introduced as complex-valued but it is also consistent to select it real-valued, and
we opt for that selection here.
For the
ansatz
(
3.12
) we have

\displaystyle\frac{\partial u}{\partial x}

\displaystyle=ia(t)e^{inx}

\displaystyle\frac{\partial u}{\partial t}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\dot{a}(t)e^{inx}

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}u}{\partial x\partial t}

\displaystyle=i\dot{a}(t)e^{inx}

We expect oscillations of
u(t,x)
and the velocity
v=\frac{\partial u}{\partial t}(t,x)
to decay to zero (for
n
large)
while oscillations of the strain
w=\frac{\partial u}{\partial x}(t,x)
, the strain rate
\frac{\partial v}{\partial x}=\frac{\partial^{2}u}{\partial x\partial t}(t,x)

are expected to persist in time. This has to be validated by studying the behavior of solutions of (
3.13
).

Setting
v=\dot{a}
, (
3.13
) is expressed as a first order system,

(3.14)

\frac{d}{dt}\begin{bmatrix}a\\
v\\
b\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}0&1&0\\
-\lambda n^{2}&-\mu n^{2}&mn^{2}\\
0&-m&-\kappa n^{2}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}a\\
v\\
b\end{bmatrix}

Let
A=A(n)
be the coefficient matrix. The eigenvalues
\rho(n)
of
A(n)
are computed as
roots of the cubic polynomial
\det(A-\rho I)=0
which reads

(3.15)

\rho^{3}+(\kappa+\mu)n^{2}\rho^{2}+(\kappa\mu n^{4}+\lambda n^{2}+m^{2}n^{2})\rho+\kappa\lambda n^{4}=0\,.

To proceed one considers the eigenvalue problem

(A(n)-\rho(n)I)\xi(n)=0

and expands the eigenvalues and eigenvectors as functions of
\frac{1}{n}
to study the behavior of high frequencies.
After a tedious but straightforward calculations (see
[
39
]
) one arrives at an asymptotic expansion of
the oscillatory solutions for (
3.8
) which read

(3.16)

\begin{bmatrix}u\\[5.0pt]
\frac{\partial u}{\partial t}\\[5.0pt]
\theta\end{bmatrix}(t,x)=\begin{bmatrix}\tfrac{1}{n}a(t)\\
\tfrac{1}{n}v(t)\\
-ib(t)\end{bmatrix}e^{inx}=\begin{bmatrix}\frac{1}{n}\mu-\frac{\lambda}{\mu}\frac{1}{n^{3}}+O(\frac{1}{n^{5}})\\[5.0pt]
-\frac{1}{n}\lambda+\frac{m^{2}\lambda}{\kappa\mu}\frac{1}{n^{3}}+O(\frac{1}{n^{5}})\\[5.0pt]
-i\frac{m\lambda}{\kappa}\frac{1}{n^{2}}+O(\frac{1}{n^{4}})\end{bmatrix}\exp\Big\{inx-\frac{\lambda}{\mu}t+O\big(\frac{1}{n^{2}}\Big)t)\Big\}

Considering (
3.16
) we see that
u
,
\frac{\partial u}{\partial t}
,
\theta
and
\frac{\partial\theta}{\partial x}
converge to zero for
n
large, but oscillations persist
for the strain
w=\frac{\partial u}{\partial x}
, the strain rate
\frac{\partial v}{\partial x}
and
\frac{\partial^{2}\theta}{\partial x^{2}}
. To leading order these solutions
behave like

(3.17)

\begin{bmatrix}w\\[5.0pt]
\frac{\partial v}{\partial x}\\[5.0pt]
\frac{\partial^{2}\theta}{\partial x^{2}}\end{bmatrix}(t,x)=\begin{bmatrix}\frac{\partial u}{\partial x}\\[5.0pt]
\frac{\partial^{2}u}{\partial t\partial x}\\[5.0pt]
\frac{\partial^{2}\theta}{\partial x^{2}}\end{bmatrix}(t,x)=i\,\begin{bmatrix}\mu+O(\frac{1}{n^{2}})\\[5.0pt]
-\lambda+O(\frac{1}{n^{2}})\\[5.0pt]
\frac{m\lambda}{\kappa}+O(\frac{1}{n^{2}})\end{bmatrix}\left(1+O\big(\frac{1}{n^{2}}\big)t\right)\exp\Big\{inx-\frac{\lambda}{\mu}t\Big\}

Remark 3.1
.

For the special case of (
3.26
) with
\kappa=0
, one may construct solutions of (
3.9
) with
\kappa=0
for which
oscillations appear to leading order for the vector function
(w,v_{x},\theta)
. This should be contrasted to the behavior
of (

) (which has
\kappa\neq 0
) where oscillations appear to leading order for the vector function
(w,v_{x},\theta_{xx})
.

3.3.
Multi-dimensional linear thermoviscoelasticity

We devise next some analogous solutions for the multidimensional system of linear thermoviscoelasticity

(3.18)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}u_{k}}{\partial t^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{\alpha}}\left(A_{kl\alpha\beta}\frac{\partial u_{l}}{\partial x_{\beta}}+M_{k\alpha}\theta\right)+\mu\Delta\left(\frac{\partial u_{k}}{\partial t}\right)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\theta}{\partial t}

\displaystyle=\kappa\Delta\theta+M_{k\alpha}\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{\alpha}}\left(\frac{\partial u_{k}}{\partial t}\right)

The system describes the evolution of a thermomechanical process
(u,\theta):(0,\infty)\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{+}
. Here, the summation convention is used,
the parameters

4.2.

In preparation, we mention a well known result from continuum mechanics. Let
\psi(x)

\mathcal{R}
is split into two subdomains,
\mathcal{R}=\mathcal{R}^{+}\cup\mathcal{R}^{-}

Suppose that

\mathcal{R}

\psi\in C^{1}\big(\overline{\mathcal{R}^{-}}\big)

\psi\in C^{1}\big(\overline{\mathcal{R}^{+}}\big)

\lim_{\scriptsize\begin{matrix}x\to x_{0}\\

and are finite. Then the deformation gradients satisfy across
\mathcal{S}

\mathcal{S}
. This result is the backbone
in the construction of twinning solutions in elasticity,
[

Consider the system of viscoelasticity of Kelvin-Voigt type,

\displaystyle\partial_{tt}y-\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\Big(\frac{\partial W}{\partial F}(\nabla y)+\nabla y_{t}\Big)=0\,.

It is written as a system of conservation laws expressed in coordinate form, for the quantities

(4.12)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}F_{i\alpha}

\displaystyle=\partial_{\alpha}v_{i}

\displaystyle\partial_{t}v_{i}

\displaystyle=\partial_{\alpha}\Big(\frac{\partial W}{\partial F_{i\alpha}}(F)+\partial_{\alpha}v_{i}\Big)

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\partial_{\beta}F_{i\alpha}-\partial_{\alpha}F_{i\beta}\,.

The last equation constrains
F
to be a gradient. It is an involution, i.e. a constraint that propagates
from the initial data to solutions.

The construction of oscillating solutions will result from two ingredients, a class of uniform shearing solutions
in several dimensions combined with conditions that guarantee joining these solutions across interfaces. This is similar in spirit as the one
dimensional example in section
4.1

We start with the interface conditions.
Suppose that

. Suppose a solution

of (

)
that has smoothness

, and such that

and the right and left limits of

exist and are finite on both sides of the space-time surface
\mathcal{S}
. The limits will satisfy the Rankine-Hugoniot conditions

(4.13)

\displaystyle-s[F_{i\alpha}]

\displaystyle=\nu_{\alpha}[v_{i}]

(4.14)

\displaystyle-s[v_{i}]

\displaystyle=\nu_{\alpha}\left[\frac{\partial W}{\partial F_{i\alpha}}(F)+\partial_{\alpha}v_{i}\right]

(4.15)

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\nu_{\beta}[F_{i\alpha}]-\nu_{\alpha}[F_{i\beta}]

where

Condition (

[F]\tau=0

We are here interested in

[v]=0

The latter precludes

This suggests to consider standing shocks

[

In summary,

where

is the total stress. Observe that (

The second ingredient is special solutions of (

One checks that

We use (

\nabla p(\rho)+\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\Big(\mu(\nabla u+\nabla u^{T})+\lambda(\mathop{\mathrm{div}}u)\,I\,\Big)=0\,,

it follows that
(\rho_{n},u_{n})
in (
5.17
) satisfies the compressible Navier-Stokes system.

When
(\rho_{n},u_{n})
is restricted to
Q=[1,2]\times B_{1}
it provides
u_{n}=\frac{y}{t}
and

(5.18)

\displaystyle\rho_{n}(t,y)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\frac{a}{t^{d}}\quad&\qquad\frac{k}{n}<\frac{|y|}{t}<\frac{k+\theta}{n}\\[5.0pt]
\frac{b}{t^{d}}\quad&\quad\frac{k+\theta}{n}<\frac{|y|}{t}<\frac{k+1}{n}\\
\end{cases}\;,\qquad k=0,1,...,n-1\,.

(\rho_{n},u_{n})
are weak solutions of (
5.13
) with the properties
u_{n}\to u=\frac{y}{t}
strongly while

\rho_{n}\rightharpoonup\rho=\theta\frac{a}{t^{d}}+(1-\theta)\frac{b}{t^{d}}\quad\mbox{ weakly-$\star$ in $L^{\infty}\big(Q\big)$ }

The Young measure associated with the sequence
\rho_{n}
is

\nu=\theta\delta_{\frac{a}{t^{d}}}+(1-\theta)\delta_{\frac{b}{t^{d}}}

The sequence
\{\rho_{n}\}
exhibits persistent oscillations induced by oscillations in the initial data.
While
(\rho,u)
is a solution of compressible Navier-Stokes system, the reader should note that

\{\rho_{n}\}
has the property that

with in general

.

6.
Homogenization for scalar conservation laws: Generalized kinetic solutions

Physical phenomena that involve sustained oscillations are commonplace, with examples appearing in problems of phase transitions,
in multi-phase fluid flows, and are quite widespread in flows associated with fluid instabilities or with turbulence.
In previous sections we gave explicit examples of oscillating solutions for viscoelastic systems describing phase transitions
and for the compressible Navier-Stokes with non-monotone pressures.
It raises the issue of devising equations describing the effective response of persistent oscillations.
The problem goes under the name nonlinear homogenization and is a difficult problem that is
open in most contexts. In simple cases that involve only one oscillating variable it can be addressed
and presenting such examples is our next task.

We start with a review of the generalized kinetic solutions that pertain to
the problem of oscillations for scalar conservation laws.
This is followed, in the next section, by effective equations for oscillating solutions in a one-dimensional hyperbolic-parabolic
system modeling phase transitions. Finally, in section
8
, we outline two approaches for the calculation of effective equations
for the homogenization problem of the compressible Navier-Stokes system with non-monotone pressures.

6.1.
Kinetic formulation of scalar conservation laws

Consider the scalar conservation law for simplicity in one dimension,

(6.1)

\partial_{t}u+\partial_{x}f(u)=0\,,

where
u(t,x)
is a scalar-valued function and
f(\cdot)
smooth. Smooth solutions for the Cauchy problem break down in finite time,
and for global solvability it is necessary to consider weak solutions involving shocks. It is then known that in order to guarantee
uniqueness one needs to exploit the nonlinear structure of the problem.

For scalar conservation laws, this is achieved using the notion of entropy solutions introduced by Kruzhkov.
We recall
\eta-q
is an entropy - entropy flux pair if they satisfy the relation

q^{\prime}(\xi)=f^{\prime}(\xi)\eta^{\prime}(\xi)

where
\lambda(\xi)=f^{\prime}(\xi)
is the wave speed. Given
\eta-q
any smooth solution of (
6.1
)
satisfies the additional conservation laws

\partial_{t}\eta(u)+\partial_{x}q(u)=0\,.

Entropy pairs serve to introduce the notion of Kruzhkov entropy solution, by requiring that a weak solution
u(t,x)

satisfies the entropy inequalities

(6.2)

\partial_{t}\eta(u)+\partial_{x}q(u)\leq 0\quad\mbox{$\forall$ entropy pairs $\eta-q$ with $\eta(u)$ convex}.

This notion is sufficient to guarantee uniqueness of solutions for scalar conservation laws in various contexts,
e.g.

[
9
]
.

The kinetic formulation for conservation laws provides an equivalent notion of solutions introduced by Lions-Perthame-Tadmor
[
27
]
.
The notion is based on representation of the entropy pairs
\eta-q
via the formulas

\displaystyle\eta(u)-\eta(0)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{u}\eta^{\prime}(\xi)d\xi

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\;\;\int_{\mathbb{R}}{{\text{\rm 1}}\kern-2.40005pt{\text{\rm l}}}_{0<\xi<u}\eta^{\prime}(\xi)d\xi&u>0\\
\int_{\mathbb{R}}-{{\text{\rm 1}}\kern-2.40005pt{\text{\rm l}}}_{u<\xi<0}\eta^{\prime}(\xi)d\xi&u<0\end{cases}

(6.3)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\chi(\xi,u)\eta^{\prime}(\xi)d\xi

(6.4)

\displaystyle q(u)-q(0)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\lambda(\xi)\chi(\xi,u)\eta^{\prime}(\xi)d\xi

The ”kinetic function”
\chi(\xi,u)

(6.5)

\chi(\xi,u)=\begin{cases}\;\;{{\text{\rm 1}}\kern-2.40005pt{\text{\rm l}}}_{0<\xi<u}&\mbox{for}\;u>0\\
-{{\text{\rm 1}}\kern-2.40005pt{\text{\rm l}}}_{u<\xi<0}&\mbox{for}\;u<0\\
\end{cases}

serves as the kernel representing all entropy pairs.

Theorem 6.1
.

[
27
]

Let
u(t,x)\in L^{\infty}((0,T)\times\mathbb{T}^{1})
. Then
u
satisfies (
6.2
) if and only if there is a positive, bounded measure
m

such that

(6.6)

\partial_{t}\chi(\xi,u)+\lambda(\xi)\partial_{x}\chi(\xi,u)=\partial_{\xi}m

in distributions.

Proof.

We only show that the Kruzhkov entropy solution imples (
6.6
). Using the representation formulas
for the entropies one may express

\partial_{t}\eta(u)+\partial_{x}q(u)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\Big(\partial_{t}\chi(\xi,u)+\lambda(\xi)\partial_{x}\chi(\xi,u)\Big)\eta^{\prime}(\xi)d\xi\leq 0

for any entropy pair
\eta-q
with
\eta
convex. If we define
\partial_{\xi}m:=\partial_{t}\chi(\xi,u)+\lambda(\xi)\partial_{x}\chi(\xi,u)

then we have

\langle\partial_{\xi}m,\eta^{\prime}(\xi)\rangle=-\langle m,\eta^{\prime\prime}(\xi)\rangle\leq 0\quad\forall\;\eta^{\prime\prime}\geq 0

This means that the distribution
m
is positive and thus a positive measure.
∎

The notion of the kinetic formulation has been extended to the compressible Euler system in one space dimension

[
28
]
as well as for the system of one-dimensional elasticity
[
35
]
. The main tool is always a representation formula
for the convex entropies. For systems, the kinetic function is associated to the Riemann function for the equation generating the entropies,
and the system that produces the kinetic formulation generally involves the presence of macroscopic moments.

6.2.
The emergence of generalized kinetic solutions from viscous limits of scalar conservation laws

The weak limits of kinetic functions can be used as a tool for deriving effective equations describing oscillations, see

[
35
]
,
[
34
, Ch 4]
. We outline the method
by introducing a kinetic description for viscous limits following the approach in
[

,
6
]

Let
\{u^{n}\}
be a sequence of approximate solutions to the initial value problem for a scalar conservation law

(6.7)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}u^{n}+\partial_{x}f(u^{n})

\displaystyle=\varepsilon_{n}\partial^{2}_{x}u^{n}

\displaystyle u^{n}(x,0)

\displaystyle=u_{0}^{n}(x)\,,

where
u^{n}:(0,T)\times\mathbb{T}^{1}\to\mathbb{R}
are scalar valued
and periodic and

is a small parameter converging to zero.
The entropy pairs
\eta(u)-q(u)
associated to the scalar conservation law
satisfy the entropy equation

(6.8)

\partial_{t}\eta(u^{n})+\partial_{x}q(u^{n})=\partial^{2}_{x}(\varepsilon_{n}\eta(u^{n}))-\varepsilon_{n}\eta^{\prime\prime}(u^{n})(\partial_{x}u^{n})^{2}\,.

The solutions
\{u^{n}\}
of the initial value problem (

\displaystyle\sup_{x\in\mathbb{T}^{1}}|u^{n}(x,t)|

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{x\in\mathbb{T}^{1}}|u^{n}_{0}(x)|

(6.10)

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{T}^{1}}\tfrac{1}{2}|u^{n}|^{2}(x,t)dx+\varepsilon_{n}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{1}}

\displaystyle|\partial_{x}u^{n}|^{2}\,dxd\tau\leq\int_{\mathbb{T}^{1}}\tfrac{1}{2}|u_{0}^{n}|^{2}(x)dx\,.

We assume the initial data are uniformly bounded in

and seek to derive an equation that describes
the effective response of the sequence of oscillating solutions as

tends to zero. (Note, we have not assumed any genuine

For simplicity of the exposition we assume the initial data are positive,

is nonnegative and bounded in

\displaystyle u^{n}

\displaystyle\rightharpoonup u\quad\mbox{wk-$\ast$ in $L^{\infty}_{t,x}$}

\displaystyle{{\text{\rm 1}}\kern-2.40005pt{\text{\rm l}}}_{u^{n}<\xi}

\displaystyle\rightharpoonup F\quad\mbox{wk-$\ast$ in $L^{\infty}_{t,x,\xi}$}\,.

The representation theory of weak limits via Young measures

that along a further subsequence represents the weak limits

for any

continuous. By (

), the support of the Young measure verifies

It is instructive to explore the relation between

Let

and consider the class of entropies

Then

is continuous and for some

and

for

.
Using (

g(u^{n})=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\theta(\xi)(1-{{\text{\rm 1}}\kern-2.40005pt{\text{\rm l}}}_{u^{n}<\xi})\,d\xi\rightharpoonup\int_{\mathbb{R}}\theta(\xi)(1-F(t,x,\xi))\,d\xi

weak-

in

for

.

By comparing (

) and (

) we deduce

\int g(\lambda)\,d\nu_{t,x}(\lambda)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}g^{\prime}(\xi)(1-F(t,x,\xi))\,d\xi

for functions

with

and

for

. If we interpret

to be the

and the formula can be deduced directly
by the integration by parts formula

. Therefore

can be thought of as the distribution function of the Young measure.

Next, the entropy dissipation is expressed via a single equation as follows. Consider entropies
\eta\in C^{1}_{c}(\mathbb{R})
with compact support
and interpret

as a test function. Using the representation formulas, we have

\displaystyle\eta(u^{n})

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\eta^{\prime}(\xi)(1-{{\text{\rm 1}}\kern-2.40005pt{\text{\rm l}}}_{u^{n}<\xi})d\xi

\displaystyle q(u^{n})

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\eta^{\prime}(\xi)\lambda(\xi)(1-{{\text{\rm 1}}\kern-2.40005pt{\text{\rm l}}}_{u^{n}<\xi})d\xi

Observe that formally we may write

\displaystyle\eta^{\prime\prime}(u^{n})\varepsilon_{n}(\partial_{x}u^{n})^{2}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\eta^{\prime\prime}(\xi)\varepsilon_{n}(\partial_{x}u^{n})^{2}\delta(\xi-u^{n})d\xi

\displaystyle=-\left\langle\eta^{\prime}(\xi),\partial_{\xi}\left(\varepsilon_{n}(\partial_{x}u^{n})^{2}\delta(\xi-u^{n})\right)\right\rangle

To make this rigorous, note the estimate (

) implies the quantity

.
We wish to define

as a distribution in

by its action on tensor products

\langle\delta(\xi-u^{n})G^{{\varepsilon_{n}}},\varphi\otimes\eta^{\prime}\rangle=\int_{x,t}G^{{\varepsilon_{n}}}(x,t)\varphi(x,t)\eta^{\prime}(u^{n}(x,t))dxdt\,.

This follows from the Schwartz kernel theorem: Define the linear map

If

in

then

in
{\mathcal{D}}^{\prime}_{t,x}
. The kernel theorem implies

is well defined as a distribution
in
{\mathcal{D}}^{\prime}_{t,x,\xi}
and acts on tensor products via (

Moreover,

(6.16)

\langle\partial_{\xi}\delta(\xi-u^{n})G^{{\varepsilon_{n}}},\varphi\otimes\eta^{\prime}\rangle=-\int_{x,t}G^{{\varepsilon_{n}}}(x,t)\varphi(x,t)\eta^{\prime\prime}(u^{n}(x,t))dxdt\,.

Thus (

\displaystyle\Big\langle\partial_{t}(1-{{\text{\rm 1}}\kern-2.40005pt{\text{\rm l}}}_{u^{n}<\xi})+\lambda(\xi)\partial_{x}(1-{{\text{\rm 1}}\kern-2.40005pt{\text{\rm l}}}_{u^{n}<\xi})-\varepsilon_{n}\partial^{2}_{x}(1-{{\text{\rm 1}}\kern-2.40005pt{\text{\rm l}}}_{u^{n}<\xi})\,,\,\eta^{\prime}(\xi)\varphi(x,t)\Big\rangle

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad=\Big\langle\partial_{\xi}\big(\delta(\xi-u^{n})G^{{\varepsilon_{n}}}\big)\,,\,\eta^{\prime}(\xi)\varphi(x,t)\Big\rangle.

Since the subspace generated by the direct sum test functions

is dense in

,
the bracket (

. So, the equation (

of the form

\partial_{t}(1-{{\text{\rm 1}}\kern-2.40005pt{\text{\rm l}}}_{u^{n}<\xi})+\partial_{x}\lambda(\xi)(1-{{\text{\rm 1}}\kern-2.40005pt{\text{\rm l}}}_{u^{n}<\xi})=\varepsilon_{n}\partial^{2}_{x}(1-{{\text{\rm 1}}\kern-2.40005pt{\text{\rm l}}}_{u^{n}<\xi})+\partial_{\xi}m^{n}

where we have set

. Now

is a positive measure and in view of (

is uniformly bounded in measures and along a subsequence

m^{n}\to m\qquad\mbox{weak-$\ast$ in measures}

where

is a positive bounded measure.
Passing to the limit, we conclude that

satisfies in the sense of distributions the initial value problem

(6.18)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}(1-F)+\partial_{x}\lambda(\xi)(1-F)

\displaystyle=\partial_{\xi}m

\displaystyle F(0,x,\xi)

\displaystyle=\mbox{wk-$\ast$ lim}{{\text{\rm 1}}\kern-2.40005pt{\text{\rm l}}}_{u_{0}^{n}(x)<\xi}\,.

Remark 6.2
.

The equation (

) should not be confused with (

) the kinetic formulation for a scalar conservation law.
The latter is an equation whose solution is constrained to be of the form

in (

For positive solutions this reduces to

. In the effective equation

is nondecreasing with

associated to a measure valued entropy solution.
Perthame called these solutions generalized kinetic solutions and has provided a proof of the DiPerna uniqueness theorem for
entropic measure-valued solutions using this concept, see
[

, Thm 4.3.1]

6.3.
Representation of weak limits for nonlinear functions via the distribution function of the Young measure

The Young measures (or parametrized measures) are the standard tool used in the representation of weak limits.
Their theory is traced back to an idea of L.C. Young that was systematized in a functional analysis framework in the
pioneering work of L. Tartar
[
38
]

[
3
]
and Perdregal

32

for systematic

Borel measures on

are in correspondence with their associated distribution functions,

[

Due to this property, oscillations of real-valued sequences

It leads to a simpler object for the representation of weak limits, which avoids the difficulties encountered in
[
3

for dealing with weak topologies on measures and is capable to describe the dynamics of oscillations.

In this subsection we review some of the technical aspects expanding on material from
[

]

Consider a sequence

\partial_{t}b(\rho^{n})+\mathrm{div}\big(b(\rho^{n})u^{n}\big)+\big(\rho^{n}b^{\prime}(\rho^{n})-b(\rho^{n})\big)\mathrm{div}u^{n}=0

is expressed in the form

understood in the sense of distributions
{\mathcal{D}}^{\prime}(Q_{T})
, using the effective viscous flux

(8.11)

z^{n}=p(\rho^{n})-(\lambda+2\mu)\mathrm{div}(u^{n})\,.

The equation (
8.10

\theta(\xi)\in C^{1}_{c}(\mathbb{R})
and consider functions

b(\rho)
defined by

b(\rho)=\int_{-\infty}^{\rho}\theta(\xi)d\xi=\int_{\mathbb{R}}{{\text{\rm 1}}\kern-2.40005pt{\text{\rm l}}}_{\xi<\rho}\theta(\xi)d\xi=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\big(1-{{\text{\rm 1}}\kern-2.40005pt{\text{\rm l}}}_{\rho<\xi}\big)\theta(\xi)d\xi

Note that
\theta=b^{\prime}\in C_{c}^{1}(\mathbb{R})
and that if
{\rm supp\,}\theta\subset[-m,m]
then
b(\rho)=0
for
\rho<-m
and
b(\rho)=const
for
\rho>m
.

The goal is to represent the last two terms in (
8.10
). Consider the first term
I^{n}_{1}
and observe
that at least formally

\displaystyle I_{1}^{n}:=\big(\rho^{n}b^{\prime}(\rho^{n})-b(\rho^{n})\big)z^{n}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}(\xi b^{\prime}(\xi)-b(\xi))\delta(\xi-\rho^{n})\,z^{n}d\xi

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}(\xi b^{\prime}(\xi)-b(\xi))\big(\partial_{\xi}({{\text{\rm 1}}\kern-2.40005pt{\text{\rm l}}}_{\rho^{n}<\xi}-1)\big)z^{n}d\xi

\displaystyle=-\int_{\mathbb{R}}\xi b^{\prime\prime}(\xi)\big({{\text{\rm 1}}\kern-2.40005pt{\text{\rm l}}}_{\rho^{n}<\xi}-1\big)z^{n}d\xi

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}b^{\prime}(\xi)\partial_{\xi}\Big(\xi({{\text{\rm 1}}\kern-2.40005pt{\text{\rm l}}}_{\rho^{n}<\xi}-1)\,z^{n}\Big)d\xi

To check the validity of this equation and interpret it appropriately as a distribution we express
b(\xi)
in terms of

\theta(\xi)=b^{\prime}(\xi)
and write

\displaystyle I_{1}^{n}

\displaystyle=\big(\rho^{n}\theta(\rho^{n})-\int_{-\infty}^{\rho^{n}}\theta(s)ds\big)z^{n}

\displaystyle=\Big(\int_{-\infty}^{\rho^{n}}s\theta^{\prime}(s)ds\Big)z^{n}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}{{\text{\rm 1}}\kern-2.40005pt{\text{\rm l}}}_{s<\rho^{n}}(s\theta^{\prime}(s))z^{n}\,ds

\displaystyle=\big\langle\theta^{\prime}(\xi),\xi{{\text{\rm 1}}\kern-2.40005pt{\text{\rm l}}}_{\xi<\rho^{n}}z^{n}\big\rangle

\displaystyle=\Big\langle\theta(\xi),-\partial_{\xi}\big(\xi\big(1-{{\text{\rm 1}}\kern-2.40005pt{\text{\rm l}}}_{\rho^{n}<\xi}\big)z^{n}\big)\Big\rangle

where the last two formulas are interpreted in distributions.

Consider next the term
I_{2}^{n}
given by

\displaystyle I_{2}^{n}

\displaystyle:=\big(\rho^{n}b^{\prime}(\rho^{n})-b(\rho^{n})\big)p(\rho^{n})

\displaystyle=\big(\rho^{n}\theta(\rho^{n})-\int_{-\infty}^{\rho^{n}}\theta(s)ds\big)p(\rho^{n})

\displaystyle=\Big(\int_{-\infty}^{\rho^{n}}s\theta^{\prime}(s)ds\Big)p(\rho^{n})

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}{{\text{\rm 1}}\kern-2.40005pt{\text{\rm l}}}_{\tau<\rho^{n}}\frac{d}{d\tau}\Big[\Big(\int_{-\infty}^{\tau}s\theta^{\prime}(s)ds\Big)p(\tau)\Big]d\tau

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\theta^{\prime}(\tau)\tau p(\tau)\big(1-{{\text{\rm 1}}\kern-2.40005pt{\text{\rm l}}}_{\rho^{n}<\tau}\big)d\tau+\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}}{{\text{\rm 1}}\kern-2.40005pt{\text{\rm l}}}_{s<\tau}s\theta^{\prime}(s)ds\right)p^{\prime}(\tau)\big(1-{{\text{\rm 1}}\kern-2.40005pt{\text{\rm l}}}_{\rho^{n}<\tau}\big)d\tau

The term

is expressed as a distribution

\displaystyle J_{1}^{n}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\theta^{\prime}(\xi)\,\xi p(\xi)\big(1-{{\text{\rm 1}}\kern-2.40005pt{\text{\rm l}}}_{\rho^{n}<\xi}\big)\,d\xi

\displaystyle=-\Big\langle\theta(\xi),\partial_{\xi}\Big(\xi p(\xi)\big(1-{{\text{\rm 1}}\kern-2.40005pt{\text{\rm l}}}_{\rho^{n}<\xi}\big)\Big)\Big\rangle

Similarly, the term
J_{2}^{n}
gives

\displaystyle J_{2}^{n}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\theta^{\prime}(\xi)\,\xi\left(\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}{{\text{\rm 1}}\kern-2.40005pt{\text{\rm l}}}_{\xi<\zeta}{{\text{\rm 1}}\kern-2.40005pt{\text{\rm l}}}_{\zeta<\rho^{n}}p^{\prime}(\zeta)\,d\zeta\right)d\xi

\displaystyle=-\Big\langle\theta(\xi),\partial_{\xi}\Big(\xi\int_{\xi}^{\infty}p^{\prime}(\zeta)\big(1-{{\text{\rm 1}}\kern-2.40005pt{\text{\rm l}}}_{\rho^{n}<\zeta}\big)d\zeta\Big)\Big\rangle

Combining the two formulas we have

I_{2}^{n}=-\Big\langle\theta(\xi),\partial_{\xi}\left(\xi p(\xi)\big(1-{{\text{\rm 1}}\kern-2.40005pt{\text{\rm l}}}_{\rho^{n}<\xi}\big)+\xi\int_{\xi}^{\infty}p^{\prime}(\zeta)\big(1-{{\text{\rm 1}}\kern-2.40005pt{\text{\rm l}}}_{\rho^{n}<\zeta}\big)d\zeta\right)\Big\rangle

We conclude that for
\theta\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R})
we have

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\theta(\xi)\big(1-{{\text{\rm 1}}\kern-2.40005pt{\text{\rm l}}}_{\rho^{n}<\xi}\big)d\xi+\mathrm{div}_{x}\Big(\int_{\mathbb{R}}\theta(\xi)u^{n}\big(1-{{\text{\rm 1}}\kern-2.40005pt{\text{\rm l}}}_{\rho^{n}<\xi}\big)d\xi\Big)

\displaystyle+\tfrac{1}{\lambda+2\mu}\big\langle\theta(\xi),\partial_{\xi}\big(\xi\big(1-{{\text{\rm 1}}\kern-2.40005pt{\text{\rm l}}}_{\rho^{n}<\xi}\big)z^{n}\big)\Big\rangle

in distributions

. The formula can be explicitly expressed for a test function

in
the form

\displaystyle-\iint\int_{\mathbb{R}}(\partial_{t}\varphi)\theta\big(1-{{\text{\rm 1}}\kern-2.40005pt{\text{\rm l}}}_{\rho^{n}<\xi}\big)+(\nabla\varphi)\theta\cdot u^{n}\big(1-{{\text{\rm 1}}\kern-2.40005pt{\text{\rm l}}}_{\rho^{n}<\xi}\big)

\displaystyle-\iint\int_{\mathbb{R}}\varphi(\partial_{\xi}\theta)\tfrac{1}{\lambda+2\mu}\big(\xi\big(1-{{\text{\rm 1}}\kern-2.40005pt{\text{\rm l}}}_{\rho^{n}<\xi}\big)z^{n}\big)

\displaystyle+\iint\int_{\mathbb{R}}\varphi(\partial_{\xi}\theta)\tfrac{1}{\lambda+2\mu}\Big(\xi p(\xi)\big(1-{{\text{\rm 1}}\kern-2.40005pt{\text{\rm l}}}_{\rho^{n}<\xi}\big)+\xi\int_{\xi}^{\infty}p^{\prime}(\zeta)\big(1-{{\text{\rm 1}}\kern-2.40005pt{\text{\rm l}}}_{\rho^{n}<\zeta}\big)d\zeta\Big)=0

An argument as in the derivation of (

{{\text{\rm 1}}\kern-2.40005pt{\text{\rm l}}}_{\rho^{n}<\xi}

8.3.
An alternative approach to obtain an effective kinetic equation

In this second approach we introduce functions
b(\rho)=\rho g(\rho)
and use
g^{\prime}(\rho)\in C_{c}^{1}(\mathbb{R})
as a test function.
The equation for the renormalized approximate solutions (
8.6
) is expressed in the form

\partial_{t}\big(\rho^{n}g(\rho^{n})\big)+\mathrm{div}\big(u^{n}\rho^{n}g(\rho^{n})\big)+(\rho^{n})^{2}g^{\prime}(\rho^{n})\mathrm{div}u^{n}=0

In turn, with the objective to exploit the special properties of the effective viscous flux (
8.11
), this is written in the form

(8.13)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\big(\rho^{n}g(\rho^{n})\big)+\mathrm{div}\big(u^{n}\rho^{n}g(\rho^{n})\big)

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{\lambda+2\mu}(\rho^{n})^{2}g^{\prime}(\rho^{n})z^{n}-\tfrac{1}{\lambda+2\mu}(\rho^{n})^{2}g^{\prime}(\rho^{n})p(\rho^{n})

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{\lambda+2\mu}I_{1}^{n}-\tfrac{1}{\lambda+2\mu}I_{2}^{n}

We next use the representation formula

g(\rho)=\int_{-\infty}^{\rho}g^{\prime}(\xi)d\xi=\int_{\mathbb{R}}g^{\prime}(\xi){{\text{\rm 1}}\kern-2.40005pt{\text{\rm l}}}_{\xi<\rho}d\xi\,.

We will think of
g^{\prime}\in C_{c}^{1}(\mathbb{R})
as a test function. Note that for
{\rm supp\,}g^{\prime}\subset[-m,m]
the associated
g
satisfy

g(\rho)=0
for
\rho<-m
and
g(\rho)=const
for
\rho>m
.

Along sequences of solutions
\{\rho^{n}\}
we have

\rho^{n}g(\rho^{n})=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\rho^{n}{{\text{\rm 1}}\kern-2.40005pt{\text{\rm l}}}_{\xi<\rho^{n}}g^{\prime}(\xi)d\xi

Define now the function
H(t,x,\xi)
to be the weak limit of

(8.14)

\rho^{n}{{\text{\rm 1}}\kern-2.40005pt{\text{\rm l}}}_{\rho^{n}<\xi}\rightharpoonup H

If the measure
H
is absolutely continuous with respect to
\rho dx
then we can visualize

H=\rho G
where
G(t,x,\xi)
is the Radon-Nikodym derivative.

The goal is to derive an approximate equation for the quantity
\rho^{n}{{\text{\rm 1}}\kern-2.40005pt{\text{\rm l}}}_{\xi<\rho^{n}}
and to use the function

as the quantity describing the effective oscillations.
In order to express (
8.13
), observe that at least formally

and

may be expressed via the representation formulas

\displaystyle I_{1}^{n}=(\rho^{n})^{2}g^{\prime}(\rho^{n})z^{n}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\xi g^{\prime}(\xi)\rho^{n}\delta(\xi-\rho^{n})z^{n}\,d\xi

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}g^{\prime}(\xi)\Big(-\xi\partial_{\xi}\big(\rho^{n}{{\text{\rm 1}}\kern-2.40005pt{\text{\rm l}}}_{\xi<\rho^{n}}\big)z^{n}\Big)\,d\xi

\displaystyle I_{2}^{n}=(\rho^{n})^{2}g^{\prime}(\rho^{n})p(\rho^{n})

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\rho^{n}\xi g^{\prime}(\xi)p(\xi)\partial_{\xi}\big(-{{\text{\rm 1}}\kern-2.40005pt{\text{\rm l}}}_{\xi<\rho^{n}}\big)\,d\xi

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}g^{\prime}(\xi)\Big(-\xi p(\xi)\partial_{\xi}\big(\rho^{n}{{\text{\rm 1}}\kern-2.40005pt{\text{\rm l}}}_{\xi<\rho^{n}}\big)\Big)\,d\xi

To validate the meaning of the first formula use the representation

\rho g^{\prime}(\rho)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}{{\text{\rm 1}}\kern-2.40005pt{\text{\rm l}}}_{\xi<\rho}\big(\xi g^{\prime}\big)^{\prime}d\xi

to interpret

as a distribution via the formulas

\displaystyle(\rho^{n})^{2}g^{\prime}(\rho^{n})z^{n}

\displaystyle=\big\langle g^{\prime}(\xi),-\xi\partial_{\xi}\big(\rho^{n}{{\text{\rm 1}}\kern-2.40005pt{\text{\rm l}}}_{\xi<\rho^{n}}z^{n}\big)\big\rangle

Similarly, for

smooth,

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}}g^{\prime}\rho^{n}{{\text{\rm 1}}\kern-2.40005pt{\text{\rm l}}}_{\xi<\rho^{n}}d\xi\right)+\mathrm{div}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}}g^{\prime}u^{n}\rho^{n}{{\text{\rm 1}}\kern-2.40005pt{\text{\rm l}}}_{\xi<\rho^{n}}d\xi\right)
