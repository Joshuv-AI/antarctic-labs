Association of concomitant congenital anomalies with long-term renal outcomes in children with posterior urethral valves
Authors: Dilşad Dereli, Baran Tokar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPosterior urethral valves (PUV) are the most common cause of congenital bladder outlet obstruction in boys and may result in long-term impairment of both bladder and renal function. In addition to the obstructive process itself, concomitant congenital anomalies may further influence renal outcome. This study aimed to evaluate the spectrum of associated congenital anomalies in children with PUV and to examine their relationship with clinically significant chronic kidney disease (CKD) during follow-up.MethodsThis retrospective single-center observational study included 103 boys with cystoscopically confirmed PUV who were diagnosed and followed at the Pediatric Urology Clinic of Eskişehir Osmangazi University Faculty of Medicine between January 2014 and December 2024. Patients with incomplete records or follow-up shorter than 12 months were excluded. Demographic characteristics, antenatal findings, presenting symptoms, vesicoureteral reflux (VUR), associated anomalies, imaging findings, interventions, bladder assessment findings, and renal outcomes were reviewed. Descriptive statistical analysis was performed. Clinically significant CKD was defined as persistent estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) < 90 mL/min/1.73 m² for more than 3 months, corresponding to CKD stage ≥ 2.ResultsThe median age at diagnosis was 2.4 years. Antenatal hydronephrosis and/or hydroureteronephrosis was present in 42 patients (40.8%), and concomitant VUR was detected in 39 (37.9%). Associated anomalies included hypospadias in 7 patients (6.8%), ureteropelvic junction obstruction in 7 (6.8%), renal hypodysplasia in 5 (4.9%), spina bifida in 5 (4.9%), anorectal malformation in 4 (3.9%), and trisomy 21 in 1 (1.0%). During follow-up, 8 patients (7.8%) developed CKD stage ≥ 2. Most of these patients had antenatal hydronephrosis, high-grade VUR, dysplastic or non-functioning kidneys, or additional congenital anomalies.ConclusionsIn this cohort, clinically significant CKD developed in a minority of children with PUV, but adverse renal outcome was concentrated in patients with severe upper urinary tract abnormalities and additional congenital anomalies. High-grade VUR, reduced functional renal mass, and complex associated anomalies appear to be important markers of poor renal prognosis. Background

Posterior urethral valves (PUV) are the most common cause of congenital bladder outlet obstruction in boys, although they remain relatively uncommon in the general population. Their clinical importance lies not only in lower urinary tract obstruction but also in their potential to cause long-term renal and bladder dysfunction. Despite improvements in prenatal ultrasonography, endoscopic treatment, and follow-up strategies, a substantial proportion of patients remain at risk for chronic kidney disease (CKD) later in childhood and adolescence [1, 2].

Renal injury in PUV often begins in utero. Persistent obstruction may lead to increased intravesical pressure, upper urinary tract dilatation, vesicoureteral reflux (VUR), impaired nephrogenesis, and renal dysplasia. Therefore, renal outcome is influenced not only by the degree of infravesical obstruction but also by associated abnormalities affecting renal parenchymal development, urinary drainage, and bladder function.

Among the accompanying urinary tract findings in PUV, VUR is particularly important. In some patients, reflux may reflect secondary high-pressure bladder dynamics related to obstruction; in others, a coexisting abnormality of the vesicoureteric junction may also contribute. In addition, associated congenital anomalies and syndromic conditions, such as hypospadias and chromosomal disorders, may further complicate the clinical course and renal prognosis [3–5].

Most previous reports have focused on the general clinical spectrum of PUV or on isolated associated conditions. The present study was designed to describe the spectrum of concomitant congenital anomalies in children with PUV and to assess whether these findings were more common among patients who developed clinically significant CKD during follow-up.

Methods

Aim, design, and setting

This retrospective single-center observational study was conducted at the Pediatric Urology Clinic of Eskişehir Osmangazi University Faculty of Medicine. Medical records of boys diagnosed with PUV between January 2014 and December 2024 were reviewed. Ethical approval was obtained from the Institutional Review Board of Eskişehir Osmangazi University Faculty of Medicine (Date: 02.05.2024; Decision No: 45). The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Participants

Patients were eligible for inclusion if they were 18 years of age or younger at diagnosis, had PUV confirmed by cystoscopy, and had sufficient clinical, imaging, and follow-up data available for analysis. Patients with incomplete medical records or follow-up shorter than 12 months were excluded. All consecutive eligible patients within the study period were included.

Diagnostic evaluation and data collection

The diagnosis of PUV was established by voiding cystourethrography (VCUG) and confirmed by endoscopic visualization in all patients. In children with conditions such as spina bifida, in whom functional urethral dilatation might mimic obstruction on VCUG, the diagnosis was accepted only after direct cystoscopic confirmation of valve leaflets.

The following variables were extracted from the medical records: age at diagnosis; antenatal hydronephrosis and/or hydroureteronephrosis; presenting symptoms; recurrent urinary tract infection (UTI); associated congenital anomalies and syndromes; radiological findings; presence and grade of VUR; type and timing of interventions; baseline serum creatinine; estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR); bladder assessment findings; and long-term renal outcome.

Definitions

Baseline renal function was assessed using serum creatinine and bedside Schwartz formula-based eGFR at diagnosis [6]. For outcome analysis, clinically significant CKD was defined as persistent eGFR < 90 mL/min/1.73 m² for more than 3 months, corresponding to CKD stage ≥ 2 [7]. Because some children may have structural kidney and urinary tract abnormalities despite preserved eGFR at baseline, the term development of CKD stage ≥ 2 was used throughout the manuscript instead of “progression of CKD”.

Renal hypodysplasia was diagnosed by ultrasonography in the presence of a small kidney (length < − 2 SD for age), increased echogenicity, loss of corticomedullary differentiation, and/or cystic change. In patients who underwent renal scintigraphy, differential renal function < 20% was considered supportive of severe hypodysplasia. In children with recurrent febrile UTIs, acquired renal scarring was also considered in the differential diagnosis, and final classification was based on the overall imaging pattern and clinical history.

Bladder assessment and follow-up

Bladder evaluation during follow-up included serial ultrasonography, post-void residual urine assessment, uroflowmetry when age-appropriate, and urodynamic studies in selected patients with persistent lower urinary tract symptoms, recurrent UTIs, elevated residual urine, worsening upper tract dilatation, or suspected poor bladder compliance. Bladder parameters considered clinically representative included bladder capacity, compliance, detrusor overactivity, and post-void residual urine. Anticholinergic treatment and clean intermittent catheterization were initiated when clinically indicated according to these findings.

Statistical analysis

Patients were grouped according to the presence or absence of concomitant VUR. Antenatal hydronephrosis was analyzed separately as an independent clinical variable. Because only eight patients reached the renal outcome of interest, the analysis was primarily descriptive. Categorical variables are presented as number and percentage, and continuous variables as median and range where available.

As this was a retrospective observational study including all eligible patients during the study period, no randomization, blinding, or prospective sample size calculation was applicable.

Results

A total of 1750 pediatric urology records were screened during the study period. Among these, 116 boys with a diagnosis of PUV were identified. Of these, 8 patients were excluded because of incomplete medical records and 5 because follow-up was shorter than 12 months. The final cohort consisted of 103 boys with cystoscopically confirmed PUV.

The median age at diagnosis was 2.4 years (range, 5 days to 15 years). The median follow-up duration was 5 years. Baseline serum creatinine at diagnosis was 0.7 mg/dL, and baseline eGFR was 110 mL/min/1.73 m².

No patient underwent antenatal intervention. Antenatal hydronephrosis and/or hydroureteronephrosis was present in 42 patients (40.8%). Fifteen patients (14.6%) were diagnosed after presenting with interrupted urinary stream and/or straining to void, and 21 (20.4%) were diagnosed during evaluation for recurrent UTIs. Discussion

In the present cohort, clinically significant CKD developed in 8 of 103 children (7.8%) with PUV. Although this proportion is lower than that reported in some previous series, renal impairment was not distributed randomly across the cohort. Rather, adverse renal outcome appeared to cluster in patients with antenatal hydronephrosis, high-grade VUR, dysplastic or non-functioning kidneys, and additional congenital anomalies.

These findings support the view that long-term renal outcome in PUV is determined not only by the presence of bladder outlet obstruction itself but also by associated abnormalities that affect nephron endowment, upper tract drainage, and bladder dynamics. In particular, high-grade VUR deserves careful interpretation. In some children, it likely reflects the consequences of secondary high-pressure bladder physiology; in others, it may coexist with an abnormal vesicoureteric junction. Regardless of the underlying mechanism, high-grade reflux was common among the patients in our series who later developed CKD stage ≥ 2. This observation is in line with earlier reports suggesting that severe reflux and reduced functional renal mass identify a subgroup at higher renal risk [8, 9].

Associated congenital anomalies were observed in a notable proportion of our cohort. Hypospadias, ureteropelvic junction obstruction, spina bifida, anorectal malformation, and trisomy 21 were among the principal accompanying conditions. Some of these associations, particularly hypospadias and chromosomal anomalies, have previously been described in observational reports and case-based literature [3–5]. In our cohort, the presence of non-urological or syndromic anomalies did not by itself establish causality, but such conditions were overrepresented among patients with poor renal outcome. This may reflect a broader developmental disturbance, more complex bladder dysfunction, recurrent infection, reduced renal reserve, or a combination of these factors.

The relationship between bladder dysfunction and renal deterioration in PUV is well recognized. For this reason, bladder assessment and bladder-directed treatment formed an important part of follow-up in our center. However, because this was a retrospective study and not all patients underwent the same investigations at the same intervals, the present analysis was not designed to quantify the independent effect of specific bladder parameters on renal outcome.

One notable finding in our cohort was that adverse renal outcome was often already foreshadowed by early anatomical abnormalities, particularly antenatal hydronephrosis, dysplastic kidneys, or non-functioning renal units. This supports the concept that a substantial component of renal injury in PUV is congenital or begins very early in life, before definitive treatment of the obstruction. Subsequent bladder dysfunction, infection, and ongoing pressure-related damage may then add to this baseline vulnerability.

This study has several limitations. Its retrospective design limits control over the uniformity of imaging, laboratory testing, and bladder evaluation. The number of patients who developed CKD stage ≥ 2 was small, precluding robust inferential statistical analysis. Follow-up duration was not identical across all patients, and some associated anomalies were represented by very small numbers. In addition, this was a single-center descriptive cohort, and the findings should be interpreted accordingly. Nevertheless, the study also has strengths, including a relatively large single-center pediatric cohort, cystoscopic confirmation of diagnosis in all cases, and a clinically focused analysis of associated anomalies in relation to renal outcome.

Conclusions

In this retrospective cohort of children with posterior urethral valves, clinically significant CKD developed in 7.8% of patients during follow-up. Adverse renal outcome was seen predominantly in patients with antenatal hydronephrosis, high-grade vesicoureteral reflux, dysplastic or non-functioning kidneys, and additional congenital anomalies. These findings suggest that associated anomalies may help identify a subgroup of patients who require closer long-term nephro-urological surveillance. Larger multicenter studies with standardized follow-up are needed to better define the prognostic value of these factors.
