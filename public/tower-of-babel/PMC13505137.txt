Introduction

Oligometastatic disease denotes limited metastatic burden that may be amenable to metastasis-directed therapy. Randomized and prospective studies have established that local consolidative treatment improves PFS and, in some settings, OS [3, 5, 6]. SBRT is a standard non-invasive ablative approach for pulmonary metastases and early-stage lung tumors.

Local control after SBRT for pulmonary metastases is dose-dependent. Consensus guidelines advocate when feasible [4], but this target is not universally attainable. Poor performance status and high comorbidity burden increase competing mortality risks and may limit tolerance of ablative doses. Geriatric screening tools, including the G8 score and CCI, have been associated with survival and toxicity following SBRT for early-stage lung cancer [1, 2, 7]. Ultracentral tumors present a separate constraint: proximity to proximal bronchial structures and major vessels elevates the risk of severe toxicity at ablative doses, sometimes precluding on anatomic grounds alone.

The outcomes of SBRT delivered at in these situations remain poorly characterized. Two distinct, non-equivalent indications drive this prescription pattern in clinical practice: limited physiological reserve (frailty) and ultracentral anatomy. Each shifts the risk–benefit calculation through a different mechanism. We report a single-institution retrospective series describing how was used in a consecutive cohort with these indications, and we compare outcomes with , recognizing that dose selection reflected clinical and anatomic constraints rather than random assignment.

Methods and materials

Study design and patient selection

Consecutive patients treated with SBRT for recurrent lung-only metastases between January 2019 and December 2022 at a single institution were included. Eligibility required lung-only recurrence confirmed by contemporaneous CT and/or PET-CT without extrapulmonary disease. Consistent with oligometastatic criteria, patients had up to four lung lesions. SBRT was delivered to all known active lesions at the time of treatment. The study was approved by the institutional review board (IRB-2021–86) with waiver of informed consent.

Definitions: recurrent lung-only metastases, frailty, and ultracentral location

“Recurrent lung-only metastases” denotes metachronous lung-limited recurrence after definitive treatment of the primary malignancy (surgery and/or radiotherapy), without concurrent extrapulmonary disease at SBRT. Synchronous presentations, metastatic disease identified within three months of primary diagnosis, were excluded.

Frailty was operationalized a priori from routinely documented clinical surrogates at SBRT consultation: ECOG performance status ≥3 and/or CCI ≥8. The cross-tabulation of frailty status against group, and the indication breakdown for each prescription (frailty alone, ultracentral anatomy alone, both, or neither), are presented in Supplementary Table S1. Ultracentral location was defined as tumor proximity to critical central thoracic structures necessitating organ-at-risk constraint prioritization during planning.

Four-dimensional CT simulation was performed at 2-mm slice thickness. An internal gross tumor volume (iGTV) was expanded with institution-standard margins to generate the planning target volume (PTV); Treatment was delivered using volumetric modulated arc therapy (VMAT). Follow-up and endpoints

Cross-sectional imaging follow-up was performed at institution-standard intervals. OS was measured from SBRT start to death from any cause or last follow-up; PFS from SBRT start to progression (local, regional, or distant) or death; FFLP was defined as the absence progressive disease within or adjacent to the treated volume and/or regional nodal stations. Adverse events were graded per the Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE), version 5.0.

Statistical analysis

OS, PFS, and FFLP were estimated by Kaplan–Meier methodology and compared between BED groups by log-rank tests. Univariable Cox proportional hazards models provided HRs and 95% CIs. The proportional hazards assumption was not formally tested given the limited event counts and therefore, Cox model results should be interpreted accordingly.

To partially address confounding by indication, a propensity score analysis was performed using IPTW. The propensity score was estimated by logistic regression with BED group as the outcome and age, ECOG performance status, CCI, lung primary status, and number of metastases as covariates. Weights were stabilized and truncated at the 1st and 99th percentiles. Weighted Cox models for OS and PFS are presented as exploratory sensitivity analyses; they do not eliminate residual confounding.

Results

Patient characteristics

Fifty-three patients were included. Median age was 71 years (IQR 65–77); 34 patients (64%) were male. ECOG performance status was 1 in 19 (36%), 2 in 13 (25%), and 3 in 21 (40%). Characteristics stratified by frailty status are provided in Supplementary Table S1. Single-lesion disease was present in 40 patients (75%). All cases met the metachronous recurrence definition; no synchronous presentations were included. The median interval from primary diagnosis to SBRT was 11 months (IQR 6–26) in the group versus 24 months (IQR 13–37) in the group (p=0.084). Among patients with evaluable recurrence data (n=26), distant failure was most frequent (73%), followed by regional recurrence (15%) and local recurrence (12%). Median time from prior SBRT to recurrence was 9 months (IQR 6–16). Smoking history was present in 64%. The CCI distribution was concentrated, with an overall median of 6 (IQR 6–7), although values extended to 12, reflecting a subset with substantial comorbidity burden.

ECOG distribution differed markedly between groups (p < 0.001): 78% of patients had ECOG 3, compared with 20% in the higher-dose group. Primary tumor site also differed (p=0.049), with a higher proportion of non-lung primaries in the cohort. Age (p=0.452), smoking status (p=0.143), CCI (p=0.578), number of metastases (p=0.760), and tumor location (p > 0.999) showed no significant between-group differences. Ultracentral lesions were more frequent in the group (22% vs. 7% of lesions, respectively). The ECOG imbalance constitutes pronounced confounding by indication; survival comparisons between groups are strictly descriptive.

Overall survival and progression-free survival

Univariable Cox models found no statistically significant difference in OS, PFS, or FFLP between the dose groups, though point estimates favored throughout.

Median OS was 47.3 months in the group versus 34.3 months in the group (HR 1.45; 95% CI 0.66–3.16; p=0.35; Figure 2). Median PFS was 22.8 versus 14.7 months, respectively (HR 1.59; 95% CI 0.82–3.11; p=0.17; Figure 3). For FFLP, the HR was 2.67 (95% CI 0.60–11.93; p=0.20), with the wide interval reflecting the small number of local–regional events (Fig. 4).

IPTW sensitivity analysis

In IPTW-weighted Cox models adjusting for age, ECOG performance status, CCI, lung primary status, and number of metastases, was not significantly associated with OS (HR 0.58; 95% CI 0.25–1.32; p=0.19) or PFS (HR 0.59; 95% CI 0.24–1.40; p=0.23). Directionality was consistent with the unadjusted estimates.

Treatment-related toxicity

No grade 3 or higher treatment-related events occurred in the group. Five patients in the group experienced grade 3 toxicity: two cases of grade 3 pneumonitis and one of grade 3 esophagitis occurred within 90 days of treatment; two additional cases of grade 3 esophagitis were identified beyond 90 days. All resolved with conservative medical management. No grade 4 or grade 5 toxicities were observed.

Discussion

The central finding is straightforward: in this retrospective cohort, characterized by marked performance status imbalance between dose groups, was not associated with statistically significantly worse OS or PFS compared with . Confidence intervals were wide and confounding by indication was substantial, as lower-dose patients had poorer baseline status. These data are descriptive and should not be read as establishing the safety profile of dose de-escalation; the absence of grade 4 or grade 5 events in either group is, however, reassuring.

These findings reflect routine clinical decision-making. When ablative regimens were not feasible because of frailty, ultracentral anatomy, or both, was prescribed. Median survival differed numerically between groups; however, this difference cannot be attributed to dose given the substantial baseline imbalance. Frailty and ultracentral anatomy operate through distinct mechanisms: frailty reflects limited physiological reserve and competing-risk mortality, whereas ultracentral location imposes anatomic dose-limiting constraints on organs at risk. The two indications were not biologically equivalent, and their relative contributions could not be analytically separated at this sample size. The cohort therefore represents a mixed-indication real-world population.

The geriatric oncology literature provides relevant context. CCI and G8 score have been associated with survival and late toxicity following thoracic SBRT in older patients [1, 2, 7]. Formal geriatric assessment was not performed systematically in this cohort; ECOG performance status and CCI served as pragmatic surrogates. The concentration of grade 3 toxicity in the group, five events versus none, is consistent with higher-dose regimens carrying greater morbidity risk, though the groups were not comparable at baseline and this observation is descriptive.

Several limitations warrant consideration. The cohort is modest in size, and the analyses are hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory. Confounding by indication is intrinsic to this design: patients receiving were preferentially those with limited physiological reserve or anatomic dose-limiting constraints. The IPTW analysis, presented as a supportive sensitivity analysis, mitigates but does not eliminate this imbalance, and the directional consistency of IPTW-weighted estimates with the unadjusted results is reassuring. FFLP event counts are low and widen the confidence intervals for that endpoint. Heterogeneity in primary tumor type and in systemic therapy before and after SBRT (Supplementary Tables S2 and S3) reflects real-world practice and contributes additional variability. The single-institution design supports internal consistency in planning and follow-up at the cost of external generalizability. Within these constraints, the dataset captures a clinically meaningful real-world question that the published literature has scarcely addressed.

Prospective work addressing this question would require pre-specified frailty and anatomic risk strata, standardized toxicity capture, and competing-risk–appropriate endpoints. The present data do not support specific clinical recommendations; rather, they define the population and dose parameters for such a trial.

Conclusions

In this retrospective cohort, was used as a risk-adapted prescription in patients with frailty, ultracentral anatomy, or both. Differences in OS, PFS, and FFLP between the two BED groups did not reach statistical significance, and IPTW-weighted estimates were directionally consistent. These observations are consistent with the feasibility of sub-ablative SBRT in selected higher-risk patients when ablative dosing is not safely deliverable, and they define the patient population and dose parameters that warrant prospective, risk-stratified evaluation.
