Chicken eggshell paste versus CPP-ACP for enamel remineralization: a pilot in vitro study using micro-computed tomography
Authors: Saskia Karenina Latif, Eva Fauziah, Sarworini Bagio Budiardjo, Fourier Dzar Eljabbar Latief
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDental caries in primary teeth affects over 80% of children in many developing countries. Minimal intervention focuses on enamel remineralization. CPP-ACP is widely used, but chicken eggshells, a calcium carbonate-rich biowaste, may offer a sustainable, low-cost alternative. To compare the remineralization potential of chicken eggshell paste versus CPP-ACP on induced white spot lesions in primary enamel using micro-CT. This pilot in vitro pretest–posttest study used consecutive sampling (n = 3/group). Nine primary incisors with induced lesions were assigned to chicken eggshell paste, CPP-ACP, or negative control. Samples underwent pH cycling. Micro-CT assessed demineralization depth and mineral density pre- and post-treatment. Data were analyzed using mixed ANOVA, paired t-tests with Bonferroni correction (p < 0.017), and one-way ANOVA with Tukey HSD. Data were normally distributed (p > 0.05). Mixed ANOVA revealed significant time×group interactions for depth (F(2,6)=431.53, p < 0.001) and density (F(2,6)=23.90, p = 0.001). Both eggshell paste (depth: 130.85 ± 2.06 µm, p < 0.001; density: 15.76 ± 3.78, p = 0.019) and CPP-ACP (depth: 102.76 ± 9.98 µm, p = 0.003; density: 13.58 ± 2.82, p = 0.014) significantly improved from baseline, while controls worsened in depth (−3.24 ± 0.66 µm, p = 0.014). Eggshell paste reduced depth more than CPP-ACP (mean difference: 28.09 µm, p = 0.003), but both were comparable for density (p = 0.618). Both treatments outperformed controls (p < 0.01). Chicken eggshell paste demonstrated remineralization efficacy comparable to CPP-ACP, supporting its potential as a sustainable alternative for early caries management in primary teeth.Keywords: Remineralization, Chicken eggshell, Primary teeth, CPP-ACP, Micro-CT

Introduction

Dental caries is a prevalent dental health issue worldwide, particularly among children. According to the World Health Organization, around 514 million children are afflicted with caries in their primary teeth (WHO 2022). As a disease, dental caries extends beyond oral health, affecting children’s overall food intake due to pain that may be acute or chronic. In advanced stages, it may lead to tooth loss, which can interfere with speech development and reduce quality of life (Genderson et al. 2013; Ballantine et al. 2018). According to data from the WHO Oral Health Data Portal 2019, untreated dental caries affects approximately 2.5 billion people globally, representing a significant public health burden (WHO). Among children, WHO data further demonstrate a high prevalence of untreated caries in primary dentition across countries. In Indonesia, 88.8% of children have active caries lesions, underscoring the widespread nature of the disease (Kemenkes 2018). Multiple factors, including high sugar consumption, inadequate toothbrushing practices, lack of oral health education, and limited access to dental care services, contribute to the high prevalence (Van Chuyen et al. 2021).

Dental caries develops through a dynamic process of demineralization and remineralization (Arifa et al. 2019). Demineralization occurs when cariogenic bacteria, such as Streptococcus mutans and Lactobacillus spp., produce acids from the fermentation of dietary carbohydrates. This acid production lowers the pH of the oral environment, leading to the loss of calcium and phosphate ions from the enamel. If not counterbalanced by remineralization, continuous demineralization dissolves hydroxyapatite, creating porosity in the enamel that clinically manifests as white spot lesions (Lopes et al. 2024).

Caries management has evolved in recent decades toward a minimal-intervention approach, emphasizing prevention, arrest of lesion progression, and early remineralization of enamel during the initial stages of demineralization (Frencken et al. 2012). As a result, remineralization strategies have become an important part of stopping and treating early carious lesions. Remineralization involves the external supply of calcium and phosphate ions to restore lost minerals and halt lesion progression before cavitation occurs (BaniHani et al. 2022). One of the most widely used agents is casein phosphopeptide-amorphous calcium phosphate (CPP-ACP), which has been shown to enhance enamel remineralization by providing bioavailable calcium and phosphate while also forming a protective layer that shields the enamel from bacterial acids (Indrapriyadharshini et al. 2018; Oliveira et al. 2020). Despite its effectiveness, CPP-ACP presents certain limitations, including relatively high production costs and the need for frequent applications to achieve optimal results, which may limit its accessibility to broader populations.

With the growing need for more affordable and accessible remineralizing agents, there is increasing interest in exploring locally available natural materials (Monica et al. 2018; Indah Sari et al. 2019). Chicken eggshell (Gallus gallus domesticus) is an easily obtainable organic by-product composed of approximately 94% calcium carbonate, along with magnesium, phosphorus, and organic matrix proteins that may contribute to enamel remineralization (Mony 2015). Eggshell offers advantages as a naturally available, cost-effective, and sustainable material, reducing agricultural waste and adding economic value. Previous experimental studies on human and animal teeth have shown that eggshell-based supplements can enhance enamel microhardness and restore mineral content in bones (Fathy Abo-Elmahasen et al. 2024; Schaafsma et al. 2002). More recent in vitro studies have further demonstrated that eggshell-derived preparations are capable of promoting mineral deposition on demineralized enamel surfaces and improving surface microhardness (Jain et al. 2025). However, compared with established remineralizing agents such as fluoride and CPP-ACP, its effectiveness appears less pronounced, although it still shows potential as an adjunctive material (Bandekar et al. 2025). These findings indicate that eggshell-based materials may serve as a promising alternative, particularly in settings where cost and accessibility are major considerations.

To comprehensively evaluate remineralization, analytical methods should measure not only changes in mineral density but also the depth of demineralization with high precision. Micro-computed tomography (micro-CT) provides three-dimensional imaging that enables quantitative and detailed assessment of mineral density and enamel structure (Ghavami-Lahiji et al. 2021). Micro-CT enables volumetric analysis without damaging samples, allowing continuous observation of the same sample across different stages of remineralization, eliminating the need for new specimens at each testing point and thereby ensuring more accurate and representative results (du Plessis et al. 2017; Al Hezaimi et al. 2023). Therefore, this pilot in vitro study was conducted to explore the feasibility of using micro-computed tomography (micro-CT) to assess changes in mineral density and demineralization depth in primary enamel following treatment with chicken eggshell paste. Additionally, this study aimed to generate preliminary data on its remineralization potential in comparison with casein phosphopeptide–amorphous calcium phosphate (CPP-ACP). The findings are intended to provide an initial basis for further investigation rather than to establish definitive conclusions.

Materials and methods

Study design and ethical approval

This pilot experimental in vitro study employed a pretest-posttest control group design and was conducted at the Laboratory for Dental Materials Research and Development, Faculty of Dentistry, Universitas Indonesia. This study used a consecutive sampling technique. All procedures, including solution preparation, artificial white spot lesion induction, and pH cycling, were performed in this facility. Ethical approval (No.32/Ethical Approval/FKG UI/VI/2025) was granted by the Research Ethics Committee, Faculty of Dentistry, Universitas Indonesia, Indonesia.

Study procedure

After obtaining informed consent from parents or guardians, caries-free mandibular primary incisors indicated for extraction were collected, rinsed with running water, and stored in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) until use. Chicken eggshell powder was prepared in accordance with the World Intellectual Property Organization protocol (WO/2004/105912: Method of Producing Eggshell Powder). Briefly, collected chicken eggshells were washed thoroughly with distilled water to remove residual impurities and membranes, and then calcined at 800 °C for 1 hour. The dried shells were then ground into a fine powder and sieved to obtain a uniform particle size suitable for formulation as a remineralizing paste. The crystalline composition of the chicken eggshell powder was verified using X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis (PT. Cipta Mikro Material, Certificate No. 066-CMiM-Cer-V-2025). The diffractogram confirmed a 100% calcite phase (CaCO₃), consistent with pure calcium carbonate reference standards International Centre for Diffraction Data (ICDD) code 01–086-4274). The chicken eggshell powder was formulated as a paste containing 10% eggshell powder (CaCO3) as the active biomaterial. The formulation included food-grade humectants (glycerol, propylene glycol, D-sorbitol), a stabilizer (sodium carboxymethyl cellulose, Na-CMC), and preservatives (ethyl, propyl, and butyl p-hydroxybenzoate) to ensure consistency and stability. Formulation was performed at the Institut Kejuruan Ilmu Farmasi (IKIFA) Academy of Pharmacy in Indonesia.

A total of 27 extracted primary incisors were initially collected and clinically inspected to ensure intact, caries-free labial enamel surfaces. After applying the inclusion and exclusion criteria, only nine teeth met the requirements and were included. These nine teeth were consecutively sampled and assigned to three groups, yielding three teeth per group (n = 3). Given the pilot nature of this investigation, no formal a priori power calculation was performed. Other specimens were excluded because the high accuracy of Micro-CT can detect fracture lines that could interfere with the measurement process. Samples with longer roots were sectioned using a water-cooled diamond bur to allow stable mounting during micro-CT scanning.

To standardize the treatment area, a self-adhesive label was placed on the labial surface of each tooth, and the remaining surface was coated with nail varnish (Fig. 1) (Revlon, shade #570 Vixen, Revlon Inc., United States) (Shaik et al. 2017). After drying, the label was removed, leaving the exposed area as the treatment site. Samples were randomly divided into three groups: chicken eggshell paste, CPP-ACP (Tooth Mousse, GC Corp., Tokyo, Japan), and an untreated negative control.

Representative primary incisors with all surfaces except the treatment area covered in nail varnish

White spot lesions representing the preliminary stage of subsurface enamel lesions were created by immersing the samples in 30 ml of demineralizing solution for 96 hours at 37 °C. The solution was prepared according to a previously published pH-cycling model, consisting of 2.2 mM calcium chloride, 0.05 M acetic acid, 2.2 mM sodium phosphate, and 1 M potassium hydroxide (Amaechi 2019). The final pH was adjusted to 4.5 using acetic acid. The solution was replaced every 24 hours to maintain effectiveness.

To stimulate the oral environment, samples were treated with remineralizing agents and subjected to pH cycling, while negative controls underwent pH cycling without any remineralizing agent. Each 24-hour cycle consisted of immersion in remineralizing solution (1.5 mM calcium chloride (CaCl), 0.9 mM monopotassium phosphate (KH2PO4), 130 mM potassium chloride (KCl), and 20 mM 4-(2-hydroxyethyl)-1-piperazineethanesulfonic acid (HEPES), with pH adjusted to 7.0 with 10 mM potassium hydroxide (KOH) and 1.0 mM sodium azide (NaN3) for 23 hours, followed by immersion in demineralizing solution (17.8 mM CaCl2, 8.8 mM KH2PO4, 100 mM acetic acid, 1.0 mM NaN3, and pH adjusted to 4.3 with 10 mM KOH) for 1 hour (Amaechi 2019; Sinfiteli et al. 2017). Samples were rinsed with distilled water between solution changes. Remineralizing agents were applied once daily with a microbrush and left in contact for 5 minutes before immersion. The protocol was repeated for 7 consecutive days, with all solutions replaced daily.

The Integrated Physics Laboratory at the Faculty of Mathematics and Natural Sciences at the Institut Teknologi Bandung (ITB) in Indonesia used micro-CT to scan the samples. Changes in demineralization depth (μm) and mineral density were measured before and after treatment to quantify remineralization. Samples were mounted in modeling clay to minimize movement and ensure consistent positioning during scanning (Fig. 2). A Bruker Micro-CT SkyScan 1173 High-Energy scanner (Bruker, Kontich, Belgium) was used to capture 1,800 radiographic images over a 360-degree rotation. The source voltage was set to 100 kV and the source current to 80 μA. An aluminum filter with a thickness of 1.0 mm was used to shape the X-ray beam. The raw projection images were acquired in TIFF format with a 16-bit depth, and each pixel had an approximate size of 5.71 micrometers. To enhance image quality, the acquisition utilized both frame averaging and random movement. Following the scan acquisition, the 3D volume reconstruction was performed using NRecon software (v. 1.7.4.6, Bruker, Köntich, Belgium). Key parameters for reconstruction included a ring artifact correction level of 12 and a beam hardening correction of 15%. A hamming filter with an alpha of 0.54 was applied, and the resulting file type was 8-bit BMP. The reconstruction was post-aligned by −7.50. These parameters were specifically chosen to generate high-quality 3D representations of the tooth samples for the study of white spot lesions in enamel. Following the reconstruction process, the image dataset was analyzed using DataViewer (v. 1.6.0.0, 64-bit, Bruker, Köntich, Belgium) and CTAn (v. 1.20.8.0, 64-bit, Bruker, Köntich, Belgium).

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS version 29.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). The Shapiro-Wilk test was used to assess whether the data were normally distributed. A 2 × 3 mixed-design analysis of variance (mixed ANOVA) was performed. We included time (before and after treatment) as a within-subjects factor and group (chicken eggshell paste, CPP-ACP, and negative control) as a between-subjects factor. We used this analysis to assess the main effects and the time ×group interaction for both demineralization depth and mineral density. We calculate partial eta squared (η2) to measure effect size. Following a significant interaction, we used post hoc analyses as follows:

Paired t-tests with Bonferroni correction to compare pre- and post-treatment values within each group. With three paired comparisons performed per variable, we adjusted the significance threshold to p < 0.017 (0.05/3).

Paired t-tests with Bonferroni correction to compare pre- and post-treatment values within each group. With three paired comparisons performed per variable, we adjusted the significance threshold to p < 0.017 (0.05/3).

One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was conducted on mean change (Δ) values. After that, Tukey’s honestly significant difference (HSD) test was performed

Results

Micro-CT imaging

Representative micro-CT cross-sections (Fig. 3) show distinct visual changes in enamel mineral density before and after treatment. Demineralized areas appeared as darker subsurface regions at baseline, indicating mineral loss (Fig. 3(a-c)). Following remineralization, both the chicken eggshell paste and CPP-ACP groups showed brighter, more homogenous enamel surfaces, reflecting increased mineral density. In contrast, the negative control retained dark, demineralized regions, indicating no remineralization effect. Quantitative micro-CT analysis further supported these qualitative observations, confirming significant improvements in mineral density and lesion depth following treatment.

Mixed ANOVA revealed a significant time × group interaction for demineralization depth (F(2, 6) = 431.53, p < 0.001, partial η2 = 0.993) and mineral density (F(2, 6) = 23.90, p = 0.001, partial η2 = 0.888), indicating that changes over time differed significantly between groups (Table 2). Within-group comparisons

Within-group comparisons using paired t-tests with Bonferroni correction (p < 0.017) showed that chicken eggshell paste significantly reduced demineralization depth (mean change: 130.85 ± 2.06 µm; p < 0.001) and increased mineral density (15.76 ± 3.78; p = 0.019) from baseline. CPP-ACP also significantly reduced demineralization depth (102.76 ± 9.98 µm; p = 0.003) and increased mineral density (13.58 ± 2.82; p = 0.014). In the negative control group, demineralization depth significantly worsened (−3.24 ± 0.66 µm; p = 0.014), while mineral density showed no significant change (1.40 ± 0.59; p = 0.055) (Table 3).

Between-group comparisons

Between-group comparisons of mean changes (Δ values) using one-way ANOVA revealed significant differences among groups for both demineralization depth (F (2, 6) = 431.53, p < 0.001) and mineral density (F(2, 6) = 23.90, p = 0.001). Post-hoc analysis using Tukey HSD (Table 4) showed that chicken eggshell paste produced a significantly greater reduction in demineralization depth compared to CPP-ACP (mean difference: 28.09 µm; 95% CI: 13.32 to 42.86; p = 0.003). Both treatment groups significantly outperformed the negative control (p < 0.001). For mineral density, no significant difference was observed between chicken eggshell paste and CPP-ACP (mean difference: 2.18; 95% CI: −4.69 to 9.05; p = 0.618), whereas both treatments showed significantly greater improvement than the negative control (p = 0.002 and p = 0.004, respectively).

Discussion

Discussion of findings

This study evaluated the remineralization potential of chicken eggshell paste by analyzing changes in demineralization depth and mineral density of primary enamel white spot lesions using micro-CT, a state-of-the-art, non-destructive analytical tool, while promoting the utilization of biowaste materials. Caries management has shifted toward minimally invasive approaches that focus on prevention, arrest of disease progression, and reversal of early enamel demineralization (Brostek et al. 2006). White spot lesions are areas of demineralization that are below the surface of an intact lesion and have a higher mineral content. They manifest as opaque whitish regions resulting from optical alterations induced by mineral depletion and variations in the refractive indices of water and air within the porous enamel matrix (Gupta et al. 2017). Without early intervention, these lesions may progress into cavitated caries, resulting in irreversible structural loss. Mineral density reflects enamel’s inorganic composition, primarily calcium and phosphate, which decreases during demineralization and increases upon successful remineralization (Xie et al. 2023). Likewise, reduced demineralization depth indicates structural repair and stabilization of the lesion, confirming the therapeutic effect of the remineralizing agent.

Micro-CT was employed for its high-resolution, non-destructive, and quantitative capability in analyzing enamel structure. It enables precise visualization and measurement of mineral density and lesion depth at the micron level, producing reproducible 2D and 3D datasets for pre- and post-treatment comparison. In this study, consistent sample positioning within fixed scanning parameters ensured full inclusion within the field of view. The three-dimensional datasets allowed accurate superimposition and localization of identical regions across scans with standardized and logged parameters, ensuring the reliability and reproducibility of the measurements (Al Hezaimi et al. 2023; Nakata et al. 2012; Akküç et al. 2023).

Primary teeth were selected to better represent the clinical condition of the target pediatric population. Primary incisors provide uniform enamel surfaces suitable for the controlled application of remineralizing agents, supporting the development of age-specific prevention strategies (Akküç et al. 2023). Although all primary tooth samples in this study underwent artificial demineralization using the same protocol, Micro-CT analysis revealed variations in demineralization depth and mineral density among samples. This variation may be attributed to intrinsic differences among teeth, including enamel thickness, mineral composition, and porosity, as well as individual factors such as nutritional status and fluoride exposure that influence enamel maturation during development (Bandekar et al. 2025). The pH cycling mode simulated the dynamic demineralization-remineralization process of the oral environment, driven by dietary acids, bacterial metabolism, and salivary buffering (Hadidi et al. 2022). The inclusion of a negative control group undergoing pH cycling without treatment confirmed that the observed effects were attributable to active agents rather than to spontaneous remineralization driven by environmental pH changes (Brostek et al. 2006).

The findings revealed that both chicken eggshell paste and CPP-ACP significantly increased mineral density and reduced lesion depth compared to the untreated control, supporting their ability to halt or reverse early enamel demineralization (Mony 2015; Fathy Abo-Elmahasen et al. 2024; du Plessis et al. 2017). Several studies show that preparations made from chicken eggshells speed up remineralization. This is shown by the increase in surface microhardness and mineral content after cycles of demineralization and remineralization (El-Monem et al. 2021). Evidence indicates that eggshell-based remineralizing agents can achieve efficacy comparable to CPP-ACP in certain contexts, particularly highlighted by a randomized clinical trial that found no significant difference between ESP and CPP-ACP in treating white spot lesions (Sarhan et al. 2021). The effectiveness of chicken eggshell paste is attributed to its high calcium carbonate content, which dissociates into calcium ions under acidic conditions and combines with phosphate to reform hydroxyapatite crystals. CPP-ACP, as the gold-standard comparator, acts via casein phosphopeptides that stabilize amorphous calcium phosphate nanocomplexes, thereby maintaining a supersaturated environment that facilitates mineral diffusion into demineralized enamel (Indrapriyadharshini et al. 2018; Oliveira et al. 2020; Cury and Tenuta 2009).

Eggshell paste promotes remineralization through diffusion of Ca2+ and PO43- ions across the pseudo-intact surface layer into subsurface enamel lesions, where hydroxyapatite deposition occurs. This layer acts as a diffusion barrier, making the concentration gradient and sustained availability of ions critical for effective subsurface mineral recovery (El-Monem et al. 2021). Eggshell paste functions as a direct inorganic calcium source, releasing ions under acidic conditions and driving passive diffusion into lesion bodies (Essa et al. 2023; Helal et al. 2024). In contrast, CPP-ACP stabilizes ions within a casein phosphopeptide matrix, enabling controlled release, pellicle binding, and enhanced diffusion, particularly in the presence of fluoride (Sarhan et al. 2021; Alkhudhairy et al. 2024). While eggshell paste relies on gradient-driven diffusion, CPP-ACP offers improved ion retention and delivery, though both approaches achieve comparable remineralization outcomes under certain conditions (Sarhan et al. 2021; Essa et al. 2023).

No significant difference was found between chicken eggshell paste and CPP-ACP, indicating comparable remineralization efficacy. This suggests that eggshell paste provides a sufficient localized calcium reservoir to promote subsurface mineral restoration. Conversely, the untreated control showed no meaningful recovery, confirming that passive remineralization alone is inadequate and that active agents are essential to halt lesion progression (Arifa et al. 2019; Arends and ten Bosch 1992).

Overall, this study demonstrated three principal findings. First, chicken eggshell paste significantly enhanced enamel remineralization, as evidenced by increased mineral density and reduced lesion depth. Second, its efficacy was statistically comparable to that of CPP-ACP, suggesting its potential as a sustainable and low-cost alternative remineralizing agent. Third, enamel changes were quantitatively assessed using micro-CT, providing reliable three-dimensional measurements. Micro-CT enabled quantitative analysis at the voxel level, allowing robust intra-sample comparisons while minimizing inter-individual variability. However, the pH cycling model, although standardized, represents a simplification of the clinical oral environment.

Limitations

This study indicated that chicken eggshell paste could remineralize teeth comparably to CPP-ACP. However, the results should be interpreted with caution due to the inherent limitations of in vitro systems. The absence of the acquired pellicle and dental plaque biofilm may affect ion diffusion and remineralization dynamics, as these structures act as semi-permeable barriers and reservoirs for mineral ions in vivo. The pH cycling model does not fully replicate the localized acidic conditions created by plaque biofilm. Additionally, the absence of salivary components limits the simulation of natural oral conditions, in which proteins and buffering systems regulate mineral balance. This study also did not include detailed physicochemical characterization of the eggshell paste (pH, particle size, abrasivity), which may influence ion release and enamel interactions. Additionally, the generalizability of the findings may be affected by the small sample size (n = 3 per group), which limits statistical power. Consequently, the observed effects may not fully reflect clinical conditions.

Recommendations

The findings suggest that chicken eggshell paste, a biowaste-derived material, may serve as an effective and economical alternative to CPP-ACP, supporting sustainable biomaterial development and preventive strategies in pediatric dentistry. As a pilot study, these results remain preliminary and require further validation through in vivo and clinical investigations before broader application.

Conclusion

Chicken eggshell paste may exhibit remineralization potential comparable to CPP-ACP in this pilot in vitro investigation, as suggested by trends of increased mineral density and reduced demineralization depth in primary enamel white spot lesions. The use of micro-computed tomography (micro-CT) demonstrated its capability to quantify enamel structural changes with high precision, supporting its application as a non-destructive and quantitative imaging method in remineralization studies. However, these findings should be interpreted as preliminary. Further research, including in vivo studies and well-designed clinical trials, is required to elucidate the underlying mechanisms, assess safety, and determine the clinical effectiveness of chicken eggshell paste. Overall, this study provides exploratory evidence supporting its potential as a biomaterial for minimally invasive caries management.
