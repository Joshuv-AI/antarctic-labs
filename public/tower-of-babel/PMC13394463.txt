High-precision time-domain parallelism photonic computing
Authors: Junyan Che, Gaofei Wang, Zhou Han, Yunqi Mu, Jiabin Shen, Zengguang Cheng, Peng Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Artificial intelligence (AI) compute demands outstrip electronic hardware scaling, creating a critical “AI compute gap.” Photonic computing promises speed, low latency, and parallelism yet faces bottlenecks in core array scalability and optoelectronic bandwidth efficiency. We propose a time-domain parallelism (TDP) computing architecture that temporally multiplexes compact cores, enabling large-scale convolutional operations while optimizing bandwidth efficiency. Supporting this framework, we develop indium tin oxide (ITO)–based photonic devices demonstrating a record 9-bit dynamic reconfigurability at 60 kilohertz and a 3-decibel bandwidth of 311 kilohertz. These devices feature compact phase shifter lengths (Lπ = 20 micrometers) and exceptional endurance (>1010 cycles with <0.1-decibel extinction ratio degradation). Validation of the TDP system using a 2 × 2 ITO photonic array achieves an accuracy of 84.91% on Fashion-MNIST classification, comparable to the full-precision (FP32) software baseline (84.66%) and outperforming a 5-bit benchmark (71.94%). Notably, the system maintains this high-fidelity performance while operating at 1694 frames per second. This work establishes a scalable pathway toward energy-efficient photonic computing for AI workloads.

INTRODUCTION

The rapid advancement of emerging artificial intelligence (AI) applications, spanning large language models (1), autonomous driving systems (2), and real-time image recognition (3), is heralding an era of intelligence systems. This progress, however, simultaneously generates exponentially escalating computational demands. Breakthroughs in AI architectures, including deep neural networks (4), convolutional neural networks (CNNs) (5, 6), and Transformer models (7), have driven a staggering 107-fold escalation in computational requirements over the past decade (8). A notable example is OpenAI’s GPT-3, which uses 175 billion parameters (9) and requires 3640 petaflops/s-days of training computation (10). In contrast, the performance scaling of mainstream AI hardware (e.g., graphics processing units) has improved by merely103-fold during the same period (11), creating a critical and unsustainable “AI compute gap.” Photonic computing emerges as a disruptive solution, leveraging ultrafast processing speed (12), subnanosecond latency (13), and massive parallelism capability (14) to deliver high-performance and energy-efficient AI accelerations (15, 16). Silicon photonics, in particular, has gained strategic traction due to its complementary metal-oxide semiconductor (CMOS) compatibility and scalability, with both academic studies (15, 16) and industrial prototypes (17) demonstrating >100 tera operations per second per watt (TOPS/W) optical neural processors, which shows a critical advancement toward bridging the AI compute gap.

In modern electronic AI accelerators, hardware reuse architectures (e.g., multicore designs) are essential for alleviating the widening bandwidth mismatch between computational throughput and memory systems. By dynamic resource sharing, multiplexing technologies can effectively amortize input and output (I/O) bottlenecks, thereby maximizing hardware utilization within scalability constraints. For emerging photonic computing, integration with electronic systems is necessary but also gives rise to a dual (optical-electrical) bandwidth challenge. In these systems, a substantial speed mismatch exists between optical and electrical processing (>1 terabit/s per wavelength photonic I/O versus <5-GHz electronic clocks), which is furtherly exacerbated by digital-to-analog converter (DAC)/analog-to-digital converter (ADC) quantization and transmission protocol overheads (18, 19). Therefore, photonic architectures must adopt analogous core-multiplexing strategies, including space-domain parallelism (SDP, akin to multicore designs) and time-domain parallelism (TDP). These means can maximize functional density, share electronic interfaces across multiple photonic operations, and help bridge the optoelectronic bandwidth gap. Mirroring electronic architectures that trade monolithic scaling for distributed efficiency, photonic systems can leverage spatial/temporal multiplexing to unlock their theoretical bandwidth potential (>5 Tb/s/mm2) (20). At the same time, the fundamental wavelength constraints of photonic integration necessitate waveguide dimensions ranging from several hundred nanometers to micrometers to maintain effective light confinement, precluding the transistor-scale miniaturization achievable in digital electronics. Furthermore, practical limitations such as waveguide insertion loss and photomask size impose an upper bound on the number of photonic and optoelectronic devices that can be integrated on a single chip (21, 22). Against this backdrop, time-multiplexing strategies offer a critical pathway to mitigate the persistent integration-density bottleneck that has long constrained silicon photonics relative to electronics.

As an analog approach, the precision of photonic computing systems is a key factor affecting accuracy in AI applications, and 8- to 9-bit precision is sufficient to maintain a high accuracy (23, 24). Unfortunately, current photonic weight modulation means, including microring resonators (MRRs) (25), Mach-Zehnder interferometers (MZIs) (26), and phase-change materials (PCMs) (27, 28), are limited to a constrained resolution of 5 to 7 bits. Researchers have also developed technologies like iterative feedback calibration to achieve higher bit resolution (maximum of 9 bits) (15, 23). However, these methods inherently slow down weight updates alongside nonnegligible power overhead, creating a trade-off with the ultralow-latency and energy-efficient requirements of multiplexed photonic cores.

Here, we propose a TDP photonic computing architecture that temporally multiplexes photonic cores while synchronizing weight updates within a unified domain. Under identical bandwidth constraints, this approach achieves superior computational frequency and energy efficiency versus SDP architectures by reducing I/O channel requirements. Crucially, as AI workloads scale (e.g., batch sizes of >32 and image size of >334), weight update time occupies <1% of total compute time, rendering their latency impact negligible while preserving throughput. To realize this paradigm, we developed arrays of indium tin oxide (ITO)–enabled MZI thermo-optic weight modulators optimized for TDP demands. Leveraging low loss of ITO, the direct waveguide integration achieves compact footprints (Lπ = 20 μm) with rapid response, exhibiting a 3-dB bandwidth of 311 kHz. Moreover, the devices exhibited excellent cycling endurance (>1010 cycles with <0.1-dB optical fluctuation) and a measured weight precision exceeding 9 bits at a reconfiguration speed of 60 kHz, effectively addressing the requirements for frequent weight updates and state stability in TDP systems. Last, we validated the TDP photonic computing system using an array of four ITO-MZIs on Fashion-MNIST (Modified National Institute of Standards and Technology) classification, achieving an accuracy of 84.91%, which closely approaches the software full-precision (FP32) baseline of 84.66% and represents a significant improvement over the 71.94% accuracy of the 5-bit analog benchmark. The proposed photonic temporally multiplexing prototype has validated photonic computing as a viable solution for future large-scale AI workloads, effectively bridging the computational gap while maintaining CMOS compatibility.

RESULTS

TDP photonic computing architecture

Figure 1A depicts typical multiplexed photonic systems (SDP and TDP), where optoelectronic interfaces are used to route input data (e.g., image inputs) and network parameters (e.g., convolution kernels) to photonic cores for computation. Postprocessing results (e.g., convolved feature maps) are returned to electronic subsystems for operations like normalization and nonlinear activation. SDP photonic computing systems use multiple photonic chips, each programmed with distinct convolution kernels, to process the same input image in parallel. This parallelism demands N-fold output bandwidth relative to the input (where N = number of chips), creating a proportional scaling challenge as chip counts increase. In contrast, TDP architectures dynamically reconfigure kernels of a single photonic chip in the temporal domain for sequential convolution operations, preserving static input/output bandwidth (∼1:1 ratio) independent of multiplexing iterations.

We benchmark SDP and TDP architectures using a convolutional layer with 1024 kernels in a CNN (see Materials and Methods for calculations). Both systems use a 64 × 64 photonic core array, with SDP spatially multiplexing 16 discrete chips. Under matched bandwidth constraints, the TDP achieves about 16× higher operational clock frequencies than the SDP (Fig. 1B). State-of-the-art communication protocols [e.g., PCIe (Peripheral Component Interconnect Express) 6.0 ×16] remain insufficient to exploit the intrinsic bandwidth capabilities of photonic chips (67 to 110 GHz) (19, 29), and the modeling indicates that TDP will dominate clock scaling until 2060 (as projected by PCIe bandwidth extrapolation; fig. S2). Energy efficiency analysis (Fig. 1C) reveals that the TDP has greater advantages due to maximizing the utilization of computing resources. The SDP yields only marginal performance improvements over TDP under ultrahigh bandwidth conditions (about 100 TB/s) through parallel processing, primarily due to the temporal latency inherent in TDP-based weight update operations. Computational power and area efficiency follow a comparable trend (fig. S1), further underscoring the scalability of the TDP for large-kernel photonic networks. Moreover, TDP benefits from single-chip packaging, avoiding the yield compounding effect and complex assembly of chiplet systems.

As depicted in Fig. 1D, TDP photonic computing dynamically updates weights on a single chip, where the time domain is sliced into two distinct phases: effective computation (e.g., convolution) and weight updating. The weight update phase is excluded from effective computation time because it is dedicated to weight loading and signal stabilization. For a TDP chip operating at 100 GHz, we quantified delays during image convolutions (see Materials and Methods). Figure 1E shows that as CNN models scale (increasing input pixel sizes), convolution latency grows, while weight update latency remains fixed, causing total delay to asymptotically approach convolution time. A weight update rate of 311 kHz (3-dB bandwidth) suffices for most modern models, although smaller architectures like LeNet5, with 20-kHz updates [as exemplified by traditional thermo-optic modulators (30, 31), 3-dB bandwidth], suffer from weight update–dominated delays (Fig. 1E). Weight update approaches relying on complex control loops (>1 ms) would make weight update latency the dominant contributor across nearly all model scales. Crucially, weight update latency diminishes as a performance bottleneck for high-resolution inputs. That said, TDP’s frequent reconfiguration necessitates photonic weight devices with three key characteristics: (i) high endurance cycles, (ii) microsecond response times, and (iii) stable dynamic precision—all essential for maintaining algorithmic accuracy.

ITO-based photonic weights for TDP

To meet stringent weight device requirements for TDP systems, we engineered a transparent conducting oxide (TCO) photonic platform, leveraging ITO for low-loss infrared operation and high-speed thermo-optic modulation. This approach exploits minimal infrared absorption of TCO-based heaters in communication waveguides (32) and enables high-efficiency phase shifters by minimizing electrode-waveguide proximity (33). Besides, TCO can be compatible with CMOS-foundry integration process, offering scalable fabrication with high wafer-level yields (34).

We fabricated ITO-based MZI devices by integrating ITO patches with silicon-on-insulator (SOI) waveguides (Fig. 2A and fig. S7). To balance the optical loss, symmetric ITO phase shifters were embedded in both MZI arms. Each ITO patch was 50-nm thick and 3-μm wide (aligned parallel to the waveguide), with electrodes offset by 5 μm. An 800-nm-thick SiO2 cladding layer was then deposited to enhance mechanical and environmental stability (for the packaging process). Postdeposition annealing in air was performed to lower the ITO sheet resistance and extinction coefficient (fig. S8). After optimizing the annealing process (400°C, 30 min), the phase shifter insertion loss was reduced to 0.109 dB/μm (Fig. 2B). Meanwhile, the ITO films exhibited excellent uniformity in both electrical and optical properties across the 4-inch (10.16 cm) wafer (figs. S9 and S10), demonstrating the potential for scalable photonic chips.

Subsequently, static and dynamic responses of the ITO-based devices were investigated using an optical measurement system (fig. S11). Applying a bias voltage to the modulator’s short arm induced a significant blue shift in the resonance wavelength, achieving a π-phase shift at a 6-V drive voltage (Fig. 2C). The device sustained a stable 30-dB extinction ratio (ER) across all operational conditions, evidencing negligible parasitic absorption (e.g., free-carrier effects). The linear relationship between phase shift and electrical power (Fig. 2D) confirms the thermo-optic effect as the dominant modulation mechanism. Furthermore, power consumption exhibits a strong dependence on device length (L). A 20-μm-long device achieved a π-phase shift at the energy (Eπ) of 22.01 mW, whereas extended structures exhibited reduced energy efficiency (e.g., L = 80 μm, Eπ = 25.78 mW). This 17% efficiency degradation stems from thermal diffusion in elongated geometries, underscoring how compact heater designs minimize energy overhead in TDP systems. Additional characterizations (fig. S12) demonstrate high interdevice consistency, with ITO resistance fluctuations within 1.1% deviation and device phase shift errors below 0.01π. The broad wavelength response of ITO-based modulators makes them well suited for high-throughput wavelength division multiplexing schemes, enabling greater scalability in photonic computing and communications systems (fig. S14).

High-speed dynamic modulations reveal a 3-dB bandwidth of 311 kHz (Fig. 2E). This operation frequency (f) has a 30× improvement over conventional titanium nitride (TiN) heaters (30). It is attributed to direct thermal coupling between the ITO heater and waveguide, avoiding the thermal barrier of SiO2. Furthermore, four-level pulse amplitude modulation (PAM4) modulation at 150 kHz yielded a wide-open eye diagram, confirming minimal intersymbol interference and robust signal integrity. Cycling endurance testing at 50 kHz validated over 1010 switching cycles with a sustained ER of ∼20 dB (Fig. 2F). After more than 55 hours of operation, the negligible ER degradation (<0.068 dB) enables extrapolated stability exceeding 1012 cycles, well beyond the reliability demands of photonic TDP (<107 cycles/hour in real-world deployment; fig. S3). The combination of high operation frequency (311 kHz), large ER (30 dB), compact footprint (20 μm), superior cycling endurance (>1010 cycles), interdevice high uniformity, and CMOS compatibility positions the platform as a cornerstone for large-scale photonic TDP systems.

High-precision TDP photonic computing system for image classification

To validate the practical applicability of the TDP computing system integrated with ITO-based photonic devices, we conducted image classification experiments using the Fashion-MNIST dataset. As shown in Fig. 4A, the network processes 28 × 28–pixel grayscale images that are optically encoded using variable optical attenuators (VOAs). Optical convolutions with eight 2 × 2 kernels were sequentially executed in the time domain via a single photonic chip embedding four ITO-based devices. Leveraging TDP, all kernel weights are dynamically programmed into the chip at designated time slots (t1 to t8), enabling the emulation of an 8-kernel convolutional layer without hardware replication. Multiply-accumulate (MAC) operations were implemented via optical multiplications in four ITO patches, followed by incoherent summation using a multiplexer (MUX). The convolutional results were converted into electrical signals through a photodetector and an ADC, while postoptical layers (fully connected and classification output layers) were digitally emulated. The packaged photonic chip (Fig. 4B) used SMA (subminiature version A) connectors for high-speed control (>100 kHz) of ITO heaters, enabled by low-latency coaxial interfaces. A thermoelectric cooler was used to stabilize the chip temperature at 23.0° ± 0.05°C, effectively mitigating ambient thermal fluctuations during the inference process. As shown in fig. S13, the system stability was monitored over 18 hours while toggling the weight between on- and off-states at 50 kHz. The average fluctuation was 0.208% in the on-state and 0.019% in the off-state, demonstrating excellent long-term stability of the system.

The devices’ >9-bit dynamic precision ensured negligible deviation between real-time deployed and deserved weights throughout consecutive batches, thereby confirming exceptional repeatability under TDP. For inference validation, the raw convolutional outputs of a handbag image exhibited a RMSE (root mean square error) ≈ 0.05 relative to the digital baseline (Fig. 5, B and C). The convolution output signal for the handbag image exhibits minor temporal artifacts due to the VOA’s limited bandwidth (∼1 kHz; Fig. 4B). Quantitative error analysis (Fig. 4C) revealed absolute errors below 0.02 (0.5% of the theoretical maximum value of 4.0) across all kernels. We validated the system across 116 batches (2320 total images), with real-time photonic convolution maintaining >9-bit precision per batch, with only batch 86 falling marginally below 9 bits, demonstrating sustained operational robustness (fig. S22). Statistical analysis of all 13,530,240 convolution samples yielded an SD of 4.828 × 10−3 and a mean error of 1.040 × 10−3, corresponding to an equivalent precision of 9.413 bits. The photonic TDP system achieved an inference accuracy of 84.91% (Fig. 5D), closely matching the 84.66% accuracy of full-precision (FP32) software simulations (Fig. 5E) and substantially exceeding low-precision baselines (e.g., 71.94% for 5-bit quantization; fig. S23). A class-specific analysis (Fig. 5D) showed that 9 of 10 Fashion-MNIST categories reached near-digital inference accuracy, with only the shirt category exhibiting a notable deviation. Achieving an accuracy closely matching the electronic baseline without any error correction validates the efficacy of 9-bit photonic MAC operations for neural inference, effectively narrowing the precision gap between analog photonic and digital electronic systems.

To overcome the bandwidth limitations of VOAs for input data loading, we evaluated an alternative approach using a high-speed electro-optic modulator (EOM) with 10-GHz bandwidth. The measurement setup (described in Materials and Methods and fig. S24) used the same linearization technique developed for ITO-MZI weight devices (fig. S15) to ensure high linearity operation. Initial testing at 50-MHz data loading frequency demonstrated clean signal transmission, with each data bit occupying a 20-ns window (fig. S25). For precision characterization, we conducted statistical analysis on 25,000 data points (500 random patterns repeated 50 times) to determine effective bit precision.

Our results (fig. S26) reveal frequency-dependent performance: The system maintains full 8-bit precision at 10-MHz loading rates while preserving approximately 6-bit precision at 100 MHz. The observed degradation at higher frequencies manifests primarily as temporal overshoot and distortion, attributable to bandwidth constraints in the arbitrary waveform generator (100 MHz). When applied to Fashion-MNIST classification tasks with 8-bit precision (fig. S27), this EOM-based approach achieves a computational throughput of 1694 images per second, demonstrating the practical advantages of TDP photonic computing architecture.

DISCUSSION

Comparison of weight device performance

We benchmark our ITO-based photonic devices against traditional platforms, including Si-based MRRs, MZIs, and PCM-based systems (15, 26, 36), across four key metrics: precision, update frequency, footprint, and endurance (table S1). Optimizing MRR-based systems can achieve high-quality factor (Q) and ER (>20 dB), yet complex feedback algorithms are required to maintain >8-bit precision—restricting practical update rates [although integrated RF-FPGA (radio frequency field-programmable gate array) feedback systems can improve precision at the expense of slowing updates to the millisecond range] (23, 37). Emerging PCM-based photonics offer ultracompact footprints (Lπ ≤ 12 μm) and near-zero static power, but their inherent stochastic phase transition behavior limits precision to <6 bits and endurance to <106 cycles (27, 28), making them better suited for edge applications rather than TDP systems requiring frequent updates. Traditional MZIs using TiN or metal heaters suffer from thermal barriers of silicon oxide, leading to slow response (f < 10 kHz) and large footprints (L > 100 μm) (31).

In contrast, our ITO-based devices exhibit distinct competitive advantages. The near-transparent ITO layer in direct contact with the waveguide enables a high 3-dB bandwidth of 311 kHz and compact integration (L = 20 μm). The thermo-optic effect, combined with the use of annealed ITO, facilitates >9-bit dynamic precision at a 60-kHz reconfiguration rate and >1010-cycle endurance. These attributes uniquely satisfy the concurrent requirements of TDP computing for high-speed reconfiguration and high dynamic precision.

Scalability of photonic chip: Impact of insertion loss, area, and latency

In photonic TDP computing systems, temporal multiplexing of compact chips presents a viable approach to circumventing large-scale integration challenges. However, an excessively small core size leads to quadratic growth in reuse time, which can degrade operational efficiency. Therefore, a larger-scale reusable core is essential. Here, we discuss the scalability of our ITO-based photonic platforms, which is primarily governed by three key factors: insertion loss, chip area, and on-chip latency.

Insertion loss is evaluated through comprehensive modeling of a crossbar architecture (14), accounting for all key photonic components: waveguides, waveguide crossings, directional couplers (splitting and combining elements), and ITO-based weight devices. Directional coupler splitting ratios are optimized to compensate for waveguides and crossing imbalances. This analysis yields an N × N port-to-port loss map (fig. S28). For a 64 × 64 array, the total insertion loss measures 43.85 dB. Notably, the loss analysis reveals that optical splitting (for multicolumn parallel computing) and incoherent summation constitute the dominant loss mechanisms, collectively accounting for 83.4% of the total system loss. In contrast, the ITO weight contribution remains minimal at just 5.0%. With 50-mW (17 dBm) input power and accounting for on-chip Ge/Si photodetector (38) and transimpedance amplifiers (39), theoretical calculations indicate scalability to 66 × 66 arrays. Chip area constraints are analyzed within the crossbar architecture framework, where each unit cell occupies 250 μm by 250 μm. Lithographic reticle limitations—exemplified by the Nikon KrF Scanner at the 110 nm node, which offers a maximum exposure field of 858 mm2—theoretically enable a 117 × 117 array (fig. S29). Optical latency mapping (fig. S30) reveals that propagation delays scale linearly with array dimensions. While certain ports exhibit shorter waveguide paths, phase-compensating delay lines ensure uniform signal timing across all ports. For 64 × 64 configurations, total latency measures ∼250 ps. Together, these results confirm the practical feasibility of scaling to 64 × 64 implementations. This feasibility has been validated in hardware through the fabrication of a 16 × 16 crossbar array using maskless photolithography, with the optical micrograph presented in fig. S31.

In summary, we propose a TDP photonic computing architecture that synchronizes temporal multiplexing of photonic cores with in-domain weight updates. Compared to SDP systems requiring integration of multiple photonic chips, this approach maximizes the utilization of now constrained optoelectronic I/O bandwidths. The TDP architecture exhibits superior photonic clock frequencies, energy efficiency, and simplified hardware design. Systematic analysis confirms that weight updates in TDP account for less than 1% of total processing time in mainstream CNNs, introducing negligible temporal overhead. To address the stringent reconfiguration demands of TDP systems, we have developed ITO-integrated photonic devices achieving state-of-the-art dynamic precision (>9 bits at 60 kHz) and high 3-dB bandwidth (f = 311 kHz). Optimized annealing processes enable relatively low optical loss (∼0.109 dB/μm) in ITO phase shifters, while exceptional endurance (>1010 cycles with <0.1-dB ER degradation) and interdevice consistency (<1% variation) ensure reliable large-scale deployment. Scalability assessments demonstrate the feasibility of 64 × 64 arrays based on three critical metrics: insertion loss, lithographically compatible chip area, and managed optical latency. Furthermore, we validate the TDP computing framework with an ITO-based photonic array, demonstrating high consistency between deployed inference weights and expected values. The system achieves 84.91% recognition accuracy on the Fashion-MNIST dataset, closely matching the 84.66% accuracy of full-precision (FP32) software simulations and significantly exceeding low-precision baselines (e.g., 71.94% for 5-bit quantization), highlighting the critical role of weight precision. Notably, the system achieves this high-fidelity performance while sustaining a real-time processing rate of 1694 frames per second, demonstrating simultaneous precision and throughput capabilities. This work establishes a comprehensive photonic computing framework, spanning from device innovation to system architecture, that provides a viable pathway toward next-generation AI accelerators.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Calculation of bandwidth requirements for SDP and TDP systems

The bandwidth requirements for TDP and SDP systems are calculated by considering the data transmission bandwidth for both I/O channels of photonic systems. It is worth noting that the SDP and TDP systems under consideration are designed to be compatible with a single PCIe compute card. Because of constraints in board area, routing, and thermal dissipation, such a system cannot be scaled in the same manner as a multicard cluster.

For the calculation, a convolutional task in a CNN is used as an example. In the case of a photonic array with a size of narray×narray, if a convolution task with a kernel size of nkernel×nkernel is deployed, then narray convolution kernels can be deployed. The number of input channels is nkernel2, and the number of output channels is narray. The clock frequency of the photonic chip in a TDP system can be computed asfTDP=min(finherent,BWnkernel2,BWnarray)

Here, finherent represents the intrinsic frequency of the photonic device. In this calculation, a typical EOM frequency of 100 GHz is used. The second and third terms represent the dominant frequency limited by the input/output port bandwidth. Since different chips in the SDP system output different data, the output bandwidth requirement is NSDP times that of the TDP system.

Calculation of delay in TDP systems

On the basis of the task requirements for training and inference in artificial neural networks, the weights in the TDP system should be updated once per batch. The total delay is divided into the weight update delay (tweight−update) and the convolution computation delay for each batch (tconv). Obviously, the tweight−update is a constant determined by the inherent response speed of the device (e.g., 50 μs for traditional thermo-optic devices in Fig. 1E). The tconv is primarily attributed to the data loading time and can be calculated as followstconv=Nbatch−sizefTDP×nkernel2×(nimage−size−nkernel+1)2

Here, the images considered are square images with equal pixel dimensions on both sides. nimage−size refers to the number of pixels on one side of the image, and nkernel is the size of the convolution kernel, accounting for different CNN models. Nbatch−size is the batch size set during training or inference, which is set to 32 in this calculation. fTDM is the photonic chip’s computation clock frequency, set to 100 GHz in this calculation. Because of significant structural differences across various CNN models, the models marked in Fig. 1E correspond to the delay results for running the first convolutional task using the TDP system.

Device fabrication

The device fabrication starts with a standard SOI wafer featuring a 3-μm buried oxide layer and a 220-nm top silicon layer. After wafer cleaning, the obtained small pieces are cleaned with deionized water, acetone, and isopropyl alcohol (IPA), followed by drying. Approximately 430-nm-thick ZEP520A photoresist is spin-coated using a spin coater and baked on a hotplate at 180°C for 5 min. Exposure is performed using the JEOL JBX-8100 electron beam lithography system, developed in pentyl acetate for 45 s. An 85-nm silicon layer is etched using the Oxford PlasmaPro 100 Inductively Coupled Plasma Reactive Ion Etching (ICP-RIE) system in an SF6 and CF4 atmosphere., forming the partially etched waveguide. The photoresist ZEP520A is then removed by immersion in N-methyl-2-pyrrolidone (NMP) solution. The sample is subsequently spin-coated with a dual-layer allresist AR-P-617.06/AR-P 662.09 polymethyl methacrylate photoresist and baked on a hotplate at 170°C for 3 min. The ITO material deposition window was then exposed using the ZEISS Sigma 300 scanning electron microscope (SEM) system with NPGS (Nanometer Pattern Generation System) for precise patterning and developed in methyl isobutyl ketone and IPA for 60 s. The 50-nm ITO material is grown using the Lesker PVD75 magnetron sputtering system, followed by lift-off in acetone. The sample is then annealed at 400°C for 30 min in an air atmosphere, with a ramp-up rate of 100°C/min and a ramp-down rate of 50°C/min. Next, the sample is spin-coated with LOR10A and S1818 UV photoresists and baked at 150° and 100°C, respectively. Exposure for the metal deposition window is carried out using the Heidelberg MLA150 laser direct-write system, developed in a 2.38 wt % tetramethylammonium hydroxide for 60 s. Titanium (5 nm) and gold (80 nm) are deposited using the Detech DE400DHL electron beam evaporation system, followed by a lift-off in NMP/acetone solution. After the single device sample preparation is completed, to protect the waveguide device, the array sample is subsequently encapsulated by growing an 800-nm silicon oxide layer as a protective layer using the Lesker PVD75 system. The sample is then spin-coated with 3510T photoresist and exposed with the laser direct-write equipment for the encapsulation lead window. The silicon oxide layer is fully etched using ICP-RIE, and the layer is completely removed using a heated NMP solution and ultrasonic cleaning. The device fabrication is completed for individual samples.

To protect the packaged array samples, 800 nm of silicon oxide is grown using the Lesker PVD75 system as a protective layer. A 3510T photoresist is spin-coated, and wire bonding windows are exposed using a laser direct-write system. The silicon oxide layer is then completely etched using ICP-RIE, and the 3510T photoresist is fully removed by heating the NMP solution and applying ultrasonic treatment. The packaging of the array samples is thus complete.

Material characterizations

The device size was characterized using an SEM (ZEISS GeminiSEM 360). The optical properties of ITO, including the refractive index and extinction coefficient, were measured using spectroscopic ellipsometry (Horiba).

Measurement setup

The ITO-phase shifter device was tested using a tunable laser source (TSL570, SANTEC). A low-speed detector (2011-FS-M, Newport) and a high-speed detector (2117-FC-M, Newport) were used to detect dc and high-frequency signals, respectively. Data acquisition was performed using a data acquisition card (USB-6363, National Instruments) and an oscilloscope (3104T, Keysight). A source meter (2400, Keithley) was used to apply the dc signal, while a radio frequency signal was applied using a pulse generator (81160A, Keysight and 4225-PMU, Keithley).

The high-speed data loading setup used a tunable laser source (N7714, Keysight) and an EOM (MXER-LN-20, iXblue) driven by an arbitrary function generator (AFG31102, Tektronix), with all instruments synchronized to a 10-MHz reference clock (4225-PMU, Keithley). The modulated optical signal was detected by a 1-GHz photodetector (1611-FS-AC, Newport) and captured with an oscilloscope (3104T, Keysight), while a data acquisition card (USB-6363, National Instruments) coordinated triggering. The entire measurement was automated via a custom Python script.

Hardware implementation of CNN for Fashion-MNIST classification

In the Fashion-MNIST classification task, we implemented a CNN consisting of eight 2 × 2 convolutional kernels, two hidden layers with 512 and 128 units, respectively, and an output layer with 10 units. Rectified linear unit activation was used as the nonlinear function between layers. The input images, each with a resolution of 28 × 28 pixels, were flattened into a 784 × 1 array. The network was trained off-chip using gradient descent with a learning rate of 0.001. The training set comprised 50,000 randomly selected images, while the validation set included 2000 randomly selected images. The test set contained the same 2320 randomly selected images used in the experiment. Each training batch consisted of 50 images. After training, the model achieved an accuracy of 87.6% on the 2320 test images. For the experiment, we used four lasers (C08 to C18, 7714A, Keysight) as light sources (see Fig. 4A), with data encoded via a VOA (V1550A, Thorlabs). The VOA was driven by two dual-channel pulse generators (AFG31102, Tektronix). Given the VOA’s relatively slow response time, the signal frequency was set to 500 Hz. The on-chip ITO-MZI devices were controlled by a multichannel voltage source (MSVS6400-A, Silicon Extreme). To maintain temperature stability, the chip package was equipped with a thermoelectric cooler and a thermistor, with temperature regulation controlled at 23°C via a proportional integral derivative module (TCM-M207). The chip’s output was combined through an optical wavelength MUX and converted into an electrical signal by a photodetector (2011-FS-M, Newport). The signal was then digitized using a data acquisition card (USB-6363, National Instruments) before further processing on a computer. To ensure synchronization between the pulse generator and the data acquisition card, a single-pulse trigger signal was used to trigger both the AFG (Arbitrary Function Generator) and the data acquisition card simultaneously.
