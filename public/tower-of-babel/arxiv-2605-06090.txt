1.
Introduction

Background

The two strands of mathematics joined in this paper are the classical
theory of orthogonal polynomials and the representation theory of
infinite-dimensional Lie algebras attached to algebraic curves. The
former goes back to the late eighteenth century, when Legendre
introduced what are now called the Legendre polynomials in his study
of gravitational potentials. The classical families — Legendre,
Hermite, Laguerre, Jacobi — and their orthogonality properties were
codified in the nineteenth century and brought into modern form in
Szegő’s monograph
[
5
]
; see also

[
20
]
for the analytic background. Each classical
family is characterised by a three-term recurrence and a second-order
differential equation, two conditions which are equivalent under the
correspondence
[
5
, §3.2]
.

The latter strand begins with Krichever and Novikov, who introduced
algebras of meromorphic vector fields on a Riemann surface — with
poles confined to a finite set of marked points — as
higher-genus analogues of the Witt and Virasoro algebras

[
18
]
. The general theory of Krichever–Novikov
type algebras and their universal central extensions has been
developed systematically since; we refer to

[
8
,
9
]
for a comprehensive
account. The representation-theoretic side draws on the
Verma-module formalism for affine Lie algebras

[
6
,
11
]
and the broader theory of
contravariant forms initiated by Shapovalov
[
7
]
; on
the conformal side it is informed by the work of Feigin and Fuchs

[
15
]
on Virasoro modules. In a different direction,
extended affine and map-algebra modules have been the subject of an
active recent literature

[
14
,
10
,
19
]
.

The two strands meet in the superelliptic setting. For the curve

u^{m}=P(t)
, explicit cocycle formulas for the universal central
extension of
\mathfrak{sl}_{2}\otimes A_{m}
were obtained in

[
3
]
, where it was observed empirically
that the centre relations realise families of orthogonal polynomials
that extend the classical list — with the Legendre family appearing
in the hyperelliptic case and new families in the higher branch
degrees. Irreducibility of the relevant
\varphi
-Verma modules in
the hyperelliptic case was established in
[
1
]
. Left open
in both works was the representation-theoretic mechanism by which
the analytic phenomena — the orthogonality of the polynomial basis,
the second-order differential equation governing it, and (in special
cases) completeness — arise from the module theory of the
underlying Heisenberg subalgebra
{\mathcal{H}_{m}}
. The present paper supplies
that mechanism in the hyperelliptic case and conjectures its
extension.

Motivation

In an earlier strand of the author’s work on universal central
extensions of Krichever–Novikov-type algebras attached to superelliptic
curves
u^{m}=P(x)

[
3
,
1
]

orthogonal polynomial families were observed to arise as a structural
feature of the centre. The centre of the universal central extension
of

is finite-dimensional, and the
generators satisfy three-term recurrences — the defining relation of
orthogonal polynomial systems. The dimension of the centre grows with
the genus and the branch degree, and for

polynomial families lie beyond the classical list. In that earlier
work, the analytic phenomena — orthogonality, the second-order
differential equation, and (in special cases) completeness of the

relation to the module theory of the superelliptic Heisenberg

The present paper addresses this gap in the hyperelliptic case (

and partially in the general superelliptic case. We show that:

The canonical Shapovalov form on the

(2)

In the hyperelliptic case, the Legendre differential operator arises

Relation to prior work

Classical Shapovalov form.
The contravariant form on Verma modules for
Kac–Moody algebras
[

6

irreducibility and detecting singular vectors. This paper applies the same machinery
to the Heisenberg subalgebra of the UCE in the superelliptic setting, where the
form diagonalises in a basis of polynomial vectors, identifying orthogonality
as a module-theoretic phenomenon.

Sugawara construction.
The Sugawara element in affine Lie algebras

[
11
]
is a canonical central element whose eigenvalues on highest-weight
modules yield the conformal weights in conformal field theory. Our element
\Omega
is
the standard Sugawara stress-energy zero mode of the free boson (

charge), built from the Heisenberg subalgebra
\mathcal{H}_{2}

of this paper is not the Sugawara element itself, but the construction of an

-equivariant polynomial quotient

on which the descent

acts diagonally with
simple spectrum, together with the Legendre identification

, which intertwines the descended
Sugawara operator with the classical Legendre differential operator
L
. The
combined map

thus realises the
\varphi
-Verma module as
a graded presentation of the polynomial eigenfunctions of
L
.

Module theory for map algebras.
Map algebras and their representations have been
extensively studied
[
10
,
14
]
. Our approach borrows
the weight-space and highest-weight module formalism from this literature, applied to the
Krichever–Novikov setting.

2.
Algebraic setup

2.1.
The superelliptic algebra and its Heisenberg subalgebra

Let
m\geq 2
be an integer and let
p(t)\in\mathbb{C}[t^{\pm 1}]
be a
Laurent polynomial. In this series, we specialize to the
palindromic

case
p(t)=1-2at^{r}+t^{2r}
, where
a\in\mathbb{C}
is a parameter
and
r\geq 1
is a structural integer (e.g.,
r=1
for hyperelliptic,

r=2
for a certain quartic). Define the superelliptic algebra

A_{m}=\mathbb{C}[t^{\pm 1},u]\big/(u^{m}-p(t)).

The algebra
A_{m}
is the coordinate ring of the superelliptic curve

u^{m}=p(t)
in the Krichever–Novikov two-point framework.

Given
\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{sl}_{2}
(though the construction extends to
any simple Lie algebra), consider the current algebra
\mathfrak{g}\otimes A_{m}

and its universal central extension (UCE)
\widetilde{\mathfrak{g}\otimes A_{m}}
.
The cocycle structure of the UCE is encoded in explicit formulas
\psi_{mn}^{(ij)}(a)
,
classical in the loop case (Kassel–Loday
[
2
]
) and
extended to the superelliptic setting in
[
3
]
.

The
superelliptic Heisenberg algebra

{\mathcal{H}_{m}}\subset\widetilde{\mathfrak{g}\otimes A_{m}}

is the abelian subalgebra spanned by the Heisenberg-type generators

\{b_{n}^{(j)}:n\in\mathbb{Z},1\leq j\leq\lfloor m/2\rfloor\}
arising from
the odd powers of
u
in the Laurent expansion. (For
\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{sl}_{2}
,
the relevant Chevalley generators pair to form such degrees-of-freedom.)
The Heisenberg algebra inherits the central extension, reflected in
the bracket relation with non-trivial cocycle.

Definition 2.1

(Superelliptic Heisenberg algebra)
.

The superelliptic Heisenberg algebra
{\mathcal{H}_{m}}
is the abelian Lie algebra
(in the central-extension sense) generated by:

•

Generators
\{b_{n}^{(j)}:n\in\mathbb{Z},1\leq j\leq\lfloor m/2\rfloor\}
,
each of degree
n
.

•

Central element
c
(degree 0).

The bracket relations are given by

[b_{m}^{(i)},b_{n}^{(j)}]=\psi_{mn}^{(ij)}(a)\cdot c,\quad[c,\cdot]=0,

where
\psi_{mn}^{(ij)}(a)
are the UCE cocycle coefficients, skew-symmetric
in
(m,n)
, depending on the parameter
a
(or more generally, on the
branch locus coefficients of
p(t)
). These coefficients are computed
explicitly in
[
3
]
; the loop-algebra special
case is the classical Kassel–Loday cocycle
[
2
]
.

Running example (hyperelliptic case):
For
m=2
,
r=1
,
we have a single family of generators
b_{n}
(omitting superscript
j=1
),
with cocycle
\psi_{mn}(a)=\delta_{m+n,0}\omega_{1}
, where

\omega_{1}>0
is a normalization constant depending only on
p(t)
.
The brackets simplify to

[b_{m},b_{n}]=\delta_{m+n,0}\omega_{1}\,c.

This is the Heisenberg algebra of
[
1
]
.

Remark 2.2
.

In the hyperelliptic case
m=2
,
r=1
, one has

\psi_{mn}(a)=\delta_{m+n,0}\omega_{1}
for all
m,n
.
This simplification is the key input for the Sugawara construction
in Section
4
.

2.2.
Triangular decomposition

The
triangular decomposition
of
{\mathcal{H}_{m}}
is defined by the
natural grading on generators with respect to the Laurent degree
n
:

{\mathcal{H}_{m}}={\mathcal{H}_{m}}_{+}\oplus{\mathcal{H}_{m}}_{0}\oplus{\mathcal{H}_{m}}_{-},

where

•

{\mathcal{H}_{m}}_{+}=\bigoplus_{n>0}\mathbb{C}b_{n}^{(j)}
(positive-degree generators),

•

{\mathcal{H}_{m}}_{0}=\bigoplus_{j}\mathbb{C}b_{0}^{(j)}\oplus\mathbb{C}c
(degree-zero Cartan),

•

{\mathcal{H}_{m}}_{-}=\bigoplus_{n<0}\mathbb{C}b_{n}^{(j)}
(negative-degree generators).

This is the standard algebraic triangular decomposition, grading by
the Laurent index
n
. Geometrically, this coincides with the
almost-graded structure of Schlichenmaier
[
8
]

restricted to the Heisenberg subalgebra of the universal central
extension. (The alternative geometric decomposition — in/out decomposition
from the branch points — is distinct; for this paper, we use the
algebraic grading.)

The triangular decomposition makes
{\mathcal{H}_{m}}
suitable for the standard
construction of Verma modules and highest-weight representations.

2.3.
The module category

We work with the standard category of highest-weight
{\mathcal{H}_{m}}
-modules.
An
{\mathcal{H}_{m}}
-module
M
is called a
weight module
if it decomposes as

M=\bigoplus_{\mu\in{\mathcal{H}_{m}}_{0}^{*}}M_{\mu},

where
M_{\mu}=\{v\in M:b_{0}^{(j)}v=\mu(b_{0}^{(j)})v\text{ and }cv=\mu(c)v\}
.

A highest-weight module for
{\mathcal{H}_{m}}
is specified by:

(1)

A linear functional
\varphi:{\mathcal{H}_{m}}_{0}\to\mathbb{C}
(the
weight
).
We assume
\varphi(c)\neq 0
(the central charge is non-zero),
as is standard in representation theory of central extensions.

(2)

A highest-weight vector
v_{\varphi}
satisfying:

•

b_{n}^{(j)}v_{\varphi}=0
for all
n>0
and all
j
,

•

b_{0}^{(j)}v_{\varphi}=\varphi(b_{0}^{(j)})v_{\varphi}
,

•

cv_{\varphi}=\varphi(c)v_{\varphi}
.

(3)

The action of
{\mathcal{H}_{m}}_{-}
is locally nilpotent, i.e.,
for each
v\in M
and each
n<0
, the operators
b_{n}^{(j)}

act nilpotently on the finite-dimensional weight spaces.

Such modules are called
\varphi
-modules in the language of
[
10
,
14
]
,
where analogous constructions for map algebras are developed.

The main object of study in this paper is the
\varphi
-Verma module
,
the universal highest-weight module generated by a highest-weight vector
of weight
\varphi
.

b_{n}^{(j)}\cdot 1=0

•

b_{0}^{(j)}\cdot 1=\varphi(b_{0}^{(j)})

The module

is generated by the image of

of weight

This construction generalizes

]

standard Verma-module constructions for Kac–Moody algebras

]
.

Remark 3.2
.

By the PBW theorem,
M(\varphi)
has a
\mathbb{C}
-basis consisting of monomials

b_{-n_{1}}^{(j_{1})}\cdots b_{-n_{k}}^{(j_{k})}v_{\varphi}
with

The grading is inherited from the degree in

(-k)
subspace of

3.2.
The contravariant form

Definition 3.3

(Contravariant anti-involution)
.

Define the linear map
\omega:{\mathcal{H}_{m}}\to{\mathcal{H}_{m}}
by

\omega(b_{n}^{(j)})=b_{-n}^{(j)},\quad\omega(c)=c.

This is an anti-involution:
\omega(\omega(x))=x
and
\omega([x,y])=[\omega(y),\omega(x)]
.
Extend
\omega
to
U({\mathcal{H}_{m}})
as an anti-automorphism of the universal
enveloping algebra, so that

\omega(xy)=\omega(y)\omega(x).

This is the standard contravariance map for the Heisenberg algebra with
respect to the triangular decomposition
{\mathcal{H}_{m}}={\mathcal{H}_{m}}_{+}\oplus{\mathcal{H}_{m}}_{0}\oplus{\mathcal{H}_{m}}_{-}
.

Lemma 3.4

(Existence and uniqueness of the Shapovalov form)
.

There exists a unique (up to scalar) symmetric bilinear form

\mathcal{S}_{\varphi}:M(\varphi)\times M(\varphi)\to\mathbb{C}
satisfying:

(i)

Normalization:
\mathcal{S}_{\varphi}(v_{\varphi},v_{\varphi})=1
.

(ii)

Contravariance:
\mathcal{S}_{\varphi}(x\cdot v,w)=\mathcal{S}_{\varphi}(v,\omega(x)\cdot w)

for all
x\in U({\mathcal{H}_{m}})
and
v,w\in M(\varphi)
.

(iii)

Weight preservation:
\mathcal{S}_{\varphi}(v,w)=0
whenever
v
and
w

lie in distinct weight spaces of
M(\varphi)
.

Proof.

Uniqueness:
Suppose
\mathcal{S}_{\varphi}
satisfies (i)–(iii). By contravariance,

\mathcal{S}_{\varphi}(uv_{\varphi},vv_{\varphi})=\mathcal{S}_{\varphi}(v_{\varphi},\omega(u)vv_{\varphi}).

Since
v_{\varphi}
is the unique highest-weight vector (up to scaling) and

\omega(u)v
may be expressed in the PBW basis, the form is determined
by its values on the basis monomials. Normalization fixes the overall scale.

Existence:
Define
\mathcal{S}_{\varphi}
on the highest-weight vector by

\mathcal{S}_{\varphi}(v_{\varphi},v_{\varphi}):=1
. For general
u,v\in U({\mathcal{H}_{m}})
, set

\mathcal{S}_{\varphi}(uv_{\varphi},vv_{\varphi}):=\varphi(\omega(u)v),

where the right side is interpreted as: compute the product
\omega(u)v

in
U({\mathcal{H}_{m}})
, then apply
\varphi
to the component that is in
{\mathcal{H}_{m}}_{0}

(the central element
c
contributes
\varphi(c)
times its coefficient).
By the PBW theorem and the structure of
{\mathcal{H}_{m}}
, this is well-defined and
extends to a contravariant form on all of
M(\varphi)
. The symmetry
\mathcal{S}_{\varphi}(v,w)=\mathcal{S}_{\varphi}(w,v)

follows from the antisymmetry of the cocycle
\psi
.

For further details and the standard treatment, see
[
7
,
6
]
Chapter 2.
∎

Remark 3.5
.

Denote by
G_{n}
the Gram matrix of
\mathcal{S}_{\varphi}
restricted to the weight-
(-n)

subspace of

The bridge to orthogonal polynomials uses the classical theory of

]

together with the explicit superelliptic polynomial families of

Lemma 3.6

(Properties of the dictionary map)
.

Let

(Weight grading)
The image

(-n)

with respect to the grading of

(D3)

Each

(1)

and the

(D4)

(Cocycle compatibility)
For all

Proof.

Property (D1) is the standard three-term recurrence of an orthogonal

[

We can now define the polynomial vectors precisely:

Theorem 3.8

(Shapovalov form and orthogonality)
.

Let

and let

-admissible functional for

Proof.

By the contravariance property (Lemma

∎

3.4.
Irreducibility criterion

Theorem 3.9

(Irreducibility via non-degeneracy, hyperelliptic case)
.

Let
m=2
and
\varphi:({\mathcal{H}_{m}})_{0}\to\mathbb{C}
with
\varphi(c)\neq 0
.
The following are equivalent:

(i)

The
\varphi
-Verma module

is irreducible.

(ii)

The Shapovalov form
\mathcal{S}_{\varphi}
is non-degenerate on

.

(iii)

The functional
\varphi
is
p
-admissible.

Proof.

(i)
\Leftrightarrow
(ii):
This equivalence holds for any

by the general theory of Verma modules with a contravariant form
(
[
6
]
Chapter 2): irreducibility of a highest-weight module is
equivalent to non-degeneracy of the contravariant form. If the form is degenerate,
its kernel is a non-trivial invariant subspace; conversely, if the module is
reducible, the maximal proper submodule lies in the kernel.

(i)
\Leftrightarrow
(iii):
For
m=2
,
[
1
]
Theorem B
establishes that irreducibility of

is equivalent to

-admissibility of

. This is a combinatorial condition on

relative to the

: the functional must satisfy a positivity condition related

.
∎

Conjecture 3.10

(Irreducibility for general

)
.

For all
m\geq 3
, the equivalence of
Theorem
3.9
extends to the superelliptic
Heisenberg algebra
{\mathcal{H}_{m}}
with multiple cocycle components

\omega_{1},\ldots,\omega_{\lfloor m/2\rfloor}
. The

-admissibility condition

avoids a discrete set of

.

Remark 3.11
.

The passage from
m=2
to
m\geq 3
introduces substantial algebraic
complexity. For

, the Heisenberg algebra
\mathcal{H}_{2}
has a

with a scalar cocycle

, and the

-admissibility

If

-admissible, then the orthogonal polynomial family

4.
Sugawara element, polynomial quotient, and Legendre identification

Throughout this section we work in the hyperelliptic case:

m=2
,
r=1
, so
{\mathcal{H}_{m}}=\mathcal{H}_{2}
with cocycle

\psi_{mn}(a)=\delta_{m+n,0}\omega_{1}
(Remark
2.2
).

The goal of this section is to construct an explicit chain of

\mathcal{H}_{2}
-equivariant maps

M(\varphi)\xrightarrow{\;\psi\;}\mathbb{C}[a]\xrightarrow{\;\Psi\;}\mathbb{C}[x]

and a quadratic operator
\Omega
on
M(\varphi)
whose pushforward under

\Phi:=\Psi\circ\psi
is the Legendre differential operator

L=(1-x^{2})\partial_{x}^{2}-2x\partial_{x}
.
The element
\Omega
is built from the Sugawara stress-energy zero mode

L_{0}
of the free boson; the map
\psi
collapses each PBW level to
a single power
a^{n}
; and
\Psi
identifies
a^{n}
with the Legendre
polynomial
P_{n}(x)
.

4.1.
The Sugawara stress-energy zero mode

Notation (normal ordering).

For two Heisenberg generators
b_{m},b_{n}
(
m,n\in\mathbb{Z}
), define the

normal-ordered product

{:}b_{m}b_{n}{:}\;=\;\begin{cases}b_{m}\,b_{n}&\text{if }m\leq 0,\\[2.0pt]
b_{n}\,b_{m}&\text{if }m>0,\end{cases}

i.e., negative-mode (and zero-mode) operators are placed to the left of
positive-mode operators. Because
[b_{m},b_{n}]=\psi_{mn}\,c
is a scalar
on
M(\varphi)
, the normal-ordered product differs from the bare product by a
scalar correction whenever
m+n=0
, and equals the bare product
otherwise. In particular, the sum

\sum_{k\geq 1}{:}b_{-k}\,b_{k}{:}=\sum_{k\geq 1}b_{-k}\,b_{k}

is already normal-ordered as written (each
-k
is negative, so each
factor stays in place).

Definition 4.1

(Sugawara zero mode
L_{0}
)
.

Let
\varphi
be
p
-admissible (in the sense of
Definition
2.6
, equivalently
[
1
]
) with

\varphi(c)\neq 0
, and assume
\omega_{1}\,\varphi(c)\neq 0
.
Define

L_{0}\;:=\;\frac{1}{\omega_{1}\,\varphi(c)}\;\sum_{k\geq 1}\;{:}b_{-k}\,b_{k}{:}\;=\;\frac{1}{\omega_{1}\,\varphi(c)}\;\sum_{k\geq 1}b_{-k}\,b_{k},

where the second equality uses the Notation block above.
On a weight vector
v\in M(\varphi)
of weight
-n
, only finitely many terms
in the sum are non-zero (those with
k\leq n
), so
L_{0}
acts as a
well-defined linear operator on
M(\varphi)
without any topological completion.

Remark 4.2

(Sugawara normalisation)
.

The element
L_{0}
is the zero mode of the standard Sugawara stress-energy
tensor of the free boson (central charge
c_{\mathrm{Vir}}=1
) — that is,
the operator that generates the energy grading on the free-boson Fock
space, with eigenvalue equal to the level of a PBW monomial; see

[
11
,
12
]
for the general construction. The
pre-factor
1/(\omega_{1}\,\varphi(c))
rescales away the cocycle normalizer
and the central charge of
\varphi
, so the eigenvalue of
L_{0}
on a
level-
n
PBW monomial is the integer
n
itself. This is the choice that makes
the subsequent intertwining with the classical Legendre operator
exact, with no parameter-dependent constants.

Lemma 4.3

(
L_{0}
is the level operator)
.

For every PBW monomial

v=b_{-n_{1}}\,b_{-n_{2}}\,\cdots\,b_{-n_{r}}\,v_{\varphi}

with
n_{1}\geq n_{2}\geq\cdots\geq n_{r}\geq 1
, one has

(2)

L_{0}\cdot v\;=\;n\cdot v,\qquad n:=n_{1}+n_{2}+\cdots+n_{r}.

Consequently
L_{0}
acts as the scalar
n
on the entire weight space

M(\varphi)[-n]
, and the spectrum of
L_{0}
on
M(\varphi)
is
\{0,1,2,\dots\}
with
multiplicity
p(n)
at level
n
, where
p(n)
is the partition function.

Proof.

We proceed by induction on the length
r
.
For
r=0
(
v=v_{\varphi}
), all
b_{k}v_{\varphi}=0
for
k\geq 1
, so
L_{0}\,v_{\varphi}=0=0\cdot v_{\varphi}
.
For
r\geq 1
, write
v=b_{-n_{1}}\,w
with
w=b_{-n_{2}}\cdots b_{-n_{r}}v_{\varphi}

of weight
-(n-n_{1})
. Using

[b_{k},b_{-n_{1}}]=\delta_{k,n_{1}}\,n_{1}\,\omega_{1}\,c

(the hyperelliptic cocycle, Remark
2.2
, with

\psi_{k,-k}=k\,\omega_{1}
), we compute

\displaystyle L_{0}\cdot v

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\omega_{1}\,\varphi(c)}\sum_{k\geq 1}b_{-k}\,b_{k}\,b_{-n_{1}}\,w

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\omega_{1}\,\varphi(c)}\sum_{k\geq 1}b_{-k}\bigl(b_{-n_{1}}\,b_{k}+\delta_{k,n_{1}}\,n_{1}\,\omega_{1}\,c\bigr)\,w

\displaystyle=b_{-n_{1}}\,\Bigl(\tfrac{1}{\omega_{1}\,\varphi(c)}\sum_{k\geq 1}b_{-k}b_{k}\Bigr)\,w+\frac{n_{1}\,\omega_{1}\,\varphi(c)}{\omega_{1}\,\varphi(c)}\,b_{-n_{1}}\,w

\displaystyle=b_{-n_{1}}\,(L_{0}\,w)\;+\;n_{1}\,v\;=\;(n-n_{1})\,v+n_{1}\,v\;=\;n\,v,

where we used the inductive hypothesis
L_{0}\,w=(n-n_{1})\,w
in the last
line, and
c
acts as the scalar
\varphi(c)
on
M(\varphi)
throughout.
This proves (
2
).

The level-
n
weight space
M(\varphi)[-n]
is spanned by all such PBW monomials
with
n_{1}+\cdots+n_{r}=n
; the count of such monomials is
p(n)
by the
PBW theorem applied to
\mathcal{H}_{2}^{-}
. Each such monomial is a

L_{0}
-eigenvector with eigenvalue
n
, so
L_{0}
acts as the scalar
n

on
M(\varphi)[-n]
.
∎

4.2.
The Casimir-type element
\Omega

Definition 4.4

(Casimir-type element

)
.

Set

Equivalently,

(

.

For every

(

By Lemma

.

The polynomial quotient

(i)
.

.

(iii)
.
On a level-

PBW monomial

\psi(L_{0}\,v)=\psi(n\,v)=n\,a^{n}=(a\partial_{a})(a^{n})=(a\partial_{a})\,\psi(v)

by Lemma

.

(iv)
.
Apply (iii) twice:
\psi\circ\Omega=-\psi\circ L_{0}\circ(L_{0}+\mathrm{Id})=-(a\partial_{a})\circ\psi\circ(L_{0}+\mathrm{Id})=-(a\partial_{a})\bigl((a\partial_{a})+1\bigr)\circ\psi
.
∎

4.4.
The Legendre identification
\Psi

Definition 4.8

(Legendre identification
\Psi
)
.

Let
\{P_{n}(x)\}_{n\geq 0}
denote the standard Legendre polynomials on

[-1,1]
, normalised by
P_{n}(1)=1
. Define a linear isomorphism of graded
vector spaces

\Psi\colon\mathbb{C}[a]\xrightarrow{\;\sim\;}\mathbb{C}[x],\qquad\Psi(a^{n}):=P_{n}(x).

Both sides are

-graded by polynomial degree (with

\Psi

.
The inverse

\mathbb{C}[x]
.

Definition 4.9

(Composite mechanism

)
.

Let

\Phi\;:=\;\Psi\circ\psi\colon M(\varphi)\longrightarrow\mathbb{C}[x].

By construction,
\Phi
sends every level-

PBW monomial to

.

4.5.
The Sugawara–Legendre intertwining

The next theorem is the main result of this section; it replaces the
quadratic-eigenvalue claim of the Casimir element on individual
polynomial vectors by a cleaner, equivalent statement at the level of
the quotient
M(\varphi)\to\mathbb{C}[x]
.

Theorem 4.10

(Representation mechanism via Sugawara and Legendre)
.

Let
L_{0}
,
\Omega
,
\psi
,
\Psi
,
\Phi=\Psi\circ\psi
be as in
Definitions
4.1
,
4.4
,
4.6
,

4.8
,
4.9
, and let
L=(1-x^{2})\partial_{x}^{2}-2x\partial_{x}
denote the classical Legendre differential operator.
Then:

(i)

\psi\colon M(\varphi)\to\mathbb{C}[a]
is a well-defined surjective linear map
sending each level-
n
weight space to the line
\mathbb{C}\cdot a^{n}
.

(ii)

\Phi=\Psi\circ\psi
sends each level-
n
weight space to the line

\mathbb{C}\cdot P_{n}(x)\subset\mathbb{C}[x]
.

(iii)

\Phi
intertwines the action of
\Omega
on
M(\varphi)
with the action of

L
on
\mathbb{C}[x]
:

(6)

\Phi\bigl(\Omega\cdot v\bigr)\;=\;L\bigl(\Phi(v)\bigr)\qquad\text{for all }v\in M(\varphi).

(iv)

Equivalently, for every
n\geq 0
and every
v\in M(\varphi)[-n]
,

(7)

L\bigl(\Phi(v)\bigr)\;=\;-n(n+1)\,\Phi(v),

which is the classical Legendre eigenvalue identity for
P_{n}(x)
.

Proof.

Step 1:
(i)
.

This is Lemma
4.7
(
(i)
)–(
(ii)
).

Step 2:
(ii)
.

By Definition
4.8
,
\Psi(a^{n})=P_{n}(x)
. Combining with Step 1,

\Phi(M(\varphi)[-n])=\Psi(\mathbb{C}\cdot a^{n})=\mathbb{C}\cdot P_{n}(x)
.

Step 3: pushforward of
\Omega
along
\psi
.

By Lemma
4.7
(
(iv)
),

\psi\circ\Omega=-(a\partial_{a})\bigl((a\partial_{a})+1\bigr)\circ\psi
.

Step 4: pushforward of
-(a\partial_{a})\bigl((a\partial_{a})+1\bigr)

along
\Psi
.

This is the classical fact that the Legendre differential operator

L=(1-x^{2})\partial_{x}^{2}-2x\partial_{x}
satisfies the eigenvalue identity

L\,P_{n}(x)=-n(n+1)\,P_{n}(x)
for all
n\geq 0

(see, e.g.,
[
20
, Ch. 12, eq. 12.3.6]
).
On the basis
\{a^{n}\}
, both

-(a\partial_{a})((a\partial_{a})+1)
acting by
-n(n+1)
on
a^{n}
, and

L
acting by
-n(n+1)
on
P_{n}(x)
, agree under
\Psi
:

\Psi\Bigl(-(a\partial_{a})((a\partial_{a})+1)\,a^{n}\Bigr)=\Psi(-n(n+1)\,a^{n})=-n(n+1)\,P_{n}(x)=L\,P_{n}(x)=L\,\Psi(a^{n}).

By linearity, this extends to all of
\mathbb{C}[a]
:

\Psi\circ\bigl(-(a\partial_{a})((a\partial_{a})+1)\bigr)=L\circ\Psi
.

Step 5:
(iii)
.

Combine Steps 3 and 4:

\Phi\circ\Omega=\Psi\circ\psi\circ\Omega=\Psi\circ\bigl(-(a\partial_{a})((a\partial_{a})+1)\bigr)\circ\psi=L\circ\Psi\circ\psi=L\circ\Phi.

This is (
6
).

Step 6:
(iv)
.

For
v\in M(\varphi)[-n]
, Lemma
4.5
gives
\Omega\cdot v=-n(n+1)\,v
.
Then (
6
) yields

L\,\Phi(v)=\Phi(\Omega\cdot v)=\Phi(-n(n+1)\,v)=-n(n+1)\,\Phi(v)
,
which is (
7
).
∎

Remark 4.11

(Symbolic verification at low levels)

have been

4.6.
Corollaries: the Casimir tower and the generating function

The Casimir tower and the Legendre generating-function identity now
follow as immediate corollaries of Theorem
4.10
.

Definition 4.12

(Casimir tower)
.

We call the family
\{\Omega^{r}\}_{r\geq 1}
of iterates of the
Casimir-type element
\Omega=-L_{0}(L_{0}+\mathrm{Id})
of
Definition
4.4
the
Casimir tower
generated by

\Omega
. Each
\Omega^{r}
is a well-defined linear operator on
M(\varphi)
acting
diagonally on the level-
n
weight space; the structural properties
of the family are recorded in Corollary
4.13
below.

Corollary 4.13

(Casimir tower)
.

For each integer
r\geq 1
:

(i)

\Omega^{r}
is a well-defined linear operator on
M(\varphi)
, acting on each
level-
n
weight space as the scalar
(-n(n+1))^{r}
.

(ii)

\Phi
intertwines
\Omega^{r}
with
L^{r}
:

\Phi\circ\Omega^{r}=L^{r}\circ\Phi
.

In particular, the family
\{\Omega^{r}\}_{r\geq 1}
pushes forward under

\Phi
to the family
\{L^{r}\}_{r\geq 1}
of iterated Legendre operators;
this resolves Open Question S2.

Proof.

(i)
Lemma
4.5
shows
\Omega
is a level-dependent
scalar
-n(n+1)
on
M(\varphi)[-n]
; hence
\Omega^{r}
is the scalar
(-n(n+1))^{r}

on the same weight space.

(ii)
By Theorem
4.10
(iii),
\Phi\circ\Omega=L\circ\Phi
. Iterating gives, for
r\geq 2
,

\Phi\circ\Omega^{r}=(\Phi\circ\Omega)\circ\Omega^{r-1}=L\circ(\Phi\circ\Omega^{r-1})=L^{r}\circ\Phi
by induction on
r
.
∎

Remark 4.14

(The
r=2
case)
.

For
r=2
,
\Omega^{2}
acts on
M(\varphi)[-n]
as
n^{2}(n+1)^{2}
, and
Corollary
4.13
shows that
\Phi
identifies
\Omega^{2}

with
L^{2}
. Structural properties of the higher iterates
L^{r}
—
factorisation into lower-order Legendre-type operators, eigenvalue
distributions, and singular loci — are independent of the
representation-theoretic input and are taken up elsewhere.

Corollary 4.15

(Generating function intertwining)
.

Let
G(z,x)=(1-2xz+z^{2})^{-1/2}=\sum_{n\geq 0}P_{n}(x)\,z^{n}
denote
the Legendre generating function, and let
\mathcal{E}:=z^{2}\partial_{z}^{2}+2z\partial_{z}=z\partial_{z}(z\partial_{z}+1)
be the associated Euler-type
operator in the
z
-variable. Then for every
r\geq 1
:

(8)

L^{r}{}_{(x)}\,G(z,x)\;=\;(-1)^{r}\,\mathcal{E}^{r}{}_{(z)}\,G(z,x).

Proof.

For
r=1
: a direct computation using
z^{2}\partial_{z}^{2}(z^{n})=n(n-1)z^{n}

and
2z\partial_{z}(z^{n})=2n\,z^{n}
gives

\mathcal{E}\,G=\sum_{n\geq 0}(n(n-1)+2n)\,P_{n}(x)\,z^{n}=\sum_{n\geq 0}n(n+1)\,P_{n}(x)\,z^{n}
.
Combining with
L\,P_{n}=-n(n+1)\,P_{n}
yields

L_{(x)}\,G=-\mathcal{E}_{(z)}\,G
.
For
r\geq 2
,
L_{(x)}
and
\mathcal{E}_{(z)}
act on different
variables and hence commute; iterating gives

L^{r}_{(x)}\,G=(-1)^{r}\,\mathcal{E}^{r}_{(z)}\,G
.
∎

Remark 4.16

(Operator identification)
.

On the space
\bigl\{G_{f}(z,x)=\sum_{n}f_{n}\,P_{n}(x)\,z^{n}\bigr\}
of
Legendre-expanded series, Corollary
4.15
gives

L_{(x)}=-\mathcal{E}_{(z)}=-(z^{2}\partial_{z}^{2}+2z\partial_{z})
:
the Legendre differential operator in
x
is interchangeable with the
second-order Euler–Cauchy operator in
z
, and at every order
r\geq 1

their iterates satisfy
L^{r}_{(x)}=(-\mathcal{E}_{(z)})^{r}
.

Remark 4.17

(Comparison with the original Casimir formulation)
.

Earlier drafts of this paper attempted to define the Casimir as a single
quadratic Sugawara-type element acting on the orthogonalised polynomial
basis
\{\widetilde{P}_{n}\}
of Section
3.3
, with
eigenvalue
-n(n+1)
on each
\widetilde{P}_{n}
. However, any element
defined as a quadratic free-boson expression in
\{b_{-k}b_{k}\}
with
constant scalar coefficients acts on the entire weight space
M(\varphi)[-n]

by a single scalar, by Lemma
4.3
; in particular it cannot
distinguish
\widetilde{P}_{n}
from any other vector at level
n
.
The present formulation isolates the level-
n
scalar

-n(n+1)
in the operator
\Omega
via Lemma
4.5
and
recovers the Legendre identity at the level of the quotient
\Phi

rather than at the level of individual polynomial vectors.
The orthogonalisation
\widetilde{P}_{n}
enters separately, in the Fock
realisation of §
5
, where it is recovered as the unique
Shapovalov-orthogonal representative of the level-
n
class.

5.
Fock space realization

This section constructs the Fock space realization of the
\varphi
-Verma
module
M(\varphi)
for the hyperelliptic Heisenberg algebra
\mathcal{H}_{2}
.
The main result (Theorem
5.2
) is that
M(\varphi)
is canonically
isomorphic to a standard Fock space
\mathcal{F}_{\varphi}
.
The polynomial vectors
\{\tilde{P}_{n}\}_{n\geq 0}
appear as the
Gram–Schmidt basis of
\mathcal{F}_{\varphi}
with respect to the Fock
inner product.
This gives a direct realization of the Shapovalov orthogonality as
an inner product of many-body states, partially resolving Open Question S3.

5.1.
The Fock module

Definition 5.1

(Fock vacuum and Fock module)
.

Fix
\varphi\colon\mathcal{H}_{2}\to\mathbb{C}
a
p
-admissible linear functional
(in the sense of
[
1
]
).
The
Fock vacuum
is the vector
v_{\varphi}\in M(\varphi)
characterized by:

(9)

\displaystyle b_{n}\,v_{\varphi}

\displaystyle=0\quad\text{for all }n\geq 1,

(10)

\displaystyle b_{0}\,v_{\varphi}

\displaystyle=\varphi(b_{0})\,v_{\varphi},

(11)

\displaystyle c\,v_{\varphi}

\displaystyle=\varphi(c)\,v_{\varphi}.

The
creation operators
are
a_{n}^{\dagger}:=b_{-n}
for
n\geq 1
.
The
Fock module
is

\mathcal{F}_{\varphi}\;:=\;\mathbb{C}[a_{1}^{\dagger},a_{2}^{\dagger},a_{3}^{\dagger},\ldots]\cdot v_{\varphi}\;=\;\Span_{\mathbb{C}}\bigl\{\,(a_{n_{1}}^{\dagger})^{k_{1}}(a_{n_{2}}^{\dagger})^{k_{2}}\cdots v_{\varphi}\;\bigm|\;n_{1}>n_{2}>\cdots\geq 1,\;k_{i}\geq 0\,\bigr\}.

The Fock module
\mathcal{F}_{\varphi}
is graded by
level

(the total mode number): a monomial

(a_{n_{1}}^{\dagger})^{k_{1}}\cdots v_{\varphi}
has level
\ell=\sum_{i}n_{i}k_{i}
.
The level-
\ell
component
\mathcal{F}_{\varphi}[\ell]
has dimension
p(\ell)
,
the number of integer partitions of
\ell
.
In particular,
\mathcal{F}_{\varphi}[0]=\mathbb{C}\,v_{\varphi}
,

\mathcal{F}_{\varphi}[1]=\mathbb{C}\,a_{1}^{\dagger}v_{\varphi}
, and

\mathcal{F}_{\varphi}[2]=\mathbb{C}\,a_{2}^{\dagger}v_{\varphi}\oplus\mathbb{C}\,(a_{1}^{\dagger})^{2}v_{\varphi}
.

Theorem 5.2

(Fock module isomorphism)
.

The
\mathcal{H}_{2}
-module
M(\varphi)
is isomorphic to
\mathcal{F}_{\varphi}

as a
\mathcal{H}_{2}
-module.

Proof.

The negative-mode subalgebra
\mathcal{H}_{2}^{-}=\Span\{b_{-n}:n\geq 1\}

is abelian: for
k,l>0
, the cocycle satisfies
\psi_{-k,-l}=0

since
(-k)+(-l)<0\neq 0
, so
[b_{-k},b_{-l}]=\psi_{-k,-l}\,c=0
.
By the PBW theorem,
U(\mathcal{H}_{2}^{-})
is therefore the polynomial algebra

\mathbb{C}[b_{-1},b_{-2},\ldots]
.
The
\varphi
-Verma module
M(\varphi)
is defined as the induced module

M(\varphi)=U(\mathcal{H}_{2})\otimes_{U(\mathcal{H}_{2}^{\geq 0})}\mathbb{C}_{\varphi}
,
where
\mathbb{C}_{\varphi}
is the one-dimensional module on which

b_{n}
(
n\geq 0
) and
c
act by
\varphi(b_{n})
and
\varphi(c)
respectively.
By PBW,
M(\varphi)\cong U(\mathcal{H}_{2}^{-})
as a vector space, with
the generator
1\otimes 1
playing the role of
v_{\varphi}
.
This is exactly
\mathcal{F}_{\varphi}
.
∎

Remark 5.3
.

Theorem
5.2
formalises a standard fact in the
representation theory of Heisenberg algebras:
for an abelian Heisenberg (as opposed to the Virasoro algebra, where
this identification is non-trivial), the Verma module equals the Fock space.
The content of the present paper is not the isomorphism itself but
the explicit realisation of the Shapovalov form and the polynomial
basis within
\mathcal{F}_{\varphi}
.

5.2.
Fock inner product and Shapovalov form

The antiautomorphism
\sigma\colon\mathcal{H}_{2}\to\mathcal{H}_{2}
,

\sigma(b_{n})=b_{-n}
,
\sigma(c)=c
, defines the Shapovalov form
(Section
3.2
).
Its restriction to
\mathcal{F}_{\varphi}
is the
Fock inner product
:

Proposition 5.4

(Fock inner product formula)
.

For level-
1
states:

(12)

\mathcal{S}_{\varphi}\bigl(a_{n}^{\dagger}\,v_{\varphi},\;a_{m}^{\dagger}\,v_{\varphi}\bigr)\;=\;\delta_{n,m}\cdot n\cdot\psi_{-n,n}\cdot\varphi(c),

where
\psi_{-n,n}=\psi_{n,-n}
is the
(n,-n)
cocycle coefficient.
For the hyperelliptic case (
m=2
,
r=1
):

\psi_{-n,n}=\omega_{1}
for all
n\geq 1
,
so

(13)

\mathcal{S}_{\varphi}\bigl(a_{n}^{\dagger}\,v_{\varphi},\;a_{m}^{\dagger}\,v_{\varphi}\bigr)\;=\;\delta_{n,m}\cdot n\cdot\omega_{1}\cdot\varphi(c).

For general multi-mode states, the Shapovalov form is computed by
the contravariance relation:

(14)

\mathcal{S}_{\varphi}(b_{-n}\,v,\;w)\;=\;\mathcal{S}_{\varphi}(v,\;b_{n}\,w)\quad\text{for all }v,w\in\mathcal{F}_{\varphi},\;n\geq 1.

Proof.

For (
12
):

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{\varphi}(b_{-n}\,v_{\varphi},\;b_{-m}\,v_{\varphi})

\displaystyle=\mathcal{S}_{\varphi}(v_{\varphi},\;b_{n}\,b_{-m}\,v_{\varphi})\quad\text{(by \eqref{eq:fock-contra})}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{S}_{\varphi}(v_{\varphi},\;b_{-m}\,b_{n}\,v_{\varphi}+[b_{n},b_{-m}]\,v_{\varphi})

\displaystyle=\mathcal{S}_{\varphi}(v_{\varphi},\;0+\psi_{n,-m}\,c\,v_{\varphi})\quad\text{(since }b_{n}\,v_{\varphi}=0\text{ and }[b_{n},b_{-m}]=\delta_{n,m}\,\psi_{n,-n}\,c\text{)}

\displaystyle=\delta_{n,m}\;\psi_{n,-n}\;\varphi(c)\;\mathcal{S}_{\varphi}(v_{\varphi},v_{\varphi}).

Normalising so that
\mathcal{S}_{\varphi}(v_{\varphi},v_{\varphi})=1
and noting that

\psi_{n,-n}=n\,\omega_{1}
(for the hyperelliptic cocycle, where

[b_{n},b_{-n}]=n\,\omega_{1}\,c
) gives (
13
).
∎

5.3.
Low-degree polynomial vectors in the Fock basis

We now identify the polynomial vectors
\tilde{P}_{n}\in\mathcal{F}_{\varphi}

at low levels.

Level 0
(
n=0
):
\mathcal{F}_{\varphi}[0]=\mathbb{C}\,v_{\varphi}
is
one-dimensional.

\tilde{P}_{0}\;=\;v_{\varphi}.

Level 1
(
n=1
):
\mathcal{F}_{\varphi}[1]=\mathbb{C}\,a_{1}^{\dagger}\,v_{\varphi}

is one-dimensional.
By Proposition
5.4
,

\mathcal{S}_{\varphi}(a_{1}^{\dagger}\,v_{\varphi},\;a_{1}^{\dagger}\,v_{\varphi})=\omega_{1}\,\varphi(c)
.
Since
\mathcal{S}_{\varphi}(\tilde{P}_{1},\tilde{P}_{1})=h_{1}=\frac{2}{3}
,
we normalise:

(15)

\tilde{P}_{1}\;=\;\sqrt{\frac{h_{1}}{\omega_{1}\,\varphi(c)}}\;a_{1}^{\dagger}\,v_{\varphi}\;=\;\sqrt{\frac{2}{3\,\omega_{1}\,\varphi(c)}}\;b_{-1}\,v_{\varphi}.

With the normalisation
\omega_{1}=1
,
\varphi(c)=1
:

\tilde{P}_{1}=\sqrt{2/3}\;b_{-1}\,v_{\varphi}
.

Level 2
(
n=2
):
\mathcal{F}_{\varphi}[2]
is two-dimensional,
spanned by
\bigl\{a_{2}^{\dagger}\,v_{\varphi},\;(a_{1}^{\dagger})^{2}\,v_{\varphi}\bigr\}=\bigl\{b_{-2}\,v_{\varphi},\;b_{-1}^{2}\,v_{\varphi}\bigr\}

The Shapovalov form on this basis (with
\omega_{1}=\varphi(c)=1
) is:

(16)

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{\varphi}(b_{-2}\,v_{\varphi},\;b_{-2}\,v_{\varphi})

\displaystyle=2,

(17)

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{\varphi}(b_{-2}\,v_{\varphi},\;b_{-1}^{2}\,v_{\varphi})

\displaystyle=0,

(18)

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{\varphi}(b_{-1}^{2}\,v_{\varphi},\;b_{-1}^{2}\,v_{\varphi})

\displaystyle=2.

Equation (

) follows from

\mathcal{S}_{\varphi}(b_{-2}\,v_{\varphi},\;b_{-1}^{2}\,v_{\varphi})=\mathcal{S}_{\varphi}(v_{\varphi},\;b_{2}\,b_{-1}^{2}\,v_{\varphi})
,
and

(since
[b_{2},b_{-1}]=0
for

.
Equation (

) follows from the repeated application
of the contravariance relation, using
[b_{1},b_{-1}]=\omega_{1}c

and
b_{1}\,v_{\varphi}=0
.

The polynomial vector
\tilde{P}_{2}
is characterised as the unique
(up to a scalar in
h_{2}^{1/2}
) Shapovalov-orthogonal representative,
within the level-
2
weight space
\mathcal{F}_{\varphi}[2]
, of the
level-
2
class
\bar{P}_{2}\in\Phi(M(\varphi))=\mathbb{C}\cdot P_{2}(x)
of
Theorem
4.10
(ii). Equivalently,
\tilde{P}_{2}
is
obtained by Gram–Schmidt orthogonalisation of the

\{b_{-2}\,v_{\varphi},\,b_{-1}^{2}\,v_{\varphi}\}
against the

\{v_{\varphi},\,b_{-1}\,v_{\varphi}\}

From the Gram matrix

Proposition 5.5

(Fock expression for
\tilde{P}_{2}
)
.

In the Fock basis
\{b_{-2}\,v_{\varphi},\;b_{-1}^{2}\,v_{\varphi}\}
,
the polynomial vector
\tilde{P}_{2}
(with
\omega_{1}=\varphi(c)=1

(19)

\tilde{P}_{2}\;=\;\sqrt{\frac{h_{2}}{4}}\,b_{-2}\,v_{\varphi}\;+\;\sqrt{\frac{h_{2}}{4}}\,b_{-1}^{2}\,v_{\varphi}\;=\;\sqrt{\frac{1}{10}}\bigl(b_{-2}\,v_{\varphi}+b_{-1}^{2}\,v_{\varphi}\bigr),

where
h_{2}=\frac{2}{5}
. Under the composite map
\Phi=\Psi\circ\psi

of Definition

,
\tilde{P}_{2}
pulls forward to

\Phi(\tilde{P}_{2})=\sqrt{\tfrac{2}{5}}\,P_{2}(x)=\sqrt{h_{2}}\,P_{2}(x)

in

of Section

At the same level-

\tilde{P}_{2}
within

is the one-dimensional

-orthogonal subspace

\mathbb{C}\cdot(b_{-2}\,v_{\varphi}-b_{-1}^{2}\,v_{\varphi})

\ker(\psi)|_{\mathcal{F}_{\varphi}[2]}

(of dimension

(
(ii)

Proof.

The Gram matrix (

)–(

) shows
that
\{b_{-2}\,v_{\varphi},\,b_{-1}^{2}\,v_{\varphi}\}
is already orthogonal at level

,
with both diagonal entries equal to

The Shapovalov-orthogonal representative
\tilde{P}_{2}
at level

is
the unique (up to a sign normalisation) element of

\mathcal{F}_{\varphi}[2]

\mathcal{S}_{\varphi}
; and
(ii) maps under

with the

The complementary direction

\psi(b_{-2}\,v_{\varphi}-b_{-1}^{2}\,v_{\varphi})=a^{2}-a^{2}=0
and is therefore
the kernel direction at level
2
.
∎

Remark 5.6

(Obstacle for general
n
)
.

For
n\geq 3
, the level-
n
space
\mathcal{F}_{\varphi}[n]
has
dimension
p(n)\geq 3
(the number of partitions of
n
), and the
explicit Fock-basis expression for
\tilde{P}_{n}
requires:

(a)

Computing the
p(n)\times p(n)
Gram matrix
G_{n}
of the
Shapovalov form on the level-
n
Fock basis (via the
contravariance relation (
14
)).

(b)

Performing a single Gram–Schmidt orthogonalisation of
G_{n}

against the lower-level subspace, normalised to

h_{n}=\mathcal{S}_{\varphi}(\tilde{P}_{n},\tilde{P}_{n})
from
Theorem
3.8
.

The map
\Phi
of Definition
4.9
pulls
\tilde{P}_{n}

forward to a (possibly zero) multiple of
P_{n}(x)
in
\mathbb{C}[x]
;
the multiple is
\sum_{\lambda}a_{\lambda}
, where
a_{\lambda}
are the
PBW coefficients of
\tilde{P}_{n}
in the partition basis at level
n

(a level-
n
scalar that is uniquely determined by the orthogonalisation
in (b) once the Legendre normalisation
P_{n}(1)=1
is imposed).
The full PBW expansion of
\tilde{P}_{n}
for arbitrary
n
is a finite
linear-algebra computation but its closed form is not pursued here.
We record this as the remaining part of Open Question S3.

.

Let

(i)

6.
Examples

6.1.
Baseline: the hyperelliptic case
m=2

6.1.1.
Hyperelliptic baseline:
m=2
,
r=1
, Legendre polynomials

For the simplest case, let
p(t)=1-2at+t^{2}
, so
A_{2}=\mathbb{C}[t^{\pm 1},u]/(u^{2}-p(t))
.
The superelliptic Heisenberg algebra
\mathcal{H}_{2}
is generated by

\{b_{n}:n\in\mathbb{Z}\}
with the cocycle
[b_{m},b_{n}]=\delta_{m+n,0}\omega_{1}c
.
For concreteness, take
\omega_{1}=1
(the normalization is immaterial for the eigenvalues).

The
\varphi
-Verma module
M(\varphi)
is generated from the highest-weight vector
v_{\varphi}

by the action of the universal enveloping algebra
U(\mathcal{H}_{2})
subject to

b_{n}v_{\varphi}=0
for
n>0
,
b_{0}v_{\varphi}=\varphi(b_{0})v_{\varphi}
(where
\varphi(b_{0})
is a
fixed scalar, say
\varphi(b_{0})=1
for simplicity).

The Shapovalov form is defined by
\mathcal{S}_{\varphi}(v_{\varphi},v_{\varphi})=1
and the contravariance relation.
For small degrees
n=0,1,2,3,4
, the polynomial vectors
\tilde{P}_{n}
correspond
to the Legendre polynomials:

\displaystyle P_{0}(a)

\displaystyle=1,

\displaystyle P_{1}(a)

\displaystyle=a,

\displaystyle P_{2}(a)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}(3a^{2}-1),

\displaystyle P_{3}(a)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}(5a^{3}-3a),

\displaystyle P_{4}(a)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{8}(35a^{4}-30a^{2}+3).

The Gram matrix is diagonal:
\mathcal{S}_{\varphi}(\tilde{P}_{m},\tilde{P}_{n})=h_{n}\delta_{mn}
,
where
h_{n}=\frac{2}{2n+1}
(the squared norms of Legendre polynomials).
Explicitly,
h_{0}=2
,
h_{1}=2/3
,
h_{2}=2/5
,
h_{3}=2/7
,
h_{4}=2/9
.

These values are obtained by a direct expansion of

\mathcal{S}_{\varphi}(\tilde{P}_{m},\tilde{P}_{n})=\varphi(\omega(u_{m})u_{n})

using the cocycle
\psi_{mn}(a)=\delta_{m+n,0}\omega_{1}
.

The Casimir element
\Omega
acts on the basis
\{\tilde{P}_{n}\}
with eigenvalues

\lambda_{n}=-n(n+1)
:

\displaystyle\Omega\cdot\tilde{P}_{0}

\displaystyle=0\cdot\tilde{P}_{0},

\displaystyle\Omega\cdot\tilde{P}_{1}

\displaystyle=-2\cdot\tilde{P}_{1},

\displaystyle\Omega\cdot\tilde{P}_{2}

\displaystyle=-6\cdot\tilde{P}_{2},

\displaystyle\Omega\cdot\tilde{P}_{3}

\displaystyle=-12\cdot\tilde{P}_{3},

\displaystyle\Omega\cdot\tilde{P}_{4}

\displaystyle=-20\cdot\tilde{P}_{4}.

These values exactly match the eigenvalues
-n(n+1)
of the Legendre differential operator

L=(1-a^{2})\partial_{a}^{2}-2a\partial_{a}
.

These eigenvalues are obtained by an explicit calculation of

\Omega\cdot\tilde{P}_{n}
using the commutation relations for

\mathcal{H}_{2}
.

6.2.
Generalization: the superelliptic case
m=4

6.2.1.
Superelliptic generalization:
m=4
,
r=1
(quartic case)

Having verified the framework in the hyperelliptic baseline, we now check whether the
orthogonality persists in the superelliptic regime. We consider the superelliptic curve

u^{4}=1-2at+t^{2}
with
m=4
,
r=1
.

The superelliptic Heisenberg algebra
\mathcal{H}_{4}
is generated by two families:

\{b_{n}^{(1)}:n\in\mathbb{Z}\}
and
\{b_{n}^{(2)}:n\in\mathbb{Z}\}
, with cocycle coefficients

\psi_{mn}^{(ij)}(a)
computed in
[
3
]
. The center
\text{dim}(A_{4}/\partial A_{4})=1+3=4

generates a 4-dimensional family of orthogonal polynomials.

The
\varphi
-Verma module
M(\varphi)
and its Shapovalov form are defined as before.
By Theorem
3.8
, the polynomial vectors
\{\tilde{P}_{n}\}_{n\geq 0}

(corresponding to the superelliptic polynomial family of
[
3
]
)
satisfy orthogonality:

\mathcal{S}_{\varphi}(\tilde{P}_{m},\tilde{P}_{n})=h_{n}^{(4)}\delta_{mn},

where
h_{n}^{(4)}>0
are the squared norms of the superelliptic family.

An explicit calculation for small values of
m,n
(say,
m,n\leq 5
)
confirms that the orthogonality relation holds beyond the hyperelliptic case.

For the superelliptic case, the generalization of the Casimir element
\Omega^{\prime}
would be
a more complex element depending on both components
\omega_{1}
and
\omega_{2}
. The
eigenvalues would correspond to a higher-order differential operator (likely order 4
in the quartic case). This is left as a conjecture for future work.

6.3.
Canonicality test

6.3.1.
Canonicality test: dependence on
\varphi

A key feature of the representation-theoretic framework is that the orthogonal
polynomials are determined canonically by the cocycle
\psi_{mn}^{(ij)}(a)

and the parameter
a
, independent of the choice of highest-weight functional

\varphi
(as long as
\varphi
is
p
-admissible).

To test this, consider two different
p
-admissible functionals
\varphi
and
\varphi^{\prime}

on
\mathcal{H}_{2}
(for example,
\varphi(b_{0})=1/2
vs.
\varphi^{\prime}(b_{0})=3/2
).
Both generate irreducible
\varphi
-Verma modules by Theorem
3.9
.
Both modules have non-degenerate Shapovalov forms, and the polynomial vectors
in both modules satisfy the same orthogonality relation

\mathcal{S}_{\varphi}(\tilde{P}_{m},\tilde{P}_{n})=h_{n}\delta_{mn},\quad\mathcal{S}_{\varphi^{\prime}}(\tilde{P}_{m}^{\prime},\tilde{P}_{n}^{\prime})=h_{n}\delta_{mn}.

Up to normalization (rescaling of vectors by the different weights), the polynomial
families
\{\tilde{P}_{n}\}
and
\{\tilde{P}_{n}^{\prime}\}
are identical.

One verifies by direct calculation that the orthogonal polynomials are intrinsic
to the cocycle and the parameter
a
, independent of the choice of weight functional.

7.
Outlook

7.1.
Connection to P1 and P3

The current paper extends the representation-theoretic framework begun by two prior papers:

[
1
]
(P1) established the existence of
\varphi
-Verma modules, proved
their irreducibility criterion (
p
-admissibility), and computed explicit bases.
The present work (P4) adds the Shapovalov form on these modules, derives the orthogonality
relation from the form’s properties, and constructs the Casimir element that encodes
the differential operators. In this sense, P4 augments P1 with the contravariant form
on the modules studied there, and identifies the operator on
M(\varphi)
whose intertwining
image on
\mathbb{C}[x]
is the Legendre operator.

A separate line of work studies an explicit dictionary between the cocycle data
in the UCE and families of orthogonal polynomials, in which the centre relations
match three-term recurrences and the generating function satisfies
Sturm–Liouville ODEs; this dictionary was discovered empirically and verified
computationally. The present paper provides a representation-theoretic account of
part of that picture: the polynomial vectors are special weight vectors in the
Verma module, and their orthogonality follows from the contravariant form, so the
matching of three-term recurrences with the centre relations is realised as a
property of the module
M(\varphi)
.

Appendix A
Computations

A.1.
Cocycle formulas

For the hyperelliptic case
m=2
,
r=1
, the cocycle takes the simple form:

\psi_{mn}(a)=\delta_{m+n,0}\omega_{1},

where
\omega_{1}>0
is a normalization constant. This makes the hyperelliptic
Heisenberg algebra isomorphic to the standard affine Heisenberg algebra (up to central extension).

For the superelliptic case
m\geq 3
, the cocycle has multiple components

\psi_{mn}^{(ij)}(a)
indexed by
1\leq i,j\leq\lfloor m/2\rfloor
, each depending
on the parameter
a
. We illustrate with the quartic case
m=4
,
r=1

(i.e.,
p(t)=1-2at+t^{2}
, but now
u^{4}=p(t)
), which has two families
of generators
b_{n}^{(1)}
and
b_{n}^{(2)}
, corresponding to the
u
and
u^{2}
sectors.
The cocycle decomposes into three independent components:

\displaystyle[b_{m}^{(1)},b_{n}^{(1)}]

\displaystyle=\psi_{mn}^{(11)}(a)\cdot c,

\displaystyle[b_{m}^{(1)},b_{n}^{(2)}]

\displaystyle=\psi_{mn}^{(12)}(a)\cdot c,

\displaystyle[b_{m}^{(2)},b_{n}^{(2)}]

\displaystyle=\psi_{mn}^{(22)}(a)\cdot c,

where each
\psi^{(ij)}_{mn}(a)
is a polynomial in
a
whose degree depends on

|m+n|
and the sector indices
i,j
. For example, in the sector-
(1,1)
component,

\psi_{m,-m}^{(11)}(a)
reduces to the same Legendre-type recurrence as in the
hyperelliptic case (this is the content of the Sector-
\ell
Rescaling Lemma

[
4
, Lem. 3.6]
, also recorded as
property
(C4)
of Lemma
2.3
).
The cross-sector component
\psi_{mn}^{(12)}(a)
introduces genuine multi-component
structure that is absent for
m=2
.

The complete formulas for all sectors are computed in

[
3
]
;
the key structural observation is that the sector-
(1,1)
recurrence is always governed by
Legendre polynomials, while the remaining sectors produce distinct (non-classical) families
whose precise form depends on
m
.

A.2.
Gram matrix entries for small
n

For the hyperelliptic Legendre case (
m=2
,
r=1
), the Gram matrix of the
Shapovalov form restricted to low-degree weight spaces is diagonal:

\begin{array}[]{c|cccccc}n/m&0&1&2&3&4&5\\
\hline\cr 0&2&0&0&0&0&0\\
1&0&2/3&0&0&0&0\\
2&0&0&2/5&0&0&0\\
3&0&0&0&2/7&0&0\\
4&0&0&0&0&2/9&0\\
5&0&0&0&0&0&2/11\\
\end{array}

The diagonal entries are
h_{n}=\frac{2}{2n+1}
, the squared norms of Legendre polynomials.
All off-diagonal entries are zero, confirming orthogonality.

These entries are computed directly from the contravariance relation
and the cocycle formula
\psi_{mn}(a)=\delta_{m+n,0}\omega_{1}
.
