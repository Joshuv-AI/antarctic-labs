ATBench: A Diverse and Realistic Agent Trajectory Benchmark for Safety Evaluation and Diagnosis
Haoyu Luo, Yuejin Xie, Yuqian Fu, Zhonghao Yang, Shuai Shao, Qihan Ren, Wanying Qu, Yanwei Fu, Yujiu Yang, Jing Shao, Xia Hu, Dongrui Liu

Abstract
Evaluating the safety of LLM-based agents is an increasingly important challenge, as risks in realistic deployments often emerge gradually over multi-step interactions rather than from isolated prompts or final responses. Existing trajectory-level benchmarks, however, remain limited in three key dimensions: (1) insufficient interaction diversity, due to restricted tool ecosystems and narrow scenario coverage; (2) limited observability of safety failures, as coarse labels fail to capture how risks arise and evolve; and (3) lack of long-horizon realism, where short or simplified trajectories underestimate delayed and context-dependent risks. To address these limitations, we introduce ATBench, a trajectory-level benchmark that enables structured, diverse, and realistic evaluation of agent safety. ATBench formulates agentic safety risks along three orthogonal dimensions—risk source, failure mode, and real-world harm—providing a controllable framework for capturing diverse risk patterns. Building on this formulation, we construct trajectories using a taxonomy-guided generation engine with heterogeneous tool pools and incorporate a long-context delayed-trigger protocol that models realistic risk emergence through multi-stage interactions. The benchmark contains 1,000 trajectories (503 safe, 497 unsafe), averaging 9.01 turns and 3.95k tokens, with 1,954 invoked tools drawn from pools spanning 2,084 available tools. Data quality is ensured through rule-based & LLM-based filtering and a human full audit. Experiments across frontier LLMs, open-source models, and specialized guard systems show that ATBench remains challenging even for the strongest evaluators—GPT-5.4 achieves only 76.7% F1 on binary safety classification and 33.6% on fine-grained risk-source diagnosis—while enabling taxonomy-stratified analysis, cross-benchmark comparison, and diagnosis of long-horizon failure patterns. Data and code will be released upon acceptance.

Introduction

LLM-based agents are rapidly evolving from single-turn text interaction to long-horizon execution involving tool use, external observations, and persistent state
(
Yuan et al. 2024a

In realistic settings, safety risks are often not revealed by a single response but instead emerge gradually over extended interaction traces — through compounding planning errors, unsafe tool use, over-reliance on environmental feedback, or delayed exploitation of previously acquired permissions. Consequently, evaluating agent safety requires moving beyond prompt-level assessment toward reasoning over full trajectories of interaction.

In this paper, we organize agent safety along three orthogonal dimensions—risk source, failure mode, and real-world harm—and use this taxonomy together with a heterogeneous tool ecosystem to support controllable and diverse benchmark construction. We further incorporate a delayed-trigger long-context protocol so that risks can be planted early, realized later, and evaluated under realistic multi-step interaction. 3
Benchmark Construction for Diversity with Realism

3.1
Task Definition and Benchmark Design

Trajectory-level safety evaluation task.

ATBench is a benchmark for trajectory-level evaluation of
agent safety under long-horizon tool use. Each example is a
complete multi-turn interaction trace containing user
requests, assistant responses, tool calls, and environment
feedback. The evaluation task is to determine whether the
overall trajectory is
safe
or
unsafe
. For
unsafe trajectories, ATBench further supports fine-grained
diagnosis beyond binary detection.

Design objective and benchmark scope.

Our construction method combines two ingredients. First, we define a three-dimensional safety taxonomy that specifies the target risk space and build a heterogeneous tool pool that provides the executable interface space needed to realize that risk space through diverse scenarios. Second, we instantiate these ingredients with a generation-and-curation pipeline that includes planner-based trajectory synthesis, paired safe/unsafe construction, a delayed-trigger long-context protocol, rule-based and LLM-based filtering, and full human audit.

3.2
Three-Dimensional Safety Taxonomy and Tool Pool

Taxonomy as a construction scaffold.

To make diversity controllable rather than incidental, we organize unsafe trajectories along three orthogonal dimensions proposed in this paper:
risk source
,
failure mode
, and
real-world harm
. These dimensions answer where risk enters a trajectory, how unsafe behavior is realized, and what consequence it produces. In the construction pipeline, this taxonomy serves as a scaffold for coverage control rather than as an end in itself. It specifies which slices of agentic risk should be instantiated, while the generation engine determines how to realize them in concrete long-horizon trajectories.

Taxonomy structure.

At the leaf level, the taxonomy contains 8 risk-source
categories, 14 failure-mode categories, and 10 harm
categories. The risk-source axis is organized around four high-level families: user input, environmental observation, external entities, and internal logic and failures. The failure-mode axis covers behavioral failures and output-content failures. The harm axis captures downstream consequences such as privacy, financial, security, physical, reputational, societal, fairness, and functional harms. These high-level axes provide structured material for sampling diverse agentic risk patterns in a controlled manner, rather than relying on an unstructured inventory of scenarios. Full taxonomy definitions remain in Appendix
A
.

Heterogeneous tool pool.

The taxonomy is paired with a heterogeneous tool pool, which provides the executable material needed to realize diverse scenarios. We assemble this tool pool from realistic public APIs(e.g. rapid api), tools adapted from prior tool-use resources such as ToolBench and ToolAlpaca
(
Qin et al. 2023a
;
Tang et al. 2023b
)
,
and simulated tools that fill underrepresented capability and risk-relevant regions. We then normalize tool names, arguments, and descriptions across sources and remove near-duplicate tools. This design allows the same taxonomy slice to be instantiated through different tool combinations, interface conditions, and interaction structures rather than through a single template. In this sense, the taxonomy defines the axes of diversity, and the
tool pool provides the interface space for realizing that diversity.

Primary-label annotation protocol.

Each trajectory receives a binary
safe
/
unsafe
verdict. For unsafe trajectories, annotators assign one primary label per taxonomy dimension in a fixed causal order. Risk source identifies the earliest factor that introduces the unsafe condition. Failure mode identifies the dominant unsafe behavior that realizes that risk.
Real-world harm identifies the principal consequence of the failure chain. When multiple labels are plausible, annotators select the label that is most central to the main causal chain. This single-primary-label policy keeps taxonomy-stratified evaluation stable. Full annotation guidelines are provided in Appendix
B.1
.

3.3
Generation Engine for Diversity under Realism

Figure 2:
Data generation engine for synthesizing multi-step agent trajectories in ATBench. Given a sampled risk and candidate tools, a planner produces a trajectory blueprint that is then executed sequentially through query generation, risk injection, tool call and response simulation, and agent response generation. A validation layer combines rule-based and LLM-based filtering to ensure realism.

The generation engine translates the diversity defined by the taxonomy and supported by the tool pool into executable trajectories while preserving realism. For each sample, the planner operates on scenario controls that jointly specify a taxonomy slice sampled along the three risk dimensions and candidate tools drawn from the tool pool, and then instantiates a concrete user task, trigger location, and intended safety outcome. Because the same risk slice can be realized through different task formulations, tool combinations, and interaction structures, the benchmark does not collapse into a small number of repeated templates.

Generation pipeline.

Figure
2
shows the end-to-end pipeline.
Given a sampled taxonomy slice and a set of scenario controls, the planner first creates a trajectory blueprint
that specifies the user task, selected tools, high-level
steps, trigger locations, and intended safety outcome. The
pipeline then executes this blueprint step by step. Specifically, a

Query Generator
produces the opening user request.
When the sampled risk source lies in the tool-interface
layer, a
Tool Description Simulator
injects the
trigger into the tool interface. For each planned step, a

Tool Call Simulator
generates the tool invocation, a

Simulated Data Generator
returns either benign or
injected environment feedback, and an
Agent Response
Generator
produces the assistant response. Together, these
components yield a full trajectory containing tool calls,
environment observations, and assistant turns.

We further construct paired safe and unsafe variants from the same scenario skeleton. In the unsafe variant, the risk is realized and the agent fails. In the safe variant, the risk is removed, resisted, or correctly handled. The taxonomy serves as a coverage controller throughout this process. The sampled risk source determines where the risk is injected, the failure mode constrains the unsafe behavior, and the real-world harm label determines the downstream consequence.

Long-context delayed-risk construction.

We also include a long-context setting to capture safety risks that arise from dependencies across multiple steps and intermediate states, rather than from local context alone. This better reflects realistic agent behavior, where earlier benign-appearing actions can later become safety-critical, than simply extending the number of turns. Implementation details are provided in Appendix
B.2
.

Rule-based and LLM-based filtering.

Rule-based and LLM-based filtering improve realism in the final dataset. We first apply rule-based structural filtering, including checks for tool-name existence, schema conformance, and required-parameter completeness. We then apply LLM-based plausibility filtering to verify that the trajectory is behaviorally reasonable and that its annotations are consistent with the observed behavior. Detailed filtering rules are provided in Appendix
B.3
.

Full human audit of benchmark labels.

To verify the released benchmark after automatic synthesis and post-generation filtering, we conduct a full-dataset human audit of ATBench. This audit functions as a release-time correction pass rather than a small-scale agreement study. In total, the audit produces 5 binary corrections and 165 fine-grained corrections across 129 unsafe trajectories, yielding a final benchmark split of 503
safe
and 497
unsafe
trajectories. Detailed audit statistics and protocol are reported in Appendix
D
.

4.2
Main Results on ATBench

Trajectory-level safety results.

Table
2
shows that ATBench remains challenging even for strong evaluators. GPT-5.4 achieves the highest coarse-grained F1 of 76.7%, followed by Gemini-3.1-Pro at 75.0% and the trajectory-trained AgentDoG-Qwen3-4B at 71.1%. Strong open-source general models score substantially lower (Qwen3.5-397B at 67.8%, Qwen3-235B at 60.8%), indicating that ATBench is far from saturated even for frontier systems.

The gap widens considerably for fine-grained diagnosis. Even strong closed-source models reach only 33.6% on risk source and 13.5% on failure mode, while real-world harm prediction remains limited at 30.2% for GPT-5.4. Recognizing that a trajectory is unsafe proves far easier than recovering where the risk originates, how it unfolds, and what harm it produces. Trajectory-trained models retain an edge here: AgentDoG-Qwen3-4B achieves the strongest fine-grained results (46.8% risk source, 16.5% failure mode, 40.6% harm), whereas off-the-shelf guard models remain considerably weaker on structured causal attribution.

5
Discussion

ATBench centers on the trade-off between diversity and realism. Broader tool coverage, richer scenario composition, and finer-grained labels reduce the risk of overfitting to narrow failure patterns, but diversity without structural checks can produce templated or implausible data. Our construction pipeline addresses this tension through delayed-trigger long-context protocols, rule-based & LLM-based filtering, and full human audit, and we expect this trade-off to remain central for future benchmark design.

Limitations.
ATBench still has limitations. First, each unsafe trajectory carries only one primary label per taxonomy dimension, which may miss valid secondary interpretations in multi-causal cases. Second, all trajectories are constructed in English; extending the benchmark to multilingual settings would broaden its applicability to global agent deployments. Third, the benchmark focuses on text-and-tool trajectories; multimodal and embodied settings remain important future extensions.

6
Conclusion

We introduced ATBench, a benchmark for long-horizon agent safety evaluation built around a simple principle: maximize diversity while preserving realism. To operationalize this objective, the benchmark uses a unified and hierarchical three-dimensional taxonomy over risk source, failure mode, and real-world harm as a structured scaffold, and then instantiates that diversity through a taxonomy-guided generation engine, a heterogeneous tool pool, a shared-state setup–exploit long-context protocol, and human-in-the-loop quality control. Our experiments show that ATBench is challenging for both general-purpose models and specialized guards, especially on trajectory-level safety evaluation and fine-grained diagnosis. We hope ATBench can help standardize more realistic evaluation of agent safety systems and encourage future work on long-context, tool-mediated risk understanding.

Appendix A
Detailed Safety Taxonomy

This appendix provides the full overview and detailed definitions of the three-dimensional safety taxonomy used by ATBench. Following AgentDoG, we organize unsafe trajectories along three orthogonal dimensions:
risk source
,
failure mode
, and
real-world harm
. These dimensions respectively answer where the risk comes from, how it manifests during agent execution, and what consequence it produces. In the main paper, this taxonomy is used as a construction scaffold for coverage control and as the label space for unsafe-trajectory diagnosis.

A.1
Taxonomy Overview

Figure 6:
Overview of the three orthogonal dimensions of the agentic safety taxonomy.

Figure
6
shows the overall structure of the taxonomy. At the leaf level, the taxonomy contains 8 risk-source categories, 14 failure-mode categories, and 10 harm categories. The risk-source axis is organized into four superclass families: user input, environmental observation, external entities, and internal logic and failures. The failure-mode axis is organized into two families: behavioral failure modes and output-content failure modes, with tool-related failures further split into incorrect parameters, choosing malicious tools, context-inappropriate use, and failure to validate tool outputs. The real-world-harm axis captures downstream impacts such as privacy, financial, security, physical, reputational, societal, fairness, and functional harms. The appendix tables below provide the full taxonomy used for detailed annotation and analysis.

Appendix B
Benchmark Construction Details

B.1
Annotation Details

This subsection describes the annotation procedure used to assign trajectory-level safety labels and fine-grained taxonomy labels.

After generation and preliminary quality filtering, each trajectory is annotated in two stages. We first assign a binary
safe
/
unsafe
verdict at the trajectory level. Safe trajectories receive no fine-grained taxonomy tuple. For unsafe trajectories, annotators assign one primary label per taxonomy dimension following a fixed causal decision order:

1.

Risk source:
the earliest external or internal factor that introduces the unsafe condition.

2.

Failure mode:
the dominant unsafe behavior or output through which the agent realizes that risk.

3.

Real-world harm:
the principal realized or clearly intended consequence of the resulting failure chain.

When multiple labels are plausible, annotators select the one most central to the main causal chain rather than tagging every incidental issue. This single-primary-label policy keeps the benchmark analyzable and renders taxonomy-stratified evaluation more stable.

B.2
Long-Context Delayed-Trigger Details

This subsection describes the implementation of delayed-trigger trajectory generation in the long-context setting.

Two-episode structure.

For long-context scenarios, the planner organizes the trajectory into two contiguous episodes: a
Setup
episode and a later
Exploit
episode. The setup episode establishes reusable state, while the exploit episode is constructed to depend on that earlier state rather than on a newly introduced local trigger.

Context carry-over.

At execution time, user, assistant, and environment messages produced in the setup episode remain in the shared conversation history and are carried forward into the exploit episode. Consequently, later generation stages condition on the visible prior transcript rather than on the current turn alone.

Grounding of delayed triggers.

When an exploit-side step is marked as
source=CONTEXT
, the planner is instructed to ground it in earlier tool outputs or previously established permissions rather than introducing a fresh trigger.

Safe and unsafe realization.

The trajectory contains a designated delayed-trigger risk point, typically in the exploit episode. In unsafe trajectories, the agent acts on the carried-over dependency and the risk is realized. In safe trajectories, the same prior context remains available, but the agent refuses, verifies, or otherwise neutralizes the dependency. Thus, the safety-relevant divergence occurs at the delayed-trigger step while both variants share the same carried-over context.

B.3
Filtering Details

This subsection describes the filtering procedure used to enforce structural validity, behavioral plausibility, and annotation consistency.

Hard-rule-based filtering.

The hard-rule-based filtering examines structural validity by checking tool call format and schema compliance, including whether each call is well-formed and whether its arguments match the declared tool interface.

LLM-based filtering.

For each candidate that passes hard-rule checking, the filter linearizes the trajectory into text, includes the exposed tool information, and asks an LLM reviewer to verify trajectory plausibility and annotation consistency. In particular, the reviewer checks whether the assigned safety label, risk taxonomy labels, and reasoning are consistent with the observed behavior in the trajectory.

Audit protocol.

After automatic synthesis and rule-based & LLM-based filtering, we conduct a full human audit over the entire ATBench release. Interpretation.

These correction counts suggest that the benchmark is relatively stable at the coarse-grained level, with only a small number of binary-label changes across the full dataset. In contrast, fine-grained taxonomy labels require substantially more manual correction within the unsafe subset. This pattern indicates that filtering is effective at removing many obvious coarse errors, but is still not sufficient on its own to guarantee release-quality fine-grained annotations. Full human audit therefore remains an important final quality-assurance step for benchmark releases that rely on automated generation pipelines.
