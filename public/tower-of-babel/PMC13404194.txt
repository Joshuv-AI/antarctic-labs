Biodegradation of vulcanized waste tire rubber by Gordonia polyisoprenivorans Kd2: mechanistic insights and ecological context from indigenous rubber-degrading communities
Authors: Pritish Raj Shukla, Utpal Roy, Sunil Bhand, Vivek Rangarajan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Waste tires pose a persistent environmental challenge and resist natural degradation due to vulcanization-induced sulfur crosslinks and the presence of toxic additives, severely limiting microbial accessibility. Gordonia polyisoprenivorans Kd2 was selected as a model organism to study biodegradation potential against ground tire rubber (GTR) of two size fractions (400–600 µm and 3–4 mm) over 28 days in mineral salts medium. GTR samples were comprehensively characterized using ATR-FTIR, FESEM-EDS, CHNS analysis, thermogravimetric analysis (TGA), and solvent swelling-based analysis. In addition, 16S rRNA amplicon profiling was performed to investigate indigenous microbial communities at waste tire dumping sites. Smaller particles supported degradation more effectively than larger fractions, yielding a 2.49 × 103-fold increase in viable cell counts and a maximum weight loss of 5.80%, compared to 1.40% in untreated GTR. Elemental analyses revealed surface oxidation and partial reductions in sulfur and zinc contents. The crosslink density decreased by 10.54% (9.58 × 10−3 to 8.57 × 10−3 mol cm−3) and the sol fraction increased from 2.73% to 5.51%, with Horikx analysis indicating a random main-chain scission mechanism. 16S rRNA amplicon profiling showed an Actinomycetota-dominated community (42.9–44.6%), with Nocardia as the most abundant genus and Gordonia present as a member, indicating that Gordonia operates within a rubber-degrading microbial community, isolated from the soil of rubber tire dumping sites, suggesting the design of synergistic microbial consortia for effective waste tire valorization.

Introduction

Waste tire rubber (WTR) is among the most persistent solid wastes present in our ecosystem. It is highly resistant to natural degradation. The vulcanization (curing) process, which introduces sulfur crosslinks in rubber polymers, and the addition of toxic additives, such as anti-retardants, antioxidants, anti-degradants, oils, waxes, and fillers, such as carbon black and silica, significantly enhance the performance of rubber tires, also making it highly resistant to natural degradation.1 Approximately 1.5 billion tires are manufactured worldwide each year, with over 17 million tons disposed of as waste. The annual generation of discarded tires is predicted to reach up to 1.2 billion by 2030.2 Existing literature suggests that most waste tires worldwide end up in landfills, where they occupy large areas, increase fire hazards, attract mosquitoes, and pollute soil and water.3

Conventional rubber waste management strategies such as pyrolysis, thermolysis, mechanical grinding, chemical scavenging, and microwave-assisted degradation are energy-intensive and generate secondary pollutants or hazardous residues.1,3 Several application-based approaches are also in practice to manage rubber wastes.4 As a material application, rubber-based composites are also being modified to pre-materials of 3D-printing inks.5 Alternatively, biodegradation, known for its minimal energy consumption, efficient reduction, and minimal production of toxic by-products, offers a more environmentally sustainable approach. The primary constituent of tires is natural rubber (NR). It is sourced from the latex of Hevea brasiliensis and composed of more than 90% cis-1,4-polyisoprene chains.6 This cis-1,4-polyisoprene can serve as a complex organic carbon source for rubber-degrading microorganisms.

The 16S rRNA gene-based metagenomics studies on soil microflora present in waste tire-contaminated sites to understand the community composition and ecological roles of microorganisms.7–9 These studies have revealed the dominance of the phylum Actinomycetota. Within this phylum, genera belonging to the order Mycobacteriales, notably Nocardia, Gordonia, Rhodococcus, and Mycobacterium, have been frequently identified for their ability to cleave cis-1,4-polyisoprene chains and utilize rubber as substrates.10 Among these Gordonia polyisoprenivorans Kd2 is a well-characterized, aerobic, mycolic acid-producing, non-motile, Gram-positive strain originally isolated from fouling water logged inside a waste tire.11 Few studies have also reported the identification and isolation of several Gordonia species from soils of rubber tree plantations in Vietnam.12 These strains have the ability to catalyze cleavage of cis-1,4-polyisoprene and use it as a sole carbon source via β-oxidation.

Despite extensive studies on the biodegradation of natural and synthetic rubbers,13 the potential of G. polyisoprenivorans Kd2 to biodegrade highly vulcanized WTR remains poorly understood. In particular, the degradation mechanism of waste passenger vehicle tire-derived ground tire rubber (GTR) by G. polyisoprenivorans Kd2 has not been investigated. Additionally, while Gordonia has been identified in rubber-associated microbial communities, its relative abundance, ecological order, and role within the indigenous rubber-colonizing microbiota remain rarely quantified.

Therefore, the objective of the present study is to elucidate the underlying mechanism of Gordonia polyisoprenivorans Kd2-mediated biodegradation of ground tire rubber (GTR), the effect of rubber particle size on bacterial growth behaviour and characterization of biochemical and physicochemical properties, including rubber surface modification and changes in rubber network structure. Since the catalytic mechanism of Lcp enzyme is already well established,14–19 the present study characterizes biodegradation at the substrate and network level to evaluate the biodegradation effect on a highly vulcanized, real-world waste-tire substrate. Moreover, this study aims to profile the indigenous bacterial community using 16S rRNA amplicon sequencing to understand the ecological context of Gordonia within the Actinomycetota-dominated microbiota to connect mechanistic insights from pure-culture studies to the broader microbial ecology of rubber-contaminated sites.

Materials and methods

Rubber material and chemicals

GTR particles of sizes 3–4 mm and 400–600 µm were procured from a local waste tire recycling facility, RS Polymers Pvt. Ltd, Hyderabad, India, and stored under ambient laboratory conditions. All chemicals and reagents used for microbiological experiments were of analytical grade, obtained from HiMedia Laboratories Pvt. Ltd, (India) and used as received without further purification.

Bacterial strain and culture conditions

G. polyisoprenivorans Kd2 (DSM44302) was kindly obtained from the Leibniz-Institut DSMZ-Deutsche Sammlung von Mikroorganismen und Zellkulturen GmbH, Germany. The strain was revived and maintained under sterile laboratory conditions. Nutrient Broth (NB) and Mineral Salts Medium (MSM) were used for the preparation and maintenance of pure cultures. NB-grown culture was used as the primary inoculum, while secondary cultures were prepared in MSM medium. The MSM medium consisted of 9.0 g per L Na2HPO4·12H2O, 1.5 g per L KH2PO4, 1.0 g per L H8N2SO4, 0.2 g per L MgSO4·7H2O, 0.02 g per L CaCl2·2H2O, 1.2 mg per L Fe(iii) ammonium citrate, with pH adjusted to 7.0 ± 0.03.

Culturing of G. polyisoprenivorans Kd2 with GTR particles

GTR particles were thoroughly washed with 70% (v/v) ethanol and dried at 45 °C in a SpeedVac for 3 h. Completely dried GTR particles of size fractions (3–4 mm and 400–600 µm) were then added at a concentration of 0.5% (w/v) to 250 mL Erlenmeyer flasks containing MSM media as a sole carbon and energy source.18 The media were inoculated with 5% (v/v) of the primary culture, with an initial optical density (OD) at 600 nm of 0.8–1.0. The flasks were incubated at 30 °C with continuous shaking at 120 rpm for 28 days.20 Bacterial growth and cell viability were monitored at 4-day intervals by measuring the OD600 with a UV-Visible spectrophotometer (Shimadzu, Japan), followed by serial dilution and colony-forming unit (CFU) counting. Bacterial growth parameters (OD600 and log10 CFU mL−1) between the two GTR particle-size fractions were compared using an unpaired two-tailed Welch's t-test, with p < 0.05.

Characterization of treated GTR particles

Mass reduction measurement

The mass of GTR was measured before (Wi) and after degradation (Wf). Mass loss was calculated using the eqn (1):211WL = [(Wi − Wf)/Wi] × 100where, WL: weight loss, %; Wi: initial weight (before degradation), g; Wf: final weight (after degradation), g.

A dedicated heat-killed or enzyme-inhibited controls were not included in the present experimental design. However, an abiotic control (uninoculated, sterile MSM medium containing only GTR particles) was incubated under identical conditions to account for any non-biological weight loss that might arise from handling, mechanical wear or abiotic leaching. The net weight loss (%) was calculated relative to the abiotic control.

Schiff's staining

Schiff's staining was performed to detect the formation of biodegradation products, as described by Linos et al. (2000).22,23 The Schiff's reagent (10 mL) was added to 10 mL of the MSM medium with GTR, and the mixture was incubated at room temperature for 10 min. The formation of a purple/violet colour was visually monitored as an indication of aldehyde-containing degradation products.

Liquid chromatography-mass spectroscopy (LC-MS) of oligo-isoprenoids

LC-MS analysis was performed for the identification of G. polyisoprenivorans Kd2-derived enzymatic degradation products (oligo-isoprenoids). Cell-free supernatant from the GTR-containing bacterial culture was extracted in ethyl acetate (liquid–liquid extraction) at a sample : solvent ratio of 1 : 1 (v/v). The mixture was vigorously vortexed, centrifuged at 11 000 rpm and 4 °C for 10 min to separate the phases. The organic phase containing the degradation products was collected and evaporated in a SpeedVac (Eppendorf) at 45 °C for 3 h. The sample was reconstituted in 1 mL of the extraction solvent, and transferred into a 2 mL glass vial. The column (ZORBAX Eclipse XDB-C8 Analytical; 4.6 × 150 mm; 5 µm; Agilent, USA) was equilibrated with 50% methanol : water. The sample was analyzed using electrospray ionization (ESI) in full-scan mode (m/z = 100–1000). The data acquisition and processing were performed using Agilent Triple Quadrupole (V12.0.313.0). An injection volume of approximately 7 µL was used for analysis, and peaks corresponding to putative oligo(cis-1,4-isoprene) derivatives were interpreted based on previously reported fragmentation patterns.24–26

Attenuated total reflectance-Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (ATR-FTIR)

ATR-FTIR spectroscopy was employed to evaluate biodegradation-induced structural changes in GTR using ATR-FTIR Spectrum 400 (PerkinElmer Spectrum Two™, U.S.). The analysis focused on characteristic vibrations associated with C–S and S–S bonds, Zn-stearate-related linkages, N–O groups, C <svg xmlns="http://www.w3.org/2000/svg" version="1.0" width="13.200000pt" height="16.000000pt" viewBox="0 0 13.200000 16.000000" preserveAspectRatio="xMidYMid meet"><metadata> Created by potrace 1.16, written by Peter Selinger 2001-2019 </metadata><g transform="translate(1.000000,15.000000) scale(0.017500,-0.017500)" fill="currentColor" stroke="none"><path d="M0 440 l0 -40 320 0 320 0 0 40 0 40 -320 0 -320 0 0 -40z M0 280 l0 -40 320 0 320 0 0 40 0 40 -320 0 -320 0 0 -40z"/></g></svg> C bending, and C–H stretching of CH2 and CH3 groups in the rubber backbone, as previously reported.27 Approximately 5 mg of GTR particles were used for each measurement. Spectra were recorded in the 400–4000 cm−1 wavenumber range at a resolution of 4 cm−1, with 45 scans accumulated per sample.28

Field emission scanning electron microscopy and energy dispersive spectroscopy

Treated GTR particles were removed from cultures and fixed with a 2.5% glutaraldehyde solution in 0.1 M phosphate buffer, pH 7.4, for 24 h. The particles were washed with sterile double-distilled water and dehydrated for 10 min in ethanol gradients (30%, 50%, 70%, 90% and absolute ethanol). Dehydrated GTR samples were subjected to critical-point drying (CPD) using liquid carbon dioxide (CO2). The samples were then mounted on aluminium stubs using a copper strip, and gold sputtered.29 Surface morphological changes on GTR particles were visually examined using field-emission scanning electron microscopy (FESEM; FEI Quanta 250 FEG) operated at an accelerating voltage of 20 kV. The instrument was coupled with energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) to assess elemental composition changes in the treated GTR at the microscale level (area-based analysis), as described by Valdés et al. (2021).30 The EDS analysis was performed in triplicate.

Elemental (CHNS) analysis of treated GTR particles

1.8–2.0 mg of GTR samples were placed into an elemental analyzer (Elementar Unicube, Germany) to quantitatively evaluate changes in total carbon, hydrogen, nitrogen, and sulfur contents resulting from the biodegradation process. The CHNS analyzer operates by combusting the sample at high temperature, resulting in complete decomposition. The resulting release of CO2 and SO2 gases is subsequently detected and quantified, allowing the corresponding percentages of C, H, N, and S to be automatically determined, as described by Roychand et al. (2021).31 The elemental analysis was performed in triplicate.

Crosslink density determination and change in molecular weight between crosslinks (Mc), sol fraction, and Horikx plot

The crosslink density of GTR samples was evaluated using the solvent swelling technique based on the Flory–Rehner theory.32,33 Dried samples (0.2 g) were immersed in toluene and allowed to swell at 25 °C for 5 days until equilibrium was achieved. After swelling, the samples were removed, carefully blotted to remove excess solvent, and weighed. The volume fraction of rubber in the swollen network (Vr) was calculated from the initial dry mass and the swollen system mass using eqn (2):342Vr = ((mr/ρr))/[(mr/ρr) + (ms/ρs)]where, mr = mass of the dry GTR (g); ms = mass of the swollen GTR (g); ρs = density of the solvent (0.867 g cm−3); ρr = density of the GTR (1.15 g cm−3).

The crosslink density (ν) was subsequently determined using the Flory–Rehner eqn (3):3ν = [−ln(1 − Vr) + Vr + χVr2]/[V1(Vr(1/3) − Vr/2)]where, ν = crosslink density (mol cm−3); Vr = volume fraction of rubber in the swollen network V1 = molar volume of toluene (106.2 cm3 mol−1); χ = Flory–Huggins interaction parameter for rubber/toluene system (0.39).

The average molecular weight between crosslinks (Mc) was estimated from calculated crosslink density using the relationship Mc = ρ/ν, where ρ denotes the GTR density. The density of GTR particles, 1.15 g cm−3, was experimentally determined using a liquid displacement test to ensure that variations in Mc reflected changes in network structure rather than differences in density.

The sol fraction of GTR was also determined by solvent swelling with toluene. Pre-dried samples with initial weight (mi) were immersed in toluene in sealed glass vials and gently agitated at room temperature for 5 days to dissolve extractable components. After extraction, the samples were removed, rinsed with fresh toluene, and dried at 70 °C to a constant final weight (mf). The sol fraction was calculated using the following eqn (4):354Sol (%) = [1 − (mf/mi) × 100]

Horikx analysis was employed to understand the underlying degradation mechanism by correlating the sol fraction with the relative reduction in crosslink density.36 Sol fraction and crosslink density values were plotted against theoretical Horikx curves representing ideal crosslink scission and main-chain scission, calculated using established equations and validated for rubber vulcanizates.37 The values relative to theoretical curves were subsequently plotted to determine whether the treatment predominantly targets selective crosslink cleavage or random main-chain scission.38 In the case of main-chain scission, eqn (5) shows the relationship between the sol fraction of the GTR and the relative decrease in the density of crosslinks.51 − (νf/νi) = 1 − [(1 − √sf)2/(1 − √si)2]where, νi = initial crosslink density (untreated GTR); νf = final crosslink density after treatment; si = initial sol fraction of untreated GTR; sf = final sol fraction of treated GTR.

In contrast, the second scenario involves only selective crosslink scission and no degradation of the polymer main-chain, using eqn (6):61 − (νf/νi) = 1 − [γf(1 − √sf)2/γi(1 − √si)2]where, νi = initial crosslink density of untreated GTR; νf = crosslink density after treatment; si = initial sol fraction of untreated GTR; sf = final sol fraction after treatment; γi = initial crosslinking index; γf = final crosslinking index after treatment.

The crosslinking index shows the average number of crosslink bonds per polymer chain, which can be determined by eqn (7)7γx = (νx × Mn)/ρwhere, γx = crosslinking index; νx = crosslink density (mol cm−3); Mn = number-average molecular weight (g mol−1); ρ = density of rubber (g cm−3).

In this work, the initial crosslinking index is approximated by the following eqn (8),38,398Sol fraction (%) = [(2 + γi) − √(γi2 + 4γi)]/2γiwhere, γi = initial crosslinking index of untreated GTR.

Thermal analysis of treated GTR particles

The thermal behaviour of degraded GTR samples was investigated using a TA Instruments (Waters; SDT650 Trios). Approximately 5–10 mg of the sample was placed into an alumina pan and subjected to heating from 25 °C to 600 °C at a heating rate of 10 °C min−1 in a nitrogen atmosphere with a gas flow rate of 100 mL min−1.40

Soil sample collection and enrichment of bacterial consortia

Soil samples were collected from two geographically distinct waste tires dumping sites (Site 1 and Site 2) in Goa, India. Geolocations have been provided in SI. At each site, soils in direct contact with waste tires were scraped and sampled. Enrichment cultures were prepared in 100 mL of MSM supplemented with 1% (w/v) 30-mesh ground tire rubber (GTR) particles as the sole carbon and energy source. Enrichment cultures were maintained at 30 °C and 37 °C with continuous agitation at 120 rpm for 90 days, with serial transfers (10 mL) into fresh MSM-GTR medium at 4-week intervals to promote active laboratory evolution and enrichment of rubber-degrading microorganisms. After the final enrichment cycle, bacterial cells were harvested at 4 °C by centrifugation at 10 000 rpm for 16S rRNA gene amplicon sequencing.8

16S rRNA gene amplicon sequencing

DNA was extracted from enriched bacterial pellets using the DNeasy PowerSoil Pro Kit (Qiagen). The V3–V4 hypervariable region of the 16S rRNA gene was amplified using standard Illumina-compatible primers. Amplicon libraries were prepared following the Illumina amplicon-based metagenomic sequencing library preparation protocol and quantified using the Qubit Fluorometer 4.0. Sequencing was performed on the Illumina platform (NovaSeq 6000) using a 500-cycle (2 × 250 bp) reagent kit on an SP flow cell. Taxonomic classification was conducted using the BaseSpace Sequence Hub metagenomics analysis pipeline with its built-in classifier against the Greengenes database (v13.5), and further analyses were performed using MicrobiomeAnalyst.8,9,21,41–43 Alpha diversity indices, including Shannon diversity (H′), Simpson diversity (1 − D), Pielou's evenness (J′), and observed richness, were calculated from genus-level count data.

Computational analysis of metagenomic reads for rubber-degradation genes

Raw sequencing reads were subjected to quality assessment using FastQC. Subsequently, quality filtering and adapter trimming were performed using fastp with a minimum base quality score of Q20. The filtered reads were assembled into contigs using MEGAHIT with default parameters, including a default minimum contig length of 200 bp. The assembled contigs were functionally annotated using Prokka with the kingdom set to bacteria and default annotation settings. All preprocessing, assembly, and annotation steps were executed on the Galaxy platform. The annotated contigs were screened for genes encoding key rubber-degrading enzymes. In addition, Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG)-based metabolic pathway reconstruction was performed through manual exploration of organism-specific KEGG maps and KEGG Orthology (KO) annotations available for G. polyisoprenivorans. KEGG is a curated database that links genes to biological functions, metabolic pathways, and cellular processes, whereas KO is a functional classification system that assigns genes to orthologous groups based on conserved biological functions, enabling pathway reconstruction across different organisms. Downstream metabolic pathways, including fatty acid degradation (β-oxidation) and the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle, were interpreted through reference genome-based KEGG pathway mapping to evaluate the metabolic framework for assimilation of polyisoprene-derived degradation intermediates.43

Results and discussion

Particle size effect on the growth of Gordonia polyisoprenivorans Kd2

The growth behaviour of the G. polyisoprenivorans Kd2 strain was evaluated under constant aeration and agitation, with different GTR particle sizes. Erlenmeyer flasks containing cultures of G. polyisoprenivorans Kd2 utilizing GTR as the sole carbon source exhibited an increase in medium pH, from 7.0 to approximately 10.0 after 28 days of incubation, possibly due to either the leaching of amine-containing additives,26 such as p-phenylenediamine antioxidants and dihydroquinoline or the release of ammonia into the medium as a result of cell biomass formation during the stationary phase.44 The difference in GTR particle size significantly affected the growth of the bacterial strain. As observed, smaller particles (400–600 µm) supported higher cell biomass and cell viability compared to larger particles (3–4 mm).

As shown in Fig. 1, a 2.49 × 103-fold increase in cell viability was achieved by decreasing the GTR particle size, confirming that particle size influences its surface area-to-volume ratio, which determines the volume of GTR accessible to bacterial cells.20 Statistical analysis using an unpaired two-tailed Welch's t-test indicated significant differences during the exponential phase (OD600: p < 0.05; log10CFU mL−1: p < 0.05). It was also observed that cell acclimatization to the substrate was delayed with the higher-particle-size substrate compared to the smaller-particle-size substrate. With larger GTR particles, cells achieved maximum viability [8.29 (±0.5) × 107 CFU mL−1] at day 24; on the other hand, with 400–600 µm particle sizes, cell viability reached maximum [2.06 (±0.52) × 1011 CFU mL−1] at day 16. This increase in bacterial cell biomass and viability indicates degradation of rubber. The bacterial growth curve showed that the phase transition from exponential to stationary phase began after day 16. Growth kinetics were also determined from the exponential phase of each condition. Cultures grown on 400–600 µm GTR particles exhibited a specific growth rate (µ) of ∼0.25 per day (doubling time ≈2.8 days; R2 = 0.98), compared with ∼0.21 per day (doubling time ≈3.4 days; R2 = 0.98) for cultures on 3–4 mm GTR. The higher growth rate on smaller particles could be linked to the higher surface-area-to-volume ratio of the 400–600 µm fraction, which increases bacterial accessibility to the substrate relative to the lower ratio of the 3–4 mm GTR, consistent with the difference in viable cell counts observed.

The biodegradation of rubber is inherently a slow process. Bacterial growth occurs gradually on rubber substrates, extending incubation periods over weeks or months to achieve efficient degradation. Although G. polyisoprenivorans belongs to the non-clear-zone-forming group and exhibits slightly faster growth than the clear-zone-forming group, which takes about 10–12 weeks, such as Streptomyces coelicolor 1A or Streptomyces sp. strain K30, cultivation periods of at least 4–6 weeks are required.14,19,45 A similar trend has been observed with Gordonia westfalica.46

Mass reduction measurement

The net weight loss of GTR particles after 28-day incubation period was around 0.4–4.5% when using a pure culture of G. polyisoprenivorans Kd2. Here, the initial weight (Wi) was 0.5 g for all the samples, including the abiotic control, which showed 1.40 (±0.85) % weight loss, attributed to surface wear and tear and handling-related losses. Compared with the smaller particle size GTR, the larger particle size GTR showed 1.80 (±0.57) % weight loss (Fig. 2).

Weight loss (%) of GTR following 28-day incubation, showing enhanced mass reduction for 400–600 µm particles compared with 3–4 mm particles and the abiotic control. Error bars represent standard deviation (n = 2).

In comparison, the smaller particle size GTR sample exhibited a maximum of 5.80 (±0.28) % weight loss, a clear indication of a size-dependent trend that aligns with our study on the effect of particle size on cell biomass and viability parameters. The net weight loss (%) relative to the abiotic control is shown in Fig. 2. Large particle size exhibited a negligible effect on biodegradation. The enhanced mass reduction observed for the smaller particle size could be associated with increased surface area, which likely facilitated improved bacterial attachment.20 However, the relatively low overall weight loss suggests that biodegradation primarily occurred at the GTR surface, consistent with the extracellular mode of action of Lcp-induced degradation reported for Gordonia species.15

The degradation of deproteinized natural rubber (DPNR) films by Gordonia isolates, previously reported by ref. 47, exhibited 9–42% weight loss after 30 days, depending on the strain and experimental conditions. However, such higher degradation rates are typically obtained with purified or deproteinized natural rubber, which lacks the dense sulfur crosslink network, antioxidants, and additives.6 Unlike many studies that employ pre-treated or purified natural rubber substrates, the present work investigated biodegradation of highly vulcanized WTR, which represents real-world waste tire materials. Although the observed 5.8% mass loss is comparable to values reported for fungal degradation of vulcanized GTR over four weeks,28 it is important to note that weight loss underestimates rubber network-level biodegradation of GTR. To understand the mechanism, the decrease in crosslink density, increase in molecular weight between crosslinks (Mc), and increase in sol fraction indicate genuine chemical scission of the polymer network rather than surface-level erosion, as discussed in Section 3.8.

Schiff's staining of treated GTR

The colonization behaviour of G. polyisoprenivorans Kd2 on the surface of GTR was evaluated using Schiff's reagent. As illustrated in Fig. S1 and S2 in the SI, biodegradation of GTR produced a characteristic purple colouration, as reported previously.6,23 This observation indicates the formation of aldehyde and ketone groups containing biodegradation products (oligo-isoprenoids) during the process, while no colour formation occurred in the untreated GTR.

The detection of oligo-isoprenoids formation as a result of G. polyisoprenivorans-mediated biodegradation of vulcanized GTR was recorded using LC-MS. The Lcp-mediated oxidative cleavage of polyisoprene resulted in production of variable lengths oligo-isoprenoids (C15–C65), as shown in Fig. 3.

Since, the substrate used in the present study is a highly vulcanized waste tire rubber, the multipeak chromatogram is likely due to the co-extraction of additives or their transformed products, that might have leached out of the GTR matrix into the culture medium, such as antioxidants, accelerators, plasticizers, waxes, and activators. The comprehensive study on the impact of additives of commercial rubber compounds on the microbial and enzymatic degradation of rubber is published earlier.26 These additives are toxic to bacterial cells and limit enzymatic actions, therefore, efficient biodegradation becomes challenging.48 However, mechanical, chemical or physical pretreatments approach might aid to make GTR more accessible for enzymatic cleavage.4,40 These oligo-isoprenoids could be considered as precursors for the synthesis of fragrances, hormones, and pharmaceutical compounds. These oligo-isoprenoids could be chemically or enzymatically cyclized to produce cyclic compounds or be further converted into biofuels. Consequently, the extraction of purified oligo-isoprenoids from the biodegradation of rubber provided a strategy for transforming GTR into value-added chemicals.25

ATR-FTIR of treated GTR

ATR-FTIR analysis of the biodegraded GTR after incubation with G. polyisoprenivorans Kd2 showed variations in spectral intensity compared to the untreated GTR (Fig. 4). Minor changes observed at 669 cm−1 and 700 cm−1, attributed to C–S and S–S stretching vibrations associated with sulfur crosslinks, suggested alterations in the crosslink environment.49 A relative decrease of the CH2 band at 833 cm−1 was observed for the treated samples compared to that of the untreated GTR. The FTIR peak at 1423 cm−1 slightly decreased after treatment, suggesting possible CC cleavage. A relative decrease at 1538 cm−1 was also observed, which corresponds to Zn-related compounds. A slight increase in band intensity at 1630 cm−1 in smaller-size GTR particles indicated the possible presence of aldehyde groups, which were absent in larger-size GTR particles compared to the control. The peaks at 2848 cm−1 and 2916 cm−1, corresponding to the symmetric and asymmetric C–H stretching vibrations of CH2 and CH3 groups, showed minor changes that reflect modifications in the hydrocarbon chains.

ATR-FTIR spectra of untreated GTR and G. polyisoprenivorans Kd2-treated GTR for 400–600 µm and 3–4 mm particles after 28 days, showing changes in characteristic functional group regions indicative of surface modification. Black spectrum represents untreated GTR (abiotic control), cyan and brown spectra represent 3–4 mm GTR particles, and purple and orange represent 400–600 µm GTR particles (spectra were recorded in duplicates).

The broadening in the 3200–3300 cm−1 (O–H stretching) region further supported evidence of GTR surface oxidation.21,50 These spectral changes were relatively more evident in the 400–600 µm fraction. These surface chemical modifications of rubber could change its interfacial properties and reactivity. Similar changes in surface chemistry have been shown to alter the interfacial behaviour of rubber materials following chemical functionalization.51,52 The absence of intensity reduction in major peaks or appearance of new carbonyl bands indicated that the treatment primarily induced surface modification rather than extensive bulk degradation, consistent with the observed net weight loss.

These analyses suggested that the biodegradation efficiency of GTR by G. polyisoprenivorans Kd2 is size-dependent, with smaller GTR fractions being better substrates for biodegradation than larger particles.

FESEM and EDS analysis of treated GTR

GTR samples were observed under FESEM at multiple magnifications, as shown in Fig. 5. Significant changes were bacterial cell adhesion and colonization on the rubber surface [Fig. 5(d)–(f)], roughness, erosions, microcracks or micropits on the GTR surface as a result of microbial treatment [Fig. 5(g)–(i)],29 whereas the untreated GTR showed smooth surface morphology, which is typical of vulcanized rubber particles [Fig. 5(a)–(c)]. During colonization, the cells adhered and embedded in the rubber matrix, forming surface penetrations.

Mechanism of biodegradation

Change in crosslink density and molecular weight between crosslinks

The changes in the integrity of the vulcanized network of the GTR matrix due to G. polyisoprenivorans Kd2 treatment were evaluated by calculating crosslink density (ν) and molecular weight of polymer chains between crosslinks (Mc). It reflects the average length of polymer chains between effective crosslinking points. The calculated values for untreated and biologically treated GTR (400–600 µm) are summarized in Table 4.

The untreated GTR exhibited a crosslink density of 9.58 (±0.74) × 10−3 mol cm−3, a densely crosslinked structure typical of vulcanized tire rubber. The relative standard deviation of 7.4% reflects the heterogeneity of waste tire materials, which vary due to differences in sulfur crosslink distribution. Following bacterial treatment, the crosslink density slightly decreased to 8.57 (±0.19) × 10−3 mol cm−3, corresponding to a 10.54% reduction. It suggested a slight disruption of sulfur crosslinks and indicated that microbial interaction modified the rubber network. With respect to the reduction in crosslink density, the molecular weight between crosslinks increased from 120 g mol−1 for untreated GTR to 134 g mol−1 after treatment (Table 4). This increase in Mc suggested partial disruption of crosslinking points within the rubber network, possibly associated with changes in sulfur-containing bonds. These observations indicated slight network loosening and surface-dominated biodegradation in a densely vulcanized rubber tire. The decrease in crosslink density and increase in molecular weight between crosslinks demonstrated that G. polyisoprenivorans treatment induced slight structural modifications within the rubber matrix. However, G. polyisoprenivorans-mediated biodegradation is inherently slow and substantially depends on the size fraction of the rubber particle.20,29 Sulfur crosslinks, inorganic fillers, and diffusional barriers within the rubber matrix constrained the bioaccessibility of GTR. Effective biodegradation requires complementary strategies. Effective pretreatment to remove sulfur and additive components could enhance microbial degradation rates.6,26 Environmentally friendly green solvents, such as deep eutectic solvents (DESs), or microwave-assisted devulcanization4 and detoxification processes could be employed as a pretreatment. Moreover, reducing GTR particle size prior to pretreatment would increase surface area and facilitate microbial attachment to rubber particles. The use of mixed cultures, microbial consortia, or recombinant strains overexpressing rubber-degrading enzymes, such as Lcp and RoxA/RoxB, might further enhance GTR degradation.15,21,56 Integration of the process into controlled bioreactor systems with optimized aeration and continuous nutrient supply could also enhance enzyme production and substrate utilization.57 These approaches may offer practical routes to overcome the bioaccessibility limitations of the rubber substrate identified in this work, such as improving the efficiency of rubber degradation and increasing the overall yield of valuable byproducts.

Soluble fraction and Horikx analysis

Following the sol fraction calculation (eqn (4)), the untreated GTR exhibited a sol fraction of 2.73 (±0.36) %, indicating the presence of a small amount of extractable low-molecular-weight components and additives. The sol fraction increased to 5.51 (±0.37) % after G. polyisoprenivorans treatment, indicating enhanced extraction of low-molecular-weight components and partial disruption of the vulcanized rubber network.37,38 The experimental point derived from these values was plotted on the Horikx diagram alongside theoretical curves for selective crosslink scission and random main-chain scission, as shown in Fig. 7.

The experimental point appeared closer to and slightly above the theoretical random main-chain scission curve. This observation indicated that the degradation process was dominated by main-chain scission of the polyisoprene backbone rather than selective cleavage of sulfur crosslinks. Such behaviour is consistent with the known enzymatic mechanism of Gordonia species, where the extracellular Lcp catalyzes oxidative cleavage of the cis-1,4-polyisoprene backbone, generating oligomeric products containing free terminal aldehyde and ketone groups (oligo-isoprenoids).15,20,29,53,56 Overall, the Horikx plot analysis, combined with swelling-derived network values, provides quantitative evidence that biodegradation proceeded predominantly through random main-chain scission of the polyisoprene backbone rather than selective crosslink cleavage, rarely addressed in rubber biodegradation studies so far. The decrease in crosslink density, increase in Mc, and 2-fold increase in sol fraction collectively indicated polymer network breakdown, though not substantial, rather than surface-level erosion, which would not alter the crosslink density of the GTR matrix. Together with the Horikx analysis and the Schiff staining (Section 3.3), these observations have established that the degradation, while confined largely to the GTR surface, proceeded through enzymatic main-chain scission rather than the mere surface-level erosion. Community composition

Site 1 replicates yielded higher read counts (3026153 and 3224399 reads, respectively) compared with Site 2 replicates (888932 and 1093588 reads), as summarized in Table S1. The sequencing identified 58 phyla, 2301 genera, and 4176 species-level taxonomic categories. However, ∼42.6% of reads at the species level remained unclassified (pooled across all four samples; Table S2), which is consistent with the 30–55% range reported for hydrocarbon- and polymer-based enrichment amplicon datasets in previous studies.66,67

The enrichment communities exhibited distinct phylum-level profiles between the two sites. Site 1 replicates were dominated by Actinomycetota (42.9–44.6%), followed by Pseudomonadota (27.1–28.8%) and Armatimonadota (10.7–12.7%), whereas Site 2 replicates were dominated by Pseudomonadota (53.2–57.7%), followed by Armatimonadota (15.4–20.0%), with Actinomycetota reduced to 8.8–8.9% (Fig. 9). Minor phyla detected across both sites included Chloroflexota (2.6–4.3%), Planctomycetota (1.7–4.4%), Candidatus saccharibacteria (1.5–5.2%), Thermomicrobiota (1.1–2.3%), Bacteroidota (0.6–2.3%), and Bacillota (1.2–2.5%). The phylum-level profile is similar with previously reported rubber-degrading consortia, in which Actinomycetota includes key taxa associated with rubber biodegradation.19,45 The co-occurrence and dynamics of phyla are also dependent on the pivotal role of climatic and geographic conditions.68

The metagenomics data provided essential ecological context for the mechanistic findings obtained with G. polyisoprenivorans. The enrichment data revealed that, in natural soil environments at the rubber tire dumping sites, Gordonia functions as part of a natural consortium rather than as a dominant taxon.

Metabolic pathway reconstruction for rubber degradation by G. polyisoprenivorans

Shotgun metagenomic sequencing and functional annotation of the GTR-enriched consortium revealed the detection of K23117, encoding endo-cleaving rubber dioxygenase corresponding to Lcp1 and Lcp2, within the assembled metagenome, suggesting functional genomic evidence that the consortium contained the enzymatic machinery for polyisoprene chain scission.16 To evaluate the downstream metabolic fate of Lcp-generated intermediates, KEGG-based pathway reconstruction was performed using organism-specific KEGG maps and KO annotations for G. polyisoprenivorans.76

As shown in Fig. 14, pathway mapping revealed a fatty acid degradation network, which included the complete enzymatic machinery for β-oxidation, including acyl-CoA dehydrogenases (EC 1.3.3.6, 1.3.8.7, 1.3.8.8), enoyl-CoA hydratases (EC 4.2.1.17, 4.2.1.74), 3-hydroxyacyl-CoA dehydrogenases (EC 1.1.1.35, 1.1.1.211), and acetyl-CoA acyltransferases (EC 2.3.1.16).17

KEGG fatty acid degradation pathway map for G. polyisoprenivorans.

KEGG reconstruction further confirmed the presence of an intact tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle (Fig. 15), with all essential enzymes represented, including citrate synthase (EC 2.3.3.1), aconitase (EC 4.2.1.3), isocitrate dehydrogenase (EC 1.1.1.42), 2-oxoglutarate dehydrogenase (EC 1.2.4.2), succinyl-CoA synthetase (EC 6.2.1.4/6.2.1.5), succinate dehydrogenase (EC 1.3.5.1), fumarase (EC 4.2.1.2), and malate dehydrogenase (EC 1.1.1.37).17

KEGG citrate cycle (TCA cycle) pathway map for G. polyisoprenivorans.

The presence of TCA cycle indicated that acetyl-CoA generated from β-oxidation of rubber-derived intermediates can be channelled into central carbon and energy metabolism.16,17,20 Based on the KEGG reconstruction, a putative metabolic framework for vulcanized rubber biodegradation by G. polyisoprenivorans could be anticipated: (i) extracellular oxidative endocleavage of poly(cis-1,4-isoprene) by Lcp (K23117), generating oligo-isoprenoids; (ii) uptake and metabolic conversion through β-oxidation (Fig. 14); (iii) acetyl-CoA generation; and (iv) entry into the TCA cycle for energy production and biomass formation (Fig. 15). This framework provided a possible gene-to-phenotype explanation for the observed GTR biodegradation in this study. Although the shotgun metagenomic analysis confirmed the presence of K23117 in the enriched community, encoding the rubber cleaving enzyme, metatranscriptomic profiling to confirm in situ expression of lcp and downstream catabolic genes within the consortium represents the future scope of the work.

Ecological context of G. polyisoprenivorans

Gordonia species are reported to show adaptation to rubber surfaces and possess well-established rubber oxygenase mechanism involved in polyisoprene cleavage. They are also reported to show better colonization, biofilm formation, and degradation performance on complex and highly vulcanized rubber.11,16,77 Although pure-culture experiments demonstrated that G. polyisoprenivorans is capable of biodegrading vulcanized GTR through Lcp-mediated oxidative main-chain scission (as confirmed by Horikx plot analysis), the enrichment data revealed that in natural soil environments, Gordonia functions as a member of a natural consortium. The community-level study suggested that the relatively low weight loss of rubber observed in pure-culture could be attributed to the absence of secondary microorganisms (Pedomicrobium, Hyphomicrobium) that could otherwise act as potential cross-feeders of degradation products or byproducts, such as aldehyde/ketone containing oligo-isoprenoids (Sections 3.3 and 3.4). The desulfurization efficiency of G. polyisoprenivorans could also be enhanced if combined with the enrichment of desulfurizing bacterial strains.

The 42.6% unclassified reads at species level falls within the 30–55% range reported for other hydrocarbon- and polymer-enrichment amplicon datasets of previous studies66,67 provides a possibility of follow-up shotgun metagenomics or metagenome-assembled genome (MAG) reconstruction. Such approaches would likely yield novel taxa and novel Lcp homologs.

Conclusions

This study shows that the pure culture of G. polyisoprenivorans Kd2 strain is capable of degrading vulcanized waste tire rubber through main-chain scission and surface erosion. The 10.54% reduction in crosslink density, the increase in soluble fraction, the Horikx analysis, and the detection of oligo-isoprenoids suggested cleavage of the rubber polyisoprene backbone. The degradation was mostly concentrated at the particle surface, with a mass loss of 5.80% over a 28-day incubation period. The microbial community profiling of waste tire dumping sites placed the biodegradation mechanism in its environmental setting that the indigenous communities were dominated by Actinomycetota, with the presence of Nocardia as the most abundant genus and Gordonia as one of the members of the microbial community, with shotgun metagenomics confirming the lcp gene de novo. Based on these findings, employing pretreatment strategies could offer a means of improving the rate of biodegradation, by reducing vulcanizing agents and toxic rubber additive contents. Time-resolved assays using purified Lcp would allow the reaction kinetics of the degradation to be determined, and genome-resolved metatranscriptomics together with stable-isotope probing could observe how non-degrading members of the community contribute to the overall biodegradation process. Together with pretreatment approaches, efficient biological treatment may hold promise in designing a synergistic mixed culture or microbial consortia. Advancing these approaches from laboratory to scaled-up processes operating on waste tire feedstock could be an essential step towards rubber waste valorization.
