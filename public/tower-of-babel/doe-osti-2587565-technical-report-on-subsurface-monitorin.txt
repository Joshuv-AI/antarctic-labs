Summary
Abilities to accurately characterize the subsurface in a geothermal setting is key to assess and
support production. An important element of geothermal reservoir monitoring is also the ability to
investigate fluid transport within fracture network.
This report focuses on improving subsurface imaging and monitoring in geothermal settings using
full waveform inversion based on the adjoint method and time-lapse imaging. To assess our
method, we rely on a dense seismic dataset collected in 2016 at the Brady Hot Springs geothermal
site in Nevada for the DOE-funded project Poroelastic Tomography by Adjoint Inverse Modeling
of Data from Seismology, Geodesy, and Hydrology. This dataset captures subsurface changes
across four stages of geothermal power plant operations, which involve varying rates of fluid
injection and extraction.
Two velocity models were previously derived from this dataset using different methods: one based
on travel times and another on sweep interferometry. Our first step is to refine these models using
adjoint tomography, which has been applied successfully at global and regional-scales but is less
common at the reservoir-scale. Two approaches are then explored for time-lapse analysis: directly
comparing refined tomographic models from different stages or backpropagating waveform
differences relative to a baseline tomographic model. The main take away is that both approaches
highlight similar reservoir behaviors, but the latter approach is more computationally effective in
capturing small-scale changes in subsurface properties.
For this work, we leverage the use of Salvus (www.mondaic.com), an end-to-end seismic imaging
solution, relying on the spectral element method to compute forward and adjoint simulations, and
developed by Mondaic Ltd. It includes integrated workflow management that handles waveform
and metadata, launches simulations, computes waveform misfits and adjoint sources, and iterates
for model updates by nonlinear optimization.
A couple of crucial strategies we used here for reservoir-scale, sweep-type signal adjoint analysis
can be summarized as follow:
 - Data processing is key to remove the sweep signature of the vibroseis source classically
 used for field survey. Here we propose the cross-correlation of the sweep source and the
 raw data.
 - Because of the signal still exhibiting high frequency and the complexity to accurately
 account for the vibroseis source, rescaling and graph-space optimal transport misfit
 function are used to mitigate cycle-skipping issues and prioritize time-shifts over amplitude
 changes.

Keywords: seismic tomography, full waveform inversion, adjoint method, 4D seismic, geothermal
monitoring

Introduction
A dataset was collected by a dense seismic array deployed for active source tomography at the
Brady Hot Springs geothermal site in Nevada for the DOE-funded project Poroelastic Tomography
by Adjoint Inverse Modeling of Data from Seismology, Geodesy, and Hydrology (PoroTomo,
Feigl et al., 2017). This rich dataset involves seismic sources using a vibroseis truck, recorded by
an array of distributed acoustic sensing (DAS) and by an array of three-components 5-Hz

LLNL-TR-2010654                                                                                    2

FairfieldNodal receivers. This field survey was repeated four times to try and capture hydraulic
changes under the subsurface through four different stages of power plant operation involving
changes of rates of pumping in the injection and production wells.

The Brady Hot Springs area is an interesting test site with a system of highly permeable conduits
along faults connecting shallow aquifers to the deep geothermal reservoir (Ali et al, 2016). Using
the nodal dataset, two velocity models were derived during the PoroTomo project, a Vp model
based on travel times (Parker et al., 2018) and a Vp, Vs, Qp, Qs model based on sweep
interferometry (Matzel et al., 2017). Parker et al. (2018) highlight the complexity of handling
swept-frequency sources, like the vibroseis used in the 2016 field survey. They investigated both
source signal cross-correlation and deconvolution to remove the vibroseis sweep source signature.
They concluded that the cross-correlation approach provides better signal-to-noise ratio and went
on to carry their inversion using that method to remove the sweep signal. Their P-wave velocity
tomography approach shows velocity contrasts across known faults detected by other studies (e.g.,
Jolie et al., 2015), low velocity zones near the surface that can be mapped to fumarole locations,
and a comparison with well logs that suggests shallower water table than previously reported.
Matzel et al. (2017), on the other hand, used a sweep interferometric approach, which in essence
also leads to the removal of the sweep source signature but by autocorrelation. The derived
anisotropic velocity model also shows good agreement with the fault system. In addition, changes
in hydraulic properties due to the four stages of power plant operation can be shown on Qs/Qp
ratio maps (Matzel et al., 2017), which highlight the correlation between dynamic changes in
seismic amplitudes and the fluid pathways at each stage. Along with the nodal dataset, a DAS
system was deployed consisting of 8,700 m of fiber-optic cable buried in shallow trenches (DAS-
Horizontal), as well as a 340 m cable in a well (DAS-Vertical). One can refer to Zeng et al. (2021)
for the demonstration of ambient noise tomography using DAS-Horizontal dataset to recover S-
wave velocities at shallow depths, thereby revealing low velocity zones correlating well with the
location of the fumaroles at the surface.

Discrepancies still exist between these models due to the different methods used. Here, we chose
to assess full waveform inversion based on adjoint method. Adjoint tomography has been
successfully applied at global (e.g., Bozdağ et al., 2016; Lei et al., 2020) and regional (e.g., Tape
et al., 2010; Zhu et al., 2015; Rodgers et al., 2022) scales. While at the reservoir scale, studies like
Luo et al. (2009) and Zhu et al. (2009) drew the connection between the density kernel from adjoint
tomography and the “image principle” introduced by Claerbout (1971), which is intensively used
in exploration geophysics to map/image geological structures. While these studies focused only
on the imaging aspect directly using the finite-frequency sensitivity kernels, full waveform
inversion was discussed in Yuan et al. (2015). The authors offer a strategy based on wavelet-
multiscale adjoint scheme including body waves and surface waves, by first fitting surface waves
then adding body waves. Their approach differs from others in performing the iterative inversion
through a sequence of nested subspace projections on a wavelet basis. They also use first an
envelope misfit function then a waveform misfit function, to efficiently handle near surface
heterogeneities which can account for complex scattering and mode conversions. Another
interesting study by Wang et al. (2016) addresses the complexity of using adjoint tomography for
exploration geophysics in reservoir scale area containing many reflections, which can prevent
gradient-based methods of recovering long wavelength of the velocity model. The authors propose

LLNL-TR-2010654                                                                                       3

to decompose the gradient in four terms after wavefield decomposition to then successfully invert
for short and long wavelength components of the velocity model alternatively.

While global and regional adjoint waveform tomography (AWT) are widely used, there is still
room to popularize and adapt AWT at reservoir scale. In the following, we first present the dataset
processing used in this study to handle the sweep signal source signature. We then discuss the type
of misfit function chosen for this problem. One key element of adjoint tomography is the choice
of the misfit function, that is, the measurement made between data and synthetics, which we aim
to minimize, and which informs the nature of the finite-frequency kernels and the gradient used to
iteratively derive a new structural model. Results of the derivation of a new baseline model based
on the dataset collected during the stage 1 of the plant operation are presented. Different strategies
are discussed to track changes over the different states of operations, with the goal of identifying
subsurface changes related to different types of fluid extraction or injection into the geothermal
reservoir.

Dataset Processing, Initial Model, and Misfit Function
Dataset
The dataset analyzed in this report is the one collected at the Brady Hot Springs geothermal site
during PoroTomo two-week field deployment in March 2016. It involved vibroseis sources using
three vibration modes (vertical, longitudinal, and transverse), recorded at 196 locations by
combined arrays of distributed acoustic sensing and 238 three-component 5 Hz FairfieldNodal
seismic nodes (Figure 1). In the present study, we are only focusing on the nodal dataset. The area
investigated covers a volume of 1,500 m by 500 m down to a depth of 400 m.

Four successive stages of operation were performed by the operator Ormat Technologies during
this deployment:
 - Stage 1: normal operations consisting of normal rates of injection and production activity,
 - Stage 2: a site shut down for maintenance characterized by limited fluid extraction or
 injection into the reservoir,
 - Stage 3: an increase infield injection,
 - Stage 4: a return to normal operations.

These different stages resulted in different subsurface responses, captured during the field
deployment, by repeated data acquisitions. Figure 2 shows the pressure changes recorded at 2
wells, located at the south-western and north-eastern quadrant of the target area, respectively,
highlighting the impact of the different stages of operation.

only use the vertical mode, which offers the strongest P-wave signals, and which is readily
synthetically mimicked by a vertical force.

To better test and assess our approach, we tackle 2D cross sections in the targeted area. To that
end, we also carry a 3D to 2D data conversion equivalent to simulating a point source response as
a line source response (Forbriger et al., 2014).

Initial Model M00
As a starting model to the adjoint based full waveform inversion described in the following
sections, we derive a roughly 1D initial model from the 3D Vp, Vs, Qp, Qs interferometry model
by Matzel et al. (2017) and the 3D gravity-based density model by Witter et al. (2016). This
strategy allows to smooth out any structural signatures inherited from the previous inverse method
(see Figure 6).

Misfit Function
While waveform differences between observed and synthetic dataset is common at reservoir scale,
this waveform misfit is highly sensitive to the initial model, which needs to be very close to the
solution to avoid cycle-skipping issues and convergence to a secondary local minimum. On the
other hand, graph space optimal transport misfit, used here, has shown to be less dependent on an
accurate initial model by avoiding cycle-skipping (Métivier et al., 2019). Our inversion strategy
also relies on windowing the waveform signal typically used in adjoint approach to focus on
meaningful signal information. Due to the high frequency content of our dataset, windows
selection is based here on the envelope of the synthetic waveforms, which roughly positions the
windows around the energy of the synthetic data. We also preform the inversion through 3 different
“legs”. During each leg, we constrain the source-receiver distance of the data used in the inversion,
extending it from 400 m (leg 1 and leg 2) to 600 m (leg 3). At the start of each leg, the data and
synthetics are rescaled, and new windows are picked. Note that the strategy to scale data and
synthetics arises from the difficulty to properly match the complexity of the vibroseis source,
removing amplitude information. Nonetheless, the use of the graph space optimal transport misfit
and Wasserstein distance helps to prioritize time shifts over amplitude changes. Finally, we use a
trust-region limited memory Broyden–Fletcher–Goldfarb–Shanno algorithm (L-BFGS) as our
optimization method (Modrak & Tromp, 2016; Boehm et al., 2018).

Time-lapse Analysis
To assess how well we can capture any dynamic deformation of the geothermal reservoir and fluid
transportation during the four successive stages of plant operation, we explored two approaches:

LLNL-TR-2010654                                                                                                  7

 - Approach 1: a straightforward approach consisting of using the datasets from stage 1 and
 the successive stages, to derive respective tomographic models, and directly evaluate the
 difference between these models.
 - Approach 2: recognizing that the mechanical subsurface behavior between stages may
 translate into small waveform amplitude differences, we derive a baseline model based on
 stage 1 dataset and backpropagate the waveform differences between stages 2, 3, 4 with
 respect to stage 1, specifically highlighting areas in the baseline model subject to changes
 during the successive stages.

Approach 1: direct tomographic model differences
Starting from the initial model M00 described in the previous section, we carry full waveform
inversion based on adjoint method, inverting for Vp, Vs, and density, to further refine the Earth
model in the 5-12Hz bandwidth during stage 1 of the plant normal operation. Then using this stage
1 model as baseline, we investigate temporal subsurface structural changes during the different
stages of operation by deriving a new tomographic model for stage 4 in the same bandwidth as the
baseline. The direct difference of these 2 tomographic models should help highlight and identify
the impact of shallow fluid circulations.

Figure 7 displays the 2D profile AA’ we are focused on, consisting of 24 nodal receivers and 7
sources. Results of the misfit reduction during the inversion for stage 1 and stage 4 models are
shown on Figures 8 and 9, respectively. By model M99, the misfit doesn’t change drastically, and
we reasonably fit the data (see Figure 10).

After deriving models M99 for both stage 1 and stage 4, we can highlight the areas that were
improved compared to the starting model M00 (Figure 6). Model updates seem to be localized in
the first 100-200 m, with sharper and smaller heterogeneities at shallow depths (see Figures 11 &
12).

Focusing on the time-lapse analysis, Figure 13 displays the differences between stage 4 model
M99 and stage 1 model M99. As a reminder, stage 1 through 4 involved different plant operations,
leading to different states of subsurface fluid loading and uploading. Stage 1 and 4 both
characterize a return to normal operations. Based on Figure 13, it appears that some differences
between these models remain, localized ~100 m below the surface, indicating that the mechanical
behavior of the reservoir has been changed through the different operations preventing a full return
to the reservoir state prior to shut down (stage 2) and increased infield injection (stage 3). This
observation agrees with another analysis based on time-lapse attenuation ratio measurements
performed by Matzel et al. (2017).

Approach 2: back propagation of waveform differences into baseline model
In the following, we focus on another 2D cross section BB’, which offers a quasi-alignment of 22
nodal receivers and 7 vibroseis source locations with data available for the 4 stages (Figure 14).
We first derived a refined baseline model up to 20Hz using the dataset of stage 1 normal operation.
Figure 15 shows the evolution of the misfit function through 180 iterations. The strategy used here
is to directly backpropagate the difference between stages 2, 3, 4 with respect to stage 1 (baseline)
to account for the fact that these differences present small amplitudes.

Figure 16 (a) displays the baseline model M180 obtained after 180 iterations. Figure 16 (b)
highlights the updated areas compared to the starting model M00. As in the previous section,
differences are localized in the first 100-200 m, with sharper details at shallow depths and larger
contrasts recorded on the shear velocity parameter.

Regarding the time-lapse analysis, our strategy to directly backpropagate the difference between
each stage and stage 1, our baseline, follows a reverse time migration (RTM) approach. RTM is
an imaging method mapping subsurface reflectivity backpropagating waveform dataset. This, in
essence, is one element of AWT. So, we can readily use the full engine of AWT for RTM. Figure
17 shows the results. With this approach, we observe very sharp and localized anomalies in the
first 50 m under the surface. The shear velocity seems to be the most sensitive parameter to detect
changes. As for approach 1, the subsurface state of stage 1 is not fully recovered at stage 4, again
confirming that the reservoir has been irreversibly changed through the different operations.

Conclusions
This study demonstrates the potential of full waveform inversion using the adjoint method to
enhance geothermal reservoir monitoring. First, we needed to address the challenges associated
with complex vibroseis source signals, leading us to successfully develop a data processing
consisting of cross-correlating the sweep function of the vibroseis source and the recorded dataset,
leading to a collapse of the sweep signal into wavelets. Next, we highlighted the importance of
employing a graph-space optimal transport misfit function, a rescaling of data and synthetics, and
windows selection based on the envelope of the synthetic waveforms, due to the high frequency
and complexity of the dataset, for proper AWT implementation. Using this data processing and
misfit expression, we are able to refine subsurface models at the Brady Hot Springs site based on
AWT.
Time-lapse analysis presents its own added challenges. Here we compared two time-lapse analysis
approaches, one based on direct AWT model comparisons and the other one based on
backpropagating waveform differences relative to a baseline model. Both approaches did provide
results in agreement with other observations, namely, the reservoir does not return to its initial

LLNL-TR-2010654                                                                                              16

state after going through different stages of operations (shutdown, increased infield injection) and
back to normal, indicating that residual deformations, located at shallow depths, are irreversible.
The first approach, however, does involved running several full AWT calculations, which can be
computationally intensive. On the other hand, the second approach is more computationally
efficient and effective in capturing subtle subsurface changes. This approach not only reduces
computational costs but also improves the detection of small-scale property variations, which are
essential for optimizing geothermal energy production.
Future work could focus on further refining these techniques and exploring their applicability to
other geothermal sites, potentially enhancing the sustainability and efficiency of geothermal
energy extraction. More work on data processing could bring reservoir-scale, sweep-type signals,
more readily suitable for AWT. This would involve more in-depth approach to fully remove the
sweep signature of the vibroseis source. Then, amplitude variations can be further integrated in the
misfit. While we had to mainly focus on time shift differences, it is fair to postulate that reservoir
changes due to fluid loading and unloading will have a signature on waveform amplitudes. It would
also be interesting to integrate DAS dataset to the time-lapse analysis. A spectral-element solver
like Salvus can readily output strain to mimic DAS dataset, and efforts are already carried in this
direction (Salvus for modeling DAS surveys).
