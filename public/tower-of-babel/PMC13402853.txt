Survival Outcomes and Prognostic Factors in Patients With Primary Central Nervous System Low‐Grade B‐Cell Lymphoma: A Population‐Based Cohort Study
Authors: Lvwen Chen, Lei Cao, Yuxiao Zhao, Hailing Liu, Sanmei Wang, Miao Zhong, Xiran Wang, Jing Zhang, Xiaoyan Qu, Zhengxu Sun, Lei Fan, Ran Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe incidence of primary central nervous system low‐grade B‐cell lymphoma (PCNSL‐LG) is rare. We aimed to provide the first large‐scale analysis of treatment modalities, survival trends, and prognostic factors for PCNSL‐LG. Patient data diagnosed between 2000 and 2021 were extracted from the Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER) database. A total of 267 adult PCNSL‐LG patients were included. The year of diagnosis was divided into the time period‐1 (2000–2009) and the time period‐2 (2010–2021). Radiotherapy alone usage remained stable over time (27.34% vs. 30.22%, p = 0.605). Chemotherapy alone showed a modest, non‐significant increase (25.00% vs. 31.66%, p = 0.229), while combined chemoradiotherapy declined significantly from 29.69% to 15.11% (p = 0.004). The 1‐, 5‐, and 10‐year overall survival (OS) rates for all PCNSL‐LG patients were 84.3%, 73.4%, and 61.8%, respectively. Overall survival improved significantly, with median OS extending from 11.25 years in period‐1 to not reached in period‐2 (HR 0.46, 95% CI: 0.28–0.74; p = 0.001). Surgery was associated with improved outcomes compared with non‐surgical management (HR 0.47, 95% CI: 0.31–0.72, p < 0.001). In patients with MALT lymphoma histology, no significant prognostic difference was observed across treatment modalities (p = 0.838). This study underscores a marked improvement in survival among PCNSL‐LG patients over the past two decades and offers valuable insights for optimizing clinical management and improving patient outcomes.Keywords: low‐grade B‐cell lymphoma, PCNSL, prognostic factors, SEER, survival outcomes

Introduction

Primary central nervous system lymphoma (PCNSL) is a rare, highly malignant non‐Hodgkin's lymphoma (NHL) that targets the central nervous system (CNS), including the brain, meninges, spinal cord, and eyes, with no evidence of systemic involvement [1]. Based on histopathological criteria, the vast majority (80%–90%) of PCNSL cases are diffuse large B‐cell lymphoma (DLBCL); less than 10% are classified as low‐grade lymphomas [2, 3, 4]. In contrast to high‐grade NHL, low‐grade NHL is defined as the presence of a high proportion of small, mature lymphocytes (more than 90% of tumor cells) and a growth fraction ≤ 20%. In a multicenter prospective clinical trial in Germany, only 3% of the PCNSL cases were low‐grade lymphoma, with 10 out of 332 cases [5]. Karschnia et al. conducted a retrospective analysis of 316 PCNSL patients and found that 6.3% (20/316) had primary dural lymphoma (PDL), with marginal zone lymphoma (MZL) being the predominant subtype of low‐grade lymphoma (6/20), followed by follicular lymphoma (FL, 2/20) [6]. Similar to extracranial lymphoma, low‐grade PCNSL tends to be less aggressive than high‐grade PCNSL and has a relatively better prognosis.

It is important to recognize that PCNSL‐LG is a biologically and clinically distinct entity from high‐grade PCNSL. Unlike high‐grade PCNSL, for which high‐dose methotrexate‐based polychemotherapy with or without whole‐brain radiotherapy has been established as the standard of care [7, 8], there is no established standard treatment paradigm for PCNSL‐LG [9]. Given the infrequency of PCNSL‐LG, the available literature consists predominantly of case reports and small single‐institution series [9, 10]. The Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER) Program of the National Cancer Institute can compensate for the limited number of cases reported by individual centers [11]. Therefore, we conducted a retrospective population‐based study of PCNSL‐LG using the SEER database to analyze therapeutic approaches and survival trends, to identify prognostic risk factors, and to provide evidence that may contribute to the clinical management of this rare disease.

Materials and Methods

Patient Selection

Study data were obtained using the SEER*Stat software (version 8.4.4). Primary CNS involvement was distinguished from secondary CNS involvement by restricting the analysis to patients whose first and only primary malignancy was coded to a CNS site (C70.0, C70.1, C70.9, C71.0–C72.9). Patients with more than one primary malignancy were excluded to avoid inadvertent inclusion of secondary CNS lymphoma. We further excluded cases without histological confirmation and those with zero months of survival. The diagnosis of PCNSL‐LG patients was based on the International Classification of Diseases for Oncology third edition (ICD‐O‐3) histologic codes (9670–9671, 9690–9699, 9823). When comparing the prognosis among various histologic subtypes, we additionally selected patients with an ICD‐O‐3 histologic code of 9680 and site‐specific codes consistent with those described above (C70.0, C70.1, C70.9, C71.0–C72.9) as a reference group.

Data Collection

Our analyses included the following demographic and clinicopathologic variables: age, gender, year of diagnosis, primary site, histology, surgery, radiotherapy, chemotherapy, survival, vital status, and cause of death. Overall survival (OS) was defined as the time from diagnosis to death from any cause. Patients who were alive at the last follow‐up were censored. Disease‐specific survival (DSS) was defined as the time from diagnosis to death attributable to PCNSL‐LG. Deaths from causes other than lymphoma were treated as censored observations. We categorized patient primary treatment into five mutually exclusive groups using the SEER variables “Reason no cancer‐directed surgery,” “Radiation recode,” and “Chemotherapy recode”: (1) no anti‐tumor therapy (no surgery, no radiation, no chemotherapy); (2) surgery alone (surgery without radiation or chemotherapy); (3) radiotherapy alone (radiation with or without surgery, no chemotherapy); (4) chemotherapy alone (chemotherapy with or without surgery, no radiation); and (5) combined chemoradiotherapy (chemotherapy + radiation, with or without surgery). Surgery was defined using SEER “Reason no cancer‐directed surgery.” These codes encompass a range of procedures from excisional biopsy to gross total resection. SEER does not reliably distinguish biopsy from definitive resection for CNS tumors. The timing of surgery relative to systemic therapy or radiation is not available in SEER.

Statistical Analysis

Categorical variables differences between groups were evaluated with the chi‐square test (or Fisher exact test when expected cell counts were < 5). Pairwise chi‐square tests were further performed to compare primary treatment strategies across different periods. Age was compared between eras using the Wilcoxon rank‐sum test. Kaplan–Meier (KM) analysis was employed to plot survival curves for OS and DSS, and the log‐rank test was applied to compare them. We evaluated the influence of underlying factors on prognosis through univariate and multivariate Cox proportional hazards regression analyses. The proportional hazards assumption of each covariate in the regression models was verified using Schoenfeld residuals. Statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.2.2). Two‐sided p values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

Demographics of PCNSL‐LG Patients

A total of 267 adult patients with PCNSL‐LG between 2000 and 2021 were enrolled in the study. The details of the patient screening process were shown in Figure 1. Of these, 128 patients were diagnosed during period‐1 (2000–2009), and 139 patients were diagnosed during period‐2 (2010–2021). Patient characteristics of the study population stratified by the period of diagnosis were shown in Table 1. The median age at diagnosis was 60 years, with a nearly equal distribution between patients aged 18–59 years (48.69%) and those ≥ 60 years (51.31%). Overall, 59% of patients were female and 41% were male. Sex distribution did not differ significantly across eras (p = 0.949). PCNSL‐LG primarily involves the brain parenchyma (54.68%). Other CNS sites include the spinal cord, cranial nerves (18.35%), and the nervous system, not otherwise specified (NOS) (17.60%), while meningeal involvement is rare (9.36%). The most common histologic type of PCNSL‐LG was FL (42.32%) and MALT lymphoma (38.95%), and rarely chronic lymphocytic leukemia (CLL)/small lymphocytic lymphoma (SLL, 12.36%) or LPL (6.37%). There was no significant difference across eras in age (p = 0.144), gender (p = 0.949), primary site (p = 0.547), surgery (p = 0.424), radiation therapy (p = 0.056), or chemotherapy (p = 0.196). Notably, histology distribution differed significantly between eras (p < 0.001). FL was more common in period‐1 (51.56% vs. 33.81%) while MALT lymphoma was more common in period‐2 (26.56% vs. 50.36%). Given that MALT lymphoma carries a more favorable prognosis, this histology shift may partially contribute to the observed improvement in survival over time.

Primary Treatment Strategies Based on the Era of Diagnosis and Age

Considering the critical role of age in determining treatment strategies, we analyzed the primary treatment approaches for the PCNSL‐LG population by age at diagnosis (Figure 2). The proportion of patients receiving no anti‐tumor therapy increased significantly from 2.34% to 10.79% across the two periods (p = 0.006), a trend predominantly observed in those aged ≥ 60 years (from 2.74% to 18.75%). The proportion of patients receiving surgery alone showed a marginal, non‐significant decline from 15.63% to 12.23% over the study period (p = 0.423), a decrease primarily observed in the younger subgroup (< 60 years), where rates fell from 21.82% to 14.67%. The utilization of radiotherapy alone remained relatively stable over time (27.34% vs. 30.22%, p = 0.605), though a modest, non‐significant increase was observed in patients aged < 60 years (21.82% vs. 28.00%, p = 0.424). The use of chemotherapy alone increased modestly from 25.00% to 31.66% over time, though this change was not statistically significant (p = 0.229). Notably, a more pronounced rise was observed in patients aged < 60 years, from 20.00% to 36.00%. Combined chemoradiotherapy declined significantly over time, from 29.69% in period‐1 to 15.11% in period‐2 (p = 0.004). Among patients who survived beyond 5 years (n = 138), the distribution of treatment modalities was as follows: chemotherapy‐based regimens alone, 23.19%; radiotherapy alone, 26.81%; surgery alone, 15.94%; and combined chemoradiotherapy, 31.16% (Table S1).

Survival

The median follow‐up duration was 9.17 years (Q1–Q3: 3.83–15.58), and the median OS for the entire cohort was 15.42 years (95% CI: 12.00–18.17). The 1‐, 5‐, and 10‐year OS rates were 84.3%, 73.4%, and 61.8%, respectively. Over the study period, OS improved significantly, with median OS extending from 11.25 years in period‐1 to not reached in period‐2 (HR 0.46, 95% CI: 0.28–0.74, p = 0.001, Figure 3A). This survival benefit was largely driven by patients aged > 60 years, whose median OS increased from 5.17 years to not reached (HR 0.57, 95% CI: 0.36–0.90, p = 0.018), whereas no significant improvement was observed in the younger subgroup (16–59 years), with median OS not reached in either period (HR 0.44, 95% CI: 0.18–1.07, p = 0.084). When analyzing survival changes over time by age, no significant improvement was detected in patients aged 18–59 years (p = 0.084, Figure 3B), whereas a significant improvement in prognosis was noted in patients aged 60 years and older (p = 0.018, Figure 3C). The DSS curves also showed similar results (Figure 3D–F).

We analyzed the influence of age, gender, period of diagnosis, primary site, histology, and treatment modalities on the relative excess risk of mortality using univariate and multivariable Cox regression analysis (Table 2). Univariate analysis revealed that age (≥ 60 vs. 18–59, 95% CI: 2.92–7.66, p < 0.001), year of diagnosis (2000–2009 vs. 2010–2021, 95% CI: 0.28–0.74, p = 0.001), primary site (meninges vs. brain parenchyma, 95% CI: 0.04–0.65, p = 0.010), histologic type (MALT lymphoma vs. FL, 95% CI: 0.17–0.52, p < 0.001), and surgery (yes vs. no/unknown, 95% CI: 0.31–0.67, p < 0.001) were strongly associated with OS. In the multivariate Cox regression analysis, younger patients, diagnosis in period‐2, primary site in the non‐brain parenchyma, histologic type of MALT lymphoma, and receiving surgical treatment were independently associated with better OS. Among different histologic types of PCNSL, the MALT lymphoma histologic type had the best prognosis (Figure 4A,B).

Prognostic Analysis of Different Treatment Strategies

Combined surgery treatments had a significantly better prognosis than treatments without surgery (95% CI: 0.31–0.78, p = 0.001, Figure 5A). Patients who underwent surgery alone had a better prognosis than those who did not have surgery (95% CI: 0.23–0.75, p = 0.010, Figure 5B). Additionally, the prognosis for patients treated with surgery alone was similar to that for patients treated with surgery combined with radiotherapy/chemotherapy (95% CI: 0.42–1.54, p = 0.541, Figure 5C). Similar results were also reflected in the DSS curves (Figure 5D–F). For PCNSL patients with MALT lymphoma histology, no significant differences in OS (p = 0.838, Figure 6A) or DSS (p = 0.613, Figure 6B) were found between different treatment modalities.

Discussion

This population‐based analysis of 267 adult patients with PCNSL‐LG, spanning two decades (2000–2009 and 2010–2021), revealed several clinically relevant findings. First, treatment patterns have shifted over time: the use of combined chemoradiotherapy declined significantly (29.69%–15.11%, p = 0.004), chemotherapy‐alone regimens slightly increased (25.00%–31.66%), and the use of radiotherapy alone remained relatively stable. Second, overall survival has significantly improved from the 2000–2009 era to the 2010–2021 era (HR 0.46, 95% CI: 0.28–0.74, p = 0.001), with the most pronounced improvement observed in patients aged 60 years and older. Third, surgery was independently associated with better OS in multivariable analysis (HR 0.47, 95% CI: 0.31–0.72, p < 0.001). Fourth, MALT lymphoma, which demonstrated the most favorable prognosis among the histologic subtypes, showed no significant survival differences across treatment modalities (p = 0.838). The improvement in survival over time may reflect a combination of factors, including advances in diagnostic imaging, improvements in supportive care, refinements in patient selection for treatment, and the broader availability of effective therapeutic options. However, specific modern agents such as BTK inhibitors, immune checkpoint inhibitors, and CAR‐T cell therapy are not captured in the SEER treatment variables, and therefore the observed survival improvement cannot be attributed to any particular drug or drug class. Future studies incorporating detailed treatment data are needed to delineate the relative contributions of specific therapeutic advances. The lack of significant improvement in the younger population may reflect the already favorable baseline prognosis in this group, limiting the statistical power to detect further improvement. The significant survival gain in patients aged 60 years and older may be attributable to a combination of less toxic treatment options, improved supportive care, and changing histology patterns over time.

There was no significant change over time in the proportion of patients treated with radiotherapy alone, and RT was not shown to prolong survival for univariate and multivariate Cox regression. Consolidating whole‐brain radiotherapy (WBRT) was recommended for patients who are clinically sound but respond well to HD‐MTX‐based induction regimens. Nevertheless, potentially serious side effects, such as delayed neurocognitive decline, raise doubts about WBRT use, especially among individuals over 60 years [12, 13]. Current research has shown that treatment‐related toxicity can be lessened by lowering the dosage of RT or avoiding RT entirely [7, 14]. A recent study has reported long‐term survival outcomes in two patients with low‐grade primary parenchymal PCNSL who underwent localized radiation treatment [10], enabling a favorable outcome while avoiding long‐term treatment toxicity. Research by Chihara et al. [15] has indicated that RT or resection alone, without further treatment, resulted in similar survival rates for patients with indolent CNS lymphomas such as FL, MZL, and SLL. Among 23 patients with primary dural CNS‐MZL, a retrospective study showed that PFS of 89% and 76% at 3 and 5 years, respectively, after radiotherapy alone or surgical resection, and all patients survived to the last follow‐up [16]. Surgery plus radiotherapy for parenchymal low‐grade PCNSL provides long‐term disease control and appears to be well tolerated. Further large‐scale randomized controlled trials are needed to investigate the role of radiotherapy in PCNSL.

In our study, patients with PCNSL‐LG who underwent surgery had better outcomes compared with those who did not, and this association remained significant in multivariable analysis (HR 0.47, 95% CI: 0.31–0.72). However, this finding should be interpreted with caution. Patients selected for surgery may have had more surgically accessible lesions, better performance status, lower disease burden, or more indolent tumor biology—factors that are not captured in the SEER database. In addition, we were unable to distinguish biopsy from definitive resection for CNS tumors due to the limited information available in the SEER database, and this constitutes an important limitation. The timing of surgery relative to systemic therapy or radiation is also not available in SEER. In recent years, the advent of new technologies and methodologies, particularly the enhanced utilization of imaging techniques like MRI and tumor visualization, has led to marked improvements in both the efficacy and patient tolerance of surgical resection [17]. Deng et al. reported that surgical resection was notably associated with better OS in a cohort of 3543 PCNSL patients in the SEER database, reinforced by coarsened exact matching (CEM) analysis [18]. Another study, focusing on retrospective data from the National Cancer Database‐Participant User File (NCDN), SEER, and an institutional series (IS), revealed that survival rates for PCNSL were higher with craniotomy than with biopsy [17]. Although systemic treatment generally provides remission in most cases of lymphoma, there is evidence that cytoreductive surgery may be helpful in the management of non‐CNS lymphomas. For patients diagnosed with primary intestinal non‐Hodgkin lymphomas, surgical intervention is linked to improved prognostic outcomes [19, 20]. For PCNSL, tumor shrinkage helps reduce intracranial pressure in patients with larger lesions, rapidly improve neurological symptoms, and enhance their tolerance to potential subsequent chemotherapy or RT, thus offering a timeframe for treatment efficacy. Advances in neurosurgical methods have increased the safety of the procedure, possibly contributing to better outcomes for PCNSL patients. Additionally, and more importantly, low‐grade PCNSL, akin to extracranial lymphomas, may imply less aggressive disease than high‐grade PCNSL, which may require more local interventions such as focused RT or surgery, potentially minimizing the risks of late neurotoxicity linked to higher‐intensity therapies. Nevertheless, large‐scale, prospective clinical trials must be conducted to assess the exact role of surgical resection.

This study has several notable strengths. To our knowledge, it is the first large‐scale, population‐based analysis of PCNSL‐LG, a very rare disease for which prospective clinical trials are unlikely to be feasible. The use of the SEER database provides a sample size (n = 267) that substantially exceeds any single‐institution series reported to date. The two‐decade study period (2000–2009, 2010–2021) allows for the evaluation of long‐term survival trends and evolving treatment patterns. The comprehensive analysis of treatment modalities, stratified by both era and age group, provides clinically relevant insights into the real‐world management of this rare entity. Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, the SEER database does not capture specific chemotherapy regimens, drug dosages, number of treatment cycles, or the use of autologous stem cell transplantation (ASCT). Second, the SEER database surgical coding variable “Reason no cancer‐directed surgery” cannot reliably differentiate excisional biopsy from curative surgical resection. Accordingly, the observed survival benefit associated with surgery may be partly driven by misclassification between patients receiving biopsy alone and those undergoing definitive resection. Third, the potential for treatment selection bias cannot be fully addressed in this observational study. Fourth, SEER lacks data on performance status, comorbidities, cerebrospinal fluid findings, molecular markers, and treatment‐related toxicities. Finally, our subgroup analyses, especially the MALT lymphoma‐specific analysis, are limited by small sample sizes and should be considered hypothesis‐generating.

In conclusion, this population‐based analysis of 267 patients with PCNSL‐LG demonstrated that treatment patterns have evolved over two decades, with a decline in combined chemoradiotherapy and an increase in chemotherapy‐alone approaches. Overall survival has significantly improved over time, particularly among patients aged 60 years and older. Surgery was associated with better outcomes in multivariable analysis, though this finding should be interpreted cautiously given potential selection bias and the inability of SEER to distinguish biopsy from resection. Among histologic subtypes, MALT lymphoma demonstrates the most favorable outcomes, with survival appearing independent of treatment modality. These findings, while subject to the inherent limitations of registry‐based research, provide clinically relevant information for the management of this rare disease and highlight important directions for future investigation.
