 Summary of Research Results:

 Task 1: Dataset collection

 NLR will isolate a small group of utility-scale systems that is both in NLR’s PV performance
 database and has been scanned via Dronebase. We expect this will be approximately 5-7 systems
 total. Drone base to provide site drawings for the associated systems and the associated Infrared
 (IR) imagery which has been captured as part of their aerial overflight campaign.

 Task 1 Results:

 For the first phase of this research, we isolated a list of systems in the NLR PV Fleets Initiative
 [1] which also had previous aerial Infrared (IR) scans provided by Zeitview. We then worked
 with our PV Fleets partners to obtain site diagrams for these sites. In total, we collected site
 diagram data, time series data, and aerial IR scan data for 12 utility and commercial sites, located
 in Colorado and California. A map of sites is shown in Figure 1. A full summary of the data set
    can be found in the following report: 10.1109/PVSC57443.2024.10749419.

 Figure 1. Map of sites analyzed in this research. Darker dots represent geolocated systems [2].

 Task 2: Dataset collation and matching

 NLR will map the site drawings to the associated inverter data streams in NLR’s internal time
 series database. Once this is done, map this data to the associated site blocks in the aerial thermal
 imagery. By doing this, there will be a direct connection between the time series data and the
 aerial IR imagery.

 Task 2 Results:

 Converting site diagrams, which were generally provided in PDF format, into machine-readable
 format where equipment can be easily geolocated, was particularly challenging. Geolocating
 equipment was imperative so aerial IR defects can be mapped to the correct Supervisory Control
 and Data Acquisition (SCADA) data streams.

 In [2], we outlined a process for automating inverter block geolocation, based on northing-
 easting coordinates provided in owner site diagrams. This process converted tables of northing-
 easting coordinates that identified the borders of unique inverter blocks into GeoJSON format.
 First, tables were converted from PDF to CSV format using optical character recognition (OCR)
 techniques. Coordinates were then reprojected to WGS84 format and converted to GeoJSON
 format. Figure 2, provided via [2], illustrates the beginning and end stages of this process.

 Figure 2. Initial site diagram table of northing-easting coordinates converted to final GeoJSON
                                                    format [2].

 The overall process for fusing all different data sources to allow for direct performance-aerial
 defect analysis is illustrated in Figure 3 [2]. This process entailed converting site diagrams to
 GeoJSON format, which identified the equipment associated geospatially with aerial defects.
 Simultaneously, time series data was mapped to specific equipment. Data was then fused during
 final analysis, allowing for a direct comparison between time series data streams and aerial IR
 defects. This process was performed on sites identified in Task 1.

 Figure 3. Diagram illustrating the data fusion process, provided in [2].

 Task 3: Data quality checks

 NLR to ensure that the Dronebase metadata lines up with the NLR’s internal time-series database
 system metadata, in terms of latitude-longitude coordinates and DC capacity. This is to ensure
 that there are no mismatched systems. NLR to compare tilt/azimuth detection between NLR’s
 data-driven approach and Dronebase’s assessment (if available) or based on Dronebase aerial
 imagery.

 Task 3 Results:

 Data quality checks were performed at the beginning of the project, when sites were being
 selected for the initial Photovoltaic Specialist Conference (PVSC) paper. Zeitview provided a list
 of scanned PV sites and their associated metadata (system capacity, mounting configuration,
 technology type), which was compared to PV Fleets metadata by NLR analysts. PV Fleets site
 information was not directly shared with Zeitview, so no Nondisclosure Agreements (NDAs)
 were violated.

 All data channels, including latitude, longitude, tilt and azimuth, were found to match properly
 between the NLR-housed data provided to us by Zeitview.

 Task 4: Phase 1 Analysis

 NLR will correlate time series measurements to aerial inspection dates, by taking a brief time
 series snapshot around the date where the images were taken. Look at the data sets on a block-
 by-block basis to be able to map outages and issues found in the imagery to inverter data
 streams. Derive relationships between the data streams and the aerial imagery. The following
 topics will be investigated:

 • Stuck tracker identification
 • String outage identification
 • Shading / snow detection
 • Possible other issues (soiling, diode failures)

 Task 4 Results:

 In the PVSC publication [2], we provided a thorough analysis comparing PV performance of
 inverter blocks against their associated site defects. This analysis included data from the twelve
 sites identified in Task 1.

 An example system time series, with its associated aerial scan date in red, is shown in Figure 4
 below [2]. This time series had 640 module-level defects that were flagged for offline strings.
 The scan date showed low alternating current (AC) power output for this inverter on the scan
 date (April 6), which aligned with the string offline defect.

 Figure 4: Normalized AC power production for a system block flagged for string offline issues,
 with the aerial IR scan date highlighted in red [2].

 5

 Figures 5 and 6 below illustrate overall offline string and stuck tracker defect percentage trends,
 with inverter blocks’ associated time series manually classified as producing, curtailed, or
 offline. These graphics were taken from the 2024 PVSC analysis publication [2].

 In cases where over 80% of inverter block modules were flagged with a string-offline defect, the
 inverter block power was generally 0. However, when only a small fraction of module defects
 was flagged in an inverter block (<10%), there was still a producing power signal.

 This overall trend also held for stuck tracker defects (see Figure 6). The process for determining
 stuck tracker defects was confirmed directly with Zeitview; specifically, if modules were flagged
 as offline during aerial defect analysis but also were misaligned, they were flagged with both
 offline and stuck tracker defects, so there was a heavy overlap between the two labels.

 Figure 5. Box plot of percentage module defects by inverter block by outage category, for inverter
 blocks flagged for offline strings.

 Figure 6. Box plot of percentage module defects by inverter block by outage category, for inverter
 blocks flagged for misaligned modules/stuck trackers.

 Overall, these results confirm the validity of aerial infrared analysis when compared directly
 against associated time series data, as both data sources show good alignment.

 Task 5: Phase 2 Analysis

 Using time-dependent aerial imagery, NLR can extend the analysis to comparing site
 degradation and/or repeating outages and other issues to aerial inspection imagery. This means
 repeating the previous steps for multiple time intervals, where each time interval represents a
 point where an aerial image was taken. The following topics will be investigated:

 • Using time-dependent inspections, can we establish a relationship between site/system
 • degradation and aerial inspection imagery?
 • Tracking hot spots and module issues against technology type. Can we determine if
 certain module types accelerate in defects over time, based on aerial imagery?
 • Comparing mounting types: does mount type related to failures over time?
 • Potentially correlating extreme weather events to aerial imagery over time

 Task 5 Results:

 For this phase of research, we identified an additional seven sites with time-dependent aerial IR
 imagery and obtained their associated site diagrams from associated PV Fleets partners. Only a
 preliminary analysis for this task was performed as the CRADA ended early.

 Figure 7 below shows a series of subsequent aerial IR scans taken of the same inverter block
 over a period of approximately 3 years. This illustration clearly shows a persistent string outage
 over the first two scans (11/24/2020 – 8/9/2021), which was then resolved by the third scan
 (7/12/2022). The authors noticed that in many cases, defects at the same module location were
 persistent, taking multiple scans to resolve. This trend is illustrated in Figure 8 below. The most
 egregious case was the 8/9/2021 scan, where 83.3% of the defects flagged were present at the
 exact same module location in the previous scan. Recurring defects included string outages,
 suspected shorted diodes, and sub-module anomaly defects.

 Figure 7. Aerial IR scans taken of a system over time. A persistent string outage was present over
 the first two scans.

 Figure 8. Repeat module defects flagged over time for system 9055 (taken on the same modules).

 For this inverter block, degradation was recalculated at each scan date, using the strategy
 outlined in Figure 9. All AC power time series data associated with the block up to each scan
 date was run through RdTools degradation analysis to calculate degradation. These normalized
 degradation results are shown in Figure 10 below. During the first two scans, the inverter block
 displayed rapid degradation, then degradation recovered and stabilized.

 It is possible that poor performance may have been due to persistent outages. However, it was
 difficult to directly tie degradation to any specific long-term module defects, as they were not
 particularly prevalent. In our PVSC publication, we were unable to find a strong relationship
 between degradation and long-term module defects (such as hotspots, suspected Potential −
 Induced Degradation (PID), etc.). This was likely because long-term and Balance-of-System
 effects were not common. During our analysis, we found that long-term defects generally
 affected less than 1% of modules total in our analysis. Table 11 provides a breakdown of
 percentage modules found with associated defect type from our 2024 analysis. Offline strings
 and misaligned modules were the most commonly occurring at 4% and 5.6% respectively, but all
 other defects were present in less than 1% of modules scanned.
