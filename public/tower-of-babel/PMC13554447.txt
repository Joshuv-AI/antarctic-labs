A combined extract of Salvia miltiorrhiza and Panax notoginseng enhances collateral flow and protects the neurovascular unit after ischemic stroke
Authors: Tae Woo Kwon, Minho Jung, Won Myoung Lee, Da Yeon Park, Han-Gyul Lee, Seungwon Kwon, Sanghyun Lee, Sang-Kwan Moon, Ik-Hyun Cho
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIschemic stroke remains a major cause of mortality and disability, with few therapeutic options outside the narrow time window for reperfusion. Collateral circulation critically sustains penumbral viability, yet no pharmacological agent is approved to enhance collateral flow. Salvia (S.) miltiorrhiza and Panax (P.) notoginseng possess complementary vascular, anti-inflammatory, and neuroprotective actions, suggesting potential synergy in acute ischemia.MethodsA combined extract of S. miltiorrhiza and P. notoginseng (SM/PN) was tested in a permanent middle cerebral artery occlusion (pMCAO) mouse model and in vitro systems. Endpoints included infarct volume, anterior cerebral artery (ACA) perfusion, vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) expression, microglial activation, inflammatory mediators, and endothelial barrier integrity.ResultsPre-ischemic SM/PN (15–60 mg/kg) significantly reduced cortical infarct volume and sustained ACA perfusion during the first 15 min after occlusion without increasing VEGF in brain or endothelial cells. SM/PN decreased Iba-1 immunoreactivity in peri-infarct regions and suppressed inducible nitric oxide synthase, interleukin-6, and tumor necrosis factor-α in lipopolysaccharide-stimulated BV2 microglia. It also reduced endothelial PECAM-1 upregulation while restoring occludin and partially recovering claudin-5, accompanied by reduced phosphorylation of STAT3 and p38.ConclusionsSM/PN provides early neurovascular protection through non-angiogenic collateral enhancement, microglial modulation, and blood–brain barrier stabilization. This integrated mechanism distinguishes SM/PN from conventional pro-collateral or angiogenic strategies and supports its potential as an adjunctive therapy for acute ischemic stroke, especially for patients who cannot undergo reperfusion.Keywords: Salvia miltiorrhiza, Panax notoginseng, schemic stroke, Collateral circulation, Neuroinflammation, Blood–brain barrier integrity

Introduction

Stroke remains a major cause of mortality and long-term disability worldwide, with ischemic stroke accounting for the vast majority of cases [1]. The abrupt occlusion of a major cerebral artery deprives downstream brain tissue of oxygen and glucose, initiating a complex ischemic cascade that includes excitotoxicity, oxidative stress, neuroinflammation, and disruption of the blood–brain barrier (BBB), ultimately leading to irreversible neuronal loss [2,3]. While reperfusion therapies—most notably tissue plasminogen activator (tPA) thrombolysis and mechanical thrombectomy—can dramatically improve clinical outcomes when implemented in the hyperacute phase, their use is constrained by a narrow therapeutic window, strict eligibility criteria, and the risk of hemorrhagic transformation [[4], [5], [6]]. Consequently, only a minority of patients receive these treatments, and many survivors continue to experience persistent neurological impairments [7]. This clinical reality underscores the urgent need for adjunctive therapeutic strategies capable of conferring neurovascular protection beyond the time limits of reperfusion.

Among the endogenous mechanisms influencing ischemic outcome, collateral circulation—a network of pre-existing arterial anastomoses—plays a pivotal role in sustaining cerebral blood flow (CBF) when primary arterial conduits are blocked [8]. Robust collateral flow has been consistently linked to smaller infarct volumes, improved functional recovery, reduced mortality, and an extended therapeutic window for reperfusion interventions [[8], [9], [10]]. Collateral vessels redistribute blood from unaffected vascular territories to ischemic regions, thereby maintaining oxygen and nutrient delivery to the penumbra and delaying the transition to irreversible injury [[8], [9], [10]]. Conversely, poor collateral status is associated with rapid infarct progression and worse outcomes, and can be negatively influenced by factors such as advanced age, hypertension, diabetes, and endothelial dysfunction [3,8]. Given these strong prognostic implications, strategies to preserve or enhance collateral flow during acute ischemia have garnered significant interest [11].

Despite this clinical relevance, no pharmacological agent has yet been approved to augment collateral circulation in stroke patients [[8], [9], [10]]. Existing approaches to collateral augmentation, such as induced hypertension or hemodilution, are limited by modest efficacy and potential adverse effects [12,13]. Mechanical methods, including external counter pulsation and partial aortic occlusion, remain largely investigational and are not widely accessible [11]. Pharmacological enhancement of collateral flow therefore represents a promising and complementary avenue to current reperfusion strategies [11], but translating this concept into clinical practice has been hindered by the lack of safe, multi-target agents capable of rapid onset without compromising vascular stability.

Natural products with multimodal vascular and neuroprotective actions offer a potential solution to this therapeutic gap [14]. Salvia (S.) miltiorrhiza Bunge (Danshen) contains bioactive phenolic acids (e.g., salvianolic acid B) and diterpene quinones (e.g., tanshinone IIA) that promote vasodilation, suppress oxidative injury, and attenuate inflammatory responses, collectively improving microvascular perfusion and protecting against ischemic damage [[15], [16], [17]]. Panax (P.) notoginseng (Sanqi), rich in ginsenosides, exhibits antithrombotic, neuroprotective, and blood-brain barrier (BBB)-preserving effects, and has been shown to modulate endothelial function and reduce post-ischemic edema [[18], [19], [20]]. While each herb independently confers cerebrovascular benefits, combining them may provide synergistic effects by simultaneously targeting multiple pathological pathways—enhancing collateral circulation, suppressing neuroinflammation, and reinforcing the structural and functional integrity of the neurovascular unit.

Notably, many pro-collateral strategies rely on angiogenesis-driven remodeling, often accompanied by vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) upregulation, which in the acute phase may increase the risk of BBB leakage and edema [21,22]. Agents capable of improving collateral perfusion without strongly inducing VEGF could therefore offer early-phase benefits with a potentially lower risk of exacerbating vascular permeability.

In the present study, we investigated the neurovascular protective potential of a combined extract of S. miltiorrhiza and P. notoginseng (SM/PN). We hypothesized that SM/PN would enhance collateral circulation, thereby improving early cerebral perfusion, attenuating inflammatory signaling, and preserving BBB integrity in acute ischemic stroke. To test this hypothesis, we evaluated SM/PN in a permanent middle cerebral artery occlusion (pMCAO) mouse model and complementary in vitro systems, assessing its effects on infarct volume, collateral-flow dynamics, inflammatory mediator expression, and endothelial barrier stability.

Material and methods

Preparation of SM/PN

SM/PN is an 80% ethanol (EtOH) extract composed of S. miltiorrhiza root (Danshen) and P. notoginseng root (Sanqi) mixed at a fixed ratio of 17.5:3.4 (w/w). A total of 315 g of the mixed herbal materials was extracted twice with 2 L of 80% EtOH under reflux for 2 h each time, as previously described [23,24]. The combined extracts were filtered, concentrated under reduced pressure using a rotary vacuum evaporator, and lyophilized with a freeze dryer, yielding 33.3% (w/w) dried extract. Each dried herbal medicine was purchased from Kyung Hee Herb Pharm (Wonju-si, Gangwon-do, Republic of Korea), and its authenticity was verified by Prof. Sang Kwan Moon of Kyung Hee University Korean Medicine Hospital. Voucher specimens were deposited in the Herbarium of the same institution.

Marker compound analysis of SM/PN by HPLC/ELSD

High-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) analysis was performed on a Waters HPLC system equipped with a 1525 Binary HPLC pump and a 2424 evaporative light scattering detector (ELSD) (Waters, Milford, MA, USA). HPLC-grade acetonitrile, trifluoroacetic acid, formic acid, and water were obtained from Scharlau (Barcelona, Spain) and Honeywell (Burdick & Jackson, Muskegon, MI, USA). Salvianolic acid B and ginsenoside Rg1 were supplied by the Natural Product Institute of Science and Technology, Anseong, Korea. For analysis, sample aliquots were dissolved in 80% EtOH to a concentration of 2 mg/mL and subsequently clarified through a 0.45 μm polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) syringe filter. A series of working standard solutions were prepared by the systematic dilution of a stock solution (also in 80% MeOH) to establish calibration curves. A YMC-Pack Pro C18 column (4.6 × 250 mm, 5 μm) was used for the analysis at a column temperature of 35°C. Detection was performed at evaporative light scattering detector with an injection volume of 10 μL. The mobile phase, comprising 0.1% formic acid in water (A) and acetonitrile (B), was pumped at 1.0 mL/min. The elution gradient was programmed as follows: an initial isocratic step at 7% B for 10 min, followed by a linear increase to 60% B over the next 40 min. The concentration of B was then rapidly increased to 100% within 5 min and held for 5 min. Finally, the column was re-equilibrated at the initial 7% B for 10 min prior to the subsequent run.

Animals and housing conditions

Male ICR mice, 7–8 weeks of age and weighing 29–30 g (Samtako BIO KOREA Co., Ltd., Osan, Republic of Korea), were maintained in standard polycarbonate cages, with five animals housed per cage, under controlled environmental conditions: a 12-h light/dark cycle (lights on at 07:00), ambient temperature of 23 ± 3°C, and relative humidity of 55 ± 5%. Standard laboratory chow and water were provided ad libitum. Prior to experimentation, all animals were acclimated to these housing conditions for a minimum of one week. Animal allocation and data collection were performed in a randomized, blinded manner, following the National Institutes of Health guidelines to ensure rigor in preclinical studies involving neurological disease models [25].

Ethics statement

All experimental procedures involving animals were performed in accordance with the ethical guidelines for the care and use of laboratory animals and received prior approval from the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Kyung Hee University (KHUASP(SE)-23-488). Every effort was taken to use the minimum number of animals necessary and to alleviate any pain or distress during the experiments.

Administration of SM/PN

SM/PN or vehicle control was administered as a single oral gavage 10 min before surgery. SM/PN was provided at doses of 15, 30, or 60 mg/kg, determined from the human equivalent dose and adjusted for mice using the standard body surface area conversion formula. Mice in the vehicle group received an equal volume of sterile distilled water.

Permanent middle cerebral artery occlusion (pMCAO) surgery

pMCAO model was induced following a modified version of a previously reported method [26]. Male ICR mice (29–30 g) were anesthetized with 2% isoflurane in a gas mixture of 70% N2O and 30% O2, and anesthesia depth was confirmed by the absence of a toe-pinch reflex. The animals were then positioned in a stereotaxic apparatus, after which a midline scalp incision was made between the left eye and ear. The temporalis muscle was gently retracted to expose the temporal bone, and a 2.0 mm burr hole was drilled over the distal segment of the left MCA using a high-speed microdrill under a surgical microscope. After removal of the dura, the main trunk of the distal MCA was coagulated using a bipolar coagulator and then severed with micro-scissors to achieve permanent occlusion. Animals that developed subarachnoid hemorrhage, failed to exhibit neurological deficits (e.g., ipsilateral circling), or died during the procedure were excluded from the study. Throughout the surgery, body temperature was maintained between 36.5°C and 37.5°C using a thermostatically regulated heating pad (Harvard Apparatus, Holliston, MA, USA), and a core temperature below 36°C was not permitted.

Measurement of collateral blood flow by laser Doppler flowmetry

Collateral blood flow was measured by laser Doppler flowmetry as previously described [27]. To assess regional cerebral blood flow (rCBF) at the distal border of the infarct before and after pMCAO, rCBF in the anterior cerebral artery (ACA) territory was recorded. ACA blood flow was monitored as an index of leptomeningeal collateral circulation. Before surgery, a laser Doppler flow probe (moorVMS-LDF2, Moor Instruments Ltd., UK) was affixed to the skull over the distal portion of the ischemic region (1.5 mm lateral to the midline and 1 mm caudal to bregma) using instant adhesive. rCBF was continuously monitored with moorVMS-PC software (Moor Instruments Ltd., UK) from the pre-occlusion baseline period until 15 min after occlusion of the distal MCA. For analysis, ACA blood flow during the stable baseline period before MCA occlusion was defined as 100%, and subsequent rCBF values were expressed as percentages of baseline at immediately after occlusion and at 5, 10, and 15 min post-occlusion.

Tissue preparation for histological analysis

Tissue preparation was conducted according to established protocols [11,28] for histological analysis following focal cerebral ischemia. In brief, 24 h after pMCAO, mice were deeply anesthetized and perfused transcardially with 0.1 M phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), followed by 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA). The brains were post-fixed in 4% PFA at 4°C for 24 h and then cryoprotected in 30% sucrose solution for a minimum of 3 days. Coronal sections (30 μm thick) encompassing the infarct core were prepared using a cryostat (CM3050S, Leica Biosystems, Germany) and stored in PBS until further processing.

Immunohistochemistry

Immunohistochemical staining was carried out following established protocols with minor modifications [29,30]. In brief, free-floating coronal brain sections were treated with 3% hydrogen peroxide for 30 min to block endogenous peroxidase activity. Sections were then rinsed three times in PBS for 10 min each, followed by blocking in PBS containing 5% normal goat serum, 2% bovine serum albumin, 2% fetal bovine serum, and 0.1% Triton X-100 for 1 h at room temperature. Subsequently, tissues were incubated for ≥18 h at room temperature with the following primary antibodies: mouse anti-platelet and endothelial cell adhesion molecule-1 (PECAM-1)/CD31 (1:500; Santa Cruz Biotechnology, USA) and rabbit anti-ionized calcium-binding adaptor molecule 1 (Iba-1) (1:2000; WAKO, Japan). After thorough washing, sections were incubated sequentially with biotinylated secondary antibodies (rabbit anti-mouse or goat anti-rabbit IgG, 1:250; Vector Laboratories, USA) and avidin–biotin complex (ABC Kit; Vector Laboratories, USA) for 1 h each at room temperature. Immunoreactivity was visualized using 3,3′-diaminobenzidine in Tris buffer (pH 7.4) for 3–5 min. Stained sections were mounted onto gelatin-coated slides, air-dried, dehydrated through graded EtOH, cleared in xylene, and cover-slipped. The immunostained specimens were examined under a light microscope.

Quantification of immunohistochemical staining

Quantification of Iba-1 immunoreactivity was conducted following previously described protocols [31,32]. In brief, Iba-1–positive signals were imaged using a DP70 digital camera system (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan) at three predefined sites: the infarct border, the ipsilateral proximal region adjacent to the infarct, and the ipsilateral distal region remote from the infarct.

Image analysis was performed using NIH ImageJ software (http://rsbweb.nih.gov/ij/) to determine the optical density of Iba-1–positive areas. The resulting relative density values were used as an index of microglial activation.

BV2 cell culture

BV2 cells, a murine microglial cell line, were kindly provided by professor Hyunsu Bae at the College of Korean Medicine, Kyung Hee University, and maintained following previously established methods [33,34]. Briefly, cells were cultured in 100-mm dishes containing Dulbecco's Modified Eagle's Medium (DMEM; Gibco, Grand Island, NY, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin (P/S; Gibco). Cultures were kept at 37°C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2, and the medium was renewed every 3 days.

bEnd.3 cell culture and SM/PN treatment

All experiments were conducted in accordance with previously established methods, with minor modifications [35,36]. In brief, bEnd.3 cells (ATCC® CRL-2299™), a murine brain microvascular endothelial cell line, were cultured in DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin–streptomycin (Gibco, USA) at 37°C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2. Once stabilized, cells were seeded into 6-well plates at a density of 3 × 105 cells/well and allowed to adhere for 24 h.

To induce BBB disruption, lipopolysaccharide (LPS, 1 μg/mL) was applied for 4 h. SM/PN was administered at concentrations of 1, 10, or 100 μg/mL, 1 h prior to LPS treatment. All procedures were carried out in a biosafety level 2 facility (LML 18-84, Kyung Hee University).

MTT assay

The cytotoxicity of SM/PN in BV2 microglial cells was evaluated using the MTT [3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)-2,5-diphenyltetrazolium bromide] assay, following previously described protocols [37]. In brief, BV2 cells were seeded into 96-well plates and exposed to SM/PN at various concentrations (0.001–400 μg/mL). After 24 h of incubation, MTT solution (10 μL per 100 μL of medium) was added to each well and incubated for an additional 4 h at 37°C. The supernatant was then discarded, and 100 μL of dimethyl sulfoxide was added to dissolve the resulting formazan crystals. Absorbance was measured at 595 nm using a microplate spectrophotometer.

Western blotting

Protein expression in BV2 cells was analyzed by Western blotting, according to standard procedures with minor modifications [33,34]. In brief, following treatment with SM/PN (at designated concentrations) for 24 h, cells were lysed in RIPA buffer supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors. Protein concentrations were determined using the bicinchoninic acid assay, and equal amounts of protein were resolved by SDS–PAGE and transferred to PVDF membranes. Membranes were blocked with 5% skim milk in tris-buffered saline with tween-20 detergent and incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies against the target proteins (e.g., VEGF, iNOS, IL-6, and β-actin). After washing, membranes were incubated with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. Immunoreactive bands were visualized using enhanced chemiluminescence and captured with a ChemiDoc imaging system. Band intensities were quantified with ImageJ software and normalized to β-actin.

Statistical analysis

All data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism (version X; GraphPad Software Inc., San Diego, CA, United States). One-way ANOVA followed by Tukey's post hoc test was used for comparisons among three or more independent groups. For comparisons between two groups, an unpaired two tailed Student's t-test was applied. For rCBF measurements obtained by laser Doppler flowmetry, two-way repeated-measures ANOVA followed by Bonferroni's multiplecomparisons test was used to evaluate the effects of treatment and time. A p-value each experiment is specified in the figure legends. Outliers were not excluded unless predetermined exclusion criteria were met, such as subarachnoid hemorrhage or failure to induce infarction in pMCAO models.

Results

Marker compounds in the SM/PN identified by HPLC/ELSD analysis

To ensure the chemical consistency of the SM/PN, the major marker compounds were analyzed using HPLC coupled with an evaporative light scattering detector (ELSD). The chromatographic profile revealed two representative constituents: ginsenoside Rg1 and salvianolic acid B. Quantitative analysis showed that the SM/PN contained ginsenoside Rg1 (18.61 mg/g extract; retention time 26.2 min) and salvianolic acid B (71.14 mg/g extract; retention time 30.8 min) (Fig. 1). These results confirm the presence of characteristic constituents derived from P. ginseng and S. miltiorrhiza in the SM/PN.

Phytochemical profiles of SM/PN by HPLC/ELSD analysis.

Peaks corresponding to major marker compounds—including ginsenoside Rg1 from P. notoginseng, and salvianolic acid B from S. miltiorrhiza—are indicated.

SM/PN reduces cortical infarct volume following permanent MCAO

To evaluate the neuroprotective effect of SM/PN in acute ischemia, mice received SM/PN (15, 30, or 60 mg/kg, p.o.) prior to pMCAO. At 24 h after surgery, 2,3,5-triphenyltetrazolium chloride (TTC) staining revealed well-demarcated pale infarcts in the ipsilateral cortex of vehicle-treated mice (Fig. 2A). In contrast, SM/PN treatment produced a dose-dependent reduction in cortical infarct size. Quantitative analysis confirmed a significant decrease in infarct volume in SM/PN-treated mice compared with vehicle controls (Fig. 2B). These results demonstrate that SM/PN confers robust protection against permanent ischemic injury in the mouse cortex.

SM/PN reduces cortical infarct volume in a mouse model of permanent ischemia.

(A) Representative TTC-stained coronal brain sections obtained 24 h after pMCAO in vehicle- and SM/PN-treated mice (15, 30, or 60 mg/kg, p.o.). Pale areas indicate infarcted cortex. (B) Quantification of infarct volume expressed as a percentage of the ipsilateral cortex. SM/PN significantly reduced infarct volume compared with vehicle-treated mice. Data are presented as mean ± SEM (n = 8 mice per group). Statistical significance was analyzed using one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey's post hoc test. ∗∗P < 0.01 vs. vehicle.

SM/PN increases anterior cerebral artery (ASA) perfusion following pMCAO

To determine whether the reduction in infarct volume was associated with improved collateral circulation, rCBF within the ACA territory was measured using laser Doppler flowmetry. In vehicle-treated mice, ACA perfusion transiently increased immediately after occlusion but gradually declined thereafter. In contrast, SM/PN-treated mice exhibited a sustained elevation of rCBF between 5 and 15 min after pMCAO (Fig. 3A–C). Quantitative analysis confirmed significantly greater perfusion in the ACA territory in SM/PN-treated groups compared with vehicle controls during the early post-occlusion period (Fig. 3B and C). Representative rCBF trace recordings are shown in Supplementary Fig. S1. These findings indicate that SM/PN promotes early redistribution of cerebral blood flow through leptomeningeal collateral circulation, thereby contributing to reduced infarct development.

SM/PN does not augment VEGF expression in ischemic cortex or endothelial cells

To determine whether the perfusion-enhancing effect of SM/PN involves VEGF-mediated angiogenic signaling, VEGF expression was examined 24 h after pMCAO. Immunofluorescence analysis revealed increased VEGF immunoreactivity in the peri-infarct cortex of vehicle-treated mice compared with sham controls; however, SM/PN treatment (15–60 mg/kg) did not significantly alter VEGF levels (Fig. 4A–C). In LPS-stimulated bEnd.3 endothelial cells, LPS induced a modest increase in VEGF expression. Treatment with SM/PN (1–100 μg/mL) produced a moderate increase in VEGF protein levels, as detected by Western blot and densitometric analysis (Fig. 4D and E). Despite this in vitro effect, SM/PN did not enhance VEGF expression in the ischemic brain at 24 h after pMCAO. Together, these findings suggest that the early perfusion benefit of SM/PN is unlikely to be primarily mediated by VEGF-dependent angiogenic signaling at this early time point.

SM/PN attenuates neuroinflammation by limiting microglial activation and pro-inflammatory mediator production

Microglial activation in the peri-infarct cortex was assessed by Iba-1 immunostaining. Vehicle-treated mice exhibited marked increases in Iba-1 immunoreactivity across the infarct border, proximal regions (Fig. 5A and B). SM/PN treatment significantly reduced Iba-1 intensity in the proximal and distal region, whereas no significant change was observed at the immediate infarct border and distal region (Fig. 5C–E). To examine the anti-inflammatory effects of SM/PN in vitro, BV2 microglial cells were stimulated with LPS. MTT assays demonstrated that SM/PN did not significantly affect BV2 cell viability at concentrations up to 100 μg/mL, whereas higher concentrations (>200 μg/mL) reduced viability (Fig. 5F). Therefore, concentrations within the non-cytotoxic range (1–100 μg/mL) were used for subsequent in vitro experiments. Under inflammatory conditions, LPS stimulation markedly increased expression of the pro-inflammatory mediators iNOS, IL-6, and TNF-α, whereas SM/PN treatment dose-dependently suppressed these proteins (Fig. 5G). Quantitative analysis confirmed significant reductions in iNOS, IL-6, and TNF-α levels following SM/PN treatment (Fig. 5H–J). Together, these results demonstrate that SM/PN attenuates ischemia-associated neuroinflammation by suppressing microglial activation and reducing the production of inflammatory enzymes and cytokines.

SM/PN preserves endothelial barrier integrity by stabilizing tight junctions and modulating inflammatory signaling

To determine whether SM/PN protects against endothelial dysfunction following ischemia, PECAM-1 immunoreactivity was examined in the peri-infarct cortex. Vehicle-treated mice showed marked upregulation of PECAM-1, consistent with endothelial activation and BBB disruption. In contrast, SM/PN treatment dose-dependently reduced PECAM-1 expression (Fig. 6A and B). Consistent with the MTT assay results (Fig. 6C), SM/PN did not significantly affect endothelial cell viability within the concentration range used (1–100 μg/mL). In bEnd.3 endothelial cells, SM/PN (1–100 μg/mL) significantly attenuated LPS-induced PECAM-1 upregulation and restored the tight-junction protein claudin-5 and occludin expressions (Fig. 6E–G). These findings indicate that SM/PN helps stabilize endothelial barrier structure under inflammatory conditions. Mechanistically, SM/PN markedly suppressed LPS-induced phosphorylation of STAT3 and p38, while TLR4 and ERK phosphorylation remained largely unchanged (Fig. 6H–K). In addition, phosphorylation levels of Akt, JNK, and NF-κB were not significantly affected under the same experimental conditions (Supplementary Fig. S2). Taken together, these results suggest that SM/PN preserves BBB integrity by stabilizing tight-junction architecture and partly through modulation of STAT3–p38 signaling in endothelial cells.

Discussion

This study identifies the combined extract of Salvia miltiorrhiza and Panax notoginseng (SM/PN) as a multi-target neurovascular protective agent in acute ischemic stroke. In a pMCAO mouse model, SM/PN reduced cortical infarct volume, maintained early ACA perfusion, suppressed microglial activation, and preserved BBB integrity. Importantly, these effects occurred without upregulating VEGF, suggesting a non-angiogenic mechanism of collateral enhancement. Our integrated findings support a mechanistic cascade in which SM/PN stabilizes the neurovascular unit, and limits inflammation-driven secondary injury.

Mechanistic integration: from collateral flow to BBB protection

The sustained ACA-territory rCBF elevation following SM/PN suggests improved leptomeningeal collateral function—a determinant of penumbral survival and clinical outcome [8,10]. Collateral augmentation typically involves VEGF-driven angiogenesis [21,22,38], which in the acute phase may compromise BBB integrity and promote vasogenic edema [22,39]. Our data indicate that SM/PN enhances perfusion without triggering VEGF (Fig. 2 andFig. 3), implying that mechanisms such as endothelial nitric oxide modulation, vasodilation, and microvascular tone regulation may predominate. Instead, these findings suggest that SM/PN may primarily act by stabilizing pre-existing vascular structures and preserving endothelial barrier integrity under inflammatory conditions. Such a mechanism may be particularly relevant during the early stages of ischemic injury, when protection of the neurovascular unit and prevention of BBB disruption are critical determinants of tissue outcome. In this context, the vascular protection conferred by SM/PN may reflect functional stabilization of the neurovascular interface rather than induction of new vessel formation. Given the critical role of leptomeningeal collaterals in sustaining penumbral perfusion without requiring angiogenesis [8,40], non-angiogenic collateral flow offers substantial benefit in the hyperacute phase—where preserving blood–brain barrier integrity is essential to avoid vasogenic edema and hemorrhagic transformation [41,42].

BBB preservation emerged as a consistent outcome, with SM/PN reducing ischemia-induced PECAM-1, restoring occludin, and partially recovering claudin-5 (Fig. 6D–G). By preserving essential tight-junction proteins (e.g., occludin and claudin-5), SM/PN likely stabilizes the endothelial barrier, synergizing with enhanced collateral perfusion to reduce vasogenic edema and limit leukocyte infiltration—two pathophysiological consequences closely associated with early barrier disruption in ischemic stroke [[43], [44], [45]]. In parallel, SM/PN reduced the phosphorylation of STAT3 and p38, whereas TLR4 expression and ERK phosphorylation remained largely unchanged. These findings suggest that STAT3/p38 signaling may represent a downstream effector pathway contributing to the anti-inflammatory and barrier-protective actions of SM/PN. Such modulation may attenuate detrimental inflammatory cascades while preserving signaling processes that support post-ischemic repair and neurovascular recovery. Although additional signaling pathways such as Akt, JNK, and NF-κB were examined and were not significantly affected under the tested conditions (Supplementary Fig. S2), the involvement of other signaling cascades cannot be completely excluded. Therefore, STAT3/p38 signaling likely represents a key downstream effector pathway within a broader regulatory network modulated by SM/PN.

Anti-inflammatory modulation and microglial control

Microglia rapidly sense ischemic injury and drive secondary neurotoxicity via cytokine release, ROS production, and BBB disruption [2,3]. SM/PN's dose-dependent suppression of iNOS, IL-6, and TNF-α in BV2 cells, alongside reduced Iba-1 in peri-infarct cortex (Fig. 5), indicates potent anti-inflammatory activity. This aligns with reported effects of S. miltiorrhiza phenolic acids and P. notoginseng saponins in modulating glial activation and NF-κB/MAPK signaling [15,20], but here these activities converge with collateral enhancement and BBB protection—yielding an integrated neurovascular benefit.

Distinct mechanistic profile and therapeutic positioning of SM/PN

In contrast to high-dose VEGF–driven angiogenic stimulation, which has been reported to exacerbate vascular permeability and promote vasogenic edema during the hyperacute phase of ischemia [22,46,47], SM/PN sustains neurovascular benefits without triggering VEGF-mediated effects. This distinction is clinically significant, as acute-phase angiogenesis often compromises BBB integrity and worsens cerebral edema [41]. Notably, SM/PN's preservation of tight junction proteins—occludin and claudin-5—likely synergizes with its early enhancement of collateral flow to limit edema formation and inflammatory cell infiltration, consistent with contemporary models of BBB stabilization [43].

The multi-faceted pharmacological profile of SM/PN is underpinned by the complementary actions of its constituent phytochemicals: salvianolic acid B, tanshinone IIA, and ginsenosides. These compounds have been shown to modulate vascular tone, attenuate oxidative stress, and suppress pro-inflammatory signaling pathways, including p-STAT3 and p-p38, without broadly inhibiting reparative cascades [17,18]. By integrating rapid augmentation of cerebral perfusion with concurrent preservation of blood–brain barrier integrity, SM/PN defines a distinct therapeutic niche as a non-reperfusion neurovascular rescue strategy. Such a mechanism may safeguard the ischemic penumbra without incurring the permeability risks associated with conventional pro-collateral approaches, including vasodilators, hemodynamic modulators, and angiogenic therapies [8,42].

Unexpected findings and interpretation

The absence of VEGF induction at 24 h in SM/PN-treated animals may reflect the temporal dynamics of HIF-1α/VEGF signaling, which peaks within hours after ischemia and declines thereafter [48,49]. Notably, VEGF expression was not increased at the 24-h time point in SM/PN-treated animals. However, VEGF signaling is known to peak earlier after ischemic injury, typically within 6–12 h post-ischemia. Therefore, a transient early VEGF response prior to the 24-h observation window cannot be excluded. Nevertheless, the absence of sustained VEGF upregulation at 24 h suggests that the neurovascular protective effects of SM/PN are unlikely to depend on prolonged VEGF-driven angiogenic signaling, but rather reflect stabilization of existing vascular structures and preservation of blood–brain barrier integrity. Partial recovery of claudin-5 following SM/PN administration suggests that pericyte–endothelial cross-talk and tight junction reassembly require longer intervention windows [50,51]. Limited reduction of Iba-1 at the infarct border under SM/PN treatment likely reflects the persistent inflammatory microenvironment of this region. In the early phase after ischemic stroke, necrotic cells within the infarct core release damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) that activate innate immune signaling and sustain activation of resident microglia and infiltrating macrophages. This DAMP-rich necrotic core environment is known to maintain strong inflammatory signaling and prolonged microglial/macrophage activation after stroke, thereby rendering the infarct border relatively resistant to anti-inflammatory modulation compared with more distal peri-infarct regions [[52], [53], [54], [55]]. Collectively, these findings highlight that SM/PN's acute benefits are primarily mediated via vascular stabilization and selective signaling modulation, while certain structural and immune components may necessitate prolonged treatment to achieve full recovery.

Clinical relevance and translational potential

For patients ineligible for reperfusion therapy, SM/PN's profile—rapid collateral enhancement, anti-inflammatory action, and BBB stabilization without a VEGF surge—addresses key unmet needs in the hyperacute phase [4,6]. Oral administration and a multi-target mechanism make prehospital or early emergency department deployment plausible—aligning with prior demonstrations of the feasibility of paramedic-initiated neuroprotection and the prognostic importance of robust collateral circulation in prolonging the salvageable penumbra, even in the absence of immediate recanalization [8]. Moreover, limiting early BBB permeability aligns with contemporary models of stroke pathophysiology and may mitigate downstream edema and inflammatory infiltration when reperfusion is not achievable [41].

Limitations and future directions

This study was restricted to an acute 24-h observation window, without evaluation of long-term functional recovery, chronic vascular remodeling, or gliovascular interactions. Future work should incorporate real-time imaging of collateral dynamics, comprehensive neurobehavioral testing, and mechanistic interrogation using pathway-specific inhibitors or genetic knockouts. Additionally, validation in reperfusion models and in stroke paradigms incorporating relevant comorbidities (e.g., hypertension, diabetes, aging) will be essential to strengthen translational applicability. Furthermore, the relationship between the effective in vivo doses and the in vitro concentrations used in this study was not directly investigated. Future pharmacokinetic/pharmacodynamic (PK/PD) studies are needed to bridge the in vivo and in vitro dosing paradigms and to support the translational interpretation of SM/PN efficacy.

Conclusion

SM/PN confers rapid-onset neurovascular protection through the integrated actions of non-angiogenic collateral augmentation, selective microglial modulation, and preservation of BBB integrity. This coordinated mechanism differentiates SM/PN from conventional pro-collateral or angiogenic approaches, mitigating the risk of acute vascular permeability while sustaining penumbral perfusion. The distinctive multi-target profile supports its potential as an adjunctive or stand-alone therapy for acute ischemic stroke, particularly in patients ineligible for timely reperfusion (Fig. 7).
