Optimal sequential tests yield log-optimal e-processes
Ashwin Ram, Aaditya Ramdas

Abstract
It has been recently shown that e-processes are sufficient for sequential testing in the following sense: every level- α \alpha sequential test can be obtained by thresholding an e-process at 1 / α 1/\alpha . However, in the above result, neither does the test have to be asymptotically optimal (in terms of stopping times) nor does the e-process have to be asymptotically log-optimal. It has separately been shown that asymptotically log-optimal e-processes yield asymptotically optimal sequential tests. In this paper, we prove the converse, arguably completing the story: it is possible to aggregate asymptotically optimal sequential tests into asymptotically log-optimal e-processes. This is accomplished by using a new class of WAIT e-processes: those that are Weighted Aggregates of Indicators of stopping Times that begin at zero, are nondecreasing and increase to infinity under the alternative at the optimal rate. Importantly, the paper discusses several nuances in the varied definitions of asymptotic (log-)optimality.



1
Introduction

Sequential tests are important because they provide the flexibility needed to adaptively stop a stream of data and reject the null hypothesis while uniformly controlling the Type-
I
error at some specified rate
\alpha
. In its simplest form, a level-
\alpha
sequential test is a stopping time
\tau_{\alpha}
that satisfies
\sup_{P\in\mathcal{P}}P(\tau_{\alpha}<\infty)\leq\alpha
for some null class
\mathcal{P}
. The relationship between such tests and
e
-processes is an important question in the literature. Formally, an
e
-value is a nonnegative random variable with null expectation at most one, while an
e
-process is the sequential analogue and valid at any stopping time, for all
P\in\mathcal{P}
.

To this end, two key facts motivate our paper. First, it is known that thresholding any
\mathcal{P}
-
e
-process
E=(E_{t})
at
1/\alpha
will yield a level-
\alpha
sequential test, thanks to Ville’s inequality
(
29
)
. Second, this representation is complete in the sense that any level-
\alpha
sequential test can be recovered by thresholding some
e
-process at
1/\alpha

(
23
)
. However, this statement is still incomplete. Suppose the representing
e
-process is a two-valued indicator process. Then it will not necessarily have meaningful logarithmic growth under alternatives. In other words, on one hand, we have completeness of
e
-processes for validity. However, the question remains as to whether asymptotically optimal tests can be converted into asymptotically log-optimal evidence processes.

In this paper, we therefore prove such a converse. Formally, suppose we are given only a level-indexed family of tests
(\tau_{\alpha_{k}})_{k\geq 1}
with no pre-existing
e
-process. We will write
b_{k}:=\log(1/\alpha_{k})
and by definition note that
\sup_{P\in\mathcal{P}}P(\tau_{\alpha_{k}}<\infty)\leq\alpha_{k}
. Then for nonnegative weights
w_{k}
that satisfy
\sum_{k}w_{k}\alpha_{k}\leq 1
, we will define the weighted indicators of stopping times as

M_{t}:=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}w_{k}\mathbf{1}_{\{\tau_{\alpha_{k}}\leq t\}}.

We will refer to such processes as WAIT
e
-processes. These start at zero, are nondecreasing in time, and are valid
\mathcal{P}
-
e
-processes (as we will prove). Importantly, their growth under an alternative
Q
is governed by the deterministic cumulative weight profile
W(x):=\sum_{b_{k}\leq x}w_{k}
. Suppose these tests are first-order optimal under
Q
as in almost surely,

\frac{\tau_{\alpha_{k}}}{b_{k}}\longrightarrow\frac{1}{I(Q)}.

And further suppose that the profile satisfies

\frac{\log W(x)}{x}\longrightarrow\rho.

Then, under the alternative
Q
, we will have with probability one that

\frac{\log M_{t}}{t}\longrightarrow\rho I(Q).

Note that the constraint
\sum_{k}w_{k}\alpha_{k}\leq 1
implies that
W(x)\leq e^{x}
. Thus,
\rho\leq 1
. As a result, the WAIT aggregation is fully log-optimal whenever the levels and weights have the full profile exponent
\rho=1
. To this end, we will present a number of different level schedules where such a rate can be achieved.

Contributions

We summarize our main contributions as follows:

1.

We introduce the concept of WAIT
e
-processes and prove their validity for any arbitrary composite nulls in full generality.

2.

We prove an exact deterministic profile theorem. That is, if the tests are first order optimal, the resulting
e
-process will have log-growth rate
\rho I(Q)
, with
\rho
determined entirely by the level and weight profile. We valid these results empirically as well.

3.

We identify several full-rate
\alpha_{k}
schedules and weights. These include log-corrected unweighted schedules and weighted geometrically space schedules.

4.

We cleanly present the first unification of several notions of optimality. These include almost-sure, in-probability,
L^{1}
, expectation (with concentration), and threshold-time optimality. In turn, we give intuition on exactly what assumptions are needed to convert optimal sequential tests (in any sense) to log-optimal
e
-processes. And in doing so, we prove (and validate empirically) why a very common notion of optimality in the testing literature (expectation optimality) on its own is not sufficient to aggregate into an optimal
e
-process.

The rest of this paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
, we formally define the level-indexed sequential tests, the
\mathcal{P}
-
e
-process, and the WAIT aggregate and profile. Then, in Section
3
, we give our general WAIT aggregation theorem, the full-profile schedules that yield log-optimal
e
-processes, and we show that the threshold tests derived from the full-profile WAIT processes are first-order optimal. In Section
4
, we present the unweighted profile theorem and a weighted improvement of a schedule that would not normally deliver log-optimality in the aggregate. We relate several types of optimality in Section
5
, as well as what notions of test optimality are needed to obtain a log-optimal aggregated
e
-process. We conclude in Section
6
. In Appendix
G
and Appendix
H
we provide experiments that validate our theoretical results. Further, all proofs of the results can also be found in the appendix.

Problem setup and WAIT aggregation

Throughout, we work on a filtered measurable space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{N}_{0}})
. We say that the composite null is a family
\mathcal{P}
of probability measures on
(\Omega,\mathcal{F})
. We define level-indexed sequential tests as follows.

Definition 2.1
.

Suppose that
(\alpha_{k})_{k\geq 1}
is some decreasing sequence in
(0,1)
with
\alpha_{k}\downarrow 0
. We will write
b_{k}:=\log(1/\alpha_{k})
. For each
k\in\mathbb{N}
, let
\tau_{k}:=\tau_{\alpha_{k}}
be an
(\mathcal{F}_{t})
-stopping rule that takes values in
\mathbb{N}_{0}\cup\{\infty\}
such that

\sup_{P\in\mathcal{P}}P(\tau_{k}<\infty)\leq\alpha_{k}.

(1)

Note that this construction only needs to have tests at each of the
(\alpha_{k})
selected levels. Starting with a full family
\{\tau_{\alpha}\}_{0<\alpha<1}
, one can choose some level schedule
(\alpha_{k})
and then take
\tau_{k}:=\tau_{\alpha_{k}}
. Let us now define what we mean by a strong asymptotic rate at the chosen levels.

Definition 2.2
.

Take some particular alternative probability measure
Q
and some constant
I\in(0,\infty)
. We say that the chosen level tests have a pathwise rate of
I
under
Q
if

\frac{\tau_{k}}{b_{k}}\longrightarrow\frac{1}{I},

(2)

Q
almost surely as
k\to\infty
.

We now define an
e
-process.

Definition 2.3
.

We say that a nonnegative adapted process
M=(M_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
is a
\mathcal{P}
-
e
-process if
\mathbb{E}_{P}[M_{\sigma}]\leq 1
for every
P\in\mathcal{P}
and every finite
(\mathcal{F}_{t})
-stopping time
\sigma
.

Note that strict positivity here will come at no cost asymptotically. In particular, strict positivity is optional here, as several of our processes throughout this paper will start at
0
. However, to obtain a strictly positive
e
-process, one may choose
\eta\in(0,1)
and define
M_{t}^{(\eta)}:=\eta+(1-\eta)M_{t}
. Then,
M^{(\eta)}
is again a
\mathcal{P}
-
e
-process because we have for every finite stopping rule
\tau
that

\mathbb{E}_{P}[M_{\sigma}^{(\eta)}]=\eta+(1-\eta)\mathbb{E}_{P}[M_{\sigma}]\leq\eta+(1-\eta)\cdot 1=1.

And moreover, whenever
M_{t}\to\infty
, along a given path it holds that

\frac{1}{t}\log M_{t}^{\eta}-\frac{1}{t}\log M_{t}\longrightarrow 0.

Therefore, strict positivity can be obtained for free in the sense that it will not come at any asymptotic cost. Now, take some particular nonnegative weights
(w_{k})_{k\geq 1}
(not all zero) which satisfy

\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}w_{k}\alpha_{k}\leq 1.

(3)

For some
t\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, let us define the weighted aggregate as

M_{t}:=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}w_{k}\mathbf{1}_{\{\tau_{k}\leq t\}}.

(4)

For
x\geq 0
, let us define the cumulative weight profile as

W(x):=\sum_{k:b_{k}\leq x}w_{k}.

(5)

Since
\alpha_{k}\downarrow 0
, we have that
b_{k}\uparrow\infty
. As such, for each finite
x
, there are only finitely many indices
k
where
b_{k}\leq x
. Therefore,
W(x)
is always a finite sum. With this in mind, we now formalize that the weighted indicator aggregation is always an
e
-process.

Proposition 2.4
.

If (
1
) and (
3
) hold, then the process
M
in (
4
) is a nondecreasing
\mathcal{P}
-
e
-process.

We will now give a proposition showing that consistency will transfer from the test family.

Proposition 2.5
.

Assume that (
3
) holds and suppose that for some index
k_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
,

Q(\tau_{k}<\infty\text{ for every }k\geq k_{0})=1.

(6)

Assume also that
\sum_{k=k_{0}}^{\infty}w_{k}=\infty
. Then,
M_{t}\to\infty
almost surely under
Q
as
t\to\infty
.

Note that the cumulative weight profile can never grow faster than
e^{x}
. We will formalize that as follows.

Lemma 2.6
.

Assume that (
3
) holds. Then for every
x\geq 0
,
W(x)\leq e^{x}
. Consequently, whenever the limit
\rho:=\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{\log W(x)}{x}

\rho\leq 1
.

The WAIT Aggregation Theorem

We now prove that the asymptotic logarithmic growth is determined by
W
.

Theorem 3.1
.

Assume that (
1
), (
3
), and (
2
) hold. Suppose that
M
is the aggregate (as in (
4
)) and let
W
be the profile (as in (
5
)). Suppose in addition
W(x)\to\infty
and for some
\rho\in[0,1]
we have that

\frac{\log W(x)}{x}\longrightarrow\rho.

(7)

Then, almost surely under
Q
as
t\to\infty
,

\boxed{\frac{1}{t}\log M_{t}\longrightarrow\rho I.}

(8)

Intuitively, Theorem
3.1
shows that once the tests reach an asymptotic speed of
b_{k}/I
, the aggregate will have a log growth rate that is simply the product of

with the exponential growth rate of
W(x)
. Therefore, the entire problem becomes about building a valid schedule with a cumulative weight profile

that is as large as possible. However, recall that thanks to Lemma
2.6
, it is not possible to exceed

. Therefore, the maximum possible exponent in this indicator aggregation class is

.

Profiles and Schedules

Let us now specialize to the unweighted case where
w_{k}\equiv 1
. Then

B_{t}:=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\mathbf{1}_{\{\tau_{k}\leq t\}}

Corollary 4.1

Assume that

Q

\frac{1}{t}\log B_{t}\longrightarrow\rho I.

We will now present a lemma to invert the asymptotic relationship between the level scale

and the counting profile

Lemma 4.2
.

Suppose that
(b_{k})_{k\geq 1}
is increasing with

. And let

1.

If for some

\frac{\log N(x)}{x}\longrightarrow\frac{1}{c}.

2.

If
b_{k}/(\log k)\to\infty

\frac{\log N(x)}{x}\longrightarrow 0.

We now present a quick corollary of rate classification for unweighted schedules.

Corollary 4.3
.

Assume that
\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\alpha_{k}\leq 1

1.

Suppose for some

\frac{1}{t}\log B_{t}\to\frac{I}{c}.

2.

If

\frac{1}{t}\log B_{t}\longrightarrow 0.

3.

Suppose that
b_{k}=\log k+o(\log k)

. That is, we have almost surely under

\frac{1}{t}\log B_{t}\longrightarrow I.

The question therefore becomes how to choose this

If we further assume that

Weighted Improvement

It has been shown that we may recover the full rate
I
with an unweighted count, assuming that we pick levels that decay like the product of
1/k
with a subpolynomial correction factor. However, a more straightforward idea is to keep the levels

Proposition 4.5
.

Let us define for

the aggregated process as

M_{t}^{\mathrm{dy}}:=\frac{6}{\pi^{2}}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{2^{k}}{k^{2}}\,\mathbf{1}_{\{\tau_{2^{-k}}\leq t\}}.

Assume in addition that almost surely under
Q

\frac{\tau_{2^{-k}}}{k\log 2}\longrightarrow\frac{1}{I}.

Then,
M^{\mathrm{dy}}
is a
\mathcal{P}

\frac{1}{t}\log M_{t}^{\mathrm{dy}}\longrightarrow I.

This weighted construction is the easiest way to fix the construction in

5
Weaker Notions of Optimality

Unfortunately, the almost sure rate of the tests is a fairly strong assumption, albeit it delivers optimality of the aggregate if
I
is optimal. One may wonder if it is feasible to weaken this assumption to other notions. That will be the focus of this section, where we prove what weaker notions of test-time optimality are sufficient to achieving optimality of the aggregate.

Throughout this section, we will consider some alternative
Q
and some information rate benchmark
I=I(Q)\in(0,\infty)
and levels
b_{k}=\log(1/\alpha_{k})\to\infty
. We now relate various types of test-time optimality to the almost-sure one used throughout the paper.

Definition 5.1
.

We say that a level-indexed family of sequential tests
(\tau_{k})
is one of four different things, defined as follows.

1.

\tau_{k}
has a
Q
-almost-sure rate
of
I
if

\frac{\tau_{k}}{b_{k}}\to\frac{1}{I}\text{ almost surely under $Q$.}

2.

\tau_{k}
has a
Q
-probability rate
of
I
if

\frac{\tau_{k}}{b_{k}}\to\frac{1}{I}\text{ in probability under $Q$.}

3.

\tau_{k}
has an
L^{1}(Q)
-rate
of
I
if

\frac{\mathbb{E}_{Q}\left[\left|\tau_{k}-\frac{b_{k}}{I}\right|\right]}{b_{k}}\to 0.

4.

\tau_{k}
has an
expectation-rate
of
I
if
\mathbb{E}_{Q}[\tau_{k}]<\infty
eventually and

\frac{\mathbb{E}_{Q}[\tau_{k}]}{b_{k}}\to\frac{1}{I}.

The implications between these are straightforward. Although
I
can be any rate, suppose
I
is optimal. Then, pathwise optimality clearly implies optimality in
Q
-probability. In addition
L^{1}
optimality implies both
Q
-probability optimality (thanks to Markov’s inequality, as shown later) and expectation optimality. Additionally, Definition
5.1
allows one to formally understand which notions weaker (than almost sure) can deliver optimality. To this end, suppose that
E=(E_{t})
is a
\mathcal{P}
-
e
-process. Then,
E
has an almost sure log-growth rate
L
under
Q
if
\log(E_{t})/t\to L
with probability one under
Q
. Similarly, it will have log-growth rate
L
in
Q
-probability if
\log(E_{t})/t\to L
in
Q
-probability.

Now, if
I(Q)
is some benchmark for
Q
, then
E
will be called almost surely log-optimal under
Q
if it achieves an almost sure log-growth rate
I(Q)
. For some
\rho\in[0,1]
, if the rate is
\rho I(Q)
, then we call
E

\rho
-log-efficient.

Having said this, we will define
threshold-time
optimality as follows.

Definition 5.2
.

For some nonnegative process
E
, let us define its threshold rejection time as

T_{\alpha}(E):=\inf\{t\in\mathbb{N}_{0}:E_{t}\geq 1/\alpha\}.

If
E
is a
\mathcal{P}
-

-process, then it follows that

is a level

if with probability one under

\alpha\downarrow 0
,

\frac{T_{\alpha}(E)}{\log(1/\alpha)}\to\frac{1}{I}.

The in-probability version is defined analogously as this.

With this defined, note that log-growth will imply threshold-time optimality. We will formalize that as follows.

Proposition 5.3
.

Suppose that
E=(E_{t})
is nondecreasing and finite valued. Set
T_{\alpha}(E):=\inf\{t:E_{t}\geq 1/\alpha\}
. Suppose for some
L\in(0,\infty)
we have that almost surely under

,

\frac{\log E_{t}}{t}\to L.

Then it follows that almost surely under
Q
as
\alpha\downarrow 0
,

\frac{T_{\alpha}(E)}{\log(1/\alpha)}\to\frac{1}{L}.

If
\log(E_{t})/t\to L
in probability under

-probability instead.

Proposition

allows us to conclude that under our assumptions in Theorem
3.1
, the threshold tests generated by our WAIT
e
-process will satisfy almost surely under
Q
that

\frac{T_{\alpha}(M)}{\log(1/\alpha)}\to\frac{1}{\rho I}.

Here,
T_{\alpha}(M):=\inf\{t:M_{t}\geq 1/\alpha\}
is the threshold test and
M_{t}
is the aggregate (the WAIT
e
-process). The takeaway is that the full-profile WAIT aggregates (with
\rho=1
) will give rise to first-order optimal tests at rate
I
.

Unfortunately, convergence in
Q
-probability will not be enough for the simultaneous control that we were able to obtain in the almost sure optimality case (and essential for the aggregation to work). However, if the tests are nested then this in probability convergence is enough. To elaborate, we will first define a nested level family.

Definition 5.4
.

We say that the particular tests we choose are nested if
j\leq k
implies that
\tau_{j}\leq\tau_{k}
pathwise on
\Omega
. Equivalently, for each particular
t
, the events
\{\tau_{k}\leq t\}
are decreasing in
k
.

One can immediately see that thresholded families of the form
\inf\{t\in\mathbb{N}_{0}:S_{t}\geq\log(1/\alpha)\}
are automatically nested in
\alpha
. Remarkably, for nested families, in probability optimality for sequential tests is enough to deliver optimality of the aggregate, an idea formalized as follows.

Theorem 5.5
.

Assume that (
1
), (
3
), and the nested property (as in Definition
5.4
) all hold. Assume further that
W(x)\to\infty
and that (
7
) holds with
\rho\in[0,1]
. Suppose now we have in
Q
-probability that

\frac{\tau_{k}}{b_{k}}\longrightarrow\frac{1}{I}.

Then, in
Q
-probability as
t\to\infty
it follows that

\frac{1}{t}\log M_{t}\longrightarrow\rho I.

Even though in-probability is weaker certainly than our almost-sure requirement, in the sequential testing literature, expectation-optimality is most typical. Unfortunately, this alone will not be sufficient as pointed out in Example
A.1
. However, if expectation-optimality and concentration are assumed, then that does deliver log-optimality of the aggregate, formalized as follows.

Corollary 5.6
.

Assume that (
1
), (
3
), and the nestedness property (as in Definition
5.4
) all hold. Assume also that
W(x)\to\infty
and that (
7
) holds with
\rho\in[0,1]
. Also, suppose that one of the following conditions hold.

1.

We have
L^{1}
optimality:

\frac{\mathbb{E}_{Q}\left[\left|\tau_{k}-\frac{b_{k}}{I}\right|\right]}{b_{k}}\longrightarrow 0.

2.

Alternatively, we have:

\frac{\mathbb{E}_{Q}[\tau_{k}]}{b_{k}}\longrightarrow\frac{1}{I}\quad\text{and}\quad\frac{\operatorname{Var}_{Q}(\tau_{k})}{b_{k}^{2}}\longrightarrow 0.

Then it follows that in
Q
-probability as
t\to\infty
,

\frac{1}{t}\log M_{t}\longrightarrow\rho I.

Lastly, if we also have summable deviations, then that also gives us pathwise optimality. This is the strongest form of log-optimality for the aggregate.

Corollary 5.7
.

Assume that for every rational
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
,

\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}Q\left(\left|\frac{\tau_{k}}{b_{k}}-\frac{1}{I}\right|>\frac{\varepsilon}{I}\right)<\infty.

Then, (
2
) holds. That is, the almost-sure growth that follows from (
8
) of Theorem
3.1
holds.

As remarked, it is important to conceptually see why on its own, the expectation optimality is too weak for the purposes of this work, as shown in Example
A.1
below. It formalizes why expectation optimality alone is not enough. In particular,
\mathbb{E}_{Q}[\tau_{k}]/b_{k}\to 1/I
does not imply in-probability optimality, almost sure optimality, or any deterministic log-growth of a WAIT aggregate. That is why, throughout this paper, any result that starts from mean optimality needs an additional assumption. This could be
L^{1}
optimality, a normalized variance that vanishes, or any other assumption with such control over the aggregate.

6
Conclusion

Prior literature has established that
e
-processes are sufficient for anytime-valid sequential testing in the sense that thresholding such a
\mathcal{P}
-
e
-process at
1/\alpha
gives a level-
\alpha
sequential test. Conversely, thresholding an appropriate
e
-process allows recovery of any level-
\alpha
sequential test. In spite of this, a major issue that remained is how to ensure that an asymptotically optimal family of sequential tests give an
e
-process that achieves asymptotically optimal logarithmic growth under any alternative
Q
. This issue is addressed by our paper. That is, we prove under an apt level-weight profile condition, an aggregation that preserves optimality is possible. In addition, we relate several notions of asymptotic optimality and clarify the conditions under which it is possible to achieve (almost-sure) optimal logarithmic-growth of the aggregated process. In doing so, we distinguish between validity, consistency, optimality at test-time, growth-rate optimality, and threshold-time optimality. To our knowledge, we are the first work to do so.

In spite of these novelties, there are numerous remaining open problems. For one, we identify the limit of
\log M_{t}/t
, but we do not give second order terms or nonasymptotic lower bounds on
M_{t}
under the alternative. In addition, we assume that the input tests are optimal. Although in Appendix
F
, we give sufficient conditions for obtaining optimal tests for some model, it is not obvious when such tests exist, as that is largely dependent on the choice of model. Thirdly, our results are log-optimal pointwise under each alternative
Q
. It would be interesting to extend these results to be simultaneously log-optimal over an entire class of alternatives
\mathcal{Q}
. With all this being said, our novel WAIT construction is an intuitive and model-independent way of converting optimal sequential tests to optimal sequential evidence processes. And in doing so, we clarify what assumptions are needed to go from different notions of test optimality to log-optimality of the resulting aggregated
e
-process. In doing so, we complete the narrative of the relationship between sequential tests and
e
-processes.

Appendix A
A counterexample to why expectation optimality alone is insufficient

Example A.1
.

Consider any particular but arbitrarily chosen increasing sequence
b_{k}\uparrow\infty
and any nonnegative weights
(w_{k})
that satisfy (
3
). Take a single null
\mathcal{P}=\{P\}
. We will work on a filtered space with constant filtration. That is, for
t\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
,
\mathcal{F}_{t}\equiv\sigma(Y,B_{1},B_{2},\dots)
. Here,
Y
and
(B_{k})
are random variables that satisfy
Q(B_{k}=1)=1
for all
k
,
P(B_{k}=1)=\alpha_{k}
for all
k
, and

Q\left(Y=\frac{1}{2}\right)=Q\left(Y=\frac{3}{2}\right)=\frac{1}{2}.

Now, define

\tau_{k}:=\begin{cases}\left\lceil\dfrac{Yb_{k}}{I}\right\rceil,&B_{k}=1,\\
\infty,&B_{k}=0.\end{cases}

Each
\tau_{k}
is an
(\mathcal{F}_{t})
-stopping rule and
P(\tau_{k}<\infty)=P(B_{k}=1)=\alpha_{k}
. Therefore the required size bound (i.e. (
1
)) holds for the null family
\mathcal{P}=\{P\}
. Under
Q
,
B_{k}=1
almost surely for every
k
. Hence,
\tau_{k}=\lceil\frac{Yb_{k}}{I}\rceil
almost surely under
Q
. Therefore it follows that almost surely under
Q
we have

\frac{\tau_{k}}{b_{k}}\longrightarrow\frac{Y}{I}.

In addition,

\frac{\mathbb{E}_{Q}[\tau_{k}]}{b_{k}}=\frac{\mathbb{E}_{Q}[Y]}{I}+O\left(\frac{1}{b_{k}}\right)\longrightarrow\frac{1}{I}.

By definition, this family is expectation-optimal at rate
I
. However, note that
Y
is nondegenerate. So
\tau_{k}/b_{k}
does not converge in
Q
-probability to
1/I
. In any case, consider the corresponding aggregate

M_{t}:=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}w_{k}\mathbf{1}_{\{\tau_{k}\leq t\}}.

With this aggregate one has almost surely under
Q
that

M_{t}:=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}w_{k}\mathbf{1}_{\{b_{k}\leq It/Y\}}=W\left(\frac{It}{Y}\right).

Consequently suppose that (
7
) holds. Then it follows that with probability one under
Q
,

\frac{1}{t}\log M_{t}\longrightarrow\frac{\rho I}{Y}.

This is a random quantity. Therefore, expectation optimality is much much too weak to conclude (
8
), or any such deterministic time control.

Appendix B
An Equivalent Version of the WAIT Aggregation Theorem

Our almost-sure assumption is equivalent to tail-uniform optimality in
Q
-probability. Informally, it can be viewed as an alternate way to intuit our main results.

Definition B.1
.

We will say that the level tests we choose are
tail uniformly optimal
at rate
I
in
Q
-probability if for every
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
and every
\delta\in(0,1)
, there exists some
K=K(\varepsilon,\delta)
such that

Q\left(\frac{1-\varepsilon}{I}b_{k}\leq\tau_{k}\leq\frac{1+\varepsilon}{I}b_{k}\text{ for all }k\geq K\right)\geq 1-\delta.

The reason this above tail uniform optimality can be viewed as equivalent to almost sure convergence can be argued as follows. For the implication from almost sure convergence to tail uniform optimality, it is immediate by continuity from below. That is, for all
\varepsilon
, the events

A_{K}^{\varepsilon}:=\left\{\frac{1-\varepsilon}{I}b_{k}\leq\tau_{k}\leq\frac{1+\varepsilon}{I}b_{k}\text{ for all }k\geq K\right\}

will increase to an event of probability one. Conversely, if tail uniform optimality holds, then for all
\varepsilon
, we have that
Q\left(\bigcup_{K=1}^{\infty}A_{K}^{\varepsilon}\right)=1
. If we now intersect over all rationals
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
we have that
\tau_{k}/b_{k}\to 1/I
almost surely under
Q
.

We are now ready to present the theorem we have been building towards: an analogue of Theorem
3.1
.

Theorem B.2
.

Assume that (
1
), (
3
), and Definition
B.1
all hold. Suppose that
M
is the aggregate (as in (
4
)). Further suppose that
W(x)\to\infty
and that (
7
) holds for some
\rho\in[0,1]
. Then it follows that in
Q
-probability as
t\to\infty
,

\frac{1}{t}\log M_{t}\longrightarrow\rho I.

Note that this Theorem
B.2
is the probability analogue of Theorem
3.1
. In particular, it necessitates that we must have simultaneous control of all large enough levels on a high probability event. Although it concludes a weaker assumption, we will be using the techniques of this proof for our assumption-free in-probability proof.

Proof of Proposition
2.4
.

Take some

\{\tau_{k}\leq t\}
belongs to
\mathcal{F}_{t}
since
\tau_{k}
is a stopping time. Therefore, the random variable
w_{k}\mathbf{1}_{\{\tau_{k}\leq t\}}
is
\mathcal{F}_{t}
measurable. For each
n\in\mathbb{N}

M_{t}^{(n)}:=\sum_{k=1}^{n}w_{k}\mathbf{1}_{\{\tau_{k}\leq t\}}.

By definition, each
M_{t}^{(n)}
will be
\mathcal{F}_{t}
measurable. And as
n\to\infty

M_{t}^{(n)}\uparrow M_{t}
pointwise by positivity of all summands. Hence,
M_{t}
is
\mathcal{F}_{t}
measurable, namely since it is the monotone limit of
\mathcal{F}_{t}
-measurable random variables. Now, if
s\leq t
, then for every
k
,
\mathbf{1}_{\{\tau_{k}\leq s\}}\leq\mathbf{1}_{\{\tau_{k}\leq t\}}

w_{k}\geq 0

M_{s}\leq M_{t}
, entailing that the process is non-decreasing. Now, let us have
\sigma
be any finite stopping rule and consider some

. Since all summands are nonnegative, thanks to Tonelli’s theorem we have

\mathbb{E}_{P}[M_{\sigma}]=\mathbb{E}_{P}\left[\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}w_{k}\mathbf{1}_{\{\tau_{k}\leq\sigma\}}\right]=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}w_{k}P(\tau_{k}\leq\sigma).

Since
\sigma
is almost surely finite, for each
k
we have that
\{\tau_{l}\leq\sigma\}\subseteq\{\tau_{k}<\infty\}
. Therefore we get that
P(\tau_{k}\leq\sigma)\leq P(\tau_{k}<\infty)
. By (
1
) and (
3
), we thus get

\mathbb{E}_{P}[M_{\sigma}]\leq\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}w_{k}P(\tau_{k}<\infty)\leq\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}w_{k}\alpha_{k}\leq 1.

This completes our proof.
∎

Proof of Theorem
3.1
.

In proving this theorem, we will first prove the following two statements.

1.

For every
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
there exists an almost sure event
\Omega_{\varepsilon}
such that for every
\omega\in\Omega_{\varepsilon}
there is an integer
K_{\varepsilon}(\omega)
for which for all
k\geq K_{\varepsilon}(\omega)
,

\frac{1-\varepsilon}{I}b_{k}\leq\tau_{k}(\omega)\leq\frac{1+\varepsilon}{I}b_{k}.

(11)

2.

Take some
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
and
\omega\in\Omega_{\varepsilon}
. Set
C_{\varepsilon}(\omega):=\sum_{k=1}^{K_{\varepsilon}(\omega)-1}w_{k}
. Then it follows that for every
t\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
,

W\left(\frac{It}{1+\varepsilon}\right)-C_{\varepsilon}(\omega)\leq M_{t}(\omega)\leq W\left(\frac{It}{1-\varepsilon}\right)+C_{\varepsilon}(\omega).

(12)

We will first prove
statement (1)
. Consider some
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
. Thanks to (
2
), the random variables
\tau_{k}/b_{k}
converge
Q
-almost surely to
1/I
. Therefore the event

\Omega_{\varepsilon}:=\left\{\omega:\exists K_{\varepsilon}(\omega)\in\mathbb{N}\text{ such that }\left|\frac{\tau_{k}(\omega)}{b_{k}}-\frac{1}{I}\right|\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{I}\text{ for all }k\geq K_{\varepsilon}(\omega)\right\}

has
Q
-probability one. If we now rearrange the inequality
\left|\frac{\tau_{k}(\omega)}{b_{k}}-\frac{1}{I}\right|\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{I}
, we have (
11
), proving the claim.

We will now prove
statement (2)
. Take some
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
and
\omega\in\Omega_{\varepsilon}
. With minor abuse of notation, let us write
K:=K_{\varepsilon}(\omega)
and
C:=C_{\varepsilon}(\omega)=\sum_{k=1}^{K-1}w_{k}
. We will prove the lower and upper bounds in (
12
) separately. We will start with the
lower bound
. Consider any
k\geq K
such that
b_{k}\leq\frac{It}{1+\varepsilon}
. Thanks to (
11
), it follows that

\tau_{k}(\omega)\leq\frac{1+\varepsilon}{I}b_{k}\leq\frac{1+\varepsilon}{I}\cdot\frac{It}{1+\varepsilon}=t.

Thus, it follows that
\mathbf{1}_{\{\tau_{k}(\omega)\leq t\}}=1
. If we then sum
w_{k}
over all such
k
we have that

M_{t}(\omega)\geq\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k\geq K\\
b_{k}\leq It/(1+\varepsilon)\end{subarray}}w_{k}.

We will now split the entire profile
W(It/(1+\varepsilon))
into indices below
K
and indices at least
K
. That is,

W\left(\frac{It}{1+\varepsilon}\right)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k<K\\
b_{k}\leq It/(1+\varepsilon)\end{subarray}}w_{k}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k\geq K\\
b_{k}\leq It/(1+\varepsilon)\end{subarray}}w_{k}.

Therefore,

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k\geq K\\
b_{k}\leq It/(1+\varepsilon)\end{subarray}}w_{k}=W\left(\frac{It}{1+\varepsilon}\right)-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k<K\\
b_{k}\leq It/(1+\varepsilon)\end{subarray}}w_{k}\geq W\left(\frac{It}{1+\varepsilon}\right)-C.

This holds because the subtracted sum will be at most
\sum_{k<K}w_{k}=C
. Therefore,
M_{t}(\omega)\geq W\left(\frac{It}{1+\varepsilon}\right)-C
. We will now prove the
upper bound
. To this end, suppose that
k\geq K
and
\tau_{k}(\omega)\leq t
. By the lower half of (
11
), we have that

\frac{1-\varepsilon}{I}b_{k}\leq\tau_{k}(\omega)\leq t.

If we multiply by
I/(1-\varepsilon)
, we have that
b_{k}\leq\frac{It}{1-\varepsilon}
. Therefore, each index
k\geq K
that contributes to
M_{t}(\omega)
must satisfy
b_{k}\leq It/(1-\varepsilon)
. Therefore, we have

M_{t}(\omega)\leq\sum_{k<K}w_{k}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k\geq K\\
b_{k}\leq It/(1-\varepsilon)\end{subarray}}w_{k}\leq C+W\left(\frac{It}{1-\varepsilon}\right).

This proves (
12
). We will now prove the

7
) holds and that
W(x)\to\infty
. Consider some
\varepsilon\in(0,1)

from the proof of
(1)
. Once again, we will write

. Since
W(x)\to\infty
, there must exist some
t_{0}(\omega)

W\left(\frac{It}{1+\varepsilon}\right)>2C

. Now, for such a

M_{t}(\omega)\geq W\left(\frac{It}{1+\varepsilon}\right)-C\geq\frac{1}{2}W\left(\frac{It}{1+\varepsilon}\right).

On a similar vein, the upper bound implies that

M_{t}(\omega)\leq W\left(\frac{It}{1-\varepsilon}\right)+C\leq 2W\left(\frac{It}{1-\varepsilon}\right).

Let us now take the logarithms and divide by

. We then have for all

\frac{1}{t}\log W\left(\frac{It}{1+\varepsilon}\right)-\frac{\log 2}{t}\leq\frac{1}{t}\log M_{t}(\omega)\leq\frac{1}{t}\log W\left(\frac{It}{1-\varepsilon}\right)+\frac{\log 2}{t}.

\frac{1}{t}\log W\left(\frac{It}{1+\varepsilon}\right)=\frac{I}{1+\varepsilon}\cdot\frac{1}{\,It/(1+\varepsilon)\,}\log W\left(\frac{It}{1+\varepsilon}\right)\longrightarrow\frac{\rho I}{1+\varepsilon}.

\frac{1}{t}\log W\left(\frac{It}{1-\varepsilon}\right)\longrightarrow\frac{\rho I}{1-\varepsilon}.

Hence it follows that
\liminf_{t\to\infty}\frac{1}{t}\log M_{t}(\omega)\geq\frac{\rho I}{1+\varepsilon}

\limsup_{t\to\infty}\frac{1}{t}\log M_{t}(\omega)\leq\frac{\rho I}{1-\varepsilon}

\varepsilon\in(0,1)

to get a new event of
Q
-probability one on which both inequalities hold simultaneously for each rational
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
. We can now let
\varepsilon\downarrow 0
through the rationals to give us

\liminf_{t\to\infty}\frac{1}{t}\log M_{t}(\omega)\geq\rho I,\quad\limsup_{t\to\infty}\frac{1}{t}\log M_{t}(\omega)\leq\rho I.

The limit therefore exists and equals
\rho I
. This proves (
8
), completing the proof.

We will now derive an upper bound. If

Taking logarithms and dividing by

We will now prove

∎

Proof of Proposition
5.3
.

Let us write

. For this, we will show the almost sure case. Note that the in probability case is identical upon replacing the eventual inequalities with probability bounds (and using monotonicity). Consider some

Therefore,

holds. As a result,

\frac{1}{(1+\eta)L}\leq\liminf_{\alpha\downarrow 0}\frac{T_{\alpha}(E)}{\log(1/\alpha)}\leq\limsup_{\alpha\downarrow 0}\frac{T_{\alpha}(E)}{\log(1/\alpha)}\leq\frac{1}{(1-\eta)L}.

Proof of Theorem

.

Consider some
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
. For

, let us define

Appendix F
How to Build First-Passage Tests from Score Processes

We will show how, concretely, one can construct such almost sure optimal sequential tests. One example of this is as follows. Suppose we have a score process that grows linearly. Then first-passage tests from this will be pathwise optimal.

Proposition F.1
.

Suppose that
S=(S_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
is some adapted real-valued process. Assume that for every
x\geq 0
,

\sup_{P\in\mathcal{P}}P\left(\sup_{t\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}S_{t}\geq x\right)\leq e^{-x}.

Now, for
\alpha\in(0,1)
, let us define the first passage time
\tau_{\alpha}:=\inf\{t\in\mathbb{N}_{0}:S_{t}\geq\log(1/\alpha)\}
. Then it follows that for every
\alpha\in(0,1)
,
\sup_{P\in\mathcal{P}}P(\tau_{\alpha}<\infty)\leq\alpha
. Now suppose in addition we have that under
Q
almost surely as
t\to\infty
for some
I\in(0,\infty)
,

\frac{S_{t}}{t}\longrightarrow I.

Then it follows that for every sequence
\alpha_{k}\downarrow 0
with
b_{k}=\log(1/\alpha_{k})
we have that almost surely under
Q
,

\frac{\tau_{\alpha_{k}}}{b_{k}}\longrightarrow\frac{1}{I}.

Proof.

Notice how we have that
\{\tau_{\alpha}<\infty\}\subseteq\{\sup_{t\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}S_{t}\geq\log(1/\alpha)\}
. Therefore,

\sup_{P\in\mathcal{P}}P(\tau_{\alpha}<\infty)\leq\exp(-\log(1/\alpha))=\alpha.

Consider an
\omega
such that
S_{t}(\omega)/t\to I
. Now let
\varepsilon\in(0,I)
. Then there must exist some
T_{\varepsilon}(\omega)
such that for all
t\geq T_{\varepsilon}(\omega)
,
(I-\varepsilon)t\leq S_{t}(\omega)\leq(I+\varepsilon)t
. Since
b_{k}\to\infty
, there must exist some
K_{\varepsilon}(\omega)
such that
b_{k}>\max_{0\leq t<T_{\varepsilon}(\omega)}S_{t}(\omega)
for all
k\geq K_{\varepsilon}(\omega)
. Now, for such a
k
, the crossing level
b_{k}
cannot be reached before time
T_{\varepsilon}(\omega)
. Therefore,
\tau_{\alpha_{k}}(\omega)\geq T_{\varepsilon}(\omega)
.

Now, for the upper bound, let us define
u_{k}:=\lceil\frac{b_{k}}{I-\varepsilon}\rceil
. We then have that
u_{k}\geq T_{\varepsilon}(\omega)
for all large
k
. Therefore,
S_{u_{k}}(\omega)\geq(I-\varepsilon)u_{k}\geq b_{k}
. Thus, it follows that
\tau_{\alpha_{k}}(\omega)\leq u_{k}
. Thus,

\frac{\tau_{\alpha_{k}}(\omega)}{b_{k}}\leq\frac{1}{I-\varepsilon}+\frac{1}{b_{k}}.

Now, for the lower bound, we have already shown for all large
k
,
\tau_{\alpha_{k}}(\omega)\geq T_{\varepsilon}(\omega)
. Therefore,
b_{k}\leq S_{\tau_{\alpha_{k}}(\omega)}(\omega)\leq(I+\varepsilon)\tau_{\alpha_{k}}(\omega)
. And from this, we can see that

\frac{\tau_{\alpha_{k}}(\omega)}{b_{k}}\geq\frac{1}{I+\varepsilon}.

Let us now take both estimates together. For all large
k
, we therefore have

\frac{1}{I+\varepsilon}\leq\frac{\tau_{\alpha_{k}}(\omega)}{b_{k}}\leq\frac{1}{I-\varepsilon}+\frac{1}{b_{k}}.

If we send
k\to\infty
and then
\varepsilon\downarrow 0
through the rationals we get that
\tau_{\alpha_{k}}(\omega)/b_{k}\to 1/I
. This holds on the almost sure event
\{S_{t}/t\to I\}
, proving the claim.
∎

Proposition
F.1
applies to the following constructions. First, suppose that
E=(E_{t})
is a strictly positive
\mathcal{P}
-
e
-process. And suppose that one sets
S_{t}:=\log E_{t}
. Then by composite Ville’s inequality for e-processes it follows that

\sup_{P\in\mathcal{P}}P\left(\sup_{t\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}S_{t}\geq x\right)=\sup_{P\in\mathcal{P}}P\left(\sup_{t\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}E_{t}\geq e^{x}\right)\leq e^{-x}.

Then, it follows that the threshold test
\tau_{\alpha}:=\inf\{t\in\mathbb{N}_{0}:E_{t}\geq 1/\alpha\}
is valid. And in addition suppose almost surely under
Q
we have that

\frac{1}{t}\log E_{t}\longrightarrow I.

Then it follows that almost surely under
Q
,

\frac{\tau_{\alpha_{k}}}{\log(1/\alpha_{k})}\longrightarrow\frac{1}{I}.

In the setting where we have a simple null versus simple alternative, we can take
S_{t}:=\sum_{i=1}^{t}\log\frac{dQ}{dP}(X_{i})
. Then by the SLLN it follows that
S_{t}/t\to\mathrm{KL}(Q\|P)
almost surely under
Q
, and so the likelihood-ratio threshold tests will be pathwise optimal.
