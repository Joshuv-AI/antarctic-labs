Kidney transplantation in sub-Sahara African countries: challenges and lessons from the experience of Cameroon: a preliminary report of the first 17 pioneer cases
Authors: Vincent de Paul Djientcheu, Gloria Ashuntantang, Aristide Nono, Justin Kamga, Sebastien Mba, Marie Paule Ngongang, Hawa Tebere, Amada Ngoundjou, Tekounegning Aghogue, Foe Essissima, Magne Tiam, Marcus Fokou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis report presents the outcomes, challenges, and lessons learned from the first 17 cases of kidney transplantation (KT) performed in Cameroon and the Central Africa sub-region. All patients who underwent kidney transplantation at the Yaoundé General Hospital (YGH) are included in this study. Seventeen kidney transplants were performed, nine females and eight males. All donors were family members. The underlying nephropathies were chronic glomerulonephritis (9 cases), hypertensive nephropathy (6 cases), and Unknown origin (4 cases). Immunological compatibility was categorized as follows: HLA-identical (7 pairs), HLA-different (3 pairs), haploidentical (6 pairs), homozygous twin sisters (1 pair) and HLA semi-identical (1 pair). After transplantation, two recipients experienced complications due to an incorrect ureteral implantation into the peritoneum (1 case), and an anastomotic leak (hematoma), another case. All our patients are currently alive, with good quality of life and stable renal function. The treatment protocol was by induction therapy; basiliximab (20 mg on days 0 and 4) or Thymoglobulin, combined with methylprednisolone while maintenance therapy included mycophenolic acid, cyclosporine or tacrolimus, and prednisone. The total cost of patient management, including immune compatibility testing for the first patients, was covered by YGH and its partners. In conclusion, kidney transplantation is a safe and effective treatment in Cameroon. The team at YGH, along with international collaborators, have successfully acquired the necessary technology, and expertise, to perform kidney transplantation, making it a viable option for patients.Keywords: Kidney transplantation (KT), donors, recipients

Introduction

Chronic kidney disease (CKD) is increasingly reported in Sub-Saharan African countries. It has become a significant public health concern due to its rising prevalence, associated morbidity and mortality, and the high cost of management. In Cameroon, kidney care services were initiated in the mid-1980s. However, it was only in 1990 that kidney replacement therapy became a consistent reality with the opening of the first hemodialysis unit at Douala General Hospital. This was followed by the establishment of a second unit at Yaoundé General Hospital in 2002. By 2007, additional hemodialysis centers were progressively opened in all regional hospitals across the country. Despite substantial government subsidies which primarily cover dialysis sessions and equipment maintenance the burden on patients remains high. Many continue to experience a diminished quality of life and face significant challenges with social and professional reintegration. According to Njamnshi et al. in 2023, kidney transplantation has proven to be an effective, though not definitive, treatment for end-stage kidney disease. However, its implementation in Sub-Saharan Africa remains complex. This paper reports the results, challenges, and lessons learned from the first 17 kidney transplant cases performed at Yaoundé General Hospital. This represents the first documented experience of kidney transplantation in both Central Africa and Francophone Africa.

Patients and method

This study was conducted in accordance with the declaration of Helsinki where by the research was of great scientific and social interest. The overall objective of this study was to analyze the outcomes, challenges and lessons learned from the first 17 cases of kidney transplantation (KT). The study procedures were well documented and clearly described. The study was conducted at Yaoundé General Hospital in Cameroon. The risks associated with the study were specified, as were the measures proposed to avoid and minimize them. The information sheet and informed consent form, in both French and English, were well written and easy to understand. Measures to ensure the confidentiality of the data collected were clearly outlined in the document. The investigators’ CVs described them as competent individuals capable of successfully conducting this study. For all these reasons, the Yaoundé General Hospital Institutional Review Board approves the implementation of this version of the protocol.

This is a cohort of patients who underwent kidney transplantation at the Yaoundé General Hospital, Cameroon, between November 2021 and March 2025. The cohort of recipients comprised nine females and eight males. All donors were biologically related to the recipients. All patients in the cohort exhibited anti-HLA antibodies. Given that this program was newly implemented in our setting and country, a cautious approach was adopted; consequently, all patients were placed on induction therapy to minimize immunological risk, one recipient did not receive any induction treatment, as the donor was her monozygotic twin. Among the remaining sixteen recipients, fifteen were administered basiliximab, while one received thymoglobulin. Further details on the cohort are presented in Table 1. The transplant team consisted of Cameroonian professionals and two foreign experts the Head of the Transplantation Department and a Nephrologist from Geneva University Hospital. These foreign experts were engaged under a consultancy contract with the hospital, which covered their professional fees, airfare, and accommodation during their stay in Yaoundé.

Our team was trained in the entire transplantation procedure, including donor and recipient selection, standard surgical techniques, immunosuppressive drug administration, as well as post-operative monitoring and follow-up. These trainings were conducted in collaboration with our partners in Geneva, where the surgeon received in-person training, while other local team members participated remotely via teleconference. The objective was to minimize costs and strengthen local capacity in order to ensure the sustainability of the program after the departure of the collaborating partner.

Donors were proposed by the recipients and presented to the transplantation team. The entire transplantation process was explained to them in detail. Each donor underwent comprehensive biological, microbiological, radiological, and when necessary, functional assessments fully covered by Yaoundé General Hospital. Immune-compatibility testing (HLA, DSA) for the first 7 cases was performed at Geneva University Hospital; for the remaining 10 cases, testing was conducted at YGH following the acquisition of an automated analyzer (LUMINEX®) in 2023. This equipment allowed for in-house typing of HLA, DSA detection, and screening for viral markers. During our study, a Donor-Specific Antibody (DSA) against Class I HLA was identified with a Mean Fluorescence Intensity (MFI) of 1090. All required specialized consultations were completed prior to surgery. Patients needing treatment such as for dental infections or otitis received appropriate care before proceeding. Donors were excluded if the recipient had donor-specific antibodies and/or ABO incompatibility.

Open donor nephrectomy surgery, that is, the donor nephrectomy team preceded the recipient surgery team by 30 to 45 min. Under general anesthesia, a trans-peritoneal flank approach was used for donor nephrectomy with the operation table in an inverted-V position for all patient. A skin incision measuring about 15 cm was used via subcostal approach to have a good access to the renal hilum. All the nephrectomies were left side. A 10 to 15 cm length of the ureter was dissected and transected to accompany the renal allograft. Control of the adrenal, gonadal and lumbar veins (when present) was done using a 3/0 prolene sutures. The renal artery and vein are dissected clean at the hilum with enough length to ease anastomosis in the recipient. Ligation of the renal hilar artery and vein were performed using a large polymer hem-o-lok ligation clips or 2 vicryl sutures completed by 3/0 prolene. The excised kidney was immediately immersed in ice and perfused with 500 mL to 1 L of cold perfusion of IGL fluid (0-4 °C) till the effluent from the renal vein was clear and devoid of blood.

The mean follow-up of patients for our cohort was 27 months. Post-operative monitoring of the graft included daily ultrasound examinations for the first week, along with routine biological testing and immunosuppressant drug level monitoring when necessary. We measured serum creatinine levels using the Jaffe method, estimated glomerular filtration rate using the CKD-EPI 2021 equation. Following transplantation, serum creatinine levels were measured daily during the first two weeks, twice weekly over the subsequent two months, and weekly throughout the third month. Induction therapy consisted of either basiliximab or Thymoglobulin, combined with methylprednisolone. The dosing regimens were as follows: basiliximab was administered at a dose of 20 mg on day 0 (day of transplantation) and 20 mg on day 4. Thymoglobulin was administered at a dose of 1.5 mg/kg on day 0, followed by 1.5 mg/kg on days 1 and 2. Two immunosuppressive agents were utilized in this cohort. Cyclosporine was initially prescribed, as it was the only agent available at the onset of the program and was more affordable.

Beginning with the seventh patient, tacrolimus was introduced when financially accessible to patients, particularly in cases characterized by lower HLA compatibility and higher levels of anti-HLA antibodies. Notably, the first recipient did not receive corticosteroid therapy. The remaining sixteen recipients were treated with corticosteroids, which were discontinued at six months post-transplantation. The corticosteroid regimen consisted of prednisolone administered as follows: day 0, 500 mg; day 1, 250 mg; day 2, 125 mg; day 3, 80 mg; day 4, 40 mg; days 5–14, 20 mg/day; days 15–30, 15 mg/day; days 30–60, 10 mg/day; and days 60–180, 7.5 mg/day, followed by discontinuation at day 180.

Maintenance immunosuppressive therapy included tacrolimus (0.075 mg/kg twice daily), cyclosporine (4 mg/kg/day), mycophenolate mofetil (1 g twice daily), and azathioprine (100 mg/day). All dosages were subsequently adjusted according to target trough levels. Target trough concentrations during the first three months were 10–12 ng/mL for tacrolimus and 150–200 ng/mL for cyclosporine.

The was no valganciclovir prophylaxis available during our studies for financial reason, so therefore antibiotic prophylaxis with co-trimoxazole was administered for six months. We made a significant observation, that all our recipients in our cohort were IgG-positive recipient (R+) and an IgG-positive donor (D+) for Cytomegalovirus (CMV) so there was no mismatch between donors and recipients, hence no antiviral prophylaxis was given.

Results

The history

The initiative for kidney transplantation was first proposed during the inauguration of the hemodialysis unit at Yaoundé General Hospital (YGH) in 2002. However, the project only came to fruition in 2019 following the appointment of a new top management team at the hospital. Originally scheduled for May 2020, the first kidney transplant was postponed due to COVID-19 restrictions. The first transplant operation was eventually carried out on November 10, 2021. This milestone was achieved thanks to the commitment of the dedicated transplantation team, the strong involvement of the first selected donor-recipient pair, the renovation of technical facilities (including the operating room, imaging unit, intensive care unit, and laboratory), and the restructuring of hospital departments internal medicine and surgery were further divided into subspecialties.

Following the initial cases, the local medical team became fully autonomous in all aspects of the procedure, including pre-operative assessments, surgery, and post-operative follow-up. Since the law regulating organ and tissue donation had remained dormant, an institutional ethics committee was established in 2019 by Administrative Board Resolution No. 1955/019 [1]. Its members were appointed the same year under Resolution No. 1956/019 [2]. In 2022, the transplantation team was officially formed by Decision No. 728/022 to oversee and formalize the procedures. In 2024, a new draft law on cell and organ transplantation was initiated and approved by the relevant departments of the Ministry of Public Health and the Prime Minister’s Office. The draft has since been forwarded to the Presidency of the Republic and is expected to be transmitted to the National Assembly for promulgation.

Post-operative outcomes and complications

The post-operative course was uneventful in 10 cases. Two initial surgical complications were observed and successfully managed; Massive uroperitone um due to inadvertent implantation of the ureter into the peritoneum. Massive bleeding without vascular leakage, requiring surgical revision, clot removal, and blood transfusion.

Other complications included:

Lymphocele causing ureteral compression and elevated creatinine levels, treated via laparoscopic (caelioscopic) fenestration into the peritoneal cavity.

One male patient developed rapidly worsening kidney function five days’ post-surgery, despite an initial improvement. A graft biopsy revealed signs of acute rejection. Since no donor-specific antibodies were detected, the result was unexpected. The recipients received pulse therapy consisting of three consecutive daily doses of 500 mg methylprednisolone, followed by oral prednisolone at 80 mg/day for one week, after which the dose was gradually tapered, following this appropriate treatment, the patient showed clinical improvement with no further evidence of rejection.

Among donors, one case of massive intraoperative bleeding due to vascular injury occurred and was corrected immediately. No other early or long-term complications were observed in any donor. Three patients developed upper urinary tract infections within the first three months’ post-transplantation, with recurrence in two cases. No deaths have been recorded, and post-operative quality of life has remained excellent to date. It is unlikely that the observed surgical complications were attributable to limited operative experience, as the surgical team had more than 20 years of experience. Rather, these events may be partly explained by the contextual pressures associated with the initiation of a new program. Notably, these issues were subsequently addressed, and all subsequent patients underwent transplantation without surgical complications.

Cost analysis

Annual cost of hemodialysis per patient is $13,581 USD (equivalent to 7,926,716 FCFA). Cost breakdown was: dialysis consumables: 87.7% – $11,910.5 USD/6,951,729.9 FCFA, transportation: 5.7% – $774.1 USD/451,822.8 FCFA and productivity loss (patient and caregiver): 6.6% – $896.4 USD/523,163.3 FCFA. Average cost of transplantation per donor-recipient pair: $10,530 USD or 6,073,720 FCFA. The Initial first-year transplantation cost was $19,070 USD or 11,004,069 FCFA. 96% of this cost was allocated to pharmaceutical (non-generic) immunosuppressive drugs, and 4% to laboratory testing. Annual follow-up costs stand at: pharmaceutical drugs: ∼12,000,000 FCF and using generic drugs: ∼1,200,000 FCFA. The preoperative work-up, surgery, and hospitalization costs for a transplant procedure are comparable to the annual cost of hemodialysis. This transplantation cost represents the actual cost per patient. Similar findings have also been reported by [3].

Discussion

Chronic kidney disease (CKD) is becoming a major public health concern in Sub-Saharan African countries, with an estimated prevalence of 10% [4]. In urban areas, prevalence ranges from 10% to 14% [5]. With ongoing lifestyle changes, the epidemiology of CKD is increasingly resembling that observed in developed countries, where diabetes mellitus, hypertension, and chronic glomerulonephritis are the most frequent causes.

In Cameroon, dialysis has been the only available treatment for end-stage kidney disease [6]. They continued to report that, despite government support covering approximately 95% of dialysis costs, the discontinuation rate remains alarmingly high estimated at 84% in Sub-Saharan Africa. This is largely due to patients’ financial constraints, as well as systemic issues such as poor equipment maintenance, shortage of machines, and limited availability of dialysis supplies. As highlighted by Halle et al. in 2015 and 2016, [7,8] the mortality rate among dialysis patients remains high, primarily due to inadequate dialysis, catheter-related sepsis, and complications arising from emergency initiation of hemodialysis.

Organ and graft donation remain poorly developed and largely enigmatic in many African countries. These procedures are only performed in a few Sub-Saharan nations, including Sudan, Seychelles, Ivory Coast, Namibia, Nigeria, Kenya, Ghana, Tanzania, Mauritius, Ethiopia, and South Africa [9–11]. Among Francophone countries, Cameroon and Ivory Coast are pioneers in this field. From Table 4 below, we can observe that local management of transplantation was less expensive compared to procedures performed abroad [3]. The overall cost of management decreased year by year. Post-operative stays in the intensive care unit were reduced from 72 h to 24 h in uncomplicated cases, and hospitalized patients were moved from individual rooms to shared wards. The use of specialty immunosuppressants was later replaced with high-quality generic alternatives. We also acquired well-performing laboratory equipment, eliminating the need to send biological samples abroad for analysis. The training of our local surgical team contributed significantly to cost reduction, as demonstrated by the last three procedures, which were performed independently by the local team without foreign assistance.

During of transplantation program, treatment was initially initiated with the non-generic medication due to the absence of generic alternatives. However, they were costly and rarely available in local pharmacies. Once generic medications previously used in transplantation programs in other countries with demonstrated efficacy were identified and procured, an immediate transition was undertaken. These generic formulations exhibited satisfactory quality and efficacy, less costly and their improved affordability and accessibility facilitated consistent procurement. Costs are expected to continue decreasing with the ongoing use of generic immunosuppressant’s and interventions conducted entirely by our local team.

The excellent results achieved at the Yaoundé General Hospital, as reported by Njamnshi et al. in 2023, demonstrate that kidney transplantation is feasible in our setting. It is a safe and effective treatment and may become a viable and sustainable option for carefully selected patients with kidney failure.

For the sustainability of this initiative, both psychosocial and financial barriers must be addressed. Fortunately, this project has helped push lawmakers to finally adopt the law on organ donation and transplantation.

Misinformation surrounding transplantation must be eradicated. Although local transplantation is more cost-effective than both hemodialysis and surgeries performed abroad (3), the cost remains unaffordable for most patients.

Financial support is essential to ensure equitable access to treatment, and immunosuppressive therapy must be both accessible and consistently available.

During our transplantation program, we encounter some challenges. From an organizational perspective, several difficulties were encountered, particularly regarding the performance of compatibility testing and renal biopsy analyses. These limitations necessitated collaboration with external laboratories abroad. In addition, procurement of immunosuppressive agents was initially challenging. Following the first cases, local capacity was progressively developed, enabling the establishment of in-country laboratory services and the training of laboratory personnel. From a social standpoint, significant barriers were observed in encouraging voluntary organ donation, particularly in persuading individuals to donate an organ to a family member without financial compensation. Maintaining and improving standard procedures including surgical techniques, immunosuppressive management, follow-up protocols, and adherence to ethical principles to prevent organ commercialization remained an ongoing priority. Finally, financial sustainability represented a major challenge. In the absence of health insurance coverage, all costs were borne by patients and the hospital, limiting the scalability and long-term viability of the program.

Conclusion

Kidney transplantation is a viable treatment option for end-stage kidney disease in Sub-Saharan African countries. When performed under well-defined and controlled conditions, it is a safe and effective procedure. Its cost-effectiveness compared to both hemodialysis and transplantation performed abroad makes it an attractive solution, with challenges that are within reach.

For long-term sustainability, several key actions are needed: establishment of a national transplantation team composed of qualified local experts, international collaboration to support specialized laboratory testing, in cluding HLA typing and HLA antibody detection, validation of patients through an adapted yet standardized protocol, using a multidisciplinary and multicenter approach and a government participation in covering the cost of treatment, similar to the support provided for patients referred abroad.
