Osteogenic and antibacterial effects of double antibiotic-loaded microspheres
Authors: Zhenchao Xu, Xiyang Wang, Guanghui Zhu, Zheng Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Infected bone defects present a significant clinical challenge, and the limitations of systemic antibiotic therapy necessitate a method for localized drug delivery to treat such wounds. In this study, vancomycin (Van)- and rifampin (Rif)-loaded poly(lactic-co-glycolic acid) (PLGA)/poly-l-lactic acid (PLLA) core–shell microspheres were prepared using coaxial electrospray technology for sequential release of the two antibiotics. Two formulations were fabricated with the drugs distributed in either the core or shell (Van/Rif and Rif/Van microspheres). Comprehensive microsphere characterization included evaluation of their morphology, drug encapsulation efficiency, and in vitro release kinetics. Biocompatibility was evaluated using MC3T3-E1 osteoblasts through adhesion, proliferation, and osteogenic differentiation assays. Antibacterial efficacy was assessed against Staphylococcus aureus in both planktonic and biofilm states in vitro and in a rat subcutaneous infection model in vivo. The results showed that uniformly sized, smooth-surfaced core–shell microspheres were successfully prepared. The Van/Rif microspheres (vancomycin in shell, rifampin in core) demonstrated superior synchronous release kinetics compared with the Rif/Van microspheres. Both formulations exhibited excellent biocompatibility, with no adverse effects on osteoblast viability, adhesion, proliferation, or differentiation. In vitro, both formulations achieved a high antibacterial rate (> 95%) against planktonic S. aureus and prevented biofilm formation. In vivo, both microsphere formulations significantly promoted wound healing, reduced bacterial load and neutrophil infiltration, and maintained high local antibiotic concentrations without systemic drug detection. In conclusion, coaxial electrospray-fabricated, dual antibiotic-loaded, core–shell microspheres provided controlled sequential drug release, excellent osteocompatibility, and potent localized antibacterial activity, representing a highly promising strategy for the treatment of bone infections.

Introduction

Infected bone defects have long been a major problem in healthcare worldwide. Despite significant surgical advances, treatment for bone infections remains intractable, creating a serious challenge in orthopedic care. According to a study in the United States, the incidence of infection after internal fixation of fractures is approximately 5%, and the infection rate after internal fixation of open fractures specifically is as high as 27%1. The reported incidence of postoperative infection among patients undergoing total hip arthroplasty has ranged from 0.2% to 2.2%, with a reported incidence of deep surgical site infection of 0.51%2,3. These rates correspond to approximately 110,000 new cases of bone infection in the United States each year, for which the average treatment cost is approximately $150,000 per case2. Infection occurring with implant placement is an important factor that increases the morbidity and even mortality of postoperative infection and generates great economic pressure.

In some cases, systemic antibiotic therapy can effectively treat bone infection, but remaining necrotic tissues, such as dead bone, sclerotic bone, and pus at the infection site, cause poor blood supply in the lesion area. Systemic administration of antibiotics can often only achieve low local drug concentrations at the lesion site, and maintaining a sufficient drug concentration exceeding the minimum inhibitory concentration at the lesion for a long time is difficult4. In addition, poor compliance by patients results in irregular medication intake and, thus, insufficient treatment, potentially leading to the eventual emergence of drug-resistant bacteria and even the failure of anti-infection therapy. Moreover, long-term antibiotic use can cause damage to liver and kidney function. Therefore, new treatment methods are needed for bone infection that not only treat the infection and reduce the occurrence of complications but also prevent infection recurrence5.

In view of the limitations of long-term intravenous or oral antibiotic therapy, a variety of drug-loaded, sustained-release materials have been developed6–8. Core-shell microspheres are one of the most promising options for sequential drug release, based on their ability to deliver multiple drugs and their easy integration into a variety of scaffold matrices. Moreover, the drug carrier material can be specifically selected to control the release time and speed of drug delivery, which is of great significance in the microsphere sustained release system9. The polymeric materials poly(lactic-co-glycolic acid) (PLGA) and poly-l-lactic acid (PLLA) have been widely studied as drug carriers due to their stability in vivo, lack of tissue toxicity, and good biocompatibility. In addition, PLGA and PLLA offer the characteristics of biodegradability and controllable degradation, making them valuable materials for the preparation of core–shell microspheres10. Some studies have shown that the drug concentration released by antibiotic-loaded PLGA sustained-release systems is higher than that released by antibiotic-loaded bone cement, and antibiotic-loaded PLGA sustained-release systems persist for longer than antibiotic-loaded bone cement in an animal model of chronic osteomyelitis, with no adverse effect on bone healing at the lesion site11,12.

The diameter of microspheres is a key parameter to be considered. Large microspheres are easily retained in porous scaffolds with complex three-dimensional structures, hindering the uniform release of the encapsulated drug in the lesion area. However, nano-sized spheres are easily surrounded by host cells in the lesion area, limiting their action time in the area and weakening their biological effects13. For the preparation of dual antibiotic-loaded microspheres in the present study, we chose micron-scale diameters. Staphylococcus aureus is the main pathogen of hematogenous osteomyelitis and bone implant infection and is responsible for 70–80% of all bone infections14. Therefore, as the antibiotics to be loaded into the microspheres, we selected water-soluble vancomycin (Van) and lipid-soluble rifampicin (Rif), which both have strong antibacterial activity against S. aureus. We prepared PLGA/PLLA core–shell microspheres loaded with Van or Rif in either the core or shell using coaxial electrostatic spray technology to achieve sequential release of the two antibiotics and evaluated the antibacterial effects and biological safety of these microspheres.

Materials and methods

Materials

PLLA, PLGA, Van, Rif, coumarin-6, dexamethasone, ascorbic acid, and sodium β-glycerophosphate were obtained from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA). Vancomycin hydrochloride (C₆₆H₇₅Cl₂N₉O₂₄) is a water‑soluble glycopeptide antibiotic. Its 5% aqueous solution has a pH of 2.5–4.5, and its pKa values are 2.18, 7.75, and 8.89. Rifampin (C₄₃H₅₈N₄O₁₂,) is a lipid‑soluble semisynthetic rifamycin derivative. It is very slightly soluble in neutral water, freely soluble in chloroform and DMSO, and soluble in ethyl acetate and methanol. Its pKa values are 1.7 (4‑hydroxyl group) and 7.9 (3‑piperazine nitrogen). Alpha Minimal Essential Medium (α-MEM), 0.25% trypsin solution, double antibiotic solution (streptomycin and penicillin), and fetal bovine serum were purchased from Hyclone (Logan, UT, USA). Cells of the MC3T3-E1 subclone-14 osteogenic precursor cell line and S. aureus (BNCC 186335) were purchased from the Cell Resource Center, Shanghai Institutes of Biological Sciences, Chinese Academy of Sciences, and BeNa culture collection (Xinyang City, Hebei Province, China), respectively. DAPI dihydrochloride, Actin-Tracker Green, a CCK-8 test kit, a Live/Dead cell staining kit, an alkaline phosphatase (ALP) detection kit, an ALP staining kit, a hematoxylin and eosin (H-E) staining kit, Giemsa stain, and Crystal Violet staining solution were purchased from Shanghai Beyotime Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Picro Sirius Red Stain and Alizarin red dye, as well as a kit for reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR), were bought from Beijing ComWin Biotech Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China). LB-agar-powder and tryptic soy agar (TSA) plates were obtained from BKMAM Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Changde City, Hunan Province, China). The SYTO9/PI Live and Dead Bacteria Stain Kit was purchased from Shanghai Maokang Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China).

The coaxial electrospray apparatus primarily comprised an electrospinning machine and a coaxial nozzle, as illustrated in Fig. 1. The outer sheath of the coaxial nozzle had inner and outer diameters of 0.50 mm and 0.72 mm, respectively, whereas the inner capillary featured corresponding dimensions of 0.20 mm and 0.40 mm.

Core–shell microsphere fabrication

Three microsphere formulations were prepared: (1) Van encapsulated in the core and Rif in the shell (denoted Van/Rif microspheres); (2) Rif incorporated into the core and Van in the shell (denoted Rif/Van microspheres); and (3) blank control microspheres not containing either drug (blank/blank microspheres).

The precursor solution for microspheres was prepared by dissolving 1000 mg of PLGA (MW: 40 kDa) in 10 mL of dichloromethane (DCM) and 1000 mg of PLLA (MW: 90 kDa) in 10 mL of ethyl acetate. For the antibiotic-loaded microspheres, Rif, being lipid-soluble, was added directly to the DCM solution containing PLGA and to the ethyl acetate solution containing PLLA. Specifically, 100 mg Rif was incorporated into 10 mL of the polymer solution to achieve a 1% (w/v) Rif concentration. Van, being water-soluble and insoluble in organic solvents, was first dissolved in 1 mL of double-distilled water at a concentration of 50 mg/mL. This aqueous solution was then mixed with the PLGA-DCM solution (10%, w/v) and PLLA-DCM solution (10%, w/v) at a volume ratio of 1:9. The mixture was subsequently subjected to low-temperature ultrasonic emulsification until a homogeneous emulsion formed.

Blank microspheres were prepared using a coaxial electrospray system with 10% PLGA and PLLA solutions, an inner/outer flow rate of 1.5 mL/h, and a voltage of 10 kV. After a stable Taylor cone formed, microspheres were collected in an ethanol bath 15 cm below the needle tip. The collected microspheres were washed three times with distilled water via centrifugation (175×g, 60 s), flash-frozen at − 80 °C, lyophilized for 72 h, sterilized with ethylene oxide, and stored at 4 °C. The same procedure was used for drug-loaded microspheres, although needle clogging occurred more frequently and required careful monitoring.

Observation of shell–core microsphere structure

The core–shell microsphere structure was observed via confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM). In the preparation of microspheres specifically for this analysis, a trace amount of coumarin-6 was added to the PLLA/DCM solution to achieve light-green fluorescence staining of the microspheres. The surface morphology and particle size of freeze-dried microspheres were observed via scanning electron microscopy (SEM). For this analysis, the samples were coated with gold for 60 s to enhance conductivity. Particle size measurement was performed with random sampling: for each microsphere formulation, two fields of view were selected, and the diameters of 10 randomly selected microspheres per field were measured.

Determination of drug encapsulation rate

Three replicates of each drug-loaded microsphere formulation (containing 20 mg of microspheres each) were separately dissolved in 1 mL of DCM using low-temperature ultrasonication. Then, 1 mL of phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) was added, and the mixture was vortexed for 10 min, followed by centrifugation at 2450×g for 3 min at 4 °C. The upper aqueous phase containing Van was carefully collected, while the organic phase contained Rif. To maximize Van recovery, this extraction step was repeated twice. The concentrations of Van and Rif were measured by spectrophotometry at 280 nm and 474 nm, respectively. The encapsulation efficiency was calculated as the percentage of the actual drug amount measured relative to the theoretical amount loaded.

Measurement of in vitro drug release

Three replicates of drug-loaded core–shell microspheres for each formulation (containing 20 mg of microspheres each) were placed in dialysis bags and immersed in 10 mL of PBS. They were incubated at 37 °C with shaking at 120 rpm. At predetermined time points (days 1, 2, 4, 6, 8, 11, 14, 18, 22, and 27), 5 mL of release medium was collected and replaced with an equal volume of fresh PBS. The collected samples were stored at − 20 °C for analysis. The concentrations of Van and Rif released from the microspheres were determined by spectrophotometry at 280 nm and 474 nm, respectively, and used to calculate the amounts of each drug released. The cumulative release percentages were calculated at each time point, and release profiles were plotted accordingly.

In vitro evaluation of microsphere biocompatibility

MC3T3-E1 cells were cultured in α-MEM supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum and 1% dual-antibiotic solution (100 U/ml streptomycin and 100 U/ml penicillin) at 37 °C under 5% CO₂ in a humidified incubator. The culture medium was replaced every 48 h. Upon reaching 80–90% confluence, the cells were passaged and expanded, and the third passage (P3) cells were used for subsequent experimental procedures.

For exposure to microspheres, P3 MC3T3-E1 cells at a density of 2 × 10⁴ cells/mL in 1 mL of culture medium were added to the lower chamber of a 24-well Transwell plate. In the upper chamber, 20 mg of the designated core–shell microsphere formulation was added. For the control group, an equivalent number of cells was seeded without the addition of microspheres. Each experimental group was prepared in triplicate, and the culture medium was replaced every 48 h.

For evaluation of the effect of microspheres on cell adhesion, P3 MC3T3-E1 cells were exposed to the microspheres in Transwell plates as described above. After 24 h, the medium was removed, and cells were washed with PBS, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 10 min at room temperature, and permeabilized with PBS containing 0.1% Triton X-100 (three washes, 5 min each). Actin staining was achieved using Actin-Tracker Green (diluted 1:100 in PBS with 5% bovine serum albumin [BSA] and 0.1% Triton X-100) for 30 min in the dark, followed by three washes. Nuclei were then stained with 1 µg/mL DAPI for 5 min in the dark. The cells were then washed with PBS, and coverslips were placed for imaging by confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM). Imaging was performed with the following excitation/emission settings: 405 nm/420–480 nm for DAPI (nuclei) and 488 nm/500–550 nm for Alexa Fluor 488-phalloidin (F-actin).

For evaluation of the effect of microspheres on cell viability, P3 MC3T3-E1 cells were exposed to the microspheres in Transwell plates as described above. Live/dead cell staining was performed on days 1 and 3 of exposure to microspheres by first preparing a 1× Assay Buffer working solution through a 10-fold dilution of 10× Assay Buffer with deionized water. Room-temperature Calcein-AM (2 mM) and propidium iodide (PI, 1.5 mM) stock solutions were used to prepare a fresh staining solution by adding 5 µL Calcein-AM and 15 µL PI to 5 mL of the 1× Assay Buffer; this solution was used immediately due to the instability of Calcein-AM. The medium in the wells was aspirated, and the cells were rinsed three times with 1× Assay Buffer, followed by the addition of an appropriate amount of the staining working solution and incubation at room temperature in the dark for 30 min. Finally, the labeled cells were observed under a fluorescence microscope.

For evaluation of the cytotoxicity of the microspheres as well as their effect on cell proliferation, P3 MC3T3-E1 cells were exposed to the microspheres in Transwell plates as described above. On days 1, 3, 5, and 7, cell numbers in specific samples were analyzed by CCK-8 assay. Briefly, the CCK-8 reagent was diluted 1:10 with culture medium, and after removal of the supernatant from each well, 1 mL of the diluted CCK-8 reagent was added (ensuring no bubble formation in the wells). The culture plate was then incubated for 4 h. The supernatant from each well was collected and transferred to a 96-well plate for measurement of the absorbance (optical density [OD] value) at 450 nm using a microplate reader.

In vitro evaluation of the effects of microspheres on cell differentiation

For in vitro experiments of the effects of microsphere exposure on MC3T3-E1 cell differentiation potential, cells were exposed to microspheres in culture using 24-well Transwell plates with 8.0-µm pores. In each upper chamber, 20 mg of sterile microspheres (three groups) were placed, and 5 × 10⁴ cells were seeded in the lower chambers. Blank control wells received the same number of cells without microspheres, with three replicates per group. After 24 h in culture, the complete medium was replaced with osteogenic induction medium (10 nM dexamethasone, 50 µg/mL ascorbic acid, 10 mM β‑glycerophosphate in α‑MEM), with medium changes every 3 days. On day 7, the medium was removed, and the cells were washed three times with PBS before being fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 10 min. After aspiration, Sirius Red staining solution was added for 1 h. The staining solution was then removed, and staining of collagen fibers was observed under a microscope. Following three thorough washes with 0.1 M acetic acid, the dye was dissolved in an eluent consisting of 0.2 M NaOH and methanol (1:1 ratio). The optical density at 540 nm was measured using a microplate reader.

On days 7 and 12 of osteogenic induction, the medium was removed, and the cells were washed three times with PBS, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 10 min, washed three times again with Tris-buffered saline containing Tween 20 (TBST), and then incubated with an appropriate amount of ALP staining working solution at room temperature in the dark for 30 min. After removal of the staining solution, the cells were washed once with PBS and observed under a microscope. In parallel, after the same induction periods, the cells were lysed with 1 mL of PBS containing 0.1% Triton X‑100 per well. The supernatant of the centrifuged lysate was collected, and ALP activity was quantitatively measured using an ALP assay kit by reading the absorbance at 405 nm with a microplate reader.

After 21 and 28 days of osteogenic induction, the culture medium was aspirated, followed by three washes with PBS buffer. The cells were then fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde solution for 10 min. After fixation, an appropriate amount of Alizarin Red staining working solution was added to each well, and the plates were kept at room temperature for 30 min. The staining solution was subsequently removed, and non-specific precipitates were eliminated by rinsing with deionized water. Samples were examined under a microscope. Then, 10% cetylpyridinium chloride solution was added to each well to dissolve the Alizarin Red precipitates. After complete dissolution, the solution was transferred to a 96-well plate, and the OD value was measured at a wavelength of 620 nm using a microplate reader.

In vitro evaluation of the antimicrobial activity of microspheres

The antibacterial assay was performed using S. aureus (BNCC 186335), beginning with the preparation of LB solid medium by dissolving 25 g in 1 L of deionized water, followed by aliquot sterilization via autoclaving for 10 min and storage at 4 °C. Subsequently, the frozen stock of S. aureus was rapidly thawed in a 37 °C water bath. Under aseptic conditions in a laminar flow hood, 100 µL of the revived bacterial suspension was inoculated into the prepared LB medium and cultured overnight at 37 °C with shaking at 220 rpm. After 24 h of visible bacterial growth, a sterile inoculation loop was used to streak the culture onto TSA plates, which were then incubated at 37 °C for another 24 h to obtain pure S. aureus.

For exposure to microspheres, bacterial colonies were gently picked from TSA plates with a sterile swab, suspended in PBS, and adjusted to a turbidity-based standard before dilution to 1.0 × 10⁶ CFU/mL. Sterilized microspheres (20 mg per group) were placed into a 24-well plate, and 1 mL of bacterial suspension was added per well and cultured at 37 °C alongside blank control wells with bacteria only. After 24 h, the supernatant was collected and diluted 10- and 100-fold in PBS, and 100 µL of each dilution was spread onto TSA plates. After 24 h of incubation, bacterial colonies were counted using ImageJ software to calculate the antibacterial rate.

For evaluation of bacterial viability after exposure to microspheres, 20 mg of microspheres were placed in wells of a 24-well plate, and 1 mL of bacterial suspension diluted to 1.0 × 10⁶ CFU/mL was added to each well. Blank control wells were inoculated with an equal amount of bacteria without microspheres. Each group was prepared in triplicate. The plate was then incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. Subsequently, a 10-fold diluted SYTO 9/PI stain in PBS buffer was added, and the samples were incubated at room temperature in the dark for 10 min before observation under a fluorescence microscope.

For the inhibition zone assay, an aliquot of 100 µL of bacterial suspension at a concentration of 1.0 × 10⁷ CFU/mL was uniformly spread on a TSA plate using a spreader. For each experimental group, 20 mg of microspheres were precisely weighed and placed at the center of the TSA plate, with triplicate samples prepared per group. The plates were then incubated at 37 °C in a constant-temperature incubator for 24 h prior to observation.

For biofilm staining, sterilized microspheres (20 mg per group) were placed in a 24-well plate, and 1 mL of bacterial suspension (1.0 × 10⁷ CFU/mL) in LB medium was added to each well. Blank control wells received the same amount of bacteria without microspheres. After 24 h in culture at 37 °C, the samples were gently washed with PBS, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 20 min, and dried for 12 h. Subsequently, they were stained with 0.5% crystal violet for 10 min at room temperature, rinsed with PBS, and dried. The biofilm was then dissolved in 33% acetic acid, and the OD was measured at 575 nm using a microplate reader.

In vivo evaluation of the antibacterial activity of microspheres

The experimental animals used in this study were provided by the department of laboratory animals of Central South University. Thirty-two 8-week-old male Sprague–Dawley rats (300–400 g) were randomly divided into four treatment groups (Van/Rif microspheres, Rif/Van microspheres, blank microspheres, and blank control; n = 8 per group). Under anesthesia with 3% pentobarbital, a circular incision measuring approximately 1 cm in diameter was made at the corresponding dorsal location, and 20 mg of microspheres wrapped in gelatin sponge were implanted with 100 µL of S. aureus suspension (1 × 10⁷ CFU/mL) applied on top. Control animals received bacteria without microspheres. For all rats, the wound was covered and secured (Fig. 2).

Fig. 2Subcutaneous soft tissue infection model with S. aureus in Sprague–Dawley rats.

Subcutaneous soft tissue infection model with S. aureus in Sprague–Dawley rats.

On postoperative day 15, the rats were euthanized in a carbon dioxide chamber, and skin tissues surrounding the incision were harvested from each group for HE staining and Giemsa staining. Additionally, liver and kidney tissues were collected in the drug-loaded microsphere groups. The excised skin, liver, and kidney tissues were homogenized and centrifuged, followed by quantitative analysis of tissue drug concentrations using high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC). Skin tissue samples were weighed, homogenized in an appropriate volume of PBS, and deproteinized with acetonitrile. Following centrifugation and filtration, the supernatant was injected into the HPLC system. Separation was achieved on a reversed-phase C18 column (30 °C) using an isocratic mobile phase of PBS and acetonitrile (70:30, v/v) at a flow rate of 1.0 mL/min. Analytes were detected at 240 nm (Van) and 334 nm (Rif) using a UV-Vis or photodiode array detector. Quantification was performed using external standard calibration curves.

For HE and Giemsa staining, tissue samples were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 24 h, dehydrated through a graded ethanol series, cleared in xylene, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned. Sections were deparaffinized, rehydrated, stained with HE, and finally dehydrated and mounted. Similar standard staining procedures were followed for Giemsa staining.

Statistical analysis

All measurement data are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (x̄ ± s). Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 22.0 software, with one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) applied for comparisons among three or more groups. Differences for which the P-value was less than 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

Structural characteristics of dual antibiotic-loaded core–shell microspheres

As shown in Fig. 3, the microspheres prepared by coaxial electrospray exhibited a distinct three-dimensional core–shell structure with good dispersity and uniformity. The boundary between the shell and core was clear, and the surfaces were smooth without wrinkles or pores. Under optical microscopy (Fig. 3a), a uniform demarcation between the shell and core layers was visible. Under fluorescence microscopy (Fig. 3b), the coumarin-6–stained core was clearly separated from the shell. SEM observation revealed spherical, regular microspheres with uniform morphology and size, good dispersion, and no obvious aggregation (Fig. 3c,d). The particle sizes measured on SEM images were 14.9 ± 0.9 μm (Van/Rif group), 15.2 ± 1.1 μm (Rif/Van group), and 14.5 ± 0.9 μm (blank group). No statistically significant difference in size was observed among the formulations (P > 0.05), indicating that the spatial distribution of the drugs did not markedly affect the microsphere size (Fig. 3d).

In vitro drug release by core–shell microspheres

The release profiles of Van and Rif from the different formulations of drug-loaded microspheres at various time points are shown in Fig. 4. In general, the drugs encapsulated in the shell exhibited an initial burst release followed by a sustained phase, whereas the core-loaded drugs displayed steady release. From the Van/Rif microspheres (Van in core, Rif in shell), Van was released steadily, whereas Rif showed a higher initial release, achieving synchronized sequential release (Fig. 4a). In contrast, from the Rif/Van microspheres (Rif in core, Van in shell), Rif release was lower compared with that of Van, indicating asynchronous release (Fig. 4b). Both drug-loaded microsphere formulations achieved cumulative drug release percentages exceeding 80% within 30 days.

In vitro biocompatibility of antibiotic-loaded microspheres

Through dual staining with FITC-phalloidin and DAPI, MC3T3-E1 cell morphology following exposure to the different formulations of microspheres was examined by CLSM. At low-magnification (Fig. 5), clearly defined cytoplasm, plump nuclei, mature cellular morphology, and an interlaced network of cytoskeletal microfilaments were visible across all groups. High-magnification imaging indicated no significant differences in the green fluorescent microfilament structures among the groups. These findings suggest that the microspheres have no adverse effect on MC3T3-E1 cell adhesion.

Fig. 5CLSM observation of the cytoskeletal morphology of MC3T3-E1 cells exposed to different microsphere formulations.

CLSM observation of the cytoskeletal morphology of MC3T3-E1 cells exposed to different microsphere formulations.

After exposure to the different formulations of microspheres for 1 or 3 days, cell viability was assessed by Live/Dead staining. Fluorescence microscopy images of the cells showed no significant difference in the number of cells among the groups. After 3 days of exposure to the microspheres in culture, cell spreading was significantly increased, and the cells were closely connected. No obvious dead cells were found in any of the groups (Fig. 6a). These results indicate that the dual antibiotic-loaded core–shell microspheres have no effect on the viability of osteoblasts.

In further evaluation of cell proliferation during microsphere exposure, the CCK-8 assay results demonstrated continuous increases in cell number over 7 days for all groups (Fig. 6b). Moreover, no significant differences in OD values were observed among the groups at each time point (P > 0.05). These results were consistent with those from the Live/Dead cell staining analysis.

In vitro osteogenic effects of antibiotic-loaded microspheres

Sirius red staining results of collagen fibers from MC3T3-E1 cells exposed to the microsphere formulations after 7 days of osteogenic induction are depicted in Fig. 7a, with quantitative analysis (Fig. 7b) indicating no significant difference in collagen production among the groups (P > 0.05). These findings suggest that the different microspheres have no discernible effect on the early osteogenic differentiation of the cells.

The ALP staining results for each microsphere-exposed cell group after 7 and 12 days of osteogenic induction are presented in Fig. 7c. Quantitative analysis of ALP activity (Fig. 7d) at both time points revealed no statistically significant differences among the groups (P > 0.05). Alizarin Red staining of the microsphere-exposed cells on day 21 and day 28 of osteogenic induction revealed no statistically significant differences in calcified nodule formation among the groups (P > 0.05, Fig. 7c), as confirmed by the quantified results (Fig. 7f). These findings indicate that the microspheres have no discernible influence on the late-stage osteogenic differentiation, specifically mineralization maturation, of the cells.

In vitro antibacterial efficacy of dual antibiotic-loaded microspheres

The bacterial counts in S. aureus cultures after 24 h of exposure to microspheres were determined by plate counting (Fig. 8a), and the antibacterial rates were calculated. No significant difference in S. aureus colony numbers was observed between the group exposed to the Van/Rif and Rif/Van microspheres (P > 0.05, Fig. 8b, c). The colony count of the group exposed to the blank/blank microspheres was significantly higher than the colony count for either group exposed to drug‑loaded microspheres (both P < 0.01), but not significantly different from that of the control group (P > 0.05). The calculated antibacterial rates against S. aureus were 96.1 ± 3.2% for the Van/Rif microspheres and 96.8 ± 3.5% for the Rif/Van microspheres, with no statistically significant difference between them (P > 0.05).

These results suggest that both drug-loaded microsphere formulations possess strong antibacterial activity, consistent with the plate counting results.

The measured diameter of the inhibition zone for the Van/Rif microsphere-exposed group was 2.88 ± 0.25 cm, while that for the Rif/Van microsphere-exposed group was 2.83 ± 0.31 cm. No statistically significant difference was observed between these two groups (P > 0.05). In contrast, no inhibition zone was observed in the blank/blank microsphere-exposed group (Fig. 8e).

Biofilm staining was performed after 1 day of exposure of bacteria in LB medium to the different microsphere formulations (Fig. 8f). A continuous deep purple biofilm was observed in both the blank/blank microsphere-exposed group and the blank control group, with no significant difference in the quantitative results for these groups (Fig. 8g). In contrast, no apparent biofilm formation was detected in the Van/Rif microsphere-exposed group or the Rif/Van microsphere-exposed group, and the quantitative values for biofilm growth in these groups were significantly lower than those of the blank/blank microsphere-exposed group and the blank control group (P < 0.01).

In vivo anti-infection efficacy of the dual antibiotic-loaded microspheres

On day 5 post-infection in the in vivo rat subcutaneous infection model, the blank/blank microsphere-treated and control groups showed local redness, swelling, yellow purulent discharge, and marginal necrosis at the site of the wound, whereas the groups treated with drug-loaded microspheres exhibited no obvious swelling or exudate. By day 10, the wounds in the blank microsphere and control groups remained dark red with poor healing, whereas granulation tissue had formed at the wound site in the drug-loaded microsphere groups. By day 15, the wounds in the drug-loaded microsphere groups were fully covered with new skin, whereas those in the blank/blank microsphere and control groups still had exposed subcutaneous tissue and incomplete wound healing (Fig. 9).

Discussion

As one of the preparation methods for core–shell microspheres, coaxial electrostatic spray technology can produce nanometer to micrometer scale core–shell microspheres by spraying and atomizing charged polymer solution or melt in a high voltage electrostatic field. As the voltage increases, when the electric field force matches the surface tension of the droplet, a stable Taylor cone forms under the needle. Many studies have demonstrated the superiority of coaxial electrostatic spray technology for generating microspheres suitable for use in drug delivery, cell culture, catalyst carriage, and other applications15–17. For example, in the field of drug delivery, coaxial electrostatic spray-based core–shell microspheres are widely used for sustained drug release and targeted delivery. Many different core and shell compositions are possible. For example, the core material can be a drug, and the shell material can be a biodegradable polymer, etc. Encapsulation of a drug in tiny microspheres allows its prolonged release, thereby extending the therapeutic effect of the drug-loaded microspheres compared with direct drug administration. In addition, core–shell microspheres can be modified to recognize target cells via surface modification to achieve accurate identification and treatment of target cells. Choi et al.18 prepared microspheres using PLGA and alginate and demonstrated that the use of high molecular weight PLGA (HMW 27000) compared with low molecular weight PLGA (LMW 4000) could prevent early burst release of the encapsulated protein. Chang et al.19 used perfluorohexane as the core and polymethylsiloxane as the shell material, along with a coaxial electrostatic spray method to evaporate perfluorohexane to prepare single-hole hollow microspheres. They could adjust the diameter of the microspheres by varying the flow rate and concentration of the liquid, as well as the magnitude of the control voltage during the preparation. In addition, many studies have prepared drug-loaded core–shell microspheres with polymer materials as the carrier by coaxial electrostatic spray technology. These micrometer to nanoscale particles have obvious core–shell structures and can sustainably release a variety of target drugs to achieve stronger efficacy than the single drug, giving them good application prospects20,21.

In the present study, to increase the output channel of the solution, we connected a customized coaxial needle to the inner and outer pumps and then fabricated the microspheres with a core–shell structure using the coaxial electrostatic spray technique at high voltage. Two antibiotics with different properties were introduced in the solutions within the inner and outer tubes of the coaxial needle through the inner pump and the outer pump, respectively, to distribute the two drugs in the core and shell separately. The two drugs were simultaneously introduced into the coaxial needle at a set flow rate through an internal and external injection pump. The inner diameter of the inner needle of the coaxial needle can be designed according to the size of the required microspheres, and the outer diameter of the inner needle can also be set to adjust the size of the inner diameter of the outer needle. The outer needle, which is connected to the high-voltage transmitter through an alligator clip, has a higher voltage than the inner needle. When spraying, electrostatic repulsion is generated under the action of the high-voltage electric field. Under the action of the flow rate and electrostatic repulsion, droplets at the tip of the coaxial needle form a spray, and a Taylor cone is formed upon stabilization. With the continuous increase in voltage, the electrostatic repulsion sufficiently overcomes the surface tension of the droplet to break it, and the solvent is continuously volatilized in the process of spraying, ultimately resulting in the formation of solidified core–shell microspheres22,23.

In the process of preparing core–shell microspheres by coaxial electrostatic spray technology, it is necessary to consider the properties of the starting materials (such as surface tension, viscosity, etc.), the preparation parameters (such as electric field strength, electrode distance, etc.), and viscoelastic forces. These factors must be comprehensively controlled to obtain the desired structure and particle size for the core–shell microspheres24. Notably, the particle size distribution of microspheres is usually not very uniform and prone to fluctuations with the adjustment of various preparation parameters. Thus, it is very important to accurately master the preparation method for microspheres25. Through continuous exploration of the experimental conditions, our research group found that with a voltage of 10 KV, optimization of the diameters of the inner and outer tubes of the coaxial needle, and a liquid flow rate in the inner and outer tubes of 1.5 mL/h, core–shell microspheres with ideal particle size and structure can be prepared. In addition, the selection of ethyl acetate as a solvent to dissolve PLGA and DCM as a solvent to dissolve PLLA was based on the limited solubility of PLLA in ethyl acetate, which effectively reduces the mutual dispersion of the inner and outer tube fluids of the coaxial tip when spraying26. At the same time, the best effect was obtained with 10% volume concentrations of PLLA and PLGA.

The two antibiotics used in the present study were the lipid-soluble Rif, which is soluble in both ethyl acetate and DCM, and the water-soluble Van, which has little solubility in ethyl acetate and DCM. Accordingly, we first dissolved 50 mg of Van in a small amount of double-distilled water, and then added that solution to ethyl acetate or DCM to prepare a homogeneous emulsion. We found that the encapsulation efficiency of the drug embedded in the core was higher than that in the shell, which may be due to the loss of a small part of the drug on the surface of the microspheres in absolute ethanol during the collection process, and to the contact between the surface of the microspheres and the outside world during the retention process. According to the in vitro release curve for the core–shell microspheres, in the early release process, the drug concentration embedded in the shell was higher than that in the core, but the overall release profiles for the two antibiotics with different properties were essentially the same. In general, the release rate of the lipid-soluble drug was slower than that of the water-soluble drug. However, if the water-soluble drug is located in the core of the microspheres, the PLGA shell can slow the diffusion rate of the water-soluble drug, allowing the two antibiotics to be released synchronously and providing a pharmacokinetic basis for clinical application of the prepared microspheres.

Although detailed kinetic modeling was not performed, the release profiles (Fig. 4) provide valuable insights into the underlying release mechanism. Both the Van/Rif and Rif/Van microspheres exhibited a biphasic release pattern: an initial burst release of the antibiotic localized in the shell, followed by a slower, sustained release of the drug encapsulated in the core. This behavior is characteristic of a diffusion-controlled mechanism from a core–shell polymeric system, as previously described for PLGA/PLLA-based microspheres10,26. The initial burst is attributed to the rapid dissolution and diffusion of drug molecules located near or on the microsphere surface, while the subsequent sustained release reflects the gradual diffusion of the core drug through the intact polymer shell. Notably, the water-soluble vancomycin exhibited a slower release when embedded in the core (PLGA shell), whereas the lipid-soluble rifampicin showed a more sustained release when encapsulated in the core. These observations suggest that the release rate is governed not only by drug solubility but also by the permeability of the polymer shell and the spatial distribution of the drug. In the absence of obvious polymer erosion or swelling within the 30-day study period, the primary mechanism is believed to be Fickian diffusion. This diffusion-controlled sequential release is highly desirable for treating infected bone defects, as it provides an initial high dose to eradicate planktonic bacteria followed by prolonged antibiotic levels to prevent biofilm re-formation.

The biosafety and antibacterial efficacy of the prepared microspheres were carefully considered in the preparation of the core–shell microspheres in the present study. Because the organic solvents ethyl acetate and DCM used in the preparation process may not be completely volatile and could remain on the microspheres, we lyophilized the collected microspheres after washing them several times to fully remove these organic solvents. According to CCK-8 assay and cell death staining analyses, the drug-loaded microspheres had no significant effect on the viability and proliferation of osteoblasts compared with the blank microspheres or the control group. In addition, based on cytoskeletal morphology, the cells in all groups had a clear and orderly cytoskeletal structure with an expanded shape, indicating that the drug-loaded and blank microspheres had no effect on cell adhesion and suggesting their good biocompatibility.

To determine whether the prepared microspheres affected the differentiation potential of osteoblasts, we performed collagen staining, ALP staining, and Alizarin Red S staining experiments, the results of which were quantified. No significant differences in collagen, ALP, and calcium nodule production were observed among the cells exposed to drug-loaded microspheres, blank microspheres, or control conditions, indicating that the prepared microspheres did not affect the in vitro differentiation ability of osteoblasts. We acknowledge that the microspheres were not designed to actively promote osteogenesis; however, for a biomaterial intended for use in infected bone defects, it is critical to confirm that it does not impair native osteogenic differentiation or bone healing. Thus, these assays served as safety assessments rather than efficacy studies.

The bacterial adhesion process can be divided into two stages: primary adhesion and stable adhesion. Primary adhesion refers to the physical and chemical adsorption of bacteria to the host surface and is usually mediated by structures such as capsules, cilia, fimbriae, and surface proteins, which form initial contact. Stable adhesion refers to the accumulation of extracellular polysaccharides (EPS) by bacteria through surface attachment structures to form biofilms, and the interaction among EPS causes firm bacterial adherence to the host surface. This process is generally irreversible. Therefore, the rapid and effective killing of bacteria during the primary adhesion stage is key to preventing biofilm formation27–30. In the present study, the antibacterial ability of the drug-loaded microspheres was first tested using diluted S. aureus bacterial solutions and then by placing the microspheres on agar plates coated with different concentrations of S. aureus. The in vitro antibacterial efficacy results showed that both the Van/Rif and Rif/Van microspheres could fully release antibiotics during the early stage to effectively kill the adhering bacteria and prevent biofilm formation. Even with a large number of free bacteria in the environment, a strong sterilization ability and excellent antibacterial properties were observed. In vivo application of the drug-loaded microspheres in the S. aureus subcutaneous infection rat model further verified that the microspheres could effectively prevent bacterial colonization and growth. HE staining revealed greater numbers of neutrophils in the soft tissue of model rats treated with blank microspheres or no microspheres compared with those treated with drug-loaded microspheres, suggesting a greater inflammatory response. Giemsa staining revealed only a small amount of residual bacteria in the distant soft tissues where drug-loaded microspheres were implanted. Finally, the presence of the released antibiotics was detected in the subcutaneous tissue of the model rats 15 days after infection and treatment. Additionally, the antibiotics were not detected in the liver and kidney tissues of the animals, indicating that the locally released antibiotics did not undergo liver and kidney metabolism, and thus, likely have no effect on liver and kidney tissues. While our in vitro antibacterial assays were performed at 24 h, the in vivo data at day 15 confirmed sustained local drug concentrations and effective bacterial clearance. We acknowledge that a direct time‑course correlation between the 30‑day release profile and antibacterial activity at intermediate time points was not performed. Nevertheless, the cumulative evidence from experiments evaluating release kinetics, in vivo efficacy, and biofilm inhibition supports the conclusion that the core–shell microspheres provide prolonged antibacterial activity. Despite the promising findings, this study has limitations. The rat subcutaneous infection model does not fully replicate an infected bone defect environment. Additionally, only the antibiotic efficacy against S. aureus was tested, and long-term bone healing was not assessed. Our in vitro antibacterial assays were limited to a single time point (24 h) and a single bacterial species (S. aureus), and the in vivo experiment was assessed only at day 15. These single‑time‑point measurements do not capture the dynamic sequential release of the two antibiotics or differentiate the functional outcomes of the two release profiles. Consequently, the in vivo study was initiated based on a positive preliminary signal rather than comprehensive in vitro profiling. Future work should include time‑resolved antibacterial assays as well as longer‑term and broader‑spectrum in vitro testing to better predict in vivo outcomes. We did not include a free antibiotic control group; therefore, the relative advantage of the sustained‑release system over conventional bolus antibiotic administration cannot be directly assessed from the current data. In the in vitro release study, removing 5 mL (half of the 10 mL release medium) at each time point may have disturbed sink conditions and introduced errors in cumulative release calculations; a larger initial volume or lower sampling fraction would be more appropriate. Future studies should employ orthotopic bone infection models, include free antibiotic and single‑antibiotic controls, optimize release assay conditions, and perform time‑dependent antibacterial assessments to better validate the sequential release strategy.

Conclusion

Core–shell microspheres capable of sequential dual-antibiotic release were successfully prepared using coaxial electrospray technology. The resulting Van- and Rif-loaded microspheres demonstrated excellent biocompatibility in vitro, with no adverse effects on osteoblast adhesion, proliferation, or differentiation. Importantly, they exhibited potent antibacterial activity in vitro, rapidly eliminating S. aureus and effectively inhibiting biofilm formation. Furthermore, in an in vivo infection model, the microspheres showed good anti-infection efficacy by achieving and maintaining high local drug concentrations at the implantation site, with no systemic drug exposure detected. These findings collectively highlight the potential of this localized delivery system as a promising strategy for the controlled release of antibiotics in the treatment of bacterial infections, particularly in contexts such as infected bone defects, where sustained local antimicrobial activity is critical.
