1 ​ Technical Overview

1.1​ Problem Statement
 The two current, major projects at Commonwealth Fusion Systems (CFS), SPARC and
ARC, have different objectives and present a different set of technical challenges. SPARC is
intended to demonstrate CFS’s capability to achieve and sustain plasma conditions for Q>1, a
necessary, but not sufficient, milestone to achieve a commercially viable fusion power plant
(FPP). ARC is CFS’s FPP concept, wherein heat generated by fusion will be converted to
electricity, as shown in Figure 1. ARC is designed to operate at higher performance levels
compared to SPARC while maximizing component lifetime for economical energy production.
Thus, ARC materials will need to withstand a more extreme environment with more intense heat
and particle fluxes, and over a longer lifetime, compared to SPARC materials.
 ARC differs significantly in the plasma facing components (PFCs) design compared to
SPARC [12, 13]. SPARC PFCs are mechanically attached to the underlying steel structure and
will be cooled inertially, while ARC PFCs will require monolithic joining to the structural
substrate and be actively cooled by liquid FLiBe [12, 13]. The use of active cooling using FLiBe
requires the PFCs to operate above the melting temperature of FLiBe (459°C). A successful
plasma facing material (PFM) candidate for PFCs will have to satisfy several distinct

 1

requirements simultaneously, including having low tritium retention, low sputtering or erosion,
high thermal conductivity after neutron irradiation, high mechanical strength to resist cracking
after neutron irradiation, and in the presence of a high internal thermal stress, and maintain a
continuous bond to the substrate structural material. It is currently unknown which of these
issues will be the lifetime determining factors for ARC PFCs. For PFCs, we hypothesize that the
divertor region presents the biggest design challenge, due to expected high levels of particle
exhaust.
 For ARC, the overall goals of the divertor are known, but the choice of the surface
material and substrate material, details of the geometrical design, and allowable plasma
parameters are still under development. Despite this seemingly wide parameter space, a major
open question for CFS is finding a divertor design that meets the ARC performance
requirements. It is possible that the currently available material candidates will not be able to
perform under the ARC conditions for the expected duration, and a new materials development
campaign would be required. The goal of the proposed work was to assess which potential
lifetime limiting factors would be the most critical for ARC PFCs. Furthermore, the results were
intended to inform future materials research and development efforts. Meaning, the most critical
materials performance concerns for PFCs could be de-risked for ARC with levels of priority and
effort commensurate with their expected likelihood, based on the results of this study.
 For typical structural applications, material suitability can be determined with a suite of
well established, industrially relevant, and commercially available testing protocols. However,
for fusion PFCs there is currently no test facility that can replicate the simultaneous particle and
neutron fluxes, heating, stress state, and active cooling that materials will experience in a power
plant. Currently, experimental capabilities for fusion PFCs worldwide can only replicate a subset
of the conditions expected in operational FPPs, and no experiment can fully duplicate the full
spectrum of fusion experience over the expected lifetime. The simultaneous effects of these three
conditions cannot be replicated by testing a material coupon sequentially with the different
conditions. Moreover, many of the single effects tests that are available are time consuming,
have longer lead times, and can be expensive. Therefore, modeling tools can play an important
role in resolving the materials knowledge gap until a prototypic test facility is available and even
thereafter for rapid alloy screening.

 2

 This INFUSE program was designed to make progress toward to overarching objectives,
1) determine the suitability of unalloyed tungsten (W) and tungsten 3% rhenium with potassium
doping (W3Re-K) for the ARC divertor [5], and 2) establish which hypothesized degradation
mechanism(s) will have the greatest impact on PFC lifetime. An existing multi-scale modeling
methodology was used to simulate ARC plasma conditions on the divertor and model the
material response to the particle and heat flux. The alloy W3Re-K is a leading candidate for
ARC PFCs because it has shown superior behavior in neutron irradiation studies as compared to
W [5]. Unalloyed W is useful as a comparison because a considerable amount of data exists on
W in various fusion-like experiments and because W is more readily available commercially
than W3Re-K. The model provided a way to investigate simultaneous conditions of plasma
exposure, high heat flux, and neutron exposure that are currently impossible to test
experimentally. The plan was to use this modeling framework to perform a parameter scan of
three variables: thickness of the surface layer of the PFC, plasma parameters incident on the PFC
surface including heat and particle flux, and material selection (W3Re-K or W). The desired
outputs as a function of these variables included: temperature and stress profile from the surface
to the interface with the structural material, gross versus net erosion and redeposition, hydrogen
isotope retention, and polycrystal evolution and grain growth dynamics. This systematic
investigation of the model outputs and corresponding sensitivities was intended to determine if
there is a self-consistent solution for the ARC divertor with a W-based material, and to inform
the highest priority research directions for future efforts on the ARC PFCs.

1.2​ Work Scope
 The project proposed modeling the inner and outer diverter composed of W3Re-K or W
as the surface of the PFC to be installed in the tightly baffled X-point divertor configuration on
the ARC FPP [2,3] using a coupled multi-scale multiphysics modeling approach. The ARC base
plasma conditions previously published [3] provided inputs for evaluating the tungsten-based
divertor performance using an integrated PSI and crystal plasticity model. The divertor was
modeled as a simplified shape where the main geometric factor was the thickness of the plasma
facing material (not a full 3D divertor model), and the structural material that is bonded to the
surface layer was represented by boundary conditions in the models. The focus was on the
materials properties and changes in the plasma facing material; the details of the plasma facing
material to substrate structural material interface were beyond the scope.
 The approach to integrated PSI modeling involved performing a time-dependent or
steady-state evaluation of plasma parameters using UEDGE. Scrape-off-layer modeling provided
heat and ion flux, and temperature estimations for diverter surfaces. For the modeled ion flux,
F-TRIDYN, a binary collision code, was used to assess the sputtering and reflection yields, as
well as the implantation profile of plasma species. This information was mapped to Xolotl,
which then calculated the temperature profile by solving the heat equation, modelled the
evolution of the divertor surface (including gross versus net erosion and redeposition), and
modelled the sub-surface gas dynamics, which influence hydrogen isotope retention and
permeation. Xolotl is an atomistically-informed cluster dynamics model that simultaneously
solves the partial differential equations governing heat and temperature, along with the
reaction-advection-diffusion cluster dynamics governing the tungsten surface evolution and
sub-surface gas dynamics. F-TRIDYN was coupled to Xolotl using Integrated Plasma Simulator
(IPS) and collectively referred to as FTX [15].
 Following the cluster dynamics calculations, the results from Xolotl were coupled to
crystal plasticity and vertex dynamics mesoscale models of irradiation and
deformation-mediated grain evolution and dynamic recrystallization [4,6,17]. A plug-in module
within COMSOL was used to solve crystal plasticity evolution equations for polycrystals with
arbitrary texture, as shown in Figure 2 from previous work on iron. A goal of this project was to
produce figures similar to Figure 2 for W and W3Re-K. The crystal plasticity outputs were
overlaid onto vertex dynamics models [6] of grain evolution that were subjected to driving forces
generated by differential dislocation density buildup, capillary forces on grain boundaries, and
differential irradiation damage accumulation. These tools yielded the polycrystal evolution and
grain growth dynamics under in-service conditions, from which critical stresses, critical
temperatures, and critical doses may be obtained for use in device design optimization.

1.3 Results

1.3.1 Task 1. Develop prototypic plasma conditions to be integrated into PSI and
thermo-mechanical modeling workflow
 This study utilized previously published [3] ARC divertor plasma parameters. It was
assumed that ARC would operate in fully detached diverter conditions with ion and electron
temperatures of ~1 eV at the target, with seeded impurities. Specifically, the profile of edge
plasma parameters was taken from the Super-X Divertor simulation in [3] with Psep = 100 MW
crossing the separatrix, 0.5% fixed fraction Ne impurity, and a lumped D-T ion species with
mass 2.5 amu. UEDGE was used to calculate plasma temperatures and magnetic field angles (Ti,
Te and Bin, respectively), particle and heat fluxes (𝚪ion and 𝚪heat, respectively) and ion charge
state (Z), using these parameters. Perpendicular heat flux density onto the target plate included
plasma, neutral, surface recombination and radiation loading. This information was then input to
F-TRIDYN, which calculated the sputtering and reflection yields and the implantation profile of
plasma species (Ysp, Rrefl, and c(z), respectively). The result of Task 1 was obtaining plasma
parameters in the proper format for input into the F-TRIDYN program, and determining the
range of these plasma parameters that served as the ARC divertor reference ranges throughout
this study.

Microstructure evolution

We tracked the temporal evolution of the system and computed the fraction of transformed
surface area by discretizing the entire grain domain into a 100 × 100 uniform grid. A given
location within the grid was said to be transformed if a grain boundary (GB) swept over it at any
point in time. Each grid point is counted only once as transformed, regardless of whether it has
been swept more than once. The fraction of the swept area, fₐ(t), is then calculated as the ratio of
transformed grid points relative to the total number.

Figure 8 shows a sequence of snapshots of the microstructure in subset “1” from t = 0 until the
maximum transformation is achieved. Visually, the smaller grains in the lower third sector of the
image were seen to undergo rapid growth, relative to the rest of the substructure. Grains with
crystal orientations close to the [101] zone axis (colored red in the figure) appeared to undergo a
slower transformation.

 13

The evolution of fₐ(t) with time is shown in Figure 8f, showing saturation after 100 s of
simulated time. The simulations predicted that grain growth would begin instantaneously upon
exposure of W to deuterium at the temperatures of the first wall (Fig. 8a). Rapid growth persisted
during the following 10 s of evolution (Figs. 8b–c), primarily by consumption of small grains by
larger grains everywhere in the specimen. Subsequently, the system experienced slower growth
(Figs. 8d–e), likely due to a “locked” microstructure, which was limited by the slow growth of
only one or a few grains. This manifested itself as a bend in the growth curve between 40 and 70
s in Fig. 8f. The system is seen to saturate at approximately 100 s of evolution, with 80% of the
total area swept.

Last, we perform grain growth simulations at 1127oC for the microstructure shown in inset “1”
of the W–3Re alloy. The segregation of Re atoms to the grain boundaries hinders their motion
through a Zener pinning effect. In our model, this is represented by an additional migration
energy barrier compared to the pure W case. Based on density functional theory (DFT)
calculations [1], this extra energy contribution is estimated to be 0.58 eV. Consequently, the
effective grain boundary mobility parameter is significantly reduced due to Re segregation.

The simulation results are presented in Figure 12, where the swept-area fraction is plotted as a
function of time. The microstructure remains essentially stable for approximately 1 s before
noticeable boundary migration begins, indicating an incubation period that is extended from 0.01
s (in pure W) to 1 s in the W–3Re alloy. Overall, the grain growth process was strongly
suppressed by the pinning effect of Re at the grain boundaries.

W under irradiation

Finally, we extended our analysis to W and W3Re-K alloys under irradiation conditions. Prior to
irradiation, the material was subjected to a controlled pre-straining step to introduce dislocations
within each grain. These dislocations play a dual role. On the one hand, they act to reduce the
ductile-to-brittle transition temperature (DBTT), thereby broadening the operational temperature
window of W and W3Re-K under irradiation environments. On the other hand, dislocations
serve as efficient sinks for irradiation-induced point defects, such as vacancies and
self-interstitial atoms (SIAs). This defect absorption mechanism may mitigate the accumulation
of defect-driven free energy differences across grain boundaries, thus delaying grain growth.

To capture these processes, we employ a two-dimensional vertex dynamics framework to
simulate grain growth. At each simulation step, a stochastic cluster dynamics (SCD) module is
called to compute the evolution of irradiation-induced defect populations, including their types,
concentrations, and cluster sizes [8]. The resulting defect energy density is then coupled back
into the vertex dynamics model as an additional driving force term acting on the grain
boundaries. In this way, the interplay between microstructural evolution and irradiation defect
kinetics was resolved self-consistently.

Within this framework, in the future we may investigate the kinetics of irradiation-induced grain
growth as a function of three key variables: temperature, degree of pre-strain (i.e., dislocation
density), and irradiation dose. In addition, Re atoms are continuously generated in tungsten
through transmutation reactions under neutron irradiation. The presence of Re is known to
influence grain boundary mobility and may further alter the long-term stability of the
microstructure.

2​ Impact

​ 2.1 Use of Project Results
The research carried out through this project directly supports the CFS vision of systematic risk
retirement to bring a simplified, compact, and economically competitive tokamak power plant to
market in a timely fashion. Specific insights and accomplishments from this project that will
guide future modeling efforts at CFS include:

 1.​ Establishment of an integrated workflow going from edge plasma conditions to changes
 in material microstructure, which is readily modifiable for varying plasma and material
 parameters of interest, thus enabling further comprehensive assessment of PFMs.
 2.​ Confirmation of our hypothesis of negligible PFM erosion when plasma is operating in a
 fully detached mode, once steady state conditions are achieved. Thus, we are inclined to
 direct future efforts for quantifying erosion toward other sputtering mechanisms not
 considered here, and toward direct plasma sputtering under other operating conditions.
 3.​ Hydrogen isotope accumulation and diffusion are driven by particle flux instead of
 surface temperature or heat flux (and therefore depend on location in the divertor).
 Furthermore, D and T content were independent of changes to the bulk boundary
 temperature for the 600-800oC range considered in this study, since this temperature only
 had an impact on the surface temperature.
 4.​ Simulating the plasma in a continuous versus pulsed fashion had a negligible difference
 on the quantities of interest examined in this study.
 5.​ The presence of Re in W3Re-K activates the Zener pinning mechanism, which has the
 potential to significantly delay grain growth.
 6.​ Highly misaligned GBs plays an important role in grain growth, and will be important to
 characterize and understand in a tungsten-based PFC for ARC.

​ 2.2 Fusion Energy Impact
 The need for advanced, robust PFCs is a common need across the fusion industry even
though the design requirements vary between companies. All the currently used PFCs in fusion
experimental devices are universally unsuited to FPPs of any design. Arguably, the most
advanced PFCs currently developed and in production are the PFCs for ITER, but they are
specifically tailored to relatively low heat fluxes, very low neutron fluence, and using water
cooling. The majority of fusion companies in the US are not pursuing water-cooled PFCs, so
new PFC development is required. In the historical workflow for designing a fusion device, the
plasma parameters are set first and then the surrounding components and materials are selected

 19

to suit the plasma design. As the industry pushes toward FPP designs, the conditions on the
materials are too severe to simply select them at the end of the process. A new holistic and
iterative approach that considers modifications in the materials selection, component design, and
plasma parameters together is required. This project serves as an example of this shift in fusion
design philosophy by varying these three aspects to attempt to find an operating point. The
establishment of a more holistic, integrated workflow which incorporates the effect on material
microstructure may prove useful to any entity wishing to model microstructural changes to
tungsten PFCs subject to certain edge plasma conditions. Additionally, the specific findings on
W and W3Re-K behavior under ARC plasma conditions may be useful for other companies
considering those materials. W continues to be a leading candidate PFM for many fusion
designs, but it is known to have significant challenges. The addition of Re appears to be
beneficial for slowing grain growth by activating Zener pinning, and may be of interest to other
companies considering W as a first wall material. By incorporating these advanced modeling and
simulation tools developed in the fusion materials community directly into our selection and
optimization workflow, CFS will validate that these workflows are industrially-relevant and
provide guidance to developers on how to increase their utility in private fusion as a whole.
