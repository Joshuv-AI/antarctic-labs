Introduction

68Ga‐DOTATOC is a radiotracer with high affinity for somatostatin receptors (SSTRs), and its use in positron emission tomography/computed tomography (PET/CT) is an established molecular imaging modality for neuroendocrine tumors (NETs) [1]. SSTR expression has also been reported in renal cell carcinoma (RCC) [2], but its clinical significance and the clinical utility of 68Ga‐DOTATOC PET/CT in RCC remain unclear. Here, we report a case of postoperative bone metastasis from clear cell RCC in which 68Ga‐DOTATOC PET/CT was useful for diagnosis and for determining the therapeutic strategy.

Case Report

A 76‐year‐old woman presented with a palpable swelling and tenderness in the anterior chest wall. Three years earlier, she had undergone open partial nephrectomy for a left renal tumor, and histopathological examination revealed clear cell RCC (grade 2, pT1bN0M0).

Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) revealed a sternal tumor (Figure 1), and a sternal biopsy confirmed the diagnosis of metastatic clear cell RCC. The patient underwent complete resection of the tumor with reconstruction using Gore‐Tex, a sternal plate, and a pectoralis major flap. The surgical margin was negative.

Sagittal T1‐weighted magnetic resonance imaging showing a sternal metastasis (red arrows). A–P: Anterior–posterior direction.

Nine months after sternal metastasectomy, follow‐up CT revealed a soft‐tissue lesion posterior to the sternum, which appeared to have increased in size compared with prior imaging (Figure 2a). The CT findings were nonspecific, and the differential diagnosis included tumor recurrence as well as possible inflammation or infection.

(a) Axial computed tomography (CT) image obtained after sternal metastasectomy showing an enlarging soft‐tissue lesion posterior to the sternum, which appeared to have increased in size compared with prior imaging (yellow arrow). (b) 68Ga‐DOTATOC positron emission tomography/computed tomography (PET/CT) shows no significant tracer uptake at the retrosternal lesion (SUV max 1.8; yellow dashed circle). (c) CT image at the level of the Th8 right transverse process demonstrates no apparent abnormality (yellow dashed arrow). (d) Corresponding 68Ga‐DOTATOC PET/CT reveals intense focal uptake at the right transverse process (SUV max 16.5; yellow circle), indicating metastatic disease.

To clarify the nature of the lesion, 68Ga‐DOTATOC PET/CT was performed after obtaining informed consent for this self‐funded examination, because the procedure is not covered by public health insurance for RCC in Japan. 68Ga‐DOTATOC PET/CT was performed approximately 60 min after intravenous administration of 4–5 mCi of tracer per patient. Image interpretation was performed by board‐certified nuclear medicine physicians and radiologists using maximum intensity projection (MIP) and original PET images to identify tracer uptake.

No significant tracer uptake was observed at the retrosternal lesion (Figure 2b), whereas intense focal uptake was detected in the Th8 vertebra, which was not detected on CT (Figure 2c,d). This finding was considered highly suggestive of metastatic disease.

The patient received stereotactic body radiation therapy (SBRT) to the Th8 lesion at a dose of 35 Gy in five fractions. Eight months after SBRT, follow‐up 68Ga‐DOTATOC PET/CT revealed persistent tracer uptake at the Th8 lesion, indicating residual viable tumor (Figure 3). Based on this finding, a Th8 transverse process resection was performed.

68Ga‐DOTATOC positron emission tomography/computed tomography obtained 8 months after stereotactic body radiation therapy showing persistent focal tracer uptake at the Th8 vertebral lesion (SUV max 3.9; yellow circle), indicating residual viable tumor.

Histopathological examination confirmed residual clear cell RCC, grade 2, and immunohistochemical staining demonstrated positive expression of somatostatin receptor 2 (SSTR2). In addition, retrospective immunohistochemical analysis of the primary tumor and the previously resected sternal lesion also confirmed SSTR2 expression (Figure 4a–f). The postoperative course was uneventful, and the patient showed no evidence of progression for more than 20 months. The retrosternal soft‐tissue lesion detected on CT showed no progression and was clinically considered unlikely to be a metastatic lesion.

The primary renal tumor (a, b), previously resected sternal metastasis (c, d), and resected Th8 transverse process lesion (e, f) showed clear cell renal cell carcinoma morphology on hematoxylin and eosin staining (a, c, e) and positive somatostatin receptor 2 (SSTR2) immunoreactivity (b, d, f). Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first report describing the longitudinal use of 68Ga‐DOTATOC PET/CT in RCC for assessing tumor viability after radiation therapy with histopathological confirmation.

RCC is characterized by heterogeneous glucose metabolism, resulting in the low sensitivity of 18F‐FDG PET/CT [3]. 68Ga‐DOTATOC PET/CT targets SSTRs and provides receptor‐based rather than metabolism‐based information [1]. Reubi et al. reported that 72% of RCC specimens expressed SSTR2 [2], and Höög et al. confirmed SSTR2 expression in both primary tumors and metastatic lesions [4]. These findings suggest that SSTR‐targeted imaging may be feasible in a substantial subset of patients with RCC. In this context, 68Ga‐DOTATOC PET/CT may complement FDG‐PET/CT by visualizing biologically distinct tumor populations, including those with low glycolytic activity but preserved receptor expression. In this case, immunohistochemical analysis demonstrated SSTR2 expression in the vertebral lesion, supporting the biological basis for the 68Ga‐DOTATOC uptake.

Nakamoto et al. performed 68Ga‐DOTATOC PET/CT in 25 patients with recurrent RCC and reported a sensitivity of 88% and a 28% rate of previously undetected metastases [5]. Their findings highlighted the potential of SSTR‐based PET imaging to reveal clinically relevant lesions that may be overlooked by conventional modalities. Consistent with these observations, 68Ga‐DOTATOC PET/CT in the present case helped distinguish postoperative changes from possible recurrence, which were indistinguishable on CT or MRI alone. This diagnostic distinction allowed early identification of the vertebral metastasis and avoided unnecessary intervention at the postoperative sternal site.

Moreover, 68Ga‐DOTATOC PET/CT provided valuable information for treatment response assessment. Persistent tracer uptake in the Th8 lesion after SBRT indicated residual viable tumor, which was subsequently confirmed histopathologically after resection. Conventional morphological imaging, such as CT or MRI, can detect size changes but is limited in distinguishing viable tumor from necrosis or fibrosis after radiation therapy. In contrast, 68Ga‐DOTATOC PET/CT may be useful for evaluating functional receptor expression and could help in estimating residual disease activity.

This case also suggests the potential theranostic implications of SSTR expression in RCC. Although peptide receptor radionuclide therapy (PRRT) with 177Lu‐DOTATATE, is primarily used for NETs, it has also been investigated in other SSTR‐expressing malignancies [6, 7]. Therefore, 68Ga‐DOTATOC uptake in RCC may indicate possible applicability of PRRT in selected cases. However, the efficacy of PRRT in RCC has not been established, and further investigation is required.

This case highlights the potential role of SSTR‐targeted molecular imaging in lesion detection, as well as longitudinal disease assessment and therapeutic decision‐making in RCC. It should also be noted that in Japan, 68Ga‐DOTATOC PET/CT is not reimbursed by the public health insurance for RCC and, therefore, the examination in this case was performed as a self‐funded procedure after informed consent.

Conclusion

We report a case of postoperative bone metastasis from clear cell RCC in which 68Ga‐DOTATOC PET/CT played a pivotal role in diagnosis and treatment planning. This modality aided evaluation of possible recurrence, detection of occult vertebral metastasis, and assessment of post‐radiotherapy tumor viability.

68Ga‐DOTATOC PET/CT may serve as a valuable molecular imaging tool for assessing disease activity and guiding therapeutic decisions in SSTR‐expressing RCC. Further studies are warranted to clarify its diagnostic value and potential for theranostic applications.
