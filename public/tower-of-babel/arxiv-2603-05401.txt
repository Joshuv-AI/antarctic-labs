On spiral steady flows for the Couette-Taylor problem
Edoardo Bocchi, Filippo Gazzola and Antonio Hidalgo-Torné

Abstract
. We investigate the Couette-Taylor problem for a steady incompressible viscous fluid in a 3D cylindrical annulus, where one of the two cylinders is still, under both Dirichlet and boundary conditions involving the vorticity that naturally appear in the weak formulation. The outcome of this study is twofold. First, we explicitly determine all the solutions with a specific geometric partial invariance , which coincide with the so-called spiral Poiseuille or Poiseuille-Couette flows depending on the boundary conditions. Second, for small boundary data, we provide stability of such solutions, that is, no steady finite-energy perturbations are admissible. To achieve this result in presence of vorticity boundary conditions, we find a substantial analytical difference depending on which cylinder is still. Mathematics Subject Classification: 35Q30, 35B06, 76D03, 35B53, 34B24. Keywords: steady Navier-Stokes equations, cylindrical annulus, invariant solutions, stability.



1.
Introduction

A long-standing question about the steady motion of an incompressible homogeneous viscous fluid in a cylindrical annulus is the description of all the solutions to the so-called Couette-Taylor problem. Given
0<R_{1}<R_{2}
, we consider the 3D unbounded domain

\begin{array}[]{c}\Omega=\left\{(x,y,z)\in\mathbb{R}^{3}:\,R_{1}^{2}<x^{2}+y^{2}<R_{2}^{2},\,\ z\in\mathbb{R}\right\}\end{array}

with boundary
\partial\Omega=\Gamma_{1}\cup\Gamma_{2}
, where

\begin{array}[]{c}\Gamma_{1}=\left\{(x,y,z)\in\mathbb{R}^{3}:\,x^{2}+y^{2}=R_{1}^{2},\,z\in\mathbb{R}\right\},\quad\Gamma_{2}=\left\{(x,y,z)\in\mathbb{R}^{3}:\,x^{2}+y^{2}=R_{2}^{2},\,z\in\mathbb{R}\right\}\,.\end{array}

The steady motion of the fluid is governed by the incompressible Navier-Stokes equations

(1.1)

-\Delta u+u\cdot\nabla u+\nabla p=0\,,\ \quad\nabla\cdot u=0\ \ \mbox{ in }\ \ \Omega\,,

where one of the two cylinders is still while the other rotates, see Figure
1.1
.

Figure 1.1
.
The cylindrical annulus
\Omega
, with one rotating cylinder.

In (
1.1
),
u:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{3}
is the velocity vector field,
p:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
is the scalar pressure, and the kinematic viscosity is set to unit. As there is no source term in (
1.1
), the fluid is driven by the movement of one cylinder, which translates into some boundary conditions, see Section
2
.

Historical survey.
This is one of the oldest problems in the theory of Navier-Stokes equations.
So, in order to explain our contribution, let us first briefly retrace some historical facts.
Since 1890, thanks to Couette
[
8
]
it is known that, for slow rotations of the cylinders, the flow is rotationally-invariant and purely azimuthal.
In 1917, Rayleigh
[
31
]
studied the dynamics of revolving fluids in annular cylinders.
In 1921, Taylor
[
33
]
constructed an apparatus where the two cylinders could rotate independently.
Two years later
[
34
]
, he investigated the stability of the Couette flow and claimed that the no-slip
condition was the correct boundary condition for viscous flows at a solid boundary.
He also experimentally observed that when the rotational velocity of the moving cylinder is increased above a certain threshold,
the Couette flow becomes unstable and a secondary steady state characterized by axially symmetric toroidal vortices emerges. Upon increasing
further the rotational velocity of the cylinder the system exhibits more instabilities that lead to states with greater spatio-temporal
complexity, generating turbulence. The overall findings are nowadays called the Couette-Taylor problem
[
6
]
.

This problem attracted a great interest of
engineers in subsequent years, see e.g.
[
1
,
9
,
22
,
23
]
. In 1907, Orr
[
27
, p.75]
wrote that
it would seem improbable that any sharp criterion for stability of fluid motion
will ever be arrived at mathematically
. And, indeed, it was only more than 40 years after the Taylor experiment that the bifurcation problem
was tackled theoretically.
In the seminal paper by Velte
[
36
]
, combined with (independently obtained) abstract results by Zeidler
[
39
]

and Rabinowitz
[
29
,
30
]
, it was demonstrated that, for large data there exists at least another solution, still axially symmetric but also vertically-periodic;
see
[
40
, Theorem 8A]
-
[
41
, Theorem 72C]
and
[
35
, Section II.4]
for slightly different expositions.

The multiplicity of solutions goes far beyond the Taylor
experiment. Maz’ya
[
25
, Problem 67]
points out a classical issue about (
1.1
) in general domains: to show that uniqueness of
the solution fails for large data. Besides the already mentioned work by Velte
[
36
]
,
multiplicity results were obtained by Yudovich
[
37
,
38
]
, see also a generalization to
forced equations in bodies of revolution around an axis
[
10
, Theorem IX.2.2]
. For
planar flows
, multiplicity results are obtained in a strip
[
12
]
and in a square under

Navier boundary conditions

[
3
]
.

Multiplicity results trigger the analysis of fluid instabilities and turbulence.
In their monograph fully dedicated to the Couette-Taylor problem, Chossat-Ioos
[
6
, p.2-3]
noticed that
observers were astonished
to see the rich variety of patterns that occur, for instance, when the rotation rate of the inner cylinder is increased. … This model problem then appears as an example of a system that progressively approaches turbulence, which is still one of the biggest challenges for scientists and engineers in fluid mechanics
.
The flow visualization and spectral studies of the Couette-Taylor problem performed in
[
2
]
reveals a
surprisingly large variety of
different states, see also
[
6
, Fig.I.2,  p.5]
and Figure
1.2
.

Figure 1.2
.
Classification of flow states, taken from
[
6
, Fig.I.2,  p.5]
.

Depending on the angular velocities of the cylinders some “unexplored regions” appear. Besides, several of the “explored regions” are yet undetectable with theoretical tools. This is related to the following profound consideration
[
21
]
by Hinshelwood (Nobel prize):

fluid dynamicists were divided into hydraulic engineers who observe what cannot be explained and mathematicians who explain things that cannot be observed
.
This is well-illustrated by the story of the Couette-Taylor problem in which many different solutions were physically/numerically
found but only few solutions were mathematically explained.
A purpose of the present paper is to bring these two
aspects closer to each other.

Contributions of the paper.

Regardless of the magnitude of the data, we explicitly determine all the solutions to (
1.1
) with a specific geometric partial invariance, thereby not
entering in the “unexplored regions” nor in possible bifurcation patterns. Rayleigh
[
31
]
attempted to classify solutions to (
1.1
) in terms of the dependence of the four
unknowns in (
1.1
) with respect to the three
space variables. We give a rigorous classification in
Section
2
, in line with
[
2
,
6
,
14
]
.

In Section
3
we study (
1.1
) under
Dirichlet boundary conditions, by imposing that one cylinder rotates and the other is still. For this problem, explicit solutions are known, see (
2.3
)-(
2.4
) below, the
so-called
Couette-Taylor flows

[
20
, Chapter II]
. As already mentioned, there may exist other
solutions depending on the magnitude of the data or, equivalently, on the Reynolds number.
Our first goal is to build a class in which all possible solutions are explicitly determined, regardless of the magnitude of the data.
This is motivated by an observation by Taylor
[
34
, p.293]
who
wrote:
the complexity of the mathematical problem
arises from the fact that it is necessary to obtain a
three-dimensional solution of
the equations of motion in which all three
components of velocity vanish at both the
cylindrical boundaries
. Such solutions do exist and
are known, the so-called
spiral Poiseuille flows

[
14
,
24
]
, usually found by
imposing a constant pressure gradient and
z
-periodicity of the velocity.
These flows are the superposition of the Couette-Taylor flows with
a vertical Poiseuille flow produced by the pressure gradient along the

z
-axis. Instead, here we find them without these constraints but after introducing suitable
partially-invariant solutions
, see Definition

2.1
. In Theorems
3.1

and
3.3
we prove that the spiral Poiseuille flows are the only solutions to (
1.1
) in the (fairly
wide) class of partially-invariant solutions, regardless
of the magnitude of the data. This explicit derivation yields a Liouville-type result for (
1.1
) inside such class of solutions. Within axially symmetric solutions, such result is known without swirl
[
15
,
17
,
18
]
in the whole
\mathbb{R}^{3}
and, under magnitude constraints on the data, in a cylindrical annulus with further restrictions: in the subclass of
vertically-periodic solutions the only admissible is the Couette-Taylor flow (see, e.g.,

[
35
, Proposition II.4.2]
), whereas
for bounded velocity fields they are the spiral Poiseuille flows, see
[
19
]
. We emphasize that our results
are obtained without these restrictions and in the class of partially-invariant solutions. By restricting to sufficiently small data,
in Theorems
3.2
and
3.4
we prove
stability results, namely, that
no steady finite-energy
perturbations
of the (infinite-energy)
partially-invariant solutions are admissible. In Proposition
A.1
we analyze how the smallness condition in Theorem
3.2
is affected by the limiting behavior of the radii
R_{1}
and
R_{2}
. The proofs are obtained
through a combination of techniques typical of PDEs and methods from ODEs. Indeed,
as a byproduct, we obtain a result for a
nonstandard Sturm-Liouville eigenvalue problem, see
Corollary
3.1
.

The possible failure of uniqueness is the first step towards
turbulence
, which is well-measured by the
vorticity
of the fluid.
In 1827, Navier
[
26
]
proposed boundary conditions with friction, but it was only much later
that a first contribution appeared in
[
32
]
, followed by many authors whose list is so lengthy that we omit here. It turns out that these slip boundary conditions may be translated in terms of the vorticity, see Section
4
.
Starting from the work by Pironneau
[
28
]
, these conditions, which naturally appear in the weak formulation of (
1.1
), were rigorously studied in a sound
functional analytical framework
[
4
,
5
,
7
]
, with applications to fluid flows in a (simply-connected) network of pipes. Motivated by all these facts, in Section

4
we study (
1.1
) under boundary conditions involving the vorticity associated with the spiral Poiseuille flows. These boundary conditions also enable us to highlight a substantial analytical difference for the stability depending on which cylinder is still, thus giving theoretical evidence to a phenomenon experimentally observed by Taylor
[
34
, pp.291-292]
and clearly depicted in Figure
1.2
.

The so-called
spiral Poiseuille-Couette flows

[
14
,
24
]
are explicit solutions to (
1.1
) found after imposing periodicity of the velocity and a translational motion of the cylinders (sliding). On the contrary, here we find them imposing partial invariance and boundary conditions involving the vorticity.
When one cylinder is still, they are explicitly given by (
4.7
) and (
4.15
) below, in which an additional term with respect to the spiral Poiseuille flows appears, showcasing more freedom than with Dirichlet boundary conditions.

In Theorems
4.1

and
4.2
we explicitly find all partially-invariant
solutions to (
1.1
) under the vorticity boundary conditions on the moving cylinder.
In order to prove stability for small data, several Poincaré-type inequalities involving the curl of the velocity need to be proved, see
Lemmas
4.1
,
4.2
,
4.3
.
These inequalities are by no means foregone, see Remark

4.2
. With the first curl-Poincaré inequality,
in Theorem
4.3
we
prove stability of solutions for small data
when the inner cylinder is still. When the outer cylinder is still, the situation is even more involved,
due to the insufficient information provided by the
vorticity on the inner cylinder (concave part of the
boundary). We manage to prove stability for small data only
under an additional assumption: either for sufficiently “thin”
cylinders in Theorem
4.4
or by considering (infinite-energy) vertically-periodic perturbations in Theorem

4.5
, in particular in the
smaller classes of helical or axially symmetric solutions.

2.
Boundary conditions and classification of solutions

The geometry of
\Omega
suggests to switch from cartesian coordinates

3.
Dirichlet boundary conditions

3.1.
Explicit derivation of partially-invariant solutions with still outer cylinder.

Under the sole Dirichlet conditions (
2.2

2.1
) is

because
there are no restrictions in the

{\mathcal{U}}^{P}(\rho):=\frac{1}{4}\left(\rho^{2}-R_{1}^{2}-\frac{R_{2}^{2}-R_{1}^{2}}{\log(R_{2}/R_{1})}\log(\rho/R_{1})\right)\qquad\text{for}\quad\rho\in[R_{1},R_{2}].

)-(

)
1
. We emphasize that, differently from

\displaystyle 0\quad

)
1
, gives

u_{\theta}(\rho)=\frac{\alpha R_{1}}{R_{2}^{2}-R_{1}^{2}}\left(\frac{R_{2}^{2}}{\rho}-\rho\right).

By replacing (

\rho

p(\rho,z)=D(\rho)+E(z)

for some smooth functions

and

. Then, (

(3.10)

\frac{\partial^{2}u_{z}}{\partial\rho^{2}}(\rho,\theta)+\frac{1}{\rho}\frac{\partial u_{z}}{\partial\rho}(\rho,\theta)+\frac{1}{\rho^{2}}\frac{\partial^{2}u_{z}}{\partial\theta^{2}}(\rho,\theta)-\frac{u_{\theta}(\rho)}{\rho}\,\frac{\partial u_{z}}{\partial\theta}(\rho,\theta)=E^{\prime}(z)\,.

Since the left hand side of (

for some
\beta\in\mathbb{R}
.
Thus,

(3.11)

and,

\frac{\partial^{2}u_{z}}{\partial\rho^{2}}(\rho,\theta)+\frac{1}{\rho}\frac{\partial u_{z}}{\partial\rho}(\rho,\theta)+\frac{1}{\rho^{2}}\frac{\partial^{2}u_{z}}{\partial\theta^{2}}(\rho,\theta)+\frac{\alpha R_{1}}{R_{2}^{2}-R_{1}^{2}}\left(1-\frac{R_{2}^{2}}{\rho^{2}}\right)\,\frac{\partial u_{z}}{\partial\theta}(\rho,\theta)=\beta\in\mathbb{R}\,.

For
2\pi
-periodic functions
\theta\mapsto v(\rho,\theta)
, we restrict ourselves to the class

(3.13)

V=\left\{v\in H^{1}((R_{1},R_{2})\times[0,2\pi));\ v(R_{1},\theta)=v(R_{2},\theta)=0\text{ for $\theta\in[0,2\pi)$}\right\},

which is a closed subspace of

Indeed, after multiplying (

B(u_{z},v)=F(v)\ \text{for any}\ v\in V,

and

for some constants
C_{1}=C_{1}(R_{1},R_{2},\beta)
and
C_{2}=C_{2}(R_{1},R_{2},\alpha)
. Furthermore, using the periodicity with respect to
\theta
in (

\displaystyle B(v,v)

\displaystyle=\int_{(R_{1},R_{2})\times(0,2\pi)}\left(\rho\left|\frac{\partial v}{\partial\rho}\right|^{2}+\frac{1}{\rho}\left|\frac{\partial v}{\partial\theta}\right|^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\frac{\partial(w_{\alpha}v^{2})}{\partial\theta}\right)d\rho d\theta

\displaystyle=\int_{(R_{1},R_{2})\times(0,2\pi)}\left(\rho\left|\frac{\partial v}{\partial\rho}\right|^{2}+\frac{1}{\rho}\left|\frac{\partial v}{\partial\theta}\right|^{2}\right)d\rho d\theta\geq\min(R_{1},1/R_{2})\|v\|^{2}_{V}\qquad\forall v\in V,

that is,

{u}_{z}(R_{1})={u}_{z}(R_{2})=0,\end{cases}

Thus, by uniqueness, we infer that (

(rotational velocity of the inner cylinder

where we have set the additive constant equal to zero.

(b) If the second alternative in (

\rho

Theorem

also deserves two deeper comments,
one related to its physical consequences and the other related
to the theory of ODEs.

First, we stress that the velocity
\mathcal{U}_{\alpha,\beta}
of the spiral Poiseuille flows
in (

) have infinite
energy since they do not vanish as
|z|\to\infty
. The
velocities (but not the pressures!) of
the solutions (

) form a linear vector space
V_{\alpha,\beta}
although the equations (
2.1
) are nonlinear. The parameter
\beta
should
be seen as the mass density so that
{\mathcal{P}}_{0,\beta}
plays the role of the hydrostatic pressure, whereas
{\mathcal{U}}_{0,\beta}
has infinite energy. On the other hand, also the circular Couette flow (

)
has infinite energy (with no pressure drop). Moreover, unbounded cylinders do not exist in nature. Hence, the solutions (
3.2
) should be seen as (mathematical) limits of finite-energy solutions on

bounded cylindrical annuli
. The taller are the cylinders, the larger is the pressure drop, increasing linearly with respect to the height of the cylinders.

Second, as a byproduct of the proof of Theorem

3.1
, we obtain a result on the eigenvalues of
the following Sturm-Liouville-type problems

(3.19)

\begin{cases}\dfrac{d}{d\rho}\left(\rho\dfrac{dW_{k}}{d\rho}(\rho)\right)-\dfrac{k^{2}}{\rho}W_{k}(\rho)=-\lambda\dfrac{kR_{1}}{R_{2}^{2}-R_{1}^{2}}\left(\rho-\dfrac{R_{2}^{2}}{\rho}\right)W_{k}(\rho)\quad\text{in}\quad(R_{1},R_{2}),\\[10.0pt]

where

are complex-valued and the weight function multiplying

in the right-hand side of (

)
vanishes
at the endpoint
\rho=R_{2}
. This makes the problem singular and does not enter into the classical (regular) Sturm-Liouville theory. However, we obtain the following statement, which has its own independent interest:

Corollary 3.1
.

Let
R_{2}>R_{1}>0
. Then, for any
k\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}
, the Sturm-Liouville-type problem (
3.19
) admits no purely-imaginary eigenvalues

.

Proof.

When
\lambda=0
, the unique solution to (

) is

and, hence
\lambda=0
is not an eigenvalue. For

, since

in (

) is

-periodic, we expand it in Fourier series and using the boundary conditions (
2.2
)

,
we obtain that the Fourier coefficients

with

solve (

and

. Since the unique solution to (

and necessarily

3.2.
Stability with still outer cylinder.

We investigate here the existence of non-partially-invariant solutions to (
2.1
)-(
2.2
)
1
by perturbing
({\mathcal{U}}_{\alpha,\beta},{\mathcal{P}}_{\alpha,\beta})
, as defined in (
3.2
). We analyze the case when the perturbation has finite energy and we show that, if
\alpha
and
\beta
are sufficiently small, then the only allowed perturbation is the trivial one. To this end, we consider the space of divergence-free
vector fields

\begin{array}[]{cc}V_{0}(\Omega):=\left\{v\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)\ ,\ \nabla\cdot v=0\text{ in }\Omega\right\}\,,\end{array}

in which the Poincaré inequality

(3.20)

\Lambda(\Omega)\|v\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq\|\nabla v\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\qquad\mbox{with}\qquad\Lambda(\Omega)=\inf_{v\in V_{0}(\Omega)\setminus\{0\}}\frac{\|\nabla v\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}}{\|v\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}}

holds for some
\Lambda(\Omega)>0
. Let us introduce a constant measuring the magnitude of the flow
({\mathcal{U}}_{\alpha,\beta},{\mathcal{P}}_{\alpha,\beta})
when the outer cylinder is still, namely,

(3.21)

M_{\rm out}(\alpha,\beta):=\left[\alpha^{2}\frac{R_{2}^{4}}{(R_{2}^{2}-R_{1}^{2})^{2}R_{1}^{2}}+\frac{\beta^{2}}{16}\left(\frac{R_{2}^{2}-R_{1}^{2}}{R_{1}\log(R_{2}^{2}/R_{1}^{2})}-R_{1}\right)^{2}\right]^{\frac{1}{2}}\qquad\forall\ \alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{R}.

Allowing a
finite-energy perturbation of the infinite-energy solution
(
3.2
), we prove the following stability result:

Theorem 3.2
.

Fix
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{R}
. Let
\Lambda(\Omega)
and
M_{\rm out}(\alpha,\beta)
be as in (
3.20
) and
(
3.21
), respectively. Assume that
(v,q)\in V_{0}(\Omega)\times L^{2}(\Omega)
is such that
({\mathcal{U}}_{\alpha,\beta}+v,{\mathcal{P}}_{\alpha,\beta}+q)
solves (
2.1
)-(
2.2
)
1
. If

(3.22)

M_{\rm out}\left(\alpha,\beta\right)<\Lambda(\Omega)\,,

then
(v,q)\equiv(0,0)
in
\Omega
. In particular, the result holds if

M_{\rm out}(\alpha,\beta)<\max\left\{\frac{\pi^{2}}{2R_{2}^{2}},\frac{8}{(R_{2}^{2}-R_{1}^{2})\log(R_{2}/R_{1})}\right\}.

Proof.
Since
({\mathcal{U}}_{\alpha,\beta},{\mathcal{P}}_{\alpha,\beta})
solves (
2.1
)-(
2.2
)
1
,
the perturbation
(v,q)\in V_{0}(\Omega)\times L^{2}(\Omega)
satisfies

(3.23)

\begin{cases}-\Delta v+v\cdot\nabla v+{\mathcal{U}}_{\alpha,\beta}\cdot\nabla v+v\cdot\nabla{\mathcal{U}}_{\alpha,\beta}+\nabla q=0,\quad\nabla\cdot v=0\quad\text{in}\quad\Omega,\\[5.0pt]
v=0\quad\text{on}\quad\Gamma_{1}\,,\qquad v=0\quad\text{on}\quad\Gamma_{2}\,,\end{cases}

and, as mentioned above, it is a classical solution to (
3.23
).
Taking the
L^{2}(\Omega)
-scalar product of (
3.23
) with
v
, integrating by parts and using the divergence-free condition then yields

(3.24)

\int_{\Omega}|\nabla v|^{2}=-\int_{\Omega}\left(v\cdot\nabla{\mathcal{U}}_{\alpha,\beta}\right)\cdot v.

After writing in cylindrical coordinates

(3.25)

\int_{\Omega}(v\cdot\nabla{\mathcal{U}}_{\alpha,\beta})\cdot v=\int_{(R_{1},R_{2})\times(0,2\pi)\times\mathbb{R}}\rho v^{T}A(\rho)v

with the symmetric matrix

(3.26)

A(\rho)=\left(\begin{matrix}0&\frac{\alpha}{2}\left(\frac{{\mathcal{U}}^{C}(\rho)}{\rho}-\frac{d{\mathcal{U}}^{C}(\rho)}{d\rho}\right)&-\frac{\beta}{2}\frac{d{\mathcal{U}}^{P}(\rho)}{d\rho}\\[5.0pt]
\frac{\alpha}{2}\left(\frac{{\mathcal{U}}^{C}(\rho)}{\rho}-\frac{d{\mathcal{U}}^{C}(\rho)}{d\rho}\right)&0&0\\[5.0pt]
-\frac{\beta}{2}\frac{d{\mathcal{U}}^{P}(\rho)}{d\rho}&0&0\end{matrix}\right).

We know that there exists
\Upsilon(\rho)\in\mathbb{R}
such that, for any
\rho\in[R_{1},R_{2}]
, the quadratic form
v^{T}A(\rho)v
is bounded from above by
\Upsilon(\rho)|v|^{2}
. The optimal
\Upsilon(\rho)
is the maximum eigenvalue of the matrix
A(\rho)
, namely,

0<\Upsilon(\rho)=\left[\alpha^{2}\frac{R_{1}^{2}R_{2}^{4}}{(R_{2}^{2}-R_{1}^{2})^{2}\rho^{4}}+\frac{\beta^{2}}{16}\left(\rho-\frac{R_{2}^{2}-R_{1}^{2}}{\rho\log(R_{2}^{2}/R_{1}^{2})}\right)^{2}\right]^{\frac{1}{2}}.

Some lengthy computations show that

h(\rho):=\rho-\frac{R_{2}^{2}-R_{1}^{2}}{\rho\log(R_{2}^{2}/R_{1}^{2})}\mbox{ is convex},\quad h(R_{1})<0<h(R_{2})<|h(R_{1})|\,.

Hence, since
\rho\mapsto\rho^{-4}
is decreasing, we infer that
\Upsilon(\rho)\leq\Upsilon(R_{1})=M_{\rm out}(\alpha,\beta)
for all

\rho\in[R_{1},R_{2}]
, with
M_{\rm out}(\alpha,\beta)
given by (
3.21
). Combining this uniform bound with (
3.24
) and (
3.20
) implies

(3.27)

\int_{\Omega}|\nabla v|^{2}\leq\int_{(R_{1},R_{2})\times(0,2\pi)\times\mathbb{R}}\Upsilon(\rho)\rho|v|^{2}\leq M_{\rm out}(\alpha,\beta)\int_{\Omega}|v|^{2}\leq\frac{M_{\rm out}(\alpha,\beta)}{\Lambda(\Omega)}\int_{\Omega}|\nabla v|^{2}.

We see from (
3.27
) that, if
(
3.22
) holds, then
\nabla v\equiv 0
in
\Omega
and, due to the homogeneous boundary conditions,
v\equiv 0
in
\Omega
. An analogous argument also gives that
q\equiv 0
in
\Omega
.

The second statement follows directly from the (just proved) first statement provided that

\Lambda(\Omega)\geq\frac{\pi^{2}}{2R_{2}^{2}}\quad\mbox{and}\quad\Lambda(\Omega)\geq\frac{8}{(R_{2}^{2}-R_{1}^{2})\log(R_{2}/R_{1})}\,,

where
\Lambda(\Omega)
is as in (
3.20
). For the first inequality, we show that

\|v\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}\leq\frac{2R_{2}^{2}}{\pi^{2}}\,\|\nabla v\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}\hskip 14.22636pt\forall v\in H_{0}^{1}(\Omega),

which, in particular, holds for all

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4}\left(\int_{R_{1}}^{R_{2}}\left|\partial_{\rho}v(r,\theta,z)\right|dr\right)^{2}\leq\frac{1}{4}\left(\int_{R_{1}}^{R_{2}}\frac{dr}{r}\right)\left(\int_{R_{1}}^{R_{2}}\left|\partial_{\rho}v(r,\theta,z)\right|^{2}rdr\right)\,

4.
Boundary conditions involving the vorticity

4.1.
Derivation and connection with Navier slip conditions.

We start this section with an analysis of the vorticity associated with the spiral Poiseuille flows (

)-(
2.2

and

(4.1)

\omega_{\alpha,\beta}:=\nabla\times{\mathcal{U}}_{\alpha,\beta}=\frac{\beta}{4}\left(\frac{R_{2}^{2}-R_{1}^{2}}{\log(R_{2}/R_{1})\rho}-2\rho\right)e_{\theta}-\frac{\alpha 2R_{1}}{R_{2}^{2}-R_{1}^{2}}e_{z},

which is constant if and only if
\beta=0
. Let us focus on its azimuthal component

\frac{\beta}{4}\left(\frac{R_{2}^{2}-R_{1}^{2}}{\log(R_{2}/R_{1})\rho}-2\rho\right)e_{\theta}.

It vanishes at
R_{0}
, see (
3.18
), independently of
\beta\neq 0

\omega_{0,\beta}(R_{0},\theta,z)=0
for any
(\theta,z)\in[0,2\pi)\times\mathbb{R}
, over the
intermediate cylinder (of radius
R_{0}
) where the
the velocity
\mathcal{U}_{0,\beta}
attains its maximum value (in modulus), see Figure
3.1
.

In this section we partially replace the boundary conditions (
2.2
)
1
with conditions involving
\nabla\times u
. To determine them, we use the explicit form (
4.1
). Following
[
7
, Section 2.1]
,
we consider the boundary value problem

(4.2)

\mbox{\eqref{SNSEcyl}}\,,\qquad u\cdot\nu=0,\quad(\nabla\times u)\times\nu=\omega_{\alpha,\beta}\times\nu\quad\text{on}\quad\Gamma_{1}\,,\qquad u=0\quad\text{on}\quad\Gamma_{2}\,.

From a mathematical point of view, the boundary condition at
\Gamma_{1}
for the vorticity naturally appears when writing down the bilinear form in the weak formulation of (
2.1
), see Theorem
4.3
, using the identity
-\Delta u=\nabla\times(\nabla\times u)
for divergence-free vector fields. The physical interpretation of the boundary conditions at
\Gamma_{1}
is related to that of the Navier slip conditions. Indeed, in the cylindrical configuration we are considering, and using
u\cdot\nu=u_{\rho}=0
on
\Gamma_{1}
, the following relation holds

(4.3)

(\nabla\times u)\times\nu=2\left[D(u)\nu\right]_{\tau}+\frac{2}{R_{1}}u_{\theta}e_{\theta}\quad\text{on}\quad\Gamma_{1},

where the symmetric part of the gradient

D(u)=\frac{\nabla u+\nabla u^{T}}{2}

is the strain-rate tensor and
[f]_{\tau}=f-(f\cdot\nu)\nu
denotes the orthogonal projection onto the tangent plane to
\Gamma_{1}
. The second term on the right-hand side of (
4.3
) represents a geometric friction along the azimuthal direction due to the (horizontal) curvature of the cylinder
\Gamma_{1}
. As the radius
R_{1}
increases and the boundary tends to be locally flat, the friction decreases and the partial slip tends to perfect slip in the azimuthal direction, whereas the fluid continues to perfectly slip in the vertical direction. We stress that the different behavior depending on the different tangential directions is a consequence of the anisotropy of the cylindrical geometry. Introducing the
3\times 3
anisotropic friction matrix

\mathcal{F}=\left(\begin{matrix}0&0&0\\
0&2/R_{1}&0\\
0&0&0\end{matrix}\right)

permits to recast (

) as

(4.4)

(\nabla\times u)\times\nu=2\left[D(u)\nu\right]_{\tau}+\mathcal{F}\left[u\right]_{\tau}\quad\text{on}\quad\Gamma_{1},

and the boundary conditions at
\Gamma_{1}
in (
4.2
) as inhomogeneous Navier slip boundary conditions with anisotropic friction. More precisely, we obtain

(4.5)

u\cdot\nu=\mathcal{U}_{\alpha,\beta}\cdot\nu=0,\quad 2\left[D(u)\nu\right]_{\tau}+\mathcal{F}\left[u\right]_{\tau}=2\left[D(\mathcal{U}_{\alpha,\beta})\nu\right]_{\tau}+\mathcal{F}\left[\mathcal{U}_{\alpha,\beta}\right]_{\tau}\quad\text{on}\quad\Gamma_{1},\,

with boundary data written in terms of the spiral Poiseuille flows
\mathcal{U}_{\alpha,\beta}
.
Passing to cylindrical coordinates, the boundary conditions in (
4.2
) then read

(4.6)

\displaystyle u_{\rho}(R_{1},\theta,z)=0,\qquad\frac{1}{R_{1}}u_{\theta}(R_{1},\theta,z)+\frac{\partial u_{\theta}}{\partial\rho}(R_{1},\theta,z)=(\omega_{\alpha,\beta})_{z}(R_{1},\theta,z),

\displaystyle\frac{\partial u_{z}}{\partial\rho}(R_{1},\theta,z)=-(\omega_{\alpha,\beta})_{\theta}(R_{1},\theta,z),\quad u_{\rho}(R_{2},\theta,z)=u_{\theta}(R_{2},\theta,z)=u_{z}(R_{2},\theta,z)=0,

for

. Note that, differently from (

and

satisfy, respectively, a Robin and a Neumann boundary condition on

4.3.
Stability.

We discuss here the smallness conditions under which suitable perturbations of the spiral Poiseuille-Couette flows (

) and (
4.15
) are admissible.
To obtain the counterparts of Theorems
3.2
and
3.4
for the new boundary conditions, we need to show the existence of strictly positive curl-Poincaré constants, that is, we seek inequalities such as

(4.16)

C\left\lVert v\right\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq\left\lVert\nabla\times v\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2},

for some constant
C>0
and any
v
in some subspace of
H^{1}(\Omega)
. However, the next remark shows that it is not clear whether this is always possible.

Remark 4.2
.

For
0<R_{1}<R_{2}
and
K>0
, consider the 3D bounded cylindrical annulus

\Omega=\left\{(x,y,z)\in\mathbb{R}^{3};\,R_{1}^{2}<x^{2}+y^{2}<R_{2}^{2},\,|z|<K\right\}\,.

Consider the vector field

u(x,y,z)=\frac{y}{x^{2}+y^{2}}e_{1}-\frac{x}{x^{2}+y^{2}}e_{2}+0e_{3}\,.

Then
u\in C^{\infty}(\overline{\Omega})
and

\nabla\cdot u=0\mbox{ in }\Omega\,,\qquad\nabla\times u=0\mbox{ in }\Omega\,,\qquad u\cdot\nu=0\mbox{ on }\partial\Omega\,.

Therefore, under the mere impermeability boundary conditions, there are non-trivial divergence-free and curl-free vector fields, even in bounded domains. This implies that the constant
C
in (
4.16
) vanishes.

Hence, on the one hand, in our setting one can expect that the curl-Poincaré constants
C
in (
4.16
) might vanish because the domain is unbounded and not simply-connected. On the other hand, we stress that the cases with still inner or outer cylinder will be significantly different. Since the former is simpler, we study it first.

Still inner cylinder
. In this case, the space of perturbations of
\widetilde{\mathcal{U}}_{\alpha,\beta,\gamma}
maintaining the boundary conditions in (
4.14
) reads

(4.17)

\underline{\widetilde{V}}:=\left\{v\in H^{1}(\Omega),\nabla\cdot v=0\text{ in }\Omega,v=0\text{ on }\Gamma_{1},v\cdot\nu=0\text{ on }\Gamma_{2}\right\},

and we prove:

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
\underline{\widetilde{V}}(\Omega)
be the space defined in (
4.17
). Then,

(4.18)

\widetilde{\underline{\Lambda}}(\Omega)=\inf_{v\in\widetilde{\underline{V}}\setminus\{0\}}\frac{\left\lVert\nabla\times v\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}}{\left\lVert v\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}}\geq\inf_{v\in\widetilde{\underline{V}}\setminus\{0\}}\frac{\left\lVert\nabla v\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}}{\left\lVert v\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}}>0.

Proof.

Let
v\in\underline{\widetilde{V}}\setminus\{0\}
with compact support in

so that

Let

with respect to the

\left\{\rho=R_{1},z\in[-K,K]\right\}\cup\left\{\rho=R_{2},z\in[-K,K]\right\}\cup\Gamma^{K}\cup-\Gamma^{K}

Taking the
L^{2}(\Omega)
-scalar product of (
4.23
) with
v
, integrating by parts and using the divergence-free condition yields

(4.24)

\int_{\Omega}|\nabla\times v|^{2}=\int_{\Gamma_{2}}((\nabla\times v)\times\nu)\cdot v-\int_{\Omega}\left(v\cdot\nabla\widetilde{{\mathcal{U}}}_{\alpha,\beta,\gamma}\right)\cdot v=-\int_{\Omega}\left(v\cdot\nabla\widetilde{{\mathcal{U}}}_{\alpha,\beta,\gamma}\right)\cdot v.

Using cylindrical coordinates,

(4.25)

\int_{\Omega}(v\cdot\nabla\widetilde{{\mathcal{U}}}_{\alpha,\beta,\gamma})\cdot v=\int_{(R_{1},R_{2})\times(0,2\pi)\times\mathbb{R}}\rho v^{T}\widetilde{A}(\rho)v

with the symmetric matrix

(4.26)

\widetilde{A}(\rho)=\left(\begin{matrix}0&\tfrac{\alpha}{2}(\frac{\widetilde{{\mathcal{U}}}^{C}}{\rho}-\frac{d\widetilde{{\mathcal{U}}}^{C}}{d\rho})&-\tfrac{1}{2}\big(\beta\frac{d\widetilde{\mathcal{U}}_{0,1,0}}{d\rho}+\gamma\frac{d\widetilde{\mathcal{U}}_{0,0,1}}{d\rho}\big)\\[5.0pt]
\tfrac{\alpha}{2}(\frac{\widetilde{{\mathcal{U}}}^{C}}{\rho}-\frac{d\widetilde{{\mathcal{U}}}^{C}}{d\rho})&0&0\\[5.0pt]
-\tfrac{1}{2}\big(\beta\frac{d\widetilde{\mathcal{U}}_{0,1,0}}{d\rho}+\gamma\frac{d\widetilde{\mathcal{U}}_{0,0,1}}{d\rho}\big)&0&0\end{matrix}\right).

We know that there exists
\widetilde{\Upsilon}(\rho)\in\mathbb{R}
such that, for any
\rho\in[R_{1},R_{2}]
, the quadratic form
v^{T}\widetilde{A}(\rho)v
is bounded from above by
\widetilde{\Upsilon}(\rho)|v|^{2}
. The optimal
\widetilde{\Upsilon}(\rho)
is the maximum eigenvalue of the matrix
\widetilde{A}(\rho)
, namely,

\displaystyle 0<\widetilde{\Upsilon}(\rho)

\displaystyle=\left[\alpha^{2}\frac{R_{1}^{4}R_{2}^{2}}{(R_{2}^{2}-R_{1}^{2})^{2}\rho^{4}}+\left(\frac{1}{\rho}\bigg(\frac{\beta-\gamma}{4}R_{2}^{2}-\frac{\beta}{8}\frac{R_{2}^{2}-R_{1}^{2}}{\log(R_{2}/R_{1})}\bigg)+\rho\frac{\gamma}{4}\right)^{2}\right]^{\frac{1}{2}}.

By (
4.21
),
\widetilde{\Upsilon}(\rho)\leq{M}_{\rm in}(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)
. Combining this bound with (
4.24
) and (
4.25
), we deduce

\int_{\Omega}|\nabla\times v|^{2}\leq\int_{(R_{1},R_{2})\times(0,2\pi)\times\mathbb{R}}\widetilde{\Upsilon}(\rho)\rho|v|^{2}\leq{M}_{\rm in}(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)\int_{\Omega}|v|^{2}\leq\frac{{M}_{\rm in}(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)}{\underline{\widetilde{\Lambda}}(\Omega)}\int_{\Omega}|\nabla\times v|^{2}.

Hence, if (
4.22
) holds,
\nabla\times v\equiv 0
in
\Omega
and, since
\widetilde{\underline{\Lambda}}>0
by Lemma
4.1
, we infer
v\equiv 0
in
\Omega
. Then, one infers that also
q\equiv 0
in
\Omega
.
\Box

Still outer cylinder
.
In this case, the space of perturbations of
{\mathcal{U}}_{\alpha,\beta,\gamma}
maintaining the boundary conditions in (
4.2
) reads

(4.27)

\underline{V}:=\left\{v\in H^{1}(\Omega),\nabla\cdot v=0\text{ in }\Omega,v\cdot\nu=0\text{ on }\Gamma_{1},v=0\text{ on }\Gamma_{2}\right\}.

We emphasize that we cannot derive a strict inequality as in (
4.18
) since (
4.20
) becomes

\left\lVert\nabla v\right\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega^{K})}+\int_{\Gamma^{K}_{1}}(\mathcal{R}v,v)ds=\left\lVert\nabla\times v\right\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega^{K})},

and the boundary integral is negative due to the concavity of
\Omega^{K}
close to
\Gamma_{1}^{K}
. This is where we see the analytical difference between imposing vorticity boundary conditions on the inner or outer cylinder. To overcome this challenge, we obtain the positivity of curl-Poincaré constants by imposing additional constraints.

We prove it by adding the geometric constraint of thin annulii, namely, considering small ratios of the radii:

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
\underline{V}(\Omega)
be the space defined in (
4.27
). If
{R_{2}}/{R_{1}}\in(1,e),
then

(4.28)

{\underline{\Lambda}}(\Omega)=\inf_{v\in{\underline{V}}\setminus\{0\}}\frac{\left\lVert\nabla\times v\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}}{\left\lVert v\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}}\geq\left(1-\log\left(R_{2}/R_{1}\right)\right)\inf_{v\in{\underline{V}}\setminus\{0\}}\frac{\left\lVert\nabla v\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}}{\left\lVert v\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}}>0.

Proof.

Following the proof of Lemma
4.1
, we arrive to the equation

(4.29)

\left\lVert\nabla v\right\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega^{K})}+\int_{\Gamma^{K}_{1}}(\mathcal{R}v,v)ds=\left\lVert\nabla\times v\right\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega^{K})}.

The second term is now negative, but we can take advantage of the cylindrical geometry we are working with. Indeed, the curvature tensor can be made explicit and, from its definition in
[
13
, Section 3.1.1.1]
, we find that

\int_{\Gamma_{1}^{K}}(\mathcal{R}v,v)ds=-\frac{1}{R_{1}}\int_{\Gamma_{1}^{K}}|v_{\theta}|^{2}ds=-\int_{(0,2\pi)\times(-K,K)}|v_{\theta}(R_{1},\theta,z)|^{2}d\theta dz.

Due to the homogeneous boundary conditions on
\Gamma_{2}^{K}
, we argue as in the proof of Theorem
3.2
and obtain that

-\int_{\Gamma_{1}^{K}}(\mathcal{R}v,v)ds=\int_{(0,2\pi)\times(-K,K)}|v_{\theta}(R_{1},\theta,z)|^{2}d\theta dz\leq\log\left(R_{2}/R_{1}\right)\|\nabla v\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega^{K})}

which, combined with (
4.29
), implies that

\left(1-\log\left(R_{2}/R_{1}\right)\right)\|\nabla v\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega_{K})}\leq\|\nabla\times v\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega_{K})},

completing the proof of (
4.28
).
\Box

Without the thin annulus assumption, it is not clear whether (
4.28
) continues to hold.
Thanks to Lemma
4.2
, the proof of Theorem
4.3
can be mimicked to obtain an analogous stability result.
Before stating it, we introduce a constant measuring the magnitude of the flow
({\mathcal{U}}_{\alpha,\beta,\gamma},{\mathcal{P}}_{\alpha,\gamma})
:

(4.30)

{M}_{\rm out}(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)\!:=\!\!\max_{\rho\in[R_{1},R_{2}]}\left[\alpha^{2}\frac{R_{1}^{2}R_{2}^{4}}{(R_{2}^{2}-R_{1}^{2})^{2}\rho^{4}}\!+\!\left(\frac{1}{\rho}\bigg(\frac{\beta-\gamma}{4}R_{1}^{2}-\frac{\beta}{8}\frac{R_{2}^{2}-R_{1}^{2}}{\log(R_{2}/R_{1})}\bigg)+\rho\frac{\gamma}{4}\right)^{2}\right]^{\frac{1}{2}}\!\!.

We observe that
{M}_{\rm out}(0,0,0)=0
, and
{M}_{\rm out}(\alpha,0,0)=|\alpha|\frac{R^{2}_{2}}{(R_{2}^{2}-R_{1}^{2})R_{1}}
for any
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
. We prove:

Theorem 4.4
.

Fix
\alpha,\beta,\gamma\in\mathbb{R}
and take
{R_{2}}/{R_{1}}\in(1,e)
. Let
\underline{{\Lambda}}(\Omega)
and
{M}_{\rm out}(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)
be as in (
4.28
) and (
4.30
), respectively. Assume that
(v,q)\in\underline{V}(\Omega)\times L^{2}(\Omega)
is such that
({\mathcal{U}}_{\alpha,\beta,\gamma}+v,{\mathcal{P}}_{\alpha,\gamma}+q)
solves (
4.2
). If

(4.31)

{M}_{\rm out}(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)<\underline{{\Lambda}}(\Omega),

then
(v,q)\equiv(0,0)
in
\Omega
.

The positivity of the curl-Poincaré constant can also be obtained assuming
z
-periodicity of the velocity vector fields, with no need of the geometric restriction
R_{2}/R_{1}\in(1,e)
. Obviously, since
{\mathcal{U}}_{\alpha,\beta,\gamma}
in (
4.7
) are independent of
z
, they are also
z
-periodic. We fix a period
L>0
and set
\Omega_{L}=\{(x,y,z)\in\mathbb{R}^{3}:R_{1}^{2}<x^{2}+y^{2}<R_{2}^{2},z\in[-L/2,L/2)\}
. We define the space

(4.32)

\displaystyle V_{L}:=\big\{

\displaystyle v\in H^{1}_{\rm loc}(\Omega),\nabla\cdot v=0\text{ in }\Omega,v\cdot\nu=0\text{ on }\Gamma_{1},v=0\text{ on }\Gamma_{2},

\displaystyle v(x,y,z)=v(x,y,z+L)\quad\forall(x,y,z)\in\Omega\big\}

and we prove:

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
V_{L}
be the space defined in (
4.32
). Then,

(4.33)

\Lambda_{L}(\Omega):=\inf_{v\in V_{L}\setminus\{0\}}\frac{\left\lVert\nabla\times v\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega_{L})}^{2}}{\left\lVert v\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega_{L})}^{2}}>0.

Proof.

We construct a suitable compact subregion of
\Omega
, as in the proof of Lemma
4.1
. Let
K>0
be a real number, and
\Gamma^{K}
be the part of the curve within the
(\rho,z)
-plane defined by

(z-K)^{4}+\frac{16}{(R_{2}-R_{1})^{4}}\left(\rho-\frac{R_{1}+R_{2}}{2}\right)^{4}=1

such that
\text{supp }\Gamma^{K}\subset\{\rho\in[R_{1},R_{2}],z\in[K,K+1]\}
. Let
-\Gamma^{K}
be the symmetric curve to
\Gamma^{K}
with respect to the
\rho
axis. Then, the curve

\left\{\rho=R_{1},z\in[-K,K]\right\}\cup\left\{\rho=R_{2},z\in[-K,K]\right\}\cup\Gamma^{K}\cup-\Gamma^{K}

is the
C^{3}
-boundary of a 2D-domain
\Omega_{2D}^{K}
. Finally, we define
\Omega^{K}
as the 3D-rotation of
\Omega^{K}_{2D}
with respect to the
z
-axis, namely

(4.34)

\Omega^{K}=\left\{(x,y,z):(\sqrt{x^{2}+y^{2}},z)\in\Omega^{K}_{2D}\right\}.

Take any
w\in V_{L}\setminus\{0\}
. We choose a smooth function
\chi_{K}(z)
such that
0\leq\chi_{K}\leq 1
,

\chi_{K}\equiv 1
if
|z|\leq K
, and
\chi_{K}\equiv 0
if
|z|\geq K+1
.
Then, it is clear that
\chi_{K}w
satisfies

\chi_{K}w\cdot\nu=0
on the boundary of
\Omega^{K+1}
, and

\chi_{K}w=0
on part of the boundary of
\Omega^{K+1}
.
Therefore, we can apply
[
7
, Theorem A.1]
to deduce that there exists
a constant
C_{\Omega^{K+1}}>0
such that

\left\lVert\chi_{K}w\right\rVert_{H^{1}(\Omega^{K+1})}\leq C_{\Omega^{K+1}}(\left\lVert\chi_{K}w\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega^{K+1})}+\left\lVert\nabla\times(\chi_{K}w)\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega^{K+1})}).

Choosing
K=L/2
, we observe that
\Omega_{L}\subset\Omega^{L/2}\subset\Omega^{L/2+1}\subset\Omega_{L\lceil 1+4/L\rceil}
, where
\lceil s\rceil
is the least integer greater than or equal to
s
. It follows that

(4.35)

\displaystyle\left\lVert w\right\rVert_{H^{1}(\Omega_{L})}\leq

\displaystyle\left\lVert\chi_{\frac{L}{2}}w\right\rVert_{H^{1}(\Omega^{\frac{L}{2}+1})}\leq C_{\Omega^{\frac{L}{2}+1}}\left(\left\lVert\chi_{\frac{L}{2}}w\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega^{\frac{L}{2}+1})}+\left\lVert\nabla\times(\chi_{\frac{L}{2}}w)\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega^{\frac{L}{2}+1})}\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\widetilde{C}_{\Omega^{L}}(\left\lVert w\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega_{L})}+\left\lVert\nabla\times w\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega_{L})}),

for some constant
\widetilde{C}_{\Omega^{L}}>0
depending on
L
.

By contradiction, assume that
\Lambda_{L}(\Omega)=0
. Then, there exists a sequence
\{w_{n}\}_{n}\subset{V}_{L}
such that
\left\lVert w_{n}\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega_{L})}=1,
and
\left\lVert\nabla\times w_{n}\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega_{L})}\leq\frac{1}{n}
. By (
4.35
), this sequence is bounded in
H^{1}(\Omega_{L})
and, up to subsequences, it converges weakly to some
w\in H^{1}(\Omega_{L})
and, by compactness, strongly in
L^{2}(\Omega_{L})
. Besides,
\nabla\times w=0
by the lower semi-continuity of the norm with respect to weak continuity. Since a slight modification of
[
7
, Lemma A.2]
shows that the map
w\mapsto\left\lVert\nabla\times w\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega_{L})}
defines a norm in
V_{L}
, we conclude that
w\equiv 0
in
\Omega_{L}
, which contradicts the original assumption
w\in V_{L}\setminus\{0\}
and proves that
\Lambda_{L}(\Omega)>0
.

\Box

By slightly modifying the proof of Theorem
4.3
, we obtain the next stability result:

Theorem 4.5
.

Fix
\alpha,\beta,\gamma\in\mathbb{R}
. Let
\Lambda_{L}(\Omega)>0
and
{M}_{\rm out}(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)
be as in (
4.33
) and (
4.30
), respectively.
Assume that
(v,q)\in V_{L}(\Omega)\times L^{2}_{\rm loc}(\Omega)
is such that
q
is
L
-periodic with respect to
z
, and
({\mathcal{U}}_{\alpha,\beta,\gamma}+v,{\mathcal{P}}_{\alpha,\gamma}+q)
solves (
4.2
). If

(4.36)

{M}_{\rm out}(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)<\Lambda_{L}(\Omega),

then
(v,q)\equiv(0,0)
in
\Omega
.

Proof.

Since
({\mathcal{U}}_{\alpha,\beta,\gamma},{\mathcal{P}}_{\alpha,\gamma})
solves (
4.2
), the perturbation
(v,q)
satisfies

(4.37)

\begin{cases}-\Delta v+v\cdot\nabla v+{{\mathcal{U}}}_{\alpha,\beta,\gamma}\cdot\nabla v+v\cdot\nabla{{\mathcal{U}}}_{\alpha,\beta,\gamma}+\nabla q=0,\quad\nabla\cdot v=0\quad\text{in}\quad\Omega,\\[5.0pt]
v\cdot\nu=0,\quad(\nabla\times v)\times\nu=0\quad\text{on}\quad\Gamma_{2},\qquad\,v=0\quad\text{on}\quad\Gamma_{1}\,,\end{cases}

and, as mentioned above, it is a classical solution to (
4.37
).

We denote
\Gamma_{2}\cap\overline{\Omega_{L}}
by
\Gamma_{2,L}
.
Taking the
L^{2}(\Omega_{L})
-scalar product of (
4.37
) with
v
, integrating by parts and using the divergence-free and periodicity conditions yields

(4.38)

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega_{L}}|\nabla\times v|^{2}=

\displaystyle\int_{\Gamma_{2,L}}((\nabla\times v)\times\nu)\cdot v-\int_{\Omega_{L}}\left(v\cdot\nabla{{\mathcal{U}}}_{\alpha,\beta,\gamma}\right)\cdot v

\displaystyle+\int_{R_{1}^{2}<x^{2}+y^{2}<R_{2}^{2}}v_{z}(x,y,z)q(x,y,z)-v_{z}(x,y,z+L)q(x,y,z+L)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\int_{\Omega_{L}}\left(v\cdot\nabla{{\mathcal{U}}}_{\alpha,\beta,\gamma}\right)\cdot v.

We then deduce from (
4.38
) that

\int_{\Omega_{L}}|\nabla\times v|^{2}\leq{M}_{\rm out}(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)\int_{\Omega_{L}}|v|^{2}\leq\frac{{M}_{\rm out}(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)}{\Lambda_{L}(\Omega)}\int_{\Omega_{L}}|\nabla\times v|^{2},

where

\displaystyle{M}_{\rm out}(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)=\max_{\rho\in[R_{1},R_{2}]}\left[\alpha^{2}\frac{R_{1}^{2}R_{2}^{4}}{(R_{2}^{2}-R_{1}^{2})^{2}\rho^{4}}+\left(\frac{1}{\rho}\bigg(\frac{\beta-\gamma}{4}R_{1}^{2}-\frac{\beta}{8}\frac{R_{2}^{2}-R_{1}^{2}}{\log(R_{2}/R_{1})}\bigg)+\rho\frac{\gamma}{4}\right)^{2}\right]^{\frac{1}{2}}.

Hence, if (
4.36
) holds,
\nabla\times v\equiv 0
in
\Omega_{L}
. Therefore, we also deduce that
v\equiv 0
in
\Omega_{L}
and, in turn, that
q\equiv 0
in
\Omega_{L}
.
\Box

Remark 4.3
.

The pressure
\mathcal{P}_{\alpha,\gamma}
is not periodic. However, Theorem
4.5
establishes that no perturbations in
L^{2}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\Omega)
of
\mathcal{P}_{\alpha,\gamma}
that are
L
-periodic in
z
are admissible. This periodicity constraint on the perturbation is necessary: without it, the perturbation
v=\mathcal{U}_{0,0,\gamma^{\prime}}
,
q=\mathcal{P}_{0,\gamma^{\prime}}
would yield distinct solutions for arbitrary values of
\gamma^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}
. This constraint has physical meaning: the parameter
\gamma
relates to mass density. Thus, enforcing periodicity ensures that the perturbation preserves the mass density of the fluid.

As a final remark, we emphasize that Theorem
4.5
applies to any periodic perturbation. The velocity of the spiral Poiseuille-Couette flows is both helical and axially symmetric. Consequently, the constraint (
4.36
) implies that no other helical or axially symmetric solutions with vertical period
L
exist. The question of whether (
4.36
) can be improved within the helical or axially symmetric class remains open. On the one hand, since they are proper subspaces, one could expect that the upper bound in (
4.36
) can be enlarged. On the other hand, the first bifurcation experimentally observed when increasing the magnitude of the boundary data enjoys axial symmetry, see
[
6
]
: this suggests that the stability thresholds might not be affected considering the smaller axially symmetric class.

Appendix A
Analysis of bounds for stability

In this section we discuss the stability condition (
3.22
) as
R_{1}
and
R_{2}
vary.
Basically, we bound the Poincaré constant in a planar annulus. Similar bounds can also be obtained
for the other stability conditions (
3.32
), (
4.22
), (
4.31
) and (
4.36
).

Proposition A.1
.

Let
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{R}
and assume that
(v,q)\in V_{0}(\Omega)\times L^{2}(\Omega)
is such that
({\mathcal{U}}_{\alpha,\beta}+v,{\mathcal{P}}_{\alpha,\beta}+q)

solves (
2.1
)-(
2.2
)
1
. Then, in order for the condition (
3.22
) to hold (which ensures that
(v,q)\equiv(0,0)
in
\Omega
)
the following behaviors are necessary:

(i)

if
R_{2}>0
is given and
R_{1}\to 0
, then the upper bounds for
|\alpha|
and
|\beta|
tend to
0
;

(ii)

if
R_{1}>0
is given and
R_{2}\to\infty
, then the upper bounds for
|\alpha|
and
|\beta|
tend to
0
;

(iii)

if
R_{1}>0
is given and
R_{2}\to R_{1}
, then the upper bounds for
|\alpha|
and
|\beta|
tend to infinity.

Proof.
We start by proving that
\Lambda(\Omega)
in (
3.20
) satisfies

(A.1)

\Lambda(\Omega)\leq\frac{10}{(R_{2}-R_{1})^{2}}\,.

For any
\varepsilon>0
, we show the existence of a vector field
V_{\varepsilon}\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
such that

(A.2)

\frac{\|\nabla V_{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}}{\|V_{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}}=3\varepsilon^{3}+\frac{10}{(R_{2}-R_{1})^{2}}\,.

We determine
V_{\varepsilon}=(v_{\varepsilon},0,0)
with two null components for some scalar
v_{\varepsilon}\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
, thus

\|\nabla V_{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}=\|\nabla v_{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)},\qquad\|V_{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}=\|v_{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}.

Setting
v_{\varepsilon}(x,y,z)=(1-\varepsilon|z|)^{+}(R_{2}-\sqrt{x^{2}+y^{2}})(\sqrt{x^{2}+y^{2}}-R_{1})
, with
f^{+}
denoting the positive part of
f
, then we have

\|v_{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}=4\pi\int_{0}^{1/\varepsilon}(1-\varepsilon z)^{2}dz\int_{R_{1}}^{R_{2}}\rho(R_{2}-\rho)^{2}(\rho-R_{1})^{2}d\rho=\frac{\pi}{45\varepsilon}(R_{2}+R_{1})(R_{2}-R_{1})^{5}\,.

Moreover, after computing

\nabla v_{\varepsilon}(x,y,z)=\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}(1-\varepsilon z)\Big[\tfrac{(R_{2}+R_{1})x}{\sqrt{x^{2}+y^{2}}}-2x\Big]\\
(1-\varepsilon z)\Big[\tfrac{(R_{2}+R_{1})y}{\sqrt{x^{2}+y^{2}}}-2y\Big]\\
\varepsilon(\sqrt{x^{2}+y^{2}}-R_{2})(\sqrt{x^{2}+y^{2}}-R_{1})\end{array}\right)\qquad\forall z\in(0,1/\varepsilon),

which implies that

\displaystyle\|\nabla v_{\varepsilon}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle=4\pi\int_{0}^{1/\varepsilon}(1-\varepsilon z)^{2}dz\int_{R_{1}}^{R_{2}}\rho(R_{2}+R_{1}-2\rho)^{2}d\rho+4\pi\varepsilon^{2}\int_{R_{1}}^{R_{2}}\rho(R_{2}-\rho)^{2}(\rho-R_{1})^{2}d\rho

\displaystyle=\frac{2\pi}{9\varepsilon}(R_{2}+R_{1})(R_{2}-R_{1})^{3}+\frac{\pi\varepsilon^{2}}{15}(R_{2}+R_{1})(R_{2}-R_{1})^{5}\,,

we obtain (
A.2
).
From (
3.28
) and (
A.1
) we have the following bounds:

\max\left\{\frac{\pi^{2}}{2R_{2}^{2}},\frac{8}{(R_{2}^{2}-R_{1}^{2})\log(R_{2}/R_{1})}\right\}\leq\Lambda(\Omega)\leq\frac{10}{(R_{2}-R_{1})^{2}}\,,

from which we infer that

(A.3)

\displaystyle\frac{\pi^{2}}{2R_{2}^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\liminf_{R_{1}\to 0}\Lambda(\Omega)\leq\limsup_{R_{1}\to 0}\Lambda(\Omega)\leq\frac{10}{R_{2}^{2}}\qquad\forall R_{2}>0\,,

(A.4)

\displaystyle 4

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\liminf_{R_{2}\to R_{1}}\big[(R_{2}-R_{1})^{2}\Lambda(\Omega)\big]\leq\limsup_{R_{2}\to R_{1}}\big[(R_{2}-R_{1})^{2}\Lambda(\Omega)\big]\leq 10\quad\forall R_{1}>0\,,

(A.5)

\displaystyle\lim_{R_{2}\to\infty}\Lambda(\Omega)=0\quad\forall R_{1}>0\,,

where in (
A.4
) we have used that
\log(R_{2}/R_{1})\leq R_{2}/R_{1}-1.

Next, let us rewrite
M
in (
3.21
) as

M(\alpha,\beta)=\left[\alpha^{2}\Phi_{1}(R_{1},R_{2})+\frac{\beta^{2}}{16}\Phi_{2}(R_{1},R_{2})\right]^{\tfrac{1}{2}},

where

\Phi_{1}(R_{1},R_{2}):=\frac{R_{2}^{4}}{(R_{2}^{2}-R_{1}^{2})^{2}R_{1}^{2}}\,,\quad\Phi_{2}(R_{1},R_{2}):=\left(\frac{R_{2}^{2}-R_{1}^{2}}{R_{1}\log(R_{2}^{2}/R_{1}^{2})}-R_{1}\right)^{2}\,,\quad\mbox{for }0<R_{1}<R_{2}\,.

Some calculus computations show that

(A.6)

\lim_{R_{1}\to 0}\Phi_{1}(R_{1},R_{2})=\lim_{R_{1}\to 0}\Phi_{2}(R_{1},R_{2})=\infty\quad\forall R_{2}>0\,,

(A.7)

\lim_{R_{2}\to\infty}\Phi_{1}(R_{1},R_{2})=\frac{1}{R_{1}^{2}}\,,\quad\lim_{R_{2}\to\infty}\Phi_{2}(R_{1},R_{2})=\infty\quad\forall R_{1}>0\,,

(A.8)

\lim_{R_{2}\to R_{1}}\big[(R_{2}-R_{1})^{2}\Phi_{1}(R_{1},R_{2})\big]=\frac{1}{4}\,,\quad\lim_{R_{2}\to R_{1}}\Phi_{2}(R_{1},R_{2})=0\,\quad\forall R_{1}>0\,.

To conclude the proof, we use (
3.22
) and the above bounds.

Item
(ii)
