Late-stage dedifferentiation and epigenetic memory of cancer stem cells in hepatocellular carcinoma
Authors: Yi-Kai Hu, Kun-Jiang Tan, Na Feng, Jing Li, Lu Chen, Yu-Xuan Lin, Hong-Yang Wang, Yu-Fei He
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCancer stem cells (CSCs) drive recurrence and drug resistance in hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), but their origin remains controversial: are they tumour-initiating cells or late-stage dedifferentiation products? Direct human single-cell evidence linking bipotent progenitors (BPs) and CSCs has been lacking.MethodsWe integrated single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) data from 109 samples (44 patients; 410,608 cells) across five public cohorts and generated EpCAM-enriched scRNA-seq from two additional HCC patients. Single-cell somatic mutations were inferred from the transcriptomic data, yielding 384,867 high-confidence variants across 31,908 cells from 20 patients. Clonal evolution was reconstructed through copy number variation (CNV) phylogenies and transcription-coupled-repair-based cell-of-origin inference. CSC and non-CSC subpopulations from Huh7 cells were flow-sorted before and after two weeks of culture and profiled by targeted bisulfite sequencing. CNV phylogenies and evolutionary distances placed CSCs at the most distal branches of the tumour tree, while cell-of-origin analysis identified BPs as a pre-malignant precursor arising from hepatocyte dedifferentiation. RNA velocity, pseudotime and SNP-integrated lineage reconstruction converged on this directionality, with CSCs arising at the terminus of tumour evolution, reproduced at single-patient resolution in the EpCAM-enriched samples. Mechanistically, CSCs upregulated DNA methyltransferases (DNMTs), and ~78% of CSC-specific methylation changes were stably retained after CSC differentiation but not reproduced during de novo stemness acquisition, indicating locked-in epigenetic memory. A 16-gene core-imprint risk score specifically predicted early recurrence (≤2 years), and CD13+ CD133+ CSCs showed elevated 5-hydroxymethylcytosine correlating with poor prognosis.ConclusionsWe propose a framework in which hepatocytes dedifferentiate into BPs as a pre-malignant state, undergo malignant transformation, and a subset acquires stemness through DNMT-mediated reprogramming stabilized by epigenetic memory. Background

Primary liver cancer imposes an increasingly heavy global disease burden, ranking sixth in incidence and third in mortality among all cancers, with hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) accounting for 75–85% of cases [1]. Cancer stem cells (CSCs) are considered the root cause of HCC recurrence and drug resistance [2, 3]. However, the cellular origin and clonal evolutionary position of CSCs have long been contentious: are they the starting point of tumourigenesis (the classical “stem cell origin” hypothesis), or late-stage dedifferentiation products of tumour evolution?

Bipotent progenitors (BPs), as the stem-like subpopulation of the liver, have been proposed by multiple research groups as potential cells of origin for HCC [4–6]. However, this hypothesis is primarily based on murine lineage-tracing experiments, and different injury models have yielded contradictory conclusions [5, 7]. More importantly, fundamental differences exist between humans and mice in hepatic regeneration and tumourigenesis mechanisms [8, 9], making it difficult to directly extrapolate conclusions from murine models to humans. To date, no study has directly traced the clonal lineage of HCC in human clinical samples at single-cell resolution.

Recent algorithmic advances in inferring somatic mutations from single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) data have opened new avenues for reconstructing clonal evolution [10, 11]. In this study, we integrated single-cell transcriptomic data from 109 samples to construct a single-cell-resolution somatic mutation landscape in HCC. Unexpectedly, we found that BPs harboured a higher mutation burden than other tumour-adjacent cells, and CSCs carried a higher mutation burden than most other tumour cells, challenging the traditional notion of their role as “genomically quiescent clonal ancestors”. By integrating mutation analysis, phylogenetic tree construction and epigenetic profiling, we propose an integrated framework: stem/progenitor cells in HCC are more likely late-stage dedifferentiation products of clonal evolution, and the stemness state of CSCs is maintained by epigenetic memory. Building on these data, we test a single hypothesis throughout this study: that CSCs in established HCC are not the quiescent ancestors of the tumour but its late-stage, epigenetically locked descendants, with BPs marking the pre-malignant origin of this trajectory. All subsequent analyses are organised around this question, with other hepatic and tumour populations examined only as the framework needed to position BPs and CSCs.

Methods

Study design and overview

This study aimed to reconstruct the clonal evolutionary trajectory of stem and progenitor cells in human hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) and to determine whether cancer stem cells (CSCs) are clonal founders or late-stage dedifferentiation products. We combined three complementary approaches: (i) integrative single-cell transcriptomic analysis of 109 scRNA-seq samples from 44 patients across five public cohorts together with two in-house EpCAM-enriched scRNA-seq samples, with single-cell somatic mutation inference performed on a subset of 20 patients (31,908 cells) with suitable raw data; (ii) in vitro dynamic methylation tracing in Huh7 cells using flow-sorted CSC and non-CSC subpopulations followed by targeted bisulfite sequencing; and (iii) in situ validation on a 97-case HCC tissue microarray by multiplex immunofluorescence.

Clinical sample collection and single-cell suspension preparation

Paired tumour and tumour-adjacent tissues from two HCC patients were obtained from the Eastern Hepatobiliary Surgery Hospital (EHBH), Shanghai. All patients provided written informed consent, and all procedures were approved by the EHBH Ethics Committee (No. EHBHKY2018-1–001). This study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Fresh tissues were transported to the laboratory within 2 hours of surgical resection. Samples were rinsed in DMEM and minced to approximately 1 mm3 fragments. Tumour tissues were dissociated using the Tumour Dissociation Kit, human (Miltenyi, 130–095-929); tumour-adjacent tissues were dissociated using an optimised enzymatic digestion solution (collagenase IV 1 mg/ml + DNase I 0.01 mg/ml + Dispase 1 mg/ml, dissolved in 1% FBS + DMEM), incubated at 37 °C in a shaking water bath at 88 r.p.m. for 25 min with gentle trituration every 10 min. Digested products were filtered through 70 µm MACS SmartStrainers (Miltenyi, 130–110-916) and centrifuged at 450g for 10 min. After ACK red blood cell lysis and re-filtration, Dead Cell Removal Kit (Miltenyi, 130–090-101) was applied when cell viability fell below 80%.

EpCAM magnetic bead enrichment and 10x genomics sequencing

A total of 2 × 107 cells were resuspended in 300 µl MACS buffer (PBS + 0.5% BSA + 2 mM EDTA), supplemented with 100 µl CD326 microbeads (Miltenyi, 130–045-801) and 100 µl Fc block, and incubated at 4 °C for 30 min. After washing and filtration through 40 µm cell strainers, positive selection was performed on MS columns using a magnetic separator. Single-cell suspensions from Patient 1 and Patient 2 were then loaded onto 10x Chromium Chips (v4, PN: 1,000,690) at target recovery counts of 10,000 and 20,000 cells, respectively, and processed on a 10x Chromium Controller for gel bead-in-emulsion (GEM) generation and cell barcode labelling. Single-cell RNA reverse transcription, amplification and library construction were performed strictly following the manufacturer’s guidelines using the 10x Chromium Single Cell 3′ Kit (v4, PN: 1,000,691) and paired library construction kit (v4, PN: 1,000,694), including GEM-RT reaction, cDNA amplification (11–13 PCR cycles), fragmentation, end repair, adaptor ligation and sample index PCR. Libraries passing quality assessment by Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer and Qubit 3.0 were sequenced on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform using a 150-bp paired-end (PE150) strategy, with a target sequencing depth of ≥50,000 reads per cell.

Public dataset collection and raw data processing

scRNA-seq data were obtained from five public datasets [12–15]: GSE149614, GSE156625, GSE282701 and GSE242889 (GEO) and SRP318499 (SRA), comprising 109 samples from 44 HCC patients (Additional file 2). For SRP318499, BAM files were converted using bam2fq (v1.4.1) and aligned to the GRCh38 genome with Cell Ranger (v9.0.1) [16]; in-house data were likewise processed with Cell Ranger. GSE282701 was processed with seeksoultools (v1.2.0) and GSE242889 with CeleScope. GSE156625 used barcode information directly provided by GEO.

Quality control and batch integration

Raw expression matrices were read using Scanpy (v1.10.3) [17], excluding genes expressed in fewer than 3 cells and low-quality cells with fewer than 150 detected genes. Quality control employed an adaptive strategy based on median absolute deviation (MAD), with 5× MAD as the dynamic threshold for filtering cells with anomalous gene detection counts, total transcript counts and mitochondrial expression fractions. Ambient RNA correction was performed using DecontX (celda v1.18.2) [18], with prior PCA dimensionality reduction and Leiden clustering at 0.3 resolution to obtain pre-clustering labels. Scrublet (v0.2.3) [19] was used to detect and remove doublets on a per-sample basis.

Dimensionality reduction of cleaned data was performed using scvi-tools (v1.1.6) [20]. An scVI model was first independently trained to map high-dimensional expression data to a low-dimensional latent space; preliminary cell-type annotations were then incorporated as priors in a scANVI model for semi-supervised learning. Nearest-neighbour graphs were constructed in the latent space, UMAP was used for two-dimensional visualisation and Leiden clustering (resolution 0.5) for unsupervised clustering. Cell subpopulation annotation was completed by integrating differentially expressed genes and canonical markers.

Malignant cell identification and hepatic lineage cell functional classification

Malignant cells were identified using the CopyKAT [21] module in infercnvpy (v0.6.1) and TCfinder [22] (v1.2.0.1). CopyKAT used tumour-adjacent hepatic lineage cells (when sufficient) or T cells as diploid references; TCfinder was run with default parameters. Cells identified as malignant by either tool were annotated as malignant. Functional classification of hepatic lineage cells used an NMF-based meta-program (MP) extraction strategy [23].

CSC and bipotent progenitor identification

CytoTRACE2 (v1.1.0) [24] was used to compute stemness scores for hepatic lineage cells, separately by dataset and by benign/malignant status. Malignant cells with stemness scores > 0.2 were integrated via scVI and clustered by Leiden to identify three CSC subtypes. Gene sets for subtype identification were derived from Lee et al. [2], Yang et al. [25] and Fan et al. [26], as well as the MSigDB Hallmark and C2 databases, scored using AUCell (v1.26.0). BPs in tumour-adjacent tissues were identified by multi-parameter scoring: hepatic lineage score < 0.3, biliary score > 0.4, progenitor score > 0.3 and TACSTD2 expression < 3, scored using the score_genes function in Scanpy, with markers referenced from Sharma et al. [15] and Andrews et al. [27].

RNA velocity and pseudotime analysis

scVelo (v0.3.3) [28] was used to process and compute RNA velocity data, with veloVI (v0.3.1) [29] for data smoothing. Raw count matrices were exported to Monocle3 (v1.4.26) [30] and scTour (v1.0.0) [31]for independent pseudotime analyses.

Single-cell SNV detection and mutation analysis

Monopogen [11] (v1.0.0) was applied to 86 BAM files from four cohorts for somatic SNV inference, leveraging population linkage disequilibrium (LD) information from the 1000 Genomes Project. Quality-control criteria were: SVM score > 0.1 (excluding sequencing errors), LD optimisation score > 0.25 (removing germline background), reference depth > 5, alternative depth > 3, and variant allele frequency (VAF) 0.05–0.5. This yielded 384,867 somatic SNVs across 31,908 cells from 20 patients. Annotation and format conversion were performed using vcf2maf (v1.6.22). Single-cell OncoPrint was constructed using ComplexHeatmap (v2.20.0) [32], sampling up to 2,000 mutation-carrying cells plus 500 non-mutant background cells.

MutationalPatterns (v3.14.0) [33] based on BSgenome.Hsapiens.UCSC.hg38 was used to obtain the trinucleotide context for each mutation, and a mutation count matrix was constructed by 96 substitution subtypes (aggregated by cell type). Given that scRNA-seq-derived mutation spectra may contain RNA editing residuals and transcription-coupled biases, known A-to-I RNA editing sites were first filtered, followed by de novo mutational signature extraction using NMF. NMF decomposition selected k = 3 as the final number of signatures. Extracted de novo signatures were compared with the COSMIC v3.2 database by cosine similarity to assess correspondence with known mutational processes. Reconstruction quality of each cell type’s mutation spectrum was measured by cosine similarity.

For SNV-based mutation burden comparisons, to eliminate analytical biases arising from uneven single-cell sequencing depth and genomic site heterogeneity, variants at splice sites or splice regions were first filtered to exclude high-frequency technical false positives inherent to the transcriptomic level. For pairwise cell subpopulation comparisons, only intersecting sites adequately covered by both cell types were extracted as the effective assessment interval. The paired cell populations were then pooled to calculate the background mutation rate (BMR) for each shared site. On the basis of this BMR, the expected mutation count for each single cell at its specific coverage was computed, yielding the O/E ratio.

SNP inference and SNP–transcriptome integrated pseudotime

cellsnp-lite (v1.2.3) [34] was used to infer SNPs from BAM files (minCOUNT = 20, minMAF = 0.1, UMItag = UB). SNP results were split by hepatic lineage cell barcodes into single-cell VCF files and input into TBSP [35] to obtain a single-cell SNP matrix. The expression matrix and a 50-fold oversampled SNP matrix were vertically concatenated to form a fused matrix, which was input into Monocle3 (v1.4.26) for pseudotime computation.

CNV analysis and phylogenetics

CNV analysis was performed using the CopyKAT mutation matrix. The CNV score was defined as the mean square of each cell’s CNV values. Neighbour-joining (NJ) phylogenetic trees were constructed from single-cell CNV profiles, and MEDALT evolutionary distances were calculated as previously described [36].

Tumour cell-of-origin inference

The tumour cell of origin was inferred on the basis of the TCR mechanism [37]. Specifically, somatic SNVs were extracted from whole-genome sequencing data of the Chinese Liver Cancer Atlas (CLCA) [38], APOBEC-related mutations were excluded on the basis of trinucleotide sequence context, and passenger mutations reflecting endogenous mutational processes were retained. Gene-level expression centroids and highly variable gene sets were computed from the single-cell transcriptomic atlas for each cell type. A negative binomial regression model was employed, with gene-level mutation counts as the dependent variable and cell-type-specific expression (log-transformed) as the primary independent variable, with simultaneous correction for mean transcriptional activity across all cell types (row mean of the centroid matrix), GC content (hg19 reference genome), replication timing (ENCODE HepG2 Repli-seq signal) and full gene length (UCSC knownGene annotation, as an offset term correcting for genomic territory). Other parameters were consistent with the original method.

Differential expression and pathway analysis

Differential expression analysis was performed using the FindMarkers function in Seurat (v5.1.0) [39] (Wilcoxon rank-sum test, |log2FC| >0.25 and adjusted p < 0.05). GO and KEGG pathway enrichment was conducted using clusterProfiler (v4.12.6) [40], and gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) based on MSigDB Hallmark gene sets was completed with fgsea (v1.30.0) [41]. AUCell (v1.26.0) [42] was used to score gene-set activity in individual cells.

Weighted gene co-expression network analysis

PyWGCNA (v2.0.1) [43] was used to construct co-expression networks at the metacell level, identifying module eigengenes (MEs), with the soft threshold set to 12. Transcriptional similarity between cell types was assessed by Pearson correlation coefficient. The prognostic significance of BP-M1 in TCGA-LIHC was validated by Cox regression and Kaplan–Meier analysis. The correlation of BP-M1 with the Hoshida poor-prognosis signature was assessed by Spearman correlation in GSE14520 and TCGA-LIHC tumour-adjacent tissues. For DNMT regulatory chain analysis in TCGA-LIHC, the DNMT activity score was defined as the mean Z-score of DNMT1/DNMT3A/DNMT3B, and Spearman correlations were calculated between this score and CSC core gene expression, differentiation gene promoter methylation levels (β-value means) and differentiation gene expression levels.

Cell line culture and flow cytometric sorting

Huh7 cells were cultured in DMEM + 10% FBS + 1% penicillin–streptomycin at 37 °C and 5% CO2. After 0.25% trypsin digestion, cells were stained with APC anti-human CD133 (BioLegend, 372,805) and PE anti-human CD13 (BioLegend, 301,703) at room temperature in the dark for 20 min. CD133+CD13+ (CSC phenotype) and CD133−CD13− (non-CSC phenotype) subpopulations were sorted (n = 4 each) on a BD LSRFortessa flow cytometer in Purity mode using 100 µm nozzle tips. Sorted cells were separately cultured for two weeks and then re-sorted, yielding four transition states: parental (F_P/F_N) and post-culture re-sorted (PP/PN/NP/NN). Sorted cells were snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen, stored at − 80 °C and shipped on dry ice for sequencing.

Targeted methylation sequencing and analysis

This study employed the EPIC-seq (935K sequencing version) platform developed by Acegen (Shenzhen, China) for genome-wide targeted methylation sequencing. After genomic DNA quality assessment, enzymatic conversion (EM-seq) was combined with the Acegen MethylDesign methylation liquid-phase capture system, utilising the 935K-seq Panel (hg38) for library construction, covering more than 4 million C sites and comprehensively encompassing Illumina 935K array sites and their flanking regions. Bisulphite conversion efficiency was evaluated using CHH sites as internal references, confirming conversion rates exceeding 99% in all samples. Pooled libraries were sequenced on Illumina/GeneMind platforms using a PE150 strategy.

Raw data underwent quality assessment with FastQC (v0.11.7), followed by adaptor removal, low-quality base trimming and removal of reads shorter than 36 nt using Trimmomatic (v0.36). Clean reads were aligned to the hg38 reference genome using BSMAP (v2.73) [44], which accommodates bisulphite-converted sequences by allowing C/T mismatches. After PCR duplicate removal, methylation sites were identified on the basis of the type of base aligned at reference genome C positions (C = methylated, T = unmethylated), with methylation levels calculated as ML = mC/(mC + umC) and the reliability of each site’s methylation status assessed by binomial distribution test (p < 0.001).

Global methylation levels were calculated as the mean β-value of all sites per sample. Epigenetic entropy was based on the Shannon entropy formula: H = −(β log2β + (1 − β) log2(1 − β)).

methylKit (v1.30.0) [45] was used for differential methylation analysis at both regional and site levels. For differentially methylated region (DMR) analysis, the genome was partitioned into 300-bp non-overlapping windows (minimum of 3 CpG sites), using a logistic regression model with the “MN” parameter to correct for overdispersion; significance thresholds were Q < 0.05 and |Δβ| >10%. For DMC analysis, filterByCoverage was used to exclude sites with coverage below 5× or above the 99.9th percentile, coverage was normalised by the median method, and significance thresholds were Q < 0.01 and |Δβ| >10%.

For chromatin state annotation, the liftOver function of rtracklayer (v1.62.0) was used to convert the H9 cell line ChromHMM 18-state model from hg19 to hg38 coordinates. GenomicFeatures (v1.54.0) was used to construct 300-bp tiling windows as the statistical background, Fisher’s exact test was applied to assess enrichment of differential sites in each chromatin state and the Benjamini–Hochberg method was used for multiple-testing correction.

Assessment of CSC methylation stability and core imprint score

The degree to which CSC-specific methylation patterns were preserved during two weeks of culture was assessed by Spearman rank correlation between per-site baseline methylation differences (FP − FN) and the corresponding two-week differences in the maintenance pathway (PP − PN) or acquisition pathway (NP − NN). DMCs were classified by memory status into three categories: core imprints (retained in both pathways), stable memory (retained only in the maintenance pathway) and no memory.

Selection criteria for the 16-gene core imprint score were: core memory site proportion (pct_core) = 100%, RNA-seq differential expression FDR < 0.05 and functional concordance between methylation direction and expression direction. This yielded 11 CSC-activated genes (ACVR1B, ANPEP, CCDC34, DUSP6, FZD7, GSPT2, ISYNA1, PLAUR, RSRC2, SERTAD3 and TECR) and 5 CSC-silenced genes (ALOX5AP, CUX2, ITIH2, SERPING1 and SMLR1). Risk score = mean(Zactivated) − mean(Zsilenced). Validation of overall survival was performed in TCGA-LIHC, ICGC LIRI-JP, OEP000321, GSE14520 and GSE10143, and of recurrence-free/disease-free survival in TCGA, GSE14520 and OEP000321. Cohort information and validation statistics across all independent datasets are summarised in Additional file 5: Table S4. Time-dependent AUC was computed using the timeROC (v0.4) [46] package at 3-month intervals, with 24 months post-surgery as the boundary for early and late recurrence subgroup Cox regression.

Tissue microarray multiplex immunofluorescence

Tissue microarrays containing 97 HCC cases (83 with paired tumour/tumour-adjacent tissues) were subjected to tyramide signal amplification (TSA) multiplex immunofluorescence, labelling EpCAM, CD13, CD133 and 5hmC. Scanning was performed using the Vectra Polaris multispectral imaging system, and inForm software was used for cell segmentation and phenotype classification.

Statistical methods

Two-group comparisons were performed using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test; multi-group comparisons used the Kruskal–Wallis test; paired analyses used the paired Wilcoxon signed-rank test. Multiple-testing correction was performed using the Benjamini–Hochberg method. Survival analysis used Cox proportional hazards regression and the Kaplan–Meier method, with between-group comparisons by log-rank test. Time-dependent AUC was computed using the timeROC package. p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. In all figures, statistical significance is denoted as *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001 and ****p < 0.0001; NS, not significant.

Use of large language models

A large language model (Claude, Anthropic) was used to assist with analysis code drafting and manuscript language editing; all scientific conclusions were independently determined and verified by the authors.

Results

To resolve whether CSCs represent the origin or a late-stage product of HCC evolution, we organised our analyses as five convergent lines of evidence, all centred on the stem/progenitor populations (BPs and CSCs): (i) identification of the stem/progenitor populations within the hepatic lineage; (ii) single-cell somatic-mutation burden; (iii) CNV phylogeny and cell-of-origin inference; (iv) RNA velocity and SNP-integrated lineage reconstruction; and (v) epigenetic memory and its clinical relevance. Each layer independently addresses the same question, and together they define a single unified trajectory rather than separate directions.

Functional heterogeneity of hepatic lineage cells and identification of stem/progenitor cells

As the foundation for tracing CSC origin, we first resolved the hepatic lineage and defined the stem/progenitor populations (BPs and CSCs) that are the focus of this study. After integrating 109 samples from five public datasets, we obtained a comprehensive single-cell transcriptomic atlas of HCC encompassing 410,608 cells (Fig. 1a,b, Additional file 1: Fig. S1a, Additional file 2). Non-negative matrix factorisation (NMF)-based decomposition of hepatic parenchymal cells (68,603 cells) identified 12 shared functional modules spanning metabolism, cell cycle and biliary features (Additional file 1: Fig. S1b). To systematically identify CSCs, we employed CytoTRACE2—a machine-learning algorithm that infers cellular differentiation potential on the basis of transcriptomic entropy [24]—to quantify the stemness potential of each malignant cell. Cells with the highest stemness scores (CytoTRACE score > 0.2) were further clustered into three subpopulations (Fig. 1c,d): CSC(classic) exhibited high expression of canonical CSC markers EpCAM and ANPEP (CD13), possessed the clearest bipotent characteristics (Fig. 1e) and showed upregulation of multiple known stemness-related pathways, including Wnt, NOTCH and Hedgehog; CSC(progenitor_cycling) scored highly in cell-cycle-related pathways, displayed overall properties more closely resembling hepatocytes and exhibited elevated proliferation-related scores, with high pathway activity in IL-6–JAK–STAT3 and other malignancy-associated signalling; CSC(progenitor_quiet) showed biliary cell characteristics, low cell-cycle and proliferation scores, but high activity in Wnt, IL-6–JAK–STAT3, EMT and other malignant phenotype pathways.

In tumour-adjacent tissues, we employed a multi-parameter scoring strategy (hepatic lineage score < 0.3, biliary score > 0.4, progenitor score > 0.3 and TACSTD2 expression < 3) to identify BPs (Additional file 1: Fig. S2a). The marker profile of these cells was highly concordant with published hepatic progenitor signatures [15, 27] (Fig. 1e). To explore the lineage relationships among these populations, we performed RNA velocity analysis. Because the tumour-adjacent parenchyma and the tumour proper contain biologically distinct populations (non-malignant hepatocytes/BPs and malignant cells/CSCs, respectively), we analysed each compartment separately and asked the same question in both: where do the stem/progenitor-like cells sit along the local trajectory? In tumour-adjacent tissue, velocity vectors pointed from hepatocytes towards BPs, indicating that BPs arise through hepatocyte dedifferentiation rather than the reverse (Fig. 1f, left). In tumour tissue, velocity flowed from other tumour cells towards CSC (classic), placing CSCs at the terminus rather than the origin of the intratumoural trajectory (Fig. 1f, right). At the global level, velocity flowed from normal towards tumour cells (Additional file 1: Fig. S2b), and CSC(progenitor_cycling) and CSC(progenitor_quiet) converged towards CSC(classic) (Additional file 1: Fig. S2c). These results were independently validated by Monocle3 pseudotime analysis (Additional file 1: Fig. S2d), scTour (Additional file 1: Fig. S2e,f) and CytoTRACE2 (Additional file 1: Fig. S2g,h).

RNA-inferred somatic mutations reveal a high mutation burden in stem/progenitor cells

We next examined whether the stem/progenitor populations indeed occupy the genomically quiescent position predicted by the classical ancestor hypothesis, beginning at the level of somatic-mutation burden. To trace clonal evolution at single-cell precision, we applied Monopogen [11]—a tool that leverages linkage disequilibrium information to infer somatic single-nucleotide variants (SNVs) from scRNA-seq data—and obtained 384,867 high-confidence mutations across 31,908 cells from 20 patients (Fig. 2a). NMF extraction yielded three de novo mutational signatures with moderate similarity to COSMIC SBS7a, SBS40 and SBS17b (cosine similarity 0.55–0.73; Additional file 1: Fig. S3b–d). Signature composition varied markedly across hepatocyte subtypes (Fig. 2c). Given the inherent limitations of scRNA-seq–based mutation inference (residual RNA editing and transcription-coupled biases), we interpret these as cell-state-dependent differences in mutation detectability rather than definitive mutational processes.

dN/dS analysis identified 11 genes under positive selection (q < 0.1; Fig. 2a, Additional file 3). Notably, CSC(classic) carried positive-selection signals for CEP57L1 and PPP2R5E, suggesting that CSCs may experience selective pressure at the level of cell-division regulation.

To compare mutation burden under strict control for single-cell sequencing coverage disparities, we extracted shared assessable genomic loci for each comparison pair and calculated the observed/expected (O/E) ratio on a per-site, per-cell basis (see Methods). Among normal cells, BPs exhibited a higher mutation burden than patient-matched hepatocytes and cholangiocytes (one-sided paired Wilcoxon test, p < 0.05) (Fig. 2d). Among tumour cells, CSC subpopulations similarly showed a higher mutation burden than non-CSC tumour cells (one-sided paired Wilcoxon test, p < 0.05) (Fig. 2e,f; Additional file 1: Fig. S3e). This finding challenges the traditional assumption: if BPs and CSCs were truly the “genomically quiescent clonal ancestors” of tumourigenesis, they should carry the lowest mutation burden. Instead, the pattern we observed suggests that they are more likely products of late-stage clonal evolution.

Copy number variation and cell-of-origin analyses validate the evolutionary position of stem/progenitor cells

To position these stem/progenitor populations within the clonal hierarchy, we next reconstructed evolutionary relationships using CNV phylogeny and cell-of-origin inference. To circumvent potential biases in RNA-inferred SNVs, we employed CNV analysis as independent validation. Phylogenetic trees constructed from single-cell CNV profiles displayed consistent topologies: BPs were the normal cell type closest to the malignant branch (Additional file 1: Fig. S4a,b), and CSCs occupied the most distal positions on the tumour phylogenetic tree (Additional file 1: Fig. S4c).

To further pinpoint the tumour cell of origin, we applied a cell-of-origin (COO) inference method [37] based on the transcription-coupled repair (TCR) principle, which exploits the lower somatic mutation density on transcriptionally active genes to trace the precursor cell type of the tumour. Using our tumour-adjacent parenchymal cell annotations, BPs emerged as the cell type with the strongest negative correlation with mutation density (inferred COO) (Fig. 3f). We further refined this analysis using 11 hepatic parenchymal cell subtypes defined in the normal human liver joint atlas constructed by Andrews et al. [27] (Fig. 3g). COO analysis ranked Chol_CV hepatocytes—a population with a hepatic–biliary dual-lineage transitional phenotype—as the top candidate cell of origin for HCC, in line with our independent identification of BPs as the COO and consistent with a bipotent precursor as the preferential origin of HCC.

Inter-subpopulation differential analysis revealed that methyltransferase-related pathways were significantly elevated in CSCs by Gene Ontology (GO) and KEGG enrichment, and stemness-associated pathways including EMT, cell cycle and Wnt–β-catenin signalling were upregulated (Fig. 5a). Weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) identified BP-specific (BP-M1) and CSC-specific (CSC-M1) stemness modules (Additional file 1: Fig. S6a–d). Hallmark pathway enrichment showed that CSC-M1 and BP-M1 shared TNF-α/NF-κB signalling, EMT and hypoxia among inflammation–mesenchymal pathways (Fig. 5c). However, BPs did not actually undergo epigenetic alterations similar to those observed in CSCs (Additional file 1: Fig. S6h).

To validate the regulatory role of DNMTs, we constructed a complete regulatory chain in TCGA-LIHC data: DNMT activity was positively correlated with CSC core gene expression (Fig. 5f), positively correlated with promoter methylation of differentiation genes (genes downregulated in CSCs relative to non-CSC tumour cells) (Fig. 5g), promoter hypermethylation of differentiation genes showed a negative correlation trend with their expression (Fig. 5h), CSC core gene expression was positively correlated with differentiation gene promoter methylation (Fig. 5i) and the total effect manifested as a strong negative correlation between DNMT activity and differentiation gene expression (Fig. 5j). This chain revealed a complete DNMT upregulation → promoter hypermethylation → differentiation programme silencing regulatory axis, indicating that DNMTs may participate in epigenetic reprogramming associated with the CSC dedifferentiated state.

These analyses indicate that CSCs upregulate DNMTs and may acquire stemness through epigenetic reprogramming. Whether this late-acquired stemness is stably maintained—and thus whether CSCs act as durable “memory units” driving recurrence—determines its functional impact. To test this temporal stability, we designed in vitro dynamic methylation experiments.

During CSC differentiation, per-site methylation differences after two weeks remained positively correlated with the baseline CSC-non-CSC difference (Spearman R = 0.463, p < 0.001; Fig. 6e), indicating that a substantial fraction of CSC-specific methylation differences persisted through cell division (Fig. 6f). On the basis of core imprinted loci, we constructed a risk score (see Methods). Discussion

This study systematically delineated the evolutionary trajectory from normal BPs to CSCs in HCC across both genetic and epigenetic dimensions. Several points merit in-depth discussion.

Bipotent progenitors: a transitional pre-malignant state arising from hepatocyte dedifferentiation. The classical view holds that quiescent BPs pre-existing in the canals of Hering are the cells of origin for HCC [52, 53]. However, the multidimensional data in this study point to a different model: BPs are more likely a transitional pre-malignant state generated by hepatocyte dedifferentiation under chronic injury. This conclusion is supported by the following evidence: (1) RNA velocity showed that BP velocity vectors originated from hepatocytes; (2) BPs had the highest mutation burden among all normal cells; (3) CNV phylogenetic trees showed that BPs had the shortest evolutionary distance to the tumour; (4) COO analysis positioned BPs as the most probable pre-malignant cells; and (5) BPs exhibited greater transcriptional similarity to CSCs than to normal cells.

This model unifies the “hepatocyte origin” and “progenitor cell origin” hypotheses: HCC originates from hepatocytes, and their dedifferentiated intermediate state, the BP, serves as the hub for mutation accumulation and malignant transformation. Notably, the COO analysis results are highly consistent with the recent observation of Zone 3 hepatocyte-derived tumours in murine liver lineage-tracing studies [54].

However, these conclusions warrant cautious interpretation. RNA velocity reflects instantaneous trends in transcriptional state transitions and cannot distinguish between “dedifferentiation” and “capture during differentiation” scenarios. Likewise, the transition between the pre-malignant and malignant compartments is not inferred from velocity but is supported by our mutation-burden, CNV-phylogeny and cell-of-origin analyses. Somatic mutations inferred from scRNA-seq are themselves subject to known biases—including residual RNA editing, transcription-coupled repair effects and uneven per-cell coverage—and should not be interpreted as definitive estimates of absolute mutational burden; all SNV-based comparisons in this study were therefore performed on coverage-controlled shared loci using O/E ratios, and the central conclusion that BPs and CSCs occupy late evolutionary positions rests on the convergence of four orthogonal analyses (SNV burden, CNV phylogeny, MEDALT evolutionary distance and cell-of-origin inference) rather than on any single method. A high mutation burden may also reflect active proliferation of BPs rather than their evolutionary position. Therefore, more direct lineage-tracing technologies will be required to definitively confirm the evolutionary trajectory of these BPs in the authentic human pathological microenvironment.

CSCs: late-stage dedifferentiation products of clonal evolution. The most challenging finding of this study is the questioning of the traditional “stem cell origin” hypothesis [55, 56]. Across SNV mutation burden, CNV accumulation, phylogenetic tree topology and RNA velocity, we obtained concordant evidence that CSCs identified by single-cell sequencing are not retained cells of tumour origin but rather arise through dedifferentiation from differentiated tumour cells.

However, this conclusion is also amenable to alternative explanations. The high mutation burden may stem from genomic instability of CSCs—the positive selection signals detected for CEP57L1 and PPP2R5E in CSCs by dN/dS analysis could themselves contribute to elevated mutation rates. The distal position of CSCs on phylogenetic trees can be interpreted in two ways: CSCs are late-stage evolutionary products (our model) or CSCs have the longest independent evolutionary time (still compatible with the origin hypothesis).

More importantly, the “CSCs” defined herein represent a current-state transcriptomic description, whereas the “tumour-initiating cells” of the classical hypothesis refer to a historical event. Even if our data demonstrate that the CSCs present in established tumours are late-stage dedifferentiation products, they cannot exclude the possibility that the tumour initially originated from a stem-cell-like cell. Strictly speaking, the hypothesis challenged by this study is that “CSCs in established tumours represent the evolutionary starting point of the tumour”, rather than “whether tumours originate from stem cells”.

Epigenetic memory: the molecular basis for CSC state maintenance. At the epigenetic level, this study constructed a complete DNMT upregulation → promoter hypermethylation → differentiation gene silencing regulatory chain, demonstrating that the DNMT family plays an important role in CSC maintenance of the dedifferentiated state. However, it is worth emphasising that DNMT upregulation may be a concomitant feature or a maintenance mechanism of the CSC state, rather than necessarily a direct driver of CSC formation. Elevated DNMTs in CSCs have been reported in pancreatic cancer [57], breast cancer [58] and other tumour types [59, 60]; our results further demonstrate that this is a conserved feature across multiple cancer types, suggesting that epigenetic reprogramming is a common pathway for CSC stemness maintenance.

Intriguingly, both EPIC-seq analysis and multiplex immunofluorescence confirmed that CSCs, while maintaining higher global methylation levels, exhibited lower epigenetic entropy—methylation values tended towards 0 or 1 (determined states) rather than drifting to 0.5 (disordered state). This observation contrasts with the well-known natural increase in epigenetic entropy during ageing and tumour evolution [61, 62]. We speculate that this “low-entropy” state reveals the active, energy-consuming nature of CSC stemness maintenance: as the core subpopulation retaining the tumour’s epigenetic “blueprint”, CSCs, driven by upstream transcription factors, must rely on DNMTs to continuously consume methyl donors (S-adenosylmethionine) and metabolic energy to overcome the natural tendency towards entropy increase. Notably, rather than contradicting the stemness of CSCs, this ordered low-entropy state fits our central model: CSCs in established HCC are not high-plasticity primitive ancestors but a late-stage, memory-locked product of tumour evolution, in which differentiation programmes are stably silenced rather than left in a poised, disordered configuration. The previous WGCNA finding that CSCs are specifically enriched for metabolic modules provides indirect evidence for this process. Therefore, therapeutic strategies targeting CSCs could focus on two directions: blocking specific metabolic pathways required for epigenetic homeostasis maintenance, or directly targeting core upstream transcription factors or intervening with downstream epigenetic modifying enzymes [63–65].

Correlation analysis of per-site methylation dynamics revealed that when non-CSC tumour cells re-acquired stemness, most pre-existing methylation differences were not recapitulated (R = 0.078), with only a small number of core imprint loci being re-established. From a translational perspective, these stably maintained loci represent the core programme by which CSCs maintain stemness, making them ideal drug targets: DNMT inhibitors directed at these loci may force CSCs to re-enter a differentiation trajectory by releasing the epigenetic lock on differentiation genes. The specific prediction of early recurrence by the core imprint score reflects the biological properties of CSCs. The search for robust molecular predictors of recurrence is an active goal across cancer types [66–68], yet predictors that exploit the epigenetic memory of CSCs for early HCC relapse remain scarce. Late recurrence may be related to the cancer field effect, although unfortunately large-scale tumour-adjacent survival data are not available to verify this conjecture.

Conclusions

In summary, this study established the evolutionary trajectory from normal BPs to CSCs in HCC: hepatocytes dedifferentiate into BPs with mutation accumulation; after malignant transformation, a subset of tumour cells acquire a stable stemness state through epigenetic reprogramming. This framework exploits mutational information provided by single-cell transcriptomics, integrated with transcriptional-level data, to offer a new exploration methodology for understanding the cellular origin and stemness maintenance of human tumours, and provides a theoretical reference for therapeutic strategies targeting CSC epigenetic memory.
