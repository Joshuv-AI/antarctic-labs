Approaching background sounds extend the duration of foreground auditory stimuli
Authors: Achille Pasqualotto, Hiroto Kawarada
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Time estimation is crucial to deal with external objects, either for their timely avoidance or interaction. Studies on the effect of approaching sounds on time estimation reported that these stimuli produce time overestimations, generally interpreted as an acceleration of the internal clock due to the attention/arousal prompted by potential approaching objects. Yet, in these studies the moving sounds were also the targets of time estimations, potentially generating confounds. Here we investigated the effect of background moving sounds on the perceived duration of separate foreground stimuli. Moreover, we provided an explicit computational model illustrating how changes in background moving sounds affect time estimation. Three groups of blindfolded participants passively listened to (background) approaching, receding, or scrambled (control) motion sounds, which were rendered using sensory substitution. Meanwhile, participants were presented with sinewave tones of different durations, which were estimated by participants using the reproduction method. The group exposed to the approaching background sound significantly overestimated the duration of the tones. This suggests that even background moving sounds can elicit the attentional/perceptual mechanism responsible for accelerating the internal clock. Moreover, shorter tones tended to be overestimated, and longer ones underestimated (Vierordt effect), indicating Bayesian regression toward the mean duration, which was successfully captured by our model.

Introduction

To catch a ball that somebody has thrown to us we must infer when the ball will reach a given position and aptly reach that position before the arrival of the ball. This simple example underlines the importance of accurate time processing, which has been necessary for our survival since prehistoric times (e.g. hunting moving animals) to nowadays (e.g. driving a car, playing ball games, etc.). Unsurprisingly, time processing has been studied since the early days of experimental psychology1,2. Decades of research empirically demonstrated that the perceived time is not entirely based on the physical time, but it is affected by external information (e.g. number of events occurring in a given period), internal states (e.g. emotions), and personal characteristics such as age3, but for exhaustive reviews please see Eagleman (2008)4 and Vicario (2005)5. For example, visual and auditory stimuli preceding a target stimulus ‘stretch’ the perceived duration of the target6,7, while visual and auditory stimuli following a target stimulus ‘shrink’ its perceived duration8. Another example is that the perceived duration of a stimulus is affected by its onset; an abrupt onset stretches the perceived duration of that stimulus9. Attentional engagement plays a role too; when it is divided between a time estimation task and a non-temporal task (e.g. solving subtractions) it has been reported that time shrinks10,11. Likewise, the emotional state of an individual affects time estimation, with research generally suggesting that negative emotions stretch the perceived time duration12–14. If time perception can be biased by so many variables, is there any empirical model that can capture such complexity?

Since the early days of experimental psychology, this question was answered by postulating an “internal clock” based on biological cycles such the oscillations of brain neurons15,16, which embodies our subjective time, and which can be accelerated or decelerated by the internal and external information we mentioned above7,17–21. Later, proponents of the Bayesian theory22 put forward that our subjective time is the “weighted sum” of the current perceptual information and the past experience23,24. In more recent times, there has been an effort to merge internal clock and Bayesian theories about time perception, where an internal clock is complemented by past experience (Bayesian inference)25. This theoretical digression about the internal clock and Bayesian inference will be relevant for our computational model (see below).

In the present study, we investigated time estimation during the contextual presentation of moving sound sources, either approaching (to the listener), receding, or in a scrambled/control fashion. The choice was motivated by the inherent relevance of this topic for our survival (e.g. to ‘timely’ avoid collisions with moving objects). Importantly, moving objects can be outside one’s visual field (e.g. coming from the backside), they can be occluded by other objects, or it could be too dark to see, hence highlighting the relevance of auditory input. Past studies on the duration of ramped26,27 sounds (with increasing loudness) reported that they were perceived as longer than damped sounds. The underlying mechanism might be the “discounting of the tail” of damped sounds from their total duration, because it would be interpreted as an echo26,27. However, although it is difficult to separate them28, in our study loudness was kept constant while we modulated the intensity of the background sounds. Studies on looming sounds28,29(with increasing intensity) reported that these stimuli were perceived as longer than those receding (with decreasing intensity), which has been explained in a variety of ways (for example, tail discounting28, salience of the looming sounds30, and lack of salience of the receding31.

Yet, in previous studies participants explicitly attended and estimated the duration of dynamic sounds, while here we manipulated the direction of background sounds (which were not the target of estimation). Therefore, approaching/receding/scrambled background sounds, which were not explicitly attended, were used to manipulate the time estimation of the same foreground stimuli (using the reproduction method). We predict that the approaching background sound would accelerate subjective time, thus resulting in the overestimation of the duration of target stimuli. An approaching sound source is likely to capture attention and/or increase arousal to select the appropriate reactions (avoidance of or reaching for the approaching object)32–34.

Indeed, approaching sounds activate brain areas involved in arousal and attention such as the insula, the cingulate cortex, and the posterior parietal cortex35. Another intriguing result of past studies was the Vierordt effect, where short durations were overestimated and long durations were underestimated36,37, which may be explained by the Bayesian tendency to regress estimations towards a previously-learned mean. Curiously, this trend was found in classical studies where participants estimated the travelled distance38,39, thus indicating a link between time and space estimations. Therefore, we expect to find evidence of the Vierordt effect.

Background motion sounds were generated using a sensory substitution device (the vOICe), a software converting visual input into auditory output, which was originally developed to help visually impaired individuals40, and later was used for basic research41–44. More precisely, this software scans the images captured by a camera from-left-to-right, turns them into greyscale, segments them into pixels, and converts each pixel into a sound based on its position and luminance. Although this conversion may seem arbitrary, it has been showed it actually follows “innate association” among sensory modalities45. For example, a study by Evans and Treisman (2011)46 reported that participants were quicker to report the correct position of visual stimuli as high or low when they were associated with auditory stimuli with congruent pitches (e.g. a high-pitched sound with a high positioned stimulus). Here we used a simple visual stimulus (a monochromatic triangle), which was converted into sound (“sonified”) to create the three types of background moving sounds. While listening to one of these backgrounds, blindfolded participants were presented with sinewave tones of different durations, and their task was to estimate the duration of the tones using the reproduction method. As abovementioned, we expect to find an accelerating effect of the approaching sound on time estimation. Moreover, the resulting computational model integrating internal clock and Bayesian accounts will provide an explicit equation on how background moving sounds quantitatively affect time duration, which is currently absent.

Materials and methods

Participants

Forty-eight participants (24 males and 24 females) aged on average 22.45 years with a standard deviation (SD) of 2.68 were recruited at the University of Tsukuba. The sample size was determined using G*Power 3.1.9.747 considering a small effect size. Participants were free from any auditory impairment. They signed the informed consent form and were randomly assigned to one of the three auditory conditions (16 participants each): Approaching (22.54 years old, SD = 1.27), Receding (21.97, SD = 1.46), and Scrambled (21.25, SD = 1.22). The study was approved by University of Tsukuba Research Ethics Committee (code 2023–57 A and 2024–201 A) and thus was compliant with the ethical guidelines of the Declaration of Helsinki. Participants received 500 Japanese Yen (JPY) for their participation.

Background motion sounds

Using the sensory substitution software the vOICe (www.seeingwithsound.com) the image of a horizontally oriented monochromatic triangle (Fig. 1a) was sonified into its auditory equivalent. As during sonification an image is scanned from-left-to-right, our triangle would create a sound of increasing intensity, compatible with the sound of an approaching object (Fig. 1b). Using Audacity 3.5.1 (www.audacityteam.org), a sound processing software, we “reversed” the approaching motion sound to create the receding motion sound. Finally, the approaching sound was “cut” into five equivalent segments (1/2/3/4/5) that were pseudo-randomly rearranged (3/1/5/2/4) to create the scrambled motion sound. Each background motion sound lasted 2 s, and the spectral analysis indicated that at their peak they had a frequency of 852 Hz and an intensity of -33.1 dB.

Tones

Using Audacity, six sinewave tones were generated; their durations were 1000, 2000, 3000, 4000, 5000, and 6000 ms; the spectral analysis indicated that at their peak they had a frequency of 311 Hz and an intensity of -2.7 dB.

Sinewave tones were chosen for sake of clarity, because they are markedly different from the background motion sounds (see Fig. 1c).

Procedure

The experimental session started with a PowerPoint presentation that explained the procedure, in particular how tones had to be estimated (i.e. by holding down the spacebar for the estimated duration of the tone and then release it). Afterwards, participants wore a blindfold, headphones, and were randomly assigned to one experimental group (Approaching/Receding/Scrambled). To equally habituate participants to the assigned background motion sound, motion sounds started being played one minute before giving any tasks to participants (see Uesaki et al.48), and continued being played until the conclusion of the time reproduction task. VLC media player (www.videolan.org/vlc) was used to continuously play the background motion sounds. After the habituation, the reproduction task started and E-Prime (Psychology Software Tools, USA) was used to randomly present each of the six tones for four times (24 trials in total). Importantly, as tones’ sequential order was entirely random and all tones were equally presented, our design is likely to be free from biases due to previous exposure and assimilation49–51. After each tone ended, participants immediately estimated its duration by pressing and holding down the spacebar for the amount of time they believed the tone was played for (and then released it). No feedback about accuracy was provided. The following trial started automatically. The inceptions of background motion sounds and target tones were not synchronised. Participants were instructed to pay attention to the duration of the tones and to ignore the “background noise” (for the participants, this was the name of the background motion sounds). The entire experiment lasted about twenty-five minutes.

Results

Pre-processing

The aim of this experiment was to investigate whether time perception is affected by background auditory information compatible with moving objects (approaching/receding/scrambled). For each participant we calculated the average estimation error percentage (characterised by a positive sign for overestimation and a negative sign for underestimation, dependent variable) across the three background motion sounds (between-subjects variable) and the six tone durations (within-subject). We noticed that one participant in the Scrambled group had very large estimation errors. A closer inspection confirmed that for four-out-of-six tone durations (i.e. the majority) estimation errors were larger than two standard deviations. We proceeded to remove this participant from further analyses, which resulted in a sample of 47 participants. See Table 1 for a summary.

Main analysis

Average estimation error percentages were processed using a two-way mixed ANOVA with Motion sound (approaching vs. receding vs. scrambled) and Tone (1000 vs. 2000 vs. 3000 vs. 4000 vs. 5000 vs. 6000) as factors. Motion Sound was significant [F(2, 44) = 3.857, p = 0.029, ηp2 = 0.149], and Bonferroni-corrected pairwise comparisons confirmed that when hearing an Approaching motion sound participants produced larger time overestimations (14.98%, SD = 24.57 [or 242.82 ms, SD = 740.11]) than when hearing a Receding motion sound (-5.95%, SD = 24.72 [or -337.93 ms, SD = 766.82]) [p = 0.034]. The comparisons involving the Scrambled motion sound (-1.58%, SD = 16.72 [or -203.74 ms, SD = 423.37) were not significant [all p > 0.05] (see Fig. 2a).

Tone was significant [F(1.879, 82.666) = 9.610, p < 0.001, ηp2 = 0.179] (the Greenhouse–Geisser correction was applied to adjust for the lack of sphericity). Bonferroni-corrected pairwise comparisons essentially showed that longer tones produced larger time underestimations than shorter tones (Vierordt effect37. Indeed, the 6000 ms tone (-14.36%, SD = 11.79 [or -814.84 ms, SD = 697.16]) was more underestimated than the 4000 ms tone (-2.05%, SD = 18.39 [(or -49.51 ms, SD = 767.73]) [p < 0.001], the 3000 ms tone (8.97%, SD = 25.36 [or 305.28 ms, SD = 757.94]) [p < 0.001], the 2000 ms tone (12.30%, SD = 25.68 [or 268.52 ms, SD = 447.72]) [p < 0.001], and the 1000 ms tone (15.75%, SD = 29.30 [or 157.54 ms, SD = 307.83]) [p = 0.022]. The 5000 ms tone (-8.78%, SD = 11.41 [or -418.74 ms, SD = 619.72]) was more underestimated than the 4000 ms tone [p = 0.033], the 3000 ms tone [p < 0.001], and the 2000 ms tone [p < 0.001]. Finally, the 4000 ms tone was more underestimated than the 3000 ms tone [p < 0.001], and the 2000 ms tone [p = 0.007]. The other comparisons were not significant (see Fig. 2b).

The interaction Motion sound x Tone was not significant [F(3.758, 82.666) = 0.830, p = 0.504, ηp2 = 0.036]

Computational model introduction

The model aims to account for two phenomena observed in the data. First, the attentional/arousal effect: the rate of intensity change in background motion sound modulates attention and arousal, which in turn bias time estimation via pacemaker speed changes (Approaching → overestimation, Receding → underestimation). Second, the Vierordt effect: estimated durations regress towards the mean of experienced durations, captured via Bayesian integration with a learned prior (see below).

An amplitude-envelope measure (mean absolute amplitude) was computed as a practical intensity-like measure for modelling, extracted from the provided audio files (2 s each) in 10 ms windows. The profiles were trimmed to the 50–1950 ms interval (1900 ms, 190 data points) to remove potential edge noise.

Intensity was normalised to 0–1 for each condition, because prior research on looming effects has shown that the temporal pattern of intensity change, rather than absolute intensity, primarily determines the effect52,53.

Parameters estimation and evaluation

As above-mentioned, intensity change rates were calculated using \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\overline{dI/dt}$$\end{document}. The Approaching condition had intensity change rate of + 0.333 /s, indicating a rising intensity (positive); the Receding condition had − 0.349 /s, indicating a falling intensity (negative); and the Scrambled condition had − 0.019 /s, where intensity fluctuations largely cancel-out (near zero). Using these values, the parameters were estimated by minimising the sum of squared errors across 18 data points (3 background motion sound conditions × 6 tone durations) via differential evolution53, yielding\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\:w$$\end{document} = 0.791 (weight on sensory input) and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\gamma\:$$\end{document} = +0.230 (coefficient of intensity change rate), with \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\mu\:}_{\mathrm{prior}}$$\end{document} = 3.5 s (fixed). The estimated \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:w=0.791\:$$\end{document}indicates that participants placed approximately 79% weight on the actual duration, with approximately 21% pulled towards the prior (3.5 s). Because w<1, estimates regress towards the prior mean, producing the Vierordt effect. The positive sign of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\gamma\:=+0.230$$\end{document} is consistent with the arousal hypothesis: rising intensity accelerates the internal clock, lengthening perceived duration.

Substituting these parameter values into the model equation yields condition-wise mean predicted errors of + 0.27 s (overestimation) for Approaching, − 0.28 s (underestimation) for Receding, and − 0.02 s (near-neutral) for Scrambled. Compared with the experimental data (Approaching: +0.24 s, Receding: −0.34 s, Scrambled: −0.20 s), predictions for Approaching and Receding closely match the observed values. The Scrambled prediction (− 0.02 s) deviates from the data (− 0.20 s), suggesting that random fluctuations in the background sound still attracted a substantial amount of attention/arousal. Nevertheless, the condition-wise ordering (Approaching > Scrambled > Receding) is maintained in both the empirical data and the model, supporting qualitative validity. The model-predicted Vierordt slopes (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\beta\:$$\end{document}; Eq. 5) are 0.85 (Approaching), 0.73 (Receding), and 0.79 (Scrambled), all below 1, confirming central tendency regression in every condition.

Model fit was evaluated using coefficient of determination (R²) and root mean square error (RMSE). The model achieved an overall R² of 0.970 (97.0% of variance explained) and RMSE of 0.24 s, with condition-specific R² values of 0.966 (Approaching), 0.970 (Receding), and 0.973 (Scrambled). Residual diagnostics confirmed no systematic bias, with non-significant correlations between residuals and duration (r = 0.014, p = 0.957), between residuals and predicted values (r = 0.011, p = 0.966), and no significant differences across conditions (F(2, 15) = 0.86, p = 0.444). In addition, Leave-One-Out Cross-Validation (LOOCV) was conducted to assess overfitting; each of the 18 data points was held out in turn, with the model refitted on the remaining 17 points to predict the held-out point. The resulting CV-R² of 0.959, representing only a 1.1% drop from the full-data R², indicates minimal overfitting. Overall, the model captures the main patterns with no unexplained systematic structure in the residuals.

Discussion

This study investigated the effect of background moving sounds on the estimation of time durations, and we discovered that an approaching background sound significantly accelerated the perceived time when compared to a receding sound. This result advances our knowledge on the effect of moving sounds on time estimation by elucidating the role background moving sounds. This result is consistent with the studies investigating the duration of looming sounds used as the targets of the time estimation28,30, which reported an equivalent phenomenon. As mentioned in the Introduction, we suggest that this may reflect a greater involvement of arousal and attention as postulated by the attentional gate11 and scalar expectancy (SET)7 theories. Since approaching background sounds are more likely to require timely responses, the subjective acceleration of time might be a consequence of this state of readiness and alertness55,56. On the other hand, when the background motion sound was receding, thus compatible with an object moving away from the listener, the arousal/attentional mechanism was less involved, and time durations were underestimated. Finally, in the scrambled condition the movement implied by the sound was mixed: approaching and receding sounds alternated and, in term of effects on time estimation, this condition was not significantly different from the previous two. Although speculative, this finding still fits with the attentional explanation, as the scrambled sound is a mix of approaching and receding sounds (see Background motion sounds), it is likely to require an amount of attention intermediate to the approaching and receding conditions.

Our general explanation is supported by the studies on the detection of moving targets; they reported that detection is faster for targets closer to the observers57–59, thus supporting the engagement of the attention10,11,60–63. Additionally, we found evidence for the Vierordt effect, with a significant difference between estimations of shorter and longer time durations where the former are overestimated and the latter underestimated37, which exemplifies Bayesian regression to the mean23.

Yet, considering alternative explanations, the instructive review by Matthews and Meck (2016)64 provides compelling evidence for perceptual (Sect. 2), attentional (Sect. 3), and memory (Sect. 4) mechanisms underlying subjective time estimation. In their Sect. 3, they provide evidence that the stimuli attracting more attentional resources are perceived as lasting longer. In our case, listening to the approaching background sound is likely to have attracted more attentional resources resulting in a faster internal clock, which produced an overestimation of the duration of the target tones. In Sect. 2 they show how the “perceptual salience” of a stimulus increases its perceived duration as well. Perceptual salience is defined as the physical attributes of a stimulus, like luminance, intensity, numerosity, etc. In our case, background moving sounds had the same intensity, but we manipulated their intensity change rate: increasing (approaching), decreasing (receding), and a mix of the previous two (scrambled). Thus, in our case, it is likely that the intensity change rate, which a perceptual feature of the stimuli, may speed-up the internal clock resulting in the overestimation of the target tones (see the computational model). Indeed, perceptual salience should be considered to interpret our results. In Sect. 4 they describe how the repeated exposure to a stimulus (adaptation/assimilation) reduces the subjective duration of that stimulus, thus implying a role of memory. However, in our case the three background moving sounds were equally played to participants: for one minute before the reproduction task, and during the reproduction task, which had a fairly constat duration for each participant. This effectively equated the amount of exposure to the background moving sounds. Besides, each of the six target tones was randomly presented for four times each (see Procedure). This equated the exposure to the target tones. In sum, by equating the exposure to all our experimental stimuli, if memory played a major role in our study, then we should have found equivalent time estimation performances for all background moving sounds, which was not the case. Thus, we reasonably conclude that memory did not play a significant role in our study.

Finally, an alternative explanation of our results could be afforded by some studies on looming sounds28,65,66, which postulated that receding sounds are perceived as shorter because of the discounting of their fading tails27. In our case, the receding background sound should have triggered the discounting the tails of our six pure tones, which nevertheless do not possess any “tail”, but their intensity and loudness are constant. Although this is possible, it is unlikely. Thus, we favour the explanation based on background sounds modulating the attention/arousal.

Turning to the model’s quantitative accuracy, the model prediction for the Scrambled condition (− 0.02 s) showed some discrepancy from the observed value (− 0.20 s). Although the Scrambled condition fell statistically between Approaching and Receding as discussed above, this discrepancy may merit further investigation. One possibility is that the abrupt intensity jumps9 where the rearranged segments are joined, repeatedly diverted attention from temporal processing of the foreground tone. Attentional competition between concurrent tasks is known to shorten perceived duration11, and the moment-to-moment irregularity of the Scrambled sound may have similarly competed for attentional resources that would otherwise be allocated to temporal processing. Whereas our model captures attention/arousal as a factor that accelerates the internal clock, this would represent an opposing effect in which attention is drawn away from timing. Incorporating a parameter that quantifies the degree of irregularity in the intensity profile could potentially address this discrepancy in future modelling work.

A final point of interest is that to generate the background moving sounds we used a sensory substitution device to generate acoustic increases (and decreases) of intensity, which were able to affect time perception. To our knowledge, this is the first time that stimuli created using sensory substitution are used to study time perception, thus being of methodological relevance. Additionally, this result contributes to the argument about the usability of sensory substitution devices, reinforcing the view that these tools can produce experiences very similar to those provided by vision67–69.

A limitation of our study is that it used a time reproduction task70,71, which requires participants to experience a time duration and then replicate it. Would results be different if another task were to be used? For example, in a time production task participants are not presented with any time durations, but are required to produce time durations (e.g. “please, press the spacebar for three seconds”). As the attentional and arousal mechanisms would still be at play, we predict that the same result will be replicated using a production task, nevertheless this should be established in follow-up experiments. Likewise, another follow-up study may investigate whether these results generalise to background sounds moving at different speeds (faster/slower) than the current stimuli. Our prediction is that, as in the present study, faster approaching background sounds will significantly speed time perception, because they would strongly engage attention. We remain agnostic about the results relative to the slower background sounds and wait for the actual results.

Another limitation is that our study cannot clarify whether background motion sounds affected the duration of the target tones per se, or whether it affected the perceived onset/offset or the target tones, which would require separate tasks where participants react to the onset/offset of the target tones72.

Each background moving sound lasted 2 s and was continuously repeated before and during the time reproduction task. Thus, in principle it is possible that participants might have counted the number of repetitions to estimate the amount of elapsed time to judge the duration of the target tones. However, participants were explicitly informed that background “noise” had to be ignored. They were not aware that each repetition lasted 2 s. Finally, the inceptions of repetitions and target tones were not synchronised but random, thus further reducing the incentive of counting the repetitions (if any). In sum, this renders very unlikely the supposition that participants counted the number of repetitions to perform the task. Moreover, if participants were simply counting the number of repetitions to estimate the duration of the tones, we should have found no estimation differences across the three types of background sounds, and no differences across the six tone durations, which was not the case.

Our model achieved a high explanatory power by capturing, first, the modulation of the internal clock by intensity changes in background motion sound, and second, the Bayesian tendency to regress time estimates towards the prior mean. However, alternative modelling approaches remain open. For example, within the predictive processing framework73, the positive slope may heighten sensitivity to prediction errors, leading to more precise temporal encoding —effectively increasing w (measurement precision) rather than requiring a separate multiplicative term. Additionally, the current model is based on group-averaged data, however, hierarchical Bayesian models could capture individual differences in time perception74.

Concluding, our study showed for the first time that background moving sounds modulate time estimation, improving our knowledge within the field of time processing, and having implications for fields such as web design, virtual reality, and gaming.
