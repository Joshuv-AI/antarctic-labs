Long non-coding RNA landscape and ceRNA networks underlying the drought response of Cannabis sativa L.
Authors: Deniz Sarel, Nilgün Yerli, İlker Büyük
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDrought is a major constraint on plant productivity, and long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs) are increasingly recognized as key regulators of plant stress responses. Yet drought-responsive lncRNAs remain largely uncharacterized in Cannabis sativa L., a species of growing economic and medicinal importance.MethodsWe combined RNA-seq analysis of C. sativa leaves under progressive drought stress with physiological monitoring and independent qRT-PCR validation. A stringent identification pipeline integrating StringTie assembly with CPC2 and Pfam coding-potential filtering was applied, followed by differential expression analysis (DESeq2), cis-target prediction, functional enrichment, ceRNA network construction, and transcription-factor profiling.ResultsThe pipeline yielded 2,096 high-confidence novel lncRNAs with the canonical structural signatures of plant lncRNAs — shorter length, simpler exonic architecture, and lower steady-state expression than mRNAs. Differential expression analysis identified 51 high-confidence differentially expressed lncRNAs and 575 differentially expressed mRNAs in response to drought. Functional enrichment of predicted lncRNA cis-targets highlighted phenylpropanoid and lignin biosynthesis, abscisic acid signaling, and vacuolar ion transport as dominant themes. Transcription-factor profiling revealed a striking over-representation of FAR1-family regulators among lncRNA targets. A DEL-anchored ceRNA sub-network further uncovered two candidate regulatory modules, both showing directionally concordant qRT-PCR expression profiles.ConclusionsThese results provide the first systematic characterisation of polyA-selected, leaf-expressed drought-responsive lncRNAs in C. sativa, propose a hypothesis of lncRNA-mediated light–drought crosstalk via FAR1/FHY3 regulators, requiring future functional validation, and nominate candidate regulatory nodes for future functional studies and stress-resilience breeding.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12870-026-09380-2.

Background

Drought is one of the most pervasive and damaging abiotic stresses constraining global agricultural productivity, a challenge that is projected to intensify under ongoing climate change scenarios [1]. Water deficit triggers a cascade of interconnected physiological responses in plants, including stomatal closure, reduced photosynthetic efficiency, osmotic imbalance, and the overproduction of reactive oxygen species (ROS), which collectively lead to oxidative damage, growth inhibition, and, in severe cases, plant mortality [2, 3]. To survive under such adverse conditions, plants have evolved sophisticated adaptive mechanisms that operate at the morphological, physiological, and molecular levels, including the dynamic reprogramming of gene expression networks [4]. Deciphering the molecular architecture of these stress-responsive regulatory networks is therefore essential for improving crop resilience and ensuring food security in an era of increasing aridity.

Cannabis sativa L. (2n = 20) is an ancient, multi-purpose crop belonging to the family Cannabaceae, originating from Central Asia and cultivated for over 6,000 years for its fiber, oilseed, and medicinal properties [5]. With the progressive legalization of industrial hemp cultivation across numerous countries in the past two decades, the global hemp industry has experienced a remarkable resurgence, driven by increasing demand for sustainable raw materials and the recognition of hemp’s environmental and health benefits [6]. Beyond its industrial and pharmaceutical applications — most notably as a source of cannabidiol (CBD) and other non-psychoactive cannabinoids — C. sativa has garnered increasing attention as a sustainable bio-resource for textiles, bioplastics, construction materials, and nutritional products [7, 8]. Despite the growing economic significance of this crop, its yield and quality are highly susceptible to drought stress, which represents a major abiotic constraint in hemp cultivation, particularly in semi-arid and Mediterranean climate zones [9, 10]. However, the molecular mechanisms underlying drought tolerance in C. sativa remain poorly characterized at the transcriptomic and post-transcriptional regulatory levels.

In eukaryotic genomes, while the vast majority of the DNA is transcribed, only a small fraction (2–5%) encodes proteins [11]. The remaining transcriptional output comprises diverse classes of non-coding RNAs (ncRNAs), which were historically dismissed as transcriptional noise but are now recognized as key regulators of gene expression [12]. Among these, long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs) — defined as transcripts exceeding 200 nucleotides in length with minimal or no protein-coding potential — have emerged as critical modulators of cellular processes through a variety of mechanisms, including chromatin remodeling, transcriptional regulation via cis- and trans-acting effects on neighboring and distal genes, and post-transcriptional control of mRNA stability and translation [13]. A particularly important post-transcriptional mechanism involves the competing endogenous RNA (ceRNA) hypothesis, whereby lncRNAs act as molecular sponges by competitively binding to shared miRNAs, thereby relieving miRNA-mediated repression of target mRNAs and indirectly controlling gene expression [14, 15]. LncRNAs contribute critically to plant growth, development, and stress adaptation, highlighting their promising applications in crop breeding [13, 16].

In the plant kingdom, accumulating evidence from model and crop species has established that lncRNAs participate in the regulation of diverse developmental programs and stress responses. A landmark discovery in this field was the identification of DROUGHT INDUCED lncRNA (DRIR) in Arabidopsis thaliana, which positively regulates drought and salt stress tolerance [17]. Since then, drought-responsive lncRNAs have been characterized in an expanding range of crops, including cotton [18], wheat [19], rice [20], barley [21], peanut [22], and rapeseed [23, 24]. In cotton, for instance, the novel drought-responsive lncRNA 1 (DRL1) was demonstrated to operate as a ceRNA by competitively binding miR477b, thereby de-repressing its target genes GhNAC1 and GhSCL3 and positively regulating drought tolerance in seedlings [18]. Similarly, in wheat, lncRNA35557 was shown to function as an endogenous target mimic of tae-miR6206, preventing the cleavage of TaNAC018 and enhancing drought tolerance [19]. In rice, a drought-responsive ceRNA network centered on the TCONS_00021861/miR528-3p/YUCCA7 regulatory axis was found to modulate auxin biosynthesis and confer drought resistance [20]. These findings collectively underscore the pivotal role of lncRNA-mediated ceRNA networks in fine-tuning plant stress adaptation.

Despite the accumulating evidence for the functional importance of lncRNAs in plant stress biology, research on the non-coding transcriptome of C. sativa has remained remarkably limited. To date, only a small number of studies have investigated lncRNAs in hemp. Wu et al. [25] predicted 7,783 lncRNAs in the industrial hemp cultivar ‘Yunma No.1’ and reported their potential regulatory roles in cannabinoid, fatty acid, and cellulose biosynthesis. Zhang et al. [26] provided the first genome-wide characterization of natural antisense transcripts (NATs) in C. sativa, and a recent transcriptomic study identified 3,245 lncRNA candidates with tissue-specific expression patterns co-expressed with secondary metabolic genes [27]. However, no study has yet performed a comprehensive, genome-wide identification and functional characterization of novel lncRNAs in C. sativa under drought stress conditions, and the potential role of lncRNA-mediated ceRNA regulatory networks in hemp drought adaptation remains entirely unexplored.

In the present study, we performed a systematic genome-wide identification and characterization of novel lncRNAs in the leaves of the C. sativa cultivar Pink Pepper (isolate KNU-18-1) subjected to drought stress, utilizing publicly available RNA-seq data (BioProject: PRJNA1108719) [28]. The promising computationally identified and characterized lncRNAs were independently validated by quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) on local C. sativa (Narlısaray cv.) plant material grown under replicated drought stress conditions, complemented by physiological measurements using the PhotosynQ platform to confirm the establishment of drought stress. To our knowledge, this represents the first genome-wide analysis of drought-responsive lncRNAs and ceRNA networks in Cannabis sativa, providing novel insights into the non-coding regulatory landscape governing drought adaptation in this economically important crop.

Methods

RNA-seq dataset retrieval

To identify drought-responsive lncRNAs at the genome-wide level, publicly available RNA-seq datasets were retrieved from the NCBI Sequence Read Archive (SRA) under BioProject accession PRJNA1108719. Three biological replicates each of drought-stressed (DS; SRX24485623, SRX24485624, SRX24485625) and control (CK; SRX24485620, SRX24485621, SRX24485622) Cannabis sativa leaf samples were selected for analysis. These datasets were originally generated from adult C. sativa cultivar Pink Pepper (isolate KNU-18-1) plants subjected to drought stress by withholding irrigation for 10 days until severe stress symptoms were observed, as described by Ryu et al. [28].

Plant material and growth conditions for physiological assessment and qRT-PCR validation

Seeds of the local, national Turkish C. sativa cultivar (cv. Narlısaray) were germinated and grown in a controlled growth chamber at 25 ± 2 °C, with a relative humidity of 60 ± 5% and a 16-h light/8-h dark photoperiod. Healthy, uniform seedlings at the four-leaf stage were randomly divided into two treatment groups: a control group (CK) maintained at field capacity (~ 70% substrate water content) by daily watering to a constant target pot weight, and a drought-stressed group (DS) in which irrigation was withheld for the duration of the experiment. Six biological replicates per treatment were established for non-destructive physiological monitoring (pot weight, PhotosynQ). For end-point qRT-PCR validation, three biological replicates per treatment were retained from this set after quality-based selection that excluded plants whose severe drought-induced tissue damage compromised RNA integrity (see Section 2.10). The growth conditions and drought stress protocol were designed to closely replicate those of Ryu et al. [28] and Büyük et al. [29] in order to maintain biological consistency between the public RNA-seq dataset and the validation experiments performed in this study.

For transcriptomic validation, young, fully expanded leaves were harvested from three biological replicates per treatment on the final day of the stress period, immediately snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at − 80 °C until RNA extraction. Because qRT-PCR quantification requires high-quality, uniformly performing cDNA templates, a pre-analytical quality control step was implemented prior to replicate selection (see Section 2.10).

Day 8 was selected as the terminal time point for physiological and molecular sampling based on the in-house physiological monitoring data: at this stage, plants in the DS group exhibited severe inward leaf rolling, growth arrest, and near-irreversible wilting, a phenotype consistent with the extreme stress state reported for the Day 10 sampling point of the original RNA-seq dataset [28]. Harvesting at Day 8 therefore ensured that the validation tissues reflected a comparably severe drought stress condition while preserving RNA integrity and avoiding tissue necrosis that could compromise downstream molecular analyses.

Collectively, the selection of Day 8 as the terminal sampling point was informed by a triangulation of (i) pot-weight-based drought severity (− 71.7 ± 0.5% relative to Day 0), (ii) the collapse of PSII photochemical efficiency captured by PhotosynQ readings up to Day 6, and (iii) direct phenotypic observations of inward leaf rolling and growth arrest, which together defined a physiological state of severe but sub-necrotic drought stress suitable for downstream RNA extraction and qRT-PCR validation.

Physiological evaluation of progressive drought stress

To dynamically monitor the physiological impact of progressive drought stress, photosynthetic parameters were measured every two days (Day 0, 2, 4, 6, and 8) throughout the experimental period. Measurements were performed on the third fully expanded leaf from the apex of both CK and DS plants using a MultispeQ v2.0 device linked to the PhotosynQ platform (PhotosynQ Inc., East Lansing, MI, USA) [30]. The key photosynthetic parameters recorded were the quantum yield of photosystem II (ΦPSII), the yield of regulated non-photochemical quenching (ΦNPQ), and the photochemical quenching coefficient (qL), which together provide complementary readouts of photochemical efficiency and photoprotective energy dissipation under water deficit. Owing to progressive inward leaf rolling and morphological deformation that compromised clamp–leaf contact, reliable non-destructive PhotosynQ readings could only be obtained through Day 6; physiological measurements beyond this time point were therefore omitted. All measurements were consistently taken within a narrow mid-photoperiod window (14:00–16:00) in order to minimise circadian variation in photosynthetic parameters and to ensure that plants were in a photosynthetically active steady state, thereby maintaining cross-day comparability of ΦPSII, ΦNPQ, and qL readings across the sampling series. Six biological replicates per treatment were measured at each time point, and statistical significance between CK and DS groups at each time point was evaluated using Student’s t-test ( p < 0.05; p < 0.01; p < 0.001).

Read processing and reference genome alignment

Raw FASTQ files were downloaded from the SRA using the SRA Toolkit (fasterq-dump, v3.0). Read quality was assessed with FastQC (v0.11.9), and raw reads were processed with Trimmomatic (v0.39) to remove adapter sequences and low-quality bases using the following parameters: LEADING:3, TRAILING:3, SLIDINGWINDOW:4:15, and MINLEN:36. The original libraries were prepared using the TruSeq Stranded mRNA Library Prep Kit (Illumina Inc., San Diego, CA, USA) with poly-A selection and paired-end sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform. Although poly-A selection primarily targets poly-adenylated coding transcripts, a substantial proportion of plant lncRNAs carry poly-A tails, enabling their robust capture under this preparation strategy [31]. Nevertheless, non-polyadenylated, tissue-restricted, or very lowly expressed lncRNAs are inherently under-represented in poly(A)-selected libraries; the 2,096-lncRNA set described here is therefore most accurately interpreted as a poly(A)-selected, leaf-expressed landscape rather than a comprehensive lncRNA repertoire of C. sativa, as explicitly acknowledged in the Discussion.

Clean reads were mapped to the most recent chromosome-level C. sativa reference genome (Assembly: GCF_029168945.1) using HISAT2 (v2.2.1) with the --dta option. Mapped reads were sorted and indexed with SAMtools (v1.17) prior to transcript assembly, and alignment statistics were summarized with samtools flagstat.

Genome-wide identification and characterization of novel lncRNAs

Reference-guided transcript assembly was performed using StringTie (v3.0.3) with the -G option on each sample individually, and sample-level assemblies were merged into a unified, non-redundant transcript set with the StringTie --merge utility. Structural comparison against the reference annotation was performed using gffcompare to assign class codes to assembled transcripts.

A stringent, multi-step pipeline was employed to identify high-confidence novel lncRNAs. First, only transcripts designated as novel by StringTie (“MSTRG” identifiers) and classified by gffcompare as intergenic (class code ‘u’), intronic (‘i’), or antisense (‘x’) relative to annotated protein-coding genes were retained as candidates. Transcripts overlapping annotated protein-coding genes on the same strand—including those classified as potentially novel isoforms of annotated genes (class codes ‘j’, ‘k’, ‘m’), generic exonic overlaps (‘c’), sense fragments (‘s’), or generic antisense overlap with a reference intron (‘o’)—were excluded from the lncRNA candidate pool to prevent the misclassification of novel mRNA isoforms as lncRNAs. This conservative restriction additionally avoided redundancy with the dedicated genome-wide characterisation of natural antisense transcripts (NATs) in C. sativa by Zhang et al. [26], which provides a complementary resource for sense-overlapping and NAT-class lncRNAs not captured by our intergenic/intronic/antisense pipeline. Candidates were then length-filtered to retain transcripts longer than 200 nt. Consistent with recent transcriptomic evidence supporting the widespread existence and functional relevance of single-exon lncRNAs in plants, both multi-exon and single-exon transcripts passing subsequent filters were retained.

The coding potential of candidate transcripts was evaluated using the Coding Potential Calculator 2 (CPC2, v1.0.1), which integrates sequence features such as Fickett score, open reading frame integrity, and isoelectric point. To further exclude transcripts encoding known functional protein domains, six-frame translated open reading frames (≥ 30 aa) were searched against the Pfam-A database (v36.0) using HMMER (v3.4) with an E-value threshold of 0.001. Only transcripts predicted as non-coding by CPC2 and showing no significant Pfam hits were retained as high-confidence novel lncRNAs. The complete filtering cascade — including each step, its retention/exclusion criteria, and the milestone transcript counts — is summarised in Additional file 1: Fig. S1.

Differential expression analysis

Following the merge step, StringTie was re-run in quantitation-only mode (-e) on each sample’s alignments using the merged transcript set as a guide, and gene- and transcript-level count matrices were generated with the associated prepDE.py script. To minimize noise from lowly expressed transcripts, only transcripts achieving at least 1 count per million (CPM) in three or more samples (corresponding to the smallest group size) were retained.

Differential expression analysis between the DS and CK groups was performed on the raw count matrix using DESeq2 (v1.42.0), which employs a negative binomial generalized linear model with median-of-ratios internal normalization. Transcripts were considered significantly differentially expressed when they satisfied both an adjusted p (Benjamini–Hochberg) < 0.05 and |log₂(fold change)| > 1. In addition, a low-expression filter (DESeq2 baseMean ≥ 9 across all libraries) was applied to exclude poorly expressed transcripts whose fold-change estimates are statistically unreliable, in line with standard DESeq2 practice. To ensure the biological integrity of the non-coding dataset, differentially expressed entries that had not passed the complete CPC2 + Pfam lncRNA validation pipeline (i.e., novel mRNA isoforms or transcripts with predicted coding potential) were excluded from the final differentially expressed lncRNA (DEL) list, and were instead retained in the differentially expressed mRNA (DEG) pool if they corresponded to annotated protein-coding loci. For cross-sample visualizations, transcript abundances were expressed as transcripts per million (TPM) or as log₂(CPM) following the voom transformation where appropriate.

Target gene prediction and functional enrichment analysis

To investigate the regulatory potential of the drought-responsive DELs, putative cis-acting protein-coding target genes were identified as annotated genes located within a 100-kb genomic window upstream or downstream of each DEL using BEDTools (v2.30.0), yielding 494 candidate target genes associated with the 51 validated DELs. The 100-kb cis-target window was adopted as a widely used convention in published plant lncRNA studies [32–34], reflecting the typical genomic range over which lncRNAs are reported to act on neighbouring genes in plant genomes; this convention is acknowledged as a conservative approximation in the absence of C. sativa-specific chromatin-conformation data that would permit a species-calibrated cis-target window, and this limitation is discussed further in the Discussion. To strengthen confidence in the predicted DEL–target pairs, Pearson correlation coefficients between each DEL and each of its predicted cis-target genes were computed across the six libraries, and a co-expression-supported high-confidence subset (|r| > 0.7, p < 0.05) was defined for downstream interpretation (Additional file 11: Table S1).

Protein sequences corresponding to the predicted target genes were extracted from the C. sativa reference protein dataset (GCF_029168945.1) using a custom Python (v3.9) script based on NCBI protein identifier mapping. Functional annotation was performed using eggNOG-mapper (v2.1.13) with DIAMOND as the sequence search method and default parameters (E-value < 0.001) against the eggNOG v5.0 database, with the taxonomic scope restricted to Viridiplantae. All annotation tasks were executed on the TRUBA (Turkish National Science e-Infrastructure, operated by TUBİTAK ULAKBİM) high-performance computing cluster, which provided the computational resources required for parallel sequence alignment, database searches, and enrichment analyses. Gene Ontology (GO; Biological Process, Molecular Function, and Cellular Component) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analyses were performed in R (v4.3.0) using the clusterProfiler package (v4.10) and the hypergeometric test. The complete annotated C. sativa transcriptome (23,899 genes; 32,173 annotated proteins) was used as the statistical background, and terms with a Benjamini–Hochberg-adjusted p < 0.05 were considered significantly enriched. Annotations derived from the GCF_029168945.1 reference were supplemented with BLASTp alignments (E-value < 1 × 10⁻⁵) against the UniProt/SwissProt database to refine functional descriptions of otherwise uncharacterized genes.

Integrative Transcription Factor (TF) identification

Transcription factor (TF) annotation was performed independently for three gene sets: up-regulated DEGs, down-regulated DEGs, and the cis-target genes of the 51 validated DELs. An integrative, two-step strategy was applied. First, the functional descriptions produced by eggNOG-mapper were parsed using custom regular expressions via the stringr package in R to flag transcripts assigned to major plant TF families (e.g., MYB, NAC, bHLH, bZIP, ERF, WRKY, FAR1). Second, the corresponding protein sequences were submitted to the PlantTFDB (v5.0) prediction server, which employs plant-specific Hidden Markov Model profiles, with the “Best hit in Arabidopsis thaliana” option enabled to support ortholog-based family assignment. The TF lists derived from eggNOG-mapper and PlantTFDB were merged, duplicate entries representing multi-domain or multi-isoform proteins with identical GeneID and TF_Family assignments were removed, and sub-family nomenclatures were standardized under their primary family designations. Family-level distributions were visualized as donut charts using the viridis color palette and assembled with the patchwork R package.

Construction of the drought-responsive ceRNA regulatory network

To explore post-transcriptional regulation mediated by drought-responsive lncRNAs, a competing endogenous RNA (ceRNA) network was constructed. Candidate lncRNA–miRNA and miRNA–mRNA interactions were predicted using psRNATarget (V2, 2017 scoring schema) with default expectation thresholds. Because C. sativa-specific miRNAs are not currently registered in miRBase, a dual-source query set was used: (i) conserved mature plant miRNA sequences from well-annotated species (A. thaliana, Oryza sativa) and from closely related species within the Rosales order available in miRBase (e.g., Morus notabilis); and (ii) previously reported, computationally predicted C. sativa miRNAs from EST-based studies [2]. Only triplets in which the lncRNA was one of the 51 validated DELs were retained, to ensure that the final drought-responsive ceRNA sub-network was anchored to differentially expressed, high-confidence lncRNAs. The resulting network topology was visualized and analyzed using Cytoscape software (v3.9.1).

RNA extraction and qRT-PCR validation

To validate the RNA-seq-derived expression profiles and the predicted ceRNA modules, total RNA was extracted from leaf tissues using the FavorPrep Plant Total RNA Mini Kit (Favorgen Biotech, Taiwan) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. RNA concentration and purity (A260/A280 and A260/A230) were assessed with a NanoDrop Lite spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). First-strand cDNA was synthesized from 1 µg of total RNA using the iScript cDNA Synthesis Kit (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Hercules, CA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Gene-specific primers were designed using the PrimerQuest tool (Integrated DNA Technologies; https://www.idtdna.com/pages/tools/primerquest), with selection criteria based on GC content, melting temperature (Tm ≈ 60 °C), and minimal hairpin/self-dimer probability (Supplementary Table S1). qRT-PCR assays were performed using SYBR Green-based detection on a Bio-Rad CFX96 Real-Time PCR Detection System, and primer specificity was confirmed by single-peak dissociation (melt) curve analysis for each amplicon. Amplification efficiency was calculated from standard dilution curves in accordance with MIQE guidelines.

Reference gene selection

In accordance with MIQE guidelines [35], two C. sativa reference genes previously validated for qRT-PCR normalization — Elongation Factor 1-alpha (CsEF-1α) and Actin (CsActin) — were used jointly to normalize target-gene expression across all biological replicates [36]. Both reference genes produced stable, reproducible Cq values with uniform amplification efficiency across the control (CK) and drought-stressed (DS) conditions retained for analysis, satisfying the stability criteria expected of plant qRT-PCR reference genes. To combine the two references, the geometric mean of CsEF-1α and CsActin Cq values was computed for each biological replicate and used as the normalization factor, following the widely adopted Vandesompele approach to multi-reference qRT-PCR quantification. Relative expression levels of all target genes were then calculated using the 2 − ΔΔCt method with the dual-reference normalization factor.

Validation design

Six genes were ultimately quantified: four DELs (MSTRG.14087, MSTRG.3722, MSTRG.13039, and MSTRG.14156) representing key drought-responsive DELs, and two DEGs (bHLH48 and PP2C) representing the corresponding ceRNA target mRNAs. Each reaction was performed with three technical replicates. Although six biological replicates per treatment were maintained for non-destructive physiological monitoring, three replicates per treatment were retained for end-point qRT-PCR analysis after pre-analytical quality control. Specifically, replicates whose drought-induced tissue damage was sufficiently severe to compromise RNA integrity — as evidenced by reduced A260/A280 or A260/A230 ratios outside the acceptance range, low RNA yield, or visibly necrotic leaf tissue at harvest — were excluded prior to cDNA synthesis. This conservative selection ensured uniformly performing cDNA templates across replicates, in compliance with MIQE recommendations for plant qRT-PCR studies. Concordance between RNA-seq and qRT-PCR quantifications was evaluated using (i) the fraction of genes showing consistent direction of change (direction concordance) and (ii) the Pearson correlation between RNA-seq and qRT-PCR log₂(fold change) values. Statistical significance of CK vs. DS differences in qRT-PCR expression was assessed by Student’s t-test, with significance thresholds reported in the corresponding figure legends.

Statistical analysis and data visualization

All statistical tests and data visualizations were performed in R (v4.3.0). Physiological data (pot weight, ΦPSII, ΦNPQ, qL) were analyzed by Student’s t-test at each time point, with results reported as mean ± SEM (n = 6 per treatment). Chromosomal distribution of novel lncRNAs was visualized with the circlize package; distribution and density plots were generated with ggplot2; heatmaps with pheatmap and Z-score row-wise normalization; and GO/KEGG enrichment plots with enrichplot. To clarify the design and statistical handling of the physiological measurements: six biological replicates per treatment (n = 6 per group; twelve plants in total across the two groups) were used for non-destructive physiological monitoring, and PhotosynQ readings were taken from the same plants at successive time points (Days 0, 2, 4, and 6). The measurements are therefore non-independent across time. Given the small per-group replicate count and the fact that PhotosynQ readings could only be obtained reliably through Day 6 due to inward leaf rolling beyond this time point, per-time-point Student’s t-tests were reported as the primary statistical readout, in line with current practice across published PhotosynQ-based studies of progressive plant drought stress. The trends observed in ΦPSII, ΦNPQ, and qL were consistent in direction across time points, and pot-weight differences between CK and DS reached statistical significance from Day 2 onward (Fig. 1A). For three of the six CK replicates, Day 0 pot weights were affected by procedural variation (measurement performed prior to baseline irrigation) and were imputed as the mean of the remaining three CK replicate Day-0 measurements; this imputation affected only the baseline anchor of the relative-pot-weight curve (Fig. 1A) and did not enter any statistical comparison, all of which were performed on raw within-day pot-weight values rather than on relative-to-Day-0 ratios.

Results

Physiological characterization of C. sativa under progressive drought stress

To comprehensively investigate the physiological effects of progressive water deficit on Cannabis sativa, plants were subjected to progressive drought stress over an 8-day experimental period (Fig. 1). Drought severity was monitored through a multi-parametric approach, combining relative pot weight measurements, PSII photochemical efficiency (PhotosynQ), and direct phenotypic observations. While the control (CK) group maintained a relatively stable baseline (reflecting routine daily evapotranspiration measured prior to morning irrigation; −22.1 ± 9.0% by Day 8; Day 0 values for a subset of CK replicates were imputed as described in Section 2.2), the relative pot weight of DS plants declined steadily, with statistically significant differences first observed at Day 2 (− 39.5 ± 5.0%, p < 0.05), reaching − 71.7 ± 0.5% by Day 8 (p < 0.001; Fig. 1A). Morphological differences became increasingly apparent as soil moisture depleted; DS plants exhibited progressive wilting that culminated in severe inward leaf rolling and structural deformation by Day 8, preventing reliable non-destructive photosynthetic measurements beyond Day 6.

To quantify the impact of progressive dehydration on the photosynthetic apparatus, real-time parameters were recorded using the PhotosynQ platform. Due to severe leaf rolling and structural deformation, reliable non-destructive measurements could only be obtained up to Day 6. The quantum yield of photosystem II (ΦPSII) remained relatively stable through Day 4 but declined significantly in DS plants by Day 6 (0.646 ± 0.046) compared to CK controls (0.760 ± 0.007; p = 0.035; Fig. 1B). Concurrently, ΦNPQ increased markedly in DS plants at Day 6 (0.185 ± 0.052) relative to CK (0.064 ± 0.006; p = 0.042; Fig. 1C), indicating activation of thermal energy dissipation as photochemical capacity became limiting. In parallel, the photochemical quenching coefficient (qL) was significantly reduced in DS plants at Day 6 (0.785 ± 0.037) compared to CK (0.884 ± 0.013; p = 0.029; Fig. 1D), reflecting a substantial reduction in the proportion of open PSII reaction centers. Together, these data confirm that DS plants entered a state of severe physiological stress by Day 8, providing a reliable foundation for the subsequent transcriptomic analyses.

Transcriptome sequencing, lncRNA identification, and structural characterization

To elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying the physiological decline of C. sativa under progressive drought stress, comprehensive transcriptome sequencing (RNA-seq) data were analyzed for the control (CK) and drought-stressed (DS) groups. The six publicly available RNA-seq libraries collectively yielded approximately 260 million clean, paired-end reads across the experimental samples. These high-quality reads were mapped to the C. sativa reference genome (cs10), achieving an average overall alignment rate of 91.07% (Table 1), thereby providing a robust foundation for transcript discovery and quantification.

Following alignment, a reference-guided transcriptome assembly was conducted using StringTie. Transcript structural comparison using gffcompare revealed a total of 67,672 assembled transcripts mapped to 33,497 loci across the genome. To systematically discover novel long non-coding RNAs responsive to drought, unannotated transcripts were subjected to a stringent, multi-step computational pipeline. Candidate selection was restricted to novel transcripts classified by gffcompare as intergenic (‘u’), intronic (‘i’), or antisense (‘x’) relative to annotated protein-coding genes, while transcripts overlapping annotated genes on the same strand, including potentially novel isoforms of annotated genes (class codes ‘j’, ‘k’, ‘m’)—were excluded to avoid misclassifying novel mRNA isoforms as lncRNAs. The remaining candidates were length-filtered (> 200 nt). Coding potential was evaluated using CPC2 (v1.0.1), retaining only transcripts predicted as non-coding. Subsequently, six-frame translated open reading frames were screened against the Pfam database (v36.0) using HMMER (v3.4, E-value < 0.001) to exclude transcripts containing known functional protein domains. This multi-layered screening pipeline identified a high-confidence set of 2,096 novel lncRNAs in the C. sativa transcriptome. The transcript counts retained at each step of this pipeline are summarised in Additional file 2: Fig. S2 and Additional file 11: Table S3. Of the 3,072 length-filtered candidates classified as non-coding by CPC2, a further 976 transcripts carrying recognisable Pfam protein domains — predominantly retrotransposon-derived (e.g., RVT, gag, rve) and leucine-rich-repeat domains — were removed, yielding the final set of 2,096 high-confidence lncRNAs (Additional file 3: Fig. S3). To further confirm that the final lncRNA set is not dominated by transposon-derived sequences, all 2,096 lncRNAs were screened with RepeatMasker; single-exon lncRNAs (n = 1,370) showed lower repeat overlap (30.4%) than multi-exon lncRNAs (n = 726; 60.9%), and no single-exon lncRNA overlapped any bona fide transposable element (LTR retrotransposon, LINE, SINE, or DNA transposon), the only RepeatMasker hits being simple sequence repeats (n = 363) and low-complexity regions (n = 110) (Additional file 4: Fig. S4, Additional file 11: Table S4).

To further characterize the identified lncRNAs, their structural and genomic features were systematically compared with those of known protein-coding mRNAs. Genomic mapping revealed that the novel lncRNAs were widely yet unevenly distributed across all ten C. sativa chromosomes (Fig. 2A). Based on their positional relationship with annotated genes, the vast majority (1,899; 90.6%) were classified as intergenic lncRNAs (lincRNAs), followed by 130 (6.2%) intronic lncRNAs and 67 (3.2%) antisense lncRNAs (Fig. 2B). Consistent with features reported for lncRNAs in other plant species, the identified lncRNAs exhibited markedly lower expression levels compared to mRNAs (Fig. 2C). They were also substantially shorter, with a median transcript length of 452 nt compared to 1,125 nt for mRNAs (Fig. 2D). Furthermore, lncRNAs displayed simpler exonic architectures: approximately 65% harbored a single exon, whereas mRNAs were predominantly multi-exonic with a median of four exons (Fig. 2E). These structural signatures are in agreement with the general characteristics of lncRNAs reported across diverse plant genomes and collectively support the reliability of our identification pipeline.

Differentially expressed lncRNAs and mRNAs in response to drought stress

Expression profiling confirmed that 1,917 of the 2,096 identified lncRNA candidates exhibited quantifiable expression across the experimental conditions. Principal component analysis (PC1, 31.2%; PC2, 23.6%) on DESeq2 variance-stabilizing-transformed counts clearly separated CK and DS libraries along PC1, and pairwise Spearman correlations were consistently higher within treatment groups (ρ = 0.928 and 0.921 for CK–CK and DS–DS pairs, respectively) than between groups (ρ = 0.914 for CK–DS pairs), supporting the biological reproducibility of the libraries (Additional file 6: Fig. S6 and Additional file 11: Table S9). To characterize the transcriptomic reprogramming driven by water deficit, differential expression analysis was performed using DESeq2, comparing the DS and CK groups (p-adj < 0.05, |log₂FC| > 1). To ensure the biological integrity of the non-coding dataset, candidates that had not passed the complete CPC2 + Pfam lncRNA validation pipeline—most notably novel mRNA isoforms of annotated protein-coding genes—were strictly excluded from the final differentially expressed lncRNA list. This rigorous filtration identified a core set of 51 high-confidence differentially expressed lncRNAs (DELs), comprising 19 up-regulated and 32 down-regulated transcripts, alongside 575 differentially expressed mRNAs (DEGs; 349 up-regulated and 226 down-regulated) (Fig. 3A).

Functional enrichment analysis of DEL-associated target genes

To investigate the regulatory functions of the drought-responsive lncRNAs, putative cis-target protein-coding genes were predicted within a 100 kb window flanking each of the 51 DELs, yielding 494 candidate target genes. Following functional annotation of these targets via eggNOG-mapper (v2.1.13) against the eggNOG v5.0 database, GO and KEGG enrichment analyses were performed using the hypergeometric test implemented in clusterProfiler, with the entire annotated C. sativa transcriptome (23,899 genes; 32,173 annotated proteins) as the statistical background and a Benjamini–Hochberg adjusted p-value threshold of 0.05.

GO enrichment analysis revealed that the DEL-associated target genes were significantly enriched in biological processes related to secondary metabolism and stress-responsive cell wall remodeling (Fig. 4A). The most prominent enriched terms included secondary metabolic process, phenylpropanoid metabolic and biosynthetic processes, lignin biosynthetic and metabolic processes, and the regulation of flavonoid biosynthesis. Notably, abscisic acid biosynthetic process and cell wall disassembly were also significantly enriched, consistent with the established role of ABA signaling and cell wall reorganization in drought adaptation. At the molecular function level, the target genes were strongly enriched for aldehyde dehydrogenase (NAD⁺) activity, aldehyde and abscisic-aldehyde oxidase activities, and several hydroxycinnamoyltransferase (HCT/HQT) activities, including the shikimate- and quinate-specific isoforms (Fig. 4B). These activities collectively underpin the enzymatic machinery for phenylpropanoid and lignin biosynthesis as well as ABA biosynthesis. Cellular component analysis indicated significant localization of these targets to the plant-type vacuole and vacuolar membrane, alongside cation- and K⁺-transporting ATPase complexes (Fig. 4C), implicating ion homeostasis and vacuolar transport as additional points of regulation under water deficit.

These results provide a functional basis for the subsequent investigation of post-transcriptional regulatory networks involving these lncRNAs.

Construction of drought-responsive lncRNA–miRNA–mRNA (ceRNA) networks

To explore the potential post-transcriptional regulatory mechanisms of drought-responsive lncRNAs, a competing endogenous RNA (ceRNA) network was constructed. LncRNA–miRNA and miRNA–mRNA interactions were predicted using psRNATarget (V2, 2017 scoring schema) with conserved mature plant miRNA sequences from miRBase, including Arabidopsis thaliana, Oryza sativa, and the closely related Morus notabilis, supplemented by computationally predicted C. sativa miRNAs from previous EST-based studies. To focus the analysis on drought-responsive, high-confidence lncRNAs, only triplets in which the lncRNA was one of the 51 validated DELs were retained. Within this DEL-anchored sub-network, six lncRNAs were predicted to function as miRNA sponges, sequestering a total of seven miRNAs and thereby derepressing 32 downstream mRNA targets, the majority of which (20/32) represent novel, previously unannotated transcripts, underscoring the potential of this ceRNA network to uncover novel drought-responsive regulatory modules (Fig. 5). The resulting ceRNA sub-network comprised 46 nodes and 44 edges (9 sponge interactions and 35 miRNA–mRNA targeting interactions) and was visualized using Cytoscape (v3.9.1).

Transcription factor profiling among ceRNA network targets

To identify key transcription factor (TF) families orchestrating the drought stress response, TF profiling was performed independently on the drought-responsive DEGs and the predicted lncRNA cis-target genes. TF identification was conducted using PlantTFDB (v5.0) for the DEGs and an integrative approach combining eggNOG-mapper functional descriptions with conserved DNA-binding domain (Pfam) annotations for the lncRNA target gene set.

Among the up-regulated DEGs, 37 proteins were classified into 12 TF families (following standardization of sub-family nomenclatures), with MYB (6), NAC (5), bHLH (4), bZIP (4), NF-YA (4), and SBP (4) representing the most abundant families (Fig. 6A). These families are well-established regulators of drought tolerance and ABA signaling in plants. In contrast, the down-regulated DEGs yielded only six TF proteins across four families, with SBP and bHLH as the predominant classes (Fig. 6B). The pronounced asymmetry between up- and down-regulated TFs indicates that the drought response in C. sativa primarily involves the transcriptional activation of stress-responsive regulators rather than the suppression of specific TF programs.

Cross-cultivar expression support via qRT-PCR

To independently validate the RNA-seq expression profiles and the predicted ceRNA regulatory relationships, six genes — four DELs (MSTRG.14087, MSTRG.3722, MSTRG.13039, and MSTRG.14156) and two DEGs representing key ceRNA target mRNAs (bHLH48 and PP2C) — were selected for qRT-PCR analysis using the 2 − ΔΔCt method with dual-reference normalization against the geometric mean of CsEF-1α and CsActin Cq values (Fig. 7). These genes were chosen to capture both global transcriptomic trends and specific ceRNA module behavior: MSTRG.14,156 and bHLH48 constitute ceRNA Module 1 (MSTRG.14156–ath-miR5649a/b–bHLH48), while MSTRG.14,087 and PP2C represent ceRNA Module 2 (MSTRG.14087–gma-miR1533–PP2C). Three biological replicates per condition were used for quantification, and primer specificity was confirmed by single-peak melt curve analysis for all amplicons (Supplementary Table S1).

Among the four lncRNAs compared with RNA-seq data, all exhibited consistent expression directionality between the two platforms, yielding a 100% direction concordance rate (4/4) (Fig. 7B). MSTRG.14,087, MSTRG.3722, and MSTRG.14,156 were up-regulated under drought stress in both RNA-seq and qRT-PCR analyses, while MSTRG.13,039 was consistently down-regulated. MSTRG.3722 displayed the most pronounced drought-induced up-regulation (21.3-fold, p = 0.0328), and MSTRG.14,156 showed an 8.3-fold increase (p = 0.0023). The Pearson correlation coefficient between RNA-seq and qRT-PCR log₂FC values was R = 0.756 (R² = 0.571), a level of concordance consistent with the typical range reported for RNA-seq validation studies in non-model plants and further supporting the reliability of the transcriptomic estimates alongside the perfect direction concordance.

For the ceRNA target mRNAs, bHLH48 exhibited a dramatic 50-fold down-regulation under drought stress (p = 0.0007), representing the most statistically significant change among all validated genes. PP2C, encoding a type 2 C protein phosphatase involved in ABA signaling, was up-regulated 2.7-fold (p = 0.024) (Fig. 7A). The qRT-PCR quantifications of both the lncRNA and the mRNA components of the two ceRNA triplets were directionally concordant with the corresponding RNA-seq estimates, providing cross-cultivar independent expression support for the transcriptomic profiles and serves as the basis for the mechanistic interpretation developed in the Discussion. Because the public RNA-seq libraries were generated from Day 10 drought-stressed tissues while the qRT-PCR validation was performed on Day 8 tissues to avoid drought-induced necrosis, the log₂(fold change) values from both time points were directly compared for the four validated DELs (Additional file 8: Fig. S8 and Additional file 11: Table S6). All four DELs were directionally concordant between the two time points (4/4 = 100%), with a Pearson correlation of R = 0.591 (n = 4) between the Day 10 RNA-seq and Day 8 qRT-PCR log₂FC values, indicating that the lncRNA-level regulatory programmes remain comparable between late and very-late drought stages in this dataset.

Discussion

Drought is among the most critical abiotic constraints limiting plant growth and productivity, triggering extensive transcriptomic reprogramming across both coding and non-coding RNA layers [1]. Long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs) have emerged as versatile participants in plant stress adaptation, acting through cis- and trans-regulation of neighboring or distal protein-coding genes, as decoys or sponges for microRNAs, and as modulators of chromatin state and transcriptional dynamics [36, 37]. Systematic identification of drought-responsive lncRNAs has progressed rapidly in Arabidopsis thaliana [17], Brassica napus [23, 24], Oryza sativa [20, 32], Zea mays [38], Beta vulgaris [39], and wild Hordeum vulgare [21]. Across these studies, the total novel lncRNA repertoire is typically in the thousands, whereas the drought-responsive DEL subset is considerably smaller and highly context-dependent. Here we identified 2,096 high-confidence novel lncRNAs in Cannabis sativa leaves and, after strict CPC2 and Pfam-based filtering, resolved a core set of 51 DELs, establishing a compact yet stringently validated foundation for dissecting lncRNA-mediated drought regulation in this species.

Despite its growing economic and medicinal importance, the non-coding RNA landscape of C. sativa remains poorly annotated. Previous cannabis transcriptomic studies have examined cannabinoid and terpenoid biosynthesis networks [40], generated transcriptome atlases [41], or profiled drought-responsive protein-coding genes in hemp [9]; however, none has systematically catalogued genome-wide, drought-responsive lncRNAs. The only prior genome-wide survey to incidentally report cannabis lncRNAs — without drought profiling or strict coding-potential validation — was a transcriptome-wide alternative-splicing and non-coding RNA analysis conducted in non-stressed hemp, which predicted approximately 7,783 putative lncRNAs in the context of cannabinoid, fatty acid and cellulose biosynthesis [25]. Systematic drought-focused lncRNA studies in C. sativa are therefore largely absent from the literature. The present work addresses this gap: the 51 validated DELs, their 494 predicted cis-target genes, and the downstream ceRNA sub-network provide a first draft of the lncRNA-mediated drought regulatory architecture of C. sativa and a foundation for subsequent mechanistic and functional studies.

The 2,096 novel lncRNAs identified here displayed the recognized structural signatures of plant lncRNAs: short median length (452 nt vs. 1,125 nt for mRNAs), simpler exonic architecture (single-exon dominance, ~ 65%), and markedly lower steady-state expression than protein-coding transcripts. These features are consistent with large-scale annotations in Arabidopsis [42, 43], rapeseed [23], and evolutionary diverse plant lineages [36, 44], and support the biological authenticity of the identified transcripts. The predominance of single-exon lncRNAs in our dataset deserves particular comment: although some earlier pipelines excluded single-exon transcripts to suppress assembly artefacts, accumulating evidence indicates that single-exon lncRNAs are both common and functionally relevant in plant genomes [36]. Our retention of this category, combined with the stringent coding-potential filtering, therefore captures a biologically meaningful fraction of the C. sativa non-coding repertoire that would otherwise be overlooked. The uneven chromosomal distribution (Fig. 2A) and predominance of intergenic lincRNAs (90.6%) further mirror patterns reported in other plant species and argue against a systematic technical bias in our identification pipeline.

Functional enrichment of the 494 DEL cis-target genes highlighted phenylpropanoid metabolism, lignin biosynthesis, and cell wall remodeling as dominant biological themes (Fig. 4A, B). Drought-induced lignification is a well-documented adaptive response in vascular plants: deposition of additional lignin in xylem vessels and cell walls enhances mechanical support, restricts radial water loss, and helps preserve xylem function under negative water potentials [45, 46]. The strong enrichment for hydroxycinnamoyl-CoA transferase activities — including the HCT/HQT shikimate- and quinate-specific isoforms — is mechanistically consistent with this interpretation, as these enzymes are central branch-point catalysts of the phenylpropanoid flux that ultimately feeds monolignol biosynthesis [47]. Concurrent enrichment of flavonoid-biosynthesis regulation may further reflect antioxidant and osmoprotective roles of phenolic compounds, extending the adaptive function of the phenylpropanoid branch beyond structural lignification. Together, these results suggest that a substantial subset of drought-responsive DELs in C. sativa may contribute to the coordinated reprogramming of secondary metabolism and cell wall architecture that underpins physiological drought tolerance.

A second coherent functional theme emerging from the enrichment analyses was abscisic acid (ABA) biosynthesis and signaling. The significant enrichment of abscisic acid biosynthetic process and abscisic-aldehyde oxidase activity terms (Fig. 4A, B) aligns with the established biochemistry of ABA synthesis, in which aldehyde oxidase 3 (AAO3) catalyzes the final oxidation of abscisic aldehyde to active ABA in leaf tissues [48]. Elevated AAO-type activity among DEL targets is therefore consistent with the well-known accumulation of ABA under progressive water deficit and its central role in stomatal regulation and downstream transcriptional responses [49]. In parallel, the up-regulation of PP2C (a type 2 C protein phosphatase) in the qRT-PCR validation set warrants careful interpretation. Clade-A PP2Cs are classical negative regulators of the PYR/PYL/RCAR–PP2C–SnRK2 core ABA signaling module [50]; however, their transcriptional induction under ABA and drought is a long-recognized feature of a negative-feedback architecture that tunes, rather than suppresses, ABA signaling intensity [51, 52]. The observed PP2C up-regulation thus fits a model in which drought-responsive DELs contribute to calibrating ABA signal amplitude rather than simply amplifying it.

An unexpected but mechanistically informative observation emerged from Module 1 (MSTRG.14156–ath-miR5649a/b–bHLH48). Under the classical ceRNA/sponge model, up-regulation of a lncRNA sponge is expected to sequester its cognate miRNA, relieve miRNA-mediated repression of the shared target mRNA, and therefore increase target expression [14, 53]. In our data, however, MSTRG.14,156 was significantly up-regulated under drought (8.3-fold by qRT-PCR), yet the shared target mRNA bHLH48 showed a dramatic 50-fold down-regulation — a direction opposite to the simple sponge prediction. The most parsimonious explanation for this apparent paradox is quantitative: rigorous stoichiometric analyses have established that effective ceRNA-mediated de-repression requires near-equimolar matching of lncRNA and miRNA abundances, and that physiologically plausible changes in sponge expression — such as the 8.3-fold up-regulation of MSTRG.14,156 observed here — are typically insufficient to produce measurable target de-repression in vivo [54–56]. In this framework, the MSTRG.14,156 up-regulation may be real and specific, yet simply below the threshold required to overcome miR5649-mediated repression of bHLH48, which is almost certainly subject to additional, dominant regulatory inputs under drought — including cis- or trans-acting effects of MSTRG.14,156 itself that are independent of miRNA sponging, or convergent repression by multiple other miRNAs or transcription factors. A specific alternative warranting consideration is that MSTRG.14,156 acts as a direct cis-repressor of bHLH48 transcription, independent of miRNA sponging activity. Such cis-acting regulatory effects of antisense and intergenic lncRNAs have been documented across plants, including in C. sativa, where natural antisense transcripts have recently been catalogued at genome scale [26]. A second, non-exclusive possibility is convergent repression of bHLH48 by additional drought-responsive transcription factors or by miRNAs not captured in our predicted network, in which case MSTRG.14,156 up-regulation may reflect a parallel but mechanistically independent regulatory output. Discrimination between these alternative models will require (i) MSTRG.14,156 loss-of-function experiments — for example, CRISPR/Cas9 knockout — with parallel quantification of bHLH48 to test whether bHLH48 expression is restored in the absence of MSTRG.14,156; (ii) miR5649 mimic and inhibitor assays in C. sativa to test for direct miRNA-mediated regulation; and (iii) chromatin-conformation or chromatin-immunoprecipitation assays to test for direct lncRNA–bHLH48 promoter interaction. Regardless of the specific mechanism at play, the MSTRG.14,156–bHLH48 relationship illustrates an important caveat for predicted plant ceRNA networks: directional concordance with the sponge model should be treated as a testable hypothesis rather than a mechanistic inference.

Module 2 (MSTRG.14087–gma-miR1533–PP2C) presents a contrasting picture consistent with the classical ceRNA scenario. MSTRG.14,087 was the most connected lncRNA in the drought-responsive sub-network, predicted to sequester gma-miR1533 and to derepress up to 23 downstream mRNA targets, including a PP2C-family phosphatase. Both MSTRG.14,087 (up-regulated) and PP2C (up-regulated 2.7-fold) moved in the direction predicted by the sponge model, and the Pearson correlation between their qRT-PCR profiles supports a coherent regulatory axis rather than independent stochastic variation. Placed in the context of the ABA signaling literature, this module plausibly represents a rheostat-type adjustment: modest amplification of PP2C at the transcriptional level, reinforced by post-transcriptional protection from miR1533-mediated cleavage, may contribute to the negative-feedback damping of ABA signaling output during sustained drought. Although miR1533 remains a primarily computationally predicted soybean miRNA with limited direct experimental validation in other plant systems [57, 58], functional validation of analogous plant lncRNA–miRNA–mRNA axes in rice has shown that ceRNA-type regulation can genuinely modulate drought-related phenotypes [20]. MSTRG.14,087 is therefore a high-priority candidate for future loss-of-function studies in C. sativa.

Perhaps the most striking observation from the transcription-factor (TF) profiling was the strong over-representation of FAR1-RELATED SEQUENCE (FAR1/FHY3-family) proteins among the lncRNA cis-target gene set (11 of 18 TFs; Fig. 6C). This proportion (61.1%) is markedly higher than the genome-wide frequency of FAR1-family TFs in C. sativa (36/1,225 = 2.9%; PlantTFDB v5.0), corresponding to a 20.8-fold enrichment and statistically robust over-representation (Fisher’s exact test, one-sided p = 7.5 × 10⁻¹⁴; odds ratio = 74.3, 95% CI [26.6, 207.5]; Additional file 9: Fig. S9 and Additional file 11: Table S7). The FAR1/FHY3 family, originally identified as phytochrome-A-signaling components required for far-red light responses in Arabidopsis [59, 60], has subsequently been shown to function as an integrative hub linking light perception with ABA sensitivity, oxidative stress tolerance, and abiotic stress responses more broadly [61–63]. In particular, FHY3 and FAR1 directly bind the ABI5 promoter and positively regulate ABA signaling, with loss-of-function mutants showing altered drought, salt and osmotic stress responses [61]. More recent work has extended FAR1/FHY3 family involvement to drought responses in crops, including a comprehensive expression analysis of the maize family under water deficit [64]. The enrichment of FAR1-family members among DEL-proximal genes is biologically meaningful for C. sativa specifically: cannabis is a photoperiod-sensitive short-day species in which light cues tightly govern the vegetative-to-reproductive transition [65, 66], and integration of light and drought-stress signaling through FAR1/FHY3-type regulators provides a plausible molecular substrate for the coordinated phenological and physiological responses that this species mounts under simultaneous environmental inputs. This enrichment thus proposes a hypothesis of lncRNA-mediated light–drought crosstalk in cannabis; functional validation of FAR1-family targets, including binding assays and loss-of-function studies, will be required to test this model.

qRT-PCR validation of four DELs and two ceRNA-target DEGs yielded a perfect 4/4 direction-concordance rate with the RNA-seq estimates and a Pearson correlation of R = 0.756 (R² = 0.571) for log₂FC values. Direction concordance is widely considered the most robust cross-platform validation metric for RNA-seq, as absolute magnitude agreement between Cq-based and count-based quantification is inherently constrained by methodological differences in normalisation, dynamic range, and reference-gene dependence [67, 68]. The observed log₂FC correlation aligns with the range reported in validation studies of non-model plants, and it is further reinforced in our case by MIQE-compliant dual-reference normalisation against the geometric mean of CsEF-1α and CsActin Cq values — both previously validated for qRT-PCR analyses in C. sativa [69]. The dual-reference scheme yielded identical direction-of-change assignments and an identical Pearson correlation with the RNA-seq estimates relative to single-reference normalisation against CsEF-1α alone, while tightening the statistical significance of the two ceRNA-module DELs (MSTRG.14156: p = 0.0023; MSTRG.3722: p = 0.0328), thereby strengthening confidence in the module-level inferences drawn from the data. Most importantly, the qRT-PCR results successfully recapitulated the expression patterns of the targeted ceRNA components (bHLH48 and PP2C), providing independent experimental validation for the two ceRNA modules and reinforcing the mechanistic interpretations drawn from our network analysis.

Several limitations of the present study should be acknowledged and frame future work. First, the RNA-seq analysis derives from a single terminal time point of progressive drought; a time-course sampling design would be required to resolve the dynamics of DEL induction and to distinguish early sensing from late damage-associated transcriptional responses. Second, cis-target prediction within a 100-kb flanking window is a conservative but necessarily imperfect proxy for genuine regulatory proximity, and the functional reality of each DEL–target pair awaits direct validation by chromatin-conformation, CRISPR, or overexpression approaches. Third, because C. sativa-specific miRNAs are not yet formally registered in miRBase, the ceRNA network was built using conserved mature miRNAs from related species and computationally predicted cannabis miRNAs; direct small-RNA sequencing of drought-stressed cannabis tissues will be essential to refine and validate the miRNA component of the network. Fourth, the paradoxical behavior of Module 1 (MSTRG.14156/bHLH48) highlights the general caveat that predicted plant ceRNA modules require functional dissection before mechanistic claims can be made. A sensitivity analysis of the DEL-anchored ceRNA network with respect to miRNA source species and prediction stringency (Additional file 10: Fig. S10, Additional file 11: Table S8) showed that the network composition reflects the underlying conservation profile of plant miRNAs. The principal Module 2 axis (MSTRG.14087–PP2C; 23 of 37 triplets) is anchored by gma-miR1533, an angiosperm-conserved miRNA family with well-documented orthologs across dicots, and this prediction is robust across psRNATarget stringency thresholds (E ≤ 2.0 retains 25 of 37 triplets), supporting the consistency of the predicted MSTRG.14,087–PP2C interaction. Module 1 (MSTRG.14156–bHLH48) is supported at the lenient end of stringency by Arabidopsis ath-miR5649a/b, consistent with its status as a borderline-confidence candidate requiring functional validation. These observations reinforce the framing of the predicted ceRNA modules as computational candidates and identify Cannabis-specific small-RNA sequencing as the most direct future step for validating the miRNA component of the network. Fifth, the publicly available RNA-seq libraries analysed here were generated using poly(A) selection, which preferentially captures polyadenylated transcripts and therefore under-represents non-polyadenylated, tissue-specific, or very lowly expressed lncRNAs; the 2,096-lncRNA set reported here should accordingly be interpreted as a poly(A)-selected, leaf-expressed landscape rather than a comprehensive C. sativa lncRNA repertoire. Sixth, the qRT-PCR validation was performed on Narlısaray seedlings sampled at Day 8 of drought, whereas the public RNA-seq libraries were generated from Pink Pepper (isolate KNU-18-1) adult plants sampled at Day 10; the qRT-PCR data therefore constitute cross-cultivar independent expression support for the transcriptomic profiles rather than a direct replicate of the RNA-seq experiment, and should be interpreted with this design feature in mind. Seventh, our lncRNA-identification pipeline retained only intergenic, intronic, and antisense transcripts (gffcompare class codes ‘u’, ‘i’, ‘x’), deliberately excluding sense-overlapping transcripts that cannot be reliably distinguished from novel mRNA isoforms in short-read poly(A) RNA-seq data; biologically important sense-overlapping or natural antisense lncRNAs may therefore remain unidentified in our dataset and would warrant dedicated long-read or strand-specific sequencing-based characterisation in follow-up work. Despite these limitations, the compact, stringently filtered 51-DEL landscape reported here — together with the functional enrichment landscape, the DEL-anchored ceRNA sub-network, the FAR1-dominated TF signature, and the two expression-supported candidate ceRNA modules — provides a concrete molecular resource for future investigations of drought tolerance and light–stress integration in Cannabis sativa, and offers candidate regulatory nodes for eventual deployment in molecular breeding programs aimed at stress-resilient cannabis cultivars.

Conclusions

In this study, we report the first systematic characterisation of polyA-selected, leaf-expressed drought-responsive long non-coding RNAs in Cannabis sativa. By combining a stringent CPC2- and Pfam-based identification pipeline with differential expression analysis, integrative transcription-factor profiling, ceRNA network inference, and experimental qRT-PCR validation, we identified 2,096 high-confidence novel lncRNAs and resolved a compact core of 51 drought-responsive DELs acting on 494 predicted cis-target genes. Functional enrichment of these targets highlighted phenylpropanoid and lignin biosynthesis, abscisic acid signaling, and vacuolar ion transport as coherent adaptive themes, while transcription-factor profiling revealed a striking over-representation of FAR1-family regulators that points to a hypothesised axis of lncRNA-mediated light–drought crosstalk in this photoperiod-sensitive crop, requiring future functional validation.

A DEL-anchored ceRNA sub-network further yielded two mechanistically informative modules — MSTRG.14,156–ath-miR5649a/b–bHLH48 and MSTRG.14,087–gma-miR1533–PP2C — both independently supported by qRT-PCR. The discordant behavior of Module 1 (concurrent lncRNA up-regulation and 50-fold target repression) illustrates that predicted plant ceRNA axes should be treated as testable hypotheses rather than mechanistic inferences, whereas Module 2 conforms to the classical sponge model and offers a candidate regulatory node for modulating ABA signal amplitude under sustained water deficit.

Taken together, these findings provide a concrete molecular resource for the drought-response biology of C. sativa and nominate specific lncRNAs, ceRNA modules, and FAR1-family regulators as priority targets for future functional dissection. Building on this resource, we propose three concrete, testable hypotheses to guide functional follow-up. First, CRISPR/Cas9 knockout of MSTRG.14,087 — the most connected sponge hub in the drought-responsive ceRNA sub-network — should reduce drought tolerance if its predicted derepression of PP2C is functionally relevant, and should abolish the drought-induced PP2C up-regulation in the knockout background. Second, ectopic overexpression of MSTRG.14,156 under a constitutive promoter is expected to recapitulate the predicted suppression of bHLH48 if the relationship is mediated by cis-repression, thereby providing a direct test that discriminates between the cis-repression and ceRNA hypotheses for Module 1. Third, miRNA mimic and inhibitor assays targeting gma-miR1533 and ath-miR5649a/b in C. sativa protoplasts or transgenic lines will directly test the functional relevance of the predicted lncRNA–miRNA interactions. In parallel, the candidate DELs — and particularly the FAR1-targeting set — provide molecular markers for marker-assisted selection programmes targeting drought tolerance in industrial-hemp germplasm, with priority for Mediterranean and semi-arid cultivation contexts where drought stress is a yield-limiting factor. The compact, stringently filtered 51-DEL set reported here is anticipated to serve as a reference dataset for subsequent mechanistic studies and for molecular breeding programs aimed at stress-resilient cannabis cultivars, in particular for cultivation under the increasingly arid conditions projected across Mediterranean and semi-arid hemp-producing regions.
