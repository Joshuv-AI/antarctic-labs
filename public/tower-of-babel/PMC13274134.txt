Genetic determinants of hypervirulence with attenuated pathogenicity in a blaNDM-1-encoding Klebsiella pneumoniae causing neonatal sepsis
Authors: Deblina Nath, Rinita Dhar, Amrita Bhattacharjee, Sanjib Das, Debadatta Dhar Chanda, Prasanta Kr Borah, Sulagna Basu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractK39 Klebsiella pneumoniae, a previously unrecognized hypervirulent serotype, was isolated from a septicemic neonate. It was assessed for the acquisition of hypervirulence determinants and virulence potential. Transmissibility of plasmids, functional expression of virulence determinants, capsule visualization (TEM), comparative genome analysis and virulence potential (in vitro, in vivo assays) were studied. Carbapenem-resistant ST985-K39 K. pneumoniae (Sil80), harbored rmpA, rmpA2, iucA, iroB, peg-344 in IncFIB(K)/IncHI1B(pNDM-MAR) hypervirulent plasmid; blaNDM-1 in IncFIB(pQIL)/IncFII(K) plasmid and other antimicrobial resistance determinants in IncFII plasmid. Comparative genomic analysis of global K39 isolates revealed, for the first time, the acquisition of a hypervirulent plasmid in the K39 study isolate. IncFIB(pQil)/IncFII(K) carrying blaNDM-1 showed successful conjugation independently. However, hypervirulent plasmid [IncFIB(K)/IncHI1B(pNDM-MAR)] could not successfully transmit alone, but was co-transmitted along with IncFII plasmid, because of nic site similarity. The strain was serum-resistant and produced a strong biofilm. Despite possessing hypervirulence determinants, Sil80 showed a negative string test, a thin capsule, and attenuated virulence in adult mice. Deletion of 10 amino acids in RmpD and mutation in rmpADC promoter having 10T track (P10T) were observed. Expression of rmpA, rmpD, and rmpC in Sil80 was also downregulated. Mutations in the rmpADC promoter and truncated RmpD of Sil80 caused attenuated virulence in adult mice; nonetheless, the isolate was capable of causing sepsis in an immunocompromised neonate. The transfer of the non self-conjugative virulence plasmid was facilitated by the helper plasmid with a similar nic site. This phenomenon could facilitate the spread of such plasmids to hitherto unknown serotypes.

Introduction

The last four decades have witnessed the evolution of Klebsiella pneumoniae (Kp) from the opportunistic classical pathotype causing sepsis and pneumonia to a hypervirulent pathotype causing community-acquired systemic infections in healthy individuals [1]. The pathogenicity of hypervirulent Kp (hvKp) ranges from liver abscesses, endophthalmitis and meningitis involving metastatic spread [2–4].

Several genetic attributes have been postulated to discern between these two pathotypes. Namely, i) presence of regulators of hypermucoid phenotype (rmpA and rmpA2); ii) iron acquisition system, aerobactin (iucABCDiutA) and salmochelin (iroBCDEN) and iii) the inner membrane transporter peg-344 that transports growth factors from the host environment [5–8]. Virulence plasmids (pLVPK, pK2044) and integrative conjugative elements (ICEKp1,10) are associated with the carriage of these genetic biomarkers [6]. Though there are >130 different capsular antigens, primarily K1 and K2 serotypes but also K5, K20, K54, K57 belonging to ST23, ST65, ST86 and ST29 are predominantly recognized as hvKps [2,9].

After the emergence of hvKps, they remained antibiotic-susceptible for several years. Lately, antibiotic-resistant hvKps have been frequently reported [10]. This change in susceptibility was primarily driven by acquisition of antibiotic resistance plasmids, or formation of hybrid plasmids carrying both resistance and virulence determinants [11]. This phenomenon led to the rapid emergence of multidrug-resistant hvKp (MDR-hvKp) isolates across diverse serotypes and STs (ST43-KNA, ST231-K51, ST2318-KNA) [12–15]. Such convergence of carbapenem resistance determinants (blaNDM, KPC, OXA-48) with virulence determinants in Kp has led to the emergence of carbapenem-resistant hvKp (CR-hvKp) [16]. Among carbapenemases, blaNDM is a potent metallo-beta-lactamase that hydrolyzes all carbapenems and cephalosporins except for aztreonam [17]. Isolates harboring blaNDM pose a limitation to the therapeutic alternatives, particularly for neonates [18]

Although virulence determinants are increasingly identified across diverse lineages, the presence of such determinants, particularly wild-type rmpA, which confers the hypermucoviscous phenotype, does not always result in an hvKp phenotype [19,20]. Detailed investigations have shown that rmpA does not function independently but functions in conjunction with rmpD and rmpC as an operon under a shared promoter. Mutations within this operon or its promoter can reduce gene expression, leading to decreased mucoidity and attenuated pathogenicity [21–23].

In this study, we identified a unique isolate carrying all five virulence markers that belonged to ST985-K39, an ST-serotype combination that was previously not associated with hvKp. We characterized the isolate with respect to its acquisition of virulence determinants and virulence potential.

As the definition of hvKp continues to evolve, systematic investigations into the acquisition of virulence determinants in unconventional hvKp lineages, along with assessments of their virulence potential in previously unrecognized serotypes, will be essential for guiding consensus.

Materials and methods

Ethics

Ethical approval was obtained from ICMR National Institute of Health Research (ICMR-NIHR), formerly ICMR-RMRC, NE Region, Dibrugarh, for using the clinical isolate under the approval No. RMRC/Dib/IEC (Human)/2017–2018/3723 dated 22 March 2018. Patient data were anonymized and deidentified before analysis. All methods were performed following the relevant guidelines and regulations stated in the Declaration of Helsinki.

Bacterial isolate

Isolates were collected from the culture-positive blood sample of septicemic neonates admitted to the neonatal intensive care unit (NICU) of Silchar Medical College, Silchar, in North East India in 2020. A subset (n = 13) of the collected samples was sent to the laboratory for further characterization and one isolate was selected for further study based on its virulence and resistance determinants. Patient consent was waived, as the sample was collected for the diagnosis and treatment of sepsis, not specifically for the project. Peripheral blood was aseptically drawn through vein puncture of the neonate and inoculated into a BD Bactec Peds Plus Vial (Becton Dickinson, MD, USA), followed by culturing through the automated Bactec 9050 system (Becton, Dickinson). Gram staining, followed by subculturing on MacConkey agar and 5% sheep blood agar (Difco Laboratories, Detroit, MI, USA), was used to determine whether the culture-positive isolate was Gram-negative or Gram-positive. The isolate was identified up to the species level through the VITEK®2 Compact system (BioMérieux, Marcy l’Etoile, France).

Antibiotic susceptibility testing

Antibiotic susceptibility pattern of Sil80 was determined using the VITEK®2 AST 280 card. The results were interpreted according to Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) guidelines for all antimicrobials. Minimum Inhibitory Concentration (MIC) of carbapenems (meropenem and ertapenem) was evaluated by broth microdilution (according to CLSI guidelines 2022). E. coli ATCC25922 was used as a quality control strain [24].

PCR screening for virulence and resistance determinants

To determine the hypervirulent pathotype, virulence genes rmpA, rmpA2, iucA, iroB, peg-344 and the genes associated with pLVPK loci (repA, silS, iutA, terW) were analyzed through PCR [24].

Different antibiotic resistance determinants including extended-spectrum β-lactamase genes (blaSHV, TEM, OXA-1, CTX-M etc), carbapenem resistance genes (blaKPC, IMP, VIM, NDM, OXA-48, GES, IMI), plasmid-mediated AmpC β-lactamases (blaMOX, CMY, DHA, ACC, MIR/ACT, FOX), 16S rRNA methylase genes (rmtA, rmtB, rmtC, rmtD, armA) and fluoroquinolone resistance genes (qnrA, qnrB, qnrS, oqxAB and aac(6′)-Ib/Ib-cr) were amplified by PCR [24].

Mating assays

Both solid and broth-mating assays were performed to check the transmission of resistance and virulence determinants using sodium azide-resistant E. coli J53 as a recipient. Donor and recipient were mixed in 1:2 and 1:5 ratios. The transconjugants were selected on Luria-Bertani agar or LA agar (BD BBL, MD, USA) supplemented with sodium azide (100 mg/L) and any one of the following – i) meropenem (0.5 mg/L); ii) potassium tellurite (8 mg/L) and iii) amikacin (32 mg/L), potassium tellurite (32 mg/L).

Virulence, resistance determinants and plasmid replicon types of transconjugants were determined by PCR (24).

Whole genome sequencing and comparative analysis

Genomic DNA was extracted using Wizard Genomic DNA Purification Kit (Promega). For short-read pair-end sequencing, the library was prepared using Nextera XT kit (Illumina Inc., San Diego, CA) and genome sequencing was executed on Illumina platform (Illumina Inc., San Diego, CA) by Illumina NovaSeq 6000 sequencer. For long-read pair-end sequencing (Oxford Nanopore Technology, UK), the genomic DNA was end-polished by NEB Next ultra II end repair kit (New England Biolabs, MA, USA). DNA sequencing library was prepared by ligating sequencing adapters onto double-stranded DNA fragments using NEB Quick T4 DNA Ligase (New England Biolabs, MA, USA) and PromethION flow cell (FLO-PRO114M) was used to generate the Nanopore sequencing data. Unicycler (v.0.5) was used to generate the hybrid assembly file for further genomic analysis. Genomic annotation of the assembled data was completed using RAST version 2.0.

The assembled data were used to determine the GC content, ST, serotype through the Pathogenwatch database (https://pathogen.watch/), VFDB and K-PAM were used to predict the virulence genes (https://cge.food.dtu.dk/services/VirulenceFinder/) (https://www.iith.ac.in/K-PAM/), resistance genes were determined using ResFinder (https://cge.food.dtu.dk/services/ResFinder/), PlasmidFinder was used for detection of plasmid replicon types (https://cge.food.dtu.dk/services/PlasmidFinder/), OriTfinder (https://bioinfo-mml.sjtu.edu.cn/oriTfinder/) was used to predict the origin of transfer site (oriT), relaxase gene, gene encoding type IV coupling protein (T4CP) and gene cluster for bacterial type IV secretion system (T4SS). PCR data was validated with this result [24,25]

Globally reported virulence plasmids, pLVPK (accession no AY378100) and pK2044 (AP006726) were chosen to compare the virulence plasmid of Sil80 by BLAST analysis and visualized through Proksee (https://proksee.ca/). Multiple sequence alignment-CLUSTALW (https://www.genome.jp/tools-bin/clustalw) was performed for comparative analysis to reveal any mutation in virulence genes (24).

Global K. pneumoniae genomes (n = 49) corresponding to the ST985-K39 serotype were downloaded from the Pathogenwatch database for the core genome phylogenetic analysis based on high-quality SNPs using CSI Phylogeny 1.4 (https://cge.food.dtu.dk/services/CSIPhylogeny/) in Center for Genomic Epidemiology platform, comparative analysis of the virulence and resistance determinants was done through ABRicate (Galaxy Version 1.0.1). Genome-to-Genome Distance Calculator (GGDC) v2.1 (http://ggdc.dsmz.de/) was used for comparative analysis and results were interpreted using Formula 2. Interactive Tree Of Life (iTOL v6.0) was used to visualize the phylogenetic tree and the heat map.

Determination of virulence potential

All in vitro and in vivo experiments were performed thrice, and ATCC700603 and SB42 were used as negative and positive controls, respectively.

In vitro assays

Mucoviscosity assay

Analysis of the hypermucoviscous nature was performed through the string test, which was further corroborated with the mucoviscosity assay. The bacterial culture was streaked on a blood agar plate and incubated overnight at 37°C. A single colony of the bacterial culture was stretched with the inoculation loop, and the string length was measured to infer the result. A string of >5 mm indicates a positive result [26]. Overnight bacterial inoculum was sub-cultured to OD600 = 1.0 (pre-spin) and centrifuged at 1000×g for 5 minutes. Post-spin OD600 was checked using 700 µl of the supernatant. Post/pre-spin ratio (OD600) was measured, and OD600 > 0.15 was considered as hypermucoid [27]. Three independent assays were performed and reported as mean ± standard deviations (SD).

Biofilm-formation assay

Biofilm-forming ability was assessed using the previously described method. Briefly, the overnight inoculated bacterial culture was adjusted to 0.5 McFarland in Mueller Hinton Broth (MHB) and 200 uL of OD-adjusted culture was incubated at 37°C for 24 h in 96 well microtiter plate. After that, 0.5% crystal violet was used to stain the biofilm followed by washing and dissolving in 200 uL of 95% ethanol. The biofilm was quantified by measuring the absorbance at 595 nm (OD595). High biofilm formation was considered when the OD was threefold higher than the negative control [24]. Three independent assays were performed and reported as mean ± SD.

Serum resistance assay

The bactericidal effect of the complement system of human serum against the isolate was performed as reported previously. Briefly, 25 uL of 2 × 106 CFU/mL bacterial culture from the sub-culture of overnight inoculation was mixed with 75 uL of pooled normal human serum donated by a healthy volunteer and incubated at 37°C. Viable bacterial colonies were counted after 1, 2 and 3 h. Heat-inactivated serum was also prepared as a control by heating the serum at 56°C for 30 min [24]. Three independent assays were performed and reported as mean ± SD.

In vivo mice mortality assay

As reported, hvKp causes infections in immunocompetent individuals. Although Sil80 was isolated from a septicemic neonate, the in vivo survival kinetics assay was performed in an adult mice model to simulate hvKp infection that is seen in immunocompetent hosts.

All the animal experiments were conducted following the standard operating procedure as outlined by Committee for the Purpose of Control and Supervision of Experiments on Animal (CPCSEA), Ministry of Fisheries, Animal Husbandry and Dairying (MoFAH&D), Government of India. ICMR-NIRBI Animal Ethical Committee approved animal experiments with the protocol number PRO/2011/-May 2024–27. A total of 30 female BALB/c mice (4 to 6-week-old, weight:15–18 g) were taken from animal resource division of ICMR-NIRBI, Kolkata, India, and acclimatized for 1 week at a temperature of 25°C with 75% humidity. In vivo virulence potential of Sil80 was assessed by the following procedures. Mice were randomly allocated to three groups of n = 5 per group (24) by a veterinarian of ICMR-NIRBI who was blind to the experimental setup. Mice were challenged with 200 μl of 2 × 105 CFU/ml bacterial inoculum through the intraperitoneal route. Symptoms and mortality were monitored for up to 7 days. ATCC700603 and hvKp-K1 SB42 were negative and positive controls, respectively. All surviving mice were euthanized after 7 days of infection by exposing to 70% CO2 for 5 min according to the CPCSEA guidelines. The experiment was performed in duplicate. Kaplan–Meier survival kinetics followed by Log rank test were performed in GraphPad Prism 8.0.1 (24). The experiment adhered to the ARRIVE guidelines, The experiment adhered to the ARRIVE guidelines.

Transmission electron microscopy

Over-night inoculated bacterial cells were pelleted down and fixed in 3% 0.1 M sodium cacodylate buffered glutaraldehyde for 4 h. 1% osmium tetroxide was used for secondary fixation, followed by dehydration with different gradations of acetone and finally embedded in Agar 100 resin and polymerized at 60°C. The ultrathin sections of the cells were cut using Leica Ultracut ultramicrotome and stained with 2% aqueous uranyl acetate and 0.2% lead citrate. Finally, the sections were visualized under 200KV accelerating voltage using UHR FEG-TEM instrument JEM 2100F (JEOL, Japan) [28]. A clinical cKp isolate lacking any virulence determinants, hypomucoid in nature (EN5219) and hvKp-K1 hypermucoid isolate carrying all virulence determinants (SB42) were chosen to visualize and compare the thickness of the capsule with that of Sil80.

Quantitative analysis of rmpADC cascade

qRT-PCR was performed to quantify the expression of the individual genes (rmpA, rmpD, rmpC) of rmpADC cascade. In brief, overnight bacterial inoculum was subcultured in LB-containing potassium tellurite (1 mg/L), and approximately 5 × 108 CFU/ml of bacterial culture was used to purify total RNA using NucleoSpin RNA, Mini kit (MACHEREY-NAGEL, Germany) according to the manufacturer’s instruction. Genomic DNA was removed using RNase-free DNase I (New England Biolabs, USA), further the concentration and purity (260/280 ratio >1.8) of yielded RNA were checked using NanoDrop biophotometer plus (Eppendorf, Hamburg, Germany). 1 µg of purified RNA was reverse transcribed to cDNA using RevertAid First Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (ThermoFisher Scientific, USA) according to the manufacturer’s guidelines. Relative expression of rmpA, rmpD, rmpC genes was measured through qRT-PCR using SYBR Green master mix for qPCR (Applied Biosystem, California, USA) in StepOnePlus Real-Time PCR system (Applied Biosystem, California, USA) where 2 µl of synthesized cDNA was used as a template. Primers of rmpA, rmpD, rmpC were designed in this study to check the expression. The sequence of the primers was rmpA Forward (CAATGGATGTGGCTTGACGT), rmpA Reverse (TGGATAATGGTTTACAATTCGGC); rmpC Forward (TGTTAATTCCACCCTCATCGG), rmpC Reverse (TGGTTTATAATCAAGCTGCCCC) and rmpD Forward (AAGGGGAGGGGATGTGAAG), rmpD Reverse (TGGCATGAGTTATATATCGCGC). A housekeeping gene rpsL was used as an endogenous reference for the normalization of the transcription level of target genes [29]. Cycling parameters were 5 min at 95°C, followed by 40 cycles of 30 sec at 95°C, 30 sec at 60°C, and 1 min at 60°C. The relative fold change of rmpA, rmpD, rmpC genes in Sil80 was compared to hypermucoid-hypervirulent clinical isolates identified in our laboratory, namely, K1 (AMR/159/23), K2 (TSK13) and K57 (AMR/122/23) K. pneumoniae isolates. All three isolates served as reference (expression = 1), which harbored wild-type rmpADC promoter along with structural genes. Relative fold change was calculated through comparative cycle threshold method (2−ΔΔCT).

Apart from pV2021-mediated virulence factors, some chromosomal virulence loci were also found such as, ICEKp12 encoding yersiniabactin synthesis and transporter (ybt16); aerobactin (iutA) and salmochelin transporter (iroE, iroN); type 1 and type 3 fimbriae; type IV and VI secretion systems (Table 1).

Presence of blaNDM-1 within pR2021.1 plasmid (genetic environment in Figure 1A) corroborated with the resistance of Sil80 to all carbapenems (Table 1).

Other β-lactamases including blaDHA-1, blaTEM-12 (in pR2021.1) and blaTEM-1, blaSFO-1 (in pR2021.2) were also observed. blaSHV-187 existed as the sole β-lactamase within the chromosome. Sil80 also harbored resistance genes against aminoglycoside, fluoroquinolones, fosfomycin, macrolide, sulfonamides and trimethoprim (Table 1).

Analysis of virulence determinants within forty-nine K39-ST985 genomes revealed that Sil80 was the first K39 isolate that harbored pLVPK-like virulence plasmid harboring hypervirulence determinants (Figure 2). Transmissibility of resistance and virulence plasmids

Differential selection of the sodium azide-resistant transconjugants (TCs) resulted in selective transfer of the plasmids (Table 1), with only pR2021.1 (possessing blaNDM-1) being transferred to TC-NDM, selected in 0.5 mg/L meropenem through solid mating assay. Potassium tellurite was used to select pV2021 harboring the tellurite-resistance cascade, but the conjugation failed. The use of amikacin and potassium tellurite-supplemented selection media led to the successful co-transmission of pV2021 and pR2021.2 in TC-VIR presumably due to the sequence similarity of 10 bp nic site.

Virulence potential of Sil80

The presence of a transmissible hypervirulent plasmid, encoding all virulence markers necessitated the study of the phenotypic virulence potential of the isolate.

I) in vitro virulence potential

Despite harboring all the hypervirulence markers, Sil80 was negative in the string test (length of string was <5mm) and showed a post/prespin OD600 = 0.01 ± 0.002, denoting non-hypermucoviscous phenotype. Sil80 showed strong biofilm-formation (OD595 = 1.185 ± 0.04) in comparison to negative control ATCC700603 (OD595 = 0.468 ± 0.04) (Figure 3A). Comparison of pV2021 with the canonical hypervirulent plasmids

The presence of hypervirulence attributes (rmpADC, rmpA2, iucA, iroB, peg-344) did not corroborate with the result of mice mortality, leading to further analysis of the pV2021 with other canonical hvKp plasmids that contributed to virulence. Comparative analysis of pV2021 with pLVPK (~218 kb) and pK2044 (~223 kb) reference plasmids resulted in high interplasmic resemblance with a DNA–DNA hybridization value of 97.70% and 99.80%, respectively (Figure 1D).

rmpA, rmpA2, peg-344 and aerobactin (iucABCDiutA) of Sil80 was detected to be identical to pLVPK and pK2044 plasmids. The salmochelin-producing cluster showed 100% similarity of iroBCD sequence with reference plasmids, but the receptor of iron-bound salmochelin iroN had undergone insertional mutagenesis by IS5 family transposase which may lead to a nonfunctional IroN receptor in pV2021. However, a functional copy of iroN in chromosome was noted, which probably compensate for the nonfunctional IroN receptor in the plasmid.

rmpC of pV2021 was 100% similar to reference plasmids. Importantly, rmpD known for the hypermucoviscous phenotype located within rmpA and rmpC was mutated in Sil80. Deletion in the C terminal end of RmpD resulted in a 42 amino acid protein shortened by 10 amino acids compared to the wild-type RmpD (Figure 5B). Mutated RmpD possibly lead to functional loss as indicated by Alphafold2 analysis. However, detailed analysis of rmpADC cascade promoter resulted in differentiation of poly T track between −10 bp and −35 bp promoter region of pV2021 compared to the archetype virulence plasmid pLVPK and pK2044. Mutation analysis of capsular polysaccharide synthesis region

In addition to the alteration in rmpD, Sil80 exhibited several point mutations in cps cluster, including a significant substitution (F199L) in ManC, a Mannose-1-phosphate guanylyltransferase with a 99.79% sequence similarity to the K39 reference isolate in the Kaptive database. Other mutations common within K39 lineage were noted in Sil80 (galF, cpsACP, wzc, mshA, epsJ, gnd, manB, ugd, rfaG, gmd, wcpO, acyltransferase and glycosyl transferase family) except ManC (details in supplementary Table S1). However, in silico study using AlphaFold2 revealed that the point mutation in β-sheet motif probably did not affect the structure of mutated ManC compared to wild-type (Figure 5A).

Expression analysis of structural genes of rmpADC cascade

To detect the factors (mutated rmpD or/and P10T rmpADC promoter) responsible for such a phenotypic aberration as shown by Sil80, qRT-PCR of all the structural genes of rmpADC operon was performed. A significant downregulation of rmpADC cascade of Sil80 was determined through qRT-PCR compared to hypermucoid-hypervirulent reference laboratory isolates K1 (AMR/159/23), K2 (TSK-13) and K57 (AMR/122/23) harboring wild-type promoter with 11T (P11T) track and rmpADC structural genes. Compared to the K1 isolate, Sil80 showed downregulation of rmpA, rmpD and rmpC genes with the mean fold change values −70.03, −14.39 and −91.79, respectively (Figure 6B.1). In contrast to the K2 isolate, Sil80 showed downregulation of rmpA, rmpD and rmpC genes with the mean fold change values −38.16, −4.51 and −12.26, respectively (Figure 6B.2). Further, compared to the K57 isolate, Sil80 showed downregulation of rmpA, rmpD and rmpC genes with the mean fold change values −58.82, −4.46 and −25.94, respectively (Figure 6B.3).

Discussion

Carbapenem-resistant Kp (Sil80) was isolated from the blood of a septicemic neonate. The outcome of the neonate could not be determined as the neonate was shifted to another hospital. Sil80 belonged to the ST985-K39 serotype, was resistant to most antibiotics including meropenem (MIC 8 mg/L) and possessed all hypervirulence determinants (rmpA, rmpA2, iucA, iroB, peg-344) in a pLVPK-like plasmid. K39 is a serotype that has not yet been known to be associated with hvKp. So, for further characterization, whole genome sequencing was done. Three plasmids – i) pV2021 {IncHI1B(pNDM-MAR)/IncFIB(K)} carrying hypervirulence determinants, ii) pR2021.1 {IncFIB(pQil)/IncFII(K)} possessing blaNDM-1, aac(3)-IId, aph(3’)-VI, rmtC, qnrB1, blaDHA-1, blaTEM-12 iii) pR2021.2 (IncFII) carrying blaTEM-1, blaSFO-1, rmtB antibiotic-resistance determinants were found in Sil80. The presence of blaNDM-1 in Sil80 explained the high MIC of meropenem.

Core genome phylogenetic analysis of Sil80 with forty-nine global ST985-K39 isolates revealed ~99.60% similarity. Two observations were made – i) carbapenem resistance was noted in K39 serotype since 2018; ii) this was the first K39 isolate that possessed the pLVPK-like virulence plasmid (>217 kb) carrying rmpA, rmpA2, iucA, iroB, peg-344. To assess the transmissibility of individual plasmids, conjugation was performed to select different plasmid-harboring transconjugants using various criteria. pR2021.1 harboring blaNDM-1 transmitted to TC-NDM. Tellurite-resistance cascade (terABCDW) was noted in pV2021 but no transconjugants were selected in potassium tellurite alone. The plasmid sequence revealed that the 10 bp nic sites of pV2021 and pR2021.2 shared sequence similarity. As pR2021.2 harbored rmtB, we used potassium tellurite and amikacin, which led to the co-transmission of pV2021 and pR2021.2. This indicated that pR2021.2 acted as a helper plasmid, and the relaxase encoded by pR2021.2 probably catalyzed the sequence-specific nic site cleavage and conjugal transfer of pV2021 along with pR2021.2 (relaxase was missing in pV2021), as also noted earlier with similar nic sites [25]. The present study indicates that with the right selection pressure, non self-conjugative plasmids can transmit across species with the aid of helper plasmids leading to the convergence of antimicrobial resistance and virulence determinants as noted in emerging hitherto unrecognized serotypes, such as in K39 (Sil80).

Sil80 was initially characterized based on the presence of hypervirulence determinants. Further, in vitro and in vivo survival kinetics assays were carried out. The string test assay was negative and was corroborated by a thin capsule as noted in TEM. Murine mortality studies are considered the gold standard for distinguishing cKp from hvKp and most studies use the adult BALB/c mice model [30]. We employed the adult mice model to compare its virulence with previously characterized hvKp isolates (from other studies), even though the strain was originally isolated from a septicemic neonate. Given that hvKp can cause infection in immunocompetent hosts, the adult mice model was considered appropriate. Results of the in vivo model showed the lack of canonical signatures of hypervirulence. To understand such deviation from conventional hvKps, we analyzed the regulators of hypermucoid phenotype (rmpADC cascade, rmpA2), and deletion was noted in rmpD, which may lead to a thin capsule. Previous studies noted spontaneous mutations in rmpA/rmpA2 or lab-induced mutations in rmpD leading to attenuated virulence [19–21]. rmpA, rmpD and rmpC are members of rmp operon, controlled by a single promoter where rmpA acts as a transcriptional regulator of rmpD and rmpC. Activated rmpC increases cps expression, while activated rmpD increases hypermucoviscosity [21]. RmpD interacts with Wzc protein (in cps cluster) through its activated conserved C-terminal cytosolic tyrosine kinase domain and helps in the lengthening of the polysaccharide chain, producing a hypermucoviscous phenotype [22]. Loss of 10 amino acids at the C-terminal end of RmpD probably led to functional loss, resulting in attenuated virulence in Sil80. Ernst et al. showed that apart from rmpADC cascade, mutation in cps cluster is important in generating hypermucoid phenotype in cKp and virulence in the in vivo model [28]. In Sil80, several point mutations were noted within cps cluster. Most of these mutations were also found in other global K39 isolates, which had been considered as cKp. Hence, these mutations in cps have not been linked to hyper/hypomucoid phenotype. In Sil80, we noted a unique F199L mutation in ManC apart from previously reported mutations in cps. The mutated ManC in Sil80 retained its structure and function, as depicted by Alphafold2. However, the structural conservation does not always ensure kinetic efficiency, and it is possible that ManC had a reduced enzymatic turnover rate, leading to the production of thin capsule. A significant downregulation of all the structural genes in rmpADC operon (including wild-type rmpA and rmpC) was noted in Sil80 compared to reference hypermucoid-hypervirulent K1, K2 and K57 clinical isolates (used in qRT-PCR assay). All the control isolates had P11T promoter and wild-type rmpADC genes, whereas Sil80 had P10T promoter, which reflected the non-functionality of the promoter of Sil80. Therefore, the heterogeneity in the promoter region of rmpADC operon in pV2021 compared to canonical pLVPK and pK2044 plasmids in terms of poly T track (P10T in pV2021 compared to P11T in pLVPK and pK2044) might cause reduced expression of rmpA, rmpD and rmpC in Sil80. An earlier study noted five different types of rmpA promoter (P9T to P13T) and found that P10T was a weaker promoter than conventional active P11T. Isolates having P10T disabled sufficient production of rmpA leading to non-hypermucoid phenotype, and showed no mortality in mice with an intravenous infection dose of 106 CFU/ml [23].

The attenuated virulence potential of Sil80 compared to the conventional hvKps was primarily due to three factors, mutated manC, truncated rmpD, and altered promoter region of rmpADC operon (P10T). Such mutations in the homopolymeric stretch of rmp promoter may act as a phase-variation switch between hypermucoidity and adhesion-colonization, as described previously [31]. However, it remains speculative whether mutations in the poly-T region of the rmp promoter are reversible or whether additional regulatory networks contribute to this phase variation. The isolate showed a strong biofilm production. An enhancement in biofilm production could have been facilitated by the unmasking of fimbrial adhesins, following the loss of hypermucoid phenotype, also described by others [31]. Sil80 failed to exhibit a hypervirulent pathotype, as evidenced by the absence of mortality in immunocompetent adult mice; nevertheless, it was serum resistant, could produce strong biofilm and met the definition of a pathogen, causing sepsis in an immunocompromised individual, a neonate.

Sil80 characterization led to several important observations. It provided evidence that the non self-conjugative virulence plasmid pV2021 can spread among different bacteria with the assistance of helper plasmids sharing sequence similarity at the nic site. The co-transmission of non self-conjugative virulence plasmid and helper resistance plasmid can transform a cKp to multidrug-resistant hvKp (MDR-hvKp) belonging to different serotypes, earlier not associated with hypervirulence. This study further elucidated that aberrations in the promoter regions of key hypervirulence determinants (such as rmpADC), along with a truncated rmpD, a regulator of the hypermucoid phenotype, may be responsible for the thin capsule and the observed attenuated virulence in adult mice. We can conclude that not only the mere presence of hypervirulence determinants but also the active promoter regions of those virulence markers should be considered to determine pathogenic potential of hvKps. Further studies to understand the importance and prevalence of these virulence markers, detailed analysis of promoters of virulence determinants, and their sequencing may guide the design of an objective test to identify hvKps.
