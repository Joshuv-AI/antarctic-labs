Solution of Erdős Problem 633
Michael Beeson, Miklos Laczokovich, Yan X Zhang

Abstract
. We classify triangles that can be tiled only into a square number of congruent triangles, settling Erdös Problem 633.



2020 Mathematics Subject Classification
51M20, 51M04, 11D25

1.
Introduction

We say that a triangle
T

tiles
into a triangle
R
(the
tile
) if
T
can be cut into a union of triangles congruent to
R
, overlapping only at their boundaries. Call such a tiling a
square
tiling if the number of tiles is a square and a
reptiling
if
T
and
R
are similar. Every triangle has “generic” square reptilings into
N^{2}
tiles for any
N
(see Fig.
1
), so the following ($25) Erdős Problem (633 by the indexing of
[
5
]
,
tracing back to
[
18
, p. 48]
) can be seen as trying to understand which triangles
T
can be “non-generically” tiled.

Classify those triangles which can only be tiled by square tilings.

Our main result, which settles the problem, is:

Theorem 1
.

A triangle
T
admits a non-square tiling if and only if it satisfies
one of the following conditions, where

(A,B,C)
are the angles of
T
in some order:

(1)

A=B
, i.e.
T
is an isosceles triangle (including equilateral);

(2)

C=\pi/2
and the legs
of the right triangle
T
are in integer ratio
M/K
, where
M^{2}+K^{2}
is not a square;

(3)

(A,B,C)=(\pi/6,\pi/2,\pi/3)
;

(4)

C=\pi/3
, with
\sqrt{3}\tan(A/2)
rational;

(5)

B=2A
, with
\sqrt{3}\tan(A/2)
rational;

(6)

B=2A
, with
\sin(A/2)
rational;

(7)

C=A/2+B
, with
2\sin(A/4)

rational, equal to
M/K
, where
2K^{2}-M^{2}
is not a square.

(8)

C=2A+B/2
, with
\sqrt{3}\tan(A/2)
rational.

As an immediate consequence we find that, apart from the isosceles triangles, the number of non-similar triangles having a non-square tiling is countable.

After giving some preliminaries in Section
2
, we prove the
left-to-right direction of Theorem
1
in Section
3
, showing that one of the given conditions must hold if we have a non-square tiling. Then in Section
5
we prove the right-to-left direction. We apply the following two steps
to each case:

•

The number
N
of tiles is given by
dividing the area of
T
by the area of the tile. That gives a formula for
N
in terms of the angles and sides of
T
, differing per case.

•

Apply number theory
to prove that
N
cannot be a square. For the tiling to be square, we would have integer solutions to Diophantine equations
of form
N=Z^{2}
. It is natural to reduce these equations to elliptic curves. We use the Nagell-Lutz theorem to reduce the search to a finite number of rational points (which is possible since the ranks of the given elliptic curves are always zero), then check that the given points do not correspond to actual tilings.

This plan is carried out, case by case, in the five propositions starting with Prop.
21
.
We end with visual examples of our tilings in Section
6
.

2.
Preliminaries

We say that a triangle has
commensurable angles
if all of its angles are rational multiples of
\pi
and
incommensurable angles
otherwise. The case in which the tiled triangle has commensurable
angles has been dealt with in
[
17
,
10
,
11
]
.
The result, however, is not stated in those sources in the exact form we need.
We therefore state it, and extract its proof from the cited
papers. We start with a lemma:

Lemma 2
.

Let triangle
T
be tiled by triangle
R
, where
T
is not equilateral. Then
R
has
commensurable angles if and only if
T
has commensurable angles.

Proof.

See the first paragraph of the proof of Theorem 5.3 in
[
10
]
.
∎

Theorem 3

(
[
10
,
11
]
)
.

Suppose
T
has commensurable angles and tiles into
R
. If
T
is not an isosceles (or equilateral) triangle, then the tiling is a reptiling.

Proof.

Following
[
10
]
, let
c(T)
is the
number of distinct non-similar triangles
R
into which
T
can be tiled. Then
c(T)=1

means that
T

can only be tiled by
R
similar to
T
(that is, a reptiling).
Theorem 5.3 of
[
10
]
says that, under the assumptions that
T
has
commensurable angles and is not isosceles, or is the isosceles right triangle,
then
c(T)=1
, and if
T
is isosceles but neither right nor equilateral, then
c(T)=2
.
Hence, under the assumptions of our theorem,
c(T)=1
.
∎

The main result related to reptiling is:

Theorem 4

(
[
17
]
)
.

Suppose
T
can be reptiled into
N
tiles. Then
N
must be one (or more) of:

•

N=M^{2}
, which is possible for any triangle
T
and any natural number

;

•

N=M^{2}+K^{2}
, in case where
N
is not a square,
T
must be a right triangle with legs having ratio
M/K
;

•

N=3M^{2}
, in which case

must be the
(\pi/2,\pi/3,\pi/6)
right triangle.

Combining these two theorems, we obtain:

Corollary 5
.

If
T
has commensurable angles, then
T
admits a non-square tiling if and only if one of the following is true:

(1)

T
is isosceles (including equilateral);

(2)

T
is a right triangle with legs in integral ratio
M/K
where
M^{2}+K^{2}
is not a square;

(3)

T
is the
(\pi/2,\pi/3,\pi/6)
right triangle.

Proof.

If
T
is isosceles,
T
has at least one non-square tiling into
2
tiles by a perpendicular bisector. If
T
is not isosceles, direct applications of Theorems
3
and
4
finish the proof.
∎

We say that a triangle has
commensurable sides
if its sides have pairwise rational ratios. Equivalently, we can assume the sides are scaled to be integers. The following result allows us to limit our attention to these cases:

Theorem 6
.

[
4
, Theorem 1.2]

Let triangle
T
be tiled by a tile
R
such that

•

R
is not similar to
T
;

•

R
is not a right triangle;

•

R
has incommensurable angles.

Then
R
must have commensurable sides.

Proposition 7
.

A triangle
R
with angles
(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)
where
3\alpha+2\beta=\pi
has commensurable sides if and only if

\sin\left(\frac{\alpha}{2}\right)\in\mathbb{Q}.

Proof.

Let the sides of
R
opposite
(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)
be
(a,b,c)
respectively. By the law of sines we have
a/c=\sin\alpha/\sin\gamma

and
b/c=\sin\beta/\sin\gamma
. Thus we have to show that
\sin\alpha/\sin\gamma
and
\sin\beta/\sin\gamma
are both rational if and only if

\sin(\alpha/2)
is.

We have
\beta=(\pi-3\alpha)/2
and
\gamma=(\pi+\alpha)/2
, and thus

\sin\beta=\cos(3\alpha/2)=4\cos^{2}(\alpha/2)-3\cos(\alpha/2)
and

\sin\gamma=\cos(\alpha/2)
. Therefore, we have

\sin\alpha/\sin\gamma=2\sin(\alpha/2)
and
\sin\beta/\sin\gamma=4\cos^{2}(\alpha/2)-3=1-4\sin^{2}(\alpha/2)
, from which the statement follows.
∎

Proposition 8
.

A triangle
R
with angles
(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)
where
\alpha+\beta
equals
\pi/3
or
2\pi/3
has commensurable sides if and only if

(1)

\displaystyle\sqrt{3}\sin(\alpha)\in\mathbb{Q}\mbox{\ and\ }\cos(\alpha)\in\mathbb{Q},

or equivalently,

\sqrt{3}\tan\left(\frac{\alpha}{2}\right)\in\mathbb{Q}.

If (
1
) holds, then

(2)

\cos\alpha=\frac{1-3t^{2}}{1+3t^{2}},\quad\sin\alpha=\frac{2\sqrt{3}t}{1+3t^{2}},

where
t\in{\mathbb{Q}}
and
0<t<1/3
.

Proof.

Let
(a,b,c)
be the sides of
R
opposite
(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)
respectively.
We have
\beta=\pi/3-\alpha
or
2\pi/3-\alpha
, and thus

\sin\beta=\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\cos\alpha\pm\frac{1}{2}\sin\alpha.

By the law of sines we have

\frac{a}{c}=\frac{\sin\alpha}{\sin\gamma}=\frac{2\sin\alpha}{\sqrt{3}}=\frac{2\sqrt{3}\sin\alpha}{3},

and

\frac{b}{c}=\frac{\sin\beta}{\sin\gamma}=\cos\alpha\pm\frac{\sqrt{3}}{3}\sin\alpha.

Hence
a/c
and
b/c
are both rational if and only if
\sqrt{3}\sin\alpha\in\mathbb{Q}
and
\cos\alpha\in\mathbb{Q}
.
That proves that
R
has commensurable
sides if and only if (
1
) holds.

Finally, we show that the two conditions given in the proposition are equivalent. Let
t=\tan(\alpha/2)/\sqrt{3}
. Then

(3)

\displaystyle\cos\alpha=\frac{1-\tan^{2}(\alpha/2)}{1+\tan^{2}(\alpha/2)}=\frac{1-3t^{2}}{1+3t^{2}}

(4)

\displaystyle\ \sin\alpha=\frac{2\tan(\alpha/2)}{1+\tan^{2}(\alpha/2)}=\frac{2\sqrt{3}t}{1+3t^{2}}.

If
t
is rational, then Equation (
1
) holds. If Equation (
1
) holds, then the identity

\tan(\alpha/2)=\frac{\sin(\alpha)}{1+\cos(\alpha)}

shows that
\sqrt{3}\tan(\alpha/2)\in\mathbb{Q}
, so
t
is rational.
∎

3.
The Forward Direction

Note that if
T
tiles into congruent copies of
R
and
T
has incommensurable angles, then
R
must also have incommensurable angles; otherwise, each angle of
T
would be equal to a sum of rational multiples of
\pi
from the tiling, so
T
would have commensurable angles. This allows us to use the following corollary of another result from
[
10
]
:

Theorem 9

(Corollary of Theorem 4.1,
[
10
]
)
.

Let a non-isosceles triangle
T
be tiled into
R
where
R
has incommensurable angles
(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)
. Then one of the following must hold:

(1)

the tiling is a reptiling;

(2)

(Group 1):
3\alpha+2\beta=\pi
and
T
has angles
(\alpha,2\alpha,2\beta)
or
(2\alpha,\beta,\alpha+\beta)
; or

(3)

(Group 2):
3\alpha+3\beta=\pi
(equivalently,
\gamma=2\pi/3
) and
T
has angles

(\alpha,2\alpha,3\beta),(\alpha,2\beta,2\alpha+\beta),(\alpha,\alpha+\beta,\alpha+2\beta),\text{or}(2\alpha,2\beta,\alpha+\beta).

Proof.

See
[
10
]
, Theorem 4.1.
That theorem has more cases in the conclusion than
shown here, but does not have our hypothesis that

T
is not isosceles.
By adding in the requirement that
T
is not isosceles, we reduce the number of cases of the original result. ∎

We say that a tiling is
Group 1
or
Group 2
if it is of the corresponding type in the statement of Theorem
9
.

Lemma 10
.

Suppose a non-isosceles triangle
T
has a non-square, non-reptile tiling into
R
where
R
has incommensurable angles
(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)
. Then one of the following four cases must hold for some permutation of the angles
(A,B,C)
of
T
:

•

C=\pi/3
;

•

B=2A
;

•

C=A/2+B
;

•

C=2A+B/2
.

Proof.

Applying Theorem
9
, we must have a reptiling, a Group 1 tiling, or a Group 2 tiling. We excluded the reptiling case in our assumptions.

In Group 2,
\alpha+\beta=\pi/3
, so in the
(\alpha,\alpha+\beta,\alpha+2\beta)
or
(2\alpha,2\beta,\alpha+\beta)
cases, we can conclude for some angle
C
,
C=\pi/3
. The Group 1
(\alpha,2\alpha,2\beta)
and Group 2
(\alpha,2\alpha,3\beta)
both have one angle twice another; in other words,
B=2A
. There is one case left in both groups.

In the final Group 1 case, permuting
(A,B,C)=(2\alpha,\beta,\alpha+\beta)
gives
C=A/2+B
. In the final Group 2 case, permuting
(A,B,C)=(\alpha,2\beta,2\alpha+\beta)
gives
C=2A+B/2
.
∎

Thus, our task reduces to carefully classifying when a non-square tiling is possible in these four cases. We
will first show that the conditions as they are outlined in Theorem

are necessary, then separately show that they are sufficient by exhibiting actual tilings.

Suppose we have a reptiling. By Theorem

4.
Some number theory

Most of the work in this paper is carefully patching together previous results. The main “new” work we need is to prove certain numbers are not squares, which comes down to showing certain Diophantine equations have no solutions. First, we cite a useful transformation from
[
8
]
:

Lemma 16

(Corollary 7.2.2, p. 477,
[
8
]
)
.

Each rational solution
(t,s)
to

s^{2}=t^{4}+at^{2}+b

gives a rational solution
(x,y)
to

y^{2}=x^{3}-2ax^{2}+(a^{2}-4b)x

via the transformation

x=2t^{2}-2s+a,y=2t(2t^{2}-2s+a).

Proof.

The algebra is routine. If
x\neq 0
, we have the inverse
t=\frac{y}{2x},s=(\frac{y}{2x})^{2}-(x-a)/2.

∎

Proposition 17
.

Suppose

and

are coprime positive integers, then

and

cannot both be squares.

Proof.

Let

. Suppose

were a square. Since

and

are coprime,
\gcd(a,a+b)=\gcd(a,b)=1
. This means
a
and
(a+b)
must both be (coprime) squares. Suppose

and
a+b=n^{2}
, with
\gcd(m,n)=1
. Then
b=n^{2}-m^{2}
and we may substitute
a
and
b
into

to get

(7)

c^{2}=n^{4}-m^{2}n^{2}+m^{4}.

By
[
9

(see also
[
7
]
,
which provides a proof), the only positive solutions to Equation
7
are of the form
(m,n,c)=(t,t,t^{2})
, which would force

, contradicting the hypothesis that

is positive.

While we are done, we provide another proof that demonstrates our common strategy for the remaining cases where the equations are not (at least immediately to us) well-known. First, via the substitution
t=n/m
and
s=c/m^{2}
, we know that each rational solution
(m,n,c)
of Equation
7
gives a solution
(t,s)
to the equation

(8)

By Lemma
16
, rational solutions of Equation

give rational solutions to the equation

(9)

x^{3}+2x^{2}-3x=y^{2}.

Let
\gamma
denote the group of
rational points of the curve (

\gamma
is zero; this can be shown by applying the method described in
[
16
, pp. 91-94]
.
By the Nagell-Lutz Theorem
[

, we only need to check a finite number of points. We can check that that there are

of finite order (and thus the whole group):

and the point of infinity. Our claims can be checked as properties of the elliptic curve with label 96.b1 at LMFDB
[

By reversing our transformation, we find that Equation

∎

5.
Existence

In this section, we prove the other direction of Theorem
1
.

Theorem 1

(Right to Left direction)
.

For each of the following conditions, suppose triangle
T
with angles
(A,B,C)
in some order satisfies the conditions. Then
T
admits a non-square tiling.

(1)

A=B
, i.e.
T
is an isosceles triangle (including equilateral);

(2)

C=\pi/2
and the legs
of the right triangle
T
are in integer ratio
M/K
, where
M^{2}+K^{2}
is not a square;

(3)

(A,B,C)=(\pi/6,\pi/2,\pi/3)
;

(4)

C=\pi/3
, with
\sqrt{3}\tan(A/2)
rational;

(5)

B=2A
, with
\sqrt{3}\tan(A/2)
rational;

(6)

B=2A
, with
\sin(A/2)
rational;

(7)

C=A/2+B
, with
2\sin(A/4)

rational, equal to
M/K
, where
2K^{2}-M^{2}
is not a square.

(8)

C=2A+B/2
, with
\sqrt{3}\tan(A/2)
rational.

We address the first three cases. For case (1), simply split
T
symmetrically into two triangles. Cases (2) and (3) have been well-studied in
[
17
]
.

We address the remaining five cases in
the next five propositions. For each of the cases, we must show that at least one non-square tiling exists.
We do not require new tilings. Cases (4), (5), and (8) have explicit constructions by the third author in
[
19
]
. Cases (6) and (7) have explicit constructions by the first author in
[
1
]
. However, the existence of at least one tiling for each case (4) - (8) was already proven by the second author in
[
10
]
. The main work we do is showing that the number of tiles in these constructions is not a square. Our methods show that all possible constructions for cases (5), (6), and (8) are non-square tilings, a specific family of constructions for (4) give non-square tilings, and constructions for (7) can be determined to be square or not based on only the angles of
T
.

.

have incommensurable angles

are

8
and the rationality conditions,

has commensurable sides, which we may take to be integers

with no common divisor, where by the law of cosines,

The 28-tiling turned out to be the smallest member of a new
family of tilings. These are the “triquadratic tilings”; they exist when
the “tiling equation”

M^{2}+N=2K^{2}

has a solution in integers
(K,M)
, such that
M|K

and
K|N
, or equivalently
K|M^{2}
. When we speak of a “solution of the tiling equation”,
we mean to include the divisibility conditions just mentioned.
Each such solution determines the tile of the corresponding
triquadratic tiling: the tile must be similar to the triangle
with sides
a=M
,
c=K
, and
b=K-M^{2}/K
. All three sides of
that tile are integers, and the tile then satisfies the condition

3\alpha+2\beta=\pi
.
