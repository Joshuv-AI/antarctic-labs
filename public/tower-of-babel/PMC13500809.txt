Effects of an indole chemical, mitochonic acid 5, in a mouse model of mitochondrial disease onset
Authors: Emi Ogasawara, Haruna Tani, Chitose Suzuki, Yuji Owada, Masaki Ogata, Naotada Ishihara, Takaaki Abe, Kazuto Nakada
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study tested the effects of mitochonic acid 5 (MA-5) using a mouse model of mitochondrial disease onset (mito-mice∆), with disease resulting from the accumulation of pathogenic mitochondrial DNA harboring a large deletion (∆mtDNA). Administration of MA-5 to mito-mice∆ inhibited the progression of clinical symptoms, such as low body weight and lactic acidosis. In the kidneys, MA-5 protected against mitochondrial respiration defects and subsequent renal failure, even when ∆mtDNA accumulated to > 80%. In the heart, MA-5 also resolved the mitochondrial respiration defects. Our findings suggested that administration of MA-5 would be effective in delaying the progression of some mitochondrial diseases caused by mutant mtDNA and especially in mitochondrial-mediated renal failure.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-57342-3.

Introduction

The accumulation of pathogenic mitochondrial DNAs (mtDNAs) harboring a large deletion called the “common deletion,” or a particular point mutation in affected tissues induces mitochondrial respiration defects, leading to a wide variety of mitochondrial diseases with heterogeneous clinical phenotypes, such as myopathy, cardiomyopathy, neuropathy, stroke-like episodes, external ophthalmoplegia, deafness, renal failure, and diabetes1–3. Although our understanding of the biochemical and molecular bases of the pathogeneses of mitochondrial diseases has progressed, potential treatment strategies are still limited4,5.

In mitochondrial diseases caused by the accumulation of pathogenic mutant mtDNA molecules, the most effective treatment strategy is to specifically eliminate the mutant mtDNA molecules, or to dilute the molecules to under the threshold for expressing mitochondrial respiration defects and the resultant disease phenotypes. Germ-line therapy using nuclear transplantation has been successfully applied to preferentially dilute mutant mtDNA molecules and completely inhibit the onset of mitochondrial diseases in mouse models6. However, it is not possible to apply this strategy in patients who have already contracted mitochondrial diseases. Therefore, the development and evaluation of effective drug therapies for patients with mitochondrial diseases are urgently needed.

Many clinical trials have been performed to establish potential drugs to treat patients with mitochondrial diseases and the beneficial effects and side effects of these chemicals have been reported7,8. Recently, we identified through screening an indole chemical, mitochonic acid 5 (MA-5) [4-(2, 4-difluorophenyl)-2-(1 H-indol-3yl)-4-oxobutanoic acid], which is a newly synthesized derivative of the plant hormone, indole-3-acetic acid, as a potential chemical for enhancing mitochondrial respiratory function9. We subsequently reported that culture with MA-5 was effective for the recovery of mitochondrial respiration deficiency in human fibroblasts carrying pathogenic mutant mtDNAs9–11. MA-5 targets Mic60/mitofilin at the cristae junction, which may facilitate the formation of mitochondrial respiratory supercomplexes and ATP synthase oligomerization10,11. Mic60/mitofilin is a core component of the mitochondrial contact site and the cristae organizing system12 and may play a role in not only cristae morphology but also protein transport, apoptosis, and mitochondrial DNA transcription with various interacting partners13,14.

Trans-mitochondrial mice carrying both wild-type and ∆mtDNA (mito-mice∆)15,16 are useful models to demonstrate the beneficial effects and side effects of MA-5 in mitochondrial diseases, because the ∆mtDNA introduced into mito-mice∆ is similar to the human pathogenic mtDNA with a “common deletion” that is carried in patients with typical mitochondrial diseases, such as chronic progressive external ophthalmoplegia (CPEO), Kearns–Sayre syndrome (KSS), and Pearson syndrome15–17. The ∆mtDNA harbors a 4,696 bp deletion spanning the tRNALys gene to the ND5 gene (nucleotide positions 7,759–12,454), encompassing six tRNA genes and seven structural genes. Mito-mice∆ are heteroplasmic mice carrying both wild-type and ∆mtDNA throughout their tissues and they exhibit clinical phenotypes only when the ∆mtDNA load exceeds the threshold, impairing the translation of mtDNA-encoded subunits in mitochondrial respiratory complexes and resulting in mitochondrial respiration defects6,15–17.

We previously applied MA-5 to mito-mice∆ and the results indicated that MA-5 may be effective in protecting mice from the induction of mitochondrial respiration deficiency in cardiac and renal tissues10. However, MA-5 was administered to mito-mice∆ before they developed mitochondrial diseases. Thus, it remained unclear whether MA-5 has beneficial effects in mito-mice∆ following the onset of mitochondrial diseases. In clinical trials, MA-5 would be applied to patients with the onset of mitochondrial diseases. Therefore, in the current study, we used mito-mice∆ with clinical signs of the onset of mitochondrial diseases due to the predominant accumulation of ∆mtDNA. Our findings showed that the progression of mitochondrial disease phenotypes in mito-mice∆ was delayed by the administration of MA-5.

Results

Mice carrying > 60% ∆mtDNA in their tails showed the onset of mitochondrial disease

In this study, we selected mice carrying ∆mtDNA that showed clinical signs of the onset of mitochondrial diseases, because it is expected that MA-5 would be administered to patients already displaying symptoms of mitochondrial diseases in the case of clinical trials. It has been reported that lactic acidosis (i.e., an increase in the blood lactate level) is frequently observed in patients with mitochondrial diseases, due to the increased production of glycolytic ATP without mitochondrial respiration18–20. To select the mito-mice∆ for this study, we first examined the blood lactate concentrations of mice carrying 0–73% ∆mtDNA (at 24–30 weeks of age) (n = 52). Mice carrying > 40% ∆mtDNA in their tails showed increased blood lactate concentrations compared with mice carrying 0% ∆mtDNA, and all mice carrying > 60% ∆mtDNA in their tails showed high blood lactate concentrations compared with mice carrying 0% ∆mtDNA (Fig. 1A). Therefore, we divided the mito-mice∆ into two groups, those with ≤ 60% ∆mtDNA and those with > 60% ∆mtDNA in their tails. Mice carrying > 60% ∆mtDNA in their tails showed a higher blood lactate concentration compared with mice carrying 0% and ≤ 60% ∆mtDNA (Fig. 1B).

We next checked the body weights of these mice, because a low body weight is another clinical sign of the onset of mitochondrial diseases (Fig. 1C). No significant differences in body weight were detected between mice carrying 0% ∆mtDNA and ≤ 60% ∆mtDNA in their tails, although higher lactate concentrations were observed in the latter (Fig. 1B, D). The body weights of mice carrying > 60% ∆mtDNA in their tails were lower than those in mice carrying 0% ∆mtDNA and ≤ 60% ∆mtDNA (Fig. 1C, D). These results suggested that mice carrying > 60% ∆mtDNA displayed the onset of mitochondrial disease. Based on these results, we selected mito-mice∆ carrying > 60% ∆mtDNA in their tails (n = 14) as the experimental group and mice carrying 0% ∆mtDNA (n = 18) as the normal control group for subsequent experiments.

Equality of pathological conditions in mice

We divided the experimental group into two subgroups, the MA-5 group (> 60% MA-5 group) (n = 7) and the DMSO group (> 60% DMSO group) (n = 7), with similar proportions of ∆mtDNA in their tails (Table 1). We again checked the blood lactate concentrations and body weights and confirmed that there were no significant differences between the two groups (Supplementary Figure S1A, B). We also divided the normal control group into two subgroups, the MA-5 group (0% MA-5 group) (n = 9) and the DMSO group (0% DMSO group) (n = 9), and confirmed that there were no significant differences in blood lactate concentrations and body weights between these groups (Supplementary Figure S1C, D). To the 0% and > 60% MA-5 groups and the 0% and > 60% DMSO groups, we orally administered MA-5 and DMSO solutions, respectively, once daily for 27 days (see the Methods).

Administration of MA-5 inhibited the progression of mitochondrial disease symptoms

It has previously been reported that the body weights of mice carrying ∆mtDNA gradually decrease after the onset of mitochondrial diseases6,21. Therefore, we first examined the body weights of mice among the groups. However, no clear effect on body weight was observed in the 0% or > 60% groups on day 27 following MA-5 treatment (Fig. 2A). The body weights of mice in the 0% MA-5 and 0% DMSO groups were maintained for approximately 1 month, with no significant differences between the body weights on day 0 and those on each day after oral administration of MA-5 and DMSO solutions (Fig. 2B). This suggested that MA-5 did not affect the body weights of normal control mice. By contrast, consistent with previous reports, the body weights of mice in the > 60% MA-5 and > 60% DMSO groups decreased, suggesting the progression of mitochondrial diseases, although there was no significant difference between the body weights of the > 60% MA-5 and > 60% DMSO groups on each day after treatment (Fig. 2C left). In contrast, in the comparison between body weights on day 0 and those on each day after treatment, a statistically significant decrease was observed in the > 60% DMSO group from day 21 onwards (Fig. 2C middle), whereas no significant decrease was observed in the > 60% MA-5 group (Fig. 2C right). We further examined the rate of body weight change relative to day 0 in each group on each day after treatment. Compared with the > 60% DMSO group, the decrease in body weights in mice in the > 60% MA-5 group was less pronounced after day 18 (Fig. 2D). These findings suggested that administration of MA-5 inhibited the decrease in body weight that is a clinical sign of the progression of mitochondrial diseases.

We next checked lactic acidosis in the > 60% MA-5 and > 60% DMSO groups on day 27 after treatment. Compared with the 0% MA-5 and 0% DMSO groups, the > 60% MA-5 and > 60% DMSO groups showed higher blood lactate concentrations after treatment (Fig. 3A). There were no significant differences between blood lactate concentrations in the > 60% MA-5 and > 60% DMSO groups and between the 0% MA-5 and 0% DMSO groups (Fig. 3A). During mitochondrial diseases, mitochondrial respiration defects can induce a compensatory increase in glycolysis leading to elevated lactate production. This is because pyruvate is inadequately used in the reoxidation of reduced cofactors through the electron transport chain. Accordingly, the glycolytic pathway produces glycolytic ATP, but without mitochondrial respiration, this results in elevated lactate production. Therefore, we performed detailed analysis of blood lactate concentrations after glucose loading. The blood lactate concentrations under glucose loading conditions were clearly lower in the > 60% MA-5 group than in the > 60% DMSO group (Fig. 3B). These findings suggested that MA-5 might have an effect of lowering lactate concentration, but this was limited to conditions following glucose loading. Thus, the effect of MA-5 on lactate concentrations must be recognized and interpreted with appropriate care. Therefore, we further examined the effects of MA-5 on the progression of lactic acidosis by comparing blood lactate concentrations on day 0 and day 27 in the > 60% DMSO and > 60% MA-5 groups. After 27 days of DMSO or MA-5 treatment, two cases clearly showed increased blood lactate concentrations in the > 60% DMSO group, but not in the > 60% MA-5 group (Fig. 3C). A significant decrease in the blood lactate concentration was observed on day 27 of treatment in the > 60% MA-5 group, but not in the > 60% DMSO group (Fig. 3C). These results indicated that MA-5 may alleviate worsening lactic acidosis, one of the symptoms of mitochondrial disease. Furthermore, in comparison with the 0% MA-5 and 0% DMSO groups, the 0% MA-5 group also showed a decreased blood lactate concentration after 27 days of treatment (Figure. 3C). These results also supported that MA-5 treatment might decrease the blood lactate concentration.

Growth differentiation factor 15 (GDF15) has recently been identified as a mitochondrial disease marker22. To further study whether MA-5 is effective at treating mitochondrial diseases, we compared the GDF15 levels in blood. Compared with the 0% MA-5 and 0% DMSO groups, the > 60% DMSO group, but not the > 60% MA-5 group, showed higher GDF15 levels in blood (Fig. 3D). We also compared the GDF15 levels between day 0 and day 27 of treatment. On day 27, the > 60% DMSO group showed an increase in GDF15 levels compared with day 0, consistent with progression of disease symptoms in the > 60% DMSO group (Fig. 3E). In contrast, no statistically significant change in GDF15 levels was observed in the > 60% MA-5 group after 27 days of treatment (Fig. 3E). In the > 60% DMSO group, 3 out of 4 individuals showed an appreciable increase in GDF15 levels. In the > 60% MA-5 group, only 1 out of 4 individuals showed an appreciable reduction in GDF15 levels, whereas 1 individual showed an appreciable increase and the remaining individuals showed little change, indicating substantial inter-individual variability. Although these results suggest that MA-5 treatment may influence changes in GDF15 levels, it is important to consider the limited sample size and substantial inter-individual variability.

Effects of MA-5 treatment in the kidney and heart

In our previous studies, we reported that renal failure was induced in mice harboring > 80% ∆mtDNA in their renal tissue6,15,16,21. Here, we compared the levels of creatinine and blood urea nitrogen (BUN) as clinical markers of renal failure. Comparison of creatinine and BUN levels among all groups on day 27 showed that creatinine levels in the > 60% MA-5 group were significantly lower than in the > 60% DMSO group and were similar to the 0% DMSO and 0% MA-5 groups, whereas the BUN levels in the > 60% MA-5 group were generally decreased compared with those in the > 60% DMSO group (Fig. 4A, B). When comparing day 0 and day 27, only the > 60% DMSO group showed increased creatinine and BUN levels after 27 days of treatment (Fig. 4C, D). For creatine, all 3 individuals in the > 60% DMSO group showed an increase, whereas only 1 of 4 individuals in the > 60% MA-5 group showed an increase (Fig. 4C). Similarly, all 3 individuals in the > 60% DMSO group showed an obvious increase in BUN levels, whereas only 1 of 3 individuals in the > 60% MA-5 showed an obvious increase (Fig. 4D). These results suggested that MA-5 mitigates the exacerbation of symptoms of renal failure, although the small sample size and large variation must be taken into consideration. To clarify whether the symptoms of renal failure were improved in mito-mice∆ following MA-5 treatment, we next examined morphological changes in the kidneys of the mice. In the 0% MA-5 and 0% DMSO groups, there were no differences in the morphology or weight of the kidneys (Fig. 4E, F). Consistent with our previous study, the kidneys harboring > 80% ∆mtDNA that were excised from the > 60% DMSO group were enlarged with a granulated surface and their weights were significantly higher than those from the 0% MA-5 and 0% DMSO groups (Fig. 4E, F). The kidneys harboring > 80% ∆mtDNA that were excised from the > 60% MA-5 group, however, were similar to those from the 0% MA-5 and 0% DMSO groups, and anemia was not detectable even when the kidneys harbored > 90% ∆mtDNA (Fig. 4E). Moreover, the weight of the kidneys harboring > 80% ∆mtDNA that were excised from the > 60% MA-5 group were much lower than those from the > 60% DMSO group, although they were still higher than those from the 0% MA-5 and 0% DMSO groups (Fig. 4F).

Having observed morphological improvement in the kidneys harboring > 80% ∆mtDNA from the > 60% MA-5 group compared with those from the > 60% DMSO group, we next examined the histological features of kidneys from these groups. In the kidneys carrying > 80% ∆mtDNA that were excised from the > 60% DMSO group, ureteral dilatation, urinary casts, and glomerulosclerosis were predominantly observed (Fig. 5A upper). By contrast, these histological renal abnormalities were drastically ameliorated in the kidneys carrying > 80% ∆mtDNA that were excised from the > 60% MA-5 group and the histological features were similar to those in the 0% DMSO and 0% MA-5 groups (Fig. 5A upper). Immunohistochemical staining revealed that F4/80, an inflammatory marker, was increased in the kidneys carrying > 80% ∆mtDNA that were excised from the > 60% DMSO group compared with those from the 0% DMSO and 0% MA-5 groups and the F4/80 signals were decreased in the > 60% MA-5 group compared with the > 60% DMSO group (Fig. 5A lower, B). Furthermore, in comparison with the 0% MA-5 and 0% DMSO groups, the 0% MA-5 group also showed decreased F4/80 signals after 27 days of treatment (Fig. 5B). Overall, the morphological and histological findings in the kidneys from all groups indicated that treatment with MA-5 was effective for recovery from renal failure in the kidneys carrying > 80% ∆mtDNA.

The accumulation of ∆mtDNA can induce mitochondrial respiration defects in affected cells, so we next performed histochemical analyses to detect the activities of electron transport complexes, cytochrome c oxidase (COX; stains brown) and succinate dehydrogenase (SDH; stains blue). The catalytic subunit of COX activity (complex IV) is encoded by mtDNA, and its activity is therefore affected by mutated mtDNA. By contrast, SDH activity (complex II) is entirely encoded by nuclear DNA, and its activity is generally unaffected by mutated mtDNA. Therefore, in cells with mitochondrial respiration defects caused by mutated mtDNA, COX-negative and SDH-positive staining can be observed in histochemical analyses. In histochemical double staining, cells showing mitochondrial respiration defects were visualized as negative for both nuclear DNA- and mtDNA-regulated COX activity and positive for nuclear DNA-regulated SDH activity (COX˗/SDH+; stained blue), whereas cells showing normal mitochondrial respiration were visualized as positive for COX and SDH activities (COX+/SDH+; stained brown). In mitochondrial diseases caused by mtDNA mutations, mitochondrial respiratory activity is not uniform among individual cells of a tissue, leading to a mosaic distribution of cells stained brown and cells stained blue by COX/SDH staining. Therefore, we analyzed the effect of MA-5 using COX/SDH staining to directly compare the distribution and degree of mitochondrial dysfunction among individual cells. The renal tissues of the 0% MA-5 and 0% DMSO groups contained only brown-stained COX+/SDH+ cells (Fig. 5C). The renal tissues carrying > 80% ∆mtDNA that were excised from the > 60% MA-5 and > 60% DMSO groups showed not only brown-stained COX+/SDH+ cells but also blue-stained COX˗/SDH+ cells (Fig. 5C), indicating mitochondrial respiration defects in these groups. However, the number of blue-stained COX+/SDH+ cells in renal tissues carrying > 80% ∆mtDNA that were excised from the > 60% MA-5 group was significantly lower than that of the > 60% DMSO group (Fig. 5D). This result indicated that treatment with MA-5 was effective for ameliorating mitochondrial respiration defects in renal tissues with predominant accumulation of ∆mtDNA. The main cause for the mitochondrial respiration defects due to ∆mtDNA is the shortage of six tRNA genes and seven structural genes encoded in the deleted region of ∆mtDNA. Therefore, the restoration of mitochondrial respiration defects by MA-5 treatment may be attributed to an increase in relative copy number of wild-type mtDNA due to an increase in total mtDNA copy number, providing a supply of the lost tRNAs and genes, and thereby restoring the translation of the electron transport complex subunits. We then examined the mtDNA copy number of the renal tissues from all groups. Compared with the mtDNA copy number in renal tissues from the 0% DMSO, 0% MA-5, and > 60% DMSO groups, only renal tissues with > 80% ∆mtDNA from the > 60% MA-5 group showed an increased mtDNA copy number (Fig. 5E). We next examined the protein levels of the subunits of electron transport complexes [mitochondrially encoded cytochrome c oxidase I (MTCOI), mitochondrially encoded cytochrome c oxidase II (MTCOII), and NADH: ubiquinone oxidoreductase subunit B8 (NDUFB8)] in renal tissues from all groups. Compared with the 0% DMSO and 0% MA-5 groups, the expression levels of these proteins were clearly decreased in the > 60% DMSO group, whereas the expression levels were restored in the > 60% MA-5 group (Fig. 5F, G).

Since a previous study has reported that MA-5 targets Mic60/Mitofilin, which is a core component of the mitochondrial contact site and the cristae organizing system (MICOS)11, we investigated whether administration of MA-5 affects mitochondrial membrane morphology. Electron microscopic analyses showed that mitochondria in renal tissues from the > 60% DMSO group exhibited lower electron density and appeared swollen compared with mitochondria in renal tissues from the 0% DMSO group, and no abnormalities were observed in mitochondria in renal tissues from the > 60% MA-5 group, resembling those in the 0% DMSO group (Fig. 6A). We further examined critical component proteins for MICOS—Mic60/Mitofilin and Mic19/CHCHD312—in renal tissues. Compared with the 0% DMSO and 0% MA-5 groups, the expression levels of Mic60/Mitofilin were clearly decreased in the > 60% DMSO group, whereas the expression levels of Mic60/Mitofilin were restored in the > 60% MA-5 group (Fig. 6B, C). Unlike Mic60/Mitofilin, the expression levels of Mic10/CHCHD3 were increased in the > 60% DMSO group compared with the 0% DMSO and 0% MA-5 groups, and this increase was suppressed in the > 60% MA-5 group (Fig. 6B, C). We also examined the expression levels of optic atrophy protein 1 (OPA1) because OPA1 plays a role in mitochondrial inner membrane fusion, mtDNA maintenance, and the cristae structure23. OPA1 exists as multiple isoforms due to alternative splicing and proteolytic processing. Inner membrane-anchored long isoforms (l-OPA1) are processed to soluble short isoforms (s-OPA1) and the ratio between l-OPA1 and s-OPA1 is considered important for mitochondrial and nucleoid regulation24. Although there were no significant differences in the expression levels of total OPA1 between the groups, in general, lower expression was observed in the > 60% DMSO group. Furthermore, the ratio of s-OPA1 to l-OPA1 was significantly increased in the > 60% DMSO group but not in the > 60% MA-5 group, compared with the 0% DMSO group (Fig. 6B, C). The data from electron microscopy and the expression levels of mitochondrial inner membrane proteins indicated that the abnormal mitochondrial membrane morphology (likely reflecting disruption of the cristae structure), which was evident in the renal tissues from the > 60% DMSO group, was restored following MA-5 treatment.

The mtDNA is organized into nucleoids with several DNA binding proteins and mitochondrial cristae associated with these nucleoids25. Disruption of the cristae structure or the loss of proteins involved with organizing the cristae are reported to be associated with mtDNA nucleoids. We next analyzed the expression levels of mitochondrial transcription factor A (TFAM), which is a major component of nucleoids and regulates mtDNA transcription and maintenance25,26. The expression levels of TFAM in the renal tissues from the > 60% DMSO group were decreased compared with those in the 0% DMSO and 0% MA-5 groups, and expression was restored in the > 60% MA-5 group (Fig. 6B, C).

Finally, we examined whether the beneficial effects of MA-5 were tissue specific in mice with the onset of mitochondrial diseases due to predominant accumulation of ∆mtDNA. In the heart, there were no significant differences in histology or weight between the groups (Fig. 7A, B). In the heart, there were no significant differences in the number of blue-stained COX˗/SDH+ cells between the > 60% MA-5 and > 60% DMSO groups, although blue-stained COX˗/SDH+ cells were slightly decreased in cardiac tissues carrying > 80% ∆mtDNA excised from the > 60% MA-5 group (Fig. 7C, D). We also examined the mtDNA copy number in cardiac tissues from these mice. Although the mtDNA copy number in cardiac tissues carrying > 80% ∆mtDNA excised from the > 60% MA-5 group and > 60% DMSO group were not significantly different, those from the > 60% MA-5 group showed an increased mtDNA copy number compared with the 0% DMSO group (Fig. 7E). Thus, MA-5 may be effective at increasing the mtDNA copy number even in cardiac tissues. We finally examined the protein levels of the mitochondrial respiratory complex subunits—MTCOI, MTCOII, and NDUFB8—in cardiac tissues. When comparing the > 60% MA-5 group with the > 60% DMSO group, no significant change in the expression level of MTCOI was observed, but the expression levels of MTCOII and NDUFB8 were restored (Fig. 7F, G).

Discussion

This study was designed to investigate whether MA-5 conferred beneficial effects in mice that had already developed mitochondrial diseases. The results suggested that administration of MA-5 to mito-mice∆ with a mitochondrial disease phenotype (the > 60% MA-5 group) delayed or inhibited the progression of mitochondrial disease. Our small sample size mouse study revealed that treatment with > 60% MA-5 mitigated the progression of typical mitochondrial diseases symptoms including body weight loss, lactic acidosis, and high levels of GDF15, compared with treatment with > 60% DMSO (Figs. 2 and 3). Importantly, histochemical analyses of COX and SDH activities showed a dramatic decrease in COX˗/SDH+ cells in renal tissue harboring > 80% ∆mtDNA excised from the > 60% MA-5 group compared with those from the > 60% DMSO group (Fig. 5C, D) and the protein levels of the mitochondrial respiratory complex subunits were restored (Fig. 5F, G). These results indicated that MA-5 may alleviate mitochondrial respiration defects due to the accumulation of ∆mtDNA. Although significant differences in the ratio of COX˗/SDH+ cells between cardiac tissues from the > 60% DMSO and > 60% MA-5 groups were not observed, the > 60% MA-5 group generally showed a reduced ratio of COX˗/SDH+ cells compared with the > 60% DMSO group and partial recovery of the protein levels of subunits of the mitochondrial respiratory complex (Fig. 7F, G). Remarkably, greater individual variability in the COX˗/SDH+ cell ratio was observed in cardiac tissues from the > 60% MA-5 group compared with the > 60% DMSO group (Fig. 7D). Therefore, these results suggested that MA-5 may have an overall effect on mitochondrial respiration defects even in cardiac tissues. Furthermore, the renal tissues from the > 60% MA-5 group showed not only a significant reduction in COX˗/SDH+ cells (Fig. 5C, D), but also a dramatic improvement in the histological morphology of the renal tissues with nephromegaly, tubular ectasia, and urinary cast (Fig. 5A). In addition to morphological improvements, the levels of renal functional markers, creatinine and BUN, were also compared between groups. The creatinine levels were significantly lower in the > 60% MA-5 group than in the > 60% DMSO group (Fig. 4A). Furthermore, the creatinine and BUN levels were increased between day 0 and day 27 in the > 60% DMSO group, whereas this increase was not observed during the 27-day treatment in the > 60% MA-5 group (Fig. 4C, D). Therefore, in contrast to the mild effect of MA-5 on cardiac tissue, MA-5 was highly effective in renal tissues with functional and morphological abnormalities caused by mitochondrial respiration defects due to the accumulation of ∆mtDNA. We concluded that MA-5 showed some beneficial effects in the > 60% MA-5 group that had already developed mitochondrial diseases. However, we acknowledge that the small sample size used in our study may limit the statistical reliability of our data. Therefore, a future, large-scale cohort study with an adequate sample size is required to strengthen our findings.

The accumulation of ∆mtDNA over the pathogenic threshold in affected tissues induces a lack of transcripts in the deleted site containing seven tRNAs that are necessary for mitochondrial translation, leading to mitochondrial respiration defects due to insufficient supply of some electron transport complexes (Complexes I, III, IV, and V), which contain mtDNA-coded subunits. Even though renal and cardiac tissues from both the > 60% DMSO and > 60% MA-5 groups retained > 80% ∆mtDNA in affected tissues, the > 60% MA-5 group showed an obvious decrease in the ratio of COX˗/SDH+ cells in renal tissue and a general decrease in the ratio of COX˗/SDH+ cells in cardiac tissue compared with the > 60% DMSO group (Figs. 5C and D and 7C and D). The regulation of mtDNA copy number is considered one of the key factors regulating mitochondrial respiratory activity27,28. In fact, it is reported that an increase in mtDNA copy number by the overexpression of TFAM, restored mitochondrial respiration defects in mice carrying > 80% ∆mtDNA29. Compared with the > 60% DMSO group, the > 60% MA-5 group showed an increase in total mtDNA copy number (Figs. 5E and 7E) and wild-type mtDNA copy number, as calculated based on the proportion of ∆mtDNA (Supplementary Figure S2) in renal and cardiac tissues. In these tissues from the > 60% MA-5 group, the protein levels of the mitochondrial electron transport complex subunits were restored compared with those from the > 60% DMSO group (Figs. 5F and G and 7F and G). Therefore, it may be that the increase in the absolute copy number of wild-type mtDNA may supply the transcription and translation of the mtDNA-encoded genes lacking in ∆mtDNA, which could lead to improvement in mitochondrial respiration defects in affected tissues with > 80% ∆mtDNA by MA-5 treatment. Thus, it was considered that the recovery of mitochondrial respiration in the > 60% MA-5 group was caused by the effect of MA-5 on the primary cause of the pathogenesis of mitochondrial respiration defects, namely the accumulation of ∆mtDNA. However, the reason for the increase in mtDNA copy number in the > 60% MA-5 group remains unclear. The increase in mtDNA copy number induced by MA-5 was observed in the > 60% MA-5-treated mice but not the 0% MA-5-treated mice, which suggested that MA-5 acts specifically in the presence of mitochondrial respiration defects.

In general, it is considered that stabilization of cristae morphology is important for maintenance of the mtDNA copy number30–32, and it has been reported that disruption of the cristae structure decreases the mtDNA copy number33,34. By contrast, it is also reported that disruption of cristae structure or the loss of cristae organizing proteins causes mitochondrial respiration defects by negatively impacting the mtDNA nucleoid, while maintaining mtDNA copy number35,36. TFAM is a major component of the mtDNA nucleoid structure and plays a role in mtDNA transcription and copy number regulation25,26. Decreased expression of the TFAM protein is associated with the loss of cristae-regulating proteins and subsequent morphological changes37,38. In our study, electron microscopic analyses revealed that the mitochondria in the renal tissues from the > 60% DMSO group were swollen and had a lower electron density, which indicated disruption of the cristae structure, and MA-5 treatment restored them to their normal morphology (Fig. 6A). In addition, the decrease in Mic60/Mitofilin proteins and the increase in Mic19/CHCHD3 proteins were restored to normal levels by MA-5 treatment, and the imbalance in the OPA1 isoform ratio of s-OPA1 to l-OPA1 observed in the > 60% DMSO group was also restored in the > 60% MA-5 group (Fig. 6B, C). These findings indicated that treatment with MA-5 may function to restore the composition and structure of the mitochondrial inner membrane. Furthermore, TFAM protein levels were decreased in renal tissues from the > 60% DMSO group compared with those from the 0% DMSO and 0% MA-5 groups, and TFAM protein levels were restored by MA-5 treatment (Fig. 6B, C). Although TFAM is known as a key regulator of mtDNA copy number, TFAM protein is decreased while mtDNA copy number is maintained in several pathological conditions, indicating a functional uncoupling between mtDNA copy number and transcriptional activity28,39. In fact, in renal tissues from the > 60% DMSO group, the mtDNA copy number was maintained while the TFAM protein level was reduced compared with the 0% DMSO and 0% MA-5 groups (Figs. 5E and 6B and C). Therefore, in renal tissues from > 60% DMSO-group, although mtDNA copy number was preserved, it may be that mtDNA transcription decreased due to the reduction in TFAM protein. The recovery of TFAM protein levels associated with improvements in mitochondrial membrane morphology and related regulatory proteins following MA-5 treatment, is thought to have contributed to mtDNA transcription and further led to an increase in mtDNA copy number. However, since mitochondrial dysfunction is known to cause cristae disruption40–42, it is possible that MA-5 increases TFAM protein through another mechanism independent of a cristae organizing function. The consequent increase in mtDNA copy number and the recovery of mitochondrial dysfunction cannot be ruled out as potentially contributing secondarily to maintenance of the membrane structure. Elucidating the underlying detailed molecular mechanism responsible for the increase in mtDNA copy number will be an important future research direction.

In the > 60% MA-5 group, the reduction in the ratio of COX˗/SDH+ cells and the recovery of mitochondrial respiratory chain protein were pronounced in renal tissues compared with cardiac tissues (Figs. 5 and 7). Compared with the > 60% DMSO group, the > 60% MA-5 group showed an increase in mtDNA copy number of approximately 2-fold in renal tissue and approximately 1.3-fold in cardiac tissue (Figs. 5E and 7E). The copy number of wild-type mtDNA, based on calculations of the total copy number and the proportion of ∆mtDNA, showed a significant increase in renal tissue, but not in cardiac tissue (Supplementary Figure S2). These findings suggested that the effect of MA-5 on mtDNA copy number may differ between renal and cardiac tissue. Mitochondrial function varies by cell type and it is therefore possible that differences may arise in the reflection of mitochondrial dysfunction on the overall tissue phenotype. In tissues composed of multiple cell types, each cell type may possess intrinsic susceptibility to ΔmtDNA and thus tissues may be considered to comprise cells ranging from low to high susceptibility, with specific cell types of high susceptibility potentially exerting a synergistic effect on the overall tissue. The kidney is an organ composed of multiple cell types and distinct structures and functions, including the glomerulus, proximal tubule, and distal tubule. Each cell type in the kidney exhibits specific metabolic states and mitochondrial requirements depending on its function, therefore, it is possible that the kidney exhibits high susceptibility to ΔmtDNA. By contrast, the heart is composed almost entirely of cardiomyocytes and its variation in susceptibility to ΔmtDNA pathogenicity due to different cell types would therefore be less, suggesting that the susceptibility of the overall tissue to ΔmtDNA pathogenicity may be lower. Indeed, compared with cardiac tissue, renal tissue exhibits a markedly more severe pathological state with a significantly altered tissue structure (Figs. 4, 5, and 7). Differences in the cellular composition between tissue types may therefore explain the differential effects of MA-5 on mtDNA copy number. The differences in mtDNA copy number increase might be associated with the tissue-specific effects of MA-5 on the recovery of mitochondrial respiratory function in these tissues; however, the mechanisms underlying the tissue-specific differences remain to be fully elucidated.

In the present study, the biodistribution of MA-5 in tissues was not examined; therefore, its tissue-specific bioavailability could not be evaluated. Accordingly, it remains unclear whether the observed differences in the effects of MA-5 between renal and cardiac tissues are attributable to differences in the bioavailability of MA-5 or inherent characteristics of specific tissue or cell types. Given that renal tissues generally exhibit a higher perfusion rate than cardiac tissues, disparities in tissue perfusion may partly account for the differences observed in the effects of MA-5 in these tissues. In addition to disparities in tissue perfusion and drug pharmacokinetics, other factors, such as tissue-specific metabolic requirements, inter-tissue differences in the accumulation of ΔmtDNA, and variation in the expression levels of mitochondrial regulatory proteins, including Mic60/mitofilin and related components of the MICOS complex, may also contribute to the tissue-specific effects of MA-5. Furthermore, because cardiac tissue predominantly consists of postmitotic cells with limited regenerative capacity compared to renal tissues, it relies on intracellular mitochondrial turnover to maintain cellular function; therefore, these differences may further influence responsiveness to MA-5. Further experimental investigations are required to elucidate the mechanisms underlying tissue-specific effects, including analyses of MA-5 concentrations within tissues, evaluation of cell type–specific sensitivity to ΔmtDNA and the extent of its accumulation, and assessment of tissue-specific bioavailability across multiple tissues. These analyses would contribute to distinguishing between pharmacokinetic effects and inherent tissue-specific characteristics, thereby providing a broader understanding of the tissue-specific effects of MA-5.

We previously reported that MA-5 could significantly improve mitochondrial respiration defects in renal and cardiac tissues when mito-mice∆ were administered MA-5 before disease symptoms appeared, i.e., at 53–57% ∆mtDNA10. As in the previous report, even when MA-5 was administered to mito-mice∆ already displaying disease symptoms, a dramatic improvement was observed in mitochondrial respiration defects in renal tissue (Fig. 5C, D). In contrast to the previous report, when MA-5 was administered to mito-mice∆ already displaying disease symptoms, no significant improvement was observed in mitochondrial respiration defects in cardiac tissues (Fig. 7C, D). These findings raise the possibility that the effect of MA-5 on improvement of mitochondrial respiration defects is influenced by the loading of mutant mtDNA or the severity of the disease condition and respiration defects at the start of MA-5 administration. Thus, mitochondrial respiration defects in cardiac tissues in the > 60% MA-5 group were considered to be beyond the limits of respiratory activity that could be successfully addressed by MA-5 at the start of treatment. Therefore, the timing of treatment initiation is considered to be important for the efficacy of MA-5 treatment for mitochondrial diseases and MA-5 may be more effective at improving mitochondrial respiration defects and controlling the progression of disease symptoms when it is used before the onset of severe symptoms of mitochondrial respiration defects.

Lactic acidosis is one of the typical symptoms of mitochondrial diseases and blood lactate levels have been used as a marker of the onset of mitochondrial disease; GDF15 is another recently reported marker for mitochondrial disease22. Comparison of changes in the blood levels of lactate and GDF15 over the 27-day treatment period in the > 60% DMSO group showed a significant increase in GDF15 levels but no clear increase in lactate levels (Fig. 3C, E). These findings suggest that GDF15 levels may reflect disease status; however, the limited sample size and substantial inter-individual variability observed in GDF15 and lactate levels should be taken into consideration. Therefore, further studies using larger cohorts, together with correlation analyses involving other disease markers and histological parameters, are required to clarify the relationship between GDF15 levels and mutation rate or disease progression, as well as to assess its potential utility as a predictor of treatment effect.

In conclusion, our previous study using MA-5 in mito-mice∆ before the onset of disease revealed a prolonged life-span and alleviation of mitochondrial respiration defects10. The current study using mito-mice∆ after the onset of disease also showed certain pathological suppressive effects, indicating that MA-5 is a potential therapeutic agent for mitochondrial diseases. Considering the limitations associated with the single-dose, short-duration design of the current study, we were unable to adequately assess the long-term progression of mitochondrial diseases, which are known to be chronic and progressive disorders. Therefore, further studies incorporating long-term MA-5 administration will be necessary to evaluate the sustained therapeutic effects, disease progression following treatment discontinuation, and the safety profile associated with prolonged administration. Determining the optimal dosing regimen—including daily, intermittent, or maintenance administration—will be a critical consideration for clinical application, with dose-response and dose-optimization studies to define the appropriate dosage range based on efficacy, safety, and tolerability. Furthermore, behavioral and physiological assessments, including in vivo assays to evaluate locomotion, anxiety-like behavior, and exercise tolerance, would provide important functional context for the therapeutic effects of MA-5.

Methods

Mice

The mice used in this study were previously generated as described15. In brief, the mice were generated by directly introducing mitochondria harboring ∆mtDNA, which were acquired by somatic mutation in mouse cultured cells, into zygotes, and they were maintained and bred in our institutional animal facility. We used 24–30-week-old male mice carrying 0–75% ∆mtDNA to examine blood lactate concentrations and body weights (n = 54). Of these 54 mice, 18 mice carrying 0% ∆mtDNA and 15 mice carrying 61%–73% ∆mtDNA were used for subsequent administration studies as the control group and the mitochondrial disease phenotype group, respectively. All animal experiments were carried out with the approval of the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of the University of Tsukuba and were performed in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations. At the end of the experiments, mice were euthanized by cervical dislocation performed by trained personnel without the use of anesthetic agents, in accordance with AVMA Guidelines for the Euthanasia of Animals (2020). This study is reported in accordance with the ARRIVE guidelines.

Experimental design

Mice without ∆mtDNA (mice with 0% ∆mtDNA) were randomly allocated into two groups using computer-generated randomization. Baseline data of the proportion of ∆mtDNA were collected from tail samples one week prior to the start of treatment and mice with ∆mtDNA were assigned into two groups, with the average proportion of ∆mtDNA balanced across groups. Once daily, MA-5 or DMSO dissolved in corn oil (50 mg/kg body weight) was administered to mice by oral gavage for 27 days. On day 28, plasma were collected, and tissues were excised for analyses. Analyses of plasma and tissues, and quantification of data were conducted in a blinded manner.

Estimation of the ∆mtDNA proportion

The proportions of wild-type mtDNA and ∆mtDNA were determined by real-time PCR, as described previously6. Total DNAs extracted from the tail, kidney, and heart were used as samples.

Measurement of mtDNA copy number

Measurement of the mtDNA copy number was performed according to a previous report43 with some modifications. Total DNAs including nuclear DNA and mtDNA were extracted from kidneys and hearts using the QIAmp DNA Mini Kit (QIAGEN, Hilden, Germany) and 1 ng of the DNAs was quantified. The mtDNA copy numbers were measured using a real-time PCR technique with Power SYBR Green (Applied Biosystems, CA, USA), including the nuclear gene glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) as a reference. The following primers were used: mtDNA forward primer, 5′-CCCACGATCAACTGAAGCAGCAA-3′ (nucleotide positions 4,060–4,082); mtDNA reverse primer, 5′-ACCGTTTGTTTGTTGTTGAAATATT-3′ (nucleotide positions 4,165–4,189); GAPDH forward primer, 5′-AACGACCCCTTCATTGAC-3′ (Chr 6: 125,141,933–125,141,950); GAPDH reverse primer 5′-TCCACGACATACTCAGCAC-3′ (Chr 6: 125,142,105–125,142,123). For each sample, the quantity of mtDNA and nuclear DNA relative to the standard curve was computed, and the ratio of these two DNAs was evaluated as the relative mtDNA copy number. A seven-point standard curve (range, 0.01–50 ng) was generated using total DNA of the heart or kidney of B6 mice. The thermal cycling conditions were 95 °C for 10 min, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 15 s and 60 °C for 60 s.

Measurement of blood lactate concentrations

Blood samples were collected from the tail veins of mice and the lactate concentration was measured using a blood lactate test meter, the Lactate Pro2 (ARKRAY, Kyoto, Japan), in accordance with the manufacturer’s procedure. To examine the blood lactate concentration under glucose loaded conditions, mice were fasted overnight and then a glucose solution (1.5 g/kg body weight) was administered orally.

Measurement of the growth differentiation factor 15 level

The levels of growth differentiation factor 15 (GDF15) in the plasma were measured using a Mouse/Rat GDF-15 Quantikine ELISA Kit (R&D Systems, Minneapolis, MN, USA).

Measurement of creatinine and blood urea nitrogen

Blood urea nitrogen (BUN) was measured using a blood analyzer (i-STAT; Fuso Pharmaceutical Industries, Osaka, Japan). Creatinine levels in plasma were measured by LC/MS/MS as we recently reported44.

Histological analysis

Renal tissue samples were fixed in 10% neutral-buffered formalin, embedded in paraffin, and sections were stained with hematoxylin and eosin or periodic acid-Schiff (PAS). For immunohistochemical analysis, sections were examined using anti-F4/80 antibody (1:100, MCA497GA, Bio-Rad Laboratories, Inc., CA, USA). F4/80 immunohistochemical staining was analyzed by measuring the area of F4/80-positive staining in 10 randomly selected fields (346 μm × 432 μm) at a 400× magnification, in a blinded manner, using Image J software.

Analysis of cytochrome c oxidase and succinate dehydrogenase activity

Histochemical analyses of nuclear genome-encoded succinate dehydrogenase (SDH) and mtDNA-encoded cytochrome c oxidase (COX) activities were carried out as described previously45. For these analyses, cryosections (10-µm thick) of renal and cardiac tissues were counted in five randomly selected fields (346 μm × 432 μm) at a 200× magnification for each section in a blinded manner.

Immunoblot analyses

Proteins were extracted from the kidney or heart using a buffer containing 50 mM Tris-HCl (pH 7.5), 150 mM NaCl, 2 mM Na3VO4, 100 mM NaF, 10 mM Na4P207, 1 mM EDTA, and 0.5% NP-40. The protein lysates were mixed with a sample buffer containing 62.5 mM Tris-HCl (pH 6.8), 2% SDS, 10% glycerol, 0.005% bromophenol blue with 20% 2-ME, separated by SDS-PAGE, and blotted onto polyvinylidene fluoride membranes (Millipore, Bedford, MA, USA). Proteins were detected using the following primary antibodies: anti-NDUFB8 (1:1000, ab110242, Abcam, Cambridge, UK), anti-MTCO1 (1:1000, ab14705, Abcam), anti-MTCO2 (1:1000, 55070-1-AP, Proteintech Group, IL, USA), anti-Tubulin (1:1000, MS-581; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), anti-Mitofilin (1:1000, 10179-1-AP, Proteintech Group), anti-CHCHD3 (1:1000, 25625-1-AP, Proteintech Group), anti-OPA1 (1:1000, 612606, BD Biosciences, CA, USA), anti-TFAM (1:1000, ab307302, Abcam), and anti-VDAC (1:1000, ab14734, Abcam). Subsequently, the following horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibodies were applied: goat anti-rabbit IgG (H + L) (1:10000, 31460; Thermo Fisher Scientific) and goat anti-mouse IgG (H + L) (1:10000, 31430; Thermo Fisher Scientific). Detection was carried out with Immobilon Western Chemiluminescent HRP substrate (Merck Millipore, Darmstadt, Germany). Tubulin and VDAC were used as an internal control for normalization, and quantification was conducted using Image J software.

Transmission electron microscopy

The fixation of samples onto slides and subsequent electron microscopic analyses were performed according to a previous report46.

Statistical analyses

The data are presented as the mean ± S.D. Statistical analyses were performed using Excel (Microsoft) and JMP (SAS Institute JAPAN) software, and comparisons were conducted using the Student’s t-test, the paired t-test, repeated measures ANOVA and William’s post-hoc test, or one-way ANOVA and Tukey–Kramer post-hoc test. A p value of < 0.05 was considered significant.
