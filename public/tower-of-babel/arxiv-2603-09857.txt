Abstract
. This paper investigates sloshing problems defined by − Δ ​ u = 0 -\Delta u=0 in Ω \Omega , with mixed boundary conditions: ∂ ν u = λ ​ u \partial_{\nu}u=\lambda u on S S , and either ∂ ν u = 0 \partial_{\nu}u=0 or u = 0 u=0 on W W . Here, Ω \Omega represents a smooth bounded domain in ℝ n \mathbb{R}^{n} with boundary ∂ Ω = S ∪ W \partial\Omega=S\cup W . We demonstrate that under small domain perturbations, all resulting eigenvalues are simple.

1.
Introduction

We examine two problems involving Steklov eigenvalues with mixed boundary conditions, which are fundamentally linked to the
sloshing problem
—a classic topic in fluid mechanics. This problem describes the linear eigenvalue system governing the small oscillations of the free surface of an ideal fluid under gravity. Mathematically, this corresponds to finding the eigenvalues for the mixed Steklov-Neumann boundary value problem:

\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}-\Delta u=0&\text{ on }\Omega\\
\partial_{\nu}u=\lambda u&\text{ in }S\\
\partial_{\nu}u=0&\text{ in }W\end{array}.\right.

(1)

where
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
(
n>2
) is a connected bounded domain with boundary components
S
(free surface) and
W
(rigid walls). The eigenvalues
\lambda
relate to the sloshing frequencies
\omega
through
\lambda=\omega^{2}/g
. We also consider the case with a homogeneous Dirichlet condition on
W

\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}-\Delta u=0&\text{ on }\Omega\\
\partial_{\nu}u=\lambda u&\text{ in }S\\
u=0&\text{ in }W\end{array}.\right.

(2)

These problems also arise in the study of Cauchy processes (see
[
2
]
) and serve as models for stationary heat distribution where the boundary
W
is either insulated (Neumann condition) or maintained at zero temperature (Dirichlet condition), while the heat flux through
S
is proportional to the temperature (Steklov condition). If
W=\emptyset
, the system simplifies to the classical Steklov eigenvalue problem

\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}-\Delta u=0&\text{ on }\Omega\\
\partial_{\nu}u=\lambda u&\text{ in }\partial\Omega\\
\end{array}\right.

(3)

It is worth noting that mixed Steklov-type eigenvalue problems have attracted considerable attention in recent years. We refer the reader to the insightful papers
[
3
,
5
,
6
]
, which provide both a comprehensive collection of open problems and an exhaustive list of references on the subject.

It is well known (see
[
1
,
6
]
) that both (
1
) and (
2
) possess a sequence of eigenvalues, counted with their multiplicities, denoted by

\lambda_{1}<\lambda_{2}\leq\lambda_{3}\leq\dots

It is straightforward to verify that the first eigenvalue is simple. For problem (
1
),
\lambda_{1}=0
and the associated eigenfunctions are the constant functions.
Regarding the classical sloshing problem (
1
), it has been conjectured in
[
9
,
5
]
that, in two dimensions, all eigenvalues are simple. In
[
10
]
, the authors proved that for certain Lipschitz planar domains—specifically those with a flat free surface—all eigenvalues
\lambda_{k}
are simple provided that
k
is sufficiently large.While this conjecture remains open in the general case, we prove here that it is generically true. More precisely, we show that for any given domain
\Omega
, there exists an arbitrarily small perturbation such that all eigenvalues of the modified problems (
1
) and (
2
) become simple. Our analysis specifically focuses on perturbations that leave one component of the boundary fixed.

The domain which we consider is an open connected bounded domain
\Omega
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, whose boundary
\partial\Omega
is
C^{1,1}
and it is composed of two sets
S
and
W
, with non empty
C^{2}
disjoint internal parts
\mathring{S}
and
\mathring{W}
with
\mathring{S}\cap\mathring{W}=\emptyset
.

We introduce a suitable Banach space of perturbations:

\mathcal{D}:=\{\psi\in C^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n},\mathbb{R}^{n}),\ \|\psi\|:=\max_{i=0,1,2}\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}|\psi^{(i)}|<+\infty\}.

Given
\psi\in\mathcal{D}
we define the perturbed domain by
\Omega_{\psi}:=(I+\psi)(\Omega)
, and we rewrite problem (
1
) and (
2
) on the domain
\Omega_{\psi}
.

Our main results are stated as follows.

Theorem 1
.

For any
\varepsilon>0
, there exists a perturbation
\psi\in\mathcal{D}
with
\|\psi\|<\varepsilon
, leaving either
S
or
W
fixed, such that all eigenvalues of the Steklov-Dirichlet problem

\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}-\Delta u=0&\text{ on }\Omega_{\psi}\\
\partial_{\nu}u=\lambda u&\text{ on }(I+\psi)S=:S_{\psi},\\
u=0&\text{ on }(I+\psi)W=:W_{\psi},\end{array}.\right.

(4)

are simple.

Theorem 2
.

For any
\varepsilon>0
, there exists a perturbation
\psi\in\mathcal{D}
with
\|\psi\|<\varepsilon
leaving
W
fixed, such that all eigenvalues of the Steklov-Neumann problem

\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}-\Delta u=0&\text{ on }\Omega_{\psi}\\
\partial_{\nu}u=\lambda u&\text{ on }(I+\psi)S=:S_{\psi},\\
\partial_{\nu}u=0&\text{ on }(I+\psi)W=:W_{\psi},\end{array}.\right.

(5)

are simple. In addition, if
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
, it is also possible to find a perturbation leaving
S
fixed such that all eigenvalues of (
5
) are simple.

Let us make some comments.

Remark 3
.

As a consequence of Theorem
1
, we deduce that all non-zero Steklov eigenvalues on a bounded domain are generically simple. More precisely, if
\Omega
is a smooth bounded domain with
C^{1,1}
boundary, for any
\varepsilon>0
there exists a perturbation
\psi\in\mathcal{D}
with
\|\psi\|<\varepsilon
such that all non-zero eigenvalues of the Steklov problem

\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}-\Delta u=0&\text{ on }\Omega_{\psi}\\
\partial_{\nu}u=\lambda u&\text{ on }\partial\Omega_{\psi},\\
\end{array}.\right.

(6)

are simple.
The generic simplicity of Steklov eigenvalues was originally established by Wang
[
21
]
on compact Riemannian manifolds with boundary, following the method developed in
[
20
]
. This result was recently extended by the same author to bounded Euclidean domains in a preprint
[
22
]
. Here, we recover the generic simplicity of Steklov eigenvalues on bounded domains using an entirely different technique.

Remark 4
.

We note that these results imply that the eigenvalues of problems (
1
) and (
2
) are simple for a generic domain
\Omega
. Indeed, for any given domain
\Omega
, there exists an arbitrarily close perturbed domain
\Omega_{\psi}=(I+\psi)\Omega
such that all eigenvalues of the corresponding problem are simple. Furthermore, the perturbation
\psi
can be chosen to act on only one component of the boundary. Notably, in our construction, the perturbations
\psi
are chosen so as not to affect the interface
S\cap W
. It would be of particular interest to investigate the behavior of the eigenvalues when the perturbations are supported precisely on this interface, which remains a compelling direction for future research.

Remark 5
.

We point out that for
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
, the statement of Theorem
2
could be refined: one can find a perturbation leaving
S
fixed such that all eigenvalues of (
5
) have multiplicity at most
n-1
(see Remark
16
). It remains an open question whether this result is sharp.

All the proofs are based on an appropriate application of Micheletti’s approach to simplicity of eigenvalues (see for example
[
11
,
13
,
14
]
). The fundamental tool of this approach is the following abstract result (see
[
14
]
and
[
4
, Section 3]
) which provides a non splitting condition for eigenvalues under perturbation of the domain.

Theorem 6
.

Let
T_{b}:X\rightarrow X
be a compact, self-adjoint operator on a Hilbert space
X
, which depending smoothly on a parameter
b
in a real Banach space
B
.
Assume that
T_{b}
is Frechét differentiable in
b=0
. Let
\bar{\lambda}
an eigenvalue for
T_{0}
with multiplicity

and let
x_{1}^{0},\dots,x_{m}^{0}
form an orthonormal basisof the corresponding eigenspace. If
\lambda(b)
is an eigenvalue for
T_{b}
such that
\lambda(0)=\bar{\lambda}
and
\lambda(b)
maintains multiplicity
m
for all
b
with
\|b\|_{C^{0}}
small, then for all such
b
there exist a
\rho=\rho(b)\in\mathbb{R}

for which

\left\langle T^{\prime}(0)[b]x_{j}^{0},x_{i}^{0}\right\rangle_{X}=\rho\delta_{ij}\text{ for }i,j=1,\dots,m.

(7)

Transversality results and their application to elliptic partial differential equation constitute a beautiful and fascinating topic. For readers wishing to explore this subject more deeply, we suggest, alongside the aforementioned papers, the fundamental references

,

2.
Variational framework for Theorem
1
.

We work in the Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}(\Omega):=\{u\in H^{1}(\Omega)\ :\ u=0\text{ on }W\}
equipped with the bilinear form

a_{\Omega}(u,v):=\int_{\Omega}\nabla u\nabla vdx.

Since each
u\in\mathcal{H}
vanishes on the portion
W\subset\partial\Omega
of positive surface measure,
a_{\Omega}(\cdot,\cdot)
is an inner product equivalent to the standard one on
H^{1}(\Omega)
. We denote by
\|u\|_{\mathcal{H}}=\|u\|=(a_{\Omega}(u,u))^{1/2}
the induced norm.

We say that
u
is an eigenfunction with eigenvalue
\lambda
, that is
u
solves (
2
) in a weak sense if and only if

a_{\Omega}(u,\varphi)=\lambda\int_{\partial\Omega}u\varphi\ d\sigma.

for all
\varphi\in\mathcal{H}(\Omega)
.

For any
f\in\mathcal{H}
, we consider the continuous linear functional on
\mathcal{H}
defined by

L_{f}(\varphi):=\int_{\partial\Omega}f\varphi\ d\sigma=\int_{S}f\varphi\ d\sigma

By Riesz representation theorem, for any
f\in\mathcal{H}
, there exists a unique
w\in\mathcal{H}
, such that

a_{\Omega}(w,\varphi)=L_{f}(\varphi),

for all
\varphi\in\mathcal{H}

We then define
E_{\Omega}
the operator, from
\mathcal{H}
in itself, which associate
f
to
w
as above. Equivalently, if
w=E_{\Omega}(f)
, then

a_{\Omega}(w,\varphi)=a_{\Omega}(E_{\Omega}(f),\varphi)=\int_{\partial\Omega}f\varphi\ d\sigma\text{ for all }\varphi\in\mathcal{H},

(8)

that is
w
is a weak solution of

\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}-\Delta w=0&\text{ on }\Omega\\
\partial_{\nu}w=f&\text{ in }S\\
w=0&\text{ in }W\end{array}\right.

(9)

So, in light of (
9
),
(\lambda,u)
, is an eigenpair of (
2
), in a weak sense, if and only if
u=E_{\Omega}(\lambda u)
, or equivalently, if
E_{\Omega}(u)=\frac{1}{\lambda}u
. In fact, if
u=E_{\Omega}(\lambda u)
it holds

a_{\Omega}(u,\varphi)=a_{\Omega}(E_{\Omega}(\lambda u),\varphi)=\lambda\int_{\partial\Omega}u\varphi\ d\sigma\text{ for all }\varphi\in\mathcal{H}.

(10)

As
\{\lambda_{k}\}_{k}
is a sequence of positive increasing eigenvalues for problem (
2
), the reciprocals
\{\mu_{k}:=1/\lambda_{k}\}_{k}
form a decreasing sequence of positive eigenvalues of
E_{\Omega}
decreasing to zero while
k
increases. Also, any
\mu_{k}
admits the following Min-Max characterization:

\displaystyle\mu_{1}=\max_{a_{\Omega}(\varphi,\varphi)=1}\int_{S}\varphi^{2}

\displaystyle\mu_{t}=\inf_{[v_{1},\dots,v_{t-i}]}\sup_{\begin{array}[]{c}a_{\Omega}(\varphi,v_{i})=0\\
a_{\Omega}(\varphi,\varphi)=1\end{array}}\int_{S}\varphi^{2},\text{ where }\varphi\in\mathcal{H}.

In this way we transformed the original problem in an eigenvalue problem for the operator
E_{\Omega}
.

This entire construction carries over to any perturbed domain
\Omega_{\psi}
. However, in order to use Theorem
6
in the proof, it is necessary to work within the fixed space
\mathcal{H}(\Omega)
. To that end we pull back any function defined on
\Omega_{\psi}
on the unperturbed domain
\Omega
via the diffeomorphism
(I+\psi)
.

Specifically, if
x\in\Omega
and
\xi=(I+\psi)(x)=x+\psi(x)
, given a function
\tilde{u}\in\mathcal{H}(\Omega_{\psi}):=\{\tilde{u}\in H^{1}(\Omega_{\psi})\ :\ \tilde{u}=0\text{ on }(I+\psi)W\}
, we define the corresponding function
u\in\mathcal{H}(\Omega)
by

\tilde{u}(\xi)=\tilde{u}(x+\psi(x)):=u(x).

This defines a map

\displaystyle\gamma_{\psi}

\displaystyle:\mathcal{H}(\Omega_{\psi})\rightarrow\mathcal{H}(\Omega);

\displaystyle\gamma_{\psi}(\tilde{u})

\displaystyle:=u(x)=\tilde{u}(x+\psi(x))=\tilde{u}\circ(I+\psi)(x).

If
\|\psi\|_{C^{2}}<1/2
, the map
I+\psi
is invertible with inverse
(I+\psi)^{-1}=I+\chi
and the following maps are continuous isomorphisms:

\displaystyle\gamma_{\psi}:\mathcal{H}(\Omega_{\psi})\rightarrow\mathcal{H}(\Omega)

\displaystyle\gamma_{\psi}^{-1}=\gamma_{\chi}:\mathcal{H}(\Omega)\rightarrow\mathcal{H}(\Omega_{\psi}).

(12)

Obviously, it is possible to consider the same pull back and push forward maps defined in the whole spaces
H^{1}(\Omega)
and
H^{1}(\Omega_{\psi})
. We will use the same notation
\gamma_{\psi}
and
\gamma_{\chi}
in both cases.
Now, given
\tilde{u},\tilde{\varphi}\in\mathcal{H}(\Omega_{\psi})
we define a bilinear form
\mathcal{A}_{\psi}
on
\mathcal{H}(\Omega)
by pulling back the bilinear form
a_{\Omega_{\psi}}
on
\mathcal{H}(\Omega_{\psi})
as follows:

a_{\Omega_{\psi}}(\tilde{u},\tilde{\varphi})=\int_{\Omega_{\psi}}\nabla\tilde{u}\nabla\tilde{\varphi}\ dx=:\mathcal{A}_{\psi}(u,\varphi)

(13)

where
u=\gamma_{\psi}\tilde{u}
and
\varphi=\gamma_{\psi}\tilde{\varphi}
.
Recalling that
E_{\Omega_{\psi}}
is introduced in (
8
), we define

\displaystyle T_{\psi}:\mathcal{H}(\Omega)\rightarrow\mathcal{H}(\Omega);

\displaystyle T_{\psi}{u}:=\gamma_{\psi}E_{\Omega_{\psi}}\gamma_{\psi}^{-1}{u}.

(14)

Notice that
T_{0}=E_{\Omega}
.
Recalling that
\tilde{u},\tilde{\varphi}\in\mathcal{H}(\Omega_{\psi})
,
{u},{\varphi}\in\mathcal{H}(\Omega)
, and in light of (
8
) we have

\mathcal{A}_{\psi}(T_{\psi}{u},\varphi)=a_{\Omega_{\psi}}(E_{\Omega_{\psi}}(\tilde{u}),\tilde{\varphi})=\int_{\partial{\Omega_{\psi}}}\tilde{u}\tilde{\varphi}\ d\sigma=\int_{\partial\Omega}u\varphi\ B_{\psi}d\sigma\ \text{ for all }\varphi\in\mathcal{H}(\Omega).

(15)

Here
B_{\psi}
represents the Jacobian of the boundary change of variables. This final identity plays a crucial role in the application of the transversality theorem, allowing us to compute the derivatives of
T_{\psi}
with respect to
\psi

3.
Computation of derivatives

In this section we collect a series of technical Lemma necessary to compute the derivatives of the integral terms appearing in (
15
), which will be the fundamental tool for our result.

Lemma 7
.

Set

\mathcal{A}_{\psi}(u,\varphi):=\int_{\Omega_{\psi}}\nabla\tilde{u}\nabla\tilde{\varphi}d\xi.

Then, the derivative of
\mathcal{A}_{\psi}
with respect to
\psi
at
\psi=0
satisfies

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}^{\prime}_{\psi}(0)[\psi](u,\varphi)=

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}-\psi_{t}\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{t}}\nabla u\nabla\varphi+\psi_{i}\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{j}}\left(\frac{\partial u}{\partial x_{i}}\frac{\partial\varphi}{\partial x_{j}}+\frac{\partial u}{\partial x_{j}}\frac{\partial\varphi}{\partial x_{i}}\right)dx

\displaystyle+\int_{\partial\Omega}\psi_{t}\nu_{t}\nabla u\nabla\varphi-\psi_{i}\nu_{j}\left(\frac{\partial u}{\partial x_{i}}\frac{\partial\varphi}{\partial x_{j}}+\frac{\partial u}{\partial x_{j}}\frac{\partial\varphi}{\partial x_{i}}\right)d\sigma

Proof.

Observe that

\tilde{u}(\xi)=(\gamma_{\psi}^{-1}u)(\xi)=(\gamma_{\chi}u)(\xi)=u(\xi+\chi\xi),

so

\frac{\partial}{\partial\xi_{j}}\tilde{u}=\frac{\partial}{\partial\xi_{j}}u(\xi+\chi\xi)=\frac{\partial u}{\partial x_{i}}\frac{\partial(\xi+\chi\xi)_{i}}{\partial\xi_{j}}=\frac{\partial u}{\partial x_{j}}+\frac{\partial u}{\partial x_{i}}\frac{\partial\chi_{i}}{\partial\xi_{j}}.

At this point (with the Einstein convention on repeated indexes)

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{\psi}(u,\varphi):=

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega_{\psi}}\frac{\partial\tilde{u}}{\partial\xi_{j}}\frac{\partial\tilde{\varphi}}{\partial\xi_{j}}d\xi

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega_{\psi}}\left(\frac{\partial u}{\partial x_{j}}+\frac{\partial u}{\partial x_{i}}\frac{\partial\chi_{i}}{\partial\xi_{j}}\right)\left(\frac{\partial\varphi}{\partial x_{j}}+\frac{\partial\varphi}{\partial x_{s}}\frac{\partial\chi_{s}}{\partial\xi_{j}}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\nabla u\nabla\varphi|J_{\psi}|dx+\int_{\Omega}\frac{\partial u}{\partial x_{j}}\frac{\partial\varphi}{\partial x_{s}}\frac{\partial\chi_{s}}{\partial\xi_{j}}|J_{\psi}|dx+\int_{\Omega}\frac{\partial u}{\partial x_{i}}\frac{\partial\chi_{i}}{\partial\xi_{j}}\frac{\partial\varphi}{\partial x_{j}}|J_{\psi}|dx

\displaystyle+\int_{\Omega}\frac{\partial u}{\partial x_{i}}\frac{\partial\chi_{i}}{\partial\xi_{j}}\frac{\partial\varphi}{\partial x_{s}}\frac{\partial\chi_{s}}{\partial\xi_{j}}|J_{\psi}|dx

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\nabla u\nabla\varphi|J_{\psi}|dx+\int_{\Omega}\left(\frac{\partial u}{\partial x_{i}}\frac{\partial\varphi}{\partial x_{j}}+\frac{\partial u}{\partial x_{j}}\frac{\partial\varphi}{\partial x_{i}}\right)\frac{\partial\chi_{i}}{\partial\xi_{j}}|J_{\psi}|dx

\displaystyle+\int_{\Omega}\frac{\partial u}{\partial x_{i}}\frac{\partial\chi_{i}}{\partial\xi_{j}}\frac{\partial\varphi}{\partial x_{s}}\frac{\partial\chi_{s}}{\partial\xi_{j}}|J_{\psi}|dx

Since
\|\psi\|_{C^{2}}<1/2
, we can express
I+\chi=(I+\psi)^{-1}=\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}(-1)^{t}(\psi)^{t}
, then, taking the linear part of the sum, we have the first derivatives of
\chi
, that is
\frac{\partial\chi_{i}}{\partial\xi_{j}}=-\frac{\partial\psi_{i}}{\partial x_{j}}
.
Also, by elementary computations

|\mathrm{det}J_{I+\varepsilon\psi}|=1+\varepsilon\textup{div}\psi+o(\varepsilon)

so
J_{\psi}^{\prime}(0)[\psi])=\textup{div}\psi
. With these equalities in mind, we can compute the derivatives of
\mathcal{A}_{\psi}(u,\varphi)
. In fact we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}^{\prime}_{\psi}(0)[\psi](u,\varphi)=

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\textup{div}\psi\nabla u\nabla\varphi-\left(\frac{\partial u}{\partial x_{i}}\frac{\partial\varphi}{\partial x_{j}}+\frac{\partial u}{\partial x_{j}}\frac{\partial\varphi}{\partial x_{i}}\right)\frac{\partial\psi_{i}}{\partial x_{j}}dx

and integration by parts yields the desired result.
∎

Remark 8
.

Notice that, when
\varphi
is compactly supported in
\Omega
, then we simply have

\mathcal{A}^{\prime}_{\psi}(0)[\psi](u,\varphi)=\int_{\Omega}-\psi_{t}\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{t}}\nabla u\nabla\varphi+\psi_{i}\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{j}}\left(\frac{\partial u}{\partial x_{i}}\frac{\partial\varphi}{\partial x_{j}}+\frac{\partial u}{\partial x_{j}}\frac{\partial\varphi}{\partial x_{i}}\right)dx.

Lemma 9
.

Let
B_{\psi}
denote the Jacobian of change of variables on
\partial\Omega
induced by
\psi
. We have

\left.\frac{\partial}{\partial\psi}\int_{\partial\Omega}u\varphi\ B_{\psi}d\sigma\right|_{\psi=0}=\int_{\partial\Omega}u\varphi\left(\textup{div}\psi-\sum_{r=1}^{n}\partial_{\nu}\psi_{r}\nu_{r}\right)d\sigma=\left.\frac{\partial}{\partial\psi}\int_{\partial\Omega_{\psi}}\tilde{u}\tilde{\varphi}\ d\sigma\right|_{\psi=0}

and

\left.\frac{\partial}{\partial\psi}\int_{\partial\Omega}d(x)u\varphi\ B_{\psi}d\sigma\right|_{\psi=0}=\int_{\partial\Omega}d(x)u\varphi\left(\textup{div}\psi-\sum_{r=1}^{n}\partial_{\nu}\psi_{r}\nu_{r}\right)d\sigma

where
d:=1_{S}

Proof.

Given
\tilde{u},\tilde{\varphi}\in H^{1}(\Omega_{\psi})
, and set
\nu_{\Omega_{\psi}}
the outward normal to
\partial\Omega_{\psi}
, we extend
\nu_{\Omega_{\psi}}
smoothly to the whole
\Omega_{\psi}
, and we define the vectorial function
\tilde{F}:\Omega_{\psi}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n}
as
\tilde{F}:=\tilde{u}\tilde{\varphi}\nu_{\Omega_{\psi}}
. Now, by the divergence theorem we have the identity

\int_{\Omega_{\psi}}\frac{\partial}{\partial\xi_{i}}\tilde{F}_{i}d\xi=\int_{\partial\Omega_{\psi}}\tilde{F}_{i}(\nu_{\Omega_{\psi}})_{i}d\sigma=\int_{\partial\Omega_{\psi}}\tilde{u}(\xi)\tilde{\varphi}(\xi)d\sigma

(16)

By the usual change of variables we have

\int_{\partial\Omega_{\psi}}\tilde{u}(\xi)\tilde{\varphi}(\xi)d\sigma=\int_{\partial\Omega}u(x)\varphi(x)B_{\psi}d\sigma

Thus, differentiating in the
\psi
variable at
\psi=0
by (
16
) we have

\left.\frac{\partial}{\partial\psi}\int_{\partial\Omega}u\varphi\ B_{\psi}d\sigma\right|_{\psi=0}=\left.\frac{\partial}{\partial\psi}\int_{\Omega_{\psi}}\frac{\partial}{\partial\xi_{i}}\tilde{F}_{i}d\xi\right|_{\psi=0}.

(17)

Now, calling
F=\gamma_{\psi}\tilde{F}
, so
\tilde{F}(\xi)=\gamma_{\psi}^{-1}F(\xi)=\gamma_{\chi}F(\xi)=F(\xi+\chi\xi)
and proceeding as in Lemma
7
, we obtain

\int_{\Omega_{\psi}}\frac{\partial}{\partial\xi_{i}}\tilde{F}_{i}d\xi=\int_{\Omega}\left[\frac{\partial F_{i}}{\partial x_{i}}-\frac{\partial F_{i}}{\partial x_{t}}\frac{\partial\psi_{t}}{\partial x_{i}}\right]J_{\psi}dx

(18)

Combining (
17
) and (
18
), and integrating by parts we get

\displaystyle\left.\frac{\partial}{\partial\psi}\int_{\partial\Omega}u\varphi\ B_{\psi}d\sigma\right|_{\psi=0}

\displaystyle=\left.\frac{\partial}{\partial\psi}\int_{\Omega}\left(\frac{\partial F_{i}}{\partial x_{i}}-\frac{\partial F_{i}}{\partial x_{t}}\frac{\partial\psi_{t}}{\partial x_{i}}\right)J_{\psi}dx\textbf{}\right|_{\psi=0}

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\frac{\partial F_{i}}{\partial x_{i}}\textup{div}\psi dx-\int_{\Omega}\frac{\partial F_{i}}{\partial x_{t}}\frac{\partial\psi_{t}}{\partial x_{i}}dx

\displaystyle=-\int_{\Omega}F_{i}\frac{\partial^{2}\psi_{t}}{\partial x_{i}\partial x_{t}}dx+\int_{\partial\Omega}F_{i}\nu_{i}\textup{div}\psi d\sigma

\displaystyle+\int_{\Omega}F_{i}\frac{\partial^{2}\psi_{t}}{\partial x_{t}\partial x_{i}}dx-\int_{\partial\Omega}F_{i}\nu_{t}\frac{\partial\psi_{t}}{\partial x_{i}}d\sigma

\displaystyle=\int_{\partial\Omega}u\varphi\textup{div}\psi d\sigma-\int_{\partial\Omega}u\varphi\nu_{i}\nu_{t}\frac{\partial\psi_{t}}{\partial x_{i}}d\sigma

and, since
\nu_{i}\frac{\partial\psi_{t}}{\partial x_{i}}=\partial_{\nu}\psi_{t}
, we get the proof.

∎

Finally, similarly to the proof of Lemma
7
, we get the last result of this section.

Lemma 10
.

We have

\displaystyle\left.\frac{\partial}{\partial\psi}\int_{\Omega_{\psi}}\tilde{u}\tilde{\varphi}d\xi\right|_{\psi=0}

\displaystyle=\left.\frac{\partial}{\partial\psi}\int_{\Omega}u\varphi|J_{\psi}|dx\right|_{\psi=0}

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}-\psi_{t}\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{t}}u\varphi dx+\int_{\partial\Omega}\psi_{t}\nu_{t}u\varphi d\sigma

To streamline notation, define:

\displaystyle L_{1}(u,\varphi)

we can, as previously shown, absorb
L_{1}
in the right hand side of (
29
). Hence, the no splitting condition becomes

{}-I_{1}(\mu e_{r},e_{s})-G(\mu e_{r},e_{s})+G(e_{r},e_{s})=C(\psi)\delta_{rs}.

(30)

6.1.
Perturbations supported in
S

Now, we take a perturbation which leaves
W
fixed. The no splitting condition simplifies as

-I_{1}(\mu e_{r},e_{s})+(1-\mu)G(e_{r},e_{s})=C(\psi)\delta_{rs},

that is

\displaystyle C(\psi)\delta_{rs}

\displaystyle=-\mu\int_{S}\psi_{t}\nu_{t}\nabla e_{r}\nabla e_{s}d\sigma+\mu\int_{S}\psi_{i}\nu_{j}\left(\frac{\partial e_{r}}{\partial x_{i}}\frac{\partial e_{s}}{\partial x_{j}}+\frac{\partial e_{r}}{\partial x_{j}}\frac{\partial e_{s}}{\partial x_{i}}\right)d\sigma

(31)

\displaystyle+(1-\mu)\int_{S}e_{r}e_{s}\left(\textup{div}\psi-\sum_{r=1}^{n}\partial_{\nu}\psi_{r}\nu_{r}\right)d\sigma.

With a strategy similar to the one performed in
4.2
, we notice that
\nu_{j}\frac{\partial e_{r}}{\partial x_{j}}=\partial_{\nu}e_{r}=\lambda e_{r}=(\hat{\lambda}-1)e_{r}
, so integrating by parts we get, for the second term of (
31
),

\displaystyle\mu\int_{S}\psi_{i}\nu_{j}\left(\frac{\partial e_{r}}{\partial x_{i}}\frac{\partial e_{s}}{\partial x_{j}}+\frac{\partial e_{r}}{\partial x_{j}}\frac{\partial e_{s}}{\partial x_{i}}\right)d\sigma

\displaystyle=\mu(\hat{\lambda}-1)\int_{S}\psi_{i}\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{i}}\left(e_{r}e_{s}\right)d\sigma

\displaystyle=-\frac{\hat{\lambda}-1}{\hat{\lambda}}\int_{S}e_{r}e_{s}\textup{div}\psi d\sigma,

and, since in the third integral of (
31
),
(1-\mu)=(\hat{\lambda}-1)/{\hat{\lambda}}
, the no splitting condition becomes

\frac{1}{\hat{\lambda}}\int_{S}\psi_{t}\nu_{t}\nabla e_{r}\nabla e_{s}d\sigma+\frac{\hat{\lambda}-1}{\hat{\lambda}}\int_{S}e_{r}e_{s}\left(\sum_{r=1}^{n}\partial_{\nu}\psi_{r}\nu_{r}\right)d\sigma\ =C(\psi)\delta_{rs}.

There exists at least one component
\nu_{t}\neq 0
. For the sake of simplicity, let us take
\nu_{1}\neq 0
.
Now choose
\psi=(\psi_{1},0,\dots,0)
with
\psi_{1}
arbitrarily defined on
S
and such that
\frac{\partial\psi_{1}}{\partial\nu}=0
. Thus
\nabla e_{r}\nabla e_{s}=0
if
r\neq s
and
|\nabla e_{1}|^{2}=\dots=|\nabla e_{m}|^{2}
almost everywhere on
S
and the term
\int_{S}\psi_{t}\nu_{t}\nabla e_{r}\nabla e_{s}d\sigma
can be absorbed in the left hand side of the condition and we remain with

C(\psi)\delta_{rs}=\frac{\hat{\lambda}-1}{\hat{\lambda}}\int_{S}e_{r}e_{s}\sum_{r=1}^{n}\partial_{\nu}\psi_{r}\nu_{r}d\sigma

Since we can choose
\sum_{r=1}^{n}\partial_{\nu}\psi_{r}\nu_{r}
as and arbitrary function on
S
, we get that
e_{r}e_{s}=0
if
r\neq s
and
|e_{1}|^{2}=\dots=|e_{m}|^{2}
almost everywhere on
S
. This implies that on
S
we get, for any
r=1,\dots,m

e_{r}=0\ \hbox{and}\ \frac{\partial e_{r}}{\partial\nu}=(\hat{\lambda}-1)e_{r}=0,

which leads to a contradiction.

6.2.
Perturbations supported in
W
(when
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
)

If
\psi
leaves
S
fixed, namely, the support of
\psi
intersects
\partial\Omega
only within the set
W
, we have that
G(e_{r},e_{s})=G(T_{0}e_{r},e_{s})\equiv 0
. Proceeding as in Subsection
4.1
, we get that the no splitting condition (
30
) becomes

C(\psi)\delta_{rs}=-\mu\int_{W}\psi_{t}\nu_{t}\nabla e_{r}\nabla e_{s}d\sigma+\mu\int_{W}\psi_{t}\left(\frac{\partial e_{r}}{\partial\nu}\frac{\partial e_{s}}{\partial x_{t}}+\frac{\partial e_{r}}{\partial x_{t}}\frac{\partial e_{s}}{\partial\nu}\right)d\sigma

(32)

Now, since
\partial_{\nu}e_{r}=\partial_{\nu}e_{s}=0
, on
W
, we have
\left(\frac{\partial e_{r}}{\partial\nu}\frac{\partial e_{s}}{\partial x_{t}}+\frac{\partial e_{r}}{\partial x_{t}}\frac{\partial e_{s}}{\partial\nu}\right)=0
, so

\mu\int_{W}\psi_{t}\nu_{t}\nabla e_{r}\nabla e_{s}d\sigma=C(\psi)\delta_{rs}.

By the arbitrariness of
\psi
we have that if
r\neq s
then
\nabla e_{r}\nabla e_{s}=0
almost everywhere on
W
, and that
|\nabla e_{1}|^{2}=\dots=|\nabla e_{m}|^{2}
almost everywhere on
W
. Unfortunately, we were not able to derive a contradiction based on this condition in general. However, we can complete the proof of the Theorem
2
in the case
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
.
Since
\partial_{\nu}e_{r}=0
on
W
for
r=1,\dots,m
, consider
\tau
the tangent vector in a point to
W
. At this point we have that, by the no splitting condition, the set
\{\partial_{\tau}e_{1}/|\partial_{\tau}e_{1}|,\dots\partial_{\tau}e_{m}/|\partial_{\tau}e_{m}|\}
is an orthonormal set in the tangent space to
W
which is
\mathbb{R}
. This is impossible unless
m=1
and this ends the proof.

Remark 16
.

We want to point out that this latter idea partially works in higher dimension
n\geq 3
. In this case the set of tangential derivatives is an orthonormal set in
\mathbb{R}^{n-1}
, so it is possible to split any eigenvalue with a perturbation which leaves
W
fixed, up to a multiplicity of
n-1
.

Appendix A
A different variational approach to Theorem
2
.

In this section we give a sketch of an alternative proof of Theorem
2
,
which could be interesting per se, since the variational framework and the pull back and push back forms are different.
The main point in the technique of the proof is the choice of a good variational space with a scalar product that make the weak formulation easy.
One possibility, when attacking problem (
1
), is to work in the Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}(\Omega):=\{u\in H^{1}(\Omega)\ :\ \int_{\partial\Omega}du=0=\int_{S}u\}
equipped with the equivalent scalar product

a_{\Omega}(u,v):=\int_{\Omega}\nabla u\nabla vdx.

One can show easily that
u
is an eigenfunction with eigenvalue
\lambda
, in a weak sense if and only if

a_{\Omega}(u,\varphi)=\lambda\int_{\partial\Omega}u\varphi\ d\sigma\text{ for all }\varphi\in\mathcal{H}(\Omega)

(33)

and that it is easy to show that any
u\in H^{1}(\Omega)
eigenfunction of (
1
) with eigenvalue
\lambda\neq 0
belongs to
\mathcal{H}(\Omega)
.

As ususal we definte
E_{\Omega}(f)
, as the operator from
\mathcal{H}(\Omega)
in itself such that

a_{\Omega}(E_{\Omega}(f),\varphi)=\int_{S}f\varphi\ d\sigma\text{ for all }\varphi\in\mathcal{H},

(34)

so,
(\lambda,u)
, is an eigenpair of (
1
), in a weak sense, if and only if
u=E_{\Omega}(\lambda u)
, or equivalently, if
E_{\Omega}(u)=\frac{1}{\lambda}u
.

Now we consider the operator
E_{\Omega_{\psi}}
on the perturbed domain
\Omega_{\psi}
and we have to pull it back in the space
\mathcal{H}(\Omega)
.

To this end the usual map

\displaystyle\gamma_{\psi}

\displaystyle:{H^{1}}(\Omega_{\psi})\rightarrow{H^{1}}(\Omega);

\displaystyle\gamma_{\psi}(\tilde{u})

\displaystyle:=\tilde{u}\circ(I+\psi)(x).

is not sufficient, since we have to deal with a change of variable in the condition
\int_{\partial\Omega}du=0
in the definition of the space
\mathcal{H}(\Omega)
.

Called, as before
B_{\psi}(x)
, defined on
\partial\Omega
, be the Jacobian of the boundary change of variables from
\partial\Omega_{\psi}
to
\partial\Omega
, induced by
\psi
., we can extend this function
B_{\psi}
to the whole
\Omega
as follows.
Let
\Sigma_{k}=\{x\in\Omega\ :\ d(x,\partial\Omega)<k\}
. Since
\Omega
is regular there exist
k
such that
\Sigma_{k}
is a tubular neighborhood. Then, for
x\in\Sigma_{k}
we can write
x=y+t\nu(y)
where
y\in\partial\Omega
and
t>0
, and we define

\bar{B}_{\psi}(x)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}1&x\in\Omega\smallsetminus\Sigma_{k}\\
\left(1-\frac{t}{k}\right)B_{\psi}(y)+\frac{t}{k}&x\in\Sigma_{k}\end{array}\right..

(35)

Also, since
\left.\frac{\partial}{\partial\psi}B_{\psi}\right|_{\psi=0}=\textup{div}\psi-\partial_{\nu}\psi_{r}\nu_{r}
, set
\beta_{\psi}(y)=\textup{div}\psi-\partial_{\nu}\psi_{r}\nu_{r}
we can write
{B}_{\psi}(y)=1+\beta(y)+o(|\psi|)
and

\bar{B}_{\psi}(x)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}1&x\in\Omega\smallsetminus\Sigma_{k}\\
1+\left(1-\frac{t}{k}\right)\beta_{\psi}(y)+o(|\psi|)&x\in\Sigma_{k}\end{array}\right..

(36)

When no ambiguity arises, we simply write
{B}_{\psi}(x)
instead of
\bar{B}_{\psi}(x)
.

Now we can define the correct pull back map by means of
\gamma_{\psi}
and
B_{\psi}
as follows.

\displaystyle\hat{\gamma}_{\psi}

\displaystyle:\mathcal{H}(\Omega_{\psi})\rightarrow\mathcal{H}(\Omega);

\displaystyle\hat{\gamma}_{\psi}(\tilde{u})

\displaystyle:=\gamma_{\psi}(\tilde{u})B_{\psi}(x)=u(x)B_{\psi}(x)

At this point, given
\tilde{u},\tilde{\varphi}\in\mathcal{H}(\Omega_{\psi})
we define a bilinear form
\hat{\mathcal{A}}_{\psi}
on
\mathcal{H}(\Omega)
by pulling back by
\hat{\gamma}_{\psi}
the bilinear form
a_{\Omega_{\psi}}
on
\mathcal{H}(\Omega_{\psi})
as follows:

a_{\Omega_{\psi}}(\tilde{u},\tilde{\varphi})=\int_{\Omega_{\psi}}\nabla\tilde{u}\nabla\tilde{\varphi}\ dx=:\hat{\mathcal{A}}_{\psi}(\hat{u},\hat{\varphi})

(37)

where
\hat{u}=\hat{\gamma}_{\psi}\tilde{u}
and
\hat{\varphi}=\hat{\gamma}_{\psi}\tilde{\varphi}
and, as before, we define

T_{\psi}({\cdot}):=\hat{\gamma}_{\psi}E_{\Omega_{\psi}}\hat{\gamma}_{\psi}^{-1}({\cdot}).

(38)

The price we pay with this approach is that the identity which is crucial to find
T^{\prime}_{\psi}(0)
becomes

\hat{\mathcal{A}}_{\psi}(T_{\psi}{\hat{u}},\hat{\varphi})=a_{\Omega_{\psi}}(E_{\psi}(\tilde{u}),\tilde{\varphi})=\int_{S_{\psi}}\tilde{u}\tilde{\varphi}\ d\sigma\\
=\int_{S}u\varphi\ B_{\psi}d\sigma=\int_{S}\hat{u}\hat{\varphi}\ \frac{1}{B_{\psi}}d\sigma\ \text{ for all }\varphi\in\mathcal{H}(\Omega).

(39)

By the following Taylor expansion of
1/B_{\psi}

B_{\psi}^{-1}=1-\left(1-\frac{t}{k}\right)\beta_{\psi}(y)\mathbbm{1}_{\Sigma_{k}}+o(|\psi|)=\\
=1-\left(1-\frac{t}{k}\right)\left(\textup{div}\psi-\partial_{\nu}\psi_{r}\nu_{r}\right)\mathbbm{1}_{\Sigma_{k}}+o(|\psi|)

(40)

we can differentiate (
39
), obtaining

a_{\Omega}(T_{\psi}^{\prime}(0)[\psi]e_{r},e_{s})=-\mu L_{1}(e_{r},e_{s})-\mu I_{1}(e_{r},e_{s})+G(e_{r},e_{s}).

(41)

which is completely analogous to (
28
), giving rise to the same no splitting condition. At this point the proof of Theorem
2
follows identically.
