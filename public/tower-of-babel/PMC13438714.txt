Lipopolysaccharide hydrolysis-targeting nano-chimeras detoxify endotoxin through specific adsorption and efficient degradation
Authors: Lulu Jin, Chenyin Zhang, Hongli Yi, Jing Tang, Kexin Yu, Yuanyuan Wang, Feihe Huang, Ketao Jin, Zhengwei Mao, Lidan Hu, Xiaozhou Mou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractLipopolysaccharide (LPS), a potent immunogenic component of the outer membrane of Gram-negative bacteria, triggers severe inflammation and organ failure even at nanomolar concentrations. However, neutralizing LPS in vivo remains challenging due to the high abundance of other biomolecules in biological fluids, which interfere with LPS detoxification. Brassicaceae species produce a transmembrane protein termed lipooligosaccharide-specific reduced elicitation (LORE) that specifically recognizes and binds LPS. Here, we prepare plant-derived nanovesicles naturally presenting LORE on their surface and integrate them with cerium-based nanozymes exhibiting LPS hydrolysis activity. We show that these hybrid nanostructures (Atv@Ce) neutralize LPS through two coordinated mechanisms: LORE captures LPS specifically, while nanozymes chemically degrade the phosphate groups and glycosidic bonds in the lipid A moiety. This dual strategy effectively attenuates both local and systemic inflammation, offering a biocompatible detoxification strategy with translational potential. Our work provides an insight into nanomaterial-mediated detoxification.Subject terms: Organic-inorganic nanostructures, Drug delivery, Biomedical materials, Nanostructures

Introduction

Bacterial lipopolysaccharide (LPS) is a highly potent immunogenic pathogen-associated molecular pattern (PAMP) that plays a pivotal role in activating inflammatory responses across diverse cell types1. Recent studies have revealed its substantial contribution to the onset and progression of numerous diseases, including inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), cardiovascular disorders, neurological conditions, and sepsis, with increasingly recognized intricate relationships between the gut microbiota and various organs2–6. At the molecular level, the hydrophobic alkyl chains of lipid A insert into the hydrophobic cavity of myeloid differentiation factor-2 (MD2) on immune cells, while the phosphate groups of lipid A engage in ionic interactions with positively charged amino acids at the periphery of the MD2 cavity. These interactions drive dimerization of the Toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4)/MD-2 complex7,8, leading to excessive activation of the myeloid differentiation primary response protein 88 (MyD88)-dependent inflammatory signaling cascade, triggering a life-threatening cytokine storm. Ultimately, these events culminate in multiple organ dysfunction and, in severe cases, death9,10. Given these detrimental effects, the timely removal or inactivation of LPS from tissues or blood is of immense significance in mitigating disease progression.

The clearance of LPS poses a formidable clinical challenge. In sepsis patients, the concentration of LPS in the bloodstream is typically at the nanogram-per-milliliter level, whereas plasma proteins and other biomolecules exceed LPS concentrations by several orders of magnitude11. This disparity imposes stringent requirements on the anti-interference capacity of LPS adsorbents. Consequently, non-specific LPS adsorbents no longer meet clinical demands, underscoring the urgent need for specific adsorption formulations. The cationic antimicrobial peptide polymyxin B is the most classic example, exhibiting high affinity for the pyrophosphate groups of lipid A12–14. Although it has obtained drug marketing authorization in countries such as China and the United States, the subsequent discovery of severe nephrotoxicity has curtailed its clinical applications as an in vivo injectable formulation, particularly for patients with renal dysfunction15. Loading it onto a dialysis column for subsequent use may be a safer alternative16,17. Compared to hemodialysis, nano-adsorbents administered via intravenous (i.v.) injection offer several distinct advantages, including no requirement for special equipment, relatively operational convenience, and rapid onset of action, making them well-suited for emergency scenarios. Furthermore, these nano-adsorbents are capable of removing LPS not only from the circulatory system but also from localized sites. Safe endogenous components within the human body, such as mannose-binding lectin (MBL), high-density lipoprotein (HDL), and immune cells, have been identified to exhibit specific binding with oligosaccharide or lipid A moieties of LPS18,19. Inspired by these natural interactions, MBL-labeled magnetic nanobeads and macrophage membrane-derived nanovesicles have been developed, achieving therapeutic efficacy in the treatment of sepsis while ensuring a high degree of safety20,21. However, the scarcity of these biomolecules and the relatively low yields obtained through recombinant protein production technologies remain significant hurdles to their widespread clinical application. Furthermore, polymer particles with LPS-matched cavities have been crafted via molecular imprinting. Upon conjugation with an LPS-binding peptide, these particles exhibited dual-mode binding, combining shape-selectivity with peptide affinity for highly specific LPS capture22.

In a manner akin to its role in animal immunity, LPS also exerts a profound influence on plant immunity. Early research revealed that LPS isolated from Escherichia (E.) coli, Pseudomonas (P.) syringae, and Salmonella (S.) minnesota can bind to Arabidopsis thaliana (At), be transported within plant cells, and trigger oxidative stress, indicating the existence of specific proteins in At responsible for LPS binding, transport, or receptor function23–25. Currently, reports have revealed that the lipooligosaccharide-specific reduced elicitation (LORE) membrane protein serves as the LPS receptor in the Brassicaceae family26–28. Therefore, it is logical to hypothesize that nanovesicles derived from plant cell membranes possess specific adsorption capacity for LPS, comparable to that of nanoparticles derived from mammalian immune cell membranes11,21. Moreover, plant cells offer greater accessibility, lower production costs, and favorable biological safety profiles, rendering plant-based nanovesicles a promising alternative to conventional LPS adsorbents.

LPS-enriched adsorbents retain the capacity to activate inflammatory signaling pathways through sustained cellular contact. Degradation of the immunogenic structures of LPS following adsorption, therefore, represents a better option29. Natural intestinal alkaline phosphatase has been reported to hydrolyze the phosphate moiety in lipid A, thereby detoxifying LPS30. Furthermore, a class of nanomaterials with phosphatase-like activity capable of degrading phosphate groups in lipid A has been identified, including cerium-based and gadolinium-based nanozymes31–33. Although these nanozymes are inferior to natural enzymes in terms of substrate specificity, they show better stability and greater potential for mass production. When they are integrated with specific LPS adsorbents, this combination not only enhances the substrate selectivity of nanozymes but also holds promise for accelerating LPS detoxification.

In this work, thermodynamic assessment reveals a negative alteration in Gibbs free energy associated with LPS binding to LORE, indicating spontaneous association. Kinetic assays further demonstrate that LORE exhibits high-affinity binding to LPS from diverse bacterial strains. Through screening of plants within Brassicaceae family, we identify that the cell membrane of At demonstrates favorable LPS adsorption capacity, a property retained by nanovesicles prepared from these membranes. To achieve the dual goals of specific LPS adsorption and detoxification, nano-chimeras (Atv@Ce) composed of LORE-bearing At cell membrane nanovesicles and LPS-hydrolyzing nanozymes are designed. This hybrid nanomaterial exhibits strong resistance to biological interference, significantly attenuating cytokine storms in both LPS-induced and bacteria-derived sepsis models. These findings establish a paradigm for designing LPS antidotes, offering promising avenues for targeted therapies against LPS-mediated pathologies.

Results

Specific adsorption of LPS by plant cell membrane nanovesicles

To date, the plant-specific bulb-type lectin receptor kinase LORE has been identified as the primary LPS receptor that is phylogenetically restricted to the Brassicaceae family27,28. Therefore, a cohort of Brassicaceae species expressing LORE were selected, encompassing At, woad, cabbage, white radish, and broccoli (Supplementary Fig. 1A, B; Supplementary Note 1; Supplementary Method 1; Supplementary Table 1). Fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC)-conjugated LPS was co-incubated with cell membranes derived from these plants, and the changes in fluorescence intensity were monitored as an indication of LPS binding (Supplementary Fig. 2; Supplementary Method 2). The At membranes demonstrated an LPS adsorption rate of 85.17%, while white radish membranes showed an adsorption rate of 75.69%. For comparison, membranes from THP-1-derived macrophages, which are equipped with the classical LPS receptor TLR4/MD2 and CD14, showed an adsorption rate of 71.63% (Fig. 1A). Other non-Brassicaceae plants, including garlic, onion, spinach, and Dendrobium officinale, also exhibited LPS adsorption capacity despite the absence of LORE expression in these species (Supplementary Fig. 1C, D). Among them, spinach membranes showed an LPS adsorption rate of 83.39%, comparable to that of At cell membranes. The non-Brassicaceae plants do elicit immune responses upon exposure to LPS, but their LPS-sensing mechanisms remain under investigation and have not yet been fully elucidated28,34.

To delve deeper into the interaction between LORE and LPS, transient transgenic Nicotiana benthamiana (Nb) overexpressing LORE fused with mCherry was constructed (Supplementary Fig. 3; Supplementary Data 1; Supplementary Method 3; Supplementary Table 1). Given that LORE functions as an LPS receptor, its overexpression in plants may heighten sensitivity to LPS, thereby activating plant immune responses. Accordingly, Nb leaves became shriveled two days post-transfection, consistent with findings from previous studies27,28,35. Nb leaves exhibiting relatively intact morphology one day post-transfection were selected, and their cell membranes were subsequently isolated. Compared to membranes of wild-type Nb, the LPS adsorption capacity of LORE-mCherry-Nb membranes increased by 4.22 times (Fig. 1B). In addition, antibody against LORE was also prepared (Supplementary Fig. 4; Supplementary Method 4; Supplementary Data 1 and 2; Supplementary Tables 2–4). Following treatment of Nb cell membranes with anti-LORE antibody, no significant alteration in LPS adsorption was observed. In contrast, when cell membranes derived from LORE-mCherry-Nb plants were subjected to anti-LORE antibody treatment, a statistically significant reduction in LPS adsorption was detected, further supporting the relationship between LORE content and LPS adsorption (Fig. 1B). Furthermore, stable transgenic At expressing LORE fused with mCherry were generated (Supplementary Fig. 5A–D; Supplementary Method 5; Supplementary Data 1). Similar to the observations in Nb, the cell membranes from LORE-mCherry-At showed 3.21-fold increase in LPS adsorption compared to wild-type At membranes and also exhibited a pronounced reduction in LPS adsorption upon antibody-mediated inhibition (Fig. 1C). Given that At itself contains the LORE protein, blocking wild-type At with anti-LORE antibody led to a discernible decline in the LPS adsorption. Furthermore, a high degree of co-localization between FITC-LPS and mCherry-LORE was detected in At leaves (Supplementary Fig. 5E; Supplementary Method 6). Collectively, these findings confirmed the key role of LORE in mediating LPS interactions in plants.

Since LORE is a transmembrane protein, its interaction with LPS occurs at the extracellular segment. Thus, the extracellular domain of LORE (eLORE) was recombinantly expressed in HEK293 cells, achieving a purity exceeding 95% for subsequent experiments (Supplementary Fig. 6; Supplementary Data 1 and 2; Supplementary Method 7). We employed isothermal titration calorimetry (ITC) to investigated the thermodynamic parameters of eLORE-LPS binding (Fig. 1H, I; Supplementary Method 8). The binding process was exothermic (ΔH < 0), accompanied by an increase in system disorder (ΔS > 0). The negative Gibbs free energy change confirmed the spontaneity of this interaction. Additionally, the stoichiometric ratio (n) was determined to be 7.567. SDS-PAGE analysis revealed that eLORE remained monomeric under native conditions and no dimmer or higher-order multimer was observed in the presence of LPS (Supplementary Fig. 7A; Supplementary Method 9), ruling out polymerization as a contributing factor to the observed stoichiometry. Even when LPS was present in substantial excess relative to eLORE, the eLORE band exhibited a marked upward shift with increasing LPS content. Given the low critical micelle concentration of LPS (pM range), LPS exists predominantly as micelles in aqueous solution, undergoing dynamic assembly and dissociation. The dynamic equilibrium between monomeric and micellar forms contributes significantly to an overestimation of the n value. During LPS transport in the human body, lipopolysaccharide-binding protein (LBP), a serum-resident glycoprotein, first binds specifically to the lipid A moiety of LPS, forming an LPS-LBP complex that subsequently delivers the LPS to immune cell LPS receptors such as CD14 and TLR4/MD236–38. Surface plasmon resonance (SPR) was further employed to comparatively analyze the kinetics of binding between LPS and eLORE, CD14, TLR4/MD2 complex, or LBP. The dissociation rate constant (KD) between LPS and its respective binding partners were determined under the present SPR conditions (1:1 binding model) as follows: eLORE exhibited a KD of 0.97×10-5 M, CD14 displayed a KD of 2.39×10-5 M, the TLR4/MD-2 complex showed a KD of 6.70×10-5 M, and LBP demonstrated a KD of 0.56×10-5 M, respectively (Fig. 1D; Supplementary Fig. 8A, E, I; Supplementary Table 5; Supplementary Method 10). These findings indicated that eLORE exhibited higher affinity for LPS than CD14 and the TLR4/MD-2 complex, yet slightly weaker binding than LBP. Furthermore, owing to the similarity in the overall structure and heterogeneity in fine structure of LPS across bacterial species39, LPS from Escherichia (E.) coli, Pseudomonas (P.) aeruginosa, and Salmonella (S.) typhimurium were extracted (Supplementary Fig. 7B-G; Supplementary Table 6-13; Supplementary Note 2; Supplementary Method 11) and SPR assays were subsequently conducted using LPS from different sources. As shown in Fig. 1E–G, Supplementary Fig. 8, and Supplementary Table 5 and 14-17, LBP demonstrated consistently enhanced binding affinity to LPS derived from different bacterial species. Under the experimental conditions tested, eLORE demonstrated LPS-binding capacity comparable to or exceeding that of CD14 and TLR4/MD-2 complex. It should be noted that the parameters measured by SPR and ITC did not reflect single-molecule LPS binding events, but rather the collective interaction between LPS in both monomeric and micellar forms with its receptors, a scenario that more accurately mimics the physiological engagement of LPS with eLORE. In addition, at the cell membrane level, At membranes also showed broad-spectrum bacterial LPS adsorption capacity (Fig. 1J).

In addition to evaluating the binding affinity between LORE and LPS, the relative abundance of LORE on At membrane represents an intriguing parameter. Unlike animal cells, which express the Na⁺K⁺-ATPase ion pump as a canonical plasma membrane marker, plant cells lack a typical Na+K+-ATPase and instead predominantly utilize H⁺-ATPase for proton gradient maintenance. Thus, H⁺-ATPase has been widely adopted as a standardized internal reference in studies of plant cell membranes40,41. The relative abundance of LORE to H⁺-ATPase was 0.73 (Supplementary Fig. 9; Supplementary Note 3; Supplementary Method 12). In parallel, TLR4 and CD14 exhibited ratios of 0.59 and 0.30 relative to Na+K+-ATPase, respectively. Although cross-system comparisons are not strictly valid, these values provided an estimate of LORE abundance on At cell membrane.

Cell membrane-derived nanovesicles retain the native components and functions of parental cell membranes while enhancing stability and bioavailability through nanoscale structural optimization42. At cell membrane nanovesicles (Atv) were prepared by sequential extrusion of extracted cell membranes through polycarbonate filters using a liposome extruder. Atv exhibited the classic Tyndall effect, with a hydrodynamic diameter of (62.78 ± 7.10) nm and a surface charge of (−16.00 ± 0.66) mV (Supplementary Fig. 10). Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) images confirmed their spherical morphology (Fig. 1L), with particle sizes consistent with dynamic light scattering (DLS) measurements. Western blot (WB) analysis demonstrated the retention of LORE in Atv after extrusion. Furthermore, Atv showed concentration-dependent and time-dependent LPS adsorption, indicating that LORE retains its biological activity in nanovesicle form (Fig. 1M, N; Supplementary Method 13). We further explored the impact of Atv on LPS-induced cellular inflammation. As illustrated in Supplementary Fig. 11 and Supplementary Method 14, LORE-overexpressing Atv more effectively counteracted LPS-induced cell death and inflammatory response compared with native Atv. The therapeutic efficacy of nanovesicles was diminished following treatment with an anti-LORE antibody. Collectively, these findings underscore the pivotal role of LORE in Atv-mediated specific adsorption of LPS.

Degradation of LPS by nanozymes

In recent years, nanozymes have emerged as promising alternatives to natural enzymes in biomedicine, owing to their improved stability, cost-effectiveness, and tunable catalytic performance43–47. A series of cerium-based nanozymes exhibiting phosphatase-like activity has been proven to effectively inactivate endotoxins31,33,48,49. The mechanism arises from the synergistic interaction between Ce3+ and Ce4+ sites. Ce4+ ions preferentially coordinate to the polar phosphoryl oxygen bond (O-P=O), whereas Ce3+ ions enable the selective adsorption of H2O molecules. Ce3+ ions facilitate heterolytic cleavage of H2O into hydroxide ions via Lewis acid-base cooperation, and the resulting hydroxide ions nucleophilically attack the phosphorus center, cleaving the O-P bond33,50. Eight phosphatase-mimicking nanozymes were synthesized based on previous studies31,33,48,49. Among them, CeOx-2 was selected owing to its promising LPS degradation efficiency, hemocompatibility, and low cytotoxicity (Supplementary Fig. 12 and 13; Supplementary Methods 15–17). Structural analysis revealed that CeOx-2 comprised ultrasmall nanoparticles of approximately 5 nm (Fig. 2A), consistent with the hydrodynamic diameter measured by DLS (Supplementary Fig. 14). Energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) identified Ce and O as the primary elements (Supplementary Fig. 15A, B), with inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS) quantifying Ce at (56.73 ± 2.71) wt% (Supplementary Table 18). The thermogravimetric curve showed two distinct mass loss stages: a 4.23% decrease at 30–150 °C (adsorbed water) and a 4.08% decrease at 150–560 °C (pyrolysis of surface-bound ligands, Supplementary Fig. 15C). X-ray diffraction (XRD) revealed narrow, intense peaks at 2θ values of 28.22°, 32.82°, 47.16°, 56.00°, 58.94°, 69.34°, 76.56°, and 79.18°, corresponding to the (111), (200), (220), (311), (222), (400), (311), and (420) crystal planes of cubic CeO2 (JCPDS #34-0394) (Fig. 2C). High-resolution TEM (HR-TEM) confirmed a (111) interplanar spacing of 0.305 nm (Fig. 2B). X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) analysis indicated the presence of residual organic ligands (C 1s at 284.8 eV and N 1s at 399.5 eV, Supplementary Fig. 16). Deconvolution of the Ce 3d and O 1s spectra revealed a Ce3+/(Ce3++Ce4+) ratio of 27.9% and an oxygen vacancy (Ov) concentration of 37.9% (Fig. 2D, E)51.

Moreover, CeOx-2 exhibited concentration- and time-dependent LPS dephosphorylation, achieving 73.5% of LPS degradation at 50 μg Ce/mL within 30 min and near-complete removal after 12 h (Fig. 2F, Supplementary Fig. 17A; Supplementary Method 18). To evaluate the broad-spectrum LPS degradation capacity of CeOx-2, LPS from E. coli, P. aeruginosa, and S. typhimurium were also tested. CeOx-2 demonstrated comparable degradation efficiency across these LPS sources (Supplementary Fig. 17B). The particle size, morphology, and crystal structure of CeOx-2 did not change significantly after LPS degradation (Supplementary Fig. 18A–C). The EDS profile of post-reaction CeOx-2 showed co-localization of P signal and Ce signals, indicating LPS binding to and hydrolysis by CeOx-2 (Supplementary Fig. 18D, E).

To elucidate the precise catalytic mechanism of CeOx-2, monophosphoryl lipid A (MPLA) was employed as a model substrate, and its structure transformations before and after CeOx-2 catalysis were analyzed using a high-resolution liquid chromatography quadrupole time-of-flight mass spectrometer with electrospray ionization (ESI-QTOF, Supplementary Method 19). As depicted in Fig. 2G, H, intact MPLA exhibited a domain m/z peak at 1745.2822, which disappeared following CeOx-2 treatment. Further analysis of the degradation products revealed two prominent dephosphorylated intermediates at m/z at 834.2375 and 560.3859 (Fig. 2I), neither of which was detectable in the untreated MPLA spectrum. The other peaks in part A of MPLA (the light yellow box) not only confirmed dephosphorylation but also suggested that CeOx-2 catalyzed the cleavage of glycosidic oxygen bonds. Matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization time-of-flight mass spectrometry (MALDI-TOF) was also employed to assess structural alterations in LPS before and after CeOx-2 treatment (Supplementary Fig. 19; Supplementary Note 4; Supplementary Method 19). The lipid A moiety, initially represented by m/z peaks in the range 1175-1285, shifted towards lower m/z values of 1090–1175 after incubation with CeOx-252. The mass difference between these peaks closely matched the molecular weight of one to two phosphate groups, confirming the phosphatase-like activity of CeOx-2. CeOx-2 preferentially degrades phosphomonoesters rather than phosphodiesters such as distearoylphosphatidylcholine (DSPC) (Supplementary Fig. 20). This substrate specificity underscores the biocompatibility of CeOx-2, as it avoids degradation of membrane phospholipids, thereby minimizing off-target effects in biological systems.

In addition to its LPS degradation capacity, the redox properties of CeOx-2 were investigated. Under the experimental conditions, CeOx-2 exhibited negligible oxidase (OXD)-like and superoxide dismutase (SOD)-like activities (Supplementary Fig. 21C–E). In contrast, it demonstrated peroxidase (POD)-like and catalase (CAT)-like activities, particularly at concentrations over 100 μg/mL (Supplementary Fig. 21A, B, F; Supplementary Methods 20–24). Among the eight nanozymes investigated, CeOx-2 exhibited high phosphatase-like activity while demonstrating comparatively low CAT and POD activities (Supplementary Fig. 12D, E). To gain deeper insights into its enzymatic characteristics, we further evaluated the Michaelis-Menten kinetic parameters for the POD-like, CAT-like, and phosphatase-like activities of CeOx-2. For phosphatase-like activity, the Michaelis constant (Km) was 3.1646 mM, with a maximal reaction velocity (Vmax) of 0.3953 mM/min. POD-like activity exhibited a Km of 2.160 mM and a Vmax of 0.025 mM/min. CAT-like activity displayed a Km of 1.077 mM and a Vmax of 0.696 mM/min (Supplementary Fig. 22). Tan et al. have elucidated the structure-activity relationship of Ce-based nanozymes, encompassing crystal facets and metal valence states49. Their findings revealed that Ce-based nanozymes with a predominant (111) crystal facet possess lower surface electron density, conferring enhanced Lewis acidity and facilitating specific binding to O–P=O bonds. Furthermore, Ce4+, being more acidic than Ce3+, endows nanozymes with comparable phosphatase-mimicking activity when present in higher proportions. In contrast, Ce-based nanozymes dominated by (100) and (110) crystal facets and enriched in Ce3+ display heightened CAT-like activity. In this study, the optimally selected CeOx-2 predominantly features the (111) crystal facet, with Ce4+ accounting for 72.1% of total Ce content (Fig. 2B–D). This composition confers phosphatase-mimicking activity on CeOx-2, which is critical for neutralizing LPS-induced inflammation.

Construction of Atv@Ce with dual LPS adsorption and degradation

Subsequently, CeOx-2 was combined with Atv to generate a composite (Atv@Ce) capable of specific LPS adsorption and degradation. The weakly positive CeOx-2 (zeta potential of (3.31 ± 0.17) mV) can interact with the negatively charged Atv via electrostatic interactions or coordinate with surface amino groups (-NH2). However, these interactions were insufficient under physiological conditions (Supplementary Fig. 23; Supplementary Note 5). Given the higher coordination stability of carboxyl groups (-COOH) with Ce ions compared to -NH253, N-hydroxysuccinimide (NHS)- polyethylene glycol (PEG)-COOH was used to partially convert -NH2 on Atv to -COOH. The modification density was controlled by adjusting the Atv: NHS-PEG-COOH feed ratio. A ratio ≤1:1 preserved the LPS-binding capacity of Atv, with 1:0.5 selected (Supplementary Fig. 24). The ratio of Atv-COOH to CeOx-2 was then varied. As the CeOx-2 dosage increased progressively, the composite gradually formed visible precipitates in both water and complex culture media, indicating enhanced structural stability (Supplementary Fig. 25A, B; Supplementary Note 6). No obvious aggregates were observed in TEM images of Atv@Ce (1:0.2), whereas DLS confirmed a single peak (Supplementary Fig. 25C, F, G). This ratio was selected for subsequent studies, yielding a composite with (12.55 ± 0.51) wt% Ce content (Supplementary Fig. 25D, E).

The hydrodynamic diameter of Atv@Ce was (96.66 ± 4.58) nm and zeta potential was (−9.60 ± 0.12) mV, as measured by DLS (Fig. 2J, K). TEM and EDS mapping confirmed the surface localization of CeOx-2 on Atv (Fig. 2L–N), and LORE protein was still present in Atv@Ce (Fig. 1K). In addition, Atv@Ce were verified to be stable in saline, PBS, and cell culture medium over 24 h (Supplementary Fig. 26; Supplementary Method 25). The LPS adsorption capacity of Atv@Ce was 1.6-fold higher than that of Atv, reflecting not only a simple effect of adsorption but also LPS degradation (Fig. 2O). The overall LPS degradation capacity of Atv@Ce remained comparatively stable, whereas CeOx-2 alone showed reduced activity in serum-containing RPMI 1640 owing to protein corona formation (Fig. 2P).

Cell protection by Atv@Ce

The safe dosage of Atv@Ce and its components was determined within the range of 0-100 μg Ce ion /mL (0.7 mg protein/mL) using CCK-8 assay, Calcein staining, and hemolysis tests (Supplementary Figs. 13A, E, 27 and 28; Supplementary Methods 16 and 17). Subsequently, LPS was pre-treated with varying concentrations of Atv@Ce within the safe range and added to THP-1-derived macrophages (Fig. 3A; Supplementary Method 26). Cells viability was reduced to 62.85% upon exposure to untreated LPS, whereas pre-treatment with 25 μg Ce/mL Atv@Ce maintained viability at ≥90% (Fig. 3B). Additionally, LPS was pre-treated with saline, CeOx-2, Atv, a physical mixture of Atv and CeOx-2 (Atv+Ce), or Atv@Ce. Cell viability upon co-incubation with these pre-treated LPS samples was 62.47%, 72.35%, 76.85%, 87.02%, and 90.75%, respectively (Fig. 3C). These results directly substantiate the detoxifying effect of Atv@Ce on LPS, with the dual-component formulation providing promising cell protection compared with single-component counterparts. We further examined the level of cellular inflammation. CellRox Green, a sensitive reactive oxygen species (ROS) probe, shows minimal fluorescence in its reduced form and emits intense photostable green fluorescence upon oxidation by ROS54. Intracellular ROS levels in both Atv+Ce and Atv@Ce groups closely approximated those observed in healthy cells using flow cytometry (Fig. 3D, Supplementary Fig. 29A). The intracellular ROS fluorescence imaging was consistent with flow cytometry results (Supplementary Fig. 29B). To assess the inflammatory status at both the transcriptional and translational levels, quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) and enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) were used to measure changes in tumor necrosis factor (TNF)-α and interleukin (IL)-6 levels55, which were concordant with the observed ROS levels (Supplementary Fig. 29C–F).

Subsequently, LPS was introduced to cells concurrently with the nanomaterials (Fig. 3E). At 50 μg Ce/mL, Atv@Ce effectively mitigated LPS-induced cell death and exhibited better LPS detoxification compared with both CeOx-2 and Atv alone (Fig. 3F, G). A reduction in intracellular ROS levels was also observed, as confirmed by both quantitative flow cytometry data and qualitative ROS fluorescence imaging (Fig. 3H, I; Supplementary Fig. 30A, E). Furthermore, TNF-α and IL-6 expression was decreased at both the mRNA and protein levels (Fig. 3N, O; Supplementary Fig. 30B, C). WB analysis was further performed to examine the TLR4/MyD88/NF-κB signaling pathway, which is most closely associated with LPS-mediated inflammatory responses. Following Atv@Ce treatment, a reduction in NF-κB phosphorylation, along with decreased TLR4 and MyD88 expression, was observed (Fig. 3J–M; Supplementary Table 19). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that Atv@Ce effectively sequesters and neutralizes LPS from the extracellular milieu.

SulfoCy3-NHS was covalently conjugated to Atv@Ce, yielding a fluorescently labeled nanoparticle with stable photophysical properties for cellular uptake tests (Supplementary Fig. 31; Supplementary Methods 27, 28). SulfoCy3-Atv@Ce is internalized via clathrin-mediated endocytosis and caveolae-mediated endocytosis, as evidenced by the inhibitory effects of cytochalasin D, nystatin, and chlorpromazine (Fig. 3P; Supplementary Fig. 30D, F). When administered to LPS-pre-stimulated cells, Atv@Ce accumulated intracellularly and facilitated reduction of intracellular LPS levels (Fig. 3Q, R; Supplementary Method 29). These observations indicate that Atv@Ce intercepts LPS at both the extracellular and cytoplasmic levels, thereby comprehensively mitigating LPS-induced inflammatory responses.

Therapeutic effect of Atv@Ce on an IBD model

The intestinal microbiota serves as the primary reservoir of LPS56. In healthy individuals, the structural and functional integrity of the intestinal barrier maintains LPS levels within a physiological range. Upon disturbance of the intestinal flora, overgrowth of Gram-negative bacteria with LPS-rich outer membranes leads to elevated local gut LPS concentrations, triggering inflammatory cascades and contributing to the pathogenesis of IBD57. Accordingly, the therapeutic potential of Atv@Ce was investigated in a dextran sodium sulfate (DSS)-induced ulcerative colitis (UC) model. Prior to in vivo studies, the stability of Atv@Ce in simulated gastric fluid (SGF), simulated intestinal fluid (SIF), and simulated colonic fluid (SCF) was evaluated, demonstrating its structural stability in the latter two cases (Supplementary Fig. 32; Supplementary Method 25). In vivo imaging revealed that intragastrically administered SulfoCy5.5-Atv@Ce was still detectable in the colon after 24 h. No discernible fluorescence was observed in major organs (heart, liver, spleen, lung, kidney) (Supplementary Fig. 33; Supplementary Method 30).

To optimize therapeutic efficacy, the optimal dosage for UC treatment was first determined (Supplementary Method 31). Atv@Ce exhibited a dose-dependent therapeutic effect in UC mice (Supplementary Fig. 34). Both 0.5 and 1 mg Ce/kg dosages similarly attenuated weight loss, reduced disease activity index (DAI) scores, and restored colon length. Histological analysis via H&E staining further confirmed partial recovery of colonic structural integrity at these dosages (Supplementary Fig. 35A; Supplementary Method 32). Consistent with these findings, pro-inflammatory cytokines TNF-α and IL-6 were suppressed in intestinal tissues (Supplementary Fig. 35B). Thus, 0.5 mg Ce/kg Atv@Ce was selected for the subsequent experiments.

Subsequently, we systematically compared the therapeutic efficacy of Atv@Ce with its individual components, following the experimental protocol outlined in Fig. 4A. After oral gavage, Atv@Ce attenuated weight loss and DAI scores in UC mice (Fig. 4B, C). Colon lengths in UC mice treated with saline, CeOx-2, Atv, Atv+Ce, and Atv@Ce were 4.34, 4.92, 5.26, 5.68, and 6.32 cm, respectively (Fig. 4D, E). Histopathological examination via H&E staining revealed severe colonic epithelial damage in saline-treated UC mice, characterized by epithelial disruption and focal cell loss (Fig. 4F). Atv@Ce treatment preserved colonic architectural integrity, as evidenced by relatively intact glandular structures and crypt-villus organization. Immunohistochemical analysis and ELISA quantification of pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-6 and TNF-α) confirmed that Atv@Ce significantly suppressed intestinal inflammation (Fig. 4G–J). Major organs were collected for H&E staining, revealing that Atv@Ce ameliorated splenic inflammation associated with enteritis without inducing detectable toxicity in other organs (Supplementary Fig. 36). These results demonstrate the efficacy of Atv@Ce for colitis therapy, attributing to the synergistic effects of Atv and CeOx-2 within the composite.

Distribution, metabolism, and biosafety of Atv@Ce in vivo via i.v. injection

i.v. administration provides rapid and direct drug delivery to the bloodstream, making it particularly suitable for the treatment of acute diseases such as sepsis. The temporal biodistribution of SulfoCy5.5-Atv@Ce following i.v. administration was first investigated. At 4 h post-administration, no fluorescence was observed in the spleens. Detectable fluorescence emerged at 12 h, followed by clearance by 24 h. Over the initial 24 h, SulfoCy5.5-Atv@Ce predominantly accumulated in the livers, lungs, and kidneys. By 48 h, fluorescence persisted exclusively in the liver, indicating prolonged hepatic retention, with near-complete systemic clearance achieved by 72 h (Fig. 5A–C; Supplementary Fig. 37). Blood samples were collected, and a semi-logarithmic plot of ln(fluorescence intensity) versus time was generated. From 0 to 10 h, drug concentration remained relatively high with a decreasing trend, reflecting the rapid distribution of SulfoCy5.5-Atv@Ce in the body. After 10 h, drug concentration continued to decrease at a slower rate, indicating the onset of drug clearance. During the elimination phase, the change in drug concentration over time demonstrated a linear relationship, consistent with elimination kinetics. The elimination rate constant (k) was calculated as 0.02615/h, with a half-life (t1/2) of 26.5 h (Fig. 5F). Feces and urine samples were collected at multiple time points to investigate the metabolic and excretory pathways of SulfoCy5.5-Atv@Ce. A modest fluorescence signal was detectable in feces (Fig. 5D, E). Fluorescence intensity in urine was two orders of magnitude higher than that in feces (Fig. 5G, H). Healthy mice were i.v. injection with unlabeled Atv@Ce. Metabolites in urine and feces over 24 h were analyzed using LC-MS (Supplementary Method 33). The metabolomic profile of mice administered Atv@Ce showed compositional changes compared to healthy controls, resulting in upregulation of 647 metabolites in urine and 91 metabolites in feces (Fig. 5I, J; Supplementary Fig. 38A, B). According to the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes‌ (KEGG) Phytochemical Compounds and Lipids database, upregulated metabolites included diverse plant-derived compounds: alkaloids (e.g., baptifoline, sinomenine), hydroxycinnamic acids and derivatives (e.g., caffeic acid, sinapic acid), terpenoids and derivatives (e.g., perillyl alcohol, cnicin), and glycosides (e.g., tuliposide B) (Fig. 5K, L; Supplementary Fig. 38C, D). Additionally, other upregulated metabolites such as fatty acids and conjugates, carbohydrates and conjugates, amino acids/peptides and analogs, were difficult to distinguish as plant- or animal-derived. Consistent with the observed renal and hepatic accumulation of SulfoCy5.5-Atv@Ce, these results indicated that liver metabolism and urinary excretion were the predominant routes for SulfoCy5.5-Atv@Ce elimination.

Following administration of two doses to mice, no significant alterations were observed in the blood biochemical indicators of liver and kidney function (AST, ALT, BUN, CRE), or in hematological parameters, including red blood cells (RBC, HGB, RDW-SD, MCV) and platelets (PLT) (Supplementary Fig. 39A–E,L–Q; Supplementary Method 34). Although a slight increase in the total white blood cell (WBC) count was noted, the proportions of other leukocyte populations (Neu, Lym, Mon, Eos, Bas) and levels of inflammatory proteins (C3a, C5a, TNF-α, IL-6) were unaffected (Supplementary Fig. 39F–K, R–U). Histological analysis of major organs via H&E staining confirmed the absence of treatment-related tissue damage (Supplementary Fig. 39V). Therefore, the Atv@Ce treatment was well-tolerated.

To assess the long-term safety profile, mice were administered Atv@Ce in a cyclic dosing regimen (5 days on, 9 days off) for a total of 42 days (Supplementary Fig. 40A; Supplementary Method 34). Body weights of treated mice did not significantly differ from those of healthy controls (Supplementary Fig. 40B). Serum analysis revealed elevated total IgG and IgM levels after each dosing cycle. During the first cycle, no Atv@Ce-specific antibody was detected. Although levels of C5a and pro-inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α, IL-6) remained unchanged, a transient increase in the complement activation fragment C3a was observed. This suggests that the initial rise in total immunoglobulins was driven by complement activation, rather than a classical inflammatory response (Supplementary Fig. 40C–J). By the second cycle, serum analysis confirmed the emergence of Atv@Ce-specific IgG antibody, while IgM levels declined. This shift suggests that repeated exposure induced a memory B cell-driven secondary immune response, characterized by robust IgG production. The concurrent suppression of IgM synthesis likely resulted from accelerated immunoglobulin class switching or the establishment of peripheral immune tolerance. H&E staining of major organs confirmed that Atv@Ce treatment did not cause tissue damage (Supplementary Fig. 40K).

Therapeutic effect of Atv@Ce on sepsis models

When the gut barrier is compromised, increased epithelial/endothelial permeability enables translocation of gut-derived LPS into the systemic circulation, triggering sepsis26,58. Two sepsis models were employed: a simplified LPS-induced sepsis model via intraperitoneal (i.p.) injection of LPS to assess rapid inflammatory responses, and a complex bacteria-derived sepsis model induced by cecal ligation and puncture (CLP) to mimic clinical sepsis with multi-organ involvement and heterogeneous bacterial etiology59,60.

We investigated the dosing regimen of Atv@Ce in the LPS-induced sepsis model (Supplementary Fig. 41A; Supplementary Method 35). Mice treated with 0, 0.1, 0.25, and 0.5 mg Ce/kg Atv@Ce exhibited rectal temperature drops of 2.83, 2.05, 1.28, and 1.08 oC, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 41C). Untreated septic mice showed rapid weight loss, whereas Atv@Ce effectively mitigated this reduction (Supplementary Fig. 41D). In addition, a significant decrease in serum LPS levels was detected following treatment with 0.25 mg Ce/kg of Atv@Ce (Supplementary Fig. 41B). This reduction was accompanied by a mitigation in elevated neutrophil levels and alleviation of organ edema and cell apoptosis (Supplementary Fig. 41E–H). Thus, 0.25 mg Ce/kg Atv@Ce was chosen for subsequent experiments.

Whereafter, the efficacy of Atv@Ce and its components against sepsis was evaluated as depicted in Fig. 6A. The Atv, Atv+Ce and Atv@Ce groups all showed LPS removal capacity, indicating rapid LPS adsorption by Atv and strong resistance to interference in complex environments (Fig. 6B). Timely Atv@Ce treatment effectively alleviated severe declines in rectal temperature and body weight in septic mice, with rectal temperature drops of 2.80 (saline), 2.02 (CeOx-2), 1.86 (Atv), 1.82 (Atv+Ce), and 1.12 oC (Atv@Ce), and body weight losses of 5.03 (saline), 4.30 (CeOx-2), 3.12 (Atv), 2.76 (Atv+Ce), and 1.83 g (Atv@Ce) on day 2 post-treatment, respectively (Fig. 6F, G). Upon systemic entry, LPS is mainly recognized and internalized by immune cells. The spleen, a key immune organ, houses abundant immune cells and mediates immune response initiation and regulation61. Excessive immune cell activation triggers the release of inflammatory mediators, leading to splenic tissue damage. Among the main organs examined, the spleen exhibited the most pronounced pathological changes (Fig. 6C; Supplementary Fig. 42A). Under physiological circumstances, the red pulp (RP) and white pulp (WP) of the spleen are structurally and functionally distinct: RP is primarily responsible for blood filtration and senescent cell clearance, whereas WP is the site of immune cell (e.g., B and T cells) residency and immune response initiation62. In saline-treated septic mice, RP and WP became intermingled, and TUNEL staining confirmed cellular injury (Fig. 6H–J). Splenic pathology was closely associated with systemic inflammation, as evidenced by elevated serum levels of neutrophils, basophils, and pro-inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α, IL-6). Atv@Ce treatment significantly attenuated splenic edema, reduced spleen cell apoptosis, and lowered systemic inflammatory cell counts and cytokine levels (Fig. 6D, E, H–J, N–Q).

Additionally, H&E staining of other tissues, along with serum alanine aminotransferase (ALT) and aspartate aminotransferase (AST) levels, confirmed that LPS-induced septic mice exhibited systemic organ damage (Fig. 6L, M; Supplementary Fig. 42B). A detailed histological assessment of renal tissue was also performed. Following LPS treatment, kidneys exhibited elevated cell apoptosis, mild interstitial fibrosis, and upregulated inflammatory factors (Fig. 6K; Supplementary Fig. 43). Atv@Ce treatment attenuated these pathological abnormalities, encompassing renal cell apoptosis and local inflammation. The LPS-related inflammatory pathways, specifically TLR4 and MyD88, were further investigated. Sepsis mice treated with saline showed an increase in the expression of both TLR4 and MyD88 compared to the healthy control group. The Atv@Ce group exhibited the lowest expression levels of TLR4 and MyD88 among all treated groups (Supplementary Fig. 44). To sum up, Atv@Ce demonstrated optimal inhibition of the LPS-induced inflammatory response. This efficacy can be attributed to the specific adsorption of LPS by Atv and its subsequent degradation by CeOx-2. The co-localization of Atv@Ce facilitated LPS detoxification on the Atv surface, thereby reducing the further pro-inflammatory effect of LPS-adsorbing Atv.

In clinical practice, sepsis is predominantly caused by bacterial infections, and its pathogenesis is highly complex, involving numerous immunogenic components such as LPS, pore-forming toxins, and bacterial metabolites63–65. Furthermore, continuous bacterial proliferation within the host triggers an uncontrolled inflammatory cascade. This dynamic process cannot be accurately replicated by the LPS-induced sepsis model. The bacteria-related sepsis model induced by CLP is currently the most widely used model that closely mimics the mechanisms of clinical sepsis. Given that Atv@Ce lacks antibacterial activity, even at a relatively high concentration of 1 mg Ce/mL, but is capable of degrading bacterial LPS (Supplementary Fig. 45; Supplementary Method 36), we combine it with antibiotics to effectively combat bacteria-related sepsis.

12 h post-CLP surgery, mice exhibited a mean weight loss of 1.5 g and a mean body temperature drop of 2 °C, whereas sham-operated controls showed minimal changes (0.44 g weight loss, 1.1 °C temperature decline) (Supplementary Fig. 46A–C; Supplementary Method 37). Sepsis mice were subsequently treated with saline, Atv@Ce, ceftriaxone (Cef), and Cef + Atv@Ce. After 24 h, bacterial loads in the blood, peritoneal fluid, and major organs (heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney) were quantified. Bacteria were predominantly localized to the liver and spleen, where they induced severe parenchymal inflammation, cellular degeneration, necrosis, and architectural disruption (Supplementary Fig. 46D–J, 47). Atv@Ce exhibited negligible antibacterial activity, consistent with in vitro results (Supplementary Fig. 45). Nevertheless, both Cef and Cef + Atv@Ce reduced bacterial burdens by 1–2 orders of magnitude, with no discernible difference in efficacy between the two groups. Physiologically, the Cef + Atv@Ce group demonstrated improved recovery, with weight loss and temperature drops of 1.66 g and 1.46 °C, respectively, compared to 3.38 g and 2.92 °C in the saline group, 2.86 g and 2.52 °C in the Atv@Ce group, and 2.24 g and 2.14 °C in the Cef group (Supplementary Fig. 46A–C). This suggests that while Cef inhibits bacteria proliferation, Atv@Ce adsorbs and degrades LPS from bacteria. This synergy effectively lowered serum LPS levels and downregulated TLR4/MyD88 expression (Supplementary Figs. 47, 48A). Infection markers, such as C-reactive protein (CRP) and procalcitonin (PCT), pro-inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α, IL-6), neutrophil levels, and organ function indicators (AST, ALT) were significantly ameliorated under the combination therapy (Supplementary Fig. 48B–H). Although the Cef+Atv@Ce group showed an 80% survival rate by day 4 (vs. 0% for saline, 30% for Atv@Ce, and 40% for Cef), this protection was not sustained, with survival declining to 30% by day 6, while all other groups reached 0% survival (Supplementary Fig. 46K).

Discussion

The clinical management of sepsis necessitates both infection control and the preservation of vital organ function. Early reduction of pathogenic PAMPs is critical for recovery from septic shock. Consequently, extracorporeal blood purification has garnered significant attention. For instance, the Efferon LPS hemoperfusion device, utilizing multimodal polymeric adsorbents, has received approval for clinical use in Russia66. Similarly, Toraymyxin PMX-20R, a hemoperfusion device featuring polymyxin B immobilized on polystyrene fibers, is approved in Japan67. Compared to extracorporeal dialysis columns that rely on specialized equipment, the direct intravenous administration of nanoscale adsorbents offers greater operational convenience and potentially faster onset of action. Currently, no intravenous preparation for LPS removal is available in clinical practice. This gap may be attributed to stringent requirements regarding the specificity and biosafety of such formulations. Furthermore, adsorbents saturated with LPS that remain in circulation may fail to completely neutralize pro-inflammatory effects.

The diverse responses of plants to LPS from various sources demonstrate the presence of membrane proteins capable of binding LPS, although the structures of these LPS-related proteins are still under active exploration. In cruciferous species such as Arabidopsis thaliana, it was initially discovered that LPS adheres to the protoplast or cell membrane23–25. Subsequent investigations explored the immunological consequences of LPS stimulation, and by 2015, this research culminated in the discovery of LORE as the LPS receptor in cruciferous plants28. It was mentioned that only LPS derived from P. aeruginosa elevates intracellular Ca2+ levels and promotes subsequent LORE phosphorylation, which are classic manifestations of plant immune activation. Later, it was clarified that short 3-hydroxy fatty acids present in less purified LPS, rather than LPS itself, serve as the primary activators of plant immunity35. Plant immune activation requires precisely regulated signal transduction involving five molecular processes: receptor-ligand interaction, receptor activation, signal amplification, cellular response, and signal termination. LPS that fails to trigger plant immune responses is not necessarily incapable of binding to its receptors via non-covalent forces, including hydrogen bonding, hydrophobic interactions, electrostatic forces, and van der Waals forces. Given that the hydroxy fatty acid structure is relatively conserved in most LPS, we daringly hypothesize that LPS from multiple bacterial species can bind to LORE.

In this study, the ITC test confirmed that the binding of eLORE to LPS is spontaneous (ΔG < 0) (Fig. 1H, I). SPR was employed to characterize the binding capacity of eLORE to diverse LPS types, benchmarked against animal immune cell receptors (CD14, TLR4/MD2 complex, LBP). Under our experimental conditions, eLORE exhibited better binding affinity to LPS compared to CD14 and TLR4/MD2 complex, though inferior to LBP (Fig. 1D–G; Supplementary Fig. 8; Supplementary Table 5, 14–17). Direct quantification of LPS binding to CD14, TLR4/MD2 via SPR experiment indeed underestimates their binding affinity, as it does not fully account for the catalytic role of LBP in facilitating LPS transfer37,38. Given the complex composition of the plant membrane, we could not rule out the involvement of other proteins or the lipid bilayer in LPS interaction. To validate the specific role of LORE, cell membranes from LORE-overexpressing plants exhibited significantly higher LPS-binding capacity than wild-type counterparts (Fig. 1B, C). Pre-treatment with anti-LORE antibody resulted in LPS adsorption by 45.77%. Furthermore, strong colocalization between LPS and LORE was observed in LORE-mCherry-At leaves (Supplementary Fig. 5E). Antibody-mediated neutralization of LORE did not fully abolish LPS binding, suggesting the existence of additional, synergistic adsorption pathways in plant membranes. Experimental evidence confirms that the lipid A moiety of LPS integrates into lipid bilayers68,69, implying a dual mechanism of protein-mediated binding and lipid-driven insertion that collectively contributes to LPS adsorption by Atv. Beyond At, diverse cruciferous species, including broccoli, white radish, woad, and cabbage, exhibited variable LPS adsorption capacities, ranging from high to moderate (Fig. 1A). Non-cruciferous plants, such as spinach, also demonstrated robust LPS-binding ability (Supplementary Fig. 1D), indicating that LPS binding is a conserved trait across multiple plant lineages rather than being exclusive to Brassicaceae family. Consequently, the observation that At cell membranes bind LPS from diverse bacterial species complements, rather than conflicts with, prior literature focusing on LORE-mediated immune activation by specific LPS derivatives. By simplifying complex immune recognition to specific LPS binding, we leveraged the structural conservation of LPS core regions to develop a plant membrane-based system for broad-spectrum LPS adsorption. These results provide a complementary perspective to the established LORE-LPS interaction model, highlighting a role for LORE in direct LPS binding.

To conduct the in vivo application, plant cell membranes nanovesicles (Atv) were generated. These nanovesicles comprise natural lipid constituents that preserve the structural integrity and biological activity of LORE (Fig. 1K, M, N). To mitigate inflammation triggered by LPS-laden adsorbents, Ce-based nanozymes were integrated with Atv to structurally degrade LPS. QTOF analysis revealed that CeOx-2 cleaves both phosphate groups and glycosidic bonds in lipid A (Fig. 2G–I). To date, nanozymes with glycosidase activity have only been reported at Zn-containing metal active site70. CeOx-2 not only manifested phosphatase-like activity but also exhibited POD-like and CAT-like activity (Supplementary Fig. 21). Comparative analysis of the eight synthesized nanozymes (Supplementary Fig. 12C–E) and kinetic evaluation of their multi-enzyme activities (Supplementary Fig. 22) confirmed that the phosphatase-like activity of CeOx-2 was the dominant contributor. In the detoxification mechanism of Atv@Ce for LPS, the alkyl chain region of lipid A is shielded by LORE, while the exposed phosphate groups and glycosidic bonds are cleaved by CeOx-2. The combination of LORE-mediated absorption and CeOx-2-driven degradation synergistically enhances LPS detoxification.

To sum up, inspired by the activation of LPS on plant immunity, we discovered that cruciferous plant-derived LORE exhibits high binding affinity for LPS, highlighting the potential of plant membrane components in designing targeted LPS antidotes. A hybrid LPS antidote (Atv@Ce) was engineered by integrating LORE-expression cell membrane nanovesicles with Ce-based nanozymes, enabling dual-mode LPS inactivation through specific adsorption and catalytic degradation. In IBD and sepsis models, Atv@Ce demonstrated promising efficacy in resolving inflammation and attenuating tissue pathology compared to single-component systems or their physical mixtures. This work establishes a platform for next-generation nanotherapeutics with high specificity and multifunctional LPS-neutralizing capabilities for LPS-driven inflammatory disorders.

Methods

Chemicals, materials, instruments, and software

Chemicals, materials, instruments, and software are described in Supplementary Method 38–40.

Cell membrane nanovesicle preparation

Plants were washed and cut into 1 mm strips using a fresh razor blade, then submerged in enzyme solution (20 mM pH 5.7 2-(N-morpholino)ethanesulphonic acid, 1.5% cellulase R10, 0.4% macerozyme R10, 0.4 M mannitol, 20 mM KCl, 10 mM CaCl2, 5 mM β-mercaptoethanol, and 0.1% bovine serum albumin, filtered with a 0.45-μm filter)71. Strips were vacuum-infiltrated for 30 min in the dark, followed by digestion at 37 °C for 5 h under normal atmospheric pressure without shaking. Protoplasts were obtained by gently swirling, filtered through a 70-μm mesh, and centrifuged (500 × g, 5 min). Protoplasts were re-dispersed in water containing plant protease inhibitor cocktail and 2.5 mM EDTA·2Na·2H2O, and disrupted by ultrasonication (20% amplitude, 5 s on, 5 s off, ice bath, 5 min). The lysate was centrifuged (4000 × g, 5 min) to remove the organelles, and the supernatant was further centrifuged (25,000 × g, 20 min) to pellet cell membranes20,72,73. Membranes were quantified using a BCA protein assay kit. THP-1-derived macrophages were processed identically using an animal protease inhibitor cocktail instead of the plant-specific cocktail. Cell membrane solutions were sequentially extruded through polycarbonate track-etch membranes (1, 0.4, 0.2, 0.1, and 0.05 μm pore sizes) using a liposome extruder. Nanovesicles were collected by ultracentrifuging (100,000 × g, 1 h) and quantified using a BCA protein assay kit74,75. Morphology was assessed by TEM after negative staining with 1% uranyl acetate. Size and zeta potential were measured by DLS.

LPS adsorption

Equal volumes of 20 μg/mL FITC-LPS (LPS from E. coli O55:B5) and saline, 1 mg protein/mL cell membrane suspensions were mixed, with saline-treated FITC-LPS serving as the control. Mixtures were incubated at 37 °C for 1 h, followed by centrifugation (25,000 × g, 10 min). Supernatant fluorescence was measured using a microplate reader (Ex/Em = 494/525 nm)13,76. The fluorescence values were interpolated from the standard curve of FITC-LPS to determine the residual FITC-LPS concentration. The saline control was set as 100% residual LPS, and LPS adsorption (%) was calculated as: 100% − percentage of residual FITC-LPS relative to control. Detailed conditions for other LPS adsorption assays are detailed in Supplementary Method 13.

Nanozyme preparation and LPS degradation

47.1 mg of Ce(NH4)2(NO3)6 was dissolved in 19 mL of water. Under stirring, 1 mL of 2.5% 1, 2-propanediamine was added dropwise. The solution was transferred to a hydrothermal reactor and heated at 180 °C for 12 h. The product was collected by centrifugation (12,000 × g, 10 min) and washed three times with water. CeOx-2 was obtained33.

2 EU/mL LPS-LAL (E. coli, O55:B5, 2 EU/mL = 1 mg/mL) was mixed with an equal volume of CeOx-2 with different concentrations (0, 6.25, 12.5, 25, 50, 100 Ce μg/mL), followed by incubation at 37 °C for 0.5 h. 0 μg/mL group was set as the control (100%). The remaining active LPS-LAL was quantified using a chromogenic LAL endotoxin assay kit according to the manufacturer’s instruction33. LPS degradation (%) was calculated as: 100% − percentage of residual LPS-LAL relative to control. Detailed conditions for other LPS degradation assays are detailed in Supplementary Method 18.

Preparation of Atv-COOH and CeOx-2 complex (Atv@Ce)

NHS-PEG-COOH was conjugated to Atv at varying mass ratios. After a 4-hour reaction in an ice bath, the mixture was centrifuged to pellet carboxyl-modified Atv (Atv-COOH), which was washed twice with water. Atv-COOH was quantified using a BCA protein assay kit. LPS adsorption was performed to optimize the modification ratio. The Atv:NHS-PEG-COOH ratio of 1:0.5 was chosen for subsequent experiments. Atv-COOH was characterized by DLS for hydrodynamic size and zeta potential.

CeOx-2 was incubated with Atv-COOH at varying mass ratios for 10 min at room temperature. Unbound CeOx-2 was removed by dialysis (MWCO 300 kDa) against saline. Metal content in Atv@Ce was quantified by ICP-MS. Composite morphology and composition were characterized by TEM, HR-TEM with EDS, and DLS. LPS adsorption and LPS degradation were evaluated as described above.

Cell protection assays

LPS (200 μg/mL) and Atv@Ce at varying concentrations, or saline, CeOx-2, Atv, a physical mixture of Atv and CeOx-2 (Atv+Ce), or Atv@Ce (350 μg protein/mL and/or 50 μg Ce/mL) were added simultaneously 96-well plates seeded with macrophages (3.0 × 104 cells/well), followed by incubation for 24 h. Cell viability was assessed by CCK-8 assay. TNF-α and IL-6 levels were quantified by ELISA kits, and corresponding mRNA expression was measured by qPCR. Intracellular ROS was detected using the CellRox Green probe via flow cytometry and fluorescence microscope. Protein-level changes were assessed by WB using the following antibodies: TLR4 rabbit pAb (diluted 1:1000), MYD88 mAb (diluted 1:1000), NF-κB p65 (D14E12) rabbit mAb (diluted 1:1000), phospho-NF-κB p65 (Ser536) (93H1) rabbit mAb (diluted 1:1000), GAPDH mAb [6C5] (diluted 1:1000).

In vivo distribution and metabolism

SPF male BALB/c mice (7–8 weeks, 20–22 g) were used. Mice were shaved and injected via the tail vein with SulfoCy5.5-labeled Atv@Ce (0.25 mg Ce/kg). In vivo fluorescence imaging was performed at 0, 2, 4, 8, 12, 24, 48, and 72 h post-injection. Ex vivo imaging of the heart, liver, spleen, lung, kidney, and feces was conducted at 0, 4, 12, 24, 48, and 72 h. Blood samples were collected in anticoagulant tubes at 0, 2, 4, 6, 8, 10, 12, 24, 48, and 72 h, and fluorescence was measured using a microplate reader (Ex/Em = 675/700 nm). Pharmacokinetic parameters were derived using a non-compartmental analysis model. The elimination rate constant (k) was determined from the slope of the terminal elimination phase (ln(fluorescence intensity) vs. time) and the half-life was calculated as: t1/2 = ln2/k. Urine and feces were collected over sequential intervals (0–4, 4–8, 8–12, 12–24, 24–48, and 48–72 h). Fecal samples were suspended in PBS (100 mg feces/mL), homogenized, centrifuged, and the supernatant fluorescence was measured. For metabolomic analysis, mice were placed in metabolic cages. Atv@Ce (0.25 mg Ce/kg) or saline (control) was administered intravenously. Total urine and feces were collected over 24 h, and metabolite profiles were analyzed by non-targeted LC-MS metabolomics (Majorbio).

DSS-induced UC model and treatment

SPF male C57BL/6 mice (7–8 weeks, 20–22 g) were used. Mice received 3% DSS in drinking water for 5 days, followed by normal water. Healthy controls received normal water throughout77. UC mice were orally administered with saline, CeOx-2, Atv, a physical mixture of Atv and CeOx-2 (Atv+Ce), or Atv@Ce (containing 3.5 mg protein/kg and/or 0.5 mg Ce/kg) once a day for 2 days. Healthy mice were treated with saline. Colons were excised, measured, and rinsed with saline. Tissues were processed for H&E staining and immunohistochemical detection of TNF-α and IL-6. The remaining colon tissue was homogenized for ELISA quantification of TNF-α and IL-6. Additionally, major organs (heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney) were collected for H&E staining.

LPS-induced sepsis model and treatment

SPF male C57BL/6 mice (7–8 weeks, 20–22 g) were used. The LPS-induced sepsis model was constructed by intraperitoneal injection of 20 mg/kg LPS59,60,78. Saline-injected mice served as healthy controls. 1 h post-injection, septic mice received intravenous saline, CeOx-2, Atv, a physical mixture of Atv and CeOx-2 (Atv+Ce), Atv@Ce (containing 1.75 mg protein/kg and/or 0.25 mg Ce/kg) once daily for 2 days. Serum LPS levels were quantified at 6 h post-treatment using a chromogenic LAL endotoxin assay kit. Rectal temperature and body weight were monitored regularly. Blood was collected for blood routine tests, blood biochemistry analysis, and ELISA (TNF-α and IL-6). Major organs were collected for H&E staining. Spleens were subjected to TUNEL staining, TLR4, and MyD88 immunohistochemical staining. Kidneys were subjected to TUNEL staining, Masson trichrome staining, TLR4, MyD88, and IL-6 immunohistochemical staining.
