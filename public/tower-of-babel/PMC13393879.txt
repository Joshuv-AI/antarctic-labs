Genetic tools for transformation of Xyleborus ambrosia beetle fungal symbionts
Authors: Ross A. Joseph, Abolfazl Masoudi, Nemat O. Keyhani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAmbrosia beetles rely on obligate fungal partners for survival, and these associations are emerging as model systems for examining the development and evolution of fungal-animal mutualism. However, genetic tools for the manipulation of such mutualistic fungi remain largely lacking, and consequently the genetic basis employed by these fungi to establish associations with their beetle partners remains almost completely unexplored. Here, we provide methods for protoplast generation and transformation of several major filamentous fungal partners of Xyleborus ambrosia beetles including Raffaela arxii, R. fusca, Harringtonia aguacate, and Graphium ambrosium using reporter constructs driven by the H. lauricola gpd promoter sequence. In addition, we developed inducible expression systems for the beetle symbiont, but plant pathogen, H. lauricola, responsible for laurel wilt disease, and show that these constructs are also functional in G. ambrosium. This work demonstrates the utility of H. lauricola genetic parts in transforming diverse groups of fungi and offers a toolbox for their genetic dissection.ResultsFungal strains showed sensitivity to hygromycin, and protoplasts derived from the fungi were transformed with plasmids expressing the hygromycin selective marker (HPH) along with either green- or red-fluorescent proteins (GFP/RFP), with expression driven by the constitutive H. lauricola gpd promoter. Transformed strains for the fungal species described above showing expression of either GFP or RFP were obtained. A series of inducible plasmids using the H. lauricola alcAp-RFP-(ethanol) and glaAp-RFP-(maltose) inducible promoters were also tested and validated.ConclusionsOur data provide methods and tools for genetic manipulation of a wide range of ambrosia beetle fungal symbionts, including marking cells with fluorescent proteins and use of inducible expression systems. Intriguingly, the method/plasmids developed did not result in transformation of the related Xyleborus fungal symbiont, Neocosmospora affinis. The fluorescent strains developed can be used to monitor symbiotic colonization of the beetle host, fungal development in host galleries, host preferences, and a range of other applications, and promoters may be used for further knockout and expression studies.

Background

Ambrosia beetles are specialized wood-boring weevils in the subfamilies Scolytinae and Platypodinae (Curculionidae) that have formed mutualistic relationships with fungi, adopting fungus-farming lifestyles (ambrosia symbioses) [1–3] Adult beetles burrow into the xylem of trees and other woody plants, forming tunnel systems called galleries where they rear their brood and inoculate gallery walls with their symbionts, which grow as lush fungal gardens and constitute the sole source of nutrition for their hosts [4, 5]. This lifestyle is polyphyletic, with at least a dozen lineages independently adopting it over time, and with each adoption forming with an independent lineage of fungi, including members of the orders Ophiostomatales, Microascales, Hypocreales, Polyporales, and Saccharomycetales [6–9]. One of the defining features of ambrosia symbioses is the host evolution of specialized organs and structures termed mycangia, which house viable propagules of their fungal symbionts while dispersing to new trees [10, 11]. These mycangia, independently involved in each different lineage of ambrosia beetles, vary in their complexity, location on/in the body, and distribution between sexes [12–14]. Interestingly, mycangia appear to be able to selectively promote the uptake and growth of their respective partner fungi and suppress growth by non-partner fungi, and different lineages of ambrosia beetles vary in their degree of host symbiont number and fidelity, with some forming relatively tight one beetle one fungus partnerships while other beetles associate with consortiums of fungi [15–18].

Ambrosia beetles in the genus Xyleborus possess paired pre-oral mycangia, two small pouches located behind their mandibles within the pre-oral cavity, and have been shown to associate primarily with ambrosia fungi in the ophiostomatalean genera Raffaelea, Dryadomyces, and Harringtonia (all previously classified as Raffaelea), as well as other filamentous and yeast-like fungi, although the specific roles of these latter fungi in the ambrosia symbiosis lifestyle remain less well-defined [14, 19, 20]. The somewhat looser fidelity between Xyleborus beetles and their partner fungi has drawn particular interest in recent years as the human-mediated spread of ambrosia beetles and their fungi into non-native ranges has led to the rise of some ambrosia fungi acting as devastating plant pathogens in their invasive ranges [21]. In the early 2000’s the beetle, Xyleborus glabratus and its fungal partner, Harringtonia lauricola, were introduced into the United States, where the fungus has since been shown to cause the disease Laurel wilt of plants in the family Lauraceae [22, 23]. This fungus has been shown to have spread from its original invasive host beetle to now be farmed and vectored by a number of native Xyleborus beetle species, with implications on the management and spread of this disease-causing agent [24, 25].

Due in part to its importance as a plant pathogen, Harringtonia lauricola has emerged as a model fungus for the study of ambrosia fungi interactions with both plant and insect hosts, with well-established laboratory techniques for culturing the fungus, rearing colonies of some of its Xyleborus hosts, and molecular genetic tools for its manipulation [15, 26, 27]. Aside from H. lauricola, genetic tools for any other ambrosia beetle symbiont are lacking, in part due to an absence of genomic resources and established methods for manipulating these fungi. Such tools are critical for better understanding both the interactions between microbe and host, and the consortium dynamics involved during myco-cultivation [8, 9], i.e. on gallery walls, and mycangial colonization, i.e. how the partner fungus is maintained on or within the beetle host [26]. Recently, we demonstrated consistent and defined isolation of a consortium of fungal species from laboratory colonies of the ambrosia beetle, Xyleborus affinis, including two previously described Raffaelea species, R. arxii and R. fusca, and previously undescribed species of Neocosmospora and Graphium, since named N. affinis and G. ambrosium, respectively. Here, we sought to test whether protocols and tools for genetically manipulating Harringtonia lauricola, including Harringtonia lauricola promoter sequences, could be used to successfully transform and drive gene expression in these other fungi. Protoplasts were successfully obtained and transformed with vectors containing GFP or RFP expression cassettes, resulting in a collection of reporter strains. In addition, vectors containing ethanol and maltose-inducible promoters for driving expression of genes under controlled conditions were validated. By transforming fungal species spanning multiple orders (Ophiostomatales, Microascales, Hypocreales), we report that promoter sequences from H. lauricola function across a diverse range of ambrosia fungi, opening avenues for further development of genetic studies in currently intractable ambrosia symbiosis systems and develop a toolbox for tunable gene expression in these fungi.

Methods

Strains, culture conditions, hygromycin sensitivity assays, inducible promoter assays

Strains of fungi utilized in this study were obtained as follows: Harringtonia lauricola CBS 127349 (RL4) was obtained from the Westerdijk Fungal Biodiversity Institute (Royal Netherlands Academy of Arts and Sciences, Utrecht, The Netherlands), Harringtonia aguacate PL1004 was a kind gift of Dr. Jeffrey Rollins; all other strains including Raffaelea arxii, Raffaelea fusca, Graphium ambrosium, and Neocosmospora affinis were cultured from the galleries of laboratory colonies of Xyleborus affinis throughout the course of standard colony maintenance and are available from the Agricultural Research Service Culture Collection (NRRL), U.S. Department of Agriculture, Peoria, Illinois, USA under accession numbers NRRL 65233, NRRL 65234, NRRL 65230, and NRRL 65229 respectively. The identity of these strains was confirmed previously in our lab by sequencing of the ITS, EF1α, beta-tubulin, LSU, SSU, RPB1, and RPB2 loci paired with multilocus phylogenetic analysis [28] (10.64898/2026.04.23.720448) and cultures of each were regularly maintained on 100 mm plates of Potato Dextrose Agar (PDA) at 25 ºC in the dark. For hygromycin sensitivity assays, 60 mm plates were prepared at 0 µg/mL, 50 µg/mL, 100 µg/mL, and 200 µg/mL hygromycin. Cell suspensions for each species were prepared in water and normalized to 1 × 108 spores/mL using a hemocytometer, and one microliter of spore suspension was spotted into the center of plates at each concentration in triplicate and resulting growth was monitored over the course of 21 days. Plates were photographed at the end of this period using a light box to obtain figure images. Transformed strains were regularly maintained on PDA amended with 200 µg/mL hygromycin. For microscopy analysis, spore suspensions of strains were obtained by scraping cells from a petri dish using a toothpick and dislodging them into 100 µL of water. This water suspension was then used to inoculate 3 mL of potato dextrose broth (PDB) and grown in a shaking incubator at 25 ºC, 180 rpm shaking for 3–5 days. For inducible promoter assays, suspensions were prepared as above and inoculated into 3 mL cultures of the respective media type and grown as above before harvesting cells for microscopy. Minimal mediums consisted of 6.7 g/L yeast nitrogen base and 2% of the specific carbon source listed. When using ethanol as a carbon source, media was autoclaved first and cooled, then 100% ethanol was added to prevent evaporation.

Identification of promoters, plasmid design and construction

For constitutive expression of RFP and eGFP genes, as well as the hygromycin HPH gene, the 1500 bp H. lauricola GAPDHp promoter was used, as described previously [27]. For inducible, context-dependent, expression of genes, the promoters of the AlcA and GlaA genes were chosen. These genes were first identified in Aspergillus species by querying the NCBI Nucleotide and Gene databases. The Aspergillus genes were then used as queries for BLAST searches against the H. lauricola RL4 genome in the NCBI Assembly database to identify H. lauricola homologs. Once identified, the sequences of these genes, as well as 2000 bp upstream of their transcription start sites, were copied into Benchling software (benchling.com) for subsequent sequence analysis and plasmid design. Conserved regulatory elements present in the promoters of these two Aspergillus genes were identified by searching previously published literature, and these motifs were searched for in the upstream sequences of the H. lauricola GlaA and AlcA homologs to identify their presence/absence, position relative to the transcription start site, and copy number. Based on the presence and position of these regulatory elements, an AlcAp promoter sequence of 1300 bp and a GlaAp promoter sequence of 900 bp were chosen. Plasmids were then designed in Benchling using pUC19 plasmid as a backbone. Constitutive expression cassettes consisted of the H. lauricola GAPDp 1500 bp promoter, the gene of interest, and a minimal terminator sequence of Aspergillus terreus totaling 119 bp in length. Inducible expression cassettes consisted of either the H. lauricola AlcAp or GlaAp promoters, the gene of interest, and the A. terreus terminator sequence. Designed plasmids were synthesized by Genscript (Genscript Biotech Corporation, Nanjing, China). Dried plasmids received from Genscript were subsequently resuspended in 20 µL water and 1 µL was transformed into E. coli DH5a competent cells. The plasmid sequence in resulting transformants was verified by colony sequencing using Plasmidsaurus (Plasmidsaurus, San Francisco, California, USA). Once verified, E. coli carrying the desired plasmid was grown in 50 mL cultures and plasmids were extracted using the Zymo MidiPrep kit (Zymo Research Corporation, Irvine, California, USA). Prior to transformation, all plasmids were linearized by cutting with the EcoRI restriction enzyme (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, Massachusetts, USA) in 10 µg reactions. Linearized plasmids were then purified and concentrated using the Thermo GeneJet PCR purification kit and eluted in 25–30 µL sterile distilled water.

Protoplast generation

To generate protoplasts of fungal isolates, 100 µL of cell suspension was prepared in water by scraping the surface of plate cultures with a toothpick and swirling the toothpick into the water to a concentration of 1–5 × 108 spores/mL as determined by quantification with a hemocytometer, adjusting concentrations accordingly using the dilution equation C1V1 = C2V2. This suspension was then added to 50 mL Potato Dextrose Broth (PDB) cultures and grown in a shaking incubator for 7 days at 25 ºC in the dark with 180 rpm rotation. Following this incubation period, cultures were transferred to 50 mL conical tubes and centrifuged at 4347 rpm for 10 min to pellet tissue. Pelleted cells were resuspended once in water to wash, then pelleted again, followed by one wash as above in protoplast buffer (consisting of 0.8 M MgSO4∙7H2O, 0.2 M sodium citrate dihydrate, pH 5.5). Washed cells were then added to clean empty 125 mL Erlenmeyer flasks. 200 mg Glucanex lysing enzyme (Sigma cat# L1412) was then dissolved in 3 mL Novozyme buffer (1 M sorbitol, 50 mM sodium citrate dihydrate, pH 5.8), filter sterilized, and added to 17 mL of protoplast buffer, and this 20 mL was added to the 125 mL flask with the fungal tissue. This mixture was incubated at 28 ºC with 100 rpm shaking for 4 h, after which 10 µL of the suspension was placed onto a microscope slide and examined for the presence of protoplasts. Once the presence of protoplasts was confirmed, the suspension was poured through a 0.4 µm filter into a 50 mL conical tube to remove residual undigested hyphae and 30 mL 0.6 M KCl was poured through the filter into the conical tube. These tubes were then centrifuged at 4347 rpm, 4ºC for 10 min to pellet protoplasts and the supernatant was discarded. Protoplasts were then washed two times by resuspension in 10 mL STC (1 M sorbitol, 50 mM Tris, 50 mM CaCl2∙2H2O, pH 8) followed by resuspension in a final volume of 500 µL STC, taking care to keep them on ice. Protoplast numbers were then counted using a hemocytometer and adjusted to a final concentration of 1 × 108 protoplasts/mL. For each 1 mL of protoplast solution after the concentration was adjusted, 12.5 µL DMSO, 62.5 µL heparin (5 mg/mL in STC), and 250 µL of 40% PEG mixture (2 parts 60% PEG 4000 in water, 1-part KTC: 1.8 M KCl, 150 mM Tris, 150 mM CaCl2∙2H2O, pH 8) was added to the mixture. Aliquots of 100–400 mL were then placed into pre-chilled 2.0 mL microcentrifuge tubes and cells were frozen and stored at − 80 ºC until use.

Transformation and screening

To transform protoplasts, 5 µg of linearized plasmid DNA was added to 100 µL of protoplast in a 2 mL tube and the mixture was incubated on ice for 30 min. Following this incubation on ice, 1 mL of the 40% PEG solution was added to the tubes, and the tubes were inverted several times to mix and allowed to incubate at 20 min at room temperature. Following this room temperature incubation period, transformation mixes were spread evenly onto 25 mL bottom agar plates (0.7 M sucrose, 0.5 g/L yeast extract, 15 g/L agar) and allowed to dry fully in a flow hood before covering. These transformation plates were allowed to recover overnight, and the next morning (approximately 15 h later) this bottom agar was covered in a layer of 5 mL top agar (0.7 M sucrose, 0.5 g/L yeast extract, 8 g/L agar) with hygromycin selection added to a final media volume of 30 mL (bottom agar + top agar). For Harringtonia lauricola transformations, selection was added at 300 µg/mL while for all other species tested hygromycin was added to a final concentration of 100 µg/mL in accordance with hygromycin sensitivity assay results. Plates were incubated in the dark at room temperature and examined for the presence of transformants emerging through the top agar onto the surface of the plate. Transformants were screened directly on transformation plates by examining colonies using a Zeiss AXIO Zoom.V16 (Zeiss, Oberkochen, Germany) fluorescent dissecting microscope to observe RFP and GFP fluorescence. Fluorescent colonies were streaked onto plates containing 200 ug/mL hygromycin for single spore purification and single spore isolates demonstrating strong fluorescent signals were picked for further culturing and cryostorage in 20% glycerol solution.

Microscopy

WT and fluorescent strains of fungi were routinely DAPI stained and prepared for fluorescence microscopy by scraping cells from a plate using a toothpick, or by growing a small PDB culture for 3–5 days, after which the cells were suspended in Vectashield Hardset mounting medium with DAPI (Vector Laboratories Newark, California). The suspension was then pipetted onto a microscope slide, a cover slip added and allowed to cure at room temperature for 1–2 h. Slides were then cured overnight at 4 ºC prior to imaging. For staining protoplasts with DAPI, aliquots of protoplast cells frozen at -80 ºC were thawed on ice and a 10 µL aliquot was placed into a new tube. To this tube, 1 µL of a 10 µg/mL DAPI working solution was added and the mixture was incubated at room temperature for 30 min. The stained cell solution was then pipetted onto a microscope slide, cover slipped, sealed with nail polish, and immediately imaged. For strains testing inducible promoters, 10 µL of a 5-day PDB culture were grown in each of the tested carbon source mediums were pipetted directly onto a microscope slide, a cover slip was added, sealed with nail polish, and imaged immediately. All microscopy was performed using Keyence BZ-X800 and BZ-X1000 microscopes (Keyence Corporation, Osaka, Japan) using brightfield, DAPI, GFP, and TRITC fluorescence channels.

Phylogenetic tree reconstruction

For phylogenetic reconstruction of the isolates tested, the Elongation Factor 1α (EF1α) locus was chosen. Sequences for Neocosmospora affinis, Graphium ambrosium, Raffaelea arxii, and Raffaelea fusca were generated in our lab and are deposited in Genbank under the following accession numbers: PV220985, PX933391, PX757121, and PX757122, respectively. For Harringtonia aguacate and Harringtonia lauricola, EF1α sequences were obtained from Genbank under the accession numbers OM631783.1 and OM631785.1, respectively. Sequences were aligned using MAFFT v7.520 with default parameters. Trees were generated using RaXml v 8.2.12 with 2000 bootstrap replicates. Trees were visualized using FigTree v1.4.4 software.

Results

To identify a suitable selection marker for transformation experiments, the Xyleborus beetle filamentous fungal symbionts, Harringtonia lauricola, H. aguacate, Raffaelea arxii, R. fusca, Graphium ambrosium, and Neocosmospora affinis were tested for their sensitivity to the antifungal agent hygromycin (Fig. 1A). With the exception of H. lauricola, all fungal strains showed high sensitivity, with little to no growth at 50 µg/ml hygromycin (Note: inhibition of H. lauricola growth has previously been shown to require > 200 µg/ml [27]. Based upon these results, two plasmids were designed and synthesized as detailed in the Methods section to yield: pHl-GRfpH and pHl-GGfpH resulting in the constitutive expression of red-fluorescent or green fluorescent (RFP/eGFP) proteins, respectively (Fig. 1B). The expression of both the fluorescent markers (RFP/eGFP) and the HPH resistance cassette were driven by a 1500 bp fragment derived from the H. lauricola glyceraldehyde dehydrogenase promoter region (Hlgpdp), which was previously identified and described [27]. Two additional promoters based on the H. lauricola: (1) ethanol inducible alcohol dehydrogenase promoter sequence (1300 bp, HlalcAp) and (2) maltose inducible glucoamylase promoter sequence (900 bp, HlglaAp) were further identified, and vectors with these promoters driving expression of RFP were also synthesized (pHl-AlcARfpH and pHl-GlaARfpH, Fig. 1B). Protoplasts were generated for all fungal strains by incubation in a solution of Trichoderma harzianum lysing enzymes (Glucanex) and transformation followed a PEG-mediated protocol, as detailed in the Methods section (outline of protocol given in Fig. 2). All fungal isolates produced mononucleate blastospores in liquid culture, and multinucleate hyphae (Fig. 3), with cells derived from the protoplasting protocol shown to be mononucleate (Fig. 4), thus simplifying transformation and screening procedures. Protoplast recovery was highest from R. fusca (> 1010 protoplasts/50 ml culture), with R. arxii, H. lauricola, and G ambrosium resulting in 4–8 × 109 protoplasts/50 ml culture, and somewhat lower protoplast recovery from H. aguacate and N. affinis of 4–7 × 108 protoplasts/50 ml culture (Table 1). All protoplast suspensions were adjusted to 1 × 108 cells/ml following digestion and transformed with 5–7 mg of linearized plasmid. Transformation efficiencies for pHl-GRfpH and pHl-GGfpH were equivalent for each strain, with G. ambrosium showing the highest (> 100 transformants/108 protoplasts), followed by H. lauricola, H. aguacate, and R. fusca (15–50 transformants/108 protoplasts), and R. arxii (1–5 transformants/108 protoplasts) (Fig. 5A, Table 1). No transformants were obtained for N. affinis. Preliminary screening of transformants was performed directly on transformation plates using a fluorescence dissecting microscope, with > 95% of colonies visualized displaying either green or red fluorescence depending upon the marker used. Colonies were single spore purified and resultant transformants typically displayed bright (H. lauricola) to light pink mycelium (remaining isolates) or greenish (again brightest for H. lauricola) for RFP- and eGFP-transformants, respectively (Fig. 5B). RFP- and eGFP-expression was further confirmed for each strain via fluorescent microscopy of spores and hyphae (Fig. 6). For each strain, fluorescence signals were generally, but not always, seen to be distributed evenly throughout the cells, indicating cytoplasmic distribution. In some transformants, e.g., R. arxii RFP, H. aguacate RFP, and R. fusca GFP, fluorescence was seen to localize to punctate spots within spores and hyphae. These signals did not overlap with nuclei as revealed by DAPI staining, indicating potential localization to vacuoles or other cellular compartments for some isolates. Although brightly fluorescent R. arxii RFP transformants were obtained, R. arxii GFP transformants were consistently difficult to obtain, even after multiple transformation attempts using the same batches of protoplasts transformed with the RFP construct. The few transformants we could obtain displayed fluorescence that was slow to develop in culture, slow growth, and/or sensitivity to hygromycin, suggesting false positives, or potential effects of the integration sites. The ethanol and maltose inducible plasmids, pHl-AlcARfpH and pHl-GlaARfpH, respectively, were constructed as detailed in the Methods section and transformed into H. lauricola and G. ambrosium to further expand the available molecular toolbox for these fungi. Transformation efficiencies for the AlcAp and GlaAp constructs ranged from 30–50 transformants per 107 protoplasts for both fungi. To test the inducibility of each construct, respective single colony purified transformants were grown in minimal media containing a variety of carbon sources (all at 2%) including glycerol, sorbitol, glucose, maltose, and ethanol as the sole carbon sources, as well as in (rich) media, i.e., potato dextrose broth (PDB) and yeast-extract peptone dextrose broth (YPD) as detailed in the Methods section. Cells were inoculated from conidia derived from PDA plates and grown for 48 h before imaging (Figs. 7 and 8 for H. lauricola and G. ambrosium, respectively). For both fungi the pHl-AlcARfpH construct showed some expression under all conditions tested (more pronounced for G. ambrosium), suggesting leaky expression by this promoter under the conditions tested, although growth on ethanol resulted in clear induction of the fluorescent (RFP) signal, that was more pronounced than on all other conditions. Signal from the pHl-GlaARfpH construct was much tighter (than pHl-AlcARfpH), with little to no fluorescent signal detected under glycerol, sorbitol, ethanol, PDB, or YPD growth conditions. Clear induction was seen in the presence of maltose, and intriguingly, for H. lauricola (but not G. ambrosium) large globose cells were seen in the glucose growth conditions (but no other) that displayed marked red fluorescence. Discussion

Mechanistic research on fungal animal mutualists remains significantly understudied as compared to saprophytes and pathogens, in part because of the lack of genetic tools required for such studies. Regarding plant-fungal mutualists, genetically tractable systems are increasingly being developed, e.g., Epichloe/Lolium interactions, Rhizophagus irregularis/plant interactions and Laccaria bicolor/Populus interactions [29–31], but aside from some yeasts, (filamentous) fungi that form beneficial associations with animals remain neglected. With over 3200 different species, the ambrosia beetle lifestyle of forming obligate nutritional mutualisms with fungi is polyphyletic, having arisen at least ~ 12 different times in different groups of beetles. In turn, there are thousands of different fungal species associated with these beetles with many, but not all within the Sordariomycetes class of the Ascomycota. To date, except for H. lauricola, which in addition to being a mutualist of ambrosia beetles is also a devastating plant pathogen, few, if any, genetic tools are available for any of the remaining species of ambrosia fungi.

Here, we have developed a simple protoplasting and transformation method applicable across a wide range of phylogenetically diverse ambrosia beetle symbionts, including members of the Ophiostomales, i.e., Raffaelea and Harringtonia sp., and Microascales (Graphium ambrosium). However, surprisingly, neither transformability nor efficiency followed the phylogeny of the fungi. Indeed, the more distantly (relative to the fungi examined) related, G. ambrosium, showed the highest transformation efficiency, even higher than that of H. lauricola from which the promoter sequences driving expression were obtained. H. lauricola, H. aguacate, and R. fusca showed similar levels of robust transformation efficiencies, whereas R. arxii transformation was low. Indeed, only 2–3 colonies for either the RFP or GFP vectors were obtained for the latter fungus, and although the R. arxii RFP-transformants obtained appeared stable and showed strong RFP-expression, the GFP isolates showed poor maintenance of resistance and/or GFP expression. As the total number of single colony purified transformants obtained for R. arxii remained low, some caution should be considered in interpreting these results, and integration and/or other factors may play a role. In addition, we were unable to successfully obtain any transformants for Neocosmospora affinis (Hypocreales) despite generation of protoplasts, suggesting that either the transformation methods employed in this study were not sufficient to deliver DNA into this fungus, or that H. lauricola promoters were incapable of driving gene expression in this species. Optimally, the use of promoter sequences derived from the fungi itself should result in higher transformation success, although the genome for N. affinis is not yet available. Finally, while for most transformants examined, GFP/RFP expression appeared cytoplasmic, in some instances, i.e., especially for the few GFP R. arxii transformants obtained, expression of the fluorescent protein appeared to be in subcellular structures (potentially vacuoles, but not in the nucleus as determined by DAPI co-staining) for reasons unknown at this time.

Overall, the successful transformation of fungi within the Microascales and subsequent gene expression by H. lauricola promoters opens the door for future efforts to dissect the genetic pathways involved in host-interactions. The generated fluorescent strains can now be used for experimental colonization of host beetles, tracing gallery and fungal garden formation, and determinations of fungal cells used as nutritive substrates for the beetles. Thus far such studies have been restricted to H. lauricola [14, 15], but can now be expanded to a wider network of the commensal community. By using differentially labeled fungal strains, i.e., one expressing GFP and the other RFP, hypotheses concerning mycangial and fungal garden colonization and/or competition can now be examined. More widely, the use of these vectors to transform other fungi within the Ophiostomatales, which includes other notable ambrosia fungi, including devastating plant pathogens such as Raffaelea quercivora and Raffaelea quercus-mongolicae, could lead to important insights into both the mutualism and pathogenicity of these important dual-nature fungi.

We further developed and tested two inducible promoters, allowing for fine-tuning gene expression within these fungal systems, demonstrating their application in both H. lauricola and the more distantly related, G. ambrosium. This is particularly useful when a gene product may be toxic to the host cell, such as secondary metabolites, and/or if researchers wish to express a gene product conditionally, e.g., after a culture has accumulated sufficient biomass to produce a protein or metabolite in high quantities or during distinct developmental stages. The AlcA promoter, typically considered to be induced by ethanol and repressed by glucose, showed somewhat “leaky” expression when grown on several different carbon sources including dextrose (glucose), but (RFP) expression was considerably higher when cells were grown on ethanol as the sole carbon source. One potential hypothesis for the “leaky” expression seen from this promoter is the production of ethanol by the fungus under aerobic conditions when grown on a variety of media types including glucose (which does not appear to occur in Aspergillus). We have anecdotally noted during routine culturing in our lab that H. lauricola, for example, produces a fruity “sour beer”-like smell, particularly in liquid culture which may hint at ethanol production during growth, although this would require further detailed experimentation to confirm. The GlaA promoter, traditionally induced by maltose and repressed by glucose, showed much tighter expression in both fungal species tested, with bright fluorescence noted on media amended with maltose. Intriguingly, for H. lauricola (but not G. ambrosium) the only other condition in which fluorescence was noted for this reporter construct was in media amended with glucose. Under these growth conditions, H. lauricola produced large, globose cells, and further experiments examining the production of these cells and what role they may play in the beetle association are warranted. In addition, future studies using these and other inducible promoter systems as sensors for infection (pathogenic host) and colonization (mutualistic host) will help to better inform the nutritional landscapes within host tissues and organs, providing crucial details into what hosts may be provisioning to their partners during mycangial symbiosis as well as what fungi may be utilizing during plant infection. The methods and tools provided here will allow researchers to expand beyond ecological studies of ambrosia beetle-fungus systems and into mechanistic and molecular studies, offering the potential of developing parallel systems within the polyphyletic ambrosia symbioses to better compare their underlying features.

Conclusions

Diverse ambrosia fungi could be genetically engineered using a simple protoplasting methodology paired with transformation vectors based upon constitutive and inducible H. lauricola promoters. The promoters derived from H. lauricola were functional in driving gene expression in this phylogenetically diverse array of tested fungi, however, species and/or isolate limitations incongruous with phylogenetic placement were noted, indicating a range of plasticity in transformation success. Isolates successfully transformed can now be used in new avenues for fluorescent tracer studies and deeper genetic dissection in ambrosia beetle-fungus symbiotic systems that are currently genetically intractable due to lack of genomic resources, genetic tools, and established laboratory protocols. Such studies will provide deeper insights into the interactions between ambrosia beetles and their fungal mutualists, particularly within mycangia, as well as the roles of specific genes and molecular pathways involved in the sustained symbiosis between these fascinating microbes and hosts.
