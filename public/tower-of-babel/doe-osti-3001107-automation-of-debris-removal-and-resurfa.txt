ABSTRACT
The increasing complexity and scale of pulsed power systems necessitate effective maintenance
strategies to ensure optimal performance and safety. This report investigates the automation of
debris removal and resurfacing of insulators in terawatt pulsed power systems using advanced laser
cleaning technologies. The motivation for this research stems from the limitations of current manual
cleaning methods, which are labor-intensive and pose health risks due to hazardous materials. The
study addresses the problem of surface flashover, a significant issue affecting insulator performance,
by evaluating two innovative cleaning techniques: Pulsed Laser Cleaning (PLC) and Flash Lamp
Annealing (FLA). Experimental results demonstrate that PLC effectively restores the dielectric
strength of Rexolite insulators, while FLA shows limited success. The findings highlight the
potential for automated cleaning solutions to enhance safety and efficiency in insulator maintenance,
paving the way for future advancements in pulsed power technology.

1. 1 BACKGROUND

1.1. Pulsed Power Systems
Scientists actively strive to recreate extreme pressures and temperatures in laboratory settings to
conduct a diverse array of experiments. These experiments aim to replicate conditions found in
outer space, explore dynamic material properties, develop weapons applications, and advance
research related to nuclear fusion. The ability to simulate such extreme environments is crucial for
understanding fundamental physical processes and testing materials under conditions that would
otherwise be impossible to achieve on Earth.
To accomplish these ambitious goals, researchers employ pulsed power technology, a sophisticated
method that involves storing energy over a period and releasing it instantaneously. This technology
operates on the principle of rapidly discharging stored electrical energy, which generates high-
intensity pulses of power. The process utilizes a series of pulse compression and transmission lines,
which effectively channel and amplify the stored energy, resulting in an immense power output at
the center load. The significant ratio of energy storage to discharge time allows the delivered power
to exceed the required input substantially, making pulsed power systems exceptionally efficient for
specific applications.
Large-scale pulsed power machines are complex systems that comprise three main sections:
 1. Energy Storage Section: This section is typically filled with large capacitors that store
 electrical energy. These capacitors are designed to handle high voltages and currents,
 allowing them to accumulate substantial amounts of energy over time. The energy storage
 capacity is critical, as it determines the potential power output during discharge.
 2. Pulse-Forming Section: This section features a network of pulse-forming and transmission
 lines that shape and direct the stored energy into a high-intensity pulse. The pulse-forming
 network is engineered to optimize the rise time and duration of the pulse, ensuring that the
 energy is delivered efficiently to the load. This section may also include various components
 such as inductors and transformers that further enhance the pulse characteristics.
 3. Load Section: In this final section, the high-power output is delivered to the experimental
 apparatus or application. The load can vary widely depending on the specific experiment or
 application, ranging from diagnostic tools to material testing setups. The design of the load
 section is crucial, as it must be capable of withstanding the extreme conditions generated by
 the pulsed power output.
These sophisticated machines typically reside in billion-dollar complexes, equipped with advanced
safety and control systems to manage the high-energy discharges. In contrast, smaller pulsed power
test stands can conveniently fit onto a single operating table, making them accessible for various
research and testing scenarios. These smaller systems allow scientists to conduct preliminary
experiments and optimize parameters before scaling up to larger setups.
Scientists and engineers continuously seek innovative ways to utilize and optimize pulsed power
technology, as it often represents the only viable method for creating the specific environments
necessary to achieve certain research objectives. The versatility of pulsed power systems enables a
wide range of applications, including [1, 2]:
 • Z-Pinch X-Ray Drivers: These systems use pulsed power to compress plasma, generating
 high-energy X-rays for imaging and diagnostic purposes in various fields, including materials
 science and medical imaging.

 12

 • Electromagnetic Launchers (EM Launchers): Pulsed power technology is employed to
 accelerate projectiles using electromagnetic forces, offering potential applications in defense
 and space exploration.
 • High-Powered Microwave Systems: These systems utilize pulsed power to generate
 intense microwave pulses for applications such as radar, communications, and directed
 energy weapons.
 • Radiographic Diagnostics: Pulsed power enables advanced imaging techniques that
 provide insights into material properties and structural integrity, essential for both scientific
 research and industrial applications.
Overall, pulsed power systems represent a critical technological advancement in the quest to explore
and understand the fundamental principles of physics, materials science, and engineering, paving the
way for breakthroughs in various scientific and industrial domains.

1.2. Z Pulsed Power Facility and Insulator Stack
The history of pulsed power technology traces back to the Cold War, driven by the urgent need for
advanced radar systems and weapons testing capabilities. In the 1960s, researchers began exploring
innovative methods for X-ray imaging and simulating weapon conditions. This pioneering work
commenced at the Atomic Weapons Establishment (AWE) in the UK, where scientists developed
stacked modules with insulation and movable spark gaps to generate X-ray pulses. As the field
evolved, researchers in the United States made significant advancements by creating more efficient
Marx generators and self-resetting spark gaps. These innovations enabled faster operation with
higher voltage, current, and energy outputs, laying the groundwork for the modern field of pulsed
power.
The evolution of pulsed power technology led to the development of the first large-scale
accelerators. Sandia National Laboratories emerged as a premier research center in this domain,
creating the PBFA-I system as one of the early large-scale pulsed power machines. This was
followed by the PBFA-II in 1985, which focused on studying fusion processes. In 1996, Sandia
National Laboratories unveiled the Z Pulsed Power Facility, also known as the Z-Machine, which
remains the world’s largest pulsed power accelerator. The Z-Machine underwent a significant
refurbishment in 2007, enhancing its ability to deliver greater energy, voltage, and more accurate
diagnostics. This refurbishment was driven by innovations in Marx generators, spark-gap switches,
pulse-forming networks, and transmission lines, allowing engineers to maximize power output while
minimizing current loss [3].
The Z-Machine, shown in Figure 1, has provided invaluable insights into radiation effects, material
dynamics, and explosive conditions. It can produce outputs of 30 megaamperes (MA) of current,
350 terawatts (TW) of power, and 2.7 megajoules (MJ) of energy within a pulse duration of just 100
nanoseconds. For brief moments, the power levels generated by the Z-Machine exceed those of all
power plants combined, resulting in extraordinary temperatures and pressures that facilitate
groundbreaking research.

 13

 Figure 1: Aerial image of the high-bay of the Z-Machine at Sandia National
 Laboratories.

The Z-Machine is divided into three distinct sections: energy storage, pulse forming, and the center
section. The process begins with large-scale Marx generators that work in conjunction with capacitor
banks to store electrical energy over several minutes. This stored energy undergoes multiple
compression steps to generate the high-current pulses necessary for Z-pinch plasma experiments.
The system includes 36 Marx generators, each equipped with 60 capacitors, with each capacitor
boasting a capacitance of 2.6 microfarads (µF). To ensure optimal electrical and thermal efficiency,
the Marx generators are immersed in 600,000 gallons of transformer oil, which serves as both a
coolant and an insulator. [4]
Once the electrical energy is stored, it is transmitted as high-voltage pulses to water-insulated
intermediate-store capacitors, which facilitate the pulse-forming process. Laser-triggered gas
switches activate these pulses, directing them through further water-insulated pulse-forming lines
toward the center section. After passing through additional outer transmission lines and the water
convolute region for further pulse compression, the energy reaches the center section. This center
section is vacuum-insulated and comprises the insulator stack, Magnetic Insulated Transmission
Lines (MITLs), and the load region. The configuration of the center section and the insulator stack
is illustrated in Figure 2

Operating machines of this size and complexity presents a unique set of challenges. One of the most
critical issues is maintaining the performance and material integrity of various components
throughout the machine’s long lifetime of testing. The Z-Machine operates as a single-shot system,
meaning that it fully destroys or severely damages components and diagnostics after each shot. This
necessitates intense refurbishment efforts to replace hardware and diagnostics for subsequent
experiments. Potential solutions to these challenges include the development of advanced coatings
or shielding technologies designed to withstand greater electric fields and prevent complications.
Additionally, engineers are focused on creating more robust versions of existing components, such
as higher-capacity capacitors, more reliable high-voltage switches, and more precise diagnostic tools.
These innovations require creative engineering solutions that can adapt to the extreme temperatures
and pressures within the machine while maintaining peak energy efficiency [5].

The insulator stack serves as a critical interface for transferring power between the liquid and
vacuum environments. It consists of six rings stacked on top of each other, separated by grading
rings, and represents the first component of the center section, positioned between the Marx banks
and the inner MITLs, as shown in Figure 2. The insulator stack features an anode at the top and a
cathode at the bottom, with a 45° angle at the cathode-triple junction where it interfaces with the
vacuum. This structure not only provides mechanical support but also serves as a location for
placing and collecting current delivery diagnostics. Given its pivotal role as an interface and the
intense electric fields it encounters, researchers continually seek the best insulator materials,
treatments, and systems to enhance performance and reliability [4, 6].

In summary, the Z Pulsed Power Facility and its insulator stack represent the forefront of pulsed
power technology, enabling groundbreaking research in a wide variety of scientific fields. The
ongoing innovations and challenges associated with this facility underscore its significance in
advancing our understanding of high-energy physics and materials science.

1.2.1. Surface Flashover of Insulators
The insulator stack in pulsed power systems is particularly vulnerable to surface breakdown at lower
electric fields compared to other sections, primarily due to the vacuum-insulator interface. Surface

 17

flashover occurs when a discharge propagates across the surface of an insulator, forming a plasma
channel that generates a short circuit, leading to system damage and energy loss. This phenomenon
arises when the enhanced electric field from an applied high voltage exceeds the dielectric strength
of the insulator. Surface flashover, defined as the plasma channel traveling across the surface of the
insulator itself, is more likely than bulk flashover, which occurs through the material. Several factors
contribute to surface flashover, including residual gases that ionize at the vacuum-insulator interface,
as well as debris and particulates that accumulate on the surface, increasing the likelihood of
electrical breakdown. Notably, surface flashover occurs at electric fields significantly lower than
those in the vacuum gap—often by a factor of 10 or more. Figure 7 demonstrates an example of
surface flashover in a non-vacuum, non-pulsed power system [6, 7].

 Figure 8: Surface flashover in a non-vacuum environment occurring on glass
 insulators with volcanic ash deposited [6].
To mitigate flashover, engineers specifically design the insulator-vacuum interface with a 45° angle,
as shown in Figure 7. Studies have demonstrated that the insulator-vacuum angle strongly influences
electric field grading at the cathode triple junction, with the 45° angle providing the highest
flashover voltage resistance. This finding was first reported in Ian Smith’s Pulse Breakdown of Insulator
Surfaces in a Poor Vacuum in 1964 [8] and later validated in Miller’s Surface Flashover of Insulators in 1989,
where insulators with a 45° angle exhibited flashover voltages 3–5 times greater than those with a 0°
angle (cylindrical insulators). Angles ranging from +20° to +70° showed increasing voltage holdoff,
while angles from -30° to -80° provided moderate holdoff levels. The least effective angle for
voltage holdoff was 0°, as well as angles slightly above or below this value. This relationship is
illustrated in Figure 9 [9].

 Figure 9: Plot of cone angle for insulator-vacuum on the x-axis and flashover
 voltage on the y-axis [10].

The chart in Figure 9 shows that both PMMA and Nylon insulators achieve the greatest voltage
holdoff at a 45° angle. Later studies confirmed that the 45° angle directs electric field lines away
from the cathode triple junction, reducing field grading at this location. However, this design can
lead to greater field enhancement at the anode triple junction instead [10, 11].
Extensive research has been conducted to understand the initiation and mitigation of flashover.
Flashover most commonly originates at the cathode triple junction rather than the anode. High-
speed photography and microscopy studies have confirmed that cathode-initiated flashover is driven
by the Secondary Electron Emission Avalanche (SEEA) mechanism. In this process, electrons are emitted
from the cathode triple junction due to the localized electric field. These electrons travel toward the
surface of the insulator, triggering further electron emissions. As the cascade of secondary electrons
accumulates, surface charge builds up, leading to desorption and ionization of gas molecules on the
insulator surface. Eventually, this process forms a plasma channel across the insulator interface to
the anode, causing electrical breakdown and potential surface damage. Flashover events can leave
tracking marks or dendrites on the insulator, which may increase the likelihood of subsequent
breakdowns at the same location. However, the insulator may also condition itself, requiring higher
electric fields for future breakdowns [12-14].

Surface flashover is a frequent issue in large-scale pulsed power systems, prompting significant
interest in engineering insulators to maintain their original dielectric strength after experiments and
to prevent tracking or debris accumulation. Flashover can cause permanent damage to insulators,
including holes, carbonization, dust deposition, and charging damage. Since pulsed power
accelerators are often in use for years or decades before upgrades, effective post-experiment
processes are essential to preserve flashover resistance. Solutions include surface polishing, plasma
fluorination, low-outgassing materials, and surface treatments such as reconditioning and specialized
coatings [15, 17].

1.2.2. Rexolite and Acrylic Properties
Acrylic was initially the most widely used insulator material for pulsed power systems, including early
machines developed at the Atomic Weapons Establishment such as SMOG and Polaris. Engineers
utilized acrylic for stacked insulator rings, housing water transmission lines, and interconnecting
insulating slabs due to its ease of production, transparency, and moderate dielectric strength.
However, as acrylic began to be used in high-voltage systems at Sandia National Laboratories,
engineers observed several issues. Acrylic-insulated switches required frequent replacement, and the
material exhibited low thermal stability under the extreme conditions of pulsed power systems.
Although other polymers, such as polyethylene, demonstrated superior dielectric performance, their
large-scale production proved challenging.
In response to these limitations, engineers developed C-Lec Rexolite®, a thermoset material
developed by the materials company C-Lec. Rexolite offered significant advantages over acrylic,
including greater thermal stability, higher dielectric strength, and lower outgassing. Additionally,
Rexolite demonstrated better recovery of dielectric performance after experiments and proved more
suitable for surface treatments, as it could withstand higher temperatures. By the 2000s, Rexolite
began replacing acrylic in large pulsed power machines, becoming the default dielectric material for
these systems [3].
Acrylic, as a thermoplastic, lacks cross-linking in its molecular structure, with PMMA (poly-methyl
methacrylate) chains held together by weak molecular forces. When exposed to heat, acrylic softens
and melts, reducing its dielectric performance. Surface treatments or cleaning methods that apply
excessive heat can damage acrylic samples, limiting their effectiveness as insulators. In contrast,

 22

Rexolite, a cross-linked polystyrene, features rigid polymer chains that resist softening or melting
under heat. Instead, Rexolite chars under extreme heat, providing greater thermal resistance and
making it more effective for surface cleaning or treatments involving heat [18, 19].
PMMA offers additional benefits, including UV resistance, optical transparency, mechanical
strength, and flexible use. Its safety and ease of production make it suitable for a variety of
industries. However, PMMA’s inert surface prevents adhesion from surface treatments, posing
challenges for experimental modifications. Forte’s study, Is PMMA a Suitable Substrate for ALD?,
concluded that atomic layer deposition (ALD) is only conditionally suitable on PMMA and requires
separate modifications beforehand. This finding, combined with PMMA’s limited thermal stability,
highlights its difficulties as a material for experimental surface treatments [20].
Jacob’s study, Microwave Properties of Low-Loss Polymers at Cryogenic Temperatures, compared Rexolite with
other polymers, including Teflon and polyethylene. The study found that Rexolite had the highest
loss tangent, marginally higher than polyethylene, but still classified as a low-loss material. This
indicates that Rexolite retains less heat energy at very low temperatures compared to other polymers,
while remaining relatively effective. The study also noted Rexolite’s mechanical and flexible
properties as notable advantages over Teflon and polyethylene [21].

1.3. Problems and Inconsistencies with Current Cleaning Methods
The current method for refurbishing the insulator stack after a shot involves hand-sanding with
silicon carbide (SiC) sandpaper. This process requires three individuals working for 2–3 hours per
session. While effective, this method has several drawbacks related to safety, replicability, and
efficiency.
The labor-intensive nature of hand-sanding leads to physical strain for workers, who report heavy
physical fatigue after refurbishment sessions. Additionally, the presence of activated or hazardous
materials from experiments poses significant health risks. Workers must adhere to strict safety
protocols, including extensive use of personal protective equipment (PPE), which increases cleaning
time and costs. The process is entirely non-standardized, relying on individual workers rather than
automation. As a result, replicating refurbishment conditions consistently is impossible, as variations
in sanding technique and force inevitably occur.
A report from the Wisconsin Occupational Health Laboratory analyzed the composition of insulator
dust in the Z-Machine and identified carcinogenic substances such as arsenic, beryllium, and
chromium, along with numerous other metal elements. Another report from Fauske & Associates
confirmed that the dust is combustible, presenting an additional hazard. These findings are
summarized in Figure 14 and Table 1, respectively.

1.4. Scope of Research
The need for a safer, standardized, and automatable cleaning technique that does not compromise
insulator performance has become increasingly urgent. This need is particularly relevant as current
pulsed power machines undergo upgrades and new larger machines are designed for future use.

1.4.1. Goal of Work
The primary goal of this research is to develop effective cleaning techniques that retain insulator
performance against flashover after debris coating. Using established statistical methods and models,
researchers aim to evaluate the effectiveness of these techniques on two widely used insulator
materials in pulsed power systems: acrylic (PMMA) and C-Lec Rexolite® (cross-linked polystyrene).
Acrylic was originally the most prevalent insulator material in pulsed power machines, but Rexolite
has gained popularity due to its superior thermal, mechanical, outgassing, and dielectric properties.
This transition represents an engineering improvement for better efficiency and longevity in pulsed
power systems. The search for new treatments capable of maintaining dielectric strengths greater
than 1 MV/cm remains ongoing.
The goal is not necessarily to prevent flashover events entirely or eliminate hand-sanding from the
refurbishment process. Instead, the aim is to reduce the time required for sanding, and hazard
exposure, while maintaining the effectiveness of insulator resistance against surface flashover.

2. STATISTICAL MODELS OF FLASHOVER

2.1. Background on Statistical Methods for Flashover Analysis
To evaluate the effectiveness of cleaning techniques, researchers require a robust statistical method
for analyzing electrical data. This method must determine whether the insulator material retains its
resistance to flashover after debris deposition.

2.1.2. Assumptions and Limitations of Past Work
Stygar’s model relies on three key assumptions, each introducing specific limitations:
 1. Flashover as a Stochastic Process: Stygar assumed that flashover occurs randomly at
 abnormal surface features, perturbations, or built-up particulates. Consequently, his
 probability model cannot be applied to samples with existing non-uniform surface
 conditions, such as chipping.
 2. Characteristic Delay Dominated by Statistical Component: Stygar assumed that the
 total characteristic delay is primarily influenced by the statistical delay time—the interval
 between voltage application and electron avalanche initiation. This delay is much longer than
 the time required for plasma channel formation and completion.
 3. Instantaneous Rate of Flashover as a Power-Law of Electric Field: Stygar assumed that
 the rate of flashover is proportional to the electric field raised to the power of the shape
 parameter, forming the Weibull fit for the probability model. However, the model cannot
 accommodate excessively large teff or C, nor insufficient Ep values that prevent flashover
 formation.[6]

2.1.3. Other Statistical Studies
Stygar acknowledged several prior statistical models in his publication. J.C. Martin’s study used
Monte Carlo simulations to create a model based on insulator dielectric strength. Anderson and
Schneider’s model also employed Weibull statistics to develop a probability model, which Stygar
used as a baseline for PMMA. O’Malley’s model focused on triple junction enhancement, but Stygar
noted that it did not account for the stochastic nature of flashover, leading him to incorporate this
assumption into his own model. By building upon these earlier studies, Stygar developed his
probability model, applied a Weibull fit, and established assumptions for its use.[6]

2.2. Past Work on Insulator Cleaning
Prior to this study we are unaware of any researchers have applying laser or flashlamp cleaning
techniques in pulsed power systems, although these methods have been successfully implemented in
other cleaning contexts and for different types of metals and insulators. Surface treatments are
hypothesized to reduce secondary electron emission during cathode-initiated flashover and to
control charge retention. Mechanical cleaning processes, however, are typically limited to addressing
geometric features on an insulator, which means they primarily inhibit localized electric fields rather
than improving the overall material properties. To achieve broader improvements, studies suggest

 27

that plasma or light-based surface treatments, coatings, or fluorination may be necessary. These
advanced processes have the potential to significantly limit surface charge and polarization
compared to mechanical techniques.
One study demonstrated that acrylic is effective for laser ablation due to its optical transparency and
favorable ablation thresholds. Researchers highlighted the ease of using acrylic in a variety of
applications and modifications. The study employed Excimer lasers with wavelengths ranging from
193–351 nm and noted that cleaning was driven by light rather than heat, which minimized thermal
application and subsequent damage. Similar results were observed with femtosecond lasers, which
proved useful for precise applications. However, the study found that Nd:YAG lasers, with their
short pulse lengths, applied excessive heat, causing thermal damage that softened and melted the
material. This deterioration compromised acrylic’s effectiveness as a dielectric, suggesting that
Nd:YAG nanosecond pulsed lasers are unsuitable for cleaning acrylic [22].
Hand-sanding remains the most common method for insulator refurbishment, typically using 240-
grit sandpaper. This process results in a surface roughness of approximately 38 μm, and the amount
of material removed has not been quantified to our knowledge. While this removal may seem
minimal, it can have significant implications for pulsed power applications operating at high voltages
[23].
Laser cleaning techniques have also been applied to metal surfaces, including monitoring debris
contamination levels before and after cleaning. Although these studies were not conducted in a
pulsed power context or specifically for flashover, they provide valuable insights into how cleaning
processes can be adapted for vacuum insulator applications. The findings emphasize the importance
of an effective monitoring system to detect debris removal during treatment [24].
Surface cleaning research is not limited to polymers; ceramics have also shown changes in flashover
resistance before and after surface polishing. One study demonstrated that polishing increased
flashover voltage by mitigating surface charging caused by secondary electron emission. However,
researchers concluded that mechanical cleaning alone is insufficient for eliminating triple junction
electric field enhancement, underscoring the need for automated surface modification methods.[11]
Other studies support the idea that automated surface treatments are more efficient than mechanical
methods. For example, phenyl-coated alumina insulators exhibited significantly higher flashover
voltages and lower secondary electron emission yields. Researchers attributed these improvements to
increased electron delocalization, which prevents the cascade of secondary electrons. These
treatments not only remove foreign particles from the surface but also alter the surface chemistry
itself.[25]
Another study investigated surface treatment on alumina insulators using perfluoro-decyl
trichlorosilane to fluorinate the surface. This process reduced gas adsorption and increased flashover
voltages. Researchers hypothesized that fluorination suppresses flashover electronically while also
introducing hydrophobic properties that further enhance performance.[26]
Past literature consistently emphasizes the importance of surface work on insulators, even in cases
where direct experimentation is not conducted. These studies highlight how achieving a uniform
finish on the surface is critical for maintaining optimal dielectric performance.
Plasma treatments have also been applied to polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) films to study their
effects on flashover voltage in epoxy-resin insulators. Researchers found that applying PDMS at a
rate of 15 mL/min increased flashover voltage by 75%, demonstrating how surface treatments can
alter insulator responses to high-voltage grading while effectively removing debris.[27]
A related study combined laser processes with mechanical techniques to achieve ultra-smooth metal
surfaces with a roughness of 0.3μm. Researchers discovered that smoother insulator surfaces reduce
localized electric fields, thereby lowering flashover probability. This study uniquely demonstrated

 28

that combining light-based surface treatments with mechanical techniques can yield effective results,
highlighting the potential for multi-step processes to enhance insulator performance.[28]

3.2. Testing Sequence Procedures

Experimental Setup
The experimental procedure began by turning off the high voltage (HV) on the power supply.
Researchers ensured that all components were properly connected via cables and powered on. They
set the appropriate attenuation for each channel on the oscilloscope and connected the roughing
pumps and turbopump to electrical outlets, turning them on. Electrical cords for the trigger
generator and power supply were removed from the AC120 outlets, and outlet covers were placed
over both devices. Locks were attached to the covers, and the keys were placed in a lock-out, tag-out
(LOTO) box. A third lock was added to the LOTO box itself, and the user retained the key for the
LOTO box until it was needed again.
Next, researchers closed the gate valve to the turbopump and turned off the ion gauge. Using a
Fluke pressure gauge, they adjusted the air pressure in the Marx tube to below 10 PSIG, then
increased it to just above 20 PSIG. The vent valve connected to the vacuum chamber was opened to
bring the system to atmospheric pressure. After opening the chamber latch, researchers used a
shorting stick to contact the anode and cathode electrodes, followed by attaching a shorting strap to
connect the test stand housing to the cathode.
Wearing nitrile gloves, researchers carefully removed the sample from the chamber and placed it on
a cleanroom wipe on the worktable. Sandpapers of 220, 400, 600, and 1000 grit were prepared, and a
bottle of isopropanol was sprayed onto the 220-grit sandpaper. Each electrode was sanded clockwise
for approximately 30 seconds, with the electrode facing the sandpaper face down. This process was
repeated for the remaining sandpapers.
After sanding, researchers sprayed a new cleanroom wipe with isopropanol and used it to wipe both
electrodes. An unused section of the wipe was sprayed again and used to clean the sample. Sanding
the electrodes occurred only once per day, but the sample and electrodes were wiped with
isopropanol each time the system broke vacuum and returned to atmospheric conditions.
The sample was placed between the anode and cathode electrodes, ensuring that the smooth faces
of each electrode contacted the sample. The sample-electrode assembly was positioned inside the
chamber directly beneath the series resistor and spring load. Researchers used a feeler gauge to
ensure no gap existed between the sample-anode or sample-cathode interface. If the blade of the
gauge (0.0015”) could slide into the interface, adjustments were made. Figure 18 shows a gap
observed during flashover.

 Figure 18: Gap occurring between the anode and sample.

the chamber door and latch. The vent valve was closed, and the roughing pump valve was opened to
vacuum the system until the pressure reached 100 mTorr. After achieving this pressure, the roughing

 32

pump valve was closed, and the turbopump valve was opened to vacuum the system further until

Researchers retrieved the keys from the LOTO box, removed the locks on the trigger generator and

After reaching the necessary vacuum pressure, a radiological control barrier was set, and the Marx

To ensure radiological safety, researchers limited the number of shots exceeding 20 kV to no more
than 10 per hour. If the vacuum was broken, the same steps for LOTO, sample removal,
refurbishment, and replacement were followed.

3.4. Testing and System Constraints
The data collected during these experiments was part of a Sandia research initiative focused on 8
mm tall samples. Researchers prioritized these samples because their height directly influences the
applied electric field during testing. Since the electric field is calculated as voltage divided by sample
height (E=V/h), taller samples experience lower applied electric fields, reducing the probability of
flashover and making them harder to break down.
During testing, researchers observed a phenomenon known as sample conditioning, where Rexolite
samples progressively became harder to break down even after undergoing reconditioning processes
such as breaking vacuum and wiping with isopropanol. This conditioning effect introduced sample-
to-sample variation, with some samples proving significantly harder to break down during the virgin
sequence, while others were noticeably easier.
In some cases, virgin samples reconditioned to withstand breakdown voltages of 30 kV and beyond,
approaching the safe charging limit of 35 kV. Since exceeding this threshold posed a risk of system
damage, researchers found it challenging to collect sufficient breakdown data for statistical analysis.
The statistical visualization process employed a Maximum Likelihood Estimator (MLE) based on
Stygar’s probability model. This method requires a high number of breakdowns to minimize
variation and narrow error bounds. During initial data collection, researchers had not yet optimized
the visualization and analysis methods, resulting in fewer breakdowns per sequence. At the time, the
focus was on achieving approximately half the shots as breakdowns.
Once researchers refined the process, they adjusted the charge voltage to ensure breakdowns
occurred at the peak of the voltage signal. This adjustment significantly reduced variation in the data,
improving the reliability of the statistical analysis.
These constraints highlight the challenges of collecting consistent breakdown data while maintaining
system safety and optimizing experimental conditions. By addressing these challenges, researchers
were able to refine their methods and improve the accuracy of their results.

3.5. Caeculus Introduction and Design

3.5.1. Physical Body and Components
Researchers required a pulsed power machine capable of running multiple experimental sequences
on various insulators. While the Z-Machine at Sandia National Laboratories could conduct
experiments, its average shot rate of once per day and the months required to change experimental
conditions posed significant limitations. Another pulsed power machine, Vulcan, offered a higher
shot rate of 2–3 times per day but featured an insulator stack too large for the current laser cleaning
system. To address these challenges, researchers utilized the Caeculus Pulsed Power Test Stand,
designed and purchased from Applied Physical Electronics Inc. (APELC). Operational since April
2023, Caeculus was specifically developed to test insulator performance against surface flashover in
smaller-scale experiments with faster shot rates.
Caeculus features a 30-stage, 50 kV charge Marx generator capable of producing a 500 kV output
with a 20 ns risetime. The Marx generator delivers a current of 20 mA and total energy of 393 J,
operating in an underdamped matching load configuration. The test stand can conduct experiments
within 30 minutes to an hour, making it ideal for rapid testing. The chassis includes an oil-insulated
section that allows the pulse to travel until it reaches an insulator wall separating it from the vacuum
chamber on the right-hand side of the machine. Figure 22 provides an image of the Caeculus test
stand, with the oil section and vacuum chamber labeled.

3.6. Innovative Insulator Cleaning Techniques
To address debris accumulation on insulator surfaces, researchers proposed two novel cleaning
techniques that utilize intensified light to remove contaminants: Pulsed Laser Cleaning (PLC) and
Flash Lamp Annealing (FLA). Dr. Aaron Van Tassle at Sandia National Laboratories proposed the
PLC method, while Dr. Mark Losego at the Georgia Institute of Technology introduced the FLA

 38

technique. Both methods were tested after establishing baseline experimental performance on virgin
samples prior to debris coating.

3.6.1. PLC Specifications
The Pulsed Laser Cleaning (PLC) method was designed to deliver effective energy for debris
removal while minimizing thermal damage to the insulator surface. Researchers employed a
Continuum Powerlite PL-9030 Nd:YAG laser for this process, operating at a wavelength (λ) of 1064
nm and a frequency of 30 Hz, with a maximum energy output of 1.6 J per pulse. The experiments
utilized a 12 mm wide laser beam turned down to delivering 300 mJ per pulse, resulting in a total
power output of 9 W and staying far below the surface damage threshold of Rexolite. After testing
alternative wavelengths of 355 nm and 532 nm, researchers determined that 1064 nm was the most
efficient for energy delivery and material ablation.
To control the laser beam, researchers used a Galvanometer 2-Axis VantagePro® scan head,
supported by digital processing via a Thorlabs DCB210-Y1. The scan head was managed by an
Asurfboard-2 XY2-100 controller from Nutfield Technology, which operated using wave runner
interface software. The laser beam occupied the full 10 mm aperture of the scan head, with a
scanning speed set to achieve 50% beam overlap between pulses at 30 Hz (125 mm/s). Each
cleaning session consisted of two passes across the 45° interface of the sample, completing the
process in approximately 10 seconds.
Researchers estimated that scaling this cleaning method to the Z-Machine Insulator Stack, which
spans 21 m², would require less than one hour at a frequency of 5 kHz. Figure 26 shows the laser
system used for PLC, while Figure 27 illustrates the experimental setup during cleaning sessions.

 Figure 26: The Continuum Powerlite PL-9030 Nd:YAG Laser used for pulsed laser cleaning.

 Figure 27: Nd:YAG laser setup during cleaning session.
Although Figure 27 depicts green light at wavelengths around 532 nm, researchers exclusively used
1064 nm for all cleaning passes. The Nd:YAG laser system operates on fundamental (1064 nm),
second (532 nm), and third (355 nm) harmonics. The green light observed in the figure is residual
leakage from harmonic mirror separators. Researchers calculated that the residual green light power
was 12 mW, negligible compared to the 9 W power of the 1064 nm light. The 532 nm and 355 nm
wavelengths were determined to be 2x and 4x less powerful than 1064 nm, respectively, making
them less efficient for material ablation.
Figure 28 shows the effectiveness of PLC on debris-coated samples before and after cleaning. These
samples qualitatively confirmed that the laser system successfully ablated material from the sample
surface. By removing debris accumulation, PLC eliminates conditions that violate Stygar’s stochastic
process assumption, allowing the insulator to perform consistently and adhere to the established
probability model.

 Figure 28: Rexolite samples before and after pulsed laser cleaning.

Through these experiments, researchers validated the PLC method as an effective cleaning
technique for insulator surfaces, demonstrating its ability to restore insulator performance while
minimizing damage.

3.6.2. FLA Specifications
The Flash Lamp Annealing (FLA) technique employs short, intense light pulses to heat and clean
the material surface. Unlike Pulsed Laser Cleaning (PLC), which focuses on a single point, FLA
applies uniform cleaning across a much larger area. This characteristic allows researchers to clean

 40

multiple samples simultaneously during a single session, significantly improving efficiency.
Researchers utilized the PulseForge Invent Machine, shown in Figure 29, at the Georgia Institute of
Technology to conduct FLA experiments. The machine operated at a maximum voltage of 950 V,
delivering a total energy of 3 J/cm² per pulse, with peak power reaching 35 kW/cm². Each pulse
lasted 60 μs, and researchers applied two pulses per cleaning session.
 It is important to note that the FLA nominally delivers broad spectral light that spans from the
near IR to the UV and that material interactions with this light generally require some amount of EM
absorption over this wavelength range. In the case of dendrite cleaning attempts discussed later we
applied pigments, specifically a sharpie brand black pen, to the polymer surface in an attempt to
increase the photothermal conversion and achieve the “healing” results desired.

 Figure 29: The model of the PulseForge Invent Machine used at the Georgia Institute of
 Technology for flashlamp annealing (FLA).
FLA pulses span a diverse spectral range, including near-infrared (IR) to ultraviolet (UV)
wavelengths. Metal debris on the insulator surface absorbs this light, initiating light-matter
interactions that effectively ablate the surface coating. This mechanism enables FLA to remove
contaminants efficiently while maintaining the integrity of the insulator material.

4 RESULTS AND DISCUSSION

4.1 Baselining and Determining Breakdown
Researchers analyzed raw V-Dot data collected through LabView and the oscilloscope to determine
breakdown events during testing. The V-Dot data represents the derivative of the voltage (dV/ dT),
providing critical information about voltage behavior over time. After ensuring that system
diagnostics were properly calibrated, researchers categorized, baselined, and analyzed the data to
identify breakdown points.
The LabView program Switcharoo.exe, shown in Figure 23, stored diagnostic data in a CSV file as a
structured array, with additional details organized into fields. Researchers used a custom program to
consolidate each structured array into a single output structure containing information such as time,
signal names, and sample names. To baseline the data, they identified the noise floor and starting
index. The noise floor was set at 1% of the maximum signal value, while the starting index was
defined as the first point where the signal magnitude exceeded three times the noise floor.
The baseline area was set at 5% of the signal starting index, and the mean of this area was subtracted
from the waveform to eliminate offsets and drift. To identify breakdown points, MATLAB
calculated the average voltage rise rate from the start of the voltage increase to its peak. The
program then analyzed the raw V-Dot data to detect points where the derivative (dV/dT) became
negative and reached a magnitude 75% of the average voltage rise rate. Once this threshold was
crossed, the program flagged the point as a potential breakdown. Within the region of this point,
MATLAB identified the exact breakdown point as the location where the negative dV/dT value
reached at least one-third of the average voltage rise rate.

 4.1.2 Post-Breakdown Details
After a breakdown event, researchers observed a strong voltage drop followed by an immediate rise,
accompanied by oscillations. These oscillations occur as the plasma channel forms and interacts with
the resistance from the series resistor. The voltage does not drop immediately to zero because the
system continues to discharge the remaining stored capacitance from the Marx tube.
The delayed voltage drop is determined by the RC time constant, which governs the discharge
behavior. Additionally, the flashover channel does not always achieve perfect conduction across the
surface, meaning certain sections may not arc over immediately, causing further delays. Once the
channel fully forms and the Marx tube finishes discharging its stored capacitance, the voltage returns
to its initial level at the start of the waveform.

 4.8 Applying to Dendrite Treatment

If a surface flashover breakdown is powerful enough, crystalline structures with branch-like
features known as dendrites can form in the insulator. This can create conductive pathways
substantially lowering the breakdown threshold and the dielectric performance. However,
previous studies on large-scale pulsed power machines show that the following breakdown will
not always occur at the dendrite location.

 63

A series of experiments on Caeculus were conducted on six 6 mm Rexolite samples to determine
the effect of dendrites on dielectric performance and whether novel cleaning techniques were
able to recover flashover resistance. This included three samples (RB14, RB15, RB17) that were
to undergo FLA and three that were to undergo PLC (RB16, RB18, RB20). The testing
sequences consisted of an initial series of 15 shots on virgin samples to establish a baseline.
After the series resistor above the load was shorted on Caeculus to allow greater current delivery,
five shots were taken on each sample to create dendrites. After the shorts were removed, a post-
dendrite sequence of 15 shots were taken on Caeculus to confirm performance worsened after
creating dendrites. Finally, each sample was taken to be cleaned with FLA or PLC respectively
followed by a final post-cleaning sequence of 15 shots on Caeculus. The final sequence set was
to be compared to the baseline sequence and post-dendrite sequence to see if the cleaning was
effective.

The post-dendrite testing was unable to have a statistically different change in insulator
performance for all samples but one. Without confirming that dendrites were able to decrease
performance to 95% confidence, it was unable to determine if the cleaning techniques could
make a difference.

The statistical data tells that the dendrite testing design needs to be amended to first find and
generate a difference in dielectric performance after creating dendrites. Connecting back to
earlier studies on larger pulsed power systems, the presence of a dendrite does not necessarily
mean a flashover will occur there. Although it does create a conductive pathway, further analysis
should be done if this decreases the overall insulator performance over a distribution. This work
is more focused on dendrite and tracking effects rather than that of novel insulator refurbishment
techniques.

The current PLC system cannot effectively treat concentrated depositions of metal back to
original flashover resistance, supported by the CDF data for RB16. This deposition had caused
the post-dendrite sequence to not overlap confidence intervals with the original distribution. The
post-PLC distribution only had confidence intervals overlapping with the post-dendrite sequence,
stating the refurbishment was not strong enough to return to the original level. This parallels
scenarios on large-scale pulsed power machines were pieces of the grading ring or electrode ring
lodges into the insulator stack. Further work on the PLC system is necessary for this scenario,
possibly increasing application strength of the laser.

The optical microscope images of RB15 support that FLA can treat the dendrite itself. The after
image shows the dendrite not visible compared to the before image. This tells that the current
FLA cleaning technique has the potential to remove dendrites, however, visual confirmation
cannot state the actual dielectric performance of the insulator. Future work should still take
qualitative images before and after treatment.

 4.9 Surface Characterization for FLA and PLC
Before analyzing the electrical performance of the cleaned samples, researchers conducted
characterization studies to evaluate the effectiveness of metal coating ablation and the impact on
sample surfaces. These studies included Energy Dispersive X-ray Spectroscopy (EDX), X-ray
Photoelectron Spectroscopy (XPS), and Profilometry. All characterization experiments were
performed on 8 mm tall, 5.67 cm diameter Rexolite samples.

 68

 4.9.1 EDX Data for FLA
Researchers used EDX to quantify the effectiveness of FLA in removing metal coatings, specifically
iron (Fe), from the sample surface. Figure 75 and Figure 76 present EDX data showing Fe atomic
percentage (atom%, at%) as a function of pulse length (μs) and the number of pulses applied.

 Figure 75: EDX data showing the effectiveness of FLA pulse length (μs) in removing Fe atom%.

 Figure 76: EDX data showing the effectiveness of the number of FLA pulses in removing Fe
 atom%.

Figure 76 reveals that Fe at% dropped significantly at a pulse length of 60 μs. However, increasing
the pulse length to 70 μs caused the sample to burn, indicating that 60 μs is the optimal pulse length
for cleaning without damaging the material. Figure 76 shows that Fe at% decreased dramatically
after two 60 μs pulses and then leveled off, suggesting that additional pulses beyond two do not
significantly improve cleaning effectiveness.
These findings confirm that FLA effectively removes nearly all metal content from coated samples
under optimal conditions. The data also highlight the importance of selecting appropriate pulse
lengths and the number of pulses to maximize cleaning efficiency while avoiding material damage.

Researchers conducted profilometry analysis to determine whether successive laser scans during
Pulsed Laser Cleaning (PLC) ablated material from the insulator sample itself. This analysis provided
critical insights into potential sample damage or surface changes resulting from the cleaning process.
Using the same method described in section 4.9.2, researchers divided a virgin Rexolite sample
(neither debris-coated nor previously experimented on) into five regions corresponding to 10, 50,
100, 200, and 500 laser passes, respectively. A full 3D scan of the sample was performed to measure
surface height, accompanied by vertical scans to analyze changes in surface profile data.
The 3D scan results revealed no significant change in surface height across the regions, as indicated
by the stagnant color gradient throughout the scan. If the laser ablation had removed Rexolite
material, the scan would have displayed horizontal color stripes indicating varying depths across the

regions. Figure 81 illustrates the 3D profilometry scan, showing that the highest point on the sample

 4.10 Strategies to Address Confidence Interval Issues
Wide confidence intervals between the lower and upper bounds were a recurring issue in many CDF
plots and error bar charts. This variation raised concerns about the reliability of the data and its
impact on conclusions. As discussed in Section 4.2, the number of breakdowns significantly
influences the size of confidence intervals due to the Maximum Likelihood Estimator (MLE) used in
CDF plots.
To address this issue, researchers tested Rexolite sample RB6, which underwent an extended shot
range of 30 shots instead of the standard 15. Additionally, RB6 was a 6 mm tall sample rather than 8
mm tall, resulting in larger applied electric fields (E=V/dE = V/dE=V/d) and increasing the
probability of flashover. These adjustments made it easier to achieve breakdowns, reducing statistical
variation and narrowing confidence intervals.

 4.11.2 Observations on Confidence Interval Improvements
The confidence intervals for RB6 were noticeably narrower compared to previous samples,
particularly for the 30-shot sequences shown in Figure 84 and Figure 86. The error bars were
approximately 25–30 γSM wide, confirming that increasing the number of breakdown shots
improves the accuracy of the MLE and reduces statistical variation. Additionally, the shorter height
of RB6 (6 mm) increased the likelihood of flashover, further contributing to narrower confidence
intervals.
These observations suggest that the data from RB6 aligns closely with prior results for 8 mm
Rexolite samples, with narrower intervals providing a more precise range for γSM . This
improvement highlights the importance of optimizing testing sequences to achieve more reliable and
statistically robust results.

 4.11.3 Potential Cause of Shift in RB6 Data
Researchers identified a critical difference in the placement of the RB6 sample during Mykonos
debris-coating, which may have contributed to its inability to recover flashover resistance post-PLC.
Unlike other samples that were placed on the lower rim of the Mykonos chamber, RB6 was
positioned on the upper outer rim, as shown in Figure 92. The upper rim is less shielded by metal
and is more directly exposed to the exploding load, resulting in greater debris deposition on the
sample.

  Figure 92: Location of RB6 placement on the Mykonos chamber. This is at the upper rim, more
 exposed to debris-coating compared to all other samples that were placed on the lower rim.

This increased exposure to debris-coating likely caused RB6 to accumulate more metal than other
samples, potentially explaining why it was the only Rexolite sample to fail in recovering flashover
resistance post-PLC. This highlights the importance of sample placement and its impact on testing
outcomes. It also underscores the need for systems that can quantify the level of metal deposited
and ablated during each sequence to reduce sample-to-sample variance. Despite RB6’s failure, the
results do not necessarily invalidate PLC as an effective cleaning method for Rexolite, as all 8 mm
tall Rexolite samples successfully recovered their flashover resistance. RB6’s results may simply
reflect testing variation rather than a fundamental limitation of PLC.

 4.12 Interpretation and Comparison of Cleaning Techniques Data
Researchers compared the effectiveness of Flash Lamp Annealing (FLA) and Pulsed Laser Cleaning
(PLC) in recovering insulator flashover resistance. FLA successfully retained the same dielectric
performance for only one sample at 95% confidence, statistically failing for the other three samples
tested. These results suggest that FLA is not a reliable cleaning method for restoring original
flashover resistance in either Rexolite or acrylic insulators. However, voltage and γSM data indicate
that FLA was still able to clean the debris-coated samples sufficiently to allow them to insulate again,
albeit not to their original performance levels.
PLC, on the other hand, was statistically effective for all Rexolite samples and only failed for the
acrylic sample (PA1). All PLC-treated Rexolite samples retained median γSM values above Stygar’s
theoretical baseline (γSM =248), further supporting PLC’s effectiveness in restoring insulator
performance to the established threshold used in other applications. Even the PLC-treated PA1
sample, which was statistically different, succeeded in insulating, indicating that PLC was able to
recover some dielectric performance, though not fully.

 80

The 95% confidence bounds for 8 mm samples showed large γSM variance but had similar median
lower bounds across samples that recovered dielectric strength. This suggests that for most testing
scenarios that do not involve extreme applied voltages, the dielectric strength before and after
debris-coating and cleaning remains comparable. These findings support the effectiveness of
cleaning techniques for typical testing conditions.
The differences in performance between acrylic and Rexolite insulators may be attributed to their
material properties. Acrylic, being a thermoplastic, has linear branched polymer chains that are not
cross-linked. This structure makes acrylic susceptible to thermal damage, causing it to soften and
eventually melt under excess heat from PLC and FLA. Rexolite, a thermoset material with cross-
linked polymer chains, exhibits superior thermal properties, preventing it from melting and allowing
it to withstand heat applications from PLC and FLA.

5 EXPERIMENTAL CONCLUSIONS

5.1 Takeaways from Data Across Cleaning Techniques
Pulsed Laser Cleaning (PLC) successfully recovered original insulator flashover resistance for all 8
mm tall Rexolite samples but failed for the 8 mm acrylic sample and the 6 mm tall Rexolite sample.
Flash Lamp Annealing (FLA) recovered original flashover resistance for only one 8 mm Rexolite
sample, failing for all other samples tested. Despite these failures, all PLC-treated samples retained
median γSM values around Stygar’s theoretical baseline (γSM =248) or greater, indicating that PLC
may be an effective cleaning method for Rexolite but not for acrylic.
FLA, on the other hand, was not effective as a standalone cleaning method for either Rexolite or
acrylic. To conclusively determine the effectiveness of these cleaning techniques, researchers
emphasized the need for a monitoring system to quantify the amount of debris material removed
during each cleaning session.

5.2 Experimental and Statistical Limitations
The study faced several limitations, including sample-to-sample variation and insufficient breakdown
data for 8 mm samples. Different samples exhibited widely varying performance during pre-
treatment baseline sequences, making it challenging to deliver results applicable on a global scale.
Additionally, there was no way to measure how well each cleaning round worked for each sample.
For example, PLC may have worked exceptionally well on one sample and completely failed on
another, but the amount of material ablated was not quantified.
Similarly, the amount of metal deposited onto each sample during debris-coating was not measured,
leaving room for variation due to differences in placement or Mykonos testing parameters. Sample
conditioning further complicated data collection, as some samples were unable to achieve sufficient
breakdowns, leading to skewed variation in electrical data. For instance, RA9 exhibited much smaller
confidence bounds compared to RA10.

5.3 Characterization Data Takeaways
Characterization data provided valuable insights into the cleaning techniques. Energy Dispersive X-
ray Spectroscopy (EDX) results from the Georgia Institute of Technology showed that FLA reached
peak efficiency in removing metal content. Fe content dropped to zero after using 50 μm and two
pulses, which were the parameters used for cleaning.
PLC demonstrated potential for improvement, as metal content did not drop to zero for chromium
or iron oxide. However, successive laser passes effectively reduced metal content, confirming the
cleaning method’s effectiveness. Profilometry data for Rexolite revealed that PLC did not cause
significant insulator ablation, even after 500 laser passes. The greatest difference observed on the 3D
profilometry profile was approximately 20 μm, translating to 40 nm per pass—three orders of
magnitude less than the 38 μm per pass observed with 240-grit sandpaper [23].
These findings highlight PLC’s advantage over hand sanding, which removes significantly more
material per pass. While sanding is expected to continue removing material with additional passes,
PLC offers a more controlled and less damaging cleaning process.

 82

5.4 Final Remarks
The study underscores the importance of optimizing cleaning techniques for insulators in pulsed
power systems. While PLC demonstrated effectiveness for Rexolite, further research is needed to
improve its performance for acrylic insulators. Additionally, implementing monitoring systems to
quantify debris removal and ablation during cleaning sessions will help reduce sample-to-sample
variation and improve the reliability of results. These advancements will pave the way for safer,
standardized, and efficient cleaning methods for insulators in high-voltage applications.

 83

6 Future Work

6.1 Debris-Removal Monitoring System
The most critical addition to future research is the development of an ablation monitoring system
capable of recording the amount of debris material present on the sample before cleaning and the
amount removed during the cleaning process. This system would address the sample-to-sample
variation observed in experiments, such as the conditioning effect noticed in RA10, where the
sample built up resistance and did not break down even at a 35 kV charge. Additionally, it would
help quantify potential differences in debris-coating levels caused by Mykonos.
Without a reliable method to measure the amount of metal coating before and after cleaning,
researchers cannot verify the effectiveness of the cleaning process. For instance, a PLC or FLA
cleaning process may perform exceptionally well in one experiment due to unknown factors or
underperform in another due to different conditions. A monitoring system would eliminate this
uncertainty, providing consistent data on cleaning efficiency and enabling researchers to optimize
cleaning techniques for insulators in pulsed power systems.

6.2 Scaling to Large-Scale Pulsed Power Systems
Future testing should transition to large-scale accelerators, such as the Z-Machine, to determine
whether the same effects observed in smaller samples apply to larger insulators. The samples used in
this study are relatively small compared to the insulator stacks on the Z-Machine, necessitating the
development of an appropriate laser system capable of scaling these experiments to larger
dimensions.
An automated PLC or FLA system would offer several advantages over robotic hand-sanding
systems. Hand-sanding creates holes in the insulator, generating significant amounts of dust and
soot that require additional cleaning. This process also necessitates frequent wiping with isopropanol
wipes and a constant supply of sandpaper. In contrast, PLC and FLA do not damage or ablate the
insulator material, as demonstrated by profilometry data for PLC. These techniques would prevent
holes or damage to the insulator stack and leave far less residue, making them more suitable for
large-scale applications.

Existing motorized articulation systems would allow for a linear motion of as much as 120cm/s. In
all cases the limiting factor becomes the PRF of the laser source. With the Continuum 30 Hz laser,
a galvanometer scan head allows a positioning time sufficiently high for the 50% beam overlap that
we designed in the system. The fluence calculated for our current cleaning method is >250mJ/cm2.
This system will allow for a coverage of 21 m2, the entire surface area of all 26 insulator rings in the
Z center section in 4.97 hr. A summary of the calculations are shown in Figure 93 . Vertical
translation of the scan head and later motions will add an addition 1.5 hours resulting in a total
cleaning time of ~6.5 hours for the Z center section.

In order to decrease this cleaning speed, we propose using a Trumpf fiber laser shown Figure 94.
This laser allows us to produce 100mJ pulses at up to 10kHz PRF. The power is lower, 100mJ

far below the surface damage threshold of Rexolite, we decrease the beam diameter to 6mm,
bringing the optical fluence to ~350mJ/cm2.

 6.3 Automation
Automating insulator cleaning processes is an essential step toward reducing human involvement
and improving efficiency. The hand-sanding process is physically demanding, and a robotic or
automated system could alleviate this burden. Automation would require implementation of
machine learning or spectral monitoring, to continue cleaning until a sufficient reduction of
contamination is removed, with an emphasis on human factors and operational times to ensure
seamless integration into existing workflows.
One challenge reported by Z-Machine workers is the difficulty of reaching the insulator-grading ring
triple-junction during hand-sanding. This issue becomes even more problematic if an object is
embedded in the Rexolite. An automated system equipped with an effective vision system could
detect debris material in these hard-to-reach spaces and quantify the level of contamination present.
Automation would not only improve the precision and consistency of cleaning processes but also
enhance safety by minimizing worker exposure to hazardous materials and reducing physical strain.

 6.4 Further Work on Insulator Cleaning Techniques
PLC requires further trials to explore different wavelengths, pulse lengths, and settings to maximize
its ability to remove metal content without charring or damaging the sample. Future experiments
should include EDX and XPS characterization to provide clearer data on the amount of debris
present after cleaning. This needs to be combined with a spectral monitoring technique to verify all
material has been removed. The results from the RB6 sample, which was placed in a different
position in the Mykonos chamber show that the optimizing parameters for varying exposures will be
essential for successful implementation of this cleaning method into pulsed power systems.

Researchers should also investigate multi-step cleaning sequences that combine multiple techniques.
This approach is supported by the results for RB6, where laser cleaning followed by hand-sanding
effectively recovered dielectric strength. Since hand-sanding is an effective technique that is unlikely
to be completely replaced, studying its impact after PLC or FLA would provide valuable insights.
Alternative methods for confirming the recovery of flashover resistance should also be explored.
Current analysis relies on visual comparisons of the median and respective bounds, but statistical
tests for comparing Weibull distributions could offer more robust conclusions. Kolmogorov-
Smirnov and t-tests are unsuitable for this data because it is not normalized and represents an entire
Weibull distribution. Recent models have proposed using Monte Carlo simulations to analyze the
ratio between the parameters of two Weibull moduli, offering a discrete statistical test that could
complement existing methods [30].

7 FINAL REMARKS
The future work outlined in this section highlights the need for continued innovation in insulator
cleaning techniques and systems. By addressing the challenges of debris removal monitoring, scaling
to large-scale systems, treating dendrite damage, and automating cleaning processes, researchers can
optimize the performance and reliability of insulators in pulsed power applications. These
advancements will pave the way for safer, more efficient, and standardized cleaning methods that
meet the demands of modern high-voltage systems.
