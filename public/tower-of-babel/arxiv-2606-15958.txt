I.0.1
The Canonical One-Form,
Oct 27

(The notes for
PIRSA:2510.0008
begin here.)

Figure 1:
Picture of the polar planimeter given in class. Also, watch
PIRSA:2510.0166

Let
\vec{N}_{\text{polar}}
be the unit vector field along which a displacement of the tracer would generate rotations of the wheel the fastest.
The change in the angle of the wheel after taking the tracer along a curve
\gamma
is therefore

\phi=\frac{1}{h}\int_{\gamma}\vec{N}_{\text{polar}}\cdot d\vec{r}

(1)

where

h=\text{radius of the meter wheel}.

(2)

Actually, the object being integrated

\lambda_{\text{polar}}=\vec{N}_{\text{polar}}\cdot d\vec{r}

(3)

is more naturally and more generally a one-form because it integrates the displacement vectors along the curve (what John Wheeler called “bongs of the bell”.)
However, for historical reasons it is common among scientists and engineers to formalize contour integrals with “vector calculus” notation.
In any case, we will soon enough end up treating the one-form directly.
We will call it the canonical one-form both because it is the very thing that defines how the planimeter works but also because it is analogous to Poincare’s canonical one-form in classical mechanics, also called the velocity one-form by Arnold.

Figure 2:
Diagram of the unit vector field given in class. Also, see
PIRSA:2510.0166
(time stamp 6:11.)

I.0.2
Deriving the Canonical One-form of the Polar Planimeter

Partially in terms of angular coördinates, the unit vector field can be clearly seen from the diagram to be

\displaystyle\vec{N}_{\text{polar}}\cdot d\vec{r}

\displaystyle=\sin(\alpha-\beta)dq+\cos(\alpha-\beta)dp

(4)

\displaystyle=(\sin\alpha\cos\beta-\cos\alpha\sin\beta)dq+(\cos\alpha\cos\beta+\sin\alpha\sin\beta)dp.

(5)

Defining half the distance between the tracer and the fixed point to be

a^{2}\equiv\frac{q^{2}+p^{2}}{4},

(6)

some basic trigonometry can be read off the diagram as well:

-\sin\beta=\frac{q}{2a}\hskip 50.0pt\text{,}\hskip 50.0pt\cos\beta=\frac{p}{2a}\hskip 50.0pt\text{,}

(7)

\sin\alpha=\frac{a}{R}\hskip 50.0pt\text{, and}\hskip 50.0pt\cos\alpha=\frac{1}{R}\sqrt{R^{2}-a^{2}}

(8)

where
R
is the length of either arm, which we are assuming are equal for simplicity.

The canonical one-form in Cartesian coördinates is therefore

\displaystyle\lambda_{\text{polar}}

\displaystyle\equiv\vec{N}_{\text{polar}}\cdot d\vec{r}

(9)

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{a}{R}\frac{p}{2a}+\frac{1}{R}\sqrt{R^{2}-a^{2}}\frac{q}{2a}\right)dq+\left(\frac{1}{R}\sqrt{R^{2}-a^{2}}\frac{p}{2a}-\frac{a}{R}\frac{q}{2a}\right)dp

(10)

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{p}{2R}+\frac{q}{2Ra}\sqrt{R^{2}-a^{2}}\right)dq+\left(\frac{p}{2Ra}\sqrt{R^{2}-a^{2}}-\frac{q}{2R}\right)dp

(11)

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{1}{2}p+\frac{q}{2a}\sqrt{R^{2}-a^{2}}\right)dq+\left(\frac{p}{2a}\sqrt{R^{2}-a^{2}}-\frac{1}{2}q\right)dp

(12)

\displaystyle=\frac{p\,dq-q\,dp}{2}+\frac{1}{2a}\sqrt{R^{2}-a^{2}}(p\,dp+q\,dq)

(13)

\displaystyle=\frac{p\,dq-q\,dp}{2}+\frac{1}{a}\sqrt{R^{2}-a^{2}}\,da^{2}

(14)

\displaystyle=\frac{p\,dq-q\,dp}{2}+2\sqrt{R^{2}-a^{2}}da

(15)

\displaystyle=\frac{p\,dq-q\,dp}{2}+dG_{\text{polar}}

(16)

\displaystyle=pdq+dF_{\text{polar}}

(17)

where

G_{\text{polar}}=R^{2}\left(2\theta+\sin 2\theta\right)\hskip 50.0pt\text{,}\hskip 50.0pt\sin\theta=\frac{a}{R}=\sqrt{\frac{p^{2}+q^{2}}{4R^{2}}}

(18)

and

F_{\text{polar}}=-\frac{qp}{2}+G_{\text{polar}}.

(19)

The exact expression for
F_{\text{polar}}
is not too important for what we are here to show, but it is nonetheless nice to just know what it is.

I.0.3
The Contact One-Form and Kinematic Transformations

The configuration space of the planimeter is 3-dimensional, two dimensions to locate the tracer and one more dimension for the angle of the wheel.
All displacements
\overline{X}
in this space that respect the planimeter must satisfy the constriant of being annihilated

\alpha_{\text{polar}}(\overline{X})=0

(20)

by the contact one-form

\alpha_{\text{polar}}\equiv\lambda_{\text{polar}}-h\,d\phi.

(21)

Such displacements will be called
kinematic
displacements.
In particular, if
X
is a vector in
(q,p)
-space, then

\overline{X}\equiv X+\frac{1}{h}\lambda(X)\partial_{\phi}

(22)

is a kinematic displacement.

This means if we move the tracer along
\partial_{q}
or
\partial_{p}
, then there must also be an accompanying movement in the wheel, generated by
\partial_{\phi}
.
It is clear that

\lambda_{\text{polar}}(\partial_{q})=p+F_{q}\hskip 50.0pt\text{and}\hskip 50.0pt\lambda_{\text{polar}}(\partial_{p})=F_{p}

(23)

where

F_{q}\equiv\frac{\partial F_{\text{polar}}}{\partial q}\hskip 50.0pt\text{and}\hskip 50.0ptF_{p}\equiv\frac{\partial F_{\text{polar}}}{\partial p}.

(24)

Therefore the lifted displacements that are annihilated by
\alpha_{\text{polar}}
are

h\overline{\partial_{q}}\equiv h\partial_{q}+(p+F_{q})\partial_{\phi}\hskip 50.0pt\text{and}\hskip 50.0pth\overline{\partial_{p}}\equiv h\partial_{p}+F_{p}\partial_{\phi}.

(25)

These diffeomorphisms do not commute.
Rather, their commutator is

[h\overline{\partial_{p}},h\overline{\partial_{q}}]=h\partial_{\phi}.

(26)

Further, the commutators to second order are all zero,

[\partial_{\phi},h\overline{\partial_{q}}]=[\partial_{\phi},h\overline{\partial_{p}}]=0.

(27)

Therefore, the physical motions of the planimeter are the displacements of a Weyl-Heisenberg group!

I.0.4
Canonical Transformations,
Oct 30

(The notes for
PIRSA:2510.0205
begin here.)

There is in fact another, deeper Weyl-Heisenberg group within the planimeter.

To motivate the definition of a Canonical Transformation, recall the Euler-Lagrange equation but express it as

p\equiv\left.\frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot{q}}\right|_{q,\dot{q}}\hskip 50.0pt\text{and}\hskip 50.0pt\dot{p}=\left.\frac{\partial L}{\partial q}\right|_{q,\dot{q}}.

(28)

These equations are equivalent to the one-form equation

\displaystyle dL

\displaystyle=\dot{p}\,dq+p\,d\dot{q}

(29)

\displaystyle=\lim_{\epsilon\to\infty}\frac{(p+\epsilon\dot{p})d(q+\epsilon\dot{q})-p\,dq}{\epsilon}.

(30)

Notice that the final expression is a kind of derivative.
It is a special case of what is known as the Lie derivative of the one-form
p\,dq
in the direction
d/dt
.

More generally, the Lie derivative
\mathcal{L}_{X}
with respect to the infinitesimal transformation generated by
X
of a differential form
\lambda(q,p)
on a two-dimensional manifold is

\mathcal{L}_{X}[\lambda]=\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\frac{\lambda(q+\epsilon X[q],p+\epsilon X[p])-\lambda(q,p)}{\epsilon}.

(31)

The Lie derivative can of course be defined for any number of dimensions.
In particular

\mathcal{L}_{X}[fdg]=X[f]dg+fdX[g].

(32)

Check that the Cartesian displacements of the planimeter tracer generate canonical transformations:

\mathcal{L}_{\partial_{q}}[\lambda_{\text{polar}}]=dF_{q}\hskip 50.0pt\text{and}\hskip 50.0pt\mathcal{L}_{\partial_{p}}[\lambda_{\text{polar}}]=d\left(q+F_{p}\right)

(33)

where the functions in their Lagrangians are shorthand for the partial derivatives,

F_{q}=\frac{\partial F_{\text{polar}}}{\partial q}\hskip 50.0pt\text{and}\hskip 50.0ptF_{p}=\frac{\partial F_{\text{polar}}}{\partial p}.

(34)

I.0.5
Contact Transformations

If
\alpha
is a contact one-form, a tangent vector
X
is said to generate a
contact transformation
if

\mathcal{L}_{X}[\alpha]=Z\alpha

(35)

for some function
Z
.
A contact transformation is said to be
strict
if
Z=0
.
Canonical transformations of
\lambda_{\text{polar}}
are equivalent to strict contact transformations of
\alpha_{\text{polar}}
because if
\mathcal{L}_{X}[\lambda_{\text{polar}}]=dL
then
\mathcal{L}_{X}[\alpha_{\text{polar}}]=0
where

\widehat{X}\equiv X+\frac{1}{h}L\partial_{\phi}.

(36)

Show this.

Therefore the two vector fields

\widehat{\partial_{q}}=\partial_{q}+\frac{1}{h}F_{q}\partial_{\phi}\hskip 50.0pt\text{and}\hskip 50.0pt\widehat{\partial_{p}}=\partial_{p}+\frac{1}{h}\left(q+F_{p}\right)\partial_{\phi}

(37)

generate (strict) contact transformations — that is

\mathcal{L}_{\widehat{\partial_{q}}}[\alpha]=\mathcal{L}_{\widehat{\partial_{p}}}[\alpha]=0.

(38)

Show that these generators have first-order Lie bracket

[h\widehat{\partial_{p}},h\widehat{\partial_{q}}]=-h\partial_{\phi}

(39)

and second-order Lie brackets

[\partial_{\phi},\widehat{\partial_{p}}]=[\partial_{\phi},\widehat{\partial_{p}}]=0.

(40)

This establishes that the polar planimeter is a perfect embodiment of the Weyl-Heisenberg group!

I.0.6
Hamiltonians

Observe that for any contact transformation
\widehat{X}

\displaystyle\alpha(\widehat{X})

\displaystyle\equiv\alpha(X+\frac{1}{h}L\partial_{\phi})

(41)

\displaystyle=\alpha\Big(-\frac{1}{h}\lambda(X)\partial_{\phi}+\frac{1}{h}L\partial_{\phi}\Big)

(42)

\displaystyle=\big(\lambda(X)-L\big)\alpha(-\partial_{\phi})

(43)

\displaystyle=\lambda(X)-L.

(44)

This can be recognized as a generalization of the Legendre transformation!
For the standard Legendre transform, just consider
\lambda=p\,dq
and
X=d/dt
.
This means
\alpha(\widehat{X})
is the Hamiltonian!

In particular the Hamiltonians of the canonical displacements are

\alpha(\widehat{\partial_{q}})=p\hskip 50.0pt\text{,}\hskip 50.0pt\alpha(\widehat{\partial_{p}})=-q\hskip 50.0pt\text{, and}\hskip 50.0pt\alpha(-\partial_{\phi})=h1.

(45)

Quantum Measuring Instruments

II.1
The system-meter interaction model and Kraus operators,
Nov 06

(The notes for
PIRSA:2511.0086
begin here.)

Figure 3:
Diagram of the system-meter interaction. Drawn in class.

Indirect measurements are modeled by a system-meter interaction, where the system of interest is not measured directly but rather interacts with a meter and then it is the meter that is measured directly.

We will be mostly interested in measurements of meter position, where we imagine a real number,
x
, (rather than an integer) is registered to within some arbitrarily small but not infinite precision
dx\ni x
.

The meter consists of a “pointer” that prepared in a normalized initial pure state,
|0\rangle_{\text{M}}
.
The interaction between the meter and the system is a joint unitary transformation,
\mathrm{U}_{\text{int}}
.
The meter then terminates in a measurement that registers the real c-number ‘
x
’ represented by the linear functional
\sqrt{dx}\langle x|_{\text{M}}
.

II.1.1
Comments on the registration of a continuous variable

In analogy to separable bases, an expression like

\text{``}\int_{\mathbb{R}}dx|x\rangle\!\langle x|_{M}=1_{\text{M}}\text{''}

(46)

is often written down.
However, it should be kept in mind that
|x\rangle_{\text{M}}
will not need to exist as a physical state for our calculations.
Indeed, such an infinitely precise state is considered impossible to prepare.
As such, the prepared initial pointer state
|0\rangle_{\text{M}}
must not be confused with the (impossible to prepare and therefore nonexistent) “state” vector
|x=0\rangle_{\text{M}}
.

On the other hand, the linear functional
\langle x|_{\text{M}}
is required to exist for our calculations.
This brings up a subtle point about what it means to register ‘
x
’ because it is equally impossible to store such a number in memory.
However, this subtle detail is managed by
dx
.

For further details about the mathematical details of directly measuring position, I recommend reading arXiv:2510.13980 [page 14, section II.A] by me, “The role of the rigged Hilbert space in Quantum Mechanics” (2005) by Rafael de la Madrid, and “Probabilistic and Statistical Aspects of Quantum Theory” by Alexander Holevo.

That said and done, the essense of it all is that we will need the operators
Q_{\text{M}}
and
\frac{1}{\hbar}P_{\text{M}}
defined by the properties

\langle x|_{M}Q_{\text{M}}=x\langle x|_{M}\hskip 50.0pt\text{and}\hskip 50.0pt\langle x|_{M}\frac{1}{\hbar}P_{\text{M}}=-i\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\langle x|_{M}

(47)

which clearly satsify the Weyl-Heisenberg canonical commutation relation,

[Q_{\text{M}},P_{\text{M}}]=i1_{\text{M}}.

(48)

II.1.2
Back to indirect measurement

Returning to the indirect measurement, the probability of registering ‘
x
’ within the infinitesimal region
dx
is, according to the Born rule,

\displaystyle dxP(x)

\displaystyle=dx\sum_{\phi}\sum_{\psi}p_{\psi}\Big|\langle\phi|_{\text{S}}\langle x|_{\text{M}}\mathrm{U}_{\text{int}}|\psi\rangle_{\text{S}}|0\rangle_{\text{M}}\Big|^{2}.

(49)

The “average over initial states and sum over final states”
\sum_{\psi}p_{\psi}\sum_{\phi}
is a mantra that field theorists are usually taught very early in their training.

A tool that is very useful for conceptualizing this calculation is the so-called
Kraus operator

\Omega_{x}\equiv\sqrt{dx}K_{x}\equiv\sqrt{dx}\langle x|_{\text{M}}\mathrm{U}_{\text{int}}|0\rangle_{\text{M}},

(50)

where I haven’t come up with a good name yet for the “Kraus operator”
K_{x}
without the square-root of the measure in it.

With this tool, we can massage the probability to register ‘
x
’ into the form

\displaystyle dxP(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\phi}\sum_{\psi}p_{\psi}\Big|\langle\phi|_{\text{S}}\Omega_{x}|\psi\rangle_{\text{S}}\Big|^{2}

(51)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\phi}\sum_{\psi}p_{\psi}\langle\phi|_{\text{S}}\Omega_{x}|\psi\rangle_{\text{S}}\langle\psi|_{\text{S}}\Omega_{x}^{\dagger}|\phi\rangle_{\text{S}}

(52)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\phi}\langle\phi|_{\text{S}}\Omega_{x}\left(\sum_{\psi}p_{\psi}|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|_{\text{S}}\right)\Omega_{x}^{\dagger}|\phi\rangle_{\text{S}}

(53)

\displaystyle=\tr_{\text{S}}\!\big(\Omega_{x}\rho\Omega_{x}^{\dagger}\big)

(54)

where

\rho\equiv\sum_{\psi}p_{\psi}|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|_{\text{S}}

(55)

is the usual (von Neumann), density operator, in this case normalized such that
\tr_{\text{S}}\rho=1
.

Note that by construction, the Kraus operators satsify a type of
completeness relation
,

\sum_{x}\Omega_{x}^{\dagger}\Omega_{x}=\int_{\mathbb{R}}dx\,K_{x}^{\dagger}K_{x}=1_{\text{S}}.

(56)

II.1.3
General definitions

A
generalized measurement
is any set of operators
\{\Omega_{x}\}
which satisfy the completeness relation (equation
56
).
The elements of a generalized measurement are called
measurement operators
,
back actions
, or
Kraus operators
.
The index
x
is called the
register
.
Upon registering
x
, a state
\rho
is understood to update to transition to the state
\Omega_{x}\rho\Omega_{x}^{\dagger}
.
There is a bit of flexibility as to where and when the state is normalized.
When simulating state evolution, it is more efficient to always renormalize the state, thus making the update rule nonlinear.
When solving for the generalized mesurements that result from sequences of generalized measurements, one must keep the linear update rule.

Every generalized measurement defines a
generalized observables
or
positive-operator-valued measure
, usually called a
POVM
, which is the set of positive operators
\{\Omega_{x}^{\dagger}\Omega_{x}\}
such that the completeness relation (equation
56
) is satisfied.
The POVM is less information than the generalized measurement because it does not specify how the states are updated.
In particular, the generalized measurements
\{U_{x}\Omega_{x}\}
all define the same POVM for any set of unitaries
\{U_{x}\}
.
The elements of a POVM are called
effects
or more often just
POVM elements
.
Given a POVM
\{E_{x}\}
, the
generalized Luders-von Neumann rule
is to assume the generalized measurement consists of its positive square-roots,
\{\sqrt{E_{x}}\}
.
Therefore, any generalized measurement with positive Kraus operators will be called a
Luders measurement
.

It is also very useful to consider the update rule
\rho\mapsto\Omega_{x}\rho\Omega_{x}^{\dagger}
more explicitly as a superoperator,

\mathcal{O}_{x}\equiv{\mathcal{O}\hskip-5.74997pt\raisebox{0.5pt}{$\bm{\cdot}$}\hskip 2.74994pt}[K_{x}]\equiv K_{x}\odot K_{x}^{\dagger}

(57)

where
\odot
denotes a tensor product with the implied action
A\odot B^{\dagger}(\rho)\equiv A\rho B^{\dagger}
.
Superoperators such as
{\mathcal{O}\hskip-5.74997pt\raisebox{0.5pt}{$\bm{\cdot}$}\hskip 2.74994pt}[K_{x}]
which represent physical state updates are generally called
operations
.
The set of operations
\{O_{x}\}
which satisfy the completeness relation (equation
56
) is called an
instrument
.
The elements of an instrument are called
selective operations
or just
instrument elements
.
The selective operations we consider here,
{\mathcal{O}\hskip-5.74997pt\raisebox{0.5pt}{$\bm{\cdot}$}\hskip 2.74994pt}[K_{x}]
, are said to be
Kraus-rank-one
.
Generally, the selective operations are positive mixtures of Kraus-rank-one operations.
Such mixtures represent imperfections in the instrument.

Often the terms “instrument”, “generalized measurement”, and just “measurement” will be used interchangably.
Similarly, the terms “selective operation”, “instrument element”, and “Kraus operator” will often be used interchangably.
Of course, one should always understand the difference between an operator and a superoperator, but otherwise the distinct terminology can be relaxed in certain circumstances.

Given an instrument
\{\mathcal{O}_{x}\}
, the superoperator

\mathcal{Z}\equiv\sum_{x}\mathcal{O}_{x}

(58)

is called the
total operation
, the
nonselective operation
, the
decoherence channel
, or just the
channel
, and often haphazardly just
the operation
.
The channel has two defining properties and is said to be
completely positive
or “
CP
” and
trace-preserving
or “
TP
”.

II.2
The Gaussian Luders Instrument

The meter preparation we will be most interested in is the Gaussian pure state, with wavefunction

\sqrt{dx}\left\langle x|0\right\rangle_{\text{M}}=\sqrt{\frac{dx}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^{2}}}e^{-x^{2}/2\sigma^{2}}}.

(59)

The two most important examples of such a meter preparation are the center-of-mass position of an ion sent through a Stern-Gerlach apparatus and a mode of the electric field in vacuum.
Such a state is most efficiently defined by the linear, first-order differential equation

\left(\frac{x}{2\sigma}+\sigma\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\right)\left\langle x|0\right\rangle_{\text{M}}=0,

(60)

which is equivalent to the more abstract annihilation property

a_{\text{M}}|0\rangle_{\text{M}}=0\hskip 50.0pt\text{where}\hskip 50.0pta_{\text{M}}\equiv\frac{1}{2\sigma}Q_{\text{M}}+i\frac{\sigma}{\hbar}P_{\text{M}}.

(61)

The first system-meter interaction we will consider is the
controlled displacement
,

\mathrm{U}_{\text{int}}=e^{-i\frac{\sigma}{\hbar}P_{\text{M}}\otimes 2X_{\text{S}}\theta}

(62)

where the parameter
\theta
is either proportional to the interaction time or square-root of the interaction time, depending on depending on whether the interactions are ballistic or diffusive.
More about this will be explained when we get to decoherence.
In the meantime, we can calculate the Kraus operators:

\displaystyle K_{x}

\displaystyle\equiv\langle x|_{\text{M}}\mathrm{U}_{\text{int}}|0\rangle_{\text{M}}

(63)

\displaystyle=e^{-2X_{\text{S}}\theta\sigma\partial_{x}}\left\langle x|0\right\rangle_{\text{M}}

(64)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^{2}}}e^{-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}(x-2\sigma\theta X_{\text{S}})^{2}}}

(65)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(2\pi\sigma^{2})^{1/4}}e^{-\frac{1}{4\sigma^{2}}(x-2\sigma\theta X_{\text{S}})^{2}}

(66)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(2\pi\sigma^{2})^{1/4}}e^{-\theta^{2}(\frac{x}{2\sigma\theta}-X_{\text{S}})^{2}}

(67)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^{2}}}e^{-x^{2}/2\sigma^{2}}}e^{\frac{x}{\sigma}\theta X_{\text{S}}-\theta^{2}X_{\text{S}}^{2}}.

(68)

Since the Kraus-operators are positive Hermitian, this instrument is Luders.

These Kraus operators are the first examples of the “universal” expressions I emphasized during the introduction of this class.
What “universal” means in this case is that these Kraus operators, their completeness relation, their POVM, and their decoherence channel are already defined without having specified anything about the system observable,
X_{\text{S}}
, such as its spectrum or even the Hilbert space it acts on.
Because of this universal nature, these Kraus operators are said to represent
positive transformations
, which I will explain more soon.

In the case of the Stern-Gerlach experiment,
X_{\text{S}}=J_{z}
.
This is still considered a universal expression because all of the instrument-related quantities still make sense without specifying the Hilbert space, which in this case means specifying the quantum spin number
j
.
By saying “
X_{\text{S}}=J_{z}
”, what we are actually doing is adding the commutation relations for rotation into the mix.

Now suppose the spectrum
is
specified and that the spectrum is discrete (i.e. separable).
Let
\lambda
be an eigenvalue of
X_{\text{S}}
and
\Pi_{\lambda}
be the corresponding orthogonal projector so that

X_{\text{S}}=\sum_{\lambda}\lambda\,\Pi_{\lambda}.

(69)

From expression
67
we see that, regardless of the state of the system, the Kraus operator
K_{x}
will go to zero in the limit of a strong interaction
\theta\gg 1
unless
x=2\sigma\theta\lambda
for some eigenvalue
\lambda
.

Recall the Kraus operators for the controlled displacement of a pure Gaussian meter,

\displaystyle K_{x}=\sqrt{\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^{2}}}e^{-x^{2}/2\sigma^{2}}}e^{\frac{x}{\sigma}\theta X-\theta^{2}X^{2}}

(70)

where now I’m dropping the ‘system’ subscript,
X=X_{\text{S}}
.
We can easily calculate their corresponding decoherence channel,

\displaystyle\mathcal{Z}

\displaystyle=\int dx\;K_{x}\odot K_{x}^{\dagger}

(71)

\displaystyle=e^{-\theta^{2}X^{2}}\odot e^{-\theta^{2}X^{2}}\circ\int\frac{dx}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^{2}}}e^{-x^{2}/2\sigma^{2}}\;e^{\frac{x}{\sigma}\theta X}\odot e^{\frac{x}{\sigma}\theta X}

(72)

\displaystyle=e^{-\theta^{2}(X^{2}\odot 1+1\odot X^{2})}\circ\int\frac{dx}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^{2}}}e^{-x^{2}/2\sigma^{2}}\;e^{\frac{x}{\sigma}\theta(X\odot 1+1\odot X)}

(73)

\displaystyle=e^{-\theta^{2}(X^{2}\odot 1+1\odot X^{2})}\circ e^{\frac{1}{2}\sigma^{2}\left(\frac{1}{\sigma}\theta(X\odot 1+1\odot X)\right)^{2}}

(74)

\displaystyle=e^{\theta^{2}\left(-X^{2}\odot 1-1\odot X^{2}+\frac{1}{2}(X\odot 1+1\odot X)^{2}\right)}

(75)

\displaystyle=e^{-\theta^{2}\frac{1}{2}(X\odot 1-1\odot X)^{2}}

(76)

\displaystyle=e^{-\theta^{2}\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{ad}_{X}^{2}}

(77)

where

\mathrm{ad}_{X}\equiv X\odot 1-1\odot X\hskip 50.0pt\text{or equivalently}\hskip 50.0pt\mathrm{ad}_{X}(\rho)=[X,\rho].

(78)

This particular decoherence channel is also called the Feynman-Vernon influence functional because it is the single-mode analog of the indirect measurements of a quantum field they considered in 1963.

Y_{\text{S}}

then the Kraus operator (which is quite a bit easier to calculate) becomes

\displaystyle K_{x}=\sqrt{\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^{2}}}e^{-x^{2}/2\sigma^{2}}}e^{i\frac{x}{\sigma}\theta Y}.

\displaystyle\mathcal{Z}

\displaystyle=\int\frac{dx}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^{2}}}e^{-x^{2}/2\sigma^{2}}\;e^{i\frac{x}{\sigma}\theta Y}\odot e^{-i\frac{x}{\sigma}\theta Y}

(81)

II.5
Sequential Measurements

If we repeat the measurement
\mathcal{I}\equiv\{\Omega_{x}\}

n
times, then we can consider this sequence as a composite instrument with Kraus operators

\Omega_{(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n})}=\Omega_{x_{n}}\cdots\Omega_{x_{1}}.

(86)

This new instrument can be expressed as
\mathcal{I}^{*n}\equiv\mathcal{I}*\mathcal{I}*\cdots*\mathcal{I}
, where
\mathcal{B}*\mathcal{A}
denotes the free product,

\mathcal{B}*\mathcal{A}\equiv\Big\{BA:B\in\mathcal{B}\;\;\&\;\;A\in\mathcal{A}\Big\}.

(87)

So for a sequence of two instruments, we could also write for the composite instrument

\{\Omega_{y}\}_{y}*\{\Upsilon_{x}\}_{x}=\{\Omega_{y}\Upsilon_{x}\}_{(x,y)}.

(88)

I will eventually call this free-product a convolution, but probably not until next season.

Given two instruments,
\mathcal{A}=\{\Upsilon_{x}\}_{x}
and
\mathcal{B}=\{\Omega_{y}\}_{y}
, the channel of their sequential instrument is therefore the superoperator composition of their individual channels,

\displaystyle\mathcal{Z}_{\mathcal{B}*\mathcal{A}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{x,y}{\mathcal{O}\hskip-5.74997pt\raisebox{0.5pt}{$\bm{\cdot}$}\hskip 2.74994pt}[\Omega_{y}\Upsilon_{x}]

(89)

\displaystyle=\sum_{x,y}{\mathcal{O}\hskip-5.74997pt\raisebox{0.5pt}{$\bm{\cdot}$}\hskip 2.74994pt}[\Omega_{y}]\circ{\mathcal{O}\hskip-5.74997pt\raisebox{0.5pt}{$\bm{\cdot}$}\hskip 2.74994pt}[\Upsilon_{x}]

(90)

\displaystyle=\sum_{y}{\mathcal{O}\hskip-5.74997pt\raisebox{0.5pt}{$\bm{\cdot}$}\hskip 2.74994pt}[\Omega_{y}]\circ\sum_{x}{\mathcal{O}\hskip-5.74997pt\raisebox{0.5pt}{$\bm{\cdot}$}\hskip 2.74994pt}[\Upsilon_{x}]

(91)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{Z}_{\mathcal{B}}\circ\mathcal{Z}_{\mathcal{A}}

(92)

where remember
{\mathcal{O}\hskip-5.74997pt\raisebox{0.5pt}{$\bm{\cdot}$}\hskip 2.74994pt}[K]\equiv K\odot K^{\dagger}
and
A\odot B^{\dagger}(\rho)\equiv A\rho B^{\dagger}
.
In particular, for a repeated instrument

\displaystyle\mathcal{Z}_{\mathcal{I}^{*n}}=(\mathcal{Z}_{\mathcal{I}})^{\circ n}.

(93)

If
\mathcal{I}
is one of the Gaussian instruments of the previous section, then

\mathcal{Z}_{\mathcal{I}^{n}}=e^{-n\theta^{2}\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{ad}_{X}^{2}}.

(94)

This means that if
t
is the time it takes to perform
\mathcal{I}
a single time, then

\theta=\sqrt{\kappa t}

(95)

for some constant
\kappa
, with units of inverse time, called the
measurement rate
.

III.1.1
The Wiener Increment

If the Gaussian instrument is weak, then

\theta=\sqrt{\kappa dt}.

(96)

In this case, the random variable for the register is instead expressed in terms of the standard Wiener increment,

\displaystyle dW\equiv\frac{x}{\sigma}\sqrt{dt},

(97)

in which case we write, for example, the Kraus operators of the Gaussian Luders instrument as

\displaystyle\Omega_{x}

\displaystyle\equiv\sqrt{\frac{dx}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^{2}}}e^{-x^{2}/2\sigma^{2}}}e^{\frac{x}{\sigma}\sqrt{\kappa dt}X-\kappa dtX^{2}}

(98)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{d(dW)}{\sqrt{2\pi dt}}e^{-dW^{2}/2dt}}e^{\sqrt{\kappa}dWX-\kappa dtX^{2}}

(99)

\displaystyle\equiv\sqrt{d\mu(dW)}K_{dW}

(100)

where I define the Wiener increment measure and the conjugate Kraus operator,

\displaystyle d\mu(dW)\equiv\frac{d(dW)}{\sqrt{2\pi dt}}e^{-dW^{2}/2dt}\hskip 50.0pt\text{and}\hskip 50.0ptK_{dW}\equiv e^{\sqrt{\kappa}dWX-\kappa dtX^{2}}.

(101)

III.1.2
Diffusive records and Itô rules

A diffusive measurement record over a time interval
[0,T)
is a sequence of
T/dt
independent Wiener increments which we denote by

dW_{[0,T)}\equiv\{dW_{0},dW_{dt},dW_{2dt},\ldots,dW_{T-dt}\}.

(102)

The measure of this sequence is called the Wiener-path-integral measure

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}\mu\left[dW_{[0,T)}\right]

\displaystyle\equiv\prod_{n=0}^{T/dt-1}d\mu(dW_{ndt})

(103)

\displaystyle=e^{-\sum_{n=0}^{T/dt-1}(dW_{ndt})^{2}/2dt}\prod_{n=0}^{T/dt-1}\frac{d(dW_{ndt})}{\sqrt{2\pi dt}}

(104)

\displaystyle\equiv e^{-\int_{t=0}^{T_{-}}(dW_{t})^{2}/2dt}\prod_{n=0}^{T/dt-1}\frac{d(dW_{ndt})}{\sqrt{2\pi dt}}

(105)

where
T_{-}\equiv T-dt
.

I will denote the average of any functional of the Wiener path against the Wiener path-integral measure by angle brackets,

\displaystyle\Big\langle f[dW_{[0,T)}]\Big\rangle\equiv\int\mathcal{D}\mu[dW_{[0,T)}]\,f[dW_{[0,T)}]

(106)

When doing calculus with these increments, various forms of integration occur which in the limit
dt\to 0
amount to what is called the Itô rule.
The Itô rule is a bit subtle, so rather than state it generally, I will introduce it first through a set of special cases.

Essentially, it is already clear by definition that

\displaystyle\langle dW_{s}dW_{t}\rangle=\delta_{st}dt

(107)

where
\delta_{st}
is a Kronecker delta and not a Dirac delta.
(In certain instances, it can be useful to think of the Dirac delta as
\delta(s-t)=\delta_{st}/dt
.)
However, in the limit
dt\to 0
, what is also true is that the fluctuations of the random variable
dW_{s}dW_{t}
never actually add up.
This means that in the limit
dt\to 0
, we can use the much stronger Itô rule,

\displaystyle dW_{s}dW_{t}=\delta_{st}dt

(108)

as I will now explain.

dW_{t}^{2}=dt

: Specifically, the fluctuations of the random variable
dW_{t}^{2}
can be neglected in the limit
dt\to 0
.

To see this, consider the random variable

x\equiv\int_{t=0}^{T_{-}}dW_{t}^{2}.

(109)

For its first moment, we have

\displaystyle\langle x\rangle

\displaystyle=\int_{t=0}^{T_{-}}\langle dW_{t}^{2}\rangle

(110)

\displaystyle=\int_{t=0}^{T_{-}}dt

(111)

\displaystyle=T.

(112)

For its second moment, we have

\displaystyle\langle x^{2}\rangle

\displaystyle=\int_{s=0}^{T_{-}}\int_{t=0}^{T_{-}}\langle dW_{s}^{2}dW_{t}^{2}\rangle

(113)

\displaystyle=\int_{t=0}^{T_{-}}\langle dW_{t}^{4}\rangle+2\int_{s=0}^{T_{-}}\int_{t=0}^{s_{-}}\langle dW_{s}^{2}\rangle\langle dW_{t}^{2}\rangle

(114)

\displaystyle=\int_{t=0}^{T_{-}}3dt^{2}+2\int_{s=0}^{T_{-}}\int_{t=0}^{s_{-}}dsdt

(115)

\displaystyle=3Tdt+T^{2}.

(116)

Therefore, the variance is

\displaystyle\langle x^{2}\rangle-\langle x\rangle^{2}=3Tdt,

(117)

which is indeed negligible in the limit
dt\to 0
.

dW_{t}dt=0

:

Consider the random variable

\displaystyle x\equiv\int_{t=0}^{T_{-}}dW_{t}dt.

(118)

Clearly

\displaystyle\langle x\rangle

\displaystyle=\int_{t=0}^{T_{-}}\langle dW_{t}\rangle dt

(119)

\displaystyle=0.

(120)

Further

\displaystyle\langle x^{2}\rangle

\displaystyle=\int_{s=0}^{T_{-}}\int_{t=0}^{T_{-}}\langle dW_{s}dW_{t}\rangle(dt)^{2}

(121)

\displaystyle=\int_{s=0}^{T_{-}}\int_{t=0}^{T_{-}}\delta_{st}(dt)^{3}

(122)

\displaystyle=\int_{t=0}^{T_{-}}(dt)^{3}

(123)

\displaystyle=T(dt)^{2}.

(124)

dW_{t}dV_{t}=0

: Specifically, the time-wise product of any two independent Wiener paths is effectively zero.

Consider the random variable

x\equiv\int_{t=0}^{T_{-}}dW_{t}dV_{t}.

(125)

Clearly

\displaystyle\langle x\rangle=0.

(126)

Further

\displaystyle\langle x^{2}\rangle

\displaystyle=\int_{s=0}^{T_{-}}\int_{t=0}^{T_{-}}\langle dW_{s}dV_{s}dW_{t}dV_{t}\rangle

(127)

\displaystyle=\int_{s=0}^{T_{-}}\int_{t=0}^{T_{-}}\langle dW_{s}dW_{t}\rangle\langle dV_{s}dV_{t}\rangle

(128)

\displaystyle=\int_{s=0}^{T_{-}}\int_{t=0}^{T_{-}}\delta_{st}dt\delta_{st}dt

(129)

\displaystyle=\int_{s=0}^{T_{-}}\int_{t=0}^{T_{-}}\delta_{st}(dt)^{2}

(130)

\displaystyle=Tdt.

(131)

III.2.1
Weak Gaussian Measurements in General and Diffusive Instruments

For weak Gaussian measurements, we can calculate the Kraus operators for the more general interaction that includes both types considered,

\displaystyle\mathrm{U}_{\text{int}}

\displaystyle=e^{-i(\frac{2\sigma}{\hbar}P_{\text{M}}\otimes X_{\text{S}}-\frac{1}{\sigma}Q_{\text{M}}\otimes Y_{\text{S}})\sqrt{\kappa dt}}

(132)

\displaystyle=e^{(a^{\dagger}_{\text{M}}\otimes L_{\text{S}}-a_{\text{M}}\otimes L^{\dagger}_{\text{S}})\sqrt{\kappa dt}}

(133)

where recall the annihilator of the meter state is

\displaystyle a_{\text{M}}\equiv\frac{1}{2\sigma}Q_{\text{M}}+i\frac{\sigma}{\hbar}P_{\text{M}}

(134)

but also introduced is the system operator

L_{\text{S}}\equiv X_{\text{S}}+iY_{\text{S}}.

(135)

Dropping the system subscripts, a careful calculation reveals two very useful expressions for the Kraus operators,

\displaystyle\Omega_{dW}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{d\mu(dW)}e^{L\sqrt{\kappa}dW-\frac{1}{2}(L^{\dagger}L+L^{2})\kappa dt}

(136)

\displaystyle=e^{iY\sqrt{\kappa}dW-i\frac{1}{2}\{X,Y\}\kappa dt}\sqrt{d\mu(dW)}e^{X\sqrt{\kappa}dW-X^{2}\kappa dt}

(137)

where
\{X,Y\}\equiv XY+YX
is the anticommutator.
Examing the second expression, one can see that it is the Hermitian component of
L
that is being measured while anti-Hermitian component of
L
generates a locally adaptive unitary as a well as introducing a nonadaptive unitary drift term generated by the anticommutator which we will see in terms of phase spaces (planar and spherical) always has the effect of squeezing the quasiprobability of the measured state.

For a good exercise, calculate these expressions for the Kraus oeprator.
It requires a few versions of the Ito rule, namely
dW^{2}=dt
and
dWdt=0
, which I will explain in a moment.
For assistance, you can look to arXiv:2510.13980, Appendix A.2, page 39.

Further, a calculation reveals the decoherence channel to be

\displaystyle\mathcal{Z}

\displaystyle=e^{(L\odot L^{\dagger}-\frac{1}{2}L^{\dagger}L\odot 1-\frac{1}{2}1\odot L^{\dagger}L)\kappa dt}.

(138)

The superoperator

\mathcal{D}_{L}\equiv L\odot L^{\dagger}-\frac{1}{2}L^{\dagger}L\odot 1-\frac{1}{2}1\odot L^{\dagger}L

(139)

is known as a
dissipator
or
Lindbladian
, which means that this operator introduced,
L
, is in fact a
Lindblad operator
.

The Lindbladian generator is the most general form of a continuous-in-time decoherence channel.
However, the Gaussian instrument which registers a so-called
diffusive process
(a.k.a.
Wiener process
or
Brownian motion
) isn’t the only instrument that generates this type of decoherence.
Namely, there are also the instruments that register
jump processes
such as indirect photodetection which can also be thought of as measurements of a qubit meter.
Diffusive and jump processes are the primary examples of continuous Markov processes.
In this course, the focus is almost entirely on instruments with diffusive registers.

III.2.2
Diffusively Measuring NonCommuting Observables Simultaneously

Consider two Wiener measurements with Lindblad operators
L
and
M
, with Kraus operators

\displaystyle\Upsilon_{dV}=\sqrt{d\mu(dV)}e^{M\sqrt{\kappa}dV-\frac{1}{2}(M^{\dagger}M+M^{2})\kappa dt}\hskip 25.0pt\text{and}\hskip 25.0pt\Omega_{dW}=\sqrt{d\mu(dW)}e^{L\sqrt{\kappa}dW-\frac{1}{2}(L^{\dagger}L+L^{2})\kappa dt}.

(140)

An essential thing to understand is that even if the Lindblad operators do not commute, the Kraus operators they generate actually do still commute because, of the Itô rules.
In particular,

\displaystyle\Upsilon_{dV}\Omega_{dW}

\displaystyle=\Omega_{dW}\Upsilon_{dV}e^{[M,L]\kappa dVdW}

(141)

\displaystyle=\Omega_{dW}\Upsilon_{dV}.

(142)

Crucially, what this means is that noncommuting observables can be diffusively measured simultaneously.
Given a tuple of Lindblad operators,
\vec{L}=(L_{1},L_{2},\ldots,L_{n})
, it therefore makes sense to consider the diffusive measurement generated by the Kraus operators

\displaystyle\Omega_{d\vec{W}}=\sqrt{\frac{d^{n}(d\vec{W})}{(2\pi dt)^{n/2}}e^{-d\vec{W}^{2}/2dt}}e^{\vec{L}\cdot\sqrt{\kappa}d\vec{W}-Q_{\vec{L}}\kappa dt}

(143)

where
d\vec{W}=(dW^{1},dW^{2},\ldots,dW^{n})
is the conjugate tuple of independent Wiener records, and the infinitesimal generator consists of the stochastic linear component

\displaystyle\vec{L}\cdot d\vec{W}\equiv L_{1}dW^{1}+\ldots+L_{n}dW^{n}

(144)

and a deterministic quadratic (drift) component,

\displaystyle Q_{\vec{L}}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}L_{i}^{\dagger}L_{i}+L_{i}^{2}.

(145)

An abbreviation I sometimes use for these measurements is DMNCOS (pronounced “demon cause”) which stands for
diffusively measuring noncommuting observables simultaneously
.
However, I tend to just say
simultaneous measurements
in my day-to-day.
If a simultaneous measurement is continuously repeated for a time
T
, then the instrument will register a record of Wiener increments
d\vec{W}_{[0,T)}
corresponding to the Kraus operator

\displaystyle\Omega_{d\vec{W}_{[0,T)}}=\sqrt{\mathcal{D}\mu\!\left[d\vec{W}_{[0,T)}\right]}\,\mathcal{T}\exp\left\{\int_{0}^{T_{-}}\vec{L}\cdot\sqrt{\kappa}d\vec{W}_{t}-Q_{\vec{L}}\kappa dt\right\}

(146)

where the (stochastic) time-ordered exponential has been introduced,

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}\exp\left\{\int_{t=0}^{T_{-}}\updelta_{t}\right\}\equiv e^{\updelta_{T-dt}}e^{\updelta_{T-2dt}}\cdots e^{\updelta_{dt}}e^{\updelta_{0}}.

(147)

III.2.3
The fundamental problem of continuous measurement

The fundamental problem of continuous measurement is whether an instrument such as
\{\Omega_{d\vec{W}_{[0,T)}}\}
can be automated, i.e. calculated with a classical computer.
In this case, the instrument can be considered an automaton.
One general instance where the instrument is an automaton is when the time-ordered exponentials universally generate a finite-dimensional group.
This is determined by considering the span of all the commutators between the Lindblad generators and the quadratic term to all orders.
If the time-ordered exponentials generate a finite-dimensional group, then the instrument is said to be a
principal instrument
.
Otherwise, the exponentials generate an infinite-dimensional group and we say we have a
chaotic instrument
.
All principal instruments can be considered automata thanks to the theory of finite-dimensional Lie groups.
Whether there are
chaotic instruments
that can also be automated or not is actually an open question.
I suspect there are.
For anyone curious, I recommend they try reading arXiv:2306.06167 or arXiv:2510.13980.

In general, I call this group generated by the time-ordered exponential the
instrumental group
.
There are 4 particularly important principal instruments that I’ve studied quite a bit, only 2 of which I have so far written about (extensively).
We will discuss them a bit next semester as they are the path to the phase space POVMs, planar, spherical, and beyond.
For now, let me just introduce them a little here:

The Isotropic Spin Measurement (ISM),
\vec{L}=(J_{x},J_{y},J_{z})

:

This measurement generates a 7-dimensional instrumental group I call
\mathrm{ISpin}(3,\mathbb{C})\cong\mathbb{R}\times\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{C})
.
For a quick read from when we first discovered this, read arXiv: 1805.01012.
For an extensive mathematical exploration of how ISM realizes the spin-coherent POVMs universally, read arXiv:2107.12396.

SPQM,
\vec{L}=(Q,P)

:

The acronym stands for
Simultaneous momentum (p) and position (q) measurement
, but it is also an homage to Antoine Barchielli at the University of Milan who first attempted to solve this problem in 1981.
SPQM is also a rif on the Milan extension of the emblematic SPQR or
Senatus Populesque Romanus
.

This measurement also generates a 7-dimensional instrumental group I call the instrumental Weyl-Heisenberg group,
\mathrm{IWH}
.
For an extensive mathematical exploration of how SPQM realizes the standard coherent POVM, read arXiv:2306.01045

Indirect heterodyne,
\vec{L}=(a,-ia)

:

This measurement generates a 3-dimensional instrumental group which is far easier to navigate.
It was first discovered and analyzed in 1994 in two independent papers, by Goetsch & Graham and by Wiseman & Milburn.

Like SPQM, indirect heterodyne realized the standard coherent POVM.
The essential difference between these measurements is that SPQM leaves the post-measurement state in a random coherent state while indirect heterodyne leaves the post-measurement state in vacuum.

Indirect spin-heterodyne,
\vec{L}=(J_{+},-iJ_{+},J_{z})

:

This measurement generates a 3-dimensional instrumental group which is also much easier to navigate.
Wiseman and Milburn got close to finding this measurement in 1995.
As Howard Wiseman put it when I explained it to him in 2022, “Who knew a third measurement could make the problem infinitely simpler?!”.

Like ISM, indirect spin-heterodyne realizes the standard coherent POVM.
The essential difference between these measurements is that ISM leaves the post-measurement state in a uniformly random spin-coherent state while indirect heterodyne leaves the post-measurement state in the spin-coherent state pointing in the positive z-direction.

III.3.1
Discovering the need to keep
\epsilon^{2}
from Weak Binary POVMs

What I was calling
\theta
at the beginning of this course I am now calling
\epsilon
for Rob Spekkens.

Consider the two-outcome weak POVM with elements

\displaystyle E_{b}\equiv\frac{1}{2}\left(1+2b\epsilon X\right)

(148)

where
b\in\{+1,-1\}
and
\epsilon\ll 1
.
One might think this means the Kraus operators of the Luders instrument are

\displaystyle\Omega_{b}\overset{?}{=}\sqrt{\frac{1}{2}}\left(1+b\epsilon X\right).

(149)

However, this would imply that the channel is

\displaystyle\mathcal{Z}

\displaystyle\equiv\sum_{b}\Omega_{b}\odot\Omega_{b}^{\dagger}

(150)

\displaystyle=\sum_{b}\frac{1}{2}\left(1\odot 1+b\epsilon(X\odot 1+1\odot X)+\epsilon^{2}b^{2}X\odot X\right)

(151)

\displaystyle=1\odot 1+\epsilon^{2}X\odot X.

(152)

This tells us two things. The first is that once again, the decoherence time must be proportional to
\epsilon^{2}
.
The second is that, since we must therefore keep around terms up to
\epsilon^{2}
, there is an additional term that must be in the weak Luders Kraus-operator, else the channel would not be CPTP:

\displaystyle\Omega_{b}=\sqrt{\frac{1}{2}}\left(1+b\epsilon X-\frac{1}{2}\epsilon^{2}X^{2}\right).

(153)

Amazingly, the POVM element still has no term of order
\epsilon^{2}
,

\displaystyle\Omega_{b}^{\dagger}\Omega_{b}\equiv\frac{1}{2}\left(1+2b\epsilon X+\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{3})\right).

(154)

III.3.2
Discovering the Gaussian Instrument from the Weak Sequential Binary Instrument

Having sorted out the Kraus operators of a weak two-outcome measurement, a sequence of such measurements would have Kraus operators

\displaystyle\Omega_{b_{n}}\cdots\Omega_{b_{1}}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{1}{2^{n}}}\left(1+\epsilon(b_{1}+\ldots+b_{n})X+\epsilon^{2}\sum_{i\neq j}b_{i}b_{j}X^{2}-\frac{1}{2}n\epsilon^{2}X^{2}\right).

(155)

This double sum is too cumbersome and can be avoided if we instead express our Kraus operators in terms of exponentials,

\displaystyle\Omega_{b}=\sqrt{\frac{1}{2}}e^{\epsilon bX-\epsilon^{2}X^{2}},

(156)

in which case

\displaystyle\Omega_{b_{n}}\cdots\Omega_{b_{1}}=\sqrt{\frac{1}{2^{n}}}e^{\epsilon(b_{1}+\ldots+b_{n})X-n\epsilon^{2}X^{2}}.

(157)

There are clearly several distinct sequences of
(b_{1},\ldots,b_{n})
that give the same total Kraus operator,
\binom{n}{k}
of them to be exact, where
k
is the number of
b_{i}=+1
.
More convenient than the counting parameter
k
is just the sum of the binomial trials,

\displaystyle m

\displaystyle\equiv b_{1}+\ldots+b_{n}

(158)

\displaystyle=2k-n.

(159)

With this, we collect all the distinct sequences which give the same total Kraus operator.
Recall
{\mathcal{O}\hskip-5.74997pt\raisebox{0.5pt}{$\bm{\cdot}$}\hskip 2.74994pt}[\Omega]\equiv\Omega\odot\Omega^{\dagger}
and define
\Omega_{m}
such that

\displaystyle{\mathcal{O}\hskip-5.74997pt\raisebox{0.5pt}{$\bm{\cdot}$}\hskip 2.74994pt}\left[\Omega_{m}\right]

\displaystyle\equiv\sum_{b_{1}+\ldots+b_{n}=m}{\mathcal{O}\hskip-5.74997pt\raisebox{0.5pt}{$\bm{\cdot}$}\hskip 2.74994pt}\left[\Omega_{b_{n}}\cdots\Omega_{b_{1}}\right]

(160)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2^{n}}\sum_{b_{1}+\ldots+b_{n}=m}{\mathcal{O}\hskip-5.74997pt\raisebox{0.5pt}{$\bm{\cdot}$}\hskip 2.74994pt}\left[e^{\epsilon(b_{1}+\ldots+b_{n})X-n\epsilon^{2}X^{2}}\right]

(161)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2^{n}}\binom{n}{\frac{n+m}{2}}{\mathcal{O}\hskip-5.74997pt\raisebox{0.5pt}{$\bm{\cdot}$}\hskip 2.74994pt}\left[e^{\epsilon mX-n\epsilon^{2}X^{2}}\right]

(162)

or back in terms of just the Kraus operator,

\displaystyle\Omega_{m}=\sqrt{\frac{1}{2^{n}}\binom{n}{\frac{n+m}{2}}}e^{\epsilon mX-n\epsilon^{2}X^{2}}

(163)

As
n
increases, the binomial distribution very quickly approximates a Gaussian distribution

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2^{n}}\binom{n}{k}

\displaystyle\sim\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi(n/4)}}e^{-\frac{1}{2(n/4)}(k-\frac{n}{2})^{2}}

(164)

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{\sqrt{2\pi n}}e^{-m^{2}/2n}.

(165)

For a finite, but large enough
n
, say
n=10
, because these weak sequential random variables become effectively Gaussian, we can substitute them for standard Wiener increments by defining

\displaystyle\kappa dt\equiv n\epsilon^{2}\hskip 25.0pt\text{,}\hskip 25.0pt\sqrt{\kappa}dW\equiv m\epsilon\hskip 25.0pt\text{, and therefore}\hskip 25.0pt\sqrt{\kappa}d(dW)=2\epsilon.

(166)

Under this coördinate transformation, the Kraus operators are therefore

\displaystyle\Omega_{m}

\displaystyle\sim\sqrt{\frac{2}{\sqrt{2\pi n}}e^{-\frac{m^{2}}{2n}}}e^{\epsilon mX-n\epsilon^{2}X^{2}}

(167)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{2\epsilon}{\sqrt{2\pi n\epsilon^{2}}}e^{-\frac{(m\epsilon)^{2}}{2n\epsilon^{2}}}}e^{\epsilon mX-n\epsilon^{2}X^{2}}

(168)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{\sqrt{\kappa}d(dW)}{\sqrt{2\pi\kappa dt}}e^{-\frac{(\sqrt{\kappa}dW)^{2}}{2\kappa dt}}}e^{\sqrt{\kappa}dWX-\kappa dtX^{2}}

(169)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{d(dW)}{\sqrt{2\pi dt}}e^{-dW^{2}/2dt}}e^{\sqrt{\kappa}dWX-\kappa dtX^{2}},

(170)

which are exactly the weak Gaussian Kraus operators we have been discussing.

This is one of the simplest (and historically the oldest [de Moivre, 1733]) examples of the central limit theorem which in turn is the simplest example of a dynamic universality class.

III.3.3
Back to Itô

So if we consider weak Gaussian Kraus operators with Hermitian Lindblad operators,

\displaystyle\Omega^{(X)}_{dW}\equiv\sqrt{\frac{d(dW)}{\sqrt{2\pi dt}}e^{-dW^{2}/2dt}}e^{\sqrt{\kappa}dWX-\kappa dtX^{2}},

(171)

the subtle point is that, by Baker-Campbell-Hausdorff

\displaystyle\Omega^{(X)}_{dW}\Omega^{(Y)}_{dV}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{d(dW)d(dV)}{2\pi dt}e^{-(dW^{2}+dV^{2})/2dt}}e^{\sqrt{\kappa}(dW+dV)X+\frac{1}{2}[X,Y]dWdV-\kappa 2dtX^{2}+\mathcal{O}(dt^{3/2})}

(172)

\displaystyle=\Omega^{(Y)}_{dV}\Omega^{(X)}_{dW}e^{[X,Y]dWdV+\mathcal{O}(dt^{3/2})}

(173)

and therefore one needs to understand, for example, why
dWdV=0
— that is, why certain fluctuating terms of order
dt
don’t matter (i.e. make no contribution to the finite-time stochastic variables), even though non-fluctuating terms of order
dt
clearly do matter.

IV
The Instrumental Group: Working with the Time-Ordered Exponential Universally (Nov 27)

(The notes for
PIRSA:2511.0097
begin here.)

Recall that the Kraus operators of a diffusive measurement are always of the form

\displaystyle\Omega_{d\vec{W}_{[0,T)}}=\sqrt{\mathcal{D}\mu\!\left[d\vec{W}_{[0,T)}\right]}\,\mathcal{T}\exp\left\{\int_{0}^{T_{-}}\vec{L}\cdot\sqrt{\kappa}d\vec{W}_{t}-Q_{\vec{L}}\kappa dt\right\}

(174)

where the quadratic generator is

\displaystyle Q_{\vec{L}}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}L_{i}^{\dagger}L_{i}+L_{i}^{2}.

(175)

and the Lindblad operators, of course, do not have to commute.

This section is a set of examples to help familiarize ourselves with the time-ordered exponential.

IV.1
The Universal Property of the Time-Ordered Exponential

The brownian motion defined by the set of Lindblad operators,

\displaystyle\gamma\left[d\vec{W}_{[0,T)}\right]\equiv\mathcal{T}e^{\int_{t=0}^{T_{-}}\updelta(d\vec{W}_{t})},

(176)

is
universal
in the sense that it is defined by just the abstract commutators of the generators in
\updelta(d\vec{W}_{t})
and holds true for
all
representations thereof.
The span of all the commutators of the generators in
\updelta(d\vec{W}_{t})
is a Lie algebra and the time-ordered exponentials and their brownian motion are said to reside in the so-called
universal covering group
of this Lie algebra.

In the context of diffusive measurements, I call this universal covering group the
Instrumental Group
and denote it by
\mathrm{IG}
.

One could define an instrumental semigroup,

\bigcup_{T\in[0,\infty)}\left\{\mathcal{T}e^{\int_{t=0}^{T_{-}}\updelta(d\vec{W}_{t})}\right\}_{d\vec{W}_{[0,T)}}\subset\mathrm{IG}

(177)

where remember that the time-ordered exponential is

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}e^{\int_{t=0}^{T_{-}}\updelta_{t}}

\displaystyle\equiv\mathcal{T}\prod_{k=0}^{T_{-}/dt}e^{\updelta_{kdt}}

(178)

\displaystyle=e^{\updelta_{T_{-}}}e^{\updelta_{T_{-}-dt}}\cdots e^{\updelta_{2dt}}e^{\updelta_{dt}}e^{\updelta_{0}}.

(179)

IV.2
Example 1: Diffusive Measurement of a Single Hermitian Observable and von Neumann Measurement

The Kraus operators for indirectly measuring a single observable diffusively (by an infinite number of weak von Neumann interactions) are

\displaystyle\Omega^{\text{Diff}}_{dW_{[0,T)}}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\mathcal{D}\mu\left[dW_{[0,T)}\right]}\mathcal{T}e^{\int_{0}^{T_{-}}X\sqrt{\kappa}dW_{t}-X^{2}\kappa dt}

(180)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\mathcal{D}\mu\left[dW_{[0,T)}\right]}e^{X\sqrt{\kappa}\int_{0}^{T_{-}}dW_{t}-X^{2}\kappa T}.

(181)

For a single Hermitian observable, we can collect all the Kraus-operators that are equal, to find

\displaystyle{\mathcal{O}\hskip-5.74997pt\raisebox{0.5pt}{$\bm{\cdot}$}\hskip 2.74994pt}\left[\Omega^{(X)}_{W_{T}}\right]

\displaystyle\equiv\int_{\int_{0}^{T_{-}}dW_{t}=W_{T}}{\mathcal{O}\hskip-5.74997pt\raisebox{0.5pt}{$\bm{\cdot}$}\hskip 2.74994pt}\left[\Omega^{(X)}_{dW_{[0,T)}}\right]

(182)

\displaystyle=\int_{\int_{0}^{T_{-}}dW_{t}=W_{T}}\mathcal{D}\mu\left[dW_{[0,T)}\right]\,{\mathcal{O}\hskip-5.74997pt\raisebox{0.5pt}{$\bm{\cdot}$}\hskip 2.74994pt}\left[e^{X\sqrt{\kappa}W_{T}-X^{2}\kappa T}\right]

(183)

\displaystyle=d(W_{T})\int\mathcal{D}\mu\left[dW_{[0,T)}\right]\delta\!\left(W_{T}-\int_{0}^{T_{-}}dW_{t}\right)\,{\mathcal{O}\hskip-5.74997pt\raisebox{0.5pt}{$\bm{\cdot}$}\hskip 2.74994pt}\left[e^{X\sqrt{\kappa}W_{T}-X^{2}\kappa T}\right]

(184)

\displaystyle=\frac{d(W_{T})}{\sqrt{2\pi T}}e^{-W_{T}^{2}/2T}\,{\mathcal{O}\hskip-5.74997pt\raisebox{0.5pt}{$\bm{\cdot}$}\hskip 2.74994pt}\left[e^{X\sqrt{\kappa}W_{T}-X^{2}\kappa T}\right].

(185)

Therefore

\displaystyle\Omega^{\text{Diff}}_{W_{T}}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{d(W_{T})}{\sqrt{2\pi T}}e^{-W_{T}^{2}/2T}}\,e^{X\sqrt{\kappa}W_{T}-X^{2}\kappa T}

(186)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{d(W_{T})}{\sqrt{2\pi T}}e^{-\frac{1}{2T}(W_{T}-2\sqrt{\kappa}TX)^{2}}}

(187)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{du}{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-\frac{1}{2}(u-2\sqrt{\kappa T}X)^{2}}}.

(188)

The most interesting part of the above is showing

\displaystyle\int\mathcal{D}\mu\left[dW_{[0,T)}\right]\delta\!\left(W_{T}-\int_{0}^{T_{-}}dW_{t}\right)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi T}}e^{-W_{T}^{2}/2T}.

(189)

We will do this by showing that the path-integral satisfies a Fokker-Planck-Kolmogorov forward equation which in this case is just the standard heat equation.
This is the simplest example of a Feynman-Kac formula.

Let

\displaystyle D_{T}(x)\equiv\int\mathcal{D}\mu\left[dW_{[0,T)}\right]\delta\!\left(x-\int_{0}^{T_{-}}dW_{t}\right).

(190)

Then

\displaystyle D_{T+dt}(x)

\displaystyle\equiv\int\mathcal{D}\mu\left[dW_{[0,T+dt)}\right]\delta\!\left(x-\int_{0}^{(T+dt)_{-}}dW_{t}\right)

(191)

\displaystyle=\int d\mu(dW_{T})\int\mathcal{D}\mu\left[dW_{[0,T)}\right]\delta\!\left(x-dW_{T}-\int_{0}^{T_{-}}dW_{t}\right)

(192)

\displaystyle=\int d\mu(dW_{T})D_{T}(x-dW_{T})

(193)

\displaystyle=\int d\mu(dW_{T})e^{-dW_{T}\frac{\partial}{\partial x}}[D_{T}](x)

(194)

\displaystyle=e^{\frac{1}{2}dt\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial x^{2}}}[D_{T}](x)

(195)

\displaystyle=D_{T}(x)+\frac{1}{2}dt\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial x^{2}}[D_{T}](x)

(196)

and therefore

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}D_{t}(x)=\frac{1}{2}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial x^{2}}[D_{t}](x).

(197)

These diffusive Kraus operators are quite different from the Kraus operators for indirectly measuring a single observable by a single von Neumann interaction,

\displaystyle\Omega^{\text{vN}}_{x}

\displaystyle\equiv\sqrt{dx}\langle x|_{\text{M}}e^{-i\frac{2\sigma}{\hbar}P_{\text{M}}\otimes XgT}|0\rangle_{\text{M}}

(198)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{dx}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^{2}}}e^{-(x-2\sigma gTX)^{2}/2\sigma^{2}}}

(199)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{du}{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-(u-2gTX)^{2}/2}}.

(200)

IV.3
Example 2: Diffusive Heterodyne, Our First Noncommutative Principal Instrument

In indirect heterodyne, the Lindblad operators are

\vec{L}=\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}a,-\frac{i}{\sqrt{2}}a\right)

(201)

where the factors of
\sqrt{2}
are just a convention.
With these Lindblad operators, the factors of
a^{2}
in the quadratic term of the Weak Gaussian Kraus operators therefore cancel and one is left with

\Omega_{d\omega}=\sqrt{d\mu(d\omega)}K_{d\omega},

(202)

where defined are the complex Weiner measure of the increment

d\mu(d\omega)=\frac{d^{2}(d\omega)}{\pi dt}e^{d\omega^{*}d\omega/dt},

(203)

and the conjugate Kraus operators

K_{d\omega}=e^{-\frac{1}{2}a^{\!{\dagger}}\!a\,\kappa dt+a\sqrt{\kappa}d\omega^{*}}.

(204)

In this complex notation, the diffusive Kraus operators are

\displaystyle\Omega_{d\omega_{[0,T)}}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{T}\prod_{t=0}^{T/dt-1}\Omega_{d\omega_{t}}

(205)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\mathcal{D}\mu\left[d\omega_{[0,T)}\right]}\;\mathrm{T}\exp\left(\int_{0}^{T}-\frac{1}{2}a^{\!{\dagger}}\!a\,\kappa dt+a\sqrt{\kappa}d\omega_{t}^{*}\right),

(206)

where the complex Wiener path measure,

\mathcal{D}\mu\!\left[d\omega_{[0,T)}\right]=\prod_{t=0}^{T/dt-1}d\mu(d\omega_{t}).

(207)

The ordered products of these Kraus operators are particular easy to solve because the only noncommutativity left boils down to the identity

\boxed{\vphantom{\Bigg(}\hskip 15.0pte^{a\,d\omega^{*}}e^{-\frac{1}{2}a^{\!{\dagger}}\!a\,\kappa T}=e^{-\frac{1}{2}a^{\!{\dagger}}\!a\,\kappa T}e^{a\,d\omega^{*}e^{-\frac{1}{2}\kappa T}}.\hskip 10.0pt\vphantom{\Bigg)}}

(208)

Applying equation
208
, the time-ordered exponential is easily solved,

\displaystyle\Omega_{d\omega_{[0,T)}}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\mathcal{D}\mu\left[d\omega_{[0,T)}\right]}\;e^{-\frac{1}{2}a^{\!{\dagger}}\!a\,\kappa T}e^{a\,\mu[d\omega_{[0,T)}]^{*}},

(209)

where defined is the fundamental linear functional of the heterodyne process

\mu\!\left[d\omega_{[0,T)}\right]=\int_{0}^{T}\!\!\sqrt{\kappa}d\omega_{t}e^{-\frac{1}{2}\kappa t}.

(210)

Notice how the POVM ceases after a time equal to just a few
1/\kappa
.

V
The Stratonovich Product and the Modified Maurer-Cartan Stochastic Differential (Dec 04)

(The notes for
PIRSA:2512.0018
begin here.)

To solve these time-ordered exponentials more generally, we are going to need a more systematic approach.
This means introducing a couple more tools.

V.1
The Stratonovich Product

Because terms of both order
dW
and order
dW^{2}=dt
contribute to stochastic integrals, products of stochastic random variables with stochastic differentials become a bit more subtle.
In accordance with the Itô rule, the usual rules of differential calculus, the chain rule and the product rule, become modified.
If
x_{t}
is a stochastic random variable, then
f_{t}\equiv f(x_{t})
is a stochastic random variable for any twice-differentiable function
f
, with stochastic differential

\displaystyle df_{t}=f^{\prime}(x_{t})dx_{t}+\frac{1}{2}f^{\prime\prime}(x_{t})dx_{t}^{2}.

(211)

If
f_{t}
and
g_{t}
are stochastic random variables, then their product is a stochastic random variable with stochastic differential

\displaystyle d(f_{t}g_{t})=(df_{t})g_{t}+f_{t}dg_{t}+(df_{t})dg_{t}.

(212)

Both of these rules can be made to look just like the chain rule and product rule of differential calculus if we define the appropriate modification to the product between a stochastic random variable and a stochastic differential.
The
Stratonovich product
of a stochastic differential
dg_{t}
on the left of a stochastic random variable
f_{t}
is defined to be

\displaystyle dg_{t}\circledS f_{t}\equiv dg_{t}(f_{t}+\frac{1}{2}df_{t}),

(213)

and similarly for the differential on the right,

\displaystyle f_{t}\circledS dg_{t}\equiv(f_{t}+\frac{1}{2}df_{t})dg_{t}.

(214)

In terms of these, the modified rules of stochastic differential calculus then take a form similar to ordinary differential calculus,

\displaystyle df_{t}=f^{\prime}(x_{t})\circledS dx_{t}

(215)

and

\displaystyle d(f_{t}g_{t})=df_{t}\circledS g_{t}+f_{t}\circledS dg_{t}.

(216)

It is also important to know (and easy to show) that the Stratonovich product is associative in the sense that

\displaystyle f_{t}\circledS(g_{t}\circledS dh_{t})=(f_{t}g_{t})\circledS dh_{t}.

(217)

I will leave showing this as an exercise to the reader.

V.2
The Modified Maurer-Cartan Stochastic Differential

Consider
dW_{[0,T)}
fixed and let
x_{t}
be the Brownian path in the instrumental group defined by

\displaystyle x_{t}\equiv\gamma\left[dW_{[0,t)}\right].

(218)

First, understand that the definition of the time-ordered exponential is equivalent to the sequence of equations

\displaystyle x_{t+dt}=e^{\updelta_{t}}x_{t}.

(219)

As a differential equation, this means

\displaystyle dx_{t}

\displaystyle\equiv x_{t+dt}-x_{t}

(220)

\displaystyle=(e^{\updelta_{t}}-1)x_{t}

(221)

\displaystyle=(\updelta_{t}+\frac{1}{2}\updelta_{t}^{2})x_{t}

(222)

\displaystyle=\updelta_{t}(x_{t}+\frac{1}{2}\updelta_{t}x_{t})

(223)

\displaystyle=\updelta_{t}\circledS x_{t}

(224)

and therefore

\displaystyle dx_{t}\circledS x_{t}^{\,\text{-}\hskip-1.0pt1}=\updelta_{t}.

(225)

The object on the left of this equation I’ve called the
modified Maurer-Cartan stochastic differential
(MMCSD) since [arXiv: 2107.12396].
However, connecting to the “Stratonovich picture” is something I only got around to in [arXiv: 2306.06167].

V.3
Example 3: Indirect Homodyne, using the new tools

In indirect homodyne we once again have just a single Lindblad operator

\displaystyle L=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}a

(226)

where
a
is a standard bosonic annihilation operator.
Therefore the infinitesimal generator of the diffusive measurement is

\displaystyle\updelta_{t}=-L^{\dagger}L\frac{1}{2}\kappa dt-L^{2}\frac{1}{2}\kappa dt+L\sqrt{\kappa}dW_{t}.

(227)

The 3 factors of
\updelta_{t}
close under Lie brackets, in particular

\displaystyle[L^{\dagger}L,L]=-\frac{1}{2}L\hskip 50.0pt\text{and}\hskip 50.0pt[L^{\dagger}L,L^{2}]=-L^{2},

(228)

and therefore the time-ordered exponential

\gamma\!\left[dW_{[0,T)}\right]\equiv\mathcal{T}e^{\int_{0}^{T_{-}}\updelta_{t}}

(229)

resides in a 3-dimensional instrumental group.

In particular, the elements of this group can always be put in the form

x=e^{-L^{\dagger}Lr}e^{-L^{2}s}e^{Lq}

(230)

as I will show inductively by translating the time-ordered exponential into stochastic differential equations for the coordinates
(r,s,q)
.

Deriving the Coordinate SDEs

If we accept the decomposition

\displaystyle x=e^{-L^{\dagger}Lr}e^{-L^{2}s}e^{Lq},

(231)

we can use it to transform infinitesimal coordinate displacements to right-invariant displacements as I will now show.
Observe that if we differentiate with respect to
r
, holding
s
and
q
constant,

\displaystyle\partial_{r}[x]

\displaystyle=\partial_{r}[e^{-L^{\dagger}Lr}]e^{-L^{2}s}e^{Lq}

(232)

\displaystyle=-L^{\dagger}Le^{-L^{\dagger}Lr}e^{-L^{2}s}e^{Lq}

(233)

\displaystyle=-L^{\dagger}\!L\,x.

(234)

A little more interesting is if we differentiate with respect to
s
, holding
r
and
q
constant,

\displaystyle\partial_{s}[x]

\displaystyle=e^{-L^{\dagger}Lr}\partial_{r}[e^{-L^{2}s}]e^{Lq}

(235)

\displaystyle=e^{-L^{\dagger}Lr}(-L^{2}e^{-L^{2}s})e^{Lq}

(236)

\displaystyle=-e^{-L^{\dagger}Lr}L^{2}e^{L^{\dagger}Lr}x

(237)

\displaystyle=-e^{r}L^{2}x.

(238)

Similarly, differentiating with respect to
s
, holding
r
and
q
constant, gives

\displaystyle\partial_{q}[x]=e^{r/2}Lx.

(239)

Expanding the stochastic differential in the coordinate basis by the chain rule and dropping the time subscript

\displaystyle dx

\displaystyle=dr\circledS\partial_{r}[x]+ds\circledS\partial_{s}[x]+dq\circledS\partial_{q}[x]

(240)

\displaystyle=\left(-L^{\dagger}\!L\,dr-L^{2}e^{r}ds+Le^{r/2}dq\right)\circledS x

(241)

or equivalently

\displaystyle dx\circledS x^{\,\text{-}\hskip-1.0pt1}=-L^{\dagger}\!L\,dr-L^{2}e^{r}ds+Le^{r/2}dq.

(242)

Combining this with equations
225
and
227
gives the system of stochastic differential equations

\displaystyle\begin{cases}dr=\frac{1}{2}\kappa dt\\
e^{r}ds=\frac{1}{2}\kappa dt\\
e^{r/2}dq=\sqrt{\kappa}dW\end{cases}

(243)

which with the initial condition
r(0)=s(0)=q(0)=0
is pretty straight-forward to solve.
The trickiest of these is probably the second equation in the system, which with the solution of the first equation is

\displaystyle s(T)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T_{-}}e^{-\frac{1}{2}\kappa t}\frac{1}{2}\kappa dt

(244)

\displaystyle=-\int_{0}^{T_{-}}de^{-\frac{1}{2}\kappa t}

(245)

\displaystyle=1-e^{-\frac{1}{2}\kappa T}.

(246)

Altogether, the solution is

\displaystyle\begin{cases}r_{t}=\frac{1}{2}\kappa t\\
s_{t}=1-e^{-\frac{1}{2}\kappa t}\\
q_{t}=\sqrt{\kappa}\int_{0}^{T_{-}}e^{-\frac{1}{4}\kappa t}dW_{t}.\end{cases}

(247)

In particular, we see this same turning off feature in the POVM.
This feature somehow makes the universal instrument far easier to interpret than
IV.2
, where the instrument can’t really make up its mind without a spectrum for the observable.

The Kraus-Operator Density and its Fokker-Planck-Kolmogorov Equation (Dec 08)

(The notes for
PIRSA:2512.0015
begin here.)

Having some experience with the time-ordered exponential, we now return to the measure component of the diffusive Kraus-operators.
We will be able to collect equivalent Kraus-operators, leaving a Kraus-operator distribution and Kraus-operator density (KOD), similar to what was done in section
IV.2
.
To solve for the KOD generally will require yet another tool, the invariant derivative.

VI.1
Invariant Derivatives, One-Forms, and Integral (Haar) Measures

Coordinate derivatives are not the natural form of differentiation when working with time-ordered exponentials.
Rather, because the measuring process generates trajectories in the form of equation
225
, what is natural are the so-called right-invariant derivatives of a group.
Given an element
X
of the Lie algebra, the right-invariant derivative at a point
x
in the Lie group of a function
f(x)
is

\underleftarrow{X}[f](x)\equiv\lim_{h\to 0}\frac{f(e^{hX}x)-f(x)}{h}.

(248)

Similarly, we can take left-invariant derivatives,

\underrightarrow{X}[f](x)\equiv\lim_{h\to 0}\frac{f(xe^{hX})-f(x)}{h}.

(249)

“Invariant” here is really short for “translation-invariant”, where every element
g
of the group defines left- and right-translation operators,

\mathfrak{L}_{g}[f](x)\equiv f(gx)\hskip 50.0pt\text{and}\hskip 50.0pt\mathfrak{R}_{g}[f](x)\equiv f(xg).

(250)

Therefore the names of the right- and left-invariant derivatives refer to the respective commutativity property

\mathfrak{R}_{g}\circ\underleftarrow{X}\circ\mathfrak{R}_{g}^{\,\text{-}\hskip-1.0pt1}=\underleftarrow{X}\hskip 50.0pt\text{and}\hskip 50.0pt\mathfrak{L}_{g}\circ\underrightarrow{X}\circ\mathfrak{L}_{g}^{\,\text{-}\hskip-1.0pt1}=\underrightarrow{X}.

(251)

Finally, on the space of analytic functions the invariant derivatives actually generate the translation operators

\mathfrak{L}_{e^{X}}=e^{h\underleftarrow{X}}\hskip 50.0pt\text{and}\hskip 50.0pt\mathfrak{R}_{e^{X}}=e^{h\underrightarrow{X}}

(252)

which can be considered the noncommutative generalization of Taylor’s theorem.

Every basis of the Lie algebra defines both a right-invariant basis and a left-invariant basis of vector fields on the universal covering group.
If
\{X_{\mu}\}
is a basis of the Lie algebra, the basis of right-invariant one-forms dual to
\{\underleftarrow{X_{\mu}}\}
will be denoted

\theta_{\mathrm{R}}^{\mu}(\underleftarrow{X_{\nu}})=\delta^{\mu}_{\nu}

(253)

and the basis of left-invariant one-forms dual to
\{\underrightarrow{X_{\mu}}\}
will be denoted

\theta_{\mathrm{L}}^{\mu}(\underrightarrow{X_{\nu}})=\delta^{\mu}_{\nu}.

(254)

The right- and left-invariant integral measures are therefore

d_{\mathrm{R}}x=|\theta_{\mathrm{R}}^{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge\theta_{\mathrm{R}}^{n}|\hskip 50.0pt\text{and}\hskip 50.0ptd_{\mathrm{L}}x=|\theta_{\mathrm{L}}^{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge\theta_{\mathrm{L}}^{n}|

(255)

where
n
is the dimension of the Lie group.

(256)

234

(257)

and we can therefore draw the righ-invariant basis.

VI.2
The Kraus-Operator Distribution, Density, and Fokker-Planck-Kolmogorov Equation

Recall that the Kraus operators of a diffusive instrument are always of the form

\Omega_{d\vec{W}_{[0,T)}}=\sqrt{\mathcal{D}\mu\left[d\vec{W}_{[0,T)}\right]}K_{\gamma\left[d\vec{W}_{[0,T)}\right]}

(262)

where
\mathcal{D}\mu\left[d\vec{W}_{[0,T)}\right]
is the Wiener path-integral measure,
\gamma\left[d\vec{W}_{[0,T)}\right]
is the universal time-ordered exponential, and
K_{yx}=K_{y}K_{x}
is a (tempered) representation of the instrumental group.

Similar to what was done in section
IV.2
, we can collect equivalent Kraus-operators

\Omega_{x}=\sqrt{d\mu_{T}(x)}K_{x},

(263)

defining a
Kraus-operator distribution

\displaystyle d\mu_{T}(x)=\int_{x=\gamma\left[d\vec{W}_{[0,T)}\right]}\mathcal{D}\mu\left[d\vec{W}_{[0,T)}\right].

(264)

The Kraus-operator distribution can be easily seen to satisfy a Chapman-Kolmogorov equation

\displaystyle d\mu_{t+dt}(x)

\displaystyle=\int_{x=\gamma\left[d\vec{W}_{[0,t+dt)}\right]}\mathcal{D}\mu\left[d\vec{W}_{[0,t+dt)}\right]

(265)

\displaystyle=\int d\mu(d\vec{W}_{t})\int_{x=e^{\updelta(d\vec{W}_{t})}\gamma\left[d\vec{W}_{[0,t)}\right]}\mathcal{D}\mu\left[d\vec{W}_{[0,t)}\right]

(266)

\displaystyle=\int d\mu(d\vec{W}_{t})\int_{e^{-\updelta(d\vec{W}_{t})}x=\gamma\left[d\vec{W}_{[0,t)}\right]}\mathcal{D}\mu\left[d\vec{W}_{[0,t)}\right]

(267)

\displaystyle=\int d\mu(d\vec{W}_{t})\,d\mu_{t}\left(e^{-\updelta(d\vec{W}_{t})}x\right).

(268)

Now, if we define the
Kraus-operator density
(KOD)
D_{T}(x)
to be the distribution function of the
Kraus-operator distribution
with respect to the
left-invariant
Haar measure,

d\mu_{T}(x)\equiv d_{\mathrm{L}}x\,D_{T}(x),

(269)

then in particular

\displaystyle d\mu_{t}\left(e^{-\updelta(d\vec{W}_{t})}x\right)

\displaystyle\equiv d_{\mathrm{L}}\left(e^{-\updelta(d\vec{W}_{t})}x\right)\,D_{t}\left(e^{-\updelta(d\vec{W}_{t})}x\right)

(270)

\displaystyle=d_{\mathrm{L}}x\,D_{t}\left(e^{-\updelta(d\vec{W}_{t})}x\right).

(271)

Returning to equation
268
, we can drop the left-invariant Haar measure on both sides, leaving

\displaystyle D_{t+dt}(x)

\displaystyle=\int d\mu(d\vec{W}_{t})\,D_{t}\left(e^{-\updelta(d\vec{W}_{t})}x\right)

(272)

\displaystyle=\int d\mu(d\vec{W})\,D_{t}\left(e^{-\updelta(d\vec{W})}x\right)

(273)

\displaystyle=\int d\mu(d\vec{W})\,e^{-\underleftarrow{\updelta(d\vec{W})}}[D_{t}]\left(x\right)

(274)

\displaystyle=\int d\mu(d\vec{W})\,e^{-\sum_{i}\underleftarrow{L_{i}}\sqrt{\kappa}dW^{i}+\underleftarrow{Q}\kappa dt}[D_{t}]\left(x\right)

(275)

\displaystyle=e^{\underleftarrow{Q}\kappa dt}\prod_{i}\int d\mu(dW^{i})\,e^{-\underleftarrow{L_{i}}\sqrt{\kappa}dW^{i}}[D_{t}]\left(x\right)

(276)

\displaystyle=e^{\underleftarrow{Q}\kappa dt}\prod_{i}e^{\frac{1}{2}\underleftarrow{L_{i}}\underleftarrow{L_{i}}\kappa dt}[D_{t}]\left(x\right)

(277)

\displaystyle=\left(1+\left(\underleftarrow{Q}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i}\underleftarrow{L_{i}}\underleftarrow{L_{i}}\right)\kappa dt\right)[D_{t}]\left(x\right).

(278)

Therefore the KOD satisfies the Fokker-Planck-Kolmogorov (forward) equation (FPKE)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\kappa}\frac{\partial}{\partial t}D_{t}(x)=\left(\underleftarrow{Q}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i}\underleftarrow{L_{i}}\underleftarrow{L_{i}}\right)[D_{t}](x).

(279)

Remember that with the universal Kraus-operator density and an instrumental-group representation
K_{x}
, the probability of registering the instrumental-group element
x
after a time
T
is

\mathrm{Prob}_{T}\left(x\in d_{L}x|\rho\right)=d_{L}x\,D_{T}(x)\tr\left(K_{x}^{\dagger}K_{x}\rho\right).

(280)

VII.2
Example 5: The Isotropic Spin Measurement (ISM)

The universal instrument generated by the Lindblad operators

\displaystyle\vec{L}=\left(J_{z},J_{x},J_{y}\right)

(294)

is principal because the quadratic term

\displaystyle Q_{\vec{L}}=J_{z}^{2}+J_{x}^{2}+J_{y}^{2}=\vec{J}^{\>2}

(295)

commutes with all the other generators.
The instrumental Lie group is therefore 7-dimensional, with Lie algebra generated by
\{Q_{\vec{L}},J_{z},J_{x},J_{y},iJ_{z},iJ_{x},iJ_{y}\}
.

The simple fact that
[J_{x},J_{y}]=iJ_{z}
means in the context of diffusive measurements that a product of two non-commuting positive transformations is not generally a positive transformation but rather a positive transformation followed by a unitary.
This simple fact is the basic reason why the problem is so mathematically rich.

This particular measuring process is special because it provided the first practical and universal method for realizing the so-called spin-coherent(-state) POVM. It was this discovery that started the entire instrument manifold program.

For a full analysis of this problem, please see
arXiv:2107.12396
.

\displaystyle Q_{\vec{L}}=J_{z}^{2}+J_{x}^{2}=\vec{J}^{\>2}-J_{y}^{2}

Of course, this identity is not universal as it is only true for spin-half representations.
