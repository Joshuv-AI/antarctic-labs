Determinants of active accident involvement among motorcycle couriers in Türkiye: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Esra Zehra Biçer Özdemir, Melih Gaffar Gözükara
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMotorcycle couriers are increasingly central to urban delivery systems, yet they experience disproportionately high rates of traffic-related injuries. Existing research often attributes crash involvement primarily to individual riding behaviours, overlooking the potential influence of employment models, environmental exposure, and physical strain. Evidence distinguishing rider-related errors from systemic working conditions, particularly for active accidents in which the rider is at fault, remains limited.MethodsThis cross-sectional study was conducted between June and December 2025 among 372 motorcycle couriers actively working in Ankara, Türkiye. Participants were recruited using a snowball sampling strategy through face-to-face and online networks. Data were collected via an electronic questionnaire capturing sociodemographic characteristics, employment model, working conditions, accident history, psychosocial status (Depression Anxiety Stress Scale–21), risky riding behaviours (Motorcycle risk-taking behaviour), and musculoskeletal symptoms. Self-reported active accident involvement (defined as at-fault crashes) within the previous 12 months was the primary outcome. Associations were examined using bivariate analyses and multivariable binary logistic regression.ResultsOverall, 79.6% of participants reported at least one active accident in the previous 12 months. Compared with freelance couriers, company-employed (OR = 7.066; 95% CI: 2.104 to 23.727) and self-employed couriers (OR = 2.783; 95% CI: 1.109 to 6.984) had significantly higher odds of active accident involvement. Non-collision falls showed the strongest association with active accident involvement, with four or more falls associated with markedly higher odds of active accident involvement (OR = 12.887; 95% CI: 4.623 to 35.923). Bad weather conditions (OR = 2.049; 95% CI: 1.029 to 4.083) and motorcycle ownership (OR = 5.540; 95% CI: 2.250 to 13.644) also remained significantly associated with active accident involvement, whereas heavy traffic exposure and risky riding behaviour scores did not.ConclusionsIn this sample, active accident involvement was more strongly associated with employment model, non-collision falls, bad weather conditions, and motorcycle ownership than with self-reported risky riding behaviour. These findings should be interpreted cautiously in light of the cross-sectional design, self-reported outcome definition, and non-probability sampling approach.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12889-026-28048-y.Keywords: Motorcycle Couriers, Self-Employment, Traffic accidents, Risky riding behaviours, Occupational health

Background

The rapid expansion of the on-demand economy, accelerated by the COVID-19 pandemic, has fundamentally transformed the urban logistics and food delivery sectors globally [1]. Within this ecosystem, motorcycle couriers have become the backbone of "last-mile" delivery services. However, this growth has introduced precarious employment models, often referred to as the "gig economy," where couriers work as independent contractors rather than direct employees. Recent scoping reviews on the health of digital platform workers highlight that this employment model generates not only physical risks but also significant "employment strain" stemming from precariousness and lack of social protection [2]. While this model offers flexibility, it has been increasingly associated with intensified work pressure, algorithmic control, and heightened occupational safety risks [3, 4].

Motorcyclists are already among the most vulnerable road users due to their lack of external protection and stability issues. For commercial riders, these inherent risks are exacerbated by occupational factors such as long working hours, bad weather conditions, and high-mileage exposure [5–7]. Recent literature suggests that the "business model" itself acts as a determinant of road safety. Studies indicate that self-employed couriers may exhibit riskier riding behaviours compared to employed riders, driven by the financial incentive to complete more deliveries in less time [4, 8]. Veen et al. describe this phenomenon as "app-etite for control," where algorithmic management creates an invisible but potent time pressure, forcing riders to prioritize speed over safety [3].

Traditionally, motorcycle accidents have been analysed through the lens of rider behaviour, focusing on errors and violations [9, 10]. However, focusing solely on rider error ignores the psychosocial and environmental context of the work. High levels of stress, anxiety, and burnout are prevalent among platform workers due to job insecurity and aggressive performance targets [11, 12]. Similarly, a 2025 study on motorcycle food delivery riders in Thailand confirms that despite the use of personal protective equipment, occupational hazards continue to drive high accident rates [13]. Furthermore, physical fatigue and musculoskeletal disorders (MSDs), which are common among riders due to prolonged static postures and vibration, can impair motor control and reaction times, potentially leading to non-collision falls and active accidents [14, 15].

In the context of Türkiye, the self-employed courier model has become the dominant employment form. While this model ostensibly provides autonomy, it transfers the operational costs (fuel, maintenance, insurance) and safety risks to the individual rider. Despite the growing prevalence of this model, there is limited empirical evidence distinguishing the accident causality between self-employed and company-employed couriers, particularly regarding "active accidents" (at-fault crashes) versus passive involvement. Understanding whether accidents stem from individual riding failures or systemic pressures (such as time constraints and performance evaluations) is crucial for developing effective occupational health policies.

This study aims to investigate the determinants of active accident involvement among motorcycle couriers. Unlike previous studies that often treat accidents as a homogeneous outcome, this research specifically focuses on "active accidents" to isolate the impact of rider behaviour and working conditions. We used a comprehensive dataset including sociodemographic characteristics, employment model (company-employed vs self-employed), working conditions, occupational exposures, and health-related factors.

Methods

Study design and participants

This cross-sectional study was conducted between 24 June 2025 and 18 December 2025 among actively working motorcycle couriers in Ankara, Türkiye. Reporting followed the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guideline.

The target population consisted of professional couriers delivering food, packages, or grocery items. Given the mobile and hard-to-reach nature of this workforce, participants were recruited using a non-probability snowball sampling approach [16]. Initial participants were approached in couriers’ common gathering/rest areas and asked to refer other eligible couriers. For online recruitment, survey dissemination was supported through courier-related unions, associations, and organized groups via WhatsApp and other social media channels. To increase participation, the survey link was reposted weekly with the support of group administrators. Figure 1. The inclusion criteria of being a participant were: (1) being aged 18 or older, (2) working as an active motorcycle courier for at least 3 months, and (3) holding a valid motorcycle license. Participants were informed about the study's purpose, and electronic informed consent was obtained prior to data collection.

Data collection

Data were collected using a structured electronic questionnaire (QR code/Google Forms), administered both in person and through online channels. The data collection instrument consisted of four sections. “Sociodemographic and occupational characteristics”, “Accident history”, “Depression Anxiety Stress Scales-21 (DASS-21)”. “Motorcycle risk-taking behaviour scale (MKRAD)”. Detailed item-level information for both psychometric instruments is provided in the supplementary appendices (Appendix 1 for DASS-21; Appendix 2 for MKRAD).

Sociodemographic and occupational characteristics

The first section is focused on sociodemographic characteristics and their work and health profiles. It included age, gender, educational status, marital status, and detailed occupational variables. The content framework of this section was developed using the European Agency for Safety and Health at Work (EU-OSHA) (report on occupational health and safety risks in the delivery and shipping sector [17].

Employment model was categorized into three groups: (a) company employee (salaried with benefits), (b) self-employed (working on their own account/gig model), and (c) freelance/hourly. Additional variables assessed in the questionnaire included daily working hours, courier experience (years), motorcycle ownership, social security status, chronic disease status, night shift work, regular motorcycle servicing, exposure to heavy traffic and bad weather conditions, aggressive customer encounters, and history of non-collision falls. The questionnaire also included work-related factors such as time pressure and performance evaluation. Time pressure referred to the perceived pressure to complete deliveries rapidly within limited time. Performance evaluation was defined as app-based, customer-mediated assessment of couriers’ work, primarily according to delivery speed/on-time delivery and delivery volume or frequency. In the context of platform-based work, this may also reflect a unilateral algorithmic control mechanism through which customer ratings and platform-generated monitoring data are used to rank workers and shape their access to tasks and work opportunities.

Accident history

Participants were asked about motorcycle crash involvement during the previous 12 months while working as a courier. For the purposes of the questionnaire, accidents were classified as either active accidents or passive accidents. Active accidents were defined as crashes in which the participant considered themselves primarily responsible, for example due to loss of control, misjudgement, speeding, or another rider-related error. Passive accidents were defined as crashes mainly attributed to another road user or external party.

The primary outcome of this study was self-reported active accident involvement within the previous 12 months. Classification of accident type was based entirely on the participant’s own assessment of fault; no external verification source, such as police reports, insurance claims, or medical records, was available. No minimum severity threshold (for example, injury, property damage, or police involvement) was applied; therefore, the outcome may have included both minor and more serious incidents.

In addition, participants were asked about the frequency of non-collision falls, defined as slipping or losing balance without impact with another vehicle. This variable was recorded separately from active accident involvement as an indicator of riding instability or near-miss experience.

Depression anxiety stress scales-21 (DASS-21)

DASS-21 is a three-factor model developed by Lovibond et al. to examine common mood disorders such as depression, anxiety, and stress and to provide helpful psychometric indicators for clinical diagnosis [18]. The 21-item short form, derived from the original 42-item version developed by Lovibond and Lovibond (1995), has been adapted into Turkish, and evidence supporting its validity and reliability is available [18–20]. In this study, we used the Turkish version of the DASS-21, which contains seven items for each condition, to examine participants' levels of depression, anxiety, and stress. This instrument consists of three subscales (depression, anxiety, stress), each assessed using a 4-point Likert response format. Higher scores reflect higher symptom severity. The reported internal consistency values ​​for all subscales are high in Turkish language (Cronbach’s α range: 0.81–0.87) [20]. Item content and scoring details are provided in Appendix 1.

Motorcycle risk-taking behaviour scale

Risk-taking riding behaviour was assessed using the Motorcycle risk-taking behaviour scale “Motosiklet Kullanımında Risk Alma Davranışları Ölçeği (MKRAD (original Turkish name))”, the Turkish validated adaptation of a motorcycle risk-taking instrument. The MKRAD questionnaire (translated as ‘Motorcycle risk-taking behaviour scale’ for use in this manuscript. Conceptually, the measure is rooted in the risk-taking attitude framework developed by Ulleberg and Rundmo, subsequently adapted to motorcycle users by Cheng and Ng, and later validated in Turkish as MKRAD [21–23].

The Turkish validation reported a 13-item, two-factor structure and good internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.87) [23]. Items are rated on a 5-point Likert format, and the total score is calculated by summing item responses; higher scores indicate greater risk-taking tendency while riding. Item content and scoring details are provided in Appendix 2.

Data analysis

Data were analysed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). The normality of data distribution was assessed using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. Descriptive statistics were presented as medians and Q1–Q3 for non-normally distributed continuous variables, and frequencies (n) and percentages (%) for categorical variables. For bivariate comparisons between couriers with and without an active accident history, the Mann–Whitney U test was used for continuous variables (e.g., age, DASS-21 scores, working hours), and the Pearson Chi-square test (X2) and Fisher’s Exact test was used for categorical variables (e.g., working model, time pressure).

To determine the independent predictors of active accidents, a Binary Logistic Regression model (Enter method) was constructed. Variables that were statistically significant (p < 0.05) or clinically relevant in univariate analyses were included in the model. The model fit was evaluated using the Hosmer–Lemeshow test and Nagelkerke R2. Multicollinearity among the predictors included in the regression model was assessed using tolerance and did not indicate problematic overlap among the predictors included in the model (all VIF values ranged from 1.060 to 1.291) (Appendix 3). The results were reported as Odds Ratios (OR) with 95% Confidence Intervals (CI).

Internal consistency of the scales in this sample was satisfactory (DASS-21 subscales: Cronbach's α = 0.96; MKRAD: Cronbach's α = 0.94). The comparison of risk factors between the groups is detailed in Table 2. Couriers who had been involved in active accidents reported significantly higher exposure to systemic and environmental pressures. Factors associated with of active accident involvement

A binary logistic regression analysis was performed to identify the independent predictors of active accidents while controlling for potential confounding factors. The model showed an acceptable fit (Nagelkerke R2 = 0.361; Hosmer–Lemeshow test, p = 0.172).

Working model remained a significant predictor after adjustment. Compared with freelance couriers, company employees had 7.07 times higher odds of active accident involvement (OR = 7.066, 95% CI: 2.104–23.727, p = 0.002), while self-employed couriers had 2.78 times higher odds (OR = 2.783, 95% CI: 1.109–6.984, p = 0.029). Factors associated with active accident involvement

The strongest association with active accident involvement was observed for non-collision falls. Compared with riders who reported no non-collision falls, those who reported "4 or more falls" had 12.88 times higher odds of active accident involvement (OR = 12.887, p < 0.001).

Among the environmental and occupational variables, exposure to bad weather conditions remained significantly associated with active accident involvement (OR = 2.049, 95% CI: 1.029–4.083, p = 0.041). Heavy traffic exposure showed an elevated odds ratio but did not remain statistically significant after adjustment (OR = 2.557, 95% CI: 0.953–6.862, p = 0.062). In addition, motorcycle ownership was independently associated with active accident involvement (OR = 5.540, 95% CI: 2.250–13.644, p < 0.001).

Time pressure, performance evaluation, depressive symptoms, courier experience, aggressive customers, motorcycle risk-taking behaviour score, regular motorcycle servicing, daily working hours, and night shift work were not significant predictors in the adjusted model (Table 4).

Discussion

This study aimed to disentangle the effects of employment models, psychosocial factors, and riding behaviours on active accident involvement among motorcycle couriers. Our findings suggest that systemic and environmental factors—specifically the working model, environmental exposure, and physical stability—are more critical determinants of accidents than individual rider errors or psychological distress alone.

Interpretation of the prevalence estimate

The 12-month prevalence of self-reported active accident involvement in our sample (79.6%) was higher than that reported in several previous studies and should be interpreted with caution. For example, a study of 393 food delivery riders in Vietnam reported that 53.94% had experienced at least one crash in the previous 12 months [24]. Other studies among commercial or motorcycle drivers have also reported high rates, such as 65.1% among commercial motorcycle drivers in Ethiopia and 68.66% among motorcycle drivers in Bangladesh [25, 26]. Although these studies reported lower estimates than those observed in our study, this discrepancy may be related to differences in accident definition, occupational group, study setting, sampling strategy, and outcome definition, as well as contextual factors such as time pressure and local traffic culture together with the 12-month recall period and the non-probability recruitment strategy, may partly explain the high prevalence observed in our sample. National traffic statistics further show that motorcycles account for a substantial share of injury-producing and fatal road traffic crashes in Türkiye; however, these data are not specific to motorcycle couriers and should be considered only as general contextual information rather than as directly comparable estimates [27].

The "paradox" of employment models

A key finding of this study is that both Company Employees and Self-Employed couriers face significantly higher accident risks compared to Freelance workers. While the raw data suggested a slightly higher accident rate for self-employed couriers, the regression analysis revealed that company employees carried the highest adjusted risk (OR = 7.06). This aligns with Christie and Ward, who argued that the risks in the gig economy are not solely defined by the contract type but by the operational nature of the job [4]. Furthermore, Matilla-Santander et al. emphasize in their systematic review that platform workers often suffer from "employment strain" regardless of their legal classification [2]. Although self-employed riders are subjected to algorithmic control [3], our results suggest that company employees might be exposed to different but equally detrimental stressors, such as rigid shift schedules and direct supervisory pressure, which Jakobsen et al. identify as critical risk factors for professional drivers [28].

Motorcycle ownership and other occupational factors

An additional finding of the adjusted model was the significant association between motorcycle ownership and active accident involvement. Previous literature suggests that motorcycle ownership may be relevant to crash risk, although the available evidence remains inconsistent [29]. For example, a study among commercial motorcycle drivers in Ethiopia reported a significant association between motorcycle ownership and crash involvement, although the direction of that association differed from our findings [25]. In addition, a study from Ghana found that riders who did not own their motorcycles were less likely to comply with road safety regulations, suggesting that ownership may also be related to broader safety-related practices [30]. However, these findings are not directly equivalent, because the Ghana study examined compliance rather than crash involvement. In studies of app-based food delivery riders, ownership is also intertwined with broader differences in business model, work pressure, and riding conditions [4]. One possible explanation is that riders using their own motorcycles may have greater discretion over maintenance timing and repair decisions and may continue working despite wear-related concerns under economic pressure.

Experience does not protect: the exposure effect

Contrary to the expectation that experienced riders are safer, our study found that increased courier experience (years) was associated with higher accident involvement (p < 0.001). This finding is consistent with Byun et al., who studied food delivery motorcyclists in Korea and found that experienced riders were more likely to engage in speeding and violations to maximize income [31]. Similarly, Byun, Jeong and Park reported that commercial pressures often override the safety benefits of riding experience [32]. This suggests an "exposure effect": the longer a courier stays in the profession, the more they are desensitized to risks and the more mathematically probable a crash becomes due to high mileage accumulation [5].

Environmental factors and self-reported risky riding behaviour

In the multivariable model, the Motorcycle Risk-Taking Behaviour Scale (MKRAD) was not a significant predictor of active accident involvement (p = 0.325). This non-significant finding should nevertheless be interpreted cautiously. Although multicollinearity did not appear to explain this result, self-reported behavioural measures may not fully capture situational or context-specific risk-taking in occupational delivery settings, and measurement-related limitations may also have attenuated the observed association. Among the environmental variables, bad weather conditions remained significantly associated with active accident involvement, whereas heavy traffic exposure showed an elevated odds ratio but did not retain statistical significance after adjustment. This pattern is partly consistent with previous studies suggesting that precarious working conditions may place couriers in hazardous environments where environmental and occupational pressures interact with rider behaviour [8, 10]. In this context, bad weather exposure may be more strongly related to accident involvement than self-reported risky riding behaviour alone.

The "near-miss" indicator and physical toll

The strongest factor associated with active accidents was the frequency of non-collision falls. Beyond indicating vehicle instability and physical fatigue, this variable may also capture unsafe riding behaviours, impaired riding control, and a broader deficiency in safety culture. Lin et al. reported that commercial motorcyclists experience high rates of work-related musculoskeletal disorders (WRMSDs) due to prolonged static postures and vibration [14]. Our findings of high "painful body regions" in the accident group align with Estember and Kwangsukstith et al., who reported that physical discomfort significantly reduces a rider's reaction time and balance control [13, 33]. A courier who frequently loses balance (non-collision fall) is likely suffering from the physical exhaustion described by Panumasvivat et al., which eventually culminates in a severe active crash [15]. Even so, this finding should be interpreted cautiously, as non-collision falls may reflect the same underlying safety problem as active accidents rather than a fully independent causal pathway.

Psychosocial stress and customer aggression

Although depression and stress scores were significantly higher in the accident group (consistent with Samal et al. and Mbare), these variables lost significance in the regression model [11, 12]. This suggests their effect may be mediated by environmental exposures. Moreover, "Aggressive Customers" were reported significantly more often by the accident group (p = 0.001). Öztürk et al., in their Measure for Angry Drivers (MAD) and Driving Anger Expression (DAX) model, showed that anger provoked by external sources (like other drivers or customers) may lead to aberrant driver behaviours [34]. The stress caused by customer conflicts may create a cognitive load that distracts the courier, making them vulnerable to traffic hazards, as suggested by Stephens and Ohtsuka [35].

Policy implications

From a policy perspective, these findings may suggest that improving the occupational safety of motorcycle couriers could require attention not only to individual rider behaviour, but also to working conditions, working models, platform-related pressures, and environmental exposures. In particular, the observed associations with working model, bad weather exposure, time-related pressure, performance evaluation, and non-collision falls may indicate that preventive approaches should not rely solely on conventional rider training. Differences across working models may reflect variations in work organization, exposure patterns, supervisory practices, and performance demands, suggesting that a uniform approach to occupational safety may be insufficient for this workforce. Possible measures could therefore include reviewing delivery demands during severe weather, reconsidering payment and performance structures that may indirectly encourage speed and continuous riding, and exploring platform-level strategies to reduce excessive time pressure. The findings may also suggest that non-collision falls could be considered in occupational safety monitoring as potential warning events.

Strengths and limitations

Several limitations of this study should be taken into account. First, the cross-sectional design does not allow causal inferences to be made and makes it impossible to determine the temporal direction of the observed associations. Accordingly, while psychosocial distress may have contributed to accident involvement, it is also possible that the accident experience itself influenced the reporting of psychological symptoms.

Another limitation is the reliance on self-reported questionnaire data. In particular, the main outcome variable was determined on the basis of participants’ own assessment of fault, which increases the possibility of recall bias, self-attribution bias, and social desirability bias. To reduce these potential sources of bias, clear instructions were provided at the beginning of the questionnaire, anonymity was emphasized, and participants were assured that honest responses would not lead to any negative consequences. Nevertheless, no external source of verification, such as police reports, insurance records, or medical records, was available.

In addition, no minimum severity threshold was applied to the accident definition used in the questionnaire, which may have led to the inclusion of both minor and more serious incidents under the same outcome. This may partly account for the high prevalence observed in the study.

A further limitation is that participants were recruited through a non-probability snowball sampling approach, using both online groups and face-to-face contact. For this reason, the prevalence estimate should not be interpreted at the population level, and the observed regression associations should be evaluated with caution.

Moreover, some potentially important occupational exposure variables, such as distance travelled per shift, number of deliveries, delivery zone density, and shift timing, were not measured directly. These factors may have acted as confounders in the relationship between employment model and accident involvement. Future studies would benefit from incorporating more objective methods of data collection, such as GPS-based tracking, traffic camera data, and analysis of accident records, in order to provide a more accurate and comprehensive picture of courier behaviours and their relationship with actual accident events.

Finally, the present study focused on central urban areas. This may limit the generalizability of the findings to rural settings. Since rural areas may differ in terms of road infrastructure, traffic density, and courier working patterns, these contextual differences may influence both accident occurrence and accident dynamics.

Despite these limitations, the study provides primary field data from a hard-to-reach occupational group and examines active accident involvement within a multidimensional framework encompassing working conditions, environmental exposures, psychosocial factors, and physical burden. The use of standardized instruments and structured analytical methods supports a systematic evaluation of the findings and contributes to the still limited literature on occupational health and safety among motorcycle couriers.

Conclusion

The study concludes that active accidents among couriers are primarily driven by systemic working conditions—specifically the employment model and the obligation to ride under bad environmental conditions—rather than individual behavioural failures. The self-employed model, while risky, is part of a broader ecosystem where professional couriers (regardless of contract type) are exposed to unsustainable physical and environmental hazards. Interventions should focus on regulatory caps for riding in bad weather and monitoring "non-collision falls" as a critical safety performance indicator. These findings should be interpreted cautiously because of the cross-sectional design, self-reported accident classification, and non-probability sampling approach. Further studies using more representative sampling and more detailed occupational exposure measures are needed to clarify these associations.
