3 EXECUTIVE SUMMARY
The project aimed to develop a Virtual Sensor Digital Twin (VS-DT) to monitor rotor rim
float displacement (RRFD) in legacy hydropower turbines. RRFD, if undetected, can cause
rotor and stator damage, leading to costly repairs and safety concerns. The purpose of
the VS-DT was to predict RRFD signals using existing conventional sensors instead of
installing dedicated, costly physical sensors, thereby reducing operational costs and
enhancing safety.
To achieve this, the project goal was to utilize utility-provided historical data collection to
determine modeling and forecasting techniques while also performing a market study to
ensure applicability across the hydro fleet. Historical data collection, focused on sourcing
time series data from hydropower plants, particularly identifying utilities that previously
tested physical RRFD sensors. Subsequent tasks were showing promising results. The
market assessment was initiated by the Electric Power Research Institute (EPRI) to
determine potential adoption across the hydropower fleet, which found several notable
characteristics of targeted units. Savannah State University (SSU) evaluated statistical
forecasting methods such as Random Forest and Extreme Gradient Boosting for
predicting turbine operational events. Additionally, student workshops were planned to
provide SSU students practical experience and industry exposure.
The project's impact and potential were significantly constrained by contracting delays
and the unprocessed No-Cost Time Extension (NCTE) prior to performance period end.
At the project’s end date, WPTO voiced the potential for an eventual NCTE approval, but
the uncertainty of if/when it would be approved and the resulting re-negotiation of
subcontracts would have created additional delays and planning complexity to the
project. (SSU’s team is restricted to fall/spring academic calendars and would likely not
have been able to restart work until spring of 2026.)
As a result, a majority of tasks, including comprehensive data collection, complete VS-DT
development and validation, forecasting integration, and community engagement
activities, were not completed. Completing these activities would have substantially
enhanced the project's outcomes, offering the hydropower sector predictive monitoring
tools, increasing operational safety and reducing costs.

4 INTRODUCTION
4.1 PROBLEM BACKGROUND
Legacy hydropower turbines are often impacted by rotor rim displacement, an issue
caused by rim deformation and rotor rim sheet relaxation. Initially, this causes increased
vibration of the rotor and other spinning elements; however, should this displacement
progress, it could lead to stator and rotor rub causing severe damage to the powertrain
along with significant safety concerns. Rotor rim float displacement (RRFD) is separate
from airgap monitoring and is not typically monitored due to cost and complexity of
instrumentation. The proposal team has deployed prototype RRFD sensors to monitor
rotor rim displacement; however, it is recognized that not all operators are able to install
new physical instrumentation due to technical or fiscal constraints. Realizing this, the
team sought to design a software package that monitors RRFD levels without a dedicated
physical sensor. Efforts leveraged the team’s prior experience implementing digital twin
technology to expand the preliminary deployment of RRFD sensor technology to other
hydropower operators. The team was strengthened by Savannah State University (SSU),
whose students brought outside-the-box thinking to the project. The project also exposed
those same students to the energy industry and provided opportunities for direct industry
engagement.
Specifically, the proposed project had two technological objectives:
1. Create a virtual sensor digital twin (VS-DT) to predict previously unmonitored rotor
 rim dynamics from existing conventional sensors.
2. Workforce development through partnering with SSU to forecast time to critical
 event by using real-time data and the VS-DT.

4.2 SCOPE AND LIMITATIONS
4.2.1 Original Scope
The beginning of the budget period primarily focused on development of RRFD VS-DT
technology with the goal of accurately forecasting critical events. Foundational research
on hydro turbines’ physical principles and their relation to RRFD took place to identify
which “conventional” sensors were appropriate for estimating the RRFD signal. The latter
half of the project was to primarily focus on integrating the VS-DT into a standalone proof-
of-concept. To support the workforce development objective, the team also planned two
workshops at SSU. The first was focused on bringing in industry and government in the
hydro and power sectors to expose students to opportunities in the field. The second
workshop would have brought in power industry personnel to provide students with
critical feedback on their work.

5.1 HISTORICAL DATA COLLECTION AND MARKET ASSESSMENT
Task Summary: This Task focused on collecting historical data (RRFD and Conventional)
from participating sites and performing a market study determining potential users of the
technology. The primary goal was to set the foundation for building and demonstrating
the VS-DT technology by ensuring the team had access to all the necessary data and site
information.
Problem statement: Each hydropower site has its own methods of collecting, storing, and
analyzing data. Each site’s construction and turbine assembly also have unique build
characteristics. It is important to capture this information and methodology for tailoring
technology to a general, rather than specific, site approach.
Value proposition: Analyzing multiple plants’ data methodologies and site characteristics
allows a general application of the VS-DT to be developed, assessed, and improved upon
as needed. Furthermore, each additional plant analyzed is anticipated to benefit from
economies of scale with respect to knowing exactly what data is needed for the analysis.
Subtask 1.1. Collect Historical Data.
Historical concurrent time series data for conventional and rotor rim displacement
sensors collected from project partner site(s). Data consistency, format, and quality
reassessed to prepare for intake. Associated metadata about the plant was also
collected, such as plant as-built drawings and installed equipment lists necessary to
create a valid VS-DT.
Subtask 1.2. Perform Study of Market and Potential Uptake Analysis. Market survey
conducted based on public data of the domestic hydropower fleet. This will entail
analysis of the EPRI member deployment site rotor characteristics such as size, capacity,
age, and rotor rim-spider connection mechanism. These characteristics of the domestic
hydropower fleet were to be gathered through queries of open-source datasets and
design standards. The objective of this fleet analysis is to provide an indication of which
plants were likely to have applicability to the tools developed in Tasks 2 and 3.
Applicability is a function of not only rotor rim and spider design but also identification of
key design characteristics such as rotor diameter, rotor inertia, and rated capacity at the
test site and the extent to which those characteristics are representative of the broader
hydropower fleet.

5.2 DEVELOP VIRTUAL SENSOR DIGITAL TWIN
Task Summary: In this Task, the VS-DT model was to be developed to predict RRFD and
other relevant hydropower signals indicative of machine health and performance. The
goal was to create a standalone program capable of monitoring RRFD levels and various
other hydropower signals without implementation of a dedicated physical RRFD sensor.
Problem statement: The installation and material cost of dedicated sensors monitoring
RRFD and other hydropower signals can be expensive and labor-intensive. Plant
operators need cost-effective means of monitoring RRFD.
Value proposition: To develop a VS-DT model capable of predicting RRFD without the use
of a dedicated physical RRFD sensor would greatly benefit hydropower operators that do
not have funds for additional specialized physical sensors and would reduce plant
outages associated with such issues.
Subtask 2.1. Research to Identify, Evaluate, and Select Appropriate AI Techniques. Review
data from Task A along with available open-source hydropower models. Create a survey
and ranking method to identify appropriate, existing toolsets which can be incorporated
into VS-DT and identify required new functionality. Select technique(s) for further
development. This survey will evaluate techniques based on prior experience, likelihood
to exceed, and direct applicability to hydro plants identified in the market study. Physics-
based models capable of incorporating site data will be of primary interest in addition to
AI techniques that can fuse physics and measured values.
Subtask 2.2. Develop Proof-of-Concept VS-DT and Validate to Historical Data. Develop
code-based VS-DT capable of predicting rotor rim displacement to within +/-5% based on
other available instrumentation and operational history. VS-DT will be capable of self-
calibrating to historical site time-series data and providing a self-assessment of accuracy.
Predictions will include traditional instrumentation and/or rotor rim displacement as
informed by the data collection efforts in Task 1. Built in change detection will be capable
of alerting the user to abrupt or long-time scale changes to input or output signals. As
part of this effort, the VS-DT will be capable of predicting other metrics such as power
generation and expected vibration.
Subtask 2.3. Develop standalone dashboard for demonstration and use by hydropower
operators. A dynamic, interactive proof of concept is developed and shared with
interested hydropower operators. Feedback is received on functionality, accuracy,
usefulness, and information display.

5.3 FORECASTING MODEL DEVELOPMENT
Task Summary: The purpose of this task was to create a comprehensive list of methods
for forecasting hydropower signals. The team would then establish advantages &

 7

disadvantages of each method, determining the best method for the VS-DT technology
and test the most appropriate method(s) for broad application.
Problem statement: As previously mentioned, each hydropower plant is unique. This effort
applies subject matter expertise and physics-based modeling to determine how best to
forecast common hydropower signals.
Value proposition: The goal is to discover and implement the best methods for
forecasting hydropower data signals, with the overall goal of forecasting critical events
in turbines possibly long before they occur.
Task 3.1. Research Appropriate Forecasting Techniques. SSU will create an
undergraduate research team with a faculty member as a mentor to research appropriate
techniques for forecasting the available hydropower historical time-series data channels.
Metrics for accuracy, complexity, and applicability to hydropower data will be used in the
research effort. Summary of methods, pros, cons, and a test plan will be developed and
presented.
Task 3.2. Downselect and Apply Forecasting Techniques to Sensor Data. The most
promising method will be chosen and the SSU team will implement the forecasting
technique and evaluate for accuracy. Adjustments will be made as needed and additional
methods will be tested as time permits. Discussions with interested hydropower
operators will help drive accuracy and complexity decisions. This will be the main part of
student participation in research, providing an opportunity for students to gain hands-on
experience in the research process.
Task 3.3. Integrate with VS-DT Demonstration. SSU will work with TL to introduce
forecasting techniques into the VS-DT. Tool with a forecasting mode will be developed.

6 RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
6.1 TASK 1: DATA SOURCING, CLEANING, AND FLEET APPLICABILITY
Subtask 1.1. Collect Historical Data
The objective of this subtask was the collection of appropriate data to support the
development of the VS-DT. The project partner initially identified for this effort was a
utility at which physical RRFD sensors had been mounted, tested, and later removed.
Unfortunately, prolonged challenges in acquiring the underlying dataset from this
deployment made it necessary to find other utilities where similar measurement
campaigns were performed. The United States Bureau of Reclamation (USBR) was
identified, as it had performed such a test on their Yellowtail facility. USBR’s effort is
documented in their “Generator Rotor Rim to Spider Arm Attachment Data Collection”
report issued in September of 2019 [1]. The details included in the report indicated the
potential for USBR to have sufficient information to support the analysis pursued for the
VS-DT development. A meeting was held between the project team and USBR staff to
introduce the project and request assistance in receiving data affiliated with the test. The
USBR team agreed to assess their ability to provide the project team with the data, but
USBR identified that the data was stored with other information that it could not provide
to the public. The project team then decided to assess other opportunities to source data.
At the time of the project’s conclusion the EPRI team had introduced the project to a
second utility with the possibility of supporting this project and receiving a virtual sensor
to leverage in its suite of monitoring capabilities. While the utility did not have physical
sensors applied to the rotor rim, virtual sensors can be designed to provide insights for a
range of potential physical sensors. The EPRI team engaged with the utility team several
times, including a formal meeting, and was in the process of gathering additional
information requested by the utility when the project concluded. The utility was
specifically interested in the application of the virtual sensor to support the plant in the
instance of a sensor failure prior to an outage. The virtual sensor would provide insight
to plant staff on what the anticipated signals from the unit would be. The physical sensor
could then be fixed during the planned outage instead of scheduling an additional outage
or simply forgoing a sensor reading entirely.
The EPRI team had also identified a third utility that was likely to benefit from this project
as the project was concluding. This specific utility was in the process of standing up their
Monitoring and Diagnostics center and had approached EPRI to support questions they
had on capabilities and opportunities in this space. The EPRI team was preparing to
approach the utility about partnering on this project, but did not initiate contact due to
uncertainty regarding the potential for a NCTE. While this task faced setbacks due to

issues regarding the initially identified utility partner, the EPRI team pursued multiple
opportunities to gather the data necessary to complete the project.
Subtask 1.2. Perform Study of Market and Potential Uptake Analysis.
The objective of this subtask was to identify additional opportunities within the United
States hydropower fleet that could support the deployment of the virtual sensor
capabilities. As the project was initially planned to be performed on the rotor rim
connection, this effort was primarily focused on gathering information related to
standards and designs for hydropower rotors and their scaling. To support this, a
literature review of more than 30 identified articles related to rotor design and operation
was compiled. Due to the potential shift from rotor rim monitoring to another sensor
within the hydropower plant, this effort was paused prior to completion. Pausing the
market study on the rotor rim allowed for the potential preservation of project resources
if a new sensor type was selected instead of the RRFD. However, the paragraph outlined
below presents some of the findings affiliated with the RRFD-related literature survey
prior to its completion.
The first objective affiliated with the literature survey was documenting the mechanisms
by which the rotor rim is connected to the rotor spider. The rotor spider is the support
structure which connects the rotor rim to the shaft and transfers the torque to the rotor.
The rotor rim is comprised of a series of laminations which are successively layered
around the spider to form a mounting location for the field poles. It is important that the
connection between the spider and the rotor rim is secure for the structural security of
the asset rather than concerns around eddy currents [2]. There are three primary types of
connections between the rotor rim and the spider hub. The first is by a connection directly
between the spider and the rotor rim via dovetail slots machined into the ends of the
spider arms. The second method is by allowing the rotor rim to “float” freely during
operation. The third, and most common type of connection, is through the assembly of
the rotor rim surrounding the spider. In this case, a clearance is maintained between
machined slots in both the rotor and spider. The rotor is then heated so it expands,
allowing for the insertion of a key. As the rotor cools and shrinks, a compressive
connection between the rotor rim and spider is formed. If the rotor reaches runaway
speed, the centrifugal forces on the rotor rim allow for the rim expand [3].
The second objective affiliated with the literature review was understanding the scaling
affiliated with rotor characteristics. This is an important part of the market study as it
helps indicate how representative the selected rotor is to the broader hydropower fleet.
Due to data confidentiality and security reasons, fleetwide engineering drawings were not
available. At the conclusion of the project, the team was compiling information to
approximate rotor rim dimensions based on rated capacity of the unit. This involved
gathering information from the USBR’s Hydrogenerator Design Manual which

demonstrates trends in generator frame diameters in related to the rated power of the
generator. (Kirkpatrick, 1992)

7 CONCLUSIONS
The project aimed to develop a VS-DT in legacy hydropower turbines using statistical
techniques to replace traditional physical sensors. This development sought to offer
cost-saving and safety-enhancing opportunities to hydropower operators, creating
additional, cheaper ways of monitoring a critical energy asset. Despite significant
uncertainty regarding the project timeline, some progress was made in achieving this
goal. Methods were identified for forecasting signals without dedicated physical sensors.
Multiple data sources were identified for building such a technology. Collaborations with
SSU had effectively engaged undergraduate and high school students in valuable
research, providing meaningful exposure to energy-sector careers.
Unfortunately, the project's potential was cut short by contracting delays and the
unprocessed NCTE. WPTO voiced that potential continuation of project funding was
possible through an eventual NCTE, but the uncertainty of if/when the NCTE would be
issued resulted in the decision to conclude the project. Turbine Logic decided not to
proceed further given the aforementioned delays and uncertainty. To summarize,
unavailability of SSU students and lack of clarity and direction involving whether the in
person workshops remained in scope were the primary reasons for choosing not to
proceed further.
The above-mentioned delays and uncertainties prevented the completion of project
milestones, including the full development and validation of the VS-DT, its coupling with
determined forecasting methodologies, and the integration of a user-interface design.
Preliminary outcomes from the SSU team's work in evaluating forecasting techniques
such as Random Forest and Extreme Gradient Boosting were promising, and it's the
project team's hope that future work will be able to further explore this arena.
