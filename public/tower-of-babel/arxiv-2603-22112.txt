Linear combination of bilateral gamma random variables: distributional theory and approximations
Kalyan Barman and Palaniappan Vellaisamy

Abstract
. In this article, we obtain the exact distribution of a linear combination of bilateral gamma (BG) random variables (r.v.s). Next, we discuss the distributional properties of the linear combination of BG r.v.s, including probability density function, cumulant generating function and characteristic function. A Stein characterization is developed, which leads us to several distributional approximation results with explicit error bounds in both Kolmogorov and Wasserstein distances. Related limit theorems are also discussed. Furthermore, we show that the associated Lévy processes are finite-variation processes with BG distributed increments having random parameters. Finally, we apply our results in exponential stock models.

62E15; 62E17; 62P05; 60E05; 60E07

1.
Introduction

The distribution of linear combination of independent gamma random variables (r.v.s) emerge naturally in various areas of probability and statistics, see Diaconis and Perlman
[
10
]
. The authors mentioned that the distribution of such a combination is not expressible in a closed form and so discussed approximations and studied tail probabilities. Exploiting the relationship between negative binomial and gamma distributions (see
[
11
]
or
[
31
]
), Vellaisamy and Upadhye
[
28
]
derived the exact distribution of a linear combination of independent gamma r.v.s with arbitrary parameters.

The bilateral-gamma (BG) distributions form a four-parameter family with a rich distributional structure (see
[
17
]
and
[
18
]
) and the properties that are useful for applications. This family includes the variance gamma (VG), symmetric variance gamma (SVG), and Laplace distributions, as special cases. In addition, gamma and normal distributions appear as limiting cases, together with several other related distributions. The BG distributions are self-decomposable, stable under convolution, and have a simple cumulant generating function (cgf). The associated Lévy processes are finite-variation processes making infinitely many jumps at each interval with positive length, and their increments are BG distributed. Hence, the BG process provides a flexible framework for modelling fluctuations in financial markets, see K
\ddot{u}
chler and Tappe
[
19
]
.

In this paper, we investigate the distribution of linear combinations of BG r.v.s. This family includes the linear combinations of gamma r.v.s, as limiting cases, studied by Vellaisamy and Upadhye
[
28
]
. We discuss several probablistic properties of linear combinations of BG r.v.s, and show how they are related to the other distributions studied in the literature. They possess a number of properties which make them useful for applications. The distribution of a linear combination of BG r.v.s is infinitely divisible, self-decomposable, stable under convolution, and has probability density function (pdf), cgf and characteristic function (cf) in closed form. We derive also a Stein characterization for these distributions, obtain several approximation results using Stein’s method, and discuss related limit theorems. For the Kolmogorov distance, the error bounds for a sequence of compound Poisson distribution that converges to the distribution of a linear combination of BG r.v.s is ponited out. For the Wasserstein distance, an error bound on the distance between two such linear combinations is obtained. Finally, following K
\ddot{u}
chler and Tappe
[
19
]
, we show that the associated Lévy processes are finite-variation processes having infinitely many jumps at each interval with positive length, and their increments are BG distributed, but with random parameters.

The paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
, we discuss some preliminary results and in Section
3
, the exact distribution of the linear combination of BG r.v.s and their distributional properties are derived. Section
4
deals with the approximation of linear combination of BG r.v.s. In Section
5
, we discuss, following K
\ddot{\text{u}}
chler and Tappe
[
19
]
, the BG process associated
with the linear combination of BG r.v.s and apply it to the exponential stock models.

2.
Preliminary results

In this section, we introduce the notations and preliminaries required for this article.

2.1.
Bilateral-gamma distributions

We start with the definition and related results of BG distributions. Let
Ga(\alpha,p)
denote the gamma distribution with parameters
\alpha
and
p
. A BG distribution (also known as gamma difference distribution) with parameters
\alpha,\beta,p,q>0
is defined as the distribution of
X=X_{1}-X_{2}
, where
X_{1}\sim Ga(\alpha,p)
and
X_{2}\sim Ga(\beta,q)
and they are independent, see
[
12
]
. The cf of a BG distribution is

(2.1)

\displaystyle\phi_{bg}(z)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(1-iz/\alpha)^{p}(1+iz/\beta)^{q}},~~z\in\mathbb{R}

(2.2)

\displaystyle=\exp\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}}(e^{izu}-1)\nu_{bg}(du)\right),~~z\in\mathbb{R},

where
\nu_{bg}
is the Lévy measure given by

(2.3)

\displaystyle\nu_{bg}(du)=\left(\frac{p}{u}e^{-\alpha u}\mathbf{1}_{(0,\infty)}(u)-\frac{q}{u}e^{-\beta|u|}\mathbf{1}_{(-\infty,0)}(u)\right)du.

Taking Fourier transform (FT) on (
2.1
), the pdf of the
BG(\alpha,p,\beta,q)
distribution has the integral form (see Equation 1.4 of
[
12
]
)

(2.4)

\displaystyle h_{X}(x)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{e^{-ixz}}{(1-iz/\alpha)^{p}(1+iz/\beta)^{q}}dz.

An alternative form of the pdf of the
BG(\alpha,p,\beta,q)
distribution follows from the convolution structure (see Equation 1.5 of
[
12
]
)

(2.5)

\displaystyle h_{X}(x)=\frac{\alpha^{p}\beta^{q}}{\Gamma p\Gamma q}\begin{cases}e^{\beta x}\int_{x}^{\infty}y^{p-1}(y-x)^{q-1}e^{-(\alpha+\beta)y}dy,&~x\in(0,\infty)\\
e^{-\alpha x}\int_{-x}^{\infty}y^{q-1}(y+x)^{p-1}e^{-(\alpha+\beta)y}dy,&~x\in(-\infty,0).\end{cases}

Some notable special cases and limiting distributions of the BG family are as follows
(see
[
17
]
). Let
\overset{d}{=}
denote the equality in distribution.

(i)

When
q=p
, then
BG(\alpha,p,\beta,p)\overset{d}{=}VG(\alpha,\beta,p)
(variance-gamma distribution).

(ii)

When
\beta=\alpha
and
q=p
, then
BG(\alpha,p,\alpha,p)\overset{d}{=}SVG(\alpha,p)
(symmetric variance-gamma distribution).

(iii)

The limiting case as
p\to\infty
, the
SVG(\sqrt{2p}/\alpha,p)
converges in law to a normal
\mathcal{N}(0,\alpha^{2})
distribution.

(iv)

The limiting case as
\beta\to\infty
, the
BG(\alpha,p,\beta,q)
converges in law to gamma
Ga(\alpha,p)
distribution.

Note that the
BG
distributions are infinitely divisible and self-decomposable, see
[
17
]
. Let now
X\sim BG(\alpha,p,\beta,q)
. Then the
k
-th cumulant (see
[
17
, Section 2]
) is given by

(2.6)

\displaystyle C_{k}(X)

\displaystyle=(k-1)!\left(\frac{p}{\alpha^{k}}+(-1)^{k}\frac{q}{\beta^{k}}\right),~k\geq 1.

In particular, the first two cumulants (that is, mean and variance) of
X
are

C_{1}(X)=\mathbb{E}(X)=\left(\frac{p}{\alpha}-\frac{q}{\beta}\right)\text{ and }C_{2}(X)=\text{Var}(X)=\left(\frac{p}{\alpha^{2}}+\frac{q}{\beta^{2}}\right).
For more details of BG distributions, we refer the reader to
[
17
]
and
[
18
]
.

2.2.
Function spaces and probability metrics

Let
\mathbb{N}=\{1,2,\ldots\}\text{ and }\mathbb{N}_{0}=\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
denote henceforth the set of positive and
non-negative integers, respectively.

The Schwartz space is defined as

\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}):=\Big\{f\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}):\lim_{|x|\to\infty}|x^{m}f^{(n)}(x)|=0,\ \ \forall\,m,n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}\Big\},

where
f^{(n)}
denotes the
n
-th derivative of
f
, with the convention
f^{(0)}=f
, and

C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
is the space of smooth functions on
\mathbb{R}
. It is well known that the Forier-transform (FT) is an automorphism on
\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R})
.
For
f\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R})
, we define its FT by
\widehat{f}(u):=\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{-iux}f(x)\,dx,u\in\mathbb{R},
and its inverse FT by
f(x):=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{iux}\widehat{f}(u)\,du,x\in\mathbb{R},
see Stein and Shakarchi
[
26
]
.

We now introduce the notion of smooth Wasserstein distance (see
[
2
]
or
[
3
]
). For a fixed integer
r\in\mathbb{N}
, consider the function class

(2.7)

\mathcal{W}_{r}:=\Big\{h:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}\;\Big|\;h\text{ is $r$-times differentiable and }\|h^{(k)}\|\leq 1,\;k=0,1,\ldots,r\Big\},

where
\|h\|:=\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}}|h(x)|
. Then, for two random variables
Y
and
Z
, the smooth Wasserstein distance of order
r
is defined by

(2.8)

d_{r}(Y,Z):=d_{\mathcal{W}_{r}}(Y,Z):=\sup_{h\in\mathcal{W}_{r}}\left|\mathbb{E}[h(Y)]-\mathbb{E}[h(Z)]\right|.

Special choices of the function class
\mathcal{W}_{r}
lead to well-known probability metrics:

\displaystyle d_{K}(Y,Z)

\displaystyle=\Big\{h:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}\;\big|\;h=\mathbf{1}_{(-\infty,x]},~x\in\mathbb{R}\Big\},

\displaystyle d_{W}(Y,Z)

\displaystyle=\Big\{h:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}\;\big|\;h\text{ is 1-Lipschitz, i.e., }|h(x)-h(y)|\leq|x-y|\Big\},

\displaystyle d_{TV}(Y,Z)

\displaystyle=\Big\{h:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}\;\big|\;h=\mathbf{1}_{A},~A\in\mathfrak{B}(\mathbb{R})\Big\},

where
d_{K}(Y,Z)
is the Kolmogorov distance,

d_{W}(Y,Z)
is the Wasserstein distance, and
d_{TV}(Y,Z)
is the total variation distance (see
[
2
]
). Since
\mathfrak{B}(\mathbb{R})
are the Borel subsets of
\mathbb{R}
, we note that (see,
[
8
, Lecture 2]
)

(2.9)

\displaystyle d_{K}(Y,Z)\leq d_{TV}(Y,Z).

It is shown in Appendix A of
[
2
]
that, for
r\geq 2
,

(2.10)

\displaystyle d_{r-1}(Y,Z)

\displaystyle\leq 3\sqrt{2}\,\sqrt{\,d_{r}(Y,Z)},\text{ and}

(2.11)

\displaystyle d_{1}(Y,Z)

\displaystyle\leq\Big(3\sqrt{2}\Big)^{\sum_{i=1}^{r-1}2^{-(i-1)}}\Big(d_{r}(Y,Z)\Big)^{2^{-(r-1)}}.

Moreover, there exists a universal constant
c>0
such that

d_{K}(Y,Z)\;\leq\;c\,\sqrt{\,d_{1}(Y,Z)}.

Combining this with (
2.11
), we obtain the useful bound

d_{K}(Y,Z)\;\leq\;c\Big(3\sqrt{2}\Big)^{\,1-2^{1-r}}\Big(d_{r}(Y,Z)\Big)^{2^{-r}},\qquad r\geq 2.

Finally, the following order relationship between the distances holds for all
r\geq 1
(see
[
2
, equation 2.16]
):

(2.12)

d_{r}(Y,Z)\;\leq\;d_{1}(Y,Z)\;\leq\;d_{W}(Y,Z).

2.3.
Essence of Stein’s Method

Stein’s method is based on the observation that a real-valued rv
Z
has distribution
F_{Z}
if and only if there exists a suitable operator
\mathcal{A}
, called the Stein operator, such that

\mathbb{E}\big[\mathcal{A}f(Z)\big]=0,

for all
f
belonging to a “nice” class of functions
\mathcal{F}
. This characterization naturally leads to the Stein equation

(2.13)

\mathcal{A}f(x)=h(x)-\mathbb{E}[h(Z)],

where
h
is a real-valued test function. Replacing
x
by
Y
and taking expectations on both sides of (
2.13
) yields

(2.14)

\mathbb{E}[h(Y)]-\mathbb{E}[h(Z)]=\mathbb{E}\big[\mathcal{A}f(Y)\big].

The identity (
2.14
) plays a crucial role in Stein’s method. For a given test function
h
, bounding the quantity
\big|\mathbb{E}[h(Y)]-\mathbb{E}[h(Z)]\big|
reduces to obtaining suitable estimates on the solution
f
of the Stein equation (
2.13
) together with information about the distribution of
Y
. For further details and applications of Stein’s method, we refer the reader to the monograph
[
22
]
and the references therein.

3.
Linear combinations of BG random variables

In this section, we discuss various properties of the distribution of linear combinations of BG r.v.s. First, we obtain the exact distribution of difference of linear combinations of gamma r.v.s with arbitrary parameters, which is a generalization of Theorem 3.1 of
[
28
]
. In
[
10
]
, the authors mentioned that the distribution of sum of independent gamma r.v.s is not expressible in a closed form and so discussed approximations and studied tail probabilities. Using the connection between the negative binomial and gamma distributions (see
[
11
]
or
[
31
]
), Vellaisamy and Upadhye
[
28
]
obtain the exact distribution of weighted sums of independent gamma random variables with arbitrary parameters.

3.1.
Notations

Let
X_{1},X_{2},\ldots,X_{n}
be independent random variables, where
X_{j}\sim Ga(\alpha_{j},p_{j})
and
Y_{1},Y_{2},\ldots,Y_{n}
be independent random variables, where
Y_{j}\sim Ga(\beta_{j},q_{j})
. Let
p=\sum_{j=1}^{n}p_{j}
and
q=\sum_{j=1}^{n}q_{j}
. For
1\leq j\leq n
, let
w_{j}^{(1)},w_{j}^{(2)}>0
be positive constants.

Define now

(3.1)

\displaystyle T_{n}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}(w_{j}^{(1)}X_{j}-w_{j}^{(2)}Y_{j}),~n\geq 1.

Note when
w_{j}^{(1)}=w_{j}^{(2)},1\leq j\leq n,
we get a linear combination of BG r.v.s.

Next, we introduce the following notations:

(3.2)

\displaystyle\alpha=\max_{1\leq j\leq n}\frac{\alpha_{j}}{w_{j}^{(1)}+\alpha_{j}}\text{ and }\beta=\max_{1\leq j\leq n}\frac{\beta_{j}}{w_{j}^{(2)}+\beta_{j}},

and for
i,n\in\mathbb{N}
,

\displaystyle c_{n}=\prod_{j=1}^{n}\Big(\frac{(1-\alpha)\alpha_{j}}{w_{j}^{(1)}\alpha}\Big)^{p_{j}},\quad d_{n}=\prod_{j=1}^{n}\Big(\frac{(1-\beta)\beta_{j}}{w_{j}^{(2)}\beta}\Big)^{q_{j}}

\displaystyle a_{i}=\frac{1}{i}\sum_{j=1}^{n}p_{j}\Big(\frac{1-(1-\alpha)\alpha_{j}}{w_{j}^{(1)}\alpha}\Big)^{i},\quad b_{i}=\frac{1}{i}\sum_{j=1}^{n}q_{j}\Big(\frac{1-(1-\beta)\beta_{j}}{w_{j}^{(2)}\beta}\Big)^{i}.

Let
L_{n}
and
M_{n}
be two discrete r.v.s with probability distributions (see
[
28
]
), for

(3.3)

\displaystyle P(L_{n}=k)=c_{n}\gamma_{k},~~P(M_{n}=k)=d_{n}\delta_{k},~~k\geq 1,

where
\gamma_{0}=1
,
\gamma_{k}=\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=1}^{k}ia_{i}\gamma_{k-i}
and
\delta_{0}=1
,
\delta_{k}=\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=1}^{k}ib_{i}\delta_{k-i}
, for
k\geq 1.
.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
T_{n}
be defined as in (
3.1
). Then
T_{n}\sim BG(\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha},L_{n}+p,\frac{\beta}{1-\beta},M_{n}+q)
, where
\alpha,\beta
are defined in (
3.2
) and the r.v.s
L_{n}
and
M_{n}
have distributions defined in (
3.3
).

Proof.

First note that

(3.4)

\displaystyle T_{n}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}(w_{j}^{(1)}X_{j}-w_{j}^{(2)}Y_{j})=\sum_{j=1}^{n}w_{j}^{(1)}X_{j}-\sum_{j=1}^{n}w_{j}^{(2)}Y_{j}.

From Theorem 3.1 of
[
28
]
, we get
\sum_{j=1}^{n}w_{j}^{(1)}X_{j}\sim Ga(\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha},L_{n}+p)
and
\sum_{j=1}^{n}w_{j}^{(2)}Y_{j}\sim Ga(\frac{\beta}{1-\beta},M_{n}+q)
. It follows, from the definition of BG distribution, that
T_{n}\sim BG(\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha},L_{n}+p,\frac{\beta}{1-\beta},M_{n}+q)
, which proves the result.
∎

Since the parameters
L_{n}
and
M_{n}
are random, the distribution of
T_{n}
is indeed a mixture of BG distributions.

The following corollary easily follows.

Corollary 3.2
.

Let the conditions of Theorem
3.1
hold and
\alpha,\beta
be defined in (
3.2
). Let
L_{n}
and
M_{n}
be defined in (
3.3
).

(i)

If
\beta\to 1
, as
n\to\infty
, then
T_{n}\overset{\mathfrak{L}}{\to}X,
where
X\sim Ga(\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha},L_{n}+p).

(ii)

If
\alpha\to 1
, as
n\to\infty
, then
T_{n}\overset{\mathfrak{L}}{\to}-Y,
where
Y\sim Ga(\frac{\beta}{1-\beta},M_{n}+q).

Proof.

(i)
Using Theorem
3.1
together with (
2.1
) the cf of
T_{n}\sim BG(\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha},L_{n}+p,\frac{\beta}{1-\beta},M_{n}+q)
can be seen as

(3.5)

\displaystyle\phi_{T_{n}}(z)=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{P(L_{n}=j)P(M_{n}=k)}{\left(1-\frac{iz(1-\alpha)}{\alpha}\right)^{p+j}\left(1+\frac{iz(1-\beta)}{\beta}\right)^{q+k}},~~z\in\mathbb{R}.

Next taking
\beta\to 1

in (
3.5
), we get

(3.6)

\displaystyle\phi_{X}(z)=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{P(L_{n}=j)}{\left(1-\frac{iz(1-\alpha)}{\alpha}\right)^{p+j}},~~z\in\mathbb{R}.

which is the cf of
X=\sum_{j=1}^{n}w_{j}^{(1)}X_{j}
, where
X\sim Ga(\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha},L_{n}+p)
(see Theorem 3.1 of
[
28
]
).

(ii)
Similarly, taking
\alpha\to 1
in (
3.5
), Part (ii) follows. This proves the result.
∎

Remark 3.3
.

When
w_{j}^{(1)}=w_{j}^{(2)}=w_{j}
,
j\geq 1
, Theorem
3.1
yields the convolution of
n
-independent BG r.v.s with arbitrary parameters. Note Theorem
3.1
gives a simple random parameter representation for the distribution of
T_{n}
, which may be helpful for analytical or inferential purposes.

Next, we obtain pdf, moments, cumulants and Stein characterizations of linear combinations of BG r.v.s.
The Stein characterization will be required when we will consider approximation for the distribution of linear combination of BG r.v.s.

3.2.
Probability density function

Taking FT on (
3.5
), the pdf of
T_{n}\sim BG(\eta,L_{n}+p,\xi,M_{n}+q)
has the integral form

(3.7)

\displaystyle h_{T_{n}}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi}\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{P(L_{n}=j)P(M_{n}=k)e^{-ixz}}{(1-iz/\eta)^{p+j}(1+iz/\xi)^{q+k}}~dz.

where
\eta=\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}
and
\xi=\frac{\beta}{1-\beta}
.

Let

\displaystyle k_{1}(x):=

\displaystyle\int_{x}^{\infty}y^{p+j-1}(y-x)^{q+k-1}e^{-(\eta+\xi)y}dy,~x\in(0,\infty)

\displaystyle k_{2}(x):=

\displaystyle\int_{-x}^{\infty}y^{q+k-1}(y+x)^{p+j-1}e^{-(\eta+\xi)y}dy,~x\in(-\infty,0).

Using Theorem
3.1
together with equation (
2.5
), an alternative form of the pdf of
T_{n}\sim BG(\eta,L_{n}+p,\xi,M_{n}+q)
follows due to convolution structure:

\displaystyle h_{T_{n}}(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{P(L_{n}=j)\eta^{p+j}P(M_{n}=k)\xi^{q+k}}{\Gamma(p+j)\Gamma(q+k)}\big(e^{\xi x}k_{1}(x)\mathbf{1}_{(0,\infty)}(x)

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\quad+e^{-\eta x}k_{2}(x)\mathbf{1}_{(-\infty,0)}(x)\big)

\displaystyle=c_{n}d_{n}\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\gamma_{j}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\delta_{k}\frac{\eta^{p+j}\xi^{q+k}}{\Gamma(p+j)\Gamma(q+k)}\big(e^{\xi x}k_{1}(x)\mathbf{1}_{(0,\infty)}(x)

(3.8)

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\quad+e^{-\eta x}k_{2}(x)\mathbf{1}_{(-\infty,0)}(x)\big)

Let
F(a,b,x)
denote the confluent hypergeometric function (see
[
6
, equation (78)]
), given by

(3.9)

\displaystyle F(a,b,x)=\frac{1}{\Gamma a}\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-xt}t^{a-1}(1+t)^{b-a-1}dt,~\text{Re}(a),\text{Re}(b),\text{Re}(x)>0.

From (
3.2
), consider the first integral

\displaystyle I_{1}(x)

\displaystyle=\int_{x}^{\infty}y^{p+j-1}(y-x)^{q+k-1}e^{-(\eta+\xi)y}dy,~x\in(0,\infty)

\displaystyle=e^{-(\eta+\xi)x}\int_{0}^{\infty}(x+u)^{p+j-1}u^{q+k-1}e^{-(\eta+\xi)u}du

(3.10)

\displaystyle=e^{-(\eta+\xi)x}x^{p+q+j+k-1}\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-(\eta+\xi)xt}t^{q+k-1}(1+t)^{p+j-1}dt.

Using (
3.9
) in (
3.2
), we write

(3.11)

\displaystyle I_{1}(x)=e^{-(\eta+\xi)x}x^{p+q+j+k-1}\Gamma(q+k)F(q+k,p+q+j+k,(\eta+\xi)x),~x\in(0,\infty).

From (
3.2
), consider the second integral

(3.12)

\displaystyle I_{2}(x)=\int_{-x}^{\infty}y^{q+k-1}(y+x)^{p+j-1}e^{-(\eta+\xi)y}dy,~x\in(-\infty,0).

Following the steps similar to previous integral, we have for
~x\in(-\infty,0)
,

(3.13)

\displaystyle I_{2}(x)=e^{(\eta+\xi)x}(-x)^{p+q+j+k-1}\Gamma(q+k)F(p+j,p+q+j+k,-(\eta+\xi)x).

Using (
3.11
) and (
3.13
) in (
3.2
), we can write the pdf of
T_{n}
as

(3.14)

\displaystyle h_{T_{n}}(x)

\displaystyle=c_{n}d_{n}\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\gamma_{j}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\delta_{k}\frac{\eta^{p+j}\xi^{q+k}}{\Gamma(p+j)\Gamma(q+k)}K_{2}(x),

where

(3.15)

\displaystyle K_{2}(x)=\small{\begin{cases}e^{-\eta x}x^{p+q+j+k-1}\Gamma(q+k)F(q+k,p+q+j+k,(\eta+\xi)x),~~x\in(0,\infty)\\
e^{\xi x}(-x)^{p+q+j+k-1}\Gamma(q+k)F(p+j,p+q+j+k,-(\eta+\xi)x),~~x\in(-\infty,0).\end{cases}}

Remark 3.4
.

(i) Scheuer
[
25
]
obtained the pdf of
S_{n}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i},
where
X_{i}\sim Ga(\alpha_{i},p_{i})
are independent r.v.s, in terms of double series involving higher order derivatives in the inner terms. Later, Vellaisamy and Upadhye
[
28
, Equation 4.4]
obtained a simpler expression for the pdf of
S_{n}
, represented as a single series involving the distribution of the random variable (r.v.)

.

Note that, if
\xi\to\infty
in (
3.7
), we get

\displaystyle h_{S_{n}}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi}\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{P(L_{n}=j)e^{-ixz}}{(1-iz/\eta)^{p+j}}~dz

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi}\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}P(L_{n}=j)\eta^{p+j}\int_{\mathbb{R}}(\eta-iz)^{-(p+j)}e^{-ixz}~dz

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}P(L_{n}=j)\frac{\eta^{p+j}x^{p+j-1}e^{-\eta x}}{\Gamma(p+j)}

(3.16)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}c_{n}\gamma_{j}\frac{\eta^{p+j}x^{p+j-1}e^{-\eta x}}{\Gamma(p+j)},~x>0,

which is the pdf of
S_{n}
, see
[
28
, Equation 4.4]
.

Similarly, if
\eta\to\infty
in (
3.7
), we get

\displaystyle h_{S_{n}}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}P(M_{n}=k)\xi^{p+j}\int_{\mathbb{R}}(\xi+iz)^{-(q+j)}e^{-ixz}~dz

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}P(M_{n}=j)\frac{\xi^{q+j}(-x)^{q+j-1}e^{\xi x}}{\Gamma(q+j)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}d_{n}\delta_{j}\frac{\eta^{q+j}(-x)^{q+j-1}e^{\xi x}}{\Gamma(q+j)},~x<0,

which is the pdf of
V_{n}=-\sum_{j=1}^{n}w_{j}^{(2)}Y_{j}
.

3.3.
Moment generating function

By Theorem
3.1
, the mgf of
T_{n}
is given by

(3.17)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(e^{zT_{n}})

\displaystyle=\bigg(\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}P(L_{n}=j)\left(\frac{\eta}{\eta-z}\right)^{p+j}\bigg)\bigg(\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}P(M_{n}=k)\left(\frac{\xi}{\xi+z}\right)^{q+k}\bigg),~z\in\mathbb{R}

(3.18)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{L_{n}}\bigg(\left(\frac{\eta}{\eta-z}\right)^{p+L_{n}}\bigg)\mathbb{E}_{M_{n}}\bigg(\left(\frac{\xi}{\xi+z}\right)^{q+M_{n}}\bigg),~z\in\mathbb{R},

where
\mathbb{E}_{L_{n}}
and
\mathbb{E}_{M_{n}}
are expectations with respect to r.v.s
L_{n}
and
M_{n}
, respectively.

3.4.
Moments

Note from Theorem
3.1
that
T_{n}:=S_{n}-V_{n}
, where
S_{n}\sim Ga(\eta,L_{n}+p)
and
V_{n}\sim Ga(\xi,M_{n}+q)
(see (
3.4
)). Hence, we have for
k\in\mathbb{N},

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left(T_{n}^{k}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left((S_{n}-V_{n})^{k}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{k}\binom{k}{j}(-1)^{j}\mathbb{E}(S_{n}^{k-j})\mathbb{E}(V_{n}^{j})

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{k}\binom{k}{j}(-1)^{j}\frac{1}{\eta^{k-j}\xi^{j}}\bigg(\sum_{l=0}^{\infty}P(L_{n}=l)\frac{\Gamma(l+p+k-j)}{\Gamma(l+p)}\bigg)

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\times\bigg(\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}P(M_{n}=m)\frac{\Gamma(m+q+j)}{\Gamma(m+q)}\bigg)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{k}\binom{k}{j}(-1)^{j}\frac{1}{\eta^{k-j}\xi^{j}}\bigg(\sum_{l=0}^{\infty}c_{n}\gamma_{l}\frac{\Gamma(l+p+k-j)}{\Gamma(l+p)}\bigg)

(3.19)

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\times\bigg(\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}d_{n}\delta_{m}\frac{\Gamma(m+q+j)}{\Gamma(m+q)}\bigg).

3.5.
Characterizations

In this section, we derive a Stein characterization for
T_{n}
. Before stating the result, recall that the BG distributions are infinitely divisible and self-decomposable, see
[
17
]
. Also,
T_{n}
can be viewed as
n
-independent sums of BG r.v.s and it has BG distribution, but with random parameters. Hence, the distribution of
T_{n}
is infinitely divisible and self-decomposable. Moreover, the Lévy-Khintchine representation of the cf of
T_{n}
as (
2.2
) is given by

\displaystyle\phi_{T_{n}}(z)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\bigg(e^{izT_{n}}\bigg)=\mathbb{E}\bigg(e^{iz\left(\sum_{j=1}^{n}(w_{j}^{(1)}X_{j}-w_{j}^{(2)}Y_{j}\right)}\bigg),~z\in\mathbb{R}

(3.20)

\displaystyle=\prod_{j=1}^{n}\left(\frac{\alpha_{j}}{\alpha_{j}-izw_{j}^{(1)}}\right)^{p_{j}}\left(\frac{\beta_{j}}{\beta_{j}+izw_{j}^{(2)}}\right)^{q_{j}},~z\in\mathbb{R}

(3.21)

\displaystyle=\prod_{j=1}^{n}\exp\left(\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}(e^{izu}-1)\nu_{j}(du)\right),~z\in\mathbb{R}~~(\text{use Frullani identity}),

where
\nu_{j}(du)=\left(\frac{p_{j}}{u}e^{-\frac{\alpha_{j}u}{w_{j}^{(1)}}}\mathbf{1}_{(0,\infty)}(u)-\frac{q_{j}}{u}e^{-\frac{\beta_{j}|u|}{w_{j}^{(2)}}}\mathbf{1}_{(-\infty,0)}(u)\right)du.
Also, the cf (
3.21
) is

(3.22)

\displaystyle\phi_{T_{n}}(z)=\exp\left(\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}(e^{izu}-1)\nu_{T_{n}}(du)\right),~z\in\mathbb{R},

where
\nu_{T_{n}}
is the Lévy measure given by

(3.23)

\displaystyle\nu_{T_{n}}(du)=\frac{1}{u}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left(p_{j}e^{-\frac{\alpha_{j}u}{w_{j}^{(1)}}}\mathbf{1}_{(0,\infty)}(u)-q_{j}e^{-\frac{\beta_{j}|u|}{w_{j}^{(2)}}}\mathbf{1}_{(-\infty,0)}(u)\right)du.

When
n=1
,
w_{j}^{(1)}=w_{j}^{(2)}=1
,
\nu_{T_{1}}
is the Lévy measure of
BG(\alpha_{1},p_{1},\beta_{1},q_{1})
distribution, see
[
17
]
. Also, the
k
th order cumulant
C_{k}(T_{n}):=i^{-k}\frac{d^{k}}{dz^{k}}\log\phi_{T_{n}}(z)|_{z=0}
is

(3.24)

\displaystyle C_{k}(T_{n})=(k-1)!\int_{\mathbb{{R}}}u^{k}\nu_{T_{n}}(du),~k\in\mathbb{N}.

Proposition 3.5
.

Let
T_{n}
be defined as in (
3.1
). Then,

(3.25)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\bigg(T_{n}f(T_{n})-\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{{R}}}uf(T_{n}+u)\nu_{T_{n}}(du)\bigg)=0,~~f\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}),

where
\nu_{T_{n}}
is the Lévy measure given in (
3.23
).

Proof.

Taking logarithms on both sides of (
3.22
), and differentiating with respect to
z
,

(3.26)

\phi^{\prime}_{T_{n}}(z)=i\phi_{T_{n}}(z)\int_{\mathbb{R}}ue^{izu}\nu_{T_{n}}(du).

Let
h_{T_{n}}(x)
denote density of
T_{n}
. Then

(3.27)

\phi_{T_{n}}(z)=\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{izx}h_{T_{n}}(x)dx~~\text{and}~~\phi^{\prime}_{T_{n}}(z)=i\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}xe^{izx}h_{T_{n}}(x)dx.

Substituting (
3.27
) into (
3.26
) and rearranging the integrals, we have

(3.28)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}xe^{izx}h_{T_{n}}(x)dx-\phi_{T_{n}}(z)\int_{\mathbb{R}}ue^{izu}\nu_{T_{n}}(du)=0

The second integral of (
3.28
) can be written as

\displaystyle\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}}ue^{izu}\nu_{T_{n}}(du)\right)\phi_{T_{n}}(z)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}ue^{izu}e^{izx}h_{T_{n}}(x)dx\nu_{T_{n}}(du)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}ue^{iz(u+x)}\nu_{T_{n}}(du)h_{T_{n}}(x)dx

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}ue^{izy}\nu_{T_{n}}(du)h_{T_{n}}(y-u)dy

(3.29)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{izx}\int_{\mathbb{R}}uh_{T_{n}}(x-u)\nu_{T_{n}}(du)dx.

Substituting (
3.29
) into (
3.28
), we have

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}xe^{izx}h_{T_{n}}(x)dx-\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{izx}\int_{\mathbb{R}}uh_{T_{n}}(x-u)\nu_{T_{n}}(du)dx

(3.30)

\displaystyle=\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{izx}\left(xh_{T_{n}}(x)-\int_{\mathbb{R}}uh_{T_{n}}(x-u)\nu_{T_{n}}(du)\right)dx.

Applying FT to (
3.30
), multiplying with
f\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}),
and integrating over
\mathbb{R},
we get

(3.31)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}f(x)\left(xh_{T_{n}}(x)-\int_{\mathbb{R}}uh_{T_{n}}(x-u)\nu_{T_{n}}(du)\right)dx=0.

The second integral of (
3.31
) can be seen as

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}uf(x)h_{T_{n}}(x-u)\nu_{T_{n}}(du)dx

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}uf(y+u)h_{T_{n}}(y)\nu_{T_{n}}(du)dy

(3.32)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}}f(T_{n}+u)u\nu_{T_{n}}(du)\right).

Substituting (
3.32
) into (
3.31
), we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left(T_{n}f(T_{n})-\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}f(T_{n}+u)u\nu_{T_{n}}(du)\right)=0,

which proves (
3.25
).

Assume conversely, (
3.25
) holds for
\nu_{T_{n}}
defined in (
3.23
). For any
s\in\mathbb{R}
, let
f(x)=e^{isx}
,
x\in\mathbb{R}
, then (
3.25
) becomes

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left(T_{n}e^{isT_{n}}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{is(T_{n}+u)}u\nu_{T_{n}}(du)\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left(e^{isT_{n}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{isu}u\nu_{T_{n}}(du)\right).

Setting
\phi_{T_{n}}(s)=\mathbb{E}(e^{isX})
, then

(3.33)

\phi_{T_{n}}^{\prime}(s)=i\phi_{T_{n}}(s)\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{isu}u\nu_{T_{n}}(du).

Integrating out the real and imaginary parts of (
3.33
) leads, for any
z\geq 0
, to

\displaystyle\phi_{T_{n}}(z)

\displaystyle=\exp\left(i\int_{0}^{z}\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{isu}u\nu_{T_{n}}(du)ds\right)

\displaystyle=\exp\left(i\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{0}^{z}e^{isu}dsu\nu_{T_{n}}(du)\right)

\displaystyle=\exp\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}}(e^{izu}-1)\nu_{T_{n}}(du)\right).

A similar computation for
z\leq 0
completes the derivation of cf of
T_{n}
.
∎

3.6.
Stein equation

Let
T_{n}
be defined as in (
3.1
). Then, from Proposition
3.5
, a Stein equation for
T_{n}
, is

(3.34)

\mathcal{A}f(x)=-xf(x)+\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}f(x+u)u\nu_{T_{n}}(du)=h(x)-\mathbb{E}(h(T_{n})),

where
h\in\mathcal{M}
, a class of test functions. For a given
h\in\mathcal{M}
, we now use the semigroup technique to obtain
f_{h}
that satisfies the integral equation (
3.34
). Barbour
[
5
]
developed this method for solving Stein equations, and Arras and Houdré
[
2
]
extended it for infinitely divisible distributions with a finite first moment. Following Barbour’s approach
[
5
]
, we choose a family of operators
(P_{t})_{t\geq 0}
, for all
x\in\mathbb{R}
, as

(3.35)

P_{t}f(x):=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\widehat{f}(y)e^{iyxe^{-t}}\phi_{t}(y)dy,~~f\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{{R}}),

where
\hat{f}
is the FT of
f
and

(3.36)

\displaystyle\phi_{t}(y):=\frac{\phi_{T_{n}}(y)}{\phi_{T_{n}}(e^{-t}y)},~y\in\mathbb{{R}}.

Since the distribution of
T_{n}
is self-decomposable,
\phi_{t}
is a cf
[
24
, p.90]
of some rv
X_{(t)}
, say. That is, for all
y\in\mathbb{R},
and
t\geq 0,

(3.37)

\displaystyle\phi_{t}(y)=\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{iyu}F_{X_{(t)}}(du),

where
F_{X_{(t)}}
is the law of
X_{(t)}
. Using (
3.37
), we get

\displaystyle P_{t}f(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\widehat{f}(y)e^{iyxe^{-t}}e^{iyu}F_{X_{(t)}}(du)dy

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\widehat{f}(y)e^{iy(u+xe^{-t})}F_{X_{(t)}}(du)dy

(3.38)

\displaystyle=\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}f(u+xe^{-t})F_{X_{(t)}}(du),

where the last step follows by inverse FT (see Section
2.2
).

Proposition 3.6
.

Let
(P_{t})_{t\geq 0}
be a family of operators defined in (
3.35
). Then

(i)

(P_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is a
\mathbb{C}_{0}
-semigroup on
\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{{R}})
.

(ii)

Its generator
L
is given by

\displaystyle Lf(x)

\displaystyle=-xf^{\prime}(x)+\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}f^{\prime}(x+u)u\nu_{T_{n}}(du),~~f\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R})

(3.39)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{A}f^{\prime}(x),

where
\mathcal{A}
is defined in (
3.34
).

Following the steps similar to Proposition 3.8 and Lemma 3.10 of
[
29
]
, the proof follows.

Next, we provide a solution of the Stein equation in (
3.34
).

Theorem 3.7
.

Let
T_{n}
be defined as in (
3.1
) and
h\in\mathcal{W}_{r}
, defined in (
2.7
). Then the function
f_{h}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
defined by

(3.40)

f_{h}(x):=-\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{d}{dx}P_{t}h(x)dt,

solves (
3.34
).

Proof.

Let

g_{h}(x)=-\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\bigg(P_{t}(h)(x)-\mathbb{E}h(T_{n})\bigg)dt.

Then
g_{h}^{\prime}(x)=f_{h}(x).
Also from (
3.39
), we get

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}f_{h}(x)

\displaystyle=-xf_{h}(x)+\int_{\mathbb{R}}f_{h}(x+u)u\nu_{T_{n}}(du)=Lg_{h}(x)

\displaystyle=-\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}LP_{t}(h)(x)dt

\displaystyle=-\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{d}{dt}P_{t}h(x)dt\text{ (see \cite[cite]{[\@@bibref{}{nourdin}{}{}, p.68]})}

\displaystyle=P_{0}h(x)-P_{\infty}h(x)

\displaystyle=h(x)-\mathbb{E}h(T_{n})~(\text{by Proposition \ref{PP2:proSem}}).

Hence,
f_{h}
is the solution to (
3.34
). ∎

Next, we obtain some regularity conditions of
f_{h}
.

Theorem 3.8
.

For
h\in\mathcal{W}_{k+1}
, let
f_{h}
be defined in (
3.40
). Then

(3.41)

\displaystyle\|f_{h}^{(k)}\|\leq\frac{1}{k+1},~k\geq 0,

where
f^{(k)}
,
k\geq 1
, denotes the
k
th derivative of
f
with
f^{(0)}=f
. Also,

(3.42)

\displaystyle|xf_{h}^{\prime}(x)|\leq 2+\frac{1}{2}|\mathbb{E}(T_{n})|\text{ and }|xf_{h}^{\prime\prime}(x)|\leq 2+\frac{1}{3}|\mathbb{E}(T_{n})|.

Proof.

For
h\in\mathcal{W}_{k+1}
,

\displaystyle\|f_{h}\|

\displaystyle=\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}}\left|-\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{d}{dx}P_{t}h(x)dt\right|

\displaystyle=\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}}\left|-\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-t}\int_{\mathbb{R}}h^{(1)}(xe^{-t}+y)F_{X_{(t)}}(dy)dt\right|

\displaystyle\leq\|h^{(1)}\|\left|\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-t}dt\right|=\|h^{(1)}\|\leq 1.

Since
f_{h}
is
k
-times differentiable, we have

\displaystyle\|f_{h}^{(1)}\|

\displaystyle=\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}}\left|-\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-2t}\int_{\mathbb{R}}h^{(2)}(xe^{-t}+y)F_{X_{(t)}}(dy)dt\right|

\displaystyle\leq\|h^{(2)}\|\left|\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-2t}dt\right|

\displaystyle=\frac{\|h^{(2)}\|}{2}\leq\frac{1}{2}.

Indeed, it follows by induction that
\|f_{h}^{(k)}\|\leq\frac{1}{k+1},~k\geq 1.

Now differentiating both sides of (
3.34
) gives

(3.43)

\displaystyle-xf^{\prime}(x)-f(x)+\int_{\mathbb{{R}}}f^{\prime}(x+u)u\nu_{T_{n}}(du)=h^{\prime}(x).

So,

\displaystyle|xf^{\prime}(x)|

\displaystyle\leq\|f\|+\|h^{\prime}\|+\left|\int_{\mathbb{{R}}}f^{\prime}(x+u)u\nu_{T_{n}}(du)\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left(2+\|f^{\prime}\|\left|\int_{\mathbb{{R}}}u\nu_{T_{n}}(du)\right|\right)

\displaystyle=2+\frac{1}{2}|C_{1}(T_{n})|~(\text{see }\eqref{cumu})

\displaystyle=2+\frac{1}{2}|\mathbb{E}(T_{n})|.

Again differentiating both sides of (
3.43
) gives

(3.44)

\displaystyle-xf^{\prime\prime}(x)-2f^{\prime}(x)+\int_{\mathbb{{R}}}f^{\prime\prime}(x+u)u\nu_{T_{n}}(du)=h^{\prime\prime}(x).

So,

\displaystyle|xf^{\prime\prime}(x)|

\displaystyle\leq 2\|f^{\prime}\|+\|h^{\prime\prime}\|+\|f^{\prime\prime}\|\left|\int_{\mathbb{{R}}}u\nu_{T_{n}}(du)\right|

\displaystyle\leq 2+\frac{1}{3}|C_{1}(T_{n})|

\displaystyle=2+\frac{1}{3}|\mathbb{E}(T_{n})|.

This proves the result.
∎

4.
Approximation of linear combinations of BG random variables

In this section, we present bounds for the distributional approximations of linear combination of independent BG r.v.s to some probability distributions. We use Stein’s method for probability approximations to derive our bounds.

4.1.
Approximation for sums

Our first result yields an error bound for the distributional approximation of two linear combinations of independent BG r.v.s. We refer the reader to
[
7
]
,
[
23
]
and
[
27
]
for a number of similar bounds for the total variation distance, between the distributions of two sums of non-negative integer-valued r.v.s.

Theorem 4.1
.

For
w_{j}^{(1)},w_{j}^{(2)},\pi_{j}^{(1)},\pi_{j}^{(2)}>0
and
j\in\mathbb{N}\{0\}
, let
T_{n}^{(1)}=T_{n}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}(w_{j}^{(1)}X_{j}-w_{j}^{(2)}Y_{j})
and
T_{n}^{(2)}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}(\pi_{j}^{(1)}X_{j}-\pi_{j}^{(2)}Y_{j})
be defined as in (
3.1
). Then

(4.1)

\displaystyle d_{W}(T_{n}^{(1)},T_{n}^{(2)})

\displaystyle\leq c_{1}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\frac{p_{j}}{\sqrt{2\alpha_{j}}}\frac{\big(w_{j}^{(1)}-\pi_{j}^{(1)}\big)}{\big(w_{j}^{(1)}+\pi_{j}^{(1)}\big)^{1/2}}+c_{2}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\frac{q_{j}}{\sqrt{2\beta_{j}}}\frac{\big(w_{j}^{(2)}-\pi_{j}^{(2)}\big)}{\big(w_{j}^{(2)}+\pi_{j}\big)^{1/2}},

where
c_{1}
and
c_{2}
are two positive constants.

Proof.

Let
(f,h)
be the solution pair of (
3.34
). Then replacing
x
by
T_{n}^{(1)}
in the Stein equation and taking expectation, we get

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[h(T_{n}^{(1)})]-\mathbb{E}[h(T_{n}^{(2)})]=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left(-T_{n}^{(1)}f(T_{n}^{(1)})+\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}f(T_{n}^{(1)}+u)u\nu_{T_{n}^{(2)}}(du)\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\bigg[\left(-T_{n}^{(1)}f(T_{n}^{(1)})+\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}f(T_{n}^{(1)}+u)u\nu_{T_{n}^{(2)}}(du)\right)

(4.2)

\displaystyle\quad\quad-\left(-T_{n}^{(1)}f(T_{n}^{(1)})+\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}f(T_{n}^{(1)}+u)u\nu_{T_{n}^{(1)}}(du)\right)\bigg],

since
\mathbb{E}\left(-T_{n}^{(1)}f(T_{n}^{(1)})+\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}f(T_{n}^{(1)}+u)u\nu_{T_{n}^{(1)}}(du)\right)=0,~f\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{{R}}).
So, for any
h\in\mathcal{M}_{W}
, (
4.1
) can be written as

\displaystyle d_{W}(T_{n}^{(1)},T_{n}^{(2)})

\displaystyle\leq\bigg|\mathbb{E}\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}f(T_{n}^{(1)}+u)u\nu_{T_{n}^{(2)}}(du)-\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}f(T_{n}^{(1)}+u)u\nu_{T_{n}^{(1)}}(du)\bigg|

\displaystyle=\bigg|\mathbb{E}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\bigg(p_{j}\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}f(T_{n}^{(1)}+u)e^{-\alpha_{j}/\pi_{j}^{(1)}u}du

\displaystyle\quad-q_{j}\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}f(T_{n}^{(1)}-u)e^{-\beta_{j}/\pi_{j}^{(2)}u}du\bigg)

\displaystyle\quad\quad-\sum_{j=1}^{n}\bigg(p_{j}\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}f(T_{n}^{(1)}+u)e^{-\alpha_{j}/w_{j}^{(1)}u}du

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad-q_{j}\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}f(T_{n}^{(1)}-u)e^{-\beta_{j}/w_{j}^{(2)}u}du\bigg)\bigg|

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad(\text{split the measures }\nu_{T_{n}^{(1)}}\text{ and }\nu_{T_{n}^{(2)}})

\displaystyle=\bigg|\mathbb{E}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\bigg(p_{j}\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}f(T_{n}^{(1)}+u)\left(e^{-\alpha_{j}/\pi_{j}^{(1)}u}-e^{-\alpha_{j}/w_{j}^{(1)}u}\right)du

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad-q_{j}\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}f(T_{n}^{(1)}-u)\left(e^{-\beta_{j}/\pi_{j}^{(2)}u}-e^{-\beta_{j}/w_{j}^{(2)}u}\right)du\bigg)\bigg|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j=1}^{n}p_{j}\bigg|\bigg(\int_{0}^{\infty}\bigg(e^{-\alpha_{j}/\pi_{j}^{(1)}u}-e^{-\alpha_{j}/w_{j}^{(1)}u}\bigg)^{2}du\bigg)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\times\bigg(\mathbb{E}\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}f^{2}(T_{n}^{(1)}+u)du\bigg)^{1/2}\bigg|

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{j=1}^{n}q_{j}\bigg|\bigg(\int_{0}^{\infty}\bigg(e^{-\beta_{j}/\pi_{j}^{(2)}u}-e^{-\beta_{j}/w_{j}^{(2)}u}\bigg)^{2}du\bigg)^{1/2}

(4.3)

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\quad\times\bigg(\mathbb{E}\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}f^{2}(T_{n}^{(1)}-u)du\bigg)^{1/2}\bigg|,

where the last inequality follows by the Cauchy-Schwartz inequality.

Next, observe that, since
f\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R})
, there exists two positive constants
c_{1}
and
c_{2}
such that
\bigg(\mathbb{E}\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}f^{2}(T_{n}^{(1)}-u)du\bigg)^{1/2}<c_{1}
and
\bigg(\mathbb{E}\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}f^{2}(T_{n}^{(1)}-u)du\bigg)^{1/2}<c_{2}
, respectively. So, (
4.1
) can be written as

\displaystyle d_{W}(T_{n}^{(1)},T_{n}^{(2)})

\displaystyle\leq c_{1}\sum_{j=1}^{n}p_{j}\bigg|\bigg(\int_{0}^{\infty}\bigg(e^{-\alpha_{j}/\pi_{j}^{(1)}u}-e^{-\alpha_{j}/w_{j}^{(1)}u}\bigg)^{2}du\bigg)^{\frac{1}{2}}\bigg|

\displaystyle\quad+c_{2}\sum_{j=1}^{n}q_{j}\bigg|\bigg(\int_{0}^{\infty}\bigg(e^{-\beta_{j}/\pi_{j}^{(2)}u}-e^{-\beta_{j}/w_{j}^{(2)}u}\bigg)^{2}du\bigg)^{\frac{1}{2}}\bigg|

\displaystyle=c_{1}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\frac{p_{j}}{\sqrt{\alpha_{j}}}\bigg|\frac{w_{j}^{(1)}+\pi_{j}^{(1)}}{2}-2\frac{w_{j}^{(1)}\pi_{j}^{(1)}}{w_{j}^{(1)}+\pi_{j}^{(1)}}\bigg|^{1/2}

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad+c_{2}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\frac{q_{j}}{\sqrt{\beta_{j}}}\bigg|\frac{w_{j}^{(2)}+\pi_{j}^{(2)}}{2}-2\frac{w_{j}^{(2)}\pi_{j}^{(2)}}{w_{j}^{(2)}+\pi_{j}^{(2)}}\bigg|^{1/2}

4.2.
Limit of compound Poisson distributions

Next, we obtain, for the Kolmogorov distance

, the error bounds for a sequence of compound Poisson distributions that converges to the distribution of linear combination of BG r.v.s. Indeed, a more general result is shown to be true in Theorem 1.2.18 of

(4.4)

.

be defined as in (

). Then

where
c>0
is some positive constant.

Proof.

Let
b=\int_{-1}^{1}u\nu_{T_{n}}(du)
, where
\nu_{T_{n}}
is defined in (
3.23
). Then by Equation (2.6) of
[
2
]
, the distribution of
T_{n}
is an infinitely divisible distribution with the triplet
b,0
and
\nu_{T_{n}}
. Note that the distribution of

is absolutely continuous with respect to the Lévy measure with a bounded density and

(see (

that, if

), then

(4.6)

\displaystyle d_{K}(Z_{m},T_{n})

\displaystyle\leq c\bigg(|\mathbb{E}[T_{n}]|+\int_{\mathbb{R}}u^{2}\nu_{T_{n}}(du)\bigg)^{\frac{2}{p+4}}\bigg(\frac{1}{m}\bigg)^{\frac{1}{p+4}},

where
|\phi_{T_{n}}(z)|\displaystyle\int_{0}^{|z|}\frac{ds}{|\phi_{T_{n}}(s)|}\leq e^{c_{0}}|z|^{p},

p\geq 1

Now observe that

Hence by (

\displaystyle d_{K}(Z_{m},T_{n})

\displaystyle=c\bigg(\sum_{j=1}^{2}|C_{j}(T_{n})|\bigg)^{\frac{2}{5}}\bigg(\frac{1}{m}\bigg)^{\frac{1}{5}},

\text{ since }\int_{\mathbb{R}}u^{2}\nu_{T_{n}}(du)=C_{2}(T_{n}).
This proves the result.
∎

Remark 4.5
.

Note that if

, and so the distribution of

is the limit of a compound Poisson distributions.

\displaystyle\phi_{sv}(z)

\displaystyle=\bigg(1+\frac{z^{2}\sigma^{2}}{2p}\bigg)^{-p},~z\in\mathbb{R}

(4.19)

\displaystyle=\exp\bigg(\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}(e^{izu}-1)\nu_{sv}(du)\bigg),~z\in\mathbb{{R}},

where the Lévy measure
\nu_{sv}
is

\displaystyle\nu_{sv}(du)=\bigg(\frac{p}{u}e^{-\frac{\sqrt{2p}}{\sigma}u}\textbf{1}_{(0,\infty)}(u)+\frac{p}{|u|}e^{-\frac{\sqrt{2p}}{\sigma}|u|}\textbf{1}_{(-\infty,0)}(u)\bigg).

Note from (
4.18

\displaystyle\lim_{p\to\infty}\phi_{sv}(z)=e^{-\frac{\sigma^{2}z^{2}}{2}}.

That is,
Z_{p}\overset{L}{\to}Z_{\sigma}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2}),
as
p\to\infty.
Also, it follows
[

that, if
Z_{p}\overset{L}{\to}Z_{\sigma},
as
p\to\infty
,

(4.20)

\displaystyle d_{3}(T_{n},Z_{\sigma})=\lim_{p\to\infty}d_{3}(T_{n},Z_{p}).

Applying Theorem
4.6
to
Z_{bg}=Z_{p}
, and taking the limit as
p\to\infty
, we get from (

)

\displaystyle d_{3}(T_{n},Z_{\sigma})

\displaystyle\leq\lim_{p\to\infty}\bigg(\left(2+\frac{1}{3}|\mathbb{E}(T_{n})|\right)\kappa_{n}\bigg|\sum_{j=1}^{n}\frac{w_{j}^{(1)}w_{j}^{(2)}}{\alpha_{j}\beta_{j}}-\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2p}\bigg|

\displaystyle\quad\quad+\left(2+\frac{1}{2}|\mathbb{E}(T_{n})|\right)\kappa_{n}\bigg|\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left(\frac{w_{j}^{(1)}}{\alpha_{j}}-\frac{w_{j}^{(2)}}{\beta_{j}}\right)\bigg|

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad+\frac{1}{2}\kappa_{n}\left|\sum_{j=1}^{n}\frac{w_{j}^{(1)}w_{j}^{(2)}(p_{j}+q_{j})}{\alpha_{j}\beta_{j}}-\sigma^{2}\right|+\kappa_{n}\left|\mathbb{E}T_{n}\right|\bigg)

\displaystyle=\left(2+\frac{1}{3}|\mathbb{E}(T_{n})|\right)\kappa_{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\frac{w_{j}^{(1)}w_{j}^{(2)}}{\alpha_{j}\beta_{j}}

\displaystyle\quad\quad+\left(2+\frac{1}{2}|\mathbb{E}(T_{n})|\right)\kappa_{n}\bigg|\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left(\frac{w_{j}^{(1)}}{\alpha_{j}}-\frac{w_{j}^{(2)}}{\beta_{j}}\right)\bigg|

(4.21)

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad+\frac{1}{2}\kappa_{n}\left|\sum_{j=1}^{n}\frac{w_{j}^{(1)}w_{j}^{(2)}(p_{j}+q_{j})}{\alpha_{j}\beta_{j}}-\sigma^{2}\right|+\kappa_{n}\left|\mathbb{E}T_{n}\right|,

which gives the error in the closeness between the distributions of
T_{n}
and
Z_{\sigma}
.

5.
Mixed Bilateral gamma process

In this section, we first introduce the mixed bilateral gamma process, which is a BG process with random parameters. We then discuss some of its important properties.

Let
\{T_{n}(t)\}_{t\geq 0}
be a Lévy process with cf

(5.1)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(e^{izT_{n}(t)})=e^{t\tau(z)},~z\in\mathbb{{R}},

where
\tau(z)
is the characteristic exponent, given by
\tau(z)=\int_{\mathbb{{R}}}(e^{izu}-1)\nu_{T_{n}}(du)
and
\nu_{T_{n}}
is the Lévy measure given in (
3.23
). Note the case
n=1
was first considered by K
\ddot{\text{u}}
chler and Tappe
[
17
]
. Recently, by incorporating a diffusion component into the BG process, Kirkby
et al.

[
16
]
introduced BG motion and applied it in stock models. From (
3.23
), we see that
\nu_{T_{n}}(\mathbb{R})=\infty
and
\int_{\mathbb{R}}|u|\nu_{T_{n}}(du)<\infty
. Since the Gaussian component is zero,
T_{n}(t)
is classified as type B according to Definition 11.9 of Sato
[
24
]
. Moreover,
T_{n}(t)
is a finite-variation process (see
[
24
, Theorem 21.9]
) with countable jumping times, almost surely (see
[
24
, Theorem 21.3]
). We note that all the increments of
T_{n}(t)
have the BG distribution, but with random parameters. More precisely,

(5.2)

\displaystyle T_{n}(t)-T_{n}(s)\sim BG\left(\eta,(L_{n}+p)(t-s),\xi,(M_{n}+q)(t-s)\right),~0\leq s\leq t,

where
\eta=\alpha/1-\alpha
,
\xi=\beta/1-\beta
, and
\alpha,\beta,L_{n},M_{n},p
, and
q
are as defined in (
3.2
) and (
3.3
), respectively. Also,

(5.3)

\displaystyle T_{n}(t)\sim BG\left(\eta,(L_{n}+p)t,\xi,(M_{n}+q)t\right),~t\geq 0.

Since
L_{n}
and
M_{n}
are random,
T_{n}(t)
is indeed a mixed BG process.

Next, we present an application of the above introduced process to stock models.

5.1.
Arbitrage-free Stock models

In the continuous time finance, one of the crucial task is to find a realistic and analytically tractable models for price evolutions of risky financial assets (see,
[
16
]
and
[
17
]
,
[
19
]
). We consider the exponential Lévy models (see, K
\ddot{\text{u}}
chler and Tappe
[
19
,
20
]
)

(5.4)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}S_{t}&=S_{0}e^{T_{n}(t)}\\
B_{t}&=e^{rt},\end{cases}

which consists two financial assets
(S,B)
. Here
S
is the dividend paying stock with deterministic initial value
S_{0}
with dividend rate
v\geq 0
. Also,
B
is the bank account with fixed interest rate
r\geq 0
. Note that the model (
5.4
) is a generalization of BG stock models considered in
[
19
]
. Recently, by incorporating a diffusion component into the BG process, Kirkby
et al.

[
16
]
developed a generalized exponential Lévy model and derived an arbitrage-free option pricing formula. In practice, it is often necessary to address the appropriate pricing of European options
\Phi(S_{T})
, where
T>0
represents the maturity time and
\Phi:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
denotes the payoff function. Also, the option price is given by
\pi:=e^{-rT}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}[\Phi(S_{T})],
where
\mathbb{Q}\sim\mathbb{P}
is a local martingale measure. Here,
\mathbb{Q}
is also a probability measure which is equivalent to the objective probability measure
\mathbb{P}
, such that the discounted stock price process

(5.5)

\displaystyle\tilde{S}_{t}:=e^{-(r-v)t}S_{t}=S_{0}e^{T_{n}(t)-(r-v)t},\quad t\geq 0

is a local
\mathbb{Q}
-martingale, see
[
19
]
.

The following lemma follows from Lemma 3.1 of
[
19
]
, which establishes necessary and sufficient conditions for the existence of a probability measure
\mathbb{Q}\sim\mathbb{P}
.

Lemma 5.1
.

Let
r\geq v\geq 0
. Let
\{T_{n}(t)\}_{t\geq 0}
be a Lévy process with cf (
5.1
). Then,
\mathbb{P}
is a martingale measure for
\tilde{S}_{t}
if and only if

(5.6)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{L_{n}}\left(\left(\frac{\eta}{\eta-1}\right)^{L_{n}}\right)\mathbb{E}_{M_{n}}\left(\left(\frac{\xi}{\xi-1}\right)^{M_{n}}\right)=(1-1/\eta)^{p}(1-1/\xi)^{q}e^{r-v},

where
\eta=\alpha/1-\alpha
,
\xi=\beta/1-\beta
, and
\alpha,\beta,L_{n},M_{n},p
, and
q
are as defined in (
3.2
) and (
3.3
), respectively.

Proof.

From (
3.17
), we have
\mathbb{E}(e^{T_{n}(1)})=\mathbb{E}_{L_{n}}\left(\left(\frac{\eta}{\eta-1}\right)^{p+L_{n}}\right)\mathbb{E}_{M_{n}}\left(\left(\frac{\xi}{\xi-1}\right)^{q+M_{n}}\right)<\infty
. By Lemma 2.6 of
[
19
]
, the discounted process
\tilde{S_{t}}
in (
5.5
) is a local martingale if and only if
\mathbb{E}[e^{T_{n}(1)-(r-v)}]=1,
which is only the case if and only if (
5.6
) holds.
∎

5.1.1.
Pricing formula

The existence of a martingale measure
\mathbb{Q}\sim\mathbb{P}
ensures that the stock market is free of arbitrage, and the price of an European option
\Phi(S_{T})
, where
T(>0)
is the time of maturity and
\Phi:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is the pay-off function, is given by

(5.7)

\displaystyle\pi=e^{-rT}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}[\Phi(S_{T})].

Also, an arbitrage-free pricing formula for a European Call Option at time
t(\geq 0)
is, provided that
S_{t}=s
, given by (see
[
17
]
)

(5.8)

\pi=e^{-rT}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}\left[(S_{T}-K)^{+}\mid S_{t}=s\right],~T>t,

where
K
denotes the strike price. Setting
t^{\prime}=(T-t)
, we can express the expectation in (
5.8
) as

\displaystyle\pi

\displaystyle=e^{-rT}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}\left[(S_{t}e^{T_{n}(T)-T_{n}(t)}-K)^{+}\mid S_{t}=s\right]

(5.9)

\displaystyle=e^{-rT}\int_{\ln\left(\frac{K}{s}\right)}^{\infty}(se^{x}-K)h_{T_{n}}(x,t^{\prime})dx,

where
h_{T_{n}}(x,t^{\prime})
denotes the pdf of
T_{n}(t^{\prime})\sim BG\left(\eta,(L_{n}+p)t^{\prime},\xi,(M_{n}+q)t^{\prime}\right)
(see, Section
3.2
).

Note that (
5.1.1
) provides a pricing formula driven by a mixed BG process, which extends the pricing formula, for the case
n\geq 2
, for BG stock models considered in Proposition 8.3 of
[
17
]
.

As a special case, we get an arbitrage-free pricing formula driven by the Lévy process associated with the linear combination of gamma r.v.s as:

(5.10)

\displaystyle\pi

\displaystyle=c_{n}e^{-rT}\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\gamma_{j}\frac{\eta^{(p+j)t^{\prime}}}{\Gamma t^{\prime}(p+j)}\int_{\ln\left(\frac{K}{s}\right)}^{\infty}(se^{x}-K)x^{(p+j)t^{\prime}-1}e^{-\eta x}~dx,

where
\eta=\alpha/1-\alpha
, and
c_{n},\gamma_{j},p,\alpha
are defined in (
3.2
) and (
3.3
). Let
\Gamma(w,z):=\int_{z}^{\infty}x^{w-1}e^{-x}dx,

~z\in\mathbb{R}
be the incomplete gamma function. Then, (
5.10
) can be written as

\displaystyle\pi

\displaystyle=c_{n}e^{-rT}\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{\gamma_{j}}{\Gamma(p+j)t^{\prime}}\bigg(s\left(\frac{\eta}{\eta-1}\right)^{(p+j)t^{\prime}}\Gamma\left((p+j)t^{\prime},(\eta-1)ln\left(\frac{K}{s}\right)\right)

\displaystyle\quad\quad-K\Gamma\left((p+j)t^{\prime},\eta ln\left(\frac{K}{s}\right)\right)\bigg).

When
s=K
, we get an exact pricing formula as

\displaystyle\pi

\displaystyle=c_{n}Ke^{-rT}\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\gamma_{j}\frac{\eta^{(p+j)t^{\prime}}}{\Gamma t^{\prime}(p+j)}\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{x}x^{(p+j)t^{\prime}-1}e^{-\eta x}~dx-Ke^{-rT}

\displaystyle=Ke^{-rT}\left(c_{n}\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\gamma_{j}\left(\frac{\eta}{\eta-1}\right)^{(p+j)t^{\prime}}-1\right)

\displaystyle=Ke^{-rT}\left(\mathbb{E}_{L_{n}}\left(\frac{\eta}{\eta-1}\right)^{(L_{n}+p)t^{\prime}}-1\right),

where

is the expectation with respect to the rv
L_{n}
