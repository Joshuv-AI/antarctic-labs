Change in Adherence to Oral Antidiabetic Medications Before and After Prostate Cancer Diagnosis: A Group-Based Trajectory Modeling Approach
Authors: Ahmed S Kenawy, James O Baffoe, Chanhyun Park
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCancer diagnoses impact adherence to antidiabetic medications, but limited research has focused on patients with prostate cancer and type 2 diabetes (T2DM). We investigated adherence trajectories to oral antidiabetic medications one year before and after a prostate cancer diagnosis and identified risk factors.MethodsThis retrospective cohort study used the 2011–2021 MarketScan Commercial and Medicare Supplemental databases. We included newly diagnosed prostate cancer patients with T2DM with continuous insurance enrollment. We applied group-based trajectory modeling with a beta distribution to evaluate adherence patterns before and after prostate cancer diagnosis. Model covariates included age, total number of medications, number of antidiabetic medications, the Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI), cost, insurance type, and complicated diabetes from the year before diagnosis. Metastasis and cancer treatments were included in the model after diagnosis.ResultsThe study included 7864 patients (mean age = 74.5 ± 7.1). Three adherence trajectories were identified before diagnosis: consistently high adherence, steady decliners, and consistently low adherence. After diagnosis, a fourth trajectory revealing a moderate decline emerged. Over half (61.2%) changed adherence patterns after diagnosis. Among those with consistently high adherence before diagnosis, 57.8% transitioned to a lower adherence trajectory. In contrast, 58.5% of steady decliners and 57.6% of consistently low adherents transitioned to a higher adherence trajectory after diagnosis. Predictors of high adherence included older age, fewer antidiabetic medications, lower CCI, and complicated diabetes before diagnosis. After diagnosis, fewer antidiabetic medications and complicated diabetes remained predictive of high adherence.ConclusionPatterns of adherence to oral antidiabetic medications undergo substantial changes after a prostate cancer diagnosis. Targeted interventions are needed to support and facilitate effective diabetes management in this population.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s40261-026-01569-5.

Key Points

Adherence to oral antidiabetic medications changes notably after a prostate cancer diagnosis, with most patients showing changes in their adherence level.Nearly half of those who were highly adherent before diagnosis experienced a decline, while many with poor or declining adherence improved afterward.Factors including age, number of antidiabetic medications, and the Charlson Comorbidity Index may influence adherence before and after prostate cancer diagnosis.

Introduction

Prostate cancer poses a substantial burden on the US healthcare system, with an estimated 314,000 new cases and an incidence rate exceeding 280 per 100,000 in 2025 [1]. Prostate cancer is the second leading cause of cancer-related death among US men, after lung cancer. Four men die from prostate cancer every hour, with an estimated 35,000 deaths in 2025 [1]. While evidence on the association between type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) and prostate cancer risk remains inconclusive, T2DM remains highly prevalent among patients with prostate cancer [2]. Notably, previous studies indicate that approximately 10–40% of patients with prostate cancer have preexisting T2DM at the time of cancer diagnosis [3, 4]. Patients with prostate cancer and T2DM encounter significant challenges due to long-term management burden, complications, and healthcare costs associated with both conditions. In particular, a cancer diagnosis may affect adherence to antidiabetic medications and complicate diabetes control [5].

Nonadherence to antidiabetic medications is associated with a higher risk of diabetes-related complications and poor treatment outcomes [6]. Nevertheless, major life events, such as a cancer diagnosis, can alter patients’ daily routines and adherence patterns [7]. Existing evidence has shown a decrease in adherence to antidiabetic medications after cancer diagnosis. A retrospective cohort study using Medicare claims data found significant declines in adherence to chronic medications, including T2DM, following diagnosis, particularly among patients with complex treatment regimens [8]. In patients with cancer, uncontrolled diabetes, often associated with nonadherence, is linked to severe consequences, including a higher risk of cardiovascular diseases, poor cancer prognosis, and higher healthcare costs [9, 10]. A retrospective study found that patients with prostate cancer and uncontrolled diabetes had higher cancer-specific mortality than those without diabetes. Interestingly, the study did not find a significant difference in cancer-specific mortality between patients without diabetes and those receiving diabetes medications [11]. These results highlight the importance of maintaining optimal adherence to antidiabetic medications after cancer diagnosis to improve survival outcomes and reduce the risk of complications.

While commonly used medication adherence measures, such as the proportion of days covered (PDC), provide an informative summary of medication adherence, they cannot detect the patterns or predictors of medication use over time [12]. Group-based trajectory modeling (GBTM) offers a more detailed approach by identifying groups of individuals with similar longitudinal adherence patterns. Group-based trajectory modeling also allows for the inclusion of predictors of group membership, identifying the factors that affect adherence. Additionally, GBTM can account for non-linear adherence patterns, making it particularly useful in studying the potentially complex adherence changes associated with cancer diagnosis over time [13].

Previous studies have demonstrated the impact of cancer diagnoses on adherence to antidiabetic medications among patients with breast and hematologic cancers; however, limited research has explored this effect in patients with prostate cancer and T2DM [14–17]. Consequently, this study aims to 1) assess adherence trajectories to oral antidiabetic medications before and after prostate cancer diagnosis and 2) evaluate the risk factors associated with the probability of group membership in the trajectory models. We hypothesize that adherence patterns to oral antidiabetic medications vary around the time of prostate cancer diagnosis.

Methods

Data Source

We conducted this study using the Merative MarketScan commercial and Medicare supplemental databases. The commercial database includes information on costs, healthcare utilization, and outcomes for patients with employer-sponsored health insurance. The Medicare supplemental database captures data on services not fully covered by Medicare, with a focus on Medicare-eligible patients who have employer-sponsored supplemental insurance. Both datasets include detailed information on demographics, inpatient, outpatient, drug dispensing, costs, and enrollment, covering nearly 250 million de-identified employer-sponsored employees, retirees, and dependents [18]. The Institutional Review Board (IRB) at The University of Texas at Austin determined this study to be exempt because it does not involve identifiable information or human subjects (IRB ID: STUDY00007413).

Study Design

This retrospective cohort study used the 2011–2021 MarketScan Commercial and Medicare supplemental databases. For each patient, we calculated monthly PDC values for two 12-month periods: one before and one after cancer diagnosis. We then applied GBTM to characterize adherence trajectories and identify predictors of trajectory-group membership within each period. Predictors of trajectory-group membership were identified using covariates measured during designated assessment periods.

Study Population

We included newly diagnosed prostate cancer patients with T2DM who had continuous enrollment in the insurance plan(s) captured in the MarketScan and Medicare supplemental databases. Patients were included if they: (1) had a new diagnosis code for prostate cancer (International Classification of Diseases [ICD]-9: 185, ICD-10: C61); a new prostate cancer diagnosis was defined as a prostate cancer diagnosis code on the index date, with no prior diagnosis code within 18 months preceding the index date. To enhance specificity, a prostate cancer diagnosis required either one inpatient primary diagnosis or two outpatient diagnoses, each at least 30 days apart, (2) were men aged ≥ 18 years at the baseline, (3) had complicated or uncomplicated T2DM before baseline, confirmed by a diagnosis code (ICD-9: 250×0 or 250×2, ICD-10: E11.x) and at least one oral antidiabetic prescription claim during the pre-cancer assessment period, (4) had continuous enrollment for 18 months before and 18 months after the index date, (5) were receiving oral antidiabetic medications as monotherapy or combination, confirmed by at least one oral antidiabetic prescription claim during the pre-cancer assessment period. We included the following antidiabetic classes: metformin, sulfonylureas, thiazolidinediones, meglitinides, dipeptidyl peptidase-4 (DPP-4) inhibitors, alpha-glucosidase inhibitors, and sodium–glucose cotransporter-2 (SGLT2) inhibitors.

Patients were excluded if they: (1) had type-1 diabetes, (2) were receiving injectable antidiabetic medications anytime during the entire study period, or (3) had any cancer before the index date. We used the International Classification of Diseases, 9th and 10th revisions (ICD-9-CM and ICD-10-CM), to identify the patients in the database [19].

Outcome

The primary outcome was the change in adherence trajectories of oral antidiabetic medications for one year before and one year after the prostate cancer diagnosis, using the PDC. The proportion of days covered is a proportion between 0 (0%) and 1 (100%), reflecting the days that patients can access their medications during a specific period [20]. We used PDC as the recommended method for reporting chronic medication adherence using claims data [21]. A monthly PDC was calculated for patients using the medication dispensing date and the number of days supplied. Inpatient days were excluded from the PDC calculation to accurately capture medication adherence associated with outpatient dispensing. Surplus days were carried over to subsequent months, with daily coverage capped at 1.0. For each patient, a total of 24 PDCs were calculated, representing 12 months before and 12 months after the prostate cancer diagnosis.

Statistical Analysis

Main Analysis

We used separate GBTMs with a beta distribution to assess adherence trajectories of oral antidiabetic medications before and after a prostate cancer diagnosis. Group-based trajectory modeling is an established statistical approach that uses longitudinal data to identify clusters or groups of patients with similar patterns or trajectories [13]. Because the PDCs in our study had values between 0 and 1 and were not normally distributed, we applied beta-family GBTMs. The beta distribution offers a valid alternative to the normal distribution for modeling continuous or proportion-type outcomes, especially when the data are skewed and bounded [22]. To select the best-fitting model for our sample, we started with a one-group model using a quadratic polynomial function. Then, we increased the number of trajectories by adding one trajectory at a time, estimating a new model at each step [23]. The best model was selected based on criteria that included the lowest Bayesian information criterion (BIC) and a minimum of 5% membership in each group for meaningful classification, as reported by Nagin and Odgers [24]. We also examined the trajectories for each model for consistency with the medication adherence patterns reported in previous research [25]. To assess the performance of the chosen model, we evaluated the average posterior probability of assignment (AvePP) and the odds of correct classification (OCC). The AvePP is the average posterior probability of assignment across all patients within each trajectory group, while the OCC represents the ratio of the odds of correct classification to the odds of random classification for each group [26]. Generally, an AvePP value of 0.7 or higher and an OCC value of at least 5 indicate good model performance [26]. A multinomial GBTM (adjusted model) was used to identify predictors of membership in each trajectory.

Model Covariates

For the 1 year before cancer diagnosis, the model accounted for the covariates collected during the pre-cancer assessment period, including age (at the start of pre-cancer period), total number of medications (total number of all medications used during the assessment period), number of antidiabetic medications (total number of all antidiabetic medications used during the assessment period), the Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI), monthly out-of-pocket (copayments, coinsurance, and deductibles), Medicare coverage, and complicated diabetes (diabetes with retinopathy, neuropathy, nephropathy, or peripheral vascular disease) [27]. All categorical variables were specified as binary indicators where applicable. For the one year after a cancer diagnosis, the model accounted for the covariates collected during the post-cancer assessment period. The same covariates included in the pre-cancer analysis were retained and measured during the post-cancer assessment period. In addition, we incorporated cancer-specific variables after cancer diagnosis, including the use of androgen deprivation therapy (ADT) (gonadotropin-releasing hormone [GnRH] agonists, GnRH antagonists, androgen receptor antagonists, androgen biosynthesis inhibitors, or estrogens), other cancer treatments (prostatectomy, radiotherapy, or chemotherapy), and metastasis (ICD-9: 196.x-199.x, ICD-10: C77.x-C80.x) [28]. These variables were also modeled as binary indicators. The specific codes used to calculate the CCI are summarized in Online Resource 13.

Additional Analysis

In addition to the multinomial GBTM, we estimated an unadjusted model to identify the natural patterns of adherence to oral antidiabetic medications over time without accounting for patient characteristics. Methodologically, this approach enables the assessment of model robustness and the comparison of trajectories before and after the inclusion of covariates. Clinically, the unadjusted model may reveal real-world behavioral patterns as they occur, independent of patient variables. This understanding can inform the design of broadly applicable interventions to improve adherence.

We conducted our statistical analysis using SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc. Cary, NC) by applying the open-source PROC TRAJ procedure [29]. McNemar's test and the paired T-test (or the Wilcoxon signed-rank test for non-normally distributed data) were used to compare patient characteristics before and after cancer diagnosis for categorical and continuous variables, respectively. Analysis of variance (for normally distributed variables) and the Kruskal–Wallis test (for non-normally distributed variables) were used to compare continuous variables across trajectories, while the chi-square test (or Fisher’s exact test, when the cell count is less than 5) was used for categorical variables. For all analyses, a priori level of significance was < 0.05. Formal closed-form power or sample size calculations for GBTM are not routinely available; based on prior simulation work, targeting at least 500 patients should be sufficient to obtain a reliable trajectory recovery [30].

Sensitivity Analysis

Patients were classified as adherent or nonadherent based on their PDC. A patient is considered adherent if the monthly PDC value is 80% or higher [31]. A logit-based multinomial GBTM was used to model the binary outcome (adherent vs nonadherent) before and after cancer diagnosis.

Results

Patient Characteristics

Of 796,368 patients with prostate cancer, 7864 were included in our study, with a mean age of 74.50 years; of these, 96% were Medicare enrollees (Fig. 2 and Table 1). A paired t-test showed significant differences in the CCI and the number of total medications between the year before and the year after prostate cancer diagnosis. The mean CCI significantly increased from (3.80, standard deviation [SD] = 1.80) before cancer diagnosis to (7.30, SD = 3.70) after diagnosis (t = 87.26; df = 7863; p < 0.001), likely reflecting the contribution of cancer diagnosis and the increase of comorbidity burden after diagnosis. The mean total number of medications also significantly increased from 10.90 (SD = 4.50) before cancer diagnosis to 11.60 (SD = 4.80) after diagnosis (t = 15.27; df = 7,863; p < 0.001). A Wilcoxon signed-rank test showed that the PDC after (mean = 64.29, median = 74, SD = 30.94, interquartile range [IQR] = 41.11–95.34) was significantly lower than the PDC before diagnosis (mean = 68.80, median = 73.9, SD = 30.70, IQR = 49.04–97.53) (Z = − 9.7; p < 0.001). Further, the prevalence of comorbidities significantly increased in the year after compared to the year before prostate cancer diagnosis. For instance, comorbidities such as congestive heart failure (from 12.20 to 28%), peripheral vascular disease (from 16.30 to 35.90%), cerebrovascular disease (from 15.20 to 31.30%), and chronic kidney disease (from 16.90 to 35.50%) almost doubled after prostate cancer diagnosis. Adherence Trajectories

Before diagnosis, three adherence trajectories were identified: consistently high adherence, steady decliners, and consistently low adherence. Half of patients (50.42%) experienced a steady decline in adherence during the year before diagnosis, while 41.09% maintained consistently high adherence, and 8.49% had a consistently low adherence to their oral antidiabetic medications (Fig. 3 and Online Resource 1). After diagnosis, a fourth trajectory emerged, reflecting a moderate decline in adherence. Approximately 30.71% of patients consistently demonstrated high adherence, 31.71% showed moderate adherence decline, 29.76% were steady decliners, and 7.82% had consistently low adherence to their oral antidiabetic medications during the year following cancer diagnosis (Fig. 4 and Online Resource 2). Overall, 61.20% of patients changed adherence patterns after diagnosis. Among those with consistently high adherence before diagnosis, 57.80% transitioned to a lower adherence trajectory. In contrast, 58.50% of steady decliners and 57.60% of consistently low adherents showed improved adherence to their oral antidiabetic medications after diagnosis (Table 2). Predictors of Group Membership

The multinomial GBTM revealed the factors associated with the probability of adherence group membership before and after a prostate cancer diagnosis. Compared to the consistently low adherent trajectory, membership in the consistently high adherence group was significantly associated with older age (estimate = 0.03, p < 0.001), fewer oral antidiabetic medications (estimate = − 0.37, p < 0.001), complicated diabetes (estimate = 0.87, p < 0.001), and a lower CCI score (estimate = − 0.18, p < 0.001), before prostate cancer diagnosis. After a prostate cancer diagnosis, fewer oral antidiabetic medications (estimate = − 0.49, p < 0.001), fewer total medications (estimate = − 0.03, p = 0.004), complicated diabetes (estimate = 0.41, p = 0.008), and receiving other cancer treatments (estimate = 0.37, p = 0.03) were significantly associated with higher adherence (Table 3). Unadjusted Model

The unadjusted model identified four adherence trajectories before and after cancer diagnosis.

The identified trajectories were similar in pattern to those observed in the adjusted model after diagnosis (Online Resources 6 and 7). Similar to the adjusted model, the unadjusted model revealed that adherence to oral antidiabetic medications considerably changes after a prostate cancer diagnosis. Approximately 60.07% of patients experienced a change in adherence trajectory following their cancer diagnosis. Among those with consistently high adherence before diagnosis, 56.58% transitioned to a lower adherence trajectory. In contrast, 53.53% of the consistently low adherence trajectory group improved their adherence after diagnosis (Online Resource 8).

Sensitivity Analysis

Using the logit-based GBTM, three and four adherence trajectories were identified before and after prostate cancer diagnosis, respectively. Before diagnosis, 44.74% of patients maintained consistently high adherence, while 32.27% experienced a steady decline, and 22.99% showed a rapid decline in their adherence. After diagnosis, approximately 33.77% of patients consistently demonstrated high adherence, 9.35% showed moderate adherence, 32.03% were steady decliners, and 24.85% had rapid decline adherence during the year following diagnosis (Online Resources 9 and 10). Overall, 58.21% of patients experienced a change in adherence trajectory following their cancer diagnosis (Online Resource 11). Membership in the consistently high adherence group was significantly associated with older age (estimate = 0.02, p < 0.001), fewer oral antidiabetic medications (estimate = − 0.37, p < 0.001), lower CCI score (estimate = − 0.09, p < 0.001), and Medicare coverage (estimate = 0.45, p = 0.006) before prostate cancer diagnosis. After prostate cancer diagnosis, membership in the consistently high adherence group was significantly associated with fewer oral antidiabetic medications (estimate = − 0.57, p < 0.001), a lower CCI score (estimate = − 0.04, p = 0.03), fewer total number of medications (estimate = − 0.03, p < 0.001), Medicare coverage (estimate = 0.39, p = 0.024), and complicated diabetes (estimate = 0.28, p = 0.009), compared to the rapid decliner trajectory (Online Resource 12).

Discussion

This study used GBTM to examine adherence patterns to oral antidiabetic medications one year before and one year after the diagnosis of prostate cancer. We also evaluated risk factors associated with trajectory-group membership. Our main analysis revealed three distinct adherence trajectories before the diagnosis and four trajectories following the diagnosis of prostate cancer. These trajectories demonstrated significant changes in oral antidiabetics adherence among patients with T2DM, with nearly half of the patients showing either an increase or a decrease in their adherence levels after diagnosis. Despite these dynamic patient-level changes, we observed a modest but statistically significant decline in PDC from 68% before diagnosis to 64% after diagnosis. However, the absolute difference was small, and both values remained below the conventional 80% adherence threshold, suggesting limited clinical significance. Previous research on adherence in patients with prostate cancer has yielded similar results. For instance, a retrospective cohort study showed no significant difference in overall mean PDC of statin therapy before and after cancer diagnosis among patients with prostate cancer (adjusted odds ratio [ORadj]: 1.01, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 0.97–1.05) [32]. Similarly, Zanders and colleagues observed only a minimal decrease in the MPR of antidiabetic medications after prostate cancer diagnosis (monthly decrease after diagnosis: 2.10%, 95% CI: 1.40–2.80) [33]. These findings highlight the limitations of using PDC and medication possession ratio (MPR) to assess medication adherence, as these methods may not effectively capture patient-level changes. In contrast, GBTM captured heterogeneous individual-level patterns, underscoring its value for evaluating adherence dynamics. Technically, GBTM uses a maximum likelihood approach to identify distinct groups of individuals who have similar longitudinal trajectories over time [34]. The use of GBTM in this study enabled the detection of both adherence improvement and decline after prostate cancer diagnosis, providing a level of detail that PDC could not reveal.

While the decline in adherence after cancer diagnosis was consistent with previous findings, the observed improvement in adherence in this study was less frequently reported [35]. Approximately 34.40% of the study participants transitioned into a better adherence trajectory after prostate cancer diagnosis. This improvement in adherence may reflect increased health awareness and more frequent medical contact. A retrospective cohort study involving patients with cancer showed a 3.6-fold increase in visits to general practitioners in the year after, compared with the year before cancer diagnosis [36]. These frequent healthcare interactions can enhance patient engagement and improve knowledge about medications, ultimately leading to higher medication adherence [37]. Additionally, the increased family support that often arises after a cancer diagnosis may further improve adherence. A review of qualitative studies has identified family support as a key factor of medication adherence, with stronger family support linked to better adherence outcomes [38]. In contrast, approximately 27% of the study participants transitioned into a lower adherence trajectory after prostate cancer diagnosis, with 89% of these patients having consistent high adherence before cancer diagnosis. This decline in adherence was expected because of previous research findings. Over 90% of studies published between 2013 and 2021 that examined medication adherence after a cancer diagnosis found a decrease in overall adherence following the diagnosis [7]. The mental and emotional stress of a cancer diagnosis often shifts patients' priorities, causing them to focus on treating cancer while ranking other chronic health conditions as a lower priority. This prioritization of cancer treatment over pre-existing chronic conditions may significantly impact medication adherence. Patients may view these conditions as less urgent, potentially reducing their adherence to medications [39]. Given the varying patterns of medication adherence, it is crucial to develop individualized care strategies to improve or maintain adherence following a cancer diagnosis. Interventions such as follow-up calls, enhanced coordination between oncology care teams and general practitioners, and integrated care models can help support treatment continuity. It is crucial to tailor these strategies to the cancer type and comorbid condition, while also considering both systemic factors and those related to the patient [40].

To provide a comprehensive evaluation of adherence trajectories, both beta and logit-based GBTM were applied to identify risk factors associated with group membership. The beta model examined the shape and variability of PDC over time, which allowed for a more detailed understanding of how medication-taking behavior changes continuously. This approach retains the full variability of PDC and avoids the loss of information that comes with dichotomization. However, its clinical interpretation can be less straightforward [22]. By contrast, the logit model estimated the probability of adherence at each time point based on a predefined threshold. While this approach offers clearer clinical interpretability, it is less sensitive to within-group PDC variability, treating patients with distinctly different adherence values (e.g., PDC values of 0.79 and 0.20) as equal. This methodological distinction explains the differences in the shapes and distribution patterns of the trajectories identified by the two models in our study [41]. Nevertheless, both models identified similar predictors associated with the consistently adherent group, including older age, fewer oral antidiabetic medications, and a lower CCI score.

The factors affecting medication adherence following a cancer diagnosis have been reported inconsistently in previous studies. For example, a retrospective cohort study using the MarketScan database reported that a CCI score of ≥ 2 did not significantly affect adherence to antidiabetic medications compared to patients with a CCI score of 0 (OR: 1.34, 95% CI: 0.94–1.91) [17]. In contrast, a separate population-based retrospective study indicated that patients with higher CCI scores (≥ 2) had lower odds of nonadherence to antidiabetic medications among patients with breast cancer, compared with a CCI score of 0 (OR: 0.49, 95% CI: 0.23–0.83) [42]. Similarly, the relationship between age and medication adherence after cancer diagnosis varies significantly. While Yang et al found that older patients were more likely to be nonadherent, Calip et al. reported that younger age was associated with nonadherence after cancer diagnosis [17, 42]. These discrepancies may be due to differences in the databases used, variations in study populations and their inclusion/exclusion criteria, as well as the heterogeneity in cancer stage.

Notably, the mean CCI score significantly increased in our cohort after the diagnosis of prostate cancer compared to the year before diagnosis. This increase was expected, as the diagnosis of cancer itself contributes additional points to the CCI, thereby elevating patients’ scores. Furthermore, our findings show a significant increase in comorbidity burden after cancer diagnosis, further elevating the mean CCI score (Table 1). Han and colleagues reported similar results, reporting a significant increase in mean CCI in the year after breast cancer diagnosis compared with the year before. Specifically, mean CCI increased from 2 to 3 among patients with localized disease, from 2 to 7 among those with regional disease, and from 3 to 8 among those with distant-stage breast cancer [43]. Ahmad and colleagues also reported comparable results by reporting an increase in the prevalence of comorbidities after cancer diagnosis across 28 cancer sites, including prostate cancer [44]. This rise in comorbidities may reflect disease progression or increased healthcare utilization, leading to greater detection of underlying conditions. This increase in comorbidities poses a challenge for patients, as it may hinder their ability to adhere to medications after cancer diagnosis due to the burdens associated with cancer and other comorbidities. Additionally, this increased comorbidity burden, along with the advanced age of the cohort, may also explain the relatively low proportion of patients receiving treatment (prostatectomy, radiotherapy, chemotherapy, or ADT) within the first 6 months after diagnosis, as older patients and those with higher CCI are more likely to choose active surveillance after prostate cancer diagnosis. The prospective cohort study of Ciccone et al has shown that older age (≥75 years) and higher comorbidity are associated with increased use of active surveillance rather than immediate treatment in prostate cancer [45]. Furthermore, limiting treatment assessments to the first 6 months after diagnosis may underestimate treatment rates, as some patients may start therapy later due to clinical decisions. Also, incomplete reporting of treatments in claims data and delays in submitting claims can further reduce the number of reported treatments.

There are several limitations to our study. First, the MarketScan database lacks some clinical (e.g., HbA1c and creatinine), lifestyle (e.g., diet), cancer-specific (e.g., PSA levels, Gleason scores, tumor differentiation), and quality-of-life (QoL) variables. These unmeasured variables are potential confounders that may lead to residual confounding, potentially biasing effect estimates and adherence measures. To address this limitation, all relevant variables (e.g., age, gender, number of antidiabetic medications, total number of medications, CCI, diabetes severity, insurance type, cost, metastasis, and cancer treatments) were included in the analysis to reflect the complexity of patients’ clinical situations. Second, the results primarily represented commercially insured patients and their relatives due to the use of the MarketScan database. The results may not be generalizable to other populations, including uninsured patients and Medicaid beneficiaries. However, the study's results would be highly robust and relevant to a broad segment of the US population with employer-sponsored insurance. Third, the study depended on prescription dispensing dates, which do not necessarily indicate medication intake or adherence. The use of dispensing dates may overestimate medication adherence or exposure to antidiabetics. In this study, PDC and GBTM approaches were used to assess medication adherence. Both methods rely on longitudinal data, thereby reducing dependence on single-time dispensing and making them widely accepted proxies for adherence [46]. Fourth, researchers using GBTM typically rely on their subjective judgment to select the optimal model or determine the final number of trajectories. To mitigate this, validated parameters were used to select the best model and determine the optimal number of trajectories, including BIC, AvePP, and OCC [13, 24]. Finally, the study required continuous enrollment as an inclusion criterion. This criterion could exclude patients with more severe illnesses (e.g., advanced cancer), potentially introducing selection bias. The characteristics of the included individuals were clearly described to assess representativeness, and sensitivity analyses were conducted to evaluate the potential impact of selection bias.

Despite these limitations, our study has notable strengths. To our knowledge, this is the first study to use the GBTM with a beta distribution to study medication adherence. This approach enabled the assessment of longitudinal trajectories and associated predictors while capturing variability in PDC. Additionally, the study utilized a large, real-world dataset from the MarketScan Commercial and Medicare Supplemental Databases, covering a broad sector of patients with employer-sponsored insurance. Future studies should explore interventions to improve adherence after cancer diagnosis, include longer follow-up periods, and incorporate additional variables such as QoL, adherence to other medication classes, and clinical factors. Further research across different cancer types may also determine whether findings in prostate cancer generalize to other populations with comorbid conditions.

Conclusion

Adherence to oral antidiabetic medications changes substantially after a prostate cancer diagnosis, reflecting complex interactions among cancer care, patient behavior, and diabetes management. Because adherence may either worsen or improve, individualized, timely follow-up, including comprehensive medication therapy management, proactive outreach during treatment transitions, and coordination between oncology and primary care, may help maintain optimal adherence and mitigate downstream complications.
