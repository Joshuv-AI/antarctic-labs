Final Technical Report for “Laboratory Investigations of Aerosol-Cloud Interactions in an Entraining,
 Turbulent Environment”, DE-SC0022128

 Will Cantrell and Raymond Shaw
 Michigan Technological University

 Fan Yang
 Brookhaven National Laboratory

The effects that aerosol particles have on clouds in Earth’s atmosphere and the role that clouds play in
determining the fate of aerosol particles play a crucial role in determining the human experience on this
planet. These interwoven interactions can determine whether it rains or snows, is comfortable, hot or
cold, or whether the cloud you gaze at in the sky looks like a tiger or a lump of porridge. How clouds
mix with the air surrounding them, a process known as entrainment, further affects these processes.

We conducted laboratory experiments of aerosol-cloud interactions in a setting in which we can
measure the effects of entrainment – the first laboratory experiments since the pioneering work of
Latham and Reed in 1977. We measured the effects of entrainment on aerosol-cloud interactions in the
Pi Chamber and also used a state-of-the-art large eddy simulation (LES) to investigate processes in fine
detail.

Primary Results

Cloud properties depend on their response to mixing of environmental air into the cloud. In one limit
(inhomogeneous mixing), droplet growth can be accelerated due to the diminished number of droplets
that compete for the available water vapor. That accelerated droplet growth may play a role in the
initiation of precipitation. We measured the response of cloud droplet sizes to mixing in clouds formed
in the laboratory. They respond differently depending on the time scales: locally inhomogeneous and
globally homogeneous. The apparent contradiction results from the system residing in multiple
equilibrium states, and this helps inform the longstanding debate about the type of mixing that occurs
in natural clouds.
See: Yeom, J.M., Helman, I., Prabhakaran, P., Anderson, J.C., Yang, F., Shaw, R.A. and Cantrell, W.,
2023. Cloud microphysical response to entrainment and mixing is locally inhomogeneous and globally
homogeneous: Evidence from the lab. Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences, 120(42),
p.e2307354120.

In turbulent clouds, mixing causes the humidity (excess or deficit of water vapor from equilibrium) to
vary spatially and temporally. The cloud response to these changes in relative humidity depend partly
on the number and size of cloud droplets. A common assumption is that droplet growth and evaporation
buffer fluctuations. However, measuring such variations has proven difficult, particularly under field
conditions. Using the Pi Chamber, we measured the in-cloud saturation ratio across a range of
conditions from clean to polluted cloud properties. Our measurements reveal that in clouds with low
droplet concentrations, saturation ratio variations are not suppressed as typically assumed.
See: Anderson, J.C., Helman, I., Shaw, R.A. and Cantrell, W., 2024. Droplet growth or evaporation
does not buffer the variability in supersaturation in clean clouds. Journal of the Atmospheric
Sciences, 81(1), pp.225-233.

Large-eddy simulation (LES) can resolve processes that cannot currently be reliably measured. Using
the System for Atmospheric Modeling (SAM), we showed inhomogeneous mixing signatures for

individual mixing events and the appearance of homogeneous mixing in an ensemble of mixing
episodes. The simulation indicates that inhomogeneous mixing is more pronounced in a polluted cloud,
but coarser grid spacing in simulations may compromise the accuracy of this signature. Finally, the
homogeneous mixing signature results from various equilibrium states established for different mixing
intensities and adjusted wall fluxes, which are challenging to measure experimentally but can be easily
analyzed in the simulations.
See: Wang, A., Ovchinnikov, M., Yang, F., Cantrell, W., Yeom, J. and Shaw, R.A., 2024. The dual
nature of entrainment-mixing signatures revealed through large-eddy simulations of a convection-cloud
chamber. Journal of the Atmospheric Sciences, 81(12), pp.2017-2039.

Mixing of cloudy with surrounding, drier air affects cloud propetties because of the change (and
gradient in) water vapor content. That mixing also carries aerosol particles, which mayu affect cloud
properties. Experiments in the Pi Chamber showed that cloud droplet number concentration increases
and mean radius of droplets decreases as dry air containing particles mixed with cloudy air, which leads
to narrower droplet size distributions and smaller relative dispersion. These behaviors are generally
consistent with the scenario expected from the first aerosol-cloud indirect effect for a constant liquid
water content (L). However, L increases significantly in the chamber experiments because removal of
droplets by sedimentation is decreased. These findings are of relevance to the mixing interface at
stratocumulus cloud top, where modeling studies have suggested sedimentation plays a strong role in
regulating L.
See: Yeom, J.M., Fahandezh Sadi, H., Anderson, J.C., Yang, F., Cantrell, W. and Shaw, R.A., 2025.
Cloud microphysical response to entrainment of dry air containing aerosols. npj Climate and
Atmospheric Science, 8(1), p.8.

Cloud reflectivity depends on the size distribution of cloud droplets. Aerosol size strongly affects
whether they become cloud droplets in a given condition. One might expect cloud properties to be
sensitive to aerosol size distributions. We showed, through a combination of cloud chamber
experiments and LES runs, that cloud microphysical and optical properties are often insensitive to
aerosol size. We resolved effects on cloud optical properties only under weak convective forcing and
high aerosol concentrations. This implies that, in most conditions, cloud reflectivity can be predicted
from aerosol number alone without detailed knowledge of aerosol size distributions.
See: Gali, K.F., Sadi, H.F., Anderson, J.C., Beeler, P., Wang, A., Richter, D., Shaw, R.A., Yang, F.,
Cantrell, W. and Fierce, L., 2025. Cloud droplet size distribution and optical properties only weakly
linked to aerosol size. arXiv preprint arXiv:2511.15832.
