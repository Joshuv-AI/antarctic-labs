Non-receptor tyrosine kinase signaling pathways and therapeutic implications
Authors: MD Anwar Hossain, Camila Marques Cuoco, Snigdha Hemant Sodaye, Joris Beci, Karim Belkas
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCell signaling regulates cellular physiological functions, including the cell cycle, survival, immune response, gene expression, and protein-protein interactions. Within this complex system and its intrinsic mechanisms, non-receptor tyrosine kinases (NRTKs) play a crucial role in signaling in the cellular cytoplasmic compartments. Owing to the involvement of NRTKs in the signaling cascade, they have made a significant contribution to the manifestation of etiologically complex diseases such as cancer, cardiovascular, neurodegenerative, autoimmune, and metabolic diseases, which are some of the major causes of mortality globally and often lack remedial measures. While understanding how NRTKs and their signaling pathways are involved in the onset of diseases is currently an emerging research topic, we noticed a lack of a broader picture of the commonalities between diseases and their corresponding pathologies. Thus, the goal of this review was to present a holistic view of NRTKs in cellular homeostasis, emphasizing both their convergent and divergent signaling roles within and across disease systems. Such approaches include shared pathways such as SRC/PI3K/AKT, JAK/STAT, MAPK, and FAK–integrin signaling that operate across disease systems, contributing to inflammation, immune modulation, metabolic reprogramming, and cellular stress responses. These common networks enable us to identify nodal points of convergence that are often co-opted or dysregulated across multiple pathologies. This review also demonstrates crosstalk concerning NRTKs’ interactions with their cellular environment and other molecules with therapeutic potential. Additionally, current FDA-approved drugs and on-going clinical trials will provide a basis for understanding the effects of NRTKs-targeting drugs and their potential for application to other diseases, given their cascading mechanisms.Subject terms: Cancer genomics, Cardiovascular diseases, Metabolic disorders

Introduction

Cell signaling is crucial for coordinating cellular activity in every organism. The delicate balance of exchanging biochemical information relies on enzymes and a plethora of other factors that mediate extracellular, intercellular, and intracellular signals. One of these key factors is protein tyrosine kinases (PTKs), which are enzymes involved in most cellular signaling pathways. Mutations or other aberrations involving PTKs can affect the regulation of cellular functions such as growth, cell survival, cell proliferation, post-translational modification, and the phosphorylation of tyrosine residues on other proteins, making an integral therapeutic target.1,2 PTKs are classified as either receptor tyrosine kinases (RTKs) or nonreceptor tyrosine kinases (NRTKs). RTKs rapidly and reversibly phosphorylate protein substrates on their serine, threonine, and tyrosine residues. RTKs get activated upon binding to growth factors, cytokines, and other factors that trigger a cascade of signals into the cytoplasm and nucleus—instrumental in cellular signaling regulation, especially during embryogenesis.1 On the other hand, NRTKs are cytosolic enzymes found in the cytoplasm or on the cell surface that are sometimes tethered to lipid membranes through lipid modifications (e.g., myristylation or palmitoylation) at their amino terminus, directly binding to specific membrane lipids through lipid-binding domains, or recruitment via protein-protein interactions with membrane-associated partners such as actin, CRK, FAK, paxillin.3 Such a phenomenon allows them to associate with membranes without being integral membrane proteins. NRTKs regulate and guide intracellular signals arising from the activation of extracellular signaling molecules, receptors, cytoskeletal reorganization, cell adhesion, and proliferation, thereby driving global interest in NRTKs as therapeutic targets (Fig. 1). Divergence in the molecular structures of NRTKs contributes to corresponding differences in their functions. RTKs possess extracellular domains for receptor-ligand interactions, whereas NRTKs do not have a transmembrane domain or an extracellular domain. Furthermore, RTKs are single-pass transmembrane proteins composed of an extracellular ligand-binding domain, a single α-helical transmembrane segment, and an intracellular region containing a juxtamembrane segment, a conserved tyrosine kinase domain, and a flexible C-terminal tail with multiple tyrosine residues for signaling. Ligand binding to the extracellular domain induces receptor dimerization, activating the intracellular kinase, which autophosphorylates the cytoplasmic tail and initiates downstream signaling. NRTKs, however, mediate interactions within the intracellular signaling cascade.1

The NRTK families are classified based on their conserved sequences, which are ABL, ACK, JAK, FES, FAK, TEC, SRC, CSK, and SYK, with PTK6/BRK as one of the outlying groups.4 Each one of the members of these families has a direct or indirect role in pathological pathways. For instance, pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) is directly affected by B-lymphoid tyrosine kinase (BLK), an NRTK family member (SFK) that inhibits the formation of the cytoplasmic destruction complex (CDC).5,6 The CDC, composed of Axin, APC, GSK-3β, and CK1α, normally targets β-catenin for phosphorylation, ubiquitination, and proteasomal degradation, thereby suppressing inappropriate Wnt signaling. In PDAC, however, BLK disrupts this process by phosphorylating FAM83A, an adaptor protein, at tyrosine 138, thereby promoting its interaction with β-catenin and impairing CDC assembly. As a result, β-catenin escapes degradation, accumulates in the cytoplasm and nucleus, and activates Wnt/β-catenin–dependent transcriptional programs that drive tumor progression.6 ACK1 and BRK demonstrate control over the proliferation, survival, and activation of B-cells and T-cells, and ACK1 is often upregulated in several types of cancer, such as breast, esophageal, lung, ovarian, pancreatic, and prostate.7,8 TEX activation promotes cardio-protection after ischemic injury through HIF-1α and ROCK1 signaling. In contrast, BTK amplifies vascular inflammation and metabolic dysfunction by driving NLRP3, NF-κB, STAT1, and PPARγ pathways, thereby exacerbating atherosclerosis, sepsis-associated cardiac dysfunction, atrial fibrillation, and hypertrophy.9 These have led to the development of BTK inhibitors, such as Ibrutinib, Acalabrutinib, and Zanubrutinib, which are currently being evaluated not only for hematologic malignancies but also for their potential to attenuate cardiovascular inflammation and maladaptive remodeling.10 Intriguingly, tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs) used in hematological oncology, such as Imatinib in chronic myeloid leukemia, have been reported to be associated with reduced cardiovascular mortality in long-term cohorts, suggesting that modulation of NRTK signaling can confer protective effects beyond cancer therapy.11 In neurodegenerative disorders, aberrant activation of ABL kinases contributes to impaired proteostasis, protein misfolding, and neuronal loss. Pharmacological inhibition with Nilotinib and Bosutinib, which penetrate the blood–brain barrier, has demonstrated the ability to suppress ABL activity, enhance autophagic degradation of tau, amyloid, and α-synuclein aggregates, and improve motor and cognitive outcomes in preclinical models and early clinical studies.12,13 Related pathways mediated by SRC and focal adhesion kinase (FAK) also regulate cytoskeletal integrity and neuronal survival, reinforcing their therapeutic significance. In addition, the FAK-related kinase Pyk2 has been implicated in synaptic plasticity and forebrain signaling, linking NRTK activity to memory function and cognitive resilience.14 Such orchestrated signaling cascades provide insight into the coordinated roles of NRTKs in disease pathologies and have explored opportunities for drug discovery research over the past few decades. However, persisting challenges remained obscure because inadequate attention was paid to the whole picture of NRTK families’ complex signaling cascades and associated cellular pathways in the different pathological progression. Thus, therapeutic development of NRTKs is hindered by the complexity of their signaling networks, where redundancy and crosstalk often enable compensatory pathway activation and resistance to single-agent inhibition. Additional barriers include kinase domain mutations, tumor and disease heterogeneity, and the high risk of off-target effects due to conserved structural domains, which together limit selectivity, dosing, and clinical efficacy.

Thus, this review aims to uncover the functional divergences and therapeutic implications of NRTKs in cellular signaling cascades and pathophysiologies such as cancer, cardiovascular diseases, neurodegenerative diseases, metabolic disorders, and autoimmune diseases. This review aimed to holistically analyze signaling mechanisms across disease states, and to elucidate scientific standpoints on the commonalities of NRTK families and their associated challenges, with therapeutic significance.

Research history of NRTKs

The search to understand the role of PTKs began in the late 1960s and differentiated into two research areas: RTKs and NRTKs. SRC—the first oncogene discovered—was instrumental in the discovery of the first tyrosine kinase, v-SRC, as outlined in the Historical Timeline (Fig. 2). Sequencing of viral genomes and characterization of proteins encoded by transforming genes revealed the molecular basis through which tumor viruses malignantly transform cells. Joan Brugge and Ray Erikson, using antitumor serum, identified the v-SRC protein and the cellular homologous c-SRC protein present in normal cells. In 1978, Erikson’s group, building on the breakthrough discovery of SV40 tumor antigens, found that the v-SRC gene in Rous Sarcoma Virus (RSV) exhibited protein kinase activity specific for threonine. In 1979, Tony Hunter and Bart Sefton subsequently confirmed that v-SRC is a tyrosine kinase, producing their antitumor serum by using immunoprecipitation and an in vitro kinase assay, revealing SRC’s phosphorylation activity. The fact that activated tyrosine kinases were demonstrably responsible, through mutant oncogenes, for the development of cancer as seen in these tumor viruses propelled subsequent research at the turn of the century. The development of TKI drugs, which began in 2001, has now been approved for cancer therapy, with many also being researched for other disease applications.15 However, it is worth noting that there are still core limitations and complications associated with the use and clarity of these therapeutics. Dasatinib and other SRC family kinase (SFK) inhibitors have shown variable efficacy in treating different cancers. These inhibitors have demonstrated some utility against solid tumors, especially in recent years.16 However, SFK inhibitors are clinically approved for treatment in leukemias and lymphomas. The consistent success of these therapeutics in treating solid tumors in other cancer types may be achievable but has not yet been realized.

A timeline highlights the research achievements of NRTKs from 1970 to 2020. ABL was discovered and connected to cancer in the 1980s, followed by SYK discovery from porcine spleen cells in the 1990s. In the 2000s and 2010s, the FDA approved inhibitors such as Imatinib for CML and GIST; Dasatinib and Nilotinib for CML; JAK inhibitors Tofacitinib for RA; and Ruxolitinib for myelofibrosis; and BTK inhibitors Ibrutinib, Acalabrutinib, and Zanubrutinib approved for B-cell malignancies and lymphomas. Some recent approvals in the 2020 s were JAK inhibitors Upadacitinib for RA and in research for autoimmune disease, Brepocitinib for plaque psoriasis, Gusacitinib in clinical trials for eczema, with many more NRTK inhibitors in the pipeline. Similar to v- SRC and c-SRC, the feline sarcoma (FES) and FES-related (FER) kinases were among the first PTKs characterized. The c-FES/FPS proto-oncogene, initially isolated as part of oncogenic Gag-FES/FPS chimeras present in some avian and feline retroviruses, led to the identification of the corresponding mammalian and avian cellular proto-oncogenes.17 Of note, c-FES has unique protein homology compared to other NRTKs such as c-SRC or c-ABL; FES kinases are characterized by their unique amino-terminal FES/FER/Cdc42-interacting protein homology domain, followed by two coiled-coil motifs, a central SRC-homology 2 (SH2) domain, and a C-terminal kinase domain. Although first identified as an oncogene, genetic evidence also suggests that c-FES acts as a tumor suppressor, with implications arising from its overexpression and inhibition in different cancer types.1

In the 1980s, the Janus tyrosine kinase (JAK) family was identified as a crucial enzyme in signal transduction, particularly in its association with signal transducer and activator of transcription (STAT) pathways. JAK/STAT is involved in proliferation, differentiation, and survival in cancer cells.18 The next NRTK family discovered was ABL (Abelson tyrosine kinase), which was found in a tumor gene in the Abelson murine lymphosarcoma virus. Such studies started on Drosophila and demonstrated evidence for ABL functions during developmental stages, one of its roles being formation of axonal connections in the CNS.19 However, ABL, JAK1, JAK2, and TYK2 were discovered using a degenerate polymerase chain reaction in a conserved kinase catalytic subdomain. In the 1990s, more JAK family members were introduced based on their functions and on the conserved structures of Spleen tyrosine kinase (SYK) and FAK. SYK was discovered by cloning cDNA from porcine spleen cells. FAK has been identified as a key factor in many malignant processes, such as overexpression in colon, ovary, pancreatic, and prostate cancer.1 The discovery of these (and additional) NRTK families has led to increased clinical research to understand their roles in pathological pathways and how they might be leveraged clinically. From the year 2000 onward, NRTKs have become a prominent target for therapies, most of which involve inhibiting their function, especially in cancer.20–24 Such progress in NRTK research facilitated the development of new therapies—for instance, Dasatinib in the 2000s—a broad-spectrum TKI known for its applications as a dual/v-ABL Abelson murine leukemia viral oncogene homolog (ABL) and SRC inhibitor.25 However, current therapies increasingly explore NRTKs in other pathologies, including cardiac diseases, skin conditions, and chronic inflammation.21 Although NRTKs have been researched extensively for decades, further research is crucial for continued elucidation of NRTKs’ function and their full potential for use in clinical therapies.

Other kinases, such as SRMS and BRK (PTK6), which are not major NRTK family members but outlying groups, are associated with differentiation, autophagy, drug resistance, and cancer progression. Structural characteristics

NRTK families share highly conserved catalytic kinase domains and non-catalytic regulatory domains, which are essential for their unique activation and function (Fig. 3).26,27 Such NRTK family domains facilitate the recruitment of signaling molecules, thereby enhancing the specificity and efficiency of intracellular communication. For instance, NRTK’s SRC homology (SH) domains, particularly the SH2 and SH3 domains, contribute to protein-protein interactions—except for JAK which has a Janus homology domain-2 (JH2).2 The SH2 domain serves as a prototype for a broad family of protein interaction modules, playing a central role in organizing cellular signaling networks. It binds to specific phosphotyrosine motifs, enabling SH2-domains containing proteins to interact with activated receptors and propagate downstream signaling cascades. Additionally, receptor binding can alleviate the inhibitory effects of intramolecular interactions between the SH2 and catalytic domains, enabling kinase activation.2,28 Proteins harboring SH2 domains often become preferred substrates for phosphorylation because their SH2-mediated sites bind to phosphotyrosine, which positions them closer to active kinases, increasing the likelihood of phosphorylation. Once phosphorylated, new SH2 binding sites are created, leading to the recruitment of more SH2-containing effectors and further amplifying signal transduction.29

In addition to SH2 domains, SH3 domains contribute to protein-protein interactions by recognizing proline-rich motifs.4 In recent years, high-resolution models of NRTK domains have been produced, revealing detailed mechanisms of kinase activation and autoinhibition. Key structural elements, including the activation loop, glycine-rich loop (G-loop), and αC-helix, have been mapped in both active and inactive states. The activation loop is a flexible segment adjacent to the catalytic site that acts as a molecular switch, blocking or exposing the substrate-binding site, depending on a phosphorylation-driven conformational change. The G-loop, which is present near the ATP-binding site, ensures efficient catalysis by coordinating the binding of nucleotide and stabilizing the γ-phosphate of ATP. The αC-helix is a conserved helical element in the N-terminal lobe that rotates to align a critical glutamate with a lysine in the β3 strand, forming the “lysine–glutamate salt bridge” that locks the active site into a catalytically competent state. These regions are highly mobile and central to the dynamic assembly and disassembly of the kinase active site. Advanced crystallography and NMR studies have captured a variety of inactive kinase conformations, notably the “DFG-out” state, in which the DFG motif at the beginning of the activation loop is dramatically reorganized compared to the active conformation. This DFG-out conformation is stabilized by specific intramolecular (in cis) interactions, enabling the activation loop to mimic a substrate. This feature is particularly enhanced in tyrosine kinases compared to serine/threonine kinases and plays a key role in autoregulation. Comparative structural analyses have revealed that tyrosine kinases, including NRTKs, possess unique sequence and structural motifs that distinguish them from serine/threonine kinases. One notable feature is the C-terminal anchor motif in the activation loop, which is preserved in the inactive conformation of TKs, allowing for substrate mimicry and precise autoregulation. Sequence variation at intramolecular interfaces, particularly within the activation loop, contributes to the conformational diversity and unique regulatory mechanisms characteristic of NRTKs.30

The ABL, SRC, and TEC families share a common feature: their SH2 and SH3 domains regulate kinase activity and mediate interactions with other signaling proteins, thereby influencing diverse cellular processes such as proliferation, differentiation, and survival (Fig. 3).31–33 What makes the ABL family stand out is that, apart from SH3, SH2, and catalytic domains, it has unique C-terminal actin-binding and DNA-binding domains, which allow the ABL family (ABL1 and ABL2) to govern cellular responses to stress, DNA damage, and cytoskeletal remodeling. Importantly, the oncogenic BCR-ABL1 fusion protein, found in chronic myeloid leukemia (CML), constitutively activates proliferative signaling, making ABL a cornerstone of targeted cancer therapy.1 The SRC family is distinguished by an SH4 domain for membrane anchoring, followed by unique SH3, SH2, and kinase domains, and a C-terminal regulatory tyrosine. The SH4 anchors the kinase to the membrane, the unique region mediates isoform-specific interactions, SH3 and SH2 regulate protein–protein interactions and substrate recognition, the kinase/SH1 domain catalyzes phosphorylation, and the C-terminal tyrosine controls kinase activity through phosphorylation-dependent inhibition.34 TEC kinases contain a pleckstrin homology (PH) domain, TEC homology (TH) region, SH3 and SH2 domains, and a kinase domain, with TXK lacking PH/TH but featuring a cysteine string for membrane anchoring. Here, the PH domain mediates membrane anchoring via phosphoinositide binding and the TH region facilitates protein–lipid interactions and kinase regulation.1 The ACK family is characterized by an N-terminal sterile alpha motif (SAM) domain, an SH3 domain, and a Cdc42/Rac-interactive binding (CRIB) domain, allowing it to regulate cytoskeletal organization and cell migration. SAM domain mediates localization and dimerization, the SH3 domain binds proline-rich motifs to assemble signaling complexes, the CRIB domain links ACK activity to Cdc42/Rac signaling and actin remodeling.28,35

The JAK family contains two kinase domains, one of which is the aforementioned JH2, functioning as a pseudokinase domain. This domain lacks important catalytic residues required for phosphotransferase activity, making it an important regulatory element that controls JAK-mediated signaling.27,36 Upstream of the pseudokinase lies the FERM domain (JH4–JH7), which mediates receptor binding by anchoring JAKs to cytokine receptor chains and is essential for catalytic activation of the JAKs. Adjacent to this FERM domain is an SH2-like domain (JH3–JH5), which serves as a structural scaffold, stabilizes receptor–JAK complexes, and facilitates the coupling of receptor engagement with downstream kinase activation. The second kinase domain, JH1, is the catalytically active kinase domain, responsible for trans- and auto-phosphorylation events that initiate JAK-STAT signaling.37 The FAK (Focal Adhesion Kinase) family includes an N-terminal FERM domain, a central kinase domain, and a C-terminal focal adhesion targeting (FAT) domain that localizes the kinase to adhesion sites. The FAK family operates at the nexus of integrin signaling and mechanotransduction. FAK facilitates focal adhesion turnover and serves as a scaffold for signaling proteins, including SRC, PI3K, and p130CAS, thereby influencing actin cytoskeleton remodeling, cell migration, survival, and chemokine expression. Its upregulation in many tumors underscores its importance in cancer progression, metastasis, and microenvironment modulation.38 On the other hand, the SYK family comprises two tandem SH2 domains that recognize ITAM motifs, which relieve autoinhibition and coupling to immune receptor signaling, while interdomain linkers further tune activation and effector recruitment, and a C-terminal kinase domain mediates immune cell activation and integrin signaling.39 SYK (Spleen Tyrosine Kinase) plays a pivotal role in immune receptor signaling through the B-cell receptor (BCR), Fc receptors, and C-type lectin receptors. It is integral to adaptive immunity and innate immune responses, activating pathways, such as CARD9-BCL10-MALT1 and the NLRP3 inflammasome, and regulating vascular development and platelet function via CLEC2 signaling.6,40 FES contains N-terminal F-BAR domains that mediate membrane interaction, tubulation, and oligomerization, ensuring proper subcellular localization. Such functions are followed by central SH2 domains that bind phosphotyrosine motifs and regulate substrate recognition, and C-terminal kinase domains that drive phosphorylation of targets essential for immune response, cytoskeletal regulation, and angiogenesis.1,41 FES is involved in regulating STAT proteins and p70 S6 kinase downstream of oncogenic KIT receptors, such as KIT D816V, commonly mutated in mastocytoma. It plays a dual role in modulating signaling: negatively regulating STAT phosphorylation (STAT-1/3/5) while positively promoting phosphorylation of p70 S6 kinase at Thr389, which is crucial for cell cycle progression and G1-S transition. These functions position FES as a key regulator in mast cell proliferation and oncogenic signaling.1,30 In contrast, CSK (C-terminal SRC kinase), while structurally similar to SRC, lacks the C-terminal regulatory tyrosine but retains SH3, SH2, and kinase domains and is a central negative regulator of SFKs. The N-terminal SH3 domain is responsible for scaffold interactions, and the internal SH2 domain binds phosphotyrosine-containing scaffolds such as Cbp/PAG1, recruiting CSK to membranes where its C-terminal kinase domain phosphorylates SRC-family kinases at inhibitory sites, thereby restraining their activity. By phosphorylating the C-terminal inhibitory tyrosine of SFKs, CSK enforces their inactive conformation, thereby downregulating signal transduction. This modulation impacts immune cell signaling (TCR, BCR), cell migration, differentiation, and bone homeostasis.42

The FER kinase, a member of the FPS/FES-related tyrosine kinase family, is characterized by a unique structural organization that includes an N-terminal FER-CIP4 homology (FCH) domain, three coiled-coil (CC) domains, a central SRC homology 2 (SH2) domain, and a C-terminal tyrosine kinase domain. The coiled-coil domains facilitate oligomerization and protein-protein interactions, while the SH2 domain enables FER to bind phosphotyrosine-containing ligands, thereby regulating its kinase activity and substrate specificity.30 Functionally, FER plays a pivotal role in modulating cell-cell adhesion by interacting with the cadherin-catenin complex, as well as in signaling cascades downstream of activated cell-surface receptors, such as the epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) and platelet-derived growth factor receptor (PDGFR). Additionally, FER regulates cytoskeletal dynamics, cell migration, and immune cell signaling, particularly in mast cells. Importantly, FER overexpression has been linked to NFκB signaling activation and the development of resistance to certain cancer therapeutics, underscoring its pathological relevance in oncogenesis.1,43,44 BRK, also known as breast tumor kinase (PTK6), is a non-receptor tyrosine kinase primarily expressed in epithelial tissues. Structurally, BRK contains an SH3 domain, an SH2 domain, and a protein tyrosine kinase domain. Unlike SFKs, BRK lacks the N-terminal myristylation site, which allows it to localize to both the nucleus and cytoplasm. BRK also features a C-terminal regulatory tyrosine residue (Y447) that negatively regulates its kinase activity, as well as a unique linker region that is critical to its regulatory mechanisms. Functionally, BRK acts as an intracellular signal transducer, promoting cell proliferation, migration, and survival, especially in carcinoma cells. It enhances mitogenic signaling in response to growth factors such as EGF and regulates cell cycle progression by modulating the phosphorylation of cyclin D/E and the retinoblastoma protein. Moreover, BRK can target tumor suppressor proteins like Dok1 for degradation, thereby facilitating oncogenic processes. Clinically, BRK is overexpressed in the majority of breast tumors and several other cancer types, making it a potential target for therapeutic intervention.45–47

Regulatory mechanisms

NRTKs play a vital role in catalyzing phosphorylation as a Post-translation Modification (PTM). PTMs function as molecular switches that regulate protein activity, interactions, localization, and stability, thereby enabling precise control over signaling pathways that govern cell proliferation, differentiation, migration, and survival. By integrating extracellular cues into finely tuned intracellular responses, NRTKs facilitate multilevel regulation spanning from immediate signal transduction at the membrane to transcriptional and post-transcriptional control within the nucleus. Dysregulation of NRTKs activity and associated PTMs is implicated in various diseases, including cancer and immune disorders, making them critical targets for therapeutic intervention.

SRC-family kinase member LYN suppresses immunoreceptor signaling in myeloid immune cells and B lymphocytes. In the absence of LYN, interleukin (IL)-6, B-cell activating factor (BAFF), and interferon-gamma (IFN-γ) promote inflammation and immature white blood cells called myeloid cells. LYN suppresses the immunoreceptor tyrosine-based activation motif (ITAM) signaling by phosphorylating immunoreceptor tyrosine-based inhibitory motifs (ITIMs), thereby inhibiting immune activation through inhibitory protein phosphatases, including lipid phosphatases.48 Furthermore, SRC phosphorylates eukaryotic initiation factor 4E (eIF4E), enhancing its binding to the mRNA 5′ cap and promoting translation initiation.49 Phosphorylating the eIF4E-binding proteins (4E-BPs) regulates them from the eIF4E complex and allows translation to proceed.50 However, there was a lack of evidence to support the implications of these NRTKs on mRNA stability. Integrins, G protein-coupled receptors (GPCRs), or RTKs can activate NRTKs like FAK, JAK, and SFKs such as FYN, which can then have downstream effects through the RAS-MAPK pathway, PI3K-Akt signaling, or STAT transcriptional activation, as shown in Fig. 4.51 ACK interacts with the AKT and p85 (PI3k regulatory subunit) factors, which regulate cellular transcription. JAKs also phosphorylate Signal Transducers and Activators of Transcription (STAT) proteins, which dimerize and relocate to the nucleus and control gene expression in cytokine signaling.52,53 As CSK intrinsically inactive or low activity state in its native form and blockage SRC, PKA regulates CSK, which in turn activates SRC; FAK and RTK signaling can also activate SRC. SRC itself is central to other signaling cascades; it can trigger the ABL/Grb2-associated RAS/RAF/MAPK cascade (Fig. 4), which can also be activated by oxidative stress. Simultaneously, it also regulates the SYK signaling pathway, which leads to BTK recruiting BLNK and phosphorylating it, thereby inducing transcription of cytokines via NFκB and NFAT. Parallel to this, SRC could also initiate the FER-dependent contractile activation that affects Arp2/3 and regulates F-actin branching, thereby regulating cytoskeletal reorganization. Cellular homeostasis relies on the distinct regulation and activation of NRTKs, while cancer, immunological diseases, and developmental abnormalities are often associated with their dysregulation.23,54,55 Other NRTKs, such as ABLs, ensure RNA stability, wherein phosphorylation can influence the binding of RNA-binding protein (RBP) HuR (human antigen R) and microRNAs (miRNAs) to mRNA, thereby preventing its degradation.56

FAK upregulation leads to cell adhesion, migration, invasion, and resistance to apoptosis, which contributes to the metastatic behavior of cancer cells.57 It operates through kinase-dependent and independent mechanisms that contribute to the metastasis of several types of cancer, such as breast cancer, ovarian cancer, and colorectal cancer. FAK can directly phosphorylate SFKs and form a heterodimeric complex, resulting in the phosphorylation of p130C as an adaptor molecule and activation of the RAS-Erk pathway in mammary tumors.38,58 Moreover, FAK activates the downstream signaling cascades of protein kinase B (AKT), phosphatidylinositol-3-kinase (PI3K), and mechanistic target of rapamycin (mTOR), which have been shown to enable epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) in colorectal cancer cells. However, in another study, it was revealed that Sulfatase 1 (SULF1) can inhibit EMT through the FAK/PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway, becoming a possible target for future cancer treatments.59

Other regulatory processes, such as ubiquitination, are also regulated by c-ABL. They recruit and regulate the activity of the E3 ubiquitin ligases, which are responsible for ubiquitin tagging.60 Similarly, ABL phosphorylates tumor suppressor p53 protein, promoting cell stability, which leads to a balanced cell cycle and apoptosis.61 Furthermore, ABL also phosphorylates the RAS protein, activating the MAPK/ERK signaling pathway, which is integral to cell growth and differentiation.62 Similarly, NRTKs such as LYN and FYN regulate immune signaling pathways by phosphorylating components of the T-cell receptor complex, thereby modulating immune responses.63 SFKs activate the focal adhesion signaling pathways and paxillin, and ABLs regulate the MAPK/ERK pathway.64 Similarly, their involvement in the crosstalk between signaling pathways is essential. SFKs have been shown to link integrin and EGFR signaling, in which integrin activation is followed by the recruitment and phosphorylation of SRC, which then transduces signals that regulate EGFR signaling. The EGFR/β5-integrin/SRC signaling axis regulates clathrin lattice expansion during growth factor stimulation, showcasing the complex interplay between these signaling molecules.65,66

SRC regulates the phosphorylation of spliceosomal proteins, which are crucial for splicing events.67 Moreover, SRC, FYN, and c-ABL regulate alternative RNA splicing by phosphorylating specific splicing factors and RNA-binding proteins (RBPs) like Sam68, YT521-B, and RBM39. SRC and FYN modulate splicing by phosphorylating Sam68 and YT521-B, altering their localization and interactions with pre-mRNA, thereby influencing spliceosome assembly and exon selection. These modifications link extracellular cues such as growth factors and immune signals to splicing outcomes, allowing cells to dynamically reshape their transcriptome in response to environmental changes.68 In contrast, c-ABL directly impacts co-transcriptional splicing by phosphorylating SR-rich proteins like RBM39, thereby modulating their recruitment and regulatory function during splice site selection. Disruption of these interactions, whether due to c-ABL dysregulation or cellular stress, can impair splicing fidelity and alter mRNA isoform profiles.69 Furthermore, mutations in these kinases or aberrant activation could also influence the splicing activity, given their direct interaction with spliceosome proteins.

The downregulation of NRTKs can be possible through proteins, such as Protein-Tyrosine Phosphatases (PTPs), which regulate several NRTKs, including JAK and SRC. Two PTPs, PTPN1 and PTPN2, are major negative regulators of the JAK pathway, thereby inhibiting the activation of STAT1, STAT3, STAT4, and STAT5. PTPs influence the maturation and activation of myeloid DCs through the JAK/STAT pathway. In turn, JAK/STAT regulates dendritic cell (DC) differentiation, cytokine production, and DC-mediated T helper 1 development, all of which are essential for antitumor response. Moreover, DCs activate interleukins and T cells. A PTB1B-deficient mouse model demonstrated decreased survival and more rapid tumor development compared to mice carrying the downregulated PTPN1 strains. The same was observed with PTPN2, where mature cell lines carrying the PTPN2 strains produced significant amounts of IL-12 and IFN-γ, and induced more activation of CD4+ and CD8+ T cells.70

Physiological pathways

As stated elsewhere, NRTKs are pivotal in a wide variety of physiological processes, including cell survival and proliferation, cell movement, apoptosis, activation of the immunogenic response, lipid synthesis and storage, and activation of ion channels. SFKs, for example, activate growth factor receptors and downstream signaling molecules like RAS, MEK, and ERK in the MAPK pathway, which are essential for cell division and survival.1,71,72 ABL and SRC regulate endocytosis and vesicular trafficking, essential for cellular communication and receptor turnover. These processes allow the internalization of membrane receptors and the proper trafficking of vehicles, ensuring efficient cellular function. Furthermore, ACK1, FAK, and CSK also regulate gene expression by modifying chromatin structure. They affect histone-modifying enzymes, such as histone acetyltransferases (HATs) and histone deacetylases (HDACs), influencing transcription during processes such as differentiation and cellular stress responses. ABL kinase is involved in actin cytoskeletal remodeling and cell cycle progression, ensuring proper cellular function.61,73 SRC and FAK regulate cell adhesion and migration by interacting with integrins and adhesion molecules at focal adhesion points. Moreover, these kinases modulate the cytoskeleton, facilitating cell movement, which is crucial for processes like tissue development, wound healing, and immune responses.38,74,75 However, overactivation of FAK can promote cancer cell invasion and metastasis, which is otherwise canonically involved in balancing cell survival and apoptosis. SRC and ABL kinases regulate anti-apoptotic pathways, such as the PI3K-Akt pathway and can activate pro-apoptotic factors like p53 under stress conditions, including DNA damage.76,77 This balance is vital for maintaining tissue homeostasis by eliminating damaged cells while preserving healthy ones.

SFKs and SYK are involved in the activation of immunogenic responses. SFKs activate pathways downstream of cytokine and growth factor receptors, leading to inflammation through molecules like NF-κB.61 These kinases also activate immune cells like macrophages and T-cells to defend against infection and injury. In immune cells, SFKs such as LCK and FYN are critical for TCR signaling. Upon antigen recognition, LCK phosphorylates components of the TCR complex, initiating downstream signaling that activates T-cells.78,79 Similarly, in B cells, NRTKs such as SYK facilitate B cell receptor (BCR) signaling, essential for adaptive immune responses.60 ABL and SRC are critical regulators of metabolic pathways by modulating enzymes such as IRS proteins, GLUTs, PFK-2, PEPCK, PDK, ACC, FASN, AMPK, and GSK-3β, involved in glucose metabolism and lipid synthesis, thereby activating insulin signaling and promoting glucose uptake.80,81 Additionally, SYK and LYN are involved in adipocyte differentiation and lipid storage, which are essential for energy balance.82 NRTKs also regulate the ion channels—NMDA receptors, AMPA receptors, voltage-gated potassium channels (Kv1.5, Kv2.1), L-type calcium channels, and nicotinic acetylcholine receptors- in the nervous system and cardiovascular systems. These kinases modulate ion channel activity and receptor trafficking at synapses, influencing neuronal signaling and plasticity. FYN and SRC kinases are involved in synaptic signaling, neurotransmitter release, and long-term potentiation (LTP), which are vital for learning and memory.83 In the cardiovascular system, SRC and ABL are involved in angiogenesis, the process of formation of new blood vessels, and SRC also regulates VEGFR signaling, promoting endothelial cell migration, proliferation, and tube formation, aiding tissue repair and vascular development.84 Taken together, it could be concluded that the NRTKs’ role in physiological function is essential for a regulated homeostasis environment in all known systems.

NRTKs role in pathology

NRTKs play a critical role in many cellular processes, which, when dysregulated, contribute to the development and progression of a wide range of pathological conditions, including cancer, tumorigenesis, cardiovascular diseases, metabolic disorders, neurodegenerative diseases, and autoimmune diseases.61 Such groups of diseases are the leading cause of death in the USA and globally, often co-occurring, complicating diagnosis and treatment.

Cancer remains one of the most prevalent and deadly diseases associated with NRTK dysregulation. In 2020, over 1,800,000 new cancer cases and over 600,000 cancer deaths were reported, and the leading cancers are lung, colorectal, kidney, bladder, breast, and prostate, which increased every year regardless of race, age, and sex.61,85–87 Although there has been a diminishing return in the number of cancer-related deaths throughout the years, due to early detection and improved treatments, it is still a critical life-threating disease with no cure that affects millions of Americans.88 Importantly, it is also imperative to assess the possible comorbidity, such as cardiovascular complications, as seen in 40% of cancer patients’ deaths.89 Despite the fact that there are medications that stabilize cardiovascular diseases and even prevent atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD) like statins, the death rate is still high for heart patients, including those who exercise and follow their treatment plans.90,91 The most prevalent cardiovascular diseases are acquired later in life; thus, understanding how the molecular pathways underlying cardiovascular pathologies are affected by age is essential.91,92 Furthermore, cardiovascular diseases can be exacerbated by metabolic disorders like diabetes and obesity.93–95 However, the metabolic diseases are often associated with age, ethnicity, family history, habits, and diet, and diabetes, more specifically, is an epidemic in the United States. Nevertheless, metabolic diseases often overlap with other disorders like autoimmune disorders, which are multisystemic conditions; thus, understanding how these diseases interact is crucial to developing an inclusive treatment.94,96 Aside from these, neurodegenerative diseases’ occurrences and deaths have increased in the last few years, showing five times more variation in trends in stroke, Alzheimer’s Disease, and migraines.97

Although the above diseases affect different systems, NRTKs such as JAK, FAK, and BTK exhibit similar dysfunctions and mutations across these diseases. FAK overexpression in tumors facilitates metastatic progression due to its signaling cascade, which activates the PI3K/AKT/mTOR and the RAS/RAF pathways.1,85,98,99 Moreover, FAK activation can also influence neuronal apoptosis and blood-brain barrier permeability, aggravating tissue damage.100–103 While FAK normally engages with the PI3K cascade, suppressing pro-apoptotic signals, its dysregulation or overactivation affects the balance between survival and death signals. Such events are controlled via the cytokine-activated JAK and Grb2, which triggers the SOS-MAPK and PI3K-AKT-mTOR cascades. In solid tumors, JAK/STAT is hyperactive, leading to uncontrolled proliferation and immune suppression. Cancer and tumorigenesis

Considerable attention has been paid to the role of the NRTK family in cancer, while some are implicated in eliciting broader roles in aberrant expression, signaling, and cascade effects. As with BRK (Breast Tumor Kinase) in breast cancers, others are more specific to a disease and its subtypes. Much remains to be determined regarding the extent to which NRTKs play a role in tumorigenesis and the development or exacerbation of cancers, yet therapeutics using TKIs have already shown promise, including applications beyond their oncology approvals. Further investigations are needed to explicitly uncover the functions of NRTKs that contribute to cancer and tumorigenesis, thereby enabling therapeutic advancements to combat the suffering associated with these diseases.

A known proto-oncogene overexpressed in various human cancers, SRC activity is considerably higher in metastatic tissue than in normal tissue. Its extensive activity in enhancing different oncogenic signaling pathways, including P13K/AKT, c-Myc, and RAS/RAF, is thus a sensible therapeutic target via SRC kinase inhibitors such as Dasatinib, which has yielded anti-proliferative activity in different tumor types.110 Inhibition of NRTK families, such as SRC, has demonstrated restorative effects in cancer, as evidenced by the restoration of E-cadherin expression in pancreatic cancer cells vulnerable to Dasatinib. In this context, SRC activation led to β-catenin phosphorylation, which in turn forms complexes with cadherins. After phosphorylation by SRC kinase, β-catenin then translocates to the nucleus and activates several EMT transcription factors, negatively regulating E-cadherin expression. Such regulation of E-cadherin ultimately disrupts cadherin-mediated cell-cell adhesion, enhancing the migration potential of tumor cells.111 When investigating c-SRC kinase in liver cancer, significantly higher expression was observed in cancerous tissues compared to that of the wild-type one. Given that the expression was tied to impaired OXPHOS subunit expression in metastatic liver cancer biopsies, it is consistent with other findings that implicate c-SRC in the regulation of mitochondrial energy metabolism in cancer.112 Additionally, downregulation of phosphorylated SRC (pSRCY416) has been shown to inhibit cell growth in cervical cancer cells. PP2, a selective kinase inhibitor of SRC, regulated pSRCY416 in HeLa and SiHa cervical cancer lines. Treatment with PP2, cisplatin, or SRC siRNA decreased expression of cyclin A1, cyclin E, and CDK-2 in SiHa cells. Expression of p21 and p27 was upregulated in both HeLa and SiHa cells, demonstrating down-regulation-induced cell cycle progression and arrest.113 However, the role of SRC as an upstream regulator of cell cycle progression has continued to be investigated and confirmed in tumorigenesis across cancers and signal pathways.

As shown in Fig. 6, the role in enhancing oncogenic signaling pathways, including PI3K/AKT, c-Myc, and RAS/RAF, demonstrates diverse functions across cancers and tumorigenesis.34,110,114,115 Works in tandem with receptor kinases to signal downstream molecules, interacting with FAK, thereby regulating integrin signaling, and was highly expressed in tumor cells.116,117 A positive feedback loop between RAS, PI3K, the cytoskeleton, and FAK had previously been shown to be a driver of RAS signaling excitability. Such phenomenon was investigated, combining FAK and PI3K inhibitors to suppress the growth of cervical and pancreatic cancer cells via increased apoptosis and decreased mitosis. Molecular mechanisms in these interactions require further investigation, although it is postulated that PI3K inhibition is followed by RTK activation post-translation (PI3K had little effect on total HER3, but significantly increased phospho-HER3 levels). Alone, PI3K inhibitors transiently suppressed downstream AKT activity, increasing FAK signaling in cancer cells. Adding FAK inhibitor Defactinib mitigated this latter effect, highlighting the potential and importance of precision and synergistic inhibition for cancer treatment.118 FAK was overexpressed in tumor cells and has also been investigated as a tumor-associated antigen due to its presence and potential application in cancer immunotherapy. Autophosphorylation of FAK in response to integrin opens a binding site for SFKs, facilitating the formation of FAK-kinase complexes. This complex mediates downstream focal adhesion proteins such as paxillin, which, along with the FAK-complex, transduces signals responsible for migration, invasion, and anchorage-independent growth. Increased expression of FAK and paxillin has been noted in several types of cancers, including breast, head and neck, colorectal cancer, and colorectal liver metastases.119 Tumors with high FAK and expression yielded poor clinical prognosis and predicted tumor recurrence in patients with colorectal cancer.120 FAK was also found to contribute to the initiation and progression of intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma (iCCA) by inducing the YAP proto-oncogene. While FAK was upregulated in human and mouse iCCA cells, its deletion suppressed iCCA initiation and progression. FAK and CDK4/6 cascades were simultaneously activated in over 70% of human iCCA samples, and the combination of FAK inhibitors with CDK4/6 inhibitors strongly inhibited iCCA growth in vitro and in vivo.121

On the other hand, some NRTKs are more centralized to distinct cancers, such as BRK, which is overexpressed in most human breast tumors. BRK expression in mammary epithelial cells plays a role in growth regulation and, thus, in tumor development by potentiating anchorage-independent proliferation and the mitogenic response to EGF.122 As addressed recently that, BRK also contributes to tamoxifen resistance in estrogen receptor-positive (ER+) breast cancers by regulating multiple downstream effectors (such as CDK1) in growth signaling pathways, especially the HER2 signaling.123 The author further clarified that the knockdown of BRK in tamoxifen-resistant cells via shRNA restores tamoxifen sensitivity, suggesting the negative regulation of the MAPK pathway and upregulation of apoptosis pathways contributed to this effect. ACK1, which is ubiquitously expressed in mammals, has been implicated in tumorigenesis and cancer development through increased activity resulting from cancer-associated mutations. Autophosphorylation of mutant forms of ACK1 demonstrated higher activation than that of wild-type counterparts. Its overexpression in cancer cell lines also led to increased invasion in vitro and in vivo. Elevated ACK1 activity may confer anchorage independence and increased motility in cancer cells.124 Therefore, ACK1 and enhanced activity have been associated with metastasis and cancer through invasion and motility. Both ACK1 and BRK play roles in controlling the proliferation, survival, and activation of B cells and T cells, and ACK1 is often upregulated in several types of cancer, including breast, esophageal, lung, ovarian, pancreatic, and prostate cancers.7,8 Auto-activating mutations in ACK1 and gene overexpression have been correlated with poor prognosis in primary lung, ovarian, and prostate tumors, and stomach cancer.125 ACK1 mRNA expression has been significantly increased in many cancer types, including lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) and lung squamous cell carcinoma. RNA-seq results revealed that silencing the ACK1 gene in a human LUAD cell line activated immune-related signaling pathways, including T cell receptor, chemokine, JAK-STAT, and Toll-like receptor pathways, suggesting that ACK1 may modulate the tumor immune microenvironment.126–130 In another study, ACK1 expression was upregulated in colon cancer cells compared with normal colon cells. Its direct binding to miR-125-a-3p affects the proliferation and invasion of colon cancer cells, and its expression has prognostic relevance.131 ACK1 phosphorylated ATP synthase F1 subunit alpha (ATP5F1A), increasing the stability of complex V and enhancing mitochondrial energy output in cancer cells. This phosphorylation prevents ATP5F1A from binding its physiological inhibitor, ATP synthase inhibitory factor subunit 1 (ATP5IF1), enabling sustained mitochondrial activity that promotes cancer cell growth.132

In triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC), several RTKs including EGFR, MET, and c-Kit have been reported to be upregulated across TNBC subsets, prompting exploration of these RTKs as therapeutic targets for TNBC treatment.133–135 However, clinical trials targeting these RTKs yielded discouraging results, such as the Phase II Open-Label Trial by Dr. Rao, at Rush University Medical Center, which assessed the clinical efficacy, biological effects and safety of the EGFR inhibitor Erlotinib in TNBC treatment, where 2/10 patients had a prolonged stable disease. This suggests some patients have a preferred molecular profile that can benefit from EGFR-TKI therapy.136 Another example is the clinical trial “A Multicenter Phase II Trial of Anti-EGFR-Immunoliposomes loaded with Doxorubicin” by Dr. Mamot at Cantonal Hospital Aarau. The clinical trial concluded that there is no efficacy signal to pursue anti-EGFR-ILs-dox for TNBC treatment as their results did not meet the targeted efficacy bar. In the first 12 months, progression-free survival was below 40%, the antitumor activity was not better than historical chemotherapy benchmarks, with overall survival of 12.6 months compared to 8–13 months, respectively, and the outcomes were similar regardless of whether the tumors were EGFR+ or EGFR++/+++.137 Other molecules investigated to date revealed ACK1 as a viable therapeutic target for TNBC, as it is hyperphosphorylated in most aggressive TNBC cell lines. Knockdown of ACK1 reduced the aggressiveness of TNBC both in vitro and in vivo, and an ACK1-specific small-molecule inhibitor, (R)-9bMS, significantly reduced TNBC proliferation.80 Recently, such an inhibitor has shown potential as a therapeutic option for breast cancer patients with resistance to CDK4/6 inhibitors, as it suppressed cell cycle genes CCNB1, CCNB2, and CDC20, and caused G2/M arrest, leading to d significantly impaired metastasis of breast cancer cells to the lung through suppression of CXCR4 receptor.138

The role of SYK as a regulator of metastatic behavior was observed in hematopoietic cells and other tissues, where it was overexpressed. Contrastingly, SYK expression was detected in melanocytes but was downregulated in melanoma cells and absent in invasive tumor growth in breast cancer.139 However, in breast cancers where SYK expression is present, it is associated with improved prognosis.80,140,141 This type of correlation has also been studied in human gastric carcinomas, where SYK expression was normal across all gastric tissue but lower in gastric carcinoma. No methylation of SYK was detected in normal tissue. However, hypermethylation of the SYK gene was significant in gastric carcinoma patients, correlating this silencing of the gene to oncogenesis and metastasis in gastric carcinoma.5,64 In these cases, SYK had been considered a possible tumor suppressor and anti-metastasis gene, with correlational evidence in cancers, mainly due to the low expression and downregulation of SYK seemingly associated with malignant phenotypes such as increased invasion and motility.142 However, it is important to note that SYK’s role in cancer and tumorigenesis has not been so straightforward. SYK also displays tumor-promoting mechanisms, acting instead as a modulator of tumorigenesis for lymphomas and leukemias, as outlined in studies focusing on SYK inhibition to target these cancers.5,143–145 This is consistent with SYK’s function in hematopoietic cells, where it is focal in immune cell signaling and maturation. In diffuse large B cell lymphoma (DLBCL), SYK is one of the components of the BCR signaling pathway that demonstrates increased expression, implying that these lymphomas rely on SYK/BCR-mediated signaling, and such signaling cascade has shown promise as a therapeutic target in clinical trials.115 TYK2, a member of the JAK family, has been implicated in migration and cell invasion in human breast epithelial cancer cells, downregulated at the initiation of nodal metastasis in de-differentiated estrogen receptor α-lacking or reduced breast cancer cells.146

Genetic mutations also play a key role in cancer and therapy resistance. JAK itself is crucial as JAK/STAT is one of the key pathways involved in cancer. Activating mutations in JAK1 (A634D, R724H, S646P, R879C, V658F), JAK2 (V617F), and JAK3 (M511I, V674A, V722I, A572V, L857P, L875H) drive constitutive JAK/STAT pathway activation, promoting uncontrolled proliferation and survival in conditions such as polycythemia vera, essential thrombocythemia, primary myelofibrosis, and leukemias. In solid tumors, including those of the lung, breast, ovary, liver, colorectum, prostate, and brain, persistent JAK/STAT activation results from JAK overexpression, cytokine/receptor upregulation, or rare somatic mutations, with JAK2 amplification notably contributing to proliferation and metastasis in LUAD. Conversely, JAK1 loss-of-function mutations, as observed in endometrial cancer, impair tumor antigen presentation, promoting immune evasion.147–151 Common activating mutations like A572V, M511I, R657Q, V674A, V722I, A572V, in the pseudokinase and L857P, L875H and L857Q in kinase domains of JAK3 lead to constitutive JAK3 signaling. The mutations in pseudokinase domains require JAK1 for their transforming potential. In contrast, kinase domain mutants can act independently of JAK1, resulting in uncontrolled cell proliferation that often leads to T-cell acute lymphoblastic leukemia, Natural killer/T-cell lymphomas, and other T/NK cell malignancies.147,152 Similarly, BTK from the TEC family of NRTKs is also notable. Mutations in the kinase domain (e.g., A428D) may confer resistance to drugs. Emerging research has also revealed that alternative BTK isoforms (e.g., BTK-C, p65BTK) are overexpressed in epithelial and solid tumors, such as breast, prostate, gastric, ovarian, colon, and glioblastoma cancers, where they correlate with advanced tumor grade, metastasis, and chemoresistance. In these cancers, BTK drives RAS/ERK signaling, thereby enhancing invasion, migration, and drug resistance.153 ABL1 mutations and dysregulation are most prominently implicated in cancer, particularly hematological malignancies, but also contribute to congenital cardiovascular and skeletal syndromes. In leukemia, the hallmark BCR–ABL1 fusion resulting from the Philadelphia chromosome produces a constitutively active tyrosine kinase that drives CML and some acute lymphoblastic leukemias by impairing the autophagy initiated by Beclin-1 and simultaneously activating multiple oncogenic pathways. These include RAS/RAF/MEK/ERK for proliferation, PI3K/AKT/mTOR for survival and metabolism, JAK/STAT5 for anti-apoptotic signaling, and CRKL-GRB2-GAB2 scaffolding complexes for signal amplification. It also engages FAK/paxillin and cytoskeletal regulators to promote the adhesion and survival of leukemic stem cells in the bone marrow niche.154 In this context, normal ABL1 has tumor-suppressive functions that promote apoptosis via p73, maintain genomic integrity through RAD51/BRCA1-mediated homologous recombination, and stabilize p53 via MDM2 phosphorylation, especially in response to DNA damage through ATM/ATR signaling. In solid tumors, ABL1 and its paralog ABL2 are frequently overexpressed or amplified in breast, lung, colorectal, hepatocellular, pancreatic, renal, gastric, bladder, and ovarian cancers, with elevated kinase activity (e.g., Tyr412 phosphorylation) correlating with poor prognosis and tumor progression. Although somatic ABL1 mutations are rare, they have been reported in lung, colorectal, and uterine cancers, often associated with an advanced stage.154 ABL1 also modulates cytoskeletal dynamics and cell migration in solid tumors through interactions with Rho GTPases (Rac1, Cdc42, RhoA), WASP/WAVE complexes, integrins, and FAK, integrating structural signals with oncogenic behavior.155 Investigation of NRTKs has continued in oncology as we gain a greater understanding of these enzymes, the signaling pathways they regulate and how to leverage this understanding therapeutically. The scope has already broadened, as several drugs approved for treating cancers, mainly leukemias, have been tested for other ailments, such as neurodegenerative diseases, with some success. This versatile application targeting fundamental pathogenic processes strongly supports the continued research into NRTKs.

PTPs and SOCS proteins play complex, context-dependent roles in cancer, acting as both tumor suppressors and oncogenes depending on the cellular environment. Traditionally viewed as negative regulators of oncogenic tyrosine kinases, these two classes of signaling molecules are now recognized as critical modulators of cancer progression, metastasis, immune evasion, and resistance to therapy.84,156,157 PTPs, such as PTP1B and SHP2 (PTPN11), are oncogenic PTPs that are frequently overexpressed in cancers, including breast, ovarian, and colorectal. These enzymes promote tumorigenesis by sustaining ERK/MAPK and integrin signaling, thereby enhancing invasion and metastasis, and in some contexts, supporting the renewal of cancer stem cells.157,158 PTPN2 modulates both tumor growth and immune checkpoint regulation, and its inhibition can sensitize tumors to anti-PD-1 therapy by promoting T cell infiltration and IFN-γ signaling.159

In parallel, recent developments in tumor biology and microenvironment underscore the multifaceted roles of SOCS proteins, particularly SOCS1 and SOCS3. SOCS1 is widely recognized as a potent tumor suppressor, often silenced via promoter hypermethylation or miRNA repression in diverse cancers, including hepatocellular carcinoma, neuroblastoma, leukemia, and cancers of the colon, breast, prostate, pancreas, and ovary.160,161 It suppresses tumorigenesis by attenuating JAK/STAT and RTK signaling, enhancing p53 activity, and negatively regulating oncogenic mediators, including NRF2 and p21. In the tumor microenvironment, SOCS1 expression in stromal and mesenchymal cells also restrains pro-tumor inflammation, shaping immune contexture.160 Similarly, SOCS3 is frequently inactivated by promoter methylation in lung, breast, and glioblastoma, with loss of SOCS3 correlating with aggressive tumor behavior and poor prognosis. SOCS3 silencing enables unchecked JAK/STAT3 activation, promoting proliferation, resistance to apoptosis, and immune evasion. Restoration of SOCS3 expression in preclinical models results in suppressed STAT3 activity, induction of apoptosis, and reduced tumor growth. Beyond their direct effects on tumors, SOCS proteins regulate immune differentiation and homeostasis, controlling both innate and adaptive immune responses within the tumor microenvironment.162,163

Cardiovascular diseases

A common underlying factor in cardiac conditions, including ischemia, hypertension, atherosclerosis, and congestive heart failure (CHF), is oxidative stress and inflammation, which are regulated by key NRTKs such as SRC, FAK, and JAK/STATpathways.9,164 Mitochondria-derived ROS can influence both mitochondrial dynamics and cellular adaptation to oxidative stress.165–167 Moreover, ROS can trigger structural changes in SRC and the phosphatase SPH2, thereby decreasing tyrosine phosphorylation at the active sites of most mitochondrial enzymes.166,168 Thus, the overproduction of ROS can induce mitochondrial DNA damage and the formation of peroxynitrite, which impairs endothelial nitric oxide (NO) synthase and NO-mediated dilation.166,169 SRC Kinases regulate cellular responses to oxygen deprivation, reperfusion injury, and are a key factor in neuronal apoptosis after ischemia.75,166 During ischemia, SRC activation contributes to endothelial dysfunction, inflammation, and increased vascular permeability.170 Furthermore, SRC signaling is involved in post-ischemic reperfusion injury by modulating inflammatory pathways and exacerbating oxidative stress.171,172 A study used mouse models of middle cerebral artery occlusion or reperfusion (MCAO/R)-induced ischemic stroke and oxygen-glucose deprivation and reoxygenation (OGD/R)-simulated in vitro ischemia in neurons to understand the relationship between interleukin-17A (IL-17A) and the inflammatory pathway.173,174 IL-17A binds to its receptor complex (IL-17RA/RC), leading to the recruitment and phosphorylation of the kinase. Activated SRC then interacts with phospholipase C gamma (PLCγ), which triggers intracellular calcium release through inositol triphosphate receptors (IP3R) (Fig. 6). This Ca²⁺ influx activates calpain-2, which in turn, leads to caspase-12-mediated apoptosis.173 The study also demonstrates that inhibiting either PLCγ or calpain can suppress IL-17A-induced apoptosis, highlighting the PLCγ-calpain pathway as a potential therapeutic target for ischemic stroke. Additionally, germline gain-of-function mutations in ABL1 cause autosomal-dominant developmental syndromes characterized by congenital heart defects, skeletal malformations, and aortic root enlargement, likely through dysregulation of the ERK/MAPK and Rho GTPase pathways during tissue morphogenesis.175

Hypertension

Hypertension is a chronic condition characterized by persistently elevated arterial pressure, which increases the risk of heart attack, stroke, kidney disease, and other complications. It is closely linked to the dysregulation of NRTKs, particularly the SFKs and JAK pathways, which are essential for cell proliferation, apoptosis, and immune regulation.176–178 The underlying pathogenesis of hypertension is not fully controlled under the current therapy options.178,179 Under normal conditions, endothelial nitric oxide synthase (eNOS) produces nitric oxide (NO) to promote vasodilation, resulting in a decrease in blood pressure.177,178,180 C-terminal SRC Kinase (CSK) is part of the SFK family. However, overactivation of SFKs leads to oxidative stress via the NADPH oxidase (NOX) pathway, generating reactive oxygen species (ROS) that reduce NO bioavailability.76,177,181 Moreover, JAK/STAT signaling, often activated by angiotensin II through the angiotensin type 1 receptor (AT1R), promotes vascular smooth muscle cell (VSMC) proliferation and inflammation, increasing vascular resistance.177,182,183 JAK2 and STAT3 are associated with pulmonary artery remodeling, inflammation, endothelial dysfunction, and smooth muscle proliferation.177,178,183,184 These biochemical changes contribute to endothelial dysfunction and arterial stiffness, and are key hallmarks of hypertension

Atherosclerosis

Atherosclerosis is a chronic progressive inflammatory disease characterized by the accumulation of lipid plaques on the inner walls of arteries, increased apoptosis of vascular smooth muscle cells (VSMCs), and vascular wall remodeling.166,185 Atherosclerosis begins when oxidized low-density lipoprotein (oxLDL) activates toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4) and the SRC pathway in endothelial cells, resulting in nuclear factor kappa B (NF-κB) activation and the upregulation of adhesion molecules like ICAM-1 and VCAM-1.185,186 Such pathological conditions promote monocyte adhesion and their differentiation into macrophages, which engulf oxLDL to form foam cells. Chronic inflammation and lipid accumulation result from multiple factors acting simultaneously, such as NRTKs—SRC, FAK, and JAK/STAT. SRC contributes to atherosclerosis by generating excessive ROS, leading to the oxidation of cellular components, including mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA), thereby causing cellular damage. Studies on apo-E-deficient mice lacking the antioxidant enzyme SOD-2 have shown that increased ROS levels damage mitochondrial DNA, accelerating atherosclerosis and VSMCs.166,187 FAK’s involvement in integrin signaling contributes to VSMCs’ migration and proliferation, which are important factors in the process of plaque development, vascular thickening, and plaque instability.185,186 As stated in Fig. 5, JAK interacts with cytokine receptors, leading to the phosphorylation of STAT. JAK signaling further exacerbates inflammation by activating downstream STAT signaling pathways that increase the recruitment and survival of immune cells. It stimulates macrophage activation and the secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-6 and TNF-α.170 Currently, FAK inhibition therapies are being studied as a new therapeutic approach for atherosclerosis.188 However, there are JAK inhibitors like Tofacitinib that have been shown to be effective in chronic inflammatory disorders, which could benefit atherosclerosis.98,173,189

Congestive heart failure

CHF is a complex syndrome characterized by inefficient myocardial output, which leads to compromised blood supply to the body.190 It involves defective cardiac remodeling driven by FAK, JAK/STAT, and SRC signaling. FAK plays an important role in promoting cell attachment and facilitating signaling by cell-surface receptors.186,191 Furthermore, FAK stimulates activation of pro-survival kinases, such as ERK and AKT, whereas FAK inactivation can lead to apoptosis. FAK-Beta3 integrins are associated with pressure-overloaded hypertrophic hearts.186,191 Increased stress activates integrins and FAK in cardiomyocytes, leading to an increase in gene expression through downstream activation of mitogen-activated protein kinases (MAPKs).182,186 Simultaneously, JAK/STAT signaling is triggered by elevated levels of cytokines like IL-6, which promotes cardiac fibrosis by activating fibroblasts and stimulating collagen deposition via TGF-β/Smad signaling.189,192 JAK is also associated with pressure overload-induced cardiac hypertrophy, ischemic preconditioning, and reperfusion-induced cardiac dysfunction, which, in turn, contribute to mitochondrial dysfunction and ROS production by impairing cardiomyocyte contractility.167,181 Further exploration of these kinases may open different routes for novel therapies.

SHP2, a non-receptor PTP, plays a dual role in cardiovascular signaling by positively regulating growth pathways such as RAS/MAPK and PI3K/AKT and by modulating NRTKs including JAK2 and SFKs. In the heart, hyperactive SHP2 signaling contributes to hypertrophy and congenital cardiomyopathies, while in macrophages, it supports efferocytosis and anti-inflammatory functions. Dysregulation of SHP2 activity in vascular cells has been linked to the progression of atherosclerosis and altered vascular remodeling.193–195 Similarly, PTP1B upregulation is associated with impaired endothelial function and enhanced hypertrophic signaling.196 Inhibiting PTP1B has been shown to have beneficial effects in experimental models of myocardial infarction and heart failure, restoring insulin sensitivity and reducing maladaptive cardiac remodeling.197 PTPN22 is primarily involved in immune cell signaling, where it interacts with SRC-family kinases, such as LCK, and downregulates TCR-mediated pathways. In heart failure, PTPN22 expression is elevated in CD4+ T cells, leading to decreased in regulatory T cell populations and exacerbation of inflammation.162 This contributes to immune dysregulation and disease progression in CHF and potentially in atherosclerosis. CD45 and SHP1 are hematopoietic-specific PTPs that fine-tune immune cell activation by regulating NRTKs, including LCK, FYN, and SYK. CD45 promotes activation by dephosphorylating inhibitory tyrosine on SRC-family kinases, while SHP1 dampens excessive cytokine signaling. In cardiovascular inflammation, particularly in autoimmune-linked atherosclerosis, SHP1 loss leads to the hyperactivation of inflammatory cascades, worsening vascular damage.198 SOCS1 and SOCS3 are key feedback inhibitors of the JAK–STAT pathway, directly binding to and inhibiting NRTKs, such as JAK1, JAK2, and JAK3. In atherosclerosis and heart failure, SOCS3 deficiency leads to unchecked JAK/STAT activity, thereby exacerbating hypertrophic responses and inflammation. Conversely, SOCS1 can modulate oxidative stress and cytokine signaling in vascular tissues. Their expression is dynamically regulated in myocardial infarction, hypertension, and inflammatory heart disease, making them promising modulators of NRTK-driven pathology.199

Metabolic disorders

NRTKs, due to their crucial role in insulin signaling, glucose metabolism, lipid homeostasis, and metabolic regulation, are of keen interest when understanding diseases, including metabolic disorders. In high-prevalence metabolic diseases, specifically type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2D), obesity, and hyperlipidemia, many NRTKs actively contribute to the signaling process and affect critical physiological processes.200–202 In obesity and T2D, the SRC/PI3K/Akt pathway is often damaged, leading to decreased insulin sensitivity. This reduces insulin-stimulated glucose uptake, GLUT4 translocation, and impairs insulin’s ability to suppress hepatic glucose production.203 Decreased IRS1 tyrosine phosphorylation, observed in insulin-resistant skeletal muscles, is linked to defective insulin receptor kinase (IRK) activity. Reduced expression or increased serine phosphorylation of IRS proteins diminishes their binding to PI3K, down-regulating PI3K activation. Similarly, impaired Akt Ser473 phosphorylation is detected in both muscle and liver insulin resistance.80,203 The PI3K/Akt pathway is damaged in various tissues, leading to obesity and type 2 diabetes due to its role in insulin resistance. In turn, insulin resistance further exacerbates the PI3K/Akt pathway, forming a vicious circle.203 Fig. 7 illustrates the crosstalk between different NRTKs and their roles in inflammation, adipogenesis, and insulin resistance, which are hallmarks of metabolic diseases. NRTKs can promote different cell physiological and biochemical mechanisms, leading to disease manifestation. SFKs like SRC modulate the JAK-STAT, BMX, SREBP1, and FAK pathways, influencing adipogenesis, fatty acid oxidation, and lipid accumulation. All of these contribute to inflammation and, as a result, influence other pathways, such as the glucose uptake pathway. As previously mentioned, IRS-PI3K pathway is activated by the insulin receptor, which triggers mTOR and PDK1, activating AKT. The AS160-GLUT4 activation is then facilitated by AKT, which canonically leads to glucose uptake, often dysregulated in metabolic diseases. The integrin/mechanical receptor triggers FAK signaling, which is regulated by SFKs. The FAK signaling promotes the accumulation of triglyceride, thereby leading to the inflammation via the SREBP1 and FAK, along with the SFK-CSK regulated JAK-STAT pathway, which leads to cytokine transcription. These cytokines are key contributors to inflammation, which, in turn, affects the insulin pathway. In addition, the BMX is modulated by the SFKs and contributes to inflammation.115 On the other hand, inflammation also results in lipid accumulation, while fatty acid oxidation prevents this. However, AMPK, which regulates this fatty acid oxidation, is modulated by CSK, and ABL-regulated mitochondrial ROS has inhibitory action on the AMPK, which suggests that ROS could, in fact, accelerate the lipid accumulation.204,205

Hyperlipidemia

Hyperlipidemia is a condition characterized by elevated levels of cholesterol (particularly LDL) and/or triglycerides in the blood, often without symptoms, but significantly increasing the risk of atherosclerosis, heart attack, stroke, and other cardiovascular complications. It is closely associated with changes in lipid metabolism, which involve several kinases that regulate lipid signaling, inflammation, and cholesterol metabolism. SFKs are crucial for lipid modifications and membrane cholesterol levels, which anchor them to cell membranes for proper function. Impaired lipid modification leads to the delocalization of SRC from membranes to the cytoplasm and nucleus.206 Moreover, SRC’s localization and activation are influenced by membrane cholesterol levels during cell detachment.207 Modified low-density lipoprotein can activate SRC via interactions with CD36, promoting LDL absorption and proinflammatory cytokine production.208 Moreover, lipid incorporation inhibits SRC-dependent assembly of fibronectin and extracellular matrix interactions.209 Furthermore, SRC is also implicated in platelet hyperreactivity related to hyperlipidemia, mediated through TLR2.210 SFKs such as FYN, which impact fatty acid uptake and oxidation in peripheral tissues. FYN negatively modulates AMP kinase activity in adipose tissue and skeletal muscle, as evidenced by FYN null mice that show increased fatty acid uptake and oxidation, improving glucose tolerance and insulin sensitivity. These mice also exhibit reduced adipose mass, smaller adipocytes, and lower lipid accumulation in liver and muscle tissues.79,211 SFKs interact with membrane lipids, determining their localization and signaling capabilities, which affect lipid metabolism pathways.212 SRC regulates enzymes involved in fatty acid synthesis by phosphorylating ACSS2, stimulating acetate-derived acetyl-CoA production, and inhibiting ACLY to divert citrate to NADPH production for fatty acid synthesis.84

In the case of ABL kinases, TKIs, such as those targeting BCR-ABL1 in CML, have been linked to altered lipid metabolism. Nilotinib, a second-generation TKI, acts in an ATP-competitive manner to the binding site of inactive BCR-ABL, worsening glycometabolic profiles and increasing LDL cholesterol levels compared to other TKIs.213 Inhibition of ABL kinases, part of the PI3K/Akt/mTOR pathway, impacts lipid metabolism by regulating Sterol Regulatory Element-binding Protein-1c (SREBP-1c), a transcription factor involved in lipid synthesis. Studies on BCR-ABL in cancer cells show that Imatinib, another ATP-competitive inhibitor of ABL kinase, reduces phosphocholine and phosphatidylcholine levels, prompting compensatory fatty acid oxidation through upregulation of CPT1C. This leads to reduced plasma cholesterol levels and atherosclerosis in animal models, suggesting that ABL kinase activity promotes cholesterol synthesis or uptake. Imatinib treatment also enhances LDL receptor expression, aiding LDL cholesterol clearance, and increases polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs) levels, indicating that ABL activity regulates PUFA metabolism.214 Similarly, the JAK-STAT signaling pathway also influences lipid metabolism, especially in adipocytes, while STAT3 promotes lipolysis and inhibits adipogenesis in mature adipocytes.215 JAK2, essential for lipid storage in adipose tissue, is implicated in high-fat diet-induced obesity and diabetes.18 JAK inhibition promotes the browning of white adipose tissue by increasing uncoupling proteins, which may mitigate obesity.216 However, the increase in cholesterol levels requires careful monitoring when using JAK inhibitors for metabolic disorders. FAK also plays a role in hyperlipidemia by regulating lipid metabolism, insulin sensitivity, and adipocyte survival. FAK-deficient mice show higher fasting serum free fatty acids and cholesterol levels and exhibit insulin resistance, promoting lipid accumulation.186 Disruption of FAK increases inflammation in adipose tissue, contributing to metabolic disturbances.217 Additionally, FAK regulates kidney function in hypercholesterolemia, further linking it to lipid metabolism.218

TEC kinases, though less studied in hyperlipidemia, are involved in inflammatory signaling pathways and regulating proteins associated with lipid membranes, suggesting a role in lipid metabolism.9,219 TECs’ influence on T-lymphocyte function may indirectly affect lipid metabolism via immune system modulation. FES, on the other hand, has been shown to have a protective function against hyperlipidemia by regulating inflammation and atherosclerosis. FES downregulates TLR4 signaling and NFκB activation, reducing chronic inflammation, a major contributor to hyperlipidemia.220 In a study, FES knockout mice showed larger atherosclerotic plaques, emphasizing FES’s protective role in inflammation and indicating its importance in lipid homeostasis.221,222 C-terminal SRC kinase (CSK), though not directly linked to hyperlipidemia, might influence lipid metabolism by regulating SFKs and immune responses. CSK knockout mice exhibit hypertension, which is often associated with hyperlipidemia, and may indirectly affect lipid metabolism through its impact on blood pressure and inflammation.223 Finally, SYK plays a significant role in hyperlipidemia by mediating macrophage responses to oxidized LDL, promoting foam cell formation, increasing lipid accumulation and inflammation, and thus contributing to conditions like atherosclerosis. SYK inhibitors are being explored for their potential to reduce lipid accumulation and inflammation in hyperlipidemia.224

Type 2 diabetes mellitus

Type 2 Diabetes Mellitus (T2D) is a metabolic disorder characterized by insulin resistance, and as a result, often by high blood glucose levels. SRC, also activated by insulin, regulates key processes in T2D by enhancing protein kinase C delta (PKCδ) activity, which is important for glucose uptake and insulin secretion. It also maintains glucokinase activity in pancreatic β-cells, essential for glucose-induced insulin secretion, and facilitates insulin-stimulated glucose uptake. Inhibition of SRC impairs insulin signaling, reducing glucose uptake and disrupting β-cell function under high glucose conditions.81,225 On the other hand, ABL negatively regulates insulin gene expression in pancreatic β-cells. It suppresses insulin transcription by reducing NKx2.2 levels, a key transcription factor. ABL also modulates endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress responses, which are crucial in β-cell survival under diabetogenic conditions. Under high glucose, ABL acts as a feedback mechanism, preventing excessive insulin production by modulating regulators like PDX-1 and NeuroD1.226,227 JAK2 impairs insulin signaling by reducing AKT phosphorylation, which is key for glucose uptake. During insulin-resistant states, JAK2 selectively depresses AKT signaling, worsening insulin resistance, while also influencing β-cell proliferation and survival, thereby affecting insulin production. Furthermore, its activation contributes to diabetic complications, such as nephropathy, promoting glomerular mesangial cell proliferation.98,228 FAK is crucial for insulin sensitivity and glucose homeostasis, specifically in adipocytes, where it regulates glucose uptake by facilitating GLUT-4 translocation. Studies also showed that FAK knockout in β-cells leads to glucose intolerance and reduced insulin secretion. FAK inhibitors have shown promise in improving hyperglycemia and regenerating β-cell mass in diabetic models, making FAK a potential therapeutic target for T2D.217,229

TEC mediates pro-inflammatory cytokine production and regulates T cell differentiation, exacerbating insulin resistance in T2D and obesity. It also affects B cell activation and the balance between pro- and anti-inflammatory signals in adipose tissue, influencing T2D pathogenesis. Targeting TEC could provide a novel approach to modulating insulin and glucose levels in T2D patients.230,231 C-terminal SRC kinase (CSK) negatively regulates SFKs by phosphorylating their C-terminal tyrosine. CSK interacts with the insulin receptor and the IGF-1 signaling, leading to regulating SFK-mediated glucose uptake and insulin secretion, thereby potentially affecting adipocyte differentiation and insulin sensitivity.232–234 ACK is linked to signaling pathways like Cdc42, which is crucial for insulin secretion and glucose uptake, and ACK’s influence on these processes might affect glucose metabolism in T2D. It also interacts with pathways like PI3K/AKT and MAPK, which are critical for insulin sensitivity and obesity.235,236 SYK activation leads to chronic inflammation, a hallmark of T2D, and disrupts insulin signaling by affecting the RhoA and JNK pathways. SYK’s involvement in diabetic complications, such as retinopathy, highlights its potential as a therapeutic target. Inhibition of SYK improves insulin sensitivity and reduces inflammation, presenting a promising avenue for T2D treatment.237–239

Obesity

Obesity is a multifactorial condition influenced by genetic, environmental, and metabolic factors, with various signaling pathways playing imperative roles in its pathogenesis. It is defined by excess body fat (BMI ≥ 30), often accompanied by fatigue, joint pain, and sleep problems, and significantly increases the risk of diabetes, cardiovascular disease, cancer, and reduced quality of life. The SRC/PI3K/Akt pathway, activated in obesity by altered factors such as insulin, leptin, and insulin-like growth factor, has been implicated in the prothrombotic state observed in obesity.203,240 Phosphoproteomic analysis of platelets in severe obesity revealed alterations in SFK-related signaling pathways, contributing to this prothrombotic state. The SRC/PI3K/Akt pathway also plays a key role in the production of inflammatory mediators like IL-17, which is associated with obesity-related inflammation. ABL, highly expressed in subcutaneous fat from obese humans and mice fed a high-fat diet (HFD), has been shown to influence obesity and insulin sensitivity. Inhibition of ABL mitigates diet-induced obesity by improving insulin sensitivity, particularly in subcutaneous fat. Conditional knockout of ABL or treatment with the ABL inhibitor Nilotinib reduces fat mass and body weight gain in HFD-fed mice, highlighting its role in metabolic homeostasis.80 Additionally, ABL promotes adipogenesis by phosphorylating and stabilizing PPARγ, a master regulator of adipocyte differentiation, which suggests its involvement in obesity-related pathological states.241 JAK signaling plays a major role in regulating adipogenesis, energy balance, and inflammation. The JAK-STAT pathway regulates adipocyte differentiation, and JAK2 activation by leptin receptors is essential for energy homeostasis and food intake, influencing body weight.216 JAK inhibition has been linked to increased expression of uncoupling proteins, such as UCP-1, which enhance energy expenditure. JAK3 plays a role in maintaining intestinal barrier function, and its deficiency exacerbates inflammation and obesity.242 However, chronic inflammation, a hallmark of obesity, can be worsened by JAK3 deficiency, leading to insulin resistance and metabolic syndrome. Additionally, it was also observed that in wild-type (WT) mice or intestinal epithelial cells, JAK3 performs a key anti-inflammatory function by activating the PI3K–Akt pathway, which suppresses TLR4–NFκB pro-inflammatory signaling. In JAK3-deficient mice (JAK3-KO), this regulatory pathway is disrupted, leading to increased inflammation, enhanced TLR4 expression, and metabolic dysfunction (e.g., hyperglycemia, obesity JAK inhibitors like Tofacitinib show promise in treating metabolic disorders but require careful monitoring in obesity-related contexts.243

FAK plays an important role in regulating adipocyte survival, expansion, and insulin sensitivity. FAK expression is significantly increased in adipose tissue from insulin-resistant obese mice and humans, suggesting its upregulation may be a compensatory mechanism in response to adipose tissue expansion. FAK-deficient mice show reduced weight gain and fat pad weight on a high-fat diet, underscoring FAK’s role in adipose tissue homeostasis and preventing excessive weight gain during obesity.217 TEC has been implicated in inflammation and immune responses, both central to obesity pathophysiology by regulating Th17 cell differentiation, which is linked to obesity-related inflammation. In TEC-deficient mice, there is increased Th17 differentiation and stronger inflammatory responses, contributing to metabolic disturbances.9 FES is involved in regulating inflammation and metabolic function, which are often dysregulated in obesity, it’s regulation of cytokine signaling pathways, particularly in adipose tissue, suggests it may help control the chronic inflammation characteristic of obesity. Dysregulated cytokine production and macrophage infiltration in adipose tissue contribute to insulin resistance and other metabolic disturbances.244,245 CSK, by regulating SFKs, plays a role in signaling pathways such as JNK and PI3K-AKT-mTOR, which are critical in obesity and insulin resistance.246,247 CSK’s regulation of immune cell signaling and cell adhesion may influence adipose tissue inflammation and thereby affect the manifestation of obesity. SYK is crucial for brown adipose tissue (BAT) metabolism and adipocyte function. Moreover, it is upregulated during brown adipocyte differentiation and is activated by β-adrenergic stimulation, promoting thermogenesis and energy expenditure. Inhibition of SYK reduces BAT mass and thermogenesis, highlighting its central role in regulating energy expenditure and linking it to obesity-related metabolic processes.83

Protein tyrosine phosphatases (PTPs) and suppressors of cytokine signaling (SOCS) proteins play a critical role in modulating insulin, leptin, and cytokine signaling pathways that govern metabolic homeostasis. PTP1B, a key negative regulator of insulin and leptin receptors, is frequently upregulated in metabolic tissues during obesity and insulin resistance. Its inhibition improves insulin sensitivity, reduces adiposity, and lowers hepatic triglyceride accumulation in preclinical models. Similarly, TCPTP (PTPN2) suppresses insulin and cytokine signaling, contributing to hepatic gluconeogenesis and lipid dysregulation when overexpressed in inflamed tissues.248,249 SHP2 (PTPN11) supports insulin signaling in peripheral tissues but can promote leptin resistance and hypothalamic dysregulation when aberrantly activated.250 Elevated SOCS1 and SOCS3 levels impair insulin and leptin signaling, promoting insulin resistance, leptin resistance, and metabolic dysfunction through enhanced gluconeogenesis and lipogenesis. Genetic and experimental models demonstrate that reducing SOCS1/3 activity through tissue-specific deletion or antisense inhibition enhances insulin sensitivity, improves glucose tolerance, and protects against obesity-related metabolic diseases.251–253 PTPs and SOCS proteins serve as key molecular nodes at the intersection of metabolic and inflammatory signaling pathways. Their dysregulation disrupts feedback control mechanisms, driving sustained insulin resistance, unresolved inflammation, and aberrant lipid metabolism hallmarks that exacerbate the progression and complexity of metabolic diseases such as obesity and type 2 diabetes. Despite the considerable attention paid to the role of NRTKs in metabolic disorders, roles of lesser-studied kinases, such as TEC, FES, and ACK, as well as the tissue- and time-specific dynamics of NRTK, PTP, and SOCS signaling during disease progression, remain to be elucidated. Investigations need to be made into how these kinases interact or compensate for each other under metabolic stress. Addressing such gaps are of essential for harnessing the full therapeutic potential of NRTKs in metabolic disorders such as type 2 diabetes, obesity, and hyperlipidemia.

Neurodegenerative diseases

Homeostasis and neurons degeneration are core processes in understanding neurodegenerative diseases. NRTKs play a role in protein aggregation, neuroinflammation, and impaired synaptic function, which contribute to diseases such as Alzheimer’s disease (AD), Parkinson’s disease (PD), and Huntington’s disease (HD).254–256 Moreover, the role of NRTKs in neurological diseases can also correlate with autoimmune disease and cardiovascular pathologies, providing broader perspectives.257

Alzheimer’s disease

AD is the most common cause of dementia, characterized by progressive memory loss, cognitive decline, and hallmark brain changes such as amyloid plaques and neurofibrillary tangles. NRTKs have been explicitly investigated in the context of neurodegeneration, and, more recently, are emerging as targets for understanding and targeting neurodegenerative diseases.258 Perhaps the most prominent NRTK in neurodegeneration is ABL. It was observed that ABL was upregulated in post-mortem AD and PD brains, along with elevated levels of RTKs, such as Discoidin Domain Receptors (DDR1/2). Knockdown of these RTKs via shRNA has significantly reduced neurotoxic proteins, including alpha-synuclein, beta-amyloid, and tau.13 Additionally, since ABL is ubiquitously expressed, it is involved in neurogenesis, neurite outgrowth, and neuronal plasticity, and has tightly regulated kinase activity. In AD, ABL co-localizes with plaques, tangles, and granulovacuolar degeneration bodies, as well as phosphorylates tau.259 It is also involved in cell signaling that regulates neuronal death response to a hallmark of AD, amyloid-β-peptide (Aβ) fibrils. Aβ fibrils induced elevated ABL activity in rat hippocampal neurons, and increased levels of nuclear p73 protein and the p73-ABL complex. Induction of neuronal cell death by Aβ fibrils was prevented through inhibition of ABL via Imatinib and RNAi.12 Another pathophysiologic hallmark of AD is microglial activation, generally associated with Aβ peptides. Research has shown that NRTKs such as SRC are involved in Aβ fibrils activated microglial stimulation. Targeting this signaling response via Dasatinib and inhibiting SRC/ABL resulted in attenuated microgliosis and reduced TNFα levels. It reduced active SRC levels in the brains of AD transgenic mice while also improving cognitive performance.260 ABL activation was examined in two lines of transgenic mice, one expressing constitutively active ABL (ABLPP/tTA mice) and the second one expressing ABL’s sister tyrosine kinase ARG (ArgPP/tTA mice), resulting in ABL expression in adult mouse forebrain neurons, which led to severe neurodegeneration.261 Microgliosis and astrocytosis were accompanied by neuronal loss, with no ABL expression found in glial cells. Despite similar protein expression and kinase activity levels between the two lines, ArgPP/tTA mice did not demonstrate neuronal loss or gliosis. Coupled with evidence of ABL activation in human AD brains, this adds to the likelihood that aberrant ABL plays a role in neurodegenerative disease.259

Parkinson’s disease

PD is a chronic neurodegenerative movement disorder caused by the degeneration of dopamine-producing neurons, leading to tremors, rigidity, bradykinesia, and postural instability. Mutations in the E3 ubiquitin ligase parkin are the most common cause of autosomal-recessive PD, and are thought to disrupt parkin activity through direct loss of function, reduced parkin solubility, or impaired substrate degradation.96,262 Parkin is phosphorylated by ABL at tyrosine-143, occurring after oxidative and dopamine stress both in vitro and in vivo, resulting in significant loss of the E3 ubiquitin ligase activity of parkin, as well as accumulation of neurotoxic parkin substrates AIMP2 and FBP-1. In this context, Imatinib was used to inhibit ABL, preventing thereby parkin phosphorylation and preserving its protective function.262 Other drugs that target NRTKs and are approved for the treatment of leukemias, such as Nilotinib, are also being actively explored for neurodegenerative therapeutics. Nilotinib most strongly inhibits ABL and DDR1/2, with evidence showing it reduces alpha-synuclein in the brain and attenuates inflammation in PD models.263 Exploring different uses of current FDA-approved drugs can open new avenues of treating neurodegeneration.

When PP2A, a major tau phosphatase, is downregulated, it promotes tau hyperphosphorylation, generating neurofibrillary tangles, which results in neurodegeneration.264,265 In cancers, it was noted that NRTKs, such as SRC, inhibit PP2A, indicating a potential association between PP2A and NRTKs.266 However, protein tyrosine phosphatase 1B (PTP1B) is increasingly recognized as a central modulator of neurodegeneration in both AD and PD. In AD, PTP1B is overexpressed, disrupting multiple neuronal signaling pathways essential for learning and memory. It acts as a negative regulator of insulin, leptin, and BDNF signaling by dephosphorylating their receptors, InsR, LepR, and TrkB, thereby impairing synaptic plasticity and cognition.267,268 Moreover, PTP1B promotes neuroinflammation in microglia via the SRC/NFκB axis, exacerbating neuronal injury.268 Inhibiting PTP1B in AD models enhances BDNF signaling, improves memory, and reduces neuronal loss, indicating its therapeutic potential.267,268 In PD, PTP1B drives neuroinflammation and dopaminergic neurodegeneration; its inhibition (e.g., via suramin) restores neurotrophic signaling, mitigates oxidative and ER stress, and reduces neuronal apoptosis. In contrast, PTP1C (SHP-1), though less extensively studied, is implicated in AD via its interaction with microglial CD33. This interaction, modulated by AD-risk variants, alters immune signaling and contributes to disease progression.269–271 SHP-1 also regulates cell survival, oxidative stress, and inflammatory cascades through PI3K/Akt and JAK/STAT pathways. While its role in PD remains less defined, SHP-1’s influence on stress and survival signaling warrants further exploration.272,273 Additionally, hyperphosphorylation of Tau and α-synuclein occurs on their tyrosine residues, but no attention has yet been paid to possible associations between PTP1B and PTP1C and these proteins in these diseases.

SOCS proteins, particularly SOCS3, primarily modulate cytokine-driven inflammation in neurodegenerative diseases via the JAK-STAT pathway. In AD, SOCS3 is upregulated in amyloid-rich, inflamed regions and suppresses IL-6/STAT3 signaling, potentially contributing to central insulin resistance and impaired neurotrophic support.274–277 SOCS proteins act as negative regulators of cytokine signaling, limiting JAK/STAT and other inflammatory pathways in the brain. All SOCS family members (SOCS1-7 and CIS) are constitutively expressed in both non-demented and AD brains, with SOCS1, SOCS2, SOCS3, and CIS mRNA upregulated in microglia exposed to amyloid-beta and inflammatory stimuli.278 In PD, SOCS3 limits neuroinflammation by inhibiting IL-6/STAT3 signaling in glial cells, thereby reducing pro-inflammatory cytokine expression and protecting dopaminergic neurons. Targeting SOCS3-mediated feedback is emerging as a promising therapeutic strategy to control inflammation in AD and PD.277,279

PYK2 is a calcium-dependent NRTK that is highly expressed in the hippocampus. It interacts directly with post-synaptic density (PSD) proteins, including PSD95. Previous research suggested a role in synaptic plasticity; its gene, PTK2B, is a susceptibility locus for AD. The protein is one of the few NRTK targets of HD. PYK2 protein levels, measured in post-partum hippocampal samples from human patients, were reduced to 64 ± 8% of controls in those with intermediate or late HD. PYK2 also decreased in the hippocampus of R6/1 mice, an HD transgenic mouse model, and its deletion impaired hippocampus-dependent memory and long-term potentiation. The findings from this study demonstrated the significance of PYK2 for hippocampal function and physiology, and imply potential reversibility of PYK2 in its contribution to hippocampal dysfunction in an HD model.258 PYK2 also decreased in the striatum of HD mice and HD patients, although to a lesser effect than the noticeable alterations observed from Giralt et al.254 Although there is much to explore when it comes to the role of NRTKs in neurodegeneration, these examples show their emerging role and significance when it comes to these pathologies. In the case of less common diseases such as HD, there may be more NRTKs relevant to the pathogenesis, and thus they merit attention as therapeutic targets for research exploration.

Autoimmune diseases

Systemic lupus erythematosus

Diseases like Systemic Lupus Erythematosus (SLE), a chronic autoimmune rheumatic disease, are directly linked to BTK’s influence on B-cell antigen receptor (BCR) activation.7,280 This disease is characterized by the immune system attacking healthy tissues throughout the body, leading to inflammation and damage in multiple organs, with symptoms that often fluctuate between flares and periods of remission. The BTK pathway mediates intracellular signal transduction and can be activated by several immunoreceptors, making it a popular target for therapeutic immunotherapy. The SYK-BTK pathway is activated by BCR engagement, which leads to the enzymatic activation of the SRC kinases LYN and FYN that in turn phosphorylate BTK (Fig. 6).63,79,281,282 SYK also phosphorylates BLNK and, once paired with BTK, induces the phosphorylation of PLC-γ2, which starts PLC-γ2-dependent hydrolysis of diacylglycerol (DAG) and inositol 1, 4, 5-trisphosphate (IP3), leading to the activation of other proteins that contribute to cellular transcription and the generation of calcium (Ca2+) mobilization. DAG also activates the CARD11-BCL10-MALT1 complex, which in turn leads to inflammatory cytokine activity and transcription.283–285 The disruption of this pathway can lead to several disorders that still lack a cure, and because of its complexity, they can be present in unique ways. SLE can be manifested in the skin, joints, kidneys, heart, lungs, nervous system, and hematopoietic system. The two genes associated with SLE code for NRTK tyrosine kinase non-receptor 2/activated Cdc42 kinase 1 (TNK2/ACK1) and protein tyrosine kinase 6/breast tumor kinase (PTK6/BRK). Mutations in NRTKs can also lead to the development of SLE. Compound heterozygous mutations in ACK1 (A156T and L161Q) and BRL (G257A and G321R) found in two different families with SLE and lead to a significant loss of kinase activity. ACK1 and BRL deficiencies can impair efferocytosis, which negatively impacts the removal of apoptotic cells and the prevention of inflammation by directly disrupting the MERTK-mediated anti-inflammatory response, diminishing the activation response of AKT, STAT3, and RAC1.7

Rheumatoid Arthritis (RA) is characterized by the inflammation of synovial joints, which is the result of the body erroneously attacking the synovial membrane.286–289 This triggers an increase in pro-inflammatory responses of cytokines such as interferon-gamma (IFN-γ), tumor necrosis factor (TNF)-α, interleukin-1 (IL-1), and interleukin-6 (IL-6).290,291 IL-6 is the prototypic proinflammatory cytokine that signals through the gp130 receptor subunit and promotes the production of TNF and IL-1, and the differentiation of T-helper cells.291 Two critical pathways influence RA pathogenesis: the JAK/STAT pathway and Bruton’s Tyrosine Kinase (BTK) signaling.212,284,290,292,293 The JAK/STAT pathway coordinates cytokine signaling, particularly through type I and type II cytokines, including interleukins, interferons, colony-stimulating factors, hormones, and growth factors.291 Cytokine receptors activate JAK kinases, which in turn phosphorylate and activate STAT proteins, and once phosphorylated, STATs translocate into the nucleus, acting as transcription factors to regulate genes associated with inflammation and immune responses.290,291 and BCR signaling wherein BTK plays a crucial role in B-cell activation, influencing autoantibody production, cytokine release, and immune complex formation, thereby exacerbating RA inflammation.284,294 Increased BTK activity leads to B-cell dysregulation and enhanced inflammatory responses, making it a promising therapeutic target.295,296 In Fig. 8, the JAK, SRC, SYK, and BTK pathways show how they interact to successfully contribute to cell proliferation and transcription.22,297 Disrupting these pathways not only leads to the RA pathophysiology described above but also affects the cellular environment, specifically through BTK disruption.297–299

Multiple sclerosis

Multiple sclerosis (MS) is a chronic autoimmune disease, where the myelin sheath, which insulates nerve fibers, is attacked and leads to impairment of nerve signal transmission and neurological dysfunction such as vision problems, depression, muscle weakness, and neurogenic bladder.300,301 This process is mediated by autoreactive T cells, B cells, and macrophages that infiltrate the CNS, releasing pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-17, interferon-gamma (IFN-γ), and tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α).107,301 As stated in Fig. 8, JAK SRC kinases, SYK, and TEC contribute to T cell and B cell signaling, cell proliferation, adhesion, and migration, as well as transcription, and if disrupted or mutated, they can lead to unfavorable results. Over time, this inflammatory response causes demyelination, axonal damage, and neuronal loss, contributing to the progressive disability seen in people with MS.256,301–303 The JAK/STAT pathway is important for cytokine-mediated immune responses, as it contributes to the activation and differentiation of pathogenic T cells, gene expression of pro-inflammatory genes, and can target different mutations in MS. Dysregulation of these pathways exacerbates neuroinflammation and promotes demyelination.260,262,266,304 SRC-family kinases (SFKs) influence cell adhesion, invasion, interactions, and immune cell migration across the blood-brain barrier, further amplifying CNS inflammation.107,228,305 Additionally, the BTK pathway activates B cells and myeloid cells, which trigger the inflammatory process of MS. Moreover, it promotes antigen presentation and cytokine production, and indirectly activates the JAK/STAT pathway.304,306,307 TYK2 is being investigated for its ability to selectively block IL-12 and IL-23 signaling, which are critical in the differentiation of pro-inflammatory T cells.308 By stopping immune activation, JAK inhibitors have the potential to limit MS relapses and slow disease progression. BTK inhibitors can help modulate immune responses, reduce inflammation, and halt damage to the myelin, microglia, and neurons.228

Type 1 diabetes

Type 1 diabetes (T1D) is an autoimmune disorder in which the immune system attacks and destroys insulin-producing beta cells in the pancreas, leading to chronic hyperglycemia.309,310 This is caused by autoreactive T cells, particularly CD4⁺ and CD8⁺ T cells, which recognize beta-cell antigens as harmful and trigger an inflammatory response. Few NRTKs share a role in the autoimmune response that drives T1D, such as JAKs and Lymphocyte-specific protein tyrosine kinases (LCKs).61,107 The JAK/STAT pathway is activated by various cytokines involved in T-cell differentiation, immune cell activation, and most importantly, pancreatic inflammation.108,311 Adipocytes produce hormones that engage the JAK/STAT signaling pathway, and the dysregulation of JAK signaling amplifies autoreactive immune responses, exacerbating beta-cell destruction.311,312 LCK, a member of the SRC family, plays a crucial role in T-cell development and function. The abnormal increased response to T cell signaling is usually seen in T1D.310,311 More specifically, variations in the Lymphocyte-specific protein tyrosine kinase (LCK) gene, as seen with the rs10914542-G allele, which is associated with increased T1D risk. This specific variation contributes to immune system dysfunction by reducing responsiveness to TCR activation signals.223,313 This genetic variation could be a great assessment of T1D susceptibility as a biomarker and a target for future therapies.313

Crohn’s disease

Crohn’s disease (CD) is a chronic inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) characterized by constant and relapsing inflammation of the gastrointestinal (GI) tract, leading to symptoms such as abdominal pain, diarrhea, weight loss, and fatigue.314 The exact mechanism of CD is not fully understood yet, but it is believed to result from a dysregulated immune response to gut microbiota in genetically predisposed individuals.315,316 Pro-inflammatory cytokines, including tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α), interleukin-12 (IL-12), interleukin-23 (IL-23), and interferon-gamma (IFN-γ), play a central role in sustaining chronic inflammation and, in the case of CD, causing tissue damage.6,314,317 NRTKs, particularly those in the Janus kinase (JAK) family, are key regulators of immune signaling pathways involved in CD.318,319 The JAK/STAT pathway is activated by multiple cytokines that drive intestinal inflammation, including IL-6, IL-12, and IL-23.37,286,318–320 Dysregulated JAK signaling promotes the differentiation and activation of pro-inflammatory T-helper (Th1 and Th17) cells, which contribute to sustained immune activation and tissue damage in the gut.6,318,319 SFK and SYK regulate immune cell migration and activation, further amplifying the inflammatory response in CD.109,314,321 Moreover, there are gain-of-function mutations that lead to the expansion of peripheral CD8 + T cells and CD4 + Th17 AND Th1 cells, which is a characteristic associated with chronic relapsing inflammatory conditions like CD.6,109 SYKs’ activation modulates intestinal mucosal immune response by engaging the inflammatory signaling pathways, and the damage caused by CD only increases the activation of SYK.314,317,321 Given their significant roles in cytokine signaling, SFKs and SYK represent promising therapeutic targets for controlling intestinal inflammation. JAK inhibitors have emerged as potential treatments for CD, particularly for patients who do not respond to conventional therapies such as corticosteroids, immunosuppressants, or TNF inhibitors.318–320 In the context of PTPs and SOCS in the earlier sections (“Cancer and tumorigenesis”, “Cardiovascular diseases”, “Metabolic disorders”, “Neurodegenerative diseases”), it was mentioned that they play dual roles as tumor suppressors or activators in cancers and as regulators of inflammation in metabolic and cardiovascular diseases. In the case of autoimmune diseases, these proteins are essential regulators of immune homeostasis. They are increasingly recognized as contributors to the pathogenesis of autoimmune diseases such as SLE, MS, T1D, CD, and Rheumatoid Arthritis (RA). PTPN22 (known as LYP) is one of the most extensively studied PPTs due to its strong genetic association with several autoimmune disorders. The gain-of-function R620W variant of PTPN22 increases T cell activation threshold and promotes survival of autoreactive lymphocytes, thereby contributing to heightened susceptibility to autoimmune diseases. This variant not only increases disease risk but is linked with earlier and more severe onset, particularly in RA.322 PTPN2 (TCPTP) plays a crucial roles in maintaining immune tolerance in both innate and adaptive immunity. Loss or haploinsufficiency of PTPN2 results in increased activation of T helper subsets and B cells, contributing to autoimmunity in diseases, such as SLE and CD. Specifically, reduced PTPN2 expression in regulatory T cells (Tregs) impairs intestinal immune tolerance and facilitates pathogenic conversion in IBD.323,324 Rare gain-of-function mutations in PTPN11 have been associated with monogenic forms of SLE, leading to the constitutive activation of the SHP2 phosphatase. This hyperactivation amplifies cytokine signaling, particularly via STAT1 and STAT3, fosters the survival and proliferation of autoreactive lymphocytes, and compromises both central and peripheral immune tolerance, ultimately driving lupus-like autoimmunity.325,326 SOCS proteins, particularly SOCS1 and SOCS3, function as negative feedback regulators of cytokine signaling through the JAK/STAT pathway, which is crucial for immune cell communication. In autoimmune diseases like SLE, decreased SOCS1 expression is associated with elevated levels of inflammatory markers and increased antigen presentation, contributing to immune dysregulation. SOCS1 haploinsufficiency is directly linked to early-onset autoimmunity. Similarly, SOCS3 modulates IL-6/JAK/STAT signaling, and its dysregulation may contribute to immune dysfunction in autoimmune diseases such as MS, CD, and RA.102,327 These findings emphasize that PTPs and SOCS proteins are vital negative regulators that oppose non-receptor tyrosine kinase (NRTK) signaling pathways, including those driven by SRC, JAKs, and ABL, to sustain immune tolerance. Disruption of this balance, whether through genetic variants or altered expression, disturbs immune homeostasis and promotes continuous activation of NRTK-driven signaling cascades (e.g., JAK/STAT, MAPK, PI3K/AKT), thereby contributing to the development and progression of autoimmune diseases.

Shared signaling cascades across multiple diseases

Based on our understanding of NRTK signaling pathways and the associated diseases that play a crucial role in their pathophysiology, numerous common signaling cascades and crosstalk contribute to their pathogenesis. Such central signaling outputs, including PI3K–AKT, mTOR, MAPK/ERK, and JAK-STAT, are frequently involved across distinct disease classes, demonstrating the functional convergence of NRTK signaling, as demonstrated in Fig. 9. One of the downstream signaling involves JAK/STAT activation and cytokine secretion, which leads to chronic inflammation. This drives mitochondrial dysfunction and ROS production, causing mitochondrial DNA damage, leading to apoptotic cell death, which contributes to various complications such as metabolic diseases and neurodegeneration. Such a signaling cascade could be used as a strategy to target other diseases, such as cancers, where anti-apoptosis pathways and associated mechanisms contribute to therapy resistance. Moreover, mechanisms such as lipid metabolism dysregulation, triglyceride accumulation, and adipogenesis not only result in metabolic diseases but also serve as precursors of cardiovascular complications and increased susceptibility to other diseases. Aberrant phosphorylation by JAKs impairs pathways like glucose uptake and insulin receptor signaling in metabolic tissues, promotes endothelial dysfunction, and enhances vascular inflammation in cardiovascular disease. However, these pathways are also targeted in cancer due to their role in promoting cancer survival and progression. ABL and FAK regulate cytoskeletal and adhesion dynamics, influencing vascular remodeling, cardiomyocyte contractility, and immune cell trafficking into metabolic and vascular tissues. Their role in such dynamic processes enables tumor invasion, immune cell trafficking, and synaptic or axonal dysfunction. Multiple NRTKs commonly hyperactivate downstream signaling pathways, such as PI3K/AKT, MAPK, JAK/STAT, and NFκB, leading to unchecked cell proliferation in cancer, chronic immune activation in autoimmunity, and neuronal dysfunction in neurodegeneration. Their aberrant phosphorylation of substrates drives oncogenic signaling, hyperactivation of immune receptors, or pathological modifications of neuronal proteins like Tau, α-synuclein, and APP, fostering aggregation and toxicity.

A shared NRTK signaling intricately linked and overlaps within a canonical cascade. When such signaling is disrupted, it leads to the manifestation of multiple complexities simultaneously in cellular systems. Aberrant NRTK signaling cascades driven by gene mutations, fusions, or overexpression are hallmarks of cancer, autoimmune diseases, and chronic inflammation. Intracellular NRTK signaling is often initiated by extracellular cues, which are transmitted to the cytosol via receptors such as RTKs, integrin receptors, and cytokine receptors. RTK phosphorylation is associated with NRTKs such as SRC, which play a central role in numerous signaling processes. SRC–SYK–BTK signaling results in the PLCγ2–PIP2 cascade and finally IP3 activation, leading to an increase in calcium signaling and consequently triggers autoimmunity and causes SLE. Elevated SRC levels can also increase the triglyceride accumulation via SREBP1 and contribute to metabolic disorders like obesity and hyperlipidemia, conditions which can also be exacerbated by the integrin–FAK associated adipogenesis. SRC can also promote cancer proliferation and survival via the ABL–activated RAS–RAF–MEK–ERK pathway, regulating cellular transcription. SRC activates the JAK–STAT signaling cascade, a major regulator of the inflammatory pathway. Dysregulation of the JAK–STAT pathway often leads to increased inflammation by activating NFκB and driving the secretion of cytokines such as IL–6 and TNFα, a hallmark of many disease pathologies, including CF, MS, RA, neurodegeneration, and cancers. The JAK is also associated with the IL receptors, wherein some mutations in the JAK can lead to cytokine–dependence, while others lead to aberrant hyperactivation of the JAK, leading to increased STAT phosphorylation and nuclear translocation, causing the activation of transcription factors (TFs) that transcribe the proliferative and inflammatory signals and lead to cancer and neurodegenerative pathologies. The JAK also regulates FAK, which in turn regulates its activation in a loop; FAK also activates STAT3, which leads to these diseases in cases of aberrant expression. FAK and JAK both associate with IRS1 of the insulin receptor and modulate the PI3K–PDK1–AKT cascade, which in turn activates AS160–GLUT receptors, NFκB, and mTOR. Contrastingly, JAK3 plays a protective role against inflammation through IRS1–PI3K–AKT, suppressing TLR4–NFκB mediated inflammation, and its loss exacerbates inflammation, leading to metabolic dysfunctions such as hyperglycemia. Additionally, PI3K indirectly activates NFκB via the PIP3–BMX–STAT3 pathway. mTOR has also been a key molecule of interest due to its role in promoting cancer metastasis by transcriptionally regulating survival genes and inhibiting apoptosis by regulating proteins like BCL–2. The ABL forms BCR–ABL1 fusion protein due to the Philadelphia chromosome and causes chronic myeloid leukemia (CML). BCR–ABL1 impairs Beclin–1, causing its dissociation from autophagy–initiating complexes and therefore inhibiting autophagy; this is also supported by mTOR suppression. However, in neuronal degenerative diseases like AD, impaired Beclin–1 function affects autophagy, leading to Tau and Amyloid-β accumulation. Aberrant activation of the BCR–ABL/cABL acts as a driver of AD and PD neurodegeneration, as it facilitates the formation of toxic protein aggregates, alpha synuclein, and Tau. Additionally, elevated BCR–ABL levels can lead to mitochondrial dysfunction and increase ROS production, resulting in lipid peroxidation, DNA damage, apoptosis, and cardiovascular diseases such as atherosclerosis. JAK overexpression, and constitutive activation of JAK/STAT due to mutations in JAK are commonly observed in tumors, leading to the activation and expression of many oncogenes and anti-apoptotic genes via the JAK/STAT/mTOR cascade. In autoimmune and inflammatory diseases, JAK3 gain-of-function mutations contribute to immune dysregulation, while loss-of-function variants cause SCID, highlighting their dual roles. JAK3 in intestinal and liver epithelial cells regulates an anti-inflammatory signaling cascade through the IRS1/PI3K/Akt pathway, which suppresses TLR4–NFκB activation and maintains metabolic homeostasis. Loss of JAK3 disrupts such a pathway, leading to increased inflammation and metabolic dysfunction, such as hyperglycemia.152,243 In a separate study, a high-fat diet-induced deficiency of JAK3 in intestinal epithelial cells promoted gut inflammation and barrier dysfunction, driving systemic inflammation that impacted the brain by reducing microglial JAK3 and TREM2 levels, impairing clearance of β-amyloid and Tau, and ultimately contributing to cognitive decline via the gut-brain axis.328 This demonstrates that many of these diseases or their causes are interlinked, leading to the complex nature of pathogenesis.

SFKs are known to activate the JAK/STAT cascade, thereby contributing to the disease, as their involvement is often upstream of that of JAKs. In cancer, they promote proliferation, survival, invasion, and therapy resistance via JAK/STAT, MAPK/ERK, PI3K/AKT, STAT3, and FAK pathways.329–331 In cardiovascular disease, SFKs mediate endothelial dysfunction and vascular remodeling through ROS and integrin–FAK signaling.332 Overactive SFKs disrupt insulin signaling and activate inflammatory pathways, such as TLR4–NFκB, in metabolic disorders.330,331 In neurodegeneration, a similar cascade contributes to the neuroinflammation through JAK/STAT-mediated cytokine release. Parallelly, FYN drives tau phosphorylation and synaptic toxicity via NMDAR and microglial signaling.333 In autoimmune diseases, SFKs regulate BCR and TCR signaling, where overactivation of these pathways enhances autoreactive lymphocyte responses and chronic inflammation. SFK dysregulation mainly results from overexpression or loss of negative regulation, converging on shared pathways that disrupt cellular homeostasis across multiple systems.334 SFKs, such as BLK and SRC trigger the MAPK cascade via RAS, leading to ERK-driven gene expression that supports proliferation and survival. Simultaneously, BLK activates PI3K, leading to AKT phosphorylation and subsequent effects on cell survival, metabolism, and angiogenesis. BLK signaling also engaged the IKK complex directly and indirectly via the PI3K-AKT pathway, driving NFκB activation and transcription of anti-apoptotic and inflammatory genes.335–337 BLK was also observed to contribute to the development of autoimmune diseases. Typically, it initiates BCR signaling by phosphorylating ITAMs on Igα/Igβ, thereby activating SYK and recruiting adaptor proteins such as BLNK and BANK1. Such recruitment triggers downstream pathways, including the PI3K/AKT, MAPK, NF-κB, and PLCγ2–Ca²⁺ signaling pathways, promoting B cell survival, proliferation, and differentiation. Reduced BLK expression, as seen in autoimmune risk variants, impairs early BCR signaling, potentially allowing autoreactive B cells to escape tolerance and contribute to autoimmunity.338,339

Similarity in the cascades has also been noted with TEC kinases, where overexpression of TEC and BMX/ETK has been implicated in liver tumorigenesis and several solid tumors, respectively. TEC stabilizes PLK4, promoting hepatocellular proliferation and survival, whereas BMX is required for STAT3 phosphorylation in SRC-driven cancers, thereby enhancing tumor growth, invasion, and inflammation.340,341 TEC kinases also regulate cytoskeletal dynamics, adhesion, and PI3K/STAT3 signaling, facilitating tumor cell migration and survival. TEC family kinases are most prominently implicated in autoimmune and immunodeficiency disorders. FAK is also frequently overexpressed in diverse malignancies primarily due to PTK2 gene amplification, transcriptional upregulation by factors such as NF-κB, AGO2, Nanog, and Ets proteins, or loss of p53-mediated repression. Overexpression correlates with tumor invasiveness, lymph node metastasis, and poor prognosis and is evident even in the early stages of tumorigenesis, especially at invasive fronts. Similar to other NRTKs, FAK overexpression promotes oncogenic signaling through the RAS/RAF/MEK/ERK pathway and enhances its expression via transcription factors like USF1, establishing a positive feedback loop. It also interacts with and facilitates the degradation of p53, further driving tumor cell survival and proliferation.119,342

ABL forms a fusion protein, BCR-ABL, in Ph+CML, which phosphorylates and impairs Beclin-1 activity, disrupting the formation of autophagy complexes.343 Additionally, although there is no direct evidence that BCR-ABL affects neurodegeneration, it has been observed that reduced Beclin-1 function disrupts the PI3KC3-C1 assembly, which is an initiating complex for autophagy, implying that it could potentially be involved in the cascade.344,345 ABL functions both as an oncogenic driver, particularly through BCR–ABL1 fusion or overexpression, and as a DNA damage-responsive regulator. Its dysregulation contributes to malignancy, congenital syndromes, and potentially broader disease contexts by integrating into key signaling cascades. Taken together, it can be seen that numerous pathways are implicated by aberrant activation or mutation of NRTKs and by certain diseases.

Therapeutic targets and clinical research

After understanding the mechanisms and crosstalk within the cellular signaling system, in which NRTKs are key players, and their involvement in disease manifestation and progression, learning about therapeutics is key to developing the best approaches to these diseases. The NRTKs have been of keen interest in the past few decades due to their intricate involvement in the disease pathways. The FDA has approved many NRTK-inhibiting drugs, which are being researched for additional applications (Table 2). One prime example of an additional application is Imatinib, an anti-cancer drug that reverses diabetes in animal models by enhancing insulin production and improving glucose homeostasis.226,227 Clinical studies suggest that TKIs may improve blood glucose control and reduce insulin dependence in diabetic patients with cancer.346,347

Current Therapeutics Targeting NRTKs and Implications of their Effects

Currently, no FDA-approved drugs specifically inhibit many NRTKs, such as the C-terminal kinase (CSK). However, some FDA-approved kinase inhibitors, such as Ibrutinib, approved for SLL/CLL, primarily target BTK but also inhibit CSK. This off-target activity has been associated with adverse effects like atrial fibrillation.348 Primarily recognized as a cyclin-dependent kinase (CDK) inhibitor, Seliciclib also exhibits inhibitory effects on CSK. It has been investigated for its potential to treat non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) and other malignancies, and to induce apoptosis in cancer cells. Additionally, Seliciclib has shown promise in addressing immunological disorders by inhibiting RNA polymerase II-dependent transcription and down-regulating proteins like MCL1.349–351 However, its trials were limited to phase-2b, as there was not much difference between Selicicib and placebo, and saw no ongoing large-scale registration trials as of 2025.

Entospletinib, a SYK inhibitor, works by blocking B-cell signaling in autoimmune diseases, such as Rheumatoid Arthritis and B-cell lymphomas.297,352–356 In the preclinical experiments on autoimmune arthritis, Entospletinib primarily attenuated disease by suppressing neutrophil Fcγ receptor- and integrin-mediated activation, thereby disrupting the positive feedback loops that drive joint inflammation. Although the passive immunization model excluded direct B-cell involvement, additional analyses revealed that Entospletinib reduced intracellular tyrosine phosphorylation in B cells, suggesting it may also impair B-cell signaling and autoantibody production in active arthritis models.297 However, the drug has completed phase 2 trials for AML with TP53 mutations or complex karyotypes, administered in combination with decitabine, with plans of phase 3 trials focusing on NPM1-mutated AML.357

Similarly, BTK inhibitors like Rilzabrutinib disrupt the B-cell signaling pathways, reducing inflammation and immune cell activity. Thus, it is being investigated for autoimmune diseases like Pemphigus, a chronic autoimmune skin disease characterized by blistering. Chronic Spontaneous Urticaria, a condition that is marked by recurrent hives with unidentifiable cause, and Immune Thrombocytopenia (ITP), a type of autoimmune disorder that leads to low platelet count in Rheumatoid Arthritis and are under clinical trials.284,285,294,358 Rilazabrutinib’s preclinical data demonstrated potent inhibition of BTK-dependent immune cell functions, including B cells, macrophages, mast cells, basophils, and neutrophils, and reduced levels of inflammatory cytokines without causing cell death.359 It has shown rapid and durable platelet responses, reduced bleeding, improved fatigue, and HRQoL in phase 1/2 clinical trials for ITP, with greater benefit when used earlier in treatment. Its mechanism involves sustained inhibition of Fc-receptor–mediated immune activation with minimal off-target effects, and trials report a favorable safety profile without typical BTK-related toxicities. Ongoing phase 2/3 LUNA studies are evaluating long-term efficacy, potential treatment-free remission, and broader use in autoimmune diseases.360 On the other hand, Spebrutinib, a BTK inhibitor, mainly blocked B-cell activity, reduced inflammatory cytokines, and limited bone breakdown in lab studies. In RA patients, it achieved high BTK occupancy, altered B-cell populations, lowered inflammatory and bone turnover markers, and showed clinical benefit, with about 42% of treated patients reaching ACR20 at week 4 versus 22% on placebo, and better responses were seen in those with favorable baseline B-cell and bone marker profiles.361 Phase 2a clinical studies evaluated Spebrutinib in patients with RA. The results showed a trend towards clinical improvement and significant reductions in inflammatory markers.362 Another drug that targets the B-cell signaling pathway by acting on the BTK inhibition is Evobrutinib, which is being primarily investigated for autoimmune diseases like MS, Rheumatoid arthritis, and SLE. In preclinical studies, Evobrutinib reduced disease severity and histological damage in mouse models of RA and SLE by inhibiting B cell activation, reducing autoantibody production, modulating plasma cell numbers, and normalizing immune cell subsets, including B and T cells. In RA models, benefits were observed even without reductions in autoantibody levels, suggesting additional mechanisms such as inhibition of innate immune cells. Similarly, in lupus-prone NZB/W F1 mice, Evobrutinib lessened kidney damage and modulated T and B cell activity comparably to mycophenolate mofetil, while inhibition of mast cell activation in a passive cutaneous anaphylaxis model indicated broad anti-inflammatory effects.363 Clinically, Evobrutinib has completed multiple phase 2 trials in patients with relapsing MS, demonstrating significant efficacy and a favorable safety profile over 24 weeks, and two phase 3 trials (EVOLUTION RMS 1 and 2) have completed enrollment to further evaluate its efficacy. In contrast, phase 2 trials in RA and SLE did not meet their primary endpoints but established acceptable safety and showed some biological activity, highlighting the complexity of these diseases and the need for further research. Across all indications, Evobrutinib was generally well tolerated, with no major dose-dependent adverse events observed.294,364 In B-cell malignancies, second-generation BTK inhibitors such as Acalabrutinib and Zanubrutinib are irreversible, covalent ATP-competitive inhibitors that bind to the cysteine residue (Cys481) in BTK, offering greater target selectivity and reduced off-target effects compared to Ibrutinib. Pirtobrutinib, in contrast, is a non-covalent, reversible BTK inhibitor designed to overcome resistance from BTK Cys481 mutations.294,365–367

Other drugs, such as GSK2256098, inhibit FAK activity, disrupting the signaling pathways crucial for cell migration, adhesion, and survival. Therefore, it is actively investigated for solid tumors and some hematological malignancies.255 It is currently in phase II trial for meningioma, while earlier phase II trials for PDAC did not show much promise.368 Kinase inhibitors such as Dasatinib have shown anti-proliferative activity across different tumor types.110 Inhibition of NRTK families demonstrates restorative effects in cancer, as evidenced by increased E-cadherin expression in pancreatic cancer cells vulnerable to Dasatinib. Activation led to β-catenin phosphorylation in this context, forming complexes with cadherins. After phosphorylation by a kinase, β-catenin translocate to the nucleus and activates several epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition (EMT) transcription factors, negatively regulating E-cadherin expression. This ultimately disrupts cadherin-mediated cell-cell adhesion, thereby enhancing tumor cell migration.111

PP1 and PP2 are selective NRTK inhibitors that have demonstrated utility in both cancer and cardiovascular research. In cervical cancer cells, PP2 regulated pY416 levels, indicating its potential to disrupt signaling pathways involved in cell survival and migration.369 Unlike its inactive analog PP3, PP1 effectively reversed post-myocardial infarction arrhythmias by inhibiting c-SRC–mediated phosphorylation of the mitochondrial Ca²⁺ uniporter (MCU), which enhances mitochondrial Ca²⁺ uptake and contributes to cardiac dysfunction.370 While these compounds exhibit favorable pharmacokinetics, their broad kinase targeting and limited bioavailability restrict clinical translation.371 Nevertheless, PP2 and related inhibitors like SU6656 show promise in blocking high-glucose–induced damage in diabetic models, suggesting a potential role in treating complications such as diabetic nephropathy.372,373 However, in the past decade, more specific inhibitors have emerged, such as Dasatinib and Bosutinib, which are ATP-competitive dual SFK/ABL inhibitors approved for (CML) and Philadelphia chromosome–positive acute lymphoblastic leukemia (Ph+ ALL), and have also demonstrated efficacy in breast and lung cancers. Saracatinib (AZD0530) is a selective ATP-competitive SFK inhibitor, orally bioavailable and has been evaluated in clinical trials for solid tumors and viral infections. Saracatinib effectively blocked fibrotic responses in multiple preclinical models of idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF), demonstrating activity comparable to or superior to FDA-approved drugs Nintedanib and Pirfenidone. It modulates SRC-dependent signaling pathways involved in fibrosis, epithelial-mesenchymal transition, and immune response genes.374 Preclinical evidence indicates Saracatinib’s ability to reduce fibrosis and related inflammation in lung tissue, and to ameliorate disease symptoms in fibrodysplasia ossificans progressiva (FOP).375 Clinically, Saracatinib is being evaluated in a Phase 1b/2a STOP-IPF clinical trial (NCT04598919) for IPF to assess safety, tolerability, pharmacodynamics, and efficacy, including biomarker analyses linked to SRC kinase activity.374,376 Early cancer trials established tolerability at doses up to 175 mg daily, though limited efficacy led to discontinuation in oncology development; they are now being researched for applications in conditions like neurotoxicity.377 Ponatinib, originally developed to overcome resistance in CML due to the BCR-ABL T315I mutation, is also a potent ATP-competitive pan-kinase inhibitor with strong activity against SFKs.11 It is FDA- and EMA-approved primarily for the treatment of Philadelphia chromosome-positive leukemias such as chronic myeloid leukemia (CML) and acute lymphoblastic leukemia (Ph+ ALL), in patients who are resistant or intolerant to prior TKIs.378 Ponatinib is currently being evaluated in ongoing clinical trials for Philadelphia chromosome-positive (Ph+) acute lymphoblastic leukemia, including a pediatric Phase 1/2 study (NCT03934372) assessing safety and efficacy in recurrent or refractory leukemias and an adult Phase II trial (NCT03147612) investigating a sequential combination of low-intensity chemotherapy and Ponatinib followed by blinatumomab and Ponatinib.379 These agents represent a shift from non-selective tools like PP2 toward clinically actionable therapies with improved specificity and tolerability. Another investigational drug is NXP900 (eCF506), a next-generation SFK inhibitor currently in phase I clinical trials. Unlike older inhibitors that bind the active “open” conformation, NXP900 locks SFKs in an inactive “closed” conformation, blocking both catalytic and scaffolding functions. It demonstrates sub-nanomolar potency (e.g., IC50 of 0.47 nM for YES1) and robust antitumor activity in preclinical models of head and neck and esophageal squamous cell carcinoma.380 In CML, the recently approved Asciminib represents a first-in-class STAMP (Specifically Targeting the ABL Myristoyl Pocket) inhibitor, acting allosterically by restoring ABL1’s autoinhibitory conformation, offering superior efficacy and safety over traditional ATP-competitive ABL inhibitors.381

For autoimmune and myeloproliferative diseases, JAK inhibitors such as Upadacitinib and Fedratinib are selective ATP-competitive inhibitors of JAK1 and JAK2, respectively, that reduce pro-inflammatory cytokine signaling. Upadacitinib, a selective JAK1 inhibitor, demonstrated potent inhibition of pro-inflammatory cytokine signaling (IL-6, IFNγ), improved intestinal barrier integrity, and reduced disease activity in preclinical models of rheumatoid arthritis and bowel inflammation.382–384 Phase 1 and 2 trials confirmed its pharmacological selectivity and rapid, dose-dependent clinical efficacy, which was further validated in large phase 3 programs across rheumatoid arthritis, atopic dermatitis, IBD, and other autoimmune conditions, leading to FDA and EMA approval. Its safety profile is generally manageable, with infections—particularly herpes zoster—representing the main clinical risk.383,384 Fedratinib, a selective JAK2 inhibitor designed to spare JAK1 and JAK3, also exhibits additional activity against FLT3 and BET proteins and effectively inhibits STAT phosphorylation in myeloproliferative neoplasm models, including Ruxolitinib-resistant disease. Clinical trials established the maximum tolerated dose and showed meaningful spleen volume reduction and symptom improvement in approximately 30–45% of myelofibrosis patients, leading to FDA approval.385 On the other hand, Deucravacitinib is a selective allosteric TYK2 inhibitor that binds the pseudokinase JH2 domain, thereby avoiding broader JAK family inhibition and minimizing infection and thrombotic risks.386–388 In parallel, multi-kinase inhibitors such as Cerdulatinib, which simultaneously targets SYK and JAK kinases, act as ATP-competitive inhibitors and are under investigation for overcoming therapeutic resistance in B-cell malignancies.204 These studies along with established therapeutic drugs or candidates targeting NRTK inhibition show promise for future therapies for these complex, multifactorial diseases, broadening our insights into the interplay of their underlying mechanisms contributing to pathophysiology. Recent years have seen significant progress in the development of PTP inhibitors as potential therapeutics, particularly in cancer, metabolic, and immune-related diseases. PTPs are enzymes that catalyze the removal of phosphate groups from phosphotyrosine residues, thereby counterbalancing protein tyrosine kinase activity and tightly regulating intracellular signaling. Historically considered “undruggable” due to the highly conserved and charged nature of their catalytic sites, PTPs have recently emerged as attractive drug targets owing to their central roles in immune regulation, tumor progression, insulin signaling, and inflammatory responses.389

Notably, PTPN2 (TCPTP) and PTP1B have been among the most intensively studied phosphatases. Dysregulation of these enzymes is linked to immune evasion, resistance to checkpoint blockade, and type 2 diabetes, among other diseases.168,389,390 Recent breakthroughs include the development of selective small-molecule inhibitors, natural product derivatives, and PROTAC degraders targeting PTPN2, which have shown the ability to enhance anti-tumor immunity and restore sensitivity to immunotherapies in preclinical models.390 For PTP1B, both antisense oligonucleotides (e.g., IONIS-PTP-1BRx) and small molecules have demonstrated improved glycemic control and insulin sensitivity in animal models and early clinical trials.162,168 PROTACs are heterobifunctional molecules that link a ligand recognizing the PTP to an E3 ligase-recruiting ligand, thereby promoting ubiquitination and subsequent proteasomal degradation of the phosphatase. Unlike classical inhibitors, they function catalytically by removing the entire protein rather than simply blocking its activity, enabling potent effects at low concentrations.391 Recent advances include the development of selective degraders for PTP1B and TC-PTP, such as DU-14, a dual degrader that shows robust in vitro and in vivo activity. DU-14 enhances interferon signaling, increases tumor antigen presentation, and stimulates immune cell activation, producing significant antitumor effects in preclinical cancer models.392 These strategies are now under preclinical investigation for metabolic diseases (e.g., diabetes, obesity), immuno-oncology, and cancer, reflecting the central role of PTPs as both tumor suppressors and oncogenic drivers depending on context.329 Complementing these approaches, peptide-based strategies offer unique opportunities to modulate PTP function. Peptide PROTACs replace the small-molecule PTP ligand with a peptide sequence that mimics natural substrates or binding motifs, exploiting the inherent recognition specificity of PTPs. When combined with high-throughput proteomic screening, this approach allows for validation of novel targets.393 In parallel, short peptides and engineered mini-proteins can act as inhibitors by binding substrate-recognition regions or regulatory domains of PTPs. While less developed than small molecules, they represent a promising means to modulate phosphatase activity or subcellular localization. Importantly, because PTP catalytic sites are often shallow and highly conserved, small-molecule inhibitors have struggled with selectivity; peptide-based modalities overcome this limitation by targeting isoform-specific features or protein–protein interaction domains, thereby opening therapeutic opportunities against PTPs previously considered undruggable.394

In parallel, SHP2 (PTPN11) allosteric inhibitors such as JAB-3068, JAB-3312, and ERAS-601 are advancing through clinical trials, often in combination with immune checkpoint inhibitors to address resistance in advanced cancers.389 In addition to pipeline compounds, several marketed drugs have been identified to possess off-target PTP inhibitory activity. Examples include sodium stibogluconate (SHP-1, SHP-2 inhibition), suramin (PTP1B inhibition), and gallium nitrate, all approved for unrelated indications such as leishmaniasis or hypercalcemia but showing PTP inhibition as a secondary property.395,396 Other agents, like vanadate, phenylarsine oxide, and bisphosphonates (alendronate, etidronate), act as broad-spectrum or experimental PTP inhibitors. Additionally, research-grade inhibitors such as Dephostatin, JMS-053, and Thienopyridone are being explored for their ability to selectively target PTPs, particularly the PRL family in cancer models.395,397,398

Despite having promising advances, key challenges remain, including achieving high isoform selectivity, minimizing off-target toxicity, and ensuring robust translation of preclinical findings into effective clinical therapies. Nevertheless, the rapid pace of drug discovery and a growing number of clinical trials suggest a paradigm shift in the therapeutic targeting of PTPs, with the potential to meet major unmet needs across oncology, immunology, and metabolic disease.2,168,389,390

Conclusions and future directions

NRTKs are integral to the regulation of cellular signaling pathways; their involvement in critical signaling pathways, including the JAK/STAT, PI3K/AKT, RAS/RAF, and MAPK/ERK, underscores their serious consequences in a diverse array of pathophysiological conditions, suggesting their potential for therapeutic intervention.

NRTKs have been shown to contribute to metastasis and tumor progression by activating TFs for oncogenes; however, SYK demonstrates anti-tumorigenesis properties, and their exact mechanisms of action remain to be elucidated. In cardiovascular diseases, NRTKs have demonstrated active engagement with angiogenesis, protection against ischemia-reperfusion injury, and preservation of endothelial integrity, highlighting their dual role as both aggravators and facilitators of recovery. Similarly, FES, FAK, and TEC demonstrated a protective role against the progression of metabolic disease, while others like, SRC and ABL have contributed to the pathology by affecting key pathways. It is known that SFK and ABL can activate the PI3K cascade, influencing insulin receptor signaling and subsequent action on glucose transporters, thereby affecting glucose uptake and metabolism in the cell. JAK/STAT activation contributes to the secretion of inflammatory cytokines, including IL-6 and TNF-α, and to NFκB activation. Such activation leads to increased inflammation, which serves as a hallmark for not only metabolic diseases but also neurodegeneration, autoimmunity, and cardiovascular diseases. Parallelly, SRC, BTK drive MAPK signaling and promote the transcriptional activation of genes that promote cell survival and proliferation. We also observed how diseases are often interrelated, wherein JAK deficiency in the gut cells not only leads to hyperlipidemia and metabolic complications but also results in a systemic reaction wherein we see a deficiency of JAK in the brain, leading to the risk of neurodegeneration. Apart from this, ABL and SFKs also play a role in neuroinflammation by Tau and α-synuclein phosphorylation and plaque formation. They also contribute to myelin sheath degradation and induce necroptosis, which is triggered by mitochondrial dysfunction and inflammation. BCR signaling is critical to the immune response, making NRTKS crucial to this process due to their role in downstream signaling cascades, and their involvement in cytokine signaling, both of which are affected in autoimmune diseases. Once these canonical pathways are disrupted, these kinases contribute to the pathogenesis of these multifactorial diseases.

NRTKs exert diverse roles that extend beyond their canonical catalytic activities, functioning as both pathway-specific regulators and multitasking integrators within complex signaling networks. In certain contexts, such as cytokine receptor signaling, JAKs demonstrate strong pathway specificity by directly activating STAT transcription factors to regulate immune and inflammatory responses. Conversely, SRC, FAK, ABL, and TEC/BTK display functional versatility, participating simultaneously in adhesion, cytoskeletal remodeling, proliferation, survival, and immune regulation. A central feature of NRTKs is their scaffolding capacity, conferred by modular non-catalytic domains including SH2, SH3, PH, and FERM motifs, which enable the recruitment and spatial organization of diverse signaling proteins into higher-order complexes. Through this scaffolding role, NRTKs not only amplify receptor-derived signals but also integrate cues from multiple upstream receptors, including integrins, growth factor receptors, and immune receptors, thereby coordinating downstream effectors across pathways such as PI3K/AKT, MAPK, and JAK/STAT. Their context dependent signaling further enhances signaling flexibility, as NRTKs often co-activate or form feedback loops with one another and with receptor tyrosine kinases and serine/threonine kinases to generate nuanced and adaptive cellular responses.

Despite significant advances being made in line with NRTKs pathogenesis, inadequate attention has been paid to elucidating their role in metabolic research. While SRC, FYN, and JAK2 have established roles in insulin resistance and lipid regulation, the mechanisms by which NRTKs interact with nutrient-sensing pathways such as AMPK or modulate metabolism under dynamic physiological conditions remain obscure. Parallel to this, ABL and SRC are known to regulate phosphatases like PP2A in cancer, thereby affecting phosphorylation dynamics and oncogenic signaling. However, their involvement in phosphatase regulation in neurodegenerative diseases like Alzheimer’s and Parkinson’s remains largely unknown despite the centrality of Tau hyperphosphorylation in these conditions. Similarly, cancer therapies often trigger metabolic shifts that overlap with pathways active in metabolic diseases, such as lipid synthesis and mitochondrial function. Additionally, individuals with a mutation in ABL that causes Ph+CML exhibit disruption of Beclin-1-associated autophagy initiation complex formation, a phenomenon observed in neurodegeneration. However, there is no established linkage between ABL and Beclin-1 phosphorylation in neurodegeneration. Nevertheless, it is unclear whether these changes reflect tissue-specific adaptations or shared cross-disease crosstalk. Unraveling these mechanistic overlaps could yield transformative insight into standard signaling hubs amenable to therapeutic targeting across disease classes.

Over the past couple of decades, advances in targeted therapies and FDA-approved drugs, particularly kinase inhibitors, have shown encouraging results in altering NRTK’s pathogenicity. Inhibitor candidates like Seliciclib had shown potential in inhibiting CSK but did not show much promise in the next phases of trial, halting further development. Such drugs could be considered either potent compounds for development or for research into a cure for other diseases further down the line. Despite this, drugs that are specific in targeting CSK and some other NRTKs are still lacking, with broad targeting drugs being the only option in the market. Moreover, these unintended effects observed with broad-targeting NRTK inhibitors show promise for new therapeutic windows. Nonetheless, challenges persist in creating selective inhibitors that reduce off-target effects while enhancing therapeutic effectiveness. Furthermore, newer therapeutic avenues are being explored, such as RTKIs that block specific receptor-mediated signaling, which is easier to target because these receptors are located on the cell membrane and serve as the primary checkpoint for signaling cascades. Current NRTK-targeted therapies include Imatinib, which has demonstrated beneficial effects on glucose homeostasis in diabetic models; and BTK inhibitors such as Ibrutinib, Rilzabrutinib, and Evobrutinib, which modulate B-cell signaling and show efficacy in autoimmune disorders and B-cell malignancies. Multi-kinase inhibitors such as Dasatinib, Ponatinib, and Saracatinib have demonstrated efficacy in hematologic malignancies, solid tumors, and fibrotic diseases, highlighting the versatility of NRTK inhibition. Additionally, selective JAK inhibitors, including Upadacitinib, Fedratinib, and Deucravacitinib, exemplify the clinical benefit of precise targeting within NRTK networks, reducing inflammatory cytokine signaling while minimizing off-target effects. Despite achieving such significant outcomes, challenges persist in developing highly selective inhibitors that balance efficacy and safety, as broad-spectrum agents often yield off-target effects but also reveal novel therapeutic windows. Emerging approaches targeting downstream scaffolding functions, protein–protein interactions, and upstream receptor tyrosine kinases might offer novel avenues to refine therapeutic interventions.

Aberrant NRTK signaling contributes to proliferation, inflammation, and metabolic dysregulation, underscoring the need for emerging strategies beyond conventional ATP-competitive inhibitors. PROTACs emerge as powerful tools in this context, as they recruit E3 ligases to degrade pathogenic kinases, such as SRC or FYN, thereby reducing compensatory activation and overcoming resistance that often limits the effectiveness of traditional inhibitors. Such an approach holds particular promise in oncology, especially NRTKs, which drive tumor survival, and in neurodegeneration, where their persistent activity contributes to the pathology of Tau or α-synuclein. Peptide-based therapeutics offer an alternative strategy by selectively disrupting SH2- and SH3-mediated protein–protein interactions, crucial for NRTK downstream scaffolding and signaling assembly. Such peptides limit pathogenic immune activation in autoimmunity or dampen vascular inflammation in cardiovascular disease, potentially resulting in fewer off-target effects. Taken together, PROTACs and peptide inhibitors represent next-generation therapies designed to exploit NRTK regulatory mechanisms with greater precision, offering new hope for treating diverse disorders driven by kinase dysregulation.

On the other hand, despite significant advances, translating PROTAC- and peptide-based strategies against PTPs into clinical applications remains challenging. For instance, peptide-based modalities, due to poor cell permeability and low bioavailability, limit efficacy, necessitating chemical modifications or specialized delivery systems. PROTACs face distinct hurdles, including limited E3 ligase availability, tissue-specific limitations, and risks of off-target degradation. Resistance mechanisms, such as mutations in target phosphatases or adaptive changes in the ubiquitin–proteasome pathway, further complicate the long-term effectiveness. Perhaps innovations in computational modeling, high-throughput proteomics, and structural biology hold more promise for developing more selective binders and optimized degrader designs. Advances in delivery platforms, including nanoparticles and cell-penetrating peptides, are expected to overcome pharmacokinetic barriers. However, combination strategies with immune checkpoint inhibitors or metabolic modulators may also broaden therapeutic impact across cancer, metabolic disease, and autoimmunity.

Several scientific and clinical challenges hinder durable therapeutic success. A major obstacle is the biological complexity and context-dependent function of NRTKs, which regulate diverse processes ranging from proliferation and apoptosis to immune signaling and metabolic control. This functional heterogeneity, compounded by inter- and intratumoral diversity, limits the development of universal therapeutic strategies and demands precision stratification of patient subgroups. Resistance mechanisms represent another significant barrier: kinase domain mutations, activation of parallel pathways, and metabolic or phenotypic adaptations collectively erode long-term efficacy. In parallel, high conservation of kinase domains contributes to toxicity and off-target effects, restricting therapeutic windows and complicating drug design. Moreover, clinical translation has lagged, with many promising inhibitors demonstrating activity in preclinical or early-phase studies but failing to provide survival benefits in larger, heterogeneous patient populations. Based on the above scientific perspectives, future research should prioritize integrated therapeutic approaches that address these challenges. Rational combination strategies: co-targeting NRTKs with compensatory kinases, immune checkpoints, or metabolic regulators hold significant potential to overcome adaptive resistance and broaden therapeutic efficacy. Additionally, some NRTKs like ABL, SFKs, and JAKs have been extensively studied; however, there are many NRTKs that are still not researched and their significance in these disease pathologies is unknown. Despite there being intensive research on certain NRTKs, there is still a lack of information regarding certain structure-functions and the effect of mutations on diseases.

Further insights into the intrinsic understanding of NRTK signaling and its context-dependent regulation could guide the development of precision therapies that account for various factors related to cell signaling cascades and drug response, thereby enhancing drug specificity, reducing off-target immune effects, and expanding treatment options across diseases.
