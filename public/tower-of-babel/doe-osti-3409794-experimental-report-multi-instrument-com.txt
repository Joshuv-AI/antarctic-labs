1.0 Introduction
 Aerosols are particles suspended in the atmosphere, ranging in size from nanometers to micrometers.
 Their size distribution affects key atmospheric processes, including nucleation, coagulation, scavenging,
 activation, and radiative properties (Seinfeld and Pandis 2016). Aerosol size distribution is a critical
 parameter in atmospheric science, influencing processes such as cloud formation and radiative forcing.
 Accurate representation of aerosol size distributions is essential for understanding their impact on climate,
 air quality, and human health. However, aerosol size and composition vary significantly across time and
 space due to meteorological conditions and natural or anthropogenic sources. (Wu and Boor 2021).

 Various instruments are used to measure aerosol size distributions, each with distinct principles,
 advantages, and limitations. This report begins with an in-depth overview of aerosol size-distribution
 comparison studies, focusing on the passive cavity aerosol spectrometer probe (PCASP), portable optical
 particle spectrometer (POPS), ultra-high-sensitivity aerosol spectrometer (UHSAS), aerodynamic particle
 sizer (APS), and scanning mobility particle sizer (SMPS). All of these instruments are used by the U.S.
 Department of Energy Atmospheric Radiation Measurement (ARM) Aerial Facility (AAF), which
 commissioned this comparison and report. The report evaluates the strengths and weaknesses of these
 instruments, highlights their applications, and discusses efforts to merge data from multiple instruments
 for comprehensive analysis.

2.0 Instrument Description
 Aerosol size distribution refers to the quantitative representation of particle sizes within an aerosol
 sample. It is typically expressed as the number, surface area, or volume of particles per unit size interval.
 The size distribution is critical for understanding aerosol behavior, including optical properties, chemical
 reactivity, and health impacts. Aerosol sizes are commonly categorized into three main modes:
  • Nucleation Mode: Particles smaller than 0.1 µm, often formed via gas-to-particle conversion
 processes.
  • Accumulation Mode: Particles ranging from 0.1 µm to 2.5 µm, resulting from coagulation or
 condensation.
  • Coarse Mode: Particles larger than 2.5 µm, typically generated by mechanical processes such as dust
 resuspension or sea spray.

 The size distribution is often represented using log-normal distributions, which provide a mathematical
 framework for describing aerosol populations. Instruments designed to measure aerosol size distributions
 must account for these modes to provide accurate data.

 Aerosol size distribution measurement relies on a variety of instruments, each optimized for specific size
 ranges, operational principles, and applications. These instruments operate on two primary principles:
 optical methods (light scattering; e.g., POPS, UHSAS, PCASP) and physical/electrical methods
 (aerodynamic time-of-flight or electrical mobility; e.g., APS, SMPS). No single instrument spans the
 entire size range, so researchers often deploy combinations (e.g., SMPS + APS) for comprehensive
 coverage. Instrument selection depends on the target size range, required resolution, deployment platform

(ground, aircraft, laboratory), type of measurement (laboratory tests versus continues monitoring), cost
constraints, and environmental conditions. Key measurement challenges include sensitivity to particle
refractive index and shape (optical instruments), hygroscopic growth effects, and the need for regular
maintenance and calibration. The key specifications of each instrument are summarized in Table 1.

 Each instrument is optimized for specific size ranges, making its selection dependent on the target aerosol
 population. For example:

 2.1 Sensitivity to Size Ranges
  • POPS and PCASP are suitable for accumulation-mode particles, while APS excels in coarse-mode
 measurements.
  • UHSAS and SMPS are ideal for nucleation mode and submicron particles due to their high
 sensitivity.

 2.2 Resolution and Accuracy
  • SMPS provides the highest resolution for submicron particles, making it indispensable for studies on
 nucleation and accumulation modes.
  • APS, while less effective for submicron particles, offers unparalleled accuracy for coarse-mode
 aerosols.

 2.3 Portability and Deployment
  • POPS and PCASP are highly portable and suitable for field and airborne measurements, respectively.
  • Instruments like SMPS and APS are more suited for laboratory or ground-based environments due to
 their size and operational requirements.

 2.4 Cost and Maintenance
  • UHSAS and SMPS are expensive but offer high precision and resolution.
  • POPS and APS are more cost-effective but require regular calibration to maintain accuracy.

3.1 Aerosol Generation and Conditioning
Polydisperse aerosol particles were generated using an atomizer with a diluted suspension of certified
PSL particles. The air was “pushed” through the atomizer to ensure delivery of polydisperse aerosol to a
differential mobility analyzer (DMA) at a specific flow rate. For smaller sizes (below 200 nm), the
atomizer output was dried and passed through a diluter before being directed into the DMA, which
selected particles with a specific electrical mobility diameter, producing a monodisperse aerosol stream.
The size-selected aerosol was then passed through a residence chamber to stabilize the flow and ensure
the particle concentration became spatially uniform before reaching the instruments.

The conditioned aerosol exiting the residence chamber was introduced into a common sampling manifold
(a ¾-inch tubing with multiple pickup points (1/4 inch) along the sampling line). This manifold was
designed to evenly distribute the aerosol to all instruments simultaneously, minimizing concentration
gradients and particle losses across the sampling ports. An additional blower was installed to circulate air
through the manifold and back to the residence chamber, thereby improving mixing and minimizing time
delays in the aerosol transport.

3.2 PSL Particles for Sizing Measurements and Calibration
PSL particles are widely recognized as the gold standard for calibrating particle sizing instruments due to
their highly spherical morphology, exceptional monodispersity, and chemical stability. Produced through
the emulsion polymerization of styrene, these particles are available in precisely controlled diameters
ranging from approximately 20 nm to several millimeters, with sizing accuracies on the order of 0.01 μm.
The sizes we used in this study are 100±3 nm, 152±5 nm, 203±5 nm, 303±6 nm, 510±7 nm, 707±9 nm,
799±9 nm, 903±12 nm, 0.99±0.03 µm, 1.93± µm, and 3.00±0.07 µm. Their narrow size distributions and
well-characterized refractive index (approximately 1.57-1.62) make them ideal reference materials for

 5

 calibrating aerosol optical particle counters, laser diffraction systems, surface scanning inspection
 systems, and electron microscopes. In practice, PSL spheres are typically supplied as aqueous suspensions
 and can be aerosolized or deposited onto substrates to verify instrument response across a range of
 particle diameters. Because their optical and physical properties are highly reproducible from lot to lot,
 PSL standards enable traceable, repeatable measurements that ensure compliance with metrology
 requirements in semiconductor manufacturing, pharmaceutical cleanroom monitoring, and environmental
 particulate analysis.

4.0 Data Analysis and Results
 This section presents the data-processing methodology and a comparative analysis of size-distribution
 measurements obtained from seven instruments: three POPS units, one UHSAS, one APS, one PCASP,
 and one SMPS. Each instrument was challenged with monodisperse PSL particles spanning two orders of
 magnitude in diameter (100 nm to 3 µm), with the smallest two sizes (100 nm and 152 nm) additionally
 cleaned via DMA selection and the remaining sizes produced from certified PSL suspensions. The
 analysis focuses on two key performance metrics: (1) sizing accuracy, evaluated by comparing each
 instrument's reported peak diameter against the nominal PSL diameter (session 4.1), and (2) counting
 efficiency, evaluated using a leave-one-out reference concentration derived from the ensemble of
 co-located sensors (session 4.2). More discussion was included in the 4.1 and 4.2 sessions. Because the
 instruments differ in measurement principle — optical scattering (POPS, UHSAS, PCASP), aerodynamic
 time-of-flight (APS), and electrical mobility (SMPS) — and in their nominal operating size ranges, not
 every instrument is expected to detect every test particle. Establishing the valid measurement domain for
 each instrument is, therefore, the necessary first step before any quantitative comparison can be made. It
 should be noted that the optical-based instruments and the APS are normally calibrated with PSLs, so no
 “refractive index” or “density” adjustments were applied in the data processing.

 Table 2 summarizes the test matrix and indicates, for each combination of nominal PSL size and
 instrument, whether the particle falls within the instrument's specified detection range. Sizes marked with
 a checkmark (✓) lie comfortably within the manufacturer-specified range and are included in the
 subsequent sizing accuracy and counting efficiency analyses. Entries marked with a tilde (~) indicate
 sizes near the upper or lower detection limit, where reduced bin resolution or declining detection
 efficiency may bias the measurement; results from these points are reported but interpreted with caution.
 Crosses (✗) denote sizes outside the instrument's operating range, for which no data are reported. This
 matrix defines the subset of instruments contributing to the leave-one-out reference at each PSL size and
 frames the cross-instrument comparisons presented in the remainder of this section.

4.1 Sizing Accuracy across Instruments
Sizing accuracy was evaluated by comparing each instrument's measured peak diameter to the nominal
PSL diameter across the test matrix defined in Table 2. For every PSL challenge, the “relevant” peak
edges are defined by a level of 50% from the peak maximum. The following subsections present the
measured-versus-nominal diameter plots for each sensor, beginning with the three POPS units to assess
unit-to-unit reproducibility, followed by the UHSAS, APS, PCASP, and SMPS. For each instrument,
deviations (10% and 20%) from the 1:1 line are shown on the plots and discussed.

4.1.1     POPS Units (x3)

Figure 4(a-c) shows the sizing response of POPS269, POPS308, and POPS434 across the full PSL test
range (100 nm to 3 µm) for four repeat experiments conducted on 13, 15, 18, and 22 May 2026. For each
PSL challenge, the marker indicates the mid-bin diameter corresponding to the averaged peak maximum,
and the error bars span the “relevant” peak edges, defined as the bin diameters at which the count drops to
50% of the peak maximum (or ±10% in size, whichever is wider, with a minimum peak width of four
bins). Reference lines indicate the 1:1 agreement and the ±10% and ±20% deviation envelopes.
(Mei et al. 2020)

Across the full test range, all POPS demonstrate good sizing accuracy and excellent day-to-day
reproducibility: the four repeat measurements at each nominal PSL size are nearly indistinguishable, with
all markers overlapping within the symbol size. The sensor's measured peak diameter agrees with the
nominal PSL size to within ±20% across nearly the entire range, and to within ±10% for most sizes
between 200 nm and 2 µm. Several size-dependent features merit attention:
 • Small-particle end (100-150 nm): The reported peak diameters lie slightly above the 1:1 line, falling
 near the +20% envelope at 100 nm. This behavior is consistent with operation near the lower
 detection limit of the POPS (~135 nm), where bin resolution is coarse, and the lowest detectable bin
 biases the reported peak diameter upward.
 • Mid-range (200-500 nm): Measurements track the 1:1 line closely, generally within ±10%,
 indicating reliable sizing in this range.

 7

 • Mie-resonance region (700-1000 nm): A systematic underestimation is observed, with measured
 diameters falling below the 1:1 line and approaching the -20% envelope near 800-900 nm. This
 deviation is characteristic of optical particle counters operating with a 405 nm laser, where the
 non-monotonic relationship between scattering cross-section and particle diameter (Mie resonances)
 causes ambiguity in the size-to-signal mapping for particles whose diameter is comparable to the laser
 wavelength.
 • Super-micron range (2-3 µm): The sensor recovers good agreement with the nominal size, returning
 to within ±10% of the 1:1 line.

The error bars (peak edges at 50% of maximum) are also informative: they are narrowest in the mid-range
and widen at both ends of the size range, reflecting the coarser bin spacing at the detection limits and the
broader scattering response in the Mie-resonance region. Overall, POPS exhibits stable, reproducible
sizing performance that is well characterized by the expected optical-sizing behavior of a 405 nm
scattering instrument, with the largest deviations confined to the Mie-resonance region near 800-900 nm.

4.1.2     UHSAS

Similar to the POPS plot, Figure 5 shows the sizing response of the UHSAS across the PSL test range for
the four repeat experiments conducted on 13, 15, 18, and 22 May 2026. Data are presented only up to
~1 µm, consistent with the UHSAS's upper detection limit; the 2 µm and 3 µm PSL challenges fall
outside the instrument's specified range and are not reported.(Kupc et al. 2018)

Across its operating range, the UHSAS exhibits excellent sizing accuracy and very high day-to-day
reproducibility:
 • Small-particle end (100-150 nm): The reported peak diameters lie essentially on the 1:1 line, well
 within ±10% of the nominal size. This is a notable advantage of the UHSAS over the 405 nm POPS
 at this end of the range — the high-power 1054 nm laser combined with two stage photodetector
 system account for fine bin resolution of the UHSAS and accurate sizing of sub-200 nm PSL particles
 without the upward bias seen near the POPS detection limit.
 • Mid-range (200-500 nm): Measurements track the 1:1 line very closely, with all four repeats falling
 inside the ±10% envelope. Error bars are narrow, indicating well-resolved, sharply peaked size
 distributions.
 • Upper range (700-1000 nm): A small but systematic underestimation appears, with measured
 diameters falling slightly below the 1:1 line and approaching the -10% envelope near 800-1000 nm.
 This is consistent with the onset of Mie-resonance ambiguity as the particle diameter approaches the
 UHSAS laser wavelength (1054 nm), as well as the reduced bin resolution near the upper end of the
 instrument's range. The deviation, however, remains substantially smaller than that observed for the
 POPS units in the same size region, because the longer UHSAS wavelength shifts the strong Mie
 oscillations to larger particle sizes.

The error bars (peak edges at 50% of maximum) are uniformly narrow across the full range, reflecting the
high size resolution of the UHSAS (~99 bins) and the sharpness of the measured PSL peaks.
Repeatability across the four test days is excellent, with the four markers at each nominal size overlapping
within the symbol size.

Overall, the UHSAS demonstrates the best sizing accuracy among the optical instruments tested, with
measurements agreeing with nominal PSL diameters to within ±10% across its full operational range, and
only minor deviations appear near 1 µm, where Mie-resonance effects begin to influence the sizing
response.

4.1.3 APS

Figure 6 shows the sizing response of the APS across the PSL test range for the four repeat experiments
conducted on 13, 15, 18, and 22 May 2026. Data are presented from 500 nm upward, consistent with the
lower detection limit of the APS; the 100 nm, 150 nm, 200 nm, and 300 nm PSL challenges fall outside
the instrument's specified range and are not reported. (Johnson et al. 2018)

Across its operating range, the APS exhibits good day-to-day reproducibility — the four repeat
measurements at each nominal size overlap closely — but the reported peak diameters are systematically
biased low relative to the nominal PSL size:
 • Lower range (500-700 nm): The measured peak diameters fall well below the 1:1 line, exceeding the
 -20% deviation envelope. At 500 nm and 700 nm the offset corresponds to roughly one to two APS
 bins, which, given the logarithmic bin spacing of the APS (32 channels per decade), translates to a
 ~6-13% downward shift per bin. The observed offset is therefore consistent with a one- to two-bin
 systematic shift in the reported diameter.
 • Mid-range (800-1000 nm): The bias is reduced but still present, with measurements falling between
 the -10% and -20% envelopes. Reproducibility across the four test days remains excellent.
 • Super-micron range (2-3 µm): The systematic underestimation persists, with measured diameters
 near the -20% envelope at 3 µm.

 11

The APS reports aerodynamic diameter rather than physical (geometric) diameter. However, for PSL
spherical particles with a density of nearly 1 g/cc, aerodynamic diameter is the same as geometric one.
The offset of one to two bins across the full size range suggests a calibration or bin-edge assignment issue
in the APS sizing channel, rather than a physical measurement effect. A similar one-to-two-bin low bias
for PSL has been reported in the literature for the APS 3321 and is commonly attributed to
particle-orientation effects during acceleration and to the sensitivity of the time-of-flight inversion to the
assumed Stokes correction.

The error bars are noticeably wider than UHSAS, particularly at 500-700 nm, where the lower edge of the
relevant peak window extends well below the marker. This reflects coarser bin structure and the broader
response function of the APS near its lower size limit, where the time-of-flight signal-to-noise degrades,
and the measured peaks become asymmetric.

Overall, the APS provides reproducible measurements but consistently undersizes PSL particles by
approximately one to two bins (~10-20%) across its full operating range.

4.1.4       PCASP

Figure 7 shows the sizing response of the PCASP across the full PSL test range (100 nm to 3 µm) for the
four repeat experiments conducted on 13, 15, 18, and 22 May 2026. The most prominent feature of the
PCASP results is the wide error bars observed across nearly the entire size range. Unlike the UHSAS,
whose error bars are typically narrower than the marker symbols, the PCASP error bars frequently span
±20% or more around the reported peak diameter. This is a direct consequence of the PCASP's coarse bin
structure: with only ~30 channels distributed across its full size range (~100 nm to 3 µm), the bin width

 12

near each PSL test size is large, and the peak edges at 50% of maximum frequently extend across one or
two adjacent bins on each side. The four-bin minimum peak-width criterion further widens the reported
error bars at sizes where the true PSL response is contained within only one or two bins. (Reid et al. 2003)

Despite the limited size resolution, the PCASP demonstrates reasonable sizing accuracy and good
day-to-day reproducibility:
 • Small-particle end (100-200 nm): The reported peak diameters lie close to the 1:1 line, generally
 within the ±10% envelope. One outlier is observed at 150 nm on 18 May 2026, where the
 measurement falls below the -20% envelope; this is likely the result of the peak being contained
 within a single bin near the lower detection limit, where the fitted mid-bin diameter is highly sensitive
 to the bin-edge assignment.
 • Mid-range (300-700 nm): Measurements track the 1:1 line within the ±10% envelope. Error bars
 remain wide owing to the coarse bin spacing, but the central marker positions are consistent across
 the four test days.
 • Mie-resonance region (800-1000 nm): A modest underestimation is observed, with measured
 diameters falling between the -10% and -20% envelopes. This behavior is qualitatively similar to that
 observed for the POPS and UHSAS in the same-size region and is consistent with the well-known
 Mie-resonance ambiguity in optical particle counters.
 • Super-micron range (2-3 µm): Sizing recovers to within ±10% of the 1:1 line, with reproducible
 markers across the four test days.

We emphasize that the wide error bars reported here primarily reflect the instrument's intrinsic size bin
structure rather than measurement noise or run-to-run variability. The marker positions themselves (the
mid-bin diameters of the peak maxima) are highly reproducible across the four test days, indicating that
the PCASP responds consistently to a given PSL size — its limitation lies in the coarseness with which
that size is reported, not in the stability of the measurement.

Overall, the PCASP provides reliable sizing within ±20% of the nominal PSL diameter across the full test
range, with reproducibility comparable to the higher-resolution optical instruments. However, its coarse
bin structure makes it less suitable than the POPS or UHSAS for applications requiring fine-scale size
discrimination, particularly in the sub-micron range, where the PCASP bin widths are largest relative to
particle diameter.

4.1.5 SMPS

Figure 8 shows the sizing response of the SMPS for the four repeat experiments conducted on
13, 15, 18, and 22 May 2026. Data are presented only for nominal PSL diameters of 100, 150,
200, and 300 nm. Although the SMPS is nominally capable of measurements up to ~1 µm, the

edges were defined as the bin diameters at which the response falls to 50% of the peak maximum (or
±10% in size, whichever is wider, subject to a minimum peak width of four bins). The sensor-reported
number concentration for that PSL size was then computed as the sum of the bin counts within these peak
edges:

The systematic UHSAS undercount above ~700 nm is likely explained by the size-dependent particle loss
in the UHSAS inlet line: super-micron particles are subject to enhanced inertial impaction at bends and
fittings, gravitational settling in horizontal sections, and turbulent deposition along internal surfaces. The
losses scale strongly with particle size, becoming significant for diameters above a few hundred
nanometers in plumbing not specifically designed for super-micron transport. This is a recognized
deficiency of the standard UHSAS inlet design and is consistent with the progressive undercount
observed here, which intensifies monotonically with increasing PSL size from 700 nm to 1 µm.

Overall, the UHSAS demonstrates excellent counting agreement with the leave-one-out reference for
sub-700 nm PSL particles, consistent with its strong sub-micron sensitivity and high size resolution. The
systematic undercount observed for PSL diameters of 700 nm and above is most plausibly attributable to
particle losses in the inlet line rather than to an intrinsic limitation in the optical block's detection
efficiency. For applications requiring quantitative counting of super-micron particles, the UHSAS results
should either be inlet-loss-corrected (e.g., using a calibrated transmission curve) or supplemented by an
instrument with a more transmissive coarse-mode inlet (such as the APS).

the observed undercount of up to a factor of five at 900 nm-1 µm.

characteristic of the APS in this range and the long downward tail produced when a low-count peak bin is

combined with the APS response in its lower size range, not an indication of measurement instability —
the marker positions remain reproducible across the four test days.

design-optimized super-micron range (2-3 µm) and at 500 nm, but substantially undercounts in the
700-1000 nm transition region, where its detection efficiency declines and its sizing response broadens.
This complementary size dependence relative to the UHSAS — which performs well at sub-700 nm but
undercounts above — illustrates a key result of this study: no single instrument provides quantitative
counting across the full sub- to super-micron range, and a composite size distribution drawing on each
instrument's optimal range is required for accurate ensemble characterization.

4.2.5 SMPS

Figure 14 shows the counting-efficiency response of the SMPS across its accessible PSL test range
(100 nm to 300 nm) for the four repeat experiments conducted on 13, 15, 18, and 22 May 2026. As
discussed in Section 4.1.5, PSL diameters above 300 nm are not accessible to the SMPS in this

In contrast to the excellent sizing accuracy demonstrated by the SMPS in Section 4.1.5, the counting
efficiency results show systematic disagreement with the leave-one-out reference. The SMPS markers
consistently lie above the 1:1 line across all four PSL sizes, with the overcount typically falling between
the 1:2/2:1 and 1:5/5:1 envelopes. The wide horizontal error bars across all sizes indicate substantial
sensor-to-sensor disagreement among the contributing instruments in the leave-one-out reference,
particularly at 100 nm and 150 nm where the UHSAS is the only other fully-in-range sensor and the
POPS and PCASP contribute only partial counts owing to their lower-detection-limit behavior (Sections
4.2.1 and 4.2.4). The reference itself is therefore noisy at these sizes, which contributes some scatter to
the SMPS-versus-leave-one-out comparison; however, the direction of the SMPS bias (consistently high)
cannot be explained by reference noise alone.

Likely cause: incomplete multiple-charge correction in the data reduction. The systematic overcount
is most plausibly attributable to an issue in the SMPS data reduction pipeline, specifically the
multiple-charge correction. When PSL particles are atomized, residual material from the suspension
liquid produces a broad sub-200 nm aerosol that is then charge-conditioned in the SMPS neutralizer. The
bipolar charge distribution places a non-negligible fraction of larger particles into doubly- and
triply-charged states, which then pass through the DMA at a smaller apparent (mobility-equivalent)
diameter than their physical diameter. A correctly implemented multiple-charge correction algorithm uses
the measured size distribution at larger diameters to estimate and subtract these multiply-charged
contributions from the smaller-size bins, recovering the true singly-charged size distribution.

5.0 Summary
 This report presents a comparative evaluation of seven co-located aerosol size-distribution sensors —
 three POPS units (POPS269, POPS308, POPS434), one UHSAS, one APS, one PCASP, and one SMPS
 — challenged with monodisperse PSL particles spanning 100 nm to 3 µm. The smallest two sizes
 (100 nm and 150 nm) were generated via DMA classification, and the remaining sizes were produced
 from certified PSL suspensions. Four repeat experiments were conducted on 13, 15, 18, and 22 May 2026
 to assess day-to-day reproducibility. Two performance metrics were evaluated for each instrument:
 (1) sizing accuracy, by comparing the measured peak diameter to the nominal PSL diameter, and
 (2) counting efficiency, by comparing the sensor-reported integrated number concentration to a
 leave-one-out reference computed from the remaining co-located sensors that have the PSL size within
 their valid measurement range.

 Key findings are set out below.

 26

5.1 Sizing Accuracy
 • POPS units (×3): All three POPS units agree with the nominal PSL diameter to within ±20% across
 nearly the entire test range, and to within ±10% for most sizes between 200 nm and 2 µm. Reported
 peak diameters lie slightly above the 1:1 line near the lower detection limit (~135 nm), consistent
 with coarse bin resolution at the limit, and a modest underestimation is observed in the Mie-resonance
 region (~700-1000 nm versus 405 nm laser wavelength).
 • UHSAS: Demonstrates the best sizing accuracy of the optical instruments tested, with measurements
 within ±10% of nominal across its full operational range (100 nm-1 µm). Minor deviations near 1 µm
 reflect the onset of Mie-resonance ambiguity as particles approach the 1054 nm laser wavelength.
 • APS: Reports diameters that are systematically biased low by approximately one to two bins
 (~10–20%) across its full operating range (500 nm-3 µm). Day-to-day reproducibility is excellent, but
 the consistent offset suggests a calibration or bin-edge assignment issue rather than a physical density
 effect. Most probably, this deficiency will be corrected with the next maintenance service/calibration.
 • PCASP: Shows reasonable sizing accuracy (within ±20% of nominal across the test range) and good
 day-to-day reproducibility, but with wide error bars reflecting the instrument's coarse bin structure
 rather than measurement noise.
 • SMPS: Provides the most accurate and reproducible sizing of all instruments tested, with all
 measurements within ±10% of nominal. The accessible range is restricted to ≤300 nm in this
 experiment by flow-balance constraints arising from the 2.5 LPM atomizer aerosol flow.

5.2 Counting Efficiency
 • POPS units (×3): Track the leave-one-out reference within a factor of two across most of the test
 range. A clear, physically expected loss of counting efficiency below ~150 nm is associated with the
 ~135 nm lower detection limit; at 100 nm the particles fall below the detection threshold and only a
 small fraction is registered, while at 150 nm the detection efficiency is rising steeply but has not yet
 reached unity.
 • UHSAS: Excellent agreement with the leave-one-out reference for sub-700 nm particles, but a
 progressive systematic undercount develops above 700 nm, getting beyond the 1:5 envelope at
 900 nm-1 µm. The size-dependent pattern, combined with the preserved sizing accuracy in the same
 range, points to size-dependent particle losses in the inlet line as the dominant mechanism — a design
 limitation of the standard UHSAS inlet size and geometry.
 • APS: Excellent counting agreement in the design-optimized super-micron range (2-3 µm) and at
 500 nm, but a substantial undercount in the 700-1000 nm transition region, where the time-of-flight
 detection efficiency declines and the inversion-algorithm response broadens. The size-driven nature
 of the undercount is supported by ruling out coincidence (high-concentration) and counting-statistics
 (low-concentration) failure modes; concentration may modulate the visibility of the effect but does
 not drive it.
 • PCASP: Reasonable counting agreement within a factor of two across the operating range, with a
 clear, physically expected reduction below ~200 nm associated with the lower detection limit. Wide
 error bars reflect coarse bin structure rather than measurement instability.

 27

 • SMPS: Counting results are anomalous — the SMPS systematically overcounts relative to the
 leave-one-out reference across all four accessible PSL sizes (100-300 nm), with the overcount largest
 at the smallest sizes. The direction, size dependence, and reproducibility of this bias, combined with
 the otherwise excellent sizing performance, point to an issue in the data reduction pipeline — most
 likely an incorrectly applied or misleading multiple-charge correction. SMPS counting results should
 therefore be regarded as provisional pending verification.

5.3 Inter-POPS Reproducibility
 • The three POPS units show near-complete overlap at every PSL size across the full test range,
 typically within a factor of two and often within the symbol size, across nearly four orders of
 magnitude in concentration. No systematic offset is apparent between any pair of units, supporting
 their treatment as a consistent ensemble for multi-sensor deployments without unit-specific correction
 factors.

 5.4 Cross-Instrument Implication
 A key result of this study is that the UHSAS and APS exhibit complementary size-dependent counting
 performance: the UHSAS performs well below ~700 nm but undercounts above, while the APS performs
 well above ~2 µm but undercounts in the 700-1000 nm transition. No single instrument provides
 quantitative counting across the full sub- to super-micron range; a composite size distribution drawing on
 each instrument's optimal range is required for accurate ensemble characterization.

6.0 Lessons Learned and Recommendations for Future
 Deployments
 This intercomparison demonstrated that aerosol size-distribution instruments should be evaluated within
 their valid measurement domains before quantitative comparison. Instruments based on optical scattering,
 aerodynamic time-of-flight, and electrical mobility do not report identical diameter definitions, and direct
 comparison requires careful interpretation. Repeated PSL challenges showed that sizing accuracy and
 counting efficiency are distinct performance metrics; both are needed to evaluate deployment readiness.
 The POPS instruments showed generally good sizing agreement across much of the tested range, while
 near-limit optical sizing and Mie-resonance regions require caution. The UHSAS results indicate that inlet
 transmission losses can significantly affect coarse-particle counting and should be characterized before
 field deployment. The APS results suggest that systematic bin offsets should be investigated through
 additional calibration checks. Overall, future deployments should include pre-deployment PSL validation,
 documented inlet-loss corrections, clear valid-range definitions, and standardized uncertainty reporting.
  • Verify and correct the SMPS multiple-charge correction algorithm. Re-examine the data reduction
 pipeline to confirm that the multiple-charge correction is enabled, algorithm corresponds to used
 neutralizer type (X-ray or rad-based), applied over a sufficient size range to capture doubly- and
 triply-charged contributions, and configured with an appropriate DMA transfer function and bipolar
 charge distribution model for the sheath-to-aerosol flow ratio used. Reprocess and revisit Section
 4.2.5 once corrected.
  • Quantify and apply UHSAS inlet-loss corrections. Develop a calibrated transmission curve for the
 UHSAS inlet line — ideally derived from co-located coarse-mode references such as the APS and
 POPS — to enable inlet-loss correction of UHSAS counts above 700 nm. Consider hardware
 modifications to the UHSAS inlet design for future deployments requiring quantitative counting near
 1 µm.
  • Investigate the APS one-to-two-bin sizing offset. Conduct a follow-up calibration check with PSL
 standards covering the 500 nm-3 µm range to determine whether the offset originates in the
 time-of-flight calibration or the bin-edge assignment. Document the offset and apply a correction
 factor for cross-instrument analyses where APS-reported diameters are compared directly to optical
 or mobility-based measurements.
