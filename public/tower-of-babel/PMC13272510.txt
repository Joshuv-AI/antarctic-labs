Road traffic crash mortality in Southwest Cameroon: a multicenter hospital study
Authors: Otang Thierry Otang, Nana Theophil Chunteng, Brandon Carl Monika Pouekoua, Leslie Tasha Mbapah, Ernest Kikah Ndifor, Ngomba Divine Martin Mokake, Alain Chichom Mefire
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundRoad traffic crashes (RTCs) are a leading cause of injury-related mortality in Africa, where weak pre-hospital and trauma care systems contribute to preventable deaths. In Cameroon, RTCs account for a substantial proportion of trauma admissions, yet hospital-based mortality trends and determinants remain poorly described.MethodsWe conducted a five-year retrospective multicentre observational study of patients admitted following road traffic crashes to three regional referral hospitals in Southwest Cameroon between January 2020 and December 2024. Hospital admission registers and individual patient records were reviewed. All in-hospital deaths were included; while a representative sample of survivors was selected using stratified random sampling by study centre. Data were extracted using a structured tool and analysed descriptively. Trends in admissions and mortality were examined, centre-specific in-hospital mortality rates calculated, and factors independently associated with mortality identified using multivariable regression analysis.Results: A total of 3266 RTC admissions were recorded during the study period, with 153 in-hospital deaths. Overall in-hospital mortality was 4.7%, with significant variation across centres (p < 0.001). Annual deaths increased by approximately 22% over the study period. On multivariable analysis, delayed presentation of 30–60 min (aRR: 2.0), interurban road crashes (aRR: 1.5), severe (aRR: 8.6) and critical injuries (aRR: 9.0), presence of comorbidities (aRR: 1.7), and traumatic head or spinal injuries (aRR: 2.3) were independently associated with increased mortality.DiscussionIn-hospital mortality following road traffic crashes in Southwest Cameroon is high and increasing, with significant variation across referral centres. Mortality is driven by a combination of injury severity, delayed access to care, crash context, and limited emergency and critical care capacity. Strengthening pre-hospital services, improving trauma care systems, and enhancing road safety measures are essential to reduce preventable deaths in similar resource-limited settings.

Introduction

Road traffic crashes (RTCs) remain a leading cause of injury-related morbidity and mortality worldwide, accounting for over one million deaths annually. The burden is disproportionately borne by low-and middle-income countries (LMICs), particularly in Africa, which records the highest road traffic fatality rates globally [1,2]. Rapid urbanisation, increasing motorization and limited enforcement of road safety regulations continue to drive this burden across the continent [3]. In many African settings, the impact of RTCs is compounded by underdeveloped pre-hospital emergency medical services and constrained hospital-based trauma care capacity. Delayed access to definitive care, limited critical care resources and inconsistent trauma systems contribute substantially to preventable deaths following injury [4,5]. Understanding in-hospital outcomes and their determinants is therefore essential for guiding improvements in emergency care delivery.

In Cameroon, RTCs constitute a major public health problem and account for a substantial proportion of trauma-related hospital admissions. Despite this, there is limited published evidence describing temporal trends in RTC-related mortality, variation in outcomes across hospitals, and factors associated with in-hospital deaths. Existing studies primarily focus on injury patterns rather than outcomes relevant to emergency care planning. This study aimed to address these gaps by examining RTC-related mortality among hospitalised patients in three major referral hospitals in Southwest Cameroon over a five-year period. The specific objectives were to: (1) describe the trends in RTC admissions and in-hospital mortality from 2020 to 2024; (2) estimate centre-specific in-hospital mortality rates; and (3) identify patient-, injury-, and system-level factors independently associated with in-hospital mortality.

Methods

Study design and setting

We conducted a retrospective multicentre observational study over a five-year period (January 2020 to December 2024) in Southwest Cameroon, a coastal region with an estimated population of over 1.8 million people. The region is characterized by a mix of urban centres, interurban high ways and rural road networks with significant commercial and commuter traffic. Buea, Kumba and Limbe Regional hospitals serve as the main secondary referral centres for trauma care in the region, receiving patients directly from the crash scenes as well as referrals from peripheral primary health centres. Formal pre-hospital emergency medical services are limited, and most patients are transported to the hospital by private vehicles, commercial motorcycles or police vehicles. While all three hospitals provide 24-hour emergency services and surgical care, access to advanced trauma resources, such as dedicated trauma teams, neurosurgical services, and intensive care units (ICUs), is limited and variable across the centres.

Data sources and collection

Data were obtained from a combination of Emergency Department admission registers and individual patient medical files. Admission registers and patient files are routinely completed by medical doctors at each facility at the time of patient presentation and during in-patient care. For this study, records were reviewed individually by the research team (medical doctors) using a structured data extraction form. Where available, physiological parameters on admission; including heart rate, systolic blood pressure and level of consciousness, were extracted. Extracted data included demographic characteristics, road user category, crash-related factors, injury characteristics, pre-hospital delay, physiological parameters on admission, in-hospital complications, length of hospital stay, and in-hospital mortality. Injury severity was assessed using the Revised Trauma Score (RTS), which is routinely applicable in low-resource settings.

Study population

The sample size was determined based on an expected exposure proportion of 30% among survivors based on a Cameroonian hospital-based study [6], and a minimum detectable risk ratio of 1.8. These parameters yielded a required sample of approximately 195 participants per group. All patients admitted following road traffic crashes during the study period were eligible for inclusion. All in-hospital RTC-related deaths were included as cases in the sample because only 153 were recorded over the five-year study period, falling short of the calculated target. The sampling frame from which the comparison group of survivors was drawn was built from admission records of RTC patients over the same period. This frame was stratified by study site; Limbe Regional Hospital Annex (LRHA), Buea Regional Hospital (BRH) and Kumba Regional Hospital Annex (KRHA) , yielding a total of 1665 eligible survivor records across the three hospitals. To ensure that each site contributed participants in proportion to the size of its local survivor pool, probability-proportional-to-size (PPS) allocation was applied using the formula ni = (Ni/N) × 251, where Ni represented the number of eligible survivors at each site and N the total frame. This produced site-specific quotas of 104 survivors from LRHA, 100 from BRH, and 47 from KRHA. Within each site, systematic random sampling was performed using a sampling interval calculated as k = Ni/ni, yielding intervals of 6.67, 6.61, and 6.62 at LRHA, BRH, and KRHA, respectively, each rounded to the nearest whole number, giving k = 7 across all three sites. A random start was selected from within the first 7 records, and every 7th record thereafter was drawn from the admission register until the site quota was reached. This stratified systematic random sampling procedure yielded a final comparison group of 251 survivors, producing an overall study sample of 404 participants. Records with incomplete key variables, including outcome status and core exposure variables, were excluded from selection.

Data management and analysis

Data were entered into a secure Google Form and exported to Microsoft Excel for cleaning and verification. The cleaned dataset was analysed using SPSS version-29. Descriptive statistics were used to summarise patient characteristics, injury patterns, and outcomes. In-hospital mortality rates were calculated for each centre and for the overall cohort, with the number of in-hospital RTC-related deaths as the numerator and the total number of RTC admissions at each site as the denominator. Univariate log-binomial regression models were first performed within the Generalized Linear Model (GLZM) framework to examine the unadjusted association between each factor and in-hospital mortality. All explanatory variables were then simultaneously entered into a multivariable log-binomial regression model to estimate the independent effect of each variable on in-hospital mortality while adjusting for all others. Results are presented as unadjusted and adjusted risk ratios with 95% confidence intervals. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05 for both stages of analysis.

Ethical considerations

Ethical approval for this study was obtained from the Regional Ethics Committee for Human and Health Research - Southwest (Approval number: 7/CRERSH/SW/C). Administrative authorization was also obtained from the participating hospitals. Patient identifiers were not collected, and all data were handled confidentially in accordance with ethical standards for research involving human subjects.

Results

A total of 3266 patients were admitted for road traffic crash-related injuries across the three hospitals between 2020 and 2024. Annual admissions increased from 525 cases in 2020 to 735 cases in 2023, with a slight decline to 718 cases in 2024. Over the same period, annual in-hospital deaths increased from 27 to 33 cases, representing an approximate 22% relative increase. Despite fluctuations in admission volume, the upward trend in mortality persisted, indicating a disproportionate rise in fatal outcomes relative to case load.

The overall in-hospital mortality rate was 4.7%, with significant variation observed across study centres (p < 0.001). Limbe Regional Hospital Annex recorded the highest mortality (6.1%), followed by Buea Regional Hospital (5.1%), while Kumba Regional Hospital Annex had the lowest mortality (2.1%). On multivariable analysis, several factors remained independently associated with in-hospital mortality. Delayed presentation was a significant predictor, with patients presenting 30–60 min after injury having approximately twice the risk of death compared to those presenting within 30 min (aRR: 2.0). Injury severity demonstrated the strongest association, with severe and critical injuries associated with markedly increased mortality (aRR: 8.6 and 9.0, respectively). Interurban road crashes were associated with higher mortality compared to other road types (aRR: 1.5). Additionally, the presence of comorbidities (aRR: 1.7) and traumatic head or spinal injuries (aRR: 2.3) independently increased the risk of in-hospital death.

Discussion

This multicentre study describes temporal trends, centre-specific mortality and factors independently associated with mortality among hospitalised RTC patients admitted to the three referral secondary-level hospitals in Southwest Cameroon over a five-year period. Our findings demonstrate a rising burden of RTC admissions and deaths, substantial variation in mortality across centres and a combination of patient-level and system-level factors independently associated with in-hospital mortality.

We observed a steady increase in RTC admissions and associated in-hospital deaths between 2020 and 2024, with an approximate 22% rise in annual fatalities over the study period. This increase exceeds the WHO-reported continental rise in road traffic deaths in Africa over a comparable timeframe, underscoring a worsening RTC burden in this setting [1,2]. The rising trend likely reflects rapid urbanization, increasing motorization, weak enforcement of road safety regulations and limited investment in trauma and emergency care systems, which are common challenges across sub-Saharan Africa [3,4]. The slight decline in admissions observed in 2024 did not translate into a proportional reduction in mortality, suggesting that case severity and systemic factors such as delayed presentation and limited critical care capacity may play a greater role than case volume alone. Similar patterns have been reported in other African settings, where improvements in road infrastructure and traffic volume have not been matched by commensurate improvements in emergency medical services (EMS) and hospital-based trauma care [5].

The overall five-year in-hospital mortality rate of 4.7% is comparable to rates reported from other hospital-based RTC studies in LMICs, but remains substantially higher than figures reported from high-income settings [7,8]. Mortality varied significantly between centres, with LRHA and BRH recording higher mortality rates than KRHA. These differences may partly reflect variation in available surgical subspecialties and critical care capacity. BRH which offers neurosurgical services, imaging and has an ICU, serves as a referral centre for patients with severe head and spinal injuries, potentially contributing to a higher case severity profile. LRHA similarly manages critically ill patients requiring intensive care support. In contrast, KRHA lacks ICU capacity hence patients with severe injuries are more likely to be referred onward, which may partly explain the lower in-hospital mortality observed at that centre.

These differences may reflect variations in patient case-mix, injury severity, referral patterns and local availability of trauma care resources such as imaging, intensive care units and specialist surgical services. Inter-centre variation in trauma outcomes has been widely reported in Africa and highlights the absence of standardized trauma systems and uneven distribution of emergency care capacity within regions. Strengthening trauma referral pathways and standardizing emergency care protocols across hospitals may help reduce these disparities.

An important finding of this study is the marked increase in mortality observed even among patients presenting within 30–60 min of injury, a timeframe traditionally considered to fall within the “golden hour” of trauma care. This suggests that, in this setting, early arrival alone may be insufficient to mitigate mortality risk; factors such as high injury severity, lack of on-scene stabilization, prolonged extrication and transport without basic life support may negate the expected protective effect of earlier hospital presentation. Therefore, the concept of the “golden hour” in this context should be interpreted as a system-dependent rather than purely time-based threshold. Similar observations have been reported in other low-resource settings, where the absence of organised pre-hospital care limits the benefits of timely arrival. These findings highlight the need to focus not only on reducing transport time but also on improving the quality of pre-hospital care and early in-hospital resuscitation [9,10].

Injury severity, measured using the Revised Trauma Score, demonstrated the strongest independent association with mortality. Patients with severe and critical injuries had markedly increased risk of death compared to those with mild-moderate injuries, consistent with global trauma literature [11]. Importantly, our findings suggest that the effect of injury severity is compounded by delayed presentation and adverse crash characteristics, highlighting the interplay between injury burden and health system response.

Road type was also independently associated with mortality, with interurban crashes carrying a higher risk of death than rural road crashes. Interurban roads in Cameroon are often characterised by higher travel speeds, poor lighting, limited pedestrian infrastructure and delayed access to emergency services, all of which contribute to more severe injuries and delayed care [12]. The presence of comorbidities independently increased mortality risk, indicating reduced physiological reserve and increased vulnerability to trauma-related complications. This finding aligns with studies from both African and non-African settings demonstrating worse trauma outcomes among patients with underlying chronic conditions [13].

The primary injury pattern was another key determinant of outcome. Traumatic head and spinal injuries were associated with significantly higher mortality compared to limb injuries. Limited access to neurosurgical and critical care services in many African referral hospitals has consistently been associated with poor outcomes following traumatic brain injury, a challenge that remains relevant in the study setting despite the availability of neurosurgical services at only one of the study centres [14].

The findings of this study indicate that in-hospital mortality is driven by a combination of modifiable system-level and patient-level factors within the local context. Key contributors include delayed pre-hospital transport, absence of organized emergency medical services, limited on-scene stabilization, and variable access to critical care resources such as intensive care units and neurosurgical services. In addition, high-speed interurban road use, poor road safety enforcement, and limited public awareness of trauma response further compound injury severity and delay access to definitive care.

Limitations

The findings of this research should be interpreted in light of the following limitations. First, its retrospective design relied on routinely collected hospital records, which may be subject to incomplete or inaccurate documentation. To minimise this, data extraction was performed by trained clinicians using a standardised tool, and records missing key outcome or exposure variables were excluded. Second, some clinically relevant variables, including alcohol use and detailed crash dynamics, were inconsistently recorded and could not be analysed. Third, the study was conducted within a specific regional context in Southwest Cameroon over a defined time period, which may limit generalisability to other settings. Nevertheless, the findings are likely applicable to similar low- and middle-income countries with comparable emergency care system constraints. Finally, although other validated trauma scoring systems such as the ISS, AIS, and TRISS may provide more comprehensive risk stratification, their application was not feasible due to the retrospective design and limited availability of detailed anatomical injury data. The Revised Trauma Score was therefore used as a pragmatic and clinically relevant alternative in this resource-limited setting.

Conclusion

RTC related in-hospital mortality in Southwest Cameroon is high and has increased over the five-year study period, with substantial variation across referral hospitals. Mortality is driven by a convergence of severe injury patterns, delayed and suboptimal pre-hospital care, crash context and limited trauma and critical care capacity. Notably, increased mortality observed even among patients presenting within 30–60 min after injury highlights that early arrival alone is insufficient in the absence of effective pre-hospital stabilisation and timely definitive care. These findings underscore the urgent need to strengthen emergency medical services, improve trauma system organization, expand critical care and neurosurgical capacity, and reinforce road safety measures. Targeted system-level interventions are essential to reduce preventable RTC-related deaths in Cameroon and similar low-resource emergency care settings.

In addition to strengthening trauma care systems, there is a critical need to enhance public health education on road safety. Targeted community-based interventions focusing on safe driving practices, helmet and seatbelt use, and early care-seeking behaviour following road traffic injuries may contribute significantly to reducing both the incidence and severity of RTC-related injuries. Integration of road safety education into broader public health strategies is essential in mitigating the growing burden of road traffic crashes in similar settings.

Dissemination of results

Findings from this study were shared informally with clinicians and hospital administrators at participating centres through departmental discussions and academic meetings. The results were also presented within the Faculty of Health Sciences, University of Buea, as part of an undergraduate medical thesis.
