Hodge Loci and Complex Multiplication via Generalized Symmetries in Calabi-Yau sigma models
Roberta Angius, Roberto Volpato

Abstract
We propose a sigma-model analogue of Hodge loci in the moduli space of geometric Calabi-Yau compactifications, characterized by the emergence of non-trivial rational Hodge endomorphisms, using generalized symmetries. In the CFT description, the complex cohomology is spanned by Ramond-Ramond ground states, the Hodge decomposition is determined by the U ⁡ ( 1 ) × U ⁡ ( 1 ) U(1)\times U(1) R-charges, and the rational structure is provided by BPS boundary states, with polarization induced by the open string Witten index. Hodge loci are identified by the existence of a non-trivial category TDL \mathrm{TDL} of topological defects preserving the 𝒩 = ( 2 , 2 ) \mathcal{N}=(2,2) superconformal algebra and acting invertibly on the spectral-flow generators. At special points on these loci, the category TDL \mathrm{TDL} exhibits additional arithmetic structure and admits embeddings of finite products of number fields with Complex Multiplication, leading to stronger constraints on the boundary states of the theory. Although the construction is general, we analyze in detail the cases of elliptic curves and K ​ 3 K3 surfaces.

Introduction

String theory compactifications on Calabi–Yau manifolds have long provided one of the most promising frameworks for exploring lower-dimensional phenomenology. Despite the extensive progress, one of the most central and still open questions in the community remains how to achieve a deeper and more complete understanding of the physics associated with the various geometries encountered throughout the corresponding moduli spaces. From the point of view of the effective field theories associated with these compactifications, the period vectors of the compact geometry constitute one of the fundamental ingredients, as they determine the exact form of the low-energy effective action through quantities such as the prepotential, the kinetic functions, and the Yukawa couplings

Another way to approach string theory compactifications on Calabi–Yau manifolds is through the corresponding two-dimensional worldsheet theories with target space
\mathcal{M}_{1,9-2d}\times X_{d}

X_{d}

Nevertheless, even within the moduli space of these

Recently, new light has been shed on certain internal regions of the geometric moduli space corresponding to loci where new discrete symmetries, acting on the moduli, are realized

In the present work we propose a sigma-model analogue of these Hodge loci, with the advantage that, from the CFT perspective, a broader class of symmetries becomes available, including non-geometric and non-invertible symmetries.

The second main outcome of the proposal is the definition of Complex Multiplication (CM) type in the CFT setting (Definition
2
in Section
2
).Roughly speaking, this occurs whenever there exists a subcategory
\mathrm{TDL}^{\prime}\subseteq\mathrm{TDL}

\left(\mathcal{H}^{\bullet}(\mathcal{C},\mathbb{Q}),\mathcal{H}^{\bullet}(\mathcal{C},\mathbb{C})\right)

\left(\mathcal{H}^{\bullet}(\mathcal{C},\mathbb{Q}),\mathcal{H}^{\bullet}(\mathcal{C},\mathbb{C})\right)

K_{i}

Returning to the comparison with the geometric case, the Hodge loci in the CFT setting, namely the loci where new generalized symmetries of the type described above appear, can be viewed as loci where the periods satisfy additional algebraic constraints. In this context, the periods are the entries of the R-R charge vectors
\Gamma_{\mathcal{B}}

\mathcal{H}^{\bullet}(\mathcal{C},\mathbb{C})
over the basis of integral homology
\mathcal{H}_{\bullet}(\mathcal{C},\mathbb{Z})
associated with the boundary states. Even when a direct geometric interpretation is not known, the emergence of generalized symmetries should enforce nontrivial algebraic relations among these periods, which should lead to significant simplifications in their structure.

We consider (Euclidean, compact, unitary) 2D superconformal field theories
\mathcal{C}

(2.1)

\begin{split}&\left[L_{m},L_{n}\right]=(m-n)L_{m+n}+\frac{c}{12}(m^{3}-m)\delta_{m+n,0}\\
&\left[L_{m},j_{n}\right]=-nj_{m+n}\\

\displaystyle{\cal A}\supseteq(\mathcal{N}=2)_{c}^{s.f.}\supset(\mathcal{N}=2)_{c}

\displaystyle G(z):=G^{+}(z)+G^{-}(z)\qquad\tilde{G}(z):=\tilde{G}^{+}(z)+\tilde{G}^{-}(z)\ ,

The resulting model has extended space-time supersymmetries, with gravitini corresponding to the R-NS and NS-R spectral flow operator, and admits BPS D-branes that are charged with respect to the massless R-R fields.

Definition:
[Rational/Real Hodge structure]

A rational (real) Hodge structure of weight

Definition:
[Polarization]

A rational (real) Hodge structure is said to be polarized if there exists a polarization, namely a non-degenerate bilinear form

\end{split}

\mathrm{TDL}_{\alpha}
of
\mathrm{TDL}
, such that the corresponding algebra of Hodge endomorphisms

is a CM field with the largest possible
\mathbb{Q}
-dimension
\dim_{\mathbb{Q}}\End_{\mathrm{TDL}_{\alpha}}=\dim_{\mathbb{Q}}W^{\alpha}_{\mathbb{Q}}
.

Let us discuss the properties of this definition and some possible modifications.

1.

While in the standard algebraic geometry one typically considers rational (or integral) Hodge structures on cohomology groups of fixed weight
w
and polarized by the cup product, in generalized geometry, necessary in string theory compactifications, one must instead consider mixed-Hodge structures polarized, in which cohomology groups of different weights are treated simultaneously. Roughly speaking, this generalization allows to treat the middle and the vertical cohomologies on an equal footing. Geometrically, this corresponds to treat simultaneously the middle cohomology of a Calabi–Yau manifold and the middle cohomology of its mirror dual. This generalized setting requires additional care in handling the decomposition of the full rational (or integral) structure into irreducible Hodge substructures, as is necessary, for example, in the identification of Complex Multiplication points. We will discuss this in detail in the case of elliptic curves (Section
3.1
) and K3 surfaces (Section
3.2
). Note that considering the full mixed Hodge structure, rather than only the individual fixed-weight cohomologies as in standard geometry, is not merely an abstract mathematical generalization, but is in fact required by string theory. Indeed, the presence of the B-field leads to a moduli space that is more naturally described in terms of generalized Calabi–Yau structures
[
43
]
(see also
[
44
]
for K3 surfaces), rather than in terms of separate symplectic and complex structures.

2.

In the case of non-linear sigma models on elliptic curves (see section
3.1
) and CY
3
-folds with full
SU(3)
holonomy, the space
\mathcal{H}^{\bullet}(\mathcal{C},\mathbb{C})
is the orthogonal sum of the middle and the vertical cohomology, that form two (not necessarily irreducible) Hodge substructures. In these cases, our definition of CM SCFT applies straightforwardly, while for K3 surfaces, complex tori of dimension at least
4
, and for CY
3
-folds with restricted holonomy, such as
K3\times T^{2}
, one needs some refinement. The main difference is that, in these cases, the generic chiral algebra of
\mathcal{C}
is larger than just a single
\mathcal{N}=(2,2)
superVirasoro. In fact, different BPS boundary states and topological defects might preserve different
\mathcal{N}=(2,2)
subalgebras of the full chiral algebra, so that the very definition of the Hodge structure on the space of R-R ground fields becomes ambiguous. A second complication arises for target spaces with even complex dimension, at the special loci in the moduli space where the intersection
\mathcal{H}^{d/2,d/2}(\mathcal{C},\mathbb{C})\cap\mathcal{H}(\mathcal{C},\mathbb{Q})
is non-empty. Each non-zero element in this intersection (i.e., a
rational Hodge class
) generates a
1
-dimensional subspace of
\mathcal{H}(\mathcal{C},\mathbb{Q})
that is by itself a trivial irreducible Hodge substructure. Due to these
1
-dimensional irreducible components in the Hodge structure, the corresponding
\mathcal{C}
cannot be a CM sigma model, according to our definition above. Nevertheless, the algebra of Hodge endomorphisms in these models can contain large CM fields, so it seems more useful to slightly modify our definition so as to accommodate these cases. As a prototypical example where both such issues occur, in section
3.2
we propose a suitable definition of complex multiplication for K3 sigma models. We leave the detailed treatment of the other cases (complex tori,
K3\times T^{2}
, …) to future work.

3.

Our construction is based on the assumption that the charges of BPS D-branes span the whole space of R-R ground fields. Let us spell this assumption more explicitly. We consider the set of all BPS D-branes in our SCFT. Different elements in this set might preserve different subsets of spacetime supercharges, so that the superposition of such boundary states might not, in general, correspond to a BPS configuration by itself. Then, we consider the set of R-R charge vectors
\Gamma_{\cal B}
associated with these D-branes. Our assumption is that, by taking
\mathbb{Z}
-,
\mathbb{Q}
-, or
\mathbb{C}
-linear combinations of such charge vectors, the corresponding lattice or vector space has the same dimension as the whole space of R-R ground states. This is sufficient for us, because on one hand we know that fusion with topological defect lines in
\mathrm{TDL}
yields a permutation on the set of BPS D-branes, and therefore a permutation on the corresponding set of R-R charges. On the other hand, we know that topological defects act linearly on the space of R-R ground states, so the permutation of the BPS brane charges is not a generic one – it is induced by a linear transformation of the corresponding lattice or vector space.

We believe that this assumption holds for all
\mathcal{N}=(2,2)
SCFTs of small central charge
c\leq 9
, or at least we do not know of any counterexample. For higher central charges, this assumption might in general fail. For example, consider a non-linear sigma model with target space a smooth Calabi-Yau
4
-fold
X
with
h^{2,1}\neq 0
. A BPS D-brane charged with respect to a R-R field in a
(2,1)
-Hodge component would have to wrap a supersymmetric
3
-cycle in
X
, but it is known that there are no such cycles
[
45
]
. In SCFT language, this statement follows simply by noticing that such a field would not satisfy either the
A
-type nor the
B
-type gluing conditions. So, unless that are different copies of the
\mathcal{N}=(2,2)
superconformal algebra, we conclude that all BPS boundary states are neutral with respect to such a field.
4
4

4

In every
\mathcal{N}=(2,2)
sigma model with target space a torus of complex dimension
\geq 2
, there are always some R-R fields that do not satisfy either the
A
-type or
B
-type boundary conditions. In this case, however, one always has multiple inequivalent copies of the
\mathcal{N}=(2,2)
superconformal algebra, so by varying which subalgebra is preserved by the gluing conditions, one can always span the whole space of R-R ground fields. The presence of multiple
\mathcal{N}=(2,2)
algebras leads to ambiguities in the definition of the Hodge grading; in the present article, we discuss this issue only in the case of K3 sigma models.
If this assumption does not hold, one needs to modify our proposal by restricting to the subspace of R-R ground fields that is spanned by the set of BPS charges.

\mathbb{Q}+\rho\mathbb{Q}
. One can show that if
x=\tau
or
\rho
is a CM point, then the intersection
(\mathbb{Q}+x\mathbb{Q})\cap S^{1}
contains infinitely many distinct points (see appendix
B.2
).

The action of
{\cal L}_{\gamma_{\tau},\gamma_{\rho}}
on the states
|p,\tilde{p}\rangle
with non-zero winding momentum is, in general, non-invertible. Indeed, the rational transformations
(\gamma_{\tau},\gamma_{\rho})
do not map
\Gamma^{2,2}
to itself, but to a lattice

(3.42)

\displaystyle(\gamma_{\rho}^{-1})^{t}\Gamma\gamma_{\tau}^{-1}=\left\{(\gamma_{\rho}^{-1})^{t}\begin{pmatrix}w_{2}&w_{1}\\
-n_{1}&n_{2}\end{pmatrix}\gamma^{-1}_{\tau}\mid n_{1},n_{2},w_{1},w_{2}\in\mathbb{Z}\right\}\subset\Gamma^{2,2}\otimes\mathbb{Q}

that is still even unimodular.
The action on the states
|p,\tilde{p}\rangle
is

(3.43)

\displaystyle\hat{\cal L}_{\gamma_{\tau},\gamma_{p}}|p,\tilde{p}\rangle=\begin{cases}\langle{\cal L}_{\gamma_{\tau},\gamma_{\rho}}\rangle\xi_{\gamma_{\tau},\gamma_{\rho}}(p,\tilde{p})|p^{\prime},\tilde{p}^{\prime}\rangle&\text{if }(p^{\prime},\tilde{p}^{\prime})\in\Gamma^{2,2}\cap(\gamma_{\rho}^{-1})^{t}\Gamma\gamma_{\tau}^{-1}\\
0&\text{otherwise,}\end{cases}

where

(3.44)

\displaystyle p^{\prime}=g_{L}({\cal L}_{\gamma_{\tau},\gamma_{\rho}})p\ ,\qquad\tilde{p}^{\prime}=g_{R}({\cal L}_{\gamma_{\tau},\gamma_{\rho}})\tilde{p}\ ,

\langle{\cal L}_{\gamma_{\tau},\gamma_{\rho}}\rangle
is the quantum dimension
and
\xi_{\gamma_{\tau},\gamma_{\rho}}(p,\tilde{p})
is a suitable phase, that is determined only up to multiplication by a
U(1)_{w-m}^{4}
phase. In fact, for each pair
(\gamma_{\tau},\gamma_{\rho})\in SO^{+}(2,2,\mathbb{Q})
satisfying (
3.37
), there are actually infinitely many topological defects, related to each other by fusion with invertible defects in
U(1)_{w-m}^{4}
.

One can verify that the quantum dimension
\langle{\cal L}_{\gamma_{\tau},\gamma_{\rho}}\rangle
is always a positive integer. More precisely, one has
\langle{\cal L}_{\gamma_{\tau},\gamma_{\rho}}\rangle=\langle{\cal L}_{\gamma_{\tau},1}\rangle\langle{\cal L}_{1,\gamma_{\rho}}\rangle
, where
\langle{\cal L}_{\gamma_{\tau},1}\rangle
and
\langle{\cal L}_{1,\gamma_{\rho}}\rangle
are the smallest positive integers such that
\langle{\cal L}_{\gamma_{\tau},1}\rangle\gamma_{\tau}
and
\langle{\cal L}_{1,\gamma_{\rho}}\rangle\gamma_{\rho}
are integral matrices. See appendix
B.3
for a proof.

The action of
\hat{\cal L}_{\gamma_{\tau},\gamma_{\rho}}
on the R-R ground fields is

(3.45)

\displaystyle\hat{\cal L}_{\gamma_{\tau},\gamma_{\rho}}|s,\tilde{s}\rangle=\langle{\cal L}_{\gamma_{\tau},\gamma_{\rho}}\rangle g_{L}({\cal L}_{\gamma_{\tau},\gamma\rho})^{s}g_{R}({\cal L}_{\gamma_{\tau},\gamma\rho})^{\tilde{s}}|s,\tilde{s}\rangle\ .

Explicitly, the action on the components
\mathcal{H}^{r,s}(\mathcal{C},\mathbb{C})
is

(3.46)

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}^{0,0}(\mathcal{C},\mathbb{C}):

\displaystyle\hat{\cal L}_{\gamma_{\tau},\gamma_{\rho}}|-\tfrac{1}{2},+\tfrac{1}{2}\rangle=\langle{\cal L}_{\gamma_{\tau},\gamma_{\rho}}\rangle(\gamma\rho+\delta)|-\tfrac{1}{2},+\tfrac{1}{2}\rangle\ ,

(3.47)

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}^{1,1}(\mathcal{C},\mathbb{C}):

\displaystyle\hat{\cal L}_{\gamma_{\tau},\gamma_{\rho}}|+\tfrac{1}{2},-\tfrac{1}{2}\rangle=\langle{\cal L}_{\gamma_{\tau},\gamma_{\rho}}\rangle(\gamma\bar{\rho}+\delta)|+\tfrac{1}{2},-\tfrac{1}{2}\rangle\ ,

(3.48)

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}^{1,0}(\mathcal{C},\mathbb{C}):

\displaystyle\hat{\cal L}_{\gamma_{\tau},\gamma_{\rho}}|+\tfrac{1}{2},+\tfrac{1}{2}\rangle=\langle{\cal L}_{\gamma_{\tau},\gamma_{\rho}}\rangle(c\tau+d)|+\tfrac{1}{2},+\tfrac{1}{2}\rangle\ ,

(3.49)

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}^{0,1}(\mathcal{C},\mathbb{C}):

\displaystyle\hat{\cal L}_{\gamma_{\tau},\gamma_{\rho}}|-\tfrac{1}{2},-\tfrac{1}{2}\rangle=\langle{\cal L}_{\gamma_{\tau},\gamma_{\rho}}\rangle(c\bar{\tau}+d)|-\tfrac{1}{2},-\tfrac{1}{2}\rangle\ .

The transformations on the B-type D-brane charges is

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\sqrt{\rho_{2}}}\begin{pmatrix}k_{0}&k_{2}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\
\rho&\bar{\rho}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\hat{\cal L}_{\gamma_{\tau},\gamma_{\rho}}|+-\rangle\\
i\hat{\cal L}_{\gamma_{\tau},\gamma_{\rho}}|-+\rangle\end{pmatrix}=\frac{\langle{\cal L}_{\gamma_{\tau},\gamma_{\rho}}\rangle}{2\sqrt{\rho_{2}}}\begin{pmatrix}k_{0}&k_{2}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\gamma\bar{\rho}+\delta&\gamma\rho+\delta\\
\rho(\gamma\bar{\rho}+\delta)&\bar{\rho}(\gamma\rho+\delta)\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}|+-\rangle\\
i|-+\rangle\end{pmatrix}

(3.50)

\displaystyle=\frac{\langle{\cal L}_{\gamma_{\tau},\gamma_{\rho}}\rangle}{2\sqrt{\rho_{2}}}\begin{pmatrix}k_{0}&k_{2}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}-\gamma\rho+2\gamma\Re\rho+\delta&-\gamma\bar{\rho}+2\gamma\Re\rho+\delta\\
\gamma|\rho|^{2}+\delta\rho&\gamma|\rho|^{2}+\delta\bar{\rho}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}|+-\rangle\\
i|-+\rangle\end{pmatrix}

(3.51)

\displaystyle=\frac{\langle{\cal L}_{\gamma_{\tau},\gamma_{\rho}}\rangle}{2\sqrt{\rho_{2}}}\begin{pmatrix}k_{0}&k_{2}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\alpha&-\gamma\\
-\beta&\delta\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\
\rho&\bar{\rho}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}|+-\rangle\\
i|-+\rangle\end{pmatrix}

where we used that the relation (
3.37
) implies
\alpha=\delta+2\gamma\Re\rho
and
\beta=-\gamma|\rho|^{2}
.
Similarly, the transformation on the A-type D-brane charges is

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\sqrt{\tau_{2}}}\begin{pmatrix}k_{\alpha}&k_{\beta}\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\
\bar{\tau}&\tau\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\hat{\cal L}_{\gamma_{\tau},\gamma_{\rho}}|++\rangle\\
-i\hat{\cal L}_{\gamma_{\tau},\gamma_{\rho}}|--\rangle\end{pmatrix}=

(3.52)

\displaystyle=\frac{\langle{\cal L}_{\gamma_{\tau},\gamma_{\rho}}\rangle}{2\sqrt{\tau_{2}}}\begin{pmatrix}k_{\alpha}&k_{\beta}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}a&-c\\
-b&d\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\
\bar{\tau}&\tau\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\hat{\cal L}_{\gamma_{\tau},\gamma_{\rho}}|++\rangle\\
-i\hat{\cal L}_{\gamma_{\tau},\gamma_{\rho}}|--\rangle\end{pmatrix}\ .

Thus, because the matrices

(3.53)

\displaystyle\langle{\cal L}_{\gamma_{\tau},\gamma_{\rho}}\rangle\begin{pmatrix}\alpha&-\gamma\\
-\beta&\delta\end{pmatrix}\ ,\qquad\langle{\cal L}_{\gamma_{\tau},\gamma_{\rho}}\rangle\begin{pmatrix}a&-c\\
-b&d\end{pmatrix}

have integral entries, one obtains that the transformation
\hat{\cal L}_{\gamma_{\tau},\gamma_{\rho}}
maps the lattice
\mathcal{H}^{\bullet}(\mathcal{C},\mathbb{Z})
to itself, as expected, and therefore it is a Hodge endomorphism
\mathsf{L}_{\gamma_{\tau},\gamma_{\rho}}
.

Thus, the algebra generated by
\mathbb{Q}
-linear combinations of
\mathsf{L}_{\gamma_{\tau},\gamma_{\rho}}
corresponding to defects
{\cal L}_{\gamma_{\tau},\gamma_{\rho}}
is isomorphic to

(3.54)

\displaystyle K_{\tau}\times K_{\rho}\ ,

where the field
K_{x}
, for a given element
x
in the upper half-plane, is defined by

(3.55)

\displaystyle K_{x}:=\begin{cases}\mathbb{Q}+x\mathbb{Q}&\text{if $x$ is CM}\ ,\\
\mathbb{Q}&\text{if $x$ is not CM}\ ,\end{cases}

where an element
x\in\mathbb{C}
,
\imag x>0
, is CM if it is the solution of a quadratic equation with rational coefficients.

The Hodge loci in the moduli space of sigma models on elliptic curves are now easily determined. Indeed, the algebra
K_{\tau}\times K_{\rho}
is non-trivial if and only if at least one of the two moduli
\tau
and
\rho
is a CM point. Thus, a Hodge locus is given by a set of
(\tau,\rho)
with either
\tau
a fixed CM point and
\rho
any point in the upper half-plane, or vice versa.

Furthermore, the algebra
K_{\tau}\times K_{\rho}
has maximal dimension over
\mathbb{Q}
, i.e.
\dim_{\mathbb{Q}}(K_{\tau}\times K_{\rho})=4
, if and only if both
\tau
and
\rho
are CM points. We conclude that a supersymmetric sigma model
\mathcal{C}
on
T^{2}
is a CM SCFT, according to our definition, if and only if both moduli
\tau
and
\rho
are CM points. Notice that these are isolated points in the moduli space.

Let us also recall that the torus sigma model
\mathcal{C}
is rational if and only if it is a CM SCFT, and in addition the two CM fields are isomorphic
K_{\tau}\cong K_{\rho}
(see for example
[
48
]
). This is a strictly stronger condition than just being CM: for example, the torus model with
\tau=i
and
\rho=e^{\frac{2\pi i}{3}}
is a CM SCFT, but it is not rational.

3.2
K3 surfaces

In this section we analyze the class of Hodge loci in the moduli space of
2d
non-linear-sigma models with target space a K3 surface where non-trivial Hodge endomorphisms, induced by topological defects in the category
\mathrm{TDL}
, emerge. We also describe the conditions under which a subset of these loci correspond to CFTs of complex multiplication type.

Let us begin with a brief discussion of the relation between the R-R ground state sector
\mathcal{H}^{\bullet}(\mathcal{C},\mathbb{C})
of these models and the complex cohomology
H^{\bullet}(K3,\mathbb{C})
of the underlying
K3
target space. Although the two are isomorphic as complex vector spaces,
\mathcal{H}^{\bullet}(\mathcal{C},\mathbb{C})\cong H^{\bullet}(K3,\mathbb{C})
, their associated Hodge decompositions, and consequently the corresponding rational Hodge structures and polarizations, do not exactly coincide but they can be identified in a non-trivial way.
First of all, the moduli of the sigma model depend on the geometric data given by the complex structure and the Kähler form together with the closed
2
-form B-field, which are naturally unified within the framework of generalized geometry
[
44
]
. A generalized
K3
structure consists of a pair of two poly-forms
(\varphi,\varphi^{\prime})\in\mathcal{A}^{2\ast}_{\mathbb{C}}(X)
, on the space of even-degree complex forms of the
K3
manifold
X
, satisfying the following conditions:

(3.56)

\begin{split}&\bullet\,\,d\varphi\,=\,0\,\,\,,\,\,\,d\varphi^{\prime}\,=\,0\,\\
&\bullet\,\,\langle\varphi,\varphi\rangle\,=\,0\,\,\,,\,\,\,\langle\varphi^{\prime},\varphi^{\prime}\rangle\,=\,0\,,\\
&\bullet\,\,\langle\varphi,\overline{\varphi}\rangle\,>\,0\,\,\,,\,\,\,\langle\varphi^{\prime},\overline{\varphi}^{\prime}\rangle\,>\,0\,,\\
&\bullet\,\,\langle\varphi,\varphi^{\prime}\rangle\,=\,0\quad(\text{K\"{a}hler})\,,\\
&\bullet\,\,\langle\varphi,\bar{\varphi}\rangle=\langle\varphi^{\prime},\bar{\varphi}^{\prime}\rangle\quad(\text{hyperk\"{a}hler})\,,\\
\end{split}

where

(3.57)

\langle\varphi,\psi\rangle\equiv\,\int_{X}\left[-\varphi_{0}\wedge\psi_{4}+\varphi_{2}\wedge\psi_{2}-\varphi_{4}\wedge\psi_{0}\right]\,\,\in\,\,\mathbb{C}

is the Mukai paring between the pair of elements
\varphi\,,\,\psi\,\in\mathcal{A}^{2\ast}_{\mathbb{C}}
.
Such structure generalizes the notions of symplectic and complex structures on
X
, in the sense that both can be viewed as particular cases of generalized Calabi–Yau structures which are respectively
\varphi=e^{i\omega}
and
\varphi^{\prime}=\sigma
with
\omega
a standard symplectic structure in
X
and
\sigma
the unique (up to a phase)
(2,0)
-form normalized so that
\int_{X}\sigma\wedge\bar{\sigma}=Vol(X)
. All the other possible generalized Calabi-Yau structures can be obtained as the B-field transform of these two simple structures defined via the following exterior product:

(3.58)

e^{B}\cdot\varphi=\left(1+B+\frac{1}{2}B\wedge B\right)\wedge\varphi\,.

For each generalized Calabi-Yau structure
\varphi
, its real and imaginary parts span a real
2
-plane
P_{\varphi}
in the vector space
\mathcal{A}^{2\ast}_{\mathbb{C}}
. Analogously, the real and imaginary parts of the associated cohomology class
\left[\varphi\right]
identify a
2
-plane
P_{\left[\varphi\right]}\subset H^{\bullet}(X,\mathbb{R})
. The
2
-plane
P_{\varphi}
(or
P_{\left[\varphi\right]}
) comes with a natural orientation from the ordered basis
(Re\varphi,Im\varphi)
, and all its vectors are positive with respect to the Mukai paring. Given a pair of generalized structures
(\varphi,\varphi^{\prime})\in\mathcal{A}^{2\ast}_{\mathbb{C}}
, the hyperkähler condition in (
3.56
) translates into the requirement that
P_{\left[\varphi\right]}
and
P_{\left[\varphi^{\prime}\right]}
are orthogonal (Kähler condition) and
\langle\varphi,\overline{\varphi}\rangle=\langle\varphi^{\prime},\overline{\varphi}^{\prime}\rangle
.

Thus, to any generalized
K3
structure
(\varphi,\varphi^{\prime})
on
X
, we can associate a pointwise oriented
4
-plane
\Pi_{(\varphi,\varphi^{\prime})}\in\mathcal{A}^{2\ast}(M)
spanned by the two
2
-planes
P_{\varphi}
and
P_{\varphi^{\prime}}
. In the same way we can define the oriented positive
4
-plane
\Pi_{\left(\left[\varphi\right],\left[\varphi^{\prime}\right]\right)}
spanned by
P_{\left[\varphi\right]}
and
P_{\left[\varphi^{\prime}\right]}
inside the cohomology
H^{\bullet}(X,\mathbb{R})
. Importantly, each plane
\Pi_{(\varphi,\varphi^{\prime})}
is always a
B
-field transform of the geometric
4
-plane
\Pi_{(\sigma,e^{i\omega})}
. This means that the positive
4
-plane inside the real cohomology is the
B
-field transform of the 4-plane spanned by

(3.59)

\Pi_{(\left[\sigma\right],\left[e^{i\omega}\right])}\,=\,\langle\,Re\,\sigma,\,Im\,\sigma,\,\omega,\,1-\frac{1}{2}\omega^{2}\,\rangle

where
\sigma
and
\bar{\sigma}
are the unique
(2,0)
- and
(0,2)
-forms,
\omega
is the
(1,1)
Kähler form and
1-\frac{1}{2}\omega^{2}
is a combination of the
0
- and the
4
-form.

Treating the
(0,0)
- and the
(2,2)
-form on an equal footing with the
2
-forms, the rational structure on the complex cohomology
V_{\mathbb{C}}=H^{\bullet}(X,\mathbb{C})
is induced by the
\mathbb{Q}
-extension of the Mukai lattice:

(3.60)

H^{\bullet}(X,\mathbb{Z})\cong U^{\oplus 4}\oplus E_{8}^{\oplus 2}(-),

where
U
denotes the hyperbolic lattice

(3.61)

U=\left(\begin{matrix}0&1\\
1&0\end{matrix}\right)

of signature
(1,1)
, while
E_{8}
is the rank-
8
negative-definite lattice defined by the
E_{8}
Cartan matrix. The resulting rational structure
\left(H^{\bullet}(X,\mathbb{Q}),H^{\bullet}(X,\mathbb{C})\right)
has overall signature
(4,20)
, with positive directions identified by
\Pi_{\left(\left[\varphi\right],\left[\varphi^{\prime}\right]\right)}
.

What has been described so far corresponds to the geometric side of the picture. Let us now turn to the physical perspective and consider the non-linear sigma model
\mathcal{C}
with target space a K3 surface, which realizes the small
\mathcal{N}=(4,4)
superconformal algebra with central charge
(c,\tilde{c})=(6,6)
. The small
\mathcal{N}=(4,4)
superconformal algebra is obtained by extending the
\mathcal{N}=(2,2)
algebra in (
2.1
) by the NS-NS spectral flow generators
V_{(\pm 1,0)}
and
\tilde{V}_{(0,\pm 1)}
corresponding to the
su(2)_{1}
currents
J^{\pm}
and
\tilde{J}^{\pm}
. The generators of the holomorphic
\mathcal{N}=4
algebra are the four supercurrents
G^{\pm}(z),\,G^{\prime\pm}(z)
of conformal weight
(\frac{3}{2},0)
, the stress energy tensor
T(z)
and the
su(2)_{1}
Kac-Moody algebra with currents
J^{3}(z),J^{\pm}(z)
. The commutation relations are the following:

(3.62)

\begin{split}\left[L_{m},L_{n}\right]=(m-n)L_{m+n}+&\frac{1}{2}(m^{3}-m)\delta_{m+n,0}\\
\left[L_{m},J^{3}_{n}\right]=-nJ^{3}_{m+n}\quad\quad\quad&\quad\quad\quad\left[L_{m},J^{\pm}_{n}\right]=-nJ^{\pm}_{m+n}\\
\left[2J^{3}_{m},2J^{3}_{n}\right]=2m\delta_{m+n,0}\quad\quad\quad&\quad\quad\quad\left[J^{\pm},J^{\pm}\right]=0\\
\left[J^{3}_{m},J^{\pm}_{n}\right]=\pm J^{\pm}_{m+n}\quad\quad\quad&\quad\quad\quad\left[J^{+}_{m},J^{-}_{n}\right]=m\delta_{m+n,0}+2J^{3}_{m+n}\\
\left[L_{m},G_{r}^{\pm}\right]=\left(\frac{m}{2}-r\right)G^{\prime\pm}_{m+r}\quad\quad\quad&\quad\quad\quad\left[L_{m},G_{r}^{\pm}\right]=\left(\frac{m}{2}-r\right)G^{\prime\pm}_{m+r}\\
\left[J^{3}_{m},G^{\pm}_{r}\right]=\pm\frac{1}{2}G^{\pm}_{m+r}\quad\quad\quad&\quad\quad\quad\left[J^{3}_{m},G^{\prime\pm}_{r}\right]=\mp\frac{1}{2}G^{\prime\pm}_{m+r}\\
\left[J^{\pm}_{m},G^{\mp}_{r}\right]=\pm\frac{1}{2}G^{\prime\mp}_{m+r}\quad\quad\quad&\quad\quad\quad\left[J^{\pm}_{m},G^{\pm}_{r}\right]=0\\
\left[J^{\pm}_{m},G^{\prime\pm}_{r}\right]=\mp\frac{1}{2}G^{\pm}_{m+r}\quad\quad\quad&\quad\quad\quad\left[J^{\pm}_{m},G^{\prime\mp}_{r}\right]=0\\
\left\{G^{\pm}_{r},G^{\prime\mp}_{s}\right\}=2(s-r)J^{\pm}_{s+r}\quad\quad\quad&\quad\quad\quad\left\{G^{\pm}_{r},G^{\prime\pm}_{s}\right\}=0\\
\left\{G_{r}^{+},G_{s}^{-}\right\}=\left\{G^{\prime+}_{r},G^{\prime-}_{s}\right\}=2L_{r+s}&\pm(r-s)J^{3}_{r+s}+2\left(r^{2}-\frac{1}{4}\right)\delta_{r+s,0}\\
\end{split}

where
r,\,s\in\frac{1}{2}+\mathbb{Z}
in the NS sector and
r,\,s\in\mathbb{Z}
in the R sector.

This algebra contains a whole family of
\mathcal{N}=(2,2)
subalgebras parameterized by
S^{2}\times S^{2}
, where each
S^{2}
corresponds to the choice of a
\mathcal{N}=1
subalgebra and a
u(1)
inside the
su(2)
R-symmetry algebra in the holomorphic and anti-holomorphic sectors.
The spectrum of any
K3

24
R-R ground states transforming in the massless unitary representations of the
\mathcal{N}=(4,4)
algebra. They consists of a single
(\mathbf{2},\mathbf{2})
multiplet with quantum numbers
\left(h,q_{J^{3}_{0}};\tilde{h},\tilde{q}_{\tilde{J}^{3}_{0}}\right)=\left(\frac{1}{4},\frac{1}{2};\frac{1}{4},\frac{1}{2}\right)
, which corresponding operators are the four R-R spectral flow generators, together with
20
singlets labeled by
\left(h,q_{J^{3}_{0}};\tilde{h},\tilde{q}_{\tilde{J}^{3}_{0}}\right)=\left(\frac{1}{4},0;\frac{1}{4},0\right)

Once a specific
\mathcal{N}=(2,2)
subalgebra is chosen inside the
\mathcal{N}=(4,4)
algebra by fixing the
u(1)
currents
j
and
\tilde{j}
, one can endow the
24

\mathcal{H}(\mathcal{C},\mathbb{C})
of R-R ground states with an
(r,s)

R-charges, as defined in (

and
\tilde{j}_{0}=2\tilde{J}^{3}_{0}

24
R-R ground states are now labeled by the quantum numbers
\left(h,q;\tilde{h},\tilde{q}\right)=\left(\frac{1}{4},\pm 1;\frac{1}{4},\pm 1\right)
and
\left(h,q;\tilde{h},\tilde{q}\right)=\left(\frac{1}{4},0;\frac{1}{4},0\right)

(3.63)

\mathcal{H}(\mathcal{C},\mathbb{C})\,=\,\mathcal{H}^{2,2}(\mathcal{C},\mathbb{C})\,\oplus\,\mathcal{H}^{2,0}(\mathcal{C},\mathbb{C})\,\oplus\,\mathcal{H}^{1,1}(\mathcal{C},\mathbb{C})\,\oplus\,\mathcal{H}^{0,2}(\mathcal{C},\mathbb{C})\,\oplus\,\mathcal{H}^{0,0}(\mathcal{C},\mathbb{C}),

where

(3.64)

\begin{split}&\mathcal{H}^{2,2}(\mathcal{C},\mathbb{C})\,\oplus\,\mathcal{H}^{2,0}(\mathcal{C},\mathbb{C})\,\oplus\,\mathcal{H}^{0,2}(\mathcal{C},\mathbb{C})\,\oplus\,\mathcal{H}^{0,0}(\mathcal{C},\mathbb{C})=\mathbb{C}\,\big|\frac{1}{4},\pm\frac{1}{2};\frac{1}{4},\pm\frac{1}{2}\big\rangle\\
&\mathcal{H}^{1,1}(\mathcal{C},\mathbb{C})\,=\,\bigoplus_{i=1}^{20}\mathbb{C}\,\big|\frac{1}{4},0;\frac{1}{4},0\big\rangle_{i}\end{split}

and Hodge vector
(h^{2,2},h^{2,0},h^{1,1},h^{0,2},h^{0,0})=(1,1,20,1,1)
.

As explained in the general construction of Section
2
, in order to endow the complex vector space
\mathcal{H}(\mathcal{C},\mathbb{C})
with a rational structure, we use
BPS
boundary states. These can be defined in the closed string sector as solutions
|\mathcal{B}\rangle
to the gluing conditions:

(3.65)

\begin{split}&\left(L_{n}-\Omega(\tilde{L}_{-n})\right)|\mathcal{B}\rangle=0\quad\quad\forall\,\,m\,\in\,\mathbb{Z}\\
&\left(J_{m}^{i}-\Omega(\tilde{J}^{j}_{-m})\right)|\mathcal{B}\rangle=0\quad\quad\forall\,\,m\,\in\,\mathbb{Z}\\
&\left(G^{I}_{r}-\Omega(\tilde{G}^{K}_{-r})\right)|\mathcal{B}\rangle=0\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\forall\,\,r\,\in\,\mathbb{Z}/2\\
\end{split}

where
I,K
label the four surpercurrents in the holomorphic and anti-holomorphic sectors, while
\Omega
denotes an automorphism of the full
\mathcal{N}=(4,4)
superconformal algebra

(3.66)

\Omega\,\,\,:\,\,\,sVir_{\mathcal{N}=(4,4)}\,\,\mapsto\,\,sVir_{\mathcal{N}=(4,4)}

The automorphism group of the (anti-)holomorphic
\mathcal{N}=4
algebra is
SU(2)_{inn}\times SU(2)_{out}
, under which the four supercurrents transform in the
(\mathbf{2},\mathbf{2})
representation. Here,

is generated by the zero modes of

, while

does not, in general, extend to a symmetry of the full CFT. Since we are interested in boundary states preserving an
\mathcal{N}=1
supercurrent
G

(and similarly
\tilde{G}^{\pm},\tilde{G}^{\prime\pm}
in the anti-holomorphic sector), the gluing automorphism
\Omega
must be restricted to the diagonal subgroup

fixing
G

[
45
]
.

The ground states components of the R-R BPS boundary states of the theory span a
24
-dimensional lattice
\Gamma^{4,20}\equiv\mathcal{H}^{\bullet}(\mathcal{C},\mathbb{Z})
as defined in (

\mathcal{H}^{\bullet}(\mathcal{C},\mathbb{Q})=\mathcal{H}^{\bullet}(\mathcal{C},\mathbb{Z})\otimes\mathbb{Q}

\left(\mathcal{H}^{\bullet}(\mathcal{C},\mathbb{Q}),\mathcal{H}^{\bullet}(\mathcal{C},\mathbb{C})\right)

The polarization on
\mathcal{H}(\mathcal{C},\mathbb{C})

\mathbb{C}

\mathcal{H}(\mathcal{C},\mathbb{C})
, equipped with this pairing, has signature
(4,20)
. The associate positive-definite
4

\Pi_{\mathcal{C}}\subset\mathcal{H}(\mathcal{C},\mathbb{C})
is spanned by the four R-R ground states transforming in the representations
\left(\frac{1}{4},\frac{1}{2};\frac{1}{4},\frac{1}{2}\right)
of the
\mathcal{N}=(4,4)

4
Concluding remarks and Outlook

In this article, we propose a sigma-model analogue of Hodge loci arising from the appearance of non-trivial Hodge tensors in the moduli space of geometric Calabi–Yau compactifications. Any
\mathcal{N}=(2,2)
SCFT endowed with boundary states satisfying the standard A-type and B-type boundary conditions contains all the ingredients needed to construct a rational Hodge structure
[
39
]
. On the other hand, topological defects in the theory that preserve these boundary-state conditions can be regarded as the objects playing the role of Hodge tensors in the geometric setting; their presence then identifies the corresponding CFT as a Hodge locus. When the topological defects in a given model are sufficient to generate a (product of) complex multiplication fields, we are also able to extend the CFT analogue of the notion of complex multiplication.

We conclude with some final remarks on the results and some open directions for future work.

•

The picture suggested by our analysis closely parallels what happens in the geometric setting, although the exact connection is not known. There, special points in moduli space where Hodge tensors arise from symmetries are typically accompanied by a reduction in the complexity of the period data
[
21
]
, which should translate into simplifications of the associated couplings in the effective action. This motivates the expectation that an analogous phenomenon should occur for the CFT Hodge loci introduced in this work. Indeed, since the appearance of Hodge endomorphisms in the worldsheet theory imposes additional algebraic structure on the period matrix, one may expect the corresponding loci to define special subspaces of the moduli space where the low-energy data become more constrained and potentially more tractable. From this point of view, the role of topological defects is not merely the one to provide a distinguished algebraic structure inside the CFT, but also to signal special regions in moduli space where the effective theory could exhibit simplification. It is therefore natural to expect that a similar mechanism, to the one responsible for the simplification of periods in the geometric case, should also operate here, leading to non-trivial restrictions on the couplings of the effective action. Exploring this expectation in detail would be very interesting, as it could clarify how far the geometric intuition behind Hodge loci extends to the CFT setting and whether the corresponding algebraic constraints admit a direct physical interpretation. More broadly, it would be worthwhile to investigate the relation between the CFT Hodge loci and their geometric counterparts in greater depth. In particular, since in the geometric counterpart in correspondence of Hodge loci have been observed specific geometries, it would be desirable to understand whether the presence of topological defects (Hodge endomorphisms) in the category
\mathrm{TDL}
of the SCFT can be used not only as an intrinsic characterization of special points in the
\mathcal{N}=(2,2)
moduli space, but also as a probe of the underlying target-space geometry.

•

One of the key ingredients underlying this work are boundary states satisfying A-type and B-type gluing conditions, and in particular the fact that these states furnish a module over the category
\mathrm{TDL}
of topological defects, which we used to define the notions of Hodge loci and complex multiplication. Roughly speaking, this means that the category
\mathrm{TDL}
is able to impose constrains on the set of boundary states. In particular, at complex multiplication points, these constraints become sufficiently restrictive to generate the entire lattice of boundary states starting from a small set of generators: one for each irreducible component of the rational Hodge structure, or equivalently one for each complex multiplication field appearing in
\prod_{\alpha}K_{\alpha}
. Therefore, the presence of non-trivial defects in
\mathrm{TDL}
gives rise to new methods for constructing boundary states. Notably, the category
\mathrm{TDL}
is non-trivial for certain non-geometric models, such as certain asymmetric orbifolds, for which the construction of boundary states remain an open problem. We plan to describe these methods of construction in detail in
[
54
]

•

The existence of generalized symmetries in a SCFT

\mathrm{TDL}
acts invertibly on the subCFT generated by the

The information about the Hodge endomorphism

\mathcal{L}
on the exactly marginal operators, and therefore to understand which deformations of the models preserve the corresponding generalized symmetry and which ones break it. Suppose, for example, that one is able to determine the existence of some topological defect at some special point in the moduli space (like at a rational conformal field theory, such as a Gepner model
[
55

36

Vice versa, one could use properties of the Hodge structure at generic points in the moduli space to exclude the existence of generalized symmetries at these points. This line of arguments was used in

Appendix A
Geometric background

In this appendix we introduce the notion of Hodge loci in the complex structure moduli space for families of
CY
manifolds using a geometric language.

Given a family of Calabi-Yau D-folds, the associated complex structure moduli space
\mathcal{M}_{cs}
is a Kähler manifold of complex dimension
h^{D-1,1}
which is in general neither smooth nor compact. This implies the existence of singular loci, whose corresponding Calabi–Yau are singular manifolds. Let us denote this set of loci by
\Delta
and refer to it as the
discriminant locus
. The coordinates of
\mathcal{M}_{cs}
parametrize deformations of the complex structure and therefore control the geometry of the middle-dimensional cycles, equivalently the structure of the middle homology. Moreover, this moduli space is accompanied by an underlying algebraic structure that varies with changes of the moduli. A way to provide a mathematical formulation of this structure is in terms of the Hodge structure of the middle cohomology and its variation. In particular, given a smooth Calabi-Yau manifold
Y_{D}
, the
k-th
cohomology groups
H^{k}\left(Y_{D},\mathbb{C}\right)
of the manifold always admit a
pure Hodge structure
of weight
k
, which means that for each level
k
we can define a vector space
V_{\mathbb{C}}=H^{k}\left(Y_{D},\mathbb{C}\right)
, which always admits an Hodge decomposition:

(A.1)

V_{\mathbb{C}}^{(k)}=H^{k,0}\oplus H^{k-1,1}\oplus...\oplus H^{1,k-1}\oplus H^{0,k}=\bigoplus_{k=p+q}H^{p,q}

where the building subspaces satisfy the following complex-conjugation property:

(A.2)

H^{p,q}=\overline{H}^{q,p},

and the weight
k
is the constant sum of the indices
p+q=k
of all the blocks in (
A.1
).

Inside the realm of string compactifications, the main interest is in general addressed to analyze the Hodge structure associated with the middle cohomology group as the corresponding elements encodes the main ingredients to construct the low energy effective field theory arising from these compactifications:

(A.3)

V_{\mathbb{C}}=H^{D}\left(Y_{D},\mathbb{C}\right)=\bigoplus_{D=p+q}H^{p,q}

An alternative way to rephrase the same information is by saying that each cohomology group
H^{D}\left(Y_{D},\mathbb{C}\right)
defines a decreasing Hodge filtration:

(A.4)

0\subset H^{D,0}=F^{D}\subset F^{D-1}\subset...\subset F^{1}\subset F^{0}=V_{\mathbb{C}}

of vector spaces

(A.5)

F^{p}=\bigoplus_{r\geq p}H^{r,D-r}.

This formulation is equivalent to the previous one via
H^{p,q}=F^{p}\cap\overline{F}^{q}
.

If we consider a full family of Calabi–Yau manifolds associated with a given moduli space
\mathcal{M}_{cs}
, then as we move across the moduli space, the Hodge decomposition (
A.3
), as well as the
F^{p}
spaces in the filtration (
A.4
), vary with the moduli. This means that, while the full vector space
V_{\mathbb{C}}
remains fixed, the orientation of the individual
H^{p,q}
subspaces depends on the moduli. This changing process corresponds to what is known under the name of variation of the Hodge structure.

If one can further equip the Hodge structure with a bilinear form
Q(\cdot,\cdot)
on
V_{\mathbb{C}}
satisfying the following two properties (see eq.(
2.28
)):

(A.6)

\begin{split}&(i)\quad\textsc{orthogonality}\quad Q\left(H^{p,q},H^{r,s}\right)=0\quad\text{for}\quad p\neq s,q\neq r;\\
&(ii)\quad\textsc{non-degeneracy}\quad i^{p-q}Q\left(v,\overline{v}\right)>0\quad\text{for}\quad v\in H^{p,q},\quad v\neq 0,\\
\end{split}

the structure
H^{D}
is called
polarized
.
For smooth Calabi-Yau manifolds such a the bilinear form is provided by the cup product:

(A.7)

Q(v,w)=\int_{Y_{D}}v\wedge w\quad\quad\quad v,w\in H^{D}\left(Y_{D},\mathbb{C}\right),

which induces a norm for the vectors of the whole filtration given by:

(A.8)

||v||^{2}=Q(v,\ast\overline{v})

and called Hodge norm. The isometry group preserving the pairing (
A.7
) is isomorphic to
SO(m,n)
, for certain integer numbers
m
and
n
such that
m+n=\dim H^{D}
, for
D
even and
Sp(\dim H^{D})
for
D
odd. In particular, we have:

•

SO(3,19)
for
K3
;

•

Sp(2h^{2,1}+2)
for
CY_{3}
;

•

SO(h^{2,2}+2,2h^{3,1})
for
CY_{4}
.

An alternative way to express the variation of the Hodge structure is in terms of the variation of the holomorphic
D
-form
\Omega
with respect to a fixed
integral
basis
\left\{\gamma^{\mathcal{I}}\right\}
of
F^{0}
, with
\mathcal{I}=1,...,dimH^{D}
, and internal pairing represented by the matrix:

(A.9)

\eta_{\mathcal{I}\mathcal{J}}=-\int_{Y_{D}}\gamma^{\mathcal{I}}\wedge\gamma^{\mathcal{J}}.

We can now use this basis to expand the form
\Omega
as:

(A.10)

\Omega=\Pi^{\mathcal{I}}(\mathbf{z})\gamma_{\mathcal{I}}.

The coefficients of such expansion are called
periods
and they are in general complicated transcendental functions of the moduli, that here we are indicating with
\mathbf{z}
. Equivalently, they are defined by:

(A.11)

\Pi^{\mathcal{I}}(z)=\int_{\Gamma^{\mathcal{I}}}\Omega,

where
\Gamma^{\mathcal{I}}
are the 4-cycles Poincaré-dual of the forms
\gamma^{\mathcal{I}}
. At this point one can understand the variations of the spaces
F^{p}
on
V_{\mathbb{C}}
over the moduli space
\mathcal{M}_{cs}
in terms of the variations of the periods (
A.11
) with respect to the coordinates
\mathbf{z}
.

This whole structure is completely well defined in the bulk of the moduli space, as long as the manifolds
Y_{D}
are smooth. However, when we approach a singular locus in
\mathcal{M}_{cs}
the periods (
A.11
) diverge, the Hodge filtration
F^{p}
and the corresponding Hodge decomposition
H^{p,q}
are not well defined anymore and we need to consider a refinement of the structure. This refinement involves a Mixed Hodge structure, where all the degree cohomologies are considered at the same time. We will skip the details of the discussion of these special loci and we refer the reader to the mathematical literature
[
67
,
68
]
. Before proceeding, let us briefly comment on the behaviour of periods near singular loci. Periods are multivalued functions of the moduli that around singular loci transform under specific monodromy matrices
M
depending on the type of degeneration occurring at the corresponding locus. These local monodromies lie in the isometry group and impose strong constraints on the local structure of the periods, leading to controlled asymptotic expansions. In particular, the periods admit local expansions near such loci whose leading behaviour is polynomial, while deviations from this behaviour are encoded in infinite towers of exponentially suppressed corrections as one moves away from the singular point into the bulk of the moduli space. The key point is that monodromy provides algebraic constraints on the periods, allowing for a local approximation in terms of finitely generated structures, corrected by infinite series of exponential corrections which are the mark that, at generic points of the moduli space, periods are highly transcendental functions.

Let us now consider the rational Hodge structure
(V_{\mathbb{Q}},V_{\mathbb{C}})
, where
V_{\mathbb{C}}
is for example the vector space defined in (
A.3
), and let us introduce the notions of Hodge classes and Hodge tensors. We will use these notions to define the Hodge loci.

Definition:
[Integral/Rational Hodge class]

Given an integral or rational Hodge structure
\left(V_{\mathbb{Z}/\mathbb{Q}},V_{\mathbb{C}}\right)
of weight
w
, we define integral and rational Hodge classes of the structures to be elements in the intersections

(A.12)

\begin{split}\omega\,\in\,V^{p,p}\cap V_{\mathbb{Z}}\quad&\quad\quad\text{integral Hodge class}\\
\omega\,\in\,V^{p,p}\cap V_{\mathbb{Q}}\quad&\quad\quad\text{rational Hodge class}\\
\end{split}

with
p=w/2
.

Note that if an element
\omega\in V^{p,p}
defines a rational/integral Hodge class depends on the moduli as the
(p,q)
splitting varies across the moduli space. This means that a given rational/integral class in
V_{\mathbb{Q}/\mathbb{Z}}
becomes an Hodge class only on some sub-loci of the moduli space where there is an alignment between
V^{p,p}
and the lattice.

Let us now define the space
\mathcal{T}^{m}_{n}V
of
({m\atop n})
tensors on
V_{\mathbb{C}}
as

(A.13)

\mathcal{T}^{m}_{n}V=V_{\mathbb{C}}^{\otimes m}\otimes(V_{\mathbb{C}}^{\vee})^{\otimes n}

where
V_{\mathbb{C}}^{\vee}
is the dual vector space to
V_{\mathbb{C}}
, i.e.

(A.14)

V_{\mathbb{C}}^{\vee}=Hom\left(V_{\mathbb{C}}\mapsto\mathbb{C}\right).

Let us indicate the space of all the tensors on
V_{\mathbb{C}}
for any value of
m
and
n
as

(A.15)

V^{\otimes}=\bigoplus_{m,n}\mathcal{T}^{m}_{n}V.

In an analogous way, the restriction of these definitions to
V_{\mathbb{Z}}
or
V_{\mathbb{Q}}
allows to define the space of integral and rational tensors
\mathcal{T}^{m}_{n}V_{\mathbb{Z}}
and
\mathcal{T}^{m}_{n}V_{\mathbb{Q}}
, respectively.

The Hodge structure of weight
w
on
V_{\mathbb{C}}
induces an Hodge structure of weight
w^{\prime}=w(m-n)
on the space of
({m\atop n})
tensors:

(A.16)

\mathcal{T}^{m}_{n}V=\bigoplus_{r+s=w}T^{r,s}

where
T^{r,s}\equiv\left(\mathcal{T}^{m}_{n}V\right)^{r,s}
contains all the
\left(\begin{matrix}m\\
n\end{matrix}\right)
tensors
t\in\mathcal{T}^{m}_{n}V
such that

(A.17)

t\in\bigoplus_{p_{i},\hat{p}_{j}\\
q_{i},\hat{q}_{j}}\bigotimes_{i=1}^{m}V^{p_{i},q_{i}}\otimes\bigotimes_{j=1}^{n}\left(V^{\hat{p}_{j},\hat{q}_{j}}\right)^{\vee}

with
\sum_{i}p_{i}-\sum_{j}\hat{p}_{j}=r
and
\sum_{i}q_{i}-\sum_{j}\hat{q}_{j}=s
.

With the
T^{r,s}
blocks in hand, we are now prepared to define integral and rational Hodge tensors.

Definition:
[Integral/Rational Hodge tensor]

The integral/rational Hodge tensors are elements in the intersection

(A.18)

\begin{split}t\,\in\,\left(\mathcal{T}^{m}_{n}V\right)^{p,p}\cap\mathcal{T}^{m}_{n}V_{\mathbb{Z}}\quad&\quad\quad\text{integral Hodge tensor}\\
t\,\in\,\left(\mathcal{T}^{m}_{n}V\right)^{p,p}\cap\mathcal{T}^{m}_{n}V_{\mathbb{Q}}\quad&\quad\quad\text{rational Hodge tensor}\\
\end{split}

with
p=D(m-n)/2
.

Notice that for
D
odd, as is the case of the elliptic and
CY_{3}
, Hodge classes do not exist, however, for
m-n\in 2\mathbb{Z}
they can support Hodge tensors.

Particularly interesting is the space
\mathcal{T}^{1}_{1}V
of
({1\atop 1})
tensors, which can be interpreted as
\mathbb{C}-
linear maps

(A.19)

t\,\,:\,\,V_{\mathbb{C}}\,\mapsto\,V_{\mathbb{C}}.

Since the Hodge decomposition of
\mathcal{T}^{1}_{1}V
is of weight
0
, then it only contains the space
T^{0,0}=\left(\mathcal{T}^{1}_{1}V\right)^{0,0}
that acts on
V_{\mathbb{C}}
preserving the Hodge structure:

(A.20)

\begin{split}t\,:\,\,V_{\mathbb{C}}\quad&\mapsto\quad V_{\mathbb{C}}\\
V^{p,q}\quad&\mapsto\quad V^{p+r,q+s}\end{split}

with
r=s=0
.

When an integral/rational tensors becomes of Hodge type, it induces by
\mathbb{C}
-extension a linear map on
V_{\mathbb{C}}
that preserves the Hodge structure. The monodromy matrices around singularities in the complex structure moduli space are examples of Hodge tensors. Hodge tensors of type
({1\atop 1})
corresponds to Hodge endomorphisms in the algebraic definition of Section
2
. This means that topological defects in the CFT
\mathcal{C}
satisfying properties
(1)
and
(2)
formulated in Section
2
are examples of Hodge tensors for the rational Hodge structure
\left(V_{\mathbb{C}},V_{\mathbb{Q}}\right)
, where
V_{\mathbb{C}}=H(\mathcal{C},\mathbb{C})
as in (
2.5
) and
V_{\mathbb{Q}}
is the rational extension of the lattice of BPS boundary states of the theory.

The set of integral/rational Hodge tensors of type
({1\atop 1})
equipped with composition define the algebra

(A.21)

Hg^{1,1}=(\mathcal{T}^{1}_{1}V)^{0,0}\cap\mathcal{T}^{1}_{1}V_{\mathbb{Q}}.

This corresponds to the algebra of endomorphisms
End_{Hdg}(V_{\mathbb{Q}})
defined in Section
2
.

With the two notions of Hodge integral/rational classes and integral/rational Hodge tensors in mind, we define the
Hodge loci
as the subspaces
HL(\mathcal{M}_{cs},V_{\mathbb{C}})\subset\mathcal{M}_{cs}
where non-trivial integral/rational Hodge classes appear, and
HL(\mathcal{M}_{cs},V^{\otimes})\subset\mathcal{M}_{cs}
where non-trivial integral/rational Hodge tensors arise. Here, “non-trivial” means classes or tensors that are not present at a generic point of the moduli space, but instead appear only on special loci.

A.1
The Deligne torus and the Mumford-Tate group

Let us now discuss an alternative group-theoretic formulation of the notion of Hodge structure, which is closely related to the techniques employed in Section
2
to endow the complex vector space of R-R ground states of the CFT model
\mathcal{C}
with a Hodge structure. Let us call
I
the isometry group of the bilinear pairing in the middle cohomology
H^{D}(Y_{D},\mathbb{C})
. The key object for the construction is the Deligne torus:

(A.22)

\mathbb{S}(\mathbb{R})=\mathbb{C}^{\times}

namely the group obtained by viewing the complex multiplicative group
\mathbb{C}^{\times}
as a real algebraic group, whose maximal compact subgroup is

U(1)
. Using this compact group, one can introduce the algebraic representation

(A.23)

h\,:\,U(1)\,\mapsto\,\,I

whose corresponding action on
H^{D}(Y_{D},\mathbb{C})
determines the Hodge decomposition. More precisely, we have

(A.24)

\forall\,\,\omega_{p,q}\in H^{p,q}\quad\rightarrow\quad h(z)\omega_{p,q}=z^{p}\bar{z}^{q}\omega_{p,q}

with
z\in\mathbb{C}^{\times}\,|\,|z|=1
.

The Hodge decomposition corresponds now to the decomposition on eigenspaces of
h(z)
. In our CFT analysis the role of this
U(1)
is played by the
U(1)\times U(1)
R-symmetries. Morally, the presence of two
U(1)
groups, allows us to distinguish the gradings of cohomologies of different degrees, the horizontal and the vertical ones.

Now, let us consider the orbit
h(U(1))
on
I
which defines a 1-parameter subgroup of the isometry group. We define the Mumford-Tate group
MT(h)
, associated with
h
as follows.

Definition
[Mumford-Tate group]

The Mumford-Tate group
MT(h)
is the smallest
\mathbb{Q}
-algebraic subgroup of
I
containing the orbit
h(U(1))
.

At a generic point of the moduli space the smallest
\mathbb{Q}
-algebraic subgroup of
I
containing
h(U(1))
is
I
itself. However, at special points of the moduli space, there exists smaller
\mathbb{Q}
-algebraic subgroups of
I
that contains these orbits. In particular, at CM points the Mumford-Tate group becomes Abelian.

(A.25)

MT(h)\,=\quad\begin{cases}&I\quad\text{generically}\\
&\subset I\quad\text{special points}\end{cases}

An alternative definition for the
MT
group is given in terms of subgroups of the group
GL(H)
that preserve rational Hodge tensors of weight
0
. See reference
[
69
]
for a detailed discussion.

(if
c=0
, then the only possibility is that
\gamma_{\tau}
is
\pm 1
). This implies that
\tau
is a solution of the quadratic equation

(B.14)

\displaystyle cx^{2}-(a-d)x-b=0\ ,

with
c\neq 0
, and the other solution is necessarily its complex conjugate
\bar{\tau}
. It follows that

(B.15)

\displaystyle\frac{a-d}{c}=\tau+\bar{\tau}=2\Re\tau\ ,\qquad\frac{-b}{c}=\tau\bar{\tau}=|\tau|^{2}\ .

Therefore,
\gamma_{\tau}
is of the form

(B.16)

\displaystyle\gamma_{\tau}=\begin{pmatrix}d+2c\Re\tau&-c|\tau|^{2}\\
c&d\end{pmatrix}

for suitable
c,d\in\mathbb{Q}
. The condition that
\det\gamma_{\tau}=1
is equivalent to

(B.17)

\displaystyle d^{2}+2cd\Re\tau+c^{2}|\tau|^{2}=1\qquad\Leftrightarrow\qquad|c\tau+d|^{2}=1\ .

Thus, we find that the group elements
\gamma_{\tau}\in SL(2,\mathbb{Q})
satisfying (
3.37
) are in one-to-one correspondence with elements
c\tau+d
in the field
\mathbb{Q}+\tau\mathbb{Q}
on the unit circle
\{z\in\mathbb{C}\mid|z|=1\}
. To show that there are infinitely many points of modulus
1
on
\mathbb{Q}(\tau)
, consider two rational numbers
m,n\in\mathbb{Q}
, and notice that

(B.18)

\displaystyle|m+n\tau|^{2}=(m+n\tau)(m+n\bar{\tau})=(m+n\tau)(m+n\frac{a-d}{c}-n\tau)

is a rational number. Indeed, it is an element of
\mathbb{Q}+\tau\mathbb{Q}
and is fixed under complex conjugation, and therefore it is in
\mathbb{Q}+\tau\mathbb{Q}\cap\mathbb{R}=\mathbb{Q}
. Thus, by setting

(B.19)

\displaystyle c\tau+d:=\frac{(m+n\tau)^{2}}{|m+n\tau|^{2}}\ ,

one gets an element in
\mathbb{Q}(\tau)\cap S^{1}
for each pair of rationals
(m,n)\neq(0,0)
. Notice that one can always rescale
m,n
by a common rational number, so that they can be chosen to be coprime integers. This procedure provides infinitely many elements in
\mathbb{Q}(\tau)\cap S^{1}
, but in some cases they are not all of them. For example, if
\mathbb{Q}(\tau)=\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-5})
, then the element
\frac{2+i\sqrt{5}}{3}\in S^{1}
cannot be obtained in this way.

B.3
Quantum dimension of non-invertible defects

In this appendix, we prove that the quantum dimension
\langle{\cal L}_{\gamma_{\tau},\gamma_{\rho}}\rangle
is a positive integer and we characterize it. First notice that if a certain pair
(\gamma_{\tau},\gamma_{\rho})
obeys (
3.37
), then also
(\gamma_{\tau},1)
and
(1,\gamma_{\rho})
satisfy them, so that
{\cal L}_{\gamma_{\tau},1}
and
{\cal L}_{1,\gamma_{\rho}}
are also topological defects, and

(B.20)

\displaystyle\langle{\cal L}_{\gamma_{\tau},\gamma_{\rho}}\rangle=\langle{\cal L}_{\gamma_{\tau},1}\rangle\langle{\cal L}_{1,\gamma_{\rho}}\rangle\ .

Thus, it is sufficient to prove that
\langle{\cal L}_{\gamma_{\tau},1}\rangle,\langle{\cal L}_{1,\gamma_{\rho}}\rangle\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
. In fact we will prove that
\langle{\cal L}_{\gamma_{\tau},1}\rangle
and
\langle{\cal L}_{1,\gamma_{\rho}}\rangle
are the smallest positive integers such that
\langle{\cal L}_{\gamma_{\tau},1}\rangle\gamma_{\tau}
and
\langle{\cal L}_{1,\gamma_{\rho}}\rangle\gamma_{\rho}
are integral matrices.

Let us focus on
\langle{\cal L}_{\gamma_{\tau},1}\rangle
; the argument with
\langle{\cal L}_{1,\gamma_{\rho}}\rangle
is completely analogous. First notice that
{\cal L}_{\gamma_{\tau},1}^{*}{\cal L}_{\gamma_{\tau},1}
acts trivially on the holomorphic and antiholomorphic free bosons and fermions
\partial Z
,
\bar{\partial}Z
,
\psi^{\pm}
,
\tilde{\psi}^{\pm}
. Therefore, it must be a superposition of invertible defects in
U(1)^{4}_{w-m}
. Furthermore, the action on the states
|p,\tilde{p}\rangle
is

(B.21)

\displaystyle\hat{\cal L}_{\gamma_{\tau},1}^{*}\hat{\cal L}_{\gamma_{\tau},1}|p,\tilde{p}\rangle=\begin{cases}\langle{\cal L}_{\gamma_{\tau},1}\rangle^{2}|p,\tilde{p}\rangle&\text{if }(p,\tilde{p})\in\Gamma^{2,2}\cap(\Gamma^{2,2}\gamma_{\tau})\\
0&\text{otherwise,}\end{cases}

where we used that
\langle\hat{\cal L}_{\gamma_{\tau},1}^{*}\hat{\cal L}_{\gamma_{\tau},1}\rangle=\langle{\cal L}_{\gamma_{\tau},1}\rangle^{2}
. Thus,
\hat{\cal L}_{\gamma_{\tau},1}^{*}\hat{\cal L}_{\gamma_{\tau},1}
is proportional to a projection operator on the states
|p,\tilde{p}\rangle
corresponding to the sublattice
\Gamma^{2,2}\cap(\Gamma^{2,2}\gamma_{\tau})
; the correct normalization can be fixed by imposing that
{\cal L}_{\gamma_{\tau},1}^{*}{\cal L}_{\gamma_{\tau},1}={\cal I}+\ldots
, as expected for simple defects. By self-duality of
\Gamma^{2,2}
and
\Gamma^{2,2}\gamma_{\tau}
, one can characterize
\Gamma^{2,2}\cap(\Gamma^{2,2}\gamma_{\tau})
as the sublattice of
\Gamma^{2,2}
that is invariant under the finite abelian subgroup
A_{\tau}\subset U(1)^{4}_{w-m}
given by

(B.22)

\displaystyle A_{\tau}=(\Gamma^{2,2}\cap\Gamma^{2,2}\gamma_{\tau})/\Gamma^{2,2}\subset(\Gamma^{2,2}\otimes\mathbb{R})/\Gamma^{2,2}\cong U(1)^{4}_{w-m}\ .

We conclude that

(B.23)

\displaystyle{\cal L}_{\gamma_{\tau},1}^{*}{\cal L}_{\gamma_{\tau},1}=\sum_{(a_{k},b_{k})\in A_{\tau}}{\cal L}_{a_{k},b_{k}}

and therefore

(B.24)

\displaystyle\langle{\cal L}_{\gamma_{\tau},1}\rangle^{2}=|A_{\tau}|\ .

Now, because
\gamma_{\tau}
acts separately on
(n_{1},n_{2})\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}
and on
(w_{1},w_{2})\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}
, one has that
A_{\tau}
is the direct product
A_{\tau}=A_{\tau}^{w}\times A_{\tau}^{n}
of two isomorphic abelian groups
A_{\tau}^{w}
and
A_{\tau}^{n}
, where

(B.25)

\displaystyle A_{\tau}^{w}=\{\begin{pmatrix}w_{2}&w_{1}\end{pmatrix}\gamma_{\tau}\mid w_{1},w_{2}\in\mathbb{Z}\}/(\mathbb{Z}\oplus\mathbb{Z})\subset U(1)_{w_{1}}\times U(1)_{w_{2}}

(B.26)

\displaystyle A_{\tau}^{n}=\{\begin{pmatrix}-n_{1}&n_{2}\end{pmatrix}\gamma_{\tau}\mid n_{1},n_{2}\in\mathbb{Z}\}/(\mathbb{Z}\oplus\mathbb{Z})\subset U(1)_{n_{1}}\times U(1)_{n_{2}}\ .

Therefore,

(B.27)

\displaystyle\langle{\cal L}_{\gamma_{\tau},1}\rangle=|A_{\tau}^{w}|=|A_{\tau}^{n}|

is a positive integer. Finally, let us prove that
A_{\tau}^{n}
and
A_{\tau}^{w}
are actually cyclic groups. It is clear that both groups
A_{\tau}^{n}
and
A_{\tau}^{w}
are generated by the columns of the matrix
\tau=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{smallmatrix}\right)
, considered mod
\mathbb{Z}
, i.e.

(B.28)

\displaystyle A_{\tau}^{n}\cong A_{\tau}^{w}\cong\langle\begin{pmatrix}[a]\\
[c]\end{pmatrix},\ \begin{pmatrix}[b]\\
[d]\end{pmatrix}\rangle\subset(\mathbb{Q}/\mathbb{Z})^{2}\ ,

where, for every
x\in\mathbb{Q}
, we denote by
[x]\in\mathbb{Q}/\mathbb{Z}
its class mod
\mathbb{Z}
.
Now, the condition
ad-bc=1\equiv 0\mod\mathbb{Z}
implies that
\left(\begin{smallmatrix}[a]\\
[c]\end{smallmatrix}\right)
and
\left(\begin{smallmatrix}[b]\\
[d]\end{smallmatrix}\right)
, are proportional to each other (mod
\mathbb{Z}
)

(B.29)

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}[a]\\
[c]\end{pmatrix}=k_{1}\begin{pmatrix}v_{1}\\
v_{2}\end{pmatrix}\mod\mathbb{Z}\qquad\begin{pmatrix}[b]\\
[d]\end{pmatrix}=k_{2}\begin{pmatrix}v_{1}\\
v_{2}\end{pmatrix}\mod\mathbb{Z}\ ,

for some
k_{1},k_{2},v_{1},v_{2}\in\mathbb{Q}
. If we rescale
\left(\begin{smallmatrix}v_{1}\\
v_{2}\end{smallmatrix}\right)
so that
k_{1},k_{2}
are coprime integers, we get that

(B.30)

\displaystyle A_{\tau}^{n}\cong A_{\tau}^{w}\cong\langle\begin{pmatrix}[v_{1}]\\
[v_{2}]\end{pmatrix}\rangle\cong\mathbb{Z}_{N}\ ,

where
N=|A_{\tau}^{n}|=|A_{\tau}^{w}|=\langle{\cal L}_{\gamma_{\tau},1}\rangle
is the smallest integer such that
N\left(\begin{smallmatrix}v_{1}\\
v_{2}\end{smallmatrix}\right)
is integral. By construction, this is also the smallest integer for which
N\gamma_{\tau}
is integral.
