1.
Introduction

In this article we continue the study of the topology of random polynomial lemniscates that was initiated in
[
13
]
. The setting of the problem is as follows. Let
\{X_{k}\}_{k\geq 1}
be an i.i.d. sequence of random variables distributed uniformly in the unit disc
\mathbb{D}
. For each
n,
consider the random polynomial

P_{n}(z)=(z-X_{1})(z-X_{2})...(z-X_{n}).

The unit lemniscate of
P_{n}
is the random open set defined by

\Lambda_{n}=\{z\in{\mathbb{C}}:|P_{n}(z)|<1\}.

Let
\mathscr{C}(\Lambda_{n})
be the number of connected components of
\Lambda_{n}
. As a first step towards understanding the random variable
\mathscr{C}(\Lambda_{n}),
we would like to know the behavior of the expectation
{\mathbb{E}}(\mathscr{C}(\Lambda_{n}))
for large
n
. Taking logarithms, we note that
\Lambda_{n}=\{z:\log|P_{n}(z)|<0\}
. The advantage with rewriting
\Lambda_{n}
this way is that for each
z\in{\mathbb{C}}
, the quantity
\log|P_{n}(z)|
is a sum of
n
i.i.d. random variables. This lends itself to a lot of probabilistic analysis in the form of the strong law and central limit theorems. Using this connection, the first named author (S.G) in
[
13
]
, proved the following Theorem.

Theorem 1.1 (Ghosh,
2024
).

There exist positive constants
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
such that for all large
n
we have

C_{1}\sqrt{n}\leq{\mathbb{E}}(\mathscr{C}(\Lambda_{n}))\leq C_{2}\sqrt{n}.

One can give a crude upper bound for the number of components from deterministic considerations. The maximum principle from complex analysis implies that every connected component of
\Lambda_{n}
must contain a root of
P_{n}
. Hence
\mathscr{C}(\Lambda_{n})\leq n
, but we expect that typically it should be of smaller order. Theorem
1.1
quantifies this belief. Let us first recall the strategy of
[
13
]
to prove Theorem
1.1
. A routine computation gives that
{\mathbb{E}}(\log|P_{n}(z)|)=n\left(\frac{|z|^{2}-1}{2}\right)
for
z\in\mathbb{D}
. This is negative throughout the disc. Next, concentration inequalities applied to the random variable
\log|P_{n}(z)|
show that ”nearly” all of the unit disc
{\mathbb{D}}
is contained in
\Lambda_{n}
with very high probability. Hence any small components must lie very close to the unit circle. But for points near the unit circle
{\mathbb{E}}(\log|P_{n}(z)|)\approx 0
. The fluctuation of
\log|P_{n}(z)|
for these points is governed by the Central Limit Theorem. Indeed, the lower bound in Theorem
1.1
comes from estimating the number of small components near the unit circle, in conjunction with the Berry-Esseen Theorem. The upper bound is a bit more delicate to handle. A basic tool here is Lemma
2.1
below, relating
\mathscr{C}(\Lambda_{n})
to the number of critical points of
P_{n}
that lie outside
\Lambda_{n}
. Combining this with a deep result on the pairing phenomenon of roots and critical points of random polynomials
[
19
]
finishes the proof (see Section
1.1
below for more on this).

Once the bounds in Theorem
1.1
are proven, a natural question is whether it is true that
\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{{\mathbb{E}}(\mathscr{C}(\Lambda_{n}))}{\sqrt{n}}
exists. Our main result below establishes this and identifies the limiting value.

Theorem 1.2.

Let
\mathscr{C}(\Lambda_{n})
be as above. Then

(1)

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\mathbb{E}[\mathscr{C}(\Lambda_{n})]}{\sqrt{n}}=\sqrt{\frac{\zeta(2)-1}{\pi}}.

Some remarks are in order:

(1)

The limit appearing in Theorem
1.2
is dependent on the distribution of
X_{1}
in that it is equal to a multiple of
\sqrt{Var(\log(|1-X_{1}|))}
. In Section
10
we explicitly compute
Var(\log(|1-X_{1}|))
and it turns out to be
\frac{1}{2}\left(\zeta(2)-1\right)
.

(2)

The
\sqrt{n}
scaling manifests a phase transition in the behaviour of
\mathbb{E}[\mathscr{C}(\Lambda_{n})]
. If
X_{n}
are instead uniformly distributed on the disc
\mathbb{D}_{r}=\{z\hskip 2.84526pt\bigl|\hskip 2.84526pt|z|<r\}
of radius
r>0
, then
\mathbb{E}[\mathscr{C}(\Lambda_{n})]
is
\Theta(1)
for
r<1
, and
\Theta(n)
for
r>1
, see
[
13
, Remark
1.3
]
for a brief discussion on this.

(3)

The convergence in (
1
) is delicate to prove. We show that an error bound of
O(\frac{1}{(\log n)^{\gamma}})
holds for any
\gamma>0
. It will be interesting to see if this can be improved to a
O(\frac{1}{n^{\varepsilon}})
bound.

(4)

Our proof crucially uses the assumption that the random variables
X_{k}
have a density with respect to the Lebesgue measure. It is also
of considerable convenience that random variables
X_{k}
are uniformly distributed. We believe that (a version of ) Theorem
1.2
will hold for more general random variables
X_{k}
satisfying appropriate assumptions, but the nature of these assumptions seem to be complicated to write down.

(5)

The polynomials
P_{n}(z)
with i.i.d. roots can be considered as a toy model for the characterstic function of a random matrix sampled from the Ginibre ensemble. It is well known that the empirical distribution of the eigenvalues of such matrices converges to the uniform distribution on the unit disc (the circular law), see
[
41
]
. With Theorem
1.2
established, it would be interesting (and natural) to ask how many components does the level set of the characterstic function of a Ginibre ensemble have?

Before we explain the strategy involved in proving Theorem
1.2
, we briefly recall prior works and relevant literature in the study of random lemniscates and related areas.

Random polynomials with i.i.d. roots were first considered by Pemantle and Rivin
[
38
]
to study a
pairing phenomena
between roots and critical points. This phenomenon asserts that for such polynomials, each root has a nearby critical point. Hence if we have an understanding of the typical distance between a root and its nearest critical point, one can in principle use Taylor’s theorem to estimate the corresponding critical value. In this way, Lemma
2.1
below acts as a bridge connecting the pairing phenomenon to the study of lemniscate components. Pemantle and Rivin
[
38
]
conjectured (and proved it for some special cases) that if roots are sampled as i.i.d. random variables, then the empirical distribution of the critical points of such polynomials converges weakly to the distribution of an individual zero. This was later proved by Kabluchko in full generality
[
21
]
. A more quantitative version of this pairing phenomena was proven in
[
19
]
: the distance between a critical point and the root nearest to it is of order
O(n^{-\alpha})
for
1/2<\alpha<1
while the typical spacing between i.i.d. roots is bigger at
O(n^{-\frac{1}{2}})
. Subsequent works established related results for the zeros of higher derivatives of random polynomials and stronger modes of convergence (see
[
3
,
7
,
29
]
).
The pairing phenomena can also be observed in the deterministic setting in the famous Sendov’s conjecture, which is now essentially proved in the recent work of Terence Tao
[
42
]
. For more on this phenomena we refer the reader to the works
[
14
,
36
,
37
,
19
]
and references therein.

The number of connected components of random rational lemniscates for rational functions
p/q
, where
p
and
q
are sampled independently from the complex Kostlan ensemble was studied by Lerario and Lundberg
[
26
]
. They showed that the expected number of connected components grows linearly with the degree
n
. Later, Kabluchko and Wigman
[
20
]
obtained the exact asymptotics for nodal components in terms of the Nazarov–Sodin constant
c_{NS}

[
34
]
. For a sequence of Kac polynomials
p_{n}
(with i.i.d complex Gaussian coefficients), it was shown by Lundberg and Ramachandran in
[
28
]
that
n^{-1}{\mathbb{E}}(\mathscr{C}_{p_{n}})\to\ 1
. Hence, in this model, the average number of components is asymptotically the maximal it can be. Another related work is
[
12
]
where the first two named authors (S.G and K.R) used probabilistic methods to answer some (deterministic) questions of Erdös on the maximal number of components when the zeros of the polynomial are constrained to lie on a given compact set. The study of certain other geometrical aspects of random polynomial lemniscates was investigated in
[
25
]
. We now move on to give a rough sketch of the proof of Theorem
1.2
.

1.1.
Sketch of the proof of Theorem
1.2
and organization of the paper

It is a well known fact that the number of connected components of
\Lambda_{n}
is one more than the number of critical points of
P_{n}
which lie outside
\Lambda_{n}
, see Lemma
2.1
. By the Gauss-Lucas theorem, all the critical points of
P_{n}
lie in
\mathbb{D}
. Hence, we have to count the number of critical points in
\mathbb{D}\setminus\Lambda_{n}
: denote this number as
L_{n}
. We assert that
\mathbb{E}[L_{n}]\approx n\mathbb{E}[Area(\mathbb{D}\setminus\Lambda_{n})]
. A heuristic argument to support this claim can be given as follows. By
the pairing phenomena
alluded to before, we can treat a critical point of
P_{n}
as uniformly distributed on
\mathbb{D}
. Hence, pretending as if critical points and
\Lambda_{n}
are independent of each other, this suggests that the probability that a critical point lies in
\mathbb{D}\setminus\Lambda_{n}
is roughly
\frac{1}{\pi}Area(\mathbb{D}\setminus\Lambda_{n})
. Since there are
n-1
critical points, this suggests that
\mathbb{E}[L_{n}]
has the same asymptotic behaviour as
n\times\mathbb{E}[Area(\mathbb{D}\setminus\Lambda_{n})]
. Taking this assertion granted for now, we next claim that
\mathbb{E}[Area(\mathbb{D}\setminus\Lambda_{n})]\sim C/\sqrt{n}
, which in turn implies Theorem
1.2
. We verify this claim in two steps. Firstly, we obtain an a priori inradius bound of
\Lambda_{n}
stating that
\Lambda_{n}
contains a large ball with high probability, see Figure
1
and Proposition
3.1
. This a priori estimate gives a
O(\sqrt{\log n/n})
bound on
\mathbb{E}[Area(\mathbb{D}\setminus\Lambda_{n})]
. Secondly, to get rid of the
\sqrt{\log n}
factor and enhance this to the asymptotic
\mathbb{E}[Area(\mathbb{D}\setminus\Lambda_{n})]\sim C/\sqrt{n}
, we write
\mathbb{E}[Area(\mathbb{D}\setminus\Lambda_{n})]
in the form of an integral representation and invoke the Edgeworth expansion theorem
(c.f. Theorem
2.2
below).

The main difficulty that arises is to rigorously prove that
{\mathbb{E}}[L_{n}]\approx n\mathbb{E}[Area(\mathbb{D}\setminus\Lambda_{n})
. We were unable to proceed along the lines of the heuristic stated above. Instead we use a Kac-Rice method to obtain an explicit formula for
\mathbb{E}[L_{n}]
, see Proposition
6.1
and Proposition
6.3
. This yields
{\mathbb{E}}[L_{n}]=nM_{n}+R_{n}
, where the term
M_{n}
is approximately equal to
\mathbb{E}[Area(\mathbb{D}\setminus\Lambda_{n})]
, and the remainder term
R_{n}
is negligible, see Section
6.3
for the details. The analysis of
R_{n}
is quite delicate. Our key input, Proposition
5.1
, combines a large deviation estimate in the heavy tail regime and an anticoncentration/small ball inequality to handle
R_{n}
. This is done in Section
5
and Section
9
.

2.
Preliminaries

2.1.
Connected components and critical points

Lemma 2.1.

Let
\{\beta_{j}\}_{j=1}^{n-1}
be the critical points of the random polynomial
P_{n}
. Then,

(a)

\mathscr{C}(\Lambda_{n})=1+\left|\left\{j:|P_{n}(\beta_{j})|\geq 1\right\}\right|
.

(b)

All the critical points of
P_{n}
are simple, i.e.
P_{n}^{\prime\prime}(\beta_{j})\neq 0
for all
j
almost surely. Furthermore,
|P_{n}(\beta_{j})|\neq 1
for all
j
almost surely. In particular,

\displaystyle\mathscr{C}(\Lambda_{n})=1+\left|\left\{j:|P_{n}(\beta_{j})|>1\right\}\right|.

Proof.

The claim
(a)
is well known, see [Lemma
2.8
,
[
13
]
] for details. For part
(b)
suppose that
P_{n}^{\prime}(z)=0
and
P_{n}^{\prime\prime}(z)=0
has a common root
z=z_{0}
. Clearly, since roots of
P_{n}
are all distinct almost surely,
z_{0}\neq X_{i}
for all
i
and
P_{n}(z_{0})\neq 0
. Using

P_{n}^{\prime}(z)=P_{n}(z)\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{1}{z-X_{k}},\textrm{ \hskip 5.69054ptand \hskip 5.69054pt}P_{n}^{\prime}(z)^{2}-P_{n}(z)P_{n}^{\prime\prime}(z)=P_{n}(z)^{2}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{1}{(z-X_{k})^{2}},

it follows that for
z=z_{0}
,

\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{1}{z_{0}-X_{k}}=0,\textrm{ \hskip 5.69054ptand \hskip 5.69054pt}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{1}{(z_{0}-X_{k})^{2}}=0.

This implies that

(2)

X_{1}=z_{0}+\biggl(\sum_{k=2}^{n}\frac{1}{z_{0}-X_{k}}\biggr)^{-1},

and

\biggl(\sum_{k=2}^{n}\frac{1}{z_{0}-X_{k}}\biggr)^{2}+\sum_{k=2}^{n}\frac{1}{(z_{0}-X_{k})^{2}}=0.

The set of
z_{0}
satisfying the above relation forms a finite subset in
\sigma(X_{2},X_{3},\cdots,X_{n})
, call it the set
S
. Then, using (
2
), we obtain that
X_{1}\in f(S)
where
f(z)=z+\bigl(\sum_{k=2}^{n}\frac{1}{z-X_{k}}\bigr)^{-1}
. Since
X_{k}
are independent of each other, by first conditioning w.r.t.
X_{2},X_{3},...,X_{n}
and then integrating against the law of
(X_{2},X_{3},...,X_{n})
, we have
\mathbb{P}[X_{1}\in f(S)]=0
which proves the claim that
P_{n}^{\prime}
and
P_{n}^{\prime\prime}
has no common root almost surely.

Similarly, if there exists
z=z_{0}
with
P_{n}^{\prime}(z_{0})=0
and
|P_{n}(z_{0})|=1
, we would obtain (
2
) and
|z_{0}-X_{2}||z_{0}-X_{3}|...|z_{0}-X_{n}|=|z_{0}-X_{1}|^{-1}
. This, in turn, implies that
z_{0}
solves the equation

\bigl|\sum_{k=2}^{n}\frac{1}{z_{0}-X_{k}}\bigr|=|z_{0}-X_{2}||z_{0}-X_{3}|...|z_{0}-X_{n}|.

This implies that
z_{0}
lies in the zero set of a non-constant multivariate polynomial measurable with respect to
\sigma(X_{2},X_{3},...,X_{n})
, call this zero set
Z
. Note that the zero set of a non-constant multivariate polynomial has Lebesgue measure zero. Hence, with
f
as above,
f(Z)
has Lebesgue measure zero and
\mathbb{P}[X_{1}\in f(Z)]=0
which finishes the proof.
∎

\displaystyle\mathop{\text{\Large$\int_{\text{\normalsize$\scriptstyle B$}}$}}\nolimits\frac{1}{|w-\alpha|^{2}}dA(w)\leq\mathop{\text{\Large$\int_{\text{\normalsize$\scriptstyle\kern-1.63885pt\theta=0$}}^{\text{\normalsize$\scriptstyle 2\pi$}}$}}\nolimits\mathop{\text{\Large$\int_{\text{\normalsize$\scriptstyle\kern-1.63885pt\inf_{z\in B}|w-\alpha|$}}^{\text{\normalsize$\scriptstyle\sup_{z\in B}|w-\alpha|$}}$}}\nolimits\frac{dr}{r}d\theta\leq 2\pi\log\left(\frac{\sup_{z\in B}|w-\alpha|}{\inf_{z\in B}|w-\alpha|}\right),

which establishes the result.

2.4.
The Kac-Rice formula.

The Kac-Rice method for counting roots of a polynomial was introduced by Kac
[

,
23
]
and Rice
[
40
]
. Their formula was designed to count the number of real roots for polynomials with i.i.d. coefficients (Kac ensembles). This methodology is nowadays a very standard tool, and it comes in many generalized forms and has been utilized in many applications, see
[
18
, Chapter 3]

[
4
]
,
[
28
,
26
]
for a detailed introduction to it and some instances of its applications. We will use the following complex variant of the Kac-Rice formula. In comparison to the more standard method of counting roots using contour integration, the following method has the advantage that one can count critical points/roots in any region
U
by simply changing the domain of integration to
U
in the formula.

Proposition 2.10.

Let
P
be a polynomial of degree
m
with only simple roots. Let
U\subset\mathbb{C}
be an open set and let
\mathscr{N}(P,U)
denote the number of roots of
P
in
U
. Then

(11)

\displaystyle\mathscr{N}(P,U)=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\frac{1}{\pi\varepsilon^{2}}\int_{U}|P^{\prime}(z)|^{2}\mathbbm{1}_{\{|P(z)|<\varepsilon\}}A(dz).

Moreover,

(12)

\sup_{\varepsilon\in(0,\infty)}\frac{1}{\pi\varepsilon^{2}}\int_{U}|P^{\prime}(z)|^{2}\mathbbm{1}_{\{|P(z)|<\varepsilon\}}A(dz)\leq m.

Proof.

Let
\mathscr{N}(P,U)=k
. Then, for
\varepsilon
small enough, the set
\{|P(z)|<\varepsilon\}=U_{1}\cup U_{2}\cup...\cup U_{k}
, where
U_{i}\subset U
are disjoint open connected sets each containing a single root
p_{i}
of
P
. Since each root of
P
is simple,
P^{\prime}(p_{i})\neq 0
. Hence, by the inverse function theorem, for
\varepsilon
small enough,
P
is injective on each
U_{i}
. Clearly,
P(U_{i})=B(0,\varepsilon)
and
Area(P(U_{i}))=\pi\varepsilon^{2}
. Also, by Lemma
2.7
-
(a)
,

Area(P(U_{i}))=\int_{U_{i}}|P^{\prime}(z)|^{2}A(dz),

which implies

k\pi\varepsilon^{2}=\int_{U}|P^{\prime}(z)|^{2}\mathbbm{1}_{\bigl\{|P(z)|<\varepsilon\bigr\}}A(dz),

for all
\varepsilon
small enough. In particular, dividing by
\varepsilon^{2}
and then letting
\varepsilon\to 0
, we obtain (
11
). The estimate (
12
) follows easily from Lemma
2.7
-
(b)
.

∎

The proof is an adaptation of ideas from

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\sup_{z\in B(0,1-r_{n})}|P_{n}(z)|<1\right)\geq 1-\frac{1}{n^{A}}.

The proof proceeds as follows. First, we fix a point

at these points using Lemma
2.8
. Finally, an application of the union bound yields the desired result.
We now begin with the probability estimates.

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[\left\{|P_{n}(z_{0})|<\frac{1}{2}\right\}\right]=\mathbb{P}\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log|z_{0}-X_{i}|<-\log 2\Big)

). Now, we use Bennett’s inequality with the mean zero random variables

Since all the moments of

\displaystyle h(u)

\displaystyle=\frac{u^{2}}{2}h^{\prime\prime}(0)+\frac{u^{3}}{6}h^{\prime\prime\prime}(\xi u)\geq\frac{u^{2}}{2}h^{\prime\prime}(0)+\frac{u^{3}}{6}h^{\prime\prime\prime}(0)\geq\frac{u^{2}}{4}.

where in the last line we have used

\displaystyle 0<\inf_{0\leq r\leq 1}\sigma(r)\leq\sup_{0\leq r\leq 1}\sigma(r)<\infty.

which is an easy consequence of the continuity of

.

Now for

It follows from above that the expression in (

\displaystyle 2\mathop{\text{\Large$\int_{\text{\normalsize$\scriptstyle\kern-1.63885pt1-\kappa\sqrt{\frac{\log n}{n}}$}}^{\text{\normalsize$\scriptstyle 1$}}$}}\nolimits

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\frac{\sum_{k=1}^{n}(\log|r-X_{k}|-u(r))}{\sqrt{n}\sigma(r)}>C_{r}(n)\right)rdr

\displaystyle=2\mathop{\text{\Large$\int_{\text{\normalsize$\scriptstyle\kern-1.63885pt1-\kappa\sqrt{\frac{\log n}{n}}$}}^{\text{\normalsize$\scriptstyle 1$}}$}}\nolimits(1-\Phi(C_{r}(n)))rdr+2\mathop{\text{\Large$\int_{\text{\normalsize$\scriptstyle\kern-1.63885pt1-\kappa\sqrt{\frac{\log n}{n}}$}}^{\text{\normalsize$\scriptstyle 1$}}$}}\nolimits\frac{Q_{1}(C_{r}(n))}{\sqrt{n}}rdr+O\left(\frac{1}{n}\right),

\mathop{\text{\Large$\int_{\text{\normalsize$\scriptstyle\kern-1.63885pt1-\kappa\sqrt{\frac{\log n}{n}}$}}^{\text{\normalsize$\scriptstyle 1$}}$}}\nolimits\frac{Q_{1}(C_{r}(n))}{\sqrt{n}}rdr\lesssim\frac{1}{n}.

Remark 4.4.

One infers from the proof above that the asymptotic
\mathbb{E}[Area(\mathbb{D}\setminus\Lambda_{n})]\sim\frac{C}{\sqrt{n}}
is an outcome of an interpolation between the large deviation regime and mean behaviour regime. More precisely, since
\mathbb{E}[\log|z-X_{1}|]=(1-|z|^{2})/2<0
for each fixed
z\in\mathbb{D}
, the event
\{\log|P_{n}(z)|>0\}
is a large deviation event. By Cramér’s theorem, this event will have exponentially small probability for each fixed
z\in\mathbb{D}
. But, for
z\in\partial\mathbb{D}
, since
\mathbb{E}[\log|z-X_{1}|]=0
,
\{\log|P_{n}(z)|>0\}
is a mean behaviour type event which will have
\Theta(1)
probability. The asymptotics
\mathbb{E}[Area(\mathbb{D}\setminus\Lambda_{n})]\sim C\sqrt{n}
fall out of computation as
z
interpolates between the interior of the unit disc and the boundary of the unit disc. As a by product it follows that only
z
at a distance
x/\sqrt{n},x\in(0,\infty),
from
\partial\mathbb{D}
contribute positively in the asymptotics of
\mathbb{E}[Area(\mathbb{D}\setminus\Lambda_{n})]
.

5.
A Large Deviated anticoncentration/small ball estimate

In this section, we prove a large deviation estimate in combination with an anticoncentration/small ball estimate. For
r\in(0,1)
, let
Y_{k}=Y_{k}(r)=r-\operatorname{Re}(1/(r-X_{k}))
and let
W_{n}=W_{n}(r)=\sum_{k=1}^{n}Y_{k}(r)
be the random walk generated by
Y_{k}(r)
. For sequences
a_{n},b_{n}
of positive real numbers such that
a_{n}\leq b_{n}
and
a_{n},b_{n}=\Theta(n)
, we want to estimate

(34)

\mathbb{P}\left(W_{n}(r)\in[a_{n},b_{n}]\right).

Note from Lemma
2.6
,
W_{n}(r)
is a mean-zero random walk, and the law of large numbers implies
W_{n}(r)/n\to 0
almost surely as
n\to\infty
. Hence, the event
\{W_{n}(r)\in[a_{n},b_{n}]\}
is a large deviation event. Moreover, we also want to account for the fact that
W_{n}(r)
is squeezed in the interval
[a_{n},b_{n}]
, which further reduces the probability of the large deviation event (
34
): this is the context of anti concentration inequalities/small ball estimates. Furthermore, we also want uniformity of our estimates with respect to the variable
r
, see Section
9
below for the basis of these aspirations. The following proposition which could be of independent interest gives us the required estimate.

Proposition 5.1.

Let
a_{n},b_{n}=\Theta(n)
,
a_{n}\leq b_{n}
be two sequences. Then, for any
\alpha,\beta\in(1/2,1)
there exists a constant
C
depending on
\alpha,\beta,
and the sequences
a_{n},b_{n}
such that for all

r\in\left[0,1-\frac{1}{n^{\alpha}}\right]

(35)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(W_{n}(r)\in[a_{n},b_{n}])\leq C\biggl(\frac{b_{n}-a_{n}}{n^{2}}+\frac{\log n}{n}\exp\bigl(-\frac{(\log n)^{1-\beta}}{2}\bigr)\biggr).

The proof of the above proposition is done in the following subsections, where we note down some facts about the law of random variables
Y_{k}(r)
and then obtain the required upper and lower bounds. At the heart of the proof of Proposition
5.1
is the large deviation theory in the regime of heavy-tailed random walks. In particular, it uses ideas from Cline-Hsing
[
8
]
but also brings in the new idea (which is using Bennett’s inequality, c.f. Theorem
2.4
), which significantly simplifies the implementation of the approach in
[
8
]
to our setting.

5.1.
The distribution of
Y_{k}(r)

The law of
Y_{k}(r)
exhibits Pareto-type tails. In fact, the density of
Y_{k}(r)
is a compact perturbation of the density of a symmetric Pareto-type law, see Remark
5.3
below. More precisely,

Lemma 5.2.

For
r\in(0,1)
, the distribution function of
Y_{k}(r)
satisfies

(36)

\displaystyle F_{r}(t)=\mathbb{P}(Y_{k}(r)\leq t)=\begin{cases}\quad\frac{1}{4(r-t)^{2}},\quad&\textit{if}\quad\quad-\infty<t\leq r-\frac{1}{1+r}\\
\quad 1-\frac{1}{4(r-t)^{2}},\quad\quad&\textit{if}\quad\quad r+\frac{1}{1-r}\leq t<\infty,\end{cases}

and, for
r<t<r+\frac{1}{1-r}

(37)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(Y_{k}(r)>t)\leq 1\wedge\frac{1}{4(t-r)^{2}}.

Figure 3
.
The unit circle is shown in black, together with curves
\mathcal{C}_{t}
for various values of
t
, corresponding to the tail probabilities of
Y_{r}(k)
: left-sided tail (left) and right-sided tail (right).

As a consequence, there exists absolute constants
C_{1},C_{2},>0
such that for all
m\geq 2
,

(38)

\biggl|\int_{-m}^{m}xdF_{r}(x)\biggr|\leq\frac{C_{1}}{m},

and

(39)

C_{2}\log m\leq\biggl|\int_{-m}^{m}x^{2}dF_{r}(x)\biggr|\leq C_{1}\log m.

Proof.

let
X_{k}=x+iy
, then
Y_{k}(r)=r-\frac{r-x}{(r-x)^{2}+y^{2}}
. For
t<r,
a simple algebraic manipulation shows that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(Y_{k}(r)\leq t)=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\pi}\mbox{Area}\left(r-\frac{r-x}{(r-x)^{2}+y^{2}}\leq t;x^{2}+y^{2}\leq 1\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\pi}\mbox{Area}\left(\Bigl(x+\frac{1}{2(r-t)}-r\Bigr)^{2}+y^{2}\leq\frac{1}{4(r-t)^{2}};x^{2}+y^{2}\leq 1\right).

That is, the probability is given by the normalized area of the intersection of the two disks ( c.f. Figure
3

\mathcal{C}_{t}:=\left\{\left(x+\frac{1}{2(r-t)}-r\right)^{2}+y^{2}\leq\frac{1}{4(r-t)^{2}}\right\}\quad\text{and}\quad\{x^{2}+y^{2}\leq 1\}.

For
t\leq r-\tfrac{1}{1+r}
, we observe that
\frac{1}{2(r-t)}\leq\frac{1+r}{2}
.
This inequality shows that the disk

\left\{\left(x+\frac{1}{2(r-t)}-r\right)^{2}+y^{2}\leq\frac{1}{4(r-t)^{2}}\right\}

is completely contained inside the unit disk for
t\leq r-\tfrac{1}{1+r}
. Therefore,

\mathbb{P}(Y_{k}(r)\leq t)\;=\;\frac{1}{\pi}\,\mathrm{Area}\!\left(\left(x+\tfrac{1}{2(r-t)}-r\right)^{2}+y^{2}\leq\tfrac{1}{4(r-t)^{2}}\right)\;=\;\frac{1}{4(r-t)^{2}}.

A similar computation yields the right tail of the distribution in (
36
). The argument above also gives (
37
). The estimate (
38
) and (
39
) follows easily by applying integration by parts formula and using (
36
) and (
37
) (note that
Y_{1}(r)
is centered, i.e.
\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}xdF_{r}(x)=0
and hence
\int_{|x|\leq m}xdF_{r}(x)=-\int_{|x|>m}xdF_{r}(x)
).
∎

Remark 5.3.

We do not explicitly compute the distribution function
F_{r}(t)
for the compact range
t\in[r-\frac{1}{1+r},r+\frac{1}{1-r}]
since it is not so neat to write an explicit expression for it. Note that in this case, as illustrated by Figure

is skew and not symmetric. In fact, for

.

5.2.
Upper bound on a large deviation event

For a sequence
c_{n}>0
such that
c_{n}=\Theta(n)
(we will take
c_{n}=a_{n}
or
c_{n}=b_{n}
), we obtain an upper bound on
\mathbb{P}[W_{n}(r)>c_{n}]
.

For
\beta\in(1/2,1)
, let
\varepsilon=\varepsilon_{n}=(\log n)^{-\beta}
.
Let
m=m_{n}=(1-\varepsilon_{n})c_{n}
. Let us write
\mu_{m}(r)=\mathbb{E}[Y_{1}(r)1_{|Y_{1}(r)|\leq m}]
and
\nu_{m}(r)=\mathbb{E}[|Y_{1}(r)|^{2}1_{|Y_{1}(r)|\leq m}]
. From Lemma
5.2
, we have
\mu_{m}(r)\leq C/m
and
C_{2}\log m\leq\nu_{m}(r)\leq C_{1}\log m
. Then,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}[W_{n}(r)>c_{n}]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}[W_{n}(r)>c_{n},\max_{k=1,2,..,n}Y_{k}(r)\leq m]+\mathbb{P}[W_{n}(r)>c_{n},\max_{k=1,2,..,n}Y_{k}(r)>m]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}[W_{n}(r)>c_{n},\max_{k=1,2,..,n}Y_{k}(r)\leq m]+n\mathbb{P}[Y_{1}(r)>m]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}[\sum_{k=1}^{n}Y_{k}(r)1_{Y_{k}(r)\leq m}>c_{n}]+n\mathbb{P}[Y_{1}(r)>m]

(40)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}[\sum_{k=1}^{n}Y_{k}(r)1_{|Y_{k}(r)|\leq m}>c_{n}]+n\mathbb{P}[Y_{1}(r)>m]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}[\sum_{k=1}^{n}(Y_{k}(r)1_{|Y_{k}(r)|\leq m}-\mu_{m}(r))>c_{n}-n\mu_{m}(r)]+n\mathbb{P}[Y_{1}(r)>m]

\displaystyle\leq\exp\biggl(-\frac{n\nu_{m}(r)}{m^{2}}h\biggl(\frac{m(c_{n}-n\mu_{m}(r))}{n\nu_{m}(r)}\biggr)\biggr)+n\mathbb{P}[Y_{1}(r)>m],

where we have used Bennett’s inequality (c.f. Theorem
2.4
) to go from the fifth line to the sixth line, and the function
h
is as in that Theorem.

Now, note that since
c_{n}=\Theta(n)
,

\frac{n\nu_{m}(r)}{m^{2}}\times\frac{m(c_{n}-n\mu_{m}(r))}{n\nu_{m}(r)}=O(1),

\frac{n\nu_{m}(r)}{m^{2}}\times\log\biggl(1+\frac{m(c_{n}-n\mu_{m}(r))}{n\nu_{m}(r)}\biggr)=o(1),

and

\frac{m(c_{n}-n\mu_{m}(r))}{n\nu_{m}(r)}=\Theta\biggl(\frac{n}{\log n}\biggr).

Hence,

(41)

\displaystyle\exp\biggl(-\frac{n\nu_{m}(r)}{m^{2}}h\biggl(\frac{m(c_{n}-n\mu_{m}(r))}{n\nu_{m}(r)}\biggr)\biggr)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\lesssim\exp\biggl(-\frac{n\nu_{m}(r)}{m^{2}}\times\frac{m(c_{n}-n\mu_{m}(r))}{n\nu_{m}(r)}\log\biggl(1+\frac{m(c_{n}-n\mu_{m}(r))}{n\nu_{m}(r)}\biggr)\biggr)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad=\exp\biggl(-\frac{c_{n}-n\mu_{m}(r)}{m}\times\log\biggl(1+\frac{m(c_{n}-n\mu_{m}(r))}{n\nu_{m}(r)}\biggr)\biggr)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\lesssim\exp\biggl(-\frac{c_{n}}{m}\times\log\biggl(\frac{n}{\log n}\biggr)\biggr),

where
\mu_{m}(r)=O(1/m)
has been used in the last line. Note that

\displaystyle\exp\biggl(-\frac{c_{n}}{m}\times\log\biggl(\frac{n}{\log n}\biggr)\biggr)

\displaystyle=\exp\biggl(-\frac{1}{1-\varepsilon_{n}}\times\log\biggl(\frac{n}{\log n}\biggr)\biggr)

\displaystyle=\frac{\log n}{n}\exp\biggl(-\frac{\varepsilon_{n}}{1-\varepsilon_{n}}\times\log\biggl(\frac{n}{\log n}\biggr)\biggr)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\log n}{n}\exp\biggl(-\frac{\varepsilon_{n}}{2}\times\log n\biggr)

\displaystyle=\frac{\log n}{n}\exp\biggl(-\frac{1}{2}\times(\log n)^{1-\beta}\biggr).

Plugging the above estimates in (
5.2
), we obtain that for some constant
C
depending on
\alpha,\beta\in(1/2,1)
and the sequence
c_{n}
,

(42)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}[W_{n}(r)>c_{n}]\leq n\mathbb{P}(Y_{1}(r)>m_{n})+C\frac{\log n}{n}\exp\biggl(-\frac{1}{2}\times(\log n)^{1-\beta}\biggr).

5.3.
Lower bound on a large deviation event

For a sequence
c_{n}
as in the previous section, we now obtain a lower bound on
\mathbb{P}[W_{n}(r)>c_{n}]
.

For any
\delta\in(1/2,1)
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\Bigl(W_{n}(r)>c_{n}\Bigr)\geq\mathbb{P}\Bigl(W_{n}(r)>c_{n};\bigcup_{k=1,\cdots,n}\{Y_{k}(r)>c_{n}+n^{\delta}\}\Bigr).

Using Bonferroni’s inequality, the right hand side of the above is further lower bounded by

(43)

\Bigg[n\mathbb{P}\Bigl(W_{n}(r)>c_{n};Y_{1}(r)>c_{n}+n^{\delta}\Bigr)\\
-\sum_{i<j}\mathbb{P}\Bigl(W_{n}(r)>c_{n};Y_{j}(r)>c_{n}+n^{\delta};Y_{i}(r)>c_{n}+n^{\delta}\Bigr)\Bigg].

Now, by independence, the first term in (
43
) is bounded below by

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\Bigl(W_{n}(r)>c_{n};Y_{1}(r)>c_{n}+n^{\delta}\Bigr)

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{P}\Bigl(\sum_{k=2}^{n}Y_{k}(r)>-n^{\delta};Y_{1}(r)>c_{n}+n^{\delta}\Bigr)

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{P}\Bigl(\sum_{k=2}^{n}Y_{k}(r)>-n^{\delta}\Bigr)\mathbb{P}\Bigl(Y_{1}(r)>c_{n}+n^{\delta}\Bigr).

Since
Y_{k}(r)
are mean-zero independent random variables, using Lemma
2.6
, Lemma
2.5
, and Markov inequality, we get that for any
1/\delta<p<2
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\Bigl(\sum_{k=2}^{n}Y_{k}(r)>-n^{\delta}\Bigr)=1-\mathbb{P}\Bigl(\sum_{k=2}^{n}Y_{k}(r)\leq-n^{\delta}\Bigr)\geq 1-\frac{C_{p}}{n^{\delta p-1}},

for some constant
C_{p}
. Also, the second term in (
43
) is bounded above by

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\Bigl(W_{n}(r)>c_{n};Y_{j}(r)>c_{n}+n^{\delta};Y_{i}(r)>c_{n}+n^{\delta}\Bigr)\leq\mathbb{P}\Bigl(Y_{1}(r)>c_{n}+n^{\delta}\Bigr)^{2}.

Plugging the above estimates into (
43
) and using Lemma
5.2
, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}

\displaystyle\Bigl(W_{n}(r)>c_{n}\Bigr)

\displaystyle\geq n\mathbb{P}\bigl(Y_{1}(r)>c_{n}+n^{\delta}\bigr)-\frac{C_{p}}{n^{\delta p-2}}\mathbb{P}\bigl(Y_{1}(r)>c_{n}+n^{\delta}\bigr)-\frac{n(n-1)}{2}\mathbb{P}\bigl(Y_{1}(r)>c_{n}+n^{\delta}\bigr)^{2}

\displaystyle\geq n\mathbb{P}\bigl(Y_{1}(r)>c_{n}+n^{\delta}\bigr)-\frac{C}{n^{\delta p}}-Cn^{2}\frac{1}{n^{4}}

\displaystyle\geq n\mathbb{P}\bigl(Y_{1}(r)>c_{n}+n^{\delta}\bigr)-\frac{C}{n^{\delta p}}.

5.4.
Proof of Proposition
5.1

Note that for
r\leq 1-\frac{1}{n^{\alpha}}
and
m_{n}=(1-\varepsilon_{n})c_{n}
, we have
m_{n},c_{n}+n^{\delta}>r+\frac{1}{1-r}
. Hence, by Lemma
5.2
,

\mathbb{P}[Y_{1}(r)>m_{n}]=\frac{1}{4(m_{n}-r)^{2}},

and

\mathbb{P}[Y_{1}(r)>c_{n}+n^{\delta}]=\frac{1}{4(c_{n}+n^{\delta}-r)^{2}}.

Then, applying the upper and lower bounds obtained in the previous section with
c_{n}=a_{n}
or
c_{n}=b_{n}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(W_{n}(r)\in[a_{n},b_{n}])

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}(W_{n}(r)>a_{n})-\mathbb{P}(W_{n}(r)>b_{n})

\displaystyle\leq n\mathbb{P}(Y_{1}(r)>(1-\varepsilon_{n})a_{n})-n\mathbb{P}(Y_{1}(r)>b_{n}+n^{\delta})

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad+C\frac{\log n}{n}\exp\biggl(-\frac{1}{2}\times(\log n)^{1-\beta}\biggr)+\frac{C}{n^{\delta p}}.

The above easily implies (
35
), which completes the proof.

6.
A Kac-Rice Formula for counting
{\mathscr{C}}(\Lambda_{n})

In this section, we obtain an explicit formula for
\mathbb{E}[\mathscr{C}(\Lambda_{n})]
and simplify it into a usable form.

6.1.
The first step towards counting
{\mathscr{C}}(\Lambda_{n})

Recall the notation (
19

to establish Theorem

. To this end, we use the following proposition to count
\overset{\sim}{\mathscr{C}}(\Lambda_{n})
.

Proposition 6.1.

For the random polynomials

, we have almost surely

(44)

\mathscr{C}(\Lambda_{n})\hskip 2.84526pt(\textrm{resp.}\hskip 2.84526pt\overset{\sim}{\mathscr{C}}(\Lambda_{n}))=1+\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\frac{1}{\pi\varepsilon^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{D}\hskip 2.84526pt(\textrm{resp.}\hskip 2.84526pt\mathbb{A}_{n})}|P_{n}^{\prime\prime}(z)|^{2}\mathbbm{1}_{\{|P_{n}^{\prime}(z)|<\varepsilon,|P_{n}(z)|>1\}}A(dz).

Moreover,

(45)

\mathbb{E}[\overset{\sim}{\mathscr{C}}(\Lambda_{n})]=1+\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\frac{1}{\pi\varepsilon^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{A}_{n}}\mathbb{E}\bigl[|P_{n}^{\prime\prime}(z)|^{2}\mathbbm{1}_{\{|P_{n}^{\prime}(z)|<\varepsilon,|P_{n}(z)|>1\}}\bigr]A(dz).

Proof.

The formula (
44
) follows from (
11
) by taking
P=P_{n}^{\prime}
with
U=\{|P_{n}(z)|>1\}
,
\{|P_{n}(z)|>1\}\cap\mathbb{A}_{n}
respectively and invoking Lemma

. Finally, (

) by Fubini and the dominated convergence theorem, where the domination property is verified using (

Remark 6.2.

A closer look at proof of the above proposition will reveal that our approach to count the critical points via Kac-Rice formula is not well suited if critical points are to be found at the boundary of the lemniscate
\Lambda_{n}
for example as in unit Bernoulli-Erdös lemniscate
\{|P(z)|<1\}
with
P(z)=z^{n}-1
. The simple reason behind this is that if a critical point lies on the
\partial\Lambda_{n}
, then only a portion of the set
\{|P_{n}^{\prime}(z)|<\varepsilon\}
will lie outside
\Lambda_{n}
. As such, area of its image under
P_{n}^{\prime}
will not fully compensate with
\pi\varepsilon^{2}
. Fortunately, for random polynomials
P_{n}(z)
with uniformly distributed i.i.d. roots, as proven in Lemma
2.1
, such a situation does not arise almost surely.

6.2.
A further refinement of the Kac-Rice formula

We now explain the idea behind the formula given in Proposition
6.1
. To bring the formula (
45
) into a usable form, we need to further simplify the integral and expected value appearing in (
45
). The expected value appearing in this formula depends on the joint distribution of
(P_{n}^{\prime\prime}(z),P_{n}^{\prime}(z),P_{n}(z))
, which are correlated with each other. The dependence on such joint distributions also appears in the original work of Kac-Rice
[
40
,
23
]
, and further simplification from this point onwards is specific to the model under investigation. In our particular setting, we reduce (
45
) as follows. We play a trick of introducing a new random variable
X_{0}
uniformly distributed on
\mathbb{D}
and independent of the sequence
\{X_{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
and writing

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{n}(\varepsilon):=\frac{1}{\pi\varepsilon^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{A}_{n}}\mathbb{E}\bigl[|P_{n}^{\prime\prime}(z)|^{2}

\displaystyle\mathbbm{1}_{\{|P_{n}^{\prime}(z)|<\varepsilon,|P_{n}(z)|>1\}}\bigr]A(dz)

(46)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\mathbb{E}\bigl[|P_{n}^{\prime\prime}((X_{0}))|^{2}\mathbbm{1}_{\{|P_{n}^{\prime}((X_{0}))|<\varepsilon,|P_{n}((X_{0}))|>1,X_{0}\in\mathbb{A}_{n}\}}\bigr].

We now utilize the symmetry between random variables
X_{0},X_{1},...,X_{n}
. In the limit
\varepsilon\to 0
, the event
\{|P_{n}^{\prime}(X_{0})|<\varepsilon\}
converges to
\{P_{n}^{\prime}(X_{0})=0\}
, i.e.
X_{0}
is a critical point of
P_{n}
. The idea is that instead of solving for
X_{0}
in terms of
X_{1},...,X_{n}
in the equation
P_{n}^{\prime}(X_{0})=0
, solve for
X_{1}
in terms of
X_{0},X_{2},...,X_{n}
. This trick allows us to perform an exact computation on the formula (
6.2
) since the equation
P_{n}^{\prime}(X_{0})=0
is highly non-linear to solve for
X_{0}
given
X_{1},X_{2},...,X_{n}
, but given
X_{0},X_{2},...,X_{n}
, it is just a linear equation in
X_{1}
which can be solved explicitly. Note that in (
6.2
), the factor
1/\varepsilon^{2}
is countered by the density of the random variable
X_{0}
. But, since
X_{1}
has the same distribution as
X_{0}
, we can also counter
1/\varepsilon^{2}
through the density of
X_{1}
.

With
\mathcal{T}_{n}(\varepsilon)
given by (
6.2
), write
\mathcal{T}_{n}(0)=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\mathcal{T}_{n}(\varepsilon)
. From (
45
), we know
\mathbb{E}[\overset{\sim}{\mathscr{C}}(\Lambda_{n})]=1+\mathcal{T}_{n}(0)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{2}\left[\frac{1}{|(X_{0}-X_{3})S_{n}|^{2}}\mathbbm{1}_{O_{n}}\right].

The proof is completed by taking the expectation with respect to
X_{0},X_{3},...,X_{n}
.
∎

To control the behaviour of the error term
R_{n}^{0}
(and hence
R_{n}^{1}
by the proposition above), we will use the following estimate:

Proposition 7.3.

Let
O_{n}
be the event defined in (
49
) and
S_{n},\overset{\sim}{S}_{n}
be the random walks defined in (
61
). Then, for some absolute constant
C
, conditional on
X_{0},X_{3},\cdots,X_{n}
,

(73)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{2}\left[\frac{1}{|(X_{0}-X_{2})S_{n}|^{2}}\mathbbm{1}_{O_{n}}\right]\leq C\min\left\{1,\frac{\log(1+2|\overset{\sim}{S}_{n}|)}{|\overset{\sim}{S}_{n}|^{2}}\right\}\mathbbm{1}_{X_{0}\in\mathbb{A}_{n}}.

Proof.

If
X_{0}\notin\mathbb{A}_{n}
, then
O_{n}
is empty and the claim follows trivially. Hence, assume
X_{0}\in\mathbb{A}_{n}
. We first write
(X_{0}-X_{2})S_{n}=1+(X_{0}-X_{2})\overset{\sim}{S}_{n}
. Now using the independence of
\overset{\sim}{S}_{n}
with
X_{2}
, and conditioning on
X_{0},X_{3},\cdots,X_{n}
, we get

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{2}\left[\frac{1}{|(X_{0}-X_{2})S_{n}|^{2}}\mathbbm{1}_{{O}_{n}}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|\overset{\sim}{S}_{n}|^{2}}\mathbb{E}_{2}\left[\frac{1}{\left|(X_{0}-X_{2})+\frac{1}{\overset{\sim}{S}_{n}}\right|^{2}}\mathbbm{1}_{O_{n}}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\pi|\overset{\sim}{S}_{n}|^{2}}\int_{O_{2,n}}\frac{1}{|\alpha-w|^{2}}\quad dA(w),

where
\alpha=X_{0}+\frac{1}{\overset{\sim}{S}_{n}}
and
O_{2,n}
is as given by (
67
). Now, by Lemma
2.9
, we can bound the above integral by

(74)

\displaystyle\int_{O_{2,n}}\frac{1}{|\alpha-w|^{2}}\quad dA(w)

\displaystyle\lesssim\log\left(\frac{\sup_{w\in O_{2,n}}|\alpha-w|}{\inf_{w\in O_{2,n}}|\alpha-w|}\right)\lesssim\log\left(\frac{1+|\alpha|}{\inf_{w\in O_{2,n}}|\alpha-w|}\right)

Using that
w\in O_{2,n}
and a simple algebraic manipulation, we obtain

\displaystyle\left|\alpha-\frac{1}{\overset{\sim}{S}_{n}(1+(X_{0}-w)\overset{\sim}{S}_{n})}\right|=\left|X_{0}+\frac{1}{\overset{\sim}{S}_{n}}-\frac{1}{\overset{\sim}{S}_{n}(1+(X_{0}-w)\overset{\sim}{S}_{n})}\right|=\left|X_{0}+\frac{X_{0}-w}{1+(X_{0}-w)\overset{\sim}{S}_{n}}\right|

\displaystyle<1.

The triangle inequality now implies

|\overset{\sim}{S}_{n}(1+(X_{0}-w)\overset{\sim}{S}_{n})|>\frac{1}{1+|\alpha|}.

Hence, we get

(75)

\displaystyle|\alpha-w|=\left|\frac{1+(X_{0}-w)\overset{\sim}{S}_{n}}{\overset{\sim}{S}_{n}}\right|>\frac{1}{|\overset{\sim}{S}_{n}|^{2}(1+|\alpha|)}.

Putting (
75
) and (
74
) together, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{2}\left[\frac{1}{|(X_{0}-X_{2})S_{n}|^{2}}\mathbbm{1}_{O_{n}}\right]\lesssim\frac{1}{|\overset{\sim}{S}_{n}|^{2}}\log(|\overset{\sim}{S}_{n}|^{2}(1+|\alpha|)^{2})\lesssim\frac{\log(1+2|\overset{\sim}{S}_{n}|)}{|\overset{\sim}{S}_{n}|^{2}}.

Finally, to show
\mathbb{E}_{2}\left[\frac{1}{|(X_{0}-X_{2})S_{n}|^{2}}\mathbbm{1}_{O_{n}}\right]\lesssim 1
, we simply use the bound

x^{2}\leq 1+x^{4}
along with Proposition
7.1
.
∎

In this section, we obtain the asymptotic of the term

For ease of notation, we write

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left({|S_{n}-(n-1)\overline{X_{0}}|>n^{\alpha}}\right)

\displaystyle\left|\mathbb{P}(O_{n})-\mathbb{P}\left(O_{n}\mathbbm{1}_{|S_{n}-(n-1)\overline{X_{0}}|\leq n^{\alpha}}\right)\right|\leq\mathbb{P}\left({|S_{n}-(n-1)\overline{X_{0}}|>n^{\alpha}}\right)=o\biggl(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\biggr).

.

9.
Estimating the error terms
R_{n}^{0}
,
R_{n}^{1}

We now prove that error terms

and

do not contribute to the asymptotic of

We prove that

and

This together with (

To this end, we use Proposition
7.3
. Using (

), we have

(83)

\displaystyle R_{n}^{0}\lesssim\mathbb{E}\biggl[\min\left\{1,\frac{\log(1+2|\overset{\sim}{S}_{n})|}{|\overset{\sim}{S}_{n}|^{2}}\right\}\mathbbm{1}_{X_{0}\in\mathbb{A}_{n}}\biggr].

Note that conditional on
X_{0}
, the random walk

(84)

\displaystyle R_{n}^{0}

\displaystyle\lesssim\mathbb{E}\biggl[\min\left\{1,\frac{\log(1+2|\overset{\sim}{S}_{n}|)}{|\overset{\sim}{S}_{n}|^{2}}\right\}\mathbbm{1}_{X_{0}\in\mathbb{A}_{n}}\biggr]

\displaystyle\lesssim\mathbb{E}\biggl[\frac{\log(1+2|\overset{\sim}{S}_{n}|)}{|\overset{\sim}{S}_{n}|^{2}}\mathbbm{1}_{X_{0}\in\mathbb{A}_{n}}\mathbbm{1}_{|\overset{\sim}{S}_{n}|\geq\delta n}\biggr]+\mathbb{E}\biggl[\mathbbm{1}_{X_{0}\in\mathbb{A}_{n}}\mathbbm{1}_{|\overset{\sim}{S}_{n}|<\delta n}\biggr]

.
Also note that for

The above argument is not sufficient to prove the stronger estimate (

10.
Computation of the Variance and proof of Theorem
1.2

Lemma 10.1.

Let
X
be a uniform random variable in the unit disk. Then

I:=\mathbb{E}\left[[\log|1-X|]^{2}\right]=\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\left(\log\left|1-re^{i\theta}\right|\right)^{2}\,r\,d\theta\,dr=\frac{\zeta(2)-1}{2}=\frac{\pi^{2}-6}{12}.

Proof.

We begin by considering the Taylor expansion of the complex logarithm:

\log(1-z)=-\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{z^{n}}{n},\quad|z|<1.

Since
z\in{\mathbb{D}}
, substituting into polar co ordinates
z=re^{i\theta}
, where
0\leq r<1
we get

\log(1-re^{i\theta})=-\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{r^{n}e^{in\theta}}{n}.

Taking the real part of the equation above gives

(90)

\log|1-re^{i\theta}|=\operatorname{Re}\left[\log(1-re^{i\theta})\right]=-\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{r^{n}}{n}\cos(n\theta).

Now we compute the squared integral using the expansion in (
90
)

(91)

\int_{0}^{2\pi}\left(\log|1-re^{i\theta}|\right)^{2}d\theta=\int_{0}^{2\pi}\left(\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{r^{n}}{n}\cos(n\theta)\right)^{2}d\theta.

Expanding the square and switching the sum and the integral, we get:

\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{r^{m+n}}{mn}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\cos(m\theta)\cos(n\theta)d\theta

Using the orthogonality of cosine functions,
we see that (
90
) transforms to

(92)

\int_{0}^{2\pi}\left(\log|1-re^{i\theta}|\right)^{2}d\theta=\pi\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\left(\frac{r^{n}}{n}\right)^{2}=\pi\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{r^{2n}}{n^{2}}.

This sum is the dilogarithm function, i.e.,

\int_{0}^{2\pi}\left(\log|1-re^{i\theta}|\right)^{2}d\theta=\pi\,\text{Li}_{2}(r^{2})

Plugging this in
I
and using the change variable:
u=r^{2}\Rightarrow r\,dr=\frac{1}{2}du

\displaystyle I

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{0}^{1}\left(\pi\,\text{Li}_{2}(r^{2})\right)r\,dr=\int_{0}^{1}\text{Li}_{2}(r^{2})\,r\,dr=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{1}\text{Li}_{2}(u)\,du

Since
u\in[0,1]
, the sum in (
92
) converges uniformly, and we can use MCT to switch the integral and the sum to get

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\text{Li}_{2}(u)\,du

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{u^{n}}{n^{2}}du=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n^{2}}\int_{0}^{1}u^{n}du=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n^{2}(n+1)}

We use partial fractions to compute the sum.

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n^{2}(n+1)}=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\left(\frac{1}{n^{2}}-\frac{1}{n}+\frac{1}{n+1}\right)=\zeta(2)-1

Therefore, putting everything together, we get the desired equality.

I=\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\left(\log|1-re^{i\theta}|\right)^{2}\,r\,d\theta\,dr=\frac{1}{2}\left(\zeta(2)-1\right)\qed

Proof of Theorem
1.2
.

We now assemble the results from the previous sections to complete the proof of the main theorem.
By Lemma
3.2
, we may replace the component count by its modified version:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\mathbb{E}[\mathscr{C}(\Lambda_{n})]=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\mathbb{E}[\widetilde{\mathscr{C}}(\Lambda_{n})]+o(1),

where
\widetilde{\mathscr{C}}(\Lambda_{n})

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\mathbb{E}[\mathscr{C}(\Lambda_{n})]=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\mathcal{T}_{n}(\varepsilon)+o(1)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\mathbb{E}\left[\left|1+\frac{R_{n}(X_{0})}{(S_{n}(X_{0}))^{2}}\right|^{2}\mathbbm{1}_{O_{n}}\right]+o(1),

with
