A comparison between early onset sepsis (EOS) risk calculator and modified new protocol in a newborn ≥ 35 weeks: a simulation study
Authors: Silvia Armiraglio, Simona Perniciaro, Angela Bossi, Massimo Agostino Agosti
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundEarly onset sepsis (EOS) is a potentially fatal condition in neonates and its correct management is still a challenge for neonatologists. Early antibiotic administration in the neonatal period may carry risks and reducing unnecessary antibiotic therapy remains a key objective. It is well known that EOS risk calculator has reduced investigations and antibiotics for suspected EOS, but many limitations have been identified (e.g. strong value to respiratory symptoms, lack of standardized neonatal monitoring). To develop a new protocol, called Neosafe, for the management of newborn ≥ 35 weeks at risk of EOS based on the EOS risk calculator and serial physical examination with the aim of reducing blood investigations and unnecessary antibiotic therapy.MethodsIn this single-center observational retrospective study 2000 Neonates ≥ 35 weeks were enrolled. A new protocol called Neosafe has been developed, which deviates from the original for two major changes: (1) different serial physical examination (SPE) recommendation for each risk situations (2) clinical scenario revisited with multiple signs. Comparison between Neosafe protocol and EOS risk calculator simulated data were performed using McNemar’s test (statistical significance p < 0,05) and the Cohen’s kappa coefficient was used to assess the degree of agreement among them.Results48/2000 infants (2,4%) would have performed blood investigations versus 82/2000 (4,1%) whether Neosafe protocol would have been applied (p < 0,0001). 43/2000 infants (2,2%) with Neosafe protocol would have received antibiotics versus 80/2000 (4,0%) with EOS risk calculator (p < 0,0001). All infants who would not receive antibiotics for Neosafe protocol were infants with equivocal signs and isolated mild to moderate respiratory distress, but predisposed to advanced medical monitoring. Proven sepsis during the study period were 4, 2 of them were lost by both (no symptoms at birth, blood tests performed for non-infectious reasons). 1829 infants (91,5%) would receive routinal nursing SPE monitoring, 128 advanced medical SPE monitoring and 43 advanced medical SPE+vital for neonatal intensive care unit (NICU).ConclusionsIn our study Neosafe protocol could have reduced blood investigations and antibiotic therapy. Neosafe protocol could be a promising tool that integrates the use of digital technologies and direct attention to the patients. More trials are needed for further improvements.

Background

The management of neonates ≥ 35 weeks’ gestation at risk for early-onset sepsis (EOS) remains a crucial aspect of neonatal practice. While the incidence of EOS in term and late preterm infants is lower than in preterm infants, the potential for severe complications warrants a low threshold for evaluation and treatment. EOS is defined as the onset of symptoms within the first 72 h of life [1, 2], typically resulting from vertical transmission through contaminated amniotic fluid or exposure to bacteria in the maternal genital tract during delivery [3]. Key risk factors include maternal Group B Streptococcus (GBS) colonization, prolonged rupture of membranes (PROM), maternal fever and intra-amniotic infection [3, 4].

In high-income countries, the incidence of EOS has declined, largely due to intrapartum antibiotic prophylaxis (IAP) aimed at preventing GBS infection and now stands at approximately 0.5 per 1,000 live births in term infants [5]. However, rates can be significantly higher in late preterm infants, reaching up to 4 per 1,000 [6]. EOS often presents with subtle, non-specific signs that can progress rapidly and early recognition is vital. Symptoms may include temperature instability (hypothermia or fever), irritability, lethargy, respiratory distress (e.g., tachypnea, grunting, hypoxia), poor feeding, tachycardia, poor perfusion, and hypotension [7]. Due to the non-specific nature of these signs, clinicians must maintain a high index of suspicion when a newborn shows deviations from their typical behavior or condition.

Diagnosing neonatal sepsis remains difficult, as many of its signs overlap with non-infectious conditions [8]. For instance, respiratory distress is often the first symptom of sepsis, but it can also result from benign conditions such as transient tachypnea of the newborn (TTN), which is far more common and occurring in approximately 60 per 1,000 live births [9, 10]. This overlap can lead to overdiagnosis and unnecessary antibiotic use. Clinicians must carefully weigh the risks of starting empirical antibiotics against close monitoring, especially in infants without clear perinatal risk factors. Although no universal definition of neonatal sepsis exists, it is generally understood as infection-induced organ dysfunction resulting from a dysregulated immune response [11]. Therefore, a thorough clinical and laboratory evaluation is essential before confirming sepsis as the underlying cause. Currently, the gold standard remains a positive blood culture [12]. However, its reliability can be affected by factors such as low-level bacteremia (≤ 4 CFU/mL) and small sample volumes (< 1 mL) [8, 13]. Some studies suggest that blood culture sensitivity can approach 100% if at least 1 mL of blood is collected and processed appropriately [14–16].

In the work-up of neonatal sepsis, the most commonly used laboratory biomarkers include complete blood count (CBC) with differential, C-reactive protein (CRP), procalcitonin and presepsin. However, these markers generally have low specificity and positive predictive value (PPV), meaning that while normal results may offer some reassurance, abnormal values must be interpreted with caution. Various non-infectious conditions (such as maternal preeclampsia, chorioamnionitis, hypoxic-ischemic injury, and intrauterine growth restriction) can elevate these markers in the absence of true sepsis [17–22].

The lack of consensus in existing guidelines for management of suspected EOS and in general, a low threshold for neonatologist in evaluation and treating a neonate with suspected EOS, lead to unnecessary treatment and hospitalization resulting in an disproportionate overtreatment [23, 24].

The american academy of pediatrics (AAP) recently outlined three main strategies for preventing EOS in newborns ≥ 35 weeks’ gestation [3]:

The categorical risk-based approach relies on predefined maternal and perinatal risk factors [25, 26]. The multivariate risk-assessment model (EOS Calculator) estimates individualized EOS probability by integrating perinatal variables with the infant’s clinical status [27–30]. The clinical-condition–based strategy, centered on serial physical examinations (SPE), focuses primarily on the newborn’s evolving clinical presentation over the first 48 h [31, 32].

Each approach has limitations. Categorical risk assessment tends to over-treat uninfected infants, while the EOS calculator promotes selective blood testing and treatment but it may still lead to overtreatment when mild, non-specific symptoms (mainly respiratory) are present [33]. Instead, SPE strategies, while promising, depends heavily on consistent and standardized clinical monitoring.

In a previous study, our team retrospectively applied with simulation the EOS calculator to newborn managed per CDC-based guidelines. We observed a significant reduction in blood testing, but not in antibiotic use [34]. Two major issues emerged: (1) respiratory signs often triggered antibiotic treatment in uninfected infants and (2) the absence of a structured protocol for serial clinical assessment. Another concern was the lack of attention to suspected chorioamnionitis, a known major risk factor for EOS. Therefore, our aim was to develop a tool that optimally integrates risk stratification at birth based on maternal-neonatal risk factors with careful clinical observation of all patients, under the assumption that, in our setting, this approach may be more accurate than the EOS calculator and currently more applicable than routine clinical observation alone.

Based on these findings, a new protocol called NeoSafe was developed to address these gaps and enhance both safety and antibiotic stewardship. The aim of the study is to assess whether Neosafe can reduce blood investigations and unnecessary antibiotic therapy.

Methods

This was a single-center observational retrospective study conducted at the Division of Neonatology at Filippo del Ponte Hospital in Varese, Italy. The study population consists of 2000 newborns with GA ≥ 35 weeks (the first 1,000 infants born in 2021 and the first 1,000 born in 2023) admitted to our department and managed according to the previous EOS prevention local hospital guideline (CDC algorithm [26]). Patients with major congenital malformations, major neurological conditions and major congenital heart diseases were excluded from the study. A comparison was made for the assessment and management of newborns at risk for early-onset sepsis through a simulation between the hypothetical application of the EOS risk calculator and the hypothetical application of the new protocol, which integrates the EOS risk calculator with serial physical examination (Neosafe protocol). Data collected included the following: neonatal data, mode of delivery, maternal and neonatal risk factors (GBS maternal status, amniotic membrane rupture ≥ 18 h, maternal fever ≥ 38 °C, intrapartum or pre-partum antibiotic therapy, suspected or confirmed intrauterine inflammation, infection, or both (Triple I) [35]. We also described the presence and type of clinical signs of EOS, laboratory tests and confirmed proven sepsis discovered in the study population. For the application of both protocol risk score and subsequent management recommendation were determined using an EOS incidence rate of 0.5/1000 live births. For EOS risk calculator each newborn has been recorded in a multivariate risk prediction model that combined neonatal/maternal data and neonatal clinical condition during the first 48 h after birth. Each newborn was classified as “well appearing”, “equivocal” or “clinical illness” based on the characteristics reported on the EOS calculator website. (https://neonatalsepsiscalculator.kaiserpermanente.org).

Possible management recommendations were as follows: (1) no culture, no antibiotics, routine vitals; (2) no culture, no antibiotics, vitals every 4 h for 24 h; (3) blood culture, vitals every 4 h for 24 h; (4) strongly consider starting empiric antibiotics, vitals for neonatal intensive care unit (NICU); (5) empiric antibiotics, vitals for NICU.

Otherwise, Neosafe protocol represents a modified version of the original EOS risk calculator from the Kaiser Permanente Division of Research. Neosafe stands for newborn and safe, highlighting the importance of safety in this population. The predictors were retained as in the original calculator: gestational age, the highest maternal antepartum temperature (measured only in Celsius), duration of membrane rupture, maternal GBS status and type and duration of antepartum antibiotics. Given its importance, a new predictor was added, considered an independent risk factor: suspected chorioamionitis using Triple I definition [35]. This condition is diagnosed if the maternal intrapartum temperature is > 39°C or between 38–39°C and persists for more than 30 minutes with one or more of the following clinical signs: maternal leukocytosis > 15,000/mm³, purulent cervical drainage, fetal tachycardia > 160 bpm for at least 10 minutes or more [35]. The answer options for Triple I is dichotomous “yes”, if present, or “no”, if absent. When present, this nullifies the numerical EOS risk composite value and different recommendations are indicated based on clinical evaluation.

Each newborn must also undergo a careful clinical examination at birth and during the scheduled monitoring evaluations, which further classifies the newborn into three clinical presentations: “well appearing,” “equivocal,” or “clinical illness.” The clinical patterns distinguishing the “equivocal” or “clinical illness” scenarios were modified from the original EOS risk calculator to better reflect multiple signs and symptoms of suspected sepsis and limit the weight of a single isolated respiratory symptoms. Particular attention was given to the type of severity and evolution of the respiratory symptoms: mild respiratory distress is defined by tachypnea and/or signs such as nasal flaring, grunting, and chest retractions, with or without low-flow supplemental oxygen. Moderate distress is characterized by the need for CPAP and/or HFNC with FiO₂ < 30%. Severe distress involves non-invasive respiratory support with CPAP/NIV and/or HFNC with FiO₂ > 30% and/or invasive support requiring endotracheal intubation.

A further change is the introduction of standardized SPE through two different monitoring sheet to be completed in the first 48 h of life. First, a standard monitoring sheet, that has been adapted from the postnatal surveillance sheet al.ready used in the Italian Society of Neonatology’s recommendations for the prevention and management of sudden unexpected postnatal collapse (SUPC) for the newborn during rooming-in practice [36]. It is filled out by obstetric and nursing staff with two initial assessments between the 3rd and 12th hours after birth, followed by further assessments approximately every 6–8 h for the first 48 h of life. If altered parameters are detected in the standard monitoring sheet, the attending physician is called to re-assess the newborn. Second, an advanced monitoring sheet contains more detailed clinical information that has been created by us. It is filled out by medical staff with two assessments between the 3rd and 12th hours of life, followed by approximately every 6–8 h for the first 48 h of life. Any change in scenario allows for the reassignment of a new score in a case of neonatal clinical deterioration (Figs. 1 and 2).

Data were collected into a designated database and a comparison between the different protocols were performed using McNemar’s test (statistical significance p < 0.05) and the Cohen’s kappa coefficient was used to assess the degree of agreement among them.

Results

Data population

This study enrolled 2,000 newborns ≥ 35 weeks’ gestation, all delivered at “F. del Ponte” Hospital in Varese, Northern Italy, and managed according to our department’s current EOS protocol (based on the CDC algorithm [26]).

The cohort consisted of 977 females (48.9%) and 1,023 males (51.1%), with a mean gestational age of 39.4 ± 1.4 weeks. Of these, 4,3% were born > 35 weeks and < 37 weeks and 12.6% ≥ 41 weeks. Mode of delivery included spontaneous vaginal birth (71.0%), operative vaginal delivery with vacuum extraction (5.4%), and cesarean Sect. (23.6%). Meconium-stained or foul-smelling amniotic fluid was present in 8.3% of births.

Average birth weight was 3,230 ± 465 g. Most newborns (83.3%) were appropriate for gestational age (AGA), while 11.3% were small for gestational age (SGA 2,548 ± 325 g) and 5.4% were large for gestational age (LGA 4,061 ± 265 g).

Regarding infection risk, 74.6% of mothers tested negative for GBS, 9.8% had unknown status, and 15.6% were GBS-positive. Among the 312 GBS-positive mothers, 49.7% received adequate intrapartum antibiotic prophylaxis (IAP), while 50.3% received incomplete or no IAP, according to the CDC 2010 revised guidelines [25].

Premature rupture of membranes (PROM > 18 h) occurred in 10% of cases; among these, 55.5% received antibiotic therapy, while 44.5% did not.

A comparable proportion of newborns were identified as symptomatic (equivocal/clinical illness) by the two approaches: 5.6% (n = 111) using the EOS risk calculator and 6.8% (n = 135) with the Neosafe protocol. However, differences emerged in how each protocol categorized and interpreted clinical signs.

In the EOS risk calculator, 31 have been categorized as having “equivocal” and 80 neonates as having “clinical illness,” of them 57 (71.3%) required respiratory support (HFNC, nCPAP, or mechanical ventilation), 10 needed supplemental oxygen ≥ 2 h (12.5%), 10 neonates developed encephalopathy with seizures or Apgar < 5 at 5 min and 3 required vasoactive drugs due to hemodynamic instability.

Many neonates labeled as “clinical illness” by the EOS calculator were considered “equivocal” by Neosafe, largely due to isolated mild/moderate respiratory symptoms. Conversely, some signs considered significant by Neosafe (e.g., altered tone or skin color) are not included in the EOS calculator algorithm, leading to divergent categorizations. Notably, 22 neonates categorized as “equivocal” by Neosafe were labeled as “well-appearing” by the EOS calculator, 50% underwent diagnostic investigations and 3 started antibiotic therapy; no cases of confirmed sepsis were observed.

The EOS risk calculator recommends assessing neonatal vital signs at predefined intervals based on risk stratification. In contrast, the Neosafe protocol integrates serial physical examinations (SPE) with standardized monitoring sheets tailored to risk categories.

Discussion

Early-onset neonatal sepsis is a complex and potentially severe condition that requires prompt diagnosis and appropriate decision-making for treatment. Identifying the appropriate diagnostic and therapeutic approach remains a challenge for neonatologists managing patients at risk of early-onset sepsis on a daily basis [3].

In this context, we propose the Neosafe protocol, designed for neonates ≥ 35 weeks’ gestation, as an integrative approach combining the strengths of the EOS risk calculator and serial physical examinations (SPE), particularly valuable in ambiguous cases such as those previously described.

In our simulated cohort, the Neosafe protocol would have led to significantly fewer diagnostic evaluations (2.4% vs. 4.1%) and antibiotic treatments (2.2% vs. 4.0%) compared to the EOS calculator, with statistically significant reductions in both outcomes.

A key divergence between the two approaches lies in the interpretation of respiratory symptoms, the most common yet non-specific clinical sign in the early neonatal period. 44 neonates categorized as “clinical illness” by the EOS calculator were reclassified as “equivocal” by Neosafe, avoiding unnecessary blood tests and antibiotics. This reclassification was grounded in the understanding that respiratory distress, particularly TTN, often mimics sepsis and leads to overtreatment, as previously noted by Cavigioli et al. [37]. By introducing a tiered classification of respiratory distress (mild, moderate, severe), the Neosafe protocol supports clinician to led observation before initiating a full diagnostic-therapeutic pathway.

Thus, if a patient exhibits only mild or moderate respiratory symptoms, the Neosafe protocol classifies the presentation as “equivocal,” leaving it to the clinician’s judgment to monitor for the appearance or progression of other clinical signs before initiating antibiotic therapy.

The protocol also refines the use of laboratory biomarkers, reserving CBC, CRP, and blood culture for neonates with concerning clinical signs rather than routine application. This targeted strategy may increases the positive predictive value of blood investigations and aligns with current concerns regarding the low specificity of inflammatory markers in the immediate neonatal period.

Importantly, Neosafe proposes a substantial revision of clinical classifications, recognizing presentations overlooked by the EOS calculator. In our cohort, seven neonates would have been classified as “well-appearing” or “equivocal” by the EOS calculator but were reclassified as “clinical illness” due to persistent multi-system abnormalities (e.g., respiratory distress plus altered tone or perfusion). Although all these neonates had negative blood cultures; they, in our view, represent a subgroup at increased risk of infection, in whom early intervention may be life-saving. The primary aim of any protocol designed to prevent early-onset sepsis (EOS) is the prompt identification of truly infected neonates. In this context, the Neosafe protocol appears to be a useful tool, as it reduces the number of neonates considered for treatment; however, its ability to accurately target therapy exclusively to cases of true infection could be further refined.

The Neosafe protocol’s emphasis on structured clinical observation, implemented via standard and advanced monitoring sheet, could provides a practical framework for continuous assessment. While this intensified clinical surveillance (91.5% standard monitoring sheet and 8.5% advanced monitoring sheet) may modestly increase the workload, it represents a targeted, high-yield investment in care quality. Importantly, it may also help prevent unnecessary NICU admissions and support rooming-in practices, which are essential for early bonding and breastfeeding. In this study, a comparison with clinical observation alone was not feasible. In our context, and considering the need for adequate training of all healthcare staff, a framework offering two levels of observation based on maternal-neonatal risk factors and clearly defined clinical criteria may represent a supportive and pragmatic approach.

Regarding proven EOS, two out of four neonates with positive blood cultures would have been promptly identified by Neosafe protocol due to clinical signs. The remaining two cases, one with GBS and one with Enterococcus faecalis, raise questions about the clinical relevance of positive cultures in well-appearing infants. These findings may indicate a sample contamination (in the case of Enterococcus faecalis) or transient bacteremia (in the case of GBS). Certainly, identifying 2 of 4 cases (or 2 of 3 if the first case is considered contaminated) may give the impression Neosafe protocol is insufficiently safe. However, this interpretation should be weighed against the very small sample size and the highly atypical presentation of the reported cases, with the exception of the last case.

A final point of divergence lies in the management of neonates born to febrile mothers. The Neosafe protocol, incorporating Higgins’ criteria for Triple I, adopts a cautious but proportionate approach. In fact it recommends advanced SPE monitoring for this subgroup, enabling early detection detection of any clinical signs without unnecessary overtreatment.

It is conceivable that an even more conservative approach could be achieved by extending advanced SPE monitoring to neonates presenting with equivocal clinical signs and this is supported by current evidence and expert consensus [35].

Conclusions

In this pilot study, our simulation suggests that implementing a more structured protocol for the management of EOS risk in newborns ≥ 35 weeks’ gestation, based on risk stratification and through clinical observation, may represent a promising strategy. The primary aim is (1) to reduce unnecessary blood tests, which can often lead to misleading interpretations and consequently (2) to limit the inappropriate use of antibiotic therapy. Such an approach aligns with the principles of diagnostic and antimicrobial stewardship, aiming to optimize clinical decision-making while minimizing unwarranted interventions. The safety of the newborn and the early identification of potentially infected infants remain the highest priorities. Therefore, we underscore the importance of meticulous collection of obstetric and neonatal history, careful clinical monitoring and the involvement of experienced and qualified healthcare professionals. Although no optimal protocol has yet been established, as several authors have noted, we believe that any new approach should be grounded in a balanced combination of careful clinical evaluation, the enduring value of neonatal semeiotics and the enhancement provided by technological and algorithmic innovations.
