Bidirectional Block Assessment Techniques During Cavotricuspid Isthmus Ablation: A Comprehensive Review
Authors: Juan Ismael Almonte, Jesús Rodríguez-Silva, Julián Rodríguez-García, Paolo D Dallaglio, Rodolfo San Antonio, Marcos Rodríguez-García, Jordi Mercé, Javier Sanaú, Rubelin Mosquea, Andrea Di Marco, Ignasi Anguera
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Ablation of common atrial flutter is one of the most commonly performed procedures in arrhythmia units worldwide. The most frequently used approach involves creating a radiofrequency ablation line between the tricuspid annulus and the inferior vena cava, at the level of the medial cavotricuspid isthmus. The generally accepted endpoint for this procedure is establishment of bidirectional block along this line. Since the late 1990s, various approaches have been used to confirm the presence of this block. However, most of the techniques discussed here have not been compared in large-scale studies. Instead, they are supported by small studies in a limited number of patients, making it difficult to assess the superiority of one technique over another. In addition, long-term follow-up data and recurrence rates are scarce. This comprehensive review presents an overview of the different techniques: we summarise the methodology, appropriate measurement procedures, degree of validity and potential pitfalls encountered in routine clinical practice. The accumulation of favourable results using a combination of different approaches increases the likelihood that the ablation line is electrically impermeable. Thus, we recommend familiarising oneself with all these methods and using multiple techniques to assess cavotricuspid isthmus ablation.

Common atrial flutter (CAF) is among the most frequent supraventricular arrhythmias. Its mechanism involves a macro re-entrant circuit with the activation wavefront rotating around the tricuspid annulus (TA), anterior to the inflow orifices of the superior and inferior vena cava (IVC). The crista terminalis (CT) often acts as a functional conduction barrier at high atrial rates.1

In CAF, the rotation is counterclockwise, resulting in craniocaudal activation of the lateral right atrial wall and caudocranial activation of the septal wall. In reverse atrial flutter, the rotation occurs in the opposite direction. On a surface ECG, CAF presents with a saw-tooth pattern (F waves) most clearly seen in the inferior leads. In reverse flutter, atrial activation is positive in inferior leads and there is a characteristic W-shaped morphology in lead V1.2

The incidence of CAF in the general population is estimated to be around 0.09%. Predisposing factors include advanced age, arterial hypertension, pulmonary diseases (particularly chronic obstructive pulmonary disease), structural heart disease and male sex.1

CAF ablation is a procedure commonly performed in arrhythmia units. The usual technique consists of creating an ablation line between the TA and IVC, usually at the level of the medial isthmus, although an inability to obtain a block at this level often makes it necessary to create more medial or lateral lines.

According to 2023 data from the Spanish Catheter Ablation Registry, CAF ablation was the second most frequently performed procedure after AF ablation, accounting for 19.5% of all ablation procedures.3 In all, 5,067 ablations were performed for CAF, an increase of 427 procedures over the previous year. The acute success rate remained consistently high at 98%, whereas the complication rate was 0.5%, primarily related to vascular access.4

The most common endpoint in CAF ablation is bidirectional block of the cavotricuspid line, which is confirmed by pacing medially and laterally to the line of block.3,5 Although extremely rare, there are reported cases of unidirectional block, where conduction only occurs in one direction and is absent in the other.3,5 Numerous techniques have been described to reveal bidirectional block along the cavotricuspid line, with varying degrees of sensitivity and specificity; these are summarised below.6

In general, techniques to test for bidirectional block of the cavotricuspid isthmus (CTI) can be divided into those based on time differences and those based on the direction of the activation front. Time-based approaches are based on the difference in activation of two points located at different distances from the ablation line. The activation of points closer to the line on the side opposite to the stimulation later than other points further away from the line suggests the presence of block. Direction-based approaches take into account the direction of the activation front in addition to temporal relationships of the activation front. The difficulty arises in cases where activation through the CTI is slow enough to allow the impulse to travel all the way around the TA before the impulse crosses the CTI.

P Wave Morphology on 12-Lead ECG

P wave morphology in sinus rhythm is neither a very sensitive nor specific method compared with the other techniques described below; however, it can be performed with a single catheter and is easily recognisable on the surface ECG while the ablation is being performed. The technique consists of pacing both sides of the line (medial to the line for clockwise block and lateral for counterclockwise block) and observing the P-wave morphology in inferior leads (II, III and aVF).

Prior to block, negative monophasic P waves are seen because the entire activation front will move away from the lower right atrium, ascending through the septal and lateral wall. After block, because the impulse cannot cross the CTI, P waves in inferior leads show biphasic morphology with a first negative component as the activation front moves away from the inferior face and a second positive component reflecting craniocaudal activation on the opposite side of the line. It is worth noting that the magnitude of the final positive component of the P wave during low lateral right atrial pacing is usually smaller than that of the same component during septal pacing. This should be interpreted bearing in mind that this approach, as noted above, has lower sensitivity and specificity (87.5% and 91.7%, respectively) than the other techniques described below (Supplementary Figure 1).7 The advantages of this technique are that it can be performed with a single catheter and the P wave can be recognised on the surface ECG even if the catheter is not positioned correctly.

Differential PR Interval

The PR interval (PRI) is another parameter that is easy to measure because only one catheter is required. The technique consists of measuring the PRI immediately medial and lateral to the ablation line and subsequently at a higher lateral position. The PRI is measured from the pacing spike to the QRS peak in lead II (Figure 1).

In an elegant 2005 study, Laurent et al. demonstrated that prolongation of the PRI measured up to the peak of the R wave in lead II could differentiate CTI block from incomplete block.8 The presence of PRI prolongation >94 ms when pacing the low lateral atrium (L1) with respect to septal pacing (S) and a shorter PRI when pacing slightly more distal to the line (L2) than immediately lateral (L1) has a sensitivity of 100%, a specificity of 98%, a positive predictive value of 98% and a negative predictive value of 100% for differentiating CTI block from incomplete block. However, the authors noted that the results in patients with cavity dilatation and those using antiarrhythmic drugs could deviate from those reported in the study.8

Madaffari et al. revisited the effect of the PRI on the interpretation of CTI block.9 In their study, after block was achieved, the difference between the PRI with pacing medial and lateral to the line was >70 ms; in turn, the PRI with pacing higher at the lateral TA was shorter than with pacing just lateral to the line. The mean (± SD) PRI difference in patients with CTI block after pacing medial and lateral to the line using a cycle between 500 and 700 ms was 127±31 ms.9

In another study, the mean (± SD) difference between the two positions after pacing with an 800 ms cycle or 10 BPM faster than the sinus rate was 96 ± 22 ms after block, compared with 32 ± 23 ms before block.10 The rationale underlying this technique is that by creating a conduction barrier after CTI ablation, the inferolateral area of the right atrium is electrically further away from the atrioventricular node despite being anatomically closer to it. The sensitivity and specificity of this approach are both 100%, with an area under the curve of 1.10

A recent study by Penela et al. compared single-catheter CTI ablation with PRI measurement as the endpoint versus multicatheter CTI ablation.12 The outcome in terms of recurrence was similar in both groups, with reduced procedure times and fluoroscopy times in the single-catheter arm.11

It is important to emphasise that because pacing is always performed at the same cycle length, atrioventricular node delay should not contribute significantly to the overall prolongation of the PRI. Instead, the main determinant of final PRI prolongation is the greater distance the impulse must travel before reaching the atrioventricular node.

Atrial Activation Sequence

The atrial activation sequence is the classic and most commonly used technique to test for bidirectional CTI block. It requires at least one multipolar catheter around the TA with its distal end lateral to the isthmus and another electrode medial to the line, usually located at the coronary sinus ostium. To test for clockwise block, an electrode stimulates medial to the line and the electrodes located on the lateral aspect should be activated in a craniocaudal manner (i.e. from proximal to distal). Conversely, when stimulating the electrodes on the lateral side, the multipolar catheter will be activated from distal to proximal, and the electrode located medial to the ablation line at the coronary sinus ostium will be the last to be activated (Figure 2).12,13

This technique has been considered the gold standard for the verification of CTI block. For correct interpretation of the results, proper positioning of the catheter must be confirmed. Fluoroscopic projection in the 30° right anterior oblique view and the presence of far-field ventricular signals in the distal electrodes of the decapolar catheter can help confirm an appropriate catheter position when using this technique. In the event that the distal end of the catheter has crossed the CT posteriorly, the activation of these electrodes will be early even after achieving CTI block (pseudoconduction). If the distal electrodes are too far away from the ablation line, slowed conduction through the CTI may occur, activating the lateral side closest to the line but not detected by the distal electrodes (pseudoblock).14

Another uncommon situation that can lead to confusion when interpreting atrial activation sequence results is when the CT is permeable to electrical activation. Normally, the CT is blocked at high cycle lengths, and therefore activation in the case of block of the lateral face is in the craniocaudal direction. If there is passage through the CT, either because anisotropic functional block is absent or because epicardial connections are present, the activation sequence will show patterns compatible with transisthmic conduction even after CTI block is achieved (pseudoconduction).15

Transisthmus Conduction Time

The transisthmus conduction time (TICT) is another well-established approach commonly used to verify the presence of CTI block. It consists of assessing the conduction time of the electrical impulse across the ablation line to evaluate bidirectional block. During the TICT test, it is important to perform pacing as close as possible to the ablation line to magnify the differences as much as possible. CTI block is considered to be present when the TICT is prolonged by at least 50% and is >150 ms. The sensitivity of the TICT test is 100%; however, its specificity is 80%, due largely to patients with atrial pathology or on antiarrhythmic drugs in whom conduction through the CTI is extremely slow, resulting in overlapping values with CTI block (Figure 3).16

Pitfalls to Consider When Evaluating the Transisthmus Conduction Time

Conduction Posterior to the Vena Cava

Sometimes the impulse can cross the IVC through its posterior portion. In this case, the CT is electrically permeable and the impulse may reach areas close to the line earlier, giving a false impression of conduction even when the CTI has been blocked. To confirm conduction across the CT, we can pace from the coronary sinus ostium and from the posterior wall of the right atrium (a leftward, behind-the-CT orientation of the catheter in the 30° right anterior oblique view is helpful). If conduction occurs through the CT, the time to the lower lateral right atrium will be reduced because the distance between the pacing and recording sites is decreased by approximately half. In the absence of conduction, the interval is not significantly altered because this distance changes very little as the impulse still travels around the TA to reach the recording site.17

Presence of Anatomical or Functional Block Lines Perpendicular to the Ablation Line

In the rare case of anatomical or functional block lines perpendicular to the ablation line, if stimulation is applied in front of this line of block, there will be longer conduction periods from close to the ablation line than if stimulation is applied further away from the line, but the impulse may still cross the posterior portion of the CTI, giving a false impression of block. If stimulation is applied behind the line of perpendicular block, the TICT will be considerably reduced (Supplementary Figure 2).18

Epicardial Connections in the Cavotricuspid Isthmus

Epicardial connections in the CTI have been well described. In these instances, the impulse may cross the CTI via an epicardial connection with insertion slightly away from the line (usually inserted at the level of the coronary sinus ostium) and will have a shorter TICT at those points further away than if approaching the ablation line (pseudoblock of the CTI). It has been hypothesised that this is caused by the presence of musculature in the minor cardiac vein, the first venous branch of the coronary sinus that runs along the epicardial portion of the TA.15,19–22

Relationship Between the P Wave (Lead V1) and the Local Atrial Electrogram

This approach to identify complete block was described in 2018 by Yokokawa et al.25 The technique consists of measuring the distance from the positive peak of the P wave in lead V1 (Ppeak) to the second potential in the ablation line (A2) or the distance from the end of the P wave (Pend) to A2. The measurement is made by pacing the proximal coronary sinus. The cut-off points for CTI block are >20 ms for Ppeak–A2 and >0 ms for Pend–A2. The sensitivity and specificity of this technique are 98% and 95%, respectively, for Ppeak–A2 and 96% and 100%, respectively, for Pend–A2. By measuring from intermediate/final deflections of the P wave, this approach aims to account for potential areas of slow septal conduction that could otherwise give a misleading impression of block when using other techniques, such as TICT.23

Double Potentials

The double potential (DP) technique consists of stimulating both sides of the ablation line while recording potentials on the ablation line. As block of the CTI line is achieved, two potentials will appear and progressively separate. Upon achieving block, the interpotential interval should ideally be >110 ms at all sites along the line. Interpotential intervals <90 ms suggest persistent conduction. Intervals between 90 and 110 ms suggest the presence of block, but the presence of some features affecting conduction, such as: an isoelectric line between the DP stimulation points; a negative second component (A2), which reflects the change in direction of activation of A2 on the other side of the line; and maximum variation between the two potentials along the line <15 ms (parallel DPs).24

The sensitivity and specificity of the DP technique are 83% and 100%, respectively, for an interpotential interval of >110 ms and 100% and 80%, respectively, for an interval of >90 ms.25

When a DP cannot be clearly seen over the ablation line, another simple method for verifying CTI block is a DP+1 interval, which measures the time difference between the two electrodes immediately medial and lateral to the ablation line. In this instance, the threshold value for CTI block would be >140 ms instead of 110 ms. A DP+1 interval of >140 ms was reported to have 100% specificity, 96% sensitivity and a positive predictive value of 100% for verifying complete bidirectional block.26

In the study of Rhee et al., flutter cycle length was used to account for potential areas of slow conduction or a highly dilated right atrium when interpreting the results based on the distance between DPs.27 In that study, the distance from the pacing spike, stimulating the lower atrial area near the ablation line, to the first component of the DP on the line was calculated and added to the distance from the pacing spike to the second component on the opposite side of the line. If the sum of these intervals exceeded 90% of the patient’s flutter cycle length, the technique had a sensitivity of 94%, a specificity of 100%, a positive predictive value of 100% and a negative predictive value of 93% for detecting conduction block.28 The slightly reduced sensitivity was explained by pacing being performed at a cycle length of 600 ms, whereas flutter activation is typically faster, often above 250 ms. This results in some degree of decremental conduction around the CTI. In that study, some patients with CTI block had intervals summing to 88% of the cycle length; Rhee et al. suggested that if pacing had been performed at a 250 ms cycle length instead of 600 ms, the 90% threshold may have been surpassed, potentially improving sensitivity.28

Differential Pacing

Differential pacing involves stimulating two points on one side of the line, one slightly further away from the ablation line than the other. For the recording, the DP can be measured on the ablation line or at a point adjacent to the line on the other side of the stimulation. If DPs are measured on the line, the first component (A1) is slightly delayed when the stimulation point is separated from the line; conversely, the second component (A2) will move closer to the stimulation spike. Overall, as the stimulation point is moved away from the line, the DPs will progressively approach each other. When the distance between the two stimulation points is 15 mm, A1 is delayed, on average, by 12 ± 9 ms and A2 is advanced by 19 ± 9 ms. The sensitivity, specificity and positive and negative predictive values of differential pacing for linear block were 100%, 75%, 94% and 100%, respectively.27,29

Incremental Pacing

The principle on which incremental pacing is based is that points with very slow conduction through the CTI have decremental properties or functional block at high frequencies. There are three variants of this technique, as described below.

Measurement of the interval between the atrial signal in the His bundle region and that in the proximal coronary sinus: With a catheter in the His bundle that records the atrial electrogram and another in the proximal coronary sinus, the interval between the atrial signals is measured. Pacing is performed lateral to the ablation line at cycle lengths of 600 ms and 300 ms. A ≥10 ms increase in the interval between the His bundle and proximal coronary sinus atrial signals is compatible with decremental conduction through the CTI (Supplementary Figure 5).31,32

Pacing is performed lateral to the ablation line at cycle lengths of 600 ms and 300 ms. A ≥10 ms increase in the interval between the His bundle and proximal coronary sinus atrial signals is compatible with decremental conduction through the CTI (Supplementary Figure 5).31,32

Measurement of DPs at the ablation line: If DPs can be recorded at the ablation line and incremental pacing performed at 600 and 300 ms, an increase of >20 ms between the potentials suggests the presence of persistent decremental conduction between them.33

Pacemaker-mediated Cavotricuspid Isthmus Block Assessment

In patients with right ventricular pacing devices who maintain preserved retrograde conduction, it is possible to test for CTI block using two different techniques.

If it is possible to record DPs in the ablation line, a line of DPs with the first potential A1 representing activation of the atrium medial to the line of block and an A2 component representing activation of the low lateral atrium will be observed, as seen when the proximal coronary sinus is stimulated. In this technique, the activation front that depolarises the atrium starts at the level of the atrioventricular node, which is further away from the CTI line than the proximal coronary sinus; thus, the distance between the DPs is less and the sensitivity of this manoeuvre is therefore lower.

If it is possible to record DPs in the ablation line, a line of DPs with the first potential A1 representing activation of the atrium medial to the line of block and an A2 component representing activation of the low lateral atrium will be observed, as seen when the proximal coronary sinus is stimulated. In this technique, the activation front that depolarises the atrium starts at the level of the atrioventricular node, which is further away from the CTI line than the proximal coronary sinus; thus, the distance between the DPs is less and the sensitivity of this manoeuvre is therefore lower.

The activation sequence of the ablation catheter placed around the TA with its distal end attached to the ablation line at its lateral portion can also be measured. As with the previous technique, activation begins at a point relatively far from the ablation line, so that slow conduction across the ablation line may not be detected correctly.35,36

The activation sequence of the ablation catheter placed around the TA with its distal end attached to the ablation line at its lateral portion can also be measured. As with the previous technique, activation begins at a point relatively far from the ablation line, so that slow conduction across the ablation line may not be detected correctly.35,36

Unipolar Electrogram Polarity

This approach uses the unipolar electrogram signal from the ablation catheter placed on one side of the line and stimulating from the opposite side. Before achieving line block, the impulse crosses the CTI line and the ablation catheter, leaving a unipolar R/S biphasic signal. Once block is achieved, and if the catheter is properly attached to the ablation line, the activation front reaches the catheter and ends there, resulting in a monophasic R signal. This technique has a sensitivity of 89%, a specificity of 100 and a positive predictive value of 100%.37,38

Bipolar Electrogram Polarity

Although it has traditionally been considered that bipolar signals cannot provide information about the direction of the activation front that generated them, this is not entirely true. Every bipolar signal is derived from the fusion of two unipolar signals, with one negative and one positive pole. When the electrode is depolarised in the negative to positive direction, the derived bipolar signal will have an initial R wave. If activation is performed in the positive to negative direction, the signal begins with an initial Q wave. The negative dipole is usually the most distal in the arrangement on the catheter, but this may vary depending on how the signals are programmed in the recording system. Regardless, if a bipolar signal is recorded on one side of the line while stimulating from the opposite side, at the moment block is achieved, a change in the initial polarity of the electrodes (whose activation is performed in the opposite direction) is evident (Supplementary Figure 7).39 For the correct execution and interpretation of the manoeuvre, high-quality, noise-free bipolar signals are required, which is not always possible. This has limited the use of this technique.

Electroanatomical mapping

Electroanatomical mapping systems are complex 3D reconstruction tools that integrate spatial and temporal information, as well as other electrical signal characteristics such as voltage amplitude. These systems help identify the mechanisms responsible for the arrhythmia and allow the precise targeting of critical regions involved in the maintenance of tachyarrhythmias. Nonetheless, it should be noted that typical atrial flutter is an arrhythmia with a well-known circuit, and fluoroscopy-guided ablation without the use of these systems has demonstrated excellent long-term outcomes in large patient series.4

Electroanatomical maps provide time-based information with classic local activation time (LAT) maps. These maps assign a temporal value to each point relative to a reference point. If the stimulation spike is selected as a reference on one side of the line and an LAT map is created, the earliest values (white and red; Supplementary Figure 8; Figure 4) will be seen next to the line being stimulated, with the remaining colours progressively being arranged around the TA until the last, most delayed, points are immediately adjacent to the ablation line on the side opposite the stimulation. If this does not occur and there are intermediate points between the ablation line and the later points, this suggests the presence of transisthmic conduction and the absence of block.40

Examples of LAT maps before and after CTI block are shown in Supplementary Figure 8 and Figure 4.

With the implementation of new omnipolar signal processing technology, it is now possible to compare the activation time of a given electrode with that of its equidistant neighbouring electrodes. This allows for the determination of the direction in which each electrode is activated, which is represented visually on electroanatomical maps as small arrows. Unlike conventional mapping, this approach incorporates not only the timing of local activation but also its directionality, thereby enhancing sensitivity for detecting areas of very slow conduction (pseudoblock) and identifying electrical activity behind the IVC. These latter signals may appear with shorter intervals and are consistent with persistent conduction during time-based manoeuvres (pseudoconduction; Supplementary Figure 9; Figure 5).41,42

High-density mapping catheters, e.g. Pentaray (Biosense Webster), Advisor HD Grid (Abbott), Octaray (Biosense Webster), Optrell (Biosense Webster), Intellamap Orion (Boston Scientific), should be noted as high-cost tools that are useful for mapping complex arrhythmias (ventricular arrhythmias and complex macro re-entrant circuits), where they have been shown to improve efficacy and reduce recurrence.40–42 The significant increase in procedural cost compared with the limited benefit they provide relative to the other techniques described for confirming CTI block does not justify their routine use for simple CTI ablations. However, in combined procedures for pulmonary vein and CTI ablation, their use for confirming the block is highly effective and rapid.

Representative activation vector and gap maps are shown in Supplementary Figure 9 and Figure 5.

Another way to check for CTI block in real time using new omnipolar signal acquisition technologies is through Abbott’s LiveView Dynamic Display system (Supplementary Figure 10). This requires the Advisor HD Grid catheter. In this mode, the activation of all the catheter electrodes is displayed beat by beat. There are two ways to represent activation. In Standard LAT combined with Autocolor, all colours are represented on the catheter. When conduction is present in the CTI, placing the catheter over the line and stimulating one side of the line will show all the colours represented proportionally, indicating the direction of activation from red to purple. When there is CTI block, the portion of the catheter placed medial to the line is shown in white/red and the lateral portion in purple. In the middle, the remaining colours are clustered in a pattern known as isochronal crowding (Supplementary Figure 10). In re-entry mode, the catheter does not display all the colours, but rather the colour corresponding to the selected cycle. This cycle is manually selected and ideally should be long enough to encompass both components of the DP but not so long that it includes the ventricular signal. Before block is achieved, either a monochromatic pattern can be seen on both sides of the line or two consecutive colours of the rainbow are seen. When CTI block is achieved, the appearance of non-continuous colours on both sides of the line (isochronal discontinuity) is observed (Supplementary Figure 10).43

Conclusion

There are many techniques available to demonstrate bidirectional CTI block. Combining findings suggestive of block from several of these approaches, particularly those based on activation timing and direction, significantly enhances the ability to detect areas of extremely slow conduction or atypical pathways that may allow conduction despite apparent isthmus block (e.g. through a patent CT, posterior to the IVC, or via epicardial connections). To optimise diagnostic accuracy, pacing and recording during these procedures should be performed as close as possible to the ablation line to accentuate any conduction time differences. Ideally, pacing should be conducted at multiple cycle lengths to rule out the presence of functional block that may permit conduction at slower rates.
