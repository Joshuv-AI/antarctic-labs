Arthroscopic Bone Graft Remplissage Using Flexible Bone Allograft for Treatment of Hill‐Sachs Lesions in Anterior Shoulder Instability
Authors: Sailesh V. Tummala, Romir P. Parmar, Eugenia A. Lin, John M. Tokish
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Hill‐Sachs lesions are defects in the posterior humeral head from anterior glenoid impaction during dislocation. Left untreated, they can contribute to failure after arthroscopic Bankart repair. Arthroscopic remplissage, involving capsulotenodesis of the posterior capsule and infraspinatus tendon, is the most frequently performed approach to address this lesion. In this technical note, we describe a bone graft remplissage that augments the remplissage with the arthroscopic insertion of a flexible bone graft between the capsule and the sutures to fill the Hill‐Sachs defect. Once secured, the graft is compressed into the defect to create a biological scaffold. This technique combines the simplicity of the knotless remplissage technique with the addition of structural bone graft to Hill‐Sachs lesions.

The Hill‐Sachs lesion (HSL) is a cortical depression fracture on the posterolateral humeral head after it impacts the anterior glenoid rim during shoulder dislocation and is a critical feature of glenohumeral instability. 1 , 2 , 3 HSLs, when of pathologic size, not only are indicators of glenohumeral instability but also reduce static stability of the shoulder. This decrease in stability supports subsequent recurrent instability events and results in progressive bone loss of both the glenoid and humeral head (“bipolar lesion”). 4 For instance, Nakagawa et al. found bipolar lesions in 33% of patients with primary instability and 62% of those with recurrent instability, with instability recurrence rates of 0% in those without lesions compared with 29% in those with bipolar lesions. 5 Burkhart and De Beer described how the HSL can engage with a Bankart lesion during motion, leading to recurrent instability (off‐track lesions). 6 Specifically, bony reconstruction of an HSL may be indicated if an engaging lesion is >25%‐30% of humeral width or continues to be “off‐track” even after bony augmentation of the glenoid with a traditional Latarjet procedure or allograft. 3 , 7

Several surgical approaches exist in the management of HSLs, including open osteochondral allograft bone augmentation, remplissage, and humeroplasty. 8 Arthroscopic remplissage has become a preferred method, involving capsulotenodesis of the posterior capsule and infraspinatus tendon into the HSL for smaller defects. 9 Although effective, remplissage alone does not restore the cortical bony support of the posterior humeral head. Thus, the use of bone allografts for additional support may be beneficial in restoring static stability. Historically, bony augmentation has been challenging due to the limitations of open surgical approaches. 3 However, arthroscopic techniques for other shoulder lesions have been well described. 10 This technical note describes an augmentation to a previously reported knotless, all‐suture anchor arthroscopic remplissage technique 11 by incorporating a flexible bone allograft to fill a Hill‐Sachs defect before tensioning, adding structural support.

SURGICAL TECHNIQUE

Preoperative Decision‐Making

A detailed history is always collected focused on the number, direction, timing of dislocations, and the age at first dislocation. Physical examination includes a standard shoulder instability examination including generalized laxity evaluation (Beighton score), sulcus sign, load and shift, and apprehension/relocation tests. Standard imaging includes anteroposterior, internal rotation, and axillary radiographs, followed by magnetic resonance imaging or computed tomography scan to measure glenoid bone loss and HSL size and evaluate for concomitant shoulder pathology. Bipolar bone loss is classified as “on‐track” or “off‐track” according to Di Giacomo et al. 12

Surgical Technique

After general anesthesia, an examination under anesthesia is performed in the supine position to evaluate for anterior instability. The patient is then positioned in the lateral decubitus position with a padded arm sleeve. A modified load and shift test is performed at 90° abduction to assess the inferior glenohumeral ligament's integrity.

Standard portals (posterior, anterosuperior, and anteroinferior) are created to address the Bankart lesion. Diagnostic arthroscopy is utilized to evaluate for glenoid bone loss and labral pathology and evaluates the HSL's size and orientation relative to the posterior rotator cuff. The arm is placed in increased abduction and external rotation to assess engagement of the HSL. Standard Bankart repair techniques are used to prepare and repair the anterior labrum, and the humeral head is approached after the Bankart repair is completed. The senior author has not found it necessary to place the anchors of the remplissage before completing the Bankart repair, and addressing the Bankart and Remplissage in a step‐wise fashion is a more efficient approach. Once the Bankart repair is complete, the arthroscope is switched to an anterior portal without a cannula. The lateral traction is removed, and the arm is placed in abduction. These steps allow optimal visualization of the HSL. The HSL is biologically prepared using a 4‐mm bone shaver, burr, or rasp from the posterior portal (Figure 1).

Arthroscopic view of biologic preparation of the Hill‐Sachs lesion using an arthroscopic shaver. With the patient in the left lateral decubitus position, viewing from the posterior portal, the lesion is debrided to promote healing prior to remplissage anchor placement. (HH, humeral head; HSL, Hill‐Sachs lesion.)

This technique modifies the arthroscopic all‐suture remplissage technique described by McQuivey et al. by incorporating a flexible bone allograft for bone graft remplissage. 11 Under direct visualization of the HSL with the arthroscope through the anterosuperior portal, a spinal needle is introduced through an accessory posterolateral portal with the arm in an abducted position. The spinal needle entry portal is adjusted until the needle can comfortably access the anterior and posterior margins of the HSL without significant tension. The spinal needle is then removed, and an incision is made through the skin and subcutaneous tissue only. A “skinny switching stick” is placed through this accessory posterolateral portal. While directly visualizing the HSL within the joint itself, the switching stick indents the cuff on each terminal end of the HSL to ensure appropriate placement.

Starting at the posterior end of the HSL, the switching stick is advanced through the infraspinatus and capsule, and a drill guide is placed over the switching stick. It is then placed at the posterior margin of the HSL, and a 1.8‐mm knotless anchor (FiberTak; Arthrex, Naples, FL) is placed at the posterior margin (Figure 2). The switching stick is then redirected to the anterior margin of the defect, and a second anchor is placed in a similar fashion (Figure 3). Both sets of sutures exit through the same accessory remplissage portal despite 2 separate passes through the deeper tissues.

(HSL, Hill‐Sachs lesion; IS, infraspinatus tendon.)

(HH, humeral head; HSL, Hill‐Sachs lesion; IS, infraspinatus tendon.)

After the anchors are placed, the flexible bone allograft (AlloSync; Arthrex, Naples, FL) is carefully introduced through the posterior portal with the use of a half‐pipe introducer. Under direct visualization, the graft is maneuvered and positioned between the 2 anchors to fill the HSL defect (Figure 4). Care is taken to ensure the allograft fully conforms within the defect boundaries without overhanging edges. The compressible nature of the graft is able to be molded to the contours of the HSL with compression of the remplissage anchors. Excess or protruding bone can be easily removed with a grasper or shaver at completion of compression of the graft.

Arthroscopic views showing placement of a flexible bone allograft (AlloSync; Arthrex, Naples, FL) into the HSL defect prior to remplissage capsulotenodesis. With the patient in the left lateral decubitus position, viewing from the anterosuperior portal, the graft is carefully introduced through the posterior portal using a half‐pipe introducer and positioned between the previously placed anchors to fill the HSL defect. (AG, flexible bone allograft; HH, humeral head; HSL, Hill‐Sachs lesion; IS, infraspinatus tendon.)

Once the graft is securely positioned, the anchors are tensioned to draw the infraspinatus and posterior capsule over the graft, effectively compressing it between the native HSL and the capsulotenodesis repair. The repair suture from the anterior anchor (anchor A) is passed through the looped end of the posterior anchor's (anchor B) passing suture, and pulling the nonlooped end shuttles the repair suture across. This step is repeated in reverse for the posterior repair suture (B) through the anterior looped suture (A). Gradual tensioning of the sutures seats the humeral head posteriorly into the capsule and infraspinatus, with the bone graft contained and compressed within the defect. Video 1 shows this process of graft insertion, positioning, and final suture tensioning. Once other intra‐articular procedures are completed, the final remplissage construct is secured, and excess suture is trimmed.

Postoperative Protocol

Patients remain protected in a sling for 6 weeks, allowing hand, wrist, and elbow motion, but with passive shoulder motion only under direct physical therapy supervision. Weeks 1‐3 allow passive external rotation to neutral (0°) and forward elevation to 90°, with no internal rotation. From week 4, passive external rotation to 30° and progression to full forward elevation begin. Active motion starts at week 6, aiming for full motion by week 12, with internal rotation allowed at this stage. Resistance exercises (e.g., rows, shrugs, bear hugs, and forward punch) begin at week 8. Lightweight training begins at week 10, avoiding stress on the anterior capsule and overhead activities. Return to noncontact sports is at 4 months, with contact sports permitted at 5‐6 months. A magnetic resonance imaging is obtained at 6 months to ensure graft incorporation is accomplished.

DISCUSSION

The present article outlines a comprehensive approach to bone graft remplissage, performing an all‐suture, knotless arthroscopic remplissage augmented with a flexible bone allograft (AlloSync; Arthrex) for the treatment of HSL. Historically, HSLs were managed with open remplissage, first described by Connolly et al. in 1972. 13 Before this, other methods such as rotational osteotomies, humeral head disimpaction, and capsular shift were employed but have become less popular due to their higher complication rates and technical demands. The modern arthroscopic remplissage technique was first detailed by Purchase et al. in 2008. 14

Since then, refinements have evolved the technique from single anchor to double pulley and most recently to knotless methods. 15 , 16 , 17 Multiple studies have confirmed the effectiveness of arthroscopic remplissage in improving outcomes for shoulder instability. 18 , 19 For instance, Woodmass et al. reported an 8% redislocation rate in remplissage with Bankart repair versus 22% in Bankart repair alone. 20 Similarly, MacDonald et al. showed higher postoperative instability without remplissage (18% vs 4%). 21 Although effective, remplissage can lead to a loss of external rotation—Pandey et al. noted an average 20.0° ± 11.7° loss compared with 15.6° ± 8.5° in Bankart repair alone, 22 possibly linked to medial anchor placement. 23 Notably, Bastard et al. found that at long‐term follow‐up (minimum 10 years), remplissage patients maintain higher functional scores and initial motion restrictions do not persist. 24

The unique aspect of the present technique is the addition of a flexible bone allograft to fill the HSL. While most literature focuses on open osteochondral allograft transplantation in case reports of large HSLs, these approaches are performed in an open fashion with significant morbidity and may not incorporate a remplissage. 25 For example, Nathan and Parikh reported a stable shoulder in a 16‐year‐old boy following an open osteochondral allograft reconstruction at 30 months, 26 with similar outcomes being noted in other reports. 27 , 28 However, these humeral reconstructions are performed in an open manner with the potential for significant morbidity, especially secondary to compromise of the subscapularis tendon during the deltopectoral approach. The utilization of a compressible, flexible bone graft supports the utilization of an all‐arthroscopic technique combined with remplissage capsulotenodesis to promote humeral bony reconstruction and support shoulder stability in a minimally invasive fashion. Early magnetic resonance imaging evaluation at 6 months showed incorporation of the flexible bone graft at the HSL site, with continuity to the infraspinatus tendon (Figures 5 and 6). These findings provide radiographic evidence of graft integration, supporting both the structural contribution and the biological healing potential of this technique. While longer‐term follow‐up and larger patient series are warranted, these early imaging results are encouraging.

In conclusion, this technique modifies a previously described method by adding a flexible bone allograft to fill the defect before tensioning, providing both structural support and biological healing potential. 11 As noted in Table 1, precise anchor placement, secure capsulotenodesis, and careful graft sizing are crucial for success, 29 while Table 2 highlights key advantages like bone preservation and the compressible nature of the graft. However, limitations exist in revision settings, given that knotless, all‐suture remplissage has been shown to have higher failure rates than primary remplissage. 30 These factors underscore the need for meticulous patient selection and surgical technique. Bone graft remplissage may provide a simple, arthroscopic approach to augment the remplissage especially in cases where significant humeral bone loss exists in the setting of anterior glenohumeral instability.
