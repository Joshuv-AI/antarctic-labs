Distinct projections of PVN oxytocin neurons regulate the divergent defensive behaviors in response to social threat
Authors: Chaobao Liu, Xixi Wang, Jindong Yao, Yuanyao Yang, Fan Wang, Yuan Li, Qiumin Le, Xing Liu, Lan Ma, Feifei Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The central oxytocin (OXT) system is involved in various aspects of social behavior and emotional regulation. However, the neuronal basis of the functional diversity of paraventricular nucleus (PVN) OXT neurons that modulate the defensive behaviors in response to social threat remains unclear. This study found that acute social defeat stress (ASDS) activates PVN OXT neurons in mice, promoting defensive behaviors during and after ASDS. When the mice were attacked, the nucleus accumbens (NAc)–projecting PVN OXT (OXTPVN-NAc) neurons were inhibited, while the central amygdala (CeA)–projecting PVN OXT (OXTPVN-CeA) neurons were activated. Studies using rabies virus tracing and circuit-specific Oxt knockout revealed that the lateral hypothalamic area (LHA) innervates both OXTPVN-NAc and OXTPVN-CeA neurons, whereas the lateral habenula (LHb) exclusively innervated OXTPVN-CeA neurons. LHA-innervated PVN OXT neurons respond to both social interaction and social defeat (SD). They promote the fight-back behavior during SD and decrease social avoidance after ASDS. In contrast, the LHb-PVNOXT-CeA circuit responded exclusively to SD promoting escape behavior during defeat and increasing the social avoidance after ASDS. Knocking down the OXT receptor (Oxtr) in the NAc decreases fight-back behavior, while knocking down Oxtr in the CeA decreases the escape behavior during defeat and immobility after defeat. These findings clarify that PVN OXT neurons embedded in different circuits promote distinct defensive behaviors in response to social threats and provide insights into the various effects of systematic OXT-based therapies for emotional and social disorders.

INTRODUCTION

While moderate stress can help individuals cope with challenges, excessive stress may have an adverse effect on an individual’s physical and mental health (1–3). Most mice are susceptible to social defeat stress (SDS), which involves long-lasting neuroendocrine changes and a substantial reduction in social interactions with attackers and conspecifics characterized by anhedonia avoidance behavior and increased anxiety levels (4–6). However, previous studies have shown that the susceptibility to social stress in mice is not significantly correlated with the weight, social hierarchy, or social interaction attributes with unfamiliar same-sex companions of mice before experiencing social defeat (SD) (7). Instead, the defensive strategies used by mice in response to social threats are associated with the consequences of experiencing stress (2, 7).

The brain’s oxytocin (OXT) system has been shown to be related to the stress response in mice when they are threatened or attacked. During SD, the activity of OXT neurons in brain nuclei such as the paraventricular nucleus (PVN), the supraoptic nucleus (SON), and the retrochiasmatic supraoptic nucleus (SOR) of the hypothalamus increases (8). However, it remains to be investigated whether central OXT signal can alter the defensive strategies in response to an attack.

The nucleus accumbens (NAc) and the central amygdala (CeA) are important extrahypothalamic projection nuclei of PVN OXT neurons (9) and are associated with social behavior, defensive behavior, and emotional regulation. The NAc and CeA can regulate active and passive defensive behaviors by receiving different projection inputs (10). Enhancing central OXT signals can restore social preference behaviors (9, 11). The expression level of the OXT receptor (OXTR) in the NAc significantly decrease after SD (12). The activation of Gq-coupled OXTR signaling in the NAc has been shown to reduce social avoidance (3). Intraperitoneal or NAc injection of exogenous OXT during the defeat period has been shown to significantly alleviate the behavioral disorders associated with defeat, thereby enhancing resilience (12). Increasing the projection activity of PVN OXT to the CeA through social interaction has been shown to alleviate the social avoidance and anxiety behaviors in rats (13). PVN OXT neurons regulate social reward behaviors via direct or indirect pathways involving dopaminergic neurons in the ventral tegmental area (VTA) that innervate NAc neurons (14). OXT neurons that project to the CeA have been observed to reduce context-dependent fear levels (15), yet increase fear responses caused by visual empathy (16). These behavioral effects of OXT in the CeA appear to depend on the physiological or emotional state of the subject (9). However, the responses of OXT signals in these two circuits to SD, and the mechanisms by which they regulate defensive behaviors in response to social threats have not been fully elucidated.

Using Oxt-cre or Oxt-floxed mice, in vivo fiber photometry recordings, neuronal activity-dependent tagging, anterograde and retrograde viruses tracing, as well as optogenetics and chemogenetics manipulations, we investigated the role of long-range projections of PVN OXT neurons in modulating defensive responses to SD. This study provides previously unknown insights into the role of the central OXT system in regulating behavior adaptations in response to social threat and offers fresh perspectives on developing OXT treatment strategies for social and emotional disorders.

RESULTS

PVN OXT neurons tagged by SD promote escape behavior during acute SD and enhance defeat-induced social avoidance and social fear

In order to gain insight into the role of PVN OXT neurons in the defensive behaviors induced by SD, the acute SDS (ASDS) model (17) was used (fig. S1A). Consistent with previous studies (8, 17), after being defeated by resident CD-1 mice (aggressor), the social interaction ratio (SI ratio) of mice in exploring the cage with an unfamiliar CD-1 mouse was markedly diminished after SD (fig. S1B). The duration of immobility of the mice in the chamber with a caged CD-1 mouse was also markedly increased (fig. S1C), indicating increased social avoidance and fear induced by the aggressor after ASDS. In addition, time spent in the open arms of the elevated plus maze (EPM) was decreased in the SDS group (fig. S1D). The total distance traveled, the ratio of central distance, and the time spent in the central area of the open field tests (OFTs) were significantly lower in the SDS group (fig. S1, E and F). This indicates elevated anxiety levels after ASDS. However, the control group, which did not experience direct SD, exhibited no significant differences in the SI ratio or the distance traveled. The increased time spent in the center indicates habituation to the open field environment in the control group (fig. S1, B to F).

Viruses encoding either the Cre-dependent fluorescent calcium sensor GCaMP6m, a genetically encoded calcium indicator, or the OXT sensor OXT1.7 (18, 19) were injected into the PVN of Oxt-cre mice (fig. S1, G and J). The calcium signal of the OXT neurons and the OXT release in the PVN were increased when the mice were defeated by a resident aggressor (fig. S1, H and I). In addition, an OXTR antagonist, L-368,899, eliminated the increased OXT1.7 signal in the PVN in response to social interaction with conspecific mice or SD by CD-1 mice (fig. S1, J to O). These findings align with previous observations (8, 9) and support the hypothesis that PVN OXT neurons are activated in response to SD.

To investigate the role of PVN OXT neurons activated by SD in social behaviors, DREADDs, hM3Dq (Gq) and hM4Di (Gi), or mCherry control was expressed in the homecage (HC)– or SD-tagged PVN OXT neurons via the neuronal activity–dependent cFOS-tTA::TRE system (Fig. 1, A and B). The mice in the HC-tagged group were separated from unfamiliar CD-1 mice by a transparent partition to prevent physical contact (17). The proportion of mCherry+ PVN OXT neurons in the HC-tagged group was significantly lower than that in the SD-tagged group (Fig. 1, C to E). Mice in the HC-tagged group did not exhibit a significant difference in conspecific social interaction or immobility with the SD-tagged group on days 18 and day 20, which rules out the confounding effects of the tagging procedure (fig. S2). The chemogenetic activation (Gq) of the SD-tagged PVN OXT neurons in the mice did not affect the decreased time spent exploring a caged conspecific stranger or the social preference index. Conversely, the chemogenetic inhibition (Gi) of the SD-tagged PVN OXT neurons increased conspecific social interaction (Fig. 1, F to H). These data suggest that SD-tagged PVN OXT neurons inhibit conspecific social preference in mice.

The proportion of time that mice spent exhibiting escape or fight-back behavior when attacked has been shown to correlate with stress resistance and susceptibility following defeat (2, 7). To investigate the role of SD-tagged PVN OXT neurons in the defensive behaviors of mice during SD, the ratio of fight-back or escape events when the mice was attacked by an aggressor was analyzed. The activation of SD-tagged PVN OXT neurons decreased the proportion of fight-back behavior and increased the escape behavior. In contrast, inhibiting these SD-tagged neuron ensembles had the opposite effect (Fig. 1, I to K). Furthermore, inhibiting SD-tagged PVN OXT neurons suppresses the SD-induced avoidance (Fig. 1, L and M), whereas activating these SD-tagged neuronal ensembles increased the immobility time of mice (Fig. 1N). These results suggest that SD-tagged PVN OXT neurons are necessary for the escape behavior when the mice are attacked and for SD-induced avoidance behavior.

Social interaction activates PVN OXT neurons that project to the CeA and NAc, whereas SD activates CeA-projecting PVN OXT neurons while inhibiting NAc-projecting PVN OXT neurons

The OXT system in both the NAc and the CeA has been shown to regulate social behavior (9, 14, 20). Retrograde viruses expressing mCherry or enhanced green fluorescent protein (EGFP) were delivered into the NAc and CeA, respectively. OXT immunostaining revealed that PVN OXT neurons projecting to the NAc and CeA (OXTPVN-CeA and OXTPVN-NAc neurons, respectively) were spatially segregated within the PVN (fig. S3, A to E). In addition, the activity of OXTPVN-CeA and OXTPVN-NAc neurons was evaluated during conspecific interaction or when attacked by aggressors. Oxt-cre mice were injected with a Cre-dependent retrograde AAV-DIO-Flp in the NAc or CeA and a Flp-dependent AAV-fDIO-GCaMP6m virus in the PVN. This enabled the expression of GCaMP6m in the OXTPVN-NAc and OXTPVN-CeA neurons (Fig. 2, A and G). Calcium signals recorded from the somata of OXTPVN-CeA and OXTPVN-NAc neurons increased when interacting with novel conspecific mice (Fig. 2, B and H). When the mice were attacked by CD-1 mice, the soma activity increased in the OXTPVN-CeA neurons (Fig. 2B) but decreased in OXTPVN-NAc neurons (Fig. 2H). In addition, calcium signals recorded from PVN OXT neuron terminals in the NAc and CeA were both increased during conspecific interaction (Fig. 2, C and D and I to J) but responded oppositely when the mice were attacked by CD-1 mice (Fig. 2, D and J).

To assess the correlation between the neuronal activity of PVN OXT neurons and OXT release during conspecific interactions or SD, a virus encoding the OXT sensor adeno-associated virus (AAV)–hSyn-OXT1.7 (18, 19) was then delivered into the CeA and NAc. The OXT1.7 fluorescent signal increased in both the NAc and CeA during the interaction with conspecific mice (Fig. 2, E and F). However, the OXT signal decreased in the NAc and increased in the CeA when the mice were attacked by the resident CD-1 aggressors (Fig. 2, F and L).

These results indicate that conspecific social interaction activates both OXTPVN-CeA and OXTPVN-NAc neurons, leading to an increased OXT release in the NAc and CeA. When the mice were attacked by the aggressors, however, the activity of OXTPVN-CeA neurons and the OXT release in the CeA increased, while the activity of OXTPVN-NAc neurons and the OXT release in the NAc decreased.

NAc-projecting PVN OXT neurons promote social interaction and fight-back behavior during SD

ChrimsonR was specifically expressed in PVN OXT neurons, and OXT1.7 was expressed in the NAc and CeA, respectively (fig. S3, F and H). The opto-activation of the PVN OXT terminals in the NAc and CeA laser (593 nm, 10-ms duration, 40 Hz; 8-s on and 2-s off) rapidly increased the OXT1.7 fluorescence signals (fig. S3, G and I), suggesting OXT release in the NAc and CeA. However, the opto-activation of the OXTPVN-NAc and OXTPVN-CeA terminals did not induce real-time place preference or aversion (RTPP/RTPA) in mice (fig. S3, J to P), suggesting that the activating OXTPVN-NAc and OXTPVN-CeA circuits does not trigger the preference or avoidance behaviors. In addition, the OXT release in the CeA or NAc did not significantly change when the mice investigated a caged CD-1 mouse before or after acute social defeat (fig. S3, R to T).

However, the opto-activation of the OXTPVN-NAc terminals increased the social interaction time and social preference for the conspecific strangers (Fig. 3, A to D). Furthermore, it increased fight-back behavior when the mice were attacked by CD-1 mice (Fig. 3, E and F). In addition, the opto-activation of OXTPVN-NAc circuits in mice that were attacked by CD-1 mice exerted no significant effect on avoidance induced by SD but reduced the immobility time, indicating a reduction in the fear response toward the aggressor (Fig. 3, F to H).

PVN OXT neurons have been shown to corelease other neurotransmitters that play a role in regulating social behavior and emotion (21, 22). The mRNA expression of Slc17a6 (which encodes VGluT2, a marker for excitatory neurons) and Slc32a1 (which encodes VGAT, a marker for inhibitory neurons) in the PVN of mice were assessed using single-molecule fluorescence in situ hybridization (smFISH). Slc17a6 mRNA was abundant while Slc32a1 mRNA was barely expressed in the PVN (fig. S3Q). These result suggest that the PVN consists primarily of excitatory neurons, which is consistent with previous reports (8). To investigate the specific role of OXT in the PVN-NAc circuits, AAV-CamKII-Cre was delivered to the PVN of Oxt-floxed mice to knock down OXT in the excitatory PVN neurons. The increased OXT signal during social interaction and the decreased OXT signal in the NAc during attacks were abolished in the Oxt conditional knockout (Oxt cKO; Oxt flox+/+) group (Fig. 3, L and M). ChrimsonR was expressed in the CaMKIIα+ PVN neurons, and the neuronal terminals were optogenetically activated during social interaction or SD (Fig. 3, J and K). The optogenetic activation of the PVN CaMKIIα+ neuronal terminals in the NAc increased social interaction with the conspecific strangers in their wild-type (WT; Oxt flox−/−) littermates but reduced it in the Oxt cKO group (Fig. 3N).

When the mice were attacked by CD-1 mice, the opto-activation of the PVN CaMKIIα+ neuronal terminals in the NAc increased fight-back behavior and decreased escape behavior in the WT group. Fight-back behavior was significantly inhibited, while escape behavior was increased in the Oxt cKO group (Fig. 3O). However, no such effects were observed in the avoidance and immobility time after defeat (Fig. 3, P and Q). These data indicate that the excitatory inputs from the PVN to the NAc promote conspecific interaction and increase the fight-back behavior when attacked by aggressors and that these functions depend on OXT. However, OXT in PVN CaMKIIα+ neurons that project to the NAc exhibited no effect on SD-induced avoidance behavior or social fear.

CeA-projecting PVN OXT neurons promote social interaction and fight-back behaviors during SD

The responses of OXTPVN-NAc and OXTPVN-CeA neurons, as well as the OXT release in the NAc or CeA, exhibited opposite changes when the mice were attacked by the aggressors. These two PVN-OXT projections may regulate the defensive response of mice during SD in different ways. The opto-activation of the OXTPVN-CeA neuronal terminals also increased the conspecific social interaction (Fig. 4, A to C). The opto-activation of the OXTPVN-CeA terminals resulted in increased escape behavior and decreased fight-back behavior of mice (Fig. 4D) and increased social avoidance and immobility time in response to a caged unfamiliar aggressor (Fig. 4, E and F) after defeat.

SD has been shown to activate CeA-projecting PVN neurons and promote OXT release in the CeA (Fig. 2, G to L). To investigate the role of OXT in these neurons activated by SD, Oxt-floxed mice and their WT littermates were injected with retrograde AAV-DIO-Flp virus in the CeA, as well as a Flp-dependent cFOS-tTA::TRE virus in the PVN, to knockdown Oxt in SD-tagged CeA-projecting PVN neurons (Fig. 4, G and H). Knocking down Oxt in SD-tagged CeA-projecting PVN neurons did not affect the OXT release during conspecific social interaction (Fig. 4I) but resulted in the suppression of OXT release in the CeA when the mice were attacked (Fig. 4J). Knocking down Oxt in SD-tagged PVN CeA-projecting neurons did not affect interaction time with a caged, unfamiliar conspecific mouse (Fig. 4K), but it increased fight-back behavior and decreased the escape behavior when the mice were attacked (Fig. 4L). Social avoidance behavior and immobility time (Fig. 4, M and N) were also inhibited in the Oxt cKO group after defeat. These data suggest that OXT in the CeA-projecting PVN neurons plays a role in promoting the escape behavior during attacks and in defeat-induced avoidance and social fear.

These findings reveal the divergent roles of PVN OXT in mediating behavioral responses to social stress and demonstrate that OXT in the PVN-NAc circuit promotes fight-back behavior during SD. In contrast, OXT in the PVN-CeA circuit promotes escape behavior and SD-induced avoidance and social fear.

OXTPVN-NAc and OXTPVN-CeA neurons receive different inputs from the LHA and LHb

Previous research has uncovered that OXTPVN-CeA neurons exhibit a similar function to those OXTPVN-NAc neurons in promoting conspecific social interaction. However, they regulate the defensive behaviors differently during or following SD. Nevertheless, the upstream nuclei that innervate these neurons remain to be identified. To address this issue, a retrograde AAV2/retro-DIO-Flp was delivered to the bilateral NAc or CeA, and an AAV2/9-fDIO-EGFP-TVA/RVG virus was delivered to the PVN of Oxt-cre mice to specifically express TVA/RVG in the OXTPVN-NAc and OXTPVN-CeA neurons. Rabies virus (RV) RV-tdTomato was then injected into the PVN to enable retrograde synaptic tracing (Fig. 5, A and B and E). The PVN OXT starter neurons that project to the NAc or CeA were comparable and were predominantly distributed in the middle and caudal PVN (Fig. 5, C and D).

The number of tdTomato+ inputs innervating OXTPVN-NAc or OXTPVN-CeA neurons was comparable throughout the entire brain (Fig. 5, E and F). The distribution of tdTomato+ cells showed that both OXTPVN-NAc and OXTPVN-CeA neurons received abundant and comparable innervation from the medial preoptic nucleus (MPO) and the zona incerta (ZI). However, OXTPVN-NAc neurons tended to receive more innervation from the lateral hypothalamic area (LHA) than the OXTPVN-CeA neurons, while OXTPVN-CeA neurons received much more innervation from the lateral habenula (LHb) than OXTPVN-NAc neurons (Fig. 5, G and H). OXT neurons are predominantly distributed in the PVN, SON, medial preoptic area (MPOA), and bed nucleus of the stria terminalis (BNST) (23–25). Therefore, anterograde viral tracing combined with OXT immunostaining was used to analyze the connections between LHA/LHb neurons and OXT neurons in the PVN, SON, MPOA, and BNST (fig. S4, A to D). The results showed that both the PVN and SON receive projections from the LHA or LHb, respectively. The PVN receives significantly more projections from the LHA than the LHb (fig. S4, B and D). The ChrimsonR was expressed in the LHA or LHb neurons, and the OXT sensor OXT1.7 was expressed in the PVN. The opto-activation of the LHA or LHb neuronal terminals in the PVN rapidly increased the OXT1.7 fluorescence signal (fig. S4, E to H), indicating that the activation of the LHA and LHb inputs to the PVN triggers the local OXT release. In addition, channelrhodopsin-2 was expressed in the LHA or LHb of Oxt-cre mice, and the LHA or LHb neuronal terminals in the PVN were optically activated. Optically evoked excitatory postsynaptic currents (O-EPSCs) were then recorded in PVN OXT neurons. The results demonstrated that the O-EPSCs were abolished by the application of tetrodotoxin (TTX) but were restored by application of 4-aminopyridine (4-AP). This suggests the monosynaptic connection from the LHA/LHb to PVN OXT neurons (fig. S4, I to L).

OXT in LHA- or LHb-innervated neurons bidirectionally regulates fight-back behavior, social avoidance, and social fear in response to social threats

OXT neurons in the PVN, which receive excitatory projections from the lateral hypothalamus (LHA), have been shown to promote parental behavior toward offspring and inhibit the aggressive behavior of naive males toward pups (22, 26). The LHb is an important “antireward” center associated with defensive behavior and negative emotional states (27). Social fear specifically activates glutamatergic inputs from the basal forebrain (BF) to the LHb (28). The activation of excitatory neurons in the LHb during SD mediates stress susceptibility (29). To investigate the function of OXT in the LHA- or LHb-innervated neurons, Oxt-floxed mice (Oxt cKO) and their WT littermates were injected with anterograde AAV1-Cre in the LHA or LHb. Cre+ OXT+ cells were barely present in the LHA- or LHb-innervated PVN neurons of the Oxt cKO group (Figs. 6, A and B and 7, A and B).

DISCUSSION

The present study examined the role of PVN OXT circuits in modulating the defensive response to acute SDS in mice. It revealed that both CeA- and NAc- projecting PVN OXT neurons are activated to promote social interaction with conspecific strangers. However, when being attacked by CD-1 aggressors, CeA-projecting PVN OXT neurons become activated, while NAc-projecting PVN OXT neurons become inhibited. The LHA-innervated PVN OXT circuits promote the fight-back behavior when the mice are attacked and reduce the SD-induced fear. In contrast, social attacks activate the LHb-projecting PVN OXT circuit, facilitating escape behavior and promoting SD-induced avoidance and fear.

The function of PVN OXT in SD

PVN OXT neurons are activated in response to negative stimuli such as tail suspension and foot shock (32). Increasing the activity of these neurons promotes social interaction and the formation of conspecific-induced conditioned place preference (CPP) (14, 33). The proportion of cFOS-positive PVN-OXT neurons increases in mice and rats after SD (8, 9). Administering exogenous OXT via the peripheral route has been shown to exert a substantial reduction in social avoidance behaviors in rats (12). The intraperitoneal administration of OXT has been shown to improve the social impairment in mouse models of autism (34). Our studies show that activating PVN OXT neurons by SD increases escape behavior in mice when they are attacked by aggressors and increases social avoidance behavior after defeat. In contrast, inhibiting these OXT neurons increases the social interaction and inhibits escape behavior and social avoidance. Extrahypothalamic OXT systems, including the BNST, are also implicated in similar functional roles (35). The diversity and complexity of the function of PVN OXT neurons may contribute to the controversy in current clinical OXT-related treatments, such as those used to treat autism (36). The different role of the central OXT system may depend on specific circuits based on changing environments and emotional states. These findings align with the social salience hypothesis, suggesting that OXT may amplify the salience of both positive social cues (promoting interaction) and negative threat cues (promoting avoidance), depending on the brain region and behavioral context (35, 37, 38). Systemic OXT manipulations (peripheral injection or intracerebroventricular infusion) have been shown to elicit conflicting social behavior effects, enhancing social approach in avoidant mice and restoring preference in autism models yet reducing approach in naive mice and exacerbating fear-induced withdrawal (39). The differences in the function of the PVN-NAc and PVN-CeA OXT circuits in our study indicate that these variable outcomes may be due to the activation of distinct OXT circuits rather than OXT itself (fig. S7).

The role of the OXT-OXTR signaling pathway in the NAc and CeA

The NAc mediates the promotion of social reward and is also an important brain region for the interaction between the OXT and dopamine systems (14, 33). PVN OXT neurons innervate the NAc to promote social reward–induced CPP (14, 33). In addition, OXT in the NAc exerts presynaptic regulatory effects of the 5-HT system, promoting social reward behavior (40). The effects of dopamine receptor agonists are inhibited by blocking OXTR, and the effect of OXT agonists are both inhibited by blocking OXTR and DRD2 signal, respectively (41). Our study shows that PVN OXT neurons projecting to the NAc are activated by social stimuli. OXTR in the NAc mediates the social preference for conspecifics in mice. SDS significantly reduced OXTR expression in the NAc (42). Knocking down Oxtr in the NAc does not alter SD-induced avoidance and fear, it only changes the defensive response of mice when they are attacked (Fig. 10). This indicates the long-term behavior changes after SD may require the cooperation of other neurotransmitters or receptor signals, a topic that requires further investigation.

The CeA receives projections from magnocellular and parvocellular OXT neurons in the PVN and SON (15). Previous studies have demonstrated that OXT neurons in the PVN and SON release OXT axonally in an environmental memory-dependent manner and that these neurons act on the lateral subdivision of the central amygdala (CeL). The activation of neurons in the CeL, in turn, inhibits GABAergic neurons in the medial subdivision of the CeA (CeM), thereby reducing the fear responses in mice (15, 43) . However, while the OXT-OXTR signal in the CeA has been observed to increase the conditioned fear response after observed fear training (16), this indicates that the OXT-OXTR signaling system may have different roles in the CeA. Our research found that the CeA-innervating PVN OXT neurons were activated by social interaction and SD. In contrast to the acute OXT signaling in the NAc, our findings suggest that OXT signaling in the CeA may rely more on longer-term plastic changes (fig. S6). This divergence highlights the functional heterogeneity of PVN OXT projections and their context-dependent contributions to social behavior. Oxt genetic knockout impairs LTD efficacy in the NAc and elevates miniature excitatory postsynaptic current (mEPSC) amplitude (44), while PVN OXT neurons projecting predominantly to the CeL switch to glutamate-dominant release upon Oxt knockdown, triggering compensatory postsynaptic AMPA receptor up-regulation and increased EPSC amplitude (15). Optogenetically activating these neurons promotes social interaction with conspecifics and fight-back behaviors during SD and reduce SD-induced avoidance. OXTPVN-CeA neurons innervated by the LHb are exclusively activated by attacks, leading to increased OXT release in the CeA and promoting escape behavior in mice during attacks and social avoidance after defeat. In contrast, OXTPVN-CeA neurons and OXTPVN-NAc neurons innervated by the LHA exert similar effects in promoting social interaction and reducing social avoidance. Collectively, these observations suggest that the differential responses of OXTPVN-NAc and OXTPVN-CeA neurons to social threat may arise from both their intrinsic cellular properties and the selective engagement of distinct upstream inputs, including a potential neurotransmitter switch in LHA projections that drives the inhibition of OXTPVN-NAc neurons while LHb inputs activate OXTPVN-CeA neurons (Figs. 8 and 9). Correlation cluster analysis between brain regions reveals that PVN OXT neurons projecting to the NAc and CeA are not correlated and are different neurons (45). These findings may also provide insight into why PVN OXT neurons projecting to the CeA respond differently to social cues and may possess different behavioral functions. Our results highlight a key limitation of systemic OXT administration, as the opposing functions of PVN-NAc and PVN-CeA projections likely contribute to the variable and often conflicting behavioral outcomes observed in previous literature due to nonselective global activation (9, 39). However, because of a lack of corresponding markers and technical limitations, our understanding of the projective circuits and the functions of the different OXT neuron subgroups remains limited.

The potential mechanisms by which OXT-mediated social support regulates the stress response and emotional reactions

A favorable social environment has been shown to effectively alleviate the negative emotional and psychological effects of stress. Social support plays a pivotal role in both rapidly and durably reducing stress and anxiety levels and is an effective treatment for patients with anxiety disorders (1, 30, 31, 46). Recent animal experiments have shown that SBF can significantly reduce the freezing behavior of rats under fear conditions (13), and the OXT pathway from PVN to CeA may regulate the effect of SBF (13). Administering OXT to the hippocampus before memory reactivation has been demonstrated to reduce the level of freezing behavior, indicating that OXT is associated with reducing fear memory through social exposure (47). Our research shows that PVN OXT neurons projecting to the NAc and CeA exhibit similar effects in promoting social interaction. OXTPVN-CeA neurons innervated by LHb are activated in response to attack stimuli, thereby promoting escape behavior in mice under threat and increasing avoidance behavior induced by SD. These findings suggest that targeting specific OXT neurons in different circuits can regulate social trauma or other stress responses.

In conclusion, this study sheds light on the regulatory roles of LHA and LHb on PVN OXT neurons and the differential regulatory functions of the OXT signaling within the CeA and NAc. These findings offer novel perspectives on the role of the central OXT system in modulating negative emotions resulting from SD, providing insight into the development and refinement of OXT treatment strategies for social and emotional disorders.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Animals

C57BL/6J mice and CD-1 mice were purchased from Shanghai SLAC Laboratory Animal Co. Ltd. Oxt-cre (B6;129S-Oxttm1.1(cre)Dolsn/J, #024234) mice and Oxtrfl/fl conditional knockout [B6.129(SJL)-Oxtrtm1.1Wsy/J, #008471] were obtained from the Jackson Laboratory (CA, USA). CRISPR-Cas9–mediated construction of Oxtfl/fl conditional knockout mice (C57BL/6Smoc-Oxtem1(flox)Smoc, NM-cko-2115124) was generated by Shanghai Model Organisms Center targeting exons 1 to 3. Genotypes were determined by polymerase chain reaction (PCR) of mouse tail DNA samples. The primers for Oxtfl/fl genotyping were: forward: 5′-ATGATTAGCCTGCACACTGC-3′; reverse: 5′-TGCATAGGTCTGCATGGGAA-3′. Mice were bred to C57BL/6J mice for more than six generations. The mice were group-housed and maintained on a 12-hour light/12-hour dark cycle (with light on from 8 a.m. to 8 p.m.) with access to food and water available ad libitum. Eight- to 12-week-male mice were taken for surgery or behavioral and other experimental analyses. Before behavioral and other experiments, the animals were acclimated to the environment for at least 3 days and were randomly assigned to different groups. All experiments were performed in accordance with the National Institutes of Health Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals and approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee of Fudan University (no. 20220228-030).

Immunohistochemistry

The mice were transcardially perfused with 0.9% saline followed by 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA). After perfusion, the mouse brain was fixed in 4% PFA for at least 4 hours and then placed in 20 and 30% sucrose to dehydrate until it sinks to the bottom. The brains were frozen and sliced into 30-μm coronal sections using a Leica CM3050 S Cryostat (Buffalo Grove, IL, USA), and the sliced were collected in a 24-well plate with 1× phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). Slices were then washed in PBS and incubated with primary antibodies (1:1000; OXT; ab212193, Abcam) overnight at 4°C. After three 5-min washes in 1× PBS, the slices were incubated with secondary antibodies for 60 min and were subsequently stained with 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) (D9534, Sigma-Aldrich, Germany) for another 5 min. Last, the slices were mounted with an anti-quenching mounting medium (F4680, Thermo Fisher Scientific) after washed with PBS, and the samples were then kept in a dark at 4°C.

RNA fluorescence in situ hybridization

Coronal sections (10 μm) were sliced from the frozen brain tissue and mounted on Colorfrost Plus microscope slides (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, USA). Slices were pretreated with hydrogen peroxide and RNAscope 2.5 Universal Pretreatment Reagents (ACD: 322380, Advanced Cell Diagnostics, Newark, CA, USA) for 10 min at room temperature. In situ hybridization was then performed for 2 hours at 40°C with the probes of Slc17a6-C2 (ACD: 319171-C2), Slc32a1-C2 (ACD: 319191-C2), and Oxtr-C3 (ACD: 412171-C3). The RNAscope Multiplex Fluorescent Reagent Kit (ACD: 323110) was used to amplify the signal. After hybridization, the brain section was washed by 1× wash buffer (PN 310091). Fluorescent images were taken by a Nikon-A1 confocal microscope with the 20× air objective lens. The mRNA levels were quantitatively analyzed using Fiji ImageJ (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA) (48).

Virus injection and optical fiber implantation

The mice were anesthetized with 2% isoflurane and positioned in a stereotactic instrument (Stoelting, Kiel, WI, USA) for surgery. Virus microinjections were administered using 33-gauge needles attached to a 10-μl microsyringe (Hamilton, Bonaduz, Switzerland) under the control of a micropump (World Precision Instruments, Florida, USA). A volume of 150 to 300 nl of AAV (1012 IU/ml) or RV (108 IU/ml) was slowly injected into the target brain region at a rate of 0.1 μl/min. After the injection, the needle was left in situ for at least 5 min and then slowly retracted (at a rate of 1 mm/min). For optical stimulation and fiber photometry recording, 200-μm-diameter 0.37 numerical aperture (N.A.) optical fibers (Inper Tech, Hangzhou, Zhejiang, China) were implanted unilaterally into the PVN, NAc, or CeA of mice. The mice were allowed to recover from the surgery for at least 3 weeks before subsequent experiments. The coordinates relative to bregma were listed as follows:

PVN: −0.75-mm anterior-posterior (AP); ±0.30-mm medial-lateral (ML); −4.8-mm dorso-ventral (DV) for virus injection; and −4.75-mm DV for optic fiber implantation; NAc: +1.65-mm AP; ±1.00-mm M; −4.35-mm DV for virus injection, and −4.30-mm DV for optic fiber implantation; CeA: −1.30-mm AP; ±2.75-mm ML, −4.45-mm DV for virus, and −4.40-mm DV for optic fiber implantation; LHb: −1.30-mm AP; ±0.55-mm ML; − 2.85-mm DV; and LHA: −1.00-mm AP; ±1.00-mm ML; −5.20-mm DV.

Rabies retrograde tracing

Oxt-cre mice were injected with AAV2/retro-DIO-Flp virus in the NAc or CeA, as well as AAV-EF1a-fDIO-H2B-EGFP-T2A-TVA and AAV-EF1a-fDIO-RVG viruses into the PVN. Three weeks later, mice were injected with RV-CVS-EnvA-N2C(ΔG)-tdTomato virus in the PVN at the same coordinates and were subsequently housed in the ABSL-3 facility for 7 days before the histological processing. Brain slices (40 μm in thickness) were collected in a consecutive manner, with the selection at every fifth interval collected throughout the entire brain. For the PVN, a slice was taken every third slices. The PVN was stained with OXT antibody, and the brain slices from other brain regions were stained with DAPI. Images were taken using a Nikon A1 confocal microscope with a 20× objective lens for the PVN and a 10× objective lens for other brain regions. The number of PVN EGFP+ tdTomato+ cells and tdTomato+ cells in each brain region was manually counted by an experimenter who was blind to the experimental condition.

Fiber photometry recording

The laser power at the tip of optical fiber was adjusted to 20 μW for 470 nm by optical power meter (PM100D, Thorlabs, USA). The fluorescence signals were recorded by the Fiber Photometry System (QAXK-FPS-SS-LED, Thinker Tech Nanjing Biotech, Nanjing, Jiangsu, China). The biosensor for OXT was generated and updated to the OXT1.7 version (19). For social interaction with conspecific strangers, a male unfamiliar C57BL mouse (4 to 6 weeks old) was introduced into the homecage of the subject mouse, allowing for freely interaction, and a distance within 1.5 cm and exhibited interactive behaviors such as sniffing were recorded as the initial of social interaction. In the social defeat test, a singly housed CD-1 mouse (aggressor) was placed in one side of a chamber with a transparent partition in the middle. The subject mouse was allowed to move freely on the other side for 3 min before the partition was removed. The CD-1 mouse then initiated attacks. The behaviors of the mice and the fiber photometry signals were recorded simultaneously. The photometry signals recorded 1 s before the event occurred were defined as the baseline.

The calcium and OXT signals were analyzed by an experimenter who was blind to the experimental conditions and plotted with MATLAB R2020a (MathWorks, Natick, MA, USA). The photometry signal values corresponding to 5 to 10 behavioral events occurring in the same animal were averaged, and then statistical analysis was performed between groups (n indicates the number of animals). ΔF/F = (F − F0)/F0, where F0 was the mean value of the baseline fluorescence signal and F was the observed fluorescence at a given time point. The area under the curve was calculated by integrating the fluorescence signal from the start of the event to the end by the trapezoidal rule.

Intracerebroventricular drug infusions

Mice were anesthetized with 2% isoflurane, followed by bilateral implantation of guide cannulas (length: 5.0 mm, RWD Life Science). The coordinates were as follows: (NAc: +1.60-mm AP; ±2.00-mm ML; −3.5-mm DV, with an angle of 15° from the middle to the lateral; CeA: −1.30-mm AP; ±2.75-mm ML; −4.45-mm DV). The mice were allowed to recover for at least 2 weeks postoperatively before subsequent experiments. The intracerebroventricular administration of an equal volume of saline or L-368,899 (1 μg, 500 nl per side) was delivered at a slow rate (200 nl/min) into the NAc or CeA through the cannula. Social interaction or SD procedures were conducted 30 min postinfusion. The mice were perfused and fixed for histological verification the accuracy of cannula placement.

Whole-cell patch-clamp recording in brain slices

Whole-cell patch-clamp recordings were performed as previously described with minor modifications (49). Acute coronal sections (300 μm) containing the PVN were prepared by a vibratome (Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA) in carbogenated (95% O2, 5% CO2) ice-cold cutting solution containing: 93 mM NMDG, 2.5 mM KCl, 1.25 mM NaH2PO4, 30 mM NaHCO3, 20 mM Hepes, 25 mM glucose, 2 mM thiourea, 5 mM Na-ascorbate, 3 mM Na-pyruvate, 0.5 mM CaCl2, and 10 mM MgCl2, 300 to 310 mOsm, pH adjusted to 7.3 with HCl. After initial recovery at 32°C for 10 min, slices were transferred to carbogenated Hepes holding artificial cerebrospinal fluid (ACSF) (92 mM NaCl, 2.5 mM KCl, 1.25 mM NaH2PO4, 30 mM NaHCO3, 20 mM Hepes, 25 mM glucose, 2 mM thiourea, 5 mM Na-ascorbate, 3 mM Na-pyruvate, 2 mM CaCl2, and 2 mM MgCl2, 300 to 310 mOsm, pH 7.4). Whole-cell patch-clamp recordings were performed using borosilicate glass pipettes in carbogenated ACSF (119 mM NaCl, 2.5 mM KCl, 1.25 mM NaH2PO4, 24 mM NaHCO3, 12.5 mM glucose, 2 mM CaCl2, and 2 mM MgCl2, 300 to 310 mOsm, pH 7.3–7.4) at a flow rate of 1.5 ml/min (30° to 32°C) using an EPC-10 amplifier and Pulse v8.78 software (HEKA Elektronik, Lambrecht/Pfalz, Germany). Recorded neurons were identified visually by location, morphology, size and fluorescence, and recordings.

To record light-evoked EPSCs (O-EPSC), a TTL-driven light-emitting diode (Lumen Dynamics) was used to generate photostimulation (470 nm, 1-ms duration). The laser was delivered through the 40× water-immersion objective lens (Nikon, Japan). Slices containing PVN were recorded in the presence of 1 μM TTX (Tocris, 1078) and 100 μM 4-aminopyridine (4-AP, Sigma-Aldrich, 275875). Neurons were voltage-clamped at −70 mV, and the stimulus intensity was 0.1 mW (470 nm, 20 ms interval). Intracellular solution used was 127.5 mM cesium methanesulfonate, 7.5 mM CsCl, 10 mM HEPES, 2.5 mM MgCl2, 4 mM Mg–adenosine triphosphate, 0.4 mM Na3-guanosine 5′-triphosphate, 10 mM sodium phosphocreatine, and 0.6 mM EGTA (290 to 300 mOsm, pH 7.2). For mEPSC recording, neurons in the CeA or NAc were voltage-clamped at −60 mV in the presence of 1 μM TTX. The series resistance was <20 mΩ. Data were analyzed with Mini Analysis Program (Synaptosoft, Fort Lee, NJ, USA) or pCLAMP10.7 (Molecular Devices, San Jose, CA, USA) by an experimenter who was blind to the experimental condition.

Behavioral experiments

ASDS model

Male CD-1 mice aged 4 to 8 months were singly housed to induce territorial awareness. Aggressive CD-1 mice were selected by introducing C57BL/6J mice into the cage of CD-1 mice as previously described. A CD-1 mouse is considered aggressive if it exhibits an attack latency of less than 1 minute and at least five attacks initiated within 3 min. Those exhibiting aggressive behavior at least twice in the 3-day prescreening period are used as attacking mice in subsequent experiments (6). A four-time acute stress model of SD within 1 day was performed in accordance with the methodology described previously (17). The experimental mouse was introduced into the permanent cage with an aggressive CD-1 mouse (375 mm by 273 mm by 165 mm) (for a duration of 5 to 7 min, during which ~15 to 20 attacks are initiated) and then separated from the attacker by a transparent partition. Holes less than 0.5 cm in diameter were made in the transparent partition to allow for visual, auditory, and olfactory contact for 10 min (isolation). The next round of attacks was then conducted. The “attack-isolation” procedure was repeated four times by changing different attackers, with a different attacker being introduced each time. The SI ratio was reduced in almost all mice after ASDS, indicating that the social interaction behavior of mice with attackers was diminished (17). Subsequent behavioral tests, including the social avoidance test (social interaction with an unfamiliar aggressor) and the OFT and EPM test, were performed 1 hour after the final round of “attack-isolation” (17).

Fightback behavior defined as confronting and actively engaging with the aggressor, as opposed to leaning backward or merely remaining in proximity to the aggressor. Escape behavior is defined as rapidly fleeing or jumping away from the aggressor (2, 7). Data were analyzed by an experimenter who was blind to the experimental condition. The ratios were calculated as follows: Number of fightback or escape events/(Number of fightback events + Number of escape events).

Social interaction test and social avoidance test

Social interaction with a conspecific stranger: Mice were allowed to move freely in the three-chamber apparatus for 3 days for habituation. In the 15-min testing session, a metal cage containing an unfamiliar C57BL mouse aged 4 to 6 weeks was placed on one side, and an empty metal cage was placed on the other side. The accumulate time of the mice explored the empty cage or the cage with unfamiliar mice (<2-cm distance from the cage) was recorded. The social preference index was calculated as = (exploration time of mouse cage − exploration time of empty cage)/exploration time of empty cage.

Social interaction with an unfamiliar aggressor: The social interaction test consisted of a 2.5-min phase with an empty wire cage, followed by a 5-min phase with a caged unfamiliar CD-1 mouse. The immobile time and the time spent by the mouse in exploring the empty cage or the caged CD-1 mouse was calculated. The SI ratio was calculated as follows: Exploring time with the cage containing CD-1 mouse/time with the empty cage. To reduce the influence of individual differences, change index was defined as the ratio of the difference between the post-SD behavior (Post) and the pre-SD behavior (Pre). Change index = (Post − Pre)/Pre, as previously described (8).

Neuronal activity–dependent tagging and chemogenetic manipulation

The cFOS-TTA::TRE system controlled by doxycycline (Dox) was used to tag PVN OXT neurons that are activated by SD. Oxt-cre mice were injected with AAV-cFOS-tTA, AAV-TRE-DIO-hM3Dq-mCherry or AAV-TRE-DIO-hM4Di-mCherry or AAV-TREtight-DIO-mCherry viruses into the PVN. Oxt-flox mice were injected with AAV-cFOS-tTA, AAV-TRE-fDIO-Cre viruses into the PVN, as well as AAVretro-DIO-Flp in the CeA. The mice were allowed to recover for 3 weeks, during which they were kept on a diet containing Dox (40 mg/kg; 360304, Huamaike Bio, Beijing, China). For neuronal tagging by SD, the mice were single housed for 5 days, and the Dox containing diet was replaced with standard chow diet 48 hours before tagging. After experiencing ASDS, the mice were returned to their homecages and the Dox-containing diet was refed after 6 hours. For chemogenetic manipulation, mice were intraperitoneally injected with Clozapine N-oxide (CNO; 2 mg/kg) 30 min before each behavior tests.

Optogenetic manipulation

Optical stimulation (593 nm, 15 to 20 mW; 40 Hz, 5 ms, 8-s on, 2-s off) (14) was delivered through the fixed fiber implanted in the head of the mouse by the equipment (ThinkerTech, Nanjing, Jiangsu, China). In three chamber social interaction tests, the laser was delivered when the mouse approached the metal cage of the unfamiliar mouse within a distance <2 cm. In the case of SD, the laser was delivered upon the introduction of the mouse into the home cage of an aggressive CD-1 mouse. In the social interaction with unfamiliar CD-1 mice, the laser was delivered when the mouse was introduced in the chamber with a caged unfamiliar CD-1 mouse. The location of mice was tracked and analyzed using Ethovision XT software (Noldus, Wageningen, Netherlands).

Data analysis and statistics

Mice with off-target fiber tips or virus infections were excluded from the behavioral and fiber photometric recording analyses. Sample sizes were based on our previous research (50). The statistical analysis of the data was performed by applying SPSS 22 (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA). MATLAB was used for the analysis and the generation of graphical representations of GCaMP and OXT sensor signal. The paired/unpaired t tests or Mann-Whitney U test was used to compare two groups of data. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), two-way ANOVA, or two-way repeated measures ANOVA was used, followed by Tukey’s post hoc test. Statistical significance was indicated as *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, and ***P < 0.001. Data are presented as means ± SEM. All statistical analyses according to figures in the text were listed in the table S1.
