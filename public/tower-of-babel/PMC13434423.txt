Resolving intrinsic dislocation structure in perovskite crystals using pulsed electron beam with atomic resolution
Authors: Xiaocui Li, Shihua Ma, Wanpeng Li, You Meng, Heyi Wang, Qi Wang, Feng Zhu, Johnny C. Ho, Yue Lu, Manling Sui, Shijun Zhao, Yang Lu, Fu-Rong Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Plasticity is critical for processing semiconductors and ceramics and advancing flexible, deformable electronics. Perovskites like CsPbX3 (X = Cl, Br, I) and SrTiO3 exhibit remarkable room-temperature plasticity through {110} < 1\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\bar{{{\boldsymbol{1}}}}$$\end{document}1¯0> dislocations, yet their atomic structures remain unresolved. While theoretical models predict mobile, glide-dissociated dislocations, scanning transmission electron microscopy (STEM) observations often reveal sessile, climb-dissociated structures, inconsistent with their plasticity and high-temperature climb requirements. Here, employing electrostatic dose modulator (EDM)-based pulsed electron beams that maintain the original sub-angstrom resolution, we successfully resolved intrinsic, glide-dissociated dislocations in perovskites at atomic level. The beam-off intervals allow beam-induced point defects to recombine, thereby truncating the sustained period of peak defect concentrations. Switching to continuous beams instead triggers glide-to-climb transitions, where the climbed structures depend on core chemistry: anion-terminated dislocations form compact structures, while cation-terminated ones are extended. These findings elucidate the intrinsic dislocation structures and deformation mechanisms in perovskites, offering insights to other semiconductors/ceramics show similar climb-dissociation, like Al2O3. The EDM-based pulsed beam technique enables non-destructive, atomic-scale observation of beam-sensitive materials, also establishes a versatile platform for active nanostructure modulation, holding great potential for semiconductor defect engineering.

Introduction

Room-temperature plasticity in inorganic semiconductors and ceramics is crucial for overcoming processing challenges and ensuring reliable applications in next-generation flexible and deformable electronic devices1–8. All-inorganic CsPbX3 perovskites, which are renowned for their optoelectronic properties, also exhibit exceptional plasticity with shape-morphing capabilities and ~64% average plastic strains in micropillars without compromising functional properties3, driven by continuous dislocation slip on multiple {110} < 1\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\bar{1}$$\end{document}1¯0> systems. Density functional theory (DFT) calculations indicate that full edge dislocations dissociate into colinear partial dislocation pairs with identical Burgers vectors of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\frac{1}{2}$$\end{document}12·b (b = a < 110 > , where a is the lattice constant), separated by an antiphase boundary (APB), forming mobile glide-dissociated configurations that facilitate slip3. However, atomic-resolution STEM reveals unexpected climb-dissociations3 that are sessile and contribute minimally to plastic deformation, inconsistent with the observed plasticity and the high-temperature requirements for dislocation climb, which relies on vacancy diffusion9–12.

Similar anomalous climb-dissociations have been widely observed in diverse room-temperature deformed perovskites, such as SrTiO3 and Pb(In1/2Nb1/2)O3-Pb(Mg1/3Nb2/3)O3-PbTiO37,13–15, despite theoretical predictions of mobile glide-dissociations9,16–18. To explain this, dislocations are supposed to glide-dissociate during deformation to enable slip, followed by self-climb after deformation to lower system energy3,16,19. However, direct experimental evidence for intrinsic glide-dissociation remains absent. Moreover, the mechanisms enabling room-temperature self-climb, particularly the factors that overcome the considerable energy barrier for point defect diffusion (e.g., ΔE ≈ 0.88 eV/Å in SrTiO3)10, remain poorly understood.

Electron beam irradiation during atomic-resolution STEM imaging raises concerns regarding the anomalous dislocation structure15, as it is well established that electron beams can induce various dynamics, including photocatalysis, metal-insulator transitions, and super-dissolution of oxides20–22. Recent low-dose techniques, notably integrated differential phase contrast (iDPC)-STEM, have proven particularly effective for visualizing light elements and lattice structures with improved signal-to-noise ratios and reduced overall damage in halide perovskites23–26. Besides, pulsed electron beams, which deliver electrons in discrete pulses and substantially reduce the beam dose rate, can be utilized27. Currently, pulsed beams are typically enabled either by laser-driven photocathodes28,29 or radio-frequency (RF) cavities that compress a continuous beam into pulses30. They possess unparalleled picosecond-to-femtosecond temporal resolution and have been widely applied in ultrafast TEM. While traditionally limited in spatial resolution due to increased energy spread and space charge effects (e.g., Boersch effect), recent instrumental advances have enabled atomic-resolution imaging at ~2 Å in some implementations31,32. Another emerging strategy employs electrostatic deflection to rapidly blank or chop a continuous beam into pulses33,34, exemplified by electrostatic dose modulator (EDM)33. This technique operates without altering source properties, such as spatial and temporal coherence. When integrated with aberration-corrected TEM, EDM significantly reduces the time-averaged electron dose rate while maintaining the pristine sub-angstrom spatial resolution of TEM.

In this study, we employed EDM-based pulsed electron beam and successfully resolved the intrinsic, glide-dissociated dislocation structures in CsPbBr3 at the atomic level. The pulsed beam introduces beam-off intervals that crucially enable point defect recombination and shorten the sustained duration of peak point defect concentrations, while also reducing the time-averaged electron dose rate which minimizes the cumulative irradiation-induced defect population, thereby preserving the intrinsic dislocation structure. Additionally, the EDM-based technique enables seamless in situ switching to a continuous beam, enabling a rapid glide-to-climb transition at room temperature, driven by the high concentration of irradiation-induced point defects. These findings were further validated in SrTiO3, where similar intrinsic glide-dissociation and dynamic irradiation-driven self-climb were observed, confirming their relevance across perovskite materials. We also reveal that dislocation core chemistry and related charge states govern climb-dissociated structures: anion-terminated cores form compact configurations (dissociation width ≈ 1b), while cation-terminated cores exhibit wider dissociation (dissociation width ≈ 3b).

These results elucidate the long-standing discrepancy regarding dislocation structures in perovskites by resolving the intrinsic glide-dissociations and beam-induced self-climb. This knowledge likely extends to other inorganic semiconductors and ceramics, such as Al2O3, where anomalous climb-dissociations are consistently observed instead of mobile glide-dissociations35,36. Our results establish the EDM-based pulsed electron beam technique as a powerful platform for probing intrinsic defect structures, as it effectively mitigates beam effects while preserving atomic resolution. By comparing the mechanical properties of glide- and climb-dissociated dislocations through molecular dynamics (MD) simulations, we demonstrate the potential to optimize mechanical properties through targeted microstructural modification, paving the way for the rational, property-by-design of advanced semiconductors and energy conversion materials through defect engineering.

Resolving intrinsic dislocation structures in CsPbBr3 by EDM-based pulsed electron beam

Conventional continuous electron beams (Fig. 2a) deliver high dose rates during atomic-resolution STEM imaging, raising concerns regarding potential beam-induced structural modification15. To address this, we employed a programmable pulsed electron beam generated by an EDM (Fig. 2b), which electrostatically deflects the continuous beam into pulses without altering beam properties, preserving native sub-angstrom resolution while substantially reducing time-averaged dose rates and mitigating irradiation effects. To achieve pixel-by-pixel dose rate modulation during STEM imaging, the pulsed beam period was synchronized with the electron probe’s dwell time on each pixel, both set to 4 µs. With a 25% duty cycle, the beam is on for 1 µs and off for 3 µs within each 4 µs pixel dwell time/pulse period. Consequently, while the beam-on electron dose rate Φon (which solely considers beam-on period) is the same as that of the continuous beam Φcontinuous \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$( \sim 1.14\cdot {10}^{11}{{\rm{e}}}^{-}{\rm{n}}{{\rm{m}}}^{-2}{{\rm{s}}}^{-1})$$\end{document}(~1.14⋅1011e−nm−2s−1), the time-averaged dose rate Φave of pulsed beam is reduced to 25% of Φcontinuous. Under such setup, we resolved the intrinsic, glide-dissociated dislocations in plastically deformed CsPbBr3 with unprecedented clarity (Fig. 2c). Remarkably, under such pulsed beam condition, the dislocation consistently retained its glide-dissociated configuration even after 26 frames of in situ imaging (Fig. 2d), despite stochastic positional fluctuations of the partial dislocations within localized regions due to electron-beam energy transfer, as can be seen from Supplementary Movie 1. Furthermore, closing the EDM enables seamless in situ switching from the pulsed electron beam to a continuous beam, whose dose rate Φcontinuous is fourfold that of Φave in the pulsed condition, without the need for realignment or focus adjustment, allowing direct comparison of dislocation behavior under different beam conditions. Imaging the same dislocation under the continuous electron beam triggers a gradual glide-to-climb transition even at room temperature, as revealed in Supplementary Movie 1 (frames 27-31) and Fig. 2e, f. Minor adjustments of dislocation positions progressively reduce the dissociation width w (distance along the slip direction) and increase the climb height h (distance perpendicular to the slip direction). Critically, once climb has initiated, the resulting configuration adopts a lower-energy state relative to the pristine glide-dissociated structure16,19, rendering the climb process irreversible. The initially parallel partial dislocations (marked by white dashed “⊥” symbols in Fig. 2f) eventually stabilize in a climb-dissociated configuration, as depicted by the yellow “⊥” symbols. This observation clarifies that the commonly observed room-temperature climb-dissociation is induced by electron beam irradiation, not an intrinsic or spontaneous process. These findings highlight the unprecedented capability of EDM-based pulsed electron beams to preserve intrinsic defect structures while maintaining atomic resolution, making them a powerful tool for studying dislocation structures and deformation mechanisms in perovskites and other semiconductors and ceramics.

Mechanism of electron beam-induced dislocation climb

To elucidate the mechanism driving glide-to-climb transition of dislocations in CsPbBr3, we analyse the electron irradiation damage, which primarily arises from radiolysis (inelastic electron-electron interactions that break chemical bonds), beam heating (phonon vibrations), and knock-on damage (atomic displacement from elastic electron-nucleus collisions)45. At the commonly used high acceleration voltage of 300 kV, radiolysis is minimized, and beam heating is negligible due to CsPbBr3’s moderate thermal conductivity ( ~ 0.5 W·m−1·K−1)46 and low probe current ( ~ 11 pA)47,48, making atomic displacement the dominant factor. Analytical calculations in Supplementary Note 4 and Table S2 reveal that bromine atoms, with an atomic displacement cross section of 79 barns, are readily displaced, generating Br vacancies and interstitials that serve as the primary source of point defects49, whereas Cs and Pb atoms with negative cross sections are less susceptible.

The structural evolution of dislocations is governed by point defect dynamics, where the net concentration of defects results from a balance between irradiation-induced generation and vacancy-interstitial recombination. Based on the standard rate equation for irradiation-induced point defect50, the time-resolved defect concentration dynamics can be analytically obtained, as detailed in Supplementary Note 5. Here, we evaluate the defect concentration dynamics during the electron probe’s dwell time on a single pixel. This approach is valid, as the probe visits each pixel only once during the acquisition of an entire image frame, with electron-sample interactions occurring exclusively within each pixel’s dwell time.

This beam-off interval allows point defects to annihilate, facilitating material recovery from atomic displacement. These unique intermittent beam-off periods in pulsed beams provide an unprecedented opportunity to preserve and investigate the intrinsic dislocation structure in perovskites.

Under continuous beam irradiation (Fig. 2h), the identical Φcontinuous with Φon yields the same initial build-up stage (i.e., identical K0, C0 and t0) as in the pulsed condition. However, unlike the pulsed case, this peak defect concentration C0 is sustained throughout the following probe dwell time (Δts = 3.77 μs). This remarkably extended duration provides a sufficient vacancy flux and time needed for defects to migrate to the core and exceed the critical supersaturation threshold, thereby activating climb at room temperature without the need for thermal activation. As noted above, climb proceeds incrementally frame by frame and is irreversible. Once the electron probe leaves a given pixel, the local region experiences a long inter-frame dark period during which residual mobile point defects undergo rapid recombination, effectively resetting the defect population. The resulting post-climb configuration remains unaffected until the subsequent scan cycle, when further illumination advances the climb process, yielding the progressive evolution observed in Supplementary Movie 1 (frames 27-31).

To decouple the effects of total dose, dose rate, and beam pulsing on dislocation climb, we performed a series of controlled experiments (see Supplementary Note 6 for details and parameters). The results rule out total accumulated dose as the primary driver, as dislocations remained in their glide state under pulsed beam, despite doses far exceeding those that triggered immediate climb under continuous beam. Instead, a material-specific dose rate threshold emerges: below it, dislocations can possibly be imaged in their glide state regardless of total accumulated dose; above it, climb is initiated. Crucially, a reaction time window is also required for defect diffusion and supersaturating at the dislocation core, and triggering climb. Therefore, the sustained period of peak concentration Δtₛ is another key. These findings collectively demonstrate that the pulsing nature itself, which crucially provides periodic recovery and lowers defect population, underlies the preservation of intrinsic dislocation structures.

Glide-dissociation and irradiation-induced climb in SrTiO3

To evaluate the broader applicability of our findings in CsPbBr3, parallel studies were conducted with SrTiO3. Single-crystalline [001]-oriented micropillars were fabricated from bulk plates using focused ion beam (FIB) milling. An SEM-SE image of an as-fabricated pillar is shown in Fig. 3a, and Fig. S7 illustrates the atomic lattice structure of it. In contrast to CsPbBr3, the SrTiO3 micropillars exhibited much inferior plasticity accompanied by shear cleavage, as evidenced by the post-deformation SEM morphology in Fig. 3b. The pillar also exhibited a high yield strength of ~2093 MPa during compression (Fig. S8) caused by the material’ high lattice stiffness7. As a result, though a high density of dislocation can be activated, they accumulate within the pillar rather than facilitating extensive slip. Nonetheless, dislocation in SrTiO3 shares key characteristics with CsPbBr3. Namely, it also favors {110} < 1\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\bar{1}$$\end{document}1¯0> slip systems, as indicated by GSFE profiles (Fig. S9), and exhibits climb-dissociated configurations under conventional continuous beam imaging (Fig. 3d). Molecular statics (MS) simulations confirm that dislocations in SrTiO3 spontaneously dissociate into glide-configuration after energy minimization (Fig. 3e, Figs. S10–11), which persisted during shear deformation (Fig. S12). These simulations confirm glide-dissociation as intrinsic and rule out plastic deformation as the cause of climb. Further total energy analysis (Fig. S13) reveals that climb-dissociated configurations are energetically more stable, consistent with prior studies16,19, suggesting climb is likely to happen whenever the energy barrier associated with point defect diffusion to climb10 can be overcome.

Configuring the EDM-based pulsed electron beam to a 20% duty cycle, we observed dislocations in SrTiO3 adopting near glide-dissociated configurations (Fig. 3f, g), with slight deviations from perfect collinearity. Due to the smaller lattice constant of SrTiO3 (0.39 nm) than that of CsPbBr3 (~0.59 nm), even the lowest magnification, dose rate, and duty cycle sufficient to resolve the lattice structure still initiated climb. As a result, the observed structure has already started to climb and is in a transitional state. The behavior is due to the SrTiO3’s higher sensitivity to electron irradiation. As illustrated in Fig. 3h and Tables S5–6, despite being irradiated under a Φon approximately half of that used for CsPbBr3 (Fig. 2c, d and 2g), SrTiO3 exhibits a comparable defect generation rate, yet ~3× higher defect concentration prior to beam-off and a ~ 15× slower recombination rate (Kiv, Table S3). The sluggish recombination prolongs defect decay and sustains high defect levels even during beam-off periods. This sensitivity complicates capturing perfect glide-dissociated configurations. Similarly, a continuous beam with a much higher dose rate than Φon induces rapid self-climb (Fig. 3i, j), driven by the elevated and longer sustained point defect concentration (Fig. 3k). While perfect glide-dissociated dislocation structures remain challenging to observe experimentally at present, the transitional configurations between near-glide and climb configurations, together with supporting MD simulations, confirm that the intrinsic structure in SrTiO3 is glide-dissociated.

These findings confirm glide-dissociation as the intrinsic dislocation structure in perovskite crystals across composition or chemistry. Moreover, we clarify that the often-observed room-temperature climb-dissociated dislocations observed under continuous-beam imaging are not intrinsic but are driven by irradiation-induced point defects. The results resolve discrepancies between theoretical predictions and experimental observations and clarify the plastic deformation mechanism in perovskites.

Core chemistry effects on dislocation structure

The stabilized structure of climb-dissociated dislocations in perovskites is found to be influenced by their dislocation core termination chemistry. In CsPbBr3, analysis of 34 dislocations reveals that 27% among them are cation-rich Cs-Pb-cored dislocations, forming extended structures characterized by a large dissociation width w with an average value of ~3b and an average climb height h of ~4.5b (Fig. 4a, c–e). To elucidate the physical origin of the differing dissociation widths in climb-dissociated dislocations, we performed charge density analyses on models in SrTiO3 (details in Supplementary Note 9), complemented by MD simulations. For the constructed anion-terminated (O-O-cored) dislocation before energy minimization in Fig. 4k, the charge density map (Fig. 4l) reveals localized negative charges at the partial dislocation cores and a positive charge region at the intervening APB junction. The opposite charge signs of the APB and the lower partial result in attractive Coulombic interactions that pull the partials together, favoring a compact configuration. To complement these experimental observations and electrostatic interpretation, MD simulations were conducted to assess the energy stability of core-specific configurations in SrTiO3. Atomic models with varying dissociation widths w and climb heights h were constructed. As shown in Fig. 4m, for O-O-cored dislocations, compact structures (w = 0.5b or 1.5b) are energetically favorable. Their energies decrease as h increases and reaching a minimum at h = 4b (the lowest-energy structure is revealed in Fig. S15a). Wider configurations (w ≥ 2.5b) are unfavorable with prohibitively high energies.

In contrast, the initial constructed cation-terminated (Sr-Ti-cored) dislocation exhibits three positive charge concentrations (Fig. 4n, o): two from the partial cores and one from the APB junction. The resulting like-charge Coulombic repulsion drives the partials apart, leading to a wider, extended configuration as observed. The charge density analyses agree well with the energy profiles of Sr-Ti-cored dislocations (Fig. 4p), where the lowest-energy structures occur at w ≈ 2.5b and h ≈ 3b (Fig. S15b). Wider configurations (w ≥ 3.5b) are unstable and their dissociation width spontaneously collapse to w ≈ 2.5b (Fig. S16). These equilibrium dimensions, derived from both charge density analyses and MD simulations, closely match experimental measurements in SrTiO3 and CsPbBr3. The consistent behavior across perovskites highlights that core charge states and the resulting electrostatic interactions primarily govern the equilibrium dissociation width, providing a robust framework for predicting dislocation behavior in diverse perovskite systems.

Effect of dislocation structures on mechanical properties

To evaluate the effect of dislocation structures on mechanical properties, we performed shear deformation simulations through MD. Glide-dissociated dislocations with O-O cores and Sr-Ti cores were first considered. As illustrated in Fig. 5a and b, both dislocations exhibit smooth and facile slip upon shear deformation. The shear strain versus dislocation displacement curves (Fig. 5c) confirm this mobile behavior, with leading and trailing partial dislocations moving together, preserving their glide-dissociation structure. In contrast, climb-dissociated dislocations, which can be induced by electron beam irradiation, are significantly less mobile (Fig. 5d, e). These dislocations remain sessile until shear strains of approximately 5% and 7.5%, respectively, as shown in Fig. 5f. Moreover, only the leading partials move, while the trailing partials remain stationary, further enhancing resistance to slip. This rigidity leads to a stress-strain response featuring a distinct yield point at a critical resolved shear stress (CRSS) of ~5 GPa (Fig. 5g), indicating enhanced strength but reduced plasticity compared to glide-dissociated dislocations.

These findings elucidate how dislocation structure influences mechanical properties, providing a pathway to tailor material performance for specific applications. For soft materials like CsPbX3, beam-induced climb-dissociation can enhance strength. Conversely, for high-strength materials like SrTiO3, avoiding irradiation to prevent embrittlement might be the priority. By leveraging precisely controlled pulsed electron beams, these results offer a strategy for engineering microstructural evolution to optimize mechanical performance.

This study resolves the long-standing discrepancy regarding dislocation structures and deformation mechanisms in perovskites by directly resolving their intrinsic, glide-dissociated configurations with EDM-based pulsed beams and revealing the dynamic glide-to-climb transition with continuous beams. Our results provide critical insights into other semiconductors or ceramics where an unexpected room-temperature climb remains elusive. The EDM-based pulsed electron beam technique effectively mitigates beam effects by incorporating beam-off intervals for point defect annihilation, while innovatively maintaining sub-angstrom resolution, thereby enabling non-destructive, dynamic observation of pristine defect structures in beam-sensitive materials like perovskites. Moreover, this approach establishes a versatile platform for atomic-scale microstructure modulation and real-time characterization via programmable beam parameters such as duty cycle and pulse periods. The ability to modulate dislocation structures provides a foundational strategy for tailoring mechanical properties, as validated by MD simulations. Looking forward, this methodology holds significant promise for broader defect engineering, enabling the manipulation of nanostructures to achieve targeted mechanical and functional performance, thereby paving the way for advancing rational, property-by-design approaches in functional materials development.

Methods

Sample fabrication

All-inorganic CsPbBr3 single-crystalline micropillars were prepared by the direct vapor-liquid-solid synthesis method37,39,52. An aqueous suspension of ~150-nm-diameter Sn nanoparticles ( ≥ 99 %, AldrichTM) in alcohol was drop-cast onto the thermally grown SiO2/Si substrates and then naturally dried to obtain growth substrates. CsBr (99.9 %, AldrichTM) and PbBr2 (99.999 %, AldrichTM) powders at a molar ratio of 2:1 were mixed together as the source powder. The growth substrates were placed in the low-temperature zone (300 °C) of a two-zone chemical vapor deposition (CVD) system, and the CsPbBr3 source powder was placed in the high-temperature zone (440 °C). After annealing for 30 min under a chamber pressure of 0.8 Torr with Ar carrier gas at a flow rate of 80 sccm, CsPbBr3 pillars of various sizes grew vertically on the substrates. SrTiO3 micropillars were fabricated from single-crystalline plates (AldrichTM) using focused ion beam milling (FIB, FEI Scios Dual Beam system). All thin-slice TEM samples for both materials were fabricated by FIB milling.

In situ compressive deformation

The in situ SEM compression experiments of CsPbBr3 and SrTiO3 pillars were based on a quantitative nanoindenter (HysitronTM PI85 SEM PicoIndenter) coupled with an SEM (FEI QuantaTM 450 FE-SEM)3,39–42. During deformation, the precise load-displacement data was recorded by the high-resolution indenter, and the deformation process was recorded by SEM.

Microstructure analysis

The STEM images were acquired on a double spherical aberration-corrected TEM JEOLTM JEM-ARM300F2 that operated at 300 KV with a convergence semi-angle of 24 mrad. The acceptance semi-angles are 64–174.5 mrad for HAADF imaging, 17 to 53 mrad for LAADF imaging, and 12 to 24 mrad for ABF imaging, respectively. The atomic-resolution STEM images were processed using an average background subtraction filter (ABSF)53. False color was applied on the HAADF images to reflect atomic column intensities, enabling clear elemental distinction based on Z-contrast, as shown in labeled models.

An electrostatic dose modulator (EDM)33 was employed to generate pulsed electron beams. The EDM system employs a fast electrostatic beam blanking method and is placed pre-sample. It rapidly deflects the continuous electron beam generated by the TEM electron gun, enabling simple on/off chopping without compromising beam performance, like coherence and resolution. The system supports a maximum switching frequency of 500 kHz. Although this falls short of the temporal resolution achievable in ultrafast TEM, it is sufficient to synchronize the pulse period with typical STEM pixel dwell times (e.g., 4 µs in our experiments), thereby enabling precise pixel-by-pixel dose control. A key advantage of the EDM is its ability to seamlessly in situ switch between pulsed and continuous modes without requiring realignment or refocusing. This capability proved essential for capturing dynamic, beam-induced structural transitions in perovskites at atomic resolution, as shown in our results. Overall, the EDM provides distortion-free imaging combined with adaptive spatiotemporal dose modulation.

For the imaging of CsPbBr3 in Fig. 2c, d, the EDM was configured to a 25% duty cycle, namely 1 μs beam-on followed by 3 μs beam-off in each 4 μs period, reducing the electron dose rate (time-averaged) to 25% of that of a continuous beam. For the imaging of SrTiO3 in Fig. 3f, the EDM was configured to a 20% duty cycle, consisting of 0.8 μs beam-on followed by 3.2 μs beam-off in each 4 μs period.

Molecular dynamics simulation

MS and MD simulations of SrTiO3 and CsPbBr3 were performed using LAMMPS (29 August 2024)54. The short-range repulsive part of the Buckingham type and the long-range part of Coulomb interactions were employed to describe the atomic interactions and dislocation behaviors in cubic SrTiO355 and orthorhombic CsPbBr356, which was parameterized based on experimental and first-principles data and has been extensively validated for reproducing the structural properties, stacking fault energies, and dislocation configurations3,9,10,56,57. The long-range electrostatic interactions were calculated using the Ewald method with an error tolerance of 1×10−5. The crystallographic orientations of the simulation cell were along x = [110], y = [1\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\bar{1}$$\end{document}1¯0], and z = [001]. Edge dislocations with a Burgers vector of b = <110> were created symmetrically along the y-axis (at y = 1/4 and y = 3/4, respectively), with the dislocation lines oriented along the z-axis. A 60×60×12 supercell (Fig. S11) was selected to minimize boundary interactions while maintaining computational efficiency. Energy minimization was performed for SrTiO3 at 0 K, using a hybrid approach in LAMMPS, alternating between the conjugate gradient (CG) and FIRE algorithms. The convergence criteria for the CG minimization were set to a stopping tolerance for energy etol = 0 and a stopping tolerance for force ftol = 1×10−25, while those for FIRE were set to etol = 0 and ftol = 1×10−12. Furthermore, MD simulations were conducted under an NPT ensemble at an ultra-low temperature (20 K) for CsPbBr3 for 100 ps.

Shear simulation models for glide- and climb-dissociated dislocations in SrTiO3 are constructed along the x = [110], y = [1\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\bar{1}$$\end{document}1¯0], and z = [001] directions with the dimensions of ~328 Å × 331 Å × 47 Å, and the system comprises 428,400 atoms. The systems were first equilibrated via energy minimization using a standard conjugate-gradient algorithm at 0 K, followed by relaxation under the NPT ensemble at 300 K for 50 ps to achieve a zero-pressure state with periodic boundary conditions applied in non-loading directions. Subsequently, shear deformation was applied under NVT conditions along the x direction at a strain rate of 5 × 109 s−1. All atomic configurations in this work were visualized using OVITO (version of 3.5.3)58.

To qualitatively validate dislocation dissociation configurations and compare the relative energies of glide-dissociated versus climb-dissociated structures, charge-neutral models were constructed by pairing a negatively charged O-O cored dislocation with a positively charged Sr-Ti cored dislocation, as shown in Figs. S10 and S11. For core-specific models with individual dislocation types in Fig. 4, we consider negatively charged systems containing O-O cored partial pairs and positively charged systems with Sr-Ti cored partial pairs, respectively. By systematically varying dissociation width w and climb height h, these models generate quantitative energy landscapes that describe the stability of core-specific configurations.

For GSFE calculations, the simulation cells were constructed with a minimum thickness of 60 atomic layers and a surface area of at least 30 × 30 Å2. Energy minimizations were performed at 0 K to obtain the energy as a function of fractional displacement along different slip directions.
