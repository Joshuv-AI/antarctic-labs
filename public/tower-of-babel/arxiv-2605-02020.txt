Introduction

In this paper, we study a critical exponent problem associated with the Grushin operator. The origin of this operator can be traced back to Tricomi
[
34
]
, who considered the prototype

\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial x^{2}}+x\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial y^{2}}\quad\text{in }\real^{2}.

This was later generalized by Baouendi
[
7
]
and subsequently by Grushin
[
19
]
, who introduced the operator in the form

\Delta_{\gamma}:=\Delta_{x}+(1+\gamma)^{2}|x|^{2\gamma}\Delta_{y},

where
\gamma>0
,
z=(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
,
N=n+m
, with
n\geq 1
and
m\geq 1
.

In
[
19
]
, Grushin established the hypoellipticity of
\Delta_{\gamma}
for
\gamma\in\mathbb{N}
, showing that if
f\in C^{\infty}(\Omega)
, then any distributional solution
u
of
\Delta_{\gamma}u=f
in
\Omega
also belongs to
C^{\infty}(\Omega)
. When
\gamma=0
, the operator
\Delta_{\gamma}
reduces to the classical Laplacian. However, unlike the Laplacian,
\Delta_{\gamma}
is not uniformly elliptic due to the presence of the weight
|x|^{2\gamma}
, which induces degeneracy along the set

\Sigma:=\{0\}\times\real^{m}\subset\real^{n}\times\real^{m}.

Since
\Delta_{\gamma}
is uniformly elliptic away from
\Sigma
, we assume that
\Omega\cap\Sigma\neq\varnothing
. Furthermore, owing to the translation invariance of
\Delta_{\gamma}
in the
y
-variable, we may assume without loss of generality that
0\in\Omega
.

The degeneracy set
\Sigma
plays a crucial role in determining the qualitative behavior of solutions. This influence is clearly illustrated in
[
1
]
, where the authors established asymptotic properties and unique continuation results for solutions near points in
\Sigma\cap\Omega
. In particular, the behavior of solutions in the vicinity of the degeneracy set differs significantly from that of solutions to the classical Laplacian. Their approach relies on an Almgren-type monotonicity formula.

Beginning in the 1980s, Franchi and Lanconelli, through a series of works
[
16
,
15
,
17
]
, introduced and studied a class of degenerate elliptic operators, of which the Grushin operator is a notable example. These operators are of the form

\Delta_{\lambda}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\partial_{x_{i}}\bigl(\lambda_{i}^{2}\,\partial_{x_{i}}\bigr),

where the coefficients
\lambda_{i}
satisfy suitable admissibility conditions.

Furthermore, the Grushin operator provides a fundamental example of an
X
-elliptic operator in the sense of
[
26
]
. More precisely, it is uniformly
X
-elliptic with respect to the family of vector fields
X=(X_{1},\dots,X_{N})
defined by

X_{i}=\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{i}},\quad i=1,\dots,n;\qquad X_{n+j}=|x|^{\gamma}\,\frac{\partial}{\partial y_{j}},\quad j=1,\dots,m.

With this notation, the Grushin operator can be written in the sum-of-squares form

\Delta_{\gamma}=\sum_{j=1}^{N}X_{j}^{2}.

Moreover, when
\gamma\in\mathbb{N}
, the Grushin operator belongs to the class of Hörmander operators, as it can be expressed as a sum of squares of smooth vector fields generating a Lie algebra of maximal rank at every point. In particular, the system
X=(X_{1},\dots,X_{N})
satisfies Hörmander’s finite rank condition; see
[
24
]
.

The Grushin operator exhibits a mixed homogeneity that depends on whether the underlying points lie on the degeneracy set or away from it. On the set
\Sigma
, the associated homogeneous dimension is given by

Q=m+(1+\gamma)n.

(1.1)

In contrast, away from
\Sigma
, the operator becomes uniformly elliptic, and the homogeneous dimension coincides with the classical Euclidean one, namely
N=n+m
.

To describe the natural geometry induced by the operator, for each
t>0
we introduce the anisotropic dilations
\delta_{t}:\mathbb{R}^{N}\to\mathbb{R}^{N}
associated with
\Delta_{\gamma}
, defined by

\delta_{t}(x,y)=(tx,t^{1+\gamma}y),\quad\text{for all }(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{N}=\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}.

(1.2)

With respect to these dilations, the operator
\Delta_{\gamma}
is homogeneous of degree two. More precisely, one has

\Delta_{\gamma}(u\circ\delta_{t})=t^{2}(\Delta_{\gamma}u)\circ\delta_{t}.

We denote by
T:\mathbb{R}^{N}\to\mathbb{R}^{N}
the infinitesimal generator of the group
\{\delta_{t}\}_{t>0}
, that is, the vector field

T(x,y)=(x,(1+\gamma)y),\quad\text{for all }(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}.

A natural gauge associated with the Grushin operator is defined by

d(z)=\left(|x|^{2(1+\gamma)}+|y|^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2(1+\gamma)}},

(1.3)

where
z=(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}
. The function
d
is homogeneous of degree one with respect to the dilations
\delta_{t}
defined in (
1.2
). Accordingly, for any
z_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
and
r>0
, we define the
d
-ball by

B_{d}(z_{0},r)=\{z\in\mathbb{R}^{N}:d(z-z_{0})<r\}.

It follows directly from the homogeneity of
d
that

\delta_{t}\bigl(B_{d}(z_{0},r)\bigr)=B_{d}\bigl(\delta_{t}(z_{0}),tr\bigr).

In fact, the gauge function introduced in (
1.3
) provides the fundamental solution of the Grushin operator. More precisely, there exists a constant
C>0
, depending only on
\gamma
and
Q
, such that

\Gamma(z)=\frac{C}{d(z)^{Q-2}}

is the fundamental solution of
-\Delta_{\gamma}
with pole at the origin.

The Grushin operator also admits a representation in polar-type coordinates adapted to its intrinsic geometry; see
[
18
]
. Specifically, one has

\Delta_{\gamma}=\vartheta_{\gamma}\left(\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\rho^{2}}+\frac{Q-1}{\rho}\frac{\partial}{\partial\rho}+\left(\frac{\gamma+1}{\rho}\right)^{2}\mathcal{L}_{\theta}\right),

where
\rho=d(x,y)
, with
d
as in (
1.3
), and
\theta\in\mathbb{S}^{N-1}:=\{(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{N}:d(x,y)=1\}
. Moreover,
\vartheta_{\gamma}(x,y)=\frac{|x|^{2\gamma}}{d(x,y)^{2\gamma}}
for
(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{N}\setminus\{0\}
, and
\mathcal{L}_{\theta}
denotes the analogue of the Laplace–Beltrami operator acting on the Grushin unit sphere
\mathbb{S}^{N-1}
.

The homogeneous dimension
Q
defined in (
1.1
) plays a fundamental role in the associated Sobolev embedding; see
[
31
]
. In particular, one has

\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}|u|^{2_{\gamma}^{*}}\,dz\right)^{\frac{1}{2_{\gamma}^{*}}}\leq C\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}|\nabla_{\gamma}u|^{2}\,dz\right)^{\frac{1}{2}},

for all
u\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\Omega)
, where
2_{\gamma}^{*}=\frac{2Q}{Q-2}
denotes the critical Sobolev exponent.

This naturally leads to the study of critical exponent problems associated with the Grushin operator. Such problems trace their origin to the seminal work of Brezis and Nirenberg
[
9
]
for the Laplacian on bounded domains. In the Grushin setting, a systematic investigation was initiated by Monti and Morbidelli
[
31
]
, who studied the critical problem

-\Delta_{\gamma}u=u^{2_{\gamma}^{*}-1}\quad\text{in }\mathbb{R}^{N}.

(1.4)

They established existence results and proved symmetry properties of solutions via a Kelvin transform adapted to the Grushin structure. Subsequently, Loiudice
[
29
]
analyzed the asymptotic behavior of solutions to (
1.4
).

These developments have stimulated extensive research on critical exponent problems in various settings; see, for instance,
[
5
,
30
,
35

36
]
and the references therein. In particular, an analogue of the Brezis–Nirenberg theorem for the Grushin operator was established in
[
3
]
, where also a result of Berestycki–Lions type was obtained.

One important class of perturbations of critical exponent problems involves singular nonlinearities. Before addressing the corresponding critical problems, it is instructive to recall the purely singular case. A seminal contribution in this direction is due to Crandall
et al.

[
12
]
, who studied the model problem

\left\{\begin{aligned} -\Delta u&=u^{-\delta},\quad u>0&&\text{in }\Omega,\\
u&=0&&\text{on }\partial\Omega,\end{aligned}\right.

(1.5)

for
\delta>0

that the solution

if and only if

The study of critical problems with singular perturbations was initiated by Hirano
et al.
, who established the existence of at least two solutions for
\delta\in(0,1)
via the fibering map method on the Nehari manifold. Independently, Haito
[
21

obtained analogous results using the sub–supersolution method. These results were later extended to the range

[
2

[

employed tools from nonsmooth analysis (see also

and the appendix of
[

Motivated by these developments, we investigate analogous problems in the Grushin setting. For the case

[
5

. More recently, in
[
6

2.1
Nonsmooth Analysis

As we are interested in problem with a singular nonlinearity, we need some tools of non-smooth analysis to deal with it. First we introduce the Fréchet subdifferential point for a non-smooth functional that will work as the notion of the critical point.

Let
H
be a Hilbert space and let
I:H\to(-\infty,\infty]
be a functional with its effective domain defined as

\mathcal{D}(I):=\{u\in H:I(u)<\infty\}.

If

I:H\to(-\infty,\infty]
be a proper, lower semicontinuous functional.
Then for every

, the Fréchet subdifferential of

at

\partial^{-}I(u)=\left\{\alpha\in H:\lim_{v\to u}\frac{I(v)-I(u)-\langle\alpha,v-u\rangle}{\|v-u\|}\geq 0\right\}.

The subdifferential satisfies the following properties.

1.

\partial^{-}I(u)
may be empty; however, it is always closed and convex.

2.

If

is a local minimizer of

, then
0\in\partial^{-}I(u)
.

3.

If

is of class

, and

, then the Fréchet subdifferential satisfies

\partial^{-}I(u)=\partial I_{0}(u)+\nabla_{\gamma}I_{1}(u)\qquad\text{for every }u\in\mathcal{D}(I)=\mathcal{D}(I_{0}),

where
\partial I_{0}
denotes the usual subdifferential of the convex functional

Finally, to work with the set
\partial^{-}I

\left|\!\left|\!\left|\partial^{-}I(u)\right|\!\right|\!\right|

\left|\!\left|\!\left|\partial^{-}I(u\right|\!\right|\!\right|=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\min\{\|\alpha\|:\alpha\in\partial^{-}I(u)\},&\text{if }\partial^{-}I(u)\neq\varnothing,\\[4.30554pt]
\infty,&\text{if }\partial^{-}I(u)=\varnothing.\end{cases}

We prove the Strong maximum principle for the Grushin operator. But before that we require the following logarithmic lemma.

Lemma 2.2

(Logarithmic Lemma)
.

Let
u\in H^{1}_{0,\gamma}(\Omega)
be a nonnegative supersolution of
-\Delta_{\gamma}u+hu
in the sense of distributions where
h\in L_{loc}^{\frac{2Q}{Q+2}}(\Omega)
and
d>0
be a positive number. Then the following holds

\int_{B_{r}}|\nabla_{\gamma}\log(u+d)|^{2}dz\leq Cr^{N-2}+\int_{B_{3r/2}}\frac{hu}{u+d}dz.

If
u\in L_{loc}^{\infty}(\Omega)
then it is enough to take
h\in L_{loc}^{1}(\Omega)
.

Proof.

Let
\phi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\Omega)
be such that

\phi\equiv 1\quad\text{in }B_{r},\qquad|\nabla_{\gamma}\phi|\leq\frac{C}{r},\qquad\operatorname{supp}(\phi)\subset B_{3r/2},

where
r>0
is chosen such that
B_{3r/2}\subset\Omega
.
Since
u\geq 0
, there exists a nonnegative sequence
\{u_{k}\}_{k\geq 1}
such that

u_{k}\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\Omega),\qquad\operatorname{supp}(u_{k})\text{ is compact in }\Omega,\qquad\|u_{k}-u\|\to 0\quad\text{as }k\to\infty.

Now, choosing the test function
\eta_{k}=(u_{k}+d)^{-1}\phi^{2}
, we obtain

\displaystyle 0\leq

\displaystyle-\int_{B_{3r/2}}\nabla_{\gamma}u\cdot\nabla_{\gamma}u_{k}\,(u_{k}+d)^{-2}\phi^{2}+\int_{B_{3r/2}}\nabla_{\gamma}u\cdot\nabla_{\gamma}\phi\,\bigl(2\phi(u_{k}+d)^{-1}\bigr)

(2.11)

\displaystyle+\int_{B_{3r/2}}hu(u_{k}+d)^{-1}\phi^{2}.

For any measurable set
E\subset\Omega
, we estimate

\displaystyle\left|\int_{E}\nabla_{\gamma}u\cdot\nabla_{\gamma}u_{k}\,\phi^{2}(u_{k}+d)^{-2}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{d^{2}}\int_{E}|\nabla_{\gamma}u|\,|\nabla_{\gamma}u_{k}|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{d^{2}}\left(\int_{E}|\nabla_{\gamma}u|^{2}\right)^{1/2}\left(\int_{E}|\nabla_{\gamma}u_{k}|^{2}\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{d^{2}}M\left(\int_{E}|\nabla_{\gamma}u|^{2}\right)^{1/2}.

Therefore, by the Vitali’s Convergence Theorem, we conclude that

\int_{\Omega}\nabla_{\gamma}u\cdot\nabla_{\gamma}u_{k}\,\phi^{2}(u_{k}+d)^{-2}\,dz\to\int_{\Omega}|\nabla_{\gamma}u|^{2}\phi^{2}(u+d)^{-2}\,dz,\quad\text{as }k\to\infty.

(2.12)

Using (
2.12
) and the Dominated Convergence Theorem in (
2.11
) together with Young’s inequality, we obtain

\displaystyle 0\leq{}

\displaystyle-\int_{B_{3r/2}}\left|\nabla_{\gamma}\log(u+d)\right|^{2}\phi^{2}+2\int_{B_{3r/2}}\nabla_{\gamma}u\cdot\nabla_{\gamma}\phi\,(\phi(u+d)^{-1})+\int_{B_{3r/2}}hu(u+d)^{-1}\phi^{2}

\displaystyle\leq{}

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\int_{B_{3r/2}}\left|\nabla_{\gamma}\log(u+d)\right|^{2}\phi^{2}+C\int_{B_{3r/2}}|\nabla_{\gamma}\phi|^{2}+\int_{B_{3r/2}}hu(u+d)^{-1}.

This completes the proof.
∎

Lemma 2.3

(Strong Maximum Principle)
.

Let
u
be a non-negative supersolution of
-\Delta_{\gamma}u+hu
where

in
\Omega

in
\Omega
.

Proof.

If

, there is nothing to prove. Let

. Using the Poincaré inequality in

and Lemma

\displaystyle\int_{B_{r}\left(z_{0}\right)}\left|\log\left(1+\frac{u}{d}\right)\right|^{2}dz

\displaystyle=\int_{B_{r}\left(z_{0}\right)}\left|\log\left(1+\frac{u}{d}\right)-m_{Z}\right|^{2}dz

\displaystyle\leq C\int_{B_{r}\left(z_{0}\right)}\left|\nabla_{\gamma}\log\left(1+\frac{u}{d}\right)\right|^{2}dz

\displaystyle\leq Cr^{N-2}+\int_{B_{3r/2}(z_{0})}hu(u+d)^{-1}dz

where

3

In this section, we undertake a rigorous analysis of the following boundary value problem

Proof.

The proof is the adaptation of the argument of

. Let

)
λ

Lemma 5.12
