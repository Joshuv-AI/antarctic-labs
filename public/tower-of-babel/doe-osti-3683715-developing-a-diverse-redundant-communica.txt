ABSTRACT
This report presents a risk-informed framework for cybersecurity by design (CSbD) in advanced
nuclear reactors. Historically, nuclear facilities have relied on wrap-around cybersecurity, airgaps, and
physical security to isolate and protect systems. Advanced reactors are designed to have digital
control systems, remote monitoring, with goals of wireless technologies and remote operations.
These new approaches introduce new cyber-physical attack vectors that need cybersecurity
mechanisms designed to be fail-safe for critical infrastructure applications. This report demonstrates
the design process and discusses the implementation of a diverse, redundant communications
module designed to provide data integrity and confidentiality protections across zone boundaries.
The methodology is built upon an updated conceptual defensive model derived from the
International Telecommunication Union Recommendation X.800 security architecture. By mapping
design features to security services and mechanisms at specific logical boundary layers, characterizing
the attack surface and defense-in-depth of cybersecurity features is simplified. This process translates
high-level regulatory expectations into traceable and testable system-level requirements to aid in
licensing.
The practical application of this framework is demonstrated through the design of a unidirectional,
hardware-isolated communication gateway. To achieve low latency, deterministic behavior, and a
reduced attack surface, the system is implemented on a Field Programmable Gate Array (FPGA).
The module utilizes the NIST recommended AES-256 in the Galois Counter Mode to achieve data
confidentiality and integrity. This research establishes a pathway for vendors to justify advanced
cybersecurity features at any level of the plant, demonstrating modern cybersecurity can enhance
rather than degrade safety.

 EXECUTIVE SUMMARY
Advanced reactors (ARs) are designed around digital instrumentation and control, with new features
such as remote monitoring, wireless technologies, and goals of remote operations. These capabilities
offer significant safety, performance and economic benefits, but vastly increase the attack surface of
the facilities. Legacy fleet facilities rely on wrap-around cybersecurity with airgaps, strong physical
protection systems, and cybersecurity features bolted-on to a completed design. For an advanced
reactor to be able to utilize modern technologic advancements, cybersecurity will need to be
addressed within the design phase, rather than after.
This report demonstrates a cybersecurity-by-design (CSbD) process from the process planning
phase to implementation and realization of a working device. The report covers the system lifecycle,
starting with process planning and requirements definition and ending with the implementation. The
demonstration is a diverse and redundant communication module designed to act as a unidirectional
gateway that utilizes cryptography to provide integrity and confidentiality guarantees to the data.
This enables utilization of untrusted network paths to reliably transmit data.
The methodology is derived from the Tiered Cybersecurity Analysis described in the NRC
Regulatory Guide 5.96. This work sits within tier 2 of the TCA where the designer is establishing the
defensive cybersecurity architecture (DCSA) and passive security measures. An updated conceptual
defensive model (CDM) derived from the security architecture of ITU-T Recommendation X.800 is
also utilized for characterizing the attack surface and defense in depth of the design. The CDM relies
on four defensive strategies: fortification, access control, chokepoint and deception. These strategies
are implemented at any of three logical boundary layers (LBLs) in a system’s communication stack.
For each security service that a system claims to provide, the model traces the service to the
mechanisms and layers that realize it and associates the absence or deficiency of the mechanism with
a consequence: deceive, distort, deny, or disclose.
This methodology results in a repeatable requirements-specification process that produces simple
and testable requirements. In the process planning phase, authoritative inputs, methodological
guidance, and reference documents are gathered to establish a baseline for what regulation and
standards require of a system. Then the minimum set of least disruptive X.800 security services are
selected that still achieve appropriate defense-in-depth. Each service is mapped to its mechanisms
and placed at applicable layers. From this, requirements are written in a fixed and testable form that
names the service, mechanism, and layer, with a rationale explaining the defensive strategy and
consequence it is preventing. This provides a tester with an objective of the consequence to achieve
in order for an implementation to fail a requirement.
The process also incorporates lessons learned from the Sandia National laboratories’ CARBON
wireless communication system, an ultra-high-reliability diverse wireless design. The design was
tested in 2025 and validated the CDM predictions. The CARBON system had strong protections at
LBL I, but the access control protections at LBL II required iteration and improvements. The design
of CARBON wireless provided insights into the DRC module’s design.
With collaboration from Advanced Reactor partners, a set of requirements was developed to ensure
the final design was useful to the industry. These outlined the use of the device and ensuring
modularity, allowing vendors to utilize pre-developed components or protocols that they choose
without a large overhaul.
The realized design implements these requirements on a field-programmable gate array (FPGA),
selected for deterministic operation, low latency, architectural compatibility with FPGA-based safety

systems and a reduced attack surface. This design contains no general-purpose or soft-core
processor, vastly reducing the execution pathways that need to be verified for use. The system uses a
transmitter FPGA and a receiver FPGA for performing the cryptographic operations and handle the
communication. This iteration relies on a unidirectional communication pathway, physically tying off
the pins that are not in use, ensuring communication only flows one direction. The data is protected
by AES-256 in Galois Counter Mode, a NIST-recommended authenticated-encryption suite
providing confidentiality and integrity in a single mechanism. The cryptographic core is tested
against the NIST Cryptographic Algorithm Validation Program test vectors, as well as a few other
community created test vectors. The hardware platform is a Digilent Nexys Video development
board with an Opsero Ethernet FMC expansion board to provide additional Ethernet ports. The
design is able to be utilized on any FPGA and utilizes modular inter-module communication, called
AXI-Stream, to allow anyone to replace a module with one that they develop.
This work demonstrates that the TCA and the X.800 CDM can be used to translate regulatory
requirements into traceable and testable system-level requirements. This enhances a vendor’s ability
to justify cybersecurity features at any level of the plant during licensing. The work further
demonstrates that modern cryptographic protections can be integrated into safety-adjacent
communications without violating real-time constraints. The inertia of ARs increases the time that
systems and operators have to respond to a transient in the plant. The project also provides a
completely open-sourced design with a modular AES-256-GCM core that can be used for
prototyping, with the requirements developed in a clear and traceable matrix.
This iteration of the design is not meant for use in a plant, but instead to demonstrate the process
and provide a starting point for vendors to use for prototyping and testing. Multiple features that are
important to the security have been described but not fully implemented, and the code is not
qualified for use in any real system. A follow on report later this year will discuss the testing of the
system based on the requirements set provided.

1. INTRODUCTION
Advanced Reactors (ARs) are increasingly reliant on digital technologies to enhance safety,
operational performance, and flexibility. Digital technologies have significant benefits over analog
systems especially in networking technologies, which enables the designers and operators to
generate, transmit, and archive vast amounts of data to improve system performance and design.
These benefits are accompanied by increased risks associated with cybersecurity. These cybersecurity
risks are optimally addressed in the earliest phases of the design process.
This report presents a framework for cybersecurity by design (CSbD) for design of a
communications module to secure communications between the reactor protective system (RPS)
and plant computer system (PCS). This framework leverages the Tiered Cyber Analysis (TCA)
detailed within Nuclear Regulatory Commission (NRC) Regulatory Guide 5.96 (RG 5.96) [1] for
design analysis and specification of requirements. TCA provides a structured process for considering
cybersecurity from the earliest stages of reactor design and ensures that cybersecurity is
foundational—from conceptual design through operation and decommissioning. The TCA not only
meets regulatory expectations but also supports innovation and the safe adoption of advanced digital
technologies in nuclear power.
The CSbD framework provides a process for risk-informing requirements to apply a graded
approach and implement Defense-in-Depth (DiD). DiD is achieved through the establishment of
multiple independent and diverse layers. This ensures that a common vulnerability or a single
adversarial action does not degrade or bypass multiple layers. A Defensive Cybersecurity
Architecture (DCSA) is the keystone feature of a reactor’s or system’s design for implementing DiD
for cybersecurity. The TCA process addresses DCSA specification within Tier 2 of its analysis
through specifying requirements for passive and prohibitive conditions to prevent access of a
potential adversary to one or more of four attack pathways.
The International Telecommunications Union (ITU) provides recommendations on the Open
Systems Interconnection (OSI) Model whereby networked entities communicate with one another.
ITU X.800 [2] defines a general cybersecurity architecture that can be applied to the specification of
a DCSA. The
X.800 security architecture provides a comprehensive listing of security services and their associated
security mechanisms that should be considered within the design.

2. BACKGROUND
This report builds upon several previous studies which include:
 1. “Field Programmable Gate Array Based Reactor Protection Systems and Potential for
 Inclusion of Secure Elements to Improve Cybersecurity” (SAND2024-12082) will be
 referred to as FPGAs and Secure Elements Report [3],
 2. “An Integrated Approach to Implementing Secure Elements Within Safety Systems”
 (SAND2025-14668O) – Secure Element Design Evaluation Report [4].
 3. CARBON Testing Report (Available upon request)
The FPGAs and Secure Elements report evaluated how secure elements (SEs) might be
incorporated into Field-Programmable Gate Array (FPGA) based safety systems. SEs, or hardware
security modules (HSMs), provide protected tamper-resistant environments for storing sensitive
information and performing cryptographic operations. SEs support authentication, maintain data
integrity, and enforce controlled access, making them useful in applications that require dependable
security for critical functions.
The FPGAs and Secure Elements Report examined the licensing and regulatory implications of
adding cryptographic capabilities to protection system hardware, including potential uses in remote
monitoring and remote control. Its primary conclusions were identification of a need for a risk-
informed performance-based framework that would help reduce obstacles to adopting cryptography
and other cybersecurity enhancements while still aligning with regulatory expectations [1], [2].
The Secure Element Design Evaluation Report examined the regulatory and methodological basis
needed to evaluate cybersecurity of four design candidates of a digital instrumentation and control
(I&C) system. The report summarized the regulatory frameworks, design criteria, system lifecycles,
and cybersecurity fundamentals. Four potential design candidates for a reactor Cavity Cooling
System for a high temperature gas cooled reactor are used to compare differing cybersecurity
approaches: (1) a virtual private network (VPN) appliance, (2) a traditional secure element, (3) a
novel secure element gateway, and (4) an FPGA based solution.
These candidates were evaluated for defense-in-depth, secure fail-safe behavior, equipment
qualification, and overall risk reduction. The study found that VPN-based approaches benefit from
regulatory familiarity but introduce vulnerabilities due to reliance on conventional IT systems;
traditional secure elements improve key management but lack chokepoint and isolation capabilities;
novel secure elements provide hardened security but require significant development effort; and
FPGA based solutions offer a balanced approach but require additional consideration for
maintenance and key management.
The Secure Element Design Evaluation Report emphasizes the need for I&C systems to realize their
robustness and reliability targets (or properties). The key outcomes of this report were:
 • Demonstrated the benefit of the Conceptual Defensive Model (CDM) [5] that maps
 Defensive Strategies (DS), Fortification, Access Control, Chokepoint, and Deception, to
 three Logical Boundary Layers (LBL) within a system’s communication stack to simplify
 DCSA specification and evaluation.
 • Established that cybersecurity ‘resilience’ is wholly included within the reliability and
 robustness properties. These properties were linked to cybersecurity attributes and elements
 resulting in a single process to meet both safety and cybersecurity demands. These

 properties, categories, attributes and elements form the foundation for the design of digital
 I&C systems.
 • Cryptographic mechanisms (e.g., encryption, secure hashes) are mature, pervasive and well-
 established in other industries (e.g., internet banking/finance), and deterministic in their
 behavior. They provide significant and robust protection for a system’s communication and
 can significantly reduce cybersecurity risks associated.
 • The need to update standards, such as IEEE 7-4.3.2, to remove barriers and bias against the
 use of cryptographic mechanisms within safety systems, particularly when not supported by
 a risk-informed analysis.
Cybersecurity by design must be part of a single, coordinated process for safety and security to
support a defensible and repeatable evaluation process. RIPB and TCA methods can help vendors
systematically evaluate and justify cryptographic safety system designs while navigating safety and
licensing expectations [2].

The design of the diverse redundant communications module builds upon insights gained from the
design of Sandia National Laboratories’ CARBON wireless system [6]. CARBON was designed to
be an ultra-high reliability wireless system that was resilient to adversarial jamming. It is composed
of a set of diverse wireless technologies such as Radio Frequency Wi-Fi and point-to-point Infrared.
It duplicated critical communications at the entry point and de-duplicated them at the exit point.
Some of the cybersecurity and nuclear safety and licensing challenges were the increased complexity
of the design, lack of turn-key configuration, as well as network-based cybersecurity vulnerabilities.
Analysis and evaluation of the CARBON system leveraged the Conceptual Defensive Model to
perform the following activities:
 • Design Analysis: The design of CARBON was analyzed to identify vulnerabilities and assess
 the effectiveness of existing security measures. The analysis revealed that Access Control and
 Chokepoint Defensive Strategies at Logical Boundary Layer II were insufficient to mitigate

 potential threats effectively. These deficiencies highlighted critical areas requiring immediate
 attention and improvement to enhance overall security posture
 • Test Strategy: A targeted testing approach was developed to focus on the predicted
 weaknesses identified during the design analysis. The tests were designed to simulate real-
 world scenarios and stress-test the identified vulnerabilities in the Access Control and
 Chokepoint Defensive Strategies. Results from the initial tests confirmed the predicted
 weaknesses, validating the accuracy of the conceptual model and emphasizing the need for
 corrective measures.

3.1.2. Analytical Model
The framework is built upon the CDM, which organizes cybersecurity protection by defensive
strategy and by logical boundary layer. Rather than selecting controls from a fixed catalog [13], the
framework begins with the security services a system must provide, drawn from the security
architecture of ITU-T Recommendation X.800 [2], and traces each service to the mechanisms,
layers, and defensive strategies needed to realize it. For each service, the absence or deficiency of a
supporting mechanism is associated with one of four consequences, namely deceive, distort, deny, or
disclose. This ensures every requirement is tied to a protection objective and to the consequence it is
intended to prevent, which maintains a risk-informed basis while achieving a defense-in-depth
posture.
The updated X.800 CDM replaced the use of requirements in the previous model. The previous
model included requirements which may be incorrect, incomplete, and incoherent with X.800
security services and security mechanisms. Figure 3 below, provides a representative visualization of
application of the updated X.800 CDM to the architecture of a secure system.

Figure 3: Illustrative example of the conceptual defensive model showing defense-in-depth across
 logical boundary layers with defensive strategies and X.800 services as requirements.

The updated X.800 CDM has the following impacts compared with the previous model:
 • Limits application of the CDM to Tier 2 only
 • Provides a comprehensive and complete scope for definition of logical boundaries
 • Provides no security of the hosts at the entry points (i.e., endpoints) of the logical
 boundaries. Tier 3 protections of these entry points will be needed.
The Updated X.800 CDM application within the design of the module is provided in Section 5.2.3.1
and further detailed in Appendix B.

3.2. Requirements Specification Phase
The following section details the requirements specification process. This process is utilized in
Section 5 to establish the requirements for the design. Figure 4 shows a flowchart for how a TCA
Tier 2 analysis feeds into requirements derivation for design. The figure starts from the cyber-
enabled accident scenarios that create adversary functional scenarios. Then, from these, a
cybersecurity plan is created and DCSA elements are specified. From this, the requirements process
begins. The report assumes that the diverse and redundant communications module has been
identified as a DCSA element and now requirements and implementation are needed.

 Figure 4: Expanded Tier 2 DCSA analysis with requirements development workflow [1]

This process aims to produce a complete and traceable set of testable security requirements for a
design from a repeatable sequence of steps. The steps proceed from gathering authoritative inputs,
through selecting services and deriving mechanism-level requirements, to confirming that every
required service is covered and appropriate defense-in-depth was achieved. The workflow is iterative
since the analysis of one service can reveal an additional layer or mechanism that returns the analysis
to an earlier step.

3.2.1. Establish Design Inputs and Constraints
The governing regulatory and standards documents are gathered along with the customer design
constraints that bound the requirements. These inputs dictate the design and the workflow for

which the requirements are developed. The location of the design element within the architecture
and the how it communicates are then identified. This step captures the placement of the element
relative to zone boundaries, the required direction of information flow, and the trust level of the
segments traversed. The location and the required direction of information flow fix the logical
boundary layers that the requirements must address. Section 3.1 identifies the four categories of
inputs: regulatory and standards input, analytical model, lessons learned and stakeholder input.
These inputs determine which security services are required, how constrained the associated
requirements must be, and where across the system and architectural boundary protections must be
placed.

3.2.2. Select Services, Mechanisms, and Layers
With the inputs established, the security services the design must provide are determined from the
supported functions and the consequences to be prevented. The candidate services are reduced to
the minimum set that still provides defense in depth, which keeps unnecessary services from adding
complexity and attack surface without improving protection.

3.2.3. Derive Requirements and Evaluate Defense-In-Depth
In the conceptual defensive model, each pairing of a defensive strategy with a logical boundary layer
represents a distinct placement for a protective measure. The framework examines, for each required
service, the layers at which its mechanisms can be positioned, then confirms that protection is not
concentrated at a single layer. Where a service can be realized at more than one layer, protections are
placed at multiple layers to avoid reliance on any one layer.
Each required security service is mapped to the security mechanisms that realize it and a requirement
is then specified for each mechanism for its appropriate logical boundary layer. Each requirement is
further refined by its LBL and linked to the DS its mechanism serves. This yields a singular, testable
requirement that allows protection across the design. This sequence repeats until every required
service has a specified requirement at each applicable LBL, assessed against the minimum service
set. Any mechanism and LBL combination left without a requirement is recorded as a gap in the
defensive posture. Recording these gaps makes the completeness of the defensive posture and
defense in depth explicit and gives the design team a defined set of items to resolve.
During the requirements specification, each requirement is linked to the defensive strategy that its
mechanism serves. The linkage is made by the protective role the mechanism plays within the
requirement rather than by the service it supports, which means a mechanism can be linked to
different strategies in different requirements as the consequence being prevented changes.
Each requirement, based upon the updated CDM, is stated in a fixed form that names the defensive
strategy, the service, the layer, the mechanism, and the consequence to be prevented. The form is:
The system shall provide [X.800 service]. The service shall implement [X.800
mechanisms]. The service [shall/should/may] be implemented [logical boundary
layer].
A rationale or justification will be provided for each requirement and shall reference the associated
defensive strategies.
As an example, a confidentiality requirement reads:
 The system shall provide connectionless confidentiality service.
 The connectionless confidentiality service shall implement

 encipherment and routing control security mechanisms. The
 service may be implemented at logical boundary layer II or III.
 Rationale: The fortification provided by both encipherment and
 routing control against disclosure is needed to prevent future
 attacks aimed at modifying or spoofing the receiving
 system/entity. Routing Control mechanism may provide chokepoints
 if implemented with strict limits on authorized networks, relays
 or links.
Stating the requirement in this way ties it to a single verifiable claim about one service with two
mechanisms at one or more layers.
The attributes that define the requirement also support a threat model-based perspective. Where the
requirement above defines the protection to confirm, the adversary-oriented statement defines the
corresponding objective a tester pursues to challenge it. It expresses that objective as achieving the
consequence by exploiting the absent or deficient mechanism, which for the example above reads:
 achieve a disclose consequence of the connectionless
 confidentiality service at logical boundary layer II by
 exploiting absent or deficient encipherment
Carrying both phrasings keeps each requirement unambiguous and traceable to the test that
confirms it. This includes the requirement describing the defensive claim and the threat model-
based statement describing the test that attempts to defeat it.
After the requirements are established, an evaluation of the defense-in-depth adequacy per NEI 18-
04 is needed. If inadequate defense-in-depth is found, identify a new DCSA item that is needed.

Once a requirement has been added to the traceability table, the verification approach for that
requirement is determined, as shown in Figure 5. Each requirement is isolated from Appendix A and
evaluated for how it can be verified. A requirement that is both testable and inspectable receives a
combined testing and inspection plan. A requirement that is testable but not inspectable receives a
testing plan, and a requirement that is inspectable but not testable receives an inspection plan. A
requirement that is neither testable nor inspectable is rejected and rewritten to ensure it is valid.

4. DIVERSE REDUNDANT COMMUNICATIONS CONTEXT AND SCOPE
The DRC module provides a secure and dependable means for transmitting sensitive information
from the RPS to the PCS. Its purpose is to establish a communication path that maintains the
deterministic characteristics of safety related digital systems while supporting the monitoring and
operational needs of non-safety systems. The DRC module supplements the security services
implemented within the RPS and PCS. A complete DiD analysis will need to evaluate all security
services implemented across the communications stack (e.g., OSI Model).

4.1. Stakeholder Needs
The following is a list of needs from AR Vendors to provide adequate interoperability, security, and
efficiencies:
 N-1) The design needs to be based upon packet-based protocols that meet the requirements of
 both systems.
 N-2) The device needs to be unidirectional communication device to be interoperable with the
 RPS and PCS. This unidirectional communication aims to function similar to a data diode,
 which is employed to isolate safety related systems [12].
 N-3) All information must pass through a protected entry point within the RPS and exit through
 an unprotected point within the PCS, preserving the one-way behavior of the communication
 path.
 N-4) The DRC module needs to provide functionalities that enable integration within an
 advanced reactor digital architecture. As it is situated as a physical and logical interface between
 two classes of digital equipment, it must also support the functional behaviors needed for safety
 related data transmission while fitting within the physical and operational environment of the
 RPS hardware.
 N-5) The functions of the DRC module need to enforce secure and deterministic transmission
 of safety related information without enabling any return flow or permitting external influence.
 N-6) The DRC needs to be transparent to the RPS and PCS.
 N-7) The DRC needs to preserve integrity of the RPS data. The DRC module will transmit
 information such as sensor values, status indicators, protective system states, alarms, and other
 safety related outputs. These data elements must be conveyed to the PCS in their original and
 unmodified form, and the communication mechanism must prevent any suppression,
 fabrication, or alteration during transit. This supports plant monitoring functions and enables
 situational awareness without exposing the RPS to untrusted network segments.
 N-8) The loss of one communication channel must not reduce safety functionality.
An FPGA based implementation is preferred for the design of the DRC module. RPSs employ
FPGA devices to achieve deterministic operation, hardened behavior, and low latency messaging (
[3]). Using an FPGA for the communication module maintains architectural compatibility and
avoids reliance on general purpose computing environments that introduce unnecessary complexity
and cybersecurity exposure. The FPGA platform enables structured logic-based enforcement of
packet structures, timing, cryptographic protections, and routing behavior and can be implemented
to support a X.800-based conceptual defense model.

4.2. Architectural Location and Communication Constraints
The DRC module should be designed to be situated within the architecture where safety related data
leaves a protected digital environment and enters a network segment that is not credited for safety
functions. The protected entry point ensures that all supported functions originate from trusted
systems whose integrity is established through the reactor protective system lifecycle. The exit point
does not possess the same trust level and must therefore receive data in a manner that guarantees
authenticity and integrity without allowing influence on the protected side.
As the module mediates communication across a zone boundary, it must operate under constraints
related to directionality, endpoint trust levels, and termination of logical connections. The direction
of information flow is fixed and must not be overridden. Logical connections terminate at clearly
defined boundaries. These constraints define where protective mechanisms must operate and where
LBLs must be addressed within the conceptual defensive model.

4.3. Communications Functions of Interest
Multiple types of communication can utilize the unidirectional channel. The unidirectional
communication channel supports several essential plant functions. Its purpose is to ensure that
safety related information originating within the reactor protective system is conveyed to the plant
computer system with accuracy, consistency, and protection against misuse. The channel transmits
sensor information, physical protection system information, plant status values, alarms, and
communication essential during abnormal or emergency conditions.

4.3.1. Sensor Data Acquisition
Sensor information provides direct observation of physical quantities within the RPS. This involves
the reliable aggregation of data from sensors within the RPS. Secure communications are essential
for this gathering, as the integrity of the data must be sufficient. The data must not be altered in
transit between devices, ensuring it represents the true, present state of the plant. As a result, the
DRC module must gather these values reliably, preserve their structure, and ensure that each

transmitted value represents the true present state of the equipment. The DRC module must protect
the information from alteration so that the PCS receives authentic data for monitoring and analysis.

4.3.2. Physical Protection System Data
Certain plant configurations may require that physical protection information be transmitted to
remote or secondary locations. These locations may coordinate access control, video monitoring,
alarm evaluation, or protective force response. The information must be transferred in a form that
reflects the true state of physical protection equipment, permitting offsite or backup facilities to act
with confidence.
If multiple plants in one region are reliant on a shared security force, the primary alarm station may
not be geographically collocated with the plant. Even with an onsite protective force, a secondary
site may need Physical Protection System (PPS) data to act as a backup response force. Ensuring
that the PPS data such as access control logs, camera feeds, etc. are true and accurate is vital for
proper function of a remote site.

4.3.3. Status Monitoring
Plant status information provides operators with insight into equipment health and operational
readiness. The status of the plant must reflect the legitimate, present status of the plant at-large. As
the operators require the data for ensuring equipment health, faulty data from a compromised node
could mislead operators. From a cybersecurity perspective, the faulty data could be caused by an
adversary. This may result in an operator to take an unsafe action or to not take a required action.
As such, the communication module must ensure that these values accurately reflect the present
condition of plant systems. Incorrect or compromised status information can influence operator
judgment. Therefore, the DRC module must preserve authenticity and integrity at all times.

4.3.4. Alarm Transmission
Alarms must be transmitted with high priority to the operators and must be delivered in their
original form on-time and on-schedule. Any alarms sent should not be able to be suppressed or
blocked by other network traffic and should not be able to be modified in-transit by any node it
passes through. Additionally, the system must be resistant to false injections that mislead the
operators into taking incorrect action, or no action at all, or overwhelm the operators with too many
alarms, leading to operational uncertainty and decision paralysis.

4.3.5. Emergency Communications
During emergency conditions, primary communication systems may be impaired. The DRC module
must ensure that essential plant information continues to reach the plant computer system so that
recovery operations can proceed safely. The diverse and redundant nature of the DRC design
supports this function by maintaining paths that do not rely on broader facility infrastructure.

4.4. Assumptions and Constraints
The design of the DRC module is guided by assumptions and constraints associated with safety
classification, real-time behavior, and timing performance. These constraints define how the module
receives information, processes it, secures it, and transmits it across the unidirectional channel.

4.4.1. Safety Classification of Interfacing Systems
Safety classification of interfacing systems influences the permissible treatments of data and the
allowable complexity of the communication logic. The safety related origin requires deterministic,
hardened, and low complexity behavior. The non-safety related destination supports more flexible
processing, but it must not influence or override any aspect of the safety related transmission path.

4.4.2. Real-Time Requirements
Real-time constraints arise from the quantity and cadence of transmitted information. The initial
performance requirement is the transmission of five hundred eight-bit sensor values at intervals of
one half second. The communication module must collect each set of values, apply the required
protective mechanisms, and forward the information to the plant computer system within this
interval. These timing expectations influence the structure of packet handling logic, buffering
approach, and cryptographic implementation so that no jitter or reordering is introduced.

4.4.3. Latency and Timing Constraints
Latency considerations are informed by the physics of advanced reactors. Advanced reactor systems
exhibit slower rates of change in plant conditions due to intrinsic design characteristics. This
moderates the risk associated with moderate communication latency, since plant values evolve
gradually and remain valid within the timing windows required for monitoring. This inherent
physical behavior allows secure communication services to incorporate bounded latencies from
cryptographic protections without compromising functional requirements. Research has
demonstrated that authenticated encryption latency is acceptable for reactor data streams that evolve
on slow physical timescales. These studies support the conclusion that cryptographic protections can
be integrated without violating real time performance constraints [16].
Timing requirements also consider the environment in which the module operates. The module
must preserve deterministic behavior within the protective system context while ensuring timely
delivery to the plant computer system. Cryptographic mechanisms, routing control, and packet
handling must be selected and configured to ensure compliance with real-time expectations and
compatibility with FPGA based deterministic architectures.

5. REQUIREMENTS SPECIFICATION
This section provides the steps employed to derive the diverse redundant communication (DRC)
module requirements. These steps include the mapping of security services to mechanisms and the
formulation of each requirement so that it can be verified via testing. A consolidated workflow
summarizes these steps in sequence. The section closes with a discussion on the intended benefits
and utility of applying the conceptual defensive model for deriving the minimum set of requirements
to establish defense-in-depth for the DRC module.

5.1. Process
In this section the requirements specification workflow from Section 3.2 is applied to the DRC
module described in Section 4. The application is the FPGA communication security gateway and
the workflow produces the requirements stated in the subsections that follow.
The process begins in process planning with the governing regulations and guidance documents
from Section 3.1 and the customer needs from Section 4. Each candidate requirement is assigned to
a requirement class of functional, performance, external and interfacing systems, human factors, or
other, and the class determines whether the requirement is associated with the DCSA. A
requirement associated with that architecture is developed through the CDM. The required ITU
X.800 security service is selected from the minimum set needed for DiD, the security mechanisms
that realize the service are identified, and the requirement is placed at its DS and LBL. The
requirement is then added to the requirements traceability table in Appendix A. A requirement
whose class is not associated with the DCSA is evaluated for whether it is needed at this stage of the
design. If it is needed, it returns to the class specification for development. If it is not, it is
designated as a future design requirement and recorded as such in the traceability matrix.
Each requirement carries the DS, LBL, and consequence it addresses, which links it to the CDM.
The complete set of traces, from governing source through security service and mechanism to the
design feature and its verification, is consolidated in the requirements traceability matrix in Appendix
A.

5.2. Functional Requirements
The functional requirements define what the communication system must do to send RPS data to
the PCS across the secure device. The device operates in line between the input module at the RPS
and the output module at the receiving PCS. It forwards RPS data in one direction without altering
it. These requirements establish the communication functions, the security services applied to the
data, and the independence the device preserves between the RPS and the PCS. The robustness of
the communication function, defined in the DRG as its ability to perform correctly under the
conditions it will face [11], is evaluated against the defensive measures shown in Figure 6.

 Figure 6: Reliability and Robustness, Defense-in-Depth measures as defined in [11].

5.2.1. Communication Functions
The primary function is forwarding of RPS data. The device receives and transmits packets in the
same order and with identical payload data (R7), without loss of payload (R7-A) and preserving
packet order (R7-B). The data carried is RPS data, which supports a safety function; its criticality is
high, and that criticality sets the security level applied to the device. Operational modes, failover, and
recovery are not defined in the current requirement set and are to be derived in a future iteration of
the design.

5.2.2. Boundary Definition and Access Control
The protected asset is the communication function that carries RPS data to the PCS. The device
mediates the boundary between the RPS and the PCS. It defines that boundary by enforcing a single,
unidirectional path from the RPS to the PCS (R6-B) and by assigning each Ethernet interface a
single functional role of ingress, logging, or egress (R20). This establishes the zone boundary at
which the remaining protections are applied.
Device identity is established by assigning each Ethernet port a unique Media Access Control
(MAC) address (R23) conforming to the applicable addressing standard (R23-A). Least privilege is
realized through functional minimization, since the device has no user accounts and each interface
performs a single function (R20), and the logic contains no general-purpose or soft-core processor
(R22).

5.2.3. Confidentiality and Integrity Protections
The device encrypts the RPS data it carries (R8), which realizes the connectionless confidentiality
service through encipherment under the Fortification defensive strategy (R8-A) and prevents a

disclose consequence. The confidentiality claim is supported by the requirements that assure the
cryptography, namely the use of qualified cryptographic mechanisms (R9), protected storage of key
material (R10), unique nonces and initialization vectors (IVs) for each operation (R11), and periodic
key rotation (R15). These supporting requirements limit the conditions under which the
encipherment could be defeated.
The device applies an authenticated encryption cipher suite to the payload (R8, R8-D), which
provides an integrity check alongside confidentiality and allows altered or corrupted data to be
detected at the PCS. This realizes the connectionless integrity service through a data integrity
mechanism under the Fortification defensive strategy (R8-C). Finally, the device preserves packet
order and payload as it forwards (R7), which maintains the integrity of the delivered data.

5.2.3.1.   X.800 CDM Application
The security services required by the DRC module are (i) Connectionless Confidentiality, and (ii)
Connectionless Integrity. These security services are both implemented at LBL II as the LBL III is
system dependent (i.e., implemented within the RPS and/or PCS application layer), and LBL I is not
implemented.
The visualization of the applied X.800 CDM to the DRC module is provided in Figure 7 below.

5.2.4. Safety and Independence
The safety and independence requirements establish that the device preserves the independence of
the RPS from the PCS, and does not introduce a path by which the PCS or a fault within the device
can affect the RPS safety function. The device enforces a single, unidirectional communication path
from the RPS to the PCS (R6-B), which separates the safety data source from the receiving system
and prevents the PCS from interacting with the RPS through the device. Within the device, each
Ethernet interface is assigned a single functional role (R20), which separates the communication
paths and keeps the data-transfer function distinct from the logging function.
The device exhibits deterministic behavior where the safety function requires predictable timing. Its
processing completes within a bounded worst-case execution time (R14), and its logic is
implemented without a general-purpose or soft-core processor (R22). Explicit fail-safe and fault-
tolerant response requirements, defining the device behavior on detection of an internal fault, are
not specified in the current requirement set.

5.2.5. Threat Coverage
The requirements above counter the specific attacks the device must resist. Malicious data injection
and spoofing are countered by source device identity (R23, R23-A). Replay is countered by the
unique nonce and initialization vector requirement (R11) together with authenticated encryption
(R8-D). Route manipulation is countered by the single, unidirectional path (R6-B) and the fixed
interface roles (R20). Denial of service directed at the RPS is countered by the unidirectional path
(R6-B), which removes the inbound route an adversary would need. Unauthorized reconfiguration is
countered by the protected bitstream (R17) and key storage (R10) and the absence of a soft-core
processor (R22).
Vulnerability management and recovery support requirements are not specified in the current
requirement set. These are to be derived and implemented in a future iteration of the design.

5.3. Performance Requirements
The performance requirements define how well the communication system must carry RPS data to
the PCS across the secure device. The device must deliver that data within a predictable timing
budget and continue to deliver it when a component fails. These requirements establish the timing,
throughput, and latency the device must meet, the redundancy and diversity that keep the
communication function available, and the logging that records the traffic the device has handled.
The reliability of the communication function, defined in the DRG as its ability to continue
performing when a component fails [11], is evaluated against the defensive measures in Figure 6.

5.3.1. Timing, Throughput, and Latency
The performance requirements establish that the device transmits RPS data to the PCS within a
predictable execution time and reliably enough to support the safety function. The device sustains a
minimum throughput sufficient to carry the RPS data without loss (R13), adds no more than a

bounded latency to the communication path (R16), and completes its processing within a bounded
worst-case execution time (R14). These establish the timing budget the device must meet while
performing its cryptographic and forwarding functions. Throughput, latency, and worst-case
execution time are defined in the requirement set and its supporting design documentation rather
than restated here.

5.3.2. Reliability, Redundancy, and Diversity
The reliability requirements establish that the communication function continues to convey RPS
data to the PCS when a component fails and that no single fault or common cause can deny the
function. The device provides diverse, redundant communication channels (R6-A) carried by
redundant, independent FPGA instances in a dual-FPGA architecture (R21), supported by a
minimum of two Ethernet interfaces (R12). This removes any single instance as a point of failure
and satisfies the single-failure criterion, and because the instances are independent, a failure in one
does not propagate to the others.
The channels and instances are also diverse (R6-C). Diversity mitigates common-cause failure by
ensuring that a single fault or exploited weakness does not defeat all instances at once. The diversity
specified in the current requirement set is at the level of the redundant channels and their
cryptographic implementation. Diversity in communication media, protocols, and routing and
control logic beyond this is not specified in the current requirement set.

5.3.3. Monitoring and Logging
The device logs the messages it processes, and the logging function is kept separate from the
cryptographic operations (R18). This provides a record of the traffic the device has handled and
supports accountability for the communication, consumed by the security operations center (SOC)
for post-event analysis. Active detection and response measures are not part of the passive
requirement set and will be addressed in a future set of requirements.

5.4. External and Interfacing System Requirements
The external and interfacing system requirements define how the device connects to the RPS and
PCS and the protocol conventions its communication must follow.

5.4.1. Physical and Network Interfaces
The device presents a defined set of network interfaces. It provides a minimum of two Ethernet
interfaces (R12), each designated to a single functional role of data ingress, logging, or data egress
(R20). Each Ethernet port is assigned a unique MAC address (R23), and those addresses conform to
the applicable addressing standard (R23-A).

5.4.2. Packet-Based Communications
Communication is packet-based, which allows the design to use the OSI model and internet-based
transport protocols (R1). The communication stack is structured so that only adjacent layers
intercommunicate (R2), which preserves the layer isolation the conceptual defensive model relies on.

5.4.3. Unidirectional and Connectionless Protocols
Communication is deterministic and unidirectional, flowing only from the RPS to the PCS (R6-B).
This one-way function follows the guidance of Regulatory Guide 1.152 that computer-based safety
systems may transfer data to other systems through one-way communication pathways, and it
establishes that data cannot pass from the PCS to the RPS. The device implements the required
security services at the link and network layers only (R3), preserving the application-layer services of
the external RPS and PCS (R3-A).

5.5. Human Factors Requirements
Human factors requirements are not applicable to this device, which operates in line without
operator interaction during normal function. The one operator-facing requirement is the indication
of operational status. The device indicates operational status for each transmitter and receiver (R19)
and provides simple visual indicators of that status to the operator (R19-A). Detailed indicator
content is to be defined in a future iteration of the design.

5.6. Other Requirements – Expansion and Fabrication Constraints

5.6.1. Device and Configuration Integrity
The device configuration is protected against unauthorized modification and extraction by
encrypting the FPGA bitstream (R17) and holding the cryptographic keys in protected storage
within the device (R10). The absence of a general-purpose or soft-core processor (R22) reduces the
attack surface of the configuration by removing the software execution path. These are treated as
design-assurance constraints under the Fortification defensive strategy.

5.6.2. Environmental Qualification
Environmental qualification requirements are outside the scope of this requirement set.
Temperature, radiation, humidity, vibration, and electromagnetic interference and compatibility will
be addressed by the equipment qualification program for the installed device in a future design
iteration. These conditions govern the physical suitability of the device for its installed location and
do not change the security services the device provides. Inspection and testability are supported by
the verifiable form in which the requirements are written.

5.6.3. Quality Assurance and Lifecycle Requirements
Quality assurance and lifecycle controls are imposed on the organization that develops and
maintains the device rather than on the device itself. The device is a passive measure within the
defensive cybersecurity architecture, and such measures are managed under the Quality Assurance
Program meeting Appendix B of 10 CFR Part 50, as established in Section 3.1. This subsection
states how the requirement set fits within that program and identifies the requirements that provide
the technical basis for the associated controls. It specifies no additional requirements on the device.

5.6.3.1. Design Control and Configuration Management
The requirements developed in this section are the design inputs for the device, and each is stated in
a verifiable form that supports design verification. Two requirements constrain the design in ways
that simplify that verification. The logic is implemented without a general-purpose or soft-core
processor (R22), which removes the software execution path from the scope of design control.

Cryptographic functions use mechanisms qualified by an internationally recognized certifying
authority (R9), which constrains the design to mechanisms whose correctness has been
independently established.
The configuration of the device is its FPGA bitstream. The bitstream is encrypted to prevent
unauthorized modification or extraction (R17), and the cryptographic keys, including the key that
decrypts the bitstream, are held in dedicated secure storage within the device (R10). These
requirements provide technical enforcement of configuration control, since a change to device
function requires a new bitstream and an unauthorized bitstream cannot be loaded. Approved
changes proceed through the design control process and are reflected in a revised bitstream, which
keeps change management and configuration management aligned to a single controlled artifact.

5.6.3.2. Procurement and Supplier Qualification
Procurement controls and supplier qualification apply to the FPGA device, its development
toolchain, and the cryptographic implementations used in the design. The requirement that
cryptographic mechanisms be qualified by an internationally recognized certifying authority (R9)
constrains the selection of those implementations, since only mechanisms carrying such qualification
satisfy it. The protection of the bitstream and its decryption key (R17, R10) depends on features
provided by the selected FPGA vendor, which makes vendor capability a procurement consideration
rather than a separate design requirement.
The cryptographic implementation in the current design iteration was developed internally and has
not been submitted for qualification by a certifying authority. It does, however, pass the test vectors
provided by the the National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) Cryptographic
Algorithm Validation Program (CAVP) [17]. R9 is therefore satisfied in a future design iteration
rather than in the current one, when the implementation is accredited by the appropriate authorities.
Future cryptographic implementations will be obtained or qualified through the procurement and
supplier qualification controls of the quality assurance program, which establishes the qualification
basis that R9 requires.
Specific procurement and supplier qualification requirements are not defined in the current
requirement set. These controls are established by the quality assurance program and the supply
chain provisions of the governing cyber security program guidance. Requirements that place
obligations on the device or its acquisition are to be developed in a future design iteration.

5.6.3.3. Documentation, Records, and Secure Maintenance
The requirements traceability matrix in Appendix A is the design record for the requirement set. It
links each requirement to its governing source, the design decision it drove, and the verification that
confirms it, which provides the traceable documentation that design control requires. The device
also produces an operational record, since messages received and transmitted are logged separately
from the cryptographic operations (R18).
Maintenance of the device is limited by its design. The absence of a general-purpose or soft-core
processor (R22) means there is no software to patch, and functional changes are made by replacing
the bitstream under configuration control rather than by patching an installed image. Future design
iterations may change this. The one recurring maintenance activity specified in the requirement set is
the periodic rotation of cryptographic keys (R15), which is performed at defined intervals by
authorized personnel. Requirements governing the records retained for these activities are not
specified in the current requirement set

project is being publicly released alongside this report for a more detailed look at the
implementation. Figure 8 shows the design that the requirements aim to define. The design breaks
down to two main components: I/O (input/output) and cryptography.

6.1. Major Design Decisions
Based on the requirements of the system and the prior research done into FPGA-based safety
systems FPGAs were selected as the implementation platform (R21, R22) [3].
For the external communication protocol, Ethernet was selected as it satisfies the requirements and
is a ubiquitous technology across nuclear power plants and any other facility (R1, R2). In order to
maintain unidirectional communication, the unused TX or RX pins will be tied off to ensure traffic
cannot flow in an undesired direction.
The cryptographic module will be a symmetric encryption algorithm, for simplicitiy’s sake, an
Authenticated Encryption with Associated Data (AEAD) algorithm will be utilized to provide
confidentiality and integrity/authenticity in one algorithm. Advanced Encryption Standard (AES)
256 in the Galois Counter Mode (GCM) satisfies the requirements and is recommended by NIST
(R8-D,R9-A,R9-B) [18]. For the initial design, keys will be hardcoded for rapid prototyping, the
future design will utilize ephemeral keys generated and stored in a secure element, only accessible
from the cryptographic domain of the FPGA. The IV will be generated in a deterministic manner to
be consistent with NIST SP 800-38D (R11,R11-A,R11-B).

The device will encrypt the payload of the incoming Ethernet packets so that the application data is
not modified (R3,R3-A)

allow for use of different FPGA vendors, cryptographic algorithms, and communication protocols
in a voting architecture of the designer’s choice. Figure 10 is how a voting architecture could be used

6.2. Hardware Selection
The design requires a minimum of two Ethernet ports, three with logging enabled, which is lacking
in the current market of FPGA development boards. The Nexys Video from Digilent was identified
as a candidate for this project as it has a large fabric for the cryptographic core, Ethernet
management, and most importantly, an FPGA Mezzanine Card (FMC) low pin connector (LPC)
that allows us to connect an expansion board with more Ethernet ports [19].
The FMC expansion board being utilized is the Opsero Ethernet FMC 2.5V, which provides four
additional Ethernet ports, enabling future work on configuration and logging [20].

6.3. Predeveloped Software Components
To save on costs and ensure the ability to share the full source code, open source and Sandia-
controlled FPGA packages were found and integrated to the design for core components. Since the
main function of the device is encryption of Ethernet traffic, the two core components are the
cryptographic component and the Ethernet component.
For the cryptographic component, the open-source search for a complete and functional
AES256GCM Verilog core found no reputable repository available. An internal search found a
functional and well documented AES core that needed to have the additional functionality to
implement the GCM mode of operation.
For the Ethernet component, a robust implementation was identified that is developed by Alex
Forencich. The repository is called Verilog-Ethernet on GitHub and is permissively licensed. This
repository is no longer maintained but, Alex Forencich actively maintains a stricter licensed version.
The AES256GCM component and the AES core utilized will be open sourced as part of this
milestone. This code is not designed for resiliency against physical attacks, side-channel attacks, or
dynamic power analysis. The purpose of the code is to provide developers access to a free core for
prototyping and testing. The encryption key and IV handling is currently under development, and is
unsafe to use in a production environment currently.

7. SUMMARY AND CONCLUSIONS
The design and requirements approach outlined in this report demonstrates how advanced reactors
can shift away from traditional wrap-around security and begin engineering security into the design
of the plant using cybersecurity-by-design principles. Utilizing the paradigms from safety system
licensing, security features can be designed to be fail-safe, fail-secure, and provide defense-in-depth
so that a single compromise or failure cannot prevent safe and secure operation of a facility.
The method begins by establishing a traceable hierarchy of authoritative inputs that constrain and
shape the system architecture. These can be things like CFR documents, NRC Regulatory Guides,
and standards documents. These documents are identified before any requirements or design
elements are written or chosen. Then, utilizing the updated conceptual defensive model utilizing
X.800, identify the security services that the design is implementing, and at what logical boundary
level. This provides a very quick path to writing simple and testable requirements.
This process was demonstrated with the FPGA device that enables a diverse and redundant
communications system. This system utilizes highly modular components, allowing vendors to
quickly adapt it for their needs or to utilize a diverse selection of FPGA hardware or IP software to
eliminate single points of failure.
This report provides a detailed description of the design process. The outcomes of this design
process are two GitHub repositories with the hardware description language (HDL) code for the
project readily available for any AR vendor to pick up and use for prototyping and design testing.
The first repository is a modular AES256GCM implementation that is compatible with any
AXIStream compatible design. The second repository is the code necessary to implement the AES
code into this project. The architecture of the project allows for a vendor to use any algorithm(s)
that they want and to replace the open-source repositories utilized with specialized, certified
repositories if they have purchased any nuclear qualified IP cores.
Diverse and redundant systems will be vital for advanced reactors, as cybersecurity features will be
required for remote monitoring and operations of facilities, as well as for enabling wireless and
satellite communications. In order to enable cybersecurity mechanisms that are integrated more
deeply into the facility, they need to be implemented at the design of the facility rather than after.
Diversity and redundancy in these features are what ensure the facility can continue functioning
even if one component fails or is compromised.
Without clearly traceable requirements, design features in a plant will not be able to be justified to
the NRC. Licensees need to be able to demonstrate to the regulatory bodies why a system is
necessary as well as demonstrating that it is properly designed and implemented. By linking each
requirement to the security service, defensive strategy, and/or the relevant logical boundary layer, a
licensee can quickly identify gaps, create test plans, and justify each feature to a regulator.
Modern plants cannot rely on the wrap-around cybersecurity of legacy fleets if the licensees and
operators want to utilize advanced technology like wireless, remote facilities, or autonomous
systems. In order to demonstrate that advanced cybersecurity features enhance the safety of a plant
rather than weaken it, CSbD principles must be used to bake in cybersecurity at the beginning of a
design, rather than the end.

7.1. Next Steps
This design is the first iteration of the device, enabling a demonstration of the process but also
setting up a pathway to future designs. Testing is necessary on the device to prove the functionality

and security of the system and show the development of test cases from the requirements laid out in
this report.
Key and IV management are vital components of the system that are not fully implemented. The
implementation also has limitations on network throughput that meet current requirements but will
hinder higher bandwidth communications such as video feeds. A public key infrastructure
implementation will be necessary to enable secure key rotation in a live environment as well as to
potentially enable bidirectional communications with greater data origin authentication and access
control measures.

B.1.1. Mapping security services to security mechanisms
Each X.800 security service is provided by a defined set of security mechanisms. The framework
maps each selected service to the mechanisms that realize it, following the service-to-mechanism
relationships established in X.800. Error! Reference source not found. lists the service and its
associated mechanism and was adopted from the X.800 standard. A single service is commonly
realized by more than one mechanism, and a single mechanism commonly supports more than one
service. For example, peer entity authentication is realized by encipherment, digital signature, and
authentication exchange, while encipherment also supports confidentiality and integrity services.
This many-to-many mapping is the basis for distributing protection across mechanisms and layers.

B.1.2. LBL-specific requirement decomposition
A service and its associated mechanism can be positioned at more than one logical boundary layer
(LBL). The framework decomposes each service and mechanism pairing into a separate requirement
for each layer at which the service and mechanism applies. Error! Reference source not found.
illustrates the mapping of the security services to their associated OSI layer. These OSI layers follow
where the protected function resides in the communication stack and are mapped to their LBL

layers. As an example, encipherment for connection confidentiality can be applied at LBL I, LBL II,
and LBL III as well as at the boundaries between adjacent layers. Decomposition yields a distinct
requirement for each mechanism and layer combination, which is what allows protection to be
layered and gaps to be identified.
