 ABSTRACT
 In the nuclear industry, acoustic emission (AE) sensors are vital for real-time
structural health monitoring as well as early detection of material degradation. By
capturing the high-frequency stress waves emitted from defects such as cracks,
corrosion, or fatigue, AE sensors enable non-invasive monitoring of critical
components (e.g., reactor vessels, piping, and containment structures). This
technology supports predictive maintenance, enhances safety, and ensures
regulatory compliance by providing early warning of potential failures. It has also
become instrumental in research, particularly in materials testing reactors, where
it is used to monitor the behavior of fuels and materials under irradiation—by
allowing for real-time detection of cracking or other acoustic signals. This in turn
enables evaluation of the performance and accident behavior of advanced fuel
concepts.

 1. INTRODUCTION
 Acoustic emission (AE) sensors play a vital role in detecting and analyzing transient elastic waves
generated by material deformation or crack initiation/propagation. Ensuring the reliability and sensitivity
of these sensors is especially critical when they are deployed in demanding environments such as nuclear
facilities. In order to offset limitations identified in [1], this project introduces a set of innovative design
and testing methodologies for enhancing radiation tolerant magnetostrictive AE sensor performance and
operational robustness.
 This project focused on three key innovations: (1) development of an active self-testing system for
use prior to sensor deployment and during in-situ sensor validation, (2) optimization of the mechanical
transition interface via horn-shaped amplifiers, and (3) integration of a direct-current (DC) coil bias
magnet to improve sensor sensitivity.

 2. ACTIVE SELF-TESTING SYSTEM
 Traditional methods of verifying AE sensor coil integrity often rely on simple resistance measurements.
While this approach can detect gross failures such as a completely open or shorted coil, it is insufficient for
identifying more subtle or progressive faults that may compromise in-service sensor performance.
 The key limitations of relying on simple resistance measurements are:
 • Partial short circuits: Resistance measurements may not detect internal shorting between a few
 turns of the coil as the total resistance change is small. These partial shorts can reduce the effective
 number of functioning turns, altering the sensor sensitivity and frequency response without
 significantly changing the overall resistance.
 • Degraded insulation: Coils with degraded insulation may function normally under low-voltage
 testing conditions but fail under the higher voltages encountered during actual operation. This
 failure mode is invisible to standard resistance checks.

 11

 • Incorrect number of turns: Manufacturing defects can result in an incorrect number of coil turns.
 This affects sensor inductance and sensitivity but may still yield a resistance value within the
 acceptable limits.
 These limitations highlight the need for a more comprehensive self-testing system that goes beyond
static resistance checks. The active self-testing system developed in this project addresses this gap by
actively stimulating the sensor and analyzing its ultrasonic response. This enables detection of subtle faults
and ensures that the sensor remains fully functional throughout its service life.
 Figure 2 shows a photo of six AE sensors fabricated in the Measurement Sciences Laboratory. Some
are fully functional; the others have been rendered non-functional as a result of various fabrication or
assembly issues.

Figure 2. Mix of AE sensors, both functional and non-functional.

 Figure 3 presents the active ultrasonic responses of the six AE sensors shown in Figure 2, along with
their corresponding resistance measurements. While sensors (a), (b), (c), (d), and (f) all exhibit similar
resistance values, only (a) and (c) produce correct ultrasonic responses when an active pulse is applied.
This indicates that resistance alone is not a reliable indicator of sensor functionality. Notably, sensor (e)
shows a resistance of 17 MΩ, suggesting an internal wire break or disconnection. Its lack of response in
the active test further confirms that it is non-functional.

Figure 3. Active test responses and resistance measurements for the six AE sensors.

 The active self-testing system is a reliable way to verify AE sensor functionality, regardless of failure
mode. It can be applied both prior to sensor installation and during in-service operation. In-service testing
is particularly critical in harsh environments where sensor performance may degrade over time due to
thermal, mechanical, or radiation-related stresses. By enabling continuous condition monitoring without
requiring sensor removal or replacement, this system ensures long-term reliability and data integrity.
 3. FACEPLATE TO TRANSDUCER COUPLING OPTIMIZATION
 USING HORN STRUCTURES
 To enhance the transmission of acoustic energy from the monitored structure to the AE sensor, three
horn-shaped transition parts were designed and analyzed. Each horn was made from stainless steel (SS)
316 and featured a consistent geometry comprising a 6-mm-diameter base, a 2-mm-diameter top, and a
height of 12 mm, forming a tapered profile intended to focus and amplify incoming acoustic signals.
 3.1. Simulation Setup and Results
 Finite-element simulations were performed in ABAQUS to evaluate the amplification performance of
three different horn-shaped transition part designs (i.e., designs A, B, and C). A 200 kHz tone burst was
applied to the bottom surface of each horn to simulate AE energy. This frequency was selected based on
prior experimental observations in which 200 kHz was commonly associated with crack-related AE
signals in metallic structures. Figure 4 illustrates the simulation setup, including the meshed geometries of
horn designs A, B, and C. A 200 kHz excitation was introduced at the base of each horn, and the resulting
displacement at the center of the top surface was recorded so as to assess signal amplification.

 13

Figure 4. ABAQUS simulation setup.

 Figure 5 presents the simulation results for designs A, B, and C, shown in Figure 4. The results
demonstrate that all three horn geometries effectively amplified the input 200 kHz signal. Specifically, the
displacement amplitude at the top surface of each horn increased by a factor of 3–5× in comparison to the
input excitation. These findings confirm the effectiveness of geometric shaping at enhancing acoustic
energy transmission to the AE sensor.

 3.2. Experimental Validation
 Two experimental setups were developed to validate the simulation results and assess the effectiveness
of the horn-shaped transition part designs:
Test 1: Controlled excitation
 In this test, a PZT disc was bonded to the bottom surface of each horn-shaped transition part, using
epoxy. A five-cycle 200 kHz sine wave was then applied to the PZT to simulate a controlled AE source.
The vibration amplitudes at each horn’s bottom and top surfaces were measured with a laser Doppler
vibrometer.

 14

 Figure 6 illustrates the experimental setup for Test 1. As shown in the left-hand image, a thin PZT
disc was bonded to the bottom surface of each horn by using epoxy, while a laser was employed to
capture the vibrational response at the top surface. The right-hand image shows the horn components after
having been flipped to expose the bonded PZT discs, thus allowing for the input response of the PZT to
be recorded as well.

Figure 6. Experimental setup for Test 1.

 Figure 7 presents the measured responses at both the bottom and top surfaces of the three horn
designs. Due to the potential variability in the PZT discs, as well as inconsistencies in epoxy bonding, the
amplitude data have been normalized to an amplitude of 1 for the bottom response for comparison. As
seen, all three designs exhibit significant amplification at the top surface relative to the bottom,
confirming the horns’ effectiveness at enhancing vibrational energy.
 Although the amplification ratios are slightly lower than the simulation-predicted ones, the overall
trends are consistent. Notably, design C demonstrated the highest amplification in the experiment,
whereas design B showed the highest in the simulation. This discrepancy may stem from several factors.
First, the coupling between the PZT and horn part may vary as a result of imperfect centering during
bonding—epoxy can be slippery prior to curing, making precise alignment difficult. Second, the
experimental response duration is longer than in the simulation, suggesting that vibrations may continue
to develop as they reflect between the top and bottom surfaces.
 Despite these differences, all the horn-shaped part designs significantly amplified the input signal. An
interesting observation from Figure 7 is the prolonged ringing in the response envelope of design C,
visible on both the top and bottom surfaces. This behavior may be attributed to its stepped geometry,
which can potentially trap vibrational energy within each step. In contrast, designs A and B feature
smoother transitions, allowing vibrations to propagate more freely between surfaces. Design B is
considered the best option for its mix of amplification and quick ringdown.

Figure 7. Responses at the bottom and top surfaces of the three horn-shaped part designs in Test 1.

Test 2: Benchtop AE simulation
 To simulate a more realistic AE scenario, a PZT disc was mounted on a 0.5-in.-thick SS plate in order
to act as the AE source. The disc was excited using a short spike pulse. The three horn-shaped transition
parts were also attached to the plate, and were each positioned at approximately equal distances from the
source. Vibration measurements were taken using a laser Doppler vibrometer at the top surface of each
horn, as well as at two nearby reference points on the plate.

 16

 Figure 8 illustrates the experimental setup for Test 2. As previously stated, a PZT disc was bonded to
a 0.5-in.-thick SS plate to serve as the AE source. The three horn-shaped designs were mounted on the
same plate to receive the AE signal. Additionally, two reference points on either side of the horns were
used to capture the baseline response prior to amplification by the horn structures. During measurements,
the laser focus was carefully adjusted for each point so as to maximize the intensity of the reflected signal
(see the right-hand image in Figure 8).

Figure 8. Experimental setup for Test 2.

 Figure 9 displays the measured responses from Test 2. As can be seen, both reference points exhibit
amplitudes of around 0.15, while designs A and B show significantly higher amplitudes of approximately
0.6. Design C, in contrast, shows a more moderate amplitude of about 0.3.
 Although all the measurement points were positioned at similar distances from the AE source,
variability in the results may still stem from factors such as epoxy bonding quality, slight differences in
sensor placement, and boundary effects on the plate. Nevertheless, the results clearly demonstrate that the
horn structures effectively amplify the AE energy.

 4. SENSITIVITY ENHANCEMENT BY USING A DC COIL AS A BIAS
 MAGNET
 To enhance the magnetostrictive AE sensor’s sensitivity (see Figure 1), a DC coil was incorporated
into the sensor assembly to induce a bias magnetic field. This field interacts with the sensor’s
magnetostrictive components, improving its responsiveness to mechanical vibrations. The magnetostrictive
effect, when properly biased, increases the conversion efficiency between the mechanical strain and the
electrical signal, thereby amplifying the sensor’s output.
 In nuclear environments, use of permanent magnets is generally infeasible due to their susceptibility to
radiation-induced demagnetization, temperature instability, and long-term degradation. These factors can
lead to unpredictable sensor behavior and reduced reliability over time. To overcome this, a DC coil was
employed to provide a controllable, stable magnetic field. This coil-based approach allows for:
 • Radiation tolerance, as the magnetic field is generated electrically and is not dependent on magnet
 material properties
 • Dynamic tuning, with the DC current being adjustable in real-time to optimize sensitivity
 • Integration with the self-testing system, thus enabling automatic calibration of the bias current, as
 based on sensor response.
 Figure 10 illustrates the design for integrating a DC bias coil to enhance the magnetostrictive
response of the material. The configuration is compact and maintains the sensor’s original dimensions.
Figure 11 presents the sensing coil’s response under active testing as the current in the bias coil is varied.

 Even though this design can efficiently enhance the AE sensing, the current design presents a thermal
challenge: the DC coil tends to overheat when operated with continuous current. This can affect sensor
stability and long-term reliability. The following potential solutions can be investigated in future research:
 • Pulse-width modulation or duty-cycled operation to reduce the average power consumption yet
 still maintain an effective magnetic bias
 • A low-resistance coil design based on using thicker wire or optimized winding geometry to
 reduce the Joule heating.
 Smart control algorithms that activate the coil only during measurement or self-test cycles, thus
minimizing continuous power draw.
 5. CONCLUSION
 This research has introduced and validated several key innovations aimed at improving the
performance, reliability, and adaptability of magnetostrictive AE sensors for structural health monitoring,
particularly in demanding environments such as nuclear facilities. An active self-testing system was
developed to ensure sensor functionality both prior to deployment and during service. This system
represents a robust method for detecting sensor degradation or failure modes that traditional resistance
checks cannot identify, in turn enabling continuous performance assurance even without physical access.
To enhance signal transmission, horn-shaped transition parts were designed using finite-element
modeling, then validated through experiments. These structures significantly amplified the acoustic
signals, improving the sensor’s ability to detect weak AEs. A DC coil bias magnet was incorporated to
enhance the sensor’s magnetostrictive response, increasing its sensitivity to low-amplitude AE events by
pre-aligning the magnetic domains. This approach circumvents the limitations of permanent magnets in
high-radiation environments, and enables automatic tuning of the magnetic bias through integration with
the self-testing system. While the current design faces thermal challenges during continuous operation,

several mitigation strategies have been identified for future development. Taken together, these
advancements contribute to the realization of a more sensitive, reliable, and intelligent AE sensing
system—one capable of operating effectively in harsh and safety-critical environments.
