Price of Coupling in Multilevel Linear Programming
Nagisa Sugishita, Margarida Carvalho

Abstract
Multilevel programming is the standard mathematical framework for modeling hierarchical decision-making, yet a comprehensive understanding of its computational complexity remains fragmented. In this paper, we characterize the computational complexity of deciding the existence of feasible and optimal solutions, as well as computing the optimal objective value in multilevel linear programming (LP). Our analysis considers various combinations of modeling assumptions, including the presence or absence of linking (coupling) constraints and whether all variables are bounded. In particular, we show the feasibility problem of k k -level LP for k ≥ 2 k\geq 2 is Σ k − 1 p \Sigma^{p}_{k-1} -complete in general. When linking constraints and unbounded variables are absent, feasibility is polynomial-time solvable for k ≤ 4 k\leq 4 but becomes Σ k − 1 p \Sigma^{p}_{k-1} -complete for k ≥ 5 k\geq 5 , indicating a sharp jump in computational complexity between four and five levels assuming the polynomial hierarchy does not collapse. Combined with other results, one major implication is that no polynomial-time Turing machine can transform a bilevel LP instance with linking constraints into one without linking constraints while preserving feasibility unless P = NP \textup{P}{}=\textup{NP}{} . In contrast, such machines exist for all k ≥ 5 k\geq 5 . We observe similar phenomena with the computational complexity of deciding the existence of an optimal solution in multilevel LP. In the bilevel case, feasibility and boundedness fully characterize the existence of an optimal solution, implying that the problem is DP-complete. However, these conditions are insufficient for k ≥ 3 k\geq 3 . We show that the problem for k ≥ 3 k\geq 3 is Δ k p \Delta^{p}_{k} -complete. Similar to the feasibility problem, the decision of the existence of an optimal solution becomes polynomially solvable for k = 2 , 3 k=2,3 without linking constraints and unbounded variables. However, the problem is Δ k p \Delta^{p}_{k} -complete for k ≥ 4 k\geq 4 , even with these simplifying assumptions. Interestingly, the computation of the optimal objective value is F ​ Δ k p \textup{F}\Delta^{p}_{k} -complete for any k ≥ 2 k\geq 2 , even without linking constraints and unbounded variables. We also discuss the extension of our results to the mixed-binary cases.

Introduction

A multilevel programming problem is one in which some decision variables
are constrained to belong to the optimal solution set of another problem.
Following the work of Bracken and McGill
[
6
]
and Candler and Norton
[
7
]
, research activity in this area has expanded considerably.
In particular, methodological progress in bilevel programming has produced a substantial literature addressing both theoretical questions and computational techniques; see Beck
et al.

[
5
]
, Carvalho
et al.

[
8
]
, and Dempe and Zemkoho
[
11
]
for recent overviews.
Interest in trilevel programming has also grown, motivated by its expressive modeling power. Notable applications include the critical node problem
[
19
]
, resource allocation models
[
9
]
, fortification–interdiction settings
[
25
]
, and cyber-security planning
[
17
]
. Further examples of multilevel applications are surveyed in
[
18
,
27
]
.

In this work, we focus on multilevel linear programming (LP).
Moreover, we concentrate on the optimistic variant of multilevel models, where, if a lower-level problem possesses multiple optimal solutions, the one that is most advantageous for the (immediately preceding) upper-level decision maker is assumed to be chosen.

In multilevel LP, a constraint is referred to as a linking (coupling) constraint if it involves variables from the followers.
The presence of linking constraints makes multilevel LP more complicated at first glance.
For example, the feasible set of a bilevel LP is connected when there are no linking constraints, but this property does not hold when linking constraints appear in the leader’s problem
[
2
,
10
]
.
Indeed, one can model any mixed-binary LP as a bilevel LP with linking constraints
[
3
]
.

However, from the perspective of computational complexity, many results hold regardless of the presence of linking constraints. For example, Jeroslow
[
14
]
showed that the decision version of
k
-level LP is
\Sigma^{p}_{k-1}
-hard, and this remains true even when the instance is restricted to have no linking constraints. Recently, Sugishita and Carvalho
[
24
]
established its membership in
\Sigma^{p}_{k-1}
, implying that the decision version of
k
-level LP is
\Sigma^{p}_{k-1}
-complete with or without linking constraints. Rodrigues
et al.

[
21
]
proved
\Sigma^{p}_{k-1}
-hardness for deciding unboundedness in
k
-level LP with linking constraints, and later Sugishita and Carvalho
[
24
]
showed that this problem is
\Sigma^{p}_{k-1}
-complete regardless of the presence of linking constraints. Similarly, it follows from Vicente
et al.

[
26
]
that, for a given bilevel LP and a candidate solution, verifying whether it is a local optimum is
coNP
-hard, with or without linking constraints.
We note that all of the above hardness results are based on transformations producing instances with coefficients of polynomial magnitude.
Consequently, under standard complexity assumptions, there cannot exist a pseudo-polynomial-time (oracle) Turing machine for the corresponding decision problems.

There is also growing interest in developing approaches that transform a bilevel LP instance with linking constraints into one without them. One notable example is the work of Henke
et al.

[
13
]
, which discusses an augmented-Lagrangian-based approach for removing linking constraints. Sugishita and Carvalho
[
23
]
presented a polynomial-time procedure that transforms a bilevel LP instance with linking constraints into an equivalent one without them, while preserving the optimal solutions. The correctness of these methods relies on certain assumptions, such as the existence of an optimal solution.

In this paper, we analyze the computational complexity of deciding the existence of feasible and optimal solutions in multilevel LP, as well as the computational complexity of computing the optimal objective value, as summarized in Table
1
.
These problems are fundamental and of interest per se.
For example, the equivalence of the decision version of LP and its feasibility problem plays a key role in the analysis of the polynomial solvability of LP.
Our results show that such an equivalence fails for bilevel LP without linking constraints.
Furthermore, our results indicate the impacts of linking and unbounded variables on the computational complexity of
k
-level LP, hence the title of this paper.
For instance, deciding the feasibility of a trilevel LP with linking constraints is
\Sigma^{p}_{2}
-complete.
Without linking constraints, the problem becomes
coNP
-complete.
Moreover, if there are neither linking constraints nor unbounded variables, the problem is solvable in polynomial time.
We observe that the computational complexity appears to increase with the number of levels
k
in multilevel LP. A somewhat surprising result is that, without linking constraints and unbounded variables, feasibility can be decided in polynomial time for
k\leq 4
, but becomes
\Sigma^{p}_{k-1}
-complete for
k\geq 5
.
Thus, there is a sharp increase in computational complexity when moving from 4-level to 5-level LPs, assuming the polynomial hierarchy does not collapse.
With linking constraints, the problem is
\Sigma^{p}_{k-1}
-complete for any
k\geq 2
.

An interesting implication of the complexity results for feasibility decisions is that unless
\textup{P}{}=\textup{NP}{}
(respectively,
\textup{coNP}{}=\Sigma^{p}_{2}
, which in turn implies
\textup{NP}{}=\textup{coNP}{}
), there is no polynomial-time Turing machine that transforms a bilevel LP (respectively, trilevel LP) instance with linking constraints into one without linking constraints while preserving feasibility.
In this regard, the assumption of the existence of optimal solutions used in the aforementioned transformation of bilevel LP to remove linking constraints in
[
23
]
seems unavoidable: we need an assumption that is not verifiable in polynomial time, or we need to give up on the efficiency of the transformation.
In contrast, for
k\geq 5
, such a polynomial-time Turing machine does exist, although such a transformation may not preserve the optimal objective value, as it is designed only to preserve feasibility.

We also analyze the computational complexity of deciding the existence of an optimal solution in
k
-level LP.
In the bilevel case, an optimal solution exists if and only if the instance is both feasible and not unbounded.
Intuitively, this means that deciding the existence of an optimal solution corresponds to the intersection of the feasibility problem and the complement of the unboundedness problem.
The complexity class
DP
introduced by Papadimitriou and Yannakakis
[
20
]
captures decision problems of this type.
For trilevel LP, the situation appears more complex: feasibility and boundedness are necessary but not sufficient for the existence of an optimal solution. In fact, we show that deciding the existence of an optimal solution in a
k
-level LP with
k\geq 3
is
\Delta^{p}_{k}
-complete.
We note that
DP
equals
\textup{P}{}_{\parallel}^{\textup{NP}{}[2]}
, the second level of the query hierarchy over
NP
containing decision problems solvable with two parallel queries to the
NP
oracle, while
\Delta^{p}_{k}
equals
\textup{P}{}^{\Sigma^{p}_{k-1}}
, a class of decision problems that can be solved by a polynomial-time Turing machine with oracle access to
\Sigma^{p}_{k-1}
(which can make polynomially many adaptive queries)
[
28
]
.
We also examine the special case where the input instance has neither linking constraints nor unbounded variables. As observed for the feasibility problem, the computational complexity of the problem appears to increase sharply from
k=3
(polynomial-time solvable) to
k=4
(
\Delta^{p}_{k}
-complete), assuming the polynomial hierarchy does not collapse.

In this paper, we also show that computing the optimal objective value of a
k
-level LP instance is
\textup{F}\Delta^{p}_{k}
-complete for
k\geq 2
, under metric reductions computed by logarithmic-space transducers
[
15
,
20
]
.
To the best of our knowledge, the computational complexity of any search problem associated with
k
-level LP has not been investigated in the literature, except for
[
12
]
.
In that reference, the author cites an unpublished manuscript and claims that the
k
-level LP problem is
\textup{F}\Delta^{p}_{k}
-complete.
However, the search problem discussed there is not clearly defined, and neither a proof nor a detailed discussion is provided.
Consequently, we are unable to compare our results with those in
[
12
]
.

) with
b=b^{(2)}
is feasible.

To formalize this argument, we adopt the notation from
[
1
]
.
Given a set-valued map
F
from
\mathbb{R}^{m}
to
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, the domain and the graph of
F
are given by

\mathrm{dom}{}(F)=\{b\in\mathbb{R}^{m}:F(b)\not=\emptyset\}

and

\mathrm{graph}{}(F)=\{(b,x)\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}:x\in F(b)\},

respectively.
We say
F
is Lipschitz continuous if and only if there exists
M
such that for any
b,b^{\prime}\in\mathrm{dom}{}(F)
,

F(b)\subset F(b^{\prime})+M\|b-b^{\prime}\|_{2}B,

where
B\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is the unit ball.

Lemma 4
.

(i)

The domain
\mathrm{dom}{}(F)
is closed.

(ii)

The set-valued function
F
is Lipschitz continuous.

Proof.

(i)

Following Basu
et al.

[
4
]
, we have

\mathrm{graph}{}(F)\allowbreak=\bigcup_{\omega\in\Omega(A,c)}U(\omega,A,b,c),

where

\Omega(A,c)=\left\{\omega\in\{0,1\}^{m}:\exists\lambda\geq\mathbf{0}\text{ s.t.\ }A_{22}^{\top}\lambda=c_{22},(\mathbf{1}-\omega)^{\top}\lambda=0\right\}

and

U(\omega,A,b,c)=\left\{\begin{pmatrix}b\\
x_{1}\\
x_{2}\end{pmatrix}:\begin{array}[]{l}\displaystyle A_{21}x_{1}+A_{22}x_{2}\geq b,\\
\displaystyle\omega^{\top}(b-A_{21}x_{1}-A_{22}x_{2})\geq 0\end{array}\right\}.

Thus,

\displaystyle\mathrm{dom}{}(F)=\mathrm{proj}_{b}\left(\bigcup_{\omega\in\Omega(A,c)}U(\omega,A,b,c)\right)=\bigcup_{\omega\in\Omega(A,c)}\mathrm{proj}_{b}\left(U(\omega,A,b,c)\right),

which is closed.

(ii)

Let
b^{(1)},b^{(2)}\in\mathrm{dom}{}(F)
.
We show

F(b^{(1)})\subset F(b^{(2)})+M\|b^{(1)}-b^{(2)}\|_{2}B

for some constant
M
.
Pick any real point
(x_{1}^{(1)},x_{2}^{(1)})\in F(b^{(1)})
.
In particular,
A_{21}x_{1}^{(1)}+A_{22}x_{2}^{(1)}\geq b^{(1)}
.
Since
A_{21}x_{1}+A_{22}x_{2}\geq b^{(2)}
is feasible, by Lemma
3
, there exists
(\bar{x}_{1}^{(2)},\bar{x}_{2}^{(2)})
such that
A_{21}\bar{x}_{1}^{(2)}+A_{22}\bar{x}_{2}^{(2)}\geq b^{(2)}
and
\|(x_{1}^{(1)},x_{2}^{(1)})-(\bar{x}_{1}^{(2)},\bar{x}_{2}^{(2)})\|_{\infty}\leq M^{\prime}\|b^{(1)}-b^{(2)}\|_{\infty}
, where where
M^{\prime}
is the constant described in Lemma
3
.

Now, observe that
(x_{1}^{(1)},x_{2}^{(1)})\in F(b^{(1)})
implies that
x_{2}^{(1)}
is optimal for the lower-level problem with
b=b^{(1)}
and
x_{1}=x_{1}^{(1)}
:

\min_{x_{2}}\{c_{22}^{\top}x_{2}:A_{21}x_{1}^{(1)}+A_{22}x_{2}\geq b^{(1)}\}.

(1)

Next, consider the lower-level problem with
b=b^{(2)}
and
x_{1}=\bar{x}_{1}^{(2)}
:

\min_{x_{2}}\{c_{22}^{\top}x_{2}:A_{21}\bar{x}_{1}^{(2)}+A_{22}x_{2}\geq b^{(2)}\}.

(2)

Note that
\bar{x}_{2}^{(2)}
is feasible for (
2
).
Furthermore, (
2
) cannot be unbounded, as otherwise it would imply the unboundedness of (
1
).
Instances (
1
) and (
2
) are LPs that only differ in their RHS: the RHS of (
1
) is
b^{(1)}-A_{21}x_{1}^{(1)}
while that of (
2
) is
b^{(2)}-A_{21}\bar{x}_{1}^{(2)}
.
Thus, by Lemma
3
there is an optimal solution
\hat{x}_{2}^{(2)}
for (
2
) such that

\displaystyle\|\hat{x}_{2}^{(2)}-x_{2}^{(1)}\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq M^{\prime\prime}\|(b^{(1)}-A_{21}x_{1}^{(1)})-(b^{(2)}-A_{21}\bar{x}_{1}^{(2)})\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq M^{\prime\prime}(\|b^{(1)}-b^{(2)}\|_{\infty}+\|A_{21}\|_{\infty}\cdot\|x_{1}^{(1)}-\bar{x}_{1}^{(2)}\|_{\infty})

\displaystyle\leq M^{\prime\prime}(1+\|A_{21}\|_{\infty}M^{\prime})\|b^{(1)}-b^{(2)}\|_{\infty},

where
M^{\prime\prime}
is the constant given in Lemma
3
.
Now, observe that
(\bar{x}_{1}^{(2)},\hat{x}_{2}^{(2)})\in F(b^{(2)})
.
Using the equivalence of norms
\|\cdot\|_{2}
and
\|\cdot\|_{\infty}
, we have

\displaystyle\left\|\begin{pmatrix}x_{1}^{(1)}\\
x_{2}^{(1)}\end{pmatrix}-\begin{pmatrix}\bar{x}_{1}^{(2)}\\
\hat{x}_{2}^{(2)}\end{pmatrix}\right\|_{2}\leq M\|b^{(1)}-b^{(2)}\|_{2},

where
M
is a constant depending on
M^{\prime}
and
M^{\prime\prime}
.
Since
(x_{1}^{(1)},x_{2}^{(1)})
was an arbitrary point in
F(b^{(1)})
, it follows that

F(b^{(1)})\subset F(b^{(2)})+M\|b^{(1)}-b^{(2)}\|_{2}B.

Next, we prove the continuity of the value function
V
.
Given a function
f
from
\mathbb{R}^{m}
to
\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty,-\infty\}
, we define the effective domain of
f
as

\mathrm{dom}{}(f)=\{b\in\mathbb{R}^{m}:f(b)<\infty\}.

Furthermore, we say that a function
f
is Lipschitz continuous if and only if
f(b)\not=-\infty
for all
b
and there exists
N
such that

|f(b)-f(b^{\prime})|\leq N\|b-b^{\prime}\|_{2}

for any
b,b^{\prime}\in\mathrm{dom}{}(f)
.
The following theorem establishes the continuity of
V
.

Theorem 5
.

(i)

The effective domain
\mathrm{dom}{}(V)
is closed.

(ii)

If there exists
b
such that
V(b)=-\infty
, then for all
b
,
V(b)=-\infty
or
\infty
.

(iii)

Suppose
V(b)>-\infty
for all
b
.
Then,
V
is Lipschitz continuous.

Proof.

Assertion (i) follows from Lemma
4
since
\mathrm{dom}{}(V)=\mathrm{dom}{}(F)
.
In the following, we prove assertions (ii) and (iii) at once.

We assume
\mathrm{dom}{}(F)\neq\emptyset
, for otherwise the statements hold trivially.
Pick any
b^{(1)}
,
b^{(2)}\in\mathrm{dom}{}(F)
and
(x^{(1)}_{1},x^{(1)}_{2})\in F(b^{(1)})
.
Then, by Lemma
4
, there is
(x^{(2)}_{1},x^{(2)}_{2})\in F(b^{(2)})
such that

\left\|\begin{pmatrix}x^{(1)}_{1}\\
x^{(1)}_{2}\end{pmatrix}-\begin{pmatrix}x^{(2)}_{1}\\
x^{(2)}_{2}\end{pmatrix}\right\|_{2}\leq M\|b^{(1)}-b^{(2)}\|_{2}.

The difference of the objective values of the two points can be bounded as

\displaystyle\left|\sum_{j=1}^{2}c_{1j}^{\top}x^{(1)}_{j}-\sum_{j=1}^{2}c_{1j}^{\top}x^{(2)}_{j}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left\|\begin{pmatrix}c_{11}\\
c_{12}\end{pmatrix}\right\|_{2}\,\left\|\begin{pmatrix}x^{(1)}_{1}\\
x^{(1)}_{2}\end{pmatrix}-\begin{pmatrix}x^{(2)}_{1}\\
x^{(2)}_{2}\end{pmatrix}\right\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\left\|\begin{pmatrix}c_{11}\\
c_{12}\end{pmatrix}\right\|_{2}M\|b^{(1)}-b^{(2)}\|_{2}.

This implies that we have
V(b^{(2)})\leq V(b^{(1)})+N\|b^{(1)}-b^{(2)}\|_{2}
for some constant
N
.
Thus, assertions (ii) and (iii) follow.

Finally, we prove that the feasible and optimal solution sets are closed.

Theorem 6
.

(i)

The graph of the feasible solution set
\mathrm{graph}{}(F)
is closed.

(ii)

The graph of the optimal solution set
\mathrm{graph}{}(S)
is closed.

Proof.

(i)

By Lemma
4
,
F
is upper semicontinuous and
\mathrm{dom}{}(F)
is closed.
Furthermore, for any real
b
,
F(b)
is closed.
Invoke Proposition 1.4.8 of
[
1
]
to see that
\mathrm{graph}{}(F)
is closed.

(ii)

If
\mathrm{dom}{}(V)=\emptyset
, or if
V(b)=-\infty
for some

,
\mathrm{graph}{}(S)=\emptyset
and hence closed.
Thus, we assume
\mathrm{dom}{}(V)\not=\emptyset
and
V(b)\not=-\infty
for all
b\in\mathrm{dom}{}(V)
.

Let
\{(x_{1}^{(i)},x_{2}^{(i)},b^{(i)}):i=1,2,\ldots\}
be a sequence in
\mathrm{graph}{}(S)
such that
(x_{1}^{(i)},x_{2}^{(i)},b^{(i)})\rightarrow(\bar{x}_{1},\bar{x}_{2},\bar{b})
.
Below, we prove that
(\bar{x}_{1},\bar{x}_{2},\bar{b})\in\mathrm{graph}{}(S)
, or equivalently, i)
(\bar{x}_{1},\bar{x}_{2})\in F(\bar{b})
(it is feasible) and ii)

Since
\mathrm{graph}{}(S)\subset\mathrm{graph}{}(F)
and since
\mathrm{graph}{}(F)
is closed, we have
(\bar{x}_{1},\bar{x}_{2},\bar{b})\in\mathrm{graph}{}(F)

.

The continuity of
V
and the closedness of
\mathrm{dom}{}(V)
(Theorem

) implies that
V(b^{(i)})\rightarrow V(\bar{b})
.
Together with
V(b^{(i)})=c_{11}^{\top}x_{1}^{(i)}+c_{12}^{\top}x_{2}^{(i)}\rightarrow c_{11}^{\top}\bar{x}_{1}+c_{12}^{\top}\bar{x}_{2}
, we have
c_{11}^{\top}\bar{x}_{1}+c_{12}^{\top}\bar{x}_{2}=V(\bar{b})
.

Thus, it follows that
(\bar{x}_{1},\bar{x}_{2})\in S(\bar{b})
, and therefore
(\bar{x}_{1},\bar{x}_{2},\bar{b})\in\mathrm{graph}{}(S)
.

As a consequence of (ii) in Theorem

, we obtain an immediate corollary.

Corollary 7
.

The feasible set of a
3\textup{-LP}
{}_{\neg\text{link}}
instance is closed.

5.2
Boolean Satisfiability Problem and Its Variants

In our hardness proofs, we use the Boolean satisfiability problem and several of its variants.
In this section, we summarize these problems.

Arguably, the most well-known problem of this type is the satisfiability problem
SAT
for Boolean formulae.
Let
\mathcal{B}
be the set of Boolean formulae.
The input of
SAT
is a Boolean formula
F{}\in\mathcal{B}{}
and it is a “yes” instance if and only if
F
is satisfiable.
In this paper, we identify truth assignments to the Boolean variables with binary vectors, and we say that a binary vector
s
satisfies
F
if and only if the corresponding truth assignment satisfies
F
.
For any
F{}\in\mathcal{B}{}
that is satisfiable, let
M(F)
be the lexicographically maximum binary vector satisfying
F
.
This quantity is undefined if
F
is not satisfiable.
The search problem
LEXSAT
takes
F
as input and requires outputting
M(F)
if
F
is satisfiable, or “no” otherwise.
The corresponding decision problem is
BITSAT
: the input is
F
, and it is a “yes” instance if and only if
F
is satisfiable and the last (least significant) component of
M(F)
is
1
.
It is known that
SAT
is
NP
-complete,
LEXSAT
is
\textup{F}\Delta^{p}_{2}
-complete, and
BITSAT
is
\Delta^{p}_{2}
-complete
[
15
]
.

The above problems can be extended by considering alternating quantifiers.
For
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
, let
\mathcal{B}_{k}
denote the set of closed quantified Boolean formulae (QBF)
H
in prenex normal form of the form

\exists\mu{}_{1}\forall\mu{}_{2}\exists\mu{}_{3}\ldots Q\mu{}_{k}\ [F(\mu{}_{1},\ldots,\mu{}_{k})=1],

(5)

where the sets of Boolean variables
\mu{}_{l}
, for
l=1,\ldots,k
, are pairwise disjoint,
F
is a quantifier-free Boolean formula and
Q
is
\exists
if
k
is odd and
\forall
otherwise.
For any
H\in\mathcal{B}_{k}
defined as (
5
), we write
H(s{}_{1})
to denote the QBF obtained by removing the quantifier
\exists\mu{}_{1}
and substituting the binary vector
s{}_{1}
for
\mu{}_{1}
:

\forall\mu{}_{2}\exists\mu{}_{3}\ldots Q\mu{}_{k}\ [F(s{}_{1},\mu{}_{2},\ldots,\mu{}_{k})=1].

For any
H\in\mathcal{B}_{k}
that is true, we define
M(H)
to be the lexicographically maximum binary vector
s{}_{1}
such that
H(s{}_{1})
is true.

For
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
, we define

k\textup{-SAT}=\{H\in\mathcal{B}_{k}:\text{$H$ is true{}}\}
,
k\textup{-LEXSAT}
as the search problem whose input is
H\in\mathcal{B}_{k}
and requires
M(H)
, or “no” if
H
is not true, and
k\textup{-BITSAT}=\{H\in\mathcal{B}_{k}:\text{$H$ is true{} and the last component of $M(H)$ is $1$}\}
.
We have that
k\textup{-SAT}
is
\Sigma^{p}_{k}
-complete
[
29
]
and
k\textup{-LEXSAT}
is
\textup{F}\Delta^{p}_{k}
-complete
[
16
]
.
Furthermore, Krentel
[
16
]
shows that the decision of any bit of
M(H)
, the language
\{(H,l)\in\mathcal{B}_{k}\times\mathbb{Z}_{>0}{}:\text{$H$ is true{} and the $l$-th component of $M(H)$ is $1$}\}
, is
\Delta^{p}_{k}
-complete.
It follows that the decision of the least significant bit, the language
k\textup{-BITSAT}
, is
\Delta^{p}_{k}
-complete.

Extensions and Limitations

In this section, we discuss several limitations of our results, as well as possible extensions.

First, we showed that deciding unboundedness of a
4
-level LP instance is
\Pi^{p}_{2}
-hard and belongs to
\Sigma^{p}_{3}
.
Whether it is complete for either of these complexity classes remains open. A closer inspection of the proof of Theorem
11
suggests that extending Lemma
9
to
4\textup{-LP}
would imply
\Pi^{p}_{2}
-completeness. Such an extension, in turn, would follow from a generalization of the sensitivity analysis developed in Section
3
, in particular, Theorem
5
, to trilevel LP. However, establishing these properties appears to require a deeper understanding of the structure and properties of trilevel LPs.

Second, this paper focuses on the search problem of computing the optimal objective value. A closely related problem is that of computing an optimal solution. A natural approach is as follows: first compute the optimal objective value. Then, iteratively refine the solution by adding a constraint enforcing that the objective value equals the computed optimum, and redefining the objective to minimize a selected component of
x_{1}
(e.g., the first component). By resolving the modified problem, one can determine the value of that component in some optimal solution (or, in the case where the optimum is not attained, after applying a suitable perturbation). Repeating this procedure for each component, fixing values one by one, yields a complete optimal solution.

This algorithm terminates in finitely many steps and correctly computes an optimal solution, when one exists. Moreover, it runs in polynomial time if the number
n_{1}
of the first player’s variables is fixed. However, it is unclear whether the algorithm runs in time polynomial in
n_{1}
or in the encoding size of the input.
In particular, each iteration (i.e., fixing a variable) introduces constraints with increasingly large coefficients, potentially causing superpolynomial growth in the encoding size.

Third, our computational complexity results depend on how the
k
-level problem is formulated.
Following Jeroslow
[
14
]
, in our formulation, each player selects an optimal solution whenever one exists.
If, for example, the second player’s optimum is unattainable, the corresponding first player’s decision is considered infeasible.
Another common formulation is based on
\epsilon
-optimal solution sets, where followers (i.e., the second through
k
-th players) are indifferent among feasible solutions whose suboptimality is below a prescribed threshold.
Under this setting, deciding attainability under conditions
(C1)
–
(C3)
becomes polynomial-time solvable.
A more comprehensive study of alternative formulations remains an interesting direction for future research.

Finally, on the positive side, our arguments extend naturally to multilevel mixed-binary LP.
A
k
-level mixed-binary LP can be transformed into a
(k+1)
-level LP by introducing an additional player who evaluates the fractionality of variables and linking constraints enforcing binary constraints
[
24
]
.
For example, this shows that the search problem for a
k

\textup{F}\Delta^{p}_{k+1}
.
Hardness follows by modifying the argument in Section
5.3.2
, together with the pure-binary instance of Jeroslow
[

]
.

7
Conclusions

In this paper, we have presented a comprehensive analysis of the computational complexity of multilevel LP, focusing on the fundamental problems of feasibility, existence of optimal solutions, and computation of optimal objective values. Our results provide a unified view of how these problems are situated within the polynomial hierarchy.

A central insight of our study is that the computational complexity of multilevel LP is highly sensitive to both the number of levels
k
and the structural properties of the model, such as the presence of linking constraints and unbounded variables.
In particular, we established that the feasibility problem is
\Sigma^{p}_{k-1}
-complete for all
k\geq 2
in the general case, while exhibiting polynomial-time solvability under restrictive assumptions when
k
is small.
The sharp transition in complexity (assuming the polynomial hierarchy does not collapse) between
k=4
and
k=5
in the absence of linking constraints and unbounded variables highlights a threshold phenomenon that, to the best of our knowledge, has not been previously identified in multilevel optimization.
We observed a similar phenomenon in the attainability decision problem for multilevel LP.

In addition, we showed that computing the optimal objective value is
\mathrm{F}\Delta^{p}_{k}
-complete for all
k\geq 2
, even under simplifying assumptions. This result suggests that, from a computational perspective, the search version of multilevel LP remains intractable across all
k\geq 2
, and does not admit the same types of tractability results observed for certain decision problems in restricted settings.

Beyond these complexity classifications, our results yield several structural implications.
Notably, they establish limitations on polynomial-time transformations between formulations with and without linking constraints, unless widely believed complexity-theoretic assumptions fail.
At the same time, they identify regimes (in particular, for sufficiently large
k
) where such transformations become possible for feasibility.
However, they do not preserve optimal objective values, as they concern only the associated decision problems.
These findings contribute to a more nuanced understanding of the modeling trade-offs inherent in multilevel LP.
