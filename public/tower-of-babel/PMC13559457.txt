A percutaneous sternal elevation technique using the Tian-Ping sternal lifter for Nuss repair of pectus excavatum
Authors: Hao-Nan Lin, Jun-Min Zhu, Zhi-Qiang Deng, Xiao-Han Jin, Yong Xiao, Xing-Bao Zhang, Zhong-Yi Zeng, Hao-Tian Du, Dong Tian, Feng Lin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The conventional Nuss procedure carries a risk of catastrophic injury during retrosternal tunnel creation. This study aimed to evaluate the feasibility, safety, and preliminary clinical outcomes of a novel percutaneous sternal elevation device, the Tian-Ping (Tian’s) sternal lifter, in assisting the Nuss procedure for pectus excavatum. This study included patients who underwent chest wall correction via the Nuss procedure assisted by the Tian-Ping (Tian’s) sternal lifter from July 2025 to October 2025. Patient demographics, operative time, intraoperative blood loss, complications, and postoperative pain scores were recorded. A total of 15 consecutive patients were included. All procedures were completed successfully without major intraoperative complications. Thoracoscopy showed retrosternal space enlargement, with estimated sternal elevation up to 6–8 cm. The median operative time was 51 minutes with a median intraoperative blood loss of 2 mL. The median postoperative hospital stay was 9 days. The median postoperative visual analog scale (VAS) pain score was 2. Postoperative pneumothorax occurred in two patients; no other postoperative complications were observed. The Tian-Ping (Tian’s) sternal lifter-assisted Nuss procedure is a safe and feasible technique for chest wall correction, with potential advantages in avoiding bone injury. We recommend this technique as an alternative for patients who value procedural safety and aesthetic satisfaction.

Surgical highlights

• We describe a percutaneous sternal elevation technique using the Tian-Ping (Tian’s) sternal lifter during the Nuss procedure. The percutaneous suspension elevates the sternum without direct skeletal traction, providing clear operative exposure while leaving only two small skin punctures.

What is conventional and what is novel/modified?

• The conventional Nuss procedure often requires sternal elevation techniques to improve retrosternal visualization. Current approaches include invasive methods and non-invasive suction devices, each with limitations such as skeletal injury risk or insufficient lifting strength.

• The modified technique is a percutaneous sternal elevation approach that circumvents these limitations by providing reliable lifting of the sternum with satisfactory safety and effectiveness.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• The percutaneous sternal elevation technique may reduce the risk of skeletal injury and facilitate safe retrosternal dissection during Nuss repair. We recommend considering this technique as a practical adjunct to the Nuss procedure, particularly for patients with fragile bones or cosmetic concerns.

Introduction

Pectus excavatum (PE) is one of the most common congenital chest wall deformities, accounting for approximately 90% of all such anomalies (1). It is characterized by anterior chest wall concavity that directly compresses mediastinal structures. This impairs cardiac function and exercise tolerance, with additional effects on psychological well-being due to abnormal appearance (2,3). Correction of the chest wall deformity is the primary goal of surgical intervention. The paradigm has evolved from traditional open surgery, such as the Ravitch technique, toward minimally invasive approaches (4).

The minimally invasive repair of PE was first introduced by Donald Nuss in 1987. This approach, now widely known as the Nuss procedure, has become the preferred treatment (5). The essential step of the procedure is to create a retrosternal tunnel. A pre-bent pectus bar is then inserted and rotated to elevate the depressed sternum, thereby reconstituting the chest wall (6). Although the Nuss procedure is performed under thoracoscopic guidance, the creation of the retrosternal tunnel remains the most hazardous step (7). This is because the concave deformity narrows the retrosternal space, bringing the heart and great vessels into close proximity to the sternum. Performing blunt dissection in this confined space may lead to severe complications, including pericardial perforation and aortic injury. Complication rates are significantly higher in adult patients and those with severe deformities (8). A safe and controllable operating space during tunnel dissection is accordingly critical to minimize procedural risks (9).

Sternal elevation techniques are vital adjuncts for expanding the retrosternal space during the Nuss procedure (10). Current techniques fall into two principal categories. Invasive techniques elevate the sternum through skeletal anchorage. The bone-hook method relies on direct traction on the sternal edge, whereas the crane system applies external traction through wires or screws (11,12). Non-invasive methods, such as the vacuum bell, elevate the sternum by applying negative pressure to the overlying skin and soft tissues (13). Both approaches elevate the depressed sternum before retrosternal tunnel dissection to improve exposure and reduce the risk of mediastinal injury (14). A quantitative study has reported a median 78% reduction in external pectus depth using crane techniques (15). However, existing sternal elevation techniques often fail to achieve both avoidance of iatrogenic bone injury and adequate sternal elevation. Invasive techniques, by concentrating mechanical stress on focal skeletal points through direct anchoring, carry an inherent risk of iatrogenic bone injury (16). Non-invasive approaches often fail to generate sufficient corrective force in patients with severe deformities (17).

A novel technique is required to optimize sternal elevation by enlarging the retrosternal space sufficiently without skeletal injury. The presternal soft tissue, devoid of critical neurovascular structures and covered by elastic skin, is ideally suited for this purpose. Therefore, we developed the percutaneous suspension technique utilizing the Tian-Ping (Tian’s) sternal lifter. The device lifts the anterior chest skin to indirectly widen the retrosternal space, circumventing direct skeletal manipulation (Figure 1). The name Tian-Ping, derived from the Chinese word for balance scale, reflects the symmetric and stable mechanical properties of the device. This technique has been routinely applied in surgery for anterior mediastinal masses in China, and we have successfully used it to perform thymectomy and superior vena cava replacement without sternotomy (18). This study reports our initial experience with this device for the Nuss procedure in 15 patients and evaluates its utility in optimizing the surgical field and perioperative safety. Preoperative preparations and requirements

This retrospective study included 15 consecutive patients who underwent chest wall correction via the Nuss procedure assisted by the Tian-Ping (Tian’s) sternal lifter. All procedures were performed between July 2025 and October 2025 at two institutions: West China Hospital of Sichuan University and Sichuan Science City Hospital. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (I) diagnosis of PE confirmed by chest computed tomography, with a Haller index of ≥3.25, or chest wall deformity below the diagnostic threshold for PE but with cosmetic concern; (II) patients were willing to undergo chest wall correction via the Nuss procedure assisted by the Tian-Ping (Tian’s) sternal lifter. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (I) chronic pain history or long-term analgesic medication to avoid confounding in postoperative pain assessment; (II) prior sternal surgery; (III) a mixed chest wall deformity combining PE and pectus carinatum.

Patient demographics, preoperative characteristics, intraoperative details, and postoperative outcomes were collected. Pain severity was assessed using the visual analog scale (VAS) (0: no pain; 1–3: mild pain; 4–6: moderate to severe pain; 7–9: very severe pain; 10: unbearable pain). Pain scores were recorded at 72 hours postoperatively. All procedures performed in this study were in accordance with the ethical standards of the institutional and/or national research committee(s) and with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. Publication of this article, accompanying images and the video was waived from patients’ consent by the Ethics Committees of West China Hospital, Sichuan University and Sichuan Science City Hospital due to the retrospective, non-interventional nature of the study and the use of deidentified patient data. All procedures were performed as part of routine clinical care, and no additional experimental interventions or risks to patients were involved.

Patient positioning and setup of the Tian-Ping (Tian’s) sternal lifter

The patient was positioned supine with the lower extremities abducted at 60–70°. General anesthesia was administered via single-lumen endotracheal intubation. The surgeon stood on the patient’s right side throughout the procedure. The suprasternal notch and xiphoid process were identified as surface landmarks preoperatively. Two 5-mm skin incisions were then made at these sites (Figure 2A). The midpoint of the line connecting these two points was designated as the central axis for the device base. The base of the Tian-Ping (Tian’s) sternal lifter was secured to the bilateral rails of the operating table, and the components were assembled to form a stable structure. The tunnel needles were inserted through the skin incisions, advanced subcutaneously over the periosteum from superior to inferior, and secured to the lifting fixator. The elevation system was then activated to lift the anterior chest wall. Under continuous thoracoscopic monitoring, the lifting height was adjusted until a satisfactory retrosternal working space was achieved (Figure 2B,2C). The technical principles and operative details of this percutaneous suspension technique have been reported in our previous work (18,19).

The insertion of pectus bar and retrosternal tunneling

Longitudinal incisions of approximately 2–3 cm were made bilaterally in the anterior axillary line, positioned at the level corresponding to the deepest point of the thoracic depression. With the sternum elevated and the heart displaced posteriorly, an introducer was inserted through the right-sided incision. The introducer was carefully advanced through the pleural cavity, maintaining close contact with the posterior sternal table. It was then maneuvered retrosternally using a gentle twisting motion rather than forceful pushing. This action bluntly dissected the anterior mediastinal soft tissue to create a tunnel, with the introducer finally emerging at the predetermined left intercostal space. A pre-bent pectus bar (TC4ELI titanium alloy; Johnson & Johnson, USA) was placed in the upside-down position, with its convex side toward the pericardium. The bar was then connected to the introducer tip via a plastic suction tube (Figure 2D). The introducer was then withdrawn smoothly, pulling the bar through the established retrosternal tunnel.

Pectus bar rotation and fixation

A rotator was then used to rotate the bar 180° once it was correctly positioned. The rotation was clockwise on the right and counterclockwise on the left. This brought the convex side to face anteriorly in the correct final position. This maneuver elevated the sternum and corrected the deformity. A stabilizer was threaded onto the bar on one side, typically the left, and advanced medially as far as possible. Pericostal fixation was performed using USP size 5 stainless steel wires with needles (ETHICON, Johnson & Johnson). The wires were passed around the two ribs on each side and securely tied to the stabilizer and to the bar ends, achieving multi-point fixation to prevent displacement and distribute forces evenly. The bar rested on two ribs on the right side, while on the left side, the stabilizer also rested on two ribs, ensuring stability and minimizing the risk of intercostal muscle stripping. A single bar was used in all patients, as the pre-elevation effectively facilitated the corrective process, enabling successful remodeling with a single bar.

Device removal and wound closure

The thoracoscope was removed after confirming adequate hemostasis and satisfactory bar positioning. The Tian-Ping (Tian’s) sternal lifter was then disassembled and withdrawn. After removal, the surgeon inspected both puncture sites and the surrounding soft tissue for skin tearing, bleeding, hematoma, ecchymosis, or excessive skin tension. Through the thoracoscopic entry point, the thoracic cavity was exsufflated via a chest drain connected to an underwater seal; the drain was then removed. The two percutaneous puncture sites were closed with sterile adhesive strips, leaving nearly invisible scars. The bilateral surgical incisions were closed in three layers with monofilament sutures (Figure 2E).

Postoperative considerations and tasks

All patients received identical patient-controlled intravenous analgesia (PCIA) for pain management. Postoperatively, the medical team evaluated the puncture sites and surgical incisions during daily ward rounds and follow-up visits for erythema, hematoma, infection, wound dehiscence, skin necrosis, or device-related skin erosion.

Tips and pearls

For patients with severe anterior chest wall depression (Haller index >6.0) or a rigid chest wall, the lifting force should be applied gradually under continuous thoracoscopic monitoring of the retrosternal space. The tunnel needle must remain tightly opposed to the sternal midline to maximize direct force transmission to the sternum. Furthermore, the patient’s back may be allowed to lift off the table, so that body weight provides counter-traction.

Discussion

A total of 15 patients underwent chest wall correction via the Nuss procedure assisted by the Tian-Ping (Tian’s) sternal lifter, including 12 males and 3 females. The median age was 16 years (range, 12–37 years), median body mass index (BMI) was 16.14 kg/m2 (range, 13.15–22.05 kg/m2), and median preoperative Haller index was 3.77 (range, 2.49–7.65). Thoracoscopic visualization demonstrated a distinct increase in the retrosternal space, with an estimated sternal elevation of up to 6–8 cm. All procedures were completed without intraoperative complications such as cardiac perforation or intercostal muscle rupture. Median operative time was 51 minutes (range, 35–80 minutes), and median intraoperative blood loss was 2 mL (range, 0–20 mL). The median postoperative hospital stay was 9 days (range, 6–11 days). Postoperative pain was assessed using the VAS, with a median score of 2 (range, 2–6). Postoperative pneumothorax occurred in two patients, both of whom recovered with conservative management. No pleural effusion, hemothorax, cardiac injury, arrhythmia, or bar-related complications were observed (Table 1).

In this study, we developed and assessed the Tian-Ping (Tian’s) sternal lifter in the Nuss procedure and demonstrated its clinical utility. The device provides a stable retrosternal workspace while potentially avoiding sternum injury. Its design may contribute to improved mechanical performance, thus yielding mild postoperative pain and satisfactory corrective outcomes. These observations support its potential value in enhancing procedural safety and early postoperative recovery in the Nuss procedure.

PE is a congenital chest wall deformity resulting from extracellular matrix disorganization and reduced biomechanical strength of the costal cartilages. This pathological process leads to progressive depression of the sternum, creating a funnel-shaped thoracic configuration (20). Surgical treatment aims to lift the sternum and secure it in position (21). The Nuss procedure has been widely adopted for PE in recent years. Compared with open approaches, it avoids costal cartilage resection, thereby reducing blood loss and surgical trauma while offering superior aesthetic outcomes (6). Nevertheless, it carries a risk of intraoperative injury to the heart and great vessels. This risk arises during retrosternal tunnel dissection, as the narrow anterior mediastinal space in PE limits visualization and leaves insufficient room for safe passage of the pectus bar (22,23). Although the incidence of cardiac or vascular injury is very low, such complications can be catastrophic (24).

Sternal elevation techniques serve as a key adjunct during the Nuss procedure to improve visualization and safety (14). By lifting the sternum prior to retrosternal dissection, it creates an adequate working space, enabling clear visualization of the retrosternal region. This also facilitates subsequent bar rotation with reduced resistance, thereby minimizing intercostal tissue trauma (25). Sternal elevation techniques comprise invasive and non-invasive methods. Retractors or bone hooks lift the sternum via lever forces or traction to widen the mediastinal space, but precise control of the applied force remains difficult with these manual techniques (10,26). The Crane system developed by Park and colleagues addresses this limitation by applying controlled vertical traction via an external device, offering more stable sternal elevation than manual retraction (27). As demonstrated by de Loos et al., even modest sternal elevation can substantially improve procedural safety in severe deformities (15). Despite these advances, all invasive techniques share a fundamental limitation rooted in skeletal biomechanics. Local strain exceeding 15,000 µε has been associated with microdamage and fracture in bony structures, meaning that externally applied forces can readily push skeletal stress beyond safe limits (28). The vacuum bell provides an effective, scarless option for selected young patients with flexible chest walls, by using negative pressure (typically −50 to −150 mbar) to elevate the sternum (29). However, its lifting efficiency is limited, particularly in patients with severe deformities or stiff chest walls, and the negative pressure may cause subcutaneous hematoma (30).

We developed the Tian-Ping (Tian’s) sternal lifter to assist the Nuss procedure in this context. The device introduces a novel percutaneous suspension. Specialized tunnel needles inserted through two small skin punctures lift the underlying soft tissue to enlarge the retrosternal space indirectly. By avoiding direct skeletal anchoring, this approach may reduce the risk of bone injury associated with invasive techniques. Human skin exhibits an average ultimate tensile strength of 277.4±94.8 kgf/cm2 and the presternal area contains no critical neurovascular structures (31). These anatomical features support the safety of the percutaneous suspension technique. Although percutaneous traction carries a theoretical risk of skin breakdown or excessive tension at the puncture sites, no such complications occurred in this series. In a previously reported case, the same technique was applied for nearly 6 hours during resection of a giant mediastinal teratoma, without skin-related complications over 18 months of follow-up (19). The technique also provides clear surgical field exposure, which has enabled us to recently demonstrate its feasibility in complex mediastinal procedures without sternal splitting (18). The integrated, portal-shaped symmetrical design enables rapid setup, typically around 5 minutes. Moreover, the percutaneous suspension provides balanced force distribution to preserve chest wall symmetry. Initial application in 15 patients suggested the safety and efficacy of the device. Notably, in the patient with the highest Haller index (7.65) in our series, the device provided 6–8 cm of retrosternal elevation, demonstrating its utility even in stiff deformities. All procedures were completed without cardiac perforation or intercostal muscle rupture, leaving only two 5-mm puncture sites at the sternal notch and subxiphoid natural depressions. These sites heal with nearly invisible scars and excellent cosmetic results. However, the Tian-Ping sternal lifter is a complementary, bone-sparing alternative rather than a universal replacement. Traditional crane techniques may provide stronger elevation forces for rigid chest walls.

Postoperative pain management is a major concern in the Nuss procedure. Pain after the procedure mainly originates from intercostal muscle dissection and pectus bar pressure (32). Current analgesic strategies for the Nuss procedure include thoracic epidural analgesia and intercostal nerve cryoablation to reduce opioid consumption and hospital stay (33). The percutaneous suspension avoids direct injury to sensitive tissues such as the periosteum and costal cartilages. Additionally, distributed force may better conform to the course of intercostal nerves along inferior rib margins, potentially reducing nerve compression. In this study, PCIA provided acceptable pain control. However, multimodal analgesia, including thoracic epidural analgesia and/or intercostal nerve cryoablation, may further improve pain control and shorten hospitalization in future studies. Of note, 2 of 15 patients (13.3%) developed minor postoperative pneumothorax. Both cases resolved rapidly with conservative management. By enhancing exposure during introducer passage, this technique may reduce pleural injury risk, though it cannot eliminate it entirely (34).

Pectus bar malposition or migration is the most common cause of failure following the Nuss procedure (35). Finite element analysis has shown that sternal displacement during correction generates significant stress on the ribs. Von Mises stress reaches up to 157.1×106 Pa at the posterior aspects of the fourth to sixth ribs (36). This stress may injure intercostal muscle attachments and thereby promote lateral bar migration. In theory, the Tian-Ping (Tian’s) sternal lifter addresses this issue by providing sustained, evenly distributed percutaneous traction. This enables elevation beyond the target level prior to bar insertion. By pre-releasing the elastic recoil of the chest wall, it may facilitate precise, low-tension bar placement. Although a single bar sufficed for all 15 patients in our initial cohort without any bar-related complications or recurrence, the bar placement strategy must be individualized. Multiple bars may be necessary for severe, long-segment, asymmetric, or rigid deformities. In the Nuss procedure, forces transmitted via ribs may displace the thoracic spine anteriorly, restricting lung volume (37). By dissipating corrective forces through soft tissues, the percutaneous suspension may mitigate spinal alignment changes, with potential benefits for pulmonary function.

There are several limitations in this study. First, as an initial report, it involves a small sample size, introducing potential selection bias. Prospective, multicenter, and large-sample randomized controlled trials are necessary to systematically evaluate the safety, efficacy, and applicability of this technique. Second, the short follow-up precludes assessment of long-term correction, recurrence, or quality of life. These outcomes will be reported once meaningful follow-up is achieved. Third, technique-specific osseous complications are rarely reported systematically in the existing literature, limiting direct safety comparisons among sternal elevation methods. This issue will be addressed in our future prospective studies. Finally, the lack of objective intraoperative measurements for sternal elevation and the reliance on qualitative assessments for skin tolerance represent limitations of this study. Future investigations should incorporate real-time intraoperative imaging, force sensors, and tissue perfusion monitors to rigorously quantify these parameters.

Conclusions

In conclusion, the Tian-Ping (Tian’s) sternal lifter is a safe and feasible adjunct for the Nuss procedure. Our initial experience demonstrates that it provides a stable retrosternal workspace, while avoiding bone injury and minimizing trauma. This technique is potentially suitable for patients with fragile bone structures and cosmetic concerns. Future studies with larger cohorts and objective intraoperative measurements are warranted to validate these promising results.
