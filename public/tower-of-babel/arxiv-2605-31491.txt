Constraining Conformal Correlators
Viktoriia Borovik, Claire de Korte, Nathan Meurrens, Dmitrii Pavlov

Abstract
We study the space of conformally covariant n n -point functions of spinning operators using methods from invariant theory, commutative algebra, and combinatorics. We show that the rational part of any such function can be expressed in terms of the basic building blocks introduced by Costa, Penedones, Poland, Rychkov, thereby providing a rigorous proof of a result that is widely used in the physics literature. We reformulate the problem of enumeration of n n -point structures in terms of counting lattice points in fractional matching polytopes, and compute these counts using vector partition functions, Hilbert functions, and Kostka numbers. We show that all algebraic relations between the building blocks follow from Gram constraints and compute the number of algebraically independent building blocks. For three-point functions, we derive closed counting formulas for arbitrary integer spins, both with and without Bose symmetry, and discuss a necessary and sufficient condition for the partial conservation operator to lift to a differential operator written in terms of the building blocks. We provide code that generates a basis of three-point structures satisfying these constraints for given values of spins and scaling dimensions.



1
Introduction

Conformal field theories (CFTs) are models of physical systems that are invariant under conformal transformations. Given a conformal field theory, its conformal correlators are correlation functions that encode the likelihood of interactions among particles as a function of their positions
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
Conformal symmetry requires correlators to transform covariantly under the relevant representation of the conformal group
[
38
, §III.C]
. Throughout this paper, we will be considering a specific subset of representations known to physicists as corresponding to conformal primaries
[
38
, §III.B]
. For bosonic operators, the relevant representations are tensor representations of the conformal group. Direct computation of these correlators can be extremely complicated. An alternative approach is to ‘bootstrap’ them, using the constraints imposed by conformal symmetry to restrict the form of a correlator and, in some cases, to determine it completely; see
[
42
]
for an introduction to conformal bootstrap. Since conformal transformations are nonlinear, deriving these constraints is nontrivial.
The embedding space formalism simplifies this dramatically, by realizing conformal transformations and their tensor representations in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
as restrictions to the Poincaré section of Lorentz transformations and their tensor representations in
\mathbb{R}^{1,d+1}
; for details we refer to
[
11
,
15
,
20
,
21
,
31
,
46
]
.

In this paper, we work primarily within the embedding space formalism. Using the constraints imposed by conformal symmetry, together with Bose symmetry and partial conservation, we characterize the space of allowed structures in bosonic conformal correlators.
We provide explicit procedures for computing these spaces for correlators of any number of operators with arbitrary fixed scaling dimensions and integer spins. Related problems have previously been studied, though from different perspectives and in more restricted settings; see, for example,
[
4
,
10
,
11
,
18
,
23
,
24
,
26
,
49
]
.

From the algebraic perspective, our starting point is the collection of
basic building blocks
introduced in
[
11
]
. These are rational functions defined as follows:

\displaystyle P_{ij}

\displaystyle=P_{i}\cdot P_{j},

(1)

\displaystyle H_{ij}

\displaystyle=-2\big[(Z_{i}\cdot Z_{j})(P_{i}\cdot P_{j})-(Z_{i}\cdot P_{j})(Z_{j}\cdot P_{i})\big],

\displaystyle V_{i,jk}

\displaystyle=\frac{(Z_{i}\cdot P_{j})(P_{i}\cdot P_{k})-(Z_{i}\cdot P_{k})(P_{i}\cdot P_{j})}{P_{j}\cdot P_{k}}.

Here
P_{i}
and
Z_{i}
are null embedding-space vectors representing position and polarisation respectively. In the physics literature, these basic building blocks are used as generators of conformally covariant structures, and it is reasoned that every bosonic conformal correlator can be expressed in terms of them. Mathematically, this means that the building blocks generate the field of rational invariants under the action of the Lorentz group, together with another group action that we call transversality. In Theorem
4.10
, we give a rigorous proof of this statement, which is standard in the conformal bootstrap literature but, to our knowledge, had not yet been established in this form.

Since the building blocks are not generally algebraically independent, it is natural to study the algebraic varieties they parametrize and their defining ideals.
This problem was recently investigated in
[
19
]
, where the authors studied these ideals as well as Gram constraints arising from the vectors
P_{i}
and
Z_{i}
.
In Section
6
, we take this as a point of departure, but with a different emphasis. We consider the building blocks
P_{ij}
,
H_{ij}
and
\mathcal{V}_{ij}:=V_{i,i+1j}
and show that any algebraic relation between them is implied by the Gram constraints satisfied by the inner products
P_{i}\cdot P_{j}
,
P_{i}\cdot Z_{j}
and
Z_{i}\cdot Z_{j}
.
We also show that any relation between the building blocks involving
V_{i,jk}
can be rewritten using only the subset of the blocks of the form
\mathcal{V}_{ij}
.
We then compute the number of building blocks
H_{ij}
and
\mathcal{V}_{ij}
that are algebraically independent over the field of rational functions in the cross-ratios in the blocks
P_{ij}
.

Once conformally covariant structures are expressed in terms of the building blocks (
1
) subject to the appropriate homogeneity conditions (
5
), one would like to count how many such structures exist for a set of prescribed spins. This counting problem appears frequently in the physics literature, especially for three- and four-point correlators, but explicit formulas are usually derived case by case or, as introduced in
[
26
]
, through Clebsch-Gordan decompositions. In Theorem
3.1
, we show that this problem admits a reformulation in terms of multigraded Hilbert functions, vector partition functions, and lattice-point counts in fractional matching polytopes. In Section
6
, we revisit this problem while accounting for algebraic dependencies among the building blocks, and compute the corresponding Hilbert function.

Finally, we give a detailed analysis of three-point correlation functions. In Propositions
7.1
and
7.3
, we derive closed-form formulas for the number of conformally covariant structures with arbitrary spins after imposing Bose symmetry. We also study the partial conservation operator in Section
8
. In Theorem
8.4
, we provide necessary and sufficient conditions for the action of a differential operator
\partial_{P_{i}}\cdot D_{Z_{i}}
on three-point functions to be captured by a differential operator
\mathcal{D}_{i,1}
acting on the numerator in terms of the building blocks, and construct this operator explicitly, reproducing the result of
[
49
]
. Moreover, we provide an algorithm to explicitly construct, for any choice of
s_{i}-t_{i}
, the differential operator
\mathcal{D}_{i,s_{i}-t_{i}}
that captures the action of
(\partial_{P_{i}}\cdot D_{Z_{i}})^{s_{i}-t_{i}}
in terms of the building blocks. We apply this to explicitly construct
\mathcal{D}_{i,2}
for
d=3
. Necessary and sufficient conditions for the existence of higher-order operators
(\partial_{P_{i}}\cdot D_{Z_{i}})^{s_{i}-t_{i}}
are the content of Conjectures
8.6
and
8.7
.

We also provide code that computes a basis of three-point structures, expressed in terms of the building blocks, satisfying all of the aforementioned constraints. This code, along with other auxiliary files for our paper, is available at
[
7
]
:

https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.20444515
.

Outline.
Our paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
we review the embedding space formalism and the necessary mathematical background. Section
3
develops the combinatorial and algebraic framework underlying the counting problem. In Section
4
we turn to invariant theory: we define the unipotent group action encoding transversality and prove that the basic building blocks generate the field of rational invariants. In Section
5
we revisit the general form of conformally covariant
n
-point structures from
[

and relate their enumeration to the multigraded counting problem studied earlier.

8
we study partial conservation and the associated differential operators on spaces of conformally covariant structures.

2.1
Embedding space formalism

Throughout this paper,
(\,\cdot\,)
between bold characters denotes the Euclidean scalar product, while
(\,\cdot\,)
between plain characters denotes the Lorentzian scalar product. We will be working in
d\geq 3
spatial dimensions. Bold vectors
\mathbf{x}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
are used for the spatial position of the
i
th particle, and plain vectors
P_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{1,d+1}
for their embedding-space counterparts, the latter transforming in the fundamental representation of the Lorentz group
{\rm O}(1,d+1)
. Restricting to the
Poincaré section
, defined as

P_{i}=(P_{i}^{0},\mathbf{P}_{i},P_{i}^{d+1})=\left(\tfrac{1}{2}[1+\mathbf{x}_{i}\cdot\mathbf{x}_{i}],\,\mathbf{x}_{i},\,\tfrac{1}{2}[1-\mathbf{x}_{i}\cdot\mathbf{x}_{i}]\right),

(2)

induces the fundamental action of the conformal group
\mathrm{Conf}(d)
on
\mathbf{x}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. See
[
15
]
for the original construction, and
[
14
, Sections 4.1–4.2]
for an introduction to the conformal group and its representations. As explained in
[
11
,
20
,
21
,
31
,
46
]
, this method of realizing conformal transformations as restrictions of Lorentz transformations extends beyond the fundamental representations of
\mathrm{Conf}(d)
and
{\rm O}(1,d+1)
to their tensor representations.
Thus, bosonic conformal correlators can be realized as restrictions to the Poincaré section (
2
) of embedding-space correlators. This is useful for the conformal bootstrap because, in the embedding space, conformal covariance lifts to Lorentz covariance together with suitable homogeneity, symmetry, tracelessness and transversality conditions (
3
)–(
6
); see
[
21
]
.

In
[
11
, p. 8–13]
, the embedding space formalism is simplified by using index-free notation, based on the correspondence between symmetric tensors of order
s
and homogeneous polynomials of degree
s
.
Concretely, embedding-space correlators are contracted with
polarisation vectors

Z_{i}\in\mathbb{C}^{1,d+1}
, one for each particle, that transform under the fundamental representation of
{\rm O}(1,d+1)
. Contracting a symmetric set of
s_{i}
indices of the correlator with
s_{i}
copies of
Z_{i}
turns the correlator into a polynomial of degree
s_{i}
in the
m=d+2
entries of
Z_{i}
; contracting all indices yields a Lorentz-invariant polynomial in the entries of the polarisation vectors. Following
[
11
]
, symmetric traceless tensors are encoded by imposing
Z_{i}\cdot Z_{i}=0
, whilst transversality is encoded by additionally imposing
P_{i}\cdot Z_{i}=0
and invariance under

Z_{i}\mapsto Z_{i}+\alpha_{i}P_{i}.

Upon restriction to the Poincaré section, one recovers the polarisation
vectors of the conformal correlator, namely the vectors

\mathbf{z}_{i}\in\mathbb{C}^{d}
satisfying
\mathbf{z}_{i}\cdot\mathbf{z}_{i}=0
. This is done by
setting

Z_{i}=(Z_{i}^{0},\mathbf{Z}_{i},Z_{i}^{d+1})=(\mathbf{x}_{i}\cdot\mathbf{z}_{i},\mathbf{z}_{i},-\mathbf{x}_{i}\cdot\mathbf{z}_{i}).

On this section one automatically has

P_{i}\cdot P_{i}=P_{i}\cdot Z_{i}=Z_{i}\cdot Z_{i}=0
.
Modulo the projective rescaling of
P_{i}
and the gauge redundancy under

Z_{i}\mapsto Z_{i}+\alpha_{i}P_{i}
, the restriction from the space of null vectors
P_{i}
and
Z_{i}
satisfying
P_{i}\cdot Z_{i}=0
to the Poincaré section is one-to-one.

An
n
-point conformal correlator
(or an
n
-point function
) depends on
n
position vectors

\mathbf{x}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
for
i=1,\ldots,n
. In the index-free
embedding space formalism, bosonic conformal correlators are represented by functions

G_{\mathbf{s},\mathbf{\Delta}}(P_{1},Z_{1},\ldots,P_{n},Z_{n}),

where, as introduced above,
P_{i}
and
Z_{i}
are the embedding-space vectors representing the position and polarisation of the
i
th particle. The tensor representation of
{\rm Conf}(d)
that these functions correspond to is determined by their spins

\mathbf{s}=(s_{1},\ldots,s_{n})\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{n}
and scaling dimensions

\mathbf{\Delta}=(\Delta_{1},\ldots,\Delta_{n})\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
. Below we
summarise the consequences of the discussion above, listing the conditions satisfied by functions
G_{\mathbf{s},\mathbf{\Delta}}(P_{1},Z_{1},\ldots,P_{n},Z_{n})
:

1.

Lorentz invariance.
For all
\Lambda\in{\rm O}(1,d+1)
, we have

G_{\mathbf{s},\mathbf{\Delta}}(\Lambda P_{1},\Lambda Z_{1},\ldots,\Lambda P_{n},\Lambda Z_{n})=G_{\mathbf{s},\mathbf{\Delta}}(P_{1},Z_{1},\ldots,P_{n},Z_{n}).

(3)

2.

Transversality.
Equivalently, gauge invariance under

Z_{i}\mapsto Z_{i}+\alpha_{i}P_{i}
:

G_{\mathbf{s},\mathbf{\Delta}}(P_{1},Z_{1}+\alpha_{1}P_{1},\ldots,P_{n},Z_{n}+\alpha_{n}P_{n})=G_{\mathbf{s},\mathbf{\Delta}}(P_{1},Z_{1},\ldots,P_{n},Z_{n}).

(4)

3.

Polynomiality and homogeneity.
The correlator is polynomial
in the entries of
Z_{i}
vectors and homogeneous of degree
-\Delta_{i}
in
P_{i}
and degree

s_{i}
in
Z_{i}
, in other words,

G_{\mathbf{s},\mathbf{\Delta}}(\lambda_{1}P_{1},\mu_{1}Z_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{n}P_{n},\mu_{n}Z_{n})=\left[\prod_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}^{-\Delta_{i}}\mu_{i}^{s_{i}}\right]G_{\mathbf{s},\mathbf{\Delta}}(P_{1},Z_{1},\ldots,P_{n},Z_{n}).

(5)

4.

Null-cone and polarisation constraints.
The correlator is
considered on the locus

P_{i}\cdot P_{i}=P_{i}\cdot Z_{i}=Z_{i}\cdot Z_{i}=0,\hskip 20.00003pti=1,\ldots,n.

(6)

Here
P_{i}^{2}=0
is the
null-cone condition and
Z_{i}^{2}=0

encodes tracelessness.

Certain conformal correlators satisfy further conditions beyond conformal
covariance. In standard quantum field theory (QFT) derivations,
these conditions, as the ones above, are built into the formalism and are therefore automatically satisfied.
In the bootstrap approach, by contrast, they become additional constraints on the correlators.
Two important examples are
Bose symmetry
and

partial conservation
, discussed in Sections
7

and
8
, respectively.

Bose symmetry applies to correlators involving identical bosonic particles. If
the particles
i
and
j
are identical, the correlator must be
invariant under exchanging
(P_{i},Z_{i})
and
(P_{j},Z_{j})
:

\begin{split}&G_{\mathbf{s},\mathbf{\Delta}}(P_{1},Z_{1},\ldots,P_{i},Z_{i},\ldots,P_{j},Z_{j},\ldots,P_{n},Z_{n})\\
&\qquad=G_{\mathbf{s},\mathbf{\Delta}}(P_{1},Z_{1},\ldots,P_{j},Z_{j},\ldots,P_{i},Z_{i},\ldots,P_{n},Z_{n}).\end{split}

(7)

Partial conservation applies if, for some
i
, one has
s_{i}\geq 1
and

\Delta_{i}=d-1+t_{i}
, where
t_{i}\in\{0,\ldots,s_{i}-1\}
denotes the
depth

of the
i
th particle. Then, by
[
17
]
,
G_{\mathbf{s},\mathbf{\Delta}}

must satisfy

\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial P_{i}}\cdot D_{Z_{i}}\right)^{s_{i}-t_{i}}G_{\mathbf{s},\mathbf{\Delta}}(P_{1},Z_{1},\ldots,P_{n},Z_{n})=0,

(8)

where
D_{Z}
denotes the
Thomas–Todorov differential operator
defined in
[
16
, 1.30a]
:

D_{Z}=\left(\frac{d}{2}-1+Z\cdot\frac{\partial}{\partial Z}\right)\frac{\partial}{\partial Z}-\frac{1}{2}Z\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial Z\cdot\partial Z}.

(9)

Here
\frac{\partial}{\partial Z}
denotes the gradient with respect to the vector
Z
, so that contractions such as

Z\cdot\frac{\partial}{\partial Z}
are taken using the Lorentzian
metric. The equation (
8
) is understood on the constraint locus

P_{i}^{2}=P_{i}\cdot Z_{i}=Z_{i}^{2}=0
, equivalently modulo the ideal generated by these
constraints.

Before moving on, we make a brief comment on parity. Functions that are invariant under
{\rm O}(1,d+1)
are called parity even, whilst those that are only invariant under the subgroup
\mathrm{SO}(1,d+1)
are called parity odd. By requiring our correlators to be invariant under
{\rm O}(1,d+1)
, we have in particular imposed them to be parity even. In practise, parity odd terms can be introduced as contractions of embedding-space vectors
P_{i}
and
Z_{i}
with the
(d+2)
-dimensional Levi-Civita tensor. Here we will focus on parity even structures, but our results can easily be adapted to account for parity odd structures using
[
11
, Section 4.2.3]
.

2.2
Mathematical background

Having outlined the embedding space formalism, we recall the main
mathematical notions used in this paper. For more details, see
[
12
,
33
,
34
,
41
]
.

Let
\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
and

R=\mathbb{K}[\mathbf{x}]=\mathbb{K}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
be the polynomial ring in
n
variables over a field
\mathbb{K}
. A
multigrading
of
R

by an abelian group
A
is a semigroup homomorphism

\deg:\mathbb{N}^{n}\to A,

which assigns to each monomial

\mathbf{x}^{\mathbf{u}}=x_{1}^{u_{1}}\cdots x_{n}^{u_{n}}
the multidegree

\deg(\mathbf{u})
.
A polynomial is
homogeneous
of multidegree
\mathbf{a}\in A
if all
its monomials have degree
\mathbf{a}
. The grading implies:

R=\bigoplus_{\mathbf{a}\in A}R_{\mathbf{a}},\hskip 20.00003ptR_{\mathbf{a}}R_{\mathbf{b}}\subseteq R_{\mathbf{a}+\mathbf{b}},

(10)

where
R_{\mathbf{a}}
is the space of homogeneous polynomials of
degree
\mathbf{a}
.
In this paper,
A=\mathbb{Z}^{m}
for some
m
, and the multigrading is encoded by an integer matrix
B\in\mathbb{Z}^{m\times n}
. If
{\bf u}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{n}
is the exponent vector of a monomial, then its multidegree is

\deg({\bf x^{u}})=B\cdot{\bf u}
.
For a multigraded ring
R
, the
multigraded Hilbert function

and
Hilbert series
are defined respectively as

h_{R}:\mathbb{Z}^{m}\to\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0},\hskip 10.00002pth_{R}(\mathbf{a})=\dim_{\mathbb{K}}R_{\mathbf{a}},\hskip 20.00003pt\text{and}\hskip 20.00003pt{\rm HS}_{R}(\mathbf{t})=\sum_{\mathbf{a}\in\mathbb{Z}^{m}}h_{R}(\mathbf{a})\mathbf{t}^{\mathbf{a}}.

(11)

In our case,
{\rm HS}_{R}(\mathbf{t})
will always be a formal power series, which we compute via

[
34
, Lem. 8.16]
.

We also use basic notions from algebraic geometry. Let
\mathbb{K}
be an
algebraically closed field of characteristic zero. An
affine variety
in

\mathbb{K}^{n}
is the common zero set of finitely many polynomials in

R=\mathbb{K}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
, while a
projective variety
in

\mathbb{P}_{\mathbb{K}}^{n}
is the common zero set of finitely many
homogeneous polynomials in
\mathbb{K}[x_{0},\ldots,x_{n}]
. A variety is

irreducible
if it is not the union of two proper subvarieties.
Polynomials vanishing on a variety form an

ideal

I\subseteq R
, which is a subset of a ring
R
, closed under addition and
under multiplication by elements of
R
. The ideal
I
is
prime
if
ab\in I

implies
a\in I
or
b\in I
. Any algebraic variety
X
is completely
determined by its vanishing ideal
\mathcal{I}(X)
, and irreducible varieties correspond to prime ideals.
Equivalently, an affine variety
X
defined by the ideal
\mathcal{I}(X)
can be identified with the
spectrum

\mathrm{Spec}(\mathcal{R})
(that is, the set of all prime ideals) of the
quotient ring

\mathcal{R}:=\mathbb{K}[\mathbf{x}]/\mathcal{I}(X)
, whose elements are given by cosets of the form
a+\mathcal{I}(X)
with
a\in R=\mathbb{K}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]
.

The spaces
\mathbb{K}^{n}
and
\mathbb{P}^{n}_{\mathbb{K}}
can be endowed with the
Zariski topology
, in which closed sets
are exactly algebraic varieties. This induces the Zariski topology on every subvariety in
\mathbb{K}^{n}
and
\mathbb{P}^{n}_{\mathbb{K}}
. The
Zariski closure
of a subset in
\mathbb{K}^{n}
and
\mathbb{P}^{n}_{\mathbb{K}}
is then the
smallest variety containing it.

Remark 2.1
.

In this paper, algebraic varieties will be described parametrically. We then pass to quotient rings as follows: if functions
f_{1}({\bf x}),\ldots,f_{k}({\bf x})
define a map to
\mathbb{\mathbb{K}}^{k}
, we introduce formal variables
y_{1},\ldots,y_{k}
and quotient
\mathbb{K}[y_{1},\ldots,y_{k}]
by the kernel of the homomorphism

\mathbb{K}[y_{1},\ldots,y_{k}]\rightarrow\mathbb{K}[\mathbf{x}],\hskip 20.00003pty_{i}\mapsto f_{i}(\mathbf{x}).

This quotient is the
coordinate ring
of the Zariski closure of the parametrized image. In our setup the role of the
y
variables is played by the building blocks
P_{ij}
,
H_{ij}
, and
V_{i,jk}
, and the role of the
x
variables is taken by the entries of the vectors
P_{i}
and
Z_{i}
. We will sometimes treat
P_{ij}
,
H_{ij}
, and
V_{i,jk}
as formal variables, which means that we ignore this quotient operation.

In this work we are interested in the number of
algebraically independent
conformally covariant
n
-point structures in the following sense. Elements

f_{1},\ldots,f_{n}
of a commutative
\mathbb{K}
-algebra are

if there exists a

p

.

Definition 2.2

(Transcendence degree)
.

Let
\mathbb{K}\subset\mathbb{L}
be a field extension.
A maximal algebraically
independent subset of
\mathbb{L}
over
\mathbb{K}
is a

transcendence basis
, and its cardinality is the

, denoted

\mathrm{trdeg}_{\mathbb{K}}\mathbb{L}
. This number does not depend on the choice of basis.

Since the
building blocks of
[
11
]
are invariants of group
actions, we recall the relevant invariant theory terminology. Here, we will be interested in actions of

, that is, groups that themselves carry the structure of an algebraic variety, on algebraic varieties.
A left action of a group
G
on a set
S

is a map
G\times S\to S
, written
(g,x)\mapsto g\cdot x
, such that

e\cdot x=x
and

. The action is

if only the identity acts

, and

if only the identity fixes any point.

In Section
3
we relate constraints on
conformal correlators to lattice-point counting of polytopes, which is one of the central problems in discrete geometry. A

polytope
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
is the convex hull of finitely many points,
equivalently a bounded intersection of finitely many half-spaces

[
50
, Sec. 1.1]
. A

is a point with integer
coordinates. If
P\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a full-dimensional lattice polytope,
then its
Ehrhart polynomial
counts lattice points in

:

L(P,t)=\#(tP\cap\mathbb{Z}^{n}),\hskip 10.00002ptt\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}.

(12)

Ehrhart’s theorem states that this function is indeed a polynomial in
t

[
34
, Theorem 12.2]
.

3
Counting monomials

This section is dedicated purely to commutative algebra and combinatorics. In Section
5
its results will be related to counting conformally covariant

n
-point structures. We discuss the problem from three perspectives: algebraic (
3.1
), geometric (
3.2
), and combinatorial (
3.3
).

Let
\mathbb{K}
be a field of characteristic zero, and denote
[n]\coloneqq\{1,2,\ldots,n\}
.
We fix a vector of non-negative integers
{\bf s}\coloneqq(s_{1},\ldots,s_{n})
and consider

n(n-2)+\binom{n}{2}
variables

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}_{ij}\hskip 10.00002pt

\displaystyle\text{for }i\in[n]\text{ and }j\in[n]\setminus\{i,i+1\},

\displaystyle H_{ij}\hskip 10.00002pt

\displaystyle\text{for }1\leq i<j\leq n.

We wish to count the number
N({\bf s})
of polynomials

Q_{\bf s}(\mathcal{V}_{ij},H_{ij})
with coefficients in
\mathbb{K}

such that

Q_{\bf s}(\alpha_{i}\mathcal{V}_{ij},\alpha_{i}\alpha_{j}H_{ij})=\alpha_{1}^{s_{1}}\alpha_{2}^{s_{2}}\cdots\alpha_{n}^{s_{n}}\cdot Q_{\bf s}(\mathcal{V}_{ij},H_{ij})\hskip 10.00002pt\forall\,\alpha\in\mathbb{K}^{n}.

(13)

We begin by stating the main outcome of this section. Subsections
3.1
–
3.3
present a detailed explanation of the two theorems below and the notions appearing in their statements.

Theorem 3.1
.

Consider polynomial rings

Q_{n}\coloneqq\mathbb{K}[\mathcal{V}_{ij},H_{ij}]
and
R_{n}\coloneqq\mathbb{K}[H_{ij}]

endowed with
\mathbb{Z}^{n}
‑gradings determined by integer matrices
B
(

3.1
An algebraic perspective: multivariate Hilbert functions

The generating function appearing in point (3) of Theorem
3.1

has a direct algebraic interpretation as the Hilbert series (
11
) of a multigraded polynomial ring.
Indeed, formula (
13
) means that the polynomials

Q_{\bf s}(\mathcal{V}_{ij},H_{ij})
are
(s_{1},\ldots,s_{n})
‑homogeneous with respect to the

\mathbb{Z}^{n}
‑grading on the polynomial ring

Q_{n}=\mathbb{K}[\mathcal{V}_{ij},H_{ij}],

given by

\deg(\mathcal{V}_{ij})={\bf e}_{i},\hskip 20.00003pt\deg(H_{ij})={\bf e}_{i}+{\bf e}_{j}.

(15)

Here,
{\bf e}_{i}
denotes the
i
th standard basis vector of
\mathbb{Z}^{n}
.
We encode this multigrading in an

n\times\bigl(n(n-2)+\binom{n}{2}\bigr)
integer matrix
B
,
whose columns are indexed by the ring generators
\mathcal{V}_{ij}
and
H_{ij}
and are given by
the multidegree vectors in (
15
):

B=(b_{k,*}),\hskip 10.00002pt\text{where }\;b_{k,\mathcal{V}_{ij}}=\begin{cases}1,&k=i,\\
0,&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}\hskip 10.00002ptb_{k,H_{ij}}=\begin{cases}1,&k=i\text{ or }k=j,\\
0,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

(16)

For a fixed integer vector
{\bf s}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{n}
, the set of homogeneous polynomials of multidegree
{\bf s}

is a finite‑dimensional
\mathbb{K}
‑vector space, which we denote by
(Q_{n})_{\bf s}
. We define

N({\bf s})\coloneqq\dim_{\mathbb{K}}(Q_{n})_{\bf s}.

By definition,
N({\bf s})
is the value of the Hilbert function
h_{Q_{n}}({\bf s})
(
11
) of the graded ring
Q_{n}
.

Lemma 3.3

(
[
34
, Lem. 8.16]
)
.

The Hilbert series
\sum h_{Q_{n}}({\bf s})\,{\bf t}^{\bf s}
of the ring
Q_{n}
is given by

\dfrac{1}{\prod\limits_{i=1}^{n}\,\prod\limits_{j\in[n]\setminus\{i,i+1\}}(1-\mathbf{t}^{\deg\mathcal{V}_{ij}})\prod\limits_{1\leq i<j\leq n}(1-\mathbf{t}^{\deg H_{ij}})}=\frac{1}{\prod\limits_{i=1}^{n}(1-t_{i})^{n-2}\prod\limits_{1\leq i<j\leq n}(1-t_{i}t_{j})}.

(17)

In particular,
h_{Q_{n}}({\bf s})
is the coefficient of

t_{1}^{s_{1}}\cdots t_{n}^{s_{n}}
in its Taylor expansion.

Remark 3.4
.

The factors
(1-t_{i})^{-(n-2)}
in the Hilbert series
{\rm HS}_{Q_{n}}({\bf t})=\sum h_{Q_{n}}({\bf s})\,{\bf t}^{\bf s}
(
17
) can be expanded using the following binomial series

\frac{1}{(1-t)^{n-2}}=\sum_{k\geq 0}\binom{n-2+k-1}{k}t^{k}.

As a result, their contribution to the coefficient of
t_{1}^{s_{1}}\cdots t_{n}^{s_{n}}
is entirely
encoded by explicit binomial coefficients. Indeed, when extracting the coefficient of the monomial
t_{1}^{s_{1}}\cdots t_{n}^{s_{n}}
, the contribution of the
variables
\mathcal{V}_{ij}
amounts to summing over all decompositions

s_{i}=b_{i}+k_{i}
with
k_{i}\geq 0
, weighted by the binomial coefficient

\binom{n-3+k_{i}}{k_{i}}
. Hence, in order to obtain closed‑form expressions,
it suffices to analyse the generating function associated with the variables
H_{ij}
alone,
namely the Hilbert series of the multigraded ring
R_{n}=\mathbb{K}[H_{ij}]
:

{\rm HS}_{R_{n}}({\bf t})=\sum h_{R_{n}}({\bf b})\,{\bf t}^{\bf b}=\frac{1}{\prod\limits_{1\leq i<j\leq n}(1-\mathbf{t}^{\deg H_{ij}})}=\frac{1}{\prod\limits_{1\leq i<j\leq n}(1-t_{i}t_{j})}.

(18)

The
\mathbb{Z}^{n}
‑grading on the ring

R_{n}=\mathbb{K}[H_{ij}]

is induced from the grading on
Q_{n}
given in (
15
) by restriction.
It is therefore determined by an
n\times\binom{n}{2}
submatrix
A
of the matrix
B
(
16
), whose columns are indexed by the variables
H_{ij}
and are given by their degrees:

A=(a_{k,*}),\hskip 10.00002pt\text{where }\;a_{k,H_{ij}}=\begin{cases}1,&k=i\text{ or }k=j,\\
0,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

(19)

We conclude this subsection with a motivating example.

Example 3.5

(
n=3
)
.

Consider the polynomial ring

Q_{3}=\mathbb{K}[\mathcal{V}_{13},\mathcal{V}_{21},\mathcal{V}_{32},H_{12},H_{23},H_{13}],

equipped with the
\mathbb{Z}^{3}
‑grading defined by

\displaystyle\deg(\mathcal{V}_{13})=(1,0,0),

\displaystyle\hskip 20.00003pt\deg(H_{12})=(1,1,0),

\displaystyle\deg(\mathcal{V}_{21})=(0,1,0),

\displaystyle\hskip 20.00003pt\deg(H_{23})=(0,1,1),

\displaystyle\deg(\mathcal{V}_{32})=(0,0,1),

\displaystyle\hskip 20.00003pt\deg(H_{13})=(1,0,1).

For
{\bf s}=(s_{1},s_{2},s_{3})

of homogeneous polynomials of multidegree

is finitedimensional. A basis is given by monomials

The Hilbert function

counts non-negative integer solutions of (

defined by (

3.2
A geometric perspective: lattice points in polytopes

Example
3.5
suggests a geometric reformulation of the counting problem above.
For general
n
, the Hilbert function
h_{Q_{n}}({\bf s})
can be interpreted as the number of
lattice points in a family of convex polytopes depending on integer non-negative parameters

{\bf s}=(s_{1},\ldots,s_{n})\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{n}
.

Definition 3.6
.

For
{\bf s}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{n}
, we define the
n
‑point conformal polytope

\mathcal{C}_{n}({\bf s})
to be the set

\mathcal{C}_{n}({\bf s})\coloneqq\{({\bf\hat{y}},{\bf x})\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{n(n-3)+{n\choose 2}}:\;\hat{B}\,({\bf\hat{y}}\mid{\bf x})\leq{\bf s}\},

(21)

where
\hat{B}
is the submatrix of
B
defined in (
16
) obtained by deleting
n
columns corresponding to some
\mathcal{V}_{1j_{1}},\ldots,\mathcal{V}_{nj_{n}}
.
Equivalently,

\mathcal{C}_{n}({\bf s})
is the projection of the solution set to
B\,({\bf y}\mid{\bf x})={\bf s}
, where
({\bf y},{\bf x})
is a point in
\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{n(n-2)+{n\choose 2}}
,
onto the
n(n-3)+{n\choose 2}
many coordinates
({\bf\hat{y}},{\bf x})
.

Polytopes of this type have appeared previously in the combinatorial literature,
where they are known as
fractional
{\bf s}
‑matching polytopes
. Let
G=(V,E)
be a finite undirected graph. The ordinary
matching polytope
associated with a graph
G
is the convex hull of incidence vectors of
matchings
, that is, of subsets of pairwise vertex-disjoint edges. Equivalently, a matching is encoded by a vector
x\in\{0,1\}^{|E|}
satisfying the following vertex constraints:

\sum_{e\in E_{v}}x_{e}\leq 1\hskip 20.00003pt\text{for all }v\in V.

Conformal polytopes
\mathcal{C}_{n}({\bf s})
introduced above have a similar form. We now elaborate on this.

A
fractional matching
is encoded by a vector

x\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{|E|}
satisfying the following inequalities

\sum_{e\in E_{v}}x_{e}\leq 1\hskip 20.00003pt\forall v\in V,

where
E_{v}
denotes the set of edges incident to
v
.
More generally, given a vertex weight function
{\bf s}:V\to\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
, a

fractional
{\bf s}
‑matching
is a vector
x\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{|E|}
such that

\sum_{e\in E_{v}}x_{e}\leq{\bf s}(v)\hskip 20.00003pt\forall v\in V.

Definition 3.7
.

A
fractional
{\bf s}
-matching polytope

{\rm FM}_{\bf s}(G)
is a convex polytope given by the convex hull of the fractional
{\bf s}
‑matchings of the graph
G
, that is,

{\rm FM}_{\bf s}(G)\coloneqq\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{|E|}:\forall v\in V\;\sum_{e\in E_{v}}x_{e}\leq{\bf s}(v)\right\}.

(22)

These polytopes are bounded rational polytopes with half-integer vertices, see, for example,
[
3
]
for details.
A
fractional perfect
{\bf s}
‑matching polytope
is obtained from a fractional
{\bf s}
‑matching polytope by replacing all inequalities by equalities, we use the notation from
[
5
]
:

\mathcal{P}(G,{\bf s})\coloneqq\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{|E|}:\forall v\in V\;\sum_{e\in E_{v}}x_{e}={\bf s}(v)\right\}.

(23)

Remark 3.8
.

The three‑point conformal polytope
\mathcal{C}_{3}(s_{1},s_{2},s_{3})
(
20
) coincides with the fractional
{\bf s}
‑matching
polytope of the complete graph
K_{3}
.
More generally, the conformal polytope
\mathcal{C}_{n}({\bf s})
can be interpreted as the fractional

{\bf\hat{s}}
‑matching polytope of the augmented graph
K_{n}^{*}
.
The graph
K_{n}^{*}
is obtained from the complete graph
K_{n}
by attaching
n-3
pending edges to
each vertex. It has
n+n(n-3)
vertices and
n(n-3)+\binom{n}{2}
edges.
The vertex weight function
{\bf\hat{s}}
assigns weight
s_{i}
to the original vertex
v_{i}
of

K_{n}
and a sufficiently large integer to each of the new degree‑one vertices, so that the
corresponding inequalities in the hyperplane description of the polytope

{\rm FM}_{\bf\hat{s}}(K_{n}^{*})
become redundant. See Example
3.14
for details.

We now illustrate the above discussion in the simplest non‑trivial case for
n=3
.
The following closed‑form formula was stated in
[
11
, eq. 4.20]
. We give a proof for completeness.

Proposition 3.9
.

Let
{\bf s}=(s_{1},s_{2},s_{3})
with
s_{1}\leq s_{2}\leq s_{3}
, and set

p=\max(0,\,s_{1}+s_{2}-s_{3}).

Then the Hilbert function value

h_{Q_{3}}({\bf s})=\bigl|{\cal C}_{3}({\bf s})\cap\mathbb{Z}^{3}\bigr|=\bigl|{\rm FM}_{\bf s}(K_{3})\cap\mathbb{Z}^{3}\bigr|

is given by

N(s_{1},s_{2},s_{3})=\frac{(s_{1}+1)(s_{1}+2)(3s_{2}-s_{1}+3)}{6}-\frac{p(p+2)(2p+5)}{24}-\frac{1-(-1)^{p}}{16}.

(24)

Proof.

We count lattice points in the convex polytope defined by

\bigl\{(x,y,z)\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{3}\mid y+z\leq s_{1},\;x+z\leq s_{2},\;x+y\leq s_{3}\bigr\}.

Fix
z
. Ignoring the constraint
x+y\leq s_{3}
, the remaining inequalities define a rectangle

[0,s_{2}-z]\times[0,s_{1}-z]
with
(s_{2}-z+1)(s_{1}-z+1)
lattice points.
Summing over
z
yields

N_{0}=\sum_{z=0}^{s_{1}}(s_{2}-z+1)(s_{1}-z+1)=\frac{(s_{1}+1)(s_{1}+2)(3s_{2}-s_{1}+3)}{6}.

If
s_{1}+s_{2}\leq s_{3}
, the inequality
x+y\leq s_{3}
is automatically satisfied and
N_{0}
is the
desired count. Otherwise, the line
x+y=s_{3}
cuts off a triangular region from each rectangle.
Setting
p=\max(0,s_{1}+s_{2}-s_{3})
, the number of lattice points to be subtracted is

\sum_{z=0}^{\lfloor(p-1)/2\rfloor}\frac{(p-2z)(p-2z+1)}{2}=\frac{p(p+2)(2p+5)}{24}+\frac{1-(-1)^{p}}{16}.

Subtracting this correction from
N_{0}
gives (
24
) and completes the proof.
∎

3.3
A combinatorial perspective: Kostka numbers

We now move on to combinatorics. We start by introducing necessary combinatorial objects.
A
partition

\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\ldots)
is a weakly decreasing sequence of non‑negative integers with finite sum
|\lambda|=\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}
.
The
Young tableau
of
\lambda
is a left‑justified array of boxes with
\lambda_{i}
boxes
in the
i
th row. A
semistandard Young tableau
of shape
\lambda
is a filling of the boxes of the Young
tableau of
\lambda
with positive integers such that the entries are weakly increasing along
each row and strictly increasing down each column.
The
weight
of a tableau is the vector recording the number of occurrences of each integer.
The
conjugate partition

\lambda^{\prime}
is obtained by transposing the Young tableau.
We say that a partition
\lambda
is
even
if all entries
\lambda_{i}
are even.

\lambda=(4,2,2)
\lambda^{\prime}=(3,3,1,1)

Figure 1:
\lambda
is even, while its conjugate
\lambda^{\prime}
is not.

Schur polynomials
are certain symmetric polynomials indexed by partitions.
For a partition
\lambda
, the Schur polynomial
s_{\lambda}
can be written as a sum
of monomial symmetric functions:

s_{\lambda}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})=\sum\limits_{\mu}K_{\lambda\mu}\,m_{\mu}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}),\hskip 10.00002ptm_{\mu}=\sum_{\sigma\in S_{n}}x_{1}^{\sigma(\mu_{1})}\cdots x_{n}^{\sigma(\mu_{n})},

(26)

where
K_{\lambda\mu}
are the
Kostka numbers
counting semistandard Young tableaux of shape
\lambda

and weight
\mu
.
The relevance of these notions to our setting is explained by the following result.

Lemma 3.15
.

For
{\bf b}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{n}
, the Hilbert function of the ring
R_{n}=\mathbb{K}[H_{ij}]
satisfies

h_{R_{n}}({\bf b})=\sum_{\lambda\in\mathcal{P}^{\prime}_{\mathrm{even}}}K_{\lambda,{\bf b}},

where
\mathcal{P}^{\prime}_{\mathrm{even}}
denotes the set of partitions
\lambda
of
n
whose conjugate
partition
\lambda^{\prime}
is even.

Proof.

By
[
34
, Lemma 8.16]
, the Hilbert function
h_{R_{n}}({\bf b})
is the coefficient of
t_{1}^{b_{1}}t_{2}^{b_{2}}\cdots t_{n}^{b_{n}}
in

{\rm HS}_{R_{n}}({\bf t})=\sum h_{R_{n}}({\bf b})\,{\bf t}^{\bf b}=\frac{1}{\prod\limits_{1\leq i<j\leq n}(1-\mathbf{t}^{\deg H_{ij}})}=\frac{1}{\prod\limits_{1\leq i<j\leq n}(1-t_{i}t_{j})}.

A variant of Cauchy’s identity (see
[
30
, p. 77]
) gives the expansion

\frac{1}{\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq n}(1-t_{i}t_{j})}=\sum_{\lambda\in\mathcal{P}^{\prime}_{\mathrm{even}}}s_{\lambda}.

Since each Schur polynomial

is homogeneous of total degree
|\lambda|
, the
homogeneous component of total degree

in this series is the sum of

over
partitions
\lambda
of
|{\bf b}|
whose conjugate
\lambda^{\prime}
is even.
Expanding each

in the symmetric monomial functions basis as

s_{\lambda}=\sum_{\mu}K_{\lambda\mu}\,m_{\mu},

and extracting the coefficient of the monomial

yields the stated formula.

4
Building blocks as fundamental invariants

In the physics literature
[
4
,
10
,
11
,
18
,
49
]
one uses the fact that rational

n
-point structures satisfying Lorentz invariance and transversality can be
expressed in terms of the
basic building blocks
(
1
). We give an
algebraic proof of this statement using tools from invariant theory: the building blocks generate
the field of rational invariants under

Z_{i}\mapsto Z_{i}+\alpha_{i}P_{i}
.
We use Weyl’s theorems on Lorentz
invariants and Rosenlicht’s theorem on rational invariants.

Let
n\geq 2
and
m=d+2\geq 5
, where

d
denotes the number of spatial dimensions, and let
\mathbb{K}
be an algebraically closed field
of characteristic zero. Consider configurations of
n
embedding-space vectors
P_{i}
together with
n
polarization vectors
Z_{i}
:

(P_{1},Z_{1},\ldots,P_{n},Z_{n})\in W:=(\mathbb{K}^{m})^{2n},

where the Lorentz group
\mathrm{O}(1,m-1)
acts on all vectors as follows:

P_{i}\mapsto\Lambda P_{i}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{ and }\hskip 10.00002ptZ_{i}\mapsto\Lambda Z_{i}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{ for }\;\Lambda\in\mathrm{O}(1,m{-}1).

We
write
(\,\cdot\,)
for the Lorentzian scalar product. A classical theorem of Weyl
[
47
, p. 65]
states that the ring of
Lorentz-invariant polynomials is generated by Lorentzian scalar products:

\mathbb{K}[P_{i},Z_{i}]^{\mathrm{O}(1,m-1)}=\mathbb{K}[P_{i}\cdot P_{j},\;Z_{i}\cdot Z_{j},\;P_{i}\cdot Z_{j}].

Moreover, these scalar products also generate the field of rational Lorentz
invariants:

\mathbb{K}(P_{i},Z_{i})^{\mathrm{O}(1,m-1)}=\mathrm{Frac}\,\bigl(\mathbb{K}[P_{i}\cdot P_{j},\;Z_{i}\cdot Z_{j},\;P_{i}\cdot Z_{j}]\bigr).

Indeed, the identity component of
\mathrm{O}(1,m-1)
, the
restricted Lorentz group

\mathrm{SO}^{+}(1,m-1)
, is semisimple
and hence has no non‑trivial characters, so the claim follows from
[
9
, Lemma 2.2]
.

The relations among these scalar products are called
Gram relations
.
When

2n\leq m
, the scalar products are algebraically independent, and in the case
2n>m
, the
ideal of Gram relations is generated by the
(m+1)\times(m+1)
minors of the Gram
matrix
[
47
, p. 75]
,

\mathcal{G}=\begin{pmatrix}P_{i}\cdot P_{j}&P_{i}\cdot Z_{j}\\
Z_{i}\cdot P_{j}&Z_{i}\cdot Z_{j}\end{pmatrix}_{1\leq i,j\leq n}.

(27)

The commutative algebra of these ideals was studied in
[
19
]
.
As described in Section
2
, we set

P_{i}^{2}=0,\hskip 10.00002ptP_{i}\cdot Z_{i}=0,\hskip 10.00002ptZ_{i}^{2}=0,\hskip 20.00003pti=1,\ldots,n.

(28)

For the remaining scalar products, we introduce the following shorthand notation:

p_{ij}=P_{i}\cdot P_{j}=p_{ji},\hskip 10.00002ptq_{ij}=P_{i}\cdot Z_{j},\hskip 10.00002ptr_{ij}=Z_{i}\cdot Z_{j}=r_{ji},\hskip 20.00003pti\neq j.

Let

S=\mathbb{K}[p_{ij},q_{ij},r_{ij}\mid i\neq j]
be the polynomial ring in these
n(2n-2)
variables,
and let
I_{\mathrm{Gram}}\subseteq S
be the ideal obtained by evaluating the minors of (
27
) on the locus (
28
). We
write

\mathcal{R}:=S/I_{\mathrm{Gram}},\hskip 10.00002pt\mathcal{F}:=\mathrm{Frac}(\mathcal{R}),\hskip 10.00002ptX:=\mathrm{Spec}(\mathcal{R}).

(29)

Thus the affine variety
X
parametrises all valid configurations of scalar products arising from null and transverse vectors in
\mathbb{K}^{m}
.
By
[
19
, Theorem 2.5]
, for
m\geq 5
we have

\operatorname{trdeg}_{\mathbb{K}}\mathcal{F}=\dim X=\begin{cases}n(2m-3)-\binom{m}{2},&m<2n\\
n(2n-2),&m\geq 2n.\end{cases}

(30)

We now discuss the transversality constraint, that is, invariance under
Z_{i}\mapsto Z_{i}+\alpha_{i}P_{i}
.

Definition 4.1

(Translation action)
.

The additive group

(\mathbb{K}^{n},+)\cong\mathbb{G}_{a}^{n}

acts on
W
by

\forall\,\alpha=(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n})\in\mathbb{G}_{a}^{n}\colon\hskip 10.00002pt\alpha\cdot P_{i}=P_{i},\hskip 10.00002pt\alpha\cdot Z_{i}=Z_{i}+\alpha_{i}\,P_{i},\hskip 10.00002pti=1,\ldots,n.

(31)

The translation action (
31
) defines an injective
algebraic group homomorphism

\rho\colon\,\mathbb{G}_{a}^{n}\;\rightarrow\;\mathrm{GL}_{2nm}(\mathbb{K}).

Explicitly,
the image of
\alpha=(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n})\in\mathbb{G}_{a}^{n}
is the
block-diagonal matrix

\rho(\alpha)\;=\;\operatorname{diag}\left(\begin{pmatrix}{\rm I}_{m}&0\\
\alpha_{1}{\rm I}_{m}&{\rm I}_{m}\end{pmatrix},\;\begin{pmatrix}{\rm I}_{m}&0\\
\alpha_{2}{\rm I}_{m}&{\rm I}_{m}\end{pmatrix},\;\ldots,\;\begin{pmatrix}{\rm I}_{m}&0\\
\alpha_{n}{\rm I}_{m}&{\rm I}_{m}\end{pmatrix}\right)\;\in\;\mathrm{GL}_{2nm}(\mathbb{K}).

In
particular, every element of
\rho(\mathbb{G}_{a}^{n})
is unipotent. Denote
\rho(\mathbb{G}_{a}^{n})
by
G
.

Proposition 4.2
.

The action (
31
) is generically free.
In particular, it is faithful.

Proof.

We show that the action of
G
is free on the open dense subset

U=\{(P_{1},Z_{1},\ldots,P_{n},Z_{n})^{\top}\in W\mid\forall\,i:\,P_{i}\neq 0\}\subset W.

Indeed, for arbitrary
x\in U
, as well as arbitrary
\alpha=(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n})\in\mathbb{K}^{n}
, we have

\rho(\alpha)x=(P_{1},Z_{1}+\alpha_{1}P_{1},\ldots,P_{n},Z_{n}+\alpha_{n}P_{n})^{\top}.

Since none of the
P_{i}
are zero, the only
\alpha
for which
\rho(\alpha)x=x
is
\alpha=0
.
∎

Since the translation action (
31
) commutes with the action of Lorentz group
\mathrm{O}(1,m{-}1)
, it induces corresponding transformation rules on the generators of the polynomial ring
S
.

Proposition 4.3
.

For
\alpha\in\mathbb{G}_{a}^{n}
, the generators of
S
transform as follows:

\alpha\cdot p_{ij}=p_{ij},\hskip 20.00003pt\alpha\cdot q_{ij}=q_{ij}+\alpha_{j}\,p_{ij},\hskip 20.00003pt\alpha\cdot r_{ij}=r_{ij}+\alpha_{i}\,q_{ij}+\alpha_{j}\,q_{ji}+\alpha_{i}\alpha_{j}\,p_{ij}.

(32)

Proof.

The
p_{ij}
are independent of the
Z_{k}
and are therefore invariant. The remaining rules follow from bilinearity and symmetry of the Lorentzian scalar product:

\displaystyle P_{i}\cdot(Z_{j}+\alpha_{j}P_{j})

\displaystyle=P_{i}\cdot Z_{j}+\alpha_{j}\,P_{i}\cdot P_{j}=q_{ij}+\alpha_{j}\,p_{ij},

\displaystyle(Z_{i}+\alpha_{i}P_{i})\cdot(Z_{j}+\alpha_{j}P_{j})

\displaystyle=Z_{i}\cdot Z_{j}+\alpha_{i}\,P_{i}\cdot Z_{j}+\alpha_{j}\,Z_{i}\cdot P_{j}+\alpha_{i}\alpha_{j}\,P_{i}\cdot P_{j}

\displaystyle=r_{ij}+\alpha_{i}\,q_{ij}+\alpha_{j}\,q_{ji}+\alpha_{i}\alpha_{j}\,p_{ij}.\qed

Lemma 4.4
.

The action of
\mathbb{G}_{a}^{n}
on
S
(
32
) induces a well-defined action on the affine variety
X=\operatorname{Spec}(\mathcal{R})
and on the field of fractions
\mathcal{F}=\operatorname{Frac}(\mathcal{R})
.

Proof.

For each
\alpha\in\mathbb{K}^{n}
, the substitution rules (
32
) are polynomial in
\alpha
and in the generators of
S
, and hence define a
\mathbb{K}
-algebra endomorphism
\phi_{\alpha}\colon S\to S
. One verifies directly from (
32
) that
\phi_{\alpha}\circ\phi_{\beta}=\phi_{\alpha+\beta}
and
\phi_{0}=\mathrm{id}
, so each
\phi_{\alpha}
is an automorphism with inverse
\phi_{-\alpha}
.

It remains to show that
I_{\mathrm{Gram}}
is preserved by
\phi_{\alpha}
, that is, that
\phi_{\alpha}(I_{\mathrm{Gram}})\subseteq I_{\mathrm{Gram}}
. To see this, observe that the translation action (
31
) is a linear automorphism of
W
, and so the Gram matrix
\mathcal{G}^{\prime}\coloneqq\mathcal{G}(\alpha\cdot P,\alpha\cdot Z)
of the transformed vectors is related to the original by

\mathcal{G}^{\prime}=M^{\top}\,\mathcal{G}\,M,\hskip 20.00003pt\text{where}\hskip 10.00002ptM=\operatorname{diag}\!\left(\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
\alpha_{1}&1\end{pmatrix},\ldots,\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
\alpha_{n}&1\end{pmatrix}\right).

Since
M
is invertible,
\operatorname{rank}(\mathcal{G}^{\prime})=\operatorname{rank}(\mathcal{G})
. In particular, the
(m{+}1)\times(m{+}1)
minors of
\mathcal{G}^{\prime}
vanish if and only if the corresponding minors of
\mathcal{G}
vanish. Thus
\phi_{\alpha}
maps
I_{\mathrm{Gram}}
to itself.
∎

Remark 4.5
.

The unipotent group
G
is non‑reductive. Consequently, Hilbert’s finiteness
theorem does not apply, and the invariant ring
\mathcal{R}^{G}
need not be
finitely generated (cf. Nagata’s counterexample to Hilbert’s 14th

[

]
). In contrast, the field of rational invariants

is finitely generated for any algebraic group.

As in Proposition
4.2
, using the

), one can verify that the induced action
of

on
\mathcal{R}
is faithful. Moreover, since a general point

does not lie on any hypersurface

, the action is generically free. We therefore obtain the
following.

Corollary 4.6
.

A general orbit of the
G

\mathrm{Spec}(\mathcal{R})
is isomorphic to
\mathbb{G}_{a}^{n}
. Thus,

\dim(G\cdot x)=n\hskip 10.00002pt\text{for a general}\;\,x\in\mathrm{Spec}(\mathcal{R}).

Definition 4.7
.

Let
G
be an algebraic group acting on an irreducible variety

\mathbb{K}
.

•

A rational invariant

separate
two orbits

\mathcal{O}_{1}
and
\mathcal{O}_{2}
if it is defined on both orbits and takes

•

A set

two orbits if it contains an invariant separating

•

Finally,
M

separates orbits in general position

there exists a nonempty Zariski-open subset
X_{0}\subset X
such that
M
separates the
orbits of any two inequivalent points of
X_{0}
.

Theorem 4.8

(Rosenlicht, 1956)
.

Let

be an algebraic group acting on an irreducible variety

There exists a finite set of rational invariants

that
separates orbits in general position. Moreover, the field of invariants

is generated by

, and

\mathrm{trdeg}_{\mathbb{K}}\bigl(\mathbb{K}(X)^{G}\bigr)=\dim X-\max_{x\in X}\,\dim(G\cdot x).

(33)

Proposition 4.9
.

The

,

f(\alpha\cdot x)=f(x)\hskip 20.00003pt\forall\,\alpha\in\mathbb{G}_{a}^{n},\hskip 10.00002ptx\in\mathrm{Spec}(\mathcal{R}),\hskip 10.00002ptf\in\{P_{ij},\,H_{ij},\,\mathcal{V}_{ij}\}.

Theorem 4.10
.

The field of rational invariants of the action induced by

) on

is

\mathcal{F}^{G}\;=\;\mathbb{K}\Bigl(P_{ij}\;\,(i<j),\;\,H_{ij}\;\,(i<j),\;\,\mathcal{V}_{ij}\;\,(i,j\in[n],\;j\neq i,i+1)\Bigr).

Invariance of the generators was established in

if and only if

P_{ij}=p_{ij}
, the equality
P_{ij}(x)=P_{ij}(x^{\prime})

implies that

p^{\prime}_{ij}=p_{ij}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{for all}\hskip 10.00002pt\;i\neq j.

(36)

Assuming (
36
), the equality
\mathcal{V}_{ij}(x^{\prime})=\mathcal{V}_{ij}(x)
then yields

p_{ij}(q^{\prime}_{i+1,i}-q_{i+1,i})=p_{i,i+1}(q^{\prime}_{ji}-q_{ji})
for
j\neq i,i+1
.
That is, the vector

(q^{\prime}_{ji}-q_{ji})_{j\neq i}
is proportional to
(p_{ij})_{j\neq i}
. Setting

\alpha_{i}:=\frac{q^{\prime}_{i+1,i}-q_{i+1,i}}{p_{i,i+1}},

it follows that

q^{\prime}_{ji}=q_{ji}+\alpha_{i}\,p_{ij}
. Finally, substituting these expressions into the relations

H_{ij}(x^{\prime})=H_{ij}(x)
gives

r^{\prime}_{ij}=r_{ij}+\alpha_{i}q_{ij}+\alpha_{j}q_{ji}+\alpha_{i}\alpha_{j}p_{ij},

Thus, by Proposition
4.3
we have
x^{\prime}=\alpha\cdot x
for a unique
\alpha\in\mathbb{G}_{a}^{n}
, and the building blocks
P_{ij},H_{ij}
and
\mathcal{V}_{ij}
separate general orbits. The claim now follows from Theorem
4.8
.
∎

As a corollary of Rosenlicht’s theorem, we obtain the dimension of the variety
parametrized by the basic building blocks
P_{ij}
,
H_{ij}
, and
\mathcal{V}_{ij}
. This variety was also studied in
[
19
]
.

Corollary 4.11
.

Combining (
30
), (
33
), and
Corollary
4.6
, for
m\geq 5
we obtain

\mathrm{trdeg}_{\mathbb{K}}\bigl(\mathcal{F}^{G}\bigr)=\dim\mathcal{R}-\max_{p\in\mathrm{Spec}(\mathcal{R})}\dim(G\cdot p)=\mathrm{trdeg}_{\mathbb{K}}\bigl(\mathcal{F}\bigr)-n=\begin{cases}n(2m-4)-{m\choose 2},&m<2n\\
n(2n-3),&m\geq 2n.\end{cases}

Recall that
\mathrm{trdeg}_{\mathbb{K}}\bigl(\mathcal{F}^{G}\bigr)
is the number of algebraically independent
P_{ij}
,
H_{ij}
and
\mathcal{V}_{ij}
over
\mathbb{K}
.

5
Conformal
n
-point functions

As described in Section
2
, an
n
-point conformal correlator
G_{\mathbf{s},\mathbf{\Delta}}(P_{1},Z_{1},\ldots,P_{n},Z_{n})
with spins
\mathbf{s}=(s_{1},\ldots,s_{n})\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{n}
and scaling dimensions
\mathbf{\Delta}=(\Delta_{1},\ldots,\Delta_{n})\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
must satisfy Lorentz invariance (
3
), transversality (
4
), polynomiality in the entries of
Z_{i}
, homogeneity (
5
), null-cone and polarisation constraints (
6
). Starting from the perspective that
G_{\mathbf{s},\mathbf{\Delta}}
could be an arbitrary function of the
P_{i}
and
Z_{i}
, these constraints put strong restrictions on the form that
G_{\mathbf{s},\mathbf{\Delta}}
can take.
The goal of this section is to describe the most general form of
G_{\mathbf{s},\mathbf{\Delta}}
allowed by the constraints (
3
)-(
6
). To do this, we first focus on the case of rational functions that satisfy (
3
)-(
6
). We call such rational functions
F_{\mathbf{s},\mathbf{\Delta}}(P_{1},Z_{1},\ldots,P_{n},Z_{n})
, and the construction of their most general form is the result of Theorem
5.2
. We then comment on how this result constrains the most general form of
n
-point conformal correlators
G_{\mathbf{s},\mathbf{\Delta}}
in Remark
5.4
.
This section is mostly expository, and we review and prove the results of
[
11
, Section 4.4]
.

To formulate the main result of this section, we will need the notion of a
cross-ratio
. For a quadruple of distinct indices
\{i,j,k,l\}\subseteq[n]
, the corresponding cross-ratio is defined as

u_{ijkl}(P):=\dfrac{P_{ij}P_{kl}}{P_{ik}P_{jl}}.

Remark 5.1
.

We emphasize that each
P_{ij}
is of the form:

P_{ij}=P_{i}\cdot P_{j}=-P_{i}^{0}P_{j}^{0}+P_{i}^{1}P_{j}^{1}+\cdots+P_{i}^{m-1}P_{j}^{m-1},

and so these cross-ratios differ from the cross-ratios on the line
\mathbb{P}^{1}
(see e.g.
[
27
, Section 1]
).

Theorem
4.10
indicates that Lorentz invariance, transversality, null-cone constraints and tracelessness mean that each rational function
F_{\mathbf{s},\mathbf{\Delta}}
satisfying (
3
)-(
6
) is a function of the variables
P_{ij}
,
H_{ij}
and
\mathcal{V}_{ij}
. The polynomiality constraint is satisfied by requiring that
F_{\mathbf{s},\mathbf{\Delta}}
be polynomial in the
H_{ij}
and
\mathcal{V}_{ij}
.
The homogeneity constraint (
5
) imposes that the terms of this polynomial have a certain fixed multidegree and, as we show in the theorem below, also fixes the dependence on the variables
P_{ij}
.
We can now conclude that the general form of
F_{\mathbf{s},\mathbf{\Delta}}
is an element of the ring
\mathbb{C}(P_{ij})[H_{ij},\mathcal{V}_{ij}]
and is thus a sum of monomial terms

F_{\mathbf{s},\mathbf{\Delta}}(P_{1},Z_{1},\ldots,P_{n},Z_{n})=\sum_{k}B_{k}(P_{ij},H_{ij},\mathcal{V}_{ij})\,,

(37)

where each
B_{k}
satisfies the homogeneity property (
5
).

We are now ready to formulate and prove the main result of this section.

Theorem 5.2
.

Let
\mathbb{C}(\mathbf{u})
be the field of rational functions in the cross-ratios
u_{ijkl}(P)
for all quadruples
\{i,j,k,l\}
.
Endow the polynomial ring
\mathbb{C}(\mathbf{u})[H_{ij},\mathcal{V}_{ij}]
with the multigrading given by (
15
). Each rational function
F_{\mathbf{s},\mathbf{\Delta}}
that satisfies (
3
)-(
6
) with spins
\mathbf{s}=(s_{1},\ldots,s_{n})
and scaling dimensions
\mathbf{\Delta}=(\Delta_{1},\ldots,\Delta_{n})
can be written in the form:

\begin{split}F_{\mathbf{s},\mathbf{\Delta}}(P_{1},Z_{1},\ldots,&P_{n},Z_{n})=\left[\prod_{i<j}P_{ij}^{-c_{ij}}\right]A(\mathbf{u},H_{ij},\mathcal{V}_{ij})\,,\\
c_{ij}&=\dfrac{\tau_{i}+\tau_{j}}{n-2}-\dfrac{1}{(n-1)(n-2)}\sum\limits_{k=1}^{n}\tau_{k}\,,\end{split}

(38)

where each
A\in\mathbb{C}(\mathbf{u})[H_{ij},\mathcal{V}_{ij}]
is a homogeneous polynomial of multidegree
\mathbf{s}
, and
\tau_{i}=\Delta_{i}+s_{i}
.

Remark 5.3
.

In the case
n=3
, there are no cross-ratios as one cannot pick a quadruple of distinct indices. The field
\mathbb{C}(\mathbf{u})
in this case is therefore just the field of constants
\mathbb{C}
and the dependence on the cross-ratios in Theorem
5.2
disappears entirely.

Proof.

We start from the assumption that
F_{\mathbf{s},\mathbf{\Delta}}
is of the form written in equation (
37
). Consider first the constraints on the monomials
B_{k}\in\mathbb{C}(P_{ij})[H_{ij},\mathcal{V}_{ij}]
imposed by the homogeneity condition (
5
). Note that the variables
P_{ij}
,
H_{ij}
and
\mathcal{V}_{ij}
transform as follows under the scalings
P_{i}\mapsto\lambda_{i}P_{j}
and
Z_{i}\mapsto\mu_{i}Z_{i}
of all
P_{i}
and
Z_{i}
:

P_{ij}\mapsto\lambda_{i}\lambda_{j}\,P_{ij},\hskip 10.00002ptH_{ij}\mapsto\lambda_{i}\lambda_{j}\mu_{i}\mu_{j}\,H_{ij},\hskip 10.00002pt\mathcal{V}_{ij}\mapsto\lambda_{i}\mu_{i}\mathcal{V}_{ij}\,.

(39)

Imposing that the
B_{k}
are all homogeneous in the
Z_{i}
with degree dictated by (
5
) and thus equivalent implies that each of the
B_{k}
be homogeneous of multidegree
\mathbf{s}
in the grading (
15
).
Recall from Section
3
that for a given
\mathbf{s}
, there are a finite number of monomials in
\mathbb{C}[H_{ij},\mathcal{V}_{ij}]
with this multidegree and so the number of
B_{k}
is finite.
The dependence of each
B_{k}
on the
P_{ij}
is then constrained by requiring that the
B_{k}
have the desired scaling properties under the transformation
P_{i}\mapsto\lambda_{i}P_{i}
.
We will now show that the dependence of each monomial
B_{k}
on the variables
P_{ij}
is the same up to cross-ratios.

Consider the coefficient of a single monomial
B_{k}
in the field
\mathbb{C}(P_{ij})
.
It is a rational function in the variables
P_{ij}
, that is, it takes the form:

\dfrac{\sum\limits_{(a_{ij})\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{L}}\alpha_{(a_{ij})}\prod\limits_{1\leq i<j\leq n}P_{ij}^{a_{ij}}}{\sum\limits_{(b_{ij})\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{L}}\beta_{(b_{ij})}\prod\limits_{1\leq i<j\leq n}P_{ij}^{b_{ij}}},

(40)

where
L=\binom{n}{2}
.
Since
B_{k}
is homogeneous of multidegree
\mathbf{s}
in the entries of the
Z_{i}
, the homogeneity constraint (
5
) requires that the function (
40
) be homogeneous of multidegree
(-\Delta_{1}-s_{1},\ldots,-\Delta_{n}-s_{n})
in the
P_{i}
. Thus, the summands of (
40
), each of which has the form

\dfrac{\alpha_{(a_{ij})}\prod\limits_{1\leq i<j\leq n}P_{ij}^{a_{ij}}}{\sum\limits_{(b_{ij})\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{L}}\beta_{(b_{ij})}\prod\limits_{1\leq i<j\leq n}P_{ij}^{b_{ij}}}=\dfrac{\alpha_{(a_{ij})}}{\sum\limits_{(b_{ij})\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{L}}\beta_{(b_{ij})}\prod\limits_{1\leq i<j\leq n}P_{ij}^{b_{ij}-a_{ij}}}

must be homogeneous of this multidegree. Defining
\tilde{c}_{ij}:=b_{ij}-a_{ij}
, this implies that the
\tilde{c}_{ij}
must satisfy the following system of
n
linear equations:

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=1\\
j\neq i\end{subarray}}^{n}\tilde{c}_{ij}=\Delta_{i}+s_{i}\,,\hskip 10.00002pti\in\{1,\ldots,n\}\,,

where we have adopted the labeling convention
\tilde{c}_{ij}=\tilde{c}_{ji}
. Writing
\tilde{\mathbf{c}}
for the vector of variables
\tilde{c}_{ij}
and defining
\tau_{i}:=\Delta_{i}+s_{i}
, we find that the linear system is of the form:

B\tilde{\mathbf{c}}=(\tau_{1},\ldots,\tau_{n})^{T},

where
B
is the
n\times\binom{n}{2}
vertex-edge incidence matrix of the undirected complete graph
K_{n}
. A particular solution of this system is given by the vector with entries

c_{ij}=\dfrac{\tau_{i}+\tau_{j}}{n-2}-\dfrac{1}{(n-1)(n-2)}\sum\limits_{k=1}^{n}\tau_{k}.

The kernel of the matrix
B
is spanned by the
\binom{n}{4}
vectors
\tilde{\mathbf{c}}
, each having four non-zero entries:
c_{ij}=c_{kl}=1
and
c_{il}=c_{jk}=-1
.
This follows for instance from the results of
[
40
]
applied to the complete graph. Each such vector is the exponent vector of the corresponding cross-ratio
u_{ijkl}
. This means that the coefficient of each monomial
B_{k}
in
\mathbb{C}(P_{ij})
is a uniquely defined Laurent monomial up to multiplication by an arbitrary rational function in the cross-ratios. We can thus remove the common factor
\prod_{i<j}P_{ij}^{-c_{ij}}

from each of the monomials
B_{k}
to the front of the sum in (
37
) to obtain an expression as in formula (
38
).
∎

Remark 5.4
.

Note that the cross-ratios satisfy Lorentz invariance, transversality, null-cone and tracelessness constraints, and are invariant under respective rescalings of the
P_{i}
and
Z_{i}
(
39
).
In view of these facts, the proof of Theorem
5.2
implies that even if one does not assume that the
n
-point function
G_{\mathbf{s},\mathbf{\Delta}}
is rational, it can still be written in the form (
38
) with
A
being a polynomial in
H_{ij}
and
\mathcal{V}_{ij}
whose coefficients are
arbitrary
functions of the cross-ratios. Following Remark
5.3
, one can moreover conclude that three-point functions take exactly the form of
F_{\mathbf{s},\mathbf{\Delta}}
in Theorem
5.2
– they are rational.

Theorem
5.2
along with the results of Section
3
(and specifically Theorem
3.1
) allows us to compute an upper bound for the dimension of the
\mathbb{C}(\mathbf{u})
-vector space of rational functions

for any value of

and any values of the spins
\mathbf{s}=(s_{1},\ldots,s_{n})
.

6
Algebraic relations between building blocks

In the previous section we have seen that any
n
-point function can be written as a homogeneous polynomial of multidegree
\mathbf{s}
in the building blocks
H_{ij}
and
\mathcal{V}_{ij}
with coefficients depending on the cross-ratios
u_{ijkl}
.
This gives an upper bound on the number of independent structures in the
n
-point function: one can just count the number of monomials of multidegree
\mathbf{s}
.
However, these monomials can still be dependent.
The goal of this section is to investigate dependencies between them. In other words, we study algebraic relations between the basic building blocks
P_{ij}
,
H_{ij}
and
\mathcal{V}_{ij}
.

As explained in the previous section, the motivation for studying these relations is that the monomial counts of Section
3
are formal counts: they treat the building blocks as algebraically independent. In fixed spacetime dimension this need not be true, because Gram constraints among the vectors
P_{i}
and
Z_{i}
can produce algebraic relations among
P_{ij}
,
H_{ij}
, and
\mathcal{V}_{ij}
. These relations reduce the number of genuinely independent conformal structures and determine the coordinate ring whose Hilbert function gives the dimension-dependent count.

The building blocks
P_{ij}
,
H_{ij}
and
\mathcal{V}_{ij}
indeed satisfy relations implied by the Gram constraints on the vectors
P_{1},\ldots,P_{n},Z_{1},\ldots,Z_{n}
.
These relations are the central topic of
[
19
]
, and Example
5.5
treats the simplest case of this situation.
Our setup differs slightly from that of
[
19
]
in that we do not consider the full set of the variables
V_{i,jk}
but only a subset consisting of
n(n-2)
many
\mathbb{C}(\mathbf{u})
-linearly independent variables
\mathcal{V}_{ij}
suggested in
[
11
, Eqn. (4.75)]
.
Our choice of variables is more natural: in fact, the only relations between
P_{ij},H_{ij}
and
\mathcal{V}_{ij}
are those implied by the Gram constraints, as the following proposition demonstrates.

Proposition 6.1
.

Let
m=d+2\geq 5
. If all the inner products
P_{i}\cdot P_{j}
,
P_{i}\cdot Z_{j}
and
Z_{i}\cdot Z_{j}
are
\mathbb{C}
-algebraically independent, i.e., if
m\geq 2n
, then so are all the building blocks
P_{ij}
,
H_{ij}
,
\mathcal{V}_{ij}
.

Proof.

If the inner products are algebraically independent, which by a result of Weyl is the case exactly when
m\geq 2n
, then by Corollary
4.11
, the transcendence degree of the field generated by the building blocks, and thus, the dimension of the corresponding variety, is equal to the number of non-zero inner products minus
n
.
The number of inner products is
2\binom{n}{2}+n(n-1)
. Subtracting
n
, we get
n(2n-3)
.
This is exactly the number of building blocks
P_{ij},H_{ij},\mathcal{V}_{ij}
, which means they are algebraically independent.
∎

Our next result generalises the second statement in
[
19
, Lemma 3.7]
.
It allows to rewrite any relation between
P_{ij}
,
H_{ij}
and
V_{i,jk}
in terms of
P_{ij}
,
H_{ij}
and
\mathcal{V}_{ij}
.

Lemma 6.2
.

For any set of indices
i,j,k,l
the following linear relation holds for the variables
V_{i,jk}
for arbitrary
d
:

V_{i,jk}P_{il}P_{jk}-V_{i,jl}P_{ik}P_{jl}+V_{i,kl}P_{ij}P_{kl}=0.

In particular, setting
j=i+1
one gets

\dfrac{P_{ik}P_{i+1,l}}{P_{i,i+1}P_{kl}}\mathcal{V}_{il}-\dfrac{P_{il}P_{i+1,k}}{P_{i,i+1}P_{kl}}\mathcal{V}_{ik}=V_{i,kl}.

Proof.

One can directly check these identities by substituting in the parametrization (
34
).
∎

Let
\mathcal{V}_{1,n,m}
be the variety of symmetric
n\times n
matrices of rank at most
m
with zeros along the diagonal, as in
[
19
, Section 2]
.
This variety is naturally isomorphic to the variety parametrised by the building blocks
P_{ij}
.
In the following result we turn to the variety parametrised by the cross-ratios
u_{ijkl}
and compute its dimension, which is equal to
\mathrm{trdeg}_{\mathbb{C}}\mathbb{C}(\mathbf{u})
.

Proposition 6.3
.

Let
\mathbb{C}(\mathbf{u})
be the field of rational functions in the cross-ratios
u_{ijkl}
for
\{i,j,k,l\}\subset[n]
.
Then
\mathrm{trdeg}_{\mathbb{C}}\mathbb{C}(\mathbf{u})=\mathrm{dim}(\mathcal{V}_{1,n,m})-n
. For
m\geq 3
(any
d\in\mathbb{N}
) and
n>m
,

\mathrm{trdeg}_{\mathbb{C}}\mathbb{C}(\mathbf{u})=n(m-2)+\binom{m}{2}\,.

(42)

For
n\leq m
, we have
\mathrm{trdeg}_{\mathbb{C}}\mathbb{C}(\mathbf{u})=\binom{n}{2}-n
.

Proof.

The variety
\mathcal{V}_{1,n,m}
is naturally parametrised by the building blocks
P_{ij}
via the matrix

\begin{pmatrix}0&P_{12}&P_{13}&\cdots&P_{1n}\\
P_{12}&0&P_{23}&\cdots&P_{2n}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
P_{1n}&P_{2n}&P_{3n}&\cdots&0\end{pmatrix},

(43)

where
P_{ij}=P_{i}\cdot P_{j}
and
P_{i}\in\mathbb{K}^{m}
for all
i
.
The algebraic torus
(\mathbb{C}^{*})^{n}
acts on
\mathbb{C}^{nm}
by

\forall\ \lambda=(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{n})\in(\mathbb{C}^{*})^{n}:\hskip 10.00002pt\lambda\cdot P_{i}=\lambda_{i}P_{i}\,,\hskip 10.00002pti=1,\ldots,n\,.

(44)

This
(\mathbb{C}^{*})^{n}
-action can be lifted from
\mathbb{C}^{nm}
to
\mathcal{V}_{1,n,m}
through

M\mapsto\mathrm{diag}(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{n})\cdot M\cdot\mathrm{diag}(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{n}).

(45)

Clearly, for generic
M\in\mathcal{V}_{1,n,m}
, the dimension of the
(\mathbb{C}^{*})^{n}
-orbit of
M
is
n
.
Furthermore, the field of invariants of
\mathbb{C}(P_{ij})
under this torus action is
\mathbb{C}(\mathbf{u})
(this follows e.g. from
[
44
, Section 1.4]
).
Thus, by Theorem
4.8
,

\mathrm{trdeg}_{\mathbb{C}}\mathbb{C}(\mathbf{u})=\mathrm{dim}(\mathcal{V}_{1,n,m})-n.

(46)

By
[
19
, Theorem 2.5]
, for
3\leq m<n
we have
\mathrm{dim}(\mathcal{V}_{1,n,m})=n(m-1)-\binom{m}{2}
and thus

\mathrm{trdeg}_{\mathbb{C}}\mathbb{C}(\mathbf{u})=n(m-2)-\binom{m}{2}.

(47)

When
m\geq n
, the building blocks
P_{ij}
are
\mathbb{C}
-algebraically independent and the dimension of the variety
\mathcal{V}_{1,n,m}
is simply the number of
P_{ij}
, which is
\binom{n}{2}
.
∎

Lemma 6.4
.

Consider the following field extensions:

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces L:=K(H_{ij},\mathcal{V}_{ij})}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces F:=K(H_{ij},\mathcal{V}_{ij})(P_{ij})=\mathbb{C}(P_{ij},H_{ij},\mathcal{V}_{ij})}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\subset}

\subset

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\subset}

We have
\mathrm{trdeg}_{K}A=n
, and
\mathrm{trdeg}_{K}L=\mathrm{trdeg}_{A}F
.
Therefore, we also have
\mathrm{trdeg}_{L}F=n
.

Proof.

The first statement follows from Proposition
6.3
: we have
\mathrm{trdeg}_{\mathbb{C}}A=\mathrm{dim}(\mathcal{V}_{1,n,m})
and
\mathrm{trdeg}_{\mathbb{C}}K=\mathrm{dim}(\mathcal{V}_{1,n,m})-n
.
To prove the second statement, it suffices to show that any algebraic relation between
H_{ij}
and
\mathcal{V}_{ij}
over
\mathbb{C}(P_{ij})
is in fact an algebraic relation over
\mathbb{C}(\mathbf{u})
.
To show this, recall that the ring
\mathbb{C}[P_{ij},H_{ij},\mathcal{V}_{ij}]
admits a natural
\mathbb{Z}^{2n}
multigrading as in (
39
).
Any algebraic relation between
P_{ij},H_{ij}
and
\mathcal{V}_{ij}
has to be homogeneous with respect to this multigrading, since these blocks are functions of the vectors
P_{i}
and
Z_{i}
whose entries are chosen independently, and the multigrading records the dependence on
P_{i}
and
Z_{i}
.
Since the last
n
entries of the multidegree vector
\mathrm{deg}(P_{ij})
are zero for all
P_{ij}
, the coefficients (that are functions in
P_{ij}
) at all monomials in
H_{ij}
and
\mathcal{V}_{ij}
in such a relation have to have the same multidegree.
Dividing by one of these coefficients, we get a relation in
\mathbb{C}(P_{ij})[H_{ij},\mathcal{V}_{ij}]
whose coefficients have multidegree zero.
The field of rational functions in
P_{ij}
of multidegree zero is the field of invariants of the
(\mathbb{C}^{*})^{n}
-action from Proposition
6.3
and is generated by the cross-ratios
\mathbf{u}
, so this relation is indeed a relation in
\mathbb{C}(\mathbf{u})[H_{ij},\mathcal{V}_{ij}]
.
∎

The following is the main result of this section. We count the number of building blocks
H_{ij}
and
\mathcal{V}_{ij}
that are algebraically independent over
\mathbb{C}(\mathbf{u})
.

Theorem 6.5
.

Let
m\geq 5
and let
K
and
L
be as in Lemma
6.4
.
Then we have

\mathrm{trdeg}_{K}L=\begin{cases}n(m-2),\quad&n>m,\\
n(2m-4)-\binom{m}{2}-\binom{n}{2},\quad&2n>m>n,\\
\binom{n}{2}+n(n-2),\quad&m>2n.\end{cases}

(48)

Proof.

Consider the following tower of field extensions:

\mathbb{C}\subset K\subset L\subset F:=\mathbb{C}(P_{ij},H_{ij},\mathcal{V}_{ij}).

(49)

We have
\mathrm{trdeg}_{K}L=\mathrm{trdeg}_{\mathbb{C}}F-\mathrm{trdeg}_{\mathbb{C}}K-\mathrm{trdeg}_{L}F
.
By Corollary
4.11
,

\mathrm{trdeg}_{\mathbb{C}}F=n(2m-4)-\binom{m}{2}\hskip 10.00002pt\mathrm{for}\hskip 10.00002ptm<2n.

(50)

By Proposition
6.3
,
\mathrm{trdeg}_{\mathbb{C}}=\mathrm{dim}(\mathcal{V}_{1,n,m})-n
and by Lemma
6.4
, we have
\mathrm{trdeg}_{L}F=n
.
Combining these equalities yields the result.
In the case
m\geq 2n
, the building blocks are independent by Proposition
6.1
.
∎

7
Bose symmetry

Physical systems are invariant under the exchange of identical particles.
At the level of correlators
G_{\mathbf{s},\mathbf{\Delta}}(P_{1},Z_{1},\ldots,P_{n},Z_{n})
involving bosonic fields, this fact manifests itself in the form of Bose symmetry: if for some
i\neq j
,
(s_{i},\Delta_{i})=(s_{j},\Delta_{j})
, the corresponding correlator
G_{\mathbf{s},\mathbf{\Delta}}
is invariant under the permutation of the pairs of variables
(P_{i},Z_{i})
and
(P_{j},Z_{j})
, in other words, (
7
) holds. For a given physical theory, this symmetry is built into how correlators are defined. However, from the bootstrap perspective Bose symmetry instead provides an additional set of constraints on the general form of certain correlators. From this perspective, for each
i\neq j
with
(s_{i},\Delta_{i})=(s_{j},\Delta_{j})
we obtain a system of linear constraints on the coefficients of monomials that can appear in a given
n
-point function.
We now describe these constraints using the language of invariant theory.

Let
S_{n}
be the symmetric group on
n
elements. A permutation
\sigma\in S_{n}
acts on the polynomial ring
\mathbb{C}[P_{ij},H_{ij},V_{i,jk}]
via

P_{ij}\mapsto P_{\sigma(i)\sigma(j)},\hskip 10.00002ptH_{ij}\mapsto H_{\sigma(i)\sigma(j)},\hskip 10.00002ptV_{i,jk}\mapsto V_{\sigma(i),\sigma(j)\sigma(k)}.

(51)

We now wish to define the induced action of
S_{n}
on the ring
\mathbb{C}(\mathbf{u})[H_{ij},\mathcal{V}_{ij}]
. For the cross ratios
u_{ijkl}
this is simple, we have
\sigma(u_{ijkl})=u_{\sigma(i)\sigma(j)\sigma(k)\sigma(l)}
. For the
\mathcal{V}_{ij}
we have the slight complication that, generally

\sigma\cdot\mathcal{V}_{ij}=\sigma\cdot V_{i,i+1j}=V_{\sigma(i),\sigma(i+1)\sigma(j)}\neq V_{\sigma(i),\sigma(i)+1\sigma(j)}=\mathcal{V}_{\sigma(i)\sigma(j)}\,.

(52)

Despite this, the action of
\sigma
on
\mathcal{V}_{ij}
can still easily be described using only the variables
\mathcal{V}_{ij}
and cross-ratios in the
P_{ij}
. Namely, applying Lemma
6.2
, we obtain

\sigma\cdot\mathcal{V}_{ij}=V_{\sigma(i),\sigma(i+1)\sigma(j)}=\dfrac{P_{\sigma(i)\sigma(i+1)}P_{\sigma(i)+1\sigma(j)}}{P_{\sigma(i)\sigma(i)+1}P_{\sigma(i+1)\sigma(j)}}\mathcal{V}_{\sigma(i)\sigma(j)}-\dfrac{P_{\sigma(i)\sigma(j)}P_{\sigma(i)+1\sigma(i+1)}}{P_{\sigma(i)\sigma(i)+1}P_{\sigma(i+1)\sigma(j)}}\mathcal{V}_{\sigma(i)\sigma(i+1)}.

(53)

Since both coefficients on the right-hand side are cross-ratios, we again land in
\mathbb{C}(\mathbf{u})[H_{ij},\mathcal{V}_{ij}]
.
Note that if
\sigma(i+1)=\sigma(i)+1
for some
i
, the above simplifies to
\sigma\cdot\mathcal{V}_{ij}=\mathcal{V}_{\sigma(i)\sigma(j)}
.

We can now define Bose symmetry as invariance under the action of a subgroup of
S_{n}
on the polynomial ring
\mathbb{C}(\mathbf{u})[H_{ij},\mathcal{V}_{ij}]
given by Equations (
51
) and (
53
).
Namely, consider the subgroup
\mathfrak{S}
of
S_{n}
generated by transpositions
(ij)
for all
i,j
such that
(s_{i},\Delta_{i})=(s_{j},\Delta_{j})
. Then Bose symmetry dictates that the rational part of an
n
-point function is an element of the ring of invariants

\mathbb{C}(\mathbf{u})[H_{ij},\mathcal{V}_{ij}]^{\mathfrak{S}}
.
Computing this ring of invariants for arbitrary
n
is a difficult, albeit algorithmic
[
13
]
problem.
In
[
23
]
the authors studied a similar problem, exploring the consequences of Bose symmetry for polynomials in the
P_{ij}
, allowing for
P_{ii}\neq 0
. Another physically-motivated paper that investigates rings of invariants under both the action of an orthogonal group and a permutation group is
[
24
]
.

We now concentrate on the case of three-point functions and treat
H_{ij}
and
\mathcal{V}_{ij}
as formal variables. This means that we do not take the algebraic relations between them into account. By Remarks
5.3
and
5.4
, three-point functions are rational and do not depend on cross-ratios, so they are elements of the ring
\mathbb{C}[H_{ij},\mathcal{V}_{ij}]
. Bose symmetry implies that they are in fact elements of the ring of invariants
\mathbb{C}[H_{ij},\mathcal{V}_{ij}]^{\mathfrak{S}}
. We present formulas for the dimension of the space of monomials that can appear in a three-point function that has Bose symmetry of some kind. Note that this question was addressed in
[
26
, Section 2.5.1]
from a representation theory perspective. We offer an explicit formula based on elementary methods. It would be interesting to generalize these results to higher
n
.

For simplicity of notation, we set
V_{1}:=V_{1,23}
,
V_{2}:=V_{2,31}
and
V_{3}:=V_{3,12}
. By Theorem
5.2
, a three-point function
G_{\mathbf{s},\mathbf{\Delta}}
is then, up to multiplication by a Laurent monomial in the
P_{ij}
that is invariant under all transpositions, a polynomial of the form
\sum A_{(a,b,c,d,e,f)}V_{1}^{a}V_{2}^{b}V_{3}^{c}H_{12}^{d}H_{23}^{e}H_{13}^{f}
, where the coefficients
A
are
\mathbb{C}
-valued and where the exponents have degree
(s_{1},s_{2},s_{3})
, i.e.,

a+d+f=s_{1}\,,\hskip 10.00002ptb+d+e=s_{2}\,,\hskip 10.00002ptc+e+f=s_{3}\,.

(54)

We first treat the case in which all spins and scaling dimensions are equal.

Proposition 7.1
.

Suppose
(s_{1},\Delta_{1})=(s_{2},\Delta_{2})=(s_{3},\Delta_{3})=(s,\Delta)
. Then the dimension of the vector space of three-point functions satisfying Bose symmetry, ignoring the algebraic dependencies between monomials of multidegree
(s,s,s)
, is

\frac{1}{4}\binom{s+4}{3}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{for s even and }\hskip 10.00002pt\frac{1}{24}s(s-1)(s+1)\hskip 10.00002pt\text{for s odd.}

(55)

Proof.

Bose symmetry imposes the following constraints on each coefficient
A_{(a,b,c,d,e,f)}
of the monomial
V_{1}^{a}V_{2}^{b}V_{3}^{c}H_{12}^{d}H_{23}^{e}H_{13}^{f}
:

\begin{split}A_{(a,b,c,d,e,f)}=(-1)^{a+b+c}&A_{(b,a,c,d,f,e)}\,,\quad A_{(a,b,c,d,e,f)}=(-1)^{a+b+c}A_{(c,b,a,e,d,f)}\,,\\
&A_{(a,b,c,d,e,f)}=(-1)^{a+b+c}A_{(a,c,b,f,e,d)}\,.\end{split}

Among the constraints written above, the number of them which are independent depends on how many of
d
,
e
and
f
are equal. Consequently, to understand by how much these constraints reduce the dimension of the vector space of monomials, we first need to know the number of monomials with;
d=e=f
, any two of
d
,
e
and
f
equal, or none of
d
,
e
and
f
equal. First recall that due to (
54
), the non-negative numbers
d
,
e
and
f
must satisfy the following system of inequalities

d+e\leq s\,,\hskip 10.00002pte+f\leq s\,,\hskip 10.00002ptd+f\leq s\,.

When
d=e=f
this reduces to
2d\leq s
, which for a given
s
this has
\left\lfloor s/2\right\rfloor+1
many non-negative integer solutions. This is the number of coefficients
A
with
d=e=f
. Now suppose
d=e\neq f
. Then the system reduces to

2d\leq s\,,\hskip 10.00002ptf\leq s-d\,.

For a given
s
, the number of non-negative solutions to this system is

\left(s-\left\lfloor\dfrac{s}{2}\right\rfloor+1\right)+\ldots+(s+1)=\left(\left\lfloor\dfrac{s}{2}\right\rfloor+1\right)\left(s+1-\frac{1}{2}\left\lfloor\frac{s}{2}\right\rfloor\right)\,.

Subtracting the number of solutions with
d=e=f
, we arrive at
(\lfloor s/2\rfloor+1)(s-\frac{1}{2}\lfloor s/2\rfloor)
. Since the number of solutions for the cases
d\neq e=f
and
d=f\neq e
is the same, we find that the total number of coefficients with any two of
d
,
e
and
f
equal is
3(\lfloor s/2\rfloor+1)(s-\frac{1}{2}\lfloor s/2\rfloor)
. Finally, using the above counts and Proposition
3.9
, the number of coefficients with none of
d
,
e
and
f
equal is given by

N(s,s,s)-3\left(\left\lfloor\dfrac{s}{2}\right\rfloor+1\right)\left(s-\frac{1}{2}\left\lfloor\frac{s}{2}\right\rfloor\right)-\left\lfloor\dfrac{s}{2}\right\rfloor-1.

We now return to the constraints. First note that, using (
54
),
a+b+c=3s-2(d+e+f)
. Consequently, for
s
odd, the relations satisfied by the coefficients
A
above result in all of the coefficients with any of
d
,
e
or
f
equal being set to zero. What remains to be considered are the constraints among coefficients with none of
d
,
e
and
f
equal. In this case, there are five independent constraints, leading to these coefficients coming in groups of six. Thus, for
s
odd, the dimension of the space of monomials reduces to

\frac{1}{6}\left[N(s,s,s)-3\left(\left\lfloor\dfrac{s}{2}\right\rfloor+1\right)\left(s-\frac{1}{2}\left\lfloor\frac{s}{2}\right\rfloor\right)-\left\lfloor\dfrac{s}{2}\right\rfloor-1\right]=\frac{1}{24}s(s-1)(s+1).

Now we consider the case of
s
even. In this case, none of the constraints on the coefficients
A
set any of them equal to zero. When
d=e=f
there are no constraints on the coefficients. When any two of
d
,
e
and
f
are equal, there are two independent constraints leading to triplets that are equal up to a sign. Thus, for
s
even the total dimension of the space of monomials reduces to

\left\lfloor\dfrac{s}{2}\right\rfloor+1+\left(\left\lfloor\dfrac{s}{2}\right\rfloor+1\right)\left(s-\frac{1}{2}\left\lfloor\dfrac{s}{2}\right\rfloor\right)+\frac{1}{6}\left[N(s,s,s)-3\left(\left\lfloor\dfrac{s}{2}\right\rfloor+1\right)\left(s-\frac{1}{2}\left\lfloor\frac{s}{2}\right\rfloor\right)-\left\lfloor\dfrac{s}{2}\right\rfloor-1\right].

Since
s
is even, this simplifies to
\frac{1}{4}\binom{s+4}{3}
.
∎

7.1
is given by sequence A006918 in OEIS

]
for

It remains to consider the case of only two of the spins and scaling dimensions equal.

Proposition 7.3
.

Let
(s_{1},\Delta_{1})=(s_{2},\Delta_{2})=(s,\Delta)
and
s<s_{3}
. Then the dimension of the vector space of three-point functions satisfying Bose symmetry, ignoring the algebraic dependencies between monomials of multidegree
(s,s,s_{3})
, is

\frac{1}{2}[N(s,s,s_{3})+(-1)^{s_{3}}N_{1}(s,s_{3})]\,,\hskip 10.00002pt\mathrm{where}\hskip 10.00002ptN_{1}(s,s_{3})=\left(\left\lfloor\dfrac{s_{3}}{2}\right\rfloor+1\right)\left(s+1-\frac{1}{2}\left\lfloor\dfrac{s_{3}}{2}\right\rfloor\right)\,,

and where
N(s,s,s_{3})
is the number of lattice points in the conformal polytope from Proposition
3.9
for
s_{1}=s_{2}=s
.
For
(s_{2},\Delta_{2})=(s_{3},\Delta_{3})=(s,\Delta)
and
s_{1}<s
this dimension is

\frac{1}{2}[N(s_{1},s,s)+(-1)^{s_{1}}N_{1}(s,s_{1})]\,.

Proof.

When only two spins are equal, Bose symmetry produces just one family of constraints

A_{(a,b,c,d,e,f)}=(-1)^{a+b+c}A_{(b,a,c,d,f,e)}\,.

Let
N(s,s,s_{3})
denote the number of non-negative solutions of the system

a+d+f=s\,,\hskip 10.00002ptb+d+e=s\,,\hskip 10.00002ptc+e+f=s_{3}\,.

This is the number
N(s_{1},s_{2},s_{3})
from Proposition
3.9
, specialized to the case
s_{1}=s_{2}=s
. For a given choice of
s
and
s_{3}
, let
N_{1}(s,s_{3})
be the number of non-negative integer solutions with
e=f
(and hence
a=b
). As in the proof of Proposition
7.1
, we find that

N_{1}(s,s_{3})=\left(\left\lfloor\dfrac{s_{3}}{2}\right\rfloor+1\right)\left(s+1-\frac{1}{2}\left\lfloor\frac{s_{3}}{2}\right\rfloor\right),

and the dimension of the vector space of three-point functions satisfying Bose symmetry is

\frac{1}{2}[N(s,s,s_{3})+(-1)^{s_{3}}N_{1}(s,s_{3})]\,.

The argument for the case
(s_{2},\Delta_{2})=(s_{3},\Delta_{3})=(s,\Delta)
and
s_{1}<s
is entirely analogous.
∎

8
Partial conservation

Beyond conformal covariance and Bose symmetry, certain
n
-point functions must satisfy additional constraints imposed by partial conservation.
These require that the
n
-point correlator be annihilated by a specific set of differential operators.
These differential operators are defined in terms of the embedding-space vectors
P_{i}
and
Z_{i}
.
More precisely, suppose the
n
-point function
G_{\mathbf{s},\mathbf{\Delta}}
has
\Delta_{i}=d-1+t_{i}
for some
i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
and
t_{i}\in\{0,\dots,s_{i}-1\}
.
Then, following
[
17
]
, it must satisfy

\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial P_{i}}\cdot D_{Z_{i}}\right)^{s_{i}-t_{i}}G_{\mathbf{s},\mathbf{\Delta}}(P_{1},Z_{1},\ldots,P_{n},Z_{n})=0,

(56)

where
D_{Z}
denotes the vector of differential operators given by

D_{Z}=\left(\frac{d}{2}-1+Z\cdot\frac{\partial}{\partial Z}\right)\frac{\partial}{\partial Z}-\frac{1}{2}Z\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial Z\cdot\partial Z}\,.

(57)

If there are multiple
i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
for which this is the case, then
G_{\mathbf{s},\mathbf{\Delta}}
must satisfy (
56
) for each such
i
. When bootstrapping
G_{\mathbf{s},\mathbf{\Delta}}
, these constraints serve as restrictions on the space of structures that can appear in
G_{\mathbf{s},\mathbf{\Delta}}
. This approach was applied to three-point functions in
[
4
]
to construct no-go theorems for the existence of certain types of particles in de Sitter space. The authors derived these constraints on a case-by-case basis for specific choices of spins
s_{i}
, scaling dimensions
\Delta_{i}
and spatial dimension
d
.
To avoid this case-by-case analysis, one can approach the problem from a different perspective. Instead, one can ask for the most general solution to the differential equation

(\partial_{P_{i}}\cdot D_{Z_{i}})^{s_{i}-t_{i}}G_{\mathbf{s},\mathbf{\Delta}}=0
that satisfies the constraints (
3
)-(
6
).
A similar approach was taken in
[
49
]
, in the case where
s_{i}-t_{i}=1
.
The success of this approach relied on the ability to model the action of
\partial_{P_{i}}\cdot D_{Z_{i}}
on three-point functions with
\Delta_{i}=d+s_{i}-2
in terms of a differential operator written using the building blocks
H_{ij}
and
V_{i,jk}
. This leads to the question: when can the action of
(\partial_{P_{i}}\cdot D_{Z_{i}})^{s_{i}-t_{i}}
on
n
-point functions be expressed entirely in terms of the building blocks
P_{ij}
,
H_{ij}
and
V_{i,jk}
?

In this section, we reproduce the construction of
[
49
]
, explain why it works and suggest its generalisation to arbitrary powers of
\partial_{P_{i}}\cdot D_{Z_{i}}
for
n=3
.
Note that, as will be made clear below, writing
(\partial_{P_{i}}\cdot D_{Z_{i}})^{s_{i}-t_{i}}
in terms of the building blocks is not the same as composing the differential operator
\partial_{P_{i}}\cdot D_{Z_{i}}
written in terms of the building blocks
s_{i}-t_{i}
times.
This is because the building blocks version of
\partial_{P_{i}}\cdot D_{Z_{i}}
is not simply a coordinate transformation of the original differential operator.
In a sense, it is a differential operator that acts in the same way as
\partial_{P_{i}}\cdot D_{Z_{i}}
, when restricted to a specific vector subspace. When thought of as differential operators acting on the larger vector space of all three-point functions, their action on the same function can produce different results.
We will mainly consider three-point functions, and describe when and how our results can be applied to higher-point functions.

As in Section
3
, in what follows we will be thinking of the building blocks as formal variables. As described in Section
5
, a three-point function
G_{\mathbf{s},\mathbf{\Delta}}
can be thought of as an element of the
\mathbb{C}
-vector space

P^{-\tau_{12,3}}_{12}P^{-\tau_{23,1}}_{23}P^{-\tau_{31,2}}_{13}\cdot W_{\mathbf{s}}\,,

(58)

where we have defined
\tau_{ij,k}:=\frac{1}{2}(s_{i}+s_{j}-s_{k}+\Delta_{i}+\Delta_{j}-\Delta_{k})
, and

W_{\mathbf{s}}:=\mathrm{span}_{\mathbb{C}}\left\{H^{h_{1}}_{12}H^{h_{2}}_{23}H^{h_{3}}_{13}V^{v_{1}}_{1,23}V^{v_{2}}_{2,31}V^{v_{3}}_{3,12}\hskip 10.00002pt:\hskip 10.00002pt\begin{aligned} v_{1}+h_{1}+h_{3}&=s_{1}\\
v_{2}+h_{1}+h_{2}&=s_{2}\\
v_{3}+h_{2}+h_{3}&=s_{3}\end{aligned}\right\}.

(59)

To permit the action of the differential operators
(\partial_{P_{i}}\cdot D_{Z_{i}})^{s_{i}-t_{i}}
on this vector space, we introduce a linear map

f:\mathbb{C}(P_{ij},H_{ij},V_{ijk})\rightarrow\mathbb{C}(P_{i},Z_{i})\,.

(60)

Choosing to work with the basis for (
58
) given by the Laurent monomials

\frac{H^{h_{1}}_{12}H^{h_{2}}_{23}H^{h_{3}}_{13}V^{v_{1}}_{1,23}V^{v_{2}}_{2,31}V^{v_{3}}_{3,12}}{P^{\tau_{12,3}}_{12}P^{\tau_{23,1}}_{23}P^{\tau_{31,2}}_{13}}\,,

(61)

we define
f
to be the evaluation map that replaces each of the formal variables
P_{ij}
,
H_{ij}
and
V_{i,jk}
in the monomial (
61
) with the embedding-space structures:

\displaystyle P_{ij}

\displaystyle\ \mapsto\ P_{i}\cdot P_{j}\,,

(62)

\displaystyle H_{ij}

\displaystyle\ \mapsto\ -2\bigl[(Z_{i}\cdot Z_{j})(P_{i}\cdot P_{j})-(Z_{i}\cdot P_{j})(Z_{j}\cdot P_{i})\bigr]\,,

(63)

\displaystyle V_{ijk}

\displaystyle\ \mapsto\ \frac{(Z_{i}\cdot P_{j})(P_{i}\cdot P_{k})-(Z_{i}\cdot P_{k})(P_{i}\cdot P_{j})}{P_{j}\cdot P_{k}}\,.

(64)

The differential operators
(\partial_{P_{i}}\cdot D_{Z_{i}})^{s_{i}-t_{i}}
can then act on the
\mathbb{C}
-vector space

f(P^{-\tau_{12,3}}_{12}P^{-\tau_{23,1}}_{23}P^{-\tau_{31,2}}_{13}\cdot W_{\mathbf{s}})\,.

(65)

8.1
Existence of the lifted operator

We begin with the following proposition.

Proposition 8.1
.

Fix
i\in\{1,2,3\}
. Allow for any
\mathbf{s}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{3}
with
s_{i}>0
and
\Delta_{j}\in\mathbb{C}
for
j\neq i
. Set
t_{i}=s_{i}-1
,
s_{i}\geq 1
. Then the differential operator
(\partial_{P_{i}}\cdot D_{Z_{i}})^{s_{i}-t_{i}}
is a map

(\partial_{P_{i}}\cdot D_{Z_{i}})^{s_{i}-t_{i}}:f(P^{-\tau_{12,3}}_{12}P^{-\tau_{23,1}}_{23}P^{-\tau_{31,2}}_{13}\cdot W_{\mathbf{s}})\rightarrow f(P^{-\tau_{12,3}}_{12}P^{-\tau_{23,1}}_{23}P^{-\tau_{31,2}}_{13}\cdot W_{\mathbf{s}-(s_{i}-t_{i})\mathbf{e}_{i}})\,,

(66)

if and only if
\Delta_{i}=d-1+t_{i}
.

Remark 8.2
.

Although not proven symbolically due to being computationally very demanding, the same statement can easily be verified to hold for
s_{i}-t_{i}=2
for a vast number of choices of
s_{1}
,
s_{2}
and
s_{3}
(see
[
7
]
). In particular, we believe that the result for
s_{i}-t_{i}=1
extends to arbitrary
s_{i}-t_{i}
. This is made precise in Conjecture
8.7
.

Proof.

The action of the differential operators
\partial_{P_{i}}\cdot D_{Z_{i}}
on

f\left(\frac{H^{h_{1}}_{12}H^{h_{2}}_{23}H^{h_{3}}_{13}V^{v_{1}}_{1,23}V^{v_{2}}_{2,31}V^{v_{3}}_{3,12}}{P^{\tau_{12,3}}_{12}P^{\tau_{23,1}}_{23}P^{\tau_{31,2}}_{13}}\right),

(67)

can be computed explicitly. In
Mathematica

[
7
]
, we show that the results can be rewritten in terms of the building blocks by reversing the action of
f
when
\Delta_{i}=d-2+s_{i}
. In particular, we explicitly perform the rewriting in the cases where we have the right scaling dimension, and show that for other scaling dimensions, there are leftover terms containing factors of
\,P_{i}\cdot Z_{j}\,
. For generic values of
\mathbf{s}
, these terms cannot be rewritten in terms of the building blocks. This can be shown by differentiating the expression with respect to these residual embedding-space inner products, and noticing that this is written solely in terms of embedding-space structures and can only be vanishing if
\Delta_{i}
takes the value specified above.

The explicit computation also reveals that the powers of the overall factors of
P_{i}\cdot P_{j}
are invariant and the spins are reduced by one.
In other words, for
\Delta_{i}=d-2+s_{i}
and
s_{i}-t_{i}=1
, we find (
66
). The reduction in spin follows from the fact that elements of the vector space (
58
) are polynomials in the
Z_{i}
. From the form of
\partial_{P_{i}}\cdot D_{Z_{i}}
it is clear that the action of
\partial_{P_{i}}\cdot D_{Z_{i}}
on polynomials in the
Z_{i}
reduces their degree in the
Z_{i}
by one.
∎

Remark 8.3
.

Note that this feature of preserving the overall factor in the

is not generic. For
\Delta_{i}
differing from the value given above, the differential operator

does not preserve the overall factor in the

. In addition, perhaps counter-intuitively, the overall factor of

is crucial in Proposition
8.1
. Indeed, via direct computation one can verify that the differential operator
(\partial_{P_{i}}\cdot D_{Z_{i}})^{s_{i}-t_{i}}
does
not
map
f(W_{\mathbf{s}})
to
f(W_{\mathbf{s}-(s_{i}-t_{i})\mathbf{e}_{i}})
. To see how this could be the case, consider the example where
s_{i}-t_{i}=1
. In this case, we have

{\small\partial_{P_{i}}\cdot D_{Z_{i}}\Bigg(f\Bigg(\frac{H^{h_{1}}_{12}H^{h_{2}}_{23}H^{h_{3}}_{13}V^{v_{1}}_{1,23}V^{v_{2}}_{2,31}V^{v_{3}}_{3,12}}{P^{\tau_{12,3}}_{12}P^{\tau_{23,1}}_{23}P^{\tau_{31,2}}_{13}}\Bigg)\Bigg)=\partial_{P_{i}}\cdot\Bigg(\frac{D_{Z_{i}}(f(H^{h_{1}}_{12}H^{h_{2}}_{23}H^{h_{3}}_{13}V^{v_{1}}_{1,23}V^{v_{2}}_{2,31}V^{v_{3}}_{3,12})}{f(P^{\tau_{12,3}}_{12}P^{\tau_{23,1}}_{23}P^{\tau_{31,2}}_{13})}\Bigg).}

However,

\partial_{P_{i}}(f(P^{-\tau_{12,3}}_{12}P^{-\tau_{23,1}}_{23}P^{-\tau_{31,2}}_{13}))\neq\mathbf{0}\,,

and so (

The existence of the overall factor in the

and

The expression (

can be modelled by the action of a differential operator in the

and

.

Fix

Remark 8.5

We note that Theorem

8.2
Construction and explicit form

We now present a computation that allows one to construct the differential operators
\mathcal{D}_{i,s_{i}-t_{i}}
.
This computation treats
s_{1}
,
s_{2}
,
s_{3}
,
\Delta_{2}
and
\Delta_{3}
as parameters.

Input:
A choice of
i\in\{1,2,3\}
,
s_{i}-t_{i}\in\mathbb{N}
and
d\geq 3
.

1.

Act with the differential operator
(\partial_{P_{i}}\cdot D_{Z_{i}})^{s_{i}-t_{i}}
on the monomial

\frac{H^{h_{1}}_{12}H^{h_{2}}_{23}H^{h_{3}}_{13}V^{v_{1}}_{1,23}V^{v_{2}}_{2,31}V^{v_{3}}_{3,12}}{P^{\tau_{12,3}}_{12}P^{\tau_{23,1}}_{23}P^{\tau_{31,2}}_{13}}\,,

where we have set

\displaystyle\Delta_{i}=d-1+t_{i},\hskip 10.00002ptv_{1}=s_{1}-h_{1}-h_{3},\hskip 10.00002ptv_{2}=s_{2}-h_{1}-h_{2},\hskip 10.00002ptv_{3}=s_{3}-h_{3}-h_{2}\,.

Here
h_{1}
,
h_{2}
and
h_{3}
should be treated as parameters.

2.

Write the result of this action in terms of the building blocks by reversing the action of
f
. That this is possible is guaranteed by Proposition
8.1
when
s_{i}-t_{i}=1
for any
d\geq 3
and is expected to hold for other choices of
s_{i}-t_{i}
. Then multiply the entire expression by
P^{\tau_{12,3}}_{12}P^{\tau_{23,1}}_{23}P^{\tau_{31,2}}_{13}

to cancel the overall factor in the
P_{ij}
. Call the result
X
.

3.

Construct the most general linear differential operator
\mathcal{D}_{i,s_{i}-t_{i}}
of order
3(s_{i}-t_{i})
in variables
H_{ij}
,
V_{i,jk}
, without partial derivatives with respect to
H_{jk}
with neither of
j,k
equal to
i
. That such partial derivatives should not appear in a differential operator modelling
(\partial_{P_{i}}\cdot D_{Z_{i}})^{s_{i}-t_{i}}
is clear from the form of
\partial_{P_{i}}\cdot D_{Z_{i}}
in terms of the embedding-space vectors.
Leave placeholders for the coefficients of the differentials. These coefficients should be understood as being elements of the polynomial ring
\mathbb{C}[H_{ij},V_{i,jk}]
.

4.

Apply
\mathcal{D}_{i,s_{i}-t_{i}}
to the monomial
H^{h_{1}}_{12}H^{h_{2}}_{23}H^{h_{3}}_{13}V^{v_{1}}_{1,23}V^{v_{2}}_{2,31}V^{v_{3}}_{3,12}
. Call the result
Y
.

5.

Fix the coefficients in
\mathcal{D}_{i,s_{i}-t_{i}}
by comparing
X
and
Y
.
This can be done by evaluating the expression
X=Y
at a sufficiently large number of choices of values for the
s_{j}
and
\Delta_{j}
,
j\neq i
with appropriate
h_{j}
, such that
s_{i}\geq h_{i}+h_{i-1}
.
Eventually one arrives at a set of equations linear in the free coefficients in the differential operator ansatz
\mathcal{D}_{i,s_{i}-t_{i}}
that can be solved in terms of
H_{ij}
,
V_{i,jk}
,
\Delta_{2}
and
\Delta_{3}
.
It could occur that not all the coefficients are fixed by this process.
If after a large number of iterations no new coefficient is fixed, we assume that the unfixed coefficients are free and thus there are multiple operators
\mathcal{D}_{i,s_{i}-t_{i}}
.
To make one choice of the operator, we set these to zero.
The chosen operator is guaranteed to act correctly on the spaces
W_{\mathbf{s}}
whose bases we used in this computation to obtain
\mathcal{D}_{i,s_{i}-t_{i}}
and we conjecture it acts correctly on all
W_{\mathbf{s}}
.

Output:
The differential operator
\mathcal{D}_{i,s_{i}-t_{i}}
.

Performing this computation for
i=1
and
s_{i}-t_{i}=1
, we obtain a unique operator
\mathcal{D}_{i,1}
and reproduce the result of
[
49
]
.
Note that the author of
[

]
uses a slightly different definition of the building blocks.
Thus, at face value the differential operator presented here looks different to that in
[

where the three components are given explicitly below.

\displaystyle+2\Big[(\Delta_{2}-\Delta_{3}-1)\,H_{12}+(d-2)\,V_{1}V_{2}\Big]\partial_{V_{1}}\partial_{H_{12}}

.

Note that the differential operator

is independent of the spins

9
Conclusions and outlook

In this paper we have developed methods to constrain the set of independent tensor structures appearing in conformal correlators from a purely algebraic and combinatorial approach. Working in terms of the building blocks
P_{ij}
,
H_{ij}
, and
V_{i,jk}
, we provide concrete and algorithmic methods for enumerating and generating these structures, taking Bose symmetry, partial conservation and algebraic relations into consideration. In doing this, we provide rigorous proofs for results used widely in the physics literature. As we focus on the algebraic perspective, we attack the problem from an angle that has largely been avoided in the literature, where representation-theoretic approaches have been used instead, see e.g.
[
18
,
26
]
.

We now summarise the open questions and conjectures that appeared in this work.

1.

In recent years, polytopes appearing in physics have received substantial attention from the discrete geometry community. A primary example is that of cosmological polytopes
[
1
]
. In the same spirit, we find that it would be interesting to investigate the mathematical properties of conformal
n
-point polytopes
\mathcal{C}_{n}
in more detail.

2.

Section
3.3
connects our counting problem to Kostka numbers and Littlewood-Richardson coefficients. This raises the question of whether there is a natural representation theoretic interpretation of our combinatorial results that connects to
[
26
]
.

3.

In Section
6
we studied algebraic relations between the basic building blocks (
1
). From the physics perspective, one wishes to understand the number of monomials in
H_{ij}
and
\mathcal{V}_{ij}
of a given multidegree that are algebraically independent over the field
\mathbb{C}(\mathbf{u})
of rational functions in the cross-ratios. This is encoded by the Hilbert function
h_{I}
introduced in the end of Section
6
. While we give an upper bound in the form of the Hilbert function
h_{\tilde{I}}
, it is an open problem to compute the function
h_{I}
itself. This is computationally challenging because both the elements of the coefficient field and the blocks
H_{ij},\mathcal{V}_{ij}
depend on the same vectors
P_{i}
, restricting the use of numerical methods.

4.

In the context of Section
7
, a challenging problem is to computationally obtain a basis of
n
-point structures respecting Bose symmetry for
n\geq 4
. The difficulty here again comes from the fact that these structures depend on cross-ratios arbitrarily.

5.

It would be interesting to obtain an explanation (physical and mathematical) of the fact that the operator
\mathcal{D}_{i,s_{i}-t_{i}}
constructed in Section
8
does not depend on the values of spins and only depends on the differences between scaling dimensions.

6.

The approach of
[
49
]
yields generating functionals for all three-point function structures of fully conserved currents (
s_{i}-t_{i}=1
) of arbitrary spin in arbitrary
d
. It remains an open problem to extend this to partially conserved currents of arbitrary depth, in other words to obtain generating functionals for the general solution to

\mathcal{D}_{i,s_{i}-t_{i}}\,g_{\mathbf{s},\mathbf{\Delta}}(H_{ij},V_{i})=0\,,

(72)

where
G_{\mathbf{s},\mathbf{\Delta}}=g_{\mathbf{s},\mathbf{\Delta}}(H_{ij},V_{i})\big/P^{\tau_{12,3}}_{12}P^{\tau_{23,1}}_{23}P^{\tau_{31,2}}_{13}
. In Section
8
we established the conditions under which
\mathcal{D}_{i,s_{i}-t_{i}}
exists as a differential operator on the
H_{ij}
and
V_{i,jk}
, and provided an algorithm for computing it explicitly. A remaining open problem is to solve the resulting differential equation. Beyond obtaining the generating functionals themselves, one could, in the spirit of
[
49
]
, investigate whether the resulting structures admit an interpretation in terms of known CFTs involving partially conserved currents like
[
8
]
, or whether there exist structures that are not generated by any known CFTs at all.

We conclude by noting that our methods have immediate applications to the conformal and cosmological bootstrap programs, providing efficient means to construct and understand the algebraic structures underlying conformal correlators.
These methods can also be applied in other contexts. Indeed, integer solutions of
[
25
, Eq. 15]
are lattice points of the transportation polytopes introduced in Remark
3.19
, and the generating function
[
25
, Eq. 17]
can be seen as the Hilbert series of a polynomial ring. More generally, Hilbert series have already been used as generating functions in effective field theory and standard-model physics
[
24
,
28
,
29
,
48
]
. Through this paper, we hope to invite physicists to further explore applications of existing, powerful results in nonlinear algebra, combinatorics and invariant theory.
