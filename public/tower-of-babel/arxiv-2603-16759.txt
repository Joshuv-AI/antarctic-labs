TurnWise : The Gap between Single- and Multi-turn Language Model Capabilities
Victoria Graf, Valentina Pyatkin, Nouha Dziri, Nathan Lambert, Hannaneh Hajishirzi

Abstract
Multi-turn conversations are a common and critical mode of language model interaction. However, current open training and evaluation data focus on single-turn settings, failing to capture the additional dimension of these longer interactions. To understand this multi-/single-turn gap, we first introduce a new benchmark, TurnWiseEval , for multi-turn capabilities that is directly comparable to single-turn chat evaluation. Our evaluation isolates multi-turn specific conversational ability through pairwise comparison to equivalent single-turn settings. We additionally introduce our synthetic multi-turn data pipeline TurnWiseData which allows the scalable generation of multi-turn training data. Our experiments with Olmo 3 show that training with multi-turn data is vital to achieving strong multi-turn chat performance, and that including as little as 10k multi-turn conversations during post-training can lead to a 12% improvement on TurnWiseEval .



1
Introduction

Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly deployed to real-world users for free-form, multi-turn conversations. However, many training and evaluation methodologies continue to rely primarily on single-turn data, creating a mismatch between real-world usage and current paradigms.
Open language modeling recipes
7
;
11
focus on and are primarily comprised of single-turn data. Where multi-turn data is included in these recipes, it is added without systematic consideration of its contribution to conversational abilities.
Moreover, collecting scalable multi-turn conversational data proves difficult due to the challenges of user simulation
10
and the expensive nature of large-scale manual collection of user interactions
18
.

While gaps in multi-turn conversational ability have been documented
6
;
15
, current measurements of this gap are incomplete and insufficiently informative. Existing multi-turn dialogue benchmarks (e.g., MTBench
(
19
)
, MTEval
(
5
)
) are being saturated,
making it difficult to assess what remains of the multi-/single-turn performance gap. Additionally, many conversational benchmarks do not distinguish whether low model performance in multi-turn conversations stems from multi-turn specific performance gaps or from general model ability for similar tasks
19
;
1
.

To improve evaluation of the multi-turn conversational abilities of LLMs,
we first introduce a new benchmark,
TurnWiseEval
(Section
2
), which isolates multi-turn abilities from other confounding signals.
Unlike previous multi-turn evaluations, our approach grounds evaluation in pairwise comparisons between responses to multi-turn and equivalent single-turn conversations (Figure
1
).
This methodology isolates multi-turn performance beyond task difficulty, minimizing sensitivity to other LLM capabilities such as factuality and knowledge and providing a more focused assessment of conversational abilities.
Additionally, we use controlled conversational context to further separate multi-turn ability from dependencies on conversational state and to enable better comparisons of results between models.
We show that even frontier models like GPT-5 Chat can underperform on
TurnWiseEval
and that open model recipes lag behind.

Our evaluation metric is the win-rate of the evaluated model as determined by an LM judge.

To address this gap between chat performance of LLMs in single- versus multi-turn conversations, we introduce a scalable method,
TurnWiseData
, for creating synthetic multi-turn context on arbitrary single-turn conversations (Section
3
). We use data generated with our methodology to analyze how incorporating multi-turn data in training can improve conversational performance with Olmo 3
11
(Section
4
).
Through these experiments, we show that adding even a small number of synthetic multi-turn conversations in training can significantly reduce the gap with single-turn performance by up to 12%, demonstrating the importance of multi-turn conversations in training.

2
The
TurnWiseEval
Benchmark

In this section we detail the motivation, design, and resulting data inside the
TurnWiseEval
benchmark. We then show that
TurnWiseEval
measures multi-turn conversational abilities for models with a range of single-turn abilities and that multi-turn conversational ability is a distinct dimension of evaluation to consider beyond single-turn chat.

2.1
Evaluation Design

To isolate multi-turn conversational ability beyond single-turn task abilities, we use pairwise comparisons of responses to single- and multi-turn conversations.
We consider two comparison settings:

•

TurnWiseEval
-Absolute.
In our first evaluation setting, we measure “absolute” multi-turn performance by comparing an evaluated model’s completions of multi-turn conversations to responses from a strong model, specifically
GPT-4 (
1106-preview
), on equivalent single-turn prompts (Figure
1
). These reference responses represent a strong baseline response to the single-turn setting. This setting uses length-controlled win rate as the primary metric and is intended for easy interpretation of
TurnWiseEval
scores across models.

•

TurnWiseEval
-Self.

Our second setting further isolates multi-turn ability by comparing an evaluated model’s responses to multi-turn conversations to its own responses to corresponding single-turn conversations.
This setting uses discrete win rate as the primary metric. The single-turn response here represents the model’s own baseline ability for the task instruction. Win rates below 50% imply that a model’s responses to multi-turn conversations are consistently worse than their single-turn counterparts, indicating degraded performance in the multi-turn setting beyond what can be attributed to general task ability.
In best-case scenarios, models can leverage context from previous turns to improve their final response, leading to win rates above 50%.

For pairwise judgments in
TurnWiseEval
, we use GPT-4.1 as judge with the judgment prompt from AlpacaEval (Appendix
A
).

2.2
Multi-turn Conversation Curation

Our evaluation captures a common pattern in real-world interactions where users explore related topics across multiple turns.
Our data curation is informed by manual inspection of multiple real-world data sources. To ground our design, we read and analyzed multi-turn interactions from the Ai2 Playground model hosting platform (Appendix
B
).

Sourcing Prompts.

To build our
TurnWiseEval
dataset,
we create multi-turn conversations of up to eight user turns where user turns pose related questions to each seed instruction drawn from AlpacaEval.
Each user turn is synthetically generated and contains a self-contained, topically-related request,
allowing the assistant to optionally leverage earlier turns for richer responses without removing any necessary contextual information from the single-turn comparison conversation.
To control the information seen by the evaluated model in the conversational context so far, we include assistant turns generated by the model used for the reference single-turn completions in
TurnWiseEval
-Absolute
to create conversational states for each evaluation sample.
We choose not to go beyond eight user turns to avoid confounding our evaluation with varying long-context abilities

3
Creating Multi-turn Training Data

We introduce a scalable method for creating synthetic multi-turn data, which we call
TurnWiseData
. Additionally, we describe a self-talk data baseline which we combine with
TurnWiseData
conversations in our experimentation in Section
4
.

Sourcing prompts.

All conversations build on seed prompts randomly selected from the WildChat subset of the Dolci Instruct SFT mixture,
a primarily single-turn dataset used for Olmo 3 post-training
11
. If seed prompts were selected from already multi-turn conversations, only the first turn was used. We exclude prompts shorter than 15 characters. For both data generation methods, we create conversations with 2 to 8 user turns.

TurnWiseData
.

To create multi-turn conversational data with
TurnWiseData
, seed prompts are used to independently generate possible user turns. By creating user turns independently, our pipeline avoids issues such as conversational drift, long contexts, and expensive online user simulation. These turns are then stacked to create a synthetic conversation with the original seed prompt as the final turn (Figure
2
). We place the seed prompt at the end of the conversation to preserve useful properties of the original dataset such as preference pairs.
User turns were generated with GPT-4.1.

Figure 2:
An example of using
TurnWiseData
to create a synthetic conversation. User turns are independently generated from the seed prompt (green) and conversations are appended with the seed prompt and response (blue).

We generate synthetic conversational context from existing prompts with two distinct methods.
Simulating conversations in which users are unsatisfied with an initial response and seek iteration, we generate paraphrased prompts as intermediate user turns.
Similarly, we represent exploratory conversations with follow-up information seeking by generating related queries as intermediate user prompts.

Self-talk

Prior work has used self-talk as a method for creating artificial conversations where LMs play the part of a user
8
;
16
. We use self-talk as a baseline for online conversation generation without user simulation. To capture a diverse range of follow-up interactions, we consider three user behaviors to create user turns: (1) amending previous instructions, (2) seeking clarification on a response, and (3) building on new assistant-provided information.

4
Training with Multi-turn Data

To understand the importance of multi-turn data during training, we conduct experiments with an open-data model, Olmo 3 7B Instruct
11
. We consider two training stages: supervised fine-tuning (SFT) and preference tuning.

4.1
Experimental Setting

We create two types of training data: supervised fine-tuning data and preference pairs. In both settings, we create data with
TurnWiseData
and with self-talk. For creating SFT data, we use GPT 4.1 and Qwen 3 32B (without thinking) to generate assistant turns.
To construct preference pairs, we use the delta-learning heuristic proposed by
2
.
We use two model pairs for data creation: (1) rejected responses produced by GPT-3.5 Turbo and preferred responses by GPT-4.1 and (2) rejected responses by Qwen 3 0.6B and preferred by Qwen 3 32B both without thinking enabled.

For our training baselines, we focus on supervised fine-tuning and Direct Preference Optimization (DPO)
13
.
We perform all training experiments on Olmo3-7B-Instruct-SFT. For SFT experiments, we continue training with additional multi-turn SFT data. For preference tuning, we use the full Olmo 3 7B preference mixture augmented with our synthetic data. All experiments use the same hyperparameters and training libraries as in Olmo 3
11
.

4.3
Effect of Previous Conversational Turns

Recent work has shown how earlier turns in a conversation can shift final responses to either improve
4
or degrade
6
performance. We control for conversational state in
TurnWiseEval
by generating intermediate assistant turns with a strong model to isolate final turn performance and decrease noise sources. To understand how this affects scores,
we experiment with the effect of intermediate response quality in Appendix
D
.

Additionally, we examined performance patterns across conversational lengths to understand how greater conversational context affects model performance (Figure
3
). Results show that
training with multi-turn data significantly increases performance at every conversational turn and flattens the decay of performance through longer conversations.

5
Related Work

Synthetic Multi-turn Data.

Prior work has considered creation of synthetic multi-turn data. SDSD
8
, for example, creates multi-turn data through LMs talking to themselves, an approach called “self-talk”
16
.
Other efforts focus on user simulation to create realistic synthetic user turns
12
;
14
;
17
. However, user simulations introduce their own challenges such as dialogue state tracking
17
and goal-alignment
10
.

Multi-turn Evaluations.

Prior evaluations of multi-turn capabilities in LLMs have introduced various core skills that arise in multi-turn settings
5
;
1
. For example, MT-Bench-101 establishes a three-tiered taxonomy founded in educational psychology
1
. To address saturation of multi-turn benchmarks, MultiChallenge combines hard tasks in long conversations
15
.

TurnWiseEval
differs from previous multi-turn benchmarks by anchoring judgments in pairwise comparisons to the single-turn setting.
While prior multi-turn benchmarks have used pairwise evaluation
19
, they do not isolate the difference between multi- and single-turn performance. Our comparison of these settings uniquely identifies how performance in multi-turn settings differs from that of single turns.

6
Conclusion

To address the single-/multi-turn data gap, we introduce
TurnWiseData
, a scalable method for generating multi-turn conversations. Additionally, we introduce a new multi-turn benchmark,
TurnWiseEval
, to evaluate the difference in a model’s performance between single- and multi-turn settings.
Our experiments with training on synthetic multi-turn conversations demonstrate significant improvements to addressing this gap in open model recipes.
Our results suggest that multi-turn conversational ability represents a distinct dimension of model ability not captured by traditional single-turn evaluation, and we recommend greater attention to multi-turn conversations in open data pipelines.

Limitations

TurnWiseEval
scores may vary with the quality of the intermediate turns, as analyzed by using a weaker model to generate the responses in context (Table
4
). While this affects absolute score, it does not typically affect overall model rankings. Relative changes in scores reflect evaluated models’ robustness to lower quality previous turns in context.

Additionally, our experimental settings are limited in scope to focus on dimensions specific to multi-turn training. Thus, we fix training hyperparameters to those of the Olmo 3 7B Instruct model since the Olmo 3 Instruct data comprises the vast majority of the training data seen by these models.

Appendix B
Manual Inspection of Data

Our benchmark creation is guided by inspection of over 200 conversations with Tülu 3 collected from the Ai2 model hosting interface. We additionally inspected multi-turn conversations from WildChat and LMSYS Chatbot Arena to verify our observed behaviors. From our inspection, we observed two common modes of user behaviors. First, users enter the conversation with a specific target and continue the conversation through multiple turns to iterate on the output. Second, users enter exploratory conversations that included following-up on information from previous turns in the conversation. Our synthetic data methodology is grounded in these observed conversation types.

Manual verification of data quality

Figure
5
shows an example of a conversation from
TurnWiseEval
. To ensure a high-quality evaluation, all conversations in
TurnWiseEval
were tagged by GPT 4.1 for coherence and degenerate turns. A total of 64 turns across 38 conversations were flagged for quality. Each flagged turn was manually inspected, and where relevant, the user turn was rewritten (and response regenerated) or the turn was removed. As a result, 22 turns were edited and 39 turns removed across 17 conversations. Final verification of a random sample of 100 conversations showed topical relevance and coherent instructions across all turns.
