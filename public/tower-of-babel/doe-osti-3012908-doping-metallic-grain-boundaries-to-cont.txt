Doping Metallic Grain Boundaries to Control Atomic Structure and Damage Tolerance

Title from 08/01/2023 - 07/31/2024 (Year 4):
Structural Short-Range Order and the Damage Tolerance of Amorphous Grain Boundary
Complexions

 Timothy J. Rupert
 Department of Materials Science and Engineering
 University of California, Irvine
                                       Irvine, CA 92697

ABSTRACT
Grain boundaries often act as sites for crack and void nucleation during plastic deformation of
metallic materials. While it is known that grain boundary character and structure can greatly
influence this damage nucleation process, the current level of control over such details is limited.
The objective of this project was to obtain a fundamental understanding of how metallic grain
boundary structure can be controlled through intelligent doping, with the idea of inducing planned
amorphous grain boundary phases or complexions. The effect of amorphous complexion structure
on dislocation accommodation mechanisms was studied, to improve the field’s understanding of
damage nucleation at a promising type of interface. Different microstructural descriptors were
studied, as grain boundaries can have large variations and complexity of local structure, and
influence the mechanical damage resistance of these features was tested. Nanocrystalline systems
that contain amorphous grain boundary complexions were prioritized, as these have more extreme
variations in interfacial structure and damage tolerance, with an emphasis on isolating the
importance of complexion population (type, thickness, etc.), network topology, local structure, and
local chemistry. This research used a combination of computational, experimental, and
characterization techniques to isolate and understand the importance of nanoscale grain boundary
structure and interfacial chemistry. Amorphous grain boundary complexions were found to clearly
increase a material’s resistance to mechanical damage, with the local distribution of structural
short-range order within the complexions found to be an important descriptor for damage and the
proposed focus of future work in this area.

INTRODUCTION
This project was focused on understanding how grain boundary structure controls the damage
resistance of metallic materials. Our three major hypotheses were: (1) Amorphous complexions
can increase the damage tolerance of nanocrystalline alloys. (2) Amorphous complexion thickness
and number fraction can be modified by processing route and/or alloy chemistry, leading to
improvements in damage tolerance. (3) Local structural order and chemical composition act as
important internal descriptors.

RESEARCH METHODS
To achieve our goals and answer these questions, we performed complementary experimental and
computational research lines which ran in parallel, but continually informed one another.
Nanocrystalline Cu-rich alloys with different complexion networks were synthesized by
mechanical alloying approaches. Binary alloys allowed complexion population and network
topology to be varied by altering cooling rate, while ternary and quaternary alloys were used to
vary local complexion structure and chemical composition. Transmission electron microscopy was
used to obtain parameters such as the complexion type, amorphous complexion thickness, grain
boundary character (misorientation angles and boundary plane normal), local atomic structure,
interfacial chemistry, triple junction type, and percolation pathways, in order to fully characterize
the complexion network. Small-scale mechanical testing was used to isolate the plasticity and
failure of nanocrystalline materials with different complexion networks due to alloy choice and/or
quenching rate. Atomistic modeling provided information on the variety of complexions that can
be accessed with different compositions, temperatures, and cooling rates, while also providing
high spatial and temporal resolution for the investigation of internal complexion structure.
Bicrystal and random polycrystalline samples were used in the simulation tasks to study the
physics behind damage in complexions with various features.

ACCOMPLISHMENTS

(1) Demonstrated that amorphous complexions improve damage tolerance
The need for clear evidence of amorphous complexions leading to increased ductility led us to
perform in situ scanning electron microscopy (SEM) tensile testing. Testing in situ allowed local
strains to be mapped to understand detailed failure modes. The ordered grain boundary specimens
exhibited failure that was typical of “normal” nanocrystalline metals, with small cross-sectional
area reductions and intense localization of plastic strain. In contrast, the sample with amorphous
complexions demonstrated roughly twice the ductility in terms of area reduction and also
experienced a homogeneous strain distribution along the gauge length, providing definitive proof
that amorphous complexions can significantly increase the ductility of nanocrystalline metals.
Amorphous complexions also aided in sintering, meaning that bulk versions of these materials can
be created and tested as well.

With amorphous complexions clearly demonstrated to improve mechanical damage tolerance, we
subsequently worked to understand how increases in complexion thickness will affect an

interface’s damage tolerance. The Ph.D. student working on this project performed small-scale
mechanical testing experiments on the samples described in the next section, which allow for
variation in complexion thickness while keeping other parameters constant, and we expect to have
these results completed by the end of the current project period. The opportunity to build a new
collaboration with Prof. Janelle Wharry of Purdue University arose as well, with her group being
interested in radiation damage in nanostructured metals. Her group observed the formation of
amorphous complexions in the Cu-Ta system, primarily at interphase boundaries in the material.
We used atomistic simulations of collision cascades near different interface types and confirmed
that amorphous complexions are efficient point defect sinks, whether at a Cu-Cu grain boundary
or at a Cu-Ta interphase boundary, with a stronger effect observed as complexion thickness is
increased. In this case, the amorphous complexions experienced a degree of chemical separation,
with clear clustering of Cu and Ta atoms rather than the near perfect mixing found in Cu-Zr.

(2) Probed the effect of transformed fraction and complexion thickness
Complexion thickness distribution and the fraction of boundaries which have transformed to
amorphous complexions refer to the complexion population present in the material, serving as
descriptors of the overall interfacial network. Controlled cooling experiments on bulk samples
with a heat sink on one side were used to induce a gradient in quenching rates, with faster cooling
resulting in thicker complexion distributions. Slow cooling allowed some of the interfaces which
are amorphous at high temperature to revert back to ordered grain boundaries. Alloy complexity
was also investigated, with the complexion thickness distributions showing that having more
elemental variety in the grain boundary region resulted in thicker amorphous complexions. The
fraction of boundaries that are a given complexion type was often an intractable experimental
problem, yet atomistic models of polycrystalline grain structures were used to provide a bridge to
understand this descriptor. Our findings showed that the measurement of a thicker amorphous
complexion distribution also means that a higher fraction of interfaces are amorphous. Hence, the
two metrics of complexion thickness and fraction are intimately coupled, with faster cooling and
increased grain boundary compositional complexity resulting in increases to both.

To demonstrate that a wide range of material systems can host amorphous complexions and
understand how alloy choice impacts complexion thickness, we also studied nanocrystalline Al-
and Fe- alloys. Three binary Al-rich alloys demonstrated varying abilities to host amorphous
complexions. Al-Ni is similar to Cu-Zr, in that amorphous complexions were found when the
alloy was rapidly quenched but not if the sample was slowly cooled. In contrast, Al-Y can host
amorphous complexions even if slowly cooled, with faster quenching freezing in thicker
complexions, while no amorphous complexions were found in Al-Mg. Preliminary data for Fe-Zr
also suggested that amorphous complexions were present and that nanocrystalline grain sizes can
be stabilized even at extremely high temperatures as a result.

(3) Showed patterning of local structure and chemistry in amorphous complexions
While most of our work was focused on complexion thickness, we were able to obtain preliminary
data on the importance of the internal structure and chemistry of amorphous complexions. We
began our study of this topic by looking at the development of structural short-range order inside
of amorphous complexions and trying to isolate the influence of grain boundary crystallography.

 3

Metallic glasses contain primarily disordered Voronoi polyhedra and the interior of an amorphous
complexion has a similar structure, but there are important deviations where the complexion meets
the grains in the amorphous-crystalline transition region. In these regions, it was common to see
ordered Voronoi polyhedra that were only slightly different than the perfect face-centered cubic
structure. Atomistic modeling showed us that transition region structure varied a great deal
between different complexions and even between the two sides of an individual complexion.
While it would seem logical that this structure was determined by the constraint from the nearest
grain, more detailed analysis showed that the entire grain-film-grain system must be considered to
understand asymmetries in the structural short-range order spatial distribution. Grain boundary
strain served as a measure of the incompatibility between the grains prior to the complexion
transition and was found to determine the ordered SSRO density difference between the two
ACTRs. Our preliminary simulations showed that these spatial patterns in SRO impacted a
complexion’s ability to absorb dislocations without nucleating cracks/voids, as it was beneficial to
have the material through the entire complexion co-deforming to accommodate the plastic strain
over a larger volume of material. Another important effect was that plasticity could be activated
in the neighboring grain under certain conditions, reducing strain localization within the
complexion.

Preliminary high resolution transmission electron microscopy results showed that Nb and Ti in a
Cu-Zr-Nb-Ti alloy had their highest concentrations at the amorphous-crystalline transition regions.
We found a similar maximum local enrichment of Ni at the ACTRs in nanocrystalline Al-Ni and
Al-Ni-Y. It was clear that local chemical composition was coupled to complexion structure,
promising a knob through which SSRO may be manipulated.

DISSEMINATION OF SCIENTIFIC RESULTS
The research results from this project have been distributed to the community in the form of invited
talks and contributed presentations, as well as well as peer-reviewed journal articles. 20 invited
talks and 10 contributed presentations were given, spread between conferences, universities, and
national laboratories. 10 journal articles were published during this reporting period, including
works appearing in top material science and engineering journals such as Acta Materialia. The
details of all presentations and journal articles are included at the end of this report. Copies of all
journal articles resulting from this project have been uploaded to the arXiv.org repository, with
PDF copies of the final articles also posted on the PI’s website.

TRAINING AND PROFESSIONAL DEVELOPMENT
The project supported the effort of three Ph.D. students, as well as one postdoctoral scholar who
received partial support. All trainees were mentored in the fields of Materials Science and Solid
Mechanics, giving them multi-disciplinary skills that will help them in the workplace. The mixture
of experimental, simulation, and theory found in this project also exposed all trainees to a broad
range of skillsets to give them a deep appreciation for the field. Each met weekly with the PI to
discuss research goals and career ambitions, while also presenting in front of the research group
quarterly to develop presentation skills. Professional development included conference and
workshop attendance, as well as career mentoring and counseling from the PI.
