Transmembrane mucin 21 drives lung adenocarcinoma growth by suppressing retinoic acid receptor β signaling
Authors: Weibin Lv, Xiang Huang, Xiangwei Cheng, Yifan Wang, Linfeng Wu, Yiping Zhu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLung cancer remains the most prevalent and lethal malignancy worldwide, with non-small cell lung cancer accounting for >85% of cases and lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) representing a predominant histological subtype. LUAD exhibits marked molecular heterogeneity, early dissemination, and a strong tendency for distant metastasis, but the key molecular circuits driving its progression remain unclear. This study aimed to elucidate molecular markers governing the growth of LUAD, along with their underlying mechanisms.MethodsMucin (MUC)21 expression was examined in LUAD patient tissues (16 paired fresh samples and 35 paraffin‑embedded cases) and cell lines (BEAS-2B, A549, NCI‑H838, NCI‑H1395) by qPCR, western blot, and immunohistochemistry. Stable knockdown (KD) and overexpression (OE) of MUC21 were established in LUAD cells to assess proliferation (CCK‑8, EdU), migration (wound healing), and invasion (Matrigel Transwell) in vitro. Xenograft models in nude mice were used to evaluate tumor growth in vivo. Mechanistic involvement of retinoic acid receptor β (RARβ) was tested via rescue experiments with RARβ co‑silencing.ResultsMUC21 was significantly upregulated in LUAD tissues and cell lines, with membrane‑localized enrichment in tumors. High MUC21 expression correlated with poorer progression‑free survival in patients. Functionally, MUC21 OE promoted cell proliferation, migration, and invasion, while MUC21 KD suppressed these malignant traits. In the xenograft model, MUC21 OE accelerated tumor growth and increased the Ki-67 proliferation index, whereas its silencing restrained tumor growth and enhanced apoptosis. Mechanistically, dysregulated MUC21 influenced gene pathways associated with retinoic acid signaling. MUC21 negatively correlated with the nuclear receptor RARβ, with MUC21 KD increasing RARβ messenger ribonucleic acid (mRNA) and protein levels, coinciding with reduced proliferation and invasiveness, and MUC21 OE repressing RARβ expression. Notably, RARβ co-silencing partially rescued MUC21-KD-induced suppression of LUAD proliferation, migration, and invasion, positioning RARβ as a key downstream mediator.ConclusionsThese findings present the MUC21-RARβ regulatory axis as a critical regulator of LUAD malignant progression and highlight the therapeutic potential of MUC21, offering novel insights and linking MUC-driven membrane signaling with RARβ-associated nuclear differentiation programs.Keywords: Lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD), mucin 21 (MUC21), retinoic acid receptor β (RARβ), cell proliferation, tumor growth

Highlight box

Key findings

• Mucin 21 (MUC21) promotes lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) proliferation, migration, and invasion by negatively regulating retinoic acid receptor β (RARβ) expression.

What is known and what is new?

• Transmembrane mucins (e.g., MUC1, MUC4) are known to promote oncogenic signaling, and MUC21 upregulation has been reported in several cancers.

• MUC21 is highly expressed in LUAD and promotes tumor malignant progression.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• MUC21 is a membrane‑enriched oncoprotein whose blockade restores RARβ‑mediated tumor suppression, offering a rationale for developing anti‑MUC21 therapies. Clinically, evaluation of MUC21 inhibitors, alone or with differentiation agents, is warranted.

Introduction

Lung cancer ranks among the leading causes of cancer incidence and mortality worldwide, imposing a major public health burden (1,2). Histologically, non-small cell lung cancer comprises over 85% of cases, with lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) as the most prevalent subtype (40–50%) (3,4). Although targeted therapies and immune checkpoint inhibitors have improved overall survival in some patients, the 5-year survival rate for LUAD remains <20% (5), largely due to substantial molecular heterogeneity, early metastatic dissemination, and therapy resistance, which drive relapse and treatment failure (6,7). These characteristics (molecular heterogeneity, early dissemination, and distant metastasis) contribute to acquired resistance to targeted therapies, early relapse, and a 5-year survival rate below 20%, underscoring an urgent need to identify key molecular drivers of LUAD progression. Hence, identifying molecular drivers of LUAD initiation and progression is essential for mechanistic understanding and therapeutic development.

Mucins (MUCs), classified as secreted or transmembrane types, are high-molecular-weight glycoproteins that lubricate and protect epithelial surfaces (8,9). Reportedly, dysregulated MUC expression and glycosylation can promote tumorigenesis and cancer progression. For instance, transmembrane MUCs, such as MUC1 and MUC4, are frequently overexpressed in epithelial cancers and regulate oncogenic signaling (10-12). Over 90% of human breast tumors exhibit MUC1 overexpression (OE), which disrupts cell polarity and glycosylation to facilitate metastasis by impairing cell-cell adhesion and immune recognition (13). Furthermore, MUC1 interacts with oncogenic proteins such as erythroblastic oncogene B (ErbB) receptors and β-catenin to promote proliferation and inhibit apoptosis (14) Similarly, MUC4 engages ErbB2/human epidermal growth factor (EGF) receptor (HER)2 and HER3 via EGF-like domains, activating downstream mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK), Janus kinase (JNK), and signal transducer and activator of transcription (STAT) pathways to enhance proliferation, motility, and metastasis (12,15). Additionally, MUC16 has been implicated in lung cancer dissemination through interleukin (IL)6/IL6 receptor-mediated inflammatory signaling that promotes migration and invasion (16). Collectively, these studies highlight the malignancy-promoting activity of transmembrane MUCs, which act both as physical modulators of adhesion and functional organizers of growth and survival signaling.

In contrast, the role of MUC21 remains insufficiently explored. MUC21 was first reported in 2007 in several normal tissues, including the lungs and thymus (17). This heavily O-glycosylated transmembrane protein possesses a large extracellular domain with tandem repeats bearing clustered sugar chains, which may generate steric hindrance at the cell surface, thereby altering cell-cell and cell-matrix interactions to promote invasion. Recently, MUC21 upregulation has been associated with aggressive features in multiple cancers. For example, in glioblastoma, MUC21 upregulation correlates with cancer recurrence, with its silencing reducing viability, motility, proliferation markers, and matrix-degrading enzymes, while its OE activates pro-tumor signaling involving STAT3/protein kinase B (AKT) to drive invasion and growth (18,19). Furthermore, in pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma, high MUC21 expression independently predicts poor prognosis and is associated with epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) and perineural invasion via JNK activation (20). Although these studies suggest a tumor-promoting role of MUC21, direct functional evidence in LUAD remains limited.

A study involving 121 cases of LUAD indicated that MUC21 expression positively correlates with the “incohesive” tumor growth pattern. In certain tumors with reduced cell-cell adhesion, MUC21 levels in non-adherent regions are slightly higher than in adherent regions (21). Although indicative, these observations do not clarify the precise role of MUC21 in driving LUAD progression or the associated downstream mechanisms.

Retinoic acid receptor β (RARβ), a ligand-dependent nuclear receptor, transduces retinoic acid signals at the transcriptional level to regulate differentiation, growth arrest, and apoptosis (22,23). RARβ is frequently downregulated or functionally silenced in multiple cancers, including lung cancer, leading to loss of differentiation and enhanced proliferation (24). Given the central role of differentiation pathways in suppressing malignancy, identifying upstream regulators of RARβ is important. Based on this, it was hypothesized that MUC21 interacts with RARβ to influence LUAD growth.

Therefore, this study aimed to systematically evaluate the clinical relevance and function of MUC21 in LUAD using patient specimens, LUAD cell line models, and in vivo xenograft models. Notably, the MUC21-RARβ regulatory axis and the role of RARβ as a downstream effector were investigated in MUC21-mediated phenotypes. The findings position MUC21 as a driver of LUAD proliferation and metastatic characteristics, with the MUC21-RARβ axis mechanistically linking surface MUC signaling to nuclear differentiation programs. We present this article in accordance with the MDAR and ARRIVE reporting checklists (available at https://jtd.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/jtd-2026-0614/rc).

Methods

Cell culture

Human LUAD cell lines A549, NCI-H838, NCI-H1395, and immortalized normal bronchial epithelial cells BEAS-2B were obtained from the Cell Bank/Stem Cell Bank, Chinese Academy of Sciences (Shanghai, China). Cells were routinely cultured in Roswell Park Memorial Institute-1640 medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) (Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin, and maintained at 37 ℃ in a humidified incubator under 5% CO2.

Stable cell line construction

For MUC21 knockdown (KD), A549 and NCI-H838 cells were infected at multiplicities of infection of 70 and 90, respectively, using lentivirus carrying MUC21-targeting short hairpin ribonucleic acid (shRNA) (Cyagen, Suzhou, China, 394263; NCBI RefSeq NM_001010909.5). Lentivirus with non-targeting shRNA served as the negative control (shNC). At 48 h post-infection, cells were selected with 2 µg/mL puromycin for 10 d to establish stable clones. For rescue experiments, RARβ-targeting shRNA (TSINGKE, Beijing, China; NCBI RefSeq NM_000965.5) was introduced into stable MUC21-KD NCI-H838 cells under identical selection conditions, yielding shMUC21 + shRARβ double-KD cells; non-targeting shRNA in stable MUC21-KD NCI-H838 cells served as the control. For MUC21 OE, cells were transfected with a pcDNA3.1 vector carrying full-length human MUC21 complementary DNA (cDNA), and an empty vector was used as the control. After 48 h post-transfection, selection was performed with 2 µg/mL puromycin for 2 weeks. KD and OE efficiencies were verified by western blotting and quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR).

Clinical tissue samples

In total, 16 pairs of LUAD tumors and matched adjacent normal lung tissues, along with 35 paraffin-embedded LUAD samples, were collected from patients who did not undergo preoperative radiotherapy or chemotherapy at the First Affiliated Hospital of Wannan Medical College. Ethical approval was obtained from the Institutional Review Board of Wannan Medical College (approval No. 2020-8), and all patients provided written informed consent. This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC)

Paraffin sections (5 µm) from 35 LUAD cases and paired adjacent tissues were analyzed using IHC. Following deparaffinization in xylene, samples were rehydrated through graded ethanol, subjected to microwave antigen retrieval in EDTA buffer (15 min), treated with 3% hydrogen peroxide, and blocked with 5% bovine serum albumin. After blocking, sections were incubated overnight at 4 ℃ with anti-MUC21 primary antibody (1:800; Thermo Fisher, Waltham, USA, Catalog No. PA5-147254). The next day, sections were incubated with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibody (1:50) for 30 min, followed by 3,3’-diaminobenzidine (DAB) development and hematoxylin counterstaining. MUC21 expression was quantified using an immunoreactive score combining staining intensity and positive staining proportion, with scores of 0–6 and 7–12 indicating low and high expression, respectively.

For nude mouse xenograft IHC, paraffin-embedded sections were processed as described above. After blocking, sections were incubated overnight at 4 ℃ with anti-MUC21 (1:800; Thermo Fisher, Waltham, USA, Catalog No. PA5-147254) and anti-Ki-67 antibodies (1:500; Abin, Shanghai, China, Catalog No. HW621014), followed by incubation with HRP-conjugated secondary antibody (30 min) and DAB chromogenic detection. Ki-67-positive nuclei were quantified in multiple random fields to assess tumor proliferation and validate the effects of MUC21 KD or OE.

Terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase deoxyuridine triphosphate (dUTP) nick-end labeling (TUNEL)

Apoptosis in xenograft sections was evaluated using the CF488 TUNEL Cell Apoptosis Detection Kit (Servicebio, Wuhan, China, Catalog No. G1504-50T). Following deparaffinization and rehydration, sections were treated with proteinase K and incubated with CF488-dUTP-labeled reaction solution at 37 ℃ for 60 min in the dark. After washing, nuclei were counterstained with 4’,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole, and slides were mounted with anti-fade medium. Images from random fields were analyzed to quantify apoptosis levels.

RNA extraction, reverse transcription, and real-time qPCR

Cell- or tissue-derived total ribonucleic acid (RNA) was isolated using the TransZol Up Plus RNA Kit (TransGen Biotech, Beijing, China), following the manufacturer’s protocol, and purity was assessed based on the A260/A280 ratio. After DNase I treatment, cDNA was synthesized using the TransScript kit (TransGen Biotech, Beijing, China), and qPCR was conducted with SYBR Green Premix Ex Taq (TransGen Biotech, Beijing, China) on a Roche LightCycler 480 II system (Gentier 96R TianLong, Xi’an, China). The cycling program was set as follows: 95 ℃ for 30 s, followed by 40 cycles at 95 ℃ for 5 s and 60 ℃ for 30 s. β-actin was used as the internal control, and relative expression was calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method. Primer sequences were as follows: MUC21 forward 5'-GAATGCACACAACTTCCCATAGT-3', reverse 5'-GGCTATCGAGGATACTGGTCTC-3'; RARβ forward 5'-GGTTTCACTGGCTTGACCATCG-3', reverse 5'-CCGTCTGAGAAAGTCATGGTGTC-3'; and β-actin forward 5'-CACCATTGGCAATGAGCGGTTC-3', reverse 5'-AGGTCTTTGCGGATGTCCACGT-3'.

Protein extraction and western blotting

Cells or frozen tissues were lysed on ice using radioimmunoprecipitation assay buffer containing phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride and protease inhibitors. Lysates were centrifuged at 12,000 ×g for 15 min, and supernatants were collected. Protein concentrations were determined using a bicinchoninic acid assay. Proteins (40 µg) were separated by sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (10% gel), transferred onto polyvinylidene difluoride membranes, and blocked with rapid blocking solution for 15 min. Membranes were incubated overnight at 4 ℃ with primary antibodies according to the manufacturer’s instructions, followed by incubation with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (90 min, room temperature, 25 ℃). Bands were visualized using electrochemiluminescence detection and quantified with ImageJ. The following primary antibodies were used: anti-MUC21 (1:1,000; Immunoway, Suzhou, China, Catalog No. YN3177), anti-RARβ (1:1,000; Abin, Shanghai, China, Catalog No. HY385104), and anti-glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (1:10,000; Immunoway, Suzhou, China, Catalog No. YM33109).

Cell-Counting Kit (CCK)-8 assay

Cells were seeded in 96-well plates (5×103 cells/well), and 10 µL/well CCK-8 reagent (Dojindo, Shanghai, China) was added at 0, 24, 48, and 72 h. After a 2-h incubation at 37 ℃, absorbance was measured at 450 nm. Each group contained six replicates, and experiments were repeated at least three times.

Cell scratch healing assay

Cells were seeded in six-well plates to reach 90% confluence. A vertical wound was created using a 200-µL pipette tip, and detached cells were removed by washing with phosphate-buffered saline. Serum-free medium was added, and images were captured at 0, 24, and 48 h. Wound healing was quantified as the percentage of closed area using ImageJ, with experiments repeated at least three times.

Transwell invasion assay

Invasiveness was evaluated using Transwell chambers (8 µm pores; Corning). The upper chamber was coated with Matrigel (1:8 dilution) and allowed to polymerize at 37 ℃ for 2 h. Cells (8×104) in serum-free medium were seeded into the upper chamber, with 20% FBS-supplemented medium placed in the lower chamber as a chemoattractant. After 24 h, non-invading cells were removed, and invading cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, stained with 0.1% crystal violet, and counted in multiple random fields. All experiments were conducted at least three times.

5-ethynyl-2’-deoxyuridine (EdU) incorporation experiment

DNA synthesis was assessed using an EdU incorporation kit (Abbkine, Wuhan, China). Cells seeded in 48-well plates were cultured to an appropriate density and incubated with EdU working solution for 2 h, then fixed, permeabilized, and stained with Apollo and Hoechst nuclear stain. After fluorescence imaging, the percentage of EdU-positive cells was quantified in random fields to indicate DNA replication activity and proliferation.

Nude mouse xenograft model

All procedures were approved by the Experimental Animal Welfare and Ethics Committee of Wannan Medical College (approval No. WNMC-AWE-2025329) and were performed in compliance with institutional guidelines for the care and use of laboratory animals. All experimental mice were housed in a specific pathogen-free (SPF) animal facility on the fifth floor of the Yifu Science and Technology Building, Binjiang Campus, Wannan Medical College. The indoor air was filtered through high-efficiency particulate air (HEPA) filters and maintained under continuous positive pressure to ensure a clean and well-controlled environment. The ambient temperature was maintained at 20–26 ℃, with relative humidity controlled between 40% and 70%. A 12 h light/12 h dark cycle was applied to simulate a natural circadian rhythm. Throughout the housing period, mice were provided with sterile food and water ad libitum.Male BALB/c nude mice (4–6 weeks old; GemPharmatech Co., Ltd.) were subcutaneously injected with 1×107 control, MUC21-KD, or MUC21-OE NCI-H838 cells (n=5/group) and grouped accordingly. Tumor dimensions, including length (L) and width (W), were measured weekly from week 2 using calipers, and volume was calculated as V = 0.5×L×W2. At 8 weeks, mice were euthanized, and tumors were excised, weighed, and paraffin-embedded for TUNEL, hematoxylin-eosin staining, and MUC21/Ki-67 IHC analyses.

Statistical analyses

Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation. Two-group comparisons were performed using Student’s t-test, and multi-group comparisons were conducted using one- or two-way analysis of variance, followed by Tukey’s post hoc test. Kaplan-Meier survival curves were analyzed using log-rank tests. Analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 10, with P<0.05 considered statistically significant.

Results

MUC21 is overexpressed in LUAD tissues and cell lines

LUAD transcriptomic datasets revealed pronounced MUC21 upregulation in tumors compared with normal lung tissues (Figure 1A). Consistently, independent Timer2.0 analyses confirmed elevated MUC21 levels in LUAD relative to normal controls (Figure 1B). GEO dataset-based Kaplan-Meier survival analysis further showed that patients with high MUC21 expression had significantly poorer overall survival than those with low MUC21 expression in LUAD, with a hazard ratio of 1.62 [95% confidence interval (CI): 1.11–2.32, P=0.032] (Figure 1C). In patient-derived paired clinical samples (16 matched LUAD specimens), qPCR showed significantly increased MUC21 mRNA levels in tumors compared with adjacent non-tumor tissues (Figure 1D), which was further supported by western blot results showing increased MUC21 protein expression (Figure 1E).

Spatial localization and clinical associations were evaluated through IHC analysis of paraffin-embedded sections from 35 patients with LUAD. The results demonstrated weak or absent MUC21 staining in normal bronchial epithelium, whereas prominent membrane-associated positivity was observed in tumor cells (Figure 1F), consistent with the expected localization of a transmembrane MUC. Semi-quantitative scoring confirmed significantly higher MUC21 expression in tumors relative to adjacent tissues. Notably, follow-up data from the 35 cases indicated that patients with low MUC21 expression exhibited better progression-free survival (PFS) than those with high expression (Figure 1G).

Among LUAD cell lines, A549, NCI-H838, and NCI-H1395 cells showed markedly higher MUC21 mRNA and protein levels than immortalized bronchial epithelial BEAS-2B cells (Figure 1H,1I). Consequently, A549 and NCI-H838 were selected for subsequent functional experiments. Overall, these findings from public datasets, clinical samples, and LUAD cell lines indicate that MUC21 is aberrantly upregulated in LUAD, supporting its potential relevance in tumor biology.

Modulating MUC21 expression alters LUAD cell proliferation

To examine the functional role of MUC21, stable MUC21 KD (shMUC21) and OE (OE-MUC21) models were generated in A549 and NCI-H838 cells, with shNC and empty vector (VEC) serving as controls. qPCR and western blotting confirmed efficient MUC21 downregulation in shMUC21 cells and several-fold increases in OE-MUC21 cells in both cell lines (Figure 2A-2D).

CCK-8 assays demonstrated that shMUC21 significantly reduced proliferative capacity in A549 cells over time (Figure 2E), whereas OE-MUC21 enhanced cell growth compared with vector controls (Figure 2F). Similar patterns were observed in NCI-H838 cells (Figure 2G,2H), supporting the tumor growth-promoting role of MUC21 across multiple LUAD models. EdU incorporation assays assessing DNA synthesis in both A549 and NCI-H838 cells further validated these effects, with MUC21 OE increasing the proportion of EdU-positive nuclei, whereas MUC21 KD reduced them in both cell lines (Figure 3A,3B). Overall, these findings indicate that MUC21 promotes cell cycle progression and proliferation in LUAD cells.

MUC21 promotes migration and invasion of LUAD cells

Considering the clinical association between MUC21 and aggressive tumor behavior, its effects on cancer cell motility and invasiveness were evaluated. Wound healing assays showed that MUC21 KD delayed wound closure in A549 cells (Figure 4A), whereas MUC21 OE accelerated migration, achieving near-complete closure by 48 h (Figure 4B). Quantitatively, the OE group covered approximately 30% of the wound area by 24 h compared with the KD group (approximately 10%). Consistently, by 48 h, the OE group exhibited approximately 60% closure, whereas the KD group remained below 40%. Comparable findings were obtained in NCI-H838 cells (Figures 4C,4D), indicating that MUC21 consistently enhances the migratory capacity of LUAD cells.

In NCI-H838 cells, shMUC21 slowed migration relative to shNC (C), whereas OE-MUC21 enhanced migration compared with VEC (D). (E-H) Matrigel Transwell invasion assays show reduced invasiveness following MUC21 KD in A549 (E) and NCI-H838 (F) cells, and increased invasion with OE-MUC21 in A549 (G) and NCI-H838 (H). Furthermore, Matrigel Transwell assays demonstrated that MUC21 KD reduced invasion, resulting in fewer invasive cells (Figure 4E,4F). In contrast, MUC21 OE increased the number of invading cells in both A549 and NCI-H838 lines, forming dense clusters on the underside of the membrane (Figure 4G,4H). Collectively, these results establish MUC21 as a promoter of migration and three-dimensional invasion, supporting its role in metastatic progression.

MUC21 influences LUAD progression through the RARβ pathway

To investigate whether RARβ directly regulates the transcription of MUC21, bioinformatics prediction was performed using the JASPAR database (https://jaspar.elixir.no/). The promoter region of the human MUC21 gene was defined as the region spanning 2000 bp upstream to 500 bp downstream of the transcription start site. The transcription start site coordinates were obtained from the UCSC Genome Browser (GRCh38/hg38 version), and the corresponding genomic sequence was downloaded in FASTA format. This sequence was then analyzed using the JASPAR SCAN tool to predict transcription factor binding sites (Figure 5A). Specifically, the position frequency matrix for RARβ (Matrix ID: MA1552.2), derived from HT-SELEX experimental data, was applied to represent the DNA-binding preference of human RARβ (UniProt ID: P10826). The matrix length is 13 bp, and a relative score threshold of ≥0.85 was used to ensure identification of high-confidence binding sites. The results revealed a highly conserved putative RARβ-binding motif within the MUC21 promoter region, suggesting potential transcriptional regulation between them. Based on this, MUC21–RARβ expression and regulatory association were further examined in vitro. In NCI-H838 and A549 cells, MUC21 KD markedly increased RARβ expression, whereas MUC21 OE significantly reduced RARβ protein levels (Figure 5B-5E), indicating that MUC21 modulates the RARβ pathway.

To determine whether RARβ functions as a critical downstream effector mediating MUC21 biological activity, rescue experiments were conducted. The following groups were established: stable MUC21 KD combined with RARβ silencing (shMUC21 + shRARβ), RARβ-silenced cells (shRARβ), MUC21 KD alone (shMUC21), and negative control cells (Control). In NCI-H838 and A549 cells, the elevated RARβ expression induced by MUC21 depletion was effectively reversed upon co-silencing of RARβ, whereas RARβ KD alone also significantly reduced its expression. RARβ expression was assessed at both mRNA and protein levels using qPCR and western blotting. qPCR analysis showed that, in NCI-H838 and A549 cells, compared with the MUC21 single-KD group (shMUC21), the increased RARβ transcript induced by MUC21 depletion was significantly reversed in the shMUC21+shRARβ group, with a marked reduction in RARβ mRNA levels, while RARβ mRNA was also significantly decreased in the shRARβ group relative to the control group. Consistently, western blot analysis further confirmed effective downregulation of RARβ at the protein level across all experimental groups (Figure 5F-5M).

CCK-8 proliferation assays demonstrated that, in NCI-H838 and A549 cells, RARβ KD significantly enhanced cell growth, whereas MUC21 KD markedly inhibited proliferation (P<0.0001; Figure 6A,6B). Importantly, RARβ co-KD (shMUC21 + shRARβ) substantially restored proliferation in MUC21-KD cells, indicating that the anti-proliferative effect of MUC21 KD largely depends on RARβ upregulation.

The wound healing assay further showed that, in NCI-H838 and A549 cells, RARβ KD enhanced migratory capacity, with higher wound closure rates at 24 and 48 h, whereas MUC21 KD significantly suppressed migration (Figure 6C,6D). Co-silencing of RARβ restored migratory ability in MUC21-deficient cells.

The role of RARβ in invasive behavior was evaluated using a Matrigel-coated Transwell assay. In NCI-H838 and A549 cells, RARβ KD significantly increased invasion, whereas MUC21 KD reduced invasive capacity (Figure 6E,6F). Notably, this inhibitory effect was reversed in the shMUC21 + shRARβ group, in which the number of invading cells returned to levels comparable with those of the control group.

Collectively, these findings identify RARβ as a key downstream mediator of MUC21. MUC21 KD elevates RARβ to suppress cell proliferation, migration, and invasion, whereas RARβ co-silencing reverses these effects. These results underscore the functional importance of the MUC21–RARβ axis in regulating malignant behaviors of LUAD cells.

MUC21 enhances tumor growth in vivo

To determine whether the pro-tumor role of MUC21 extends beyond in vitro systems, nude mouse subcutaneous xenograft models were established by injecting NCI-H838 cells with MUC21 KD, MUC21 OE, or corresponding controls (NC) (n=5 mice/group). Tumor growth was monitored over 8 weeks (Figure 7A,7B), and tumor volumes were measured weekly starting from week 2 post-injection. Growth curves diverged clearly by week 4, and by week 6, MUC21 OE tumors averaged approximately 400 mm3, exceeding the control group (KD tumor size: approximately 170 mm3; control: approximately 230 mm3). At the endpoint (week 8), tumor volumes reached the following approximate sizes: OE tumors, 2000 mm3; controls, 1000 mm3; and KD tumors, 600 mm3 (Figure 7C).

Histopathological analyses further supported these growth differences. IHC revealed stronger Ki-67 staining in OE tumors and reduced Ki-67 in KD tumors compared with controls. TUNEL staining demonstrated decreased apoptosis in OE tumors and increased apoptosis in KD tumors (Figure 7D). Overall, these findings indicate that MUC21 accelerates tumor growth in vivo by enhancing proliferation and suppressing apoptosis, consistent with its in vitro effects.

Discussion

Extensive progress in cancer research over recent years has identified the MUC family as a key contributor to tumor malignancy and progression. Reportedly, aberrant MUC expression and altered glycosylation remodel cell-cell interactions, influence receptor signaling, and promote invasive phenotypes. Transmembrane MUCs, in particular, act as multifunctional regulators by combining physical effects at the cell surface with active participation in signaling networks.

MUC1, a paradigmatic example, functions as a signaling scaffold, with its intracellular domain associating with EGF receptor/HER2 and β-catenin, activating phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase (PI3K)/AKT, MAPK, and STAT3 pathways. This facilitates β-catenin nuclear translocation and activation of Wnt target genes, enhancing proliferation, EMT, and therapy resistance (14,25-27). Similarly, MUC4 drives malignant progression through its EGF-like extracellular domain, which promotes ErbB receptor interactions and sustains MAPK and PI3K/AKT signaling, thereby reinforcing growth and metastatic behavior (12,28,29). Notably, MUC16 contributes to tumor adhesion and dissemination through multiple interactions (e.g., with mesothelin) and is associated with increased proliferation and migration via signaling axes such as JAK2/STAT3 (30-34). Secreted MUCs have been reported to influence cancer progression by modulating the tumor microenvironment. For instance, MUC5AC OE in gastrointestinal malignancies forms barriers that limit drug diffusion and regulate immune infiltration (35-38), whereas MUC6 functions in a context-dependent manner as a differentiation marker in certain settings while potentially supporting invasiveness in others (39,40). These examples underscore both broad and tissue-specific effects of MUCs on tumor behavior.

MUC21, a highly O-glycosylated transmembrane MUC, likely modulates adhesion and receptor accessibility at the tumor cell surface. Previous studies increasingly associate its dysregulation with aggressive disease features, including enhanced tumor growth, motility, and resistance to apoptosis, with reviews further emphasizing the link between abnormal MUC glycosylation and invasion, metastasis, and immune escape (19,41,42). However, despite such clinical associations in lung cancer, the mechanistic role of MUC21 in LUAD remains unclear.

In this study, multiple findings indicate that MUC21 is pathologically elevated in LUAD and has clinical relevance. The results showed increased MUC21 mRNA and protein levels in paired patient samples, with membrane-localized enrichment in tumor tissues. Moreover, MUC21 OE correlated with poorer PFS in the clinical cohort, indicating its prognostic significance. In cell-based models, MUC21 upregulation consistently enhanced proliferation (CCK-8 and EdU assays), migration (scratch assay), and invasion (Transwell assay), whereas its depletion impaired these malignant traits. Collectively, these findings position MUC21 as an active driver of LUAD progression rather than a passive biomarker.

Importantly, this study establishes a mechanistic link between MUC21 and retinoic acid signaling via RARβ, a ligand-dependent nuclear receptor involved in transcriptional programs regulating differentiation, cell cycle arrest, and apoptosis (43-47). RARβ downregulation is frequently implicated in tumorigenesis, and in lung cancer, RARβ is often silenced by promoter methylation, insufficient ligand availability, or microenvironmental suppression (46,48,49). The present results demonstrate a negative correlation between MUC21 and RARβ, with MUC21 OE suppressing RARβ and MUC21 KD elevating its levels. Genetic rescue experiments confirmed functional causality, and MUC21 silencing-induced defects in growth and invasion were substantially reversed following RARβ co-KD, indicating that RARβ upregulation mediates the anti-tumor effects of MUC21 depletion.

These findings support a model in which MUC21 promotes an aggressive, dedifferentiated state by suppressing RARβ-dependent differentiation signaling. Elevated MUC21 levels inhibit differentiation programs, thereby enabling proliferative and invasive phenotypes. Although this study established a functional MUC21-RARβ axis, the broader regulatory network linking transmembrane MUC21 to RARβ suppression remains to be clarified. In addition to potential upstream regulators and parallel pathways affecting EMT and retinoic acid responsiveness, the intermediate mechanisms by which MUC21 downregulates RARβ require further investigation. One plausible explanation is that MUC21 activates oncogenic signaling cascades, such as STAT3, AKT, or MAPK, which may transcriptionally repress RARβ. Alternatively, MUC21 may reduce RARβ protein stability through ubiquitin–proteasome-mediated degradation (20,50). Future studies using co-immunoprecipitation, ubiquitination assays, and pathway-specific inhibitors will be needed to distinguish direct from indirect regulatory mechanisms and to identify the signaling intermediates connecting MUC21 to RARβ suppression.

From a translational perspective, the MUC21-RARβ axis provides a rationale for therapeutic strategies aimed at restoring differentiation signaling. Retinoic acid-based therapies have shown limited efficacy in lung cancer, partly due to reduced or absent RARβ expression (48,51). The present findings suggest that targeting MUC21 via antibodies, small molecules, or other inhibitors may relieve RARβ suppression, thereby reactivating retinoic acid signaling and re-sensitizing tumors to differentiation cues. This study establishes the functional relevance of the MUC21-RARβ axis in LUAD; however, the upstream drivers of MUC21 dysregulation and its broader signaling interactions remain insufficiently characterized. For instance, the MUC21 promoter region contains putative binding sites for NF-κB, STAT3, and AP-1, suggesting that inflammatory signals or oncogenic transcription factors could drive MUC21 upregulation in LUAD (18,52). Moreover, epigenetic alterations such as promoter hypomethylation cannot be excluded (53). Given the established roles of transmembrane mucins in activating PI3K/AKT, MAPK, and WNT/β-catenin pathways, MUC21 may promote LUAD progression not only via RARβ suppression but also through these parallel oncogenic networks (18,50). Transcriptomic or phosphoproteomic profiling may help delineate the full spectrum of MUC21-regulated pathways (18,50,54). In addition, the use of a limited number of cell lines and a subcutaneous xenograft model may not fully capture LUAD heterogeneity. Future studies employing diverse preclinical models and integrated multi-omics approaches will be essential to clarify the regulatory landscape of MUC21 and to evaluate the therapeutic potential of targeting this axis in LUAD.

This study has several limitations. First, the sample size was relatively small, including only 35 paraffin-embedded LUAD specimens and 16 fresh tumor samples. Although such sample sizes are common in clinical studies, the limited number of samples may reduce statistical power, particularly for detecting subtle effects or subgroup differences. In addition, due to Another limitation is that we did not establish an in vivo metastasis model; therefore, the observed effects on migration and invasion are currently supported only by in vitro experiments. Future studies using tail vein injection or orthotopic models are warranted to confirm the metastatic role of MUC21 in LUAD. constraints in sample selection, the clinical characteristics of these patients may differ from those of other populations, potentially influencing the observed tumor biological behavior. Finally, given the limited sample size, multivariate analyses were not performed, and potential confounding factors, such as smoking history and key gene mutations (e.g., EGFR and KRAS), were not fully controlled. Therefore, future studies are required to expand the sample size and to further validate the MUC21–RARβ regulatory axis through in vivo models and more comprehensive mechanistic analyses.

Conclusions

The findings of this study demonstrate that aberrantly upregulated MUC21 promotes malignant progression of LUAD by enhancing proliferation, migration, and invasion capacity. MUC21 OE correlates with poorer patient outcomes, highlighting its potential as a prognostic biomarker and therapeutic target. Furthermore, MUC21 expression is low in normal lung tissue but enriched on tumor cell surfaces, making it an attractive therapeutic target. Mechanistically, RARβ was identified as a key downstream mediator of MUC21-driven phenotypes, suggesting that MUC21 promotes LUAD aggressiveness by suppressing RARβ-dependent differentiation signaling. Altogether, these findings define the MUC21-RARβ regulatory axis as a critical molecular link between MUC-mediated surface regulation and nuclear transcriptional programs, providing a conceptual and experimental basis for targeting this pathway in LUAD management.
