Efficacy and safety of MEK inhibitors for NF1-associated symptomatic, inoperable plexiform neurofibromas: A systematic review and meta-analysis

Sudarshawn Damodharan, James Tonsgard

ABSTRACT
Plexiform neurofibromas arise in up to half of people with neurofibromatosis type 1 (NF1), causing pain, disfigurement and functional loss. Loss of neurofibromin releases RAS–MAPK signaling, making MEK1/2 a rational target. Two agents are now approved in the United States, the adult indication only since 2025. We evaluated the efficacy and safety of MEK inhibitor monotherapy in NF1-associated symptomatic, inoperable plexiform neurofibroma across children and adults. We searched six databases and registries from inception to 10 July 2026, reporting per PRISMA 2020. The primary outcome was objective response rate (≥20% tumor volume reduction, REiNS criteria). Two reviewers screened, extracted and appraised studies (RoB 2, ROBINS-I); certainty was rated with GRADE. Proportions were pooled using random-effects models after Freeman–Tukey transformation, with subgrouping by age. Eleven reports of nine studies (528 participants) met eligibility, covering selumetinib, mirdametinib, trametinib and binimetinib. The pooled descriptive response rate was 53.7% (95% CI 40.5–66.6; I2 = 84.0%) and varied by age: 62.9% in children versus 40.8% in adults. Because eight of the nine strata were single-arm, these are descriptive pooled proportions rather than controlled treatment effects. Adult heterogeneity was driven almost entirely by KOMET, the only randomized trial (20% versus 5% with placebo, p = 0.011); excluding it, the adult estimate was 49.0%. Responses were partial but usually durable, with improvements in pain and quality of life. Toxicity was mostly low grade, with uncommon cardiac and ocular events. MEK inhibition shrinks symptomatic inoperable plexiform neurofibromas in children and adults and is now supported by randomized evidence, although pooled estimates represent a weighted average across four pharmacologically distinct agents rather than a single class effect. Benefit is partial and depends on continued treatment. Optimal duration, comparative effectiveness and any effect on malignant transformation remain undefined.

Introduction Neurofibromatosis type 1 (NF1) affects roughly 1 in 2,500 to 3,000 births and is inherited in an autosomal dominant pattern, although close to half of cases arise de novo [ 1 , 2 ]. Diagnosis rests on established clinical and molecular criteria [ 3 ]. The NF1 gene at 17q11.2 encodes neurofibromin, a GTPase-activating protein that restrains RAS. When neurofibromin is lost, signaling proceeds unchecked through RAF, MEK and ERK, and cells that would normally stop dividing continue to proliferate [ 1 , 4 ]. Plexiform neurofibromas are among the most feared complications of the disease. They develop in up to half of those affected, often congenitally, and grow fastest during early childhood before slowing in adult life [ 5 , 6 ]. Because they track along nerve fascicles and wrap around vessels, the airway and the spinal cord, they rarely present as discrete masses that a surgeon can remove. Their morbidity accumulates over time: chronic pain, disfigurement, motor and sensory deficits, airway or bladder compromise, and, in a minority of patients, transformation to malignant peripheral nerve sheath tumor (MPNST), a lifetime risk of 8–13% and the leading cause of premature death in this population [ 5 , 7 , 8 ]. When resection is incomplete, a substantial proportion of tumors regrow, particularly in young children with large lesions [ 9 ]. For two decades the therapeutic record was disappointing. Farnesyltransferase inhibition, antifibrotic therapy, mTOR inhibition, interferon and KIT inhibition were each taken into phase 2 trials, and each fell short: volumetric responses were rare, and where a signal did emerge it amounted to a modest delay in progression rather than tumor shrinkage [ 10 – 14 ]. This history is relevant, because it set the benchmark against which MEK inhibition was later judged and provided the placebo control that anchored the single-arm studies which followed [ 10 ]. This began to change in 2016, when selumetinib produced confirmed partial responses in 71% of children in a phase 1 study [ 15 ], and again in 2020, when the SPRINT phase 2 trial supported the first regulatory approval for this disease [ 16 , 17 ]. Development has since accelerated. Mirdametinib was approved in February 2025 for adults and children aged two years and older [ 18 , 19 ]. In September 2025 the selumetinib indication was extended down to age one based on the SPRINKLE granule-formulation study [ 20 ], and in November 2025 selumetinib gained an adult indication on the strength of KOMET, the first randomized, placebo-controlled trial completed in plexiform neurofibroma [ 21 , 22 ]. Trametinib and binimetinib have generated supportive data in children [ 23 – 25 ], and cabozantinib has shown that meaningful responses are achievable outside the MEK class [ 26 ]. Existing syntheses have not kept pace with these developments. One meta-analysis addressed trametinib alone and closed its search in mid-2022 [ 27 ] and a more recent review examined patient-reported pain across MEK inhibitors but did not pool tumor response [ 28 ]. Neither incorporates the randomized adult evidence, the mirdametinib approval or the binimetinib data. We therefore set out to synthesize the efficacy and safety of MEK inhibitors across agents and age groups, to quantify response through a formal meta-analysis, and to situate this class within the wider landscape of targeted therapy for plexiform neurofibroma. Materials and methods Reporting and protocol This review follows the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) 2020 statement [ 29 ]. Eligibility criteria, outcomes and the analysis plan were specified in writing before screening began. The protocol was not registered with PROSPERO. The full search strategies for every database and registry are provided in S1 File , and the completed PRISMA 2020 checklist in S2 File . Ethics approval was not required. This study is a systematic review of previously published aggregate data and did not involve new research on human participants or animals. Eligibility criteria Participants were of any age with a clinical or molecular diagnosis of NF1 and at least one symptomatic plexiform neurofibroma that was inoperable or not amenable to complete resection. The intervention was monotherapy with a MEK1/2 inhibitor at any dose or schedule. Comparators, where present, were placebo or an external natural-history control; single-arm studies were eligible. The primary outcome was objective response rate, defined as a ≥ 20% reduction in target tumor volume on magnetic resonance imaging according to REiNS criteria [ 30 ]. Secondary outcomes were the depth and durability of response, progression-free survival, tumor-related pain, health-related quality of life (HRQoL) and functional outcomes, and adverse events graded by the Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE). We accepted randomized trials, prospective phase 1, 2 and 2b studies, and prospective or retrospective multicenter cohorts of at least five patients. We excluded preclinical work, narrative reviews and editorials, case reports and series of fewer than five patients, studies of non-NF1 nerve sheath tumors, and combination-therapy studies in which the contribution of MEK inhibition could not be isolated. Congress abstracts were eligible where they reported extractable outcome data not yet available in full publication. No language restriction was applied. Search strategy We searched MEDLINE (PubMed), Embase, the Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials and Web of Science from inception to 10 July 2026, together with ClinicalTrials.gov and the WHO International Clinical Trials Registry Platform. Conference proceedings from the American Society of Clinical Oncology, the Society for Neuro-Oncology and the Children’s Tumor Foundation were searched for the preceding five years. We hand-searched the reference lists of all included studies and of prior reviews and contacted investigators where outcome data appeared incomplete. Search terms combined controlled vocabulary and free-text terms for neurofibromatosis type 1, plexiform neurofibroma and the individual MEK inhibitors; the full strategy for each database, with all terms and limits, is given in S1 File . Study selection, data extraction and appraisal After de-duplication, two reviewers independently screened titles and abstracts, then full texts, resolving disagreements by discussion with a third reviewer. Agreement at the full-text stage was substantial (Cohen’s κ = 0.87). Data were extracted independently in duplicate onto a piloted form that captured design, registration, agent and schedule, population, sample size, response and the method by which it was assessed, pain and quality-of-life instruments, durability, and safety. Where both investigator-assessed and independently reviewed response rates were reported, the independently reviewed estimate was used for pooling. Risk of bias was assessed with the revised Cochrane risk-of-bias tool (RoB 2) for the randomized trial [ 31 ] and with the Risk Of Bias In Non-randomized Studies of Interventions (ROBINS-I) tool for non-randomized studies [ 32 ], supplemented by the Methodological Index for Non-Randomized Studies (MINORS) for the single-arm designs [ 33 ]. Certainty of evidence for each outcome was rated using the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) approach [ 34 ], starting at high for the randomized evidence and at low for the non-randomized body, with downgrading for risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness and imprecision, and upgrading where effect sizes were large. Data synthesis Objective response proportions were pooled using a DerSimonian–Laird random-effects model [ 35 ] after Freeman–Tukey double-arcsine transformation [ 36 ], which stabilizes the variance for proportions near the bounds and for small samples. Estimates were back-transformed for presentation. Heterogeneity was quantified with Cochran’s Q, τ 2 and I 2 [ 37 ]. Age group (pediatric versus adult) was the single pre-specified subgroup, chosen because the growth kinetics of plexiform neurofibroma differ markedly between children and adults [ 6 ]. In response to peer review we added a post hoc subgroup analysis by agent within the adult stratum, using a between-groups Cochran’s Q test; a meta-regression on agent was considered but not performed because the number of strata was too small to support it and two agents contributed a single stratum each. Where a trial reported both pediatric and adult strata, each stratum entered the analysis separately. Studies contributing only long-term follow-up of an already-included cohort, along with retrospective real-world cohorts, were retained in the narrative synthesis but excluded from pooling to avoid double-counting. A pre-specified sensitivity analysis removed the randomized trial, whose defined assessment window and independent review differ structurally from the single-arm studies. Publication bias was not formally assessed, because fewer than ten studies contributed to any single pooled estimate and funnel-plot asymmetry tests are unreliable below that threshold. Analyses were performed in Python 3 (SciPy 1.17). Comparisons between agents are indirect and are presented as such; no network meta-analysis was attempted, since the studies share neither a common comparator nor a common assessment window. Results Study selection The search returned 1,067 records from databases and 66 from trial registries. After 389 duplicates were removed, 744 records were screened by title and abstract, of which 661 were excluded. Of 83 reports sought, 79 were retrieved and assessed in full. Sixty-eight were excluded, most often because the population was not NF1-associated plexiform neurofibroma (n = 17) or because MEK inhibition was not given as monotherapy (n = 14). Eleven reports of nine unique studies, comprising 528 enrolled participants, were included; nine study strata with 358 evaluable participants contributed to the meta-analysis of objective response ( Fig 1 ). Objective response Across nine strata and 358 evaluable participants, 178 achieved a confirmed volumetric response. The pooled descriptive response rate was 53.7% (95% CI 40.5–66.6), with substantial heterogeneity (I 2 = 84.0%; τ² = 0.134; Q = 50.1, p < 0.001); because eight of the nine strata were single-arm, this pooled proportion is descriptive and should not be read as a controlled treatment effect. Pre-specified subgrouping by age accounted for most of this heterogeneity. Among children, the pooled response rate was 62.9% (95% CI 54.8–70.6) with little heterogeneity (I 2 = 17.4%; p = 0.30 for Q), whereas among adults it was 40.8% (95% CI 22.6–60.4) with severe heterogeneity (I 2 = 85.3%) ( Fig 2 ). 10.1371/journal.pone.0359957.g002 Fig 2 Forest plot of objective response rate (≥20% volumetric reduction, REiNS criteria) to MEK inhibitors, stratified by age group. Within the adult subgroup, KOMET is the source of that inconsistency [ 21 ]. Its response rate of 20% sits well below the three adult single-arm studies, which cluster between 41% and 64% [ 18 , 39 , 41 ]. Removing KOMET in the pre-specified sensitivity analysis reduced adult heterogeneity by more than half and raised the pooled adult estimate to 49.0% (95% CI 34.8–63.4; I 2 = 54.6%); across all eight single-arm strata the corresponding estimate was 58.2% (95% CI 49.6–66.5; I 2 = 53.2%). One plausible explanation is that this discrepancy reflects differences in trial design rather than any loss of drug activity, although with only four adult strata a contribution from the populations enrolled cannot be excluded. KOMET fixed its assessment at cycle 16, required confirmation on independent central review, and enrolled adults whose tumors grow slowly. The same pattern appears within SPRINT, where the investigator-assessed rate of 68% fell to 44% on independent review [ 16 ]. Agent-level patterns should be read with caution, because no trial compared agents directly and the populations differ. Among children, point estimates ranged from 51.8% for mirdametinib in ReNeu [ 18 ] to 73.7% for binimetinib in PNOC010 [ 25 ], with overlapping confidence intervals throughout. Among adults, mirdametinib produced consistent estimates in two independent studies (42.1% in NF106 [ 41 ] and 41.4% in ReNeu [ 18 ]), which lends those figures more weight than their individual sample sizes would suggest. A post hoc subgroup analysis by agent within the adult stratum ( S4 Table ), the only stratum in which two agents were each represented by more than one study, showed near-identical pooled estimates for selumetinib and mirdametinib (40.1% and 41.5%) and no significant between-agent difference (Q = 0.00, df = 1, p = 0.95). The high within-selumetinib inconsistency (I 2 = 94.7%) tracked the KOMET-versus-single-arm design contrast, not agent identity. Excluding KOMET the two estimates diverged (63.6% versus 41.5%; p = 0.036), but on one study per agent and confounded by assessment window. Agent is therefore best regarded as an untested rather than excluded contributor to residual heterogeneity. Depth and durability of response Responses were partial rather than complete in every study, with no participant in any trial achieving a complete radiographic response. Median maximal volume reductions clustered between 20% and 30%: 23.6% in the adult selumetinib study [ 39 ], 25.5% with binimetinib [ 25 ], roughly 28% in NF106 [ 41 ] and 30% in TRAM-01 [ 24 ]. The typical responder loses about a quarter of tumor volume rather than the majority. Depth of response improved with time on treatment in ReNeu, where 58% of adult responders had achieved a deep response by the September 2023 data cut and 67% by June 2024, while the confirmed adult response rate itself rose from 41% to 47% [ 18 , 40 ]. Durability was consistent across studies. In SPRINT, 82% of responses lasted at least twelve months [ 16 ], and shrinkage was maintained through roughly five additional years of follow-up [ 38 ]. In TRAM-01, 93% of patients remained progression-free beyond a year [ 24 ]. No participant progressed on treatment in the adult selumetinib study [ 39 ] or in the cabozantinib trial [ 26 ]. Against this, tumors regrow after discontinuation, an observation reported across agents and reinforced by a case series of three patients aged 13–27 years in whom neuropathic pain returned whenever selumetinib was interrupted and remitted when it was resumed [ 43 ]. These agents are cytoreductive and suppressive rather than curative. Pain, function and quality of life Every pivotal trial that measured pain reported improvement. SPRINT documented clinically meaningful reductions in tumor pain intensity alongside gains in quality of life and function, sustained on extended follow-up [ 16 , 38 ]. ReNeu reported significant improvement in pain severity, pain interference and HRQoL in both adults and children [ 18 ]. NF106 recorded durable decreases in pain ratings despite a response rate of only 42%, showing that symptomatic benefit did not depend on radiographic response [ 41 ]. A recent meta-analysis restricted to patient-reported pain reached the same conclusion across the class [ 28 ]. KOMET is the informative exception. Chronic pain intensity fell more with selumetinib than with placebo among participants with baseline scores of at least 3 (least-squares mean change −2.0 versus −1.3), but the difference did not reach significance (p = 0.070) [ 21 ]. Interpretation is constrained by the crossover at cycle 12, by the twelve-cycle window over which the comparison was made, and by the placebo response inherent in a subjectively measured symptom. We do not read this as evidence against an analgesic effect, but it is the only controlled test of that effect, and it was not positive. Gastrointestinal events followed. Most were grade 1 or 2 and were managed with supportive care and dose modification. Two categories warrant structured surveillance despite being uncommon. Asymptomatic reductions in left ventricular ejection fraction occur across the class and carry labelled warnings [ 17 , 19 ]. Ocular events, including retinal pigment epithelial detachment and retinal vein occlusion, are rare but potentially sight-threatening and require baseline and interval ophthalmologic examination. Serious toxicity was infrequent: in KOMET, 9.5% of participants discontinued because of adverse events [ 21 ]. Long-term SPRINT follow-up identified no new safety signal but established that known toxicities can first appear years after initiation, a finding with direct implications for monitoring schedules in children who may remain on therapy through adolescence [ 38 ]. Risk of bias and certainty of evidence KOMET was rated at low risk of bias across all RoB 2 domains, with adequate randomization, blinding of participants and assessors, and independent central review of the primary outcome [ 21 ]. The eight single-arm studies were rated at moderate to serious risk on ROBINS-I, principally in the domains of confounding and outcome measurement. None had a concurrent control, and spontaneous cessation of tumor growth is documented in this disease [ 6 ]. A propensity-score analysis comparing SPRINT with an age-matched natural-history cohort found a consistent reduction in the risk of progression with selumetinib [ 44 ], which partly mitigates this concern for the pediatric selumetinib data but does not extend to the other agents. The two abstract-only reports [ 24 , 40 ] were downgraded further for incomplete reporting. Applying GRADE, we rate the evidence that MEK inhibition produces objective volumetric response in adults as moderate, resting on a single well-conducted randomized trial with an imprecise effect estimate. Evidence for response in children is low: consistent, with a large effect size that warrants upgrading, but derived entirely from uncontrolled studies. Evidence for improvement in pain and quality of life is low, being consistent across trials but uncontrolled except in KOMET, where the comparison did not reach significance. Evidence for the safety profile is moderate, reflecting consistent and complete adverse-event reporting across several hundred exposed patients. Discussion Principal findings and the influence of assessment method Pooled across nine study strata and 358 evaluable participants, MEK inhibition produced an objective volumetric response in 53.7% of patients with NF1-associated symptomatic, inoperable plexiform neurofibroma. That headline figure needs unpacking. Its confidence interval spans 40.5% to 66.6%, and an I 2 of 84% indicates that the contributing studies are not estimating a single underlying quantity. The pre-specified subgroup analysis explains why: separating children from adults reduces pediatric heterogeneity to 17.4% around a pooled estimate of 62.9%, a tight and credible figure, while the adult estimate of 40.8% remains severely heterogeneous because it combines a randomized trial with a fixed assessment window and three single-arm studies with open-ended follow-up. Removing KOMET raised the pooled adult estimate to 49.0% and halved the inconsistency. Even after this exclusion, however, residual heterogeneity in the adult subgroup remained substantial (I 2 = 54.6%) across only four studies, so the adult estimate should be regarded as the least stable of the three and not as fully resolved by the KOMET-exclusion analysis alone. Its stability rests on the sensitivity analysis rather than on genuine consistency among the adult cohorts. This point is methodological rather than clinical, and it deserves emphasis because these numbers circulate widely. Selumetinib’s response rate has been reported as 68%, 66%, 44% and 20%, and each value is correct for the study and assessment method that generated it: investigator assessment in SPRINT, National Cancer Institute assessment of the same patients, independent central review of those same scans [ 16 ], and independent central review within a placebo-controlled adult trial reading at cycle 16 [ 21 ]. Comparing figures across trials without accounting for who read the imaging, when and in whom produces a false impression of declining efficacy. The pediatric-to-adult gradient The pediatric-to-adult gradient is at least partly biological, though an assessment-related artefact cannot be excluded. Plexiform neurofibromas grow fastest in the first decade, slow through adolescence and, in many adults, approach quiescence [ 6 ]; an agent that works by suppressing proliferation has correspondingly less to suppress in a mature tumor. The clinical corollary is that treatment goals should be framed differently by age. In a child, volumetric shrinkage is a reasonable expectation and a reasonable endpoint. In an adult, the realistic aims are symptom control, functional preservation and prevention of further growth, and response rate becomes a partial and potentially misleading measure of benefit. KOMET illustrates the tension directly: powered on objective response, it produced a positive trial but a statistically inconclusive pain result [ 21 ]. The same reasoning applies to when treatment should begin. Selumetinib has been studied in children with asymptomatic but at-risk tumors [ 45 ] and intervening during the window of maximal growth has obvious appeal. Whether earlier treatment prevents morbidity that later becomes irreversible or simply commits a young child to a decade of exposure and cumulative toxicity, cannot be resolved by the studies reviewed here. At the same time, the lower adult response rate could in part be an artefact of KOMET’s fixed cycle-16 assessment window and blinded central review, which differ from the open-ended follow-up of the single-arm adult studies; the biological and methodological explanations are not mutually exclusive, and the present data cannot fully separate them. Volumetric response versus symptomatic benefit Volumetric response and symptomatic benefit are correlated but far from equivalent, and the field’s reliance on the former owes more to its measurability than to its importance. A 25% reduction in a tumor measured in liters may be radiographically modest yet clinically transformative or radiographically striking yet barely felt. NF106 recorded durable pain relief alongside a response rate of only 42% [ 41 ], and the peginterferon experience was the mirror image, with clinical improvement in patients whose tumors had shrunk by less than 5% and worsening pain in one patient whose tumor had shrunk by 10% [ 13 ]. Responders in the cabozantinib trial reported reduced pain intensity and interference with no change in global quality-of-life scores [ 26 ], which suggests that the instruments in common use are not capturing the same construct and that the choice of instrument materially shapes the conclusions. Future trials should carry harmonized, NF1-specific pain and functional measures as co-primary endpoints rather than relegating them to secondary analysis, where a crossover design can render them uninterpretable, as it arguably did in KOMET [ 21 ]. Harmonization through the REiNS collaboration [ 30 ] is the natural remedy and is already partly underway. Selecting among agents At present, choosing among agents is done largely without comparative evidence. No head-to-head trial exists, and the indirect comparisons available here are confounded by population, era and assessment method. Mirdametinib is the only agent approved across the full age range [ 19 ], and its two independent adult datasets converge on a response rate near 41–42% [ 18 , 41 ], which lends those estimates more weight than their individual sample sizes would suggest. Selumetinib carries the longest safety record [ 38 ] and the only randomized evidence [ 21 ]. Trametinib and binimetinib, though not approved for this indication, achieved response rates in children within the same range [ 23 – 25 ] and are used off-label where access to approved agents is limited [ 42 ]. In practice, the intermittent three-weeks-on schedule of mirdametinib versus continuous selumetinib, the formulation and the availability of a dispersible preparation for young children, and cost will often settle the choice before efficacy data do. Toxicity offers little discrimination, since the class effects are shared and reported differences between agents plausibly reflect reporting convention as much as pharmacology. Where a patient carries a specific vulnerability such as a pre-existing retinopathy, a reduced ejection fraction, or a severe acneiform reaction to a prior MEK inhibitor, that consideration should drive selection, and switching within the class after intolerance is reasonable, though formally untested. This raises the question of how the pooled figures should be interpreted. Selumetinib, mirdametinib, trametinib and binimetinib differ in potency, dosing schedule (continuous versus intermittent), formulation and approval status, and with two of the four agents represented by a single study each, the data are too sparse to isolate agent effects by meta-regression. The pooled 53.7%, 62.9% and 40.8% estimates are therefore best read as weighted averages across pharmacologically non-equivalent drugs rather than as a single class-level response rate; where our formal adult subgroup analysis could test it, agent did not emerge as a significant moderator, but that null result is itself limited by the small number of strata. Safety and the duration of monitoring The safety profile is predictable and, for the most part, manageable. Dermatologic and gastrointestinal events dominate and are usually low grade. Two categories warrant structured surveillance despite being uncommon: asymptomatic reductions in left ventricular ejection fraction, and ocular events including retinal pigment epithelial detachment and retinal vein occlusion. Discontinuation for toxicity was 9.5% in the only randomized trial [ 21 ]. The most instructive safety observation comes from extended SPRINT follow-up, which identified no new signal but established that known toxicities can first appear years after initiation [ 38 ]. For a child who may remain on therapy from early childhood through adolescence, this turns monitoring from a short-term precaution into an indefinite commitment, and it should be discussed as such at the outset. Duration of therapy and malignant transformation How long to treat is the central unanswered question, because tumors regrow when treatment stops. The binimetinib protocol explicitly asked whether responders sustain benefit for a year off therapy [ 25 ], and a published case series described neuropathic pain returning within weeks of interruption and remitting on resumption [ 43 ]. If the drug must be continued indefinitely, a child started at the age of four faces decades of exposure, continuous surveillance, uncertain effects on growth and puberty, and a lifetime cost that no health system has yet properly costed. If instead a defined course can consolidate a durable reduction in tumor burden during the period of rapid growth and then be withdrawn, the calculus changes entirely. No trial has randomized continuation against discontinuation, and none is currently planned; this is the trial the field most needs. A related and equally unresolved question is whether MEK inhibition alters the risk of malignant transformation. Plexiform neurofibromas are the substrate from which MPNSTs arise, and MPNST accounts for much of the excess mortality in NF1, with a lifetime risk of 8–13% [ 7 , 8 ]. Transformation is now understood to proceed through an intermediate stage rather than as an abrupt event: atypical neurofibromatosis neoplasms of uncertain biologic potential (ANNUBP) are defined by cytologic atypia, loss of neurofibroma architecture, hypercellularity and a raised mitotic index [ 46 ], typically carry homozygous CDKN2A/B deletion [ 47 ], and frequently appear on imaging as distinct nodular lesions that precede overt malignancy by years [ 48 ]. This creates a testable question the current trials were not designed to answer. If MEK inhibition suppresses proliferation in the plexiform compartment, it might plausibly reduce the substrate from which ANNUBP arises; equally, sustained suppression could in principle select for clones that have already lost CDKN2A/B. Neither hypothesis has been tested, and no study reviewed here reported ANNUBP incidence or CDKN2A/B status as an outcome. Follow-up in the pivotal trials is measured in years rather than decades, and MPNST is too infrequent for these sample sizes to detect a difference. Long-term registries linked to national cancer data, ideally capturing distinct nodular lesions and ANNUBP rather than only frank MPNST, are the only realistic instrument, and they should be established while the first treated cohorts remain young. MEK inhibition in the wider therapeutic landscape MEK inhibitors entered a field with a long prior record, and reading their results against that record shows how much has changed. Having two mechanistically distinct classes with comparable activity opens the way to rational sequencing and to combination; a phase 1/1b/2 study of cabozantinib with selumetinib in adults and adolescents (NF113, NCT06502171) is now open [ 50 ]. The pipeline now extends beyond MEK. HLX-1502, an orally administered small molecule discovered through a machine-learning platform and acting outside the MEK class, entered a phase 2 open-label single-arm study (INSPIRE-NF1/NF119, NCT06541847). It is enrolling approximately 20 participants aged 16 and above with progressive or symptomatic plexiform neurofibroma, with tumor response rate, safety and pharmacokinetics as endpoints [ 49 ]. Strengths and limitations This review searched four databases and two registries, applied duplicate independent screening and extraction, appraised bias with design-appropriate instruments, and quantified response with a pre-specified random-effects model and a subgroup hypothesis that proved explanatory. To our knowledge it is the first synthesis to pool tumor response across all four MEK inhibitors studied in this disease, to separate pediatric from adult effects formally, and to incorporate both the randomized adult evidence and the mirdametinib registration data. Its limitations are substantive. The protocol was not prospectively registered, which weakens the safeguard against selective outcome reporting; we have mitigated this by reporting every pre-specified analysis, including the sensitivity analysis that attenuated our headline estimate. Eight of the nine pooled strata are single-arm and open-label, so the pooled proportion cannot be read as a treatment effect: spontaneous arrest of tumor growth occurs in this disease [ 6 ], and only KOMET quantifies the counterfactual directly, at 5% [ 21 ]; the SPRINT propensity-score analysis provides an indirect external comparison for pediatric selumetinib only [ 44 ] and does not extend to mirdametinib, trametinib or binimetinib in either age group. The low statistical heterogeneity of the pediatric estimate (I 2 = 17.4%) should therefore be read as consistency across uncontrolled cohorts, not as evidence of a treatment effect: the majority of the 62.9% pediatric figure has no concurrent comparator arm. Two included reports are congress abstracts pending full publication [ 24 , 40 ]. Two of the four agents contribute a single study each, so agent-level estimates are fragile. Publication bias could not be formally tested with nine strata, and small early-phase studies with negative findings are unlikely to have reached publication. Finally, follow-up for mirdametinib and for the adult indications remains short relative to the duration of therapy these patients face. Implications for practice and research For practice, these data support offering MEK inhibition to children and adults with symptomatic, inoperable plexiform neurofibroma, with the expectation of partial shrinkage in roughly six of ten children and four of ten adults, and of symptomatic benefit in a broader group. The six-in-ten pediatric estimate, however, is derived predominantly from uncontrolled single-arm cohorts rather than from controlled comparisons, and this uncertainty should be conveyed when counselling families. Patients should be counselled that a response means a reduction of roughly a quarter of tumor volume rather than resolution, and that benefit depends on continued treatment. Baseline echocardiography, ophthalmologic examination, and creatine phosphokinase and hepatic panels should be obtained and repeated at intervals for as long as therapy continues. Radiographic surveillance should remain alert to distinct nodular lesions, which may signal atypical transformation and warrant separate evaluation rather than being read as part of the treated plexiform mass [ 48 ]. Response should be assessed volumetrically, as RECIST underestimates response in this disease by more than half and should not be used [ 24 ]. Pain and function deserve consideration as outcomes, and an agent that controls symptoms should not be withdrawn solely because a volumetric threshold has not been crossed. For research, the priorities are a randomized trial of continued versus interrupted therapy in responders, harmonized patient-reported endpoints, comparative or platform designs across MEK inhibitors and against cabozantinib, long-term registries linked to cancer data that capture atypical transformation and not only frank malignancy, and the identification of predictive biomarkers, which remain absent. Neither NF1 genotype nor baseline tumor volume reliably distinguishes responders, and pharmacokinetic exposure explained only a fraction of the response variance in NF106 [ 41 ]. Conclusions A disease that had no effective systemic therapy in 2015 now has two approved drugs spanning infancy to adulthood, further agents with demonstrated activity, randomized evidence in adults, and a mechanistically distinct competitor in cabozantinib. Pooled across nine study strata, the descriptive volumetric response rate was 62.9% in children and 40.8% in adults; these are weighted averages across four pharmacologically distinct agents, and only the adult estimate is anchored by a randomized comparator. Benefit is durable and the toxicity profile predictable and manageable, though responses are partial and treatment is suppressive rather than curative. The questions that remain are how long to treat, what to do when treatment stops, whether these drugs prevent malignant transformation, and how to measure benefit in a way that reflects what patients experience. With current studies and data collection ongoing, further insight should help to answer them.
