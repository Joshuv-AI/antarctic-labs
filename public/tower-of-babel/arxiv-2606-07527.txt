Post-training is (Massive) Supervised Learning
Michael Hassid, Yossi Adi, Roy Schwartz

Abstract
The prevailing paradigm for training LLMs has evolved to rely on a massive post-training phase consisting of SFT and RL. In this position paper, we argue that this methodology effectively marks a reversion to the “pre-train then fine-tune” approach of the BERT era, explicitly tailoring models to the desired behaviors and specific benchmarks on which they are evaluated. We begin with a historical overview of LLMs, describing the different phases of the LLM evolution. We argue that the current landscape is remarkably similar to the early days of LLMs, where task performance heavily relied on fitting the models to in-distribution datasets. To empirically demonstrate this, we compare pre-trained models to randomly initialized ones, by fine-tuning both variants on modern reasoning datasets and evaluating them on competitive math and code benchmarks. We show that models post-trained from scratch yield highly non-trivial performance. Our findings suggest that current post-training methodologies function primarily as a distribution-fitting mechanism. We finish by positing that developing generally capable models and systems requires moving beyond extensive post-training for predefined behaviors, shifting instead toward training procedures where models “learn how to learn”.

Introduction

Historically, the prevailing machine learning paradigm relied on a fully supervised approach - training models directly on data distributions that corresponded to target tasks. This methodology was overtaken by the self-supervised approach that became dominant with large language models (LLMs). Over the years, the LLMs training paradigm has evolved to incorporate a post-training stage, consisting of supervised fine-tuning (SFT) and reinforcement learning (RL). These steps were designed to align the model towards specific behaviors. Today, this phase is a massive part of the training pipeline, consuming up to hundreds of billions of tokens
(
49
;
79
;
57

.

In this position paper, we argue that the NLP field has come full circle (

(
21
;
52
;
59
)
. Rather than pursuing true zero-shot generalization
(
10
)
, the prevailing strategy has shifted back toward multi-task optimization, explicitly fitting models to the distributions on which they are evaluated.

We begin with a brief historical overview of LLMs. We start by charting their early days as small-scale backbones for supervised learning models
(
21
;
43
)
. We then trace the shift toward massive pre-training scale, where models began achieving high performance via in-context learning with only a handful of inference-time examples (few-shot learning;
10
). Next, we describe the evolution of the post-training phase: what began as a short stage aimed at aligning a model’s behavior and formatting has expanded into a massive phase using hundreds of billions of tokens for SFT and RL trainings. We follow with a short discussion of the differences between the two approaches. We conclude by arguing that, by systematically shifting model distributions toward specific benchmarks and desired behaviors, current methodologies effectively transform evaluation distributions into explicit training distributions.

To empirically demonstrate this observation, we design a series of experiments isolating the impact of large-scale post-training (specifically SFT) from traditional pre-training. We posit that if post-training merely unlocks or aligns pre-existing knowledge acquired during pre-training, a model trained from scratch strictly on SFT data should fail on reasoning tasks. Conversely, if it acts as an aggressive distribution-fitting mechanism, the non-pretrained model should achieve non-trivial performance.

We evaluate models across varying parameter scales on mathematical reasoning and competitive programming benchmarks. Remarkably, our results validate the latter scenario, demonstrating that post-training enables models to achieve surprisingly high results even in the complete absence of traditional pre-training. We find that models trained entirely from scratch, using SFT datasets only and without any prior linguistic or domain exposure, manage to achieve non-negligible results on highly difficult benchmarks. E.g., a 14B model scores 51% on LCB V5
(
32
)
and a 7B model obtains 43% on AIME-24
(
6
)
. Importantly, these models underperform their pre-trained counterparts by a margin of 8–20%, gaps that mirror the BERT and GPT-1 era, where pre-trained models held a similar advantage over supervised-only baselines.
Furthermore, we show that a minimal pre-training setup of less than 0.1% of the original pre-training compute bridges up to half of the remaining performance deficit between non-pretrained and fully pre-trained models.
Ultimately, this suggests modern post-training depends far less on pre-training’s emergent capabilities than previously thought, as high performance on desired benchmarks can rely on pulling targeted tasks directly into the model’s distribution.

We conclude that current LLM development relies on a massive, yet fundamentally reactive, post-training paradigm. Because this approach is inherently reactive, the field is trapped chasing the ever-evolving demands of LLM applications.
We suggest that the path to generally capable models requires moving beyond static post-training for predefined benchmarks. We advocate for a shift in the training procedures, and suggest that they should include meta-learning where models
learn how to learn
.
This will lead toward models and agentic systems designed to inherently self-evolve to solve novel, out-of-distribution tasks
(
39
;
75
;
80
;
24
;
27
)
.

As a position paper, our contributions are not a proposal of a new method or training paradigm. Instead, our goal is to provide a critical perspective on the current state of LLM development, particularly the growing role of post-training in shaping model capabilities. Through both historical analysis and empirical observations, we argue that modern practices increasingly resemble traditional supervised learning at scale and distribution-fitting approaches. We highlight the broader implications of this trend for the field, and propose a high level direction for future research.

Pre-training and Post-training in LLMs: A Historical Overview

From supervised learning to pre-train then fine-tune.

The prevailing approach prior to the current era of LLMs involved a straightforward supervised learning pipeline

A model was subsequently trained on the training data, and its performance was evaluated on the validation and test sets. These evaluation sets were inherently highly in-distribution, meaning they were highly similar to the training data.

The introduction of models like ELMO, BERT, and GPT-1 marked a paradigm shift with the advent of the modern

These pre-trained models were subsequently fine-tuned on task-specific training sets and evaluated on their corresponding test sets
(
70
;

. This fine-tuning approach yielded performance improvements of up to 20% over their supervised-only counterparts
(

;
59
)
.
This “pre-train then fine-tune” approach quickly became the new standard for training LLMs
(

;

;

, and was also adapted for other domains like vision and audio
(
42
;
47
;
29

.

Scaling and few-shot learning.

Subsequent research focused on progressively increasing the scale of this pre-training phase, eventually making it the sole dominant component of the training process. A notable example is GPT-3
(
10
)
, which was exclusively pre-trained and demonstrated advanced few-shot capabilities. This showed that models could achieve strong performance on benchmarks even without being explicitly trained on those specific data distributions. This approach became the de-facto approach for the following couple of years
(
40
;
85
;
9

.

Post-training as a tool for model alignment.

Although large-scale pre-trained models demonstrated impressive capabilities on leading benchmarks, they often failed to follow explicit instructions or exhibit human-desired behaviors.
To bridge this alignment gap, a post-training stage based on SFT and RL was established. Using a relatively small amount of curated data, this phase aligned a general-purpose base model to instruction and chat formats

New, hard benchmarks arise.

With the advancement of LLM performance, the research community started building harder and more complicated benchmarks, with the goal of pushing the boundaries of the LLM technology.
Early benchmarks such as GSM8k for mathematics and HumanEval for coding

The LLM community response: Post-training.

As the difficulty of benchmarks increased, the LLM community responded by tailoring models to these specific performance metrics

Consequently, post-training has evolved into a pivotal stage of the development lifecycle. This phase typically employs SFT datasets to bootstrap the RL process, targeting high-value tasks — such as software engineering

Back to in-distribution specialization.

We argue that the growth of post-training suggests a skepticism regarding the few-shot generalization capabilities of pre-training alone
(
10
)
. By systematically shifting model distributions toward specific benchmarks and desired behaviors, current methodologies effectively transform the evaluation distributions into explicit training distribution
(
66
)
.

In that sense, the NLP field has come full circle (
Figure

1
). Current LLMs are reminiscent of early LLMs like GPT-1 and BERT: we have pre-trained models that we fine-tune using supervised learning over specific distributions, which are the relevant distributions on which we evaluate them.
This trajectory effectively marks a reversion to the “pre-train then fine-tune” methodology. Analogous to the BERT era, the prevailing strategy has shifted away from zero-shot generalization and back toward specific multi-task optimization, where models are explicitly fitted to the specific distributions on which they are evaluated and
currently
used.
While these strategies may generalize to distributions similar to the training set, they often fail to generalize to underrepresented or significantly divergent distributions
(
64
)
.

This methodology has established a recurring cycle. When a new capability is required, a benchmark is introduced, typically one where initial model performance is much worse compared to humans. Yet, these benchmarks are often saturated within a short time (a year or so). This rapid progress is frequently driven by the post-training process, where the benchmark is effectively pulled in-distribution via the curation of analogous SFT datasets and the construction of proxy RL environments.

2.2
Traditional vs. Modern Post-training

While the current LLM development pipeline marks a philosophical reversion to the “pre-train then fine-tune” methodology characteristic of the BERT era, critical distinctions between the two paradigms remain. Below, we discuss several key aspects of these differences.

Historically, the traditional post-training (or fine-tuning) phase was a relatively short stage. It used minimal data compared to the pre-training phase, serving merely to unlock capabilities or adapt a backbone to a specific format. During the BERT era, it was mainly used for training on the specific training examples of each dataset
(
21
;
43
)
, while in the subsequent instruction-tuning era, it used millions of tokens
(
50
;
68
)
. In contrast, modern post-training has expanded into a massive phase. Current pipelines process diverse data sources at a scale of hundreds of billions of tokens for SFT and extensive RL
(
56
;
34
;
18
;
79
)
. Notably, this phase now processes data volumes equivalent to the entire pre-training runs of leading models from just four to five years ago, such as GPT-3 and OPT
(
10
;
85
)
.

Furthermore, these traditional and modern paradigms differ in their data-generation mechanisms. Traditional post-training relies exclusively on static, task-specific datasets
(
70
;
69
)
. In contrast, modern post-training employs a more dynamic approach. The SFT phase remains analogous to traditional supervised learning, optimizing over explicit input-output pairs (often distilled from other models). However, the RL phase shifts the supervisory signal exclusively to the terminal output (e.g., via a reward model); consequently, the intermediate training trajectories are generated dynamically by the model actively undergoing training.

A related mechanical distinction lies in the formulation of the training objective. Traditional fine-tuning framed downstream evaluations as classification or extraction tasks, typically applying the loss over a single output token—such as a dedicated classification head
(
70
;
69
)
. Modern post-training, by contrast, is fundamentally generative. During SFT and RL, the model optimizes the next-token prediction loss over the entire output trajectory.

Importantly, similarly to the traditional fine-tuning setup, the whole modern post-training paradigm can be categorized as a supervised learning phase. Given that both SFT and RL ultimately rely on annotated data, the former through direct label mapping and the latter through the evaluation of model outputs, the underlying mechanics of this entire phase are fundamentally supervised.

Another difference lies in the relationship between training and test datasets. In the classic supervised pipeline, where each task was evaluated individually, a dataset was first collected and then randomly partitioned into training and test subsets.
Because they originated from the exact same source, the test set was inherently highly in-distribution. Today, this dynamic is entirely inverted. The modern cycle begins with the introduction of a novel, highly difficult benchmark (the test set), where initial model performance is poor. To solve it, the LLM community systematically mines, synthesizes, and curates analogous SFT datasets and proxy RL environments designed to closely resemble the evaluation criteria
(
48
;
2
;
79
)
. Despite these differences, the result is strikingly similar: in both setups the training distribution closely resembles the test distribution.

Finally, a prominent distinction between modern post-training and the traditional approach is that the latter typically focused on a single task. In contrast, modern post-training objectives aim for the development of unified, generalist models by leveraging
multi-task
environments. This simultaneous exposure to various data distributions is intended to promote broader generalization beyond the explicit post-training data. For example, a model post-trained on competitive programming in a primary language is expected to generalize to alternative languages, assuming those secondary languages maintain some representation within the broader post-training corpus.

In the following section, we show that modern post-training can yield highly non-negligible results completely independent of pre-training. This highlights just how heavily current post-training pipelines shift model distributions to fit specific tasks.

3
Massive SFT can do without Pre-training

In this section, we investigate the efficacy of large-scale post-training in isolation. We empirically demonstrate that training LLMs on current SFT datasets yields surprisingly high in-distribution performance on hard math and code benchmarks, even in the absence of pre-training. This finding suggests that current post-training methods heavily optimize models to excel on specific benchmarks, and that certain desired behaviors may rely less on the pre-training phase than previously thought.

We outline our experimental setup in
Section

3.1
. Next, we detail our findings in
Section

3.2
. Finally, we discuss our experimental scope and limitations in
Section

3.3
.

3.1
Experimental Setup

To understand the models’ performance of pre-trained vs. scratch initialization using SFT on desired distributions, we focus on competitive code and math benchmarks. We train models with a range of sizes and compare the results between the pre-trained versions to their from-scratch counterparts.

We train both variants on two distinct, large-scale SFT reasoning datasets. The math models are trained on the Open Math Reasoning dataset (OMR;
48
), consisting of
\sim
3.2M question-answer pairs.
The code models are trained on the Open Code Reasoning II dataset (OCR-II;
1
), which contains about 1.4M Python and 1.2M C++ question-answer pairs.

We compare the different models’ performance on highly tracked math and competitive programming benchmarks to understand their reasoning capabilities — which is a primary focus and desired proficiency of recent model releases
(
28
;
58
;
79
)
. Our math evaluation uses MATH-500
(
41
)
, and four competition math benchmarks from the MathArena leaderboard which are used as the current standard for testing leading math reasoning models
(
6
)
: AIME 24, AIME 25, HMMT February 25, and HMMT November 25. To evaluate reasoning for coding, we test the models over the LCB coding benchmark
(
32
)
, using the V5 and V6 splits. We provide further details about the benchmarks in
Appendix

A
.

To isolate the impact of pre-training on specific target distributions - in our case, reasoning math and code - we focus on models that lack explicit reasoning data (i.e., thinking trajectories) in their pre-training stage. Specifically, we use the Qwen-2.5-Base family of models, which offers extensive training on 18 trillion tokens, strong performance, and availability across multiple scales
(
55
)
. Our experiments use the 1.5B, 3B, 7B, and 14B parameter sizes.

We train all models on each of the SFT datasets for 50k steps, using a sequence length of 32k and a batch size of
\sim
2M tokens (a total of
\sim
100B tokens). Models trained from scratch use depth-scaled initialization
(
81
)
. For evaluation, we sample 10 answers per question (temperature = 0.6, top-p = 0.95) and report both pass@1 (average accuracy) and pass@5 (success rate within five attempts). See
Appendix

A
for more details.

3.3
Experimental Scope and Limitations

Our experimental scope is subject to two primary limitations. First, our experiments are restricted to reasoning data: competitive math and code. We focused on these areas because they represent a primary focus of the current LLM field
(
28
;
58
;
79
)
and benefit from the availability of large-scale, open-source SFT datasets
(
1
;
48
;
2
)
.
Second, our analysis focuses exclusively on SFT, whereas modern post-training pipelines typically include a subsequent RL phase.

Fundamentally, RL requires a pre-training stage for the model’s capacity to generate correct solutions to be rewarded on. Coupled with the fact that our pre-trained models consistently outperform their non-pretrained counterparts, we highlight that pre-training remains critical. Rather, our findings highlight the extent to which current post-training optimizes for specific target distributions, enabling even models initialized from scratch to achieve surprising results on competitive benchmarks.

4
Learning how to Learn

As discussed in
Section

2
, the evolution of LLMs has transitioned from pre-trained backbones used for task-specific SFT to a massive post-training paradigm that aligns desired behaviors into the model’s distribution. Yet, this current pipeline remains fundamentally reactive: the post-training process is inherently restricted to specific use cases and behaviors that are
already known
, and for which large-scale post-training can be performed.
These models demonstrate robust generalization within their established training distributions. However, adapting them to entirely novel behaviors necessitates full re-(post-)training cycles, a limitation that contrasts with the promise of few-shot learning
(
10
)
.

This limitation drives the current trend of iterative model releases built upon the same pre-trained backbone, distinguished only by enhanced post-training setups that incorporate newly desired capabilities (e.g., the incremental progressions of GPT-5.x or Claude-4.x). Under the current paradigm, acquiring a novel capability demands a costly, ground-up re-post-training effort: curating new SFT datasets, engineering new RL environments, and executing continuous model updates just to bridge the newly identified capability gap.
Proponents of the current paradigm may argue that scaling post-training data and compute can lead to achieving broader capabilities. Yet, this relies on the assumption that scaling predefined data and environments can efficiently keep pace with the ever-evolving demands placed on LLM systems. Therefore, we posit that the field must advance beyond these static, predefined post-training procedures toward a new evolutionary stage.

We argue for the development of significantly more robust systems. In our vision, models must inherently
“learn how to learn”
. Under this proposed paradigm, when an LLM system encounters a problem it cannot solve to the required degree of accuracy, it should autonomously adapt and evolve its capabilities to overcome that limitation.

One approach to realizing this vision is through autonomous, self-adapting LLM agents. We envision an agentic system capable of dynamically modifying its own prompts, underlying code, and even its model weights. When presented with a novel task where superficial adjustments (such as prompt engineering or modifying code execution) are insufficient, the system could autonomously replicate the LLM post-training pipeline. Specifically, it could search for and curate SFT data, engineer custom RL environments, and subsequently retrain its internal models. This method can include attempting a solution, while iteratively learning through any failures.
The system would then evaluate the updated model’s performance on the target task, iterating on this process as necessary. Furthermore, under resource constraints, such a system could intelligently opt to train a smaller, highly specialized model to serve as a tool for future instances of the new task.

Of course, the acquisition of the above meta-learning capabilities must be integrated as a core objective within modern training paradigms. Just as traditional pre-training serves as the foundational phase for general knowledge and linguistic skills, this proposed meta-learning phase acts as the mechanism for dynamic, post-deployment task acquisition (similar to the promise of few-shot learning).
Ultimately, this establishes a new level of machine intelligence defined by the ability to “learn how to learn”.

Crucially, this foundational training would ideally only be required once. Once a model acquires the capacity to self-evolve, it will be able to theoretically improve indefinitely, limited only by computational resources. Consequently, this paradigm will relieve researchers from continuously engineering and maintaining post-training procedures by delegating the control of ongoing adaptation directly to the models themselves.

We note that our suggested paradigm idea is not limited to the specific mechanism described above, and can have several forms and generalizability
(
7
)
. Various approaches can successfully enable systems to learn how to solve unknown tasks
(
39
;
80
;
82
;
71
;
75
;
24
;
27
;
83
;
74
;
76
)
. Achieving this self-evolving capacity may also require moving beyond standard Transformer backbones to alternative architectures that yield highly effective in-context learning for novel tasks without massive supervised learning
(
84
)
.
The unifying idea for optimizing all such systems is the shared principle of “learning how to learn”. Different approaches to this objective will naturally result in varying systems, which will result in a robust ecosystem of self-improving models.

5
Conclusion

In this position paper, through a historical overview of LLMs, we argued that current training paradigms effectively revert to the "pre-train then fine-tune" methodology of the BERT era. Under this approach, models are explicitly tailored to desired behaviors and specific evaluation benchmarks. We empirically demonstrated this by showing that models post-trained from scratch yield highly non-trivial performance, suggesting that current post-training methodologies function primarily as a distribution-fitting mechanism. Finally, we argued that prevailing post-training approaches are fundamentally reactive. To achieve more generalized capabilities, we advocate for a shift toward meta-learning paradigms, where models “learn how to learn”.
