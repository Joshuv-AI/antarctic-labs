Mirror Descent for Deterministic Optimal Control
Ye Feng, Jianfeng Lu

Abstract
. We study an explicit mirror-descent method for finite-horizon deterministic optimal control problems. The method is motivated by Pontryagin’s maximum principle: at each iteration, one solves the state and adjoint equations and updates the control by maximizing a first-order approximation of the regularized Hamiltonian penalized by a Bregman divergence. In the Euclidean case, the update reduces to a projected gradient step in the control variable. Under global smoothness assumptions and uniform convexity of the mirror map, we prove a relative smoothness estimate for the cost functional and derive an energy dissipation inequality for sufficiently small step sizes. Under an additional concavity assumption on the unregularized Hamiltonian and convexity of the terminal cost, we establish relative convexity of the regularized objective. These estimates yield an O ⁡ ( 1 / n ) O(1/n) convergence rate in the unregularized convex case and a geometric rate when the control regularization parameter is positive. Numerical examples illustrate the behavior of the method in linear-quadratic, degenerate convex, and nonlinear high-dimensional settings.



1.
Introduction

Optimal control problems are a fundamental class of optimization problems with applications in engineering, economics, and scientific computing. A classical approach to such problems is based on Pontryagin’s maximum principle (PMP), which characterizes optimal controls through a system of forward and adjoint differential equations coupled with a pointwise optimality condition.

Beyond its role as a theoretical tool, PMP also naturally leads to iterative numerical methods. A prominent example is the method of successive approximations (MSA), which alternates between solving the state and adjoint equations and updating the control via a pointwise optimization of the Hamiltonian
[
4
,
1
]
. In practice, this update step is often implemented either as a projected gradient-type step or through implicit schemes that solve a regularized local problem (for example, see
[
8
]
). While these methods are widely used, a systematic convergence analysis, especially for explicit update rules, remains only partially understood.

In this work, we study a class of explicit control update schemes derived from PMP and provide a unified convergence analysis using a Bregman divergence framework. We consider a regularized optimal control problem of the form

J^{\tau}(u)=\int_{0}^{T}\big(f_{t}(x_{t},u_{t})+\tau h(u_{t})\big)\,\mathrm{d}t+g(x_{T}),

and analyze an iterative scheme in which the control is updated by maximizing a first-order approximation of the regularized Hamiltonian penalized by a Bregman proximal term. When the regularizer
h(u)=\tfrac{1}{2}|u|^{2}
, this update reduces to an explicit projected gradient step in the control variable. In contrast to previously studied implicit or modified MSA schemes
[
8
]
, the resulting algorithm is fully explicit and thus more directly comparable to standard first-order methods.

The use of a general convex regularizer allows us to formulate the algorithm in terms of Bregman divergences, which provides a convenient and flexible framework for the analysis. Although our primary interest is in the Euclidean setting, this formulation enables a unified treatment of the update step and simplifies the derivation of key estimates.

Our work is related to several strands of literature. On the one hand, there has been growing interest in mirror descent and Bregman proximal methods in optimal control and related infinite-dimensional optimization problems, including stochastic or measure-valued control settings, as well as continuous-time limits formulated as gradient flows
[
7
,
3
,
9
]
. On the other hand, recent works have studied variants of MSA with implicit or regularized updates
[
2
,
8
]
. In contrast, the present paper focuses on a deterministic control setting and provides a direct analysis of a fully explicit iterative scheme derived from PMP.

Following a variational perspective similar to that of
[
3
]
, we base our analysis on the first variation formula, which expresses the derivative of the cost functional in terms of the gradient of the Hamiltonian. This allows us to interpret the update step as a mirror-descent-type iteration. We show that the cost functional satisfies a relative smoothness property with respect to the induced Bregman divergence, leading to an energy dissipation inequality along the iterates. Under an additional convexity assumption on the Hamiltonian, we further establish a relative convexity property with modulus given by the regularization parameter. Combining these properties, we derive convergence rates for the proposed algorithm: a sublinear rate of order
O(1/n)
in the unregularized case and a geometric rate in the presence of regularization. Together, these results provide a unified variational framework for analyzing explicit first-order PMP-based methods in deterministic optimal control.

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
, we introduce the problem formulation and standing assumptions. Section
3
reviews Pontryagin’s maximum principle for deterministic controls and establishes well-posedness of the state and adjoint equations. In Section
4
, we derive the first variation formula for the cost functional, introduce the Bregman divergence, and formulate the mirror descent-based MSA algorithm. Section
5
establishes relative smoothness, and Section
6
proves an energy dissipation property. Section
7
establishes relative convexity under an additional structural assumption. Finally, Section
8
combines the above ingredients to obtain convergence rates. Section
9
includes three numerical examples: a linear-quadratic example with an explicit solution as a sanity check, a nonquadratic example highlighting the qualitative difference between the regularized and unregularized cases in line with the theory, and a high-dimensional nonlinear example used to study dimension dependence.

Related work

Iterative methods for optimal control derived from Pontryagin’s maximum principle (PMP) have been studied extensively. A classical example is the method of successive approximations (MSA), and several recent works have analyzed modified variants designed to ensure convergence. In particular,
[
2
]
proposed a modified MSA for stochastic control problems with controls entering both drift and diffusion, and established convergence of the method under general assumptions, together with convergence rates under additional structural assumptions.

A related line of work interprets PMP-based iterative schemes through continuous-time dynamics. In
[
8
]
, the authors showed that a modified MSA can be viewed as an implicit Euler discretization of a gradient flow system, and proved convergence of interpolated iterates to the corresponding gradient flow, along with asymptotic convergence results. Similarly,
[
7
]
studied proximal policy gradient algorithms for finite-horizon continuous-time stochastic control problems with feedback controls, establishing linear convergence to stationary points under suitable assumptions. While these approaches include deterministic control as a special case, their analysis is fundamentally formulated in a stochastic and continuous-time (gradient-flow) framework.

More recently, mirror descent and Bregman proximal methods have been introduced in stochastic control. In
[
3
]
, the authors studied mirror descent for stochastic control problems with measure-valued controls, establishing relative smoothness and relative convexity of the objective with respect to the Bregman divergence induced by the regularizer, and deriving convergence rates. Related developments can also be found in
[
9
]
, which studies mirror-descent-type algorithms in stochastic control settings with Markov (feedback) controls and analyzes their convergence properties.

There is also some earlier literature on Bregman-based methods for optimal control, primarily from a regularization perspective. In
[
5
]
, an iterative Bregman regularization method was proposed for optimal control problems with inequality constraints, together with convergence and error estimates under suitable structural assumptions. Extensions to inexact settings were further studied in
[
6
]
, where robustness with respect to discretization and numerical error was established. These works demonstrate the usefulness of Bregman distances in optimal control, but they are formulated as regularization schemes for constrained optimization problems rather than as PMP-based iterative update methods.

In summary, existing convergence analyses for PMP-based algorithms have largely been developed either in stochastic settings or via continuous-time gradient-flow formulations, while Bregman and mirror-descent techniques have mainly been applied in stochastic or regularization-based control frameworks. In contrast, the present paper provides a direct and self-contained analysis of a fully explicit iterative scheme in a deterministic continuous-time control setting. Our use of a general convex regularizer and the associated Bregman divergence serves primarily as an analytical tool, allowing for a unified treatment of projected gradient-type updates and their convergence properties within the PMP/MSA framework.

2.
Problem Formulation

We consider a deterministic control problem on a finite horizon
T>0
. The state
x_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
evolves according to

(1)

\dot{x}_{t}=b(t,x_{t},u_{t})

with fixed initial condition
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Here
b:[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\times U\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and the control
u
belongs to
\mathcal{U}:=C([0,T];U)
, where
U
is a closed convex subset of
\mathbb{R}^{m}
. We often write
b_{t}(x,u):=b(t,x,u)
and similarly for
f_{t}
.

We define the regularized cost functional

(2)

J^{\tau}(u):=\int_{0}^{T}\bigl(f_{t}(x_{t},u_{t})+\tau h(u_{t})\bigr)\,\mathrm{d}t+g(x_{T}),

with running cost
f:[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\times U\to\mathbb{R}
, terminal cost
g:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
, convex regularizer
h:U\to\mathbb{R}
, and regularization parameter
\tau\geq 0
.
It is convenient to decompose

J^{\tau}(u)=J^{0}(u)+\tau H(u),\qquad H(u):=\int_{0}^{T}h(u_{t})\,\mathrm{d}t.

A pair
(u,x)
is
admissible
if
u\in\mathcal{U}
and
x
is the unique solution of (
1
). An admissible pair
(u^{*},x^{*})
is
optimal
if

\displaystyle J^{\tau}(u^{*})\leq J^{\tau}(u)

for every admissible pair
(u,x)
. Formally, the control problem is to find an optimal control
u^{*}
by solving the minimization problem

\displaystyle(\mathbf{P})\quad\inf_{u\in\mathcal{U}}J^{\tau}(u).

Assumption A
.

We make the following standing assumptions:

(1)

U
is a nonempty, closed, convex subset of
\mathbb{R}^{m}
.

(2)

The maps
b:[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\times U\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
f:[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\times U\to\mathbb{R}
are continuous, and there exists a constant
M>0
such that for
\varphi=b,f
,

\displaystyle|\varphi_{t}(x,u)-\varphi_{t}(x^{\prime},u^{\prime})|\leq M(|x-x^{\prime}|+|u-u^{\prime}|),\quad|\varphi_{t}(0,u)|\leq M,

for any
t\in[0,T]
,
x,x^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and
u,u^{\prime}\in U
.

(3)

For
\varphi=b,f
, the map
\varphi_{t}(x,u)
is twice continuously differentiable in
(x,u)
, and, possibly after increasing
M
, the same constant satisfies

\displaystyle|\nabla_{x}\varphi_{t}(x,u)-\nabla_{x}\varphi_{t}(x^{\prime},u^{\prime})|\leq M(|x-x^{\prime}|+|u-u^{\prime}|),

for any
t\in[0,T]
,
x,x^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and
u,u^{\prime}\in U
.
Further, assume that there exist constants
M_{b,uu},M_{f,uu}>0
such that

\|\nabla^{2}_{uu}b_{t}(x,u)\|\leq M_{b,uu},\qquad\|\nabla^{2}_{uu}f_{t}(x,u)\|\leq M_{f,uu},

for all
(t,x,u)\in[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\times U
.

(4)

The map
g:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
is continuously differentiable and satisfies

\max\{|g(x)-g(x^{\prime})|,|\nabla g(x)-\nabla g(x^{\prime})|\}\leq M|x-x^{\prime}|,\quad|g(0)|\leq M,

for any
x,x^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

(5)

The regularizer
h:U\to\mathbb{R}
is convex and continuously differentiable.

(6)

There exists a (continuous) optimal solution to Problem (
P
).

Remark
.

Assumption
A
(2) and (3) imply that, on
[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\times U
, the first-order derivatives

\nabla_{x}b_{t}(x,u),\quad\nabla_{u}b_{t}(x,u),\quad\nabla_{x}f_{t}(x,u),\quad\nabla_{u}f_{t}(x,u),

and the second-order derivatives

\nabla^{2}_{xx}b_{t}(x,u),\quad\nabla^{2}_{xu}b_{t}(x,u),\quad\nabla^{2}_{xx}f_{t}(x,u),\quad\nabla^{2}_{xu}f_{t}(x,u)

are uniformly bounded by
M
.

3.
Pontryagin’s Maximum Principle

In this section, we recall a necessary condition for optimality in Problem (
P
), namely Pontryagin’s maximum principle. We also establish well-posedness of the state and adjoint equations for continuous controls, together with a uniform boundedness estimate.

Define the unregularized Hamiltonian

(3)

\mathcal{H}_{t}^{0}(x,p,u):=p\cdot b_{t}(x,u)-f_{t}(x,u),

and the regularized Hamiltonian

(4)

\mathcal{H}_{t}^{\tau}(x,p,u):=\mathcal{H}_{t}^{0}(x,p,u)-\tau h(u).

Given
u\in\mathcal{U}
and the corresponding state
x
, we define the adjoint
p
by

(5)

\dot{p}_{t}=-\nabla_{x}\mathcal{H}_{t}^{0}(x_{t},p_{t},u_{t}),\qquad p_{T}=-\nabla g(x_{T}).

Equivalently, one may use
\nabla_{x}\mathcal{H}_{t}^{\tau}
in (
5
), since the regularizer
h
is independent of the state variable.

Lemma 1
.

Under Assumption
A
, for any control
u\in\mathcal{U}=C([0,T];U)
, the state equation (
1
) admits a unique solution
x
, and the adjoint equation (
5
) admits a unique solution
p
. Moreover,
|x_{t}|\leq M_{X}
and
|p_{t}|\leq M_{P}
for all
t\in[0,T]
with constants given by
M_{X}:=(|x_{0}|+MT)e^{MT}
and
M_{P}:=M(1+T)e^{MT}
.

Proof.

Let
u\in\mathcal{U}
, under Assumption
A
, the state equation (
1
) has a unique solution
x
for the control
u
. Since
x
is continuous, Assumption
A
also yields a unique solution
p
of the adjoint equation (
5
) associated with
(u,x)
. To establish the boundedness estimate, observe that
x
satisfies the integral form of the forward equation,

\displaystyle x_{t}=x_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}b_{s}(x_{s},u_{s})\,\mathrm{d}s,\quad\forall t\in[0,T].

Taking norms and applying Assumption
A
(2),

\displaystyle|x_{t}|

\displaystyle\leq|x_{0}|+\int_{0}^{t}|b_{s}(x_{s},u_{s})|\,\mathrm{d}s,

\displaystyle\leq|x_{0}|+\int_{0}^{t}\left(M|x_{s}|+M\right)\,\mathrm{d}s,\qquad\forall t\in[0,T].

By Grönwall’s inequality,

(6)

\displaystyle|x_{t}|

\displaystyle\leq(|x_{0}|+Mt)e^{Mt}\leq(|x_{0}|+MT)e^{MT}=M_{X},\quad\forall t\in[0,T].

Similarly,
p
satisfies the integral form of the adjoint equation,

\displaystyle p_{t}=-\nabla_{x}g(x_{T})+\int^{T}_{t}\nabla_{x}b_{s}(x_{s},u_{s})^{\top}p_{s}\,\mathrm{d}s-\int_{t}^{T}\nabla_{x}f_{s}(x_{s},u_{s})\,\mathrm{d}s,\quad\forall t\in[0,T].

Taking norms and applying Assumption
A
together with (
6
),

\displaystyle|p_{t}|

\displaystyle\leq|\nabla_{x}g(x_{T})|+\int_{t}^{T}|\nabla_{x}b_{s}(x_{s},u_{s})|\cdot|p_{s}|\,\mathrm{d}s+\int_{t}^{T}|\nabla_{x}f_{s}(x_{s},u_{s})|\,\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\leq M+M\int_{t}^{T}|p_{s}|\,\mathrm{d}s+M(T-t),\quad\forall t\in[0,T].

By Grönwall’s inequality,

\displaystyle|p_{t}|

\displaystyle\leq M(1+T-t)\cdot e^{M(T-t)}\leq M(1+T)e^{MT}=M_{P},\quad\forall t\in[0,T].\qed

We now state the necessary condition for being an optimal control.

Theorem 1

(Pontryagin’s maximum principle)
.

Let
(u^{*},x^{*})
be an optimal pair for Problem (
P
). Under Assumption
A
, there exists
p^{*}:[0,T]\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
satisfying the adjoint equation (
5
) associated with
(u^{*},x^{*})
and

(7)

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{t}^{\tau}(x^{*}_{t},p^{*}_{t},u^{*}_{t})=\max_{u\in U}\mathcal{H}_{t}^{\tau}(x^{*}_{t},p^{*}_{t},u),\quad\text{for a.e. }t\in[0,T].

The condition (
7
) is called the
maximum condition
. The proof of Theorem
1
is standard and hence omitted, see e.g.,
[
10
, Chapter 3]
.

4.
First Variation and Bregman Mirror Descent

4.1.
First variation formula

We now derive a first variation formula for
J^{\tau}
that expresses its derivative in terms of the gradient of the Hamiltonian with respect to the control variable.

Lemma 2

(First variation formula)
.

Under Assumption
A
, for every
u,v\in\mathcal{U}
,
the one-sided directional derivative of
J^{0}
at
u
along the feasible segment from
u
to
v
exists and is given by

(8)

dJ^{0}(u)(v-u)=-\int_{0}^{T}\nabla_{u}\mathcal{H}_{t}^{0}(x_{t},p_{t},u_{t})\cdot(v_{t}-u_{t})\,\mathrm{d}t,

where
(x,p)
is the state-adjoint pair corresponding to
u
.

Moreover, the corresponding one-sided directional derivative of
J^{\tau}
is

(9)

dJ^{\tau}(u)(v-u)=-\int_{0}^{T}\nabla_{u}\mathcal{H}_{t}^{\tau}(x_{t},p_{t},u_{t})\cdot(v_{t}-u_{t})\,\mathrm{d}t,

where

\nabla_{u}\mathcal{H}_{t}^{\tau}(x,p,u)=\nabla_{u}\mathcal{H}_{t}^{0}(x,p,u)-\tau\nabla h(u).

The proof of Lemma
2
is deferred to Appendix
A
.

4.2.
Bregman divergence and the mirror descent algorithm

We next introduce the Bregman divergence induced by the convex regularizer and propose a mirror descent algorithm, based on Pontryagin’s maximum principle, for approximating the optimal control.

Definition 2
.

Assume
h:U\to\mathbb{R}
is convex and differentiable. The Bregman divergence induced by
h
is

(10)

D_{h}(v\mid u):=h(v)-h(u)-\nabla h(u)\cdot(v-u),\qquad u,v\in U.

The corresponding integrated version on the control space is

(11)

\mathcal{D}_{h}(v\mid u):=\int_{0}^{T}D_{h}(v_{t}\mid u_{t})\,\mathrm{d}t,\qquad u,v\in\mathcal{U}.

To ensure sufficient curvature of the mirror map and enable descent estimates for the algorithm, we additionally assume that
h
is uniformly convex.

Assumption B

(Uniform convexity of the mirror map)
.

Assume that
h:U\to\mathbb{R}
is continuously differentiable and there exists
\sigma_{h}>0
such that
for all
u,v\in U
,

(12)

D_{h}(v\mid u)\geq\frac{\sigma_{h}}{2}|v-u|^{2}.

Consequently,

(13)

\mathcal{D}_{h}(v\mid u)\geq\frac{\sigma_{h}}{2}\|v-u\|_{L^{2}}^{2},\qquad\forall u,v\in\mathcal{U}.

Algorithm
1
gives the mirror descent method for optimal control. The method can be viewed as a mirror-descent variant of the classical method of successive approximations.

Algorithm 1

Mirror Descent Algorithm for Optimal Control

1:

Input:

\lambda>0
, initial guess
u^{0}\in\mathcal{U}

2:

for

n=0,1,2,\dots

do

3:

Step 1 (State equation):
Solve

\dot{x}_{t}^{n}=b_{t}(x_{t}^{n},u_{t}^{n}),\qquad x_{0}^{n}=x_{0}.

4:

Step 2 (Adjoint equation):
Solve

\dot{p}_{t}^{n}=-\nabla_{x}\mathcal{H}_{t}^{0}(x_{t}^{n},p_{t}^{n},u_{t}^{n}),\qquad p_{T}^{n}=-\nabla g(x_{T}^{n}).

5:

Step 3 (Mirror update):
For every
t\in[0,T]
, compute

(14)

\displaystyle u_{t}^{n+1}

\displaystyle\in\arg\max_{v\in U}\Big\{\nabla_{u}\mathcal{H}_{t}^{\tau}(x_{t}^{n},p_{t}^{n},u_{t}^{n})\cdot(v-u_{t}^{n})-\lambda D_{h}(v\mid u_{t}^{n})\Big\}

(15)

\displaystyle=\arg\max_{v\in U}\Big\{\nabla_{u}\mathcal{H}_{t}^{0}(x_{t}^{n},p_{t}^{n},u_{t}^{n})\cdot(v-u_{t}^{n})-\tau\nabla h(u_{t}^{n})\cdot(v-u_{t}^{n})-\lambda D_{h}(v\mid u_{t}^{n})\Big\}.

6:

end

for

Remark
.

Euclidean projected gradient descent is a special case of mirror descent. To see this, take

h(u)=\frac{1}{2}|u|^{2}.

Then

D_{h}(v\mid u)=\frac{1}{2}|v-u|^{2},\qquad\nabla h(u)=u.

Hence the mirror step (
15
) becomes

(16)

u_{t}^{n+1}\in\arg\max_{v\in U}\Big\{\nabla_{u}\mathcal{H}_{t}^{0}(x_{t}^{n},p_{t}^{n},u_{t}^{n})\cdot(v-u_{t}^{n})-\tau u_{t}^{n}\cdot(v-u_{t}^{n})-\frac{\lambda}{2}|v-u_{t}^{n}|^{2}\Big\}.

Equivalently,

(17)

u_{t}^{n+1}=\Pi_{U}\left(u_{t}^{n}+\frac{1}{\lambda}\nabla_{u}\mathcal{H}_{t}^{\tau}(x_{t}^{n},p_{t}^{n},u_{t}^{n})\right).

If
U=\mathbb{R}^{m}
, then

(18)

u_{t}^{n+1}=\left(1-\frac{\tau}{\lambda}\right)u_{t}^{n}+\frac{1}{\lambda}\nabla_{u}\mathcal{H}_{t}^{0}(x_{t}^{n},p_{t}^{n},u_{t}^{n}).

Thus Euclidean projected gradient descent is the special case of mirror descent associated with the quadratic mirror map
h(u)=\tfrac{1}{2}|u|^{2}
.

Lemma 3

(Preservation of admissibility)
.

Assume that Assumptions
A
and
B
hold. Let
\{u^{n}\}_{n\geq 0}
be the control sequence generated by Algorithm
1
. If
u^{0}\in\mathcal{U}
, then
u^{n}\in\mathcal{U}
for all
n\geq 0
.

Proof.

Suppose that
u^{n}\in\mathcal{U}
. By definition of the mirror step (
14
), for each
t\in[0,T]
we have

u_{t}^{n+1}\in U
.

Set

a_{t}:=\nabla_{u}\mathcal{H}_{t}^{\tau}(x_{t}^{n},p_{t}^{n},u_{t}^{n}),\qquad\eta_{t}:=a_{t}+\lambda\nabla h(u_{t}^{n}).

Then (
14
) is equivalent to

u_{t}^{n+1}\in\arg\max_{v\in U}\bigl\{\eta_{t}\cdot v-\lambda h(v)\bigr\}.

By Assumption
B
,
h
is
\sigma_{h}
-strongly convex. The same lower quadratic bound on
h
makes
\lambda h(v)-\eta_{t}\cdot v
coercive, so the maximizer exists even when
U
is unbounded; strong convexity gives uniqueness.
It satisfies the variational inequality

\bigl(\eta_{t}-\lambda\nabla h(u_{t}^{n+1})\bigr)\cdot(w-u_{t}^{n+1})\leq 0,\qquad\forall\,w\in U.

Let
s,t\in[0,T]
. Testing the above inequality at time
t
with
w=u_{s}^{n+1}
and at time
s
with
w=u_{t}^{n+1}
, then adding the two inequalities, we obtain

\lambda\bigl(\nabla h(u_{t}^{n+1})-\nabla h(u_{s}^{n+1})\bigr)\cdot(u_{t}^{n+1}-u_{s}^{n+1})\leq(\eta_{t}-\eta_{s})\cdot(u_{t}^{n+1}-u_{s}^{n+1}).

Assumption
B
implies

(\nabla h(u_{t}^{n+1})-\nabla h(u_{s}^{n+1}))\cdot(u_{t}^{n+1}-u_{s}^{n+1})\geq\sigma_{h}|u_{t}^{n+1}-u_{s}^{n+1}|^{2}.

Hence

\lambda\sigma_{h}|u_{t}^{n+1}-u_{s}^{n+1}|^{2}\leq(\eta_{t}-\eta_{s})\cdot(u_{t}^{n+1}-u_{s}^{n+1}).

By the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality,

\lambda\sigma_{h}|u_{t}^{n+1}-u_{s}^{n+1}|^{2}\leq|\eta_{t}-\eta_{s}|\,|u_{t}^{n+1}-u_{s}^{n+1}|,

hence

|u_{t}^{n+1}-u_{s}^{n+1}|\leq\frac{1}{\lambda\sigma_{h}}|\eta_{t}-\eta_{s}|.

Since
u^{n}
,
x^{n}
, and
p^{n}
are continuous and
\nabla_{u}\mathcal{H}_{t}^{\tau}(x,p,u)
is continuous under Assumption
A
, the map
t\mapsto\eta_{t}
is continuous. Therefore
t\mapsto u_{t}^{n+1}
is continuous on
[0,T]
, so
u^{n+1}\in C([0,T];U)=\mathcal{U}
. The conclusion follows by induction on
n
.

5.
Relative Smoothness

This section shows that, under Assumption
B
, the cost functional satisfies a relative smoothness estimate derived from the first variation formula above.

Lemma 4

(Stability of the state and adjoint)
.

Under Assumption
A
, there exist constants
C_{x},C_{p}>0
such that for all
u,v\in\mathcal{U}
, if
x^{u},x^{v}
are the corresponding state trajectories and
p^{u},p^{v}
are the corresponding adjoints, then

(19)

\|x^{v}-x^{u}\|_{L^{2}}\leq C_{x}\,\|v-u\|_{L^{2}},

and

(20)

\|p^{v}-p^{u}\|_{L^{2}}\leq C_{p}\,\|v-u\|_{L^{2}}.

Proof.

Fix
u,v\in\mathcal{U}
and write

\delta u:=v-u,\qquad\delta x_{t}:=x_{t}^{v}-x_{t}^{u},\qquad\delta p_{t}:=p_{t}^{v}-p_{t}^{u}.

Step 1: Estimate for the state.

By the state equation,

\dot{\delta x}_{t}=b_{t}(x_{t}^{v},v_{t})-b_{t}(x_{t}^{u},u_{t}).

Hence, by Assumption
A
(2),

|\delta x_{t}|\leq\int_{0}^{t}|b_{s}(x_{s}^{v},v_{s})-b_{s}(x_{s}^{u},u_{s})|\,\mathrm{d}s\leq M\int_{0}^{t}\bigl(|\delta x_{s}|+|\delta u_{s}|\bigr)\,\mathrm{d}s.

By Grönwall’s inequality,

\sup_{t\in[0,T]}|\delta x_{t}|\leq Me^{MT}\|\delta u\|_{L^{1}}\leq M\sqrt{T}e^{MT}\|\delta u\|_{L^{2}}.

Hence

\|\delta x\|_{L^{2}}\leq\sqrt{T}\sup_{t\in[0,T]}|\delta x_{t}|\leq MTe^{MT}\|\delta u\|_{L^{2}}.

Defining
C_{x}:=MTe^{MT}
proves (
19
).

Step 2: Estimate for the adjoint.

Recall that

\dot{p}_{t}^{u}=-\nabla_{x}b_{t}(x_{t}^{u},u_{t})^{\top}p_{t}^{u}+\nabla_{x}f_{t}(x_{t}^{u},u_{t}),\qquad p_{T}^{u}=-\nabla g(x_{T}^{u}),

and similarly for
p^{v}
. Therefore

\displaystyle\dot{\delta p}_{t}

\displaystyle=-\nabla_{x}b_{t}(x_{t}^{v},v_{t})^{\top}p_{t}^{v}+\nabla_{x}b_{t}(x_{t}^{u},u_{t})^{\top}p_{t}^{u}+\bigl(\nabla_{x}f_{t}(x_{t}^{v},v_{t})-\nabla_{x}f_{t}(x_{t}^{u},u_{t})\bigr)

\displaystyle=-\nabla_{x}b_{t}(x_{t}^{v},v_{t})^{\top}\delta p_{t}-\bigl(\nabla_{x}b_{t}(x_{t}^{v},v_{t})-\nabla_{x}b_{t}(x_{t}^{u},u_{t})\bigr)^{\top}p_{t}^{u}

\displaystyle+\bigl(\nabla_{x}f_{t}(x_{t}^{v},v_{t})-\nabla_{x}f_{t}(x_{t}^{u},u_{t})\bigr).

Also,

\delta p_{T}=-(\nabla g(x_{T}^{v})-\nabla g(x_{T}^{u})).

Hence, using Assumption
A
(2)–(4) and Lemma
1
, for every
t\in[0,T]
,

\displaystyle|\delta p_{t}|

\displaystyle\leq|\delta p_{T}|+\int_{t}^{T}|\nabla_{x}b_{s}(x_{s}^{v},v_{s})|\,|\delta p_{s}|\,\,\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle+\int_{t}^{T}\bigl|\nabla_{x}b_{s}(x_{s}^{v},v_{s})-\nabla_{x}b_{s}(x_{s}^{u},u_{s})\bigr|\,|p_{s}^{u}|\,\,\mathrm{d}s+\int_{t}^{T}\bigl|\nabla_{x}f_{s}(x_{s}^{v},v_{s})-\nabla_{x}f_{s}(x_{s}^{u},u_{s})\bigr|\,\,\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\leq M|\delta x_{T}|+M\int_{t}^{T}|\delta p_{s}|\,\,\mathrm{d}s+M(M_{P}+1)\int_{t}^{T}\bigl(|\delta x_{s}|+|\delta u_{s}|\bigr)\,\,\mathrm{d}s.

Using
Step 1
and Grönwall’s inequality, we obtain

\|\delta p\|_{L^{2}}\leq\sqrt{T}\sup_{t\in[0,T]}|\delta p_{t}|\leq e^{MT}M\Big(C_{x}+(M_{P}+1)(C_{x}+1)T\Big)\|\delta u\|_{L^{2}}=:C_{p}\|\delta u\|_{L^{2}}.

This proves (
20
).
∎

Lemma 5

(Relative smoothness)
.

Under Assumptions
A
and
B
, there exists a positive constant

L
such that for all
u,v\in\mathcal{U}
,

(21)

J^{\tau}(v)-J^{\tau}(u)\leq dJ^{\tau}(u)(v-u)+L\,\mathcal{D}_{h}(v\mid u).

Equivalently, by Lemma
2
,

(22)

J^{\tau}(v)-J^{\tau}(u)\leq-\int_{0}^{T}\nabla_{u}\mathcal{H}_{t}^{\tau}(x_{t}^{u},p_{t}^{u},u_{t})\cdot(v_{t}-u_{t})\,\mathrm{d}t+L\int_{0}^{T}D_{h}(v_{t}\mid u_{t})\,\mathrm{d}t.

Proof.

Fix
u,v\in\mathcal{U}
and set

\delta u:=v-u.

For each
\theta\in[0,1]
, define

u^{\theta}:=u+\theta\delta u.

Let
x^{\theta}
be the state corresponding to
u^{\theta}
, and let
p^{\theta}
be the adjoint associated with

(x^{\theta},u^{\theta})
.

Step 1: Fundamental theorem of calculus along the segment.

Define

\Phi(\theta):=J^{\tau}(u^{\theta}),\qquad\theta\in[0,1].

For
\theta\in[0,1)
, Lemma
2
applied at the base point
u^{\theta}
in the direction
\delta u
gives

\Phi^{\prime}_{+}(\theta)=dJ^{\tau}(u^{\theta})(\delta u)=-\int_{0}^{T}\nabla_{u}\mathcal{H}_{t}^{\tau}(x_{t}^{\theta},p_{t}^{\theta},u_{t}^{\theta})\cdot\delta u_{t}\,\mathrm{d}t.

The right-hand side is continuous in
\theta
, so the fundamental theorem of calculus along the segment yields

(23)

J^{\tau}(v)-J^{\tau}(u)=\int_{0}^{1}\Phi^{\prime}_{+}(\theta)\,\mathrm{d}\theta=-\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{T}\nabla_{u}\mathcal{H}_{t}^{\tau}(x_{t}^{\theta},p_{t}^{\theta},u_{t}^{\theta})\cdot\delta u_{t}\,\mathrm{d}t\,\mathrm{d}\theta.

Subtracting (
9
) from (
23
) gives

(24)

\displaystyle J^{\tau}(v)-J^{\tau}(u)-dJ^{\tau}(u)(v-u)

\displaystyle=-\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{T}\Bigl(\nabla_{u}\mathcal{H}_{t}^{\tau}(x_{t}^{\theta},p_{t}^{\theta},u_{t}^{\theta})-\nabla_{u}\mathcal{H}_{t}^{\tau}(x_{t}^{u},p_{t}^{u},u_{t})\Bigr)\cdot\delta u_{t}\,\mathrm{d}t\,\mathrm{d}\theta.

Step 2: Split the regularized and unregularized parts.

Since

\nabla_{u}\mathcal{H}_{t}^{\tau}(x,p,u)=\nabla_{u}\mathcal{H}_{t}^{0}(x,p,u)-\tau\nabla h(u),

the right-hand side of (
24
) equals

I_{1}+I_{2},

where

I_{1}:=-\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{T}\Bigl(\nabla_{u}\mathcal{H}_{t}^{0}(x_{t}^{\theta},p_{t}^{\theta},u_{t}^{\theta})-\nabla_{u}\mathcal{H}_{t}^{0}(x_{t}^{u},p_{t}^{u},u_{t})\Bigr)\cdot\delta u_{t}\,\mathrm{d}t\,\mathrm{d}\theta,

and

I_{2}:=\tau\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{T}\bigl(\nabla h(u_{t}^{\theta})-\nabla h(u_{t})\bigr)\cdot\delta u_{t}\,\mathrm{d}t\,\mathrm{d}\theta.

By the fundamental theorem of calculus applied pointwise to
h
,

h(v_{t})-h(u_{t})-\nabla h(u_{t})\cdot(v_{t}-u_{t})=\int_{0}^{1}\bigl(\nabla h(u_{t}^{\theta})-\nabla h(u_{t})\bigr)\cdot\delta u_{t}\,\mathrm{d}\theta.

Integrating in time yields

I_{2}=\tau\mathcal{D}_{h}(v\mid u).

Step 3: Estimate the unregularized remainder
I_{1}
.

We first write

\nabla_{u}\mathcal{H}_{t}^{0}(x,p,u)=\nabla_{u}b_{t}(x,u)^{\top}p-\nabla_{u}f_{t}(x,u).

Fix
t\in[0,T]
and two triples
(x,p,u)
,
(\tilde{x},\tilde{p},\tilde{u})
satisfying

|x|,|\tilde{x}|\leq M_{X},\qquad|p|,|\tilde{p}|\leq M_{P}.

Then

\displaystyle\nabla_{u}\mathcal{H}_{t}^{0}(x,p,u)-\nabla_{u}\mathcal{H}_{t}^{0}(\tilde{x},\tilde{p},\tilde{u})

\displaystyle=\bigl(\nabla_{u}b_{t}(x,u)-\nabla_{u}b_{t}(\tilde{x},\tilde{u})\bigr)^{\top}p+\nabla_{u}b_{t}(\tilde{x},\tilde{u})^{\top}(p-\tilde{p})-\bigl(\nabla_{u}f_{t}(x,u)-\nabla_{u}f_{t}(\tilde{x},\tilde{u})\bigr).

Hence,

\displaystyle\bigl|\nabla_{u}\mathcal{H}_{t}^{0}(x,p,u)-\nabla_{u}\mathcal{H}_{t}^{0}(\tilde{x},\tilde{p},\tilde{u})\bigr|

\displaystyle\leq|p|\,\bigl\|\nabla_{u}b_{t}(x,u)-\nabla_{u}b_{t}(\tilde{x},\tilde{u})\bigr\|+\|\nabla_{u}b_{t}(\tilde{x},\tilde{u})\|\,|p-\tilde{p}|+\bigl\|\nabla_{u}f_{t}(x,u)-\nabla_{u}f_{t}(\tilde{x},\tilde{u})\bigr\|.

By the mean value theorem,

\bigl\|\nabla_{u}b_{t}(x,u)-\nabla_{u}b_{t}(\tilde{x},\tilde{u})\bigr\|\leq M|x-\tilde{x}|+M_{b,uu}|u-\tilde{u}|,

and similarly,

\bigl\|\nabla_{u}f_{t}(x,u)-\nabla_{u}f_{t}(\tilde{x},\tilde{u})\bigr\|\leq M|x-\tilde{x}|+M_{f,uu}|u-\tilde{u}|.

Therefore,

\displaystyle\bigl|\nabla_{u}\mathcal{H}_{t}^{0}(x,p,u)-\nabla_{u}\mathcal{H}_{t}^{0}(\tilde{x},\tilde{p},\tilde{u})\bigr|

\displaystyle\leq M_{P}\bigl(M|x-\tilde{x}|+M_{b,uu}|u-\tilde{u}|\bigr)+M|p-\tilde{p}|+M|x-\tilde{x}|+M_{f,uu}|u-\tilde{u}|

\displaystyle=M\bigl(M_{P}+1\bigr)|x-\tilde{x}|+M|p-\tilde{p}|+\bigl(M_{P}M_{b,uu}+M_{f,uu}\bigr)|u-\tilde{u}|.

In summary, for all
t\in[0,T]
and all

(x,p,u),(\tilde{x},\tilde{p},\tilde{u})
with
|x|,|\tilde{x}|\leq M_{X}
and
|p|,|\tilde{p}|\leq M_{P}
,

(25)

\bigl|\nabla_{u}\mathcal{H}_{t}^{0}(x,p,u)-\nabla_{u}\mathcal{H}_{t}^{0}(\tilde{x},\tilde{p},\tilde{u})\bigr|\leq C_{H}\bigl(|x-\tilde{x}|+|p-\tilde{p}|+|u-\tilde{u}|\bigr),

where

C_{H}:=\max\Bigl\{M(M_{P}+1),\,M_{P}M_{b,uu}+M_{f,uu}\Bigr\}.

Applying (
25
) with
(x,p,u)=(x_{t}^{\theta},p_{t}^{\theta},u_{t}^{\theta})
and

(\tilde{x},\tilde{p},\tilde{u})=(x_{t}^{u},p_{t}^{u},u_{t})
, we obtain

\displaystyle|I_{1}|

\displaystyle\leq C_{H}\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{T}\Bigl(|x_{t}^{\theta}-x_{t}^{u}|+|p_{t}^{\theta}-p_{t}^{u}|+|u_{t}^{\theta}-u_{t}|\Bigr)|\delta u_{t}|\,\mathrm{d}t\,\mathrm{d}\theta.

By the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality,

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\Bigl(|x_{t}^{\theta}-x_{t}^{u}|+|p_{t}^{\theta}-p_{t}^{u}|+|u_{t}^{\theta}-u_{t}|\Bigr)|\delta u_{t}|\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\qquad\leq\Bigl(\|x^{\theta}-x^{u}\|_{L^{2}}+\|p^{\theta}-p^{u}\|_{L^{2}}+\theta\|\delta u\|_{L^{2}}\Bigr)\|\delta u\|_{L^{2}}.

By Lemma
4
,

\|x^{\theta}-x^{u}\|_{L^{2}}+\|p^{\theta}-p^{u}\|_{L^{2}}\leq(C_{x}+C_{p})\|u^{\theta}-u\|_{L^{2}}=\theta(C_{x}+C_{p})\|\delta u\|_{L^{2}}.

Hence

\displaystyle|I_{1}|

\displaystyle\leq C_{H}\int_{0}^{1}\theta(C_{x}+C_{p}+1)\|\delta u\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\theta=\dfrac{C_{H}}{2}(C_{x}+C_{p}+1)\|\delta u\|_{L^{2}}^{2}.

Therefore, by (
13
),

|I_{1}|\leq\frac{C_{H}(C_{x}+C_{p}+1)}{\sigma_{h}}\mathcal{D}_{h}(v\mid u).

Step 4: Conclusion.

Combining the estimates for
I_{1}
and
I_{2}
with (
24
), we obtain

J^{\tau}(v)-J^{\tau}(u)-dJ^{\tau}(u)(v-u)\leq\left(\tau+\frac{C_{H}(C_{x}+C_{p}+1)}{\sigma_{h}}\right)\mathcal{D}_{h}(v\mid u).

Thus (
21
) holds with

L:=\tau+\frac{C_{H}(C_{x}+C_{p}+1)}{\sigma_{h}}.

This completes the proof.
∎

6.
Energy Dissipation

Using Lemma
5
, we now show that the cost functional decreases monotonically along the iterates
\{u^{n}\}_{n\geq 0}
.

Theorem 3

(Energy dissipation)
.

Under Assumptions
A
and
B
, let
\lambda\geq L
and let
\{u^{n}\}_{n\geq 0}
be the sequence generated by Algorithm
1
. Then

(26)

J^{\tau}(u^{n})-J^{\tau}(u^{n+1})\geq(\lambda-L)\,\mathcal{D}_{h}(u^{n+1}\mid u^{n})\geq 0,\qquad n\geq 0.

In particular, if
\lambda>L
and
\inf_{u\in\mathcal{U}}J^{\tau}(u)>-\infty
, then

(27)

\mathcal{D}_{h}(u^{n+1}\mid u^{n})\to 0.

Proof.

Apply (
22
) with
u=u^{n}
and
v=u^{n+1}
:

J^{\tau}(u^{n+1})-J^{\tau}(u^{n})\leq-\int_{0}^{T}\nabla_{u}\mathcal{H}_{t}^{\tau}(x_{t}^{n},p_{t}^{n},u_{t}^{n})\cdot(u_{t}^{n+1}-u_{t}^{n})\,\mathrm{d}t+L\mathcal{D}_{h}(u^{n+1}\mid u^{n}).

By the definition of the mirror step (
14
), for every
t\in[0,T]
,

\nabla_{u}\mathcal{H}_{t}^{\tau}(x_{t}^{n},p_{t}^{n},u_{t}^{n})\cdot(u_{t}^{n+1}-u_{t}^{n})-\lambda D_{h}(u_{t}^{n+1}\mid u_{t}^{n})\geq\\
\geq\nabla_{u}\mathcal{H}_{t}^{\tau}(x_{t}^{n},p_{t}^{n},u_{t}^{n})\cdot(u_{t}^{n}-u_{t}^{n})-\lambda D_{h}(u_{t}^{n}\mid u_{t}^{n})=0.

Integrating over
t
yields

\int_{0}^{T}\nabla_{u}\mathcal{H}_{t}^{\tau}(x_{t}^{n},p_{t}^{n},u_{t}^{n})\cdot(u_{t}^{n+1}-u_{t}^{n})\,\mathrm{d}t\geq\lambda\mathcal{D}_{h}(u^{n+1}\mid u^{n}).

Substituting this estimate back into the relative smoothness inequality,

J^{\tau}(u^{n+1})-J^{\tau}(u^{n})\leq-(\lambda-L)\mathcal{D}_{h}(u^{n+1}\mid u^{n})\leq 0,

since
\lambda\geq L
. This proves (
26
).

If, in addition,
\lambda>L
and
\inf_{u\in\mathcal{U}}J^{\tau}(u)>-\infty
, then summing in
n
gives

\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}(\lambda-L)\mathcal{D}_{h}(u^{n+1}\mid u^{n})\leq J^{\tau}(u^{0})-J^{\tau}(u^{N})\leq J^{\tau}(u^{0})-\inf_{u\in\mathcal{U}}J^{\tau}(u).

Since
\lambda-L>0
, we conclude that
\sum_{n\geq 0}\mathcal{D}_{h}(u^{n+1}\mid u^{n})<\infty
and, in particular,

\mathcal{D}_{h}(u^{n+1}\mid u^{n})\to 0.\qed

7.
Relative Convexity

In this section, we establish a relative convexity property of the cost functional with modulus
\tau
.

Assumption C

(Convexity of the control problem)
.

The terminal cost
g
is convex, and for each
t\in[0,T]
and
p\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
the map

(x,u)\mapsto\mathcal{H}_{t}^{0}(x,p,u)

is concave.

Remark
.

While the uniform concavity condition seems restrictive, it is a standard assumption to guarantee the sufficiency of the Pontryagin’s maximum principle, see
[
10
, Chapter 3, Theorem 2.5]
.

Theorem 4

(Relative convexity)
.

Suppose that Assumptions
A
and
C
hold. Then, for all
u,v\in\mathcal{U}
,

(28)

J^{\tau}(v)-J^{\tau}(u)\geq dJ^{\tau}(u)(v-u)+\tau\mathcal{D}_{h}(v\mid u).

Equivalently, by Lemma
2
,

(29)

J^{\tau}(v)-J^{\tau}(u)\geq-\int_{0}^{T}\nabla_{u}\mathcal{H}_{t}^{\tau}(x_{t},p_{t},u_{t})\cdot(v_{t}-u_{t})\,\mathrm{d}t+\tau\int_{0}^{T}D_{h}(v_{t}\mid u_{t})\,\mathrm{d}t,

where
(x,p)
is the state-adjoint pair associated with
u
.

Thus the regularization parameter
\tau
serves as a modulus of relative convexity with respect to the Bregman geometry induced by
h
.

Proof.

Let
x
and
x^{\prime}
be the states associated with
u
and
v
, respectively, and let
p
be the adjoint associated with
u
:

\dot{p}_{t}=-\nabla_{x}\mathcal{H}_{t}^{0}(x_{t},p_{t},u_{t}),\qquad p_{T}=-\nabla g(x_{T}).

We start from

J^{\tau}(u)-J^{\tau}(v)=g(x_{T})-g(x_{T}^{\prime})+\int_{0}^{T}\bigl(f_{t}(x_{t},u_{t})-f_{t}(x_{t}^{\prime},v_{t})\bigr)\,\mathrm{d}t+\tau\int_{0}^{T}\bigl(h(u_{t})-h(v_{t})\bigr)\,\mathrm{d}t.

By convexity of
g
,

g(x_{T})-g(x_{T}^{\prime})\leq\nabla g(x_{T})\cdot(x_{T}-x_{T}^{\prime})=-p_{T}\cdot(x_{T}-x_{T}^{\prime}).

Using integration by parts and the state and adjoint equations,

p_{T}\cdot(x_{T}-x_{T}^{\prime})=\int_{0}^{T}p_{t}\cdot(\dot{x}_{t}-\dot{x}_{t}^{\prime})\,\mathrm{d}t-\int_{0}^{T}\nabla_{x}\mathcal{H}_{t}^{0}(x_{t},p_{t},u_{t})\cdot(x_{t}-x_{t}^{\prime})\,\mathrm{d}t.

Since
\dot{x}_{t}=b_{t}(x_{t},u_{t})
and
\dot{x}_{t}^{\prime}=b_{t}(x_{t}^{\prime},v_{t})
, we obtain

p_{T}\cdot(x_{T}-x_{T}^{\prime})=\int_{0}^{T}\Bigl[p_{t}\cdot\bigl(b_{t}(x_{t},u_{t})-b_{t}(x_{t}^{\prime},v_{t})\bigr)-\nabla_{x}\mathcal{H}_{t}^{0}(x_{t},p_{t},u_{t})\cdot(x_{t}-x_{t}^{\prime})\Bigr]\,\mathrm{d}t.

Therefore

\displaystyle J^{\tau}(u)-J^{\tau}(v)

\displaystyle\leq-\int_{0}^{T}\Bigl[\mathcal{H}_{t}^{0}(x_{t},p_{t},u_{t})-\mathcal{H}_{t}^{0}(x_{t}^{\prime},p_{t},v_{t})+\nabla_{x}\mathcal{H}_{t}^{0}(x_{t},p_{t},u_{t})\cdot(x_{t}^{\prime}-x_{t})\Bigr]\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\quad+\tau\int_{0}^{T}\bigl(h(u_{t})-h(v_{t})\bigr)\,\mathrm{d}t.

Now use the concavity of
(x,u)\mapsto\mathcal{H}_{t}^{0}(x,p_{t},u)
:

\mathcal{H}_{t}^{0}(x_{t}^{\prime},p_{t},v_{t})\leq\mathcal{H}_{t}^{0}(x_{t},p_{t},u_{t})+\nabla_{x}\mathcal{H}_{t}^{0}(x_{t},p_{t},u_{t})\cdot(x_{t}^{\prime}-x_{t})+\nabla_{u}\mathcal{H}_{t}^{0}(x_{t},p_{t},u_{t})\cdot(v_{t}-u_{t}).

Rearranging,

\mathcal{H}_{t}^{0}(x_{t},p_{t},u_{t})-\mathcal{H}_{t}^{0}(x_{t}^{\prime},p_{t},v_{t})+\nabla_{x}\mathcal{H}_{t}^{0}(x_{t},p_{t},u_{t})\cdot(x_{t}^{\prime}-x_{t})\geq-\nabla_{u}\mathcal{H}_{t}^{0}(x_{t},p_{t},u_{t})\cdot(v_{t}-u_{t}).

Hence

J^{\tau}(u)-J^{\tau}(v)\leq\int_{0}^{T}\nabla_{u}\mathcal{H}_{t}^{0}(x_{t},p_{t},u_{t})\cdot(v_{t}-u_{t})\,\mathrm{d}t+\tau\int_{0}^{T}\bigl(h(u_{t})-h(v_{t})\bigr)\,\mathrm{d}t.

Using the identity

h(v_{t})-h(u_{t})=D_{h}(v_{t}\mid u_{t})+\nabla h(u_{t})\cdot(v_{t}-u_{t}),

that is,

h(u_{t})-h(v_{t})=-D_{h}(v_{t}\mid u_{t})-\nabla h(u_{t})\cdot(v_{t}-u_{t}),

we obtain

J^{\tau}(u)-J^{\tau}(v)\leq\int_{0}^{T}\bigl(\nabla_{u}\mathcal{H}_{t}^{0}(x_{t},p_{t},u_{t})-\tau\nabla h(u_{t})\bigr)\cdot(v_{t}-u_{t})\,\mathrm{d}t-\tau\int_{0}^{T}D_{h}(v_{t}\mid u_{t})\,\mathrm{d}t,

that is,

J^{\tau}(v)-J^{\tau}(u)\geq-\int_{0}^{T}\bigl(\nabla_{u}\mathcal{H}_{t}^{0}(x_{t},p_{t},u_{t})-\tau\nabla h(u_{t})\bigr)\cdot(v_{t}-u_{t})\,\mathrm{d}t+\tau\int_{0}^{T}D_{h}(v_{t}\mid u_{t})\,\mathrm{d}t.

Since

\nabla_{u}\mathcal{H}_{t}^{\tau}(x_{t},p_{t},u_{t})=\nabla_{u}\mathcal{H}_{t}^{0}(x_{t},p_{t},u_{t})-\tau\nabla h(u_{t}),

the claimed inequality (
29
) follows.
∎

8.
Convergence Rates

We now combine the relative smoothness and relative convexity properties to obtain convergence rates for the mirror descent algorithm.

Lemma 6

(Three-point inequality)
.

Fix
u,\bar{u},w\in U
and
\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
. Suppose that

\bar{u}\in\arg\max_{v\in U}\bigl\{\xi\cdot(v-u)-\lambda D_{h}(v\mid u)\bigr\}.

Then

(30)

\xi\cdot(w-u)-\lambda D_{h}(w\mid u)\leq\xi\cdot(\bar{u}-u)-\lambda D_{h}(\bar{u}\mid u)-\lambda D_{h}(w\mid\bar{u}).

Proof.

Define

\Phi(v):=\xi\cdot(v-u)-\lambda D_{h}(v\mid u)=\xi\cdot(v-u)-\lambda h(v)+\lambda h(u)+\lambda\nabla h(u)\cdot(v-u).

Since
h
is convex,
\Phi
is concave, with

\nabla\Phi(v)=\xi-\lambda\nabla h(v)+\lambda\nabla h(u).

By optimality of
\bar{u}
and the first-order variational inequality,

\bigl(\xi-\lambda\nabla h(\bar{u})+\lambda\nabla h(u)\bigr)\cdot(w-\bar{u})\leq 0\qquad\forall w\in U.

Rearranging,

\xi\cdot(w-\bar{u})\leq\lambda\bigl(\nabla h(\bar{u})-\nabla h(u)\bigr)\cdot(w-\bar{u}).

Add
\xi\cdot(\bar{u}-u)
to both sides to obtain

\xi\cdot(w-u)\leq\xi\cdot(\bar{u}-u)+\lambda\bigl(\nabla h(\bar{u})-\nabla h(u)\bigr)\cdot(w-\bar{u}).

Now apply the standard Bregman identity

D_{h}(w\mid u)-D_{h}(\bar{u}\mid u)-D_{h}(w\mid\bar{u})=\bigl(\nabla h(\bar{u})-\nabla h(u)\bigr)\cdot(w-\bar{u}),

which yields (
30
).
∎

Theorem 5

(Convergence rates)
.

Under Assumptions
A
,
B
, and
C
, let
\lambda\geq L
, where
L
is the relative-smoothness constant in Lemma
5
. Let
\{u^{n}\}_{n\geq 0}
be the sequence generated by Algorithm
1
.

(i)

If
\tau=0
, then for any
v\in\mathcal{U}
such that
\mathcal{D}_{h}(v\mid u^{n})<\infty
for all
n
,

J^{0}(u^{n})-J^{0}(v)\leq\frac{\lambda}{n}\mathcal{D}_{h}(v\mid u^{0}),\qquad n\geq 1.

(ii)

If
\tau>0
and
u^{\ast}
minimizes
J^{\tau}
over
\mathcal{U}
, then

0\leq J^{\tau}(u^{n})-J^{\tau}(u^{\ast})\leq\lambda\Bigl(1-\frac{\tau}{\lambda}\Bigr)^{n-1}\mathcal{D}_{h}(u^{\ast}\mid u^{0}),\qquad n\geq 1.

Thus, in the unregularized case (
\tau=0
), the scheme attains the standard mirror-descent rate
O(1/n)
, while a positive regularization parameter (
\tau>0
) improves the convergence to a geometric (exponential) rate.

Proof.

Fix
n\geq 0
. By the mirror-step definition (
14
), applying Lemma
6
pointwise in time with

u=u_{t}^{n},\qquad\bar{u}=u_{t}^{n+1},\qquad w=v_{t},\qquad\xi=\nabla_{u}\mathcal{H}_{t}^{\tau}(x_{t}^{n},p_{t}^{n},u_{t}^{n}),

yields

\nabla_{u}\mathcal{H}_{t}^{\tau}(x_{t}^{n},p_{t}^{n},u_{t}^{n})\cdot(v_{t}-u_{t}^{n})-\lambda D_{h}(v_{t}\mid u_{t}^{n})\\
\leq\nabla_{u}\mathcal{H}_{t}^{\tau}(x_{t}^{n},p_{t}^{n},u_{t}^{n})\cdot(u_{t}^{n+1}-u_{t}^{n})-\lambda D_{h}(u_{t}^{n+1}\mid u_{t}^{n})-\lambda D_{h}(v_{t}\mid u_{t}^{n+1}).

Integrating in time gives

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\nabla_{u}\mathcal{H}_{t}^{\tau}(x_{t}^{n},p_{t}^{n},u_{t}^{n})\cdot(v_{t}-u_{t}^{n})\,\mathrm{d}t

(31)

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{T}\nabla_{u}\mathcal{H}_{t}^{\tau}(x_{t}^{n},p_{t}^{n},u_{t}^{n})\cdot(u_{t}^{n+1}-u_{t}^{n})\,\mathrm{d}t-\lambda\mathcal{D}_{h}(u^{n+1}\mid u^{n})-\lambda\mathcal{D}_{h}(v\mid u^{n+1})+\lambda\mathcal{D}_{h}(v\mid u^{n}).

On the other hand, the relative smoothness (
22
) in Lemma
5
applied to
(u^{n},u^{n+1})
gives

J^{\tau}(u^{n+1})\leq J^{\tau}(u^{n})-\int_{0}^{T}\nabla_{u}\mathcal{H}_{t}^{\tau}(x_{t}^{n},p_{t}^{n},u_{t}^{n})\cdot(u_{t}^{n+1}-u_{t}^{n})\,\mathrm{d}t+L\mathcal{D}_{h}(u^{n+1}\mid u^{n}).

Substituting (
31
) and using
\lambda\geq L
gives

\displaystyle J^{\tau}(u^{n+1})

\displaystyle\leq J^{\tau}(u^{n})-\int_{0}^{T}\nabla_{u}\mathcal{H}_{t}^{\tau}(x_{t}^{n},p_{t}^{n},u_{t}^{n})\cdot(v_{t}-u_{t}^{n})\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\qquad+\lambda\mathcal{D}_{h}(v\mid u^{n})-\lambda\mathcal{D}_{h}(v\mid u^{n+1})-(\lambda-L)\mathcal{D}_{h}(u^{n+1}\mid u^{n})

(32)

\displaystyle\leq J^{\tau}(u^{n})-\int_{0}^{T}\nabla_{u}\mathcal{H}_{t}^{\tau}(x_{t}^{n},p_{t}^{n},u_{t}^{n})\cdot(v_{t}-u_{t}^{n})\,\mathrm{d}t+\lambda\mathcal{D}_{h}(v\mid u^{n})-\lambda\mathcal{D}_{h}(v\mid u^{n+1}).

Now apply relative convexity (
29
) with
u=u^{n}
and
v
arbitrary:

J^{\tau}(v)\geq J^{\tau}(u^{n})-\int_{0}^{T}\nabla_{u}\mathcal{H}_{t}^{\tau}(x_{t}^{n},p_{t}^{n},u_{t}^{n})\cdot(v_{t}-u_{t}^{n})\,\mathrm{d}t+\tau\mathcal{D}_{h}(v\mid u^{n}).

Substituting this lower bound for the middle term in (
32
), we obtain

(33)

J^{\tau}(u^{n+1})-J^{\tau}(v)\leq(\lambda-\tau)\mathcal{D}_{h}(v\mid u^{n})-\lambda\mathcal{D}_{h}(v\mid u^{n+1}).

Case 1:
\tau=0
.

Then (
33
) becomes

J^{0}(u^{n+1})-J^{0}(v)\leq\lambda\mathcal{D}_{h}(v\mid u^{n})-\lambda\mathcal{D}_{h}(v\mid u^{n+1}).

Summing from
n=0
to
m-1
,

\sum_{n=0}^{m-1}\bigl(J^{0}(u^{n+1})-J^{0}(v)\bigr)\leq\lambda\mathcal{D}_{h}(v\mid u^{0})-\lambda\mathcal{D}_{h}(v\mid u^{m})\leq\lambda\mathcal{D}_{h}(v\mid u^{0}).

By the energy dissipation property,
J^{0}(u^{m})\leq J^{0}(u^{n+1})
for all
n=0,\dots,m-1
, so

m\bigl(J^{0}(u^{m})-J^{0}(v)\bigr)\leq\sum_{n=0}^{m-1}\bigl(J^{0}(u^{n+1})-J^{0}(v)\bigr)\leq\lambda\mathcal{D}_{h}(v\mid u^{0}).

Hence

J^{0}(u^{m})-J^{0}(v)\leq\frac{\lambda}{m}\mathcal{D}_{h}(v\mid u^{0}).

Case 2:
\tau>0
.

Let
v=u^{\ast}
, where
u^{\ast}
minimizes
J^{\tau}
. Since

J^{\tau}(u^{n+1})-J^{\tau}(u^{\ast})\geq 0,

the inequality (
33
) implies

\lambda\mathcal{D}_{h}(u^{\ast}\mid u^{n+1})\leq(\lambda-\tau)\mathcal{D}_{h}(u^{\ast}\mid u^{n}),

that is,

\mathcal{D}_{h}(u^{\ast}\mid u^{n+1})\leq\Bigl(1-\frac{\tau}{\lambda}\Bigr)\mathcal{D}_{h}(u^{\ast}\mid u^{n}).

Iterating,

\mathcal{D}_{h}(u^{\ast}\mid u^{n})\leq\Bigl(1-\frac{\tau}{\lambda}\Bigr)^{n}\mathcal{D}_{h}(u^{\ast}\mid u^{0}).

Finally, returning to (
33
) with
v=u^{\ast}
and dropping the nonpositive term

-\lambda\mathcal{D}_{h}(u^{\ast}\mid u^{n+1})
, we obtain

J^{\tau}(u^{n+1})-J^{\tau}(u^{\ast})\leq(\lambda-\tau)\mathcal{D}_{h}(u^{\ast}\mid u^{n})\leq\lambda\mathcal{D}_{h}(u^{\ast}\mid u^{n}).

Hence

J^{\tau}(u^{n+1})-J^{\tau}(u^{\ast})\leq\lambda\Bigl(1-\frac{\tau}{\lambda}\Bigr)^{n}\mathcal{D}_{h}(u^{\ast}\mid u^{0}).

Reindexing
n\mapsto n-1
yields the claimed geometric estimate.
∎

9.
Numerical Examples

9.1.
A one-dimensional linear-quadratic example

We consider a one-dimensional linear-quadratic (LQ) optimal control problem for which the optimal solution is explicit. Let

\dot{x}_{t}=ax_{t}+u_{t},\qquad x_{0}=\xi,\qquad t\in[0,T],

and define the regularized cost functional

J^{\tau}(u)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{T}\Big(qx_{t}^{2}+\tau u_{t}^{2}\Big)\,\mathrm{d}t+\frac{s}{2}x_{T}^{2},

where
a\in\mathbb{R}
,
q\geq 0
,
s\geq 0
. Here
\tau
is the effective quadratic weight on the control, which also plays the role of regularization. To ensure the existence of an optimal solution, we set
\tau>0
.

This corresponds to the data

b(x,u)=ax+u,\qquad f(x,u)=\frac{1}{2}qx^{2},\qquad h(u)=\frac{1}{2}u^{2},\qquad g(x)=\frac{s}{2}x^{2}.

The regularized Hamiltonian is given by

\mathcal{H}_{t}^{\tau}(x,p,u)=p(ax+u)-\frac{1}{2}qx^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\tau u^{2}.

The Pontryagin maximum principle yields the state and adjoint equations

\dot{x}_{t}=ax_{t}+u_{t},\qquad x_{0}=\xi,

\dot{p}_{t}=-\nabla_{x}\mathcal{H}_{t}^{0}(x_{t},p_{t},u_{t})=-ap_{t}+qx_{t},\qquad p_{T}=-sx_{T},

together with the maximum condition

0=\nabla_{u}\mathcal{H}_{t}^{\tau}(x_{t},p_{t},u_{t})=p_{t}-\tau u_{t}.

Hence the optimal control satisfies

u_{t}^{*}=\frac{p_{t}^{*}}{\tau}.

Introducing the ansatz
p_{t}^{*}=-P(t)x_{t}^{*}
, one obtains the Riccati equation

P^{\prime}(t)=\frac{1}{\tau}P(t)^{2}-2aP(t)-q,\qquad P(T)=s.

In one dimension, this equation admits an explicit solution. Setting

\gamma:=\sqrt{a^{2}+\frac{q}{\tau}},

and defining

P_{\pm}=\tau(a\pm\gamma),\qquad\kappa=\frac{s-P_{+}}{s-P_{-}},

we obtain

P(t)=\frac{P_{+}-\kappa e^{-2\gamma(T-t)}P_{-}}{1-\kappa e^{-2\gamma(T-t)}}.

The optimal state and control are then given by

x_{t}^{*}=\xi\exp\!\left(\int_{0}^{t}\Big(a-\frac{P(\sigma)}{\tau}\Big)\,\mathrm{d}\sigma\right),\qquad u_{t}^{*}=-\frac{P(t)}{\tau}x_{t}^{*}.

In the Euclidean case
h(u)=\frac{1}{2}|u|^{2}
and
U=\mathbb{R}
, the mirror descent update reduces to

u_{t}^{n+1}=\left(1-\frac{\tau}{\lambda}\right)u_{t}^{n}+\frac{1}{\lambda}\nabla_{u}\mathcal{H}_{t}^{0}(x_{t}^{n},p_{t}^{n},u_{t}^{n}).

Since
\nabla_{u}\mathcal{H}_{t}^{0}(x,p,u)=p
,
we obtain the explicit update

u_{t}^{n+1}=\left(1-\frac{\tau}{\lambda}\right)u_{t}^{n}+\frac{1}{\lambda}p_{t}^{n}.

In the numerical experiments, we take

T=1,\quad\xi=0.5,\quad a=1,\quad q=1,\quad s=1,\quad\lambda=30,

and initialize the algorithm with a constant control
u_{t}^{0}\equiv 4
. The result is plotted in Figure
1
. We observe clearly geometric convergence.

Figure 1
.
Objective convergence of Algorithm
1
for the one-dimensional linear-quadratic example with different values of the regularization parameter
\tau
. The vertical axis shows the cost error
|J^{\tau}(u^{n})-J^{\tau}(u^{*})|
on a logarithmic scale.

9.2.
A one-dimensional quartic terminal example illustrating the rate difference between
\tau=0
and
\tau>0

We next present a simple one-dimensional control problem in which the convergence behavior differs qualitatively between the cases
\tau=0
and
\tau>0
, due to the lack of strong convexity.

Consider state dynamics

\dot{x}_{t}=u_{t},\qquad x_{0}=0,\qquad t\in[0,T],

and define the cost functional

J^{\tau}(u)=\frac{1}{4}x_{T}^{4}+\frac{\tau}{2}\int_{0}^{T}u_{t}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}t,\qquad x_{T}=\int_{0}^{T}u_{t}\,\mathrm{d}t.

For
\tau>0
, the unique optimal control is

u_{t}^{*}\equiv 0,\qquad x_{t}^{*}\equiv 0.

For
\tau=0
, every control with zero terminal state is optimal, including
u_{t}^{*}\equiv 0
.

The regularized Hamiltonian is

\mathcal{H}_{t}^{\tau}(x,p,u)=pu-\frac{\tau}{2}u^{2}.

The adjoint equation is

\dot{p}_{t}=0,\qquad p_{T}=-x_{T}^{3}.

Hence

p_{t}\equiv p_{T}=-x_{T}^{3}.

In the Euclidean case
h(u)=\frac{1}{2}u^{2}
and
U=\mathbb{R}
, Algorithm
1
reduces to

u_{t}^{n+1}=u_{t}^{n}+\frac{1}{\lambda}\bigl(p_{t}^{n}-\tau u_{t}^{n}\bigr).

If the initial guess is chosen to be constant, say

u_{t}^{0}\equiv\alpha_{0},

then all iterates remain constant in time, and we may write

u_{t}^{n}\equiv\alpha_{n}.

Since

x_{t}^{n}=\alpha_{n}t,\qquad x_{T}^{n}=T\alpha_{n},\qquad p_{t}^{n}\equiv-(T\alpha_{n})^{3},

the algorithm reduces to the scalar iteration

\alpha_{n+1}=\alpha_{n}-\frac{1}{\lambda}\bigl(T^{3}\alpha_{n}^{3}+\tau\alpha_{n}\bigr).

Thus, when
\tau=0
, the linear term vanishes and the iteration reduces to

\alpha_{n+1}=\alpha_{n}-\frac{T^{3}}{\lambda}\alpha_{n}^{3},

so that the leading-order behavior is governed by the cubic term, yielding only sublinear convergence.

In contrast, when
\tau>0
, the additional linear damping term
-\frac{\tau}{\lambda}\alpha_{n}
dominates near the optimum
\alpha^{*}=0
. More precisely, as
\alpha_{n}\to 0
, the cubic term is of higher order and can be neglected, so that the iteration can be linearized as

\alpha_{n+1}\approx\left(1-\frac{\tau}{\lambda}\right)\alpha_{n}.

Consequently, the iteration behaves asymptotically like a linear contraction, and the convergence becomes geometric.

In the numerical experiments, we set
T=1
and
\lambda=10
, and initialize the algorithm with a constant control
u_{t}^{0}\equiv 2
. The results are shown in Figure
2
, which illustrates the different convergence behaviors for
\tau=0
and
\tau>0
.

(a)

Semilog scale

(b)

Log-log scale

.

1
for the one-dimensional quartic terminal example with different values of the regularization parameter
\tau
. The cost error
|J^{\tau}(u^{n})-J^{\tau}(u^{*})|
is shown on semilog (left) and log-log (right) scales, highlighting the difference between exponential convergence for
\tau>0
and sublinear behavior when
\tau=0
.

Therefore, this example provides a simple control problem in which the regularization parameter
\tau
qualitatively changes the convergence rate: the unregularized case is only sublinear, whereas any positive regularization yields geometric convergence near the optimum.

9.3.
A high-dimensional nonlinear example with coupled dynamics

We next study how the mirror descent behaves as the state dimension increases. To this end, we consider a family of fully coupled nonlinear
control problems in dimensions

d\in\{5,10,20\}.

For each dimension
d
, we consider the controlled system

\dot{x}_{t}=Ax_{t}+Bu_{t}+\gamma\sin(Cx_{t}),\qquad x_{0}=x^{\rm init},\qquad t\in[0,T],

where
x_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and the control
u_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. The sine nonlinearity is applied componentwise, and the matrices

A,B,C\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}

are chosen to be dense so that the coordinates are genuinely coupled and the
problem is not reducible to independent one-dimensional subsystems.

The cost functional is given by

J^{\tau}(u)=\frac{q}{2d}\int_{0}^{T}|x_{t}|^{2}\,\,\mathrm{d}t+\frac{s}{2d}|x_{T}-x^{\rm tar}|^{2}+\frac{\tau}{2}\int_{0}^{T}|u_{t}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}t.

The normalization by
d
is included so that the objective remains comparable across dimensions.

The corresponding Hamiltonian is

\mathcal{H}_{t}^{0}(x,p,u)=p^{\top}\bigl(Ax+Bu+\gamma\sin(Cx)\bigr)-\frac{q}{2d}|x|^{2}.

The adjoint equation takes the form

\dot{p}_{t}=-\left(A^{\top}+\gamma C^{\top}\operatorname{diag}(\cos(Cx_{t}))\right)p_{t}+\frac{q}{d}x_{t},\qquad p_{T}=-\frac{s}{d}(x_{T}-x^{\rm tar}).

For
h(u)=\frac{1}{2}\|u\|^{2}
and
U=\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the Euclidean mirror update becomes

u_{t}^{n+1}=u_{t}^{n}+\frac{1}{\lambda}\left(B^{\top}p_{t}^{n}-\tau u_{t}^{n}\right).

In the numerical experiments, we fix
T=1
and discretize the time interval using
N_{t}=500
uniform steps. The state dimension is varied over
d\in\{5,10,20\}
. The system matrices are generated randomly as

A=0.15\frac{M_{1}}{\sqrt{d}}-0.6I,\quad B=0.6\frac{M_{2}}{\sqrt{d}}+0.3I,\quad C=0.8\frac{M_{3}}{\sqrt{d}},

where
M_{1},M_{2},M_{3}
have i.i.d. standard normal entries. The cost parameters are chosen as

q=1,\quad s=5,\quad\gamma=1,\quad\tau=0.5.

The mirror descent step size is set to
\lambda=20
. The initial state and target are defined componentwise by

x^{\mathrm{init}}_{i}=0.4\sin\left(\frac{i}{d}\pi\right),\quad x^{\mathrm{tar}}_{i}=0.8\cos\left(\frac{i}{d}\pi\right),\qquad 1\leq i\leq d.

The initial control is chosen as

u_{t}^{0}=2\sin(2\pi t)\mathbf{1}+0.5\cos(4\pi t)\,v,

where
v
is a fixed vector with entries uniformly spaced in
[-1,1]
.

The convergence behavior is illustrated in Figure
3
, where we compare the objective error across different state dimensions
d\in\{5,10,20\}
. For each experiment, the algorithm is run up to
N=1000
iterations, while only the initial portion
n=0,\dots,200
is displayed in the figure to highlight the pre-asymptotic regime.

We observe that all dimensions exhibit an approximately linear decay on the semilog scale, consistent with the geometric behavior predicted by the theory in convex settings when
\tau>0
. Moreover, while the objective decreases at a similar rate, higher-dimensional problems require more iterations to achieve the same level of accuracy.

Appendix A
Proof of the First Variation Formula

Proof of Lemma
2
.

Fix
u,v\in\mathcal{U}
and set

\delta u:=v-u.

For
\varepsilon\in[0,1]
, define the perturbed control

u^{\varepsilon}:=u+\varepsilon\delta u.

Since
U
is convex,
u^{\varepsilon}_{t}\in U
for every
t
whenever
\varepsilon\in[0,1]
,
hence
u^{\varepsilon}\in\mathcal{U}
. We compute the one-sided directional derivative at
\varepsilon=0
along this feasible segment.

Let
x
and
x^{\varepsilon}
denote the states associated with
u
and
u^{\varepsilon}
respectively:

\displaystyle\dot{x}_{t}=b_{t}(x_{t},u_{t}),\qquad x(0)=x_{0};

\displaystyle\dot{x}_{t}^{\varepsilon}=b_{t}(x_{t}^{\varepsilon},u_{t}^{\varepsilon}),\qquad x^{\varepsilon}(0)=x_{0}.

We first identify the first-order variation of the state.
Define
y
as the solution of the linearized equation

(34)

\dot{y}_{t}=\nabla_{x}b_{t}(x_{t},u_{t})\,y_{t}+\nabla_{u}b_{t}(x_{t},u_{t})\,\delta u_{t},\qquad y_{0}=0.

We claim that

(35)

\frac{x^{\varepsilon}-x}{\varepsilon}\to y\qquad\text{in }C([0,T];\mathbb{R}^{d})\quad\text{as }\varepsilon\to 0^{+}.

To prove this, define the remainder

r_{t}^{\varepsilon}:=x_{t}^{\varepsilon}-x_{t}-\varepsilon y_{t}.

Then
r_{0}^{\varepsilon}=0
, and

\dot{r}_{t}^{\varepsilon}=b_{t}(x_{t}^{\varepsilon},u_{t}^{\varepsilon})-b_{t}(x_{t},u_{t})-\varepsilon\Big(\nabla_{x}b_{t}(x_{t},u_{t})y_{t}+\nabla_{u}b_{t}(x_{t},u_{t})\delta u_{t}\Big).

Now write

x_{t}^{\varepsilon}=x_{t}+\varepsilon y_{t}+r_{t}^{\varepsilon},\qquad u_{t}^{\varepsilon}=u_{t}+\varepsilon\delta u_{t}.

Using the first-order Taylor expansion of
b_{t}(\cdot,\cdot)
in the variables
(x,u)

around
(x_{t},u_{t})
, we obtain

b_{t}(x_{t}^{\varepsilon},u_{t}^{\varepsilon})-b_{t}(x_{t},u_{t})=\nabla_{x}b_{t}(x_{t},u_{t})\bigl(\varepsilon y_{t}+r_{t}^{\varepsilon}\bigr)+\nabla_{u}b_{t}(x_{t},u_{t})\,\varepsilon\delta u_{t}+\rho_{t}^{\varepsilon},

where the remainder
\rho_{t}^{\varepsilon}
satisfies

|\rho_{t}^{\varepsilon}|\leq C\Big(|\varepsilon y_{t}+r_{t}^{\varepsilon}|+|\varepsilon\delta u_{t}|\Big)^{2}

for a constant
C>0
independent of
\varepsilon
and
t
.
Hence

\dot{r}_{t}^{\varepsilon}=\nabla_{x}b_{t}(x_{t},u_{t})r_{t}^{\varepsilon}+\rho_{t}^{\varepsilon}.

Therefore

(36)

|r_{t}^{\varepsilon}|\leq\int_{0}^{t}|\nabla_{x}b_{s}(x_{s},u_{s})|\,|r_{s}^{\varepsilon}|\,\mathrm{d}s+\int_{0}^{t}|\rho_{s}^{\varepsilon}|\,\mathrm{d}s.

By Assumption
A
,
\sup_{s\in[0,T]}|\nabla_{x}b(x_{s},u_{s})|\leq M
.
Moreover,

(37)

|\rho_{t}^{\varepsilon}|\leq C\Bigl(\varepsilon^{2}|y_{t}|^{2}+|r_{t}^{\varepsilon}|^{2}+\varepsilon^{2}|\delta u_{t}|^{2}\Bigr).

We estimate the square term
|r_{t}^{\varepsilon}|^{2}
as follows. By the definition of
r_{t}^{\varepsilon}
,

\sup_{t\in[0,T]}|r_{t}^{\varepsilon}|\leq\sup_{t\in[0,T]}|x_{t}^{\varepsilon}-x_{t}|+\varepsilon\,\sup_{t\in[0,T]}|y_{t}|.

By Assumption
A
(2) and
x_{0}^{\varepsilon}=x_{0}
,

|x_{t}^{\varepsilon}-x_{t}|\leq\int_{0}^{t}|b_{s}(x_{s}^{\varepsilon},u_{s}^{\varepsilon})-b_{s}(x_{s},u_{s})|\,\mathrm{d}s\leq\int_{0}^{t}M\left(|x_{s}^{\varepsilon}-x_{s}|+|u_{s}^{\varepsilon}-u_{s}|\right)\,\mathrm{d}s.

By Grönwall’s inequality,

\sup_{t\in[0,T]}|x_{t}^{\varepsilon}-x_{t}|\leq Me^{MT}\|u^{\varepsilon}-u\|_{L^{1}}=Me^{MT}\varepsilon\,\|\delta u\|_{L^{1}},

By the linearized equation (
34
) and
y_{0}=0
,

|y_{t}|\leq\int_{0}^{t}|\nabla_{x}b_{s}(x_{s},u_{s})|\,|y_{s}|\,\mathrm{d}s+\int_{0}^{t}|\nabla_{u}b_{s}(x_{s},u_{s})|\,|\delta u_{s}|\,\mathrm{d}s.

Assumption
A
yields

|y_{t}|\leq M\left(\int_{0}^{t}|y_{s}|\,\mathrm{d}s+\int_{0}^{t}|\delta u_{s}|\,\mathrm{d}s\right).

By Grönwall’s inequality,

\sup_{t\in[0,T]}|y_{t}|\leq Me^{MT}\|\delta u\|_{L^{1}}.

Therefore,

\sup_{t\in[0,T]}|r_{t}^{\varepsilon}|\leq C^{\prime}\varepsilon,\qquad C^{\prime}:=2Me^{MT}\|\delta u\|_{L^{1}}.

Substituting back to (
37
) and
using that
y\in C([0,T];\mathbb{R}^{d})
and
\delta u\in L^{2}([0,T];\mathbb{R}^{m})
, we obtain

\int_{0}^{T}|\rho_{t}^{\varepsilon}|\,\mathrm{d}t=o(\varepsilon).

By applying Grönwall to (
36
),

\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\left|\frac{r_{t}^{\varepsilon}}{\varepsilon}\right|\to 0,

which proves (
35
).

We next compute the derivative of the cost.
By definition,

J^{0}(u^{\varepsilon})-J^{0}(u)=\int_{0}^{T}\Big(f_{t}(x_{t}^{\varepsilon},u_{t}^{\varepsilon})-f_{t}(x_{t},u_{t})\Big)\,\mathrm{d}t+g(x_{T}^{\varepsilon})-g(x_{T}).

We treat the running cost and terminal cost separately.

For the running cost, by the first-order Taylor expansion of
f_{t}(\cdot,\cdot)

around
(x_{t},u_{t})
,

f_{t}(x_{t}^{\varepsilon},u_{t}^{\varepsilon})-f_{t}(x_{t},u_{t})=\nabla_{x}f_{t}(x_{t},u_{t})\cdot(x_{t}^{\varepsilon}-x_{t})+\nabla_{u}f_{t}(x_{t},u_{t})\cdot(u_{t}^{\varepsilon}-u_{t})+\tilde{\rho}_{t}^{\varepsilon},

where

|\tilde{\rho}_{t}^{\varepsilon}|\leq C\Big(|x_{t}^{\varepsilon}-x_{t}|+|u_{t}^{\varepsilon}-u_{t}|\Big)^{2}.

Since

x_{t}^{\varepsilon}-x_{t}=\varepsilon y_{t}+r_{t}^{\varepsilon},\qquad u_{t}^{\varepsilon}-u_{t}=\varepsilon\delta u_{t},

and

\sup_{t\in[0,T]}|r_{t}^{\varepsilon}|=o(\varepsilon),

it follows that

\int_{0}^{T}|\tilde{\rho}_{t}^{\varepsilon}|\,\mathrm{d}t=o(\varepsilon).

Hence

\int_{0}^{T}\Big(f_{t}(x_{t}^{\varepsilon},u_{t}^{\varepsilon})-f_{t}(x_{t},u_{t})\Big)\,\mathrm{d}t=\varepsilon\int_{0}^{T}\Big(\nabla_{x}f_{t}(x_{t},u_{t})\cdot y_{t}+\nabla_{u}f_{t}(x_{t},u_{t})\cdot\delta u_{t}\Big)\,\mathrm{d}t+o(\varepsilon).

For the terminal cost, since
g
is continuously differentiable,

g(x_{T}^{\varepsilon})-g(x_{T})=\nabla g(x_{T})\cdot(x_{T}^{\varepsilon}-x_{T})+o(|x_{T}^{\varepsilon}-x_{T}|).

Using

x_{T}^{\varepsilon}-x_{T}=\varepsilon y_{T}+r_{T}^{\varepsilon}\qquad\text{and}\qquad r_{T}^{\varepsilon}=o(\varepsilon),

we obtain

g(x_{T}^{\varepsilon})-g(x_{T})=\varepsilon\,\nabla g(x_{T})\cdot y_{T}+o(\varepsilon).

Combining the two expansions, we conclude that

J^{0}(u^{\varepsilon})-J^{0}(u)=\varepsilon\int_{0}^{T}\Big(\nabla_{x}f_{t}(x_{t},u_{t})\cdot y_{t}+\nabla_{u}f_{t}(x_{t},u_{t})\cdot\delta u_{t}\Big)\,\mathrm{d}t+\varepsilon\,\nabla g(x_{T})\cdot y_{T}+o(\varepsilon).

Therefore,

(38)

dJ^{0}(u)(\delta u)=\int_{0}^{T}\Big(\nabla_{x}f_{t}(x_{t},u_{t})\cdot y_{t}+\nabla_{u}f_{t}(x_{t},u_{t})\cdot\delta u_{t}\Big)\,\mathrm{d}t+\nabla g(x_{T})\cdot y_{T}.

It remains to eliminate the state variation
y
using the adjoint equation.
Recall that the adjoint
p
satisfies

\dot{p}_{t}=-\nabla_{x}\mathcal{H}_{t}^{0}(x_{t},p_{t},u_{t}),\qquad p_{T}=-\nabla g(x_{T}).

Since

\mathcal{H}_{t}^{0}(x,p,u)=p\cdot b_{t}(x,u)-f_{t}(x,u),

we have

\dot{p}_{t}=-\nabla_{x}b_{t}(x_{t},u_{t})^{\top}p_{t}+\nabla_{x}f_{t}(x_{t},u_{t}).

We now compute

\frac{d}{dt}(p_{t}\cdot y_{t})=\dot{p}_{t}\cdot y_{t}+p_{t}\cdot\dot{y}_{t}.

Using the equations for
p
and
y
, we get

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}(p_{t}\cdot y_{t})

\displaystyle=\Big(-\nabla_{x}b_{t}(x_{t},u_{t})^{\top}p_{t}+\nabla_{x}f_{t}(x_{t},u_{t})\Big)\cdot y_{t}

\displaystyle\quad+p_{t}\cdot\Big(\nabla_{x}b_{t}(x_{t},u_{t})y_{t}+\nabla_{u}b_{t}(x_{t},u_{t})\delta u_{t}\Big).

The two terms involving
\nabla_{x}b_{t}
cancel, and thus

\frac{d}{dt}(p_{t}\cdot y_{t})=\nabla_{x}f_{t}(x_{t},u_{t})\cdot y_{t}+p_{t}\cdot\nabla_{u}b_{t}(x_{t},u_{t})\delta u_{t}.

Integrating over
[0,T]
and using
y_{0}=0
and
p_{T}=-\nabla g(x_{T})
, we obtain

-\nabla g(x_{T})\cdot y_{T}=\int_{0}^{T}\nabla_{x}f_{t}(x_{t},u_{t})\cdot y_{t}\,\mathrm{d}t+\int_{0}^{T}p_{t}\cdot\nabla_{u}b_{t}(x_{t},u_{t})\delta u_{t}\,\mathrm{d}t.

Substituting this identity into (
38
), the
terms involving
\nabla_{x}f_{t}(x_{t},u_{t})\cdot y_{t}
cancel and we obtain

dJ^{0}(u)(\delta u)=\int_{0}^{T}\Big(-p_{t}\cdot\nabla_{u}b_{t}(x_{t},u_{t})\delta u_{t}+\nabla_{u}f_{t}(x_{t},u_{t})\cdot\delta u_{t}\Big)\,\mathrm{d}t.

Equivalently,

dJ^{0}(u)(\delta u)=\int_{0}^{T}\Big(-\nabla_{u}b_{t}(x_{t},u_{t})^{\top}p_{t}+\nabla_{u}f_{t}(x_{t},u_{t})\Big)\cdot\delta u_{t}\,\mathrm{d}t.

Since

\nabla_{u}\mathcal{H}_{t}^{0}(x_{t},p_{t},u_{t})=\nabla_{u}b_{t}(x_{t},u_{t})^{\top}p_{t}-\nabla_{u}f_{t}(x_{t},u_{t}),

we conclude that

dJ^{0}(u)(\delta u)=-\int_{0}^{T}\nabla_{u}\mathcal{H}_{t}^{0}(x_{t},p_{t},u_{t})\cdot\delta u_{t}\,\mathrm{d}t.

Recalling that
\delta u=v-u
, this proves (
8
).

Finally, since

J^{\tau}(u)=J^{0}(u)+\tau\int_{0}^{T}h(u_{t})\,\mathrm{d}t,

and
h
is continuously differentiable, we have

dJ^{\tau}(u)(\delta u)=dJ^{0}(u)(\delta u)+\tau\int_{0}^{T}\nabla h(u_{t})\cdot\delta u_{t}\,\mathrm{d}t.

Therefore

dJ^{\tau}(u)(\delta u)=-\int_{0}^{T}\nabla_{u}\mathcal{H}_{t}^{\tau}(x_{t},p_{t},u_{t})\cdot\delta u_{t}\,\mathrm{d}t,

which proves (
9
).
