Prediction of distribution areas of the rare Iris kuschakewiczii B.Fedtsch. species across its range using the MaxEnt model
Authors: Aruzhan Alikhanova, Saule Abugalieva, Shyryn Almerekova, Yerlan Turuspekov
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIris kuschakewiczii B.Fedtsch. is a rare ornamental plant endemic to the Northern Tien Shan mountains. The analysis of the species’ distribution range is important given the declining habitat area. This study employed MaxEnt to project the distribution of I. kuschakewiczii under current and three future climate change scenarios (SSP126, SSP245, SSP585) for two time periods (the 2050s and the 2090s). Prediction models showed high validity (AUC = 0.997), strong accuracy, and reliability. Moreover, key environmental variables affecting the distribution of I. kuschakewiczii were identified. It included Weight and Percentage of Sand (70%), Temperature Seasonality (~ 2300), and Elevation (700–1300 m above the sea level). Highly suitable distribution areas were mainly found in the Chu-Ili Mountains (Southeastern Kazakhstan). The analysis showed that only 26.7% of the total highly suitable area is protected by reserves and nature parks, underlying the need for conservation. The recently founded Kurty Valley Important Plant Area in Kazakhstan provides a strong foundation for the scientific conservation of I. kuschakewiczii and other rare and endangered plants. This research provides insights into the potential dynamics of habitat distribution for the endangered I. kuschakewiczii and offers a basis for strengthening cooperative international conservation and sustainable management strategies in the face of climate change.Keywords: Iris kuschakewiczii, Maximum entropy, Species distribution models, Conservation strategies, Climate changeSubject terms: Climate sciences, Ecology, Ecology, Environmental sciences

Introduction

Climate change is reshaping the distribution of plant species worldwide at an unprecedented rate1–6. Rising temperatures, shifting precipitation patterns, and increasing climatic variability are forcing species to track suitable conditions across latitudes and elevations, often faster than natural dispersal allows. For rare and narrowly distributed species, these pressures are particularly acute, as shrinking habitat windows and fragmented landscapes leave little room for adaptation3,4. In biodiversity hotspots such as Central Asia7–10, where warming is progressing faster than the global average and endemic species are concentrated in ecologically sensitive mountain systems11–13, understanding how climate change will affect species distributions is an urgent conservation priority.

Iris kuschakewiczii B.Fedtsch. is a perennial herbaceous plant belonging to the subgenus Scorpiris within the genus Iris Tourn. ex L. It commonly grows on gravelly, fine-earth, and clayey slopes of foothills, plateau-like peaks, and hills, and on foothill plains of the Northern Tien Shan (Kazakhstan, Kyrgyzstan). The plant is a 5–15 cm tall, early-flowering bulbous iris (juno) that holds ornamental and melliferous value14,15. The lectotype of I. kuschakewiczii, preserved in the Herbarium of the Komarov Botanical Institute (LE 00050068)16, was collected in Zaitzewka (now Shelek Village), Almaty region, by Kushakevich in 1877 and described in 1905 by Fedtchenko17. In Kyrgyzstan, the earliest documented record is reported near Frunze (now Bishkek) by Nikitina in 192918. The species grows in open communities dominated by grasses and low shrubs, often on south-facing slopes that warm quickly in spring. As a bulbous geophyte, it spends most of the year underground, making it particularly sensitive to soil conditions, especially drainage, during dormancy. These ecological characteristics make I. kuschakewiczii highly dependent on a narrow set of habitat conditions, which partly explains its restricted distribution and vulnerability to both land use change and climate-driven habitat shifts. Kokoreva et al.19 comprehensively studied the morphology of species in different ontogenetic states and described the ecological characteristics of I. kuschakewiczii. More recently, the distribution range was specified in accordance with the floristic regions of Kazakhstan20, and its chloroplast genome was characterized21. To date, these are the only studies published on this species, notwithstanding the inclusion of I. kuschakewiczii in the Red Data Book of Kazakhstan under Category I14. The given category indicates that the species is endangered, with a declining geographic range, and that its continued existence is impossible without the implementation of specific protection measures22.

As a narrow endemic confined to the Northern Tien Shan14, one of the most botanically significant yet climatically vulnerable mountain systems in Central Asia, it exemplifies the category of species for which SDM is most urgently needed but least applied. Moreover, the increasing impacts of negative anthropogenic factors, such as the popularization of ecotourism in vulnerable species’ communities, land-use policies, and aridification, place the naturally small populations of I. kuschakewiczii at great risk of extinction and of interest to conservation. Although its inclusion in the Red Data Book provides legal protection, the species remains vulnerable to extinction without effective, targeted conservation strategies.

To address this issue, species distribution models (SDMs) could be employed to predict species’ geographic distributions based on occurrence records and environmental data23,24. One widely used modeling tool is the Maximum Entropy (MaxEnt) model3,13,25,26. MaxEnt works best for a small number of occurrence sites27,28, which is ideal for projecting the distribution areas of rare and endangered species. Therefore, the implementation of SDM provides a robust framework for assessing the potential distribution of I. kuschakewiczii, identifying high-priority areas for protection, and contributing to the development of evidence-based conservation strategies for this endangered species.

In this study, we used the MaxEnt program to model the current and future distribution of I. kuschakewiczii. Future projections were based on three climate change scenarios across two time periods and incorporated bioclimatic, edaphic, and solar radiation variables. We assessed habitat suitability, identified high-priority areas for conservation, and proposed applicable protection measures. The findings of this study constitute a valuable scientific resource, guiding the development of rational policies aimed at the long-term conservation and controlled cultivation of I. kuschakewiczii.

Materials and methods

Species occurrence data

The geographical distribution data for I. kuschakewiczii were mainly obtained from field surveys, the Global Biodiversity Information Facility (GBIF)29, and the Herbarium Fund of the Institute of Botany and Phytointroduction (AA, Almaty, Kazakhstan, accessed December, 2024). The photo of I. kuschakewiczii in nature and its habitat were provided in Fig. 1. To minimize spatial clustering, the R package ‘spThin’30 was used to remove samples within 1 km2. The thinning distance was set below the grain of the environmental predictor layers, using a predictor-resolution thinning distance to reduce spatial autocorrelation while retaining the maximum number of records31. As a result, from the total of 190 occurrence points, 51 unique sites were compiled into a CSV file for further modeling analysis (Supplemetary File).

Photo of the I. kuschakewiczii species and its habitat.

Environmental variables

This study used 35 environmental variables, including 19 bioclimatic (WorldClim)32, 9 edaphic (Harmonized World Soil Database, HWSD v2.0)33, 6 global UV-B radiation (gIUV)34, and the human footprint index35. Bioclimatic data sourced from WorldClim at 2.5 arc-minute resolution was used for projecting conditions for 1970–2000 (current) and future time periods of 2041–2060 (2050s), and 2081–2100 (2090s) under three Shared Socioeconomic Pathways (SSP) by sixth IPCC assessment report36 SSP126 (the sustainable pathway), SSP245 (the medium pathway), and SSP585 (fossil-fueled development). The MIROC6 climate model from the Coupled Model Intercomparison Project Phase 6 (CMIP6) was selected for future scenarios because of its reliable simulations of Central Asia37,38.

Environmental variables were selected through a sequential three-step procedure. First, all 35 candidate variables were assessed for ecological relevance to I. kuschakewiczii by overlaying species occurrence records with each variable layer and retaining only those with a plausible mechanistic link to the species’ habitat requirements, including soil texture, thermal stability, elevation, seasonal precipitation, and UV-B radiation39. Second, pairwise Pearson correlation analysis (|r| ≤ 0.7) was performed on the ecologically screened subset using the R package ‘stats’40 to identify and remove redundant variables. Third, variable contribution and permutation importance were evaluated across multiple preliminary models representing different SSPs and time periods to ensure the stability of the final predictor set across scenarios. Seven non-collinear environmentally relevant variables were retained for all subsequent modeling (Table 1, Supplementary File).

All environmental layers were converted from GeoTIFF to ASCII format to ensure full compatibility for MaxEnt, harmonized by normalizing to a common spatial extent and resolution using the R software package ‘terra’41.

Species distribution modeling of I. kuschakewiczii on MaxEnt

MaxEnt v.3.4.4 was used to model 51 geographic records of I. kuschakewiczii and 7 environmental variables under three SSPs and two time periods. The parameters included 75% of the distribution points as training data, the remaining 25% as testing data, and cross-validation was repeated 10 times to enhance model precision. The maximum number of iterations was set to 1500 to increase the gain. Other parameters were kept at the default. The model was configured to produce logistic output, yielding suitability values between 0 and 1 that correspond to the probability of species occurrence.

Model performance was evaluated based on the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC). The value of AUC ranges from 0 to 1; 0.8 to 0.9 indicates good performance; 0.9 to 1.0 represents excellent performance of the model with all threshold values1,3. Thus, the AUC is directly proportional to the accuracy of the model’s predictions. Jackknife test, Percent contribution, Permutation importance by MaxEnt were employed to estimate the value of environmental layers.

For subsequent analysis of habitat suitability, the output logarithmic (0–1) distribution file from MaxEnt was classified into four levels of suitability on QGIS v.3.40.8: unsuitable (0-0.2), low suitability (0.2–0.5), moderate suitability (0.5–0.7), high suitability (0.7-1.0)1,42. Further, the area of each suitability category was calculated using PyQGIS.

Centroid migration analysis

Centroids of potential habitats were calculated using the package ‘terra’41 in R. Centroids represent the area-weighted geographic centers of suitable habitat areas (suitability ≥ 0.5). Consequently, habitat suitability classes representing moderate and high suitability were converted to binary raster layers, then converted to polygons, and centroid coordinates were extracted. The centroid shift between time periods was quantified as the distance between the centroids. This analysis allows us to assess the trajectory of habitat suitability changes and interpret potential migration trends of the species under future climate conditions.

Conservation status of I. kuschakewiczii

To evaluate the conservation status of I. kuschakewiczii, whose distribution area is shrinking, Specially Protected Natural Territories (SPNTs) of Kazakhstan were downloaded from www.oopt.kz, and the protected areas of adjacent countries, projected in MaxEnt models, were derived from www.protectedplanet.net. Protected areas included National and Regional Reserves, State Nature Reserves (SNRs), State National Nature Parks (SNNPs), and Other Effective Area-based Conservation Measures (OECMs). Highly suitable distribution areas (suitability ≥ 0.7) of all periods were merged and overlaid with protected area territories. Suitable habitat areas were spatially overlaid with protected natural areas to assess the degree of overlap43,44.

Predicted future suitable distribution

As shown in Fig. 7, the potentially suitable area for I. kuschakewiczii was modeled under three SSPs representing future emission levels: SSP126, SSP245, and SSP585. Generally, future distribution range has shown an increase in the northeastern boundary of the currently suitable areas. The suitability map for the 2090s under the SSP126 scenario showed an increase in highly suitable habitat along the northwestern boundary, including Karatau. Under the SSP245 scenario, both moderate and highly suitable predicted distribution areas achieved the highest results, with remarkable increases in the northwestern direction towards Karatau and Talas Alatau, while along the southern borders of the Fergana Range in Kyrgyzstan (Table 3). On the contrary, under the SSP585 scenario, a shrinkage was observed in the projected moderate- and high-suitability areas, with a decrease in highly suitable areas in the northwestern territory (Karatau and Talas Alatau) (Fig. 7). The total suitable area reached a peak of 351,397.56 km2 in the SSP585 scenario. In general, the most suitable areas are in the Almaty and Zhambyl regions of Kazakhstan. The results show that the suitable distribution areas for I. kuschakewiczii remain stable, with only small fluctuations in the Karatau and Talas Alatau Mountain ridges, despite future climate change scenarios.

Discussion

Model performance and validity

The escalating global warming is profoundly influencing plant species distribution and biodiversity45,46. Climate change heightens the urgency of applying effective conservation strategies, particularly habitat modeling, to protect rare and endangered plant species47–49. Moreover, according to the Red Data Book of Kazakhstan14, I. kuschakewiczii is listed as an endangered species with a shrinking distribution. Until recently, however, limited data availability led to the misconception that the species was endemic to Kazakhstan. The expansion of digital biodiversity records has since revealed a broader distribution. The current predicted distribution area is consistent with other studies, which report that the species predominantly occurs in the Chu Ili Mountains19,50,51. Also, distribution extends to the west and northeast of the Chu Ili Mountains and to the gravelly slopes of the Karatau, Talas Alatau, and Kirghiz Alatau mountain ridges14,21,50. This study used MaxEnt to build current and future suitable habitat area models for I. kuschakewiczii across its range under three climate change projections. It also identified territories with high conservation priority (Fig. 7). The results obtained could be applied to biodiversity conservation action strategies.

This MaxEnt-based study of the distribution of I. kuschakewiczii reveals that the total suitable area generally remains stable despite future climate change and is concentrated mainly in the Almaty and Zhambyl regions (Fig. 7). The results also show high validity, with a firmly valid AUC (0.997), indicating robust predictive efficiency of predictive models46,52. All response curves for environmental variables largely followed the same trend, with a narrow standard deviation (Fig. 5). The consistent trend in predictor-response curves across the current climate and different SSPs suggests that these relationships capture fundamental niche characteristics that remain stable under varying levels of stress.

Current and future distribution patterns under SSP scenarios

SSPs define the extent of future climate change, incorporating factors such as technological development, economic growth, and human population. SSPs were reported in 2023 and are combined with the equivalent Representative Concentration Pathways (RCPs) from the previous IPCC Fifth Assessment Report (2014)53,54. SSP126 is a low-emission scenario that combines the SSP1 socioeconomic pathway with the RCP2.6 radiative forcing target, representing a sustainable future. SSP246: middle-of-the-road pathway with intermediate greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions, the combination of SSP2 and RCP4.6. SSP5 and RCP8.5, in collaboration, give fossil-fueled development, resulting in the highly emissive SSP585 (IPCC6). Nowadays, SSPs are extensively used in habitat suitability studies3,54.

Predicted current distribution of I. kuschakewiczii refines its western border range (Fig. 6), extending it beyond the geographic points represented by thinned occurrence records. The occurrence dataset is concentrated predominantly in the Chu-Ili Mountains of Southeast Kazakhstan. However, the model predicts suitable conditions extending further westward into the foothill zones of the Western Tien Shan, including areas around the Aksu Zhabagly Nature Reserve in South Kazakhstan. Rather than representing a model artefact, this westward prediction is corroborated by botanical literature documenting the species in these regions56–59, suggesting that the current occurrence dataset underrepresents the true western extent of the range due to limited field sampling in remote foothill areas. This highlights a broader challenge in SDM studies of rare Central Asian endemics, where occurrence data are inherently sparse and geographically biased toward more accessible localities. The model, therefore, complements and extends the documented range, identifying undersampled western areas as priorities for targeted field verification. Future surveys confirming presence in these localities would not only validate model predictions but also substantially improve the accuracy of subsequent distribution modeling and conservation assessments for this species.

Under future climate models, compared with the current, the extent of highly suitable areas extends southward, encompassing Northwest Kyrgyzstan. Based on the calculated suitability area, it can be assumed that among climate change scenarios, SSP245 provides the greatest moderate habitat suitability in the 2050s and the highest suitable habitat for the species by the 2090s. The possible reason for an inclination towards these time periods could give us the range of estimated warming degrees within which the I. kuschakewiczii distribution occurs. Under SSP245, by the 2050s, the global temperature increases by 2.0 °C, while preferable SSP245-2090s cumulative effect rises the global temperature by 2.7 °C36. According to our findings, the distribution of I. kuschakewiczii reaches peak values at temperature elevations of 2.0–2.7 °C. However, according to the latest revision by Hausfather60, warming by 2100 is estimated at 2.9–3.7 °C. This value exceeds the preferred temperature-increase range for the future distribution of I. kuschakewiczii. Therefore, actions to conserve plant communities with I. kuschakewiczii should be given the utmost priority.

Key environmental drivers and their ecological interpretation

The most influential environmental variable among the models is SAND, indicating that the distribution of I. kuschakewiczii is strongly influenced by the weight and percentage of sand in the soil (Table 2). The response curve of the variable SAND shows a strong positive relationship between sand content and habitat suitability (Fig. 5). Predicted suitability remains negligible at low sand percentages, increases sharply beyond 70–80% sand, and reaches optimal values at very high sand content. Soil can act as a determinant of plant species spatial distribution61. In our case, sandy soils might be preferable for habitat due to their ability to retain water62, and they are described as plant communities with I. kuschakewiczii. These results suggest that soil aridification does not represent a limiting factor for the growth of I. kuschakewiczii. There are currently no available datasets in soil databases for future timelines applicable to species modeling. Consequently, the SAND variable was held constant across all future scenarios, representing a simplification that may affect the accuracy of future habitat suitability projections.

The strong positive response of I. kuschakewiczii to sand content exceeding 70% is ecologically interpretable in the context of its life form as a tunicated bulbous geophyte. Sandy soils offer rapid drainage, a condition critical for bulb survival during summer dormancy63. This interpretation is corroborated by the BIO19 response curve, which shows the highest habitat suitability at low winter precipitation values, consistent with adaptation to dry dormancy conditions. These two independent model outputs thus converge on a single ecological explanation: I. kuschakewiczii is physiologically constrained to substrates that remain well-drained during its dormant period.

The second predictor, with high contribution across most models, is BIO4, which shows the range of temperature seasonality. The response curve is strongly negative, implying that I. kuschakewiczii avoids seasonal temperature shifts and adapts to stable thermal conditions (Fig. 5). Elevation contributed by 14.4% (Table 2), and the response curve of ELEV shows consistent results with centroid migration analysis (Fig. 5; Table 4). The findings indicate that habitats located between 800 and 1300 m above sea level are most suitable for the growth of I. kuschakewiczii. Precipitation-related variables, BIO19 and BIO14, which specify the moisture preference of modeled species, contributed 13.1% and 5.6%, respectively (Table 2). Our findings indicate that precipitation is not a limiting factor for I. kuschakewiczii. The least influential predictors in the prediction models are UVB2 and UVB3. The species requires moderate seasonal variation in UV-B, with suitability peaking at intermediate levels (1.5 × 105 J/m2/day) rather than extreme values. It also shows high habitat suitability only in areas with low UV-B exposure during the month with the highest UV-B exposure, indicating sensitivity to high ultraviolet radiation. To sum up, the principal drivers of the distribution of I. kuschakewiczii are SAND, BIO4, and ELEV.

Centroid migration and its conservation implications

The centroid shifts to lower elevations (834–912 m) under SSP245 and SSP585 represent areas where future climate conditions may become physiologically tolerable for I. kuschakewiczii, but several factors may prevent actual distribution. First, lower elevations in Southeastern Kazakhstan and Northern Kyrgyzstan are subject to intensive agricultural cultivation, permanent settlement, and unregulated livestock grazing, the same anthropogenic pressures already documented as the primary drivers of population decline in the species14. Climatic suitability in these zones is therefore largely theoretical in the absence of habitat integrity. Second, I. kuschakewiczii is a bulbous geophyte with no documented long-distance dispersal mechanism. Natural downslope colonization of new habitat patches would likely occur over timescales far exceeding the 2050s and 2090s projection windows modeled here, even if suitable climate conditions materialized. Third, no field surveys have documented I. kuschakewiczii at elevations below approximately 700 m, and our elevation response curve shows a sharp decline in suitability below this threshold under current conditions. The projected centroid shifts to lower elevations, therefore reflecting a change in the center of mass of suitable habitat, not evidence of immediate range expansion into lowland areas.

We therefore interpret the lower-elevation centroid positions as a signal that conservation planning should account for potential future habitat at intermediate elevations (700–1000 m) in less anthropogenically disturbed terrain, rather than as a prediction that the species will colonize lowland agricultural zones. Field surveys across the elevation gradient in the Chu-Ili Mountains are needed to confirm these projections.

Conservation gap analysis and recommendations

The study shows that the area of overlap between the protected natural territories of Central Asia (Kazakhstan, Kyrgyzstan) and the highly suitable habitats of I. kuschakewiczii is 27,116.79 km² (26.7%). The other 73.3% of highly suitable habitat requires protection. Protected areas have been proven effective for protecting endangered species51,64. However, under climate change scenarios, their distributions could shift outside protected areas. The limiting factor in the natural population decline of I. kuschakewiczii is anthropogenic factors, such as unregulated livestock overgrazing and land cultivation14. During our field investigations, specimens of I. kuschakewiczii grazed by livestock were documented. The species is only protected in Aksu-Zhabagyly SNR14, though I. kuschakewiczii is absent in the latest list of vascular plants of the reserve (www.kazmab.kz/index.php/). Therefore, mitigation of distribution area shrinkage should include previously defined centroids, as these represent core priority areas for conservation. Enhancing international collaboration on biodiversity conservation and ecological security across Central Asian countries and regions, and supporting joint protection initiatives, is critically important today.

The priority area for conservation identified by centroid migration analysis is only partially protected by the Korday-Zhaysan SNR, which is of local significance. As mentioned above, most (73.3%) of highly suitable areas are outside protected areas (Fig. 8). This means that most of the distribution and high-priority areas of I. kuschakewiczii are not protected from environmental and anthropogenic influence. Consequently, conservation in a single SPNT is insufficient; establishing small protected areas and wildlife corridors within high-priority conservation areas is recommended. Most importantly, the population dynamics of I. kuschakewiczii should be monitored at monitoring sites on a long-term basis, using cultivation methods such as ex situ (botanical gardens, seed and DNA banks) and in situ (national parks, wildlife sanctuaries, and biosphere reserves), as well as integral quasi-in situ. The latter quasi in situ was proposed by Volis and Blecher as a conservation method within an ex situ-in situ cultivation strategy in highly suitable legally protected natural sites and showed promising results with Iris atrofusca Baker65,66. We suppose that the application of OECMs in vulnerable populations is most applicable to the case of rare and endangered I. kuschakewiczii.

While expanding formal protected areas remains a long-term goal, the extensive pastoralist communities across Southeastern Kazakhstan and Kyrgyzstan make this approach socially and politically complex. Within this regard, OECMs offer a pragmatic complementary pathway recognized under the Kunming-Montreal Global Biodiversity Framework (Target 3)67. As defined by the CBD (2018), OECMs are areas outside formal protected areas, which achieve sustained positive outcomes for in situ biodiversity conservation and associated ecosystem and cultural values through their governance and management67. Also, previously emerged terms, such as Important Plant Areas (IPAs) that fall within the definition of OECMs, are included in it, contributing to the development of conservation policies’ management67,68. In the past decade, 31 IPAs in the Northern Tien Shan botanical-geographical subprovince69,70 of Kazakhstan have been identified51,64. IPAs are key biodiversity conservation sites established by evaluating species distribution and botanical richness, with an emphasis on threatened plants and habitats (https://www.plantlife.org.uk/international). Recently, Kurty Valley IPA was described in 202471. The new IPA includes habitats of Tulipa regelii Krasn. and I. kuschakewiczii. The identification of IPAs is particularly relevant under the Kunming-Montreal Global Biodiversity Framework67, which encourages the development of national strategies. IPAs have been proposed as the primary tool for identifying priority areas for vegetation protection72–74. Therefore, establishing the legal status and common formation criteria for IPAs is required to protect rare and endangered plants.

To address anthropogenic influences on I. kuschakewiczii, livestock breeders and farmers should emphasize sustainable land use and minimize interactions with wildlife. In pastoralist communities in the range of I. kuschakewiczii, OECMs can align conservation with traditional land stewardship, reducing land-use conflicts while maintaining habitat connectivity for I. kuschakewiczii. Collaborative agreements with livestock breeders, already mentioned in our conservation recommendations, could serve as a foundation for OECM designation in high-priority centroid areas. However, a critical distinction must be drawn between climatic suitability and realized distribution.

These findings are crucial for identifying the conditions most suitable for conserving plant communities with I. kuschakewiczii. In the present study, a suitable distribution habitat was modeled for the current timeline and under future climate change using MaxEnt, incorporating bioclimatic, UV-B, and edaphic factors. Although the research demonstrated strong reliability of the results (AUC = 0.997) and included various types of variables, biotic factors such as I. kuschakewiczii species interactions with other plant species and animals, as well as human activities, should be studied. Plant species growth is affected by many additional factors not included in the study, indicating that there remains considerable potential to investigate future population projection of I. kuschakewiczii by incorporating other variables and alternative prediction models.

Limitations

The MaxEnt forecast included several biotic factors affecting the distribution of I. kuschakewiczii, including edaphic and radiation factors. Appropriate conservation pathways were proposed to conserve species in the high-priority areas identified by the center-of-mass shift analysis. Centroids could not distinguish northern and southern populations, as they represent the geographical center of the suitable habitat. Therefore, future studies should delineate northern and southern population groups as separate units and integrate genetic and demographic data to determine whether these groups constitute distinct conservation units with differentiated vulnerability to climate change. Such population-level analyses would substantially improve the resolution of conservation recommendations for this species.

The life history of I. kuschakewiczii, including dispersal mechanisms, pollinator specificity, and soil biotic interactions, remains largely unstudied at the species level. Our model identifies habitat associations but cannot explain the biological mechanisms underlying them. We therefore call for targeted autecological research on this species, particularly in the high-priority centroid areas identified in the Chu-Ili Mountains, where field access and monitoring infrastructure could realistically be established.

Conclusion

Using MaxEnt modeling, we projected the current and future distribution areas of I. kuschakewiczii under three climate change scenarios. The results showed perfect validity, with a mean AUC of 0.997. Among the variables used, key environmental factors influencing the distribution of I. kuschakewiczii include weight and sand percentage, temperature seasonality, and elevation. In the future, the distribution area of I. kuschakewiczii would remain consistent, with only minor losses, under climate change conditions. The estimated temperature increase range under which I. kuschakewiczii reaches high distribution is 2.0–2.7 °C. Centroid shift analysis identified a high-priority conservation area for I. kuschakewiczii in the western Almaty and Zhambyl regions of Kazakhstan. For I. kuschakewiczii, an endangered species, distribution areas and conservation measures were suggested, such as the establishment of protected areas, monitoring sites, and the strengthening of the legal status of Important Plant Areas. Given the ongoing trend of global warming, the findings of this study could serve as a valuable theoretical basis for devising efficient policies to conserve plant communities with I. kuschakewiczii.
