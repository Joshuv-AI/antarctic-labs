Influence of Sb-doping on structural, optical and UV photodetector properties of CdS thin films
Authors: Prashantha Murahari, Devarajan Alagarasan, S. S. Hegde, Ramakanta Naik, Mohamed Benghanem, Mohd. Shkir, Shiva Prasad Kollur
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The demand for high-performance photodetectors has driven extensive research into materials with superior optoelectronic properties. This study presents the fabrication and ultraviolet (UV) photodetection capabilities of antimony (Sb)-doped cadmium sulfide (CdS) thin films. The thin films were deposited via the nebulizer spray pyrolysis (NSP) method onto glass substrates. X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis confirms the presence of a hexagonal crystal structure in all films, with the 2 wt% Sb-doped CdS film exhibiting the highest crystallinity. Morphological examination using FESEM revealed that Sb-doping led to the formation of larger, densely packed grains for 2 wt% doped film, enhancing its charge transport properties. Optical studies showed enhanced light absorption with Sb incorporation, making the material more effective for UV detection. The photodetector performance was assessed through current vs. voltage (I-V) measurements. The photodetector performance was evaluated under 365 nm UV illumination with an intensity of 1 to 5 mW cm⁻² at an applied bias of ± 5 V. Among the prepared thin films, the 2 wt% Sb-doped CdS film demonstrated remarkable photoresponse, achieving a responsivity (R) of 1.84 A W−1, detectivity (D*) of 4.60 × 109 Jones and an external quantum efficiency (EQE) of 626%. The device exhibited rapid response and recovery times of 1.02 s and 0.56 s, respectively. Furthermore, the photodetector maintained stable performance over extended cycles, highlighting its reliability for long-term UV sensing applications. These results highlight the potential of Sb-doped CdS thin films as efficient and durable materials for advanced UV photodetectors.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-58741-2.

Introduction

Photodetectors (PDs) play a crucial role in modern optical and electronic systems, finding applications in areas such as optical communications, transparent electronics, night vision, security, flame detection, astronomical studies and medical diagnostics1–6. With the rapid advancement of technology, there is an increasing demand for intelligent, highly sensitive, energy-efficient and miniaturized optoelectronic devices. Photodetectors are central to these developments, as they enable the conversion of incident light into electrical signals for sensing and analysis. They are essential for next-generation optoelectronic applications because their integration into the Internet of Things (IoT)-based systems improves biological diagnostics, environmental sensing, real-time monitoring and intelligent automation7.

Photodetectors are designed to operate across a wide spectral range spanning the ultraviolet, visible and infrared regions, enabling the detection of specific wavelengths as well as broad spectral bands2,6,8,9. Among them, UV radiation plays a significant role in both natural and artificial environments, influencing various scientific, industrial and medical applications7,10–15. It spans the wavelength range from 400 to 100 nm, with photon energies ranging from 3.1 to 12 eV10,11. Based on the physiological effect, the UV spectrum can be categorized mainly into three regions: UV-A (400 to 320 nm), UV-B (320 to 280 nm), UV-C (280 to 200 nm). In addition, electromagnetic radiation spanning 200 to 120 nm is typically classified as far ultraviolet (FUV), whereas wavelengths in the range of 120 to 10 nm are referred to as extreme ultraviolet (EUV)10,12. While a significant portion of solar UV radiation is absorbed by the ozone layer, wavelengths longer than 200 nm are able to penetrate the atmosphere and reach the Earth’s surface10,11. In addition to the Sun as a natural source, a wide range of artificial UV sources has been developed to utilize the unique characteristics of UV radiation. These include phototherapy lamps for medical applications, plasma torches for industrial processing, UV light-emitting diodes (LEDs) for sterilization and sensing, welding arcs that emit intense UV radiation, and xenon–mercury arc lamps used in spectroscopy and disinfection systems7. The controlled use of such sources has significantly contributed to advancements in healthcare, manufacturing, environmental monitoring, and photonic technologies.

Controlled exposure to UV radiation is beneficial for applications such as sterilization, disinfection and vitamin D synthesis. However, excessive exposure can lead to serious health issues, including skin aging, cataracts and cancer10. UV radiation also affects agriculture and infrastructure, influencing crop productivity and the durability of buildings. Although the ozone layer absorbs much of the harmful UV radiation, its depletion has increased UV levels reaching the Earth7. Since UV light is not visible to the human eye, efficient photodetectors are essential for its monitoring and utilization. An ideal UV photodetector should offer high sensitivity, good responsivity, spectral selectivity, fast response and long-term stability, especially for applications in harsh environments. In high-temperature environments, such as flame detection systems, thermally stable photodetectors are particularly valuable10. Various UV detection technologies have been developed, with semiconductor-based sensors being widely used due to their tunable bandgap properties2,11.

The performance of photodetectors is evaluated based on parameters such as responsivity, detectivity, response time and spectral detection range6,16,17. Responsivity measures how much electrical output a photodetector produces for a given amount of incoming light, indicating its sensitivity. Detectivity represents the detector’s capability to sense weak light signals despite background noise, influencing the signal-to-noise ratio. Response time describes how quickly the detector reacts to changes in light, which is essential for high-speed applications such as optical communication. Finally, the spectral range specifies the range of wavelengths the detector can effectively operate within, allowing customization for applications in visible, near-infrared or ultraviolet regions of the electromagnetic spectrum6,18–20.

Thin film technology has played a key role in advancing high-performance photodetectors, offering a practical alternative to conventional bulk devices. Thin films allow better control over thickness, reduce material usage and enable cost-effective fabrication, making them well-suited for large-scale production. Among the various materials explored, semiconductors have attracted significant attention due to their excellent optoelectronic properties, which support efficient light absorption, high sensitivity and improved response characteristics18,19,21. Wide bandgap metal oxides such as ZnO and TiO₂ are extensively studied for UV photodetectors because of their high transparency and strong UV response22–26. At the same time, metal chalcogenides have emerged as a promising class of materials, offering a good balance of electrical performance, availability and low cost. A key advantage of these materials is the tunability of properties such as carrier concentration, defect density and band structure, can be effectively modified through controlled doping6. Materials like Bi₂S₃, SnS, In₂S₃ and CdS have been widely investigated due to their suitable bandgaps, strong light–matter interaction, and compatibility with low-cost thin film deposition techniques, making them attractive for scalable photodetector applications6,27–31.

Cadmium sulfide (CdS), a II-VI semiconductor with a bandgap of around 2.4 eV, is commonly used in optoelectronics. Its strong absorption coefficient of light, ranging from 104 to 105 cm−1, along with moderate carrier mobility between 0.1 and 10 cm2 V−1s−1, makes it a valuable material for various applications21,32,33. Its cost-effectiveness and versatile synthesis routes, combined with its favorable optoelectronic properties, make it a suitable candidate for thin film based photodetector fabrication21. Its bandgap can be tuned over a broad range by controlling factors such as grain size, morphology and preparation parameters, enabling spectral selectivity in photodetection34. Cadmium sulfide (CdS) is an n-type semiconductor35,36, primarily due to the presence of intrinsic donor-type defects such as sulfur vacancies (VS) and cadmium interstitials (Cdi), which contribute free electrons to the conduction band37–40. When doped with antimony (Sb), CdS would remain predominantly n-type, since Sb tends to occupy Cd lattice sites or interstitial positions and acts as a donor impurity. Sb incorporation introduces shallow donor levels close to the conduction band, which enhance electron concentration. The carrier concentration and Hall mobility usually lie in the order of 1017 cm−3 and 101 cm2 V−1 s−1, respectively35,36,41.

Introducing suitable metal or non-metal dopants into CdS at optimized concentrations has been widely shown to enhance photodetector performance21,42–45. Dopants such as In, Na, B, Ga, Ag, Sn, Al, P and Sb can modify key material properties, including bandgap, electronic energy levels, and grain size, thereby improving charge transport, sensitivity, and overall device efficiency21,44. Doping also introduces defect states that influence carrier recombination dynamics, enabling further tuning of the photoresponse. When incorporated during film growth, dopants can effectively tailor the electronic structure and carrier concentration without significantly disturbing the crystal lattice21,44. In addition to doping, controlling the size and morphology of CdS nanostructures through cost-effective deposition techniques further impacts light absorption and carrier transport, providing an additional route to improve photodetector performance1.

While CdS-based UV photodetectors have been reported46–49, their development remains limited compared to devices targeting the visible spectrum. Owing to its direct bandgap of ~ 2.42 eV, with an absorption edge extending into the UV region and the potential for property enhancement via controlled doping, CdS offers significant promise for UV detection. In this context, doping CdS with Sb can greatly alter its structural, optical and electrical properties, leading to enhanced photodetector performance. Since the ionic radius of Sb³⁺ (0.78 Å) is relatively close to that of Cd²⁺ (0.95 Å), Sb can be effectively incorporated into the CdS lattice. This integration modifies the electronic structure and carrier transport, enhancing the material’s sensitivity to ultraviolet light.

The choice of deposition technique plays a critical role in defining film quality and device performance. Thin film fabrication methods such as PVD, CVD, ALD and MBE generally operate under vacuum or carefully controlled low-pressure conditions to ensure high film purity and accurate compositional control. Techniques based on physical and chemical vapor deposition, including thermal evaporation, e-beam evaporation, sputtering, CVD, ALD, and similar approaches, are commonly employed as vacuum- or reduced-pressure processes for the controlled growth of thin films50–53. Various methods have been employed to fabricate CdS thin films, including chemical bath deposition (CBD), thermal evaporation, pulsed laser deposition (PLD) and sputtering1,41,54. CBD, though low-cost and widely used, often yields films with poor adhesion and requires post-annealing to improve crystallinity. Thermal evaporation and sputtering produce dense films but involve high-vacuum systems, making them less suitable for large-scale, low-cost production. PLD enables precise control over stoichiometry and crystallinity but is limited by small deposition areas and high equipment cost. In contrast, nebulizer spray pyrolysis (NSP), employed in the present study, provides a simple and scalable approach6. It allows the deposition of uniform and stoichiometric thin films at relatively low substrate temperatures, with controllable grain size and morphology. Importantly, NSP offers the ability to incorporate dopants efficiently during growth, enabling bandgap engineering and defect modulation without the need for complex post-processing steps. In terms of fabrication, NSP used in the present work is particularly advantageous compared to conventional methods such as CBD, thermal evaporation or PLD1.

This study explores how doping CdS thin films with varying concentrations of antimony (Sb) (0, 1, 2 and 3 wt%) influences their UV photodetection capabilities. By systematically analyzing the impact of Sb incorporation, the research aims to improve the films’ optoelectronic properties, making them more effective for UV sensing. The insights gained from this work could help optimize Sb-doped CdS thin films for next-generation UV photodetectors, enhancing their performance in advanced sensing applications.

Experimental methods

The undoped and Sb-doped thin films were deposited on glass substrates at 350 °C using nebulizer spray pyrolysis (NSP). This technique helps create uniform, high-quality thin films, which are crucial for improving photodetector performance6. For the synthesis, 99.99% pure cadmium chloride (CdCl2), thiourea (NH₂-CSNH₂) and antimony nitrate Sb(NO3)3 were used. With Sb-doping levels set at 0, 1, 2 and 3 wt%, 0.03 M thiourea and 0.02 M CdCl₂ were dissolved in 10 mL of deionized water to prepare the spray solution. The solution was stirred magnetically for an hour to ensure thorough mixing. During the continuous nebulizer spray process, air at 1.5 kg cm−2 pressure generated fine aerosol droplets, enabling the formation of uniform and consistent thin films. For optimum film crystallization, a substrate temperature of 350 °C was maintained6, along with a rate of spray of 1 mL min−1, a deposition period of 10 min and a nozzle-substrate distance of 5 cm. A substrate temperature of 350 °C was found to be optimum for thin film deposition55. At 300 °C, poor crystallinity suppressed photoresponse, while at 400 and 450 °C, partial precursor evaporation reduced film thickness. Thus, 350 °C provided the best balance between crystallinity and thickness for enhanced photoresponse.

Characterization techniques

The structural properties of undoped and Sb-doped CdS thin films were studied using X-ray diffraction (XRD) with a PANalytical X’Pert Pro diffractometer, utilizing Cu Kα radiation (λ = 1.5405 Å) over a 2θ range of 10° to 80°. The surface structure and elemental composition of the films were examined using Field Emission Scanning Electron Microscopy (FESEM, Apreo S) and Energy Dispersive Spectroscopy. ImageJ software was used to analyze particle size distribution. To study optical properties, a UV-VIS-NIR spectrophotometer (Perkin Elmer-Lambda 35) was used for measurements in the 350 to 900 nm range. Photoluminescence (PL) spectra were recorded with a PerkinElmer LS-55 fluorescence spectrometer. The thicknesses of the prepared films were measured using a stylus profilometer and confirmed by cross-sectional measurement by SEM. The photodetector’s performance was assessed by measuring the current-voltage (I-V) response under 365 nm laser illumination, using a Keithley 2450 source meter. The laser system was equipped with a tunable power control, allowing adjustment of the incident light intensity. For the photoresponse measurements, the optical power density was systematically varied in the range of 1 to 5 mW cm− 2.

No additional diffraction peaks corresponding to impurity phases are noticed in the patterns. It is also observed that the microstrain reduces with Sb-doping, indicating reduced defects and long-range atomic order in the sample. The estimated lattice parameters (a and c) and unit cell volume (V) are slightly lower than the JCPDS value of bulk CdS. The lattice parameter a gradually increases while c values decrease steadily up to 2 wt% Sb concentration. The unit cell volume of CdS exhibits a rising trend as the Sb-doping concentration increases from 1 to 3 wt%. The progressive variation in the lattice parameter indicates the effective incorporation of Sb3+ in CdS lattice. As Sb³⁺ has a smaller ionic radius (0.78 Å) compared to Cd²⁺ (0.95 Å), the substitution of Cd²⁺ with Sb³⁺ is feasible57. The observed variation in lattice parameters is consistent with previous reports on Sb-doped CdS58. In Sb-doped CdS, the a-axis expands while the c-axis contracts, leading to an overall increase in unit cell volume. This increase in unit cell volume upon Sb incorporation can be attributed to the lattice strain introduced by the dopant. Owing to the significant difference in ionic radii, the substitution of Sb³⁺ ions for Cd²⁺ generates local distortions in the CdS lattice. To accommodate this size mismatch, the surrounding atoms adjust, which results in a net expansion of the unit cell.

The enhanced average crystallite size of CdS thin films observed for 1–2 wt% Sb-doping can be primarily attributed to the substitution of Cd²⁺ ions at regular lattice sites by Sb³⁺ ions. This substitution, during the early stages of film growth, can promote the formation of additional nucleation centres, thereby facilitating a more orderly and well-aligned growth of crystallites6. Such controlled nucleation helps in minimizing structural defects, leading to better crystallite size. However, at higher doping concentrations, the presence of smaller Sb³⁺ ions in interstitial positions induces lattice strain, disrupting the periodic arrangement of atoms and thereby reducing crystallite size and resulting in diffraction features similar to the undoped film. In addition, excess dopant incorporation into the CdS lattice may result in Sb-rich regions at grain boundaries. The excess dopant incorporated into CdS lattice may also segregate at grain boundaries and hinder the grain growth by obstructing atomic diffusion, which is crucial for crystallization. Interstitial dopant atoms also induce local lattice strain and structural distortions. These factors could explain the observed reduction in crystallite size for 3 wt% Sb-doped film. Several studies have reported enhanced crystallite size and reduced microstrain properties of CdS thin films with moderate levels of elemental doping. For instance, a study on nickel-doped CdS thin films prepared by spray pyrolysis revealed that 2 wt% incorporation of nickel into CdS lattice can lead to increased crystallite size59. Increased crystallite size in 2 wt% Sb-doped CdS thin film enhances the charge transport, improves optical absorption and make photodetectors more efficient and reliable.

A comparison of the lattice parameters obtained by the present study and the literature is provided in Table 2. The calculated lattice parameters are found to be in close agreement with standard and previously reported values for CdS, with slight variations observed upon Sb incorporation. These deviations can be attributed to lattice distortion arising from dopant inclusion, confirming successful modification of the CdS crystal structure. Although the crystallite size is slightly smaller compared to some reported doped CdS systems, the present films demonstrate comparable or improved photodetector performance. In particular, the significantly enhanced responsivity and EQE as observed by photosensing studies indicate that Sb-doping effectively optimizes the balance between structural disorder and carrier dynamics, leading to superior photoresponse. These results highlight the potential of Sb-doped CdS as an efficient material for high-performance photodetection applications.

Surface morphology and particle size distribution

The FESEM micrographs of the undoped and 1–3 wt% Sb-doped CdS thin films are displayed in Fig. 2a. The FESEM images show a compact granular structure, with noticeable variations in particle size across different samples. The particle size distribution histograms of the prepared films fitted with a Gaussian distribution provide the average size of particles on the film surface. The surface morphology of the undoped CdS film exhibits densely packed small particles of an average size of 84 nm that are unevenly distributed on the surface. The histogram of the undoped CdS film shows a relatively narrow particle size distribution, indicating uniform growth of particles. The black areas observed in the images are identified as pores, possibly caused by solvent evaporation and incomplete grain packing during spray deposition.

Further, the average grain size increases to 136 nm and 222 nm for the films doped with 1 wt% and 2 wt% of Sb, respectively, and the pinholes are significantly reduced. The progressive increase in particle size as shown in Fig. 2a indicates a systematic growth process influenced by the substitutional incorporation of Sb ions into the CdS lattice. Doping can lower the density of nucleation sites and facilitate the growth of grains, leading to fewer but larger grains. Film doped with 3 wt% of Sb shows an average particle size of 112 nm, with a wide particle size distribution, indicating aggregation of particles on the surface. Excess dopant atoms can act as growth inhibitors by occupying grain boundaries or interstitial sites. This may explain the observed surface morphology of 3 wt% Sb-doped CdS films. The FESEM and particle size analysis confirm that Sb-doping is an effective synthesis method to control grain growth in CdS thin films. Larger particles, as observed in the 2 wt% Sb-doped sample, may exhibit better crystallite nature and reduced surface defects. Larger grains reduce the extent of grain boundaries, minimize grain boundary scattering and enhance charge carrier mobility, making them suitable for optoelectronic applications21,43. The film thickness was measured using a profilometer, and all samples exhibited a thickness of approximately 600 nm. This was further confirmed by cross-sectional SEM analysis. Additionally, EDS measurements were performed on the CdS films to verify the incorporation of Sb. The EDS spectra for 2 wt% Sb-doped CdS thin film is shown in Fig. 2c. The cross-sectional thickness measurement and further details on EDS results are provided in Supplementary Information Figure S2 and Table S1.

UV-Visible optical absorption studies

Figure 3a presents the optical absorption spectra for pristine and doped CdS thin films measured across the 350 to 900 nm wavelength range. All the films demonstrate strong absorption in the UV-visible region, with a distinct absorption edge near 500 nm, a characteristic feature of CdS60. The observed shift in the absorption edge and changes in absorbance indicate that Sb-doping influences the optical properties of CdS thin films. As can be seen, the intensity of absorption of CdS film is enhanced after Sb-doping and the highest absorbance is recorded for 2 wt% Sb-doping concentration. The optical absorbance of the 3 wt% Sb-doped CdS thin film sample drops drastically. The absorption edge of the CdS film gradually shifts towards longer wavelengths as the Sb concentration increases, indicating bandgap narrowing. Shkir et al.45 reported a similar red shift in the absorption edge for 1–5 wt% Fe-doped CdS thin films. The enhanced optical absorption in CdS films doped with 1–2 wt% Sb is likely due to the formation of impurity states within the bandgap. The introduction of Sb creates defect-induced localized states, which facilitate additional electronic transitions, leading to increased light absorption. The presence of midgap states enables the absorption of longer-wavelength photons, broadening the absorption spectrum beyond the intrinsic bandgap of CdS. These factors contribute to the optical absorption of Sb-doped CdS thin films, making them most suitable for light-sensing applications. where α represents the absorption coefficient, A is a proportionality constant, and n is 1/2 for direct allowed transitions and 2 for indirect allowed transitions.

The direct optical bandgap of the films is determined using the (αhν)² versus photon energy (eV) plot, as shown in Fig. 3b. By extending the linear portion of the plot to the x-axis, the optical bandgap is obtained. The estimated direct bandgap of 2.38 eV for the undoped CdS thin film closely aligns with previously reported values for CdS films fabricated using the spray pyrolysis technique32. The estimated bandgap values of 1, 2 and 3 wt% Sb-doped CdS films decreased to 2.37, 2.35 and 2.30 eV, respectively. A similar bandgap narrowing effect is observed earlier in Fe, Sm, Gd and Al-doped CdS thin films14,24,27,33. As a result of Sb-doping, defect-related energy levels are introduced within the bandgap. These localized states facilitate the excitation of electrons by lower-energy photons, leading to a reduction in the optical bandgap61,62. The substitution of Sb³⁺ for Cd²⁺ introduces additional electronic states and causes lattice distortions due to the difference in ionic radii and valence states. These distortions generate defect states and increase the density of localized states near the band edge. As a result, the effective optical bandgap decreases with increasing Sb concentration. The reduction in the optical bandgap allows the material to absorb lower energy photons and may extend the sensitivity over a broader wavelength range. Higher optical absorption also results in enhanced quantum efficiency, larger photocurrent and better responsivity63. Narrowing of the bandgap and the enhanced optical absorbance observed in 2 wt% Sb-doped CdS are practically advantageous for photodetector applications.

The light absorption near the band edge in semiconductor thin films is not always abrupt. It is often influenced by localized defect states and structural disorder, which introduce an exponential absorption tail extending into the bandgap, commonly referred to as the Urbach tail. This tailing reflects the presence of disorder and can affect the accurate estimation of the optical bandgap from Tauc plots, as the part of the absorption may arise from sub-bandgap transitions rather than the true band edge15,64–67. The extent of this tailing is quantified by Urbach energy Eu, which serves as an indicator of the degree of disorder in the material. It is determined from the inverse of the slope of the linear region in a plot of ln(α) versus photon energy (hν), based on the relation ln(α) = ln(α0) + (hν/Eu), where α is the absorption coefficient, α₀ is a constant, and Eu represents the Urbach energy. The ln(α) versus photon energy plot to determine Eu is shown in Figure S3 (Supplementary Information), and the estimated values are presented in Table S2 (Supplementary Information). It is observed that Eu gradually increases from 0.85 eV for the undoped film to 1.16 eV for the 3 wt% Sb-doped film, indicating an increase in defect-induced local disorder and localized states within the bandgap65. This increase in Eu with Sb-doping is commonly associated with the reduction in optical bandgap due to the formation of intermediate energy states, showing an inverse relationship between Eu and bandgap66,68–71. While moderate disorder (2 wt% Sb) enhances light absorption and photoresponse, excessive disorder at higher doping (3 wt%) leads to increased recombination and degraded device performance.

Photoluminescence (PL) studies

Photoluminescence (PL) is widely used to analyze defect levels in the deposited films and also to study the recombination behaviour of the photogenerated electron-hole pairs. Figure 4 presents the PL spectra of undoped and 1–3 wt% Sb-doped CdS thin films, recorded using a 325 nm laser source. The PL spectra show characteristic emission peaks and variations in intensity across different wavelengths, indicating the impact of Sb-doping on the defect states of CdS. The PL spectrum of the glass substrate, shown in the inset, confirms that it is optically inactive in the studied region, indicating no contribution to the peaks observed in the main spectrum. The weak feature that appears is attributed to broad luminescence arising from defects, impurities or trapped states in the amorphous silica network.

The PL spectra of all the synthesized films exhibit distinct emission peaks around 525 nm and 680 nm. As the Sb-doping concentration increases, the PL intensity also rises, reaching its highest value at 2 wt% Sb-doping. However, at 3 wt% doping, the emission peak intensity decreases. Different types of defects are possible in CdS, such as cadmium vacancy (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\mathrm{V}}_{\mathrm{C}\mathrm{d}}^{2-}$$\end{document}), sulfur vacancy (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\mathrm{V}}_{\mathrm{S}}^{2+}$$\end{document}), cadmium interstitial (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\mathrm{C}\mathrm{d}}_{\mathrm{i}}^{2+}$$\end{document}), sulfur interstitial (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\mathrm{S}}_{\mathrm{i}}^{2-}$$\end{document}), plus antisites such as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\mathrm{C}\mathrm{d}}_{\mathrm{S}}^{2+}$$\end{document} (sulfur atom substituted by a cadmium atom) and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\mathrm{S}}_{\mathrm{C}\mathrm{d}}^{2-}$$\end{document} (cadmium atom substituted by a sulfur atom)72. The near band edge (NBE) emission peak at 525 nm is attributed to excitonic recombination via surface and defect states associated with sulfur vacancies32. In addition, cadmium interstitials act as luminescent centres, contributing to the defect-related emission observed in CdS73. Further, the PL spectra of the thin film samples show a sharp intense PL emission peak at 680 nm attributed to surface defects caused by radiative transition from the Cd-vacancy defect states to the valence band45,74,75. Importantly, defect states with the positive charge state tend to capture photogenerated electrons, whereas negatively charged defect states preferentially trap holes, with the corresponding hole trap levels typically located close to the valence band72,76,77.

Among all the samples, the 2 wt% Sb-doped CdS thin film exhibits the highest PL peak intensity, which can be due to the following reasons: substitutional incorporation of Sb3+ into CdS creates intrinsic defect states and increases PL intensity by inducing radiative recombination. The increase in carrier concentration within the conduction band due to Sb-doping may lead to an enhancement in PL intensity. The higher crystallite size and reduced crystal defects enhance the radiative recombination of electron-hole pairs and cause a rise in PL intensity56. The observed reduction in PL intensity for 3 wt% Sb-doping concentration is due to the quenching phenomenon, causing an enhanced number of nonradiative recombinations74. The observed results of PL spectra are consistent with previous reports on CdS. Yılmaz et al.73 obtained similar results in PL intensity of Al-doped CdS thin films, while Shkir et al.45 observed high crystallite size and enhanced PL intensity for 4 wt% Fe-doped CdS thin film.

Photoluminescence (PL) spectra provide critical insights into the optical and electronic properties of Sb-doped CdS, directly impacting the performance of PDs. The observed intense NBE peak (at 2.36 eV, 525 nm) in the PL spectra corresponds to the bandgap energy of Sb-doped CdS and also confirms efficient light absorption crucial for photodetectors. The observed higher PL intensity correlates with fewer defect-related non-radiative emissions and effective electron-hole separation and transport. Although the present work primarily focuses on UV photodetection, the observed PL emissions and optical absorption also suggest the potential for photoresponse in the visible region due to band-edge transitions and defect-related states. A brief discussion, supported by relevant literature and a comparison with reported CdS-based visible photodetectors, is provided in Sect. 4.7 and Table 4.

A subsequent reduction at higher doping (3 wt%) suggests the influence of defect-induced scattering and possible carrier trapping, which limit free carrier transport. This behaviour indicates that the effect of doping on dark current is governed by a balance between enhanced carrier density and increased defect states at higher concentrations, rather than a simple linear increase.

Response and recovery time (τr and τf ) are critical parameters in evaluating the speed and efficiency of photodetectors. A short response and recovery time ensures a quick reaction to incident light pulses and rapid resetting, which is essential for accurate and repeated signal detection. Response time refers to the duration required for the photocurrent to reach 90% of its peak value after the light is switched ON, whereas recovery time is the time taken for the current to decrease to 10% of its maximum value once the light is turned OFF. Figure 8 illustrates the response and recovery time estimation of the undoped and 1–3 wt% Sb-doped CdS thin film photodetectors and the obtained values are presented in Table 3. The parameters were obtained under illumination at 365 nm UV source with an intensity of 5 mW cm− 2. From the Table, it is clear that the undoped CdS exhibits the fastest response time (0.30 s) but a relatively longer recovery time (1.16 s). The film with 2 wt% Sb-doped CdS shows a balanced low response time of 1.02 s and a fast recovery time of 0.56 s. The estimated response and recovery times of Sb-doped CdS are found to be comparable to previously reported values for CdS-based photodetectors56. Barman et al.83 reported a high response and recovery time of 1.8 s and 10.5 s, respectively, for Zn-doped CdS, while for Al-doped CdS, it was 0.75 s and 0.85 s21. Table 4 presents a comparison of the response and recovery times obtained in this study with previously reported values for CdS-based photodetectors.

Thin films with a high crystallite nature typically exhibit fast response and recovery times due to fewer defect states and high electron mobility84. Sensing materials with high electron and hole mobility allow charge carriers to drift quickly toward the electrodes, reducing transit time and enabling faster response. Higher crystallite size reduces grain boundary scattering and improves the mobility of charge carriers, which enables faster response and recovery time in a photodetector. Defect states play a vital role in determining the speed and efficiency of a photodetector. Deep-level defect states trap the photogenerated charge carriers and may slow down the response time by delaying their transport to the electrodes85. Trapped electrons and holes in defect states may also take a longer time to get released after the light is turned OFF, leading to a prolonged photocurrent and increased recovery time. This explains the fast response and recovery time observed in the 2 wt% Sb-doped CdS thin film photodetector; and, a deteriorated response time for 3 wt% Sb-doping.

It is important to note that the assessment of UV photoresponse in the present study is primarily based on the optical absorption characteristics and current-voltage (I-V) measurements under monochromatic UV illumination (365 nm), rather than on full spectral responsivity analysis. The strong absorption observed below 500 nm, along with the enhanced photocurrent under 365 nm illumination, clearly indicates the sensitivity of the Sb-doped CdS films to UV radiation. However, considering the bandgap of CdS of ~ 2.4 eV (~ 515 nm), the material is inherently responsive to both UV and visible light. Therefore, the present device may not be strictly UV-selective, and the absence of wavelength-dependent responsivity measurements limits a comprehensive evaluation of its spectral selectivity. It is also worth noting that only a limited number of studies have reported detailed spectral responsivity measurements for CdS-based photodetectors over a wide wavelength range78,86,87. For instance, the nanostructured CdS thin film exhibits relatively enhanced responsivity for illumination at 370 nm and 435 nm for a bias of -10 V46. To provide a broader context, previously reported responsivity values at different wavelengths are summarized in Table 4. These studies indicate that CdS typically exhibits strong photoresponse in the visible region, particularly near 495–515 nm, which is consistent with the absorption behavior observed in the present work. Therefore, while the current results confirm effective UV photoresponse, a detailed investigation of wavelength-dependent responsivity would provide deeper insight into the spectral selectivity of the device.

Under optical illumination, photogenerated carriers undergo generation, recombination, trapping via defect states and eventual transport to the electrodes. Mechanism of photodetection

When Sb is doped into Cadmium sulfide, it introduces donor-like defect states located just below the conduction band minimum (CBM), which facilitate electron excitation and enhance carrier concentration. As a result, the Fermi level shifts towards the conduction band, indicating an increase in n-type conductivity. A schematic energy band diagram of the Sb-doped CdS thin film photodetector under (a) dark and (b) UV illumination conditions is shown in Fig. 10.

Under dark conditions, these donor-like states are partially occupied, contributing to the background carrier density. Upon UV illumination, electrons are excited from the valence band (VB) to the conduction band (CB), leading to an increased electron population resulting in the Fermi level shifting further toward the CB. In addition, the presence of defect states within the bandgap enables a stepwise excitation process, where electrons can be promoted via intermediate states before reaching the CB, thereby enhancing photogeneration97,98. Furthermore, intrinsic defect states associated with vacancies and interstitials, as evident from PL analysis, introduce localized energy levels within the bandgap that act as carrier trapping centers. The photogenerated carriers can be temporarily captured by these trap states, suppressing direct recombination. The trapped carriers can be thermally or optically re-excited into the CB (electrons) and VB (holes), contributing to the photocurrent. This trapping-detrapping mechanism effectively prolongs the carrier lifetime and increases the probability of charge collection at the electrodes. As a result, electrons can circulate multiple times under an applied bias before recombination, leading to photoconductive gain.

The observed sub-linear exponent (θ = 0.82) further supports the presence of trap-assisted transport, indicating a non-uniform distribution of localized states near the Fermi level. Such a distribution governs carrier recombination dynamics and is consistent with defect-mediated photoconduction. Therefore, the combined effects of Sb-induced donor states, Fermi level modulation and defect-assisted trapping mechanisms are responsible for the enhanced photoresponse in Sb-doped CdS thin film photodetectors. Additionally, improved crystallite size and reduced crystal defects in the optimally doped Sb: CdS films result in improved mobility and reduced transit time of charge carriers. At a Sb concentration of 2 wt%, the combined effect of enhanced optical absorption, efficient charge separation and reduced recombination maximizes device responsiveness. However, excessive Sb-doping can introduce deep trap states that act as non-radiative recombination centers, increase dark current and degrade performance. The proposed mechanism is presented as a plausible interpretation of the observed behavior. Further investigations, such as detailed trap state analysis and temperature-dependent measurements, would provide more direct insight into the underlying processes.

Comparison with literature

The comparison of the photosensing parameters of Sb-doped CdS (CdS: Sb) with previously reported CdS-based photodetectors as provided in Table 4, shows that 2 wt% of Sb-doping is effective in improving the performance parameters of CdS photodetectors. The responsivity of CdS: Sb is higher than that of some of the rare earth and other metal-doped (Sm, Mg, Eu, Fe, Y) photodetectors. The response and recovery times of CdS: Sb is not superior but fall within an acceptable range compared to other doped CdS materials. The presented data show that the currently developed photodetectors are reasonably better than the previously developed CdS-based photodetectors in terms of their responsivity, EQE and cost-effective fabrication process. Therefore, CdS: Sb is a promising alternative material for UV light photodetectors where high quantum efficiency and good responsivity are more important than ultra-fast response or recovery times.

The response times observed in the present Sb-doped CdS photodetectors are relatively slower compared to recently reported other systems with high-speed operation in the millisecond or microsecond range. This limitation is attributed to trap-assisted carrier dynamics associated with defect states, which tend to prolong carrier recombination. However, these same processes contribute to enhanced photocarrier lifetime, leading to improved responsivity and EQE. It is also important to note that a majority of the reported CdS-based photodetectors are focused on the visible region, and studies on UV-responsive CdS devices remain comparatively limited. As a result, direct comparison of response times with recently reported high-speed photodetectors is not always straightforward. From an application perspective, the present device is well-suited for scenarios where high sensitivity and stable signal detection are prioritized, such as environmental UV monitoring, flame detection, UV sterilization control and low-intensity light sensing. These results highlight the practical relevance of Sb-doped CdS thin films for reliable UV sensing applications15,17.

Stability test

Stability is a critical parameter governing the long-term operational reliability and performance consistency of photodetectors6,102. Sustained stability ensures the retention of key characteristics such as responsivity, sensitivity and switching behavior over extended durations, thereby minimizing performance degradation under practical operating conditions. External factors, including temperature variations, humidity and prolonged ultraviolet exposure, can adversely affect material quality, highlighting the importance of systematic stability evaluation, particularly for applications requiring continuous and accurate detection. In general, the stability of photodetectors can be evaluated under different illumination sources, depending on the intended spectral range of operation102. In the present work, the assessment was carried out under 365 nm UV illumination (5 mW cm⁻²), consistent with the targeted UV sensing application. The photocurrent was monitored immediately after fabrication and subsequently at ten-day intervals over a period of 50 days. As illustrated in Fig. 11, the devices exhibit negligible variation in photocurrent throughout the evaluation period, indicating excellent temporal stability and reproducible photoresponse. The consistent electrical behaviour under prolonged UV exposure reflects the robustness of the material system and the integrity of the device architecture. These results confirm the suitability of Sb-doped CdS thin films for reliable and sustained UV photodetection applications.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates the successful fabrication of Sb-doped CdS thin films via nebulizer spray pyrolysis (NSP) and their remarkable performance in UV photodetection. The UV photoresponse inferred from the optical absorption below 500 nm and the enhanced photocurrent under 365 nm illumination confirms the UV sensitivity of the Sb-doped CdS films. Structural, morphological and optical analyses confirm that 2 wt% Sb-doping improves crystalline nature, grain size and optical absorption, resulting in superior photodetector characteristics. The 2 wt% Sb-doped CdS photodetector exhibited high responsivity (1.84 A W-1), detectivity (4.60 × 109 Jones) and external quantum efficiency (626%), along with fast photoresponse times. The stable current response over multiple cycles reflects the durability and reliability of the photodetectors, confirming their potential for long-term practical applications. These findings highlight the potential of Sb-doped CdS thin films for advanced UV photodetectors, contributing to the development of high-sensitivity and efficient optoelectronic devices.
