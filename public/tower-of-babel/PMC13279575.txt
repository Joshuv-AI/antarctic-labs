Targeting Neuroinflammation in Adult Ischemic Stroke: Direct Immunotherapy, Regenerative Cell-Based Strategies, and Translational Challenges—A Systematic Review with Exploratory Meta-analysis
Authors: Júlia Cera, Wirginia Krzyściak
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Neuroinflammatory and immune-mediated processes are increasingly recognized as important contributors to adult ischemic stroke pathobiology, contributing not only to acute neuronal injury and secondary tissue damage, but also to later phases of repair and recovery. Experimental and early clinical evidence suggests that immunotherapeutic interventions may modulate post-ischemic inflammatory cascades and immune-cell activation, thereby potentially contributing to neuroprotective and neurorestorative responses. However, the clinical efficacy, safety, and translational relevance of these approaches remain incompletely defined. This systematic review aimed to critically synthesize contemporary preclinical and clinical evidence on neuroinflammation-targeted immunotherapeutic strategies in adult ischemic stroke, while distinguishing direct immunotherapy from regenerative cell-based approaches with immunomodulatory relevance. A systematic search of PubMed/MEDLINE and Embase was conducted in October 2025 and updated in January 2026 to identify original English-language studies published between January 2020 and December 2025. Eligible studies included preclinical and clinical investigations evaluating direct immunotherapeutic interventions or regenerative cell-based therapies with immunomodulatory relevance in adult ischemic stroke, compared with placebo, standard care, or control conditions appropriate to study design. In total, 913 records were screened, of which 55 studies met all inclusion criteria and were included in the qualitative synthesis. Of these, three randomized controlled trials (N = 637) provided comparatively compatible, although methodologically heterogeneous, outcome data for an exploratory meta-analysis of excellent functional outcome at day 90. Methodological quality was assessed using validated design-specific tools for randomized controlled trials, observational studies, and preclinical animal experiments. Primary outcomes included functional recovery and infarct-related measures, while secondary outcomes encompassed neuroinflammatory biomarkers, immune modulation, and safety. Therapeutic approaches were categorized into molecular immunotherapy, biological immunotherapy, and regenerative cell-based therapies with immunomodulatory properties, according to their dominant mechanism of action and translational rationale. Molecular interventions targeting inflammatory and immunometabolic signaling pathways were associated with reductions in neuroinflammatory signaling and infarct-related measures in preclinical models in preclinical models, although corresponding human evidence remained limited. Regenerative cell-based therapies, including mesenchymal stromal/stem cells, progenitor cells, and related cellular products, demonstrated neuroregenerative, paracrine, and immunomodulatory characteristics in preclinical and early translational studies with favorable safety profiles; however, clinical functional benefits remained modest, heterogeneous, and inconsistent. Biological approaches, particularly monoclonal antibodies targeting leukocyte adhesion and immune-cell trafficking, demonstrated generally acceptable safety profiles but did not demonstrate consistent functional benefit across currently available randomized clinical trials. In the exploratory quantitative synthesis, no significant pooled clinical benefit was observed (OR = 0.87; 95% CI, 0.27–2.77), with moderate between-study heterogeneity (I2 = 46.3%). Cross-study comparability was further limited by substantial heterogeneity in intervention timing, dosing regimens, patient selection, biological targets, and outcome definitions, precluding definitive conclusions regarding efficacy. Neuroinflammation-targeted immunotherapy in adult ischemic stroke, together with selected regenerative cell-based strategies of immunomodulatory relevance, appears biologically plausible and mechanistically supported by current preclinical and early clinical evidence based on current preclinical and early clinical evidence and has demonstrated generally acceptable safety profiles in early-phase studies; however, current evidence remains insufficient to support consistent clinical benefit, and available trials may be insufficiently powered to reliably detect moderate but clinically meaningful treatment effects. Future research should prioritize mechanism-driven trial designs, standardized outcome measures, biomarker-informed patient stratification, optimized therapeutic windows, and integrative strategies combining immunomodulation with established reperfusion therapies. Well-powered, methodologically harmonized clinical trials aligned with the temporal and biological heterogeneity of post-stroke inflammation are essential to clarify translational potential and define the role of immune-targeted therapies in stroke management. Importantly, the present review supports a mechanistically differentiated framework in which direct immunotherapy and regenerative cell-based therapy should not be treated as interchangeable categories, even when both modulate post-stroke neuroinflammation. Overall, the available evidence may support further investigation of a stage-specific and mechanistically differentiated model of immune-targeted intervention in ischemic stroke.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12035-026-05980-z.

Introduction

Ischemic stroke remains one of the leading causes of long-term disability and mortality worldwide and continues to impose a major global socioeconomic burden [1]. Rather than being understood solely as a vascular occlusive event—or, conversely, as a primarily inflammatory disorder—ischemic stroke is now increasingly conceptualized as a disorder of the neurovascular unit and a complex neurovascular syndrome involving dynamic interactions among endothelial cells, pericytes, astrocytes, microglia, neurons, and immune compartments [2, 3].

Despite major advances in reperfusion therapies, including intravenous thrombolysis and mechanical thrombectomy, many patients experience incomplete recovery because of narrow therapeutic windows, incomplete tissue rescue despite successful recanalization, microvascular dysfunction, and reperfusion-associated secondary injury [4]. These downstream processes involve not only inflammatory cascades, but also endothelial dysfunction, blood–brain barrier disruption, altered neurovascular coupling, and multicellular injury within the neurovascular unit [3].

Accumulating evidence indicates that the ischemic cascade initiates a temporally orchestrated series of neurovascular and immune responses that critically influence secondary brain injury and subsequent tissue repair. Importantly, these responses do not emerge exclusively within the ischemic parenchyma, but begin early within the vascular compartment through endothelial activation, leukocyte adhesion, platelet–immune interactions, complement signaling, and thromboinflammatory processes [5]. Complement activation represents an additional mechanistically important component of this response, contributing to leukocyte recruitment, endothelial injury, synaptic remodeling, and reperfusion-associated tissue damage in a temporally dynamic manner [5].

Early events include activation of resident microglia and astrocytes, endothelial dysfunction, disruption of the blood–brain barrier, and infiltration of peripheral immune cells such as neutrophils, monocytes, and T lymphocytes [6]. These processes unfold within the disrupted neurovascular unit and contribute to maladaptive amplification of innate and adaptive immune signaling within the ischemic core and penumbra, while simultaneously altering vascular permeability, tissue perfusion, and cellular homeostasis [2, 3].

These immune mediators amplify neuronal injury through the release of proinflammatory cytokines—including TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6—together with oxidative stress and matrix metalloproteinase activation, thereby further compromising blood–brain barrier integrity and expanding infarct size [6]. At the same time, this inflammatory response is not uniformly deleterious, as selected immune and glial pathways may also contribute to debris clearance, tissue remodeling, and neurovascular repair during later phases of stroke [2, 3]. This biological duality supports the rationale for targeted, stage-specific immunotherapeutic strategies rather than nonspecific immune suppression.

Within this broader neurovascular framework, recent mechanistic studies have substantially refined our understanding of post-stroke immune dysregulation, shifting the field toward more precise and mechanism-driven immunomodulatory strategies. Accordingly, immunotherapy in ischemic stroke may be more appropriately viewed not as treatment of an isolated inflammatory disorder, but as selective modulation of one biologically important dimension of a multifactorial neurovascular disease.

Single-cell transcriptomic analyses have shown that microglia exhibit substantial heterogeneity rather than conforming to the classical M1/M2 paradigm, with distinct transcriptional states emerging across the acute and subacute phases of ischemia [7]. These findings suggest that nonspecific immune suppression is unlikely to be sufficient and that precision immunomodulation may represent a more biologically appropriate therapeutic direction. Additional experimental work indicates that targeting immune-cell phenotypes and inflammatory signaling pathways may influence post-stroke injury responses: modulation of mechanisms such as NLRP3 inflammasome activation and AMPK–mTOR signaling has demonstrated promising preclinical findings regarding tissue protection and reparative signaling [8, 9], thereby informing the ongoing development of molecular immunotherapies.

Metabolic dysfunction within immune cells has emerged as an important regulator of post-stroke neuroinflammation, with impaired immunometabolic homeostasis exacerbating inflammatory signaling, leukocyte dysfunction, and neuronal injury [10, 11].

Advances in neuroimmunology have also underscored the importance of adaptive immune responses: CD4⁺ T cells influence microglial activation and functional outcomes, whereas IL-17A–producing γδ T cells have been associated with amplification of post-ischemic inflammatory injury in experimental models [12, 13]. Clinical studies further suggest that dynamic changes in lymphocyte subsets may be associated with neurological severity, systemic immune status, and functional prognosis after stroke, although the causal and therapeutic significance of these observations remains incompletely understood.

Beyond local neuroinflammation, ischemic stroke also induces systemic immune dysregulation involving brain–periphery crosstalk through neuroendocrine and autonomic pathways. Stroke-induced systemic immunosuppression—characterized by lymphopenia, altered splenic responses, impaired peripheral immune competence, and increased susceptibility to infection—has emerged as a potentially clinically relevant component of post-stroke immunopathology, although its implications for immunotherapeutic timing and patient stratification remain to be fully established.

Importantly, post-stroke neuroinflammation does not occur in a biologically neutral host. Common vascular comorbidities—particularly arterial hypertension—may precondition the immune system before stroke onset by promoting myeloid activation, endothelial dysfunction, and chemokine-driven leukocyte recruitment, thereby potentially amplifying secondary inflammatory injury after cerebral ischemia [14, 15]. This has important translational implications, as the baseline inflammatory state may influence both the magnitude of post-stroke neuroinflammation and the responsiveness to immunomodulatory interventions.

Despite these mechanistic advances, clinical translation has consistently lagged behind biological discovery. Recent consensus frameworks, including STAIR XIII, emphasize that the success of neuroprotective and immunomodulatory strategies is likely to depend on alignment with the temporal dynamics of ischemic injury [16, 17]. Rather than a single therapeutic window, ischemic stroke is now understood as a sequence of overlapping pathophysiological phases—including hyperacute (pre-reperfusion), acute (peri-reperfusion), and subacute (post-reperfusion) stages—each characterized by distinct but interconnected mechanisms of injury, inflammation, and repair [16, 18]. Inflammatory responses are particularly relevant in this context because they evolve over extended timeframes and contribute not only to early ischemic damage, but also to reperfusion-associated secondary injury and later repair-associated processes [13, 18]. This temporal framework provides a biologically plausible rationale for stage-specific immunotherapeutic approaches tailored to the evolving neuroimmune landscape of ischemic stroke [13, 16–18].

Given these developments, a neurovascularly contextualized and mechanism-oriented synthesis of immunotherapeutic strategies is needed to clarify translational barriers and distinguish mechanistically distinct therapeutic categories. Such an approach may also help guide future clinical trial design in this rapidly evolving field. Therefore, in this systematic review conducted and reported in accordance with the PRISMA 2020 Statement, we systematically and critically evaluate contemporary preclinical and clinical evidence on therapeutic strategies targeting neuroinflammatory and immune-regulatory pathways within the broader neurovascular pathobiology of adult ischemic stroke, while acknowledging the substantial biological, methodological, and translational heterogeneity across the available evidence base. Our objectives are to (1) identify contemporary mechanism-driven immunomodulatory strategies with translational relevance, (2) delineate barriers to clinical translation, (3) distinguish direct immunotherapy from regenerative cell-based approaches with immunomodulatory relevance, and (4) propose priorities for future research aimed at integrating immune-targeted therapies with established reperfusion interventions.

For conceptual and translational clarity, the present review does not define “immunotherapy” in a narrow pharmacological sense only. Instead, it evaluates a broader spectrum of therapeutic strategies that either directly target immune/inflammatory pathways or exert clinically relevant immunomodulatory effects within post-stroke neuroinflammation. However, these categories are analyzed separately and should not be interpreted as mechanistically equivalent.

Methods

Study Design

This systematic review and exploratory quantitative synthesis were conducted in accordance with the PRISMA 2020 statement [19], aiming to ensure transparency, reproducibility, and methodological rigor, and were designed to distinguish systematically synthesized evidence from narrative contextual interpretation. The review aimed to identify, systematically synthesize, and critically evaluate evidence on immunotherapeutic interventions for adult ischemic stroke across clinical and translational preclinical studies, and—where appropriate—to quantitatively synthesize comparative clinical trial data.

Search Strategy

A systematic, reproducible search of PubMed/MEDLINE and Embase was initially conducted in October 2025 and subsequently updated in January 2026 to maximize the likelihood of capturing all eligible studies published through 31 December 2025. The update search was performed using the same databases, search logic, and eligibility criteria as the primary search to ensure methodological consistency across the full review period. Only peer-reviewed, English-language original research articles were eligible for inclusion in the formal systematic synthesis. Review articles, mechanistic syntheses, and conceptual papers were not included in the formal systematic evidence synthesis but were used exclusively to provide biological and translational context in the Introduction and Discussion.

The temporal restriction to studies published between 2020 and 2025 was applied to preferentially focus on contemporary mechanism-driven and translational immunotherapeutic strategies that emerged following earlier first-generation anti-inflammatory approaches already comprehensively synthesized in prior systematic reviews and meta-analyses.

The PubMed search strategy was: (“ischemic stroke” OR “cerebral infarction”) AND (immunotherapy OR “immune modulation” OR “neuroinflammation” OR “anti-inflammatory” OR “natalizumab” OR “stem cells” OR “PKM2” OR “STAT3” OR “immune checkpoint” OR complement OR thromboinflammation OR “neutrophil extracellular traps” OR immunosuppression) AND (adults[MeSH Terms]) AND (“2020”[Date—Publication]–“2025”[Date—Publication]).

The restriction of the search window to the period January 2020–December 2025 was based on three considerations. First, the immunotherapy landscape for ischemic stroke underwent a substantive transformation around 2019–2020, with the emergence of mechanism-driven, pathway-specific strategies (e.g., PKM2–NF-κB axis modulation, single-cell transcriptomics-informed microglial targeting, second-generation allogeneic cell therapies) that are qualitatively distinct from earlier first-generation anti-inflammatory approaches comprehensively reviewed elsewhere [7, 13, 22–25].

Second, restricting the search to the most recent publication period increased the likelihood of capturing studies conducted and reported under contemporary methodological standards, thereby improving comparability across included studies and prioritizing evidence generated under current standards of trial design, reporting quality, and bias assessment [26].

Third, this temporal scope is consistent with precedent in the neuroinflammation systematic review literature, where comparable or narrower windows have been employed [27, 28]. Foundational and historical studies were referenced in the Introduction to preserve contextual continuity.

Although this temporal restriction may have limited the historical breadth of the evidence base, it was considered unlikely to materially distort the contemporary translational interpretation of the field, given that earlier foundational studies have already been synthesized in prior systematic reviews and meta-analyses.

Study Selection

The study selection process was conducted and reported in accordance with the PRISMA 2020 framework [19]. A total of 913 records were identified through database searching (PubMed/MEDLINE and Embase), with no additional records identified through other sources. Before screening, 229 records were removed (187 duplicates and 42 non-English publications, reviews, or editorials), leaving 684 records for title and abstract screening. Of these, 477 were excluded because they did not meet the predefined relevance criteria for immunotherapeutic approaches in ischemic stroke. The remaining 207 full-text articles were assessed for eligibility, of which 152 were excluded for predefined reasons. Mechanistic, foundational, and conceptual studies that did not meet formal eligibility criteria for the systematic evidence synthesis were not treated as included efficacy studies and were not incorporated into comparative evidence appraisal. Quantitative Synthesis: Materials and Methods

Meta-analysis objectives

This exploratory meta-analysis was designed as a hypothesis-generating quantitative extension of the systematic review to explore pooled effect estimates of immunotherapeutic and cell-based interventions on excellent functional outcome at day 90 in adults with acute ischemic stroke, expressed as the odds ratio (OR) with 95% confidence interval (CI). The analysis was conducted and reported in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) 2020 statement [19], the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions, version 6.4 [29], and the Meta-analysis Of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (MOOSE) reporting guidelines [34] where applicable. Given the limited number of eligible trials and the heterogeneity of both intervention classes and composite endpoint definitions, this synthesis is characterized as an exploratory meta-analysis and is intended to complement rather than supplant the narrative synthesis presented elsewhere in this review.

Study Design, Data Structure, and Eligibility Criteria for the Quantitative Synthesis

As described in the “Methods” section, 55 studies were included in the qualitative synthesis, of which three randomized controlled trials (N = 632) were eligible for quantitative analysis. Title and abstract screening of the remaining 684 records led to the exclusion of 477 entries that did not pertain to immunotherapeutic interventions in ischemic stroke. Full-text assessment of the 207 remaining articles resulted in the exclusion of 152 studies for predefined reasons—most frequently, preclinical studies focused exclusively on descriptive or mechanistic pathophysiology without direct therapeutic or translational immunological applicability (n = 44), focus on hemorrhagic or pediatric stroke (n = 31), and absence of an immunotherapeutic intervention (n = 28)—leaving 55 studies eligible for the qualitative synthesis. The complete study selection pathway, including the subset subsequently assessed for quantitative synthesis, is depicted in the PRISMA 2020 flow diagram (Fig. 1).

Importantly, the quantitative synthesis was performed exclusively on trials already eligible within the predefined systematic review framework and timeframe. The reference to March 2026 pertains solely to outcome data extraction and verification and does not indicate an extension of the literature inclusion period. Of the 55 studies included in the overarching systematic review, 16 clinical trials (Table 3) were subjected to a dedicated eligibility assessment for the quantitative synthesis. Eligibility for the quantitative synthesis was determined by three prespecified criteria that had to be fulfilled jointly: (a) the trial was completed and had sufficiently detailed efficacy results publicly available by the time of data extraction (March 2026), while still falling within the predefined review period of studies published through December 2025; (b) the trial employed a randomized, controlled design with a placebo or standard-of-care comparator arm permitting the computation of an odds ratio (OR) or risk ratio (RR) for binary endpoints, or a standardized mean difference (SMD) for continuous endpoints; and (c) raw numerical data—event counts per arm, means with standard deviations, or reported effect estimates with confidence intervals—were extractable from the primary publication or its supplementary materials.

Application of these criteria yielded three trials eligible for the comparative (OR-based) meta-analysis: ACTION [35], ACTION II [36], and TREASURE [25], encompassing a combined sample of N = 637 participants (362 intervention, 275 control). One additional study, SB623 [37, 38], was included in a supplementary pre-post analysis exclusively, owing to its open-label, single-arm design, chronic stroke population (6–60 months post-onset), and stereotactic delivery route, all of which precluded its incorporation into the comparative synthesis. The remaining 12 trials were excluded, most frequently because quantitative efficacy results had not been published (k = 8) or because the trial lacked a comparator arm (k = 3). Full eligibility assessments with individual rationales are documented in Table S1 (included studies) and Table S2 (excluded studies).

Data extraction was performed independently by two reviewers using a standardized extraction form (Supplementary Appendix). Extracted variables comprised: study identifiers (authors, year, NCT registration number, phase), population characteristics (sample size per arm, age, baseline NIHSS, therapeutic window), intervention details (agent, dose, route, timing), and efficacy outcomes (event counts, proportions, OR with CI, risk differences). Any discrepancies were resolved by consensus; inter-rater agreement was evaluated using Cohen’s kappa coefficient [39]. For ACTION II, which employed a three-arm design (natalizumab 300 mg, natalizumab 600 mg, and placebo in a 1:1:1 ratio), the two active treatment arms were combined into a single intervention group (n = 179) versus placebo (n = 91), in accordance with Cochrane Handbook recommendations for multi-arm trials [29]. The reported OR was adopted directly from the publication, and its sampling variance was recovered algebraically from the confidence interval bounds.

The primary efficacy outcome was defined as the proportion of patients achieving an excellent functional outcome at day 90, operationalized as a composite of modified Rankin Scale (mRS) score ≤ 1 and Barthel Index (BI) ≥ 95 (ACTION, ACTION II) or mRS ≤ 1, National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale (NIHSS) ≤ 1, and BI ≥ 95 (TREASURE). Importantly, the composite endpoint definition differed across trials, with TREASURE employing a more restrictive criterion (mRS ≤ 1, NIHSS ≤ 1, and BI ≥ 95) compared with the ACTION program (mRS ≤ 1 and BI ≥ 95). This definitional heterogeneity represents a non-trivial source of clinical and statistical inconsistency and was explicitly considered in sensitivity and subgroup analyses. Where the primary endpoint in the original trial differed from the present meta-analytic endpoint—as was the case for ACTION, whose prespecified primary outcome was change in MRI infarct volume at day 5 [35]—the mRS ≤ 1 proportion at day 90 was extracted as a secondary clinical outcome from the published results. For the ACTION trial, the day 90 mRS ≤ 1 outcome was not the prespecified primary endpoint but was selected as a clinically relevant secondary endpoint to ensure comparability across trials reporting functional outcomes.

Statistical Analysis

1.3.1. Effect Size Estimation

The effect size measure employed throughout the comparative analysis was the odds ratio (OR) on the natural logarithmic scale. For consistency, the odds ratio was coded such that values below 1 indicate lower odds of achieving an excellent functional outcome in the intervention group compared with the control. For interpretative clarity, the direction of the odds ratio was defined a priori; however, this coding differs from some conventional meta-analytic presentations and should therefore be interpreted with caution throughout the Results and Discussion. For ACTION and TREASURE, log-ORs and their sampling variances were computed with the Mantel–Haenszel method. For ACTION II, whose raw cell counts were not available per arm owing to the three-arm pooling, the log-OR and variance were derived from the publication-reported OR and 95% CI as described above. Individual study estimates were back-transformed to the OR scale for presentation in forest plots; all modeling was conducted on the log-OR scale. The significance level was set at α = 0.05 (two-tailed) for all inferential tests unless otherwise stated.

1.3.2. Meta-analytic Model Specification

A random-effects model was selected a priori as the primary analytic framework, on the grounds that the included trials differed in their biological class of intervention (monoclonal antibody vs. multipotent adult progenitor cells), composite endpoint definitions, therapeutic windows, and patient populations—factors that make the assumption of a single common true effect unlikely to be biologically or methodologically plausible [40]. Between-study variance (τ2) was estimated using the DerSimonian and Laird (DL) method [41], the most widely adopted moment-based estimator in the meta-analytic literature and the default in RevMan 5.4 [42]. Because the number of included studies was small (k = 3), the Hartung–Knapp–Sidik–Jonkman (HKSJ) adjustment was applied to the confidence interval of the pooled estimate [43–45]. The HKSJ correction replaces the standard normal z-based inference with a t-distribution on k − 1 degrees of freedom and incorporates a quadratic adjustment factor, thereby producing wider and more conservative confidence intervals that are better calibrated at small k [46]. The pooled OR with its 95% HKSJ-corrected CI was used as the principal summary measure for exploratory comparative interpretation.

1.3.3. Assessment of Heterogeneity

Statistical heterogeneity among the included trials was quantified using three complementary metrics. First, Cochran’s Q statistic was computed as the weighted sum of squared deviations of individual study estimates from the pooled estimate, evaluated against a χ2 distribution with k − 1 degrees of freedom at a significance threshold of α = 0.10, following the recommendation of Cochrane Handbook §10.10.2 [29] that a liberal threshold is warranted because the Q test has low statistical power when k is small. Second, the I2 statistic [47] was reported to express the proportion of total variability attributable to between-study heterogeneity rather than sampling error, interpreted according to the conventional benchmarks: < 25% (low), 25–75% (moderate), and > 75% (high). Third, the between-study variance τ2 and its square root τ (the standard deviation of the true effect distribution) were reported to characterize the absolute magnitude of heterogeneity on the log-OR scale. Confidence intervals for τ2 and I2 were obtained using the Q-profile method [48].

1.3.4. Subgroup and Moderator Analysis

Given the biologically grounded heterogeneity between the two intervention classes represented in the analysis—monoclonal antibody-mediated immunotherapy (natalizumab, targeting α4-integrin/VLA-4 to block leukocyte transmigration across the blood–brain barrier) and allogeneic cell therapy (HLCM051/MultiStem, a multipotent adult progenitor cell product with paracrine immunomodulatory properties)—a prespecified subgroup analysis stratified by biological class was conducted. Subgroup-specific random-effects estimates were computed, and the test for subgroup differences was performed using a mixed-effects model with biological class as a categorical moderator variable. It is noted that the cell therapy subgroup contained a single trial (TREASURE; k = 1), for which no pooled subgroup estimate was generated; instead, the individual point estimate with its 95% CI was reported without a summary diamond.

1.3.5. Sensitivity Analyses

Three preplanned sensitivity analyses were performed to evaluate the robustness of the pooled estimate. First, a leave-one-out (LOO) analysis was conducted by sequentially removing each study from the meta-analysis and recomputing the pooled OR, thereby assessing whether any single trial exerted a disproportionate influence on the summary effect [49]. Given that k = 3, each LOO iteration reduced the analysis to k = 2, and the resulting estimates were interpreted as exploratory. Second, a fixed-effects (common-effect) model was fitted as a comparator to the primary random-effects model, providing an assessment of the sensitivity of results to the choice of meta-analytic framework. Third, the influence of the heterogeneous composite endpoint definition was evaluated by comparing the overall estimate inclusive of TREASURE (whose composite additionally requires NIHSS ≤ 1) against the mAb-only subgroup in which composite definitions were more closely aligned.

1.3.6. Risk of Bias Assessment

Methodological quality of the included randomized controlled trials was assessed using the Cochrane Risk of Bias 2 (RoB 2) tool [30], which evaluates five domains: (D1) bias arising from the randomization process, (D2) bias due to deviations from intended interventions, (D3) bias due to missing outcome data, (D4) bias in measurement of the outcome, and (D5) bias in selection of the reported result. An overall risk-of-bias judgment was derived for each study. Assessments were performed independently by two reviewers, with discrepancies resolved through discussion. 1.3.7. Assessment of Publication Bias

Assessment of publication bias was planned a priori and included visual inspection of a funnel plot [51] depicting individual study effect estimates against their standard errors, supplemented by Egger’s regression test for funnel plot asymmetry [52]. However, both the funnel plot and the Egger test are acknowledged to possess insufficient statistical power when the number of studies falls below 10 [29, 53] (§13.3.5). Accordingly, the funnel plot and Egger test are presented for methodological transparency only and should not be interpreted as reliable evidence regarding the presence or absence of publication bias. This limitation is disclosed in accordance with GRADE (Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation) guidance on rating down for publication bias [54].

1.3.8. Supplementary Pre–post Analysis (SB623)

For the SB623 trial [37, 38], which lacked a control group, a within-subject pre–post effect size was computed as Hedges’ g (the bias-corrected standardized mean difference) using the formula g = d × J, where d = ΔM/SD and the correction factor J = 1 − 3/[4(n − 1) − 1] adjusts for small-sample upward bias in Cohen’s d [55]. The variance of g was estimated as Var(g) = (1/n + g2/2n) × J2. The resulting effect size estimates are presented in a separate supplementary forest plot (FP-5) and were not pooled with the comparative RCT data, as the absence of a control arm precludes attribution of the observed change to the intervention.

Software and Reproducibility

All statistical computations and forest plot generation were performed in R version 4.3.3 [56] using the metafor package version 4.6–0 [57] for effect size computation, random-effects modeling, heterogeneity estimation, leave-one-out analysis, funnel plot construction, and the meta package version 7.0–0 [58]. Risk-of-bias visualizations were implemented following the RevMan-style recipe documented by Viechtbauer (2024) [59].

Limitations

Methodological limitations included heterogeneity in study designs, intervention timing, dosing protocols, endpoint definitions, small sample sizes (particularly in early-phase trials), and language restrictions, all of which were explicitly considered in the interpretation of findings. Despite these limitations, PRISMA compliance, systematic screening, and validated quality assessment tools support the methodological transparency and overall internal consistency of the review process, although they do not eliminate the limitations inherent to the available evidence base.

These constraints were particularly relevant to the exploratory quantitative synthesis, which was intentionally restricted to a very small number of completed randomized trials with extractable comparative outcome data. Consequently, the pooled estimates should be interpreted cautiously and viewed as hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory evidence of clinical efficacy.

Results

The study selection process is summarized in Fig. 1. In total, 913 records were identified through database searching. After removal of duplicates and ineligible publication types, 55 studies met all predefined eligibility criteria and were included in the formal qualitative synthesis. Of these 55 studies, 24 were classified as preclinical or translational, 16 as clinical trials, and 15 as observational or biomarker-oriented studies, reflecting the broad translational scope represented within the included evidence base, although the methodological and clinical heterogeneity across study categories should be taken into account when interpreting the overall findings. A subset of 16 clinical trials was subsequently evaluated using predefined eligibility criteria for quantitative synthesis, of which three randomized controlled trials fulfilled all criteria for inclusion in the comparative meta-analysis. In addition to the 55 studies formally included in the qualitative systematic synthesis, selected mechanistic, foundational, and conceptual references were incorporated narratively to support biological interpretation and translational framing. These contextual sources were not eligible for the formal systematic evidence synthesis, were not used for comparative efficacy interpretation, and were not counted among the included studies.

The number of included studies increased progressively from 2020 to 2025, with a marked rise after 2022 and the highest concentration observed in 2023–2025, suggesting increasing publication activity in this area over the review period.

Methodological Quality and Risk of Bias Across Included Studies

Formal methodological quality and risk-of-bias assessment was conducted for all studies included in the formal systematic synthesis using design-specific validated tools. Randomized clinical trials were evaluated using the Cochrane Risk of Bias 2 (RoB 2) tool, observational studies using the Newcastle–Ottawa Scale (NOS), and preclinical animal studies using SYRCLE’s Risk of Bias tool.

Overall, the methodological quality of the included evidence was variable and reflected the developmental stage of the field. Randomized clinical trials were predominantly judged as having low risk-of-bias or some concerns, with the main limitations related to selective reporting, endpoint structure, or incomplete reporting of allocation procedures. Observational studies generally showed moderate methodological quality, although limitations related to sample size, confounding, and comparability of cohorts were common. Preclinical studies frequently demonstrated incomplete reporting of randomization methods, allocation concealment, and blinding, which reduced confidence in internal validity despite often strong mechanistic relevance.

Importantly, these methodological assessments informed the interpretation of study findings, the appraisal of translational relevance, and the cautious interpretation of the exploratory quantitative synthesis; however, residual methodological uncertainty remained across several study domains.

Studies are grouped by immunotherapeutic class (molecular, cellular, biological) and ordered chronologically within each category. Study quality was assessed using design-specific validated tools: RoB 2 for randomized clinical trials, NOS for observational studies, and the SYRCLE Risk-of-bias tool for preclinical studies. These assessments informed the interpretation of evidentiary strength and translational relevance across studies.

Table 1 presents representative interventional studies selected to illustrate the major mechanistic classes identified in the review; the full study-level dataset is provided in Supplementary Table S1.

Molecular Immunotherapy

Molecular immunotherapy studies primarily targeted intracellular inflammatory and immunometabolic signaling pathways implicated in post-stroke neuroinflammation. Among the included preclinical studies, PKM2 inhibition represented one of the more extensively investigated mechanistically targeted strategies within the currently available preclinical literature and was associated with reduced infarct volume, attenuated thromboinflammatory responses, decreased pro-inflammatory cytokine expression, and improved neurological outcomes in experimental models. Additional mechanistic work suggests that PKM2 may function upstream of NF-κB– and inflammasome-related inflammatory signaling, supporting its potential relevance as a molecular therapeutic target [3].

Cell-Based Regenerative Therapy with Immunomodulatory Properties

Studies involving mesenchymal stromal/stem cells (MSCs), progenitor cell products, and related cell-based interventions generally suggested multimodal regenerative, paracrine, neurotrophic, and immunomodulatory effects across both preclinical and early-phase clinical settings. Importantly, these approaches are more appropriately interpreted as regenerative cell-based therapies with immunomodulatory properties rather than as classical immunotherapies sensu stricto. Reported findings included modulation of inflammatory signaling, promotion of reparative and inflammation-resolving immune states, enhancement of neurovascular repair, and signals toward improved motor or functional recovery. However, clinical efficacy remained inconsistent and was limited by small sample sizes, variable study designs, and heterogeneity in treatment timing, cell source, and delivery route [61, 62].

Biological Immunotherapy

Biological immunotherapy studies were predominantly represented by monoclonal antibody–based approaches aimed at limiting leukocyte trafficking or immune cell infiltration into the ischemic brain. Natalizumab was the most extensively studied agent in this category and demonstrated a generally acceptable short-term safety profile within the available randomized clinical trials, although consistent functional benefit was not demonstrated. Other biologically targeted approaches remained largely preclinical or insufficiently developed for efficacy assessment within the review period [35, 36].

Mechanistic and Foundational Studies (Table 2)

Studies are organized by conceptual relevance (foundational/contextual, innate immune–microglial–immunometabolic mechanisms, and adaptive immune mechanisms) and ordered chronologically within thematic groupings where applicable. These studies were used narratively to contextualize the biological plausibility and translational rationale of immunotherapeutic strategies rather than to contribute comparative efficacy data.

While Table 1 focuses on interventional studies evaluating immunotherapy in clinical and preclinical models, Table 2 presents mechanistic and foundational research supporting the biological plausibility of these therapeutic approaches and was included to aid biological and translational interpretation rather than comparative efficacy assessment. The included contextual studies highlight key components of the post-stroke immune response, including microglial heterogeneity, T-cell subset dynamics, innate lymphocyte activity, and inflammatory cytokine signaling, thereby identifying potential targets for immunomodulation.

Collectively, these findings support the view that post-stroke immunomodulation likely involves coordinated interactions between innate and adaptive immune compartments, rather than by a single dominant cellular population.

Results of the Quantitative Synthesis

Pooled Effect Estimate Across All Eligible Trials

The exploratory random-effects model yielded a pooled odds ratio of 0.87 (95% CI, 0.27–2.77; t = − 0.52, p = 0.66), indicating no statistically significant difference between immunotherapeutic or cell-based interventions and placebo with respect to the proportion of patients achieving excellent functional outcome at day 90. No statistically significant evidence suggestive of consistent clinical efficacy was identified within the constraints of the currently available and methodologically heterogeneous evidence base. The point estimate fell below unity and, under the coding applied in this analysis, nominally reflected lower odds of achieving an excellent functional outcome in the intervention group than in the control group. However, the confidence interval was wide and encompassed effect estimates compatible with both potential benefit and potential harm, reflecting the substantial imprecision inherent to an analysis of k = 3 trials with heterogeneous interventions. Individual study estimates ranged from OR = 0.60 (ACTION II; 95% CI, 0.39–0.93) to OR = 1.24 (ACTION; 95% CI, 0.58–2.64), with ACTION II being the only trial whose confidence interval excluded the null (see Fig. 2).

Between-study Heterogeneity

Moderate statistical heterogeneity was observed across the three included trials. Cochran's Q statistic was 3.73 (df = 2, p = 0.16), which did not attain conventional significance at the recommended α = 0.10 threshold but should be interpreted cautiously given the low power of the Q test at small k. The I2 statistic was 46.3%, falling within the moderate range (25–75%) per the Higgins and Thompson (2002) benchmarks, and indicating that approximately half of the observed variance in effect estimates was attributable to genuine between-study differences rather than sampling error. The estimated between-study variance was τ2 = 0.1029 (τ = 0.321 on the log-OR scale). These heterogeneity parameters may reflect substantial biological and methodological diversity among the included trials: ACTION and ACTION II evaluated natalizumab (a monoclonal antibody targeting α4-integrin) within a ≤ 24-h therapeutic window, whereas TREASURE assessed an entirely different therapeutic modality—intravenously administered allogeneic multipotent adult progenitor cells (MAPC)—with a broader 18–36-h treatment window and a more restrictive composite endpoint definition.

Subgroup Analysis by Biological Class of Intervention

Prespecified subgroup analysis stratified by biological class revealed divergent point estimates between the two intervention categories, although neither attained statistical significance (see Fig. 3). Within the monoclonal antibody (anti-α4 integrin) subgroup, comprising ACTION and ACTION II (combined n = 426), the pooled OR was 0.80 (95% CI, 0.01–73.33), reflecting extreme imprecision driven by the conflicting direction of the two constituent estimates (ACTION: OR = 1.24; ACTION II: OR = 0.60) and the attendant high residual heterogeneity (I2 = 62%). The cell therapy subgroup contained a single trial (TREASURE; OR = 1.20; 95% CI, 0.49–2.92), for which no pooled subgroup estimate was computed. The formal test for subgroup differences did not demonstrate a statistically significant interaction between biological class and treatment effect (χ2 = 0.48, df = 1, p = 0.49), though this test was underpowered at k = 3. Sensitivity Analyses: Leave-One-Out and Model Comparison

The leave-one-out analysis revealed that the pooled estimate was sensitive to the exclusion of individual trials, a finding expected at k = 3, where each study commands substantial leverage (see Figure FP-4 in Supplementary materials). Excluding ACTION II—the largest trial and the sole study with a confidence interval excluding the null—shifted the pooled OR to 1.22 (95% CI, 0.69–2.17), changing the direction of the pooled point estimate across the null value. Residual heterogeneity dropped to I2 = 0.0% upon removal of ACTION II, suggesting that the directional inconsistency between ACTION II (OR = 0.60) and the other two trials was the principal source of the observed between-study variance. Conversely, excluding either ACTION (pooled OR = 0.76; 95% CI, 0.40–1.44) or TREASURE (pooled OR = 0.80; 95% CI, 0.40–1.61) preserved the sub-unity direction of the overall estimate, though neither exclusion yielded a statistically significant result. The fixed-effects sensitivity model produced a pooled OR of 0.78 (95% CI, 0.55–1.10; p = 0.16), directionally concordant with the random-effects estimate but with a narrower confidence interval, as expected when the between-study variance component is not modeled. This instability of the pooled estimate further highlights the limited robustness and exploratory nature of the current evidence base and reinforces the exploratory nature of the meta-analysis.

Risk of Bias in the Included Trials

Domain-level RoB 2 assessments are displayed within the traffic-light matrix in Fig. 2. All three trials employed double-blind, placebo-controlled designs with adequate randomization procedures (D1: low-risk for ACTION II and TREASURE; some concerns for ACTION owing to limited reporting of allocation concealment details). Performance bias was assessed as low-risk or some concerns across trials, with no evidence of unblinding. Missing outcome data (D3) posed minimal concern, as all three trials reported modified intention-to-treat analyses with low attrition rates. Outcome measurement (D4) was adjudicated as low-risk in all trials, given the use of validated, assessor-blinded neurological scales (mRS, NIHSS, BI). The most notable domain-level concern pertained to the selection of the reported result (D5): ACTION received a high-risk rating because the mRS ≤ 1 outcome at day 90 was a secondary endpoint and was originally reported with a 90% rather than 95% confidence interval, necessitating post-hoc recalculation; ACTION II received some concerns because of the post-hoc three-arm pooling required for meta-analytic compatibility. TREASURE was rated low-risk across all five domains. Overall risk-of-bias judgments were “some concerns” for ACTION and ACTION II and “low risk” for TREASURE.

Given the very small number of eligible comparative trials (k = 3), no formal quantitative analysis examining the association between risk-of-bias profile and effect size was performed. Instead, the potential influence of study quality on effect estimates was explicitly incorporated qualitatively into the interpretation of pooled and individual study effects.

Assessment of Publication Bias

Visual inspection of the funnel plot (see FP-4a in Supplementary materials) revealed an asymmetric distribution of the three study estimates, with ACTION II (the largest trial) positioned to the left of the pooled estimate and the two smaller trials to the right. However, with only k = 3 studies, no reliable inference regarding publication bias can be drawn from the funnel plot, as its interpretive validity requires a minimum of approximately 10 studies [53]. Egger’s regression test for funnel plot asymmetry yielded t = 4.19 (df = 1, p = 0.15), with a limit estimate of b = − 1.28 (95% CI, − 4.53 to 1.98). Although the t-statistic was numerically large, the test did not attain significance, and at k = 3, the Egger test possesses negligible statistical power. Accordingly, the present analysis does not provide a reliable basis for confirming or excluding the presence of publication bias because of the very limited number of eligible studies, and this limitation was taken into account in the overall interpretation of certainty and evidentiary strength [53]. Accordingly, no conclusions regarding the presence or absence of publication bias can be drawn from this analysis.

Summary and Conclusions of the Quantitative Synthesis

The present exploratory meta-analysis did not demonstrate a statistically significant pooled effect or consistent evidence suggestive of clinical benefit for immunotherapeutic or cell-based interventions with respect to excellent functional outcome at day 90 in adults with acute ischemic stroke (OR = 0.87; 95% CI, 0.27–2.77; p = 0.66). The wide confidence interval, spanning effect estimates compatible with both potentially meaningful benefit and potential harm, likely reflects three major methodological constraints: a small number of eligible trials (k = 3), moderate between-study heterogeneity (I2 = 46%), and the application of the HKSJ correction, which, while methodologically appropriate, further widens the interval at small k.

Several findings of the quantitative synthesis merit emphasis. First, ACTION II was the only trial whose confidence interval excluded the null (OR = 0.60; 95% CI, 0.39–0.93), yet under the coding applied in this analysis, an OR below unity corresponded to a lower probability of achieving the composite excellent-outcome endpoint in the natalizumab group relative to placebo. This finding should be interpreted cautiously in the clinical context and aligns with the original trial’s conclusion that natalizumab did not confer functional benefit in acute ischemic stroke [36]. Second, the leave-one-out analysis confirmed that ACTION II exerted the greatest leverage on the pooled estimate, such that its exclusion reversed the direction of the point estimate (OR from 0.87 to 1.22), underscoring the fragility of the overall synthesis. Third, TREASURE, the sole cell therapy trial, demonstrated no treatment effect at day 90 (OR = 1.20; 95% CI, 0.49–2.92; risk difference = 0.5%), although exploratory post hoc analyses at 365 days reported by Houkin et al. identified a trend favoring HLCM051 on selected long-term functional endpoints [25].

The supplementary SB623 analysis yielded a large pre–post effect size (Hedges’ g = − 0.96) in a chronic stroke population, suggesting a potential association that modified mesenchymal stem cell implantation may be associated with neurological improvement; however, the absence of a control arm renders this finding hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory.

The assessment of publication bias was constrained by the small number of studies. Egger’s regression test did not reach significance (t = 4.19, p = 0.15), but this result carries no inferential weight at k = 3. The risk-of-bias evaluation identified “some concerns” in two of the three trials, primarily in the domain of selective outcome reporting; only TREASURE received an overall “low risk” judgement across all RoB 2 domains.

In aggregate, the currently available quantitative evidence remains insufficient to support firm conclusions regarding treatment efficacy for either monoclonal antibody-mediated immunotherapy or intravenous cell therapy in acute ischemic stroke. This overall null finding should be interpreted in light of the exploratory nature of the analysis, the limited and heterogeneous trial base, and the conservative statistical framework applied. The field would benefit from the publication of pending trial results—most notably MASTERS-2 (NCT03545607) and J-REPAIR (NCT04608838), both of which employ endpoint definitions compatible with the present synthesis—and from the conduct of adequately powered, harmonized multicenter trials designed to resolve the clinical equipoise identified by this meta-analysis. Until such data become available, the current evidence base is more appropriately interpreted as being of low to very low certainty, principally because of serious imprecision and indirectness arising from heterogeneous composite endpoint definitions across trials.

Synthesis

Taken together, the evidence summarized across Tables 1, 2, and 3 and the exploratory quantitative synthesis suggest potential translational relevance of these approaches while also underscoring the substantial methodological and clinical limitations of the currently available evidence base of mechanistically distinct strategies, including direct immunotherapeutic approaches and regenerative cell-based therapies with immunomodulatory relevance in adult ischemic stroke.

Substantial variability in study design, intervention timing, dosing, delivery route, and endpoint definitions contributes to heterogeneous findings and limits robust inference regarding clinical efficacy.

Accordingly, further well-designed, adequately powered, and methodologically harmonized trials are required to better define the therapeutic potential and optimal positioning of these strategies within the evolving neurovascular treatment framework.

Discussion

Interpretation of Findings

This systematic review is broadly consistent with the current understanding that neuroinflammatory responses represent an important and sustained component of ischemic stroke pathophysiology, but that these responses unfold within a broader neurovascular context involving endothelial dysfunction, blood–brain barrier disruption, thromboinflammatory signaling, and multicellular injury–repair interactions across the neurovascular unit [83, 84].

In the context of contemporary stroke care, the translational relevance of neuroinflammation is particularly evident in relation to reperfusion-based treatment. Although recanalization remains the cornerstone of acute ischemic stroke management, successful vessel reopening does not fully prevent secondary tissue injury. Reperfusion itself may amplify inflammatory cascades involving oxidative stress, endothelial dysfunction, blood–brain barrier disruption, and activation of innate and adaptive immune responses. Unlike ultra-early neuroprotective strategies, immunomodulatory interventions may remain biologically relevant during peri- and post-reperfusion phases, supporting their investigation as adjunctive rather than competing therapeutic approaches [17, 18]. This translational framing is further supported by growing recognition that reperfusion-associated injury is shaped not only by parenchymal inflammation, but also by thromboinflammatory mechanisms involving endothelial activation, neutrophil extracellular traps, complement-related innate immune signaling, and peripheral immune–vascular crosstalk. This is particularly relevant because neutrophil extracellular traps and related thromboinflammatory mechanisms may contribute not only to blood–brain barrier injury and microvascular dysfunction, but also to incomplete reperfusion and persistent tissue vulnerability despite successful recanalization.

As summarized in Fig. 5, the therapeutic strategies identified in this review can be conceptualized as acting at three distinct but complementary levels: intracellular inflammatory signaling, intercellular immunoregulatory and reparative networks, and systemic immune trafficking. This distinction may provide a useful conceptual framework within the present review and supports the interpretation that direct immunotherapy and regenerative cell-based therapy should not be treated as interchangeable intervention classes, even when both influence post-stroke neuroinflammation.Fig. 5Translational architecture of neuroinflammation-targeted interventions in adult ischemic stroke

This perspective is strongly supported by recent STAIR XIII consensus statements, which highlight that therapeutic efficacy in ischemic stroke is highly dependent on matching intervention strategies to specific temporal phases of injury. In particular, therapies targeting early ischemic cascades must be administered within a narrow hyperacute window, whereas interventions modulating inflammatory responses may retain efficacy in later stages, including during and after reperfusion. This distinction may carry translational implications, as it suggests that immunotherapy may help address one of the major limitations of traditional neuroprotective approaches—namely, the restricted therapeutic time window. Accordingly, immunotherapy is best conceptualized as a temporally adaptive adjunct to reperfusion-based care rather than as a static standalone intervention [16].

The evidence summarized across Tables 1, 2, and 3 suggests that molecular, biological, and cell-based strategies with immunomodulatory relevance appear biologically plausible and have demonstrated generally acceptable safety profiles within the currently available studies in early clinical translation, although the available evidence remains limited and heterogeneous. However, consistent and clinically meaningful efficacy signals have not yet been demonstrated, particularly when these interventions are evaluated as standalone rather than adjunctive therapies. This interpretation is supported by the exploratory pooled analysis, which yielded a null overall estimate with moderate heterogeneity and substantial imprecision [25, 36, 60, 85–88]. Notably, the pooled estimate must also be interpreted in light of the methodological profile of the included trials, as even modest differences in selective reporting, endpoint definition, or analytical structure may disproportionately influence effect size estimates in such a small evidence base. These conclusions must also be interpreted in light of the methodological profile of the included evidence, as internal validity limitations were more frequent among preclinical and early-phase translational studies than among completed randomized clinical trials. A further important limitation of the quantitative synthesis is the heterogeneity in composite endpoint definitions across trials, which may have influenced both the direction and magnitude of estimated treatment effects.

An important methodological limitation concerns the statistical power of the available clinical evidence [#18]. Among the 16 trials cataloged in Table 3, none was a completed confirmatory Phase III efficacy trial designed and powered to detect a clinically meaningful treatment effect on functional outcome endpoints. The three largest completed trials—ACTION (n = 161), ACTION II (n = 265), and TREASURE (n = 206)—were Phase II or Phase II/III studies powered primarily for safety, dose-finding, or proof-of-concept objectives. Post hoc power considerations suggest that these sample sizes would provide only limited power to detect moderate treatment effects. Accordingly, the absence of statistically significant efficacy signals should not be interpreted as evidence of therapeutic futility, but rather as a limitation of underpowered early-phase designs. The field may therefore still be considered to remain in a state of unresolved clinical equipoise and requires adequately powered, preregistered Phase III trials with harmonized endpoints [89–91].

An additional translational consideration is that post-stroke neuroinflammation is strongly modified by the host’s pre-existing vascular and immune status [6, 13, 14]. Experimental evidence from hypertensive stroke models suggests that arterial hypertension may amplify post-ischemic innate immune activation through increased chemokine expression, enhanced myeloid cell recruitment, and greater infiltration of monocytes, macrophages, and granulocytes into the ischemic brain [14, 92]. Notably, these inflammatory changes have been associated with larger infarct volumes despite comparable early lesion size, supporting the concept that comorbidity-driven immune priming may aggravate secondary injury after stroke [14]. This reinforces the view that immunotherapeutic strategies should be interpreted and designed within the broader context of patient-specific inflammatory heterogeneity rather than assuming a uniform post-stroke immune response [6, 13].

Among molecular strategies, targeting immunometabolic regulators such as PKM2 has been identified as a potentially relevant translational target within preclinical and early translational research within preclinical and early translational research. Dhanesha et al. identified PKM2 in neutrophils as a key mediator of post-stroke thromboinflammatory responses. Both genetic deletion of PKM2 in myeloid cells and pharmacological inhibition with ML265 reduced infarct volume, attenuated proinflammatory cytokine release (TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6), and limited neutrophil extracellular trap formation, leading to improved cerebral perfusion and motor recovery [85, 93, 94]. Complementary studies further suggest that PKM2-driven signaling via STAT3 and focal adhesion kinase promotes angiogenesis and neurogenesis, supporting the potential therapeutic relevance of immunometabolic modulation in ischemic stroke [95]. Emerging evidence further supports PKM2 as a convergent immunometabolic regulator linking inflammatory amplification with innate immune activation in ischemic stroke [96]. In parallel, inhibition of the JAK2/STAT3 axis has been shown to suppress cGAS-mediated neuronal senescence and neuroinflammation, further reinforcing STAT3-related signaling as a plausible upstream target within post-ischemic inflammatory cascades [97, 98].

Regenerative cell-based therapy, particularly mesenchymal stromal/stem cell (MSC)-based approaches, may hold translational potential owing to its pleiotropic paracrine, neurorestorative, and immunomodulatory properties. Preclinical and translational evidence suggests that MSCs exert therapeutic effects primarily through paracrine and regenerative mechanisms, including secretion of trophic factors, cytokines, extracellular vesicles, and other bioactive mediators, while also contributing to secondary immunomodulatory rebalancing, including paracrine signaling, modulation of microglial and lymphocyte phenotypes, and direct mitochondrial transfer to T lymphocytes. This mitochondrial transfer has been reported to influence the Th17/Treg axis, reduce oxidative stress, restore mitochondrial membrane potential, and partially reverse pathological shifts in T-cell differentiation and exhaustion states. Importantly, disruption of mitochondrial function within MSCs abolishes these immunomodulatory and neuroprotective effects, highlighting mitochondrial transfer as one potentially important mechanism contributing to the observed therapeutic effects of regenerative cell-based therapy in ischemic stroke models [25, 60, 86, 88, 94, 99]. Early-phase clinical trials, including those evaluating allogeneic products such as MultiStem, have generally demonstrated favorable safety profiles with signals toward functional improvement, although statistical significance has been limited by cohort size, variable endpoints, and heterogeneous treatment timing [87]. Notably, the SB623 study, although not eligible for comparative meta-analysis, demonstrated a large within-subject effect size in a chronic stroke population, suggesting that regenerative cell-based strategies may warrant further investigation in later neurorestorative phases in later, neurorestorative phases rather than in the acute setting targeted by most randomized trials. These findings suggest that MSC-based therapies may require optimized dosing, standardized delivery routes, and combination with reperfusion strategies to achieve robust clinical benefit [25, 60, 99].

Accordingly, MSC-based interventions in ischemic stroke are more appropriately framed as regenerative cell therapy with immunomodulatory properties rather than as a subtype of immunotherapy in a strict mechanistic sense [61, 62, 86].

In contrast, biological immunotherapies targeting leukocyte trafficking, exemplified by monoclonal antibodies such as natalizumab, have thus far yielded neutral efficacy results despite acceptable safety profiles. In the ACTION II trial, natalizumab administered within 24 h of stroke onset did not significantly improve functional outcomes at 90 days [36, 100]. Earlier experiences with nonselective immune blockade, including enlimomab, further highlight the potential risks of broadly suppressing immune responses in the acute phase of stroke [100]. Collectively, these findings may indicate that biological agents targeting single immune pathways could have limited efficacy when used as monotherapy within the currently available clinical framework and may be better positioned as components of multimodal treatment strategies. This interpretation is also consistent with the broader view that post-stroke inflammation is distributed across multiple temporally dynamic immune pathways, thereby limiting the likelihood that single-target blockade alone will yield reproducible clinical benefit. In this context, targeting upstream regulators such as PKM2 may represent a potentially broader mechanistic strategy, as it simultaneously influences multiple downstream inflammatory pathways, including NF-κB signaling and NLRP3 inflammasome activation, rather than selectively inhibiting a single cytokine or receptor [96].

Taken together, the available evidence suggests a translational continuum in which molecular immunomodulatory strategies currently provide comparatively strong mechanistic rationale within the currently available preclinical literature, regenerative cell-based approaches have shown preliminary signals suggestive of possible functional benefit in selected early-phase studies, and biological therapies have thus far contributed more to safety characterization than to reproducible efficacy demonstration. Within this framework, interventions targeting immunometabolic regulators, microglial signaling pathways, or selective adaptive immune rebalancing appear to warrant further investigation in future translational studies, although these directions remain to be validated in adequately powered and biologically stratified clinical trials [25, 83, 84, 96, 97].

This distinction is particularly important when interpreting cell-based therapies, which often combine immunomodulatory, neurotrophic, and reparative effects within a broader regenerative framework.

More broadly, MSC-based approaches should not be interpreted in isolation or conceptualized as classical immunotherapy in the strict mechanistic sense. Rather, they appear to represent one component of a broader post-stroke immunoregulatory and reparative network involving coordinated interactions between innate and adaptive immune populations [13, 61, 62, 84, 101]. In this framework, repair-associated macrophages may contribute to phagocytosis, remyelination, angiogenesis, and tissue remodeling, whereas adaptive immune subsets—including Th1, Th17, and Treg populations—help shape whether the inflammatory milieu remains tissue-destructive or transitions toward resolution and repair [70, 102, 103].

An additional emerging dimension of stroke immunology involves immune checkpoint signaling, including PD-1/PD-L1, CTLA-4, and Tim-3 pathways, which may function as context-dependent regulators of post-stroke immune activation and immune exhaustion. Although these pathways remain only preliminarily explored in intervention-oriented stroke studies, they may represent areas for future investigation as potential future targets for selective immunoregulatory strategies aimed at limiting maladaptive inflammation while preserving reparative immune function.

Importantly, contemporary evidence increasingly supports a move away from binary polarization models and nonspecific immunosuppression toward selective, stage-specific immune rebalancing informed by cellular heterogeneity, functional state, and immunometabolic context. Accordingly, therapeutic interpretation should increasingly rely on functionally defined and transcriptomically informed immune states rather than on simplified M1/M2 categorizations [7, 101–103].

Comparison with Other Studies

The findings of this review are consistent with recent syntheses indicating that most immunomodulatory interventions for ischemic stroke remain at an early translational stage, with relatively few strategies supported by robust clinical efficacy data [16–18, 28]. In particular, molecular approaches targeting PKM2-, STAT3-, and related RNA-regulated inflammatory pathways have shown anti-inflammatory and tissue-protective effects across multiple preclinical studies in preclinical models, although their relevance to human stroke pathobiology remains to be established [24, 85, 95–98]. Likewise, recent syntheses of MSC-based therapies support favorable safety profiles and suggest that their therapeutic activity is driven predominantly by paracrine, neuroregenerative, and neurovascular repair mechanisms, with immunomodulation representing an important but non-exclusive component of their biological action. Functional outcomes, however, remain variable and are influenced by substantial heterogeneity in cell source, dose, timing, and delivery route [27, 86]. The lack of functional benefit observed with natalizumab parallels earlier studies targeting leukocyte adhesion, reinforcing the notion that ischemic stroke involves highly dynamic and multifactorial immune responses that may not be adequately addressed by single-target interventions [36]. Emerging approaches aimed at enhancing regulatory T-cell function or selectively modulating pathogenic immune subsets may offer opportunities for more personalized immunotherapy and could synergize with molecular or cellular strategies [84, 88, 104].

Notably, recent studies published in 2025 further support a shift toward more refined immunomodulatory strategies, emphasizing immune cell heterogeneity, immunometabolic regulation, and adaptive immune responses. At the same time, the broader stroke immunology literature increasingly points to additional underexplored therapeutic dimensions—including stroke-induced systemic immunosuppression, peripheral immune organ responses, complement-related innate cascades, and immune checkpoint signaling—which remain only partially represented in the current intervention-focused evidence base. Greater integration of these pathways may be particularly important for understanding why apparently promising anti-inflammatory strategies fail to translate uniformly across biologically heterogeneous patient populations [8].

From a therapeutic standpoint, these observations support a shift toward integrated treatment paradigms combining recanalization with targeted immunomodulation. Rather than viewing reperfusion and immunotherapy as separate strategies, emerging evidence suggests that their combination may provide complementary therapeutic effects by simultaneously restoring blood flow and mitigating inflammation-driven secondary injury [16–18]. Crucially, the success of such combined strategies will likely depend not only on target selection but also on precise temporal alignment with evolving ischemic, reperfusion-associated, and reparative phases of injury.

Such approaches may include inhibition of key inflammatory mediators (e.g., IL-1β, IL-6, TNF-α), modulation of inflammasome activation (e.g., NLRP3), regulation of microglial activation states and neuroimmune signaling, and targeting of immune cell trafficking or immunometabolic pathways [84, 97, 98]. Notably, these strategies align with recent expert recommendations advocating for multi-target and stage-specific therapeutic approaches that address the dynamic and multifactorial nature of post-stroke inflammation [16–18, 83].

Taken together, the present findings suggest that the limited clinical efficacy observed to date should not necessarily be interpreted as evidence against the broader therapeutic concept of immunomodulation in ischemic stroke, but may instead reflect a mismatch between biological complexity and current trial design frameworks. Future progress will depend on aligning immunomodulatory strategies with the temporal, cellular, and metabolic heterogeneity of post-stroke neuroinflammation, rather than applying uniform interventions across biologically distinct patient populations.

Limitations

Several limitations of the current evidence base should be acknowledged. First, substantial heterogeneity in study design, patient populations, intervention classes, dosing regimens, endpoint definitions, and treatment timing limits comparability and constrains quantitative synthesis [17–19, 49, 51, 57]. Although an exploratory meta-analysis was technically feasible, and the interpretability and generalizability of the pooled estimates were substantially constrained, its interpretability was limited by the very small number of eligible randomized trials, moderate between-study heterogeneity, and differences in endpoint definition and analytical structure across studies. These limitations were compounded by endpoint harmonization challenges and the fragility of pooled estimates in sensitivity analyses [17–19, 49, 51, 57]. These concerns are reinforced by the variable methodological quality and risk-of-bias profile across the included evidence base, particularly among preclinical and early-phase translational studies, where incomplete reporting of randomization, blinding, and allocation procedures remains common [25, 36, 60, 86, 88, 99]. In addition, although mechanistic and foundational studies were incorporated to support biological interpretation and translational framing, these sources were not used to infer comparative therapeutic efficacy and should be interpreted as contextual rather than confirmatory evidence. Second, translational gaps persist between preclinical and clinical research, as animal models incompletely recapitulate the biological and clinical complexity of human ischemic stroke, comorbidities, or immune aging, contributing to frequent failures in clinical translation [85, 93, 96]. Third, the optimal therapeutic window for immunomodulation remains insufficiently defined and is likely to differ across mechanistically distinct intervention classes. As emphasized in recent STAIR XIII recommendations, therapeutic efficacy is highly dependent on the temporal alignment between intervention and the evolving pathophysiological processes of ischemic stroke. Early interventions may interfere with endogenous repair mechanisms, whereas delayed treatment may fail to adequately suppress established inflammatory cascades. The lack of standardized, phase-specific treatment frameworks across studies represents a major barrier to translation and highlights the need for future trials explicitly incorporating time-stratified therapeutic approaches [36, 83, 84].

Additional limitations include the lack of validated human biomarkers for patient stratification, such as circulating PKM2, STAT3 activation, or neutrophil extracellular trap markers, as well as relatively short follow-up durations in most clinical trials [85, 95–97].

Finally, restriction to English-language publications published between January 2020 and December 2025 may have introduced language-related selection bias and reduced historical breadth; however, this decision was made a priori to prioritize contemporary, methodologically comparable, and clinically relevant evidence reflecting the current phase of development in stroke immunotherapy [86].

Another important limitation of the current translational evidence base is the insufficient incorporation of comorbidity-associated immune heterogeneity. Common stroke comorbidities, particularly arterial hypertension, may alter baseline immune activation and thereby influence both the magnitude of post-stroke neuroinflammation and the responsiveness to immunomodulatory treatment. This issue remains underrepresented in both preclinical modeling and clinical trial design [14].

Accordingly, studies with favorable biological or clinical signals but important methodological constraints were interpreted cautiously and considered hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory.

Future Directions

Recent comprehensive reviews of inflammatory biomarkers in ischemic stroke further support a paradigm shift toward biomarker-driven and precision immunotherapy approaches. Over the past decade, increasing emphasis has been placed on cytokine profiling, composite inflammatory indices, and their integration into prognostic and translational models. In particular, markers such as IL-6, CRP, and NLR have consistently emerged as correlates of stroke severity, functional outcome, and post-stroke complications. Importantly, these biomarkers may also serve as actionable tools for patient stratification in immunotherapy trials, helping identify individuals most likely to benefit from targeted interventions such as cytokine blockade, inflammasome inhibition, or cell-based immunomodulation [83].

Accordingly, future immunotherapy trials should move beyond descriptive biomarker reporting toward prospectively integrated immune profiling for patient selection, treatment timing, and mechanistic response assessment. Such designs may be particularly valuable for distinguishing patients with predominantly thromboinflammatory, innate immune–dominant, or maladaptive adaptive immune signatures, thereby improving biological matching between intervention and disease state.

Furthermore, recent data emphasize the importance of temporal and phase-specific interpretation of biomarkers, as the same mediators may exert divergent effects depending on the stage of stroke. This dynamic perspective is particularly relevant for designing immunomodulatory strategies that require precise timing to balance neuroprotection and immune-mediated repair.

Future research should prioritize large, multicenter phase III trials with rigorous methodology, standardized protocols, and stratification based on stroke severity, comorbidities, and time from symptom onset [25, 83, 84]. Specifically, forthcoming trials should be powered to detect effect sizes in the range of OR = 1.3–1.5 for composite functional endpoints (e.g., mRS ≤ 1 combined with BI ≥ 95 at day 90), which would likely require enrolment of approximately 500–800 participants per arm under conventional statistical assumptions depending on control-arm event rates and allocation ratio [105]. Adaptive platform trial designs incorporating interim analyses, dose optimization, and biomarker-driven enrichment strategies may provide a pragmatic approach to achieving adequate statistical power while preserving efficiency and ethical flexibility [106]. The MASTERS-2 trial (NCT03545607) [72], if completed and fully reported, may represent the first major Phase III dataset in this field and may provide important additional evidence within this evolving field.

Integration of immunotherapy with reperfusion strategies may mitigate reperfusion-associated neuroinflammatory injury, while multimodal approaches combining molecular, cellular, and biological interventions may yield synergistic effects when applied within optimized therapeutic windows [25, 60, 70, 85, 104].

Finally, long-term follow-up and mechanistic validation in human studies are essential to establish durable efficacy, assess late adverse effects, and define the true risk–benefit profile of immunotherapeutic strategies in ischemic stroke [25, 36, 60, 87, 88].

In particular, future strategies should incorporate selective targeting of adaptive immune components, including CD4⁺ T-cell subsets, to achieve precise modulation of neuroinflammatory responses across different stages of stroke. Such approaches may support the future development of more biologically stratified immunotherapeutic approaches tailored to the temporal and biological heterogeneity of ischemic stroke [8].

Future therapeutic strategies should prioritize immunometabolic modulation, biomarker-guided patient stratification, and cell-type-specific immune targeting as central components of stroke immunotherapy. Additional priority areas include thromboinflammatory pathways, complement-related innate signaling, systemic immune dysregulation after stroke, and peripheral immune organ responses—particularly splenic and lymphoid compartment remodeling—which may offer clinically relevant but currently underdeveloped therapeutic entry points. In particular, the PKM2–NF-κB–NLRP3 axis and coordinated regulation of reparative versus pathogenic immune populations may provide a potentially more integrative biological framework for future investigation than broadly nonspecific single-target immunosuppression approaches. Future trials should therefore align treatment timing, immune profiling, and combination with reperfusion therapies to better reflect the evolving neuroimmune phases of ischemic stroke, consistent with recent STAIR XIII recommendations [16, 83, 88, 96, 102, 103].
