An exploratory study of anxiety symptoms and associated factors among Kenyan youth recruited from colleges and communities in urban and peri-urban areas in the Nairobi Metropolitan
Authors: David M. Ndetei, Victoria Mutiso, Christine Musyimi, Eric Jeremiah, Pascalyne Nyamai, Samuel Walusaka, Veronica Onyango, Daniel Mamah, Kamaldeep Bhui
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAnxiety disorders have a significant impact on youth globally; however, data from low- and middle-income countries such as Kenya are scant. This study aimed to determine the frequency and patterns of anxiety symptoms among Kenyan youth, as well as assess the relationships between anxiety symptoms and sociodemographic, economic, and comorbid mental health factors.MethodsThis was a cross-sectional study conducted in urban and peri-urban locations in the Nairobi Metropolitan area among 1,972 participants aged 14–25 years. The study used validated instruments, including the Anxiety Symptoms Questionnaire (ASQ), Beck Depression Inventory, and WHO-ASSIST. Descriptive statistics, t-tests, ANOVA, and Pearson correlations were used for statistical analysis, and a univariate GLM was used to determine the predictors of the anxiety symptoms.ResultsAnxiety symptoms were common, with the most reported cognitive symptoms being worry and nervousness. Females and participants from urban areas had significantly higher anxiety scores (p < 0.001), with mean differences ranging from 6 to 15 points across frequency, intensity, and total scores compared to males and peri-urban participants. Anxiety scores had a negative correlation (p < 0.01) with lower wealth index and moderate positive correlations with psychosis and bipolar disorder. Depression (r = 0.51; p < 0.01), PTSD, and substance use (i.e., alcohol and cannabis) were comorbid conditions.ConclusionsThe study unearths the complexity of anxiety among Kenyan youth, with a nexus of social, economic, and psychological factors involved. These findings call for comprehensive mental health strategies focusing on reducing socioeconomic disparities and addressing comorbid conditions. Longitudinal trends and the efficacy of such interventions should be explored in future research for similar populations.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12888-026-08328-y.

Background

About 4% of the global population has anxiety, affecting approximately 301 million people, including 58 million children and adolescents [1, 2]. The prevalence of anxiety disorders is estimated to be 31% in high-income countries and 8–15% in Low and Middle-Income Countries (LMICs) [3]. In the European Union (EU), anxiety is regarded as the most prevalent mental condition, with over 60 million people affected by anxiety disorders in a given year [4]. Anxiety, anxiety symptoms, and anxiety disorders are related but different, where anxiety is the general construct, anxiety symptoms are the self-reported emotional experience, cognitive experience, or physical experience, and anxiety disorders are the clinically diagnosed syndromes based on DSM-5/ICD-10.

A population-based cross-sectional study of 1,560 youths aged 18–24 years in Brazil, an upper-middle-income country, found the prevalence of anxiety disorders to be 20.9%. The proportion of anxiety disorders was significantly higher among women and individuals with lower socioeconomic status, those whose mother or father had psychiatric disorders, and those who abused alcohol or tobacco [5]. Women are more likely to experience these disorders compared to men, which has been attributed to biological, hormonal, and social factors [6–8]. Other social demographics risk factors for increased anxiety include lower education and income levels [9], unstable economy, poverty, under-employment, and job insecurity [10, 11]. Substance use and mental health disorders like depression, post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD), and bipolar disorder have also been consistently identified as significant predictors of anxiety [12].

The lifetime prevalence of anxiety disorders ranged from 5.7% to 15.8% from 36 studies carried out in 12 African countries [13]. Social demographics such as poverty, lower education, living in rural or peri-urban areas, and rapid urbanization and economic problems like unemployment and instability have been associated with higher levels of anxiety in many African countries [14–18]. Further risk factors include violence and uprooting, such as refugees and internally displaced people, who exhibit higher rates of anxiety symptoms, mostly as a result of exposure to trauma, PTSD, unstable economies, and the disintegration of social networks [19, 20].

Co-occurrence of substance use and anxiety disorders is found with tobacco, alcohol, cannabis, and sedatives [21]. Moreover, comorbid anxiety is exacerbated by the prevalence of mental health conditions such as PTSD and depression in individuals exposed to trauma [22].

A Kenyan study found that anxiety prevalence varied by sex and age, with 12.9% of students showing anxiety on the Multidimensional Anxiety Scale for Children (MASC) and 49.3% having moderate to severe anxiety on the Ndetei-Othieno-Kathuku (NOK) scale [23]. Females, older adolescents, and minority tribes had higher anxiety rates [24]. Another study found a pooled anxiety disorder prevalence of 29% in Kenya from 17 studies, with a 95% confidence interval (CI) of 15.3% to 44%. After adjusting for publication bias and excluding outliers, the rate was revised to 25.12% (CI: 19.32%–31.40%) [25]. Poverty, older adolescents, females, never married, divorced, self-employed, lower education, and living in rural or peri-urban areas have been associated with higher levels of anxiety [26]. Other studies also reveal that high anxiety is significantly associated with younger, unemployed, and married women [27, 28]. Substances such as cannabis and amphetamines are also significant predictors of anxiety, particularly among young men [29–31].

Excessive worry, fear, uncomfortable emotional state, and associated physical symptoms are typical features of these diseases, which can seriously hinder day-to-day functioning [32]. Anxiety symptoms in young people are sometimes disregarded as a normal component of the phase shift that occurs between adolescence and early adulthood. These are crucial times for development and transition.

Previous studies have focused mainly on clinical anxiety, but not the actual symptoms of anxiety, their frequency and intensity, associated economic indicators, and co-morbidities with various types of substance abuse. This study forms part of a broader research program examining mental health outcomes among Kenyan youth, including depression, PTSD, psychosis-related symptoms, and suicidality [33]. Within this context, the use of multiple validated instruments enables comprehensive assessment of co-occurring mental health conditions and improves understanding of the overlapping nature of psychological distress in this population. This approach allows for more robust modelling of shared risk factors and comorbid symptom patterns across common mental health disorders. This study therefore aimed to fill those knowledge gaps among Kenyan youth by examining anxiety symptoms, their associated socioeconomic and clinical correlates.

Generally, this study aimed to determine the frequency and patterns of anxiety symptoms among Kenyan youth, and to examine their associations with sociodemographic, economic, and comorbid mental health factors. Specifically, the objectives were: (i) to determine the symptom levels of anxiety and anxiety symptoms in the study population, (ii) to evaluate the associations between anxiety (intensity, frequency, and totals) with social demographics, economic indicators, and mental disorders, including substance use.

Methods

Study design and setting

This was a population-based cross-sectional study in urban and peri-urban locations in the Nairobi Metropolitan area. Participants were directly recruited from the community, a middle-level college, and a medical training college located in the two designated areas.

Sampling and inclusion/exclusion criteria

This population-based study included available youths aged 14 to 25, residing within the designated neighborhoods, and willing to participate in the study. Employing this volunteer sampling strategy ensured a large exploration sample. The exclusion criteria were: individuals outside the designated age range, those incapable of comprehending the questionnaire due to factors such as intoxication or illiteracy, those unwilling to participate, those who did not consent to the study; and those under the age of 18, regarded as minors by the Kenya law whose parents/guardians did not give consent for them or the minors who did not assent to the study despite full explanation about the study. While the sample was not drawn from a national probabilistic sampling frame, recruitment across two distinct county types (urban informal settlement and peri-urban) and from both community and college settings was designed to maximize heterogeneity. Nevertheless, the volunteer sampling approach limits generalizability, a point we address in the limitations.

Procedures

Research assistant selection and training

Twelve Research Assistants were selected through a competitive hiring process designed to assess their prior experiences and interpersonal skills. Subsequently, successful candidates underwent a 2-day comprehensive in-person training. This comprehensive training not only covered data collection techniques and protocols relevant to the study but also incorporated mock interviews to simulate real-world scenarios in community engagement, and the consenting and assenting explanation and process, including the right to withdraw from these at any time without any coercion.

Recruitment and resulting sample

All data was collected in the daytime from September 21, 2022, to December 15, 2022, based on a schedule that also accommodated a crucial sensitization phase through existing local networks. We approached the County Commissioners in which the study sites were located, and upon approval, linked with chiefs and sub-chiefs who worked in collaboration with community opinion leaders to sensitize and mobilize the youths in their areas. We provided a script with details about the study to the chiefs, sub-chiefs, and the community opinion leaders, who used to not only inform the youth about the purpose, importance, and goals of the initiative but also actively engage, organize, and encourage them to be part of the study. The script also included an invitation to the study on a scheduled date, time, and venue within the study sites. This detailed community engagement ensured maximum recruitment from the available population, and those attending for the data collection were already informed about the study.

A total of 1,972 participants who participated provided written informed consent/assent, thereby affirming their voluntary participation per ethical guidelines. Adults affirmed their voluntary participation by signing informed consent and assent for those below 18 years, which is the legal age in Kenya for minors. Minors were also accompanied by a parent or a legal guardian. Verbal explanations of the study’s purpose, procedures, risks, benefits, and confidentiality were provided by research assistants, including their right to decline participation or withdraw their consent at any time without incurring any penalty. This was followed by opportunities for participants to ask questions and clarify doubts before signing the consent form.

Data collection

Data was collected on anxiety and other psychiatric disorders, including substance use, together with accompanying socio-demographics and economic indicators. These questionnaires were self-administered by participants in a group setting in social halls within the study sites. All instruments were administered in English, the official language of instruction in Kenya. Trained research assistants provided verbal clarification when needed, without altering the wording or response options. The instruments have been previously used and validated in Kenyan youth populations, supporting their appropriateness in this context [24, 34, 35]. The RAs were randomly assigned numbers (1-12), which formed a distinct group. Using restricted randomization with printed vouchers, participants were allocated evenly across groups of up to 25, solely for logistical convenience and ease of management by a research assistant. Each participant completed the survey privately, and no discussions were permitted, minimizing the risk of response bias, exactly the way it happens in examination papers in a classroom with many students. The RA was always present and observed to make sure there was no communication among students. The RAs facilitated the distribution of the questionnaires and collected written informed consent/assent upon being signed. They also cross-checked the participants’ ages to ensure compliance with the inclusion criteria. To ensure standardization, participants were instructed to respond to the questions based on their own understanding, thereby minimizing any potential influence from the research assistant.

Instruments

The anxiety symptoms questionnaire (ASQ)

The ASQ is a 17-item self-report inventory designed to measure the frequency and intensity of anxiety symptoms [36]. The intensity (how intense or bothersome the symptom has been) and the frequency of each symptom (within the past week) are rated on a 10-point scale. Intensity includes none (0), mild (1-3), moderate (4-6), severe (7-9), and extreme (10) distress. Frequency includes never (0), occasionally (1-3), often (4-6), usually (7-9), and all the time (10). When clusters of symptoms are presented in a single item (e.g., gastrointestinal symptoms of stomach upset, nausea, constipation, diarrhea, or irritable bowel), the participant is instructed to rate the intensity and severity of the most troublesome symptoms. Total ASQ scores range from 0 to 340, and intensity and frequency subtotals range from 0 to 170. Constructs assessed include anxiety, nervousness, worry, irritability, muscle tension, trouble relaxing, restlessness or being on edge, concentration and memory difficulties, anticipatory anxiety, four clusters of somatic symptoms, and functional impairment due to anxiety. The reliability test shows high internal consistency in all anxiety constructs, indicating the validity and reliability of the scale for measuring anxiety symptoms among the sample population, because it indicates that each scale’s components are well-correlated and measure the same underlying construct [37]. In the development and validation of the ASQ, strong reliability and validity were demonstrated, with high internal consistency, significant test-retest reliability, and a confirmed single-factor structure. The ASQ also showed superior discriminative ability compared to the Hamilton Anxiety Scale in identifying patients with anxiety disorders [36].

Wealth index

The wealth index is created from the socioeconomic items, i.e., the recorded household items (source of water, the type of flooring, the type of toilet, and the method used for cooking), to estimate the economic status of participants. It is based on the World Bank recommendation for LMICs. PCAs based on a demographic health survey (DHS) model are used to construct socioeconomic data on household assets, water source, floor type, toilet facilities, and primary fuel source for cooking. It is arranged according to wealth index on a 5-point Likert scale (whereby 5 indicates high wealth, and 1, low wealth). Improved or unimproved was classified to indicate higher or lower wealth levels of the assets and resources. If households had better utilities or housing materials, ownership was scored as “improved.” If they lacked these or had lower-quality versions, ownership was scored as “unimproved” [38].

The beck depression inventory (BDI)

The BDI is a 21-item self-report tool that was designed by Beck et al. in 1961 to assess depressive symptoms and their intensity [39]. It contains statements, graded on a scale of 0–3, that describe the individuals’ depression symptoms during the last week of their life. The overall score, which ranges from 0 to 63, is calculated by adding the scores of the 21 components. The final classification of the total score was as follows: A person’s mood is classified as normal from 1 to 10, somewhat disturbed from 11 to 16, clinically depressed from 17 to 20, moderately depressed from 21 to 30, severely depressed from 31 to 40, and extremely depressed from over 40. It takes about ten minutes to finish the BDI.

The BDI has been widely utilized in Africa to assess depressive symptoms, including studies in Nigeria, South Africa, and Ethiopia, among others. Its psychometric properties demonstrate high internal consistency (α > 0.80) and validity across diverse cultural settings. For example, the BDI has been used in Ethiopia to evaluate depression among HIV patients, showing reliability and cultural adaptability [40]. In Nigeria, it has assessed postpartum depression with robust results [41]. Furthermore, in South Africa, the BDI has been validated in clinical and non-clinical populations, confirming its utility [42].

World health organization -alcohol, smoking, and substance involvement screening test (WHO-ASSIST)

The WHO ASSIST was developed by the World Health Organization (WHO) and has been tested in several LMIC and recommended by the WHO as a tool that collects information on and determines the presence or absence of different types of substance use and also levels of risk from the use of tobacco products, alcohol, cannabis, amphetamine-type stimulants, cocaine, sedatives and sleeping pills, hallucinogens, opioids, and “other” drugs [43]. It is a more comprehensive tool that enquires about the different types of substance use and uses a time pattern (current and lifetime). Question 1 consists of 8 questions for lifetime use. Question 2; frequency of use in the past three months. Question 3; frequency of experiencing a strong desire or urge to use each substance in the last 3 months. Question 4; frequency of health, social, legal, or financial problems related to substance use in the last 3 months. Question 5; frequency with which use of each substance has interfered with roles or responsibilities in the past 3 months. Question 6; whether anyone has ever expressed concern about the respondent’s use of each substance, and how recently that occurred. Question 7; whether the respondent has ever tried to cut down or stop the use of a substance. Question 8; whether the respondent has ever injected a substance. For each substance, the scores for questions 2 through 7 are calculated to generate its risk. The risk is classified as: low risk (0 to 10 for alcohol, and 0 to 3 for all other substances); moderate risk (11 to 26 for alcohol, and 4 to 26 for the other substances); and high risk (27 and above). The WHO ASSIST has been used in Kenya [44] and other countries in Africa [45]. It takes approximately 15 min to complete.

The Harvard Trauma Questionnaire (HTQ) for PTSD

The Harvard Trauma Questionnaire (HTQ) was used to assess PTSD. It was developed in the early 1990s as a cross-cultural screening instrument to document trauma exposure, head trauma, and trauma-related symptoms. The HTQ is a four-part instrument that focuses on (i) experiences of torture and other traumas frequently experienced, (ii) a subjective description of the most severe traumatic event(s) experienced, (iii) events associated with head injuries, and (iv) symptoms of PTSD and expressions of functional distress. While the original tool focused on refugee segments (Indochinese refugee populations), where it exhibited strong psychometric properties, it is increasingly being used among contexts, including low-prevalence community samples [46]. It has since been translated and validated in several languages for many regions and population samples [47–50]. The first 16 items were derived from DSM-IV criteria for PTSD. The DSM-IV PTSD score is calculated from the first 16 items. The higher the scores on the DSM-IV PTSD items, the more likely it is that the respondent will have a PTSD diagnosis. Response options were “yes” and “no.” Cumulative trauma exposure scores were calculated by a count of “yes” responses to all items.

Washington early recognition center affectivity and psychosis (WERCAP) screen

The WERCAP Screen is a self-reported instrument that was developed to identify the psychosis-risk state, a pre-psychotic phase that denotes individuals presenting with potentially prodromal symptoms [51]. The screen focuses on symptoms that revolve around functional decline, sub-threshold psychotic symptoms, and associated affectivity [52]. WERCAP is a validated tool that can be rapidly deployed at the population level to identify high-risk individuals who require further assessment, as happened in this data collection exercise. The utility of a WERCAP screen with a 3-month symptom time limit mirrored the approach adopted in a past validation study in a community setting in Kenya, where the results reported an excellent reliability of 0.83 against the Structured Interview for Psychosis-Risk Syndromes (SIPS), a gold standard for diagnosing high-risk syndromes for psychosis [53–55].

The primary objective of WERCAP is to identify affective disorders and psychosis before they develop into full-blown disorders by providing a basis for surveillance, follow-up, and early intervention.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics in terms of frequencies and percentages were calculated for anxiety symptoms, while mean and standard deviation were used to summarize anxiety scores (Intensity, frequency, and total scores) to provide a comprehensive overview of the data distribution. Item-level descriptive analysis of individual anxiety symptoms was conducted a priori to identify the most frequently endorsed cognitive and somatic symptoms in this population, given the absence of prior detailed symptom-pattern data among Kenyan youth. This approach is intended to inform targeted screening and intervention development. Comparative analyses using t-tests and one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) were conducted to examine differences in anxiety scores across different social demographics. Pearson correlation coefficients assessed the associations between anxiety scores, socioeconomic indicators, mental health disorders (e.g., depression, bipolar disorder, PTSD), and substance use. Additionally, univariate GLM approach models were used to predict anxiety scores based on demographic, economic indicators, psychiatric disorders, and substance use. This approach provided a detailed understanding of the patterns and determinants of anxiety within the study population. A factor analysis using exploratory methods evaluated the grouping pattern of ASQ items through analysis of their 17 intensity symptom measures. Factor extraction was guided by Eigenvalues and interpretability, while acknowledging limitations of this approach. The statistical procedure reveals how symptoms inside the ASQ form different clustering patterns or dimensions between emotional and physical symptomatology. Cognitive symptoms include difficulties in thinking, concentrating, decision-making, and memory. Emotional symptoms refer to feelings such as sadness, irritability, anxiety, and emotional distress, while physical symptoms refer to bodily or somatic manifestations associated with anxiety and related conditions, such as fatigue, sleep disturbances, headaches, muscle tension, and changes in appetite. Principal component analysis with the Varimax rotation technique serves to simplify the grouping patterns of the items. The identified groupings help us comprehend the anxiety symptom structure in this population. All the analyses were done in Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (IBM SPSS version 25) with all the tests being two-sided, and alpha set at p < 0.05. Given the exploratory nature of this study, no adjustments were made for multiple comparisons (e.g., Bonferroni correction) to minimize the risk of Type II error and to identify all potential associations for hypothesis generation. Findings should be interpreted as exploratory, and replication in confirmatory studies is recommended. Exact p-values are reported throughout.

Ethics

The study was approved by the Nairobi Hospital Ethics Research Committee (approval no. TNH-ERC/DMSR/ERP/022/22) and was in accordance with the Helsinki Declaration of 1975, as revised in 2008. The study obtained licensing from the National Commission for Science, Technology, and Innovation (NACOSTI) license number NACOSTI/P/22/18,097. Administrative permissions were also sought from the county-level offices, as well as Institutional approval from the colleges. Written informed consent for youths aged 18 years and above was obtained before data collection commenced. For participants under 18 years of age, written consent was obtained from a parent or legal guardian, and the minors themselves also provided written assent. Importantly, if a minor declined assent, they were not enrolled, even if parental consent had been granted. The participants were also informed of the risk that some of the questions may evoke memories or trigger difficult emotions. However, they were allowed to pause, skip or withdraw from the study at any time without penalty. Additionally, school participants who experienced discomfort during or after participation were referred to their school counselor, while those in the community were attended to by trained research assistants (RAs) who provided initial psychosocial support and guidance. They were further referred to the nearest health facility for evaluation and ongoing care.

Results

We found a high internal consistency across all measures. The total anxiety scale (34 Items, Cronbach’s Alpha of 0.953), the anxiety intensity scale (17 items, Cronbach’s Alpha of 0.910), and the anxiety frequency scale (17 items, Cronbach’s Alpha of 0.919) all indicate excellent reliability. All statistical tests were two-tailed, and results are reported with corresponding effect sizes and confidence intervals where applicable.

Socio-demographics (Narrative)

The study participants were mainly from urban backgrounds (91.5%), while only 8.5% were from the peri-urban area. In terms of gender, the ratio was slightly in favor of females (55.0%) as compared to males (45.0%). The mean age was 20.49, ranging from 14 to 25 years. Most of the participants had completed their secondary education, with a smaller number of participants having university or primary education. Most of them lived with both parents, with a smaller percentage living with siblings, partners, children, and other non-relatives, in the extended family, colleagues, or others, which points to the variety of housing tenure within the sample group.

Frequency of anxiety symptoms (Patterns)

Overall, anxiety symptoms (Intensity) such as worrying, nervousness, and general anxiety were the most common, with about one-third to one-half of participants reporting these experiences. In contrast, physical symptoms such as shortness of breath, muscle tension, and numbness or tingling were less frequently reported, with a higher proportion of participants selecting “none.”

The frequency of anxiety symptoms shows that many individuals experience these issues occasionally or often. General anxiety is reported occasionally by 41.2% and often by 25.3%, while nervousness is reported occasionally by 39.9% and often by 28.6%. Worrying is experienced often by 33.3%, irritability by 25.5%, and trouble relaxing by 24.1%. Trouble falling or staying asleep is reported often by 23.8%. Physical symptoms like shortness of breath and numbness are less common, with the majority never experiencing these. Overall, 20–50% of the students did not experience anxiety at all in the various symptoms, while 9–14% experienced severe anxiety. (See Supplementary Table 1. Explanatory factor analysis of anxiety intensity measure

The Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin (KMO) value was excellent (0.952), and Bartlett’s test of sphericity was significant (p < 0.001), confirming the data’s suitability for factor analysis. Although the scree plot suggested one dominant factor, eigenvalues > 1 supported a two-factor solution. Factor 1 (Cognitive/Emotional Symptoms) included items such as anxiety, worry, and concentration issues, while Factor 2 (Somatic Symptoms) included physical complaints like shortness of breath and dizziness. The significant predictors of anxiety include possession of electricity, radio, and the type of flooring. Specifically, having electricity is consistently associated with lower levels of anxiety across intensity (B=-4.316, 95%CI=-8.475, -0.158, p = 0.042), frequency (B=-5.429, 95%CI=-10.059, -0.799, p = 0.022), and total scores (B=-9.907, 95%CI=-18.446, -1.367, p = 0.023). The radio also shows a significant negative relationship with anxiety intensity, indicating that having a radio might help reduce anxiety in that dimension. Conversely, having a wood floor is significantly associated with higher anxiety frequency, suggesting a potential link between flooring type and anxiety levels. These results should also be interpreted cautiously given the risk of Type I error arising from multiple statistical comparisons. Other predictors, such as the refrigerator and various toilet facilities, show mixed significance across models, indicating their effects on anxiety are less clear or vary depending on the dimension of anxiety measured.

Discussion

We present the first study in Kenya looking at the patterns of anxiety symptoms in terms of intensity and frequency, the association between the anxiety intensity, frequency, and totals with the social demographics, economic indicators, substance use, and psychiatric disorders, and independent predictors of the anxiety (intensity, frequency, and totals).

Patterns of anxiety symptoms

Our findings show that anxiety symptoms were highly prevalent among Kenyan youth, with about one-third to one-half reporting core symptoms at least occasionally and up to 14% experiencing severe levels. This aligns with global estimates indicating that approximately 20.5% of youth experience clinically elevated anxiety symptoms [56]. Cognitive symptoms of anxiety, such as nervousness, worrying, and general anxiety, were the most frequently reported. This finding is consistent with global studies that show cognitive symptoms tend to be more prevalent in younger populations due to the heightened emotional and psychological sensitivity during this developmental stage [57]. Physical symptoms like shortness of breath and muscle tension, on the other hand, were less commonly reported, aligning with findings from similar studies where physical symptoms of anxiety are less pronounced among youth but tend to develop as the disorder progresses or in more chronic cases [1, 32].

EFA of anxiety intensity measure

The outcomes of the factor analysis indicated that Kenyan youth anxiety symptoms show two primary groupings between cognitive/emotional and somatic factors. Cognitive/emotional and somatic dimensions of mental health must be recognized to create effective mental health intervention strategies because some individuals will present physical symptoms rather than emotional ones. It is recommended that more studies perform additional analysis on these findings to explore deeper insights and verify these results through different population samples, potentially uncovering more nuanced patterns or relationships that could inform practice or policy.

Socio-demographics and anxiety symptoms

That urban participants reported higher anxiety levels compared to those from peri-urban areas, with the difference being statistically significant (p < 0.001) is likely a reflection varying stressors that participants in urban environments particularly informal settlements experience, such as high population density, poor sanitation, inadequate clean water or toilets, noise, pollution, lack of employment, and economic pressure, which are associated with higher levels of anxiety in other studies [58, 59]. The lower anxiety scores observed in peri-urban youth may be due to the presence of stronger social cohesion and family support structures, which may buffer against anxiety despite relatively lower material resources. At the same time, peri-urban households in our sample often had more reliable access to basic utilities such as water and toilet facilities [60]. It is to be noted that the urban area was an overcrowded informal settlement with minimal infrastructure and sanitation, whereas the peri-urban area was less overcrowded and had some functional infrastructure. Similarly, gender differences were notable, with females reporting significantly higher levels of anxiety than males, consistent with global findings showing that women are generally more susceptible to anxiety disorders. This may be attributed to a combination of biological, temperamental, hormonal, and social factors [8, 61].

Our findings agree with findings in Canadian research that found higher symptom levels of anxiety among individuals in unstable marital situations, suggesting that social support and relational stability play key roles in mental health [62]. However, contrary to some studies that suggest education and wealth are strong predictors of anxiety, we found that education level had only a modest influence on anxiety levels. This may reflect the unique socio-economic context in Kenya, where factors such as family support or lack thereof might be more immediately impactful on anxiety than educational attainment alone [9, 63]. These patterns can be understood through the stress vulnerability model, which emphasizes how individual vulnerabilities, such as female gender or developmental stage, interact with environmental stressors to influence the development of mental health symptoms [64, 65].

Associations between socio-economic indicators and anxiety

Our findings indicate a negative correlation between access to basic resources like electricity, radio, television, and piped water, and anxiety levels. This suggests that access to these resources can provide a sense of stability and reduce uncertainty, potentially alleviating anxiety. These findings align with research from other regions, where access to modern amenities has been shown to buffer the impact of environmental stressors on mental health [66]. In contrast to this, poor housing conditions, such as living in homes with wood flooring or lacking basic sanitation facilities, were positively correlated with higher anxiety scores. This aligns with a Nigerian study showing that inadequate living conditions can serve as stressors that exacerbate mental health issues, particularly anxiety [67]. The results underscore the importance of addressing environmental factors in interventions aimed at reducing anxiety, particularly in low-resource settings. Our findings also align with the social determinants of health framework, suggesting that access to structural resources such as water, electricity, and safe housing can serve as protective factors that buffer against psychological distress [68]. Material deprivation (example, lack of electricity, radio, or other household assets) emerged as a key correlate of anxiety, highlighting the need for integrated mental health and socio-economic interventions that address both psychological and structural drivers of distress. Correlation Between Anxiety, Depression, PTSD, Bipolar, Psychosis, and Substance Use.

A key finding from our study was the strong correlation between anxiety and substance use, particularly with alcohol, sedatives, and cannabis. This aligns with extensive literature suggesting that substance use disorders are frequently comorbid with anxiety disorders, creating a cycle of mutual reinforcement with individuals who use substances to cope with anxiety, though the substance use itself may exacerbate the anxiety over time [69, 70]. In addition, depression, PTSD, and bipolar disorders were also found to correlate with anxiety, thereby indicating that anxiety and other mental health disorders may in fact be interrelated. This finding aligns with other studies, which indicate that anxiety is usually accompanied by other psychiatric disorders, which makes diagnosis and treatment even more challenging [69].

Correlation between anxiety and psychosis and bipolar prodromal symptoms. Our findings support meta-analysis findings that schizophrenia and bipolar disorder exhibit comorbidity with anxiety disorders [71, 72]. However, our study did not involve youth with clinical diagnoses of schizophrenia and bipolar disorder, but rather non-clinical level symptoms. Thus, these symptoms are comorbid, and there is a possibility that anxiety symptoms may be early symptoms of schizophrenia and bipolar disorder.

Predictors of anxiety symptoms

We identified various predictors of anxiety symptoms, including gender, age, and marital status. In line with previous studies, females show significantly higher anxiety scores than males, which corresponds with the higher rate of anxiety in females [7]. Age was positively correlated with anxiety frequency and total scores, which may reflect the developmental challenges faced by older adolescents transitioning into adulthood. In contrast, variables such as wealth index, religion, and employment status had less impact on anxiety scores in this sample, which suggests that these factors might be less significant predictors in youth populations or that their effects may be masked by other more immediate stressors [73, 74].

Economic factors such as access to electricity, type of flooring, and having a radio were found to be protective against anxiety, highlighting the role of economic security and the availability of social support through media. These findings are similar to the studies that indicated people who live in more privileged environments have lower anxiety, resulting from the availability of few stressors and enhanced ways of handling them [74]. The lack of significant findings related to employment status and wealth index in this study may suggest that these factors do not exert as direct an influence on anxiety in the young urban population we studied.

All of these findings provide evidence for a broad-based approach to potential intervention points for youth with anxiety and anxiety symptoms.

Beyond theoretical perspectives, the findings have direct implications for service delivery in Kenya. Routine screening for anxiety symptoms in schools, colleges, and primary healthcare settings could facilitate early identification and support. Integrating digital tools into primary care and training community health workers to deliver brief, evidence-based interventions represents a feasible strategy to address the large treatment gap. Furthermore, tackling structural determinants such as poverty and inadequate housing through intersectoral policies is essential in reducing the burden of anxiety symptoms among young people.

Limitations

Participation was voluntary, which may introduce selection bias; however, this is inherent to ethically conducted population-based studies. Participants were recruited through colleges and community settings using non-probability sampling, which may limit representativeness of the broader Kenyan youth population. Although not drawn from a national sampling frame, the two counties reflected socioeconomic and demographic characteristics of Kenyan youth, and the colleges enrolled students from across the country, providing a diverse sample. Even so, generalization to all Kenyan youth should be made with caution.

Our cross-sectional study design cannot establish causality. Future research should explore these relationships longitudinally, incorporating clinical diagnostic tools, and examine intervention strategies that could mitigate the impact of anxiety in young populations. In addition, the study relied on self-report questionnaires, which may be subject to reporting bias, and did not include structured diagnostic interviews, as the focus was on assessing symptom patterns and associated factors rather than establishing a formal psychiatric diagnosis. Additionally, the exploratory factor analysis identified some items with cross-loadings across factors, indicating that the factor structure should be interpreted with caution. Moreover, estimates derived from this sample should not be interpreted as population-level prevalence but rather as symptom burden within the study population.

The investigation excluded several factors that may have an impact on anxiety, such as genetic predisposition, early childhood hardship, and access to mental health care.

Implications for policy and practice

Four specific implications emerge from our findings. First, the independent association between basic material resources (electricity, wood flooring) and anxiety scores suggests that mental health strategies should include intersectoral collaboration with housing, energy, and sanitation sectors, not solely health services. Second, the predominance of cognitive symptoms (worry, nervousness) over somatic symptoms indicates that school and community screening should prioritize cognitive items, as physical symptoms alone would under-identify affected youth. Third, the consistent correlations with alcohol, cannabis, and sedatives support integrated screening for substance use within any anxiety detection program. Fourth, the two-factor structure (cognitive/emotional vs. somatic) suggests that brief interventions can be tailored: cognitive reframing for the first group and relaxation/breathing techniques for the second. Addressing material deprivation, particularly access to electricity and improved flooring, should be evaluated as a structural mental health intervention in future research.

Conclusion

Therefore, our study has provided an important insight into the burden, patterns, and predictors of anxiety symptoms in Kenyan youth, showing high levels of anxiety symptoms, with substantial proportions reporting symptoms at least occasionally and a notable minority experiencing severe levels. Our findings highlight the multifaceted nature of anxiety, with socio-demographic factors, economic indicators, substance use, and comorbid mental health conditions all playing significant roles. The results underscore the need for comprehensive mental health strategies that address both the social and psychological drivers of anxiety. Specifically, three practice and policy recommendations emerge: (i) implement school and college-based screening for anxiety symptoms using validated tools such as the ASQ, (ii) integrate anxiety symptom detection into primary healthcare settings, including the use of digital platforms, and (iii) train community health workers to deliver brief, evidence-based anxiety interventions. Addressing structural determinants such as poverty and access to basic amenities should also be prioritized through intersectoral collaboration. Together, these efforts may contribute to reducing the burden of anxiety among youth in Kenya.
