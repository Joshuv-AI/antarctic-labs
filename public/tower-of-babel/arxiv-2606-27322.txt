Weighted Fruit Diophantine Equations and Hyperelliptic Curves
Jewel Mahajan, Apeksha Sanghi

Abstract
. We study the weighted fruit Diophantine equation a ​ x d − c ⁡ ( m 2 ​ y 2 + n 2 ​ z 2 ) + x ​ y ​ z − b = 0 ax^{d}-c\bigl(m^{2}y^{2}+n^{2}z^{2}\bigr)+xyz-b=0 , generalising the results established in [ 10 , 20 , 13 ] . Subject to specific hypotheses on the parameters, our main result shows that for any prime l ≡ 3 ( mod 4 ) l\equiv 3\pmod{4} and b = a ​ ( 2 ​ c ​ m ​ n ) d − l ​ c s ​ t 2 ​ q b=a(2cmn)^{d}-l\,c^{s}t^{2q} , the above equation has no integer solutions except for certain residue classes of x x modulo 4 ​ l 4l . 1.
Introduction

Diophantine equations have a remarkably rich literature in number theory. A significant part of this research focuses on determining whether a given Diophantine equation admits integer solutions. This area of study also shares deep connections with algebraic geometry, particularly concerning integral points on elliptic (resp., hyperelliptic) curves via the Nagell–Lutz theorem (resp., Grant’s theorem).

Consider the Diophantine equation

(1)

y^{2}-xyz+z^{2}=x^{3}-5.

Majumdar and Sury
[
10
]
proved that (
1
) has no integer solution. They also showed that the associated elliptic curve
y^{2}-mxy=x^{3}-(m^{2}+5)
has no integral point for any integer
m
. Equations of a similar form,
ax^{3}+by+c-xyz=0
, were previously investigated by Luca and Togbé
[
8
,
19
]
, Subburam and Thangadurai
[
16
,
18
]
, and Subburam and Togbé
[
17
]
.

In an effort to generalise existing results in the literature, we consider the Diophantine equation

(2)

ax^{d}-c\bigl(m^{2}y^{2}+n^{2}z^{2}\bigr)+xyz-b=0.

Extending the work of Majumdar and Sury, who studied (
2
) for the specific case where
a=c=m=n=1
,
b=5
, and
d=3
, Vaishya and Sharma
[
20
]
proved the insolvability of (
2
) when
a\equiv 1\pmod{12}
,
c=m=n=1
,
b=8a-3
, and
d=3
. This yielded an infinite family of elliptic curves with torsion-free Mordell–Weil group over
\mathbb{Q}
(see
[
20
, Corollary 1.4]
).

Along the same line, further extending the work of Vaishya and Sharma, Prakash and Chakraborty
[
13
]
established the insolvability of (
2
) for the case when
a\equiv 1\pmod{12}
,
c=m=n=1
,
b=a2^{d}-3
, and
d
is an odd multiple of
3
. By applying Grant’s hyperelliptic analogue of the Nagell–Lutz theorem
[
6
]
, they obtained infinitely many hyperelliptic curves with torsion-free Mordell–Weil groups. This is consistent with Faltings’ finiteness theorem
[
5
]
and Bhargava’s result
[
1
]
that most hyperelliptic curves over
\mathbb{Q}
have no rational points. We remark that
[
13
, Theorem 3.3]
and
[
20
, Theorem 1.2]
are valid only for
a=1
, genus
g=1
, and when
m
specified therein is even (see Remark
8.6
).

This article aims to investigate the insolvability of (
2
) under some hypotheses on its parameters. The remainder of this article is organised as follows. In Section
2
, we define the sets
\mathcal{N}_{1}(l)
and
\mathcal{N}_{2}(l)
(see Definition
2.1
) and establish several propositions and lemmas for later use. In Section
3
, assuming the parameters
a,c,d,m,n,q,s,t
satisfy the hypotheses of Theorem
3.1
, we establish the insolvability of the generalised Diophantine equation (
2
) for a prime
l\equiv 3\pmod{4}
and parameter
b=a(2cmn)^{d}-lc^{s}t^{2q}
. Our proof generalises methods from previous work by analysing the residue classes of
x
modulo
4l
, with the careful observation made in Theorem
3.1
(d)
. A significant portion of the proof of Theorem
3.1
remains valid even if
l
is replaced by an odd power of
l
in the definition of
b
. This yields a similar insolvability result, with the only exception that integer solutions
(x,y,z)
with
x\equiv 2cmn\pmod{l}
cannot be ruled out (see Corollary
3.3
). Subsequently, we apply Theorem
3.1
to some small values of
l
in Section
4
, such as
l\in\{3,7,11,19\}
, and explicitly determine the exceptional residue classes outside of which (
2
) has no solutions. In particular, for
l=3
, this yields complete insolvability (see Theorem
4.3
), while for the small primes
l=7,11
, and
19
, we list the set of residues modulo
28,44
, and
76
, respectively, and exhibit solutions in the exceptional classes, showing that Theorems
4.6
,
4.8
and
4.10
are sharp. Similar ideas also yield some insolvability results for
l=-1
.

In subsequent sections, we investigate (
2
) under various hypotheses, establishing a connection between an associated family of hyperelliptic curves and the insolvability of (
2
). In Section
5
, we explore a variant of Theorem
4.3
under weakened hypotheses. We observe that non-existence results continue to hold, though with specific coprimality restrictions on any potential solutions (see Theorem
5.1
). Section
6
considers a more general variant of (
2
), where the coefficients of
y^{2}
and
z^{2}
are independent, specifically allowing them to be squarefree, and we also provide some insolvability results depending on the residue of
a
modulo
4
. In Section
7
, we focus on positive solutions to (
2
). In particular, under the hypothesis on the parameters of (
2
), we show that there are only finitely many positive integer solutions
(x,y,z)
satisfying
x\leq 2cmn
. Finally, in Section
8
, we associate a family of hyperelliptic curves with (
2
) and analyse their rational torsion points. As a consequence, Theorem
4.3
yields a family of elliptic curves with torsion-free Mordell–Weil groups (see Theorem
8.5
). We believe that this framework of connecting rational torsion points to the solution of certain Diophantine equations could possibly be extended to superelliptic curves, assuming an analogue to the Grant or Nagell-Lutz theorem exists in that setting. We leave this direction for future work.

2.
Preliminaries

Throughout this article,
\bigl(\frac{\cdot}{\cdot}\bigr)
denotes the Jacobi symbol. Note that this reduces to the Legendre symbol when the denominator is an odd prime
p
. This section establishes the preliminary definitions, lemmas, and propositions necessary for subsequent sections.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
l
be a positive integer. We define the sets
\mathcal{N}_{1}(l)
and
\mathcal{N}_{2}(l)
by

\mathcal{N}_{1}(l)=\bigg\{x\in\mathbb{Z}\;\bigg|\;1\leq x<4l,\,\gcd(x,4l)=1,\,\left(\frac{l}{x}\right)=1\bigg\},

\mathcal{N}_{2}(l)=\bigg\{x\in\mathbb{Z}\;\bigg|\;1\leq x<4l,\,\gcd(x,4l)=1,\,\left(\frac{l}{x}\right)=-1\bigg\}.

Remark 2.2
.

Following the convention that an empty product evaluates to
1
, we always have
1\in\mathcal{N}_{1}(l)
.

Proposition 2.3
.

Let
l
be a prime such that
l\equiv 3\pmod{4}
. For any odd prime
p\neq l
, the Legendre symbol
\left(\frac{l}{p}\right)
is determined by the congruence class of
p
modulo
4l
. Specifically,
\left(\frac{l}{p}\right)=1
if and only if
p
satisfies one of the following conditions:

(a)

p\equiv 1\pmod{4}
and
p\equiv a\pmod{l}
, where
a
is a quadratic residue modulo
l
;

(b)

p\equiv 3\pmod{4}
and
p\equiv b\pmod{l}
, where
b
is a quadratic nonresidue modulo
l
.

By the Chinese Remainder Theorem (CRT), there are exactly
l-1
residue classes modulo
4l
for which
\left(\frac{l}{p}\right)=1
, and
\left(\frac{l}{p}\right)=-1
for the remaining
l-1
residue classes.

Proof.

By the law of quadratic reciprocity (see
[
2
, Theorem 9.9]
), for an odd prime
p\neq l
with
l\equiv 3\pmod{4}
, we have

\left(\frac{l}{p}\right)=\left(\frac{p}{l}\right)(-1)^{\frac{l-1}{2}\cdot\frac{p-1}{2}}=\left(\frac{p}{l}\right)(-1)^{\frac{p-1}{2}}.

We analyse the following cases for
p
modulo
4
to determine the sign of the second factor.

(a)

If
p\equiv 1\pmod{4}
, then
(-1)^{\frac{p-1}{2}}=1
. Thus,
\left(\frac{l}{p}\right)=\left(\frac{p}{l}\right)
. For this to equal
1
,
p
must be a quadratic residue modulo
l
.

(b)

If
p\equiv 3\pmod{4}
, then
(-1)^{\frac{p-1}{2}}=-1
. Thus,
\left(\frac{l}{p}\right)=-\left(\frac{p}{l}\right)
. For this to equal
1
,
p
must be a quadratic nonresidue modulo
l
.

Since
\gcd(4,l)=1
, the CRT guarantees that each possible combination mentioned above produces a unique residue class modulo
4l
. These computations yield the exact residue classes for which
\left(\frac{l}{p}\right)=1
, and the remaining residue classes yield
\left(\frac{l}{p}\right)=-1
.
∎

Using Proposition
2.3
, we now determine the residue classes
p
modulo
4l
for which
\left(\frac{l}{p}\right)=\pm 1
for each
l\in\{3,7,11,19\}
.

Proposition 2.4
.

For an odd prime
p\neq 3
, we have

\left(\frac{3}{p}\right)=\begin{cases}1&\text{if }p\equiv 1,11\pmod{12},\\
-1&\text{if }p\equiv 5,7\pmod{12}.\end{cases}

Proof.

Applying Proposition
2.3
with
l=3
, we have
\left(\frac{3}{p}\right)=1
if either
p\equiv 1\pmod{4}
with
p\equiv 1\pmod{3}
, or
p\equiv 3\pmod{4}
with
p\equiv 2\pmod{3}
. Solving these simultaneous congruences via the CRT yields
p\equiv 1,11\pmod{12}
, with the remaining residue classes yielding
-1
.
∎

Proposition 2.5
.

For an odd prime
p\neq 7
, we have

\left(\frac{7}{p}\right)=\begin{cases}1&\text{if }p\equiv 1,3,9,19,25,27\pmod{28},\\
-1&\text{if }p\equiv 5,11,13,15,17,23\pmod{28}.\end{cases}

Proof.

Applying Proposition
2.3
with
l=7
, we have
\left(\frac{7}{p}\right)=1
if either
p\equiv 1\pmod{4}
with
p\equiv 1,2,4\pmod{7}
, or
p\equiv 3\pmod{4}
with
p\equiv 3,5,6\pmod{7}
. Solving these simultaneous congruences via the CRT yields
p\equiv 1,3,9,19,25,27\pmod{28}
, with the remaining residue classes yielding
-1
.
∎

Proposition 2.6
.

For an odd prime
p\neq 11
, we have

\left(\frac{11}{p}\right)=\begin{cases}1&\text{if }p\equiv 1,5,7,9,19,25,35,37,39,43\pmod{44},\\
-1&\text{if }p\equiv 3,13,15,17,21,23,27,29,31,41\pmod{44}.\end{cases}

Proof.

Applying Proposition
2.3
with

\left(\frac{11}{p}\right)=1
if either
p\equiv 1\pmod{4}
with
p\equiv 1,3,4,5,9\pmod{11}
, or

-1

∎

Proposition 2.7
.

For an odd prime
p\neq 19
, we have

\left(\frac{19}{p}\right)=\begin{cases}1&\text{if }p\equiv 1,3,5,9,15,17,25,27,31,\\

∎

Lemma 2.9
.

Let
l
and
d
be positive integers such that
l\mid d
. If
x\equiv A\pmod{l}
for some integer
A
, then
\sum_{i=0}^{d-1}x^{d-1-i}A^{i}\equiv 0\pmod{l}
.

Proof.

Since
l\mid d
, we have

(3)

\sum_{i=0}^{d-1}x^{d-1-i}A^{i}\equiv\sum_{i=0}^{d-1}A^{d-1-i}A^{i}\equiv\sum_{i=0}^{d-1}A^{d-1}\equiv dA^{d-1}\equiv 0\pmod{l}.

∎

Lemma 2.10
.

Let
l
be a prime, and let
t\in\mathbb{Z}
be such that every prime factor of
t
is congruent to
r
for some
r\in\mathcal{N}_{1}(l)
(see Definition
2.1
). Then
t
is odd and
l\nmid t
.

Proof.

Let
p
be a prime factor of
t
. By assumption,
p\equiv r\pmod{4l}
. Since
r\in\mathcal{N}_{1}(l)
is odd,
p
must also be odd. Because every prime factor of
t
is odd, it follows that
t
is odd.

Also, since
r\in\mathcal{N}_{1}(l)
, we have
\gcd(r,4l)=1
, which means that
l\nmid r
. Consequently,
l\nmid p
(i.e.,
p\neq l
). Because
l
is a prime and it is different from all the prime factors of
t
, it follows that
l\nmid t
.
∎

Definition 2.11
.

A polynomial
h(x)\in\mathbb{Z}[x]
is squarefree if it is not
divisible by
g(x)^{2}
for any non-constant polynomial
g(x)
. Equivalently,
h(x)
is squarefree if it has no repeated roots in
\overline{\mathbb{Q}}
, the algebraic closure of
\mathbb{Q}
.

Let
h(x)\in\mathbb{Z}[x]
be monic of degree
n\geq 1
, and let

\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{n}\in\mathbb{C}
denote its roots, repeated as many times according to multiplicity, so that
h(x)=\prod_{i=1}^{n}(x-\alpha_{i})\in\mathbb{C}[x]
. The discriminant of
h
is defined as

\disc(h)\;=\;\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq n}(\alpha_{i}-\alpha_{j})^{2}.

Remark 2.12
.

The product above is equal to
P(e_{1},\ldots,e_{n})
for some
P\in\mathbb{Z}[X_{1},\dots,X_{n}]
, where
e_{1},\dots,e_{n}
are the elementary symmetric polynomials in
\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{n}
. By Vieta’s formulas, each

e_{k}=e_{k}(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{n})
equals a
coefficient of
h
(up to sign), and is therefore an integer. Consequently,
\disc(h)\in\mathbb{Z}
. Furthermore,
h
is squarefree if and only if
\disc(h)\neq 0
.

Lemma 2.13

(
[
7
, Theorem 4]
)
.

For an integer
d>2
, the discriminant of the trinomial
f(X)=X^{d}+\lambda X^{2}+\mu
is given by

(4)

\disc(f)=(-1)^{d(d-1)/2}\mu\left(d^{N}\mu^{N-K}-(-1)^{N}(d-2)^{N-K}2^{K}\lambda^{N}\right)^{\tilde{g}},

where
\tilde{g}=\gcd(d,2)
,
N=d/\tilde{g}
, and
K=2/\tilde{g}
.

In particular, when
d>2
is odd, the discriminant simplifies to

\disc(f)=(-1)^{d(d-1)/2}\mu\big(d^{d}\mu^{d-2}+4(d-2)^{d-2}\lambda^{d}\big).

3.
Insolvability for primes
l\equiv 3\pmod{4}

We now prove one of the main results of this article, the key insolvability result for (
2
) under some assumptions on the parameters
a,c,d,m,n,t,q,s
and
b
.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
l
be a prime such that
l\equiv 3\pmod{4}
. Let
a,c,m,n,t\in\mathbb{Z}
and
d,q,s\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
be such that
d,m,n
, and
s
are odd integers with
l\mid d
. Assume
a\equiv c\equiv 1\pmod{4l}
and
mn\equiv 1\pmod{l}
, and suppose that every prime factor of
cmt
is congruent to
r
modulo
4l
for some
r\in\mathcal{N}_{1}(l)
. Set
b=a(2cmn)^{d}-lc^{s}t^{2q}.
Then (
2
) has no integer solutions
(x,y,z)
for which
x
satisfies any of the following conditions:

(a)

x
is even;

(b)

x\equiv 3\pmod{4}
;

(c)

x\equiv\pm 2cmn\pmod{l}
;

(d)

x-2cmn\equiv v\pmod{4l}
for some
v\in\mathcal{N}_{2}(l)
.

Proof.

Since
d\geq 3
,
c\equiv 1\pmod{4}
, and
t
is odd (by Lemma
2.10
), it follows that
2^{d}\equiv 0\pmod{4}
and
c^{s}\equiv t^{2q}\equiv 1\pmod{4}
. Therefore,

(5)

b\equiv-lc^{s}t^{2q}\equiv-l\equiv-3\equiv 1\pmod{4}.

We divide our analysis into two cases based on the parity of
x
.

Case 1
:
x
is even.
Let
x=2\beta
. Multiplying (
2
) by
cm^{2}
and completing the square via the substitution
U\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=cm^{2}y-\beta z
yields

(6)

U^{2}-(\beta^{2}-c^{2}m^{2}n^{2})z^{2}=cm^{2}\bigl(a(2\beta)^{d}-b\bigr).

Using the definition of
b
and the fact that
4\mid 2^{d}
, the right-hand side of (
6
) becomes

cm^{2}\bigl(a(2\beta)^{d}-b\bigr)=acm^{2}\bigl((2\beta)^{d}-(2cmn)^{d}\bigr)+lc^{s+1}m^{2}t^{2q}\equiv lc^{s+1}m^{2}t^{2q}\pmod{4}.

Because
m
and
n
are odd,
m^{2}\equiv n^{2}\equiv c^{2}m^{2}n^{2}\equiv 1\pmod{4}
. Since
c\equiv t^{2}\equiv 1\pmod{4}
, the right-hand side of (
6
) is congruent to
3\pmod{4}
.

•

If
\beta
is even, then
\beta^{2}-c^{2}m^{2}n^{2}\equiv-1\pmod{4}
. Thus (
6
) reduces to
U^{2}+z^{2}\equiv 3\pmod{4}
, which is impossible, since a sum of two squares must be congruent to
0,1
, or
2\pmod{4}
.

•

If
\beta
is odd, then
\beta^{2}-c^{2}m^{2}n^{2}\equiv 0\pmod{4}
. Thus (
6
) reduces to
U^{2}\equiv 3\pmod{4}
, which is also impossible, since
3
is not a quadratic residue modulo
4
.

This proves
(a)
.

Case 2
:
x
is odd.
Reducing (
2
) modulo
2
, noting that
a,c,m,n,x
, and
b
are all odd, gives

y^{2}+z^{2}-yz\equiv a-b\equiv 0\pmod{2}.

This forces
y\equiv z\equiv 0\pmod{2}
. Write
y=2Y
and
z=2Z
. Multiplying (
2
) by
cm^{2}
and completing the square via the substitution
w\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=2cm^{2}Y-xZ
yields

(7)

w^{2}-(x^{2}-4c^{2}m^{2}n^{2})Z^{2}=acm^{2}\bigl(x^{d}-(2cmn)^{d}\bigr)+lc^{s+1}m^{2}t^{2q}.

The left-hand side of (
7
) expands to
4cm^{2}(cm^{2}Y^{2}-xYZ+cn^{2}Z^{2})
, which is divisible by
4
. Using
(2cmn)^{d}\equiv 0\pmod{4}
,
x^{d}\equiv x\pmod{4}
(since
d
is odd), and
acm^{2}\equiv c^{s+1}m^{2}t^{2q}\equiv 1\pmod{4}
, the reduction of (
7
) modulo
4
yields

(8)

\displaystyle 0\equiv x+3\pmod{4}\implies x\equiv 1\pmod{4}.

This immediately shows that (
2
) admits no integer solutions when
x\equiv 3\pmod{4}
, thereby proving
(b)
. For the remaining conditions, we assume
x\equiv 1\pmod{4}
and analyse (
7
) in the following three subcases.

Subcase 2.1:
x\equiv 2cmn\pmod{l}
.
Assume, for the sake of contradiction, that (
2
) admits an integer solution
(x,y,z)
with
x\equiv 2cmn\pmod{l}
.

Therefore, we can write
x-2cmn\equiv el\pmod{4l}
for some
e\in\{-1,0,1,2\}
. Since
x-2cmn
and
l
are odd, we have
e\in\{-1,1\}
. Thus, there exists an integer
\delta^{\prime}
such that
x=2cmn+el+4l\delta^{\prime}=2cmn+l(e+4\delta^{\prime})
. Setting
\delta=e+4\delta^{\prime}
, we have
x=2cmn+l\delta
, where
\delta\equiv e\pmod{4}
.

If
e=-1
, then
\delta\equiv-1\pmod{4}
. This implies
x=2cmn+l(-1+4\delta^{\prime})
. Since
c\equiv 1\pmod{4}
and
mn
is odd, we have
2cmn\equiv 2\pmod{4}
. This yields
x\equiv 2-l\equiv-1\equiv 3\pmod{4}
, contradicting (
8
).

Therefore, we must have
e=1
, which means that
\delta\equiv 1\pmod{4}
and
x-2cmn=l\delta
. Evaluating (
7
) modulo
l
, we have
w^{2}\equiv 0\pmod{l}
. Therefore,
w=lw_{1}
for some
w_{1}\in\mathbb{Z}
. Substituting
w=lw_{1}
and
x=2cmn+l\delta
back into (
7
) yields

(9)

l^{2}w_{1}^{2}-l\delta(x+2cmn)Z^{2}=cm^{2}\big(a(x^{d}-(2cmn)^{d})+lc^{s}t^{2q}\big).

By the binomial expansion,
x^{d}-(2cmn)^{d}=(x-2cmn)S=l\delta S
, where
S=\displaystyle\sum_{i=0}^{d-1}x^{d-1-i}(2cmn)^{i}
. Dividing (
9
) by
l
gives

(10)

lw_{1}^{2}-\delta(x+2cmn)Z^{2}=m^{2}(ac\delta S+c^{s+1}t^{2q}).

As
l\mid d
, Lemma
2.9
yields
S\equiv 0\pmod{l}
. Recall that
a\equiv c\equiv mn\equiv 1\pmod{l}
, and
x\equiv 2cmn\pmod{l}
. Reducing (
10
) modulo
l
yields

(11)

\displaystyle-\delta(4cmn)Z^{2}\equiv m^{2}c^{s+1}t^{2q}\pmod{l}

(12)

\displaystyle\implies

\displaystyle-4\delta Z^{2}\equiv t^{2q}m^{2}\pmod{l}

(13)

\displaystyle\implies

\displaystyle-4\delta n^{2}Z^{2}\equiv t^{2q}m^{2}n^{2}\equiv t^{2q}\pmod{l}.

Observe that
l\nmid 2\delta nZ
; otherwise, we would have
l\mid t^{2q}
, which contradicts Lemma
2.10
. Consequently,
\left(\frac{\delta}{l}\right)
,
\left(\frac{n}{l}\right)
and
\left(\frac{2Z}{l}\right)
are nonzero. Since
t\neq 0
(by Lemma
2.10
) and
l\equiv 3\pmod{4}
, we have

1=\left(\frac{t^{2q}}{l}\right)=\left(\frac{-4\delta n^{2}Z^{2}}{l}\right)=\left(\frac{-1}{l}\right)\left(\frac{\delta}{l}\right)\left(\frac{n}{l}\right)^{2}\left(\frac{2Z}{l}\right)^{2}=-\left(\frac{\delta}{l}\right).

Therefore,
\left(\frac{\delta}{l}\right)=-1
. We can write
\delta=\text{sgn}(\delta)|\delta|
, where
\text{sgn}(\cdot)
denotes the signum function. Since
l\equiv 3\pmod{4}
, we have
\left(\frac{\text{sgn}(\delta)}{l}\right)=\text{sgn}(\delta)
. Using the multiplicative property of the Jacobi symbol, this gives

(14)

\displaystyle-1\;=\;\left(\frac{\delta}{l}\right)\;=\;\left(\frac{\text{sgn}(\delta)}{l}\right)\left(\frac{|\delta|}{l}\right)\;=\;\text{sgn}(\delta)\left(\frac{|\delta|}{l}\right).

Furthermore, the condition
\delta\equiv 1\pmod{4}
implies that
|\delta|\equiv\text{sgn}(\delta)\pmod{4}
. We now apply the generalised law of quadratic reciprocity (see
[
2
, p. 192]
) to the positive odd integers
l
and
|\delta|
. Since
l\equiv 3\pmod{4}
, the reciprocity factor simplifies to
(-1)^{\frac{l-1}{2}\frac{|\delta|-1}{2}}=(-1)^{\frac{|\delta|-1}{2}}=\text{sgn}(\delta).

Therefore, by (
14
), we have

\left(\frac{l}{|\delta|}\right)\;=\;(-1)^{\frac{l-1}{2}\frac{|\delta|-1}{2}}\left(\frac{|\delta|}{l}\right)\;=\;\text{sgn}(\delta)\left(\frac{|\delta|}{l}\right)\;=\;-1.

Let
\gamma\in[1,4l]\cap\mathbb{Z}
satisfy
|\delta|\equiv\gamma\pmod{4l}
. Since
l\nmid|\delta|
and
|\delta|
is odd, we have
\gcd(|\delta|,4l)=1
. Combined with
\left(\frac{l}{|\delta|}\right)=-1
, Lemma
2.8
guarantees
\gamma\in\mathcal{N}_{2}(l)
. Since the Jacobi symbol is multiplicative and
\mathcal{N}_{1}(l)
is
closed under multiplication modulo
4l
, not every prime factor of

|\delta|
can be congruent modulo
4l
to an element of

\mathcal{N}_{1}(l)
. Thus,
|\delta|
must possess an odd prime divisor
p
such that
p\equiv u\pmod{4l}
for some
u\in\mathcal{N}_{2}(l)
. Therefore,
\left(\frac{l}{p}\right)=\left(\frac{l}{u}\right)=-1
, by Lemma
2.8
. Finally, since
p\mid|\delta|
and
l\nmid|\delta|
, it follows that
\gcd(p,l)=1
.

Since
p\mid l\delta=(x-2cmn)
, evaluating (
7
) modulo
p
yields

\displaystyle w^{2}

\displaystyle\equiv w^{2}-(x^{2}-4c^{2}m^{2}n^{2})Z^{2}\pmod{p}

\displaystyle\equiv acm^{2}\bigl(x^{d}-(2cmn)^{d}\bigr)+lc^{s+1}m^{2}t^{2q}\pmod{p}

\displaystyle\equiv lc^{s+1}m^{2}t^{2q}\pmod{p}.

By hypothesis, every prime factor
\mu
of
cmt
is such that
\mu\equiv r\pmod{4l}
, where
r\in\mathcal{N}_{1}(l)
, which means that
\left(\frac{l}{\mu}\right)=\left(\frac{l}{r}\right)=1
. Since
\left(\frac{l}{p}\right)=-1
, we have
p\nmid cmt
. Defining
T\coloneqq c^{(s+1)/2}mt^{q}
, we see that
(wT^{-1})^{2}\equiv l\pmod{p}
. Since
p\nmid l
, this forces
\left(\frac{l}{p}\right)=1
, a contradiction.

Subcase 2.2:
x\equiv-2cmn\pmod{l}
.

Since
d
is odd, we have
x^{d}-(2cmn)^{d}\equiv(-2cmn)^{d}-(2cmn)^{d}\equiv-2(2cmn)^{d}\pmod{l}
.

Reducing the right-hand side of (
7
) modulo
l
yields

acm^{2}\bigl(x^{d}-(2cmn)^{d}\bigr)+lc^{s+1}m^{2}t^{2q}\equiv-2acm^{2}(2cmn)^{d}\pmod{l}.

Since
a\equiv c\equiv mn\equiv 1\pmod{l}
, (
7
) simplifies to
w^{2}\equiv-2^{d+1}m^{2}\pmod{l}
, that is,

(wn)^{2}\equiv-2^{d+1}(mn)^{2}\equiv-2^{d+1}\pmod{l}.

Since
d
and
l
are odd positive integers, we have
R\coloneqq 2^{\frac{d+1}{2}}\not\equiv 0\pmod{l}
. Therefore,
(wn)^{2}\equiv-R^{2}\pmod{l}
, and hence
(wnR^{-1})^{2}\equiv-1\pmod{l}
. This forces
\left(\frac{-1}{l}\right)=1
, a contradiction.

Subcase 2.3:
x\equiv 2cmn+v\pmod{4l}
for some
v\in\mathcal{N}_{2}(l)
.

Assume (
2
) admits an integer solution
(x,y,z)
, where
M\coloneqq x-2cmn\equiv v\pmod{4l}
for some
v\in\mathcal{N}_{2}(l)
. We evaluate the Jacobi symbol for the positive odd integer
|M|
.

If
M>0
, then
|M|\equiv v\pmod{4l}
, and Lemma
2.8
immediately yields
\left(\frac{l}{|M|}\right)=\left(\frac{l}{v}\right)=-1
.

If
M<0
, then
|M|\equiv-v\pmod{4l}
. Because
l\equiv 3\pmod{4}
, the Legendre symbol evaluates to
\left(\frac{|M|}{l}\right)=\left(\frac{-v}{l}\right)=-\left(\frac{v}{l}\right)
. Note that
|M|
and
v
are both odd, and the condition
|M|\equiv-v\pmod{4}
implies that one of
|M|
and

is congruent to
1\pmod{4}
, whereas the other is congruent to

. Consequently,
(-1)^{\frac{|M|-1}{2}}=-(-1)^{\frac{v-1}{2}}
. Applying the generalised law of quadratic reciprocity together with the fact that

, we have

\left(\frac{l}{|M|}\right)\;=\;\left(-(-1)^{\frac{v-1}{2}}\right)\left(-\left(\frac{v}{l}\right)\right)\;=\;(-1)^{\frac{v-1}{2}}\left(\frac{v}{l}\right)\;=\;\left(\frac{l}{v}\right)\;=\;-1.

Therefore, in either case,
\left(\frac{l}{|M|}\right)=-1
. By the multiplicativity of the Jacobi symbol, there exists an odd prime factor
p
of
|M|
(i.e.,
p\mid M
) such that
\left(\frac{l}{p}\right)=-1
. However, proceeding identically to Subcase 2.1, reduction of (

) modulo

yields
(wT^{-1})^{2}\equiv l\pmod{p}
, forcing
\left(\frac{l}{p}\right)=1
, a contradiction.
∎

Theorem 4.1
.

Let

, and

(b)

6.
A generalised variant of (
2

Generalising (

where the coefficients of

restricts any possible solution

of (

for

or

and on the parity of

Theorem 6.1
.

Let
l
be a prime such that

. Let

be such that

. Let

and

be integers, and let

and

be odd integers with

) has no integer solutions with

even. Furthermore, the following statements hold:

(a)

If

and

is even, (

) has no integer solutions.

(b)

If
a\equiv 1\pmod{4}
and

is odd, (
20
) has no integer solutions with

.

(c)

If
a\equiv 3\pmod{4}
and

is odd, (

) has no integer solutions with

.

Proof.

We begin by evaluating the residue of

modulo
4
. Because

and

are odd, we have

. Since

is odd,

. As

and

, we have

.

Suppose, for a contradiction, that there exists a solution
(x,y,z)
with

. As

, and

and

is even and

is odd. Consequently,

,

, and

. Since

and

, reducing (

) modulo

a contradiction. Therefore,

cannot be even.

Case 1
:

is odd, with

and

even.

and

Proceeding similarly as in Case

Substituting either

7.
Non-existence of positive integer solutions

In this section, rather than ruling out solutions within specific residue classes, we establish bounds on the size of any potential positive solution to (
2
) for any positive integer
l
. In particular, (
2
) admits only finitely many positive solutions satisfying
x\leq 2cmn
.

Theorem 7.1
.

Let
a,c,d,l,m,n
be positive integers such that
b\coloneqq a(2cmn)^{d}-lc>0
. Then (
2
) has no positive integer solution
(x,y,z)
such that
x\leq\big(\frac{b}{a}\big)^{1/d}
.

Proof.

Assume that (
2
) has a positive integer solution
(x,y,z)
such that
x\leq\big(\frac{b}{a}\big)^{1/d}
, that is,

c(m^{2}y^{2}+n^{2}z^{2})-xyz=ax^{d}-b.

Since
a,b>0
, the quantity
R\coloneqq(b/a)^{1/d}
is well-defined and positive. For
x\in(0,R]
, we have
x^{d}\leq\frac{b}{a}
, that is,
ax^{d}-b\leq 0
. Since
(my-nz)^{2}\geq 0
and
c>0
, we obtain

2cmnyz\leq c(m^{2}y^{2}+n^{2}z^{2})=xyz+ax^{d}-b\leq xyz.

Since
yz>0
, we have
2cmn\leq x\leq R
. Thus
2cmn\leq R
, that is,
lc/a\leq 0
, a contradiction.
∎

Theorem 7.2
.

Let
a,c,d,l,m,n
be positive integers such that
b\coloneqq a(2cmn)^{d}-lc>0
.

(a)

If either
l=1
and
\gcd(m,n)>1
, or
l\geq 2
is squarefree, then (
2
) has no integer solution
(x,y,z)
with
x=2cmn
.

(b)

Every positive integer solution
(x,y,z)
of (
2
) with
x<2cmn
satisfies

x>(b/a)^{1/d},\qquad 2cmn-\frac{lc}{1+ad}\leq x<2cmn,

yz\leq\frac{lc}{2cmn-x}-ad,\qquad\text{and}\qquad|my-nz|\leq\big\lfloor\sqrt{l}\big\rfloor.

In particular, the number of such solutions is finite.

Proof.

Let
(x,y,z)
be an integer solution where
x\leq 2cmn
. Rearranging (
2
) yields

(22)

(2cmn-x)yz+c(my-nz)^{2}+a\bigl((2cmn)^{d}-x^{d}\bigr)=lc.

(a)
x=2cmn
.

In this case, (
22
) simplifies to
c(my-nz)^{2}=lc
, which implies
(my-nz)^{2}=l
. Since
l\geq 2
is squarefree, it cannot be a perfect square. Also,
l=1
forces
\gcd(m,n)=1
, a contradiction. Thus, there are no integer solutions
(x,y,z)
satisfying
x=2cmn
.

(b)
x<2cmn
.

Let
(x,y,z)
be a positive integer solution. Define
k=2cmn-x
. By hypothesis,
k\geq 1
, so (
22
) becomes

kyz+c(my-nz)^{2}+a\bigl((2cmn)^{d}-x^{d}\bigr)=lc.

Because
k,y,z\geq 1
, we have
kyz+c(my-nz)^{2}>0
. Therefore, (
22
) yields
x>(b/a)^{1/d}
.

Since
a,d\geq 1
, the Lagrange’s mean value theorem guarantees some
\gamma\in(x,2cmn)
such that

a\bigl((2cmn)^{d}-x^{d}\bigr)=ad\gamma^{d-1}(2cmn-x)=kad\gamma^{d-1}.

Because
\gamma>x\geq 1
, we have
\gamma^{d-1}\geq x^{d-1}\geq 1
, which yields
a\bigl((2cmn)^{d}-x^{d}\bigr)\geq kad
. Substituting this into (
22
), we obtain

lc=kyz+c(my-nz)^{2}+a\bigl((2cmn)^{d}-x^{d}\bigr)\geq kyz+c(my-nz)^{2}+kad.

Since
a,c,d,k,y,z\geq 1
, every term on the right-hand side is nonnegative and bounded above by
lc
. In particular, we have
c(my-nz)^{2}\leq lc
, which yields
|my-nz|\leq\big\lfloor\sqrt{l}\big\rfloor
.

Similarly, we have
k(1+ad)\leq k(yz+ad)\leq lc
, which proves

k\leq\frac{lc}{1+ad}
. Since
x=2cmn-k
, we have
2cmn-\frac{lc}{1+ad}\leq x<2cmn
.

Finally,
k(yz+ad)\leq lc
yields
yz\leq\frac{lc}{k}-ad
, that is,
yz\leq\frac{lc}{2cmn-x}-ad
.

For each of the finitely many integers
x
in
2cmn-\frac{lc}{1+ad}\leq x<2cmn
, the condition
1\leq yz\leq\frac{lc}{2cmn-x}-ad
restricts
y
and
z
to finitely many pairs. Consequently, (
2
) has only finitely many positive integer solutions
(x,y,z)
satisfying
x<2cmn
.
∎

Remark 7.3
.

The bounds established in Theorem

with the choice of parameters

8.
Hyperelliptic curves

In this section, we construct a family of (hyper)elliptic curves corresponding to (

satisfying the hypotheses of Theorem

, and study their rational torsion points using the insolvability of the associated Diophantine equation. The main tool is Grant’s generalisation of the Nagell–Lutz theorem to hyperelliptic curves (Theorem

), which connects rational torsion points (defined later) to integer points on the curve.

8.1.
Preliminaries on hyperelliptic curves and Grant’s theorem

Grant’s theorem
[
6
, Theorem 3]
is the genus-
g
analogue of the classical Nagell–Lutz theorem
[
14
, Theorem 2.5, Remark 2.6]
, which was proved independently by T. Nagell
[
12
]
and É. Lutz
[
9
]
. To state the Nagell–Lutz theorem, we start with the definition of an elliptic curve (or, more generally, a hyperelliptic curve).

Throughout,
C
denotes the smooth (i.e., nonsingular) projective curve over
\mathbb{Q}
attached to an affine model
y^{2}=f(x)
, where
f\in\mathbb{Z}[x]
is a monic, squarefree (see Definition
2.11
) polynomial of degree
\deg f\geq 3
, and its
genus
is the integer
g=\lfloor\frac{\deg f-1}{2}\rfloor
. We call
C

elliptic
over
\mathbb{Q}
if
g=1
, and
hyperelliptic
over
\mathbb{Q}
if
g\geq 2
.

From now on, we assume that
\deg f
is odd (i.e.,
\deg f=2g+1
) to ensure that the smooth projective model
C
contains exactly one point at infinity, denoted
\infty
. This establishes the setting for Grant’s theorem below.

For a field
K\supseteq\mathbb{Q}
, the set of

K
-rational points
on
C
, denoted
C(K)
, consists of the point at infinity together with all the solutions to
y^{2}=f(x)
with coordinates in
K
, that is,

(23)

\displaystyle C(K)=\bigl\{(\alpha,\beta)\in K\times K\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\beta^{2}=f(\alpha)\bigr\}\cup\{\infty\}.

An
elliptic curve

E
over
\mathbb{Q}
can be given by a Weierstrass equation of the form

(24)

\displaystyle E\colon\;y^{2}=f(x)=x^{3}+Ax^{2}+Bx+C,\qquad A,B,C\in\mathbb{Z},

where
f
is squarefree, so that
E
is nonsingular. The rational points on
E
, denoted
E(\mathbb{Q})
(as defined in (
23
)), form a group under the chord–tangent group law, with
\infty
(often written as
\mathcal{O}
) as its identity element.

For an elliptic curve
E
(as in (
24
)), the
Nagell–Lutz theorem
says that every nontrivial rational torsion point
P=(x,y)\in E(\mathbb{Q})
has integer coordinates, that is,
x,y\in\mathbb{Z}
, and if
y\neq 0
, then
y^{2}
divides
\disc(f)
, where

\disc(f)=18ABC-4A^{3}C+A^{2}B^{2}-4B^{3}-27C^{2}
. Since
\disc(f)\neq 0
(by Remark
2.12
), the condition
y^{2}\mid\disc(f)
ensures that there are only finitely many choices for
y
. Consequently, while the group
E(\mathbb{Q})
itself may be infinite, its torsion subgroup
E(\mathbb{Q})_{\mathrm{tors}}
is always finite. Mordell

[
11
]
established that the group
E(\mathbb{Q})
is finitely generated. In sharp contrast, Faltings
[
5
]
proved the Mordell conjecture, establishing that for any smooth, projective, and absolutely irreducible curve
C
over
\mathbb{Q}
of genus
g\geq 2
, the set of rational points
C(\mathbb{Q})
is finite.

Unlike
E(\mathbb{Q})
, the set
C(\mathbb{Q})
carries no natural group structure for a curve
C
of genus
g\geq 2
. This obstruction is overcome by embedding
C
into its Jacobian
J(C)
. To define the Jacobian, we first define divisors.

A (Weil)
divisor
is a finite formal sum
\sum_{i}n_{i}P_{i}
, where
n_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}
and
P_{i}\in C(\overline{\mathbb{Q}})
; equivalently, it is an element of the free abelian group on the set
C(\overline{\mathbb{Q}})
, and its
degree
is
\sum_{i}n_{i}
.

To define principal divisors, we attach an integer
\ord_{P}(h)
, namely the
order of
h
at
P
, to each point
P\in C(\overline{\mathbb{Q}})
and each nonzero rational function
h
on
C
. Since
C
is smooth at
P
, there is a rational function
t
vanishing to order exactly
1
at
P
, called a
local parameter
(or
uniformizer
) at
P
. Any nonzero rational function
h
can then be written uniquely as
h=t^{m}w
with
m\in\mathbb{Z}
and
w
defined and nonzero at
P
, and we set
\ord_{P}(h)=m
. If
\ord_{P}(h)>0
, we say
h
has a
zero
at
P
of that order; if
\ord_{P}(h)<0
, we say
h
has a
pole
at
P
of order
-\ord_{P}(h)
.

A divisor is
principal
if it is the divisor of a nonzero rational function

h
on
C
, namely
\ddiv(h)=\sum_{P}\ord_{P}(h)\,P
. Since a nonzero rational function
on
C
has as many zeros as poles, counted with multiplicity, every principal divisor
has degree
0
; consequently the principal divisors form a subgroup

\operatorname{Prin}(C)
of the group
\operatorname{Div}^{0}(C)
of degree-zero
divisors. Two divisors are
linearly equivalent
if they differ by a principal
divisor. The
Jacobian
of
C
is the quotient group

J=J(C)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\operatorname{Div}^{0}(C)/\operatorname{Prin}(C),

with addition induced from addition of divisors. For a degree-zero divisor

D\in\operatorname{Div}^{0}(C)
, we write
[D]\in J
for its linear-equivalence class;
thus
[D_{1}]=[D_{2}]
if and only if
D_{1}-D_{2}
is principal, and the class
[0]
of
the zero divisor is the identity element of
J
.

The absolute Galois group
G_{\mathbb{Q}}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\operatorname{Gal}(\overline{\mathbb{Q}}/\mathbb{Q})

acts on
C(\overline{\mathbb{Q}})
coordinatewise, fixing
\infty
, and hence acts on divisors and on divisor classes. The group of
rational points

J(\mathbb{Q})
is the subgroup of degree-zero divisor classes (with
\overline{\mathbb{Q}}
-coordinates) fixed by this action. Equivalently,
J(\mathbb{Q})
consists of divisor classes that admit a representative divisor whose points are permuted among themselves by every field automorphism of
\overline{\mathbb{Q}}
fixing
\mathbb{Q}
. This group
J(\mathbb{Q})
is called the Mordell–Weil group of
C
(or of
J
) over
\mathbb{Q}
. By the Mordell–Weil theorem
[
21
]
,
J(\mathbb{Q})
is a finitely generated abelian group, so its torsion subgroup
J(\mathbb{Q})_{\mathrm{tors}}
is finite. Equipped with this necessary terminology, we are now in a position to state Grant’s theorem.

Theorem 8.1

(
[
6
, Theorem 3]
)
.

Let
C
be the nonsingular projective curve of genus
g\geq 1
given by
y^{2}=f(x)=x^{2g+1}+b_{1}x^{2g}+\dots+b_{2g}x+b_{2g+1}
, where
b_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}
and
f
is squarefree. Let

\phi\colon C(\mathbb{Q})\rightarrow J(\mathbb{Q})
be the Abel–Jacobi map, defined by
\phi(P)=[P-\infty]
. If
P=(x,y)\in C(\mathbb{Q})\setminus\{\infty\}
and

\phi(P)\in J(\mathbb{Q})_{\mathrm{tors}}
, then
x,y\in\mathbb{Z}
and either
y=0
or
y^{2}
divides
\disc(f)
.

Let
C\colon y^{2}=f(x)
be a (hyper)elliptic curve over
\mathbb{Q}
of genus
g\geq 1
, with
\deg f=2g+1
, and let
\infty\in C(\mathbb{Q})
be the unique point at infinity. Let
\phi
be the Abel–Jacobi map with basepoint
\infty
as defined in Theorem
8.1
. We call a point
P\in C(\mathbb{Q})\setminus\{\infty\}
a
rational torsion point
if
\phi(P)\in J(\mathbb{Q})_{\mathrm{tors}}
.

8.2.
From Diophantine equations to hyperelliptic curves

For a fixed integer
k
, let
C_{k,a,b,c,m,n}
be the affine plane
curve over
\mathbb{Q}
defined by

(25)

\displaystyle C_{k,a,b,c,m,n}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\qquad cm^{2}y^{2}-kxy=ax^{d}-cn^{2}k^{2}-b.

Since (
25
) is precisely (
2
) with
z=k
fixed, a point
(x,y)\in\mathbb{Q}^{2}
lies on
C_{k,a,b,c,m,n}
if and only if
(x,y,k)
is a solution of (
2
).

Assuming
d\geq 3
, we now define the corresponding families of (hyper)elliptic curves based on the parity of
k
.

(a)
k
is even.

Multiplying (
25
) by
cm^{2}
, and completing the square, we get

\displaystyle\left(cm^{2}y-\frac{kx}{2}\right)^{2}-\frac{k^{2}x^{2}}{4}

\displaystyle=cm^{2}ax^{d}-cm^{2}(cn^{2}k^{2}+b).

Multiplying by
(cm^{2}a)^{d-1}
, and using the fact that
d
is an odd integer, we have

\left(\Big(cm^{2}y-\frac{kx}{2}\Big)(cm^{2}a)^{\frac{d-1}{2}}\right)^{2}-\frac{(kx)^{2}(cm^{2}a)^{d-1}}{4}=(cm^{2}ax)^{d}-cm^{2}(cn^{2}k^{2}+b)(cm^{2}a)^{d-1}.

Substituting
X=cm^{2}ax
and
Y=\left(cm^{2}y-\frac{kx}{2}\right)(cm^{2}a)^{\frac{d-1}{2}}
, we obtain the following family of hyperelliptic (or elliptic when
d=3
) curves

(26)

\displaystyle H_{k,a,b,c,m,n}^{e}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}Y^{2}-(cm^{2}a)^{d-3}\frac{k^{2}X^{2}}{4}=X^{d}-cm^{2}(cn^{2}k^{2}+b)(cm^{2}a)^{d-1}.

(b)
k
is odd.

Multiplying (
25
) by
4cm^{2}
, and completing the square, we get

\displaystyle(2cm^{2}y-kx)^{2}-k^{2}x^{2}

\displaystyle=4cm^{2}ax^{d}-4cm^{2}(cn^{2}k^{2}+b).

Multiplying this by
(4cm^{2}a)^{d-1}
yields

\displaystyle\left((2cm^{2}y-kx)(4cm^{2}a)^{\frac{d-1}{2}}\right)^{2}-(4cm^{2}a)^{d-1}k^{2}x^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(4cm^{2}ax)^{d}-4cm^{2}(cn^{2}k^{2}+b)(4cm^{2}a)^{d-1}.

Finally, substituting
X=4cm^{2}ax
and
Y=(2cm^{2}y-kx)(4cm^{2}a)^{\frac{d-1}{2}}
, we obtain the following family of hyperelliptic (or elliptic when
d=3
) curves

(27)

\displaystyle H_{k,a,b,c,m,n}^{o}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}Y^{2}-(4cm^{2}a)^{d-3}k^{2}X^{2}=X^{d}-4cm^{2}(cn^{2}k^{2}+b)(4cm^{2}a)^{d-1}.

Consider the family of (hyper)elliptic curves defined by

(28)

\displaystyle H_{k,a,b,c,m,n}=\begin{cases}H_{k,a,b,c,m,n}^{e}&\text{if }k\text{ is even,}\\
H_{k,a,b,c,m,n}^{o}&\text{if }k\text{ is odd.}\end{cases}

We now analyse the rational torsion points of
H_{k,a,b,c,m,n}
.

Lemma 8.2
.

Let
a,c,d,m,n
and
b
satisfy the hypotheses of Theorem
4.3
. A rational point
(X,Y)\in H_{k,a,b,c,m,n}(\mathbb{Q})\setminus\{\infty\}

(a)

(b)

\frac{Y}{2^{d}(cm^{2})^{\frac{d+1}{2}}a^{\frac{d-1}{2}}}+\frac{kX}{8c^{2}m^{4}a}\in\mathbb{Z}

Proof.

To establish the stated conditions, we examine the inverse transformations for both parities of

(a)

is even.

Let

be defined as in (
25
) and (

\psi^{e}\colon H^{e}_{k,a,b,c,m,n}\cap\mathbb{Q}^{2}\rightarrow C_{k,a,b,c,m,n}\cap\mathbb{Q}^{2}

(29)

\displaystyle\phi^{e}(x,y)

\displaystyle=\left(\,cm^{2}ax,\ \left(cm^{2}y-\frac{kx}{2}\right)(cm^{2}a)^{\frac{d-1}{2}}\,\right),

(b)

\displaystyle\phi^{o}(x,y)

\displaystyle\psi^{o}(X,Y)

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{X}{4cm^{2}a},\frac{1}{2cm^{2}}\left(\frac{Y}{(4cm^{2}a)^{\frac{d-1}{2}}}+\frac{kX}{4cm^{2}a}\right)\right).

Therefore, we have

and

∎

Lemma 8.3
.

Let
a,c,m,n,b\in\mathbb{Z}
and let
d\geq 3
be an odd integer, as given in Theorem
4.3
. Let
k\in\mathbb{Z}
and let
f(X)=X^{d}+\lambda X^{2}+\mu
, where

(\lambda,\mu)=\begin{cases}\bigl(\frac{k^{2}}{4}(cm^{2}a)^{d-3},\,-cm^{2}(cn^{2}k^{2}+b)(cm^{2}a)^{d-1}\bigr),&\text{if }k\text{ is even},\\[4.30554pt]
\bigl(k^{2}(4cm^{2}a)^{d-3},\,-4cm^{2}(cn^{2}k^{2}+b)(4cm^{2}a)^{d-1}\bigr),&\text{if }k\text{ is odd}.\end{cases}

Then

is squarefree if and only if

and

(33)

(d-2)^{d-2}k^{2d}\neq 4^{d-1}d^{d}(cm^{2})^{d}a^{2}(cn^{2}k^{2}+b)^{d-2}.

Proof.

By Lemma
2.13
, the discriminant is given by

Thus,

and

Since

if and only if

. We now establish the condition under which

.

Case 1
:
k
is even.
Substituting
\lambda
and
\mu
into
4(d-2)^{d-2}\lambda^{d}\neq-d^{d}\mu^{d-2}
yields

) holds.

:
k
is odd.
Substituting
\lambda
and
\mu
into

yields

Since

is odd, the above is true if and only if

∎

Lemma 8.4
.

Let

and let

be an odd integer, as given in Theorem

. For a fixed

In both parities for

,

,

, and

[D]\in\phi\big(H_{k,1,b,1,1,n}(\mathbb{Q})\big)\cap J(\mathbb{Q})_{\mathrm{tors}}
, where
D\in\operatorname{Div}^{0}\!\big(H_{k,1,b,1,1,n}\big)
. Then
[D]=\phi(P)
for some
P\in H_{k,1,b,1,1,n}(\mathbb{Q})
. If
P\neq\infty
, then
P\in H_{k,1,b,1,1,n}(\mathbb{Q})\setminus\{\infty\}
and
\phi(P)=[D]\in J(\mathbb{Q})_{\mathrm{tors}}
, contradicting
(i)
. Therefore,
P=\infty
, whence
[D]=\phi(\infty)=[\infty-\infty]=[0]
. Consequently,
\phi\big(H_{k,1,b,1,1,n}(\mathbb{Q})\big)\cap J(\mathbb{Q})_{\mathrm{tors}}=\{[0]\}
. This proves
(ii)
.

For
d=3
, the curve
H_{k,1,b,1,1,n}
is elliptic, so
H_{k,1,b,1,1,n}(\mathbb{Q})
is an abelian group (with identity
\infty
), and the Abel–Jacobi map
\phi\colon E(\mathbb{Q})\to J(\mathbb{Q})
,
P\mapsto[P-\infty]
, is a group isomorphism (see
[
15
, p. 330]
). Hence, any nontrivial
P\in H_{k,1,b,1,1,n}(\mathbb{Q})_{\mathrm{tors}}
satisfies
\phi(P)\in J(\mathbb{Q})_{\mathrm{tors}}
. This means that
P
is a rational torsion point, contradicting
(i)
. Therefore,
H_{k,1,b,1,1,n}(\mathbb{Q})_{\mathrm{tors}}
is trivial. This proves
(iii)
.
∎

Remark 8.6
.

Certain claims in
[
20
]
and
[
13
]
are not fully justified by the arguments provided there. Throughout, our
k
corresponds to
m
, and our
a
to
A
, in
[
20
]
.

First
, in mapping an integer point
(X_{0},Y_{0})
on the associated (hyper)elliptic curve back to an integer solution of the Diophantine equation, the authors used the substitution
x=aX_{0}
for
k
even (resp.,
x=4aX_{0}
for
k
odd); see the proof of
[
20
, Theorem 1.2]
(where
(X_{0},Y_{0})
is mapped to
(aX_{0},\,aY_{0}-\tfrac{akX_{0}}{2},\,k)
for even
k
) and
[
13
, Theorem 3.3]
(where
(X_{0},Y_{0})
is sent to
(aX_{0},\,(Y_{0}-\tfrac{kX_{0}}{2})a^{\frac{d-1}{2}},\,k)
for even
k
). The correct inverse map is instead
x=\tfrac{X_{0}}{a}
for
k
even (resp.,
x=\tfrac{X_{0}}{4a}
for
k
odd), which is an integer only when
a\mid X_{0}
(resp.,
4a\mid X_{0}
). For a torsion point of the curve to yield an
integer
solution of the Diophantine equation, this divisibility must hold. However, it fails for general integer points on the curve. Consequently, the reduction to the Diophantine equation does not transform torsion points to integer solutions when
a\neq 1
, so the argument is incomplete for every odd
k
and for even
k
with
a\neq 1
. It works unconditionally when
a=1
and
k
is even, since the inverse map is then integral; this is the case that we establish in Theorem
8.5
.

Second
, for the hyperelliptic curves of
[
13
]
(those with
\deg f\geq 5
, i.e., genus
g\geq 2
), a further gap remains even if the inverse map is corrected, because the Abel–Jacobi map need not be surjective for
g\geq 2
. In particular, there is no guarantee that every class in
J(\mathbb{Q})_{\mathrm{tors}}
is of the form
[P-\infty]
for some
P\in C(\mathbb{Q})
, the only classes to which Grant’s theorem applies. Consequently, the claim that such a hyperelliptic curve has torsion-free Mordell–Weil group cannot be obtained from Grant’s theorem alone. Indeed, a nontrivial rational torsion class can be of the form
[P_{1}+P_{2}-2\infty]
, where
\{P_{1},P_{2}\}
is a Galois-conjugate pair of non-rational points; such a class is not in general of the form
[P-\infty]
, so Grant’s theorem does not apply to it. Hence, the argument for a torsion-free Mordell–Weil group does not extend to
g\geq 2
. By contrast, in the elliptic case (
\deg f=3
), the Abel–Jacobi map is an isomorphism onto
J
. Therefore, every torsion class is represented by a rational torsion point and the argument goes through, although this further requires that the above divisibility constraint is satisfied. We carry out this analysis for
a=1
in Theorem
8.5
. In doing so, we demonstrate that the proof of
[
20
, Theorem 1.2 (1)]
, as originally presented, requires the additional assumption that
a=1
, and the method presented there is insufficient to prove
[
20
, Theorem 1.2 (2)]
. This discussion suggests that
[
20
, Corollary 1.4]
also requires a valid proof.

Third
, to apply Grant’s theorem to a (hyper)elliptic curve
Y^{2}=f(X)
, we require
f
to be squarefree, which we establish by showing that the discriminant of
f
is nonzero (see Lemma
8.4
). We remark that the squarefreeness of
f
was not explicitly mentioned in
[
20
]
(resp.,
[
13
]
) before applying the Nagell–Lutz (resp., Grant) theorem.

Although these gaps invalidate the proofs of
[
20
, Theorem 1.2]
and
[
13
, Theorem 3.3]
, they do not necessarily make the underlying statements false. Indeed, it is possible that the statements may still be true and admit correct independent proofs. In particular, the computational evidence detailed in the following remark suggests that the statement of
[
20
, Theorem 1.2]
may still hold.

Remark 8.7
.

The divisibility obstruction of Remark

,
