Integrative analysis identifies the intratumoral Fusobacterium nucleatum as a driver of cervical cancer malignancy
Authors: Mei Feng, Yaojie Yu, Jiayuan Du, He Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundFusobacterium nucleatum (F. nucleatum) is an oral commensal bacterium that acts as a pathobiont with pro-tumorigenic activity in various gastrointestinal cancers. However, its functional role, invasive capacity, and mechanistic contributions in cervical cancer remain largely unexplored.MethodsWe identified F. nucleatum in cervical cancer tissues using bioinformatics and clinical 16S rRNA sequencing. Its spatial localization and intracellular presence were confirmed by fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) and transmission electron microscopy (TEM), respectively. Functional validation included in vitro assays for proliferation, migration, and apoptosis in cervical cancer cell lines, with bacterial invasion visualized by confocal microscopy, and in vivo tumor growth assessment in a xenograft model. The underlying mechanism involving high mobility group box 1 (HMGB1) and the NF-κB pathway was analyzed by western blot, qPCR, immunofluorescence, and ELISA.ResultsF. nucleatum was enriched in cervical cancer and correlated with poor patient survival. It invaded cervical cancer cells, promoted proliferation, migration, and invasion, suppressed apoptosis in vitro, and accelerated tumor growth in vivo. Mechanistically, infection triggered HMGB1 upregulation and specific activation of the canonical NF-κB pathway (via IκBα degradation, p65 phosphorylation/nuclear translocation), leading to selective secretion of IL-6/IL-8.ConclusionOur study suggests that F. nucleatum is associated with cervical cancer malignancy, potentially acting through upregulation of HMGB1 and activation of the canonical NF-κB signaling pathway, thereby contributing to an altered tumor microenvironment. These findings reveal a previously unrecognized microbial-driven oncogenic mechanism in cervical cancer and highlight its potential as a prognostic marker and a therapeutic target.Clinical trial numberNot applicable in our manuscript.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12896-026-01193-3.

Introduction

Cervical cancer (CC) persists as a leading cause of cancer-related morbidity and mortality among women globally, despite being largely preventable through vaccination against and screening for high-risk human papillomavirus (HR-HPV) [1]. While persistent infection with HR-HPV is the necessary causative agent, only a fraction of infected individuals develop malignancy, indicating that HR-HPV infection is necessary but not sufficient for carcinogenesis [2]. This underscores the critical involvement of additional cooperating factors in disease pathogenesis.

The human microbiome has recently emerged as a pivotal component of the tumor microenvironment, capable of influencing cancer initiation, progression, and treatment response [3–5]. Notably, intracellular bacteria can directly interact with host cells to dysregulate oncogenic signaling pathways, remodel the tumor immune microenvironment, and influence cellular metabolism, thereby promoting tumor growth and therapeutic evasion [6–8]. Among these, F. nucleatum, a Gram-negative, anaerobic oral commensal, has been established as a key oncomicrobe in colorectal, oral, and esophageal cancers [9–11]. Its pro-tumorigenic mechanisms are multifaceted, involving adhesion molecule-mediated activation of Wnt/β-catenin signaling [10, 12] and bacterial component-driven chronic inflammation [13, 14].

Accumulating clinical evidence also links F. nucleatum to cervical cancer. Metagenomic analyses consistently report its enrichment in cervical carcinoma tissues compared to adjacent normal epithelium or tissues from healthy individuals [15–17]. Importantly, its elevated abundance correlates with advanced disease stage, lymph node metastasis, and poorer patient prognosis [18, 19]. Given its strong association with cancer progression in multiple tumor types, F. nucleatum has spurred the development of potential diagnostic strategies and therapeutic interventions, particularly in colorectal cancer where antibiotic targeting of the bacterium has been shown to disrupt tumor growth in xenograft models [20, 21]. Notably, such functional importance has also been observed in breast cancer [22], highlighting the translational potential of targeting this pathobiont. However, in cervical cancer, current evidence remains largely associative. A direct causal role for F. nucleatum has yet to be established, and the underlying mechanisms driving its pro-tumorigenic effects are still unclear.

Therefore, to address this gap, we conducted an integrated study combining clinical observation with systematic functional validation. We aimed to: (1) confirm the enrichment and prognostic significance of F. nucleatum in clinical cervical cancer specimens; (2) determine its direct effects on cancer cell phenotypes in vitro and tumor growth in vivo; and (3) elucidate the signaling mechanism involved. We demonstrated that F. nucleatum directly invades cervical cancer cells to promote malignant phenotypes. Mechanistically, our data implicate a potential signaling axis where F. nucleatum triggers the upregulation of HMGB1, concomitant with the activation of the canonical NF-κB pathway and a pro-inflammatory response. Our findings suggest F. nucleatum as a functional contributor to cervical cancer aggressiveness and identify a novel microbial-host signaling axis, which may represent a potential therapeutic target for this disease.

Materials and methods

Bioinformatics analysis of the microbiome

The cervical cancer microbiome dataset was obtained from the TCMbio database (https://microbiomex.sdu.edu.cn/), consisting of 304 tumor tissue samples and 3 non-cancer control tissues. We quantified the relative abundance of the Fusobacterium genus and Fusobacterium nucleatum species. Patients were stratified into high- and low-abundance groups based on the median abundance of F. nucleatum. Overall survival and progression-free survival between these groups were compared using Kaplan-Meier curves and the log-rank test.

Clinical samples

A total of 92 clinical tissue specimens were collected for this study. This comprised 46 cervical cancer tissues and 46 normal cervical tissues from patients who underwent surgical resection at the Affiliated Tumor Hospital of Guangxi Medical University. Cancer tissues were obtained from patients diagnosed with cervical cancer who underwent curative resection without prior adjuvant therapy. Normal cervical tissues were collected from patients who underwent hysterectomy for benign conditions (e.g., uterine fibroids). To minimize potential endogenous contamination, clean tissue blocks from the core of the resected specimens were carefully selected for all experimental procedures. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Affiliated Tumor Hospital of Guangxi Medical University (Approval No. KY2025784). Written informed consent was obtained from all participating patients.

Cell and bacterial culture

The human cervical cancer cell lines SiHa (HPV16 + cervical squamous carcinoma cells) (HTB-35), and HeLa (HPV18 + cervical epithelial adenocarcinoma) (CRM-CCL-2) were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC). Cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM; Gibco, USA) supplemented with 10% Fetal Bovine Serum (FBS; Gibco, USA) and 1% Penicillin/Streptomycin at 37 °C in a humidified 5% CO2 atmosphere.

F. Nucleatum (ATCC 25586) was purchased from ATCC and cultured anaerobically on Columbia blood agar at 37 °C for 3 days. Single colonies were then inoculated into fastidious anaerobe broth and cultured anaerobically to mid-log phase. Planktonic growth of F. nucleatum was monitored by measuring the optical density at 600 nm (OD600nm). Bacteria were harvested by centrifugation, washed, and resuspended in antibiotic-free medium. For co-culture, cervical cancer cells were infected at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 100:1 (bacteria: cells), an optimized ratio consistent with established infection models [23], and incubated under standard conditions (37 °C, 5% CO2) for the indicated durations. To specifically assess intracellular bacteria, extracellular F. nucleatum was eliminated by treating the co-cultured cells with fresh media containing gentamicin (300 µg/ml) and metronidazole (200 µg/ml) for 1 h [24], prior to live-cell imaging by laser scanning confocal microscopy (LSCM) and qPCR analysis of intracellular F. nucleatum load.

16S rRNA gene amplicon sequencing

A total of 20 clinical specimens, comprising 10 cervical cancer tissues and 10 normal cervical tissues, were initially collected. To minimize exogenous DNA contamination, tissue collection was performed under aseptic conditions in the operating room using sterilized instruments, and clean tissue blocks from the core of each specimen were selected. All subsequent steps were carried out in a laminar flow hood. Negative extraction controls (blank reagents without tissue) and no-template PCR controls were included in each batch. To ensure the quality and amplifiability of bacterial DNA, all samples were first subjected to real-time quantitative PCR (qPCR) targeting the 16S rRNA gene V9 region. Samples whose cycle threshold (Ct) values fell outside the linear range of the standard curve were excluded. Based on this quality threshold, 2 cancer and 5 normal samples were excluded. To assess whether this exclusion introduced bias, we compared the clinical characteristics (e.g., age, stage) of the excluded and included samples and found no systematic differences. Consequently, a final set of 13 samples (8 cervical cancer and 5 normal controls) were subjected to 16S rRNA gene amplicon sequencing.

16S rRNA gene amplicon sequencing of the V4 region was performed by Shanghai G&C Biotechnology Co., Ltd (Shanghai, China). The region was amplified using primers V4F (5′-GTGYCAGCMGCCGCGGTAA-3′) and V4R (5′-GGACTACNVGGGTWTCTAA-3′), which were flanked by Illumina adapter sequences and sample-specific barcodes. Paired-end sequencing (2 × 250 bp) was conducted on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform. The resulting reads were quality-filtered, and any operational taxonomic unit (OTU) detected in the negative control was removed as a potential contaminant before clustering.

DNA extraction and qPCR for F. nucleatum

Genomic DNA was extracted from 30 cervical cancer and normal tissues using the TIANamp Genomic DNA Kit (Tiangen Biotech), followed by concentration and purity assessment on a NanoDrop spectrophotometer. The abundance of F. nucleatum was quantified by qPCR (QuantStudio 5 system) using SYBR Premix Ex Taq (Takara) in 20 µL reactions containing 2 µL of template DNA and specific primers. The human prostaglandin transporter (PGT) was used as an endogenous reference for normalization [25]. The primers used for PGT were 5′-ATCCCCAAAGCACCTGGTTT-3′ (forward) and 5′-AGAGGCCAAGATAGTCCTGGTAA-3′ (reverse); primers for F. nucleatum were 5′-TGGTGTCATTCTTCCAAAAATATCA-3′ (forward) and 5′-AGATCAAGAAGGACAAGTTGCTGAA-3′ (reverse). The relative abundance of F. nucleatum was calculated using the comparative 2−ΔCt method [26], where △Ct = the Ct value of the F. nucleatum - the Ct value of PGT.

Fluorescence in situ hybridization

FISH was performed to localize F. nucleatum in six cervical cancer and six normal cervical tissues. Frozen sections (5 μm) were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, dehydrated, and subjected to hybridization. A Cy3-labeled probe targeting F. nucleatum 16S rRNA (FUS664: 5′-CTTGTAGTTCCGCYTACCTC-3′) was used for specific detection. A FITC-labeled nonsense probe (non-EUB338: 5′-CGCAATACAGAGTTGAGCCCTGC-3′) served as a negative control. Hybridization was carried out at 37 °C for 16 h in buffer containing 0.9 M NaCl, 20 mM Tris-HCl (pH 7.4), 0.01% SDS and 20% formamide. After stringent washes, nuclei were counterstained with DAPI. Images were acquired using a fluorescence microscope under identical exposure settings for both channels.

Functional assays

The functional impact of F. nucleatum on cervical cancer cells (SiHa and HeLa) was evaluated through proliferation, migration, invasion, and apoptosis assays. In all experiments, control groups were cultured in standard medium, while experimental groups were co-cultured with F. nucleatum at a MOI of 100.

Proliferation was assessed using the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) assay. Briefly, cells were seeded into 96-well plates at a density of 5 × 103 cells per well. After 0, 12, 24, 36, and 48 h of co-culture, CCK-8 reagent was added to each well, incubated for 2 h at 37 °C, and absorbance was measured at 450 nm.

Migration and invasion were evaluated using Transwell inserts with 8 μm pores, uncoated for migration or pre-coated with Matrigel for invasion. In both assays, cells were suspended in serum-free medium and seeded into the upper chamber: 2 × 10⁴ cells for migration, and 4 × 10⁴ cells for invasion. The lower chamber contained complete medium with 10% FBS as a chemoattractant. After incubation for 24 h (migration) or 48 h (invasion), cells that had traversed the membrane were fixed, stained with 0.1% crystal violet, and counted under a light microscope.

Wound-healing assay was performed to assess cell migration. Confluent cell monolayers in 6-well plates were scratched with a sterile pipette tip and then co-cultured in serum-free medium. Wound closure was photographed and quantified at 0, 24, and 48 h.

Apoptosis was analyzed by flow cytometry after 24 h of co-culture using Annexin V-APC and 7-AAD dual staining. Cells were harvested, washed, and stained according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Stained cells were analyzed immediately on a flow cytometer. Cell populations were classified as viable (Annexin V⁻/7-AAD⁻), early apoptotic (Annexin V⁺/7-AAD⁻), and late apoptotic/necrotic (Annexin V⁺/7-AAD⁺). Data were processed using FlowJo software (BD Biosciences).

Western blot analysis

Protein expression was analyzed by western blot. SiHa and HeLa cells were co-cultured with or without F. nucleatum (MOI = 100) for indicated times. Cells were lysed in RIPA buffer containing protease and phosphatase inhibitors. Protein concentrations were determined by BCA assay. Equal amounts of protein (30 µg) were separated by 10% SDS-PAGE and transferred to PVDF membranes. After blocking, membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against: HMGB1 (#6893, CST), NF-κB p65 (#MA9199, Abmart), Phospho-NF-κB p65 (Ser536) (#TP56372, Abmart), IκBα (#T55026, Abmart), Phospho-IκBα (Ser32/36) (#T56280, Abmart), GAPDH (#2118, CST), and β-actin (#8480, CST). All antibodies were used at 1:1000 dilution. Following TBST washes, membranes were incubated with IRDye® 800CW-conjugated secondary antibodies (1:10,000) for 1 h at room temperature. Signals were detected using a LI-COR Odyssey® CLx system and quantified with ImageJ software, normalized to GAPDH or β-actin. Each experiment was repeated with three independent biological replicates, and representative blots are shown. Uncropped blots are provided in the Supplementary Information.

Animal experiments

Four-week-old female BALB/c nude mice were obtained from the Animal Experiment Center of Guangxi Medical University and maintained under specific pathogen-free (SPF) conditions with autoclaved food and water ad libitum. All procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of the Affiliated Tumor Hospital of Guangxi Medical University (Approval No. KY2025785). To establish xenograft tumors, mice were subcutaneously inoculated in the right flank with 1 × 106 SiHa cells suspended in 100 µL PBS. When tumor volumes reached approximately 100 mm3, mice were randomly assigned to three groups (n = 6 per group): PBS group: received intratumoral injections of sterile PBS; PBS-Abx group: mice were administered doxycycline (1 mg/mL) in drinking water for 5 days to deplete potential endogenous intratumoral bacteria as described in previous study [4]. After a 3-day washout period, mice received intratumoral PBS injections. Fn-Abx group: mice underwent the same doxycycline pretreatment, followed by intratumoral injections of F. nucleatum. F. nucleatum was resuspended in sterile PBS at a concentration of 5 × 109 CFU/mL. Each intratumoral injection delivered 1 × 108 CFU in a 20 µL volume. Injections were given twice weekly for two weeks. Tumor dimensions were measured every 5 days using a caliper, and volume was calculated as (length × width2) / 2. Body weight was monitored weekly as a surrogate for systemic health. On day 30, all mice were euthanized by cervical dislocation under deep isoflurane anesthesia for subsequent analysis.

Cytokine analysis by ELISA

The concentrations of IL-6, IL-8, and TNF-α were quantified by enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA). Cell culture supernatants were collected and centrifuged (3,000 × g, 15 min, 4 °C) to remove debris. Mouse serum was isolated from terminal blood samples by centrifugation under the same conditions. All samples were stored at -80 °C until analysis. The levels of inflammatory cytokines were detected by ELISA kits (Elabscience, Wuhan, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Each experiment was performed with at least three independent biological replicates, and all samples were measured in duplicate.

Statistical analysis

Data are expressed as mean ± SD. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 10.0. Group comparisons used two-tailed Student’s t-test (two groups) or one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post-hoc test (multiple groups). For microbiome differential abundance, false discovery rate (FDR) correction (Benjamini-Hochberg) was applied where indicated. Statistical significance was defined as P < 0.05. All experiments included at least three independent replicates.

Results

F. nucleatum is enriched in cervical cancer and correlates with poor prognosis

To characterize the microbial landscape of cervical carcinoma, we performed differential abundance analysis at both the genus and species levels using the TCMbio dataset. At the genus level, Fusobacterium was among the most enriched taxa in cancer tissues (log2FC = 4.88, P = 0.0127; Fig. 1A), along with Pasteurella and Hydrogenophaga. Conversely, genera such as Sinimarinibacterium were more abundant in normal tissues. At the species level, Fusobacterium nucleatum exhibited a consistent upward trend in cancer samples (log2FC = 3.23, P = 0.0631; Fig. 1B). Other species including Pasteurella multocida and Staphylococcus hominis were also markedly elevated in carcinoma tissues. Together, these multi-level profiles reveal a distinct microbiota signature associated with cervical cancer, highlighting Fusobacterium, and specifically F. nucleatum, as a consistently altered taxon warranting further investigation. Clinically, when patients were stratified by the median abundance of the Fusobacterium genus, high abundance was strongly associated with significantly poorer overall survival (P < 0.0001; Fig. 1C). Concordantly, using the same median cut-off for F. nucleatum, elevated levels also predicted worse overall survival (P = 0.00012; Fig. 1D). Moreover, both high Fusobacterium genus and F. nucleatum abundance were robustly correlated with decreased progression-free survival (both P < 0.0001; Fig. 1E, F). These consistent findings underscore the potential of Fusobacterium, particularly F. nucleatum, as a prognostic biomarker in cervical cancer.

16S rRNA sequencing reveals distinct microbiome features in cervical cancer patients

After removing potential contaminant OTUs detected in the negative control and excluding low-quality samples, 16S rRNA sequencing of 13 clinical specimens (8 tumors, 5 normal tissues) yielded 912,118 high-quality reads, which were clustered into 857 OTUs. Alpha diversity analysis showed no significant differences between tumor and normal tissues (all P > 0.05; Fig. 2A), indicating comparable microbial richness and evenness. In contrast, beta diversity analysis revealed distinct microbial community structures. Principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) based on Bray-Curtis distances demonstrated clear separation between groups (Fig. 2B), which was statistically supported by PERMANOVA (R2 = 0.14, P = 0.012). DEICODE analysis, which accounts for compositionality and sparsity, corroborated this separation (Fig. 2C), confirming an altered microbiota composition in cervical cancer. Taxonomic profiling identified a core microbiota shared between groups, primarily comprising Pseudomonas, Lactobacillus, Acinetobacter, Prevotella, Bacteroides, Streptococcus, and Fusobacterium (Fig. 2D, E). Fusobacterium was ubiquitously present in all samples and showed a higher average abundance in tumor tissues, although this difference was not statistically significant, underscoring its potential as a persistent resident with biological importance.

F. nucleatum colonizes and invades cervical cancer cells

To validate the enrichment of F. nucleatum in cervical cancer, we quantified its DNA levels in tumor tissues by qPCR. F. nucleatum was detected in 16 out of 30 cases (53.3%; Fig. 3A), with significantly higher DNA levels in cancer tissues compared to normal controls (Fig. 3B). We next visualized its spatial distribution and invasive capacity. Fluorescence in situ hybridization using a Cy3-labeled FUS664 probe revealed abundant bacterial signals within tumor sections, which were markedly sparse in normal tissues. Semiquantitative analysis revealed a significantly greater abundance of FISH-positive signals in cancer tissues compared to normal controls (Fig. 3C, D). Transmission electron microscopy further identified characteristic bacterial structures within the cytoplasm of cancer cells, an observation absent in normal tissues (Fig. 3E).

To confirm active invasion, we performed a co-culture assay. Live-cell imaging by confocal microscopy showed F. nucleatum associated with and internalized by cervical cancer cells (Fig. 3F). Following co-culture and antibiotic removal of extracellular bacteria, qPCR analysis of host cell lysates confirmed a significant enrichment of intracellular F. nucleatum DNA compared to controls (Fig. 3G). Collectively, these data establish that F. nucleatum not only colonizes cervical cancer tissues but also actively invades and persists within host cells.

F. nucleatum promotes malignant phenotypes of cervical cancer cells

To assess the functional impact of F. nucleatum on cervical cancer cells, we performed a series of phenotypic assays. Co-culture with F. nucleatum significantly promoted the proliferation of both SiHa and HeLa cells at 24 and 48 h, as determined by CCK-8 assays (Fig. 4A, B). Conversely, flow cytometric analysis revealed that F. nucleatum co-culture markedly inhibited apoptosis in the two cell lines (Fig. 4C, D). The pro-tumorigenic effects extended to metastatic behaviors. F. nucleatum infection robustly enhanced the migratory and invasive capacities of cervical cancer cells in Transwell assays (Fig. 4E, F). Consistently, wound healing assays demonstrated that infected cells closed the scratch wound significantly faster than controls at 24 h (Fig. 4G, H). In contrast to live bacteria, treatment with heat-killed F. nucleatum (60 °C, 1 h) did not significantly affect cell proliferation or migration (Supplementary Figure S1), indicating that the observed malignant phenotypes require live bacteria. Together, these results demonstrate that F. nucleatum drives a multifaceted aggressive phenotype in cervical cancer cells, characterized by enhanced proliferation, migration, and invasion, alongside attenuated apoptosis.

F. nucleatum promotes cervical tumor growth in a xenograft model

To investigate the tumor-promoting role of F. nucleatum in vivo, we established a xenograft model. SiHa cells were subcutaneously inoculated into BALB/c nude mice. Following oral doxycycline pretreatment to deplete the resident microbiota, established tumors were intratumorally injected with PBS or F. nucleatum twice per week (Fig. 5A). F. nucleatum treatment significantly promoted tumor growth, resulting in larger final tumor volumes and increased tumor weights compared to both PBS and antibiotic-pretreated (PBS-Abx) control groups (Fig. 5B-D). To confirm successful colonization, we performed qPCR for F. nucleatum DNA in tumor tissues at the endpoint. The presence of the bacterium was readily detected in the Fn-Abx group (Supplementary Figure S2), confirming successful colonization of F. nucleatum in the xenografts. Consistent with the enhanced growth, immunohistochemical analysis revealed increased staining of the proliferation marker Ki-67 in F. nucleatum-treated xenografts (Fig. 5E). Furthermore, ELISA of mouse serum showed that F. nucleatum infection elevated systemic levels of IL-6 and IL-8 (Fig. 5F, G), whereas TNF-α concentrations remained unchanged across groups (Fig. 5H). Collectively, these in vivo data demonstrate that F. nucleatum not only accelerates local tumor growth by stimulating cancer cell proliferation but also induces a systemic inflammatory response characterized by increased IL-6 and IL-8.

F. nucleatum activates a pro-tumorigenic inflammatory response associated with HMGB1 upregulation and NF-κB activation

To elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying F. nucleatum-mediated tumor promotion, we investigated its impact on inflammatory signaling. RT-qPCR and western blot analyses showed that infection significantly upregulated the damage-associated molecular pattern (DAMP) molecule HMGB1 at both mRNA and protein levels in cervical cancer cells (Fig. 6A, B). Concordantly, ELISA of cell-culture supernatants revealed that F. nucleatum infection selectively enhanced the secretion of IL-6 and IL-8 (Fig. 6C, D), while TNF-α levels were unaffected (Fig. 6E). Given the upregulation of HMGB1, a known regulator of inflammation, we hypothesized that the NF-κB pathway might be involved. Immunofluorescence staining demonstrated pronounced nuclear translocation of the NF-κB subunit p65 in infected cells (Fig. 6F). Western blot analysis confirmed canonical NF-κB pathway activation, as evidenced by increased phosphorylation of p65, concomitant degradation of IκBα, and a significantly elevated ratio of phosphorylated to total IκBα (p-IκBα/IκBα) (Fig. 6G).

To further establish the causal roles of HMGB1 and NF-κB, we performed pharmacological inhibition experiments. First, we used glycyrrhizin (GA), a HMGB1 inhibitor. Western blot analysis showed that GA pretreatment (100 µM) markedly suppressed F. nucleatum-induced phosphorylation of IκBα and p65, whereas GA alone had no effect on baseline NF-κB activity (Supplementary Figure S3). These results suggest that HMGB1 plays a role in F. nucleatum-triggered NF-κB activation. Second, we used BAY 11-7082 (10 µM pretreatment), an inhibitor of IκBα phosphorylation (commonly referred to as an NF-κB inhibitor). As shown in Supplementary Figure S4, BAY 11-7082 alone moderately reduced baseline proliferation, migration and invasion, consistent with the known role of constitutive NF-κB activity in maintaining basal cellular functions. Importantly, F. nucleatum infection significantly enhanced these malignant phenotypes, and this enhancement was completely abrogated by BAY 11-7082 pretreatment: the F. nucleatum + BAY group exhibited significantly lower proliferation, migration and invasion than the F. nucleatum alone group, reaching levels comparable to the BAY alone group. Collectively, these data delineate a sequential mechanism wherein F. nucleatum infection upregulates HMGB1, which in turn activates the NF-κB pathway, leading to selective secretion of pro-tumorigenic cytokines and malignant phenotypes; pharmacological inhibition of HMGB1 or NF-κB abrogates these effects, suggesting that this signaling axis is functionally involved in F. nucleatum-induced malignant phenotypes.

Discussion

Cervical cancer remains a significant global health burden. While high-risk human papillomavirus is its primary etiological agent, the complex tumor microenvironment, including the intratumoral microbiota, is increasingly recognized as a critical modulator of disease progression [7, 27]. Intriguingly, F. nucleatum, an emerging pathobiont firmly implicated in gastrointestinal carcinogenesis [28] and suggested to play a role in various gynecological conditions [19], has been poorly characterized in the context of cervical cancer. Herein, through an integrative approach encompassing bioinformatics, clinical validation, systematic functional assays, and mechanistic exploration, we establish F. nucleatum as a functionally active contributor to cervical carcinogenesis. We present a comprehensive chain of evidence demonstrating that F. nucleatum not only colonizes and invades cervical cancer cells but also drives key malignant phenotypes both in vitro and in vivo. Mechanistically, these effects are mediated, at least in part, by the activation of a specific pro-tumorigenic inflammatory axis centered on HMGB1 and the canonical NF-κB pathway.

Our study establishes a direct and functional link between F. nucleatum and cervical carcinogenesis, moving beyond associative observations to mechanistic causation. We first confirmed its clinical relevance, demonstrating consistent enrichment in tumor tissues across independent cohorts and a significant correlation with poorer patient survival, reinforcing its potential as a prognostic biomarker akin to its role in gastrointestinal cancers [29–31]. However, the microbiome cohort used for initial discovery was relatively small and imbalanced, particularly the limited number of normal controls (n = 3 in the TCMbio dataset). Therefore, these differential abundance results should be considered preliminary, and validation in larger independent cohorts is needed. Notably, the enrichment of F. nucleatum in cervical cancer was independently validated by qPCR in our own clinical cohort (30 cancer vs. 30 normal tissues), which is less susceptible to contamination artifacts, providing orthogonal support for the microbiome findings. Consistent with published studies showing that high intratumoral F. nucleatum abundance correlates with advanced FIGO stage, poor tumor differentiation, and worse survival in cervical cancer [18], our findings support the clinical relevance of F. nucleatum as a potential prognostic biomarker. Future large-scale prospective studies incorporating HPV genotyping, viral load, and treatment history are needed to further validate its independent prognostic significance.

A pivotal morphological finding from our study is the visualization of intracellular bacteria within cervical cancer cells via transmission electron microscopy. While TEM alone cannot specify the bacterial species, this observation provides the crucial ultrastructural evidence that cervical cancer cells can harbor internalized bacteria. Critically, in our functional co-culture assays, the internalization of live F. nucleatum into cervical cancer cells was directly captured by confocal live-cell imaging and quantified by post-invasion qPCR. Together, these complementary lines of evidence suggest that F. nucleatum can invade cervical cancer cells and may directly influence host cell signaling [28, 32, 33]. Nevertheless, we recognize that our evidence for intracellular localization remains largely descriptive. TEM cannot definitively identify the bacterial species, and while confocal imaging and gentamicin protection assays indicate internalization of live bacteria, we have not demonstrated long-term intracellular persistence, replication, or endosomal trafficking. Therefore, we refer to F. nucleatum as an intratumoral bacterium rather than an established intracellular pathobiont. Future studies using species-specific probes, immunogold labeling, or single-cell sequencing are needed to definitively characterize its intracellular lifestyle. Collectively, our work shifts the perspective of F. nucleatum in cervical cancer from an extracellular correlate to a functionally relevant intratumoral bacterium, opening new avenues for biomarker development and host-directed interventions.

The functional consequences of this intracellular colonization are profound and consistent. F. nucleatum robustly enhanced proliferation, migration, and invasion while inhibiting apoptosis in vitro. These pro-tumorigenic phenotypes were faithfully recapitulated in vivo, where intratumoral inoculation of F. nucleatum significantly accelerated xenograft growth. Of note, this intratumoral injection model bypasses the natural ascending infection route from the lower genital tract; therefore, it primarily reflects direct tumor-bacteria interactions rather than the full complexity of natural colonization. The inclusion of an antibiotic-pretreated control group effectively ruled out non-specific effects of the pretreatment regimen, strengthening the causal link between bacterial colonization and tumor promotion. This pattern of functional impact mirrors that reported for F. nucleatum in other malignancies [34, 35], where multiple studies have established that the bacterium accelerates cell proliferation, migration, invasion, and xenograft tumor growth. Nevertheless, we acknowledge that conditioned medium was not tested, and other anaerobic bacteria were not included as controls. Therefore, whether secreted bacterial factors contribute to the observed effects and whether the phenotypes are specific to F. nucleatum remain to be determined in future investigations.

Mechanistically, we delineated a novel signaling pathway through which F. nucleatum exerts its effects. Consistent with its known ability to induce the danger signal HMGB1 in other settings like gingival infections [36, 37], our study now demonstrates that F. nucleatum infection also potently upregulates HMGB1 in cervical cancer cells. This upregulation triggered the specific activation of the canonical NF-κB pathway, evidenced by IκBα degradation, an increased p-IκBα/IκBα ratio, p65 phosphorylation, and its nuclear translocation. Notably, this activation exhibited a remarkable downstream selectivity, significantly elevating the secretion of IL-6 and IL-8 but not TNF-α. This selective cytokine profile is biologically significant. IL-6 and IL-8 have been established as central drivers of cervical cancer progression [38]. Furthermore, they promote malignancy through distinct pathways: IL-6 primarily stimulates proliferation via STAT3 activation [39], whereas IL-8 facilitates angiogenesis and immune evasion by engaging the CXCR1/CXCR2 axis [40, 41]. By selectively inducing these specific cytokines, F. nucleatum may shape a chronic, pro-tumorigenic inflammatory niche that supports tumor growth and its own persistence.

The pathogenic mechanisms of F. nucleatum are highly context-dependent, exhibiting remarkable tissue specificity. In colorectal cancer, it often employs the FadA adhesin to activate Wnt/β-catenin signaling or engages TLR4 to trigger NF-κB activation [14, 24]. While NF-κB is a common inflammatory node activated by F. nucleatum across different cancers [32, 42], our study identifies a distinct upstream trigger and a selective downstream outcome in cervical cancer. We demonstrate that infection potently upregulates the DAMP molecule HMGB1, which is associated with the subsequent activation of the canonical NF-κB pathway and, notably, the selective induction of the pro-tumorigenic cytokines IL-6 and IL-8. This IL-6/IL-8 specific inflammatory signature distinguishes its mode of action in the cervical tumor microenvironment from that reported in the gut [43]. Although HPV status was not assessed in this study, we hypothesize that F. nucleatum may act as a co-factor that amplifies HPV-driven NF-κB activation and immune suppression, thereby accelerating disease progression. This speculation is supported by observations that F. nucleatum upregulates PD-L1 [44] and promotes M2-like macrophages [45], both of which can facilitate viral persistence. Furthermore, recent studies have identified Fusobacterium spp. as potential microbiome markers for HPV-positive cervical cancer [46], noting their increased abundance in HPV-infected women [47]. The interplay between HPV and the cervicovaginal microbiome is thought to create a dysbiotic environment that enhances viral persistence and promotes carcinogenesis [46]. Future studies incorporating HPV genotyping and functional co-infection models are warranted to definitively test this interaction. From a translational perspective, our findings point to one promising application: intratumoral F. nucleatum abundance may serve as a prognostic biomarker, given its correlation with aggressive disease features. The mechanistic elucidation of the HMGB1/NF-κB/IL-6/IL-8 axis provides a foundation for future studies aimed at understanding how this pathobiont contributes to cervical cancer progression.

Our study has several limitations. First, the sample size of tumor subgroups and normal tissues is relatively small, limiting broad generalizations. Second, while pharmacological inhibition of HMGB1 suggests its involvement in F. nucleatum-induced NF-κB activation, genetic knockdown or knockout experiments would be required to definitively establish causality; moreover, the specific bacterial virulence factors that upregulate HMGB1 remain unknown. Third, the intratumoral injection model does not fully recapitulate the natural ascending route of cervical microbial colonization, and the use of immunodeficient nude mice limits assessment of adaptive immune responses. Fourth, HPV status was not assessed, and multivariate survival analysis could not be performed due to limited sample size. Fifth, the role of F. nucleatum in modulating the immune microenvironment of cervical cancer warrants further investigation.

Conclusion

In summary, this study suggests a pathogenic axis in cervical cancer in which intratumoral colonization by F. nucleatum is associated with tumor progression, potentially through upregulation of HMGB1, activation of the canonical NF-κB pathway, and a selective pro-tumorigenic cytokine response (Fig. 7). These findings may contribute to our understanding of pathobiont-related microbial oncogenesis and point to possible opportunities for prognostic stratification and biotechnological targeted therapy.
