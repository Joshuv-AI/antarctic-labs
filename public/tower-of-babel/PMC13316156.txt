Antifungal Susceptibility of Rare Yeasts Causing Vulvovaginal Candidiasis in the Acidic Vaginal Environment
Authors: Fang Liu, Yanxia Sun, Rongfen Zhao, Lu Zhang, Shuwen Deng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo investigate the in vitro potency of 13 antifungals against emerging rare yeast species causing vulvovaginal candidiasis (VVC) under the influence of acidic pH.MethodsAntifungal susceptibility was determined according to CLSI M27/M44-ed3 with minor modifications. In total, 42 isolates of rare yeast were isolated from the vaginal samples based on molecular identification, including Saccharomyces cerevisiae (n=18); Clavispora lusitaniae (n=9); Cyberlindnera fabianii (n=4); Kodamaea ohmeri, Candida metapsilosis, and Nakaseomyces bracarensis (n=2 each); and Pichia norvegensis, Kluyveromyces marxianus, Diutina rugosa, Nakaseomyces nivariensis, and Candida africana (n=1 each).ResultsThe antifungal agents most affected by a lower pH were terconazole (3 two-fold dilutions higher, MIC >0.5–8 mg/L) for all yeast isolates, followed by amphotericin B (2 two-fold dilutions higher, MIC >2 mg/L) for 93% of isolates, nystatin (2 two-fold dilutions higher, MIC >4–8 mg/L) for 95% of isolates, and micafungin (2 two-fold dilutions higher, MIC >0.125–1 mg/L) for 50% of isolates, compared with those at pH 7.0, indicating a high percentage of low activity against the yeast species. Fluconazole, voriconazole, miconazole, and clotrimazole were the least affected, while anidulafungin, caspofungin, and 5-flucytosine were not affected significantly by pH, showing activity against most rare yeast species at both pH levels. Nakaseomyces bracarensis and N. nivariensis were the species affected most by the lower pH for all seven azoles tested, but they still remained susceptible to azoles.ConclusionThe noted shift toward rare yeasts could present a challenge to the clinical treatment of VVC. Considering the influence of acidic pH, our results reveal apparently drug-specific and genus-specific trends in antifungal susceptibility. The variability of rare yeasts with regard to antifungal susceptibility at acidic pH warrants further research on this topic.Keywords: in vitro antifungal potency, rare yeast species, vulvovaginal candidiasis, acidic pH

Introduction

Vulvovaginal candidiasis (VVC) affects millions of women annually.1 Although it represents a problem of global importance in public health, its exact incidence is unknown. Approximately one-third of vaginitis cases are caused by Candida spp.1 VVC is most commonly caused by Candida albicans, but can also be caused by non-albicans Candida species, including Nakaseomyces glabratus, Candida parapsilosis, Candida tropicalis, Pichia kudriavzevii, and others. However, rare yeasts other than the common Candida spp. have emerged as significant pathogens of VVC.2,3 Whether this is due to improved diagnostics or to an actual rise in uncommon yeasts emerging in the clinic, or both, remains unclear. Based on recent nomenclature and taxonomy, some species have been moved to different genera, such as Clavispora lusitaniae (synonym: Candida lusitaniae), Diutina rugosa (syn.: Candida rugosa), Kluyveromyces marxianus (syn.: Candida kefyr), and Pichia norvegensis (syn.: Candida norvegensis). These fungi are commonly encountered in the environment and are frequent colonizers of human skin and mucosal surfaces.4,5 These rare yeasts occur in a low percentage (<0.01%) as causative agents, but are highly polyphyletic.6 Infections by these agents are hard to manage owing to limited clinical experience and a lack of susceptibility data.7 Furthermore, the epidemiology of these rare and emerging infections has not been well studied, particularly with regard to VVC,7,8 although Stavrou et al9 reported that similar minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) values are found between phylogenetically closely related Saccharomycotina yeast species and appear to be clade specific to a large extent. Thus, phylogeny might provide a first indication for inferring the susceptibility patterns of rare yeasts observed in the clinic, and it may serve as a first guide for treatment choice, epidemiological cut-off values (ECVs), and clinical breakpoints (CBPs), as the international standard methods of CLSI and EUCAST have not yet been established for the susceptibility of rare yeast species to the majority of antifungal drugs. A few case reports have pointed to elevated MICs of at least one azole or of whole drug classes, such as in Candida ciferrii,7 Meyerozyma guilliermondii, Candida haemulonii, Candida inconspicua, and P. norvegensis.4,8

Moreover, since the normal pH of the vagina is 4–4.5, which remains unchanged during VVC,10 previous studies found that some Candida species that were susceptible to antifungals in vitro (pH 7.0) were resistant to the same drug in the acidic vaginal environment.11 Spitzer and Wiederhold12 measured antifungal potency at pH 7.0 and pH 4.5 of fluconazole, itraconazole, miconazole, clotrimazole, terconazole, and nystatin against 10 yeast species isolated from VVC, and found that antifungals reduced in vitro potency when tested at lower pH, particularly for the treatment of recurrent N. glabratus vaginitis. Thus, clinicians should recognize the limitations of in vitro susceptibility testing at pH 7.0. However, most studies focused on antifungal potency against selected yeast species (eg, C. albicans or N. glabratus) causing VVC in an acidic vaginal environment. Few data are available on rare emerging yeast species.

The aim of this study is to investigate the antifungal susceptibility of the rare emerging yeasts causing VVC to 13 antifungals in an acidic environment, to determine the extent to which the reduced effectiveness of these agents may contribute to the clinical failure of treatment.

Materials and Methods

Isolates and Identification

A total of 42 rare yeast isolates were recovered from patients with VVC in the gynecology clinic of the People’s Hospital of Suzhou New District from January to December 2021. Information on case definition, patient data, clinical information, and collection of vaginal samples has been reported in a previous publication.3 The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Ethical approval for the study was obtained from the Ethics Committee of the People’s Hospital of Suzhou New District (IRB ethics number: 2022023). All participants signed an informed consent form prior to the investigation.

All isolates were identified to the species level by sequencing the rDNA LSU (D1/D2) as described previously.3 GenBank accession numbers of D1/D2 sequences for the 42 isolates studied are listed in Supplementary Table S1.

Antifungal Susceptibility Testing

All isolates were tested for in vitro susceptibility to 13 antifungal drugs according to CLSI M27A4/M44-ed3.13,14 The antifungal drugs, comprising fluconazole (FLC), itraconazole (ITR), voriconazole (VRC), posaconazole (POS), terconazole (TEC), miconazole (MIC), clotrimazole (CLO), amphotericin B (AmB), 5-flucytosine (5-FC), nystatin (NYS), anidulafungin (ANF), and caspofungin (CAS), micafungin (MFG), were obtained from Sigma Aldrich. Stock solutions of each antifungal were prepared in dimethyl sulfoxide. Further dilutions were made using RPMI1640 and buffered with 0.165 M 4-morpholinepropanesulfonic acid (MOPS) (Sigma-Aldrich) to pH 7.0 and pH 4.5 to produce the final test medium (Table S2). Candida parapsilosis ATCC 22019 and P. kudriavzevii ATCC 6258 were used as control strains. Colonies were suspended in sterile saline, and the final inoculum concentration of the suspension was adjusted to 0.5–2.5×103 CFU/mL. The minimum inhibitory concentrations (MICs) at both pH 7.0 and pH 4.5 were read after 24 h incubation. The MIC range, geometric mean (GM), and MIC90 were calculated for each species when testing four or more isolates; otherwise, MIC range and GM were calculated for two isolates, or only MICs for one isolate.

The CBPs or ECVs of C. albicans set by CLSI served as a comparator for D. rugosa and Candida africana; CBPs of N. glabratus served as a comparator for N. nivariensis and N. bracarensis; and CBPs of P. kudriavzevii (=Candida krusei) served as a comparator for P. norvegensis, as these species are phylogenetically closely related to the respective species.9 The interpretive breakpoints used in this study are listed in Table S3. Because of the lack of CBPs and ECVs for rare yeast species, the terms sensitive and resistant are not relevant, thus, the respective terms susceptible and decreased susceptibility are used instead. Essential agreement (EA) was defined as a maximum difference of 2 two-fold dilution steps between two MIC values.9

Results

Identification of Rare Yeasts at Species Level

In our study, 42 isolates of rare yeasts were obtained from the vaginal samples, representing 11 species based on molecular identification. Saccharomyces cerevisiae (n=18) was the most common species, followed by Cl. lusitaniae (n=9), Cyberlindnera fabianii (n=4), Kodamaea ohmeri, Candida metapsilosis, and N. bracarensis (n=2 each), and P. norvegensis, Kl. marxianus, D. rugosa, N. nivariensis, and C. africana (n=1 each) (Figure 1).

The distribution of 11 rare yeast species recovered from vulvovaginal candidiasis.

Antifungal Susceptibility

Overall, the most dramatic changes in MIC values were observed for terconazole, for which most species had >3 two-fold dilution steps increasing at value of MIC90 at pH 4.5 compared with those at pH 7.0 (Tables 1, 2 and Figures 2, 3). For amphotericin B, eight species (ie, except for S. cerevisiae, D. rugosa, and K. ohmeri) had 2 two-fold dilution steps increasing at MIC value (>2–8 mg/L) at pH 4.5 compared with those at pH 7.0 (≤2 mg/L). Saccharomyces cerevisiae (MIC90 4/2 mg/L) and D. rugosa (MIC 2 mg/L) had high MIC values, while K. ohmeri (0.5 mg/L) had a low MIC, although those species had unchanged MICs at both pH levels. For nystatin, eight species (ie, except for N. nivariensis, C. africana, and D. rugosa) had ≥2 two-fold dilution steps increasing of MICs (>2–8 mg/L) at pH 4.5 compared with those at pH 7.0 (≤2 mg/L); S. cerevisiae (MIC90 16 mg/L) and P. norvegensis (MIC 8 mg/L) were the most affected, with >4 two-fold dilution steps more at lower pH. Notably, three species (N. nivariensis, C. africana, and D. rugosa) contained only one isolate, and did not change the MIC at both pH levels.

Overall, the azoles that were least affected by a lower pH were fluconazole, itroconazole, voriconazole, miconazole, and clotrimazole for most rare yeast species, except for N. bracarensis and N. nivariensis, with >2 two-fold dilution steps increasing at MIC value to all azoles tested.

Among the three echinocandins tested in this study, micafungin was the most frequently affected by lower pH against eight species (Cl. lusitaniae, Cy. fabianii, K. ohmeri, N. bracarensis, N. nivariensis, Kl. marxianus, C. africana, and P. norvegensis), which had >2 two-fold dilution steps increasing at MIC value at pH 4.5 compared with those at pH 7.0, while anidulafungin and caspofungin were not affected significantly by pH for all tested yeast species, and showed activity against most rare yeast species at both pH levels. By contrast, 5-flucytosine showed unchanged MIC against the rare yeast species, and had lower MICs against S. cerevisiae, C. bracarensis, and P. norvegensis at pH 4.5 compared with those at pH 7.0.

Based on the established ECV for S. cerevisiae, the majority of isolates displayed a stable MIC value at both pH levels and sensitivity to six azoles (all azoles except for terconazole), three echinocandins, and 5-flucytosine. However, three out of 18 isolates showed an MIC >0.5 mg/L to 5-flucytosine at pH 7.0, but this was reclassified as sensitive to 5-flucytosine owing to the decreased MIC (<0.5 mg/L) at pH 4.5. However, for amphotericin B, the rate of non-wild type (NWT) increased to 50% (nine of 18 isolates), while regarding the rate of decreased susceptibility, for nystatin, 22.2% isolates (four of 18 isolates) had MICs >4 mg/L, and for terconazole 94.4% (17 of 18 isolates) isolates had MICs >2 mg/L at pH 4.5 compared with those at pH 7.0 (Table 3).

Based on the established ECV of Cl. lusitaniae, all nine isolates showed sensitivity/WT to all 13 antifungals tested at pH 7.0. At an acidic pH, the majority of isolates had stable MICs to fluconazole, except for one isolate with an increased MIC (2 mg/L > ECV) as NWT. All nine isolates had ≥3 two-fold dilution steps increasing at MIC90 for itraconazole, posaconazole, terconazole, amphotericin B, and nystatin at pH 4.5, and the rate of NWT increased to 100% for amphotericin B; while regarding the rate of decreased susceptibility, 88.9% (eight of nine isolates) for posaconazole, 44.44% (four of nine isolates) for micafungin, and 33.3% isolates (three of nine isolates) had MICs >2 mg/L for terconazole, and one of the nine isolates had an MIC >4 mg/L for nystatin. However, all nine isolates of Cl. lusitaniae had unchanged MICs to voriconazole, miconazole, clotrimazole, anidulafungin, caspofungin, and 5-flucytosine at both pH levels (Table 3).

Four isolates of Cy. fabianii had 2 two-fold dilution steps increasing at MIC value to terconazole (MIC90 8 mg/L), amphotericin B (MIC90 4 mg/L), micafungin (MIC90 1 mg/L), and nystatin (MIC90 8 mg/L) at pH 4.5 compared with those at pH 7.0.

Nakaseomyces bracarensis (n=2) and N. nivariensis (n=1) were the species affected most by the lower pH for all seven azoles tested, with an increase in MIC >2 two-fold dilution steps at pH 4.5 compared with those at pH 7.0. However, these isolates remained susceptible to azoles with increased MIC at pH 4.5 (Tables 1 and 2). All three isolates were sensitive to micafungin at pH 7.0, but two of them had an MIC (0.5 mg/L) above the CBP of N. glabratus (0.25 mg/L) at pH 4.5. All three isolates appeared to be sensitive to anidulafungin and caspofungin, with a stable MIC at both pH levels. The remaining species were affected less by lower pH for the antifungals tested, except for terconazole, amphotericin B, nystatin, and micafungin (Tables 1–3 and Figures 2, 3).

Discussion

The current study has highlighted the occurrence of a large number of rare yeasts in VVC samples, mainly from Saccharomycotina, with high diversity based on recent phylogenetic relationships.13 A report reviewing 10 years of studies related to the species distribution of causative agents of VVC produced similar findings, that the spectrum of non-albicans yeast causing VVC has shifted significantly.2 Considering the influence of acidic pH, our MIC data reveal apparently drug-specific and genus-specific trends in antifungal susceptibility. A high percentage of low sensitivity to terconazole, nystatin, and amphotericin B was established in an acidic environment (Tables 1–3 and Figures 2, 3). Our results are comparable to those of a previous study by Spitzer and Wiederhold,12 who found that terconazole was the agent most affected by lower pH with regard to C. albicans, N. glabratus, and eight other yeast species. Previous studies11,12 reported that the activity of polyene amphotericin B and nystatin was profoundly affected by pH for C. albicans and N. glabratus, with a dramatic increase in MIC90 at lower pH. In the present study, the 42 rare yeast isolates tested displayed the same trend of high MICs for terconazole, amphotericin B, and nystatin as those for common yeast species, suggesting a drug-specific trend. Danby et al11 reported that MIC90 values for 5-flucytosine were unchanged for N. glabratus, but a dramatic increase in MIC was seen for C. albicans when tested at lower pH. Here, as observed in our study, the opposite effect was seen in 5-flucytosine for all rare yeast isolates tested, for which the MICs tended to be stable or even lower at the acidic pH. Among the three echinocandins tested in this study, caspofungin and anidulafungin were not affected significantly by lower pH, showing activity against the rare yeast species at both pH levels, which is a similar result to that reported by Danby et al,11 that caspofungin retained a stable MIC and activity against C. albicans and N. glabratus isolates with a decrease in pH. However, a new finding in the present study was that micafungin, among the three echinocandins, was affected the most by the lower pH of 4.5 (Tables 1–3 and Figure 3). Nevertheless, additional studies need to be performed to evaluate the in vivo response to echinocandins as a topical compound.

Saccharomyces cerevisiae has been recently established as a causative agent of VVC, and has shown an increasing trend.15 Our data indicated that S. cerevisiae was the species affected least by acidic pH among all the yeast species tested, and was susceptible to most of the antifungal agents, with a stable MIC values at both pH levels, except for terconazole, polyene amphotericin B, and nystatin, which showed remarkable increases in MIC90, resulting in the rate of NWT being up to 50% for amphotericin B; the rate of decreased susceptibility was 22.2% for nystatin and 94.4% for terconazole at pH 4.5 (Table 3). Notably, our results at pH 7.0 align with those reported by Górzyńska et al,16 who found that clinical isolates of S. cerevisiae from clinical samples (including genital tract samples) were mostly susceptible to commonly used antifungal drugs. With vaginal isolates, the effect of acidic pH on selected drugs, such as amphotericin B, nystatin, and terconazole, may be a problem and therefore should be investigated further. In the case of 5-flucytosine, no interpretative criteria for any yeast species are included in the current CLSI/EUCAST recommendations. Previous research has suggested a value of ≤0.5 mg/L for susceptible strains, which was the criterion applied in this study, leading to three of the isolates (MIC range >0.5 mg/L) being less susceptible at pH 7.0; the opposite effect was observed at pH 4.5, where all three isolates were reclassified as sensitive (MIC range ≤0.5 mg/L) (Table 3).

Clavispora lusitaniae is a vaginal commensal17 and has been isolated from vagina as a causative agent of VVC, accounting for around 5% of non-albicans yeast.18–20 Clavispora lusitaniae was the second most prevalent rare yeast causing vaginitis in this study. This species is well known as being resistant to treatment with amphotericin B because of clinical failures associated with Cl. lusitaniae despite primary in vitro sensitivity to amphotericin B.21 A study reported that C. lusitaniae showed a high rate of intrinsic resistance to amphotericin B, so susceptibility testing was not required.22 In our study, nine isolates of Cl. lusitaniae exhibited low MIC values to all 13 antifungals, including amphotericin B, tested at pH 7.0, while the acidic pH increased the MICs of several antifungals (posaconazole, terconazole, amphotericin B, and micafungin), resulting in a high percentage of NWT isolates to amphotericin B (100%), posaconazole (77.8%), and micafungin (44.4%) (Tables 1–3 and Figures 2, 3). In addition, one isolate was classified as NWT/lower susceptibility to fluconazole and nystatin, respectively. There is no valid CLSI CBP for less prevalent yeast species, such as S. cerevisiae and Cl. lusitaniae. In these cases, the ECVs can facilitate the detection of the emergence of in vitro resistance, but do not predict clinical response.

Previous studies reported that isolates of Cy. fabiani, recovered mainly from urine, displayed no resistance to most antifungals.23 However, our study found a significantly reduced susceptibility at pH 4.5 to terconazole (MIC90 8 mg/L), amphotericin B (MIC90 4 mg/L), micafungin (MIC90 1 mg/L), and nystatin (MIC90 8 mg/L).

The significantly reduced susceptibility of N. glabratus to azoles at lower pH was reported by Danby et al11 and Spitzer and Wiederhold.12 In our study, N. bracarensis and N. nivariensis, which are phylogenetically closely related to N. glabratus, were the species most affected by a lower pH with regard to susceptibility to azoles, with increased MIC values as well, but remained susceptible to all azoles (Tables 1, 2 and Figure 2). Therefore, a rapid and efficient identification at species level would be clinically important to decide whether azoles would be a suitable treatment for members of the Nakaseomyces clade, especially members of the N. glabratus complex.24

Most of the data on the antifungal susceptibility of K. ohmeri isolates are derived from individual case reports. Since K. ohmeri exhibits variable susceptibility to fluconazole, in agreement with the literature,23 two isolates in our study showed an elevated MIC to posaconazole (0.125/0.25 mg/L), one of the two isolates also had a higher MIC to fluconazole (8 mg/L) at pH 7.0, and an increased MIC to fluconazole (16 mg/L), micafungin (0.5 mg/L), terconazole (8 mg/L), and nystatin (8 mg/L) at pH 4.5. Thus, rapid and accurate identification of K. ohmeri, as well as timely antifungal susceptibility testing at pH 4.5, are critical to enhance VVC patient care.

Candida metapsilosis is one of clinically relevant members of the C. parapsilosis complex, belonging to the azole- and amphotericin B-sensitive clade of lodderomyces, as reported by Stavrou et al.9 In our study, two isolates of C. metapsilosis from the vaginal tract showed similar results at pH 7.0, but at pH 4.5 this species appears to be resistant to amphotericin B (4 mg/L) owing to the drug-specific effect of pH.

Pichia norvegensis, a close relative of P. kudriavzevii (=C. krusei) in the Pichia clade,9 is known to exhibit high MICs to fluconazole,25 itraconazole, and posaconazole,5,26 and seems to be more frequently isolated from human samples.13,27 In our study, one isolate of P. norvegensis displayed a significant influence of acidic pH, with higher MIC values to fluconazole (32 mg/L), three echinocandins (0.125–1 mg/L), nystatin (8 mg/L), and amphotericin B (8 mg/L) (Table 2).

For the other species, it is hard to draw clear conclusions from only one isolate, which is a limitation in our study. Owing to the lack of the breakpoints for these rare yeast species, interpretation of these data and the clinical relevance of increased MICs are open to question. Nevertheless, 42 isolates of rare yeasts could indicate a basic trend regarding the influence of the acidic vaginal environment on antifungal susceptibility of rare yeast species causing VVC. Further studies are necessary, with more samples from different hospitals, to investigate the antifungal potency of rare yeast species causing VVC at acidic pH.

Conclusion

The current study has underscored the large number of uncommon yeast species from diverse taxonomic groups that can be associated with clinical samples. Our MIC data reveal apparently drug-specific trends in antifungal susceptibility, with a high percentage of low sensitivity to terconazole, nystatin, and amphotericin B in an acidic environment, while clotrimazole, miconazole, and 5-flucytosine were the most efficient agents in vitro against rare vaginal yeast species. Further investigations assessing the clinical utility of antifungal susceptibility testing should take these findings into consideration.
