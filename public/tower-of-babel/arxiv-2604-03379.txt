Coxeter and Schubert combinatorics of μ \mu –Involutions
Jack Chen-An Chou, Zachary Hamaker

Abstract
. The variety of complete quadrics is the wonderful compactification of G ​ L n / O n GL_{n}/O_{n} and admits a cell decomposition into Borel orbits indexed by combinatorial objects called μ \mu –involutions. We study Coxeter–theoretic properties of μ \mu –involutions with results including a combinatorial description for their atoms, an exchange lemma, and transposition-like operators that characterize their Bruhat order. The corresponding orbit closures can be realized inside the flag variety. In this setting, we study the cohomology representatives of these orbits, which are, up to a scalar, the μ \mu –involution Schubert polynomials. We expand μ \mu –involution Schubert polynomials as a multiplicity-free sum of ν \nu –involution Schubert polynomials when ν \nu refines μ \mu and provide recurrences analogous to Monk’s rule for Schubert polynomials.



1.
Introduction

The complete flag variety
\text{FL}_{n}=GL_{n}/B
has a natural stratification given by Borel orbit closures, indexed by permutations, whose classes form a basis for its cohomology ring.
This ring is the modern setting for Schubert calculus, an area of algebraic geometry that studies enumerative aspects of intersection theory.
Schubert polynomials, introduced by Lascoux and Schützenberger
[
20
]
, represent the cohomology classes of these orbit closures and provide a combinatorial model for this theory.
Their rich structure can be seen through the lens of geometry, representation theory, and combinatorics.

Significant effort has been devoted to extending Schubert calculus beyond the setting of the Schubert stratification for
\text{Fl}_{n}
.
One important such extension is the symmetric space given by orthogonal group orbit closures on
\text{Fl}_{n}
, which are indexed by involutions in the symmetric group.
These were initially studied by Richardson and Springer
[
23
,
24
]
, with polynomial representatives introduced by Wyser and Yong in
[
28
]
.
These representatives, divided by a predictable power of 2, are the
involution Schubert polynomials
.
A general theorem of Brion
[
5
]
shows involution Schubert polynomials are multiplicity-free sums of Schubert polynomials.
The terms in this sum, which we call
atoms
, have a combinatorial description due to Can, Joyce and Wyser
[
6
]
(see also
[
13
]
).
Hamaker, Marberg, and Pawlowski
[
15
]
demonstrate a recurrence relation on involution Schubert polynomials analogous to Monk’s rule.

Orthogonal orbits of
\text{Fl}_{n}
can be viewed as Borel orbits on
GL_{n}/O_{n}
.
This latter space is not compact, but has a wonderful compactification as the variety of complete quadrics
\text{Q}_{n}

[
9
]
.
In this paper, we study the Borel orbits of
\text{Q}_{n}
and their realizations as subvarieties of
\text{Fl}_{n}
.
Passing from
GL_{n}/O_{n}
to
\text{Q}_{n}
introduces boundary strata corresponding to partial degenerations of a quadric, and these strata are indexed by compositions of
n
.
In the stratum indexed by
\mu=(\mu_{1},\dots,\mu_{k})
the Borel orbits correspond to
H_{\mu}
orbits of
\text{Fl}_{n}
where
H_{\mu}
is the semidirect product of
O_{\mu_{1}}\times\dots\times O_{\mu_{k}}
and the parabolic subgroup
P_{\mu}\subseteq GL_{n}
.
These orbits are indexed by
\mu
–involutions
, which are permutations
\pi
expressible as a concatenation of blocks
B_{1},\dots,B_{k}
with
|B_{i}|=\mu_{i}
such that each
B_{i}
has the relative order of an involution.
Since
H_{\mu}
interpolates between
O_{n}
when
\mu=(n)
and
B
when
\mu=(1^{n})
,
\mu
–involutions interpolate between involutions and permutations.

Orbit closure containment defines a Bruhat order on
\mu
–involutions as
\mu
varies, which is a special case of a more general family of orders studied in
[
26
]
.
For fixed
\mu
, there is also a weak order studied in
[
8
]
, which determines analogues of reduced expressions for
\mu
–involutions.
In
[
1
]
, the authors show Bruhat order on
\mu
–involutions is determined by subword containment (see Theorems
2.10
and
2.11
).
Using this realization, we provide a novel characterization of Bruhat order on
\mu
–involutions using transposition-like operators
t^{\mu}_{ab}
(see Section
3.3
and specifically Theorem
3.11
).

The Brion results extend to this setting, so the classes of
H_{\mu}
orbit closures in
\text{Fl}_{n}
can be represented as sums of Schubert polynomials.
Again rescaling by a predictable power of 2, for each
\mu
–involution
\pi
we obtain the
\mu
–involution Schubert polynomial

\mathfrak{S}^{\mu}_{\pi}
as a multiplicity-free sum of Schubert polynomials.
The set of atoms in this expansion is denoted
\mathcal{A}_{\mu}(\pi)
.
These polynomials can also be defined using divided difference operators applied to
\mathfrak{S}^{\mu}_{w_{0}}
, which is computed geometrically as a product of monomials and binomials in
[
7
]
or as a sum over
\mathcal{A}_{\mu}(w_{0})
in
[
8
]
.
See
[
18
]
for a summary of these results.

Our first main result is a combinatorial description of
\mathcal{A}_{\mu}(\pi)
, generalizing the description of
\mathcal{A}_{\mu}(w_{0})
from
[
8
]
.
The definition of atoms for involutions can be extended to blocks, with
\mathcal{A}(B)
the set of block atoms for
B
(see Section
3.1
for details).
The description is more easily stated in terms of inverse atoms, the set of which is
\mathcal{A}_{\mu}^{-1}(\pi)
.

Theorem 1.1

(= Theorem
3.1
)
.

For
\pi
a
\mu
–involution with blocks
B_{1},\dots,B_{k}
,

\mathcal{A}_{\mu}^{-1}(\pi)=\{Q_{1}Q_{2}\dots Q_{k}:Q_{i}\in\mathcal{A}^{-1}(B_{i})\text{ for }i\in[k]\}.

where
Q_{1}Q_{2}\dots Q_{k}
is the concatenation of
Q_{1},Q_{2},\dots,Q_{k}
.

Our proof of Theorem
1.1
adapts the proof from
[
8
]
to this more general setting.
As an application of this theorem, we expand
\mu
–involution Schubert polynomials as a multiplicity-free sum of
\nu
–involution Schubert polynomials when
\nu
refines
\mu
(see Theorem
3.7
).
This result generalizes Theorem
1.1
and allows for an improved description of Bruhat order for
\mu
–involutions when
\mu
varies (see Corollary
3.6
).

The other main result of this paper is a family of transition equations for
\mu
–involution Schubert polynomials, which is closely related to Monk’s rule for Schubert polynomials.
The formula relies on the transposition operators
t_{ab}^{\mu}
we define that describe the strong Bruhat cover relations of
\mu
–involutions.

Theorem 1.2

(= Theorem
3.18
)
.

Let
\tau
be a
\mu
involution and
(i\geq j)
a cycle in one of its blocks,

\displaystyle 2^{-\delta(i,j)}(x_{i}+x_{j})\mathfrak{S}_{\tau}^{\mu}=\sum_{\sigma\in\Phi(\tau;i,j)}\mathfrak{S}_{\sigma}^{\mu}-\sum_{\sigma\in\Psi(\tau;i,j)}\mathfrak{S}_{\sigma}^{\mu}\,,

where

\displaystyle\Phi(\tau;i,j)=\{t^{\mu}_{ab}(\tau)\neq\tau\,:\,a\in\{i,j\}\},\quad\Psi(\tau;i,j)=\{t^{\mu}_{ab}(\tau)\neq\tau\,:\,b\in\{i,j\}\}.

A key technical tool in our proof of Theorem
1.2
is a novel exchange lemma like result for reduced expressions of
\mu
–involutions (see Theorem
3.16
) generalizing an analogous result for involutions from
[
15
]
.

2.
Background

We first introduce some notation. Let
[n]=\{1,2,\dots,n\}
. For the rest of the paper, we will use
w,u,v
to denote permutations,
y,z
to denote involutions,
\pi,\tau,\sigma
to denote
\mu
-involutions, and
\mu,\nu
to denote compositions. Recall
\mu=(\mu_{1},\dots,\mu_{k})
is a
composition
of
n
with
length

\ell(\mu)=k
, denoted
\mu\vDash n
, if
\sum_{i=1}^{k}\mu_{i}=n
and
\mu_{i}\in[n]
for all
i
.
For
\mu,\nu\vDash n
, we say
\mu

refines

\nu
if

\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{j}\mu_{i}:j\in[\ell(\mu)]\right\}\supseteq\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{j}\nu_{i}:j\in[\ell(\nu)]\right\}.

For example,
(3,2,3)
refines
(5,3)
,
(3,5)
and
(8)
.

2.1.
Permutations

For
A\subseteq\mathbb{N}
finite, let
S_{A}
be the set of permutations of
A
and
S_{n}=S_{[n]}
.
We typically write permutations in one-line notation— for
Q\in S_{A}
write
Q=Q(1)\dots Q(k)
with
Q(i)\in A
distinct for each
i\in[k]
.
The
standardization
of
Q
is the permutation
\mathrm{st}(Q)\in S_{|A|}
whose one-line notation has the same relative order as
Q
.
For
Q\in S_{A}
and
a\in A
, let
Q^{-1}(a)
be the index
i
so that
Q(i)=a
.
Then the
relative value
of
a
in
Q
is
r_{Q}(a)=\mathrm{st}(Q)(Q^{-1}(a))
.
For example, with
Q=63274
we have
\mathrm{st}(Q)=42153
so
Q^{-1}(4)=5
and
r_{Q}(4)=3
.

When
n
is clear, let
e=12\dots n
and
w_{0}=n\dots 21
be the identity and reverse permutations in
S_{n}
.
Let
t_{ij}
denote the transposition
(i,j)
and
s_{i}=(i,i{+}1)
.
Recall for
w\in S_{n}
that
wt_{ij}
is the same as
w
except that
w(i)
and
w(j)
are swapped, while
t_{ij}w
and
w
are the same except the positions containing
i
and
j
are swapped.
For example, with
w=4231
we have
wt_{13}=3241
and
t_{13}w=4213
.

Viewing permutations as functions,
S_{n}
is a Coxeter group with presentation

S_{n}=\langle s_{1},\dots,s_{n-1}\mid s_{i}^{2}=1,s_{i}s_{j}=s_{j}s_{i}\ \mathrm{for}\ |i-j|>1,\ s_{i}s_{i+1}s_{i}=s_{i+1}s_{i}s_{i+1}\ \mathrm{for}\ i\in[n-2]\rangle.

For
w\in S_{n}
, an expression
w=s_{a_{1}}\dots s_{a_{p}}
is
reduced
if it is of minimal length with associated
reduced word

\textbf{a}=(a_{1},\dots,a_{p})
.
The
length

\ell(w)
is then the length of a reduced word for
w
.
Let
\mathcal{R}(w)
be the set of reduced words for
w
.
Expressions for
w
satisfy:

Lemma 2.1

(Deletion Property,
[
4
]
*Prop. 1.4.7)
.

For
w=s_{a_{1}}\dots s_{a_{p}}\in S_{n}
so that the word
(a_{1},\dots,a_{p})
is not reduced, there exists
k,m\in[p]
so that
w=s_{a_{1}}\dots\widehat{s_{a_{k}}}\dots\widehat{s_{a_{m}}}\dots s_{a_{p}}
.

We make extensive use of two partial orders on
S_{n}
.
The first is
(strong) Bruhat order

(S_{n},\leq)
, where
v\leq w
if every
\textbf{a}\in\mathcal{R}(w)
has a subword in
\mathcal{R}(v)
.
The second is
weak (Bruhat) order

(S_{n},\leq_{W})
where
v\leq w
if some
\textbf{a}\in\mathcal{R}(w)
has a prefix in
\mathcal{R}(v)
.
Both partial orders are graded with rank function
\ell
.
The cover relations for these partial orders are
v\lessdot w
if
w=vt_{ij}
and
\ell(w)=\ell(v)+1
and
v\lessdot_{W}w
if
w=vs_{i}>w
for some
i\in[n{-}1]
.

A
parabolic subgroup
of
S_{n}
is one generated by a subset of the simple transpositions.
In particular, for
\mu=(\mu_{1},\dots,\mu_{k})\vDash n
we have the parabolic subgroup
S_{\mu}=S_{\mu_{1}}\times\dots\times S_{\mu_{k}}
; here we identify
S_{\mu_{i}}
with
\langle s_{m_{i}+1},\dots,s_{m_{i}+\mu_{i}-1}\rangle
where
m_{i}=\mu_{1}+\dots+\mu_{i-1}
(so
m_{1}=0)
.
The
parabolic quotient

S^{\mu}
is the set of minimal length coset representatives for
S_{\mu}
.
We have:

Proposition 2.2

(
[
4
]
*Prop. 2.4.4)
.

For
\mu\vDash n
and
w\in S_{n}
there exist unique
u\in S_{\mu}
,
v\in S^{\mu}
so that
w=uv
.
Furthermore
\ell(w)=\ell(u)+\ell(v)
.

The
0–Hecke monoid
is the unique monoid
(S_{n},\circ)
satisfying

w\circ s_{i}=\begin{cases}ws_{i}&ws_{i}>w,\\
w&ws_{i}<w.\end{cases}

This monoid is obtained from the Coxeter presentation of
S_{n}
by replacing the relation
s_{i}^{2}=1
with
s_{i}^{2}=s_{i}
.
We say
\textbf{a}=(a_{1},\dots,a_{p})
is a
word
for
w
if
w=s_{a_{1}}\circ\dots\circ s_{a_{p}}
, and let
\mathcal{H}_{k}(w)
be the set of all words of length
k
for
w
.
Note
\mathcal{R}(w)=\mathcal{H}_{\ell(w)}(w)
.

2.2.
Involutions

Let
\mathcal{I}_{n}=\{y\in S_{n}:y^{-1}=y\}
be the set of involutions in
S_{n}
.
For
y\in\mathcal{I}_{n}
, the
cycles
of
y
are elements in the set of ordered pairs

\displaystyle\mathrm{Cyc}(y)=\{(j,i)\in[n]\times[n]\,|\,y(i)=j\,\text{ and }i\leq j\}.

Note that fixed points correspond to terms
(i,i)\in\mathrm{Cyc}(y)
.
We say the cycles
(i,j),(k,l)\in\mathrm{Cyc}(y)

cross
if
j<l<i<k
or
l<j<i<k
and
nest
if
j<l\leq k<i
or
l<j\leq i<k
.

For
w\in S_{n}
and
y\in\mathcal{I}_{n}
, the 0–Hecke monoid acts on
\mathcal{I}_{n}
by
y\,\hat{\circ}\,w=w^{-1}\circ y\circ w

[
23
]
.
Bruhat order restricts to a graded poset
(\mathcal{I}_{n},\leq^{\mathcal{I}})
with rank function

\hat{\ell}(y)=\frac{1}{2}(\ell(y)+n-|\mathrm{Cyc}(y)|).

The
weak order for involutions

(\mathcal{I}_{n},\leq^{\mathcal{I}}_{W})
is the partial order with
y\leq^{\mathcal{I}}_{W}z
if
z=y\,\hat{\circ}\,w
for some
w\in S_{n}
.
In
[
17
]
, Incitti characterized the cover relations of
\leq^{\mathcal{I}}
combinatorially in terms of operators
t^{\mathcal{I}}_{ij}
, which mimic the action of transpositions on permutations.
For
y\in\mathcal{I}_{n}
with
(b,a),(d,c)\in\mathrm{Cyc}(y)
and
i,j\in\{a,b,c,d\}
, let
t^{\mathcal{I}}_{ij}(y)
differ from
y
on the set
\{a,b,c,d\}
by modifying
\mathrm{Cyc}(y)
at these values as depicted in Figure
1
and fixing
y
otherwise.
Then
y\lessdot^{\mathcal{I}}z
if and only if
\hat{\ell}(z)=\hat{\ell}(y)+1
and
z=t^{\mathcal{I}}_{ij}(y)
for some
i<j
.

[
15
]
and differ slightly from
[

2.4.
Schubert polynomials and transition equations

Schubert polynomials, introduced by Lascoux and Schützenberger, represent the cohomology classes of Schubert varieties in the flag variety
[
20
]
. They are a family of polynomials indexed by permutations.
For
w\in S_{n}
, the Schubert polynomial of
w
, denoted
\mathfrak{S}_{w}
, can be defined recursively using divided difference operators.

Let
S_{n}
act on the polynomial ring
\mathbb{Z}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]
by permuting variable subscripts.
For
i\in[n]
, the
divided difference operator
\partial_{i}
is defined by

\displaystyle\partial_{i}(f):=\frac{f-s_{i}(f)}{x_{i}-x_{i+1}}.

Note that
\partial_{i}(f)\in\mathbb{Z}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]
and
s_{i}\circ\partial_{i}(f)=\partial_{i}(f)
, that is
\partial_{i}(f)
is symmetric in the variables
x_{i}
and
x_{i+1}
.
The
Schubert polynomial
\mathfrak{S}_{w}
is defined by

Computing the product of Schubert polynomials combinatorially is an important open problem.

For

\displaystyle\mathfrak{S}_{s_{r}}\mathfrak{S}_{w}=(x_{1}+\cdots+x_{r})\mathfrak{S}_{w}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k\leq r<l\\
w\lessdot wt_{k,l}\end{subarray}}\mathfrak{S}_{wt_{k,l}}\,.

Transition equations are similar recurrences on Schubert polynomials.
They are direct consequences of Monk’s rule, and are often more useful in computation.

Theorem 2.14
.

For

\displaystyle x_{r}\mathfrak{S}_{v}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}r<s\\

v\lessdot(vt_{h,r})\end{subarray}}\mathfrak{S}_{vt_{h,r}}.

Remark 2.15

We do not adopt this convention.

In
[
28
]
, Wyser and Yong introduced polynomial representatives
\Upsilon_{y}^{\mathrm{O}_{n}}
indexed by
y\in\mathcal{I}_{n}
for cohomology classes of orthogonal group orbit closures in
\text{Fl}_{n}
.
For
y\in\mathcal{I}_{n}
the
involution Schubert polynomial
, originally defined in terms of divided differences, is

\mathfrak{S}_{y}^{\mathcal{I}}=\sum_{w\in\mathcal{A}(y)}\mathfrak{S}_{w},

following work of Brion
[
5
]
.
Involution Schubert polynomials are rescaled versions of Wyser and Yong’s polynomial representatives, satisfying
\Upsilon_{y}^{\mathrm{O}_{n}}=2^{\kappa(y)}\mathfrak{S}_{y}^{\mathcal{I}}
, where
\kappa(y)
is the number of two cycles in
y
.
We work with
\mathfrak{S}_{y}^{\mathcal{I}}
rather than
\Upsilon^{\mathrm{O}_{n}}_{Y}
to simplify our computations, as the power of 2 can easily be added back to recover the geometric significance.

Involution Schubert polynomials satisfy an analogue of the transition equation.

Theorem 2.16

(
[
15
]
*Thm. 1.5)
.

Let
y\in\mathcal{I}_{\infty}
and
(p,q)\in\mathrm{Cyc}(y)
then

\displaystyle 2^{-\delta(p,q)}(x_{p}+x_{q})\mathfrak{S}_{y}^{\mathcal{I}}=\sum_{z\in\Phi^{+}(y,q)}\mathfrak{S}_{z}^{\mathcal{I}}-\sum_{z\in\Phi^{-}(y,p)}\mathfrak{S}_{z}^{\mathcal{I}}

where

\displaystyle\Phi^{+}(y,q)=\{\,z\in\mathcal{I}_{\mathbb{Z}}:y\lessdot_{I}z\text{ and }z=t_{qj}^{\mathcal{I}}(y)\text{ for an integer }j>q\,\}

\displaystyle\Phi^{-}(y,p)=\{\,z\in\mathcal{I}_{\mathbb{Z}}:y\lessdot_{I}z\text{ and }z=t_{ip}^{\mathcal{I}}(y)\text{ for an integer }i<p\,\}

As mentioned in the introduction, there is a parallel story for the cohomology representatives of
H_{\mu}
-orbit closures in
\text{Fl}_{n}
.
Up to rescaling, these representatives are the
\mu
–involution Schubert polynomials
, defined for

as

\displaystyle\mathfrak{S}_{\tau}^{\mu}=\sum_{w\in\mathcal{A}_{\mu}(\tau)}\mathfrak{S}_{w}.

To recover the geometric meaning, one multiplies
\mathfrak{S}^{\mu}_{\tau}
by

where
\kappa(\tau)
is the number of 2–cycles in
\tau

Note

can also be defined in terms of divided difference operators

3.1.
Atoms of
\mu
–involutions

For
\mu
a composition, we now characterize the
\mu
–atoms of
\pi\in\mathcal{I}_{\mu}
in terms of their inverses. Recall that
\mathcal{A}(y)
and
\mathcal{A}^{-1}(y)
denote the set of atoms and inverse atoms of an involution
y
respectively. These definitions can be naturally extended to block involutions. Let
A\subset\mathbb{N}
be finite and
B\in S_{A}
be a block involution.
The
block atoms
for

are elements in the set
\mathcal{A}(B)=\{Q\in S_{A}:\mathrm{st}(Q)\in\mathcal{A}(\mathrm{st}(B)\},

that is the permutations in
S_{A}
whose standardization is an atom for
\mathrm{st}(B)
.
Similarly, the
inverse block atoms
for
B
are elements in the set
\mathcal{A}^{-1}(B)=\{Q\in S_{A}:\mathrm{st}(Q)\in\mathcal{A}^{-1}(\mathrm{st}(B))\}
, that is the permutations in
S_{A}
whose standardization is an inverse atom for
\mathrm{st}(B)
.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
\mu\vDash n
and
\pi\in\mathcal{I}_{\mu}
with blocks
B_{1},\dots,B_{k}
.
Then

\mathcal{A}_{\mu}^{-1}(\pi)=\{Q_{1}Q_{2}\dots Q_{k}:Q_{i}\in\mathcal{A}^{-1}(B_{i})\text{ for }i\in[k]\}.

where
Q_{1}Q_{2}\dots Q_{k}
is the concatenation of
Q_{1},Q_{2},\dots,Q_{k}
.

Proof.

For
w\in\mathcal{A}^{-1}_{\mu}(\pi)
, write
w=uv
with
u\in S_{\mu}
and
v\in S^{\mu}
using Proposition
2.2
.
Then
w^{-1}=v^{-1}u^{-1}\in\mathcal{A}_{\mu}(\pi)
with
u^{-1}\in S_{\mu}
.
Necessarily,
e\,\circ_{\mu}\,u
cannot swap values between blocks, so
u^{-1}\in\mathcal{A}_{\mu}(\tau)
where
\tau
has blocks
B^{\prime}_{1},\dots,B^{\prime}_{k}
.
Since
\,\circ_{\mu}\,
acts on the left when swapping values between blocks, we see
\tau\,\circ_{\mu}\,v^{-1}
acts by swapping values between blocks in the same way as
v
.
Therefore
\ell(v)=\ell^{B}_{\mu}(\pi)
, hence
\ell(u)=\ell^{\mathcal{I}}_{\mu}(\pi)
.
Since
w^{-1}
is a
\mu
–atom for
\pi
and
v
only swaps values between blocks, necessarily
\mathrm{st}(B_{i})=\mathrm{st}(B_{i}^{\prime})
for all
i\in[k]
.
Then
u
splits into inverse block atoms
Q_{1}^{\prime},\dots,Q_{k}^{\prime}
with
Q_{i}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}^{-1}(B_{i}^{\prime})
and
v
permutes the values of each
Q^{\prime}_{i}
to produce
Q_{i}\in\mathcal{A}^{-1}(B_{i})
.
A permutation of the form
Q_{1}\dots Q_{k}
with each
Q_{i}\in\mathcal{A}(B_{i})
necessarily has the form

with
u\in\mathcal{A}_{\mu}(\tau)
, so the opposite containment holds.
∎

3.4.
Transitions for
\mu
–involutions

We conclude this section by proving transition equations for
\mu
–involution Schubert polynomials.
To do so, we require several definitions.
Let
\mu^{\prime}=(\mu_{1},\dots,\mu_{k},1)
be the composition obtained from appending
1
at the end of
\mu
.
Define
\tau^{\prime}
by appending
n+1
as a fixed point to
\tau
, so
\tau^{\prime}\in\mathcal{I}_{\mu^{\prime}}
.
In the remainder of this section, we identify
\tau
with
\tau^{\prime}
as they have the same set of atoms by Theorem
3.1
.
This identification is required to obtain all the necessary Bruhat covers in the subsequent definitions, as demonstrated in Example
3.20
.

For
\mu\vDash n
,
\tau\in\mathcal{I}_{\mu}
, and
i,j
not necessarily distinct, let

\displaystyle\Phi(\tau;i,j)=\{t^{\mu}_{a,b}(\tau^{\prime})\neq\tau\,:\,a\in\{i,j\}\},

\displaystyle\Psi(\tau;i,j)=\{t^{\mu}_{a,b}(\tau^{\prime})\neq\tau\,:\,b\in\{i,j\}\}.

We now state the transition equations for
\mu
–involution Schubert polynomials.

Theorem 3.18
.

Let
\tau\in\mathcal{I}_{\mu}
.
For
(i,j)\in\mathrm{Cyc}_{\mu}(\tau)
,

\displaystyle 2^{-\delta(i,j)}(x_{i}+x_{j})\mathfrak{S}_{\tau}^{\mu}=\sum_{\sigma\in\Phi(\tau;i,j)}\mathfrak{S}_{\sigma}^{\mu}-\sum_{\sigma\in\Psi(\tau;i,j)}\mathfrak{S}_{\sigma}^{\mu}.

Proof.

We first define the following two sets of permutations:

\displaystyle A:=\bigcup_{v\in\mathcal{A}_{\mu}(\tau)}\{u\in S_{n}\,:\,v\lessdot u,u=vt_{ir}\text{ or }u=vt_{jr}\},

\displaystyle C:=\bigcup_{v\in\mathcal{A}_{\mu}(\tau)}\{u\in S_{n}\,:\,v\lessdot u,u=vt_{ri}\text{ or }u=vt_{rj}\}.

Viewed as multisets, there is no multiplicity in
A
or
C
.
To see this for
A
, given
v_{1},v_{2}\in\mathcal{A}_{\mu}^{-1}(\tau)
and any
r
,
t_{ir}v_{1}=t_{jr}v_{2}
cannot occur as
i
must appear before
j
in any inverse atom of
\tau
.
Therefore any multiplicity is of the form
t_{ir}v_{1}=t_{js}v_{2}
where
r\neq s
.
In this case, the subword formed by
\{i,j,r,s\}
must be
irsj
in
v_{1}
and
rijs
in
v_{2}
. However, since
r>i>j
,
r
cannot appear to the left of
i,j
, so
rijs
cannot be a subword in any inverse atom, a contradiction.
A similar argument holds for
C
.

By Theorem
2.14
, the left hand side expands as

\displaystyle 2^{-\delta(i,j)}(x_{i}+x_{j})\mathfrak{S}_{\tau}^{\mu}

\displaystyle=2^{-\delta(i,j)}\sum_{v\in\mathcal{A}_{\mu}(\tau)}(x_{i}+x_{j})\mathfrak{S}_{v}=\sum_{u\in A}\mathfrak{S}_{u}-\sum_{u\in C}\mathfrak{S}_{u}.

Now define
A^{\prime}:=\{u\in\mathcal{A}_{\mu}(\sigma):\sigma\in\Phi(\tau;i,j)\}
and
C^{\prime}:=\{w\in\mathcal{A}_{\mu}(\sigma):\sigma\in\Psi(\tau;i,j)\}
.
Note
A^{\prime}\subseteq A
and
C^{\prime}\subseteq C
by Theorem
3.11
.
Then the right hand side is

\sum_{u\in A^{\prime}}\mathfrak{S}_{u}-\sum_{w\in C^{\prime}}\mathfrak{S}_{w}.

Then the result will follow by showing
A\setminus A^{\prime}=C\setminus C^{\prime}
.

Let
w\in A\setminus A^{\prime}
.
By the definition of
A
, there exists
v\in\mathcal{A}_{\mu}(\tau)
so that
w=vt_{ab}
with
a<b
and
a\in\{i,j\}
.
By Corollary
3.17
, we see
a,b
are in the same block of
\tau
, hence
y_{\tau}(a)
and
y_{\tau}(b)
as well.
Furthermore, the result guarantees there exists
a^{\prime}<b^{\prime}
with
u=wt_{a^{\prime}b^{\prime}}
so that
b^{\prime}\in\{i,j\}
.
Therefore,
w\in C\setminus C^{\prime}
.
By a symmetric argument, every
w\in C\setminus C^{\prime}
is also in
A\setminus A^{\prime}
, so the result follows.
∎

4.
Future directions

The results of this paper suggest several natural directions of further study, both for combinatorial and geometric aspects of the theory of
\mu
–involutions and their associated Schubert polynomials.

4.1.
Shellability of
\mu
–Bruhat order

A natural question to ask is whether the
\mu
–Bruhat order is thin and/or shellable.
Thinness is a key structural property often appearing in posets arising from Coxeter theory and geometry, while shellability shows that the order complex is homotopic to a wedge of spheres.
The two together would provide a combinatorial description of the Möbius function of the poset.
Such results would parallel the well-known properties of Bruhat order on permutations and on involutions
[
11
,
17
]
.

4.2.
\mu
–involution Stanley symmetric functions

Another natural direction is to define and study Stanley symmetric functions associated to
\mu
–involutions.
Given
\pi\in I_{\mu}
, one may define

F^{\mu}_{\pi}=\sum_{w\in A_{\mu}(\pi)}F_{w}

where
F_{w}
is the classical Stanley symmetric function from
[
25
]
associated to a permutation
w
.
By construction, the coefficient in
F^{\mu}_{\pi}
of
x_{1}\dots x_{\ell_{\mu}(\pi)}
is
|\mathcal{R}_{\mu}(\pi)|
, the number of reduced
\mu
–involution words for
\pi
.

This construction interpolates between two well-studied cases.
When
\mu=(1^{n})
, we recover the usual Stanley symmetric functions, which are known to be Schur positive
[
10
]
.
When
\mu=(n)
, we obtain the involution Stanley symmetric functions studied in
[
14
]
, which are Schur-
P
positive.

Question 4.1
.

Can one define a notion of positivity interpolating between Schur and Schur-P positivity that the
F^{\mu}_{\pi}
satisfy?

4.3.
Combinatorial formulas for
\mu
–involution Schubert polynomials

Schubert polynomials exhibit rich combinatorial structure through their many combinatorial formulas, including the famous pipedream
[
2
,
3
,
12
]
and bumpless pipedream
[
19
,
27
]
formulas.
An analogous pipedream formula for involution Schubert polynomials was also introduced in
[
16
]
.

Question 4.2
.

Is there a pipedream like formula for
\mu
–involution Schubert polynomials generalizing these two formulas?
Or a bumpless pipedream formula?

One possible approach is through the framework of co-transition or transition formulas, which have been successful in other settings.
However, preliminary computations indicate that the co-transition approach does not behave as cleanly for
\mu
–involutions as it does for permutations or involutions.
The crux of the strategy is to construct a sequence of transition equations where each
\Psi
set is empty, eventually terminating with the base case
w_{0}
.
In fact, for the permutation and involution cases any such sequence with
n
fixed will arrive at
w_{0}
.
The following example shows that such an approach requires some modification if it is to be applied for
\mu
–involutions.

Example 4.3
.

Let
\tau=653|421|7
with
\mathrm{Cyc}(\tau)=\{(6,3),(5,5),(4,2),(1,1),(7,7)\}
.
Using Theorem
3.18
, we have the following sequence of products with
\Psi(\tau;i,j)
empty:

\displaystyle x_{2}\cdot\mathfrak{S}_{\tau}^{\mu}

\displaystyle=\mathfrak{S}_{t_{27}^{\mu}(\tau)}^{\mu}=\mathfrak{S}_{653|417|2}^{\mu},

\displaystyle(x_{3}+x_{6})\mathfrak{S}_{653|417|2}^{\mu}

\displaystyle=\mathfrak{S}_{763|421|5}^{\mu}+\mathfrak{S}_{647|321|5}^{\mu},

\displaystyle x_{2}\cdot\mathfrak{S}_{763|421|5}^{\mu}

\displaystyle=\mathfrak{S}_{763|415|2}^{\mu}.

For
\pi=763|415|2
and every
(i,j)\in\mathrm{Cyc}_{\mu}(\pi)
, the resulting transition equation either requires the auxiliary value
8
(so
t_{i8}^{\mu}
is applied) or has nonempty
\Psi(\pi;i,j)
.
∎

In particular, it is unclear whether the cotransition outline will terminate with
w_{0}
starting with an arbitrary
\mu
–involution.
Therefore, the upward induction on
\ell^{\mu}
does not have a clear base case.

4.4.
K-theoretic generalizations

Finally, it is natural to ask whether the theory developed here extends to
K
–theory of Borel orbits in
Q_{n}
.
Grothendieck polynomials
\mathfrak{G}_{w}
represent
K
–theory classes of Schubert varieties, so are the
K
-theoretic analogues of Schubert polynomials.
We define
K
–theoretic analogues of
\mu
–involution Schubert polynomials as a sum of certain Grothendieck polynomials.
For
\pi\in\mathcal{I}_{\mu}
, the
Hecke atoms
of
\pi
are
\mathcal{B}_{\mu}(\pi)=\{w:w^{-1}\,\circ_{\mu}\,w=\pi\}
.
The
\mu
–involution Grothendieck polynomial
indexed by
\pi
is

\displaystyle\mathfrak{G}_{\pi}^{\mu}=\sum_{w\in\mathcal{B}_{\mu}(\pi)}\mathfrak{G}_{w}.

It is not clear whether these polynomials have any geometric significance.
Even in the involution case, this is unknown.
The powers of 2 required to obtain Wyser and Yong’s representatives from involution Schubert polynomials do not have a neat translation to the
K
–theory setting
[
21
]
.
We hope
\mu
–involution Grothendieck polynomials can shed some light on the challenge of describing
K
–theory classes for
O_{n}
–orbit closures combinatorially.
