Screening and identification of key genes related to the immune microenvironment of rectal cancer influenced by radiotherapy based on bioinformatics methods
Authors: Juan Fang, Rugang Zhao, Shanshan Wu, Juyi Wen, Xia Zhang, Junpeng Ma, Jingjiao Li, Lipin Gao, Yanbo Yu, Xiangfei Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveRadiotherapy (RT) plays a crucial role in the comprehensive treatment of rectal cancer. However, the impact of radiotherapy on the tumor microenvironment (TME), especially its effect on immune cell infiltration and immune-related gene expression, has not been fully studied. This study aims to screen and analyze key genes related to the immune microenvironment of rectal cancer influenced by radiotherapy based on bioinformatics methods for the purpose of identifying potential biomarkers and providing new insights for the personalized therapy of rectal cancer.MethodsUsing data from the Public Gene Expression Database (GEO) and the Cancer Genomics Database (TCGA), the impact of radiotherapy on the immune microenvironment of rectal cancer was explored using bioinformatics tools. Through screening differentially expressed genes (DEGs), correlation analysis, TIMER database analysis, immune infiltration score, and correlation analysis between key genes and prognosis, the effects of radiotherapy on the immune microenvironment of rectal cancer were investigated.ResultsTotally 7 upregulated and 4 downregulated differentially expressed genes were identified, among which MASP1, LTK, SLC9A3R2 were negatively correlated with myeloid suppressor cell infiltration (MDSCs), while ZP2 was positively correlated. The expression of MASP1 and SLC9A3R2 was closely related to the level of immune cell infiltration and played significant roles in the immune microenvironment. High expression of MASP1 was significantly correlated with survival benefits from immune checkpoint inhibitor therapy, while SLC9A3R2 was closely related to the efficacy of PD-L1 inhibitors and CTLA4 inhibitors.ConclusionsMASP1 and SLC9A3R2, as two key genes that may be related to the immune microenvironment of rectal cancer radiotherapy, deserve further exploration of their roles in the mechanism. The combination of radiotherapy and immunotherapy holds promising prospects in the treatment of rectal cancer, and exploration of related mechanisms will provide new strategies and targets for the treatment of various tumors and rectal cancer.Keywords: Rectal cancer, Radiotherapy, Immune microenvironment, Bioinformatics, MASP1, SLC9A3R2

Background

Rectal cancer, as one of the malignant tumors with high incidence and mortality rates globally, has been a hotspot in medical research for the optimization of treatment modalities.

Introduction

Rectal cancer is one of the malignant tumors with persistently high incidence and mortality rates globally, and the optimization of its treatments, as well as related studies, has been a hot topic around the world. Radiotherapy (RT) plays a pivotal role in the comprehensive treatment of rectal cancer, particularly in the management of Locally Advanced Rectal Cancer (LARC). RT can not only reduce tumor volume which enhances surgical resection rates, but also improve long-term survival rates for patients. In recent years, the emergence of total neoadjuvant therapy (TNT) has significantly enhanced treatment outcomes and has become the standard-of-care for locally advanced rectal cancer (LARC), The implementation of TNT not only improved local control rates but also significantly reduced the risk of recurrence.In multiple clinical trials, TNT has shown the potential to improve disease-free survival rates [1]. Radiotherapy (RT) not only exerts therapeutic effects through the direct cytotoxicity of tumor cells but also augments the efficacy of immunotherapy by modulating the tumor immune microenvironment. Recent studies have increasingly demonstrated that RT can influence the immune system via multiple mechanisms, thereby playing a pivotal role in cancer treatment [2, 3]. RT facilitates immune system activation by releasing tumor antigens and immune-stimulating signals, thereby enhancing immune recognition and tumor targeting [4, 5]. Furthermore, RT can promote the infiltration and activation of immune cells by modifying the cellular composition and signaling pathways within the tumor microenvironment. For instance, RT can enhance the recruitment and activation of T cells, thereby strengthening the antitumor immune response [6, 7]. Nonetheless, RT may also induce immunosuppressive reactions, potentially diminishing therapeutic efficacy. Consequently, achieving a balance between the immunostimulatory and immunosuppressive effects of RT is essential for optimizing treatment outcomes [8, 9].Consequently, to enhance the synergistic potential of radiotherapy (RT) and immunotherapy, it is imperative to further investigate various RT dosing regimens and fractionation strategies in order to optimize their immunomodulatory effects.

This study aims to screen and analyze key genes related to the immune microenvironment of rectal cancer influenced by radiotherapy by using bioinformatics methods. Based on the data from public gene expression databases such as the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) and cancer genomics databases like The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA), the impact of radiotherapy on the immune microenvironment of rectal cancer, particularly its effect on immune cell infiltration and immune-related gene expression were analysed and identified. Our results would provide new insights and potential biomarkers for personalized treatment of rectal cancer.

Materials and methods

Data collection

The Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) is a public functional genomics data repository that supports the submission of data analysis conforming to the MIAME (Minimum Information About a Microarray Experiment) standards. This database accepts array and sequence-based data and provides tools to assist users in querying and downloading experiments as well as curated gene expression profiles (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/). In this study, we searched for relevant microarray datasets in the GEO database using the keywords “radiotherapy” and “rectal cancer.” Inclusion criteria comprised: (1) samples of human rectal cancer before and after radiotherapy; (2) datasets containing raw matrix data and platform annotation information. Based on these criteria, we downloaded the GSE26027 and GSE35452 datasets from the GEO database using the GEOquery package. Both datasets are based on the GPL570 [HG-U133_Plus_2] Affymetrix Human Genome U133 Plus 2.0 Array platform.

Differential gene expression screening

The data analysis was conducted using R software (version 4.2.1) with the following R packages: GEOquery (2.64.2), limma (3.52.2), ggplot2 (3.3.6), and ComplexHeatmap (2.13.1). The ComBat function from the sva package was employed for batch effect correction across different datasets. During preprocessing of the data, probes corresponding to multiple molecules were excluded, and only the probe with the highest signal value for each molecule was retained. The criteria for selecting differential gene expression (DEG) were set as |log2FoldChange| > 1 and adjusted p-value < 0.05. The clustering of samples across groups was evaluated using PCA plots. Differential analysis was performed using the limma package, and the results were visualized using volcano plots and heatmaps.

Correlation analysis between differential genes and immune infiltration

Based on the DEGs data, combined with RNA-seq data (in TPM format), and clinical data obtained from the TCGA-READ project within TCGA, ssGSEA analysis was conducted to assess the levels of immune cells and associated pathway activities using GSVA software package [10]. 24 immune cell markers from an article in Immunity were referenced [11], and the correlation between the main variables and immune infiltration data was visualized using the ggplot2 package with lollipop plots.

TIMER database analysis

A systematic analysis of immune infiltration across different cancer types was conducted based on the TIMER database (https://cistrome.shinyapps.io/timer/) [12]. which comprises samples from 32 cancer types in TCGA utilized to estimate immune infiltration abundance. TIMER uses the EPIC algorithm for MDSC infiltration estimation. Kaplan-Meier curves and Cox regression analysis were utilized to explore the impact of MDSC expression on the prognosis of READ patients.

Immune infiltration score

Immune scores were calculated using R package estimate [1.0.13] based on RNAseq data (in TPM format) and clinical data from the TCGA-READ project and visualize the data using the ggplot2 package.

Analysis of the correlation between key genes and prognosis

The data from RNA-seq and clinical data from the TCGA-READ project was also used to perform analysis of proportional hazards and Cox regression using R software (version 4.2.1) and packages survival [3.3.1] and rms [6.3-0]. Prognostic data are sourced from the Cell article [13]. The results were visualized using forest plots with ggplot2.

The correlation between key genes and levels of immune infiltrating cells

Based on the ssGSEA algorithm from the R package GSVA [1.46.0] and immune cell markers from the article in Immunity [4], immune infiltration levels were analyzed and visualized using lollipop plots with the ggplot2 package.

Protein expression analysis

Based on HPA database (https://www.proteinatlas.org), the protein expression levels of key genes in rectal cancer tissues were compared.

Tumor immune feature correlation analysis

The TISIDB database (https://cis.hku.hk/TISIDB/index.php) was used to analyze the immune subtypes of key genes, their correlation with immune regulatory molecules, and their association with immunotherapy efficacy across various tumor types.

Analysis of predictive efficacy of immunotherapy

The K-M plotter database (http://kmplot.com/analysis) was used to analyze the correlation between the expression of key genes and the efficacy of immunotherapy across various tumor types.

In GSE26027, 569 differentially expressed genes were identified, with 494 upregulated and 276 downregulated. In GSE35452, 770 differentially expressed genes were identified, with 206 upregulated and 168 downregulated. The sample information and differential gene expression results are shown in the figures. Discussion

Colorectal cancer (CRC) remains a significant global health burden, ranking among the leading causes of cancer-related mortality worldwide. According to recent estimates, CRC accounts for approximately 10% of all cancer cases globally, with approximately 150,000 new cases diagnosed annually [14]. Despite advances in therapeutic interventions including surgery, chemotherapy, radiotherapy, and immunotherapy, significant challenges persist in achieving durable clinical responses, particularly due to therapeutic resistance and metastatic progression [15].Tumor cells employ multiple mechanisms to adapt to the unique immune microenvironment within cancer tissues, including metabolic cascades, protein kinase (AMPK) phosphorylation, mitochondrial metabolism, and ferroptosis [15-17]. Understanding the immunosuppressive mechanisms in CRC microenvironment remains crucial for developing effective immunotherapeutic strategies.

In recent years, In recent years, the combination of TNT with immune checkpoint inhibitors has shown good short-term efficacy, especially in LARC patients with normal mismatch repair function (pMMR)/microsatellite instability (MSS), However, the total dose and fractionation of radiotherapy remain unresolved issues in this combination therapy, requiring further research into the impact of radiotherapy on the tumor microenvironment and their interactions [18]. Novel immunotherapeutic approaches have significantly improved outcomes for patients with colorectal cancer, particularly those with Microsatellite Instability High (MSI-H) tumors. Immunotherapy response rates have significantly increased in MSI-H subgroups, even in metastatic MSI colorectal cancer patients [19–21]. However, traditional immunotherapy strategies have not achieved the expected success in the Microsatellite Stable (MSS) subgroup, which constitutes a large proportion of CRC cases and has a poorer prognosis. In recent years, researchers are exploring new strategies to enhance the clinical response of MSS CRC patients to immunotherapy-based treatments. These strategies include but are not limited to immune checkpoint blockade (ICB) immunotherapy, such as radiation therapy, and the application of immunotherapy in the early non-metastatic disease setting, such as introducing it in the neoadjuvant treatment phase when the tumor’s immune escape mechanisms may not have fully developed [22].

The incorporation of neoadjuvant treatment into immunotherapy for rectal cancer is a hot topic in recent years. Studies have evaluated the safety and efficacy of neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy combined with immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) in the treatment of locally advanced rectal cancer (LARC). Based on four treatment regimens, including long-course and short-course radiotherapy combined with PD-1 inhibitors and chemotherapy, the results showed that the pCR rate was 43.3% in 96.8% of pMMR patients, while it was 100% in dMMR patients. The overall tumor response rate was 79%, and the sphincter preservation rate was 88.7%. Regimen A showed the highest pCR rate (60%). Radiation enteritis was the main adverse event. These studies concluded that this combination therapy is effective and safe for LARC [23]. The NECTAR study, a prospective, multicenter, single-arm Phase 2 trial, investigated the safety and efficacy of PD-1 blockade combined with long-course chemoradiotherapy (CRT) in MSS/pMMR locally advanced rectal cancer (LARC) neoadjuvant treatment. The study included 50 patients and the results showed a pathological complete response rate (pCR) of 40.0% with good treatment tolerance. Although treatment-related adverse events (TRAEs) were observed, the study supported the potential of PD-1 blockade combined with CRT in the treatment of LARC [24]. Wang et al. (2023) evaluated the efficacy of short-course radiotherapy (SCRT) combined with PD-1 inhibitors in neoadjuvant treatment for locally advanced rectal cancer (LARC). In the TORCH II trial, LARC patients were randomized to receive SCRT followed by Trifluridine and tipiracil hydrochloride (TAS-102) and oxaliplatin (ToriCAPOX) chemotherapy. The results showed a complete response rate of 58.1% and a pCR rate of 56.2% with good tolerance, and the main adverse event was thrombocytopenia. The study supported the potential of this treatment regimen in improving tumor regression and organ preservation in MSS/pMMR LARC patients [25].

The importance of immune checkpoint inhibitors combined with chemotherapy and radiotherapy in neoadjuvant treatment for rectal cancer is increasingly evident. Therefore, a deep understanding of how radiotherapy (RT) affects the tumor microenvironment (TME), especially its immune components, is crucial for the advancement of immunotherapy in CRC treatment. The interaction between RT and TME is extremely complex, involving the innate and adaptive branches of the immune system. RT can not only trigger inflammatory reactions but also produce immunosuppressive effects, which may promote or hinder the development of anti-tumor immune responses. It is worth noting that although radiation therapy initially triggers an inflammatory effect, as chronic inflammation progresses, it may lead to subsequent immunosuppressive reactions [26]. RT alters TME through various mechanisms, which may affect the progression of immune response. Specifically, radiation can increase cellular stress response, promote immunogenic cell death, and subsequently lead to the release of DNA exonuclease Trex1. The release of Trex1 plays a crucial role in the activation of DNA sensor cGAS and its downstream effector molecule STING, ultimately leading to upregulation of interferon levels and activation of inflammatory cascade reactions [27, 28]. In addition, the inflammatory response induced by radiotherapy can significantly enhance the secretion of CXCL16 in mouse and human breast cancer cells. As a chemokine, CXCL16 can bind to CXCR6 on Th1 cells, activate CD8 + effector T cells, and play a key role in the recruitment of these cells to inflammatory sites [29].

In this study, we identified 7 upregulated and 4 downregulated DEGs by analyzing two datasets of rectal cancer radiotherapy patients, and six of these genes have research data in the bioinformatics database, BC043291, GPR113, RP11-266L9.1, RP11-413B19.2, BC113958, no data available in the database. Further analysis revealed that MASP1, LTK, SLC9A3R2 were negatively correlated with the infiltration of myeloid suppressor cells (MDSCs), while ZP2 was positively correlated. In the survival analysis of rectal cancer(TCGA READ data), the expression level of SLC9A3R2 is close to statistical significance. In addition, except for ZP2, the expression of the other genes exhibited differences in benign and malignant tumors. In terms of immune infiltration score, MASP1, PDZD7, SLC9A3R2 are positively correlated with immune infiltration score, while ZP2 is negatively correlated. These results indicate that MASP1 and SLC9A3R2 are closely related to immune cell infiltration levels and play important roles in the immune microenvironment, which deserves further in-depth analysis. Immuno-histochemical analysis of protein expression gene levels using the THPA database revealed a correlation between high and low expression of MASP1 and the prognosis of rectal cancer. The 5-year survival rate of the MASP1 high expression group was 67%, while the low expression group was 33%. The 5-year survival rate of the high expression group of SLC9A3R2 was 74%, while the low expression group was 44%, but it did not reach statistical significance. These data further confirmed the potential value of MASP1 and SLC9A3R2 in the prognosis of rectal cancer. Through TISIDB database analysis, we found that the expression of MASP1 and SLC9A3R2 is mainly concentrated in tissue healing type and IFN- γ Dominant and inflammatory immune microenvironments. MASP1 is positively correlated with the expression levels of multiple immune regulatory molecules, including IDO1, TIM3, CTLA-4, MHC-E, CD27, IL2RA, and IL-6. Meanwhile, the expression of SLC9A3R2 is positively correlated with the expression levels of MHC-HLA-A, MHC-HLA-C, MHC-HLA-E, MHC-HLA-F, CXCL12, and CXCR4. In addition, MASP1, LTK, and SLC9A3R2 are negatively correlated with the infiltration level of myeloid derived suppressor cells (MDSCs), while the expression of ZP2 is positively correlated with the infiltration level of MDSCs. These results support the view that key genes have a close relationship with immune cell infiltration levels and play a role in the immune microenvironment.

In the pan tumor analysis related to the efficacy of immune checkpoint inhibitors, the expression of MASP1 is associated with the survival benefits of atezolizumab treatment for urothelial carcinoma, while the expression of SLC9A3R2 is closely related to the survival benefits of dual immunotherapy for melanoma and atezolizumab treatment for urothelial carcinoma. These findings suggest that MASP1 and SLC9A3R2 may serve as potential biomarkers for predicting immune therapy responses. Using the K-M database analysis, we explored the significance of MASP1 and SLC9A3R2 in predicting the efficacy of immunotherapy. The results showed that high expression of MASP1 was significantly correlated with the survival benefits of immune checkpoint drug therapy, while SLC9A3R2 could not predict the efficacy of PD-1 inhibitors but was closely related to the efficacy of PD-L1 inhibitors and CTLA4 inhibitors. These results further emphasize the potential role of MASP1 and SLC9A3R2 in immunotherapy.

SLC9A3 (Solid Carrier Family 9 Member 3), as a sodium hydrogen exchanger, is involved in regulating the pH balance and ion homeostasis inside and outside the cell, and its role in oncology is gradually receiving attention. As a scaffold protein, NHERF2 organizes protein complexes at the plasma membrane and links membrane proteins to the cytoskeleton through interactions with the ERM (ezrin-radixin-moesin) complex, which couples the actomyosin cortex with the cell membrane.5 SLC9A3R2 is part of the solute carrier family 9 (SLC9) and regulates trafficking and localization of ion channels and receptors, thereby influencing various signaling pathways [30]. Gene ontology enrichment analyses have associated SLC9A3R2 with negative regulation of phosphorylation, positive regulation of catabolic processes, epithelial cell proliferation, regulation of vasculature development, and autophagy [31]. Research has shown that SLC9A3 may play a role in the proliferation, invasion, and metastasis of tumor cells, and its expression level may be associated with the prognosis of certain cancers [32]. A study has found that long non-coding RNA SLC9A3-AS1 regulates the sensitivity of liver cancer cells to TP by sponge forming miR-449b-5p. The downregulation of SLC9A3-AS1 inhibited the viability and invasive ability of liver cancer cells, while overexpression of miR-449b-5p increased the sensitivity of cells to TP. The study revealed the role of SLC9A3-AS1/miR-449b-5p/LDHA axis in liver cancer glycolysis and TP sensitivity, providing new targets for liver cancer treatment [33]. Huang et al. investigated the role of long-chain noncoding RNA SLC9A3-AS1 in non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC). It was found that SLC9A3-AS1 is upregulated in NSCLC tissues and cell lines and is associated with poor prognosis in patients. Knockdown of SLC9A3-AS1 can inhibit the proliferation, migration, and invasion of NSCLC cells. It was also found that SLC9A3-AS1 promotes the progression of NSCLC by sponge forming miR-760. Therefore, SLC9A3-AS1 may become a potential biomarker and therapeutic target for NSCLC [34]. Another studies found that knocking down SLC9A3-AS1 can inhibit the proliferation and invasion of NPC cells, induce cell apoptosis, and inhibit tumor growth in vivo. Mechanistically, SLC9A3-AS1 upregulates E2F6 by sponge forming miR-486-5p, thereby promoting the carcinogenic effect of NPC cells. This discovery provides potential new targets for the treatment of nasopharyngeal carcinoma [35]. There are few studies on SLC9A3R2. In some researche on markers related to tumor genesis and development, SLC9A3R2 is a good predictor of disease-free survival of breast cancer [36], and one of the genes with significant differential methylation in lung cancer [37], which may be explored as a hypothetical microvascular drug target [38].

MASP1 (Mannan binding lectin serine protease 1) is a serine protease associated with the mannan binding lectin pathway, and its role in tumor biology is being elucidated in recent years. MASP1 is believed to play a role in the occurrence, development, invasion, and metastasis of tumors [39]. Lv et al. screened out 187 genes associated with hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) based on weighted gene co expression network analysis (WGCNA) and machine learning methods, and identified six characteristic genes, including AADAT, APOF, GPC3, LPA, MASP1, and NAT2 [40]. These genes may become potential targets for the diagnosis, prevention, and treatment of hepatocellular carcinoma. In recent years, quite a lot of studies have found that MASP1 is associated with immunotherapy. Some studies have used clinical and transcriptome data from TCGA and CCLE databases to analyze immune infiltration through TIMER, TISIDB, and CIBERSORT. It has been found that MASP1 is downregulated in gastric adenocarcinoma and positively correlated with the infiltration of various immune cells. The prognostic features of four genes related to MASP1 can independently predict the survival of gastric adenocarcinoma patients, indicating that MASP1 may be a potential target for STAD diagnosis and immunotherapy [41]. Zhang et al. developed an immune related risk score (IRS) model for patients with oral squamous cell carcinoma (OSCC) to predict overall survival and disease-free survival. An IRS model containing genes such as MASP1 was developed by analyzing OSCC queue data in databases such as TCGA. This model can accurately predict patient prognosis and serve as an independent risk factor. In addition, IRS is positively correlated with the non-inflammatory features of the tumor microenvironment and can predict the sensitivity of patients to immunotherapy and chemotherapy drugs, providing a new tool for personalized treatment of OSCC [42]. Hang et al. utilized NSCLC patient data from databases such as TCGA and constructed a prognostic feature and risk model based on immune related genes using WGCNA and machine learning methods. Research has found that immune related central genes are involved in the migration and activation of immune cells, with the highest mutation rates observed in MASP1 and SEMA5A genes. Prognostic features help distinguish subgroups of NSCLC and are associated with sensitivity to multiple drugs, providing a new strategy for individualized immunotherapy of NSCLC [43].

MASP1 is also related to the diagnosis and prognosis of multiple tumor types. Gabriele analyzed 32 N-glycosylated peptides, quantified 163 serum samples using PRM technology, and developed a new prediction model combined with machine learning algorithms. The model is based on six proteomic variables such as MASP1 and five clinical variables and can distinguish PCa and benign prostatic hyperplasia (BPH) with an AUC of 0.93, which is superior to PSA detection alone. In addition, a factor for predicting the invasiveness of PCa has been developed, providing a new tool for clinical management of PCa [44]. Li et al. identified six N-glycopeptides such as MASP1 as biomarkers for predicting chemotherapy sensitivity by comprehensively screening serum glycoproteins from locally advanced cervical cancer (LACC) patients. These glycopeptides have significantly higher expression levels in patients who respond to chemotherapy and decrease after chemotherapy, indicating that they may be used to predict the response of locally advanced cervical cancer patients to neoadjuvant chemotherapy [45]. Additionally, this study analyzed the expression profiles of extracellular vesicle miRNAs released from five ovarian cancer cell lines and found that compared with normal cells, 143 types of extracellular vesicle miRNAs were upregulated in ovarian cancer cells. Among them, miR-21 was overexpressed in CCCO, which may play a role by targeting tumor suppressor genes such as MASP1. These miRNAs are expected to become new biomarkers for diagnosing and monitoring the progression of ovarian cancer [46]. Pan-cancer analyses across 33 cancer types reveal that MASP1 serves as a promising prognostic biomarker with significant associations between MASP1 expression levels and tumor-infiltrating immune cells, mismatch repair mechanisms, tumor mutation burden, and microsatellite instability [47]. Experimental studies demonstrate that overexpression of MASP1 in hepatocellular carcinoma cell lines significantly inhibits proliferation, invasion, and migration, suggesting a tumor-suppressive function in certain contexts.1 Furthermore, MASP1 interacts with the coagulation cascade and the kallikrein/kinin system, and can activate endothelial cells eliciting pro-inflammatory signaling that modulates the tumor immune microenvironment [48]. Radiotherapy may regulate MASP1 and SLC9A3R2 expression through several interconnected mechanisms. First, radiation-induced DNA damage activates the DNA damage response (DDR), a complex signaling pathway that detects and repairs DNA lesions, resulting in activation of downstream effectors including transcription factors that may modulate expression of immune-related genes such as MASP1 [6, 7]. Second, radiation triggers inflammatory signaling cascades, including activation of NF-κB and cGAS-STING pathways, which induce type I interferon responses and pro-inflammatory cytokines that can alter complement gene expression [6, 8]. Third, radiation-induced cellular stress and damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) released from dying tumor cells can activate pattern recognition receptors and downstream signaling pathways that regulate genes involved in innate immunity [6, 8]. The interplay between DDR pathways, radiation immunogenicity, and immune checkpoint efficacy has been increasingly recognized, suggesting that radiation may modulate the tumor immune microenvironment through effects on complement components and scaffold proteins involved in receptor signaling [49–51]. Emerging evidence shows signaling molecules regulate the tumor immune microenvironment and therapy response in colorectal cancer (CRC). Zhou et al. found phospholipase C gamma 2 (PLCG2) is overexpressed in CRC and promotes tumor progression via Akt-mTOR signaling. High PLCG2 correlates with an immunosuppressive microenvironment, including reduced CD8 + T cells, increased Tregs, and upregulated PD-1/PD-L1. PLCG2 knockdown combined with anti-PD-1 therapy synergistically inhibits tumors, turning “cold” tumors “hot.” Additionally, immune-related long non-coding RNA (lncRNA) signatures serve as biomarkers for prognosis and therapy prediction [52]. Zhou et al. developed a six IR-lncRNA signature that stratifies CRC patients into high- and low-risk groups with distinct immune features. Low-risk patients show higher immune cell infiltration, cytolytic activity, and better immunotherapy response, while high-risk patients have immunosuppressive traits and higher chemotherapy sensitivity. These studies underscore the value of immune-related gene signatures in understanding CRC heterogeneity and guiding personalized treatment [53].

Our study describe two key genes that may be related to the immune microenvironment affected by radiotherapy for rectal cancer. However, there are still certain limitations in our research results. Firstly, the inherent heterogeneity and potential selection bias in our GEO dataset may result in a certain degree of bias in the results. Secondly, we have not further verified the screened key genes and their mechanisms of action through in vitro or in vivo experiments. Therefore, additional studies are necessary to overcome these limitations and further deepen our understanding of the mechanisms related to the immune microenvironment affected by radiotherapy for rectal cancer.

In summary, MASP1 and SLC9A3R2, as two key genes that may be related to the impact of radiation therapy on the immune microenvironment in rectal cancer, deserve further exploration of their roles in the mechanism. the integration of radiotherapy and immunotherapy in the neoadjuvant treatment of colorectal cancer represents a promising therapeutic strategy that has the potential to improve treatment responses. Research efforts aimed at elucidating the underlying mechanisms of this combination therapy and identifying predictive biomarkers are essential for guiding treatment decisions and optimizing treatment strategies for CRC patients in the future.
