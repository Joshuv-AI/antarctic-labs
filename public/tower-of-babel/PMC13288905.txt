Convergent evolution of the N156K mutation in A(H1N1)pdm09 hemagglutinin contributes to antigenic drift and cluster transition
Authors: Yabin Tian, Deyun Jiang, Wentai Ma, Jianhui Nie, Qianqian Li, Chenyan Zhao, Sihong Xu, Weijin Huang, Youchun Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe continuous antigenic evolution of influenza A(H1N1)pdm09 poses a persistent challenge to vaccine efficacy. While recent viral lineages have diverged significantly from ancestral strains, the precise molecular determinants driving the latest antigenic cluster transitions remain incompletely characterized. Here, using the broadly neutralizing monoclonal antibody 32D6 as a molecular probe, we identified a critical escape mechanism of antibody evasion in recent A(H1N1)pdm09 isolates. Through pseudovirus neutralization assays and LASSO regression analysis, we identified residue 156 in the Sa antigenic site as the key determinant of escape. Furthermore, molecular dynamics (MD) simulations and MM-GBSA calculations indicated that the N156 K mutation reduces the binding free energy at the antibody-antigen interface due to steric hindrance and electrostatic repulsion. Notably, while the N156 K mutations caused a significant reduction in pseudovirus infectivity within the earlier GD19 genetic backbone, it was fully accommodated in recent lineages. Phylogenetic analysis revealed the rapid, convergent fixation of N156 K across global isolates. These findings provide insights into the mechanism of the recent GD19-to-VI19 antigenic transition and highlight the hypothesized role of compensatory mutations in sustaining viral fitness during antigenic evolution.

Introduction

Influenza A(H1N1)pdm09 virus, which originated from the 2009 pandemic, continues to circulate seasonally, posing a significant public health burden worldwide [1,2]. The primary strategy for mitigating the impact of influenza is annual vaccination [3]. However, the effectiveness of these vaccines is frequently limited by the continuous evolution of the viral hemagglutinin (HA), the principal target of neutralizing antibodies [4–6]. The evolutionary trajectory of the A(H1N1)pdm09 HA has followed a pattern of “approximately linear evolution,” where periods of relative antigenic stasis are punctuated by cluster transitions driven by key amino acid substitutions [4,7,8]. Since 2009, several major antigenic clusters have emerged sequentially, including CA09, MI15, BR18, GD19, and VI19, each displacing its predecessor by acquiring key mutations that facilitate immune evasion [9]. Most recently, our previous work demonstrated that a new cluster, represented by the VI19, WI19, VI22, and VI22 vaccine strain, has risen to dominance [10]. However, the precise molecular determinants and the fitness landscape governing this latest antigenic transition remain incompletely understood.

The HA globular head comprised the receptor-binding site (RBS) and is surrounded by five major antigenic sites (Sa, Sb, Ca1, Ca2, and Cb) [11,12]. These sites are under intense positive selection pressure from the host immune system. Among them, the Sa and Sb sites, located near the RBS, are particularly immunodominant [13,14]. Antibodies targeting these regions can potently neutralize the virus by blocking receptor attachment, but they also drive the selection of escape mutants [15]. Traditional influenza surveillance relies heavily on the hemagglutination inhibition (HI) assay using ferret antisera to detect these antigenic changes [16–18]. While indispensable, ferret antisera provide a polyclonal average of the immune response, which can sometimes mask subtle yet clinically relevant mutations driven by human-specific immunity [19]. In contrast, broadly neutralizing monoclonal antibodies (mAbs) targeting defined epitopes can serve as precise molecular probes. By mapping the escape profiles of these mAbs, emerging cluster-transition determining sites can be identified before they are fully characterized by traditional serology [20].

In this study, we employed the human monoclonal antibody 32D6 as a representative probe to investigate the recent antigenic evolution of A(H1N1)pdm09. This antibody was previously characterized as a potent neutralizer against A(H1N1)pdm09 strains circulating between 2009 and 2019, mainly targeting the Sa and Sb antigenic sites [21]. However, our study observed a marked reduction in neutralizing efficacy against recent isolates. This differential neutralization profile provided a unique opportunity to dissect the molecular mechanisms driving the recent evolution of the A(H1N1)pdm09. Combining pseudovirus neutralization assays, LASSO regression modelling, and structural analysis, we identified a convergent N156 K mutation (H1 numbering) as a primary determinant of this antibody evasion. Furthermore, by reconstructing the mutation in different genetic backbones, we revealed a complex interplay between antigenic drift and viral fitness, highlighting the hypothesized role of epistatic compensatory mutations in facilitating the global fixation of the N156 K variant.

Materials and methods

Cell lines

293 T (human embryonic kidney) and MDCK (Madin-Darby canine kidney) cell lines were maintained at 37°C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO₂. Cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM, Gibco) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin. 293 T cells were obtained from Invitrogen (Carlsbad, CA), and MDCK cells were from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, Manassas, VA).

Monoclonal antibody and antisera

The human monoclonal antibody 32D6 (PDB ID: 6A4 K) was generated as previously described [22]. Briefly, the variable region genes were synthesized and cloned into pTT5 expression vectors, which contain the constant regions of the human IgG1 heavy and kappa light chains. The recombinant antibody was produced by transient transfection in Chinese hamster ovary (CHO) cells and purified from cell culture supernatants using Protein A affinity chromatography by TongYong Biological (Anhui, China).

The guinea pig antisera against the GD19 and VI22 vaccine strains were obtained from our earlier work [10]. Animals were inoculated with the plasmid DNA encoding the HA gene of GD19 and VI22 by electroporation. The immunizations were performed three times at 14-day intervals, and blood was collected two weeks after the final boost. All animal procedures were conducted in compliance with the ARRIVE guidelines.

Generation of H1N1 pseudovirus and titration

To generate H1N1 pseudoviruse, the full-length HA genes were codon-optimized and cloned into the pcDNA3.1 expression vector. 293 T cells were seeded in 6-well plates and co-transfected with the HA plasmid (1.25 μg) and the HIV-1 backbone plasmid pSG3.Δenv-Fluc (2.5 μg) expressing firefly luciferase, using Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen). To facilitate viral release, 10 μL exogenous neuraminidase (3.5 U/mL; Sigma-Aldrich, N2876) was added to the culture medium. Supernatants containing pseudoviruses were harvested at 48 h post-transfection, centrifuged at 3000 × g to remove cell debris, and stored in aliquots at −80°C. Prior to the entry assay, the pseudoviruses were treated with exogenous TPCK-trypsin (Sigma-Aldrich, T1426) at a concentration of 50 μg/mL for 30 min at 37°C to facilitate the cleavage of HA0 into HA1 and HA2. Viral titres were determined in MDCK cells using a luciferase assay kit (Promega) and the TCID₅₀ was calculated as previously described [23,24].

Site-directed mutagenesis

Mutations at HA residue 156 were introduced into the wild-type pcDNA3.1-HA plasmids of GD19 and VI22 strains using an inverse PCR-based site-directed mutagenesis method. Primers containing the nucleotide substitutions were used to amplify the entire plasmid. The products were digested with DpnI (New England Biolabs) to eliminate the methylated parental template DNA. The reaction products were transformed into chemically competent E. coli DH5α cells. Individual colonies were picked, and plasmid DNA was extracted. The presence of the desired mutations and the absence of off-target errors were confirmed by Sanger sequencing.

Pseudotyped virus-based neutralizing assay (PBNA)

The neutralization potency of antibodies was evaluated using a standard PBNA. mAbs or sera were serially diluted (3-fold) in DMEM and incubated with ∼1000 TCID₅₀ of pseudovirus for 1 h at 37°C to allow for antibody binding. The mixture was then transferred to 96-well plates containing confluent MDCK monolayers. After 48 h of infection, luciferase activity was measured using Bright-Glo™ substrate. The half-maximal effective concentration (EC50) was calculated using the Reed-Muench method.

LASSO regression analysis

To objectively identify HA residues associated with neutralization resistance, we performed Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) regression. The dataset consisted of 141 H1N1 HA sequences and their corresponding log-transformed EC50 values against 32D6. Amino acid positions were one-hot encoded as binary variables. The model was fitted using the glmnet package in R. The shrinkage parameter lambda (λ) was optimized via 10-fold cross-validation to minimize the mean squared error. Predictors with non-zero coefficients at the optimal λ were considered significant determinants of the phenotype.

Evolutionary and phylogenetic analysis

Full-length A(H1N1)pdm09 HA sequences collected from 2009 to early 2025 were retrieved from the GISAID database. Sequences were filtered to remove incomplete entries and aligned using MAFFT. The frequency of amino acids at residue 156 was calculated for each year. For phylogenetic reconstruction, a representative subset of sequences was selected. A maximum likelihood tree was constructed using IQ-TREE with the JTT substitution model and 1000 bootstrap replicates. The tree was time-calibrated using the molecular clock method in the Treetime package and visualized using R (v4.4.2) software ggtree.

Molecular dynamics simulation analysis

The crystal structure of the 32D6 Fab and H1N1 HA complex (PDB ID: 6A4 K) with an N156 K mutation, introduced via the PyMOL Mutagenesis Wizard, was used as the initial model. Molecular dynamics (MD) simulations were performed in GROMACS 2021 using the AMBER99SB-ILDN force field. The system was solvated with the TIP3P water model in a cubic box (1.0 nm minimum distance from the protein) and neutralized with 0.15 M NaCl to mimic physiological conditions. Long-range electrostatics were handled by the Particle Mesh Ewald (PME) method (1.0 nm cutoff), and covalent bonds were constrained using the LINCS algorithm. To remove steric clashes, energy minimization was conducted in three sequential stages (restraining the solute, restraining counterions, and fully unconstrained) using 3,000 steps of steepest descent and 2,000 steps of conjugate gradient. Finally, a 100-ns production MD simulation was executed under the NPT ensemble with a 2-fs time step, maintaining the temperature at 300 K (Nosé–Hoover thermostat) and pressure at 1.0 bar (Parrinello–Rahman barostat).

To evaluate the system stability, structural conformational changes, and solvent effects, trajectory analyses were conducted using built-in GROMACS tools. The gmx rms and gmx rmsf utilities were employed to calculate Root-mean-square deviation (RMSD) and Root-mean-square fluctuation (RMSF), assessing the overall conformational equilibration and local residue flexibility, respectively. The overall structural compactness and the exposure degree of the hydrophobic core were evaluated by calculating the Radius of Gyration (Rg, gmx gyrate) and Solvent Accessible Surface Area (SASA, gmx sasa). Additionally, internal structural integrity and interaction dynamics were monitored by analyzing the number of Hydrogen Bonds (H-bonds, gmx hbond) throughout the simulation.

MM-GBSA methodology

The binding free energy of the protein-protein complex and the residue-level energy decomposition were calculated using the MM/GBSA method implemented on the HawkDock server. For structure preparation, non-standard amino acids, heteroatoms, and original hydrogen atoms were removed. Missing heavy atoms and polar hydrogens were subsequently added using the tleap module, and the chain IDs for the 32D6 and HA protein were assigned accordingly. The calculations were performed based on the AMBER ff02 force field and the GB-OBC1 implicit solvent model with an internal dielectric constant set to 1. Prior to free energy calculation, the system was subjected to 5,000 steps of energy minimization (2,000 steps of steepest descent followed by 3,000 steps of conjugate gradient) to remove steric clashes, with the van der Waals cutoff distance set to 999 Å. The method ultimately outputs the total binding free energy, which is decomposed into four individual energy terms: van der Waals interaction ΔEvdw, electrostatic interaction ΔEele, polar solvation energy ΔGgb and non-polar solvation energy ΔGsurf. ΔGbind=ΔEvdw+ΔEele+ΔGgb+ΔGsurf

Results

Neutralization breadth of mAb 32D6 indicates an abrupt antigenic transition

To systematically assess the neutralization breadth and potency of mAb 32D6, we utilized a pseudovirus-based neutralization assay against a panel of 157 H1N1 influenza strains. This panel comprised 141 representative circulating isolates spanning from 1918 to early 2025 and 16 WHO-recommended vaccine candidates. Consistent with the antibody’s initial characterization, 32D6 exhibited potent cross-neutralizing activity against the majority of A(H1N1)pdm09 strains isolated between the 2009 pandemic and early 2019 (Figure 1). EC50 values for these historical strains, such as A/California/7/2009 and A/Michigan/45/2015, ranged from 0.63 ng/mL to 81.86 ng/mL, suggesting the recognition to a highly conserved epitope. The antibody showed negligible activity against pre-2009 seasonal H1N1 strains (e.g. A/Solomon Islands/3/2006), which is consistent with the distinct antigenic properties of the pre-pandemic lineage. However, a marked discontinuity in neutralization efficacy was observed beginning with isolates from late 2019. mAb 32D6 showed no detectable neutralizing capacity against strains circulating from 2021 to 2025. Specifically, for the recent WHO-recommended vaccine strains A/Victoria/2570/2019 (VI19), A/Wisconsin/588/2019 (WI19), A/Victoria/4897/2022 (VI22), and A/Wisconsin/67/2022 (WI22), the EC50 values exceeded the highest tested concentration (>10000 ng/mL). This represents a greater than 1000-fold reduction in neutralization sensitivity compared to the ancestral A/California/7/2009 strain. This dichotomous neutralization profile suggested that a specific antigenic drift event has mediated viral escape from this antibody in currently circulating H1N1 lineages.

Identification of residue 156 as a key determinant of immune escape via LASSO regression

To investigate the molecular determinants mediating this abrupt loss of neutralization, we employed Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) regression. This approach allows for the selection of relevant predictor variables (amino acid mutations) from high-dimensional sequence data while mitigating noise. Correlation of the amino acid sequences of the tested panel with their respective log-transformed EC₅₀ values identified five residues (156, 250, 288, 72, and 195) as primary determinants of neutralization escape (Figure 2, Coefficient > 0.5). We prioritized these candidates based on structural analysis and epidemiological relevance. Residues 72, 195, 250, and 288 are located either in the stem region or at buried interfaces that are less accessible to the paratope of head-binding antibodies [25]. In contrast, residue 156 is situated at the apex of the HA globular head within the Sa antigenic site, a region known for high plasticity. Sequence alignment of the 16 vaccine strains (Supplementary Figure S1) revealed a clear change: strains sensitive to 32D6 (CA09, MI15, BR18, GD19, and HA19) uniformly possess an asparagine (N) at position 156. Conversely, the resistant lineages (VI19, WI19, VI22, and WI22) harbour a lysine (K) substitution. Additionally, some sporadic resistant strains carried an aspartic acid (D) at this position (Supplementary Figure S2). The strong correlation between the N156 K/D substitution and the resistance phenotype suggests that residue 156 is the primary mediator of 32D6 escape. To exclude the possibility of phylogenetic hitchhiking, we analyzed the data using phylogenetic generalized least squares (PGLS). The significant association (p < 0.0001) indicates that site 156 is an independent contributor to the observed antigenic differences. Functional impact of mutations at residue 156

To evaluate the functional role of residue 156, we introduced point mutations into the HA genes of two representative vaccine strains: GD19 (sensitive cluster) and VI22(resistant cluster). We first assessed the impact of these mutations on viral infectivity (Figure 3(A)). In the genetic background of GD19, the introduction of the N156 K substitution resulted in a measurable reduction in infectivity. The GD19-N156 K mutant exhibited an approximately 100 fold reduction in infectivity compared to the wild-type GD19, indicating that the K156 residue is less tolerated in the genetic context of this earlier lineage. The N156D mutation in the GD19 backbone had a negligible effect on infectivity, yet N156D has not become dominant in nature, likely due to other evolutionary constraints or codon usage barriers. We observed an asymmetric effect in the VI22 background. The wild-type VI22 naturally carries K156 and maintains infectivity. However, reversion of this residue to the ancestral state (K156N) significantly reduced the infectivity of VI22. Together, these reciprocal mutational effects indicate that the functional compatibility of residue 156 is strongly dependent on the broader genetic background of the viral lineage. We next evaluated the impact of these mutations on antigenicity. Introducing either N156 K or N156D into the sensitive GD19 backbone abrogated neutralization by mAb 32D6, raising the EC₅₀ to above >1000 ng/mL (Figure 3(B)). This demonstrates that a single amino acid change at position 156 is sufficient to confer resistance to this antibody. To further evaluate the biological relevance of this mutation in a polyclonal context, we tested the mutant viruses against sera collected from guinea pigs immunized with GD19 or VI22 vaccines (Figure 3(C and D)). The GD19-induced sera neutralized the homologous GD19 virus efficiently but showed significantly reduced titres against the GD19-N156 K mutant and the VI22 strain(>4-fold). Conversely, sera raised against VI22 neutralized both the VI22 wild-type and the GD19-N156 K mutant. These results suggest that the N156 K substitution may be a major contributor to the antigenic difference between the GD19 and VI19/VI22 clusters, effectively altering the antigenic profile of the virus.

Global prevalence and convergent evolution of N156K

To reconstruct the evolutionary history of N156 K substitution, we analyzed over 100000 full-length A(H1N1)pdm09 HA sequences from the GISAID database. Temporal tracking of residue 156 (Figure 4(A)) indicates that N156 was conserved in nearly all isolates from 2009 until 2019. The K156 substitution first appeared at low frequencies during the 2018–2019 season. It subsequently underwent a rapid increase in frequency, rising from <10% prevalence in early 2019 to >99% global prevalence by 2023. Spatiotemporal reconstruction suggests that variants carrying this mutation initially appeared as sporadic cases in Asia, Oceania and Europe. After 2019, the substitution spread to multiple continents and circulated over subsequent epidemic seasons, eventually becoming predominant variant (Figure 4(B)). Phylogenetic analysis (Figure 4(C)) indicates that the N156 K mutation did not arise from a single founder event but on multiple independent branches of the tree. This pattern of convergent evolution suggests that N156 K provides an adaptive advantage, likely selected by population immunity directed against the Sa antigenic site. Structural mechanism of immune evasion

To elucidate the structural basis of the viral neutralization escape observed in our functional assays, we constructed structural models of the wild-type (N156) and mutant (N156 K) 32D6-Fab/HA1 complexes and performed 100-ns MD simulations.

Trajectory analysis indicated that the N156 K mutation destabilized the complex. RMSD of the wild-type complex remained stable between 0.5 and 1.5 nm. In contrast, the RMSD of the mutant complex continuously climbed to 2.5 nm, indicating severe conformational distortion and partial unfolding of the complex (Figure 5(A and B)). Rg analysis showed that the wild-type complex fluctuated narrowly between 3.5 and 3.65 nm. Conversely, the mutant exhibited a decreased Rg with broader fluctuations (Figure 5(C)). Although a decreased Rg typically implies a more compact structure, in this context, it suggests structural collapse following the disruption of the native interface. This alteration likely leads to a loss of the spatial geometry required for effective antibody binding. Furthermore, RMSF profile of the mutant displayed multiple peaks exceeding 0.4 nm, indicating increased local residue dynamics (Figure 5(D)). Concurrently, SASA of the mutant increased, exceeding 320 nm². This reflects a less compact conformation and greater exposure of internal residues, consistent with the observed decrease in stability (Figure 5(E)). Biophysically, the substitution of a neutral N with a bulky, positively charged K at position 156 introduces steric hindrance and alters the local electrostatic environment at the binding interface. These structural alterations corresponded to a reduction in non-covalent interactions at the 32D6-HA1 interface. While the wild-type complex maintained a network of 5–15 interfacial hydrogen bonds, the number of H-bonds in the mutant frequently dropped to near zero during the later stages of the simulation (Figure 5(F)). Consistent with these dynamic changes, binding free energy calculations indicated a binding affinity of ΔG = –52.08 kcal/mol for the N156 complex. In contrast, the calculated binding free energy for the N156 K complex was less negative (ΔG = –47.22 kcal/mol), indicating a reduction in binding affinity (Table 1). Taken together, this structural analysis provides a mechanistic rationale that is consistent with the viral neutralization escape observed in vitro. Discussion

The continuous antigenic evolution of influenza A(H1N1)pdm09 necessitates constant vigilance to maintain vaccine efficacy. Influenza virus evolution is typically characterized by a series of mutations in the HA that cause minimal apparent changes in antigenicity, forming an antigenic cluster. This is followed by a critical mutation that triggers significant antigenic drift, a process known as cluster transition [26,27]. Many studies have developed computational models for predicting key cluster-transitions sites, which evaluates the impact of HA protein amino acid sites on antigenic evolution [9,28]. For example, Cheng and colleagues applied machine learning to map the residues, specifically 156, 161, and 250, responsible for the shift from BR18 to VI19 [9]. However, its limitation lies in the lack of experimental validation. In this study, we identify the N156 K mutation within the Sa antigenic site as a potential driver of the recent antigenic drift, facilitating the transition from the GD19 antigenic cluster towards the VI19 lineage.

Substitutions in or near the RBS can affect the antigenic properties of influenza A(H1N1)pdm09 viruses. Position 156 is located on the 150-loop (amino acids 151–159) within the highly variable Sa antigenic site, a classic hotspot for viral evolution and antigenic drift. Previous studies have shown that substitutions in this loop, such as K153E/G or G155E, not only reduce anti-serum reactivity but also alter receptor binding specificity [29,30]. Furthermore, the N156 K mutation has been observed to naturally emerge in ferret models under immune pressure [31]. While our study mainly utilized mAb 32D6, N156 K clearly drives a generalized antigenic shift. This is supported by literature showing residue 156 is a common escape target for multiple distinct Sa-directed mAbs [32]. Furthermore, our study demonstrated that N156 K caused a 4-fold neutralization drop against diverse polyclonal guinea pig antisera, confirming broad immune evasion rather than merely single-antibody escape.

To analyze the escape mechanism of N156 K from a biophysical perspective, we performed molecular dynamics (MD) simulations. The results indicate that when the short-chain, electrically neutral asparagine (N) in the wild type is replaced by the bulky, positively charged lysine (K), large steric hindrance and electrostatic repulsion are introduced at the closely packed antigen-antibody interface. This local physical repulsion forcibly disrupts the original “lock-and-key” fit model, leading to severe dynamic conformational instability (as indicated by increased RMSD and SASA), nearly abolishing the specific hydrogen bond network and significantly reducing the binding free energy (Figure 5). Consequently, this severe structural disruption not only destroys the neutralizing ability of mAb 32D6 but also likely impedes the binding of other Sa-directed antibodies, thereby may contribute to the broad antigenic shift observed at the population level. Importantly, these biophysical data suggest that the N156 K substitution alone is the primary and direct driver for the loss of specific antibody binding. Because mAb 32D6 exclusively targets the HA glycoprotein surface, substitutions in other viral genes are unlikely to physically mediate this localized immune evasion.

It is noteworthy that while the N156 K mutation mediates efficient immune escape, it incurs a significant reduction in pseudovirus entry efficiency. In the context of the ancestral GD19 strain, we found that the N156 K single mutation drastically reduced pseudovirus infectivity by approximately 100-fold. Conversely, when we reverted this residue in the evolved VI22 background back to the ancestral state (VI22-K156N), pseudovirus infectivity was similarly compromised. This bidirectional penalty underscores how the functional consequence of position 156 is profoundly dictated by its co-evolving genetic background. Over time, the evolved lineages have likely accumulated permissive mutations that structurally or functionally entrench the K156 residue, making reversion deleterious. Previous study suggested that the impact of a given mutation on receptor binding or antigenicity rely on highly conditional nature [33,34]. For instance, while certain mutations associated with the 151–159 loop (e.g. N156D or N156Y) can drive antigenic escape while retaining replicative fitness, the severe defect observed with N156 K aligns with the well-documented pleiotropic effects of mutations near the RBS [35]. As previously demonstrated, mutations that alter spatial conformation to evade neutralizing antibodies often inadvertently compromise binding efficiency to receptor [32,36,37]. However, attributing this infectivity defect strictly to a receptor-binding fitness cost remains speculative without further biochemical validation. Alternative mechanisms, such as differences in HA protein expression, defective proteolytic cleavage, or inefficient HA incorporation into pseudovirions, could also account for the observed reduction in entry assays.

Paradoxically, real-world sequence data indicate that strains carrying the N156 K mutation have rapidly gained absolute dominance. Although influenza mutations are multi-directional, the fixation of 156 K suggests a strong evolutionary advantage. The primary advantage of N156 K may lie in its potent immune escape capability, which allows the virus to evade the immense selective pressure imposed by pre-existing herd immunity accumulated from prior infections or vaccinations. We postulate that this strong immune evasion advantage initially compensates for the decreased entry efficiency observed in vitro. However, based on known patterns of influenza virus evolution, single mutations rarely operate in a vacuum [34,38]. If this entry defect reflects a genuine functional penalty, other compensatory mutations must exist within the recently circulating VI19/VI22 lineages to rectify the phenotypic defects caused by N156 K. Notably, since our pseudovirus entry assay utilized an exogenous NA, the compensation required to rescue this specific defect likely resides within the HA protein itself. However, sequence alignments between the GD19 and VI19/VI22 lineages do not reveal a single, obvious point mutation that universally co-occurs with N156 K. This suggests that the epistatic compensation is a highly complex process, likely relying on a combination of intra-HA mutations rather than a simple one-to-one substitution. Future studies employing reverse genetics and deep mutational scanning are warranted to systematically map these epistatic networks. Ultimately, this critical antigenic shift, hypothesized to be coupled with epistatic compensation, served as a potential driver for the rapid global spread, facilitating the global replacement and dominance of the VI22 lineages.

The utilization of mAbs to screen for escape mutants and map antigenic epitopes has become a cornerstone in influenza virus research. By offering precise recognition of HA sites, these biological tools have provided crucial insights into immune evasion mechanisms and antigenic drift [39,40]. Current surveillance relies heavily on ferret antisera, while essential for defining broad antigenic clusters, often obscure subtle intra-cluster evolution due to their polyclonal nature. In this study, our use of mAb 32D6 as a molecular probe demonstrates the necessity of supplementing traditional methods with high-resolution biological tools. Our data suggest that establishing panels of representative human mAbs targeting key epitopes (Sa, Sb, Ca1, Ca2 and Cb) could serve as a molecular early warning system, capable of detecting the emergence of specific escape variants like N156 K that might otherwise be overlooked.

However, a limitation of this study is the lack of direct serological validation using ferret antisera and human convalescent sera from the relevant epidemic seasons, largely due to ethical and accessibility constraints. While our highly precise pseudovirus and guinea pig antisera assays correlate well with standard methods and can detect reproducible fine-scale antigenic changes [10,23], they cannot fully replace standard public health surveillance assays. Therefore, the hypothesis that the N156 K mutation is the potential driver of the cluster transition requires further validation using large-scale human cohort studies and standardized ferret models.

In conclusion, our study identifies N156 K as a key determinant of immune escape in influenza A(H1N1)pdm09. Our biophysical and in vitro analyses reveal that this mutation confers profound broad-spectrum immune evasion by structurally disrupting the antigen-antibody interface. While this localized alteration may incur a reduction in pseudovirus entry efficiency, its significant advantage in evading pre-existing herd immunity, potentially supported by concurrent intra-HA mutations, ultimately drove its rapid global fixation.
