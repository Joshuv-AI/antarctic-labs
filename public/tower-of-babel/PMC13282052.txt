Management of Asymptomatic Breast Implant Rupture in Older Adult Patients: A Scoping Review, Discussion, and Recommendations
Authors: Nicholas A. Vernice, Carter J. Boyd, Kshipra Hemal, Chris Amro, Thomas J. Sorenson, Jenn Park, Nolan S. Karp, Oriana Cohen, Mihye Choi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Summary:Breast implants are a fundamental adjunct in aesthetic and reconstructive breast surgery, with more than 3.5 million recipients in the United States. Implant rupture risk increases with device age, although modern cohesive silicone implants demonstrate markedly improved durability. The Food and Drug Administration does not mandate routine replacement, but recommends surveillance screening for silicone implant rupture beginning 5–6 years postplacement and every 2–3 years thereafter. However, no guidelines exist for managing asymptomatic ruptures in older adult patients. A scoping review was conducted in MEDLINE, Embase, and the Cochrane Library through January 2025 for guidelines on the management of asymptomatic implant rupture in older adult patients; none was identified. Evidence indicates that although surveillance can detect silent ruptures and minimize silicone migration, the clinical impact in older adult patients is limited given the low incidence of symptomatic complications with modern devices and reduced life expectancy. Risks of surveillance include false positives, overdiagnosis, patient anxiety, and unnecessary surgery—particularly relevant in older patients with higher perioperative complication rates. The authors propose discontinuing surveillance for asymptomatic silicone implants after age 75 years and foregoing routine implant exchange. In asymptomatic patients older than 75 with known or suspected rupture, observation is reasonable unless symptoms develop. Symptomatic cases should undergo diagnostic imaging and individualized risk–benefit discussion using a comprehensive geriatric assessment framework. A shared decision-making approach is emphasized, balancing surgical risks, patient comfort with uncertainty, and aesthetic concerns. These recommendations aim to optimize quality of life and avoid overtreatment in older adult patients with breast implants.

Takeaways

Question: What are the appropriate guidelines for managing asymptomatic silicone breast implant rupture in older adult patients, given the lack of current recommendations?

Findings: A scoping review (using MEDLINE, EMBASE, and the Cochrane Library) was conducted and identified no published guidelines for this patient group. Given the limited clinical impact of silent ruptures in older adult patients, we recommend discontinuing surveillance and routine implant exchange after age 75 years.

Meaning: For asymptomatic older adult patients, discontinuing routine silicone breast implant surveillance and avoiding unnecessary explantation after age 75 years can optimize quality of life and mitigate surgical risks.

INTRODUCTION

Breast implants are a ubiquitous tool for augmenting existing glandular tissue or reconstructing the native breast.1,2 More than 3.5 million people in the United States currently live with breast implants.3 In 2024 alone, more than 300,000 individuals underwent breast augmentation in the United States, where it remains one of the most commonly performed plastic surgery procedures.4 Breast implant rupture can occur due to various factors, including trauma, manufacturing defects, or device age. In a 1993 retrospective analysis of symptomatic women desiring explantation of silicone gel-filled breast implants, de Camara et al5 concluded that all silicone breast implants in their cohort that were 10 years old or older showed evidence of either silicone leakage or rupture. The 1993 introduction of highly cohesive, fifth-generation, anatomical form-stable silicone implants, colloquially known as “gummy bear implants,” to the European market established devices designed to withstand silicone leakage and rupture for far longer durations; studies indicate that modern implants have a rupture-free survival rate of 98% at 5 years and 83%–85% at 10 years.3,6 Despite this, historical data on older-generation implant leakage and rupture have maintained their influence. It remains standard of care to educate patients that these devices are inherently semipermanent and require replacement. Two of the largest implant manufacturers, Allergan (AbbVie, North Chicago, IL) and Mentor (Johnson & Johnson MedTech, Santa Barbara, CA), offer lifetime implant replacement with 10 years of financial assistance in the event of rupture; however, Sientra (Tiger Aesthetics, Franklin, WI) offers a lifetime implant warranty with 20 years of financial assistance, and Motiva offers a 10-year warranty against rupture and Baker grades III or IV capsular contracture.7–10 Ultimately, the precise interval of implant replacement is largely left up to the surgeon’s discretion.

Interestingly, the US Food and Drug Administration does not recommend a formal timeline for the routine replacement of breast implants. Instead, it has implemented recommendations for surveillance screening of silicone breast implants, the most recent of which recommend magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) or ultrasound screening at 5–6 years postimplantation, followed by screening every 2–3 years thereafter.11 MRI is considered the gold standard for detecting silicone implant ruptures, with higher sensitivity and specificity compared with ultrasound, particularly in the case of intracapsular ruptures, as silicone is hypoechoic and can produce artifact. For individuals with saline implants, there is no official recommendation for their surveillance, and imaging is often not required to diagnose a rupture, as volume loss alone is usually sufficient.3 Despite this now-universal imaging practice, no randomized trials have evaluated the risks and benefits of surveillance or intervention in asymptomatic patients; moreover, there are even fewer data describing the effects of this practice in women older than 75 years. In response, this study sought to evaluate any preexisting guidelines for the management of asymptomatic implant rupture in older adult patients via a literature search and provide new recommendations to optimize patient satisfaction and clinical outcomes.

METHODS FOR LITERATURE REVIEW

A comprehensive scoping review was performed from inception through January 2025 by authors N.A.V. and C.J.B. in the following databases: Ovid MEDLINE (US National Library of Medicine, Bethesda, MD) (1946 to present); Ovid Embase (Elsevier, Amsterdam, the Netherlands) (1974 to present); and the Cochrane Library (Wiley, Hoboken, NJ), with the search strategy available in the supplemental digital content (See Supplemental Digital Content 1, which describes the full search strategy for searches run in January 2025, http://links.lww.com/PRSGO/F81.). Searches included all appropriate subject headings and keywords related to the concepts of “breast implant rupture,” “breast implant failure,” “elderly,” “geriatric,” or “older patient.” There were no publication date or article type restrictions. Articles were evaluated for the presence of any preexisting guidelines for the treatment of asymptomatic implant rupture in older adult patients. This search did not yield any articles meeting these criteria.

DISCUSSION

Benefits of Breast Implant Rupture Surveillance Screening

Symptomatic breast implant rupture may cause changes in breast shape, size, firmness, capsular contracture, palpable lumps in the breast or axilla, or pain. However, most intracapsular silicone ruptures are asymptomatic and go undetected without surveillance imaging. The sensitivity of plastic surgeons diagnosing silicone breast implant rupture on clinical examination has been estimated to be as low as 30%; as such, most cases of rupture require imaging for diagnosis.12–14 MRI remains the gold standard for detecting asymptomatic silicone implant rupture, as ultrasound, although more cost- and time-efficient than MRI, remains “operator-dependent,” with a high false-positive rate secondary to anterior reverberation artifacts; reductions in image quality from capsular contracture; and difficulties in visualizing the posterior implant surface.15–17

The clear benefit of surveillance is an increased likelihood of detecting asymptomatic rupture, thereby facilitating explantation of ruptured devices and preventing silicone leakage. Silicone is an inert synthetic polymer whose inertia is generally contained well within its prosthetic shell. However, silicone migration can occur from both intact and ruptured implants, traveling within the breast, regional lymph nodes, and to sites as distant as the pleura, ribs, proximal musculature, and abdomen.18–22 Although often clinically silent, palpable siliconomas may develop in the breast or axilla. Concerns regarding this, particularly in patients with a history of malignancy, are warranted as a means of sparing patients unnecessary anxiety or interventions. In a case series of 18 patients with ruptured silicone breast implant–induced lymphadenopathy, 84% of patients experienced systemic nonspecific symptoms warranting further imaging. In 3 of the 5 patients who underwent positron emission tomography/computed tomography scanning, the siliconomas exhibited FDG avidity requiring diagnostic biopsy, which ruled out recurrence of malignancy and confirmed granulomatous reaction to extramammary silicone.23,24 Of note, the median age of the cohort was 56 (48–67) years.24 Additionally, the propensity of older-generation, noncohesive silicone implants to “sweat” silicone has been associated with the formation of capsular contracture.25 However, symptomatic silicone migration is exceedingly rare in newer-generation implants. This risk is further diminished in older adult patients with shorter remaining life expectancies with respect to their younger counterparts.

Despite concerns, silicone leakage has not been definitively linked to the development of any systemic disease process or an increased risk of connective tissue disease.26,27 This is further supported by research demonstrating no significant difference in the levels of circulating autoantibodies between patients with intact versus ruptured silicone implants. In patients whose implants had ruptured, the authors also compared autoantibody levels between ruptured saline and silicone implants, as well as between subglandular and submuscular silicone implants, and found no differences in autoantibody levels among all groups.28,29 Moreover, although increasing attention has been directed toward a potential link between breast implant illness and the presence of silicone implants, the data do not support any difference in the incidence of breast implant illness between the presence of intact or ruptured silicone implants.28,30,31

Saline breast implant rupture is often entirely asymptomatic, presenting as an acute or gradual loss of breast projection. However, because their rupture releases a sterile isotonic solution that is resorbed over time, the potential sequela of maintaining a deflated saline implant in situ are primarily aesthetic.3 As aforementioned, routine surveillance of saline implants is not formally recommended. However, diagnostic imaging in a patient with asymmetrical breasts remains useful as a means of confirming rupture.

Risks to Breast Implant Rupture Surveillance Screening

The most feared risk associated with surveillance screening, although rare, is a false-positive result in an asymptomatic patient who undergoes unnecessary surgery.26 Lotan et al32 reported that more than one-third of asymptomatic ruptures classified as having “suspected rupture” on imaging were found to have intact implants upon removal.33 An international multicenter study by Zingaretti et al33 also reported a 33% false-positive rate at silicone implant explantation for suspected rupture, although most patients had been evaluated with ultrasound alone (51%). This finding is particularly salient given the growing use of clinic-based high-frequency ultrasound (HFUS) performed by plastic surgeons for implant surveillance. Although the sensitivity and specificity of HFUS in detecting implant rupture have continued to improve, older second- and third-generation implants contain less cohesive gel, and these patients more often present with shell collapse, rupture, and thick calcified capsules compared with patients with newer implants, which complicate imaging and lead to higher false-positive rates than their newer generation counterparts, even with HFUS.34,35

More commonly, continued surveillance risks overdiagnosis. The National Cancer Institute defines overdiagnosis as “finding cases of cancer with a screening test (such as a mammogram)… that will never cause any symptoms,” going on to state “some of the harms caused by overdiagnosis are anxiety and having treatments that are not needed.”36,37 The authors extend this principle to breast implants and define overdiagnosis of breast implant rupture as the detection of an asymptomatic implant rupture that would not have caused symptoms in a patient’s lifetime. As with the diagnosis of an indolent cancer, a new diagnosis of implant rupture may galvanize patients to choose explanation without meaningful benefit.

Although there is no absolute definition of “older adult,” the authors have looked to evidence-based cutoffs in the breast cancer screening literature as a basis upon which to inform the development of their recommendations. For example, the United States Preventive Task Force recommends discontinuing breast cancer screening after age 75 years, as there is insufficient evidence to support any net benefit beyond 74 years.38,39 The American Cancer Society, in contrast, bases its recommendations on life expectancy, stating that women 55 years and older can continue surveillance imaging for “as long as a woman is in good health and is expected to live at least 10 more years.”40 This is further supported by data suggesting a possible mortality benefit through screening up to 79 years.41 The average life expectancy for women in the United States is currently 81.1 years.42 As such, although the authors acknowledge that the conversation is nuanced and complex, they have selected age 75 years as the cutoff upon which their recommendations are based, as they believe it is sensitive to the considerations of both the United States Preventive Task Force and the American Cancer Society.

Importantly, the authors maintain that there is a clear benefit to confirmatory diagnostic imaging for symptomatic patients with concern for silicone implant rupture; in fact, the diagnostic accuracy of MRI in patients with clinical signs and symptoms concerning for rupture increased 14-fold as well, and this article maintains that diagnostic imaging remains appropriate at any age for symptomatic patients with concern for rupture.26,43 As previously stated, symptomatic breast implant rupture can present with changes in breast size, shape, or character; breast pain; palpable lumps in the breast or axilla; or the onset or worsening of capsular contracture. If a patient older than 75 years of age experiences any of these symptoms, imaging remains appropriate. Additionally, although the recommendations in this article are intended to apply to both cosmetic and reconstructive cases, the imaging recommendations of the breast surgery and medical oncology teams should always supersede these guidelines in the event that more frequent monitoring is indicated from an oncological perspective or if there are additional related concerns.

Health, Comorbidities, and Life Expectancy

Older adult patients may have unique considerations, including reduced skin elasticity, decreased healing capacity, and an increased incidence of chronic comorbidities.44 Additionally, these patients have a higher incidence of non-disease-related risk factors, such as frailty, polypharmacy, suboptimal nutrition, and cognitive decline, which may further increase the risks associated with surgery.45,46 These factors can influence surgical outcomes and should be carefully weighed when planning treatment and optimized when pursuing any potential surgery.

The authors agree that age alone should not be a contraindication for index implant-based breast reconstruction in older adult patients, given the well-established psychosocial benefits of reconstruction47; however, there is a paucity of data suggesting similar benefits from ruptured implant removal in this population. Research in implant-based breast reconstruction has revealed significant increases in the risk of mastectomy skin flap necrosis, infection, and seroma in older patients with respect to their younger counterparts. This has been quantified by Kim et al48 as a 2% increase in mastectomy skin flap necrosis, 1% increase in infection risk, and 2% increase in seroma risk for every additional year of age from 40 to 70 years; with every additional 10 years of age, the rates of skin flap necrosis increase 20% and infection by 10%. Additionally, Marcadet et al49 reported more broadly that in patients undergoing subpectoral implant-based breast reconstruction after total mastectomy, older adult patients (70 y or older) had a significantly higher incidence of postoperative complications (27.9% versus 14.8% in younger patients).49 These findings are supported by data demonstrating the association between age, impaired wound healing, and reduced immune function.48,50 These concerns and nuances are present in the analogous discussion in the orthopedic surgery literature regarding replacement of other failed prosthetic devices in older adult patients. For example, several studies have concluded that advanced age is an independent risk factor for increased perioperative mortality, with the perioperative risk increasing sharply with age in these cohorts.51,52 For example, Sinclair et al53 reported that the 30-day mortality burden after total knee arthroplasty was approximately 1 in 80 among patients aged 80–89 years, compared with approximately 1 in 720 among patients aged 60–69 years. Despite this, both patients and surgeons continue to proceed with revision procedures with a full understanding of this increased risk burden; moreover, older adult patients without complications have been reported to experience comparable pain reduction and functional improvements with respect to their younger counterparts.

As such, the authors have put forth a comprehensive assessment, adapted from a model offered by Kong et al45 in the orthopedic surgery literature, as a useful adjunct for plastic surgeons in determining whether an asymptomatic older adult patient desiring breast implant removal is an appropriate operative candidate. Shared Decision-making

Ultimately, the decision of whether or not to intervene in these cases is complex. As such, the authors recommend a shared decision-making approach with the patient in which their own discomfort with uncertainty, preoperative surgical risk, associated cost for aesthetic patients, and willingness to undergo additional procedures are all weighed against the benefit of explanting a potentially ruptured implant.54 Zingaretti et al33 offer a salient caution that radiology reports of “suspected implant rupture” on surveillance imaging in asymptomatic patients can result in heightened fear, resulting in psychological and somatized pain, thereby influencing the surgeon’s decision to intervene. In this study, the authors reported a 100% increase in complaints of chest pain and discomfort during clinic visits after routine surveillance imaging reported a suspected rupture.35 It is therefore prudent to have a comprehensive, evidence-based conversation with the patient about the risks of surgery versus the risks of preserving a ruptured implant, and to advise against letting the patient’s fears, though warranted, galvanize the surgeon to operate.

A focus on older adult patients carries with it the inevitability that some of these patients will have received older-generation implants, which carry known higher rates of both rupture and silicone migration. However, given the lack of standardized screening and internal reporting of rupture rates, the multiple implant generations and manufacturers available, and the possibility that patients may not recall or have access to their implant specifications, the authors do not believe there to be a substantial benefit to stratifying recommendations based on implant generation.55 Although knowledge of the implant generation would provide the surgeon with a higher suspicion of rupture, if the patient remains asymptomatic, the recommendations presented in this article remain the same. However, as part of the shared decision-making framework, the authors do believe there is value in discussing with patients that older-generation implants carry these higher rates of rupture and silicone bleed, and associated risk of developing capsular contracture in the future, even if the implant remains intact.

Our recommendations for symptomatic patients with imaging-confirmed evidence of rupture align with current best-practice guidelines advocating for their removal. Symptomatic patients should be advised that extracapsular rupture may necessitate serial surgical intervention to remove all of the extravasated silicone. In cases of rupture secondary to augmentation, some surgeons offer immediate replacement, whereas others will opt to replace the implant in a delayed fashion, thereby allowing the native breast to scar down and restore its normal contour, with the decision ultimately left up to physician preference.3,14 For patients with ruptured saline implants in whom size discrepancy or rippling of the deflated implant shell has rendered the cosmetic result unacceptable, the authors believe it is reasonable to pursue explantation and replacement of the implant as well, although a conversation regarding the risks and benefits of undergoing explantation should be conducted with the patient, including the potential need for a contralateral procedure to achieve symmetry.

CONCLUSIONS

Breast implant rupture is a significant concern in older adult patients due to the increased risk associated with aging implants. Screening for asymptomatic ruptures and surgical intervention for these device failures may not be warranted, given the increased risks associated with the procedure. We believe that this article can be used as a basis upon which a shared decision can be made between the patient and the surgeon regarding screening for asymptomatic rupture and treating asymptomatic device failures of silicone implants in patients older than 75 years.
