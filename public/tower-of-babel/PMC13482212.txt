Supporting the economic and health resilience of last mile adolescent girls and young Women in mining communities: A global and national level comparative policy analysis
Authors: Nina Randolph-Koranteng, Hanna Chidwick, Betty Kwagala, Deborah Mensah, Lydia Kapiriri
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeThe Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) recognize the complex intersections of health, economic development, and mining activities. This is exemplified among adolescent girls and young women (AGYW) who work in artisanal mining (ASM), in Ghana and Uganda, who experience general and poor sexual and reproductive health (SRH) outcomes, partly due to risky behaviors, poverty and poor access to quality healthcare. Achieving the SDGs necessitates translating global goals into actionable national policies, considering local contexts. To date, the literature assessing the degree to which policies address intersecting health and economic needs of AGYW in ASM communities is limited. This paper examines the extent to which global and national (Ghana and Uganda) policies focus on the economic and health needs of last-mile AGYW in ASM communities in Ghana and Uganda.MethodsUsing Daglish's (2020) Ready your materials, Extract data, Analyze data and Distil (READ) policy analysis approach, a comparative analysis was conducted on 14 global (2010−2023) and 27 national (2010–2023) Uganda and Ghana policy documents on mining, SRH, socio-economic well-being, gender and adolescents.Results & conclusionThe global and national policies showed alignment in their commitment to addressing adolescent SRH, attaining Universal Health coverage, youth employability, and child labor. Although the national policies focused on SRH, equitable healthcare access, education, and vocational skills training, they did not specifically address the unique challenges of last-mile adolescents in ASM communities. It is important that ASM-AGYW are identified as vulnerable last mile populations and ensure their SRH needs and intersecting challenges are addressed and prioritized in policies.

Introduction

The intersection of economic development, public health, and mining activities has gained significant attention at both the global and national levels in recent years. Internationally, nations are facing complex challenges associated with the extraction of mineral resources, while simultaneously striving to foster economic growth and enhance public health outcomes, especially for marginalized populations in the mining communities. Recognizing this, the United Nations (UN) Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) were developed to provide comprehensive blueprints for addressing the intersecting challenges of health, economic growth, gender inequality, and promoting decent work conditions [1]. The SDGs may be influencing the increasing focus of national policies on informal economic sectors in developing nations [2]. In order to impact population health, the aspirational targets set forth through the SDGs should be translated into actionable national policies which are customized and adapted to fit specific local contexts [3].

Central to the SDGs and actionable policies is economic well-being, health, and industry, such as the mining sector. Artisanal and small-scale mining (ASM), prevalent in rural and economically disadvantaged regions in lower-income countries such as Uganda and Ghana, offer a means of livelihood for millions of people [4]. The sector often employs women, men, girls and boys, who participate in ASM activities for many reasons e.g. poverty alleviation, livelihood diversification and as a means to finance their education [5], [6], [7], [8], [9], [10], [11]. Although ASM offers poverty reduction and sometimes economic empowerment prospects, it also presents significant social and health challenges that exacerbate age and gender-based health inequalities between the ASM communities and the rest of the country [12].

In ASM communities, vulnerabilities and intersecting factors such as gender and age-related inequalities, economic insecurity, and violence are prevalent. Last-mile children and adolescents working in ASM (classified as one of the “worst forms of child labor) are often school drop outs and have their overall development hampered [13], [14], [15]. Relative to the general populations and other marginalized populations, extreme poverty, young age, and patriarchy in the ASM sector exposes AGYW to risky sexual behaviors and sexual exploitation that often results in Sexually Transmitted Infections (STIs) and unplanned pregnancies and HIV [16], [17], [18]. Sex and gender based violence multiple sexual partnerships, transactional sex, and unprotected sex, which are common practices in ASM communities, increase the AGYW's risk of contracting STIs and HIV [17], [19], [20], [21], [22]. Studies in sub-Saharan Africa, for instance Mali, show that HIV is much higher in mining communities, and among female non-sex workers in particular [23]. This is further exacerbated by migration ([20], [21]; Mkodzongi & Spiegel, 2020) alcohol and substance misuse [17], [22], and limited SRH information. Informal settlements in remote low-resource locations, the availability, access to, affordability, and poor quality of services are potential challenges to SRH management [20].

This evidence is consistent with a recently completed study in ASM communities in Ghana and Uganda, which involved a survey of the ASM-AGYW, focus group discussions and key informant interviews. The findings indicated that ASM remoteness, cultural gender norms, the transient nature of ASM communities, and the economic contexts negatively impacted the ASM-AGYW's social and economic status, often leading them to engage in increased risky behaviors. Limited access to SRH services was also noted [24]. These populations, since they are marginalized, may be contributing to the slow progress of some of Sub-Saharan Africa countries such as Ghana and Uganda, towards achieving SDG targets. The progress has been plagued with challenges such as persisting high prevalence of STIs, unmet contraceptive needs, and insufficient quality of SRH care for adolescents [25], [26], [27], [28], [29], [30], [31], [32], [33].

Ensuring that no one is left behind in the progress to achieving the SDGs demands that the intersecting health and economic needs of the last mile populations such as those working in the ASM communities are identified and addressed. An initial step would be to ensure that their needs and vulnerabilities are recognized in global and national policies. The degree to which this has been achieved is unclear. To the best of our knowledge, there has been limited focus on the global and national policies that impact the intersecting health and economic vulnerability of adolescents, especially those in ASM communities. This paper fills this gap in the literature.

This paper presents a comparative analysis of global and national policy documents in Ghana and Uganda related to health, mining, economic well-being, and gender. We explore if and how the existing policies address the complex socio-economic and health challenges faced by last-mile adolescents in ASM communities in Ghana and Uganda. While the gap between policies and implementation is discussed in the literature, policies provide basis and can support advocacy and context for program implementation [34].

There are three main objectives of this analysis, i) to examine the main economic, health and mining policies at the global level and national level (Uganda and Ghana) that may impact the health and wellbeing of the adolescents working in ASM communitises; ii) to assess if and how the global level policies influence national policies; iii) to identify any gaps and opportunities for integrating the needs of adolescents into policy formulation.

Methods

Study design

Through a descriptive and comparative approach, this study analyzed existing global policy documents and plans along with national policy documents and plans in Uganda and Ghana related to mining, health, economic livelihood/labor, gender, and adolescents. The analysis adopted Daglish's READ approach to policy analyses: (i) ready your materials (ii) extract data (iii) analyze data and (iv) distil your findings [35].

Document search strategy

NRK led the document search, which was completed in two phases. Phase 1 included, i) a manual internet search for policies completed in 2023 through Google and specialized databases, including government repositories from Ghana and Uganda; ii) a manual review of reference lists in the policy documents from Uganda and Ghana; and, iii) a manual search of websites from international organizations such as the World Health Organization (WHO), International Labour Organization (ILO), United States Agency for International Development (USAID), United Nations Educational, Scientific and Cultural Organization (UNESCO), United Nations Population Fund (UNFPA) and United Nations Children's Fund (UNICEF). This search of international organization websites consisted of manually searching the “policy documents” webpage and selecting relevant documents based on the inclusion criteria.

Given the limited number of policies that were found in the initial search, a second phase of the search which expanded the search strategy to include other policy-related documents, such as strategies/strategic plans, and plans of action, to ensure a comprehensive list of relevant documents was obtained, was completed in 2024. This was conducted through Google in addition to the same databases used in the first phase.

In both Phase 1 and Phase 2, search terms were comprised of a combination of keywords including, “Adolescents,” “Youth,” “Child Labour”, “Mining,” “Policy,” “Ghana,” “Uganda,” “Gender,”, “Sexual Reproductive Health”, “Gender Equality”, “Health,” “Technical vocation,” “Education,” “Technical and Vocational Education and Training”, “TVET”, “WHO,” “Decent work,” “Employment,” “ASM,” “Working conditions,” “Girls”, “Women,” “UNICEF,” “ILO,” “UN”, “UNESCO”, “UNFPA”. The second phase added terms like “Strategies”, “Plans of Action”, “Strategic Plan” (see Supplementary file 1, Table 1 for full search strings).

Selection of policy documents

Documents were selected based on an inclusion/exclusion criteria that was aligned with the study objectives (see Table 1, Fig. 1). We included Ugandan, Ghanaian and global policy documents and strategic plans/plans of action that were published between 2010 and 2023. This timeframe was selected because it captured evolutions of national and global priorities before and after the COVID-19 pandemic in the areas of mining, economic growth, employment, education, Technical and Vocational Education and Training, child labour, gender, general health, and adolescent sexual reproductive health, targeting women, young people, adolescents, and children. Data were extracted based on, 1) the focus of the document; 2) if and how the document included adolescents; and 3) any key aspects of the document that were relevant to it being a national or global policy or plan. Analytical strategy

Extracted data were synthesized and summarized according to the categories we identified in the data extraction tool described above. NRK conducted deductive coding based on the data extraction framework and study objectives. The team then grouped these codes by identifying common themes across national policies/plans and global policies/plans, and subsequently compared these themes. The thematic categories included the policy/plan focus, target populations, and scope of interventions. Researcher reflexivity was integrated in the discussion of themes among the team to mitigate bias. Despite some team discussion, the majority of the data extraction and analysis was conducted by NRK. We acknowledge the limitations of this approach and therefore integrated regular team discussions of the data to ensure rigour and limit bias.

Results

Overview

Forty-one documents were reviewed – 27 national documents (14 from Uganda, 13 from Ghana) and 14 global documents. Of the 27 national-level documents analyzed, 16 were policies, 6 were strategic plans, and 5 were Plans of Action from government institutions in Ghana and Uganda. The global documents, however, comprised five policies, one policy guideline, three plans of action, one strategic plan, and four strategies. Documents were identified as policies, strategic plans, plans of action etc., based on the formal title of the documents identified by the government. Of the 27 national-level policy documents, 9 were health-related, 13 focused on socio-economic livelihood/well-being, 2 focused on gender equality, while 3 were mining-related. Policy focus

Health

Ten national (Ghana, n = 5; Uganda, n = 5) and five global documents focused on various health-related aspects such as quality of services, adolescent and youth-focused care, communicable diseases and SRH [36], [37], [38], [39], [40], [41], [42], [43], [44]; WHO and PAHO, 2018; [45], [46], [47]). Ghana and Uganda each had a national document that focused on promoting and maintaining good population health, strengthening healthcare systems, and achieving Universal Health Coverage (UHC) by 2030 [40]. The main objectives of these documents included encouraging healthy lifestyles, equitable access to care, prevention of disease and availability of medicines, infrastructure, and research/innovation. Four national (2 in Ghana, 2 in Uganda) and four global documents also emphasized SRH specifically noting affordable access to care and sexual health (GHS, 2016; GHS, 2014; Uganda MOH, 2017; Uganda MOH, 2020; [44], [45], [46], [48]. In addition to general SRH, Ghana and Uganda each had a policy with a specific focus on preventing HIV/AIDS and STIs, minimizing stigma, and providing guidance and counselling [36], [42]. Improvement of the health and well-being of adolescents and young people through mainstreaming of information, addressing adolescent health concerns, and gender-sensitive and responsive health services in national development processes were predominant areas of focus in one national (Ghana) policy and two global plans [38], [44], [45].

Economic

Uganda and Ghana each had a strategic plan that focused on enhancing and ensuring equitable access to quality education at all levels [49], [50]. One strategic plan in Uganda aimed at narrowing gender gaps in education with a specific emphasis on girls [51]. Similarly, two national (1 in Ghana; 1 in Uganda) and two global plans, aimed at promoting technical and vocational education and training (TVET) to generate employment and entrepreneurship opportunities and encourage innovation [52], [53], [54], [55]. The TVET strategic plan in Uganda also emphasized “business incubation/innovation centres in TVET institutions to boost entrepreneurship and other aspects of TVET” (Uganda MOES, 2019, p. 11). In terms of economic growth, one Ghana, one Uganda and three global documents focused on employment, promotion of decent work, livelihood empowerment for the youth and ensuring access to reliable information and secured services [56], [57], [58], [59], [60]. For example, both Ghana and Uganda each had a plan of action, which sought to reduce all forms of child labour [61], [62], [63]. These documents also focused on implementing systems that provide accessibility to social protection for households affected by and at risk of child labour, boost education enrollment, and provide skills training for children withdrawn from child labour, including school drop-outs.

Mining

Mining-centred documents aimed to address challenges of environmental degradation, occupational safety (including protection from health hazards), community displacement, child labour, inequitable access to and control of resources (especially among women), and lack of a legal and regulatory framework for the ASM sector. At the national level, the main focus of the policy (1 in Uganda, 1 in Ghana) was to ensure adequate occupational health, safety and protection for miners [64], [65]. Furthermore, the Uganda mining policy and related action plan highlighted gender mainstreaming, equity and human rights, eradication of child labour, and formalization of ASM as key priority areas [65], [66]. The Ugandan action plan particularly aimed at eliminating mercury access, use, and environmental emissions from ASM to protect both human health and the environment [66]. The Minerals and Mining Policy of Ghana on the other hand sought to establish a framework for sustainable development by ensuring that small-scale miners operate in a manner that is both economically viable and environmentally sustainable [64]. The policy specifically states the need to, “provide opportunities for artisanal and small scale miners to access financing to upscale their activities” [64], p. 17). Similarly, global plans aimed to create sustainable mining processes for socio-economic growth and poverty reduction [67], [68].

Gender

The national and global documents which included gender considerations focused on addressing intersectional issues that have significant negative impacts on vulnerable groups such as women and children. Two national policies (1 in Uganda, 1 in Ghana) and two global policies had gender equality as their focal areas [69]; MGLSD Uganda, 2019; [14], [70]. All these policies sought to advance gender equality, eliminate gender-based violence (GBV), and advance structural changes and equitable gender norms that prioritize women and girls in attaining sustainable employment, growth, education etc. The national gender-related policy in Uganda particularly highlighted providing comprehensive care for GBV survivors, noting that “the causes and effects of GBV are multidimensional and require a multisectoral approach” [71], p. 20). USAID's gender policy document also emphasized gender equity through the advancement of women's leadership, promoting equitable access to skills development and employment opportunities, and comprehensive support for adolescent girls including sexuality education for all [70]. (See supplementary file, Table 2).

Target populations

We extracted information on the populations identified in the policies and plans – focusing on whether or not the documents highlighted vulnerable populations such as women, children, and adolescents.

Women

Three national policies (2 in Uganda, 1 in Ghana) and two global strategic plans focused on the specific health of women related to SRH, HIV/AIDS, communicable diseases, and equitable access to health services [37], [41], [42]; Uganda MOH, 2020; [44], [46], [48]. Similarly, the African Mining Vision and the IGF Mining Policy Framework specifically directed some interventions towards women who were involved in mining activities [67], [68]. Women were also the primary focus in mining-related policies in Ghana and Uganda [64], [65], [66]. Only one national policy in each country focused on women who experience GBV [69], [71]. Three global gender-related documents (one policy and two strategic plans) also focused on the empowerment and promoting leadership of women [14], [46], [70].

Children

One national policy in each country focused specifically on interventions for children experiencing a lack of health services and basic needs such as water, sanitation, and nutrition [39], [72]. Additionally, five national (3 in Ghana; 2 in Uganda) and three global policies and plans addressed social protection for children including girls prone to and involved in all forms of child labour including the informal sector [39], [61], [62], [72], [73], [74]. Two national documents (1 plan in Ghana; 1 policy in Uganda) and two global documents (one plan, one policy) noted interventions on education and TVET for children [52], [54], [55], [60]. One Uganda plan particularly focused on children in the area of sensitization on the harmful effects of mercury usage in mining [66]. At the global level, two plans focused on children, prone to early and forced marriages, HIV/AIDs, with restricted access to education and skills development and employment opportunities [53], [55].

Concurrently, the national child-centred documents (2 in Ghana; 2 in Uganda) aimed to establish enabling environments for safeguarding children from various forms of violence, abuse, and exploitation through social protection initiatives and providing Early Child Development Services (MGCSP Ghana, 2014; UMGLSD, 2015; UMGLSD, 2020; MESW Ghana, 2010).

Adolescents

Adolescent SRH including STIs, unsafe abortion, and female genital mutilation were the principal focus in one Ghanaian policy and three global plans [38], [44], [45], [48]. Five national health documents (2 Ghana policies, 2 policies and 1 plan Uganda) specifically focused on SRH services for out-of-school adolescents and unemployed youth [37], [38], [41], [42], [43]. The National Youth Policy in Ghana targeted marginalized youth including those in impoverished or remote areas, with disabilities, unemployed, facing mental health issues, abuse, and homelessness [58]. One Ugandan action plan identified interventions for youth, and other marginalized groups who were involved in mining activities [66]. For example, the plan included “developing a curriculum…to train learners, youth… with skills and safer mining practices” ([66], p. 151).

Majority of the policies and plans at a global level included mention or a focus on youth and addressed the susceptibility among youth to early and forced marriages, health, including SRH and HIV/AIDs, and restricted access to education, skills development and employment opportunities [14], [44], [45], [46], [47], [48], [53], [55], [56], [70]. The African Mining Vision, the IGF Mining Policy Framework, similarly directed some interventions towards youth, and other marginalized groups who were involved in mining activities [67], [68]. One global policy also emphasized fostering the collective voices of youth in decision-making and formulation of effective policies rooted in Positive Youth Development (PYD) principles [60].

Across sectors, many documents included specific mention of or focus on AGYW, including four in Ghana, [37], [38], [58], [69], twelve in Uganda [40], [41], [42], [43], [49], [57]; MoES et al., 2013; NEMA, 2019; UMEMD, 2018; UMGLSD, 2015; UMGLSD, 2020; MGLSD, 2019), and nine globally [44], [45], [46], [47], [48], [60], [70]; ECA, 2018; [14]. For example, the Global Accelerated Action for Health of Adolescents in Uganda (2023) focuses on adolescent-responsive health systems and states that AGYW should have “timely access to information, advice, support and protection [about SRH], and they should be enabled to make safe, health and informed choices as they transition through puberty” ([47], p. 18). (See supplementary file Table 3).

Scope of interventions/programs

All documents identified both sector specific and cross-sector interventions.

Sector-specific interventions

Eight national policies and plans (4 in Ghana; 4 in Uganda) identified health specific interventions including peer-to-peer, mass media and entertainment education, community empowerment, youth advocacy, and durbars to promote HIV/AIDS and STI awareness, and behaviour change [36], [37], [38], [40], [41], [42], [43], [75].

Related to the economic sector, ten national documents (5 in Uganda; 5 in Ghana) and four global policy documents identified socio-economic well-being interventions including improving access to quality education, vocational training and apprenticeship, strengthening of STEM-based programs, and the learning of entrepreneurial skills to enhance employability for the youth [45], [49], [52], [53], [54], [55], [57], [60], [72]; MoES et al., 2013; Ghana MELR, 2023; Ghana MOE, 2018; MYSNYA Ghana, 2022; MGCSP Ghana, 2015). Some of these strategies specifically encompassed the integration of Career Guidance and Counselling to encourage the girl child to enter “male-dominated” professions. Two Ghana documents focused on strengthening social protections and socio-economic empowerment for children through schemes such as Livelihood Empowerment Against Poverty (LEAP), National Health Insurance Scheme, free maternal care and school uniforms, school feeding programs and increased Early Childhood Development Centers (MESWG, 2010; GMELR, 2017). Economically, one national and two global documents highlighted the strengthening of youth participatory approaches, and the establishment of opportunities for youth to explore business/entrepreneurship through Incubation programs, apprenticeships, and financial literacy training [50], [53], [55], [58], [60].

In the context of the informal sector, six Ugandan, three Ghanaian, two global documents also indicated the development of a National Workplace Productivity Programme, and the provision of support to young people, especially women and out-of-school youth [45], [57], [60]; CTVET, 2018; MOE Ghana, 2018; GMELR, 2017; Uganda MOH, 2012; Uganda MOH, 2017b; UMGLSD, 2020; UMGLSD, 2012; UMGLSD, 2015. This was intended to help them make the transition from informal to formal employment through improved access to training, business development and financial services, and innovative technologies. Both national and global mining related documents emphasized the establishment of legal and regulatory frameworks for the mining sector (GOG, 2014; NEMA, 2019; UMEMD, 2018; ECA, 2018; IGFMMMSD, 2023). For example, the Ugandan national ASM action plan emphasized, “developing a legal and regulatory framework that clearly defines ASGM operations and provides for the adequate monitoring of their activities, to build the capacity of ASGM” (NEMA, 2019, p. xvi).

Additionally, the African Mining Vision also encouraged the creation of conducive policy environments for increased investment in infrastructure, education and skills development, and enhancement of the viability of ASM through increased access to resources [67].

Cross-cutting interventions

Policy documents integrated health-related and socioeconomic interventions, alongside gender equity initiatives. The gender-related and mining policy documents highlighted most of the cross-cutting interventions. For example, in both mining policies in Ghana and Uganda, health-related interventions included campaigns on HIV/AIDS and Voluntary Counselling and Testing (VCT) programs, alongside gender equity awareness in the mining industry; these were complemented by socioeconomic interventions focusing on laws prohibiting child labour in the mining sector and the creation of sustainable livelihoods for rural mining communities (UMEMD, 2018; GOG, 2014). Similarly, another Ugandan mining plan focused on providing training on alternative incomes, entrepreneurship, and financial skills for women and youth, as well as sensitization campaigns to raise awareness about the impact of mercury usage on human health [66]. These initiatives involved dialogues and meetings with vulnerable populations such as women, youth and children. On the other hand, Ghana's National Gender Policy highlighted promoting decent employment for women through capacity building and training to address economic inequalities, while tackling health issues such as maternal mortality and HIV/AIDS prevention and delivering age-appropriate sexuality education for disadvantaged women, and girls (GMGCSP, 2015).

At the global level, two gender-related policy documents outlined interventions for addressing GBV through behaviour change and communication campaigns, for example, promoting the leadership and well-being of girls and women, sexuality education and equitable access to skills and employment [14], [70]. Another global mining policy document emphasized empowering women in ASM through skill enhancement, and resource provision for their well-being and protection, as well as enforcing laws against child labour [68]. Discussion

To the best of our knowledge, this is one of the few papers that systematically analyzes the degree to which global and national policies and plans address the health and socio-economic needs of vulnerable populations in ASM communities. We found significant alignment between the goals and populations of focus in both global and national policies/plans. It is likely that the global policies and plans, spearheaded by organizations such as WHO, UNICEF, and UNFPA provided frameworks for addressing shared challenges faced by vulnerable populations. The discussion section is organized around the areas where there was alignment and those that were lacking.

Alignment of policies/plans

Health, socioeconomic and gender policies and plans displayed significant alignment between national and global levels, indicating a unified commitment to addressing critical issues such as adolescent SRH, youth employability, child labour and GBV. Global policies from organizations like WHO, UNFPA, and Pan American Health Organization (PAHO) pushed for SRH and comprehensive approaches to health especially for adolescents [47]; WHO and PAHO, 2018; [45], [46], [48]. This was mirrored in national policies (Ghana MOH, 2020; Uganda MOH, 2020; GHS, 2016; GHS, 2014; Uganda MOH, 2017a; Uganda MOH, 2017b; Uganda MOH, 2020). This alignment may be a response to the SDGs, which advocate for global and national efforts to enhance key priority areas, including adolescent SRH. The SDGs represent a universal acknowledgement of how important health and gender equality are and may have garnered the broad support and commitment at national and global levels.

Furthermore, consistent with SDGs 4 (quality education) and 8 (decent work and economic growth), the policies were aligned on the importance of equipping youth with employable skills. The national and global policy documents consistently recognized the complexity of socio-economic issues such as poverty, youth unemployment, and educational disparities, particularly among women and children. These policies prioritized strategies like technical vocational education (TVET), access to quality education, and social protection schemes to foster inclusive economic development (CTVET, 2018; MOE Ghana, 2018; MoES Uganda, 2017; [53], [55], [56], [60]). Literature suggests that TVET, in particular, has garnered significant attention in low- and middle-income countries for its ability to mitigate poverty and enhance youth employment [76]; AU, 2019. This approach of national and global policies is also reflected in the unified decision from African leaders that designated 2018–2027 as the African Decade for Technical, Professional, and Entrepreneurial Training and Youth Employment (AU, 2019).

Differences in policies

It is also important to reflect on the economic and mining policies where there was misalignment between national and global implementation strategies. This could be accounted for by the unique socio-economic, cultural, and political contexts within each country. For example, factors unique to countries such as resource availability, political structures, alignment with local needs, and cultural/religious practices, among others have, been highlighted as pertinent in informing national policies [77], [78], [79]. Furthermore, there were differences between the mining policies in Uganda and Ghana, whereby Uganda emphasized gender mainstreaming and human rights, while Ghana prioritized economic viability and environmental sustainability (UMEMD, 2018; GOG, 2014). These variations may be rooted in the differing local realities, priorities and progress made in the different areas. The need to address context-specific challenges and leverage local opportunities could account for policy variations between countries and at the global level, despite overarching international guidelines [80].

Consideration of ASM adolescents and young Women

Findings revealed that there was limited explicit identification of last-mile adolescents, and ASM adolescents in particular, although there was a significant focus on adolescents and women generally across health, economic, and mining sectors in both global and national documents. For example, although global and national policies recognized interventions for marginalized groups, including youth, adolescents, and children the emphasis remained broad on populations that live in remote or impoverished areas, out-of-school adolescents, and those involved in child labour, without specific targeted attention to last-mile adolescents [37], [38], [41], [48], [58], [66], [67], [68]. Global policies highlighted the importance of prioritizing the needs of adolescents and tailoring strategies to specific regions to effectively tackle a wide range of challenges [56]. National policy documents specifically focused on adolescent SRH, equitable access to health services, educational opportunities, and vocational skills for young girls, women and youth [37], [38], [41], [42], [44], [45], [48]. However, the literature highlights the continued challenge to include and reach last-mile adolescents who reside in mostly rural, hard to reach areas, with programs and services [15], [81], [82]. To effectively address the socio-economic and health challenges experienced by last-mile adolescents, it is important to consider and tackle the multiple intersecting factors that impact their wellbeing including decision-making ability, economic security, and access to healthcare [83]. In addition, TVET at global and national levels could be used as pivotal tools for mitigating poverty and offering opportunities to vulnerable adolescents [39], [61], [72], [73], [74].

Meaningful adolescent involvement in policy-making is crucial for developing holistic approaches aligned with their needs and preferences [84]. International organizations have therefore called for enhanced youth engagement in policy formulation [85]. Current initiatives often show a limited understanding of the most effective strategies for engaging adolescents, thereby impeding their meaningful involvement [86]. This is even more so when it comes to last-mile adolescents who are hard to reach and experience intersecting vulnerabilities, which makes it difficult for them to engage meaningfully [82]. Comprehensive involvement of adolescents (especially those who are most vulnerable) in decision-making processes will foster a sense of ownership and empowerment among young people, ultimately leading to more inclusive and impactful policies tailored to their unique circumstances.

Policy implications for last mile adolescents

While national policies showed alignment with SDGs and global policies, results show significant gaps in specifically targeting last-mile adolescents and young women, especially those in ASM communities. Leaving no one behind in achieving the SDGs by 2030 will necessitate enacting and implementing multi-sectoral policies as well as multi-sectoral collaborations among governments, non-governmental organizations, development partners, the private sector, and communities, among others, to ensure that the last mile populations (such as adolescents in ASM communities) are not left behind [87]. Policy development needs to prioritize last-mile adolescents by including them as a vulnerable group requiring urgent attention, as existing policies lack specific target of this demographic. To fully comprehend the plight of last-mile adolescent girls and young women and the various intersectionalities involved, it is also crucial to increase their appearance and integration in decision-making processes concerning their health and socio-economic well-being at national and global levels. Additionally, these engagement approaches need to take into account the geographic barriers faced by last-mile adolescents who are rurally located, making it challenging for them to access programs and services effectively [82].

Limitations

The study has some limitations. First, we cannot claim that we reviewed all relevant documents since the study was based on only publicly available documents. Second, while we recognized the merit of conducting a systematic gender-based analysis, this was beyond the scope of the study. Lastly, we recognize that what is reflected in the documents may not always translate into action. It, however, provides context for interpreting and understanding any empirical findings.

Conclusion

There were some areas of alignment and misalignment between the global and national (Ghana and Ugandan) policy goals on health, mining, socioeconomic well-being, and gender. While there were variations in the scope and focus between national policies in Uganda and Ghana, there were shared commitments globally and nationally to attain universal healthcare access and promote adolescent SRH, along with empowering women and youth through education, vocational training, employment opportunities and skills development. There was also a consistent emphasis on eliminating GBV and promoting gender equity across gender-related policies globally and nationally.

However, there was inadequate contextualized focus on the specific intersectional challenges of last-mile adolescents in ASM communities in the policies. Adolescents and young women in ASM need to be identified and included among the vulnerable groups that require attention in policy documents. Addressing these gaps in global and national policies and leveraging growth opportunities would require a multi-sectoral and collaborative approach that considers the intersectionalities involved and prioritizes the specific needs and vulnerabilities of last-mile adolescents in mining communities. Incorporating adolescents' perspectives into policy-making processes is therefore crucial for ensuring that their unique needs are effectively addressed. By promoting inclusive decision-making, policies can be more accurately tailored to empower young people, helping them navigate and overcome the challenges they face for a better future.
