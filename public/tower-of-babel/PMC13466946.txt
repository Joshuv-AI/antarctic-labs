Conduction system pacing vs. biventricular pacing for cardiac resynchronization therapy in heart failure: a systematic review and meta-analysis considering randomized controlled trials and observational studies
Authors: Karanjeet Chauhan, Marwan Shawki, Thalys Sampaio Rodrigues, Han S Lim, Geoffrey R Wong, Nathaniel M Hawkins, Gareth Wynn
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimsBiventricular pacing (BiVP) is the gold standard for cardiac resynchronization therapy (CRT) in heart failure with reduced ejection fraction (HFrEF), yet up to 30% of patients fail to respond. Conduction system pacing (CSP) has emerged as an alternative with potential for physiological superiority. Multiple 2026 multicentre randomized controlled trials (RCTs) reported contradictory results, necessitating an updated meta-analysis. This analysis aims to compare the safety and efficacy of CSP vs. BiVP for CRT in patients with HFrEF.Methods and resultsPubMed, Cochrane CENTRAL, and Embase were searched (January 2010–May 2026) for RCTs and observational studies. Pooled risk ratios (RRs) and mean differences (MDs) were estimated using random-effects restricted maximum likelihood models. Twenty-five studies (11 RCTs, n = 1201; 14 observational, n = 3376) involving 4577 participants were included. In the RCT-only analysis, no significant differences were found in all-cause mortality (RR 0.96; P = 0.91), heart failure (HF) hospitalization (RR 0.67; P = 0.12), or procedural complications (RR 0.83; P = 0.44). Conduction system pacing significantly shortened paced QRS duration (MD −19.52 ms, P < 0.001) and improved New York Heart Association (NYHA) class (MD −0.19, P = 0.05). Combined analysis showed larger effect sizes, including lower all-cause mortality (RR 0.74; P = 0.004) and HF hospitalization (RR 0.64; P < 0.001) in the CSP group.ConclusionRandomized evidence demonstrates no significant difference between CSP and BiVP in hard clinical endpoints, despite superior electrical resynchronization and modest functional improvement with CSP. Confirmation of any clinical superiority of CSP awaits adequately powered trials reporting objective outcomes.

Introduction

Heart failure (HF) remains a leading cause of morbidity and mortality worldwide, with an estimated prevalence of over 64 million people globally.1 In patients with HF and reduced ejection fraction (HFrEF), the co-existence of left bundle branch block (LBBB) further impairs cardiac performance by inducing intraventricular desynchrony and accelerating adverse ventricular remodelling.2,3 Cardiac resynchronization therapy (CRT) is a well-established device treatment for this population, reducing HF hospitalization and all-cause mortality.4,5 Both the 2021 European Society of Cardiology (ESC) and the 2023 Heart Rhythm Society (HRS)/Asia Pacific Heart Rhythm Society (APHRS) Guidelines recommend biventricular pacing (BiVP) as the gold-standard approach for achieving CRT in patients with left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) < 35% and LBBB.6,7

Despite its established benefits, BiVP is burdened by important limitations. Coronary sinus lead implantations fail or are complicated in a proportion of procedures, with rates that may exceed 10%.8,9 Furthermore, left ventricular activation from an epicardial site, rather than through the native conduction system, fails to reliably achieve true physiological resynchronization. Overall, up to 30% of patients do not respond clinically or echocardiographically to BiVP.10 Conduction system pacing (CSP)—encompassing His bundle pacing (HBP) and left bundle branch pacing (LBBP), has emerged as a physiologically superior alternative. By directly stimulating the His–Purkinje network, CSP may overcome LBBB at its source and achieve rapid, synchronous ventricular activation.11–13

The comparative evidence base has remained fragmented. Individual studies have varied considerably in their design, CSP modality, patient selection, follow-up duration, and reported outcomes.14–18 Findings on hard clinical endpoints such as HF hospitalization and all-cause mortality have been particularly inconsistent. Initial observational studies strongly supported the concept of superiority of CSP over CRT.11,19,20 However, two large multicentre randomized controlled trials published simultaneously in March 2026 produced contrasting results.21,22

This comprehensive updated meta-analysis aims to provide a contemporaneous estimate of the comparative effectiveness and safety of CSP vs. BiVP.

Methods

Data sources and search strategy

This study was conducted in accordance with the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions, and results are reported following the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) statement.23 Ethics committee approval was not required as this was an analysis of previously published data. This study was prospectively registered on PROSPERO (CRD420261345722). PubMed (MEDLINE), Cochrane CENTRAL, and Embase were systematically searched from 1 January 2010 to 10 May 2026. Additional manual searches were performed on reference lists of included articles. The search strategy comprised controlled and free-text terms across domains related to ‘left bundle branch pacing’, ‘conduction system pacing’, ‘biventricular pacing’, and ‘heart failure’, combined using Boolean operators ‘OR’ and ‘AND’. The full search strategy is detailed in the Supplementary material.

Study selection and data extraction

Randomized controlled trials (RCTs) and observational studies reporting outcomes of CSP compared with BiVP in patients aged > 18 years with a diagnosis of HFrEF (LVEF ≤ 40%) and an indication for CRT were included. Conduction system pacing modalities included HBP, LBBP, and left bundle branch area pacing (LBBAP). Studies were required to include a direct BiVP comparator arm. Exclusions comprised patients with congenital heart disease or prior cardiac transplantation, single-arm CSP studies, studies comparing CSP against right ventricular pacing alone, conference abstracts without full-text data, and animal or paediatric studies. In cases of multiple reports from the same study, the article with the largest patient cohort or longest follow-up containing the relevant outcome data was selected. Three investigators (K.C., M.S., and T.S.R.) independently performed the data search and study selection. Discrepancies were resolved by consensus with the senior investigator (G.W.). Data extraction was performed using a pre-defined extraction checklist into a structured database. This was carried out independently and in duplicate by two investigators blinded to one another’s extraction, with discrepancies resolved by consensus and adjudicated by the senior author where necessary. For one included trial reported only in congress proceedings at the time of writing (LECART; Le Polain de Waroux et al., EHRA 2026), study-level data were extracted from the late-breaking science presentation, accompanying press release, and independent congress coverage. The corresponding author was contacted on two occasions to request individual-level data and clarification of baseline characteristics; no response was received. Extracted values were cross-checked across all publicly available sources for consistency.

Outcome assessment

The following outcome data were extracted where possible for each study, as per the 2021 ESC Guidelines on Cardiac Pacing and Cardiac Resynchronization Therapy and the 2023 HRS/APHRS/LAHRS expert consensus statement on Cardiac Physiologic Pacing for the Avoidance and Mitigation of Heart Failure2,6:

Change in left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF, %) and left ventricular end-systolic volume (LVESV) assessed as mean difference (MD) from baseline to last available follow-up.

Change in left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF, %) and left ventricular end-systolic volume (LVESV) assessed as mean difference (MD) from baseline to last available follow-up.

Post-pacing QRS duration (ms), pacing threshold at implant and at follow-up (V), procedure time (min), and fluoroscopy time (min).

Post-pacing QRS duration (ms), pacing threshold at implant and at follow-up (V), procedure time (min), and fluoroscopy time (min).

New York Heart Association (NYHA) functional class (post-pacing), HF hospitalization [risk ratio (RR)], all-cause mortality (RR), and procedural complications (RR).

Data analysis

Before quantitative synthesis, individual study characteristics were summarized. For binary outcomes (all-cause mortality, HF hospitalization, procedural complications, CRT response, and super-response), RRs with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were used as the measure of effect size; for continuous outcomes (LVEF, QRS duration, LVESV, and pacing parameters), pooled MDs with 95% CI were computed. A random-effects model was fitted using restricted maximum likelihood with the Hartung–Knapp adjustment to estimate between-study heterogeneity (τ2), allowing for study-specific effects. Statistical heterogeneity was quantified using the I2 statistic. Randomized controlled trials constituted the pre-specified primary analysis for all clinical endpoints. Combined (RCT and observational) estimates were pre-planned as secondary sensitivity analyses to assess the consistency and robustness of the randomized findings and to characterize the influence of confounding inherent to observational designs. Combined estimates are accordingly reported throughout as sensitivity rather than primary effect estimates.

Publication bias was assessed using funnel plots with Egger’s regression test for asymmetry. Simple (univariable) meta-regression analyses were performed to explore whether study-level characteristics moderated the estimated treatment effects, with results displayed in bubble plots. A P-value < 0.05 was considered significant for statistical interaction. All statistical analyses were performed using Stata 17.BE (StataCorp, College Station, TX, USA).

Risk of bias and quality assessment

The risk of bias in individual studies was independently assessed by two investigators (KC and MS) using the Cochrane Collaboration’s Risk of Bias 2 (RoB-2) tool for randomized controlled trials. For observational studies, risk of bias was assessed using the Risk of Bias in Non-randomized Studies of Interventions (ROBINS-I) tool. Any disagreements were resolved by a senior investigator (G.W.) to reach consensus. Where key information was missing from published reports, corresponding authors were contacted in an attempt to retrieve full data. Any missing summary statistics were either computed from available data (e.g. deriving total patient counts from subgroup figures) or excluded from the relevant analysis.

Results

Literature search

The initial search strategy yielded 871 papers across all databases searched. A total of 576 duplicates were automatically removed through Covidence, leaving 295 papers for title and abstract screening. On reviewing the titles and abstracts, 255 studies were excluded for unrelated subjects, case series, letters, and editorials. Full manuscripts for the remaining 40 articles were retrieved and analysed. This analysis excluded 15 articles based on full-text unavailability or irrelevance with either the study design (n = 5), intervention/comparator groups (n = 5), patient population (n = 3), or wrong setting (n = 2). A total of 25 studies were therefore included in the final analysis.

All steps of the review process up to and including data extraction and quality assessment were performed through Covidence. A PRISMA diagram illustrating the screening process is outlined in Figure 1.

Clinical outcomes

All-cause mortality data were available from 21 studies. In the primary analysis restricted to nine RCTs, all-cause mortality did not differ significantly between CSP and BiVP (RR 0.96, 95% CI 0.45–2.04, P = 0.91) (Figure 2). In the combined sensitivity analysis of all 21 studies including observational data, CSP was associated with a lower risk of all-cause mortality (RR 0.74, 95% CI 0.61–0.91, P = 0.004) (Figure 3).

Forest plot illustrating all-cause mortality with CSP vs. BiVP in RCTs only.

Forest plot illustrating all-cause mortality with CSP vs. BiVP across all studies.

Heart failure hospitalization was reported in 18 studies. In the primary analysis of nine RCTs, there was a non-significant trend towards lower HF hospitalization rates with CSP compared with BiVP (RR 0.67, 95% CI 0.40–1.11, P = 0.12) (Figure 4). In the combined analysis across all 18 studies, CSP was associated with a lower risk of HF hospitalization (RR 0.64, 95% CI 0.53–0.77, P < 0.001) (Figure 5).

Forest plot illustrating heart failure hospitalization with CSP vs. BiVP stratified by study design.

Forest plot illustrating heart failure hospitalization with CSP vs. BiVP across all studies.

Echocardiographic and functional outcomes

New York Heart Association functional class was assessed in 10 studies. In the primary RCT-only analysis (n = 4), CSP showed a borderline improvement in NYHA class (MD −0.19, 95% CI −0.38 to 0.00, P = 0.05). In the combined analysis across all 10 studies, the pooled improvement was significant (MD −0.32, 95% CI −0.50 to −0.14, P < 0.001) (Figure 6).

Forest plot illustrating change in NYHA functional class with CSP vs. BiVP across all studies.

Echocardiographic reverse remodelling was assessed through LVESV across five studies (one RCT, four observational). In the combined analysis, CSP was associated with a significantly greater reduction in LVESV compared with BiVP (MD −36.95 mL, 95% CI −52.48 to −21.42, P < 0.001) (Figure 7). Cardiac resynchronization therapy response rates (defined as ≥ 5% absolute improvement in LVEF) were higher with CSP in the combined analysis across 17 studies (RR 1.11, 95% CI 1.04–1.19, P = 0.002), with the RCT-only analysis showing a similar direction of effect that did not reach significance (seven RCTs; RR 1.06, 95% CI 0.99–1.13, P = 0.07). Super-response rates (defined as ≥ 15% absolute LVEF improvement or improvement of LVEF to ≥ 50%) were higher with CSP across 14 studies in the combined analysis (RR 1.28, 95% CI 1.11–1.49, P < 0.001); the corresponding RCT-only pooled estimate did not reach significance (four RCTs; RR 1.47, 95% CI 0.78–2.77, P = 0.24). Cardiac resynchronization therapy-defibrillator and CRT-pacemaker implantation rates did not differ between groups (RR 1.01 and 1.00, respectively).

Forest plot illustrating change in left ventricular end-systolic volume with CSP vs. BiVP across all studies.

Safety

Beyond hospitalization, procedural complications were reported in 17 studies. In the primary RCT-only analysis (six RCTs), procedural complication rates did not differ significantly between CSP and BiVP (RR 0.83, 95% CI 0.51–1.34, P = 0.44). In the combined analysis across all 17 studies, CSP was associated with a lower risk of procedural complications (RR 0.62, 95% CI 0.41–0.93, P = 0.02) (Figure 8).

Forest plot illustrating procedural complications with CSP vs. BiVP across all studies.

Electrophysiological and procedural outcomes

The post-pacing QRS duration, a marker of electrical resynchronization, was available from 18 studies. Conduction system pacing was associated with significantly shorter paced QRS duration compared with BiVP across both the combined analysis (MD −16.63 ms, 95% CI −23.59 to −9.67, P < 0.001) (Figure 9) and the RCT-only analysis of seven trials (MD −19.52 ms, 95% CI −29.45 to −9.59, P < 0.001).

Forest plot illustrating post-pacing QRS duration with CSP vs. BiVP across all studies.

At the time of implantation, pacing capture thresholds were lower with CSP across 13 studies in the combined analysis (MD −0.39 V, 95% CI −0.68 to −0.11, P = 0.01) (Figure 10), although in the RCT-only analysis the difference was not significant (four RCTs; MD 0.01 V, 95% CI −0.67 to 0.69, P = 0.98). The same pattern was observed at follow-up: combined analysis across 14 studies showed lower thresholds with CSP (MD −0.43 V, 95% CI −0.58 to −0.29; P < 0.001) (see Supplementary material online, Figure S3), while the RCT-only analysis was not significant (five RCTs; MD −0.14 V, 95% CI −0.59 to 0.32, P = 0.55).

Forest plot illustrating pacing capture threshold at implantation with CSP vs. BiVP across all studies.

In contrast, procedural efficiency outcomes showed no consistent pooled difference. Procedure time was reported in nine studies and did not differ significantly between groups in the combined analysis (MD −10.22 min, 95% CI −29.45 to 9.01; P = 0.30) (see Supplementary material online, Figure S4) or in the RCT-only analysis (four RCTs; MD 4.13 min, 95% CI −18.13 to 26.38; P = 0.72). Fluoroscopy time was reported in 11 studies, with the combined estimate showing a borderline trend towards shorter fluoroscopy with CSP (MD −5.47 min, 95% CI −11.03 to 0.09; P = 0.05) (see Supplementary material online, Figure S5). In the RCT-only analysis, fluoroscopy time did not differ significantly between groups (four RCTs; MD 1.24 min, 95% CI −4.46 to 6.94; P = 0.67).

Risk of bias and publication bias

Among the 11 RCTs, 8 were rated as low risk of bias across all domains and 3 as raising some concerns, as summarized in Supplementary material online, Figure S1. The primary sources of concern related to the open-label design inherent to device trials, absence of blinding for outcome assessment in several studies, and the potential for selective outcome reporting in earlier single-centre trials. Among the 14 observational studies assessed with ROBINS-I, seven were rated as moderate overall risk of bias, six as serious, and one as critical, principally driven by concerns related to confounding and missing outcome data (see Supplementary material online, Figures S2). Publication bias was assessed using Egger’s regression test for the all-cause mortality outcome. The test was non-significant (beta = 0.17, SE = 0.332, z = 0.51, P = 0.37), and the funnel plot did not provide evidence of a significant small-study effect (see Supplementary material online, Figure S6).

Meta-regression and sensitivity analyses

Exploratory meta-regression analyses for the all-cause mortality outcome examined total number of participants, average participant age, mean LVEF, mean baseline QRS duration, and year of publication as potential moderators; no statistically significant interactions were identified (see Supplementary material online, Figures S7–S9), including no significant temporal trend in the treatment effect across the 2015–2026 study period (year of publication, P = 0.78).

Discussion

This comprehensive meta-analysis compares the safety and efficacy of cardiac resynchronization strategies in patients with HF. In the primary RCT-only analysis, CSP resulted in significantly shorter paced QRS duration, with borderline improvements in NYHA functional class and CRT response rates, and without a significant difference in all-cause mortality, HF hospitalization or procedural complications. In the combined analysis including observational data, larger effect sizes were observed across several endpoints including all-cause mortality, HF hospitalization, and procedural complications; however, the majority of observational studies carried moderate-to-serious risk of bias, and these estimates likely overstate the true magnitude of benefit. In particular, the divergence between the randomized and combined estimates for all-cause mortality and HF hospitalization is most plausibly explained by confounding by indication, whereby patients selected for CSP in non-randomized studies may have been at systematically lower baseline risk than those receiving BiVP. This is a clear limitation of the existing literature rather than a property of CSP itself.

In the RCT-only analysis, all-cause mortality did not differ significantly between CSP and BiVP. This finding should not, however, be interpreted as evidence of equivalence. Across the 11 included RCTs, mortality rates were low, follow-up duration was generally modest, and the aggregate effect size remains likely underpowered to detect a meaningful difference in this endpoint. In the combined analysis incorporating observational data, mortality was numerically lower with CSP; however, given that half of the observational studies carried serious or critical risk of bias, this estimate may reflect bias and residual confounding rather than a true treatment effect and should not be interpreted as conclusive evidence of a mortality benefit from CSP. Interpreting the RCT mortality pool is further complicated by the heterogeneous event rates across the five 2026 randomized trials. HeartSync-LBBP recorded an annualized mortality of approximately 1% per year over 36 months,21 substantially lower than the 3–4% per year observed in contemporaneous HFrEF device trials such as DANISH,41 raising the possibility of patient selection or trial conduct effects (such as the unblinded collection of HF hospitalizations in HeartSync-LBBP and three procedure-related deaths in the CSP arm of PhysioSync-HF that may have influenced early event ascertainment). PhysioSync-HF, in contrast, reported approximately 8.4% per year over 12 months,22 a rate more consistent with a high-risk HFrEF population, though one that may also reflect differences in trial populations. The three remaining 2026 randomized trials recorded few deaths and sit between these extremes: His-Alternative I reported 1 of 19 deaths with CSP vs. 7 of 31 with BiVP over a median 64 months, while the two 12-month non-inferiority trials accrued very few events, with LEFT-BUNDLE-CRT recording 0 of 92 vs. 3 of 84 and LECART 1 of 80 vs. 1 of 88. The sparse and unevenly distributed mortality events across these five trials, spanning annualized rates from approximately 1% to 8% per year, underscore that the pooled randomized mortality estimate rests on a small number of events. These divergent baseline event rates introduce substantial heterogeneity into the pooled RCT mortality estimate and together preclude a reliable conclusion regarding the effect of CSP on mortality from the currently available randomized data.

Heart failure hospitalization is a patient-centred endpoint with major implications for symptom burden, quality of life, treatment satisfaction, and healthcare utilization.42 The accompanying improvement in NYHA functional class further supports a meaningful symptomatic benefit with CSP. These findings are physiologically plausible. In contrast to conventional BiV pacing, CSP aims to recruit the native His–Purkinje system and restore a more physiological pattern of ventricular activation, thereby potentially promoting reverse remodelling.43 The direction of effect was numerically consistent between RCTs and observational studies; however, this should be interpreted cautiously given that effect sizes in observational studies were substantially larger, potentially consistent with confounding rather than a uniform treatment effect.

The magnitude of effect varied across studies, particularly for QRS narrowing and LVEF improvement. This likely reflects both patient-level and procedural heterogeneity. Individual-level factors may relate to differences in baseline QRS morphology, differences in comorbidities and HF aetiology and severity. Procedurally, CSP is not a single uniform intervention, but an umbrella term that includes His bundle pacing, left bundle branch area pacing, and, in some studies, deep septal or left ventricular septal pacing approaches that differ in their likelihood of achieving true conduction system capture, therefore outcomes are also likely influenced by operator experience, capture definitions, lead positioning strategy, and success rates for stable LBBAP.44 Furthermore, even standardized LBBAP strategies may not produce uniform resynchronization across patients, as left bundle branch block represents a heterogeneous substrate in which the degree of electrical correction varies with the site and nature of block, lead position, and individual anatomy.45 The 2025 ESC/EHRA consensus statement on conduction system pacing recommends standardized criteria for confirming LBBAP capture—including left ventricular activation time (LVAT) and selective conduction capture—to improve reproducibility across centres.7 This is particularly relevant when interpreting results from high-volume centres reporting very high LBBAP success rates and shorter procedure times than BiV pacing, findings that may not yet be generalisable to lower-volume settings or earlier phases of the learning curve.46 This distinction is powerfully illustrated by the two 2026 index trials. HeartSync-LBBP enrolled exclusively patients with confirmed left bundle branch capture using strict electrocardiographic criteria, operated by physicians performing 286–652 LBBP procedures each prior to the trial, and achieved a 98% CSP success rate.21 PhysioSync-HF, in contrast, employed a heterogeneous CSP strategy in which 20% of patients received deep septal pacing and 9% left bundle branch pacing optimised -CRT rather than true LBBP, with 43% of operators having fewer than 40 prior CSP cases and an overall LBBP success rate of 69%.22 HeartSync-LBBP was a superiority trial powered for a hard composite endpoint (all-cause death or HF hospitalization) over a median of 36 months, whereas PhysioSync-HF was a non-inferiority trial (margin OR 1.2) with a hierarchical composite primary endpoint at 12 months weighted substantially by LVEF change—a fundamental design mismatch that limits direct comparison. These differences in modality definition and operator experience are the most plausible explanation for their diametrically opposed conclusions, and they directly map onto the extreme heterogeneity observed in this meta-analysis for QRS narrowing and pacing thresholds. Future trials should avoid treating CSP as a single homogeneous modality, and instead distinguish between true LBBAP, HBP, and non-selective septal pacing approaches, while standardizing definitions of successful CSP and investigating predictors of clinical HF response beyond QRS duration alone, including LVAT and selective conduction capture.

Pacing capture thresholds were lower with CSP at implant and follow-up in nearly every study despite high between-study heterogeneity in absolute threshold values. Lower and stable capture thresholds reduce battery drain, extend device longevity, and reduce the frequency of generator replacement and its attendant risk of infection and lead-related complications.47 More importantly, CSP uses a single ventricular lead as opposed to BiVP. Battery longevity will therefore be markedly better, with major implications for patients and health system payers.48

Procedural complication rates did not differ significantly between CSP and BiVP in the RCT-only analysis (RR 0.83, 95% CI 0.51–1.34; P = 0.44), though they were lower with CSP in the combined analysis of all 17 reporting studies (RR 0.62, 95% CI 0.41–0.93; P = 0.02). As with other clinical endpoints, this discrepancy between the RCT and combined estimates is consistent with observational confounding rather than a true treatment effect. It is nonetheless worth noting that in the Palmisano et al. registry, complication rates was significantly better with LBBAP than BiVP (4.3% vs. 12.9%, P = 0.036) where the majority of BiVP complications were attributable to the coronary sinus lead, a structural limitation of conventional BiVP that is avoided entirely with CSP.32 Nonetheless, the absence of a significant increase in complications with CSP in the RCT-only analysis is an important safety reassurance, confirming that CSP does not carry a higher procedural risk than BiVP when performed by adequately trained operators. When considered alongside the favourable pacing threshold profile—with lower and more stable thresholds reducing battery drain and generator replacement risk21—the overall safety data are compatible with CSP being at least as safe as BiVP in experienced centres, though long-term prospective safety data remain needed.

Impact on clinical practice and guideline implications

These findings support CSP as a credible resynchronization strategy in HF, particularly in centres with established expertise. Currently, major guidelines from the ESC (2021), AHA/ACC/HFSA (2022), and HRS (2023) maintain biventricular pacing (BiVP) as the gold-standard Class I recommendation, generally positioning CSP as a Class IIa or IIb alternative when coronary sinus lead implantation is unsuccessful.2,6,49 However, the evidence base is evolving rapidly; the 2025 ESC Clinical Consensus Statement acknowledges the expansion of LBBAP and recommends its use by adequately trained operators in centres capable of providing HBP, LBBAP, and BiVP.7 While CSP appears equivalent to BiVP in this analysis, it has not yet demonstrated superiority in hard clinical endpoints.50 Consequently, CSP remains an acceptable alternative for carefully selected patients but should not routinely replace BiVP until larger, adequately powered trials report otherwise.51 This meta-analysis underscores that current randomized evidence is insufficient to elevate CSP to a first-line recommendation for mortality reduction. Definitive guidance is expected from two large ongoing randomized trials—the Left vs. Left Randomized Clinical Trial (NCT05650658) and the Nordic-CSP trial (NCT07492979), each enrolling over 2000 participants—that are currently underway and should provide adequately powered evidence to detect clinically meaningful differences in hard endpoints between CSP and BiVP. However, neither trial is expected to report primary outcome data for several years, and current clinical decision-making must therefore rely on the existing randomized and observational evidence base summarized here.

Limitations

This meta-analysis has several limitations. First, it was based on study-level rather than individual patient data, which precluded time-to-event analyses and limited subgroup exploration according to baseline patient characteristics, HF substrate, and specific CSP technique. Second, CSP was not a uniform intervention across studies, with variation in the use of HBP, LBBAP, and septal pacing approaches, as well as differences in operator experience, capture definitions, and centre expertise. Third, the number of RCTs and their follow-up duration were modest for hard endpoints such as mortality and procedure-related complications, which limits power to detect longer-term differences. Fourth, outcome definitions were not fully uniform across studies, particularly for HF hospitalization, procedural complications, and echocardiographic response, introducing the possibility of residual heterogeneity despite outcome harmonization. Lastly, most studies were performed in experienced centres, which may limit the generalisability of these findings to lower volume settings and routine clinical practice. In addition, the secondary combined analyses pooled randomized and non-randomized evidence; although reported only as sensitivity analyses, the larger effects observed on adding observational studies are most plausibly attributable to confounding by indication and are therefore interpreted as hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory.

Conclusion

Among patients with HF requiring cardiac resynchronization, the primary RCT-only analysis demonstrated no significant difference between CSP and BiVP in all-cause mortality, HF hospitalization, or procedural complications. Conduction system pacing was consistently associated with significantly shorter paced QRS duration, with borderline improvements in NYHA functional class and CRT response rates. The combined analyses incorporating observational data showed larger effect sizes across several endpoints but should be interpreted cautiously given the moderate-to-serious risk of bias characterizing the majority of observational studies. Conduction system pacing represents a reasonable alternative to BiVP in experienced centres in the context of shared decision-making, although adequately powered randomized trials with hard clinical endpoints remain necessary before guideline-level superiority can be claimed.
