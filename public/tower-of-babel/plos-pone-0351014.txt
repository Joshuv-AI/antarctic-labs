Nutritional status, clinical burden, and healthcare utilization among pediatric outpatients with congenital heart disease: A retrospective cross-sectional study from Indonesia

Putri Amelia, Lourents Christian Dika Sahertian, Rizky Adriansyah, Johnson Kannady

ABSTRACT
Congenital heart disease contributes substantially to chronic morbidity, growth impairment, and repeated healthcare utilization among children. Evidence regarding nutritional burden and outpatient healthcare patterns among pediatric patients with congenital heart disease in Indonesia remains limited. This study aimed to evaluate clinical characteristics, nutritional status, healthcare utilization, and factors associated with malnutrition among pediatric outpatients with congenital heart disease at Adam Malik General Hospital, Indonesia. A retrospective observational study was conducted using medical records of pediatric outpatients treated between January and December 2024. Demographic characteristics, cardiac diagnoses, nutritional status, complications, and outpatient visit history were analyzed. Logistic regression analysis was performed to identify factors associated with malnutrition. A total of 606 pediatric outpatients were included. Non cyanotic congenital heart disease predominated the cohort, with ventricular septal defect representing the most common diagnosis followed by patent ductus arteriosus and atrial septal defect. Nearly half of all patients demonstrated underweight or severe underweight nutritional status, while pulmonary hypertension emerged as the most frequent complication. Younger pediatric age groups and higher cumulative clinical burden were independently associated with greater odds of malnutrition. Children with congenital heart disease at this tertiary referral center carried a substantial nutritional and clinical burden. Routine nutritional screening, particularly among younger children and those with greater clinical burden may help identify patients who warrant closer nutritional assessment and multidisciplinary follow-up.

Introduction Congenital heart disease (CHD) is the most common congenital anomaly in children and remains an important cause of pediatric morbidity and mortality worldwide [ 1 , 2 ]. Although survival has improved with advances in diagnosis and treatment, children with CHD in low and middle income countries still frequently experience delayed diagnosis, limited access to specialized care, and chronic hemodynamic stress that may impair growth and nutritional status [ 3 – 6 ]. Malnutrition is a clinically important comorbidity in this population because it affects immune function, neurodevelopment, surgical readiness, and long-term outcomes [ 3 , 4 , 7 ]. In addition to nutritional impairment, children with CHD often require repeated outpatient follo – up, reflecting the chronic burden of their disease [ 8 ]. However, data from Indonesia remain limited, particularly regarding the relationship between nutritional status, clinical burden, and healthcare utilization in pediatric outpatient settings. Adam Malik General Hospital is a major tertiary referral center in North Sumatra that receives pediatric cardiology referrals from multiple districts across western Indonesia. Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate the clinical characteristics, nutritional status, healthcare utilization, and factors associated with malnutrition among pediatric outpatients with CHD at Adam Malik General Hospital in 2024. Methods Study design and setting This study used a retrospective observational design based on secondary data obtained from medical records of pediatric outpatients with congenital heart disease at Adam Malik General Hospital, Medan, North Sumatra, Indonesia. Adam Malik General Hospital functions as a tertiary referral center for pediatric cardiology services in western Indonesia and receives referrals from multiple districts across North Sumatra and surrounding provinces. Data collection covered outpatient visits recorded between January 1 and December 31, 2024. Study population and sampling The study population consisted of all pediatric patients diagnosed with congenital heart disease who attended the pediatric outpatient clinic during the study period. A total sampling approach was applied to include all eligible patients who fulfilled the study criteria. Inclusion criteria consisted of pediatric patients aged 0–18 years with a confirmed diagnosis of congenital heart disease documented by pediatric cardiologists based on transthoracic echocardiography in the medical record. Patients were included only when demographic information, congenital heart disease classification, nutritional assessment, and outpatient visit history were available. Patients with incomplete core demographic data or unclear congenital heart disease diagnosis were excluded from the analysis. Variables and operational definitions The primary outcome of this study was malnutrition. Nutritional status was obtained from medical records, where anthropometric assessments had been routinely performed during outpatient visits according to age appropriate growth standards. For children aged <5 years, nutritional status was assessed using the World Health Organization (WHO) Child Growth Standards (2006) based on body mass index (BMI)-for-age z-scores. Underweight was defined as a BMI-for-age z-score between −3 SD and <−2 SD, whereas severe underweight was defined as a BMI-for-age z-score <−3 SD. For children aged ≥5 years, nutritional status was assessed using the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) Growth Charts based on BMI-for-age percentiles routinely applied in the institution. Underweight was defined as a BMI-for-age percentile below the 5th percentile. Patients with normal nutritional status served as the reference group. For regression analyses, patients classified as underweight or severe underweight were combined into a single malnutrition category. Congenital heart disease (CHD) was classified according to physiological status as cyanotic or non-cyanotic CHD based on the documented echocardiographic diagnosis. Individual cardiac lesions, including atrial septal defect, ventricular septal defect, patent ductus arteriosus, pulmonary stenosis, coarctation of the aorta, tetralogy of Fallot, and transposition of the great arteries, were also recorded and summarized descriptively. Physiological classification was selected for multivariable analysis because detailed lesion-specific hemodynamic parameters, including shunt magnitude, ventricular function, and pressure gradients, were not consistently available in the retrospective medical records. Demographic variables included sex and age group. Age categories consisted of neonates, infants, young toddlers, toddlers, preschool children, school aged children, and adolescents. Because of sparse observations in the extreme age categories, neonatal and adolescent groups were combined in the primary multivariable analysis to improve model stability. Clinical variables included dyspnea, poor feeding, easy fatigability, cyanosis, poor weight gain, pulmonary hypertension, respiratory tract infection, heart failure, failure to thrive, and Eisenmenger syndrome. Healthcare utilization was assessed using the number of outpatient visits recorded during the study period. A cumulative burden score was developed as an investigator designed exploratory composite measure to summarize overall clinical burden. The score was calculated as the total number of documented symptoms and complications recorded for each patient during the study period, with higher scores indicating greater cumulative clinical burden. It was used solely for exploratory analyses within the present study because this composite measure has not undergone formal validation. Data collection procedure Medical records were reviewed systematically using hospital registration databases and outpatient cardiology records. Eligible patients were identified from the hospital outpatient registry using congenital heart disease diagnostic codes recorded in the electronic medical record system and subsequently verified through transthoracic echocardiographic documentation. The diagnosis of congenital heart disease was established by pediatric cardiologists according to standard echocardiographic assessment documented in the medical record. Nutritional status assessments were routinely performed during outpatient visits using anthropometric measurements obtained by trained clinical staff as part of standard pediatric care and interpreted according to the institution’s established growth assessment procedures. Relevant demographic, clinical, nutritional, and healthcare utilization variables were subsequently extracted and entered into a structured data collection form. Data cleaning and verification were performed before statistical analysis to reduce entry inconsistencies and duplicate records. Missing data handling Data completeness was evaluated for all study variables before analysis. Demographic variables, congenital heart disease classification, nutritional status, and outpatient visit history were complete. Symptom and complication variables showed substantial missingness, as summarized in S1 Table . Complete case analysis was applied because of missing data in covariates included in the regression model. Therefore, only patients with complete data for all variables included in the final multivariable model were retained for regression analysis. No imputation was performed because the retrospective nature of the dataset and the pattern of missingness did not support reliable imputation. Missing clinical documentation was considered likely related to inconsistent recording practices rather than true biological absence of symptoms or complications. No imputation procedures were performed because of the retrospective nature of the dataset and the non random pattern of missingness. Complete case analysis was therefore applied for regression modeling. Statistical analysis Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics and R statistical software. Categorical variables were summarized as frequencies and percentages, whereas continuous variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation or median (minimum–maximum), as appropriate according to data distribution. Univariate analyses were conducted to describe demographic characteristics, CHD subtype distribution, nutritional status, clinical manifestations, complications, and healthcare utilization patterns. Factors associated with malnutrition were evaluated using logistic regression analysis. Univariable logistic regression was initially performed to estimate crude odds ratios (ORs) and identify candidate variables. Variables considered clinically relevant or demonstrating statistical association were subsequently entered into the multivariable logistic regression model to estimate adjusted ORs with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). The primary multivariable model included CHD physiological classification (cyanotic versus non cyanotic), sex, age group, and cumulative burden score as prespecified covariates. Odds ratios (ORs) greater than 1 indicated increased odds of malnutrition, and statistical significance was defined as a two sided p-value <0.05. Sensitivity analysis was performed using the original non collapsed age group classification to evaluate the robustness of the primary model. Multicollinearity was assessed using generalized variance inflation factor (GVIF) values. Model performance was evaluated using the Akaike information criterion (AIC), Bayesian information criterion (BIC), Tjur’s R 2 , root mean square error (RMSE), logarithmic score (log-loss), and proportion correctly predicted (PCP). Ethical consideration The Health Research Ethics Committee of the Faculty of Medicine Universitas Sumatera Utara approved this study with ethical clearance number 653/KEPK/USU/2025. Permission to access medical records was obtained from Adam Malik General Hospital before data collection. Medical records were accessed for research purposes between 17/07/2025 and 31/12/2025. Authors had access to identifiable participant information during data collection; however, all data were anonymized prior to statistical analysis. Because of the retrospective observational design using secondary medical record data, the requirement for informed consent was waived by the ethics committee. Discussion This study demonstrated that non cyanotic congenital heart disease dominated the outpatient pediatric population at Adam Malik General Hospital, with ventricular septal defect, patent ductus arteriosus, and atrial septal defect representing the most frequently identified lesions. Nearly half of all patients experienced malnutrition, while pulmonary hypertension emerged as the most commonly documented complication. Younger pediatric age groups and higher cumulative clinical burden were independently associated with greater odds of malnutrition in the primary multivariable model. Higher healthcare utilization was also associated with malnutrition in univariable analysis and remained statistically significant in the sensitivity model, but this finding should be interpreted separately from the primary multivariable results. The predominance of ventricular septal defect, patent ductus arteriosus, and atrial septal defect is also consistent with reports from Southeast Asia. Previous Indonesian studies from Medan and Bandung similarly identified acyanotic or left-to-right shunt lesions as the predominant CHD phenotype, with ventricular septal defect representing the most common diagnosis. The Medan cohort reported malnutrition in approximately 37% of children with CHD, whereas our study observed malnutrition in nearly half of the outpatient population, suggesting a greater nutritional burden in our cohort. A study from Bandung likewise demonstrated that malnutrition was associated with recurrent pneumonia among children with left-to-right shunt CHD, reinforcing the close interaction between nutritional impairment and chronic clinical morbidity in Indonesian children with congenital heart disease [ 9 – 12 ]. Young toddlers represented the largest age group within the present cohort. This distribution likely reflects the period when persistent symptoms, growth disturbances, recurrent respiratory infections, or delayed developmental progress become more apparent to caregivers and healthcare providers. Children within this age range also undergo rapid somatic growth and increased nutritional demands, making underlying hemodynamic abnormalities more clinically visible. School aged children formed the second largest subgroup and may reflect long term outpatient follow up among survivors with stable congenital lesions requiring periodic cardiac evaluation. Earlier studies from tertiary referral centers have reported comparable age distributions with continued healthcare utilization extending beyond infancy [ 11 , 13 ]. Nearly half of the study population demonstrated underweight or severe underweight nutritional status. This finding reinforces the substantial nutritional burden carried by children with congenital heart disease in resource limited settings. Chronic hemodynamic stress may be associated with increased resting energy expenditure and impaired feeding patterns. Pulmonary overcirculation, recurrent respiratory symptoms, and heart failure further increase metabolic demand while reducing caloric intake efficiency. Recurrent illness and prolonged inflammatory stress may also be associated with impaired nutrient absorption and weight gain during critical periods of childhood growth [ 3 , 4 , 8 , 14 ]. Nutritional impairment appeared across both cyanotic and non cyanotic lesions. Although cyanotic congenital heart disease has traditionally been associated with severe malnutrition because of chronic hypoxemia, ventricular septal defect demonstrated similarly high rates of nutritional problems within this cohort. Large left to right shunts may be associated with pulmonary congestion, tachypnea, feeding intolerance, and recurrent respiratory tract infections, which may contribute to impaired growth despite preserved systemic oxygenation. This observation suggests that chronic hemodynamic burden may contribute to malnutrition as strongly as cyanotic physiology itself [ 15 , 16 ]. Similar observations have been reported in Thailand, where approximately two – thirds of children undergoing corrective cardiac surgery were malnourished before intervention. However, unlike the Thai surgical cohort, cyanotic physiology was not independently associated with malnutrition in our study. This discrepancy likely reflects differences in study populations, as the Thai study enrolled children awaiting surgery, whereas the present investigation evaluated a heterogeneous outpatient population with varying disease severity and follow – up duration. These findings suggest that cumulative clinical burden and persistent hemodynamic stress may have a stronger association with nutritional status than cyanotic physiology alone in this outpatient population [ 17 ]. Pulmonary hypertension represented the most common complication identified in the study population. Persistent exposure to increased pulmonary blood flow in untreated or hemodynamically significant shunt lesions may be related to this finding. Pulmonary vascular remodeling may develop progressively when left to right shunting remains uncontrolled over prolonged periods. Previous studies have also documented pulmonary hypertension as a frequent complication among pediatric patients with congenital heart disease, especially in settings where delayed diagnosis or limited access to definitive intervention remains common [ 3 , 6 , 14 ]. Healthcare utilization patterns demonstrated wide variability across the cohort. Most patients attended outpatient services only once or twice during the study year, whereas a smaller subgroup required repeated follow up with substantially higher visit frequencies. Patients with severe underweight status showed broader healthcare utilization distributions compared with children who maintained normal nutritional status. In univariable analysis, high healthcare utilization was associated with greater odds of malnutrition, and this association remained statistically significant in the sensitivity model (adjusted OR 1.54, 95% CI 1.07–2.22; p = 0.020). However, because healthcare utilization was not included in the primary multivariable model, this finding should not be interpreted as an independent association in the primary analysis. The observed relationship may reflect greater clinical burden or other factors that were not fully captured in the retrospective medical records [ 9 , 12 ]. Multivariable regression analysis identified young toddlers and toddlers as the age groups with the highest odds of malnutrition. Early childhood represents a vulnerable developmental period characterized by rapid growth velocity and increased nutritional demand. Children with congenital heart disease may have difficulty meeting these metabolic requirements because chronic cardiac dysfunction may be associated with reduced energy utilization and feeding capacity. Changes in dietary intake during early childhood may also be associated with nutritional vulnerability particularly among children with persistent respiratory symptoms or feeding difficulties [ 4 , 8 , 14 ]. Sensitivity analysis using the original age group classification produced findings that were generally consistent with the primary analysis, although estimates for the neonatal subgroup remained unstable because of the very small number of observations ( S3 Table ). Higher burden scores were associated with greater odds of malnutrition after adjustment for the prespecified covariates. This finding is consistent with the concept that nutritional deterioration in congenital heart disease reflects cumulative clinical stress rather than a single isolated manifestation. Recurrent respiratory symptoms, feeding problems, pulmonary hypertension, and repeated healthcare encounters may collectively reflect greater clinical complexity and nutritional vulnerability. Burden based assessment may therefore provide a broader representation of disease complexity among pediatric congenital heart disease outpatients [ 10 , 12 , 18 ]. Cyanotic physiology did not demonstrate a statistically significant association with malnutrition after adjustment for covariates. Several explanations may account for this finding. First, the relatively small number of cyanotic cases reduced statistical power for subgroup comparison. Second, non cyanotic lesions with substantial left to right shunting may produce severe metabolic stress and feeding dysfunction comparable to cyanotic lesions. Third, outpatient survivors with cyanotic congenital heart disease may already represent a relatively stable subgroup receiving long term specialized care. Similar observations have been reported by studies demonstrating that nutritional impairment in congenital heart disease depends not only on oxygen saturation status but also on hemodynamic severity and chronic symptom burden. The present study has several limitations. The retrospective design relied on medical record documentation that demonstrated substantial missingness within symptom and complication variables. Detailed completeness assessment and proportions of missing data across study variables are summarized in S1 Table . The cumulative burden score was an investigator developed exploratory measure rather than a validated clinical instrument; therefore, its findings should be interpreted cautiously and require external validation. The analysis classified congenital heart disease according to cyanotic and non-cyanotic physiology because detailed lesion hemodynamic severity parameters (echocardiographic measures of shunt magnitude, ventricular function, and pressure gradients) were not consistently available in the medical records and therefore could not be included in the multivariable analyses. Residual confounding by disease severity cannot be excluded and should be considered when interpreting the observed associations. In addition, the study was conducted at a single tertiary referral center, which introduces referral bias. Adam Malik General Hospital receives patients from multiple districts across North Sumatra and neighboring provinces, resulting in a cohort enriched with children requiring specialized cardiac evaluation, long term follow – up, or management of more complex disease. Therefore, these findings should be interpreted as representative of a tertiary care outpatient population rather than the overall pediatric CHD population in Indonesia. The findings support routine nutritional screening among children with congenital heart disease, particularly younger children and those with greater cumulative clinical burden. These patients may warrant closer nutritional assessment and multidisciplinary follow-up as part of ongoing outpatient care. Conclusion Pediatric outpatients with congenital heart disease at Adam Malik General Hospital had a substantial burden of malnutrition, with nearly half of the cohort classified as underweight or severely underweight. Younger age groups and higher cumulative clinical burden were associated with greater odds of malnutrition in the primary multivariable model. Healthcare utilization was associated with malnutrition in univariable analysis and remained statistically significant in the sensitivity analysis, but it was not retained in the primary multivariable model. These findings should be interpreted cautiously because the retrospective cross-sectional design does not establish temporal or causal relationships while missing clinical data and unmeasured disease severity may have influenced the observed associations. In practice, routine nutritional screening during pediatric CHD follow-up, with particular attention to younger children and those with greater documented clinical burden, may facilitate earlier nutritional assessment and appropriate multidisciplinary management. Prospective studies using standardized nutritional and clinical severity measures are needed to confirm these associations.
