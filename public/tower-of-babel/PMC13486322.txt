Compassionate care at end of life and nurses' coping with the patient's death: a cross-sectional correlational study
Authors: Ihab Watad, Daniel Sperling
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCompassionate care and coping with patient death are central components of nursing practice, particularly in internal medicine settings where end-of-life encounters are frequent and emotionally demanding.AimTo examine the relationships between compassionate care, self-compassion, compassion fatigue, compassion satisfaction, and coping with a patient’s death among nurses in internal medicine wards.DesignA cross-sectional quantitative correlational study.MethodsA sample of 256 nurses from two Israeli hospitals completed validated questionnaires assessing compassion competencies and coping with death. Data were analyzed using correlations, t-tests, ANOVA, and linear regression.ResultsCompassionate care was positively related to coping with death (r = .561, p <.001, 95% CI [.46, .64], β = .561, R² = .315), self-compassion (r = .494, p <.001), and compassion satisfaction (r = .550, p <.001), and negatively related to compassion fatigue (r = −.622, p <.001). Both self-compassion (r = .561, p <.001) and compassion satisfaction (r = .595, p <.001) were positively associated with better coping with death, while compassion fatigue was negatively associated with coping scores (r = −.499, p <.001). Participation in advanced professional training was associated with higher levels of Compassionate care (d = 0.36, p <.05), coping with death (d = 0.33, p <.05), self-compassion (d =0.23, p <.05), and compassion satisfaction (d = 0.31, p <.05), and with lower compassion fatigue scores (d = −0.34, p <.05). Additionally, age and clinical experience were positively associated with self-compassion (r = .153, p = .015) and coping with death (r = .195, p = .002) and negatively associated with compassion fatigue (r = −.201, p = .001).ConclusionCompassionate care and self-compassion are positively associated with nurses' ability to manage death-related challenges, while compassion fatigue is linked to lower emotional well-being. Supportive strategies correlate with higher resilience.ImplicationsOrganizational efforts could usefully explore emotional support and training to strengthen compassionate care and address emotional strain in acute care.ImpactThis study explored emotional mechanisms associated with nurses’ capacity to deliver compassionate care and cope with death. It highlights compassionate care as a central factor correlated with professional coping. Findings offer guidance for future interventions targeting variables associated with higher resilience and lower emotional burden.Patient or public involvementThis study did not include patient or public involvement in its design, implementation, or reporting.

Background

Death is a natural part of life, affecting both patients and their caregivers. Nurses frequently accompany patients in their final moments, making end-of-life care a profound and emotionally significant aspect of their professional role (Khalaf et al., 2018; Wilson and Kirshbaum, 2011). This emotional burden occurs within the context of a global nursing shortage compounded by increasing evidence of widespread burnout among nurses across healthcare systems (Getie et al., 2025).

Compassion is central to end-of-life care, as it aims to relieve suffering and enhance patients’ well-being (Larkin, 2015). However, while providing such care can be deeply meaningful, it is also associated with substantial emotional burden and moral challenges (Anderson and Gaugler, 2007), highlighting the need to better understand how nurses cope with repeated exposure to death.

The experience of caring for dying patients

Caring for dying patients is a deeply emotional and often distressing experience for nurses, who frequently develop close relationships with both patients and their families (Khalaf et al., 2018; Kostka et al., 2021). Continuous exposure to suffering may evoke fear, anxiety, and grief (Zhang et al., 2022), especially as nurses accompany patients physically, emotionally, and spiritually (Anderson and Gaugler, 2007).

This proximity to death intensifies the emotional burden, further compounded by professional responsibility and physical demands (Peters et al., 2013; Romão et al., 2025). These challenges underscore the importance of identifying the emotional and professional factors that enable nurses to cope effectively with patient death.

Nurses’ coping and emotional resilience

Nurses employ a variety of coping strategies, ranging from psychological defense mechanisms such as denial (Meller et al., 2019) to more adaptive approaches, including self-care and finding meaning in their work (Peters et al., 2013; Zhang et al., 2022). Seeking support from colleagues and family members also provides important emotional relief (Puente-Fernández et al., 2020).

Despite the growing body of research, existing studies have primarily focused on specialized settings such as oncology or intensive care. In contrast, internal medicine wards represent a unique clinical context, where exposure to death is frequent but often occurs without structured emotional support systems. This highlights a critical gap in the literature regarding how nurses in these settings cope with repeated exposure to patient death.

Theoretical synthesis and study aim

Previous research has largely examined compassionate care, self-compassion, and compassion fatigue as separate constructs. However, emerging evidence suggests that these variables are dynamically interconnected, forming a complex regulatory system. Compassionate care may function not only as a patient-directed practice but also as an internal emotional resource that supports coping (Sinclair et al., 2016). At the same time, self-compassion represents a key variable correlating with emotional resilience and relating to compassionate engagement (Neff, 2003; Beaumont et al., 2016). In contrast, compassion fatigue may represent a significant vulnerability factor, showing strong negative statistical correlations with both compassionate care and coping capacity (Figley, 1995; Sinclair et al., 2017).

Despite these conceptual links, these constructs have rarely been examined within a single integrative framework, limiting a comprehensive understanding of the emotional mechanisms underlying nurses’ coping with patient death. Addressing this gap is essential not only at the local level but also from an international perspective, given the growing need to support a resilient nursing workforce worldwide (Raab, 2014). Furthermore, recent studies have highlighted variability in nurses’ knowledge, attitudes, and preparedness for palliative care across different healthcare contexts, emphasizing the need for further research in diverse clinical settings (Toqan et al., 2023; Awad et al., 2025).

Therefore, the aim of this study was to examine the relationships between compassionate care, self-compassion, compassion fatigue, and nurses’ coping with patient death within a unified empirical model.

Compassion

Definitions

Originating from Latin and Greek roots, compassion means “to suffer with” or “feel for” another person (Ortega-Galán et al., 2021). Though definitions vary, a widely accepted definition of compassion describes it as “a deep awareness of one’s own suffering and that of others, with a desire to alleviate it” (Gilbert, 2009, p. 13). Compassion involves emotional sensitivity to another’s pain, combined with a genuine wish to help them (Lazarus, 1991; Neff, 2003). It is a multivariable concept encompassing empathy, kindness, and a non-judgmental attitude, even toward those who fail or err (Neff, 2003; Goetz et al., 2010; Feldman and Kuyken, 2011). Compassion is often expressed through emotional support, active listening, or altruistic acts (Perez-Bret et al., 2016). Viewed also as a moral expression of shared humanity, compassion blurs the line between self and others and fosters unity and solidarity (Ortega-Galán et al., 2021) and as such is central to nursing care (Sperling, 2025).

Compassionate care

Compassionate care refers to the human reaction to another person's vulnerability that is founded on compassionate interpersonal interactions (Burnell, 2009; Watts et al., 2023). Compassionate care is communicated with a dedication to intervene and alleviate both the other person's and one's own pain and suffering in a courteous, sympathetic manner that upholds their human dignity (Strauss et al., 2016). This involves determining and attending to the patients' physical, emotional, and spiritual needs and listening to them (Brito-Pons and Librada-Flores, 2018). Its foundation is trust and positive relationships between the patient, family, and medical or nursing staff (Skorpen et al., 2020). As a result, compassionate care promotes better mental and physical health as well as stronger social ties. Furthermore, because compassionate care is an interpersonal intervention, it also calls for awareness of the patient's personality and culture as well as the ability to recognize and eliminate any impediments that may be in the way (Ortega-Galán et al., 2021).

Studies also associate compassionate care with better patient health, recuperation, treatment satisfaction, and overall well-being (Robinson et al, 2024; Varghese and Khakha, 2025). Compassionate care is associated with higher well-being, quality of life, and lower pain, worry, and fear (Sinclair et al., 2016). Linked to good communication, it correlates with positive clinical outcomes, including disease management and pain control (Malenfant et al., 2022). Additionally, by boosting patients' hope for recovery, compassionate care may save treatment costs and time (Ortega-Galán et al., 2021), while also resulting in patients experiencing greater contentment, safety, self-control, responsibility, and happiness, which related to their therapeutic experience and healthcare quality (Boss et al., 2024).

Compassion fatigue

Compassion fatigue is a concept derived from compassionate care, describing a specific form of burnout marked by emotional exhaustion, lower empathy, and psychological distress (Figley, 1995; Sinclair et al., 2017). The literature also describes compassion fatigue as secondary trauma, vicarious trauma, or post-traumatic stress (Coetzee, 2010). Two theoretical models explain compassion fatigue: one sees burnout as a precursor to compassion fatigue (Figley, 1995), while another places compassion fatigue along a continuum related to burnout (Mento et al., 2020). In either case, compassion fatigue correlates with negative outcomes for nurses, patients, healthcare systems, and society (Ledoux, 2015). It is generally recognized as a state of emotional and physical exhaustion that results from prolonged exposure to others’ suffering, leading to diminished empathic capacity and compromised professional functioning (Peters, 2018; Baqeas et al., 2021). Exposure to patient suffering and death, especially in palliative care, has been identified as a key contributor to compassion fatigue and emotional burnout in nurses (Wilson and Kirshbaum, 2011; Cross, 2019). Conversely, compassion satisfaction the fulfillment derived from helping others (Stamm, 2010) is an important factor negatively associated with burnout and secondary traumatic stress (Hunsaker et al., 2015), and correlates with higher coping and lower burnout (Galiana et al., 2022).

Self-compassion

Self-compassion is a relatively recent concept in clinical psychology, referring to the ability to relate to one’s own suffering with warmth, care, and understanding (Neff et al., 2023). Neff (2003) identifies three core components of self-compassion: self-kindness (vs. self-judgment), common humanity (vs. isolation), and mindfulness (vs. over-identification).

Studies have shown that self-compassion is linked to better psychological well-being, including lower anxiety and depression, and greater happiness and optimism (Zessin et al., 2015). In nursing, self-compassion is defined as treating oneself with kindness, recognizing one’s shared humanity, and maintaining mindful awareness (Andrews et al., 2020). Clinically, self-compassion correlates negatively with emotional disconnection from dying patients and families and compassion fatigue, while tracking positively with compassionate care (Galiana et al., 2022). Understood as such, self-compassion is considered essential for maintaining nurses' emotional resilience and their ability to provide care to others over time (Andrews et al., 2020).

Compassionate care and coping with a patient's death

Compassion is considered a core element in end-of-life-care, fostering comfort for patients and their families (Skorpen et al., 2020). Nurses who demonstrate high levels of compassion tend to have strong self-worth and a deep commitment to helping others (Malenfant et al., 2022). Compassionate care influences nurses’ perspectives on death, as it requires understanding the patient’s experience and supporting their involvement in care decisions (Mills et al., 2017). Studies have found that a dignified approach to dying is strongly associated with both compassion and resilience (Larkin, 2015). This evidence suggests that compassionate care not only alleviates patient suffering but also protects nurses’ emotional well-being, promoting professional longevity in end-of-life contexts (Andrews et al., 2020; Sinclair et al., 2016).

Conceptual framework

The study draws on Watson’s Theory of Human Caring, which emphasizes the healing power of compassionate nurse–patient relationships (Watson, 1997). Although the theory does not explicitly refer to the situation of a patient’s death, its core concepts are highly relevant to end-of-life-care and to this study. These include the following: Caring presence, that is being emotionally and compassionately present during a patient's final moments; Transpersonal caring relationships, i.e. forming deep emotional and spiritual connections with patients and families; Caring moments, recognizing and responding to meaningful moments in the dying process; Holistic care, addressing physical, emotional, social, and spiritual needs; Promotion of comfort and dignity, that is ensuring culturally and personally respectful end-of-life-care; and Support during grief, which means extending compassionate care to family members during bereavement.

Recent scholarly work increasingly utilizes Watson’s theory as a framework to understand compassionate care and its positive association with nurses’ coping with death. For example, Costello and Barron (2017) evaluated a Watson’s theory-based educational program for senior nursing students, finding higher competence and awareness in delivering end-of-life compassionate care following exposure. Similarly, Dias et al. (2023) conducted a qualitative study on pediatric palliative nursing, where humanized, dialogical, playful, and transpersonal practices align with Watson’s Clinical Caritas Processes, compassionate care, and emotional resilience in coping with death. In addition, Aghaei et al. (2020) explicitly grounded palliative care practice in Watson’s theory, highlighting that caring presence is a powerful nursing intervention. Nurses remain present throughout the dying process, integrating holistic care by addressing the physical, emotional, social, and spiritual needs of patients and their families. These studies emphasize that Watson’s holistic caring approach provides a conceptual lens for understanding compassionate care, caregivers’ emotional resilience, and their coping with death.

Despite theoretical frameworks associating compassionate care, self-compassion, and compassion fatigue with nurses’ well-being, limited empirical evidence examines how compassionate care concurrently correlates with nurses’ coping with death. Prior studies focus on compassionate care alongside quality of care and emotional support in end-of-life contexts (Costello and Barron, 2017; Dias et al., 2023). However, compassionate care is seldom examined as a distinct strategy correlating with coping with death. While self-compassion shares positive linkages with lower emotional burden (Beaumont et al., 2016; Neff, 2023) and compassion fatigue relates to lower resilience (Sorenson et al., 2016), few studies have integrated these variables into a single model to clarify their joint association with nurses’ coping with death.

To our knowledge, no empirical studies have examined the combined associations among compassionate care, self-compassion, compassion fatigue, and nurses’ coping with patient death in internal medicine wards. The present study addresses this gap by examining these variables within a single empirical framework. The findings extend prior research by indicating that compassionate care and self-compassion were positively associated with coping with patient death, whereas compassion fatigue was negatively associated with these variables. Taken together, the findings contribute to current understanding by highlighting compassionate care as both a central component of patient-centered nursing practice and a factor positively associated with nurses’ coping with patient death.

Methodology

Research purposes and questions

The research aims to examine the relationships among compassionate care, self-compassion, compassion fatigue, and the nurses' ability to cope with a patient's death by answering the following research questions: 1) What is the association between compassionate care and nurses' coping with death? 2) How are self-compassion and compassion fatigue correlated with compassionate care and nurses' coping strategies with a patient's death?

Study design

The study pursued a descriptive quantitative design through the delivery of a validated cross-sectional questionnaire to hospital nurses to answer the research main questions. A cross-sectional, quantitative design was employed as it is an effective approach for examining the prevalence of professional and psychological constructs and establishing associations within a specific timeframe. To mitigate the risk of common-method bias (CMB) inherent in self-report data, several procedural remedies were implemented as suggested by Podsakoff et al. (2003). First, participants were guaranteed complete anonymity and confidentiality to reduce social desirability bias and encourage honest reporting. Second, the survey items were pre-tested to ensure clarity and avoid ambiguous wording that could lead to systematic response errors. Finally, the measurement of independent and dependent variables was separated within the survey instrument to minimize cognitive priming. The study was conducted and reported in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines for cross-sectional studies.

Study setting

The participants were nurses working in internal medicine wards at two general hospitals in Israel. The study was conducted in two general hospitals in Israel selected to reflect institutional diversity while maintaining comparable clinical contexts. One hospital is a large tertiary medical center located in the central region, characterized by a high patient volume, advanced medical services, and a heterogeneous patient population. The second is a smaller regional hospital in the northern part of the country, serving a more localized population and operating with fewer specialized resources.

Both institutions include internal medicine wards with similar clinical demands, including the management of chronic conditions, acute medical cases, and end-of-life care. This multicenter design was intended to enhance the external validity of the findings by capturing variability across different healthcare settings while preserving comparability in patient care characteristics (Kennedy-Martin et al., 2015; Rothwell, 2005). The inclusion of hospitals with differing organizational structures (e.g., large tertiary vs. regional settings) allows for a more nuanced understanding of how institutional context may influence nurses’ coping processes.

In addition, multicenter designs are methodologically advantageous because single-center trials tend to overestimate treatment effects relative to multicenter trials, so drawing from multiple sites helps mitigate institution-specific bias (Dechartres et al., 2011; Unverzagt et al., 2013). Proper handling of centre effects and heterogeneity (e.g., via stratification/analysis by centre) associates with greater rigor in multicenter research (Kahan and Morris, 2012) alongside higher transferability and generalizability of findings across settings (Rockenschaub et al., 2024).

Questionnaire building process and description

Research instrument

The study utilized a structured questionnaire comprising of 84 items organized in five sub-scales: 1) Coping with death was measured using the short version of Bugen’s coping with death Scale (Bugen, 1981), containing 9 items rated on a 6-point Likert scale (1–6). Reliability: α = .854; CRI = .870 (Galiana et al., 2019). 2) Coping with death (additional dimension) was also assessed using the Self-Competence in Death Work Scale (SC-DWS) (Chan et al., 2015), including 16 items across two subscales (existential, emotional), rated 1–5. Reliability: total α = .88; existential α = .84; emotional α = .78. 3) Compassionate care was measured via the Compassion Competence Scale (Lee et al., 2016), including 17 items (communication, sensitivity, insight) rated on a 4-point scale. The scale’s total reliability: α = .91, and its subscales: communication α =.88, sensitivity α =.77, insight α = .73. Test–retest: r = .80, p =.001 (Sinclair et al., 2017). 4) Compassion fatigue and compassion satisfaction were measured using the Professional Quality of Life Scale (ProQOL) (Stamm, 2010), 30 items rated 1–5, including three subscales: compassion satisfaction (α =.88), compassion fatigue (burnout α = .75, secondary trauma α = .81); 5) Self-compassion was assessed using the SCS–SF (Neff, 2003), a 12-item tool rated 1–5, covering six subscales (self-kindness, self-judgment, common humanity, isolation, mindfulness, over-identification). Reliability: α = .78 (Raes et al., 2011).

All questionnaires demonstrated good internal consistency in the current sample, with Cronbach’s alpha coefficients exceeding the acceptable threshold of .70. Specifically, reliability coefficients were as follows: compassionate care (α =.798, 17 items), coping with death—scale 1 (CD1; α =.735, 9 items), coping with death—scale 2 (CD2; α =.803, 14 items), compassion fatigue (α =.841, 20 items), compassion satisfaction (α =.867, 10 items), and self-compassion (α =.82, 12 items). These results indicate satisfactory to high internal consistency across all study measures.

The questionnaire also surveyed the respondents’ demographic and professional information including, e.g. age, gender, end-of-life training, and experience with death e.g. causes and medical conditions leading to a patient’s death, age of deceased patients, and frequency of treating terminal patients. Content validity of the study questionnaire was established by three expert nurses (at least master’s level), following a forward–backward translation procedure (English–Hebrew–English) prior to final approval (Patel and Joseph, 2016). The initial translation into Hebrew was performed by a bilingual expert, and the back-translation was conducted independently by a second bilingual translator blinded to the original version. Discrepancies were reviewed and resolved by the research team to ensure linguistic accuracy and cultural relevance. Minor revisions were made based on expert feedback to enhance clarity and contextual appropriateness.

Research population, sampling, and sample

The study population included registered nurses working in inpatient wards, primarily internal medicine, who have experience caring for dying patients for at least one year. A total of 256 nurses were recruited from two hospitals in Israel, based on sample size estimation using G*Power 3.1.9.7. Sampling methods included purposive, snowball, and convenience strategies.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The study included registered nurses working in internal medicine wards who had at least one year of clinical experience and prior exposure to caring for dying patients. Participants were required to be able to read and understand the questionnaire language (Hebrew) and to have access to the internet, as the survey was administered online.

A total of 327 nurses accessed the survey link. Of these, 27 participants did not respond to any items and were excluded from the analysis. An additional 44 questionnaires were incomplete and were therefore excluded. The final analytic sample consisted of 256 participants.

Due to the use of open online distribution methods and snowball sampling, a precise response rate could not be calculated. However, the participant flow is reported to enhance transparency.

Data collection

Initially and following ethics approvals, a pilot study was conducted, during which the questionnaire was sent to 15 nurses from internal medicine departments in hospitals throughout the country. Based on the feedback received, only minor adjustments were made to the questionnaire. These changes were primarily related to clarifying the wording of specific items during their translation into Hebrew and refining the accompanying instructions, without altering the core content or structure of the items. The study was presented during departmental staff meetings, and posters with a code for participation in the study were hung on the departmental bulletin boards. A link to the questionnaire was sent to WhatsApp groups of nursing teams in the hospitals’ internal medicine departments and was also distributed by "snowball" method and through direct invitations from the researchers using contact lists. Data were collected between October 2024 and April 2025.

Prevention of duplicate and fraudulent responses

To ensure the integrity and validity of the dataset, all submitted responses were systematically screened using unique Response ID and IP address identifiers generated by the Qualtrics platform. A comprehensive quality control procedure was implemented, including cross-verification of response patterns and metadata (e.g., completion time and response consistency) to identify potential duplication or irregularities.

No indications of duplicate entries or fraudulent activity were detected. All responses were confirmed to be unique, and no exclusions were required. These procedures enhance the robustness and reliability of the collected data.

Data analysis

Descriptive statistics (means, standard deviations, frequencies) were calculated to summarize participant characteristics and key variables. Pearson and Spearman correlations were used to examine relationships between the quantitative variables. Independent-samples t-tests and one-way ANOVA were conducted to assess differences across demographic groups. Linear regression analyses were conducted to examine associations among the study variables. Skewness and kurtosis were examined to assess normality assumptions. coping with death was assessed using two questionnaires (CD1 and CD2). For combined analyses, scores were converted to standardized z-scores and averaged to create a composite coping with death measure. All analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics, version 29.

Ethical considerations

Ethical approval for this study was obtained from the University of Haifa Research Ethics Committee (Approval No. 286/24 dated 29 July 2024) and from the institutional Helsinki Committees at both hospitals (Approval Nos. 1532-24, dated 18 December 2024 and 0041-24, dated 13 October 2024). All procedures were conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and Good Clinical Practice (GCP) guidelines. Participation was voluntary and anonymous. Data was collected using a coded system to ensure participant confidentiality.

Participation was voluntary, and all participants provided informed consent prior to completing the questionnaire. The consent form included information regarding the study purpose, procedures, confidentiality, and the right to withdraw at any time without penalty.

Participants were recruited through professional networks, including departmental meetings, online communication platforms, and direct invitations. No financial or material incentives were offered for participation.

The survey was conducted anonymously, and no identifying personal information was collected. All data were stored securely and used solely for research purposes. Access to the data was restricted to the research team. Data were analyzed and reported in aggregate form to ensure participant confidentiality.

No questionnaires with missing data were included in the analysis. The online survey was designed to require completion of all items prior to submission; therefore, the final dataset contained complete responses only, and no imputation procedures were necessary.

Results

While several demographic and professional variables did not show significant differences (Table 3), other factors demonstrated significant associations with the study outcomes.

Age and professional experience

Significant positive associations were found between age and coping with death (r =.195, p =.002) and self-compassion (r =.153, p =.015), and a significant negative association was found between age and compassion fatigue (r = -.201, p =.001). No significant associations were found between age and compassionate care and compassion satisfaction. Professional seniority was found to be positively correlated with compassionate care (r =.175, p =.005), coping with death (r =.184, p =.003), and self-compassion (r =.155, p =.013), and negatively correlated with compassion fatigue (r = -.219, p <.001). No significant association was found with compassion satisfaction.

Despite the significant relationships, their strength is weak, indicating a limited contribution of age and seniority to explaining the variation in the indicators.

Religion

A one-way ANOVA revealed a significant difference in self-compassion across religious groups (F (3,252) = 3.84, p = .010). Post hoc analysis using Bonferroni correction indicated that the only significant difference was between Muslim and Jewish participants, with higher mean self-compassion scores observed among Muslim participants (M = 3.72, SD =0.53) compared to Jewish participants (M =3.45, SD = 0.71).

No significant differences were found in compassionate care, coping with death, compassion fatigue, or compassion satisfaction across religious groups. These findings should be interpreted with caution, as they may reflect broader cultural or contextual factors not examined in the current study.

Religiosity

A one-way ANOVA examining levels of religiosity revealed significant differences in compassion fatigue and compassion satisfaction. Secular participants reported higher levels of compassion fatigue (M = 2.39) compared to very religious participants (M = 2.04, p = .031). In contrast, compassion satisfaction was higher among very religious (M = 4.26) and religious participants (M = 4.24) compared to secular participants (M = 3.90), with significant differences observed between religious and secular (p = .003) and very religious and secular participants (p = .025).

No significant differences were found in compassionate care, coping with death, or self-compassion. These findings suggest that religiosity may function as a contextual or personal resource associated with emotional experiences in clinical practice; however, they should be interpreted cautiously and without implying causal or normative conclusions.

Advanced nursing course

Approximately 55.1% of the nurses reported participating in advanced nursing courses. To analyze the statistical association between course completion and study variables, a T-Test was performed. Significant differences (p <.05) were found between those who completed post-basic course and those who did not in all five variables: compassionate care, coping with death, compassion satisfaction, self-compassion were higher, and compassion fatigue was lower among those who took a course. The effect size ranged from small to medium (d = 0.23– 0.36).

Professional training

Initial t-tests indicated that nurses who had undergone End-of-Life or palliative care training reported significantly higher coping with death scores (t (254) = −2.55, p = .006).

To address potential confounding variables, a multiple regression analysis was conducted including age and years of clinical experience.

After adjustment, end-of-life training retained a significant independent association with coping with death (β = .28, p <.001, 95% CI [0.18, 0.39]), whereas age (β = .12, p = .14) and years of experience (β = .09, p = .21) were not significant. These findings suggest that targeted clinical training correlates more strongly with coping ability than accumulated professional experience alone

Marital status

ANOVA by marital status found significant differences in only two variables: coping with death (F (2,253) = 3.78, p =.024, η² = 0.029) and compassion fatigue (F (2,253) = 4.54, p =.012, η² = .035). Bonferroni analysis showed that single people coped with a patient’s death less well than divorced people (p = .024) and reported higher levels of compassion fatigue than married people (p = .009). No significant differences were found in compassionate care, self-compassion, and compassion satisfaction scores, with negligible variance across marital status groups (η² = .006– .010).

Age of dead patients

Non-parametric correlation analysis (Spearman) between groups of ages of deceased patients and compassionate care, coping with death, compassion fatigue, compassion satisfaction and self-compassion revealed weak and non-significant relationships with most variables: compassionate care (rho = .113, p = .071), coping with death (rho = .069, p = .270), compassion satisfaction (rho = .093, p = .137), self-compassion (rho = .108, p =.085). The only relationship that was statistically significant was between age of deceased patients and compassion fatigue (rho = -.128, p = .041), a weak negative relationship. This finding may indicate that death in old age is experienced as less traumatic and may be accompanied by a higher sense of emotional preparedness among the staff.

The regression model was found to be statistically significant (F (1,254) = 95.27, p <.001) and accounted for approximately 31.5% of the variance in coping with death (R² = .315; adjusted R² =.312). Self-compassion

Self-compassion was significantly and moderately positively associated with compassionate care at end of life (r = .494, 95% CI [0.395, 0.581]). Nurses reporting higher levels of self-compassion also tended to report higher levels of compassionate care at end of life.

In addition, self-compassion was significantly and moderately to strongly positively associated with coping with the patient's death (r = .561, 95% CI [0.471, 0.640]) and compassion satisfaction (r = .534, 95% CI [0.440, 0.616]). Self-compassion was also significantly and moderately negatively associated with compassion fatigue (r = −.448, 95% CI [−.0541, −0.344]).

Compassion fatigue

Compassion fatigue was significantly and strongly negatively associated with compassionate care at end of life (r =−.622, 95% CI [−0.688, −0.541]). Nurses reporting higher levels of compassion fatigue tended to report lower levels of compassionate care at end of life.

In addition, compassion fatigue was significantly and moderately negatively associated with self-compassion (r = −.448, 95% CI [−0.541, −0.344]) and coping with the patient's death (r = −.499, 95% CI [−0.586, −0.401]). A strong negative association was also observed between compassion fatigue and compassion satisfaction (r = −.688, 95% CI [−0.750, −0.611]).

Compassion satisfaction

Although not included in the original hypotheses, compassion satisfaction emerged as an important variable in the pattern of associations observed in this study. Compassion satisfaction was significantly and moderately to strongly positively associated with compassionate care at end of life (r = .550, 95% CI [0.455, 0.629]).

In addition, compassion satisfaction was significantly and moderately positively associated with self-compassion (r = .534, 95% CI [0.440, 0.616]) and moderately to strongly positively associated with coping with the patient's death (r = .595, 95% CI [0.504, 0.668]).

Overall, the findings indicate that self-compassion, compassion fatigue, compassion satisfaction, compassionate care at end of life, and coping with the patient's death were significantly interrelated. Positive associations were observed among self-compassion, compassion satisfaction, compassionate care at end of life, and coping with the patient's death, whereas compassion fatigue was negatively associated with all other study variables. Given the cross-sectional design, these findings should be interpreted as associations rather than evidence of causal relationships.

Discussion

Compassionate care and ability to cope with a patient’s death

The significant relationship between compassionate care and coping with death reflects the importance of a compassionate approach to dealing with frequent mortality. Professional compassion is identified in this study as an emotional resource that is linked to maintaining a sense of meaning even in traumatic situations (Sinclair et al., 2016). Our findings are aligned with Figley’s (1995) assertion that compassion functions within an emotional regulation framework associated with lower emotional detachment and are consistent with Duarte and Pinto-Gouveia (2017), who noted that compassion is positively related to psychological resilience and a sense of mission.

However, a cautious interpretation is required regarding their practical implications, given that some studies indicate that providing high levels of compassionate care may co-occur with emotional overload in situations where there is a lack of supportive emotional balance (Lombardo and Eyre, 2011). Indeed, recent findings (e.g., Brillon et al., 2025; Zhai et al., 2025) demonstrate that inadequate emotion regulation and lower psychological resilience share a significant statistical link with higher scores of compassion fatigue, especially in high-stress healthcare and therapeutic professions. These findings suggest that it is important to focus on regulated compassion rather than excessive compassion.

Self-compassion and its association with compassionate care, compassion fatigue, and coping with death

Self-compassion was found to be positively related to both compassionate care and coping with death, and negatively to compassion fatigue, consistent with recent evidence in healthcare showing that higher levels of self-compassion are associated with lower burnout, lower secondary traumatic stress, and better professional quality of life (Beaumont et al., 2016; Jung and Jung, 2025). This pattern aligns with Neff’s (2003) model of self-compassion, which highlights the role of non-judgmental acceptance of personal suffering and emotional regulation (Neff, 2023).

Empirical studies also support these findings: Germer and Neff (2013), Raab (2014), and Beaumont et al. (2016) demonstrate that self-compassion is linked to lower burnout, higher emotional engagement, and presence, even in highly stressful therapeutic contexts. More recent systematic reviews and meta-analyses reinforce this evidence, indicating that while longitudinal interventions aimed at cultivating self-compassion are known to be positively associated with psychological resilience and overall well-being among healthcare professionals (Wasson et al., 2020; Conversano et al., 2020; Martins et al., 2025). The current cross-sectional findings further substantiate these linkages by demonstrating strong concurrent correlations within the baseline nursing cohort.

Compassion fatigue and its influence on compassionate care and coping with death

Consistent with previous research, the present study found that compassion fatigue was negatively associated with both compassionate care and coping with a patient’s death. These findings support the growing body of evidence identifying compassion fatigue as a significant challenge for nurses working in emotionally demanding clinical environments (Sorenson et al., 2016; Karabey, 2023). Compassion fatigue, defined as emotional and psychological exhaustion resulting from repeated exposure to others’ suffering (Sinclair et al., 2017), has been associated with increased emotional distress, lower empathy, and difficulties maintaining therapeutic engagement during end-of-life care (Papworth et al., 2023). Previous studies have further shown that compassion fatigue may contribute to emotional withdrawal, diminished professional fulfillment, and a sense of detachment from patients (Hinderer et al., 2014). Such consequences may negatively affect both nurses’ well-being and the quality of care provided to patients facing the end of life.

At the same time, the literature suggests that compassion fatigue is a modifiable phenomenon. Resilience-building strategies, supportive organizational cultures, reflective practice, and professional development programs correlate with lower levels of compassion fatigue and stable emotional functioning among nurses (Sorenson et al., 2016). From the perspective of Watson’s Theory of Human Caring, excessive emotional burden may interfere with nurses’ capacity to establish authentic caring relationships, whereas supportive environments may help preserve compassionate engagement despite repeated exposure to death and suffering.

Taken together, the findings highlight compassion fatigue as both a risk factor and an important target for intervention. Promoting self-compassion, resilience, support, and balanced workloads are relevant, as these factors correlate with lower compassion fatigue and sustained compassionate care. Such interventions target resources linked to higher levels of effective coping with death and stable, high-quality, person-centered end-of-life care (Sinclair et al., 2017; Hunsaker et al., 2015).

Compassion satisfaction as a reinforcing factor

Compassion satisfaction reflects the emotional fulfillment and sense of meaning healthcare professionals derive from delivering compassionate care (Baqeas et al., 2021). Our findings underscore its central role and strong statistical overlap with nurses’ emotional and professional responses. Though not part of the initial hypotheses, compassion satisfaction emerged as a key variable strongly associated with compassionate care, self-compassion, and coping with death.

This pattern aligns with recent evidence: Yu and Gui (2022) demonstrated that compassion satisfaction shares a strong inverse correlation with burnout and compassion fatigue, while social support is positively related to compassion satisfaction and negatively linked to compassion fatigue. Similarly, Yeşil et al. (2023) found that compassion satisfaction correlates negatively with occupational psychological risks and positively with emotional resilience. Moreover, Rayani et al. (2024) reported that compassion satisfaction is positively correlated with job satisfaction and negatively associated with both burnout and secondary traumatic stress among nurses. These studies converge with our findings, affirming that compassion satisfaction functions as a psychological buffer concurrently linked to a nurse's capacity to maintain compassionate care during emotionally burdensome conditions, such as coping with death. Combined, these findings suggest that higher compassion satisfaction is closely aligned with higher coping capacities, emotional well-being, and sustained compassionate care during end-of-life clinical situations.

Compassion differences between medical institutions

Despite similar patient profiles, smaller hospitals exhibited higher compassionate care and coping with death scores. This may reflect differences in organizational climate, leadership practices, and interpersonal connectedness, although these factors were not directly measured in the present study (Laschinger and Fida, 2014). Research consistently shows that a positive workplace climate and supportive leadership are associated with higher emotional resilience and engagement (Manomenidis et al., 2019) and lower stress through better communication (Kiptulon et al., 2024). Conversely, large hospitals often operate under heavier workloads and rigid bureaucratic structures that limit autonomy and co-occur with fewer opportunities for meaningful patient interaction, which are critical conditions for sustaining compassion (Kelly et al., 2021; Wei et al., 2018; Leach et al., 2024). Greater exposure to high-intensity clinical environments is also linked to higher scores of compassion fatigue (Burden et al., 2023; Upton, 2018). These patterns underscore the link between organizational climate and nurses’ emotional resources. Further qualitative work could help identify which elements of smaller institutions are concurrently related to supportive compassionate care.

Taken together, the findings support an integrative perspective in which compassionate care, self-compassion, and compassion fatigue operate as interrelated components correlated with nurses’ coping with patient death. Consistent with Watson’s Theory of Human Caring (Watson, 1997) and recent empirical evidence (Bidik and Sisman, 2024; Nikbakht et al., 2025; Galica et al., 2025), the results highlight the central role of emotional and professional resources regarding resilience. This integrated framework advances current understanding by mapping how resource factors (compassionate care, self-compassion) and risk factors (compassion fatigue) are jointly associated with nurses’ ability to cope with the emotional demands of end-of-life care.

Conclusions

The findings of this study highlight the significant concurrent links between emotional and professional factors and nurses’ ability to cope with patient death. Older and more experienced nurses, as well as those who completed advanced professional training, tended to report more favorable emotional outcomes.

The results underscore the interconnected nature of compassionate care, self-compassion, and compassion fatigue as key components of nurses’ emotional functioning. Self-compassion correlated positively with concurrent resilience and compassionate engagement, whereas compassion fatigue was linked to lower coping capacity and quality of care.

Moreover, the positive association between compassionate care and coping with death suggests a strong psychological alignment. Nurses who report higher levels of compassionate care also exhibit a higher capacity to cope with repeated exposure to patient death, suggesting that compassionate care is concurrently tied to a vital psychological resource that co-occurs with stable professional functioning in emotionally demanding contexts.

Taken together, this study provides a more nuanced and integrative understanding of the emotional processes underlying nurses’ coping in end-of-life care. By positioning compassionate care, self-compassion, and compassion fatigue within a unified framework, the findings extend current knowledge beyond patient outcomes to include caregiver resilience, highlighting the statistical relevance of nurses’ emotional well-being as a core component of high-quality, sustainable care.

Implications for practice, education, and research

The findings suggest that nurses’ emotional resources are closely associated with compassionate care and coping with patient death. Healthcare organizations may benefit from implementing strategies that promote self-compassion, emotional resilience, and supportive work environments, particularly in settings characterized by frequent exposure to suffering and death. Such efforts may align with lower compassion fatigue levels and the presence of compassionate care.

The results also highlight the importance of integrating self-compassion, emotional regulation, and end-of-life communication into nursing education. Educational frameworks informed by Watson’s Theory of Human Caring may support the development of reflective and compassionate practitioners who are better prepared for the emotional demands of clinical practice.

Future research should employ longitudinal and intervention-based designs to clarify causal relationships among self-compassion, compassion fatigue, compassionate care, and coping with death. In addition, qualitative studies may provide deeper insight into the organizational and personal factors that support nurses’ resilience in end-of-life care settings.

Study limitations

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference regarding the relationships among compassionate care, self-compassion, compassion fatigue, and coping with patient death. Longitudinal and intervention-based studies are needed to clarify the directionality of these associations.

Second, data were collected using self-report questionnaires, which may be subject to social desirability and response biases (Tourangeau and Yan, 2007). Although validated instruments were employed, common method bias cannot be completely ruled out because all variables were assessed using the same source (Podsakoff et al., 2003).

Third, the use of convenience and snowball sampling may have introduced selection bias and limited sample representativeness. Furthermore, the study was conducted among nurses working in internal medicine wards in Israel, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to other clinical settings and cultural contexts.

Finally, the study focused primarily on individual emotional factors and did not examine organizational variables, such as workplace culture, leadership support, staffing levels, or team dynamics, which may also influence nurses’ coping with patient death. Future research should incorporate these factors to provide a more comprehensive understanding of the mechanisms underlying resilience and compassionate care.
