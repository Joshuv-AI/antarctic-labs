Neglected cutaneous clear-cell squamous cell carcinoma of the scalp in an elderly patient with medical avoidance: a case report

Background

Cutaneous squamous cell carcinoma (SCC) encompasses a diverse range of histopathological subtypes, including keratoacanthoma, acantholytic, spindle-cell, verrucous, papillary, clear cell, signet-ring, pigmented, and desmoplastic variants. Among these, the clear cell subtype (whether intraepidermal or invasive) stands out as an exceptionally rare and aggressive entity whose clinical behavior and biological nature are not yet fully understood. Microscopically, this variant is characterized by a strikingly abundant clear cytoplasm [1–3]. Historically, clear cell squamous cell carcinoma (ccSCC) of the skin was first characterized in 1980 by Kuo et al., who presented a series of six cases and proposed a classification into three distinct histologic types: Type I (keratinizing), Type II (non-keratinizing), and Type III (pleomorphic) [4]. Interestingly, within that original series, one case was identified as a metastasis from a primary SCC of the lower lip, while the remaining cases were considered primary cutaneous lesions [4]. The term “clear cells” refers to the microscopic appearance of a transparent cytoplasm that fails to take up hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining [3]. This distinctive clarity results from cytoplasmic buildup of glycogen, water, or intermediate filaments, alongside a loss of organelles or immature zymogen granules. Although such changes can occur in many tumors, the ‘pure form’ is exceptionally rare in cutaneous head-and-neck SCC. Consequently, while the World Health Organization classifies ccSCC as a separate, aggressive entity in sites like the penis, it remains officially uncategorized in the head and neck region [3]. The precise etiology of ccSCC remains a subject of ongoing research; however, several predisposing factors have been implicated, including chronic radiation exposure, arsenic ingestion, immunosuppression, and long-standing chronic ulceration [1, 5]. Clinically, this malignancy has most often been reported in elderly men with a history of chronic ultraviolet exposure and multiple prior skin neoplasms, typically manifesting in sun-exposed areas [6]. Importantly, these tumors lack specific clinical hallmarks that might distinguish them from conventional SCC variants [1]. In this report, we report the first clinical case documented from Syria of this rare tumor, involving a massive, neglected frontal lesion in an 80-year-old farmer, highlighting the diagnostic challenges and the impact of delayed surgical intervention.

Case presentation

An 80-year-old Syrian male, a long-term field farmer with chronic solar exposure, presented to the Dermatology Clinic with a painful, gradually enlarging lesion on the left frontal region. The lesion was elevated, ulcerative, and fungating and measured 7 × 4 cm, extending from the frontal scalp to the nasal bridge and the left eyebrow. The patient had fear-driven medical avoidance; despite having been scheduled for the surgical excision of the frontal lesion on two previous occasions, he consistently refused to undergo diagnostic biopsies or surgical procedures because he had a fear that it might be cancerous, though he was informed of the potential malignancy of the lesion, which allowed the mass to progress to its current massive size (Fig. 1).

A small coexisting lesion was also noted on the right hand (Fig. 2).

Clinical image showing the lesion on the patient’s hand

The patient’s fear-driven medical avoidance was associated with his experience, since his medical history was significant for a Grade I, moderately differentiated SCC of the lower lip, surgically excised 6 years prior (with a 2 cm central ulcer and infiltrative irregular sheets of atypical squamous cells). However, he had no history of chronic diseases and was not on any medications. Clinical examination revealed a firm, painful, and non-mobile mass with multiple fibrotic points; notably, there was no associated fever, headache, night sweats, or remarkable weight loss. His vitals were all within normal ranges, and laboratory tests revealed unremarkable findings. Moreover, there was no regional lymphadenopathy. The lesion had initially appeared 2 years ago as a small 1.5 cm growth and progressed steadily. Consultation from a psychologist to address the patient’s concerns was requested, which later helped the patient’s decision to undergo subsequent assessment and surgical intervention. Subsequently, preoperative contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CE-CT) of the head revealed a large, irregular soft-tissue mass in the left superior frontal region, measuring approximately 7.6 × 4.2 cm. The lesion exhibited significant contrast enhancement, suggesting a proliferative neoplastic process. Anatomically, the mass extended inferiorly to the level of the superior orbital rim without involving the intraorbital structures. Crucially, there was no apparent evidence of intracranial extension or bony destruction; while the lesion's deep borders showed an impression on the adjacent outer table of the skull, no frank osseous infiltration was observed. Intracranially, the brain parenchyma, ventricular system, and posterior fossa were within normal limits for the patient’s age, with mildly prominent subarachnoid spaces consistent with age-related changes. No midline shift, acute hemorrhage, or focal ischemic lesions were identified (Fig. 3).

ce-CT radiological findings. Axial view of the lesion in A and sagittal view in B. The red arrows depict the tumor in both images

Remarkably, Chest and abdominal CE-CT revealed no signs of systemic involvement. Contrast-enhanced magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), despite its diagnostic value, could not be obtained since our MRI unit was out of service, and the patient declined transfer to a specialized imaging center due to financial constraints. Preoperative neurosurgical consultation was requested and supported the decision of surgical intervention. Upon the current presentation, a surgical intervention was performed under general anesthesia. The procedure commenced with a wide-margin skin incision surrounding the massive frontal lesion to ensure clinical clearance, followed by the meticulous dissection and excision of the tumor base from the underlying periosteum. Given the tumor’s impression on the periosteum, and to enhance the vascular bed and facilitate successful skin grafting, and minimize the risk of microscopic infiltration of the bone, bone burring was performed as an adjunct on the outer table of the frontal bone. Following bone burring, no intraoperative clinical evidence of deep periosteal infiltration was noted, therefore eliminating the need for a more aggressive cranial approach. Finally, the resulting large scalp defect was reconstructed using a split-thickness skin graft, which was carefully secured to the prepared recipient site (Fig. 4).

Bone burring was performed on the outer table of the frontal bone (C), and the resulting large scalp defect was reconstructed using a split-thickness skin graft (D)

En-bloc craniotomy was proposed but was not advocated for our patient.

Histopathological analysis of the 13 × 10 cm skin specimen demonstrated a large ulcerated mass (11 × 8 cm) characterized by an infiltration of atypical squamous cells with an increased N/C ratio, pleomorphic nuclei, and prominent nucleoli. Microscopic examination specifically identified nests of polygonal tumor cells with clear or vacuolated cytoplasm (≥ 80% clear cells) and hyperchromatic nuclei, confirming a diagnosis of Type II non-keratinizing ccSCC, a rare histological variant. While peripheral surgical margins were clear, the deep surgical margins were involved (Fig. 5).

Concurrently, Histopathological examination of the lesion excised from the right hand demonstrated alternating orthokeratosis and parakeratosis (flag sign) with downward budding of atypical cells, consistent with actinic keratosis (solar keratosis), a recognized precancerous lesion (Fig. 6).

Given that our patient had several factors that would impose a high risk of recurrence, the instructed follow-up protocol included regular clinical assessment every 2 weeks, and surveillance contrast-enhanced MRI or CE-CT every 4 months for 2 years, then once every 12 months. However, our patient has been consistently declining surveillance imaging. Therefore surgical re-exploration would only be pursued if clinical signs of recurrence were noted. Should recurrence be clinically identified, the planned management would be determined by whether osseous or intracranial recurrence are detected, and in that case, the management options may encompass craniectomy with cranioplasty and/or palliative radiotherapy. At the 6-month follow-up, the surgical site exhibited satisfactory healing with no clinical evidence of local recurrence or regional lymphadenopathy (Fig. 7).

The surgical site 6 months following surgery

Although adjuvant radiotherapy was strongly recommended by our oncologist, the patient was unable to pursue this treatment due to significant socioeconomic constraints and the logistical challenge of traveling to a distant specialized center. Consequently, the patient remains under strict clinical surveillance every 2 weeks to monitor for any potential tumor regrowth.

Discussion

Cutaneous ccSCC, also known as the hydropic variant of SCC, was originally characterized by Kuo et al. in 1980 as a form of SCC exhibiting marked hydropic change. Lawal et al. highlighted that its clear-cell morphology can be attributed to the pronounced intracellular fluid accumulation due to hydropic degeneration, rather than from deposits of glycogen, lipid, or mucin [7]. This etiologic uncertainty reinforces the need for further research to clarify the basis of the clear-cell change. Reported cases to date have arisen almost exclusively in the head and neck region; most frequently involving the mandible [7]. The underlying cause of ccSCC remains uncertain, although factors such as prolonged exposure to ultraviolet radiation, chronic ulcerations, immunosuppression, and arsenic exposure have been proposed by many authors as contributors. Notably, almost all previously documented cases have involved older Caucasian men with extensive outdoor occupational exposure, supporting the potential role for ultraviolet radiation in its development [7]. Nevertheless, Lawal et al. reported the first case in the literature of ccSCC in a black patient [7]. Our patient had chronic sun exposure since he was a farmer, and he had a remarkable history of a typical SCC in his lower lip. The development of actinic keratosis in our patient’s hand further supports the etiology of prolonged sun exposure, since actinic keratosis is an early precancerous skin lesion with the potential to evolve into SCC, and typically arises on chronically sun-exposed areas in individuals with significant cumulative ultraviolet damage [8]. Importantly, since ccSCC lacks specific clinical hallmarks that might distinguish it from conventional SCC variants, and due to the large variety in differential diagnoses, the preoperative diagnosis is very challenging and may not be feasible without appropriate histopathological examination [1]. The differential diagnosis of ccSCC includes clear cell acanthoma, clear cell hidradenoma, and its malignant counterpart; clear cell hidradenocarcinoma, balloon cell melanoma, and balloon cell nevus, as well as sebaceous neoplasms [1]. The lesion in our case was initially a small ulcerative lesion, and progressed into a sizable mass within 2 years due to our patient’s fear-driven medical avoidance. Rapid enlargement has similarly been documented in previously published cases in the literature [7]. The clinical presentation in our patient included a firm, painful, and non-mobile mass with multiple fibrotic points. Health-care avoidance represents a form of patient disengagement in which individuals defer or refrain from accessing necessary medical services, a pattern that can adversely affect overall health status. Although inadequate insurance coverage and the absence of a consistent source of care remain well-established barriers to timely medical attention, emerging evidence underscores the substantial contribution of psychosocial determinants to health-care utilization. Lauver’s Theory of Care-Seeking Behavior posits that psychological and social factors (such as denial, anxiety, personal value systems, and prevailing social norms) significantly influence an individual’s decision to pursue or delay clinical evaluation [9]. Consultation from a psychologist in our case helped the patient’s decision to undergo further medical assessment, and CE-CT was performed. Generally, imaging modalities such as computed tomography (CT) and MRI may be necessary to delineate the true extent of the lesion and evaluate for possible metastatic disease, and are particularly important in cases of large, deeply infiltrative tumors with suspected extension into underlying soft tissue or bone [10]. CE-CT of our patient delineated the tumor and revealed no apparent bone infiltration, and chest and abdominal CE-CT excluded systemic involvement. Subsequently, the patient underwent surgical excision of the tumor. Surgical excision (sometimes supplemented with reconstructive procedures) remains the preferred and most reliable approach for curing invasive cutaneous SCC, as it enables definitive tumor identification and verification of clear resection margins. Even in frail elderly patients, or in cases involving large or anatomically challenging tumors with potential functional or cosmetic impact, surgery is rarely contraindicated when managed appropriately [10]. Neurosurgical consultation should be employed in cases of scalp tumors, especially in patients who present with a history of repeated superficial resections and local recurrences, eventually necessitating wider margins and deeper excision. Lesions with calvarial or intracranial extension warrant neurosurgical participation, as radiological assessment may underestimate the true depth of invasion. Radiological suspicion of bone involvement is the usual indication for neurosurgical consultation. MRI is preferred for evaluating perineural spread and marrow infiltration, whereas CT provides superior delineation of direct bony invasion [11]. Achieving complete removal of malignant lesions requires securing adequate margins in all directions to reduce the likelihood of recurrence. Notably, many incomplete excisions of cutaneous tumors occur at the deep margin. Similar to other soft-tissue cancers, optimal management of scalp tumors involves removing one additional anatomic layer beyond the deepest level of involvement [12]. Neurosurgical management of scalp tumors invading the calvarial region can range from selective removal of the outer calvarial table to full-thickness excision of both tables, and in advanced cases, resection of the dura or even portions of the underlying brain. The extent of bony and intracranial removal is determined not only by radiological and intraoperative assessment of tumor invasion but also by the neurosurgeon’s expertise and judgment [11]. When the pericranium represents the deep margin, this approach supports removing the outer cortical layer of the skull, which is typically performed with a burr or surgical drill [12]. Huntoon et al recommend in their study that malignant scalp tumors with calvarial involvement be managed with en-bloc excision of the lesion and surrounding margins, including full-thickness removal of the affected scalp and underlying bone. When the tumor extends through the inner table, resection of the involved dura is also advised [11]. Wound-closure planning should account for the size of the defect, the condition of the surrounding scalp tissue, whether the periosteum is intact, and the anticipated need for postoperative radiotherapy [11]. Surgical removal of the tumor was carried out followed by outer-table burring based on the radiological findings and the neurosurgeon’s assessment without intraoperative complications. En-bloc craniotomy was proposed but not advocated in our case for several important reasons. First, given the patient’s age, minimizing the duration of general anaesthesia was essential to reduce intraoperative and postoperative risks, and an en-bloc craniotomy would have significantly prolonged operative time. Second, the large tumor size and the required resection of the underlying fascia meant that an en-bloc approach would expose the dura and neural tissue directly to the external environment, markedly increasing the risk of infection, cerebrospinal fluid contamination, and secondary neural injury. Third, outer-table burring was preferred because the planned split-thickness skin graft required a well-vascularized bed; en-bloc craniotomy would necessitate titanium cranioplasty, which provides a poorly vascularized surface and is unsuitable for graft take, especially when both skin and fascia have been resected. Finally, the patient’s preference and financial constraints strongly influenced decision-making, as titanium cranioplasty would impose a substantial financial burden. The excised tumor was subjected to histopathological examination. Histologically, ccSCC is largely made up of neoplastic keratinocytes exhibiting abundant pale cytoplasm. In addition to this clear-cell component, scattered areas of conventional SCC may be present, underscoring the true nature of the lesion [2]. While there is no established consensus regarding the proportion of clear cells required to define ccSCC, a threshold exceeding 25% has been proposed [1]. Moreover, a classification system stratifying tumors according to the percentage of clear cells was introduced in 2007; lesions containing ≥ 80% clear cells are designated as ccSCC, and those with < 80% but ≥ 50% are classified as SCC with marked clear-cell change; and tumors with < 50% but > 10% clear cells are defined as SCC with moderate clear-cell change [6]. Benbiba et al. reported that the prognosis of ccSCC may be comparable to other SCC variants [1]. Follow-up strategies in cutaneous SCC must be individualized according to tumor risk and clinical context. For low-risk lesions, current guidelines support a conservative approach, with annual clinical examinations during the first 2 years being sufficient. In contrast, high-risk, locally advanced, and metastatic SCC require more intensive surveillance, often incorporating imaging. For patients with locally advanced or metastatic disease, follow-up at 3–6-month intervals is also advised, with imaging tailored to clinical suspicion. CT is often preferred in this setting, particularly when evaluating potential intracranial extension, osseous involvement, or monitoring therapeutic response. This is especially relevant for high-risk scalp tumors, where assessment of calvarial invasion is essential for surgical planning [13]. Unfortunately, the follow-up of our patient was strictly through clinical assessment since he declined follow-up imaging. Cases of cutaneous ccSCC were reported in the literature, such as the case by Lawal et al., who reported ccSCC in a 62-year-old black male; their patient presented with a non-healing ulcer on the left aspect of his face, associated with pain and purulent discharge, and had been growing for 6 months [7]. Additionally, the cases reported by Batrôs et al. are noteworthy, since they reported two patients with cutaneous ccSCC; one was a 78-year-old male, and the other was a 79-year-old female, in which the studied sections for the cutaneous ccSCC in both patients showed a clear cell predominance of ≥ 80% [2]. In contrast to previously reported cases, our 80-year-old patient exhibited pronounced fear-driven medical avoidance stemming from his prior lower-lip SCC, which allowed the scalp lesion to progress to a substantial size over 2 years. Moreover, although ccSCC has been described in the literature as an aggressive SCC variant, the lack of systemic metastasis in our case represent distinctive findings, particularly given the prolonged delay before definitive management. Conclusions

Our case underscores the importance of a multidisciplinary approach in patients with medical avoidance and highlights the psychological impact on patients’ decisions. Furthermore, it points out the need for advanced research targeting ccSCC as a distinct variant of SCC, to elucidate appropriate diagnostic characteristics and allow specialized surveillance. ccSCC remains rarely reported in literature and histopathologists should remain vigilant regarding this variant. Of note, our case underlines the rapid growth of ccSCC. The diagnosis of ccSCC should be considered in elderly patients who have a marked prolonged sun exposure.
