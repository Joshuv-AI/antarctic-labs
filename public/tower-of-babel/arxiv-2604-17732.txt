Exact Simulation from Tempered Stable Distributions with Infinite Variation ( α ≥ 1 \alpha\geq 1 )
Michael Grabchak

Abstract
We develop the first exact and computationally tractable method for simulating from tempered stable distributions in the infinite variation case, which corresponds to α ∈ [ 1 , 2 ) \alpha\in[1,2) . A small simulation study shows that the approach works well.



1
Introduction

Tempered stable (TS) distributions are a class of models obtained by modifying the tails of stable distributions to make them lighter, which leads to models that are more realistic in practice. They have been used successfully in fields such as finance, physics, computer science, actuarial science, and biostatistics, see the references in
Rachev et al. 2011
or
Grabchak 2016
. TS distributions were introduced in
Tweedie 1984
, rediscovered in
Koponen 1995
, and then generalized and systematically studied in
Rosiński 2007
. In the finite variation case (where
\alpha<1
), several exact simulation methods are known. These include the simple rejection sampling method of
Brix 1999
and the double rejection method of
Devroye 2009
. Other exact methods can be found in
Hofert 2011
,
Dassios, Qu, and Zhao 2018
,
Qu, Dassios, and Zhao 2021
, and
Grabchak 2021
. However, to date, no computationally tractable exact simulation method for the infinite variation case (where
\alpha\geq 1
) has been proposed. There are several approximate methods, which include approaches based on truncating an infinite series representation
(
Rosiński 2007
)
and on rejection sampling
(
Baeumer and Meerschaert 2010
)
. A comparative survey of these and other approximate methods, along with several new proposals, is provided by
Kawai and Masuda 2011
.

In this paper, we develop the first computationally tractable exact method for simulating TS distributions in the infinite variation case (
\alpha\geq 1
). This enables simulation of related stochastic processes, including the corresponding Lévy and Ornstein-Uhlenbeck processes, see
Grabchak and Sabino 2023
. This also allows for the use of simulation-based inference, which is important since the distribution and density functions of TS distributions are computationally difficult to evaluate. Our simulation methodology is based on rejection sampling and relies on the following observation. While the densities of TS distributions are difficult to evaluate, they can be viewed as marginal distributions of a joint distribution with a simple density. In the finite variation case, simulation methods based on such a representation are given in
Devroye 2009
and
Qu, Dassios, and Zhao 2021
.

The rest of this paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
, we present our main results. In Section
3
, we explain how to use these results for simulation and give our algorithms. In Section
4
, we present the results of a small simulation study. In Section
5
, we introduce a refinement of our methodology that can improve performance in some cases. In Section
6
, we briefly discuss
extensions of our methodology to the simulation of several related classes of distributions and give some directions for future work.

Before proceeding, we introduce our notation. We write
N(0,1)
to denote the standard normal distribution,
\mathrm{Exp}(1)
to denote the exponential distribution with mean
1
, and, for
a<b
, we write
U(a,b)
to denote the uniform distribution on
(a,b)
. For
x\in\mathbb{R}
we define

\mathrm{sign}(x)=\begin{cases}1&\mbox{if}\ \ x>0\\
0&\mbox{if}\ \ x=0\\
-1&\mbox{if}\ \ x<0\end{cases}.

2
Main Results

Fix
\alpha\in(0,2)
and let
f
be the density of a fully left-skewed
\alpha
-stable distribution with scale parameter
\sigma>0
. Proposition 1.2.12 in
Samorodnitsky and Taqqu 1994
tells us that for
z\geq 0

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{zx}f(x)\mathrm{d}x=\begin{cases}e^{-\sigma^{\alpha}\frac{z^{\alpha}}{\cos\left(\frac{\pi\alpha}{2}\right)}}&\mbox{if }\alpha\neq 1\\
e^{\sigma\frac{2}{\pi}z\log z}&\mbox{if }\alpha=1\end{cases}.

(1)

We define a fully left-skewed TS distribution as one with density

\displaystyle g(x)=Ce^{x/\ell}f(x),

(2)

where
\ell>0
is a parameter and the normalizing constant is given by

C=\begin{cases}e^{\frac{\sigma^{\alpha}}{\ell^{\alpha}\cos\left(\frac{\pi\alpha}{2}\right)}}&\mbox{if }\alpha\neq 1\\
e^{\sigma\frac{2}{\ell\pi}\log\ell}&\mbox{if }\alpha=1\end{cases}.

In this case, for
z\geq-1/\ell
, the moment generating function (mgf) is given by

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{zx}g(x)\mathrm{d}x=\begin{cases}e^{\frac{\sigma^{\alpha}}{\cos\left(\frac{\pi\alpha}{2}\right)}\left(\ell^{-\alpha}-(z+1/\ell)^{\alpha}\right)}&\mbox{if }\alpha\neq 1\\
e^{\sigma\frac{2}{\pi}\left(\ell^{-1}\log(\ell)+(z+1/\ell)\log(z+1/\ell)\right)}&\mbox{if }\alpha=1\end{cases}.

(3)

We denote this distribution by
\mathrm{TS}_{-}(\alpha,\ell,\sigma)
, where the minus sign emphasizes that this is fully left-skewed.

When
\alpha\in(0,1)
, the support of
f
is
(-\infty,0]
, and hence
g(x)\leq Cf(x)
in this case. This leads to a simple rejection sampling algorithm, where the proposal distribution is the underlying stable. This was first published in
Brix 1999
, see also Algorithm 0 in
Kawai and Masuda 2011
. On the other hand, for
\alpha\in[1,2)
, the support of
f
is the entire real line. Consequently, no such bound and, hence, no such algorithm exists. As we will see, in this case, the proposal distribution will be, essentially, a mixture of the underlying stable distribution and a distribution that is, itself, a mixture of half normal distributions.

Proposition 1
.

1. Let
X\sim\mathrm{TS}_{-}(\alpha,\ell/\sigma,1)
. If
\alpha\neq 1
, then
\sigma X\sim\mathrm{TS}_{-}(\alpha,\ell,\sigma)
. If
\alpha=1
, then
\sigma X-(2/\pi)\sigma\log\sigma\sim\mathrm{TS}_{-}(\alpha,\ell,\sigma)
.

2. If
X_{1}\sim\mathrm{TS}_{-}(\alpha,\ell,\sigma_{1})
and
X_{2}\sim\mathrm{TS}_{-}(\alpha,\ell,\sigma_{2})
are independent random variables, then
X_{1}+X_{2}\sim\mathrm{TS}_{-}\left(\alpha,\ell,(\sigma_{1}^{\alpha}+\sigma_{1}^{\alpha})^{1/\alpha}\right)
.

The proposition follows directly from (
3
) and thus the proof is omitted. In light of this result, we generally focus only on the case
\sigma=1
.

Remark 1
.

In the above, we are working with fully left-skewed TS distributions. However, it is more common to work with fully right-skewed TS distributions. Our choice is motivated by the fact that, for our purposes, it is convenient to place the lighter tail on the positive half-line. Ultimately, this does not matter as a random variable
X
has a fully right-skewed TS distribution if and only if
-X
has a fully left-skewed TS distribution.

Remark 2
.

There are many parameterizations of stable distributions. In the one that
Nolan 2020
calls Parametrization
1
,
f
is the density of an
\alpha
-stable distributions with parameters
\beta=-1
,
\gamma=\sigma
, and
\delta=0
. Note that the transform in (
1
) looks like a mgf, but we do not call it that since it does not converge on an open interval containing
0
. The transform does, however, uniquely determine the distribution. On the other hand, the transform in (
3
) is a mgf.

2.1
Results for
\alpha\in(1,2)

In this subsection, we focus on the case
\alpha\in(1,2)
and
\sigma=1
. First note that, in this case,

C=e^{\frac{1}{\ell^{\alpha}\cos\left(\frac{\pi\alpha}{2}\right)}}=e^{\frac{-1}{\ell^{\alpha}\left|\cos\left(\frac{\pi\alpha}{2}\right)\right|}}<1.

An explicit representation of
f
can be found, in, e.g., Theorem 3.3 of
Nolan 2020
, and is given by

\displaystyle f(x)=\frac{1}{\pi}\frac{\alpha}{\alpha-1}|x|^{1/(\alpha-1)}*\begin{cases}\int_{-\theta_{0}}^{\pi/2}V(\theta)e^{-x^{\alpha/(\alpha-1)}V(\theta)}\mathrm{d}\theta,&\mbox{if }x>0\\
\int_{-\pi/2}^{-\theta_{0}}V(\theta)e^{-|x|^{\alpha/(\alpha-1)}V(\theta)}\mathrm{d}\theta,&\mbox{if }x<0\end{cases},

where

\theta_{0}=\frac{\pi}{\alpha}-\frac{\pi}{2}>0

and for
\theta\in[-\pi/2,\pi/2]

V(\theta)=\left(\cos(\alpha\theta_{0})\right)^{1/(\alpha-1)}\left(\cos(\theta)\right)^{1/(\alpha-1)}\cos((\alpha-1)\theta+\alpha\theta_{0})\left|\sin(\alpha(\theta+\theta_{0}))\right|^{-\alpha/(\alpha-1)}.

Since
1<\alpha<2
, it follows that
0<\theta_{0}<\pi/2
.

It is easily seen that
f
is a marginal density of the joint density

\displaystyle f^{*}(x,\theta)=\frac{1}{\pi}\frac{\alpha}{\alpha-1}|x|^{1/(\alpha-1)}*\begin{cases}V(\theta)e^{-x^{\alpha/(\alpha-1)}V(\theta)},&x>0,-\theta_{0}<\theta<\pi/2\\
V(\theta)e^{-|x|^{\alpha/(\alpha-1)}V(\theta)},&x<0,-\pi/2<\theta<-\theta_{0}\end{cases}

and that
g
is a marginal density of the joint density

\displaystyle g^{*}(x,\theta)=Ce^{x/\ell}f^{*}(x,\theta).

(4)

We now state our main result.

Theorem 1
.

Fix
\epsilon\in(0,1)
and set

m_{\epsilon}^{*}=(\ell\epsilon)^{-(\alpha-1)}\frac{\alpha}{|\cos(\pi\alpha/2)|}\ \ \mbox{and}\ \ m_{\epsilon}^{\sharp}=\max\{m_{\epsilon}^{*},1\}.

1. For
x\leq m^{\sharp}_{\epsilon}
and
-\frac{\pi}{2}<\theta\leq\frac{\pi}{2}
, we have

g^{*}(x,\theta)\leq C_{1}f^{*}(x,\theta),

where

C_{1}=Ce^{m_{\epsilon}^{\sharp}/\ell}.

2. For
x\geq m_{\epsilon}^{\sharp}
and
-\theta_{0}<\theta\leq\frac{\pi}{2}
, we have

g^{*}(x,\theta)\leq C_{2}\frac{\alpha}{\pi}e^{-\xi_{\epsilon}(\theta)(x-m_{\epsilon}(\theta))^{2}/2}\sqrt{\frac{2\xi_{\epsilon}(\theta)}{\pi}},

where

m_{\epsilon}(\theta)=\left(\frac{\alpha-1}{\ell\epsilon V(\theta)\alpha}\right)^{\alpha-1},\ \ \ \xi_{\epsilon}(\theta)=(V(\theta))^{\alpha-1}\frac{(\epsilon\alpha)^{\alpha-1}}{(\alpha-1)^{\alpha}}\ell^{-(2-\alpha)},

and

C_{2}=C^{1-\epsilon^{1-\alpha}}\sqrt{\pi}\frac{(\epsilon\alpha)^{(1-\alpha)/2}}{(\alpha-1)^{1-\alpha/2}}\left(\frac{3-\alpha}{e(1-\epsilon)}\right)^{\frac{3-\alpha}{2}}\ell^{1-\alpha/2}2^{-(4-\alpha)/2}.

Before giving the proof, we introduce two lemmas.

Lemma 1
.

1.
V
is nonnegative, strictly increasing on
(-\pi/2,-\theta_{0})
, strictly decreasing on
(-\theta_{0},\pi/2)
, and takes the values:
V(-\pi/2)=0
,
V(-\theta_{0})=\infty
, and

V(\pi/2)=(\alpha-1)\left(\alpha^{-\alpha}|\cos(\pi\alpha/2)|\right)^{1/(\alpha-1)}.

2. For
-\theta_{0}<\theta\leq\frac{\pi}{2}
, we have
m_{\epsilon}(\theta)\leq m^{*}_{\epsilon}\leq m^{\sharp}_{\epsilon}
and
V(\theta)\geq V(\pi/2)
.

Proof.

The first part is given in Lemma 3.8 of
Nolan 2020
, although we use different notation. The second follows from the first and the easily checked fact that
m_{\epsilon}^{*}=m_{\epsilon}(\pi/2)
.
∎

Lemma 2
.

1. Let
p>2
,
a,b>0
, and

m=\left(\frac{a}{bp}\right)^{1/(p-1)}.

If

L(x)=ax-bx^{p},\ \ x\geq 0,

then for
x\geq m

L(x)\leq L(m)-\frac{|L^{\prime\prime}(m)|}{2}(x-m)^{2}

where

|L^{\prime\prime}(m)|=bp(p-1)m^{p-2}.

2. For any
t\geq 0
and
a,b>0

\displaystyle t^{a}e^{-bt}\leq(a/b)^{a}e^{-a}.

(5)

Proof.

The second part can be checked using basic calculus. For the first part, we have

L^{\prime}(x)=a-bpx^{p-1},\ \ L^{\prime\prime}(x)=-bp(p-1)x^{p-2}<0,

and
L^{\prime\prime\prime}(x)<0
. Noting that
L^{\prime}(m)=0
and applying Taylor’s theorem gives

L(x)=L(m)-\frac{|L^{\prime\prime}(m)|}{2}(x-m)^{2}-\frac{|L^{\prime\prime\prime}(x^{*})|}{6}(x-m)^{3}\leq L(m)-\frac{|L^{\prime\prime}(m)|}{2}(x-m)^{2},

where
x^{*}
is some point that lies between
x
and
m
.
∎

Proof of Theorem
1
.

The first part is immediate. Henceforth, assume
x\geq m^{\sharp}_{\epsilon}
,
-\theta_{0}<\theta\leq\frac{\pi}{2}
, and note that, by Lemma
1
,
x\geq m_{\epsilon}(\theta)
. We have

\displaystyle e^{x/\ell}x^{\frac{1}{\alpha-1}}V(\theta)e^{-x^{\alpha/(\alpha-1)}V(\theta)}

\displaystyle\qquad=x^{\frac{1}{\alpha-1}}\left(V(\theta)\right)^{(3-\alpha)/2}e^{-(1-\epsilon)x^{\alpha/(\alpha-1)}V(\theta)}e^{x/\ell-\epsilon x^{\alpha/(\alpha-1)}V(\theta)}\sqrt{\frac{\pi(\alpha-1)^{\alpha}\ell^{2-\alpha}}{2(\epsilon\alpha)^{\alpha-1}}}\sqrt{\frac{2\xi_{\epsilon}(\theta)}{\pi}}.

Applying (
5
) with
a=(3-\alpha)/2
and
b=1-\epsilon
gives

\displaystyle x^{\frac{1}{\alpha-1}}\left(V(\theta)\right)^{(3-\alpha)/2}e^{-(1-\epsilon)x^{\alpha/(\alpha-1)}V(\theta)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle x^{\alpha/2-1}x^{\frac{\alpha}{\alpha-1}(\frac{3-\alpha}{2})}\left(V(\theta)\right)^{(3-\alpha)/2}e^{-(1-\epsilon)x^{\alpha/(\alpha-1)}V(\theta)}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle x^{\alpha/2-1}\left(\frac{3-\alpha}{2(1-\epsilon)e}\right)^{\frac{3-\alpha}{2}}\leq\left(\frac{3-\alpha}{2(1-\epsilon)e}\right)^{\frac{3-\alpha}{2}},

where the last inequality follows from the fact that
x\geq 1
and
\alpha<2
. Next, applying Lemma
2
with
a=1/\ell
,
b=\epsilon V(\theta)
, and
p=\alpha/(\alpha-1)>2
gives

\displaystyle e^{x/\ell}e^{-\epsilon V(\theta)x^{\alpha/(\alpha-1)}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle e^{m_{\epsilon}(\theta)/\ell-\epsilon V(\theta)(m_{\epsilon}(\theta))^{\alpha/(\alpha-1)}}e^{-\xi_{\epsilon}(\theta)(x-m_{\epsilon}(\theta))^{2}/2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle e^{\epsilon^{1-\alpha}\ell^{-\alpha}/|\cos\left(\frac{\pi\alpha}{2}\right)|}e^{-\xi_{\epsilon}(\theta)(x-m_{\epsilon}(\theta))^{2}/2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle C^{-\epsilon^{1-\alpha}}e^{-\xi_{\epsilon}(\theta)(x-m_{\epsilon}(\theta))^{2}/2}.

Here the second inequality follows from the fact that

\displaystyle m_{\epsilon}(\theta)/\ell-\epsilon V(\theta)(m_{\epsilon}(\theta))^{\alpha/(\alpha-1)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(V(\theta)\right)^{1-\alpha}\epsilon^{1-\alpha}(\alpha-1)^{\alpha-1}(\alpha\ell)^{-\alpha}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left(V(\pi/2)\right)^{1-\alpha}\epsilon^{1-\alpha}(\alpha-1)^{\alpha-1}(\alpha\ell)^{-\alpha}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\epsilon^{1-\alpha}\ell^{-\alpha}/\left|\cos\left(\frac{\pi\alpha}{2}\right)\right|,

which, itself, follows from Lemma
1
. Combining the above with (
4
) gives the result.
∎

2.2
Results for
\alpha=1

In this subsection we consider the case
\alpha=1
and
\sigma=1
. The approach is similar to that used for
\alpha\in(1,2)
, but the formulas differ, so we provide a detailed derivation. In this case

C=e^{\frac{2}{\ell\pi}\log\ell}=\ell^{\frac{2}{\ell\pi}}.

An explicit representation of
f
can be found, in, e.g., Theorem 3.3 of
Nolan 2020
, and is given by

\displaystyle f(x)=\frac{1}{2}e^{\pi x/2}\int_{-\pi/2}^{\pi/2}V(\theta)e^{-e^{\pi x/2}V(\theta)}\mathrm{d}\theta,

where

V(\theta)=\frac{1-\frac{2}{\pi}\theta}{\cos(\theta)}\exp\left\{(\theta-\pi/2)\tan(\theta)\right\},\ \ \ \theta\in[-\pi/2,\pi/2].

Lemma 3.8 in
Nolan 2020
tells us that
V
is nonnegative, strictly decreasing on
(-\pi/2,\pi/2)
, and takes the values:

V(-\pi/2)=\infty\mbox{ and }V(\pi/2)=\frac{2}{\pi e}.

Clearly,
f
is a marginal density of the joint density

\displaystyle f^{*}(x,\theta)=\frac{1}{2}e^{\pi x/2}V(\theta)e^{-e^{\pi x/2}V(\theta)}\ \ x\in\mathbb{R},-\pi/2<\theta<\pi/2.

and
g
is a marginal density of the joint density

\displaystyle g^{*}(x,\theta)=Ce^{x/\ell}f^{*}(x,\theta).

We can now state our main result for
\alpha=1
.

Theorem 2
.

Fix
\epsilon\in(0,1)
and let

m^{\sharp}_{\epsilon}=\frac{2}{\pi}\left(1-\log(\ell\epsilon)\right).

1. For
x\leq m^{\sharp}_{\epsilon}
and
-\frac{\pi}{2}<\theta\leq\frac{\pi}{2}
, we have

g^{*}(x,\theta)\leq C_{1}f^{*}(x,\theta),

where

C_{1}=Ce^{m^{\sharp}_{\epsilon}/\ell}=e^{\frac{2}{\pi\ell}\left(1-\log\epsilon\right)}.

2. For
x>m^{\sharp}_{\epsilon}
and
-\frac{\pi}{2}<\theta\leq\frac{\pi}{2}
, we have

g^{*}(x,\theta)\leq C_{2}\frac{1}{\pi}e^{-\xi(x-m_{\epsilon}(\theta))^{2}/2}\sqrt{\frac{2\xi}{\pi}},

where

\xi=\frac{\pi}{2\ell},\ \ \ m_{\epsilon}(\theta)=\frac{2}{\pi}\log\left(\frac{2}{\epsilon\ell\pi V(\theta)}\right),

and

C_{2}=\frac{\pi\sqrt{\ell}}{2e(1-\epsilon)}\epsilon^{-\frac{2}{\pi\ell}}.

Remark 3
.

To simplify the discussion going forward, in the
\alpha=1
case, we sometimes set
\theta_{0}=\pi/2
and write
\xi=\xi_{\epsilon}(\theta)
, even though
\xi
does not actually depend on
\theta
or
\epsilon
in this case. This notation unifies the presentation of Theorems
1
and
2
, with the only difference being the formulas for
m^{\sharp}_{\epsilon}
,
C_{1}
,
C_{2}
,
\xi_{\epsilon}(\theta)
, and
m_{\epsilon}(\theta)
.

Before proving the theorem, we give a lemma whose proof is similar to that of Lemma
3
and is thus omitted.

Lemma 3
.

Let
a,b,c>0
and let

m=\frac{1}{b}\log\left(\frac{a}{bc}\right).

If

L(x)=ax-ce^{bx},\ \ x\in\mathbb{R},

then, for
x\geq m
,

L(x)\leq L(m)-\frac{ab}{2}(x-m)^{2}=\frac{a}{b}\left(\log\left(\frac{a}{bc}\right)-1\right)-\frac{ab}{2}(x-m)^{2}.

Proof of Theorem
2
.

The first part is immediate; we now turn to the second part. By arguments similar to those in the proof of Lemma
1
, we have
m_{\epsilon}(\theta)\leq m^{\sharp}_{\epsilon}
. From here, we get

\displaystyle C\frac{1}{2}e^{x/\ell}e^{\pi x/2}V(\theta)e^{-e^{\pi x/2}V(\theta)}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C\frac{1}{2e(1-\epsilon)}e^{x/\ell-\epsilon e^{\pi x/2}V(\theta)}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C\frac{1}{2e(1-\epsilon)}e^{\frac{2}{\pi\ell}\left(\log\left(\frac{2}{\ell\pi\epsilon V(\theta)}\right)-1\right)}e^{-\xi(x-m_{\epsilon}(\theta))^{2}/2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C\frac{1}{2e(1-\epsilon)}e^{\frac{2}{\pi\ell}\log\left(\frac{1}{\ell\epsilon}\right)}e^{-\xi(x-m_{\epsilon}(\theta))^{2}/2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\pi\sqrt{\ell}}{2e(1-\epsilon)}\epsilon^{-\frac{2}{\pi\ell}}e^{-\xi(x-m_{\epsilon}(\theta))^{2}/2}\sqrt{\frac{2\xi}{\pi}}\frac{1}{\pi},

where the first inequality follows by applying (
5
) with
a=1
and
b=1-\epsilon
, the second by applying Lemma
3
with
a=1/\ell
,
b=\pi/2
, and
c=\epsilon V(\theta)
, and the third by the fact that
V(\theta)\geq V(\pi/2)=2/(\pi e)
.
∎

3
Simulation Algorithms

From Theorems
1
and
2
, we immediately get the following result. See Remark
3
for an explanation of some of the notation in the
\alpha=1
case.

Corollary 1
.

For any
\epsilon\in(0,1)
and
p_{1},p_{2}\in(0,1)
with
p_{1}+p_{2}=1
, we have

\displaystyle g^{*}(x,\theta)\leq Kh(x,\theta),\ \ x\in\mathbb{R},\ -\frac{\pi}{2}\leq\theta\leq\frac{\pi}{2},

(6)

where

\displaystyle h(x,\theta)=p_{1}h_{1}(x,\theta)+p_{2}h_{2}(x,\theta)

(7)

with

h_{1}(x,\theta)=f^{*}(x,\theta),\ \ x\in\mathbb{R},\ -\pi/2<\theta<\pi/2,

h_{2}(x,\theta)=\frac{\alpha}{\pi}e^{-\xi_{\epsilon}(\theta)(x-m_{\epsilon}(\theta))^{2}/2}\sqrt{\frac{2\xi_{\epsilon}(\theta)}{\pi}},\ \ -\theta_{0}<\theta\leq\frac{\pi}{2},\ x\in\left(m_{\epsilon}(\theta),\infty\right),

and

K=\max\left\{C_{1}/p_{1},C_{2}/p_{2}\right\}.

Here,
h_{1}
and
h_{2}
are bivariate densities, for which we now develop simulation methods. With these, we will be able to simulate from
h
and, thus, through the use of rejection sampling, from
g^{*}
and
g
. We can simulate from
h_{1}=f^{*}
using Algorithm
1
, which is a variant of the method of
Chambers, Mallows, and Stuck 1976
for simulating from stable distributions. The algorithm is justified by the following result, whose proof is similar to the proof of Theorem 1.3 in
Nolan 2020
and is thus omitted.

Algorithm 1

Simulation from
h_{1}=f^{*}

1:

independently simulate

\Theta\sim U(-\pi/2,\pi/2)

and

W\sim\mathrm{Exp}(1)

2:

if

\alpha=1

then

3:

set

X=\frac{2}{\pi}\left[\left(\frac{\pi}{2}-\Theta\right)\tan(\Theta)+\log\left(\frac{\pi W\cos(\Theta)}{\pi-2\Theta}\right)\right]

4:

else

5:

set

X=\mathrm{sign}(\theta_{0}+\Theta)\left(V(\Theta)/W\right)^{(1-\alpha)/\alpha}

6:

end

if

7:

return

(X,\Theta)

Algorithm 2

Simulation from
h_{2}
. Tuning parameter
\epsilon\in(0,1)
.

1:

independently simulate

\Theta\sim U(-\theta_{0},\pi/2)
and
Z\sim N\left(0,1\right)

2:

set

X=m_{\epsilon}(\Theta)+|Z|/\xi_{\epsilon}(\Theta)

3:

return

(X,\Theta)

Proposition 2
.

Let
\Theta\sim U(-\pi/2,\pi/2)
and
W\sim\mathrm{Exp}(1)
be independent random variables. If
\alpha\in(1,2)
, set

X=\mathrm{sign}(\theta_{0}+\Theta)\left(V(\Theta)/W\right)^{(1-\alpha)/\alpha}

and if
\alpha=1
, set

X=\frac{2}{\pi}\left[\left(\frac{\pi}{2}-\Theta\right)\tan(\Theta)+\log\left(\frac{\pi W\cos(\Theta)}{\pi-2\Theta}\right)\right].

We have
(X,\Theta)\sim h_{1}=f^{*}
.

We can simulate from
h_{2}
using Algorithm
2
. To explain the algorithm, we first recall the following fact. If
Z\sim N(0,1)
and
\sigma>0
,
\mu\in\mathbb{R}
, then the random variable
X=\sigma|Z|+\mu
has density

\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi\sigma^{2}}}e^{-\frac{(x-\mu)^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}},\ \ x>\mu.

This is the half normal distribution with location parameter
\mu
and scale parameter
\sigma
. It is easily seen that
h_{2}
is the joint distribution of
\Theta
and
X
, where the marginal distribution of
\Theta
is
U(-\theta_{0},\pi/2)
and the conditional distribution of
X
given
\Theta
is half normal with location parameter
\mu=m_{\epsilon}(\Theta)
and scale parameter
\sigma=1/\sqrt{\xi_{\epsilon}(\Theta)}
. Unconditionally, the distribution of
X
is a mixture of half normal distributions.

Algorithm 3

Simulation from
h
. Tuning parameters
\epsilon\in(0,1)
,
p_{1},p_{2}\in(0,1)
with
p_{1}+p_{2}=1
.

1:

simulate

U\sim U(0,1)

2:

if

U\leq p_{1}

then

3:

simulate

(X,\Theta)
using Algorithm
1

4:

else

5:

simulate

(X,\Theta)
using Algorithm
2

6:

end

if

7:

return

(X,\Theta)

Since
h
has the mixture representation given in (
7
), we can simulate from it using Algorithm
3
. From here, the bound in (
6
) allows us to simulate from
g^{*}
using rejection sampling, as summarized in Algorithm
4
. We can now simulate from
\mathrm{TS}_{-}(\alpha,\ell,\sigma)
by first simulating
(X,\Theta)
using Algorithm
4
, and then applying the result in Proposition
1
. The procedure is summarized in Algorithm
5
.

On a given iteration of Algorithm
4
, the probability of acceptance is
1/K
and the expected number of iterations until the first acceptance is
K
. Thus, the smaller the value of

Refinement

It can be checked that
C_{1}
approaches
\infty
as
\ell\to 0
, with the other parameters fixed. Thus, small values of
\ell
can lead to large values of
K
. In this section, we introduce a methodology that can help in such situations. A similar methodology for the case
\alpha\in(0,1)
is discussed in
Hofert 2011
and
Grabchak 2019
. To simplify the discussion, throughout this section we emphasize the dependence of
K
on
\ell
by writing
K(\ell)
. Of course,
K
also depends on other parameters.

Assume that we want to simulate from
\mathrm{TS}_{-}(\alpha,\ell,1)
with
\ell
small and
K(\ell)
large and that we cannot improve the situation significantly by better calibrating the tuning parameters. We can consider the following approach. Select an integer
m\geq 2
and simulate
m
observations
X_{1},X_{2},\dots,X_{m}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{iid}}}{{\sim}}\mathrm{TS}_{-}\left(\alpha,m^{1/\alpha}\ell,1\right)
. Proposition
1
implies that

\displaystyle Y=m^{-1/\alpha}\left(X_{1}+X_{2}+\cdots+X_{m}\right)\sim\mathrm{TS}_{-}(\alpha,\ell,1).

(8)

When
\alpha=1
, we also need to add
\frac{2}{\pi}\log m
. This gives an alternative approach to simulating from this distribution. On the one hand, this requires simulating
m
random variables to get just one observation. On the other hand,
K\left(m^{1/\alpha}\ell\right)
may be much smaller than
K(\ell)
, and the tradeoff may be worth it. Specifically, this is an improvement when
mK\left(m^{1/\alpha}\ell\right)\leq K(\ell)
.

We now consider a concrete example, specifically simulation from
\mathrm{TS}_{-}(1.5,0.3,1)
. We take
p_{1}=p_{2}=1/2
and
\epsilon=0.95
, which are reasonable choices. However, here
K(0.3)=206.8
, which is quite high. If we take
m=2
, we can improve things significantly. In this case, we simulate two observations from
\mathrm{TS}_{-}\left(1.5,0.3*2^{1/1.5},1\right)
, where
0.3*2^{1/1.5}=0.476
. Using the same tuning parameters, we get
K\left(0.3*2^{1/1.5}\right)=20.33
and
2K\left(0.3*2^{1/1.5}\right)=40.67
, which is a huge improvement.

We perform a small simulation to verify the performance of this approach. First, we consider direct simulation from
\mathrm{TS}_{-}(1.5,0.3,1)
. We simulate
N=10^{7}
observations from the proposal distributions and, after applying rejection sampling, we obtained
n=48,551
observations. Next, we consider the approach with
m=2
. We again simulated
N=10^{7}
observations from the proposal distribution. After rejection sampling we obtained
491,480
observations from
\mathrm{TS}_{-}\left(1.5,0.3*2^{1/1.5},1\right)
and, after aggregation, we get
245,740
observations from
\mathrm{TS}_{-}(1.5,0.3,1)
. In Figure
2
, we plot the KDE from both datasets with the true density overlaid. We can see that the fit is almost perfect.

6
Extensions and Conclusions

In this paper we developed the first exact and computationally tractable method for simulating from fully left-skewed TS distributions in the infinite variation case, where
\alpha\in[0,2)
. Several useful classes of distributions arise as linear combinations of such random variables, and our method extends naturally to these cases. We discuss several such examples in this section and, then, give some directions for future work.

The first example is the class of fully
right
-skewed TS distributions, which we briefly mentioned in Remark
1
. These have the same parameters as fully
left
-skewed TS distributions and are denote by
\mathrm{TS}_{+}(\alpha,\ell,\sigma)
. A random variable
X\sim\mathrm{TS}_{+}(\alpha,\ell,\sigma)
if and only if
-X\sim\mathrm{TS}_{-}(\alpha,\ell,\sigma)
.

The next example is the class of classical tempered stable (CTS) distributions. A random variable
X
is said to have a CTS distribution if

X\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}X_{+}-X_{-}+b,

where
X_{+}\sim\mathrm{TS}_{+}(\alpha,\ell_{+},\sigma_{+})
and
X_{-}\sim\mathrm{TS}_{+}(\alpha,\ell_{-},\sigma_{-})
are independent and
b\in\mathbb{R}
. We denote this distribution
\mathrm{CTS}(\alpha,\ell_{+},\sigma_{+},\ell_{-},\sigma_{-},b)
. In the literature, CTS distributions are sometimes also called KoBoL, CGMY, smoothly truncated Lévy flights (STFL), or just tempered stable distributions.

Multivariate CTS distributions are studied in, e.g.,
Xia and Grabchak 2022
and
Xia and Grabchak 2024
. These distributions are characterized by the so-called spectral measure, which is a finite measure on the unit sphere in
d
dimensions. When the support of the spectral measure is finite, with masses
s_{1},s_{2},\dots,s_{k}
, we can simulate from this distribution by taking

X=s_{1}X_{1}+s_{2}X_{2}+\cdots+s_{k}X_{k},

where
X_{1},X_{2},\dots,X_{k}
are independent random variables with appropriate fully right-skewed TS distributions. Moreover, as shown in
Xia and Grabchak 2022
, even when the support of the spectral measure is infinite, the above leads to a good approximation for an appropriate choice of the masses.

Finally, large and general classes of univariate and multivariate TS distributions are considered in
Rosiński 2007
and
Grabchak 2016
. Theorem 4.18 in
Grabchak 2016
shows that all of these can be approximated by linear combinations of fully right-skewed TS distributions.

We leave detailed investigations of the application of our methodology to simulation from the above classes of distributions for future work. Another important direction is the development of systematic approaches for calibrating the tuning parameters.
