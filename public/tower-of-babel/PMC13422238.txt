Robot-assisted gait training using a robotic exoskeleton during inpatient rehabilitation in acute Guillain–Barré Syndrome
Authors: Joon Sin Ser, Siew Kwaon Lui
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryA woman in her early 60s with acute Guillain–Barré syndrome (GBS) developed rapidly progressive tetraparesis and respiratory failure requiring non-invasive ventilation. Following intravenous immunoglobulin and medical stabilisation, she entered inpatient rehabilitation with severe weakness, poor truncal control and inability to ambulate. Despite intensive conventional therapy, early gait training was limited. Exoskeleton-based robot-assisted gait training (RAGT) using the Hybrid Assistive Limb (HAL; Cyberdyne Inc, Tsukuba, Japan) was introduced during inpatient rehabilitation as an adjunct to standard therapy. The patient completed 10 HAL sessions over a 15-day period without adverse events. During this time, progressive improvements in ambulatory distance and functional independence were observed. By completion of HAL training, she was able to ambulate 50 m with a walking stick and minimal assistance. She subsequently continued conventional rehabilitation and achieved independent ambulation by discharge. This case suggests that HAL-based RAGT may be a feasible adjunct to inpatient rehabilitation in medically stable patients with acute GBS. The intervention was well tolerated in this patient. Further studies are required to establish patient-selection criteria, optimal timing and the effectiveness of exoskeleton-assisted gait training in GBS.Keywords: Rehabilitation medicine, Physiotherapy (rehabilitation), Disability

Background

Guillain–Barré syndrome (GBS) is an acute immune-mediated polyneuropathy that predominantly affects the peripheral nervous system, most commonly presenting as acute inflammatory demyelinating polyneuropathy. It can result in significant neurological deficits, including progressive weakness and sensory disturbances, frequently leading to loss of independent ambulation.1 Despite timely treatment with intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG), the clinical course of GBS is highly variable, with a wide spectrum of disease severity and functional outcomes. Consequently, many patients require a period of inpatient rehabilitation to optimise recovery and restore functional independence.

Robot-assisted gait training (RAGT) in rehabilitation has been well established in multiple neurological conditions, including stroke, spinal cord injury, multiple sclerosis, cerebral palsy and traumatic brain injury.2 The Robot Institute of America defines a robot as a programmable, multi-functional manipulator designed to move material, parts or specialised devices through variable programmed motions for the performance of a variety of tasks.3 RAGT uses advanced robotic devices and systems designed to support, enhance and guide the lower limbs during ambulation.4 These robotic devices can generally be divided into exoskeleton and end-effector devices. Exoskeletons are wearable robots which are strapped to the lower limbs and have electrically actuated motors that control joint motion to automate overground ambulation, while end-effectors are based on the principle of a patient’s feet being placed on foot-plates, whose trajectories simulate the stance and swing phases during gait training.5 6 Among the exoskeletons, there are two types: (1) grounded exoskeletons which allow ambulation on a treadmill, for example, Lokomat (Hocoma AG, Volketswil, Switzerland); (2) overground wearable exoskeletons, for example, Hybrid Assistive Limb (HAL; Cyberdyne Inc, Tsukuba, Japan), Ekso GT (Ekso Bionics, San Rafael, California, USA) and ReWalk (Lifeward Ltd, Yokneam Illit, Israel).7 8

Based on a literature review of PubMed, Embase, and the Cochrane Library, evidence supporting the use of RAGT as an adjunct to rehabilitation in the acute phase of GBS remains limited. To date, four studies have described the use of RAGT in patients with GBS: one mixed-methods feasibility study, one cross-sectional study and two case reports where one described the use of a hip-wearable exoskeleton in a patient during the chronic phase of the disease and the other described the use of Lokomat in a geriatric patient with GBS.9–12

We report the case of a patient with acute GBS who underwent inpatient rehabilitation incorporating adjunctive RAGT using the HAL, an exoskeleton that facilitates overground ambulation. HAL is a wearable robotic device that assists walking by detecting bioelectric signals from electrodes placed on the extensor and flexor muscles of the knee and hip joints. HAL also uses information from the hip and knee angular sensors and force-pressure sensors in the shoes to determine the motion support and the power units located at the hip and knee joints are driven to assist the joint motion (figure 1). Movement is initiated based on the user’s voluntary intention.13

The aim of this case report is to highlight the feasibility and potential role of an exoskeleton-based robotic device as part of rehabilitation in patients with acute GBS.

Case presentation

A woman in her early 60s with no significant medical history was admitted acutely to the department of neurology of a tertiary hospital with a 3-day history of ascending bilateral paraesthesia involving the hands and lower limbs, on a background of a preceding 10-day history of diarrhoea. On initial neurological examination, deep tendon reflexes were absent at the triceps, knees and ankles. Muscle strength was assessed using the Medical Research Council (MRC) scale, which grades muscle power from 0 (no visible contraction) to 5 (normal strength). Upper limb strength was preserved (MRC grade 5/5), except for bilateral finger and thumb abduction (MRC grade 4/5). Lower limb strength was reduced (MRC grade 4/5). Sensory examination revealed a glove-and-stocking pattern of sensory loss, with reduced pinprick sensation in the upper limbs up to the wrists and in the lower limbs up to the calves. Based on the clinical presentation and the presence of positive anti-ganglioside antibodies, a diagnosis of GBS was made. The patient received a 5-day course of IVIG, initiated on day 3 of admission.

On day 7 of admission, she developed respiratory compromise secondary to neuromuscular weakness and was transferred to the intensive care unit, where she required non-invasive ventilation for 9 days. Thereafter, she was weaned off non-invasive ventilation and transferred to the general medical ward. She did not require supplemental oxygen. She reached her neurological nadir on day 8 of admission. At this point, her upper limb strength was MRC grade 2/5 proximally and 3/5 distally; her lower limb strength further deteriorated (table 1). Her Modified Erasmus GBS Outcome Score (mEGOS) on day 7 of admission was 12. The mEGOS is a validated prognostic model that estimates the probability of independent ambulation at specified time points following GBS.14

She was reviewed by the rehabilitation medicine physician and was subsequently transferred to the inpatient rehabilitation medicine service within the same tertiary hospital on day 23 of admission. She received conventional rehabilitation therapy totalling 180 min daily, comprising physiotherapy, occupational therapy and speech therapy, 5 days per week. Therapy intensity was individualised according to her endurance and activity tolerance, with daily physiotherapy sessions lasting 60 min, focusing on balance, standing and ambulation.

Functional recovery was monitored using several outcome measures, including the motor subscale of the Functional Independence Measure (FIM), the 6-min walk test and the level of assistance required for ambulation and distance achieved during physiotherapy sessions. The FIM motor subscale is a validated and objective measure of functional status, consisting of 13 items related to self-care, mobility and transfers. Each item is scored from 1 (total assistance) to 7 (complete independence), yielding a total motor score ranging from 13 to 91, with higher scores indicating greater functional independence. Her motor FIM score on admission to inpatient rehabilitation was 24 points (table 1).

During the initial phase of inpatient rehabilitation, the patient demonstrated profoundly impaired trunk control, poor activity tolerance and an inability to maintain standing balance during conventional therapy sessions. Over time, she showed gradual improvement in truncal control and balance. To ensure safe use of HAL during the rehabilitation, she was required to demonstrate adequate trunk and postural control to maintain upright posture without severe involuntary or ataxic movements that could increase the risk of falls. In addition, she was required to demonstrate stable vital signs and adequate activity tolerance to safely participate in RAGT using HAL without becoming overly fatigued. By day 35 of admission, she was able to ambulate 12 m using an Arjo walker (Arjo, Cook County, Illinois, USA) (a standing and raising mobility device) with moderate assistance. At this stage, she demonstrated improved trunk and postural control, adequate activity tolerance, stable vital signs and the ability to participate meaningfully in conventional therapy sessions. Based on her clinical progress, the inpatient rehabilitation team established additional specific, meaningful and feasible ambulatory goals with the aim of achieving independent ambulation. She was deemed suitable for more intensive, task-specific gait training with the use of HAL.

Therefore, HAL was initiated on day 36 of admission. To ensure safety, the patient was supported by a mobile suspension system, All-In-One Walking Trainer (Ropox A/S, Naestved, Denmark; figure 1). The system was operated by a physiotherapist with assistance from an assistant. The patient was instructed to walk as she normally would, with the system seamlessly augmenting her intended movements. Learning to use HAL was facilitated through visual feedback. The patient could view the bioelectric signals of her muscles on the monitor and would learn to adjust her mental intention to more effectively activate her lower limb muscles. The patient completed 10 sessions of HAL training between days 36 and 50 of admission. Each session lasted 60 min and was conducted in addition to her ongoing standard 180 min of daily conventional rehabilitation therapy. During HAL sessions, the patient practiced overground ambulation, with the level of robotic assistance and ambulatory distance tailored to her endurance. Throughout the training period, functional gains were observed, including improvements in ambulation and motor FIM scores. She initially tolerated approximately 60 m of assisted ambulation while on HAL. Her ambulatory distance progressively increased with each session, and by the 10th HAL session, she was able to consistently ambulate 300 m with supervision at an increased preset walking speed. She tolerated all the HAL sessions well and no adverse events were reported during these training sessions. Without the use of HAL, she could ambulate 50 m using a walking stick with minimal assistance from one person, indicating a progression towards independent ambulation. As the patient had achieved the predefined ambulatory goals and was progressing towards independent gait with minimal assistance, the rehabilitation team determined that subsequent therapy should focus on conventional functional gait training rather than continued robotic assistance.

Outcome and follow-up

Following completion of HAL training, the patient continued to show further functional improvement with conventional rehabilitation alone. She was discharged home on day 61 of admission, that is, after 38 days of inpatient rehabilitation and was able to ambulate independently without the use of assistive devices (figure 2). Her discharge motor FIM score was 82 points, and she achieved a motor FIM gain of 58 points, corresponding to a motor FIM efficiency of 1.52 points/day (table 1). She was seen in the outpatient clinic 2 months post discharge, and remained well, ambulating independently without residual neurological deficits.

Discussion

GBS is characterised by rapid-onset, often severe neuromuscular weakness, frequently resulting in prolonged immobility, deconditioning and delayed functional recovery. Although immunomodulatory treatment such as IVIG can halt disease progression, neurological recovery is often protracted and unpredictable, necessitating intensive rehabilitation to restore mobility and independence. Early mobilisation and gait training are key components of rehabilitation; however, profound weakness, poor truncal control and limited endurance can significantly restrict participation in conventional overground gait training during the early stages of rehabilitation, as observed in this patient. The early stage of rehabilitation refers to the period after the acute medical phase, when the patient is medically stable and able to actively participate in rehabilitation interventions. This stage typically occurs while in the acute hospital setting or under the care of an acute inpatient rehabilitation unit, led by a rehabilitation medicine physician, where intensive and goal-orientated rehabilitation is provided.

Based on our patient’s mEGOS score of 12— where the mEGOS is a clinical prediction model estimating the population-level likelihood of independent ambulation in GBS—a score of 12 is associated with a low probability of independent ambulation at 4 weeks, 3 months and 6 months (5%, 21% and 38%, respectively).14 Despite a low predicted probability of independent ambulation in the first 3–6 months, she demonstrated independent ambulation by week 8.5 after admission. In addition, she showed remarkable improvements in her motor FIM score with an efficiency of 1.52. Although there is limited published literature regarding FIM efficiency in cohorts of patients with GBS, a FIM efficiency exceeding 0.6 in the stroke population is considered clinically significant, indicating effective functional improvement relative to the length of stay.15 The observed functional gains likely reflect a combination of natural neurological recovery, prior immunomodulatory treatment, intensive multidisciplinary rehabilitation and the use of RAGT. These outcomes cannot be attributed to RAGT alone. Nevertheless, this case demonstrates that RAGT can be feasibly incorporated within an inpatient rehabilitation programme and may provide an additional means of delivering task-specific overground gait practice in appropriately selected patients.

RAGT has been shown to improve gait parameters and functional outcomes across a range of neurological conditions, including stroke, spinal cord injury and multiple sclerosis, by enabling repetitive, task-specific gait practice at an intensity that may not be achievable with conventional therapy alone.4 6 16 17

In contrast, evidence supporting the use of RAGT in GBS remains limited, with existing publications mainly involving a case report describing the use of grounded exoskeleton treadmill training in a geriatric patient,9 a case report detailing a wearable robotic exoskeleton restricted to the chronic phase of recovery10 and a cross-sectional study and mixed-methods feasibility study, both of which used end-effector devices.11 12 In the case described by Chen et al, the patient’s lower limb strength improved from 0/5 to 4/5, and the Barthel Index Score improved from 15 to 65. However, she still required the use of rollators and moderate assistance from a caregiver.9 In comparison, the case reported by Yabuki et al achieved independent ambulation which was similar to the outcome seen in our patient. Differences in outcomes between reports could be related to variations in age, disease severity, baseline functional status, rehabilitation intensity and number of RAGT sessions received, of which Chen et al reported the use of only 2 Lokomat sessions in their patient.9

From a neurorehabilitation perspective, end-effector and exoskeleton systems may differ in their influence on motor learning. End-effector devices primarily emphasise repetitive stepping and endurance, whereas exoskeleton-based systems—particularly those incorporating voluntary-driven or bioelectrical signal–based control such as the HAL—may facilitate active participation by amplifying the patient’s motor intent.18 19 The HAL is hypothesised to work via an interactive closed bio-feedback loop where voluntary central nervous system (CNS) motor signals are conducted to the muscles via peripheral nerves and are picked up as bioelectrical signals which trigger the motion of the HAL. This movement, in turn, sends afferent sensory feedback back to the CNS to amplify motor output.18 This distinction may be especially relevant in GBS, where recovery depends on peripheral nerve regeneration and the re-establishment of voluntary motor control.20

As there is currently insufficient published data on selection criteria for use of HAL in GBS, the decision to commence HAL was based on clinical judgement and safety principles adapted from its use in other neurological populations. HAL was initiated when the patient was medically stable following the acute phase of illness, could tolerate upright positioning and was able to participate actively in therapy. The patient had also demonstrated improvements in trunk control and standing tolerance for safe use of HAL but continued to require substantial assistance for ambulation. Future studies are needed to establish standardised patient-selection criteria and optimal timing for exoskeleton-assisted gait training in GBS.

From a therapist’s workload perspective, overground robotic exoskeleton gait training has been associated with lower physiological and perceptual demands placed on therapists compared with conventional gait training.21 Moreover, economic studies in other neurological populations suggest that RAGT could lead to cost savings. A recent study by Shankar et al showed that RAGT was cost-effective compared with conventional physiotherapy in patients with stroke, especially in those who had severe mobility impairments.22 Pinto et al showed that overground robotic exoskeleton reduced annual gait training costs (ie, net savings) between US$1114 and US$4784 per annum in patients with spinal cord injury.23

Safety is a critical consideration when introducing advanced rehabilitation technologies in medically complex patients with acute neuromuscular disease. In this case, RAGT with the use of HAL was well tolerated, with no reported adverse events such as falls, autonomic instability, excessive fatigue or pain; this is consistent with findings reported in other inpatient rehabilitation populations.24 This supports the feasibility of exoskeleton-based RAGT in carefully selected patients with acute GBS who are medically stable and able to actively participate in therapy, provided appropriate supervision and individualised progression are maintained.25 While the contribution of HAL to the observed outcomes cannot be determined from a single case, our case report supports that exoskeleton-based RAGT may be considered as a feasible adjunct for delivering repetitive, task-specific overground gait practice in patients with GBS who have significant mobility limitations. Future prospective studies are required to evaluate effectiveness, determine optimal patient selection and timing and assess long-term outcomes.

Limitations

Despite these encouraging observations, several limitations must be acknowledged. As a single case report, causal inferences regarding the effectiveness of RAGT using HAL cannot be made, and spontaneous neurological recovery inherent to GBS cannot be excluded as a major contributor to the observed functional gains. As HAL was introduced after the patient had already demonstrated neurological and functional improvement with conventional rehabilitation, it is challenging to determine whether similar outcomes would have been achieved without its use. In addition, the patient received concurrent high-intensity conventional rehabilitation, making it difficult to isolate the specific effects of HAL. The absence of a comparator and the single-patient design limit the generalisability of the findings. Furthermore, objective gait parameters such as cadence, step length or energy expenditure were not systematically measured, which may have provided additional insight into the mechanisms of improvement. Finally, the mEGOS was used strictly to provide prognostic information regarding the functional recovery, enabling the rehabilitation team in setting realistic expectations for the patient and family regarding the timeline and care needs. It is not meant to serve as a benchmark against which treatment efficacy of HAL can be evaluated.
