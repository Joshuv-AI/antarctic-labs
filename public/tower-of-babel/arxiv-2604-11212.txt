Sofic measures
Marie-Pierre Béal ID Vincent Jugé, Jean Mairesse, Dominique Perrin

Abstract
Sofic measures, also known as hidden Markov measures, have been extensively studied. In this paper, we survey some equivalent definitions of this notion and improve a bound for deciding whether a sofic measure is k k -step Markov. We prove that if an invariant sofic measure with a linear representation of dimension n n is a k k -step Markov chain, then k k can be chosen at most equal to 2 n 2 − 1 2^{n^{2}-1} .

Introduction

Sofic measures are also known as hidden Markov measures and have been widely studied.
They
are of frequent use in applications.
They were studied in
[
4
]
under the name of rational measures, and defined as a particular class of rational series.
Several equivalent definitions are given in the survey of Boyle and Petersen
[
2
]
, where the term of sofic measure is introduced.
A result due to Heller
[
6
]
characterizes the sofic measures that are
k
-step Markov.
A bound established in
[
2
]
shows that the problem of whether a sofic measure is
k
-step Markov is decidable.
In this paper, we improve significantly this bound (Theorem
5.1
).
The proof uses a result of extremal set theory
[
5
]
, itself a variation of a result of Lovász
[
8
]
.

The paper is organized as follows.
A preliminary section contains definitions from symbolic dynamics and probability theory.
Section
3
introduces sofic measures as images of
1
-step Markov measures by a factor map:
this is the traditional definition of hidden Markov measure.
We prove the equivalence to the definition as a particular class of rational series (Theorem
3.1
), originally due to Furstenberg
[
3
]
.
In section
4
, we recall a result due to Holland
[
7
]
characterizing the sofic measures that are
k
-step Markov (Theorem
4.4
).
Section
5
contains our main result (Theorem
5.1
).

Preliminaries

Let
A
be a finite alphabet.
Let
A^{*}
denote the set of finite words over
A
.
The empty word is denoted by
\varepsilon
.

Let
A^{\mathbb{Z}}
denote the set of two-sided infinite sequences on the alphabet
A
, considered as a compact topological space for the usual topology.
For
x\in A^{\mathbb{Z}}
and
i\leqslant j
, the factor

is denoted by

.
Let

denote the shift transformation on
A^{\mathbb{Z}}
defined by
y=S(x)

y_{n}=x_{n+1}
for every

.

A

infinite sequences on an alphabet

.
The

of the shift space
X
, denoted by
\mathcal{L}(X)
, is the set of words appearing as factors of elements of
X
, that is, the set of words
x_{[i,j)}
for which
x\in X
and
i<j
.
For an individual sequence, let
\mathcal{L}(x)
denote the set of factors of
x
.
Thus,
\mathcal{L}(X)=\cup_{x\in X}\mathcal{L}(x)
.
Let
\mathcal{L}_{n}(X)
also denote the elements of
\mathcal{L}(X)
of length
n
.

A shift space is of

if there is a finite set
F
of words such that
\mathcal{L}(X)
is the set of all sequences without any factor in
F
.

Let
X
and
Y
be shift spaces on alphabets
A
and
B

Let

and

.
The

defined by

\phi

to

\phi(x)_{i}=f(x_{i-m}\cdots x_{i+n})

.
We say that

is a
k
-block map
if

.

If

maps

onto

from

to
Y
.
A one-to-one factor map is called a

.

A
sofic shift space
is the image of a shift space of finite type by factor map.

Let
X
be a shift space.
For
k\geqslant 1
, let

be a bijection from the set

onto an alphabet

.
The sliding block code

is a conjugacy, called the
k
th

, and

-block presentation

.

A probability measure on a shift space is a probability measure on the
family of Borel subsets of
X
(that is, the
\sigma
-algebra generated by open sets).
A probability measure

on a shift space

\mu(S^{-1}U)=\mu(U)
for every Borel subset

.

If
\phi\colon X\to Y
is a factor map, the measure

, called the

of

by

for every Borel subset

of
Y
.
If
\mu
is invariant, the image of
\mu
is invariant.

For

, the cylinder defined by a word

of length

is the set
[w]_{X}=\{x\in X\mid x_{[0,n)}=w\}
.

A probability measure
\mu
on a shift space

defines a map

, also commonly denoted by

, such that
\pi(w)=\mu([w]_{X})
.
It satisfies

(2.1)

and, for every

\pi(w)=\sum_{a\in A,wa\in\mathcal{L}(X)}\pi(wa).

(2.2)

If

\pi(w)=\sum_{a\in A,aw\in\mathcal{L}(X)}\pi(aw).

(2.3)

Conversely, for every

),
(
2.2
)
and (

) for every

on

\hat{\mu}=\pi
.

As a particular case, a
Bernoulli measure
on
A^{\mathbb{Z}}
is a probability measure
\mu
such that

is a monoid morphism from

If

Given a finite set

A sofic measure is also known as a

The term

is also of common use instead of Markov measure, just as

is used instead of hidden Markov measure.

Given an integer
n\geqslant 1
, a row vector
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, a column vector
\gamma\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and a morphism
\varphi
is a morphism from
A^{*}
to the monoid of
n\times n
real matrices, the triple
(\lambda,\varphi,\gamma)
defines a map
\pi\colon A^{*}\to\mathbb{R}
by

\pi(w)=\lambda\varphi(w)\gamma.

(3.1)

for
w\in A^{*}
.

The triple
(\lambda,\varphi,\gamma)
is said to be a
linear representation
of
\pi
.
The representation is non-negative if
\lambda
,
\gamma
and the matrices
\varphi(w)
are non-negative.
Two triples are equivalent if they define the same map.

The following result is a combination of ideas appearing in
[
3
]
,
[
6
]
and
[
4
]
(see
[
2
, Theorem 4.20]
). A linear representation of a probability measure

is a linear representation of the map
\hat{\mu}
.

Theorem 3.1

Let
Y
be a shift space over the alphabet
A
.
An invariant probability measure on
Y
has a non-negative linear representation if and only if it is sofic.
In this case, the linear representation can be chosen such that

(i)

the matrix
M=\sum_{a\in A}\varphi(a)
is stochastic;

(ii)

the vector
\lambda
is stochastic and
\gamma
has all its components equal to
1
;

(iii)

the vector
\lambda
satisfies the relation
\lambda M=\lambda
.

Proof.

Let
\nu=\phi\mu
be a sofic measure on
Y
, with
\phi\colon X\to Y
a factor map and
\mu
a 1-step Markov on the shift of finite type
X
. Let
(v,M)
be the pair of a vector
and a
B\times B
-stochastic matrix defining the Markov measure
\mu
on
X=X_{M}
.
We may assume that the factor map
\phi
is
1
-block, and therefore defined by a block map
f\colon B\to A
.
Consider the graph on
B
with edges
(p,a,q)
when
f(q)=a
.
The measure
\nu
is then defined by

\hat{\nu}(w)=\sum_{\text{$c$ a path labelled by~$w$}}\hat{\mu}(c).

We obtain a linear representation of
\nu
by setting
\lambda=v
, taking
\gamma
as the column vector of all ones, and defining
\varphi(w)
as the
B\times B
-matrix with coefficients

\varphi_{p,q}(a)=\begin{cases}M_{p,q}&\text{ if~$f(q)=a$,}\\
0&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

The triple
(\lambda,\varphi,\gamma)
satisfies conditions (i), (ii)
and (iii).

Conversely, assume that
\mu
has a non-negative linear representation
(\lambda,\varphi,\gamma)
.
We first show that we can assume that the triple satisfies conditions (i), (ii) and (iii).

Set
M=\sum_{a\in A}\varphi(a)
.
We may assume that the representation is trim, that is, that for every index
q
, there exist integers
k,\ell\geqslant 0
such that
(\lambda M^{k})_{q}>0
and
(M^{\ell}\gamma)_{q}>0
.

Then we can change
(\lambda,\varphi,\gamma)
into
(\lambda,\varphi,M^{k}\gamma)
.
Indeed, arguing by induction on
k
, we have, for every word
u
,

\lambda\varphi(u)M^{k}\gamma=\sum_{a\in A}\lambda\varphi(u)\varphi(a)M^{k-1}\gamma=\sum_{a\in A}\hat{\mu}(ua)=\hat{\mu}(u).

Since the representation is trim, the coefficients of
M^{n}
remain bounded:
if
M^{n}_{pq}
is unbounded,
\lambda M^{k+n+\ell}\gamma
is unbounded whenever
\lambda M^{k}_{p}>0
and
M^{\ell}_{q}\mu>0
.
Let
\gamma^{\prime}
be a vector which is the limit of a subsequence of the sequence
\gamma_{k}=\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=1}^{k}M^{k}\gamma
.
Then
M\gamma^{\prime}=\gamma^{\prime}
and
(\lambda,\varphi,\gamma^{\prime})
is a non-negative linear representation of
\mu
.

We may also assume that
\gamma^{\prime}
is positive;
otherwise, we can simply omit the corresponding indices (if
\gamma^{\prime}_{q}=0
, we have
(\varphi(w)\gamma^{\prime})_{q}=0
for every word
w
).
Let
D
be the diagonal matrix with coefficients
D_{i,i}=\gamma^{\prime}_{i}
.
The triple
(\lambda D,\psi,D^{-1}\gamma^{\prime})
given by
\psi(w)=D^{-1}\varphi(w)D
satisfies conditions (i) and (ii).

Finally, a similar argument shows that we can change
\lambda
to a vector
\lambda^{\prime}
such that
\lambda^{\prime}M=\lambda^{\prime}
.

We can now consider the set
B=\{1,2\ldots,n\}\times A
, where
n
is the dimension of the matrices
\varphi(w)
.
Let
N
be the matrix

N_{p,q}=\begin{cases}\varphi(b)_{i,j}&\text{ if~$p=(i,a)$ and $q=(j,b)$,}\\
0&\text{ otherwise.}\end{cases}

We have

\sum_{q\in B}N_{p,q}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\sum_{b\in A}\varphi(b)_{i,j}=1,

and thus
N
is stochastic.
It is easy to verify that
\mu
is the sofic measure defined by the Markov measure corresponding to
(v,N)
with
v
such that
\sum_{a\in A}v_{i,a}=\lambda_{i}
.

We now focus on the problem of determining when a sofic probability measure is a

We first need to recall the notion of minimal linear representation.
A linear representation
(\lambda,\varphi,\gamma)

It is classical (see

\varphi(w)=\begin{bmatrix}*&0&0\\
*&\varphi^{\prime}(w)&0\\
*&*&*\end{bmatrix}.

Lemma 4.5

A probability measure
\mu

is
1

\hat{\mu}(abw)=\frac{\hat{\mu}(ab)\hat{\mu}(bw)}{\hat{\mu}(b)}

for every

and

.

Proof.

If
\mu
is the
1
-step Markov measure defined by the pair
(v,M)
,
we have for

and

\displaystyle\hat{\mu}(abw)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle v_{a}M_{a,b}M_{b,a_{1}}M_{a_{1},a_{2}}\cdots M_{a_{n-1},a_{n}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{(v_{a}M_{a,b})(v_{b}M_{b,a_{1}}\cdots)}{v_{b}}=\frac{\hat{\mu}(ab)\hat{\mu}(bw)}{\hat{\mu(b)}}.

Conversely, set
v_{a}=\hat{\mu}(a)
and

Then

\displaystyle\hat{\mu}(a_{1}\cdots a_{n})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\hat{\mu}(a_{1}a_{2})\hat{\mu}(a_{2}\cdots a_{n})}{\hat{\mu}(a_{2})}=\hat{\mu}(a_{1})\frac{\hat{\mu}(a_{1}a_{2})}{\hat{\mu}(a_{1})}\frac{\hat{\mu}(a_{2}\cdots a_{n})}{\hat{\mu}(a_{2})}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle v_{a_{1}}M_{a_{1},a_{2}}\frac{\hat{\mu}(a_{2}\cdots a_{n})}{\hat{\mu}(a_{2})},

proving by induction on
n
that
\hat{\mu}(a_{1}a_{2}\cdots a_{n})=v_{a_{1}}M_{a_{1},a_{2}}\cdots M_{a_{n-1},a_{n}}
.
∎

Proof
of Theorem
4.4
.

Assume first that
k=1
.

If
\mu
is
1
-step Markov, let
(v,M)
be the pair defining
\mu
.
Then
\mu
has the linear representation
(\lambda,\varphi,\gamma)

with
\lambda=v
,
\gamma
with all coefficients
1
and
for every
a,b,c\in A

\varphi(a)_{b,c}=\begin{cases}M_{b,c}&\text{ if~$a=c$,}\\
0&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

In this case, all matrices
\varphi(a)
have only one non-zero
column and are thus of rank
1
. This implies that
the matrices of the minimal representation have the same property.

Conversely, consider
a,b\in A
and
w\in A^{*}
. Since
\varphi(a),\varphi(b)
have rank
1
, we can write
\varphi(a)=c_{a}\ell_{a}
,
\varphi(b)=c_{b}\ell_{b}

where
c_{a},c_{b}
are column vectors and
\ell_{a},\ell_{b}
are row
vectors. We have, permuting the factors which are scalars,

\displaystyle\hat{\mu}(ab)\hat{\mu}(bw)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(\lambda c_{a}\ell_{a}c_{b}\ell_{b}\gamma)(\lambda c_{b}\ell_{b}\varphi(w)\gamma)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lambda c_{a}\ell_{a}c_{b}(\ell_{b}\gamma)(\lambda c_{b})(\ell_{b}\varphi(w)\gamma)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lambda c_{a}\ell_{a}c_{b}(\ell_{b}\varphi(w)\gamma)(\lambda c_{b})(\ell_{b}\gamma)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\hat{\mu}(abw)\hat{\mu}(b),

which shows that
\mu
is
1
-step Markov by Lemma
4.5
.

In the general case, assume first that
\mu
is
k
-step Markov on
X
.
Recall that
\gamma_{k}
denotes a bijection from
\mathcal{L}_{k}(X)
onto
A_{k}
.
Let
(v,M)
be the pair defining a
1
-step Markov measure on

such that

for every

.
Let
(\lambda,\varphi,\gamma)
be the linear representation of
\mu

\lambda=v
,
\gamma=\begin{bmatrix}1&&\ldots&1\end{bmatrix}^{t}
and,
for every

,

\varphi(a)=\begin{cases}M_{u,v}&\text{ if~$ua=bv$ for some~$b\in A$}\\
0&\text{ otherwise}.\end{cases}

Then, for every

Conversely, let

5
Identification of Markov sofic measures

We will now give an effective version of Theorem
4.4
, by giving a bound
K
such that if a sofic measure is not
K
-step Markov, then it is not
k
-step Markov for any
k\geqslant 1
.
This improves the result given in
[
2
]
, in which the bound
K
is much larger (it is expressed as
an unbounded pile-up of exponentials).

We prove the following result.

Theorem 5.1

Let
\mu
be an invariant sofic measure and let
n
be the dimension of its minimal representation.
Set
K=2^{n^{2}-1}
. If
\mu
is not a
K
-step Markov measure, then it is not a
k
-step Markov measure for any
k\geqslant 1
.

The proof uses the following result from
[
5
, Theorem 1.8]
, itself a variation on a result of
[
8
]
.

Theorem 5.2

Let
W
be an
n
-dimensional vector space, and let
U_{1},\ldots,U_{m}
and
V_{1},\ldots,V_{m}
be subspaces of
W
.
Assume that there exists an integer
t\geqslant 0
such that
\dim(U_{i}\cap V_{i})\leqslant t
for
1\leqslant i\leqslant m
and
\dim(U_{i}\cap V_{j})>t
for
1\leqslant i<j\leqslant m
.
Then,
m\leqslant 2^{n-t}
.

Corollary 5.3

Let
S
be a set of
n\times n
-matrices of rank
r
such that any long enough product of elements from
S
has rank at most
r-1
.
Then, every product of
2^{n+1}
elements from
S
has rank at most
r-1
.

Proof.

Let
M_{1}\cdots M_{k}
be a product of matrices
M_{i}\in S
of rank
r
, with
k
maximal.
For all
i\leqslant k-1
, let
U_{i}=\ker(M_{i+1})
and
V_{i}=\text{{Im}}(M_{i})
:
since
\rank(M_{i}M_{i+1})=r
, we have
U_{i}\cap V_{i}=\{0\}
.
Furthermore, when
1\leqslant i<j\leqslant k-1
, if
U_{i}\cap V_{j}=\{0\}
,
then
M_{1}\cdots M_{j}(M_{i}\cdots M_{j})M_{j+1}\cdots M_{k}
has rank
r
, in contradiction with the maximality of
k
.
Thus,
\dim(U_{i}\cap V_{j})>0
.

By Theorem
5.2
applied with
m=k-1
and
t=0
, we obtain
k-1\leqslant 2^{n}
,
and therefore
k\leqslant 2^{n+1}
.
∎
