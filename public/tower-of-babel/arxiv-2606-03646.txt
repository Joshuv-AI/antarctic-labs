A Benchmark for Semi-supervised Multi-modal Crowd Counting
Haoliang Meng, Xiaopeng Hong, Yabin Wang, Wangmeng Zuo

Abstract
This paper constructs the first benchmark on semi-supervised multi-modal crowd counting. To lay the foundation for this unexplored task, we first formulate the semi-supervised multi-modal setting and a standardized protocol that specifies the labeled-unlabeled data partition across different labeled ratios. Next, to establish solid reference points, we carefully tailor a diverse set of representative baselines, including existing fully supervised multi-modal methods and semi-supervised single-modal methods. Then, we carefully evaluate their performance under our proposed benchmark. Codes and the data partition will be released on https://github.com/HenryCilence/Semi-supervised-Multimodal-Crowd-Counting .



1
Introduction

Multi-modal crowd counting
[
21
,
20
,
14
,
24
]
aims to estimate crowd density and distribution by integrating heterogeneous sensing data, such as RGB, thermal, and depth. By leveraging complementary cues, it significantly enhances accuracy and robustness, especially in challenging conditions where single-modal methods are unreliable. However, existing methods are predominantly developed under fully supervised settings, which heavily depend on large-scale annotated data.

While semi-supervised learning has been explored in RGB-based counting to alleviate annotation dependency
[
10
,
12
]
, single-modal methods remain highly vulnerable to extreme illuminations or severe occlusions. Under such conditions, the generated pseudo-labels or predictions are often extremely noisy, leading to catastrophic confirmation bias
[
1
]
during semi-supervised training. Fortunately, heterogeneous sensors (
e.g.
, optical-thermal cameras) are typically co-registered in hardware
[
14
,
24
]
, which suggests that massive amounts of
unannotated multi-modal image pairs
can be acquired efficiently. Thus, extending semi-supervised learning to the multi-modal domain is not only crucial for breaking the bottleneck of single-modal methods but also highly feasible in practice.

In this paper, we pioneer the task of semi-supervised multi-modal crowd counting. To lay the foundation for this unexplored task, we first formulate a standardized protocol that specifies the labeled-unlabeled data partition across different labeled-unlabeled ratios. Next, to establish solid reference points, we carefully tailor a diverse set of representative baselines, including existing fully supervised multi-modal methods and semi-supervised single-modal methods, thereby ultimately constructing a comprehensive benchmark.

Related Work

2.1
Fully supervised multi-modal crowd counting

To overcome the limitations of single-modal crowd counting
[
17
,
18
,
19
,
11
,
9
,
8
,
25
]
, recent studies explore multi-modal approaches that fuse information from heterogeneous sources such as thermal imagery
[
21
,
14
]
, depth
[
7
,
6
]
, and LiDAR
[
5
,
2
]
. Most existing works therefore focus on designing effective modality fusion or alignment modules. Representative strategies include cross-modal interaction through cross-modal attention
[
32
]
, pixel-level cross-modal adversary
[
24
]
, asymmetric modal fusion
[
30
]
, decision-level density fusion
[
3
]
, multi-modal information decomposition
[
23
]
, and multi-scale modal deformation
[
16
]
. Other researchers propose intermediate branches that gradually converge information from two source modals through gated modal collaborative
[
14
]
, auxiliary broker modality
[
21
]
, and modal difference composition
[
36
]
.

However, these fusion methods rely on accurate annotations for optimization, which are limited under the semi-supervised setting. It is an urgent demand to tailor these methods to the semi-supervised setting and further develop semi-supervised multi-modal models.

2.2
Semi-supervised RGB crowd counting

Due to the high cost of dense point annotations, semi-supervised crowd counting has gained increasing attention. Representative approaches employ dual models based on Mean Teacher
[
28
]
, and organize self-supervision signals by enforcing their prediction consistency to make efficient use of unlabeled data. For instance, OT-M
[
13
]
enforces consistency between Optimal Transport-generated point maps and student predictions. SUA
[
22
]
enforces the consistency between dual models on main and several pretext tasks. There are also other methods that employ independent density decoders for prediction consistency. For instance, GP
[
27
]
introduces a Gaussian Process-based iteration as the counterpart of the decoding network and minimizes the distance between their outputs. P
3
Net
[
12
]
enhances consistency between two independent decoders with interleaved density scales. There are also other semi-supervised paradigms based on active learning strategies
[
34
]
and foreground-background relationships
[
10
]
.

3
Benchmark and Baseline Construction

3.1
Semi-supervised setting

Given that RGB and thermal images are naturally co-registered, and the annotation bottleneck primarily lies in point labeling, we assume that both modalities are available for all training samples, while only a small subset is annotated. Following prior RGB-based semi-supervised settings
[
10
,
12
]
, we adopt labeling ratios of 5%, 10%, and 40%, corresponding to stringent, moderate, and relaxed annotation budgets, respectively. For each ratio, a fixed subset of training samples is selected as labeled data, while the remaining samples are treated as unlabeled. Formally, given the training set containing
N
samples and the labeling ratio
\mu
, we split it into a labeled subset
\mathcal{D}_{l}=\{(x_{l}^{v},x_{l}^{t},y_{l})\}_{l=1}^{\mu N}
and an unlabeled subset
\mathcal{D}_{u}=\{(x_{u}^{v},x_{u}^{t})\}_{u=1}^{(1-\mu)N}
. To avoid sampling bias caused by the acquisition order of images, the labeled subset is constructed via fixed-interval sampling over the filename-sorted training set. All labeled splits will be released upon acceptance of the paper.

Model performances are evaluated on two widely used RGB-Thermal crowd counting datasets, RGBT-CC
[
14
]
and DroneRGBT
[
24
]
. Considering that the two datasets contain thermal images acquired under different sensing modes, all models are trained and evaluated separately on each dataset rather than merging their training sets. The numbers of training and testing samples under different labeling ratios are summarized in
Table

1
. Following prior multi-modal crowd counting works, we adopt GAME
[
4
]
and RMSE
[
26
]
as the evaluation metrics. Compared with evaluating only the total count, GAME imposes stricter constraints on regional density estimation.

\mathrm{GAME}(l)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{j=1}^{4^{l}}\left|\hat{\mathcal{D}_{i}^{j}}-\mathcal{D}_{i}^{j}\right|,

(1)

\mathrm{RMSE}=\left(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}(\hat{\mathcal{D}_{i}}-\mathcal{D}_{i})^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}},

(2)

where
N
denotes the number of testing samples,
\hat{\mathcal{D}_{i}}
and
{\mathcal{D}_{i}}
denote the estimated count and the ground-truth count for the
i
-th sample,
\hat{\mathcal{D}_{i}^{j}}
and
{\mathcal{D}_{i}^{j}}
denote the estimated count and the ground-truth count for the
j
-th region of the
i
-th image pair,
4^{l}
denotes the number of the divided non-overlapping regions and
l\in\{0,1,2,3\}
.

Conclusion

This paper establishes the first semi-supervised multi-modal crowd counting benchmark. To lay the foundation for this unexplored task, we first formulate a standardized protocol that specifies the labeled-unlabeled data partition across different labeled-unlabeled ratios. Next, to establish solid reference points, we carefully tailor a diverse set of representative baseline methods, including existing fully supervised multi-modal methods and semi-supervised single-modal methods. Finally, we carefully evaluate their performance under our proposed benchmark. We hope that our work opens new avenues for future studies of semi-supervised multi-modal crowd counting.
