Finite reservoirs lead to Wentzell boundary conditions for independent random walks and exclusion process
Matheus Franco 1 , Tertuliano Franco 2 and Patrícia Gonçalves 3

Abstract
. We analyze the scaling limits (hydrodynamic limit/propagation of local equilibrium) of two particle systems in the discrete one-dimensional segment where the left boundary is in contact with a reservoir, which may stow any (finite) number of particles. These two particle systems are independent random walks and the symmetric exclusion process. At rate one a particle (if there is one there) jumps from site 1 1 to a finite reservoir, and at rate α ​ η ​ ( 0 ) ​ N − θ \alpha\eta(0)N^{-\theta} a particle jumps from the finite reservoir to the site 1 1 (if the site 1 1 is empty in the exclusion case), where η ⁡ ( 0 ) \eta(0) is the total number of particles in the reservoir at that moment and θ ≥ 0 \theta\geq 0 is a parameter whose tuning leads to a dynamical phase transition. For all values of θ \theta , the hydrodynamic equation is the heat equation with Neumann boundary conditions at the right boundary for both systems. On the other hand, the left boundary condition depends on the chosen value of θ \theta . For θ ∈ [ 0 , 1 ) \theta\in[0,1) , it is given by the Neumann boundary condition, which means that the deposit is asymptotically empty, acting as a barrier. For θ ∈ ( 1 , ∞ ) \theta\in(1,\infty) , in the random walk scenario, it is given by a non-homogeneous Dirichlet boundary condition, which means that the reservoir becomes asymptotically infinite, acting as a heat bath, while in the exclusion scenario it is given by a homogeneous Dirichlet boundary condition, meaning that the reservoir behaves as a sink. Finally, at the critical value θ = 1 \theta=1 , we obtain a non-local Dirichlet boundary condition relating the value at zero to the total mass of the system, which is additionally non-linear in the exclusion scenario. As a by-product of these results, we find an equivalence between solutions to the heat equation with Wentzell boundary conditions and solutions to the heat equation with certain non-local Dirichlet boundary conditions related to the total mass of the system.

The boundary conditions of the parabolic equations obtained in those papers vary among Dirichlet (involving the function itself), Neumann (involving the first derivative), and Robin (relating the function and its first derivative). On the other hand, to the best of our knowledge, no paper has attained a parabolic equation with Wentzell boundary conditions such as the hydrodynamic equation of some interacting particle system. This is precisely the content of this work, where a heat equation with Wentzell boundary conditions is obtained as the scaling limit of a discrete system of random particles. Additionally, as a by-product of this scaling procedure, we achieve a correspondence between parabolic PDEs with Wentzell boundary conditions and parabolic PDEs with non-local Dirichlet boundary conditions.

Two particle systems are considered here, both in the diffusive scaling. The first is a system of independent random walks in a finite box
\{0,1,\ldots,N\}
where the jump rate to a neighbor site is always one, except from the site
0
to the site
1
, whose associated jump rate is given by
\alpha N^{-\theta}
, where
\alpha>0
and
\theta\in[0,\infty)
.
Regarding this system, we prove two results. The first one is the propagation of local equilibrium, which means that, starting the system from a proper measure associated to a continuous profile
\gamma
, the system converges locally to a product measure whose parameter
\rho(t,u)
is given by the evolution of
\gamma
by a partial differential equation, which depends on the chosen value of
\theta
.
If
\theta\in[0,1)
, the limiting PDE is given by the heat equation with Neumann boundary conditions on the left boundary.
If
\theta\in(1,\infty)
, we obtain the heat equation with homogeneous Wentzell boundary condition
\rho^{\prime\prime}(0)=0
.
Finally, at the critical value
\theta=1
we obtain the heat equation with Wentzell boundary conditions of the form
\rho^{\prime\prime}(0)=\alpha\rho^{\prime}(0)
. Since the box is isolated at the rightmost site, the right boundary condition is always of homogeneous Neumann type, so we do not mention the right boundary condition from now on.

In the sequel, we prove the hydrodynamic limit for the above system of independent random walks, which also follows a dynamic phase transition. For
\theta\in[0,1)
, the strength of the site zero is not enough to have a macroscopic effect, and the hydrodynamic equation is the heat equation with Neumann boundary conditions. For
\theta\in(1,\infty)
, the reservoir has a small rate of exiting particles, of order
N^{-\theta}
, but at same it has a huge number of particles, of order
N^{\theta}
. This leads the system to stabilize the density close to the reservoir, and in this case the hydrodynamic equation is given by the heat equation with a (local) Dirichlet boundary condition.
Finally, at the critical value
\theta=1
, we obtain a non-local Dirichlet boundary condition involving the total mass of the system. Since the propagation of local equilibrium implies the hydrodynamic limit, as a by-product of these two previous results, we obtain an interesting correspondence between the PDEs above, which is also made clear directly through a simple heuristic argument at the macro, see the Remark
2.8
.

The second particle system that we consider in this work is the symmetric exclusion process in the box
\{1,\ldots,N\}
, where the site
1
is in contact with a finite reservoir at the site zero. The sites from
1
to
N
can have at most one particle, but the reservoir may have any finite number of particles. At rate one a particle jumps from site
1
to site
0

\alpha N^{-\theta}\eta(0)
a particle jumps from the reservoir to the site
1

1

\eta(0)

\theta\in[0,1)
, the reservoir does not have the strength to maintain mass within it, so the hydrodynamic equation is given by the heat equation with the Neumann boundary conditions. If
\theta\in(1,\infty)
, the small rate of order

on the incoming particles from the reservoir plus the exclusion rule at the site

leads to a homogeneous Dirichlet boundary condition
\rho(0)=0

\theta=1

Furthermore, via the correspondence deduced in the previous case about independent random walks, we present an equivalent PDE with non-linear Wentzell boundary for this hydrodynamic equation.

As the main features in the proofs, we cite the understanding of the propagation of local equilibrium in terms of a central limit theorem for the underlying random walk and its reversible measure. In terms of the hydrodynamic limit in the exclusion setting, we cite: a characterization of the reservoir in terms of the total mass of the system; attractiveness, which is used in many places along the text; and two apparently contradictory facts: the zero site is a trap which retains many particles, but it does not contribute to the limit of the empirical measure in any range of
\theta
. On the other hand, its presence in the empirical measure helps in many steps of the proofs. Characterization of reversible measures is provided in all cases, as well as the uniqueness of weak solutions of the corresponding PDE, whose proofs are based on the knowledge of the eigenfunctions of the corresponding Sturm-Liouville problem. We highlight that each regime requires sharp arguments that only hold for the corresponding range of
\theta
.

Remark 2.8
.

The propagation of local equilibrium implies the hydrodynamic limit, see

First, the solution to the heat equation with Wentzell boundary condition (

the solution to the heat equation with homogeneous Wentzell boundary condition (

The Dirichlet boundary condition (

\rho(t,0)\;=\;\alpha\Big(M-\int_{0}^{1}\rho(t,u)du\Big).

Formally differentiating the above equation in time gives us

\partial_{t}\rho(t,0)\;=\;-\alpha\int_{0}^{1}\partial_{t}\rho(t,u)du\,.

Since
\partial_{t}\rho(t,u)=\partial_{uu}^{2}\rho(t,u)
and
\partial_{u}\rho(t,1)=0
, this leads to

\displaystyle\partial_{uu}^{2}\rho(t,0)

\displaystyle=-\alpha\int_{0}^{1}\partial_{uu}^{2}\rho(t,u)du=-\alpha\Big(\partial_{u}\rho(t,1)-\partial_{u}\rho(t,0)\Big)=\alpha\partial_{u}\rho(t,0)\,,

which is the Wentzell boundary condition in (

An analogous heuristic argument can be made to deduce that the homogeneous Wentzell boundary condition in (

Remark 2.9
.

In view of the previous remark, which establishes a correspondence between the heat equation with the Wentzell boundary condition (

the reader may wonder why the constant

Observing that

Assume that the left boundary of the interval

is in contact with a finite reservoir of length

, see Figure

where the diffusion coefficient inside the finite reservoir is infinite, so the reservoir immediately achieves the equilibrium state, causing the diffusion quantity to be constant inside it.
Assume also that the finite reservoir is in contact with the left boundary of the interval
[0,1]
, implying that the reservoir’s height is always equal to

, and that the finite reservoir is isolated from the exterior world. Denote by

which is the non-local Dirichlet boundary condition in (

).

Remark 2.11
.

The homogeneous Wentzell boundary condition
f^{\prime\prime}(0)=0
appearing in (
2.8
) is also the condition that defines the domain of the absorbed Brownian motion, where the process is stopped when it reaches zero.
This agrees with the idea of a system in contact with a reservoir that behaves as a sink: once the diffusion quantity enters the reservoir, it stays there forever. On the other hand, the homogeneous Dirichlet boundary condition
f(0)=0
is also the condition that defines the domain of the killed Brownian motion (in (
2.11
) the Dirichlet boundary condition is not homogeneous, but it is intimately related to it). In the killed Brownian motion, once the particle hits zero, the process ceases to exist (to be more precise, zero is not part of the state-space of the killed BM, that is why the process must cease to exist once it goes to zero). This agrees with the interpretation of a boundary PDE that kills mass through it. In the same way that killed and absorbed Brownian motions are essentially the same process, equations (
2.8
) and (
2.11
) are also essentially the same PDE.

3.

The argument is split in two parts: the convergence in distribution of a single random walk, and an explicit formula for the joint Laplace transform of the system. We start with the former.

3.1.
Convergence in distribution of the underlying RW

In the proof of propagation of local equilibrium, we will have to understand the convergence in distribution of a single random walk (see also Figure

\displaystyle{\mathbb{L}}_{N,\theta}\widetilde{f}_{N}(\genfrac{}{}{}{1}{N}{N})

\displaystyle=N^{2}\big[\widetilde{f}_{N}(\genfrac{}{}{}{1}{N-1}{N})-\widetilde{f}_{N}(1)\big]

\displaystyle=\frac{f^{\prime\prime}(1)}{2}\cdot N\Big[h\big(\genfrac{}{}{}{1}{N-1}{N}\big)-h(1)\Big]\;\longrightarrow\;-h^{\prime}(1)\frac{f^{\prime\prime}(1)}{2}=\frac{f^{\prime\prime}(1)}{2}\,.

Recall (
3.5
). Since
h
has compact support in
(0,1]
, then
h(1-u)
has compact support in
[0,1)
, hence
{\mathbb{L}}_{N,\theta}\widehat{f}_{N,\theta}(1)=0
for
N
large enough.
Therefore,

{\mathbb{L}}_{N,\theta}f_{N}(1)\;\longrightarrow\;f^{\prime\prime}(1)={\mathbb{L}}_{\theta}f(1)\,.

It remains to study the convergence of
{\mathbb{L}}_{N,\theta}f_{N}(0)
. To do so, we divide the analysis according to the regime of
\theta
.

\bullet

Case
\theta\in[0,1)
.

By a Taylor expansion of
f
around the point
u=0
,

{\mathbb{L}}_{N,\theta}\pi_{N}f(0)\;=\;\alpha N^{2-\theta}\big[f(\genfrac{}{}{}{1}{1}{N})-f(0)\big]\;=\;\alpha N^{2-\theta}\Big[\frac{f^{\prime}(0)}{N}+\frac{f^{\prime\prime}(\zeta)}{2N^{2}}\Big]\,,

for some
\zeta\in[0,1/N]
. Since
f^{\prime}(0)=0
and
f\in C^{2}[0,1]
, we conclude that

{\mathbb{L}}_{N,\theta}\pi_{N}f(0)\;\longrightarrow\;\begin{cases}\frac{\alpha}{2}f^{\prime\prime}(0),&\text{ if }\theta=0,\\
0,&\text{ if }\theta\in(0,1),\end{cases}

as
N\to\infty
. Since
h
has compact support in
(0,1]
, then
{\mathbb{L}}_{N,\theta}\widetilde{f}_{N}(0)=0
.
From (
3.5
), we have that

\displaystyle{\mathbb{L}}_{N,\theta}\widehat{f}_{N,\theta}(0)\;\longrightarrow\;\begin{cases}\big(1-\frac{\alpha}{2}\big)f^{\prime\prime}(0),&\text{ if }\theta=0,\\
f^{\prime\prime}(0),&\text{ if }\theta\in(0,1),\end{cases}

thus
{\mathbb{L}}_{N,\theta}f_{N}(0)\to f^{\prime\prime}(0)={\mathbb{L}}_{\theta}f(0)
.

\bullet

Case
\theta=1
.
Since the boundary condition is
\alpha f^{\prime}(0)=f^{\prime\prime}(0)
,

{\mathbb{L}}_{N,\theta}\pi_{N}f(0)=\alpha N\big[f(\genfrac{}{}{}{1}{1}{N})-f(0)\big]\;\longrightarrow\;\alpha f^{\prime}(0)={\mathbb{L}}_{\theta}f(0)\,,

hence
{\mathbb{L}}_{N,\theta}f_{N}(0)\to f^{\prime\prime}(0)={\mathbb{L}}_{\theta}f(0)
.

\bullet

Case
\theta\in(1,\infty)
.
Since
f
is Lipschitz,

{\mathbb{L}}_{N,\theta}\pi_{N}f(0)=\alpha N^{2-\theta}\big[f(\genfrac{}{}{}{1}{1}{N})-f(0)\big]\;\longrightarrow\;0=f^{\prime\prime}(0)={\mathbb{L}}_{\theta}f(0)\,.

so
{\mathbb{L}}_{N,\theta}f_{N}(0)\to f^{\prime\prime}(0)={\mathbb{L}}_{\theta}f(0)
.
Putting together all the cases above, we establish (
3.3
), finishing the proof.
∎

3.2.
Joint Laplace transform of independent RWs

The proof of the propagation of local equilibrium is based on the analysis of the Laplace transform of the vector

\eta_{t}=(\eta_{t}(0),\eta_{t}(1),\ldots,\eta_{t}(N))
, where we recall that
\eta_{t}(x)
denotes the number of particles at the site
x
at time
t
. Let
(\zeta(x))_{x=0}^{N}
be a family of parameters and consider the joint Laplace transform

\Phi(\zeta)\;=\;\Phi_{\eta_{t}}(\zeta)\;:=\;\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{N}}\bigg[\exp\Big\{-\sum_{x=0}^{N}\zeta(x)\eta_{t}(x)\Big\}\bigg]\,.

Let
X_{t}^{y,k}
denote the position at time
t
of the
k
-th random walk that started at site
y
.
Then

\eta_{t}(x)\;=\;\sum_{y=0}^{N}\sum_{k=1}^{\eta_{0}(y)}\mathbf{1}\{X_{t}^{y,k}=x\}\,,

and then

\displaystyle\sum_{x=0}^{N}\zeta(x)\eta_{t}(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{x=0}^{N}\zeta(x)\sum_{y=0}^{N}\sum_{k=1}^{\eta_{0}(y)}\mathbf{1}\{X_{t}^{y,k}=x\}=\sum_{y=0}^{N}\sum_{k=1}^{\eta_{0}(y)}\zeta(X_{t}^{y,k})\sum_{x=0}^{N}\mathbf{1}\{X_{t}^{y,k}=x\}

\displaystyle=\sum_{y=0}^{N}\sum_{k=1}^{\eta_{0}(y)}\zeta(X_{t}^{y,k})\,.

Thus

\displaystyle\exp\Big\{-\sum_{x=0}^{N}\zeta(x)\eta_{t}(x)\Big\}

\displaystyle\;=\;\prod_{y=0}^{N}\prod_{k=1}^{\eta_{0}(y)}\exp\Big\{-\zeta(X_{t}^{y,k})\Big\}\,.

Since the random walks
X^{y,k}_{t}
are independent, we get

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{N}}\Big[\exp\Big\{-\sum_{x=0}^{N}\zeta(x)\eta_{t}(x)\Big\}\Big]

\displaystyle\;=\;\prod_{y=0}^{N}\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{N}}\bigg[\exp\Big\{-\sum_{k=1}^{\eta_{0}(y)}\zeta(X_{t}^{y,k})\Big\}\bigg].

Since the random walks
X_{t}^{y,k}
are also independent of the initial quantity of particles
\eta_{0}(y)
,
we can apply the Substitution Principle (see
[
8
, Example 5.1.5]
) to rewrite the last display as

\displaystyle\prod_{y=0}^{N}\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{N}}\Bigg[\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{N}}\bigg[\exp\Big\{-\sum_{k=1}^{\eta_{0}(y)}\zeta(X_{t}^{y,k})\Big\}\Big|

\displaystyle\eta_{0}\bigg]\Bigg]\;=\;\prod_{y=0}^{N}\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{N}}\bigg[\prod_{k=1}^{\eta_{0}(y)}\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{N}}\Big[e^{-\zeta(X_{t}^{y,k})}\Big]\bigg]

\displaystyle\;=\;\prod_{y=0}^{N}\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{N}}\bigg[\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{N}}\Big[e^{-\zeta(X_{t}^{y,1})}\Big]^{\eta_{0}(y)}\bigg]\,,

(3.6)

where in the last equality we used the fact that random walks starting from
y
are identically distributed.
Recall the definition of the initial measure
\mu_{N}
in (
2.5
)
and the standard fact that a random variable
Z\sim\mathrm{Poisson}(\lambda)
has a probability generating function given by
{\mathbb{E}}[s^{Z}]=\exp\{\lambda(s-1)\}
. We can thus rewrite (
3.6
) as follows:

\displaystyle\prod_{y=0}^{N}\exp\Big(\lambda(y)\big(\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{N}}[e^{-\zeta(X_{t}^{y,1})}]-1\big)\Big)\;=\;\exp\bigg[\sum_{y=0}^{N}\lambda(y)\Big(\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{N}}[e^{-\zeta(X_{t}^{y,1})}]-1\Big)\bigg],

(3.7)

where

\lambda(y)\;=\;\begin{cases}\frac{N^{\theta}}{\alpha}\gamma(\genfrac{}{}{}{1}{0}{N}),&\text{ if }y=0,\\
\gamma(\genfrac{}{}{}{1}{y}{N}),&\text{ if }y=1,\ldots,N\\
\end{cases}

(3.8)

is the parameter of the Poisson at the site
y
, see (
2.5
). We now rewrite the right-hand side of (
3.7
) as

\displaystyle\exp\bigg[\sum_{y=0}^{N}\lambda(y)\sum_{z=0}^{N}\big\{e^{-\zeta(z)}\!-\!1\big\}\,p_{t}(y,z)\bigg]=\exp\bigg[\sum_{z=0}^{N}\big\{e^{-\zeta(z)}\!-\!1\big\}\sum_{y=0}^{N}\lambda(y)\,p_{t}(y,z)\bigg],

(3.9)

where
p_{t}(y,z)=\mathbb{P}_{\mu_{N}}(X^{y,1}_{t}=z)
is the transition probability of the random walk. Now we want to write the rightmost sum on (
3.9
) as an expectation with respect to
p_{t}(y,z)
. To do so, we note that the probability measure
\pi
on
\{0,\ldots,N\}
given by

\pi(0)\;=\;\frac{\frac{N^{\theta}}{\alpha}}{\frac{N^{\theta}}{\alpha}+N}\quad\text{ and }\quad\pi(x)\;=\;\frac{1}{\frac{N^{\theta}}{\alpha}+N}\quad\text{ for }x=1,\ldots,N

(3.10)

is reversible for
p_{t}(y,z)
, that is,
\pi(y)p_{t}(y,z)=\pi(z)p_{t}(z,y)
. Applying this fact, we can rewrite (
3.9
) as

\displaystyle\exp\bigg[\sum_{z=0}^{N}\big\{e^{-\zeta(z)}-1\big\}\sum_{y=0}^{N}\lambda(y)\,\frac{\pi(z)}{\pi(y)}p_{t}(z,y)\bigg]

\displaystyle\;=\;\exp\bigg[\sum_{z=0}^{N}\big\{e^{-\zeta(z)}-1\big\}\pi(z)\sum_{y=0}^{N}p_{t}(z,y)\,\frac{\lambda(y)}{\pi(y)}\bigg].

Renaming the variable
z
to
x
, we arrive at the formula

\begin{split}\Phi_{\eta_{t}}(\zeta)&\;=\;\exp\bigg[\sum_{x=0}^{N}\big\{e^{-\zeta(x)}-1\big\}\pi(x)\sum_{y=0}^{N}p_{t}(x,y)\,\frac{\lambda(y)}{\pi(y)}\bigg].\end{split}

(3.11)

Remark 3.2
.

Up to this point, we have not yet used the expression (
3.8
) for the Poisson parameter
\lambda(x)
.
Assuming instead that

\lambda(y)\;=\;\begin{cases}\frac{N^{\theta}}{\alpha}\cdot\lambda,&\text{ if }y=0,\\
\lambda,&\text{ if }y=1,\ldots,N\\
\end{cases}

(3.12)

for a constant
\lambda>0
(here we are abusing of notation), one can infer from (
3.11
) that the measure
\nu_{\lambda}
defined in (
2.4
) is invariant. In the next section we will prove that
\nu_{\lambda}
is actually reversible, as stated in Proposition
2.1
.

Note that the formula (
3.11
) already tells us that, for any time
t>0
, the distribution of
\eta_{t}
is a Poisson product measure whose parameter at the site
x
is given by
\pi(x)\sum_{y=0}^{N}p_{t}(x,y)\,\frac{\lambda(y)}{\pi(y)}
. In the next proof we estimate this parameter.

Proof of Theorem
2.2
.

Fix
0<u<1
and a any positive integer
k
. Since we are going to take the limit in
N\to\infty
, suppose, without loss of generality, that
\lfloor uN\rfloor>k
so the random vector

\eta_{t}^{k,u}:=\big(\eta_{t}(\lfloor uN\rfloor-k),\ldots,\eta_{t}(\lfloor uN\rfloor+k)\big)

does not contain the site zero. By the Laplace transform formula (
3.11
), choosing
\zeta(x)=0
for
x\notin\{\lfloor uN\rfloor-k,\ldots,\lfloor uN\rfloor+k\}
we infer that

\displaystyle\Phi_{\eta_{t}^{k,u}}(\zeta)

\displaystyle\;:=\;\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{N}}\bigg[\exp\Big\{-\sum_{x=\lfloor uN\rfloor-k}^{\lfloor uN\rfloor+k}\zeta(x)\eta_{t}(x)\Big\}\bigg]

\displaystyle\;=\;\exp\bigg[\sum_{x=\lfloor uN\rfloor-k}^{\lfloor uN\rfloor+k}\big\{e^{-\zeta(x)}-1\big\}\pi(x)\sum_{y=0}^{N}p_{t}(x,y)\,\frac{\lambda(y)}{\pi(y)}\bigg].

Since the convergence of the Laplace transform characterizes the weak convergence of probability measures concentrated on Cartesian products of non-negative half-lines, our proof reduces to characterize the limit of

\psi^{N}_{t}(x)\;:=\;\pi(x)\sum_{y=0}^{N}p_{t}(x,y)\,\frac{\lambda(y)}{\pi(y)}

for
x\in\{\lfloor uN\rfloor-k,\ldots,\lfloor uN\rfloor+k\}
as
N\to\infty
.
Recalling the Poisson parameters (
3.8
) and the invariant measure (
3.10
) and having in mind that
x\neq 0
, one can check that

\psi^{N}_{t}(x)\;=\;\sum_{y=0}^{N}p_{t}(x,y)\gamma\big(\genfrac{}{}{}{1}{y}{N}\big)\;=\;E\Big[\gamma\Big(\frac{X_{t}^{x,1}}{N}\Big)\Big]\,,

(3.13)

where
E
is the expectation with respect to the random walk
X_{t}^{x,1}
that starts at
x\neq 0
. Since
\gamma
is a bounded continuous function, by Proposition
3.1
and Portmanteau’s Theorem, for any
x\in\{\lfloor uN\rfloor-k,\ldots,\lfloor uN\rfloor+k\}
,

E\Big[\gamma\Big(\frac{X_{t}^{x}}{N}\Big)\Big]\longrightarrow P_{t}^{\theta}\gamma(u)\quad\text{ as }N\to\infty\,,

where
P^{\theta}_{t}
is, as explained in Proposition
3.1
:

•

If
\theta\in[0,1)
, the semigroup of a Brownian motion on
[0,1]
reflected both at
0
and
1
.

•

If
\theta=1
, the semigroup of a Brownian motion on
[0,1]
sticky at
0
and reflected at
1
.

•

If
\theta\in(1,\infty)
, the semigroup of a Brownian motion on
[0,1]
absorbed at
0
and reflected at
1
.

For the three semigroups above, it is true that
P_{t}^{\theta}f\in{\mathfrak{D}}({\mathsf{L}})
for any time
t>0
and for any
f\in C[0,1]
.
This implies that
P_{t}^{\theta}\gamma(u)
satisfies the boundary conditions of (
2.6
),
(
2.7
) or (
2.8
) if, respectively,
\theta\in[0,1)
,
\theta=1
or
\theta\in(1,\infty)
. Moreover, since in any case the process is a Brownian motion in
[0,1]
, the Feller semigroup
P_{t}^{\theta}\gamma(u)
satisfies the heat equation with initial condition
\gamma
. This concludes the proof.
∎

Remark 3.3
.

Although the Poisson parameter at zero was used in the proof above at (
3.13
), we did not explicitly study the behavior at the site zero. For the sake of completeness, see that

\psi^{N}_{t}(0)\;:=\;\pi(0)\sum_{y=0}^{N}p_{t}(0,y)\,\frac{\lambda(y)}{\pi(y)}\;=\;\frac{N^{\theta}}{\alpha}E\Big[\gamma\Big(\frac{X_{t}^{0,1}}{N}\Big)\Big]\,,

which explodes in the same order as
\frac{N^{\theta}}{\alpha}P^{\theta}_{t}\gamma(0)
.

4.
Proof of hydrodynamics for independent RWs

4.1.
Reversible measure

We start by proving Proposition
2.1
which says that
\nu_{\lambda}
is reversible for independent random walks.

Proof of Proposition
2.1
.

The statement is equivalent to the identity

\int g(\eta){\mathsf{L}}_{N}f(\eta)\,d\nu_{\lambda}(\eta)\;=\;\int f(\eta){\mathsf{L}}_{N}g(\eta)\,d\nu_{\lambda}(\eta)

(4.1)

for every
f,g:\Omega_{N}\to{\mathbb{R}}
, see
[
27
]
.
It is well-known that a product Poisson measure of constant parameter is reversible for the dynamics of symmetric independent random walks. Therefore, we only need to check the contributions to (
4.1
) of jumps involving the sites
0
and
1
.
Recall (
2.4
). For ease of notation, denote
\psi_{0}=\frac{N^{\theta}}{\alpha}\lambda
and
\psi_{1}=\lambda
, so

\nu_{\lambda}\;=\;\mathrm{Poisson}(\psi_{0})\otimes\bigotimes_{x=1}^{N}\mathrm{Poisson}(\psi_{1})\,.

Let
\xi
be the configuration obtained from
\eta
by moving one particle from the site
0
to the site
1
, if possible. That is,
\xi=\eta^{0,1}
as in (
2.3
).
Thus, assuming that
k\geq 1
, we have
\eta(0)=k
and
\eta(1)=\ell
if, and only if,
\xi(0)=k-1
and
\xi(1)=\ell+1
.
On the other hand,
\eta(0)=0
if, and only if,
\eta=\xi
.
The Radon-Nikodym derivative of this transformation on the set
A=\{\eta:\eta(0)\geq 1\}
is given by

\frac{\nu_{\lambda}(\eta)}{\nu_{\lambda}(\xi)}=\frac{e^{-\psi_{0}}\psi_{0}^{k}}{k!}\cdot\frac{e^{-\psi_{1}}\psi_{1}^{\ell}}{\ell!}\cdot\frac{(k-1)!}{e^{-\psi_{0}}\psi_{0}^{k-1}}\cdot\frac{(\ell+1)!}{e^{-\psi_{1}}\psi_{1}^{\ell+1}}=\frac{\psi_{0}}{\psi_{1}}\cdot\frac{\ell+1}{k}=\frac{\psi_{0}}{\psi_{1}}\cdot\frac{\xi(1)}{\xi(0)+1}\,.

We compute the contribution of the jump
0\to 1
by performing the change of variables
\xi=\eta^{0,1}
as follows. Note that the integrand in the first integral below vanishes in the set
\{\eta:\eta(0)=0\}
.

\displaystyle\int g(\eta)\frac{\alpha}{N^{\theta}}\eta(0)\Big[f(\eta^{0,1})-f(\eta)\Big]d\nu_{\lambda}(\eta)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\geq 1}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\eta\in\Omega_{N}:\\
\eta(0)=k\end{subarray}}g(\eta)\frac{\alpha}{N^{\theta}}\eta(0)\Big[f(\eta^{0,1})-f(\eta)\Big]\nu_{\lambda}(\eta)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\geq 1}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\xi\in\Omega_{N}:\\
\xi(0)=k-1\end{subarray}}g(\xi^{1,0})\frac{\alpha}{N^{\theta}}(\xi(0)+1)\Big[f(\xi)-f(\xi^{1,0})\Big]\frac{\nu_{\lambda}(\eta)}{\nu_{\lambda}(\xi)}\nu_{\lambda}(\xi)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\geq 1}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\xi\in\Omega_{N}:\\
\xi(0)=k-1\end{subarray}}g(\xi^{1,0})\frac{\alpha}{N^{\theta}}(\xi(0)+1)\Big[f(\xi)-f(\xi^{1,0})\Big]\frac{\psi_{0}}{\psi_{1}}\frac{\xi(1)}{(\xi(0)+1)}\nu_{\lambda}(\xi)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\geq 0}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\xi\in\Omega_{N}:\\
\xi(0)=k\end{subarray}}\frac{\alpha}{N^{\theta}}\,\frac{\psi_{0}}{\psi_{1}}g(\xi^{1,0})\xi(1)\Big[f(\xi)-f(\xi^{1,0})\Big]\nu_{\lambda}(\xi)

\displaystyle=\int\frac{\alpha}{N^{\theta}}\,\frac{\psi_{0}}{\psi_{1}}g(\xi^{1,0})\xi(1)\Big[f(\xi)-f(\xi^{1,0})\Big]d\nu_{\lambda}(\xi)\,.

Now we can check the detailed balance condition related to the bond
\{0,1\}
.

\displaystyle\int g(\eta)\frac{\alpha}{N^{\theta}}\eta(0)\big[f(\eta^{0,1})-f(\eta)\big]d\nu_{\lambda}+\int g(\eta)\eta(1)\big[f(\eta^{1,0})-f(\eta)\big]d\nu_{\lambda}

\displaystyle-\bigg[\int f(\eta)\frac{\alpha}{N^{\theta}}\eta(0)[g(\eta^{0,1})-g(\eta)]d\nu_{\lambda}+\int f(\eta)\eta(1)\big[g(\eta^{1,0})-g(\eta)\big]d\nu_{\lambda}\bigg]

\displaystyle=\int\frac{\alpha}{N^{\theta}}\,\frac{\psi_{0}}{\psi_{1}}g(\eta^{1,0})\eta(1)\big[f(\eta)-f(\eta^{1,0})\big]d\nu_{\lambda}+\int g(\eta)\eta(1)\big[f(\eta^{1,0})-f(\eta)\big]d\nu_{\lambda}

\displaystyle-\int\frac{\alpha}{N^{\theta}}\,\frac{\psi_{0}}{\psi_{1}}f(\eta^{1,0})\eta(1)\big[g(\eta)-g(\eta^{1,0})\big]d\nu_{\lambda}-\int f(\eta)\eta(1)\big[g(\eta^{1,0})-g(\eta)\big]d\nu_{\lambda}\,,

which vanishes since
\psi_{0}=\frac{N^{\theta}}{\alpha}\,\psi_{1}
, concluding the proof.
∎

4.2.
Scaling limit

Let
\{\pi^{N}_{t}:t\in[0,T]\}
be the
empirical measure
defined by

\pi^{N}_{t}\;=\;\frac{1}{N}\sum_{x=1}^{N}\eta_{t}(x)\delta_{\frac{x}{N}}\,,

(4.2)

which characterizes the spatial density of the particles of the process as embedded in the interval
[0,1]
.
Note that the empirical measure
does not
include the zero site, and it is a random element in the Skorokhod space
D([0,T];{\mathcal{M}})
of càdlàg trajectories, where
{\mathcal{M}}
is the space of non-negative measures on
[0,1]
.

Let
\mu_{N}
be the Poisson product measure introduced in (
2.5
), let
{\mathbb{Q}}_{\mu_{N}}^{\theta,N}
be the measure on the path space
D([0,T];{\mathcal{M}})
induced by the measure and
the empirical measure
\pi^{N}_{t}
introduced in (
4.2
) above. Recall that
\mu_{N}
is defined in terms of a fixed continuous profile
\gamma:[0,1]\to[0,\infty)
.

Definition 4.1
.

Let
{\mathbb{Q}}^{\theta}
be
the probability measure on
D([0,T];{\mathcal{M}})
concentrated on the
deterministic path
\pi(t,du)=\rho(t,u)du
, where
\rho(t,u)
is:

•

if
\theta\in[0,1)
, the unique strong solution to the heat equation (
2.6
).

•

if
\theta=1
, the unique strong solution to the heat equation (
2.7
).

•

if
\theta\in(1,\infty)
, the unique strong solution to the heat equation (
2.8
).

Since the propagation of local equilibrium implies the hydrodynamic limit, as a consequence of Theorem
2.2
we have that:

Corollary 4.2
.

As
N\uparrow\infty
, the sequence of probability measures
\{{\mathbb{Q}}_{\mu_{N}}^{\theta,N}:N\geq{1}\}
converges weakly to
{\mathbb{Q}}^{\theta}
.

4.3.
A second characterization of
{\mathbb{Q}}^{\theta}
for
\theta=1
and
\theta\in(1,\infty)

By Corollary
4.2
, we know that, as
N\to\infty
, the sequence of measures
\{{\mathbb{Q}}^{\theta,N}_{\mu_{N}}:N\geq{1}\}
converges weakly to
{\mathbb{Q}}^{\theta}
, which is a delta of Dirac on the trajectory
\pi(t,du)=\rho(t,u)du
, whose density

\rho(t,u)
is a weak solution to (
2.6
), (
2.7
) or (
2.8
) according to
\theta\in[0,1)
,
\theta=1
or
\theta\in(1,\infty)
, respectively.

Our task now is to give a second characterization of
{\mathbb{Q}}^{\theta}
for
\theta=1
and
\theta\in(1,\infty)
via the convergence of the Dynkin’s martingale.
Namely, we will show that, for
\theta=1
, the density
\rho(t,u)
is (also) the unique weak solution to (
2.9
) and, for
\theta>1
, it is (also) the unique weak solution to (
2.11
), providing a rigorous proof of what was already discussed in the heuristic arguments given in Remarks
2.8
and
2.9
.

A second characterization of
{\mathbb{Q}}^{\theta}
for
\theta\in[0,1)
is omitted because our approach would lead to the same hydrodynamic equation already found in the Theorem
2.2
(the heat equation with Neumann boundary conditions at both
0
and
1
), thus providing no extra information.

Assume for the moment the uniqueness of weak solutions of the PDE’s (
2.9
) and (
2.11
), which is the topic of the next subsection.
Recall (
2.1
).
For any function
H
, the process

M^{N}_{t}(H)\;:=\;\langle\pi^{N}_{t},H\rangle-\langle\pi^{N}_{0},H\rangle-\int_{0}^{t}{\mathsf{L}}_{N}\langle\pi^{N}_{s},H\rangle\,ds

(4.3)

is a martingale with respect to the natural filtration
\mathcal{F}_{t}:=\sigma(\eta_{s}:s\leq{t})
, the so-called Dynkin’s martingale, see
[
27
]
.
Assume that
H\in C^{2}[0,1]
and
H(0)=H^{\prime}(1)=0
.
By the carré-du-champ formula, the quadratic variation of the martingale
M^{N}_{t}(H)
is given by

\begin{split}\langle M^{N}(H)\rangle_{t}\;=\;&\int_{0}^{t}\Big(\frac{\alpha}{N^{\theta}}\eta_{s}(0)+\eta_{s}(1)\Big)\Big[H(\genfrac{}{}{}{1}{1}{N})-H(\genfrac{}{}{}{1}{0}{N})\Big]^{2}ds\\
&+\int_{0}^{t}\sum_{x=1}^{N-1}\big(\eta_{s}(x)+\eta_{s}(x+1)\big)\Big[H(\genfrac{}{}{}{1}{x+1}{N})-H(\genfrac{}{}{}{1}{x}{N})\Big]^{2}ds\,.\end{split}

(4.4)

Consider the partial order
\lesssim
in
\Omega_{N}
defined by

\eta_{1}\lesssim\eta_{2}\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad\eta_{1}(x)\leq\eta_{2}(x),\,\forall\,x=0,1,\ldots,N.

(4.5)

The initial measure
\mu_{N}
defined in (
2.5
) is stochastically dominated by the product measure

\widetilde{\mu}_{N}\;:=\;\mathrm{Poisson}\Big(\frac{N^{\theta}}{\alpha}\cdot\|\gamma\|_{\infty}\Big)\otimes\bigotimes_{x=1}^{N}\mathrm{Poisson}\big(\|\gamma\|_{\infty}\big)\,,

(4.6)

which, by Proposition
2.1
, is an invariant measure. Independent random walks is an attractive system with respect to (
4.5
), so this partial order is preserved in time, see
[
29
, Chapter 2]
. Since the quadratic variation (
4.4
) is an increasing function with respect to the partial order (
4.5
), we have that

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}_{\mu_{N}}^{\theta,N}\Big[\langle M^{N}(H)\rangle_{t}\Big]

\displaystyle\;\leq\;{\mathbb{E}}_{\widetilde{\mu}_{N}}^{\theta,N}\Big[\langle M^{N}(H)\rangle_{t}\Big]

\displaystyle\;=\;{\mathbb{E}}_{\widetilde{\mu}_{N}}^{\theta,N}\bigg[\int_{0}^{t}\Big(\frac{\alpha}{N^{\theta}}\eta_{s}(0)+\eta_{s}(1)\Big)\Big[H(\genfrac{}{}{}{1}{1}{N})-H(\genfrac{}{}{}{1}{0}{N})\Big]^{2}ds

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+\int_{0}^{t}\sum_{x=1}^{N-1}\big(\eta_{s}(x)+\eta_{s}(x+1)\big)\Big[H(\genfrac{}{}{}{1}{x+1}{N})-H(\genfrac{}{}{}{1}{x}{N})\Big]^{2}ds\bigg]

\displaystyle\;\leq\;\frac{2t}{N}\|\partial_{u}H\|_{\infty}^{2}\cdot\|\gamma\|_{\infty}\,,

which goes to zero as
N\to\infty
in view of
H\in C^{2}[0,1]
. Since
\big(M^{N}_{t}(H)\big)^{2}-\langle M^{N}(H)\rangle_{t}
is a zero mean martingale (see for instance
[
27
, Appendix 1]
), by Doob’s inequality, for every
\delta>0
,

\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}{\mathbb{P}}_{\mu_{N}}^{\theta,N}\bigg[\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}|M^{N}_{t}(H)|>\delta\bigg]\;=\;0\,,

(4.7)

hence the sequence of martingales
\{M^{N}_{t}(H):t\in[0,T]\}_{N\geq 1}
converges in distribution to zero in the Skorokhod topology. Our goal now is to understand the limit of each term in the martingale (
4.3
) aiming to achieve in the limit the integral equation (
2.10
) for
\theta=1
, or the integral equation (
2.12
) for
\theta\in(1,\infty)
.

The definition (
2.5
) of
\mu_{N}
implies that the sequence of time constant processes
\{\langle\pi^{N}_{0},H\rangle:t\in[0,T]\}_{N\geq 1}
converges in distribution to the time-constant process
\{\langle\gamma,H\rangle:t\in[0,T]\}
. Moreover, Corollary
4.2
implies that the sequence of process

\{\langle\pi^{N}_{t},H\rangle:t\in[0,T]\}_{N\geq 1}
converges in distribution to
\{\langle\rho_{t},H\rangle:t\in[0,T]\}
.
Thus, it only remains to study the integral term in (
4.3
). Performing elementary calculations,

\displaystyle{\mathsf{L}}_{N}\langle{\pi}_{s}^{N},H\rangle\;=\;

\displaystyle N\cdot H\big(\genfrac{}{}{}{1}{1}{N}\big)\Big[\frac{\alpha}{N^{\theta}}\eta(0)-2\eta(1)+\eta(2)\Big]

\displaystyle+N\cdot\sum_{x=2}^{N-1}{H}\big(\genfrac{}{}{}{1}{x}{N}\big)\big[\eta_{s}(x-1)-2\eta_{s}(x)+\eta_{s}(x+1)\big]

\displaystyle+N\cdot H\big(\genfrac{}{}{}{1}{N}{N}\big)\big[-\eta_{s}(N)+\eta_{s}(N-1)\big]\,.

Since
H(0)=0
, this can be rewritten as

\displaystyle\frac{\alpha\eta_{s}(0)}{N^{\theta}}\cdot N\Big[H\big(\genfrac{}{}{}{1}{1}{N}\big)-H\big(\genfrac{}{}{}{1}{0}{N}\big)\Big]

(4.8)

\displaystyle+\eta_{s}(1)\cdot N\Big[H\big(\genfrac{}{}{}{1}{0}{N}\big)-2\,H\big(\genfrac{}{}{}{1}{1}{N}\big)+H\big(\genfrac{}{}{}{1}{2}{N}\big)\Big]

(4.9)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{N}\sum_{x=2}^{N-1}\eta_{s}(x)\cdot N^{2}\Big[H\big(\genfrac{}{}{}{1}{x+1}{N}\big)+H\big(\genfrac{}{}{}{1}{x-1}{N}\big)-2H\big(\genfrac{}{}{}{1}{x}{N}\big)\Big]

(4.10)

\displaystyle+\eta_{s}(N)\cdot N\Big[H\big(\genfrac{}{}{}{1}{N-1}{N}\big)-H\big(\genfrac{}{}{}{1}{N}{N}\big)\Big]\,.

(4.11)

Keep in mind that the attractiveness property discussed at (
4.5
) and (
4.6
) assures domination. Since
H^{\prime}(1)=0
, the Dominated Convergence Theorem guarantees that the integral in time from
0
to
t
of (
4.11
) converges to zero as
N\to\infty
.
By a similar argument, the sequence of processes
\{\frac{1}{N}\eta_{t}(N):t\in[0,T]\}_{N\geq 1}
converges to zero in probability. Thus, since
H\in C^{2}[0,1]
, applying the Corollary
4.2
, the integral in time of (
4.9
) plus (
4.10
) converges in distribution (thus in probability) to the constant

\int_{0}^{t}\langle\rho_{s},H^{\prime\prime}\rangle\,ds\,.

Since the Markov process
\{\eta_{t}:t\in[0,T]\}
on
\Omega_{N}
is conservative, we can rewrite the expression (
4.8
) as

\displaystyle\alpha N\Big[H\big(\genfrac{}{}{}{1}{1}{N}\big)-H\big(\genfrac{}{}{}{1}{0}{N}\big)\Big]\Bigg[\frac{1}{N^{\theta}}\sum_{x=0}^{N}\eta_{s}(x)-\frac{1}{N^{\theta}}\sum_{x=1}^{N}\eta_{s}(x)\Bigg]

\displaystyle\;=\;\alpha N\Big[H\big(\genfrac{}{}{}{1}{1}{N}\big)-H\big(\genfrac{}{}{}{1}{0}{N}\big)\Big]\Bigg[\frac{1}{N^{\theta}}\sum_{x=0}^{N}\eta_{0}(x)-\frac{1}{N^{\theta}}\sum_{x=1}^{N}\eta_{s}(x)\Bigg].

From the definition (
2.5
) of
\mu_{N}
, an application of the law of large numbers for triangular arrays (see
[
8
]
for instance) leads to

\frac{1}{N^{\theta}}\sum_{x=0}^{N}\eta_{0}(x)\;\longrightarrow\;\begin{cases}\displaystyle\frac{\gamma(0)}{\alpha}+\int_{0}^{1}\gamma(u)du\;=:\;M,&\text{ if }\theta=1,\\
\displaystyle\frac{\gamma(0)}{\alpha},&\text{ if }\theta\in(1,\infty)\\
\end{cases}

(4.12)

in probability as
N\to\infty
. From the Corollary
4.2
,
we have the following convergence (of processes)

\frac{1}{N^{\theta}}\sum_{x=1}^{N}\eta_{s}(x)=N^{1-\theta}\langle\pi^{N}_{s},1\rangle\longrightarrow\begin{cases}\langle\rho_{s},1\rangle=\int_{0}^{1}\rho_{s}(u)du,&\text{if }\theta=1,\\
0,&\text{if }\theta\in(1,\infty)\end{cases}

(4.13)

in probability as
N\to\infty
. By (
4.12
) and (
4.13
), we deduce that
(
4.8
) converges in probability to

\alpha H^{\prime}(0)\times\begin{cases}\displaystyle M-\int_{0}^{1}\rho_{s}(u)du,&\text{ if }\theta=1,\\
\displaystyle\frac{\gamma(0)}{\alpha},&\text{ if }\theta\in(1,\infty).\end{cases}

(4.14)

Putting together the convergences of (
4.8
), (
4.9
), (
4.10
) and (
4.11
),
we get

\int_{0}^{t}\!{\mathsf{L}}_{N}\langle{\pi}_{s}^{N},H\rangle ds\to\int_{0}^{t}\!\langle\rho_{s},H^{\prime\prime}\rangle ds+\alpha H^{\prime}(0)\!\!\int_{0}^{t}\!\!ds\begin{cases}M-\int_{0}^{1}\rho_{s}(u)du,&\text{if }\theta=1\\
\frac{\gamma(0)}{\alpha},&\text{if }\theta\in(1,\infty)\\
\end{cases}

in probability. We have therefore obtained that, for any
H\in C^{2}[0,1]
such that
H(0)=H^{\prime}(1)=0
, if
\theta=1
, it holds

\big\langle\rho_{t},H\big\rangle-\big\langle\gamma,H\big\rangle\;=\;\int_{0}^{t}\big\langle\rho_{s},\partial^{2}_{uu}H\big\rangle\,ds+\int_{0}^{t}\alpha\bigg(M-\int_{0}^{1}\rho_{s}(u)du\bigg)H^{\prime}(0)\,ds\,,

and, if
\theta\in(1,\infty)
, it holds

\big\langle\rho_{t},H\big\rangle-\big\langle\gamma,H\big\rangle\;=\;\int_{0}^{t}\big\langle\rho_{s},\partial^{2}_{uu}H\big\rangle\,ds+\int_{0}^{t}\gamma(0)H^{\prime}(0)\,ds

These are, respectively, the integral equations (
2.10
) and (
2.12
). This result, together with the uniqueness of weak solutions to be proved in the next subsection, characterizes
\rho(t,u)
for
\theta=1
or
\theta\in(1,\infty)
as the unique weak solution to (
2.9
) or to (
2.11
), respectively, and henceforth concludes the proof of Theorem
2.7
.

4.4.
Uniqueness of weak solutions

In this subsection we prove uniqueness of weak solutions to (
2.9
) and (
2.11
).
Let
\Psi_{k}:[0,1]\to{\mathbb{R}}
be given by

\Psi_{k}(u)\;:=\;\sqrt{2}\sin\Big(\pi\big(k+\genfrac{}{}{}{1}{1}{2}\big)u\Big),\qquad k=0,1,2,\ldots

(4.15)

These functions are the solutions of the following Sturm-Liouville problem associated to the Laplacian operator with Dirichlet and Neumann boundary conditions

\begin{cases}-f^{\prime\prime}(u)=\lambda f(u),&\text{ for }u\in(0,1),\\
f(0)=f^{\prime}(1)=0,\end{cases}

(4.16)

having
\lambda_{k}=\pi^{2}\big(k+\genfrac{}{}{}{1}{1}{2}\big)^{2}
as the
k^{\text{th}}
-eigenvalue. Moreover, the set
\{\Psi_{k}\}_{k\geq 0}
is an orthonormal complete basis of
L^{2}[0,1]
, see
[
33
]
, for instance, on the subject.
We start below showing uniqueness of weak solutions to (
2.9
).

Proof of Proposition
2.4
.

Let
\rho^{1}
and
\rho^{2}
be weak solutions of (
2.9
). For
\xi=\rho^{1}-\rho^{2}
, it holds

\langle\xi_{t},H\rangle\;=\;\int_{0}^{t}\langle\xi_{s},H^{\prime\prime}\rangle\,ds-\alpha H^{\prime}(0)\int_{0}^{t}\langle\xi_{s},1\rangle\,ds\,,

for any
H\in C^{2}[0,1]
such that
H(0)=H^{\prime}(1)=0
. In particular,

\langle\xi_{t},\Psi_{k}\rangle\;=\;-\lambda_{k}\int_{0}^{t}\langle\xi_{s},\Psi_{k}\rangle\,ds-\alpha\Psi^{\prime}_{k}(0)\int_{0}^{t}\langle\xi_{s},1\rangle\,ds\,.

(4.17)

Consider the energy functional

{\mathcal{E}}(t)\;=\;\sum_{k\geq 0}\frac{\langle\xi_{t},\Psi_{k}\rangle^{2}}{2\lambda_{k}}\,,

(4.18)

which by (
4.17
) satisfies

{\mathcal{E}}^{\prime}(t)\;=\;-\sum_{k\geq 0}\Big[\langle\xi_{t},\Psi_{k}\rangle^{2}+\frac{\alpha\Psi_{k}^{\prime}(0)}{\lambda_{k}}\langle\xi_{t},\Psi_{k}\rangle\langle\xi_{t},1\rangle\Big].

(4.19)

The fact that
\{\Psi_{k}\}_{\ell\geq 0}
is an orthonormal complete basis of
L^{2}[0,1]
implies that
1=\sum_{\ell\geq 0}\langle 1,\Psi_{\ell}\rangle\Psi_{\ell}
, and a simple calculation gives us that

\langle 1,\Psi_{\ell}\rangle\;=\;\frac{\sqrt{2}}{\pi(\ell+\frac{1}{2})}\;=\;\frac{2}{\Psi_{\ell}^{\prime}(0)}\,,

hence

1\;=\;\sum_{\ell\geq 0}\frac{2}{\Psi_{\ell}^{\prime}(0)}\Psi_{\ell}\,.

(4.20)

Applying (
4.20
) into (
4.19
) yields

\displaystyle{\mathcal{E}}^{\prime}(t)

\displaystyle\;=\;-\sum_{k\geq 0}\Big[\langle\xi_{t},\Psi_{k}\rangle^{2}+\frac{\alpha\Psi_{k}^{\prime}(0)}{\lambda_{k}}\langle\xi_{t},\Psi_{k}\rangle\sum_{\ell\geq 0}\frac{2}{\Psi_{\ell}^{\prime}(0)}\langle\xi_{t},\Psi_{\ell}\rangle\Big]

\displaystyle\;=\;-\sum_{k\geq 0}\langle\xi_{t},\Psi_{k}\rangle^{2}-\alpha\bigg(\sum_{k\geq 0}\frac{\Psi_{k}^{\prime}(0)}{\lambda_{k}}\langle\xi_{t},\Psi_{k}\rangle\bigg)\bigg(\sum_{\ell\geq 0}\frac{2}{\Psi_{\ell}^{\prime}(0)}\langle\xi_{t},\Psi_{\ell}\rangle\bigg).

Since

\frac{2}{\Psi_{\ell}^{\prime}(0)}\;=\;\frac{\Psi_{\ell}^{\prime}(0)}{\lambda_{\ell}}\,,

(4.21)

we deduce that

\displaystyle{\mathcal{E}}^{\prime}(t)

\displaystyle\;=\;-\|\xi_{t}\|_{2}^{2}-\alpha\bigg(\sum_{k\geq 0}\frac{\Psi_{k}^{\prime}(0)}{\lambda_{k}}\langle\xi_{t},\Psi_{k}\rangle\bigg)^{2}

(4.22)

\displaystyle\;=\;-\|\xi_{t}\|_{2}^{2}-\alpha\bigg\langle\xi_{t},\sum_{k\geq 0}\frac{\Psi_{k}^{\prime}(0)}{\lambda_{k}}\Psi_{k}\bigg\rangle^{2}

(4.23)

\displaystyle\;=\;-\|\xi_{t}\|_{2}^{2}-\alpha\langle\xi_{t},1\rangle^{2}\leq 0\,.

(4.24)

Thus,
{\mathcal{E}}(t)=0
for all
t\geq 0
, which in its turn implies that
\langle\xi_{t},\Psi_{k}\rangle=0
for all
t\geq 0
.
Since the set
\{\Psi_{k}\}_{k\geq 0}
is an orthonormal complete basis of
L^{2}[0,1]
, by Parseval’s formula we have that
\|\xi_{t}\|_{2}=0
for all
t\geq 0
, ensuring the uniqueness of weak solutions
of (
2.9
).
∎

Remark 4.3
.

At first sight, it seems that (
4.22
) was already enough to conclude the proof, whose right-hand side is non-positive. However, it was necessary to arrive at (
4.24
) to assure that the series (
4.19
) is convergent.

Proof of Proposition
2.6
.

Let
\rho^{1}
and
\rho^{2}
be weak solutions of (
2.11
). For
\xi=\rho^{1}-\rho^{2}
, it holds

\langle\xi_{t},H\rangle\;=\;\int_{0}^{t}\langle\xi_{s},H^{\prime\prime}\rangle\,ds\,.

(4.25)

As before, consider the energy functional
{\mathcal{E}}(t)=\sum_{k\geq 0}\frac{\langle\xi_{t},\Psi_{k}\rangle^{2}}{2\lambda_{k}}
,
which satisfies
{\mathcal{E}}(0)=0
and
{\mathcal{E}}(t)\geq 0
for all
t>0
. By (
4.25
) and the fact that
\Psi_{k}
are the solutions of the Sturm-Liouville problem (
4.16
), we deduce that

{\mathcal{E}}^{\prime}(t)\;=\;-\sum_{k\geq 0}\langle\xi_{t},\Psi_{k}\rangle^{2}\;\leq\;0\,.

leading to the uniqueness of weak solutions to (
2.11
).
∎

5.
Proof of hydrodynamics for exclusion process

5.1.
Scaling limit

Let
\{\pi^{N}_{t}:t\in[0,T]\}
be the
empirical measure
defined by

\pi^{N}_{t}\;=\;\frac{1}{N}\sum_{x=0}^{N}\eta_{t}(x)\delta_{\frac{x}{N}}\,,

(5.1)

which characterizes the spatial density of the particles of the process as embedded in the interval
[0,1]
.
The empirical measure is a random element in the Skorokhod space
D([0,T];{\mathcal{M}})
of càdlàg trajectories, where
{\mathcal{M}}
is the space of non-negative measures on
[0,1]
.

Unlike what we did in Subsection
4.2
, the definition above of the empirical measure
includes
the site
x=0
. Nevertheless, the mass at
x=0
(the reservoir) does not play any role in the limit of (
5.1
), as we shall see. For
\theta=1
and
\theta\in(1,\infty)
, this is due to fact that test functions are assumed to satisfy the boundary condition
H(0)=0
. And for
\theta\in[0,1)
, this is due to fact that the number of particles in the reservoir is of order
N^{\theta}\ll N
.
On the other hand, although the zero site does not contribute to the limit of (
5.1
), such a definition is essential in our calculations, helping to prove the tightness and to characterize the limit.

Let
\mu_{N}
be as defined in (
2.24
) in terms of a continuous profile
\gamma:[0,1]\to[0,1]
. Let
{\mathbb{Q}}_{\mu_{N}}^{\theta,N}
be the measure on the path space
D([0,T];{\mathcal{M}})
induced by the initial measure
\mu_{N}
and
the empirical measure
\pi^{N}_{t}
introduced in (
5.1
) above.

Definition 5.1
.

Let
{\mathbb{Q}}^{\theta}
be
the probability measure on
D([0,T];{\mathcal{M}})
concentrated on the
deterministic path
\pi(t,du)=\rho(t,u)du
, where
\rho(t,u)
is:

•

If
\theta\in[0,1)
, the unique weak solution to the heat equation (
2.18
).

•

If
\theta=1
, the unique weak solution to the heat equation (
2.20
).

•

If
\theta\in(1,\infty)
, the unique weak solution to the heat equation (
2.22
).

Uniqueness of weak solutions is postponed to Subsection
5.6
. The Theorem
2.19
is an immediate consequence of the next proposition.

Proposition 5.2
.

As
N\uparrow\infty
, the sequence of probability measures
\{{\mathbb{Q}}_{\mu_{N}}^{\theta,N}:N\geq{1}\}
converges weakly to
{\mathbb{Q}}^{\theta}
.

Let us prove the proposition above subject to forthcoming results.

Proof of Proposition
5.2
.

In Subsection
5.4
,
we show that, for any
\theta\in[0,\infty)
, the sequence
\{{\mathbb{Q}}_{\mu_{N}}^{\theta,N}\}_{N\geq 1}
is tight, thus relatively compact by Prokhorov’s Theorem. In Subsection
5.5
we show that, if
{\mathbb{Q}}_{*}^{\theta}
is the limit along a subsequence of
\{{\mathbb{Q}}_{\mu_{N}}^{\theta,N}\}_{N\geq 1}
, then
{\mathbb{Q}}_{*}^{\theta}
is concentrated on trajetories
\rho(t,u)du
such that
\rho(t,u)
is a weak solution to the corresponding PDE according to the range of
\theta
. Uniqueness of weak solutions presented in Subsection
5.6
implies that
{\mathbb{Q}}_{*}^{\theta}={\mathbb{Q}}^{\theta}
, concluding the proof.
∎

We start with Subsections
5.2
and
5.3
, which contain some technical results needed in the sequel.

5.2.
Reversible measure and entropy

Let us prove Proposition
2.12
, which says that the measure

\nu_{p}\;=\;\mathrm{Poisson}\Big(\frac{N^{\theta}}{\alpha}\cdot\frac{p}{1-p}\Big)\otimes\bigotimes_{x=1}^{N}\mathrm{Bernoulli}(p)

for
0<p<1
is reversible for the Markov process defined by the generator (
2.14
).

Proof of Proposition
2.12
.

It must be shown that for every
g,f\in L_{\nu_{p}}^{2}(\Omega_{N})
,

\int g{\mathsf{L}}_{N}f\,d\nu_{p}\;=\;\int f{\mathsf{L}}_{N}g\,d\nu_{p}\,,

see the Appendix of
[
27
]
for instance. It suffices to verify the condition for the bond
\{0,1\}
since the dynamics over the remaining bonds follows the symmetric exclusion part of the dynamics, for which the product of Bernoulli measure of constant parameter is known to be reversible, see
[
27
]
.

For the jump cross the bond
\{0,1\}
, fix a configuration
\eta
with
\eta(0)\geq 1
and
\eta(1)=0
, and denote by
\xi
the configuration obtained from
\eta
by moving a particle from
0
to
1
, if possible. That is,

\xi:=\eta^{0,1}
as in (
2.16
). Let
\lambda:=\frac{N^{\theta}}{\alpha}\cdot\frac{p}{1-p}
. Since
\xi(0)=\eta(0)-1
and
\xi(1)=1
in the set
A=\{\eta:\eta(0)\geq 1\}
, the Radon-Nikodym derivative in the set
A
is given by

\frac{\nu_{p}(\eta)}{\nu_{p}(\xi)}\;=\;\frac{\frac{e^{-\lambda}\lambda^{\xi(0)+1}}{(\xi(0)+1)!}\cdot(1-p)}{\frac{e^{-\lambda}\lambda^{\xi(0)}}{\xi(0)!}\cdot p}\;=\;\frac{\lambda}{\xi(0)+1}\cdot\frac{1-p}{p}\;=\;\frac{N^{\theta}}{\alpha}\cdot\frac{1}{\xi(0)+1}\,.

Keeping in mind that the integrand in the first integral below vanishes outside the set
A
, we consequently have that

\displaystyle\int\frac{\alpha}{N^{\theta}}\eta(0)f(\eta)(1-\eta(1))\Big[g(\eta^{0,1})-g(\eta)\Big]d\nu_{p}(\eta)

\displaystyle\;=\;\sum_{k\geq 1}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\eta\in\Omega_{N}:\\
\eta(0)=k\end{subarray}}f(\eta)\frac{\alpha}{N^{\theta}}\eta(0)(1-\eta(1))\Big[g(\eta^{0,1})-g(\eta)\Big]\nu_{p}(\eta)

\displaystyle\;=\;\sum_{k\geq 1}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\xi\in\Omega_{N}:\\
\xi(0)=k-1\end{subarray}}f(\xi^{1,0})\frac{\alpha}{N^{\theta}}(\xi(0)+1)\xi(1)\Big[g(\xi)-g(\xi^{1,0})\Big]\frac{\nu_{p}(\eta)}{\nu_{p}(\xi)}\nu_{p}(\xi)

\displaystyle\;=\;\sum_{k\geq 1}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\xi\in\Omega_{N}:\\
\xi(0)=k-1\end{subarray}}f(\xi^{1,0})\frac{\alpha}{N^{\theta}}(\xi(0)+1)\xi(1)\Big[g(\xi)-g(\xi^{1,0})\Big]\frac{N^{\theta}}{\alpha(\xi(0)+1)}\nu_{p}(\xi)

\displaystyle\;=\;\sum_{k\geq 0}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\xi\in\Omega_{N}:\\
\xi(0)=k\end{subarray}}f(\xi^{1,0})\xi(1)\Big[g(\xi)-g(\xi^{1,0})\Big]\nu_{p}(\xi)

\displaystyle\;=\;\int f(\xi^{1,0})\xi(1)\Big[g(\xi)-g(\xi^{1,0})\Big]d\nu_{p}(\xi)\,.

With the change of variables above at hand, we can check the detailed balance condition related to the
\{0,1\}
bond:

\displaystyle\int f(\eta)\frac{\alpha}{N^{\theta}}\eta(0)(1-\eta(1))\big[g(\eta^{0,1})-g(\eta)\big]d\nu_{p}+\int f(\eta)\eta(1)\big[g(\eta^{1,0})-g(\eta)\big]d\nu_{p}

\displaystyle-\int g(\eta)\frac{\alpha}{N^{\theta}}\eta(0)(1\!-\!\eta(1))\big[f(\eta^{0,1})\!-\!f(\eta)\big]d\nu_{p}-\int g(\eta)\eta(1)\big[f(\eta^{1,0})\!-\!f(\eta)\big]d\nu_{p}

\displaystyle=\int f(\eta^{1,0})\eta(1)\big[g(\eta)-g(\eta^{1,0})\big]d\nu_{p}+\int f(\eta)\eta(1)\big[g(\eta^{1,0})-g(\eta)\big]d\nu_{p}

\displaystyle-\int g(\eta^{1,0})\eta(1)\big[f(\eta)-f(\eta^{1,0})\big]d\nu_{p}-\int g(\eta)\eta(1)\big[f(\eta^{1,0})-f(\eta)\big]d\nu_{p}\;=\;0\,,

finishing the proof.
∎

Let
\mathbf{H}(\mu|\nu_{p})=\sum_{\eta\in\Omega_{N}}\mu(\eta)\log\genfrac{}{}{}{1}{\mu(\eta)}{\nu_p(\eta)}
be the relative entropy of a probability measure
\mu
with respect to the invariant state
\nu_{p}
.

Proposition 5.3
.

Recall (
2.24
) and let
0<p<1
be such that
\frac{p}{1-p}=\gamma(\genfrac{}{}{}{1}{0}{N})
.
Then, there exists a finite constant
K
such that

\mathbf{H}(\mu_{N}|\nu_{p})\;\leq\;KN\,.

(5.2)

Proof.

Note that
\nu_{p}
and
\mu_{N}
are product measures, and its marginals at the site
x=0
have Poisson
distribution with the same parameter. Thus,

\displaystyle\mathbf{H}(\mu_{N}|\nu_{p})

\displaystyle\;=\;\sum_{\eta\in\Omega_{N}}\mu(\eta)\log\bigg[\frac{\prod_{x=1}^{N}\big[\gamma(\genfrac{}{}{}{1}{x}{N})\eta(x)+(1-\gamma(\genfrac{}{}{}{1}{x}{N}))\big(1-\eta(x)\big)\big]}{\prod_{x=1}^{N}\big[p\eta(x)+(1-p)\big(1-\eta(x)\big)\big]}\bigg]

\displaystyle\;\leq\;\sum_{\eta\in\Omega_{N}}\mu(\eta)\log\bigg[\frac{1}{\big(p\wedge(1-p)\big)^{N}}\bigg]\;=\;N\log\frac{1}{\big(p\wedge(1-p)\big)}\,.

∎

5.3.
Replacement lemma and energy estimate

For the regime
\theta=1
, two technical ingredients are needed: first, a replacement lemma that allows to substitute in
L^{1}
the time integral of the occupation at the site
x=1
by the time integral of the average occupation at the sites
x=1,\ldots,\varepsilon N
. Here, by
\varepsilon N
we mean
\lfloor\varepsilon N\rfloor
. Second, an energy estimate that allows to conclude that any limit of
{\mathbb{Q}}_{\mu_{N}}^{\theta,N}
along some subsequence
is concentrated in trajectories whose density with respect to the Lebesgue measure belongs to the Sobolev space
L^{2}([0,1];{\mathcal{H}}^{1}(0,1))
. These two technical facts are standard for the symmetric simple exclusion
processes, and they can be promptly adapted from
[
18
]
for example. In what follows, we precisely state these two results. Afterwards, we explain the required hypothesis and why they are satisfied for the present model.
Define the empirical average by

\eta^{\varepsilon N}(x)\;=\;\genfrac{}{}{}{1}{1}{\eps N}\sum_{y=x+1}^{x+\varepsilon N}\eta(y)\,.

Lemma 5.4

(Local Replacement Lemma)
.

Let
x\in\{1,\ldots,(1-\varepsilon)N\}
. Then, for any
\theta\geq 0
,

\limsup_{\varepsilon\to 0}\limsup_{N\to\infty}{\mathbb{E}}_{\mu_{N}}^{\theta,N}\bigg[\,\Big|\int_{0}^{t}\{\eta_{s}(x)-\eta_{s}^{\varepsilon N}(x)\}\,ds\Big|\,\bigg]\;=\;0\,.

Proposition 5.5

(Energy Estimate)
.

Let
{\mathbb{Q}}_{*}^{\theta}
be a weak limit of
{\mathbb{Q}}_{\mu_{N}}^{\theta,N}
along some subsequence. Then
{\mathbb{Q}}_{*}^{\theta}
is concentrated on paths
\pi(t,u)=\rho(t,u)du
such that there exists a function in
L^{2}([0,T]\times[0,1])
, denoted by
\partial_{u}\rho
, such that

\int_{0}^{T}\int_{[0,1]}\,(\partial_{u}G)(s,u)\,\rho(s,u)\,du\,ds\;=\;-\;\int_{0}^{T}\int_{[0,1]}\,G(s,u)\,(\partial_{u}\rho)(s,u)\,du\,ds\,,

for all
G
in
C^{0,1}([0,T]\times[0,1])
with compact support contained in

[0,T]\times(0,1)
.

Below we explain the necessary hypothesis to adapt the proofs of two results above (from
[
18
]
for example) and why they are satisfied here:

•

The dynamics on the sites
x=1,\ldots,N
must be of symmetric simple exclusion type, which is the case.

•

The marginal of the reversible distribution at the sites
x=1,\ldots,N
must be product of Bernoulli measures of constant parameter, which is provided by Proposition
2.12
.

•

The entropy between the distribution of the process at initial time and the reversible invariant state must grow at most linearly, that is, we must have
\mathbf{H}(\mu_{N}|\nu_{p})\leq KN
, which is the content of Proposition
5.3
.

5.4.
Tightness

To assure tightness of
\{\pi^{N}_{t}:0\leq t\leq T\}
it is sufficient to
show tightness of the real-valued processes
\{\langle\pi^{N}_{t},H\rangle:0\leq t\leq T\}
for
H\in{C[0,1]}
, c.f.
[
27
, Chapter 2, Prop. 1.7]
. More than that, it is enough to show tightness of
\{\langle\pi^{N}_{t},H\rangle:0\leq t\leq T\}
for a dense set of functions in
C[0,1]
with respect to the uniform topology, since
(C[0,1],\|\cdot\|_{\infty})
is a separable metric space. For any function
H
, the process

M^{N}_{t}(H)\;:=\;\langle\pi^{N}_{t},H\rangle-\langle\pi^{N}_{0},H\rangle-\int_{0}^{t}{\mathsf{L}}_{N}\langle\pi^{N}_{s},H\rangle\,ds

(5.3)

is a martingale with respect to the natural filtration
\mathcal{F}_{t}:=\sigma(\eta_{s}:s\leq{t})
. In order to prove tightness of
\{\langle\pi^{N}_{t},H\rangle:t\in[0,T]\}_{N\geq 1}
, we prove tightness
of the sequence of the martingales and the integral terms in the decomposition above. We start by the former.
By the carré-du-champ formula, the quadratic variation of the martingale
M^{N}_{t}(H)
is given by

\begin{split}\langle M^{N}(H)\rangle_{t}\;=\;&\int_{0}^{t}\Big(\frac{\alpha}{N^{\theta}}\eta_{s}(0)\big(1-\eta_{s}(1)\big)+\eta_{s}(1)\Big)\Big[H(\genfrac{}{}{}{1}{1}{N})-H(\genfrac{}{}{}{1}{0}{N})\Big]^{2}ds\\
&+\int_{0}^{t}\sum_{x=1}^{N-1}\big(\eta_{s}(x)-\eta_{s}(x+1)\big)^{2}\Big[H(\genfrac{}{}{}{1}{x+1}{N})-H(\genfrac{}{}{}{1}{x}{N})\Big]^{2}ds\,.\end{split}

(5.4)

Assume that
H\in C^{2}[0,1]
. Similarly to what we have done before in Subsection
4.3
, consider the partial order in
\Omega_{N}
defined by

\eta_{1}\lesssim\eta_{2}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\eta_{1}(x)\leq\eta_{2}(x),\,\forall\,x=0,1,\ldots,N.

(5.5)

The initial measure
\mu_{N}
defined in (
2.24
) is stochastically dominated by the product measure

\widetilde{\mu}_{N}\;:=\;\mathrm{Poisson}\Big(\frac{N^{\theta}}{\alpha}\cdot\|\gamma\|_{\infty}\Big)\otimes\bigotimes_{x=1}^{N}\mathrm{Bernoulli}\big(\|\gamma\|_{\infty}\big)\,,

(5.6)

which is an invariant measure by Proposition
2.12
. By the attractiveness property and the fact that
\eta(x)\in\{0,1\}
for
x=1,\ldots,N
, we have

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}_{\mu_{N}}^{\theta,N}\big[\langle M^{N}(H)\rangle_{t}\big]\;\leq\;

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}_{\widetilde{\mu}_{N}}^{\theta,N}\big[\langle M^{N}(H)\rangle_{t}\big]

\displaystyle\;\leq\;

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}_{\widetilde{\mu}_{N}}^{\theta,N}\bigg[\int_{0}^{t}\Big(\frac{\alpha}{N^{\theta}}\eta_{s}(0)+1\Big)\Big[H(\genfrac{}{}{}{1}{1}{N})-H(\genfrac{}{}{}{1}{0}{N})\Big]^{2}ds\bigg]

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{t}\sum_{x=1}^{N-1}\Big[H(\genfrac{}{}{}{1}{x+1}{N})-H(\genfrac{}{}{}{1}{x}{N})\Big]^{2}ds

\displaystyle\;\leq\;

\displaystyle\bigg[\frac{t}{N^{2}}\big(\|\gamma\|_{\infty}+1\big)+\frac{t}{N}\bigg]\cdot\|H^{\prime}\|_{\infty}^{2}\,,

which goes to zero as
N\to\infty
. Since
\big(M^{N}_{t}(H)\big)^{2}-\langle M^{N}(H)\rangle_{t}
is a zero mean martingale (see
[
27
, Appendix 1]
), by Doob’s inequality, for every
\delta>0
,

\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}{\mathbb{P}}_{\mu_{N}}^{\theta,N}\bigg[\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}|M^{N}_{t}(H)|>\delta\bigg]\;=\;0\,,

(5.7)

implying tightness of the sequence
\{M^{N}_{t}(H):t\in[0,T]\}_{N\geq 1}
.
Let us examine the tightness of the integral term
\big\{\int_{0}^{t}{\mathsf{L}}_{N}\langle\pi^{N}_{s},H\rangle\,ds:t\in[0,T]\big\}_{N\geq 1}
appearing in (
5.3
). Our goal is to apply the Aldous’ Criterion:

Theorem 5.6

(Aldous’ Criterion
[
1
]
)
.

A sequence
\{{\mathsf{X}}_{t}^{N}:t\in[0,T]\}_{N\geq 1}
of real-valued processes is tight with respect to the Skorokhod topology of
D([0,T];{\mathbb{R}})
if:

i)

\displaystyle\lim_{A\rightarrow{+\infty}}\;\limsup_{N\rightarrow{+\infty}}\;\mathbb{P}\Big(\sup_{0\leq{t}\leq{T}}|{\mathsf{X}}_{t}^{N}|>A\Big)=0,

ii)

for any
\varepsilon>0,

\displaystyle\lim_{\delta\to 0}\;\limsup_{N\to{+\infty}}\;\sup_{\zeta\leq\delta}\;\sup_{\tau\in{\mathcal{T}}_{T}}\;\mathbb{P}\Big(|{\mathsf{X}}_{\tau+\zeta}^{N}-{\mathsf{X}}_{\tau}^{N}|>\varepsilon\Big)=0,

where
{\mathcal{T}}_{T}
is the set of stopping times bounded by
T
.

Note that

\displaystyle{\mathsf{L}}_{N}\langle{\pi}_{s}^{N},{H}\rangle\;=\;

\displaystyle N\cdot H\big(\genfrac{}{}{}{1}{0}{N}\big)\Big[\eta(1)-\frac{\alpha}{N^{\theta}}\eta(0)\big(1-\eta(1)\big)\Big]

\displaystyle+N\cdot H\big(\genfrac{}{}{}{1}{1}{N}\big)\Big[\frac{\alpha}{N^{\theta}}\eta(0)\big(1-\eta(1)\big)-2\eta(1)+\eta(2)\Big]

\displaystyle+N\cdot\sum_{x=2}^{N-1}{H}\big(\genfrac{}{}{}{1}{x}{N}\big)\big[\eta_{s}(x-1)-2\eta_{s}(x)+\eta_{s}(x+1)\big]

\displaystyle+N\cdot H\big(\genfrac{}{}{}{1}{N}{N}\big)\big[-\eta_{s}(N)+\eta_{s}(N-1)\big]\,,

which can be rewritten as

\displaystyle\Big\{\frac{\alpha}{N^{\theta}}\eta_{s}(0)\big(1-\eta_{s}(1)\big)-\eta_{s}(1)\Big\}\cdot N\Big[H\big(\genfrac{}{}{}{1}{1}{N}\big)-H\big(\genfrac{}{}{}{1}{0}{N}\big)\Big]

(5.8)

\displaystyle+\eta_{s}(1)\cdot N\Big[H\big(\genfrac{}{}{}{1}{2}{N}\big)-H\big(\genfrac{}{}{}{1}{1}{N}\big)\Big]

(5.9)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{N}\sum_{x=2}^{N-1}\eta_{s}(x)\cdot N^{2}\Big[H\big(\genfrac{}{}{}{1}{x+1}{N}\big)+H\big(\genfrac{}{}{}{1}{x-1}{N}\big)-2H\big(\genfrac{}{}{}{1}{x}{N}\big)\Big]

(5.10)

\displaystyle+\eta_{s}(N)\cdot N\Big[H\big(\genfrac{}{}{}{1}{N-1}{N}\big)-H\big(\genfrac{}{}{}{1}{N}{N}\big)\Big]\,.

(5.11)

Since
\eta(x)\in\{0,1\}
for
x=1,\ldots,N
, we get

\big|{\mathsf{L}}_{N}\langle{\pi}_{s}^{N},{H}\rangle\big|\;\leq\;\Big\{3+\frac{\alpha}{N^{\theta}}\eta_{s}(0)\Big\}\cdot\|H^{\prime}\|_{\infty}+\|H^{\prime\prime}\|_{\infty}\,.

(5.12)

\bullet

Cases
\theta=1
and
\theta\in(1,\infty)
.

Since the system is mass-conservative,

\displaystyle\eta_{s}(0)\;\leq\;\sum_{x=0}^{N}\eta_{s}(x)\;=\;\sum_{x=0}^{N}\eta_{0}(x)\,.

(5.13)

From (
2.24
), by an application of the Law of Large Numbers, under
\mu_{N}
we have that

\displaystyle\frac{\alpha}{N^{\theta}}\sum_{x=0}^{N}\eta_{0}(x)\;\overset{N\to\infty}{\longrightarrow}\;\begin{cases}\gamma(0)+\alpha\int_{0}^{1}\gamma(u)du,&\text{ if }\theta=1\\
\gamma(0),&\text{ if }\theta\in(1,\infty)\\
\end{cases}

(5.14)

in
L^{1}
.
Putting together (
5.12
) and (
5.13
) allows us to deduce that

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}_{\mu_{N}}^{\theta,N}\bigg[\Big|\int_{\tau}^{\tau+\zeta}{\mathsf{L}}_{N}\langle{\pi}_{s}^{N},{H}\rangle\,ds\Big|\bigg]

\displaystyle\;\leq\;{\mathbb{E}}_{\mu_{N}}^{\theta,N}\bigg[\int_{\tau}^{\tau+\zeta}\big|{\mathsf{L}}_{N}\langle{\pi}_{s}^{N},{H}\rangle\big|ds\bigg]

\displaystyle\;\leq\;\zeta\bigg({\mathbb{E}}_{\mu_{N}}^{\theta,N}\Big[3+\frac{\alpha}{N^{\theta}}\sum_{x=0}^{N}\eta_{0}(x)\Big]\cdot\|H^{\prime}\|_{\infty}+\|H^{\prime\prime}\|_{\infty}\bigg)\,,

and the tightness of the integral term is a consequence of the inequality above, (
5.14
), Aldous’ criteria and Chebyshev’s inequality.

\bullet

Case
\theta\in[0,1)
.
Here we cannot use (
5.14
), whose limit is infinite in this case, so a different approach is needed. Recall (
5.6
). By the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, attractiveness, and invariance of
\widetilde{\mu}_{N}
, we have that

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}_{\mu_{N}}^{\theta,N}\Big[\Big(\int_{r}^{t}\frac{\alpha\eta_{s}(0)}{N^{\theta}}ds\Big)^{2}\Big]

\displaystyle\;\leq\;{\mathbb{E}}_{\mu_{N}}^{\theta,N}\Big[|t-r|\int_{r}^{t}\Big(\frac{\alpha\eta_{s}(0)}{N^{\theta}}\Big)^{2}ds\Big]

\displaystyle\;\leq\;|t-r|\,{\mathbb{E}}_{\widetilde{\mu}_{N}}^{\theta,N}\Big[\int_{r}^{t}\Big(\frac{\alpha\eta_{s}(0)}{N^{\theta}}\Big)^{2}ds\Big]\;\leq\;|t-r|^{2}\|\gamma\|_{\infty}^{2}\,.

Applying the Kolmogorov-Centsov’s tightness criteria (c.f. for instance
[
26
, Ex. 4.11, page 64]
) we obtain tightness of the time integral of (
5.8
) in the space
C([0,T];{\mathbb{R}})
, which implies tightness in the space

D([0,T];{\mathbb{R}})
. Tightness of the time integrals of (
5.9
), (
5.10
), and (
5.11
) can be managed for
\theta\geq 1
via Aldous’ criteria.

We have therefore deduced tightness of the integral term.
Due to the fact that the sum of tight sequences of processes is a tight sequence, we complete the proof of tightness of
\{\langle\pi^{N}_{t},H\rangle:t\in[0,T]\}_{N\geq 1}
.

.

11
, Appendix C4, page 629]
for an example. Let
\Psi_{\varepsilon}(u):=\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\Psi(\frac{u}{\varepsilon})
be the standard mollifier, whose support is contained in
[-\varepsilon,\varepsilon]
.

Define the test function
H_{n}:[0,1]\to{\mathbb{R}}
by

H_{n}(u)\;=\;\big(G_{n}*\Psi_{1/n^{2}}\big)(u)\,,

(5.21)

the restriction of the convolution to the interval
[0,1]
. It is straightforward to check that
H_{n}\in C^{\infty}[0,1]
,
H_{n}
coincides with
G_{n}
in the set
[0,1]\setminus[\frac{1}{n}-\frac{1}{n^{2}},\frac{1}{n}+\frac{1}{n^{2}}]
,
H_{n}(0)=H_{n}^{\prime}(1)=0
,
0\leq H_{n}\leq\frac{1}{n}
,
0\leq H_{n}^{\prime}\leq 1
and
H_{n}^{\prime}(u)=0
for
u>\frac{1}{n}+\frac{1}{n^{2}}
.

Let
\rho
be a weak solution to (
2.20
). Hence, the integral equation (
2.21
) holds for for any time
t\in[0,T]
and for any
H\in C^{2}[0,1]
such that
H(0)=H^{\prime}(1)=0
. Since
\rho\in L^{2}([0,1];{\mathcal{H}}^{1}(0,1))
, applying an integration by parts for Sobolev spaces (see
[
18
, Lemma 7.1]
for instance) and using that
H^{\prime}(1)=0
, we obtain

\displaystyle\big\langle\rho_{t},H\big\rangle-\big\langle\gamma,H\big\rangle=

\displaystyle-\int_{0}^{t}\big\langle\partial_{u}\rho_{s},H^{\prime}\big\rangle\,ds-\int_{0}^{t}\rho_{s}(0)H^{\prime}(0)\,ds

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{t}\alpha\big(1-\rho_{s}(0)\big)\bigg(M-\int_{0}^{1}\rho_{s}(u)du\bigg)H^{\prime}(0)\,ds

for any time
t\in[0,T]
and for any
H\in C^{2}[0,1]
such that
H(0)=H^{\prime}(1)=0
.
Choosing the test function as the function
H_{n}
defined in (
5.21
), taking the limit as
n\to\infty
and noting that
H_{n}
and
H_{n}^{\prime}
converge to zero in
L^{2}[0,1]
, we arrive at

\begin{split}\int_{0}^{t}\rho_{s}(0)\,ds\;=\;\int_{0}^{t}\alpha\big(1-\rho_{s}(0)\big)\bigg(M-\int_{0}^{1}\rho_{s}(u)du\bigg)\,ds\end{split}

for any time
t\in[0,T]
, concluding the proof of the lemma.
∎

Proof of Proposition
2.16
.

For ease of notation, denote

m_{s}\;:=\;M-\int_{0}^{1}\rho_{s}(u)\,du\,,

which represents the mass in the reservoir. Since weak solutions of (
2.20
) satisfy
0\leq\rho\leq 1
,
Lemma
5.7
ensures that
m_{s}\geq 0
a.e. in time.
Lemma
5.7
also implies that

\rho_{s}(0)\;=\;\frac{\alpha m_{s}}{1+\alpha m_{s}}

(5.22)

a.e. in time.
Let
\rho^{1}
and
\rho^{2}
be two weak solutions of (
2.20
) and denote
\xi=\rho^{1}-\rho^{2}\in L^{2}([0,1];{\mathcal{H}}^{1}(0,1))
, which satisfies the integral equation

\begin{split}&\big\langle\xi_{t},H\big\rangle=\int_{0}^{t}\big\langle\xi_{s},H^{\prime\prime}\big\rangle\,ds\\
&-\alpha H^{\prime}(0)\int_{0}^{t}\bigg[\Big(M-\int_{0}^{1}\rho^{1}_{s}(u)du\Big)\xi_{s}(0)+\Big(1-\rho^{2}_{s}(0)\Big)\int_{0}^{1}\xi_{s}(u)du\bigg]ds\end{split}

(5.23)

for any time
t\in[0,T]
and for any
H\in C^{2}[0,1]
such that
H(0)=H^{\prime}(1)=0
.
Denote also
m^{1}_{s}
and
m^{2}_{s}
the corresponding masses in the reservoir.
Both
\rho^{1}
and
\rho^{2}
satisfy (
5.22
), thus:

\begin{split}\xi_{s}(0)&\;=\;\frac{\alpha m^{1}_{s}}{1+\alpha m^{1}_{s}}-\frac{\alpha m^{2}_{s}}{1+\alpha m^{2}_{s}}\;=\;\frac{\alpha A_{s}}{(1+\alpha m_{s}^{1})(1+\alpha m_{s}^{2})}\end{split}

(5.24)

a.e. in time, where
A_{s}:=m_{s}^{1}-m_{s}^{2}=-\int_{0}^{1}\xi_{s}(u)du
.
Applying (
5.24
) and the equality
1-\rho^{2}_{s}(0)=\frac{1}{1+\alpha m_{s}^{2}}
, we can rewrite the expression inside the bracket of (
5.23
), to get

\displaystyle m^{1}_{s}\xi_{s}(0)-(1-\rho_{s}^{2}(0))A_{s}

\displaystyle\;=\;\frac{\alpha m_{s}^{1}A_{s}}{(1+\alpha m_{s}^{1})(1+\alpha m_{s}^{2})}-\frac{A_{s}}{1+\alpha m_{s}^{2}}

\displaystyle\;=\;\frac{-A_{s}}{(1+\alpha m_{s}^{1})(1+\alpha m_{s}^{2})}\;=\;-\Gamma_{s}A_{s}\,,

where
\Gamma_{s}:=\frac{1}{(1+\alpha m_{s}^{1})(1+\alpha m_{s}^{2})}>0
.
Therefore, equation (
5.23
) rewrites as

\big\langle\xi_{t},H\big\rangle\;=\;\int_{0}^{t}\big\langle\xi_{s},H^{\prime\prime}\big\rangle\,ds+\alpha H^{\prime}(0)\int_{0}^{t}\Gamma_{s}A_{s}\,ds\,.

(5.25)

Recall the eigenfunctions
\{\Psi_{k}\}_{k\geq 0}
defined in (
4.15
). By (
5.25
),

c_{k}^{\prime}(t)\;=\;-\lambda_{k}c_{k}(t)+\alpha\Psi^{\prime}_{k}(0)\Gamma_{t}A_{t}\,.

Considering the energy functional
{\mathcal{E}}(t):=\sum_{k\geq 0}\frac{c_{k}(t)^{2}}{2\lambda_{k}}
, we obtain that

{\mathcal{E}}^{\prime}(t)\;=\;\sum_{k\geq 0}\frac{c_{k}(t){c}_{k}^{\prime}(t)}{\lambda_{k}}\;=\;-\sum_{k\geq 0}c_{k}^{2}+\alpha\Gamma_{t}A_{t}\sum_{k\geq 0}\frac{\Psi^{\prime}_{k}(0)}{\lambda_{k}}c_{k}\,.

From (
4.20
), (
4.21
) and (
4.23
), we know that
\frac{\Psi^{\prime}_{k}(0)}{\lambda_{k}}=\frac{2}{\Psi^{\prime}_{k}(0)}
and
\sum_{k\geq 0}\frac{2}{\Psi^{\prime}_{k}(0)}c_{k}=\langle\xi_{t},1\rangle=-A_{t}
. Since
\Gamma_{t}>0
, we obtain

{\mathcal{E}}^{\prime}(t)\;=\;-\|\xi_{t}\|_{2}^{2}-\alpha\Gamma_{t}A_{t}^{2}\;\leq\;0\,,

leading the proof of uniqueness of weak solutions to (
2.20
).
∎
