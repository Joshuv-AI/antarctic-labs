Seasonal water quality index prediction in Guilin karst water system: an explainable machine learning approach
Authors: Kun Dong, Yang Huang, Jiayu Yang, Haixiang Li, Sze-Mun Lam, Jin-Chung Sin, Dunqiu Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAccurate prediction of the water quality index (WQI) in karst water systems remains challenging due to pronounced seasonal hydrological heterogeneity, dual-porosity flow regimes, and complex multi-factor pollution mechanisms. This study developed a season-stratified, explainable machine-learning framework for the Guilin karst water system (Guangxi, China), based on 208 samples from 104 stations covering the wet (June to August) and dry (December to February) seasons. A sample-adaptive feature engineering step, followed by a three-stage selection pipeline (Pearson correlation filtering, variance inflation factor (VIF) screening, and Random Forest importance ranking), reduced 16 candidate features to ten predictors per season. Ten base learners (Ridge, Lasso, ElasticNet, support vector regression (SVR), random forest (RF), gradient boosting (GBM), eXtreme gradient boosting (XGBoost), light gradient boosting machine (LightGBM), categorical boosting (CatBoost) and adaptive boosting (AdaBoost)) were trained separately for each season, and the top three per season were stacked through a regularised Ridge meta-learner. XGBoost performed best in the wet season (R2 = 0.9655, RMSE = 3.0938, MAE = 2.6754); the Stacking ensemble performed best in the dry season (R2 = 0.9785, RMSE = 2.9142, MAE = 2.4278), beating every individual learner. regression receiver operating characteristic (RROC) analysis confirmed these rankings via the Area Over the Curve (|AOC|) and exposed a cross-seasonal inversion: ensemble models improved from wet to dry season (|AOC| ratio 0.45–0.54), while linear baselines worsened by a factor of 4.1 to 4.3, supporting season-specific model selection. SHAP analysis showed a clear seasonal shift in the dominant drivers. NH3–N controlled wet-season WQI (53.47% importance); in the dry season, control was shared by NH3–N (26.00%), TN (25.09%), DO (21.29%) and the pollution-load index (20.19%), and total phosphorus dropped from 7.73 to 0.06%, reflecting carbonate-mediated sequestration specific to karst. Introduction

Karst water is an essential freshwater resource that supports human production and daily life. Karst regions cover approximately 15% of the world’s unfrozen land area1. Globally, about 678 million people rely on karst water for drinking and irrigation. In Southwest China, over 70% of towns and villages primarily depend on karst water as their main drinking water source2,3. Consequently, karst water serves as a vital water source in sustaining human survival, agricultural production, and ecological balance, particularly in arid and water-scarce regions4. However, anthropogenic pollutants have significantly degraded karst water quality worldwide, attracting increasing scientific and public attention5. Contaminated karst water bodies not only endanger human health but may also lead to agricultural losses, environmental degradation, and economic burdens. Therefore, continuous monitoring and precise assessment of karst water quality is essential for mitigating environmental risks and ensuring sustainable water resources management. Water quality assessment was initially developed to evaluate the suitability of water for human consumption, and typically involves various techniques to analyze data from different locations and samples. Surface water assessment typically entails indicators such as DO, pH, TN, TP, CODCr, and NH3-N, followed by processing these data to derive a Water Quality Index (WQI)6,7.

The WQI serves as a simplified tool that integrates multiple physical, chemical, and biological parameters into a single quantifiable index with assigned weights. This approach enables data visualization, interpretation, and comparative analysis of water quality across different spatiotemporal conditions8. Based on predefined thresholds, WQI values classify water bodies into different quality categories, enabling researchers and policymakers to identify pollution hotspots, optimize remediation priorities, and develop targeted management strategies9. Nevertheless, water quality is influenced by multiple factors including weather conditions, land use patterns, and seasonal variations, resulting in a complex formation mechanism that poses significant challenges for predicting the WQI10,11.

Although numerous indicators are used to characterize water quality, this study specifically focuses on predicting the WQI in karst water systems and their surrounding major rivers and lakes. Such water bodies typically receive direct urban domestic and industrial discharges, and are subject to intensive human disturbances. Consequently, their water quality status is closely linked to urban ecological security and public health. Traditionally, assessing these parameters in karst water systems requires measuring multiple physicochemical parameters through extensive field sampling and laboratory analysis, which is both time-consuming and resource-intensive. Meanwhile, traditional WQI calculations rely on direct physicochemical and microbiological measurements but lack predictive capabilities for unmonitored locations12.

In recent years, machine learning techniques have emerged as powerful tools for water quality prediction due to their strong ability to capture nonlinear relationships and complex interactions among environmental variables13. By analyzing historical monitoring data, machine learning models can automatically select relevant features, reduce data dimensionality, and uncover hidden relationships between water quality parameters and WQI14. Consequently, they enable precise WQI prediction based on existing information15,16. Over the past decades, extensive academic research has been conducted on water quality prediction and management for watershed systems to address their inherent complexity17,18. These studies consistently emphasize that developing precise assessment tools is crucial for supporting regional water resource management decisions and establishing effective early warning systems. Karst water systems are characterized by strong nonlinearity, non-stationarity, and high spatial heterogeneity. Water quality data in these environments are influenced by multiple factors simultaneously, including complex hydrogeological structures, variable flow conditions, and intense anthropogenic disturbances. These characteristics complicate data processing, model development, and interpretation.

Artificial intelligence models have demonstrated prominent theoretical and practical value in water quality simulation and modeling19–21. Numerous machine learning models have been employed for WQI prediction. Niazkar et al. found that Gaussian process regression (GPR) achieved optimal WQI prediction performance among 11 stacked methods22. Chen et al. compared 10 learning models using large-scale Chinese river and lake data, finding that Decision Tree (DT), RF, and Deep Cascade Forest (DCF) significantly improved prediction accuracy across six water quality grades23. Further studies confirm strong predictive performance: Support Vector Regression (SVM) achieved superior correlation and error metric for WQI prediction23, RF and SVM yielded correlation coefficients exceeding 0.97 across diverse environments24,25, and Artificial Neural Network (ANN) with RF demonstrated best performance among 14 ML methods26,27.

Building on this foundation, recent years have witnessed the rapid emergence of hybrid machine-learning architectures engineered for water-resource prediction. Weighted ensemble frameworks coupled with linear-programming-based weight optimization have been developed to enhance streamflow prediction28,Kalman-filter-driven hybrid models with feature analysis under uncertainty have been applied to the water-quality and ecosystem nexus29, stacked-hybridization strategies combining ANN with multi-stage preprocessing have been used for WQI prediction in riverine karsts30,and stacking ensemble regressors with SHAP-based explanation have been demonstrated on large-scale Indian river datasets31. Although mechanistic models such as SWAT and its enhanced variants are widely used to simulate hydrological processes and pollutant transport, their use in karst environments is often limited by parameter-calibration difficulties and high observational-data demand17,18.

Despite these advances, three interrelated gaps persist in the karst water quality prediction literature. First, ensemble methods particularly stacking, which integrates complementary base learners through a meta-learner remain underutilized relative to single-model approaches; no comprehensive study has yet evaluated stacking strategies tailored specifically to karst WQI estimation32–35, although stacking has demonstrated robust gains in variance reduction and predictive stability in adjacent fields22,36, Li, Ma et al. 2025 30. Second, although machine learning has been widely applied to non-karst water modeling and groundwater quality assessment14, its deployment in karst surface water environments characterized by dual-pore structures, pronounced seasonal hydrological variability, and rapid pollutant transport remains limited37. Existing karst-related studies have primarily focused on groundwater vulnerability or single-season snapshots, without addressing the seasonal regime shifts that govern surface water quality dynamics in karst systems. Third, where machine learning has been applied to karst or seasonally variable water quality, model interpretability has rarely been treated systematically,most studies report feature importance rankings without quantifying how driving mechanisms shift across hydrological seasons.

Guilin’s karst water system presents a compelling case study. Comprising four major rivers and associated wetlands arranged around the urban center, it serves critical water supply, ecological, and landscape functions. Accelerated urbanization has imposed mounting pressures: agricultural nonpoint nitrogen and phosphorus pollution in upper reaches, point source degradation in urban river sections, and severe nitrogen contamination in shallow groundwater during dry seasons. The system’s karst hydrology produces pronounced seasonal differentiation conduit flow can contribute up to 11.40% of total discharge during wet seasons, rapidly transporting over 90% of nitrate nitrogen load within the first two months, while dry seasons are characterized by flow attenuation and pollutant accumulation38,39. Yet no study has applied machine learning to evaluate WQI in karst systems using dry and wet seasons as temporal boundaries.

To address the knowledge gaps identified above, this study establishes a seasonally differentiated, interpretable machine-learning framework for WQI prediction in the Guilin karst water system. Within this overall objective, three research questions are posed and three corresponding hypotheses are tested. The three research questions are: Do machine-learning models with different structural assumptions exhibit season-dependent predictive performance under the pronounced hydrological heterogeneity of karst environments? Does the dominant driving mechanism of WQI variation undergo a systematic transition between wet and dry seasons? Can a Stacking ensemble effectively integrate the complementary strengths of heterogeneous base learners to enhance prediction stability under small-sample conditions? Three testable hypotheses are correspondingly formulated: the optimal algorithm differs between seasons because of distinct pollutant-input regimes and hydrodynamic characteristics; wet-season WQI is dominated by single-factor pulse inputs driven by rainfall-runoff, whereas dry-season WQI is governed by multi-factor synergistic constraints linked to flow attenuation; and a two-layer Stacking ensemble outperforms individual base learners through adaptive integration of complementary predictive advantages. To test these hypotheses, ten base algorithms are deliberately selected to span three methodological paradigms and to provide a fair comparative basis across model-complexity levels: regularized linear models (Ridge, Lasso, ElasticNet) for benchmarking linear assumptions, a kernel-based model (SVR) for capturing moderate non-linearity, and tree-based ensemble models (RF, GBM, AdaBoost, XGBoost, LightGBM, CatBoost) for modeling high-order interactions. For each season independently, the top three cross-validation performers are then integrated as Level-1 base learners into a two-layer Stacking meta-model with a strongly regularized Ridge regressor serving as the Level-2 meta-learner, so that the Stacking architecture exploits the complementary predictive strengths of season-specific base learners rather than enforcing a fixed combination across the annual cycle.

The novelty of this work is positioned on three interconnected but conceptually distinct levels. At the application level, every individual component used here, Ridge, Lasso, ElasticNet, SVR, RF, GBM, AdaBoost, XGBoost, LightGBM, CatBoost, two-layer Stacking, and SHAP, is an established technique; what is new is the specific integrated workflow that couples seasonal stratification, sample-adaptive feature engineering, a three-stage progressive feature-selection pipeline (Pearson correlation filtering, VIF multicollinearity diagnostics, Random Forest importance ranking), Stacking-ensemble construction with season-specific base-learner re-selection, and SHAP-based mechanistic decomposition, deployed for the first time on a karst surface-water WQI prediction task in the Guilin karst system. To our knowledge, this exact combination has not been reported in the karst water-quality literature, and it differs substantively from the single-temporal-block paradigm of prior data-driven WQI studies14, Najafzadeh et al. 2021) by re-identifying the optimal algorithm separately for wet and dry seasons rather than fixing it across the annual cycle. At the methodological level, this study introduces Regression Receiver Operating Characteristic (RROC) signal analysis into karst water-quality machine learning for the first time. RROC characterizes each model through the magnitude of the integrated Area Over the Curve (|AOC|) and resolves two diagnostic patterns that conventional scalar metrics obscure: a clear algorithmic regime boundary separating SVR from the linear baselines, and a cross-seasonal inversion in which ensemble methods improve from wet to dry season while linear baselines deteriorate by approximately a factor of four, thereby providing direct operational evidence for season-specific model selection. At the scientific-knowledge level, the SHAP-based decomposition yields new mechanistic evidence that the Guilin karst water system undergoes a hydrologically driven seasonal regime shift in pollution mechanisms, from single-factor NH3-N dominance under wet season pulse loading to multi-factor synergistic control by NH3-N, total nitrogen, dissolved oxygen, and the pollution-load index under dry-season baseflow conditions, accompanied by the near-complete disappearance of total-phosphorus influence in the dry season interpretable through karst-specific carbonate-mediated phosphorus sequestration. This regime-shift pattern is reproducible across multiple algorithms, consistent with independent biogeochemical evidence, and directly translatable into season-differentiated management interventions targeting agricultural non-point-source pulse loads in the wet season and point-source nitrogen baseflow accumulation in the dry season. The remainder of this paper is organized as follows: Section "Pearson correlation analysis" describes the study area, data acquisition, and the proposed modeling framework; Section "Multicollinearity detection using variance inflation factor (VIF)" presents the comparative performance evaluation, error diagnostics, and SHAP based driver identification across wet and dry seasons; Section "Random forest (RF) feature importance evaluation" discusses the findings in the context of recent literature and management implications; and Section "Final feature set and standardization" summarizes the conclusions, limitations, and future research directions.

Materials and methods

Research area

The study site is located within the urban water system of the central Lingui New District, Guilin City, Guangxi Zhuang Autonomous Region. Guilin situated in northeastern Guangxi at the southern end of the Xiang-Guang Corridor within the southwestern section of the Nanling Mountain Range. The region is characterized by a typical karst landscape dominated by peak forest plains. The geographical coordinates of the study area ranged from 109°57′46.90’' to 110°24′27.19’' east longitude, and 25°03′2.39’' N to 25°25′59.94’' N, covering a total area of approximately 1,786 km2. Administratively, it encompasses six urban districts of Guilin City (Lingui District, Xiufeng District, Xiangshan District, Diecai District, Qixing District, and Yanshan District) and two counties (Lingchuan County and Yongfu County) (see Fig. 1).

Observation data and preprocessing

The primary water bodies within the study area comprise four rivers, two streams, one ring, and one wetland, namely the Taohua River, Li River, Xiangsi River, Luoqing River, Liangfeng River, Taiping River, the urban water system surrounding the Lingui New District, and the Huixian karst wetland. These water bodies formed a north–south river network that surrounded the urban center (see Fig. 1). The spatial relationships among water bodies are as follows: the Taohua River flows eastward from the west and joins the middle reach of the Li River. The Li River flowed southward through the study area from the north, while the Liangfeng River flowed northeastward from the southwest and ultimately converged into the Li River. The Huixian Wetland study area exhibited distinct hydrological characteristics demarcated by the Fenshui Pond: water bodies in the west of the pond flowed toward the Xiangsi River, whereas water in east of the pond drained into the Liangfeng River. The Luoqing River flowed southward through the eastern part of the study area, with its tributary Xiangsi River joining the Luoqing River mainstem downstream.

The region’s complex river network and unique karst topography provided a representative study subject for this research. Considering the robustness of rivers under varying environmental conditions, this study aims to demonstrate their generalization capabilities across different hydrological and aquatic environments40. WQI includes pH (dimensionless), TN (mg/L), TP (mg/L), NH3-N (mg/L), DO (mg/L), and CODCr (mg/L). The average monthly rainfall in Lingui New District from June to August (wet season) was 244 mm, while the average monthly rainfall from December to February of the following year (dry season) was 72 mm.

Sampling station coordinates were determined by Google Earth Pro combined with GPS positioning, and the locations of all 104 stations are listed in Supplementary Table S1. Field sampling was carried out monthly from June to August 2025 and from December 2025 to February 2026. For each station, the three monthly measurements within a season were averaged to obtain one representative seasonal value per water quality parameter, giving 104 station-level records per season and 208 records in total. This aggregation was chosen to represent the integrated seasonal pollution state at each station and to reduce the influence of single-event outliers on spatial comparisons; the resulting dataset is therefore a seasonal cross-sectional snapshot rather than a time series. We acknowledge that monthly averaging inevitably attenuates within-season hydrological variability, including short term pulse events characteristic of karst conduit flow; this is noted as a limitation in Section "Final feature set and standardization".

Each seasonal dataset was processed in four steps before feature engineering (see Fig. 2). First, records with any missing value among the six core parameters (TN, TP, CODCr, NH3-N, DO, pH) or the WQI target were removed by listwise deletion; leaving 104 complete records per season. Second, within-season Z-score screening (|Z|> 3) was used to flag potential extreme values caused by instrumental error or transcription mistakes; since all samples were collected by trained personnel under standardised protocols, no record exceeded this threshold and none was excluded. Third, to ensure reliable model evaluation under the modest seasonal sample size (n = 104 per season), each wet- and dry-season dataset was independently partitioned via stratified random splitting (random state = 42) using an 80/20 train–test ratio (training set: n = 83; test set: n = 21). Feature engineering, the three-stage selection process, hyperparameter optimization, and final model training were conducted exclusively on the training portion through fivefold cross-validation. The held-out test set was reserved solely for unbiased final performance assessment, thereby preventing information leakage and providing a realistic gauge of model generalizability under distinct seasonal hydrochemical conditions. Feature engineering and selection

Adaptive feature engineering strategy

Given the pronounced seasonal differentiation in hydrodynamic and material transport characteristics between wet and dry periods in typical karst water systems of Guilin, an adaptive feature engineering strategy was developed in this study. The core objective of this strategy is to address the balance between feature dimensionality and sample size under small-sample conditions. This balance is achieved by strictly following the well-established “10 samples per predictor” rule (p/n ≤ 0.1) of statistical learning, which has been validated both theoretically and empirically across linear, generalised linear, and modern machine-learning settings. Duke (2004)76 demonstrated in a widely cited tutorial that the variance inflation of regression coefficients grows rapidly when the sample-to-predictor ratio drops below 10. Jame (2015)99 extended this guidance to non-linear settings, recommending an events-per-variable ratio of 10–20 to keep the effective degrees of freedom within the regime of statistically stable estimation. van der Ploeg, Austin et al.42 further empirically validated the rule for support vector machines, random forests, and neural networks, showing that prediction-error variance and miscalibration increase sharply once p/n exceeds approximately 0.1 in cohorts of fewer than 200 samples. With a sample size of n = 104 per season in this study, unrestricted generation of derived features would substantially increase model variance and destabilize coefficient estimation. The adaptive feature engineering strategy developed herein therefore controls feature dimensionality while fully preserving the essential seasonal hydrodynamic and material transport information.

To mitigate this issue, a sample-size-dependent adaptive strategy was implemented: for n < 30, two core features are generated; for 30 ≤ n < 50, six to eight features are constructed; and for n ≥ 50, eight to twelve features are created. This hierarchical approach achieves an optimal trade-off between information gain and overfitting risk43 and enables dynamic adjustment of the feature space according to seasonal data characteristics, thereby preventing information loss while maintaining statistical robustness44. It is important to note that the engineered features, although constructed from the same raw parameters used in WQI calculation, do not constitute algebraic proxies for the target variable. The WQI formula involves piecewise linear normalization of raw concentrations against externally defined regulatory threshold values (see Supplementary Table S3 online), rendering WQI a non-linear, threshold-dependent composite that cannot be directly reconstructed from raw parameter values or their ratios. To verify the absence of information leakage, we conducted four empirical validation experiments (see Supplementary Tables S7-S10 online), these results collectively demonstrate that the derived features provide genuine, non-redundant predictive information that complements the original parameters without encoding WQI structure, and that the cross-validation procedure fully preserves the integrity of evaluation.

Based on the sample size of 104, ten candidate derived features were initially constructed in this study, encompassing nutrient limitation dynamics (N/P ratio, NH3-N/P ratio), nitrogen speciation transformation (TN/NH3-N ratio), pollution accumulation effects (pollution load index), and hydrodynamic responses(comprehensive nutrient index). These candidate features subsequently underwent a rigorous three-stage screening process (described below) to retain a final feature subset characterized by strong explanatory power for WQI and acceptable multicollinearity for modeling.

VIF quantifies the degree to which the variance of a regression coefficient is inflated due to linear dependencies with other predictors, calculated as Eq. (4):

where represents the coefficient of determination when regressing the i-th feature against all other features.

The VIF screening process was implemented iteratively. In each iteration, the feature with the maximum VIF value was removed, and VIF values were recalculated for the remaining feature set. This procedure continued until all retained features satisfied the VIF threshold criterion. A VIF value of 1 indicates complete independence among predictors. Although VIF > 10 is commonly cited as evidence of severe multicollinearity, this threshold is a practical convention rather than a formally derived criterion, and its appropriateness varies with sample size and modeling purpose (O'Brien. 2007;45). In environmental modeling contexts, relaxed thresholds up to 20 are frequently and justifiably adopted46. Because the present study is primarily predictive rather than inferential, moderate collinearity carries limited consequence for model accuracy47. Accordingly, the iterative removal procedure is bounded by a second stopping criterion: elimination terminates when the number of remaining variables reaches the target dimensionality prescribed by the Hawkins48 sample to feature rule (n/10), whichever occurs first. This safeguard prevents the feature pool from falling below the minimum needed for stable estimation under our modest sample size (n = 104 per season). The robustness of this strategy is confirmed by a sensitivity analysis across VIF thresholds of 5, 7, and 10, which produced identical retained feature sets, identical test-set performance, and perfectly stable SHAP importance rankings (Spearman ρ = 1.000) across all threshold settings.

To prevent overfitting under limited sample conditions (n = 104), a regularized Random Forest configuration was implemented with conservative hyperparameters: 50 trees, a maximum depth of 3, a minimum samples per split of 10% of sample size, and a minimum samples per leaf of 5% of sample size. This conservative parameterization ensures stable importance rankings while reducing sensitivity to noise and outliers. Following importance evaluation, features were ranked in descending order of their importance scores, and the top 10 features were retained for final model training to maintain model parsimony and avoid overfitting in small-sample regression contexts44.

Final feature set and standardization

Through the three-stage selection process (Table 1), the candidate feature pool for both wet and dry seasons was reduced from 16 features (6 original water quality parameters and 10 derived features) to 10 features. All six original parameters (TN, TP, CODCr, NH3-N, DO, pH) were retained due to their direct environmental significance and established roles in water quality assessment frameworks. Among the 10 derived features, four-N/P ratio, NH3-N/P ratio, TN/NH3-N ratio, and pollution load index successfully passed all three screening stages, while the remaining six derived features were excluded due to either severe multicollinearity (VIF > 10) or weak correlation (|r|< 0.03).

Machine learning algorithms

To comprehensively evaluate predictive performance across diverse model architectures, this study implemented ten base machine learning algorithms spanning linear, kernel-based, and tree-ensemble methodologies, complemented by a stacking meta-ensemble strategy. The algorithm portfolio was designed to capture a spectrum of model complexity, ranging from interpretable linear models to sophisticated non-linear ensembles, enabling systematic assessment of algorithm-specific advantages under seasonal hydrological regimes (Tiyasha et al. 2020).

Tree-based ensemble models

The RF model is an ensemble machine learning method used for classification and regression problems. It is based on a range of decision trees (CARTs) known as combined classifiers that randomly select predictors and calibration data for each independent tree and combine all the trees to provide more reliable predictions of the dependent variable . The number of trees (NTree) and the number of predictors chosen at each tree split (NPred) are the two most sensitive hyper-parameters of the RF algorithm (Huang et al. 2016). Since the RF algorithm does not overfit data, the number of trees does not have a significant impact on the model performance (Guan H et al. 2013)103 and a minimum of 500 trees is typically adequate (1300 trees were used in this study). But reducing the number of predictors at each split impacts the computation time, the correlations between trees and their predictive ability54,55. The number of trees (NTree) was searched over {20, 30, 50, 70, 100} and the number of candidate features at each split (NPred) over {sqrt, log2, 0.3, 0.5, 0.7} of total features. Maximum tree depth was restricted to {2, 3, 4, 5, 6}. Minimum samples required to split an internal node and to form a leaf node were set adaptively as {2, 5, 10, 0.1n, 0.2n} and {1, 2, 3, 0.05n, 0.1n} respectively, where n is the training set size, ensuring each leaf retained a statistically representative number of samples.

Stacking ensemble strategy

A two-layer stacking architecture was implemented to synthesize complementary strengths of diverse base learners59. First, all base models were ranked based on their R2 scores obtained from fivefold cross-validation, and the top three models exhibiting optimal performance and positive R2 values were selected as Level-1 Learners. The cross-validation predictions of these base models on the training set were then used as meta-features to construct a new feature matrix for input into the Level-2 Meta-learner. The meta-learner employed strongly regularized Ridge regression ( = 50 for small sample sizes < 30, = 10 for sufficient samples) to learn the optimal linear combination of base model predictions and generate the final output. The stacking strategy enables adaptive weight allocation by the meta-learner, effectively integrating locally optimal predictions from individual models while reducing the bias of single models and controlling the variance of ensemble predictions60.

Hyperparameter optimization

Exhaustive grid search combined with cross-validation was employed to identify the optimal hyperparameter configurations for each algorithm61,62. Moreover, hyperparameter ranges were dynamically adjusted based on sample size to ensure statistical robustness and the search space was systematically designed to balance model complexity and generalization capability under small-sample conditions (n = 104 per season).

All hyperparameter optimization employed k-fold cross-validation (k = 5 for n ≥ 50, k = 3 for n < 50) with R2 as the optimization objective. Grid search was parallelized across CPU cores to maintain computational efficiency. The complete hyperparameter search grids for all models are provided in Supplementary Table S4.

To enhance the transparency and interpretability of machine learning models, this study employed the SHapley Additive Explanations (SHAP) method to conduct an in-depth analysis of the prediction mechanism of the optimal model. SHAP is based on the Shapley value theory from cooperative game theory, which decomposes the model’s predicted value into the sum of independent contributions from each input feature by calculating the marginal contribution of each feature across all possible feature combinations64. This approach enables both global and local explanations of the model’s output. The SHAP analysis method is particularly suitable for characterizing water quality response processes in karst water systems, which exhibit threshold effects, seasonal dependence, and nonlinear amplification characteristics. Because both the predictors and the target WQI are derived from the same physicochemical measurements, the absolute SHAP importance values reported below are partly conditioned by the WQI formula’s weighting structure and cannot be read as a fully independent feature ranking. To avoid circular interpretation, our mechanistic conclusions are anchored in the contrast of SHAP importance between wet and dry seasons rather than in absolute values within a single season. Since the formula weights Pi are season invariant by construction, any systematic shift in ranking across seasons must originate in the environmental data and cannot be recovered from the formula alone.

Regression receiver operating characteristic (RROC) signal analysis

Spatial block cross validation

Conventional random k-fold cross validation assumes that samples are independent and identically distributed; however, water quality monitoring stations on the same river network often exhibit substantial spatial autocorrelation, with geographically adjacent stations sharing similar pollutant inputs and hydrodynamic conditions. Random k-fold CV applied to such spatially-clustered data can therefore overestimate predictive performance, because some training-set stations will be spatially adjacent to test set stations and the model effectively interpolates rather than extrapolates. To address this concern explicitly, a spatial block cross-validation protocol was implemented following the conceptual framework of David et al. (2017)75 and Jonne et al. (2017)104.

The 104 monitoring stations of each season were partitioned into five spatial blocks by K-means clustering on the (latitude, longitude) coordinates, with the number of blocks chosen to balance fold size (≥ 4 stations per block) and inter-block geographic separation. Leave-one-block-out cross-validation was then performed: in each of the five folds, one spatial block was held out as the test set while the remaining four blocks formed the training set. Each fold independently re-fitted the StandardScaler and re-trained the model with the same best_params and stacking_config as in the main results. The same five-fold split was used to run a standard random k-fold cross-validation as a paired comparison; the spatial-vs-random gap is summarised by the R2_drop metric expressed in Eq. 33:

A small R2_drop (close to zero) indicates that the model is insensitive to spatial autocorrelation and therefore generalises robustly to new geographic locations; a large R2_drop indicates that the model’s high random-CV performance is partly attributable to spatial-adjacency leakage and that the true generalisation performance is materially lower. To quantify inter-block geographic separation, we additionally report for each block the minimum, mean, and maximum distance from each held-out test point to the nearest training point.

Results

This study systematically evaluated the performance of ten machine learning algorithms and their ensemble variants for predicting the WQI using 208 valid water samples collected from the urban water system of Guilin during wet and dry seasons. The dataset included six key water quality parameters: pH, DO, CODCr, TP, TN, and NH3-N. Model development incorporated comprehensive feature engineering and selection, rigorous fivefold cross-validation, and SHAP-based interpretability analysis to identify key drivers of WQI variation. The findings reveal pronounced seasonal differences in both model applicability and pollution driving mechanisms, providing robust scientific evidence for season-specific precision management of karst water systems.

To reduce redundancy and collinearity among raw water quality indicators, systematic feature screening was conducted prior to model construction. The screening workflow employed a three-stage progressive strategy, correlation analysis, multicollinearity test and random forest importance evaluation, aimed at retaining core features with significant contribution to WQI prediction while eliminating redundant variables and reducing model complexity66. Random Forest scatter points tightly clustered along the 1:1 reference line, exhibiting a narrow linear banding distribution with only minor deviations in several individual high-concentration samples. The fitted lines nearly coincided with the reference lines. Gradient Boosting showed similar performance, with small vertical scatter dispersion and stable prediction accuracy across the full concentration range. XGBoost and LightGBM showed further improvement, displaying more compact scatter distribution. Even in high-pollution sample ranges (WQI > 70), scatter points remained close to the reference line without obvious systematic overestimation or underestimation, and residual magnitudes were substantially smaller than those of linear models and SVR. CatBoost and AdaBoost showed scatter distributions intermediate between SVR and RandomForest, with moderate clustering around the 1:1 reference line but noticeable scatter in the moderate-to-high WQI range (50–70 mg/L).

The Stacking model achieved optimal performance during the dry season, with scatter plots exhibiting extremely high linear consistency. Nearly all sample points were distributed tightly within an extremely narrow band along the 1:1 reference line, and the fitted and reference lines perfectly coincide (p < 0.1 indicating high statistical significance).

The predictive performance of each model under wet season conditions is illustrated in Fig. 7(b). Overall prediction accuracy improved across all models, although inter-model performance differences remained significant. Scatter dispersion for linear model decreased, yet Ridge, Lasso, and ElasticNet still exhibited relatively wide dispersion patterns. Unlike in the dry season, wet season linear models showed reduced prediction bias in the low concentration range (WQI < 35). Nevertheless, obvious underestimation tendencies persisted in the moderate-to-high concentration range (WQI 45–65), with fitted line slopes less than 1, indicating persistent systematic bias.

SVR showed markedly improved performance during the wet season, with scatter points converging toward the reference line, a reduced number of outliers, and a smaller angle between the fitted line with the reference line. However, sporadic large deviations remained observable at high-concentration extreme samples (WQI > 65), indicating room for improvement in predicting extreme pollution events.

Tree-based and ensemble models maintained excellent performance during the wet season. Random Forest and Gradient Boosting scatter distributions were extremely compact, with fitted lines highly coincident with the reference lines and no obvious prediction blind spots across the full concentration range. XGBoost achieved optimal wet season performance, with scatter plots displaying a near-perfect linear arrangement. Even for samples with drastic pollution level changes, prediction-measurement deviations remained extremely limited, with residual magnitudes controlled within ± 3 mg/L. LightGBM performed comparably, with minimal vertical scatter dispersion, demonstrating a strong capability for capturing nonlinear relationships and feature interactions. CatBoost and AdaBoost scatter plots contracted toward the reference line relative to SVR, with reduced outlier frequency, though fitted line slopes remained slightly below 1, indicating residual systematic underestimation at high WQI concentrations.

The Stacking model also performed excellently during the wet season. Although R2 was slightly lower than in the dry season, the scatter distribution remained highly concentrated near the reference line, maintaining extremely high overall fitting quality. Comprehensive scatter plots showed that XGBoost and Stacking distributions nearly overlap during the wet season, while the scatter of linear models still exhibited obvious vertical dispersion, further validating the superiority of ensemble learning models.

From dry to wet season, all models showed varying degrees of R2 improvement and RMSE reduction, indicating that wet season water quality variation patterns are relatively easier to capture. Linear models showed average R2 improvements of approximately 0.2–0.3, yet even during the wet season, their maximum R2 remained far below the minimum values achieved by tree-based models, indicating fundamental limitations of linear assumptions in water quality prediction. Conversely, tree-based and ensemble models demonstrated consistently strong and stable predictive capabilities across both seasons. XGBoost performed optimally during the wet season, which is highly correlated with wet season characteristics such as diverse pollution sources, drastic indicator fluctuations, and complex feature interactions. Through the gradient boosting mechanisms, XGBoost effectively captures complex feature interaction patterns under conditions of large sample sizes and strong nonlinear relationships. Its scatter plots exhibited virtually no systematic bias or obvious outliers, validating its powerful fitting capability for multi-factor coupled water quality prediction tasks.

In contrast, the Stacking model achieved more stable predictive capability during the dry season, significantly outperforming its wet season performance. This difference was associated with the fact that dry season water quality changes controlled by a few dominant factors. By integrating the prediction advantages of diverse structural models (Gradient Boosting, XGBoost, LightGBM), the Stacking model achieves more precise ensemble effects when data structure is relatively simple and dominant factors are clear. Its dry season scatter plots exhibited near-perfect linear consistency, with deviations between the fitted and reference line being almost negligible.

Furthermore, the sharp rise in |AOC| from 17,879 for SVR (rank 8) to 38,210 for Lasso (rank 9) delineates a clear threshold. This boundary separates models capable of capturing multi factor synergies of dry season water quality parameters including NH3–N, TN, DO and pollution load from those limited to simple linear additivity.

Under wet season conditions, XGBoost attained the smallest |AOC| (3215.5), followed closely by Stacking (3434.4) and GradientBoosting (4345.2). RandomForest (|AOC|= 4492.0) ranked fourth, with LightGBM (5806.0), CatBoost (6130.3) and AdaBoost (7131.2) trailing among the tree ensembles. For Ridge, this cumulative integrated residual area exceeds 12,000 units, which is not acceptable in practical application. This is especially true because the high NH3-N period in the wet season exactly overlaps with pollution events, when accurate WQI prediction is most urgently needed.

For the top three ensemble methods, this ratio falls within the narrow band of 0.45 to 0.54 (Stacking: 1543.0/3434.4 = 0.45; GradientBoosting: 1961.4/4345.2 = 0.45; XGBoost: 1723.1/3215.5 = 0.54), confirming that ensemble approaches benefit from the structurally simpler dry season target. Second, the dry-season Stacking |AOC| (1543.0) is 52.0% lower than the wet season XGBoost |AOC| (3215.5), demonstrating that when an algorithm is well matched to the hydrological regime, the residual envelope in the dry season can be reduced to a far smaller magnitude than that in the wet season. This asymmetric outcome reflects the underlying hydrology: dry season WQI variation is controlled by stable multi-factor synergy that ensemble methods can synthesise tightly, whereas wet season WQI is driven by stochastic ammonia pulses that fundamentally bound the achievable predictive precision.

Spatial block cross validation

The five-block K-means partition produced spatial blocks of sizes 25, 35, 10, 4, and 30 monitoring stations in each season. The minimum distance from each test point to the nearest training point ranged from 0.008°(about 0.9 km, the closest case in blocks 0 and 4) to 0.071°(about 7.9 km, in block 3); the mean distance ranged from 0.148°(about 16.4 km) to 0.228°(about 25.3 km). These distance statistics confirm that the spatial blocks are meaningfully separated and that no test-set station lies immediately adjacent to a training-set station, which is a necessary precondition for the spatial CV protocol to provide an unbiased generalisation estimate.

Table 7 and Fig. 13 summarises the spatial CV performance of all eleven models in both seasons, alongside the paired random CV result and the R2_drop diagnostic. The Stacking ensemble achieves the best spatial CV performance in both seasons (R2_mean = 0.923 in dry, 0.855 in wet) and simultaneously achieves the smallest R2_std (0.043 in dry, 0.091 in wet), demonstrating that its predictive accuracy is both the highest and the most stable across spatial blocks. The GradientBoosting and XGBoost models follow closely (dry-season R2_mean of 0.916 and 0.912, respectively); their slightly larger R2_std values (0.054 and 0.050 in dry, 0.164 and 0.219 in wet) reflect modestly higher sensitivity to which spatial block is held out, consistent with their reliance on single-model variance versus the Stacking ensemble’s multi-model averaging.

SHAP-based key driver identification and optimal model mechanism interpretation

Table 4 quantitatively measures the contribution of each driving factor to WQI prediction during the wet and dry seasons. The SHAP importance value is defined as the mean absolute value of SHAP values for each feature across all samples (mean|SHAPvalue|), reflecting the overall impact strength of features on model outputs64,67.

A comprehensive comparison of SHAP feature importance across the ten main models for both seasons is presented in Fig. 8. In the wet season, the feature importance distribution exhibited obvious algorithm dependency. In gradient boosting algorithms (Gradient Boosting, XGBoost, Random Forest), NH3–N importance values ranked the highest, which is consistent with these algorithms’ proficiency in capturing nonlinear relationships and feature interaction effects. Conversely, linear models (Ridge, Lasso, ElasticNet, CatBoost and AdaBoost) showed significant changes in feature importance rankings. Pollution load inedx and DO rose to the first positions, while NH3–N retreated to third or fourth position. This difference reflected the tendency of linear models to directly associate with baseline indicators that carry larger weights in WQI calculation formulas. Derived ratio features (N/P ratio, TN/NH3–N ratio, NH3–N/P ratio) ranked last across all algorithms with importance values below 0.05, indicating that wet season water quality fluctuations were primarily driven by absolute pollutant concentrations, while nutrient structural proportions played limited roles.

In the dry season, feature importance distribution exhibited more complex multi-factor competitive patterns. The importance of DO increased significantly across most algorithms, ranking first in Ridge and XGBoost, second in Gradient Boosting and LightGBM, with importance values generally exceeding 0.20. This change suggested that dry season water body redox conditions exerted enhanced constraining effects on water quality status. The importance of TN showed a dramatic increase during the dry season, jumping to first position in Random Forest algorithms and maintaining top-three positions in Gradient Boosting, LightGBM, and XGBoost, corroborating the increase reported in Table 4 from 0.0234 to 0.2509. Although NH3–N maintained first position in Gradient Boosting , its importance declined notably in linear models, ranking only third in Lasso and Random Forest, and seventh in ElasticNet. The importance of pollution load index exhibited algorithm differentiation during the dry season, ranking second in Lasso but declining in gradient boosting algorithms. The importance of pH increased during the dry season, with importance values rising to the 0.0150–0.0250 range across most algorithms, possibly related to decreased buffering capacity of water body. The ranking of TP remained low, further confirming that phosphorus was not a limiting nutrient element during the dry season.

The sample-level distribution patterns of SHAP values are further illustrated in Fig. 9. In the wet season, NH3-N SHAP value scatter in gradient boosting algorithms showed an obvious positive aggregation patterns, where high concentrations corresponded to high SHAP values. However, in linear models, NH3–N SHAP value scatter exhibited greater vertical span, with enhanced variability at identical concentration levels, reflecting the difficulty of linear models in accurately characterizing NH3–N interactions with other factors. The pollution load index showed stronger concentration in linear models, with SHAP value distributions displaying narrow positive banding, while gradient boosting algorithms exhibited richer color gradients. DO SHAP value distributions appeared relatively regular across all algorithms, however, LightGBM scatter color gradients were most pronounced, validating its high sensitivity to DO. The TP and TN indicator SHAP value scatter plots were highly dispersed with alternating positive and negative values, indicating that their WQI impacts are modulated by other factors. Ratio feature SHAP value scatter densely distributed near zero and predominantly displayed in light blue colors, validating their marginal roles during the wet season.

Dry season SHAP value distributions exhibited distinctly different characteristics from the wet season. TN SHAP value scatter in ElasticNet and Random Forest showed significant bidirectional distribution, where high-concentration samples could generate both strong positive contributions and negative impacts. This complex non-monotonic relationship reflected TN’s potential influence on water quality through multiple pathways during the dry season (e.g., nitrification–denitrification balance, algal growth limitation transitions). The DO SHAP value scatter exhibited wider numerical spans and richer color gradients during the dry season, particularly in Ridge and XGBoost, indicating enhanced DO impact intensity on WQI with nonlinear characteristics. Although NH3-N SHAP values remained predominantly positive, the vertical dispersion of scatter points increased across most algorithms, suggesting enhanced synergistic constraints from other factors on WQI impacts. The pollution load index SHAP value distributions in Lasso showed obvious positive aggregation, yet displayed stronger dispersion in gradient boosting algorithms. It should be acknowledged that the absolute SHAP magnitudes reportedare not fully independent of the WQI calculation, because predictors and target share their measurement basis. The interpretation in this section therefore rests on the seasonal contrast in importance rather than on the within-season ordering. Pi does not vary with season, so the shifts most notably TN’s 10.7-fold increase, TP’s near disappearance, and DO’s elevation from 13.67 to 21.29% cannot be derived from the formula and must reflect seasonal changes in the field distributions of these parameters. This contrast is the basis on which we read SHAP as informative about pollution driving mechanisms rather than as a reflection of formula structure.

Analysis of spatial and temporal distribution of water quality factors and water environment

The study systematically analyzed the concentration ranges, mean values, and coefficient of variation of key water quality parameters (DO, pH, TN, NH3–N, CODCr, and TP) during both wet and dry seasons across monitoring areas. This comprehensive analysis provides critical insights into the pollution characteristics and spatial distribution patterns of Guilin’s urban water systems, establishing a robust data foundation for developing water quality assessment models and identifying key influencing factors. The karst water monitoring results are shown in Supplementary Table S5.

The spatial distribution during the wet season, as shown in Fig. 11(a), reveals that the highest CODCr concentrations are concentrated in the central and northwestern river sections, with a peak value of 54.26 mg/L, while the eastern section shows a significantly lower concentration of 0.26 mg/L, resulting in a concentration range of 54.00 mg/L, which demonstrates a pronounced spatial gradient. DO exhibits a distribution pattern of higher levels in the east and lower levels in the west, ranging from 0.09 to 12.99 mg/L (12.90 mg/L). The pH values range from 7.32 to 8.69, showing minimal spatial variation. TN concentrations range from 0.00 to 53.77 mg/L, with high values concentrated in the southwest region but dispersed. TP reached a peak at 1.65 mg/L, occurring in localized sections of the northwestern and central river segments. The NH3-N concentrations ranged from 0.00 to 10.67 mg/L, with the highest values located in the central river section.

Figure 11(b) illustrates the spatial distribution during the dry season, with CODCr peaking at 81.73 mg/L. The high-value zone expanded toward the northwest and southward, while the eastern region dropped to 0.00 mg/L. The DO range narrowed to 3.22–8.99 mg/L (range: 5.77 mg/L), with the high-value zone concentrated in the central area. pH ranged from 6.85 to 9.53 showed no significant changes in their spatial distribution. TN peaked at 66.68 mg/L, forming a large high-value zone in the southern region. TP reached 2.65 mg/L, with the southern high-value zone expanding significantly. From a modeling perspective, the broad concentration ranges of various indicators require accurate prediction of WQI across different concentration intervals. Linear models use uniform regression coefficients, which struggle to adapt to the varying concentration-WQI response relationships across river sections. In contrast, XGBoost’s tree splitting mechanism enables the construction of differentiated rules for different concentration intervals, while the Stacking integration model combines the predictive strengths of multiple algorithms. This explains the superior performance of both approaches compared with linear models in spatially heterogeneous water systems.

Discussion

This study conducted comprehensive monitoring of water-quality parameters in the karst water system of Guilin across the wet and dry seasons. Karst aquifers are characterized by high heterogeneity and complex subsurface hydrological systems, making water-quality monitoring and management particularly challenging (Goldscheider et al. 2005)5. Six core physico-chemical parameters including pH, DO, CODCr, TN, TP, and NH3–N, were measured at 104 stations, and ten machine-learning algorithms (Ridge, Lasso, ElasticNet, SVR, Random Forest, Gradient Boosting, AdaBoost, XGBoost, LightGBM, CatBoost) were independently trained and evaluated for the wet and dry seasons. The three best cross-validation performers in each season were then integrated as base learners into a two-layer Stacking meta-model. Conventional scalar metrics (R2, RMSE, MAE) were complemented by RROC signal analysis to resolve the over versus under-prediction balance, and SHAP attribution was applied across all models to identify the seasonally dominant pollution drivers. This integrated workflow is intended to address the structural challenges of karst water-quality modelling: small sample sizes, dual-porosity hydrogeological heterogeneity, and pronounced seasonal non-stationarity.

The methodological contribution lies not in the invention of a new algorithm, but in the theoretically principled integration of existing tools: seasonal stratification, adaptive feature engineering, progressive selection, and stacking ensemble into a coherent framework specifically designed for the structural challenges of karst water quality modeling: small samples, dual-porosity heterogeneity, and pronounced seasonal nonstationarity. This framework addresses what Goldscheider1 identified as a critical barrier to karst water resource management: the absence of predictive tools capable of capturing the system’s inherent temporal complexity. The scientific contribution lies in the SHAP-quantified evidence that karst water systems exhibit a hydrologically driven seasonal regime shift in pollution mechanisms, a pattern that is robust across multiple algorithms and consistent with independent biogeochemical evidence59,68, and that carries direct implications for the design of precision management strategies.

Evaluation via R2, RMSE, and MAE revealed marked distinctions in predictive capability under different hydrological conditions. During the wet season, XGBoost performed optimally (R2 = 0.9655, RMSE = 3.0938, MAE = 2.6754), effectively capturing complex relationships between pollutant inputs from rainfall-runoff and dilution from increased water volume. During the dry season, the Stacking ensemble model demonstrated the highest stability (R2 = 0.9785, RMSE = 2.9142, MAE = 2.4278) by integrating multi-model advantages to more accurately characterize pollutant distribution. These results indicate that model selection should be determined by seasonal water-quality process characteristics, providing a reference for practitioners selecting appropriate machine-learning methods for karst-water-quality prediction.

These findings are broadly consistent with, yet extend, recent comparative literature on machine-learning-based WQI prediction. Choudhary et al.31 developed a stacked regression ensemble using XGBoost, CatBoost, Random Forest, Gradient Boosting, Extra Trees, and AdaBoost as base learners with linear regression as meta-learner, achieving R2 = 0.9952 on 1,987 river samples in India, slightly higher than the present dry season Stacking performance (R2 = 0.9785). This disparity likely reflects the substantially larger training set used by Choudhary et al. (nearly tenfold the 208 valid samples available here), which affords the meta-learner greater capacity to integrate base-learner outputs. Critically, that study applied a single static dataset, whereas the present study implements season-differentiated modelling to reveal temporal shifts in pollution-driving mechanisms with stronger implications for precision seasonal management. At the individual-model level, Hridoy et al.8 demonstrated that XGBoost achieved the highest WQI regression accuracy (R2 = 0.9685) among the evaluated algorithms, consistent with the XGBoost superiority observed here in the wet season. Zhang et al.33,34 further reported that CatBoost and XGBoost both achieved R2 = 0.994 with high inter-model consistency in karst-influenced coastal rivers in South China, corroborating the robustness of tree-based ensembles in spatially heterogeneous aquatic systems. Alshehri and Rahman69 proposed a CNN-Stacking ensemble for groundwater WQI prediction and showed that the Stacking framework (R2 = 0.99) substantially outperformed the standalone CNN (R2 = 0.90); this finding parallels the dry season pattern in the present study, where Stacking consistently surpassed individual base learners, affirming that integrating complementary model strengths is a robust strategy for stable prediction under low-flow, low-variance hydrological conditions. Mo et al.70 examined seasonal WQI prediction in a coastal city and identified NH3-N and TP as pivotal drivers, reporting that prediction accuracy for the “good” grade declined to approximately 70% in summer and autumn, which is consistent with the wet season prediction challenges observed here, further justifying season-differentiated modelling. Taken together, the present work advances the understanding of ML-based WQI prediction in karst water systems by integrating three components that have rarely been combined in prior classical-ML studies: (i) a seasonally stratified modelling strategy, (ii) an adaptive feature-engineering pipeline constrained by statistical-learning-theory bounds, and (iii) SHAP-based decomposition of prediction mechanisms. While previous studies have generally focused on single temporal snapshots or aggregated annual datasets22,31, the present framework explicitly treats wet and dry seasons as hydrologically distinct regimes requiring separate model selection and interpretation.

Recent years have witnessed rapid development of hybrid architectures that couple machine learning with deep sequential models (Transformers, LSTM), state-space data-assimilation techniques (Kalman filters), weighted ensemble schemes, and meta-heuristic optimization algorithms. Because these architectures attack water-quality prediction with fundamentally different inductive biases, a critical comparison with the present season-stratified Stacking-with-SHAP framework helps to clarify where our contribution sits within the broader landscape and which application contexts each family is best suited to.

First, Transformer-based architectures have recently been deployed to capture the long-range temporal dependencies of water-quality time series. Orozco-López et al. (2024) 27 developed an interpretable Transformer Neural Network for one to fourteen-day-ahead forecasting of streamflow, stage, water temperature, and salinity across the Peace River watershed (Florida) and the northern Gulf of Mexico, combining historical observations with spatially distributed weather forecasts. Chen et al.71 proposed a coupled Transformer-based model to address non-stationarity and data limitations in river-water-quality prediction over coastal Chinese basins. These deep sequential models excel when high-frequency, long-record monitoring data are available and when the forecasting horizon is sub-daily to multi-day. The present study, by contrast, focuses on a seasonally aggregated cross-sectional dataset (one observation per station per season; 208 stations × seasons in total) for which the temporal axis is collapsed by design; the predictive question we address is therefore the spatial diagnosis of seasonal WQI states rather than the temporal forecasting of future values. The Stacking ensemble we adopt is well matched to this small-sample, cross-sectional, structurally heterogeneous setting, where a Transformer would be over-parameterized and under-trained, but Transformer-based models remain the appropriate choice for future sub-monthly or event-based extensions of the present framework.

Second, Gao et al.72 developed an ML-Kalman filter data-assimilation framework integrating Kalman filtering with LSTM, RF, XGBoost, and SVR for the Dahei River Basin, reporting accuracy gains of 4.3–17.6% for TN, TP, and CODMn, with the largest improvement for TP. Poursaeid29 introduced a Kalman-filter-driven hybrid model with feature analysis under uncertainty for the water-quality–ecosystem nexus. These approaches are powerful for operational forecasting under continuous-sensor deployment because they explicitly propagate observation and process noise. The present framework operates upstream of this regime: it characterizes the seasonal driving structure from historical field samples and, in doing so, identifies which parameters (NH3-N in the wet season; the NH3-N, TN, DO, pollution-load synergy in the dry season) should be prioritized for high-frequency assimilation in future Kalman-filter-coupled deployments. The two approaches are therefore complementary.

Third, weighted ensemble learning has emerged as an effective strategy for combining multiple ML models when no single algorithm consistently dominates across operating conditions. Poursaeid28 developed a linear-programming-optimized weighted ensemble for streamflow prediction in the Kashkan River Basin, demonstrating that mathematically optimized weights outperform equal-weight averaging. Our two-layer Stacking framework occupies a related but distinct position: rather than externally optimizing fixed scalar weights, the Level-2 Ridge meta-learner adaptively combines Level-1 base-learner predictions through regularized regression on the cross-validated out-of-fold predictions, which can be viewed as a learned, data-driven weighting that is automatically re-fitted for each season. Applying linear or nonlinear programming optimization to the meta-learner weights of our season-specific Stacking ensembles is a natural extension we identify for future work.

Fourth, optimization-ML hybrids couple meta-heuristic algorithms (e.g., particle swarm, genetic algorithms, simulated annealing) with single ML learners to navigate the hyperparameter space more efficiently than grid or random search. Poursaeid73 integrated single-layer feedforward networks with particle swarm optimization (PSO-SLFN) and genetic algorithms (GA-SLFN) for dissolved oxygen and electrical conductivity prediction in the Colorado River, with PSO-SLFN attaining R2 = 0.865 for EC. In the present study, hyperparameters of all ten base learners are tuned via fivefold grid-search cross-validation; while this approach is computationally tractable for the present sample size, meta-heuristic optimization would offer a clear advantage in scaled-up cross-karst deployments with substantially larger search spaces, and represents another natural extension of the framework.

Finally, mechanistic-data hybrid models such as SWAT and its variants have been used to simulate hydrological processes and pollutant transport17,18, but their use in karst environments is typically limited by parameter-calibration difficulties and high observational-data demand. Our purely data-driven framework circumvents these calibration challenges, at the cost of not explicitly representing conduit-flow physics; a hybrid mechanistic-ML formulation, in which a calibrated SWAT-karst variant supplies process-based features to a SHAP-interpretable ensemble, is a promising direction for future work.

Taken together, these four comparisons clarify the scope of the present contribution: the season-stratified, SHAP-interpretable Stacking framework is designed for the data regime that characterizes most karst surface-water monitoring programmes in subtropical China, namely modest cross-sectional sample sizes, season-differentiated pollution inputs, and the need for a defensible mechanistic interpretation that can inform season-specific management. It is not a substitute for Transformer-based long-horizon forecasting, Kalman-filter-driven assimilation, or weighted-ensemble optimization where those approaches are warranted by the available data, but it is complementary to all of them and provides the diagnostic foundation on which any of them could be subsequently layered.

The study further revealed seasonal transition patterns in the pollution-driving mechanisms of the Guilin karst water system based on SHAP interpretability analysis. During the wet season (June to August), NH3–N exhibited absolute dominance, accounting for 53.47% of feature importance and reflecting agricultural non-point-source pollution input during rainfall events. This single-factor dominance pattern aligns with hydrological processes in which ammonium nitrogen accumulated in soil rapidly enters water bodies via surface runoff during heavy rainfall, causing sharp increases in NH3–N concentrations within short timeframes. The pollution load index (18.25%) and dissolved oxygen (13.67%) served as secondary drivers, reflecting overall pollution pressure and re-aeration capacity of the water body, respectively. In contrast, during the dry season (December to the following February), the driving pattern transitioned toward multi-factor synergistic control. Although NH3–N remained the primary factor (26.00%), the importance of TN (25.09%) increased substantially, indicating enhanced nitrogen accumulation in water bodies following flow reduction. Dissolved oxygen elevated to 21.29%, reflecting reduced flow velocity and decreased re-aeration capacity, while the pollution load index maintained relatively high contribution (20.19%), embodying the increased relative influence of point-source discharges under baseflow conditions. Notably, the contribution of TP declined from 7.73% in the wet season to only 0.06% in the dry season, possibly related to enhanced phosphorus adsorption and fixation in sediments during the dry season.

A caveat applies when SHAP is used on a composite index built from the same predictor variables: the absolute importance values partly reflect the index’s own weighting scheme and cannot be cleanly separated from it. Our seasonal design helps absorb this. If only the fixed weights were driving SHAP, the rankings should stay the same across seasons. Under identical Pi, TN rises from 2.34% to 25.09% and TP falls from 7.73% to 0.06% in this study. This instability shows the model is reading environmental signal beyond the formula.

The seasonal pattern we identify is consistent with other karst and riverine studies and extends them. In the Wujiang karst watershed of Southwest China, Xu et al.59 used XGBoost-SHAP and found CODMn, TN and TP to be the main drivers of impairment, attributing rapid pollutant transport to the high permeability of carbonate rocks. The same mechanism underlies the wet-season NH3–N dominance we observe. The strong wet-season role of NH₃–N (53.47%) is also consistent with Fang et al.68, who reported that NH4+–N in the Yanshan River karst near Guilin was positively correlated with agricultural and built-up land cover, with a stronger coupling in the wet season. Our SHAP analysis reproduces this pattern at the model-feature level. Taken together, the shift from single-factor NH3–N dominance in wet seasons to four-factor synergistic control in dry seasons appears to be a real feature of subtropical karst water systems rather than an artifact of our modelling choices.Several findings warrant explicit discussion as unexpected. First, the near-complete disappearance of TP as a driver during the dry season, declining from 7.73% SHAP importance in the wet season to only 0.06%, is more pronounced than typically reported in non-karst water systems. In conventional riverine systems, TP typically remains a meaningful water-quality driver throughout the year due to continuous point-source discharge from wastewater treatment plants. The extreme attenuation of TP influence in the dry season can be explained by enhanced sediment phosphorus sequestration under low-flow, aerobic, and high-pH conditions characteristic of karst aquifer baseflow. Carbonate-rich sediments have a much higher phosphorus adsorption capacity than silicate-dominated systems, and the alkaline pH measured in the dry season (up to 9.53) favours calcite co-precipitation of phosphate. This geochemical pathway is largely absent in non-karst waters.

Based on these findings, differentiated control measures should be implemented for wet and dry seasons. During the wet season, agricultural NH3-N interception should be strengthened by constructing ecological ditches and vegetative buffer zones in agricultural concentration areas to intercept ammonium nitrogen in surface runoff. Additionally, improving early-stage rainwater storage facilities can reduce pollutant pulse loads during storm periods. During the dry season, stricter control of point-source total nitrogen discharge should be implemented by adopting more stringent nitrogen reduction standards for urban sewage treatment plants. Increasing ecological water supplementation projects can also improve river hydrodynamic conditions, enhancing self-purification and re-aeration efficiency of the water body.

Conclusions, limitations, and future research

The current study provides baseline insights into the complexity and variability of the urban water system around Guilin karst region and its surrounding major rivers and lakes. Based on systematic monitoring data from 208 valid water samples, a three-stage feature selection framework was applied, including correlation filtering-VIF collinearity elimination random forest importance ranking. Ten advanced machine learning models (Ridge, Lasso, ElasticNet, SVR, Random Forest, Gradient Boosting, XGBoost, LightGBM, AdaBoost and CatBoost) were subsequently evaluated. Furthermore, a Stacking ensemble was developed in which the top three cross-validation performers were re-selected as base learners independently for each season, with adaptive weight allocation supplied by a regularized Ridge meta-learner. This framework enabled high-precision prediction of WQI and facilitated analysis of key driving mechanisms of water quality during both wet and dry seasons in the Guilin karst region. The study systematically evaluated machine learning model robustness and determined that XGBoost performed optimally during the wet season (R2 = 0.9655, RMSE = 3.0938, MAE = 2.6754), effectively capturing complex relationships between pollutant inputs from rainfall runoff and water volume dilution. During the dry season, the Stacking ensemble model demonstrated highest stability by integrating multi-model advantages to more accurately identify pollutant distribution characteristics (R2 = 0.9785, RMSE = 2.9142, MAE = 2.4278). Complementary RROC signal analysis confirmed these conventional rankings with greater operational resolution: the Stacking ensemble achieved the smallest dry season |AOC| (1543.0), XGBoost achieved the smallest wet season |AOC| (3215.5), and the cross seasonal |AOC| ratios revealed a fundamental algorithmic inversion in which ensemble methods improve from wet to dry season (|AOC| ratio 0.45–0.54) while linear baselines deteriorate by a factor of 4.13–4.31, providing direct quantitative evidence that operational deployment in karst water systems requires season-specific model selection rather than a single fixed-season model. Additionally, the study revealed significant spatiotemporal variability among various water quality parameters in the dynamic and complex karst water systems. Feature importance analysis indicated that NH3–N dominated during the wet season, accounting for 53.47% of the total importance. During the dry season, water quality was controlled by a multi-factor synergistic mechanism, including NH3-N (26.00%), TN (25.09%), DO (21.29%), and pollution load index (20.19%). These findings suggest that the combined effects of urbanization processes and seasonal hydrological variations significantly impacted karst water system quality, posing potential threats to karst water systems. This study shows that combining machine learning with SHAP interpretation provides an effective route for uncovering the driving mechanisms of complex aquatic systems and supports water-quality evaluation and precision management in similar karsts. We draw a clear distinction between the karst-specific findings and the methodological contribution. The numerical SHAP importance values reported here are tied to Guilin’s karst hydrogeology, monsoonal rainfall regime and land-use composition, and should not be extrapolated as such to other regions.The analytical framework itself, however, is designed to be portable, and we justify its external validity on four grounds. First, it relies only on routinely monitored parameters (TN, TP, CODCr, NH3–N, DO, pH) that are available in standard water-quality programmes worldwide, so no Guilin-specific input is required at deployment. Second, the three-stage adaptive feature-selection module is fully data-driven; it re-identifies the dominant predictors from each new dataset rather than inheriting them from Guilin, which avoids any hard-coded local prior. Third, the seasonally stratified Stacking design targets the wet–dry contrast that characterises most monsoonal subtropical karst, and the same stratification logic remains valid wherever a comparable hydrological seasonality exists. Fourth, SHAP is a model-agnostic explanation method, so its diagnostic capability is preserved on any retrained model regardless of region.External validity is therefore strongest for other subtropical karst with carbonate-dominated hydrogeochemistry and pronounced wet/dry seasonality, provided that feature-selection thresholds and learner hyperparameters are recalibrated to local conditions. Transfer to non-karst lowland rivers, alpine systems or arid catchments would require further validation; we acknowledge this as a limitation of the present single-karst study and a priority for future cross-karst work. We therefore offer this study as both a karst-scale diagnosis of Guilin’s seasonal water-quality drivers and a transferable analytical template for interpretable, seasonally aware water-quality modelling in karst environments. Despite employing systematic modeling approaches and achieving high prediction accuracy, this study has several limitations requiring full consideration in result interpretation and application extension. Accurately identifying these limitations is crucial for rationally assessing research conclusion applicability boundaries and guiding future research directions. First, sample size imposes certain constraints on machine learning model performance. Although this study employed 208 valid samples for modeling and effectively controlled overfitting risks through strong regularization strategies, adaptive cross-validation, and conservative feature selection, limited sample size still constrains application of high-parameter-complexity models. Particularly, insufficient coverage of extreme pollution events may affect model prediction reliability in tail distribution intervals. Recommendations include increasing long-term continuous monitoring, especially strengthening high-frequency sampling during extreme hydrological events to enhance model generalization capability for non-routine pollution scenarios. Second, monitoring points in the study area were deployed along river corridors, with hydraulic connectivity existing among adjacent stations. Global Moran’s I tests confirmed statistically significant positive spatial autocorrelation in WQI (dry season: I = 0.1724, p < 0.001; wet season: I = 0.1816, p < 0.001) and in several driving parameters (see Supplementary Fig. S1 and Table S6 online), indicating that the independence assumption underlying random k-fold cross validation is partially violated. Although the independent 20% hold out test set was reserved prior to all model fitting to provide an unbiased external evaluation, residual spatial dependence between training and test samples may still generate slightly optimistic generalization estimates. Future work should therefore adopt explicitly spatial cross validation schemes (e.g., spatial block, buffered leave one out, or hydrologically connected fold partitioning) and consider models that explicitly encode spatial topology, such as Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) using the karst river network as the adjacency structure, to formally accommodate hydraulic connectivity. Third, current models employ separate static dataset modeling for wet and dry seasons without explicitly characterizing temporal evolution patterns of water quality indicators. Karst water system water quality processes are regulated by factors like rainfall, yet static models struggle to capture these dynamic mechanisms. The three monthly measurements per station were averaged into a single seasonal value; while this approach stabilises spatial comparisons across 104 stations, it inevitably attenuates within-season hydrological pulses particularly conduit-driven storm events that are ecologically significant in karst environments. Future studies should incorporate event-based or sub-monthly sampling to resolve these short-term dynamics. Integrating Long Short-Term (LSTM) Memory networks or Temporal Convolutional Networks with existing machine learning models to construct dynamic prediction frameworks with spatiotemporal collaborative modeling capabilities could potentially enhance model responsiveness to dynamic scenarios like storm events and seasonal transitions (Ouyang,36).

Despite the above limitations, this study’s model prediction results and SHAP driving factor analysis still provide practical guidance for precision management of karst water systems. First, season-differentiated pollution control strategies should be established. Wet season requires strengthened agricultural non-point source NH3-N interception by constructing ecological ditches and vegetative buffer zones in agricultural concentration areas to intercept ammonium nitrogen in surface runoff, while improving early-stage rainwater storage facilities to reduce pollutant pulse loads during storm periods. Dry season should strictly control point source TN discharge by implementing more stringent nitrogen reduction standards for urban sewage treatment plants and increasing ecological water supplement projects to improve river hydrodynamic conditions and enhance water body self-purification and re-aeration efficiency. Second, water quality monitoring network layouts should be optimized. Automatic monitoring stations should be added at key nodes where nutrient concentration seasonal differences are significant, achieving high-frequency online monitoring of key indicators like NH3–N, TN, and DO to provide real-time data support for early warning models. Additionally, the machine learning prediction framework constructed in this research can be integrated with existing water quality monitoring systems to achieve dynamic WQI prediction and advance pollution risk warning, providing scientific basis for management department emergency decision-making. Finally, the carbonate buffering effect characteristic of karst regions maintains relatively stable pH conditions in karst water systems, yet also means water bodies have limited buffering capacity for acidic pollutants. Therefore, special attention should be paid to potential impacts of industrial wastewater discharge on the acid–base balance of karst water systems. Future water quality protection standards should be formulated with explicit consideration of karst hydrogeological characteristics to ensure sustainable management of these vulnerable aquatic environments.
