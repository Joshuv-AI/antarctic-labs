Green synthesis of cadmium sulfide quantum dots coated with quercus infectoria and Heracleum persicum: cytotoxicity studies on RBCs and HFF-2 cells
Authors: Negin Hashemi, Alireza Yazdinezhad, Narjes Ghaseminejad Koushali, Amirhossein Zangene Motlagh, Hossein Danafar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCancer remains to be a major life-threatening illness, prompting ongoing efforts to develop innovative treatment methods. Nanotechnology presents distinct advantages by enabling targeted delivery to malignant cells while minimizing damage to surrounding healthy tissue. Quantum dots (QDs), nanoscale semiconductor particles, have emerged as promising tools for tumor imaging, diagnosis, prevention and treatment. Nonetheless, their application in biomedicine faces challenges due to cytotoxicity, which may affect normal cells and red blood cells (RBCs). To mitigate these issues, green synthesis utilizing plant extracts offers an environmentally friendly and cost-effective method to enhance biocompatibility. This study investigated the synthesis of cadmium sulfide (CdS) QDs, which were subsequently coated with ethanolic extracts from Heracleum persicum (HP) and Quercus infectoria (QI). The successful synthesis and surface modifications were verified through various techniques, including ultraviolet–visible (UV–Vis) spectroscopy, Fourier-transform infrared (FT-IR) spectroscopy, X-ray diffraction (XRD), dynamic light scattering (DLS), and scanning electron microscopy (SEM). DLS results indicated hydrodynamic sizes of 258 nm for CdS@HP and 253 nm for CdS@QI, with zeta potential values shifting from − 22.4 mV for bare CdS to − 25.0 mV for CdS@HP and − 23.9 mV for CdS@QI, suggesting enhanced colloidal stability. Biological assessments, incorporating 3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)-2,5-diphenyl tetrazolium bromide (MTT) and hemolysis assays, revealed that bare CdS QDs exhibited substantial cytotoxicity and hemolytic activity. The HP-coated QDs achieved the highest cell viability and the lowest hemolysis, whereas the QI-coated QDs showed moderate protective effects but a slightly increased hemolytic response. These findings highlight the potential of plant-based coatings to improve the safety and stability of CdS QDs for future biomedical applications.Keywords: Cancer, Nanotechnology, Green synthesis, Quantum dots, Plant extractsSubject terms: Biochemistry, Biological techniques, Biotechnology, Cancer, Chemistry, Materials science, Nanoscience and technology

Introduction

Cancer remains a major global health burden, with its incidence steadily increasing worldwide, Over recent decades, extensive efforts have been directed toward improving therapeutic strategies1,2. Conventional treatment modalities, including chemotherapy, radiotherapy, and surgical interventions, have been widely applied in cancer management. While these traditional approaches significantly contribute to tumor reduction and patient survival, they also often impose substantial damage on healthy tissues, resulting in severe adverse effects. Common side effects include vomiting, nausea, and immune suppression; in severe cases, they can even lead to life‑threatening complications3,4. In response to the challenges outlined, these limitations have motivated researchers to pursue safer and more effective therapeutic strategies, to enhance cancer treatment approaches5,6. Nanotechnology has emerged as one of the most promising and rapidly advancing fields, whose diverse sub‑disciplines provide innovative platforms aimed at identifying optimal intervention pathways. Although determining optimal strategies remains challenging, recent studies have indicated that hybrid approaches, particularly those combining chemical synthesis with green methodologies, enhance therapeutic efficacy while reducing toxicity to non-cancerous cells and red blood cells (RBCs)7,8. Green synthesis offers additional advantages, including cost‑effectiveness and time efficiency, requiring merely a single step, but it is also environmentally friendly. Additionally, it eliminates the requirement for extreme temperature, pressure, or hazardous chemical conditions, which helps reduce reliance on large quantities of hazardous chemicals9,10. Among the various nanomaterials investigated, quantum dots (QDs) demonstrate remarkable size‑dependent optical and electronic properties, making them as another promising category of nanomaterials11,12. Quantum dots are semiconductor nanoparticles composed of elements from groups II–VI or III–V. Their unique optical and electronic characteristics—including a broad absorption spectrum, a narrow emission spectrum, and excellent photostability—make them highly valuable for numerous technological and biomedical applications, particularly in the medical sector11,13. They are widely used in tumor imaging and diagnostic applications and in targeted drug delivery systems due to their capability to detect cancer biomarkers in blood tests or in biopsies taken from cancerous tissues14. Additionally, their intrinsic antibacterial, antioxidant, and anticancer activities contribute significantly to therapeutic interventions in various diseases15,16. CdS QDs have demonstrated anticancer activity through several mechanisms. These include the induction of programmed cell death and the generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS), particularly reactive oxygen species17,18. In 2017, Prasad and his team synthesized water-soluble CdS nanoparticles and demonstrated their ability to induce DNA damage19. In 2014, Haj Hashemi and his research team conducted a study examining the impact of Heracleum persicum extract on lipid profiles. Their results indicated that the essential oil derived from the plant’s fruits may offer potential benefits in mitigating cardiovascular risk factors20. In 2018, Kavitha and her research team conducted investigations into the synthesis and characterization of CdS QDs. They synthesized these quantum dots using a green chemistry approach, employing an ethanolic extract from the tea plant. Their study further explored the potential applications in biomedical imaging, as well as their antimicrobial properties and effects on lung cancer cells. Through the implementation of a hemolysis assay, the researchers demonstrated a correlation between the concentration of CdS QDs and an increase in the hemolysis rate of red blood cells15.Dr. Dayang Fredalina Basri and her colleagues conducted a study to evaluate the antibacterial potential of aqueous and acetone extracts from the galls of Quercus infectoria. They tested these extracts against six bacterial strains, including Staphylococcus aureus and Escherichia coli O157:H7, using MIC and MBC assays. Their results showed that S. aureus was the most susceptible, while E. coli O157 showed no inhibition. The study concluded that these gall extracts could be promising sources of antimicrobial agents21. In the present study, CdS QDs were synthesized and subsequently coated with an alcoholic extract of dried fruit from the Heracleum persicum (Persian hogweed) and Quercus infectoria (Oak tree) plants, utilizing the maceration method. This functionalization strategy aims to enhance physicochemical properties, boost stability, and manage their toxicity levels. After the synthesis of CdS QDs, as well as CdS QDs coated with ethanolic extracts of Heracleum persicum and Quercus infectoria, comprehensive physicochemical and structural characterization was conducted. Characterization was performed using analytical techniques, including UV-Vis, FT-IR, XRD, DLS, and SEM. One of the assessments used to evaluate and compare their toxicity was the hemolysis assay, which specifically examined their effects on red blood cells. Additionally, cytotoxicity was evaluated using the MTT assay in normal HFF-2 cell lines. The novelty of this work lies not merely in the green synthesis of CdS quantum dots, but in the comparative investigation of two distinct phytochemical-rich coatings derived from Heracleum persicum and Quercus infectoria. While earlier studies have explored plant-mediated synthesis of CdS nanoparticles, there has been no direct comparative assessment of these two medicinal plant extracts concerning nanoparticle stabilization, hemocompatibility, and cytotoxicity towards normal HFF-2 cells. This study provides further understanding of how varying phytochemical compositions impact the physicochemical properties, colloidal stability, and biological interactions of CdS quantum dots.

Materials and methods

Materials

Cadmium chloride (CdCl₂), ethanol, thiourea (SC(NH₂)₂), and sodium hydroxide (NaOH) were purchased from Merck (Germany), while dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) was obtained from Sigma-Aldrich (USA). Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM, High Glucose) and fetal bovine serum (FBS) were sourced from Gibco (USA). Phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), 0.25% ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) solution, and penicillin-streptomycin antibiotic solution were acquired from Inoclon (Iran). The MTT reagent was purchased from Atocel (Austria). The human foreskin fibroblast cell line (HFF2) was kindly provided by the Pasteur Institute of Iran.

Methods

Plant collection and identification

The Persian hogweed and oak tree specimens, corresponding to herbarium codes 4019 and 5992, were acquired from a reputable herbal store in Zanjan city. Following their confirmation and identification by the Department of Medicinal Plants at the Zanjan Faculty of Pharmacy, these specimens were subsequently stored at that institution.

Preparation of the complete extract from the Heracleum persicum plant utilizing the maceration technique

Following the preparation of the Heracleum persicum plant material, the fruits of the Persian hogweed were cleaned and dried at ambient temperature. In this study, the ethanolic extract of the Persian hogweed was obtained through the maceration technique. Initially, the dried fruits were ground using a laboratory-grade medicinal plant grinder. Subsequently, 3 g of the resulting powder were combined with 30 mL of 70% ethanol and allowed to macerate for a duration of 24 h. After extraction, the plant extract was separated and filtered using filter paper. The resulting Persian hogweed extract was then stored in dark, sealed containers at a temperature of 4 °C in a refrigerator, ensuring protection from light exposure for future experimental applications.

Preparation of the complete extract from the Quercus infectoria plant utilizing the maceration technique

To prepare the alcoholic extract of the oak tree utilizing the maceration technique, the dried fruits of the oak tree were subjected to grinding with a laboratory-grade medicinal plant grinder. Subsequently, 3 g of the resultant powdered material were combined with 30 mL of 70% ethanol, and this mixture was left to macerate for a duration of 24 h. Following the extraction period, the oak tree extract was filtered for purification using filter paper. The purified extract was stored in a dark, airtight container at 4 °C within a refrigerator, ensuring it was adequately protected from light exposure for utilization in future experimental procedures.

Synthesis of Cadmium sulfide Quantum Dots

CdS QDs were synthesized via the reaction between cadmium chloride and thiourea in a basic pH environment, under controlled temperature conditions, and in the presence of nitrogen gas. For the synthesis of these quantum dots, 10 mg of cadmium chloride and 4.4 mg of thiourea were accurately weighed. Initially, 10 mL of distilled water was transferred into a round-bottom flask, which was subsequently placed in an oil bath on a heater until the reaction temperature reached 100 °C. To minimize water loss due to evaporation during the heating process, the neck of the round-bottom flask was sealed. Upon reaching the target temperature of 100 °C, the pre-measured cadmium chloride was introduced into the water. Under alkaline conditions, thiourea acts as a sulfur precursor and undergoes hydrolysis and stepwise decomposition to release sulfide species. In basic media, thiourea first forms thiocarbamate intermediates, which subsequently decompose to generate reactive sulfide ions (S²⁻/HS⁻). These sulfide species then react with cadmium ions in solution to form CdS nuclei according to the reaction:

The alkaline environment facilitates the generation of sulfide ions from thiourea and promotes the nucleation of CdS quantum dots. Prior to the addition of thiourea, the pH of the reaction mixture was adjusted to 11 using a 3 M sodium hydroxide solution, after which the thiourea was added. The resulting reaction mixture was then refluxed for 2 h on the heater with the aid of a condenser, water flow, and continuous nitrogen gas flow. Following the reaction, the solution underwent centrifugation, and after three washes with distilled water, a precipitate of CdS QDs was obtained. To determine the concentration of the quantum dots, the precipitate was transferred onto a watch glass, placed in an oven, and subsequently dried.

Coating Cadmium sulfide Quantum Dots with alcoholic extract of the Heracleum persicum

CdS QDs were coated using an ethanolic extract obtained from Persian hogweed through the interaction of cadmium chloride and thiourea under basic pH conditions, elevated temperature, and a nitrogen gas atmosphere. To initiate the coating process, 10 mL of the alcoholic extract of Persian hogweed was introduced into a round-bottom flask, which was then sealed and placed in an oil bath set to 100 °C. After reaching the desired temperature, 10 mg of cadmium chloride was added to the plant extract. Subsequently, the pH was adjusted to 11 by adding 3 M NaOH, followed by the addition of 4.4 mg of thiourea to the mixture. The reaction was then subjected to reflux. Throughout the 2-h reaction period, the mixture was continuously stirred using a magnetic stirrer, and the setup was equipped with a condenser and water flow while nitrogen gas was continuously supplied, maintaining the temperature at 100 °C. Following the completion of the reaction, the resulting solution was centrifuged to isolate the precipitate, which was subsequently washed three times with distilled water. This process produced CdS QDs coated with the alcoholic extract of Persian hogweed. For concentration measurement, the coated quantum dots were transferred to a watch glass and dried in an oven.

Coating Cadmium sulfide Quantum Dots with alcoholic extract of the Quercus infectoria

To coat the CdS QDs, 10 mL of ethanolic extract derived from Quercus infectoria was added to a round-bottom flask. This flask was subsequently sealed and positioned in an oil bath on a heating apparatus to attain a temperature of 100 °C. Upon reaching the target temperature, 10 mg of cadmium chloride was added to the oak extract. The pH of the reaction was then adjusted to 11 by the gradual addition of 3 M sodium hydroxide. Following this, 4.4 mg of thiourea was incorporated into the reaction mixture. The reaction mixture was maintained under reflux conditions with a condenser, continuous water flow, and nitrogen gas, along with magnetic stirring for a duration of 2 h at 100 °C. After the 2-hour reaction period, the solution was subjected to centrifugation. Following this, the precipitate of CdS QDs coated with the alcoholic oak extract was obtained after performing three wash cycles with distilled water.

Characterizations

UV–Visible spectroscopy using a UV–Visible spectrophotometer

A UV–Visible spectrophotometer allows qualitative and quantitative analysis based on the Beer–Lambert Law (A = ε.C.L), which states that absorbance is proportional to molar absorptivity, concentration, and path length. This method is sensitive, accurate, and non-invasive. In this study, a T80 spectrophotometer was used to identify and compare the optical properties of the synthesized quantum dots.

Fourier transform infrared (FT-IR) spectroscopy

Infrared spectroscopy is used to identify substances based on their functional groups, producing a unique molecular fingerprint for each compound. The peaks observed in the spectrum correspond to specific chemical bonds, with each peak representing absorption intensity at its characteristic wavenumber22. For this research, Fourier Transform Infrared (FT-IR) spectroscopy was conducted utilizing a Bruker Tensor 27 spectrometer.

X-ray diffraction (XRD) technique

XRD analysis was conducted using a Bruker D8 Advance instrument to determine the crystal structure of the synthesized CdS quantum dots and to compare them with the extract‑coated samples. This technique distinguishes crystalline materials, which produce characteristic diffraction patterns, from amorphous structures that generate scattered signals.

Dynamic light scattering (DLS)

Determination of quantum dot size using DLS

DLS analysis was performed at room temperature using a Zetasizer Nano ZS90 to determine the hydrodynamic size distribution of CdS quantum dots and coated samples. Before measurement, the samples were dispersed in distilled water and sonicated to reduce aggregation and ensure uniform dispersion. The particle size was calculated from the intensity distribution of scattered light based on Brownian motion, and the reported values represent the average hydrodynamic diameter.

Investigation of the Zeta Potential of Quantum Dots

Zeta potential measurements were performed at room temperature using a Zetasizer Nano ZS90 to evaluate the surface charge and colloidal stability of the nanoparticles. The samples were diluted in deionized water to obtain suitable conductivity, placed in disposable cuvettes, and analyzed. This measurement allowed direct comparison of the electrostatic stability between uncoated CdS QDs and those coated with alcoholic plant extracts.

Morphological characterization of Quantum Dots via scanning electron microscopy (SEM)

SEM is widely regarded as a fundamental technique in microscopy. The surface morphology of the synthesized CdS quantum dots and the coated nanoparticles was examined using SEM. For sample preparation, a small amount of the nanoparticle suspension was dropped onto a clean glass substrate and allowed to dry at room temperature. The dried samples were then sputter‑coated with a thin gold layer to improve electrical conductivity prior to imaging. SEM images at different magnifications were obtained to evaluate particle morphology, distribution, and approximate size. In the present study, following the preparation of quantum dot samples, their morphological characteristics were examined using a TESCAN MIRA 3 scanning electron microscope (SEM).

Assessment of hemocompatibility of quantum dots

Hemocompatibility of the nanoparticles was assessed using a hemolysis assay. Fresh human red blood cells were collected and washed several times with PBS to remove plasma components. The erythrocyte suspension was then incubated with different concentrations (50, 100, 200, and 400 µg/mL) of the nanoparticles at 37 °C for a predetermined time. After incubation, the samples were centrifuged (15 min at 13,500 rpm), and the absorbance of the supernatant was measured using a UV–visible spectrophotometer. The percentage of hemolysis was determined by comparing the absorbance of treated samples with that of the positive (complete hemolysis) and negative (PBS) controls. The percentage of hemolysis for each concentration was then calculated using a standard formula. Finally, a comparative analysis of the hemolysis percentages between the equivalent dilutions of CdS QDs and those coated with hydroethanolic extracts was performed.

Cell culture

To culture and maintain HFF-2 cells, a complete growth medium was prepared under sterile conditions, consisting of DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin/streptomycin. The components were mixed in a laminar flow hood and stored at 4 °C until needed. For the thawing of cells, a cryovial containing HFF-2 cells was removed from a -80 °C freezer and immediately placed in a 37 °C water bath for rapid thawing. This step is essential because DMSO becomes cytotoxic at temperatures above 4 °C. Following thawing, the cell suspension was handled in sterile conditions, diluted with pre-warmed culture medium, and transferred into a new tube. After centrifugation at 1700 rpm for five minutes, the supernatant was discarded, and the cell pellet was resuspended in medium containing 20% FBS and devoid of antibiotics. The cells were seeded into a T75 flask and incubated at 37 °C in a humidified incubator. To mitigate cell death due to nutrient depletion or overconfluency, regular passaging of the cells was performed. The old medium was removed, and the cell monolayer was washed twice with PBS to eliminate residual serum, which can inhibit the activity of trypsin. A trypsin-EDTA solution was introduced and gently distributed by tilting the flask. After a brief incubation at 37 °C, the flask was tapped to dislodge any remaining adherent cells. Once detachment was confirmed microscopically, FBS-containing medium was added to neutralize the trypsin. The cell suspension was gently pipetted to ensure a homogeneous suspension, transferred to a Falcon tube, and centrifuged at 1700 rpm for seven minutes. The supernatant was discarded, and the pellet was resuspended in fresh medium proportional to the cell density before being reseeded into a new flask. Cultures were monitored daily for growth and contamination. Before cryopreservation, cells were examined microscopically to confirm their viability and the absence of contamination. Only cultures exhibiting over 80% confluency were selected for freezing. The cells were trypsinized, centrifuged, and resuspended in a freezing medium consisting of 95% complete culture medium and 5% DMSO. This formulation allows for a gradual freezing rate and minimizes ice crystal formation, thereby reducing cellular damage. One milliliter of the cell suspension was aliquoted into cryovials, which were sequentially placed at -20 °C for 30 min, followed by transfer to -80 °C overnight. Finally, the vials were stored in liquid nitrogen for long-term preservation.

MTT assay

Cell viability was assessed using the MTT assay, which relies on the enzymatic reduction of the yellow tetrazolium salt MTT into purple formazan crystals by mitochondrial enzymes in viable cells. HFF‑2 human foreskin fibroblast cells were harvested at 80% confluency using trypsin and centrifuged at 1700 rpm for 7 min. The resulting cell pellet was resuspended in 1 mL of complete culture medium. Approximately 7,000 cells were seeded per well of a 96‑well plate containing 100 µL of culture medium and incubated at 37 °C in a 5% CO₂ atmosphere until cell attachment occurred. The medium was then replaced with serial dilutions of CdS QDs, CdS QDs coated with hydroethanolic Quercus infectoria extract, and CdS QDs coated with hydroethanolic Heracleum persicum extract. Each dilution was tested in five replicates. After treatment, the medium was removed, and 15 µL of MTT solution (5 mg/mL) was added to each well, followed by incubation for 4 h at 37 °C with 5% CO₂. Subsequently, 100 µL of DMSO was added to dissolve the formazan crystals, and the plate was shaken for 15 min. Absorbance at 570 nm was measured using a spectrophotometer to determine cell viability.

Statistical Analysis

All experiments were conducted in triplicate, and the results are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical analysis was performed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) to assess differences among the experimental groups. A p-value of less than 0.05 was deemed statistically significant.

Results

UV/Vis spectrum of synthesized Quantum Dots and Quantum Dots coated with alcoholic extract of Heracleum persicum(HP)

In the first step, the synthesis of CdS QDs as well as the successful coating of these quantum dots with HP hydroethanolic extract, was verified. This was accomplished by plotting and comparing the spectrophotometric spectra of three samples: CdS QDs, CdS QDs coated with HP alcoholic extract, and the HP extract itself. The UV/Vis spectrum analysis of CdS QDs reveals a prominent peak at approximately 440 nm, indicating successful synthesis of these quantum dots23. Additionally, the spectrum of the alcoholic extract of HP exhibits a sharp peak at 258 nm, accompanied by a broad peak centered at 320 nm. Upon analyzing the UV/Vis spectrum of CdS QDs coated with HP extract, a prominent peak centered at 442 nm is observed above 400 nm. This observation corroborates the synthesis of the quantum dots. Additionally, the spectrum reveals a broad peak around 332 nm, as well as a distinct, sharp peak at 246 nm. These spectral features indicate effective coating of the CdS QDs with the HP alcoholic extract. The UV–Vis spectra presented in Fig. 1 provide important information about the optical properties of the synthesized CdS quantum dots and their modification with the HP extract. The absorption peak observed around 440 nm corresponds to the characteristic excitonic transition of CdS quantum dots, confirming their successful formation. The spectrum of the plant extract exhibits absorption bands in the ultraviolet region, which are associated with the presence of various phytochemical compounds such as phenolic and flavonoid structures. After coating the CdS QDs with the plant extract, slight shifts in the absorption peaks and changes in spectral intensity are observed. These changes suggest interactions between the functional groups of the extract and the surface of the quantum dots, indicating successful surface modification and stabilization of the nanoparticles.

X‑ray diffraction analysis of the synthesized quantum dots

The analysis of XRD patterns for CdS QDs coated with the ethanolic extract of HP is presented in Figs. 5. The XRD pattern of the synthesized CdS quantum dots shows several characteristic diffraction peaks at approximately 2θ ≈ 25.1°, 37.8°, 41.9°, 43.9°, 53.4°, 55.8°, and 60.5°, which can be indexed to the (101), (011), (200), (111), (112), (211), and (103) planes of crystalline CdS, respectively. The positions of these reflections are consistent with the standard diffraction data for CdS reported in the JCPDS database (card No. 41‑1049). After coating, peak intensities decreased slightly, indicating partial masking of crystalline domains by phytochemical layers without altering the crystal structure26. A comparative analysis of the XRD patterns shows minimal changes after coating with the ethanolic extract of HP; the primary effects noted are a slight shift in peak positions and a decrease in their heights attributed to the coating.

Hemolysis assay of the synthesized quantum dots

A hemocompatibility test was conducted to evaluate and compare the toxicity of CdS QDs, CdS QDs coated with HP extract, and CdS QDs coated with QI extract. The results are shown in Fig. 13. Deionized water was employed as a positive control, while PBS served as a negative control. As shown in Fig. 13, CdS QDs exhibit the highest hemolytic activity among the tested formulations, although all values remain below the 5% safety threshold, indicating acceptable but less favorable blood compatibility. CdS@HP QDs show the lowest hemolysis at all concentrations, making them the most blood‑compatible option. CdS@QI QDs exhibit slightly higher hemolysis than HP‑coated QDs at higher concentrations, but the values remain within safe limits, suggesting suitability for systemic use with minor caution. Evaluation and comparative analysis of the toxicity of the synthesized quantum dots on the HFF‑2 cell line.

Following the synthesis of CdS QDs and their coating with the alcoholic extract of HP at various concentrations, their cytotoxicity on the normal HFF‑2 cell line was examined. As illustrated in Fig. 14, CdS QDs exhibit significant cytotoxic effects that increase with concentration, leading to a marked reduction in cell viability at higher concentrations. Conversely, CdS@QI QDs reduce toxicity to some extent, although a significant decline in cell viability is still observed at elevated doses. In comparison, CdS@HP QDs show the highest cell viability across all tested concentrations, consistently surpassing both bare and QI-coated QDs. Most differences within the CdS@HP group are not statistically significant, which suggests improved cellular biocompatibility and a more effective reduction of CdS-induced cytotoxicity. Discussion

The investigation into green synthesis methods for nanoparticles, particularly CdS quantum dots, has garnered considerable interest due to its implications for reducing cytotoxicity while preserving therapeutic efficacy, which is a central focus of the present study. In 2018, Shivaji and colleagues conducted a green synthesis of CdS quantum dots utilizing tea leaf extract (Camellia sinensis), revealing reduced cytotoxicity on normal cells alongside significant anticancer activity against lung cancer cells (A549). This research underscores the potential of plant‑based coatings to enhance the safety of CdS quantum dots, aligning with the current study’s use of Quercus infectoria and Heracleum persicum extracts to improve biocompatibility15. Further contributing to this field, in 2021, Naranthatta and colleagues synthesized CdS nanoparticles using plant leaf extracts, demonstrating reduced toxicity and augmented anticancer effects on human cell lines. Their findings support the efficacy of plant-derived coatings in reducing the inherent toxicity of CdS cores, a vital consideration for safe biomedical applications. This is particularly relevant to the current study, where coatings of Quercus infectoria and Heracleum persicum are used to achieve comparable biocompatibility on HFF‑2 cells28. Additionally, in 2019, Garmanchuk and colleagues investigated green-synthesized CdS quantum dots, reporting lower cytotoxicity in comparison to chemically synthesized counterparts and minimal effects on cell adhesion in human cell lines. This research provides a direct basis for evaluating cytotoxicity on HFF‑2 cells in the present study, highlighting the advantages of green synthesis for mitigating toxic effects while maintaining functional integrity29. The specific utilization of Quercus infectoria extracts is bolstered by a 2020 study conducted by Yusof and colleagues, which illustrated significant anticancer activity of its ethyl acetate extracts against HeLa cells, coupled with low toxicity to normal cells. This indicates that Quercus infectoria not only enhances biocompatibility but also offers bioactive properties. thus supporting its selection as a coating for CdS quantum dots in the current research30. In a related development, Hanachi and colleagues, in 2022, employed Heracleum persicum stem extract for the green synthesis of silver nanoparticles, reporting reduced toxicity to normal cells and substantial anticancer activity through apoptosis. These findings reinforce the feasibility of using Heracleum persicum as a coating material. corresponding with its application in this study to diminish CdS quantum dot toxicity on HFF2 cells31. Moreover, research conducted by Mohammadi and colleagues in 2025 focused on the synthesis of silver sulfide quantum dots using tarragon extract, observing low cytotoxicity and high biocompatibility on HFF2 cells. This work is directly relevant to the current evaluation of CdS quantum dots on the same cell line, further substantiating the hypothesis that plant-based coatings can alleviate toxicity while preserving therapeutic potential9. A broader context is offered by a 2021 review by Dabhane and colleagues, which highlighted the reduced cytotoxicity and enhanced biomedical applications of green-synthesized CdS nanoparticles, particularly in the realm of cancer therapy. This comprehensive analysis situates the current study’s approach within a wider framework, linking the utilization of plant extracts like Quercus infectoria and Heracleum persicum to an expanding body of evidence advocating for green synthesis in the pursuit of safer nanoparticle applications32. The collected evidence indicates a consistent trend: the utilization of green synthesis with plant extracts reduces the cytotoxicity linked to heavy metal-based nanoparticles, such as CdS, while simultaneously enhancing their therapeutic potential in cancer treatments. The inclusion of Quercus infectoria and Heracleum persicum in this study builds on this foundation, utilizing their bioactive properties to improve the safety profile of CdS quantum dots on HFF2 cells, in accordance with the broader objective of promoting biocompatible nanomaterials for medical applications.

The cytotoxicity of CdS quantum dots is predominantly linked to mechanisms of oxidative stress, including the generation of intracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS), mitochondrial dysfunction, membrane damage, and the gradual release of Cd²⁺ ions. These mechanisms can lead to lipid peroxidation, protein oxidation, inflammatory responses, and DNA damage in affected cells, ultimately compromising cellular viability. Moreover, direct interactions between the surfaces of nanoparticles and erythrocyte membranes may play a role in hemolytic activity. The observed reduction in toxicity following coating with Heracleum persicum and Quercus infectoria extracts may be attributed to the presence of antioxidant phytochemicals, such as phenolic and flavonoid compounds. These compounds can stabilize the nanoparticle surface, diminish cadmium ion leakage, enhance colloidal stability, and lessen direct interactions between the nanoparticles and cells. These results underscore the potential of plant-derived surface modifications in enhancing the biological compatibility of CdS-based nanomaterials.

Characterization discussion

The UV–Vis absorption spectra confirmed the successful formation of CdS quantum dots, which exhibited optical features characteristic of semiconductor nanostructures governed by quantum confinement effects. Compared with bulk CdS materials, the observed absorption behavior indicates nanoscale particle formation. After coating with Heracleum persicum and Quercus infectoria extracts, slight variations in the absorption profile were observed. These changes can be attributed to interactions between surface phytochemicals and the nanoparticle surface, which may influence the local dielectric environment and surface electronic states. Similar optical modifications have been reported in plant-mediated CdS quantum dots, where biomolecular capping agents alter surface states and optical responses.

FTIR analysis further confirmed the surface modification of CdS quantum dots. The appearance of absorption bands associated with functional groups such as hydroxyl and carbonyl groups suggests the presence of phytochemical compounds from the plant extracts on the nanoparticle surface. These functional groups likely originate from phenolic compounds, flavonoids, and other biomolecules present in the extracts. Their presence indicates that the plant extracts function not only as coating agents but also as stabilizing and capping agents, preventing particle aggregation and enhancing colloidal stability. Comparable FTIR signatures have been widely reported in green-synthesized nanoparticles where plant-derived molecules act as surface stabilizers.

The XRD patterns demonstrated that the synthesized CdS quantum dots possess a crystalline structure consistent with CdS nanomaterials. The main diffraction peaks correspond to crystalline CdS, confirming successful nanoparticle formation. Importantly, the diffraction pattern remained largely unchanged after coating with plant extracts, indicating that the organic coating layer did not disrupt the crystal structure of the CdS core. This suggests that the extracts mainly modified the nanoparticle surface while preserving the internal crystalline framework. Maintaining crystallinity while modifying surface chemistry is essential for preserving the optical and functional properties of semiconductor quantum dots.

SEM analysis provided further insight into nanoparticle morphology. The images revealed nanoscale particles with relatively uniform distribution. In comparison with bare CdS nanoparticles, the extract-coated samples exhibited improved dispersion and reduced aggregation. This observation is consistent with the presence of organic molecules on the nanoparticle surface that act as steric stabilizers. Reduced aggregation is particularly important for biological applications because it enhances dispersion in aqueous environments and may contribute to improved biocompatibility.

DLS measurements revealed hydrodynamic diameters of approximately 258 nm for CdS@HP and 253 nm for CdS@QI. These values are larger than the expected core nanoparticle size because DLS measures the hydrodynamic diameter of particles in solution, which includes the hydration layer and surface-bound biomolecules. The increase in hydrodynamic size, therefore, supports the successful coating of CdS nanoparticles with plant-derived compounds. The discrepancies in particle sizes observed between DLS analysis and SEM imaging are anticipated. DLS quantifies the hydrodynamic diameter of nanoparticles in suspension, accounting for both the hydration layer and adsorbed biomolecules, whereas SEM exclusively captures the physical dimensions of the nanoparticle core in a dry state. Furthermore, the occurrence of partial nanoparticle aggregation in aqueous media may additionally influence the larger hydrodynamic sizes reported by DLS. Furthermore, the zeta potential shifted from − 22.4 mV for bare CdS to − 25.0 mV and − 23.9 mV for CdS@HP and CdS@QI, respectively. This increase in negative surface charge suggests improved colloidal stability due to electrostatic repulsion between particles. Similar zeta potential behavior has been reported in plant-extract-mediated nanoparticle systems, where phenolic and carboxyl-containing compounds contribute to enhanced surface charge and stability.

Overall, the combined UV–Vis, FTIR, XRD, SEM, and DLS analyses collectively confirm the successful synthesis of CdS quantum dots and their effective surface modification by plant extracts. The results demonstrate that plant-derived biomolecules play an important role in stabilizing the nanoparticles, improving dispersion, and potentially enhancing their biological compatibility.

Despite the promising findings obtained in this study, several limitations warrant attention. Advanced structural characterization techniques, such as transmission electron microscopy (TEM) and high-resolution transmission electron microscopy (HRTEM), were not employed to further verify particle size distribution and lattice structure at the atomic level. Additionally, photoluminescence spectroscopy and quantum yield analysis were not conducted, which restricts a more thorough evaluation of the optical behavior and quantum confinement properties of the synthesized cadmium sulfide quantum dots. Moreover, the biological evaluation was confined to hemolysis and MTT assays on HFF-2 cells, necessitating further mechanistic studies that involve oxidative stress markers, apoptosis pathways, inflammatory responses, and the long-term stability of nanoparticles to comprehensively assess biomedical safety and functionality.

Conclusion

CdS QDs were synthesized through the reaction of cadmium chloride and thiourea at a temperature of 100 degrees Celsius, under alkaline pH conditions, and with the introduction of nitrogen gas. Additionally, to coat the CdS QDs, a hydroethanolic extract of the HP plant was added to the reaction following the incorporation of thiourea, thereby facilitating the coating of the quantum dots with the hydroethanolic extract of HP. Similarly, the coating process was also carried out using the hydroethanolic extract of the QI plant. After the synthesis of the CdS QDs and their coating with the alcoholic extracts of HP and QI, various characterization techniques, including UV-Vis, FT-IR, XRD, DLS, and SEM, were employed to identify, examine, and compare the properties of the uncoated quantum dots with those that had undergone coating. UV/Vis spectroscopy has confirmed the synthesis and coating of CdS QDs. The CdS QDs exhibited a broad absorption peak in the range of 400–500 nm, while the QI extract showed peaks between 200 and 300 nm, and the HP extract displayed peaks at 250 and 320 nm. Upon coating, the CdS@QI and CdS@HP QDs retained the characteristic CdS peak along with the peaks related to the extracts, indicating successful coating. FT-IR analysis supported these findings; the CdS QDs demonstrated a Cd–S band at 640 cm⁻¹, whereas the CdS@HP QDs exhibited both this band and a furanocoumarin band from the HP extract at 1050 cm⁻¹. The CdS@QI QDs showed the Cd–S bond along with hydroxyl, ether, and carbonyl bands from the QI extract, although some of these bands were diminished, further verifying the coating. XRD results indicated that bare CdS, CdS@HP, and CdS@QI corresponded with the standard CdS pattern. There were slight peak shifts for CdS@HP and broader peaks for CdS@QI, which suggest interactions with HP and reduced crystallinity with QI. DLS measurements revealed hydrodynamic sizes at the nanoscale, with measurements of 258 nm for CdS@HP and 253 nm for CdS@QI. The zeta potential shifted from − 22.4 mV for bare CdS to -25.0 mV for CdS@HP and − 23.9 mV for CdS@QI, indicating enhanced colloidal stability attributed to functional groups in the extracts. SEM imaging revealed that both CdS@HP and CdS@QI QDs were uniformly spherical, approximately 40 nm in size, and evenly distributed without aggregation, confirming successful synthesis and coating. For further investigation, a hemolysis assay and an MTT assay were conducted. Based on the results, CdS QDs show low levels of biocompatibility, exhibiting significant cytotoxic effects and heightened hemolytic activity, which renders them unsuitable for biomedical applications without modifications. In contrast, CdS@HP QDs represent the most effective formulation, providing robust protection in both cellular and blood contexts. These QDs consistently exhibited higher cell viability across all tested concentrations and exhibited reduced hemolysis, suggesting improved systemic safety. CdS@QI QDs also demonstrated enhanced biocompatibility compared to uncoated CdS QDs, displaying good cellular tolerance and satisfactory blood compatibility. Nonetheless, their slightly increased hemolytic response compared to HP-coated QDs suggests a need for further surface optimization, especially for intravenous use. These results highlight the importance of plant‑based coatings in reducing the toxicity of CdS QDs, with HP extract yielding the most significant improvements in both cytocompatibility and hemocompatibility. Among the formulations examined, CdS@HP exhibited the most advantageous equilibrium among nanoparticle stability, hemocompatibility, and cellular biocompatibility. This finding indicates that Heracleum persicum extract may function as a more efficacious phytochemical coating for mitigating the toxicity associated with CdS. In vitro assays, including MTT and hemolysis tests, offer valuable preliminary insights into the cytotoxicity and blood compatibility of quantum dot formulations. However, these methods do not fully capture the complexities of biological systems. To thoroughly assess the safety, biodistribution, and therapeutic potential of these nanomaterials, future research should incorporate in vivo studies. Such studies are essential for evaluating systemic responses, organ-specific accumulation, clearance pathways, and potential immunogenicity in physiological environments. The integration of in vivo models will facilitate a more accurate translation of findings toward clinical applications and help confirm the biocompatibility observed in vitro.
