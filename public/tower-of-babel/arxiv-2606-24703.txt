Scheduling jobs with unknown size distribution in a M/G/1 queue: the shifted empirical Gittins
Nicolas Gast, Bruno Gaujal and Adrien Obrecht1. Univ. Grenoble Alpes, Inria, CNRS, LIG, F-38000 Grenoble, France.2. ENS de Lyon, Lyon, France

Abstract
In this paper we consider a M/G/1 queue for which we want to minimize the expected response time. We show how to compute indices from n n samples of the job size distribution such that the corresponding index policy is asymptotically optimal as n n grows. This construction is based on a discretization of the bounded support of the job size distribution and a shift of the samples to their nearest discrete point to the right. We show that the Gittins index of the empirical distribution of these shifted samples is close to the Gittins index of the original distribution. This translates to the asymptotic optimality of the corresponding index policy for minimizing the expected response time. Numerical comparison with other approaches further confirm the efficiency of our approach.



1
Introduction

In this paper, we focus on the problem of scheduling jobs in a M/G/1 queue to minimize their mean response time under the following conditions.

•

All jobs have the same size distribution (technical conditions on the distribution will be discussed later),

•

jobs arrive according to a Poisson process,

•

jobs are executed by a single server that can switch from one job to another instantaneously.

This problem has drawn a lot of interest recently for several reasons. Indeed, the solutions under different information levels on the job sizes are quite elegant and they are enlightening on what features are crucial to obtain small response times.

•

In the
clairvoyant case
(jobs announce their size when they arrive in the
queue), the optimal scheduling policy is SRPT (Smallest Remaining Processing Time) (see
[
5
]
for a proof).

•

In the
non-clairvoyant case
, (the size of the job is only revealed at completion time), the optimal scheduling policy is the famous Gittins index policy (see
[
6
]
for a detailed proof) when the distribution of the job sizes is known in advance.

•

In this paper, we consider the
non clairoyant black box case
: The size of the job is only known at completion time and the distribution of the job sizes is also unknown.

Our approach to solve this problem is of the “
explore then commit
” type: We first observe
n
samples of job sizes and infer a scheduling policy that becomes asymptotically optimal as the number of samples
n
goes to infinity.
The main challenge comes from the fact that the empirical distribution induced by
n
samples is made of
n
atoms all this equal probability. This is quite distinct from the actual distribution of jobs when it is continuous w.r.t. the Lebesgue measure.
To overcome this difficulty, one may infer an empirical density from the
n
samples, for example using a Gaussian kernel (see
[
4
]
for example) and compute the Gittins indexes for this estimated density. Unfortunately, the special shape of the Gittins indexes is highly sensitive to the distribution and this direct approach is not very efficient, as seen in our numerical tests (Section
6
).
In this paper, we use a different approach by introducing a transformation on the samples that we call the “right shift” that is compatible with the way index policies work and this is a key ingredient in the proof of our main result, namely asymptotic optimality of the learned index policy.

When finalizing this manuscript, we found that the paper
[
3
]
has just been published. This paper provides a convergence analysis of the empirical Gittins policy without the need to resort to a shifted index.

2
Problem description

We consider jobs that arrive in an online fashion on a queue with a single processing node,
with an associated execution time (denoted
X_{i}
for job number
i
). At each given point, the processor
can execute a single job at speed one. Once a
job
i
has been executed for
X_{i}
units of time, it is considered finished and leaves the system.
We denote by
T_{i}
the response time of job
i
, namely the difference between its completion time and its arrival time. Our
goal will to minimize the expected mean response time of the jobs.

We also suppose that jobs
arrive following a Poisson arrival process of rate
\lambda
and that their
size
X
follows a distribution
\varphi
. We denote by
f
the probability density function (PDF), when it exists, by
F
the cumulative distribution function (CDF) and by
\bar{F}
the complementary CDF (
\bar{F}:=1-F
) of the job size.
The processor works at speed one and can only execute one job at a time. The job being currently executed is called the
active
job.
The
age

a
of a job is the total amount of time the processor has been executing the job.
The set of ages of a job is partitioned into two sets,
\mathcal{X}_{\mbox{\tiny Pre}}
and
\mathcal{X}_{\mbox{\tiny NonPre}}
, where the job can be preempted and cannot be preempted, respectively.

1
Gittins Index, Gittins index policy.

The Gittins index function at age
x
of a job with size
measure
\varphi
and remaining probability function
\bar{F}
is

\nu(x):=\sup_{y>x}\frac{\mathbb{P}(X\leqslant y|X>x)}{\mathbb{E}(\min(X,y)-x|X>x)}=\sup_{y>x}\frac{\int_{x}^{y}d\varphi(u)}{\int_{x}^{y}\bar{F}(u)du}.

(1)

The corresponding Gittins index policy works as follows:
Whenever the age
x
of the current active job is preemptable (
x\in\mathcal{X}_{\mbox{\tiny Pre}}
), the Gittins index policy activates one job with the maximal current Gittins index.

The Gittins index function is right-continuous, but may not be continuous.
Note that when
\varphi
contains an atom (say at age
x_{0}
,) then
\lim_{x\to x_{0},x<x_{0}}\nu(x)=+\infty
, and
\lim_{x\to x_{0},x>x_{0}}\nu(x)=\nu(x_{0})
.
The Gittins index policy is known to be optimal for minimizing the expected response time of the jobs when the only information available on the jobs is their ages. Several versions of this result exist in the litterature. The most recent and general proof is given in
[
6
]
. This result is very strong: for any job in the queue, the Gittins index of a job is a function that only depends on its age (and not on the other jobs, nor on the preemption set) and yet, it completely characterizes which job to schedule first in an optimal fashion. However, the indices can only be computed when the distribution
\varphi
is known. In this work we show how to define an approximate value of the Gittins indices while only having samples from
\varphi
.

2.1
Contributiuon and positioning

In this paper, we propose a way to estimate the Gittins index function based on
n
independent job samples and show that the corresponding Gittins index policy becomes optimal when
n
goes to infinity.
We will leverage the definition of Gittins index to construct an
ad hoc
estimated distribution function and
ad hoc
estimated indices that make the corresponding policy close to the Gittins index policy.

Our approach works in two stages: we first fix the set of ages where jobs can be preempted as
\mathcal{X}_{\mbox{\tiny Pre}}^{\Delta,\varepsilon}:=\{\frac{i}{\Delta}<1-\varepsilon,i\in\mathbb{N},\}
, where
\Delta\in\mathbb{N}
is a free parameter and
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
.
Therefore, in our first framework, the scheduling decisions are reexamined every
1/\Delta
units of time (here the processor has speed one) or when the active job finishes. We show (Section
5.1
) that when
n
grows, the estimated Gittins index policy becomes optimal under this
\Delta
-preemption case.

While this result has some interest by its own, the second step (Section
5.2
) shows how to let
\Delta
grow as
n
goes to infinity so that
the preemption set becomes dense in the set of ages. n this case, the estimated Gittins index policy becomes optimal without any restriction on the preemptive set.

3
Discretization and right shift

The main idea that underlies our estimated index is based on the construction of a shifted distribution. In this section, we consider any distribution with a support included in
\mathbb{R}_{+}
the set of non-negative real numbers and we discretize it using an integer parameter
\Delta\in\mathbb{N}
.

3.1
Right-shifted distribution

We first define a shifted discretization of the measure
\varphi
: we denote by
\varphi_{\Delta}
the
shifted version of
\varphi
, where the new distribution is atomic with mass at points that are multiples of
1/\Delta
.
We will use the handy notations
\lfloor t\rfloor_{\Delta}
and
\lceil t\rceil_{\Delta}
to be the greatest (resp. smallest) multiple of
1/\Delta
smaller (resp. greater) than
t
;

\displaystyle\lfloor t\rfloor_{\Delta}:=\frac{\lfloor t\Delta\rfloor}{\Delta},\text{ and }\lceil t\rceil_{\Delta}:=\frac{\lceil t\Delta\rceil}{\Delta}.

We also use a strict version of the floor operator:

\lfloor\!\lfloor t\rfloor\!\rfloor_{\Delta}:=\frac{\lceil t\Delta\rceil-1}{\Delta}.

2
shifted discrete distribution.

Let
\Delta\in\mathbb{N}
and let
\varphi
be any positive measure on
\mathbb{R}
.
The measure
\varphi_{\Delta}
is defined by its value on any interval
[u,v]
:

\varphi_{\Delta}(\big[u,v\big]):=\varphi(\big[\lfloor\!\lfloor u\rfloor\!\rfloor_{\Delta},\lfloor v\rfloor_{\Delta}\big]).

A more operational view of
\varphi_{\Delta}
is
\forall t
,

\displaystyle\varphi_{\Delta}(t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\delta_{i/\Delta}(t)\varphi\left(\left(\frac{i-1}{\Delta},\frac{i}{\Delta}\right]\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\delta_{i/\Delta}(t)\int_{\frac{(i-1)^{+}}{\Delta}}^{\frac{i}{\Delta}}d\varphi(s)

where the integral
\int_{\frac{(i-1)^{+}}{\Delta}}^{\frac{i}{\Delta}}d\varphi(s)
excludes any atom at
(i-1)/\Delta
and includes any atom at
i/\Delta
. For a given
x\in\mathbb{R}
,
\delta_{x}
denotes the Dirac distribution in
x
.

We can bound the cumulated distribution function
F_{\Delta}
of this shifted distribution by the original cumulated distribution
F
.

1
.

\forall{\Delta}\in\mathbb{N},\forall t\in\mathbb{R}_{+}F_{\Delta}(t)\leqslant F(t)
.

Proof.

This is a quite straightforward property: Let
{\Delta}\in\mathbb{N}
and
t\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
.

\displaystyle F_{\Delta}(t)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}d\varphi_{\Delta}(t)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{\Delta}\delta_{i/\Delta}(t)\int_{\frac{i-1}{\Delta}}^{\frac{i}{\Delta}}d\varphi(s)\right)dt

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{\Delta}\int_{0}^{t}\left(\delta_{i/\Delta}(t)\int_{\frac{i-1}{\Delta}}^{\frac{i}{\Delta}}d\varphi(s)\right)dt

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\lfloor t\rfloor_{\Delta}}\varphi(s)

\displaystyle\leqslant\int_{0}^{t}d\varphi(s)=F(t).

∎

One could wonder how much of a negative impact the replacement of the real Gittins function by its shifted sample version can have on of the resulting scheduling policy. We will show that the impact is minimal, and decreases as a function of
{\Delta}
in the next section. Actually shifting the samples to the right is designed to reduce the impact on the policy. Here some high level intuition on why shifting samples to the right is a good idea. The right shift can be thought as overestimating the duration of jobs. Now if a job is selected to be executed at time
t
because its index is large, then any premature completion due to its real size can only be a good thing for the response time. On the other hand, shifting the samples to the left would have a much bigger negative impact: think of a distribution with two atoms, one at
A

1
, where
A
is not a multiple of
1/\Delta
. Shifting the samples from
A

A

A
. The corresponding index policy will stop the execution of the current job before time
A
, missing an opportunity to complete the job (if the job is of size
A
). Similar observations were done in
[
2

to construct a robust scheduling for Gittins indices.

4
Convergence of the shifted empirical index

The goal of this section is to establish relations between the various Gittins index functions. We show that the shifted empirical index is quite close to the real Gittins index when the number of samples grows. We make the following technical assumptions on the density
f
:

1
.

The distribution
\varphi
has a density
f
(no atoms) and has the following properties.

•

The support of
f
is
[0,1]
.

•

\forall t\in[0,1],f(t)\geqslant\ell
.

•

f
is a
L
-Lipschitz continuous function.

This assumption may look quite strong, although any distribution with finite support can be approached by a distribution satisfying this assumption. The finite support assumption is useful to get simple closed form expressions for the bounds. We believe it could be removed to the price of replacing uniform bounds by distribution dependent bounds.

2
.

Let
\varepsilon>0
and
\Delta\in\mathbb{N}
. For all
i\in\mathbb{N}
such that
\frac{i}{\Delta}<1-\varepsilon
, then:

\nu(\frac{i}{\Delta})\frac{1-\frac{L}{\Delta\ell\varepsilon}}{1+\frac{L}{\Delta\ell\varepsilon}}\leqslant\nu_{\Delta}(\frac{i}{\Delta})\leqslant\nu(\frac{i}{\Delta}).

Proof.

Right inequality:

\displaystyle\nu_{\Delta}(\frac{i}{\Delta})

\displaystyle=\sup_{t^{\prime}>\frac{i}{\Delta}}\frac{\int_{\frac{i}{\Delta}}^{t^{\prime}}f_{\Delta}(s)ds}{\int_{\frac{i}{\Delta}}^{t^{\prime}}\bar{F}_{\Delta}(s)ds}

\displaystyle\leqslant\sup_{t^{\prime}>\frac{i}{\Delta}}\frac{\int_{\frac{i}{\Delta}}^{t^{\prime}}\sum_{j=1}^{\Delta}\delta_{j/\Delta}(s)\left(\int_{\frac{j-1}{\Delta}}^{\frac{j}{\Delta}}f(u)du\right)ds}{\int_{\frac{i}{\Delta}}^{t^{\prime}}\bar{F}(s)ds}

\displaystyle\leqslant\sup_{t^{\prime}>\frac{i}{\Delta}}\frac{\int_{\frac{i}{\Delta}}^{\lfloor t^{\prime}\rfloor_{\Delta}}f(s)ds}{\int_{t}^{t^{\prime}}\bar{F}(s)ds}

\displaystyle\leqslant\sup_{t^{\prime}>\frac{i}{\Delta}}\frac{\int_{\frac{i}{\Delta}}^{t^{\prime}}f(s)ds}{\int_{t}^{t^{\prime}}\bar{F}(s)ds}

\displaystyle\leqslant\nu(\frac{i}{\Delta}).

Left inequality:

\displaystyle\nu_{\Delta}(\frac{i}{\Delta})

\displaystyle=\sup_{t^{\prime}>\frac{i}{\Delta}}\frac{\int_{\frac{i}{\Delta}}^{t^{\prime}}\sum_{j=1}^{\Delta}\delta_{j/\Delta}(s)\left(\int_{\frac{j-1}{\Delta}}^{\frac{j}{\Delta}}f(u)du\right)ds}{\int_{\frac{i}{\Delta}}^{t^{\prime}}\bar{F}_{\Delta}(s)ds}

\displaystyle\geqslant\frac{\int_{\frac{i}{\Delta}}^{\lceil t^{*}\rceil_{\Delta}}\sum_{j=1}^{\Delta}\delta_{j/\Delta}(s)\left(\int_{\frac{j-1}{\Delta}}^{\frac{j}{\Delta}}f(u)du\right)ds}{\int_{\frac{i}{\Delta}}^{\lceil t^{*}\rceil_{\Delta}}\bar{F}_{\Delta}(s)ds}

\displaystyle\geqslant\frac{\int_{\frac{i}{\Delta}}^{\lceil t^{*}\rceil_{\Delta}}f(s)ds}{\int_{\frac{i}{\Delta}}^{\lceil t^{*}\rceil_{\Delta}}\bar{F}_{\Delta}(s)ds},

where
t^{*}
achieves the supremum in the first equality.
Using
t^{*}\geqslant\frac{i}{\Delta}+\varepsilon
, we get

\displaystyle\geqslant\frac{\int_{\frac{i}{\Delta}}^{t^{*}}f(s)ds\left(1-\frac{\int_{\lceil t^{*}\rceil_{\Delta}}^{t^{*}}f(s)ds}{\int_{\frac{i}{\Delta}}^{t^{*}}f(s)ds}\right)}{\int_{\frac{i}{\Delta}}^{\lceil t^{*}\rceil_{\Delta}}(\bar{F}(s)+L\frac{1}{\Delta})ds}

\displaystyle\geqslant\frac{\int_{\frac{i}{\Delta}}^{t^{*}}f(s)ds\left(1-\frac{\int_{\lceil t^{*}\rceil_{\Delta}}^{t^{*}}f(s)ds}{\int_{\frac{i}{\Delta}}^{t^{*}}f(s)ds}\right)}{\int_{\frac{i}{\Delta}}^{\lceil t^{*}\rceil_{\Delta}}\bar{F}(s)ds\left(1+\frac{(\lceil t^{*}\rceil_{\Delta}-\frac{i}{\Delta})L\frac{1}{\Delta}}{\int_{\frac{i}{\Delta}}^{\lceil t^{*}\rceil_{\Delta}}\bar{F}(s)ds}\right)}

\displaystyle\geqslant\nu(\frac{i}{\Delta})\frac{1-\frac{\int_{\lceil t^{*}\rceil_{\Delta}}^{t^{*}}Lds}{\int_{\frac{i}{\Delta}}^{t^{*}}\ell ds}}{1+\frac{(\lceil t^{*}\rceil_{\Delta}-\frac{i}{\Delta})L\frac{1}{\Delta}}{\int_{\frac{i}{\Delta}}^{\lceil t^{*}\rceil_{\Delta}}\varepsilon\ell ds}}

\displaystyle\geqslant\nu(\frac{i}{\Delta})\frac{1-\frac{L}{\Delta\ell\varepsilon}}{1+\frac{L}{\Delta\ell\varepsilon}}.

∎

After proving that the shifted distribution gives Gittins indices close to the indices of the
real distribution, we now show that the sampled version of the indices are close to
the shifted ones. Since both density distributions
\widehat{f_{n,\Delta}}
and
f_{\Delta}
are purely atomic, both with all atoms at positions
i/\Delta
, we use the 1-Wasserstein distance that becomes the maximal difference between the size of their atoms in our case:
The size of the atom at
i/\Delta
for
\widehat{f_{n,\Delta}}
is
\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{1}{n}{\bf 1}_{\{(i-1)/\Delta<X_{k}\leqslant i/\Delta\}}
. The size of the atom at
i/\Delta
for
f_{\Delta}
is
\int_{(i-1)^{+}/\Delta}^{i/\Delta}f_{\Delta}(s)ds
.
Here, the 1-Wasserstein distance is

d(\widehat{f_{n,\Delta}},f_{\Delta}):=\max_{i=1,...,\Delta}\left|\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{1}{n}{\bf 1}_{\{(i-1)/\Delta<X_{k}\leqslant i/\Delta\}}-\int_{(i-1)^{+}/\Delta}^{i/\Delta}f_{\Delta}(s)ds\right|.

We have the following lemma:

3
.

Let
\Delta\in\mathbb{N}
. Denoting
A:=d(\widehat{f_{n,\Delta}},f_{\Delta})
. Then for all
i\in[0,\Delta]
:

\left(\frac{1-\frac{A\Delta}{L}}{1+\frac{A\Delta}{\ell}}\right)\nu_{\Delta}(\frac{i}{\Delta})\leqslant\widehat{\nu_{n,\Delta}}(\frac{i}{\Delta})\leqslant\left(\frac{1+\frac{A\Delta}{\ell}}{1-\frac{A\Delta}{L}}\right)\nu_{\Delta}(\frac{i}{\Delta}).

(2)

Proof.

We first check that
\forall i\in[1,\Delta]
, we have:

\frac{\ell}{\Delta}\leqslant\int_{\frac{i}{\Delta}}^{\frac{i+1}{\Delta}}f_{\Delta}(s)ds\leqslant\frac{L}{\Delta}.

Left part:
we simply use the fact that
f\geqslant\ell
.

Right part:

f
is
L
-lipschitz continuous and has support
[0,1]
. Thus
f\leqslant L
.
Let us now prove the right inequality in (
2
)

\displaystyle\widehat{\nu_{n,\Delta}}(\frac{i}{\Delta})

\displaystyle=\sup_{t^{\prime}>\frac{i}{\Delta}}\frac{\int_{\frac{i}{\Delta}}^{t^{\prime}}\widehat{f_{n,\Delta}}(s)ds}{\int_{\frac{i}{\Delta}}^{t^{\prime}}\widehat{\bar{F}_{n,\Delta}}(s)ds}

\displaystyle=\max_{j>i}\frac{\int_{i/\Delta}^{j/\Delta}\widehat{f_{n,\Delta}}(s)ds}{\int_{i/\Delta}^{j/\Delta}\widehat{\bar{F}_{n,\Delta}}(s)ds}

\displaystyle=\max_{j>i}\frac{\sum_{k=i}^{j-1}\int_{k/\Delta}^{(k+1)/\Delta}\widehat{f_{n,\Delta}}(s)ds}{\sum_{k=i}^{j-1}\sum_{l=k}^{\Delta-1}\int_{l/\Delta}^{(l+1)/\Delta}\widehat{f_{n,\Delta}}(s)ds}

\displaystyle\leqslant\max_{j>i}\frac{\sum_{k=i}^{j-1}\int_{k/\Delta}^{(k+1)/\Delta}(f_{\Delta}(s)+A)ds}{\sum_{k=i}^{j-1}\sum_{l=k}^{\Delta-1}\int_{l/\Delta}^{(l+1)/\Delta}(f_{\Delta}(s)-A)ds}

\displaystyle\leqslant\max_{j>i}\frac{\left(\sum_{k=i}^{j-1}\int_{k/\Delta}^{(k+1)/\Delta}f_{\Delta}(s)ds\right)(1+\frac{A\Delta}{\ell})}{\left(\sum_{k=i}^{j-1}\Delta\sum_{l=k}^{\Delta}\int_{k/\Delta}^{(k+1)/\Delta}f_{\Delta}(s)ds\right)(1-\frac{A\Delta}{L})}

\displaystyle\leqslant\nu_{\Delta}(\frac{i}{\Delta})\frac{1+\frac{A\Delta}{\ell}}{1-\frac{A\Delta}{L}}.

Similarly :

\displaystyle\widehat{\nu_{n,\Delta}}(\frac{i}{\Delta})

\displaystyle\geqslant\max_{j>i}\frac{\sum_{k=i}^{j-1}\int_{k/\Delta}^{(k+1)/\Delta}(f_{\Delta}(s)-A)ds}{\sum_{k=i}^{j-1}\Delta\sum_{l=k}^{\Delta-1}\int_{k/\Delta}^{(k+1)/\Delta}(f_{\Delta}(s)+A)ds}

\displaystyle\geqslant\max_{j>i}\frac{\left(\sum_{k=i}^{j}\int_{k\Delta}^{(k+1)\Delta}f_{\Delta}(s)ds\right)(1-\frac{A\Delta}{L})}{\sum_{k=i}^{j-1}\sum_{l=k}^{\Delta-1}\int_{k/\Delta}^{(k+1)/\Delta}f_{\Delta}(s)ds(1+\frac{A\Delta}{\ell})}

\displaystyle\geqslant\nu_{\Delta}(\frac{i}{\Delta})\frac{1-\frac{A\Delta}{L}}{+\frac{A\Delta}{\ell}}.

∎

We are now ready to compare the index of the shifted sampled distribution with the real ones.
To do so, we need
n
to be large enough compared with
\Delta
.

4
.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
be the number of samples. Assume
n\geqslant\Delta^{2+4\alpha}
, where
1/2>\alpha>0
. Under the foregoing assumptions,
with probability greater than
1-2\Delta\exp(-2n^\alpha)
we get for all
0<i\leqslant\Delta
,

\frac{1-\frac{n^{-\alpha(1-2\alpha)}}{L}}{1+\frac{n^{-\alpha(1-2\alpha)}}{\ell}}\nu_{\Delta}(i/\Delta)\leqslant\widehat{\nu_{n,\Delta}}(i/\Delta)\leqslant\frac{1+\frac{n^{-\alpha(1-2\alpha)}}{\ell}}{1-\frac{n^{-\alpha(1-2\alpha)}}{L}}\nu_{\Delta}(i/\Delta).

Proof.

The proof is based on Hoeffding inequality.

Let
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
be
n
iid samples from the distribution with density
f
.
Let
Y^{i}_{j}={\bf 1}(i/\Delta<X_{j}\leqslant(i+1)/\Delta)
.
For all
i
,
(Y^{i}_{j})_{j=1...n}
are iid random variables with values in
\{0,1\}
and mean
\int_{i/\Delta}^{(i+1)/\Delta}f(s)ds
.
We also denote the sum
S_{n}^{i}:=\sum_{j=1}^{n}Y^{i}_{j}

Now by definition of the two shifted densities, for all
A
:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(d(\widehat{f_{n,\Delta}},f_{\Delta})\geqslant A)

\displaystyle=\sup_{i=0...\Delta-1}\mathbb{P}(|(S_{n}^{i}/n-\int_{i/\Delta}^{(i+1)/\Delta}f(s)ds)|\geqslant A).

Bounding the supremum by the sum, we get

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(d(\widehat{f_{n,\Delta}},f_{\Delta})\geqslant A)

\displaystyle\leqslant\sum_{i=0}^{\Delta-1}\mathbb{P}(|(S_{n}^{i}/n-\int_{i/\Delta}^{(i+1)/\Delta}f(s)ds)|\geqslant A)

(3)

\displaystyle\leqslant\sum_{i=0}^{\Delta-1}2\exp(-2nA^2)

(4)

\displaystyle=2\Delta\exp(-2nA^2),

(5)

where (
4
) uses Hoeffding inequality.

We now tune the variables:
A:=n^{-\frac{1}{2}+\frac{\alpha}{2}}
and
n\geqslant\Delta^{2+4\alpha}
. We get:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(d(\widehat{f_{n,\Delta}},f_{\Delta})\geqslant n^{-\frac{1}{2}+\frac{\alpha}{2}})

\displaystyle\leqslant 2\Delta\exp(-2n(n^{\frac{-1 +
\alpha}{2}})^2)

\displaystyle\leqslant 2\delta\exp(-2n(n^{\frac{-1 +
\alpha}{2}})^2)

\displaystyle\leqslant 2\delta\exp(-2n^\alpha)

Using Lemma
3
, we get that with probability greater than
1-2\Delta\exp(-2n^\alpha)
,

\frac{1-\frac{n^{-\alpha(1-2\alpha)}}{L}}{1+\frac{n^{-\alpha(1-2\alpha)}}{\ell}}\nu_{\Delta}(i/\Delta)\leqslant\widehat{\nu_{n,\Delta}}(i/\Delta)\leqslant\frac{1+\frac{n^{-\alpha(1-2\alpha)}}{\ell}}{1-\frac{n^{-\alpha(1-2\alpha)}}{L}}\nu_{\Delta}(i/\Delta).

∎

5
The (shifted) empirical Gittins index policy

5.1
Performance of the shifted empirical index policy

The fact that the shifted empirical index is close to the original one does not imply directly
that the performance of the policy based on this index is close to the performance of the policy using the real index because the choice of the next job is based on the comparison between indices that is not continuous in their values.
For that we first construct a preemptive set
\mathcal{X}_{\mbox{\tiny Pre}}^{\Delta,\varepsilon}:=\{i/\Delta,s.t.i/\Delta<1-\varepsilon\}
.
In other words, preemption is not allowed if
x
is above
1-\varepsilon
and if
x
is not an integer multiple of
1/\Delta
.

The following result from
[
7
]
(Theorem 16.5) gives a robustness guarantee of the performance of a policy based on indexes, that may not be the Gittins indexes:
Consider a stable M/G/1 queue (
\lambda\mathbb{E}(X)<1
).
Let
\nu
be the Gittins index function derived from the job
size distribution. Assume there exist
\Gamma>0
and a priority policy
\pi
using an index
function
\nu^{\prime}
satisfying for all
t
,

\frac{1}{\gamma}\nu(t)\leqslant\nu^{\prime}(t)\leqslant\gamma\nu(t).

Then the policy
\pi
induces a mean response time within a factor of
\gamma^{2}
of the optimal:

\mathbb{E}(T_{\pi})\leqslant\gamma^{2}\mathbb{E}(T_{\textsc{Gittins}}).

This result combined with our previous lemmas allow us to state our first main result.

1
.

Consider a stable M/G/1 queue in which the scheduler does not observe individual
job sizes. Assume that
f
(the density of job size distributions) satisfies assumption
1
. Let
n
be the number of samples,
\varepsilon>0
,
1/2>\alpha>0
be two constants and let
n\geqslant\Delta^{2+4\alpha}
.
Define

\gamma_{n}=\frac{1+\frac{n^{-\alpha(1-2\alpha)}}{\ell}}{1-\frac{n^{-\alpha(1-2\alpha)}}{L}}\frac{1+\frac{L}{\Delta\varepsilon\ell}}{1-\frac{L}{\Delta\varepsilon\ell}}\quad\mbox{ and }\quad p_{n}=2\Delta\exp(-2n\alpha),

(note that
\gamma_{n}
goes to 1 and
p_{n}
goes to 0 when
\Delta
goes to
+\infty
).
Let
\pi_{\textsc{shifted-samples}}
be the priority policy using the shifted empirical index function
\widehat{\nu_{n,\Delta}}
and preemption set
\mathcal{X}_{\mbox{\tiny Pre}}^{\Delta,\varepsilon}
. Then, by denoting
K
a bound on the expected response time of any work-conserving policy,

\mathbb{E}(T_{\textsc{shifted-samples}})\leqslant\gamma_{n}^{2}\mathbb{E}(T_{\textsc{Gittins}})+p_{n}K.

Proof.

From Lemma
2
we get for all states in the preemptive set
\mathcal{X}_{\mbox{\tiny Pre}}^{\Delta.\varepsilon}
,

\nu(\frac{i}{\Delta})\frac{1-\frac{L}{\Delta\ell\varepsilon}}{1+\frac{L}{\Delta\varepsilon\ell}}\leqslant\nu_{\Delta}(\frac{i}{\Delta})\leqslant\nu(\frac{i}{\Delta}).

Now, let us define the good event
\mathcal{E}_{n}=\{\lVert\widehat{f_{n,\Delta}}-f_{\Delta}\rVert_{\infty}\leqslant n^{-1/2+\alpha/2}\}
. Lemma
4
says the
\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{E}_{n})\geqslant 1-p_{n}
.

Under the good event
\mathcal{E}_{n}
,

\frac{1-\frac{n^{-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}}}{L}}{1+\frac{n^{-\alpha(1-2\alpha)}}{\ell}}\nu_{\Delta}(i/\Delta)\leqslant\hat{\nu}_{n,\Delta}(i/\Delta)\leqslant\frac{1+\frac{n^{-\alpha(1-2\alpha)}}{\ell}}{1-\frac{n^{-\alpha(1-2\alpha)}}{L}}\nu_{\Delta}(i/\Delta).

Thus setting
\gamma=\frac{1+\frac{n^{-\alpha(1-2\alpha)}}{\ell}}{1-\frac{n^{-\alpha(1-2\alpha)}}{L}}\frac{1+\frac{L}{\Delta\varepsilon\ell}}{1-\frac{L}{\Delta\varepsilon\ell}}
we have

\frac{1}{\gamma}\hat{\nu}_{n}(i/\Delta)\leqslant\nu(i/\Delta)\leqslant\gamma\hat{\nu}_{n}(i/\Delta).

(6)

We now use
K
a bound on the response time of any work-conserving scheduling policy in a M/G/1 queue and the robusteness theorem of
[
7
]
previously given. to conclude:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(T_{\textsc{shifted-samples}})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}(T_{\textsc{shifted-samples}}\mathbb{1}_{\mathcal{E}_{n}}+T_{\textsc{shifted-samples}}\mathbb{1}_{\mathcal{E}_{n}^{\mathsf{c}}})

\displaystyle\leqslant\mathbb{E}(T_{\textsc{shifted-samples}}\mathbb{1}_{\mathcal{E}_{n}})+K\mathbb{E}(\mathbb{1}_{\mathcal{E}_{n}^{\mathsf{c}}})

\displaystyle\leqslant\gamma_{n}^{2}\mathbb{E}(T_{\textsc{Gittins}})+p_{n}K,

where first inequality follows from the definition of
K
and the last one uses Eq. (
6
) and Theorem 16.5 in
[
7
]
.

The remaining point is to find an explicit bound on the mean response time in a stable M/G/1 queue, under any work-conserving policy. The following is pure queueing theory and can be seen as a more or less direct consequence of the study of the M/G/1 queue in
[
1
]
. The following derivation was given to us by Ziv Scully.
Everything holds as soon as (i) the size distribution has a finite mean
1/\mu
smaller than the mean interarrival time
1/\lambda
(
\rho:=\lambda/\mu<1
) and (ii) it has a finite variance (which is true here since
f
has a finite support).
Let us consider the workload
W(t)
in the M/G/1 queue at time
t
.
W(t)
is the same under of any work conserving policy
\pi
, and so are the busy periods (periods where
W(t)>0
).
Therefore, the response time of any policy
\pi
is bounded by the length of a busy period
B(W_{a}+X)
starting with
W_{a}
(the work present in the system when a job arrives) plus
X
(the size of the job).
This means
\mathbb{E}(T_{\pi})\leqslant\mathbb{E}(B(W_{a}+X))=\mathbb{E}(W_{a}+X)/(1-\rho)
.

Using PASTA, the workload at job arrivals
W_{a}
has the same law as the time-average worload
W
. The Pollaczek–Khinchine formula gives

\mathbb{E}(W)=\frac{\lambda\mathbb{E}(X^{2})}{2(1-\rho)}
.

Putting everything together,

\mathbb{E}(T_{\pi})\leqslant\frac{\mathbb{E}(X)}{1-\rho}+\frac{\lambda\mathbb{E}(X^{2})}{2(1-\rho)^{2}}=:K
.

5.2
Empty non-preemptive set

So far, we considered the case where the preemptive set is restricted to
\mathcal{X}_{\mbox{\tiny Pre}}^{\Delta,\varepsilon}
.
As announced at the beginning, we now consider the case where the set
\mathcal{X}_{\mbox{\tiny Pre}}=\mathcal{X}
and
\mathcal{X}_{\mbox{\tiny NonPre}}=\emptyset
.
The robusteness theorem in
[
7
]
is not directly applicable here because outside states in
\mathcal{X}_{\mbox{\tiny Pre}}^{\Delta,\varepsilon}
,
the indices are quite different, as seen in Figure
2
: the empirical index approaches infinity while the true index remains bounded as the age approaches
i/\Delta
from below.

To deal with

\mathcal{X}_{\mbox{\tiny Pre}}=\mathcal{X}
, we will let the discretization step
\Delta
grow with
n
, instead of keeping it fixed and we also let
\varepsilon
go to
0
.
To comply with the assumptions in Theorem
1
, we typically choose
\Delta
to grow roughly like
n^{2}
and
\varepsilon
grow like
\sqrt{\Delta}
.
The next theorem gives the asymptotic behavior of our empirical Gittins policy without any restrictions on preemptions.

2
.

Consider a stable M/G/1 queue in which the scheduler does not observe individual
job sizes. Assume that
f
(the density of job size distributions) satisfies assumption
1
. Let
n
be the number of samples,
\varepsilon>0
,
1/2>\alpha>0
be the two constants as before and let
\Delta=n^{2/(1+8\alpha)}
and
\varepsilon=1/\sqrt{\Delta}
. Under preemption set
\mathcal{X}_{\mbox{\tiny Pre}}=\mathcal{X}
, as
n
goes to infinity,

\mathbb{E}(T_{\textsc{shifted-samples}})
converges to

\mathbb{E}(T_{\textsc{Gittins}})
.

Proof.

Let us denote by
\pi(\nu)
the scheduling policy index the true index
\nu
.

The first step is to remove the preemption restriction
x>1-\varepsilon
.
Under Assumption
1
on the size distribution, the rank function is continuous and remains bounded away from
0
in
[0,1)
. Furthermore,
\nu(x)>1/1-x
for all
x\in(0,1)
. Therefore, if
\varepsilon
is small enough (smaller than a critcal value
\varepsilon_{c}
), then
\nu(x)>\nu(y)
for all
x>1-\varepsilon_{c}
and all
y<1-\varepsilon_{c}
. This implies that if a job reaches age
x>1-\varepsilon_{c}
then the job will not be preempted before completion even is preemption is allowed, so that restricting the preemption after
1-\varepsilon_{c}
has no impact on the execution of these large jobs as long as
\varepsilon<\varepsilon_{c}
.

In the second step, we now focus on one busy period of the M/G/1 queue (remember that this busy period does not depend on the work-conserving policy used to scedule jobs).
under
\pi(\nu)
, the busy period is decomposed into a finite number of time intervals, corresponding to which jobs are executed at any time (this may include time intervals with processor sharing.
The end points of these intervals are of two types: preemptions and completions of a job.

Now, let us construct a new scheduling policy
\pi_{\Delta}
that mimics
\pi(\nu)
but shifts all the preemption end points to next point in
\{i/\Delta,i\in\mathbb{N}\}
. As for the completion end points, they are moved to be the new completion times under the new policy. This policy is not work conserving (it may have to wait to start a new job after a completion, if this completion happens before the conpletion under the Gittins policy) and it is not an index policy
The policy
\pi^{\Delta}
only preempts at points in
\mathcal{X}_{\mbox{\tiny Pre}}^{\Delta}
so its response time is greater than the response time of the optimal polcy under
\mathcal{X}_{\mbox{\tiny Pre}}^{\Delta}
, namely
\pi(\nu_{\Delta})
. On the other hand, its response time is close to the response time of
\pi(\nu)
, missing the mark on the response time for each job by only a gap smaller than
m^{2}/\Delta
, where
m
is the number of intervals in the busy period for the job. On average, by explicitly mentionning the preemption set,

\mathbb{E}(T_{\textsc{Gittins},\mathcal{X}})+M_{2}/\Delta\geqslant\mathbb{E}(T_{\textsc{Gittins},\mathcal{X}_{\mbox{\tiny Pre}}^{\Delta}})\geqslant\mathbb{E}(T_{\textsc{Gittins},\mathcal{X}}),

(7)

where
M_{2}
is the expectation of
m^{2}
with
m
the number of intervals in a busy period under
\pi(\nu)
.
Finally, when
\Delta
goes to infinity, this implies that the performance of the policy under discrete preemption times converges to the perfomance of the policy
\pi(\nu)
under all preemption times.
Finally, since the performance of the policy using empirical estimates is close to the performance of the Gittins policy under discrete preemptions when
n
grows, this implies together with Eq. (
7
), that it also converges to the performance of the Gittins policy when
\mathcal{X}_{\mbox{\tiny Pre}}=\mathcal{X}
.
∎
