Generalized Tadmor Conditions and Structure-Preserving Numerical Fluxes for the Compressible Flow of Real Gases
R.B. Klein, B. Sanderse, P. Costa, R. Pecnik, R.A.W.M Henkes

Abstract
We generalize Tadmor’s algebraic numerical flux condition for entropy-conservative discretizations of conservation laws to a broader class of secondary structures , i.e. possibly non-convex secondary quantities whose evolution can consist of both conservative and non-conservative contributions. The resulting generalized Tadmor condition yields a discrete local balance law for secondary structures alongside the discrete conservation law that is solved. In contrast to the convex entropy setting, non-convex secondary quantities can have singular Hessians and non-injective gradients; this introduces an additional necessary structural requirement, which we term (discrete) null-consistency . Null-consistency constrains admissible numerical work terms and is required for the existence and well-posedness of fluxes satisfying the generalized Tadmor condition. To construct such fluxes in practice, we show how discrete gradient operators provide systematic construction methods even when some of the functions entering the secondary structure are arbitrary, as in compressible flow closed by an arbitrary equation of state. As an application, we derive an entropy-conserving and kinetic-energy-consistent numerical flux for the Euler equations with an arbitrary (non-ideal) equation of state. We demonstrate the performance of the resulting scheme on a set of supercritical/transcritical compressible-flow test cases using several non-ideal equations of state, including a fully turbulent transcritical flow with a state-of-the-art equation of state and models for viscosity and heat conductivity. Computations are performed with our new open-source, flexible, JAX-based, multi-GPU compressible flow solver for Helmholtz-based equations of state available at github.com/rbklein/HelmEOS2 .

Introduction

Many continuum models in physics take the form of systems of conservation laws, i.e. PDEs that evolve a set of
primary
conserved variables through flux balances
[
8
]
. In practical computations—e.g. compressible turbulence, multi-scale flows, or in strongly non-uniform thermodynamic regimes—standard discretizations may develop severe stability and robustness issues.

A promising strategy to alleviate these issues is the design of
structure-preserving
schemes: discretizations that retain a certain property of the conservation law. For example, schemes can retain the Hamiltonian structure
[
41
]
, the differential geometric structure
[
45
]
, or the dynamics of extra quantities
[
52
]
in addition to solving the conservation law. In this research, we are particularly interested in the latter: discretizations that, in addition to the primary conservation law, remain consistent with the evolution of selected
secondary
quantities. Typical examples in compressible flow are discrete consistency with transport relations for kinetic energy
[
51
,
29
,
31
,
47
]
, entropy
[
35
,
34
,
59
,
58
]
, and pressure
[
48
,
3
]
. When such secondary structure is preserved, one can often reproduce discrete analogues of continuous stability mechanisms, stabilizing the discretization. For instance, entropy conservation/dissipation can be used to derive nonlinear stability statements
[
56
,
55
]
, while kinetic-energy consistency balances and controls the discrete exchange between mechanical and thermodynamic energy components
[
51
,
29
]
and prevents excessive numerical dissipation.

A particularly general approach to secondary-structure preservation is Tadmor’s framework for
entropy conservative
fluxes
[
58
,
57
]
. Convex mathematical entropies are functions generalizing the thermodynamic entropy of gas dynamics to other conservation laws
[
39
,
13
,
65
]
. They are conserved when solutions are smooth and thus satisfy an
additional conservation law
. Tadmor derives an algebraic numerical flux condition (the Tadmor condition) that guarantees a discrete local entropy conservation law is also satisfied by the discretization. This approach has enabled systematic constructions of highly stable entropy-conserving fluxes for, among others, the Burgers equation
[
30
]
, compressible Euler flow with ideal-gas thermodynamics
[
26
,
7
,
50
,
51
,
49
]
, shallow-water/ocean models
[
14
]
, and MHD
[
67
]
. Furthermore, entropy-conserving fluxes form a basis for designing schemes that handle non-smooth, more specifically discontinuous, solutions
[
15
]
.

However, many secondary quantities of practical interest are
non-convex
and/or satisfy
non-conservative balance laws
rather than additional conservation laws (e.g. kinetic energy and pressure in compressible turbulence
[
29
,
51
,
3
]
). For such quantities, a general analogue of Tadmor’s framework is missing, and structure-preserving treatments are often performed on a case-by-case basis. Two fundamental obstacles arise:
(i) for non-convex secondary quantities, the necessary gradients may fail to be injective and their Hessian may be singular, which imposes additional compatibility constraints; and
(ii) in real-gas or general-equation-of-state settings, the fluxes and thermodynamic potentials entering the secondary relations may only be defined implicitly (or only up to an arbitrary choice of thermodynamic model), which limits standard construction methods
[
50
,
14
,
7
,
49
]
.

In this work, we generalize Tadmor’s approach from convex entropies to
general secondary structures
whose evolution consists of conservative and non-conservative parts. Our first contribution is a generalized compatibility framework at the differential level and its discrete analogue, leading to a
generalized Tadmor condition
that enforces a discrete balance law for a prescribed secondary structure. A key new feature is a structural requirement we call
(discrete) null-consistency
: whenever the Hessian of the secondary quantity has a non-trivial kernel, the remaining terms in the generalized compatibility relations must satisfy additional constraints along the associated null directions. We show that discrete null-consistency is not optional, but that it is a necessary condition for well-posedness and for the existence of numerical fluxes that satisfy the generalized Tadmor condition. In this article, we develop our framework for smooth solutions only, to avoid the difficulties associated with discontinuous solutions and non-conservative balance laws
[
9
,
60
]
.

Our second contribution is methodological: we show how
discrete gradient operators

27
,
40
,
20
]
can be used to solve the generalized Tadmor conditions in settings where the relevant functions are only partially specified, as is the case for compressible flow with an arbitrary equation of state. In particular, discrete gradients provide systematic jump expansions for thermodynamic potentials without requiring closed-form expressions
[
50
,
14
,
7
,
49
]
.

The framework is motivated by, and applied to, compressible turbulent flow of
supercritical
fluids, where thermodynamic properties can vary extremely rapidly (
Figure 2
) near so-called pseudo-phase-transition regimes which can only be described with more general non-ideal equations of state

4

5
,
24
,
21

. In this case, structure-preserving schemes must be adapted to deal with these general equations of state
[

,

The resulting flux removes a singularity present in a recently proposed entropy-conserving and kinetic-energy-consistent flux for general EoS

2
Secondary structures of conservation laws

In this section, we develop a theoretical framework that ensures a solution of a conservation law also satisfies an additional
non-conservative balance law
. We will use this compatibility framework later in the article to inform a discrete framework that, in turn, allows us to construct structure-preserving discretizations. The theoretical framework extends and uses tools of an existing framework for convex extensions of conservation laws
[
16
,
19
]
. Hence, we will first introduce this framework. After that, we will continue with our novel non-conservative extension.

2.1
Conservation laws and tools in entropy analysis

We are interested in general systems of conservation laws, which are systems of partial differential equations (PDEs) formulated in the following form:

\frac{\partial{\boldsymbol{u}}}{\partial t}+\sum_{i=1}^{d}\frac{\partial}{\partial{x_{i}}}\left({\boldsymbol{f}^{i}(\boldsymbol{u})}\right)=0,

(1)

where
d\in\mathbb{N}
and
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a spatial coordinate,
t\in(0,t_{f}]
is a temporal coordinate with
t_{f}>0
,
\boldsymbol{u}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\times[0,t_{f}]\rightarrow\mathcal{U}
is the solution function taking values in an open (often convex) set
\mathcal{U}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
is the number of conservative variables,
\boldsymbol{f}^{i}:\mathcal{U}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n}
is the flux function in coordinate-direction
i=1,...,d
and
x_{i}
denotes the
i
-th component of the vector
\boldsymbol{x}
. At
t=0
, the solution function is equal to some continuously differentiable initial condition
\boldsymbol{u}(\boldsymbol{x},0)=\boldsymbol{u}_{0}(\boldsymbol{x})
. When the solution function
\boldsymbol{u}
is continuously differentiable on
\mathbb{R}^{d}\times[0,t_{f}]
, many conservation laws describing physical systems often also satisfy additional conservation laws:

\frac{\partial{s(\boldsymbol{u})}}{\partial t}+\sum_{i=1}^{d}\frac{\partial}{\partial{x_{i}}}\left({\mathcal{F}^{i}(\boldsymbol{u})}\right)=0,

(2)

where
s:\mathcal{U}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is a strictly convex
C^{2}(\mathcal{U})
function and
\mathcal{F}^{i}:\mathcal{U}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is
C^{1}(\mathcal{U})
. The prototypical example of such a function is the negated entropy density of the Euler equations of gas dynamics. For (
2
) to be satisfied by smooth solutions of (
1
), it is sufficient if, for all
i
, the following compatibility condition holds:

\boldsymbol{\eta}(\boldsymbol{u})^{T}\frac{\partial{\boldsymbol{f}^{i}}}{\partial{\boldsymbol{u}}}=\frac{\partial{\mathcal{F}^{i}}}{\partial{\boldsymbol{u}}}^{T},

(3)

where
\boldsymbol{\eta}:\mathcal{U}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n},\boldsymbol{u}\mapsto\frac{\partial{s}}{\partial{\boldsymbol{u}}}(\boldsymbol{u})
. In the case of entropy functions, the gradient function
\boldsymbol{\eta}
is given a name. They are called entropy variables, as
\boldsymbol{\eta}
is an injective map due to the convexity of
s
. If the compatibility condition is satisfied, the general function
s
is called a mathematical entropy of the conservation law (
1
) and
\mathcal{F}^{i}
the associated entropy flux in analogy to the Euler equation. Gathering all entropy fluxes in a vector
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{F}}
, a pair
(s,\boldsymbol{\mathcal{F}})
satisfying (
3
) is referred to as an entropy pair of the conservation law (
1
). The entropy conservation law (
2
) now follows from the straightforward manipulations:

\displaystyle 0=\boldsymbol{\eta}(\boldsymbol{u})^{T}\left(\frac{\partial{\boldsymbol{u}}}{\partial t}+\sum_{i=1}^{d}\frac{\partial}{\partial{x_{i}}}\left({\boldsymbol{f}^{i}(\boldsymbol{u})}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\eta}(\boldsymbol{u})^{T}\frac{\partial{\boldsymbol{u}}}{\partial t}+\sum_{i=1}^{d}\boldsymbol{\eta}(\boldsymbol{u})^{T}\frac{\partial{\boldsymbol{f}^{i}(\boldsymbol{u})}}{\partial{\boldsymbol{u}}}\frac{\partial{\boldsymbol{u}}}{\partial{x_{i}}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial{s(\boldsymbol{u})}}{\partial t}+\sum_{i=1}^{d}\frac{\partial}{\partial{x_{i}}}\left({\mathcal{F}^{i}(\boldsymbol{u})}\right).

We reiterate that these manipulations are only valid when
\boldsymbol{u}
is continuously differentiable. A useful tool in entropy analysis is the so-called entropy flux potential, defined as:

\psi^{i}(\boldsymbol{u}):=\boldsymbol{\eta}(\boldsymbol{u})^{T}\boldsymbol{f}^{i}(\boldsymbol{u})-\mathcal{F}^{i}(\boldsymbol{u}).

(4)

Its use comes from the fact that the compatibility condition (
3
) can be alternatively phrased in terms of the entropy flux potential as follows:

\boldsymbol{f}^{i}(\boldsymbol{u})^{T}\frac{\partial{\boldsymbol{\eta}}}{\partial{\boldsymbol{u}}}=\frac{\partial{\psi^{i}}}{\partial{\boldsymbol{u}}}^{T},

(5)

which should hold for all
i
. We will see later that discrete variants of specifically this form of compatibility condition are crucial for proofs of structure preservation. We refer to (
3
) as the primal compatibility condition, while (
5
) is referred to as the dual compatibility condition. The primal and dual compatibility relations are equivalent for
C^{1}(\mathcal{U})
flux functions
\boldsymbol{f}^{i}
in light of the product rule identity:

\frac{\partial}{\partial{\boldsymbol{u}}}\left({\boldsymbol{\eta}^{T}\boldsymbol{f}^{i}}\right)^{T}=\boldsymbol{\eta}(\boldsymbol{u})^{T}\frac{\partial{\boldsymbol{f}^{i}}}{\partial{\boldsymbol{u}}}+\boldsymbol{f}^{i}(\boldsymbol{u})^{T}\frac{\partial{\boldsymbol{\eta}}}{\partial{\boldsymbol{u}}}.

Substituting either the primal or dual compatibility condition into the above immediately results in satisfaction of the other condition with
\psi^{i}=\boldsymbol{\eta}^{T}\boldsymbol{f}^{i}-\mathcal{F}^{i}
and
\mathcal{F}^{i}=\boldsymbol{\eta}^{T}\boldsymbol{f}^{i}-\psi^{i}
, respectively. The entropy conservation law can be derived from the dual compatibility condition using the product rule:

\displaystyle 0=\boldsymbol{\eta}(\boldsymbol{u})^{T}\left(\frac{\partial{\boldsymbol{u}}}{\partial t}+\sum_{i=1}^{d}\frac{\partial}{\partial{x_{i}}}\left({\boldsymbol{f}^{i}(\boldsymbol{u})}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial{s(\boldsymbol{u})}}{\partial t}+\sum_{i=1}^{d}\frac{\partial}{\partial{x_{i}}}\left({\boldsymbol{\eta}(\boldsymbol{u})^{T}\boldsymbol{f}^{i}(\boldsymbol{u})}\right)-\sum_{i=1}^{d}\boldsymbol{f}^{i}(\boldsymbol{u})^{T}\frac{\partial{\boldsymbol{\eta}(\boldsymbol{u})}}{\partial{x_{i}}}

(6)

\displaystyle\overset{\eqref{eq:pote}}{=}\frac{\partial{s(\boldsymbol{u})}}{\partial t}+\sum_{i=1}^{d}\frac{\partial}{\partial{x_{i}}}\left({\mathcal{F}^{i}(\boldsymbol{u})}\right)+\sum_{i=1}^{d}\left(\frac{\partial{\psi^{i}}}{\partial{\boldsymbol{u}}}^{T}-\boldsymbol{f}^{i}(\boldsymbol{u})^{T}\frac{\partial{\boldsymbol{\eta}}}{\partial{\boldsymbol{u}}}\right)\frac{\partial{\boldsymbol{u}}}{\partial{x_{i}}}

\displaystyle\overset{\eqref{eq:dcond}}{=}\frac{\partial{s(\boldsymbol{u})}}{\partial t}+\sum_{i=1}^{d}\frac{\partial}{\partial{x_{i}}}\left({\mathcal{F}^{i}(\boldsymbol{u})}\right).

In this way, the entropy flux potential
\psi^{i}
is just the residual that has to be canceled out by the terms appearing outside of the divergence in the third line to obtain
\mathcal{F}^{i}
as an entropy flux. This provides a natural way to extend the tools from entropy analysis to general secondary quantities.

2.2
General secondary quantities and generalized compatibility

Besides conserved secondary quantities like entropy functions, many physical systems also exhibit secondary quantities adhering to more general balance laws containing conservative
and
non-conservative terms. The approach and tools developed in the analysis of entropy functions of conservation laws remain relatively unexplored in this more general non-conservative setting. Existing literature has considered several of such non-conserved secondary quantities, for example kinetic energy
[
51
,
29
]
and pressure
[
48
,
3
,
10
]
in compressible flow applications, but not through the lens of entropy analysis
[
58
,
57
]
. In this article, we aim to explore how these approaches developed for convex entropies can be generalized to the analysis of more general non-conserved secondary quantities, especially for the construction of numerical schemes.

The more general evolution equations associated with secondary quantities can often be formulated as consisting of a conservative and a non-conservative part. Specifically, we will assume a secondary quantity
q:\mathcal{U}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, which we allow to be
non-convex
, evolves according to:

\frac{\partial{q(\boldsymbol{u})}}{\partial t}+\sum_{i=1}^{d}\frac{\partial}{\partial{x_{i}}}\left({\mathcal{F}^{i}_{q}(\boldsymbol{u})}\right)+\sum_{i=1}^{d}c_{q}^{i}(\boldsymbol{u})\frac{\partial}{\partial{x_{i}}}\left({\mathcal{G}^{i}_{q}(\boldsymbol{u})}\right)=0.

(7)

This precise form is assumed due to its prevalence in fluid dynamics applications (e.g. kinetic energy
[
51
]
, pressure
[
3
,
61
]
satisfy this). Here,
\mathcal{F}_{q}^{i}:\mathcal{U}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is the conservative
q
-flux and the non-conservative part of the evolution is governed by
c_{q}^{i},\mathcal{G}_{q}^{i}:\mathcal{U}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
with
i=1,...,d
. We will refer to
\mathcal{G}_{q}^{i}
as the non-conservative
q
-flux and to
c_{q}^{i}
as the non-conservative
q
-flux coefficient. As it often models a form of work performed on or by the system, we will refer to the product of the non-conservative
q
-flux derivative and coefficient as it appears in (
7
) as the
work term
. With (
7
), we can generalize the primal compatibility condition (
3
) of conserved secondary quantities to the case of general secondary quantities. Assuming
q
is twice-continuously differentiable and
\mathcal{F}_{q}^{i},\mathcal{G}_{q}^{i}
are continuously differentiable, additionally denoting the gradient
\boldsymbol{\xi}:\mathcal{U}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n},\boldsymbol{u}\mapsto\frac{\partial{q}}{\partial{\boldsymbol{u}}}(\boldsymbol{u})
,
C^{1}
solutions to (
1
) adhere to (
7
) if for all
i
the following compatibility condition is satisfied:

\boldsymbol{\xi}(\boldsymbol{u})^{T}\frac{\partial{\boldsymbol{f}^{i}}}{\partial{\boldsymbol{u}}}=\frac{\partial{\mathcal{F}_{q}^{i}}}{\partial{\boldsymbol{u}}}^{T}+c_{q}^{i}(\boldsymbol{u})\frac{\partial{\mathcal{G}_{q}^{i}}}{\partial{\boldsymbol{u}}}^{T}.

(8)

This is a
generalized primal compatibility condition
. It follows from equivalent manipulations as for convex entropies that:

\displaystyle 0=\boldsymbol{\xi}(\boldsymbol{u})^{T}\left(\frac{\partial{\boldsymbol{u}}}{\partial t}+\sum_{i=1}^{d}\frac{\partial}{\partial{x_{i}}}\left({\boldsymbol{f}^{i}(\boldsymbol{u})}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\xi}(\boldsymbol{u})^{T}\frac{\partial{\boldsymbol{u}}}{\partial t}+\sum_{i=1}^{d}\boldsymbol{\xi}(\boldsymbol{u})^{T}\frac{\partial{\boldsymbol{f}^{i}(\boldsymbol{u})}}{\partial{\boldsymbol{u}}}\frac{\partial{\boldsymbol{u}}}{\partial{x_{i}}}

(9)

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial{q(\boldsymbol{u})}}{\partial t}+\sum_{i=1}^{d}\frac{\partial}{\partial{x_{i}}}\left({\mathcal{F}_{q}^{i}(\boldsymbol{u})}\right)+\sum_{i=1}^{d}c_{q}^{i}(\boldsymbol{u})\frac{\partial}{\partial{x_{i}}}\left({\mathcal{G}^{i}_{q}(\boldsymbol{u})}\right).

This motivates us to generalize the definition of an entropy pair to a more general secondary structure.

Definition 1

(Secondary structures)
.

A secondary structure
(q,\boldsymbol{\mathcal{F}}_{q},\boldsymbol{\mathcal{G}}_{q},\boldsymbol{c}_{q})
of a conservation law (
1
) is an ordered set consisting of a
C^{2}
secondary quantity
q:\mathcal{U}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, a vector of
C^{1}
conservative fluxes
\mathcal{F}_{q}^{i}:\mathcal{U}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, a vector of
C^{1}
non-conservative fluxes
\mathcal{G}_{q}^{i}:\mathcal{U}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
and a vector of non-conservative flux coefficients
c_{q}^{i}:\mathcal{U}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
that satisfy the set of PDEs (
8
). The secondary structure is conservative if (
8
) is satisfied with
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{G}}_{q},\boldsymbol{c}_{q}=0
.

An entropy pair of (
1
) thus forms a conservative secondary structure. A generalized dual compatibility condition can also be constructed, for this we define the conservative flux residual function:

\psi_{q}^{i}(\boldsymbol{u}):=\boldsymbol{\xi}(\boldsymbol{u})^{T}\boldsymbol{f}^{i}(\boldsymbol{u})-\mathcal{F}_{q}^{i}(\boldsymbol{u}),

(10)

which takes the same form as the entropy flux potential (
4
). The generalized primal compatibility condition can be rephrased in terms of this residual function to obtain the following
generalized dual compatibility condition
:

\frac{\partial{\psi_{q}^{i}}}{\partial{\boldsymbol{u}}}^{T}-\boldsymbol{f}^{i}(\boldsymbol{u})^{T}\frac{\partial{\boldsymbol{\xi}}}{\partial{\boldsymbol{u}}}=c_{q}^{i}(\boldsymbol{u})\frac{\partial{\mathcal{G}_{q}^{i}}}{\partial{\boldsymbol{u}}}^{T}.

(11)

Equivalence between the generalized primal and dual compatibility condition follows for
C^{1}
flux functions
\boldsymbol{f}^{i}
using the same product rule identity as for entropy. The secondary evolution equation (
7
) can be obtained from the generalized dual compatibility and the same product rule approach as for entropy in (
6
):

\displaystyle 0=\boldsymbol{\xi}(\boldsymbol{u})^{T}\left(\frac{\partial{\boldsymbol{u}}}{\partial t}+\sum_{i=1}^{d}\frac{\partial}{\partial{x_{i}}}\left({\boldsymbol{f}^{i}(\boldsymbol{u})}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial{q(\boldsymbol{u})}}{\partial t}+\sum_{i=1}^{d}\frac{\partial}{\partial{x_{i}}}\left({\boldsymbol{\xi}(\boldsymbol{u})^{T}\boldsymbol{f}^{i}(\boldsymbol{u})}\right)-\sum_{i=1}^{d}\boldsymbol{f}^{i}(\boldsymbol{u})^{T}\frac{\partial{\boldsymbol{\xi}(\boldsymbol{u})}}{\partial{x_{i}}}

\displaystyle\overset{\eqref{eq:potq}}{=}\frac{\partial{q(\boldsymbol{u})}}{\partial t}+\sum_{i=1}^{d}\frac{\partial}{\partial{x_{i}}}\left({\mathcal{F}_{q}^{i}(\boldsymbol{u})}\right)+\sum_{i=1}^{d}\left(\frac{\partial{\psi_{q}^{i}}}{\partial{\boldsymbol{u}}}^{T}-\boldsymbol{f}^{i}(\boldsymbol{u})^{T}\frac{\partial{\boldsymbol{\xi}}}{\partial{\boldsymbol{u}}}\right)\frac{\partial{\boldsymbol{u}}}{\partial{x_{i}}}

\displaystyle\overset{\eqref{eq:dcondq}}{=}\frac{\partial{q(\boldsymbol{u})}}{\partial t}+\sum_{i=1}^{d}\frac{\partial}{\partial{x_{i}}}\left({\mathcal{F}_{q}^{i}(\boldsymbol{u})}\right)+\sum_{i=1}^{d}c_{q}^{i}(\boldsymbol{u})\frac{\partial}{\partial{x_{i}}}\left({\mathcal{G}_{q}^{i}(\boldsymbol{u})}\right).

In the generalized case, the flux residual
\frac{\partial{\psi_{q}^{i}}}{\partial{\boldsymbol{u}}}
should not cancel out the non-conservative remainder of the product rule application
\boldsymbol{f}^{i}(\boldsymbol{u})^{T}\frac{\partial{\boldsymbol{\xi}}}{\partial{\boldsymbol{u}}}
, but instead should correct it to the work term we expect. This is precisely the statement of the dual compatibility condition, equation (
11
).

Remark 1

(General secondary balance law)
.

We have stated the work term in (
7
) with only one term per coordinate direction, as this is how it often appears in fluid dynamical contexts. However, the most general form of the work term that can appear in the evolution equation of a function
q(\boldsymbol{u})
of the solution
\boldsymbol{u}
of a conservation law (
1
) is:

\sum_{i=1}^{d}\sum_{j=1}^{n}c_{q}^{i,j}(\boldsymbol{u})\frac{\partial}{\partial{x_{i}}}\left({\mathcal{G}_{q}^{i,j}(\boldsymbol{u})}\right).

It is straightforward to show by an application of the product rule that any sufficiently smooth function
q
of the solution
\boldsymbol{u}
of a conservation law (
1
) satisfies a balance law with a work term as above and a conservative flux
\mathcal{F}_{q}^{i}(\boldsymbol{u})=\boldsymbol{\xi}(\boldsymbol{u})^{T}\boldsymbol{f}^{i}(\boldsymbol{u})
. All results in this article can be extended to this case by replacing the single work term with a sum.

2.3
Null-consistency

Figure 1:
Illustration of the null-consistency property at a point
\boldsymbol{u}_{s}\in\mathcal{U}
where
\mathcal{H}_{q}(\boldsymbol{u}_{s}):=\frac{\partial^{2}{q}}{\partial{\boldsymbol{u}}^{2}}(\boldsymbol{u}_{s})
is singular for
n=2
and
d=1
. Note how
f_{1}(\boldsymbol{u}_{s})\nabla_{u}\xi_{1}(\boldsymbol{u}_{s})+f_{2}(\boldsymbol{u}_{s})\nabla_{u}\xi_{2}(\boldsymbol{u}_{s})=\nabla_{u}\psi_{q}(\boldsymbol{u}_{s})-c_{q}(\boldsymbol{u}_{s})\nabla_{u}\mathcal{G}_{q}(\boldsymbol{u}_{s})
to satisfy the dual compatibility condition.

The generalized dual compatibility condition (
11
) is a system of PDEs
on

\mathcal{U}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
relating a secondary structure
q,\psi_{q}^{i},\mathcal{G}_{q}^{i},c_{q}^{i}:\mathcal{U}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
to a conservation law flux
\boldsymbol{f}^{i}:\mathcal{U}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n}
. We now examine a property of solutions to these PDEs that appears when
q
is allowed to be non-convex. We call this property null-consistency. When
q
is non-convex, its Hessian matrix:

\mathcal{H}_{q}(\boldsymbol{u}):=\frac{\partial^{2}{q}}{\partial{\boldsymbol{u}}^{2}}(\boldsymbol{u})=\frac{\partial{\boldsymbol{\xi}}}{\partial{\boldsymbol{u}}}(\boldsymbol{u}),

is no longer guaranteed to be non-singular, in contrast to the case of convex entropy functions
[
65
]
. At any point
\boldsymbol{u}_{s}\in\mathcal{U}
where
\mathcal{H}_{q}(\boldsymbol{u}_{s})
is singular, null-consistency constrains how the functions
\psi_{q}^{i},\mathcal{G}_{q}^{i}
may vary in state space at the point
\boldsymbol{u}_{s}
. This property will be important to consider, especially due to the fact that it occurs in multiple discrete forms as a
necessary
condition for the
existence
of structure-preserving discretizations, as we will see in later sections where we analyze numerical schemes.

In more detail, the null-consistency property can be derived as follows. We momentarily use the classical vector calculus notation
\nabla_{u}:=\frac{\partial}{\partial\boldsymbol{u}}
for the gradient on
\mathcal{U}
and use
\left<\cdot,\cdot\right>:\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
and
||\cdot||:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{+}
for the Euclidean inner-product and norm, respectively. Consider a point
\boldsymbol{u}_{s}\in\mathcal{U}
where
\mathcal{H}_{q}(\boldsymbol{u}_{s})
is singular. Let
\delta\boldsymbol{u}\in\ker(\mathcal{H}_{q}(\boldsymbol{u}_{s}))
where
\delta\boldsymbol{u}\neq 0
. Multiplying the generalized dual compatibility (
11
) from the right with
\delta\boldsymbol{u}
, we get:

\left<\nabla_{u}\psi_{q}^{i}(\boldsymbol{u}_{s}),\delta\boldsymbol{u}\right>-\boldsymbol{f}^{i}(\boldsymbol{u})^{T}\mathcal{H}_{q}(\boldsymbol{u}_{s})\delta\boldsymbol{u}=\left<c_{q}^{i}(\boldsymbol{u}_{s})\nabla_{u}\mathcal{G}_{q}^{i}(\boldsymbol{u}_{s}),\delta\boldsymbol{u}\right>.

Since
\delta\boldsymbol{u}\in\ker(\mathcal{H}_{q}(\boldsymbol{u}_{s}))
, we have
\mathcal{H}_{q}(\boldsymbol{u}_{s})\delta\boldsymbol{u}=0
and the expression simplifies to:

\left<\nabla_{u}\psi_{q}^{i}(\boldsymbol{u}_{s}),\delta\boldsymbol{u}\right>=c_{q}^{i}(\boldsymbol{u}_{s})\left<\nabla_{u}\mathcal{G}_{q}^{i}(\boldsymbol{u}_{s}),\delta\boldsymbol{u}\right>\quad\forall\delta\boldsymbol{u}\in\ker(\mathcal{H}_{q}(\boldsymbol{u}_{s})).

(12)

We can scale both sides of the equation with
||\delta\boldsymbol{u}||^{-1}
to normalize
\delta\boldsymbol{u}
. Then (
12
) states that, at some point
\boldsymbol{u}_{s}\in\mathcal{U}
where the kernel of
\mathcal{H}_{q}(\boldsymbol{u}_{s})
is non-trivial,
the directional derivatives of
\psi_{q}^{i}
and
\mathcal{G}_{q}^{i}
in direction
\delta\boldsymbol{u}
must become proportional with factor
c_{q}^{i}(\boldsymbol{u}_{s})
. We call this property
null-consistency
. Note that these directional derivatives are taken in
\mathcal{U}
-space.

In
Figure 1
, an example of null-consistency is sketched for
n=2
, setting
d=1
to forgo the need to indicate a spatial direction with superscripts. The rows of
\mathcal{H}_{q}(\boldsymbol{u}_{s})
are given by the gradients
\nabla_{u}\xi_{1}(\boldsymbol{u}_{s}),\nabla_{u}\xi_{2}(\boldsymbol{u}_{s})
on
\mathcal{U}
-space. Assuming
\delta\boldsymbol{u}\in\ker(\mathcal{H}_{q}(\boldsymbol{u}_{s}))
, we have that the gradients
\nabla_{u}\xi_{1}(\boldsymbol{u}_{s}),\nabla_{u}\xi_{2}(\boldsymbol{u}_{s})
are orthogonal to
\delta\boldsymbol{u}
so that
f_{1}(\boldsymbol{u}_{s})\nabla_{u}\xi_{1}(\boldsymbol{u}_{s})
and
f_{2}(\boldsymbol{u}_{s})\nabla_{u}\xi_{2}(\boldsymbol{u}_{s})
are collinear. If
\nabla_{u}\psi_{q}(\boldsymbol{u}_{s})
is not orthogonal to
\delta\boldsymbol{u}
, the vector identity (
11
) can only hold if the component of
c_{q}(\boldsymbol{u}_{s})\nabla_{u}\mathcal{G}_{q}(\boldsymbol{u}_{s})
along
\delta\boldsymbol{u}
is equal to that of
\nabla_{u}\psi_{q}(\boldsymbol{u}_{s})
i.e.
\langle\nabla_{u}\psi_{q}(\boldsymbol{u}_{s}),\delta\boldsymbol{u}\rangle=\langle c_{q}(\boldsymbol{u}_{s})\nabla_{u}\mathcal{G}_{q}(\boldsymbol{u}_{s}),\delta\boldsymbol{u}\rangle
. Since these are the components of gradients, it thus requires that the change of
\psi_{q}
and
\mathcal{G}_{q}
at
\boldsymbol{u}_{s}\in\mathcal{U}
along
\delta\boldsymbol{u}
becomes proportional through
c_{q}(\boldsymbol{u}_{s})
. If
\nabla_{u}\xi_{1}(\boldsymbol{u}_{s}),\nabla_{u}\xi_{2}(\boldsymbol{u}_{s})\neq 0
,
\delta\boldsymbol{u}
must then also be tangent to the level curves of
\xi_{1},\xi_{2}
at
\boldsymbol{u}_{s}
.

3
Structure-preserving numerical schemes

In this section, we develop a discrete framework that ensures a finite-volume method for a conservation law also satisfies a compatible discrete secondary balance law. This framework is a discrete analogue of the theoretical framework developed in the previous section. Additionally, it extends an existing discrete framework to ensure discrete entropy conservation laws
[
58
,
57
]
, which we briefly review first.

3.1
Entropy-conserving discretizations

We will discretize the conservation law (
1
) on a spatial grid, indexed by multi-indices
(i_{1},...,i_{d})\in\mathbb{N}^{d}
, with a finite-volume scheme:

\frac{d{\boldsymbol{u}_{\boldsymbol{I}}}}{dt}+\sum_{j=1}^{d}\frac{1}{\Delta x_{j}}\left(\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{I}+\boldsymbol{1}_{j}/2}^{j}-\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{I}-\boldsymbol{1}_{j}/2}^{j}\right)=0,

(13)

where
\boldsymbol{u}_{\boldsymbol{I}}:[0,t_{f}]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n}
is the solution in grid cell
\boldsymbol{I}:=(i_{1},...,i_{d})
and
\Delta x_{j}>0
is the cell-width in coordinate direction
j
, which we assume uniform in direction
j
to ease notation. We refer to neighboring grid cells with respect to grid cell
\boldsymbol{I}
by adding a multi-index of zeros with a
1
at position
j
, which we denote
\boldsymbol{1}_{j}
. For example, the index of the neighboring grid point in the first coordinate direction is
\boldsymbol{I}+\boldsymbol{1}_{1}=(i_{1}+1,...,i_{d})
. Finally,
\boldsymbol{f}^{j}_{\boldsymbol{I}+\boldsymbol{1}_{j}/2}
is short for
\boldsymbol{f}_{h}(\boldsymbol{u}_{\boldsymbol{I}},\boldsymbol{u}_{\boldsymbol{I}+\boldsymbol{1}_{j}})
, where
\boldsymbol{f}_{h}:\mathcal{U}\times\mathcal{U}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a sufficiently smooth numerical flux function. We consider numerical flux functions which are consistent and symmetric in the following sense:

1.

consistency:
\boldsymbol{f}_{h}^{j}(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{u})=\boldsymbol{f}^{j}(\boldsymbol{u}),\quad\forall\boldsymbol{u}\in\mathcal{U}
,

2.

symmetry:
\boldsymbol{f}_{h}^{j}(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v})=\boldsymbol{f}_{h}^{j}(\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{u}),\quad\forall\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathcal{U}
.

The framework due to Tadmor provides a third algebraic condition for a numerical flux in order to be entropy-conserving
[
58
,
57
]
. We are interested in generalizing this framework for entropy-conserving schemes due to Tadmor to general secondary quantities. Therefore we first review this framework. We speak of discrete entropy conservation when the local entropy value
s_{\boldsymbol{I}}:=s(\boldsymbol{u}_{\boldsymbol{I}})
evolves according to a discrete conservation law of the same form as (
13
) and consistently discretizes (
2
). The local entropy evolution must thus be of the following form:

\frac{d{s_{\boldsymbol{I}}}}{dt}+\sum_{j=1}^{d}\frac{1}{\Delta x_{j}}\left(\mathcal{F}_{s,\boldsymbol{I}+\boldsymbol{1}_{j}/2}^{j}-\mathcal{F}_{s,\boldsymbol{I}-\boldsymbol{1}_{j}/2}^{j}\right)=0,

(14)

where
\mathcal{F}_{s,\boldsymbol{I}+\boldsymbol{1}_{j}/2}^{j}:=\mathcal{F}_{s,h}^{j}(\boldsymbol{u}_{\boldsymbol{I}},\boldsymbol{u}_{\boldsymbol{I}+\boldsymbol{1}_{j}})
and
\mathcal{F}_{s,h}:\mathcal{U}\times\mathcal{U}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is a consistent and symmetric numerical approximation to the entropy flux
\mathcal{F}_{s}
between the cells. Let
\boldsymbol{\eta}_{\boldsymbol{I}}:=\boldsymbol{\eta}(\boldsymbol{u}_{\boldsymbol{I}})
be a vector of local entropy variable values. To obtain (
14
) it is then required to satisfy a discrete analogue of the primal compatibility condition:

\boldsymbol{\eta}_{\boldsymbol{I}}^{T}\left(\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{I}+\boldsymbol{1}_{j}/2}^{j}-\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{I}-\boldsymbol{1}_{j}/2}^{j}\right)=\mathcal{F}_{s,\boldsymbol{I}+\boldsymbol{1}_{j}/2}^{j}-\mathcal{F}_{s,\boldsymbol{I}-\boldsymbol{1}_{j}/2}^{j}.

(15)

The discrete primal compatibility condition (
15
) is difficult to work with as the flux appears twice and three grid points are involved. Let
\overline{(\cdot)}
and
\Delta(\cdot)
applied to any function
g
denote:

\overline{g}(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v}):=\frac{g(\boldsymbol{v})+g(\boldsymbol{u})}{2},\quad\Delta g(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v}):=g(\boldsymbol{v})-g(\boldsymbol{u}),

(16)

respectively. This issue can then be solved by defining the numerical flux as:

\mathcal{F}_{s,h}(\boldsymbol{u}_{\boldsymbol{I}},\boldsymbol{u}_{\boldsymbol{I}+\boldsymbol{1}_{j}}):=\overline{\boldsymbol{\eta}}(\boldsymbol{u}_{\boldsymbol{I}},\boldsymbol{u}_{\boldsymbol{I}+\boldsymbol{1}_{j}})^{T}\boldsymbol{f}_{h}^{j}(\boldsymbol{u}_{\boldsymbol{I}},\boldsymbol{u}_{\boldsymbol{I}+\boldsymbol{1}_{j}})-\overline{\psi}_{s}^{j}(\boldsymbol{u}_{\boldsymbol{I}},\boldsymbol{u}_{\boldsymbol{I}+\boldsymbol{1}_{j}}),

(17)

which is clearly consistent if
\boldsymbol{f}_{h}^{j}
is consistent, as can be seen from (
4
). With the numerical entropy flux defined as in (
17
), we can instead use a discrete dual compatibility condition:

\boldsymbol{f}_{h}^{j}(\boldsymbol{u}_{\boldsymbol{I}},\boldsymbol{u}_{\boldsymbol{I}+\boldsymbol{1}_{j}})^{T}\Delta\boldsymbol{\eta}(\boldsymbol{u}_{\boldsymbol{I}},\boldsymbol{u}_{\boldsymbol{I}+\boldsymbol{1}_{j}})=\Delta\psi_{s}^{j}(\boldsymbol{u}_{\boldsymbol{I}},\boldsymbol{u}_{\boldsymbol{I}+\boldsymbol{1}_{j}}).

(18)

To show that this condition equivalently leads to (
14
), we give a proof in
Appendix A
. This condition is due to Tadmor and beares his name
[
57
]
. For reference, we provide the following formal definition. Let
\boldsymbol{\psi}_{s}:=[\psi_{s}^{1},...,\psi_{s}^{d}]^{T}
.

Definition 2

(Tadmor condition)
.

A symmetric and consistent numerical flux function
\boldsymbol{f}_{h}^{j}:\mathcal{U}\times\mathcal{U}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n}
for a conservation law (
1
) with an entropy pair
(s,\boldsymbol{\mathcal{F}}_{s})
with entropy variables
\boldsymbol{\eta}
and entropy flux potential
\boldsymbol{\psi}_{s}
is said to satisfy the Tadmor condition if:

\Delta\psi_{s}^{j}(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v})=\boldsymbol{f}_{h}^{j}(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v})^{T}\Delta\boldsymbol{\eta}(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v}),

(19)

for all pairs
\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathcal{U}
.

Note that for any
\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathcal{U}
the set of vectors
\mathcal{P}_{s}^{j}(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v}):=\{\boldsymbol{w}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\Delta\psi_{s}^{j}(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v})-\boldsymbol{w}^{T}\Delta\boldsymbol{\eta}(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v})=0\}
is non-empty, as the case
\Delta\boldsymbol{\eta}(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v})=0
implies
\boldsymbol{u}=\boldsymbol{v}
and thus
\Delta\psi_{s}^{j}(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v})=0
, due to injectivity of the entropy variables. Non-emptiness of
\mathcal{P}_{s}^{j}(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v})
for any
\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathcal{U}
is a necessary condition for the existence of a numerical flux satisfying the Tadmor condition.

3.2
Secondary-structure-consistent discretization

Our goal is to establish similar conditions on the numerical flux functions
\boldsymbol{f}_{h}^{j}
used in (
13
) so that, given a general secondary structure, the discretization (
13
) also implies a consistent discretization of the general balance law (
7
) for
q
. This discrete balance law should be conservative in the same sense as (
13
) if the secondary structure is conservative and otherwise has a clearly defined conservative and non-conservative part. Letting
q_{\boldsymbol{I}}:=q(\boldsymbol{u}_{\boldsymbol{I}})
, we will look for discrete secondary balance laws of the form:

\displaystyle\frac{d{q_{\boldsymbol{I}}}}{dt}

\displaystyle+\sum_{j=1}^{d}\frac{1}{\Delta x_{j}}\left(\mathcal{F}_{q,\boldsymbol{I}+\boldsymbol{1}_{j}/2}^{j}-\mathcal{F}_{q,\boldsymbol{I}-\boldsymbol{1}_{j}/2}^{j}\right)+\sum_{j=1}^{d}\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\left[c_{q}^{j}\Delta\mathcal{G}_{q}^{j}\right]_{\boldsymbol{I}+\boldsymbol{1}_{j}/2}}{\Delta x_{j}}+\frac{\left[c_{q}^{j}\Delta\mathcal{G}_{q}^{j}\right]_{\boldsymbol{I}-\boldsymbol{1}_{j}/2}}{\Delta x_{j}}\right)=0.

(20)

This form is a consistent discretization of (
7
) and, as we will show, follows naturally from an extension of the approach taken by Tadmor for entropy
[
58
,
57
]
. In particular,
\mathcal{F}_{q,\boldsymbol{I}+\boldsymbol{1}_{j}/2}^{j}:=\mathcal{F}_{q,h}^{j}(\boldsymbol{u}_{\boldsymbol{I}},\boldsymbol{u}_{\boldsymbol{I}+\boldsymbol{1}_{j}})
where
\mathcal{F}_{q,h}:\mathcal{U}\times\mathcal{U}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is a consistent and symmetric numerical approximation to the conservative flux
\mathcal{F}_{q}
of (
7
) between grid cells. Furthermore,
\left[c_{q}^{j}\Delta\mathcal{G}_{q}^{j}\right]_{\boldsymbol{I}+\boldsymbol{1}_{j}/2}:=\left[c_{q}^{j}\Delta\mathcal{G}_{q}^{j}\right](\boldsymbol{u}_{\boldsymbol{I}},\boldsymbol{u}_{\boldsymbol{I}+\boldsymbol{1}_{j}})
where
\left[c_{q}^{j}\Delta\mathcal{G}_{q}^{j}\right]:\mathcal{U}\times\mathcal{U}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is an anti-symmetric function that, when divided by the corresponding cell-width
\Delta x_{j}
, approximates the non-conservative term in (
7
) consistently between the cells. Here, anti-symmetric is meant in the sense that, for all
\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathcal{U}
:

\left[c_{q}^{j}\Delta\mathcal{G}_{q}^{j}\right](\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v})=-\left[c_{q}^{j}\Delta\mathcal{G}_{q}^{j}\right](\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{u}).

When deriving a generalization of (
18
), it will become clear why this anti-symmetry is necessary. As
\left[c_{q}^{j}\Delta\mathcal{G}_{q}^{j}\right]
often describes a form of physical work performed on or by the system, we will refer to
\left[c_{q}^{j}\Delta\mathcal{G}_{q}^{j}\right]
as the
numerical work term
. For reasons that will become clear later, we do not fully specify the work term; we only specify its behavior.

We will proceed in a similar fashion as for entropy to derive a generalization of the Tadmor condition (
19
). Let
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{\boldsymbol{I}}:=\boldsymbol{\xi}(\boldsymbol{u}_{\boldsymbol{I}})
, then a discrete generalized primal compatibility condition can be stated as follows:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\xi}_{\boldsymbol{I}}^{T}\left(\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{I}+\boldsymbol{1}_{j}/2}^{j}-\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{I}-\boldsymbol{1}_{j}/2}^{j}\right)

(21)

\displaystyle\qquad=\mathcal{F}_{q,\boldsymbol{I}+\boldsymbol{1}_{j}/2}^{j}-\mathcal{F}_{q,\boldsymbol{I}-\boldsymbol{1}_{j}/2}^{j}+\frac{1}{2}\left[c_{q}^{j}\Delta\mathcal{G}_{q}^{j}\right]_{\boldsymbol{I}+\boldsymbol{1}_{j}/2}+\frac{1}{2}\left[c_{q}^{j}\Delta\mathcal{G}_{q}^{j}\right]_{\boldsymbol{I}-\boldsymbol{1}_{j}/2}.

The manipulations to obtain (
20
) from the discrete generalized primal compatibility condition are straightforward and mimic exactly the procedure at the differential level (
9
):

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\xi}_{\boldsymbol{I}}^{T}\left(\frac{d{\boldsymbol{u}_{\boldsymbol{I}}}}{dt}+\sum_{j=1}^{d}\frac{1}{\Delta x_{j}}\left(\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{I}+\boldsymbol{1}_{j}/2}^{j}-\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{I}-\boldsymbol{1}_{j}/2}^{j}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\xi}_{\boldsymbol{I}}^{T}\frac{d{\boldsymbol{u}_{\boldsymbol{I}}}}{dt}+\sum_{j=1}^{d}\frac{1}{\Delta x_{j}}\boldsymbol{\xi}_{\boldsymbol{I}}^{T}\left(\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{I}+\boldsymbol{1}_{j}/2}^{j}-\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{I}-\boldsymbol{1}_{j}/2}^{j}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{d{q_{\boldsymbol{I}}}}{dt}+\sum_{j=1}^{d}\frac{1}{\Delta x_{j}}\left(\mathcal{F}_{q,\boldsymbol{I}+\boldsymbol{1}_{j}/2}^{j}-\mathcal{F}_{q,\boldsymbol{I}-\boldsymbol{1}_{j}/2}^{j}\right)+\sum_{j=1}^{d}\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\left[c_{q}^{j}\Delta\mathcal{G}_{q}^{j}\right]_{\boldsymbol{I}+\boldsymbol{1}_{j}/2}}{\Delta x_{j}}+\frac{\left[c_{q}^{j}\Delta\mathcal{G}_{q}^{j}\right]_{\boldsymbol{I}-\boldsymbol{1}_{j}/2}}{\Delta x_{j}}\right).

However, the discrete generalized primal compatibility condition is cumbersome to work with and again requires the consideration of three grid points. As for entropy, the generalized dual compatibility condition provides a solution. Defining a conservative numerical
q
-flux as:

\mathcal{F}_{q,h}(\boldsymbol{u}_{\boldsymbol{I}},\boldsymbol{u}_{\boldsymbol{I}+\boldsymbol{1}_{j}}):=\overline{\boldsymbol{\xi}}(\boldsymbol{u}_{\boldsymbol{I}},\boldsymbol{u}_{\boldsymbol{I}+\boldsymbol{1}_{j}})^{T}\boldsymbol{f}_{h}^{j}(\boldsymbol{u}_{\boldsymbol{I}},\boldsymbol{u}_{\boldsymbol{I}+\boldsymbol{1}_{j}})-\overline{\psi}_{q}^{j}(\boldsymbol{u}_{\boldsymbol{I}},\boldsymbol{u}_{\boldsymbol{I}+\boldsymbol{1}_{j}}),

(22)

we can define the following
discrete generalized dual compatibility condition
:

\Delta\psi_{q}^{j}(\boldsymbol{u}_{\boldsymbol{I}},\boldsymbol{u}_{\boldsymbol{I}+\boldsymbol{1}_{j}})-\boldsymbol{f}_{h}^{j}(\boldsymbol{u}_{\boldsymbol{I}},\boldsymbol{u}_{\boldsymbol{I}+\boldsymbol{1}_{j}})^{T}\Delta\boldsymbol{\xi}(\boldsymbol{u}_{\boldsymbol{I}},\boldsymbol{u}_{\boldsymbol{I}+\boldsymbol{1}_{j}})=\left[c_{q}^{j}\Delta\mathcal{G}_{q}^{j}\right](\boldsymbol{u}_{\boldsymbol{I}},\boldsymbol{u}_{\boldsymbol{I}+\boldsymbol{1}_{j}}).

(23)

Here, we see that the anti-symmetry of
\left[c_{q}^{j}\Delta\mathcal{G}_{q}^{j}\right]
is necessary. Specifically, both
\Delta\psi_{q}^{j}
and
\Delta\boldsymbol{\xi}
are anti-symmetric functions, while the flux
\boldsymbol{f}_{h}^{j}
is assumed symmetric, hence the left side of (
23
) is anti-symmetric. Equality then necessarily forces the numerical work term
\left[c_{q}^{j}\Delta\mathcal{G}_{q}^{j}\right]
to be anti-symmetric. Before we show that (
23
) leads to (
20
), we introduce some short notation and useful identities. When the arguments of
g
in (
16
) originate from the grid function
\boldsymbol{u}_{\boldsymbol{I}}
, it will be useful to define the following even more compact notation:

\displaystyle\overline{g}_{\boldsymbol{I}+\boldsymbol{1}_{j}/2}:=\overline{g}(\boldsymbol{u}_{\boldsymbol{I}},\boldsymbol{u}_{\boldsymbol{I}+\boldsymbol{1}_{j}}),\quad\Delta g_{\boldsymbol{I}+\boldsymbol{1}_{j}/2}

\displaystyle:=\Delta g(\boldsymbol{u}_{\boldsymbol{I}},\boldsymbol{u}_{\boldsymbol{I}+\boldsymbol{1}_{j}}).

Two identities also hold between the
\overline{(\cdot)}
and
\Delta(\cdot)
operators, which we denote in the compact grid function notation, as that is the setting in which we will mainly use them:

\displaystyle g_{\boldsymbol{I}}=\overline{g}_{\boldsymbol{I}\pm\boldsymbol{1}_{j}/2}\mp\frac{1}{2}\Delta g_{\boldsymbol{I}\pm\boldsymbol{1}_{j}/2},

(24)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\Delta g_{\boldsymbol{I}+\boldsymbol{1}_{j}/2}+\frac{1}{2}\Delta g_{\boldsymbol{I}-\boldsymbol{1}_{j}/2}=\overline{g}_{\boldsymbol{I}+\boldsymbol{1}_{j}/2}-\overline{g}_{\boldsymbol{I}-\boldsymbol{1}_{j}/2}.

(25)

We are now ready to provide the following derivation to show that (
23
) leads to (
20
), which is one of our main contributions in this article:

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\xi}_{\boldsymbol{I}}^{T}\left(\frac{d{\boldsymbol{u}_{\boldsymbol{I}}}}{dt}+\sum_{j=1}^{d}\frac{1}{\Delta x_{j}}\left(\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{I}+\boldsymbol{1}_{j}/2}^{j}-\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{I}-\boldsymbol{1}_{j}/2}^{j}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\xi}_{\boldsymbol{I}}^{T}\frac{d{\boldsymbol{u}_{\boldsymbol{I}}}}{dt}+\sum_{j=1}^{d}\frac{1}{\Delta x_{j}}\boldsymbol{\xi}_{\boldsymbol{I}}^{T}\left(\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{I}+\boldsymbol{1}_{j}/2}^{j}-\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{I}-\boldsymbol{1}_{j}/2}^{j}\right)

\displaystyle\overset{\eqref{eq:usefulid}}{=}\frac{d{q_{\boldsymbol{I}}}}{dt}+\sum_{j=1}^{d}\frac{1}{\Delta x_{j}}\left(\overline{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{\boldsymbol{I}+\boldsymbol{1}_{j}/2}^{T}\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{I}+\boldsymbol{1}_{j}/2}^{j}-\overline{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{\boldsymbol{I}-\boldsymbol{1}_{j}/2}^{T}\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{I}-\boldsymbol{1}_{j}/2}^{j}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad-\sum_{j=1}^{d}\frac{1}{\Delta x_{j}}\frac{1}{2}\left(\left(\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{I}+\boldsymbol{1}_{j}/2}^{j}\right)^{T}\Delta\boldsymbol{\xi}_{\boldsymbol{I}+\boldsymbol{1}_{j}/2}+\left(\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{I}-\boldsymbol{1}_{j}/2}^{j}\right)^{T}\Delta\boldsymbol{\xi}_{\boldsymbol{I}-\boldsymbol{1}_{j}/2}\right)

\displaystyle\overset{\eqref{eq:numqflux}}{=}\frac{d{q_{\boldsymbol{I}}}}{dt}+\sum_{j=1}^{d}\frac{1}{\Delta x_{j}}\left(\mathcal{F}_{q,\boldsymbol{I}+\boldsymbol{1}_{j}/2}^{j}-\mathcal{F}_{q,\boldsymbol{I}-\boldsymbol{1}_{j}/2}^{j}\right)+\sum_{j=1}^{d}\frac{1}{\Delta x_{j}}\left(\overline{\psi}_{q,\boldsymbol{I}+\boldsymbol{1}_{j}/2}^{j}-\overline{\psi}_{q,\boldsymbol{I}-\boldsymbol{1}_{j}/2}^{j}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad-\sum_{j=1}^{d}\frac{1}{\Delta x_{j}}\frac{1}{2}\left(\left(\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{I}+\boldsymbol{1}_{j}/2}^{j}\right)^{T}\Delta\boldsymbol{\xi}_{\boldsymbol{I}+\boldsymbol{1}_{j}/2}+\left(\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{I}-\boldsymbol{1}_{j}/2}^{j}\right)^{T}\Delta\boldsymbol{\xi}_{\boldsymbol{I}-\boldsymbol{1}_{j}/2}\right)

\displaystyle\overset{\eqref{eq:commdifavg}}{=}\frac{d{q_{\boldsymbol{I}}}}{dt}+\sum_{j=1}^{d}\frac{1}{\Delta x_{j}}\left(\mathcal{F}_{q,\boldsymbol{I}+\boldsymbol{1}_{j}/2}^{j}-\mathcal{F}_{q,\boldsymbol{I}-\boldsymbol{1}_{j}/2}^{j}\right)+\sum_{j=1}^{d}\frac{1}{\Delta x_{j}}\frac{1}{2}\left(\Delta{\psi}_{q,\boldsymbol{I}+\boldsymbol{1}_{j}/2}^{j}+\Delta{\psi}_{q,\boldsymbol{I}-\boldsymbol{1}_{j}/2}^{j}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad-\sum_{j=1}^{d}\frac{1}{\Delta x_{j}}\frac{1}{2}\left(\left(\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{I}+\boldsymbol{1}_{j}/2}^{j}\right)^{T}\Delta\boldsymbol{\xi}_{\boldsymbol{I}+\boldsymbol{1}_{j}/2}+\left(\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{I}-\boldsymbol{1}_{j}/2}^{j}\right)^{T}\Delta\boldsymbol{\xi}_{\boldsymbol{I}-\boldsymbol{1}_{j}/2}\right)

\displaystyle\overset{\eqref{eq:numdcondq}}{=}\frac{d{q_{\boldsymbol{I}}}}{dt}+\sum_{j=1}^{d}\frac{1}{\Delta x_{j}}\left(\mathcal{F}_{q,\boldsymbol{I}+\boldsymbol{1}_{j}/2}^{j}-\mathcal{F}_{q,\boldsymbol{I}-\boldsymbol{1}_{j}/2}^{j}\right)+\sum_{j=1}^{d}\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\left[c_{q}^{j}\Delta\mathcal{G}_{q}^{j}\right]_{\boldsymbol{I}+\boldsymbol{1}_{j}/2}}{\Delta x_{j}}+\frac{\left[c_{q}^{j}\Delta\mathcal{G}_{q}^{j}\right]_{\boldsymbol{I}-\boldsymbol{1}_{j}/2}}{\Delta x_{j}}\right),

where in the third equality the identity (
24
) was used, in the fourth equality definition (
22
), in the fifth equality identity (
25
) and in the final equality the discrete generalized dual compatibility condition (
23
). The derivation again closely mimics the differential setting, where the differences in the residual function
\Delta\psi_{q}^{j}
now are used to correct the
\left(\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{I}+\boldsymbol{1}_{j}/2}^{j}\right)^{T}\Delta\boldsymbol{\xi}_{\boldsymbol{I}+\boldsymbol{1}_{j}/2}
terms to the correct numerical work terms instead of canceling them out. Unlike for entropy, however, we are not yet ready to define a generalization of the Tadmor condition at this point. We must still deal with possible degeneracies that can occur in (
23
) due to the lack of a convexity requirement on
q
.

3.3
Generalized Tadmor condition and weak discrete null-consistency

Inspecting the discrete dual compatibility condition (
23
), we can note the following. Evaluating (
23
) at some pair
\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathcal{U}
gives a relation between the numerical work term
[c_{q}^{j}\Delta\mathcal{G}_{q}^{j}]
, change in
\psi_{q}^{h}
and
\boldsymbol{\xi}
between
\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and the form of the numerical flux
\boldsymbol{f}_{h}^{j}(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v})
. In this relation the form of the numerical flux
\boldsymbol{f}_{h}^{j}
and of the numerical work term
\left[c_{q}^{j}\Delta\mathcal{G}_{q}^{j}\right]
are degrees of freedom that can be determined to satisfy the discrete dual compatibility in the respective
\boldsymbol{v}-\boldsymbol{u}
direction.

However, the fact that for general secondary structures
q
is not necessarily a convex function, puts constraints on the form of the numerical work term
\left[c_{q}^{j}\Delta\mathcal{G}_{q}^{j}\right]
. Namely, where the entropy variables
\boldsymbol{\eta}
are injective due to the convexity of the entropy function
s
as mentioned in
subsection 2.1
,
\boldsymbol{\xi}
can fail to be injective. This is the case as we assume there is no such convexity constraint on
q
. Consequently, there can exist pairs of points
\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathcal{U}
so that
\Delta\boldsymbol{\xi}(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v})=0
while
\boldsymbol{u}\neq\boldsymbol{v}
. In such a case, the contribution of the numerical flux to the discrete dual compatibility condition (
23
) drops out like in (
12
). When this occurs, equality should still hold in (
23
) if we want our discretization (
13
) to satisfy (
20
). Therefore, when
\Delta\boldsymbol{\xi}_{\boldsymbol{I}+\boldsymbol{1}_{j}/2}=0
, we must require:

\Delta\psi_{q,\boldsymbol{I}+\boldsymbol{1}_{j}/2}^{j}=\left[c_{q}^{j}\Delta\mathcal{G}_{q}^{j}\right]_{\boldsymbol{I}+\boldsymbol{1}_{j}/2}.

(26)

This is a discrete analogue to the null-consistency property (
12
) of general secondary structures for the non-conservative numerical work term. To obtain a scheme that is always consistent with (
20
), we
must
require this discrete null-consistency as an additional constraint. As we will see later, it will also be needed to consider a stronger form of discrete null-consistency, hence we refer to this form as
weak discrete null-consistency
. We provide the following more precise definition.

Definition 3

(Weak discrete null-consistency)
.

An anti-symmetric and consistent numerical work term
\left[c_{q}^{j}\Delta\mathcal{G}_{q}^{j}\right]:\mathcal{U}\times\mathcal{U}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
for a discretization of a conservation law (
1
) with a secondary structure
(q,\boldsymbol{\mathcal{F}}_{q},\boldsymbol{\mathcal{G}}_{q},\boldsymbol{c}_{q})
with gradient
\boldsymbol{\xi}
and residual function
\boldsymbol{\psi}_{q}
is said to satisfy the weak discrete null-consistency property if the following implication holds:

\Delta\boldsymbol{\xi}(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v})=0\implies\Delta\psi_{q}^{j}(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v})=\left[c_{q}^{j}\Delta\mathcal{G}_{q}^{j}\right](\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v}),

(27)

for all
\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathcal{U}
.

When a discretely null-consistent numerical work term
\left[c_{q}^{j}\Delta\mathcal{G}_{q}^{j}\right]
is found, then for any
\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathcal{U}
the set
\mathcal{P}_{q}^{j}(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v}):=\{\boldsymbol{w}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\Delta\psi_{q}^{j}(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v})-\boldsymbol{w}^{T}\Delta\boldsymbol{\xi}(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v})=\left[c_{q}^{j}\Delta\mathcal{G}_{q}^{j}\right](\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v})\}
is never empty, as when
\Delta\boldsymbol{\xi}(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v})=0
we have
\mathcal{P}_{q}^{j}(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v})=\mathbb{R}^{n}
and otherwise
\mathcal{P}_{q}^{j}(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v})
is simply a hyper-plane. The non-emptiness of
\mathcal{P}_{q}^{j}(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v})
for all
\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathcal{U}
is a necessary condition for the existence of a flux
\boldsymbol{f}_{h}^{j}
that always satisfies (
23
). We are now ready to generalize the Tadmor condition to general secondary structures.

Definition 4

(Generalized Tadmor condition)
.

A symmetric and consistent numerical flux function
\boldsymbol{f}_{h}^{j}:\mathcal{U}\times\mathcal{U}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n}
for a conservation law (
1
) with a secondary structure
(q,\boldsymbol{\mathcal{F}}_{q},\boldsymbol{\mathcal{G}}_{q},\boldsymbol{c}_{q})
with
q
-gradient
\boldsymbol{\xi}
and residual function
\boldsymbol{\psi}_{q}

\Delta\psi_{q}^{j}(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v})-\boldsymbol{f}_{h}^{j}(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v})^{T}\Delta\boldsymbol{\xi}(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v})=\left[c_{q}^{j}\Delta\mathcal{G}_{q}^{j}\right](\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v}),\quad\forall\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathcal{U},

where

is an anti-symmetric and consistent numerical work term satisfying weak discrete null-consistency (

).

This condition reduces to the standard Tadmor condition,
Definition 2
, if the secondary structure is an entropy pair. We have proven the following theorem.

.

(Satisfying discrete null-consistency)

3.4
The method of jump expansions using discrete gradients

Having derived a generalized Tadmor condition, we now propose and analyze a method for finding solutions given a secondary structure. The usual method for solving Tadmor’s entropy conservation condition is the method of jump expansions
[
50
,
14
,
7
,
49
]
. The method is systematic and applicable to any entropy-conserving system of conservation laws. For these reasons, we will generalize this approach to the generalized Tadmor condition and analyze some of its properties in this setting.

In our applications, however, a problem immediately arises. The method of jump expansions requires explicit knowledge of all involved functions
\boldsymbol{\psi}_{s}
and
\boldsymbol{\eta}
. In our application of interest, compressible flow of real gases, these functions
\boldsymbol{\psi}_{q}
and
\boldsymbol{\xi}
and the additional functions
\boldsymbol{c}_{q}
and
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{G}}_{q}
may only be defined up to an arbitrary function. For us, this arbitrary function is specified by a choice of thermodynamic model, i.e. an equation of state, as will be explained in
section 4
. To solve this, we will introduce discrete gradient operators
[
20
,
27
,
40
]
, a discretization method that enables us to apply jump expansions to secondary structures defined only up to an arbitrary function.

The method of jump expansions finds a numerical flux by deriving an
n\times n
linear system for which a solution satisfies the scalar Tadmor condition (
19
) for any fixed
\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathcal{U}
. The linear system is constructed by expanding the jumps involved in the Tadmor condition with respect to a common set of auxiliary variables
\boldsymbol{z}:\mathcal{U}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n}
obtained from a change-of-variables (sufficiently smooth and bijective with smooth inverse). Discrete gradient operators can be very helpful in these expansions. We will first focus on constructing these expansions, then analyze the properties of the resulting linear system for general secondary structures. This analysis is mainly based on functions defined on
\mathcal{U}
, without any spatial considerations related to the physical domain. Hence, for better readability, we will suppress the superscript
(\cdot)^{j}
denoting physical coordinate directions throughout this subsection.

3.4.1
Jump expansions

For the method of jump expansions, it is crucial that the Tadmor condition consists of a linear combination of
\Delta(\cdot)
expressions with coefficients consisting of the numerical flux components. To ensure the same approach works in our generalized setting we assume that the numerical work term takes the form
[c_{q}\Delta\mathcal{G}_{q}](\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v})=c_{q}(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v})\Delta\mathcal{G}_{q}(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v})
, where
c_{q}(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v})
is any symmetric and consistent average of
c_{q}
. However, more generally, the approach also works for a sum of multiple jumps. If the numerical work term is not a sum of
\Delta(\cdot)
terms the analysis and method described in what follows does not apply.

For the functions involved in the generalized Tadmor condition, jump expansions generally take the following form:

\Delta g(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{g,i}(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v})\Delta z_{i}(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v}),\\

where
g\in\{\psi_{q},\xi_{1},...,\xi_{n},\mathcal{G}_{q}\}
and
a_{g,i}:\mathcal{U}\times\mathcal{U}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
are expansion coefficients, constructed by suitable applications of the mean value theorem (cf.
[
50
]
). This approach of constructing expansion coefficients is particularly effective when jumps in univariate functions of
z_{i}
for some
i
can be isolated during the expansion process. Moreover, this approach is not possible when functions are not explicitly or only partially defined, as in our case. By partially defined we mean for example that
g(\boldsymbol{z})=3z_{1}^{2}+2z_{2}+g_{u}(\boldsymbol{z})
where
g_{u}
is some arbitrary user-supplied function. With some algebra the jumps
\Delta(3z_{1}^{2})
and
\Delta(2z_{2})
are easily expanded in terms of
\Delta z_{1}
and
\Delta z_{2}
, however the user-supplied function
g_{u}
cannot be expanded.

To still apply jump expansions in this partially defined and/or multivariate setting, we propose using discrete gradient operators
[
20
,
27
,
40
]
, which are gradient discretization methods satisfying the following definition
[
20
,
40
]
. Let
\mathcal{P}
be an open subset of
\mathbb{R}^{m}
.

Definition 5

(Discrete gradient functions)
.

A discrete gradient for a differentiable function
g:\mathcal{P}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is a continuous mapping
\widetilde{\nabla}g:\mathcal{P}\times\mathcal{P}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{m}
with the following properties.

1.

Discrete gradient,
\widetilde{\nabla}g(\boldsymbol{p}_{1},\boldsymbol{p}_{2})^{T}(\boldsymbol{p}_{2}-\boldsymbol{p}_{1})=\Delta g(\boldsymbol{p}_{1},\boldsymbol{p}_{2})\quad\forall\boldsymbol{p}_{1},\boldsymbol{p}_{2}\in\mathcal{P}
.

2.

Consistency,
\widetilde{\nabla}g(\boldsymbol{p},\boldsymbol{p})=\nabla g(\boldsymbol{p})\quad\forall\boldsymbol{p}\in\mathcal{P}
.

Note that, according to the discrete gradient property in
Definition 5
, the components of discrete gradients can precisely be used as expansion coefficients in the jump expansions. For our convenience we denote
\widetilde{\nabla}_{\boldsymbol{z}}g(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v}):=\widetilde{\nabla}g(\boldsymbol{z}(\boldsymbol{u}),\boldsymbol{z}(\boldsymbol{v}))
when the gradient is with respect to
\boldsymbol{z}
. We can thus use discrete gradients with respect to
\boldsymbol{z}
to construct our expansions:

\displaystyle\Delta\psi_{q}(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v})

\displaystyle=\widetilde{\nabla}_{\boldsymbol{z}}\psi_{q}(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v})^{T}\Delta\boldsymbol{z}(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v}),

\displaystyle\Delta\boldsymbol{\xi}(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v})

\displaystyle=\widetilde{\nabla}_{\boldsymbol{z}}\boldsymbol{\xi}(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v})^{T}\Delta\boldsymbol{z}(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v}),

(29)

\displaystyle\Delta\mathcal{G}_{q}(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v})

\displaystyle=\widetilde{\nabla}_{\boldsymbol{z}}\mathcal{G}_{q}(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v})^{T}\Delta\boldsymbol{z}(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v}).

Here
\widetilde{\nabla}_{\boldsymbol{z}}\boldsymbol{\xi}(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v})\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
contains the discrete gradient of
\xi_{i}
as its
i
-th column. Discrete gradient operators are operators that, given any arbitrary sufficiently smooth function
g
, produce a valid discrete gradient function
\widetilde{\nabla}g
. We will see that this property lets us define, e.g., entropy-conserving fluxes for general equations of state. For
n>1
, discrete gradient operators are not unique
[
40
]
, so we list a number of classical and/or practically useful examples in
Appendix B
.

3.4.2
Linear system

We will now focus on the construction and properties of the linear system. By substituting the discrete gradient expansions (
29
) into the generalized Tadmor condition (
28
), we obtain the following expression:

\left(\widetilde{\nabla}_{\boldsymbol{z}}\psi_{q}(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v})-c_{q}(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v})\widetilde{\nabla}_{\boldsymbol{z}}\mathcal{G}_{q}(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v})-\widetilde{\nabla}_{\boldsymbol{z}}\boldsymbol{\xi}(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v})\boldsymbol{f}_{h}(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{v}})\right)^{T}\Delta\boldsymbol{z}(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v})=0.

A linear system that can be solved for a numerical flux can then be obtained by requiring:

\widetilde{\nabla}_{\boldsymbol{z}}\psi_{q}(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v})-c_{q}(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v})\widetilde{\nabla}_{\boldsymbol{z}}\mathcal{G}_{q}(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v})-\widetilde{\nabla}_{\boldsymbol{z}}\boldsymbol{\xi}(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v})\boldsymbol{f}_{h}(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{v}})=0.

(30)

This system and, if they exist, its solutions, depend on the choice of auxiliary variables
\boldsymbol{z}(\boldsymbol{u})
through the gradients and the specific form of the discrete gradient functions. Thus, different numerical fluxes can potentially be obtained for different expansion variables
\boldsymbol{z}
and discrete gradient functions
[
7
,
49
]
. In the case of entropy pairs, by consistency of the discrete gradient, we know that at
\boldsymbol{v}=\boldsymbol{u}
a unique solution exists (the differential flux
\boldsymbol{f}(\boldsymbol{u})
) since
\nabla_{\boldsymbol{u}}\boldsymbol{z}(\boldsymbol{u})
and
\nabla_{\boldsymbol{u}}\boldsymbol{\eta}(\boldsymbol{u})
are invertible. By continuity of the determinant and discrete gradients, this argument extends to
\boldsymbol{v}
for which
||\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{v}||
is sufficiently small. Such an argument cannot be made for general secondary structures as
\nabla_{\boldsymbol{u}}\boldsymbol{\xi}(\boldsymbol{u})
can generally not be assumed to be non-singular. We should therefore not expect to recover unique fluxes from a single generalized Tadmor condition, but rather to obtain general solution sets. From these solution sets, numerical fluxes can be chosen that satisfy other desirable properties or design criteria.

3.4.3
Strong discrete null-consistency

For fixed
\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathcal{U}
, the system in (
30
) has at least one solution if
\widetilde{\nabla}_{\boldsymbol{z}}\psi_{q}(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v})-c_{q}(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v})\widetilde{\nabla}_{\boldsymbol{z}}\mathcal{G}_{q}(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v})\in\text{col}(\widetilde{\nabla}_{\boldsymbol{z}}\boldsymbol{\xi}(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v}))
. This condition is, in fact, a stronger form of discrete null-consistency as is given in definition (
3
). To see this, we note that a vector is an element of
\text{col}(\widetilde{\nabla}_{\boldsymbol{z}}\boldsymbol{\xi}(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v}))
if and only if it is orthogonal to any element of
\text{ker}(\widetilde{\nabla}_{\boldsymbol{z}}\boldsymbol{\xi}(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v})^{T})
. Thus for existence of a solution at
\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathcal{U}
, we require that for all
\delta\boldsymbol{z}\in\ker(\widetilde{\nabla}_{\boldsymbol{z}}\boldsymbol{\xi}(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v})^{T})
:

\widetilde{\nabla}_{\boldsymbol{z}}\psi_{q}(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v})^{T}\delta\boldsymbol{z}=c_{q}(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v})\widetilde{\nabla}_{\boldsymbol{z}}\mathcal{G}_{q}(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v})^{T}\delta\boldsymbol{z},

(31)

must hold. From this relation, (
12
) can immediately be recognized. In contrast to the scalar condition for weak discrete null-consistency, this is a full discrete vector analogue of (
12
). This
strong discrete null-consistency
property implies weak discrete null-consistency. Assume
\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathcal{U}
are such that
\Delta\boldsymbol{\xi}(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v})=0
, then, by the discrete gradient property of discrete gradient functions:

0=\Delta\boldsymbol{\xi}(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v})=\widetilde{\nabla}_{\boldsymbol{z}}\boldsymbol{\xi}(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v})^{T}\Delta\boldsymbol{z}(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v}),

so
\Delta\boldsymbol{z}(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v})\in\ker(\widetilde{\nabla}_{\boldsymbol{z}}\boldsymbol{\xi}(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v})^{T})
. Hence, by strong discrete null-consistency:

\displaystyle\Delta\psi_{q}(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v})

\displaystyle=\widetilde{\nabla}_{\boldsymbol{z}}\psi_{q}(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v})^{T}\Delta\boldsymbol{z}(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v})

\displaystyle=c_{q}(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v})\widetilde{\nabla}_{\boldsymbol{z}}\mathcal{G}_{q}(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v})^{T}\Delta\boldsymbol{z}(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v})=c_{q}(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v})\Delta\mathcal{G}_{q}(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v}),

and so the implication of weak null-consistency holds. The extra constraints that need to be satisfied in addition to weak discrete null-consistency are a consequence of the fact that we are now solving a linear system (
30
) instead of the scalar generalized Tadmor condition (
28
).

3.4.4
Flux symmetry and consistency

If they exist, solutions to (
30
) satisfy a certain notion of symmetry. In particular, if symmetric discrete gradients (i.e.
\widetilde{\nabla}g(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})=\widetilde{\nabla}g(\boldsymbol{y},\boldsymbol{x})
) are used in (
30

30

\boldsymbol{u}
and
\boldsymbol{v}
. This holds since the linear system itself is invariant to such an exchange. We also have a notion of consistency. Take
\boldsymbol{u}=\boldsymbol{v}
, then by consistency of the discrete gradient functions:

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\widetilde{\nabla}_{\boldsymbol{z}}\psi_{q}(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{u})-c_{q}(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{u})\widetilde{\nabla}_{\boldsymbol{z}}\mathcal{G}_{q}(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{u})-\widetilde{\nabla}_{\boldsymbol{z}}\boldsymbol{\xi}(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{u})\boldsymbol{f}_{h}(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{u})

\displaystyle=\nabla_{\boldsymbol{z}}\psi_{q}(\boldsymbol{u})-c_{q}(\boldsymbol{u})\nabla_{\boldsymbol{z}}\mathcal{G}_{q}(\boldsymbol{u})-\nabla_{\boldsymbol{z}}\boldsymbol{\xi}(\boldsymbol{u})\boldsymbol{f}_{h}(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{u})

\displaystyle=\nabla_{\boldsymbol{z}}\boldsymbol{u}(\boldsymbol{u})\left(\nabla_{\boldsymbol{u}}\psi_{q}(\boldsymbol{u})-c_{q}(\boldsymbol{u})\nabla_{\boldsymbol{u}}\mathcal{G}_{q}(\boldsymbol{u})-\nabla_{\boldsymbol{u}}\boldsymbol{\xi}(\boldsymbol{u})\boldsymbol{f}_{h}(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{u})\right)

\displaystyle=\nabla_{\boldsymbol{z}}\boldsymbol{u}(\boldsymbol{u})\left(\nabla_{\boldsymbol{u}}\boldsymbol{\xi}(\boldsymbol{u})(\boldsymbol{f}(\boldsymbol{u})-\boldsymbol{f}_{h}(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{u}))\right),

since
\nabla_{\boldsymbol{z}}\boldsymbol{u}(\boldsymbol{u})
is invertible, this hold if and only if
\nabla_{\boldsymbol{u}}\boldsymbol{\xi}(\boldsymbol{u})(\boldsymbol{f}(\boldsymbol{u})-\boldsymbol{f}_{h}(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{u}))=0
. Because
\nabla_{\boldsymbol{u}}\boldsymbol{\xi}
is symmetric, this implies that the flux
\boldsymbol{f}_{h}
is consistent at
\boldsymbol{u}
within the subspace defined by
\text{col}(\nabla_{\boldsymbol{u}}\boldsymbol{\xi}(\boldsymbol{u}))
, since:

\nabla_{\boldsymbol{u}}\boldsymbol{\xi}(\boldsymbol{u})^{T}(\boldsymbol{f}(\boldsymbol{u})-\boldsymbol{f}_{h}(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{u}))=0,

and thus the components of
\boldsymbol{f}_{h}(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{u})
and
\boldsymbol{f}(\boldsymbol{u})
along all

agree. However, the generalized Tadmor condition does not ensure consistency outside of
\text{col}(\nabla_{\boldsymbol{u}}\boldsymbol{\xi}(\boldsymbol{u}))

\text{col}(\nabla_{\boldsymbol{u}}\boldsymbol{\xi}(\boldsymbol{u}))
must be ensured by proper design choices; we will see an example of this in the following section. Once the proper design choices outside of
\text{col}(\nabla_{\boldsymbol{u}}\boldsymbol{\xi}(\boldsymbol{u}))

4
Application: numerical fluxes for real gases

In this section, we apply the discrete framework developed in the previous section to construct an entropy- and kinetic-energy-preserving numerical flux function, given in equation (

4.1
Real-gas Euler equations

The compressible flow of a fluid can be accurately modeled by the Euler equations when irreversible processes such as viscous stresses and heat transfer can be neglected. The Euler equations are given as:

\frac{\partial}{\partial{t}}\left({\begin{bmatrix}\rho\\
\boldsymbol{m}\\
E\end{bmatrix}}\right)+\sum_{i=1}^{d}\frac{\partial}{\partial{x_{i}}}\left({\begin{bmatrix}m_{i}\\
v_{i}(\boldsymbol{u})\boldsymbol{m}+p(\boldsymbol{u})\boldsymbol{\delta}_{i}\\

(32)

where
\rho:\mathbb{R}^{d}\times[0,t_{f}]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{+}
is the mass density,
\boldsymbol{m}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\times[0,t_{f}]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
the momentum density and
E:\mathbb{R}^{d}\times[0,t_{f}]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
the total-energy density. Together, these form the conservative variables of the Euler equations which we gather in a vector
\boldsymbol{u}
of conservative variables. Furthermore,
v_{i}:\mathcal{U}\rightarrow\mathbb{R},\boldsymbol{u}\mapsto m_{i}/\rho

2

For the remainder of the article

will be used for the velocity, in contrast to its usage as some point in

in earlier sections.
in direction

is the thermodynamic pressure as a function of the conservative variables.

To close the system of conservation laws in (
32

is required. Such a specification is provided by a thermodynamic equation of state (EoS). A thermodynamic EoS for a single component fluid relates two so-called natural variables
\boldsymbol{\tau}\in\mathcal{T}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{2}
(e.g. density and temperature) to any other thermodynamic quantity. Hence, an EoS provides us with a pressure in terms of natural variables

\boldsymbol{u}

\boldsymbol{\tau}
, which lets us, by some abuse of notation, define

Appendix C

Secondary quantities of the Euler equations (
32

and the classical mathematical entropy

\frac{\partial{k(\boldsymbol{u})}}{\partial t}+\sum_{i=1}^{d}\frac{\partial}{\partial{x_{i}}}\left({k(\boldsymbol{u})v_{i}(\boldsymbol{u})}\right)+\sum_{i=1}^{d}v_{i}(\boldsymbol{u})\frac{\partial}{\partial{x_{i}}}\left({p(\boldsymbol{u})}\right)=0,

4.3
Entropy-conserving and kinetic-energy-consistent flux for real gases

We will now derive an entropy-conserving numerical flux that incorporates the kinetic-energy consistency condition derived earlier. For an arbitrary EoS the entropy variables
\boldsymbol{\eta}
and residual function
\psi_{s}^{i}
are given by:

\psi_{s}^{i}(\boldsymbol{u})=\frac{v_{i}(\boldsymbol{u})p(\boldsymbol{u})}{T(\boldsymbol{u})},\quad\eta_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{u})=\frac{g(\boldsymbol{u})-\frac{1}{2}\frac{||\boldsymbol{m}||_{2}^{2}}{\rho^{2}}}{T(\boldsymbol{u})},\quad\eta_{\boldsymbol{m}}(\boldsymbol{u})=\frac{\boldsymbol{m}}{\rho T(\boldsymbol{u})},\quad\eta_{E}(\boldsymbol{u})=-\frac{1}{T(\boldsymbol{u})},

(40)

where
T:\mathcal{U}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{+}
is the thermodynamic temperature and
g:\mathcal{U}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is the specific Gibbs energy (

solutions, no numerical work term is necessary. Moreover, since the entropy variables are injective, the Tadmor condition is automatically discretely null-consistent.

Following the approach taken in the ideal-gas case in
[
7
]
, we will perform jump expansions with the auxiliary variables
\boldsymbol{z}=[\rho,\boldsymbol{v},\beta]
, where
\beta=1/T
denotes the inverted temperature. To obtain a flux that is also kinetic-energy-consistent on top of entropy conservative, we will directly substitute the relation (
39
) into the Tadmor condition for entropy. Instead of expanding on a term-by-term basis, as done for kinetic energy, we will see fit to take an integrated approach now due to the greater complexity of the involved terms. In contrast to kinetic energy, this will allow us to combine terms arising from jumps in different quantities during the expansion process, thereby reducing some of the complexity. Written out in full, the Tadmor condition (
19
) is:

\Delta\left(v_{i}p\beta\right)-f_{\rho}^{i}\Delta\left(\left(g-\frac{1}{2}||\boldsymbol{v}||^{2}\right)\beta\right)-\left(f_{\rho}^{i}\overline{\boldsymbol{v}}+\overline{p}\boldsymbol{\delta}_{i}\right)^{T}\Delta\left(\beta\boldsymbol{v}\right)+f_{E}^{i}\Delta\beta=0.

We can use the product rule (
38
) to write:

\displaystyle\Delta\left(-\frac{1}{2}||\boldsymbol{v}||^{2}\beta\right)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\overline{||\boldsymbol{v}||^{2}}\Delta\beta-\overline{\beta}\Delta\left(\frac{1}{2}||\boldsymbol{v}||^{2}\right),

and:

\displaystyle\overline{\boldsymbol{v}}^{T}\Delta\left(\beta\boldsymbol{v}\right)

\displaystyle=||\overline{\boldsymbol{v}}||^{2}\Delta\beta+\overline{\beta}\hbox{ }\overline{\boldsymbol{v}}^{T}\Delta\boldsymbol{v}

\displaystyle=||\overline{\boldsymbol{v}}||^{2}\Delta\beta+\overline{\beta}\Delta\left(\frac{1}{2}||\boldsymbol{v}||^{2}\right),

which allows us to simplify the Tadmor condition to:

\Delta\left(v_{i}p\beta\right)-f_{\rho}^{i}\Delta\left(g\beta\right)-f_{\rho}^{i}\left(||\overline{\boldsymbol{v}}||^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\overline{||\boldsymbol{v}||^{2}}\right)\Delta\beta-\overline{p}\Delta\left(v_{i}\beta\right)+f_{E}^{i}\Delta\beta=0.

The terms involving pressure in the Tadmor condition can be rewritten as follows:

\Delta(v_{i}p\beta)=\overline{pv_{i}}\Delta\beta+\overline{\beta}\Delta(pv_{i}),

and:

\displaystyle\overline{p}\Delta(v_{i}\beta)

\displaystyle=\overline{p}\hbox{ }\overline{v}_{i}\Delta\beta+\overline{p}\hbox{ }\overline{\beta}\Delta v_{i}

\displaystyle=\overline{p}\hbox{ }\overline{v}_{i}\Delta\beta+\overline{\beta}\Delta(pv_{i})-\overline{v}_{i}\overline{\beta}\Delta p

\displaystyle=2\overline{p}\hbox{ }\overline{v}_{i}\Delta\beta+\overline{\beta}\Delta(pv_{i})-\overline{v}_{i}\Delta(p\beta).

Combining these manipulations further simplifies the Tadmor condition to:

\displaystyle\overline{v}_{i}\Delta(p\beta)-f_{\rho}^{i}\Delta\left(g\beta\right)-\left(f_{\rho}^{i}\left(||\overline{\boldsymbol{v}}||^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\overline{||\boldsymbol{v}||^{2}}\right)+(2\overline{v}_{i}\overline{p}-\overline{v_{i}p})-f_{E}^{i}\right)\Delta\beta=0.

We are left with two jumps
\Delta(p\beta),\Delta(g\beta)
that are only defined up to an arbitrary choice of EoS. As both of the terms are thermodynamic in nature, they can also be described using natural EoS variables
\boldsymbol{\tau}
. A choice of natural variables that fits our set of auxiliary variables
\boldsymbol{z}
is
\boldsymbol{\tau}=[\rho,\beta]
.
A novel application of discrete gradients is now the following
. With this choice of variables, we can apply discrete gradient operators to our arbitrary EoS to still expand the jump
\Delta(p\beta),\Delta(g\beta)
even though we lack explicit algebraic forms. We then obtain the following expansions:

\displaystyle\Delta\left(p\beta\right)

\displaystyle=\widetilde{\nabla}_{\rho}(p\beta)\Delta\rho+\widetilde{\nabla}_{\beta}(p\beta)\Delta\beta,

\displaystyle\Delta\left(g\beta\right)

\displaystyle=\widetilde{\nabla}_{\rho}(g\beta)\Delta\rho+\widetilde{\nabla}_{\beta}(g\beta)\Delta\beta.

Substituting these discrete gradient expansions into the Tadmor condition and rewriting gives the following equation:

\displaystyle\left(\begin{bmatrix}\overline{v}_{i}\widetilde{\nabla}_{\rho}(p\beta)\\
\overline{v}_{i}\widetilde{\nabla}_{\beta}(p\beta)-(2\overline{v}_{i}\overline{p}-\overline{v_{i}p})\end{bmatrix}-\begin{bmatrix}\widetilde{\nabla}_{\rho}(g\beta)&0\\
\widetilde{\nabla}_{\beta}(g\beta)+\left(||\overline{\boldsymbol{v}}||^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\overline{||\boldsymbol{v}||^{2}}\right)&-1&\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}f_{\rho}^{i}\\
f_{E}^{i}\end{bmatrix}\right)^{T}\begin{bmatrix}\Delta\rho\\
\Delta\beta\end{bmatrix}=0,

for the jump expansion method. This is a reduced form resulting from the drop in the number of free parameters due to substituting the kinetic-energy condition into the Tadmor condition for entropy. It is sufficient for solutions
f_{\rho}^{i},f_{E}^{i}
to exist if:

\det\begin{bmatrix}\widetilde{\nabla}_{\rho}(g\beta)&0\\
\widetilde{\nabla}_{\beta}(g\beta)+\left(||\overline{\boldsymbol{v}}||^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\overline{||\boldsymbol{v}||^{2}}\right)&-1&\end{bmatrix}=-\widetilde{\nabla}_{\rho}(g\beta)\neq 0,

(41)

for all
\boldsymbol{u}_{1},\boldsymbol{u}_{2}\in\mathcal{U}
. We will show later that
\widetilde{\nabla}_{\rho}(g\beta)>0
for some choices of discrete gradient and under physically realistic conditions on the real-gas EoS
[
24
]
. Solving for a flux yields the final form of the mass-density flux component, along with the momentum flux components from the kinetic-energy condition (
39
) and the total-energy-density flux component:

\displaystyle f_{\rho}^{i}

\displaystyle=\widetilde{\rho}\hbox{ }\overline{v}_{i},

(42)

\displaystyle f_{\boldsymbol{m}}^{i}

\displaystyle=f_{\rho}^{i}\hbox{ }\overline{\boldsymbol{v}}+\overline{p}\boldsymbol{\delta}_{i},

\displaystyle f_{E}^{i}

\displaystyle=f_{\rho}^{i}\hbox{ }\widetilde{e}+f_{\rho}^{i}\left(||\overline{\boldsymbol{v}}||^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\overline{||\boldsymbol{v}||^{2}}\right)+(2\overline{v}_{i}\overline{p}-\overline{v_{i}p}),

where the density mean satisfies:

\widetilde{\rho}=\frac{\widetilde{\nabla}_{\rho}(p\beta)}{\widetilde{\nabla}_{\rho}(g\beta)},

(43)

and the specific-internal-energy mean satisfies:

\widetilde{e}=\widetilde{\nabla}_{\beta}(g\beta)-\frac{\widetilde{\nabla}_{\beta}(p\beta)}{\widetilde{\rho}}.

(44)

Note that the density mean has no singularities if
\widetilde{\nabla}_{\rho}(g\beta)>0
, which results from the determinant condition (
41
). When assuming
\widetilde{\nabla}_{\rho}(p\beta)>0
, and thus that
\widetilde{\rho}>0
, the internal-energy mean also has no singularities.

We will show that for some choices of discrete gradient we always have
\widetilde{\nabla}_{\rho}(p\beta),\widetilde{\nabla}_{\rho}(g\beta)>0
and thus
\widetilde{\rho}>0
under appropriate conditions on the EoS. This resulting flux (
42
) is
consistent with the kinetic-energy equation
(
34
) with numerical work term (
37
) according to
Theorem 1
and
conserves entropy for any EoS
in the sense of (
14
). In the literature, similar fluxes for the ideal-gas EoS are often referred to as a kinetic-energy- and entropy-preserving (KEEP). Here, preservation of kinetic energy is intended to mean that only pressure work can produce it. Since our flux (
42

42

5.3
Inviscid real-gas Taylor-Green vortex

We now proceed with the compressible turbulent supercritical TGV test case. We will carry out a number of inviscid and viscous experiments, starting with the inviscid case in this subsection. These experiments are meant, respectively, to test the conservation properties and stability of the scheme against under-resolution, and its physical fidelity for resolved transcritical turbulence. In particular, in inviscid form, there is no lower bound on the size of the turbulent length-scales that can form in this flow due to the lack of a turbulent dissipation mechanism. In fact, spatial scales become progressively smaller over time. Therefore, for sufficiently long times, the flow will become under-resolved at any mesh resolution. It has been noted in
[
17
]
that even in the presence of some under-resolution, practically useful results can be obtained from turbulent computations under certain conditions. It is therefore interesting to assess the stability of our KEEP-DG flux (
42
) in the presence of strong under-resolution in turbulent flows of real gases.

We will perform a comparison with other fluxes that are suitable to be used with arbitrary EoS and are noted for their entropy-conserving capabilities, specifically the recently proposed
singular
GEoS-KEEP flux
[
2
]
, which we will refer to as KEEP-S with S standing for singular, and the KEEP-Q flux of
[
59
,
35
]
. For KEEP-S, singular is meant in the sense that divisions by zero can occur. Specifically, this singularity takes place when neighboring cells have the same temperature. It should also be noted that KEEP-Q is not strictly entropy-conserving in the sense of Tadmor
[
58
,
57
]
and therefore not a GEoS-KEEP flux, but has still shown good entropy conservation properties
[
59
]
. The singularity of the KEEP-S flux in
[
2
]
is dealt with through a switching mechanism, where the flux is replaced by another non-singular flux when nearly singular. The KEEP-S flux of
[
2
]
has been shown to be very stable under non-isothermal flow configurations and to satisfy the GEoS-KEEP property; however, when combined with non-entropy-conserving schemes to deal with the singularity, the resulting scheme loses strict entropy conservation. We will show that the switching mechanism and the resulting loss of strict entropy conservation of the singular KEEP-S flux
[
2
]
can still lead to instability in flow configurations that require a lot of switch activations. We will show that our novel flux prevents these issues.

A configuration of the Taylor-Green vortex
[

,

[

]

6
Conclusion

We have proposed a framework that generalizes the tools usually applied to the analysis of convex entropy functions
[
58
,
39
,
8
]
to non-convex, non-conserved secondary functions of smooth solutions of conservation laws. Within this framework, we showed that general balance laws of secondary quantities are satisfied by solutions of conservation laws whenever the different terms in those balance laws satisfy primal or dual compatibility conditions. These compatibility conditions naturally extend those of entropy pairs and thereby place general secondary quantities into a unified theoretical setting. At the same time, the analysis highlights structural differences with the entropy case: the balance laws of general secondary quantities may involve non-conservative work terms, so that the domain integral of a general secondary quantity is not necessarily conserved for smooth solutions even in the absence of boundary effects. Moreover, the framework reveals additional structural properties that are specific to general non-conservative secondary structures. In particular, the generalized compatibility relations imply that the secondary structure necessarily satisfies a null-consistency property whenever the Hessian of the secondary quantity has a non-trivial kernel.

We have further shown that the generalized compatibility conditions of secondary structures admit discrete analogues, in close analogy with the discrete compatibility conditions of convex entropy functions. In particular, the discrete analogue of the generalized dual compatibility condition naturally yields a numerical flux condition that ensures that the discretization satisfies discrete balance laws of secondary quantities. This condition recovers Tadmor’s condition when the secondary structure is an entropy pair, showing that the classical entropy-conservative setting is contained as a special case of the present theory. For non-conservative secondary structures, there is a degree of freedom in the choice of the numerical work term. To ensure a well-posed flux condition, this choice of numerical work term must satisfy additional structural properties. Specifically, a weak discrete analogue of the null-consistency property is required to ensure that the flux condition is well posed. In addition, a stronger form of discrete null-consistency is needed for the generalized Tadmor conditions to be solvable by jump expansions. Furthermore, we demonstrated that discrete gradient operators from geometric integration
[
40
]
provide an effective tool for solving the generalized Tadmor conditions, thereby supplying a constructive mechanism for the design of admissible numerical fluxes.

This framework enables the design of stable non-dissipative, kinetic-energy- and entropy-preserving (KEEP) numerical fluxes for supercritical fluids, which require non-ideal general equations of state (GEoS). Using the proposed framework, we derived a GEoS-KEEP numerical flux for the Euler equations (
32
), referred to as KEEP-DG. The resulting flux (
42
) removes a singularity present in the recently proposed GEoS-KEEP flux in
[
2
]
, demonstrating the practical value of the generalized framework. We also showed, through a number of experiments on a transcritical extension of a canonical compressible turbulent test case, that no instabilities occur for the KEEP-DG flux and that it remains conservative to machine precision. These results indicate that KEEP-DG, derived from the generalized Tadmor framework, provides a dissipation-free and robust approach for accurately resolving compressible turbulent flows of transcritical fluids with state-of-the-art equations of state and transport models on modest grids.

Although we have focused on kinetic energy and entropy in this work, we note that another popular secondary quantity to consider in the numerical simulation of compressible flow is pressure
[
48
,
3
,
10
]
. It would be especially interesting to see whether the framework can be used to obtain numerical fluxes that satisfy the so-called pressure-equilibrium-preservation (PEP) property for real gases
[
3
]
. This type of structure-preservation property is fundamentally different from what we consider in this work, as it does not aim to satisfy an extra balance law, but only to exactly reproduce certain solutions of the conservation law (
1
). However, pressure balance laws are still often considered in the analysis of the PEP property
[
61
,
3
]
, and thus our framework could be useful here. Initial investigations of some numerical work terms for a discrete pressure balance law show that for real gases, weak discrete null-consistency is not trivial and requires extra conditions on the equation of state (EoS). In turn, verifying these conditions requires detailed knowledge of the respective EoS. We leave this as future work.

Mean value discrete gradient operator (MVDG)

\widetilde{\nabla}g(\boldsymbol{p}_{1},\boldsymbol{p}_{2}):=\int_{0}^{1}\nabla g(\boldsymbol{p}_{1}+\lambda(\boldsymbol{p}_{2}-\boldsymbol{p}_{1}))d\lambda.

\widetilde{\nabla}g(\boldsymbol{p}_{2},\boldsymbol{p}_{1}):=\nabla g\left(\frac{\boldsymbol{p}_{1}+\boldsymbol{p}_{2}}{2}\right)+\frac{\Delta g(\boldsymbol{p}_{1},\boldsymbol{p}_{2})-\nabla g\left(\frac{\boldsymbol{p}_{1}+\boldsymbol{p}_{2}}{2}\right)^{T}(\boldsymbol{p}_{2}-\boldsymbol{p}_{1})}{||\boldsymbol{p}_{2}-\boldsymbol{p}_{1}||^{2}}(\boldsymbol{p}_{2}-\boldsymbol{p}_{1}).

g(x_{k},y_{2})-g(x_{k},y_{1})\end{bmatrix}.

\text{tol}_{x}=10\epsilon+\sqrt{\epsilon}\max\{|x_{1}|,|x_{2}|\},

Appendix C
Helmholtz-energy equation of state

In our applications we are mainly interested in EoS given by a specific
4
4

4

per unit mass
Helmholtz energy
\mathcal{A}:\mathcal{T}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
. Here,
\mathcal{T}\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
is the thermodynamic state space of natural variables, for the Helmholtz energy these are mass density
\rho
and temperature
T
. In many applications of fluid dynamics the values
\rho,T
are assumed as pointwise evaluations of their associated fields
\rho,T:\mathbb{R}^{d}\times[0,t_{f}]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{+}
, which we denote with the same symbol. We gather the natural variables in a vector
\boldsymbol{\tau}\in\mathcal{T}
to ease notation. The Helmholtz energy is defined as the Legendre transformation with respect to specific entropy of the first law of thermodynamics
[
24
]
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}(\boldsymbol{\tau})

\displaystyle=e(\boldsymbol{\tau})-T\sigma(\boldsymbol{\tau}),

(57)

\displaystyle d\mathcal{A}

\displaystyle=\frac{p(\boldsymbol{\tau})}{\rho^{2}}d\rho-\sigma(\boldsymbol{\tau})dT,

(58)

where
e:\mathcal{T}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is the specific internal energy,
\sigma:\mathcal{T}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is the specific entropy, and
p:\mathcal{T}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{+}
is the thermodynamic pressure now expressed as a function of natural variables. Derived thermodynamic quantities can be computed by comparison of the differential of
\mathcal{A}
against the identity provided in (
58
):

\displaystyle\sigma(\boldsymbol{\tau})

\displaystyle=-\frac{\partial{\mathcal{A}}}{\partial{T}}(\boldsymbol{\tau}),

(59)

\displaystyle p(\boldsymbol{\tau})

\displaystyle=\rho^{2}\frac{\partial{\mathcal{A}}}{\partial{\rho}}(\boldsymbol{\tau}),

(60)

\displaystyle e(\boldsymbol{\tau})

\displaystyle=\mathcal{A}(\boldsymbol{\tau})-T\frac{\partial{\mathcal{A}}}{\partial{T}}(\boldsymbol{\tau}).

(61)

Another derived quantity we will make use of is the specific Gibbs energy
g:\mathcal{T}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, whose definition follows from thermodynamic considerations
[
24
]
:

g(\boldsymbol{\tau})=\mathcal{A}(\boldsymbol{\tau})+\rho\frac{\partial{\mathcal{A}}}{\partial{\rho}}(\boldsymbol{\tau}).

(62)

We can then continue to define the required map
\mathcal{U}\rightarrow\mathcal{T}
by considering the following definition of the total-energy density
E=\rho e+k
where:

k(\boldsymbol{u})=\frac{1}{2}\frac{||\boldsymbol{m}||^{2}}{\rho},

(63)

is the fluid’s kinetic energy. With this definition of the total energy we can express the internal energy explicitly in terms of conservative variables
e(\boldsymbol{u})=(E-k(\boldsymbol{u}))/\rho
. Realizing that the mass density in the conservative variables is identical to the mass density in the thermodynamic state space variables, we are left to solve:

E-k(\boldsymbol{u})=\rho\mathcal{A}(\boldsymbol{\tau})-\rho T\frac{\partial{\mathcal{A}}}{\partial{T}}(\boldsymbol{\tau}),

for
T
at fixed
\rho
to finish the map
\mathcal{U}\rightarrow\mathcal{T}
. The previous equation follows from equating
e(\boldsymbol{u})=e(\boldsymbol{\tau})
. If a solution
T
exists the physical consideration that
\frac{\partial{e}}{\partial{T}}(\boldsymbol{\tau})>0
for all
\boldsymbol{\tau}\in\mathcal{T}
and thus that
e(\boldsymbol{\tau})
is injective at fixed
\rho
lets us conclude
T
is unique. From here, we will simply assume that a solution indeed exists, as is often done. Finally, we will also use the squared speed of sound
c^{2}:\mathcal{T}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{+}
, which in terms of Helmholtz energies satisfies:

c^{2}(\boldsymbol{\tau})=2\rho\frac{\partial{\mathcal{A}}}{\partial{\rho}}(\boldsymbol{\tau})+\rho^{2}\frac{\partial^{2}{\mathcal{A}}}{\partial{\rho}^{2}}(\boldsymbol{\tau})-\left[\rho\frac{\partial\mathcal{A}}{\partial\rho\partial T}(\boldsymbol{\tau})\right]^{2}/\left[\frac{\partial^{2}{\mathcal{A}}}{\partial{T}^{2}}(\boldsymbol{\tau})\right],

(64)

and follows from computing the derivative of pressure
p
with respect to
\rho
while keeping the entropy
\sigma
constant.

Appendix D
Proposition proofs

D.1
Consistency

Proof.

By consistency of the arithmetic mean, the flux (
42
) can easily be seen to be consistent if the density mean (
43
) and internal-energy mean (
44
) are consistent. Let
\boldsymbol{u}\in\mathcal{U}
and
[\rho,T]=\boldsymbol{\tau}(\boldsymbol{u})
. By the consistency property of discrete gradients
Definition 5
and equations (
60
), (
62
):

\displaystyle\widetilde{\rho}(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{u})

\displaystyle=\frac{\widetilde{\nabla}_{\rho}(p\beta)(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{u})}{\widetilde{\nabla}_{\rho}(g\beta)(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{u})}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial{p/T}}{\partial{\rho}}(\rho,T)/\frac{\partial{g/T}}{\partial{\rho}}(\rho,T)

\displaystyle=\left[\frac{2\rho}{T}\frac{\partial{\mathcal{A}}}{\partial{\rho}}(\rho,T)+\frac{\rho^{2}}{T}\frac{\partial^{2}{\mathcal{A}}}{\partial{\rho}^{2}}(\rho,T)\right]/\left[\frac{2}{T}\frac{\partial{\mathcal{A}}}{\partial{\rho}}(\rho,T)+\frac{\rho}{T}\frac{\partial^{2}{\mathcal{A}}}{\partial{\rho}^{2}}(\rho,T)\right]

\displaystyle=\rho.

Since
\frac{\partial}{\partial\beta}=-T^{2}\frac{\partial}{\partial T}
and using (
62
), (
60
) and (
61
), we have for the internal-energy mean (
44
):

\displaystyle\widetilde{e}(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{u})

\displaystyle=\widetilde{\nabla}_{\beta}(g\beta)(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{u})-\frac{\widetilde{\nabla}_{\beta}(p\beta)(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{u})}{\widetilde{\rho}(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{u})}

\displaystyle=-T^{2}\frac{\partial{g/T}}{\partial{T}}(\rho,T)+\frac{T^{2}}{\rho}\frac{\partial{p/T}}{\partial{T}}(\rho,T)

\displaystyle=-T^{2}\left[-\frac{1}{T^{2}}\mathcal{A}(\rho,T)+\frac{1}{T}\frac{\partial{\mathcal{A}}}{\partial{T}}(\rho,T)-\frac{\rho}{T^{2}}\frac{\partial{\mathcal{A}}}{\partial{\rho}}(\rho,T)+\frac{\rho}{T}\frac{\partial^{2}\mathcal{A}}{\partial\rho\partial T}(\rho,T)\right]

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad+\frac{T^{2}}{\rho}\left[-\frac{\rho^{2}}{T^{2}}\frac{\partial{\mathcal{A}}}{\partial{\rho}}(\rho,T)+\frac{\rho^{2}}{T}\frac{\partial^{2}\mathcal{A}}{\partial\rho\partial T}(\rho,T)\right]

\displaystyle=\mathcal{A}(\rho,T)-T\frac{\partial{\mathcal{A}}}{\partial{T}}(\rho,T)

\displaystyle=e(\rho,T).

∎

D.2
Symmetry

Proof.

Since, by assumption, the discrete gradients
\widetilde{\nabla}(p\beta),\widetilde{\nabla}(g\beta)
are symmetric, the density mean (
43
) and internal-energy mean (
44
) are symmetric. Since the arithmetic mean is symmetric, all terms in the flux (
42
) are therefore symmetric, and so it is also symmetric.
∎

D.3
Density mean positivity

Proof.

Suppose
\boldsymbol{u}_{1},\boldsymbol{u}_{2}\in\mathcal{U}
are arbitrary points so that
\mathcal{A}
is both convex in
1/\rho
and
C^{2}
on
\widetilde{\mathcal{T}}(\boldsymbol{u}_{1},\boldsymbol{u}_{2})
. The
\rho
-components of the symmetrized Itoh-Abe discrete gradients
\widetilde{\nabla}(p\beta),\widetilde{\nabla}(g\beta)
are:

\displaystyle\widetilde{\nabla}_{\rho}(p\beta)(\boldsymbol{\tau}_{1},\boldsymbol{\tau}_{2})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\widetilde{T}\in\{T_{1},T_{2}\}}\frac{1}{2}\frac{p(\rho_{2},\widetilde{T})/\widetilde{T}-p(\rho_{1},\widetilde{T})/\widetilde{T}}{\rho_{2}-\rho_{1}},

\displaystyle\widetilde{\nabla}_{\rho}(g\beta)(\boldsymbol{\tau}_{1},\boldsymbol{\tau}_{2})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\widetilde{T}\in\{T_{1},T_{2}\}}\frac{1}{2}\frac{g(\rho_{2},\widetilde{T})/\widetilde{T}-g(\rho_{1},\widetilde{T})/\widetilde{T}}{\rho_{2}-\rho_{1}}.

By equations (
60
), (
62
) and the assumption that
\mathcal{A}\in C^{2}(\widetilde{\mathcal{T}}(\boldsymbol{u}_{1},\boldsymbol{u}_{2}))
, both
p(\cdot,\widetilde{T})/\widetilde{T}
and
g(\cdot,\widetilde{T})/\widetilde{T}
, where
\widetilde{T}\in\{T_{1},T_{2}\}
, are continuous and differentiable functions on the interval
[\rho_{1},\rho_{2}]
. Thus, the mean value theorem (MVT) applies, and:

\text{MVT: }\quad\text{there exist }\widetilde{\rho}_{1},\widetilde{\rho}_{2}\in[\rho_{1},\rho_{2}]:\widetilde{\nabla}_{\rho}(p\beta)(\boldsymbol{\tau}_{1},\boldsymbol{\tau}_{2})=\sum_{i=1}^{2}\frac{1}{2}\frac{\partial p/T}{\partial\rho}(\widetilde{\rho}_{i},\widetilde{T}_{i}).

Note that
(\widetilde{\rho}_{i},\widetilde{T}_{i})\in\widetilde{\mathcal{T}}(\boldsymbol{u}_{1},\boldsymbol{u}_{2})
for
i=1,2
. Considering (
60
), for all
(\rho,T)\in\widetilde{\mathcal{T}}(\boldsymbol{u}_{1}\boldsymbol{u}_{2})
we have:

\frac{\partial{p/T}}{\partial{\rho}}=\frac{2\rho}{T}\frac{\partial{\mathcal{A}}}{\partial{\rho}}+\frac{\rho^{2}}{T}\frac{\partial^{2}{\mathcal{A}}}{\partial{\rho}^{2}}=\frac{1}{\rho^{2}T}\frac{\partial^{2}{\mathcal{A}}}{\partial{(1/\rho)}^{2}}>0,

due to convexity in
1/\rho
of
\mathcal{A}
on
\widetilde{\mathcal{T}}(\boldsymbol{u}_{1}\boldsymbol{u}_{2})
and positivity of
(\rho,T)\in\widetilde{\mathcal{T}}(\boldsymbol{u}_{1}\boldsymbol{u}_{2})
. Thus
\widetilde{\nabla}_{\rho}(p\beta)(\boldsymbol{\tau}_{1},\boldsymbol{\tau}_{2})>0
. Similarly, we have:

\text{MVT: }\quad\text{there exist }\widetilde{\rho}_{1},\widetilde{\rho}_{2}\in[\rho_{1},\rho_{2}]:\widetilde{\nabla}_{\rho}(g\beta)(\boldsymbol{\tau}_{1},\boldsymbol{\tau}_{2})=\sum_{i=1}^{2}\frac{1}{2}\frac{\partial g/T}{\partial\rho}(\widetilde{\rho}_{i},\widetilde{T}_{i}),

with
(\widetilde{\rho}_{i},\widetilde{T}_{i})\in\widetilde{\mathcal{T}}(\boldsymbol{u}_{1},\boldsymbol{u}_{2})
for
i=1,2
. Now considering (
62
), for all
(\rho,T)\in\widetilde{\mathcal{T}}(\boldsymbol{u}_{1}\boldsymbol{u}_{2})
we have:

\frac{\partial{g/T}}{\partial{\rho}}=\frac{2}{T}\frac{\partial{\mathcal{A}}}{\partial{\rho}}+\frac{\rho}{T}\frac{\partial^{2}{\mathcal{A}}}{\partial{\rho}^{2}}=\frac{1}{\rho^{3}T}\frac{\partial^{2}{\mathcal{A}}}{\partial{(1/\rho)}^{2}}>0,

and therefore
\widetilde{\nabla}_{\rho}(g\beta)(\boldsymbol{\tau}_{1},\boldsymbol{\tau}_{2})>0
.
∎

D.4
Ideal gas behavior

For an ideal gas, the specific Helmholtz energy is given as in (
67
), the pressure
p(\rho,T)
, computed using (
60
), satisfies:

p(\rho,T)=\rho\overline{R}T,

(65)

and the specific Gibbs energy, computed using (
62
), satisfies:

g(\rho,T)=-\frac{\overline{R}T}{\gamma-1}\ln(T)+\overline{R}T\ln(\rho),

(66)

where we set
s_{0}=0
.

Proof.

Let
\boldsymbol{u}_{1},\boldsymbol{u}_{2}\in\mathcal{U}
and
[\rho_{1},T_{1}]=\boldsymbol{\tau}(\boldsymbol{u}_{1})
,
[\rho_{2},T_{2}]=\boldsymbol{\tau}(\boldsymbol{u}_{2})
. We first analyze the density mean (
43
). For the ideal-gas EoS, the symmetrized Itoh-Abe discrete gradient
\widetilde{\nabla}_{\rho}(p\beta)(\boldsymbol{u}_{1},\boldsymbol{u}_{2})
satisfies:

\displaystyle\widetilde{\nabla}_{\rho}(p\beta)(\boldsymbol{u}_{1},\boldsymbol{u}_{2})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\widetilde{{T}}\in\{T_{1},T_{2}\}}\frac{1}{2}\frac{p(\rho_{2},\widetilde{T})/\widetilde{T}-p(\rho_{1},\widetilde{T})/\widetilde{T}}{\rho_{2}-\rho_{1}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\widetilde{{T}}\in\{T_{1},T_{2}\}}\frac{1}{2}\frac{\overline{R}\rho_{2}-\overline{R}\rho_{1}}{\rho_{2}-\rho_{1}}

\displaystyle=\overline{R}.

The discrete gradient
\widetilde{\nabla}_{\rho}(g\beta)(\boldsymbol{u}_{1},\boldsymbol{u}_{2})
satisfies:

\displaystyle\widetilde{\nabla}_{\rho}(g\beta)(\boldsymbol{u}_{1},\boldsymbol{u}_{2})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\widetilde{{T}}\in\{T_{1},T_{2}\}}\frac{1}{2}\frac{g(\rho_{2},\widetilde{T})/\widetilde{T}-g(\rho_{1},\widetilde{T})/\widetilde{T}}{\rho_{2}-\rho_{1}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\widetilde{{T}}\in\{T_{1},T_{2}\}}-\frac{1}{2}\frac{\overline{R}}{\gamma-1}\frac{\ln(\widetilde{T})-\ln(\widetilde{T})}{\rho_{2}-\rho_{1}}+\frac{1}{2}\frac{\overline{R}\ln(\rho_{2})-\overline{R}\ln(\rho_{1})}{\rho_{2}-\rho_{1}}

\displaystyle=\overline{R}\left(\overline{\rho}^{\ln}\right)^{-1},

where
\overline{(\cdot)}^{\ln}
denotes the logarithmic average. The density mean (
43
) is thus:

\widetilde{\rho}(\boldsymbol{u}_{1},\boldsymbol{u}_{2})=\frac{\overline{R}}{\overline{R}\left(\overline{\rho}^{\ln}\right)^{-1}}=\overline{\rho}^{\ln},

for the ideal-gas EoS. We now consider the
\beta
-components of the discrete gradients. Since
p\beta=\rho\overline{R}
, we have
\widetilde{\nabla}_{\beta}(p\beta)(\boldsymbol{u}_{1},\boldsymbol{u}_{2})=0
as
p\beta
is constant with respect to changes in
\beta
. Finally, for
\widetilde{\nabla}_{\beta}(g\beta)(\boldsymbol{u}_{1},\boldsymbol{u}_{2})
, we have:

\displaystyle\widetilde{\nabla}_{\beta}(g\beta)(\boldsymbol{u}_{1},\boldsymbol{u}_{2})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\widetilde{{\rho}}\in\{\rho_{1},\rho_{2}\}}\frac{1}{2}\frac{g(\tilde{\rho},T_{2})/T_{2}-g(\tilde{\rho},T_{1})/T_{1}}{1/T_{2}-1/T_{1}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\widetilde{{\rho}}\in\{\rho_{1},\rho_{2}\}}-\frac{1}{2}\frac{\overline{R}}{\gamma-1}\frac{\ln(T_{2})-\ln(T_{1})}{1/T_{2}-1/T_{1}}+\frac{1}{2}\frac{\overline{R}\ln(\widetilde{\rho})-\overline{R}\ln(\widetilde{\rho})}{1/T_{2}-1/T_{1}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\overline{R}}{\gamma-1}\left(\overline{(1/T)}^{\ln}\right)^{-1}.

Since, according to the ideal-gas EoS,
T=p/(\rho\overline{R})
, the ideal-gas internal-energy mean (
44
) is:

\widetilde{e}(\boldsymbol{u}_{1},\boldsymbol{u}_{2})=\frac{\overline{R}}{\gamma-1}\left(\overline{(\overline{R}\rho/p)}^{\ln}\right)^{-1}.

Comparison against
[
48
,
51
,
50
]
, shows the Ranocha flux is identical to (
42

\overline{R}=1
).
∎

Discretization of viscous stress and heat flux divergence

For simplicity, we will describe the viscous and heat discretization for
d=2
and only in the
x_{1}
-direction. The extensions to
d=3
and other directions are straightforward. We start with the viscous discretization. In two space dimensions, with velocity
\boldsymbol{v}=[v_{1},v_{2}]^{T}
, the viscous stress tensor can be written as:

\boldsymbol{\tau}=\mu\left(\nabla\boldsymbol{v}+\nabla\boldsymbol{v}^{\top}\right)-\mu\,(\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{v})\,I.

For the viscous flux in the
x_{1}
-direction, we evaluate a two-point approximation of the stress-tensor column associated to the
x_{1}
-direction
\boldsymbol{\tau}_{h}^{1}:\mathcal{U}\times\mathcal{U}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{2}
between
\boldsymbol{u}_{\boldsymbol{I}}
and
\boldsymbol{u}_{\boldsymbol{I}+\boldsymbol{1}_{1}}
, and denote the full viscous flux between these points
\boldsymbol{f}^{\mathrm{visc}}_{\boldsymbol{I}+\boldsymbol{1}_{1}/2}
. The two-point approximation is given by centered differences and arithmetic averaging of the transport coefficients
\mu
.
Denote
\boldsymbol{\tau}_{\boldsymbol{I}+\boldsymbol{1}_{1}/2}:=\boldsymbol{\tau}_{h}^{1}(\boldsymbol{u}_{\boldsymbol{I}},\boldsymbol{u}_{\boldsymbol{I}+\boldsymbol{1}_{1}})
, then the viscous
x_{1}
-flux is given by:

\boldsymbol{f}^{\mathrm{visc}}_{\boldsymbol{I}+\boldsymbol{1}_{1}/2}=\begin{bmatrix}0\\
(\tau_{1})_{\boldsymbol{I}+\boldsymbol{1}_{1}/2}\\
(\tau_{2})_{\boldsymbol{I}+\boldsymbol{1}_{1}/2}\\
(\tau_{1})_{\boldsymbol{I}+\boldsymbol{1}_{1}/2}(\overline{v}_{1})_{\boldsymbol{I}+\boldsymbol{1}_{1}/2}+(\tau_{2})_{\boldsymbol{I}+\boldsymbol{1}_{1}/2}(\overline{v}_{2})_{\boldsymbol{I}+\boldsymbol{1}_{1}/2}\end{bmatrix}.

Here, the velocity components
(\overline{v}_{1})_{\boldsymbol{I}+\boldsymbol{1}_{1}/2},(\overline{v}_{2})_{\boldsymbol{I}+\boldsymbol{1}_{1}/2}
are approximated with arithmetic means. The viscosity coefficient is also approximated using an arithmetic mean:

\overline{\mu}_{\boldsymbol{I}+\boldsymbol{1}_{1}/2}=\frac{\mu_{\boldsymbol{I}}+\mu_{\boldsymbol{I}+\boldsymbol{1}_{1}}}{2},

and the required derivatives at the face are discretized by

\displaystyle\left(\partial_{x_{1}}v_{1}\right)_{\boldsymbol{I}+\boldsymbol{1}_{1}/2}

\displaystyle=\frac{\Delta(v_{1})_{\boldsymbol{I}+\boldsymbol{1}_{1}/2}}{\Delta x_{1}},

\displaystyle\left(\partial_{x_{1}}v_{2}\right)_{\boldsymbol{I}+\boldsymbol{1}_{1}/2}

\displaystyle=\frac{\Delta(v_{2})_{\boldsymbol{I}+\boldsymbol{1}_{1}/2}}{\Delta x_{1}},

\displaystyle\left(\partial_{x_{2}}v_{1}\right)_{\boldsymbol{I}+\boldsymbol{1}_{1}/2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{(v_{1})_{(i_{1},i_{2}+1)}-(v_{1})_{(i_{1},i_{2}-1)}}{2\Delta x_{2}}+\frac{(v_{1})_{(i_{1}+1,i_{2}+1)}-(v_{1})_{(i_{1}+1,i_{2}-1)}}{2\Delta x_{2}}\right),

\displaystyle\left(\partial_{x_{2}}v_{2}\right)_{\boldsymbol{I}+\boldsymbol{1}_{1}/2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{(v_{2})_{(i_{1},i_{2}+1)}-(v_{2})_{(i_{1},i_{2}-1)}}{2\Delta x_{2}}+\frac{(v_{2})_{(i_{1}+1,i_{2}+1)}-(v_{2})_{(i_{1}+1,i_{2}-1)}}{2\Delta x_{2}}\right),

where we have written out the grid multi-indices in full for convenience. Thus,

\displaystyle(\tau_{1})_{\boldsymbol{I}+\boldsymbol{1}_{1}/2}

\displaystyle=2\mu_{\boldsymbol{I}+\boldsymbol{1}_{1}/2}\left(\partial_{x_{1}}v_{1}\right)_{\boldsymbol{I}+\boldsymbol{1}_{1}/2}-\mu_{\boldsymbol{I}+\boldsymbol{1}_{1}/2}\left(\left(\partial_{x_{1}}v_{1}\right)_{\boldsymbol{I}+\boldsymbol{1}_{1}/2}+\left(\partial_{y}v\right)_{\boldsymbol{I}+\boldsymbol{1}_{1}/2}\right),

\displaystyle(\tau_{2})_{\boldsymbol{I}+\boldsymbol{1}_{1}/2}

\displaystyle=\mu_{\boldsymbol{I}+\boldsymbol{1}_{1}/2}\left(\left(\partial_{x_{2}}v_{1}\right)_{\boldsymbol{I}+\boldsymbol{1}_{1}/2}+\left(\partial_{x_{1}}v_{2}\right)_{\boldsymbol{I}+\boldsymbol{1}_{1}/2}\right).

It is a custom in the literature to plot the dissipation rate of kinetic energy
[
64
,
11
,
53
,
62
]
. To standardize our results with respect to the literature, we must compute the kinetic-energy dissipation rate using the following normalized quantities:

\rho^{*}:=\frac{\rho}{\rho_{0}},\quad\boldsymbol{v}^{*}:=\frac{\boldsymbol{v}}{V_{0}},\quad\boldsymbol{x}^{*}:=\frac{\boldsymbol{x}}{2\pi L},\quad t^{*}:=\frac{t}{t_{c}}.

Let:

\mathcal{K}(t):=\int_{\Omega}k(\boldsymbol{u}(\boldsymbol{x},t))d\Omega.

Then we compute the normalized kinetic-energy dissipation rate
\left(\frac{d{\mathcal{K}}}{dt}\right)^{*}
from the physical
\frac{d{\mathcal{K}}}{dt}
as:

\left(\frac{d{\mathcal{K}}}{dt}\right)^{*}=\frac{t_{c}}{\rho_{0}V_{0}^{2}|\Omega|}\frac{d{\mathcal{K}}}{dt},

where
|\Omega|\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
is the volume of the spatial domain
\Omega
. To further assure the kinetic-energy budget as in (
50
) still holds, the quantities
\Pi,\mathcal{D},\mathcal{E}
in (
50
) are normalized using the same normalization factor:

\Pi^{*}=\frac{t_{c}}{\rho_{0}V_{0}^{2}|\Omega|}\Pi,\quad\mathcal{D}^{*}=\frac{t_{c}}{\rho_{0}V_{0}^{2}|\Omega|}\mathcal{D},\quad\mathcal{E}^{*}=\frac{t_{c}}{\rho_{0}V_{0}^{2}|\Omega|}\mathcal{E}.

We consistently use normalized quantities in all results related to the kinetic-energy dissipation rate; hence, we drop the normalization notation
(\cdot)^{*}
throughout. The results of the verification study are shown in
Figure 11
and
Figure 12
. The results in
Figure 11
show a close agreement in kinetic-energy dissipation between our second-order GEoS-KEEP scheme and the high-order, dispersion-relation-preserving scheme used in
[
11
]
, also defined on a mesh of
512^{3}
cells. As discussed in
subsection 5.4
, we expect a near exact match between
\frac{d{\mathcal{K}}}{dt}
and
\mathcal{E}
, which
Figure 11
shows to be almost attained. The inexact match can be attributed to insufficient grid convergence. The lack of grid convergence is further corroborated by
Figure 12

\omega
as in (

[

shows that our solution is nearly grid-converged, which we consider sufficient for our experiments.
