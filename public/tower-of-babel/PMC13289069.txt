Prevalence and associated factors of rheumatic heart disease among school-aged children in Taiz Governorate, Yemen: a cross-sectional echocardiographic screening study
Authors: Mohammed Abdullah Al Amad, Abdulwahab Ahmed Al-Nehmi, Abudar Abdo Al-Ganadi, Sedqi Abdulmahmoud Al- Kubati, Naseem Saeed Al-Wsabi, Mahmood Hsan Abdulrazzaq, Osamah Abdulqawi Moqbel, Tariq Mustafa Farhan, Reaaf Abdullah Qahtan, Mohammed Abdulla Al-Fohidi, Ismail Samer Al-Shameri
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractObjectivesTo estimate the prevalence of rheumatic heart disease (RHD) and identify associated factors among school-aged children in Taiz Governorate, Yemen.DesignCross-sectional, school-based echocardiographic screening study.SettingPublic schools in urban and rural areas of Taiz Governorate, Yemen.ParticipantsA total of 4135 school-aged children (5–15 years) were screened using a purposive stratified sampling approach to ensure representation by sex and place of residence.Main outcome measuresRHD status (definite and borderline) was determined using echocardiography based on the World Heart Federation criteria. Socio-economic status was assessed using a modified Water/Sanitation, Assets, Maternal Education and Income index and nutritional status using body mass index-for-age Z-scores. Multinomial logistic regression was used to identify factors associated with RHD.ResultsThe overall RHD prevalence was 55.9 per 1000 children, including 9.9 per 1000 definite cases and 45.9 per 1000 borderline cases. Prevalence was higher among females (63.8 vs 45.9 per 1000; p=0.013), rural children (63.1 vs 47.3 per 1000; p=0.022) and undernourished children (69.9 per 1000). Caregiver awareness of RHD was low (36%).In multivariable analysis, definite RHD was associated with older age (relative risk ratio (RRR)=1.22, 95% CI 1.10 to 1.36), female sex (RRR=3.21, 95% CI 1.51 to 6.86) and rural residence (RRR=2.21, 95% CI 1.16 to 4.20). Borderline RHD was associated with increasing age (RRR=1.07; 95% CI 1.01 to 1.14), while moderate socio-economic status was protective (RRR=0.71, 95% CI 0.52 to 0.97).ConclusionThe burden of RHD among schoolchildren in Taiz is high, with borderline disease substantially exceeding definite cases. Higher risk among girls, rural populations and socio-economically disadvantaged groups highlights important health inequities. These findings support the need for a national RHD control strategy in Yemen, including school-based screening, improved access to penicillin prophylaxis, strengthened referral systems and community awareness programmes.Keywords: Schools, Mass Screening, Risk Factors

Introduction

Rheumatic heart disease (RHD) remains a preventable yet major public health problem in many low-income and middle-income countries, contributing substantially to cardiovascular morbidity and mortality.1 It is a chronic complication of acute rheumatic fever, a non-suppurative sequela of pharyngeal infection caused by group A β-hemolytic Streptococcus. Although RHD can affect all age groups, it occurs most commonly among children aged 5–15 years.2

Globally, nearly 40 million people were estimated to be affected by RHD in 2018, causing approximately 305 000 deaths, with the greatest burden occurring in sub-Saharan Africa, South Asia and the Eastern Mediterranean region.3 4 In endemic areas, echocardiographic screening studies among school-aged children have reported prevalence rates ranging from 5.7 to over 30 per 1000 children5 6

The reported prevalence varies considerably depending on the diagnostic criteria used. A systematic review and meta-analysis of echocardiographic screening studies showed that the World Heart Federation (WHF) criteria yield a higher pooled prevalence of 26.1 per 1000, as they include both definite and borderline cases, including subclinical disease. In contrast, the WHO criteria, which rely more on clinical and symptomatic cases, estimate a lower prevalence of 11.3 per 1000, underestimating the true burden of disease.4 7

In the Eastern Mediterranean region, RHD remains endemic.3 8 Studies from Egypt have reported prevalence ranging from 6.2 to 19.6 per 1000 school-aged children,9 10 while higher estimates of up to 61 per 1000 have been reported in Sudan.11

In Yemen, as in many other countries in the region, the absence of a national RHD control programme and the limited number of studies have resulted in an inconsistently reported burden of RHD. School-based studies conducted before the conflict showed widely varying prevalence rates across different governorates, ranging from 3.6 per 1000 among children aged 5–18 years to 36.5 per 1000 among schoolchildren aged 6–15 years in Aden.12 13 More recent hospital-based studies during the conflict highlight a substantial burden of RHD, with high rates of severe complications reported in Sana’a and Dhamar City.14 15 Despite this, no recent school-based data are available from conflict-affected areas such as Taiz Governorate.

To our knowledge, this is the first school-based echocardiographic screening study conducted in Yemen and the first in a conflict-affected governorate, providing an important snapshot of the current burden of RHD among school-aged children. This study aimed to estimate the prevalence of RHD and identify associated factors among school-aged children in Taiz Governorate, Yemen, thereby generating essential baseline evidence to inform the planning and implementation of future RHD prevention and control programmes in the region.

Methods

Study design and setting

A cross-sectional, school-based screening survey for RHD was conducted in Taiz Governorate, Yemen, a conflict-affected setting with limited healthcare resources. Data collection was carried out from November 2024 to February 2025.

Study population

The target population included male and female students aged 5–15 years enrolled in public schools in Taiz Governorate. To ensure geographic and sex representation, the study included schools from both urban and rural areas. Separate lists of boys’, girls’ and mixed-gender schools were obtained from official school records.

Sampling strategy

A purposive stratified sampling approach was used to ensure representation by sex and residence. Five schools were selected: one boys’ and one girls’ school from urban districts, one boys’ and one girls’ school from rural districts and one mixed-gender school, predominantly located in a rural district. Within each stratum, the largest accessible schools were selected to maximise student coverage and facilitate efficient deployment of the specialist screening team and hospital-grade echocardiography equipment provided by the Cardiovascular and Kidney Transplant Centre in Taiz. The study population primarily comprised school-aged children from Taiz City and surrounding accessible rural districts.

This pragmatic design was implemented to provide an initial snapshot of the burden of RHD in one of the most conflict-affected governorates in the country.

Standard probability sampling was not feasible due to security constraints, safety concerns and logistical challenges related to transporting and operating hospital-based equipment in the field. Intermittent school closures due to a teachers’ strike further limited access during the study period.

Eligibility criteria

All students in grades 1–9 within the selected schools were invited to participate through mass school-based screening, subject to written parental or guardian consent.

Sample size

The study was conducted as a mass screening of all eligible students in the selected schools; therefore, no formal sample size calculation was performed. The final sample included all students with parental/guardian consent who were present on the day of screening.

Patient and public involvement

Patients and the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination plans of this research. The study was based on a school-based screening programme, and participants were not engaged in shaping the research questions or study procedures.

Data sources and measurements

Two data collection tools adapted from WHO guidelines were used:

Caregiver questionnaire: a self-administered questionnaire, translated into Arabic, was distributed to caregivers 3 days prior to screening via school health teams. It was completed by the father or household head and collected information on socio-demographic characteristics, household socio-economic status (SES) and history of sore throat, fever and known RHD. Completed questionnaires were returned to schools and entered into an electronic database.

It was completed by the father or household head and collected information on socio-demographic characteristics, household socio-economic status (SES) and history of sore throat, fever and known RHD. Completed questionnaires were returned to schools and entered into an electronic database.

Clinical and echocardiographic assessment: a standardised electronic data collection form was used onsite to document clinical and echocardiographic findings. Echocardiographic screening was conducted by trained cardiologists from the Cardiovascular and Kidney Transplant Centre in Taiz using hospital-grade ultrasound equipment transported to the selected schools. Cardiologists performed the examinations and provided immediate verbal interpretation of findings, whereas trained data entry staff simultaneously entered the results into the electronic form in real time. Examinations were conducted using a Mindray DC-40 ultrasound machine (Mindray, Shenzhen, China) equipped with a P4-2s phased-array transducer. Two-dimensional imaging, colour Doppler, pulse-wave Doppler and continuous-wave Doppler were used to assess cardiac chamber size, ventricular function (by visual estimation) and mitral and aortic valve morphology and function. All findings were interpreted according to the WHF criteria for RHD diagnosis.

Study variables

Outcome variable

RHD status was classified as follows:

Definite RHD: pathological mitral and/or aortic regurgitation with characteristic morphological valve features.

Definite RHD: pathological mitral and/or aortic regurgitation with characteristic morphological valve features.

Borderline RHD: abnormal regurgitation without clear morphological features or morphological changes without pathological regurgitation.11

Borderline RHD: abnormal regurgitation without clear morphological features or morphological changes without pathological regurgitation.11

Exposure variables

Socio-economic status

SES was assessed using the modified Water/Sanitation, Assets, Maternal Education and Income index.16 Scores were normalised from 0 (lowest) to 10 (highest) and categorised as low (0–4), moderate (4.1–7) and high (>7).

Nutritional status

Anthropometric measurements, including weight and height, were used to calculate body mass index-for-age Z-scores (BAZ), which were generated based on age and sex, and classified according to WHO guidelines as follows: BAZ < −2 was considered undernutrition, BAZ between −2 and +1 was considered normal, BAZ > +1 to +2 indicated overweight and BAZ > +2 was categorised as obesity. These categories were used for further statistical analysis of nutritional outcomes in the study population

Potential sources of bias

Several potential sources of bias were considered. Selection bias may have arisen from the use of purposive school selection rather than a probability-based sampling frame, which may limit the generalisability of the findings to all school-aged children in Taiz Governorate. However, the sampling design incorporated stratification by sex and residence, including separate boys’ and girls’ schools from both urban and rural districts, as well as one mixed-gender school, to improve geographic and demographic representativeness. In addition, within the selected schools, all eligible students in grades 1–9 were invited to participate through mass screening, thereby minimising within-school selection bias.

Information bias was reduced through the use of standardised WHO-adapted data collection tools, trained data-entry staff and predefined echocardiographic diagnostic criteria based on the WHF guidelines. Misclassification bias in RHD diagnosis was further minimised by having trained cardiologists perform the echocardiographic screening using a standardised protocol and hospital-grade ultrasound equipment. Nevertheless, screening under field conditions and transportation of equipment to schools may have introduced minor measurement variability. Caregiver-reported clinical history may also be subject to recall bias.

Statistical analysis

Data were entered into Epi Info version 7.2, exported to Microsoft Excel and analysed using Stata version 14.0. Descriptive statistics were used to summarise participant characteristics and caregiver awareness of RHD. Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages.

Unadjusted (bivariate) analysis was performed to examine the distribution of RHD (overall, borderline and definite) across socio-demographic, socio-economic, nutritional and clinical characteristics. Results were presented as prevalence per 1000 with corresponding p values.

Multinomial logistic regression was used to identify factors associated with borderline and definite RHD, with ‘no RHD’ as the reference category. Variables with p<0.25 in the bivariate analysis were included in the multivariable model. Relative risk ratios (RRRs) and 95% CIs were reported. Model fit was assessed using likelihood ratio tests, and robust standard errors were used to account for heteroskedasticity. Statistical significance was set at p<0.05. A binary logistic regression model was conducted as a sensitivity analysis to assess factors associated with overall RHD (definite or borderline combined). Adjusted ORs with 95% CIs were reported.

Data quality assurance

All study tools were digitised using Epi Info to enable real-time electronic data capture during field implementation. Data entry staff received prior training on electronic data collection procedures, standard operating workflows and quality control measures. A pilot screening involving 130 students was conducted to assess the feasibility of the screening workflow and to refine data collection procedures before full implementation.

To ensure data quality, echocardiographic findings were communicated verbally by the cardiologists at the point of screening and entered in real time by trained data entry staff using the electronic form. Trained nurses reviewed completed caregiver questionnaires for completeness during screening activities, and any missing or unclear responses were verified through follow-up phone contact with parents/guardians when needed. Electronic data collection forms incorporated required fields, conditional logic, alerts and skip patterns to minimise missing or incomplete data. Daily data cleaning, verification and merging were performed by a three-member data management team. A unique coding system was used to maintain participant anonymity while enabling tracking by school and grade. Data were routinely reviewed for completeness, consistency and potential entry errors, which were corrected through daily verification procedures.

Results

Participants and response rate

A total of 4952 eligible students from the five selected schools were invited to participate in the school-based echocardiographic screening programme. Of these, 4135 students completed screening and were included in the analysis, yielding an overall response rate of 83.5%. Response rates varied by school type, with 87.3% in boys’ schools (1558/1784), 80.5% in girls’ schools (1946/2418) and 84.1% in the mixed school (631/750). Non-participation was primarily due to non-return of parental consent forms or refusal (n=817).

Among the screened students, 190 (4.6%) were classified as having borderline RHD and 41 (1.0%) as definite RHD, whereas 3904 (94.4%) had no echocardiographic evidence of RHD (figure 1).

Socio-economic characteristics of screened children

Of the total 4135 screened children, 55.7 (95% CI 54.2 to 57.2) were female and 54.0% (95% CI 52.5 to 55.5) resided in rural areas. Nearly half of the participants were aged ≥12 years (46.7%, 95% CI 45.2 to 48.2). Children were distributed across grades 1–9, with the lowest proportion in grade 1 (9.0%, 95% CI 8.1 to 9.9) and the highest in grade 9 (12.5%, 95% CI 11.5 to 13.5).

Regarding SES, 53.0% (95% CI 51.5 to 54.5) were classified as moderate, 36.0% (95% CI 35.0 to 38.0) as low and 10.0% (95% CI 9.0 to 11.0) as high. Most participants had normal nutritional status (76.0%, 95% CI 75.0 to 77.0), whereas 12.0% (95% CI 11.0 to 13.0) were undernourished and 12.0% (95% CI 11.0 to 13.0) were overweight or obese (table 1).

Medical history of participants

A history of recurrent throat infection (tonsillitis or frequent sore throat) was reported in 52.0% of children, and a family history of rheumatic fever or RHD was reported in 20.0% of participants. Previous diagnosis of rheumatic fever was reported in 7.0%, and 13.0% had received long-acting penicillin prophylaxis. Other medical history details are presented in online supplemental table S1.

Caregiver awareness of rheumatic heart disease

Among caregivers of screened children, 36.0% (95% CI 35.0 to 38.0) reported awareness of RHD. The most common sources of information were a family member with illness (38%), mass media including radio and television (29%) and healthcare or educational exposure (22%). Social media accounted for 19% of responses, and formal awareness activities such as seminars contributed 9% (online supplemental table S2).

Echocardiographic assessment identified 231 children with RHD and 3900 without RHD. Mitral valve findings predominated, with mitral regurgitation (MR) more frequent in the RHD group (69 vs 7). Pathological MR parameters were also higher in the RHD group, including MR jet velocity ≥3 (43 vs 3) m/s, MR jet length ≥2 (42 vs 1) cm and both criteria combined (31 vs 1). Mitral leaflet thickening was common but more frequent in RHD (189 vs 113).

As for aortic findings, aortic leaflet thickening (18 vs 1) and aortic regurgitation (15 vs 0) were predominantly observed in the RHD group. In contrast, other cardiac findings such as patent ductus arteriosus (0 vs 4) and ventricular septal defect (0 vs 2) were identified only in the non-RHD group (online supplemental table S3).

The representative echocardiographic images for definite RHD show continuous-wave Doppler demonstrating a high-velocity MR jet (≥3 m/s), while colour Doppler imaging reveals a clearly visualised regurgitant jet extending into the left atrium (figure 2). In contrast, continuous-wave Doppler across the mitral valve showing a low-velocity regurgitant jet (<3 m/s), accompanied by a limited colour Doppler signal with minimal extension into the left atrium, does not fulfil the criteria for pathological MR. Such findings are more consistent with borderline RHD, as they lack the functional echocardiographic characteristics required for definite disease classification (figure 3).

Prevalence of rheumatic heart disease

Among the 4135 screened children, the overall prevalence of RHD was 55.9 per 1000 (n=231). This included 9.9 per 1000 (n=41) definite RHD and 45.9 per 1000 (n=190) borderline RHD. The prevalence of RHD was higher among rural children (63.1 per 1000 vs 47.3 per 1000, p=0.022) and female students (63.8 per 1000 vs 45.9 per 1000, p=0.013) than counterpart groups. Definite RHD was higher among females (13.9 vs 4.9 per 1000; p=0.003). RHD prevalence increased with age, from 43.7 per 1000 in children aged 5–8 years to 65.3 per 1000 in those aged ≥12 years (p=0.041). The prevalence was 71.4 per 1000 in the high SES group and 69.9 per 1000 among undernourished children (table 2).

Factors associated with rheumatic heart disease

In multinomial logistic regression analysis, increasing age was significantly associated with both borderline RHD (RRR 1.07, 95% CI 1.01 to 1.14; p=0.016) and definite RHD (RRR 1.22, 95% CI 1.10 to 1.36; p<0.001). Female sex was significantly associated with higher odds of definite RHD (RRR 3.21, 95% CI 1.51 to 6.86; p=0.003), whereas the association with borderline RHD was not statistically significant. Children from rural areas had higher odds of definite RHD (RRR 2.21, 95% CI 1.16 to 4.20; p=0.016), with no statistically significant association for borderline disease. Moderate SES was associated with reduced odds of borderline RHD (RRR 0.71, 95% CI 0.52 to 0.97; p=0.032), whereas no association was observed for definite RHD. Nutritional status was not significantly associated with either borderline or definite RHD (table 3).

A sensitivity analysis using binary logistic regression for overall RHD yielded consistent findings. Increasing age (OR 1.10, 95% CI 1.04 to 1.16; p<0.001), female sex (OR 1.52, 95% CI 1.15 to 2.02; p=0.003) and rural residence (OR 1.43, 95% CI 1.08 to 1.88; p=0.011) were significantly associated with higher odds of RHD. SES showed no statistically significant association, although moderate SES showed a non-significant protective trend compared with low SES. Nutritional status was not associated with RHD (online supplemental table S4).

Discussion

This study provides a comprehensive assessment of RHD among school-aged children from Taiz Governorate using echocardiographic screening. The overall RHD prevalence was 55.9 per 1000, with borderline cases accounting for the majority (45.9 per 1000) and definite RHD at 9.9 per 1000. The high prevalence was mainly driven by borderline RHD, which likely reflects early or non-specific valvular changes and should be interpreted with caution.17 The observed prevalence in this study is higher than that reported in earlier school-based studies in Yemen, including 3.6 per 1000 in a clinical screening study and 36.5 per 1000 in a school-based echocardiographic study in Aden.12 13 Compared with studies in other low-income and middle-income countries, the prevalence in Taiz exceeds estimates from India and Nepal (7.7–10.8 per 1000) and Ethiopia (19 per 1000) and is higher than reports from Egypt and Tanzania (31–34 per 1000), although similar to the highest estimates reported from high-burden settings.914 15 18,20 These differences likely reflect variations in diagnostic methods, health system access and the impact of prolonged conflict on prevention and treatment of streptococcal infections in Yemen.

The echocardiographic findings in this study indicated consistent pathological MR and structural valvular abnormalities among definite RHD cases, supported by Doppler-confirmed evidence, supporting the robustness of this classification.21

Age showed a consistent association with RHD, with prevalence increasing in older children. Each additional year of age increased the risk of borderline RHD by 7% and definite RHD by 22%. This pattern is consistent with evidence from Tanzania and other African studies showing increasing RHD prevalence with age, reflecting cumulative exposure to group A streptococcal infections and progression of subclinical disease.19 22 23 These findings support the inclusion of older school-aged children in screening programmes.

Female students had a significantly higher risk of definite RHD, with more than threefold increased odds compared with male students. Similar patterns have been reported in Nepal and India, as well as in global estimates from low-income and middle-income countries.24,26 The reasons may include biological susceptibility or differences in health-seeking behaviour, although further investigation is required.

Rural residence was independently associated with higher risk of definite RHD, with more than double the odds compared with urban areas. This finding aligns with evidence from other low-resource settings and likely reflects inequities in access to primary care, early treatment of streptococcal infections and preventive services.20 27 These results highlight the need for geographically targeted interventions.

Overall, although borderline RHD accounted for the majority of detected cases, multivariable multinomial regression confirmed that definite RHD showed a clearer and more robust epidemiological pattern, reinforcing its clinical and public health significance

SES showed a mixed pattern. While bivariate analysis suggested higher prevalence in both low and high SES groups, regression analysis indicated that moderate SES was associated with reduced risk of borderline RHD. Generally, lower socio-economic conditions remain consistently associated with increased RHD risk in low-income and middle-income countries, although findings vary across settings.25 28,29

Nutritional status was not significantly associated with RHD after adjustment, suggesting that any observed crude associations may be confounded by socio-economic and geographic factors.

Caregiver awareness of RHD was low, with most knowledge derived from personal or familial experience rather than formal education or media exposure, indicating gaps that may contribute to delayed diagnosis and prevention. These gaps may partly explain non-participation (17%), mainly due to the lack of parental consent, and may reflect screening hesitancy or sociocultural factors influencing the acceptability of screening, particularly given the lower participation observed in girls’ school.

This study was conducted in a fragile, conflict-affected setting, with operational challenges including insecurity, restricted access for the screening team and school closures due to strikes. Despite efforts to improve demographic and geographic coverage, the selected schools may not fully represent all school-aged children in Taiz Governorate, particularly smaller or less accessible schools; therefore, findings should be interpreted with appropriate caution regarding generalisability. Despite the relatively high response rate (83.5%), it may still have introduced some degree of selection bias due to differential participation by sex. The cross-sectional design limits causal inference, and residual information bias may exist in caregiver-reported data. Inter-observer variability in echocardiographic interpretation was not formally assessed.

Despite these limitations, the study included a relatively large, sex-stratified and residence-stratified sample, improving internal representativeness. The use of standardised WHF criteria and screening by trained cardiologists using hospital-grade equipment strengthened diagnostic validity. To our knowledge, this is the first school-based echocardiographic screening study for RHD in Yemen, providing important baseline data from a high-burden, conflict-affected setting.

Conclusion

RHD remains highly prevalent among school-aged children in Taiz Governorate, Yemen, with low caregiver awareness. Higher risk was observed among older children, female students, those in rural areas and children from lower socio-economic backgrounds. These findings highlight priority groups for targeted prevention and early detection. Strengthening school-based screening, particularly in rural settings, alongside improved early diagnosis and management of streptococcal infections and increased community awareness is needed. A coordinated national strategy for RHD prevention and control, aligned with recommendations from the WHO, is urgently required.
