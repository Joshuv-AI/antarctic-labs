A multi-state photoswitch regulated by higher excited states
Authors: Shuang Xu, Le Yu, Yang Cheng, Yidan Li, David Lee Phillips, Tomáš Slanina, Jiani Ma, Yu Fang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Integrating multiple photoswitching units into a single molecule is a challenging way to achieve the manipulation of molecule geometry by selectively activating individual photoresponsive moieties. A lack of understanding from a mechanism viewpoint obstructs the advancement and realization of such systems. Herein, we construct a new hybrid ionic photoswitch (PZ-SP-MeSO4) that combines spiropyran (SP) and the ionic arylazopyrazolium (PZ) counterpart. Independent modulation of PZ (E/Z) and SP (closed/open) moieties via specific irradiation wavelengths has been achieved. The photostationary state composition (>66% Z-isomer content), fatigue resistance (>15 cycles), thermal stability of the Z-isomer (t1/2 = 717 days in water) and pH -dependent behavior enabled PZ-SP-MeSO4 to act as a potential hybrid photoswitch for molecular logic gate application, in addition to excellent water solubility (18.4 mM). More importantly, we elucidated the wavelength-gated regulating mechanisms for PZ-SP-MeSO4: 450 nm (S1) excites E → Z isomerization primarily and 365 nm (S3) triggers the SP → MC transformation. Uniquely, upon 420 nm excitation (S2), soon after the E → Z isomerization, the relay SP → MC transformation occurs from the hot ground state species due to excess vibrational energy. The understanding of the multi-state photoswitch regulated by higher excited states in this work establishes a blueprint for designing advanced light-responsive molecules.

Introduction

Photoswitches are capable of reversible, non-invasive geometry changes upon stimulation of light with specific wavelengths, offering exceptional spatiotemporal control over their chemical and physical properties.1–5 Decades of dedicated research have yielded a diverse toolkit of synthetic photoswitches,6–11 with the azobenzene unit as the most common photoswitching structural motif. Among pioneering developments, Herges and Fuchter's group introduced heteroaryl azoarenes as promising alternatives to traditional azobenzenes.12–15 Subsequently, Li's group further advanced heterocyclic azoswitches through structural refinements.16–22 By leveraging the inherent diversity of heterocyclic aromatic systems, these molecules expand the scope of photopharmacological applications and enhance switching performance.23–25 Notably, Venkataramani et al. and Carreira's group improved the major drawback of azoswitches, limited stability of the Z-isomer in water, by introducing ionic groups onto the switching scaffold (Fig. 1a), and achieved superior thermal stability for the Z-isomer of up to several years at room temperature.26,27

Although significant improvements have been made in the performance of photoswitches in recent years, the absorption spectra of the two isomers often suffer from overlap. This limits the usable spectral range of available photoswitching and makes all-visible-light switching particularly challenging.11,28–30 Meanwhile, most of the typical photoswitches are activated by the first excited singlet state (S1). A special case is noted in a very recent study where the transformations are due to the third excited singlet state (S3).31 This raises an important mechanistic question of how the different excited states govern the switching pathways. And this could offer a strategy to circumvent spectral overlap limitations.

From the design perspective, significant progress in the field of photoswitch investigation is integrating multiple switches within a single system to achieve advanced functions, enhanced addressability, and complex photoresponsive behavior.32–43 A key requirement for such systems is sufficient spectral separation between the constituent chromophores, allowing wavelength-selective switching, i.e., the ability to trigger each switch using distinct wavelengths of light. When such selectivity is achieved to the extent that each switch can be addressed independently, the system reaches orthogonal control, which represents the most rigorous form of photochemical addressability.44,45 However, this severely limits the combination of available photoswitches, as most commonly used molecular scaffolds exhibit strongly overlapping absorption bands in the ultraviolet-visible region. Additionally, undesirable intramolecular energy transfer between chromophores can further compromise the switching selectivity.46 Notable advances have been made by Dube's and Feringa's groups, who developed molecular systems incorporating multiple photoswitchable units to enable sophisticated photochemical control over materials and nanostructures.47–50 Wegner et al. reported an orthogonal photoswitching system based on azobenzene (AB) and spiropyran (SP),51 and Hirsch et al.52 developed an orthogonal system based on norbornadiene–triazine architectures. These two photoswitches could be independently addressed with specific wavelengths of light, and reverse isomerization is achieved through thermal activation. What distinguishes Wegner et al.’s AB–SP (Fig. 1b) orthogonal system is the involvement of higher-lying electronic states in its photoisomerization pathways: the S1 state only decays through the rapid isomerization of AB, and the ring-opening reaction of SP is initiated not only from S2 but also from the S5 excited state. An interesting point left to be answered is how these higher-lying state-mediated reactions occur.

Inspired by the work of Venkataramani et al. and Wegner et al.,26,51 we propose a new orthogonal photoswitch design guided by the following considerations. First, the primary objective is to enable independent optical control of AB and SP moieties, allowing wavelength-selective switching to their respective photostationary states (PSSs). Second, we introduce an arylazopyrazolium group (PZ) that functions as a push–pull system, enabling fully visible-light excitation. Its cationic nature, particularly that of the PZ ring, concurrently enhances water solubility, significantly expanding the photoswitch's applicability in photopharmacology and related fields. Third, we aim to improve thermal stability through synergistic effects of the cationic PZ ring and electron-donating functional groups on the SP.26 Guided by the above considerations, we rationally designed and synthesized PZ-SP-MeSO4, a novel hybrid orthogonal photoswitch, by strategically conjugating PZ and SP moieties through para-position linkage on the azo bridge. The photophysical behavior of this hybrid photoswitch is investigated by studying the relative abundance of E and Z isomers at the PSS, the thermal stability of the isomers, and fatigue-resistance within multiple photoswitching cycles. Based on the results from steady-state and time-resolved absorption spectroscopy and quantum chemical calculations, PZ-SP-MeSO4 is shown to function as an orthogonal photoswitch system in which the individual photoswitch components can be independently regulated, and the reverse isomerization can also be independently controlled with specific wavelengths of light. The initial test of this system in logic gates demonstrates practical applications. Importantly, mechanistic investigation reveals that excitation at 450 nm (S1) primarily induces E → Z isomerization. Upon excitation to the S3 state, rapid C–O bond cleavage becomes the preferred pathway, resulting in SP → MC conversion. Interestingly, upon 420 nm (S2) excitation, the system initially undergoes E → Z photoisomerization followed by SP → MC transformation driven by excess vibrational energy in the hot ground state of the Z-isomer. This work provides an explicit clarification of how higher excited states function in orthogonal photoswitching systems and is expected to offer useful principles for the rational design of complicated photoswitch systems.

Results and discussion

Photophysical studies

As shown in Fig. 1c, we designed and synthesized PZ-SP-MeSO4 that merges two switches, AB and SP (see Section S1 in the SI for synthetic details). The photophysical properties of PZ-SP-MeSO4 were characterized in a MeCN–H2O mixture (1 : 1, v/v) and pure aqueous solutions (Section S2).

The electronic absorption spectral properties of PZ-SP-MeSO4 isomers were computed using the time-dependent density functional theory (TD-DFT) method at the B3LYP-D3BJ/6-311G(d,p) level of theory53–55 with water solvent represented by the SMD model (Fig. 2 and for details, see Section S3 in the SI) based on the DFT optimized geometries. Starting from (E)-PZ-SP-MeSO4, following the opposite twisting direction of the PZ moiety yields two (Z)-PZ-SP-MeSO4 isomers with the counterion positioned adjacent to the pyrazolium ring and located between the aryl and pyrazolium rings, respectively. The relative energies suggest that the latter conformer is more stable (9.4 vs. 18.6 kcal mol−1) (Section S3). On top of it, the SP moiety can undergo photoconversion to the MC form, existing as CCC, CTC, and CTT configurational isomers. The comparative energy analysis revealed the CTT conformer to be thermodynamically favored (Table S1). Consequently, all subsequent discussion is limited to the most populated structural forms: the (E)-PZ-SP-MeSO4 structure with the counterion situated between the aryl and pyrazolium rings and (E)-PZ-MC-MeSO4 (E-CTT). The simulated UV-vis spectrum of (E)-PZ-SP-MeSO4 shows acceptable consistency with the experiment (Fig. S22), validating the computational methodology for investigating this molecular system.

(a) Detachment/attachment (left/right) densities of the relevant electronic transitions of (E)-PZ-SP-MeSO4 and (b) the absorption bands for related isomers calculated at the B3LYP-D3BJ/6-311G(d,p) SMD (H2O) level of theory (blue indicates experimental values).

The global minimum energy isomer (E)-PZ-SP-MeSO4, originating directly from the synthesis, displayed two distinct absorption maxima at 396 nm and 435 nm (Fig. S22 in SI). TD-DFT calculations on (E)-PZ-SP-MeSO4 reveal that the 396 nm absorbance gains contribution from the π–π* transition, and the absorbance at 435 nm is due to the n–π* transition. Compared to MeCN–H2O (1 : 1, v/v) solution, those in pure aqueous solution exhibit weakened n–π* absorption and strengthened π–π* bands. This spectral change is consistent with the increased solvent polarity. Notably, the n–π* transition exhibited a significant red-shift of 30 nm relative to the E-isomer of the known (E)-AB-SP (Fig. 1b),51 induced by the pronounced push–pull electron effect between the indole ring and the electron-deficient pyrazolium unit, mediated by the N <svg xmlns="http://www.w3.org/2000/svg" version="1.0" width="13.200000pt" height="16.000000pt" viewBox="0 0 13.200000 16.000000" preserveAspectRatio="xMidYMid meet"><metadata> Created by potrace 1.16, written by Peter Selinger 2001-2019 </metadata><g transform="translate(1.000000,15.000000) scale(0.017500,-0.017500)" fill="currentColor" stroke="none"><path d="M0 440 l0 -40 320 0 320 0 0 40 0 40 -320 0 -320 0 0 -40z M0 280 l0 -40 320 0 320 0 0 40 0 40 -320 0 -320 0 0 -40z"/></g></svg> N bridge. This interaction enhances the ground-state polarization and facilitates intramolecular charge transfer, which is responsible for the narrowed optical gap. Electrostatic potential analysis reveals a predominant negative charge localization on the SP moiety (Section S3), confirming the push–pull character of the electronic density. Owing to its red-shifted absorption, PZ-SP-MeSO4 is less susceptible to UV-induced photodamage. The intramolecular electrostatic interaction between the methanesulfonate anion and the cationic pyrazolium moiety lowers its LUMO energy while slightly raising the HOMO energy, further red-shifting the absorption (see details in Section S3).

Bichromophoric systems are intriguing with respect to the degree of their electronic coupling. Specifically, the S1 state (associated with the 435 nm band) corresponds to a local π → π excitation confined predominantly within the PZ moiety, where the electronic transition is largely localized on the pyrazolium-based conjugated framework, as evidenced by the difference density maps (Fig. 2a). In contrast, the S3 state (associated with the 396 nm band) corresponds to a well-defined SP → PZ charge-transfer excitation, where electron density migrates from the SP-based HOMO (indole ring) to the PZ-based LUMO (PZ unit), indicating a significant inter-fragment charge transfer (Fig. 2a and b). This charge localization in the excited state allows for the independent optical addressability of each photoswitch. Reverse isomerization, i.e. MC to SP and Z to E transitions, shows no wavelength dependence and occurs primarily from the S1 state upon irradiation. A similar effect is observed for other isomers, (Z)-PZ-SP-MeSO4, (E)-PZ-MC-MeSO4, and (Z)-PZ-MC-MeSO4 (Fig. S22). Both (E)-PZ-MC-MeSO4 and (Z)-PZ-MC-MeSO4 exhibit absorption in the 550–600 nm range, attributed to enhanced electronic coupling of the PZ moiety with the MC part. Notably, the Z-conformers display a blue shift relative to their E-counterparts, as the twisted azo-conformation inhibits the push–pull interaction through the entire conjugated molecule backbone to only the MC moiety.

Photophysical studies

Owing to the separated absorption band of individual isomers in PZ-SP-MeSO4, selective photoisomerization could be achieved using different wavelengths. According to the computation results, 365 nm, 420 nm and 450 nm light was selected to excite PZ-SP-MeSO4 to the S1, S2 and S3 states in spectroscopy experiments, respectively. After 450 nm irradiation, the observed slight red shift at 430 nm and the blue shift at 390 nm are consistent with the computed absorption spectra (Fig. 3a and S22), indicating that PSS450 primarily consists of (Z)-PZ-SP-MeSO4 (77%), with a photoisomerization quantum yield (Φ) of 0.30 (Section S4). Critically, 450 nm irradiation does not activate the SP → MC photoconversion, as evidenced by both (i) the complete absence of characteristic MC absorption bands (550–600 nm) and (ii) no observable MC proton resonances in the 1H-NMR spectrum of PSS450 (Fig. 3e, blue line). Subsequent 500 nm irradiation drives reverse Z → E isomerization, with a conversion yield of 72%. Irradiation at 420 nm generates PSS420 (Fig. 3b), whose spectral evolution reveals two distinct changes compared to the initial state: blue-shift of a 396 nm band and development of the characteristic MC absorption above 550 nm, collectively confirming the formation of (Z)-PZ-MC-MeSO4. The conversion from (E)-PZ-SP-MeSO4 to (Z)-PZ-MC-MeSO4 reaches 66% (Φ = 0.28), as unambiguously identified using the characteristic signals for both the Z and MC isomers in the 1H-NMR spectra of PSS420 (Fig. 3e, purple line). Reverse isomerization can be triggered using 600 nm light with a conversion of 61%. Selective photoisomerization of the SP moiety to MC can be achieved through 365 nm excitation. The resulting state PSS365 is predominantly composed of (E)-PZ-MC-MeSO4, as evidenced by no blue-shift absorption at 396 nm in the UV-vis spectra and the characteristic MC signal at approximately 8.3 ppm in the 1H-NMR analysis (Fig. 3c and e, yellow line). Furthermore, irradiation at 365 nm drove SP–MC isomerization with an exceptional conversion exceeding 80%, surpassing the ∼50% conversion for previously reported orthogonal photoswitches,48 and a quantum yield of 0.15 at 365 nm (Table 1). Due to spectral overlap, the conversion between (E)-PZ-MC-MeSO4 and (Z)-PZ-MC-MeSO4 could not be achieved experimentally. We also tested the photophysical properties of the switch in the solvent mixture, MeCN–H2O (1 : 1, v/v), and the results were largely consistent with those observed in H2O (Fig. S23). To test whether reversible switching between any two states is achievable, we first irradiated (E)-PZ-SP-MeSO4 with 450 nm light to fully convert it to (Z)-PZ-SP-MeSO4, followed by irradiation with 365 nm light (Fig. 3d). Steady-state absorption spectra indicated successful conversion to (E)-PZ-MC-MeSO4. Upon further irradiation with 600 nm light, we successfully drove the system through the sequential pathway (E)-PZ-SP-MeSO4 → (Z)-PZ-SP-MeSO4 → (E)-PZ-MC-MeSO4 → (E)-PZ-SP-MeSO4, thus completing a full reaction cycle and returning to the initial state. Additionally, when (E)-PZ-SP-MeSO4 was sequentially exposed to 365 nm and then 450 nm light, it ultimately converted to (Z)-PZ-SP-MeSO4 (Fig. S22e). These results demonstrate that (E)-PZ-SP-MeSO4 can be precisely directed to any of the other three states through the careful selection of irradiation wavelengths. Due to the spectral overlap between (Z)-PZ-MC-MeSO4 and (Z)-PZ-SP-MeSO4, as well as between (Z)-PZ-MC-MeSO4 and (E)-PZ-MC-MeSO4 (Fig. S22c), reversible conversion can only be achieved between (Z)-PZ-SP-MeSO4 and (E)-PZ-MC-MeSO4. Meanwhile, we failed to achieve near complete isomerization. Based on the composition of the PSS, it is assumed that its spectrum consists of two distinct spectral components. Therefore, we deconvoluted the experimental spectra to obtain the individual spectra (Z)-PZ-SP-MeSO4, (E)-PZ-MC-MeSO4, and (Z)-PZ-MC-MeSO4 (Fig. S22). We also examined the long-term photoswitching stability of PZ-SP-MeSO4 by alternating irradiation, inducing forward E–Z and reverse Z–E photoisomerization and reversible interconversion between the SP and MC state over several cycles. PZ-SP-MeSO4 demonstrated good fatigue resistance, retaining its photochemical performance through 15 consecutive cycles in water (Fig. S24). The combination of enhanced photoisomerization yield in aqueous media and exceptional water solubility (18.4 mM, see the test details in section S5, SI) demonstrates its outstanding potential for physiological applications.

Thermal reverse isomerization kinetics and Z isomer stability

An additional criterion for photoswitches is high thermal stability. The MC form is known to have a short half-life (seconds to minutes) at room temperature (T = 25 °C),50 which consequently affects the stability of both the (E)-PZ-MC-MeSO4 and (Z)-PZ-MC-MeSO4 configurations. We investigated the thermal reverse Z → E isomerization of PZ-SP-MeSO4 in both DMSO and aqueous solutions. Following irradiation at 450 nm, the reaction kinetics were studied using UV-vis spectroscopy. From the first-order kinetics of the (Z)-PZ-SP-MeSO4 → (E)-PZ-SP-MeSO4 isomerization measured at four temperatures, the half-lives at 25 °C were extrapolated using the Arrhenius equation to be 79 days in DMSO and 717 days in water (see Section S6 in the SI) This demonstrates significantly enhanced stability of the Z-isomer's thermal relaxation in both DMSO and aqueous media compared to the reported pyrazolium heteroazoarene 1-C1-MeSO4 in Fig. 1a (t1/2 = 58 days in DMSO; 157 days in water).

The exceptional stability of (Z)-PZ-SP-MeSO4 can be attributed to a combination of electrostatic and electronic effects. A key stabilization factor is the cation–π interaction between the positively charged pyrazolium moiety and the π-electron cloud of the adjacent phenyl ring directly linked to the azo group in the SP unit. This effect is further enhanced by electron-donating substituents at the para-position of the relevant benzene ring, which strengthen the cation–π interaction and result in a pronounced increase in the half-life of the Z-isomer (Section S3).

pH effect and molecular logic gate devices

The isomerization of AB photoswitches is influenced not only by light and temperature but also by the pH of the medium. Previous studies on the compound 1-C1-MeSO4, which shares the same PZ moiety with PZ-SP-MeSO4, showed that the Z isomer is stable across acidic to neutral pH (2–8). However, above pH 8, it undergoes rapid thermal Z → E relaxation.26 Therefore, we propose that the isomerization of (Z)-PZ-SP-MeSO4 → (E)-PZ-SP-MeSO4 can be achieved not only by irradiation at 500 nm but also by increasing the pH beyond 8. Given this established pH sensitivity and the rapid SP → MC conversion under acidic conditions, we investigated the pH effect on PZ-SP-MeSO4 to explore additional pathways for controllable switching.

Absorption and switching properties of PZ-SP-MeSO4 were studied in a series of aqueous-based buffer solutions with pH values (pH in the range of 2–11; see Fig. S31 in the SI). In the acidic to neutral pH range (2 ≤ pH ≤ 7), the thermal conversion from the SP to the MC form proceeds with high efficiency, reaching near-complete conversion at pH = 2. This is clearly demonstrated by the emergence of the characteristic MC absorption band at 575 nm. Under alkaline conditions (pH ≥ 8), the SP configuration remains stable and does not open to MC form (<5% conversion). pH modulation compensates for the limitation that the (Z)-PZ-SP-MeSO4 to (Z)-PZ-MC-MeSO4 conversion cannot be achieved at the available wavelengths. Compared with multi-stimuli-responsive photoswitches,56 which rely on distinct types of external stimuli such as light, pH, temperature, or redox potential, this pH-mediated process can only serve as an auxiliary method to achieve unidirectional regulation of the PZ-SP-MeSO4 orthogonal photoswitch. Motivated by the remarkable pH- and light-driven switching behavior of (E)-PZ-MC-MeSO4, we evaluated its potential as a molecular logic gate system.57–59 In this configuration, 450 nm, 365 nm light and pH 2–7 (acidic) are defined as inputs, while the absorption spectra of different isomers serve as the output signal. The successful demonstration of the multi-valued logic gate highlights its potential for applications in information processing and storage (Fig. S32).

Mechanistic investigations

Transient absorption spectroscopy (TAS) experiments were performed for PZ-SP-MeSO4 in pure H2O (Fig. 4) and MeCN–H2O (1 : 1, v/v) (Section S7), and 450, 420 and 365 nm excitation wavelengths were utilized to probe the dynamics of photo-induced transformation processes to specific PSS. Given the similarity of TAS signals in these two solvents, the results in H2O were discussed to elucidate the underlying mechanisms.

TAS and potential energy profiles at the B3LYP-D3BJ/6-311G(d,p) SMD (H2O) level of theory of PZ-SP-MeSO4 in H2O after excitation with (a) 450 nm, (b) 355 nm and (c) 420 nm light.

450 nm excitation of PZ-SP-MeSO4 probes the ultrafast dynamics initiated at the S1 state, capturing the isomerization of (E)-PZ to (Z)-PZ (Fig. 4a and S33). The ground state bleach (GSB) signal (400–470 nm) persists throughout the observation window (0–1000 ps), while excited-state absorption (ESA) bands appear at 330, 500 and 650 nm. Kinetic fitting for 337, 503, and 657 nm bands yielded decay lifetimes of 1.2–1.4 ps, indicating that these spectral features originate from the same species. This result is consistent with the previously reported rate constant (1.1 ps) for the excited-state decay of (E)-AB-SP (Fig. 1b).51 After the initial decay process at around 1.0 ps, an intermediate state with a 503 nm signal is formed (71.3 ps), which later decays to the ground state (S0) (Z)-PZ-SP-MeSO4, whose absorption corresponds with steady-state spectroscopy data. Theoretical results indicate that (E)-PZ-SP-MeSO4 is initially promoted to the Franck–Condon (FC) region of the S1 state (S1–FC) with n–π* excitation character and subsequently undergoes a barrierless photochemical relaxation process along –NN- twisting mode. Along the (E)-PZ → (Z)-PZ isomerization pathway, internal conversion to the S0 takes place via a conical intersection (CI) labeled Z-CI, leading to vibrationally hot (Z)-PZ-SP-MeSO4 (71.3 ps).

TAS measurement of (E)-PZ-SP-MeSO4 upon excitation at 355 nm is presented in Fig. 4b. As unveiled from the calculated results, 361 nm corresponds to the S3 state and 337 nm corresponds to the S4 state (Fig. 2). Therefore, both 355 nm and 365 nm (the excitation wavelength used in steady-state measurements) excite the system to the S3 state. In contrast to excitation at 450 nm, TAS of (E)-PZ-SP-MeSO4 upon 355 nm excitation reveals a broad ESA band spanning the 500–700 nm region, which exhibits a distinct peak structure at approximately 0.5 ps. Subsequent temporal evolution reveals a continuous blue-shift of the 500 nm band, followed by the emergence of a new absorption feature centered at 480 nm at longer time delays. This behaviour can be explained by a schematic energy diagram (Fig. 4b) depicting the ring-opening dynamics of (E)-PZ-SP-MeSO4. Upon 355 nm excitation, (E)-PZ-SP-MeSO4 is populated to high-lying excited states (S3) and the subsequent rapid sequential S3 → S2 → S1 internal conversion, accompanied by C–O bond cleavage, is observed within the instrument response time of 130 fs (in line with reported values of ∼90 fs60 for similar systems). Excited-state potential energy curves (PECs) scanned along the –NN- twisting and C–O stretching coordinates further confirm that the rapid C–O bond cleavage is an energetically favored process upon S3 excitation (Fig. S34). Within the relaxation of excited-state intermediates along the MC formation pathway (C–O stretching), stepwise conical intersections with gradually extending bond lengths between the spiro-carbon and pyran oxygen mediate the cascade Sn → S0 decays. The evolution of these intermediates is characterized by absorption features at 500–700 nm, appearing within approximately 500 fs, which are in good agreement with literature values.60 The fitting at 503 and 657 nm yield a lifetime of 2.7 ps (Fig. S33), which is significantly elongated with respect to the 1.2–1.4 ps component observed at 450 nm and in agreement with the complex multi-state mediated stepwise relaxation processes upon S3 excitation versus direct barrierless isomerization starting from S1–FC. Subsequent ground state relaxation dynamics after passing through S1/S0 CI (MC-CI) first yield a metastable E-CCC intermediate and ultimately give rise to the E-CTT isomer. The blue shift of the product band stems from the vibrational cooling process in the ground state, which corresponds to dissipation of thermal energy and redistribution of vibrational occupations. The concomitant persistent signature of a long-lived species (80.7 ps) is attributed to the ground state relaxation involving rotation along the C1C2C3C4 (E-CCC → E-CTC) and C2C3C4C5 (E-CCC → E-CCT) dihedrals, thereby populating different conformers (Fig. S35). For E-CCC, since the isomerization to E-CCT faces an obstructive energy barrier (19.9 kcal mol−1), the alternative pathway for E-CTC formation (12.8 kcal mol−1) is preferred in the ground state (Fig. S35). This E-CTC intermediate is still unstable and subsequently overcomes the second transition state barrier of 25.3 kcal mol−1 and finally transforms into the stable (E)-PZ-MC-MeSO4 (E-CTT) configuration.

TAS measurements at the early stage of (E)-PZ-SP-MeSO4 following 420 nm excitation reveal dynamics similar to those observed with 450 nm excitation (Fig. 4c). However, a distinct difference emerges in the longer time evolution with the peak maximum centered at 480 nm, which is analogous to the results obtained upon 355 nm excitation, providing direct evidence for an SP to MC photoisomerization process. Theoretical calculations reveal a hybrid photoisomerization mechanism for the 420 nm photoexcited (E)-PZ-SP-MeSO4, which involves efficient E–Z isomerization of the PZ moiety, similar to that of the 450 nm excitation, followed by SP → MC conversion of the SP unit (Fig. 4c) in accordance with that under 355 nm excitation. According to the computed PECs (Fig. S34), once (E)-PZ-SP-MeSO4 is populated to the FC region of the S2 state, two S2/S1 CIs associated with C–O extension of the SP unit and planarization of the PZ unit are accessible. Between these two relaxation pathways, the latter one is favorable due to descending PECs, and thus the E–Z isomerization towards Z-CI governs the initial stage excited-state relaxation process, followed by internal conversion to the ground-state and formation of a vibrationally excited (Z)-PZ-SP-MeSO4 intermediate. This photochemical process is generally consistent with that observed under 450 nm excitation. As shown by the single-wavelength fitting results, PZ-SP-MeSO4 returns to the ground state at 1.0 ps. We thus compared the TAS spectra of PZ-SP-MeSO4 recorded at 1.0 ps under 420 nm and 450 nm excitation, respectively. The observed absorption shift to above 700 nm (λex = 420 nm) (650 nm at λex = 450 nm, Fig. S37) is due to the generation of the high-energy excited state intermediate.61 Thus, the greater vibrational energy imparted from 420 nm excitation enables the hot ground-state (Z)-PZ-SP-MeSO4 to distribute enough kinetic energy into the C–O stretching mode via vibrational relaxation that ultimately drives the thermally activated ring-opening process. The ground-state activation energy barrier via the formation of the transition state 420-TS1, possessing an elongated semi-broken C–O bond, is 12.4 kcal mol−1. After the C–O bond scission, the molecule undergoes processes analogous to the ground-state isomerization observed at 355 nm excitation, except that in this case the PZ moiety is in the (Z)-form rather than in the (E)-form. These processes involve rapid isomerization and relaxation to the most stable (Z)-PZ-MC-MeSO4 (Z-CTT) isomer.

The combined TAS and computational results elucidate how the photoisomerization proceeds upon higher-energy excitation: the spatial localization of the electronic transition within the excited state determines which switch unit is addressed. Notably, this mechanistic picture is not an isolated finding. In previous studies on orthogonal photoswitch systems,16,62 the same wavelength-dependent behavior governed by excited-state localization/delocalization was observed, confirming that this principle operates across different molecular architectures. We therefore propose that the orthogonal switch behavior accessed via higher-energy excitation arises from the spatial characteristics of the excited-state electron density—rather than from the excitation energy. This mechanism is expected to be general, providing a practical design guideline for future orthogonal photoswitch systems.

Conclusions

By utilizing the red-shifting effect through para-connectivity between PZ and SP units, we developed PZ-SP-MeSO4, an ionic hybrid photoswitch that effectively inherits the advantages of both photochromic functional groups. The current molecular design achieved multi-wavelength addressability and excellent bidirectional photoswitching performance across both organic and aqueous media. The UV-vis spectrum of PZ-SP-MeSO4 serves as a non-destructive readout method for logic operations, demonstrating its potential applications in optical information storage. The demonstrated properties of PZ-SP-MeSO4 position it as a promising candidate for applications in photopharmacology and adaptive materials.

Importantly, mechanistic investigations suggest specifically (de)coupled photodynamics: E–Z isomerization of the PZ part proceeds rapidly on a picosecond timescale (1.2–1.4 ps), while SP → MC conversion occurs on a significantly elongated timescale (80.7 ps). More interestingly, excitation to the S3 state enables the SP → MC ring-opening reaction initiated in the excited state, whereas upon excitation into the lower S2 state, ring-opening occurs as a follow-up process at the S0 state, induced by excess vibrational energy upon internal conversion from higher excited states. This mechanistic information will guide the development of advanced orthogonal hybrid photoswitches by combining ionic heteroaryl azomotifs with secondary photochromic units.
