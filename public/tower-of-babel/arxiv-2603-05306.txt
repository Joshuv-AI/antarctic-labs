Maximum of sparsely equicorrelated Gaussian fields and applications
Johannes Heiny, Tiefeng Jiang, Tuan Pham, Yongcheng Qi

Abstract
We investigate the extreme values of a sparse and equicorrelated Gaussian field on a triangle: the correlations on every vertical or horizontal line are all equal to a parameter r ∈ [ 0 , 1 / 2 ] r\in[0,1/2] and are zero everywhere else. This problem is closely linked with various problems in high-dimensional statistics and extreme-value theory. We identify the threshold for r r at which the standard Gumbel law breaks down. Our result is based on a subtle application of the Chen-Stein method for Poisson approximation. As applications, we discuss the implication of our results on multiple testing and resolve several questions that were left open in 15 , 30 and 12 .



1
Introduction

Consider a centered Gaussian field
\mathcal{G}_{n}=\left\{G_{ij}\right\}_{1\leq i<j\leq n}
with correlation structure given by

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left(G_{ij}G_{kl}\right)=\begin{cases}0~~&\text{if}\ \big|\left\{i,j\right\}\cap\left\{k,l\right\}\big|=0;\\
r~~&\text{if}\ \big|\left\{i,j\right\}\cap\left\{k,l\right\}\big|=1;\\
1~~&\text{if}\ \big|\left\{i,j\right\}\cap\left\{k,l\right\}\big|=2.\end{cases}

(1.1)

The field
\mathcal{G}_{n}
can be regarded as a Gaussian field defined in the triangle such that all the elements belonging to the same row or column are equicorrelated with a common parameter
r\in[0,1/2]
, and are independent otherwise. The restriction that
r\in[0,1/2]
is natural since the correlation structure of
\mathcal{G}_{n}
is not well-defined if
r
is negative or exceeds
1/2
. The requirement
r\leq 1/2
, for instance, can be seen by noting that

\mbox{Var}\left(G_{12}-G_{23}+G_{34}-G_{41}\right)=4-8r\geq 0.

Despite having an exotic form, the Gaussian field
\mathcal{G}_{n}
in (
1.1
) appears in a number of different problems in high-dimensional statistics and extreme-value theory. In particular, understanding the extreme values of
\mathcal{G}_{n}
leads to improvement of many existing results. To the best of our knowledge, all existing results that require dealing with the correlation structure (
1.1
) are limited to the case
r\leq 1/3
, where the maximum of
\mathcal{G}_{n}
behaves like that of i.i.d. standard normals. Let us describe in what follows several contexts in which (
1.1
) shows up.

•

Maximum interpoint distance in high-dimensions.
In
15
;
30
, the authors investigate the asymptotic distribution of the statistic

\displaystyle D_{n}:=\max_{1\leq i<j\leq p}\|\bm{X}_{i}-\bm{X}_{j}\|

(1.2)

where the vectors
\{\bm{X}_{i}=(x_{k1},\dots,x_{kn})\}_{i=1}^{p}
are i.i.d. in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and the coordinates are i.i.d. with mean zero and unit variance. We note that, in (
1.2
),
n
denotes the dimension of the data, while
p
denotes the sample size.
We adopt this notation from
14
.
By contrast,
30
uses the opposite convention, where
n
is the sample size and
p
is the dimension. Despite the difference in the notation, the condition (
1.3
) below is the same in both works.

It was shown in
15
and
30
that
D_{n}
asymptotically follows a Gumbel distribution under the high-dimensional settings, appropriate moment conditions and

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}x_{11}^{4}\leq 5.

(1.3)

The asymptotic distribution of
D_{n}
when (
1.3
) fails was left open in these works. Notably, the analysis of
D_{n}
in these papers essentially reduces to studying the maximum of the Gaussian field
\mathcal{G}_{n}
in (
1.1
), with correlation parameter

r=\frac{\mathbb{E}[x_{11}^{4}]-1}{2\mathbb{E}[x_{11}^{4}]+2}.

The condition (
1.3
) is equivalent to requiring
r\leq\tfrac{1}{3}
.

Sample coefficients of equicorrelated populations.
In
12
, the authors investigated the asymptotic distribution of the largest sample coefficients in the ultra high-dimensional settings. This includes the largest entries of sample covariance and correlation matrices described in (
1.5
) and (
1.4
) below. Some background and related results are presented in Section
4
.

Let us briefly introduce the settings. Given
n
i.i.d. data points
\bm{X}_{k}=\left(x_{k1},x_{k2},\dots,x_{kp}\right)^{\top}
,
1\leq k\leq n
from a multivariate normal distribution with covariance matrix

\mathbb{E}\left(\bm{X}_{1}\bm{X}_{1}^{\top}\right)=\left(1-\rho\right)\bm{I}_{p}+\rho\bm{1}\bm{1}^{\top}\,,

define the sample correlation and (non-normalized) sample covariance coefficients

\displaystyle\hat{\rho}_{ij}

\displaystyle:=\frac{\sum_{k=1}^{n}(x_{ki}-\bar{x}_{i})(x_{kj}-\bar{x}_{j})}{\sqrt{\sum_{k=1}^{n}(x_{ki}-\bar{x}_{i})^{2}}\cdot\sqrt{\sum_{k=1}^{n}(x_{kj}-\bar{x}_{j})^{2}}}\,,

(1.4)

\displaystyle\hat{r}_{ij}

\displaystyle:=\sum_{k=1}^{n}(x_{ki}-\bar{x}_{i})(x_{kj}-\bar{x}_{j}).

(1.5)

It was shown in
12
that

\max_{i<j}\hat{\rho}_{ij}
and
\max_{i<j}\hat{r}_{ij}

are asymptotically Gumbel, normal, or a mixture of the two in the high-dimensional setting, depending on whether

\rho\sqrt{\log p}\ll 1
,
\rho\sqrt{\log p}\gg 1
, or
\rho\sqrt{\log p}\to c\in(0,\infty)
, respectively.

Interestingly, the analysis of both

\max_{i<j}\hat{\rho}_{ij}
and
\max_{i<j}\hat{r}_{ij}

can be reduced to the study of the maximum of the Gaussian field
\mathcal{G}_{n}
in (
1.1
),
with correlation parameter

r=\frac{\rho}{1+\rho}\quad\text{for }\eqref{sample covariance},\qquad r=\frac{\rho-\rho^{2}/2}{1+2\rho-\rho^{2}/2}\quad\text{for }\eqref{pearson}.

Note that, as
\rho\to 1
, the parameter
r
approaches
1/2
in the case of (
1.5
),
and
1/5
in the case of (
1.4
).
Therefore, the technical condition

\displaystyle\limsup\rho<\tfrac{1}{2}

(1.6)

was imposed in
12
for their analysis of
\max_{i<j}\hat{r}_{ij}
.
It is worth pointing out that this assumption is equivalent to requiring
r\leq\tfrac{1}{3}
in (
1.1
).

Interestingly, it can be seen that the corresponding
r
for (
1.4
) is always less or equal to
1/5
in the Gaussian case, which explains why no restriction on
\rho
is needed for the analysis of
\max_{i<j}\hat{\rho}_{ij}
in
12
.

Family-wise error rate (FWER) control in mutiple testing.
Consider the multiple testing problem

H_{0ij}:\theta_{ij}=0\ \text{against}\ H_{1ij}:\theta_{ij}>0

where one obtains a single observation
Y_{ij}\sim N\left(\theta_{ij},1\right)
for each
1\leq i<j\leq n
.

Suppose the joint distribution of
\mathcal{Y}_{n}=\left\{Y_{ij};1\leq i<j\leq n\right\}
is a Gaussian graphical model with covariance satisfying

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}Y_{ij}Y_{kl}:=\begin{cases}0\ &\text{if}\ |\left\{i,j\right\}\cap\left\{k,l\right\}|=0;\\
r_{ijkl}\in[0,1/2-\delta]&\text{if}\ |\left\{i,j\right\}\cap\left\{k,l\right\}|=1;\\
1&\text{if}\ |\left\{i,j\right\}\cap\left\{k,l\right\}|=2\end{cases}

(1.7)

for some
\delta>0
.

The graphical covariance structure described above commonly arises in brain imaging data: adjacent brain regions are typically correlated, while non-adjacent regions tend to be independent. A natural test statistic for controlling the family-wise error rate (FWER) is to reject if

\displaystyle\max_{1\leq i<j\leq n}Y_{ij}>u_{n}

(1.8)

for some threshold
u_{n}
.
It is therefore of interest to understand the behavior of
\max_{1\leq i<j\leq n}Y_{ij}
under this graphical model. Standard methods based on excursion probabilities of smooth Gaussian fields
7
;
6
;
8
rely on smooth manifold structures and are not applicable here due to the discrete nature of the graph. Nevertheless, our main result shows that analyzing the maximum of
\mathcal{G}_{n}
with correlation structure (
1.1
) yields an asymptotically exact approximation for
u_{n}
.

In general, controlling the FWER under complex dependence is challenging: one often resorts to union bounds, which give overly conservative thresholds. For this reason, much of the multiple testing literature instead focuses on controlling the false discovery rate (FDR). As an application of our results, we demonstrate that asymptotically exact thresholds for FWER control can in fact be obtained for the Gaussian graphical model (
1.7
).

The goal of the present article is to resolve all these gaps by establishing new results on the maximum of the Gaussian field
\mathcal{G}_{n}
in (
1.1
). We show that, perhaps surprisingly, the maximum of
\mathcal{G}_{n}
still behaves like that of i.i.d. standard normal random variables as long as

1-2r\gg\frac{\log\log n}{\sqrt{\log n}}.

This constrasts the common belief that the Gumbel asymptotic distribution no longer holds when
r>1/3
.

Moreover, at the finer scale where
(1-2r)\log n\to\lambda\in[0,\infty)
, the i.i.d. behavior breaks down. In this regime, we show that the asymptotic distribution of the maximum resembles either the right-most point of a perturbed Poisson process or the sum of the two right-most points of a Poisson process. Our proof relies on a delicate application of Chen–Stein’s method for Poisson approximation via a carefully designed truncation argument to create asymptotic independence.

As a consequence of the newly developed results, we show the following.

1.

The assumption for the maximum interpoint distance
D_{n}
that the fourth moment is bounded above by
5
, that is (
1.3
), can be removed. Thus, the results in
15
;
30
hold without this assumption.

2.

The i.i.d. behavior breaks down and there are different limiting distributions for
D_{n}
when the fourth moment diverges to infinity at the scale
\log p
.

3.

For sample coefficients, we recover the results in
12
for non-Gaussian distributions without imposing the restriction that the correlation parameter
\rho
is bounded away from
1/2
in (
1.6
). Morever, we show that in the non-Gaussian setting, the situation is far more complicated: the phase transition involving a non-Gumbel distribution and central limit theorem can still hold even in the weakly dependent regime
\rho\sqrt{\log p}\to 0
if the fourth moment of the marginal is allowed to diverge.

In contrast, the limiting distribution is always Gumbel in the Gaussian case (or fixed-marginal setting, see Sections
3
and
4
below). Similarly, a non-Gumbel distribution can appear for the maximum interpoint distance in
15
.

4.

The results in Theorem
2.1
below can be used to provide asymptotically exact thresholds for multiple testing in a Gaussian graphical model.

2
Main results

To describe the limiting distribution of the maximum of the Gaussian field
\mathcal{G}_{n}
in (
1.1
), we need the sequences

\displaystyle c_{n}

\displaystyle:=\sqrt{2\log n}\quad\text{ and }\quad d_{n}:=\sqrt{2\log n}-\frac{\log\log n+\log 4\pi}{2\sqrt{2\log n}}.

(2.1)

We assume that the correlation parameter
r=r_{n}
depends on
n
, unless explicitly stated otherwise. Throughout this paper, all limits are for
n\to\infty
.

Our first result concerns the weakly dependent regime.

Theorem 2.1
.

Suppose
(1-2r)\frac{\sqrt{\log n}}{\log\log n}\to\infty
and recall the Gaussian field
\mathcal{G}_{n}
in (
1.1
). Then,

\sqrt{2}c_{n}\left(\max_{1\leq i<j\leq n}G_{ij}-\sqrt{2}c_{n}+\frac{\log\left(4\sqrt{\pi}c_{n}\right)}{\sqrt{2}c_{n}}\right)

converges in distribution to the standard Gumbel law with CDF
\exp\left(-e^{-x}\right)
.

It is well-known that the normal distribution is in the maximum domain of attraction of the Gumbel law, which corresponds to the special case
r=0
in Theorem
2.1
(see for instance
11
). Theorem
2.1
states that even under dependence the maximum of
\mathcal{G}_{n}
behaves like that of i.i.d. standard normal random variables, as long as
(1-2r)\sqrt{\log n}/\log\log n\to\infty
. Next, we describe the limiting distribution at the critical regime where this i.i.d. resemblance breaks down.

Let
\left\{\zeta_{k};k\geq 1\right\}
be a sequence of i.i.d. standard exponential random variables and define

\displaystyle\eta_{i}:=-\log\left(\sum_{k=1}^{i}\zeta_{k}\right)\,,\qquad i\geq 1.

(2.2)

Then the set
\left\{\eta_{i};i\geq 1\right\}
forms a realization of a Poisson Point Process (P.P.P.) with intensity measure
e^{-x}dx
. We will use this equivalence interchangeably throughout the paper.

At the critical correlation regime, the limiting distribution of
\max_{1\leq i<j\leq n}G_{ij}
can be expressed as the supremum of the points
\eta_{i}
and i.i.d. normal variables, as described in the next result.

Theorem 2.2
.

Suppose
(1-2r)\log n\to\lambda\in(0,\infty)
and recall the Gaussian field
\mathcal{G}_{n}
in (
1.1
). Then it holds

\displaystyle c_{n}\left(\max_{1\leq i<j\leq n}G_{ij}-\sqrt{2}d_{n}\right)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\to}}\sup_{i<j}\left(\frac{\eta_{i}+\eta_{j}}{\sqrt{2}}+\sqrt{2\lambda}\cdot Z_{ij}\right)-\lambda,

(2.3)

where
\eta_{i}
’s are defined in (
2.2
) and
Z_{ij}
’s are i.i.d. standard normal independent from
\eta_{k}
’s.

Note that by Lemma
9.3
, the supremum on the right-hand side of (
2.3
) has an almost surely finite, non-degenerate distribution.
In Theorem
2.2
, the correlation parameter
r
tends to
1/2
at a specific rate. If we let
r\to 1/2
at a faster rate, it turns out that the
Z_{ij}
’s disappear in the expression for the limiting distribution.

Theorem 2.3
.

Suppose
(1-2r)\log n\to 0
. Then it holds

\displaystyle c_{n}\left(\max_{1\leq i<j\leq n}G_{ij}-\sqrt{2}d_{n}\right)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\to}}\frac{\eta_{1}+\eta_{2}}{\sqrt{2}}.

Remark 2.1
.

Let us compare the scalings in Theorem
2.1
versus Theorems
2.2
and
2.3
, respectively.
In all three cases, the scaling sequences are of the same order, but the second-order terms in the centering sequences differ.
For Theorem
2.1
, the second-order term in the centering is
\log\log n/\bigl(4\sqrt{\log n}\bigr)
, whereas for Theorems
2.2
and
2.3
it is
\log\log n/\bigl(2\sqrt{\log n}\bigr)
.

This subtle difference can be explained by examining which terms contribute to the maximum.
Our proofs reveal that in Theorem
2.1
we have
\Theta(p^{2})
terms
G_{ij}
’s that contribute to the maximum on the same scale, whereas in Theorems
2.2
and
2.3
only a set of size
\Theta(p)
contributes significantly to the maximum. Here the notation
a_{n}=\Theta(b_{n})
means that
b_{n}/C\leq a_{n}\leq Cb_{n}
for some universal constant
C>0
.

3
Application I: Maximum interpoint distance

The maximum interpoint distance is a classical object in stochastic geometry and statistics and has been studied extensively over the last few decades.

Suppose
\bm{X}_{1},\dots,\bm{X}_{p}
are i.i.d. random vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, where each
\bm{X}_{i}=(x_{i1},\dots,x_{in})^{\top}
has i.i.d. components with the same distribution as some generic random variable
\xi
, which we assume to be centered with unit variance. Here
n
denotes the dimension and
p
the sample size.
We adopt this convention to be consistent with
14
.
Note that
30
uses
(n,p)
to denote the sample size and the dimension, respectively.

The goal is to investigate the asymptotic distribution of

\displaystyle D_{n}:=\max_{1\leq i<j\leq p}\|\bm{X}_{i}-\bm{X}_{j}\|_{2}

(3.1)

as
n,p\to\infty
.

This problem has a long history, and an incomplete list of results includes
28
;
17
;
20
; see also the references therein. While these early results were established in fixed-dimensional settings, recent advances in high-dimensional statistics motivate the need to understand the asymptotic distribution when the dimension diverges.

The limiting distribution of
D_{n}
is much less understood in this high-dimensional regime and to the best of our knowledge, it has only been studied in
30
;
15
. In both works, the authors show that
D_{n}
is asymptotically Gumbel distributed when
p,n\to\infty
and under the condition that

\mathbb{E}\xi^{4}\leq 5.

In what follows, we improve their results by dropping the above condition. Furthemore, we show that different limiting distributions appear if the law of
\xi
is allowed to be
n
-dependent, that is,
\xi=\xi_{n}
. As a consequence, these limiting distributions may crucially depend on the quantity
\mathbb{E}\xi_{n}^{4}
.

3.1
The fixed marginals case

We impose one of the following conditions on
\xi
:

\displaystyle\exists\,s>2,\varepsilon>0:\quad

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\!\left[\,|\xi|^{2s+\varepsilon}\,\right]<\infty.

(B1)

\displaystyle\exists\,\eta>0:\quad

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\!\left[e^{\eta|\xi|^{2}}\right]<\infty.

(B2)

\displaystyle\exists\,K<\infty:\quad

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(|\xi|\leq K)=1.

(B3)

One can see that our conditions (
B1
)–(
B3
) are similar to those of Theorem 2.1 in
15
, except that we do not impose any restriction on the size of
\mathbb{E}\xi^{4}
. We refer to Remark
3.1
for technical details.

Under either one of these assumptions, the maximum interpoint distance is asymptotically Gumbel distributed, provided that
p
does not grow too fast relative to
n
.

Proposition 3.1
.

Under one of conditions (
B1
)–(
B3
), we have,

\displaystyle\sqrt{2}c_{p}\left(\frac{D_{n}^{2}-2n}{\sqrt{2n(1+\mathbb{E}\xi^{4})}}-\sqrt{2}c_{p}+\frac{\log\left(4\sqrt{\pi}c_{p}\right)}{\sqrt{2}c_{p}}\right)\

(3.2)

converges in distribution to the standard Gumbel law with CDF
\exp\left(-e^{-x}\right)
, where
c_{p}
is defined in (
2.1
) with
n
being replaced by
p
, and
p
satisfies

(i)

p=O(n^{(s-2)/4})
if (
B1
) holds.

(ii)

p=\exp\!\big(o(n^{1/5})\big)
if (
B2
) holds.

(iii)

p=\exp\!\big(o\left(\frac{n^{1/3}}{(\log n)^{2/3}}\right)\big)
if (
B3
) holds.

Note that the scaling in (
3.2
) is exactly the same as in
14
.

Proof of Proposition
3.1
.

Let
\mathcal{G}_{n}=\left\{G_{ij}:1\leq i<j\leq p\right\}
be the Gaussian field in (
1.1
) with correlation parameter

r=(\mathbb{E}\xi^{4}-1)/(2(\mathbb{E}\xi^{4}+1))
. Since

1-2r=\frac{2}{\mathbb{E}\xi^{4}+1}>0,

an application of Theorem
2.1
yields that

\mathbb{P}\left(\sqrt{2}c_{p}\Big(\max_{1\leq i<j\leq p}G_{ij}-\sqrt{2}c_{p}+\frac{\log\left(4\sqrt{\pi}c_{p}\right)}{\sqrt{2}c_{p}}\Big)\ \leq x\right)\to\exp\left(-e^{-x}\right)\,,\qquad x\in\mathbb{R}.

Therefore, it is suffices to check that

\displaystyle\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}}\Big|\mathbb{P}\left(\frac{D_{n}^{2}-2n}{\sqrt{2n(1+\mathbb{E}\xi^{4})}}\leq t\right)-\mathbb{P}\Big(\max_{1\leq i<j\leq p}G_{ij}\leq t\Big)\Big|\to 0

(3.3)

if either one of (
B1
)–(
B3
) holds with the corresponding requirement on
p
as in the statement of Proposition
3.1
.
To verify (
3.3
), observe that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(D_{n}^{2}\leq t\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{n}\left(x_{ki}-x_{kj}\right)^{2}\leq t:1\leq i<j\leq p\right)

is the probability of a sum of i.i.d. random vectors staying in the hyperrectangle
[-\infty,t]^{p(p-1)/2}
. For high-dimensional normal approximation of the sum, we calculate the covariance structure of the vectors:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left(x_{12}-x_{13}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=2,

\displaystyle\mbox{Var}\left[\left(x_{12}-x_{13}\right)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=2\left(1+\mathbb{E}\xi^{4}\right),

\displaystyle\mbox{Corr}\left[\left(x_{12}-x_{13}\right)^{2},\left(x_{12}-x_{14}\right)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathbb{E}\xi^{4}-1}{2\left(\mathbb{E}\xi^{4}+1\right)}=r.

We then obtain (
3.3
) by applying Theorem 2.1(b) of
23
under condition (
B1
) with
q=s+\varepsilon/2
(see also the bound on the right-hand side of (
3.6
) below where we derive the bounds explicitly). Under condition (
B2
), we apply Corollary 2.1 in
23
to deduce (
3.3
).

Finally, under (
B3
), we get (
3.3
) due to the condition
\log p=o\left(n^{1/3}/(\log n)^{2/3}\right)
by Corollary 2.1 in
9
. Note that such a result is applicable because the smallest eigenvalue of the covariance matrix of

\left\{\left(x_{1i}-x_{1j}\right)^{2}-2\right\}_{1\leq i<j\leq p}

is strictly away from
0
. This will be verified in Lemma
9.1
below, completing the proof of (
3.3
).
\square

Remark 3.1
.

A few remarks about the assumptions of Proposition

are in place.

(a)

Condition (

) coincides with condition (B3) in

3.2
The
n
-dependent marginals case

In this subsection, we will use Theorems
2.2
and
2.3
to demonstrate that new limiting distributions can arise when
\xi=\xi_{n}
is allowed to be
n
dependent and diverges. To this end, we will focus on the simple high-dimensional linear regime that
p\asymp n
for simplicity. It is worth mentioning that the arguments below can be adapted to other regimes and moment conditions in a relatively straightforward manner.

We assume the following conditions:

\displaystyle 0<\inf_{n\geq 1}\frac{p}{n}\leq\sup_{n\geq 1}\frac{p}{n}<\infty\ \quad\text{and}\quad n,p\to\infty.

(C1)

\displaystyle\exists\varepsilon>0:\quad

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left(|\xi_{n}|^{12+\varepsilon}\right)<\infty,\ \forall n\geq 1.

(C2)

\displaystyle\exists C_{\varepsilon}>0:\quad

\displaystyle\sup_{n\geq 1}\frac{\mathbb{E}\left(|\xi_{n}|^{12+\varepsilon}\right)}{\left[\mathbb{E}\left(\xi_{n}^{4}\right)\right]^{C_{\varepsilon}}}<\infty.

(C3)

Condition (
C1
) requires that
p
grows at most linearly with
n
.
Condition (
C2
) assumes that
\xi_{n}
has a finite
(12+\varepsilon)
-moment for every
n
(though it may diverge as
n\to\infty
).
Finally, Condition (
C3
) is a type of reverse Hölder inequality, which states that the
(12+\varepsilon)
-moment of
\xi_{n}
grows at most polynomially in terms of its fourth moment.
This condition is needed to guarantee the validity of the Gaussian approximation argument.

Intuitively, Conditions (
C2
) and (
C3
) imply that the mass of the distribution of
\xi_{n}
can be gradually pushed toward infinity, but at a scale where its higher moments grow at most polynomially fast.

One such example of
\xi_{n}
is the mixture distribution

\displaystyle\xi_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\delta_{n}\mbox{Unif}\left[-1,1\right]+(1-\delta_{n})\mbox{Unif}\left[(-2e_{n},-e_{n})\cup(2e_{n},e_{n})\right]\,,

(3.4)

where
\delta_{n}:=2/\left(7e_{n}^{2}-1\right)
and
e_{n}\geq 10
.
Note that
\xi_{n}
chosen above is centered, unit variance, and satisfies

\mathbb{E}\xi_{n}^{2k}=\frac{1}{2k+1}\Big[\,1+\delta_{n}\big((2^{\,2k+1}-1)\,e_{n}^{\,2k}-1\big)\Big]

for every fixed
k\in\mathbb{N}
. Clearly, for every fixed
k>1
, the
2k
-th moment of

diverges if

diverges.
It is easy to check that this mixture distribution satisfies (
C2
) and (
C3
) with
\varepsilon=2
and
C_{\varepsilon}=5
for every sequence
\left\{e_{n};n\geq 1\right\}
.

As a consequence of Theorems
2.2
and
2.3
, we can show under (
C1
)–(
C3
) the maximum interpoint distance can have a non-Gumbel limiting distribution.

Proposition 3.2
.

Suppose (
C1
)–(
C3
) hold and recall the sequences
c_{n}
and
d_{n}
from (
2.1
). Assume that

(\log p)/\mathbb{E}\xi_{n}^{4}\to\lambda

for some
\lambda\in[0,\infty)
, where in the case
\lambda=0
we additionally require that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left(\xi_{n}^{4}\right)=O\left((\log p)^{A}\right)

(3.5)

for some constant

. Then it holds

\displaystyle c_{p}\left(\frac{D_{n}^{2}-2n}{\sqrt{2n(1+\mathbb{E}\xi_{n}^{4})}}-\sqrt{2}d_{p}\right)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\to}}

\displaystyle\sup_{i<j}\left(\frac{\eta_{i}+\eta_{j}}{\sqrt{2}}+2\sqrt{\lambda}\cdot Z_{ij}\right)-2\lambda

where
\eta_{i}
’s are defined in (
2.2
) and
Z_{ij}
’s are i.i.d. standard normal random variables independent from
\eta_{i}
’s, and
c_{p}
and
d_{p}
are defined as in (
2.1
).

The limiting random variable in the case
\lambda=0
in Proposition
3.2
simplifies to

\sup_{i<j}\frac{\eta_{i}+\eta_{j}}{\sqrt{2}}=\frac{\eta_{1}+\eta_{2}}{\sqrt{2}},

where we used the fact that
\left\{\eta_{k};k\geq 1\right\}
forms a decreasing sequence.

Remark 3.2
.

Note that Proposition
3.2
does not cover the case
\lambda=\infty
, for which one would need the stronger condition

\big(\sqrt{\log p}\big)/\mathbb{E}\xi_{n}^{4}\to\infty
.
If this condition holds, then the limiting distribution is the standard Gumbel law as in Proposition
3.1
(i). The gap between the scales
\sqrt{\log p}
and
\log p
arises from the gap in Theorems
2.1
and
2.2
. We conjecture that Theorem
2.1
remains valid up to the scale

, although the analysis based on Poisson approximation is unlikely to extend that far.

Proof of Proposition
3.2
.
Let us first check that (
3.3
) is still valid under conditions (
C1
)–(
C3
). Observe that

\mathbb{E}[\left(x_{12}-x_{13}\right)^{2}]=2\quad\text{ and }\quad\operatorname{Var}\left[\left(x_{12}-x_{13}\right)^{2}\right]=2\left(1+\mathbb{E}\xi_{n}^{4}\right).

Let
\mathcal{G}_{n}=\left\{G_{ij};1\leq i<j\leq p\right\}
be the Gaussian field in (
1.1
) with correlation parameter

r_{n}:=\frac{\mathbb{E}\xi_{n}^{4}-1}{2\left(\mathbb{E}\xi_{n}^{4}+1\right)}.

By using the high-dimensional central limit theorem (see Theorem 2.1 in
23
), we obtain

\displaystyle\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}}

\displaystyle\left|\mathbb{P}\left(\frac{D_{n}^{2}-2n}{\sqrt{2n\left(1+\mathbb{E}\xi_{n}^{4}\right)}}\leq t\right)-\mathbb{P}\left(\max_{1\leq i<j\leq p}G_{ij}\leq t\right)\right|

\displaystyle\lesssim_{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle\left(\frac{B_{n1}^{2}(\log p)^{5}}{n}\right)^{1/6}+\left(\frac{B_{n2}^{2}\left(\log p\right)^{3-2/q}}{n^{1-2/q}}\right)^{1/3}\,,

(3.6)

where
q:=6+\varepsilon/4
and

\displaystyle B_{n1}^{2}

\displaystyle:=\frac{\mathbb{E}\left[\big(\left(x_{12}-x_{13}\right)^{2}-2\big)^{4}\right]}{\left[1+\mathbb{E}\xi_{n}^{4}\right]^{2}}\leq\frac{128+8\mathbb{E}\left(x_{12}-x_{13}\right)^{8}}{\left[1+\mathbb{E}\xi_{n}^{4}\right]^{2}}\leq\frac{128\left[1+16\mathbb{E}\xi_{n}^{8}\right]}{\left[1+\mathbb{E}\xi_{n}^{4}\right]^{2}};

\displaystyle B_{n2}

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+\mathbb{E}\xi_{n}^{4}}}\Big\|\max_{1\leq i<j\leq p}\left|\left(x_{1i}-x_{1j}\right)^{2}-2\right|\Big\|_{L_{q}}.

By using assumption (
C2
) and Hölder’s inequality, we conclude that
B_{n1}^{2}=O\left((\log p)^{C_{1}}\right)
for some constant

depending only on
\varepsilon
. For the term

, note that assumption (

) implies that

\displaystyle\Big\|\max_{1\leq i<j\leq p}\left|\left(x_{1i}-x_{1j}\right)^{2}-2\right|\Big\|_{L_{q}}

\displaystyle\leq p^{2/q}\times\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\left(x_{1i}-x_{1j}\right)^{2}-2\right|^{q}\right]^{1/q}

\displaystyle=O\left(p^{2/q}(\log p)^{C_{2}}\right)

for some constant

not depending on

.

Thus, to deduce that (
3.6
) tends to

so that

\frac{B_{n2}^{2}}{n^{1-2/q}}\ll n^{-C_{3}}.

This is true since

\frac{p^{4/q}}{n^{1-2/q}}=O\left(n^{6/q-1}\right)=O\left(n^{-\varepsilon/(24+\varepsilon)}\right).

The proof of Proposition
3.2
is completed by invoking Theorems
2.2
,
2.3
and using (

(1-2r_{n})\log p=\frac{2\log p}{1+\mathbb{E}\xi_{n}^{4}}\to 2\lambda.

\square

4
Application II: Sample coefficients of equicorrelated populations

The study of extremes of sample covariance and sample correlation coefficients can be traced back to the seminal work
22
. Similar results and techniques have since been used in a number of different problems ranging from testing covariance structures and independence
5
;
2
, two-sample tests
4
, to community detection
19
.

Let us briefly recall the problem settings and related results in what follows. We are given i.i.d. data points
\bm{X}_{1},\dots,\bm{X}_{n}
with
\bm{X}_{i}=\left(x_{i1},x_{i2},\dots,x_{ip}\right)^{\top}
. Recall the sample covariance coefficients
\hat{r}_{ij}
and sample correlation coefficients
\hat{\rho}_{ij}
defined in (
1.5
) and (
1.4
), respectively. Their maxima are given by

\displaystyle R_{n}

\displaystyle:=\max_{1\leq i<j\leq p}\hat{r}_{ij}\qquad\text{ and }\qquad M_{n}:=\max_{1\leq i<j\leq p}\hat{\rho}_{ij}.

(4.1)

There is a long line of literature on the asymptotic distributions of
R_{n}
and
M_{n}
in the independent setting, where the coordinates
x_{ki}
are i.i.d. The asymptotic Gumbel law of
R_{n}
and
M_{n}
established in
22
was extended in
31
to a more general framework that only requires finiteness of the sixth moment of the
x_{ij}
’s and the condition
p=O(n)
. In subsequent works
24
;
25
;
26
, necessary and sufficient conditions were derived for the Gumbel law to hold. With respect to the dependence between
p
and
n
, optimal dimension-dependence results were obtained in
27
,
29
,
16
and
14
. Results regarding the banded covariance matrices can be found in
3
.

The asymptotic distributions of
R_{n}
and
M_{n}
in high dimensions are highly intricate. High dimensionality, dependence structures and heavy-tails can disrupt the classical Gumbel law. High dimensionality typically breaks the Gumbel limit by inducing clustering in the extremes (often quantified by the extremal index), whereas dependence structures (even when being simple) can alter the limiting distribution entirely, leading to asymptotic laws that no longer belong to the classical extreme-value families of Gumbel, Fréchet or Weibull type.

Results in the dependent settings are much scarcer. To the best of our knowledge,
12
and
21
are the only works that investigate this case. In particular,
12
considers the Gaussian population with an equicorrelated correlation structure, while
21
studies the auto-regressive correlation structure. Both papers show that in these settings the asymptotic distributions of
R_{n}
and
M_{n}
are no longer Gumbel.

We now describe the results of
12
in more detail. Suppose the distribution of
\bm{X}_{i}=(x_{i1},x_{i2},\dots,x_{ip})^{\top}
admits the representation

\displaystyle x_{ik}:=\sqrt{\rho}\,\xi_{i}+\sqrt{1-\rho}\,\xi_{ik}

(4.2)

where
\left\{\xi_{i},\xi_{ik}:1\leq i\leq n,\,1\leq k\leq p\right\}
are i.i.d. with mean
0
and unit variance. For simplicity, denote their common law by
\xi
. It was shown in
12
that when
\xi
is standard normal and
\log p=o(n^{1/3})
the following hold:

(i)
For
R_{n}
defined in (
4.1
), under the condition
\limsup\rho_{n}<1/2
,
R_{n}
is asymptotically Gumbel, normal, or a mixture of independent Gumbel and normal, depending on whether
\rho\sqrt{\log p}
converges to
0
,
\infty
, or a finite nonzero constant, respectively.

(ii)
For
M_{n}
defined in (
4.1
), under the condition
\limsup\rho_{n}<1
,
M_{n}
is asymptotically Gumbel, normal, or a mixture of independent Gumbel and normal, depending on whether
\rho\sqrt{\log p}
converges to
0
,
\infty
, or a finite nonzero constant, respectively.

The proofs in
12
are highly technical and involved. In this section, we recover the results for both
R_{n}
and
M_{n}
with very simple proofs, and moreover without the
1/2
restriction on the range of
\rho
. We further show that different limiting distributions can arise when the distribution of
\xi
is allowed to depend on
n
.

4.1
The fixed marginal case

We will first show the results under the stronger assumption that
\log p=o(n^{1/5})
. While this condition is stronger than
\log p=o(n^{1/3})
, the proofs are much shorter and more intuitive, and more importantly, it requires no restriction on the range of
\rho
. In what follows, we put
\kappa=\mathbb{E}\xi^{4}
and make the additional assumption that the distribution of
\xi
is sub-Gaussian, that is,

\mathbb{E}\exp\left(t\xi^{2}\right)<\infty

for some
t>0
.

Proposition 4.1
.

Suppose
\log p=o(n^{1/5})
,
\limsup\rho_{n}<1
and recall
R_{n}
from (
4.1
). Define

R_{n}^{*}:=\frac{R_{n}}{\sqrt{n}}-\rho\sqrt{n}-\left(2\sqrt{\log p}-\frac{\log\log p}{4\sqrt{\log p}}\right)\sqrt{1-\rho^{2}}.

Then the following statements hold.

(i)

If
\rho\sqrt{\log p}\to 0
, we have

2\sqrt{\log p}\,R_{n}^{*}\;\;\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\to}}\;G_{1}\,,

where
G_{1}
has the Gumbel law with CDF
x\mapsto\exp\!\left(-\tfrac{1}{4\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-x}\right)
.

(ii)

If
\rho\sqrt{\log p}\to\lambda\in(0,\infty)
, we have

\tfrac{1}{\rho}R_{n}^{*}\;\;\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\to}}\;\tfrac{1}{2\lambda}G_{1}+N(0,\kappa-1),

where
G_{1}
and
N(0,\kappa-1)
are independent.

(iii)

If
\rho\sqrt{\log p}\to\infty
, we have

\tfrac{1}{\rho}R_{n}^{*}\;\;\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\to}}\;N(0,\kappa-1).

Note that, in contrast to
12
and
15
, we do not impose the condition
\limsup\rho_{n}<1/2
. Moreover, with
\kappa=3
, we recover the results in
12
, which corresponds to the Gaussian setting.

Proof of Proposition
4.1
.

Let us first get rid of the sample mean in (
1.5
). We will show that

\displaystyle\frac{R_{n}}{\sqrt{n}}=\max_{1\leq i<j\leq p}\frac{\sum_{k=1}^{n}x_{ki}x_{kj}}{\sqrt{n}}+O_{\mathbb{P}}\left(\frac{\log p}{\sqrt{n}}\right).

(4.3)

To see this, write

\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\left(x_{ki}-\bar{x}_{i}\right)\left(x_{kj}-\bar{x}_{j}\right)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{k=1}^{n}x_{ki}x_{kj}-\sqrt{n}\cdot\bar{x}_{i}\bar{x}_{j}.

By using either Lemma 2 in
21
or Bernstein’s inequality, we get

\max_{1\leq i\leq p}\sqrt{n}|\bar{x}_{i}|=O_{\mathbb{P}}\left(\sqrt{\log p}\right).

Thus, (
4.3
) is justified.
Straightforward calculation using (
4.2
) gives

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(x_{1i}x_{1j}x_{1k}x_{1l})=\begin{cases}(\kappa-1)\rho^{2},&\quad\big|\left\{i,j\right\}\cap\left\{k,l\right\}\big|=0;\\
\rho+(\kappa-2)\rho^{2},&\quad\bigm|\left\{i,j\right\}\cap\left\{k,l\right\}\big|=1;\\
1+(\kappa-2)\rho^{2},&\quad\bigm|\left\{i,j\right\}\cap\left\{k,l\right\}\big|=2.\end{cases}

Next, by using the high-dimensional central limit theorem (Theorem 2.1 in
23
), we get
\log p=o(n^{1/5})
that

\displaystyle\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}}\left|\mathbb{P}\left(\max_{1\leq i<j\leq p}\frac{\sum_{k=1}^{n}x_{ki}x_{kj}-n\rho}{\sqrt{n(1+(\kappa-2)\rho^{2})}}\leq t\right)-\mathbb{P}\left(\max_{1\leq i<j\leq p}Z_{ij}\leq t\right)\right|\to 0\,,

(4.4)

where
\left\{Z_{ij};1\leq i<j\leq p\right\}
is the centered Gaussian field with correlation structure

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(Z_{ij}Z_{kl})=\begin{cases}\frac{(\kappa-1)\rho^{2}}{1+(\kappa-2)\rho^{2}},&\big|\left\{i,j\right\}\cap\left\{k,l\right\}\big|=0;\\
\frac{\rho+(\kappa-2)\rho^{2}}{1+(\kappa-2)\rho^{2}},&\bigm|\left\{i,j\right\}\cap\left\{k,l\right\}\big|=1;\\
1,&\bigm|\left\{i,j\right\}\cap\left\{k,l\right\}\big|=2.\end{cases}

From this, one can write

\displaystyle\max_{1\leq i<j\leq p}Z_{ij}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\sqrt{\frac{(\kappa-1)\rho^{2}}{1+(\kappa-2)\rho^{2}}}\cdot G+\sqrt{\frac{1-\rho^{2}}{1+(\kappa-2)\rho^{2}}}\cdot\max_{1\leq i<j\leq p}G^{*}_{ij}\,,

(4.5)

where
G
is a standard normal distribution independent from
G_{ij}
’s and the Gaussian field
\left\{G_{ij};1\leq i<j\leq p\right\}
is as in (
1.1
) with correlation parameter

r=\frac{\rho}{1+\rho}
.
In view of the assumption on
\rho
, it follows that

1-2r=\frac{1-\rho}{1+\rho}

is strictly greater than zero. Consequently,
(1-2r)\sqrt{\log p}/\log\log p\to\infty
. The proof is completed by combining (
4.3
), (
4.4
), (
4.5
) and then applying Theorem
2.1
together with some simple (but tedious) algebra.
\square

Regarding the largest sample correlation cofficients, we can show the following result.

Proposition 4.2
.

Suppose
\log p=o(n^{1/5})
,
\limsup\rho_{n}<1
and recall

from (
4.1
). Define

M^{*}_{n}:=\sqrt{n}M_{n}-\rho\sqrt{n}-(1-\rho)\sqrt{1+2\rho+\frac{\kappa-5}{2}\rho^{2}}\cdot\left(2\sqrt{\log p}-\frac{\log\log p}{4\sqrt{\log p}}\right).

Then the following statements hold.

(i)

If
\rho\sqrt{\log p}\to 0
, we have

2\sqrt{\log p}\,M_{n}^{*}\;\;\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\to}}\;G_{1}\,,

where
G_{1}
has the Gumbel law with CDF
x\mapsto\exp\!\left(-\tfrac{1}{4\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-x}\right)
.

(ii)

If
\rho\sqrt{\log p}\to\lambda\in(0,\infty)
, we have

\tfrac{1}{\rho}M_{n}^{*}\;\;\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\to}}\;\tfrac{1}{2\lambda}G_{1}+N(0,\kappa-1),

where
G_{1}
and
N(0,\kappa-1)
are independent.

(iii)

If
\rho\sqrt{\log p}\to\infty
, we have

\tfrac{1}{\rho(1-\rho)}M_{n}^{*}\;\;\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\to}}\;N(0,\kappa-1).

The condition
\limsup\rho_{n}<1
imposed in Proposition
4.2
is the same as in
12
, which is to prevent the sample correlation coefficients from collapsing to
1
too quickly. It is easy to check that for
\kappa=3
, we recover the results in
12
.

Proof of Proposition
4.2
.
By a minor modification of Step 1 in the proof of Theorem 1 in
21
, we have

\displaystyle\max_{1\leq i<j\leq p}\sqrt{n}\hat{\rho}_{ij}-\rho\sqrt{n}=\max_{1\leq i<j\leq p}\left\{\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{k=1}^{n}x_{ki}x_{kj}-\frac{\rho}{2}\left(x_{ki}^{2}+x_{kj}^{2}\right)\right\}+O_{\mathbb{P}}\left(\frac{\log p}{\sqrt{n}}\right).

(4.6)

For
k=1,\ldots,n
, put

T^{(k)}_{ij}:=x_{ki}x_{kj}-\frac{\rho}{2}\left(x_{ki}^{2}+x_{kj}^{2}\right).

Some moment calculations using (
4.2
) give

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left(T_{ij}^{(1)}T_{kl}^{(1)}\right)=\begin{cases}(1-\rho)^{2}\left(1+2\rho+\frac{\rho^{2}}{2}(3\kappa-7)\right),&\bigm|\left\{i,j\right\}\cap\left\{k,l\right\}\big|=2;\\
(1-\rho)^{2}\bigg[\rho+\left(\frac{5\kappa-9}{4}\right)\rho^{2}\bigg],&\bigm|\left\{i,j\right\}\cap\left\{k,l\right\}\big|=1;\\
(1-\rho)^{2}(\kappa-1)\rho^{2},&\bigm|\left\{i,j\right\}\cap\left\{k,l\right\}\big|=0.\end{cases}

By the high-dimensional central limit theorem (Theorem 2.1 in
23
), we have for
\log p=o(n^{1/5})

\displaystyle\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}}\left|\mathbb{P}\left(\max_{1\leq i<j\leq p}\frac{\sum_{k=1}^{n}T^{(k)}_{ij}}{(1-\rho)\sqrt{n(1+2\rho+\frac{\rho^{2}}{2}(3\kappa-7)}}\leq t\right)-\mathbb{P}\left(\max_{1\leq i<j\leq p}Z_{ij}\leq t\right)\right|\to 0\,,

(4.7)

where
\left\{Z_{ij};1\leq i<j\leq p\right\}
is the centered Gaussian field with correlation structure

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(Z_{ij}Z_{kl})=\begin{cases}\frac{(\kappa-1)\rho^{2}}{1+2\rho+\frac{\rho^{2}}{2}(3\kappa-7)},&\big|\left\{i,j\right\}\cap\left\{k,l\right\}\big|=0;\\
\frac{\rho+\left(\frac{5\kappa-9}{4}\right)\rho^{2}}{1+2\rho+\frac{\rho^{2}}{2}(3\kappa-7)},&\bigm|\left\{i,j\right\}\cap\left\{k,l\right\}\big|=1;\\

Thus, we further have the decomposition

\displaystyle\max_{1\leq i<j\leq p}Z_{ij}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\sqrt{\rho_{1}}G+\sqrt{1-\rho_{1}}\cdot\max_{1\leq i<j\leq p}G_{ij}\,,

(4.8)

where
\rho_{1}:=\rho^{2}(\kappa-1)(1+2\rho+(\rho^{2}/2)(3\kappa-7))^{-1}
,

is a standard normal distribution independent from

’s and the Gaussian field
\left\{G_{ij};1\leq i<j\leq p\right\}
is as in (

) with correlation parameter

r=\frac{\rho+\frac{\kappa-5}{4}\rho^{2}}{1+2\rho+\frac{\kappa-5}{2}\rho^{2}}.

Using the assumption on
\rho
and the fact that
\kappa\geq 1
, we deduce that

1-2r=\frac{1}{1+2\rho+\frac{\kappa-5}{2}\rho^{2}}

is strictly greater than zero. Consequently,

.
The proof is completed by combining (
4.6
), (
4.7
), (
4.8
) and applying Theorem
2.1
together with some simple (but tedious) algebra.
\square

4.2
The
n
-dependent marginals case

Recall
R_{n}
in (
4.1
). We now show that if
\xi=\xi_{n}
is allowed to depend on
n
, the limiting distribution of
R_{n}
can change drastically, depending on the magnitude of
\kappa_{n}:=\mathbb{E}[\xi_{n}^{4}]
. We will investigate the behavior of
R_{n}
under two scenarios:
\xi_{n}
behaves like a heavy-tailed distribution with masses shifted toward infinity, and when
\xi_{n}
behaves like a Rademacher distribution with masses shifted toward two points. In both scenarios, the behaviors of
R_{n}
are quite different than in Section
4.1
.

Assume (
C1
)–(
C3
). We will consider the following two regimes for
\kappa_{n}
:

(i)

\kappa_{n}/(\log p)\to\lambda_{1}\in(0,\infty)
and
\rho_{n}\log p\to\lambda_{2}{\in(0,\infty)}
as
n\to\infty
.

(ii)

(\kappa_{n}-1)(\log p)^{2}\to\lambda_{1}\in(0,\infty)
and
(1-\rho_{n})\log p\to\lambda_{2}\in(0,\infty)
.

An example of a distribution that satisfies conditions (
C1
)–(
C3
) and (i) is given in (
3.4
) with

e_{n}=\Omega(\sqrt{\log p})
.

An example of a distribution that satisfies conditions (
C1
)–(
C3
) and (ii) is the distribution

\displaystyle\xi_{n}=\begin{cases}\sqrt{\frac{(\log p)^{2}+\lambda_{1}}{(\log p)^{2}}},\ &\text{with probability}\ \frac{(\log p)^{2}}{2(\lambda_{1}+(\log p)^{2})};\\
0,\ &\text{with probability}\ \frac{\lambda}{\lambda+(\log p)^{2}};\\
-\sqrt{\frac{(\log p)^{2}+\lambda_{1}}{(\log p)^{2}}},\ &\text{with probability}\ \frac{(\log p)^{2}}{2(\lambda_{1}+(\log p)^{2})}.\end{cases}

Indeed, one can check that

\mathbb{E}\xi_{n}=0,\quad\ \mathbb{E}\xi_{n}^{2}=1,\quad\ \mathbb{E}\xi_{n}^{4}=1+\frac{\lambda_{1}}{(\log p)^{2}},\quad\ \mathbb{E}\xi_{n}^{14}=\left(1+\frac{\lambda_{1}}{(\log p)^{2}}\right)^{6}\,.

More generally, condition (ii) implies that
\xi_{n}
converges in distribution to a Rademacher variable. To see this, note that (ii) requires
\kappa_{n}\to 1
which in turn implies that
\mathbb{P}(|\xi_{n}|\leq 1)\to 1
. Since
\xi_{n}
is mean zero and unit variance, one concludes that
\xi_{n}
converges to Rademacher distribution.

The following result describes the limiting distribution of
R_{n}
in the
n
-dependent case.

Proposition 4.3
.

Assume (
C1
)–(
C3
).

(i)

In regime (i),

\displaystyle 2\sqrt{\log p}\left[\frac{R_{n}}{\sqrt{n}}-\rho\sqrt{n}-\sqrt{1-\rho^{2}}\left(2\sqrt{\log p}-\frac{\log\log p}{4\sqrt{\log p}}\right)\right]\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\rm d}}{{\rightarrow}}N(0,2\lambda_{1}\lambda_{2}^{2})+G_{1},

(4.9)

where
G_{1}
is the Gumbel law in Proposition
4.1
, independent of the normal term.

(ii)

In regime (ii),

\displaystyle\log p\!\left[\frac{R_{n}}{\sqrt{n}}-\rho\sqrt{n}-\sqrt{1-\rho^{2}}\!\left(2\sqrt{\log p}-\frac{\log\log p+\log(4\pi)}{2\sqrt{\log p}}\right)\right]

(4.10)

converges in distribution to

N(0,\lambda_{1})+\sqrt{\lambda_{2}}\,\sup_{i<j}\left\{\frac{\eta_{i}+\eta_{j}}{\sqrt{2}}+\sqrt{\lambda_{2}}\,Z_{ij}-\frac{\lambda_{2}}{2}\right\},

where
\eta_{i}
are as in (
2.2
),
Z_{ij}\sim N(0,1)
are i.i.d., and the normal component is independent of the rest.

Proof of Proposition
4.3
.
One can check that (
4.4
) still goes through under conditions (
C1
)–(
C3
).
The desired result then follows from applying Theorem
2.2
to (
4.5
). We omit details for brevity.
\square

Let us compare regimes (i) and (ii) with the results of
12
.
In regime (i),
12
showed that the statistic in (
4.9
) converges to a standard Gumbel distribution when
\rho\sqrt{\log p}\to 0
.
In our setting, although
\rho\sqrt{\log p}=O\!\left((\log p)^{-1/2}\right)\to 0
, the diverging fourth moment contributes an additional Gaussian component to the limiting distribution.

In regime (ii), the behavior is more intricate. The data points
\bm{x}_{i}
have strongly dependent coordinates with marginals converging to the Rademacher distribution.
Heuristically, the Gaussian contribution in (
4.5
) persists, while the Gumbel component breaks down in the second term of (
4.5
). Also, the scaling of the statistic in (
4.10
) is of order
\Omega\big((\log p)^{-1}\big)
, which is nonstandard. This scaling can be regarded as the asymptotic variance. In contrast, it is known (see, e.g.,
10
) that for a collection
X_{1},\dots,X_{N}
of normal distributions with unit variance and arbitrary covariance,

\operatorname{Var}\Big(\max_{1\leq i\leq N}X_{i}\Big)\;\geq\;\frac{C}{\big(1+\mathbb{E}\max_{1\leq i\leq N}X_{i}\big)^{2}},

for some universal constant
C
.

Thus, in all existing results, the asymptotic variance of the largest element is of order
(\log p)^{-1}
since the maximum of
O(p^{2})
standard normal distributions is of size
\Theta(\sqrt{\log p})
. By contrast, in (
4.10
) the asymptotic variance is only of order
\Theta\big((\log p)^{-2}\big)
, which is caused by the degeneracy effect of
\kappa_{n}
.

Application III: FWER control in mutiple testing

As a direct consequence of Theorem
2.1
and Slepian’s lemma, we can see that the test in (

. Thus, we can pick the threshold

also reveals that if

is allowed to be

would remain valid as long as

We now provide two examples of some popular situations where this type of mutiple testing problem shows up in statistical analysis. The first example is the classical Analysis of Variance (ANOVA), and the second one is a network model known as social relation model (see
18
for more details).

Example 1: Two-way ANOVA with random effects.
Analysis of Variance is probably the oldest statistical technique for mean comparison. We shall consider the two-way ANOVA with random effects, which is arguably the most common model used in experimental design. In this context, one considers a collection of observations of random variables

Example 2: Social relation models.
This is the model taken from

and
\eta_{i}
’s are as in (
2.2
).

The proof of Theorem
2.2
is based on the following result

Proposition 7.1
.

For any
c>0
we have

\sup_{1\leq i<j\leq n}\left\{\frac{Z_{(i)}+Z_{(j)}}{\sqrt{2}}+cY_{ij}\right\}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\to}}\sup_{i<j}\left\{\frac{\eta_{i}+\eta_{j}}{\sqrt{2}}+c\mathcal{Y}_{ij}\right\}

where
\left\{\eta_{i}\right\}_{i=1}^{\infty}
are as in (
2.2
) and
\mathcal{Y}_{ij}
’s are i.i.d. standard normals independent from
\eta_{i}
’s.

It is worth noting that Proposition
7.1
does
not
follow directly from a continuous mapping argument. This is because the map

f:\sum_{i}\delta_{x_{i}}\mapsto\sum_{i<j}\delta_{x_{i}+x_{j}}

is not almost surely continuous at
N
. In fact, the map
f
is almost surely discontinuous at
N
. Interestingly, the point process
f(N)
is locally finite (and thus, is well-defined) but has infinite intensity measure on every compact intervals. To see this, take
[a,b]\subset\mathbb{R}
and observe that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}f(N)[a,b]=\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\mathbf{1}_{\left\{x+y\in[a,b]\right\}}\cdot e^{-x-y}dxdy

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{-x}\int_{a-x}^{b-x}e^{-y}dydx

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{-x}\left(e^{x-a}-e^{x-b}\right)dx=\infty.

However, the forms of
\eta_{i}
’s in (
2.2
) imply that
f(N)
is locally finite almost surely. An explicit example showing that
f
is not continuous at any realization of
N
can be constructed as follows: take
M>0
large enough and define

\mathcal{N}_{n}:=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{\eta_{i}}+\delta_{-\eta_{n}+M}.

It is easy to see that
\mathcal{N}_{n}
converges to
N
in the vague topology but the image
f(\mathcal{N}_{n})
contains
\delta_{M}
for all
n\geq 1
. Therefore,
f(\mathcal{N}_{n})
does not converge to
f(N)
if
M>\eta_{1}+\eta_{2}
. This construction shows that
f
is not continuous at any point configuration that forms a sequence decreasing to negative infinity.

Proof of Proposition
7.1
.
For
x\in\mathbb{R}
, define

\displaystyle F_{n}(x)

\displaystyle:=\mathbb{P}\left(\forall 1\leq i<j\leq n:\frac{Z_{(i)}+Z_{(j)}}{\sqrt{2}}+cY_{ij}\leq x\right),

\displaystyle F(x)

\displaystyle:=\mathbb{P}\left(\forall 1\leq i<j:\frac{\eta_{i}+\eta_{j}}{\sqrt{2}}+c\mathcal{Y}_{ij}\leq x\right).

It suffices to prove that

\displaystyle\limsup_{n\to\infty}F_{n}(x)\leq F(x)

(7.2)

and

\displaystyle\liminf_{n\to\infty}F_{n}(x)\geq F(x).

(7.3)

Proof of (
7.2
).
Fix a number
1\leq K\leq n
and notice that

\displaystyle F_{n}(x)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\left(\forall 1\leq i<j\leq K:\frac{Z_{(i)}+Z_{(j)}}{\sqrt{2}}+cY_{ij}\leq x\right).

By (
7.1
) and the continuous mapping theorem, we have

\left(Z_{(i)}+Z_{(j)}\right)_{1\leq i<j\leq K}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\to}}\left(\eta_{i}+\eta_{j}\right)_{1\leq i<j\leq K}.

Therefore,

\displaystyle\limsup_{n\to\infty}F_{n}(x)

\displaystyle\leq\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\left(\forall 1\leq i<j\leq K:\frac{Z_{(i)}+Z_{(j)}}{\sqrt{2}}+cY_{ij}\leq x\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left(\forall 1\leq i<j\leq K:\frac{\eta_{i}+\eta_{j}}{\sqrt{2}}+c\mathcal{Y}_{ij}\leq x\right)

and since this is true for all
K
, we get (
7.2
) by letting
K\to\infty
.

Proof of (
7.3
).
Fix
\varepsilon>0
, by Lemma
9.2
, there exists
C_{\varepsilon}>0
which depends only on
\varepsilon
such that

\liminf_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\left(\operatornamewithlimits{argmax}_{i<j}\left(\frac{Z_{(i)}+Z_{(j)}}{\sqrt{2}}+cY_{ij}\right)\in\left\{1,2,\dots,K\right\}^{2}\right)\geq 1-\varepsilon

for all
K\geq C_{\varepsilon}
.
Therefore, for any
K\geq C_{\varepsilon}
, we have

\displaystyle\liminf_{n\to\infty}F_{n}(x)

\displaystyle\geq\liminf_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\left(\forall 1\leq i<j\leq K:\frac{Z_{(i)}+Z_{(j)}}{\sqrt{2}}+cY_{ij}\leq x\right)-\varepsilon

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left(\forall 1\leq i<j\leq K:\frac{\eta_{i}+\eta_{j}}{\sqrt{2}}+c\mathcal{Y}_{ij}\leq x\right)-\varepsilon.

Since this is true for any
K\geq C_{\varepsilon}
, we get (
7.3
) by first letting
K\to\infty
and then letting
\varepsilon\to 0
. This completes the proof of Proposition
7.1
.
\square

We are now ready to prove Theorem
2.2
. Without loss of generality, we may assume that

\frac{(1-2r)\log n}{2r}=\lambda\in(0,\infty).

(7.4)

In fact, given a sequence of Gaussian fields
\mathcal{G}_{n}
as in (
1.1
), with corresponding parameters
r_{n}
such that
(1-2r_{n})\log n\to\lambda
, one can obtain two-sided bounds on
\mathcal{G}_{n}
by considering the Gaussian fields
\overline{\mathcal{G}}_{n}
and
\underline{\mathcal{G}}_{n}
with parameters
\overline{r}
and
\underline{r}
, respectively, chosen so that

\displaystyle(1-2\overline{r})\log n

\displaystyle=\lambda-\varepsilon,

\displaystyle(1-2\underline{r})\log n

\displaystyle=\lambda+\varepsilon,

for some
\varepsilon\in(0,c)
.

This further implies that for all
n
large enough,
\underline{r}\leq r\leq\overline{r}
. Thus, Slepian’s lemma gives

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(c_{n}\left(\max_{1\leq i<j\leq n}G_{ij}-\sqrt{2}d_{n}\right)\leq x\right)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\left(c_{n}\left(\max_{1\leq i<j\leq n}\overline{G}_{ij}-\sqrt{2}d_{n}\right)\leq x\right),

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(c_{n}\left(\max_{1\leq i<j\leq n}G_{ij}-\sqrt{2}d_{n}\right)\leq x\right)

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{P}\left(c_{n}\left(\max_{1\leq i<j\leq n}\underline{G}_{ij}-\sqrt{2}d_{n}\right)\leq x\right).

for all
x\in\mathbb{R}
. We get the desired conclusion by first letting
n\to\infty
and then letting
\varepsilon\to 0
.

Now suppose (
7.4
). By Proposition
7.1
, we have

\displaystyle c_{n}\left(\max_{1\leq i<j\leq n}\frac{G_{ij}}{\sqrt{2r}}-\sqrt{2}d_{n}\right)

\displaystyle=\max_{1\leq i<j\leq n}\left\{\frac{Z_{i}+Z_{j}}{\sqrt{2}}+\sqrt{2\lambda}Y_{ij}\right\}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\max_{1\leq i<j\leq n}\left\{\frac{Z_{(i)}+Z_{(j)}}{\sqrt{2}}+\sqrt{2\lambda}Y_{ij}\right\}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\to}}\sup_{i<j}\left(\frac{\eta_{i}+\eta_{j}}{\sqrt{2}}+\sqrt{2\lambda}\cdot Z_{ij}\right).

Note that the second line in the display above follows from the exchangeability of
\left\{Y_{ij};1\leq i<j\leq n\right\}
: if
\pi
is a permutation of
[n]
such that
Z_{(i)}=Z_{\pi(i)}
, then
\pi
is uniformly distributed on the permutation group, independent from
Y_{ij}
’s, and

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\max_{1\leq i<j\leq n}\left\{\frac{Z_{i}+Z_{j}}{\sqrt{2}}+\sqrt{2\lambda}Y_{ij}\right\}\leq x\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\max_{1\leq i<j\leq n}\left\{\frac{Z_{(i)}+Z_{(j)}}{\sqrt{2}}+\sqrt{2\lambda}Y_{\pi(i)\pi(j)}\right\}\leq x\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbb{P}\left(\forall 1\leq i<j\leq n:\sqrt{2\lambda}\cdot Y_{\pi(i)\pi(j)}\leq x-\frac{Z_{(i)}+Z_{(j)}}{\sqrt{2}}\Big|Z_{1},\dots,Z_{n}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbb{P}\left(\forall 1\leq i<j\leq n:\sqrt{2\lambda}\cdot Y_{ij}\leq x-\frac{Z_{(i)}+Z_{(j)}}{\sqrt{2}}\Big|Z_{1},\dots,Z_{n}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\max_{1\leq i<j\leq n}\left\{\frac{Z_{(i)}+Z_{(j)}}{\sqrt{2}}+\sqrt{2\lambda}Y_{ij}\right\}\leq x\right).

We now complete the proof of Theorem
2.2
by noting that

\displaystyle c_{n}\left(\max_{1\leq i<j\leq n}\frac{G_{ij}}{\sqrt{2r}}-\sqrt{2}d_{n}\right)

\displaystyle=(1+o(1))\cdot c_{n}\left(\max_{1\leq i<j\leq n}G_{ij}-\sqrt{2r}\cdot\sqrt{2}d_{n}\right)

and

c_{n}\left(1-\sqrt{2r}\right)\sqrt{2}d_{n}=\frac{(1-2r)2\log(n)\cdot(1+o(1)))}{1+\sqrt{2r}}\to\lambda.

\square

2.2

\overline{\mathcal{G}}_{n}
and
\underline{\mathcal{G}}_{n}
of the form (
1.1

9
Technical results

Some useful asymptotic expansions will be collected in this Section.

Lemma 9.1
.

Define the square matrix
\bm{A}
of size
p(p-1)/2
on
I=\left\{(i,j):1\leq i<j\leq p\right\}
as

\bm{A}_{\bm{e},\bm{f}}=\begin{cases}0,&\big|\left\{i,j\right\}\cap\left\{k,l\right\}\big|=0;\\
b\in[0,1/2),&\bigm|\left\{i,j\right\}\cap\left\{k,l\right\}\big|=1;\\
1,&\bigm|\left\{i,j\right\}\cap\left\{k,l\right\}\big|=2;\end{cases}

where
\bm{e}=(i,j)
and
\bm{f}=(k,l)
. Then, for all
p\geq 4
, the smallest eigenvalue of
\bm{A}
is
\lambda_{min}\left(\bm{A}\right)=1-2b
.

Proof of Lemma
9.1
.

We do not have to worry about the particular choice of the ordering on
I
since
\bm{A}
is the covariance matrix of a random vector and permuting the components of a random vector does not change the smallest eigenvalue of its covariance matrix.

Let
d=p(p-1)/2=|I|
. Define the linearly independent vectors
\bm{s}^{(v)}:=\big(s^{(v)}_{ij}\big)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
1\leq v\leq p
through

s^{(v)}_{ij}=\bm{1}_{\left\{v\in\left\{i,j\right\}\right\}},~1\leq i<j\leq p.

The linear independence can be seen by observing that for all real numbers
\alpha_{i}
’s:

\left[\sum_{v=1}^{p}\alpha_{v}\bm{s}^{(v)}\right]_{(i,j)}=\alpha_{i}+\alpha_{j}.

From the display above, we also have

\displaystyle\sum_{v=1}^{p}\bm{s}^{(v)}=2\cdot\bm{1}_{d}.

(9.1)

Decompose
\mathbb{R}^{d}
into the direct sum of orthogonal subspaces as

\mathbb{R}^{d}=\mbox{span}\left\{\bm{1}_{d}\right\}\oplus S_{1}\oplus S_{2}
,
where

\displaystyle S_{1}

\displaystyle:=\left\{\sum_{v=1}^{p}\alpha_{v}\bm{s}^{(v)}:\sum_{v=1}^{p}\alpha_{v}=0\right\}\quad\text{ and }\quad S_{2}:=\Big[\mbox{span}\left\{\bm{1}_{d}\right\}\oplus S_{1}\Big]^{\perp}.

The orthogonality between the first two subspaces is due to (
9.1
). The subspace
S_{2}
can be characterized equivalently as

\displaystyle S_{2}=\Big[\mbox{span}\left\{\bm{1}_{d}\right\}\oplus S_{1}\Big]^{\perp}

\displaystyle=\mbox{span}\left\{\bm{s}^{(v)};1\leq v\leq p\right\}^{\perp}

\displaystyle=\left\{\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\ \sum_{u:(u,v)\ \text{or}\ (v,u)\ \text{in}\ I}x_{(u,v)}=0\ \text{for all $v\in[p]$}\right\}.

We will show below that these are precisely the eigenspaces of
\bm{A}
. On
\mbox{span}\left\{\bm{1}_{d}\right\}
, we have

\left[\bm{A}\bm{1}_{d}\right]_{(i,j)}=1+2b(p-2)

for all
(i,j)\in I
. This is because for each
(i,j)\in I
, there are
2p-2
pairs sharing a vertex with it. Therefore, the
\lambda_{1}=1+2b(p-2)
is the eigenvalue corresponding to this eigenspace.

On
S_{1}
, we have for
\bm{x}=\sum_{v=1}^{p}\alpha_{v}\bm{s}^{(v)}
that

[\bm{A}\bm{x}]_{(i,j)}=\sum_{v=1}^{p}\alpha_{v}[\bm{A}\bm{s}^{(v)}]_{(i,j)}=\begin{cases}1+b(p-2),\ &\text{if}\ v\in\left\{i,j\right\};\\
2b,&\text{if}\ v\notin\left\{i,j\right\}.\end{cases}

Thus,

\bm{A}\bm{x}=\sum_{v=1}^{p}\alpha_{v}\bm{A}\bm{s}^{(v)}=\sum_{v=1}^{p}\alpha_{v}\left(2b\cdot\bm{1}_{d}+(1+b(p-4)\bm{s}^{(v)})\right)=\left[1+b(p-4)\right]\cdot\bm{x}.

Consequently,
\lambda_{2}=1+b(p-4)
is the eigenvalue on the subspace
S_{1}
.

Finally, consider
S_{2}
. For
\bm{x}\in S_{2}
, we have

\displaystyle[\bm{A}\bm{x}]_{(i,j)}=x_{(i,j)}+\sum_{u\neq i,j}x_{(i,u)}+\sum_{u\neq i,j}x_{(j,u)}=1-bx_{(i,j)}-bx_{(i,j)}=(1-2b)x_{(i,j)}.

Thus
\lambda_{3}=1-2b
is the eigenvalue on the subspace
S_{2}
.

Among the three eigenvalues
\lambda_{1}=1+2b(p-2)
,
\lambda_{2}=1+b(p-4)
,
\lambda_{3}=1-2b
, the smallest one is
\lambda_{3}
if
p\geq 4
. The proof is complete.
\square

Lemma 9.2
.

With the same notation as in Proposition
7.1
, for all
\varepsilon>0
there exists
C_{\varepsilon}>0
such that

\displaystyle\liminf_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\!\left(\operatornamewithlimits{argmax}_{(i,j):i<j}\left\{\frac{Z_{(i)}+Z_{(j)}}{\sqrt{2}}+cY_{ij}\right\}\in[K]\times[K]\right)\geq 1-\varepsilon

(9.2)

for all
K\geq C_{\varepsilon}
. Here,
[K]=\left\{1,2,\dots,K\right\}
.

Proof of Lemma
9.2
.

Let
K\geq 1
and define

\displaystyle M_{in}(K,n)

\displaystyle:=\max_{1\leq i<j\leq K}\left\{\frac{Z_{(i)}+Z_{(j)}}{\sqrt{2}}+cY_{ij}\right\}\quad\text{ and }\quad M_{out}(K,n):=\max_{i\vee j\geq K}\left\{\frac{Z_{(i)}+Z_{(j)}}{\sqrt{2}}+cY_{ij}\right\}.

It suffices to show that

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\left(M_{out}(K,n)\leq M_{in}(K,n)\right)\geq 1-\varepsilon

for all
K
sufficiently large.

Let us first estimate the size of
Z_{(i)}
for large
i
. Put
b_{k}:=-(\log k)/2
. Observe that for all
k\leq n
, using Mill’s ratio we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[N_{n}\ (b_{k},\infty)\right]=n\mathbb{P}\left(Z_{1}\geq b_{k}\right)

\displaystyle=n\mathbb{P}\left(N(0,1)\geq\frac{b_{k}}{c_{n}}+d_{n}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{10n}{\sqrt{\log n}}\exp\left(-\frac{\left(\frac{b_{k}}{c_{n}}+d_{n}\right)^{2}}{2}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{10n}{\sqrt{\log n}}\exp\left[b_{k}\left(\frac{1}{2}+o(1)\right)-\frac{d_{n}^{2}}{2}-\frac{b_{n}^{2}}{2c_{n}^{2}}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{10n}{\sqrt{\log n}}\exp\left[b_{k}\left(\frac{1}{2}+o(1)\right)-\log n+\frac{\log\log n}{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq k^{3/4}

(9.3)

uniformly for all
n
and
k
sufficiently large, say
M_{0}
(a universal constant).

Recall that for a binomial distribution
X
of size
n
and mean
\mu
, Chernoff’s bound yields

\mathbb{P}\left(X\geq k\right)\leq\exp\left[-nD_{KL}\left(\frac{k}{n},\frac{\mu}{n}\right)\right]

for all
k\geq\mu
, where the
KL
-divergence is given by

D_{KL}(p,q):=p\log\left(\frac{p}{q}\right)+(1-p)\log\left(\frac{1-p}{1-q}\right).

Note that
N_{n}\left(b_{k},\infty\right)
has a binomial distribution with mean of order
O(k^{3/4})
. Consequently, an application of Chernoff’s bound gives

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(Z_{(k)}\geq b_{k}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left(N_{n}(b_{k},\infty)\geq k\right)\leq\exp\left(-\frac{k}{4}\log k+2k\right)

and therefore,

\mathbb{P}\left(\exists k\geq K:Z_{(k)}\geq b_{k}\right)\leq\sum_{k\geq K}\exp\left(-\frac{k}{4}\log k+2k\right)
.
Thus, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\forall k\geq K_{1}(\varepsilon):Z_{(k)}\leq b_{k}\right)\geq 1-\varepsilon/10,

(9.4)

where

\displaystyle K_{1}(\varepsilon):=\min\left\{K\geq 1:\sum_{k\geq K}\exp\left(-\frac{k}{4}\log k+2k\right)\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{10}\right\}.

(9.5)

Step 1: A high-probability lower bound for
M_{in}(K,n)
.

By (
7.1
), we have

\displaystyle\limsup_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\left(M_{in}(K,n)\geq x\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left(\max_{1\leq i<j\leq K}\left\{\frac{\eta_{i}+\eta_{j}}{\sqrt{2}}+c\mathcal{Y}_{ij}\right\}\geq x\right)\geq\mathbb{P}\left(\frac{\eta_{1}+\eta_{2}}{\sqrt{2}}+c\mathcal{Y}_{12}\geq x\right)

for all
x\in\mathbb{R}
and
K\geq 1
.
Choose
T_{\varepsilon}\in\mathbb{R}
such that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\frac{\eta_{1}+\eta_{2}}{\sqrt{2}}+c\mathcal{Y}_{12}\geq T_{\varepsilon}\right)=1-\frac{\varepsilon}{20}.

(9.6)

With this choice of
T_{\varepsilon}
, we have

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\left(M_{in}(K,n)\geq T_{\varepsilon}\right)\geq 1-\frac{\varepsilon}{10}
.

Step 2: A high-probability upper bound for
M_{out}(K,n)
.
Note that

M_{out}(K,n)=\max\left\{M_{1n},M_{2n}\right\}
,
where

\displaystyle M_{1n}

\displaystyle:=\max_{K+1\leq i<j\leq n}\left\{\frac{Z_{(i)}+Z_{(j)}}{\sqrt{2}}+cY_{ij}\right\}\quad\text{ and }\quad M_{2n}:=\max_{K+1\leq j\leq n;1\leq i\leq K}\left\{\frac{Z_{(i)}+Z_{(j)}}{\sqrt{2}}+cY_{ij}\right\}.

We define
W_{j}:=\max_{1\leq i\leq K}Z_{ij}

and recall
T_{\varepsilon}
in (
9.6
). By virtue of (
9.4
), we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(M_{out}(K,n)\geq T_{\varepsilon}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\left(M_{1n}\geq T_{\varepsilon}/2\right)+\mathbb{P}\left(M_{2n}\geq T_{\varepsilon}/2\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{5}+\mathbb{P}\left(\max_{K+1\leq i<j\leq n}\left\{\frac{-\log(i)-\log(j)}{2\sqrt{2}}+cY_{ij}\right\}\geq\frac{T_{\varepsilon}}{2}\right)

\displaystyle+\mathbb{P}\left(\frac{Z_{(1)}}{\sqrt{2}}+\max_{j\geq K+1}\left\{-\frac{\log(j)}{2\sqrt{2}}+cW_{j}\right\}\geq\frac{T_{\varepsilon}}{2}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{5}+\mathbb{P}\left(\max_{K+1\leq i<j\leq n}\left\{\frac{-\log(i)-\log(j)}{2\sqrt{2}}+cY_{ij}\right\}\geq\frac{T}{2}\right)

\displaystyle+\mathbb{P}\left(\frac{Z_{(1)}}{\sqrt{2}}\geq v\right)+\mathbb{P}\left(\max_{j\geq K+1}\left\{-\frac{\log(j)}{2\sqrt{2}}+cW_{j}\right\}\geq\frac{T_{\varepsilon}}{2}-v\right)

for all
v\in\mathbb{R}
.
We proceed by estimating each term on the right-hand side for
K\geq\exp\left\{-T_{\varepsilon}(\sqrt{2}+1)\right\}
. For the first probability, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\max_{K+1\leq i<j\leq n}\left\{\frac{-\log(i)-\log(j)}{2\sqrt{2}}+cY_{ij}\right\}\geq\frac{T_{\varepsilon}}{2}\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{K+1\leq i<j\leq n}\mathbb{P}\left(Y_{ij}\geq\frac{1}{2c}\left(T_{\varepsilon}+\frac{\log(i)+\log(j)}{\sqrt{2}}\right)\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{K+1\leq i<j\leq n}\mathbb{P}\left(Y_{ij}\geq\frac{\log(i)+\log(j)}{4c}\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left(\sum_{i\geq K+1}\exp\left(-\frac{\log^{2}(i)}{32c^{2}}\right)\right)^{2}\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{5}\,,

whenever

\displaystyle K\geq K_{2}(\varepsilon):=\min\left\{K\geq 1:\sum_{i\geq K+1}\exp\left(-\frac{\log^{2}(i)}{32c^{2}}\right)\leq\sqrt{\varepsilon/5}\right\}.

(9.7)

For the second probability, note that for
v=v_{\varepsilon}
such that
\mathbb{P}\left(\eta_{1}\geq\sqrt{2}v_{\varepsilon}\right)=\varepsilon/10
, we have

\displaystyle\limsup_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\left(\frac{Z_{(1)}}{\sqrt{2}}\geq v_{\varepsilon}\right)=\mathbb{P}\left(\eta_{1}\geq\sqrt{2}v_{\varepsilon}\right)=\frac{\varepsilon}{10}<\frac{\varepsilon}{5}

(9.8)

due to (
7.1
).
Finally, we estimate the second probability as

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}

\displaystyle\left(\max_{j\geq K+1}\left\{-\frac{\log(j)}{2\sqrt{2}}+cW_{j}\right\}\geq\frac{T_{\varepsilon}}{2}-v_{\varepsilon}\right)\leq\sum_{j\geq K+1}\mathbb{P}\left(W_{j}\geq\frac{1}{c}\left(\frac{T_{\varepsilon}}{2}-v_{\varepsilon}+\frac{\log(j)}{2\sqrt{2}}\right)\right)

\displaystyle\leq K\sum_{j\geq K+1}\mathbb{P}\left(N(0,1)\geq\frac{1}{c}\left(\frac{T_{\varepsilon}}{2}-v_{\varepsilon}+\frac{\log(j)}{2\sqrt{2}}\right)\right)\leq K\sum_{j\geq K+1}\mathbb{P}\left(N(0,1)\geq\frac{\log(j)}{4c}\right)

for all
K\geq\exp\left(4(\sqrt{2}+1)\left(v_{\varepsilon}-\frac{T_{\varepsilon}}{2}\right)\right)
.
The right-hand side above is further bounded by

K\sum_{j\geq K+1}\exp\left(-\frac{\log^{2}(j)}{32c^{2}}\right)\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{5}

whenever

\displaystyle K\geq K_{3}(\varepsilon):=\min\left\{K\geq 1:K\sum_{j\geq K+1}\exp\left(-\frac{\log^{2}(j)}{32c^{2}}\right)\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{5}\right\}.

(9.9)

Step 3: Conclusion.

With
T_{\varepsilon}
chosen in (
9.6
), we have

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\left(M_{in}(K,n)\geq T_{\varepsilon}\right)\geq 1-\frac{\varepsilon}{10}.

for all
K\geq 1
.

Moreover, from Step 2, we know that
\mathbb{P}\left(M_{out}(K,n)\geq T_{\varepsilon}\right)\leq 4\varepsilon/5
whenever

\displaystyle K\geq K_{\varepsilon}:=\max\left\{K_{1}\left(\varepsilon\right),M_{0},K_{2}(\varepsilon),K_{3}(\varepsilon),\exp\left\{-T_{\varepsilon}(\sqrt{2}+1)\right\},\exp\left\{4(\sqrt{2}+1)\left(v_{\varepsilon}-\frac{T_{\varepsilon}}{2}\right)\right\}\right\}\,,

(9.10)

where
M_{0}
is the universal constant such that (
9.3
) holds and
K_{1}(\varepsilon),K_{2}(\varepsilon),K_{3}(\varepsilon),T_{\varepsilon},v_{\varepsilon}
are defined in (
9.5
), (

), (

), (

), (

We conclude that

\displaystyle\liminf_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\left(M_{in}(K,n)\geq M_{out}(K,n)\right)\geq 1-\frac{\varepsilon}{10}-\frac{4\varepsilon}{5}>1-\varepsilon.

Note that the proof of Lemma
9.2
above actually implies that (
9.2
) holds not just at one value of
c
, but also uniformly for all
c
in a compact interval. The next lemma states that the limiting distribution in Theorem
2.2
is non-degenerate.

Lemma 9.3
.

Recall
\eta_{i}
’s in (
2.2
). For
c\geq 0
, define

Y_{c}:=\sup_{i<j}\left\{\frac{\eta_{i}+\eta_{j}}{\sqrt{2}}+cZ_{ij}\right\}

where
Z_{ij}
’s are standard normal random variables independent of
\eta_{i}
’s. Then,

(i)

Y_{c}<\infty
almost surely for all
c\geq 0
.

(ii)

Y_{c_{n}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\to}}Y_{c}
if
c_{n}\to c
.

Proof of Lemma
9.3
.
Argue similarly to the proof of Lemma
9.2
to deduce that for all
\varepsilon,A>0
, there exists
C_{\varepsilon,A}>0
such that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\!\left(\operatornamewithlimits{argmax}_{\left\{(i,j):i<j\right\}}\left\{\frac{\eta_{i}+\eta_{j}}{\sqrt{2}}+cZ_{ij}\right\}\in[K]\times[K]\right)\geq 1-\varepsilon

(9.11)

for all
K\geq C_{\varepsilon,A}
and
c\leq A
.

We get (i) from the above by letting
\varepsilon\to 0
. To prove (ii), note that

Y_{c_{n}}^{(M)}:=\sup_{1\leq i<j\leq M}\left\{\frac{\eta_{i}+\eta_{j}}{\sqrt{2}}+c_{n}Z_{ij}\right\}

almost surely increases to
Y_{c_{n}}
as
M\to\infty
. Thus, for every
x\in\mathbb{R}
and
M\geq 1
,

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\left(Y_{c_{n}}\leq x\right)\leq\limsup_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\left(Y^{(M)}_{c_{n}}\leq x\right)=\mathbb{P}\left(Y^{(M)}_{c}\leq x\right),

where the last equality follows from the continuous mapping therem. By letting
M\to\infty
, we deduce that

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\left(Y_{c_{n}}\leq x\right)\leq\mathbb{P}\left(Y_{c}\leq x\right)
.
To prove the other direction, use (
9.11
) with
A=\sup_{n\geq 1}c_{n}
, to obtain that for all
\varepsilon>0
and
M
large enough,

\liminf_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\left(Y_{c_{n}}\leq x\right)\geq\liminf_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\left(Y^{M}_{c_{n}}\leq x\right)-\varepsilon\geq\liminf_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\left(Y^{M}_{c}\leq x\right)-\varepsilon.

We get (ii) from this by first letting
M\to\infty
and then letting
\varepsilon\to 0
.
\square
