1 Executive Summary

The Tulare County Carbon Storage Project (TCCSP) proposes to construct an industrial-scale
carbon capture and storage (CCS) hub located in Tulare County, California. Regional and site-
specific geological characterization indicates that the subsurface geology of the Southern San
Joaquin Basin is comprised of high-quality storage reservoirs and confining units that can achieve
large-scale CO2 storage of at least 50 million metric tons (Mmt) of CO2 over a 20-year injection
period. TCCSP aims to capture CO2 emissions from Calgren Renewable Fuels and Calgren Dairy
Fuels, amongst other potential emitters. The project has been designed to inject a total of 50 Mmt
of CO2 into saline reservoirs at the site.
Initial phases of the project involved in-depth geological site characterization to confirm the
suitability of the subsurface formations for permanent CO2 storage. Alongside the geological
evaluation, the project team has integrated planning for permitting activities, storage field
development, and site-specific monitoring. The surface infrastructure for TCCSP is designed to
enable CO2 capture from local sources, including Calgren Renewable Fuels and Calgren Dairy
Fuels, and accommodate future contributions from third-party emitters. The surface facilities
include CO2 capture and compression systems, a receiving terminal for transported CO2, and a
pipeline connecting the capture site to the injection site. This phased infrastructure approach
allows for immediate implementation while providing flexibility for future expansion. The CO2
Management Plan ensures reliable handling of CO2 from capture through injection, accounting
for characteristics of the CO2 stream, custody transfer processes, and the technical specifications
of the pipeline and injection system.
Risk assessment is a central element of the development strategy. A comprehensive site-specific
risk analysis has been conducted to evaluate the likelihood of CO2 or brine leakage, as well as
broader technical and non-technical risks. The project team hosted a Risk Assessment Workshop,
involving subject matter experts and regional stakeholders, to identify and mitigate potential
challenges. These findings were used to develop a Risk Assessment Matrix, which continues to
guide decisions related to project safety, feasibility, and long-term integrity.
Financial planning is integrated into all phases of project development. Cost estimates have been
completed for each stage, with contingency planning and potential scale-up factored in. This
supports the transition from pilot operations to commercial injection while maintaining technical
and economic feasibility.
Community engagement is also included in the project framework. A Community Benefits Plan
has been prepared to identify potential impacts and benefits, particularly for historically
disadvantaged communities near the site. Stakeholder input is being collected to inform project
decisions and maintain transparency.

2 Objectives and Project Scope

The primary objective of Tulare Country Carbon Storage Project (TCCSP) is to establish a feasible
commercial-scale CO2 sequestration hub in the California Central Valley. The TCCSP
CarbonSAFE Phase II efforts focused on developing the technical, community, and economic
foundation for a commercial-scale regional geologic storage complex for CO2 captured. TCCSP
has conducted a thorough evaluation of the regional, local, and site-specific geology, legacy well
infrastructure, injection site design, and project planning as part of the CarbonSAFE Phase II
TCCSP. Specially, the work performed under the CarbonSAFE Phase II has significantly furthered
the advancement towards the commerciality of the TCCSP storage complex by delivering on the
following objectives:
 • Objective 1: Acquire site-specific characterization data to validate carbon dioxide
 (CO2) storage estimates of reservoir units and the competency of confining layers in
 the region to prevent CO2 leakage to develop a commercial-scale CO2 project.

 • Objective 2: Assess technical, economic, regulatory, and stakeholder aspects of the
 TCCSP.
To meet the goals of Objective 1, the project team designed and drilled a stratigraphic test well,
TCCSP_OBS-1, and gathered and analyzed the reservoir and confining layer data. Subsurface
data were acquired to evaluate the subsurface storage complex and include two-dimensional
seismic data, legacy oil/gas well logs and core, subsurface well test data, groundwater well data,
and published technical literature. These subsurface data were used to generate a static geologic
model to characterize subsurface reservoirs and confining units and assess the viability of CO2
storage. Using data acquired from the TCCSP_OBS-1 stratigraphic test well, the three-
dimensional static geologic model was updated and then implemented into a dynamic reservoir
simulation to evaluate CO2 plume behavior for 100 years post-injection to confirm plume
stabilization. The computational model updates enhanced the understanding of the subsurface,
including the underground sources of drinking water (USDWs), and produced a more accurate
delineation of the Area of Review (AoR) for the storage complex storage site. The project team
also identified and assessed key project risks and developed corresponding mitigation strategies.
The Risk Assessment effort included relevant data collected from the TCCSP_OBS-1 well. At the
completion of the CarbonSAFE Phase II, the storage complex geology has been thoroughly
defined and found to be adequate for advancement into a commercial CCS site.
To meet the goals of Objective 2, the project team conducted a thorough techno-economic
analysis to develop CO2 source-transport-storage options, identify technical, financial, legal, and
associated logistical challenges in the project area. A thorough evaluation of the environmental
justice conditions in Tulare County was also conducted. The project team engaged with local
residents (including targeted outreach to disadvantaged communities), local businesses, key
community groups, schools, and local environmental justice groups to gather their input on the
development of TCCSP. The public input was used to inform the project’s design and assessment
process.
In parallel with the CarbonSAFE Phase II efforts, TCCSP developed and submitted a UIC Class
VI application with the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) Region 09 (Class VI Pre-
Construction Permit Application No. R9UIC-CA6-FY25-1.1-1.2). The TCCSP EPA UIC Class VI

 9

permit has been deemed administratively complete and is currently addressing the EPA’s
requests for additional information as part of the technical review phase.
Building on the CarbonSAFE II, TCCSP LLC. will continue to work towards establishing their
commercial-scale CO2 sequestration hub through continued advancement of the capture,
transport and storage elements that were not fully matured during Phase II. Table 1 highlights the
path forward for TCCSP in the context of CarbonSAFE Phase III and the work completed during
CarbonSAFE Phase II. Table 2 highlights the path forward for TCCSP Phase IV building on a
completed CarbonSAFE Phase II and III.
The progress of the capture, transport, and storage elements will be tracked by the completion of
key milestones set out by federal, state and local entities. In addition to the potential of a

part of this process feedback from U.S. EPA Region 09, CalGEM, Tulare Resource Management

process. The construction of surface infrastructure, the CO2 transport system, and the injection

and transport systems.

The development of a CO2 storage terminal at, or near, Calgren Renewable Fuels’ ethanol plant

3 Storage Field Development Approach

3.1 Development Strategy

This Development Strategy represents a multi-phase process, starting with the extensive
geological site characterization, followed by available results of the permitting process, the
construction of injection wells plans, monitoring plans, existing infrastructure and plans for
developing additional infrastructure, site specific data and analysis, and finally, the plans for
commercial injection of CO2 and long-term monitoring to ensure safe and secure storage within
the designated geological formations. In addition to technical assessment and site development,
a thorough assessment of project risks is integrated into project development as well as
identification of the various regulatory requirements. Financial planning is another key part of the
overall project development and project risks, along with other important considerations, are
incorporated into the financial assessment. Each element will be addressed and developed to
ensure the long-term success of the project.
The Development Plan describes all elements of the proposed storage field facilities and
establishes the necessary order and timing for the development of all anticipated facilities. A
guiding principle in the development of this plan is to maximize all potential utility of the storage
field. This plan also accounts for the evolving needs related to monitoring, use of pore space, the
changing CO2 delivery rates over time, and the anticipated costs over the life of the project. In
keeping with the CarbonSAFE initiative and the guidance provided for that program TCCSP, LLC.
has modeled their development plan after the Storage Field Development Plan (SFDP) as it is
described by DOE.
The SFDP must provide sufficient information to determine that the selected site is adequate to
meet the expected injection rates and volumes to securely store a minimum of 50 million metric
tons of CO2 within a 30-year period. The elements required for a storage field development plan
as specified from the DOE FOA are as follows; the Storage Field Development Plan should: (1)
explain the strategy for developing the storage field to maximize its storage potential utility; (2)
describe all elements of the proposed storage field facilities and establish a logical order and
timing for the development of all anticipated facilities, accounting for changing needs for
monitoring and use of pore space and changing CO2 delivery rates over time; and (3) present a
cost plan over the proposed life of the project.
The TCCSP CarbonSAFE Phase II efforts have addressed a significant portion of the required
elements for the DOE defined SFDP. This completed work as well as the planned future work is
provided in this section and is termed the TCCSP SFDP. For some parts of the TCCSP SFDP, a
summarized or abbreviated description is provided with references to separate documents that
provide more detail.

3.2 Development Scenario

3.2.1 Proposed Storage Field Elements
The surface facilities for TCCSP will include: (1) CO2 capture and compression systems at
Calgren Renewable Fuels (CRF) and Calgren Dairy Fuels (CDF), (2) a CO2 receiving terminal at
the Calgren site to accept CO2 delivered by rail or truck from third-party emitters, and (3) a pipeline
connecting the Calgren facilities to the remote injection site (with associated injection surface

 12

equipment). The surface facility components will be developed in a phased manner with scalable
capabilities integrated into design and development to allow for acceptance of additional CO2. A
more detailed description of the storage field elements is provided in the Surface Facility Design
Approach section.

3.2.2 Timing of Development
Land agreements and permitting are the first steps for the development of facility infrastructure
at TCCSP. Next steps include engineering design and the development of an approach for
repurposing the existing infrastructure. Once legal agreements and regulatory elements are in
place, procurement of service contracts and materials for construction will be initiated for the
capture and storage elements and will then immediately be followed by procurement for the
pipeline components and services. Once procurement activities are completed, site
construction will begin and will include site preparation, construction of infrastructure and
installation of equipment. Well drilling and testing will then begin after the site work is matured
to a point to accommodate those activities. Baseline monitoring will be implemented during
well drilling as is outlined in relevant permits (EPA UIC Class VI permit and Low Carbon Fuel
Standard Certification). During injection operations, testing and monitoring will continue and
facility maintenance will be ongoing throughout this phase. Regulatory reporting and oversight
will also be implemented during the injection phase of the project for all relevant permits that
will be acquired for the project (see Permitting and Compliance section).

3.3 Geologic Background

The TCCSP storage complex is located in Tulare County and occurs in the central part of the
Buttonwillow sub-basin, also called the Tulare sub-basin (Figure 1) (Bartow, 1991). This sub-
basin is in the southern part of the San Joaquin basin, a larger structural feature within the Great
Vally of California (Bartow, 1991; Scheirer and Magoon, 2007). Sedimentary rocks within the San
Joaquin basin represent over 100 million years and 24,000 feet thick of marine and marginal-
marine deposition that resulted from changes in accommodation space and sediment supply
which was ultimately controlled by global eustatic sea level change, subsidence/uplift, and climate
change (Johnson and Graham, 2007).
The regional and local subsurface stratigraphy at TCCSP is comprised of a sequence of largely
Upper Cretaceous through Miocene-aged siliciclastic rocks that nonconformably overlie
crystalline basement. Here, the subsurface stratigraphy contains a mixed assemblage of
sandstones and shale of the Upper Cretaceous Panoche Group through Pleistocene Tulare
Formation. A regional stratigraphic cross-section displays the geologic formations, primary
lithologies, lateral corrections, and type wells for the region. A stratigraphic chart (Figure 2),
shows the lowermost USDW, overburden, and geologic zones in the AoR.

3.3.1 Field Characterization Results
The characterization well program for TCCSP_OBS-1 was designed to obtain the physical and
chemical characteristics of the TCCSP injection and confining zones prior to CO2 injection to meet
the regulatory requirements of the EPA UIC Class VI rule (40 CFR §146.82). Data sets outlined
in this section detail the necessary baseline data for comparison to future measurements and
demonstrate compliance with the injection well construction requirements as set forth in the EPA
UIC Class VI rule (40 CFR §146.86). This characterization program includes a combination of
well logging, mechanical integrity testing, geologic coring, fluid sampling, and hydrogeologic

 15

testing. These methods generated datasets to aid in determining and/or verifying the depth,
thickness, porosity, permeability, mineralogy, and geochemical profiles of the confining zones
(Upper Monterey Zone and Kreyenhagen) and storage reservoirs (Lower Monterey, Temblor
Formation (Olcese Sand, Jewett Sand, and Vedder Sand), and Lodo-Martinez Zone).
Petrophysical properties of the TCCSP primary confining zone (Upper Monterey Zone) and
secondary confining zone (Kreyenhagen) were characterized by core and, to a lesser degree,
conventional petrophysical analysis techniques. Porosity and permeability values indicate cored
sealing facies of the Monterey Group (Upper and Lower) range between 18-37% and 0.001-0.07
mD, exhibit non-effective microporosity, and are impermeable. Although no core analysis data
exists for the Kreyenhagen, the Nuclear Magnetic Resonance (NMR) log indicates the zones to
be dominated by T2 signatures indicative high amounts of bound-fluid (clay-bound and capillary),
indicating the unit is impermeable. Collectively, these properties demonstrate the ability of sealing
facies of the Upper and Lower Monterey Zones and Kreyenhagen Formation to safely and
effectively confine commercial quantities of CO2 for permanent storage.
The storage reservoir system is comprised of the Lower Monterey Zone, Temblor Formation
(Olcese, Jewett, Vedder), and the undifferentiated Lodo-Martinez Zone (Domengine Sandstone,
Walker Formation, Lodo Formation, and Martinez Formation). Core analysis results from the
TCCSP reservoirs indicate the complex possesses excellent properties for commercial carbon
storage. Petrophysical modeling and core analysis data indicate the TCCSP reservoirs are very
porous (Average PHIE ~24-32%) and permeable (10’s-1000’s of mD) and feature variable
concentrations of ambient CO2 and methane. High permeability streaks of upwards of 8,000-
10,000 mD occur throughout the reservoir section, most commonly within the Temblor Formation.
3.3.1.1 Wireline Well Logging Program
Open-hole and cased-hole wireline well log data was collected throughout TCCSP_OBS-1 well
(surface to total depth (TD)), with logging suite coverage varying per borehole segments (surface,
intermediate, TD section) and cased segments (surface and intermediate strings).
During intermediate open-hole logging, bridging clays were encountered while running in hole.
Upon review of the mud log and discussion with the mud logger, this was the top of a seemingly
monolithic section with significant volumes of smectitic clay. The intermediate open-hole log
program past was cancelled to reduce risk. To fill data gaps within the intermediate section, dipole
sonic, gamma ray, neutron porosity logs were acquired through casing, while pulling out of hole
during logging of the open-TD section.
The surface hole of the TCCSP_OBS-1 well was logged with a suite of open-hole and cased-hole
tools designed to characterize the USDW and above-zone intervals. Open-hole logs including
mudlog data, spontaneous potential (SP), gamma ray, resistivity, density, neutron, photoelectric
factor, sonic, and caliper were collected to support geologic correlation, salinity modeling,
lithologic interpretation, and petrophysical analysis. After casing, a comprehensive cased-hole
program was run to evaluate cement placement and wellbore integrity, including cement bond,
variable density, temperature, isolation scanner, and pulsed neutron spectroscopy logs.
The intermediate hole logging program built on the surface hole data with a similar set of open-
hole logs focused on geologic correlation, petrophysics, salinity characterization, and lithologic
evaluation through the USDW, above-zone, and caprock intervals. Additional cased-hole
measurements including spectral gamma ray, neutron, and full dipole sonic were acquired to

 16

further refine geomechanical properties and strengthen correlations across the wellbore. This
interval also included a full cement and well integrity evaluation through cement bond, variable
density, temperature, isolation scanner, and pulsed neutron spectroscopy logs to ensure proper
casing isolation and establish baseline conditions for long-term monitoring.
The long-string open-hole logging program provided the most extensive characterization,
targeting both the caprock and reservoir intervals. This included a comprehensive petrophysical
and geologic suite including SP, gamma ray, resistivity, density, neutron, photoelectric factor,
sonic, NMR, and elemental spectroscopy to define lithology, fluid properties, mineralogy, and
reservoir quality. Specialized logs such as borehole imaging and dipole sonic shear were also
acquired to evaluate rock textures, structural features, and geomechanical properties critical for
reservoir and seal assessment. Caliper measurements supported log quality control and cement
volume calculations, while the broad toolset collectively established a detailed foundation for site
characterization and CCS evaluation.

3.3.1.4 Reservoir Fluid Sample Program and Analysis
Fluid samples were collected from two different depths in TCCSP_OBS-1 using the SLB’s single-
probe MDT tool in the Lower Monterey and the Jewett-Upper Vedder Zone. Fluid samples were
acquired, maintained and analyzed at in-situ formation pressure and temperature (live fluid). The
samples were analyzed via inductively coupled plasma (ICP) and ion chromatography (IC)
methods to determine the baseline ionic (cation and anion) and trace metal concentrations,
isotopic and physiochemical profile (such as pH, temperature, salinity, and resistivity of water).
Additional fluid samples will be collected from the TCCSP above-zone and lowermost USDW (San
Joaquin) at a later date. Analytical data will be used to calibrate geochemical models and guide
log-based water salinity models.
3.3.1.5 Hydrogeologic Testing Program and Analysis
Formation testing within the TCCSP_OBS-1 well included modular formation dynamics tester
(MDT) pretests, injection fall-off (IFO) tests, and step-rate tests (SRTs) across the caprock and
reservoirs. MDT pretests were performed at 19 depths, two within the Olcese Formation and 17
within the Jewett-Upper Vedder, to measure formation pressure, fluid mobility, and permeability.
Injection fall-off tests using straddle packers were completed at three depths, including one in the
Lower Vedder and two in the Lodo-Martinez interval, to assess reservoir pressure, injectivity, and
potential formation boundaries. In addition, a step-rate test was conducted in the Lower Monterey
to estimate the fracture gradient, providing critical information on geomechanical limits and safe
operating pressures for future injection activities.

3.3.2 Storage Reservoir Description
The Temblor Formation, is comprised of several members including the Olcese Sand, Jewett
Sand/Freeman Silt, and Vedder Sand. Within the TCCSP_OBS-1 well, the Olcese Sand was
interpreted to be ~730 feet thick. The zone is comprised of coarse to fine, poorly sorted,
calcareous to non-calcareous sand/sandstone interbedded with calcareous to non-calcareous,
partially lithified clay/claystone. Reservoir quality is interpreted to decrease in these finer-grained
sands, although they still allowed transmittal of fluid through the sediment. Total porosity is
calculated at ~30% (average), with an effective average NMR porosity of ~25%. Intrinsic
permeability estimated from uncalibrated NMR averages ~220 mD.
The Jewett-Upper Vedder Sands were interpreted to occur in a ~620-foot zone. This zone consists
of partially cemented, poorly sorted, clayey to non-clayey, fine-to-coarse sand with interbeds of
sandy clay. Total porosity within the zone averaged ~30%. Analyses of advanced logs indicated
the presence of clean, porous and permeable sands with large pores. Effective NMR porosity
averaged at ~25% and uncalibrated NMR permeability averaged to ~540 mD, with some of the
cleanest zones estimated to contain darcies of permeability.
Underlying the Jewett Sand-Upper Vedder zone, a ~115-foot-thick prominent shale bed,
interpreted as the Tumey Shale, occurs. This tight, impermeable claystone/shale interval
separates the Upper and Lower Vedder Sand zones.
The Lower Vedder Sand zone is ~150 feet thick at the TCCSP_OBS-1 well. The zone is comprised
of partially lithified, calcareous to non-calcareous, fine to coarse sand interbedded with
claystone/clay. Two distinct sand packages occur, with interbedded clay zones featuring large
amounts of clay bound water. Total porosity within the zone averaged at ~30%. The uppermost

 20

sand package appears to represent notably higher reservoir quality, with effective porosity near
~30%, high counts of large pore body sizes, and estimated permeability values up to a Darcy.
Across the Lower Vedder Sand, effective porosity averaged at ~25% and permeability averaged
at ~110 mD. The Lower Vedder Sand is underlain by ~350 feet of tight clay/claystone of the
Kreyenhagen Formation, forming the bottom seal to the Temblor Formation.
The Kreyenhagen formation underlies the Lower Vedder Sand zone. The interval is a 308 foot-
thick shale bed which occurs between 8,450-8,700 foot MD. This tight, impermeable claystone
interval separates the Lower Vedder Sand zones and the Lodo-Martinez zone.

The Lodo-Martinez Zone is comprised of several units including the Domengine Sandstone,
Walker Formation, Lodo Formation and the Martinez Formation. The interval is a ~600 foot-thick
shale bed which occurs between 8,757-9,358 foot MD. The upper portion of the zone (Domengine
Sandstone & Walker Formation) was observed in mud cuttings to be comprised of clayey and
feldspathic sands which persist throughout the formation. The remaining portion of the zone (Lodo
Formation and Martinez Formation) was observed to be comprised of gray, poorly cemented,
coarse-grained feldspathic sand interbedded with sandy clay and clay/claystone. Total NMR
porosity is calculated at 24% (average), with an effective average NMR porosity of 18%. Intrinsic
permeability estimated from NMR averages 1,207 mD.

3.3.3 Seismic Interpretation and Structural Configuration
Nine 2D seismic lines, selected based on their quality, were chosen from a private database for
licensing and analysis within the designated Static Earth Model (SEM) boundary. The selected
lines create a crosshatch pattern, ensuring each seismic line could be tied to one another
providing more complete coverage throughout the SEM boundary. Seismic well ties were
established for two wells in the area and subsurface horizons were interpreted.
TCCSP is located along the stable and little deformed east limb of the southern San Joaquin
Basin. Regionally, the east limb of the basin is effectively a homocline that dips gently to the
southwest and is internally structurally homogenous with few interpreted faults in seismic. Within
the SEM, interpreted seismic and formation well tops indicate an average structural dip of 3.6˚ to
the southwest. The crystalline basement produces a sharp acoustic impedance contrast and
indicates a more steeply dipping geometry to the southwest. The Panoche Formation and
Kreyenhagen Formation pinch out against the crystalline basement east of the AoR. The Vedder
Sand is interpreted above the Kreyenhagen Formation and is laterally continuous across the
region.
Evaluation of the historic 2D seismic around the site found very few faults to be present across
the region and no faults were observed to penetrate the TCCSP storage complex (confining zone
or reservoirs) within or near the project AoR. In the study area, one basement fault was observed
to partially penetrate the storage complex and two small faults were observed within the above-
zone section. These above-zone faults are not interpreted to impose much risk to site suitability
as (1) they do not penetrate the storage complex; (2) are not observed to be connected to
basement fault-systems; (3) based on geology observed at TCCSP_OBS-1 occur within a thick
clay/claystone section, which will likely promote clay smearing along fault planes, limiting fault
transmissivity and (4) geomechanical modeling indicates faults within TCCSP area to not in a

 21

critically stressed state, requiring substantial increases in pore pressure to reactivate
(approximately >1,000 psi).

3.3.4 Baseline Seismicity and Plan for Managing the Risk of Induced Seismicity
The United States Geological Survey Earthquake Catalogue was screened for all earthquakes
within 30 miles of the AoR. The low historical magnitude of earthquakes within this radius supports
that earthquake seismicity does not pose a high risk to the storage zone geology or
surface/subsurface CO2 infrastructure.
3D seismic data will be acquired within the AoR and will help decrease the uncertainty of unknown
faults. If faults that penetrate the reservoir and caprock are identified in the 3D seismic, the
existing seismic monitoring plan will be modified to appropriately monitor those faults.
Any potential seismic activity will be monitored using several pressure gauges. Injection pressure
will be continuously monitored at the wellhead and below the base of the caprock using wellhead
and downhole pressure gauges, respectively. Wellhead injection and annulus pressures will be
monitored to ensure surface pressures are maintained below the maximum allowable pressure
for each well. Formation pressure and temperature measurements will be obtained to not exceed
90% of the fracture pressure of the injection zone as required by the EPA UIC Class VI rule (40
CFR §146.82).

3.3.5 Geologic and Dynamic Model
Reservoir Units and Modeling Approach
The development and results of both the Static Earth Model (SEM) and Dynamic Reservoir Model
(DRM) are summarized in this section. The purpose of the SEM for TCCSP, generated in SLB’s
Petrel, was to represent the petrophysical properties (porosity and permeability) of the thick
sandstone reservoirs and the shaley confining units. The framework for the TCCSP SEM is based
on contoured surfaces derived from the interpretation of well logs and formations tops from deep
regional wells. Key regional model surfaces were created; Figure 3, for example, shows the
contoured surface of the top of the Olcese Sandstone with 18 wells within the SEM’s framework;
other regional wells outside this helped constrain the contouring of the Olcese and other formation
tops.

The regional dip is 3⁰ to 4⁰ to the southwest. Wells providing petrophysical information are shown
as black dots in Figure 3. The SEM’s framework has 13 model zones from ground level to
basement, and eight of these zones received petrophysical modeling.

The DRM was developed to predict the lateral and vertical movements of CO2 injected into the
reservoir zones, the Lower Monterey, Olcese, Jewett, Vedder and Lodo-Martinez Formations at
TCCSP. The model incorporates physical flow dynamics geochemical reactions and trapping
processes associated with CO2 injection into subsurface reservoirs. GEM (General Equation of
State Model), developed by the Computer Modeling Group, was used as the simulator. A multi-
component, multi-phase fluid flow process was employed to evaluate the development of the CO2
plume, the pressure front, and the long-term fate of the injection.

3.3.6 Reservoir Pressure
Dynamic reservoir simulations indicate that TCCSP reservoirs can safely sequester the targeted
50 Mmt of CO2, with additional storage capacity remaining. The TCCSP SEM was exported and
used to facilitate dynamic modeling, where a scenario of two wells injecting 50 Mmt into the Lodo-
Martinez, Temblor, and Lower Monterey reservoir zones over a 20-year period was modeled.
Dynamic simulations indicate pressure buildups in response to CO2 injection to be well below the
80% operational fracture pressure limits and core-derived CO2 threshold entry pressures of the
confining zones.
Due to the high permeability of storage formations, the overall pressure buildup in the system is
low. A large difference between the average pressure buildup and 80% fracture pressure limit
indicates that formations can accommodate additional CO2 volume than modeled.

3.3.7 Injection Rate and Mass Over Time
The TCCSP injection wells have been designed to accommodate the maximum instantaneous
mass rate per well of CO2 that could be delivered to the site in the case that one injection well is
shut-in for workover operations. Key characteristics of the Temblor Formation (Lower Monterey,
Olcese, Jewett, and Vedder Sand) and Lodo-Martinez, which are the storage reservoirs, were
considered in the design of these wells.
SLB’s Pipesim was used to determine normal operating ranges for the wellhead pressures for
injection wells at modeled injection rates. The operational injection values were obtained by
constructing a hypothetical wellbore model that simulated multiphase fluid flow using SLB’s
Pipesim. A nodal analysis was used to determine the range of possible injection rates. The only
possible changes to operational parameters may stem from variations in volume of the CO2
source, which may lead to fluctuations in injection volumes.

3.4 Area of Review (AoR) Calculation

The Area of Review (AoR) is defined by the lateral and vertical migration extents of the CO2 plume,
formation fluids, and the pressure front in the subsurface. It encompasses the region surrounding
the TCCSP where underground sources of drinking water (USDWs) may be endangered by
injection activities. The AoR is determined as the larger of either the CO2 plume’s extent 50 years
post-injection or the elevated pressure front during the injection phase. This is usually the largest
of either the CO2 plume at 50 years post-injection period or the elevated pressure front during the
injection phase. The AoR for TCCSP is defined by the 50 years post-injection CO2 lateral plume
extent, as the threshold pressure plume is encompassed by the CO2 plume. After injection
commences, monitoring and operational data will be added to ongoing modeling to continue to
reevaluate model assumptions, calibrate the reservoir model, and validate the AoR.

3.5 Legacy Well Evaluation

There is one legacy oil and gas borehole found within the TCCSP AoR that will need corrective
action. The California DWR) Sustainable Groundwater Management Act (SGMA) Data Viewer
was consulted to identify water wells in the AoR and a 1,000-foot buffer, resulting in the
identification of 44 water well completion reports within the AoR and the 1,000-foot buffer. Most
of the water wells are agricultural irrigation wells. Based on a review of the SGMA Data Viewer,
there are no water wells within the AoR with depth records that indicate they penetrate the primary
confining unit. None of the water wells within the AoR were found to require corrective action.

3.6 Volumetrics

Using a 3D SEM area, CO2 storage resource estimates were calculated for reservoir zones from
the Lower Monterey down to the base of the Lodo Martinez, excluding the Kreyenhagen
Formation. The resource estimates were computed on a “cellular level,” meaning that each model
cell had a corresponding volume, depth, pressure, temperature, facies, effective porosity, CO2
density, storage efficiency factors, and resulting CO2 mass. The CO2 mass could then be
computed (summed) for each reservoir zone based on sandstone facies.

3.7 Storage Resource Management System

Derived from the Petroleum Resource Management System, the Storage Resource Management
System (SRMS) was developed to assess the commercial viability of carbon capture and storage
projects over their developmental lifespan. The SRMS first divides projects into two major
resource groups: Discovered and Undiscovered resources (Figure 4). Undiscovered storage
resources are formations which could act as suitable storage reservoirs; however, a lack of data
prevents a definite assertation of the resource’s total capacity. Discovered Storage Resources are
resources in which the quantity of storage availability has been more thoroughly ascertained
through the geologic analysis of the target formation. These two major resource groups are further
divided into classes and subclasses based on their degree of commerciality. Additionally, the
storage resource classes are categorized depending on their degree of uncertainty (Figure 4).
This categorization can be done using probabilistic or deterministic methods and is meant to
reflect the degree of uncertainty in the storage capacity of target formations.

Undiscovered Storage Resources are classified as Prospective Storage Resources, which are
further divided into three sub-classes: Play, Lead, and Prospect. The subclass with the lowest
degree of commerciality is the Play subclass (Figure 4). To increase the subclassification of the
project to Lead or Prospect status, informed estimates of the storage capacity of target reservoirs
must be made. Before Phase II, TCCSP made volumetric estimates of storage quantities for their
prospective storage resource using the CO2SCREEN methodology. These volumetric estimates
established a 90% certainty that 146 Mmt CO2 could be stored in the target formation, a 50%
certainty that 467 Mmt could be stored in the target formation, and a 10% certainty that 1,155
Mmt could be stored in the target formation. These initial simplistic prospective storage estimates
were generated using regional and near local data due to limited site-specific data prior that was
available at the time. While the estimates did not rely on extensive and site-specific geologic data,
they provided a justification to advance TCCSP to a higher subclassification of the Prospective
Storage Resource category.

 25

Figure 4. SRMS Classification of TCCSP at the completion of the CarbonSAFE Phase II (modified from SPE,

Contingent Storage Resources are those where discovered storable quantities are estimated, as
of a given date, to be potentially accessible, in known geologic formations by the application of
development projects, but which are not considered to be commercially viable due to one or more
contingencies. Contingencies are a critical aspect of this storage resource class. They are defined
as the economic, market, legal, environmental, social, and government factors forecasted to exist
and impact the project and could include scenarios like the lack of a viable carbon market, a lack
of technology to sufficiently inject or develop a reservoir, or minimal public acceptance of the
project. This resource class has 4 subclasses, Development Not Viable, Development Not
Clarified, Development on Hold, and Development Pending (Figure 4). To increase the
commerciality of the project, the contingencies must be adequately addressed or be planned to
be addressed within a reasonable time frame.

The highest storage class available is Storage Capacity. To achieve this level of commerciality,
several benchmarks must be reached. The storage reservoir must be discovered, characterized,
injectable, and economically viable. To advance to Storage Capacity all contingencies must be
addressed or be planned to be addressed within a reasonable timeframe, and a development
plan must be established and be set to be executed within a reasonable timeframe. Storage
capacity is divided into 3 sub-classes: Justified for development, Approved for Development, and
On Injection (Figure 4).

storage complex at the site. Further, the field development approach will target the deeper, sub-

These findings confirm that the storage reservoirs have sufficient pore space to inject and store

TCCSP has progressed to commercial status with a development plan in place containing

4 Permitting and Compliance

To ensure the success of TCCSP, the proper regulatory guidelines must be followed, and the
proper permits obtained to ensure the establishment of a competent carbon storage project.
Permits at the federal, state and local levels are required for CCS projects. A study by Lawrence
Livermore National Lab (LLNL) on the permitting requirements for CCS projects in California
noted that project developers should anticipate and be equipped to handle a complex and
technically involved authorization process (LLNL, 2021).
Environmental review required for CCS projects, such as the California Environmental Quality Act
(CEQA) and the National Environmental Policy Act (NEPA), will be a key determinant of project
authorization timelines and can span multiple years (LLNL, 2021). In particular, the review
required for CEQA is a significant undertaking and can require mitigation measures (LLNL, 2021).
Table 6 provides a summary of the permits TCCSP is required to obtain for this project and
includes a high-level estimate of turnaround times for each.
CO2 pipelines are critical to the operation of TCCSP and must adhere to a unique set of
regulations. Pipeline sitting in California offers a complex challenge which must be overcome prior
to the commencement of injection. Since 2022, the state has had a partial moratorium on CO2
pipelines, preventing the establishment of new CO2 transportation infrastructure. However, many
California organizations have urged the state senate to repeal the moratorium, presenting an
opportunity to overcome this obstacle.
At the federal level, CO2 pipeline safety is regulated by the Pipeline and Hazardous Materials
Safety Administration and adheres to the Hazardous Liquid Pipeline Safety Act of 1979 (HLPSA).
While this administrative branch does have authority over pipeline permitting, intrastate pipelines
are often governed at the state level. In California, the Office of the State Fire Marshal oversees
CO2 pipeline safety, who regulate pipelines in accordance with the Elder California Pipeline Safety
Act of 1981. The Office of the State Fire Marshal requires a jurisdictional evaluation to ensure the
competency of any pipeline project.

5.1.1.1 Phase 1: CO2 Capture and Compression at Calgren (CRF & CDF)
In Phase 1, TCCSP will construct dedicated CO2 capture, compression, and dehydration units at
the two Calgren facilities (CRF ethanol plant and CDF biogas plant). These two sources provide
high-purity CO2 streams, approximately 99.5 mol% CO2 from CRF (fermentation off-gas) and ~95
mol% CO2 from CDF (biogas upgrading off-gas, containing up to ~5% methane). The combined
CO2 flow from CRF and CDF is on the order of 200,000 metric tons per year (~160,000 from CRF
and 40,000 from CDF) under current operation. The raw CO2 exits the processes at near-
atmospheric pressure (0-1 psig) and ambient temperature and is currently vented or liquefied on-
site. Phase 1 will implement capture so that this CO2 can be routed into TCCSP.
Each source will have its own compression train to boost the low-pressure CO2 to pipeline
pressure. Initially, a blower unit at each source will collect and push the gas to a central
compressor station – for example, a 14-inch line from CRF’s blower and a 6-inch line from CDF’s
blower feed into the main compressors. The CO2 gas from each source is compressed in multiple
stages (using reciprocating compressors with interstage coolers) to reach the required pipeline
pressure (on the order of ~2,000 psig). After each compression stage, the gas is cooled in an air-
cooled exchanger and condensed water is knocked out in a separator. This removes the bulk of
moisture (and any entrained compressor lubricants, which are sent to an oily water handling unit)
before the next stage. A dehydration system is incorporated at an intermediate pressure where
the CO2 is cooler and has a lower moisture content – this maximizes dryer efficiency and
minimizes equipment size. A triethylene glycol (TEG) dehydration unit is planned to dry the CO2
to <30 lb/MMscf of water (no free water), meeting pipeline specifications. After dehydration, the
CO2 is further compressed (and/or pumped as a dense fluid) up to the pipeline delivery pressure.
The Phase 1 design will produce pipeline-quality CO2 at <120 °F and ~2,000 psig, with a purity
>95% CO2 and impurities (O2, N2, CH4, H2S) below required thresholds. All equipment in contact
with wet, acidic CO2 (e.g. initial stages and piping up to the dryer) will be constructed from
corrosion-resistant materials (316L stainless steel), while the dry CO2 product lines can use
carbon steel pipeline material. Sufficient plot space at the Calgren site has been allocated for the
compression/dehydration facilities and any necessary ancillary systems (power supply, cooling,
controls) (see Storage Site CO2 Supply Infrastructure). With completion of Phase 1, the
Calgren plants will have fully functional CO2 capture systems in place, ready to supply
compressed, dehydrated CO2 for transport.

5.1.1.2 Phase 2: CO2 Trunkline and Injection Facilities
Phase 2 involves building the pipeline and injection site facilities to transport the CO2 from Calgren
facilities to the selected storage reservoirs. The CO2 transport infrastructure will be comprised of
a trunkline to transport CO2 from the Calgren CO2 terminal to the proposed injection site. The
exact route will be determined during detailed engineering, but it is expected to traverse primarily
agricultural land with minimal elevation change. The pipeline is anticipated to be underground
carbon steel line pipe, designed for supercritical CO2 service. Operating above CO2’s critical point
(~1,070 psia) keeps the CO2 in a dense-phase fluid state for efficient transport. A dense-phase
CO2 pipeline can carry 4 to 7 times more CO2 per diameter than a gas-phase pipeline and avoids
two-phase flow issues.
At the injection site, surface facilities will be installed to receive the CO2 from the pipeline, boost
its pressure as needed, and inject it into the geologic storage formation. The arriving CO2 will be
metered and then fed to high-pressure injection pumps. These centrifugal pumps will elevate the
CO2 pressure to the required well injection pressure to overcome reservoir pressure and inject
CO2 at the desired rate. The injection system is relatively simple since the CO2 is delivered as a
single-phase liquid-like fluid, and liquid pumps and mass flow meters are the primary equipment
needed, rather than large gas compressors. The Phase 2 injection facility design will include
safety and flow control to regulate injection and protect against over-pressure. Importantly, the
design philosophy provides for expandability and reliability. The concept design envisions two
injection well sites, with a valved manifold to direct flow to either well, and redundant pumps at
each site. By the end of Phase 2, the project will be capable of continuous injection of the CO2
captured from the Calgren plants into the dedicated storage formations.
5.1.1.3 Phase 3: CO2 Receiving Terminal Expansion
Phase 3 will expand the surface infrastructure to handle significantly larger CO2 volumes from
third-party emitters. This involves building out the CO2 receiving terminal at the Calgren site and
making any necessary upgrades to the pipeline and injection system for the higher throughput. In
Phase 3, a rail/truck unloading terminal will be constructed to receive liquid CO2 delivered from
external sources. According to the design basis, the terminal will be equipped to offload standard
CO2 rail tank cars and tanker trucks. The project has confirmed the need for flexibility to accept
CO2 via both railcars and highway tankers (initial design includes facilities for both modes). The
design includes tracks for storing full and empty cars, a railcar mover (e.g. Trackmobile) for
positioning cars, and top-access unloading racks since the CO2 railcars have top connections.
Similarly, a truck unloading bay with pump connections will be provided for CO2 tanker trucks.
Unloaded liquid CO2 will be routed either directly into the pipeline or into on-site buffer storage
tanks before injection.
The CO2 receiving terminal will be built with an initial capacity sized for early third-party volumes
and then expanded modularly. The project’s philosophy is to avoid overbuilding upfront and
instead scale up as additional CO2 supply agreements are secured. The design will include a
minimal storage capacity on site, providing operational flexibility to buffer deliveries and maintain
continuous pipeline feed. As demand grows, the terminal facilities (unloading bays, storage tanks,
refrigeration units, etc.) can be incrementally increased. The pipeline designed to be built in Phase
2 is sized for the maximum flow and will accommodate the volume. If increased compression
power or added pumping units are needed to maintain pressure and flow at the maximum rate,
these operational adjustments will be made.

5.1.1.4 Site Constraints and Design Considerations
The expansion of the CO2 terminal will be executed in line with site-specific constraints at Calgren.
Notably, the rail yard size is limited by available land – the facility may not support a full unit train
(100+ cars) at once, so the logistics will involve smaller batches of railcars handled continuously.
This constraint has influenced the modular design approach. The build-out will ensure that fixed
infrastructure (e.g. mainline piping, central storage, refrigeration systems) is in place, while
additional unloading spurs and equipment can be added as throughput grows. All new facilities
will comply with applicable codes and environmental regulations. For example, equipment and
foundations will be designed to the California Department of Transportation (Caltrans) Seismic
Design Criteria (SDC) given the site’s seismic hazard, and high ambient temperature ranges. The
design also incorporates operational safety features such as vapor recovery or vent systems for
boil-off gas, emergency shut-off valves, and spill containment for the storage area. By the end of
Phase 3, TCCSP’s surface facilities will be capable of receiving CO2 from various sources and
reliably injecting it into the storage reservoir, all while maintaining flexibility and scalability to
accommodate future growth in CO2 capture in the region.

car shipping company typically will move the cars to their switching yard. In the switching yard,
the shipper will move cars to prepare a train for shipment. Then transport the cars to the

destination. The overall shipment time is impacted by car dwell in the switching yards and the

6.2.1.7 CO2 Transmission Pipeline
A pipeline will transport the combined CO2 stream from the surface facilities and the loading
terminal to the injection well; the final alignment is under development. Final line size and
operating setpoints will be confirmed by detailed hydraulics to accommodate initial operations and
future throughput increases within the project’s build-out plan.
The purpose of the CO2 injection facility is to boost the pressure of the CO2 delivered via pipeline
to the required injection pressure of the storage reservoir. The delivery pressure of the pipeline
and the injection pressure of the reservoir are key parameters in the design of the injection facility.
The design and operation of the CO2 injection equipment can accommodate a change in pipeline
delivery or formation injection pressure if this occurs after further evaluation.
The pipeline delivers the CO2 to the injection facility as a dense phase, supercritical fluid; this
allows pumps to boost the pressure of the CO2 for storage in one or more injection wells (design
point for surface injection pressure). Operation of the pipeline above the CO2 critical pressure
allows for efficient transport of the fluid; transport of CO2 as a supercritical fluid moves 4 to 7 times
more product versus a gas-phase pipeline with the same diameter. In addition, the density of the
supercritical fluid simplifies the equipment used at the injection facility. Centrifugal pumps and
mass flow meters are the primary equipment at the injection facility required to boost the pressure
and provide a measurement of the amount of CO2 injected into the reservoir, respectively.
The injection facility boosts the pressure of CO2 from the pipeline to the injection well. The primary
design parameters for the injection facility are the delivery pressure of the CO2 pipeline (inlet), the

 37

surface pressure requirement of the CO2 at the injection well (outlet), and the flow rate of CO2 to
be injected. Pumps are used to boost the pressure of CO₂ in many process applications. CO2
pumps are common equipment in pipeline booster stations and at the wellhead to inject CO2 into
subsurface geologic reservoirs associated with sequestration projects and enhanced oil recovery
(EOR) projects. The common pump types used are horizontal multi-stage centrifugal pumps and
split-case centrifugal pumps; these pumps are familiar to operators for moving fluids in the oil and
gas industry.
The horizontal multi-stage centrifugal pump utilizes numerous face-to-back, stacked stages of
identical impellers and diffusers mounted inside of the pump barrel. The pump line-shaft and
stacked impeller assembly uses line-shaft bushings and impeller wear-rings/bushings to maintain
the rotational stability of the rotating element assembly during pump operation. Due to the
continuous, face-to-back, stacked impeller design, all the hydraulic forces resulting from the pump
differential head are applied as a thrust force in a single axial direction towards the pump suction.
To counter the high axial thrust forces, the pump design incorporates a large thrust bearing
mounted in a bearing frame (thrust chamber) located between the pump suction chamber /
mechanical seal assembly and the motor coupling section.
The split case centrifugal pump is also referenced as an API 610 (Type BB3), horizontally (axially)
split, multistage, between-bearing type of pump design. The impellers of the pumps are mounted
on a relatively large diameter, stiff shaft which is concentrically fixed by bearings located at each
end to provide a well-centered rotating element having uniformly close radial clearances. A center
bushing and throttle bushing is commonly used to further improve rotor axial balance and rotor
dynamic stability. Each impeller stage also utilizes impeller wear rings and bushings to prevent
recirculation between stages, like the horizontal centrifugal pump. The pump has a hydraulically
balanced rotor assembly with impeller stages mounted in two groups facing opposite directions.
This results in low axial thrust forces and reduced probability of thrust bearing failure. Due to the
between-bearing design, two mechanical seals are required for a Type BB3 pump. Seal chamber
pressure for each of the seals is typically balanced to a pressure slightly above the pump suction
pressure.
Both style pumps have good operating experience in CO2 service. The split-case centrifugal pump
is more typical of pipeline booster stations because of the API design rating. The split-case
centrifugal pump can achieve higher flow rates, has slightly higher efficiency, but costs about two
times more for similar capacity, and delivery time will be more than double that of a horizontal
centrifugal pump. Although not an API rated pump, the reliability of a horizontal centrifugal pump
is high based on experience and the pumps are widely used in field and plant operations. Typical
pump efficiency is above 80%, and equipment delivery is less than 20 weeks.
To provide flexibility for a long-term injection program, two injection sites are included. CO2 from
the source is transported via pipeline to the injection facility and custody transfer meters located
at either end of the pipeline confirm the integrity of the system. From the pipeline, CO2 would flow
through a manifold (not shown) and route to one of two injection sites. At each site, two pumps
are positioned in parallel; either pump can provide 100% flow when operating. The discharge CO2
flows through a back pressure valve (not shown) before injection. This valve is used to maintain
a differential pressure across the pumps during startup, but it is typically open during normal
operation. The design supports wide turndown for variable receipts, provides pump/line bypass
for maintenance, and includes launcher/receiver connections to enable routine pigging and

 38

internal inspection. Emergency shutdown (ESD) logic provides automatic isolation and controlled
blowdown on high-high pressure, leak detection, or fire/gas alarm; leak detection is based on
mass-balance between custody meters, with alarmed thresholds and operator response
procedures.
Only specification-compliant CO2 is routed past the injection-site custody meter; analyzer checks
and periodic lab confirmation verify conformance, and interlocks prevent introducing off-spec fluid
to the injection manifold.

The TCCSP injection wells will be constructed with casing and cement that will be compatible with

The design implemented concentric casing sizes required to isolate the injection reservoir from

surveys, open hole logging, and formation testing) will be completed.
6.4.1.3 Tubing and Packer
The tubing connects the injection zone to the wellhead, providing a pathway for safely injecting
and storing CO2. In accordance with the EPA UIC Class VI rule (40 CFR § 146.86 (c)), the tubing
and packer material used for the construction of TCCSP’s injection wells will be compatible with

set to a depth opposite a cemented interval. Any change to the tubing and packer specifics
detailed below will be communicated to the Region 09 UIC Program Director.
6.4.1.4 Annular Fluid
The annular space above the packer, between long-string casing and the injection tubing, will be
filled with fluid to maintain a positive pressure differential. This pressure differential serves to
stabilize the injection tubing and inhibit corrosion.

 41

During injection operations, the surface pressure of the annular fluid will be maintained. Combined
with the hydrostatic pressure of the fluid column, this will ensure that the downhole annular
pressure remains higher than the injection pressure. The annular fluid will consist of freshwater
treated with chemical additives.
The fluid may be mixed onsite using freshwater and a combination of liquid and dry additives or
may be sourced pre-mixed, depending on availability. Regardless of how it is prepared, the fluid
will be filtered to remove solids that could interfere with the packer or other components of the
annular protection system.
6.4.1.5 Proposed Stimulation Program
A well stimulation program will be performed in both injection wells during the drilling operations.
After perforation of all injection wells, an acid wash will take place at low pressures and will not
endanger the confining zone or create a leakage pathway while improving the injectivity of the
near wellbore and allow for injection of CO2 to take place at lower startup pressures. No other
stimulation is proposed.

6.5.1.1 Startup Procedures
Prior to initiating CO2 injection operations, all surface facilities at the injection site will be fully
commissioned and tested to ensure safe and reliable function. These facilities are designed to
receive CO2 from the pipeline. Upon arrival, the CO2 will be metered and routed to high-pressure
centrifugal injection pumps, which will elevate the pressure to the required wellhead injection
pressure to overcome reservoir pressure and achieve the targeted injection rate.
Startup procedures will be clearly defined to manage pressure transients, avoid system shocks,
and ensure the protection of USDWs, in accordance with the EPA UIC Class VI rule (40 CFR
§146.88). The Surface Facilities section provides in more detail the startup procedures for
receiving CO2 at the injection site. The CO2 will be introduced gradually into the system under
controlled conditions, allowing pressure to build incrementally while monitoring flow rates, pump
performance, and system integrity. Because the CO2 arrives as a single-phase, liquid-like fluid,
the injection system is relatively straightforward, relying primarily on liquid pumps and mass flow
meters rather than gas compressors.
6.5.1.2 Operational Procedures

During operations, the CO2 will enter a header and be piped to each injection well and injected
continuously. The CO2 will be in the supercritical phase as it enters the wellhead and will remain
in a supercritical phase within the wellbore. Each injection well will be monitored to ensure safe
operations. Safety monitoring includes monitoring the injection pressure at the wellhead and

 42

bottomhole, monitoring the pressurized annulus, continuous fiberoptic temperature monitoring
along the well or equivalent, and corrosion coupon monitoring to identify corrosion.

A hypothetical wellbore model was constructed that simulated multiphase fluid flow using SLB’s
Pipesim, built to conduct a nodal analysis which was used to determine the range of possible
injection rates. Operational parameters are expected to remain constant throughout the duration
of the injection period. The only possible changes to operational parameters may stem from
variations in volume of the CO2 source, which may lead to fluctuations in injection volumes.

6.5.1.3 Shutdown Procedures
TCCSP will establish and implement detailed shutdown procedures to ensure safe and compliant
operation of the injection system under both routine and emergency conditions. These procedures
will be documented in the comprehensive Emergency and Remedial Response Plan, which will
outline protocols for identifying, responding to, and recovering from operational anomalies or
system failures. This plan will include specific instructions for both surface-level and down-hole
shutdown scenarios, aligned with EPA regulations.
The injection system will be equipped with alarms and automatic surface shut-off systems
designed to respond to deviations in key operating parameters such as pressure, injection rate,
or flow anomalies. Where required, down-hole shut-off systems, such as automatic shut-off
valves, check valves, or equivalent mechanical devices, will be installed to provide additional
protection against uncontrolled releases. These systems are designed to isolate the well in the
event of mechanical failure or pressure excursions that exceed permitted thresholds.
In the event that a shutdown is triggered, either at the surface or down-hole, or if a loss of
mechanical integrity is detected, TCCSP will immediately initiate an investigation to identify the
cause. If the well is determined to lack mechanical integrity or monitoring indicates a potential
issue, the following steps will be taken without delay:
 1. Cease injection immediately;
 2. Take all necessary measures to assess whether there has been any release of CO2 or
 formation fluids into unauthorized zones;
 3. Notify the Director within 24 hours of the incident;
 4. Restore and verify mechanical integrity to the satisfaction of the Director before resuming
 injection; and
 5. Inform the Director when injection is expected to resume.
These shutdown procedures are integral to USDWs and ensuring that injection operations remain
compliant with Class VI regulatory requirements throughout the project lifecycle.
6.5.1.4 Monitoring Plan

TCCSP will monitor injection operations for the duration of the injection phase of this project (i.e.,
20 years). The TCCSP monitoring approach adheres to the requirements of the EPA UIC Class
VI rule (40 CFR §146.90) and is designed to ensure that the well is operating as planned, that the
CO2 plume and pressure front are moving as predicted and that the CO2 plume does not become
a contamination risk to USDWs. Monitoring data collected will also be used to validate and adjust
geological models used to predict the movement of CO2 within the storage zone to support AoR

 43

re-evaluations. TCCSP plans to use the project wells to inject and monitor injection operations
and groundwater resources.
The establishment of concrete baselines is critical to ensuring the competency of TCCSP.
Following the commencement of injection, these baseline measurements will be referenced for
injection and post injection monitoring measurements, helping to determine if these readings
reflect a potential leakage scenario.
TCCSP, LLC. will use both intermittent and continuous monitoring methods to detect any surface
and near-surface CO2 leakage. Surface and near-surface measurements will be collected
throughout the year leading up to injection, throughout the injection phase, and throughout the
post-injection phase. Surface monitoring consists of monitoring the surface conditions near and
around the AoR, with a particular focus on areas near and around wellbores which penetrate the
storage complex.
Prior to injection, TCCSP, LLC. will implement an extensive pre-injection monitoring program
focused on soil, atmospheric, and vegetation conditions to establish accurate baseline data.
Baseline testing will begin at least one year prior to injection and occur quarterly and annually to
capture seasonal variations, ensuring that any future CO2 anomalies can be distinguished from
natural background fluctuations.
Geochemical monitoring will consist of sampling the groundwater in and around the AoR for fluid
temperature, pH, conductivity, reservoir pressure, and static fluid level. The sampling of
groundwater will take place above the injection zone to monitor the groundwater characteristics
present under pre-injection conditions. To accurately conduct this monitoring, baseline measures
must first be established. These measurements will consist of taking quarterly fluid samples of
groundwater from above-zone and shallow formations, commencing at least a year prior to the
start of injection. Pressure and temperature monitoring will start concurrently at least a year prior
to the start of injection and will be monitored continuously. By establishing the baseline in this
manner, a comprehensive understanding of the standard groundwater characteristics at TCCSP
will be established, allowing for accurate geochemical monitoring of these groundwater sources.
Mechanical integrity demonstrations will be performed periodically throughout the project to
ensure there is no leakage and/or fluid movement in and around the wellbore that may result in
the endangerment of USDW. Internal and external mechanical integrity will be demonstrated in
the injection wells prior to injection, throughout the injection phase, and throughout the PISC
phase up to well plugging and abandonment.
The CO2 stream will be periodically analyzed during the injection phase to collect representative
characteristic data on the chemical composition of the CO2 stream. This data will ensure the
injected stream complies with regulatory standards. To do so, baseline measurements of the CO2
stream will be collected for comparison during the injection phase.
To meet the regulatory requirements of the EPA UIC Class VI rule, the well and pipeline materials
will be monitored during the injection phase for loss of mass, thickness, cracking, pitting, and
other signs of corrosion to ensure that the components directly in contact with CO2 injectate meet
the minimum standards for material strength and performance. Materials used in the construction
of injection infrastructure will be weighed, measured, and photographed prior to initial exposure,
serving as a baseline to ensure quality and uniformity through laboratory analysis. If the quality of

 44

this infrastructure significantly deviates from the established baseline, the components will be
replaced and the site investigated for other potential leakage indicators.
Following the commencement of injection, the CO2 plume will be tracked as it progresses through
the target reservoir. 3D seismic surveys will be acquired prior to injection, serving as a baseline
for the seismic surveys during the injection phase and the PISC phase. Should the CO2 plume
breach parameters set during the initial seismic survey, this will be seen as a potential leakage
scenario and corrective action will ensue.
The injection phase monitoring includes monitoring for CO2 chemical and physical characteristics,
gas leaks in the wellheads and valves, corrosion, injection well mechanical integrity, groundwater
quality and geochemistry, direct pressure and temperature measurements, direct pressure front
movement, indirect and direct CO2 plume tracking, surface and near surface CO2 leak monitoring,
and seismicity monitoring for induced and natural seismic events. This testing and monitoring plan
will be reviewed periodically, at minimum every five years and will be adjusted accordingly to meet
any changes to the facility or site conditions over time.

6.5.2 Post-Injection Site Care (PISC) Plan and Decommissioning
6.5.2.1 Post-Injection Site Care Plan
The Post Injection Site Care (PISC) phase will begin when all CO2 injection ceases and will meet
the requirements of the EPA UIC Class VI rule (40 CFR §146.93). The CO2 plume movement at
TCCSP in the post-injection phase is predictable.
The post-injection phase will include monitoring for gas leaks in the wellheads and valves,
external mechanical integrity testing, groundwater sampling, direct pressure and temperature
measurements, indirect and direct plume tracking, surface and near surface CO2 leak monitoring,
and seismicity monitoring for induced and natural seismic events. Every five years during the
post-injection phase of the project, the monitoring data will be incorporated into computational
models and the monitoring plan will be reviewed and updated, if needed, based on modeling
results.
Data generated during the PISC period will be used to demonstrate that the computational model
accurately represents the storage site and simulation results can be used to determine CO2 and
pressure plume’s properties and size. The validation of the computational model with the large
volume of available data will be a significant element to support the demonstration of non-
endangerment of USDWs at the end of the project.
6.5.2.2 Site Closure/Decommissioning
Following the cessation of injection, and when equipment required to inject CO2 is no longer
necessary equipment decommissioning will be completed in two stages:
 Step 1: After cessation of injection, surface equipment necessary to safely sequester CO2
 such as pumps, flowlines, flowmeter, annular pressure monitoring equipment, and piping
 and control equipment will no longer be necessary and shall be dismantled and removed
 from the TCCSP.
 Step 2: After the PISC period, surface equipment related to the monitoring activities as
 part of the PISC period will be decommissioned. This includes the plugging and

 45

 abandonment of all project wells, removal of surface facilities, and reclamation of the land
 to pre-injection conditions. The plugging and abandonment procedures are outlined below
 and are designed to ensure containment of the injected CO2 and for protection of USDWs.
TCCSP may not cease post-injection monitoring until a demonstration of non-endangerment of
USDWs has been approved by the UIC Program Director (EPA UIC Class VI rule, 2010). This will
make a demonstration of USDW non-endangerment based on the evaluation of the site
monitoring data used in conjunction with the project’s computational model. The report will detail
how the non-endangerment demonstration evaluation uses site-specific conditions to confirm and
demonstrate non-endangerment. The report will include all relevant monitoring data and
interpretations upon which the non-endangerment demonstration is based, model documentation
and all supporting data, and any other information necessary for the UIC Program Director to
review the analysis.

Once the CO2 plume has been demonstrated to be moving as predicted and no longer poses a
risk to USDWs, TCCSP will notify the UIC Program Director with a Notice of Intent for Site Closure
at least 120 days before initiating site closure procedures. Following approval for site closure,
TCCSP will plug all monitoring wells, restore the site to its original condition, and submit a site
closure report and associated documentation.
The following steps are to be used as a general guide during site closure.
 1. Notify the Program Director and all relevant local, state, and federal government agencies
 of intent to close the project site.
 2. Decommission equipment.
 3. P&A all monitoring and related project wells.
 4. Return well locations to pre-injection conditions including site reclamation, as necessary.
 5. Complete and submit the Site Close Report to the Program Director within 90 days.

Once TCCSP demonstrates plume and pressure stabilization, as well as non-endangerment of
local USDWs, well plugging and abandonment will commence. Abandonment shall be performed
to not allow the movement of injection or formation fluids out of the storage complex. Prior to well
plugging, the mechanical integrity of the wells will be verified by the distributed temperature
sensing (DTS) placed in the monitoring wells. The well plugging and abandonment will follow the
methodology described in the EPA UIC Class VI rule (40 CFR §146.93), except CO2-resistant
cement need not be utilized in wells that do not encounter CO2 at depth.
6.5.2.2.1 Site Closure Report
Within 90 days of site closure, TCCSP shall submit a site closure report. This report will be
retained at a location designated by the Program Director for 10 years. The report will contain at
minimum the following information:

 • Plugging of the verification and geophysical wells (and the injection well if it has not
 previously been plugged),
 • Location of sealed injection well on a plat of survey that has been submitted to the local
 zoning authority,
 • Notifications to state and local authorities,

 46

 • Records regarding the nature, composition, and volume of the injected CO2, and
 • Post-injection monitoring records.

TCCSP will record a notation to the property’s deed on which the injection well was located that
will indicate the following:

 • That the property was used for carbon dioxide sequestration,
 • The name of the local agency to which a plat of survey with injection well location was
 submitted,
 • The volume of fluid injected,
 • The formation into which the fluid was injected, and
 • The period over which the injection occurred.

6.5.2.2.2 Site Restoration
Under the EPA UIC Class VI rule (40 CFR §146.93) the site operator is required to restore the
site to a condition agreed upon with the UIC Class VI Program Director, with the general directive
that the site be returned as close to pre-injection conditions as possible. This includes removing
surface equipment, road access, and any other facilities that remain on location. The preliminary
vegetation type and density of the area will be utilized to ensure that pre-injection conditions are
established.
The site closure report will be submitted to the UIC Class VI permitting agency and maintained
by the owner or operator for a period of 10 years following site closure. Additionally, TCCSP will
maintain the records collected during the post-injection period for a period of 10 years after which
these records will be delivered to the U.S. EPA Region 09 UIC Program Director.

7.1 Legal/Logistical

Legal and logistical risk scenarios identified for the TCCSP are various and include delays related
to permitting activities or approval, shifts in local or federal politics and claims that the project is
not in compliance with permit requirements. Permitting requirements for CCS projects are
extensive and TCCSP is aware that timing is essential and has been proactive in pursuing and
submitting key federal and state required permits that must be approved and accepted for the
TCCSP to proceed to the injection phase.
Shifts in political view at the local or federal level could impact any of the regulatory entities’
approach to the oversight of project activities. Additionally, permit requirements and overall project
expectations could shift resulting in a decrease in project viability. TCCSP will engage with
legislators and will continue their engagement with regulators to stay informed on any shifts in
regulations that may have an impact on the project.
Risk scenarios related to false public claims of CO2 leakage, CO2 trespass outside of the AoR,
and induced seismicity were rated as medium-risk. An essential part of the monitoring program
that will be implemented at TCCSP includes the collection of baseline testing and monitoring data
to identify any significant changes that may occur over the life of the project related to the injectate
stream, wellbore integrity, properties of the storage complex and/or above-zone, and CO2 plume
and pressure front migration.

7.2 Well Construction and Operation

Well operations or construction challenges could present risk to TCCSP as identified in various
risks presented and discussed at the Risk Workshop. TCCSP has identified a combination of
engineering and administrative safeguards to prevent the probability of the occurrence of these
risks as low as reasonably possible and will proactively work to reduce the severity of any risks
related to construction and operations. To address construction challenges and any subsequent
risks TCCSP will implement well design strategies and materials focused on preventing
movement of fluids into or between USDWs or into any unauthorized zones, permit the use of
appropriate testing devices and workover tools and permit continuous monitoring of the annulus
space between the tubing and long string casing. The injection wells for TCCSP are designed to
accommodate the maximum instantaneous mass rate that is expected from the capture facility
while considering critical characteristics of the CO2 storage reservoir.

7.3 Monitoring

The findings from the risk assessment workshop outlined the need for a robust and redundant
monitoring program to ensure plume tracking and CO2 leakage detection monitoring is not
compromised due to potential equipment failure. With the multitude of systems to be monitored,
and the complexities of processing and interpreting the resulting data, the potential of
misinterpretation of data or conflicting monitoring results was classified as a medium risk. TCCSP
is aware of the challenges that can be encountered by a complex monitoring program and is
defining and implementing SOPs, quality assurance checks, and implementation of engineering
controls to minimize this risk.

on mechanical integrity of the pipeline and project wells.

7.6 Geology

Geologic risks that received a medium rating include leakage of CO2 out of the storage zone due
to the primary confining zone not providing an adequate seal and high permeability zones in the
storage reservoir acting as a thief zone resulting in an unexpected and significant expansion of
the CO2 plume and limited storage capacity. To address these risks TCCSP will pursue a robust
assessment of the subsurface to obtain the physical and chemical characteristics of the geologic
zones of interest. To date, TCCSP has conducted a thorough characterization of the geology at
TCCSP and will continue to pursue a rigorous characterization of the subsurface as the project
evolves and additional data, from the construction of project wells, becomes available. Additional
preventative measures that can be used to address potential geologic risks will include periodic
geologic and dynamic model updates, uncertainty modeling, 3D seismic surveys, and additional
characterization or testing.

The current status of the geologic assessment indicates that the project area provides a
geologically favorable setting for safe, long-term storage for CO2. The CO2 injection zones
comprise thick and porous sandstones with interbedded mudstone and the primary seal
immediately caps the storage zones and provides a thick and laterally extensive seal across the
area. Structurally there is a lack of faulting in the area in and around the site and only one existing
wellbore within the modeled plume area.

Pre-operational testing of the injection wells will establish necessary baseline data for comparison
to future measurements and demonstrate compliance with the injection well construction

 51

requirements set forth in the permits that will be obtained for TCCSP through regulatory entities.
This additional geologic characterization at the site will include well logging and geophysical
surveying, mechanical integrity testing, geologic coring, fluid sampling, and hydrogeologic testing.

These methods will generate datasets to aid in determining and/or verifying the depth, thickness,
porosity, permeability, mineralogy, and geochemical profiles of the primary confining zone and
storage reservoir, and shallower intervals to establish a baseline description of the geology,
geochemistry, and groundwater quality of the above-zone (i.e., formations above the confining
zone) which will later be compared against data obtained throughout the injection phase. Testing
and monitoring of the above-zone will aid in the protection of USDWs.

7.7 Seismic Event

Southwestern Tulare County, where the project is located, has a history of relatively low seismicity
indicating that seismic related hazards are unlikely. TCCSP will pursue a geotechnical
assessment of the surface facilities to identify potential issues in the infrastructure and design that
may be impacted by a seismic event. The geotechnical assessment results will be used to inform
SOPs developed for design and operations. Pressures and saturations within the storage system
will be monitored to avoid fluid leakage and induced seismicity. If a seismic event occurs, a site
assessment will be performed to evaluate infrastructure integrity and monitoring data will be
reviewed and analyzed to determine if there is any drift from baseline that may indicate that the
seismic event has compromised containment of the CO2.

The following administrative and engineering controls will be implemented to mitigate fluid
leakage or induced seismicity related concerns during injection operations:

 • Periodically monitor for local seismic activity.
 • Develop protocols to systematically check monitoring network after earthquake of a certain
 magnitude.
 • TCCSP will develop a master TCCSP health, safety, and environment (HSE) plan to cover
 safety of project personnel, local communities, local infrastructure, and natural resources
 during all site operations. TCCSP will incorporate recommendations and SOPs from
 subcontractors and their respective HSE plans into the master TCCSP HSE plan.
 • TCCSP will develop emergency shutdown procedures that will be implemented as
 appropriate and will be outlined in a plan that will outline, in detail, all emergency and
 remedial responses developed for the project.
 • TCCSP will conduct timely, prompt, and transparent public outreach to communicate any
 project findings or events that pose a threat to the safety of local communities, resources,
 and infrastructure.
 • TCCSP will use CO2 resistant well materials to mitigate formation of potential leakage
 pathways for CO2 up the wellbore.
 • TCCSP will use a combination of temperature and pulsed neutron capture data to monitor
 the CO2 plume and for fluid leaks in the vicinity of the injection well as well as 3D surface
 seismic surveys to monitor the CO2 plume.

 52

 • The injection wells will be modeled to inject below 80% of the fracture pressure
 corresponding to the shallowest perforation within the well.

7.8 Post-Mitigation Ratings

TCCSP risk workshop identified appropriate post-mitigation strategies for all medium and high
risks based on current state-of-the-art knowledge of mitigation techniques and principles. All
scenarios originally classified as “medium” risk have post-mitigation ratings of four (4) or less,
placing them in the “medium” or “low” risk classification. TCCSP is aware that there is inherent
uncertainty associated with large-scale, long-term geologic projects and will continue to manage
risk over the life of the project to further reduce the currently identified risks and any additional
risks identified in future.

7.9 Contingency Plan

TCCSP will continually evaluate findings from project activities starting with drilling and
construction through operations, maintenance, and public outreach to identify any additional
potential project risks. Any new risk scenarios identified over the course of the project will be
assessed, and prevention and mitigation strategies will be developed and added to the RAM.
Key elements of the project framework directly support and inform the contingency planning
process. The Financial Responsibility Requirements section outlines TCCSP’s approach to
addressing long-term obligations through third-party insurance.
In the Cost Uncertainties section, the approach to managing variability in capital expenditures
is outlined, including the application of a practical cost range (±30 percent for long-lead items and
±15 percent for other components based on vendor feedback and experience. This methodology
supports contingency planning by incorporating financial buffers into cost estimates to address
potential fluctuations in material prices, supply chain disruptions, and inflationary trends.
The Project Critical Path section outlines key dependencies, including regulatory approvals,
procurement of major components, and construction timelines. Delays in permitting, pipeline
development, or delivery of long-lead materials such as compressors and well tubulars may affect
the overall project schedule. To address this, the project schedule includes time contingencies to
limit disruption. Financial safeguards, cost allowances, and schedule buffers allow TCCSP to
manage uncertainty and meet project and regulatory requirements.
8 Project Timeline

8.1 Project Schedule

TCCSP’s commercialization strategy centers on building out infrastructure capable of
compressing, transporting, and injecting 50 Mmt of CO2. The project is phased to ensure technical
and regulatory readiness for large-scale operation.
Phase 1: Site Assessment / Pre-Feasibility
The focus of Phase 1 was the preliminary evaluation of the site’s CO2 storage potential. This
phase involved geologic screening and the identification of potential storage systems. Key
activities included the collection of nearby well data, along with the development of initial

 53

estimates for storage capacity and injectivity. These early assessments provided a foundation for
determining project viability and guiding subsequent phases.
Phase 2: Storage Complex Feasibility
Phase 2 centered on advanced site characterization and the execution of Tasks 1 through 10.
This included expanded subsurface data collection, the drilling and data acquisition from the
TCCSP_OBS-1 stratigraphic well, and the development and submission of the Class VI permit
application. Geologic modeling and simulations were performed to support technical design and
regulatory review. In parallel, techno-economic and risk assessments were conducted, along with
community engagement and outreach activities to inform and involve local stakeholders.
Phase 3: Permitting & Design Development
During Phase 3, the focus will be on technical design, permitting, and regulatory preparedness.
Key activities will include securing land agreements and engineering the CO2 storage field and
transport systems. This phase also involves environmental assessment work under NEPA and
CEQA, securing the Class VI Permit to Construct, and negotiating CO2 supply agreements. These
efforts will ensure the project is ready for full-scale construction.
Phase 4: Construction
Phase 4 emphasizes the build-out of infrastructure and the finalization of regulatory approvals.
Major work includes the construction of surface infrastructure, the CO2 transport system, and the
injection and monitoring wells. Additional efforts focus on securing financing, collecting baseline
environmental and operational data, and refining models. This phase also anticipates receiving
EPA’s Authorization to Inject, marking a key regulatory milestone for the commencement of
injection operations.
Phase 5: Injection Operations (20 Years)
This phase covers the commercial-scale injection of CO2 over an expected 20-year operational
period. During this time, TCCSP will implement continuous geologic and operational monitoring
to track the injected CO2 plume. Modeling and tracking will be regularly updated, with ongoing site
maintenance and periodic reporting to regulatory authorities to ensure compliance and
performance.
Phase 6: Post-Injection Site Care (PISC)
The final phase is focused on long-term monitoring and site closure following the end of injection.
Over the post-injection period, the project will continue to monitor the CO2 plume, maintain site
infrastructure, and submit required regulatory reports. This phase also includes well plugging, site
decommissioning, and the demonstration of non-endangerment to support the formal closure of
the storage site.

8.2 Milestones

Phase 2 of the project was driven by a series of targeted technical, regulatory, and community
engagement milestones that drove the project schedule and advanced progress toward permitting
and construction. Looking forward, Phase 3 will be driven by key permitting milestones including
obtaining the Class VI Permit to Construct from the EPA, which is essential for initiating
development of the CO2 storage facility. Concurrently, the project team will advance permitting
activities for the CO2 transport pipeline, involving coordination with the relevant authorities to

 54

obtain all necessary federal, state, and local approvals. Establishing regulatory certainty for the
pipeline permitting pathway is a key milestone in this phase.
An additional milestone in Phase 3 will be the completion of all required environmental
assessments under both the California Environmental Quality Act (CEQA) and the National
Environmental Policy Act (NEPA) to ensure full environmental compliance. Meanwhile, TCCSP
will work towards solidifying the project's commercial basis by securing commitments from CO2
emitters. These agreements are critical to ensure that the project captures sufficient volumes of
CO2 to support the storage system's operational and economic viability. Additionally, the project
will focus on securing land agreements and rights-of-way for both the storage site and the pipeline
corridor.
Phase 4 marks a significant transition from planning to execution, with a primary focus on the
construction of project infrastructure. Milestones of this phase include the finalization of equipment
and tender specifications for all critical systems, including the CO2 capture and transport system
and the injection and monitoring well infrastructure at the storage site. Construction of the capture,
transport, and storage infrastructure constitutes another major milestone of this phase, along with
validation and receiving EPA’s Authorization to Inject to move into Phase 5 for injection operations.

8.3 Project Critical Path

TCCSP’s project development schedule depends on alignment across permitting and
construction activities, each of which poses risks to the project’s critical path if delayed. The timing
of the EPA Class VI Permit to Construct and Authorization to Inject are crucial schedule points,
with delays in their issuance affecting construction schedules and site development in Phase 4.
Similarly, pipeline permitting is a key dependency and must align with subsequent infrastructure
installation plans, with any delays pushing construction off-schedule.
Long lead items such as well tubulars, compressors, and pipeline materials for construction can
also extend the project timeline by several months depending on their material grade and country
of origin, or if procurement is not initiated early enough. These items are essential for well and
capture system construction, and delays will cascade through the construction and
commissioning phases. Therefore, built-in time contingencies in the project schedule for these
long-lead components are critical to avoiding disruptions.
Economic feasibility remains an overarching risk with shifts in capital costs, inflation, or policy
incentives potentially impacting the financial viability of the project and timing of investment
decisions. These elements of the project’s critical path make up the backbone of the project
schedule and are buffered with contingencies to safeguard against schedule delays.

TCCSP has prepared the legal easement agreements necessary to access land within the project

maintenance, monitoring, and eventual closure of the project.
The agreements establish several easement rights. They include an exclusive easement in the
subsurface sequestration zone, granting TCCSP sole rights to inject, store, and manage CO2
within the designated formations. In addition, TCCSP is granted easement rights to access
between the surface of the property and the sequestration zone. This includes the ability to drill
wells, inject or remove CO2, and install, operate, and maintain all necessary equipment, materials,

 58

and infrastructure required. The agreements also provide for surface access, including ingress
and egress across the property, for construction, operation, and maintenance activities. This
encompasses the right to build and maintain roads, pipelines, fences, buildings, utility lines, and
other related improvements, as well as to conduct testing, monitoring, and any corrective actions
deemed necessary by TCCSP or regulatory authorities.
The easements explicitly state that they do not dedicate any portion of the property for public use.

natural gas, or other hydrocarbons; however, TCCSP retains the right to withdraw fluids and gases
from the sequestration zone as necessary to support the injection and sequestration process. The
landowners acknowledge that, based on current information, no commercially producible
hydrocarbon reserves exist within the sequestration zone. As such, the landowners waive any
potential right to terminate the easement based on future discoveries that contradict this
understanding. Furthermore, if any mineral rights within the area are held or leased by parties
other than TCCSP, they are obligated to avoid any unreasonable interference with those third-
party rights.

11.1 Justice40 Plan

The TCCSP project team conducted an Energy and Environmental Justice Assessment (EEJA)
to evaluate how the distribution of environmental benefits and burdens affects communities
surrounding TCCSP, with a focus on the socioeconomic conditions of disadvantaged populations.
This assessment incorporates both quantitative and qualitative research methods to ensure that
historically marginalized communities are equitably included in the project’s benefits. Findings
from the EEJA have informed the development of the Justice40 Plan which outlines the specific
metrics, milestones, and resources required to implement the Justice40 strategy effectively, with
the goal of directing at least 40% of project benefits to local communities. It serves as a framework
for tracking progress toward Justice40 goals and includes key performance indicators such as job
creation, community engagement, and environmental impacts. Additionally, the plan identifies
information gaps, risks, opportunities, and barriers related to the equitable distribution of benefits.
During Phase II of the CarbonSAFE initiative, the TCCSP team focused on developing the
Justice40 Plan, identifying potential benefits and disbenefits, and strategizing ways to ensure that
project outcomes align with Justice40 goals. This included evaluating how the project could
deliver tangible benefits to disadvantaged communities while minimizing potential negative
impacts. As the project moves into Phases III and IV, the focus shifts to gathering baseline data
and testing the framework and strategies developed in Phase II. These efforts will help refine the
Justice40 approach and ensure it is responsive to community needs and grounded in measurable
outcomes.
11.1.1 Phase II Justice40 Efforts
During Phase II of the CarbonSAFE, the TCCSP team developed the Justice40 Plan, refining the
strategy and resources needed to implement the initiative while identifying the project’s potential
benefits and disbenefits. TCCSP identified a range of potential benefits that will be directed toward
and maximized for nearby communities, particularly those that may be designated as
disadvantaged. These benefits include the potential reduction of CO2 emissions and the
development of a workforce action plan related to construction and clean energy jobs.
At the same time, the project has acknowledged potential disbenefits to disadvantaged
communities located near the TCCSP site. These impacts have been categorized by timeframes
to support targeted mitigation strategies. Short-term disbenefits (expected to occur over a period
of less than two years) include increased traffic and road congestion, elevated generator and
tailpipe emissions, and higher levels of dust and noise pollution during construction and related
activities. Long-term disbenefits (those potentially occurring over five years or more) may include
the use of limited natural resources, potential contamination of USDWs, atmospheric leakage of
fugitive CO2, and the risk of asphyxiation in the event of a catastrophic leak.
To address these impacts, the TCCSP project team, including ARI and Visage Energy, developed
a framework as part of the Justice40 strategy. This framework outlines a clear methodology for
identifying quantifying, tracking, and reporting impacts from construction through operations. It
supports targeted mitigation planning and aims to lessen negative outcomes for affected
communities. The framework is structured around key milestones across the project’s life cycle to
help maximize community benefits while minimizing potential harm:

 61

 1) Establish Project Baseline Conditions (Construction)
 2) Launch Ongoing Monitoring Efforts (Operations)
 3) Implement Project Data Communication Strategy
 4) Conduct Ongoing Information Gap Analyses
 5) Execute Stakeholder Engagement and Integrate Community Feedback
 6) Conduct EEJA Reassessments and Updates

The Justice40 framework combines environmental, economic, and community health monitoring
with active community engagement and clear communication to ensure equitable distribution of
benefits, supporting both technical objectives and community priorities in line with Justice40
milestones. Engaging with stakeholders is a top priority, with clear communication channels, data-
sharing tools, and feedback loops to make sure community input is heard and acted on. The
project will establish baseline conditions for air, water, and soil quality, and use adaptive
management to address any potential impacts on the environment. At the same time, economic
and workforce programs will focus on supporting local communities through hiring, supplier
diversity, and training opportunities. More information on the Justice40 strategy developed for
TCCSP can be found in the Justice40 Strategy and Planning Report.

To support environmental, social, and economic monitoring, TCCSP will also prepare for

including environmental indicators and socioeconomic metrics. Standards for data collection,

engagement with Pixley Town Hall and other interested parties will continue as the project moves
forward. Additionally, the TCCSP project team is interested in continuing and potentially
expanding the Climate in the Classroom program to include more educators and students, as well
as ways to expand access to field trip opportunities to see green energy in action.
The project team may explore hosting additional CEWs, if feedback from project stakeholders
indicates that they would be of value. Currently, “engagement fatigue” from those who may
participate in such events means that engagement would be limited and thus of limited value.
The TCCSP project team continues to explore opportunities with local higher education
institutions, such as Porterville College, to engage students and educators on the project and
related green energy enterprises. This type of engagement often takes time to establish,
particularly navigating logistical obstacles such as transportation for students and integration into
existing established programs.

11.3 Diversity, Equity, Inclusion, and Accessibility Plan

The Tulare County Carbon Sequestration Project (TCCSP) recognizes that a robust Diversity,
Equity, Inclusion, and Accessibility (DEIA) strategy is essential to advancing equitable outcomes
for historically underserved communities and ensuring broad participation in the economic and
workforce opportunities created by the project. This DEIA Plan addresses barriers to participation,
expands access for underrepresented groups, and fosters a culture of inclusion throughout all
phases of project implementation.
Building on Phase II efforts, TCCSP has developed comprehensive frameworks for workforce
development, workplace training, and organizational culture assessment to guide implementation
and meet regulatory expectations. These efforts reflect a proactive approach to embedding DEIA
principles into TCCSP’s operations and ensuring accountability to both project stakeholders and
affected communities.
The initial DEIA strategy leveraged partnerships with the SocioEnvironmental and Education
Network (SEEN) and Visage Energy, which have supported engagement with disadvantaged
communities (DACs), stakeholder education, and the development of targeted community benefit
strategies.

11.3.1 Phase II DEIA Efforts
TCCSP committed to promoting inclusive business partnerships during Phase II. The project team
conducted an inventory of Minority Business Enterprises (MBEs), Women-Owned Businesses
(WBEs), and Veteran-Owned Businesses (VOBs), and developed a procedure (led by Visage

 66

Energy) to engage with MBEs, WBEs, and VOBs for contractor needs. This list and procedure
were utilized during drilling procurement.
TCCSP’s workforce plan focuses on increasing access to quality jobs for all residents, including
disadvantaged residents, individuals with disabilities, returning citizens, opportunity youth, and
veterans. The plan emphasizes equitable workforce development and addresses systemic
barriers to community participation. The TCCSP team has engaged with local educational
institutions, such as Porterville College, to identify opportunities for internship programs.
Additionally, the TCCSP team implemented the Climate in the Classroom program (see CSE
section, above) to expose pre-college students to green careers and opportunities.
TCCSP also conducted a project-wide “culture and climate” survey to assess staff member views
on DEIA within the project. Overall, respondents felt generally supported in the dimensions of
DEIA culture and climate assessed, indicating that TCCSP has done reasonably well in providing
an environment where participants feel supported. Respondents felt that there was a need for
more professional development and training, indicating an area of growth for future phases.
