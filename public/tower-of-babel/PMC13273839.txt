Identification and Optimization of Risk for Stroke With Abdominal Obesity Patients: Insights From NHANES 2005–2018
Authors: Lei Zhou, Mengyang He, Feng Xiao, Qixing Liu, Xuezhen Li, Binghua He, Xiangang Tan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveStroke is the leading cause of disability worldwide, and it is now estimated that one in four individuals may experience a stroke during their lifetime. Early detection and rapid access to treatment can save lives and improve recovery. This study aimed to identify and characterize the potential influencing factors in patients with obesity who have had strokes.MethodsThe research screened the 2005–2018 NHANES database and analyzed potential risk factors in eligible stroke patients with abdominal obesity using 10 machine model learning. Multivariable‐adjusted least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression, restricted cubic spline (RCS) analysis, and Shapley Additive Explanations (SHAP) plots were used to identify important risk factors for obese individuals who have experienced strokes.ResultsThe 8764 eligible individuals were divided into training set (6,134) and validation set (2,630) for predictive model development. In addition, the random forest model achieved the highest performance in predicting stroke incidence (area under the curve: 0.823) and all‐cause mortality (area under the curve: 0.741). The SHAP values showed that age was the highest predictor followed by hypertension, diabetes, heart failure, smoking history, alcohol use, total cholesterol (TC), TyG‐BMI, and cardiovascular artery disease (CDAI).ConclusionsTyG‐BMI, CDAI, and TC are innovative and clinically viable predictive biomarkers for stroke in patients with abdominal obesity, exhibiting age‐ and gender‐specific effects that are particularly pronounced in elderly females. These findings provide an evidence‐based basis for personalized stroke risk assessment and targeted prevention strategies in the growing abdominally obese population.

Introduction

Stroke, also known as a cerebrovascular accident, is the leading cause of disability and second greatest cause of death worldwide, imposing a severe burden on public health systems due to its elevated incidence, disability rate, and economic costs [1, 2]. According to the 2023 Update of Heart Disease and Stroke Statistics from the American Heart Association, approximately 795,000 people in the United States experience a new or recurrent stroke annually, with 610,000 representing first‐time occurrences. The total economic burden of stroke, including healthcare services, pharmaceutical expenses, and work absenteeism, reached $56.2 billion 2019 to 2020 [1]. Notably, the 2022 Global Stroke Factsheet reported that the lifetime risk of stroke has increased by 50% over the past 17 years; from 1990 to 2019, the incidence, mortality, prevalence, and disability‐adjusted life years (DALYs) associated with stroke escalated by 70%, 43%, 102%, and 143%, respectively [2, 3]. This burden is disproportionately concentrated in low‐ and lower‐middle‐income countries, where resource‐poor households face unprecedented health and economic challenges [2].

According to the World Obesity Federation, more than 1 billion people worldwide are currently suffering from obesity, and more than half of the world's population will face overweight and obesity by 2035 [4]. A cross‐sectional study of 15.8 million Chinese adults by Chen et al. [5] demonstrated that the number of complications in obese populations significantly escalates with increasing BMI.

While traditional risk factors such as hypertension, smoking, unhealthy diet, alcohol consumption, and dyslipidemia have been widely recognized in clinical practice, the mechanisms underlying insulin resistance (IR) as an emerging risk factor require further exploration [6, 7].

Additionally, the Composite Dietary Antioxidant Index (CDAI), a valid tool for evaluating total dietary antioxidant capacity, includes six dietary antioxidants (vitamins A, C, E, manganese, selenium, and zinc) [8]. However, the studies regarding the correlation between CDAI and stroke have yielded conflicting results: Hantikainen et al. [9] observed a reduced stroke risk with higher CDAI in a Swedish female cohort, Colarusso et al. [10] reported no such association, and Del Rio et al. [11] only confirmed a negative correlation between CDAI and ischemic stroke risk in an Italian sample.

The purpose of the research was to explore and verify the application value of new markers in patients with obesity who have had strokes and provided evidence‐based evidence for developing individualized clinical stroke prevention strategies. The analysis used cross‐sectional data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) from 2005 to 2018 to construct a prediction model of stroke risk and mortality for patients with abdominal obesity through 10 machine learning algorithms.

Method

Study Design and Population

The research was a retrospective study based on the NHANES database from 2005 to 2018 (https://www.cdc.gov/nchs/nhanes/). The NHANES study protocol was approved by the NCHS Research Ethics Review Board and all participants provided written informed consent. The criteria for inclusion were as follows: (1) those with complete data on covariates (race, marital status, education) and on sampling weights; (2) those with complete baseline data on underlying diseases (e.g., stroke, hypertension, diabetes mellitus, etc.); (3) those with complete data on alimentary intake (e.g., TG, Vitamin E, Vitamin B2, and fiber, etc.); and (4) those who were older than 18 years and had been diagnosed with abdominal obesity. A total of 8764 abdominally obese stroke patients were included in the final analysis.

Definitions of Abdominal Obesity, TyG, TyG‐WC, TyG‐BMI, VAI, LAP, CDAI

Abdominal obesity was defined as waist circumference ≥ 88 cm and ≥ 102 cm for women and men, respectively [12]. The TyG Index was a measure of insulin resistance that combines fasting glucose and triglyceride levels. TyG, TyG‐WC, TyG‐BMI, VAI, LAP, and CDAI were calculated based on values from laboratory tests such as WC, BMI, TG, and HDL‐C. The specific calculation formulas can be found in the article of the research team [13].

Covariates

Demographic information, other lifestyle factors such as dietary factors, and self‐reported past medical history were collected by standardized questionnaires at the start of the study. Glycated fasting plasma glucose hemoglobin (HbAlc), total cholesterol (TC), TG, and HDL‐C values were among the baseline laboratory tests. Further details on variable assessment and classification can be found in Supporting Information S1: STable1.

Survival Outcome

The survival outcomes were all‐cause mortality, which were sourced from the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention website (https://wwwn.cdc.gov/nchs/nhanes/Default.aspx) and were updated until December 31, 2023. The causes of mortality were identified using the tenth‐vision International Statistical Classification of Diseases and Related Health Problems.

Data Analysis

All continuous variables were expressed as medians (Q1‐Q3), and comparisons between groups were conducted using the independent samples t‐test or the Kruskal‐Wallis rank sum test. Categorical variables were expressed as percentages, and the chi‐square test or Fisher exact test was used to detect differences between groups. The analysis used NHANES sample weights in logistic regression analysis to obtain nationally representative estimates. All values were weighted using sampling weights provided by NHANES. A two‐sided p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Simultaneously, the RCS model was employed to explore the dose‐response relationship between the selected variables under consideration and patients with obesity who have had strokes. The analysis filtered the optimal predictor variables from the risk factors by LASSO binomial regression [14].

Construction of the Machine Learning Model

The dataset was randomly divided into two subsets: a training set (70%, N = 6134) and a testing set (30%, N = 2630). Multicategorical variables were converted to one‐hot variables. We used randomly assigned training and test sets to apply nine machine learning algorithms to predict the occurrence of stroke in patients with obesity who have had strokes. A comprehensive introduction to these specific machine learning algorithms can be found in another article of ours [13]. These models were implemented using the DecisionLinnc1.0 software [15]. Supporting Information S1: STable2 provided the specific hyperparameter configurations.

Evaluation of Machine Learning Model

The receiver operating characteristic curve (ROC), area under the subject operating curve (AUC), sensitivity/recall, specificity, Matthews correlation coefficient (MCC), and F1 scores were used to evaluate the performance and accuracy of the nine machine models [13]. A sensitivity study employing k‐fold cross‐validation was conducted to evaluate the robustness of the prediction model's performance. To enhance the application of model results to the understanding of real clinical scenarios, the analysis performed a threshold effect analysis based on the generalized additive model.

Shapley Additive Explanations (SHAP) Method

The analysis used SHAP to evaluate the significance of the predictor variables [16]. The SHAP plot was a method for interpreting machine learning models based on a game theoretic approach, which helped to understand the model decision‐making process by calculating SHAP values to quantify the contribution of each feature to the model predictions. The SHAP bee plot illustrated the SHAP values corresponding to each characteristic across all samples, with purple indicating elevated characteristic values and yellow indicating lower characteristic values. SHAP bar charts were produced to illustrate the common effects of different features on predictions related to patients with obesity who have had strokes.

The analysis used RCS regression to further explore the correlation between TC, TyG‐BMI and CDAI in relation to patients with obesity who have had strokes. RCS regression was adjusted for age, race, education, marital and gender.

The analysis explored whether there were potential age differences in the influence of these factors on patients with obesity who have had strokes. In the 18–40 age group, there was no nonlinear correlation between CDAI (p non‐linear = 0.323), TC (p non‐linear = 0.731), TyG‐BMI (p non‐linear = 0.966) and patients with obesity who have had strokes (Figure 2A–C). There was no nonlinear correlation between CDAI (p non‐linear = 0.793) and TC (p non‐linear = 0.751) and patients with obesity who have had strokes in the 40–60 age group (Figure 2D,E). There was a linear correlation between TyG‐BMI (p overall = 0.042) and patients with obesity who have had strokes (Figure 2F). Notably, the nonlinear correlations between CDAI (p non‐linear = 0.029), TC (p non‐linear = 0.008) and patients with obesity who have had strokes remained significant in the 60–80 age group (Figure 2G,H). However, there was a linear correlation between TyG‐BMI (p overall < 0.001) and patients with obesity who have had strokes (Figure 2F).

Model Decision of SHAP

The SHAP plot of the Random Forest model is shown in Figure 4. The SHAP plot showed an overview of the factors that influence the increase or decrease in the risk of stroke in patients with obesity. Based on the SHAP plot, the CDAI, TC and TyG‐BMI had comparatively more influence on the model.

Age was the most influential predictor, followed by a history of hypertension, heart failure, diabetes, TC, CDAI, TyG‐BMI, smoking history, and alcohol. The SHAP bee plot (Figure 4B) illustrated that the color gradient of the horizontal bars corresponded to the magnitude of the change, which ranged from purple (high) to yellow (low).

Discussion

The analysis leveraged 10 machine learning algorithms to construct a stroke risk prediction model for abdominally obese individuals based on NHANES 2005–2018 data, identifying three novel predictive biomarkers—TyG‐BMI, CDAI, and TC—alongside established risk factors (age, hypertension, diabetes, heart failure, smoking, and alcohol consumption).

Notably, the random forest model achieved superior performance in predicting stroke occurrence (AUC = 0.823) and all‐cause mortality (AUC = 0.741), and SHAP analysis confirmed the independent predictive value of TyG‐BMI, CDAI, and TC. These findings not only validated the utility of composite metabolic and dietary indices in stroke risk stratification but also highlighted age‐ and gender‐specific effects, particularly among elderly females, thereby addressing critical gaps in existing literature.

TyG‐BMI was validated as a simple, powerful, and clinically valuable surrogate marker that simultaneously captured multiple clinical variables such as BMI, glucose, and lipids [17]. The prospective cohort study conducted by Shao et al. illustrated that elevated TyG‐BMI was significantly and positively correlated with the risk of stroke in a middle‐aged and elderly Chinese population [7]. This was consistent with the results of the research. In the RCS analysis, a linear positive association was observed between TyG‐BMI and obese stroke patients in women over 60 years of age. Although the exact mechanism between TyG‐BMI and stroke risk remains unclear, it may be associated with insulin resistance (IR).

Initially, IR was closely associated with inflammatory markers that accelerate the formation of atherosclerosis and can stimulate the release of pro‐inflammatory markers [18, 19]. Second, in the presence of hyperinsulinemia, the function of foam cells and endothelial cells involved in atherosclerosis is disturbed, which also predisposed to the formation of atherosclerotic pathology [20]. Third, IR may alter platelet function, resulting in enhanced adhesion, activation, and aggregation, potentially causing arterial stenosis or blockage, which leads to stroke [21, 22]. Fourth, IR may promote atherosclerosis formation and advanced plaque progression. Changes in the arterial wall lead to narrowing, blockage, or rupture of the lumen, and this impaired blood flow may lead to ischemia and hypoxia in brain tissue, leading to the possibility of stroke [23, 24].

There is inconsistency in the association between TC and stroke. Findings from both extensive prospective and cohort studies indicated that TC was strongly associated with increased risk of ischemic stroke [25, 26]. Conversely, some research did not establish a correlation between TC and the risk of ischemic stroke [27, 28]. The study indicated that TC was markedly linked to a negative nonlinear correlation among obese female stroke patients over the age of 60.

The mechanisms underlying the association between TC and stroke are unclear. However, the results of clinical trials showed that the risk of stroke in patients with cardiovascular diseases was significantly reduced after the statins to reduce cholesterol levels [29, 30, 31, 32]. A meta‐analysis of 11 clinical trials of 20,163 stroke patients from 1970 to 2021 found that intensive LDL‐C‐lowering statin‐based therapy reduced the risk of ischemic stroke recurrence compared with less intensive LDL‐C‐lowering statin therapy [33]. Reduced cholesterol levels may play a role in promoting smooth muscle cell necrosis in the middle layer of arteries. Microaneurysms were more prevalent in instances of vascular endothelial injury. In contrast, microaneurysms were the main pathological manifestation of cerebral hemorrhage, resulting in stroke [34, 35, 36, 37].

The analysis found a negative non‐linear correlation between CDAI and patients with obesity who have had strokes. The mechanism between CDAI and stroke was mostly related to oxidative stress, neurological damage and inflammation. The non‐enzymatic antioxidants contained in CDAI significantly contributed to mitigating oxidative stress. CDAI as a dietary antioxidant not only reduced the production of reactive oxygen species and protected LDL from oxidative damage, reducing the state of atherosclerosis [38, 39]. It can also effectively eliminate oxygen free radicals and protect the cerebrovascular system by enhancing endothelial function, reducing platelet aggregation, lowering blood pressure, and alleviating inflammatory responses [40, 41, 42].

The model of patients with obesity who have had strokes constructed by CDAI, TyG‐BMI and TC deserved serious attention. However, there are some limitations to our study. Although the results of the developed prediction model were promising, additional prospective investigations are required for future validation. Second, the interpretive properties of the model were more applicable to American and European populations. Third, the risk variables derived from the results of the study do not predict causal associations, and further application of the results needs to be cautious.

Conclusion

Our findings identified the association between CDAI, TyG‐BMI, and TC with patients with obesity who have had strokes, which was more significant among the older female population. These biomarkers can be properly applied in the future to reduce the risk of clinical stroke in patients with obesity who have had strokes, hence informing the development of individualized treatment strategies. It also provided important evidence for future large‐scale prospective studies.
