Sedation is not sleep: changes in airway physiology during deep sedation and the duty to rescue
Authors: Seungoh Kim
Published: 2026

INTRODUCTION

A recently reported death during implant treatment under sedation in the Republic of Korea has renewed concerns about the safety of the so-called “sleep dentistry” [1,2]. Although the circumstances and causal relationships remain under investigation, this event highlights a misconception. Sedation is not ordinary sleep. Instead, a patient who appears to be sleeping may be progressing into deep sedation, upper airway obstruction, hypoventilation, or loss of protective airway reflexes [3]. Therefore, every dental institution providing sedation must monitor patients rigorously, recognize deterioration early, and ensure that the dentist administering the sedation can directly rescue patients whose sedation becomes deeper than intended [4].

This concern is important because intravenous sedation is rapidly expanding in Korean dentistry. Nationwide data from 2020 to 2024 show a continuous increase in sedative use, with most administration occurring in dental clinics [5]. Midazolam remained the most commonly used agent; however, propofol and ketamine use also increased, with ketamine showing rapid growth. Propofol can induce deep sedation or general anesthesia when administered beyond the range required for moderate sedation [3]. Ketamine may preserve spontaneous respiration when used alone; however, when combined with benzodiazepines, propofol, or opioids, the sedation depth and respiratory risk may become less predictable [4]. Therefore, the increase is not merely quantitative. The greater use of agents and combinations capable of producing deeper anesthesia increases the likelihood of unintended deep sedation and demands stronger monitoring and rescue capabilities.

Understanding the airway pathophysiology during sedation explains why deterioration may occur suddenly. Deep sedation suppresses the upper airway dilator muscles, including the genioglossus and geniohyoid muscles, allowing the tongue and pharyngeal soft tissues to move posteriorly. Because airway resistance increases sharply as the airway radius decreases, even a modest narrowing can increase breathing work. A stronger inspiratory effort then generates more negative intrathoracic pressure, further collapsing the compliant pharynx through a paradoxical Starling resistor mechanism [6]. Therefore, snoring or partial obstruction may rapidly progress to paradoxical breathing, the absence of airflow, hypoxemia, and cardiac arrest.

Dental treatments are associated with unique risks. Blood, saliva, irrigation fluid, instruments, and dental materials may cause obstruction, aspiration, or laryngospasm, whereas intraoral procedures limit airway access. Pulse oximetry alone may not detect hypoventilation early, particularly when supplemental oxygen is administered. Therefore, continuous assessment of ventilation, including capnography, is essential [4].

The central principle is the duty of rescuers. Clinicians administering sedation must be capable of managing sedation levels that are deeper than intended. Safe practices require appropriate patient selection; airway assessment; careful drug titration; dedicated monitoring personnel; and immediate access to suction, oxygen, bag-mask ventilation, airway devices, reversal agents, and defibrillation [3]. All team members should receive regular training in basic and dental advanced life support. Monitoring must be continued during recovery because residual drug effects may cause recurrent airway obstruction after treatment.

Terminology is also important. Expressions such as “sleep dentistry” or “painless sleep treatment” may lead patients to underestimate the risk and view sedation as a convenience rather than a high-risk medical intervention. The future of dental sedation depends not only on wider availability, but also on accurate communication, standardized protocols, vigilant monitoring, and an unquestioned ability to rescue every patient.
