 B. OVERALL ACCOMPLISHMENTS

 B.1 WHAT ARE THE MAJOR GOALS OF THE PROJECT?

 The National User Resource for Biological Accelerator Mass Spectrometry (User Resource) will provide isotopic analysis (primarily
 radiocarbon or 14C) by accelerator mass spectrometry (AMS) for NIH- funded researchers across the United States and will be the
 only User Resource of its type in the United States. The User Resource will provide measurement capability and expertise to a
 research community that requires highly sensitive, quantitative isotope analyses. Since commissioning a new accelerator mass
 spectrometer in June 2014, we have measured over 4000 samples a year for collaborators and service users. The User Resource
 will enable us to continue to meet these research needs, as well as provide for new users whose research programs would benefit
 from AMS as a measurement tool. The User Resource’s forte will be ultra-high sensitivity quantitation of radiocarbon and selected
 other radioisotopes for research studies where isotopes are required. Radioisotope labeling studies have been and will continue to
 be an important tool for addressing many complex biomedical science problems. AMS is a specialized and unique type of mass
 spectrometry that provides absolute quantitation of radiocarbon and other relevant radioisotopes with extreme sensitivity, having
 limits of detection in real samples on the order of a few attomol/mg of sample at measurement precisions of ~3%. It is the only
 instrumental method capable of quantifying radioisotope-labeled agents routinely in real-world samples with such precision and
 sensitivity. The sensitivity of AMS allows for the quantification of radiolabeled metabolites in extremely complex matrices of cells
 and organisms at very low concentrations and in small samples. AMS allows studies to be conducted without perturbing
 metabolism leading to more relevant quantification of metabolic rates and pathways. In addition, it enables quantification of
 pharmacokinetic and metabolic properties of toxicants at environmentally relevant concentrations in model systems as well as the
 ability to quantify pharmacokinetics and other molecular endpoints directly in humans. Such quantitative assessments can 1)
 improve risk assessment for toxicants, 2) address safety and efficacy considerations for therapeutic entities, 3) deepen
 understanding of xenobiotic and intermediary metabolism, 4) help understand the interactions between critical molecular
 pathways, and 5) improve efforts to model and predict various metabolic and biological states. These capabilities have been
 applied in a number of areas including research in carcinogenesis, toxicology, nutrition, pharmacology/drug development and
 basic biological science. As a NIGMS National Resource the National User Resource for Biological Accelerator Mass Spectrometry
 will help NIH funded scientists achieve a deeper understanding of the etiology of human health concerns by (1) enabling the
 quantification of pharmacokinetics and other molecular endpoints directly in humans; (2) offering the ability to conduct
 quantitative studies using biologics such as proteins or lipids, and thereby reducing the amount of radioisotope usage in
 biomedical labs; and (3) enabling more relevant studies of metabolic pathways in health and disease through the use of much
 lower, more biologically-relevant, concentrations of metabolic substrates in cells and intact organisms. Such studies support
 NIGMS’s basic biomedical research areas that contribute to the understanding of fundamental cellular and physiological principles
 and enable research supported by the Biophysics, Biomedical Technology, and Computational Biosciences (BBCB); Genetics and
 Molecular, Cellular, and Developmental Biology (GMCDB); Pharmacology, Physiology, Biological Chemistry (PPBC) and Training,
 Workforce Development, and Diversity (TWD) Divisions. Over the next five years, our goals are to: 1. Improve the efficiency of
 operation for AMS measurements through installation of new interfaces to our AMS systems, technical modifications to improve
 gas accepting ion source efficiency and upgrading our data analysis software for improved ease of use and data reporting. 2.
 Increase the accessibility and visibility of ultra-sensitive 14C measurements for the biomedical research community by training of
 new investigators and expanding our national user base. 3. Provide high throughput, ultra-sensitive 14C analysis for the NIGMS
 and NIH user community.

 B.2 Accomplishments

 The website for the Resource was under revision during late summer of 2025 and updates will
 go live fall 2025.

 The purchase and installation of the individual sample combustion interface for CO2 gas
 injection to the ion source has moved forward this year after prolonged delay. The vendor,
 National Electrostatics Corporation (NEC), delivered the interface in November 2023 and was
 installed on the SSAMS in December 2023. Control software was not included with the unit and
 was installed March 2024. The operation manual was incomplete, so Dr. Ted Ognibene has
 been developing a standard operating procedure (SOP) for its use. In June 2024 test samples
 were combusted, CO2 trapped, CO2 released, flow regulated, gas sample ionized and
 measured by SSAMS. During the most recent reporting period 9/1/24-8/31/25, Dr. Ognibene
 established sample measurement parameters to minimize sample memory. Numerous
 interactions with the manufacturer instigated improvements in computer control of the system
 but it was not yet able to operate in unattended mode at the end of August 2025.

 We secured internal LLNL funding to replace and upgrade the LC and molecular MS of the
 parallel accelerator and molecular mass spectrometry (PAMMS) configuration. A Thermo
 Vanquish UHPLC and Thermo Orbitrap Eclipse mass spectrometer was installed after our
 previous annual report and was fully operational fall 2024.

 LLNL allows graduate students or postdocs from other institutions into laboratories following site
 and laboratory specific training. Three new projects were added during 2025 and one project
 was retired to bring the number of projects to 44 at the end August 2025. Additionally, we
 performed several feasibility study or preliminary data collection measurement sessions for
 external users submitting proposals to NIH. These sessions are small investments in time but
 provide crucial information for proposal submission. Training new users has been handled
 through email exchanges, sharing of protocols, phone conversations, and online meetings in the
 past year. Hands on training for sample prep in our labs is always preferable. Two graduate
 students from UC Davis have student visitor appointments at LLNL and will be complete their
 own sample prep as part of graduate training. We hosted one summer intern from UC Davis.
 We have always conducted remote training of experimental design (appropriate controls and
 backgrounds) and sample preparation for users across the country and around the world and
 anticipate the practice to continue.

 User Resource staff chaired and presented at a Hot Topics Symposium “Impacts and
 Implications of Wildfire Smoke on the Lung-Brain Axis” at the Society of Toxicology Meeting in
         March 2025.

 User Resource staff hosted a general information booth at the American Association of
 Pharmaceutical Scientists Meeting in October 2025.

 The BioAMS facility has been available for measurements throughout the year. Metrics
 regarding the usage of the user resource are found in section G.1.

 User Survey Summary

Final RPPR Page 6

 B.2 (B2 accomplishments 2025.pdf)

 A survey on interactions with the Resource was sent to all project PIs when requesting input for
 the annual report. Half of projects completed the survey, and a summary of the replies follows,
 including scoring and comments. Formal check-ins with each project PI occur at least annually
 and, in most cases, semi-annually. When asked about desired frequency of check-ins nearly all
 PIs preferred the current annual check-ins prior to completion of the annual report.

 When asked, “Are you satisfied with the response times when contacting the BioAMS
 Resource?’ and given a 1 to 5 scoring choice with 1 “not satisfied” and 5 “very satisfied”, the PIs
 unanimously selected 5 very satisfied. No comments were submitted on how to improve
 response. PIs were also pleased with timeliness of data reports. When asked to respond to a 1
 to 5 scale on data reporting with 1 “not timely” and 5 “very timely”, the average response was
 4.85. Data reports were generally provided within 2-4 weeks of submitting samples and
 stretched up to 8-12 weeks when the sample queue became large from hundreds of samples
 being submitted at once.

 Explanations of data results were also unanimously rated 5 out of 5 by PIs. Each project has a
 specific person-of-contact on the resource staff. When asked, “Do you find the BioAMS website
 a useful resource for information?” the average response was 4.95 out of 5 on a scale from 1
 “not useful” to 5 “very useful.” The website was revised in May 2024 to be ADA compliant and
 improve readability.

 Projects that sent team members (grad student or postdoc) to LLNL to participate in sample
 prep universally found the experience helpful in understanding why we follow specific
 procedures and insist on certain types of controls. They also gained a better understanding of
 how isotope ratio measurements are converted into biologically relevant units. We strongly
 encouraged all projects visit the Resource if possible.

 B.4 Professional Development and Training

 Dr. David Baliu-Rodriguez continued training by Dr. Ted Ognibene in maintenance and
 troubleshooting the AMS instrument. He is also primary operator of the Thermo
 molecular mass spectrometer associated with the PAMMS configuration and oversees
 its maintenance. We have three frequent AMS operators (Dr. Ted Ognibene, Dr. Baliu-
 Rodriguez, Esther Ubick) to insure continuous operation is maintained.

 Several external project PIs requested informal general BioAMS seminars for group
 meetings at their institutions in response to a question on our user survey. We schedule
 these to fit the needs of each group.

 Training new users has been handled through email exchanges, sharing of protocols,
 phone calls, and online meetings. Hands on training for sample prep in our labs is
 always preferable but often impractical for projects beyond commute distance from
 Livermore. We have always conducted remote training of experimental design
 (appropriate controls and backgrounds) and sample preparation for users across the
 country and around the world and anticipate the practice to continue. This training is
 generally done on a case-by-case basis after trainees have completed the online
 BioAMS course on our website. Morgan Domanico, a graduate student of Dr. Laura Van
 Winkle at University of California, Davis had a student visitor appointment at LLNL to
 complete naphthalene adduct research. Dr. Domanico completed all sample prep on
 her samples as part of her graduate training and graduated with her Ph.D. in December
 2024. Angela Encerrado, a graduate student of Dr. Sascha Nicklisch at UC Davis also
 has a student visitor appointment at LLNL. Angela is studying the interaction of
 chemicals and disease vectors in insects for potential impacts to human health,
 especially in a warming climate in California’s major agricultural areas. Ms. Encerrado
 completed all bee micro-colony exposures at LLNL and performed sample prep
 activities. She is expected to graduate with her Ph.D. in spring 2026. We hosted a
 student summer intern July-September 2025 who learned basic AMS sample prep and
 histology working with User Resource 47 examining deposition of wildfire particulates
 and biological responses.

 Use of Individual Development Plans (IDPs): the User resource currently has no
 graduate students or post-doctoral fellows being directly supported be the grant. No
 graduate students or post-doctoral fellows have been directly supported by the grant
 during the reporting period. LLNL has all post-doctoral fellows complete an IDP that
 includes a mentoring plan that assesses goals of the postdoc, describes short- and
 long-term career objectives, and identifies professional development activities needed
 to reach them. This IDP for each post-doctoral fellow is regularly discussed and updated
 with their mentor and post-doctoral coordinator every few months to assist them with
 their career development.

 C.5.b Resource Sharing

 Progress implementing the resource sharing plan:

 Data Sharing Plan. The purpose of this Resource was to make biomedical AMS accessible to the scientific community for use in
 investigations that require ultra-high sensitivity quantitation when isotope-labeled agents
 are used. All investigators working as part of this Resource are committed to the timely release and sharing of final research data,
 as well as the protection of the rights and privacy of all research subjects. All requests for data developed as part of a
 collaboration will be referred to the appropriate project PI for release.
 Software Sharing Plan. Data analysis programs developed and used by this User Resource are made available to users upon
 request. Executable versions compatible with the MacOS and Windows PC operating
 systems are freely available and can be used without restriction. While there is no plan, at present, to provide source code, users
 have the ability to request modifications, which are reviewed by Resource staff and, if possible, implemented into future releases.
 It is anticipated that updates will be released on a regular schedule and will be made available and published on the Resource
 website, following LLNL Information Management Review.

 1. Science Highlights

 Evaluation of Subetadex-a-methyl (SBX-Me), a polyanionic cyclodextrin scaffold, as a
 medical countermeasure against fentanyl and related opioids.
 Subetadex-a-methyl (SBX-Me), a modified, polyanionic cyclodextrin scaffold, has been evaluated
 for its potential utilization as a medical countermeasure to neutralize the toxic effects of fentanyl and
 related synthetic opioids. In vitro toxicity assays that include hemolytic activity and cholesterol
 binding propensity demonstrate that SBX-Me has a non-toxic profile, and is comparable to the
 structurally similar, FDA-approved cyclodextrin-based drug Sugammadex (SGX). Pharmacokinetic
 (PK) and ADME studies, employing a 14C-radiolabeled version of SBX-Me (SBX-Me*), showed a
 rapid clearance of SBX-Me with a circulation half-life of t1/2 ~ 2 hrs and little accumulation in major
 organs collected during the studies (e.g., heart, liver, brain, spleen, kidney and lung). In addition to
 the SBX-Me PK/ADME studies, SBX-Me was evaluated in vivo for its ability to counteract the effects
 of fentanyl, carfentanil and remifentanil in rats. To this end, the recovery times in rats exposed sub-
 lethal fentanyl doses were found to be shorter when treated with SBX-Me, thus leading to a faster
 recovery after administration of SBX-Me. The, observed recovery times were reduced from ~35 to
 ~17 min. for fentanyl, from ~172 to ~59 min. for carfentanil and from ~18 ® ~12 min. for remifentanil.
 SBX-Me increased the elimination half-life for fentanyl and remifentanil from 5.37 hr to 6.42 hr. for
 fentanyl, and from 8.24 hr to 9.74 hr for remifentanil. Collectively, the data presented herein validates
 SBX-Me as a solid platform from which further research work can be launched from for the
 development of a medical counter measure (MCM) against the effects of fentanyl and its analogs.
 More importantly, the data suggests that SBX-Me and other analogs are attractive candidates for
 use as broad spectrum, opioid targeting MCMs.

 Optical 14C Tracing for Biological and Pharmaceutical Applications Using Two-Color Cavity
 Ringdown Spectroscopy
 Laser-based 14C quantitation has been proposed as a more affordable, higher-throughput, table-top
 alternative to accelerator mass spectrometry (AMS). Here, we demonstrate the feasibility of a mid-
 IR 14C detector based on two-color cavity ringdown spectroscopy (2C-CRDS) for low-level 14C

Final RPPR Page 23

 isotope tracing in biological studies. The 2C-CRDS technique quantifies the sample 14C content by
 measuring the 14CO2 absorption signals from the combusted samples with mid-IR lasers. With 2C-
 CRDS, we previously demonstrated the most sensitive and accurate optical measurements of 14CO2.
 The current detection sensitivity and quantitation accuracy of the instrument, at a few parts per
 quadrillion (where a quadrillion = 1015) 14C/C mole fraction, is competitive against AMS. Here, by
 applying the 2C-CRDS 14C sensor to two applications relevant to 14C-labeled biochemical analysis
 and pharmaceutical studies, we demonstrate sub-fCi level (where 1 fCi = 10−15 Ci) quantitation of
 sample 14C activity, with a minimum sample-size requirement of 3 mg of carbon. The current
 measurement throughput, ∼25 min/sample, is largely limited by the sampling efficiency of the online
 combustion and CO2 processing interface to the 2C-CRDS instrument. The possibility of a
 significantly improved measurement throughput of a few minutes per sample is suggested by the
 results of a flow-through 14CO2 sampling scheme. This improved measurement efficiency, combined
 with the relatively low cost and compact size of a 2C-CRDS sensor, could potentially revolutionize
 high-sensitivity 14C tracing in biological, pharmaceutical, and clinical studies.

 Asynchronous aging and turnover of human circulating and tissue-resident memory T cells
 across sites.
 Memory T cells are maintained in tissues as circulating effector-memory (TEM) and tissue-resident
 (TRM) populations for protective immunity, though the role of site and subset in memory persistence
 remains undefined. Here, we investigated age-associated dynamics of human T cells in lymphoid
 organs, mucosal sites, and blood over 10 decades of life using retrospective radiocarbon (14C) birth
 dating, along with cellular, transcriptome, and epigenetic profiling. Memory T cells across peripheral
 sites exhibited continuous turnover with mean lifespans of 1-2 years, while the spleen contained

Final RPPR Page 24

 longer-lived T cells. Over age, TEM cells expressed senescent markers and a GZMK transcriptional
 signature, while TRM cells maintained site-specific resident phenotypes without exhibiting features of
 senescence. Both TEM and TRM cells showed age-associated DNA hypomethylation, though
 TRM cells exhibited more epigenetically regulated genes. Together, our findings reveal asynchronous
 aging of human memory T cells by subset and site, as well as persistence of TRM cells without
 immunosenescence.

 2. Challenges and Solutions

 The greatest challenge to the User Resource was pandemic hangover limiting interactions with
 new potential users. Many university researchers closed radiological permits for cost saving during
 the pandemic as they were not able to access labs for months. Reopening radiological permits has
 lagged reopening of lab facilities and some faculty have not reopened radiological permits due to
 costs imposed by their institutions. Making the broad research community aware of the resource
 has slowed the development of new user projects. We are continuing to present virtual seminars
 also and expect this practice may continue since it removes travel obstacles and costs for cross-
 county presentations.

 Human Subject Report (Benzo[a]pyrene Ultralow Dose-Response Study)

 Protection of human subjects- Benzo[a]pyrene Ultralow Dose-Response Study
 1- Risk to human subjects
 a) Human Subjects Involvement:
 This is a validation study to determine if plasma levels of [14C]-BaP and metabolites will exhibit a dose-
 response consistent with predictions based on a previously developed Physiologically-Based
 Pharmacokinetic (PBPK) model for BaP. The consistency of the predictions will be tested by micro-dosing
 individuals with [14C]-BaP at levels of 25, 50, 100 and 250 ng (2.7-27 nCi). To date, our accelerator mass
 spectrometry (AMS) studies with PAHs have employed a single dose. Pharmacokinetic parameters for
 [14C]-BaP and metabolites will be assessed in plasma and urine samples collected over 48 hours using the
 User Facilities’ unique moving wire interface to directly couple UPLC to AMS.
 Subjects are recruited, screened and enrolled by collaborators at Oregon State University. LLNL will be
 receiving de-identified samples only. At OSU, up to 50 people will be enrolled in the study. Subjects of both
 genders, aged 21-65 years of age, will be eligible. The race and ethnicity of the subjects is expected to
 reflect the local Oregon population demographics. No attempt will be made to exclude or enrich for any
 ethnic or racial group. Subjects will be pre-screened for eligibility based on defined health status criteria,
 established by the OSU collaborators and approved by the OSU-IRB.
 b) Study Procedures, Materials, and Potential Risks
 The following study procedures will occur at OSU: Subjects will be screened by telephone to determine if
 they meet minimum entry criteria. Subjects who qualify will be scheduled for a screening visit, which
 includes an in-depth informed consent discussion, brief health assessment and physical exam. All study
 activities will take place in the Clinical Research Center in the Linus Pauling Science Center, at Oregon
 State University. The nurse coordinator will review the informed consent document including study activities,
 schedule and diet restrictions, answer questions, and provide further information as requested. After written
 consent is obtained, the nurse coordinator will collect demographic information, health history, height,
 weight, blood pressure and heart rate. Female subjects will be asked to provide spot urine samples now,
 and at the start of each study cycle, for a pregnancy test. The study physician will perform a physical exam.
 The study physician will review Health Assessment information to assess eligibility to continue with the
 study. Qualifying subjects will be asked to avoid consuming cruciferous vegetables, I3C or DIM
 supplements, smoked or cured meat or cheeses, or charcoal-grilled meats for 2 weeks prior to and during
 each study cycle. Subjects will be asked to complete a food diary covering the 3 days prior to, and the day
 of, each study cycle.
 On the study day, an IV catheter will be placed in an appropriate vein in the forearm by the nurse
 coordinator. Subjects will swallow a capsule containing the [14C]-BaP with 100 mL water. Blood will be
        sampled at 0, 0.25 0.5, 1.0, 1.5, 2, 3, 4, 8, 24, and 48 hours (11 draws total, 8-9 of which come from the
 catheter). The catheter will remain in place for blood draws through hour 4. After the 4 hour blood draw,
 subjects will have the choice of having the catheter removed and returning for the 8 hour blood draw by
 straight stick, or they can keep the catheter in place and remain on-site until after the 8 hour blood draw.
 The subjects will be monitored by the nurse coordinator between blood draws. The 8 (possibly), 24, and 48
 hour blood draws will be done with straight stick phlebotomy. No more than three (3) skin punctures will be
 made in an attempt to draw blood at each visit. The first blood draw of each cycle will be 20 mLs, with 10
 mL analyzed for background plasma levels of BaP (and 62 additional PAHs) with GC- MS/MS, and then
 subsequent blood draws will be 10 mL for a total amount of 120 mLs (8 tablespoons) per cycle.
 Subjects will be instructed to collect all urine during the entire 48-hour study cycle in containers provided by
 the study team. Subjects will be provided a discrete soft-sided cooler bag to store collected urine samples.
 They will be instructed to store samples at room temperature between visits and to return any filled
 containers at their next visit.
 This process will be followed for each dose (25, 50, 100 and 250 ng [14C]-BaP) for a total of 4 cycles per
 subject. There will be at least three weeks’ washout period between cycles.
 Blood samples will be centrifuged to obtain plasma and peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs). The
 [14C]-BaP and metabolites will be extracted directly from plasma by liquid/liquid extraction with ethyl
 acetate. The urine will be pooled over the time periods pre-capsule (baseline), 0 – 12, 12-24, and 24-48

 The plasma and urine extracts will be blown down under argon, coded, and sealed in amber vials and

 counted directly on the AMS to check for levels of covalent DNA binding.
 Potential risks are as follows:
 BaP: The International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC) has determined that BaP is a Class 1 known
 human carcinogen. Humans are exposed to PAHs, including BaP, from a number of sources, the highest
 being occupational and smoking, so we are excluding these populations. In the general population, the
 greatest BaP exposure is through diet. The dietary restrictions during this study are intended to offset the
 total 425 ng BaP consumed in 4 study cycles. This is equivalent to less than or equal to 4.2 ng/day over the
 101 day (minimum) study period. We estimate that following our dietary restrictions will reduce subject daily
 dietary exposure to BaP by 35-54 ng/day. Therefore, subjects are not exposed to an increased risk of
 cancer.
 Radiation: The total radiation dose of 45.9 nCi for the 4 dose cycles of [14C]- benzo[a]pyrene is equivalent
 to about 170 minutes of natural background radiation. The risk from the ultra-low doses in this study is
 negligible.
 Blood Sampling: Risks of blood sampling include pain, bruising, and in rare instances, infection. Some
 individuals can become lightheaded or nauseated. A qualified research nurse or certified phlebotomist will
 perform blood daws and carry out all necessary precautions to reduce the risk of injury to subjects.
 Blood Loss: While the risk of negative impact due to blood loss is negligible, subjects will be asked to refrain
 from blood donation one month before the study until one month after completion of the final cycle.
 Food allergens: Foods and beverages provided during this study may contain allergens. Ingredient lists and
 packaged foods with clearly labeled allergen information will be provided to subjects upon request. Subjects
 will be asked to notify the study nurse of any food allergies before ordering breakfast.
 Confidentiality/anonymity: Loss of confidentiality or anonymity is a potential risk.
 There are no alternative treatments or procedures. The only alternative is choosing not to participate in the
 study.
 2- Adequacy of protection against risks
 Waiver of documentation- A waiver of documentation of written informed consent for the telephone
 screening was approved by the OSU IRB to be able to collect information over the telephone prior to an in-
 person visit. This is necessary so that subjects can be screened for entrance criteria by telephone, thus
 saving the subject the inconvenience of making an unnecessary visit to the Clinical Research Center (CRC)
 if they do not qualify.
 Written consent – The nurse coordinator at the OSU CRC verbally reviews the informed consent document
 at the screening visit prior to any study activities taking place. The subjects are offered an opportunity to
 voice any questions or concerns, to take time to consult others (family members, health care providers), or
 do any other research that would help them understand the study activities before they sign the consent
 form. They are given a copy of the signed document with study team contact information to take with them.
 All research records will be handled as confidentially as possible and retained for three years post-study
 termination. All computer records will be protected by passwords and all paper study records will be kept in
 a locked cabinet in a locked room accessible only to the study team. Only the nurse coordinator at OSU will
 have access to the document linking identifiers to subject code numbers. Personal information collected
 during telephone screening, the consent documents, and compensation acknowledgement form will be the
 only documents containing subject name and personal information. Direct identifiers (name, mailing

 3- Potential Benefits to the Proposed Research to Research Participants and others
 This study is not designed to be of benefit to individual subjects. Potential benefits to society include
 essential information to aid regulatory agencies with respect to how environmental PAHs, such as BaP, at
 environmentally relevant levels of exposure are taken up by the G.I. (96% of carcinogenic PAH exposure in
 humans is dietary), metabolized and excreted from the body. That information can be used in modeling risk
 assessment rather than high-dose animal studies. This will provide a mechanism for improvement of public
 health
 4- Importance of knowledge to be gained
 Benzo[a]pyrene (BaP) is the most intensely studied polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbon (PAH), environmental
 pollutants formed naturally from forest fires, volcanoes, etc. Anthropogenic sources include coal, tobacco
 smoke, creosotes, coal tar-based pavement sealants, petroleum products including diesel and gasoline,
 wood and mixtures from production of coke, aluminum and graphite, among others. BaP is a class 1, known
 human carcinogen (Internal Agency for Research on Cancer) currently 8th on the ATSDR (Agency for Toxic
 Substances and Disease Registry, a division of the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention) list of
 agents of concern at high-priority pollutant sites. BaP is strongly associated with lung cancer (number one
 cause of cancer mortality). The EPA risk assessment levels for BaP exposure are currently derived from
 high dose rodent studies and then scaled to humans. This risk assessment may not adequately protect
 some populations. Studies with PAH mixtures indicate the linear extrapolated dose may under-estimate
 actual human risk. Our premise is- the best model for humans is humans. From low-dose exposures given
 to people, we can provide a much more accurate estimate of BaP levels in human tissues following
 exposure at environmental levels, as well as individual metabolites representing reduced or enhanced risk.

 I. OVERALL OUTCOMES

 I.1 What were the outcomes of the award?

 The National Resource for Biological Accelerator Mass Spectrometry (Resource) provided ultrasensitive Carbon-14 isotopic
 analysis by accelerator mass spectrometry (AMS) for NIH-funded researchers and was the only resource in the nation providing
 access to AMS. The Resource advanced a deeper understanding of the causes of human health concerns by
 (1) enabling accurate and precise measurement of absorption, distribution, metabolism and elimination of drugs, toxins, and
 medical interventions directly in humans;
 (2) offering the ability to conduct quantitative studies using biologics such as proteins or lipids; and
 (3) facilitating studies of metabolic pathways in health and disease through the use of biologically relevant concentrations of
 chemicals in cells and whole organisms.
 This unique Resource increased the accessibility and visibility of ultra-sensitive Carbon-14 measurements by training new
 investigators and expanding the national user base while concurrently improving the efficiency of AMS measurements through
 refinement of existing interfaces, expansion of measurement modes and the inclusion of small-scale pilot studies to generate
 preliminary data for exploratory research.
