Shadowing and Hyperbolicity for Endomorphisms of Locally Compact Groups
Dekui Peng

Abstract
. We study the shadowing property for continuous endomorphisms of locally compact groups, using the left uniformity. For Lie groups we obtain a complete infinitesimal characterization: an endomorphism has shadowing if and only if its differential is hyperbolic. As consequences, positively expansive Lie group endomorphisms are automatically topologically expanding, and for Lie group automorphisms, expansiveness, shadowing, two-sided shadowing and being topologically Anosov are equivalent. We also show that, for connected semisimple Lie groups, shadowing endomorphisms are precisely nilpotent endomorphisms. In contrast, for totally disconnected locally compact groups, shadowing is automatic: every continuous endomorphism has shadowing. The proof uses Willis’ tidy-above decomposition for endomorphisms. This yields, in the totally disconnected case, that topological expansion is equivalent to positive expansiveness and that being topologically Anosov is equivalent to expansiveness. We also discuss connections with group shifts and derive a compactness consequence for topologically mixing automorphisms.

1.
Introduction

The shadowing property, also known as the pseudo-orbit tracing property, is
one of the central notions in the qualitative theory of dynamical systems.
Roughly speaking, a system has shadowing if every approximate orbit is
uniformly followed by a genuine orbit. This idea originates in the study of
hyperbolic dynamics, where it provides a robust way of comparing true
orbits with numerically or perturbatively generated trajectories. In
particular, shadowing is closely related to stability phenomena: a classical
theorem of Walters asserts that an expansive homeomorphism of a compact
metric space with the pseudo-orbit tracing property is topologically stable

[
14
]
. Since then, shadowing and its variants have been studied in
many settings, including compact metric systems, non-compact spaces,
uniform spaces, group actions, linear dynamics, and symbolic dynamics; see,
for example,
[
3
,
4
,
6
,
20
]
.

Expansiveness is another classical topological feature of hyperbolic
dynamics. While shadowing says that approximate orbits can be traced by
true orbits, expansiveness says that two distinct points must eventually be
separated by the dynamics. These two properties are often paired together:
for instance, expansive systems with shadowing form one of the standard
topological models of hyperbolic behaviour.

In the theory of topological group automorphisms, however, expansiveness
and shadowing have played rather different roles. Expansiveness has been
studied extensively, partly because its group-theoretic form is rigid and
structural: it is expressed by the existence of a neighbourhood
U
of the
identity such that

\bigcap_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\alpha^{-n}(U)=\{e\}.

This condition interacts naturally with invariant subgroups, quotients,
contraction groups, and, in the totally disconnected locally compact
setting, with Willis’ scale theory and the nub
[
17
]
. By contrast, shadowing for
group endomorphisms has received less systematic attention. One reason is
that shadowing is not always a restrictive structural condition: in
symbolic algebraic dynamics it often appears through finite-type or Markov
conditions, while in the totally disconnected locally compact setting it
turns out to be automatic.

The purpose of this paper is to make this contrast precise for locally
compact groups. We study shadowing for continuous endomorphisms of
topological groups, using the left uniformity. The main results show that
Lie groups and totally disconnected locally compact groups exhibit two
opposite behaviours. For Lie groups, shadowing is exactly infinitesimal
hyperbolicity. For totally disconnected locally compact groups, every
continuous endomorphism has shadowing.

1.1.
Shadowing, expansiveness, and mixing

All topological spaces considered in this paper are Hausdorff. By a
dynamical system
(X,f)
we mean a topological space
X
together with a
continuous map
f:X\to X
.

Definition 1.1
.

Let
(X,d)
be a metric space, and let
f:X\to X
be continuous. For

\delta>0
, a finite or infinite sequence
(x_{k})
is called a

\delta
-pseudo-orbit
if

d(f(x_{k}),x_{k+1})<\delta

whenever both
x_{k}
and
x_{k+1}
are defined. Given
\varepsilon>0
, we say
that
(x_{k})
is
\varepsilon
-shadowed
by a point
x\in X
, or by the
orbit of
x
, if

d(f^{k}(x),x_{k})<\varepsilon

for every index
k
for which
x_{k}
is defined.

We say that the system
(X,f)
, or simply
f
, has
shadowing
if for
every
\varepsilon>0
there exists
\delta>0
such that every infinite

\delta
-pseudo-orbit is
\varepsilon
-shadowed by some point of
X
. We say
that
f
has
finite shadowing
if the same condition holds for every
finite
\delta
-pseudo-orbit.

This notion extends naturally to uniform spaces. Since every topological
group carries a canonical left uniformity, it makes sense to define
shadowing for self-maps of topological groups. In this paper we mainly
consider endomorphisms rather than arbitrary continuous self-maps.

Throughout the paper, an endomorphism is always assumed to be continuous,
and an automorphism is always assumed to be a topological automorphism. We
denote by
\operatorname{End}(G)
and
\operatorname{Aut}(G)
the monoid of
endomorphisms and the group of automorphisms of
G
, respectively.

Definition 1.2
.

Let
G
be a topological group and let
\alpha\in\operatorname{End}(G)
.
For a neighbourhood
V
of
e
, a finite or infinite sequence
(x_{k})
in

G
is called a
V
-pseudo-orbit
for
\alpha
if

\alpha(x_{k})^{-1}x_{k+1}\in V

whenever both
x_{k}
and
x_{k+1}
are defined.

Let
U
be a neighbourhood of
e
. We say that a finite or infinite sequence

(x_{k})
is
U
-shadowed
by a point
x\in G
, or by the orbit of
x
,
if

\alpha^{k}(x)^{-1}x_{k}\in U

for every index
k
for which
x_{k}
is defined.

We say that
\alpha
has
shadowing
if for every neighbourhood
U
of

e
there exists a neighbourhood
V
of
e
such that every infinite

V
-pseudo-orbit for
\alpha
is
U
-shadowed by some point of
G
. When

\alpha\in\operatorname{Aut}(G)
, we say that
\alpha
has

two-sided shadowing
if both
\alpha
and
\alpha^{-1}
have
shadowing.

We say that
\alpha
has
finite shadowing
if for every neighbourhood

U
of
e
there exists a neighbourhood
V
of
e
such that every finite

V
-pseudo-orbit for
\alpha
is
U
-shadowed by some point of
G
.

In topological groups, expansiveness has a particularly simple form, since
it is enough to compare points with the identity.

Definition 1.3
.

Let
G
be a topological group.

(1)

An automorphism
\alpha\in\operatorname{Aut}(G)
is called

expansive
if there exists a neighbourhood
U
of
e
such that

\bigcap_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\alpha^{-n}(U)=\{e\}.

Such a neighbourhood
U
is called an
expansive neighbourhood
for

\alpha
.

(2)

An endomorphism
\alpha\in\operatorname{End}(G)
is called

positively expansive
if there exists a neighbourhood
U
of
e
such
that

\bigcap_{n\geq 0}\alpha^{-n}(U)=\{e\}.

Such a neighbourhood
U
is called a
positively expansive
neighbourhood
for
\alpha
.

It is worth noting that if a locally compact group admits an expansive
automorphism, or a positively expansive endomorphism, then it is
first-countable and hence metrizable by the Birkhoff–Kakutani metrization
theorem.

Expansive automorphisms of topological groups have been studied for a long
time. In the compact group setting, early results go back to Wu

[
19
]
; for locally compact solvable groups and Lie groups, see
Aoki
[
1
]
and Morimoto
[
12
]
. More recently,
expansive automorphisms of locally compact groups were studied
systematically by Shah
[
13
]
. In the totally disconnected locally
compact setting, Glöckner and Raja
[
8
]
studied
expansive automorphisms in relation to contraction groups, quotients, and
the nub.

We also use the following standard mixing notion.

Definition 1.4
.

Let
X
be a topological space and let
f:X\to X
be continuous. We say that

f
is
topologically mixing
if, for every pair of non-empty open
subsets
A,B\subseteq X
, there exists
N\geq 1
such that

f^{n}(A)\cap B\neq\emptyset

for all
n\geq N
.

Following standard terminology in topological dynamics
[
3
,
7
]
, we recall the terminology combining expansiveness and shadowing.

Definition 1.5
.

Let
(X,f)
be a topological dynamical system with
X
infinite. We give a compatible uniformity on
X
.

(1)

The system
(X,f)
is called
topologically expanding
if
f
is
positively expansive and has the shadowing property.

(2)

If
f
is a homeomorphism, then
(X,f)
is called

topologically Anosov
if
f
is expansive and has the two-sided
shadowing property.

1.2.
Main results

Our first main result is the following complete characterization for Lie
groups. See Theorem
2.7
below.

Theorem A
.

Let
G
be a Lie group with Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
, and let

\alpha:G\to G
be a continuous endomorphism. Then
\alpha
has shadowing if
and only if the differential

\operatorname{d}\alpha:\mathfrak{g}\to\mathfrak{g}

is hyperbolic.

Here a linear endomorphism is called
hyperbolic
if its spectrum is disjoint
from the unit circle. The proof has two parts. The implication from
hyperbolicity to shadowing is proved by solving a nonlinear error equation
in local coordinates. This is a local version of the classical linear
shadowing argument, but it requires a careful treatment of the nonlinear
group multiplication. Conversely, if
\alpha
has shadowing, then the
differential
\operatorname{d}\alpha
has local finite shadowing near the origin. Using
the finite-dimensional linear characterization of shadowing, this forces

\operatorname{d}\alpha
to be hyperbolic.

Several consequences follow immediately. For automorphisms of Lie groups,
one-sided shadowing and two-sided shadowing coincide. Moreover, combining
Theorem A with the known characterization of expansive Lie group
automorphisms, one obtains that, for
\alpha\in\operatorname{Aut}(G)
, the
following conditions are equivalent:
\alpha
is topologically Anosov,

\alpha
is expansive,
\alpha
has shadowing, and
\alpha
has two-sided
shadowing.

We also obtain a corresponding statement for positively expansive
endomorphisms.

Theorem B
.

Let
G
be a Lie group, and let
\alpha:G\to G
be a continuous
endomorphism. If
\alpha
is positively expansive, then
\alpha
has
shadowing. Consequently,
\alpha
is topologically expanding if and only if
it is positively expansive. Moreover, if
G
admits a positively expansive
endomorphism, then the identity component
G_{0}
is nilpotent.

This is Theorem
2.12
. The infinitesimal form of positive expansiveness is stronger than
hyperbolicity: a Lie group endomorphism is positively expansive exactly
when every eigenvalue of its differential has modulus strictly larger than

1
. The final assertion in Theorem B uses a classical theorem of Bourbaki:
a finite-dimensional Lie algebra admitting a hyperbolic automorphism is
nilpotent
[
5
]
.

The Lie group characterization also gives a clean description in the
semisimple case. We call an endomorphism
\alpha

nilpotent
if some iterate
of
\alpha
is the trivial endomorphism. For a connected Lie group, this is
equivalent to the nilpotency of
\operatorname{d}\alpha
. We prove that if
G
is a
connected semisimple Lie group and
\alpha\in\operatorname{End}(G)
, then

\alpha
has shadowing if and only if
\alpha
is nilpotent. Thus, in the
semisimple Lie setting, non-trivial recurrent group structure obstructs
shadowing for automorphic dynamics.

This conclusion is genuinely Lie-theoretic and does not extend to all
connected compact groups. Indeed, if
S
is a non-abelian simple connected
compact Lie group, then the full shift on
S^{\mathbb{Z}}
is a shadowing
automorphism of a connected semisimple compact group. This example shows
that infinite-dimensional compact groups may admit symbolic shadowing
phenomena which are invisible at the finite-dimensional Lie level.

Our second main theorem concerns totally disconnected locally compact
groups. Here the situation is strikingly different. The proof uses Willis’
tidy subgroup theory for endomorphisms of totally disconnected locally
compact groups
[
15
,
16
,
18
]
.

Theorem C
.

Let
G
be a totally disconnected locally compact group, and let

\alpha:G\to G
be a continuous endomorphism. Then
\alpha
has shadowing.

Thus, for totally disconnected locally compact groups, shadowing is not an
additional hyperbolicity assumption; it is a structural consequence of the
existence of compact open subgroups and the tidying procedure. The
tidy-above decomposition plays the role of a zero-dimensional substitute
for the stable–unstable correction mechanism familiar from hyperbolic
dynamics. In this setting, topological hyperbolicity reduces to
expansiveness: a continuous endomorphism is topologically expanding if and
only if it is positively expansive, and an automorphism is topologically
Anosov if and only if it is expansive.

Theorem C also recovers, from the viewpoint of shadowing, a classical
finite-type phenomenon for group shifts. Kitchens and Schmidt proved that
closed shift-invariant subgroups over finite group alphabets are shifts of
finite type, as a special case of their finite local constraint theorem for
compact group shifts
[
11
, Corollary 3.9]
. If

X\leq A^{\mathbb{Z}}
is a group shift over a finite group
A
, then
X
is
a compact totally disconnected group and the shift is a continuous
automorphism of
X
. Hence Theorem C implies that the shift on
X
has
shadowing; since a subshift over a finite alphabet has shadowing if and
only if it is of finite type, this recovers the one-dimensional finite
alphabet case.

Finally, we apply Theorem C to a compactness theorem of Aoki. Aoki proved
that if a locally compact totally disconnected metrizable group admits a
topologically mixing automorphism with the pseudo-orbit tracing property,
then the group is compact
[
2
, Proposition 3]
. In view of
Theorem C, the shadowing assumption is automatic, and a standard quotient
argument removes metrizability. Therefore every totally disconnected
locally compact group admitting a topologically mixing automorphism is
compact.

2.
The Lie Case

In this section we study shadowing for endomorphisms of Lie groups. The main goal is to prove that, in the Lie setting, shadowing is completely determined by the induced linear map on the Lie algebra. More precisely, an endomorphism
\alpha
of a Lie group has shadowing if and only if its differential
\operatorname{d}\alpha
is hyperbolic. We begin with a general compactness observation. For locally compact groups, finite shadowing is already equivalent to shadowing. This will be useful later, since the converse direction of the Lie group characterization is proved by passing from shadowing of
\alpha
to local finite shadowing of
\operatorname{d}\alpha
. The local compactness assumption is essential here, as the following example shows.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
G
be a locally compact group and
\alpha:G\to G
be a continuous endomorphism. Then
\alpha
has shadowing if and only if it has finite shadowing.

Proof.

Only the sufficiency needs a proof.

For any compact neighbourhood
U
of
e
, we take a neighbourhood
V
of
e
such that every finite
V
-pseudo-orbit is
U
-shadowed by some point.
Now let
(x_{n})_{n\geq 0}
be an infinite
V
-pseudo-orbit. For each
n\geq 0
, we let

C_{n}=\bigcap_{i=0}^{n}\alpha^{-i}(x_{i}U).

Then each
C_{n}
is compact and nonempty by the choice of
V
.
Hence
C:=\bigcap_{n\geq 0}C_{n}=\bigcap_{i\geq 0}\alpha^{-i}(x_{i}U)
is nonempty.
It is trivial that every
y\in C
shadows
(x_{n})
. Thus,
\alpha
has the shadowing property.
∎

Example 2.2
.

Let
G=H(\mathbb{C})
be the Fréchet space of entire functions endowed with
the compact-open topology, regarded as an abelian Polish group under addition.
For
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
with
|\lambda|>1
, define

M_{\lambda}(f)=\lambda f,\qquad f\in H(\mathbb{C}).

Then
M_{\lambda}
is a continuous automorphism of the Polish group
G
, with
inverse
M_{\lambda^{-1}}
. By Bernardes and Peris
[
4
]
,
M_{\lambda}
has the
finite shadowing property but does not have the shadowing property.

We now turn to Lie groups. The relevant linear notion is hyperbolicity. We first recall the definition for finite-dimensional linear endomorphisms, and then prove that hyperbolicity of the differential gives shadowing for the original group endomorphism.

Definition 2.3
.

Let
E
be a finite-dimensional real vector space, and let
T:E\to E
be a
linear endomorphism. We say that
T
is
hyperbolic
if

\sigma(T)\cap S^{1}=\emptyset,

where
\sigma(T)
is the spectrum and
S^{1}
is the unit circle in
\mathbb{C}
.
Equivalently, every eigenvalue of
T
has modulus either strictly smaller
than
1
or strictly larger than
1
.

The next lemma is the technical core of the sufficiency direction. It says that, when the differential is hyperbolic, every sufficiently small sequence of local errors can be corrected by a bounded sequence near the identity.

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
G
be a Lie group with Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
, let
\alpha:G\to G
be a continuous endomorphism,
and put
A=\operatorname{d}\alpha:\mathfrak{g}\to\mathfrak{g}
. If
A
is hyperbolic, then
for every neighbourhood
U
of
e
in
G
, there exists a neighbourhood

V
of
e
such that, for every sequence
(e_{n})_{n\geq 0}
in
V
, the
equation

(2.1)

d_{n+1}=\alpha(d_{n})e_{n},\qquad n\geq 0,

has a solution
(d_{n})_{n\geq 0}
contained in
U
.

Proof.

Choose an exponential chart
\theta:O\to E
from a neighbourhood
O
of

e
in
G
onto a neighbourhood of
0
in
E=\mathfrak{g}
, with

\theta(e)=0
and
(D\theta)(e)=\operatorname{id}_{\mathfrak{g}}
. We shall
shrink
O
several times below.

In these coordinates, define

F(\xi,a)=\theta\bigl(\alpha(\theta^{-1}(\xi))\theta^{-1}(a)\bigr),

whenever
\xi
and
a
are sufficiently close to
0
. Then

F(0,0)=0
and
D_{\xi}F(0,0)=A
. Write

F(\xi,a)=A\xi+\psi(\xi,a).

Thus
\psi(0,0)=0
and
D_{\xi}\psi(0,0)=0
.

Since
A
is hyperbolic, there is an
A
-invariant decomposition

(2.2)

E=E^{\mathrm{s}}\oplus E^{\mathrm{u}},

where the spectrum of
A_{\mathrm{s}}=A|_{E^{\mathrm{s}}}
lies inside the
open unit disk in
\mathbb{C}
, and
A_{\mathrm{u}}=A|_{E^{\mathrm{u}}}
is invertible with
spectrum outside the closed unit disk. Choose norms on

E^{\mathrm{s}}
and
E^{\mathrm{u}}
, and use the product norm

\|\xi\|=\max\{\|\xi^{\mathrm{s}}\|,\|\xi^{\mathrm{u}}\|\}

on
E
, so that, for some
0<\lambda<1
,

(2.3)

\|A_{\mathrm{s}}\|\leq\lambda,\qquad\|A_{\mathrm{u}}^{-1}\|\leq\lambda.

We next choose the constants used in the contraction argument. Since

D_{\xi}\psi(0,0)=0
and
D_{\xi}\psi
is continuous as a function of the pair

(\xi,a)
, we may make the Lipschitz constant of
\psi
in the first
variable arbitrarily small, uniformly for all sufficiently small values of
the second variable. More precisely, choose
L>0
with

(2.4)

\frac{L}{1-\lambda}<\frac{1}{4}.

Then choose
\rho>0
such that
\theta^{-1}(B_{\rho}(0))\subseteq U
,

F
is defined on
B_{\rho}(0)\times B_{\rho}(0)
, and

\|D_{\xi}\psi(\xi,a)\|\leq L

whenever
\|\xi\|\leq\rho
and
\|a\|\leq\rho
. Hence, for

\|\xi\|,\|\zeta\|\leq\rho
and
\|a\|\leq\rho
,

(2.5)

\|\psi(\xi,a)-\psi(\zeta,a)\|\leq L\|\xi-\zeta\|.

Since
\psi(0,0)=0
, choose
0<\eta<\rho
such that

(2.6)

\|\psi(0,a)\|\leq\frac{(1-\lambda)\rho}{4}

whenever
\|a\|\leq\eta
. Combining (
2.4
),
(
2.5
), and
(
2.6
), we obtain, for all
\|\xi\|\leq\rho
and

\|a\|\leq\eta
,

(2.7)

\|\psi(\xi,a)\|\leq\|\psi(\xi,a)-\psi(0,a)\|+\|\psi(0,a)\|\leq L\rho+\frac{(1-\lambda)\rho}{4}\leq\frac{(1-\lambda)\rho}{2}.

Let
V=\theta^{-1}(B_{\eta}(0))
. Now let
(e_{n})_{n\geq 0}
be a sequence
in
V
, and put
a_{n}=\theta(e_{n})
. We shall solve

(2.8)

\xi_{n+1}=A\xi_{n}+\psi(\xi_{n},a_{n})

with
\|\xi_{n}\|\leq\rho
for all
n\geq 0
. Then

d_{n}=\theta^{-1}(\xi_{n})
will solve (
2.1
) and will
belong to
U
.

Let
\mathcal{X}
be the complete metric space of all bounded sequences

\xi=(\xi_{n})_{n\geq 0}
in
E
satisfying
\|\xi\|_{\infty}\leq\rho
. For

\xi\in\mathcal{X}
, write

\psi(\xi_{n},a_{n})=\psi^{\mathrm{s}}(\xi_{n},a_{n})+\psi^{\mathrm{u}}(\xi_{n},a_{n})

according to the decomposition (
2.2
). Define

\Gamma:\mathcal{X}\to\ell^{\infty}(E)
by

(2.9)

\displaystyle(\Gamma\xi)_{n}^{\mathrm{s}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}A_{\mathrm{s}}^{\,n-1-k}\psi^{\mathrm{s}}(\xi_{k},a_{k}),

(2.10)

\displaystyle(\Gamma\xi)_{n}^{\mathrm{u}}

\displaystyle=-\sum_{k=n}^{\infty}A_{\mathrm{u}}^{\,n-1-k}\psi^{\mathrm{u}}(\xi_{k},a_{k}).

The second series converges because

\|A_{\mathrm{u}}^{-1}\|\leq\lambda<1
.

We now check that
\Gamma
maps
\mathcal{X}
into itself. Put

M_{\xi}=\sup_{n\geq 0}\|\psi(\xi_{n},a_{n})\|.

By (
2.7
),
M_{\xi}\leq(1-\lambda)\rho/2
. For the stable
component, using (
2.3
), we have

\|(\Gamma\xi)_{n}^{\mathrm{s}}\|\leq\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\lambda^{n-1-k}M_{\xi}\leq\frac{M_{\xi}}{1-\lambda}.

For the unstable component,

\|(\Gamma\xi)_{n}^{\mathrm{u}}\|\leq\sum_{k=n}^{\infty}\lambda^{k+1-n}M_{\xi}\leq\frac{M_{\xi}}{1-\lambda}.

Since the norm on
E
is the maximum of the stable and unstable norms, it
follows that

\|\Gamma\xi\|_{\infty}\leq\frac{M_{\xi}}{1-\lambda}\leq\frac{\rho}{2}<\rho.

Thus
\Gamma(\mathcal{X})\subseteq\mathcal{X}
.

Next we show that
\Gamma
is a contraction. Let
\xi,\zeta\in\mathcal{X}

and put

M_{\xi,\zeta}=\sup_{n\geq 0}\|\psi(\xi_{n},a_{n})-\psi(\zeta_{n},a_{n})\|.

By (
2.5
),

M_{\xi,\zeta}\leq L\|\xi-\zeta\|_{\infty}
. Again, the estimates in (
2.3
) give

\|(\Gamma\xi-\Gamma\zeta)_{n}^{\mathrm{s}}\|\leq\frac{M_{\xi,\zeta}}{1-\lambda},\qquad\|(\Gamma\xi-\Gamma\zeta)_{n}^{\mathrm{u}}\|\leq\frac{M_{\xi,\zeta}}{1-\lambda}.

Therefore,

\|\Gamma\xi-\Gamma\zeta\|_{\infty}\leq\frac{L}{1-\lambda}\|\xi-\zeta\|_{\infty}<\frac{1}{4}\|\xi-\zeta\|_{\infty}.

Hence
\Gamma
is a contraction on
\mathcal{X}
. By the Banach fixed point
theorem,
\Gamma
has a fixed point
\xi=(\xi_{n})_{n\geq 0}
in
\mathcal{X}
.

It remains to check that this fixed point solves
(
2.8
). For the stable component, the definition
(
2.9
) gives

\xi_{n+1}^{\mathrm{s}}=A_{\mathrm{s}}\xi_{n}^{\mathrm{s}}+\psi^{\mathrm{s}}(\xi_{n},a_{n}).

For the unstable component, shifting the series in
(
2.10
) gives

\xi_{n+1}^{\mathrm{u}}=A_{\mathrm{u}}\xi_{n}^{\mathrm{u}}+\psi^{\mathrm{u}}(\xi_{n},a_{n}).

Thus

\xi_{n+1}=A\xi_{n}+\psi(\xi_{n},a_{n})=F(\xi_{n},a_{n}).

Consequently, with
d_{n}=\theta^{-1}(\xi_{n})
, we have

d_{n+1}=\alpha(d_{n})e_{n}

for all
n\geq 0
. Since
\|\xi_{n}\|\leq\rho
and

\theta^{-1}(B_{\rho}(0))\subseteq U
, the sequence
(d_{n})_{n\geq 0}
is
contained in
U
. This completes the proof.
∎

We now use the local error equation to trace pseudo-orbits of the group endomorphism.

Proposition 2.5
.

Let
G
be a Lie group, and let
\alpha:G\to G
be a continuous
endomorphism. If
\operatorname{d}\alpha
is hyperbolic, then
\alpha
has shadowing.

Proof.

Let
U
be a neighbourhood of
e
in
G
. By Lemma
2.4
,
choose a neighbourhood
V
of
e
such that every sequence
(e_{n})
in
V

admits a solution
(d_{n})
in
U
of

d_{n+1}=\alpha(d_{n})e_{n}.

Let
(x_{n})_{n\geq 0}
be a
V
-pseudo-orbit for
\alpha
. Write

x_{n+1}=\alpha(x_{n})e_{n}

with
e_{n}\in V
. Choose a solution
(d_{n})
in
U
of the above
equation, and put

y=x_{0}d_{0}^{-1}.

We claim that

x_{n}=\alpha^{n}(y)d_{n}

for all
n\geq 0
. This is true for
n=0
. If it holds for
n
, then

x_{n+1}=\alpha(x_{n})e_{n}=\alpha(\alpha^{n}(y)d_{n})e_{n}=\alpha^{n+1}(y)\alpha(d_{n})e_{n}=\alpha^{n+1}(y)d_{n+1}.

Hence the claim follows by induction. Therefore

\alpha^{n}(y)^{-1}x_{n}=d_{n}\in U

for every
n\geq 0
. Thus
(x_{n})
is
U
-shadowed by
y
.
In other words,
\alpha
has shadowing.
∎

For the converse implication, we shall need the corresponding linear criterion. We record it in a form adapted to the local argument below.

For a linear endomorphism
A:E\to E
, we say that
A
has

local finite shadowing
if there exists a neighbourhood
W
of
0

such that, for every
\varepsilon>0
, there exists
\delta>0
such that
every finite
\delta
-pseudo-orbit of
A
contained in
W
is

\varepsilon
-shadowed by some point of
E
.

Lemma 2.6
.

Let
E
be a finite-dimensional real normed vector space, and let

A:E\to E
be a linear endomorphism. Then the following conditions are
equivalent:

(1)

A
has shadowing;

(2)

A
has finite shadowing;

(3)

A
has local finite shadowing;

(4)

A
is hyperbolic, that is,
\sigma(A)\cap S^{1}=\emptyset
.

Proof.

The implication
(1)\Rightarrow(2)
is immediate: a finite pseudo-orbit can
be extended by a true orbit after its last term. The implication

(2)\Rightarrow(3)
is also immediate.

We prove
(3)\Rightarrow(4)
. Suppose that
A
has local finite shadowing.
We shall show that
A
is hyperbolic. Arguing by contradiction, assume that

A
is not hyperbolic. Then the complexification
A_{\mathbb{C}}
has an
eigenvalue
\lambda
with
|\lambda|=1
.

Choose a Jordan block
B
for
\lambda
of size
q\geq 1
. Thus, in a suitable
basis
v_{0},\ldots,v_{q-1}
of this block, we have

A_{\mathbb{C}}v_{0}=\lambda v_{0},\qquad A_{\mathbb{C}}v_{j}=\lambda v_{j}+v_{j-1}\quad(1\leq j<q).

Extend this to a Jordan basis of
E_{\mathbb{C}}
. Define a complex linear
functional

\ell:E_{\mathbb{C}}\to\mathbb{C}

as follows: if

Z=\sum_{j=0}^{q-1}z_{j}v_{j}+Z^{\prime},

where
Z^{\prime}
belongs to the span of all the other Jordan basis vectors, then

\ell(Z)=z_{0}.

Thus
\ell
extracts the coefficient of
v_{0}
in the chosen Jordan block
and vanishes on all other Jordan blocks.

We claim that, for every
Y\in E_{\mathbb{C}}
, the function

i\mapsto\lambda^{-i}\ell(A_{\mathbb{C}}^{i}Y)

is the restriction to
\mathbb{N}
of a polynomial in
i
of degree at most

q-1
. Indeed, write the component of
Y
in
B
as

Y_{B}=\sum_{j=0}^{q-1}y_{j}v_{j}.

Only this component contributes to
\ell(A_{\mathbb{C}}^{i}Y)
. On
B
we have

A_{\mathbb{C}}=\lambda I+N,

where

Nv_{0}=0,\qquad Nv_{j}=v_{j-1}\quad(1\leq j<q).

Hence

A_{\mathbb{C}}^{i}=(\lambda I+N)^{i}=\sum_{k=0}^{q-1}\binom{i}{k}\lambda^{i-k}N^{k}

on
B
, since
N^{q}=0
. Therefore

\lambda^{-i}\ell(A_{\mathbb{C}}^{i}Y)=\sum_{k=0}^{q-1}\binom{i}{k}\lambda^{-k}\ell(N^{k}Y_{B}).

The right-hand side is a polynomial in
i
of degree at most
q-1
, because
each
\binom{i}{k}
is a polynomial in
i
of degree
k
.

Let
\varphi(t)=t^{q}
on
[0,1]
. Since
\varphi
is not a polynomial of
degree at most
q-1
, there exists
c>0
such that

(2.11)

\sup_{0\leq t\leq 1}|\varphi(t)-p(t)|\geq c

for every polynomial
p
of degree at most
q-1
. We shall use the following
discrete consequence. After decreasing
c
if necessary, there exists

N_{0}\geq 1
such that, for every
N\geq N_{0}
and every polynomial
p
of
degree at most
q-1
,

(2.12)

\max_{0\leq i\leq N}|\varphi(i/N)-p(i/N)|\geq c.

Indeed, if this failed, then for a sequence
N_{j}\to\infty
one could find
polynomials
p_{j}
of degree at most
q-1
such that

\max_{0\leq i\leq N_{j}}|\varphi(i/N_{j})-p_{j}(i/N_{j})|\to 0.

The polynomials
p_{j}
are bounded at
q
separated grid points, for all
large
j
, hence their coefficients are bounded. Passing to a subsequence,

p_{j}
converges uniformly on
[0,1]
to a polynomial
p
of degree at most

q-1
. Since the grids become dense in
[0,1]
, we get
p=\varphi
on

[0,1]
, contradicting (
2.11
).

Let
B_{\rho}(0)
be a ball on which local finite shadowing is assumed to hold. We shall construct, inside
B_{\rho}(0)
, arbitrarily fine
finite pseudo-orbits which cannot be shadowed with a fixed positive
accuracy.

Choose
\tau>0
small enough so that the real vectors constructed below all
belong to
B_{\rho}(0)
. For
N\geq N_{0}
, put

Z_{i}=\tau\lambda^{i}\varphi(i/N)v_{0},\qquad 0\leq i\leq N.

If
\lambda
is non-real, then the conjugate block for
\overline{\lambda}

also appears in
E_{\mathbb{C}}
. Our functional
\ell
is chosen to vanish
on that conjugate block. Hence, for

X_{i}=2\operatorname{Re}Z_{i}=Z_{i}+\overline{Z_{i}},

we have

\ell(X_{i})=\lambda^{i}\tau\varphi(i/N).

If
\lambda
is real, then let
X_{i}=Z_{i}
and

\ell(X_{i})=\lambda^{i}\tau\varphi(i/N).

Thus, in both cases, we have

(2.13)

\ell(X_{i})=\lambda^{i}\tau\varphi(i/N)\qquad(0\leq i\leq N).

We next check that
(X_{i})_{i=0}^{N}
is an arbitrarily fine pseudo-orbit as

N\to\infty
. Since
v_{0}
is an eigenvector with eigenvalue
\lambda
,

Z_{i+1}-A_{\mathbb{C}}Z_{i}=\tau\lambda^{i+1}\bigl(\varphi((i+1)/N)-\varphi(i/N)\bigr)v_{0}.

Hence, by uniform continuity of
\varphi
,

\max_{0\leq i<N}\|Z_{i+1}-A_{\mathbb{C}}Z_{i}\|\to 0\quad\text{as }N\to\infty.

Taking real parts in the non-real case, we also get

(2.14)

\max_{0\leq i<N}\|X_{i+1}-AX_{i}\|\to 0\quad\text{as }N\to\infty.

We now show that these finite pseudo-orbits cannot be shadowed with a fixed
positive accuracy. Let
C>0
be such that

|\ell(W)|\leq C\|W\|\qquad(W\in E_{\mathbb{C}}).

Choose
\varepsilon_{0}>0
with
C\varepsilon_{0}<\tau c
.
Suppose that there is some
Y\in E

\varepsilon_{0}
-shadowing

X_{0},\ldots,X_{N}
, that is,

\|A^{i}Y-X_{i}\|<\varepsilon_{0}\qquad(0\leq i\leq N).

Applying
\ell
after complexification and using (
2.13
), we get

\left|\lambda^{-i}\ell(A_{\mathbb{C}}^{i}Y)-\tau\varphi(i/N)\right|=\left|\ell(A_{\mathbb{C}}^{i}Y)-\lambda^{i}\tau\varphi(i/N)\right|\leq C\varepsilon_{0}\qquad(0\leq i\leq N).

Remind that the function

i\mapsto\lambda^{-i}\ell(A_{\mathbb{C}}^{i}Y)

is the restriction of a polynomial in
i
of degree at most
q-1
. Hence
there is a polynomial
p
of degree at most
q-1
in the variable
t
such
that

p(i/N)=\frac{1}{\tau}\lambda^{-i}\ell(A_{\mathbb{C}}^{i}Y)\qquad(0\leq i\leq N).

Therefore

|\varphi(i/N)-p(i/N)|\leq\frac{C\varepsilon_{0}}{\tau}<c\qquad(0\leq i\leq N),

contradicting (
2.12
). Thus no point

\varepsilon_{0}
-shadows
X_{0},\ldots,X_{N}
.

Now let
\delta>0
be arbitrary. By (
2.14
), for all
sufficiently large
N
we have

\|X_{i+1}-AX_{i}\|<\delta\qquad(0\leq i<N).

Thus, inside
B_{\rho}(0)
, we have constructed a finite
\delta
-pseudo-orbit
which is not
\varepsilon_{0}
-shadowed by any point. This contradicts
local finite shadowing. Hence
A
must be hyperbolic.

It remains to verify
(4)\Rightarrow(1)
. Regard the vector space
E
as an
abelian Lie group under addition. Then
E
is simply connected, and the
linear map
A:E\to E
is a continuous endomorphism of this Lie group.
Moreover, under the canonical identification
T_{0}E=E
, we have

\operatorname{d}A=A.

The exponential map of the additive Lie group
E
is the identity map

\operatorname{id}_{E}:E\to E
. Hence the usual uniformity induced by any norm
on
E
coincides with the left uniformity of the Lie group
E
.

Since
A
is hyperbolic by assumption, Proposition
2.5

applies to this abelian Lie group and gives that
A
has shadowing. This
proves
(4)\Rightarrow(1)
.

The proof of the equivalence is complete.
∎

We can now prove the Lie group characterization. The sufficiency has already been obtained in Proposition
2.5
. The converse is obtained by showing that shadowing of
\alpha
forces the differential to have local finite shadowing.

Theorem 2.7
.

Let
G
be a Lie group with Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
, and let
\alpha:G\to G
be a continuous endomorphism.
Then
\alpha
has shadowing if and only if

\operatorname{d}\alpha:\mathfrak{g}\to\mathfrak{g}
is hyperbolic.

Proof.

The sufficiency is exactly Proposition
2.5
. We prove the converse.
Assume that
\alpha
has shadowing and put

A=\operatorname{d}\alpha.

By Lemma
2.6
, it is enough to show that
A
has local finite
shadowing.

Choose

r>0
so small that the exponential map is injective on an open
neighbourhood containing both
\overline{B_{r}(0)}
and

A(\overline{B_{r}(0)})
. Let
0<\varrho<r
and
\varepsilon>0
be given.
Replacing
\varepsilon
by a smaller positive number if necessary, we may
assume that
\varepsilon<r
.

Set

C=\exp(\overline{B_{\varrho}(0)}).

Then
C
is compact and
C\subseteq\exp(B_{r}(0))
. Hence there exists a
symmetric neighbourhood
W
of
e
in
G
such that

(2.15)

CW\subseteq\exp(B_{r}(0)).

Choose a symmetric neighbourhood
O\subseteq W
of
e
such that, whenever

P,Q\in B_{r}(0)
,

(2.16)

\exp(P)^{-1}\exp(Q)\in O\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad\|P-Q\|<\varepsilon.

Such an
O
exists because
\exp
is a uniform isomorphism from
B_{r}(0)
onto its image.

Let
N
be a neighbourhood of
e
witnessing
O
-shadowing for
\alpha
;
that is, every
N
-pseudo-orbit is
O
-shadowed by some
y\in G
. Shrinking

N
if necessary, we may assume that
N\subseteq W
.

By continuity of the map

(P,Q)\mapsto\exp(AP)^{-1}\exp(Q),

and compactness of
\overline{B_{\varrho}(0)}
, there exists
\delta>0
such
that, whenever
P,Q\in B_{\varrho}(0)
and

we have

(2.17)

\|Q-AP\|<\delta\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad\exp(AP)^{-1}\exp(Q)\in N.

Now let
X_{0},\ldots,X_{m}\in B_{\varrho}(0)
be a finite
\delta
-pseudo-orbit
for
A
, that is,

\|X_{i+1}-AX_{i}\|<\delta\qquad(0\leq i<m).

Put

x_{i}=\exp(X_{i})
for
0\leq i\leq m
,
and extend this finite sequence by the true
\alpha
-orbit of
x_{m}
:

x_{m+j}=\alpha^{j}(x_{m})\qquad(j\geq 1).

Since

\alpha(\exp X)=\exp(AX),

for any
X\in\mathfrak{g}
,
condition (
2.17
) implies that

(x_{n})_{n\geq 0}
is an
N
-pseudo-orbit for
\alpha
.

Therefore there exists
y\in G
such that

\alpha^{i}(y)^{-1}x_{i}\in O,

for all
0\leq i\leq m
.
Since
O
is symmetric, this gives

\alpha^{i}(y)\in x_{i}O\subseteq CW\subseteq\exp(B_{r}(0))\qquad(0\leq i\leq m),

where we used (
2.15
). In particular, taking

i=0
, there is
Y\in B_{r}(0)
such that

y=\exp(Y).

We claim that

(2.18)

\alpha^{i}(y)=\exp(A^{i}Y)\quad\text{and}\quad A^{i}Y\in B_{r}(0)

for all
0\leq i\leq m
. This is clear for
i=0
. Suppose it holds for
some
i<m
. Then

\alpha^{i+1}(y)=\alpha(\exp(A^{i}Y))=\exp(A^{i+1}Y).

On the other hand, we have already shown that

\alpha^{i+1}(y)\in\exp(B_{r}(0))
. Hence

\alpha^{i+1}(y)=\exp(Z)
for some
Z\in B_{r}(0)
. Since

A^{i}Y\in B_{r}(0)
, we have
A^{i+1}Y\in A(B_{r}(0))
. By the choice of
r
,
the exponential map is injective on a set containing both
B_{r}(0)
and

A(B_{r}(0))
. Therefore
A^{i+1}Y=Z\in B_{r}(0)
. This proves the claim.

Combining the inclusion
\alpha^{i}(y)^{-1}x_{i}\in O

with
(
2.18
), we get

\exp(A^{i}Y)^{-1}\exp(X_{i})\in O\qquad(0\leq i\leq m).

By (
2.16
), it follows that

\|A^{i}Y-X_{i}\|<\varepsilon\qquad(0\leq i\leq m).

Thus every finite
\delta
-pseudo-orbit of
A
contained in

B_{\varrho}(0)
is
\varepsilon
-shadowed by
Y
.
This completes the proof.
∎

Remark 2.8
.

The argument above uses more than the fact that the exponential map is a
local diffeomorphism, or even a local uniform homeomorphism. Indeed, a finite
pseudo-orbit
X_{0},\ldots,X_{m}
of
A
contained in a small neighbourhood of

0
gives a finite pseudo-orbit
\exp(X_{0}),\ldots,\exp(X_{m})
of
\alpha
in
a small neighbourhood of
e
. The shadowing property of
\alpha
then gives
a point
y\in G
which shadows it. However, the local chart alone does not
imply that this shadowing point
y
, or its iterates
\alpha^{i}(y)
, remain
inside the same coordinate neighbourhood. This is why, in the proof, the
shadowing neighbourhood
O
is chosen so small that

\exp(\overline{B_{\varrho}(0)})O\subseteq\exp(B_{r}(0)).

This forces all relevant points
\alpha^{i}(y)
to stay inside the exponential
chart, allowing us to pull the shadowing relation back to the Lie algebra.

If
A
is an invertible linear map on a finite-dimensional vector space, then

A
is hyperbolic if and only if
A^{-1}
is hyperbolic. Therefore
Theorem
2.7
immediately yields the following consequence.

Corollary 2.9
.

Let
G
be a Lie group and let
\alpha\in\operatorname{Aut}(G)
. Then

\alpha
has shadowing if and only if
\alpha^{-1}
has shadowing.
Consequently, either of these conditions is equivalent to
\alpha
having
two-sided shadowing.

2.1.
Consequences and examples

We collect several consequences of Theorem
2.7
. First we discuss
positively expansive endomorphisms and expansive automorphisms. In this
setting, shadowing is not an additional assumption: once the appropriate
expansiveness condition holds, shadowing follows automatically from the Lie
algebra criterion.

For Lie group endomorphisms, positive expansiveness can also be read from
the differential.

Proposition 2.10
.

Let
G
be a Lie group with Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
, let

\alpha:G\to G
be a continuous endomorphism, and put

A=\operatorname{d}\alpha:\mathfrak{g}\to\mathfrak{g}
. Then
\alpha
is positively
expansive if and only if every eigenvalue of
A
has modulus strictly larger
than
1
. Consequently, if
G
is connected, then
\alpha
is surjective;
if
G
is simply connected, then
\alpha
is bijective.

Proof.

Assume first that
\alpha
is positively expansive. Let
V
be a
neighbourhood of
e
such that

\bigcap_{n\geq 0}\alpha^{-n}(V)=\{e\}.

Shrinking
V
if necessary, we may choose a neighbourhood
U
of
0
in

\mathfrak{g}
such that
\exp|_{U}:U\to V
is a homeomorphism.

Suppose that
A
has an eigenvalue of modulus at most
1
. Then there is a
non-zero vector
X\in\mathfrak{g}
such that
(A^{n}X)_{n\geq 0}
is bounded.
Replacing
X
by
tX
for a
sufficiently small
t>0
, we may assume that
A^{n}X\in U
for every

n\geq 0
. Hence

\alpha^{n}(\exp X)=\exp(A^{n}X)\in V\qquad(n\geq 0).

Thus
\exp X\in\bigcap_{n\geq 0}\alpha^{-n}(V)
. Since
X\neq 0
and

\exp
is injective on
U
, this contradicts the choice of
V
. Therefore
all eigenvalues of
A
have modulus strictly larger than
1
.

Conversely, assume that all eigenvalues of
A
have modulus strictly larger
than
1
. Choose an equivalent norm on
\mathfrak{g}
and a number

\lambda>1
such that
\|AX\|\geq\lambda\|X\|
for all

X\in\mathfrak{g}
. Choose
r>0
such that
\exp
is injective on
B_{r}(0)
.
Let
M>1
satisfy
\|AX\|\leq M\|X\|
for all
X\in\mathfrak{g}
, and set

V=\exp(B_{r/M}(0)).

We show that
V
witnesses positive expansiveness.

Let
g\neq e
. If
g\notin V
, there is nothing to prove. Otherwise write

g=\exp X
with
X\in B_{r/M}(0)
. Since
\exp
is injective on
B_{r}(0)
,
we have
X\neq 0
. As
\|A^{k}X\|\to\infty
, let
k\geq 0
be the least
integer such that
A^{k}X\notin B_{r/M}(0)
. Then
k\geq 1
and

A^{k-1}X\in B_{r/M}(0)
, so
\|A^{k}X\|<r
. Hence

A^{k}X\in B_{r}(0)\setminus B_{r/M}(0)
. Therefore

\alpha^{k}(g)=\exp(A^{k}X)\notin V,

because
\exp
is injective on
B_{r}(0)
. Thus no non-trivial element remains
in
V
under all forward iterates, and

\bigcap_{n\geq 0}\alpha^{-n}(V)=\{e\}
. Hence
\alpha
is positively
expansive.

The final assertions follow as follows. The condition on the eigenvalues
implies that
A
is invertible, and hence
\alpha
is a local diffeomorphism
at
e
. Therefore
\alpha(G)
is an open subgroup of
G
. If
G
is
connected, every open subgroup is all of
G
, so
\alpha
is surjective. If

G
is simply connected, then this surjective local diffeomorphism is a
covering map of
G
onto itself; since
G
is simply connected, the covering
is trivial. Hence
\alpha
is injective, and therefore bijective.
∎

Lemma 2.11
.

[
5
]

Let
\mathfrak{g}
be a finite-dimensional real Lie algebra. If

\mathfrak{g}
admits a hyperbolic automorphism, then
\mathfrak{g}
is
nilpotent.

Combining the preceding proposition with Theorem
2.7
, we obtain the promised relation between positive expansiveness and topological expansion.

Theorem 2.12
.

Let
G
be a Lie group, and let
\alpha:G\to G
be a continuous endomorphism.
If
\alpha
is positively expansive, then
\alpha
has the shadowing property.
Consequently,
\alpha
is topologically expanding if and only if it is
positively expansive. Moreover, if
G
admits a positively expansive
endomorphism, then the identity component
G_{0}
is nilpotent.

Proof.

Put
A=\operatorname{d}\alpha:\mathfrak{g}\to\mathfrak{g}
. If
\alpha
is positively
expansive, then by Proposition
2.10
, every eigenvalue of
A
has
modulus strictly larger than
1
. In particular,
A
is hyperbolic. Hence,
by Theorem
2.7
,
\alpha
has the shadowing property.

The equivalence with being topologically expanding follows immediately from
the definition: a topologically expanding endomorphism is a positively
expansive endomorphism with shadowing, and we have just shown that positive
expansiveness already implies shadowing.

It remains to prove the last assertion. Suppose that
G
admits a positively
expansive endomorphism
\alpha
. By Proposition
2.10
,
\operatorname{d}\alpha

is hyperbolic. Hence, by Lemma
2.11
, the Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
is
nilpotent. Therefore the identity component
G_{0}
is nilpotent.
∎

We next turn from positively expansive endomorphisms to expansive
automorphisms. We shall use the following known characterization.

Lemma 2.13
.

[
13
]

Let
G
be a Lie group and let
\alpha:G\to G
be an automorphism. Then

\alpha
is expansive if and only if
\operatorname{d}\alpha
is hyperbolic.

Together with Theorem
2.7
and Corollary
2.9
, this gives the following characterization of topologically Anosov automorphisms of Lie groups.

Theorem 2.14
.

Let
G
be a Lie group and let
\alpha\in\operatorname{Aut}(G)
. Then the
following conditions are equivalent:

(1)

\alpha
is topologically Anosov;

(2)

\alpha
is expansive;

(3)

\alpha
has the shadowing property;

(4)

\alpha
has the two-sided shadowing property.

Moreover, these conditions hold only when the identity component of
G
is nilpotent.

Proof.

By Lemma
2.13
,
\alpha
is expansive
if and only if
\operatorname{d}\alpha
is hyperbolic. By Theorem
2.7
, this is
equivalent to
\alpha
having the shadowing property. Moreover, by
Corollary
2.9
, for Lie group automorphisms, shadowing is equivalent
to two-sided shadowing. The equivalence with being topologically Anosov follows directly from the
definition.

By Proposition
2.10
, every eigenvalue of
\operatorname{d}\alpha
has modulus
strictly larger than
1
. Hence
\operatorname{d}\alpha
is a hyperbolic automorphism
of
\mathfrak{g}
, where
\mathfrak{g}
is the Lie algebra of
G
. By Lemma
2.11
,
\mathfrak{g}
is nilpotent.
∎

We shall also need a simple permanence property of shadowing under taking powers. Although elementary, it is useful for treating nilpotent endomorphisms.

Lemma 2.15
.

Let
G
be a topological group and let
\alpha\in\operatorname{End}(G)
.
Then, for every
n\geq 1
,
\alpha
has shadowing if and only if

\alpha^{n}
has shadowing. In particular,
\alpha
has shadowing if and only
if there exists
n\geq 1
such that
\alpha^{n}
has shadowing.

Proof.

First assume that
\alpha
has shadowing, and fix
n\geq 1
. Let
U
be a
neighbourhood of
e
. Choose a neighbourhood
V
of
e
such that every

V
-pseudo-orbit for
\alpha
is
U
-shadowed by an orbit of
\alpha
.

Let
(y_{k})_{k\geq 0}
be a
V
-pseudo-orbit for
\alpha^{n}
, that is,

\alpha^{n}(y_{k})^{-1}y_{k+1}\in V\qquad(k\geq 0).

Define a sequence
(x_{i})_{i\geq 0}
by

x_{kn+j}=\alpha^{j}(y_{k}),\qquad k\geq 0,\ 0\leq j<n.

Then
(x_{i})
is a
V
-pseudo-orbit for
\alpha
: all intermediate steps are
exact, and the only possible error occurs from
x_{kn+n-1}
to
x_{kn+n}
,
where it is precisely

\alpha^{n}(y_{k})^{-1}y_{k+1}\in V.

Hence there exists
z\in G
such that

\alpha^{i}(z)^{-1}x_{i}\in U\qquad(i\geq 0).

Taking
i=kn
, we get

(\alpha^{n})^{k}(z)^{-1}y_{k}=\alpha^{kn}(z)^{-1}x_{kn}\in U\qquad(k\geq 0).

Thus
(y_{k})
is
U
-shadowed by the
\alpha^{n}
-orbit of
z
. Therefore

\alpha^{n}
has shadowing.

Conversely, assume that
\alpha^{n}
has shadowing. Let
U
be a
neighbourhood of
e
. Choose neighbourhoods
W,H
of
e
such that

WH\subseteq U.

By continuity of the maps
\alpha^{j}
,
0\leq j<n
, choose a neighbourhood

R
of
e
such that

\alpha^{j}(R)\subseteq W\qquad(0\leq j<n).

Let
S
be a neighbourhood of
e
such that every
S
-pseudo-orbit for

\alpha^{n}
is
R
-shadowed by an orbit of
\alpha^{n}
.

Choose a neighbourhood
V
of
e
so small that, for every

1\leq j<n
,

\alpha^{n-1}(V)\alpha^{n-2}(V)\cdots\alpha(V)V\subseteq H\cap S.

This is possible by continuity of multiplication and of the finitely many
maps
\alpha^{j}
.

Let
(x_{i})_{i\geq 0}
be a
V
-pseudo-orbit for
\alpha
. Put

e_{i}=\alpha(x_{i})^{-1}x_{i+1}\in V
. For
j\geq 1
we have

x_{i+j}=\alpha^{j}(x_{i})\alpha^{j-1}(e_{i})\alpha^{j-2}(e_{i+1})\cdots e_{i+j-1}.

In particular, the subsequence
(x_{kn})_{k\geq 0}
is an
S
-pseudo-orbit
for
\alpha^{n}
, since

\alpha^{n}(x_{kn})^{-1}x_{(k+1)n}\in\alpha^{n-1}(V)\alpha^{n-2}(V)\cdots\alpha(V)V\subseteq S.

Hence there exists
z\in G
such that

\alpha^{kn}(z)^{-1}x_{kn}\in R\qquad(k\geq 0).

Now fix
i\geq 0
and write
i=kn+j
with
0\leq j<n
. Then

\alpha^{i}(z)^{-1}x_{i}=\alpha^{j}\bigl(\alpha^{kn}(z)^{-1}x_{kn}\bigr)\cdot\alpha^{j}(x_{kn})^{-1}x_{kn+j}.

The first factor lies in
\alpha^{j}(R)\subseteq W
, and the second factor
lies in
H
by the choice of
V
. Hence

\alpha^{i}(z)^{-1}x_{i}\in WH\subseteq U.

Thus
(x_{i})
is
U
-shadowed by the
\alpha
-orbit of
z
. Therefore

\alpha
has shadowing.
∎

We now apply the preceding results to semisimple Lie groups. Let us recall that an endomorphism
\alpha
of a topological group
G
is called
nilpotent
if
\alpha^{n}
is the trivial endomorphism for some
n\geq 1
. If
G
is a connected Lie group, this is equivalent to the nilpotency of
\operatorname{d}\alpha
. Indeed,
d(\alpha^{n})=(\operatorname{d}\alpha)^{n}
, and if
d(\alpha^{n})=0
, then
\alpha^{n}(G)
is a connected Lie subgroup with zero Lie algebra, hence is trivial.

Proposition 2.16
.

Let
G
be a connected semisimple Lie group, and let

\alpha\in\operatorname{End}(G)
. Then
\alpha
has shadowing if and only if

\alpha
is nilpotent.

Proof.

Put
A=\operatorname{d}\alpha:\mathfrak{g}\to\mathfrak{g}
.

Assume first that
\alpha
has shadowing. By Theorem
2.7
,
A
is
hyperbolic. Since
\mathfrak{g}
is semisimple, we claim that
A
must be
nilpotent. Indeed, choose
m\geq 1
such that

A^{m}(\mathfrak{g})=A^{m+1}(\mathfrak{g}).

Set
\mathfrak{h}=A^{m}(\mathfrak{g})
. Then
\mathfrak{h}
is semisimple,
because it is isomorphic to the quotient
\mathfrak{g}/\ker A^{m}
. Moreover,

A(\mathfrak{h})=\mathfrak{h}
, so
A|_{\mathfrak{h}}
is an automorphism of

\mathfrak{h}
. Since
\mathfrak{h}
is
A
-invariant, the spectrum of

A|_{\mathfrak{h}}
is contained in the spectrum of
A
. Hence

A|_{\mathfrak{h}}
is hyperbolic. If
\mathfrak{h}\neq 0
, then
Lemma
2.11
implies that
\mathfrak{h}
is nilpotent, contradicting
semisimplicity. Therefore
\mathfrak{h}=0
, and hence
A
is nilpotent.

Since
G
is connected, nilpotency of
A=\operatorname{d}\alpha
is equivalent to
nilpotency of
\alpha
. Therefore
\alpha
is nilpotent.

Conversely, suppose that
\alpha
is nilpotent. Then
\alpha^{n}
is the
trivial endomorphism for some
n\geq 1
. The trivial endomorphism has
shadowing. Hence, by Lemma
2.15
,
\alpha
has shadowing.
∎

This last conclusion is specific to the Lie setting. It cannot be extended to all connected compact groups. Recall that a connected compact group
G
is called
semisimple
if
[G,G]=G
, or equivalently, if
G
is a quotient of a possibly infinite product of simple connected compact Lie groups by a totally disconnected closed normal subgroup
[
9
]
. The following example shows that a connected semisimple compact group may admit a shadowing automorphism.

Example 2.17
.

Let
S
be a simple connected compact Lie group, and let

G=S^{\mathbb{Z}}.

Then
G
is a connected semisimple compact group.
Let
\sigma:G\to G
be the left shift, given by

\sigma((g_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{Z}})=(g_{i+1})_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}.

Then
\sigma
is a continuous automorphism of
G
. We show that
\sigma

has shadowing.

Let
U
be a neighbourhood of
e
in
G
. Since
G
has the product
topology, there exist an integer
N\geq 1
and a neighbourhood
W
of
e

in
S
such that

\{g\in G:g_{i}\in W\text{ for all }|i|\leq N\}\subseteq U.

Choose a neighbourhood
L
of
e
in
S
such that every product of at most

N
elements of
L\cup L^{-1}
lies in
W
. Define a neighbourhood
V
of

e
in
G
by

V=\{g\in G:g_{i}\in L\text{ for all }|i|\leq N\}.

Let
(x^{n})_{n\geq 0}
be a
V
-pseudo-orbit for
\sigma
. Write

x^{n}=(x^{n}_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}
. The condition means that, for every

n\geq 0
and every
|i|\leq N
,

(x^{n}_{i+1})^{-1}x^{n+1}_{i}\in L.

Define
y=(y_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}\in G
by

y_{i}=\begin{cases}x^{i}_{0},&i\geq 0,\\
x^{0}_{i},&i<0.\end{cases}

We claim that the orbit of
y

U
-shadows
(x^{n})_{n\geq 0}
.

Fix
n\geq 0
and
|i|\leq N
. We need to show that the
i
-th coordinate
of
\sigma^{n}(y)^{-1}x^{n}
lies in
W
, that is,

y_{n+i}^{-1}x^{n}_{i}\in W.

If
n+i\geq 0
, then
y_{n+i}=x^{n+i}_{0}
. Moving along the pseudo-orbit
from time
n+i
to time
n
changes the coordinate by at most
|i|
steps,
and each step contributes an element of
L
or
L^{-1}
. Hence

y_{n+i}^{-1}x^{n}_{i}\in W.

If
n+i<0
, then
y_{n+i}=x^{0}_{n+i}
. In this case necessarily

0\leq n\leq|i|\leq N
, and moving from
x^{0}_{n+i}
to
x^{n}_{i}
again uses
at most
N
pseudo-orbit errors, each lying in
L
or
L^{-1}
. Thus

y_{n+i}^{-1}x^{n}_{i}\in W

also in this case.

Therefore
\sigma^{n}(y)^{-1}x^{n}\in U
for every
n\geq 0
. Hence
\sigma

has shadowing.

3.
The Totally Disconnected Case

We now turn to totally disconnected locally compact groups. The starting
point of the theory is van Dantzig’s theorem, which asserts that every
totally disconnected locally compact group has a neighbourhood basis at the
identity consisting of compact open subgroups. Thus, in the totally
disconnected case, compact open subgroups play a role analogous to small
neighbourhoods of the identity in Lie theory.

For a long time, however, van Dantzig’s theorem remained one of the few
general structural tools available for arbitrary totally disconnected
locally compact groups. A major breakthrough was Willis’ theory
[
15
,
16
]
of the scale
function and tidy subgroups for automorphisms. This theory gives a
quantitative measure of how an automorphism moves compact open subgroups,
and it provides canonical compact open subgroups adapted to the dynamics of
the automorphism. It has become one of the basic tools in the modern
structure theory of totally disconnected locally compact groups, and it is
also inherently dynamical in nature.

Willis’ theory was later extended from automorphisms to endomorphisms. We
shall use only the tidy-above part of this theory. Let
G
be a totally
disconnected locally compact group, let
\alpha\in\operatorname{End}(G)
,
and let
U
be a compact open subgroup of
G
. Define

U_{-}=\{u\in U:\alpha^{n}(u)\in U\text{ for all }n\geq 0\}=\bigcap_{n\geq 0}\alpha^{-n}(U),

and

\displaystyle U_{+}=\{u\in U:

\displaystyle\text{there exists }(u_{n})_{n\geq 0}\subseteq U\text{ such that }u_{0}=u,

\displaystyle\text{and }\alpha(u_{n+1})=u_{n}\text{ for all }n\geq 0\}.

The subgroup
U
is said to be
tidy above
for
\alpha
if

U=U_{+}U_{-}.

By the tidying procedure for endomorphisms, compact open subgroups tidy
above for
\alpha
form a neighbourhood basis at the identity.

Lemma 3.1
.

[
18
, Proposition 3]
Let
G
be a totally disconnected locally compact group,
\alpha\in\operatorname{End}(G)
. Then
for every compact open subgroup
U
of
G
, there exists a compact open
subgroup
V\leq U
which is tidy above for
\alpha
.

We shall show that this decomposition is exactly what is needed to trace
pseudo-orbits. The
U_{-}
part controls forward errors, while the
U_{+}
part
allows us to correct them by choosing suitable regressive trajectories.

Theorem 3.2
.

Let
G
be a totally disconnected locally compact group, and let

\alpha:G\to G
be a continuous endomorphism. Then
\alpha
has the
shadowing property.

Proof.

Let
U\leq G
be compact open. By Lemma
3.1
, there is a compact open subgroup
V\leq U
which is
tidy above for
\alpha
. Recall that, in this setting,

V=V_{+}V_{-}
. Since
V_{+}
and
V_{-}
are subgroups, the equality
V=V_{+}V_{-}
also gives

V=V_{-}V_{+}
.

Let
(x_{n})_{n\geq 0}
be a
V
-pseudo-orbit, and put

e_{n}=\alpha(x_{n})^{-1}x_{n+1}\in V
. We shall prove that, for every

m\geq 0
, there exists
y_{m}\in G
such that

\alpha^{i}(y_{m})\in x_{i}V
for all
0\leq i\leq m
. More precisely, we prove
by induction that
y_{m}
can be chosen so that

\alpha^{m}(y_{m})=x_{m}b_{m}
for some
b_{m}\in V_{-}
.

For
m=0
, take
y_{0}=x_{0}
and
b_{0}=e
. Suppose that
y_{m}
and

b_{m}\in V_{-}
have been chosen. Since
x_{m+1}=\alpha(x_{m})e_{m}
, we have

\alpha^{m+1}(y_{m})=\alpha(x_{m})\alpha(b_{m})=x_{m+1}e_{m}^{-1}\alpha(b_{m}).

Set
u=e_{m}^{-1}\alpha(b_{m})
. Then
u\in V
, since
e_{m}\in V
and

\alpha(b_{m})\in\alpha(V_{-})\leq V_{-}\leq V.

Write
u=ba
with
b\in V_{-}
and

a\in V_{+}
. Choose

a_{0},a_{1},\ldots,a_{m+1}
in
V_{+}
with
a_{0}=a
and

\alpha(a_{j+1})=a_{j}
for
0\leq j\leq m
. Put

y_{m+1}=y_{m}a_{m+1}^{-1}
.

For
0\leq i\leq m
, we have
\alpha^{i}(a_{m+1})\in V
, and hence

\alpha^{i}(y_{m+1})\in\alpha^{i}(y_{m})V=x_{i}V
. Moreover,

\alpha^{m+1}(y_{m+1})=\alpha^{m+1}(y_{m})\alpha^{m+1}(a_{m+1})^{-1}=x_{m+1}ua^{-1}=x_{m+1}b,

with
b\in V_{-}
. This completes the induction.

Now define

K_{m}=\{y\in x_{0}V:\alpha^{i}(y)\in x_{i}V\text{ for }0\leq i\leq m\}
.
The preceding paragraph shows that each
K_{m}
is non-empty. The sets
K_{m}

are closed subsets of the compact set
x_{0}V
, and they form a decreasing
sequence. Hence
\bigcap_{m\geq 0}K_{m}\neq\emptyset
. Choose

y\in\bigcap_{m\geq 0}K_{m}
. Then
\alpha^{n}(y)\in x_{n}V
for every

n\geq 0
, so
\alpha^{n}(y)^{-1}x_{n}\in V\leq U
for every
n\geq 0
.
Thus
(x_{n})_{n\geq 0}
is
U
-shadowed by the forward orbit of
y
.

Since
U
was arbitrary,
\alpha
has shadowing property.
∎

Let us also point out that Theorem
3.2
recovers, from the
viewpoint of shadowing, a classical finite-type phenomenon for group
shifts. A closed shift-invariant subgroup of
A^{\mathbb{Z}}
, where
A
is
a finite group, is called a group shift. Kitchens and Schmidt proved more
generally that if
A
is a compact Lie group and
X\leq A^{\Gamma}
is a
closed
\Gamma
-invariant subgroup, then
X
is determined by finitely many
local group constraints
[
11
, Corollary 3.9]
. In
particular, when
A
is finite, every group shift is a shift of finite type.

Our theorem gives a dynamical explanation for the one-dimensional finite
alphabet case. Indeed, if
X\leq A^{\mathbb{Z}}
is a group shift over a
finite group
A
, then
X
is a compact totally disconnected group and the
shift is a continuous automorphism of
X
. Hence Theorem
3.2

implies that the shift on
X
has shadowing. Since a subshift over a finite
alphabet has shadowing if and only if it is of finite type, it follows that

X
is a shift of finite type. Thus Theorem
3.2
may be viewed as
a non-symbolic extension of the shadowing mechanism behind the
Kitchens–Schmidt finite-type theorem for group shifts.

Corollary 3.3
.

Let
G
be a totally disconnected locally compact group.

(1)

If
\alpha:G\to G
is a continuous endomorphism, then
\alpha
is
topologically expanding if and only if it is positively expansive.

(2)

If
\alpha\in\operatorname{Aut}(G)
, then
\alpha
is topologically
Anosov if and only if it is expansive.

Proof.

By Theorem
3.2
, every continuous endomorphism of a totally
disconnected locally compact group has shadowing. Hence, for endomorphisms,
positive expansiveness is equivalent to positive expansiveness together
with shadowing, that is, to being topologically expanding.

Similarly, every automorphism
\alpha
of a totally disconnected locally compact
group has two-sided shadowing, since both
\alpha
and
\alpha^{-1}
are endomorphisms. Therefore, for automorphisms, expansiveness
is equivalent to expansiveness together with two-sided shadowing, that is, to being
topologically Anosov.
∎

We conclude with a compactness consequence of Aoki’s theorem on dense orbits
of automorphisms of totally disconnected locally compact groups.

Aoki proved that if
G
is a locally compact totally disconnected
metrizable group and if an automorphism
\alpha\in\operatorname{Aut}(G)
is
topologically mixing and has the pseudo-orbit tracing property with respect
to a compatible left-invariant metric, then
G
is compact

[
2
, Proposition 3]
. In view of Theorem
3.2
, the
shadowing assumption in Aoki’s criterion is automatic. Moreover, the
metrizability assumption can also be removed by a standard quotient
argument.

Corollary 3.4
.

Let
G
be a totally disconnected locally compact group, and let

\alpha\in\operatorname{Aut}(G)
. If
\alpha
is topologically mixing, then

G
is compact.

Proof.

First observe that
G
is
\sigma
-compact. Indeed, let
U
be a compact
open subgroup of
G
. For each
x\in G
, topological mixing applied to the
open sets
U
and
xU
gives some
n\geq 0
such that

\alpha^{n}(U)\cap xU\neq\emptyset.

Hence
x\in\alpha^{n}(U)U
, and therefore

G=\bigcup_{n\geq 0}\alpha^{n}(U)U,

a countable union of compact sets.

By the Kakutani–Kodaira theorem, and replacing the compact normal subgroup
obtained there by the intersection of its
\alpha
-iterates, there exists a
compact normal
\alpha
-invariant subgroup
K
of
G
such that
G/K
is
metrizable.

Indeed, if
K_{0}
is a compact normal subgroup such that
G/K_{0}
is
metrizable, then one may take

K=\bigcap_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\alpha^{n}(K_{0}).

The quotient
G/K
embeds into the countable product

\prod_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}G/\alpha^{n}(K_{0})
, and hence is metrizable.

The induced automorphism

\bar{\alpha}:G/K\to G/K

is again topologically mixing, since quotient maps are open.

The group
G/K
is locally compact and totally disconnected. By
Theorem
3.2
,
\bar{\alpha}
has shadowing. Since
G/K
is
metrizable, this agrees with the pseudo-orbit tracing property for a
compatible left-invariant metric. Thus Aoki’s compactness criterion applies
to
(G/K,\bar{\alpha})
, and so
G/K
is compact. Since
K
is compact, it
follows that
G
is compact.
∎
