 ABSTRACT

  Wire-arc direct energy deposition (Wire-arc DED) enables efficient fabrication of large metallic
components because of its high deposition rate and high material efficiency. However conventional Wire-
arc DED relies on a build plate to constrain part deformation during deposition, which requires post-
processing removal and can increase material waste and residual strain redistribution. This project
investigated the feasibility of shifting Wire-arc DED toward build plate free, freeform fabrication by
examining residual strain and distortion development in three walls: (1) as-deposited, (b) base plate
removal, and (3) freeform wall printed without a base plate using a small anchor support. Residual strains
were measured using neutron diffraction method at ORNL’s High Flux Isotope Reactor (HFIR). A
sequentially coupled thermo-mechanical finite element framework was developed to simulate deposition,
cooling, boundary condition changes associated with base plate removal and freeform deposition. The
model captured characteristic strain bands, edge tension, mid-height compression, and strain re-distribution
after base plate removal. Statistical comparisons using standard deviation, RMSE, normalized RMSE, and
bias showed better quantitative agreement for the base-plate and base plate removal cases than for the
freeform wall although the freeform simulation still reasonably captured the overall strain magnitude and
trend. Overall, the predicted and measured results demonstrate the capability of a physics-based, 5-axis tool
path aware modeling framework for residual strain prediction in Wire-arc DED. Also, the work identified
key modeling gaps including free surface deformation, bottom layer sagging, material hardening behavior,
5-axis associated heat distribution, and geometric uncertainty and discussed for improved residual strain
prediction.

 1. STATEMENT OF OBJECTIVES

1.1 BACKGROUND

  This CRADA is an Oak Ridge National Laboratory (Contractor) Manufacturing Demonstration Facility
(MDF) Technical Collaboration project with Granularity, LLC (Participant). Technical objectives are: 1)
Simulate residual stress and deformation of Wire Arc Additive Manufacturing (WAAM) or Wire Arc Direct
Energy Deposition (Wire-arc DED) parts produced with and without a build plate using the contractor-
developed residual stress model. 2) Validate simulation by measuring residual stress (e.g., neutron
diffraction, x-ray etc.). The primary product of research will be a validated simulation of WAAM part
residual stress which is required for developing tool path compensation techniques that minimize
production of out-of-tolerance parts and improve quality of parts produced using patent-pending technology
that enables WAAM production without a build plate. The participant is a startup company formed to
commercialize the patent-pending WAAM technology that enables elimination of the build plate and
printing angle-agnostic overhangs using a three-axis computer numerical control (CNC) machine. Instead
of the build plate, a single anchor having a small cross section capable of supporting the part’s weight is
used. Parts produced without a build plate are subject to deformation resulting from unrestrained residual
stress that develops during printing. Products of this proposed research advances the commercialization
effort by a) informing decisions for tool path compensation to improve dimensional tolerances of parts
produced both with and without a build plate, and b) improving overall part quality. ORNL previously

 3

validated an ABAQUS fine element method (FEM) residual stress simulation, providing a suitable
foundation for the necessary simulation of part deformation.

1.2 OBJECTIVES

  Building on a plate has been a necessary nuisance with all metal additive manufacturing techniques. The
part must be removed from the build plate, and, with WAAM, the build plate typically cannot be reused.
Cutting a small anchor from a large part is trivial in comparison to separating large parts from large build
plates; it’s faster, consumes less energy and wastes less material. Since the anchor isn’t subjected to forces
resulting from residual stress, it can be reused. The elimination of stress concentration resulting from the
use of the build plate may have beneficial effects on the printed part’s mechanical properties
  In the present study, instead of the build plate, a single anchor having a small cross section capable of
supporting the part’s weight is used. Removing a small anchor from a large component is comparatively
straightforward relative to separating large parts from full build plates, reducing post-processing time,
energy consumption, and material waste. Because the build plate is well known to act as a heat sink during
printing, eliminating it reduces heat dissipation and increases peak temperatures and thermal gradients. The
resulting implications of build-plate-free manufacturing on residual strains and distortion therefore require
further scrutiny, motivating the need for development of computational models.
  This project aims to (1) develop a physics-based finite element simulation framework that captures the
thermal, mechanical, and boundary condition physics of wire-arc DED with a and without a base plate using
the modified Hilbert tool path, (2) validate the model by comparing its predicted residual strains with
neutron diffraction measurements, (3) quantify the influence of the base plate removal, freeform geometry,
and 5-axis deposition on the magnitude and spatial distribution of residual strains, and (4) identify the
dominant modeling simplifications and potential measurement uncertainties that govern model-experiment
discrepancy between the model prediction and experimental measurements.

2.1.2 Measurement of Residual Strain

  Residual strains were measured using the 2nd generation Neutron Residual Stress Facility (NRSF2) at
High Flux Isotope Reactor (HFIR), Oak Ridge National Laboratory (ORNL). The residual strains were
characterized in three principal directions: build direction (BD), longitudinal direction (LD), and thickness
direction (TD). A gauge volume of 5 mm × 5 mm × 5 mm was used in the strain mapping. The spatial
distance between consecutive measurement points was 5 mm. The stress-free lattice spacing 𝑑0 was
previously measured for the same material and geometry in literature [1]. Eventually, force-balance
reconstruction was performed assuming 𝜀𝑡𝑜𝑡𝑎𝑙 ≈ 0. The three principal strains in x-, y-, and z-direction
were measured for the three walls: 1) as-deposited, 2) base-cutoff, and 3) freeform. The gauge volume of 5
× 5 × 5 mm3 was used with 5 mm step size.

2.2 SIMULATION OF RESIDUAL STRAINS

Thermo-mechanical simulations can be performed using either a fully coupled formulation, in which the
thermal and mechanical fields are solved simultaneously, or a sequentially coupled formulation, in which
the transient thermal problem is solved first, and the resulting temperature history is then applied in a
subsequent mechanical analysis. The influence of deformation on the thermal field is typically negligible
in DED, and the Abaqus [2] Additive Manufacturing module currently supports only sequential coupling.
Sequential thermomechanical coupling was therefore adopted in this study. This section presents the
modeling framework, key implementation details, and a comparison between experimental measurements
and computational predictions for all three samples.

  During AM, the part gradually grows as deposition progresses. To mirror this physical deposition process,
progressive element activation method [5-8] was used to represent the incremental material addition in a

 7

digital environment. This method uses event series (i.e., tool path) to represent the temporospatial behavior
of the heat source movement and printing parameters. The event series is converted directly from the AM
machine’s G-code or log-file using a custom Python script developed in-house. When a new material is
deposited based on the event series, the simulation finds the intersections between the finite element meshes
and the tool path. This process enables tracking of the volume fraction of each element ensuring proper
material deposition at the precise moment when the heat source passes through. The moving heat source is
also computed based on the actual tool path and power distribution. An arbitrary amount of heating can be
featured as a sequence of heat fluxes at a given location. In progressive element activation method, heat
loss is calculated through the continuously evolving surface of each element. This approach ensures that
both convective and radiational heat losses are dynamically applied with improved accuracy as the
deposition progresses. As the part size increases, these heat losses become ever more important because of
largely increased surface area.
  The 3-axis Goldak’s model assumes that the heat source orientation remains fixed and is aligned with the
build direction. By fixing the heat source orientation, the simulation does not change the local surface
normal throughout the deposition making it simpler in handling tool path and temperature distribution. This
3-axis approach is more suitable for straight-wall or builds not requiring non-planar slicing or multi-axis
tool path or large overhanging where the geometry and tool path do not require adjustments to the heat
source orientation. Unlike 3-axis depositions, the normal direction of heat source in 5-axis varies over the
deposition process due to changing local surface orientation. The normal vector orientation influences the
heat source orientation and consequently spatial temperature distribution on the surface and within the part.
In the 5-axis simulation, normal vector to the part at the current location of the heat source is defined as
additional inputs including 2 rotational degrees of freedom on top of 3 translational degrees of freedom in
3-axis system. The governing heat source equation remains unchanged, but the spatial coordinates in the
Goldak’s equation are replaced by time-dependent local coordinates calculated thorough a rotation-
translation transformation. The transformation between the local heat source coordinates and the global
coordinate system is handled internally by Abaqus. Note that the governing heat-conduction equation
remains unchanged, but the spatial coordinates in the Goldak heat-source term are replaced by time-
dependent local coordinates obtained through a rotation–translation transformation.

required. The transformation process begins with the calculation of the local travel direction, which is
determined by the change in the heat source position. This step is necessary for accurately describing the
movement and orientation of the heat source throughout the deposition process. It ensures proper spatial
alignment and temperature distribution in the 5-axis model. The heat-source position and the corresponding
unit normal to the deposited surface at discrete time intervals are provided as a model input.

2.2.3 Simulation Methodology

  The model geometry for the as-deposited wall is shown in Figure 3 consisting of the deposited wall, build
base plate, and table. The dimensions of the wall were 200 mm (L) × 15.4 mm (T) × 81.12 mm (H). The
dimensions of the base plate were 210 mm (L) × 26.4 mm (T) × 9.6 mm (H). The table dimensions used in
the simulation were 400 mm (L) × 230 mm (T) × 70 mm (H) in Figure 3(a) and (b). A simple isotropic
plasticity model was used for mechanical simulation. The materials were low-carbon mild steel (S355J2)
and low-carbon steel (AISI1018 and AISI1020) for wall, base plate, and table, respectively. The latent heat
of fusion is 272.0 kJ/kg in the range from 1440 oC (solidus) to 1530 oC (liquidus). The constant thermo-
physical properties were used for the table with AISI1020 where the density is 7870 kg/m3, thermal
conductivity is 89 W/m·oC, and specific heat is 481 J/kg·oC. The Poisson ratios used were 0.3 and 0.29 for
mild and low carbon steel, respectively. The other temperature-dependent material properties used in this
work can be found in literature [9-12]. The baseline yield strength of S355J2 was adjusted by applying yield
strength reduction factors reported in the literature [13] to consider property variations introduced by the
AM process.
  The sequentially coupled thermo-mechanical simulation was carried out. Upon completion of the
mechanical simulation, the constraints imposed from clamps were released. This step is important to
evaluate elastic spring-back which refers to the recovery of elastic deformation once the external restrictions
are removed. The resulting changs in residual stress were evaluated to ensure the prediction accuracy after
the release of the constraints. The mesh size of 4.5 mm (x) × 3.8 mm (y) × 1.5 mm (z) was used for the
wall, 5.0 mm (x) × 4.4 mm (y) × 4.8 mm (z) for the base, 20.0 mm (x) × 19.1 mm (y) × 17.5 mm (z) for the
table. The finite element method (FEM) model had 10,616 hexahedral elements in entire computation
domain. Element types of DC3D8 and C3D8R were used for thermal and mechanical simulation,
respectively.
  The simulation used a printing time of 5570s (approximately 93 minutes) at travel speed of 11.2 mm/s
followed by 1350s cooling time to reach room temperature. Each layer, on average, has 73s layer time to

 9

cover tool path length of 821 mm. The wall and base plate are fully constrained using tie boundary
condition, which assumes fusion bonding between the surfaces. At the beginning of the process, the base
plate is generally considered to be in intimate contact with the table resulting in stable heat transfer path.
When two solid surfaces are in contact, heat flows from the hotter to the cooler side and a temperature drop
develops at the interface. This phenomenon is referred to as thermal conductance or thermal contact
resistance [14]. However, during repeated heating and cooling cycles, the base plate deforms due to thermal
expansion and contraction of the material during printing. This accumulated deformation opens a gap
between the base plate and table. Consequently, heat is transferred only through the remaining contact areas
resulting in a non-uniform heat flow pattern between contact and non-contact areas. A gap conductance
was applied as a boundary condition in Figure 3 to capture the non-uniform heat transfer caused by the
evolving interface. The contacting-based plate and table interface is divided into two distinct zones: gap 1
and 2. The gap 1 near the center region represents an almost intimate contact having a higher gap
conductance value of 1000 mW/ (mm2 C). The gap 2 around side-edge areas represents a larger separation
or gap having a lower gap conductance value of 0.1 mW/ (mm2 C). This multi-zone approach enables the
thermal simulation to capture the different heat fluxes across contact and separate areas.
  Similar to the thermal simulation, the interface between the base plate and table was subdivided into two
distinct contact zones in Figure 3(c). In structural simulation, the gap 1 represents an intimate contact region
not allowing gap separation. A rough friction condition was applied to effectively prohibit sliding. The gap
2 represents a separated contact that allows gaps between the base plate and table to open. A friction
coefficient of 0.2 was applied to allow the interface slip. In addition, the table is assumed to be fully fixed
in x, y, and z directions, and its bottom surface temperature is fixed at 25 oC.

  During the strain predictions of as-deposited wall and the wall after base plate removal, the tool path was
generated by converting the nominal G-code into a planar layer-wise deposition path. In this 2D approach,
each layer is deposited with a prescribed constant layer thickness, so the z-coordinate does not vary within
an individual layer. Therefore, the model cannot capture the in-process z-height adjustment used to
compensate for the large shape changes that occur during deposition. As seen in Figure 1(c), the shape of
part is concave where z-height varies from 85 mm to 103 mm by 18 mm that is about 11-layer thickness
during the last layer deposition. These z-position corrections were identified from the machine log rather
than the G-code. The tool path from machine-log indicates that the first few layers are non-planar as shown
in Figure 5. The tool path develops a concave profile and gradually shifts to convex. This requires a full 3D
deposition path within a layer involving simultaneous variation in the x-, y-, and z-directions. Such a path
cannot be modeled by the conventional planar layer-by-layer deposition algorithm, which assumes
deposition on approximately flat layers with a fixed global stacking direction. When the z-coordinate
substantially varies within a layer, the algorithm can become numerically unstable because of inconsistent
element activation and poor correspondence between the heat source position and the activated material.

For this reason, the 5-axis deposition strategy was adopted in this work. This strategy defines material
deposition along an arbitrary 3D tool path and uses a local deposition direction allowing the element
activation to follow the non-planar freeform trajectory more consistently.

2.4 VALIDATION OF SPRING-BACK FROM BASE PLATE REMOVAL WALL

  The wall is printed on a base plate, which is usually removed before use. After the base plate is removed,
the residual strains in the wall are released and recover elastic deformation, which rebalances the amplitude
and distribution of residual strains. This subsection aims to distinguish this effect from parts printed without
a base plate. By comparing the strain fields in scenarios with and without a base plate, the influence of base
plate on the overall residual strain behavior can be effectively isolated and precisely quantified.
  Figure 12 shows the predicted and measured residual strain maps in x-, y-, and z-direction after removal
of base plate. The removal of the base plate does not change the overall qualitative pattern of the strain
distribution. Both pre- and post-cut show high tensile area near the top of the wall in Figure 12(a) and (b)

compression in Figure 8(a) to Figure 12(a). The residual strains in y-direction remain same trend, but their
magnitude is reduced in Figure 12(c) and (d). Another notable tensile strain development was observed in
the predicted x-directional strain near the base plate regime in Figure 12(b). This strain development is
associated with the removal of the base plate. When the base plate is cut, the residual strains produced
during deposition are redistributed throughout the wall. In the simulation, the base plate is initially treated
as a stress-free elastic component. Therefore, upon removal, the model only captures the relaxation and

  This overprediction can be attributed to simplifications in the model and limitations of the FE simulation.
The FE simulation assumes the wall as a simple rectangular box, which geometry is intended to represent
the final freeform shape. In practice, the first few layers are deposited onto a narrow anchor and onto a

underlaying base plate, the newly melted material can sag downward before solidification under the
combined effect of gravity, wetting, and capillary forces. As a result, the deposited layers initially form a
concave shape, and the subsequent deposits tend to conform to this geometry for approximately the first
three layers. As deposit continues, repeated thermal cycling, solidification shrinkage, and thermal
contraction can cause upward warping in the weakly constraint layers and gradually transition toward a
convex shape. Consequently, the z-height within each layer varies substantially, particularly near the lower
wall corners. Although the FE simulation can capture thermal contraction and resulting thermo-mechanical
deformation, it does not resolve melt pool flow, free-surface deformation, or the associated sagging at the

bottom layers. Therefore, the FE model may contain more material near the bottom than the real part, which
could increase the local thermal mass and shrinkage-induced deformation and contribute to the
overprediction of residual strains in the part.

 3. CONCLUSIONS

  This project developed thermo-mechanical modeling framework for base plate free wall in 5-axis scheme.
The model accurately reproduces the overall residual strain trend such as M-, and W-shaped strain bands,
edge-tension, mid-height compression in as-deposited part. Also, the bass-plate removal is correctly
captured. The spring-back reduces tensile strain amplitude while preserving the qualitative pattern. For the
freeform part, the thermal validation for 5-axis tool path shows good agreement between predicted
temperature profile and thermo-couple recordings. This confirms the proper implementation of the 5-axis
tool path and boundary conditions. Also, the predicted strains are in satisfiable agreement with the neutron
measured strains. Although the FE simulation can capture thermal contraction and resulting thermo-
mechanical deformation, it does not resolve melt pool flow, free-surface deformation, or the associated
sagging at the bottom layers. Additional sources of discrepancy are identified as (1) an isotropic,
rate‑independent elasto‑plastic law that underrepresents path‑dependent hardening and phase
transformations; (2) a geometrical representation that neglects the concave/convex transition of the first
couple of layers in the freeform build. Therefore, the FE model may contain more material near the bottom
than the real part, which could increase the local thermal mass and shrinkage-induced deformation and
contribute to the overprediction of residual strains in the part.
  The statistical measures (Std, RMSE, RMSE/Exp.Std, Bias) were applied to quantitatively compare the
simulation accuracy with the measured residual strains. The statistics showed that the residual strain
prediction shows better accuracy in the wall with base and without base plate than the freeform wall
although the model reasonably captures the trend. In the freeform model, the larger scatter in residuals
strains is observed than the scatter in the measurement. This may be associated with the large z-directional

 27

variations caused by the sagging and deformation induced concave/convex geometry, which can produce a
heterogeneous heat distribution within a layer under the 5-axis deposition scheme. The statistics for
freeform suggest that the simulation reasonably captures the magnitude and trend of residual strains in
freeform wall.
  Overall, the work demonstrates that a physics-based, tool path strategy-aware FE framework can predict
the residual strain field of the wall with and without a base plate with good quantitative agreement while
reasonably capturing the dominant magnitude and trend of residual strains in the more complex freeform
wall. It also shows critical modeling and measurement limitations that provide a roadmap for future
improvement of the process.
