Abstract
. We consider a model Hamiltonian, introduced by Fermi in 1936, describing a two-particle system made of a neutron and a harmonically bound proton, where the neutron-proton interaction has the form of a δ \delta -potential. For such Hamiltonian we prove the Limiting Absorption Principle and describe the stationary scattering theory. Finally, we derive Fermi’s formula for the scattering cross-section valid in the Born approximation.

1.
Introduction

In the 1930s, Fermi and his research group in Rome were involved in fundamental experimental studies on radioactivity induced by slow neutrons. However, as is well known, a typical aspect of Fermi’s scientific personality was to combine experimental activity with the development of theoretical models capable of clarifying and explaining the experimental results. Following this approach, in 1936 Fermi published an article (
[
12
]
) where he proposed a model for analysing the scattering of slow neutrons by protons. Taking into account that the proton-neutron interaction is extremely intense and its range is much shorter than the wavelength of the incident neutron, Fermi assumed that the interaction could be reasonably described by a Dirac delta function. Then he considered separately the three cases of a fixed proton, a free proton and a proton harmonically bound around an equilibrium position and he computed in each case the scattering cross-section in the Born approximation. In particular, in the case of a collision between the neutron and a harmonically bound proton he found the following scattering cross-section (see also
[
17
]
p. 644):

(1.1)

\frac{d\sigma_{\mathbf{n}}}{d{\Omega}}=\frac{4a^{2}}{|\mathbf{n}|!}\,\frac{|\mathbf{p}|}{|\mathbf{p}_{0}|}\left(\frac{(\mathbf{p}-\mathbf{p}_{0})^{2}}{2m\hbar\omega}\right)^{\!|\mathbf{n}|}e^{-\frac{(\mathbf{p}-\mathbf{p}_{0})^{2}}{2m\hbar\omega}}.

In (
1.1
) the oscillator undergoes the transition from the ground state to the state labeled by
\mathbf{n}=(n_{1},n_{2},n_{3})
, with
|\mathbf{n}|=n_{1}+n_{2}+n_{3}
,
a
is a constant known as scattering length,
\mathbf{p}_{0},\mathbf{p}
are initial and final momenta of the neutron,
m
is the mass and
\omega
is the frequency of the oscillator.
In the special case of elastic collision, formula (
1.1
)
reduces to:

(1.2)

\frac{d\sigma_{\mathbf{0}}}{d{\Omega}}=4a^{2}\,e^{-\frac{(\mathbf{p}-\mathbf{p}_{0})^{2}}{2m\hbar\omega}}\Big|_{|\mathbf{p}|=|\mathbf{p}_{0}|}.

From (
1.2
), one also compute the total cross-section

(1.3)

\sigma_{\mathbf{0}}=4\pi a^{2}\,\frac{\hbar\omega}{E}\left(1-e^{-\frac{4E}{\hbar\omega}}\right),

where
E
is the energy of the neutron. The above formulas show the explicit dependence of the collision process on the frequency of the oscillator, and therefore on the motion of the proton.
It is important to emphasize once again that Fermi’s approach was perturbative and, due to the singular nature of the interaction, could not go beyond Born’s approximation.

In the same years, Bethe and Peierls (
[
5
]
) developed a complete theory, i.e. valid at any perturbative order, for the case of a fixed proton and later Berezin and Faddeev (
[
4
]
) provided a rigorous mathematical construction of the Hamiltonian (see also
[
2
]
for a comprehensive treatment of the whole subject).

Here we are interested in the case of a harmonically bound proton. The corresponding Hamiltonian can be formally written as:

(1.4)

\widetilde{H}=-\Delta_{\mathbf{x}}-\Delta_{\mathbf{y}}+\frac{1}{4}\,\omega^{2}|\mathbf{y}|^{2}-\frac{3}{2}\,\omega+\delta(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}),

where
\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}
denote the coordinates of the neutron and the proton respectively. For simplicity, we have fixed
\hbar=1
, the masses of both particles equal to
1/2
and the ground state energy of the oscillator equal to zero.
Notice that in (
1.4
) the unperturbed Hamiltonian is

(1.5)

H_{0}=-\Delta_{\mathbf{x}}-\Delta_{\mathbf{y}}+\frac{1}{4}\,\omega^{2}|\mathbf{y}|^{2}-\frac{3}{2}\,\omega,

and the interaction term is non trivial only on the coincidence hyperplane

(1.6)

\pi=\big\{(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\in\mathbb{R}^{6}\,|\,\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{y}\big\}.

A rigorous counterpart
H,\mathcal{D}(H)
of (
1.4
) as a well defined self-adjoint and bounded from below operator in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{6})
was constructed in
[
8
]
(see also
[
7
]
) using a renormalisation technique and the theory of quadratic forms. Roughly speaking, the Hamiltonian
H,\mathcal{D}(H)
is characterised by the following two conditions

(C-1)

\;H\psi=H_{0}\psi
for any
\psi\in\mathcal{D}(H)
such that
\text{Tr}_{\pi}\psi=0
,
where
\text{Tr}_{\pi}
denotes the trace on
\pi
;

(C-2)

\;\psi\in\mathcal{D}(H)
satisfies the boundary condition on
\pi

(1.7)

\psi(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})=\frac{\xi(\mathbf{y})}{|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}|}+\alpha\,\xi(\mathbf{y})+o(1)\;\;\;\;\;\;\text{for}\;\;\mathbf{x}\to\mathbf{y},

where

(1.8)

\xi(\mathbf{y}):=\lim_{\mathbf{x}\to\mathbf{y}}|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}|\psi(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}),

and
\alpha\-\in\-\mathbb{R}
is the inverse of the two-body scattering length.

Condition (C-1) expresses that fact that the interaction takes place only when the positions of the neutron and the proton coincide,
while (C-2) is the boundary condition imposed on
\pi
and it is the direct generalisation of the Bethe and Peierls boundary condition for the case of fixed proton.

The aim of this work is to show the validity of the Limiting Absorption Principle (LAP) and to develop the stationary scattering theory for
H,\mathcal{D}(H)
. As a final result, we also derive Fermi’s formula (
1.1
) in the Born approximation.

In the next Section we will recall the rigorous definition and the main properties of
H,\mathcal{D}(H)
and then we will give the precise formulation of our main results.

For the convenience of the reader, we collect here some notation used throughout the paper.
We denote the integer numbers with zero included by
\mathbb{N}_{0}=\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
. We adopt a unitary definition of the
Fourier transform in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})

\hat{f}(\mathbf{k})=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d/2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\!\!d\mathbf{x}\,e^{-i\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{x}}\,f(\mathbf{x}).

Weighted Sobolev spaces are defined in the following way:

\displaystyle L^{2}_{s}(\mathbb{R}^{d})=\{f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{C}\,|\langle x\rangle^{s}f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\}\qquad\|f\|_{L^{2}_{s}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}=\|\langle x\rangle^{s}f\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\;\;s\in\mathbb{R},

\displaystyle H^{2}_{s}(\mathbb{R}^{d})=\{f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{C}\,|f,\,\Delta f\in L^{2}_{s}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\}\qquad\|f\|_{H^{2}_{s}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}=\|f\|_{L^{2}_{s}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}+\|\Delta f\|_{L^{2}_{s}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}.

Given two Hilbert spaces
\mathcal{K}_{1}
and
\mathcal{K}_{2}
, we denote by
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{K}_{1},\mathcal{K}_{2})
the space of bounded linear operators
from
\mathcal{K}_{1}
to
\mathcal{K}_{2}
equipped with the uniform topology, while
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{K}_{1})
stands for
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{K}_{1},\mathcal{K}_{1})
.
We also denote the closed ideal of compact operators in
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{K}_{1})
by
\mathcal{B}_{\infty}(\mathcal{K}_{1})
.

For a free particle in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
we have

(1.9)

\displaystyle h_{0}=-\Delta_{\mathbf{x}}\,,\;\;\;\;\;\;\mathcal{D}(h_{0})=H^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3})\,,\;\;\;\;\;\;r_{0}(z)=(h_{0}-z)^{-1}\,,\;\;\;\;\;\;z\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\overline{\mathbb{R}^{+}}.

For an harmonic oscillator in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
we have

(1.10)

\displaystyle h_{\omega}=-\Delta_{\mathbf{y}}+\frac{1}{4}\,\omega^{2}|\mathbf{y}|^{2}-\frac{3}{2}\,\omega\,,\;\;\;\;\;\;\mathcal{D}(h_{\omega})=H^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3})\cap L^{2}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3})\,,\;\;\;\;\;\;r_{\omega}(z)=(h_{\omega}-z)^{-1}\,,\;\;\;\;\;\;z\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\mathbb{N}_{0}.

Notice that the characterization of
\mathcal{D}(h_{\omega})
follows from the separation property, see
[
9
,
10
,
11
]
.
For
\mathbf{n}=(n_{1},n_{2},n_{3})\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{3}\,,

\;\phi_{\mathbf{n}}
is a normalized eigenfunction of
h_{\omega}
corresponding to the eigenvalue

\omega|\mathbf{n}|
, where
|\mathbf{n}|=(n_{1}+n_{2}+n_{3})\,.
The degeneracy of the eigenvalue
\,\omega|\mathbf{n}|\,
is
\,\frac{1}{2}(|\mathbf{n}|+1)(|\mathbf{n}|+2)
.

2.
Model Hamiltonian and main results

We first recall the definition and some properties of our model Hamiltonian (see
[
8
]
,
[
7
]
). Let us denote the free resolvent

(2.1)

R_{0}(z)=(H_{0}-z)^{-1}\,,\;\;\;\;\;\;z\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\overline{\mathbb{R}^{+}}

and the corresponding integral kernel

(2.2)

\displaystyle G^{z}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y};\mathbf{x}^{\prime},\mathbf{y}^{\prime})=\!\sum_{\mathbf{n}\in\mathbb{N}^{3}_{0}}r_{0}(z-\omega|\mathbf{n}|)(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime})\,\phi_{\mathbf{n}}(\mathbf{y})\phi_{\mathbf{n}}(\mathbf{y}^{\prime})\!=\!\sum_{\mathbf{n}\in\mathbb{N}^{3}_{0}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\!\!d\mathbf{k}\,\frac{1}{|\mathbf{k}|^{2}\!+\!\omega|\mathbf{n}|\!-\!z}\frac{e^{i\mathbf{k}\cdot(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{\prime})}}{(2\pi)^{3}}\,\phi_{\mathbf{n}}(\mathbf{y})\phi_{\mathbf{n}}(\mathbf{y}^{\prime}).

Let us also introduce the quadratic form in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
for any
\lambda>0

(2.3)

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}(\Phi^{\lambda})=H^{1/2}(\mathbb{R}^{3})\cap L^{2}_{1/2}(\mathbb{R}^{3})

(2.4)

\displaystyle\Phi^{\lambda}(\xi)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{6}}G^{-\lambda}(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{y};\mathbf{y}^{\prime},\mathbf{y}^{\prime})|\xi(\mathbf{y})-\xi(\mathbf{y}^{\prime})|^{2}\,d\mathbf{y}\,d\mathbf{y}^{\prime}+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}a^{\lambda}(\mathbf{y})|\xi(\mathbf{y})|^{2}d\mathbf{y}

where

is a spherically symmetric and increasing function such that

, see
[
7
]
for the explicit expression. In
[
8
]
it is proved that there exists
\lambda_{0}>0
such that for any
\lambda>\lambda_{0}
the quadratic form
\Phi^{\lambda},\mathcal{D}(\Phi^{\lambda})
is closed and

(2.5)

\Phi^{\lambda}(\xi)\geqslant a^{\lambda}(0)\,\|\xi\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}

Let us denote by
\Gamma(-\lambda)
,
\mathcal{D}(\Gamma(-\lambda))
the unique self-adjoint and positive operator associated to
\Phi^{\lambda},D(\Phi^{\lambda})
for
\lambda>\lambda_{0}
. Let us also define the “potential” produced by the “charge distribution”

(2.6)

\mathcal{G}(z)\xi(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\!\!\,G^{z}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y};\mathbf{y}^{\prime},\mathbf{y}^{\prime})\xi(\mathbf{y}^{\prime})\,d\mathbf{y}^{\prime}

and notice that
\mathcal{G}(-\lambda)\in\mathcal{B}(L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3}),L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{6}))
. Then for any
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
we define our model Hamiltonian in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{6})
as

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}(H)=\big\{\psi\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{6})\,|\,\psi=\phi^{\lambda}+\mathcal{G}(-\lambda)\xi,\,\phi^{\lambda}\in\mathcal{D}(H_{0}),\,\xi\in\mathcal{D}(\Gamma(-\lambda)),\,

(2.7)

\displaystyle\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\big(\Gamma(-\lambda)+\alpha\big)\xi=\text{Tr}_{\pi}\phi^{\lambda}\big\},

(2.8)

\displaystyle(H+\lambda)\psi=(H_{0}+\lambda)\phi^{\lambda}.

The definition is independent of the particular choice of
\lambda
. It turns out that
H,\mathcal{D}(H)
is self-adjoint and bounded from below.
Taking
\lambda>\lambda_{0}
,
\lambda_{0}
sufficiently large, we have that
\,\Gamma(-\lambda)+\alpha\,
is invertible
and
\big(\Gamma(-\lambda)+\alpha\big)^{-1}\!\in\mathcal{B}(L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3}))

Then we can represent the resolvent

(2.10)

R(z)=(H-z)^{-1}\,,\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;z\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\sigma(H)

for
z=-\lambda
,
\lambda>\lambda_{0}
, as

(2.11)

R(-\lambda)=R_{0}(-\lambda)+\mathcal{G}(-\lambda)(\Gamma(-\lambda)+\alpha)^{-1}\mathcal{G}^{\ast}(-\lambda)\,.

In the following
\lambda
will be a fixed number such that
\lambda>\lambda_{0}
. Formula (
2.11
) shows that the free Hamiltonian is recovered in the limit
\alpha\to+\infty
, i.e., when the two-body scattering length
a
goes to zero. The relation between

and
\alpha
will be discussed in Section 5.

One can check that
H,\mathcal{D}(H)
defined in (
2
), (
2.8
) satisfies the conditions C-1, C-2 written in the Introduction. In particular, for
\,|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}|\to 0\,
one has the asymptotic expansion

\mathcal{G}(-\lambda)\xi(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})=\frac{\xi(\mathbf{y})}{|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}|}-\Gamma(-\lambda)\xi(\mathbf{y})+\text{o}(1)

and therefore, for any
\psi\in\mathcal{D}(H)
one finds

(2.12)

\displaystyle\psi(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})=\phi^{\lambda}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})+\mathcal{G}(-\lambda)\xi(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})=\frac{\xi(\mathbf{y})}{|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}|}+\text{Tr}_{\pi}\phi^{\lambda}(\mathbf{y})-\Gamma(-\lambda)\xi(\mathbf{y})+\text{o}(1)\,.

for
|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}|\to 0
. Using the last equation in (
2
), we find that
\psi
satisfies the boundary condition (
1.7
). In other words, the equation
\big(\Gamma(-\lambda)+\alpha\big)\xi=\text{Tr}_{\pi}\phi^{\lambda}

We also recall that in
[

it is proved that the essential spectrum of

Let us now formulate our main results.
As a first preliminary step, we show that representation (
2.11
) extends to the complex plane.

Proposition 2.1
.

The resolvent
R(z)
is a meromorphic operator valued function on
\mathbb{C}\setminus\overline{\mathbb{R}^{+}}
with values in
\mathcal{B}(L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{6}))
and

R(z)=R_{0}(z)+\mathcal{G}(z)(\Gamma(z)+\alpha)^{-1}\mathcal{G}^{\ast}(\overline{z})\qquad z\in\mathbb{C}\setminus(\mathbb{R}^{+}\cup\sigma_{p}(H))

where
\mathcal{G}(z)\in\mathcal{B}(L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3}),L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{6}))
,

(2.14)

\Gamma(z)=\Gamma(-\lambda)-(\lambda+z)\mathcal{G}^{\ast}(-\lambda)\mathcal{G}(z)\qquad z\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\mathbb{R}^{+}

and

for
\,z\in\mathbb{C}\setminus(\mathbb{R}^{+}\cup\sigma_{p}(H))
.

Starting from the representation (
2.13
), we prove the LAP for
H,\mathcal{D}(H)
.

Theorem 2.2
.

There exists a discrete set
\mathscr{N}\subset[0,\infty)
such that for
\mu\in[0,\infty)\setminus\mathscr{N}
and
\mu/\omega\notin\mathbb{N}_{0}
the limits

(2.15)

\lim_{\varepsilon\downarrow 0}R(\mu\pm i\varepsilon):=R^{\pm}(\mu)=R_{0}^{\pm}(\mu)+\mathcal{G}^{\pm}(\mu)\left(\Gamma^{\pm}(\mu)+\alpha\right)^{-1}\mathcal{G}^{*\mp}(\mu)

exist in

for

and they are continuous in
\mu
in the uniform topology. The same statement holds for
s>1
if
\mu/\omega\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
.
Moreover, if we denote by
\mathcal{E}
the set of positive eigenvalues of
H
of finite multiplicity, we have

.

The proofs of Proposition
2.1
and Theorem

are given in Section 3, where also the mapping properties of each operator in the r.h.s. of (

\;\sigma_{ac}(H)=[0,\infty)
,
\;\sigma_{sc}(H)=\emptyset\;
and the wave operators exist and are complete.

The second main result is the eigenfunction expansion theorem for
H,\mathcal{D}(H)
and the corresponding representation for the wave operators
W_{\pm}
.
More precisely, let us define for

\left(\mathcal{F}_{0}f\right)(\mathbf{k},\mathbf{n})=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\overline{\Phi_{0}(\mathbf{k},\mathbf{n},\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})}f(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\,d\mathbf{x}\,d\mathbf{y}\qquad\qquad\Phi_{0}(\mathbf{k},\mathbf{n},\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{3/2}}e^{i\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{x}}\phi_{\mathbf{n}}(\mathbf{y}).

The map

, that is for

we have

We also define the generalized eigenfunctions
\Phi_{\pm}(\mathbf{k},\mathbf{n})
,
{|\mathbf{k}|^{2}}+\omega|\mathbf{n}|\notin\mathscr{N}

(2.18)

W_{\pm}=\mathcal{F}_{\pm}^{\ast}\mathcal{F}_{0},

The proof of Theorem

H

and the limiting absorption principle for

.
We first prove Proposition
2.1
.

Proof.

Let us observe that
R_{0}(z)
is an analytical operator valued function on
\mathbb{C}\setminus\overline{\mathbb{R}^{+}}
with values in
\mathcal{B}(L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{6}),\mathcal{D}(H_{0}))
.
Due to (
1.9
) and (

\mathcal{D}(H_{0})\subset H^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{6})

Hence
R_{0}(z)
is an analytical function on
\mathbb{C}\setminus\overline{\mathbb{R}^{+}}
with values in
\mathcal{B}(L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{6}),H^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{6}))
.
In particular, this implies that
\mathcal{G}^{\ast}(z)=\Tr R_{0}(z)
is analytical
on
\mathbb{C}\setminus\overline{\mathbb{R}^{+}}
with values is in
\mathcal{B}(L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{6}),L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3}))
by trace theorems. By duality
\mathcal{G}(z)
takes values in
\mathcal{B}(L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3}),L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{6}))

Let us discuss the analytical continuation
\Gamma(z)
of
\Gamma(-\lambda)

We notice that the resolvent identity implies that for
\lambda,\mu>0

\mathcal{G}(-\lambda)\xi=\mathcal{G}(-\mu)\xi+(\mu-\lambda)R_{0}(-\lambda)\mathcal{G}(-\mu)\xi.

Since
\mathcal{G}(-\lambda)\xi\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
for
\xi\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
, the second term on the r.h.s. belongs to
H^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{6})
and therefore can be traced on
\pi
.
This implies the following identity

(3.1)

\Gamma(-\mu)=\Gamma(-\lambda)-(\lambda-\mu)\mathcal{G}^{\ast}(-\lambda)\mathcal{G}(-\mu).

Motivated by (
3.1
),
we define the analytical continuation
\Gamma(z)
as in (
2.14
). Moreover, for notational convenience we set

(3.2)

K(z):=\mathcal{G}^{\ast}(-\lambda)\mathcal{G}(z)\qquad z\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\mathbb{R}^{+}.

We observe that
K(z)
is an analytic operator valued function with values in
\mathcal{B}_{\infty}(L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3}))
due to the above analiticity properties of
\mathcal{G}(z)
and that
\mathcal{G}^{\ast}(-\lambda)
is a compact operator from
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{6})
to
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
(see
[
7
]
).
Using (
2.14
), we can cast
\Gamma(z)+\alpha
in the following form

\displaystyle\Gamma(z)+\alpha

\displaystyle=\left(\Gamma(-\lambda)+\alpha\right)^{1/2}\left[I-(\lambda+z)(\Gamma(-\lambda)+\alpha)^{-1/2}K(z)(\Gamma(-\lambda)+\alpha)^{-1/2}\right]\left(\Gamma(-\lambda)+\alpha\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle=\left(\Gamma(-\lambda)+\alpha\right)^{1/2}\left(I-M(z)\right)\left(\Gamma(-\lambda)+\alpha\right)^{1/2}

where we have introduced

We know that

for
\lambda>\lambda_{0}
.
Therefore

for

is an analytic function on

with values in
\mathcal{B}_{\infty}(L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3}))

such that

. Hence, by analytical Fredholm theorem,

is meromorphic on
\mathbb{C}\setminus\mathbb{R}^{+}
with values in
\mathcal{B}(L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3}))
and the same statement holds true for

(3.3)

(\Gamma(z)+\alpha)^{-1}=(\Gamma(-\lambda)+\alpha)^{-1/2}(I-M(z))^{-1}(\Gamma(-\lambda)+\alpha)^{-1/2}.

We notice that the poles of (

.
The representation (
2.13

The analysis is divided in several steps. We first discuss the boundary limits of

The main idea for the whole analysis is the decomposition of the relevant operators into a high energy part and a low energy part.
Using a representation by series, a crucial simplification is that the low energy part is always made by a finite number of terms. On the other hand the high energy part is related to the resolvent of a truncated unperturbed Hamiltonian. Taking the boundary limits of the high part is trivial
since we are considering points in the resolvent set of the truncated Hamiltonian.

In the following proposition we prove the LAP for the free resolvent

Proposition 3.2

\displaystyle R_{0}(\mu+i\varepsilon)

The same arguments can be repeated for

,
by Proposition
3.1
, Proposition
3.3
and Proposition
3.4
.

It remains to prove is that
\mathcal{E}
is discrete.
Suppose by contradiction that
\mathcal{E}
is not discrete, then there exists a sequence of positive eigenvalues
\{\mu_{n}\}
such that
\mu_{n}\to\mu
and, using the same notation as above, there exists also
\{f_{n}\}
,
\{g_{n}\}
,
\{\psi_{n}\}
such that

\psi_{n}=\mathcal{G}^{+}(\mu)g_{n}=\mathcal{G}^{+}(\mu)(\Gamma(-\lambda)+\alpha)^{-1/2}f_{n}.

We do not know a priori if
\mu
is a positive eigenvalue. We can assume that
\|f_{n}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}=1
, then there exists a subsequence, still denoted by
\{f_{n}\}
, such that
f_{n}\rightharpoonup f
.
Each
f_{n}
satisfies

f_{n}=-\left(K^{+}(\mu_{n})-K^{+}(\mu)\right)f_{n}-K^{+}(\mu)f_{n}.

The first term on the r.h.s. converges strongly to zero by the continuity of
K^{+}(\mu)
, since
\|f_{n}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}=1
.
The second term on the r.h.s. converges strongly since
K^{+}(\mu)
is compact. Therefore we obtain that
f_{n}\to f
in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
. Then
\|f\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}=1
and
f\neq 0
. Moreover
g_{n}\to g=(\Gamma(-\lambda)+\alpha)^{-1/2}f
and
\|g\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}\leqslant c
.
Furthermore
\|\psi_{n}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{6})}\leqslant c
by (
3.23
).
Since the collection of the eigenvectors
\psi_{n}
is an orthogonal set, we have
\psi_{n}\rightharpoonup 0
which is absurd. Then
\mathcal{E}
admits no accumulation point and it is discrete.
The same argument shows that these positive eigenvalues must have finite multiplicity and therefore the proof of Theorem
2.2
is complete.
∎

Further results on the spectral and the scattering properties of
H
are direct consequences of Theorem
2.2
.

Corollary 3.5
.

\;\sigma_{ac}(H)=[0,\infty)
,
\;\sigma_{sc}(H)=\emptyset\;
and the wave operators exist and are complete.

Proof.

All these statement follow from standard results, see
[
22
]
. Notice that part of these results were proved also in
[
7
]
by different techniques.
∎

Remark 3.6
.

In the proof of Theorem
2.2
we have not characterized the singular values
\mu\in\mathscr{N}\cap\mathbb{N}
. If we had a non trivial solution of
(I+M^{+}(\mu))f=0
for such
\mu
, and we would repeat the previous arguments
we would not be able to prove that
\psi\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{6})
. In fact, the terms in
\langle f,M^{+}(\mu)f\rangle
with
|\mathbf{n}|=n_{0}=\mu/\omega
would be real and therefore (
3.22
) would hold for
|\mathbf{n}|<n_{0}
.
In other words, we would be not able to infer that

\left.\widehat{\phi_{\mathbf{n}}(\Gamma(-\lambda)+\alpha)^{-1/2}f}\right|_{|\mathbf{k}|=\sqrt{\mu-\omega|\mathbf{n}|}}=0\qquad\qquad|\mathbf{n}|=n_{0}.

This would imply that the corresponding terms in
\mathcal{G}^{+}(\mu)g
have a slower decay in the
\mathbf{x}
variable and do not belong in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{6})
. In particular they would decay at infinity like
|\mathbf{x}|^{-1}

like a zero energy resonance in the one-body case.

Remark 3.7
.

For the one-body case, the proof that positive eigenvalues do not exists relies on some continuation properties of
-\Delta
, see
[
16
]
, or some properties of the virial in conjunction with Mourre theory, see
[
14
]
.
Such technical results do not apply to
H_{0}
due to the presence of the harmonic hoscillator. The extension of such results to our case is outside the scopes of this paper.

4.
Stationary scattering theory

In this section we develop the stationary scattering approach for
H
. We follow and adapt
[
24
]
(for general results in the case of singular interactions, studied with different techniques, see also
[
18
,
19
,
20
]
). For sake of notation, in the following we denote

E=E(\mathbf{k},\mathbf{n})=|\mathbf{k}|^{2}+\omega|\mathbf{n}|\qquad E^{\prime}=E(\mathbf{k}^{\prime},\mathbf{n}^{\prime})={|\mathbf{k}^{\prime}|^{2}}+\omega|\mathbf{n}^{\prime}|.

Then we can cast (
2.16
)
in a more compact form

(4.1)

\displaystyle\Phi_{\pm}(\mathbf{k},\mathbf{n})

\displaystyle=\Phi_{0}(\mathbf{k},\mathbf{n})+\mathcal{G}^{\pm}(E(\mathbf{k},\mathbf{n}))(\Gamma^{\pm}(E(\mathbf{k},\mathbf{n}))+\alpha)^{-1}\Tr\Phi_{0}(\mathbf{k},\mathbf{n})

(4.2)

\displaystyle=\left(I+\mathcal{G}^{\pm}(E(\mathbf{k},\mathbf{n}))(\Gamma^{\pm}(E(\mathbf{k},\mathbf{n}))+\alpha)^{-1}\Tr\right)\Phi_{0}(\mathbf{k},\mathbf{n}).

Since
\Tr\Phi_{0}(\mathbf{k},\mathbf{n})\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
, we have that
\Phi_{\pm}(\mathbf{k},\mathbf{n})
is well defined by Proposition
3.4
and it belongs to
L^{2}_{-s}(\mathbb{R}^{3})\otimes L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
for
s>3/2
.
For later convenience we define in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{6})

\displaystyle Q(z)

\displaystyle=I+\mathcal{G}(z)(\Gamma(z)+\alpha)^{-1}\Tr\qquad\text{Im }z\neq 0

\displaystyle Q^{\pm}(\mu)

\displaystyle=I+\mathcal{G}^{\pm}(\mu)(\Gamma^{\pm}(\mu)+\alpha)^{-1}\Tr\qquad\mu>0\;\;\mu\notin\mathscr{N}.

We also define the formal adjoint operators w.r.t. the inner product of
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{6})

\displaystyle Q^{\ast}(z)

\displaystyle=I+\delta_{\pi}(\Gamma^{\ast}(z)+\alpha)^{-1}\mathcal{G}(z)\qquad\text{Im }z\neq 0

\displaystyle Q^{\pm,\ast}(\mu)

\displaystyle=I+\delta_{\pi}(\Gamma^{\pm,\ast}(\mu)+\alpha)^{-1}\mathcal{G}^{\pm,\ast}(\mu)\qquad\mu>0\;\;\mu\notin\mathscr{N}.

Some remarks are in order. From the definition, we have
Q(z):H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{6})\mapsto L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{6})
and
Q^{\ast}(z):L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{6})\mapsto H^{-s}(\mathbb{R}^{6})
,
s>3/2
as bounded operators due to trace theorems,
but the results of the previous section, see in particular Proposition
3.2
and Lemma
3.3
, show that in fact
Q(z)
is actually defined on functions with trace over
\pi
in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
with values in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{6})
, like
\Phi_{0}
, and
for
Q^{\ast}(z)
a dual statement holds. In the following we will often use this property.
For the boundary values,
Q^{\pm}(\mu)
is defined on function with trace over
\pi
in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
with values in
L^{2}_{-s}(\mathbb{R}^{3})\otimes L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3})

and dual statement holds for
Q^{\pm,\ast}(\mu)
. Moreover, they are continuous in
\mu
when acting on such functions.
With this notation we can write

(4.3)

\Phi_{\pm}(\mathbf{k},\mathbf{n})=Q^{\pm}(E(\mathbf{k},\mathbf{n}))\Phi_{0}(\mathbf{k},\mathbf{n})

and

(4.4)

\displaystyle R(z)=Q(z)\,R_{0}(z)\qquad\text{Im }z\neq 0

(4.5)

\displaystyle R^{\pm}(\mu)=Q^{\pm}(\mu)\,R^{\pm}_{0}(\mu)\qquad\mu>0\;\;\mu\notin\mathscr{N}.

In the next Proposition we prove a crucial identity which relates
\Phi_{\pm}(\mathbf{k},\mathbf{n})
and
R^{\pm}(\mu)
.

Proposition 4.1
.

Let
\mu>0\,,
with
\;\mu\notin\mathscr{N}
. Then following identity holds in
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{6})\otimes\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{6})

(4.6)

\frac{1}{2\pi i}\left(R^{+}(\mu)-R^{-}(\mu)\right)=\sum_{|\mathbf{n}|<n_{0}}\frac{|\mathbf{k}_{n}|}{2}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}\Phi_{\pm}(\mathbf{k}_{n},\mathbf{n})\otimes\overline{\Phi_{\pm}(\mathbf{k}_{n},\mathbf{n})}d\boldsymbol{\omega}

where

\mathbf{k}_{n}=\sqrt{\mu-\omega|\mathbf{n}|}\,\boldsymbol{\omega}.

Proof.

We start from

(4.7)

\frac{1}{2\pi i}\left(r_{0}^{+}(\mu)-r_{0}^{-}(\mu)\right)=\frac{\sqrt{\mu}}{2}\,\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}e(\mathbf{k})\otimes\overline{e(\mathbf{k})}d\boldsymbol{\omega}\qquad\mathbf{k}=\sqrt{\mu}\boldsymbol{\omega}\qquad e(\mathbf{k},\mathbf{x})=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{3/2}}e^{i\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{x}}

where
identity (
4.7
) must be understood in
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{3})\otimes\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
and it is just a rephrasing of (
3.4
) keeping into account the rescaling of the surface measure.
Let us prove that

(4.8)

\frac{1}{2\pi i}\left(R_{0}^{+}(\mu)-R_{0}^{-}(\mu)\right)=\sum_{|\mathbf{n}|<n_{0}}\frac{|\mathbf{k}_{n}|}{2}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}\Phi_{0}(\mathbf{k}_{n},\mathbf{n})\otimes\overline{\Phi_{0}(\mathbf{k}_{n},\mathbf{n})}d\boldsymbol{\omega}

in
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{6})\otimes\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{6})
. Let us notice that, see the final part of the proof of Proposition
3.2
,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\pi i}\left(R_{0}^{+}(\mu)-R_{0}^{-}(\mu)\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\left(R_{0}^{<,+}(\mu)-R_{0}^{<,-}(\mu)\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{|\mathbf{n}|<n_{0}}\frac{1}{2\pi i}(r_{0}^{+}(\mu-\omega|\mathbf{n}|)-r_{0}^{-}(\mu-\omega|\mathbf{n}|))\otimes P_{\mathbf{n}}

since the high-energy parts cancels out. Using (
4.7
), we obtain (
4.8
). We observe that in the sum we can substitute
\{\omega|\mathbf{n}|\leqslant\mu\}
with
\{\omega|\mathbf{n}|<\mu\}
since the terms with
\omega|\mathbf{n}|=\mu
are null.

We now prove (
4.6
).
Using the resolvent identity, we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\pi i}\left(R^{+}(\mu)-R^{-}(\mu)\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\lim_{\varepsilon\downarrow 0}\left(R(\mu+i\varepsilon)-R(\mu-i\varepsilon)\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\pi}\lim_{\varepsilon\downarrow 0}\varepsilon\,R(\mu+i\varepsilon)\,R(\mu-i\varepsilon)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\pi}\lim_{\varepsilon\downarrow 0}\varepsilon\,Q(\mu+i\varepsilon)R_{0}(\mu+i\varepsilon)\,R_{0}(\mu-i\varepsilon)Q^{\ast}(\mu-i\varepsilon)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\lim_{\varepsilon\downarrow 0}Q(\mu+i\varepsilon)\left(R_{0}(\mu+i\varepsilon)-R_{0}(\mu-i\varepsilon)\right)Q^{\ast}(\mu+i\varepsilon).

The same argument can be repeated exchanging signs. We have already discussed the convergence of
(\Gamma(\mu\pm i\varepsilon)+\alpha)^{-1}
to
(\Gamma^{\pm}(\mu)+\alpha)^{-1}
in Proposition
3.4
and
the convergence of
\mathcal{G}(\mu\pm i\varepsilon)
of
\mathcal{G}^{\pm}(\mu)
in Proposition
3.3
. The
delicate point is the presence of the traces in
Q
and
Q^{\ast}
,
because we know that
R_{0}(\mu\pm i\varepsilon)
converge only in
\mathcal{B}(L^{2}_{s}(\mathbb{R}^{3}),L^{2}_{-s}(\mathbb{R}^{3}))\otimes\mathcal{B}(L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3}))
.
In fact, it is sufficient to prove that also the traces of
R_{0}(\mu\pm i\varepsilon)
converge. We write

\displaystyle R_{0}(\mu+i\varepsilon)-R_{0}(\mu-i\varepsilon)

\displaystyle=R_{0}^{<}(\mu+i\varepsilon)-R_{0}^{<}(\mu-i\varepsilon)+2\varepsilon R_{0}^{>}(\mu+i\varepsilon)R_{0}^{>}(\mu-i\varepsilon)

\displaystyle=\sum_{|\mathbf{n}|<n_{0}}\left(r_{0}(\mu+i\varepsilon-\omega|\mathbf{n}|)-r_{0}(\mu-i\varepsilon-\omega|\mathbf{n}|)\right)\otimes P_{\mathbf{n}}

\displaystyle+2i\varepsilon R_{0}^{>}(\mu+i\varepsilon)R_{0}^{>}(\mu-i\varepsilon).

Let us focus on the last term on the r.h.s..
We known that
R_{0}^{>}(\mu\pm i\varepsilon)
converges in
\mathcal{B}(L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{6}),H^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{6}))
and by duality it converges also in
\mathcal{B}(H^{-2}(\mathbb{R}^{6}),L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{6}))
.
Therefore the product converges (and it is bounded in the uniform topology) in
\mathcal{B}(H^{-2}(\mathbb{R}^{6}),H^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{6}))
and its contribution vanishes due to the
\varepsilon
in front.

The low-energy part
R_{0}^{<}(\mu+i\varepsilon)-R_{0}^{<}(\mu-i\varepsilon)
is a finite sum of operators, with integral kernel of the form
A_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y},\mathbf{x}^{\prime},\mathbf{y}^{\prime})
.
Therefore it is sufficient to prove that
A_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime},\mathbf{x}^{\prime})
converges in
\mathcal{B}(L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3}))
.
In fact, they are of the form
\phi_{\mathbf{n}}\,r_{0}(\nu\pm i\varepsilon)\,\phi_{\mathbf{n}}
,
\nu>0
and we have

\|\phi_{\mathbf{n}}\left(r_{0}(\nu\pm i\varepsilon)-r_{0}^{\pm}(\nu)\right)\phi_{\mathbf{n}}\|_{\mathcal{B}(L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3}))}\leqslant c\|\langle x\rangle^{s}\phi_{\mathbf{n}}\|^{2}_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}\|r_{0}(\nu\pm i\varepsilon)-r_{0}^{\pm}(\nu)\|_{\mathcal{B}(L^{2}_{s}(\mathbb{R}^{3}),L^{2}_{-s}(\mathbb{R}^{3}))}

and the r.h.s. goes to zero by Proposition
3.1
.
Therefore we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\pi i}\left(R^{+}(\mu)-R^{-}(\mu)\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi i}Q^{\pm}(\mu)\left(R_{0}^{+}(\mu)-R_{0}^{-}(\mu)\right)Q^{\pm,\ast}(\mu)

\displaystyle=\sum_{|\mathbf{n}|<n_{0}}\frac{|\mathbf{k}_{n}|}{2}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}Q^{\pm}(\mu)\Phi_{0}(\mathbf{k}_{n},\mathbf{n})\otimes\overline{\Phi_{0}(\mathbf{k}_{n},\mathbf{n})}Q^{\pm,\ast}(\mu)d\boldsymbol{\omega}

\displaystyle=\sum_{|\mathbf{n}|<n_{0}}\frac{|\mathbf{k}_{n}|}{2}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}\Phi_{\pm}(\mathbf{k}_{n},\mathbf{n})\otimes\overline{\Phi_{\pm}(\mathbf{k}_{n},\mathbf{n})}d\boldsymbol{\omega},

and this concludes the proof.
∎

We are now in position to prove Theorem
2.3
.

Proof.

We know that
\mathbb{R}^{+}\setminus\mathscr{N}=\bigcup_{j}I_{j}
, where
I_{j}
are open intervals. Let us fix an interval
I\subset I_{j}
and
u\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{6})
. Then by Stone’s formula and Proposition
4.6
we have

\displaystyle\langle E_{I}(H)u,u\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{I}\langle\left(R^{+}(\mu)-R^{-}(\mu)\right)u,u\rangle d\mu

\displaystyle=\int_{I}\sum_{|\mathbf{n}|<n_{0}}\frac{|\mathbf{k}_{n}(\mu)|}{2}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}\left|\mathcal{F}_{\pm}u(\mathbf{k}_{n}(\mu),\mathbf{n})\right|^{2}d\boldsymbol{\omega}\,d\mu

(4.9)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\sum_{\mathbf{n}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}\chi_{\{{k^{2}+\omega|\mathbf{n}|}\in I\}}\left|\mathcal{F}_{\pm}u(\mathbf{k},\mathbf{n})\right|^{2}d\mathbf{k}.

In the last step we changed variable in the integration over
\mu
, introducing
k
defined by
k^{2}=\mu-\omega|\mathbf{n}|
and
\mathbf{k}=k\,\boldsymbol{\omega}
. Notice that the series over
\mathbf{n}
is actually a
finite sum due to the indicator function.
Since
|\langle E_{H}(I)u,u\rangle|\leqslant\|u\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{6})}
, we can extend (
4.9
) in a twofold way: we let
I
invade
I_{j}
and then sum over
j
and we extend the domain from
\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{6})
to

L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{6})
. Since the set
\{k^{2}+\omega|\mathbf{n}|\in\mathscr{N}\}
has zero measure in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
for
\forall\mathbf{n}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{3}
, we obtain
\mathcal{F}_{\pm}:L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{6})\mapsto L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3})\otimes\ell^{2}(\mathbb{N}_{0}^{3})
as a bounded operator and

(4.10)

\|\mathcal{F}_{\pm}u\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3})\otimes\mathbb{N}_{0}^{3}}=\|E_{H}(\mathbb{R}^{+}\setminus\mathscr{N})u\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{6})}.

Let us now prove (
2.18
) and (
2.19
). Let us fix
I
compact interval,
I\subset I_{j}
, and
let us consider
u
such that
(\mathcal{F}_{0}u)(\mathbf{k},\mathbf{n})=\tilde{u}_{\mathbf{n}}(\mathbf{k})
is different from zero for a finite number of
\mathbf{n}
,
\tilde{u}_{\mathbf{n}}(\mathbf{k})\in\mathbb{C}^{\infty}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
and
\text{supp}\,\tilde{u}_{\mathbf{n}}\subset\{|\mathbf{k}|\in I-\omega|\mathbf{n}|\}
.
In particular, we have
E_{H_{0}}(I)u=u
and
u\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{6})
. Let us also consider
v\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{6})
. Using the standard stationary representation of the wave operator
W_{+}
, see
[
24
]
, we have

\displaystyle\langle W_{+}u,v\rangle

\displaystyle=\lim_{\varepsilon\downarrow 0}\frac{\varepsilon}{\pi}\int_{I}\langle R(\mu-i\varepsilon)\,R_{0}(\mu+i\varepsilon)u,v\rangle d\mu

\displaystyle=\lim_{\varepsilon\downarrow 0}\frac{\varepsilon}{\pi}\int_{I}\langle Q(\mu-i\varepsilon)\,R_{0}(\mu-i\varepsilon)\,R_{0}(\mu+i\varepsilon)u,v\rangle d\mu

\displaystyle=\lim_{\varepsilon\downarrow 0}\frac{\varepsilon}{\pi}\int_{I}\langle R_{0}(\mu-i\varepsilon)\,R_{0}(\mu+i\varepsilon)u,Q^{\ast}(\mu-i\varepsilon)\,v\rangle d\mu

\displaystyle=\lim_{\varepsilon\downarrow 0}\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{I}\langle\left(R_{0}(\mu+i\varepsilon)-R_{0}(\mu-i\varepsilon)\right)u,Q^{\ast}(\mu-i\varepsilon)\,v\rangle d\mu.

In order to take the limit in
\varepsilon
under the integral it is sufficient to repeat the arguments used in Proposition
4.6
, to notice that the limit is a continuous function of
\mu
and to recall that we are integrating over a compact set.

Using (
4.8
), (
4.3
) and the definition of
\mathcal{F}_{0}
and
\mathcal{F}_{+}
, we have

\displaystyle\langle W_{+}u,v\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{I}\langle\left(R_{0}^{+}(\mu)-R_{0}^{+}(\mu)\right)u,Q^{+,\ast}(\mu)\,v\rangle d\mu

\displaystyle=\int_{I}d\mu\,\sum_{|\mathbf{n}|<n_{0}}\frac{|\mathbf{k}_{n}|}{2}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}\overline{\langle\Phi_{0}(\mathbf{k}_{n},\mathbf{n}),u\rangle}\langle\Phi_{0}(\mathbf{k}_{n},\mathbf{n}),Q^{+,\ast}(\mu)\,v\rangle d\boldsymbol{\omega}

\displaystyle=\int_{I}d\mu\,\sum_{|\mathbf{n}|<n_{0}}\frac{|\mathbf{k}_{n}|}{2}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}\overline{\langle\Phi_{0}(\mathbf{k}_{n},\mathbf{n}),u\rangle}\langle\Phi_{+}(\mathbf{k}_{n},\mathbf{n}),\,v\rangle d\boldsymbol{\omega}

\displaystyle=\int_{I}d\mu\,\sum_{|\mathbf{n}|<n_{0}}\frac{|\mathbf{k}_{n}|}{2}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}\overline{(\mathcal{F}_{0}u)(\mathbf{k}_{n},\mathbf{n})}(\mathcal{F}_{+}\,v)(\mathbf{k}_{n},\mathbf{n})d\boldsymbol{\omega}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\sum_{\mathbf{n}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}\chi_{\{{k^{2}}+\omega|\mathbf{n}|\in I\}}\overline{(\mathcal{F}_{0}u)(\mathbf{k}_{n},\mathbf{n})}(\mathcal{F}_{+}v)(\mathbf{k},\mathbf{n})d\mathbf{k}

\displaystyle=\langle\chi_{I}\mathcal{F}_{0}u,\mathcal{F}_{\pm}v\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3})\otimes\ell^{2}(\mathbb{N}_{0}^{3})}.

In the last step we changed variable in the integration over
\mu
, introducing
k
defined by
k^{2}=\mu-\omega|\mathbf{n}|
and
\mathbf{k}=k\,\boldsymbol{\omega}
. We can again let
I
invade
I_{j}
and sum over
j
. Since
the set of functions
u
such that
\mathcal{F}_{0}u
has support away from
\mathscr{N}
is dense in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{6})
, we obtain

(4.11)

\langle W_{+}u,v\rangle=\langle\mathcal{F}_{0}u,\mathcal{F}_{+}v\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3})\otimes\ell^{2}(\mathbb{N}_{0}^{3})}.

The same argument can be repeated with minor modifications for
W_{-}
and (
2.18
) is proved.
Properties (
2.19
) immediately follow from the fact that the wave operators exist and are complete. This concludes the proof of Theorem
2.3
.

5.
Born Approximation

In this section we show how one can formally recover Fermi’s formula (
1.1
) for the scattering cross-section in the Born approximation. We plan to perform a more detailed perturbative analysis of the scattering cross-section beyond the Born approximation
in a forthcoming paper.

Due to the result of the previous section we can cast the scattering matrix as

S=W_{+}^{\ast}W_{-}=\mathcal{F}_{0}^{\ast}\mathcal{F}_{+}\mathcal{F}_{-}^{\ast}\mathcal{F}_{0}\,.

Passing to the momentum representation, we write

\hat{S}=\mathcal{F}_{0}S\mathcal{F}_{0}^{\ast}=\mathcal{F}_{+}\mathcal{F}_{-}^{\ast},

and we focus on the
\mathcal{T}
-matrix

\hat{S}=I-2\pi i\mathcal{T}\qquad\quad\mathcal{T}=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\mathcal{F}_{+}\left(\mathcal{F}_{+}^{\ast}-\mathcal{F}_{-}^{\ast}\right).

For
f,g\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3})\otimes\ell^{2}(\mathbb{N}_{0}^{3})
we have

(5.1)

\left(\mathcal{F}_{\pm}^{\ast}f\right)(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}d\mathbf{k}\sum_{\mathbf{n}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}\Phi_{\pm}(\mathbf{k},\mathbf{n},\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})f(\mathbf{k},\mathbf{n})

and

(5.2)

\langle g,\mathcal{T}f\rangle=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{6}}d\mathbf{k}\,d\mathbf{k}^{\prime}\sum_{\mathbf{n},\mathbf{n}^{\prime}}\overline{g(\mathbf{k},\mathbf{n})}\,f(\mathbf{k}^{\prime},\mathbf{n}^{\prime})\int_{\mathbb{R}^{6}}d\mathbf{x}\,d\mathbf{y}\,\overline{\Phi_{+}(\mathbf{k},\mathbf{n},\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})}\left(\Phi_{+}(\mathbf{k}^{\prime},\mathbf{n}^{\prime},\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})-\Phi_{-}(\mathbf{k}^{\prime},\mathbf{n}^{\prime},\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\right).

Therefore the integral kernel of the
\mathcal{T}
-matrix reads

(5.3)

\mathcal{T}(\mathbf{k},\mathbf{n},\mathbf{k}^{\prime},\mathbf{n}^{\prime})=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{6}}d\mathbf{x}\,d\mathbf{y}\,\overline{\Phi_{+}(\mathbf{k},\mathbf{n},\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})}\left(\Phi_{+}(\mathbf{k}^{\prime},\mathbf{n}^{\prime},\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})-\Phi_{-}(\mathbf{k}^{\prime},\mathbf{n}^{\prime},\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\right).

For
\alpha\to+\infty
,
\langle g,\mathcal{T}f\rangle\to 0
and
\hat{S}\to I
, that is the scattering becomes trivial.
The Born approximation consists in finding the first non trivial correction of
\mathcal{T}
for
\alpha
large.
Notice that the perturbative regime near
H_{0}
is achieved for
\alpha\to+\infty
not simply
\alpha\to\infty
as for
\alpha\to-\infty

the Hamiltonian admits infinitely many bound states and the lower bound of
\sigma(H)
diverges to
-\infty
, see
[
7
]
.
The crucial point, as (
4.2
) suggests, is an expansion of
(\Gamma^{\pm}(\mu)+\alpha)^{-1}
in power of
\alpha^{-1}
.
For this purpose, it is sufficient to consider the identity:

(5.4)

(\Gamma^{\pm}(\mu)+\alpha)^{-1}F=\frac{1}{\alpha}F-\frac{1}{\alpha}(\Gamma^{\pm}(\mu)+\alpha)^{-1}\Gamma^{\pm}(\mu)F\qquad F\in\mathcal{D}(\Gamma(-\lambda))

which gives

(5.5)

\Phi_{\pm}(\mathbf{k},\mathbf{n})\simeq\Phi_{0}(\mathbf{k},\mathbf{n})+\frac{1}{\alpha}\mathcal{G}^{\pm}(E(\mathbf{k},\mathbf{n}))\Tr\Phi_{0}(\mathbf{k},\mathbf{n}).

In particular the leading term is

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}(\mathbf{k},\mathbf{n},\mathbf{k}^{\prime},\mathbf{n}^{\prime})

\displaystyle\simeq\frac{1}{2\pi i\alpha}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{6}}d\mathbf{x}\,d\mathbf{y}\,\overline{\Phi_{0}(\mathbf{k},\mathbf{n},\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})}\left(\mathcal{G}^{+}(E^{\prime})-\mathcal{G}^{-}(E^{\prime})\right)\Tr\Phi_{0}(\mathbf{k}^{\prime},\mathbf{n}^{\prime})

\displaystyle\;\;\;\;\sum_{\mathbf{m}<E^{\prime}}\left(r_{0}^{+}(E^{\prime}-\omega|\mathbf{m}|,\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{\prime})-r_{0}^{-}(E^{\prime}-\omega|\mathbf{m}|,\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{\prime})\right)\phi_{\mathbf{m}}(\mathbf{y})\phi_{\mathbf{m}}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime})\,e^{i\mathbf{k}^{\prime}\cdot\mathbf{x}^{\prime}}\phi_{\mathbf{n}^{\prime}}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime})
