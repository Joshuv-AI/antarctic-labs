Adaptive Stabilizer State Fidelity Certification
Kun Wang

Abstract
Certifying the fidelity of a prepared state to a target stabilizer state is a fundamental task in quantum information processing. Ref. [ Phys. Rev. A 99 , 042337 (2019) ] gave the optimal worst-case lower bound from one fixed stabilizer generator gauge, but gauge dependence can leave a large fidelity ambiguity. We develop an adaptive extension that reports the full certified fidelity interval. First, for a single gauge, we derive the complementary optimal worst-case upper bound. We then formulate gauge selection as an adaptive design problem in which each round solves exact endpoint linear programs and chooses a new gauge by a witness elimination policy. We prove monotonic tightening, exact recovery once all nontrivial stabilizers are covered, and the worst-case necessity of full coverage. Finally, we identify structured syndrome distributions for which adaptivity beats this exponential benchmark, and we numerically confirm faster concentration.



I
Introduction

Stabilizer states are pure quantum states specified as the common
+1
eigenspace of an
abelian Pauli group generated by independent commuting Pauli operators
Gottesman 1997
.
This compact Pauli description makes them central to quantum information processing, with
quantum error correction (QEC)
Terhal 2015
and measurement-based quantum computing
(MBQC)
Raussendorf and Briegel 2001
as two prominent examples.
In QEC, stabilizer codes protect logical information through Pauli checks,
while in MBQC, cluster states are stabilizer states that provide the entangled resource.
Stabilizer state resources have been demonstrated on leading quantum
platforms and are advancing rapidly. Particularly,
photonic platforms have demonstrated four-qubit cluster states
Walther et al. 2005
;
superconducting processors have realized surface code and repetitive error detection
experiments
Barends et al. 2014
;
Kelly et al. 2015
, multipartite and cluster state
entanglement
Cao et al. 2023
;
Jiang et al. 2026
, topologically ordered stabilizer states
Satzinger et al. 2021
,
and surface code logical qubits
Google Quantum AI 2023
; trapped-ion platforms have
demonstrated fault tolerant control of logical qubits
Egan et al. 2021
;
and neutral-atom arrays have enabled reconfigurable logical processors
based on stabilizer codes
Bluvstein et al. 2024
;
Bluvstein et al. 2025
.

When such states are used as quantum resources, experimental progress must be accompanied by a
quantitative check of preparation quality:

how close is the prepared state to the target stabilizer state?

This is the task of stabilizer fidelity estimation and certification.
Quantum state tomography can answer this question after reconstructing the state,
but its measurement and classical postprocessing costs grow rapidly with system size,
even though compressed sensing and tensor network variants reduce the cost under
additional structure
James et al. 2001
;
Altepeter et al. 2005
;
Gross et al. 2010
;
Cramer et al. 2010
. Stabilizer state
verification gives powerful hypothesis testing guarantees using Pauli or local
measurements
Hayashi and Morimae 2015
;
Dangniam et al. 2020
;
Li et al. 2020
;
Chen et al. 2025
;
Zheng et al. 2026
,
but it is formulated as accepting or rejecting a preparation under a prescribed test
strategy rather than as computing the tight fidelity interval implied by a fixed data set.
Direct fidelity estimation is sample efficient for
stabilizer targets
Flammia and Liu 2011
;
da Silva et al. 2011
, but it produces an
estimator rather than a worst-case fidelity certificate from the observed stabilizer expectations.
The closest precursor to this work is the fidelity certificate of Kalev, Kyrillidis, and
Linke
Kalev et al. 2019
, hereafter the KKL certificate,
which measures one chosen stabilizer generator and returns the optimal worst-case lower fidelity
consistent with those data. This certificate is experimentally friendly and adversarially meaningful,
but it provides only the lower endpoint and may leave large fidelity ambiguity
when different generator gauges reveal different stabilizer constraints.

Contributions.

We address these drawbacks by making the certificate
adaptive
and
interval-valued
.
First, we derive the complementary optimal worst-case upper bound,
so that one chosen generator gauge yields a full certified fidelity interval.
Second, we develop an adaptive certified interval framework that treats the generator gauge as a design variable and uses a witness-elimination principle to select informative new gauges.
Geometrically, the method progressively cuts down the feasible syndrome polytope;
analytically and numerically, it tightens the certified interval and concentrates
faster than uniform random gauge selection.

Organization.

II
reviews stabilizer gauges and the KKL

III
explains two limitations of the fixed
gauge certificate. Sec.
IV
gives the companion certified upper
endpoint from one chosen gauge. Sec.
V
introduces the adaptive algorithm, and
Sec.
VI
proves its main guarantees. Sec.
VII

presents the numerical simulations. The final sections compare with related methods and discuss
extensions.

II
Gauge freedom in stabilizer fidelity certification

This section reviews the stabilizer states, syndrome, gauge, and KKL certification task.

II.1
Stabilizer states and generator gauge freedom

Let
|{\psi}\rangle
be a stabilizer state on
n
qubits with a fixed reference stabilizer group

\displaystyle S=\langle g_{1},\cdots,g_{n}\rangle\subseteq\mathcal{P}_{n},

(3)

where
g_{1},\cdots,g_{n}
are independent commuting stabilizer generators.
For each
\bm{u}=(u_{1},\cdots,u_{n})\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
, write

\displaystyle g(\bm{u})\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\prod_{j=1}^{n}g_{j}^{u_{j}}\in S.

(4)

Thus each vector in
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
labels a unique stabilizer element of
|{\psi}\rangle
.
We call this fixed map
\bm{u}\mapsto g(\bm{u})
, determined by the original
generators
g_{1},\cdots,g_{n}
, the
reference labeling
;
\bm{u}
is the
reference label of the stabilizer element
g(\bm{u})
.
The standard syndrome projectors are

\displaystyle\forall\bm{s}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n},\quad\Pi_{\bm{s}}=\frac{1}{2^{n}}\prod_{j=1}^{n}\bigl(\mathbbm{1}+(-1)^{s_{j}}g_{j}\bigr).

(5)

Because the generators are independent and commuting, the operators

\{\Pi_{\bm{s}}\}_{\bm{s}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}}
are mutually orthogonal rank-one projectors resolving the identity.
They project onto the simultaneous eigenspaces of the
chosen generators, and
g_{j}\Pi_{\bm{s}}=\Pi_{\bm{s}}g_{j}=(-1)^{s_{j}}\Pi_{\bm{s}}
.
Consequently,

\displaystyle g(\bm{u})=\sum_{\bm{s}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}}(-1)^{\bm{u}\cdot\bm{s}}\Pi_{\bm{s}}.

(6)

For a state
\rho
, define the
syndrome distribution
:

\displaystyle p_{\rho}(\bm{s})\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\tr\left[\rho\Pi_{\bm{s}}\right],\qquad\bm{s}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}.

(7)

The stabilizer fidelity of
\rho
with respect to the stabilizer state
|{\psi}\rangle
is simply the probability of the trivial syndrome
\bm{s}=\bm{0}
,

\displaystyle F(\rho,\psi)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\tr\left[\rho|{\psi}\rangle\!\langle{\psi}|\right]=\tr\left[\rho\Pi_{\bm{0}}\right]=p_{\rho}(\bm{0}).

(8)

On the other hand, taking the expectation of a stabilizer element
g(\bm{u})
in
\rho
gives, for every
\bm{u}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
,

\displaystyle\mu_{\rho}(\bm{u})\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\tr\left[\rho g(\bm{u})\right]=\sum_{\bm{s}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}}(-1)^{\bm{u}\cdot\bm{s}}p_{\rho}(\bm{s})=\widehat{p}_{\rho}(\bm{u}).

(9)

Thus each stabilizer expectation of
\rho
is exactly one Walsh character of the syndrome distribution.

Let
A\in\mathrm{GL}(n,2)
with columns
\bm{a}_{1},\cdots,\bm{a}_{n}
. Since these columns form a basis of
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
,

A
induces a stabilizer generator basis with respect to
\{g_{1},\cdots,g_{n}\}
via

\displaystyle h_{i}=g(\bm{a}_{i}),\qquad i=1,\cdots,n.

(10)

The elements
\{h_{i}\}_{i}
are independent and generate the same stabilizer group
S
. A label
\bm{v}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
in the induced basis
H(A)=(h_{1},\cdots,h_{n})
corresponds to the stabilizer

\displaystyle h(\bm{v})\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\prod_{i=1}^{n}h_{i}^{v_{i}}=g\!\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}v_{i}\bm{a}_{i}\right),

(11)

so the induced basis label
\bm{v}
represents the same stabilizer element as the reference label vector

\bm{u}=\sum_{i}v_{i}\bm{a}_{i}=A\bm{v}
.
Thus
A
records the reference labels of the induced generators column by column:
its
i
th column
\bm{a}_{i}
is the reference label of
h_{i}
, and a general
induced basis label
\bm{v}
is converted to the reference label of the same
stabilizer element by multiplication by
A
.

Throughout this work, we call the choice of an invertible matrix
A\in\mathrm{GL}(n,2)
, or equivalently the induced generator
basis
H(A)
together with its coordinate map back to the reference labels, a
gauge
. The

gauge freedom
is the freedom to change this choice: replacing
A
changes the binary
coordinates used to name the generators and their products, while leaving the stabilizer group
S

and all physical observables unchanged.

II.2
Stabilizer fidelity certification

We now recall the KKL certification setting
Kalev et al. 2019
in the gauge notation: one fixed
gauge determines the accessible generator expectation data, and the certificate is the worst-case
fidelity compatible with those data.

Data access model.
A data acquisition round first chooses a gauge

A\in\mathrm{GL}(n,2)
, equivalently the induced generator basis
H(A)
. The accessible experimental
summary consists of the expectation values of the
n
generators in that chosen gauge,

\displaystyle\mu_{\rho}(\bm{a}_{1}),\cdots,\mu_{\rho}(\bm{a}_{n}).

(12)

We idealize these expectation values as exact. This model does not assume access to retained
individual shot outcomes, nor does it assume that expectation values of products of the chosen
generators can be inferred as additional constraints.
This is the KKL data interface
Kalev et al. 2019
expressed in the present notation based on gauge
covariance, and it leads to the following certification task for a fixed gauge.

KKL certification task.

For a chosen gauge
A
with columns
\bm{a}_{1},\cdots,\bm{a}_{n}
, the KKL certification task
Kalev et al. 2019

asks for the
smallest
target fidelity compatible with the returned generator expectations.
Within the syndrome distribution language, this is the optimization problem

\displaystyle F_{\min}(A;\rho)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\min_{p\in\Delta(\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n})}

\displaystyle\;p(\bm{0})

(13a)

s.t.

\displaystyle\;\widehat{p}(\bm{a}_{i})=\mu_{\rho}(\bm{a}_{i})\quad\forall i\in[n].

(13b)

Because both the constraints and the objective depend on the unknown state
\rho
through its syndrome
distribution, this distributional optimization is equivalent to minimizing over all quantum states
that could have produced the same expectation data from one gauge.
Ref.
Kalev et al. 2019
solves this problem in
closed form, giving the optimal lower fidelity certificate from one gauge

\displaystyle F_{\min}(A;\rho)=\max\left\{0,\,1-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\bigl(1-\mu_{\rho}(\bm{a}_{i})\bigr)\right\}.

(14)

We call this quantity the KKL certificate.
It is optimal for the data produced by the
chosen
gauge
A
.

The importance of KKL certification is twofold.
First, it uses only one fixed gauge.
The experiment needs to estimate the expectations of the chosen generators, rather
than the ability to measure the full stabilizer group or adaptively choose many observables,
which makes the data access model simple and experimentally friendly.
Second, it addresses a critical
adversarial certification
scenario:
among all quantum states consistent with the measured generator expectations, it reports
the smallest possible stabilizer fidelity, and therefore gives a rigorous worst-case lower fidelity
certificate without assuming a specific noise model.

III
Drawbacks of the KKL certificate

This section explains why the fixed-gauge KKL certificate is not the end of the story. First, it
reports the optimal lower endpoint from one chosen gauge, but the same generator data also define a
certified upper endpoint. This motivates the companion upper certificate in
Sec.
IV
. Second, the lower certificate can depend sharply on the chosen
gauge, and additional gauges can remove fidelity ambiguity without measuring the full non-identity
stabilizer group.

III.1
Gauge dependence and residual ambiguity

From Eq. (
14
), the KKL certificate can depend strongly on the chosen gauge:
two gauges for the same stabilizer
group can yield different lower certificates for the same quantum state
\rho
. Sec.
III.2

gives explicit examples. Prop.
1
isolates the underlying information theoretic
reason. For any fixed gauge, the
n
generator expectations need not uniquely determine
p(\bm{0})
.

Proposition 1
.

For every
A\in\mathrm{GL}(n,2)
with columns
\bm{a}_{1},\cdots,\bm{a}_{n}
and every
n\geq 2
, there exist two syndrome distributions
p,q\in\Delta(\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n})
such that

\displaystyle\widehat{p}(\bm{a}_{i})=\widehat{q}(\bm{a}_{i})\qquad\forall i\in[n],

(15)

but
p(\bm{0})\neq q(\bm{0})
.

The proof in Appx.
A.1
constructs such
p
and
q
explicitly.
For any such pair, the two diagonal states

\displaystyle\rho_{p}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\sum_{\bm{s}}p(\bm{s})\Pi_{\bm{s}},\qquad\rho_{q}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\sum_{\bm{s}}q(\bm{s})\Pi_{\bm{s}}

(16)

produce identical generator expectations in the chosen gauge
A
, but have different target fidelities.
Thus data from a single gauge are not sufficient for fidelity determination in general.
We emphasize that Prop.
1
does
not
contradict
Kalev et al. 2019
:
the KKL certificate remains optimal for the data from one chosen gauge.
Rather, Prop.
1
says that the data from a single gauge may be
insufficient to identify the fidelity. Additional stabilizer expectations are needed to remove this
residual ambiguity.

This motivates replacing fixed-gauge certification by
adaptive
gauge choice: each new gauge adds
further Walsh character constraints,
shrinking the feasible set and tightening the certified fidelity interval.

III.2
Illustrative examples

The following three qubit example gives compact motivation for adaptive certification by gauge
choice. Take the target state
|{\psi}\rangle=|{000}\rangle
with reference generators
Z_{1},Z_{2},Z_{3}
, and
consider the diagonal state

\displaystyle\rho_{\mathrm{ex}}=\frac{1}{4}\bigl(

\displaystyle|{000}\rangle\!\langle{000}|+|{100}\rangle\!\langle{100}|

\displaystyle+|{010}\rangle\!\langle{010}|+|{001}\rangle\!\langle{001}|\bigr),

(17)

whose true fidelity is
F(\rho_{\mathrm{ex}},|{\psi}\rangle)=1/4
.

Gauge choice matters.

For the canonical gauge
A_{\mathrm{can}}
, whose induced generators are

(Z_{1},Z_{2},Z_{3})
, the measured expectations are
(1/2,1/2,1/2)
, and
Eq. (
14
) is already exact, giving
L_{\mathrm{KKL}}=1/4
. For the equally valid
parity gauge
A_{\mathrm{par}}
, whose induced generators are

(Z_{1}Z_{2},\ Z_{1}Z_{3},\ Z_{1}Z_{2}Z_{3})
, the measured expectations are instead
(0,0,-1/2)
, and the same
KKL formula gives the trivial lower certificate
L_{\mathrm{KKL}}=0
. Thus two
gauges for the same stabilizer group and the same state can produce radically different KKL
certificates. The same one gauge data also carry upper endpoint information: the canonical gauge
gives the upper certificate
3/4
, while the parity gauge gives the upper certificate
1/4
. Thus
the upper endpoint can be informative even when the lower KKL certificate is loose.

Additional gauges help.

The parity gauge also shows why an additional gauge can be genuinely useful. If only

A_{\mathrm{par}}
is used, the data are compatible with a zero fidelity explanation, so the lower
certificate from one gauge is vacuous. Adding the canonical gauge
A_{\mathrm{can}}
supplies the
missing single qubit stabilizer expectations,

\displaystyle\widehat{p}(\bm{100})=\widehat{p}(\bm{010})=\widehat{p}(\bm{001})=1/2.

(18)

Together with the parity gauge constraints, these data force the syndrome distribution of

\rho_{\mathrm{ex}}
, and hence force the fidelity to be exactly
1/4
. However, the two queried
gauges cover only the labels

\bm{100},\bm{010},\bm{001},\bm{110},\bm{101},\bm{111}
;
the stabilizer
Z_{2}Z_{3}
, corresponding to
\bm{u}=\bm{011}
, is never queried. Thus the
improvement comes from a genuinely informative additional gauge, not from measuring the whole
non-identity stabilizer group.

IV
Certified upper bound

The KKL certificate gives the optimal certified lower bound on the stabilizer fidelity
from the generator data of a chosen gauge.
We show that the same data also determine an optimal certified upper bound,
so one chosen gauge already certifies a full fidelity interval.
All proofs in this section are collected in Appx.
B
.

Replacing the minimization in Eq. (
13
) by a maximization gives the
certified upper bound on the stabilizer fidelity for the same one gauge feasible set:

\displaystyle F_{\max}(A;\rho)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\max_{p\in\Delta(\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n})}

\displaystyle\;p(\bm{0})

(19a)

s.t.

\displaystyle\;\widehat{p}(\bm{a}_{i})=\mu_{\rho}(\bm{a}_{i})\quad\forall i\in[n].

(19b)

We show that this problem admits an analytic solution.

Proposition 2
.

The optimal value of Eq. (
19
) is

\displaystyle F_{\max}(A;\rho)=\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{2}\min_{i\in[n]}\mu_{\rho}(\bm{a}_{i}).

(20)

Prop.
2

complements the KKL certificate in Eq. (
14
). Together, the generator data
from one chosen gauge certify the full fidelity interval

\displaystyle\left[\max\left\{0,1-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1-\mu_{\rho}(\bm{a}_{i})}{2}\right\},\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{2}\min_{i\in[n]}\mu_{\rho}(\bm{a}_{i})\right].

This interval corresponds to the case of
t=1
in Fig.
2
.

Finite estimates.

Experimentally, the expectation values of stabilizers from a chosen gauge can only
be approximated from measurement statistics and therefore have shot noise.
The upper fidelity certificate also admits a direct finite-shot version.
For the generator labelled by
\bm{a}_{i}
, let
Y_{i,j}\in\{0,1\}
record the outcome of the
j
th measurement, with
Y_{i,j}=1
for
the
+1
outcome and
Y_{i,j}=0
for the
-1
outcome. Then

\displaystyle a_{i}

\displaystyle\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\mathbb{E}[Y_{i,j}]=\frac{1+\mu_{\rho}(\bm{a}_{i})}{2},

(21)

\displaystyle\widehat{a}_{i}

\displaystyle\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\frac{1}{m_{i}}\sum_{j=1}^{m_{i}}Y_{i,j}=\frac{1+\widehat{\mu}_{i}}{2}.

(22)

Define the empirical upper endpoint

\displaystyle\widehat{U}_{A}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\min_{i\in[n]}\widehat{a}_{i}=\min_{i\in[n]}\frac{1+\widehat{\mu}_{i}}{2}.

(23)

Prop.
3
gives a confidence guarantee for this empirical upper endpoint.

Proposition 3
.

Fix a chosen gauge
A
, an accuracy parameter
\varepsilon>0
, and a failure probability

\delta\in(0,1)
. If every generator in the gauge is measured independently at least

\displaystyle m_{i}\geq\left\lceil\frac{\log(2n/\delta)}{2\varepsilon^{2}}\right\rceil

(24)

times, then with probability at least
1-\delta
,

\displaystyle\left|\widehat{U}_{A}-F_{\max}(A;\rho)\right|\leq\varepsilon.

(25)

Since the true fidelity is never larger than the exact maximum compatible with the one gauge data,
Prop.
3
immediately gives a certified upper fidelity bound
on the stabilizer fidelity from finite samples.

Corollary 4
.

Under the sampling rule in Eq. (
24
), the true stabilizer fidelity
obeys

\displaystyle F(\rho,\psi)\leq\widehat{U}_{A}+\varepsilon

(26)

with probability at least
1-\delta
. Thus a chosen gauge upper fidelity certificate with additive
statistical slack
\varepsilon
uses

\displaystyle M_{\mathrm{u}}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}m_{i}=n\left\lceil\frac{\log(2n/\delta)}{2\varepsilon^{2}}\right\rceil

(27)

copies of the state.

The finite-shot behavior differs from the KKL lower certificate because the upper certificate is a
minimum of the estimated success probabilities. A uniform error
\varepsilon
in all estimated

a_{i}
changes the upper endpoint by at most
\varepsilon
, whereas the KKL lower certificate contains
a sum over all
n
generator errors. Hence the statistical overhead for the upper certificate is
linear in
n
up to logarithmic factors. This completes the one gauge certified interval:
Ref.
Kalev et al. 2019
gives the lower fidelity certificate, while Prop.
2
and
Cor.
4
give the companion upper fidelity certificate
and its finite-shot version.

V
Adaptive stabilizer fidelity certification

Sec.
III
showed that a fixed gauge can leave substantial fidelity
ambiguity, while Sec.
IV
turned one gauge data into a certified
interval with a KKL certified lower bound and a complementary certified upper bound.
The next question is how to reduce this interval when more generator data can be acquired.

This section develops an adaptive answer. After each queried gauge, we keep all syndrome
distributions compatible with the accumulated Walsh constraints, report the certified fidelity
interval over this feasible set, and choose the next gauge to shrink the current ambiguity as much as possible.

V.1
Feasible polytope

After
t
rounds, suppose gauges
A_{1},\cdots,A_{t}
have been queried, where the columns of
A_{r}
are
\bm{a}_{r,1},\cdots,\bm{a}_{r,n}
. In the ideal data access model, querying the gauge
A_{r}
reveals the
exact expectations
\mu_{\rho}(\bm{a}_{r,1}),\cdots,\mu_{\rho}(\bm{a}_{r,n})
. Let

\displaystyle Q_{t}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\{\bm{a}_{r,i}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}r\leq t,\ i\in[n]\}

(28)

be the set of stabilizer labels queried up to round
t
.
The exact
feasible polytope
determined by the first
t
rounds is

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{t}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\{p\in\Delta(\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n})\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\widehat{p}(\bm{u})=\mu_{\rho}(\bm{u})\ \forall\bm{u}\in Q_{t}\}.

(29)

Correspondingly, the certified
fidelity interval
is given by

\displaystyle L_{t}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\min_{p\in\mathcal{F}_{t}}p(\bm{0}),\qquad U_{t}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\max_{p\in\mathcal{F}_{t}}p(\bm{0}),

(30)

where
L_{t}
represents the lower fidelity certificate and
U_{t}
represents the upper fidelity certificate
given by the
t
-th round. Define the
interval width

W_{t}=U_{t}-L_{t}
.
By construction, the true distribution belongs to
\mathcal{F}_{t}
, so

\displaystyle L_{t}\leq F(\rho,\psi)\leq U_{t}.

(31)

When
t=1
, this construction recovers the one gauge fidelity interval obtained in
Sec.
IV
.

V.2
Adaptive choice of the next gauge

The core adaptive question is how to choose
A_{t+1}
so that we can further shrink the interval width. Let

\displaystyle p_{t}^{-}\in\argmin_{p\in\mathcal{F}_{t}}p(\bm{0}),\qquad p_{t}^{+}\in\argmax_{p\in\mathcal{F}_{t}}p(\bm{0})

(32)

be the lower and upper endpoint witnesses for the certified interval. These witnesses are feasible syndrome distributions that attain the two optimal values defining
[L_{t},U_{t}]
.
For any candidate stabilizer
\bm{u}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}\setminus\{\bm{0}\}
, define its
disagreement score

\displaystyle d_{t}(\bm{u})\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\bigl|\widehat{p}_{t}^{+}(\bm{u})-\widehat{p}_{t}^{-}(\bm{u})\bigr|.

(33)

To make the acquisition policy in Eq. (
32
) reproducible,
we can fix once and for all an ordering

\bm{s}_{1},\cdots,\bm{s}_{2^{n}}
of
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
. If either endpoint LP has multiple optimal
solutions, choose the lexicographically smallest probability vector on the corresponding optimal face.
Equivalently, one may solve a fixed sequence of secondary linear optimizations over the optimal face. This convention
determines
p_{t}^{-}
,
p_{t}^{+}
, and hence
d_{t}
, without changing the certified endpoints

L_{t}
and
U_{t}
.

The witness elimination principle.

The key design idea behind the disagreement score metric is the
witness elimination principle
.
The current interval width
W_{t}
is certified by two feasible endpoint witnesses,
p_{t}^{-}
and
p_{t}^{+}
,
which agree with all data collected so far but assign different values to the target coordinate
p(\bm{0})
.
Querying a new stabilizer label
\bm{u}
adds the linear constraint
\widehat{p}(\bm{u})=\mu_{\rho}(\bm{u})
,
i.e., it cuts the feasible polytope by a hyperplane. If
\widehat{p}_{t}^{-}(\bm{u})
and
\widehat{p}_{t}^{+}(\bm{u})
are far apart,
then the two current endpoint witnesses make strongly different predictions for the outcome of this query.
Once the true value
\mu_{\rho}(\bm{u})
is revealed, at least one of these two particular witnesses is eliminated unless both predictions coincide. Thus
d_{t}(\bm{u})
measures how directly the query tests the witnesses responsible for the present fidelity ambiguity. By contrast, if
d_{t}(\bm{u})=0
, then this query does not separate the two current endpoint witnesses and is not expected to reduce the interval in the direction of
p(\bm{0})
, although it may still remove other feasible points.
This principle is the design criterion behind the disagreement score and the subsequent gauge selection policy.
This geometry is summarized in Fig.
1
: in the two-dimensional projection onto the target coordinate
p(\bm{0})
and the queried Walsh coordinate
\widehat{p}(\bm{u})
, the before-query region
\mathcal{F}_{t}
and the after-query section
\mathcal{F}_{t+1}
can be seen separately, while
d_{t}(\bm{u})
is the separation between the two endpoint witnesses in the queried direction. The updated feasible set remains a polytope in the ambient syndrome-probability space; it appears as a line segment in this projection only because the plotted coordinate
\widehat{p}(\bm{u})
has been fixed by the new query.

Disagreement score of a gauge.

A candidate gauge
A
with columns
\bm{a}_{1},\cdots,\bm{a}_{n}
is then scored by

\displaystyle\Phi_{t}(A)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}d_{t}(\bm{a}_{i}).

(34)

This additive score is a tractable surrogate for the ideal but more expensive one step lookahead
objective that would recompute the fidelity interval for every possible next gauge and every
possible returned data vector. In the generator-only interface, one round returns the
n
Walsh
coefficients associated with the columns of one gauge. Summing
d_{t}(\bm{a}_{i})
therefore favors gauges
whose generators collectively test many high disagreement directions, while the independence
constraint prevents spending a round on a linearly dependent column set. The additivity also has an
algorithmic advantage: it converts gauge selection into the maximum weight problem over a vector
matroid below, so the next gauge can be found greedily rather than by enumerating
\mathrm{GL}(n,2)
. We use

\Phi_{t}
in this sense: not as a proof of optimal one step interval contraction, but as a
certificate driven acquisition policy designed to attack the current fidelity witnesses with a
computationally controlled gauge choice.

To avoid an entirely redundant round, the next gauge is chosen from gauges containing at least one
previously unqueried stabilizer label. Specifically, for a gauge
A
with column set

C(A)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\{\bm{a}_{1},\cdots,\bm{a}_{n}\}
, define

\displaystyle\Gamma_{t}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\{A\in\mathrm{GL}(n,2)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}C(A)\not\subseteq Q_{t}\}.

(35)

Thus every
A\in\Gamma_{t}
contains at least one stabilizer label that has not
previously been queried. In a nonterminal round, the next gauge is chosen as

\displaystyle A_{t+1}\in\argmax_{A\in\Gamma_{t}}\Phi_{t}(A).

(36)

We refer to the deterministic gauge selection rule in Eq. (
36
) as the

witness elimination policy
.

Converting to a maximum weight problem.

Because the score in Eq. (
34
) is additive over the columns of
A
, the column set
version of Eq. (
36
) is a non-redundant maximum weight problem over a vector matroid
on
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
. The ground set is
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}\setminus\{\bm{0}\}
, with weight
d_{t}(\bm{u})
assigned to element

\bm{u}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}\setminus\{\bm{0}\}
. A feasible set is any linearly independent subset of

\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}\setminus\{\bm{0}\}
, and a vector matroid basis is any set

B=\{\bm{b}_{1},\cdots,\bm{b}_{n}\}\subset\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}\setminus\{\bm{0}\}
with
\rank(B)=n
. Thus the column set of the
next gauge is obtained by solving

\displaystyle B_{t+1}\in\argmax_{\begin{subarray}{c}B\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}\setminus\{\bm{0}\}\\
|B|=n,\ \rank(B)=n\\
B\not\subseteq Q_{t}\end{subarray}}\sum_{\bm{u}\in B}d_{t}(\bm{u}).

(37)

Any ordering of the elements of
B_{t+1}
gives the columns of a matrix
A_{t+1}\in\Gamma_{t}
, and
Eq. (
37
) is equivalent to Eq. (
36
).
By the standard greedy theorem for matroids, sorting elements by nonincreasing weight and adding an
element whenever it preserves independence returns a maximum weight basis for the underlying vector
matroid. Operationally, this greedy vector matroid scan computes the desired column set in every
nonterminal round. Indeed, if
\bm{u}\in Q_{t}
, then every
p\in\mathcal{F}_{t}
has the same Walsh coefficient

\widehat{p}(\bm{u})=\mu_{\rho}(\bm{u})
, so
d_{t}(\bm{u})=0
. If
W_{t}>0
, the endpoint witnesses
p_{t}^{-}
and
p_{t}^{+}

must disagree on at least one unqueried nonzero Walsh coefficient; otherwise Walsh–Hadamard
inversion would force
p_{t}^{-}=p_{t}^{+}
and hence
W_{t}=0
. Therefore some unqueried label has positive
weight, and any maximum weight vector matroid solution has positive total score and cannot be
contained in
Q_{t}
. One may thus sort all nonzero labels
\bm{u}
in nonincreasing order of

d_{t}(\bm{u})
, scan this list, add
\bm{u}
exactly when it increases the rank of the selected set, and
stop once
n
independent vectors have been selected. Zero weight ties should be broken in favor of
unqueried labels, which adds extra constraints at no loss in the acquisition score.

V.3

p(\bm{0})
over

attain these two values. A stabilizer whose Walsh coefficient differs strongly between these
witnesses is expected to cut through the current ambiguity, so the next gauge is chosen to collect

n
independent high disagreement stabilizers.

Performance analysis

VI.1
Classical computational complexity

The exact implementation of Alg.
1
has three classical components in each
nonterminal round:
1) solving the two endpoint linear programs,
2) computing the disagreement spectrum, and
3) selecting the next gauge.
We account for these components separately and then add their costs to obtain
the total classical complexity.

After
t
rounds, the number of queried nontrivial stabilizer labels satisfies

\displaystyle q_{t}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=|Q_{t}|\leq\min\{tn,2^{n}-1\}.

(38)

At round
t
,
\mathcal{F}_{t}
has one variable
p(\bm{s})
for each syndrome

\bm{s}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
, so it has
2^{n}
variables and
O(2^{n}+q_{t})
linear constraints, including
nonnegativity, normalization, and the queried Walsh constraints.

1) Endpoint LP cost.

The lower and upper endpoint problems are two linear programs over this syndrome vector.
A generic LP solver therefore has classical cost

\displaystyle C_{t,\mathrm{LP}}=\operatorname{poly}\!\left(2^{n},q_{t},\log(1/\tau_{\rm num})\right)=2^{O(n)},

(39)

where
\tau_{\rm num}
denotes the requested numerical accuracy. We do not assume a specialized
linear time LP algorithm; the polynomial degree can depend on the solver and numerical model.

2) Disagreement spectrum cost.

After the endpoint witnesses
p_{t}^{-}
and
p_{t}^{+}
are obtained, the disagreement spectrum is the
Walsh transform of the length
2^{n}
vector
p_{t}^{+}-p_{t}^{-}
. A fast Walsh–Hadamard transform gives

\displaystyle C_{t,\mathrm{dis}}=O(n2^{n}).

(40)

3) Gauge selection cost.

The next gauge is obtained by the greedy vector matroid algorithm in Eq. (
37
).
It scans the
2^{n}-1
nonzero stabilizer labels and performs rank updates over
\mathbb{F}_{2}
, giving

\displaystyle C_{t,\mathrm{gauge}}=O\!\left(2^{n}\operatorname{poly}(n)\right),

(41)

where the precise polynomial factor depends on the implementation of the rank updates.

Combining the components gives the per round cost

\displaystyle C_{t}=\operatorname{poly}\!\left(2^{n},q_{t},\log(1/\tau_{\rm num})\right)+O\!\left(2^{n}\operatorname{poly}(n)\right).

(42)

For
T
rounds,

\displaystyle C_{T}=\sum_{t=1}^{T}C_{t}\sim O(2^{n}\operatorname{poly}(n)).

(43)

The classical memory required to store explicit syndrome probability vectors and their Walsh transforms is

O(2^{n})
. Thus the exact implementation is exponential in
n
. The memory and non-LP
postprocessing scale as
O(2^{n}\operatorname{poly}(n))
, while the endpoint LPs require

\operatorname{poly}(2^{n},q_{t},\log(1/\tau_{\rm num}))
time with generic solvers. The matroid
formulation is still important because it keeps gauge selection at the same

O(2^{n}\operatorname{poly}(n))
scale and avoids enumerating ordered gauges in
\mathrm{GL}(n,2)
.

Endpoint optimization relaxation.

The exponential dependence is the main computational limitation of the exact formulation. The
theorems and numerical simulations in this work use the exact optimization layer so that the
reported intervals are the tight certificates for the acquired data. For larger systems, the same
certification layer could be paired with a scalable relaxation of the endpoint optimization. To
remain certified, such a relaxation should optimize over an outer feasible set

\widetilde{\mathcal{F}}_{t}\supseteq\mathcal{F}_{t}
, giving

\displaystyle\widetilde{L}_{t}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\min_{p\in\widetilde{\mathcal{F}}_{t}}p(\bm{0})\leq F(\rho,\psi)\leq\max_{p\in\widetilde{\mathcal{F}}_{t}}p(\bm{0})=\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\widetilde{U}_{t}.

(44)

If the relaxed feasible sets are nested as new gauge constraints are added, the relaxed interval is
monotone by the same argument as Prop.
5
below.

Possible scalable optimization layers include cutting plane or column generation methods with
certified separation
Kelley 1960
;
Bertsimas and Tsitsiklis 1997
;
Desaulniers et al. 2005
;
Lübbecke and Desrosiers 2005
.
Other possibilities include sparse support or coarse grained syndrome relaxations with verified residual
mass bounds, tensor network surrogates with rigorous truncation control, and linear or semidefinite
moment relaxations that exploit low weight or geometrically local stabilizer structure. These
directions are not part of the main results here, and we do not numerically validate them in this
work. Their role is to indicate how the exact benchmark in Eq. (
43
)
could be replaced by conservative optimization layers in future large-scale implementations.

VI.2
Monotonicity

We first record the basic nesting property of the certified interval in the ideal data access
model. Querying a new gauge adds Walsh constraints without removing any old constraints, so
the feasible set can only shrink.

Proposition 5
.

The feasible sets are nested:

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{t+1}\subseteq\mathcal{F}_{t}.

(45)

Consequently,
L_{t}
is nondecreasing,
U_{t}
is nonincreasing, and
W_{t}
is nonincreasing in
t
.

Full stabilizer coverage.

Monotonicity explains why the certified interval can only tighten. The corresponding endpoint
statement below says that, once the queried gauges have covered all nontrivial stabilizer labels, the
feasible set has no remaining freedom and the interval collapses to the true fidelity. The missing
label
\bm{u}=\bm{0}
need not be queried, because
\widehat{p}(\bm{0})=1
for every
probability distribution.

Proposition 6
.

Suppose that for some round
t_{\star}
,

\displaystyle Q_{t_{\star}}=\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}\setminus\{\bm{0}\}.

(46)

Then, for every
t\geq t_{\star}
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{t}=\{p_{\rho}\},\qquad L_{t}=U_{t}=F(\rho,\psi).

(47)

Consequently, if the sequence of queried labels satisfies

\displaystyle\bigcup_{t\geq 1}Q_{t}=\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}\setminus\{\bm{0}\},

(48)

then
L_{t}
and
U_{t}
converge in finite time to the true fidelity.

Prop.
5
and Prop.
6

together give the formal justification for the qualitative behavior shown in Fig.
2
:
the certified interval moves monotonically inward and becomes exact at full stabilizer coverage.

Worst case limitation.

The preceding proposition is a completeness statement: full stabilizer coverage is sufficient for
exactness. In the unrestricted state space, however, full coverage is also necessary in the
worst-case setting.
Thus the interval in Fig.
2
is
guaranteed to move monotonically inward and to collapse after full coverage, but the collapse can
require exponentially many stabilizer labels for adversarial states.

Proposition 7
.

Consider any adaptive generator-only protocol that always outputs the exact fidelity
F(\rho,\psi)
.
Then, in the worst-case setting, the protocol must query all
2^{n}-1
nontrivial stabilizers
g(\bm{u})
with
\bm{u}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}\setminus\{\bm{0}\}
. Consequently, exact fidelity determination
for arbitrary states requires at least

\displaystyle\left\lceil\frac{2^{n}-1}{n}\right\rceil

(49)

full gauge rounds in the worst-case setting.

VI.3
Random gauges and coverage

Prop.
7
rules out a universal fast certification guarantee for
arbitrary states, but it is still useful to quantify conservative rates before full coverage. One
might try to prove such a rate directly for the witness elimination policy in Alg.
1
.
However, its selection criterion is endpoint disagreement, not uniform coverage of all missing labels:
it can keep concentrating on a small set of high disagreement directions, while giving no

state-independent
lower bound on the probability of querying an arbitrary missing label.
Thus a universal coverage argument for the witness elimination policy would require assumptions on the
state or on how disagreement is distributed over the unqueried labels.

We therefore separate two kinds of guarantees. The following random gauges provide a

conservative coverage baseline
: each missing label has a controlled probability of appearing, so the
remaining ambiguity can be bounded without any assumption on the state. The adaptive witness
elimination policy is analyzed separately through the endpoint-disagreement bounds,
which show that it targets the current ambiguity but do not by themselves give a uniform coverage rate.

Randomized gauge policy.

A
uniform random gauge
means a matrix
A\in\mathrm{GL}(n,2)
sampled uniformly from
the finite group
\mathrm{GL}(n,2)
. Equivalently, its columns are an ordered random basis of
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
:
the first column is a uniformly random nonzero label, and each later column is sampled uniformly
outside the span of the previous columns. Since the algorithm records only the queried stabilizer
labels, the relevant object is the unordered column set of this random basis. By symmetry, for any
fixed nonzero label
\bm{u}
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\!\left(\bm{u}\text{ is a column of }A\right)=\frac{n}{2^{n}-1}.

(50)

More generally, a
randomized gauge policy
is any history-dependent procedure that samples

A_{t+1}\in\mathrm{GL}(n,2)
from a probability distribution determined by the current history
\mathcal{H}_{t}
.
The
uniform random gauge policy
samples a uniform random gauge at each round, independently
of the history.

The following Prop.
8
gives a
state-independent

coverage benchmark. It bounds the largest possible remaining interval using only the number of
nontrivial stabilizer labels not yet queried. It also gives an expected decay rate for
W_{t}
under
any randomized gauge policy that gives every missing label a uniform chance of being included in
the next gauge.

Proposition 8
.

Let
m_{t}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=|(\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}\setminus\{\bm{0}\})\setminus Q_{t}|
be the number of nontrivial stabilizer labels not yet queried after round
t
. For every state
\rho
and every sequence of queried gauges,

\displaystyle W_{t}\leq\min\left\{1,\frac{2m_{t}}{2^{n}}\right\}.

(51)

Moreover, suppose the gauge choices follow a randomized gauge policy, set
Q_{0}=\emptyset
, and assume that for some
\alpha\in[0,1]
, every unqueried label has conditional probability at least
\alpha
of being included in the next queried gauge:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\!\left(\bm{u}\in Q_{t+1}\mid\mathcal{H}_{t}\right)\geq\alpha,\qquad\bm{u}\in(\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}\setminus\{\bm{0}\})\setminus Q_{t},

(52)

where
\mathcal{H}_{t}
is the history through round
t
. Then

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[W_{t}]\leq\frac{2^{n}-1}{2^{n-1}}(1-\alpha)^{t}.

(53)

In particular, under the uniform random gauge policy,
\alpha=n/(2^{n}-1)
and

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[W_{t}]\leq\frac{2^{n}-1}{2^{n-1}}\left(1-\frac{n}{2^{n}-1}\right)^{t}\leq 2\exp\!\left(-\frac{nt}{2^{n}-1}\right).

(54)

Prop.
8
is deliberately conservative. It treats all unqueried labels as
potentially important and therefore gives a universal guarantee, but the uniform random rate has the
small exponent
n/(2^{n}-1)
. On the other hand, the deterministic witness elimination policy uses more information: it
looks at the two endpoint witnesses that currently certify the interval width and asks which
unqueried labels distinguish them best. The following quantities measure this remaining endpoint
disagreement:

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} D_{t}&\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\sum_{\bm{u}\in(\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}\setminus\{\bm{0}\})\setminus Q_{t}}d_{t}(\bm{u}),\\
\Delta_{t}&\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\max_{\bm{u}\in(\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}\setminus\{\bm{0}\})\setminus Q_{t}}d_{t}(\bm{u}).\end{aligned}

(55)

Proposition 9
.

For every nonterminal round of Alg.
1
,

\displaystyle W_{t}\leq 2^{-n}D_{t}\leq\frac{m_{t}}{2^{n}}\Delta_{t}.

(56)

Moreover, the witness elimination gauge chosen by Eq. (
36
) satisfies

\displaystyle\Phi_{t}(A_{t+1})\geq\Delta_{t}\geq\frac{2^{n}}{m_{t}}W_{t}.

(57)

Prop.
9
turns the LP endpoint gap into an actionable query signal. Because
the endpoint witnesses already agree on all queried Walsh coefficients, any remaining width must be
carried by unqueried labels; hence a nontrivial
W_{t}
forces nontrivial endpoint disagreement among
those labels. The witness elimination policy then chooses a gauge whose score is at least the largest
such single-label disagreement, so the next gauge is guaranteed to target the labels currently
responsible for the certified gap. This is a deterministic targeting guarantee, not a uniform
coverage guarantee.

Mixed randomized gauge policy.

The deterministic targeting statement in Prop.
9

is not the same as the coverage condition in Eq. (
52
).
In particular, the witness elimination policy may repeatedly focus on the same
region of endpoint disagreement and need not give every still-unqueried label a uniform chance of
being tested. To obtain a state-independent rate while retaining the adaptive choice,
we adopt a mixed randomized gauge policy:
each nonterminal round queries one gauge, but the gauge is sampled from a mixture of
the gauge selected by the witness elimination policy and a uniform random gauge.
Fix
\gamma\in[0,1]
. In each nonterminal round, use the witness elimination gauge with probability

1-\gamma
and sample a uniform random gauge in the sense of
Eq. (
50
) with probability
\gamma
. Then every missing label
has conditional inclusion probability at least
\gamma n/(2^{n}-1)
, coming from the uniform-random
branch of this policy. The witness elimination branch may add further labels, and by monotonicity it
cannot increase the certified width. Therefore Prop.
8
applies with

\alpha=\gamma n/(2^{n}-1)
. Starting from
Q_{0}=\emptyset
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[W_{t}]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2^{n}-1}{2^{n-1}}\left(1-\frac{\gamma n}{2^{n}-1}\right)^{t}

(58a)

\displaystyle\leq 2\exp\!\left(-\frac{\gamma nt}{2^{n}-1}\right).

(58b)

Taken together, Prop.
8
, Prop.
9
, and the mixed
randomized gauge policy bound in Eq. (
58
) separate two kinds of statements.
The uniform random gauge policy gives a universal but slow worst-case benchmark.
The witness elimination gauge policy gives a deterministic certificate that the next
gauge chosen by the witness elimination policy targets the current endpoint ambiguity.
The mixed randomized gauge policy combines both:
the uniform random branch supplies a universal convergence rate, while the witness elimination
branch obtains stronger contraction on structured instances.

VI.4
Affine-support syndrome states

Props.
6
and
7

establish the full coverage benchmark: querying all
2^{n}-1
nontrivial stabilizer labels is
sufficient for exact certification and is necessary in the worst-case setting over unrestricted states.
Here we explore structured states for which this exponential barrier can be avoided, so that the
adaptive certification protocol converges quickly. We identify structured syndrome distributions for
which the fidelity is determined by a much smaller set of Walsh characters.

Let
\bm{s}_{0}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
and
V\leq\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
be a linear subspace of dimension
r
.
Define the probability distribution

\displaystyle\forall\bm{s}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n},\quad p_{\bm{s}_{0},V}(\bm{s})\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=2^{-r}\mathbbm{1}[\bm{s}\in\bm{s}_{0}+V].

(59)

A state
\rho
is called an
r
-dimensional
affine-support syndrome state
relative to

|{\psi}\rangle
if its syndrome distribution satisfies
p_{\rho}=p_{\bm{s}_{0},V}
for some affine subspace

\bm{s}_{0}+V
. This name emphasizes the relevant state structure: the
syndrome support is an affine subspace, and the distribution is flat on that support.
This class of states is experimentally meaningful from a noisy preparation perspective:
an experimenter may aim to prepare
|{\psi}\rangle
, but the preparation can be followed by a classical
random Pauli error whose syndrome is sampled uniformly from the affine set
\bm{s}_{0}+V
.
The resulting state is then an affine-support syndrome state.

For this class, the useful object is not the full collection of Walsh characters but the annihilator

V^{\perp}
. Indeed,

\displaystyle\widehat{p}_{\bm{s}_{0},V}(\bm{u})=(-1)^{\bm{u}\cdot\bm{s}_{0}}\mathbbm{1}[\bm{u}\in V^{\perp}].

(60)

Thus labels in
V^{\perp}
are exactly the fully biased characters, and their signs determine whether
the affine-support
\bm{s}_{0}+V
contains the zero syndrome. If the support contains the zero syndrome,
then the remaining task is to certify that the distribution on
V
is uniform.
For this, one needs the nonzero Walsh characters on
V
. Labels that differ by an element of

V^{\perp}
have the same restriction to
V
, so the quotient
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}/V^{\perp}
parametrizes the
Walsh characters on
V
. Thus choosing one representative of every nonzero coset of

\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}/V^{\perp}
means choosing labels whose restrictions to
V
realize all nontrivial Walsh
characters on
V
.

Proposition 10
.

Run Alg.
1
with
\varepsilon=0
on an

r
-dimensional affine-support syndrome state
p_{\rho}=p_{\bm{s}_{0},V}
, and let

d=n-r
. If, by some round
t
, the queried label set
Q_{t}
contains a basis of
V^{\perp}
and, in
the case
\bm{s}_{0}\in V
, also contains one representative of every nonzero coset of

\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}/V^{\perp}
, equivalently every nontrivial Walsh character restricted to
V
, then
Alg.
1
terminates by round
t
and returns

\displaystyle F(\rho,\psi)=p_{\rho}(\bm{0})=\begin{cases}2^{-r},&\bm{s}_{0}\in V,\\
0,&\bm{s}_{0}\notin V.\end{cases}

(61)

Thus the number of structurally relevant stabilizer labels is
d
if
\bm{s}_{0}\notin V
, and at most

d+2^{r}-1
if
\bm{s}_{0}\in V
.

Prop.
10
states the performance of Alg.
1
in terms of the
labels it has actually acquired. It is a sufficient stopping condition based on the acquired label
set
Q_{t}
; it does not assert that the witness elimination policy necessarily discovers these labels
within
d+2^{r}-1
queried stabilizers for every affine-support instance. For fixed support dimension

r
, the number of structurally relevant labels is only
O(n)
, far below the
2^{n}-1
labels needed
for full coverage. This is the sense in which affine-support structure can remove the
exponential information barrier: the algorithm does not need all Walsh characters, but only the
characters that certify the affine support and, when necessary, its uniformity.

The affine line case also shows why changing gauges is essential. For any predetermined gauge
A

and any
n\geq 2
, there exist two affine line distributions with identical generator expectations in
that gauge but fidelities
1/2
and
0
. Here is the construction.
Let
\bm{e}_{1}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=(1,0,\cdots,0)\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
be the first standard basis
vector of
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
.
In the coordinates of the chosen gauge, take

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}(\delta_{\bm{0}}+\delta_{\bm{1}}),\qquad\frac{1}{2}(\delta_{\bm{e}_{1}}+\delta_{\bm{e}_{1}+\bm{1}}),

(62)

and pull them back through
A
. Every coordinate generator has expectation zero for both
distributions, while only the first distribution contains the zero syndrome. Thus a fixed gauge can
leave a constant ambiguity on affine lines, whereas the adaptive search can learn the relevant
annihilator directions.

Finite shot effects

The second experiment replaces exact access to
\mu_{\rho}(\bm{u})

Alg.

variant, each newly queried stabilizer label

N_{s}
shots, while labels already
measured in earlier rounds retain their existing confidence intervals. We simulate independent
outcomes
X_{j}\in\{\pm 1\}
with mean
\mu_{\rho}(\bm{u})
and use

\displaystyle\widehat{\mu}(\bm{u})=\frac{1}{N_{s}}\sum_{j=1}^{N_{s}}X_{j}.

(65)

To retain a certified feasible set, every measured label enters the endpoint LP through an interval
constraint, not through an equality constraint. Specifically, after round
t
we use the relaxed
finite-shot feasible set

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{t}^{\mathrm{fs}}

\displaystyle\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\{p\in\Delta(\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n})\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}

\displaystyle\qquad\;\;\widehat{\mu}(\bm{u})-\eta\leq\widehat{p}(\bm{u})\leq\widehat{\mu}(\bm{u})+\eta\;\forall\bm{u}\in Q_{t}\},

(66)

with each interval clipped to the physical range
[-1,1]
, and define the reported finite-shot
endpoints by minimizing and maximizing
p(\bm{0})
over
\mathcal{F}_{t}^{\mathrm{fs}}

\displaystyle\eta

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{2\log(2nT_{\max}/\delta)}{N_{s}}},

\pm 1

nT_{\max}
newly
queried labels.

\mathcal{H}_{t-1}
be
the finite-shot history before round
t

\mathcal{H}_{t-1}

t

labels are independent with means

, so Hoeffding’s inequality applies conditionally
to each selected label. A union bound over all selected labels in all rounds up to
T_{\max}
gives,

its confidence interval. On this event, the true syndrome distribution

belongs to

VIII
Comparison with existing works

This work differs from existing methods in its measurement interface, inference target, and type of
guarantee. These distinctions show that the methods below solve certification tasks different from
ours.

VIII.1
Comparison with direct fidelity estimation

Direct fidelity estimation (DFE)
Flammia and Liu 2011
starts from the following stabilizer identity:

\displaystyle F(\rho,\psi)=2^{-n}\sum_{g\in S}\tr\left[\rho g\right]=\mathbb{E}_{g\sim\mathrm{Unif}(S)}\,\tr\left[\rho g\right],

(68)

where
\mathrm{Unif}(S)
denotes the uniform distribution on
S
.
DFE treats Eq. (
68
) as a Monte Carlo average over arbitrary stabilizer elements.
It estimates the fidelity additively by sampling

O(\varepsilon^{-2}\log(1/\delta))
stabilizers from
S
, with constant shot cost per sampled
observable for stabilizer targets.
This is why the DFE observable count does not scale with the number of qubits
n
.

Our protocol solves a different task. It retains only the generator expectations in the queried
gauges and outputs a certified interval over all syndrome distributions compatible with those data.
The distinction can be seen directly. Suppose the queried labels after round
t
are

Q_{t}\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}\setminus\{\bm{0}\}
and some nontrivial label

\bm{v}\notin Q_{t}
remains unqueried. Define two syndrome distributions

\displaystyle p_{\pm}(\bm{s})=2^{-n}\left(1\pm(-1)^{\bm{v}\cdot\bm{s}}\right).

(69)

Both distributions are valid. Their Walsh coefficients agree on every queried label in
Q_{t}
, but
their fidelities differ by
p_{+}(\bm{0})-p_{-}(\bm{0})=2^{1-n}
.
Thus the same generator gauge data can be compatible with two fidelities separated by
2^{1-n}
.
Any valid monotone certified interval for this data record must therefore have width at least

2^{1-n}
. Exact certification, or a uniform guarantee of width below
2^{1-n}
, requires all

2^{n}-1
nontrivial stabilizer labels and hence at least
\lceil(2^{n}-1)/n\rceil
full gauge rounds.
This does not contradict DFE: DFE estimates an average by sampling arbitrary stabilizers, whereas
generator gauge certification asks for a worst-case compatibility guarantee under a weaker retained
data model.

VIII.2
Comparison with KKL fixed-gauge certification

Our closest antecedent is the KKL protocol
Kalev et al. 2019
.
The measurement interface is the same: one measures

n
commuting generators in a chosen gauge and computes the worst-case lower fidelity consistent
with these expectations.
Sec.
IV
complements this result by giving the companion upper endpoint
and its finite-shot certificate.
To summarize, the KKL supplies the lower endpoint
L_{1}
, while
we provide the upper endpoint
U_{1}
from the same retained data.

The adaptive stabilizer fidelity certification algorithm described in Sec.
V

solves a different problem: KKL fixes the gauge, whereas we treat the gauge as a
design variable and then adapt it across rounds. Thus the one gauge interval is only the first step
of our certified interval framework.
Indeed, the examples elaborated in Sec.
III.2
show the consequence.
Two gauges for the same
state can produce very different lower certificates, and one additional gauge can collapse the
feasible region even when one non-identity stabilizer has not been queried. Our contribution is
therefore both a complete one gauge interval and a policy for choosing which new gauge should be
queried next.

VIII.3
Comparison with stabilizer state learning

Stabilizer states also admit efficient learning algorithms under learning-oriented access models.
For example, stabilizer states are PAC-learnable
Rocchetto 2018
, and Bell sampling can
identify an unknown stabilizer state from
O(n)
copies
Montanaro 2017
. These results
build on the fact that stabilizer states have compact classical descriptions
Aaronson and Gottesman 2004
.

These learning results solve a different problem from the one considered here. A learning algorithm
aims to output a hypothesis stabilizer description, or a predictor for future measurement outcomes,
under a specified learning model. Our target state is already known. The unknown object is the
prepared, possibly nonstabilizer, noisy state, and the output is not a learned stabilizer state but a
certified interval
[L_{t},U_{t}]
for its fidelity with the target. The guarantee is adversarial: every
state compatible with the retained generator gauge expectations must have fidelity inside the
reported interval.
The measurement interface is also different: stabilizer learning uses learning oriented access such
as Bell sampling or PAC queries, whereas our protocol retains only generator expectations from
chosen gauges of the known target stabilizer group.

VIII.4
Comparison with adaptive tomography

Adaptive tomography with active learning
Lange et al. 2023
aims to reconstruct an unknown quantum
state by choosing informative measurement configurations. Our method uses the same adaptive
philosophy but targets a different object: a certified interval for fidelity to a known stabilizer
state. The representation is the syndrome distribution rather than the full density operator, and
the allowed actions are generator gauges rather than general tomographic settings.

Briefly, adaptive tomography seeks global reconstruction,
whereas our framework seeks the tightest certified fidelity interval available from generator gauge data.
The shared lesson is that adaptive measurement choice can help, but the tasks and guarantees are different.

IX
Conclusions and outlook

We have developed an adaptive, interval-valued framework for stabilizer fidelity certification from
generator gauge data. Starting from the KKL fixed-gauge lower certificate, we first derived the
complementary optimal worst-case upper certificate for one chosen gauge, thereby completing the
single-gauge fidelity interval. We then treated the generator gauge as an adaptive design variable:
each round updates the feasible syndrome polytope, reports the certified interval
[L_{t},U_{t}]
, and
chooses the next gauge by the witness elimination policy. This formulation makes the gauge
dependence of fixed-gauge certification explicit and turns additional gauges into new Walsh
constraints on the syndrome distribution.

Our analysis establishes both guarantees and limitations. The certified interval tightens
monotonically and collapses to the true fidelity once all nontrivial stabilizer labels have been
covered, while the worst-case lower bound shows that arbitrary states may still require full
coverage. Randomized gauge policies give conservative coverage baselines, and affine-support
syndrome states provide a structured regime in which the exponential full-coverage benchmark can
be avoided. The numerical simulations support these conclusions: witness elimination concentrates
faster than the uniform random gauge policy for both affine-support and generic full-support
ensembles, and the finite-shot study shows the expected statistical floor as the shot budget varies.
We also clarified how our results differ from existing direct fidelity estimation,
KKL fixed-gauge certification, stabilizer state learning, and adaptive tomography.

Two directions remain important. First, the exact LP over the full syndrome simplex
should be replaced or complemented by scalable relaxations that preserve useful certificate
guarantees. Second, a more complete finite-shot theory should integrate noisy stabilizer estimates,
adaptive gauge choice, and statistically valid confidence intervals in a single end-to-end
guarantee.

Proof of Prop.

Work in the coordinates of the chosen gauge
A

\bm{e}_{1},\cdots,\bm{e}_{n}

\displaystyle a_{i}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\frac{1+\mu_{\rho}(\bm{a}_{i})}{2}.

(83)

For any feasible syndrome distribution

\displaystyle\sum_{\bm{s}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}s_{i}=0}p(\bm{s})=a_{i}.

(84)

The event
\{\bm{s}=\bm{0}\}
is contained in every event

\displaystyle p(\bm{0})\leq a_{i}\qquad\forall i\in[n].

(85)

Therefore

\displaystyle p(\bm{0})\leq\min_{i\in[n]}a_{i}.

(86)

It remains to show that this upper bound is attainable. After a permutation of the chosen gauge

\displaystyle a_{1}\leq a_{2}\leq\cdots\leq a_{n}.

Define a probability distribution in the chosen gauge coordinates by placing mass on the nested

\displaystyle\bm{0},\quad\bm{e}_{1},\quad\bm{e}_{1}+\bm{e}_{2},\quad\cdots,\quad\bm{e}_{1}+\cdots+\bm{e}_{n}

\displaystyle p(\bm{0})

\displaystyle=a_{1},

\displaystyle p(\bm{e}_{1}+\cdots+\bm{e}_{j})

Appendix C

Fig.
1

the main text. The disagreement score
d_{t}(\bm{u})
is defined for each individual stabilizer label

\bm{u}

1
must package its choices into a full generator gauge. This
packaging is natural for the generator-only KKL interface, but it can be restrictive if the
experimental interface allows an arbitrary stabilizer
g(\bm{u})
to be queried as a standalone
observable. In this stronger, fine grained access model, one can query the single most informative
stabilizer at each step.

Initial generator query.

The fine grained certification algorithm still starts from a KKL gauge. To make the comparison with
Alg.
1
direct, it uses the same input data rather than assuming that a single-gauge
certificate has already been measured. It first fixes reference generators for
|{\psi}\rangle
, chooses
an initial gauge
A_{0}
with columns
\bm{a}_{0,1},\cdots,\bm{a}_{0,n}
, and queries the corresponding

n
stabilizer expectations. After this common initialization, set

\displaystyle Q_{0}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\{\bm{a}_{0,1},\cdots,\bm{a}_{0,n}\}.

(103)

This initialization is necessary. Suppose only a queried label set

R\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}\setminus\{\bm{0}\}
with
\operatorname{rank}(R)<n
is available. One can
still write partial-data lower and upper LPs by optimizing
p(\bm{0})
over all syndrome
distributions satisfying
\widehat{p}(\bm{u})=\mu_{\rho}(\bm{u})
for
\bm{u}\in R
, in the same spirit as
Eqs. (
13
) and (
19
). However, these
partial-data endpoints can be completely vacuous. Since
\operatorname{rank}(R)<n
, there exists a
nonzero syndrome
\bm{s}_{\star}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
such that
\bm{u}\cdot\bm{s}_{\star}=0
for every
\bm{u}\in R
. The
two syndrome distributions

\displaystyle p=\delta_{\bm{0}},\qquad q=\delta_{\bm{s}_{\star}}.

(104)

then satisfy

\displaystyle\widehat{p}(\bm{u})=1=\widehat{q}(\bm{u}),\qquad\forall\bm{u}\in R,

(105)

but have fidelities
p(\bm{0})=1
and
q(\bm{0})=0
. Hence the partial-data interval
is
[0,1]
for this valid data set. Fewer than
n
independent labels therefore cannot, in general,
give nontrivial certified lower and upper bounds.

The closed one gauge formulas in Eqs. (
14
) and (
20
) are
therefore certificates only after a full generator gauge has been queried. Applying them earlier
would either require unmeasured expectations from a completion of
R
to a basis, or would
incorrectly treat the incomplete set as a gauge. Once
A_{0}\in\mathrm{GL}(n,2)
has been queried, its columns
form a basis of
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
and the measured constraints are precisely

\displaystyle\widehat{p}(\bm{a}_{0,i})=\mu_{\rho}(\bm{a}_{0,i}),\qquad i\in[n].

(106)

Thus the resulting feasible set is exactly the one-gauge feasible polytope in
Eqs. (
13
) and (
19
) with
A=A_{0}
. The initial
generator query is therefore not an additional resource beyond the gauge-level protocol; it is the
common KKL starting point needed for a fair comparison.

Single-label query.

After the initial generator query, the refined adaptive procedure is to choose one stabilizer,
rather than a gauge, at a time.
This is the loop implemented in Alg.
2
: at each nonterminal step, the algorithm recomputes
the endpoint witnesses, queries one unmeasured stabilizer, and updates the cumulative queried set.
For any current queried set
Q_{k}\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}\setminus\{\bm{0}\}
, define

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{k}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\{p\in\Delta(\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n})\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\widehat{p}(\bm{u})=\mu_{\rho}(\bm{u})\ \forall\bm{u}\in Q_{k}\},

(107)

and define
L_{k},U_{k},W_{k},p_{k}^{-}
, and
p_{k}^{+}
exactly as in
Eqs. (
30
) and (
32
). The disagreement score is
again

\displaystyle d_{k}(\bm{u})\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\left|\widehat{p}_{k}^{+}(\bm{u})-\widehat{p}_{k}^{-}(\bm{u})\right|,\qquad\bm{u}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}\setminus\{\bm{0}\}.

(108)

The next stabilizer is chosen by the greedy single-label policy

\displaystyle\bm{u}_{k+1}\in\argmax_{\bm{u}\in(\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}\setminus\{\bm{0}\})\setminus Q_{k}}d_{k}(\bm{u}).

(109)

After querying
g(\bm{u}_{k+1})
and obtaining
\mu_{\rho}(\bm{u}_{k+1})
, update

\displaystyle Q_{k+1}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=Q_{k}\cup\{\bm{u}_{k+1}\}.

(110)

Comparison with Alg.
1
.

Both algorithms have the same input and output, and both first acquire
n
independent stabilizer
expectations through an initial gauge. If the same initial gauge is used, their first certified
interval is the same one-gauge KKL interval. The difference begins after this initialization:

•

Alg.
1
continues to acquire data in full gauges, adding up to
n

independent labels before the next endpoint-LP solve.

•

Alg.
2
adds only the single unqueried label with largest endpoint
disagreement and then resolves the endpoint LPs.

Alg.
2
avoids completing a gauge with low-disagreement labels, but it uses a
different single stabilizer measurement interface and can require about
n
endpoint optimization
cycles to acquire
n
additional labels.

Performance analysis for Alg.
2
.

The performance analysis for Alg.
2
follows the same logic as the analysis
of Alg.
1
. Because each single-label query adds one exact Walsh constraint and never
removes previous constraints, the certified interval is monotone. Once all nontrivial stabilizer
labels have been queried, Walsh–Hadamard inversion identifies the syndrome distribution exactly,
so the interval collapses to the true fidelity. Conversely, the same worst-case obstruction shows
that full coverage can be necessary in the absence of additional structure.

This appendix proves the performance guarantees in Sec.

in the

D.1
Proof of Prop.
5

Since the constraints are cumulative,
Q_{t}\subseteq Q_{t+1}
. Hence every distribution satisfying all constraints at round
t+1
also satisfies all constraints at round
t
, so

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{t+1}\subseteq\mathcal{F}_{t}.

(111)

Therefore

\displaystyle L_{t+1}=\min_{p\in\mathcal{F}_{t+1}}p(\bm{0})\geq\min_{p\in\mathcal{F}_{t}}p(\bm{0})=L_{t},

(112)

and similarly

\displaystyle U_{t+1}=\max_{p\in\mathcal{F}_{t+1}}p(\bm{0})\leq\max_{p\in\mathcal{F}_{t}}p(\bm{0})=U_{t}.

(113)

It follows immediately that
W_{t+1}=U_{t+1}-L_{t+1}\leq U_{t}-L_{t}=W_{t}
.

D.2
Proof of Prop.
6

For any probability distribution
p\in\Delta(\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n})
, the zeroth Walsh coefficient is

\displaystyle\widehat{p}(\bm{0})=\sum_{\bm{s}}p(\bm{s})=1.

(114)

If
Q_{t_{\star}}=\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}\setminus\{\bm{0}\}
and
p\in\mathcal{F}_{t_{\star}}
, then every Walsh
coefficient of
p
agrees with the corresponding Walsh coefficient of
p_{\rho}
: the nonzero
coefficients agree by definition of
\mathcal{F}_{t_{\star}}
, while the zeroth coefficient agrees by
normalization. Walsh–Hadamard inversion gives
p=p_{\rho}
, so

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{t_{\star}}=\{p_{\rho}\}.

(115)

For every
t\geq t_{\star}
, monotonicity gives
\mathcal{F}_{t}\subseteq\mathcal{F}_{t_{\star}}
, and the true syndrome
distribution always satisfies the queried exact constraints, so
p_{\rho}\in\mathcal{F}_{t}
. Hence

\mathcal{F}_{t}=\{p_{\rho}\}
and

\displaystyle L_{t}=U_{t}=p_{\rho}(\bm{0})=F(\rho,\psi).

(116)

Finally, if
\bigcup_{t\geq 1}Q_{t}=\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}\setminus\{\bm{0}\}
, then the right-hand side is
finite, so there is a finite round
t_{\star}
by which every nonzero label has appeared. The preceding
argument gives finite-time convergence.

D.3
Proof of Prop.
7

Assume an adaptive generator-only protocol has queried a set

Q\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}\setminus\{\bm{0}\}
of nontrivial stabilizer labels, and suppose that
some nonzero direction
\bm{v}\notin Q
remains unqueried. For any
\eta\in(0,1]
, define

\displaystyle p_{\pm}(\bm{s})\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=2^{-n}\bigl(1\pm\eta(-1)^{\bm{v}\cdot\bm{s}}\bigr),\qquad\bm{s}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}.

(117)

These are valid probability distributions because

1\pm\eta(-1)^{\bm{v}\cdot\bm{s}}\in[0,2]
and the Walsh character

(-1)^{\bm{v}\cdot\bm{s}}
has zero average over
\bm{s}
. By Walsh orthogonality,

\displaystyle\widehat{p}_{\pm}(\bm{u})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\bm{s}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}}(-1)^{\bm{u}\cdot\bm{s}}2^{-n}\bigl(1\pm\eta(-1)^{\bm{v}\cdot\bm{s}}\bigr)

\displaystyle=\mathbbm{1}[\bm{u}=\bm{0}]\pm\eta\,\mathbbm{1}[\bm{u}=\bm{v}].

(118)

Therefore
\widehat{p}_{+}(\bm{u})=\widehat{p}_{-}(\bm{u})
for every
\bm{u}\in Q
, because
\bm{v}\notin Q
and all queried
labels are nonzero. The protocol receives the same data from
p_{+}
and
p_{-}
, but

\displaystyle p_{+}(\bm{0})=2^{-n}(1+\eta),\qquad p_{-}(\bm{0})=2^{-n}(1-\eta),

(119)

so the target fidelities differ. No protocol can always output the exact fidelity before every
nonzero character has been queried. Since one gauge contributes at most
n
nontrivial stabilizer
labels, worst-case exact determination requires at least

\displaystyle\left\lceil\frac{2^{n}-1}{n}\right\rceil

(120)

full gauge rounds.

D.4
Proof of Prop.
8

Let
p,q\in\mathcal{F}_{t}
. Walsh–Hadamard inversion at the zero syndrome gives

\displaystyle p(\bm{0})-q(\bm{0})=2^{-n}\sum_{\bm{u}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}}\bigl(\widehat{p}(\bm{u})-\widehat{q}(\bm{u})\bigr).

(121)

The term
\bm{u}=\bm{0}
vanishes because both
p
and
q
are probability distributions, and every term with
\bm{u}\in Q_{t}
vanishes because
p
and
q
satisfy the same queried Walsh constraints. Hence

\displaystyle p(\bm{0})-q(\bm{0})=2^{-n}\sum_{\bm{u}\in(\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}\setminus\{\bm{0}\})\setminus Q_{t}}\bigl(\widehat{p}(\bm{u})-\widehat{q}(\bm{u})\bigr).

(122)

For every probability distribution

\displaystyle|p(\bm{0})-q(\bm{0})|\leq 2^{-n}\sum_{\bm{u}\in(\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}\setminus\{\bm{0}\})\setminus Q_{t}}2=\frac{2m_{t}}{2^{n}}.

Taking

\mathcal{F}_{t}

is a probability interval width.

For the randomized statement, let

Iterating from

force the character
