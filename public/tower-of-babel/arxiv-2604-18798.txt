Abstract
We study the BCS–BEC crossover of the large N N attractive Fermi-Hubbard model on a one-dimensional lattice using the mean field approximation in the presence of an imaginary chemical potential. We show that the crossover is governed by three parameters. The imaginary chemical potential i ​ θ i\theta , the temperature via a thermal kernel g ⁡ ( β ​ E k , β ​ θ ) g(\beta E_{k},\beta\theta) and the parameter δ u \delta_{u} whose sign controls the weak and strong coupling regimes. At the unitarity point ( U = U c U=U_{c} ), we find a thermal window ϕ = β ​ θ = 2 ​ π / 3 , 4 ​ π / 3 \phi=\beta\theta=2\pi/3,4\pi/3 where the gap vanishes while the fermion number N f N_{f} , which quantifies the balance between particle-like and hole-like excitations, has a local maximum/minimum. Inside this thermal window BCS and BEC physics are await changes in the coupling to be selected as the dominant regime. We expect that our results will unveil a better understanding of pairing correlations in lattice many-body physics.

Introduction

The BCS–BEC crossover is one of the most intriguing phenomena in quantum many-body physics
[
1
,
2
,
3
,
4
,
5
]
. It describes the transition from weakly coupled (BCS) superconductivity to strongly-coupled Bose-Einstein condensation (BEC). Recently, this crossover has been studied from ultracold atomic gases to high-temperature superconductors and twisted graphene multilayers
[
6
,
7
]
but also as a microscopic model in an optical lattice
[
8
]
. In order to understand these phenomena and to capture the competition between parameters like external gauge fields, pairing interactions and temperature, we use the simplified theory of the attractive Fermi-Hubbard model.

In this work, we investigate the phase crossover of the large
N
attractive Fermi-Hubbard model on a one-dimensional lattice using mean-field approximation in the presence of an imaginary chemical potential. This imaginary chemical potential arises naturally from the canonical ensemble when one introduces a constraint on the fermion number. Although an imaginary chemical potential is not directly observable in lattice theory, it corresponds to a background
U(1)
gauge field creating a holonomy along the thermal circle, which is analogous to Aharonov-Bohm phases
[
9
]
. Surprisingly, this temporal gauge field picture connects our analysis to a variety of phenomena where fermions acquire phases from moving around closed loops.

Our main result is the discovery of a "thermal window" at the imaginary chemical potential values
\phi=\beta\theta=2\pi/3
and
4\pi/3
. In particular, when the system is tuned to unitarity (
U=U_{c}
), the superconducting gap
\Delta
vanishes while the fermion number
N_{f}
, which separates particle-like from hole-like excitations, reaches a critical value. We find that the curvature of the thermodynamic potential changes sign precisely at these points, defining the boundaries between BCS and BEC regimes.

These angles are special since the condition
\cos(\beta\theta)=-1/2
creates a pattern for fermions propagating in imaginary time. This same mathematical condition appears across a variety of physical contexts like topological band theories where staggered magnetic fluxes generate Berry phases
[
10
]
, cold atom experiments with synthetic lattices
[
11
]
and Floquet-engineered gauge fields leading to nontrivial topological phases
[
12
]
.

In Section 2, we introduce the large-
N
attractive Fermi-Hubbard model with an imaginary chemical potential. In Section 3 we derive the gap equation and number equation, introducing the key parameters governing the crossover. In Section 4 we analyse the phase structure, focusing on the thermal kernel
g(\beta E_{k},\beta\theta)
and then we are able to show the emergence of the special angles. We also derive the exact values of fermion number as a function of
T/t
ratio for small and large temperature and the value of
T/t
for which the two expressions are equal. In Section 5 we
summarise and offer a few ideas for future work. Three Appendices contain some
technical results and useful formulae.

2
Attractive Fermi-Hubbard Model and Large-
N
Formulation

We consider the spin-
\frac{1}{2}
Fermi–Hubbard model on a lattice at inverse temperature

\beta

[
13
]
, with a fixed total fermion number
N_{f}

[
9
]
which gives the balance between particle-like and hole-like excitations and
n_{i\sigma}=c_{i\sigma}^{\dagger}c_{i\sigma}
, so the canonical partition function is

Z_{N_{f}}=\mathrm{Tr}\left[\delta(\hat{N}-N_{f})\,e^{-\beta H}\right],\qquad\hat{N}=\sum_{i,\sigma}n_{i\sigma}.

(1)

Introducing the Fourier representation of the projector,

\delta(\hat{N}-N_{f})=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}d\theta\;e^{i\theta(\hat{N}-N_{f})},

(2)

the canonical partition function
[
14
,
15
]
can be written as

Z_{N_{f}}=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}d\theta\;e^{-i\theta N_{f}}\,Z(\theta),

(3)

where
Z(\theta)
is a grand-canonical partition function with an
imaginary chemical potential
\mu=i\theta
, for
\theta
to be dimensionless.

2.1
Pairing-channel formulation and large-
N
effective action

To describe the BCS–BEC crossover, the Hubbard interaction must be
decoupled in the
pairing channel
, so we introduce a large-
N
generalization by extending the fermions to
N
flavors
\alpha=1,\dots,N
, which provides a controlled
saddle-point limit. The momentum version of the Hamiltonian is
[
16
]

H=\sum_{\mathbf{k},\alpha,\sigma}\epsilon_{\mathbf{k}}\,c_{\mathbf{k}\alpha\sigma}^{\dagger}c_{\mathbf{k}\alpha\sigma}-\frac{U}{2N}\sum_{i}\sum_{\alpha,\beta}c_{i\alpha\uparrow}^{\dagger}c_{i\alpha\downarrow}^{\dagger}c_{i\beta\downarrow}c_{i\beta\uparrow},

(4)

where
U>0
is the attractive interaction. The factor
1/N
ensures that
the interaction energy scales extensively in the large-
N
limit.
The Euclidean action then takes the form

S=\int_{0}^{\beta}d\tau\Bigg[\sum_{\mathbf{k},\alpha,\sigma}c_{\mathbf{k}\alpha\sigma}^{\dagger}(\partial_{\tau}+\epsilon_{\mathbf{k}}-i\theta)c_{\mathbf{k}\alpha\sigma}-\frac{U}{2N}\sum_{i,\alpha,\beta}c_{i\alpha\uparrow}^{\dagger}c_{i\alpha\downarrow}^{\dagger}c_{i\beta\downarrow}c_{i\beta\uparrow}\Bigg].

(5)

The quartic interaction is decoupled via a Hubbard–Stratonovich
transformation in the pairing channel
[
17
]
by introducing a complex bosonic
field
\Delta_{i}(\tau)
and simpler notation,

\exp\!\left[\frac{U}{2N}\int d\tau\,c_{\uparrow}^{\dagger}c_{\downarrow}^{\dagger}c_{\downarrow}c_{\uparrow}\right]=\int\mathcal{D}\Delta\,\mathcal{D}\Delta^{*}\;\exp\!\left[-\int d\tau\left(\frac{N|\Delta|^{2}}{2U}-\Delta^{*}c_{\downarrow}c_{\uparrow}-\Delta c_{\uparrow}^{\dagger}c_{\downarrow}^{\dagger}\right)\right].

(6)

After this transformation, the fermionic action becomes quadratic.
Introducing the Nambu spinor

\Psi_{\mathbf{k}\alpha}=\begin{pmatrix}c_{\mathbf{k}\alpha\uparrow}\\
c_{-\mathbf{k}\alpha\downarrow}^{\dagger}\end{pmatrix}

(7)

the inverse fermionic Green’s function is

\mathcal{G}^{-1}(i\omega_{n},\mathbf{k})=\begin{pmatrix}i\omega_{n}-\epsilon_{\mathbf{k}}-i\theta&\Delta\\
\Delta^{*}&i\omega_{n}+\epsilon_{\mathbf{k}}+i\theta\end{pmatrix}.

(8)

Its determinant yields the quasiparticle spectrum

E_{\mathbf{k}}=\sqrt{\epsilon_{\mathbf{k}}^{2}+|\Delta|^{2}},

(9)

which governs the BCS–BEC crossover physics.

Since the fermionic action is quadratic, the Grassmann fields can be
integrated out and the effective action is
[
18
]

S_{\mathrm{eff}}[\Delta,\theta]=\int_{0}^{\beta}d\tau\frac{N|\Delta|^{2}}{2U}-N\,\mathrm{Tr}\,\ln\mathcal{G}^{-1}[\Delta,\theta]

(10)

where the trace is taken over lattice sites, imaginary time (or
Matsubara frequencies), Nambu indices, and internal degrees of freedom. The static saddle-point configuration is

\Delta_{i}(\tau)=\Delta
.
The trace over Nambu space yields

\det\mathcal{G}^{-1}(i\omega_{n},\mathbf{k})=(i\omega_{n}-i\theta)^{2}-\left(\epsilon_{\mathbf{k}}^{2}+|\Delta|^{2}\right),

(11)

and the functional trace can therefore be written as

\mathrm{Tr}\,\ln\mathcal{G}^{-1}=\sum_{\mathbf{k}}\sum_{n}\ln\!\left[(i\omega_{n}-i\theta)^{2}-E_{\mathbf{k}}^{2}\right].

(12)

Performing the Matsubara frequency sum using contour-integration gives

\sum_{n}\ln\!\left[(i\omega_{n}-i\theta)^{2}-E_{\mathbf{k}}^{2}\right]=\beta E_{\mathbf{k}}+\left[\ln\!\left(1+e^{-\beta(E_{\mathbf{k}}+i\theta)}\right)+\ln\!\left(1+e^{-\beta(E_{\mathbf{k}}-i\theta)}\right)\right]

(13)

Substituting this result back into the effective action yields

S_{\mathrm{eff}}[\Delta,\theta]=N\Big[\frac{|\Delta|^{2}}{2U}-\sum_{\mathbf{k}}\Big(E_{\mathbf{k}}+\left[\ln\!\left(1+e^{-\beta(E_{\mathbf{k}}+i\theta)}\right)+\ln\!\left(1+e^{-\beta(E_{\mathbf{k}}-i\theta)}\right)\right]\Big)\Big].

(14)

In the thermodynamic limit, the lattice momentum sum is replaced by an
integral over the first Brillouin zone,

\sum_{\mathbf{k}}\;\longrightarrow\;\int_{\mathrm{BZ}}\frac{d^{d-1}k}{(2\pi)^{d-1}},

(15)

The effective action density then becomes

S_{\mathrm{eff}}=N\Big[\frac{|\Delta|^{2}}{2U}-\int_{\mathrm{BZ}}\frac{d^{d-1}k}{(2\pi)^{d-1}}\Big(E_{\mathbf{k}}+\left[\ln\!\left(1+e^{-\beta(E_{\mathbf{k}}+i\theta)}\right)+\ln\!\left(1+e^{-\beta(E_{\mathbf{k}}-i\theta)}\right)\right]\Big)\Big].

(16)

3
Gap Equations

The first gap equation of the model for gap
\Delta
is

\frac{\partial S_{\mathrm{eff}}}{\partial\Delta^{*}}=0

(17)

and yields the equation

\frac{1}{U}=\int_{\mathrm{BZ}}\frac{d^{d-1}k}{(2\pi)^{d-1}}\,\frac{1}{E_{\mathbf{k}}}\left[1-f(E_{\mathbf{k}}+i\theta)-f(E_{\mathbf{k}}-i\theta)\right],

(18)

where

f(x)=\frac{1}{e^{\beta x}+1}
.
At zero temperature this reduces to

\frac{1}{U}=\int_{\mathrm{BZ}}\frac{d^{d-1}k}{(2\pi)^{d-1}}\,\frac{1}{E_{\mathbf{k}}},

(19)

while for
\theta=0
we recover the standard BCS gap equation

\frac{1}{U}=\int_{\mathrm{BZ}}\frac{d^{d-1}k}{(2\pi)^{d-1}}\,\frac{1-2f(E_{\mathbf{k}})}{E_{\mathbf{k}}}.

(20)

The canonical constraint is enforced by the saddle-point condition
with respect to
\theta
:

\frac{\partial S_{\mathrm{eff}}}{\partial\theta}=0.

(21)

This yields the number equation at half-filling

N_{f}=\int_{\mathrm{BZ}}\frac{d^{d-1}k}{(2\pi)^{d-1}}\,\Big[f(E_{\mathbf{k}}-i\theta)-f(E_{\mathbf{k}}+i\theta)\Big],

(22)

The gap equation for
\Delta
may split into two parts,

I_{1}=\int_{\mathrm{BZ}}\frac{d^{d-1}k}{(2\pi)^{d-1}}\,\frac{1}{E_{\mathbf{k}}}

(23)

and

I_{2}=-\int_{\mathrm{BZ}}\frac{d^{d-1}k}{(2\pi)^{d-1}}\,\frac{1}{E_{\mathbf{k}}}\left[f(E_{\mathbf{k}}+i\theta)+f(E_{\mathbf{k}}-i\theta)\right]

(24)

For
1D
chains at finite temperature we take simply

I_{1}=\int_{\mathrm{BZ}}\frac{dk}{2\pi}\frac{1}{E_{\mathbf{k}}}

(25)

and

I_{2}=-\int_{\mathrm{BZ}}\frac{dk}{2\pi}\frac{1}{E_{\mathbf{k}}}\left[f(E_{\mathbf{k}}+i\theta)+f(E_{\mathbf{k}}-i\theta)\right]

(26)

If we define the vacuum (critical) coupling by

\frac{1}{U_{c}}\equiv I_{1}(\Delta=0)=\int_{\mathrm{BZ}}\frac{dk}{2\pi}\frac{1}{|\epsilon_{k}|}.

(27)

and subtracting this value, since there is a logarithmic divergent part at the edges of Brillouin zone, the renormalized gap equation becomes

\frac{1}{U}-\frac{1}{U_{c}}=\int_{\mathrm{BZ}}\frac{dk}{2\pi}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{\epsilon_{k}^{2}+\Delta^{2}}}-\frac{1}{|\epsilon_{k}|}\right)+I_{2}(\Delta,\theta).

(28)

The sign of
\frac{1}{U}-\frac{1}{U_{c}}
controls the crossover:
positive corresponds to the BCS regime, negative to the BEC regime, and

\frac{1}{U}=\frac{1}{U_{c}}
marks the crossover (unitarity) point.

4
Phase Structure: BCS, Unitarity, and BEC Regimes

4.1
General Setup

Using the identity
[
19
]

f(E_{k}+i\theta)+f(E_{k}-i\theta)=1-\frac{\sinh(\beta E_{k})}{\cosh(\beta E_{k})+\cos(\beta\theta)},

(29)

and
\phi\equiv\beta\theta
as a dimensionless variable, the gap equation becomes:

\delta_{u}\equiv\frac{1}{U}-\frac{1}{U_{c}}=\int_{\mathrm{BZ}}\frac{dk}{2\pi}\left[\frac{\sinh(\beta E_{k})}{E_{k}\big[\cosh(\beta E_{k})+\cos\phi\big]}-\frac{1}{|\epsilon_{k}|}\right]

(30)

For a lattice dispersion
\epsilon_{k}=-2t\cos k
(half
-
filling at
k=\pm\pi/2
), near the Fermi points
k=\pi/2+q
and
k=-\pi/2+q
:

\epsilon_{k}\approx\pm 2tq,\qquad E_{k}\approx\sqrt{(2tq)^{2}+\Delta^{2}}\equiv E_{q}.

(31)

If we split the integral into contributions near
\pm\pi/2
(within a cutoff
|q|<\Lambda
) and the remainder
C_{\mathrm{UV}}
(higher momentum part inside BZ, where UV has only symbolic character):

\delta_{u}=\underbrace{\int_{0}^{\Lambda}\frac{dq}{\pi}\left[\frac{\sinh(\beta E_{q})}{E_{q}\big[\cosh(\beta E_{q})+\cos\phi\big]}-\frac{1}{2tq}\right]}_{\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{low}}(\Delta,T,\phi;\Lambda)}\;+\;C_{\mathrm{UV}}(\Lambda)

(32)

and

\frac{1}{U_{c}}\equiv I_{1}(\Delta=0)=\int_{0}^{\Lambda}\frac{dq}{\pi}\frac{1}{2tq}.

(33)

Since
C_{\mathrm{UV}}(\Lambda)
comes from the rest of the Brillouin zone it is
independent of
\Delta
and
T
in the low
-
energy limit (
\Lambda\ll 1
, low
T
and
\Delta
).
The thermal kernel we define is

g(x,\phi)\equiv\frac{\sinh x}{\cosh x+\cos\phi},\qquad x=\beta E_{q}.

(34)

If we use the key identity

g(x,\phi)-1=-\dfrac{e^{-x}+\cos\phi}{\cosh x+\cos\phi}

(35)

hence

g(x,\phi)\gtreqless 1\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\cos\phi\lesseqgtr-e^{-x}.

(36)

This inequality depends on
x
, and therefore on
q
through
E_{q}
. Since
\phi
is constant, the sign of
g-1
can change with
q
. At angle
\phi=2\pi/3
(and similarly at
4\pi/3
), where
\cos\phi=-1/2
, the kernel becomes

g(x,2\pi/3)=\frac{\sinh x}{\cosh x-\tfrac{1}{2}},

(37)

and satisfies

g(x,2\pi/3)-1=-\frac{e^{-x}-\tfrac{1}{2}}{\cosh x-\tfrac{1}{2}}.

(38)

This expression changes sign at
x_{*}=\ln 2
. Consequently, the integrand of the gap equation exhibits a nontrivial structure: energy modes (
x<x_{*}
) contribute with
g<1
, while-energy modes (
x>x_{*}
) contribute with
g>1
.

At the critical point
\delta_{u}=0
, these contributions balance exactly after inclusion of the high energy term
C_{\mathrm{UV}}
. This cancellation is not due to a symmetry of the integrand, but rather reflects a redistribution of spectral weight between low- and high-energy scales controlled by the imaginary chemical potential.

Therefore, the angles
\phi=2\pi/3
and
4\pi/3
mark the points where the thermal kernel produces a sign-changing contribution across energy scales. This leads to a particularly sensitive balance in the gap equation at the critical coupling.

4.2
Thermal window at unitarity

The parameter
\delta_{u}
at zero gap is (if we assume for simplicity that
C_{\text{UV}}\approx 0
)

\delta_{u}^{0}=\frac{1}{U}-\frac{1}{U_{c}}=\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{low}}(0,T,\phi),

(39)

where
\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{low}}
is evaluated at
\Delta=0
.

Explicitly, setting
E_{q}\to|2tq|
in the low-energy integral, we obtain

\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{low}}(0,T,\phi)=\int_{0}^{\Lambda}\frac{dq}{\pi}\left[\frac{g(\beta|2tq|,\phi)}{2tq}-\frac{1}{2tq}\right],

(40)

so that

\delta_{u}^{0}\sim\frac{1}{U}-\frac{1}{U_{c}}=\int_{0}^{\Lambda}\frac{dq}{2\pi t\,q}\big[g(\beta|2tq|,\phi)-1\big].

(41)

The sign of
\delta_{u}^{0}
is therefore controlled by the kernel
g(x,\phi)-1
, whose structure determines whether low-energy or higher-energy modes dominate the integral.
At the special angle
\phi=2\pi/3
(and similarly at
4\pi/3
), where
\cos\phi=-1/2
, the kernel satisfies (
35
)
which changes sign at the finite value
x_{*}=\ln 2
. As a result, the integral in Eq. (
41
) receives competing contributions:

•

Energies (
x<x_{*}
):
g(x,\phi)<1
, giving a
negative
contribution,

•

Energies (
x>x_{*}
):
g(x,\phi)>1
, giving a
positive
contribution.

At the critical coupling
U=U_{c}
, the parameter
\delta_{u}^{0}
is zero so,

0=\int_{0}^{\Lambda}\frac{dq}{2\pi t\,q}\big[g(\beta|2tq|,\phi)-1\big]\rightarrow g(\beta|2tq|,\phi)=1.

(42)

We may say that the sign of
\delta_{u}^{0}
depends on how the spectral weight is distributed across energy scales.
In particular, at
\phi=2\pi/3
and
4\pi/3
, the presence of a sign change in
g(x,\phi)-1
within the thermal window leads to an easily changeable balance between lower and higher energy contributions. So this makes these angles important.
At
\phi=2\pi/3,\,4\pi/3
, the competition of low and high energy physics is more clear, since the crossover between
g(x,\phi)<1
and
g(x,\phi)>1
occurs at
x_{*}=\ln 2
. This identifies these angles as points where thermal fluctuations most effectively compete with the interaction scale
1/U-1/U_{c}
, controlling the stability of the normal state. If
C_{\text{UV}}
is negative (without high energy contribution from thermal kernel) then if
\delta_{u}=0
we must have
U>U_{c}
in order to cancel that contribution and if
C_{\text{UV}}
is positive (with high energy contribution from thermal kernel) then if
\delta_{u}=0
we must have
U<U_{c}
in order to cancel that contribution. On the other hand if we consider that
C_{\text{UV}}
is a shift to
\delta_{u}
like

\delta_{u}-C_{\mathrm{UV}}=\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{low}}(\Delta,T,\phi)

(43)

we take the regimes:

•

\delta_{u}-C_{\mathrm{UV}}>0
: Requires
\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{low}}>0
. This tends to favour
\cos\phi
small or negative to enhance
g
at low
q
.

•

\delta_{u}-C_{\mathrm{UV}}<0
: Requires
\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{low}}<0
. This occurs naturally at large
\Delta
(BEC) or if
\cos\phi\approx 1
and
T
small (since
g<1
suppresses the integrand).

Interestingly, the condition
\cos\phi=-\frac{1}{2}
plays a role analogous to the appearance of
\ln 2
in the Gross–Neveu model at imaginary chemical potential
[
21
,
22
]
. In both cases, the phase boundary is determined by a balance between interaction energy and a universal thermal contribution arising from low-energy fermionic modes. But here, while in the Gross–Neveu model this contribution appears explicitly as the free energy density, in our formulation it emerges through the structure of the thermal kernel, which maximally enhances low-energy spectral weight at
2\pi/3
.

4.3
Extrema of
N_{f}(\phi)
at
\phi=2\pi/3,\,4\pi/3

Introducing the density of states, the fermion number is
1
1

1

Note that the expression in (
22
) is purely imaginary. The physical fermion number is obtained by multiplying by
-i
, yielding a real quantity that measures the imbalance between particle-like and hole-like excitations.

N_{f}(\phi,T)=\int_{-2t}^{2t}d\epsilon\,\rho(\epsilon)\,\frac{\sin\phi}{\cosh(\beta|\epsilon|)+\cos\phi},

(44)

where we have used the identity:

f(E-i\phi)-f(E+i\phi)=\frac{i\sin\phi}{\cosh(\beta E)+\cos\phi},

(45)

We assume particle–hole symmetry of the band,

\rho(\epsilon)=\rho(-\epsilon).

(46)

Note that the integrand depends on
|\epsilon|
, so it is an
even
function of
\epsilon
.
Differentiating
N_{f}
with respect to
\phi
:

\frac{dN_{f}}{d\phi}=\cos\phi\int_{-2t}^{2t}d\epsilon\,\rho(\epsilon)\frac{1}{\cosh(\beta|\epsilon|)+\cos\phi}\;+\;\sin\phi\frac{\partial}{\partial\phi}\int_{-2t}^{2t}d\epsilon\,\rho(\epsilon)\frac{1}{\cosh(\beta|\epsilon|)+\cos\phi}.

(47)

If we define:

I(\phi)\equiv\int_{-2t}^{2t}d\epsilon\,\rho(\epsilon)\frac{1}{\cosh(\beta|\epsilon|)+\cos\phi}.

(48)

Then:

\frac{dN_{f}}{d\phi}=\cos\phi\,I(\theta)+\sin\phi\,I^{\prime}(\phi).

(49)

Now if we compute
I^{\prime}(\phi)
:

I^{\prime}(\phi)=\sin\phi\int_{-2t}^{2t}d\epsilon\,\rho(\epsilon)\frac{1}{(\cosh(\beta|\epsilon|)+\cos\phi)^{2}}.

(50)

and define

J(\phi)\equiv\int_{-2t}^{2t}d\epsilon\,\rho(\epsilon)\frac{1}{(\cosh(\beta|\epsilon|)+\cos\phi)^{2}}.

(51)

Thus:

\frac{dN_{f}}{d\phi}=\cos\phi\,I(\phi)+\sin^{2}\phi\,J(\phi).

(52)

Both
I(\phi)
and
J(\phi)
are
even
functions of
\phi
(they depend only on
\cos\phi
), and the integrands are even in
\epsilon
(since they depend on
|\epsilon|
), so the integrals are nonzero in general.

Setting
\frac{dN_{f}}{d\phi}=0
gives:

\cos\phi\,I(\phi)+\sin^{2}\phi\,J(\phi)=0.

(53)

This equation determines the stationary points of
N_{f}(\phi)
.
At
\phi=2\pi/3
and
4\pi/3
, we have:

\cos\phi=-\frac{1}{2},\qquad\sin^{2}\phi=\frac{3}{4}.

(54)

Substituting into Eq. (
53
):

-\frac{1}{2}\,I\!\left(\frac{2\pi}{3}\right)+\frac{3}{4}\,J\!\left(\frac{2\pi}{3}\right)=0.

(55)

This implies:

J\!\left(\frac{2\pi}{3}\right)=\frac{2}{3}\,I\!\left(\frac{2\pi}{3}\right).

(56)

Differentiating Eq. (
52
) once more:

\displaystyle\frac{d^{2}N_{f}}{d\phi^{2}}

\displaystyle=-\sin\phi\,I(\phi)+\cos\phi\,I^{\prime}(\phi)+2\sin\phi\cos\phi\,J(\phi)+\sin^{2}\phi\,J^{\prime}(\phi)

\displaystyle=-\sin\phi\,I(\phi)+3\sin\phi\cos\phi\,J(\phi)+\sin^{2}\phi\,J^{\prime}(\phi).

(57)

At
\phi=2\pi/3
,
\sin\phi=\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\neq 0
, and using the relation
J=\frac{2}{3}I
:

\frac{d^{2}N_{f}}{d\phi^{2}}\bigg|_{\phi=2\pi/3}=-\sqrt{3}\,I+\frac{3}{4}J^{\prime}.

(58)

The function
I(\phi)
is strictly positive, since its integrand is positive definite. The sign of
J^{\prime}(\phi)
depends on the details of the spectrum. Therefore we could say that the sign of second derivative depends on the competition between the two terms.
The angles
\phi=2\pi/3
and
4\pi/3
do not correspond to universal inflection points of
N_{f}(\phi)
. Instead, they are special because the kernel

\frac{1}{\cosh(\beta|\epsilon|)+\cos\phi}

(59)

is critical at low energies when
\cos\phi=-1/2
, which as we know it controls the sign of the quadratic coefficient in the Landau expansion (
Appendix C. The
a_{2}=0
critical point
). The extrema and curvature of
N_{f}(\phi)
near these angles are determined dynamically and must be evaluated numerically or within controlled approximations. Using a standard approximation in low-temperature expansions (
Appendix B. Local maximum/minimum of fermion number
N_{f}
) we take

•

At
\phi=2\pi/3
:
\displaystyle\frac{dN_{f}}{d\phi}=0
and

\displaystyle\frac{d^{2}N_{f}}{d\phi^{2}}<0
, therefore
N_{f}
has a

local maximum
. This corresponds to the maximal particle-hole
asymmetry with particle-like excitations dominating.

•

At
\phi=4\pi/3
:
\displaystyle\frac{dN_{f}}{d\phi}=0
and

\displaystyle\frac{d^{2}N_{f}}{d\phi^{2}}>0
, therefore
N_{f}
has a

local minimum
. This corresponds to the maximal (negative)
particle-hole asymmetry with hole-like excitations dominating.

These results are consistent with the physical expectation that

N_{f}(\phi)
is an odd function of
\phi
about
\phi=\pi
,

N_{f}(\pi+\delta)=-N_{f}(\pi-\delta)
, so a maximum at
2\pi/3
implies
a minimum at
4\pi/3
.

Phase structure and the special angle
2\pi/3
.

It is extremely interesting that the condition
\cos\phi=-\frac{1}{2}
arises in our formulation through the thermal kernel (
34
).
We obtain that at specific angles, corresponding to
\phi=2\pi/3
and
4\pi/3
, the kernel leads to a nontrivial redistribution of spectral weight across energy scales. In particular, it seems like the sign of
g(x,\phi)-1
depends on the competition between
e^{-x}
and
|\cos\phi|=1/2
. This implies that low-energy and high-energy contributions enter the gap equation with opposite signs. As a result, the integral determining the interaction shift
\delta_{u}=1/U-1/U_{c}
is governed by a balance between these regions. Physically talking, this identifies
\phi=2\pi/3,\,4\pi/3
as crossover angles where the thermal kernel changes character: low-energy modes are enhanced while higher-energy contributions are suppressed and this affects the sign of
\delta_{u}
.

Notably,
\phi=2\pi/3
corresponds to the primitive third root of unity
e^{i2\pi/3}
, suggesting that the three crossover regimes
(BCS, unitarity, and BEC) may be naturally organized by interpolation between three phase regimes of the fermionic boundary condition.

Interestingly, the same phase value appears in several other systems with
U(1)
phases. For example, lattice models with magnetic flux
\phi=2\pi/3
per plaquette exhibit nontrivial Berry curvature
and topological band structures
[
10
]
. Also, cold-atom quantum walks
[
11
]
and Floquet-engineered gauge fields
[
12
]
can realize tunneling phases close
to
2\pi/3
, modifying the interference of hopping paths and the resulting quasienergy spectra. In those systems the phase acts in
real space or momentum space, but in our case it appears as a twist along the imaginary-time direction.

Although the microscopic physics of these systems are different, the angle
2\pi/3
is special. We may say that it reflects an algebraic structure with interpolation between three phase regimes.
In our thermal problem this shows up as a reorganization of the
kernel governing particle–hole excitations. This phenomenon produces the behavior of the fermion number
N_{f}
and the gap equation near the unitarity point.

4.4
Exact values of
N_{f}
as functions of ratio
\frac{T}{t}
for small
T

We now compute explicitly the fermion number
N_{f}
at the special angles
\phi=2\pi/3
and
4\pi/3
. Starting from Eq. (
44
):

N_{f}(\phi,T)=\int_{-2t}^{2t}d\epsilon\,\rho(\epsilon)\,\frac{\sin\phi}{\cosh(\beta|\epsilon|)+\cos\phi},

(60)

with the 1D density of states
\rho(\epsilon)=\frac{1}{\pi\sqrt{4t^{2}-\epsilon^{2}}}
for
|\epsilon|\leq 2t
. At
\phi=2\pi/3
, we have
\sin(2\pi/3)=\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}
and
\cos(2\pi/3)=-\frac{1}{2}
. Using the fact that the integrand is even we take:

N_{f}\!\left(\frac{2\pi}{3},T\right)=\sqrt{3}\int_{0}^{2t}d\epsilon\,\rho(\epsilon)\,\frac{1}{\cosh(\beta\epsilon)-\frac{1}{2}}.

(61)

Introduce the variable
x=\beta\epsilon
. Then
\epsilon=x/\beta
,
d\epsilon=dx/\beta
, and:

\rho(\epsilon)=\frac{1}{\pi\sqrt{4t^{2}-(x/\beta)^{2}}}=\frac{1}{2\pi t\sqrt{1-(x/(2t\beta))^{2}}}.

(62)

Thus:

N_{f}\!\left(\frac{2\pi}{3},T\right)=\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2\pi t\beta}\int_{0}^{2t\beta}\frac{dx}{\sqrt{1-(x/(2t\beta))^{2}}}\,\frac{1}{\cosh x-\frac{1}{2}}.

(63)

If we use

I=\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{dx}{\cosh x-\frac{1}{2}}=\frac{4\pi}{3\sqrt{3}},

(64)

which follows from the identity
\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{dx}{\cosh x+a}=\frac{2}{\sqrt{1-a^{2}}}\arctan\left(\sqrt{\frac{1-a}{1+a}}\right)
for
|a|<1
, specialized to
a=-1/2
and insert this result in Eq. (
63
) with the approximation
\sqrt{1-(x/(2t\beta))^{2}}\approx 1
for the dominant small-
x
region:

N_{f}\!\left(\frac{2\pi}{3},T\right)\approx\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2\pi t\beta}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{dx}{\cosh x-\frac{1}{2}}=\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2\pi t\beta}\cdot\frac{4\pi}{3\sqrt{3}}=\frac{2}{3t\beta}.

(65)

Since
\beta=1/T
, we obtain the simple linear temperature dependence:

\boxed{N_{f}\!\left(\frac{2\pi}{3},T\right)=\frac{2}{3}\cdot\frac{T}{t}}.

(66)

At
\theta=4\pi/3
, we have
\sin(4\pi/3)=-\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}
and
\cos(4\pi/3)=-\frac{1}{2}
. The calculation proceeds identically, yielding:

N_{f}\!\left(\frac{4\pi}{3},T\right)=-\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2\pi t\beta}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{dx}{\cosh x-\frac{1}{2}}=-\frac{2}{3t\beta}=-\frac{2}{3}\cdot\frac{T}{t}.

(67)

Thus the fermion number at the special angles is simply:

N_{f}(\phi,T)=\frac{2}{3}\cdot\frac{T}{t}\times\begin{cases}+1&\phi=2\pi/3\\
-1&\phi=4\pi/3\end{cases}

(68)

4.5
Exact values of
N_{f}
as functions of ratio
\frac{T}{t}
for large
T

We now examine the behavior of
N_{f}
at angle
\phi=2\pi/3
in the opposite limit of high temperature, i.e., small
\beta=1/T
. Starting from Eq. (
44
):

N_{f}(\phi,T)=\int_{-2t}^{2t}d\epsilon\,\rho(\epsilon)\,\frac{\sin\phi}{\cosh(\beta|\epsilon|)+\cos\phi},

(69)

At
\phi=2\pi/3
,
\sin(2\pi/3)=\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}
and
\cos(2\pi/3)=-\frac{1}{2}
, giving:

N_{f}\!\left(\frac{2\pi}{3},T\right)=\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\int_{-2t}^{2t}d\epsilon\,\rho(\epsilon)\,\frac{1}{\cosh(\beta|\epsilon|)-\frac{1}{2}}.

(70)

In the high-temperature limit
\beta\to 0
, we have
\beta|\epsilon|\ll 1
we expand
\cosh(\beta|\epsilon|)
:

\cosh(\beta|\epsilon|)=1+\frac{(\beta|\epsilon|)^{2}}{2}+\frac{(\beta|\epsilon|)^{4}}{24}+\mathcal{O}(\beta^{6}).

(71)

Thus the denominator becomes:

\cosh(\beta|\epsilon|)-\frac{1}{2}=\frac{1}{2}+\frac{(\beta|\epsilon|)^{2}}{2}+\frac{(\beta|\epsilon|)^{4}}{24}+\cdots.

(72)

To leading order in small
\beta
:

\frac{1}{\cosh(\beta|\epsilon|)-\frac{1}{2}}\approx\frac{1}{\frac{1}{2}+\frac{(\beta|\epsilon|)^{2}}{2}}=\frac{2}{1+(\beta|\epsilon|)^{2}}.

(73)

Substituting this approximation into Eq. (
70
):

N_{f}\!\left(\frac{2\pi}{3},T\right)\approx\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\int_{-2t}^{2t}d\epsilon\,\rho(\epsilon)\,\frac{2}{1+(\beta|\epsilon|)^{2}}=\sqrt{3}\int_{-2t}^{2t}d\epsilon\,\rho(\epsilon)\,\frac{1}{1+(\beta|\epsilon|)^{2}}.

(74)

Introduce the dimensionless variable
u=\beta\epsilon
. Then
\epsilon=u/\beta
,
d\epsilon=du/\beta
, and the density of states becomes:

\rho(\epsilon)=\frac{1}{\pi\sqrt{4t^{2}-\epsilon^{2}}}=\frac{1}{\pi\sqrt{4t^{2}-(u/\beta)^{2}}}=\frac{1}{\pi\sqrt{4t^{2}-u^{2}/\beta^{2}}}.

(75)

The integration limits become
u=\pm 2t\beta
. Since
\beta
is small, these limits are small, so the integration range in
u
is narrow. The integral becomes:

N_{f}\!\left(\frac{2\pi}{3},T\right)\approx\sqrt{3}\int_{-2t\beta}^{2t\beta}\frac{du}{\beta}\,\frac{1}{\pi\sqrt{4t^{2}-u^{2}/\beta^{2}}}\,\frac{1}{1+u^{2}}.

(76)

Factor
2t
out of the square root:

\sqrt{4t^{2}-\frac{u^{2}}{\beta^{2}}}=2t\sqrt{1-\left(\frac{u}{2t\beta}\right)^{2}}.

(77)

Thus:

N_{f}\!\left(\frac{2\pi}{3},T\right)\approx\frac{\sqrt{3}}{\pi\beta}\int_{-2t\beta}^{2t\beta}\frac{du}{2t\sqrt{1-(u/(2t\beta))^{2}}}\,\frac{1}{1+u^{2}}.

(78)

Let
v=\frac{u}{2t\beta}
. Then
u=2t\beta v
,
du=2t\beta dv
, and the integration limits become
v=\pm 1
. Substituting:

N_{f}\!\left(\frac{2\pi}{3},T\right)\approx\frac{\sqrt{3}}{\pi\beta}\int_{-1}^{1}\frac{2t\beta dv}{2t\sqrt{1-v^{2}}}\,\frac{1}{1+(2t\beta v)^{2}}=\frac{\sqrt{3}}{\pi}\int_{-1}^{1}\frac{dv}{\sqrt{1-v^{2}}}\,\frac{1}{1+(2t\beta v)^{2}}.

(79)

The factors
\beta
and
t
cancel nicely, leaving an integral that depends on
\beta
only through the denominator.
For small
\beta
, we expand the denominator in powers of
\beta^{2}
:

\frac{1}{1+(2t\beta v)^{2}}=1-(2t\beta v)^{2}+(2t\beta v)^{4}-(2t\beta v)^{6}+\cdots.

(80)

Thus:

\displaystyle N_{f}\!\left(\frac{2\pi}{3},T\right)

\displaystyle\approx\frac{\sqrt{3}}{\pi}\int_{-1}^{1}\frac{dv}{\sqrt{1-v^{2}}}\Big[1-4t^{2}\beta^{2}v^{2}+16t^{4}\beta^{4}v^{4}-64t^{6}\beta^{6}v^{6}+\cdots\Big]

\displaystyle=\frac{\sqrt{3}}{\pi}\left[I_{0}-4t^{2}\beta^{2}I_{2}+16t^{4}\beta^{4}I_{4}-64t^{6}\beta^{6}I_{6}+\cdots\right],

(81)

where we define the integrals:

I_{n}=\int_{-1}^{1}\frac{v^{n}dv}{\sqrt{1-v^{2}}}.

(82)

These integrals are standard and can be evaluated using the substitution
v=\sin\phi
(
91
).
Substituting these values into Eq. (
81
):

\displaystyle N_{f}\!\left(\frac{2\pi}{3},T\right)

\displaystyle\approx\frac{\sqrt{3}}{\pi}\Big[\pi-4t^{2}\beta^{2}\cdot\frac{\pi}{2}+16t^{4}\beta^{4}\cdot\frac{3\pi}{8}-64t^{6}\beta^{6}\cdot\frac{5\pi}{16}+\cdots\Big]

\displaystyle=\frac{\sqrt{3}}{\pi}\cdot\pi\left[1-2t^{2}\beta^{2}+6t^{4}\beta^{4}-20t^{6}\beta^{6}+\cdots\right]

\displaystyle=\sqrt{3}\left[1-2\left(\frac{t}{T}\right)^{2}+6\left(\frac{t}{T}\right)^{4}-20\left(\frac{t}{T}\right)^{6}+\cdots\right].

(83)

where
T=1/\beta
.
Obviously since
\sin(4\pi/3)=-\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}

N_{f}\!\left(\frac{4\pi}{3},T\right)=-N_{f}\!\left(\frac{2\pi}{3},T\right)

(84)

The high-temperature expansion reveals several remarkable features:

•

Leading term
: As
T\to\infty
,
N_{f}
approaches the constant
\sqrt{3}\approx 1.732
, independent of both
t
and
T
.

•

Correction terms
: The expansion is in even powers of
t/T
, with coefficients
2,6,20,\ldots
that follow the pattern from (
Appendix A. Useful integrals
). These coefficients are the same as those appearing in the expansion of
\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-x^{2}}}
, pointing out the geometry of the density of states.

•

Crossover scale
: The expansion is valid for
T\gg t
, i.e., when thermal energy dominates over the hopping amplitude. In this regime, thermal fluctuations cancel the details of the lattice, and
N_{f}
goes to its high-temperature limit.

•

Contrast with low-
T
limit
: At low temperatures (
T\ll t
), we found
N_{f}\approx\frac{2}{3}\cdot\frac{T}{t}
, which is linear and small. The crossover of
N_{f}
happens when the low-T and high-T expressions are equal. If we solve numerically

\frac{2x}{3}=\sqrt{3}\Big[1-\frac{2}{x^{2}}\Big]

(85)

with
x=\frac{T}{t}
, we take

x=\frac{T}{t}\approx 2

(86)

The high-temperature limit
N_{f}\to\sqrt{3}
shows that the imaginary chemical potential
\phi=2\pi/3
induces a fixed particle-hole asymmetry, independent of material parameters, even at infinite temperature. For example at
T>2t
, fermions beginning from the Fermi-Level, have energies to overcame the Brillouin zone boarders and "hit" the walls of the zone. So now the fermion number cannot keep growing linearly and goes to a specific limit.

5
Conclusions

In this work, we have investigated the BCS-BEC crossover of the large
N
attractive Fermi-Hubbard model on a one-dimensional lattice in the presence of an imaginary chemical potential
i\beta\theta
. The main messages of our work are that the crossover is governed by three key parameters
g(\beta E_{k},\beta\theta)
,
\delta_{u}
and the imaginary chemical potential
i\theta
and at unitarity there is a thermal window where superconducting gap
\Delta
vanishes and the fermion number
N_{f}
is maximum/minimum at specific points. Inside this thermal window BCS and BEC regimes dominate the phase.

Through an approximation at half-filling and near the Fermi points, we find the exact edges of the thermal window at
2\pi/3
and
4\pi/3
. Finally, we have proven that these angles mark a change in
N_{f}
curvature through the condition
\cos\phi=-1/2
. If first derivative vanishes at the edges, then second derivative is negative at
2\pi/3
and positive at
4\pi/3
and we may say that they are special points that separate particle-like (
\phi<2\pi/3
) from hole-like (
\phi>4\pi/3
) excitation regimes.

We think that our results offer a new window into the BCS-BEC crossover physics and can be extended in several directions. For example one might be tempted to extend our theory to higher dimensions (for example in
2d

[
23
]
) to determine whether the special angles are independent from dimension or not. Possible
2d
lattice geometries could be the square and honeycomb. Another interesting future research could be the thermal mapping between our model and the repulsive Bose Hubbard model at imaginary chemical potential. Furthermore, it would be interesting to examine the possibility of connecting the imaginary chemical potential with experiment results of Floquet engineering or synthetic gauge fields offers an opportunity to test our results in ultracold atoms.

Appendix B. Local maximum/minimum of fermion number
N_{f}

We begin with

\frac{d^{2}N_{f}}{d\phi^{2}}=-\sin\phi\,I(\phi)+3\sin\phi\cos\phi\,J(\phi)+\sin^{2}\phi\,J^{\prime}(\phi).

(92)

•

At
\phi=2\pi/3
we have
\sin\phi=\sqrt{3}/2
,
\cos\phi=-1/2
,
and the extremum condition
\cos\phi\,I+\sin^{2}\phi\,J=0
yields

J=\frac{2}{3}I
. Substituting these into Eq. (
92
)
gives

\displaystyle\frac{d^{2}N_{f}}{d\phi^{2}}\bigg|_{\phi=2\pi/3}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}I+3\cdot\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\!\left(-\frac{1}{2}\right)\!\left(\frac{2}{3}I\right)+\frac{3}{4}J^{\prime}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}I-\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}I+\frac{3}{4}J^{\prime}=-\sqrt{3}\,I+\frac{3}{4}J^{\prime}.

(93)

We now compute
J^{\prime}(\phi)
. Differentiating
J(\phi)
,

J^{\prime}(\phi)=4\sin\phi\int_{0}^{2t}d\epsilon\,\rho(\epsilon)\,\frac{1}{\bigl(\cosh(\beta\epsilon)+\cos\phi\bigr)^{3}}.

(94)

At
\phi=2\pi/3
,
\sin(2\pi/3)=\sqrt{3}/2>0
, so

J^{\prime}\!\left(\frac{2\pi}{3}\right)=2\sqrt{3}\int_{0}^{2t}d\epsilon\,\rho(\epsilon)\,\frac{1}{\bigl(\cosh(\beta\epsilon)-\frac{1}{2}\bigr)^{3}}>0.

(95)

The integrand is strictly positive because
\rho(\epsilon)>0
and

\cosh(\beta\epsilon)-1/2\geq 1/2
for all
\epsilon
. Hence

J^{\prime}(2\pi/3)
is positive.

To determine the sign of
d^{2}N_{f}/d\phi^{2}
we evaluate the two terms in
Eq. (
93
) explicitly. At low temperatures
(
\beta\to\infty
) the integrals are dominated by
\epsilon\approx 0
.
Using the approximations
\rho(\epsilon)\approx 1/(2\pi t)
,

\cosh(\beta\epsilon)\approx 1+(\beta\epsilon)^{2}/2
, and extending the
integration to infinity, we obtain

\displaystyle I

\displaystyle\approx\frac{2}{2\pi t}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{2}{1+(\beta\epsilon)^{2}}\,d\epsilon=\frac{2}{t\beta},

(96)

\displaystyle J^{\prime}

\displaystyle\approx\frac{2\sqrt{3}}{2\pi t}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{8}{\bigl(1+(\beta\epsilon)^{2}\bigr)^{3}}\,d\epsilon=\frac{3\sqrt{3}}{2t\beta}.

(97)

Inserting these into Eq. (
93
) gives

\frac{d^{2}N_{f}}{d\phi^{2}}\bigg|_{\phi=2\pi/3}\approx-\sqrt{3}\cdot\frac{2}{t\beta}+\frac{3}{4}\cdot\frac{3\sqrt{3}}{2t\beta}=-\frac{7\sqrt{3}}{8t\beta}<0.

(98)

Thus, at low temperatures,
\phi=2\pi/3
is a
local maximum

of
N_{f}(\phi)
.

•

At
\phi=4\pi/3
we have
\sin\phi=-\sqrt{3}/2
,
\cos\phi=-1/2
,
and the same extremum condition gives
J=\frac{2}{3}I
again.
Substituting into Eq. (
92
) yields

\displaystyle\frac{d^{2}N_{f}}{d\phi^{2}}\bigg|_{\phi=4\pi/3}

\displaystyle=-\left(-\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\right)I+3\left(-\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\right)\!\left(-\frac{1}{2}\right)\!\left(\frac{2}{3}I\right)+\frac{3}{4}J^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}I+\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}I+\frac{3}{4}J^{\prime}=\sqrt{3}\,I+\frac{3}{4}J^{\prime}.

(99)

From Eq. (
94
), at
\phi=4\pi/3
,

\sin(4\pi/3)=-\sqrt{3}/2
, so

J^{\prime}\!\left(\frac{4\pi}{3}\right)=-2\sqrt{3}\int_{0}^{2t}d\epsilon\,\rho(\epsilon)\,\frac{1}{\bigl(\cosh(\beta\epsilon)-\frac{1}{2}\bigr)^{3}}<0.

(100)

Using the same low-temperature approximations as above,

I\approx 2/(t\beta)
and
J^{\prime}\approx-3\sqrt{3}/(2t\beta)
, we find

\frac{d^{2}N_{f}}{d\phi^{2}}\bigg|_{\phi=4\pi/3}\approx\sqrt{3}\cdot\frac{2}{t\beta}+\frac{3}{4}\!\left(-\frac{3\sqrt{3}}{2t\beta}\right)=\frac{7\sqrt{3}}{8t\beta}>0.

(101)

Hence
\phi=4\pi/3
is a
local minimum
of
N_{f}(\phi)
.

Appendix C. The
a_{2}=0
critical point

If one wants to find the critical thermal window at unitarity then using the Landau theory of phase transitions by expanding the thermal potential near a critical point, then:

\Omega(\Delta)=\Omega(0)+a_{2}\Delta^{2}+a_{4}\Delta^{4}+a_{6}\Delta^{6}+..

(102)

where
a_{2}=0
gives the critical point where the curvature of thermal potential changes sign.
We start from the thermodynamic potential

\Omega(\Delta)=\frac{\Delta^{2}}{2U}-\int\frac{dk}{2\pi}E_{k}-\frac{1}{\beta}\int\frac{dk}{2\pi}\ln\!\left[1+2e^{-\beta E_{k}}\cos\phi+e^{-2\beta E_{k}}\right],

(103)

where
E_{k}=\sqrt{\epsilon_{k}^{2}+\Delta^{2}}
. The first derivative with respect to
\Delta
is

\frac{\partial\Omega}{\partial\Delta}=\frac{\Delta}{U}-\int\frac{dk}{2\pi}\frac{\Delta}{E_{k}}-\frac{1}{\beta}\int\frac{dk}{2\pi}\frac{1}{X}\frac{\partial X}{\partial\Delta},

(104)

with
X=1+2e^{-\beta E_{k}}\cos\phi+e^{-2\beta E_{k}}
.

We have

\frac{\partial X}{\partial\Delta}=-2\beta\frac{\Delta}{E_{k}}e^{-\beta E_{k}}\cos\phi-2\beta\frac{\Delta}{E_{k}}e^{-2\beta E_{k}}=-2\beta\frac{\Delta}{E_{k}}e^{-\beta E_{k}}\bigl(\cos\phi+e^{-\beta E_{k}}\bigr).

(105)

Substituting,

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\Omega}{\partial\Delta}

\displaystyle=\frac{\Delta}{U}-\int\frac{dk}{2\pi}\frac{\Delta}{E_{k}}-\frac{1}{\beta}\int\frac{dk}{2\pi}\frac{1}{X}\left[-2\beta\frac{\Delta}{E_{k}}e^{-\beta E_{k}}\bigl(\cos\phi+e^{-\beta E_{k}}\bigr)\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{\Delta}{U}-\int\frac{dk}{2\pi}\frac{\Delta}{E_{k}}+2\int\frac{dk}{2\pi}\frac{\Delta}{E_{k}}\frac{e^{-\beta E_{k}}\bigl(\cos\phi+e^{-\beta E_{k}}\bigr)}{X}.

(106)

Using the identity

X=1+2e^{-\beta E_{k}}\cos\phi+e^{-2\beta E_{k}}=2e^{-\beta E_{k}}\bigl(\cosh(\beta E_{k})+\cos\theta\bigr),

(107)

we obtain

\frac{e^{-\beta E_{k}}\bigl(\cos\phi+e^{-\beta E_{k}}\bigr)}{X}=\frac{\cos\phi+e^{-\beta E_{k}}}{2\bigl(\cosh(\beta E_{k})+\cos\phi\bigr)}.

(108)

Thus,

\frac{\partial\Omega}{\partial\Delta}=\frac{\Delta}{U}-\int\frac{dk}{2\pi}\frac{\Delta}{E_{k}}+\int\frac{dk}{2\pi}\frac{\Delta}{E_{k}}\frac{\cos\phi+e^{-\beta E_{k}}}{\cosh(\beta E_{k})+\cos\phi}.

(109)

The second derivative is

\frac{\partial^{2}\Omega}{\partial\Delta^{2}}=\frac{1}{U}-\int\frac{dk}{2\pi}\frac{1}{E_{k}}+\int\frac{dk}{2\pi}\frac{1}{E_{k}}\frac{\cos\phi+e^{-\beta E_{k}}}{\cosh(\beta E_{k})+\cos\phi}+\Delta\frac{\partial}{\partial\Delta}\bigl[\cdots\bigr],

(110)

where the term proportional to
\Delta
is very small for
\Delta\rightarrow 0
.
Hence, the Landau coefficient
a_{2}(T,\phi)=\frac{\partial^{2}\Omega}{\partial\Delta^{2}}
is

a_{2}(T,\phi)=\frac{1}{U}-\int\frac{dk}{2\pi}\frac{1}{E_{k}}+\int\frac{dk}{2\pi}\frac{1}{E_{k}}\frac{\cos\phi+e^{-\beta E_{k}}}{\cosh(\beta E_{k})+\cos\phi}.

(111)

Since

\frac{\cos\phi+e^{-x}}{\cosh x+\cos\phi}=1-\frac{\sinh x}{\cosh x+\cos\phi}
