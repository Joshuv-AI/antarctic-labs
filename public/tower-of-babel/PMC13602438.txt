Point‐of‐View Video Recording Devices in Plastic Surgery: Educational Utility and Medicolegal Documentation Potential
Authors: Frank Bevacqua, Emma Choong, Nelson Low
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroductionOperative video supports performance review and education, but consistent surgeon point‐of‐view (POV) capture in plastic and reconstructive surgery is challenging. We assessed the perceived educational effectiveness of a smart‐glasses POV system and documentation/implementation considerations, including medicolegal use.MethodsDe‐identified operative segments were recorded using second‐generation Meta AI Glasses. Curated videos/stills were shown to consultants, trainees, and medical students, who completed an anonymous questionnaire with optional free‐text.ResultsThirty‐five respondents participated (17 students, 12 junior doctors/registrars, 6 consultants); 24/35 (68.6%) provided free‐text. Video quality and clarity/stability were rated good/excellent by 35/35 (100%); field of view and audio by 31/35 (88.6%) and 29/35 (82.9%). Documentation quality and clinically relevant detail were rated good/excellent by 33/35 (94.3%), and the added value of commentary was rated good/excellent by 32/35 (91.4%); 29/35 (82.9%) would recommend using POV recording for documentation. Educational value was strongly endorsed (34/35, 97.1% agreed POV recordings effectively teach/review technique). Patient‐facing and medicolegal‐related items were endorsed less consistently (22/35–30/35, 62.9%–85.7%). Consultants were more critical of the field‐of‐view/audio (good/excellent 50% each) and less likely than non‐consultants to recommend routine documentation (1/6, 16.7% vs. 28/29, 96.6%). Free‐text prioritised zoom/loupe compatibility, audio/lighting and alignment improvements, and governance around consent, privacy, storage, and access.ConclusionSmart‐glasses POV recording was considered a feasible, high‐value adjunct to surgical education, especially when accompanied by narration. Wider adoption for routine documentation and medicolegal utility depends less on conceptual acceptance and more on resolving current technical limits and establishing robust consent, privacy, storage, access, and governance.

Introduction

Video recording of surgical procedures provides an effective means to analyse operative performance, enhance surgical education, and disseminate operative techniques to a broader surgical audience [1, 2]. In plastic and reconstructive surgery, however, obtaining consistently high‐quality footage from the surgeon's own vantage point has historically been difficult. While many operating rooms have fixed recording systems for documentation, these often fail to capture the fine, first‐person perspective required to teach intricate technical steps [3]. In response, point‐of‐view (POV) video recording devices have been increasingly adopted as a practical, cost‐effective way to obtain an unobstructed surgeon's‐eye view of the operative field while maintaining standard PPE.

Early attempts to record operative video frequently relied on custom configurations to achieve acceptable image quality [4]. More recently, lightweight consumer “action” cameras have been repurposed for intraoperative use. Surgeons have mounted compact camcorders or GoPro (GoPro Inc., USA) cameras on headgear to capture procedures in high definition from their POV [5, 6]. The availability of affordable, high‐resolution cameras, such as the GoPro HERO series represented a key inflection point, enabling continuous first‐person video capture with minimal disruption to operative workflow [1, 6]. Beyond GoPro devices, wearable smart glasses, such as Meta AI Glasses (Meta Platforms Inc., USA; EssilorLuxottica, France and Italy) and Google Glass (Google, USA), have also been used to record and livestream operations, demonstrating the feasibility of real‐time sharing of a surgeon's perspective [7, 8, 9]. More recently, specialised ultra‐high‐definition cameras designed to attach to surgical loupes or headlights have emerged, offering a more unobtrusive method for documenting procedures, including fine microsurgical detail, without adding substantial weight or discomfort for the operator [10].

These head‐mounted systems may provide meaningful educational value. Video review enables trainees and surgeons to revisit technique, identify errors or inefficiencies, and support objective reflection on performance. Within plastic surgery training, procedure recording has been proposed to extend the traditional apprenticeship model and extend learning beyond the operating theatre [5]. Wearable‐camera footage has also been used for postoperative self‐review and skills refinement; for example, recordings from devices such as Google Glass or GoPro have been utilised to critique technique and guide improvement [8]. Further, live streaming from a surgeon's POV, enabled by smart glasses and related wearable technologies, creates opportunities for remote mentoring and real‐time consultation during complex cases [7]. This capacity to deliver an immersive operative viewpoint to remote learners may expand access to expert teaching and feedback beyond geographical constraints.

In addition to educational applications, routine intraoperative video capture may offer medicolegal and quality improvement benefits. The “operating room black box” concept has been proposed to continuously capture operative audiovisual data for subsequent analysis of errors and near misses, with the goal of improving patient safety and surgical outcomes [11]. Video archives can provide an objective record of intraoperative events, supporting structured review of complications and adverse outcomes. Such recordings may also assist clinicians and institutions in medicolegal contexts by providing contemporaneous evidence of the care delivered [12]. There are reports in which intraoperative video has been pivotal in clarifying events and exonerating surgical teams from false allegations [12]. At the same time, broader adoption of operative video documentation raises ethical and legal considerations, including consent, privacy, data security, and ownership and permissible use of recorded material. Recent legal reviews have emphasised the need for clear guidance and legislative frameworks governing surgical video data as recording technologies become more prevalent [13]. This study aims to assess the potential effectiveness of using a POV camera for surgical education and medicolegal documentation.

The project was conducted within a single metropolitan tertiary hospital plastic and reconstructive surgery unit. Ethics approval was obtained from the institutional Ethics Committee, and informed consent for intraoperative recording and educational/research use was obtained from all participating patients.

Targeted Literature Search

To inform protocol development and questionnaire domains, we conducted a targeted search of MEDLINE (via PubMed), Embase, and Scopus for English‐language articles published in the past 20 years describing the intraoperative use of head‐mounted or wearable POV video devices in human surgery. Search terms combined device‐related terms (e.g., “head‐mounted”, “wearable”, “smart glasses”, “first‐person”, “POV”, “action camera”, “GoPro”, “Google Glass”, “Meta”) with surgical terms. Relevant studies were used to identify key domains (educational utility, usability/workflow, and documentation/medicolegal considerations) that guided the local evaluation.

POV Recording Protocol and Pilot Implementation

A commercially available head‐mounted smart‐glasses device with a forward‐facing high‐definition camera (second‐generation Meta AI Glasses) was used by the primary surgeon during selected plastic surgery cases (Figure 1). Recordings captured brief, focused segments immediately before and/or during key steps, with pauses permitted during non‐critical intervals. The camera was directed toward the operative field to minimise capture of identifying features, and the operating team was notified when recording was active. Following each case, raw files were transferred to a secure, password‐protected institutional server, renamed using study identifiers, and stored in accordance with institutional requirements.

A structured questionnaire was developed to capture the perceptions of consultants, trainees, and medical students regarding POV recordings. Item generation was informed by domains identified in the targeted literature search (e.g., image quality, stability, visibility of key steps, perceived educational value, potential for feedback/skills assessment, and perceived value as an adjunct to operative notes). Consultants, trainees, and medical students in the unit were invited to view the selected POV videos in person or via a secure platform and to complete the questionnaire anonymously.

Statistical Analysis

Responses were collated in a de‐identified dataset and analysed using RStudio. Categorical variables were summarised as frequencies and percentages. Likert‐scale items were summarised as mean (standard deviation) or median [interquartile range], as appropriate. Where feasible, exploratory comparisons between consultants and trainees were performed. Free‐text responses were reviewed and summarised into descriptive themes relating to educational value, documentation utility, technical limitations, and ethical or medicolegal issues.

Results

Participants

Thirty‐five respondents completed the questionnaire (17 medical students, 3 interns, 6 residents, 2 unaccredited registrars, 1 SET registrar, and 6 consultants). Free‐text comments were provided by 24/35 (68.6%).

Exploratory Differences by Role

Consultants (n = 6) rated video quality and stability highly (Good/Excellent 100% for both) but were more critical of the field of view and audio (Good/Excellent 50% for each). Consultants were substantially less likely than non‐consultants to recommend routine future use for documentation (Good/Excellent 1/6, 16.7%, mean 2.50 vs. 28/29, 96.6%, mean 4.59). Consultants were also less likely to agree that recordings would assist if questions/complaints arise (Agree/Strongly agree 50% vs. 93.1%) or provide patient reassurance (33.3% vs. 69.0%).

Free‐Text Feedback

Comments commonly addressed multiple domains and clustered into four themes. Educational value was most prominent (11/24 comments), with respondents describing the surgeon's‐eye perspective and commentary as helpful for learning operative anatomy and key steps. Technical improvement requests (8/24) focused on fine‐detail capture, particularly on zoom and loupe compatibility, as well as audio, lighting, and field‐of‐view alignment. Patient‐facing utility was viewed cautiously (9/24), with many suggesting intraoperative footage may be too intricate or confronting for some patients and recommending selective, curated use. Governance/medicolegal issues were raised less frequently (3/24), centring on secure storage, institutional acceptability, and downstream risks if recordings are disseminated beyond their intended purpose.

Discussion

In this single‐centre pilot study, a smart‐glasses POV system (Meta AI glasses) produced recordings that were perceived as technically strong and educationally valuable in plastic and reconstructive surgery. Overall, video quality and stability were rated highly, and nearly all respondents agreed that POV recordings are an effective method for teaching or reviewing surgical technique. These findings reinforce the benefit of POV wearables: they provide an immersive surgeon's‐eye view of anatomy and operative flow with minimal set‐up and integrate readily into teaching or asynchronous learning, consistent with the growing use of wearable systems to share first‐person operative perspectives [1, 2, 5, 7, 8].

A clear educational signal emerged across both the Likert data and the comments: perceived usefulness was highest when recordings were paired with narration and anatomical orientation. Respondents repeatedly described the surgeon's‐eye viewpoint as addressing a common limitation of in‐theatre learning, where trainees may be present but unable to consistently visualise key manoeuvres. The high endorsement of teaching/review, alongside strong ratings for the added value of commentary, suggests that short, curated clips with structured narration may represent the most immediately implementable and highest‐yield application, particularly for junior learners.

Building on the educational value that participants themselves most frequently highlighted, these recordings could be developed into more structured and shareable educational outputs. Short, narrated POV segments lend themselves to formats such as video case reports, “how I do it” technique articles, and content disseminated through surgical society‐affiliated channels or curated online platforms, extending the reach of an individual case well beyond the local unit. The educational yield of such outputs, however, depends on how they are constructed rather than simply on their availability. The surgical literature consistently emphasises that effective educational videos should undergo peer review, explicitly label key anatomical structures, and state clear learning objectives and sequential procedural steps. Validated frameworks developed for laparoscopic and robotic surgery, the LAP‐VEGaS guidelines and assessment tool, and the analogous robotic consensus statement, provide a practical standard against which such content can be produced and appraised; no plastic‐surgery‐specific equivalent yet exists, but these principles translate readily to reconstructive POV footage [14, 15, 16]. Applying these principles to smart‐glasses footage—curating segments, adding labelled anatomical orientation, and pairing each clip with defined learning goals—would help ensure that the high perceived educational value reported here translates into resources of reproducible instructional quality.

A further opportunity lies in editing the same source footage for different intended audiences. Content can be tailored along the training continuum. For example, simplified, reassurance‐oriented clips for patient pre‐operative education; broader anatomical and procedural overviews for medical students; and detailed, step‐specific technical commentary for registrars undertaking the procedure themselves. Aligning the depth and framing of each output to its audience would maximise educational value while accommodating the differing needs of patients, learners, and operating surgeons.

However, consultant feedback identified where the technology has not yet reached its full potential for operative use. While rating video quality and stability highly, consultants were more critical of field of view and audio and less likely to recommend routine documentation use. Free‐text comments also made clear that the most important technical gap is fine‐detail capture. Requests for zoom and loupe compatibility were prominent and consultant‐led, effectively positioning optical integration and magnification as prerequisites for broader adoption in a specialty where precision handling and small structures are routine. Because photography is foundational to plastic and reconstructive practice, POV video can complement still imaging by capturing dynamic sequences and intraoperative rationale, but its utility is limited if key details cannot be reliably visualised. Consumer smart glasses may thus suit teaching anatomy and general step progression, but may require optical zoom, loupe‐mounting, stabilisation tuned for fine movements, and better low‐light performance to meet the demands of reconstructive work. Dedicated loupe/headlight‐mounted systems may remain preferable for routine capture of microsurgical or fine reconstructive detail in current practice [10].

Lighting was a further limitation: operating room and headlight illumination can create glare, hot spots, and exposure shifts that degrade interpretability in deeper planes or small cavities. Improved exposure control, handling of specular reflections, and dedicated ‘medical mode’ settings are actionable targets for manufacturers.

Perceptions of documentation and medicolegal utility were more nuanced and should be interpreted as future‐facing rather than definitive. Most respondents felt recordings could be helpful if questions or complaints arise, yet consultants were substantially more cautious about recommending routine use of documentation. This appears to reflect thresholds for adoption, rather than definitive scepticism: support was conditional on technical optimisation and clear governance. Qualitative feedback emphasised concerns around storage, institutional acceptability, and downstream risk, reflecting the distinction between “this could be useful” and “this should become part of the clinical record.” Overall, POV recording has perceived documentation and quality‐improvement potential, but real‐world deployment depends on robust governance, not just technical feasibility. If medicolegal applications are contemplated, recording should be embedded within an institutional governance framework that defines consent and appropriate use; secure transfer and storage to hospital‐controlled systems; retention; access controls; and permitted secondary use, with recordings avoiding patient identifiers (including faces and labels) and strict controls on copying and dissemination [11, 12, 13].

Patient‐facing applications were the least endorsed. While many respondents agreed that recordings could help explain procedures and outcomes, fewer perceived consistent value in reassurance or enjoyment, and comments cautioned that unedited intraoperative footage may be too intricate or confronting for some patients. This supports a selective approach focused on curated content and patient preference. Given the frequency of paediatric care in plastic and reconstructive practice, future work should also explore parent and guardian perspectives, including which imagery and consent language are acceptable and which use cases are perceived as beneficial versus intrusive.

Finally, this evaluation was conducted without industry involvement or conflicts of interest, strengthening its credibility as an early feasibility assessment. Maturation into a dependable tool will likely require collaboration with manufacturers to develop clinically oriented features and secure ‘hospital mode’ handling. The value proposition appears real, but the devices still have room to grow before meeting the full demands of plastic surgery practice.

Limitations

This study was a single‐centre pilot with a modest sample and a small consultant subgroup, limiting precision for role‐based comparisons. Participants viewed curated short clips and still images rather than continuous full‐length recordings, which may not fully capture the workflow burden or governance complexity associated with routine recording. The questionnaire was purpose‐built and not validated, and responses may be subject to selection and response bias, given the educational setting.

Conclusion

This single‐centre pilot suggests that head‐mounted POV recording using commercially available smart glasses can generate high‐quality footage and is perceived to have substantial educational value in plastic and reconstructive surgery. Video quality and stability were rated highly, and near‐universal agreement supported POV recordings as an effective method for teaching or reviewing surgical technique, particularly when paired with surgeon narration and clear anatomical orientation. These findings support POV recording as a practical adjunct to traditional apprenticeship learning, with immediate utility in structured teaching and asynchronous review.

Participants also perceived potential value for documentation and future medicolegal or quality‐improvement applications, but consultant responses highlighted a clear gap between feasibility and routine clinical adoption. Concerns regarding secure storage, institutional acceptability, and downstream risk emphasise that any move toward documentation use requires robust governance, including explicit consent processes, secure data transfer and storage pathways, strict avoidance of identifiers within recordings, and clear policy on access, retention, ownership/stewardship, and permitted secondary use of recorded material. Technically, the most consistent barrier to broader intraoperative usefulness was fine‐detail capture, with consultant‐driven calls for zoom and loupe compatibility, as well as improvements in audio and lighting/exposure performance. Overall, the technology appears useful but has not yet reached its full potential; targeted collaboration with manufacturers to develop medically oriented features, combined with rigorous governance and stakeholder engagement (including patients and parents), will be central to safe and scalable implementation.
