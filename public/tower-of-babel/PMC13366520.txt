Graphlet- and motif-based link prediction in large networks
Authors: Wayne B. Hayes, Kimia Yazdani, S.M.A. Nahian, Ezra Alemu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryLink prediction is important across biological, social, and technological networks, but many methods either require domain-specific node attributes, do not scale to large graphs, or miss higher-order topology. We present BLANT-Predict, a topology-only framework that uses sampled graphlets and orbit-pair frequencies to rank likely missing edges. Across 12 real-world networks (up to about 1 million nodes and 3 million edges), we compare against 13 baseline methods and observe higher precision with strong scalability. Beyond standard k-fold cross-validation, we evaluate predictions against future out-of-sample network snapshots to better reflect real deployment. BLANT-Predict maintains superior precision in these future tests, indicating practical value for predicting previously unobserved links.

Introduction

Link prediction has applications across diverse domains, including biology, social networks, user product networks, and recommendation systems.1 For instance, understanding protein-protein interactions (PPIs) can aid in mapping cellular functions, leading to better understanding of disease mechanisms and enabling drug discovery as well as reducing the costs of wet-lab experiments.2,3,4 In social and recommendation networks, predicting connections is challenging due to the sparsity of the networks, but it can aid friend discovery and has economic benefits by connecting consumers to products they may not otherwise know about.5,6 Despite its wide range of applications, edge prediction remains a challenging problem, whose computational complexity in both time and space can grow exponentially with network size. Additionally, evaluating prediction algorithms is challenging because we often do not have access to ground truth. Therefore, edge prediction is a critical and complex area of research.1

Some methods use node attributes, either fully or partially, to make edge predictions.7,8,9 However, node attributes are often domain specific and can overshadow the contribution of the network’s structural relationships.10,11,12 In this work, we use network topology alone, using only the existing connections between nodes to predict new ones. This approach allows our method to work on any network, regardless of the domain. Although this method could later be combined with attribute-based methods for improved predictions, this paper focuses solely on network-based approaches, as it serves as a litmus test to demonstrate that topology alone is capable of making accurate predictions; combining multiple sources of information is left for future work.

Our approach was inspired by the physical interpretation of PPI networks, where edges represent actual physical bindings between protein interfaces. We leverage the experimentally observed fact that certain network “motifs” that occur with high frequency will occasionally appear in the data with a missing edge—indicative of a false negative. More specifically, consider that a pair of proteins (u,v) can be connected to each other not only by a single interaction but also by being embedded in many copies of the same network motif, say μ, that recurs among nodes in their neighborhood. When such a multitude of identical overlapping motifs exist—all containing u and v in the same locations in each copy of μ—it means that the same walk through the network exists between u and v multiple times. Each step on that walk is an edge whose protein endpoints must, by definition, have physically compatible interfaces allowing them to interact. At each end of these identical walks lie u and v, thereby providing information about potential compatibilities between the interfaces of u and v themselves, constrained by the multitude of topologically identical walks μ between them. Now, assume the same multitude of walks, defined by network motif μ, exists for many distinct pairs of nodes (u,v) across the network, and that in most cases u and v also interact directly. Then, if we observe two proteins x and y that also appear at the appropriate locations in many copies of μ but are not directly connected in our data, it suggests the edge (x,y) is missing from our data. This is the basis on which Basic Local Alignment of Network Topology (BLANT)-Predict can predict the edge (x,y).

With BLANT-Predict, we generalize the above principle: given any input network G, we sample graphlets from G and track how often node pairs co-occur in specific structural roles, then we look for cases in which that edge appears to be “missing.” In this sense, the physical topology of the network itself provides evidence for likely but undetected interactions. As in statistical physics, we treat the network as a collection of small structural patterns (graphlets) and estimate the likelihood of an edge between two nodes based on how often they appear together in specific roles within these patterns. This estimated likelihood is analogous to an interaction potential, which reflects how local structural evidence supports the existence of a connection.

Previous work in link prediction

Existing methods use a variety of approaches. Similarity-based methods use metrics like common neighbors or Jaccard coefficient to assess how likely it is that two nodes are connected.13,14,15,16 Matrix factorization methods decompose the adjacency matrix to uncover latent information tied to the network’s structure.17 Graph-embedding approaches represent nodes in a low-dimensional space while preserving topological relationships.18 Machine-learning and deep-learning models, including graph neural networks (GNNs), attempt to learn patterns from the topology to predict edges.19,20 Lastly, local topology patterns, such as graphlets and motifs, identify recurring subgraph structures that reveal higher-order connectivity in the network.4,21 Related work has also explored motif-based prediction in hypergraphs, where hyper-edges connect more than two nodes, extending motif concepts beyond binary interactions.22

Motif-based approaches in edge prediction focus on identifying local patterns in the network, but they are often limited to very simple structures due to their high computational cost. Finding all motifs in a network is exponential both with the size of the network and the motif.23 Existing methods typically analyze motifs with only a few nodes, usually up to size 5, because finding and counting larger or more complex patterns is prohibitively expensive.24 The simplest example of these patterns is the common neighbor pattern, a path of length 2, which is well known to be highly predictive of missing links in social networks.25,26 Kovács et al. show that this pattern does not work well in PPI networks,27 instead showing that a path of length 3 is a better structural pattern for edge prediction in these networks.

The contrast between the common neighbors used in social networks and the work of Kovács et al. suggests that different types of networks differ fundamentally in what local structures have predictive value—presumably because different types of networks are built via fundamentally different processes. Thus, determining which network motifs might have predictive value is a difficult problem possibly requiring a fundamental understanding behind the structure of any given network—obviously a very tall order.

In this work, we introduce an extension of BLANT,28 which leverages higher-order motifs for edge prediction. BLANT efficiently samples graphlets of up to eight nodes and utilizes pre-computed orbit-pair statistics to capture detailed structural information about node pairs. BLANT-Predict uses pre-computed mappings to make this task significantly faster.29 Our method is applicable to undirected and unweighted networks of any domain, including physical interaction, social, and recommendation networks—although extending it to directed and weighted graphs, as well as graphs in which nodes and/or edges have “types” (i.e., colors), is possible in principle. We will show here that this method not only outperforms existing methods in all domains in terms of prediction accuracy but also enables us to make a significantly higher number of predictions at higher accuracy. Additionally, we will show that despite being able to consider more complicated patterns, our method is dramatically faster than the existing methods.

Finally, we note that the most stringent test of any predictive mechanism is its ability to predict the future—i.e., predicting events not only that it has not been trained, tested, or validated on, but also events that have not yet happened. In this spirit, we introduce a novel testing mechanism we call “future testing.” This method uses historical network data to generate predictions using only data that were available as of a certain date, then testing the predictions against future data. By avoiding artificial balancing of positive and negative samples, future testing provides a more realistic measure of a method’s ability to predict truly novel, never-before-seen events.

Definitions

Graph

Let graph G = (V,E) be an unweighted, undirected graph with node set V = {v1,v2, …,vn} and edge set E ⊆ {(vi,vj)∣vi,vj ∈ V}.

Graphlets and motifs

A graphlet g is a connected, induced subgraph of G consisting of k nodes—where “induced” means that g contains all the edges between the k nodes that exist in G. Formally, g = (V′,E′), where V ′⊆ V and E′ = {(vi,vj) ∈ E∣vi,vj ∈ V′}. A motif is similar to a graphlet except that a motif is not required to be induced. For example, as shown in Figure 1, given a k-node graphlet g induced on G, a motif can be constructed by taking any subset of the edges of g that maintains the property that the resulting subgraph is still connected (Lemma: a graphlet that is a tree on k nodes has no motifs other than itself). An orbit refers to the specific role or position of a node within a graphlet or motif. Nodes in the same orbit are topologically indistinguishable within the graphlet or motif, as shown in Figure 2. Given a graph g1 with adjacency matrix A1, we say that graph g2 with adjacency matrix A2 is isomorphic to g1 if there exists a permutation of the nodes of g2 that makes its adjacency matrix identical to A1. Orbit-pair frequency

To understand the fundamental intuition behind our method, consider a social network where each node is a person and two people are connected if they know each other (“are friends”). Now imagine that we have two people, Alice and Bob, who do not know each other, but they have some common friends. Clearly, the more friends they have in common, the more likely they are to know each other. If the number of friends they share is m, and p(m) represents the probability that Alice and Bob know each other, we can confidently say that in any real-world social network, p(m) → 1 as m → ∞. More generally, if we have a large network with perfect friendship information (i.e., no erroneous or missing edges), we could plot the mean of p(m) across all node pairs (not just Alice and Bob) as a function of m, and we would expect it to asymptotically approach 1 as m → ∞.

The intuition behind our method lies in observing that even in an imperfect network (i.e., one that is missing edges compared to the perfect one), we can empirically estimate p(m) by creating a histogram of the fraction f(m) of pairs of people that are connected in our data, as a function m—the number of friends they have in common. If the data are not overly noisy, then f(m) will be a good approximation of p(m). Thus, whenever our imperfect network shows that two people with m common friends are not connected, f(m) provides an estimate of the probability that they “should” be connected. Note that if m is small and f(m) ≪ 1, we have little confidence that they should be connected; but as m increases and f(m) → 1, our confidence in predicting an edge between them increases.

The function f(m) above, also known as the common neighbors method, has long been known to be a good predictor of links in social networks.26 In more graph-theoretical terms, a friend that is common to Alice and Bob induces a length-2 path or L2 path—a path containing two edges—between Alice and Bob; the number of common friends thus counts the number of L2 paths between Alice and Bob. Unfortunately, as demonstrated by Kovács et al.,27 the common neighbors algorithm is not a good predictor of a missing edge between proteins u and v in a PPI network; however, they found that the number of length-3 paths (L3 paths) connecting u and v is an excellent predictor. The reason has to do with the physical shape of folded proteins, and is explained in detail by Kovács et al., but the general point is clear: common neighbors is not universally applicable, and predicting missing links in an arbitrary type of network may require a deep understanding of the mechanism of interaction between its constituent nodes.

Kovács et al. demonstrated the utility of L3 path counts in PPI networks by empirically constructing a histogram of L3 counts similar to our f(m) function described above (cf. their Figure 1f on page 2 of their paper).27 They then showed that high L3 counts could make genuinely novel predictions of missing links. This suggests that, even with imperfect network data, one can empirically demonstrate the predictive value of a proposed scoring mechanism by showing it has a strong positive correlation with observed edge existence.

We now rephrase both the L2 and L3 path counts in terms of graphlets. Referring to Figure 2, the L2 path is graphlet number 1—the first 3-node graphlet; Alice and Bob would be at the endpoints, which are both orbit number 1. Thus, in counting the number of L2 paths between Alice and Bob, we are counting the orbit-pair frequency of the (o,p) = (1,1) orbit pair between Alice and Bob. We can empirically verify that the L2 path count (also known as the (1,1) orbit-pair frequency) has predictive value in a social network by plotting a histogram, across all node pairs, of the fraction of node pairs that are connected as a function of their mutual (1,1)-orbit-pair count and showing that it approaches 1 as the count increases.

Similarly, the L3 path is graphlet number 3—the first 4-node graphlet; proteins u and v would lie at its endpoints, which are both orbit 4. Thus, in counting the number of L3 paths between u and v, we are counting the orbit-pair frequency of the (o,p) = (4,4) orbit pair between u and v. We can also empirically verify that the L3 path count (also known as the (4,4) orbit-pair frequency) has predictive value in a PPI network by plotting a histogram of the L3 count f(l3) and showing it approaches 1 as L3 increases.

In this paper, we generalize the above ideas by demonstrating that, given imperfect data representing any real-world network, it is possible to discover which orbit pairs have predictive value and then use them to predict missing links. We do this by first globally accumulating, for every pair of nodes (u,v) in the network, the complete set of orbit-pair frequencies between (u,v) for all possible orbit pairs (o,p). Then, for each orbit pair (o,p), we create a histogram across all node pairs of the proportion of node pairs that have an edge between them as a function of their (o,p) frequency. If the histogram shows a strong positive correlation —and the observed edge proportion approaches 1 as the (o,p) count increases—then we say that (o,p) is a predictive orbit pair. We can then use the (o,p) count of predictive orbit pairs to predict missing links between node pairs that have a high (o,p) count but no observed link in the data.

A pair of nodes is more likely to be connected if they frequently co-occur as connected with a certain orbit pair in a motif. For example, in social networks, the common neighbors heuristic asks “how many times do two nodes both appear at the endpoints of a 3-node path, each connected to a shared neighbor?” In motif terms, this is equivalent to asking “how often do two nodes co-occur in orbit 1 of the 3-node path (Figure 2), with different intermediate nodes occupying orbit 2?” Similarly, in PPI networks, the L3 motif (a 4-node path) is was shown to be predictive by Kovács et al.27 In this case, we ask “how often do two nodes co-occur at the endpoints of a 4-node path (both in orbit 4), connected via different intermediate nodes in orbit 5?”

We formalize this intuition through the notion of orbit-pair frequency. Let OP={o:p∣o,p∈Oik,whereOik∈Ok} denote the set of all possible orbit pairs that a node pair can occupy in k-node graphlets. A node-orbit quadruple (u,v,o,p) is defined as a node pair (u,v) in the input graph G along with the orbit pair o:p ∈ OP. The orbit-pair frequency C(u,v)(o,p) is the number of times (u,v) appear at orbits (o,p), respectively, across distinct copies of a particular motif in G.

Across most networks, existing methods show a clear decline in precision as network size increases. However, BLANT-Predict, particularly with k = 4 and k = 5, maintains strong precision values even as the node count increases. For example, in the Twitter network, BLANT-Predict with k = 5 achieves a precision of 65% at rank 103, outperforming DotHash’s 45%. BLANT-Predict with k = 4 performs even better with a precision of 80%, a 40% advantage over DotHash. Note that the Routers network was a challenge for most methods, in that most methods performed worse on Routers than on any other network. It is also the only network where BLANT-Predict was beaten at all, and only by one method, DotHash. We believe the universally bad performance—and especially our own—is due to the Routers network consisting of ad hoc connections between nodes with arbitrary locations in a building, rather than connections built by some natural process as in all the other networks.

When running DotHash across the several networks, the algorithm demonstrates high-precision values in networks such as Routers and CondMat. It is the only method that comes even remotely close to challenging BLANT-Predict. However, DotHash performs considerably worse in biological networks. For example, in the bio-HS-LC network, DotHash’s validation precision is severely non-monotonic with rank—its most confident predictions have low precision, then later higher precision, then later low again, and so forth. We see similar—though less drastic—fluctuations in its precision across about half of the other networks. This makes it difficult to have confidence in its ranking system.

Additionally, only BLANT-Predict and the CommonNeighbor family of methods are capable of directly generating candidate predictions rather than merely assigning scores to all possible node pairs. The other methods do not inherently produce predictions; they require exhaustively scoring every node pair in the network, which is challenging to scale to large networks (we can do it only due to BLANT-Predict’s excellent efficiency).

When comparing the real times across methods in Table 5, we find that BLANT-Predict performed faster than other high-precision methods. Using BLANT-Predict with k = 7 graphlets, we observed that it took 6,138.92 s for the bio-HS-LC network, while DotHash and CRS took 1,800.50 s and 2,012.50 s, respectively. However, for HI-union, a larger network, BLANT-Predict with k = 7 required 1.25 h. Meanwhile, it took DotHash 34.35 h, and CRS was not able to predict edges for the HI-union network. For a number of folds, CRS had high variability and termination due to running out of memory for the AstroPh and CondMat networks. This demonstrates BLANT-Predict’s ability to scale efficiently and run on large networks in a reasonable amount of time.

To further quantify how many links can be predicted reliably in practice, we additionally report threshold-based precision statistics in Tables 1 and 2 For each dataset and method, we record the rank at which precision first drops below 0.5 and, where applicable, below 0.2. This provides an interpretable estimate of how many predictions can be trusted at moderate to high confidence, which is particularly relevant for large-scale and applied settings. Across most networks, BLANT-Predict sustains precision above 0.5 for substantially more predictions than competing methods. While some baselines do eventually surpass 50% precision at later ranks despite having low precision among their top-ranked predictions, these gains are often inconsistent and reflect highly non-monotonic ranking behavior. In contrast, BLANT-Predict produces a precision-versus-rank curve that is significantly closer to monotonic, meaning that early predictions are reliably the most accurate. This stability makes it easier for practitioners to select a cutoff appropriate for their application without requiring extensive post hoc calibration.

These observations suggest that BLANT-Predict scales more effectively than existing methods, retaining high precision in large and complex networks where others falter. It also suggests that BLANT’s ability to leverage higher-order structures provides a robustness that the current methods in the literature cannot match.

We further evaluated all methods using our future-testing framework, where predictions are made on the BioGRID human PPI network (release 3.0.064, April 2010) and validated against a recent human PPI network (release 4.4.233, April 2024). This setup mimics a real-world scenario where predictions are made in a vacuum with no knowledge of their validity until after the fact.

Discussion

BLANT-Predict predicts new links using network topology alone by leveraging orbit pairs of graphlets of up to 8 nodes. When applied to 12 diverse real-world networks from the biological, social, and technological domains, BLANT-Predict clearly outperforms existing methods in accuracy, scalability, and efficiency.

Our standard 10-fold cross-validation results demonstrate that BLANT-Predict achieves superior precision across networks of varying sizes. While most methods degrade as network size increases, BLANT-Predict maintains strong performance, especially on large-scale networks such as Twitter, Amazon, and YouTube. BLANT’s scalability—including being trivially parallelizable—is particularly important considering that real-world networks are already large and continue to grow. We note that due to resource constraints on our workstations, the largest networks were run only on k = 4, although a typical enterprise server would have no problem with higher k.

We noticed a subtle change that depended on the number of folds into which we split each network. We initially attempted 2-fold and 5-fold cross-validations—2-fold means training on 50% of edges and testing on the other 50%, as was done by Kovács et al.27 We saw a small improvement as the number of folds increased (because training on 90% of edges is better than training on 50%), but the improvement was small. More importantly, we observed that as the number of folds increased, there was a shift in the distribution of which orbits were predictive: the 90% set had a similar set of predictive orbits to “training” on 100% (which would be cheating from a test perspective); we also noticed that orbit pairs from more dense graphlets became more prominent as the number of folds increased, because using 90% of the edges results in graphs almost twice as edge dense as using only 50%. In the end we decided it was best to train on subnetworks that more closely resemble the full network, which is why we went with ten folds.

Finally, while fold-based cross-validation is a standard tool for evaluating predictive mechanisms, we believe that our “future” method is a far superior testing strategy, since it more closely resembles the real-world “in-the-wild” scenario of predicting future events that have literally never been seen before. In that context, Figure 5 suggests that larger graphlets produce superior predictions when tested across a 14-year time span: predictions made using only data from 2010, validated against data available in 2024. In particular, all values of k > 4 outperform k = 4 by about a factor of 2 in precision: k = 4 only breaks out beyond 40% precision in its top 30 ranked predictions and then hovers between 20% and 30% out to rank 10,000. In stark contrast, all the k > 4 methods stay above 60% precision for their top 1,000 predictions—with k = 6, 7, 8 performing best—and they stay above 40% precision out to rank 5,000. Furthermore, the “combined k” maintains 40% precision out to an astounding 20,000 predictions.

We hypothesize that our method’s success—even on large networks and even testing on future data—arises because it naturally “discovers” what kinds of network structures tend to be associated with edge existence, at least in networks that are built by a “natural” process. In most real networks, the processes that result in such structures do not necessarily undergo fundamental changes as the networks grow; thus, the structures that are predictive in one snapshot in time or space are likely to remain predictive at future times or in different places, so long as the underlying “natural process” that creates the network does not fundamentally change. Finally, in the context of trying to discover or understand these underlying processes, we note that AI/machine-learning methods often produce predictions that are opaque and unexplainable. In contrast, contemplating the predictive orbits produced by our method may provide insights into the underlying network processes, thus accelerating our understanding of network dynamics.

Future directions

Despite its promising results, BLANT-Predict has several limitations. First, it currently operates only on undirected, unweighted networks. Many real-world applications involve directed, weighted, or heterogeneous graphs. Although our current framework does not currently support these features, we are already actively working on implementing them. Second, while BLANT-Predict efficiently samples graphlets up to size k = 8, larger graphlets or exhaustive enumeration remain computationally expensive. Although the method is scalable to large networks, extending it to even more complex structures would require additional optimization or parallelization.

We believe that BLANT’s efficiency and efficacy lend it to many possible future directions. In particular, one of the reviewers suggested many interesting questions that arise directly from the results presented here, which include the following. Is the success of the relatively small motifs related to the relevance of short paths (or short hop-distance)—e.g., the famous “6 degrees of separation” idea of social networks? Are the predicted links somehow related to their “importance”—e.g., centrality and other widely studied properties?

Additionally, we may attempt comparisons with more recent GNN-based link prediction methods under standardized benchmarks and possibly integrating orbit-pair signals into GNN models.

Conclusion

In conclusion, BLANT-Predict advances topology-based edge prediction by combining higher-order structural analysis, scalability, and practical validation through future testing. By addressing the challenges of edge prediction with these contributions, this work aims to enhance the accuracy and applicability of link prediction methods. We have shown that many of the current measures are either too resource intensive to work on large real networks or are far too expensive to be practical. In contrast, BLANT-Predict is both generalizable and practical for a variety of network types. Its strong performance across diverse networks and ability to generalize without relying on node attributes make it a valuable tool for both research and real-world applications. Future work will focus on extending its capabilities to more graph types and hypergraphs and integrating it into practical systems.

Methods

Using BLANT for sampling

BLANT28 uses pre-computed isomorphism tables29 to quickly determine graphlets, motifs, and orbits for sampled nodes. BLANT’s ability to sample millions of graphlets per second of up to 8 nodes provides a significant advantage over existing graphlet/motif methods, which are often limited to graphlets of size 5 or smaller due to computational constraints.31 Other graphlet enumeration methods, whether exhaustive or sampling based, have been limited to k ≤ 6.32,33 Additional pipeline and resource-stability details are provided in Figure S1 and Tables S1–S6.

Extension of BLANT for edge prediction

We extend BLANT by introducing a framework for edge prediction that leverages motif orbit pairs. Our approach considers complicated structural patterns by using graphlets of up to size 8. We utilize the information inside every submotif of a graphlet as well. To take the information of each node pair into account, we consider each of the orbit pairs inside each submotif as node-orbit quadruple. It provides us with over 7 billion possibilities when considering all combinations of orbit pairs for k = 8. In all the measurements, we are using our framework using a single thread, but all the processes are easy to parallelize.

Here is the breakdown of the steps.

Sampling motifs

We sample k-node graphlets from the network using BLANT’s algorithm. For each sampled graphlet g, we identify its canonical form and update a data structure that tracks the frequency of each node pair (ui,uj) appearing in orbit pair o:p within g and its submotifs. Sampling continues until the estimated graphlet frequencies reach a user-defined precision or stopping condition (e.g., max samples or time).

Evaluation methods

Edge prediction is inherently challenging because it involves discovering unknown connections, and there is no comprehensive gold standard to validate them. In practice, the set of “negative samples”—i.e., the set over which we wish to make predictions—is more complex. In the real world, the set of negative samples usually vastly outnumbers the number of positive samples, turning prediction into a “needle-in-a-haystack” problem. This is a true facet of prediction in the real world, but it makes the problem too difficult for some methods.

To counter this, some methods15,16,21 drastically whittle down the “negative set” to a size that is comparable with the positive set. This form of balancing creates a simplified binary classification task, where an equal number of removed edges (“positive samples”) and randomly sampled non-edges (“negative samples”) are used for evaluation. In this artificially constructed space, a random guess yields 50% accuracy, and a method is judged as “good” if its predictions among the non-edges is significantly higher than 50%. However, this is an extraordinarily bad heuristic—to say the least—for approximating the real-world “needle-in-a-haystack” problem. For example, consider the 2024 H. sapiens network in Table 7: it has 19,872 nodes and 831,821 edges. Thus, the number of non-edges is (19,872 choose 2)–831,821, or about 196.6 million. Assume we train on an artificially balanced dataset with 800,000 true edges and 800,000 non-edges chosen at random from the 196 million. Further, let us very generously assume the method achieves 99% precision when tested on the balanced training set. This looks deceptively good, but if applied to the “needle in the haystack” of 196.6 million non-edges, it could not possibly maintain the 99% true-positive rate, and worse still, its 1% false-positive rate would result in about 2 million spurious predictions—very likely drowning out any signal remaining among its true positives. We call this the “50-50” method of testing, and we consider it wholly inadequate for real-world application.

One might expect that we are at a disadvantage to methods that employ node features. However, to our knowledge, recent methods that employ node features (such as GATNE,38 muxGNN,39 and SSN_GATNE-T40) suffer two failings: first, they have been applied only to small networks (or tiny subsamples of larger ones); and second, they universally use the 50-50 evaluation metric.

To address this, we only compare our approach to methods that can produce a ranked list of predicted node pairs (u,v) to connect across all currently disconnected node pairs (any method that cannot do this is inapplicable for the “needle-in-a-haystack” problem, unless it can somehow produce the top-scoring pairs without looking at all pairs—we know of no such method). We then report precision@rank across the full prediction space, avoiding any artificial balancing. Additionally, we apply a more realistic evaluation method called future testing, which tests a method’s ability to predict truly never-before-seen edges.

Precision@rank

We carry out a traditional (non-future) 10-fold cross-validation on each dataset. Each fold includes 90% of the edges as the training set and the remaining 10% as the test set, repeated ten times so that over the 10-folds, every true edge has been a test edge exactly once. Thereafter, the precision@rank is measured for the top 1 million ranked predictions for each fold, if existent for a method. Precision@rank is calculated asPrecision@rank=Numberofvalidatedpredictionsuptoandincludingrankrank.In the end, the precision@rank is combined for all folds as the mean precision@rank.

Future testing

As shown in the results section, 10-fold cross validation gives an unrealistic view of prediction precision when compared to predicting the future—at least for PPI networks: sometimes optimistic but also, surprisingly, sometimes pessimistic. We evaluate the performance of all methods through a “future-testing” approach by leveraging historical data for predictions and validating them with future data. The goal of edge prediction ultimately is to predict, at the time, unknown connections. With this metric, we measure the performance of edge prediction through its real-life usage. We use the H. sapiens April 2010 (BioGRID release 3.0.064) network to make predictions and then validate the predictions of the historical data, looking at the H. sapiens April 2024 (BioGRID release 4.4.233) network. The April 2010 network has 28,117 edges and by using it for prediction, it is comparable to conducting future testing to the year 2024 (831,821 edges) and evaluating the percentage of the predictions that were validated.

Competing methods

Among all the methods that use node attributes, Gelato41 is the only one that explicitly frames link prediction as a “needle-in-a-haystack” problem, yet it still relies on sampled hard negatives via graph partitioning rather than ranking against the literal full set of all non-edges. No published methods utilizing node attributes reports results on our full-scale datasets without these sampling and balancing problems, so we restrict our empirical comparison to scalable topology-only methods.

We did an extensive comparison with other edge prediction methods. To ensure a fair comparison, we ran each method identically on the same set of networks with the same folds. Several methods already exist that claim to use topology alone,42 and we compare against the ones that had working source code either online or directly from the authors, or which had errors we could easily fix. We focus on topology-only, pattern-based link prediction methods; while GNN-based approaches are important, fair comparison requires carefully controlled training and benchmark setups, and recent work has highlighted potential evaluation pitfalls in some settings.43 We do, however, include Node2Vec, a deep-learning method, because it does not suffer this deficiency.44 In addition, recent data-centric analyses have shown that link prediction performance is highly dependent on underlying graph-formation mechanisms, and that no single model class performs uniformly well across different structural regimes.45

Many methods were only able to run on small datasets. To highlight the scalability of our method, we compared only against methods that were able to make predictions on networks with more than 5,000 nodes and 100,000 edges. Also, they had to be runnable on topology-alone information. Most tests were performed on a machine with 92 GB of RAM, but for methods that failed on such machines we also attempted them on a machine with 500 GB of RAM. Any method that failed on the 500 GB machine was labeled “out of memory.” We classify a run as “out of time” if it exceeds 48 h of wall-clock time execution. However, we report total CPU (user) time for parallel methods.

We compare our method against several baselines. NMF-CR17 leverages non-negative matrix factorization to capture both row and column space structure in the adjacency matrix. DotHash16 generates fixed-size sketch vectors using high-dimensional random projections and estimates similarity via dot products. CommonNeighbor21 explores not just the number of shared neighbors between two nodes but also how those neighbors are interconnected; we use their implementation to also apply naive link prediction algorithms (Adamic-Adar,46 resource allocation,47 common neighbors,26 Jaccard coefficient,48 and preferential attachment49) on the common neighbor graph using their candidate generation framework. MapSim15 uses an information-theoretic approach based on modular compression of network flows to assess node similarity. CRS (community relationship strength)14 combines node similarity with the structural closeness of their communities. Finally, Node2Vec44 learns low-dimensional node embeddings that preserve network neighborhoods through biased random walks.
