The imprinted Peg3 gene is a potent regulator of the DNA methylome in trophoblast cells
Authors: Caroline Kokorudz, Noura Ballasy, Bethany N Radford, Anshul S Jadli, Alexa Krala, Ankita Narang, Wendy Dean, Myriam Hemberger
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryPaternally expressed gene 3 (Peg3) is an imprinted gene whose knockout (KO) causes placental and fetal growth deficits as well as maternal behavioral defects, but its molecular role remains poorly understood. Here, we report that Peg3-null trophoblast stem cells (TSCs) exhibit increased rates of mitotic errors and double-strand DNA breaks as a likely consequence of a global reduction in H3K9me3 and DNA methylation levels. Transcriptional profiling highlights a deficiency of KO cells to adequately silence genes, resulting in elevated expression levels of differentiation stage-inappropriate genes. Peg3 KO TSCs also exhibit a loss of DNA methylation and H3K9me3 at a subset of developmentally regulated CpG islands that likely rewire CTCF-regulated transcriptional networks. Collectively, our data identify PEG3 as an intrinsic component of the epigenetic repressive machinery, thus solidifying its putative role as a KRAB-ZFP protein that functions to regulate gene expression and genome integrity in the placental trophoblast lineage.Keywords: trophoblast stem cells, imprinted genes, DNA methylation, DNA repair, KRAB-ZFPs, CRISPR-Cas9 knockout, epigenetic repression

Introduction

DNA methylation is the covalent modification of the cytosine base in the C5 position by a methyl group, resulting in the formation of 5-methylcytosine (5mC). This chemical change is the best-studied epigenetic modification, with a clearly established mechanism of heritability mediated through UHRF1 that links the repressive histone mark H3K9me3 and the maintenance DNA methyltransferase DNMT1 (Arita et al., 2008; Ren et al., 2020; Sharif et al., 2007). DNA methylation is critical for cell identity and survival, ensuring genome stability, retroviral silencing, X chromosome inactivation and imprinted gene regulation (Jaenisch and Bird, 2003; Reik and Lewis, 2005). Moreover, at gene promoters, DNA methylation is commonly associated with transcriptional repression, whereas a hypomethylated state is required for the gene to be permissive for transcription (Deaton and Bird, 2011).

While each mammalian cell type is characterized by a distinct DNA methylation profile (Loyfer et al., 2023), the extra-embryonic trophoblast lineage is unique as it is globally hypomethylated compared to somatic cells. This affects in particular centromeric satellite repeats and retroviral elements, whereas many promoters of lineage-inappropriate genes are hypermethylated to mediate stable, heritable gene repression, such as those of the Hox gene cluster (Nakanishi et al., 2012; Senner et al., 2012).

While the molecular mechanisms underlying the maintenance of existing DNA methylation marks are well established, how DNA methylation patterns are established remains poorly understood. The DNMTs lack sequence specificity and hence rely on sequence-specific targeting conferred by interacting factors for cell lineage- and cell type-specific DNA methylation. The best-understood mechanism through which specificity of DNA methylation marking is afforded is via the family of Krüppel-associated box-domain containing zinc finger proteins (KRAB-ZFPs) (Ecco et al., 2017; Rowe et al., 2013a; Wiznerowicz et al., 2007). The zinc finger motifs of these proteins allow sequence-specific DNA binding, while the KRAB domain serves as adaptor for TRIM28 (also known as KAP1), which constitutes a hub for multiple epigenetic repressors including DNMTs, histone H3K9 methyltransferases, histone deacetylases and others. KRAB-ZFPs can trigger de novo DNA methylation of specific genomic elements via TRIM28/KAP1 recruitment (Macfarlan et al., 2012; Rowe et al., 2013a, 2013b; Wolf et al., 2015). Some of the most prominent examples of KRAB-ZFPs include ZFP809, required for silencing of endogenous retroviral elements, and ZFP57 as well as ZNF445 at imprinting control regions (Quenneville et al., 2011; Wolf and Goff, 2009; Wolf et al., 2015). In general, this concept suggests that the DNA methylation profile of any given cell may be determined by the specific combination of KRAB-ZFPs expressed in that cell (Corsinotti et al., 2013; Rowe et al., 2013a; Wiznerowicz et al., 2007).

Beyond these relatively few examples, however, our understanding of the genomic targets of the large family of KRAB-ZFPs remains poorly explored. PEG3 has been identified as a KRAB-A box-containing ZFP (He et al., 2016). The KRAB-A domain is the critical component for recruiting TRIM28 (Urrutia, 2003), and indeed co-immunoprecipitation experiments have shown that PEG3 physically interacts with TRIM28 (He et al., 2016). These findings suggest that PEG3 could be considered a member of the KRAB-ZFP family.

Peg3 is subject to genomic imprinting and is expressed only from the paternal allele (Li et al., 1999). While a paternally inherited null mutation of Peg3 is viable, the placentas of these mutant conceptuses are smaller, and the pups exhibit postnatal growth retardation (Tunster et al., 2018). Additionally, Peg3-null females show a profound impairment in maternal behavior, which frequently results in the death of their offspring (Li et al., 1999). In the current study, we generated Peg3-mutant trophoblast stem cell (TSC) lines and analyzed their phenotype in terms of self-renewal and differentiation dynamics, as well as for changes in DNA methylation and H3K9me3 occupancy. We find that Peg3 deficiency leads to a general failure to adequately repress differentiation stage-inappropriate genes combined with a loss of DNA methylation at numerous promoter CpG islands (CGIs). These findings implicate the imprinted Peg3 gene as a core component of the cell type-specific DNA methylation machinery.

Results

Peg3 is abundantly expressed in trophoblast cells

Peg3 is amongst the most abundant transcriptional regulators in the mid-gestation placenta (Kokorudz et al., 2022). Due to our general interest in the transcriptional and epigenetic regulation of trophoblast differentiation, we set out to characterize the function of PEG3 in murine TSCs. Thus, we first characterized the expression dynamics of Peg3 mRNA and PEG3 protein in TSCs. We found that Peg3 was expressed at levels comparable to other well-known TSC markers, such as Cdx2, Eomes, Elf5, and Tfap2c (Figure 1A). Upon in vitro differentiation of TSCs for 2, 4, and 6 days (D), Peg3 mRNA expression further increased over 6-fold (Figure 1B). In line with its role as a transcriptional regulator, immunostaining localized the PEG3 protein to the nucleus of TSCs (Figure 1C). Reflecting the mRNA expression pattern, PEG3 protein staining intensity was notably increased in trophoblast giant cells (TGCs) differentiated from TSCs (Figure 1C). Immunostaining on E12.5 placentas showed a broad distribution of nuclear PEG3 staining across virtually all trophoblast cell types, with prominent expression in labyrinth trophoblast, the junctional zone (JZ), and particularly high expression in parietal TGCs (Figure 1D). Thus, PEG3 is a transcriptional regulator that exhibits pan-trophoblast expression and is up-regulated upon the transition of TSCs to differentiated trophoblast subtypes. (Figure S1A).

Peg3 ablation causes proliferation defects

To gain a mechanistic understanding of the role of Peg3 in trophoblast, we generated knockout (KO) TSC clones in two independent TSC lines by deleting a large portion of the main coding exon 9, thereby eliminating nine of the twelve zinc finger motifs (Figure 1E). This strategy was designed to effectively abolish the DNA-binding capacity of PEG3. Homozygous deletion of the 3,450 bp genomic fragment was confirmed by genotyping PCRs, RT-qPCRs, and visualization of RNA-sequencing (RNA-seq) reads across the Peg3 locus (Figures S1B–S1G). PEG3 immunostaining confirmed severely reduced signals in KO cells (Figure 1F; Figure S1E), thus corroborating the depletion of functional PEG3 protein. Peg3 KO TSCs could be grown and expanded in stem cell conditions with no obvious differences in proliferation rates (Figure 1G). However, when induced to differentiate, Peg3 KO cells ceased to proliferate more acutely than their wild-type (WT) counterparts (Figures 1H and 1I).

Transcriptomic changes link PEG3 to DNA replication, repair and the regulation of DNA methylation

To gain first insights into the transcriptional changes induced by Peg3 ablation, we performed RNA-seq on WT (n = 4) and KO (n = 3) TSCs grown in stem cell conditions (“STEM”) and after 2D and 4D of differentiation. Principal component analysis (PCA) on all expressed genes clearly separated the WT from the KO clones along PC2, while PC1 was driven by differentiation stage (Figure 2A). It was also obvious that the level of transcriptional heterogeneity was greater in KO compared to WT cells at all time points assessed (Figure 2A). DESeq2 analysis identified 1419, 1925 and 2232 differentially expressed (DE) genes in STEM, 2D and 4D differentiation conditions, respectively, of which approximately two-thirds were up-regulated (Figure 2B). The general propensity toward gene activation was further exemplified by the greater number of up-regulated genes that were shared between all three time points (249 genes) compared to the shared down-regulated genes (61 genes) (Figure 2C).

To assess which pathways were impacted across all stages of differentiation, gene ontology (GO) analyses were performed on DE genes between WT and KO cells regardless of differentiation stage. For the down-regulated genes, enriched terms were related to cell cycle regulation and DNA replication, with the most prominent associations involving DNA repair pathways (Figure 2D). The up-regulated genes produced “trophoblast stem cells” as a term indicative of lineage-specific up-regulated genes, as well as an enrichment in genes identified in knockdown of the ten-eleven translocation methylcytosine dioxygenase 1 (Tet1), a key enzyme in the DNA demethylation pathway (Figure 2D). The apparent overlap between Peg3 and Tet1 KO TSCs prompted us to assess the gene expression changes in Peg3 KO TSCs in the context of TET1 genomic occupancy profiles that we had established earlier (Chrysanthou et al., 2018; Senner et al., 2020). This analysis identified consistent trends, insofar as down-regulated genes tended to be enriched for TET1 binding sites, whereas genes up-regulated in Peg3 KO TSCs were significantly depleted for TET1 binding sites (Figure 2E). These data might suggest an element of competitive genome occupancy between PEG3 and TET1, where PEG3 normally blocks or counteracts the activity of TET1 at genes that are up-regulated in the Peg3 KO.

Peg3 deletion causes increased DNA damage

Prompted by the DNA repair-linked enrichment terms, we investigated the genes driving these terms more closely and found significantly reduced expression levels of many pivotal DNA damage and repair machinery components (Figure 3A). This included the X-ray repair cross-complementing protein 1 (Xrcc1) which acts as a scaffold to recruit the DNA repair machinery to sites of DNA damage, crucial sensors of DNA double-strand breaks including Brca2 (Breast Cancer gene 2) and Mre11a, and the minichromosome maintenance complex component Mcm7 whose depletion can lead to premature sister chromatid separation and genomic instability (Figure 3A) (Lavin, 2007; London, 2015; Yadav and Polasek-Sedlackova, 2024; Zheng et al., 2018). These genes exhibit overlapping roles related to DNA replication and cell cycle progression, and include replication factor Rfc2 and Geminin (Gmnn), a factor critically regulating endoreduplication in the formation of TGCs (Figure 3A) (Gonzalez et al., 2006).

Because of these enrichment terms, we stained WT and Peg3 KO TSCs for the DNA damage marker γH2A.X, a sensitive indicator of DNA double-strand breaks (Mah et al., 2010). Overall, more Peg3 KO than WT cells were strongly positive for γH2A.X foci, in particular as cells underwent differentiation and their nuclei enlarged during TGC formation (Figures 3B and 3C). These data demonstrated a key role of PEG3 in regulating the DNA repair machinery, which becomes increasingly important as TSCs differentiate into polyploid TGCs.

Upon visual observation of nuclei stained with 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI), we also noted the appearance of many “donut-shaped” nuclei in the Peg3 KO cells, a general indicator of centrosome separation and chromosome segregation defects (Figure 3D). Indeed, quantification of this morphological phenotype confirmed the higher frequency in Peg3 KO compared to WT cells (Figure 3D). This observation prompted us to stain for the centrosome marker gamma(γ)-tubulin together with the microtubule marker alpha(α)-tubulin, which revealed a significantly higher fraction of KO cells that exhibited mitotic errors such as anaphase bridges caused by lagging chromosomes during metaphase plate separation (Figure 3E). These defects resembled our previous observations in Tet1 KO and Tet1/2 double KO TSCs that also exhibited an increase in “donut-shaped” nuclei (Chrysanthou et al., 2018), hence resonating with the GO term analysis that identified a link with TET1 (Figure 2D). Collectively, these mitotic defects may account for the reduced proliferation rates observed, with the deleterious effects becoming most evident upon onset of TSC differentiation when PEG3 levels increase.

Peg3 KO TSCs are globally depleted for epigenetic repressive marks

Amongst the down-regulated genes were prominent epigenetic modifiers related to DNA methylation and to the repressive H3K9me3 mark. Thus, Dnmt3l was robustly down-regulated at all stages of differentiation, while Dnmt1 exhibited around 1.5-fold lower expression levels in Peg3 KOs in STEM and 2D differentiation conditions (Figure 4A; Figure S2A). Similarly, the expression levels of histone lysine methyltransferases Suv39h1, Suv39h2, and Ehmt2 (also known as G9a) that confer H3K9me3 at heterochromatic and euchromatic sites, respectively, were reduced (Figure 4A; Figure S2A). These changes were also globally observed on the protein level, albeit they were less pronounced (Figure 4B). The chromatin regulatory and methyltransferase-specific enrichment terms were evident in GO analyses when the down-regulated gene lists of STEM and 2D conditions were assessed (Figure S2B).

While immunostaining did not reveal an obvious loss or aberrant clustering of heterochromatic foci labeled by H3K9me3 in Peg3 KO TSCs (Figure 4C) (Gilbert et al., 2007), overall H3K9me3 abundance was markedly reduced by about 50% in the absence of PEG3 (Figures 4D and 4E). Moreover, immunostaining for 5-methylcytosine (5mC) revealed lower staining intensities in KO cells (Figures 4F and 4G). Thus, ablation of PEG3 causes a global reduction in the two most prominent epigenetic repressive marks, H3K9me3 and 5mC, most likely through transcriptional regulation of the corresponding modifying activities.

Peg3 KO TSCs exhibit reduced DNA methylation and H3K9me3 at a cohort of CpG islands

Given the reduction in global 5mC and H3K9me3 levels, we set out to determine how Peg3 ablation alters the epigenetic repressive landscape by performing meDIP-seq and H3K9me3 ChIP-seq, and assessing the DNA methylation and H3K9me3 enrichment across the mappable genome. We compared the DNA methylation profiles between WT and KO cells by using 1kb running windows, or by using a set of ∼23,000 developmentally regulated CGIs (Illingworth et al., 2010) which has proven to be biologically highly informative in defining cell states and cell types (Senner et al., 2012, 2020). Both analysis methods yielded similar results, insofar as the vast majority (∼80%) of differentially methylated regions (DMRs) were hypomethylated in Peg3 KO TSCs (Figures 5A–5C; Figures S3A–S3C; Table S1). Thus, overall, the mean enrichment of normalized read counts over CGIs and 1kb running windows was drastically reduced in Peg3 KO TSCs (Figure 5B; Figure S3B). Heatmaps of enrichment profiles at statistically significant DMRs highlighted the dominance of DNA-hypomethylated over -hypermethylated sites in Peg3 KO cells (Figure 5C; Figure S3C). Focusing on CGIs due to their defining role in gene regulation, we observed that DNA-hypomethylated CGIs were preferentially associated with up-regulated genes (at 15.6% of up-regulated DE genes vs. 4.6% of all genes).

To associate these changes with H3K9me3 profiles, we performed ChIP-seq for this repressive histone modification, as well as for the activating mark H3K4me3 as a control (Figures S4A and S4B). Despite the global reduction in H3K9me3 (Figures 4D and 4E), none of the 52,543 consensus peaks showed significant changes in abundance between WT and KO TSCs (n = 3 each) using the stringent criteria defined in DiffBind. However, when assessing H3K9me3 abundance at the 1240 DNA-hypomethylated CGIs, an overall depletion (Log2FC(WT/KO) > 1) in the repressive histone mark was observed at 135 of these loci, while only one single site was showing higher H3K9me3 enrichment levels (Figure 5D). When applying a Log2FC cut-off of >1.5, 20 sites were identified (including the Peg3 deleted region) that were substantially depleted for both 5mC and H3K9me3 (Figure 5E). 13 of the genes associated with these sites (∼70%) were also transcriptionally up-regulated. Although one of the de-repressed CGIs was located within the Zbtb45 and overlapping Trim28 locus, expression of neither of these genes was significantly changed. By comparison, previously identified PEG3-binding sites were less altered but, overall, also depleted for H3K9me3 enrichment (Figure S4C) (Thiaville et al., 2013; Ye et al., 2016).

Using the genes associated with DNA-hypomethylated CGIs to identify transcription factor regulons revealed a pronounced enrichment of Ascl2 (also known as Mash2), an imprinted basic-helix-loop-helix factor with critical roles in placental development (Figure S3D) (Guillemot et al., 1995). Furthermore, examination of these hypomethylated CGIs with the motif discovery tool Homer (Heinz et al., 2010) or ENCODE ChIP-seq data showed a clear enrichment for the chromatin regulator CTCF (Figure 5F; Figure S3D) (Dehingia et al., 2022). These hits suggest that Peg3 deletion causes a loss of DNA methylation at loci critical for overall chromatin organization and architecture (i.e., CTCF-binding) and rewires key transcriptional circuits, such as those regulated by Ascl2.

We then inspected the genes associated with hypomethylated CGIs more closely and identified examples such as Foxe1, a transcription factor-encoding gene tightly linked to the stem cell state of TSCs (Latos et al., 2015), that was consistently more highly expressed in Peg3 KO cells across all time points assessed (Figure 5G). Hypomethylated CGIs were also associated with trophoblast differentiation genes such as placental cadherin Cdh3 and the syncytiotrophoblast (SynT)-enriched Krt7 (Figure 5G) (Marsh and Blelloch, 2020). These epigenetic changes, which correlated with transcriptional deregulation, were corroborated at key sites in an entirely independent TSC line (Figures S3E and S3F). Thus, the loss of DNA methylation at a subset of developmentally informative promoter CGIs caused dysregulation of stem cell- as well as differentiation-associated genes.

Peg3 is required to repress differentiation stage-inappropriate genes

To investigate whether the depletion in epigenetic repressive modifications had a broader impact on gene silencing, we identified genes that were either highly expressed in self-renewal (STEM) conditions but strongly down-regulated upon differentiation of WT TSCs, or conversely that were not or only barely expressed in stem cell conditions but up-regulated upon differentiation. This was accomplished by selecting genes that exhibited ≥16-fold expression differences (Log2FC > 4) between STEM and 2D differentiation conditions in WT TSCs. Plotting the mean expression values of this cohort of genes in WT vs. KO cells revealed that genes normally stably silenced upon differentiation remained more highly expressed in KO cells (Figure 6A). Similarly, genes normally silenced in the stem cell state and only activated upon differentiation were already expressed in STEM conditions in Peg3 KO TSCs (Figure 6B). The same lack of appropriate repression was observed for genes that are down-regulated at later stages of differentiation (Figure S4D).

In line with these overall observations, Peg3 KO TSCs exhibited elevated expression levels of genes associated with TSC function while concomitantly over-expressing JZ trophoblast and/or TGC markers as well as SynT markers (Figure 6C). The lack of appropriate silencing of TSC markers in differentiating cells, as well as the precocious expression of differentiation markers in STEM conditions, was also observed in independent TSC differentiation time course experiments and in an independent TSC line (Figure 6D; Figures S5A and S5B). Re-introducing the Peg3 coding sequence into KO TSCs reversed these expression changes, thus confirming that the transcriptional mis-regulation was indeed caused by loss of PEG3 function (Figure 6E; Figure S6A). These lines of evidence showed that Peg3 KO TSCs simultaneously mis-express markers of stemness and differentiation as they fail to adequately repress differentiation stage-inappropriate genes, thereby uncovering an essential role of PEG3 in fine-tuning trophoblast gene expression via epigenetic gene repression.

Discussion

In the current study, we uncover a novel role for PEG3 in the fine-tuning of transcriptional regulation across trophoblast differentiation, with Peg3 loss causing a general propensity for genes to be insufficiently silenced. This lack of gene repression is in line with the suspected function of PEG3 as a KRAB-ZFP, where the KRAB domain acts to recruit the TRIM28 repressive hub. Accordingly, Peg3 KO TSCs exhibit a loss of DNA methylation and H3K9me3 at numerous developmentally regulated promoter CGIs that correlates with elevated expression levels of the associated genes.

The paradigm KRAB-ZFP that maintains DNA methylation at the majority of germline DMRs (gDMRs) is ZFP57. Overlapping Peg3 KO methylation scars with ZFP57 ChIP-seq peaks and gDMR locations demonstrated only two sites, the Nap1l5/Herc3 locus and the Nhlrc1 gene (Quenneville et al., 2011). Thus, overall, PEG3 does not direct DNA methylation to imprinting control regions. Even at the Peg3 locus itself, the deletion did not significantly interfere with DNA methylation levels at the upstream Peg3 gDMR. Interestingly, it did lead to the demethylation of the downstream promoter CGI associated with the maternally expressed Zim1 gene (Figure S6B). PEG3 zinc finger deletion also caused mis-expression of the adjacent, paternally expressed Usp29, whereas the other imprinted genes in this imprinting cluster (Zim2, Zim3 and Zfp264) are not expressed in TSCs at any stage of differentiation (Figure S6B). Intriguingly, all DE genes at this locus were down-regulated in KOs, suggesting that the deleted region must harbor transcription factor binding sites that positively affect Usp29 and Zim1 expression.

The most significant finding of our study is that Peg3 deletion leads to the loss of DNA methylation despite the continued presence of the maintenance DNA methylation machinery composed of DNMT1 and UHRF1. The loss of DNA methylation in conjunction with reduced levels of the associated repressive H3K9me3 mark in Peg3-mutant TSCs is causally tied to the de-repression of numerous genes, thus demonstrating the likely function of PEG3 as a KRAB-ZFP. While overlap with TRIM28 binding sites in TSCs remains to be established, our findings are corroborated by earlier reports of a transcriptional repressor role of PEG3 and its protein-protein interaction with TRIM28 (He et al., 2016; Ye et al., 2016). PEG3 is not widely known as a KRAB-ZFP, but our findings justify its inclusion in this list of important genome regulators (Corsinotti et al., 2013). Yet, PEG3 is unique amongst this group, as it is itself an imprinted gene. Thus, the precise epigenetic control of Peg3 is important for its parent-of-origin-dependent expression, while fine-tuning PEG3 levels is critical for global epigenome regulation.

Beyond DNA methylation, Peg3 deletion also causes a global reduction of H3K9me3 levels. Globally measurable depletion of 5mC and H3K9me3 suggests a significant loss at constitutive heterochromatin, composed of elements such as centromeric and subtelomeric satellite repeats where these modifications are critical for chromosome integrity and genome stability. The concomitant depletion of 5mC and H3K9me3 likely underlies the increased rates of mitotic errors, “donut-shaped” nuclei, and DNA double-strand breaks in Peg3-mutant cells. Given the phenotypic consequences of homozygous Peg3 deletion in TSCs, it might seem surprising that Peg3 mutant mice are viable (Li et al., 1999), even if their placentas are around 20% smaller (Tunster et al., 2018). However, these in vivo studies were conducted on conceptuses and mice upon paternal transmission of the mutant allele, while the maternally inherited allele was WT. Indeed, some relaxation of the imprinted status of the normally silenced maternal allele has been observed in these circumstances that might attenuate the phenotype to some extent (Tunster et al., 2018). In any case, our findings suggest that PEG3-depleted embryos and mice might be more susceptible to environmental stressors due to their propensity to pre-existing disruptions to the epigenome.

Overall, we find that Peg3 deletion in TSCs results in the relaxation of gene repression, in particular of genes whose expression is dynamically regulated during trophoblast differentiation. Thereby, a concomitant up-regulation of both stem cell- as well as differentiation-associated genes is observed. These findings are highly reminiscent of previous reports of the ectopic expression of placental genes in the brains of Peg3-mutant embryos (Kim et al., 2013). While Peg3 deletion in TSCs did not lead to the activation of lineage-foreign genes, we did see higher expression levels of prominent placental genes, most notably the prolactin gene cluster, even in TSCs grown in stem cell conditions. Thus, PEG3 does indeed appear to be a key regulator of placenta-specific genes, as had been previously suggested (Kim et al., 2013).

Undoubtedly, many genes that are up-regulated in Peg3 KO TSCs do not harbor a DNA- and/or H3K9me3-hypomethylated promoter CGI, and hence are likely transcriptionally dysregulated as a secondary consequence of Peg3 deletion. Along similar lines, a number of pivotal genes are indeed down-regulated in Peg3 KO TSCs, such as Dnmt3l and the highlighted cohort of DNA repair genes. A possible explanation for this latter finding is a double-negative regulation, where a more highly expressed repressor causes such opposing effects on transcription of a select group of genes. Conversely, the up-regulation of a transcriptional activator, or the unimpeded action of TET1, may lead to transcriptional activation at loci not directly regulated by PEG3. One possible candidate for such a transcription factor is Foxe1, a gene we identified among the DE genes associated with a hypomethylated promoter CGI in Peg3 KO TSCs. Although Foxe1 is not currently known for its role in TCS maintenance and trophoblast differentiation, its expression is tightly associated with the stem cell state of TSCs (Latos et al., 2015). In other cell and tissue contexts, FOXE1 expression has been found to be acutely regulated by DNA methylation (Abu-Khudir et al., 2014). Thus, our analysis has uncovered a vital function of PEG3 in the epigenetic control of gene regulation in the trophoblast lineage, while also revealing factors with unexplored roles in trophoblast that may significantly contribute to shaping placenta formation.

Experimental model details

Two independent mouse trophoblast stem cell (TSC) lines were used throughout this manuscript: The majority of experiments were performed in the male mouse blastocyst-derived TSC line TS-Rs26, a kind gift of the Rossant laboratory (Toronto, Canada) (Tanaka et al., 1998). KO generation and key results were confirmed in the in-house generated male TSC line TS-B6. For stem cell maintenance (“STEM conditions”), TSCs were cultured in Full Medium consisting of 20% fetal bovine serum (FBS) (Wisent 098–150), 1mM sodium pyruvate (ThermoFisher Scientific 11360070), 1× Antibiotic-Antimycotic (Wisent 450-115-EL), 55 μM 2-mercaptoethanol (ThermoFisher Scientific 21985023), 37.5 ng/mL bFGF (Cambridge Stem Cell Institute), and 1μg/mL heparin (Sigma H3149) in RPMI 1640 with L-Glutamine (Wisent 350-000-CL), with 70% of the medium pre-conditioned on mouse embryonic fibroblasts. Differentiation experiments were carried out in Base Media only, consisting of the same components minus conditioned medium, bFGF and Heparin. Cells were grown in a standard tissue culture incubator at 37°C and 5% CO2. Trypsinization was carried out with 0.25% Trypsin/EDTA (ThermoFisher Scientific 25200056) at 37°C for about 5 min.

Method details

Peg3 KO generation in TSCs

gRNAs targeting 9 out of 12 zinc finger motifs in Peg3 exon 9 were designed using the http://crispor.tefor.net/interface and cloned into the Cas9.2A.EGFP plasmid (Plasmid #48138 Addgene (Ran et al., 2013)). Transfection was carried out with Lipofectamine 2000 (ThermoFisher Scientific 11668019) reagent. EGFP-positive cells were single cell-sorted into 96-well plates and expanded for KO TSC clone generation. Deletion of the targeted genomic region was confirmed by genotyping PCRs. EGFP-negative single cell-sorted cells from the same transfected TSC pools were used to generate WT control clones. For the TS-Rs26 TSC line, 6 independent single cell-derived WT and KO clones were generated. For cell morphological and growth assessments, all n = 6 WT and n = 6 KO clones were analyzed; for RNA-seq and meDIP-seq, n = 4 WT and n = 3 KO clones were analyzed throughout. Confirmatory experiments in TS-B6 cells were performed in n = 1 WT and KO clone each.

Additional verification of results was performed by re-introducing a Peg3-encoding plasmid into the KO TS-Rs26 clones (n = 4 independent KOs). For this, the 4,713 bp Peg3 open reading frame was cloned into a pCAG-IRES-Blasticidin vector, and sequence-verified. As control, an EGFP-encoding sequence was cloned into the same vector. EGFP- and Peg3-encoding plasmids were transfected into each of the KO clones, and 24h hours after transfection, Blasticidin (InvivoGen ant-bl-05) was added at a concentration of 20μg/mL. Cells were harvested for RNA after 48h of selection. Transfection and selection success was visually confirmed as virtually all EGFP-transfected cells were green.

Immunostaining

For immunostaining of TSCs, cells were routinely fixed for 10 min in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA). For anti-5mC immunostaining, cells were first denatured in 2N HCl for 30 min prior to fixing. PFA-fixed cells were permeabilized for 30 min in PBS, 0.1% Triton X-100. Blocking was performed using PBS, 0.1% Tween 20, 1% bovine serum albumin (PBT/BSA) for 30–60 min at room temperature. Primary antibody incubations were performed for 90 min at room temperature, followed by three washes in PBT/BSA and incubation with the corresponding AlexaFluor-conjugated secondary antibodies (ThermoFisher Scientific) diluted 1:500 in PBT/BSA. Counterstaining was performed with DAPI (4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole). Primary antibodies and dilutions used were: PEG3 1:300 (ThermoFisher PA5-99683), γ−tubulin 1:100 (Abcam ab11316), α-tubulin 1:100 (Cell Signaling Technology 2144S), γ−H2A.X 1:800 (Abam ab26350). H3K9me3 1:200 (Abcam ab8898) and 5-methylcytosine [33D3] 1:200 (EpigenTek A-1014-050). All dilutions were in PBT/BSA.

For staining intensity quantification, images were processed in ImageJ v1.53 to create nuclear masks on the DAPI channel which were then used to measure pixel intensities (arbitrary units) on the corresponding antibody-stained channels. All data were imported into GraphPad Prism v10 for statistical analyses and data plotting.

For immunostaining of E12.5 placentas, placentas recovered from C57BL/6N pregnant females (Charles River, counting the day of the vaginal plug as E0.5) were fixed overnight in 4% PFA, washed in PBS and embedded for cryosectioning. 10μm sections were processed for immunostaining against PEG3 and MCT1 as above. Primary antibody incubations were carried out overnight at 4°C. Primary antibodies and dilutions used were: PEG3 1:200 (ThermoFisher PA5-99683) and MCT1 1:100 (EMD Millipore AB1286-I). Detection was carried out with the appropriate Alexa Fluor 488 or -568 conjugated secondary antibodies (ThermoFisher Scientific) diluted 1:500 in PBT/BSA. Counterstaining was with DAPI.

Proliferation assay

To assess the proliferative capacity of WT and Peg3 KO TSC clones in self-renewal (i.e., STEM) conditions, 25,000 cells per well of a 6-well plate were plated; for the analysis of growth rates in differentiation conditions, 20,000 cells per well of a 6-well plate were used. Cells were counted at the indicated time points using the Logos Biosystems LUNA-II Automated Cell Counter.

Western blotting

WT and KO TSCs grown in 6cm dishes in STEM conditions were washed with PBS and lysed in RIPA buffer (Sigma R0278), and sonicated in a Bioruptor (30 s on/off cycles, two repeats). 50 μg of protein lysate per sample was loaded onto 4–20% gradient mini-PROTEAN TGX pre-cast SDS gels (BioRad 4568094), transferred onto PVDF membrane (Novex Life Technologies LC2002) and blocked with 1% milk powder in Tris-buffered saline containing 0.1% Tween 20 (TBST). Primary antibodies were against H3K9me3 (Abcam ab8898), DNMT3L (Abcam ab194094), SUV39H1 (Cell Signaling Technology 8729S) and SUV39H2 (Abcam ab190870), all used at 1:1000 and detected with appropriate HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies using Pierce ECL Western Blotting Substrate (ThermoFisher 32209) reagent. After stripping, the blot was probed for pan-H3 (Invitrogen PA5-16183) or GAPDH (Cell Signaling Technology 2118S). Quantification of band intensities was performed in ImageJ v1.54p and statistical analysis in GraphPad Prism v10.4.1. All primary blots are shown in Figure S7.

TSC differentiation time course

All WT and Peg3 KO clones were plated in 6-well plates in quadruplicate designated for STEM, 2, 4, and 6 days of differentiation. Cells in the STEM plates were placed in Full Media and collected after 2 days, while cells in the 2, 4, and 6 days of differentiation plates were placed in Base Media. The starting cell density for all clones in STEM conditions and at 2 days of differentiation was 50,000 cells/well, whereas the starting cell density for 4 and 6 days of differentiation was 25,000 cells/well to prevent cells from becoming over-confluent. At the indicated days, cells were collected for RNA isolation.

RNA isolation and RT-qPCR

Total RNA was extracted using TRI reagent (Sigma T9424), and 1–2 μg were used for cDNA synthesis with RevertAid H-Minus reverse transcriptase (ThermoFisher Scientific EP0451). Quantitative (q)PCR was performed using QuantiNova SYBR reagent (Qiagen, 208057) and intron-spanning primer pairs on a Bio-Rad CFX96 or CFX384 thermocycler. All RT-qPCR reactions were performed in technical triplicate. Expression values were normalised to the housekeeping gene Sdha and are displayed relative to vehicle control-treated samples cultured in parallel. All data were imported, plotted and statistically analyzed in Microsoft Excel (v16.100.1) or GraphPad Prism (v10.5). All primers used are listed in Table S2.

RNA-sequencing

RNA sequencing was performed by UCalgary Center for Health Genomics and Informatics, using the NEB Ultra II Directional RNA Library Prep kit and sequencing on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 (50bp paired-end). Read pairs were aligned with STAR (2.6.1a_08–27) to the reference mouse genome (GRCm38/mm10 from Ensembl FTP https://uswest.ensembl.org/info/data/ftp/index.html). Count tables were assembled with htseq-count (bioconda 2018.11) using the reverse strand and non-unanimous settings, along with all other default settings, resulting in 14M–31M gene counts per sample. Further analyses were performed using the SeqMonk program v1.48.1 (https://www.bioinformatics.babraham.ac.uk/projects/seqmonk/) with integrated R (v4.0.3) for DESeq2 analysis (https://www.R-project.org/). Differential expression was calculated using DESeq2 and adjusted for multiple testing correction using the Benjamini-Hochberg method (padj<0.05). Most graphs including heatmaps, PCAs and StarWars plots were generated in SeqMonk. Read counts per million (RPM) were calculated normalized to library size and with merged transcript isoform settings; to avoid negative values after logarithmic transformation, a numerical value of 1 was added to each gene count prior to Log2-transformation to generate Log2(RPM+1) values. Expression values for individual genes were plotted in GraphPad Prism v10.5. Gene enrichment analyses were performed using Enrichr (Kuleshov et al., 2016), ToppGene (Chen et al., 2009), DAVID (Huang da et al., 2009) and the ShinyGO 0.85 web tool (https://bioinformatics.sdstate.edu/go/).

meDIP-sequencing

MeDIP-sequencing was carried out as described previously (Senner et al., 2012, 2020). Briefly, purified genomic DNA was sonicated to yield 150–600 bp fragments, and adaptors ligated using NEB Next DNA Sample Prep Reagent Set 1 (New England Biolabs E7335S, E7645S). Immunoprecipitations were carried out using 1 μg DNA and 2μg anti-5mC antibody (Eurogentec BI-MECY-0100), and 10μL M-280 sheep anti-mouse IgG Dynabeads (ThermoFisher 112-01D). Library generation was carried out at UCalgary’s Center for Health Genomics and Informatics facility. Indexed libraries were sequenced using a 50bp paired-end protocol on an Illumina NovaSeq6000 sequencer. Raw fastq data were trimmed with trim-galore, using default parameters, and mapped to the Mus musculus GRCm38 genome assembly using Bowtie2 v2.2.6 (Langdon, 2015), allowing only a single hit per read and guided by gene models from Ensembl v61. Data analysis was carried out using the Seqmonk program v1.48.1 (https://www.bioinformatics.babraham.ac.uk/projects/seqmonk/).

ChIP-seq

Native ChIP-seq was performed as described previously (Andrews et al., 2023; Tsolova et al., 2025) on approximately 250,000 cells per sample (n = 3 WT and KO clones each), resuspended to a concentration of approximately 5,000 cells/μL in nuclear isolation buffer (Sigma NUC-101). MNase (New England Biolabs M0247S) digestion was carried out at 21°C for 8 min. Digested chromatin was suspended in Complete IP buffer [20 mM Tris–HCl (pH 8), 2 mM EDTA, 150 mM NaCl, 0.1% Triton X-100 (Sigma 93443), 1 ×Protease inhibitor cocktail (Sigma 4693159001), and 1 mM phenylmethanesulfonyl fluoride (PMSF; Sigma P7626-5G)]. Following pre-clearing the chromatin on a mix of protein A/protein G beads (ThermoFisher Scientific 10001D and 10003D), and after 10% was set aside for input, IP was performed overnight rotating at 30 rpm, at 4°C, using 250 ng antibodies against H3K9me3 (Abcam ab8898) and on a subset of samples also for H3K4me3 (Diagenode C15200152). DNA was washed in low salt and then high salt washes, before eluting in ChIP elution buffer (100 mM NaHCO3 and 1% SDS). Pulled-down DNA was used for library generation at UCalgary’s Center for Health Genomics and Informatics facility. Indexed libraries were sequenced using a 50bp paired-end protocol on an Illumina NextSeq2000 sequencer. Raw fastq data were trimmed with trim-galore, using default parameters, and mapped to the Mus musculus GRCm38 (mm10) genome assembly using Bowtie2 v2.2.6. Data quality was assessed using ChIPQC (v1.34.0) (Carroll et al., 2014). Peaks were identified using MACS2 (v2.2.9.1) (Zhang et al., 2008) with q < 0.01. The DiffBind R package (v3.8.0) (Stark and Brown, 2011) was used to find consensus peaks in WT and KO groups and perform differential binding analysis. Consensus peaks were defined as those present in >66% of samples within each group.

COBRA

DNA methylation changes were confirmed by Combined Bisulfite Restriction Analysis (COBRA) in WT and KO TSC clones of the TS-B6 line. In brief, 2 μg of genomic DNA were subjected to bisulfite conversion using the EpiTect Bisulfite Kit (Qiagen 59104). Nested PCRs were performed with primers in CpG-free regions. Equal amounts of PCR products were digested with restriction enzymes AciI (New England Biolabs R0551S), TaqI-V2 (New England Biolabs R0149S) and HinfI (New England Biolabs R0155S) that only cleave DNA that was methylated prior to bisulfite conversion. Digestion products were resolved by gel electrophoresis on 2% agarose gels.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Pairwise comparisons of RT-qPCR data were performed by unpaired two-tailed t test. Multi-comparison graphs were analyzed by two-way ANOVA with Šídák’s multiple comparisons test. The test applied is detailed in the legends to every figure element.
