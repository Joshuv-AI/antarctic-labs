Predictors of Treatment Outcomes in Pediatric Graves’ Disease
Authors: Do Young Shin, Eungu Kang, Hyo-Kyoung Nam, Kee-Hyoung Lee, Young-Jun Rhie
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundGraves’ disease (GD), the most common cause of hyperthyroidism in children, is primarily treated with antithyroid drugs (ATDs). The aim of this study was to evaluate factors that could predict remission, relapse, and the need for persistent high doses of methimazole (MMI) in pediatric GD.MethodsThis was a retrospective study of medical records that included GD diagnosed before 19 years of age from January 2004 to December 2023. Remission was defined as maintaining the euthyroid state for more than 6 months after stopping ATDs. The high-dose group was defined as those receiving MMI doses of more than 5 mg/day at last follow-up, regardless of euthyroid status.ResultsOf the 113 patients (95 girls and 18 boys), 47 (41.6%) achieved remission at a mean of 37.17 ± 29.01 months after treatment. Compared to the non-remission group, the remission group showed significant differences in T3, fT4 and thyroid-stimulating hormone-binding inhibitor immunoglobulin (TBII) at diagnosis (330.92 ± 177.72 vs. 413.75 ± 179.09 ng/dL, P = 0.017; 3.31 ± 1.39 vs. 4.12 ± 1.34 ng/dL, P = 0.002; 15.68 ± 14.37 vs. 23.37 ± 16.61 IU/L, P = 0.012, respectively); time to TBII normalization (25.26 ± 29.88 vs. 45.33 ± 37.17 months, P = 0.003); and ratio of 6-month to initial TBII (54.98 ± 42.04 vs. 87.92 ± 55.94%, P < 0.001). Eleven patients (11/47, 23.4%) experienced relapse at a mean of 17.91 ± 16.81 months after remission. Compared to the non-relapse group, the relapse group was predominantly male (5.6 vs. 45.5%, P < 0.001) and exhibited a higher ratio of 12-month to initial TBII (36.64 ± 33.78 vs. 87.18 ± 78.14%, P = 0.025). In the non-remission group, 38/66 patients (57.6%) received persistent high doses of MMI, with a mean dose of 0.39 ± 0.17 mg/kg/day at the last follow-up. Compared to the low-dose group, the high-dose group showed significant differences in time to T3, fT4 and TBII normalization (1.21 ± 0.69 vs. 2.39 ± 1.76 months, P < 0.001; 1.50 ± 0.84 vs. 2.61 ± 2.47 months, P = 0.013; 7.00 ± 2.61vs. 9.55 ± 5.14 months, P = 0.011, respectively) and ratio of 6-month to initial TBII (61.57 ± 36.96 vs. 107.34 ± 59.90%, P < 0.001). Based on the receiver-operating characteristic curve for the ratio of 6-month to initial TBII, the cut-off value for remission was 62.5%, and the cut-off value for persistent high doses of MMI was 82.5%.ConclusionThyroid hormone and TBII levels at diagnosis, time to thyroid hormone and TBII normalization, and the TBII ratio after ATD treatment can be used to predict remission, relapse, and the persistent need for high-dose MMI in pediatric GD patients.Keywords: Graves’ Disease, Hyperthyroidism, Thyroid-Stimulating Hormone-Binding Inhibitory Immunoglobulin, Antithyroid Drugs, Methimazole

INTRODUCTION

Graves’ disease (GD) is an autoimmune thyroid disorder characterized by hyperthyroidism that results from the activation of the thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH) receptor by thyrotropin receptor antibodies. The diagnosis is based on biochemical hyperthyroidism—suppressed TSH with elevated free thyroxine (fT4) and/or triiodothyronine (T3)—along with clinical features such as diffuse goiter, and ophthalmopathy.1,2 In addition, the presence of TSH receptor antibodies (TRAbs), including TSH-binding inhibitor immunoglobulin (TBII) or TSH receptor-stimulating immunoglobulin (TSI), supports the diagnosis.1,2,3 Thyroid ultrasonography showing a diffusely enlarged gland with increased vascularity may also be helpful in confirming the diagnosis.3 GD is the most common cause of hyperthyroidism in children and adolescents, representing nearly 95% of cases in Korea4 and 10–15% of all pediatric thyroid disease.5 The incidence of GD among children and adolescents varies globally, ranging from 1 to 14 per 100,000 person-years. Compared to adults, it is relatively uncommon, comprising less than 5% of all GD patients.6

In pediatric GD, methimazole (MMI) is used as the first-line antithyroid drug (ATD) to suppress thyroid hormone production. Ablative treatments, such as radioactive iodine (RAI) therapy or thyroidectomy, are considered in cases of persistent recurrence following ATD therapy or when discontinuation of ATD treatment is not possible.1 ATD is effective in managing GD; however, the remission rate with ATD alone remains low, approximately 21–49%.7,8,9,10,11 ATD can cause adverse effects (AEs) ranging from pruritic rash, urticaria, and arthralgia to agranulocytosis, thrombocytopenia, hepatitis, lupus-like syndrome, and long-term hypothyroidism.1,12,13,14

Clinical guidelines regarding the optimal timing for discontinuation of ATD treatment in children and adolescents with GD have not been established. Moreover, predicting the clinical course of GD remains challenging, which hinders patients’ and caregivers’ understanding of the treatment process and may negatively affect treatment adherence.

Therefore, we analyzed pediatric GD patients undergoing ATD therapy to identify factors that could enable the early and accurate prediction of treatment outcomes, including remission, relapse, and the need for persistent high-dose MMI therapy.

METHODS

This retrospective study reviewed the medical records of children and adolescents diagnosed with GD before 19 years of age at the Department of Pediatrics, Korea University Ansan Hospital, between January 2004 and December 2023. Diagnosis of GD was based on medical history, clinical symptoms, physical examination findings (tachycardia, weight loss, tremor, increased sweating, goiter, and ophthalmopathy, among others), thyroid function test and thyroid autoantibody test results. The exclusion criteria were as follows: 1) patients with follow-up periods of less than 6 months, 2) patients who received ATD treatment for more than 6 months at another hospital, 3) patients with other thyroid disorders (e.g., chronic lymphocytic thyroiditis, papillary carcinoma), and 4) patients with incomplete medical records. After applying the exclusion criteria, a total of 113 patients were included in the study (Fig. 1).

Flow chart of the patient cohort in this study on Graves’ disease.

GD = Graves’ disease, ATD = antithyroid drug.

Demographic data, clinical characteristics and biochemical results at diagnosis were collected from medical records, including sex, age at diagnosis, body mass index (BMI) standard deviation score (SDS), symptoms, family history, duration of ATD treatment, ATD dose, AEs during treatment, total T3, fT4, TSH, TBII, anti-thyroglobulin antibodies (TG Abs), anti-thyroperoxidase antibodies (TPO Abs), time to thyroid hormone and TBII normalization, and TBII ratio over the treatment. BMI (kg/m2) was calculated using weight divided by the square of height, and the BMI SDS was determined based on data from the 2017 Korean National Growth Chart.15 The presence or absence of goiter was determined according to the World Health Organization guideliens.16 The concentrations of T3, fT4, TSH, TBII, and TG Ab were measured using a radioimmunoassay (GammaPro, fully automatic gamma counter system, Kaien, Seoul, Korea). TPO Ab was measured using immunoassay (Architect i2000 SR, immuno-chemiluminescent analyzer, Abbott, Abbott Park, IL, USA).

ATD treatment was initiated immediately after diagnosis of GD in all patients. The initial doses of MMI ranged 0.5–1.0 mg/kg/day, (maximum 30 mg/day), while the initial doses of propylthiouracil (PTU) ranged 2.0–7.5 mg/kg/day (maximum 300mg/day). PTU was previously widely utilized as a first-line drug; however, its use has diminished due to the high incidence of hepatotoxicity and its short half-life.14,17,18 Current guidelines suggest PTU use only in cases where MMI cannot be tolerated. Among 113 patients, only 10 patients started treatment with PTU and three patients were temporarily switched from MMI to PTU due to AEs; all 13 patients transitioned to MMI during GD treatment at last. All 10 patients who started treatment with PTU had initiated therapy prior to the year 2009. MMI doses were gradually tapered using a titration regimen and were discontinued when euthyroidism and symptom resolution were maintained at the lowest dose (≤ 5.0 mg/day).

Remission was defined as the maintenance of euthyroid status for more than 6 months after discontinuation of ATD. Remission in GD has been defined at 6 to 24 months after ATD cessation, with 12 months being the most commonly used.1,19 However, we adopted the 6-month criterion, as it is clinically feasible,20,21 allows earlier outcome prediction,22 and is associated with lower relapse rates beyond this point.23,24 Moreover, recent studies on pediatric GD have also used the 6-month criterion,25 supporting our focus on early prediction of treatment outcomes and disease course. Relapse was defined as the resumption of MMI after achieving remission. Non-remission was defined as the failure to achieve remission throughout treatment. Among non-remission patients, low-dose MMI patients were defined as those receiving an MMI dose of 5 mg/day or less at last follow-up regardless of thyroid hormone levels and body weight. High-dose MMI patients were defined as those receiving an MMI dose of more than 5mg/day at last follow-up regardless of thyroid hormone levels and body weight.

Continuous variables are expressed as mean ± standard deviation, while categorical variables are expressed as rates and proportions. Comparisons of continuous variables were conducted using the Mann-Whitney U test (nonparametric test). Comparisons of categorical variables were performed using the χ2 test or the Fisher exact test (for expected frequencies < 5). Kaplan–Meier survival analysis was used to estimate the time to cumulative remission rate. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was performed to determine the optimal cut-off value for the TBII ratio after ATD treatment in predicting prognosis. The optimal cut-off value was identified using the Youden index (J), calculated as the maximum value of [sensitivity + specificity − 1]. Statistical significance was defined as a P value < 0.05.

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS Statistics software, version 20 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

Ethics statement

The study design was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of Korea University Ansan Hospital (K2024-2812-001, IRB No. 2024AS0302), and the requirement for written informed consent was waived due to the retrospective nature of the study.

RESULTS

Baseline characteristics and treatment outcomes

Among the 113 patients, 95 (84.1%) were female and 18 (15.9%) were male. The mean age at diagnosis was 11.74 ± 2.95 years, and the mean BMI SDS was −0.28 ± 1.33. The mean follow-up duration was 59.80 ± 40.70 months, with a mean ATD treatment duration of 42.44 ± 34.62 months. A total of 91 (79.6%) had goiter and 21 (18.6%) had ophthalmopathy. Non-thyroid autoimmune diseases such as insulin-dependent diabetes mellitus or Sjögren’s syndrome were observed in 4 patients (3.5%). A familial history of thyroid disease was reported in 25 patients (22.1%) (Table 1).

Of the 113 patients, 100 were treated exclusively with MMI, while 13 received short-term PTU treatment. Among these, 10 initiated treatment with PTU, including 1 patient hospitalized for a thyroid storm. Two patients switched MMI to PTU due to skin rash and itching during MMI treatment; one patient started with MMI but transitioned to PTU after hospitalization for a thyroid storm. Of the 13 patients who received PTU, six achieved remission (five in the non-relapse group and one in the relapse group), while seven remained in the non-remission group (three in the low-dose group and four in the high-dose group). Currently, none of the 113 patients are receiving PTU treatment. All are either undergoing treatment with MMI or have achieved remission following MMI therapy.

A total of 47 patients (41.6%) achieved remission and discontinued ATD treatment, including 36 (31.9%) in the non-relapse group and 11 (9.7%) in the relapse group. The remaining 66 patients (58.4%) were in the non-remission group, requiring ongoing ATD treatment; among these, 28 (24.8%) were in the low-dose group, and 38 (33.6%) were in the high-dose group (Fig. 1).

Kaplan-Meier survival analysis of the 113 patients treated with ATD estimated cumulative remission rates of 8.4% at 12 months (1 year), 26.1% at 36 months (3 years), 50.6% at 60 months (5 years), 56.6% at 84 months (7 years), and 69.7% at 120 months (10 years) (Fig. 2).

Within the remission group, the mean time to achieve remission was 37.17 ± 29.00 months, while the mean time from remission to relapse was 17.91 ± 16.81 months. 11 patients (11/47, 23.4%) experienced relapse (Table 3). Comparison between the non-relapse and relapse groups revealed that males (5.6 vs. 45.5%, P < 0.001, odds ratio of 14.17 according to the chi-square test) and higher ratio of 12-month to initial TBII (36.64 ± 33.78 vs. 87.18 ± 78.14%, P = 0.025) were associated with higher relapse rates. Within the non-remission group, 38/66 patients (57.6%) received persistent high doses of MMI, with a mean dose of 0.39 ± 0.17 mg/kg/day at last follow-up; in the low-dose group, this value was 0.08 ± 0.03 mg/kg/day. Comparison between the low-dose and high-dose subgroups revealed that a longer time to normalization of T3 (1.21 ± 0.69 vs. 2.39 ± 1.76 months, P < 0.001), fT4 (1.50 ± 0.84 vs. 2.61 ± 2.47 months, P = 0.013), and TSH (7.00 ± 2.61 vs. 9.55 ± 5.14 months, P = 0.011) and higher ratio of 6-month to initial TBII (61.57 ± 36.96 vs. 107.34 ± 59.90%, P < 0.001) was associated with the need for sustained high-dose MMI treatment (Table 3).

Predictors of treatment outcomes and prognosis

To evaluate patient prognosis, we analyzed TBII ratios during treatment relative to initial diagnosis (Fig. 3). Lower TBII reduction rates over time were associated with lower remission rates, higher relapse rates, and the need for continued high-dose MMI treatment. In the high-dose group, TBII values exceeded 100% of the initial diagnosis levels 6 and 12 months after treatment initiation, indicating that TBII levels remained elevated despite treatment. Patients exhibiting an upward trend in TBII between 6 months and 12 months of treatment were more likely to experience future relapse or require sustained high-dose MMI treatment.

TBII ratios relative to initial diagnosis over the treatment period.

TBII = thyroid-stimulating hormone-binding inhibitor immunoglobulin.

According to ROC curve analysis, a TBII ratio 62.5% after 6 months of ATD treatment was identified as the optimal cut-off value for predicting remission with an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.692 (95% confidence interval [CI], 0.589–0.794; P < 0.001), a sensitivity of 71.2%, and a specificity of 66.0% (Fig. 4). This indicates that a TBII level of > 62.5% of the initial diagnosis value—equivalent to a reduction rate of < 37.5%—at 6 months predicts failure to achieve remission with the stated confidence.

Receiver-operating characteristic curve of the thyroid-stimulating hormone-binding inhibitor immunoglobulin ratio at 6 months of treatment in non-remission group.

Similarly, when predicting the need for persistent high-dose MMI treatment, the optimal cut-off value for the TBII ratio was 82.5% after 6 months of treatment with an AUC of 0.791 (95% CI, 0.709–0.872; P < 0.001), a sensitivity of 71.1%, and a specificity of 69.3% (Fig. 5). This indicates that a TBII level of > 82.5% of the initial diagnosis value—equivalent to a reduction rate of < 17.5%—at 6 months predicts progression to persistent high-dose MMI therapy with the stated confidence.

Side effects of ATD

A total of 20 (20/113, 17.7%) patients experienced AEs during ATD treatment (Table 4). The most common AEs were skin reactions, including urticaria and rash (45%), followed by abnormal liver function test (30%), complete blood count abnormalities such as neutropenia (10%), arthralgia (5%), myalgia (5%), and alopecia (5%). All symptoms resolved with temporary discontinuation of the medication or symptom treatment. Most AEs occurred within the first 3 months (15/20, 75%) of treatment, and their incidence did not significantly increase with prolonged treatment duration. Among the 13 patients who took PTU even for a short period, AEs occurred in 3 cases (3/13, 23.1%) during PTU administration. Of these, 2 patients experienced skin rash and pruritus, prompting a switch to MMI. One patient exhibited elevated liver enzyme levels (aspartate transaminase, 165 IU/L; alanine aminotransferase, 230 IU/L) and decreased white blood cell count (3,140/µL) with an absolute neutrophil count of 1,796/µL, leading to a transition to MMI treatment.

DISCUSSION

This retrospective study examined the outcomes of ATD treatment in children and adolescents with GD at a single tertiary hospital in Korea. In our cohort, the remission rate with ATD treatment alone was 41.6% during the follow-up period. Reported remission rates for pediatric GD generally range from 21% to 49%,7,8,9,10 and vary based on the ATD regimen or the definition of the term remission. Remission rates tend to increase with longer ATD treatment duration, reaching up to 50% in studies spanning about 10 years or more. Azizi et al.26 reported that long-term MMI treatment for a mean of 9.1 years is a safer and more effective treatment option than short-term MMI treatment in a randomized, parallel-group trial of 66 adolescent patients with GD. In an ongoing study, 59 patients underwent very long-term ATD treatment with low-dose MMI for an average of 14.2 years, and most had euthyroidism without significant MMI-related AEs.27 However, rates appear to plateau thereafter, as noted in several studies.8,9 The Kaplan-Meier survival curve in our study showed cumulative remission rates of 8.4% at 1 year, 26.1% at 3 years, 50.6% at 5 years, 56.6% at 7 years, and 69.7% at 10 years after initiating ATD treatment. However, remission rates showed minimal improvement beyond 10 years, likely due to the limited number of patients with follow-up periods exceeding that. The Kaplan–Meier curve in this study reflects the cumulative estimated probability of achieving first remission, not actual sustained remission or relapse-free survival. This approach is consistent with previous studies and should be interpreted as a statistical estimation over time.

Lower levels of T3, fT4, and TBII at diagnosis, shorter time to TBII normalization were associated with improved remission outcomes in pediatric GD patients. A longer time to normalization of T3, fT4, and TSH was associated with the need for sustained high-dose MMI treatment. Lower TBII reduction rates were linked to decreased likelihood of remission, increased risk of relapse, and higher probability of prolonged high-dose MMI therapy. Our findings align with previous studies. In a retrospective study of 64 pediatric GD patients in Korea by Lee et al.,28 rapid achievement of TBII normalization can be a predictor of remission in children with GD. Normalization of TBII after treatment was faster in the remission group than in the non-remission group (remission group, 15.5 ± 12.1 vs. non-remission group, 41.7 ± 35.7 months). In addition, the authors proposed that the TRH stimulation test may be useful in predicting the maintenance of remission following ATD withdrawal. Rho et al.29 reported that both TBII levels and the time to TBII normalization after ATD initiation were significantly associated with remission outcomes in pediatric GD patients. When considering whether ATD should be discontinued, TRAb levels can help predict the risk of relapse.30 Persistently elevated TRAb titers are associated with a low probability of sustained remission. A prospective multicenter study of 154 children revealed that higher TRAb levels and elevated fT4 at diagnosis, shorter duration of initial euthyroid status, non-Caucasian ethnicity, and younger age were significantly associated with relapse.31 According to previous studies, the likelihood of achieving remission is higher in patients diagnosed at an older age,31,32 of Caucasian ethnicity,31 who are female,33 with smaller goiter size,10 who exhibit mild biochemical derangement (low T3, fT4) at diagnosis,8 who undergo a longer duration of ATD treatment,8,25,34,35 who present with lower TBII levels at diagnosis,1,29,31 and who achieve TBII normalization more quickly following ATD treatment.29,31

Based on the ROC curve cut-off values identified in this study, a 6-month to initial TBII ratio > 62.5% was predictive of failure to achieve remission, and a ratio > 82.5% indicated a high likelihood of requiring prolonged high-dose MMI therapy. These results are clinically significant in identifying patients with persistent disease activity or poor response to ATD, suggesting the need for prolonged or ablative therapy. In such cases, patients should be informed of the anticipated outcome and prognosis. Importantly, they enable early prediction—at 6 months—of those unlikely to achieve remission or likely to require sustained high-dose MMI treatment.

Previous studies in adults have shown that a more rapid initial decline in TSI titer correlates with a higher likelihood of remission and a lower risk of relapse.36,37 However, the clinical utility of TSI is limited due to the technical challenges associated with its measurement and the difficulty of performing the test in routine laboratory settings. In contrast, although TBII is involved in stimulating, neutral, and blocking activities, it is widely used in clinical practice due to its relative ease of measurement and cost-effectiveness. Our study demonstrated that TBII reduction ratio is a reliable predictor of remission and the ability to maintain low-dose MMI therapy.

Although this study was conducted at a single institution over a 20-year period, it was retrospective in nature, and a clearly defined standard treatment protocol was not pre-established. However, upon reviewing the treatment courses, we found that most patients were treated with similar initial doses and tapering strategies, suggesting a relatively consistent therapeutic approach that was implicitly agreed upon among pediatric endocrinologists at our institution. In our study, MMI was initiated at 0.5–1.0 mg/kg/day based on thyroid level at diagnosis, then gradually tapered following a titration regimen. Treatment was discontinued when euthyroidism and symptom resolution were maintained at the lowest dose (≤ 5.0 mg/day) in our study. In some patients who continued long-term low-dose MMI therapy, the decision to maintain treatment was influenced by persistently low-positive TBII levels (≥ 1.5 IU/L) in combination with additional clinical or psychosocial factors, such as anxiety about relapse, refusal of definitive treatments (e.g., radioactive iodine therapy or surgery), and personal circumstances (e.g., school entrance exams, employment, or marriage). These individualized and overlapping factors reflect the real-world complexity of managing pediatric GD and may have affected the treatment duration and outcomes in a subset of patients. At last follow-up, a total of 66 patients in the non-remission group continued to receive MMI at doses ranging from 0.08 ± 0.03 mg/kg/day to 0.39 ± 0.17 mg/kg/day.

The prevalence of ATD-related AEs was 17.7% (20/113). Most AEs occurred within the first 3 months (15/20, 75%) of treatment, and their severity and incidence did not significantly increase with prolonged treatment duration. This finding was consistent with the results of most previous studies; the prevalence of MMI-related AEs in children and adolescents with GD tends to vary (6–35%), with fatal AEs being rare and primarily occurring within the first 3 months.26,38,39 However, several previous studies reported conflicting conclusions. Specifically, some studies reported that ATD-related AEs were more frequent and severe with higher doses and prolonged treatment duration.1,40,41,42 In a previous study involving 56 pediatric patients with hyperthyroidism, the incidence of AEs was significantly higher in patients receiving high-dose MMI (≥ 0.7mg/kg/day) compared to those receiving low-dose MMI (< 0.7 mg/kg/day; 50% vs. 20%, respectively).40 High-dose treatment was associated with a greater occurrence of neutropenia and skin rash.40 In our study, patients in the high-dose group continued MMI treatment without discontinuation at doses of 0.39 ± 0.17 mg/kg/day up to a maximum of 30 mg/day or 0.68 mg/kg/day. Prolonged treatment at such doses may increase the severity and frequency of AEs. Thus, the relationship between ATD dosage, treatment duration, and AEs remains controversial, with varying conclusions reported across different studies.1

The remission rate in pediatric GD is lower compared to that in adults.7,8,9,10,11 Consequently, frequent relapses, prolonged treatment duration, and the need for modification of therapeutic approach can impose a substantial burden on both the patients and their caregivers.43,44 Early prediction of treatment outcomes can assist patients and healthcare providers in making informed decisions regarding treatment options, such as continuing medication or transitioning to ablative therapy. It considers the probability of remission, the risk of recurrence, and the potential for AEs resulting from long-term high-dose MMI therapy. This approach would also improve patient understanding of the treatment process and contribute to maintaining higher quality of life.

The key strength of our study was its evaluation of long-term MMI treatment outcomes over an average follow-up duration of 59.80 ± 40.70 months (4.98 ± 3.39 years) for over 20 years at a single institution. There are few studies with a mean duration of ATD treatment of 3.5 years or more in children and adolescents with GD.8,26,40,45 In our study, patients received relatively long-term MMI treatment for a mean of 42.44 ± 34.62 months (3.54 ± 2.89 years) and the remission rate increased according to the duration of MMI treatment in the Kaplan-Meier curve. As a retrospective study, a major strength is the long-term follow-up of a substantial number of pediatric GD patients. Moreover, this study identifies quantitative indicators such as TBII levels and their reduction ratio during treatment as predictors of treatment response, which may aid in early clinical decision-making. The use of ROC analysis further strengthens the clinical utility of the findings by providing practical cut-off values.

There are some limitations to this study. Ophthalmopathy was identified based solely on the presence of eyelid edema or proptosis described in retrospective medical records. As a result, standardized grading systems such as the NOSPECS classification or the European Group on Graves’ Orbitopathy (EUGOGO) criteria were not applied. Among our 113 patients, 100 received MMI exclusively, while 13 received short-term PTU treatment. Although PTU was administered temporarily, all patients were either undergoing treatment with MMI or have achieved remission following MMI therapy. However, the use of different therapeutic agents may have introduced bias, and because of the small number of patients affected, its impact on overall treatment outcomes and prognosis could not be thoroughly assessed. TSI measurement was not available at our institution due to technical constraints and challenges in implementing the assay in routine clinical laboratory settings. As a result, assessment of stimulating antibody activity was limited.

In conclusion, thyroid hormone and TBII levels at diagnosis, the time to normalization of these markers, and the rate of TBII reduction are meaningful predictors of remission and relapse in pediatric GD patients. Among these, The TBII reduction rate at 6 months after treatment initiation demonstrated strong predictive value for the early identification of patients who are unlikely to achieve remission or who may require long-term high-dose MMI therapy. This suggests that early monitoring of TBII can assist in stratifying patients by treatment responsiveness and tailoring individualized treatment strategies. While extended ATD therapy increases cumulative remission rates, prolonged high-dose MMI use raises concerns about treatment resistance and the psychological burden on patients and families. If such a course is anticipated early, clinicians should proactively counsel patients and caregivers regarding the potential need for definitive treatments such as radioactive iodine therapy or surgery, and support them in preparing both practically and psychologically. This study highlights the potential utility of early TBII monitoring in guiding long-term management decisions. Further prospective studies are warranted to validate these findings and to establish safe and effective treatment durations for children and adolescents with GD. Additionally, future research should investigate the clinical utility of TSI and other potential biomarkers for predicting remission, relapse, and the need for sustained ATD therapy.
