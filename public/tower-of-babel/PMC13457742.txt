Early Childhood Caries and Oral Hygiene Practices in Preschool Children in Dubai: A Cross-sectional Study
Authors: Noura Bin Hussain, Anas Salami, Manal Al Halabi, Iyad Hussein, Amar Khamis Hassan, Mawlood Kowash
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimThis study aims to assess the oral health status of preschool children in Dubai, United Arab Emirates, with a specific focus on their brushing habits, the prevalence of dental caries, and gingival health.Materials and methodsA cross-sectional observational study was conducted among children under 6 years of age attending pediatric dental clinics at Dubai Academic Health Corporation (DAHC). Clinical examinations were performed using the International Caries Detection and Assessment System (ICDAS), oral debris index (ODI), gingival index (GI), and calculus index (CI) to evaluate oral hygiene and gingival condition. A structured questionnaire was administered to parents or guardians to gather data on brushing habits, including supervision status, frequency, and technique.ResultsA total of 145 children were screened (51% male; mean age 4.06 ± 0.88 years). Extensive dental caries was prevalent, with most children presenting ICDAS scores equivalent to ICDAS codes 5 and 6. Treatments commonly included preformed metal crowns (PMCs) and extractions. Gingival health was generally mild, with a mean GI score of 0.19, while the mean ODI score of 1.05 indicated fair oral hygiene. Brushing supervision was common, primarily by mothers, though unsupervised brushing was also reported. Children who were regularly supervised during brushing tended to show better oral hygiene and lower gingival inflammation.ConclusionPreschool children in Dubai exhibit a high prevalence of dental caries and mild gingival inflammation. Brushing habits, particularly the presence of supervision, play a crucial role in maintaining oral hygiene. These findings highlight the need for targeted oral health education and preventive strategies to improve brushing practices and reduce caries risk in early childhood.Keywords: Early childhood caries, Gingivitis, Oral hygiene, Parent education, Preschool children, Toothbrushing

Introduction

Early childhood caries (ECC) is among the most prevalent oral health challenges affecting preschool-aged children worldwide.1 Despite advancements in dental care accessibility, ECC continues to pose a significant public health concern, particularly in the Middle East. In the United Arab Emirates (UAE), the prevalence of ECC among children aged 4–5 years ranges from 74.1% to 83%, with decayed, missing, and filled teeth (dmft) scores reported between 3.07 and 10.9, even though free dental services are available to citizens.2–4

Dental caries is a multifactorial disease characterized by an imbalance between enamel demineralization and remineralization. ECC is defined as the presence of one or more decayed (noncavitated or cavitated lesions), missing (due to caries), or filled tooth surfaces in any primary tooth in a child younger than 72 months.5 “Severe early childhood caries (S-ECC) is further classified as: (1) any sign of smooth-surface caries in a child younger than 3 years; (2) for children aged 3–5 years, one or more cavitated, missing (due to caries), or filled smooth surfaces in primary maxillary anterior teeth; or (3) a dmft score of ≥4 (age 3), ≥5 (age 4), or ≥6 (age 5).5”

Dietary habits, particularly frequent sugar intake, play a critical role in shaping the composition of dental plaque—a biofilm primarily composed of bacteria that adheres to tooth surfaces.6 Cariogenic bacteria, such as Streptococcus mutans metabolize sugars to produce acids, which initiate enamel demineralization.7,8 The earliest clinical manifestation of this process is a chalky white lesion, which may progress to cavitation with continued acid exposure.9

Plaque accumulation also contributes to gingival inflammation, commonly observed in plaque-induced gingivitis. This condition affects both the free and attached gingiva and is reversible with proper oral hygiene.10

Early childhood caries can lead to pain, discomfort, and nutritional deficiencies, ultimately affecting a child's overall well-being and quality of life.11

To mitigate ECC and maintain gingival health, the American Academy of Pediatric Dentistry (AAPD) recommends twice-daily brushing with fluoridated toothpaste under parental supervision until the child develops adequate manual dexterity, typically around age seven.12 Supervised brushing ensures correct technique, appropriate toothpaste usage, and minimizes the risk of ingestion. A Cochrane systematic review demonstrated that parental supervision during brushing significantly reduces caries incidence compared to unsupervised brushing.13

Given the strong association between plaque, caries, and gingival inflammation, effective toothbrushing under supervision is essential for maintaining oral health in young children. However, limited data are available in the UAE regarding the influence of brushing habits, particularly supervision, on oral health outcomes in preschool-aged children. This study aims to evaluate the oral health status of preschool children in Dubai, UAE, with a focus on brushing practices, dental caries and its risk factors, and gingival health.

The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and Good Clinical Practice (GCP) guidelines. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. Ethical approval was granted by the Research Ethics Review Committee at Mohammed Bin Rashid University of Medicine and Health Sciences (MBRU IRB-2021-88) and the Dubai Scientific Research Ethics Committee (DSREC-SR-06/2022_05).

Materials and Methods

This prospectively study included preschool children under 6 years and their parents/legal guardians attending pediatric dental clinics at Dubai Academic Health Corporation (DAHC). Conducted in accordance with STROBE guidelines,14 clinical assessments comprised dmft, ICDAS, ODI, GI, and CI indices. Data collection occurred from July 3, 2022 to February 28, 2023. Eligible children were healthy, in the primary dentition stage, cooperative during examination, and classified as ASA I or II.15

Following the clinical examination, a structured questionnaire was administered to parents. This questionnaire was adapted from the National Diet and Nutrition Survey for UK preschool children16 and had been previously used in an earlier publication.3 It comprised four sections: demographics, oral health practices, oral health knowledge, and oral health attitudes, totaling 36 questions. Responses were formatted as multiple-choice and Likert-scale items.

To minimize bias, clinical examinations were conducted prior to questionnaire completion. A tooth was considered present if any part was visible through the oral mucosa.

Oral hygiene was assessed using the oral debris index (ODI) on six primary teeth (55, 61, 64, 75, 81, 84) following Green and Vermillion's criteria.17 Debris scores ranged from 0 (no debris) to 3 (debris covering most of the tooth surface). The average score was interpreted as excellent (0), good (0.1–0.9), fair (1–1.9), or poor (2–3). Calculus presence was recorded using Ramfjord's criteria. Gingival health was evaluated using the gingival index (GI) by Löe and Silness18 on the six Ramfjord teeth.19 GI scores ranged from 0 (healthy) to 3 (severe inflammation), and the mean GI was categorized as healthy (0), mild gingivitis (0.1–1), moderate gingivitis (1.1–2), or severe gingivitis (2.1–3). Before ICDAS scoring, all teeth were cleaned and dried. A ball-ended explorer was used to remove residual plaque and assess surface integrity. ICDAS codes were applied to classify caries severity based on lesion progression.20

A pilot study with 15 children was conducted prior to the main investigation to assess feasibility, refine the study protocol, and identify potential challenges. All clinical examinations were performed by the principal investigator using a mouth mirror and dental unit light. The investigator underwent training and calibration in ICDAS, GI, and ODI scoring systems. Reliability was evaluated using Kappa statistics, yielding intraexaminer agreement of 0.825 and interexaminer agreement of 0.70 for ICDAS. For GI and ODI, interexaminer agreement values were 0.75 and 0.74, respectively, indicating substantial reliability.

Data were analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 28.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL). Descriptive statistics were used for both categorical and continuous variables. Chi-square or Fisher's exact tests were applied to assess associations between categorical variables. Mann–Whitney U and Kruskal–Wallis H tests were used for nonparametric comparisons. A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

The study complied with the Declaration of Helsinki and Good Clinical Practice (GCP) guidelines. Written informed consent was obtained, and ethical approval was granted by the MBRU Research Ethics Committee (MBRU IRB-2021–88) and the Dubai Scientific Research Ethics Committee (DSREC-SR-06/2022_05).

Regarding brushing habits, 78.6% of parents reported that their children brushed their teeth at least once daily, while only 42.1% confirmed twice-daily brushing. Parental supervision during brushing was reported in 65.5% of cases, and 58.6% of children used fluoridated toothpaste. Despite these practices, only 31.7% of children had ever visited a dentist, with the most common reason for the visit being pain or visible decay.

Early childhood caries level was notably high, with 71.7% of children presenting with at least one decayed, missing, or filled tooth. The mean dmft score was 3.9 (SD ± 3.6), indicating a substantial burden of untreated caries. ICDAS scoring revealed that 46.2% of children had advanced carious lesions (codes 4–6), while 25.5% exhibited early enamel lesions (codes 1 and 2). Only 28.3% of children were caries-free. Caries prevalence was significantly associated with brushing frequency (p = 0.014), parental supervision (p = 0.021), and use of fluoridated toothpaste (p = 0.033), suggesting that oral hygiene practices play a critical role in caries prevention.

Discussion

This study underscores the persistent challenges in promoting optimal oral health among preschool children in Dubai, particularly in relation to brushing habits, caries prevalence, and gingival health. While most parents reported that their children brushed at least once daily, only 42.1% adhered to the recommended twice-daily routine. Furthermore, parental supervision during brushing was reported in just 65.5% of cases, a factor shown to significantly influence oral hygiene outcomes.

These findings highlight the critical role of parental involvement in establishing effective oral hygiene practices during early childhood, when children lack the manual dexterity and cognitive awareness to clean their teeth adequately.21,22

The level and severity of ECC in this cohort were notably high, with 71.7% of children presenting with at least one decayed, missing, or filled tooth, and a mean dmft score of 3.9. These results align with previous studies conducted in the UAE and neighboring regions, where ECC continues to be a major public health concern.23,24

The high proportion of advanced carious lesions (ICDAS codes 4–6) suggests delayed diagnosis and limited access to preventive care. A recent study reported that 15.7% of preschool children exhibited ICDAS codes 5, 6, indicating cavitated dentine lesions. The authors emphasized the importance of early intervention to prevent progression from enamel demineralization to cavitation and noted that higher ICDAS scores were associated with increased caries activity.25

Alarmingly, only 31.7% of children had ever visited a dentist, and most visits were prompted by pain or visible decay rather than routine check-ups. This reflects a reactive rather than preventive approach to oral health. Similar patterns have been observed in other regions; for example, an Egyptian study found that 63% of children visited the dentist for the first time due to pain, despite recommendations for early dental visits.26 The American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP) also advocates for preventive dental visits beginning by age one, yet many children still access care only when symptomatic.27 Another ECC caries risk factor that might have contributed to the high level and severity of ECC in this study was the high consumption of sweets and bottle feeding. A finding that aligns with that reported by other studies in Emirati preschool children.3,4

Gingival health assessments revealed that 39.3% of children had mild gingivitis and 21.4% had moderate inflammation, with a mean GI score of 0.9. These findings indicate that gingival disease is common even in early childhood and is significantly associated with brushing frequency and oral hygiene status. Likewise, a recent study by Olczak-Kowalczyk et al. involving 3,558 children found that 12.25% had gingivitis, with strong correlations between gingival inflammation and poor brushing habits, caries experience, and socioeconomic factors.28 ODI scores further supported these findings, with over 60% of children exhibiting fair-to-poor hygiene. This emphasizes the need for improved brushing techniques and enhanced caregiver education. Recent studies have similarly recommended targeted interventions to improve oral hygiene in young children.29,30

The use of fluoridated toothpaste was associated with lower caries prevalence, reinforcing its role in caries prevention.31 However, only 58.6% of children in this study used fluoridated toothpaste, indicating gaps in parental awareness or access. Educational initiatives aimed at caregivers could help bridge this gap and promote evidence-based oral hygiene practices.32

This study has several strengths. The use of the ICDAS system allowed for a detailed and standardized assessment of caries severity, and examiner reliability was substantial for both caries and gingival indices. Additionally, the use of a comprehensive, prepiloted, and validated questionnaire enhanced the consistency and quality of data collection. Importantly, this study provides foundational data that can inform future large-scale research and support the development of community-specific oral health strategies in Dubai. Such strategies could guide preventive programs, interceptive care, and treatment planning tailored to the needs of local children.

Nonetheless, the study has limitations. The relatively small sample size and predominance of UAE nationals may limit the generalizability of findings to Dubai's diverse, multiethnic population. Moreover, the nonrandom selection of participants from DAHC clinics introduces potential selection bias. As with other survey-based studies, response bias may have influenced the accuracy of self-reported data. Despite these limitations, the study offers valuable insights into the oral health status of preschool children in Dubai and identifies key areas for intervention.

Future research employing broader sampling and longitudinal designs will be essential to validate these findings and support the development of targeted oral health policies and programs.

Conclusion

This study highlights a concerning prevalence of advanced dental caries and its risk factors and gingival inflammation among preschool children in Dubai, with limited impact observed from supervised brushing alone. The findings emphasize the critical role of parents in shaping children's oral health. To address these challenges, early intervention through parental education, integration of oral health guidance into maternal care, and reinforcement of proper hygiene practices are essential. Future research with broader, more diverse samples is recommended to support the development of targeted, Dubai-specific oral health policies and preventive programs.
