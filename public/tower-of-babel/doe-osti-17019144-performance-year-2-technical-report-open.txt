Executive Summary

This report presents the Year 2 technical progress of the OPEN COG Grid (Open Extend-
able Coherent Models-Datasets for Cognitive Power Grids) project, a collaborative effort
between Lawrence Livermore National Laboratory (LLNL), Texas A&M University, Harvard
University, and the National Renewable Energy Laboratory (NREL). The project aims to
develop synthetic power system datasets that are accessible to the broader data sciences
community and provide ready-to-use simulation datasets for training artificial intelligence
models in power system applications.
 During Year 2, the project addressed critical challenges in modeling and forecasting
emerging load classes, developed new methodologies for large-scale dataset generation, and
released open-source software tools to facilitate access by the research community.
 The first major contribution addresses the forecasting challenges posed by cryptocur-
rency mining facilities—highly flexible loads that respond to both electricity prices and
cryptocurrency market conditions. We developed a transformer-based deep learning model
for probabilistic forecasting of cryptocurrency mining power consumption. The model com-
bines autoregressive transformer architecture with Temporal Fusion Transformer components
to perform multi-horizon quantile forecasting, providing a measure of uncertainty quantifi-
cation. The architecture is specifically designed to capture complex interactions between
historical load patterns, weather conditions, energy market prices, regional grid load fore-
casts, and cryptocurrency hash prices—enabling risk-aware decision-making for both grid
operators and mining facility managers.
 Building on this forecasting capability, we conducted a rigorous causal analysis of bitcoin
miner curtailment behavior in Texas. Using ERCOT’s Four Coincident Peak (4CP) trans-
mission pricing mechanism as a natural experiment, we causally estimated miners’ price
elasticity and examined how cryptocurrency market incentives interact with grid signals.
The analysis revealed that miners reduce energy consumption by approximately 20% during
summer peak hours (11 AM to 7 PM). More significantly, we found that miners’ electricity
price elasticity is strongly modulated by Bitcoin profitability: the effective elasticity ranges
from -0.42 at low hash prices (moderately elastic) to -0.04 at high hash prices (nearly in-
elastic). These findings demonstrate that cryptocurrency mining represents a new class
of financially coupled loads whose behavior cannot be adequately captured by traditional
price-responsive load models, and suggest the need for new electricity market paradigms
that account for cross-market dependencies.
 The third major contribution developed an open, machine-learning-oriented dynamic
simulation dataset constructed from the Texas 2000-bus synthetic system. This dataset ad-

dresses a critical gap in power system research: the lack of large-scale, realistic, and openly
accessible dynamic time-series data for training ML models. We converted and refined the
Texas 2000-bus system for use in the ANDES open-source simulation platform, developed
a scalable high-performance computing workflow using embarrassingly parallel MPI-based
execution, and generated tens of thousands of contingency-based time-domain simulations
across multiple operating conditions. The simulations consumed 44,820 core-hours of com-
pute time and produced 29.9 TB of raw data. To make this massive dataset accessible,
we developed a novel Transient Stability Index (TSI)-based balanced sampling method that
preserves the distribution of system behaviors while creating manageable dataset sizes (re-
ducing to 80 GB for a 250-event sample per operating condition). The balanced sampling
approach uses kernel density estimation and inverse-density weighting to ensure represen-
tative coverage across the feature space rather than over-sampling trivial events (from a
power systems dynamics perspective).
 To encourage adoption and following the aims of the project of accessible power grid
data, we released three open-source software packages and two open datasets, all with com-
prehensive documentation. The psdatagen package provides the complete implementation
of the methodology and workflow for generating ML-ready dynamic simulation datasets. The
crypto-miner-forecast package implements the Transformer-based forecasting model
with data preprocessing utilities, training scripts, and inference tools. The dr-decoder
package provides a Conditional Variational Autoencoder decoder for generating synthetic
residential demand response data. The augmented Texas 7000-bus system dataset provides
a large-scale synthetic system in Common Transmission Model format with infrastructure
data and comprehensive time series for renewable generation and loads. The Texas 2000-bus
dynamic simulation dataset provides the ANDES-ready implementation of the Texas2k sys-
tem with models, simulation code, and documentation. Together, these software packages
and datasets provide the research community with both the tools and base system models
necessary to generate new results as research questions evolve.
 The work presented in this report conveys the team effort to lowering barriers to power
system research by providing openly accessible datasets, validated simulation workflows, and
practical machine learning tools. The integration of large-scale synthetic system models,
open-source simulation frameworks, and advanced ML methods positions the OPEN COG
Grid project as a foundational resource for the next generation of AI-enabled power system
applications.

Chapter 1

Introduction

The OPEN COG Grid (Open Extendable Coherent Models-Datasets for Cognitive Power
Grids) project addresses a fundamental challenge in applying artificial intelligence and ma-
chine learning methods to power system applications: the lack of large-scale, realistic, and
openly accessible datasets suitable for training and evaluating advanced models across do-
mains. While machine learning has emerged as a potential enabler for applications ranging
from transient stability assessment to learning-based control and secure operational plan-
ning, progress has been constrained by limited access to high-quality dynamic time-series
data that capture the complexity and diversity of modern power system behavior.
 This challenge stems from multiple sources. Most realistic datasets developed by utilities
and manufacturers remain proprietary due to confidentiality requirements, preventing their
release for public research. Meanwhile, widely used public datasets such as IEEE benchmark
test systems are limited in scale and often lack detailed representations of system com-
ponents. Furthermore, most commercial simulation tools are closed-source, require paid
licenses, and are not designed for the high-performance computing workflows necessary to
generate diverse large-scale simulation datasets.
 The OPEN COG Grid project addresses these barriers through a coordinated effort
spanning dataset generation, load modeling and forecasting, and software tool development.
This Year 2 Technical Report presents four major technical contributions that collectively
advance the state of the art in power system datasets and analytical tools:

 • Development of a Transformer-based forecasting model for cryptocurrency mining
 loads, addressing the unique challenges posed by financially coupled loads that respond
 to both electricity market conditions and external commodity prices.

 • Causal analysis of bitcoin miner curtailment behavior using quasi-experimental meth-
 ods, revealing that traditional price-responsive load models are inadequate for this
 emerging class of flexible demand and demonstrating the need for multi-market mod-
 eling approaches.

 • Creation of an open, ML-ready dynamic simulation dataset from the Texas 2000-
 bus system, generated through large-scale HPC workflows and curated using novel

 balanced sampling methods to ensure representative coverage of system behaviors
 while maintaining manageable dataset sizes.

 • Release of three open-source software packages and two open datasets providing
 tools for dataset generation (psdatagen), cryptocurrency mining load forecasting
 (crypto-miner-forecast), residential demand response data synthesis (dr-decoder),
 a large-scale synthetic system in Common Transmission Model format (Texas 7000-
 bus), and ANDES-ready system models (Texas 2000-bus).

2.1 Introduction
In recent years, Texas has emerged as a global hotspot for large-scale cryptocurrency mining,
fueled by abundant wind and solar generation, competitive wholesale electricity markets, and
a regulatory environment receptive to flexible industrial loads. This rapid influx of mining
capacity has created a significant new class of demand in the ERCOT system—one whose
operational patterns interact in intricate ways with electricity prices, ambient temperatures,
and grid reliability events.
 Early econometric analysis [1] revealed a challenge: conventional regression methods
struggled to disentangle the influence of these drivers. Strong correlations between mar-
ket and environmental variables caused them to mask each other’s effects, obscuring the
miners’ intrinsic operational cycles. To overcome this, we adopted a heuristic time-based
segmentation strategy, identifying “signal-rich” intervals where miners’ responses to exoge-
nous factors were most visible—such as 4CP designation hours or extreme real-time price
spikes—and separating these from baseline operational periods.
 As shown in Fig. 2.1, our model showed strong autocorrelation structure. There-
fore, within the baseline windows, the residual load series—purged of identifiable exogenous
shocks—was modeled using autoregressive structures to capture the endogenous temporal
patterns in miner consumption. This revealed distinct seasonal regimes. In the summer
months, the optimal fit was obtained with an ARIMA(1, 0, 0)(1, 1, 1)[24] process, showing
strong short-term persistence (ϕ1 = 0.84), a weaker seasonal AR term (Φ1 = −0.09), and
a strong seasonal moving-average term (Θ1 = −0.93), effectively capturing short-lived daily
shocks. Including these autoregressive dynamics improved the model’s explanatory power
from R2 = 0.93 to 0.99, nearly eliminating residual variance.

 AR-X structure parallels the Transformer architecture of Vaswani et al. [2]. As shown
in Fig. 2.2, in the Transformer’s encoder–decoder framework, the encoder processes ex-
ogenous inputs—e.g., historical market prices, temperature, ERCOT-wide demand—while
the decoder autoregressively generates the target sequence, conditioning each step on the
encoded context and its own past outputs. In SARIMA models, exogenous variables play
a similar role to encoder context vectors, while autoregressive terms act as the decoder’s
feedback loop, incorporating historical consumption patterns into future forecasts. In both
cases, temporal dependencies are preserved: in Transformers via masked self-attention, and

in econometric models via lagged demand and seasonal components. This analogy moti-
vates the use of sequence-to-sequence Transformers as a more flexible generalization of the
ARX framework, capable of modeling nonlinear interactions among prices, temperatures,
and 4CP signals while retaining the autoregressive principle. We further hypothesize that
miners’ operational policies may be implicitly encoded in the cross-attention latent, which
the decoder can query to decide whether to curtail or restore load given the exogenous
context.
 The Temporal Fusion Transformer (TFT) [3] blends a sequence-processing backbone
with attention-based interpretability. Its temporal modeling relies on an LSTM encoder for
past and known-future covariates, coupled with masked, interpretable multi-head attention
to highlight important timesteps across the forecast horizon. While effective for many fore-
casting tasks, the LSTM backbone is more rigid than Transformer-style architectures—TFT
produces the entire forecast horizon in a single forward pass, conditioned on the full en-
coded context, rather than autoregressively generating outputs with decoder feedback. A
notable strength of TFT is its native quantile forecasting capability, which outputs multiple
quantiles to capture predictive uncertainty. This is particularly useful for cryptocurrency min-
ing demand, where price spikes, cooling loads, and 4CP avoidance can create asymmetric,
fat-tailed deviations from typical consumption.
 We propose a hybrid forecasting framework that combines the autoregressive sequence-
to-sequence structure of the Transformer with TFT’s embedding and feature selection mech-
anisms. The encoder incorporates Gated Residual Networks (GRNs) and Variable Selection
Networks (VSNs) to embed and dynamically weight heterogeneous inputs, producing a con-
textual knowledge base. Hourly tokens include:

 • Continuous covariates: prices, temperature, ERCOT load, prior miner demand (nor-
 malized + small projection)

 • Categorical/state flags: summer indicator, 4CP window indicator, site/zone IDs
 (learned embeddings)

 • Temporal encodings: hour-of-day, day-of-week, seasonal Fourier/positional terms

 Multiple time series enter the encoder at identical timestamps, first processed by the
VSN to weight their relative importance, then either concatenated into a per-hour feature
vector or passed through modality-specific mini-encoders with late fusion before the decoder
attends to the fused representation. The decoder retains a Transformer design with masked
self-attention for temporal autoregression and cross-attention to the encoder context, allow-
ing each forecasted step to condition on both historical predictions and real-time exogenous
inputs. The output head is probabilistic, generating multiple quantiles at each horizon step
to explicitly model uncertainty—supporting risk-aware decision-making and capturing asym-
metric miner responses to market and environmental events. This architecture merges TFT’s
interpretability and variable importance mechanisms with the flexibility of Transformer-based
autoregression, while preserving probabilistic forecasting capabilities.

2.2 Model Architecture
Our proposed forecasting model, as shown in Fig. 2.3, combines the autoregressive sequence-
to-sequence structure of the Transformer with key components from the Temporal Fusion
Transformer (TFT) to handle heterogeneous time series inputs and produce probabilistic
forecasts. The architecture consists of the following stages:

 Figure 2.3: Developed autoregressive transformer model.

  1. Input embedding and gating: Continuous and categorical covariates (both static
 and time-varying) are embedded into a common latent dimension. Each embedded
 variable is processed by a Gated Residual Network (GRN) to allow nonlinear transfor-
 mation while preserving residual pathways via gated linear units.

  2. Variable selection: A Variable Selection Network (VSN) computes instance-specific
 attention weights over the set of embedded variables at each time step, conditioning
 on static context. This produces a weighted combination of transformed features,
 emphasizing the most relevant drivers for the current forecast horizon.

LLNL-TR-2017219                                                                         12

  3. Encoder context generation: The selected features, along with static metadata
 embeddings, are passed through GRN layers to generate a contextual memory rep-
 resentation, serving as the encoder output. This memory encodes both historical
 observations and known-future exogenous inputs.
  4. Autoregressive decoding with attention: The decoder operates autoregressively,
 using masked self-attention to ensure causal temporal dependence and cross-attention
 to query the encoder memory at each forecast step. This enables conditioning on both
 prior predictions and the full exogenous context.
  5. Probabilistic output head: At each horizon step, the decoder outputs multiple
 quantiles (e.g., 10th, 50th, 90th percentiles), capturing predictive uncertainty rather
 than only point forecasts.
  6. Loss function: The model is trained with the quantile (pinball) loss, summing over all
 forecast steps and quantiles, enabling asymmetry-aware error penalization for heavy-
 tailed and skewed demand distributions.

 This design retains the interpretability of TFT’s feature selection and gating mecha-
nisms, while introducing a Transformer-style autoregressive decoder for greater flexibility
in modeling temporal dependencies. The probabilistic output layer preserves the ability to
quantify uncertainty, which is critical for applications such as cryptocurrency mining demand
forecasting under volatile price and weather conditions.

3.1 Introduction
In Texas, every large power user—including bitcoin-mining data centers—faces a distinctive
summer risk: ERCOT designates one 15-minute coincident peak in each month from June
to September, and transmission charges for the following year are based on a customer’s
average demand across those four intervals. Because these intervals typically fall in the
late afternoon (˜2–6 p.m.) and cannot be known in advance, firms confront a coordination
problem: if many curtail tightly around the expected window, the system peak could in
principle shift. In practice, by 2023–24 crypto-related large flexible loads totaled only ˜2–3
GW (˜3% of system summer peak), too small to move ERCOT-wide peaks. The stakes are
large—transmission charges can comprise as much as 30–40% of annual electricity costs—so
miners broaden their precautionary window and curtail for hours at a time. SEC filings and
public disclosures confirm that miners actively manage 4CP exposure and, in chasing this
elusive signal, have become among the most flexible and responsive loads on the Texas grid.
 Bitcoin-mining economics are simple on paper: revenue depends on hash price while
electricity dominates costs. Actual operating behavior, however, reveals a more complex
calculus. During Winter Storm Uri, for example, power prices reached the cap and remained
extreme, yet some large loads curtailed less than half their demand. This was not an
anomaly. As shown in Fig. 1, miners often curtail less when power prices surge if hash prices
are also elevated.
 We treat miners’ response to ERCOT’s four coincident peak (4CP) mechanism as a
natural experiment, exploiting ex-ante 4CP-risk variation to identify short-run price elastic-
ity and to examine how crypto-specific incentives interact with grid signals. Rather than
targeting realized 4CP days, we focus on hours when miners anticipate elevated 4CP risk
and respond collectively; within our causal framework, we refer to this responsiveness as
the intention-to-treat (ITT). To construct the ITT, we implement a diurnal (hour-of-day)

3.2 Research Design
To estimate the effect of ITT, I begin with a standard two-way fixed-effects (TWFE) frame-
work, which relies on a generalized parallel-trends assumption in the multi-period setting
(see, e.g., Angrist & Pischke, 2009). Credibility of this assumption improves when condition-
ing on relevant covariates such as firm capacity, which is plausibly predetermined with respect
to 4CP exposure. Since infrastructure investment is largely irreversible (Pandey, Brelsford,
and Seto, 2025), we construct a capacity covariate as the cumulative maximum of the load
time series, computed outside summer-day hours to avoid contamination by treatment ef-
fects. Mining firms’ responsiveness increases with capacity, and log-transforming demand
ensures that both firm growth and responsiveness enter additively. Under a multiplicative
decomposition, subtracting the growth covariate from log load yields residuals that remain
close to zero across all regions—except during summer days, when demand dips sharply in
anticipation of ITT. This pattern supports the parallel-trends assumption in DiD conditional
on the capacity covariate.
 Figure 1(d) also shows dynamic responses. Following the cryptocurrency-mining ban in
mid-2021, the departure of mining firms from China coincided with accelerated growth in
the U.S. share of global Bitcoin hashrate, especially in Texas. Comparing 2021 and 2022
reveals important differences: muted responsiveness in 2021—consistent with the onset
of the Texas mining boom (Dallas News, 2021) and firms’ lack of prior experience with
4CP—followed by vigorous, broad-based responses in 2022. To capture this heterogeneity, I
estimate dynamic ITT effects using a dynamic difference-in-differences (event-study) design
that allows the 4CP response to vary both by time since exposure and across entry cohorts
(Heckman & Navarro, 2005).
 We estimate the ITT effect of bitcoin miners’ energy consumption using the following
specification:

 log(Demandit ) = αi + γt + ∑ βk ITTkt + λ log(Capacityit ) + δ log(Capacityit ) ⋅ ITTkt + ϵit

Where log(Demandit ) is the log electricity demand of mining firm i at time t, αi is the region
fixed effect, γt are various time-related fixed effects (year, season, day-of-week, time-of-day),
βk represents miners’ dynamic curtailment response across years, log(Capacityit ) is the log
of cumulative maximum load outside of summer-days, proxying for firm capacity, δ measures
how responsiveness scales with firm size, and ϵit denotes the error term. Observations are
weighted using coarsened exact matching (CEM) weights, and standard errors are clustered
at region-day level.
 Our ability to causally identify the ITT rests on several assumptions, many standard in
DiD analyses. First, we assume that treated and untreated outcomes (load during summer-
day hours versus otherwise) would have evolved in parallel absent the 4CP incentive; the
residual patterns above support this conditional on capacity. Second, we assume strict
exogeneity of treatment timing: the ITT interval is driven by system conditions rather than
firms’ outcomes. This is supported by institutional features (4CP charges are mandated
off summer coincident peaks) and by the fact that historically these peaks arise in late-
afternoon hours largely due to weather-driven demand and net-load conditions; given the

high financial stakes, firms widen their precautionary windows in response to ex-ante risk,

Figure 1 demonstrates that miners exhibit non-constant elasticities with respect to electricity
and hash prices: when electricity prices rise and hash prices are low, miners curtail; as hash

homogeneous production frontiers and motivate a more flexible specification. Our three
input factors are electricity purchased in the day-ahead and real-time markets, and hash price.
Because the day-ahead and real-time markets represent alternative trading mechanisms

substitution analysis. Variance inflation factors (VIFs) grow dramatically once squared terms

structure of demand.
 Our model takes the following form:

 log(Demandit ) = Translog(DAM, RTM, HashPrice) + Controls + ϵit

3.5 Discussion
Bitcoin mining is one of the clearest examples of this new class of financially coupled load.
In 2023, bitcoin mining accounted for an estimated 0.6% to 2.3% of total electricity use in
the United States. By 2024, it was responsible for nearly 5% of total electricity demand
growth. In Texas alone, the state’s grid operator, ERCOT, had approved up to 2,600 MW
of crypto mining capacity—roughly 3.5% of the state’s 2023 peak load.
 Here, we use ERCOT’s Four Coincident Peak (4CP) transmission policy as a quasi-
experimental framework to causally estimate miners’ curtailment behavior. Because 4CP
risk is exogenous to real-time electricity prices, it allows us to isolate the effect of perceived
risk and then evaluate how price and hash price interact. Our results suggest that the
current model of price-responsive load is insufficient for today’s grid. We are entering a new
era of electricity demand—one shaped by multi-commodity economics and cross-market
dependencies. Grid operators, regulators, and planners worldwide must recognize that load
flexibility is not just a technical function—it is an economic behavior embedded within larger
financial ecosystems.
 Failing to account for this paradigm shift will increasingly jeopardize reliability, distort
forecasting, and undermine demand-side resource planning. What is needed now is a new
market paradigm, one that anticipates the growing influence of financial incentives on elec-
tricity demand, and designs markets accordingly.

4.1 Introduction
Machine learning (ML) is rapidly emerging as a key enabler for power system studies, ad-
vancing applications in transient stability assessment, learning-based control, fault diagnosis,
and secure operational planning [7]. These applications depend on access to high-quality
dynamic time-series datasets that are realistic, diverse, and openly accessible to support ML
model training and evaluation. Large-scale dynamic simulation provides a controlled and
fully observable means to generate such data. However, constructing dynamic datasets that
meet these requirements remains challenging in practice.
 Most existing dynamic simulation datasets used in power system research are limited in
accessibility, realism, and scale. Many realistic dynamic models developed by utilities and
manufacturers are proprietary due to confidentiality requirements, preventing their release for
public research. At the same time, widely used public datasets such as the IEEE benchmark
test systems are small in scale and lack detailed control representations of inverter-based
resources (IBRs), making them insufficient for studying modern IBR-dominated grids. Fur-
thermore, most commercial dynamic simulation tools are closed-source, require paid licenses,
and are not designed for high-performance computing (HPC) workflows such as large-scale
parallel execution or automated batch simulation. These factors create practical barriers to
generating diverse sets of dynamic simulation results across varied operating conditions and
contingency scenarios.
 To address these limitations, the research community has increasingly turned to open-
source power system models and dynamic simulation tools [8]. Large-scale transmission
system models such as the Texas A&M electric grid test cases [9], NREL’s reduced Western
Electricity Coordinating Council (WECC) models [10], and PNNL’s DR POWER reposi-
tory [11] provide realistic and openly available system representations for stability and plan-
ning research. In parallel, open-source dynamic simulation tools including ANDES [12],

LLNL-TR-2017219                                                                              25

GridDyn [13], and SIENA [14] offer transparent model structures, user-extensible dynamic
component libraries, and support for scalable simulation workflows.
 While these models and tools [9, 10, 11, 12, 13, 14] represent important progress toward
openness and realism, they do not, on their own, provide large-scale ML-ready dynamic time-
series datasets. Using them to generate such datasets requires (i) integrating a large-scale
dynamic model with an open simulation framework, (ii) designing a HPC workflow capable
of running tens of thousands of diverse disturbance scenarios, and (iii) curating the resulting
time-domain outputs into structured, labeled data suitable for ML training and evaluation.
To the best of the authors’ knowledge, there is currently no publicly available resource that
combines all of these elements into a coherent and openly accessible dataset.
 In this work, we release an open, ML–oriented dynamic simulation dataset constructed
from the Texas 2000-bus synthetic system (Texas2k), simulated using the ANDES open-
source platform and executed through large-scale HPC workflows. The contributions of this
work are threefold:
 • Conversion and refinement of the Texas2k dynamic models in the ANDES environ-
 ment, ensuring realistic dynamic response and stable time-domain simulation under
 contingency conditions.

 • Development of a scalable HPC simulation workflow that generates tens of thousands
 time-domain simulations across diverse system conditions and contingency events,
 together with a transient-stability–index based sampling method to produce balanced
 ML-ready subsets.

 • Public release of the ML-ready dynamic simulation dataset, workflow scripts, and
 documentation, lowering barriers to applying and evaluating ML methods in power

4.3 HPC Simulation Framework
This section describes, first, the HPC workflow we implemented to generate and store our
large-scale database of dynamic simulations, and, second, how we sample a balanced subset

4.3.1 HPC Setup
We run the dynamics simulations using an embarrassingly-parallel coordinator-worker
workflow (one coordinator, many workers). The simulation starts from a system on
a given condition. The coordinator holds a list of events (e.g., generator outages, line
outages, etc.), that it iteratively assigns to the workers as they become available. Each
worker waits for an event from the coordinator, it applies the event to the system, run the
time domain simulation for the event, and save the integration result in a worker-specific
file. Once all the events have been simulated, the workers idle and the coordinator
merges all different worker files into a single file for the specified system and condition.
 This workflow is implemented in Python with MPI communications, specifically, we
implement through mpi4py [19], with OpenMPI [20] backend. The time-domain simulation
results are extracted from the ANDES system data structures, converted to single-point
precision for storage considerations, and saved in HDF5 format using h5py [21].

data using a specialized sampling process that looks for events that are representative of

 We select a number of TSIs and compute them for every event simulated, thereby, pro-

4.5 Conclusions
We presented an open, ML-ready dynamic simulation dataset for power system studies,
constructed from the 2000-bus Texas synthetic system and refined in the ANDES environ-
ment for realistic and stable dynamic behavior. A scalable HPC workflow was developed
to generate tens of thousands contingency-based time-domain simulations across multiple
operating conditions. A TSI-based sampling method was then applied to create balanced
subsets suitable for ML training and evaluation. The dataset and workflow are publicly
released to lower barriers to applying and evaluating ML methods in power system dynamic
studies.

Additional Work Products

As part of the Year 2 activities, the OPEN COG Grid project has developed and released
three open-source software packages and two open datasets. The software packages provide
specialized tools for modeling and forecasting different types of power system loads, and
for generating large-scale training datasets for machine learning applications. The datasets
provide simulation-ready system models and large-scale synthetic system data in standard-
ized formats. All software packages and datasets are publicly available and documented to
facilitate adoption by the broader research community.

5.1 Power System Dynamics Training Dataset Generation Util-
 ities
The psdatagen package provides a comprehensive toolkit for generating large-scale power
system dynamics training datasets using time-domain simulations with high-performance
computing (HPC) parallelism. This package is based on the work presented in Chapter 4 and
provides the complete implementation of the methodology and workflow used to generate
the ML-ready dynamic simulation datasets from the Texas 2000-bus synthetic system.

5.1.1 Overview and Capabilities
The toolkit leverages the ANDES open-source time-domain simulation framework and imple-
ments an embarrassingly parallel MPI-based workflow for efficient large-scale computation.
The package provides four main capabilities:

 • Contingency Generation: Systematic generation of N-1 and N-2 contingency sce-
 narios including generator outages and transmission line trips.

 • Parallel Simulation: Distribution of time-domain simulations across MPI processes,
 enabling efficient computation of tens of thousands of contingency scenarios.

 • Stability Assessment: Computation of Transient Stability Index (TSI) values from
 simulation results to quantify transient stability margins.

LLNL-TR-2017219                                                                          36

 • Representative Sampling: Balanced density-based sampling methods implemented
 in R to select representative contingencies from large datasets while preserving the
 distribution of system behaviors.

5.1.2 Package Structure and Workflow
The package is organized into core library modules (src/), workflow scripts (scripts/),
and example templates for SLURM-based HPC clusters (examples/). The typical workflow
consists of four sequential stages:

  1. Generation of contingency case files from system data (01 generate casefile.py)

  2. Parallel execution of time-domain simulations using MPI with results aggregated into
 HDF5 format (02 run simulations.py)

  3. Computation of TSI values for each simulated contingency (03 compute tsi.py)

  4. Selection of representative contingencies using balanced sampling (04 balanced subsample.R)

5.1.3 Data Management API
A key contribution of this package is a Python API for efficient access to large HDF5
simulation datasets. The DynamicSimulationDirectory and DynamicSimulationFile
classes provide lazy-loading data access patterns that enable processing of multi-terabyte
datasets on standard computing resources. These classes automatically discover HDF5 files
in directory hierarchies and provide iterator-based access to individual simulation results
without loading entire datasets into memory.

5.1.4 Data Sources and Integration
The package uses power system test cases from the Open COG Grid Dataverse hosted at
Harvard University. System files in Excel format (.xlsx) are provided in the cases/ direc-
tory for convenience, while large simulation result datasets are made available through the
dataverse upon publication. This integration ensures reproducibility and provides researchers
with ready access to both the tools and the underlying system models.

5.2 Crypto Miner Forecaster
The crypto-miner-forecast package implements a Transformer-based deep learning model
for probabilistic forecasting of cryptocurrency mining power consumption, based on the work
presented in Chapter 2. This specialized forecasting tool addresses a growing challenge in
power system operations, particularly in Texas where cryptocurrency mining facilities par-
ticipate in demand response programs and represent significant controllable loads.

LLNL-TR-2017219                                                                           37

5.2.1 Model Architecture
The model combines an autoregressive Transformer architecture with components from the
Temporal Fusion Transformer (TFT) framework to perform multi-horizon quantile forecast-
ing with uncertainty quantification. The architecture is specifically designed to capture
complex interactions between multiple input factors:

 • Historical load patterns from the mining facility

 • Weather conditions including temperature, humidity, wind speed, and solar radiation

 • Energy market prices in both real-time and day-ahead markets

 • Regional grid load forecasts

 • Cryptocurrency hash prices

 The model outputs probabilistic forecasts across nine quantiles (1%, 5%, 10%, 25%,
50%, 75%, 90%, 95%, 99%), providing a comprehensive characterization of forecast uncer-
tainty. This multi-quantile output enables risk-aware decision-making for both grid operators
managing system reliability and mining facility managers optimizing economic dispatch.

5.2.2 Application Context
Cryptocurrency mining facilities present unique forecasting challenges due to their opera-
tional flexibility and responsiveness to economic signals. Unlike traditional industrial loads,
mining operations can rapidly adjust their power consumption in response to electricity
prices and grid conditions while maintaining economic viability. This makes them valuable
participants in demand response programs, but also introduces complex behavioral patterns
that traditional load forecasting methods struggle to capture.
 The model addresses these challenges through its attention-based architecture, which
can learn temporal dependencies across multiple time scales and identify relevant feature
interactions without explicit feature engineering. The probabilistic nature of the forecasts
further enables quantification of the inherent uncertainty in mining facility behavior under
varying market and system conditions.

5.2.3 Implementation and Documentation
The package provides a complete implementation including data preprocessing utilities,
model training scripts, and inference tools. A command-line interface enables straightfor-
ward application of the trained model to new data, while comprehensive documentation
covers installation procedures, data format requirements, and the underlying model archi-
tecture. The implementation is based on PyTorch and can leverage GPU acceleration when
available.

5.3 Demand Response Decoder
The dr-decoder package implements a Variational Autoencoder (VAE) decoder for gen-
erating synthetic demand response data from residential customers. This tool enables re-
searchers and grid planners to generate realistic load profiles for customers participating in
demand response programs under various operating conditions.

5.3.1 Model Training and Data Source
The pre-trained model parameters were developed using a Conditional Variational Autoen-
coder (CVAE) architecture trained on real-world data from the Low Carbon London (LCL)
project conducted by UK Power Networks between 2010 and 2014. The training dataset
comprises approximately 1,100 residential customers participating in a dynamic Time-of-Use
(TOU) tariff program, with power consumption measurements at 30-minute resolution.
 The CVAE architecture learns conditional distributions of customer demand response
behavior based on four categorical input conditions:

 • Responsiveness level: Characterizes the customer’s propensity to modify consump-
 tion in response to price signals (low, medium, or high responsiveness)

 • Temperature: Captures weather-driven load variations based on monthly temperature
 percentiles (low, medium, or high)

 • Price signals: Represents electricity pricing levels (low or high prices)

 • Day type: Distinguishes between weekday and weekend consumption patterns

5.3.2 Dynamic Architecture System
A distinguishing feature of this implementation is its dynamic architecture system, where the
complete model structure is defined in JSON configuration files rather than hardcoded in
Python. This design enables researchers to modify the network architecture without altering
source code, facilitating experimentation with different model structures. The configuration
system supports parameter expressions, allowing layer dimensions to be defined in terms of
variables such as latent dimension and condition length.
 The default architecture consists of two sequential fully connected hidden layers with 256
dimensions each, using LeakyReLU activation functions. The model accepts 304-dimensional
input (300 latent dimensions plus 4 condition variables) and produces 48-dimensional output
representing 30-minute power consumption intervals over a 24-hour period.

5.3.3 Usage and Applications
The package provides both a command-line interface and a Python API for generating
synthetic demand response data. Users can specify desired operating conditions either as
integer codes or descriptive strings, and generate batches of synthetic load profiles. Output

LLNL-TR-2017219                                                                            39

can be saved in CSV format for integration with power system simulation tools or other
analysis workflows.
 This capability supports multiple use cases including scenario analysis for demand re-
sponse program design, generation of training data for machine learning models when real
customer data is unavailable due to privacy constraints, and stress testing of grid operations
under various combinations of weather, pricing, and customer behavior conditions.

5.3.4 Documentation and Reproducibility
The package includes comprehensive documentation covering model training methodology,
validation results, API usage, and module structure. The pre-trained model parameters
are provided in compressed JSON format, enabling immediate application without requiring
access to the original training data. This design balances reproducibility with practical data
privacy considerations inherent in residential customer information.

5.4 Open Power System Datasets
In addition to the software packages described above, the OPEN COG Grid project released
two open power system datasets during Year 2. These datasets are hosted on Harvard
Dataverse and provide the research community with access to both simulation-ready system
models and large-scale synthetic system data in standardized formats.

5.4.1 Augmented Texas 7000-bus System Dataset
The augmented Texas 7000-bus system dataset provides a large-scale synthetic power sys-
tem model with comprehensive time series data for renewable generation and load profiles.
This dataset uses the Common Transmission Model (CTM) format developed by Lawrence
Livermore National Laboratory [27], providing a standardized representation of power system
infrastructure and time-varying operational data.
 The dataset consists of two primary components:

 • Infrastructure Data: A JSON file containing detailed static network characteristics
 including bus parameters, transmission line characteristics, generator specifications,
 and topology information. This structured format follows the CTM schema and can
 be parsed using the open-source CTM Python library.

 • Time Series Data: An HDF5 file containing hourly time series data for renewable
 energy production (PV and wind) and load profiles spanning one year. The HDF5
 format enables efficient storage and access to large multivariate time series datasets.

 The dataset includes a Python-based data loader that automatically parses both the in-
frastructure and time series components, providing researchers with convenient access to the
complete system representation. The infrastructure data object provides detailed network
parameters suitable for power flow analysis, optimal power flow studies, and production cost

LLNL-TR-2017219                                                                            40

modeling. The time series data enables studies of system operations under realistic temporal
variations in renewable generation and demand.
 This standardized data format addresses a key challenge in power system research: the
lack of interoperability between different simulation platforms and data representations. By
providing both infrastructure and operational data in well-documented, open formats (JSON
and HDF5), the dataset can be readily converted to formats required by various simulation
tools and optimization frameworks. The use of CTM as the underlying data model provides
a formal schema that ensures consistency and enables automated validation of data integrity.
 The dataset is released under an open license and is available on Harvard Dataverse [28].
Installation scripts are provided to set up the required Python environment and clone the
CTM library repository, lowering barriers to adoption for researchers new to the CTM format.

5.4.2 Texas 2000-bus Dynamic Simulation Dataset
The Texas 2000-bus dynamic simulation dataset provides an open-source implementation
of the Texas2k synthetic power system configured to run on the ANDES open-source dy-
namic simulator. This dataset directly supports the methodology described in Chapter 4
by providing the base system models used to generate the large-scale ML-ready simulation
results.
 The Texas2k system is a publicly available, fully synthetic power grid model developed
by Texas A&M University to resemble the structural and operational characteristics of real-
world grids while not corresponding to any actual network. The system includes 2000 buses,
436 synchronous generators, and 107 inverter-based resources (PV and wind), making it
suitable for transient stability analysis and high renewable penetration studies.
 The dataset includes:
 • System Models: Detailed Texas2k models and parameters in structured Excel spread-
 sheets formatted for ANDES, including a Full SG and IBR Dispatch Case and a Sum-
 mer Peak Load Case.
 • Simulation Code: Python scripts to run dynamic simulations of the Texas2k system
 on ANDES, enabling researchers to reproduce results and conduct their own studies.
 • Documentation: Files explaining the model structure, dispatch cases, and code usage
 instructions.
 Significant modifications were made to the original Texas2k system to ensure compatibil-
ity with ANDES and improve dynamic behavior. The PSS2A stabilizer model was replaced
with IEEEST due to ANDES model support limitations. Control parameters for exciter
models (ESST1A, ESST4B, IEEET1) were recalibrated to improve stability performance.
The REECA1 renewable exciter model was configured for constant Q control rather than
local coordinated Q/V control due to initialization issues in ANDES. Additionally, wind tur-
bine generator drive-train models (WTDTA1) were added, and wind turbine control models
(WTPTA1, WTTQA1) were recalibrated.
 The dataset is released under the Creative Commons Attribution 4.0 International (CC
BY 4.0) license and is available on Harvard Dataverse [29].

Conclusions

During Year 2, the OPEN COG Grid project achieved significant milestones in its goal of
developing open, coherent datasets and analytical tools for power system research. The work
presented in this report advances the state of the art across multiple dimensions: modeling
emerging load classes, causal inference methods for grid behavior analysis, large-scale dataset
generation workflows, and open-source software tools.

6.1 Summary of Accomplishments
The project’s major accomplishments during Year 2 include:

6.1.1 Advanced Load Modeling and Forecasting
We developed and validated a Transformer-based probabilistic forecasting model for cryp-
tocurrency mining loads, addressing a critical gap in modeling highly flexible, financially
coupled loads. The model’s multi-horizon quantile forecasting capability provides compre-
hensive uncertainty quantification, enabling risk-aware decision-making for grid operators
and facility managers. This work establishes a methodological foundation for modeling
other emerging load classes that exhibit similar cross-market dependencies, such as indus-
trial hydrogen production, electric vehicle charging hubs, and data centers with flexible
computing workloads.

6.1.2 Causal Analysis of Grid Behavior
The causal analysis of bitcoin miner curtailment behavior represents a methodological ad-
vance in understanding financially coupled loads. By exploiting ERCOT’s Four Coincident
Peak pricing mechanism as a quasi-experimental framework, we demonstrated that miners’
price elasticity ranges from -0.42 to -0.04 depending on Bitcoin profitability. This finding has
important implications beyond cryptocurrency mining: it reveals that traditional constant-
elasticity assumptions are inadequate for loads whose operational decisions are influenced
by external commodity markets. The restricted translog specification developed in this

LLNL-TR-2017219                                                                              42

work provides a tractable approach for capturing non-constant elasticities while maintaining
consistency with causal inference requirements.

6.1.3 Large-Scale ML-Ready Dataset Generation
The development of an open ML-ready dynamic simulation dataset from the Texas 2000-bus
system addresses a fundamental barrier to applying machine learning methods in power sys-
tem applications. The dataset generation workflow consumed 44,820 core-hours of compute
time and produced comprehensive simulation results across four system configurations and
tens of thousands of contingency scenarios. The novel TSI-based balanced sampling method
developed in this work solves a critical practical problem: how to reduce massive simula-
tion datasets (29.9 TB) to tractable sizes (80 GB for 250 events per case) while preserving
representative coverage of system behaviors. This sampling approach is generalizable be-
yond transient stability applications to other domains requiring representative subsampling
of concentrated empirical distributions.

6.1.4 Open-Source Software and Dataset Releases
The release of three open-source software packages and two open datasets extends the
project’s impact beyond the immediate research team. The psdatagen package provides
a complete implementation of the dataset generation workflow, enabling other research
groups to generate similar datasets from different power system models or under different
operating conditions. The crypto-miner-forecast package makes the Transformer-based
forecasting methodology accessible to practitioners without requiring expertise in deep learn-
ing architectures. The dr-decoder package addresses data privacy constraints by enabling
generation of synthetic residential demand response data that preserves statistical proper-
ties of real customer behavior. The Texas 7000-bus dataset provides a large-scale system in
Common Transmission Model format with infrastructure and time series data suitable for
production cost modeling and renewable integration studies. The Texas 2000-bus dataset
provides ANDES-ready dynamic models that serve as the foundation for the ML-ready sim-
ulation results. All packages and datasets include comprehensive documentation and are
designed to lower barriers to adoption by the broader research community.

6.2 Closing Remarks
The OPEN COG Grid project demonstrates that creating high-quality, openly accessible
power system datasets requires coordinated efforts spanning realistic system modeling, scal-
able simulation workflows, statistical sampling methods, and software engineering. The
Year 2 accomplishments establish a foundation for addressing increasingly complex mod-
eling challenges posed by the evolving grid, including high penetrations of inverter-based
resources, and emerging flexible load classes with multi-market dependencies.
 By making datasets, methodologies, and software tools openly available, the project
lowers barriers to power system research and enables participation by researchers from the
broader data sciences community. The continued development of these resources will support
