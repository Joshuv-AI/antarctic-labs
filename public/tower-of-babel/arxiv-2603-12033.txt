Vector spin glasses with Mattis interaction I: the convex case
Hong-Bin Chen, Victor Issa

Abstract
. This paper constitutes the first part of a two-paper series devoted to the systematic study of vector spin glass models whose energy function involves a spin glass part and a general Mattis interaction part. In this paper, we focus on models whose spin glass part satisfies the usual convexity assumption. We identify the limit free energy via a Parisi-type formula and prove a large deviation principle for the mean magnetization. The proof is remarkably simple and short compared to previous approaches; it relies on treating the Mattis interaction as a parameter of the model. In the companion paper [ 12 ] , we establish similar results in the high-temperature regime for models whose spin glass part is not assumed to satisfy the usual convexity assumption. Keywords and phrases: Disordered systems, Large deviation principles, Spin glasses. MSC 2020: 82B44, 60F10, 35F21.



1.
Introduction

1.1.
Preamble

This paper is the first of a two-part series in which we investigate general mean-field spin glass models with Mattis interaction. In this paper, we focus on models whose spin glass part satisfies the usual convexity assumption (
1.2
).
A simple instance of those models is given by a system of
\pm 1
spins interacting through the energy function

(1.1)

E_{N}(\sigma)=\frac{\beta}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{i,j=1}^{N}W_{ij}\sigma_{i}\sigma_{j}+\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\chi_{i}\sigma_{i}\right)^{2},\hskip 20.00003pt\forall\sigma\in\{-1,1\}^{N},

where
(W_{i,j})_{1\leqslant i,j\leqslant N}
are independent centered gaussian random variables with variance
1
and
(\chi_{i})_{1\leqslant i\leqslant N}
are centered
\pm 1
random variables.
Our investigation is motivated in part by the study of statistical inference problems with mismatched prior and noise. In modern statistical inference, many high-dimensional estimation problems, such as recovering a low-rank signal from noisy observations, can be formulated in terms of energy-based models. In particular, when the assumed prior on the signal or the noise distribution used by the estimator does not match the true generative model, the resulting posterior landscape behaves like a generalized Sherrington–Kirkpatrick spin glass with additional Mattis interaction
[
3
,
4
,
37
,
34
,
16
,
10
]
. Roughly speaking, the idea is that
for inference problems with mismatched prior and noise, one can reduce the computation of the mutual information in the statistical problem to the computation of the free energy of a modified spin glass model similar to (
1.1
) and derive asymptotic formulas for the mutual information and overlaps (order parameters) that characterize estimator performance. This connection provides rigorous large deviation principles for key quantities, such as overlaps between the estimate and the true signal.

In this series of papers
[
11
,
12
]
, our interest is twofold. First, we prove variational formulas for the large
N
limit of the free energy defined by

f_{N}=-\frac{1}{N}\log\left(\frac{1}{2^{N}}\sum_{\sigma\in\{-1,1\}^{N}}e^{E_{N}(\sigma)}\right).

Second, we establish large deviation principles for the mean magnetization

m_{N}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\chi_{i}\sigma_{i}

under the Gibbs measure
\langle\cdot\rangle_{N}
defined by

\langle g(\sigma)\rangle_{N}\propto\frac{1}{2^{N}}\sum_{\sigma\in\{-1,1\}^{N}}g(\sigma)e^{E_{N}(\sigma)}.

Our main results are stated for very general models in Theorem
1.2
and Theorem
1.4
. Here we state a specialized version of those results for the model defined by (
1.1
), which is an instance of a model satisfying the convexity assumption (
1.2
) defined below. The definition of the function
\psi=\psi(q;x)
appearing in this statement is given below in (
1.9
).

Theorem 1.1
.

Almost surely over the randomness of
(W_{i,j})_{i,j}
and
(\chi_{i})_{i}
, we have

\lim_{N\to+\infty}f_{N}=\inf_{m}\sup_{x,p}\left\{\psi(\beta^{2}p;x)-\frac{\beta^{2}}{2}\int_{0}^{1}p(s)^{2}\mathrm{d}s+mx-m^{2}-\frac{\beta^{2}}{2}\right\},

where the infimum is taken over
m\in\mathbb{R}
and the supremum over
x\in\mathbb{R}
and
p:[0,1)\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
bounded increasing càdlàg paths. Furthermore, still almost surely over the randomness of
(W_{i,j})_{i,j}
and
(\chi_{i})_{i}
, the random variable
m_{N}
satisfies a large deviation principle under
\langle\cdot\rangle_{N}
with rate function

J(m)=-m^{2}+\phi^{*}(m)+\sup_{m^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}}\left\{(m^{\prime})^{2}-\phi^{*}(m^{\prime})\right\}.

Here
\phi^{*}(m)=\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}}\left\{xm+\phi(x)\right\}
and
\phi(x)
is the almost sure limit as
N\to+\infty
of the free energy
f_{N}
where the term
\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\chi_{i}\sigma_{i}\right)^{2}
in
E_{N}
is replaced by
x\sum_{i=1}^{N}\chi_{i}\sigma_{i}
.

Note that as a consequence of Theorem
1.2
below, the quantity
\phi(x)
used to define the rate function
J
can be expressed as a variational formula.

For models satisfying the aforementioned convexity assumption (
1.2
), computing the free energy in the absence of Mattis interaction is a classical problem, and it is now well known that the limit of the free energy can be understood as the supremum of an explicit functional, this is the celebrated Parisi formula
[
33
,
22
,
36
]
.
The free energy can also be studied from the perspective of Hamilton–Jacobi equations, which was initiated in
[
21
]
and followed by
[
5
,
1
,
7
,
6
,
8
,
20
]
.
Recently, a more systematic and general treatment through the prism of Hamilton–Jacobi equations was proposed in
[
28
,
26
,
18
]
, the progress of which is summarized in
[
29
]
.

On the other hand, models with Mattis interaction have not yet received a similar systematic treatment. The identification of their free energy relies on model-dependent proofs. Furthermore, especially in cases where authors are interested in large deviation principles for the mean magnetization, the proofs involve computing a constrained version of the free energy defined by

f_{N}(A)=-\frac{1}{N}\log\left(\frac{1}{2^{N}}\sum_{\sigma\in A}e^{E_{N}(\sigma)}\right).

The proofs can sometimes be long and technical
[
23
,
19
,
10
,
32
,
16
]
. Similar techniques have also been employed to prove large deviation principles for the overlap parameter in the context of classical spin glass models
[
9
,
24
]
, also see
[
25
]
for related content. For spin glass models with additional Mattis interaction, there are no known general results that allow for a systematic identification of the free energy, nor the verification of large deviation principles for the mean magnetization.

In this series of papers, we resolve this problem and establish systematic results using a new approach that relies on the computation of the following object

f_{N}^{G}=-\frac{1}{N}\log\left(\frac{1}{2^{N}}\sum_{\sigma\in\{-1,1\}^{N}}\exp\left(\frac{\beta}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{i,j=1}^{N}W_{ij}\sigma_{i}\sigma_{j}+NG(m_{N})\right)\right).

The main difference in our approach is that the continuous function
G
encoding the Mattis interaction is now treated as a
parameter of the model
instead of being fixed. Furthermore, as opposed to the traditional approach, we do not impose constraints on the spin configuration
\sigma
. This allows for smoother, shorter, and less technical computations that one can carry out in a model-independent way. Our main theorems cover many previous model-specific results. For example, this includes
[
23
, Theorems 2.6 & 2.7]
,
[
10
, Theorem 1]
, and
[
16
, Theorem 1]
.

We start with the case
G(m)=xm
, where the sum structure of
NG(m_{N})=x\sum_{i=1}^{N}\chi_{i}\sigma_{i}
allows for the computation of
\phi(x)=\lim_{N\to+\infty}f_{N}^{m\mapsto xm}
using very classical arguments similar to those needed to establish the Parisi formula with essentially no modification. Then, we verify that
\phi
is a continuously differentiable function of
x
. This ensures, through the Gärtner–Ellis theorem, that
m_{N}
satisfies a large deviation principle under
\langle\cdot\rangle^{0}_{N}
. Varadhan’s lemma then allows us to compute the large
N
limit of
\frac{1}{N}\log\langle e^{NG(m_{N})}\rangle^{0}_{N}
for all continuous functions
G
. From this, we deduce a large deviation principle for
m_{N}
under
\langle\cdot\rangle^{G}_{N}
and an expression for
\lim_{N\to+\infty}f_{N}^{G}
. For the first part, this is a direct consequence of Bryc’s inverse Varadhan lemma, and the second part follows by observing that

\frac{1}{N}\log\langle e^{NG(m_{N})}\rangle^{0}_{N}=-f_{N}^{G}+f_{N}^{0}.

Once this is done, one can recover Theorem
1.1
by setting
G(m)=m^{2}
.

1.2.
Setting

In this section, we give a definition of the class of models that we treat.

For
m,n\in\mathbb{N}
, we denote by
\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}

m\times n

a=(a_{ij})_{1\leqslant i\leqslant m,\,1\leqslant j\leqslant n}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}

j

a_{\bullet j}=(a_{ij})_{1\leqslant i\leqslant m}
and its
i

a_{i\bullet}=(a_{ij})_{1\leqslant j\leqslant n}

a
, we denote by
a^{\intercal}
its transpose.
For
a,b\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
, we write
a\cdot b=\sum_{ij}a_{ij}b_{ij}

|a|=\sqrt{a\cdot a}
, and similarly for vectors.
For
n\in\mathbb{N}
, let
\mathrm{S}^{n}
be the linear space of
n\times n
real symmetric matrices
and also let
\mathrm{S}^{n}_{+}
(resp.
\mathrm{S}^{n}_{++}

a,b\in\mathrm{S}^{n}
, we write
a\geqslant b
provided
a-b\in\mathrm{S}^{n}_{+}
, which gives a natural partial order on
\mathrm{S}^{n}
.

We start by defining the spin glass part of the model. Let
D\in\mathbb{N}
be the dimension of a single spin. Let
P_{1}
be a finite positive measure supported on the unit ball
\left\{\tau\in\mathbb{R}^{D}:\>|\tau|\leqslant 1\right\}
of
\mathbb{R}^{D}
and we assume that the linear span of vectors in
\supp P_{1}
is
\mathbb{R}^{D}
.

For each
N\in\mathbb{N}
, we denote by
\sigma=(\sigma_{\bullet i})_{i=1}^{N}=(\sigma_{di})_{1\leqslant d\leqslant D,\,1\leqslant i\leqslant N}\in\mathbb{R}^{D\times N}
the spin configuration consisting of
N

\mathbb{R}^{D}
-valued spins
\sigma_{\bullet i}
as column vectors. We view
\sigma
as a
D\times N
matrix. We sample each spin independently from
P_{1}
, and thus the distribution of
\sigma
is given by

\mathrm{d}P_{1}^{\otimes N}(\sigma)=\otimes_{i=1}^{N}\mathrm{d}P_{1}(\sigma_{\bullet i}).

We let
\xi:\mathbb{R}^{D\times D}\to\mathbb{R}
be a smooth function that can be expressed as a power series,
and we assume that

(1.2)

the function
\xi
is convex on
S^{D}_{+}
.

For each
N
, we consider a centered Gaussian field
(H_{N}(\sigma))_{\sigma\in\mathbb{R}^{D\times N}}
with covariance structure given by

(1.3)

\mathbb{E}\left[H_{N}(\sigma)H_{N}(\sigma^{\prime})\right]=N\xi\left(\tfrac{\sigma\sigma^{\prime\intercal}}{N}\right),\hskip 20.00003pt\forall\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{D\times N}.

We also define
\theta:\mathbb{R}^{D\times D}\to\mathbb{R}
by

(1.4)

\displaystyle\theta(a)=a\cdot\nabla\xi(a)-\xi(a),\hskip 10.00002pt\forall a\in\mathbb{R}^{D\times D}.

We now describe the Mattis interaction part of the model. Let
L\in\mathbb{N}
and let
(\chi_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
be i.i.d.
\mathbb{R}^{L}
-valued random vectors, which are, in particular, independent from
H_{N}(\sigma)
. Let
d\in\mathbb{N}
and
h:\mathbb{R}^{D}\times\mathbb{R}^{L}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a bounded
1
1

1

Throughout it will be enough to assume that
h
is bounded on the support of the positive measure
P_{1}\otimes\text{Law}(\chi_{1})
.
measurable function. For each
i\in\mathbb{N}
, we view
h(\sigma_{\bullet i},\chi_{i})
as a generalized spin and define the mean magnetization

(1.5)

\displaystyle m_{N}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}h(\sigma_{\bullet i},\chi_{i}).

Let
G:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
be a continuous function, and we add to the system the Curie–Weiss-type interaction
NG\left(m_{N}\right)
.

At this point, we have described the spin glass part and the Mattis interaction part of our energy function. As explained in the introduction, recently a new approach has been put forward to describe the free energy of spin glass models (without Mattis interaction). In this approach, one needs to enrich the model by adding several new parameters. This more sophisticated object plays a key role in our companion paper
[
12
]
, so we will work with this one in the present paper as well. In order to stick to the classical setting, the reader not interested in this enriched version of the model can throughout the paper choose the parameter
q
to be the constant path taking the value
0
and set
t=\beta^{2}/2
.

To define the enriched version of the model, we need to add external fields parameterized by increasing paths and driven by Poisson–Dirichlet cascades. Let
\mathcal{Q}_{\infty}
be the collection of bounded increasing càdlàg paths
q:[0,1)\to\mathrm{S}^{D}_{+}
, where the monotonicity is understood as
q(s)-q(s^{\prime})\in\mathrm{S}^{D}_{+}
for
s>s^{\prime}
. Throughout, given
q\in\mathcal{Q}_{\infty}
, we will define

q(1)=\lim_{s\to 1^{-}}q(s),

which is well-defined by monotonicity of
q
.

Throughout, let
\mathfrak{R}
be the Ruelle probability cascade (RPC) with overlap uniformly distributed over
[0,1]
(see
[
31
, Theorem 2.17]
). Precisely,
\mathfrak{R}
is a random probability measure on the unit sphere of a fixed separable Hilbert space (the exact form of the space is not important), with the inner product denoted by
\alpha\wedge\alpha^{\prime}
. Let
\alpha
and
\alpha^{\prime}
be independent samples from
\mathfrak{R}
. Then, the law of
\alpha\wedge\alpha^{\prime}

[0,1]

\mathfrak{R}
.
This overlap distribution uniquely determines
\mathfrak{R}
(see
[
31
, Theorem 2.13]

Almost surely, the support of
\mathfrak{R}
is ultrametric in the induced topology. For rigorous definitions and basic properties, we refer to
[
31
, Chapter 2]
(also see
[
18
, Chapter 5]
).
We also refer to
[
13

for the construction and properties of
\mathfrak{R}
useful in this work.

For
q\in\mathcal{Q}_{\infty}
and almost every realization of
\mathfrak{R}
, let
(w^{q}(\alpha))_{\alpha\in\supp\mathfrak{R}}
be the
\mathbb{R}^{D}
-valued centered Gaussian process with covariance

(1.6)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[w^{q}(\alpha)w^{q}(\alpha^{\prime})^{\intercal}\right]=q(\alpha\wedge\alpha^{\prime}).

Its existence and properties are given in
[
13
, Section 4]
.
Conditioned on
\mathfrak{R}
, let
(w^{q}_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
be i.i.d. copies of
w^{q}
and also independent of other randomness.

For
N\in\mathbb{N}
,
q\in\mathcal{Q}_{\infty}
,
t\geqslant 0
, the full energy function of the system will be chosen to be

(1.7)

\displaystyle\begin{split}H^{G}_{N}(\sigma,\alpha)&=NG(m_{N})+\sqrt{2t}H_{N}(\sigma)-Nt\xi\left(\tfrac{\sigma\sigma^{\intercal}}{N}\right)\\
&+\sum_{i=1}^{N}w^{q}_{i}(\alpha)\cdot\sigma_{\bullet i}-\tfrac{1}{2}q(1)\cdot\sigma\sigma^{\intercal}.\end{split}

We are interested in the infinite-volume limit of the associated free energy defined as

(1.8)

\displaystyle F_{N}^{G}=-\frac{1}{N}\log\iint\exp\left(H_{N}^{G}(\sigma,\alpha)\right)\mathrm{d}P_{1}^{\otimes N}(\sigma)\mathrm{d}\mathfrak{R}(\alpha).

In principle,
H_{N}^{G}
and
F_{N}^{G}
depend on the choice of
t
and
q
. Throughout this paper, we will keep those parameters fixed so we do not make this dependence explicitly appear in our notations for
H_{N}^{G}
and
F_{N}^{G}
.

As is explained in full detail in Section
1.4
, usual models such as (
1.1
) can be obtained as special cases of the Hamiltonian
H_{N}^{G}
. Briefly, to do so one can set
t=\beta^{2}/2
and
q=0
and make a wise choice for

in order to absorb
-\tfrac{N\beta^{2}}{2}\xi\left(\tfrac{\sigma\sigma^{\intercal}}{N}\right)
in the Mattis interaction.

Here, we have chosen to introduce the correction terms
Nt\xi\left(\sigma\sigma^{\intercal}/N\right)
and
\frac{1}{2}q(1)\cdot\sigma\sigma^{\intercal}
as a convenience to simplify the forthcoming variational formulas. As explained in Section
1.4
, they are easily removed. Also, notice that those terms are one-half of the variance of
\sqrt{2t}H_{N}(\sigma)
and
\sum_{i=1}^{N}w^{q}_{i}(\alpha)\cdot\sigma_{\bullet i}
, respectively. So they correspond to drifts in exponential martingales.

The large deviation principle we prove holds almost surely with respect to the randomness of
(H_{N})_{N\geqslant 1}
,
(\chi_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
, and
\mathfrak{R}

1.3.
Main results

We let
\overline{F}_{N}^{G}=\mathbb{E}F_{N}^{G}
, where
\mathbb{E}
averages over the randomness of
H_{N}
,
(\chi_{i})_{i}
, and
\mathfrak{R}
. The random variable

concentrates strongly around its expectation
\overline{F}_{N}^{G}
in the large

[
31

. Hence, to describe the limit as

of

, it suffices to describe the limit of
\overline{F}_{N}^{G}
. To describe this limit, we need to define, for
q\in\mathcal{Q}_{\infty}
and

,

\displaystyle\psi(q;x)

(1.9)

\displaystyle=-\mathbb{E}\log\iint\exp\left(w^{q}(\alpha)\cdot\tau-\tfrac{1}{2}q(1)\cdot\tau\tau^{\intercal}+x\cdot h(\tau,\chi_{1})\right)\mathrm{d}P_{1}(\tau)\mathrm{d}\mathfrak{R}(\alpha),

where
\mathbb{E}
integrates the randomness of
\mathfrak{R},(\chi_{i})_{i}
and
H_{N}^{G}
so that

is a deterministic function.

Theorem 1.2
.

Recall that we assume (
1.2
). For every continuous function
G:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
, we have

(1.10)

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\lim_{N\to\infty}\overline{F}_{N}^{G}=\inf_{m}\sup_{x,\,p}\left\{\psi\left(q+t\nabla\xi(p);x\right)-\int_{0}^{1}\theta(p(s))\mathrm{d}s+m\cdot x-G(m)\right\},\end{split}

where
\inf
is taken over
m\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\sup
over
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
p\in\mathcal{Q}_{\infty}
.

When
G=0
, (
1.10
) boils down to the classical Parisi formula as written in
[
13
, Proposition 8.4]
. Note that here and in
[
13
, Proposition 8.4]
the convexity of
\xi
is assumed on
S^{D}_{+}
and not on the whole space
\mathbb{R}^{D\times D}
. This weaker convexity assumption should not be a surprise. Indeed, in the

case, it can be seen that the Parisi formula still holds assuming only that
\xi
is convex on
\mathbb{R}_{+}
as a consequence of Talagrand’s positivity principle, and this weaker convexity assumption is simply a consequence of a generalization of this observation (but with a different proof).

Throughout the paper, the free energy of models with linear Mattis interaction will play a special role. So for every
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we let

(1.11)

\varphi(x)=\lim_{N\to+\infty}\overline{F}_{N}^{m\mapsto x\cdot m},

and we let
\varphi^{*}(m)=\sup_{x}\left\{x\cdot m+\varphi(x)\right\}
denote the convex dual of
-\varphi
.

Definition 1.3

(Large deviation principle)
.

Let
(X_{N})_{N\geqslant 1}
be a sequence of random variables in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with associated probability measures
(\mathbb{P}_{N})_{N\geqslant 1}
and let
I:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to[0,\infty]
. We say that the random variables
X_{N}
satisfy a large deviation principle under
\mathbb{P}_{N}
with rate function
I
when for every Borel measurable set
A\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we have

\displaystyle-\inf_{A^{\circ}}I\leqslant\liminf_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{N}\log\mathbb{P}_{N}\{X_{N}\in A\}\leqslant\limsup_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{N}\log\mathbb{P}_{N}\{X_{N}\in A\}\leqslant-\inf_{\overline{A}}I,

where
A^{\circ}
and
\overline{A}
are the interior and the closure of
A
, respectively.

In the following, we refer to the large deviation principle as LDP.

Let
\left\langle\cdot\right\rangle^{G}_{N}
be the probability measure defined by

\left\langle f(\sigma)\right\rangle^{G}_{N}\propto\int f(\sigma)e^{H^{G}_{N}(\sigma)}\mathrm{d}P_{1}^{\otimes N}(\sigma).

Observe that
\left\langle\cdot\right\rangle^{G}_{N}
is a
random
probability measure. Recall that the random variables
(H_{N})_{N\geqslant 1}
and
(\chi_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
are defined on a common probability space
\Omega
. For a random variable
X:\Omega\to\mathfrak{X}
, we let
X^{\omega}\in\mathfrak{X}
denote the realization of the random variable
X
on the event
\omega\in\Omega
. Recall the mean magnetization
m_{N}
defined in (
1.5
) and
\varphi
defined in (
1.11
). Our next main result is a (quenched) large deviation principle for the random variables
m_{N}
under
\left\langle\cdot\right\rangle^{G}_{N}
.

Theorem 1.4

(LDP for the magnetization)
.

Recall that we assume (
1.2
). There exists a full-measure subset
\Omega^{\prime}\subseteq\Omega
such that, for every continuous function
G:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
and every
\omega\in\Omega^{\prime}
, the random variables
m_{N}
satisfy a large deviation principle under
\left\langle\cdot\right\rangle^{G,\omega}_{N}
with rate function

defined by

I^{G}(m)=-G(m)+\varphi^{*}(m)+\sup_{m^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left\{G(m^{\prime})-\varphi^{*}(m^{\prime})\right\},\hskip 10.00002pt\forall m\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

Recovering concrete models from the general setting

The main results of this paper are stated for very general models as introduced in Section

2.
Identification of the limit free energy with linear Mattis interaction

The plan to prove Theorem
1.2
is divided into two steps. First, we consider the free energy with
G(m)=x\cdot m
, namely,
F_{N}^{m\mapsto x\cdot m}
. In this case, we can think of
F_{N}^{m\mapsto x\cdot m}
as adding some linear random external field to
F_{N}^{0}
. For
F_{N}^{m\mapsto x\cdot m}
, since the linear field decouples easily in the cavity computation, the standard set of tools is effective with minimal modifications. Thus, the following result holds.

Proposition 2.1
.

Recall that we assume (
1.2
), for every
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
we have

(2.1)

\displaystyle\lim_{N\to\infty}\overline{F}_{N}^{m\mapsto x\cdot m}=\sup_{p\in\mathcal{Q}_{\infty}}\left\{\psi(q+t\nabla\xi(p);x)-t\int_{0}^{1}\theta(p(s))\mathrm{d}s\right\}.

Observe that at
x=0
, (
2.1
) reduces to the classical Parisi formula as stated in
[
13
]
. At
x\neq 0
the proof (
2.1
) follows the same arguments than for the classical Parisi formula. The proof is done in two separate steps, first to prove that the limit free energy is lower-bounded by the right-hand side in (
2.1
), one can write a variant of the so-called of the Aizenman–Sims–Starr scheme. Roughly speaking, the principle of the computation is comparing
\overline{F}_{N+1}^{m\mapsto x\cdot m}
with
\overline{F}_{N}^{m\mapsto x\cdot m}
by “integrating out” one of the spin variables. This leads to expressions of the form

(N+1)\overline{F}^{m\mapsto x\cdot m}_{N+1}-N\overline{F}^{m\mapsto x\cdot m}_{N}\simeq\psi(q+t\nabla\xi(p);x)-t\int_{0}^{1}\theta(p(s))\mathrm{d}s,

with some
p\in\mathcal{Q}_{\infty}
possibly depending on
N
, which yields the desired bound. The second step of the proof consists in verifying that the limit free energy is upper-bounded by the right-hand side in (
2.1
). To proceed, it is possible to use Gaussian interpolation as in Guerra’s original proof of this bound for the classical Parisi formula
[
22
]
. Alternatively, one can also use the more modern point of view in
[
27
,
26
]
which consists in verifying that the free energy at finite
N
is, as a function of
(t,q)
, a supersolution of a partial differential equation of Hamilton–Jacobi type with time parameter
t
and initial condition
q\mapsto\psi(q;x)
. The comparison principle then yields the desired bound.

Sketch of proof.

Since the external field
Nx\cdot m_{N}
is linear, the usual strategy — Guerra’s interpolation for the upper bound and the Aizenman–Sims–Starr scheme together with Panchenko’s ultrametricity for the lower bound — works without any significant modification. For completeness, we still sketch the key steps in the said strategy to highlight where the convexity is needed and where the linearity of the external field is used.
After these are clarified, we refer to
[
14
]
for the detail for the lower and upper bounds below (but in the setting of vector spin glasses without random external field).
For brevity, let us write
\overline{F}_{N}=\overline{F}_{N}^{m\mapsto x\cdot m}
.

Lower bound via Guerra’s interpolation
[
22
]
.

Let us display the dependence on
t
in the Hamiltonian in (
1.7
) by writing
H^{G}_{N}(\sigma,\alpha;t)
. Fix any
p\in\mathcal{Q}_{\infty}
and recall
\theta
from (
1.4
). We consider an independent Gaussian field
(V(\alpha))_{\alpha\in\supp\mathfrak{R}}
with covariance

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[V(\alpha)V(\alpha^{\prime})\right]=\theta\left(p(\alpha\wedge\alpha^{\prime})\right).

We also take an i.i.d. sequence of Gaussian field
(w^{t\nabla\xi(p)}_{i}(\alpha))_{\alpha\in\supp\mathfrak{R}}
indexed by
i\in\mathbb{N}
with covariance given as in (
1.6
) with
q
therein replaced by
t\nabla\xi(p)
.
For
r\in[0,1]
, we consider the interpolating Hamiltonian

\displaystyle H_{N}(\sigma,\alpha;r)=H^{G}_{N}(\sigma,\alpha;rt)+\sqrt{1-r}\sum_{i=1}^{N}w^{t\nabla\xi(p)}_{i}(\alpha)\cdot\sigma_{\bullet i}-\tfrac{1-r}{2}t\nabla\xi(p(1))\cdot\sigma\sigma^{\intercal}

\displaystyle+\sqrt{rNt}V(\alpha)-\tfrac{rNt}{2}\theta(p(1)).

With this Hamiltonian, we define the interpolating free energy

\displaystyle\phi(r)=-\frac{1}{N}\mathbb{E}\log\iint\exp\left(H_{N}(\sigma,\alpha;r)\right)\mathrm{d}P_{1}^{\otimes N}(\sigma)\mathrm{d}\mathfrak{R}(\alpha).

Then, we have

\displaystyle\phi(1)=\overline{F}_{N}-\frac{1}{N}\mathbb{E}\log\int\exp\left(\sqrt{Nt}V(\alpha)-\tfrac{Nt}{2}\theta(p(1))\right)\mathrm{d}\mathfrak{R}(\alpha),

where the term
\frac{1}{N}\mathbb{E}\log\int\cdots\,\mathrm{d}\mathfrak{R}(\alpha)
can be computed to be
-t\int_{0}^{1}\theta(p(s))\mathrm{d}s
appearing in (
2.1
).
Here, we used the independence between the Gaussian randomness of
V(\alpha)
and that of
\sum_{i=1}^{N}w_{i}^{q}(\alpha)\cdot\sigma_{\bullet i}
in
H^{G}_{N}(\sigma,\alpha;rt)
together with some property of the cascade to decompose
\phi(1)
into the two terms as in the above display.
On the other hand, using
w_{i}^{q}(\alpha)+w_{i}^{t\nabla\xi(p)}(\alpha)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{d}}}{{=}}w_{i}^{q+t\nabla\xi(p)}(\alpha)
, we have

\displaystyle\phi(0)=-\frac{1}{N}\mathbb{E}\log\iint\exp\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{N}x\cdot h(\sigma_{\bullet i},\chi_{i})+\sum_{i=1}^{N}w^{q+t\nabla\xi(p)}_{i}(\alpha)\cdot\sigma_{\bullet i}

\displaystyle-\tfrac{1}{2}\big(q+t\nabla\xi(p)\big)(1)\cdot\sigma\sigma^{\intercal}\Big)\mathrm{d}P_{1}^{\otimes N}(\sigma)\mathrm{d}\mathfrak{R}(\alpha),

which is equal to
\psi(q;x)
in (
1.9
) using the independence of random variables indexed by
i
. Notice that the linearity of the external field was used here. Then, the upper bound in (
2.1
) follows once
\phi(1)\geqslant\phi(0)
. To show this, we compute the derivative

(2.2)

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}r}\phi(r)=\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}\left\langle\xi(\sigma\sigma^{\prime\intercal})-\nabla\xi(p(\alpha\wedge\alpha^{\prime}))\cdot\sigma\sigma^{\prime\intercal}+\theta(p(\alpha\wedge\alpha^{\prime}))\right\rangle_{r},

where we used the standard tool of Gaussian integration by parts and
\left\langle\cdot\right\rangle_{r}
is the Gibbs measure associated with
H_{N}(\sigma,\alpha;r)
.
Notice that in the above display there are no terms involving self-overlaps

\sigma\sigma^{\intercal}
or
\alpha\wedge\alpha=1
, which might otherwise
arise from Gaussian integration by parts. This is because such terms are exactly
canceled by the derivatives of
-\frac{rNt}{2}\xi(\frac{\sigma\sigma^{\intercal}}{N})
,
-\tfrac{1-r}{2}t\nabla\xi(p(1))\cdot\sigma\sigma^{\intercal}
, and
-\tfrac{rNt}{2}\theta(p(1))
appearing in the Hamiltonian.
The definition of
\theta
in (
1.4
) and the convexity of
\xi
on
\mathbb{R}^{D\times D}
ensures that
\xi(a)-\nabla\xi(b)\cdot a-\theta(b)\geqslant 0
for every
a,b\in\mathbb{R}^{D\times D}
. Therefore, we get
\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}r}\phi(r)\geqslant 0
and completes the proof of the lower bound.

Upper bound via Aizenman–Sims–Starr scheme
[
2
]
and Panchenko’s ultrametricity
[
30
]
.

Let
(Z(\sigma))_{\sigma\in\mathbb{R}^{D\times N}}
and
(Y(\sigma))_{\sigma\in\mathbb{R}^{D\times N}}
be independent centered
\mathbb{R}^{D}
-valued and real-valued Gaussian processes with covariances

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[Z(\sigma)Z(\sigma^{\prime})^{\intercal}\right]=\nabla\xi\left(\tfrac{\sigma\sigma^{\prime\intercal}}{N}\right)\hskip 10.00002pt\text{and}\hskip 10.00002pt\mathbb{E}\left[Y(\sigma)Y(\sigma^{\prime})\right]=\theta\left(\tfrac{\sigma\sigma^{\prime\intercal}}{N}\right).

We can compute that

\displaystyle(N+1)\overline{F}_{N+1}-N\overline{F}_{N}=A_{N}^{(1)}-A_{N}^{(2)}+o(1),

with an error term
o(1)
that vanishes as
N\to\infty
.
Here,

\displaystyle A_{N}^{(1)}=-\mathbb{E}\log\bigg\langle\int\exp\Big(\left(\sqrt{2t}Z(\sigma)+w^{q}_{N+1}(\alpha)\right)\cdot\tau-\tfrac{1}{2}\left(2t\nabla\xi\left(\tfrac{\sigma\sigma^{\intercal}}{N}\right)+q(1)\right)\cdot\tau\tau^{\intercal}

\displaystyle+h(\tau,\chi_{N+1})\Big)\mathrm{d}P_{1}(\tau)\bigg\rangle_{N}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{and}\hskip 10.00002ptA_{N}^{(2)}=-\mathbb{E}\log\left\langle\exp\left(\sqrt{2t}Y(\sigma)-t\theta\left(\tfrac{\sigma\sigma^{\intercal}}{N}\right)\right)\right\rangle_{N},

where
\tau=\sigma_{\bullet N+1}
is called the cavity spin and
\left\langle\cdot\right\rangle_{N}
is the Gibbs measure associated with
\overline{F}_{N}
. Notice that
w^{q}_{N+1}(\alpha)
and
h(\tau,\chi_{N+1})
have randomness independent from that of
\left\langle\cdot\right\rangle_{N}
. It is important to note that we used the linearity
(N+1)x\cdot m_{N+1}=Nx\cdot m_{N}+h(\tau,\chi_{N+1})
in this computation, where
Nx\cdot m_{N}
is hidden in
\left\langle\cdot\right\rangle_{N}
and
h(\tau,\chi_{N+1})
is displayed in the above.

We can see
\limsup_{N\to\infty}\overline{F}_{N}\leqslant\limsup_{N\to\infty}A^{(1)}_{N}-A^{(2)}_{N}
. Heuristically, by passing to a subsequence, we may assume that the distribution of the spin overlap
\frac{\sigma\sigma^{\prime\intercal}}{N}
under
\mathbb{E}\left\langle\cdot\right\rangle_{N}
converges to that of
p(U)
for some
p\in\mathcal{Q}_{\infty}
where
U
is a uniform random variable over
[0,1]
. Also, we can
synchronize
the spin overlap with the cascade overlap of
\alpha
as spelled out in
[
32
, Theorem 4]
. Panchenko’s ultrametricity result along with Ghirlanda–Guerra’s identities tells us that this is enough to identify the limit of
A^{(1)}_{N}
and
A^{(2)}_{N}
, which are
\psi(q+t\nabla\xi(p);x)
and
t\int_{0}^{1}\theta(p(s))\mathrm{d}s
, respectively.

This completes the lower bound modulo many technical results. For instance, we need to add some perturbation to
\left\langle\cdot\right\rangle_{N}
to ensure that the above sketch works.

Relaxation of convexity of
\xi
.

In view of the above proof, the convexity of
\xi
on
\mathbb{R}^{D\times D}

is only used to ensure that the right-hand side of (
2.2
) is
non-positive.
When
D=1
, Talagrand’s positivity principle allows one to relax this assumption
and require
\xi
to be convex only on
\mathbb{R}_{+}
.
When
D>1
, one can verify that
[
13
, Proposition 8.1]

remains valid in the presence of the random external field
Nx\cdot m_{N}
,
which yields Proposition
2.1
with
\xi
assumed to be convex only
on
S^{D}_{+}
.

Indeed, the proof of
[
13
, Proposition 8.1]
is based on cavity
computations (similar to those used in the above proof of the upper bound) developed
in
[
13
]
, together with an input from
[
26
]

that provides an lower bound (in place of Guerra’s bound) via the solution of a
relevant Hamilton–Jacobi equation. The variational representation of this solution
is precisely the right-hand side of (
2.1
). In both
[
13
]
and
[
26
]
, the analysis is
carried out for vector spin glass models without a random external field. However,
as illustrated in the sketch above, the inclusion of such a field does not require
any substantial modification of the arguments.

∎

Recall that in (
1.11
) we have defined
\varphi(x)=\lim_{N\to\infty}\overline{F}^{m\mapsto x\cdot m}_{N}
. It follows from Proposition
2.1
that

(2.3)

\varphi(x)=\sup_{p\in\mathcal{Q}_{\infty}}\left\{\psi(q+t\nabla\xi(p);x)-t\int_{0}^{1}\theta(p(s))\mathrm{d}s\right\}.

As explained in the following lemma,
\varphi
is a well-behaved function of
x
. This follows from similar arguments as the ones for
[
15
, Lemma 2.2]
.

Lemma 2.2
.

The function
\varphi
is Lipschitz, concave, and continuously differentiable.

Proof.

We denote by by
\mathscr{P}(p,x)
the expression inside
\sup_{p\in\mathcal{Q}_{\infty}}\{\cdots\}
in (
2.3
), so that

\varphi(x)=\sup_{p\in\mathcal{Q}_{\infty}}\mathscr{P}(p,x).

Furthermore, we have
\nabla_{x}\overline{F}^{m\mapsto x\cdot m}_{N}=-\mathbb{E}\left\langle m_{N}\right\rangle_{N}^{m\mapsto x\cdot m}
, which is bounded uniformly in
N
. For any
v\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we can compute

(v\cdot\nabla_{x})^{2}\overline{F}^{m\mapsto x\cdot m}_{N}=-\mathbb{E}\left\langle(v\cdot m_{N})^{2}\right\rangle_{N}^{m\mapsto x\cdot m}+\left(\mathbb{E}\left\langle v\cdot m_{N}\right\rangle_{N}^{m\mapsto x\cdot m}\right)^{2}\leqslant 0.

Therefore,
\varphi
is Lipschitz and concave.

It is classical that the combination of convexity and differentiability implies continuous differentiability (e.g.
[
35
, Theorem 25.5]
). Hence, it only remains to show that
\varphi
is differentiable everywhere.
Fix any
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
A vector
a\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is said to be a superdifferential of
\varphi
at
x
when for every
y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

\varphi(y)-\varphi(x)\leqslant a\cdot(y-x).

Since
\varphi
is concave, it suffices to show that any superdifferential
a
of
\varphi
at
x
is unique.
For each
\varepsilon>0
, we choose
p_{\varepsilon}
to satisfy

(2.4)

\displaystyle\mathscr{P}(p_{\varepsilon},x)\geqslant\varphi(x)-\varepsilon.

Fix any
y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and let
r\in(0,1]
.
Using the definition of the superdifferential, the fact that
\varphi
is a supremum, and (
2.4
), we have

\displaystyle y\cdot a

\displaystyle\geqslant\frac{\varphi(x+ry)-\varphi(x)}{r}\geqslant\frac{\mathscr{P}(p_{\varepsilon},x+ry)-\mathscr{P}(p_{\varepsilon},x)-\varepsilon}{r},

\displaystyle y\cdot a

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{\varphi(x)-\varphi(x-ry)}{r}\leqslant\frac{\mathscr{P}(p_{\varepsilon},x)-\mathscr{P}(p_{\varepsilon},x-ry)+\varepsilon}{r}.

Using the expression in (
1.9
), we can compute derivatives of
\mathscr{P}(p_{\varepsilon},\cdot)
at
x
and verify that they are bounded.
Hence, we can use Taylor’s expansion of
\mathscr{P}(p_{\varepsilon},\cdot)
at
x
in the above display to get for some constant
C
independent of
r
and
\varepsilon
, we have

\displaystyle\left|y\cdot a-y\cdot\nabla\mathscr{P}(p_{\varepsilon},x)\right|\leqslant Cr+\varepsilon r^{-1}.

Setting
r=\sqrt{\varepsilon}
and sending
\varepsilon\to 0
, we can see that
y\cdot a
is uniquely determined. Since
y
is arbitrary, we conclude that the subdifferential of
\varphi
at
x
is unique, and thus
\varphi
is differentiable at
x
. As explained earlier, this implies that
\varphi
is continuously differentiable.
∎

Remark 2.3
.

Since
\varphi
is Lipschitz we have
\varphi^{*}(m)=+\infty
for
|m|>L
where
L
is a Lipschitz constant of
\varphi
.

3.
Proofs of main results

In this section, we provide a proof of our main results Theorem
1.2
and Theorem
1.4
. We will rely on the differentiability of
\varphi(x)=\lim_{N\to+\infty}\overline{F}_{N}^{m\mapsto x\cdot m}
as well as classical large deviation tools.
Namely we will use the Gärtner–Ellis theorem
[
17
, Theorem 2.3.6]
, Varadhan’s lemma
[
17
, Theorem 4.3.1]
, and Bryc’s inverse Varadhan lemma
[
17
, Theorem 4.4.13]
.

Lemma 3.1
.

There is a full-measure subset
\Omega^{\prime}\subseteq\Omega
such that, for every
\omega\in\Omega^{\prime}
,
m_{N}
under
\left\langle\cdot\right\rangle^{0,\omega}_{N}
satisfies large deviation principle with rate function
I
defined by

(3.1)

\displaystyle I(m)=\varphi^{*}(m)-\varphi(0),\hskip 10.00002pt\forall m\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

Proof.

Let
\Omega^{\prime}\subseteq\Omega
be the full-measure subset such that for every
\omega\in\Omega^{\prime}
, we have for all
y\in\mathbb{Q}^{d}
,

\lim_{N\to\infty}F^{m\mapsto m\cdot y,\omega}_{N}=\varphi(y).

Henceforth, we fix
\omega\in\Omega^{\prime}
and define for each
y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

\Lambda_{N}(y)=\frac{1}{N}\log\left\langle e^{Ny\cdot m_{N}}\right\rangle^{0,\omega}_{N}.

Notice that
\Lambda_{N}(y)=-F_{N}^{m\mapsto y\cdot m,\omega}+F_{N}^{0,\omega}
. Hence, we have for every
y\in\mathbb{Q}^{d}

\lim_{N\to\infty}\Lambda_{N}(y)=-\varphi(y)+\varphi(0).

Since
\Lambda_{N}
is Lipschitz uniformly in
N
and
\varphi
is also Lipschitz, the previous display in fact holds for every
y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Finally, since by Lemma
2.2
,
\varphi
is differentiable everywhere, the Gärtner–Ellis theorem (e.g., see
[
17
, Theorem 2.3.6]
) guarantees that
m_{N}
satisfies a large deviation principle under
\left\langle\cdot\right\rangle^{0,\omega}_{N}
with rate function

I(m)=\sup_{y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left\{y\cdot m-\lim_{N\to+\infty}\Lambda_{N}(y)\right\}=\varphi^{*}(m)-\varphi(0).

∎

Lemma 3.2
.

For every continuous function
G:\mathbb{R}^{\mathrm{d}}\to\mathbb{R}
and every
\omega\in\Omega^{\prime}
(given as in Lemma
3.1
), we have

\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{N}\log\left\langle e^{NG(m_{N})}\right\rangle^{0,\omega}_{N}=\sup_{m\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left\{G(m)-I(m)\right\},

with
I
given as in (
3.1
).

Proof.

Since
m_{N}
is bounded uniformly in
N
, so is
G(m_{N})
, which allows us to apply Varadhan’s lemma (e.g., see
[
17
, Theorem 4.3.1]
) to deduce the desired result from the large deviation principle in Lemma
3.1
.
∎

Using Lemma
3.2
in conjunction with Bryc’s inverse Varadhan lemma, we obtain a proof of Theorem
1.4
.

Proof of Theorem
1.4
.

Fix any
G
and let
\Gamma:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
be any bounded continuous function. Since

\frac{1}{N}\log\left\langle e^{N\Gamma(m_{N})}\right\rangle^{G,\omega}_{N}=\frac{1}{N}\log\left\langle e^{N(G+\Gamma)(m_{N})}\right\rangle^{0,\omega}_{N}-\frac{1}{N}\log\left\langle e^{NG(m_{N})}\right\rangle^{0,\omega}_{N},

Lemma
3.2
implies that for every
\omega\in\Omega^{\prime}
we have

\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{N}\log\left\langle e^{N\Gamma(m_{N})}\right\rangle^{G,\omega}_{N}=\sup\left\{G+\Gamma-\varphi^{*}\right\}-\sup\left\{G-\varphi^{*}\right\}=\sup\{\Gamma-I^{G}\}.

Since this holds for any bounded continuous
\Gamma
, we can apply Bryc’s inverse Varadhan lemma (e.g., see the “
\Longleftarrow
” direction in
[
17
, Theorem 4.4.13]
) which guarantees that
m_{N}
satisfies a large deviation principle under
\left\langle\cdot\right\rangle^{G,\omega}_{N}
with rate function
I^{G}
.
∎

From Lemma
3.2
, we can derive the Parisi-type formula for
\lim_{N\to+\infty}F_{N}^{G}
.

Proof of Theorem
1.2
.

Notice that

\frac{1}{N}\log\left\langle e^{NG(m_{N})}\right\rangle^{0,\omega}_{N}=-F^{G,\omega}_{N}+F^{0,\omega}_{N}.

Using Lemma
3.2
, we get that for every
\omega\in\Omega^{\prime}

\lim_{N\to\infty}F^{G,\omega}_{N}=\inf_{m\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left\{\varphi^{*}(m)-G(m)\right\}.

Finally, using the variational formula for
\varphi(x)
of Proposition
2.1
, we observe that the right-hand side in the previous display is equal to the right-hand side in (
1.10
).
∎
