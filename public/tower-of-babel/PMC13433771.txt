Intercellular chemerin-cmklr1 couples epicardial mechanosensing to fibroblasts in pressure overload
Authors: Guowei Zeng, Xinjie Zhang, Longming Huang, Kai Luo, Xiaoyang Zhang, Bo Chen, Cong Li, Haoyu Li, Jinghao Zheng, Bozhong Shi, Xiaomin He
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Mechanical sensation has become a recent focus and key pathological driver of cardiac fibrosis. However, the crosstalk between major mechanosensitive cell and fibroblasts remains to be elucidated. In this study, single-cell temporal atlas of mechanosensitive ion channels is depicted in transverse aortic constriction male mouse heart. Piezo1 is the most abundant mechanosensitive ion channel and exhibits the highest expression in epicardial cells. Epicardial-specific Piezo1 knockout mice exhibit decreased fibrosis and cardiac dysfunction. Piezo1 mediates epithelial-mesenchymal transition only contributes to subepicardial fibrosis. Further functional experiments and human samples validation reveal that Piezo1 facilitates intraventricular fibroblast activation via the Chemerin-Cmklr1 paracrine signaling pathway. The activation of cardiac fibroblasts is mediated by Pi3k-Akt1-Pou3f1 pathway. The Cmklr1 inhibitor α-NETA effectively mitigates myocardial fibrosis and dysfunction, holding therapeutical potential. Collectively, this study untangles the global landscape of mechanosensitive channels and the spatiotemporal mechanism of epicardial cells to activate fibroblast via the Chemerin-Cmklr1 paracrine signaling pathway.

Introduction

Myocardial fibrosis represents a common pathological outcome of various cardiovascular diseases (e.g., myocardial infarction, heart failure), with pressure overload-induced ventricular remodeling particularly characterized by diffuse reactive fibrosis, leading to increased ventricular stiffness and diastolic dysfunction1. Currently, effective therapeutic strategies targeting fibrosis remain limited, with most drugs exhibiting anti-fibrotic effects primarily through nonspecific off-target mechanisms. Although transforming growth factor-β (TGF-β) is widely recognized as a central regulator of fibrotic processes, direct inhibition of TGF-β may trigger adverse effects, including aberrant inflammatory responses, carcinogenic risks, and autoimmune dysregulation2. Consequently, researchers have begun exploring alternative or combinatorial therapeutic approaches for myocardial fibrosis. In recent years, increasing attention has been directed toward the biological effects of altered mechanical stress on the heart under physiological and pathological conditions3,4. Mechanotransduction plays a pivotal role in cardiovascular development, physiological function, and disease progression, prompting investigations into mechanotherapy as a potential strategy for myocardial fibrosis5.

Cardiac tissue is perpetually subjected to intricate hemodynamic forces throughout the cardiac cycle, with fibroblasts sensing extracellular matrix stress, myocytes experiencing rhythmic stretch and contractile forces, endocardial cells exposed to shear stress, and epicardial cells perceiving tensile strain and pericardial pressure3,6. These distinct mechanical stimuli across diverse cardiac cell types suggest substantial variability in the molecular mechanisms governing mechanosignal transduction and downstream biological responses. Intracellular transmission of mechanical signals in cardiac tissue has been demonstrated to involve mechanosensitive ion channels. For instance, endocardial cells utilize Trpv4 channels to detect shear stress and pressure7, cardiac fibroblasts employ Kcnk2 and Piezo2 channels to sense mechanical tension8,9. The key mechanosensitive ion channels driving myocardial fibrosis and their outcomes under various pathological conditions are summarized in Supplementary Table 1. However, the spatiotemporal dynamics of mechanosensing mechanisms at single-cell resolution in pressure overload-induced heart failure remain uncharted. The absence of systematic characterization of channel expression profiles across key mechanoresponsive cardiac cell populations represents a critical gap, potentially obscuring targeted therapeutic strategies5. Thus, elucidating mechanical stress signaling across cardiac cell types is essential to enhance our understanding of mechanobiological crosstalk and optimize anti-fibrotic therapeutic efficacy.

By depicting a single-cell atlas of mechanosensitive channel expression in a pressure-overloaded mouse model, this study identified and validated the pivotal role of Piezo1 in epicardial cells. Beyond undergoing canonical epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT), epicardial cells engage in fibrotic activation via intercellular communication with cardiac fibroblasts (CFs). These findings elucidate cell-specific mechanical stress transduction mechanisms and position the epicardium as a pivotal mechanosensory hub in fibrosis regulation, laying a theoretical foundation for the development of precision therapies targeting mechanotransduction pathways.

Results

Single cell atlas of mechanosensitive channels reveals Piezo1 and epicardial cells as primary target in pressure overload

To elucidate the expression dynamics of force-gated ion channels in pressure-overloaded left ventricle (LV), we utilized transverse aortic constriction (TAC) mice as a paradigmatic model. Single-nucleus RNA sequencing was performed on LV samples at the early stage (2 weeks), remodeling stage (5 weeks), and heart failure (8 weeks) stage. 14 major cell types were annotated (Fig. 1a; Supplementary Fig. 1a, b; Supplementary Table 2). Next, we examined the expression of 12 prevalent mechanosensitive ion channels in mammalian hearts10, revealing that Piezo1 was the most abundant in heart. Piezo1 is a mechanosensitive ion channel that regulates the influx of various ions into cells upon mechanical stimulation11. Furthermore, among 14 identified cell types, epicardial cells expressed the highest levels of Piezo1, suggesting epicardial cells was the most relevant with Piezo1-dependent mechano-sensation in pressure overload (Fig. 1b; Supplementary Fig. 1c). Comparatively, the expression of other well-documented channels in specific cells, including Piezo2 and Kcnk2 in CFs8,9, as well as Trpv4 in endocardial cells7, are lower than those of Piezo1 in epicardial cells. In pressure-loaded LV, the epicardial layer predominantly governs torsional mechanics and stretch force in the circumferential-longitudinal plane12, distinct from the shear and pressure forces experienced by the endocardium. The high expression of Piezo1 in the epicardial region, but not in quiescent or activated fibroblasts closely associated with ventricular remodeling, was further confirmed by immunofluorescence (Supplementary Fig. 1e). Notably, Piezo1 expression in epicardial cells remained elevated until the remodeling stage but was significantly downregulated during late heart failure (Supplementary Fig. 1d, f), potentially reflecting diminished mechanical stretch during the transition from concentric hypertrophy to eccentric dilatation. Thus, TAC 5 weeks was selected for subsequent experiments. To elucidate the role of epicardial Piezo1 in HF, we generated Piezo1fl/fl and Wt1-Cre (Piezo1CKO) mice (Fig. 1c). Specific knockout of Piezo1 in epicardial cell was generated by crossing Piezo1-floxed (Piezo1fl/fl) mice with Wt1-CreERT2 mice, thus allowing tamoxifen (TAM)-inducible deletion of Piezo1 in epicardial cell (Fig. 1c). Successful deletion of the Piezo1 was validated by both PCR and western blotting in isolated epicardial cell from mouse heart (Supplementary Fig. 2a–c). Immunofluorescence staining also demonstrated a significant reduction in Piezo1 expression in the epicardial layer (Supplementary Fig. 2d). At 5 weeks post-TAC, Piezo1CKO mice exhibited significantly reduced heart weight-to-body weight (HW/BW) and heart weight-to-tibia length (HW/TL) ratios compared to Piezo1fl/fl controls (Supplementary Fig. 2e). Echocardiography revealed that Piezo1CKO mice exhibited improved left ventricular systolic function and wall thickness (Fig. 1d; Supplementary Fig. 2f, g). Hematoxylin-eosin (HE) and Wheat germ agglutinin (WGA) staining showed that Piezo1 knockout in epicardial cells reduced hypertrophy (Fig. 1e, f). Picrosirius red, Masson, western blot, and immunofluorescence staining of fibrosis markers showed that TAC-induced fibrosis was significantly attenuated in the Piezo1CKO mice compared with the Piezo1fl/fl mice (Fig. 1g–i; Supplementary Fig. 2h, i; Supplementary Fig. 3a, c). Consistently, mRNA levels of fibrotic markers Col1a1, Col3a1, Ctgf, and Fn1 were markedly reduced in Piezo1CKO mice at 5 weeks post-TAC (Fig. 1j; Supplementary Fig. 3b). However, cardiomyocytes hypertrophy genes merely partly improved by Piezo1CKO, with Myh7, Mybpc3 did not change significantly (Supplementary Fig. 3b). Further examination at 8 weeks post-TAC revealed that Piezo1CKO mice maintained relatively less fibrosis as shown by Masson staining, but no significant difference was found in cardiomyocyte hypertrophy by WGA staining (Supplementary Fig. 3d, e). The differences in the expression of cardiac hypertrophy-related genes remained consistent with previous findings, with improvements observed only in Nppa and Nppb (Supplementary Fig. 3f). These findings suggest that epicardial Piezo1 may predominantly contributes to pressure overload-induced fibrosis rather than hypertrophy.

Epithelial-mesenchymal transition of epicardial cells only contributes partly to Piezo1-dependent fibrosis

Prior studies have elucidated the capacity of epicardial cells to undergo EMT following cardiac injury, contributing to fibrosis in subepicardial regions13,14. However, its role in non-ischemic fibrosis remains uncertain. To this end, we re-clustered epicardial cells into 5 subclusters (Epi1-5) (Fig. 2a). Enrichment analysis of marker genes revealed that Epi1 and Epi3 are subjected to signals such as mechanical stress, Epi2, Epi4 and Epi5 are associated with EMT, while Epi4 and Epi5 are subjected to inflammatory signaling stimulation and are responsible for synthesizing and secreting various proteins (Supplementary Fig. 4a). In the early stage of ventricular remodeling, the proportions of the Epi4 and Epi5 clusters exhibited a significant and dynamic increase (Fig. 2b). Pseudotime trajectory analysis revealed a transition from Epi1 and Epi3 subclusters, predominantly expressing epithelial genes (e.g., Wt1), to Epi4 and Epi5, which exhibited elevated expression of interstitial genes (e.g., Col3a1, Col1a1, Timp1, Fn1) (Fig. 2c, d; Supplementary Fig. 4b–f). EMT was validated by the following experiments in vivo and in vitro. In vitro, to simulate the pulling force of epicardium under pressure overload, epicardial cells were treated with a cell stretching device with cyclic mechanical stretch at 10% deformation for 12 h at 1 Hz15. EMT could be induced by stretching or Piezo1 agonist Yoda1 (Supplementary Fig. 5a–g), following calcium signaling pathway activation (Supplementary Fig. 5f), a well-defined bioprocess mediated by Piezo1 activation16. Furthermore, by applying mechanical stretch to epicardial cells isolated from the hearts of Piezo1fl/fl mice, we established that a 10% deformation stimulus was sufficient to mimic the EMT process under pressure‑overload conditions in vivo (Supplementary Fig. 5h). Ex vivo stretch of isolated human epicardial cells also induced EMT and altered the expression of marker genes among subclusters (Supplementary Fig. 5i–n). Silencing Piezo1 with siRNA abolished EMT in epicardial cells (Supplementary Fig. 6a–h), a finding that was confirmed using epicardial cells isolated from Piezo1fl/fl and Piezo1CKO mice (Supplementary Fig. 6i–k). Chelation of intracellular calcium with BAPTA-AM significantly attenuated EMT progression (Supplementary Fig. 7a–g). In vivo, compared with Piezo1fl/fl mice, Piezo1CKO mice exhibited attenuated reduction of E-cadherin expression and suppressed elevation of Vimentin expression in the epicardial layer after TAC, indicating Piezo1-dependent EMT (Fig. 2e–g). Furthermore, when a 15% stretch deformation was applied to epicardial cells, a parameter previously used to simulate the pathological environment of cardiomyocytes pressure overload17,18, no significant difference was induced in the EMT markers compared to that at 10%, which suggests that epicardial cells may possess higher mechanosensitivity.

After identifying EMT of epicardial cells may contribute to fibrosis under pressure overload, we injected adeno-associated virus 9 (AAV9)-CMV-DIO-GFP virus into Wt1- CreERT2 mice to label epicardial cells via GFP signals following TAM induction. This method utilizes the Cre-LoxP principle and has been applied in other disease studies19,20. The results showed that the GFP⁺ cell expression pattern did not change after TAC, with Col1a1 signals still confined to the epicardial layer and a small number of GFP⁺ Col1a1⁺ cells in the subepicardium, suggesting that the EMT process in epicardial cells may be limited (Fig. 2h, i; Supplementary Fig. 8a, b). Therefore, we further tended to use EMT inhibitor, Methacycline21 as an anti-fibrosis treatment. Methacycline was administered intrapericardially following TAC surgery to target epicardial cells, and was demonstrated to suppress EMT alterations in the epicardial layer (Supplementary Fig. 8c, d). Notably, PSR and Masson staining revealed that methacycline-mediated EMT inhibition attenuated myocardial fibrosis within 200 μm of the subepicardial layer but failed to achieve the same antifibrotic efficacy as Piezo1CKO mice, which exhibited significant fibrosis reduction both subepicardially and within the LV (Fig. 2j–m). These results suggest that while epicardial EMT contributes to localized subepicardial fibrosis, Piezo1 activation in epicardial cells likely promotes intraventricular fibrosis through alternative mechanisms.

Piezo1 activated epicardial-to-fibroblasts communication via Chemerin-Cmklr1 paracrine signaling

To investigate the potential alternative mechanisms epicardial cells-fibrosis interaction, fibroblasts were re-clustered into 10 clusters (CF1-10) (Fig. 3a). Based on functional effects on fibroblasts22, functional enrichment analysis of marker genes revealed that CF1, CF6, and CF7 were identified as myofibroblasts; CF3, CF4, CF8, and CF10 were classified as activated fibroblasts; and CF2, CF5, and CF9 were characterized as resting fibroblasts (Supplementary Fig. 9a). Among these, CF1 cluster experienced the most dramatic increase in TAC groups (Fig. 3b). CellChat analysis demonstrated that epicardial cells in TAC groups predominantly expressed extracellular matrix (ECM) proteins (e.g., Collagen, Laminin, Fn1) and secreted cytokines (e.g., Bmp, Pdgf, Egf, Chemerin) (Fig. 3c). Notably, Chemerin, encoded by the Rarres2 gene, displayed the highest differential expression among epicardial-secreted proteins and mediated fibroblast communication via the Cmklr1 receptor, with CF1 receiving Chemerin signals from multiple epicardial subclusters exclusively in TAC groups, unlike sham controls (Fig. 3d). Further analysis identified that Epi 5 exhibited the highest expression level of Chemerin, while CF1 also expressed the highest Cmklr1 level (Supplementary Fig. 9b). To ascertain whether Piezo1 regulates Chemerin secretion in epicardial cells under pressure overload, in vivo experiments revealed significantly attenuated Chemerin secretion in the epicardial layer, accompanied by a marked reduction in Cmklr1 expression in fibroblasts of Piezo1CKO mice compared to Piezo1fl/fl controls following TAC (Supplementary Fig. 10a–c). In vitro, Piezo1 silencing markedly suppressed Chemerin secretion in epicardial cells subjected to mechanical stretch (Supplementary Fig. 10d, e). To elucidate how Piezo1 regulates Chemerin secretion in epicardial cells, SCENIC analysis revealed that Cebpb exhibits high regulon activity scores in the Epi5 subset and represents a specific regulon for both Epi4 and Epi5 (Supplementary Fig. 11a, b). Ex vivo experiments demonstrated that Cebpb expression was significantly upregulated under cyclic mechanical stress (Supplementary Fig. 11c, d). In epicardial cells of Piezo1fl/fl and Piezo1CKO mice, this alteration was also observed (Supplementary Fig. 11e). Previous studies have indicated that CamkII binds to Creb and activates its phosphorylation level, and Creb is also a key target essential for Cebpb transcription23–25. Therefore, we conducted validation experiments both in vitro and in vivo. The results demonstrated that inhibition of Piezo1 gene expression significantly suppressed the activation of the CamkII/Creb/Cebpb/Chemerin signaling pathway induced by cyclic mechanical stress. (Supplementary Fig. 11f, g). Our in vivo immunofluorescence assays revealed co-localization of Chemerin secretion with the Epi5 cluster of Cebpb-expressing epicardial cells, as well as co-localization of Chemerin signaling with the activated CF1 cluster expressing Acta1, Timp1, and Ptgds. Consequently, specific Piezo1 knock-out in the epicardium markedly suppressed Chemerin expression, along with fibroblast activation in the subepicardial layer and left ventricle (Supplementary Fig. 12). These findings suggest that Chemerin, secreted in a paracrine manner by epicardial cells, may reach the myocardium of the left ventricle and bind to the cmklr1 receptor on fibroblasts. Silencing Cebpb via siRNA did not affect the cyclic mechanical stress-induced EMT in epicardial cells but suppressed Chemerin expression (Supplementary Fig. 13a–h). Furthermore, we demonstrated through CUT&Tag qPCR experiments that mechanical stretch stimulation significantly enhanced the binding of Cebpb to the Rarres2 gene (Supplementary Fig. 13i). After predicting the binding sites of CEBPB and the RARRES2 promoter region using the JASPAR database, a dual-luciferase assay was conducted to validate the direct binding effect between them (Supplementary Fig. 13j, k).

To further clarify the role of Piezo1-mediated epicardial Chemerin secretion in pressure overload-induced myocardial fibrosis, we administered exogenous Chemerin to Piezo1CKO mice subjected to TAC. Chemerin was delivered via intraperitoneal injection (initiated 2 days after surgery, 20 mg/kg every other day for 5 weeks) (Supplementary Fig. 14a). Compared with Piezo1CKO mice injected with PBS only, Chemerin supplementation exacerbated TAC-induced cardiac hypertrophy, as indicated by increased HW/BW and HW/TL ratios (Supplementary Fig. 14b, c). Echocardiographic analysis revealed impaired cardiac systolic function (Supplementary Fig. 14d). Histological examination using HE and WGA staining confirmed aggravated cardiac hypertrophy, while Masson’s trichrome and PSR staining demonstrated enhanced myocardial fibrosis following TAC (Supplementary Fig. 14e–k). Chemerin administration significantly upregulated the expression of fibrosis-related genes at both protein and mRNA levels (Supplementary Fig. 14l, m). These results demonstrate a significant correlation between Piezo1-mediated Chemerin secretion and myocardial fibrosis.

In ex vivo experiments, it was found that Chemerin treatment directly enhanced the migration and collagen contraction capabilities of fibroblasts, while also promoting increased protein expression of Fn1, Col1a1, α-SMA, and Cmklr1 (Supplementary Fig. 15a–f). Furthermore, compared to direct mechanical stretch stimulation of fibroblasts, the conditioned medium from epicardial cells subjected to mechanical stretch exhibited a stronger pro-fibrotic effect (Supplementary Fig. 15g–k). To investigate the effects of epicardial-derived chemerin on fibroblasts, a co-culture system was established using transwell chambers (Supplementary Fig. 16a). Fibroblasts co-cultured with Rarres2 (Chemerin coding gene)-overexpressing epicardial cells exhibited increased protein expression of Fn1, Col1a1, α-SMA, and Cmklr1, alongside enhanced migration and collagen contraction capacities (Supplementary Fig. 16b–k). In the opposite, fibroblasts co-cultured with Rarres2-knockdown epicardial cells by siRNA subjected to stretch stimulation exhibited suppressed expression of Fn1, Col1a1, α-SMA, and Cmklr1 proteins, along with diminished collagen contraction capacity and invasive abilities (Supplementary Fig. 17a–j).

To elucidate the in vivo role of Rarres2 in epicardial cells, we crossed Rarres2-floxed (Rarres2fl/fl) mice with Wt1-CreERT2 mice and employed a TAM-inducible gene knockout strategy to generate Rarres2fl/fl; Wt1-Cre (Rarres2CKO) mice, achieving specific Rarres2 knockout in epicardial cells (Fig. 3e), confirmed by PCR and western blot analyses (Supplementary Fig. 18a–c). Echocardiographic analysis revealed that compared with Rarres2fl/fl mice, Rarres2CKO mice exhibited improved left ventricular systolic function and hypertrophy parameters (Fig. 3f; Supplementary Fig. 18e, f), alongside decreased HB/BW and HB/TL (Supplementary Fig. 18d). Gross examination of whole hearts demonstrated that Rarres2 deletion in epicardial cells attenuated TAC-induced cardiac hypertrophy (Fig. 3g). Masson’s trichrome and Sirius red staining indicated that the extent of fibrosis was significantly reduced in Rarres2CKO mice compared with Rarres2fl/fl mice (Fig. 3h, i). WGA staining revealed that Rarres2CKO mice exhibited diminished cardiomyocytes hypertrophy along with fibrosis (Supplementary Fig. 18g). Further studies revealed that Rarres2CKO mice exhibited attenuated upregulation of cardiac fibrosis markers, including Fn1, Col1a1, and α-SMA levels at both mRNA and protein levels following TAC (Fig. 3j; Supplementary Fig. 18h). Immunofluorescence staining demonstrated that Rarres2CKO mice showed reduced Cmklr1 expression levels and ameliorated TAC myocardial fibrosis (Fig. 3k). The specific knockout of the Rarres2 gene in epicardial cells (mostly in Wt1high subclusters Epi 1, 3) might alter the secretory capability of Epi 5 (derived from Epi 1, 3 EMT). Epicardial cells may communicate and activate fibroblasts by Chemerin-Cmklr1 axis.

To clarify the role of epicardial Chemerin secretion in pressure overload–induced myocardial fibrosis, Rarres2CKO mice subjected to TAC were administered exogenous Chemerin using the same method as described previously (Supplementary Fig. 19a). Compared with Rarres2CKO mice injected with PBS alone, Chemerin supplementation exacerbated TAC-induced cardiac hypertrophy, as indicated by increased HW/BW and HW/TL ratios (Supplementary Fig. 19b, c). Echocardiographic analysis revealed impaired cardiac systolic function (Supplementary Fig. 19d). HE and WGA staining confirmed aggravated cardiac hypertrophy, while Masson and PSR staining demonstrated enhanced myocardial fibrosis after TAC (Supplementary Fig. 19e–k). Chemerin administration significantly upregulated the expression of Cmklr1 and fibrosis-related genes at both the protein and mRNA levels (Supplementary Fig. 19l–n). These results indicate that epicardial-derived Chemerin secretion correlates well with myocardial fibrosis.

Our findings prompt further investigation into whether Cmklr1 serves as an initiation for fibroblast activation. Compared to the control group, Cmklr1-knockdown by siRNA in fibroblasts exhibited markedly suppressed Fn1, Col1a1, and α-SMA, though co-cultured with Rarres2-overexpressing epicardial cells (Supplementary Fig. 20a–f). Additionally, these fibroblasts demonstrated diminished collagen contraction capacity, along with significantly reduced migratory and invasive abilities (Supplementary Fig. 20g–i). Furthermore, it was observed that the pro-fibrotic effect of TGF-β was also inhibited following the knockdown of Cmklr1 in fibroblasts (Supplementary Fig. 21a–e).

To validate the consistency of mechanosensation-mediated epicardial-fibroblast communication in other cardiac fibrotic diseases, we conducted verification in additional models. In the myocardial infarction (MI) model using Piezo1fl/fl and Piezo1CKO mice, a reduction in heart mass accompanied by improved cardiac systolic function was observed compared with the control group (Supplementary Fig. 29a–e). HE, Masson, and PSR staining demonstrated that specific Piezo1 knockout ameliorated cardiac dilation and partially alleviated the progression of myocardial fibrosis (Supplementary Fig. 29f–j). Immunofluorescence staining further revealed that the expression of cebpb, chemerin, Cmklr1, and pou3f1 was significantly decreased in Piezo1CKO mice after MI injury (Supplementary Fig. 29k). Furthermore, consistent results were observed in hypertension models induced by angiotensin II (Ang-II) and the two-kidney, one-clip (2K1C) method (Supplementary Fig. 30a; 31a). Compared with Piezo1fl/fl controls, Piezo1CKO mice exhibited reduced heart mass, improved systolic function, and attenuated myocardial hypertrophy (Supplementary Fig. 30b–d; 31b–d). Masson and PSR staining, along with immunofluorescence staining, also showed marked improvement in myocardial fibrosis, with significantly decreased expression of cebpb, chemerin, Cmklr1, and pou3f1 (Supplementary Fig. 30e–k; 31e–k). These findings suggest that mechanosensation-mediated Chemerin-Cmklr1 signaling plays a role in myocardial fibrotic diseases, particularly in pressure overload-induced myocardial fibrosis.

Cmklr1 inhibitor α-NETA demonstrates promising pharmacological effects in preventing pressure overload-Induced cardiac remodeling

To investigate the therapeutic effects of blocking epicardium-derived Chemerin binding to Cmklr1 signaling in CFs for pressure overload-induced ventricular remodeling, we evaluated the potential therapeutic value of α-NETA, a Cmklr1 receptor inhibitor previously applied in pulmonary hypertension and cancer treatment29,30, for TAC-induced cardiac remodeling. In this study, α-NETA was administered via intraperitoneal injection (initiated 2 days post-surgery, 5 mg/kg every other day for 5 weeks) to both TAC-operated and sham-operated mice (Fig. 7a). Compared with the vehicle control group, α-NETA treatment significantly ameliorated TAC-induced cardiac hypertrophy, as evidenced by markedly reduced HW/BW and HW/TL ratios, along with improved cardiac dilation observed in full-thickness cardiac tissue sections (Fig. 7b; Supplementary Fig. 32a). Echocardiographic data further demonstrated that α-NETA treatment improved left ventricular systolic function and attenuated ventricular enlargement (Fig. 7c, d; Supplementary Fig. 32b). Histological analyses confirmed that α-NETA effectively reduced myocardial fibrosis areas and decreased cardiac cell cross-sectional areas in TAC model mice (Fig. 7e–h). Notably, α-NETA treatment significantly downregulated protein expression levels of cardiac fibrosis markers (Fn1, Col1a1, and α-SMA) and gene expression levels of heart failure markers (Nppa, Nppb) (Fig. 7i, k; Supplementary Fig. 32c, e). The protein expression of p-Pi3k, p-Akt1, and Pou3f1 was also found to be suppressed in CFs from the TAC model (Supplementary Fig. 32d). Furthermore, immunofluorescence results revealed that α-NETA treatment significantly downregulated the expression of Cmklr1 and Pou3f1 in TAC-induced fibrotic areas compared with the vehicle group (Fig. 7j). Discussion

Mechanotransduction signaling has garnered increasing attention for its crucial role in ventricular remodeling31. However, current research on mechanosensory effector cells is predominantly confined to fibroblasts, cardiomyocytes, and endocardial cells, lacking a comprehensive characterization of mechanosensitive ion channels at the single-cell level. Moreover, the specific mechanisms by which mechanical signals mediate intercellular communication beyond extracellular matrix regulation remain insufficiently elucidated32. In this study, we identified Piezo1 as the most abundantly expressed mechanosensitive ion channel protein in the heart, with epicardial cells exhibiting the highest Piezo1 expression levels. The identification of epicardial cells as pivotal mechanosensory cells regulating myocardial fibrosis under pressure overload, a role not previously recognized. Piezo1 deficiency in epicardial cells alleviates pressure overload-induced left ventricular dysfunction and remodeling progression. Notably, selective inhibition of epicardial EMT alone failed to replicate this protective effect. This study also elucidated the previously unreported crucial role of Piezo1 in activating the paracrine signaling of epicardial cells, a mechanism distinct from and additive to its canonical role in driving EMT. Further analysis illustrated that epicardial cells additionally communicate with fibroblasts via the Chemerin-Cmklr1 axis and induce fibroblast activation through Pi3k-Akt1 pathway-mediated Pou3f1 expression. Collectively, this study identified epicardial cells as pivotal mechanosensory cells that regulate myocardial fibrosis under pressure overload, revealing a previously unrecognized mechanistic insight into the pathogenesis of fibrotic remodeling. The epicardium continuously experiences tensile forces and pericardial pressure throughout the cardiac cycle, which are distinct from the mechanical signals perceived by CFs in the extracellular matrix and the hemodynamic shear stress detected by the endocardium3,6. During left ventricular motion, torsional forces are generated through the synergistic contraction of subendocardial and subepicardial myocardial fibers, thereby effectively “squeezing” the left ventricle33. According to the results of a 2D-STE research, children with congenital valvular AS or coarctation of the aorta and moderate LV pressure load have higher maximal LV torsion than healthy children34. In this process, the epicardial layer exhibits mechanical dominance in controlling the rotational direction12. In previous studies related to myocardial fibrosis, it has been found that Piezo1 can mediate the response of fibroblasts to the surrounding matrix environment, thereby promoting fibrosis35. In diabetic cardiomyopathy, Piezo1 affects cardiac function and fibrosis by impairing mitochondrial function in cardiac cells36. However, these studies have not elucidated the cardiac expression pattern of Piezo1. In this study, we identified that the mechanosensitive ion channel protein Piezo1 is most abundantly expressed in the heart, with particularly high levels observed in epicardial cells. This finding suggests that epicardial cells serve as pivotal mechanosensory cells. Of particular interest is how epicardial cells respond to mechanotransduction signals under pressure overload. Prior research has confirmed that epicardial cell proliferation occurs under high mechanical stress37. During pressure overload, the mechanical signals perceived by the epicardium shift from high tension in early stages to gradual attenuation as ventricular remodeling progresses38. This study also revealed that the expression of Piezo1 in epicardial cells initially increased from TAC 2 to 5 weeks and subsequently decreased at 8 weeks, suggesting its potential involvement in early ventricular remodeling. Inhibition of Piezo1 expression may attenuate the progression of ventricular remodeling at the early stage, thereby preventing heart failure. Our results further demonstrated that specific knockout of Piezo1 in epicardial cells significantly suppressed the progression of early myocardial fibrosis, with the fibrotic area proportion decreasing by approximately 10%. Piezo1 inhibitors targeting the epicardium may represent a potential therapeutic strategy to enhance anti-fibrotic efficacy or improve the therapeutic effects of TGF-β inhibitors. Given the high expression of Piezo1 in epicardial cells, targeting Piezo1 inhibition for therapeutic purposes may render epicardial cells more susceptible than other cardiac cell types. Considering the potential risks associated with the clinical application of intrapericardial Piezo1 inhibitor injection, this approach was not experimentally validated in the present study but is worthwhile explored in subsequent research.

Epicardial cells originate from embryonic progenitor cells and play a pivotal role in regulating cardiomyocytes proliferation and angiogenesis during development. Numerous studies have confirmed their close association with cardiac development, homeostasis, and disease39. Although epicardial cells have been shown to modulate myocardial fibrosis via EMT in ischemic ventricular remodeling, their role in non-ischemic ventricular remodeling remains unclear39. As dynamic regulators, epicardial cells primarily function through cell differentiation (e.g., EMT) and paracrine signaling40. In our study, single-cell analysis revealed that epicardial cells do undergo EMT during pressure overload, transforming into a mesenchymal cell state. In a pressure-overload mouse model, we attempted to validate previous findings demonstrating that Piezo1 mediates EMT changes in epicardial cells via calcium influx. Previous studies have generally postulated that pressure overload primarily promotes the activation of cardiac-resident fibroblasts originating from both the endocardium and epicardium41. Through lineage tracing in mice, it was further confirmed that the activated fibroblast population is derived from endocardial cells42. This finding is consistent with our observation that although EMT serves as a significant contributor to myocardial fibrosis, inhibition of EMT in epicardial cells fails to achieve therapeutic effects comparable to those observed in epicardial Piezo1-specific knockout mice. We found that the epicardial cell population undergoing EMT accounted for only a minimal fraction of total cardiac cell types and was restricted to localized fibrotic areas beneath the epicardial layer. Selective inhibition of epicardial EMT failed to attenuate the degree of left ventricular myocardial fibrosis. Based on this observation, we therefore revealed that epicardial cells, upon mechanical signal transduction, regulate the progression of myocardial fibrosis through paracrine signaling mechanisms. This discovery enriches our understanding of the potential pro-fibrotic mechanisms of epicardial cells—mechanisms that extend beyond the scope of EMT, thereby providing important scientific insights.

Intercellular communication plays a profound role in both physiological processes and pathological conditions43. During ventricular remodeling in heart failure, CFs can be activated in response to diverse stimuli. While macrophages are known to directly or indirectly activate fibroblasts44,45, the involvement of other cardiac cell types in this process remains unclear. Epicardial cells, a pivotal cell population during development, retain substantial paracrine functionality even in their mature state, regaining their secretory capacity under pathological conditions46,47. In this study, we untangle the mechanism by which pressure overload-stimulated epicardial cells induce fibroblast activation via secretion of the adipokine Chemerin and its receptor Cmklr1. Furthermore, the secretion of Chemerin is mediated by Piezo1 through the transcription factor Cebpb. Chemerin, a multifunctional adipokine, serves as a pivotal regulator in maintaining vascular homeostasis, modulating inflammatory responses, and metabolic syndrome48. Previous studies have identified it as a circulating biomarker for chronic HF and systemic sclerosis-associated pulmonary arterial hypertension29,49. Prior research has also established chemerin’s association with renal fibrosis progression and its role in promoting fibroblast migration50,51. However, the precise role of chemerin in the progression of pressure overload-induced myocardial fibrosis remains unclear. In our study, we demonstrated that epicardial cells express and secrete chemerin. Notably, the gene encoding chemerin, Rarres2, has been previously characterized as a marker gene for epicardial cells52. The Chemerin-Cmklr1 signaling pathway serves as a critical downstream pathway of Piezo1-mediated epicardial function, and its significant role was further validated in our in vivo rescue experiments. Experimental inhibition of Cmklr1 using α-NETA (a selective antagonist) ameliorated pressure overload-induced left ventricular dysfunction and myocardial fibrosis. In contrast to the targeted epicardial therapy via intrapericardial injection, we opted for the safer approach of intraperitoneal injection to block fibroblast receptors and thereby intervene in the progression of myocardial fibrosis. α-NETA has been demonstrated to exert beneficial effects in the treatment of chronic inflammatory diseases, malignancies, and obesity-related metabolic disorders30,53. Furthermore, monoclonal antibodies targeting Cmklr1 are currently in preclinical development and may play a significant role in the future management of cardiac fibrosis. These results position Cmklr1 as a promising therapeutic target for future interventions against myocardial fibrosis.

Cmklr1, the primary functional receptor of the adipokine Chemerin, belongs to the G protein-coupled receptor (GPCR) family. Although Cmklr1 has been identified as an imaging biomarker for pulmonary fibrotic diseases, the mechanism by which its activation mediates fibroblast activation remains unclear54. Through further sequencing of the CFs in epicardial cell-specific Rarres2 knockout mice, it was observed that Cmklr1 promotes fibroblast activation by activating the PI3K-Akt1 pathway, thereby upregulating the expression of the transcription factor Pou3f1. This finding is consistent with previous research conclusions indicating that Cmklr1 can activate the Pi3k-Akt1 pathway, and that this pathway is associated with the expression of Pou3f155,56. Pou3f1, a member of the Pou transcription factor family, plays pivotal roles in behavioral regulation within the nervous system and tumor-associated inflammatory responses57,58. While prior research has not established a clear association between Pou3f1 and fibrotic processes, its family member Pou2f1 has been implicated in cardiac fibrosis59,60. In our study, suppression of Pou3f1 expression in fibroblasts did not affect the expression levels of the Pi3k-Akt1 pathway, demonstrating that Pou3f1 functions as a downstream transcription factor. Furthermore, AAV9-mediated specific knockdown of Pou3f1 in CFs effectively attenuated the progression of pressure overload-induced myocardial fibrosis, indicating that Pou3f1 serves as a critical effector in myocardial fibrosis. Through investigation of the intracellular signaling pathways in CFs, we further elucidated the mechanistic role of epicardial-fibroblast communication in regulating pressure overload-induced myocardial fibrosis, thereby comprehensively untangling the molecular basis underlying this pathological process. However, our experiments do not rule out concurrent direct mechano-activation of fibroblasts. Instead, we propose that our findings reveal a complementary, cell-extrinsic mechanism where epicardial cells, acting as specialized mechanosensors, provide a critical pro-fibrotic signal to fibroblasts. This concept may open alternative therapeutic angles to solve the limited efficacy of therapies targeting fibroblast-matrix sensing alone.

This study also has several limitations. First, the downregulation of Wt1 expression in mature epicardial cells may affect tamoxifen-induced recombination61,62, but no better strategy is currently available to specifically target mature epicardial cells. Nevertheless, the study still observed a significant and specific downregulation of Piezo1 and Rarres2 under pressure overload. This effect is likely achieved by inhibiting the EMT and subsequent secretory function of the Epi1 subcluster, a major epicardial subcluster characterized by high Wt1 expression. Second, genetic mouse models using Tbx18- or other epicardial marker-driven CreERT2 lines were not employed to verify and exclude potential contributions from other cell types; this will be further validated in future experiments. Finally, this study did not construct lineage-tracing mice to evaluate EMT changes in the epicardium. However, previous studies have confirmed that no significant migration of epicardial cells was observed under pressure overload, and epicardium-derived fibroblasts primarily proliferated in the upper region of the left ventricle41,42.

Collectively, this study untangles the pivotal role of epicardial cell-derived Chemerin as a key effector molecule in pressure overload-induced fibrosis under Piezo1-dependent mechanical signaling. Chemerin promotes fibroblast activation through direct binding to its receptor Cmklr1 and subsequent activation of the Pi3k-Akt1-Pou3f1 pathway. These studies untangle previously unappreciated perspectives on how mechanical force signals mediate the progression of myocardial fibrosis through epicardial cells. Furthermore, our study demonstrates that the small-molecule compound α-NETA may serve as a promising candidate drug for early intervention. However, several limitations inherent to experimental studies should be noted. Due to ethical constraints, a sufficient number of human cardiac tissue samples could not be obtained in this study. The precise proportion of epicardial cells undergoing EMT under pressure overload could not be determined, as epicardium-derived fibroblast lineage-tracing mice were not employed in this study. Piezo1 inhibitor therapy via intrapericardial injection was not performed due to its suboptimal safety profile, whereas systemic administration for heart failure treatment has been previously investigated. Addressing these aspects would further strengthen the reliability of our experimental findings.

Methods

Animal

All animal experiments were conducted in accordance with the National Institutes of Health Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals and were approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of Shanghai Children’s Medical Center (SCMC-LAWEC-2021-082). Male C57BL/6 J mice were used because cardiac fibrosis models in this disease context show sex-specific differences; sex was considered as a variable in the study design. The experimenters were blinded to group allocation. All mice were housed under specific pathogen-free (SPF) conditions in a controlled environment with a temperature of 23 ± 2 °C and relative humidity of 40 ± 5%. They had ad libitum access to water and food and were maintained on a 12-hour light/dark cycle. Mice were euthanized by cervical dislocation under isoflurane (RWD Life Science Co., #R510-22-16) inhalation anesthesia (2%). All experimental animals were included in the data analysis.

Human sample

This study involving human participants was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Ethics Committee of Shanghai Children’s Medical Center (SCMCIRB-K2022121-1). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants upon enrollment. Myocardial tissue samples were obtained from the left ventricular free wall of heart failure recipient before cardiac transplantation. Detailed patient information is provided in Supplementary Table 3.

Single-nucleus sequencing

After isolating cells from the left ventricles of mice in each group (n = 6), the raw sequencing data were processed using Cell Ranger analysis software (10X Genomics, version 6.0.2) to generate a gene-cell barcode count matrix for each sample. Following the removal of doublets, multiples, and apoptotic cells, the sequencing data were aligned to the mouse reference genome GRCm38 (mm10) (Genome Reference Consortium). High-quality cells meeting the following criteria were retained for subsequent analysis: detected genes >200, unique molecular identifiers (UMIs) > 1000, log10 genes per UMI > 0.7, mitochondrial UMI percentage <15%, and erythrocyte gene percentage <5%. The expression data were log-normalized, and highly variable genes were selected via principal component analysis (PCA) for linear dimensionality reduction. The statistical significance of principal components was determined using the JackStraw test. Unsupervised graph-based clustering was then performed using the selected principal components, with visualization achieved through the Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) algorithm. Marker genes for each cell subpopulation were identified using the FindMarkers tool in Seurat, while differential gene expression analysis across subpopulations was conducted with DESeq2. Pseudotemporal developmental trajectories were reconstructed through machine learning-based analysis of key gene expression patterns using the Monocle package. Intercellular communication networks were interrogated via the CellChat database by examining ligand-receptor pairs between cell types to identify the most relevant cellular interactions.

Construction of epicardial cell-specific gene knockout mice

Piezo1 conditional knockout allele mice (#S-CKO-07775, Piezo1fl/fl) were purchased from Cyagen Biosciences (Suzhou, Jiangsu, China). This model carries LoxP site insertions flanking exons 3 to 5. Rarres2 conditional knockout allele mice (#S-CKO-15117, Rarres2fl/fl) were similarly obtained from Cyagen, featuring LoxP sites inserted between exons 1 and 3. Wt1-CreERT2 mice expressing Cre-ERT recombinase (#NM-KI-200127, Wt1-CreERT2) were acquired from Shanghai Model Organisms Center (Shanghai, China). All three transgenic strains were maintained on a C57BL/6 J genetic background. To achieve adult mouse epicardium-specific Piezo1 or Rarres2 gene knockout, Wt1-CreERT2 mice were crossed with Piezo1fl/fl or Rarres2fl/fl mice, respectively. Cre recombinase expression was induced by intraperitoneal administration of 100 mg/kg tamoxifen (Sigma, Cat# 10540-29-1) dissolved in corn oil for 5 consecutive days starting at 6 weeks of age. Genotyping of different mouse strains (Piezo1fl/fl, Rarres2fl/fl, and Wt1-CreERT2) was performed using polymerase chain reaction (PCR) with the following primers:

Piezo1fl/fl: Forward 5’-AGCAAGGCCAATGTAGTATCTGG-3’, Reverse 5’-CTATTGGTGCCTAGTTGGCAGAC-3’ (210 bp product).

Rarres2fl/fl: Forward 5’-ATCATGGTCTCACCTTACAGACAG-3’, Reverse 5’-TTGGTTCTGGGGTGGTAGAGTG-3’ (249 bp product).

Wt1-CreERT2: Forward 5’-GATTCTCGGCGCTGGACTT-3’, Reverse 5’-GTGTCTCCGAGATGAACGCT-3’ (874 bp product).

AAV9 production and injection

The AAV9 vector carrying the Col1a2 promoter was constructed by Vigene Biosciences (Jinan, Shandong, China), including AAV9-Vector, AAV9-Cmklr1, and AAV9-Pou3f1. Six-week-old mice were administered the viral vector via tail vein injection at a dose of 200 μl per mouse (n = 10 per group), with a viral titer of 0.5×10¹² vector genomes. Transverse aortic constriction surgery was performed after a 2-week acclimatization period. AAV9-CMV-DIO-GFP was constructed by Vigene Biosciences. P1 Wt1-CreERT2 neonatal mice were subcutaneously injected with the virus at a titer of 0.5×10¹¹ vector genomes. At 4 weeks of age, tamoxifen was administered intraperitoneally at a dose of 75 mg/kg for 5 consecutive days.

Transverse aortic constriction (TAC) models

The surgical procedure was performed on 8-week-old mice in accordance with established protocols63. Briefly, the mice were anesthetized via inhalation of 2% isoflurane and immobilized in a supine position. A small thoracotomy was performed through an incision at the second intercostal space along the left sternal border. Under microscopic guidance, the aortic arch was carefully dissected, and a 6-0 silk suture was looped around the aortic arch segment between the innominate artery and the left common carotid artery, using a 27-gauge needle as a guide. The needle was subsequently removed to induce aortic constriction. Finally, the intercostal space and skin were closed in layers with 6-0 silk sutures. For the sham-operated group, all procedures were identical except for the aortic constriction step.

Myocardial infarction model

Mice were anesthetized via inhalation of 2% isoflurane, and anesthesia was maintained via an endotracheal tube. A minimal left thoracotomy was performed at the fourth intercostal space to expose the heart. The left anterior descending coronary artery was identified and permanently ligated using an 8-0 surgical suture. Following ligation, the thoracic muscle and skin incisions were sutured layer by layer. Postoperative analgesia was administered subcutaneously for three consecutive days, and the animals were monitored closely during this period.

Ang-II-induced hypertensive model

Mice were anesthetized via inhalation of 2% isoflurane, and an osmotic minipump (Alzet, Model 2004, USA) was subcutaneously implanted in the dorsal region. Subsequently, Ang-II (Sigma, # 9525) (750 ng/kg/min) was continuously infused for 4 weeks.

2K1C-induced hypertensive model

Mice were anesthetized with 2% isoflurane, and the right kidney was exposed. The right renal artery was then constricted using a polyurethane cuff (Braintree, #MRE025). Mice were assessed 4 weeks later.

Pericardial cavity injection

The mice were anesthetized and immobilized, followed by endotracheal intubation with maintenance of anesthesia using 2% isoflurane. After establishing the TAC model, the thoracic cavity was opened, and a PBS solution containing methacycline was carefully injected into the pericardial cavity with an injection volume of 20 μL. The thoracic cavity was then closed, allowing the experimental animals to recover spontaneously.

Echocardiography

Cardiac function was evaluated 5 weeks after TAC using the Vevo 2100 ultrasound imaging system (VisualSonics, Toronto, Canada). Mice were anesthetized with 2% isoflurane inhalation and placed in a supine position on a heating pad. Electrocardiogram and respiratory rate were monitored simultaneously. Transthoracic echocardiography was performed under conditions where the heart rate was maintained at 500-700 beats per minute (bpm) (Supplementary Table 4). Parameters were acquired and measured via M-mode echocardiography. The measured indices included: Left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF), Fractional shortening (FS), Left ventricular internal diameter at end-diastole (LVIDd), Left ventricular internal diameter at end-systole (LVIDs), Interventricular septal thickness at end-diastole (IVSd), Interventricular septal thickness at end-systole (IVSs). All data were recorded over at least six cardiac cycles.

Histological analysis and Immunofluorescence staining

The cardiac tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde overnight, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned longitudinally (5 μm thickness). Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining was performed according to the manufacturer’s protocol (Solarbio, #G1120). Picrosirius red (PSR; Solarbio, #S8060) and Masson’s trichrome staining (Solarbio, #G1340) were employed to evaluate cardiac fibrosis. Fibrosis area was calculated as the ratio of the collagen area to the total area. To determine the cross-sectional area of cardiomyocytes, tissue sections were stained with wheat germ agglutinin (WGA; Solarbio, #I3300) for membrane labeling and DAPI (Solarbio, #C0065) for nuclear counterstaining. The cross-sectional area (CSA) of cardiac cells was quantified using ImageJ software. For statistical analysis, the mean CSA value was calculated from 50–60 randomly selected circular cardiac cells per section.

For immunofluorescence, cardiac sections, epicardial cells, or cardiac fibroblasts (CFs) were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, permeabilized, and blocked with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) containing 0.3% Triton X-100 and 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA) for 1 hour. Subsequently, the samples were incubated with primary antibodies against α-SMA (Abcam, #ab124964), Col1a1 (Abcam, #ab270993), Fn1 (Abcam, #ab2413), Cmklr1 (Abcam, #ab306554), Vimentin (Abcam, #ab92547), E-cadherin (Proteintech, #20874-1-AP), Pou3f1 (Proteintech, #18997-1-AP), Cebpb (Proteintech, #23431-1-AP), Chemerin (Proteintech, #10216-1-AP), Timp1 (Proteintech, #26847-1-AP), Ptgds (Genuinbiotech, #5215), Acta1 (Genuinbiotech, #1971), Piezo1 (Proteintech, #15939-1-AP), Wt1 (Abcam, #ab89901), Periostin (Proteintech, #66491-1-Ig), PDGFRα (Abcam, #ab203491), Col3a1 (Proteintech, #22734-1-AP), and α-Actinin (Sigma, #A7732), followed by incubation with Alexa Fluor 647/488-conjugated secondary antibodies. After PBS washing, nuclei were counterstained with DAPI. Images were acquired using a confocal laser scanning microscope (Leica, Germany), and fluorescence intensity was quantified by blinded operators using ImageJ software.

Cell culture

The isolation of primary cardiac fibroblasts (CFs) was performed as previously described64. Hearts were harvested from 1- to 3-day-old C57BL/6 J neonatal mice, minced, and digested overnight at 4 °C in HBSS containing 0.01 mg/mL trypsin (Gibco, #15050065). The following day, the predigested tissue fragments were transferred to a solution containing 1500 U/mL collagenase type II (Worthington, #LS004176) and subjected to enzymatic digestion at 37 °C with constant agitation for 17 min. The resulting cell suspension was gently triturated and filtered through a 70 μm cell strainer to remove tissue debris. After centrifugation, the pelleted cells were resuspended in high-glucose DMEM medium (Gibco, #11965092) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco). The cells were then seeded onto 10 cm culture dishes and incubated at 37 °C in a 5% CO₂ humidified incubator for 1.5 hours. Non-adherent cells were removed by washing, and the adherent cells were identified as CFs. The immortalized mouse embryonic epicardial cell line (MEC1, #CTCC-001-0801) was purchased from Meisen CTCC (Hangzhou, Zhejiang, China). Human cardiac fibroblasts (HCF, # BNCC354381) were obtained from Bena Culture Collection (Henan, China). All cells were cultured according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Isolation of human epicardial cells

Human epicardial cells were isolated following a previously established protocol for epicardial progenitor cell isolation65. Samples of the right atrial appendage were obtained from transplanted hearts. The epicardial layer was carefully dissected as thoroughly as possible and minced into small pieces using scissors. The tissue fragments were then incubated in 0.01 mg/mL trypsin (Gibco, #15050065) at 37 °C for 10 min with gentle agitation at approximately 60 rpm. The supernatant was collected and transferred into complete DMEM/F12 medium. This digestion step was repeated until the tissue was completely digested. The cell suspension was filtered through a 100 μm cell strainer to remove tissue debris. Following centrifugation, the cells were resuspended and seeded onto gelatin-coated culture dishes. The cells were subsequently cultured in a humidified incubator at 37 °C with 5% CO₂.

Isolation of adult mice cardiac fibroblasts and epicardial cells

The isolation of primary cardiac fibroblasts and epicardial cells was performed according to previously established protocols using a Langendorff ex vivo perfusion system66. Mice were anesthetized with isoflurane and euthanized by cervical dislocation. The hearts were excised and mounted on a Langendorff perfusion apparatus. Perfusion was carried out with PBS at a constant flow rate of 2 mL/min at 37 °C for 3 min. The perfusate was then switched to a collagenase solution (containing 1200 U/mL collagenase II in PBS) for tissue digestion at 37 °C for 8 min. Simultaneously, the heart was immersed in a 5 mL EP tube containing pre-warmed collagenase solution to generate a constant, moderate shear force ensuring detachment of epicardial cells from the cardiac surface. The epicardial cells were collected via an overflow outlet at the top of the EP tube. The collected overflow was centrifuged at 300 × g for 7 min at room temperature, and the cell pellet was resuspended in PBS supplemented with 2% FBS and 1 mM EDTA. For cardiac fibroblasts, the atria were removed, and the digested ventricular tissue was transferred to DMEM medium containing 10% FBS to terminate collagenase activity. The digested tissue was gently teased apart with forceps and triturated 10 times to disperse the cells, yielding a single-cell suspension. The cell suspension was filtered through a 100 μm cell strainer and centrifuged at 55 × g for 1 minute at room temperature to separate cardiac fibroblasts from cardiac cells. The supernatant was then filtered through a 40 μm cell strainer and centrifuged at 300 × g for 7 min at room temperature. The supernatant was discarded, and the cell pellet was resuspended in PBS supplemented with 2% FBS and 1 mM EDTA. Cell purification was performed using magnetic bead-based removal of CD31-positive (endothelial cells) and CD45-positive (immune cells) populations. Subsequently, epicardial cells or fibroblasts were resuspended in DMEM containing 10% FBS, 100 U/mL penicillin, and 100 U/mL streptomycin for cell culture or downstream experiments.

Cell treatment

The small interfering RNAs (siRNAs) targeting Rarres2, Cmklr1, Pou3f1, CMKLR1, POU3F1, and Piezo1, as well as the Rarres2 overexpression plasmid and negative control plasmid, were designed and synthesized by Genomeditech (Shanghai, China). Transfection of epicardial cells and fibroblasts was performed using Lipofectamine 3000 transfection reagent (Invitrogen, L3000015). Cells were harvested 48 hours post-transfection and subjected to Western blot analysis.

Epicardial cells were first subjected to serum starvation for 12 hours in serum-free medium, followed by 24-h stimulation with 20 μM Yoda1 (Targetmol, #T7506). To evaluate the effect of intracellular calcium concentration on epicardial EMT, 10 μM BAPTA-AM (Targetmol, #T6245) was administered to both experimental and control groups during cell stretching. Cardiac fibroblasts were similarly subjected to 12-hour serum starvation in serum-free medium, then stimulated for 24 hours with 100 ng/mL chemerin (Targetmol, #TMPY-00367), 10 ng/mL TGF-β (Abclonal, #RP00671), 10 μM MK2206 (MCE, #HY-108232) or 10 μM LY294002 (MCE, #HY-10108).

Mechanical stretch application

Prior to the experiment, the cell culture chambers were coated with 150 µg/mL type I collagen (Corning, #354236) and allowed to dry overnight under sterile conditions. Epicardial cells or cardiac fibroblasts were seeded onto the collagen-coated chambers and cultured under standard conditions (37 °C, 5% CO₂). Upon reaching 80% confluence, the cells were subjected to mechanical stretch using the Cell Tank stretching system (Surface Force, Hangzhou, China). A sinusoidal waveform stretching mode was applied. Cells in the experimental group underwent cyclic mechanical stretch at 10% deformation for 12 hours at 1 Hz, whereas control group cells were maintained under static conditions for the same duration15.

Co-culture strategy for Epicardial cells and Fibroblasts

Prior to co-culture of epicardial cells and fibroblasts, the following pretreatments were performed according to the experimental design: Epicardial cells were transfected with either the Rarres2 overexpression plasmid (100 nM) or Rarres2 knock-down siRNA (100 nM) and their respective negative controls using Lipofectamine 3000 transfection reagent (ThermoFisher). Fibroblasts were transfected with Cmklr1 knock-down siRNA (100 nM) and its corresponding negative control. Stretched epicardial cells were then co-cultured with fibroblasts under different combinatorial conditions.

The co-culture system was established at a 3:1 ratio of CFs to epicardial cells. A 0.4-μm pore-size polyester membrane Transwell insert (Corning, #3460) was used, which selectively prevents cell migration while allowing chemoattractant diffusion. The upper chamber was seeded with 1 × 10⁴ epicardial cells, while the lower chamber contained 3 × 10⁴ fibroblasts. After 48 hours of co-culture, fibroblasts were subjected to scratch wound healing assays and immunofluorescence staining. For the collagen contraction assay, fibroblasts were trypsinized and centrifuged prior to experimentation. For Transwell migration assays, an 8-μm pore-size polyester membrane Transwell insert (Corning, #3464) was employed, with fibroblasts seeded in the upper chamber and epicardial cells in the lower chamber.

Ca2+ influx studies

To evaluate the impact of intracellular calcium levels on the EMT of epicardial cells, this study treated epicardial cells with Fluo-4 acetoxymethyl ester (Fluo-4 AM, Beyotime, #S1060). The experimental protocol was as follows: epicardial cells were first subjected to mechanical stretch or Yoda1 treatment, then incubated with Fluo-4 AM according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Changes in intracellular calcium ion (Ca2+) concentrations were assessed by measuring fluorescence intensity in response to cellular stimuli.

Transwell migration assay

Following co-culture, the cell chambers were carefully removed and fixed with 4% formaldehyde for 15 min. Subsequently, the cells were stained with 0.25% crystal violet for 15 min, rinsed with PBS, and air-dried before mounting. Finally, migrated cells were photographed under a microscope, and the number per field was quantified.

Collagen lattice contraction assays

Under sterile conditions, 10× PBS was mixed with 0.1 mol/L NaOH on ice, followed by the addition of an appropriate volume of Type I rat tail collagen (Corning, #354236, 3.34 mg/mL) to prepare a working collagen solution with a final concentration of 1 mg/mL. Fibroblasts after co-culture were digested and resuspended, then added to the collagen working solution at a density of 1 × 10⁵ cells/mL. The mixture was incubated at 37 °C with 5% CO₂ for 30 min to allow collagen release, after which an appropriate volume of culture medium was added. Collagen contraction was observed 24 hours later. Each experimental condition was independently repeated in triplicate, and all experiments were performed with at least three biological replicates.

Wound healing assay

For the processed fibroblasts and epicardial cells, when the cells reached 100% confluence, a straight linear scratch was created by vertically pressing a 200 μl pipette tip against the bottom of the culture plate within a biological safety cabinet. The detached cell debris was removed by PBS washing. Culture medium containing 2% FBS was then added. The scratch area was identified under microscopy, and at least three visual fields at marked positions were photographed for each sample, with six replicates per group.

Co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP)

Cardiac fibroblasts treated with Chemerin were washed once with cold PBS and then scraped in lysis buffer. After thorough lysis by vortexing on ice for 30 min, the homogenate was obtained and centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was collected and subjected to pre-clearing by incubation with Protein A and Protein G with rotation at 4 °C for 1 h. Subsequently, the pre-cleared lysate was incubated overnight at 4 °C with rotation using 5 μg of antibodies against IgG (Abclonal, #AC005), Cmklr1 (Abcam, #ab306554), and p-Pi3k (Absin, #abs130868). The following day, Protein A and Protein G were added to the lysate and incubated at 4 °C for 3 hours. After centrifugation, the precipitates were washed three times with wash buffer and resuspended in 20 μl of 1X SDS sample buffer. The samples were then boiled at 95 °C for 10 min to dissociate the proteins from the beads. Finally, the samples were analyzed by immunoblotting.

Quantitative real-time PCR

Total RNA was extracted from tissues or cells using an RNA extraction kit (Vazyme, #RC112-01) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Tissue homogenates or cell suspensions were sequentially subjected to genomic DNA removal and RNA purification. RNA purity and concentration were then measured using a Nanodrop spectrophotometer (ThermoFisher). Subsequently, 1 μg of RNA was reverse-transcribed into cDNA using a chain-specific cDNA synthesis kit (Vazyme, #R323). Real-time quantitative PCR amplification was performed using ChamQ Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme, #Q341) and gene-specific primers. The ΔΔCt method was employed for data analysis. Primer sequences for target genes are listed in the Supplementary Table 5.

CUT&Tag qPCR

The experiment was performed according to the protocol provided by the Hyperactive Universal CUT&Tag Assay Kit for Illumina Pro (Vazyme, #TD904). Briefly, approximately 10 × 10⁴ cells were collected and counted, mixed with activated ConA magnetic beads, and co-incubated overnight with primary antibodies against Cebpb (Proteintech, #23431-1-AP) and IgG (Abclonal, #AC005). After incubation, the supernatant was removed, and secondary antibodies (Vazyme, #Ab207) were added, followed by rotational incubation at room temperature for 1 h. The supernatant was again removed, and the ConA beads were rotationally incubated with pA/G-Tnp Pro at room temperature, then incubated with TTBL for 1 hour each at room temperature and 37 °C. Following TTBL incubation, 2 μL of 10% SDS and 1 pg of Spike-in DNA were added to each sample, followed by incubation at 55 °C for 10 min. Fragmented DNA in the supernatant was subsequently enriched using activated DNA Extract Beads Pro and identified by qPCR. The threshold cycle values and the 2–ΔΔCt method were used to determine the fold change in enrichment of the Cebpb binding pocket relative to Spike-in DNA.

Luciferase assay

A dual-luciferase reporter assay was performed to assess promoter activity in human epicardial cells. The cells were co-transfected using the following plasmid combinations: 100 ng of either the pGL3-RARRES2-Wild-Type, pGL3-RARRES2-Mutant, together with 200 ng of a CEBPB overexpression plasmid or its negative control plasmid. For normalization of transfection efficiency, 20 ng of a Renilla luciferase plasmid was included in all transfections. All plasmids were synthesized by Vigene Biosciences (Jinan, Shandong, China). At 48 h post-transfection, luciferase activity was measured using a dual-luciferase reporter assay kit. The relative reporter activity was expressed as the ratio of firefly luciferase luminescence to Renilla luciferase luminescence.

Western blotting analysis

Total protein was extracted using RIPA lysis buffer (Beyotime, #P0013B), and protein concentration was determined using a BCA assay kit (Vazyme, #E112-4). Protein samples were separated by SDS-PAGE (4–12% gradient gel, ThermoFisher, #NP0336BOX) and subsequently transferred onto PVDF membranes using an iBlot 2 Dry Blotting System (ThermoFisher). After blocking with skim milk for 2 h at room temperature, the membranes were incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C. Following washing, the membranes were incubated with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies for 2 h at room temperature. Protein bands were visualized using an ECL chemiluminescence detection kit (Vazyme, #E423-01). Data analysis was performed using ImageJ software.

The following primary antibodies were used:

α-SMA (Abcam, #ab124964), Col1a1 (Abcam, #ab270993), Fn1 (Abcam, #ab2413), Cmklr1 (Abcam, #ab306554), E-cadherin (Proteintech, #20874-1-AP), N-cadherin (Proteintech, #22018-1-AP), Snail (Abclonal, #A5243), Slug (Proteintech, #12129-1-AP), p-CamkII (Abclonal, #AP1386), CamkII (Abclonal, #A9196), p-Pi3k (Absin, #abs130868), Pi3k (Absin, #abs131773), p-Akt1 (Abclonal, #AP0140), Akt1 (Abclonal, #A17909), Piezo1 (Proteintech, #15939-1-AP), Pou3f1 (Proteintech, #18997-1-AP), Cebpb (Proteintech, #23431-1-AP), Chemerin (Proteintech, #10216-1-AP), Creb (Abclonal, #A27461), p-Creb (Abclonal, #AP0333), GAPDH (Abclonal, #A19056), and Tubulin (Proteintech, #11224-1-AP).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis results were presented as mean ± standard deviation (mean ± SD) and analyzed using GraphPad Prism 8 software (GraphPad Software, Inc). Data normality was verified by the Shapiro–Wilk test. For normally distributed datasets between two groups, a two-tailed unpaired Student’s t-test was employed; non-normally distributed data were analyzed using the Mann–Whitney U test. Multiple group comparisons were performed using one-way or two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s post hoc test, while non-parametric data were analyzed by the Kruskal-Wallis test with Dunn’s post hoc test. A P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.
