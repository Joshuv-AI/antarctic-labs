Suprathreshold non-volatile flavour perception is associated with multiple species rather than any single species via bacterial metabolism
Authors: Shuyuan Zhang, Saeed Shoaie, Guy H Carpenter
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract BackgroundThe microbiome is the characteristic microbial community inhabiting a well-defined habitat with distinct physicochemical properties. During eating, food is masticated and mixed with saliva to form a bolus; non-volatile flavour molecules are released into the saliva and delivered to receptors on the tongue. Throughout this process, the oral microbiome is in direct contact with non-volatile flavour molecules. In this cross-sectional study, we hypothesised that the oral microbiome interacts with non-volatile flavour molecules to modulate suprathreshold taste perception.Materials and methodsFifty-three participants were included, with suprathreshold sensory data, salivary microbiome data (including both taxonomic and functional profiles), and salivary metabolome data. Associations were explored using fixed-effects linear-regression models and unsupervised clustering.ResultsSuprathreshold non-volatile flavour perception was associated with the salivary microbiome via multispecies interactions rather than single-species effects. Taurine (an amino acid and confirmed bacterial metabolite) and ethanolamine (a phospholipid-related metabolite and confirmed bacterial metabolite) showed the greatest number of associations with suprathreshold taste perception in this study (6 of 8 sensory solution each).ConclusionThis study suggested that suprathreshold taste perception was associated with inferred microbial metabolic pathways via multispecies interactions. Because inferred microbial metabolic pathways are encoded by multiple bacteria, they may better capture multispecies contributions than species-level taxonomic profiles.Keywords: Flavour, microbiome, taste, saliva, metabolome

Introduction

Within food science, non-volatile flavour molecules perform several functions, including conveying the intrinsic characteristics of food and signalling the presence of rancidity or harmful substances [1]. In Western markets, particularly Australia and the United States, taste (subset of non-volatile flavour molecules) has been reported as the primary determinant of food choice [2,3]. In many European countries, taste ranks third, after quality or freshness and price [4]. From a biological perspective, humans exhibit innate predispositions towards sweet, fatty and salty foods, independent of environmental and experiential influences (e.g. living conditions and learning processes) [5]. From a psychological perspective, taste conveys a hedonic signal [6]. For example, sucrose elicits positive hedonic facial expressions in human infants, whereas quinine provokes aversive reactions [7].

Due to the close proximity of the oral microbiome to the taste buds, suprathreshold non-volatile flavour perception is increasingly understood as a function not only of host biology but also of the oral microbiome [8–10]. Previous work had linked microbial features to both threshold and suprathreshold measures of non-volatile flavour.

At the microbiome level, species-resolved profiles have shown weak associations with detection thresholds across the five basic tastes (sweetness, sourness, bitterness, saltiness and umami) [11]. At the genus level, weak associations with suprathreshold ratings for sweet, salt, sour and bitter had also been reported [12]. Beyond simple correlations, unsupervised clustering of the oral microbiome has been used to compare sensory outcomes, with genus-level associations observed for suprathreshold sour and bitter [13]. Collectively, studies from Italy, France and Spain, each enrolling large cohorts (N ≥ 100), suggest microbiome-taste associations exist at the population level.

A complementary route to mechanism is to examine the salivary metabolome, since whole-mouth saliva comprises a mixture of microbial metabolites, microbial catabolites and glandular secretions. Higher salivary glucose has been associated with lower (i.e. better) sweet-detection thresholds [14], and salivary sodium has been reported to correlate positively with the salt-recognition threshold [15]. At suprathreshold levels, a single exposure to citric acid solution (78 or 156 mmol/L) increase salivary sodium and reduce perceived saltiness [16]. Individuals with high sweet suprathreshold intensity have been reported to show greater tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle activity, whereas those with low intensity have been reported to show greater conversion of pyruvate to lactate [17]. Intriguingly, the use of nitrate supplements as an oral rinse has been observed to alter salivary sucrose metabolism, shifting it from lactate production towards the citric acid cycle, although no taste tests were reported [18]. However, these studies did not incorporate salivary microbiome sequencing, limiting mechanistic interpretation and precluding taxonomic or functional integration.

Nevertheless, important limitations remained. In the threshold cohort (France), associations between functional gene and taste thresholds were not examined. In the two suprathreshold cohorts (Italy and Spain), species-level resolution and functional gene data were unavailable because 16S rRNA sequencing provided genus-level information only. Accordingly, there is a need to investigate microbiome–taste interactions using functional data rather than relying solely on taxonomic proxies. The present cross-sectional study aimed to determine whether variation in suprathreshold non-volatile flavour perception was attributable to single-species effects or multispecies interactions, and to assess whether the oral microbiome or salivary metabolites contributed more strongly to suprathreshold non-volatile flavour perception.

Methods

Ethics, volunteer recruitment and study design

Participants data was pooled from three previous intervention studies (first study, N = 13: HR/DP-21/22-26684; second study, N = 20: HR/DP-22/23-35869; third study, N = 20: HR/DP-23/24-40263) only using data from before the intervention. Prior to recruitment, ethical approval for all three studies was granted by the King's College London Research Ethics Subcommittee and conducted in accordance with declaration of Helsinki. Participants were aged 18–65 years and had not had Covid-19 in the previous three months, had not taken prescribed medication within the previous six months, did not smoke, and reported no symptoms suggestive of taste or smell disorders. All three studies used the same inclusion criteria and experiment protocol. Informed consent was obtained from all individuals before participation. Fifty-three participants were recruited for the cross-sectional study. The results of the first and second studies have already been published [19,20].

Before the sensory test day (the final day, on which sensory data were collected for downstream analyses), the first three to four days were allocated to training to familiarise participants with the evaluation criteria using the same sensory solutions. Sensory testing was conducted once daily at a consistent time.

Saliva collection

On the sensory test day, participants first provided an unstimulated saliva sample. Before collection, each individual underwent a COVID-19 lateral flow test and was only allowed to proceed if the result was negative. Participants were instructed to abstain from eating for at least one hour and drinking for at least 30 min prior to sample collection. Each participant received a pre-weighed, sterilised 50 mL tube for saliva deposition and asked to expectorate unstimulated saliva into the tube for 5 min. If the volume collected was <2.5 mL after 5 min, participants were asked to continue expectorating for a further 5 min. Once collected, the tube was securely sealed and immediately placed on ice. The sample was subsequently weighed to calculate the unstimulated saliva flow rate (assuming 1 g = 1 ml; ml/min), aliquoted, and stored at −20 °C.

Sensory tests

Sensory testing was conducted between 13:00 and 17:00. Across three studies, each participant evaluated nine tastant solutions containing eight different molecules: 0.05 M oleic acid (fat), 0.08 M monosodium glutamate (umami), 0.0052 M citric acid (sour), 0.008 M caffeine (bitter), 0.25 M sucrose (sweet), 0.15 to 0.25 M sodium chloride (salt; EMSURE, Darmstadt, Germany), 1 ppm capsaicin (spice, trigeminal sensation), and 100 ppm menthol L pellets (menthol odour (trigeminal sensation + olfaction) and taste (trigeminal sensation); Symrise AG, Holzminden, Germany). Unless otherwise stated, all tastant compounds were sourced from Sigma (Gillingham, UK). Menthol odour and menthol taste solutions were prepared separately, despite containing the same concentration of menthol pellets. Because sodium chloride concentration varied across the three studies, sodium chloride data were excluded from this cross-sectional study.

The concentrations of the sensory solutions used in the baseline study were based on previous publications. Specifically, 0.0052 M citric acid, 0.008 M caffeine, 0.25 M sucrose, 1 ppm capsaicin, and 100 ppm L-menthol were selected because prior work indicated medium taste intensity at these levels [21]. The oleic acid concentration was set at 0.025 M, adjusted from a low–medium reference of 0.016 M [21]. A concentration of 0.08 M MSG was chosen based on existing protocols [22,23].

Eight of the tastant solutions (excluding oleic acid) were prepared by weighing and dissolving the compounds in 1.5 L of Natural Mineral Water (Buxton, Skelmersdale, UK). Given the low solubility of capsaicin and menthol in water, ethanol was added to aid dissolution (0.095% for capsaicin; 0.475% for menthol odour and taste; SAFC, Darmstadt, Germany). Sensory test solutions were stored at 4 °C until use. The oleic acid solution was freshly prepared before each sensory test by emulsifying oleic acid in mineral water. Due to supplier issues, still mineral water (Evian, Trowbridge, UK) was used instead in the second and third intervention studies, including for all sensory solutions.

The order of presentation was structured as follows: fat, umami, sour, bitter, sweet, spice, menthol odour, and menthol taste. Non-volatile flavour intensity assessments were conducted using the generalised labelled visual analogue scale (glVAS). To minimise carryover effects, participants observed a 30-s rest period before tasting each solution. Participants rated the maximum perceived intensity while holding the solution (10 s) in their mouth using the glVAS scale. To ensure consistency, each participant completed the sensory test at approximately the same time each day, with a maximum variation of two hours from their previous session.

NMR tests

A 0.5 mL aliquot of saliva supernatant from each sample was combined with 0.125 mL of TSP buffer. The TSP buffer comprised 0.03125 mL deionised water, 0.03125 mL of 2 mM sodium trimethylsilyl-[2,2,3,3-²H₄]-propionate (Sigma, UK), 0.0625 mL deuterium oxide (Sigma, UK), 28.4 mg/mL Na₂HPO₄ (Sigma, UK), and 5.28 mg/mL NaH₂PO₄ (Sigma, UK). The resulting mixture was then transferred into a 5 mm NMR tube (Bruker, Coventry, UK). Each tube was securely sealed and analysed using a 600 MHz spectrometer for the first and third studies and a 700 MHz spectrometer for the second study (Bruker, UK). Both ¹H 1D-NMR and 2D-NMR analyses were conducted at the Biomolecular Spectroscopy Centre, King's College London, UK.

Metabolite concentrations were quantified using Chenomix NMR Suite version 9.0 (Chenomix Ltd., Edmonton, Canada), while 2D-NMR, include homonuclear j-resolved spectroscopy (JRES) and total correlation spectroscopy (TOCSY), were processed with TopSpin version 3.6.5 (Bruker, UK). The final NMR metabolite list, consisting of 36 salivary metabolites, was validated using 2D-NMR, the Human Metabolome Database 5.0, and relevant literature [24–28]. Spectrometer settings for the 600 and 700 MHz analyses followed previously published protocols [29]. To minimise interference from the water peak in saliva samples, the volume of deuterium oxide was increased from 0.0625 mL to 0.09375 mL without deionised water in the second and third studies.

DNA extraction, shotgun metagenomic sequencing, taxonomic and functional profiling

The GenElute Bacterial Genomic DNA Kit (Sigma, UK) was used to extract DNA from saliva samples collected during the study. The quality of DNA was assessed using a NanoDrop 1000 Spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific, Basingstoke, UK), and the quantity was measured using a Qubit High Sensitivity DNA Quantification Assay Kit (Thermo Scientific, UK). During DNA extraction, DNase-free water (Sigma, UK) was used as a negative control to indicate cross-contamination. Shotgun sequencing was performed at Novogene (Cambridge, UK) using the NovaSeq 6000 system for the first study and the NovaSeq X Plus system for the second and third study (Illumina, Cambridge, UK). Sequencing was conducted using a 150 paired-end approach, generating an average of 53.8 ± 9.9 million reads per sample.

Sequencing documents were processed using bioBakery Workflows [30]. In brief, KneadData 0.12.1 was used to remove low-quality reads, adaptors, repetitive sequences. Host genomes, as well as microbial ribosomal and structural RNAs, were removed using the expanded human reference genome (hg37_and_human_contamination) and the SILVA Ribosomal RNA reference database via Bowtie 2 in KneadData [31–33]. The quality of the sequencing documents was checked with FastQC and MultiQC [34,35]. Taxonomic profiling was performed using MetaPhlAn 4.1.1 [36], while functional profiling was carried out with HUMAnN 3.9, using the ChocoPhlAn and full UniRef90 databases [30]. The final functional gene abundances were provided in Kyoto Encyclopaedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG), MetaCyc reaction (RXN) and Enzyme Commission (EC) features. The final inferred microbial metabolic pathway abundances were provided in RXN features. The functional gene and inferred microbial metabolic pathways abundances were normalised to 1 using total sum scaling during the functional profiling process.

Statistics analysis

Statistical analyses and figure generation were performed using GraphPad Prism (La Jolla, USA) and RStudio. The Maaslin2 test was applied to assess changes in individual tastants [37]. Mann–Whitney test was applied in conjunction with Maaslin2 results.

Functional gene, RXN feature inferred microbial metabolic pathway abundance, species, and metabolite data were analysed using Maaslin2. Beta diversity (Euclidean distance and Bray–Curtis distance) calculations were conducted using the vegan package [38]. Principal coordinates analysis (PCoA) was used to visualise between sample variation in microbial community structure.

Hierarchical divisive clustering analysis, based on Euclidean distances (cluster package, version 2.1.6), was applied to evaluate participants functional gene, RXN feature inferred microbial metabolic pathway abundance, species, and metabolite data throughout the study.

The relationships between taxonomic-non-volatile flavour, metabolic pathway-non-volatile flavour, and metabolite-non-volatile flavour were examined using a fixed-effects linear regression model in the lme4 package (version 1.1–35.5). Before applying the fixed-effects model, preliminary linear regression analyses without adjustments were conducted to exclude irrelevant variables. All data were standardised to a mean of 0 and a standard deviation of 1 to ensure comparability. For the three intervention studies, mixed-effects linear-regression models were used to investigate these associations. Only species-level taxonomic profiles and inferred microbial metabolic pathway abundance data with at least 10 non-zero (20% prevalence threshold) and 27 non-zero (50% prevalence threshold) values were included in the final analysis. Adjusted p-values were obtained using the Benjamini–Hochberg method.

Results

Species and inferred microbial metabolic pathway summary

In this study, 468 species were annotated by MetaPhlAn 4 (Figure 1A). The median species prevalence was 14.2% across samples, and 18.8% of species were sample-specific. Both values were higher than those reported for the gut species-level taxonomic profiles in the TwinsUK study (median species prevalence 2.7%, with 12% of species sample-specific) [39]. This pattern is consistent with the typical properties of the salivary species-level taxonomic profiles, which generally exhibits higher alpha diversity (greater richness) but lower beta diversity (greater similarity across individuals) than stool-based gut species-level taxonomic profiles [40]. Six taxa were ubiquitous in all saliva samples (prevalence = 1.0): Streptococcus oralis, Streptococcus mitis, Neisseria subflava, Granulicatella sp001071995, Rothia mucilaginosa, and Rothia dentocariosa.

Dataset summary. (a) Species level taxon prevalence distribution across the dataset; (b) inferred microbial metabolic pathway prevalence distribution across the dataset.

At the inferred microbial metabolic pathway level, 352 inferred microbial metabolic pathways were annotated by HUMAnN 3 (Figure 1B). The median inferred microbial metabolic pathway prevalence was 98.1%, with only 4.3% of pathways being sample-specific. Nearly half of the inferred microbial metabolic pathways were present in all samples (175 out of 365 inferred microbial metabolic pathways). The overall consistency of inferred microbial metabolic pathway is comparable to that observed in the MOAFTS and HMP cohorts [40,41].

Inferred microbial metabolic pathway profiles derived from salivary species-level taxonomic profiles were associated with suprathreshold non-volatile flavour perception

Using a 50% prevalence threshold, 102 significant pathway–non-volatile flavour associations were identified at the statistical level (adj p < 0.05; Supplementary Table 1). When relative abundance was additionally considered, 46 of these 102 associations involved inferred microbial metabolic pathways among the 50 most abundant (Figure 2A). Each of the top 50 inferred microbial metabolic pathways had a mean relative abundance at least twice the filtered dataset-wide mean. Taken together, these findings suggested that inferred microbial metabolic pathways were associated with suprathreshold non-volatile flavour perception. At the tastant level, most associations involved citric acid; among the remaining tastants, all but oleic acid and menthol odour showed similar counts (around 10–15 significant associations) (Supplementary Figure 1A).

Using an unsupervised clustering approach on inferred microbial metabolic pathway profiles, samples were partitioned into four inferred microbial metabolic pathway clusters (IMPCs; Figure 2B and Supplementary Figure 2). When these cluster labels were mapped onto the sensory data, citric-acid perception in IMPC 3 was higher than in IMPC 1 and IMPC 4 in both MaAsLin2 and the Mann–Whitney U test (p < 0.05) (Figure 2C). In MaAsLin2 only, citric-acid perception in IMPC 3 also exceeded that in IMPC 2, and menthol perception in IMPC 3 exceeded that in IMPC 4 (Figure 2C and D).

Feature-wise (single-microbial metabolic pathway) analyses indicated that IMPC 3 differed systematically from IMPCs 1, 2, and 4: 502 of 954 conditions were significant (p < 0.05; adj p < 0.1) using a 10% prevalence filter with IMPC 3 as the reference (Supplementary Figure 3). Overall, significant microbial metabolic pathway abundance in IMPC 3 were lower than in IMPCs 1, 2, and 4; IMPC 1 had the highest significant microbial metabolic pathway abundance (Supplementary Figure 3–5).

Because inferred microbial metabolic pathways were defined by oral bacteria, two pathways—PWY-6121 (5-aminoimidazole ribonucleotide biosynthesis I) and PWY-6122 (5-aminoimidazole ribonucleotide biosynthesis II)—were selected to characterise bacterial contributions across samples. These inferred microbial metabolic pathways were chosen because they were significantly associated (adj p < 0.05) with three non-volatile flavour molecules (citric acid, sucrose, and capsaicin). Inferred microbial metabolic pathway characterisation suggested that no single dominant bacterial taxon defined these inferred microbial metabolic pathways; rather, contributions were distributed across multiple species and genera (Figure 2E and Supplementary Figure 6).

Salivary species-level taxonomic profiles showed limited association with suprathreshold non-volatile flavour perception

Using a 50% prevalence threshold, 19 significant species-level taxonomic–non-volatile flavour associations were identified at the statistical level (adj p < 0.05; Supplementary Table 2). When relative abundance was additionally considered, 12 of these 19 associations involved species among the 50 most abundant (Figure 3). Rothia dentocariosa (SGB49305 in Figure 3), Neisseria sicca and Gemella haemolysans was associated with at least two tastants (Rothia dentocariosa: oleic acid and sucrose, Neisseria sicca: oleic acid and citric acid; Gemella haemolysans: caffeine, sucrose, menthol odour and menthol taste). At the species level, only Neisseria sicca has previously been reported, and only as being negatively correlated with bitter detection thresholds [11]. No study has reported associations for the other two species at either threshold or suprathreshold level.

Overall, Streptococcus mitis, Rothia mucilaginosa, and Neisseria subflava were the most abundant taxa in this cohort, together accounting for about 20% of the salivary species-level taxonomic profiles (Figure 3). For the species-level taxonomic profiles, unsupervised clustering identified six or ten clusters at different resolutions (Supplementary Figure 7). However, suprathreshold taste perception did not differ across species-level taxonomic profile clusters (p > 0.05). These results suggested that species-level taxonomic information alone was insufficient to establish robust associations with suprathreshold non-volatile flavour perception.

Multiple salivary metabolites were associated with suprathreshold non-volatile flavour perception

In metabolome-non-volatile flavour fixed effect linear regression model, most associations involved amines and amino acids (Supplementary Tables 3 and 4): 29 of 49 (≈59.2%), all positive. On the gustatory side, most associations were observed for oleic acid and sucrose (Supplementary Figure 1C). For the trigeminal modality, menthol odour, menthol taste, and capsaicin showed strong associations with salivary metabolites (Figure 4A). Among high-abundance salivary metabolites (mean concentration above the dataset-wide mean), acetate only showed a positive association with menthol taste. Ethanolamine and taurine showed the largest number of significant associations with non-volatile flavour molecules (n = 6 each; adj p < 0.05).

Unsupervised clustering of the metabolite data identified four metabolite clusters (MBC 1–4) (Figure 4B and Supplementary Figure 8). When mapped onto the sensory data, MBC2 showed higher perception intensity than MBC1 for oleic acid, citric acid, sucrose, and menthol (odour and taste) in both MaAsLin2 and the Mann–Whitney U test (p < 0.05) (Figure 4C–E and Supplementary Figure 9A and B). Feature-wise (single-metabolite) analysis via MaAsLin2 indicated systematic differences between MBC2 and MBC1; in general, MBC2 exhibited higher concentrations of short-chain fatty acids and amino acids (Supplementary Figures 10 and 11). Overall, median metabolite concentrations in MBC2 lay between those of MBC1 and MBC3. Together, these findings indicated that suprathreshold taste intensity was associated with multiple salivary metabolites rather than any single metabolite. Notably, metabolite clusters and inferred microbial metabolic pathway clusters showed different distributions (Supplementary Figure 9C), indicating that they captured distinct aspects of the data. This observation was consistent with previous work showing that metabolites are not equivalent to functional genes [42].

Inferred microbial metabolic pathway is better predictor than species level taxonomy information

Fixed-effects linear-regression results were compared for metabolite–taste, metabolic pathway–taste, and species-level taxonomy–taste associations at the cohort level. Generally, inferred microbial metabolic pathways showed a greater number of significant associations (adj p < 0.05) compared to the species-level taxonomic profiles and salivary metabolites (Figure 5A and B). When the sum of estimated effects across different conditions was calculated, inferred microbial metabolic pathways exhibited a strong negative direction of association, while salivary metabolites showed a strong positive direction; in contrast, the species-level taxonomic profiles displayed no clear directional trend. The predominance of positive metabolite associations, contrasted with negative microbial metabolic pathway associations, suggests that the measured salivary metabolites were more likely pathway substrates than end-products. For example, urea can be produced by oral bacteria and subsequently hydrolysed by bacterial urease for further utilisation [43,44].

From a statistical perspective, for a given species or inferred microbial metabolic pathway to be meaningfully associated with a particular tastant, it must be ubiquitous within the dataset—a necessary condition. We further tested this by fitting baseline fixed-effects regression models. Under more stringent filtering (raising the non-zero count threshold from 10 to 27, i.e. features present in more than half of the dataset), species-level analysis yielded 19 significant associations (adj p < 0.05), whereas inferred microbial metabolic pathways retained 102 significant associations (Supplementary Tables 1, 2 and 4). In the less stringent model (non-zero count threshold = 10), 62 species-level associations and 105 pathway-level associations reached significance (Figure 5A and B). Taken together, these findings suggested that species were poorer predictors than inferred microbial metabolic pathways, owing to their greater variability (Figure 1).

Notably, adjusted R square (a measure of model fit) did not differ significantly across the three predictor sets (salivary metabolites, species-level taxonomic profiles, and inferred microbial metabolic pathways; p > 0.05), suggesting broadly similar predictive performance (Figure 5C). Accordingly, the apparent advantage of inferred microbial metabolic pathways over species-level taxonomy is more attributable to lower feature variability and greater prevalence, rather than to superior overall model fit. Moreover, because inferred microbial metabolic pathways capture multispecies functional contributions, they better reflect biological effects that are not well captured by single-taxon associations. In this cross-sectional study, all significant models (adj p < 0.05) exceeded effect-size benchmarks reported for at least 50% of published studies [45].

Suprathreshold non-volatile flavour sensitive tasters and insensitive tasters differed in inferred microbial metabolic pathways and metabolomic features, rather than in taxonomic feature

Using the classification of supertasters and non-tasters adapted from a previous publication [46]: a modality score (the mean sensory score across tastants) was calculated for each participant, following the approach described previously [19,20]. Participants were then ranked by modality score: individuals in the top quartile (highest 25%, N = 13) were classified as suprathreshold non-volatile flavour sensitive tasters, those in the bottom quartile (lowest 25%, N = 13) as suprathreshold non-volatile flavour insensitive tasters, and the remainder as normal tasters. To evaluate this classification, hierarchical divisive clustering was applied to the sensory dataset and the resulting clusters were compared with the suprathreshold non-volatile flavour sensitive tasters/insensitive tasters labels. Divisive clustering indicated that the sensory data separated into four clusters (Supplementary Figure 12). Nearly all suprathreshold non-volatile flavour sensitive tasters belonged to Cluster 2, whereas Clusters 1 and 4 consisted predominantly of suprathreshold non-volatile flavour insensitive tasters; in contrast, cluster membership among normal tasters was more evenly distributed (Figure 6A). Overall, the clustering results supported the validity of the suprathreshold non-volatile flavour sensitive tasters and insensitive tasters classification.

At the sensory level, suprathreshold non-volatile flavour sensitive tasters and insensitive tasters showed clear separation in beta diversity (Figure 6B). Specifically, perception intensities for all tastants were higher in suprathreshold non-volatile flavour sensitive tasters than in insensitive tasters (Supplementary Figure 13D). Metabolomic profiles also showed separation at the beta-diversity level (p < 0.05), but only two salivary metabolites differed significantly between groups (p < 0.05): ethanolamine and trimethylamine N-oxide (Supplementary Figure 13B and E). This pattern suggested that suprathreshold taste intensity was associated with coordinated shifts across multiple salivary metabolites rather than any single metabolite, consistent with the metabolome–non-volatile flavour fixed-effects model results.

Inferred microbial metabolic pathway profiles similarly showed clear separation between suprathreshold non-volatile flavour sensitive tasters and insensitive tasters at the beta-diversity level (Supplementary Figure 13A). In total, 13 inferred microbial metabolic pathways differed significantly between groups (Figure 6C). Notably, with the exception of P163-PWY, PWY-7234, and POLYISOPRENSYN-PWY, the remaining ten inferred microbial metabolic pathways had also been identified in the microbial metabolic pathway–non-volatile flavour fixed-effects linear-regression model (Figure 2A and Supplementary Table 1). Taken together, these results suggested that inferred microbial metabolic pathways showed strong associations with non-volatile flavour perception.

In contrast, taxonomic profiles showed no separation between suprathreshold non-volatile flavour sensitive tasters and insensitive tasters at the beta-diversity level (Supplementary Figure 13C). In total, five species differed significantly between groups (Supplementary Fgure 13F), with all five being higher in suprathreshold non-volatile flavour sensitive tasters and lower in insensitive tasters. None of these species had been identified in the species-level fixed-effects model of non-volatile flavour (supplementary Table 2), consistent with the greater variability of taxonomic profiles across samples reported in the HMP study [40]. Previous work reported that tongue Parvimonas micra was negatively associated with umami detection threshold, whereas salivary Veillonella tobetsuensis and tongue Fusobacterium periodonticum were positively associated with umami detection threshold [11]. However, threshold scores correlate poorly with suprathreshold measures, indicating that these reflect distinct processes [47,48]. Accordingly, no published studies have reported suprathreshold associations for the five species identified within suprathreshold non-volatile flavour sensitive tasters and insensitive tasters.

Next, we extracted the G-protein-coupled taste measures (oleic acid, monosodium glutamate, sucrose and caffeine) from the full sensory dataset to assess inter-individual variability in G-protein-mediated taste across the cohort. Divisive clustering separated these measures into four clusters (Supplementary Figure 14A). Clusters 1 and 2 comprised the majority of participants. When we compared the G-protein clusters with the clusters derived from the full sensory dataset, sensory cluster 1 contributed most to G-protein cluster 1, whereas sensory cluster 2 contributed most to G-protein cluster 2 (Supplementary Figure 14B). This pattern suggested that G-protein-mediated taste variation covaries with responses to other tastants assessed in this study.

Discussion

In this study, we emphasised the role of inferred microbial metabolic pathways in baseline suprathreshold taste perception, arguing that changes in taste are driven primarily by sub-communities within the oral taxonomic profiles rather than by individual bacterial species. This contrasts with previous publications, which focused solely on associations between single species or genera and taste [11,12,51,52]. We attribute this shift in emphasis to two factors: statistical considerations and host-microbiome interactions.

Statistically, in the cross-sectional cohort (N = 53 across three studies), only 19.02% (89/468) of species were present in more than half of the samples, whereas 77.5% (283/365) of inferred microbial metabolic pathways were detected in at least half. These findings mirror the TwinsUK study, in which only 14.0% (81/580) of gut microbial species were present in more than half of participants, compared with 66.0% (286/434) of gut inferred microbial metabolic pathways [39]. This pattern suggests that direct analysis of taxonomic data is prone to substantial zero inflation (datasets with an excess of zeros), as discussed in the MaAsLin2 paper and previous review [37,53]. Zero inflation arises because taxonomic profiles vary widely among healthy individuals, whereas inferred microbial metabolic pathway profiles are comparatively stable, as reported by the HMP paper [40]. HMP findings also suggest that different bacteria can share similar metabolic functions. Increasing resolution to the species level further exacerbates zero inflation. Overall, the pattern we observed for suprathreshold taste parallels findings from the TwinsUK and LifeLines-DEEP cohorts, where gut inferred microbial metabolic pathways more strongly capture associations with plasma metabolites than taxonomic composition per se [39,54]. The negative direction observed for pathway–taste associations is consistent with prior work reporting that gut inferred microbial metabolic pathways are negatively associated with the plasma metabolome and with gut species level taxonomy information at a single time point [54].

Statistically significant associations also had biological plausibility. For monosodium glutamate, the significant inferred microbial metabolic pathways were linked to glutamate/glutamine utilisation. In particular, L-isoleucine biosynthesis I proceeds via L-threonine and uses glutamate as an amino-group donor during transamination steps. Through glutamate–glutamine interconversion, L-tryptophan biosynthesis and UMP biosynthesis III utilise L-glutamine to synthesise L-tryptophan and uridine 5′-monophosphate (UMP), respectively; UMP is the ribonucleotide precursor for all pyrimidine nucleotides [55]. Aromatic amino-acid biosynthesis similarly draws on L-glutamine to produce L-tyrosine, L-phenylalanine, and L-tryptophan. These inferred microbial metabolic pathways were consistent with the fixed-effects linear-regression results for MSG: higher relative microbial metabolic pathway abundances implied greater consumption of glutamate/glutamine, thereby lowering perceived MSG intensity. This interpretation accounted for the negative association observed between MSG perception and inferred microbial metabolic pathway abundance (Figure 2A).

Sucrose provided another example. Oral bacteria (e.g. Streptococcus mutans) utilise sucrose to generate ribose-5-phosphate (a pentose sugar) [56], then convert to 5-phospho-D-ribosyl α-1-diphosphate (PRPP) [57]. PRPP enters UMP biosynthesis pathways I–III directly. In two inosine-5′-phosphate (IMP) biosynthesis pathways that were significant (adj p < 0.05), PRPP is first converted to 5-amino-1-(5-phospho-β-D-ribosyl) imidazole via the 5-aminoimidazole ribonucleotide (AIR) biosynthesis route (3 pathways, adj p < 0.05) before entering IMP biosynthesis. Taken together, higher sucrose-perception scores were associated with lower IMP- and UMP-biosynthesis pathway abundances (Figure 2A).

At the species-level taxonomic profiles, aggregating to the genus level might seem a plausible way to mitigate zero inflation. For example, using genus-level unsupervised clustering, suprathreshold sour and bitter perception differ clearly between two species-level taxonomic profile clusters [13]. In another study, Haemophilus, Lachnoanaerobaculum, Fusobacterium, Oribacterium and Aggregatibacter are negative correlate with sweet detection threshold [58]. However, substantial functional heterogeneity undermines this approach. Within the genus Streptococcus—a dominant genus in both the oral and gut taxonomic profiles—only 4.63% (574/12,403) of genes are shared across all species, indicating marked variability in metabolic potential [59]. Thus, genus-level data lack the resolution needed for reliable comparative analyses. Moreover, focusing solely on taxonomy conflicts with prevailing views of host–microbiome interactions, which emphasise microbial function as central [60,61].

Using unsupervised clustering, inferred microbial metabolic pathway profiles yielded clearer structure than taxonomic profiles (Supplementary Figures 2 and 7). Specifically, when sensory data were stratified by inferred microbial metabolic pathway clusters, suprathreshold citric-acid perception was significantly higher for certain clusters (e.g. IMPC 3), whereas stratification by species-level taxonomic profile clusters showed no differences in suprathreshold perception (Figure 3 and Supplementary Table 2). Taken together, the fixed-effects linear regression and clustering results suggested that taste–species-level taxonomic profiles interactions are driven by multispecies consortia, consistent with the fact that individual inferred microbial metabolic pathways are encoded by multiple taxa [40]. Supporting this interpretation, single-microbial pathway analyses showed systematic differences between IMPC 3 and the other clusters (Supplementary Figures 3 to 5). This pattern echoes the polymicrobial aetiology of periodontitis, which is driven by consortia rather than a single pathogen [62].

Human salivary enzyme–flavour interactions were also relevant to this study, as many enzymes can be synthesised by the oral bacteria, and these enzymes can degrade relevant compounds to release perceptible flavour molecules. For example, amylase can degrade starch to release sweet molecules for perception. In our dataset, α-amylase (EC 3.2.1.1; K01176 and K07405), β C–S lyase (EC 4.4.1.13; K14155 and K01760), and β-glucosidases (EC 3.2.1.21; K05349) were ubiquitous (prevalence ≥ 50%) [63–65]. However, there is currently no definitive evidence for direct interactions between salivary enzymes and flavour molecules. Although α-amylase can degrade starch to release sweet molecules, this process does not appear to affect sweet taste perception [66]. Glycosidases can hydrolyse glycosides, which are sugar-bound precursors of aroma compounds present in many fruits and vegetables, thereby contributing to retronasal perception. However, differences in overall glycosidase hydrolysis do not appear to affect overall retronasal perception [67]. Moreover, enzymes constitute a complex system: esterase comprises at least 100 enzyme types within EC 3.1.1, and glycosidases within EC 3.2.1 comprise at least 200 enzyme types. This area therefore requires further in-depth research, and the current findings should be interpreted with caution.

Although baseline metabolome–suprathreshold regression models and unsupervised metabolite clustering indicated that salivary metabolites were associated with suprathreshold taste perception (Figure 4 and Supplementary Figure 13B and E), one possible explanation is that salivary metabolites provide additional suprathreshold input (e.g. salivary sucrose contributing sweetness), thereby modulating perception through taste–taste interactions [68,69]. However, this explanation conflicts with the principle of sensory adaptation: salivary metabolites are unlikely to deliver a sustained suprathreshold stimulus over 24 h. Over short timescales, a single sodium-chloride rinse can make a subsequent solution taste more sour or bitter when its chloride concentration is lower than that of the adapting solution [70]; over longer timescales, salivary metabolites would be expected to act as background noise and primarily influence taste thresholds [71].

Notably, at the genetic level, human genetic variation at 11 loci was associated with 126 different oral bacterial species (adjusted p < 0.05) [72]. This suggests that host factors may help shape the oral species-level taxonomic profiles, just as Mendelian randomisation analyses have suggested that higher BMI is associated with lower gut species-level taxonomic profiles alpha diversity [73]. More specifically, Mendelian randomisation analyses found that increased BMI was associated with increases in 39 gut species and decreases in 65 gut species (p < 0.05). Under these conditions, we cautiously suggest that host factors may influence suprathreshold non-volatile flavour perception by shaping oral species-level taxonomic profiles composition, which may in turn alter metabolic patterns relevant to perception. In a previous study, sucrose supertasters tended to channel sucrose towards sustained flux through the citric acid cycle, whereas sucrose non-tasters more efficiently converted pyruvate to lactate [17]. However, the direct contribution of human genetics to suprathreshold non-volatile flavour perception is difficult to quantify, because final suprathreshold non-volatile flavour perception is also influenced by multiple factors, such as culture and living environment, as discussed in a previous review of olfaction [74]. For example, at the recognition-threshold level, the first variant site of TAS2R38, denoted A49P, was found to reduce the PROP bitter recognition threshold when the allele had a substitution from alanine (A) to proline (P) at position 49 [75]. However, at the suprathreshold level, PROP bitterness appears to depend on more than TAS2R38 alone [76].

Ethanolamine (a phospholipid-related metabolite and confirmed bacterial metabolite) and taurine (an amino acid and confirmed bacterial metabolite) showed the largest number of significant associations with non-volatile flavour molecules (Figure 4A) [77,78]. Although oleic acid sensitive group (subjects able to perceive an oleic-acid emulsion at 0.099 mM) have lower salivary ethanolamine concentrations than the insensitive group (subjects unable to perceive an oleic-acid emulsion at 1.77 mM), there was no evidence that salivary ethanolamine directly affect suprathreshold non-volatile flavour perception [79]. Taurine has a sour taste, and excessive taurine intake can induce taste aversion [80,81], but no published data indicate that salivary taurine directly modulates suprathreshold non-volatile flavour perception.

Moreover, threshold scores correlate poorly with suprathreshold measures, indicating that these reflect distinct processes [47,48]. It was therefore necessary to consider alternative mechanisms by which the oral taxonomic profiles might affect suprathreshold taste.

Previous research has identified three possible mechanisms by which the oral taxonomic profiles may influence taste perception. First, microbial biofilms form a viscous layer over taste buds, impeding tastant access to receptor sites [82,83]. Second, microbial by-products affect suprathreshold non-volatile flavour perception. A representative example is lipopolysaccharide (LPS), which engages Toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4) on taste-bud cells to elicit innate immune signalling and alter gustatory perception. Chronic LPS exposure in type II cells enhance bitterness perception while attenuating sweet-taste intensity [84–86]. In salt-sensitive cells, LPS increase IL-1β production, which in turn promotes sodium flux through epithelial sodium channels (ENaC), thereby amplifying the salt-taste signal [87,88].

Third, the oral bacteria metabolise tastants (e.g. sucrose) before their interaction with taste receptors, altering the availability of taste stimuli and, over short timescales, shifting salivary metabolomic features in ways that influence suprathreshold perception. For example, a 10% sucrose solution reduce salivary pH by about one unit after 20 min of consumption [89]. Over the same period, sucrose supertasters tend to channel carbon into the citric-acid cycle, whereas sucrose non-tasters more efficiently convert pyruvate to lactate [17]. These additional salivary metabolites have contributed suprathreshold taste inputs and thereby modulated perception.

This study was subject to several limitations. First, the modest sample size precluded the use of structural equation modelling (SEM) to fully characterise pathway–taste relationships; unlike ordinary linear regression, SEM can simultaneously incorporate observed and latent variables [90]. Moreover, the small cohort size meant that inter-cohort variability (also referred to as batch effects) could not be eliminated. Second, although shotgun DNA sequencing elucidates the functional potential of the oral taxonomic profiles, it cannot determine which genes are actively expressed; metatranscriptomic approaches (for example, RNA sequencing or proteomics) would distinguish inferred microbial metabolic pathways directly engaged in specific functions from those acting merely as proxies for community composition. Third, participants were recruited only in the United Kingdom; given cross-country differences in microbial functional profiles, residents of different countries may show different associations with inferred microbial metabolic pathways [91,92]. Fourth, this study did not test taste thresholds. Because thresholds showed no association with suprathreshold perception, it was possible that salivary metabolites played a more critical role than inferred microbial metabolic pathways in threshold modulation, with salivary metabolites acting as a background signal that influenced detection limits [47,48,71]. Fifth, the study had only a single time point, so we could not evaluate changes in taste following perturbations to the oral taxonomic profiles or its functional capacity. Sixth, we assessed suprathreshold perception at a single concentration per tastant. Using a range of concentrations (dose–response) could be more sensitive to changes in perception. For example, increasing temperature enhance the sweetness of low-concentration sucrose, but this effect disappear at 0.5 M sucrose [93]. Seventh, although the influence of retronasal olfaction on menthol taste perception was minimised by asking participants to keep their mouths closed while holding the sensory solution, participants could not distinguish the source of menthol odour perception, as menthol odour reflects the combined effects of trigeminal sensation and olfaction [94]. Eighth, even if associations were observed using RNA or proteomic data, they would still need to be confirmed by biochemical experiments. Finally, because all data were standardised prior to linear regression, the reported associations express proportional effects of inferred microbial metabolic pathways on taste perception rather than their absolute impact.

Conclusions

This study suggested that suprathreshold taste perception associated with inferred microbial metabolic pathways via multispecies interactions. Because inferred microbial metabolic pathways are encoded by multiple bacteria, they capture multispecies contributions.
