Perceptions of healthcare professionals and community members on acute febrile illness management in Maputo and Quelimane City: a qualitative study
Authors: Vanessa Da Conceição Onofre Monteiro, Pedroso Nhassengo, Elidio Muamine, Pascoal Alho, Yolanda Manganhe, Américo Zandamela, Maria I Cambe, Eduardo S Gudo, Isabel Craveiro
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractIntroductionAcute febrile illness is a major cause of morbidity and healthcare seeking in sub-Saharan Africa, including Mozambique. However, few studies have explored the perceptions and practices related to clinical management of acute febrile illness. Our aim was to understand the perceptions, knowledge and practices of healthcare professionals and community members regarding the management of acute febrile illness.MethodsA qualitative study was conducted using a thematic analysis approach. Eight focus group discussions (FGDs) and three in-depth interviews (IDIs) were conducted in two Mozambican provinces across four healthcare facilities. Purposive sampling was used to select participants, comprising both healthcare professionals and community members. Data were analysed thematically through manual coding, applying inductive and deductive approaches, in line with established qualitative analytical frameworks.ResultsThe study included a total of 60 participants, 28 healthcare professionals who participated in FGDs (25) and IDIs (3) and 32 community members who participated in FGDs. Major causes of fever, according to healthcare professionals, were upper and lower respiratory infections, malaria, diarrhoeal diseases and COVID-19. The diagnostic approach for febrile patients primarily included screening, malaria testing and other non-specific investigations depending on availability at health facilities. Antibiotics and antipyretics were the most commonly prescribed treatments. The healthcare professionals reported a decrease in prescribing antimalarials due to extensive training on malaria case management. In contrast, community members reported relying primarily on home-based and traditional practices to treat acute febrile illness before seeking care at health facilities. These practices are related to sociocultural habits, such as the use of traditional medicine and self-medication. The main barriers to seeking care at health facilities were the perception of poor quality of care, long waiting times, cultural beliefs and lack of medicines.ConclusionProper management of non-malarial febrile illness remains a major challenge for both healthcare professionals and communities. Training, adherence to case management protocols and efforts to change behaviours to mitigate harmful sociocultural practices are urgently necessary to improve febrile illness management.Keywords: INFECTIOUS DISEASES, Public health, Tropical medicine

Introduction

Acute febrile illness is a frequent and non-specific clinical presentation associated with a wide spectrum of infectious and non-infectious diseases.1 In sub-Saharan Africa, fever has historically been strongly associated with malaria, which remains one of the most common causes of febrile illness in the region.2,5

The management of febrile illness depends on the epidemiological context, the availability of diagnostic tools and access to treatment guidelines, the burden of febrile diseases and the availability of diagnostic and treatment methods. In malaria-endemic settings, significant challenges arise when patients present with fever but test negative for malaria, particularly where diagnostic capacity for alternative causes is limited.6 7

To support clinical decision-making, the WHO has developed guidelines and algorithms for the management of acute febrile illness, including recommendations based on laboratory-confirmed malaria diagnosis. While rapid diagnostic tests (RDTs) have improved malaria case management, they have also introduced new challenges for healthcare professionals when results are negative, increasing diagnostic uncertainty and complicating treatment decisions.8,10

In Mozambique, a flowchart for the management of non-malarial febrile illness was incorporated into national malaria guidelines in 2018 to address these challenges.11 However, evidence suggests that a substantial proportion of febrile illness is caused by viral and other non-malarial infections, which are often not systematically diagnosed due to limited laboratory capacity.12,15 As a result, patients are frequently treated empirically with antimalarials or antibiotics, contributing to inappropriate treatment and increasing the risk of antimicrobial resistance.16 17

In addition to health system constraints, perceptions and practices related to febrile illness management are shaped by sociocultural, economic and contextual factors at both healthcare and community levels.18 19 These factors influence how fever is recognised, interpreted and managed, including decisions to seek care and adherence to recommended treatments.

Despite these challenges, limited evidence exists on how healthcare professionals and community members perceive and manage acute febrile illness in Mozambique, particularly in the context of changing malaria epidemiology and increasing recognition of non-malarial causes of fever.

Therefore, this study aimed to understand the perceptions, knowledge and practices of healthcare professionals and community members regarding the management of acute febrile illness in Mozambique.

Methods

Setting

This study was conducted in Maputo city and Zambézia province, situated in southern and central Mozambique, respectively.

Mozambique is a malaria endemic country,20 with an estimated prevalence of 39% among children aged 6–59 months. The prevalence of malaria varies significantly across provinces, with Maputo City having the lowest rate at 1%, while higher rates are seen in the central and northern regions of the country, such as Niassa at 59% and Cabo Delgado at 57%.21

Study design and participants

We conducted a qualitative study using a thematic analysis approach22,24 to explore participants’ perceptions and practices.

Data were collected through in-depth interviews (IDIs) and focus group discussions (FGDs) with healthcare professionals and community members.

Purposive sampling was conducted in two stages: first, for the selection of provinces and healthcare facilities, and second for participant selection. Participants were purposively selected to capture diverse perspectives from healthcare professionals and community members across different settings relevant to acute febrile illness management. The study sites were chosen due to their role as sentinel sites for acute febrile illness surveillance or their relevance within the national surveillance system, ensuring alignment with the study objectives and relevance to febrile illness management contexts. To ensure representation of both urban and suburban areas, two healthcare facilities per province were included (see study sites in figure 1).

Location of the study sites in Maputo City and Zambézia Province, Mozambique. The selected sites represent contrasting malaria transmission settings, with Maputo City characterised by low malaria prevalence (1%) and Zambézia Province by high malaria prevalence (44%), based on the 2018 National Malaria Indicator Survey (IIM).

Within study settings, selection of participants followed predefined eligibility criteria:

Eligible professionals were invited verbally during protocol presentations and by telephone contact. Community members were invited through focal points from the advisory councils of the respective health facilities. Invitations were made verbally during community meetings or via telephone. Up to three call attempts were made, and individuals with inactive numbers, who did not answer, or who were unavailable, were excluded.

The target sample size was not predetermined; instead, the sample was finalised on reaching thematic saturation, defined as the point at which no new themes emerged from successive interviews and FGDs. Saturation was assessed iteratively during data collection and preliminary analysis.25

We conducted a total of eight FGDs (four with healthcare professionals and four with community members).

A total of four FGDs (two per province) were conducted with healthcare professionals, including medical doctors, nurses, technicians and preventive medicine agents without leadership roles in the health facilities. FGDs were conducted with group sizes ranging from six to ten participants, consistent with qualitative research standards, to optimise group interaction and facilitate active participation from all participants. Additionally, three IDIs were conducted with healthcare professionals in leadership positions. This allowed for the collection of detailed insights and helped avoid potential influence on the responses of participants without leadership roles.

A total of four community FGDs (two per province) were performed, each with 6–10 participants. In each province, two types of FGDs occurred: one group included community leaders (neighbourhood secretaries, block chiefs, religious leaders, traditional leaders, state delegates and neighbourhood delegates, among other community representatives) and another with health facility users who held no leadership roles in the community.

Data collection

Data were collected between May and July 2022 in the cities of Maputo and Quelimane.

The IDIs and FGDs with healthcare professionals were conducted using a semistructured interview guide designed to explore their perceptions, practices, attitudes and knowledge regarding the management of acute febrile illness from patient screening to diagnosis and treatment. The same set of guiding questions was used for both IDIs and FGDs. The interview and FGD guides are provided as supplementary material (see online supplemental file 1).

The interview and discussion guides were developed by the research team during the study design phase and refined through input from anthropologists with experience in qualitative research and a medical researcher trained in qualitative data collection. The guides were pilot tested prior to data collection and adjusted to ensure clarity, contextual relevance and alignment with the study objectives.

For community members, the FGD guide addressed key topics such as the definition of fever, care-seeking behaviour, treatment practices and the use of self-medication.

Three trained researchers (two women and one man), including two anthropologists and one medical doctor, conducted all interviews and group discussions. At the time of the study, all were employed at the National Institute of Health in Mozambique. The anthropologists held bachelor’s and master’s degrees, respectively, and had over 10 years of experience in qualitative research. The medical doctor had 10 years of experience in public health research. All team members received additional training specific to this study prior to data collection.

All FGDs were conducted in Portuguese, with healthcare professionals at the health facilities and with community members at schools and open spaces near neighbourhood secretariats.

The FGDs lasted between 43 and 70 min. The IDIs ranged from 41 to 53 min.

At the beginning of each FGD, moderators explained the purpose of the study, how the information would be used, and obtained written informed consent from participants to audio-record the discussions and analyse the data after anonymisation. The same procedure was followed for the IDIs.

The interviewers had no prior relationship with participants. At the beginning of each session, they introduced themselves as public health researchers and explained the study objectives. Participants were informed of the purpose of the research, and interviewer influence was minimised through neutral facilitation.

Data analysis

All interviews were audio recorded, and notes were taken to capture non-verbal communication. Audio recordings were transcribed verbatim. Data were coded based on the data and analysed thematically using deductive and inductive approaches.26 The thematic analysis was carried out manually, according to the six steps described by Braun & Clarke, 2006: data familiarisation, generation of initial codes, elaboration of themes, review of themes, definition and renaming of themes and subthemes, and writing a report.

Initial codes were generated systematically from relevant data extracts in relation to the study objectives, and subsequently grouped into potential themes. These themes were reviewed and refined through iterative discussions within the research team to ensure coherence and consistency.

Coding was conducted independently by more than one researcher, and discrepancies were discussed and resolved through consensus to enhance analytical rigour. Data from both IDI and FGD were analysed together; combining these two sources enabled exploration of individual perspectives and group dynamics, yielding a richer and more detailed understanding of participants’ experiences, opinions and perceptions on the topics of interest.

Efforts were made to retain and consider less dominant or divergent perspectives during analysis, while themes with limited or insufficient supporting data were critically reviewed and either refined or excluded to ensure analytical robustness.

Reflexivity was addressed through regular team discussions to minimise the influence of researchers’ prior assumptions and support consistent data interpretation.

Perception of what fever is

Most healthcare professionals defined fever as an increase in body temperature above 37.5°C, which can be measured in the armpits, oral cavity or anal region. This definition reflects an understanding of fever shaped by clinical training and standardised in routine clinical practice.

…. I understand fever as a change to the normal temperature that is caused by various pathologies; it is an increase in body temperature that goes from 37.5 degrees Celsius… (FGD, Quelimane, medical doctor).

Community participants defined fever as the heating of the body, associated with other symptoms such as cough, back pain, headache, joint pain and feelings of cold and heat. They described fever in more experiential and symptom-based terms, reflecting a different conceptualisation of illness. While healthcare professionals’ understanding is shaped by formal clinical training, community interpretations are influenced by lived experience, sociocultural context and environmental factors, which together shape how illness is perceived and managed at the household level.

Fever is an increase in body temperature; some may cough, and others may not; using a wet towel to lower the heat before taking the child to the hospital is recommended. (FGD, Maputo, community participant).

Causes of fever

According to the healthcare professionals, there are several causes of fever, namely malaria, upper and lower respiratory tract infections, COVID-19, gastroenteritis, meningitis, immunosuppression due to HIV, tuberculosis, hyperthermia, hypoglycaemia and other pathologies.

…Frequently, malaria, lower respiratory infections, flu, and some gastroenteric conditions also lead to acute fever, some cases of pneumonia; we currently have few cases of pneumonia, some inflammatory arthropathies, but conditions such as suspected rheumatoid arthritis are rare, hypoglycemia, gout. (IDI, Quelimane, medical doctor).

Other causes of persistent non-infectious fever cited by healthcare professionals were cancers, inflammatory arthropathies and medication-induced fever.

…you can think of malignant diseases as cancers, and in addition, you can have changes in body temperature resulting from using some medications… (IDI, Maputo, medical doctor).

Overall, healthcare professionals identified a broad range of causes of fever, reflecting their clinical training and routine practice, encompassing both infectious and non-infectious conditions, as well as a broader clinical context in which fever may occur alongside different clinical presentations.

Negative experiences in fever management

Healthcare professionals have expressed that managing fever-related illnesses remains a significant challenge in their daily work, despite their accumulated experience from internships to their current professional practice. Even with a minimum of 1 year of experience, caring for patients with acute fever illnesses proves difficult due to factors such as the severity of cases and limited resources for diagnosis and treatment. These challenges were consistently described as arising within resource-constrained clinical environments, where limitations in diagnostic capacity and treatment options shape clinical decision-making. Participants reported that adverse outcomes, including patient fatalities, occurred in the context of these technical constraints.

…I have been here since 2014, and it has always been challenging to work with fever patients because of many difficulties…; It was a challenge because I had to manage the fever with something that didn’t exist because there were no conditions in the health facilities. (FGD, Maputo, medical doctor).

… a newborn baby had a warm body, a malaria test was positive, a 2 week-old newborn, so what happened was that the nurse who was at this health facility, the clinician prescribed quinine added to dextrose, but when the nurse saw the quantity based on the weight, she decided to put it in the syringe so, it didn’t take long for the child to die… (FGD, Quelimane, general medicine technician).

This account also points to challenges related to clinical experience and familiarity with treatment protocols, particularly in complex clinical situations.

Patient assessment

Participants were unanimous in stating that usually, when they have a patient complain of fever, they start the clinical assessment by listening to the medical history of the current illness, including the duration of symptoms, specific complaints and comorbidities, in order to inform clinical reasoning and support a possible diagnosis.

Few healthcare professionals participating in the FGD mentioned carrying out the physical examination. This suggests variability in clinical assessment practices, with some participants emphasising history-taking, while others, particularly those in leadership roles, highlighting the importance of physical examination in patient assessment.

The most frequent lab request was: RDTs and microscopic examination for malaria (when available), blood count, urinalysis, chest X-ray, blood culture, tuberculosis screening or another available exam. These diagnostic practices reflect reliance on available tests, with limited mention of diagnostic tools for alternative causes of febrile illness.

…here in the hospital, we do a urine test, X-ray, blood count, culture, they say they do it, but we are not doing it effectively, GeneXpert for suspected tuberculosis, and COVID-19… (IDI, Quelimane, medical doctor).

Places to seek healthcare

Healthcare professionals noted that prior to visiting healthcare facilities, community members experiencing fever usually attempt to address or alleviate the fever through sociocultural practices, traditional medicine (such as plants or roots) and beliefs.

What we have heard in the community is that they breathe in the steam from the eucalyptus and some lemon tree leaves…”, “Often, many patients also say that I went to the healer, did this, did that, I had an unfortunate experience. (FGD, Quelimane, nurse).

Participants described that health facilities are typically sought only when initial treatments are perceived as ineffective, often at later stages of disease. When patients are successfully treated for their illness in the community, they do not immediately turn to health facilities for care. This pattern indicates that health facilities are not consistently the first point of care for acute febrile illness in these settings.

The community tends to first seek treatment for fevers outside of health facilities due to concerns about delayed care, frequent unavailability of essential medicines and the need to obtain prescriptions from private pharmacies, which adds to the financial burden on patients and their families. These accounts reflect how both perceived and experienced barriers influence initial care-seeking decisions.

From experience, many times they don’t rush to the hospital because they say there are a lot of people, because of the time of day, they look for traditional medicine… (FGD, Quelimane, nurse).

… They go to the pharmacy, buy paracetamol, and give it to the child; this also means the person reaches the health facilities in another phase. Sometimes they don’t even go to the pharmacy; they take the medicines that they have at home… (IDI, Maputo, medical doctor).

According to the community participants, the main concerns regarding healthcare provision in health facilities are the lack of availability of diagnostic tools and medications. Participants also reported that healthcare professionals were perceived as focusing on prescribing medications without adequately listening to patients, and that laboratory testing was often not performed to support diagnosis.

Additionally, there were complaints about having to purchase medications from private pharmacies at higher prices than public health facilities, highlighting concerns related to affordability and trust in the health system.

… we leave home very early, and when we arrive at the hospital, they don’t do any analysis; they just give us a prescription to buy paracetamol. And that’s why many people don’t go to the hospital… (FGD, Maputo, community participant).

… I have complaints about the hospital because they leave people anyway, they don’t provide good care, we keep quiet because we’re afraid of being harmed. (FGD, Maputo, community leaders).

Treatment

Healthcare professionals reported prescribing analgesics, antipyretics, antibiotics and other medications. In some cases, a patient presenting with a fever who has a negative result for malaria is often prescribed paracetamol and an antibiotic such as amoxicillin or co-trimoxazole. This pattern was described as part of routine clinical practice in the management of febrile illness.

Fever is the same as paracetamol and amoxicillin; unfortunately, we have already drawn the attention. I understand that sometimes it is a technical limitation or worsening… (IDI, Quelimane, medical doctor).

A patient who comes in with a negative RDT, then we assess whether he has an abscess, whether he has an abscess, whether there is anything that could be causing that fever, we give paracetamol and ibuprofen if there is a cause like that in addition to malaria which we have already ruled out if we can give antibiotics. (FGD Maputo, medical doctor).

The pressure on healthcare professionals to prescribe antimalarials or antibiotics was also part of the challenges faced by health facilities. These accounts suggest that prescribing decisions may be influenced not only by clinical assessment but also by patient expectations and contextual pressures.

… I have treated several adults who, even with a negative test, said that it was malaria. They asked for treatment, but I didn’t give it; some people accepted, and others bought medicine at the pharmacy. (FGD, Maputo, preventive medicine agent).

The treatment of fever was described as depending on the availability of medicines at health facilities. Some healthcare professionals have previously used antimalarials to treat non-malarial fevers and sporadic cases, which can still happen. However, there are now control measures in place to prevent the inappropriate use of antimalarials for negative cases.

In the first phase, I treated patients with Coartem even with a negative test… In the second phase, I waited for the laboratory results to be able to give them. (FGD, Maputo, medical doctor).

When the community identifies a febrile episode, they typically attempt to reduce the fever by cooling the body (using baths and cold compresses) and taking medications like paracetamol or other prescribed drugs. These practices were also influenced by sociocultural traditions, including the use of traditional remedies such as eucalyptus, aloe vera, moringa leaves, mango leaves, basil and lemon tree leaves.

… when I have a fever before going to the hospital, I boil eucalyptus, and I also drink the water from the leaves; when I see that I am still sick, that’s when I think about going to the hospital when I see that the eucalyptus didn’t work… (FGD, Quelimane community participant).

Other practices within the community, such as requests for prayer in church and purchases of medicines in private pharmacies, were identified. These practices illustrate the coexistence of biomedical, traditional and faith-based approaches in the management of febrile illness and other health conditions.

One of the things we can do is say a prayer for the person because it is a source of trust in God, and then we can go to the health facilities to see. (FGD, Maputo, Community participant).

The community goes to the pharmacy and comes with that medication from the pharmacist; it doesn’t pass and go to the hospital; sometimes it passes, and sometimes it doesn’t pass. (FGD, Quelimane, community leader).

Participants also reported that acceptance of treatment at health facilities was influenced by familiarity with certain medications, such as Coartem, which were perceived as more effective. Access to medicines through private pharmacies was often preferred due to frequent stock-outs in public health facilities, limited access to diagnostic testing and concerns about the availability or quality of medicines. These factors appear to shape treatment preferences and perceptions of effectiveness.

If I go to the hospital and they give me Coartem, I feel happy and cured, but when we go to the hospital, and they give us different medicines, we don’t feel cured; we get upset because we don’t know that medicines are, …rarely in the hospital pharmacy they give you antibiotics, they always don’t have these medicines in the hospital, you have to go and buy them at a pharmacy outside (FGD, Maputo, community leader).

In Quelimane, some participants reported concerns related to the quality and safety of medications provided in healthcare settings.

… The doctor took medicine from his pocket and gave it to us in addition to the prescription. I started taking it, and I saw that it was out of date. (FGD, Quelimane, Community leader).

Information search places

According to most healthcare professionals, the best place to update information about fever is in training, clinical sessions, scientific sessions, health facilities statistics committees, universities, morning lectures at the health facilities, research on the internet and through the fever flowchart management manual. These sources were described as important for strengthening knowledge and supporting clinical decision-making.

Healthcare professionals also mentioned that scientific sessions provide opportunities to improve the dissemination and communication of messages related to disease prevention and treatment measures for fever and malaria.

… we have updates on fever cases in research via the internet, and also the statistics commission evaluates the processes in the sector; after carrying out an evaluation, they publish results for each sector; that also gives an idea… (FGD, Quelimane, medical doctor).

Regarding the search for information on the fever management flowchart in the malaria treatment manual, most participants mentioned that the manuals were not available in the health facilities. This lack of availability was described as a barrier to accessing up-to-date clinical guidance and supporting informed clinical decision-making.

It exists, but we don’t have it; I am a focal point for malaria; we don’t have malaria working instruments… (FGD, Quelimane, medical doctor).

Actions to improve healthcare

According to healthcare professionals, several measures were identified at health facilities to enhance the management of acute febrile illness. Among those, improving the dissemination of information about acute febrile illness, developing fever management protocols, strengthening diagnostic capacity, ensuring the availability of medicines, improving administrative infrastructure, increasing the number of trained personnel, conducting regular maintenance of hospital equipment and providing ongoing training, supervision and support for healthcare professionals were identified as key measures. These measures reflect efforts to strengthen both clinical capacity and health system support.

… At the hospital level, it would be training colleagues and providing on-the-job training to follow the protocols to know what to do in case of fever, even taking an anamnesis and physical examination, it is necessary to be trained. At the Ministry of Health level, it is necessary to have qualified personnel… (IDI, Maputo, medical doctor).

… lack of some fever protocols, we have to make them available at some point so that we can also have the knowledge, and we need continuous training. (FGD, Quelimane, general medicine technician).

Regarding sources of information in the community about fever, most participants reported seeking information from community health agents or health facilities during consultations, as well as through lectures during health or vaccination campaigns, television, radio and schools. These channels provide opportunities for health education and communication at the community level.

… through television, radio, health facilities, lectures, and schools where students upload information… (FGD, Quelimane community participant).

Discussion

Acute febrile illness is generally a symptom of an infectious disease, but it can also result from non-infectious causes. Depending on each country’s epidemiological context, the causes of fever are diverse and contribute to significant morbidity and mortality, particularly in low-income countries.27 28 Malaria is endemic in Mozambique, and fever is one of its main clinical manifestations. For many years, healthcare professionals in Mozambique have attributed fever without focal signs to malaria,29 reflecting a clinical approach primarily focused on malaria in clinical reasoning.

With the expansion of laboratory diagnostic capacity, including malaria RDTs and microscopy, treatment can now be more accurately targeted to confirmed cases.11 30 While these tools have improved malaria case management, they have also introduced new diagnostic challenges when test results are negative, particularly in settings with limited capacity to investigate other causes of febrile illness.

Through FGDs and IDIs, respiratory infections, malaria, gastroenteritis and COVID-19 were highlighted as the main febrile illnesses affecting individuals seeking healthcare. Although national epidemiological data on febrile illnesses in Mozambique remain limited, literature suggests that infections represent the primary cause of fever in sub-Saharan Africa, particularly viral infections of the lower and upper respiratory tract and gastrointestinal infections.31 32

These findings suggest that, although healthcare professionals were able to identify a broad range of causes of febrile illness, there remains limited capacity to determine the specific underlying aetiological agent in routine clinical practice. This gap likely reflects structural and diagnostic constraints rather than limitations in clinical knowledge alone and has important implications for treatment and management. In settings with limited diagnostic capacity, healthcare providers may rely on empirical treatment, which can lead to inappropriate management, unnecessary use of healthcare resources, reduced confidence in the healthcare system and increased antimicrobial resistance.33 34 These challenges reflect broader systemic constraints in resource-limited settings and highlight the critical need to strengthen diagnostic capacity at the primary care level to support more accurate and evidence-based case management.

In addition, Mozambique has documented dengue cases among fevers of undetermined origin, particularly in the northern provinces,33 and subsequent studies and surveillance have highlighted the presence of arboviruses and other emerging infectious diseases.34,36 These data underscore the diversity of febrile illness aetiologies and the need for broader diagnostic consideration in clinical practice.37

Reports from healthcare professionals during FGDs highlight significant challenges in managing febrile illness, particularly in relation to resource constraints and the occurrence of adverse events. These findings likely reflect the challenges of clinical decision-making in resource-constrained settings and highlight the need to strengthen service quality and support professional development to enhance patient safety. Strengthening training at different stages of professional practice may contribute to improving provider capacity to apply existing algorithms for non-malarial fever management. Addressing these challenges requires a broader focus on health system strengthening, including supportive supervision and continuous professional development.38,40

When treating patients with negative malaria test results, an antipyretic such as paracetamol or an anti-inflammatory medicine is often prescribed, frequently in combination with an antibiotic like amoxicillin, according to reports from FGDs and IDIs. These findings are consistent with other studies that report an increase in the prescription of antibiotics, mainly penicillins and sulfonamides, due to the introduction of rapid tests for malaria in regions where it is endemic.41 42 This pattern may reflect reliance on empirical treatment in the absence of aetiological confirmation, as the cause of fever is often not clearly defined, and may contribute to adverse effects and antimicrobial resistance due to inappropriate antibiotic use.

The Ministry of Health’s fever management flowchart represents an important tool for guiding healthcare professionals. However, low awareness and availability of this guideline in health facilities suggest limited uptake. Improved dissemination strategies, coupled with supportive supervision and routine mentorship, may enhance adherence to fever management guidelines at the facility level. These findings suggest that limited availability and awareness of clinical guidelines may reduce their effective use in practice, highlighting the need not only to distribute clinical tools but also to ensure adequate training and supervision for their effective use.

From the community’s perspective, health facilities are often not the first point of care for febrile illness. Many individuals turn to traditional treatments, pharmacies or home remedies before seeking medical attention. These practices likely reflect the interaction between sociocultural norms and structural barriers, including lack of trust, long waiting times, absence of diagnostic capacity and medication stock-outs.43 44

Self-medication and the use of traditional remedies are widespread, yet often unregulated. While culturally embedded, these practices may also reflect limited access to formal healthcare services and may be associated with risks, including toxicity, delayed treatment and the development of drug resistance.45,47 Few studies in Mozambique have explored these community-level factors, highlighting the importance of further research on the safety and efficacy of commonly used plant-based treatments. Community engagement strategies, incorporating local beliefs and behaviours, may strengthen early care-seeking and improve timely access to appropriate care.

While these findings are consistent with patterns observed in similar settings, the present study adds contextual depth by capturing local perceptions from both healthcare professionals and community members, a perspective that remains underrepresented in the literature on febrile illness management in Mozambique.

The findings from this study highlight important gaps in clinical practice and community health-seeking behaviour that affect the effective management of febrile illness. They underscore the need to strengthen clinical training, reinforce adherence to existing protocols, improve access to essential diagnostics and medicines, and enhance community engagement.

A context-specific, integrated approach will be essential to improving fever case management and promoting more rational, effective and equitable healthcare responses. Such approaches may also contribute to reducing unnecessary antimicrobial use and strengthening health system performance.
