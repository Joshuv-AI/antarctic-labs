Non-Bloch band theory of nonlinear eigenvalue problems
Kota Otsuka, Kazuki Yokomizo

Abstract
Nonlinear eigenvalue problems arise in a wide range of physical systems, in which system parameters depend on the eigenvalue. Such systems have been proposed to exhibit an extreme sensitivity of their spectra to boundary conditions, which leads to the breakdown of conventional topological characterizations. In this work, we establish a non-Bloch framework for calculating continuum bands that reproduce the spectra of the nonlinear system with open boundary conditions. This non-Bloch band theory enables us not only to calculate the eigenvalues but also to reveal phenomena unique to the nonlinear system. We further investigate the topological bulk-boundary correspondence in a nonlinear Chern insulator within an extended version of this framework.



I
Introduction

Nonlinear dynamics gives rise to various intriguing phenomena due to the breakdown of the superposition principle
38
, and appears, for example, in optics
22
;
33
and weakly interacting bosonic systems
11
;
30

In recent years, there has grown interest in how the nonlinearity affects topological systems and non-Hermitian systems. For instance, the bulk-boundary correspondence has been established in terms of nonlinear topological invariants

;
56
;
35
, and novel nonlinear phenomena utilizing topological edge states have been proposed

;

;

. In addition, lasing modes can be excited through the localization of numerous eigenstates induced by the non-Hermitian skin effect

;
39
;
57
;
25
.

Meanwhile, nonlinear eigenvalue equations
12
;
42
, in which the generator of time evolution depends on the eigenvalue, have also attracted much attention. These equations describe a variety of physical systems, such as mechanical oscillators
8
;
46
;
15
;
16
;
24
;
37
, electrical circuits
45
;
55
, and dispersive metamaterials
21
;
18
;
23
;
34
;
40
;
31
;
28
. Recent theoretical studies have shed light on topological aspects of this type of nonlinear systems. A pioneering work has established the bulk-boundary correspondence in terms of topology of an auxiliary system
17
, which is analogous to that of Hermitian systems. Subsequent studies have shown that the auxiliary eigenvalue approach can be applied to investigate several other models
5
;
2
;
3
. Furthermore, Refs.
53
;
27
have investigated complex-valued eigenvalues by analogy with non-Hermitian topology
10
;
19
;
54
;
29
, providing topological characterizations of the skin effect. In both cases, the Bloch band description provides a useful way to obtain the spectra of the nonlinear systems.

The intriguing feature of the nonlinear system is its extreme sensitivity to boundary conditions. Indeed, spatial nonreciprocity has been proposed to induce the discrepancy between the spectra under periodic and open boundary conditions (PBCs and OBCs)
53
;
27
. In other words, the conventional Bloch band theory fails to describe the spectra under OBCs, which complicates the analysis of the nonlinear system. For example, it is hard to calculate the OBC spectra, since solving the nonlinear eigenvalue problem is challenging even numerically. In addition, topological edge states are difficult to access due to the breakdown of the bulk-boundary correspondence
27
. Therefore, it is necessary to establish a systematic framework for calculating bands that reproduce the spectra of the nonlinear system with OBCs.

In this paper, we develop a theoretical framework for calculating the continuum bands of tight-binding systems described by the nonlinear eigenvalue equations. The key idea is that this construction is analogous to that of the non-Bloch band theory of non-Hermitian systems
47
;
48
;
49
. Within this framework, we study several one-dimensional (1D) models and demonstrate that the continuum bands obtained from the generalized Brillouin zones (GBZs) reproduce the spectra under OBCs. We further show that the nonlinearity induces the skin effect in a system with nonlinear pseudo-Hermiticity. Additionally, we find that the present non-Bloch framework can be extended to two-dimensional (2D) tight-binding systems with certain symmetry. We then establish the bulk-boundary correspondence between a Chern number defined from the GBZs of auxiliary bands and the emergence of topological edge states.

The rest of this paper is organized as follows. In Sec.
II
, we introduce a general framework for the nonlinear eigenvalue problem and construct the corresponding non-Bloch band theory. In Sec.
III
, we present a simple model and explain how to calculate the continuum bands. We further investigate the localization behavior of a model with nonlinear pseudo-Hermiticity. In Sec.
IV
, we introduce 2D nonlinear systems to which the non-Bloch band theory investigated here can be applied, and establish the bulk-boundary correspondence in a Chern insulator with nonreciprocal hopping amplitudes. In Sec.
V
, we summarize our results and discuss future perspectives.

II
general framework

In this section, we consider a 1D tight-binding system whose Hamiltonian exhibits the eigenvalue dependence. We then construct a theoretical framework for calculating the continuum bands that reproduce the spectra of the system with OBCs.

II.1
Nonlinear eigenvalue problem

We consider a 1D tight-binding system described by a nonlinear eigenvalue equation

H(\omega)|\psi(\omega)\rangle=f(\omega)|\psi(\omega)\rangle,

(1)

where
\omega\in\mathbb{C}
is the eigenvalue,
H(\omega)
is the eigenvalue-dependent Hamiltonian, and
f(\omega)
is an analytical function of
\omega
. Let
L
and
q
denote the system size and the number of internal degrees of freedom per unit cell, respectively. The eigenvalue-dependent Hamiltonian can then be expressed as

\displaystyle H(\omega)=\bm{c}^{\dagger}A(\omega)\bm{c},

(2)

\displaystyle\bm{c}=(c_{1,1},c_{1,2},\ldots,c_{1,q},\ldots,c_{L,1},c_{L,2},\ldots,c_{L,q})^{\mathrm{T}},

(3)

where
c_{n,\mu}
represents the annihilation operator for the
\mu
th internal degree of freedom at the
n
th unit cell, and
A(\omega)
is a
qL\times qL
eigenvalue-dependent matrix. The eigenvalues of Eq. (
1
) can be calculated by solving the equation given by

\det M(\omega)=0.

(4)

Here,
M(\omega)
is defined as

M(\omega):=A(\omega)-f(\omega)\bm{1}_{qL},

(5)

where
\bm{1}_{qL}
is a
qL\times qL
identity matrix. We note that, when the matrix
A(\omega)
is independent of
\omega
and
f(\omega)=\omega
, the above procedure reduces to solving a conventional eigenvalue equation.

When the system size is relatively large, it is hard to solve the nonlinear eigenvalue equation (
1
) both analytically and numerically. The conventional Bloch band theory allows us to readily obtain the spectra of the nonlinear system with PBCs
17
. However, the nonlinear system exhibits the extreme sensitivity to boundary conditions; the discrepancy between the Bloch bands and the OBC spectra has been observed
53
;
27
. Thus, it is necessary to construct a general theory to calculate bands that reproduce the OBC spectra.

II.2
Non-Bloch band theory for nonlinear systems

We construct a theoretical framework for calculating the continuum bands of the nonlinear system, which is analogous to the non-Bloch band theory of non-Hermitian systems
47
;
48
;
49
. We rewrite the eigenvalue-dependent Hamiltonian (
2
) as

H(\omega)=\sum_{n}\sum_{i=-N}^{N}\sum_{\mu,\nu=1}^{q}t_{i,\mu\nu}(\omega)c_{n+i,\mu}^{\dagger}c_{n,\nu},

(6)

where particles can hop up to the
N
th nearest unit cell, and
t_{i,\mu\nu}(\omega)
are the eigenvalue-dependent hopping amplitudes toward the
i
th nearest unit cell. The eigenstate in Eq. (
1
) is represented by

|\psi(\omega)\rangle=(\psi_{1,1}(\omega),\ldots,\psi_{L,q}(\omega))^{\mathrm{T}}.

(7)

Building on a general theory of linear difference equations, for a given
\omega
, the eigenstate components can be written as a linear combination,

\psi_{n,\mu}(\omega)=\sum_{j}\beta_{j}^{-n}\phi_{\mu}^{(j)}(\omega).

(8)

Here, the set of
(\beta_{j},\phi_{\mu}^{(j)}(\omega))
is determined by the eigenvalue equation

\sum_{\nu=1}^{q}[\mathcal{H}(\omega,\beta)]_{\mu\nu}\phi_{\nu}(\omega)=f(\omega)\phi_{\mu}(\omega),

(9)

where

[\mathcal{H}(\omega,\beta)]_{\mu\nu}:=\sum_{i=-N}^{N}t_{i,\mu\nu}(\omega)\beta^{i}.

(10)

The condition for the nontrivial solutions of eigenvalue equation (
9
) is then given by

\det\mathcal{M}(\omega,\beta)=0.

(11)

Here, the matrix
\mathcal{M}(\omega,\beta)
is defined as

\mathcal{M}(\omega,\beta):=\mathcal{H}(\omega,\beta)-f(\omega)\bm{1}_{q},

(12)

where
\bm{1}_{q}
is a
q\times q
identity matrix. We note that Eq. (
11
) is an algebraic equation for
\beta
of
2qN
th degree.

By analogy with the conventional non-Bloch band theory, the solutions of Eq. (
11
) form continuum sets of
\beta=e^{ik}
, where
k
is the complex-valued Bloch wavenumber, and these sets allow for obtaining the continuum bands. The continuum sets of
k
determine the trajectories on the
\beta
-complex plane, which are referred to as the GBZs. For the solutions of Eq. (
11
), the condition for the GBZs is given by

|\beta_{qN}|=|\beta_{qN+1}|,

(13)

where the solutions satisfy

|\beta_{1}|\leq|\beta_{2}|\leq\cdots\leq|\beta_{2qN-1}|\leq|\beta_{2qN}|.

(14)

In Appendix
A
, we show that the OBC spectra under the condition (
13
) form the continuum sets in the limit of a large system size. The continuum bands obtained by combining Eqs. (
11
) and (
13
) reproduce the OBC spectra. We note that solving Eq. (
11
) with
\beta
replaced by
e^{ik}~(k\in\mathbb{R})
leads to the Bloch bands, which reproduce the PBC spectra.

II.3
Skin effect

The non-Bloch band theory allows for determining the GBZs formed by
\beta=e^{ik}~(k\in{\mathbb{C}})
. Physically, the complex-valued Bloch wavenumber means that the bulk eigenstates are localized at the edges of the system. This localization phenomenon is referred to as the skin effect, analogous to the non-Hermitian skin effect in non-Hermitian systems. According to Ref.
53
, the skin effect in the nonlinear system can be characterized by a
\mathbb{Z}
topological invariant defined from the real-valued Bloch wavenumber. This topological invariant is given by

W(\omega_{\text{ref}}):=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\mathrm{d}k\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}k}\ln\det\mathcal{M}(\omega_{\text{ref}},e^{ik}),

(15)

where
\omega_{\text{ref}}\in\mathbb{C}
is a reference point satisfying
\det\mathcal{M}(\omega_{\text{ref}},e^{ik})\neq 0
. This quantity is called the winding number, and its value remains the same unless the reference point crosses the Bloch band. The nonzero winding number coincides with the localization of the bulk eigenstates, and, for example, for regions with
W(\omega_{\text{ref}})>0
, the bulk eigenstates are localized at the right boundary of the system. We describe the correspondence between nonzero values of the winding number and the emergence of the skin modes in Appendix
B.1
.

III
One-dimensional system

In this section, we explore the band structures of 1D tight-binding systems described by the nonlinear eigenvalue equation (
1
). We first focus on a simple model and demonstrate that the continuum bands reproduce the OBC spectra. We next investigate a system with nonlinear pseudo-Hermiticity and find that the nonlinearity can induce the skin effect. The non-Bloch band theory then enables us to analyze the distinctive localization behavior.

III.1
Nonreciprocal hopping system

We consider a 1D nonlinear system with nonreciprocal hopping amplitudes, which has been introduced in Ref.

\displaystyle+\sum_{n=1}^{L-1}\left(t_{\mathrm{L}}c_{n,2}^{\dagger}c_{n+1,1}+t_{\mathrm{R}}c_{n+1,1}^{\dagger}c_{n,2}\right),

where

\mu=2

III.2
Nonlinearity-induced skin effect

In this section, we investigate a localization phenomenon unique to the nonlinear system. We then consider a system that preserves the nonlinear pseudo-Hermiticity
53
defined as

M(\omega^{\ast})=UM(\omega)^{\dagger}U,

(22)

where
M(\omega)
is given in Eq. (
5
), and
U
is a unitary and Hermitian matrix. Since the matrix
M(\omega)
satisfies

\det M(\omega^{\ast})=\det M(\omega)^{\dagger}=(\det M(\omega))^{\ast},

(23)

the eigenvalues are restricted to either be real or appear in complex-conjugate pairs. We find that the nonlinearity induces the skin effect, and the skin modes are localized at both ends of the system. The non-Bloch band theory enables us to reveal this localization behavior by calculating the GBZs.

III.2.1
Model

We consider a nonlinear system whose Hamiltonian is given by

H(\omega)=\sum_{n=1}^{L}{\bm{c}}_{n}^{\dagger}V(\omega){\bm{c}}_{n}+\sum_{n=1}^{L-1}({\bm{c}}_{n}^{\dagger}T{\bm{c}}_{n+1}+{\bm{c}}_{n+1}^{\dagger}T^{\dagger}{\bm{c}}_{n}),

(24)

where

V(\omega)=\begin{pmatrix}m+2\delta\omega&\delta\omega\\
\delta\omega&m\end{pmatrix},\quad T=\begin{pmatrix}-t&-iv\\
-iv&t\end{pmatrix},

(25)

and
\bm{c}_{n}=(c_{n,1},c_{n,2})^{\mathrm{T}}
. We set
f(\omega)=\omega
in Eq. (
1
). Suppose that all the parameters take positive real values, and
m<2\min(v,t)
. We note that the parameter
\delta
characterizes the strength of the nonlinearity. We also remark that this system preserves the nonlinear pseudo-Hermiticity (
22
), with
U
being an identity matrix. The characteristic equation (
11
) of this system is then given by

\det[V(\omega)+\beta^{-1}T+\beta T^{\dagger}-\omega\bm{1}_{2}]=0,

(26)

and it is a quartic equation for
\beta
. As a result, the condition for the GBZs is obtained by

|\beta_{2}|=|\beta_{3}|,

(27)

where the solutions of Eq. (
26
) satisfy
|\beta_{1}|\leq|\beta_{2}|\leq|\beta_{3}|\leq|\beta_{4}|
.

When
\delta=0
, the governing equation of the system reduces to a conventional eigenvalue equation with a Hermitian Hamiltonian. This indicates that all the eigenvalues of the system remain real for relatively small values of
\delta
, and the GBZ obtained in Eq. (
27
) forms a unit circle on the
\beta
-complex plane (gray curve in Fig.
2
(c)). As
\delta
increases, the transition from real- to complex-valued eigenvalues occurs. By solving Eq. (
26
) with
\beta
replaced by
e^{ik}~(k\in\mathbb{R})
, we find that the complex-valued eigenvalues emerge when
\delta>\sqrt{2}-1

The complex-valued continuum bands are calculated by combining Eqs. (
26

2

2
(b)). The continuum bands deviate from the Bloch bands, which means that the winding number (
15
) takes nonzero values in this system. Therefore, we find that the nonlinearity can induce the skin effect under the nonlinear pseudo-Hermiticity.

III.2.2
Localization property

We show the GBZs of this system in Fig.
2
(c). As
\delta
increases, the GBZs deviate from the unit circle, which indicates the emergence of the nonlinearity-induced skin effect. We point out that one trajectory lies inside the unit circle, while the other lies outside it. Physically, this means that the bulk eigenstates are localized at both ends of the system. In Fig.
2

\rho_{n}(\omega):=|\psi_{n,1}(\omega)|^{2}+|\psi_{n,2}(\omega)|^{2}.

(28)

We note that the eigenstates in Fig.
2

Meanwhile, the bidirectional localization behavior can also be understood in terms of the winding number (
15
). Figure
2
(a) shows that the winding number takes
+1
and
-1
in the red and blue regions, respectively. This indicates that the skin modes corresponding to
W=+1
and
W=-1
are localized at the right and left ends of the system, respectively. In Appendix
B.2
, we show that, for a given eigenvalue
\omega

|\psi(\omega)\rangle

|\psi(\omega^{\ast})\rangle

IV
Two-dimensional system

In this section, we study a 2D tight-binding system in which the skin effect occurs only in one direction due to, for example, mirror symmetry. We find that this property enables us to calculate the continuum bands within the non-Bloch framework constructed in Sec.
II
. To demonstrate the validity of this approach, we consider a nonlinear Chern insulator with nonreciprocal hopping amplitudes. We also explore the bulk-boundary correspondence in terms of a Chern number defined from the GBZs of auxiliary bands.

IV.1
Non-Bloch framework

We consider a 2D tight-binding system on a square lattice and assume that particles hop only in the
x
and
y
direction. Let
c_{(n_{x},n_{y}),\mu}
denote the annihilation operator for the
\mu
th internal degree of freedom at the site
(n_{x},n_{y})
. The Hamiltonian in the nonlinear eigenvalue equation (
1
) is then given by

\displaystyle H(\omega)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{n_{x},n_{y}}\sum_{\mu,\nu=1}^{q}\Bigg(\sum_{i=-N_{x}}^{N_{x}}t_{i,\mu\nu}^{(x)}(\omega)c_{(n_{x}+i,n_{y}),\mu}^{\dagger}c_{(n_{x},n_{y}),\nu}

(29)

\displaystyle+\sum_{i=-N_{y}}^{N_{y}}t_{i,\mu\nu}^{(y)}(\omega)c_{(n_{x},n_{y}+i),\mu}^{\dagger}c_{(n_{x},n_{y}),\nu}\Bigg),

where
N_{x}
and
N_{y}
represent the hopping range,
q
is the number of internal degrees of freedom, and
t^{(x)}_{i,\mu\nu}(\omega)
and
t^{(y)}_{i,\mu\nu}(\omega)
represent the eigenvalue-dependent hopping amplitudes in the
x
and
y
directions, respectively. The eigenstate of Eq. (
1
) is represented by

|\psi(\omega)\rangle=(\dots,\psi_{(n_{x},n_{y}),1}(\omega),\dots,\psi_{(n_{x},n_{y}),q}(\omega),\dots)^{\rm T}.

(30)

Similar to the 1D case, for a given
\omega
, the eigenstate component in Eq. (
30
) can be constructed by a linear combination,

\psi_{(n_{x},n_{y}),\mu}(\omega)=\sum_{j_{x},j_{y}}(\beta_{x,j_{x}})^{-n_{x}}(\beta_{y,j_{y}})^{-n_{y}}\phi_{\mu}^{(j_{x},j_{y})}(\omega).

(31)

Here, the set of
(\phi_{\mu}^{(j_{x},j_{y})}(\omega),\beta_{x},\beta_{y})
is determined by the eigenvalue equation

\displaystyle\sum_{\nu=1}^{q}[\mathcal{H}_{\rm 2D}(\omega,\beta_{x},\beta_{y})]_{\mu\nu}\phi_{\nu}(\omega)=f(\omega)\phi_{\mu}(\omega),

(32)

\displaystyle[\mathcal{H}_{\rm 2D}(\omega,\beta_{x},\beta_{y})]_{\mu\nu}=\sum_{i=-N_{x}}^{N_{x}}t_{i,\mu\nu}^{(x)}(\omega)\beta_{x}^{i}+\sum_{i=-N_{y}}^{N_{y}}t_{i,\mu\nu}^{(y)}(\omega)\beta_{y}^{i}.

(33)

We note that solving the eigenvalue equation (
32
) can be mapped to finding the solutions of the characteristic equation

\det\mathcal{M}(\omega,\beta_{x},\beta_{y})=0,

(34)

where the matrix
\mathcal{M}(\omega,\beta_{x},\beta_{y})
is defined as

\mathcal{M}(\omega,\beta_{x},\beta_{y}):=\mathcal{H}_{\rm 2D}(\omega,\beta_{x},\beta_{y})-f(\omega)\bm{1}_{q}.

(35)

By substituting
(\beta_{x},\beta_{y})=(e^{ik_{x}},e^{ik_{y}})~(k_{x},k_{y}\in\mathbb{R})
into Eq. (
34
), we obtain the Bloch bands that reproduce the spectra under full PBCs. In contrast, it is generally unclear how to calculate the continuum bands of the 2D system that reproduce the spectra under full OBCs. Nevertheless, we find that, in the system where the skin effect occurs only in one direction, the condition for the GBZs can be written in the same form as in the 1D case. A similar simplification of the condition has been studied in non-Hermitian systems
50
. In the following, we consider mirror symmetry defined as

\mathcal{U}\mathcal{M}(\omega,e^{ik_{x}},e^{-ik_{y}})^{\mathrm{T}}\mathcal{U}=\mathcal{M}(\omega,e^{ik_{x}},e^{ik_{y}}),

(36)

where
\mathcal{U}
is a unitary and Hermitian matrix. Physically, this symmetry suppresses nonreciprocal hopping amplitudes in the
y
direction, which indicates that the skin effect can occur only in the
x
direction. Therefore, one can calculate the continuum bands by combining the GBZs and Eq. (
34
) with
\beta_{y}
replaced by
e^{ik_{y}}~(k_{y}\in\mathbb{R})
. For the
2qN_{x}
solutions of the characteristic equation

\det\mathcal{M}(\omega,\beta_{x},e^{ik_{y}})=0~(k_{y}\in\mathbb{R}),

(37)

the condition for the GBZs is given by

|\beta_{x,qN_{x}}|=|\beta_{x,qN_{x}+1}|,

(38)

where the solutions satisfy

|\beta_{x,1}|\leq|\beta_{x,2}|\leq\cdots\leq|\beta_{x,2qN_{x}}|.

(39)

We remark that the continuum bands obtained from the GBZ condition (
38
) reproduce the spectra under full OBCs in this case.

IV.2
Nonreciprocal-hopping Chern insulator

In the following, we consider a nonlinear Chern insulator that exhibits the skin effect only in the
x
direction. We demonstrate that the continuum bands obtained by combining Eqs. (
37
) and (
38
) reproduce the spectra under full OBCs. We further show that a Chern number defined from the GBZs of auxiliary bands can predict the emergence of topological edge states.

IV.2.1
Model

Suppose that the system is on an
L\times L
square lattice. The Hamiltonian of the nonlinear Chern insulator is given by

\displaystyle H(\omega)=\sum_{n_{x},n_{y}=1}^{L}\bm{c}_{(n_{x},n_{y})}^{\dagger}V(\omega)\bm{c}_{(n_{x},n_{y})}

\displaystyle+\sum_{n_{x}=1}^{L-1}\sum_{n_{y}=1}^{L}(\bm{c}_{(n_{x},n_{y})}^{\dagger}T_{x}\bm{c}_{(n_{x}+1,n_{y})}+\bm{c}_{(n_{x}+1,n_{y})}^{\dagger}T_{x}^{\dagger}\bm{c}_{(n_{x},n_{y})})

\displaystyle+\sum_{n_{x}=1}^{L}\sum_{n_{y}=1}^{L-1}(\bm{c}_{(n_{x},n_{y})}^{\dagger}T_{y}\bm{c}_{(n_{x},n_{y}+1)}+\bm{c}_{(n_{x},n_{y}+1)}^{\dagger}T_{y}^{\dagger}\bm{c}_{(n_{x},n_{y})}),

(40)

where

\displaystyle V(\omega)=\begin{pmatrix}\alpha+m+\delta\omega&i\gamma_{x}\\
i\gamma_{x}&\alpha-m-\delta\omega\end{pmatrix},

\displaystyle T_{x}=\frac{1}{2}\begin{pmatrix}-t_{x}&-iv_{x}\\
-iv_{x}&t_{x}\end{pmatrix},\quad T_{y}=\frac{1}{2}\begin{pmatrix}-t_{y}&-v_{y}\\
v_{y}&t_{y}\end{pmatrix},

and

Bulk-boundary correspondence

V
Summary and Outlook

In summary, we develop the theoretical framework for calculating the continuum bands of tight-binding systems described by the nonlinear eigenvalue equation. We show how to calculate the continuum bands from the GBZs and demonstrate that they reproduce the spectra under OBCs. The GBZs allow us to analyze the localization behavior associated with the skin effect and various phenomena, such as the nonlinearity-induced skin effect. In the nonlinear Chern insulator with nonreciprocal hopping amplitudes, we establish the bulk-boundary correspondence between the Chern number defined from the GBZs of the auxiliary bands and the existence of the topological edge states.

The nonlinear eigenvalue equation considered here describes lattice systems, such as mechanical oscillators whose effective mass and effective stiffness depend on the eigenfrequencies
8
;
46
;
15
;
16
;
24
;
37
and electrical circuits that exhibit frequency-dependent gain
55
. Accordingly, the non-Bloch band theory can provide a powerful tool for investigating topological properties of such systems in the presence of the skin effect. Furthermore, it would be possible to study the continuum bands of general 2D nonlinear systems in terms of the amoeba formulation
43
. In addition, we expect that the non-Bloch band theory can be extended to a framework for analyzing continuous systems, which is analogous to previous studies on non-Hermitian systems
52
;
51
;
14
. For example, photonic crystals composed of metamaterials with frequency-dependent permittivity and permeability
21
;
18
;
23
;
34
;
40
;
31
;
28
can be described within this framework. It should be intriguing to explore complex-frequency excitations
20
;
26
in these photonic crystals by invoking the non-Bloch band theory.

Appendix A
Condition for the generalized Brillouin zone

In this section, we show that, in the 1D nonlinear tight-binding system with Hamiltonian (
6
), the condition for the GBZs is given by Eq. (
13
). The characteristic equation (
11
) is an algebraic equation for
\beta
of
2qN
th degree, where
N
and
q
are the hopping range and the internal degrees of freedom, respectively. For a given
\omega
, the components of the eigenvector can be written in terms of the
2qN
solutions of Eq. (
11
) as

\psi_{n,\mu}(\omega)=\sum_{j=1}^{2qN}\beta_{j}^{-n}\phi_{\mu}^{(j)}(\omega)~(n=1,\ldots,L,\;\mu=1,\ldots,q),

(52)

where
|\beta_{1}|\leq|\beta_{2}|\leq\dots\leq|\beta_{2qN-1}|\leq|\beta_{2qN}|
.

For the sake of simplicity, we impose the open boundary condition
\psi_{0,\mu}=\psi_{L+1,\mu}=0~(\mu=1,2,\dots,q)
. From Eq. (
52
), we obtain the simultaneous equations involving the
2q^{2}N
coefficients
\phi_{\mu}^{(j)}(\omega)
. Meanwhile, the eigenvalue equation (
9
) determines the relations among the coefficients
\phi_{1}^{(j)}(\omega),\phi_{2}^{(j)}(\omega),\dots,\phi_{\mu}^{(j)}(\omega)
. Hence, the
2q^{2}N
coefficients can be reduced to
2qN
unknown variables. Suppose that
\phi_{1}^{(1)}(\omega),\phi_{1}^{(2)}(\omega),\dots,\phi_{1}^{(2qN)}(\omega)
are taken as independent variables. The simultaneous equations can be then rewritten as
qN
equations at the left end of the open chain,

\sum_{j=1}^{2qN}F_{l}(\beta_{j},\omega,\mathcal{S})\phi_{1}^{(j)}(\omega)=0,~(l=1,2,\ldots,qN),

(53)

and
qN
equations at the right end of the open chain,

\sum_{j=1}^{2qN}\tilde{F}_{l}(\beta_{j},\omega,\mathcal{S})\beta_{j}^{-L}\phi_{1}^{(j)}(\omega)=0,~(l=1,2,\ldots,qN),

(54)

where
F_{l}
and
\tilde{F}_{l}
are functions of
\beta_{j}
,
\omega
, and the set of system parameters
\mathcal{S}
. We note that these functions do not depend on the system size
L
. By combining these equations, we obtain the condition for the existence of nontrivial solutions for
\phi_{1}^{(j)}
as

\begin{vmatrix}F_{1}(\beta_{1},\omega,\mathcal{S})&\cdots&F_{1}(\beta_{2qN},\omega,\mathcal{S})\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
F_{qN}(\beta_{1},\omega,\mathcal{S})&\cdots&F_{qN}(\beta_{2qN},\omega,\mathcal{S})\\
\tilde{F}_{1}(\beta_{1},\omega,\mathcal{S})\beta_{1}^{-L}&\cdots&\tilde{F}_{1}(\beta_{2qN},\omega,\mathcal{S})\beta_{2qN}^{-L}\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\tilde{F}_{qN}(\beta_{1},\omega,\mathcal{S})\beta_{1}^{-L}&\cdots&\tilde{F}_{qN}(\beta_{2qN},\omega,\mathcal{S})\beta_{2qN}^{-L}\end{vmatrix}=0.

(55)

The spectra of the open-boundary system can be obtained by solving Eq. (
55
). However, it is difficult to solve Eq. (
55
) for sufficiently large system sizes. Nevertheless, we can investigate the asymptotic behavior of its solutions in the limit of a large system size. Equation (
55
) can be rewritten as

\sum_{P,Q}G(\beta_{i\in P},\beta_{j\in Q},\omega,\mathcal{S})\prod_{k\in Q}\beta_{k}^{L}=0,

(56)

where
P
and
Q
are two disjoint subsets of
\{1,2,\ldots,2qN\}
, and both have
qN
elements. First, when
|\beta_{qN}|\neq|\beta_{qN+1}|
, there exists a single leading term on the left-hand side of Eq. (
56
), which is proportional to
(\beta_{qN+1}\beta_{qN+2}\cdots\beta_{2qN})^{L}
. Therefore, in the limit
L\rightarrow\infty
, Eq. (
56
) reduces to

G(\beta_{i\in P^{\prime}},\beta_{j\in Q^{\prime}},\omega,\mathcal{S})=0,

(57)

with
P^{\prime}=\{1,2,\ldots,qN\}
and
Q^{\prime}=\{qN+1,,qN+2,\ldots,2qN\}
. This condition leads to discrete sets of
\omega
. In contrast, when
|\beta_{qN}|=|\beta_{qN+1}|
, there are two leading terms on the left-hand side of Eq. (
56
): one proportional to
(\beta_{qN}\beta_{qN+2}\cdots\beta_{2qN})^{L}
and the other proportional to
(\beta_{qN+1}\beta_{qN+2}\cdots\beta_{2qN})^{L}
. Hence, Eq. (
56

\left(\frac{\beta_{qN}}{\beta_{qN+1}}\right)^{L}=-\frac{G(\beta_{i\in P_{0}},\beta_{j\in Q_{0}},\omega,\mathcal{S})}{G(\beta_{i\in P_{1}},\beta_{j\in Q_{1}},\omega,\mathcal{S})},

(58)

where
P_{0}=\{1,2,\ldots,qN\},Q_{0}=\{qN+1,qN+2,\ldots,2qN\},P_{1}=\{1,2,\ldots,qN-1,qN+1\}
, and
Q_{1}=\{qN,qN+2,\ldots,2qN\}
. This condition indicates that the relative phase between
\beta_{qN}
and
\beta_{qN+1}
can be changed almost continuously, which leads to continuum sets of
\omega
. Therefore, the eigenvalues under the OBC lie in the continuum sets of
\omega

\beta_{qN}
and
\beta_{qN+1}
. The continuum sets of
\beta_{qN}
and
\beta_{qN+1}
are formed by the condition
|\beta_{qN}|=|\beta_{qN+1}|
, which is nothing but the condition for the GBZs.

Appendix B
Topological aspect of the skin effect in the nonlinear system

In this section, we show that, when the winding number (
15
) takes nonzero values, the localized eigenstates emerge at one edge of the nonlinear system. We also show that, under the nonlinear pseudo-Hermiticity, the bulk eigenstates are localized at both edges of the system.

B.1
Topological origin of the skin effect

We first consider a tight-binding system described by the nonlinear eigenvalue equation (
1
). Solving the characteristic equation

\det\mathcal{M}(\omega,e^{ik})=0

(59)

enables us to calculate the Bloch bands, which reproduces the spectra under PBCs. We note that this equation is obtained by replacing
\beta
with
e^{ik}~(k\in\mathbb{R})
in Eq. (
11
). We recall that the winding number is defined as

W(\omega_{\text{ref}}):=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\mathrm{d}k\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}k}\ln\det\mathcal{M}(\omega_{\text{ref}},e^{ik}),

(60)

where
\omega_{\text{ref}}\in\mathbb{C}
is a reference point. Below, we show the bulk-edge correspondence between the nonzero winding number and the emergence of the skin modes. To this end, we define a Bloch Hamiltonian as

\tilde{\mathcal{H}}(k):=\begin{pmatrix}O&\mathcal{M}(\omega_{\text{ref}},e^{ik})\\
\mathcal{M}(\omega_{\text{ref}},e^{ik})^{\dagger}&O\end{pmatrix}.

(61)

This Bloch Hamiltonian preserves the chiral symmetry given by

\varGamma\tilde{\mathcal{H}}(k)\varGamma^{-1}=-\tilde{\mathcal{H}}(k),

(62)

where

\varGamma=\sigma_{z}\otimes\bm{1}_{q},~\sigma_{z}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&-1\end{pmatrix}.

(63)

Since the Hermitian Hamiltonian (
61
) belongs to class AIII in the Altland-Zirnbauer symmetry class
1
, its topological phase is characterized by the winding number (
60
)
32
.

As a consequence of the conventional bulk-boundary correspondence, when
W(\omega_{m})\neq 0
, zero-energy edge states emerge in the open-boundary Hamiltonian given by

\tilde{H}:=\begin{pmatrix}O&M(\omega_{m})\\
M(\omega_{m})^{\dagger}&O\end{pmatrix},

(64)

where
M(\omega)
is given by Eq. (
5
), and
\omega_{m}
are obtained by solving Eq. (
4
). We define the vectors
|\psi^{\mathrm{R}}(\omega_{m})\rangle
and
|\psi^{\mathrm{L}}(\omega_{m})\rangle
as

M(\omega_{m})|\psi^{\mathrm{R}}(\omega_{m})\rangle=0,~M(\omega_{m})^{\dagger}|\psi^{\mathrm{L}}(\omega_{m})\rangle=0.

(65)

The topological edge states of this Hamiltonian can then be constructed by

|\tilde{\psi}_{+}\rangle=\begin{pmatrix}|\psi^{\mathrm{L}}(\omega_{m})\rangle\\
\bm{0}\end{pmatrix},~|\tilde{\psi}_{-}\rangle=\begin{pmatrix}\bm{0}\\
|\psi^{\mathrm{R}}(\omega_{m})\rangle\end{pmatrix}.

(66)

The subscript
\pm
denotes the chirality eigenvalue of
\Gamma=\sigma_{z}\otimes\bm{1}_{qL}
, namely,
\Gamma|\tilde{\psi}_{\pm}\rangle=\pm|\tilde{\psi}_{\pm}\rangle
. Since
\omega_{m}
are the bulk spectra of the original nonlinear system,
|\psi^{\mathrm{R}}(\omega_{m})\rangle
represent the right eigenstates that are localized at one edge of the system. In other words, the nonzero winding number coincides with the emergence of the numerous localized states in the nonlinear system. We note that the sign of Eq. (
60
) determines the localization direction of the skin modes.

B.2
Localization property under the nonlinear pseudo-Hermiticity

We next investigate the localization property of the skin mode under the nonlinear pseudo-Hermiticity. As shown below, the bulk eigenstates are localized at both edges of the system. In this case, we recall that the matrix
\mathcal{M}(\omega,e^{ik})
satisfies

\mathcal{M}(\omega^{\ast},e^{ik})=\mathcal{U}\mathcal{M}(\omega,e^{ik})^{\dagger}\mathcal{U},

(67)

where
\mathcal{U}
is a
q\times q
unitary and Hermitian matrix. For a given reference point
\omega_{\rm ref}\in\mathbb{C}
, we define a Bloch Hamiltonian as

\tilde{\mathcal{H}}_{\mathcal{U}}(k):=\begin{pmatrix}O&\mathcal{U}\mathcal{M}(\omega_{\text{ref}},e^{ik})\\
\mathcal{M}(\omega_{\text{ref}},e^{ik})^{\dagger}\mathcal{U}&O\end{pmatrix}.

(68)

Since this Bloch Hamiltonian preserves the chiral symmetry

\varGamma\tilde{\mathcal{H}}_{\mathcal{U}}(k)\varGamma^{-1}=-\tilde{\mathcal{H}}_{\mathcal{U}}(k),

(69)

where
\varGamma
is given by Eq. (
63
), it is characterized by a
\mathbb{Z}

W_{\mathcal{U}}(\omega_{\text{ref}}):=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\mathrm{d}k\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}k}\ln\det[\mathcal{U}\mathcal{M}(\omega_{\text{ref}},e^{ik})].

(70)

We note that this invariant is equivalent to the winding number (
60
) because
\det[\mathcal{U}\mathcal{M}(\omega_{\text{ref}},e^{ik})]=\det\mathcal{M}(\omega_{\text{ref}},e^{ik})
.

Under the nonlinear pseudo-Hermiticity, the matrix
M(\omega)
given in Eq. (
5

M(\omega^{\ast})=UM(\omega)^{\dagger}U,

(71)

where
U
is a
qL\times qL
unitary and Hermitian matrix. From the conventional bulk-edge correspondence, when
W_{\mathcal{U}}(\omega_{m})\neq 0
, there exist zero-energy edge states of the Hamiltonian given by

\tilde{H}_{U}:=\begin{pmatrix}O&UM(\omega_{m})\\
M(\omega_{m})^{\dagger}U&O\end{pmatrix},

(72)

where
\omega_{m}
is obtained by solving Eq. (
4

71

|\tilde{\psi}_{U,+}\rangle=\begin{pmatrix}|\psi(\omega_{m}^{\ast})\rangle\\
\bm{0}\end{pmatrix},~|\tilde{\psi}_{U,-}\rangle=\begin{pmatrix}\bm{0}\\
|\psi(\omega_{m})\rangle\end{pmatrix},

(73)

where
|\psi(\omega_{m})\rangle
satisfies

M(\omega_{m})|\psi(\omega_{m})\rangle=0,

(74)

and the meaning of the subscript
\pm
is the same as in Sec.
B.1
. We here remark that the topological edge states with opposite chirality are localized at the left and right edges of the system, respectively. This means that the bulk eigenstates
|\psi(\omega_{m})\rangle
and
|\psi(\omega_{m}^{\ast})\rangle
of the nonlinear system are localized at the edges opposite to each other. Therefore, systems with the nonlinear pseudo-Hermiticity exhibit the bidirectional localization behavior.
