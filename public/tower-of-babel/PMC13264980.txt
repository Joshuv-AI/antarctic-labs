Prolonged Prescription Rates of Z-Hypnotics Among Individuals with Chronic Musculoskeletal Pain and Insomnia: The HUNT Study
Authors: Nora Boldermo Rian, Eivind Schjelderup Skarpsno, Anne Lovise Nordstoga, Karoline Moe, Bjarne Austad, Turid Follestad, Melanie Rae Simpson, Lene Aasdahl
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeMusculoskeletal pain and insomnia frequently co-occur. Although insomnia is often treated with hypnotics, prolonged use is not recommended. The prevalence of prolonged hypnotic prescriptions among individuals with both conditions remains unknown. This descriptive epidemiological study aimed to estimate prolonged prescriptions among individuals with insomnia and chronic musculoskeletal pain.Patients and MethodsIn this cross-sectional study, data from 40,790 participants in the Trøndelag Health Study (HUNT4) were linked to the Norwegian Prescription Database for Z-hypnotic prescriptions during the participation year. Prolonged prescriptions were defined as ≥90 defined daily doses. Prevalence estimates were calculated by insomnia and chronic pain status, stratified by age, sex, and pain severity.ResultsAmong participants with insomnia, prolonged prescription prevalence was higher in those with chronic pain (12.9%) compared to insomnia alone (8.7%). Stratification revealed marked age and sex differences: women aged 45–69 had prevalences of 13.7% if they had pain and insomnia versus 10.2% if they had insomnia alone, increasing to 31.1% and 25.3% for women ≥70 years. For men, rates were 8.1% versus 9.3% at ages 45–69 years and 19.2% versus 14.9% at ≥70 years. Younger participants had prevalences between 1.4% and 2.7%. Pain severity influenced prevalence only among participants aged ≥70 years, and more strongly among men (22.5% with high impact pain versus 14.9% without pain).ConclusionChronic musculoskeletal pain was associated with higher prescription rates, though not statistically significant within age categories, and pain severity had some influence among older individuals. Regardless of pain status, prolonged Z-hypnotic prescriptions increased with age, with women receiving more than men.Keywords: Norwegian prescription database, prevalence, age and sex differences, sleep medication, musculoskeletal pain, HUNT

Introduction

Musculoskeletal pain and insomnia are highly prevalent conditions that frequently co-occur.1 Globally, the prevalence of musculoskeletal pain is estimated to be around 31% in the adult population,2 while 12.4% suffer from insomnia.3 Among individuals with chronic musculoskeletal pain defined as pain lasting more than 3 months, more than half report insomnia symptoms.4,5 Insomnia among individuals with chronic pain is associated with greater pain severity, longer pain duration, more disability, additional comorbidities, and poorer prognosis.6,7 It may therefore be important to manage insomnia effectively in these individuals, and to ensure that treatment aligns with established clinical guidelines, while recognizing the need for a holistic approach to this complex patient group.

Guidelines for managing insomnia recommend cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia (CBT-I) as the initial treatment,8 also for individuals with chronic pain and insomnia.9 However, due to lack of resources and few trained CBT-I practitioners, sleep medication remains a commonly used therapeutic modality.8,10 Pain-related symptoms can also complicate compliance with the main components of CBT-I (ie, stimulus control and sleep restriction), potentially making sleep medication prescription more likely in patients with pain conditions. Z-hypnotics are the most commonly prescribed sleep medications in Norway with an annual prevalence of approximately 9%.11 These medications may offer an effective short-term solution for transient sleep disturbance.12 However, use beyond four weeks requires careful consideration due to potential risks, including development of dependence, pharmacological tolerance and adverse side effects, in addition to the lack of robust scientific evidence supporting long-term efficacy.8,12

Few studies have assessed the use of sleep medication in individuals with chronic musculoskeletal pain and co-occurring insomnia. Existing research has largely relied on self-reported medication use4 and has seldom differentiated between specific medication classes (eg, Z-hypnotics, benzodiazepines, melatonin).7,13–18 Moreover, important factors such as pain severity, along with age- and sex-related differences, have received limited attention, despite evidence showing substantial variability in both medication use patterns and treatment modalities.19,20 To date, no studies have examined prolonged prescriptions of sleep medication using registry-based prescription data among individuals who experience both chronic musculoskeletal pain and insomnia. It also remains unclear whether individuals with these co-occurring conditions receive hypnotic prescriptions more frequently than those with insomnia alone, and how prescription rates vary according to pain-related functional limitations (ie, high impact pain).

The aim of this study was to examine prolonged prescription rates of Z-hypnotics among individuals with chronic musculoskeletal pain and insomnia, and to assess whether the associations vary according to age, sex, and pain severity.

Materials and Methods

Study Sample

This descriptive study used cross-sectional data from the fourth survey of the population-based Trøndelag Health Study (HUNT4), conducted between 2017 and 2019. These data were linked to the Norwegian Prescription Database and Statistics Norway using a unique personal identification number assigned to all Norwegian citizens. All inhabitants aged 20 years and above living in a county in the middle of Norway were invited to participate. A total of 56,039 of 103,800 invited individuals (54.0%) agreed to participate, met for clinical examination, and answered questionnaires on lifestyle and health. Of these, 40,790 (72.8%) answered a second questionnaire including musculoskeletal health, which was distributed following the clinical examination. A detailed description of HUNT4 can be found elsewhere.21 Compared to those who attended the clinical examination, those who did not respond to the second questionnaire were somewhat younger (mean age 50.7 versus 54.6 years), and a higher proportion were men (51.7% versus 43.0%), reported slightly more insomnia (19.8% versus 18.8% for women and 12.6% versus 10.4% for men), and a comparable proportion were prescribed Z-hypnotics during the study period (8.3% versus 8.2%).

Ethics

The study was original approved by the Regional Committee for Medical and Health Research Ethics in Central Norway (Ref. no. 264465, 22.06.2021). An amended protocol including this specific study was later submitted and approved (04.12.2024). This approval is a prerequisite for data access, analysis and publication on HUNT data, ensuring compliance with Norwegian health research regulations and safeguarding participant confidentiality. The results are presented according to the STROBE statement.22

Chronic Musculoskeletal Pain and Pain Severity

Chronic musculoskeletal pain was assessed by a question adopted from the Standard Nordic Questionnaire:23 “During the last year, have you had pain in muscles and joints that lasted for at least 3 consecutive months?”, with the response option “Yes” and “No”. Participants who answered “Yes” to this question were asked to indicate whether the pain reduced their work ability and/or leisure time activity based on the two following questions: 1) “Has the pain in muscles or joints reduced your ability to work”, and 2) “Has the pain in muscles or joints reduced your leisure activity?”, with the response options “Yes” and “No”. Participants who answered “Yes” to at least one of these two questions were classified as having high impact pain.

Insomnia Disorder

Insomnia was assessed by four questions: 1) “How often during the last 3 months have you had difficulty falling asleep at night?”, 2) “How often during the last 3 months have you woken up repeatedly during the night?”, 3) “How often during the last 3 months have you woken up too early and could not get back to sleep?” and 4) “How often during the last 3 months have you had poor daytime functioning (socially or professionally) due to sleep problems?” with three response options; “never/seldom”, “sometimes”, and “at least three times a week”.24 If the participants answered “at least three times a week” on at least one of the three first questions, and in addition answered “sometimes” or “at least three times a week” on question 4, they were classified as having insomnia. This classification approximates insomnia diagnosis according to the International Classification of Sleep Disorders (ICSD-3).25,26 Participants who did not fulfill the insomnia diagnostic classification were classified as having no insomnia.

Prescribed Z-Hypnotics

Prescribed sleep medications the year of participation (1st of January - 31st of December, 2017–19) was extracted from The Norwegian Prescription Database. The registry contains information on prescription drugs dispensed by pharmacies to individuals outside of hospital settings and institutional care in Norway. Specifically, the registry contains information about the person receiving the drug(s) and the product (name, dose, and Anatomical Therapeutic Chemical (ATC) code). In this study, information on medication type was available down to the ATC 4th level (chemical/therapeutic/pharmacological subgroup). Drugs dispensed without prescriptions are not recorded in the database.

Sleep medications included were benzodiazepine related drugs, often referred to as Z-hypnotics, which include agents with ATC code N05CF (zopiclone and zolpidem). In clinical practice, agents with broader indications are also frequently used in treatment of sleep problems.8 Based on the level of detail in the available data, we included benzodiazepine derivatives with ATC codes N05CD (nitrazepam, midazolam and remimazolam) and N05BA (diazepam, oxazepam, lorazepam and alprazolam), as well as melatonin-based products (ATC code N05CH), in two separate supplementary analyses to examine their impact on prevalence estimates. Prescriptions for palliative treatment were identified by a specific reimbursement code and excluded from the study.

The dispensed amount of Z-hypnotics is registered as defined daily doses (DDD), an international standardized unit corresponding to the assumed average daily dose of a drug used for its main indication in adults. For each participant, information on year and month of drug dispensing was available, allowing calculation of total number of DDDs dispensed during the year of participation in HUNT4. Prolonged use was defined as ≥90 DDD within 12 months. Clinical guidelines recommend restricting hypnotic use to short-term courses (typically 2–4 weeks).8 Theoretically 90 DDD could reflect several short treatment courses within guideline recommendations; however, insomnia disorder was defined by symptoms lasting at least three months, indicating a chronic condition for which long-term pharmacological treatment is not recommended. Therefore, accumulating ≥90 DDD over one year was considered a clinically meaningful indicator of extended use that exceeds guideline recommendations. In addition, we included a secondary threshold of ≥30 DDD to capture use exceeding the recommended duration of a single treatment episode and providing a broader overview of prescribing patterns across symptom groups and age categories.

Other Variables

Sociodemographic variables included were age, sex, and highest level of completed education (primary school, high school, college ≤4 years, and college >4 years). Education was obtained from Statistics Norway. Symptoms of anxiety and depression were assessed using the Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS).27 The instrument comprises 7 items assessing anxiety and 7 items assessing depression. Each item is scored from 0 (no symptoms) to 3 (severe symptoms), and sum subscale scores range from 0 to 21, with higher scores indicating greater symptom severity. In accordance with established recommendations, a cutoff score of 8 or higher on both anxiety and depression subscales was applied.27 Body mass index (BMI) was calculated as weight in kilograms divided by height in meters squared (kg/m2), using standardized measurements of height (to the nearest centimeter) and weight (to the nearest 0.5 kg) obtained during the clinical examination. Physical activity was assessed by three questions, with categorical response options, on frequency (never, less than once a week, once a week, 2 to 3 times per week, and almost every day), intensity (easy without breaking into sweat or losing my breath, so hard that I lose my breath and break into a sweat, and near-exhaustion) and duration (less than 15 minutes, 15–29 minutes, 30–60 minutes and more than 60 minutes) of physical exercise per week.28 Participants were classified as meeting physical activity recommendations if they engaged in at least 150 minutes of moderate intensity exercise or at least 75 minutes of vigorous intensity exercise per week.29

Statistical Analysis

Descriptive statistics for all sociodemographic factors and health-related symptoms were presented according to the symptom combinations of chronic musculoskeletal pain and insomnia, stratified by sex. Data are presented as counts with percentages, or as means with standard deviations (SD), as appropriate. For variables with missing values, percentages are calculated based on the number of available responses. Poisson regression models with robust variance estimation were used to estimate the proportion of participants with ≥90 DDD (prolonged use, primary descriptive outcome) and ≥30 DDD (secondary descriptive outcome) of sleep medications, based on the presence of musculoskeletal pain and/or insomnia. The reference group in all analyses was defined as “Insomnia + No pain”, and both the prevalence differences (PDs) and prevalence ratios (PRs) were presented with 95% confidence intervals (CI). Poisson regression was chosen over logistic regression because it provides direct estimates of PR, which are more interpretable than odds ratios.30 Analyses were initially conducted on the entire sample, after which they were stratified by sex and subsequently by age category (20−44, 45−69, and ≥70 years) within each sex. Due to the descriptive design of the study, we harmonized the prevalence estimates across important demographic variables by adjusting all models for age (as a continuous variable within each age category) and education level (primary school, high school, college ≤4 years, college >4 years, and unknown). The decision not to include lifestyle factors (eg, BMI and physical activity) or other health-related variables (eg, mental health) as covariates was based on the principle that adjustment should be applied cautiously in descriptive studies without a causal aim, consistent with a framework for descriptive epidemiology.31 Adjusted prevalence differences are reported in percentage points (pp) to accurately reflect absolute differences between proportions. Finally, six interaction analyses for the primary threshold (≥90 DDD) were conducted to assess whether the prevalence differed by symptom group, age and sex; insomnia × pain (overall and by sex) and sex × symptom group within each age category. The overall insomnia × pain interaction was the primary comparison, while the remaining tests were exploratory. No formal multiplicity correction was applied due to the limited number and the exploratory nature of these analyses. Forest plots were used to illustrate the proportion of women and men with insomnia disorder, across pain and age categories, who received ≥90 DDD and ≥30 DDD during the 12-month study period, with corresponding 95% CIs. In addition, Poisson regression was also conducted to estimate prevalence, PDs and PRs across different pain categories (no pain, low impact pain, and high impact pain).

We conducted supplementary analyses in which benzodiazepines and melatonin were separately included alongside Z-hypnotics to evaluate their impact on the prevalence estimates and potential differences across age categories. In addition, to assess whether the timing of prescribed medication influenced the estimates, we performed a sensitivity analysis examining the proportion of individuals who received ≥90 DDD of Z-hypnotics during the study period and also received ≥90 DDD in the year preceding and the year following their participation in HUNT4. We also conducted a sensitivity analysis adjusting for symptoms of anxiety and depression (HADS subscales) to evaluate how co-occurring mental health symptoms influenced the prevalence estimates. Additionally, to capture a more chronic pattern of use, we conducted a sensitivity analysis using ≥180 DDD of Z-hypnotics as a threshold in the whole sample and in sex-stratified analyses (age-stratified analyses were not possible due to small cell sizes). Finally, we conducted a sensitivity analysis where the no-insomnia groups were restricted to participants with no recorded dispensing of sleep medications (ATC codes N05CF, N05CH or N05CD) in the 12 months prior to completion of the insomnia questionnaire, to reduce the risk of differential misclassification due to pharmacological suppression of symptoms. The total amount of Z-hypnotics dispensed during the 12 months after participation in HUNT4 was subsequently calculated.

We considered p-values (two-tailed) <0.05 to be statistically significant. Precision was assessed using 95% confidence intervals. The Poisson regression and interaction analyses were conducted exploratorily to aid statistical interpretation of subgroup differences and were not based on predefined hypotheses. The increased risk of false positives is acknowledged. All analyses were performed using Stata, version 19 (StataCorp. 2025. Stata Statistical Software: Release 19. College Station, TX: StataCorp LLC).

Results

Among 40,790 participants, 53.6% (n=21,859) reported chronic musculoskeletal pain, 15.2% (n=6196) reported insomnia, and 11.2% (n=4568) reported both chronic musculoskeletal pain and insomnia (Table 1). Women comprised 56.9% of the sample. The mean (SD) age was 54.6 (17.1) years among women and 57.1 (16.9) years among men. The overall prevalence for receiving at least one prescription of Z-hypnotics (>0 DDD) was 8.2% (10.8% among women and 4.9% among men), whereas the overall prevalence of receiving ≥90 DDD of Z-hypnotics was 4.6% (6.1% among women and 2.6% among men). Approximately 88% of all participants were prescribed neither z-hypnotics, benzodiazepine, nor melatonin (Table 1).

Prolonged Prescriptions in Individuals with and without Chronic Musculoskeletal Pain

The overall prevalence of prolonged prescriptions (≥90 DDD) of Z-hypnotics among those with insomnia, was 12.9% if they had chronic musculoskeletal pain and 8.7% if they had insomnia alone (Table 2). Prevalence rates for all symptom groups are presented in Table 2, both for the total sample and stratified by sex. These findings showed that the prevalence of prolonged prescriptions varies by pain status in the total sample. In sex-stratified analyses, the difference between those with insomnia alone and those with insomnia and pain was more pronounced among women than men. Individuals with pain had higher prevalence, supported by a significant interaction term between insomnia and pain in the overall sample (p<0.001), as well as for women (p=0.001) and for men (p=0.004).

Prevalence across age groups varied substantially, but adjusted PDs and PRs comparing individuals with insomnia alone to those with insomnia and chronic musculoskeletal pain were not statistically significant within the age groups (Table 3). For example, among women aged 20–44 years, the prevalence was 1.4% for insomnia alone and 2.7% for insomnia and chronic musculoskeletal pain, corresponding to an adjusted PD of 0.6 pp (95% CI −1.1 to 2.2) and a PR of 1.3 (95% CI 0.5 to 3.4). In the 45–69-years age group, prevalence was 10.2% in women with insomnia alone and 13.7% in women with insomnia and chronic musculoskeletal pain (adjusted PD 2.2 pp (95% CI −1.4 to 5.7) and PR 1.2 (95% CI 0.9 to 1.6)). Among those aged ≥70 years, prevalence was 25.3% for insomnia alone and 31.1% for insomnia and chronic musculoskeletal pain, corresponding to an adjusted PD of 5.1 pp (95% CI −2.3 to12.6) and PR of 1.2 (95% CI 0.9 to 1.6).

Overall, prescription rates across age groups and pain and sleep categories remained consistently higher in women than in men. Among men, the prevalence of receiving ≥90 DDD varied largely across age groups but was only modestly higher in those with insomnia and chronic musculoskeletal pain compared to those with insomnia alone in the oldest age group (≥70 years) (Table 3). Specifically, among men aged 70 years, the prevalence was 14.9% in those with insomnia alone and 19.2% in those with insomnia and chronic musculoskeletal pain, corresponding to an adjusted PD of 3.9 pp (95% CI −3.6 to 11.4) and PR of 1.3 (95% CI 0.8 to 2.1). In the 45–69-years age group, prevalence was 9.3% for insomnia alone and 8.1% for insomnia and chronic musculoskeletal pain (adjusted PD of −2.2 pp (95% CI −6.7 to 2.2) and PR of 0.8 (95% CI 0.5 to 1.2)). Among the youngest age group, few received prolonged prescriptions. There was some evidence of an interaction between sex and symptoms category in the middle-aged (p=0.02) and the oldest age groups (p=0.02), indicating that the association between symptom categories and prolonged prescriptions differed by sex in these age strata. However, these results should be interpreted with care due to the issue of multiple testing.

A secondary analysis, applying the ≥30 DDD cutoff revealed a more pronounced difference in Z-hypnotic prescriptions among men aged ≥70 years with insomnia alone compared with those reporting both insomnia and chronic musculoskeletal pain (Table 3). In specific, prevalence was 17.4% in men aged ≥70 years with insomnia alone and 27.6% among those with insomnia and chronic musculoskeletal pain, corresponding to an adjusted PD of 9.7 pp (95% CI 1.5 to 17.9) and PR of 1.6 (95% CI 1.0 to 2.4).

Prolonged Prescriptions in Individuals with High Impact Chronic Musculoskeletal Pain

Figure 1 presents the prevalence of Z-hypnotic prescriptions among women and men with insomnia, stratified by pain severity (no pain, low impact pain, and high impact pain). Differences in the proportion receiving ≥90 DDD across pain categories within each age group were not statistically significant (Supplementary Table S1). Among individuals younger than 70 years, prevalence was comparable across pain levels irrespective of sex (Supplementary Table S1). In women aged ≥70 years, prevalence increased from 25.3% (no pain) to 29.6% (low impact pain) and 32.2% (high impact pain), with an adjusted PD of 6.3 pp (95% CI −1.8 to 14.4) for high impact pain compared with no pain. Among men aged ≥70 years, the proportion prescribed Z-hypnotics was comparable between those reporting no pain and those with low impact pain, whereas a higher prevalence was observed among those with high impact pain. Specifically, the prevalence among men with high impact pain was 22.5% compared to 14.9% among those with no pain, corresponding to an adjusted PD of 7.5 pp (95% CI −1.4 to 16.4).

Supplementary Analyses

When benzodiazepine was included in the prevalence estimates of receiving ≥90 DDD, the largest absolute increase in prevalence was observed among women ≥70 years with insomnia alone (from 25.3% to 29.3%). The change among the youngest age groups was below 1 pp (Supplementary Table S2). The inclusion of melatonin modestly increased the prevalence of receiving ≥90 DDD in the middle-aged and oldest age groups. However, among women 20–44 years with insomnia and pain, the prevalence increased from 2.7% in the main analysis to 6.4% after inclusion of melatonin. Among the youngest men with insomnia and pain, the prevalence increased from 2.2% to 4.8% (Supplementary Table S3). Additionally, there was also observed a higher prevalence among those with insomnia alone in the youngest age group in both sexes (1.4% versus 4.1% in women, 1.7% versus 3.9% in men) (Supplementary Table S3). When comparing the year preceding- and the year following the study period, the prevalence of individuals prescribed ≥90 DDD of Z-hypnotics remained consistent across both sexes and all symptom groups. Among individuals with ≥90 DDD prescribed Z-hypnotics during the year of participation in HUNT4, 86.2% had been prescribed ≥90 DDD the year before and 87.8% in the following year (Supplementary Table S4). Adjustment for mental health symptoms resulted in smaller differences between those with insomnia with and without pain across all symptom groups and age categories (Supplementary Table S5). The patterns observed in the analyses of the overall sample and in the sex-stratified analyses (Table 2), were quite similar when applying a threshold of ≥180 DDD (Supplementary Table S6). Excluding participants who had received any sleep medications the year before completion of the insomnia questionnaire from the no insomnia groups, did not alter the primary estimates to any substantial degree when comparing the groups with insomnia (Supplementary Table S7).

Discussion

The overall prevalence of prolonged prescriptions (≥90 DDD) of Z-hypnotics during the year of participation in HUNT4 was 12.9% among individuals with both insomnia disorder and musculoskeletal pain, compared to 8.7% among those with insomnia alone. When stratified by age and sex, it became evident that the prevalence increased substantially with age and remained consistently higher among women than men. Prolonged prescriptions were somewhat more common in women with both insomnia and chronic musculoskeletal pain compared to women with insomnia alone. Pain severity appeared to influence prolonged prescription patterns only among participants aged 70 years and older. Among women, prevalence tended to increase with pain severity; however, among men, a higher prevalence of prolonged prescriptions was evident only for men with high impact pain compared to men with no pain.

Few studies have specifically examined the prevalence of sleep medication prescriptions among individuals experiencing both musculoskeletal pain and insomnia, which limits direct comparison with previous research. However, a register-based study from primary care in Iceland found that individuals with multimorbidity, including several musculoskeletal pain diagnoses, had a substantially higher likelihood of receiving prescriptions of hypnotics and anxiolytics compared to those without multimorbidity.32 In that study, individuals with pain-related and mental health diagnoses were overrepresented among those receiving these medications, particularly among individuals with multiple prescriptions.32 Given that the co-occurrence of insomnia and pain is associated with higher symptom severity, more complex treatment needs, and poorer prognosis,6 this may contribute to a prolonged need for treatment of both conditions. In line with findings from the Icelandic study, this could also imply a higher risk of extended or repeated prescriptions for Z-hypnotics in this group. This is supported by the finding that a higher proportion of individuals with both conditions received prolonged prescriptions during the study period, as well as in the preceding and following year.

Our findings suggest that women with both insomnia and chronic musculoskeletal pain were more likely to have prolonged use of Z-hypnotics compared to those with insomnia alone. However, even though this difference was statistically significant among women overall, it was not statistically significant within any of the age groups. Among men, there was a somewhat higher prevalence of prolonged prescription among those with both insomnia and pain only in the oldest age group, and no statistically significant difference overall. These sex-specific differences may partly be explained by variation in pain-related symptom burden, which have been linked to sex differences in the physiological pain modulation.33 Moreover, pain catastrophizing is more prevalent among women,34 potentially adding to the overall burden and contributing to higher treatment needs. In line with this, we observed a statistically significant interaction between sex and symptom category among the middle-aged and oldest participants, indicating higher prescription rates among women than men with chronic pain. As noted earlier, these results should be interpreted with caution because no adjustments for multiple testing were applied. Nevertheless, differences in health-seeking behavior may also play a significant role in shaping prescription patterns. Men are generally less likely to visit their general practitioner than women,35 which may result in fewer opportunities for receiving prescriptions. While general practitioner visits tend to increase with age,36 they are likely also influenced by symptom severity. This may explain why we observed a tendency toward higher prescription rates among those with both insomnia and pain compared to insomnia alone only among the oldest men, and not among younger men, as well as even higher rates among the oldest men with high impact pain.

The results clearly indicate that age is a major factor driving higher prescription rates, consistent with previous research.37 Notably, insomnia prevalence was lowest among the oldest age group and therefore cannot fully explain the age-related increase. One possibility is that older adults may respond differently to sleep questions,26 as age-related changes in sleep architecture, increased prevalence of conditions disturbing sleep, like nocturia, as well as changes in daily routines after retirement, may alter expectations, needs, and perceptions of sleep. Such differences could contribute to underreporting of insomnia symptoms in older adults despite their higher use of hypnotics. Due to more multimorbidity, polypharmacy is more common in older adults, which in turn can affect sleep quality and potentially increase the likelihood of both sleep disturbances and hypnotic prescribing. Long-term users may come to view sleep medication as a normal part of their routine, which can influence both recall and expectations regarding sleep quality. This could explain the higher prevalence rates among the “no insomnia”-symptom groups in the oldest age groups compared to the younger age groups.

While greater multimorbidity among older adults likely contributes to the higher prescribing rates, prescribing culture may also play a role. In clinical practice, physicians may prioritize symptom relief over curative treatment when full recovery is less realistic. This interpretation is supported by a recent Norwegian study showing that general practitioners’ hypnotic prescribing to older adults was characterized by a pragmatic, patient-centered approach shaped by shared decision-making, limited treatment alternatives, and constrained resources.38

The inclusion of melatonin in the supplementary analyses substantially increased the estimated prevalence of prolonged sleep medication use among individuals with insomnia in the youngest age group for both women and men. Prevalence estimates more than doubled when melatonin was included, suggesting that prolonged use of sleep medication in this age group is far more common than in the analyses limited to Z-hypnotics. Importantly, the difference between those with and without pain remained unchanged in this age group. Current treatment recommendations state that melatonin can be considered for individuals older than 55 years.8 That at least 3%–7% of individuals under 45 years with insomnia disorder received ≥90 DDD of hypnotics, including melatonin, warrants attention in research and clinical practice. This concern is further reinforced by the fact that melatonin use is likely to be higher since it is readily available over the counter in Norway.

It should be noted that prescribed medication does not necessarily reflect actual use. However, previous research has shown moderate to substantial agreement between self-reported use and dispensed hypnotics, and moderate agreement for anxiolytics (includes benzodiazepines included in this study).39 Hypnotics and anxiolytics are often prescribed for intermittent use, which may reduce agreement between registry data and actual consumption. Nonetheless, a substantial proportion of those prescribed ≥90 DDD of Z-hypnotics also had prescriptions in the preceding and following year, suggesting prolonged use.

Strengths and Limitations

Strengths of this study include the large sample from the general population and the use of national registry-based information on prescriptions and educational level. However, some limitations should be acknowledged. Questionnaire-based classification of insomnia disorder carries a risk of misclassification. First, it does not account for evaluation of other conditions and/or drugs as sources of the insomnia symptoms.26 In our study, the overall prevalence of insomnia disorder was 15.2%. This was somewhat higher than in a recent meta-analysis3 that used standardized structured clinical interviews, the gold standard for estimating prevalence in the general population, but somewhat lower than in comparable Norwegian studies relying on self-report measures.5,40 The latter discrepancy is likely explained by differences in questionnaires used to assess insomnia.26 Second, differential misclassification may have occurred among participants already using sleep medication, as these individuals may report fewer insomnia symptoms and thus be classified as having no insomnia. However, the sensitivity analysis addressing this issue did not substantially alter the primary estimates when comparing the insomnia groups.

Because the Norwegian Prescription Database does not capture medications dispensed in hospitals or institutional care, prescription rates may be underestimated in the oldest age groups, where a larger share resides in nursing homes. This concern may be partly mitigated by the generally healthier profile of HUNT participants.41 Moreover, age-related differences in recommended Z-hypnotic dosages reduce the comparability of DDD-based duration measures, as older adults are typically advised lower doses due to higher risk of adverse effects. Importantly, this does not affect comparison of the total amount of prescribed medication.

Chronic musculoskeletal pain and pain severity were assessed using two dichotomous questions, which may be considered a crude measure. Furthermore, other health conditions were not accounted for, which may have influenced prescription rates, particularly in older age groups. Lastly, the regression and interaction analyses should be interpreted with caution, as they were exploratory, and no formal adjustment of multiple testing was carried out.

In Norway, the prescription of medications such as Z-hypnotics is subject to strict regulatory controls and is routinely monitored through national health registries. These regulatory conditions may limit the generalizability of our findings to countries with different prescribing systems or monitoring practices.

Implications

Our findings highlight important age- and sex-specific patterns in prolonged Z-hypnotic prescribing that have direct implications for clinical practice and guideline implementation. Although most individuals with insomnia did not receive prescriptions, prolonged pharmacological management remains common among the users. This suggest that current clinical guidelines do not fully account for the complexities of managing insomnia in patients with multimorbidity.42 Treatment decisions are complex and often guided by a holistic assessment influenced by factors such as patient preferences, time constraints, legal requirements, and resource limitations,43 in addition to the guideline-recommended assessment. Nevertheless, the high prevalence of prolonged Z-hypnotic use, especially among older adults, suggests a need for targeted deprescribing initiatives, particularly given their elevated risks of falls,44 cognitive impairment,45 and adverse drug interactions associated with polypharmacy.11 Community- and system-level programs shows that reducing hypnotic use is feasible,46 and can contribute to increased quality of life.47 The age- and sex-related patterns identified in this study, suggest that subgroup-specific approaches to prescribing reviews, deprescribing efforts, and clinical guidance for managing insomnia may be warranted.

Furthermore, CBT-I remains the recommended first-line treatment for insomnia8 and has proven effective in older adults48 and individuals with chronic musculoskeletal pain.49 Expanding access to CBT-I, especially for subgroups at highest risk of prolonged hypnotic use, may help reduce reliance on pharmacological treatment. Digital CBT-I offers a promising avenue50 for overcoming geographic, capacity-related, and informational barriers to care.8 Future efforts should prioritize the development of tailored CBT-I approaches for specific diagnostic groups,9,51,52 including older adults with chronic musculoskeletal pain, who appear to be at a heightened risk of prolonged pharmacological management.

Conclusion

The prevalence of prolonged prescriptions of Z-hypnotics increased markedly with age and was higher among women than men across all age categories. Individuals with both insomnia and chronic musculoskeletal pain showed slightly higher rates of prolonged prescribing than those with insomnia alone, but these differences were not statistically significant within age categories. This indicates that age and sex were stronger drivers of prolonged prescribing than chronic pain. Pain severity appeared to influence prolonged prescription patterns only among participants aged 70 years and older. Among older women, prevalence tended to rise with pain severity, whereas among older men, only high impact pain was associated with increased prescription prevalence. These patterns may reflect the cumulative symptom burden, physicians’ prescribing behaviors, patients’ health-seeking tendencies, and systemic barriers, all interrelated factors shaping clinical practice. The findings suggest a need for subgroup-specific approaches and clinically relevant guidance to reduce reliance on pharmacological treatment for insomnia. Equally important is expanding access to, and further development of, evidence-based non-pharmacological treatments, which offer safer and more sustainable alternatives to prolonged pharmacotherapy.
