Nursing care for adult urostomy patient in China: synthesis of evidence and a modified delphi study
Authors: Longmei Si, Yin Zhang, Dong Pang, Hui Chen, Yajuan Weng, Yujie Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundOstomy care primarily relies on professional nursing and guidance, including basic stoma care skills, prevention and management of peristomal complications, and care of stenting tubes. The specialized skills and expertise of healthcare professionals play a crucial role in enhancing patients’ self-care ability, reducing the incidence of peristomal complications and urinary tract infections, and improving patients’ quality of life. This study aims to develop a set of clinical guidelines applicable to healthcare providers for nursing adult patients with urostomy.MethodsThis study was conducted in two phases. In the first phase, key issues in the nursing care of adult patients with urostomy were identified, and evidence-based questions were formulated. A systematic search, quality appraisal, and evidence synthesis were performed to develop a draft guideline. In the second phase, a Delphi survey among experts was conducted to finalize the strength of recommendations and revise the consensus statements.ResultsThe expert consensus on Nursing Care of Adult Patients with Urostomy comprises a total of 57 recommendations across four domains: basic requirements, psychological support, preoperative care, and postoperative care. All recommendations achieved strong consensus, with over 50% of experts endorsing a strong recommendation (indicating that benefits clearly outweigh risks).ConclusionsThis study developed an expert consensus applicable to healthcare providers in various medical institutions for the care of adult patients with urostomy, with the goal of improving the quality of specialized stoma care, reducing the occurrence of stoma-related and peristomal skin complications, and enhancing patients’ quality of life.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12912-026-04676-y.

Background

Urostomy, primarily including Ileal conduit and Uretero-cutaneostomy, represents a crucial urinary diversion procedure for diseases such as muscle-invasive (pathological stage T2 and above) bladder cancer, bladder absence, refractory bladder dysfunction, and severe neurogenic bladder [1]. Patients undergoing stoma surgery heavily rely on professional care and guidance during the perioperative period [2], shifting to self-care and/or caregiver assistance for ostomy management and related complication control during the recovery phase [3]. However, due to the continuous urine excretion from the urostomy, the skin around the stoma is prone to complications such as moisture-associated skin damage, fungal/candida infections, and hyperplasia. Consequently, the skills and proficiency of healthcare professionals play a pivotal role in enhancing patients’ self-care abilities, reducing stoma and surrounding complications, preventing urinary system infections [4], and ultimately improving patients’ quality of life [5, 6].

Currently, urostomy care, as a critical aspect of medical care, faces numerous challenges that directly impact the quality of care, patient rehabilitation, and quality of life [7]. On one hand, patients with urostomy often require indwelling ureteral stents postoperatively, increasing the risk of urinary tract infections [8]. Especially for patients with cutaneous ureterostomy, long-term indwelling stent drainage of urine is necessary in most medical institutions in China. The presence of the stent as a foreign body in the body can easily lead to the attachment of biological bacterial biofilms [9, 10]. Moreover, during the high incidence of upper respiratory infections in autumn and winter, it can easily cause symptoms such as stent blockage, renal pain, and hydronephrosis [8]. Presently, there is a scarcity of effective clinical evidence for ureteral stent care, and the primary care method relies heavily on observation [11].

On the other hand, the occurrence of skin complications around the urostomy is associated with multiple factors. Long-term leakage, maceration, and damp environments are the main contributing factors [7, 12]. Additionally, patients’ health conditions, such as diabetes, can significantly increase the risk of fungal infections [1]. Therefore, reasonable selection and use of stoma baseplates/stoma skin barriers [13, 14], guiding patients on proper ostomy pouching system changing [3], and controlling primary diseases are crucial for reducing the psychological and economic burden caused by skin complications around the stoma [12].

Furthermore, a scarcity of research on urostomy care and the imbalance between the supply and demand of professional stoma care talents are also urgent issues that need to be addressed [11]. Currently, research in the field of urostomy care is significantly insufficient compared to enterostomy care. Most of the relevant original researches [15], guidelines [16–19], expert consensus [20], and group standards [21] at home and abroad are focused on enterostomy care, and a unified standard, consensus, and practice guideline for urostomy care have not been established. This leads to a lack of scientific guidance for nursing staff in practice, potentially resulting in inappropriate care measures that can affect patient rehabilitation and quality of life. Developing expert consensus can standardize nursing operation procedures and technical requirements, provide a unified reference for nursing staff, reduce nursing variations, improve nursing quality, and effectively prevent stoma and surrounding complications. In terms of talent, the number of domestically certified enterostomal therapists/specialist nurses is far from meeting the growing demand for urostomy patient care. The low popularity of urostomy care makes it difficult for patients to obtain professional nursing services, leading to the aggravation of some preventable and treatable complications, increasing medical burdens and patient suffering.

Given the aforementioned background, this study aims to develop an expert consensus through evidence synthesis and the Delphi expert consultation method. The objective is to provide clear guidance for the training of urostomy professionals, promote skill enhancement, achieve homogenization of care, and cultivate more professionals. Simultaneously, the formulation of such standards or consensus will also facilitate academic exchanges and research collaborations in the field of stoma care, promoting sustained development in this area. In summary, establishing group standards or expert consensus for urostomy care is of utmost importance for enhancing nursing quality, meeting patient needs, and driving professional development.

Methods

This study aims to provide standardized guidance for adult urostomy care among registered nurses in China. The research process consists of two stages: first, summarizing evidence from various research contents to form a preliminary draft, and then validating it using the Delphi consensus method [22].

Stage one: evidence synthesis

Expert consensus development methodology

Formation of an expert panel

The expert panel consists of 10 members, including 5 nursing experts, 4 master’s degree ostomy therapists, and 1 evidence-based nursing expert. Among them, the nursing experts are responsible for drafting the research themes and frameworks, as well as selecting consultation experts. Under the guidance of methodological experts, the 4 ostomy therapists are tasked with reviewing and organizing domestic and international literature related to urostomy care, evaluating the literature, assessing the level of evidence, drafting recommendation tables, preparing expert consultation forms, and performing statistical analysis on the results, as well as item deletion and modification.

Identification of research questions

To standardize nursing procedures and skills for adult urostomy care across different levels of nurses, an expert panel discussion was first convened under the guidance of the Chinese Nursing Association’s Group Standards Committee to define the framework of the standard. five PICOS (Population, Intervention, Comparison, Outcome, Study design) questions were identified, including stoma site marking, preoperative health education, postoperative health education, selection and use of ostomy appliances, care of ureteral stents and management of urostomy and peristomal complications. Following the “6S” evidence hierarchy model (from highest to lowest level of evidence), a systematic search was conducted for clinical decision support tools, evidence-based guidelines, evidence summaries, recommended practices, best practices, and expert consensus related to the five PICOS questions. After rigorous literature quality assessment, evidence-based recommendations were formulated. Subsequently, a draft of the adult urostomy care standard was developed through three rounds of Delphi expert consultation.

Literature search and quality evaluation

Inclusion and exclusion criteria for literature

Inclusion criteria: ① Studies focusing on adult patients with urinary stomas, including ileal conduit and ureterostomy patients; ②Topics covering relevant content related to the above five PICOS elements; ③ Published literature in both Chinese or English; ④ Literature types include recommended practices, clinical practice guidelines, evidence summaries, expert consensus, and systematic reviews. Exclusion criteria: ① Duplicate or directly translated literature; ② Incomplete information; ③ Literature where the full text cannot be obtained; ④ Re-published or updated literature; ⑤ Studies that fail the literature quality evaluation.

Literature search

Following the “6S” evidence pyramid model, a top-down search was conducted on UpToDate, the U.S. Guidelines Network, the British National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) Guidelines, the Registered Nurses’ Association of Ontario (RNAO), the Scottish Intercollegiate Guidelines Network (SIGN), the New Zealand Guidelines Group, Guidelines International Network (GIN), Medline, PubMed, Wanfang Database, CNKI, as well as professional websites including the World Council of Enterostomal Therapists (WCET), Wound, Ostomy and Continence Nurses Society (WOCN), American Society of Colon and Rectal Surgeons (ASCRS), European Hernia Society (EHS), and Association of Stoma Care Nurses UK (ASCN) for content related to urostomy care. For each PICOS question, we developed specific search strategies using combinations of keywords and MeSH terms. A brief summary of the search approach is as follows: taking ureteral stent nursing care as an example, Chinese search terms included “输尿管支架管” (ureteral stent), “护理” (care), “感染” (infection), “移位” (displacement), and “管理” (management), while English search terms were “stent*”, “nursing”, “complications”, “prevention”, and “management”. Similar term combinations were applied for other PICOS questions (e.g., stoma site marking, selection and use of stoma care products, observation and nursing care of urostomy and peristomal skin complications, patient and caregiver education). The detailed search strings for each database (including all the above-mentioned Chinese and English databases) are available in Supplementary File 1. The search period was from the establishment of the database to April 1st, 2024.

Literature quality evaluation

The Appraisal of Guidelines for Research and Evaluation II (AGREE II) was used to evaluate the quality of the guidelines. For evidence summaries and recommended practices, the original literature was traced back, and the corresponding evaluation criteria (2016) from the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) for Evidence-Based Healthcare were selected for quality evaluation based on the literature type. In case of any disagreement, a third party, namely an evidence-based nursing expert, was consulted for assessment. When the conclusions of evidence from different sources are repeated or conflicting, evidence-based evidence should be given priority, high-quality evidence should be given priority, and the latest evidence should be given priority when selecting evidence.

Evidence extraction and formation of the delphi draft

After completing the literature screening, four ostomy therapists who have undergone systematic evidence-based training and possess professional backgrounds extracted data and summarized evidence based on the research content. They also pre-graded the evidence according to the JBI 2014 evidence grading system (Levels 1–5). In cases where evidence from different sources conflicted, selection was based on the principles of prioritizing high-quality evidence and evidence-based findings. Finally, a Delphi draft was formed through deliberation by an expert panel.

Phase two: delphi consensus process

Expert consultation

Establishment of the expert coordination group

An expert coordination group was established in March 2024, comprising eight members. The panel included two Senior Chief Nurses, three Associate Chief Nurses, one Associate Professor, and four Nurse-in-Charge, all of whom held a bachelor’s degree or higher. The responsibilities of the expert coordination group primarily involved drafting the initial item pool for the indicator system, developing and distributing the Delphi expert consultation questionnaires, selecting consultation experts, collating and providing feedback on the suggestions proposed by the experts, and performing statistical analysis of the consultation results.

Development of the delphi questionnaire for expert consultation

Based on evidence summaries, a Delphi consultation questionnaire (Attachment 1) was developed, including a letter to experts, a consultation form, a basic information form for consulted experts, and a survey form for expert judgment and familiarity. Among them, the letter to experts introduces the background and purpose of the group standard development, the method of forming the first draft, and the steps of the consultation process. The consultation form presents the description of recommended opinions for each item, the evaluation of importance, and experts’ suggestions for modifying the standards.

Clarifying the direction and strength of recommendations

Furthermore, to better apply the expert consensus on adult urostomy care in clinical practice, it is necessary to combine evidence with the actual situation of patients, the clinical context, and the judgment of professionals. In this study, based on the FAME structure, the feasibility, appropriateness, clinical significance, and effectiveness of the evidence were evaluated. Strong recommendations were given when the benefits clearly outweighed the risks, weak recommendations when the benefits might outweigh the risks, no significant recommendation when the benefits and risks were comparable or uncertain, weak disapproval when the risks might outweigh the benefits, and strong disapproval when the risks clearly outweighed the benefits. The completed opinion resolution form was distributed to 26 experts, and items suitable for clinical use were selected based on expert opinion. Finally, a total of 45 items were included.

Selection of experts

Using purposive sampling, between April and August 2024, we invited 26 experts from general hospitals, specialized hospitals, and universities across the country for consultation, including experts in stoma care, nursing management, nursing research, and doctors. The inclusion criteria were: ① bachelor’s degree or above; ② intermediate professional title or above; ③ more than 5 years of work experience. The exclusion criteria were: ① currently not working in a nursing position; ② nurses on sick leave, maternity leave, or parental leave.

Data collection and analysis

A modified Delphi method was implemented from July to September 2024 and used to consult experts with a semi-open questionnaire. Likert 5-point scale (1 = strongly disagree, 2 = disagree, 3 = neutral,4 = agree, and 5 = strongly agree) was used to let experts evaluate the importance, familiarity, judgment basis and influence of each index, and feedback relevant opinions and suggestions. Experts were selected who had some experience in urostomy and were interested in research. Prior to the formal Delphi survey, a pre-test was conducted with six experts. Explanatory notes for the recommendations in the questionnaire were added to facilitate better understanding by the experts. Data were collected during three rounds and experts were reminded weekly to complete the e-Delphi, and all questionnaires were feedback within two weeks. Based on the statistical analysis results of the questionnaire, combined with expert opinions and suggestions, each item indicator was optimized to form the next round of the questionnaire. For the statistics of the questionnaire, Microsoft Excel software was used to analyze the expert background information such as the source, professional title, position, education background, working years, professional field and so on. SPSS (5.0)was used to conduct a descriptive analysis of the mean(‾x), standard deviation (SD), coefficient of variation (CV) and other indicators. Results

Literature screening and quality assessment results

A total of 2 clinical decision-making, 6 clinical practice guidelines, and 4 expert consensus documents were ultimately included in this study. The quality appraisal results indicated that among the guideline documents, one clinical practice guideline was rated as grade A [16] and five as grade B [1, 11, 17, 19, 23], all of which were included; all evaluation items for the 4 expert consensus documents [13, 18, 20, 21] were rated as “yes”.

Recommendations and strength of evidence

Evidence related to adult urostomy care was extracted and synthesized, resulting in the development of a preliminary Delphi draft comprising 4 thematic dimensions and a total of 45 recommendations. Evidence synthesis followed the principles of evidence-based evidence priority, high-quality evidence priority, and the latest published authoritative literature priority when evidence sources were different, but complementary or consistent. 28. Hyperplasia

(1) The location, extent, and affected skin manifestations of hyperplasia should be evaluated. Skin biopsy should be performed for pathological examination of uncertain wart-like protrusions.

(1) The location, extent, and severity of fungal/Candida infection should be assessed, including the presence of skin breakdown or bleeding.

(1) Clinical signs and symptoms of UTI should be evaluated, including cloudy urine, foul odor, and flank pain.

Dicussion

This study aimed to clarify the key techniques and issues in adult urostomy nursing care, providing a scientific basis for healthcare professionals across various levels and types of medical institutions. Based on evidence-based medicine, five clinical questions were systematically retrieved, and recommendations were formulated following quality assessment and evidence synthesis. Furthermore, the strength and direction of the recommendations were determined through expert consultation. Ultimately, the expert consensus for adult urostomy care were established, encompassing basic requirements, psychological support, preoperative care, and postoperative care.

The results of this study show Kendall’s W coefficients of 0.092, 0.144, and 0.122, indicating that the level of consistency among the expert panel was relatively low, with considerable divergence in the ranking of items across different experts. This suggests, to some extent, that there may be substantial heterogeneity in the backgrounds or professional perspectives of the experts. Although the W coefficients were small, the corresponding significance tests all yielded p-values less than 0.05, reaching statistical significance. On the one hand, this indicates that the experts’ ratings were not entirely random and that a weak but statistically meaningful common trend existed. This may be attributable to the fact that the consensus was divided into six sections, with relatively large differences in ratings across dimensions. On the other hand, it also suggests that due to the influence of the number of items being evaluated or the degrees of freedom, even when the strength of consensus is very low (i.e., a small effect size), it may still be identified as statistically significant. Future studies could further focus on the practical significance of the effect size by optimizing rating criteria or enhancing expert training, thereby improving the consistency and reliability of evaluation results.

This study employed a three-round Delphi process and achieved the following results: All included recommendations were classified as “strong recommendations.” However, for certain items under specific dimensions, the supporting evidence was primarily derived from levels 3 ~ 5, indicating that further research is needed to provide a stronger scientific basis. During the first round of consultation regarding recommendation strength, it was found that the proportion of items with clearly “benefits greater than harms” in the Joanna Briggs Institute Levels of Evidence and Grades of Recommendation Working Party ranged from 57.69% to 100% [24]. Although a few items received relatively low scores (< 70%) in the “benefits greater than harms” category, the experts generally agreed that the implementation of all recommended items would be beneficial for patients with urostomy.

For Item 6, the relatively low percentage (57.69%) for a strong recommendation is attributed to the recommended distance of 5–7 cm from the incision. In clinical practice, due to the relatively small abdominal circumference of many patients, the stoma site for ileal conduit patients must be positioned on the rectus abdominis muscle to prevent parastomal hernia, making this recommendation difficult to implement. This item primarily aims to facilitate stoma appliance wear and prevent leakage from contaminating the wound. In patients with cutaneous ureterostomy, stoma site marking can be performed in accordance with this recommendation. For Item 9, which emphasizes preoperative trial wear of a stoma appliance system to monitor for allergic reactions, the percentage for a strong recommendation is 61.54%. The relatively lower percentage is because in some regions of China, stoma appliance systems are not yet covered by medical insurance. A single set of stoma appliance costs approximately 80–170 RMB, resulting in low cost-effectiveness for preoperative trial wear, as well as poor appropriateness and feasibility. For Item 12, which indicates that patients and their partners should be educated about the impact of a stoma on intimate relationships, bathing, and other activities of daily living, the lower percentage is due to cultural factors. Specifically, intimate relationships are influenced by traditional Chinese cultural norms, and healthcare professionals tend to remain relatively silent regarding the side effects of surgery. Consequently, the majority of healthcare providers only address these topics when patients or their partners initiate inquiries.

The Urostomy Site Selection for Patients is very Important [1, 16, 17]. The positioning of a urostomy is crucial in the postoperative recovery and daily life of patients, primarily for three reasons: it facilitates self-care and observation, reduces skin problems, prevents stomal and peristomal complications [25, 26]. Firstly, appropriate stoma positioning eases self-care for patients. A stoma site that is easily accessible and visible allows patients to perform daily cleaning and change pouching system with ease, enhancing their self-care ability and confidence while minimizing care errors. Secondly, reasonable stoma positioning helps reduce skin issues. A well-positioned stoma on flat skin ensures a tight fit of the ostomy bag, preventing leakage of excreta [27]. Such leakage not only increases economic burden but can also irritate the skin, causing redness, itching, pain, eczema, and even ulcers, which affect the adhesion of the stoma baseplate and prolong the patient’s recovery time. Finally, improper stoma positioning or the absence of preoperative positioning can lead to stoma-related complications, with parastomal hernia being the most common [25, 28]. Parastomal hernias can cause abdominal discomfort and pain, and in severe cases, they can even be life-threatening. Rational stoma positioning can avoid defects and weaknesses in abdominal wall tissue, thereby reducing the risk of parastomal hernias. Therefore, before surgery, healthcare professionals should comprehensively consider the patient’s physical condition, lifestyle habits, and surgical needs to select the optimal stoma location, ensuring surgical success and patient recovery.

In China, cutaneous ureterostomy is one of the primary methods for total cystectomy and urinary diversion; however, the management of ureteral stents remains frequently overlooked. Patients require lifelong indwelling stents with regular replacements, which presents the following challenges: First, as a foreign body in the urinary tract, the stent is prone to cause urinary tract infections and carries a risk of blockage, further increasing the likelihood of infection. To address this issue, this study integrated evidence related to urostomy care and expert opinions from clinical nursing specialists to formulate observational recommendations [8–11]. Accordingly, both nurses and patients/caregivers should regularly check for stent blockage or dislodgement. Any abnormality should be reported immediately to the physician to prevent mucus plugs could lead to undue tension on a surgical anastomosis.

Furthermore, “If stents are producing no urine, they should be flushed with 5–7 ml NaCl 0.9% using a sterile technique” in the European Association of Urology Nurses guidelines [11] should be performed only under physician authorization and on the condition that stent displacement has been ruled out. Stent dislodgement or migration may occur, resulting in inadequate drainage and potential impairment of renal function. Therefore, close monitoring of changes in the external length of the stent is essential. Any abnormality should prompt immediate medical attention. Finally, long-term indwelling stents can lead to inflammatory reactions, recurrent infections, patient discomfort, decline in renal function, and increased financial burden. Thus, strengthening the prevention and treatment of urinary tract infections, along with timely stent replacement, is crucial for preserving renal function. In summary, postoperative care for patients with cutaneous ureterostomy requires meticulous and comprehensive attention. Healthcare providers should emphasize the management of ureteral stents and provide detailed guidance and support to patients. Meanwhile, patients should actively engage in self-care and monitoring to enhance their quality of life and reduce the risk of complications.

This study focuses on the management of peristomal skin complications in patients with urostomies, particularly three common issues: uric acid crystal deposition, hyperplasia, and fungal/candidal infections. For these complications, the following assessment and management measures are proposed: ① Uric Acid Crystal Deposition: This complication primarily results from poor hygiene and prolonged wear of the ostomy pouch without timely cleaning. The recommended treatment involves the application of diluted white vinegar soaks to dissolve and remove the crystals. ② Hyperplasia: Effective management of hyperplasia depends on ensuring an adequate seal of the ostomy pouch barrier. This includes frequent barrier changes, adjustment of the barrier aperture size, and the use of accessory products to minimize leakage.

If hyperplasia is suspected to be malignant or does not respond to management, prompt referral to a physician is indicated. ③ Fungal/Candidal Infection: Hyperglycemia and a moist peristomal environment predispose patients to such infections. Topical antifungal agents should be applied locally. In severe cases, oral or intravenous antifungal therapy should be administered under medical supervision. These recommendations are derived from expert consensus and clinical guidelines [1, 27], and aim to provide practical guidance for nurses in managing peristomal skin complications in urostomy patients.

Another noteworthy dimension is the issue of urinary tract infections (UTIs) in patients with urostomy [8]. Patients with urinary stomas frequently face the problem of UTIs. Specifically, those with ileal conduit diversion are prone to colonization by *Enterococcus faecalis*, *Escherichia coli*, and *Staphylococcus aureus* due to the use of ileal segments for diversion. This often presents as asymptomatic bacteriuria, which generally does not require specific treatment, and routine urinalysis is not performed postoperatively. In contrast, patients with cutaneous ureterostomies require long-term indwelling and regular replacement of stents. These stents, as foreign bodies, can easily trigger immune responses. Long-term indwelling increases the risk of bacterial biofilm formation, and obstruction may lead to symptoms such as high fever, lumbar pain, and pyuria. Therefore, patients should be advised to maintain adequate fluid intake and ensure unobstructed drainage. Persistent infections should be promptly addressed.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, there is a scarcity of research specifically addressing urostomy, with only two clinical practice guidelines identified. Furthermore, current stoma care practices remain largely experience-based. Although evidence retrieval and integration were conducted, this still represents a shortcoming of the present study. Additionally, the data collection and analysis involved in the Delphi method make it susceptible to various biases. For instance, the inability to confirm conflicting evidence or opinions in person may inevitably introduce subjectivity from the researchers [29]. Moreover, although we endeavored to include experts who as experts with knowledge and experience of a specific subject from diverse fields such as education, clinical stoma nursing, and healthcare to enhance representativeness, they may still have insufficient understanding of urostomy care, which could compromise the scientific rigor and operational applicability of the consensus [22, 30].

Conclusions

Based on evidence-based research, the developed indicator system—the expert consensus *Nursing Care for Adult Urostomy*—was officially issued by the Chinese Nursing Association on October 14, 2025, for future promotion and application. This expert consensus comprises 45 items across four domains. Its clinical application can significantly enhance the quality of specialized urostomy care, reduce the incidence of postoperative complications and stoma- or peristomal skin-related complications, and improve patients’ quality of life.
