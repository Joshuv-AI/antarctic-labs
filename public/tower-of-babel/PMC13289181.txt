Association of nurse staffing ratios with patient mortality and length of stay in Chilean public hospitals: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Marta Simonetti, Alejandra Galiano, Amaru Agüero, Karen B Lasater, Eileen T Lake, Linda H Aiken
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesTo evaluate variation in nurse staffing ratios in intensive care unit (ICU) and medical-surgical units in Chilean public hospitals and the extent to which nurse staffing is associated with patient mortality and length of stay.DesignCross-sectional.SettingOut of a population of 45 general high-complexity Chilean public hospitals, 43 participated in the study.Participants514 ICU and 1850 medical-surgical bedside nurses answered an online survey to provide data on their working conditions, including staffing. Patient outcomes were measured using the 2023 national discharge database from the Ministry of Health containing data from 344 567 adult patients admitted to an ICU or a medical-surgical unit.Primary outcome measuresPrimary patient outcomes were mortality within 30 days after admission and length of stay. The association between nurse staffing and the primary outcomes was tested using mixed logistic regression models for mortality and mixed zero-truncated negative binomial regression for length of stay.ResultsThe analytical sample represented 95.6% of public Chilean hospitals. In the average hospital, ICU nurses cared for 3.0 patients at a time (range: 1.1–4.5); nurses on medical-surgical units cared for 10.1 patients (range: 6.9–13.6). Each additional patient in a nurse’s workload was associated with a statistically significant higher odds of death and longer length of stay in medical-surgical units (OR 1.043, 95% CI 1.042 to 1.043; incidence rate ratio 1.032, 95% CI 1.007 to 1.058, respectively) and higher odds of death in ICU (OR 1.12, 95% CI 1.112 to 1.128).ConclusionsThere is considerable variation in nurse staffing among Chilean public hospitals with negative consequences for patient outcomes. Regardless of the unit type, patients in hospitals where nurses care for more patients at a time are more likely to die; in medical or surgical units, they are more likely to have longer lengths of stay. Systematically reducing the numbers of patients in a nurses’ workload has the potential to decrease inequalities across the public healthcare system by decreasing preventable deaths and decreasing the length of patient stays to thereby improve access for patients on waitlists.

Introduction

There is a large international evidence base established over the past several decades that hospital nurse staffing levels are associated with patient outcomes but little of this evidence comes from Latin America1 and none since the COVID-19 pandemic. A landmark study of the outcomes of hospital nurse staffing in Chile before the pandemic documented that nurse staffing levels failed to meet international standards especially in public hospitals.2 Perhaps because of that influential study, the Chilean government acted promptly when the COVID-19 pandemic came to Latin America to increase nursing resources in public hospitals. However, as the pandemic recedes, so too does the funding to maintain sufficient levels of nursing staff at the bedside.

This is the first comprehensive study on hospital nurse staffing and patient outcomes following the pandemic in Chile. The pre-pandemic study showed not only poor hospital nurse staffing ratios, but significant variation in staffing across public hospitals raising concerns about equity of access to effective and safe care in some public hospitals.2 Nurses in medical-surgical units were caring for 14 patients, on average, and in some hospitals, nurses were caring for more than 20 patients.2 3 Nurse staffing ratios were significantly associated with patient mortality, length of stay (LOS) and readmissions.2 Aside from higher mortality, longer LOS associated with less favourable nurse staffing is concerning in Chile as there are thousands of patients on waitlists for elective surgeries and procedures.46 Additionally, a recent study reported that a significant proportion of patients being cared for on medical-surgical units in Chile should have been cared for in an intensive care unit (ICU).7 Shorter hospital stays could be an effective strategy to reduce waitlists and provide more ICU care without the high costs of building additional inpatient beds.

The purpose of this study was to evaluate nurse staffing ratios in intensive care and medical-surgical units in Chilean public hospitals post-pandemic and to determine the extent to which variation in nurse staffing across hospitals is associated with increased patient mortality and longer LOS.

Methods

This is a cross-sectional study of hospital nurse staffing and patient outcomes in adult intensive care and medical-surgical units in general high-complexity public hospitals in Chile. The study used two sources of data. First, nurse survey data collected from August 2022 to November 2023. And second, patient data from December 2022 to December 2023 recorded by hospitals at discharge and reported to the Ministry of Health.

Participants selection

The study population comprised all adult intensive care and medical-surgical units in general high-complexity public hospitals in Chile with more than 150 beds, excluding specialty hospitals (n=45). All bedside registered nurses (RNs) in the targeted units were invited to participate.

All adult patients in the 2023 Chile National Discharge Database admitted to intensive care or medical and surgical units in the participating hospitals were included in the outcomes analysis. Patients with obstetric or psychiatric main diagnoses, patients with hospital stays shorter than 24 hours and patients who experienced a hospital transfer were excluded.

Data collection

Through the chief nurse officers and nurse managers of the targeted units, emails and WhatsApp messages were sent to all ICU and medical and surgical nurses inviting them to participate in the study. Participation entailed answering a self-administered online survey (Research Electronic Data Capture). The survey asked demographic, education and job-related questions, including questions about staffing. Patient data were obtained from the 2023 Chilean Hospital Discharge Database, which is publicly available. The database has detailed patient-level data for each hospital, including patient demographics, admission diagnoses, comorbidities, in-hospital procedures, international refined diagnosis-related group (IR-GRD) codes, LOS and in-hospital mortality.

Measures

Nurse staffing

Staffing was the main predictor. It was measured at the hospital-level (separately among ICU and medical-surgical units) as a continuous variable expressing the mean patient-to-nurse ratio. To compute the hospital-level nurse staffing measure, we used a validated method.2 First, we asked nurses about the numbers of patients and registered nurses on their unit during their last shift. The patient-to-nurse ratio was calculated by dividing these two numbers. From all nurses working in the same hospital and in the same type of unit (either ICU or medical-surgical), a mean patient-to-nurse ratio for the unit type was derived.

A self-reported measure of staffing, rather than staffing measures from administrative sources was preferred, since administrative data tend to overstate clinical staffing because they include nurses in roles other than inpatient direct care. In our study, we only included bedside nurses to ensure our staffing measure will represent nursing resources at the bedside. Since the survey asked nurses to report on their last shift, the problem of recall bias was unlikely.

Patientmortality

The national discharge database reports on patients that died during their hospital stay. Mortality, within 30 days after admission, was measured as a dichotomous outcome.

Length of stay

Patients’ LOS was calculated using admission and discharge dates, recorded in the national discharge database. LOS was measured as a discrete variable expressing number of days. We excluded patients that were hospitalised for less than 24 hours or more than 30 days.

Control variables

Multiple variables were measured to account for confounders, especially patient case-mix, including patients’ demographic characteristics (age and sex) and measures of patient complexity derived from the IR-GRD coding system. Among these patient complexity measures were mortality risk (low, medium, high), illness severity (low, medium, high) and main admission diagnosis. Admission diagnoses included a list of more than 200 conditions, identified by IR-GRD code. We created dummy variables for each admission diagnosis presented in the dataset. Following Elixhauser and colleagues’ procedure8 we selected a set of comorbid conditions, identified by International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision codes and created dummy variables to record the presence or absence of each specific comorbid condition (ie, congestive heart failure, cardiac arrhythmias, valvular disease, pulmonary circulation, diabetes and so on). For each patient, those comorbid conditions that could have resulted from the admission diagnosis, instead of being preexisting conditions, were not counted as a comorbidity. Additionally, those conditions that may have occurred in the hospital and that by their nature are acute (ie, pneumonia, urinary tract infection, cardiogenic shock, respiratory failure, among others), were also excluded from the list of comorbidities.

Data management and statistical analysis

Raw nurse survey data and hospital outcomes data were merged. Data cleaning procedures involved the standardisation and recategorisation of some variables. For the main predictor (ie, nurse staffing), completeness of data was of most importance. Staffing values for all medical-surgical units were available. As for ICUs, we had missing data for two units (4.6%); missing data were imputed using the median staffing value of all other ICUs. The decision about using the median staffing value was supported by a formal sensitivity analysis, comparing four imputation approaches (online supplemental material, table S1, panel A). First, a complete case analysis, excluding the two hospitals with missing values. Second and third, median and mean imputation, replacing missing ICU staffing values with the median or the mean of all available ICU staffing ratios (values 2.88 and 3.09, respectively). And fourth, multivariate imputation by chained equations.

Numerical variables were described using either means and SDs or median and IQR. Minimum and maximum values were also analysed. Qualitative variables were described using frequencies and percentages.

We used different procedures for inferential analyses related to the two outcomes of interest, mortality and LOS using regression models that reflect the hierarchical structure of our data. The models use random intercepts to handle unobserved hospital characteristics.

Mortality

The association between nurse staffing and mortality was assessed using multivariable mixed logistic regression models. The models were fitted to estimate the probability of death during the hospital stay. Covariates included standardised patient age, sex, diagnosis-related group severity, mortality risk index and comorbidity indicators. Model coefficients were exponentiated to obtain ORs with 95% CIs, summarising the association between each predictor and the odds of death. Model performance was evaluated using the area under the receiver-operating characteristic (ROC) curve (AUC) and related classification metrics such as sensitivity, specificity, positive and negative predictive values, accuracy, balanced accuracy, F1 score and the Matthews correlation coefficient. Pseudo-R² statistics provided a measure of explanatory power. To account for differences in case-mix across care settings, separate mixed logistic regression models were estimated for the intensive care units and medical-surgical units.

Length of stay

LOS was modelled as count data using mixed zero-truncated negative binomial regression, an approach that accounts for the absence of zero values in LOS data. To capture differences across care settings and outcomes, separate models were estimated for the ICUs and for the medical-surgical wards and within each unit type a mixed zero-truncated negative binomial model was fitted modelling the duration until discharge among survivors. Parameter estimates from this model were exponentiated to obtain incidence rate ratios with 95% CIs. Covariates included patient demographics (age and sex), measures of clinical severity (diagnosis-related group severity index and mortality risk index) and a comprehensive set of comorbid conditions. Model adequacy was evaluated using pseudo-R² measures such as McFadden and Nagelkerke coefficients, together with information criteria (eg, the Akaike information criterion) to compare alternative specifications. Residual analyses and multicollinearity diagnostics were examined to assess the assumptions and fit of the models.

Patient and public involvement in the study

Patients and the public were not involved either in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination plans of our research.

Results

Out of a population of 45 high-complexity public Chilean hospitals, 43 were included in this study, yielding a hospital-level response rate of 95.6%. One hospital was excluded from this analysis due to a lack of patient data in the national discharge database. Another hospital was excluded due to a delay in the authorisation by the hospital directive board. One-third of participating study hospitals were in Santiago which is aligned with the population distribution of the country.

The entire population of ICU nurses totalled 1346 RNs. Out of them, 514 participated in the study (ICU nurse-level response rate: 38.2%). Of a population of 3169 medical-surgical nurses, 1850 participated (medical-surgical nurse-level response rate: 58.4%). These response rates are consistent with those found in a recent large systematic review of surveys of health professionals.9 Prior research using a similar questionnaire, staffing measures and research protocol found no differences in responses on the variables in this study between nurses who responded initially and those that required substantial effort to motivate a response representing non-respondents.10

Nurses in medical and surgical units were close to 33 years of age and had an average of 6.4 years of experience. Nurses in ICUs tended to be slightly older, have more years of experience, and were more likely to be male as compared with nurses working on medical-surgical units (table 1). Patients averaged close to 58 years of age and were slightly younger in ICUs. ICU patients were less likely to be women. Slightly over 26% of ICU patients died within 30 days of hospital admission compared with 3.4% of patients in medical-surgical units. The average LOS in intensive care was 17.6 days compared with 7.0 days in medical-surgical units.

Discussion

This is the most current and comprehensive study conducted in Chile to document significant variation in hospital nurse staffing ratios across public hospitals and the association of that variation to preventable deaths in both intensive care and medical and surgical units and longer than necessary length of hospital stays in medical and surgical units. In the ICU, each additional patient per nurse was associated with a 12% increase in the odds of death. On medical-surgical units, each additional patient was associated with a 4% increase in the odds of death. In ICU, the maximum average patient-to-nurse ratio was 4.5 patients, more than double the number of patients recommended by international standards.11

This study provides a Latin American perspective to the problem of nurse understaffing and quality of care. Our results are consistent with those reported in international studies.1215 In ICUs, other studies have shown increased mortality16 17 and LOS18 19 associated with increased nurses’ workloads; however our study did not find a significant result for the association between ICU staffing and LOS. In hospital general wards the same associations have been reported internationally.17 20 When nurses take care of too many patients at a time, they cannot maintain appropriate patient surveillance, complications are not detected in a timely manner and missed care increases.21 22

Preventable in-hospital mortality and avoidable long hospital stays have the potential to be improved by reducing the variation across hospitals in the number of patients per nurse. Reduction in LOS is particularly salient in the Chilean context, where patients wait long periods of time on waiting lists for elective procedures.23 Recent data report that patients are waiting an average of 422 days for elective surgery.24 Improving nurse staffing may be an effective strategy to systematically shorten the LOS for patients in medical or surgical units, thus increasing patient throughput in the existing beds. Investments in nursing via staffing enhancements in already existing hospitals is likely a more cost-efficient way to increase access to healthcare than the alternative of building more hospital beds.

Relative to hospital nurse staffing ratios in Chile prior to the pandemic, we found that the mean patient-to-nurse ratio on medical-surgical units decreased from 14 to 10 patients per nurse.2 3 7 Despite improvements over time in staffing, likely as a function of intentional investments by the Ministry of Health to create more positions for RNs in patient care, variations across hospitals have persisted. In Chile, the Ministry of Health investments to fund more nursing positions due to the COVID-19 crisis resulted in better staffing, showing that there were RNs available in the country to fill those positions suggesting that the solution for nurse understaffing in Chile is not primarily to ‘produce’ or educate more nurses, but rather to create more paid nurse positions in public hospitals.

Since the pandemic showed that Chile has nurses available to better staff hospitals, the Ministry of Health should consider a revision of the hospital staffing recommendations that were issued in Chile more than a decade ago.25 These recommendations are not aligned with international standards. Around the world, places with established staffing mandates, like California in the USA, Queensland in Australia and British Columbia in Canada, have shown improved patient and nurse outcomes associated with establishing minimum safe nurse staffing mandates.11 26 27

The study has some limitations. The cross-sectional design does not permit causal inferences. We could not match individual nurses to individual patients. However, patient outcomes were assessed during the same period in which we collected data among nurses. It should be noted that Chile has successfully implemented one of the best hospital patient outcomes data systems in South America which enabled our rigorous evaluation of the association of nurse staffing with risk-adjusted mortality and LOS. Because almost all target hospitals participated in the study, there was no response bias at the organisation level. While response rates for nurse informants were comparable to those of other studies of healthcare professionals, we cannot rule out the possibility of non-response bias in estimated nurse staffing levels. Additionally, we had a small percentage of missing ICU staffing data; however, sensitivity analyses demonstrated the robustness of the study outcomes to various imputation approaches. Finally, these results represent only high-complexity public hospitals in Chile. There are many smaller low-complexity hospitals in Chile for which further research is needed.

In conclusion, there is considerable variation in nurse staffing among Chilean public hospitals with negative consequences for patient outcomes. Patients in hospitals where nurses care for more patients at a time are more likely to die and, in medical or surgical units, they are more likely to have longer LOS. Public policies to reduce variation in nurse staffing across public hospitals and improving the overall level of nurse staffing to international standards have the potential to reduce preventable hospital deaths and use the existing hospital bed supply more efficiently by reducing average LOS thus potentially reducing the waiting time for hospital care.
