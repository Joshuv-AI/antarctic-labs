The relationship between the transition functions of the labeled and unlabeled versions of the infinitely-many-neutral-alleles diffusion model
S. N. Ethier

Abstract
The transition function of the unlabeled infinitely-many-neutral-alleles diffusion model, as expressed by Zhou 2015 , is derived from the transition function of the labeled infinitely-many-neutral-alleles diffusion model, slightly simplifying the derivation by Feng 2010 .



1
Introduction

Given
\theta>0
, let
\{D_{t},\,t\geq 0\}
be the pure death process in
\textbf{Z}_{+}\cup\{\infty\}
starting at
\infty
with death rates

\lambda_{n}:=\textstyle{\frac{1}{2}}n(n-1+\theta),\quad n\geq 0,

(
\infty
is an entrance boundary) and define

d_{n}^{\theta}(t):=\textbf{P}(D_{t}=n),\quad n\geq 0,\,t>0.

Explicit but complicated formulas are available for these functions (
Tavaré 1984
,
Tavaré 1984
); they will not be needed here.

The
unlabeled
infinitely-many-neutral-alleles diffusion model was characterized by
Ethier and Kurtz 1981
as a diffusion process in the Kingman simplex

\overline{\nabla}_{\infty}:=\bigg\{\bm{p}=(p_{1},p_{2},\ldots)\in[0,1]^{\infty}:p_{1}\geq p_{2}\geq\cdots\geq 0,\,\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}p_{i}\leq 1\bigg\}.

with a specified generator depending on
\theta
. Its transition function was shown by
Zhou 2015
to have the form

Q(t,\bm{p},\cdot)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}d_{n}^{\theta}(t)\,\Lambda_{n}^{\theta}(\bm{p},\cdot),

(1)

where
\Lambda_{n}^{\theta}(\bm{p},\cdot)
is a certain one-step transition function on
\overline{\nabla}_{\infty}
for each
n\geq 0
.

The
labeled
infinitely-many-neutral-alleles diffusion model is a Fleming–Viot process in
\mathscr{P}(S)
, the space of Borel probability measures on a compact metric space
S
, with mutation operator
A
on
C(S)
given by

(Af)(x):={\textstyle{\frac{1}{2}}}\theta\int_{S}(f(\xi)-f(x))\,\nu_{0}(d\xi),

where
\nu_{0}\in\mathscr{P}(S)
is assumed to be nonatomic. Its transition function was shown by
Ethier and Griffiths 1993
to have the form

P(t,\mu,\cdot)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}d_{n}^{\theta}(t)\,\Gamma_{n}^{\theta,\nu_{0}}(\mu,\cdot),

(2)

where
\Gamma_{n}^{\theta,\nu_{0}}(\mu,\cdot)
is a certain one-step transition function on
\mathscr{P}(S)
for each
n\geq 0
.

Let the mapping
\Phi:\mathscr{P}(S)\mapsto\overline{\nabla}_{\infty}
be given by

\Phi(\mu):=(p_{(1)},p_{(2)},\ldots),

where
p_{(1)},p_{(2)},\ldots
are the descending order statistics of the sizes (or masses) of the atoms of
\mu
. We would like to apply the Markov mapping theorem of
Dynkin 1965
(see the next section) to conclude that, for each
t>0
and
\bm{p}\in\overline{\nabla}_{\infty}
,

Q(t,\bm{p},\cdot)=P(t,\mu,\Phi^{-1}(\cdot))\text{ whenever }\mu\in\Phi^{-1}(\bm{p}),

(3)

thereby deriving
Q(t,\bm{p},\cdot)
from
P(t,\mu,\cdot)
. In fact, this has already been done by
Feng 2010
, but we want to give a more direct derivation by showing that, for each
n\geq 0
and
\bm{p}\in\overline{\nabla}_{\infty}
,

\Lambda_{n}^{\theta}(\bm{p},\cdot)=\Gamma_{n}^{\theta,\nu_{0}}(\mu,\Phi^{-1}(\cdot))\text{ whenever }\mu\in\Phi^{-1}(\bm{p}).

Feng 2010
’s (
Feng 2010
) derivation was based on
Ethier 1992
’s (
Ethier 1992
) eigenfunction expansion for
Q(t,\bm{p},\cdot)
instead of
Zhou 2015
’s (
Zhou 2015
) more elegant formula (
1
) (and (
5
) below). In particular, Feng’s derivation relied on the explicit formulas for the functions
d_{n}^{\theta}(t)
and ours does not.
Zhou 2015
’s (
Zhou 2015
) proof of (
1
) also relied on these formulas, but
Ethier and Griffiths 1993
’ (
Ethier and Griffiths 1993
) proof of (
2
) (and (
7
) below) used instead the fact that the functions
d_{n}^{\theta}(t)
satisfy the Kolmogorov forward equations.

There is an alternative version of the Markov mapping theorem due to
Rogers and Pitman 1981
(see the next section), which can also be used to prove (
3
).

Let us specify
\Lambda_{n}^{\theta}(\bm{p},\cdot)
and
\Gamma_{n}^{\theta,\nu_{0}}(\mu,\cdot)
.

First, Kingman’s Poisson–Dirichlet distribution with parameter
\theta>0
, denoted by
\text{PD}_{\theta}
, is the unique stationary distribution for the transition function
Q(t,\bm{p},\cdot)
, and it is concentrated on

\nabla_{\infty}:=\bigg\{\bm{p}=(p_{1},p_{2},\ldots)\in[0,1]^{\infty}:p_{1}\geq p_{2}\geq\cdots\geq 0,\,\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}p_{i}=1\bigg\},

a dense subset of
\overline{\nabla}_{\infty}
as the notation suggests.

Define the functions
P_{\bm{n}}\in C(\overline{\nabla}_{\infty})
for
\bm{n}=(n_{1},\ldots,n_{l})
with
n_{1}\geq\cdots\geq n_{l}\geq 1
and
l\in\textbf{N}
by

P_{\bm{n}}(\bm{p}):=\binom{n}{n_{1},\ldots,n_{l}}\frac{1}{\alpha_{1}!\cdots\alpha_{n}!}\sum_{i_{1},\ldots,i_{l}\in\textbf{N}\text{ distinct}}p_{i_{1}}^{n_{1}}\cdots p_{i_{l}}^{n_{l}},\quad\bm{p}\in\nabla_{\infty},

(4)

where
n=|\bm{n}|:=n_{1}+\cdots+n_{l}
and
\alpha_{j}=|\{i:n_{i}=j\}|
for
j=1,\ldots,n
. These functions, defined on
\nabla_{\infty}
, extend uniquely to
C(\overline{\nabla}_{\infty})
. Observe that (
4
) is the probability that a random sample of size
n
from a population with allele frequencies
p_{1},p_{2},\ldots
contains
l
alleles, one with
n_{1}
representatives, another with
n_{2}
representatives, and so on.

We can now define

\Lambda_{n}^{\theta}(\bm{p},d\bm{q}):=\begin{cases}{\rm PD}_{\theta}(d\bm{q})&\text{if $n=0$ or 1}\\
\vskip 6.0pt plus 2.0pt minus 2.0pt\cr\displaystyle{\sum_{\bm{n}:|\bm{n}|=n}\frac{P_{\bm{n}}(\bm{p})P_{\bm{n}}(\bm{q})}{\int_{\overline{\nabla}_{\infty}}P_{\bm{n}}\,d{\rm PD}_{\theta}}\,{\rm PD}_{\theta}(d\bm{q})}&\text{if $n\geq 2$}\end{cases}

(5)

for
\bm{p}\in\overline{\nabla}_{\infty}
. The integral in the denominator is given by the Ewens sampling formula, namely

\int_{\overline{\nabla}_{\infty}}P_{\bm{n}}\,d\text{PD}_{\theta}=\frac{n!}{n_{1}\cdots n_{l}}\,\frac{1}{\alpha_{1}!\cdots\alpha_{n}!}\,\frac{\theta^{l}}{\theta_{(n)}},

(6)

where
a_{(n)}:=a(a+1)\cdots(a+n-1)
.

For
\beta>0
and
\nu\in\mathscr{P}(S)
, define
\Pi_{\beta,\nu}\in\mathscr{P}(\mathscr{P}(S))
by

\Pi_{\beta,\nu}(\cdot):=\textbf{P}\bigg(\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\rho_{i}\,\delta_{\xi_{i}}\in\cdot\bigg),

where
(\rho_{1},\rho_{2},\ldots)
is
\text{PD}_{\beta}
and independent of
\xi_{1},\xi_{2},\ldots
, which are i.i.d.
\nu
. Here
\delta_{x}\in\mathscr{P}(S)
is the unit mass at
x\in S
. The unique stationary distribution of
P(t,\mu,\cdot)
is
\Pi_{\theta,\nu_{0}}
, and we can now define

\Gamma_{n}^{\theta,\nu_{0}}(\mu,\cdot):=\begin{cases}\Pi_{\theta,\nu_{0}}(\cdot)&\text{if $n=0$}\\
\vskip 6.0pt plus 2.0pt minus 2.0pt\cr\displaystyle{\int_{S^{n}}\mu^{n}(dx_{1}\times\cdots\times dx_{n})}&\\
\par\hrule height=0.0pt\penalty\vskip-2.84526pt\cr\qquad\quad{}\cdot\Pi_{n+\theta,(n+\theta)^{-1}(\delta_{x_{1}}+\cdots+\delta_{x_{n}}+\theta\nu_{0})}(\cdot)&\text{if $n\geq 1$}\end{cases}

(7)

for
\mu\in\mathscr{P}(S)
, where
\mu^{n}\in\mathscr{P}(S^{n})
is the
n
-fold product measure
\mu\times\cdots\times\mu
.

Let us state formally the result we aim to establish.

Theorem 1
.

For each
n\geq 0
and
\bm{p}\in\overline{\nabla}_{\infty}
,

\Lambda_{n}^{\theta}(\bm{p},\cdot)=\Gamma_{n}^{\theta,\nu_{0}}(\mu,\Phi^{-1}(\cdot))\text{ whenever }\mu\in\Phi^{-1}(\bm{p}).

(8)

This immediately implies that, for each
t>0
and
\bm{p}\in\overline{\nabla}_{\infty}
, (
3
) holds.

2
The Markov mapping theorem

There are two versions of the Markov mapping theorem that can be expressed in terms of transition functions, one due to
Dynkin 1965
and another due to
Rogers and Pitman 1981
.

Theorem 2

(
Dynkin 1965
,
Dynkin 1965
, §6.10)
.

Let
E
and
E_{0}
be complete separable metric spaces, and let
\Phi:E\mapsto E_{0}
be Borel measurable and surjective. Let
P(t,x,\cdot)
be the transition function for a Markov process
X
in
E
. If for each
t>0
the condition

P(t,x,\Phi^{-1}(\cdot))\text{ depends on }x\text{ only through }\Phi(x)

(9)

holds, then
Y:=\Phi\circ X
is a Markov process in
E_{0}
with transition function

Q(t,y,\cdot):=P(t,x,\Phi^{-1}(\cdot)),\text{ where }x\in\Phi^{-1}(y).

(10)

Theorem 3

(
Rogers and Pitman 1981
,
Rogers and Pitman 1981
)
.

Let
E
and
E_{0}
be complete separable metric spaces, and let
\Phi:E\mapsto E_{0}
be Borel measurable and surjective. Let
\lambda
be a one-step transition function from
E_{0}
to
E

(
i.e.,
y\in E_{0}\mapsto\lambda(y,\cdot)\in\mathscr{P}(E)
is Borel measurable
)
satisfying
\lambda(y,\Phi^{-1}(y))=1
for all
y\in E_{0}
. Let
P(t,x,\cdot)
be the transition function for a Markov process
X
in
E
with initial distribution
\mu:=\int_{E_{0}}\lambda(y,\cdot)\,\mu_{0}(dy)
for some
\mu_{0}\in\mathscr{P}(E_{0})
. If the condition

\int_{E}P(t,x,\cdot)\,\lambda(y,dx)=\int_{E}\bigg[\int_{E_{0}}\lambda(z,\cdot)\,P(t,x,\Phi^{-1}(dz))\bigg]\lambda(y,dx),

(11)

holds for every
t>0
and
y\in E_{0}
, then
Y:=\Phi\circ X
is a Markov process in
E_{0}
with initial distribution
\mu_{0}
and transition function

Q(t,y,\cdot):=\int_{E}P(t,x,\Phi^{-1}(\cdot))\,\lambda(y,dx).

(12)

Remark
.

Under the conditions of Theorem
3
, one can show that

\textbf{P}_{\mu}(X(t)\in A\mid\Phi\circ X(s),\,0\leq s\leq t)=\lambda(\Phi\circ X(t),A)

for all
A\in\mathscr{B}(E)
, which gives an interpretation to
\lambda
.

Theorem
3
is intended for situations in which Theorem
2
does not apply. If Theorem
2
does apply, then
Theorem
3
applies as well provided one additional condition, namely (
11
), is satisfied, which in this case reduces to

\int_{E}P(t,x,\cdot)\,\lambda(y,dx)=\int_{E_{0}}\lambda(z,\cdot)\,P(t,w,\Phi^{-1}(dz))

(13)

for every
t>0
and
y\in E_{0}
, where
w\in\Phi^{-1}(y)
. Furthermore, Theorem
3
yields a slightly weaker conclusion because
X
is required to have an initial distribution of a particular form that depends on
\lambda
.

Nevertheless, we will see that both theorems are applicable to our problem.

\displaystyle C(1,1)\int_{\overline{\nabla}_{\infty}}P_{(1,1)}^{0}P_{(2,1)}^{0}\,d\text{PD}_{\theta}\bigg/\int_{\overline{\nabla}_{\infty}}P_{(2,1)}^{0}\,d\text{PD}_{\theta}

\displaystyle\quad{}=\int_{\overline{\nabla}_{\infty}}(2P_{(3,2)}^{0}+2P_{(3,1,1)}^{0}+2P_{(2,2,1)}^{0}+P_{(2,1,1,1)}^{0})\,d\text{PD}_{\theta}\bigg/\int_{\overline{\nabla}_{\infty}}P_{(2,1)}^{0}\,d\text{PD}_{\theta}

\displaystyle\quad{}=\bigg(2\cdot 2\,\frac{\theta^{2}}{\theta_{(5)}}+2\cdot 2\,\frac{\theta^{3}}{\theta_{(5)}}+2\,\frac{\theta^{3}}{\theta_{(5)}}+\frac{\theta^{4}}{\theta_{(5)}}\bigg)\bigg/\frac{\theta^{2}}{\theta_{(3)}}

\displaystyle\quad{}=1-\frac{8+\theta}{(3+\theta)(4+\theta)}.

(24)

To explain the first equality, the integral in the numerator on the left side is the probability that an ordered random sample of size 5 from
\text{PD}_{\theta}
has the form
(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3},x_{3},x_{4})
with
x_{1}\neq x_{2}
and
x_{3}\neq x_{4}
. Let

denote the event just mentioned. Then

\displaystyle\textbf{P}(D)

\displaystyle=\textbf{P}(D\cap\{x_{1}=x_{3},\,x_{2}=x_{4}\})+\textbf{P}(D\cap\{x_{1}=x_{4},\,x_{2}=x_{3}\})

\displaystyle\quad{}+\textbf{P}(D\cap\{x_{1}=x_{3},\,x_{2}\neq x_{4}\})+\textbf{P}(D\cap\{x_{2}=x_{3},\,x_{1}\neq x_{4}\})

\displaystyle\quad{}+\textbf{P}(D\cap\{x_{1}=x_{4},\,x_{2}\neq x_{3}\})+\textbf{P}(D\cap\{x_{2}=x_{4},\,x_{1}\neq x_{3}\})

\displaystyle\quad{}+\textbf{P}(D\cap\{x_{1}\neq x_{3},\,x_{1}\neq x_{4},\,x_{2}\neq x_{3},\,x_{2}\neq x_{4}\}),

which is the integral in the numerator on the right side of the first equality. The second equality uses (
22
).

We can now state the general result.

Lemma 4

(
Feng 2010
,
Feng 2010
, Eq. (5.101))
.

For
\bm{m}=(m_{1},\ldots,m_{k})
with
m_{1}\geq\cdots\geq m_{k}\geq 1
,
\bm{n}=(n_{1},\ldots,n_{l})
with
n_{1}\geq\cdots\geq n_{l}\geq 1
,
k,l\in{\bf N}
,
m=|\bm{m}|
, and
n=|\bm{n}|

\displaystyle\frac{\int_{\overline{\nabla}_{\infty}}P_{\bm{m}}P_{\bm{n}}\,d\text{PD}_{\theta}}{\int_{\overline{\nabla}_{\infty}}P_{\bm{n}}\,d\text{PD}_{\theta}}

\displaystyle=C(\bm{m})\sum_{r=0}^{k\wedge l}\bigg[\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}I\subset\{1,\ldots,k\}:\\
|I|=r\end{subarray}}\;\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\gamma:I\mapsto\{1,\ldots,l\}\\
\text{\rm one-to-one}\end{subarray}}

\displaystyle\quad\;{}\dfrac{\prod_{i\in I}(m_{i}+n_{\gamma(i)}-1)!\,\prod_{i\in I^{c}}(m_{i}-1)!}{\prod_{i\in I}(n_{\gamma(i)}-1)!}\bigg]\frac{\theta^{k-r}}{(n+\theta)_{(m)}},

(25)

where
I^{c}:=\{1,\ldots,k\}-I
.

Proof.

We calculate

\int_{\overline{\nabla}_{\infty}}P_{\bm{m}}^{0}P_{\bm{n}}^{0}\,d\text{PD}_{\theta}=\int_{\overline{\nabla}_{\infty}}\bigg[\sum_{r=0}^{k\wedge l}\;\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}I\subset\{1,\ldots,k\}:\\
|I|=r\end{subarray}}\;\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\gamma:I\mapsto\{1,\ldots,l\}\\
\text{is one-to-one}\end{subarray}}P_{\zeta^{\circ}(\bm{m},\bm{n},I,\gamma)}^{0}\bigg]\,d\text{PD}_{\theta},

where first
\zeta(\bm{m},\bm{n},I,\gamma)\in(\textbf{Z}_{+})^{k+l}
is defined by

\zeta(\bm{m},\bm{n},I,\gamma)_{i}:=\begin{cases}m_{i}+n_{\gamma(i)}&\text{if $i\in I$},\\
m_{i}&\text{if $i\in\{1,\ldots,k\}-I$},\\
n_{i-k}&\text{if $i\in\{k+1,\ldots,k+l\}-\text{Range}(k+\gamma)$},\\
0&\text{if $i\in\text{Range}(k+\gamma)$}.\end{cases}

and then
\zeta^{\circ}(\bm{m},\bm{n},I,\gamma)\in\textbf{N}^{k+l-r}
deletes the
|I|
zeros in
\zeta(\bm{m},\bm{n},I,\gamma)
.
It follows that

\displaystyle\int_{\overline{\nabla}_{\infty}}P_{\bm{m}}^{0}P_{\bm{n}}^{0}\,d\text{PD}_{\theta}

\displaystyle\quad{}=\sum_{r=0}^{k\wedge l}\bigg[\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}I\subset\{1,\ldots,k\}:\\
|I|=r\end{subarray}}\;\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\gamma:I\mapsto\{1,\ldots,l\}\\
\text{one-to-one}\end{subarray}}\;\prod_{i\in I}(m_{i}+n_{\gamma(i)}-1)!\prod_{i\in I^{c}}(m_{i}-1)!

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad{}\cdot\prod_{i\in\text{Range}(\gamma)^{c}}(n_{i}-1)!\,\bigg]\frac{\theta^{k+l-r}}{\theta_{(m+n)}},

where
I^{c}:=\{1,\ldots,k\}-I
and
\text{Range}(\gamma)^{c}:=\{1,\ldots,l\}-\text{Range}(\gamma)
,
and therefore (
4
) follows.
∎

We turn to the evaluation of the right side of (

). Just as we did for the left side, we first consider the example

and

, but we prefer to use a method that generalizes to arbitrary

\displaystyle\textbf{E}[P_{(1,1)}(\rho(\varepsilon V_{1}^{(2,1)},\varepsilon V_{2}^{(2,1)},(1-\varepsilon)\rho_{1},(1-\varepsilon)\rho_{2},\ldots))]

\displaystyle\quad{}=\int_{\overline{\nabla}_{\infty}}P_{(1,1)}(\bm{p})\,\textbf{P}(\rho(\varepsilon V_{1}^{(2,1)},\varepsilon V_{2}^{(2,1)},(1-\varepsilon)\rho_{1},(1-\varepsilon)\rho_{2},\ldots)\in d\bm{p})

\displaystyle\quad{}=\int_{\overline{\nabla}_{\infty}}\bigg(2p_{1}p_{2}+2p_{1}\sum_{j\geq 3}p_{j}+2p_{2}\sum_{j\geq 3}p_{j}+\sum_{i,j\geq 3:i\neq j}p_{i}p_{j}\bigg)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad{}\cdot\textbf{P}(\rho(\varepsilon V_{1}^{(2,1)},\varepsilon V_{2}^{(2,1)},(1-\varepsilon)\rho_{1},(1-\varepsilon)\rho_{2},\ldots)\in d\bm{p})

\displaystyle\quad{}=2\textbf{E}[\varepsilon^{2}]\,\textbf{E}[V_{1}^{(2,1)}V_{2}^{(2,1)}]+2\textbf{E}[\varepsilon(1-\varepsilon)]\,\textbf{E}[V_{1}^{(2,1)}]\,\textbf{E}\bigg[\sum_{i\geq 1}\rho_{i}\bigg]

\displaystyle\qquad\quad{}+2\textbf{E}[\varepsilon(1-\varepsilon)]\,\textbf{E}[V_{2}^{(2,1)}]\,\textbf{E}\bigg[\sum_{i\geq 1}\rho_{i}\bigg]+\textbf{E}[(1-\varepsilon)^{2}]\,\textbf{E}[P_{(1,1)}(\rho_{1},\rho_{2},\ldots)]

\displaystyle\quad{}=2\,\frac{3\cdot 4}{(3+\theta)(4+\theta)}\,\frac{1}{6}+2\,\frac{3\,\theta}{(3+\theta)(4+\theta)}\,\frac{2}{3}+2\,\frac{3\,\theta}{(3+\theta)(4+\theta)}\,\frac{1}{3}

\displaystyle\qquad\quad{}+\frac{\theta(1+\theta)}{(3+\theta)(4+\theta)}\,\frac{\theta}{1+\theta}

\displaystyle\quad{}=1-\frac{8+\theta}{(3+\theta)(4+\theta)},

where
\varepsilon
is beta
(3,\theta)
,
(V_{1}^{(2,1)},V_{2}^{(2,1)})
is Dirichlet
(2,1)
,
(\rho_{1},\rho_{2}.\ldots)
is
\text{PD}_{\theta}
, and all are independent. This matches (
3
).

We can now treat the general case.

Lemma 5

(
Feng 2010
,
Feng 2010
, Lemma 5.9)
.

For
\bm{m}=(m_{1},\ldots,m_{k})
with
m_{1}\geq\cdots\geq m_{k}\geq 1
,
\bm{n}=(n_{1},\ldots,n_{l})
with
n_{1}\geq\cdots\geq n_{l}\geq 1
,

,
m=|\bm{m}|
, and
n=|\bm{n}|

\displaystyle\textbf{E}[P_{\bm{m}}(\rho(\varepsilon V_{1}^{\bm{n}},\ldots,\varepsilon V_{l}^{\bm{n}},(1-\varepsilon)\rho_{1},(1-\varepsilon)\rho_{2},\ldots))]

\displaystyle\quad{}=C(\bm{m})\sum_{r=0}^{k\wedge l}\bigg[\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}I\subset\{1,\ldots,k\}:\\
|I|=r\end{subarray}}\;\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\gamma:I\mapsto\{1,\ldots,l\}\\
\text{\rm one-to-one}\end{subarray}}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\quad\dfrac{\prod_{i\in I}(m_{i}+n_{\gamma(i)}-1)!\,\prod_{i\in I^{c}}(m_{i}-1)!}{\prod_{i\in I}(n_{\gamma(i)}-1)!}\bigg]\,\frac{\theta^{k-r}}{(n+\theta)_{(m)}}.

(26)

Proof.

We calculate

\displaystyle\textbf{E}[P_{\bm{m}}(\rho(\varepsilon V_{1}^{\bm{n}},\ldots,\varepsilon V_{l}^{\bm{n}},(1-\varepsilon)\rho_{1},(1-\varepsilon)\rho_{2},\ldots))]

\displaystyle\quad{}=C(\bm{m})\int_{\overline{\nabla}_{\infty}}P_{\bm{m}}^{0}(\bm{p})\,\textbf{P}(\rho(\varepsilon V_{1}^{\bm{n}},\ldots,\varepsilon V_{l}^{\bm{n}},(1-\varepsilon)\rho_{1},(1-\varepsilon)\rho_{2},\ldots)\in d\bm{p})

\displaystyle\quad{}=C(\bm{m})\int_{\overline{\nabla}_{\infty}}\;\sum_{r=0}^{k\wedge l}\;\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}I\subset\{1,\ldots,k\}:\\
|I|=r\end{subarray}}\;\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\gamma:I\mapsto\{1,\ldots,l\}\\
\text{one-to-one}\end{subarray}}\;\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\lambda:I^{c}\mapsto\{l+1,l+2,\ldots\}\\
\text{one-to-one}\end{subarray}}\;\prod_{i\in I}p_{\gamma(i)}^{m_{i}}\prod_{i\in I^{c}}p_{\lambda(i)}^{m_{i}}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad{}\textbf{P}(\rho(\varepsilon V_{1}^{\bm{n}},\ldots,\varepsilon V_{l}^{\bm{n}},(1-\varepsilon)\rho_{1},(1-\varepsilon)\rho_{2},\ldots)\in d\bm{p})

\displaystyle\quad{}=C(\bm{m})\sum_{r=0}^{k\wedge l}\;\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}I\subset\{1,\ldots,k\}:\\
|I|=r\end{subarray}}\;\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\gamma:I\mapsto\{1,\ldots,l\}\\
\text{one-to-one}\end{subarray}}\textbf{E}[\varepsilon^{\sum_{i\in I}m_{i}}(1-\varepsilon)^{\sum_{i\in I^{c}}m_{i}}]

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\quad\;{}\cdot\textbf{E}\bigg[\prod_{i\in I}(V_{\gamma(i)}^{\bm{n}})^{m_{i}}\bigg]\,\textbf{E}\bigg[\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\lambda:I^{c}\mapsto\textbf{N}\\
\text{one-to-one}\end{subarray}}\prod_{i\in I^{c}}\rho_{\lambda(i)}^{m_{i}}\bigg].

(27)

The three remaining expectations are readily evaluated: First,

\displaystyle\textbf{E}[\varepsilon^{\sum_{i\in I}m_{i}}(1-\varepsilon)^{\sum_{i\in I^{c}}m_{i}}]

\displaystyle=\frac{\Gamma(n+\theta)}{\Gamma(n)\Gamma(\theta)}\,\frac{\Gamma(n+m_{0})\Gamma(\theta+m-m_{0})}{\Gamma(n+m+\theta)}

\displaystyle=\frac{n_{(m_{0})}\,\theta_{(m-m_{0})}}{(n+\theta)_{(m)}},

where

\displaystyle\textbf{E}\bigg[\prod_{i\in I}(V_{\gamma(i)}^{\bm{n}})^{m_{i}}\bigg]

\displaystyle=\frac{\Gamma(n)}{\Gamma(n_{1})\cdots\Gamma(n_{l})}\,\dfrac{\prod_{i\in I}\Gamma(m_{i}+n_{\gamma(i)})\prod_{i\in\text{Range}(\gamma)^{c}}\Gamma(n_{i})}{\Gamma(m_{0}+n)}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n_{(m_{0})}}\,\dfrac{\prod_{i\in I}(m_{i}+n_{\gamma(i)}-1)!}{\prod_{i\in I}(n_{\gamma(i)}-1)!},

and third, with
\widetilde{\bm{m}}\in\textbf{N}^{k-r}
defined to have components
m_{i}
for

\textbf{E}\bigg[\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\lambda:I^{c}\mapsto\textbf{N}\\
\text{one-to-one}\end{subarray}}\prod_{i\in I^{c}}\rho_{\lambda(i)}^{m_{i}}\bigg]=\int_{\overline{\nabla}_{\infty}}P_{\widetilde{\bm{m}}}^{0}\,d\text{PD}_{\theta}=\prod_{i\in I^{c}}(m_{i}-1)!\,\frac{\theta^{k-r}}{\theta_{(m-m_{0})}}.

Substituting these expectations into (
3
), we obtain (
5
).
∎
