1 Introduction
Wind plant operations and maintenance (O&M) costs account for up to 35% of the levelized cost
of energy. Approximately half of the wind plant O&M costs are from turbine O&M, a cost of
approximately $22 per kilowatt per year for land-based wind plants (Wiser, Bolinger, and Lantz
2019; Wiser et al. 2024). Turbine O&M costs therefore represent the single largest component of
wind plant O&M costs and are the primary source of potential O&M cost reductions. Blades,
drivetrains, and related components are significant contributors to existing wind turbine O&M
costs. With more reliable components and better O&M strategies, the levelized cost of energy
could potentially be reduced by 10% for land-based wind plants (Stehly and Duffy 2021).

An example of a wind turbine component with relatively low reliability is the slew ring or
“pitch” bearing, illustrated in Figure 1. Pitch bearings connect the blade root to the hub and
support the resulting simultaneous radial, axial, and overturning moment loads while allowing
relative rotation of the blade to the hub.

 Figure 1. Wind turbine pitch bearing (left) and nacelle (right).
 Adapted from Bayles (2020)

As shown in Figure 2, the Electric Power Research Institute (EPRI) Wind Network for Enhanced
Reliability (WinNER) database indicates the cumulative replacement rate for pitch bearings on
turbines with capacity ratings of 3-MW or greater can reach 10% by the eighth year of service.
This is a far higher replacement rate than for pitch bearings in older 1.5–2-MW and 2–3-MW
turbines, which reach a 10% replacement rate by 15 to 20 years of service (compared to the
expected design life of at least 20 years) (Haus, Sheng, and Pulikollu 2025; Pulikollu et al.
2025). Newer, larger turbines have increased power rating, rotor diameter, and pitch bearing
diameter; they also commonly use independent pitch control systems with increased pitch
activity. Regardless, the trend of worsening reliability is worrisome. Typical pitch bearing
replacements require the use of a large, expensive crane and removal of the blade(s) or rotor,

 1

with a significant amount of downtime. The damage mode with the most critical consequence is
pitch bearing ring cracking, which is becoming more prevalent in larger turbines (Haus, Sheng,
and Pulikollu 2025; Pulikollu et al. 2025) and is caused by pure fatigue or fatigue accelerated by
corrosion. Cracks in pitch bearing rings can cause blades to detach from the turbine, especially if
the blade is mounted to the outer ring. Ring cracks can occur at the outer rings at bolt holes, fill
holes, or locations of significant stiffness changes (Shapiro 2017; Schleich 2025). Cage wear,
raceway damage due to soft zones at fill plugs or edges, raceway wear, and the failure of bolted
connections are also reported to occur in field applications (Kotzalas and Doll 2010; Dvorak
2016; Shapiro 2017; Hornemann 2019; Bayles 2020; Mittleider and Stang 2023; Stammler et al.
2024; Bartschat, Stammler, and Wenske 2024; Molina and Long 2025).

 Figure 2. Pitch bearing cumulative replacement rates (Pulikollu et al. 2025)
Note: EPRI WinNER replacement rates are for all 3 pitch bearings in the rotor assembly and include all failure modes,

Rolling bearings are rated per International Organization for Standardization (ISO) 76, ISO 281,
and ISO 16281. However, because pitch bearings are exposed to discontinuous, oscillatory
motion rather than a relatively constant rotation, the International Electrotechnical Commission
(IEC) 61400-1 wind turbine design standard only requires that the ratio of static rating to design
load is at least 1 according to ISO 76 and that the effects of flexibility on load distribution and
insufficient lubrication due to small movements be considered. The National Laboratory of the
Rockies (NLR) published the first edition of a pitch and yaw bearing design guide meant to

 2

expand on these basic requirements, which summarized the design criteria, calculation methods,
and applicable standards recommended for use in rating analyses of these bearings (Harris,
Rumbarger, and Butterfield 2009). The first edition of the design guide included an example
rating analysis of a relatively small, 1-meter-diameter pitch bearing. More recently, a rating
analysis was performed on a 1.9-meter-diameter pitch bearing representative of that installed on
a 1.5-MW wind turbine (Keller and Guo 2022), a 5-MW reference wind turbine (Rezai and
Nejad 2025), and a 7.5-MW reference wind turbine (Menck, Schleich, and Stammler 2025).
NLR also installed an instrumentation package on a pitch bearing of an operating 1.5-MW wind
turbine in late 2023 (Keller et al. 2024). Since then, bearing ring strain, axial displacement of the
bearing rings relative to each other, and bearing ring temperature measurements were acquired
during turbine operation.

Based on the increasing importance of pitch bearing reliability, the Fraunhofer Institute for Wind
Energy & Energy Systems, the Technical University of Denmark, and NLR recently led a team
of industry, academia, and other researchers to update and revise the pitch and yaw bearing
design guide (Stammler et al. 2024). This updated design guide includes advances in research
and field experiences, enhances understanding of the operating conditions and damage
mechanisms, adds clarification to fatigue life calculations and life modification factors, and
incorporates computational design and publicly available test results from many ground-based
pitch bearing test programs (Menck 2025).

Given the most recent developments in pitch bearing reliability and the publication of the
updated design guide, the purpose of this report is to update the rating analysis of the example
1.9-meter-diameter pitch bearing representative of that installed on a 1.5-MW wind turbine,
compare the rating life to the observed service life of similar pitch bearings, and assess the
validity of the service life modification factor with this large population of bearings. The report
will also summarize the pitch bearing contact conditions, thereby serving as a guide for planned
bench-level fretting wear tests at Argonne National Laboratory.

2.2 Bearing Rating Life
The dynamic equivalent load calculation method used in this analysis follows the “approximate
calculation” method described in the updated design guide for estimating rolling bearing fatigue
life (Menck, Stammler, and Schleich 2020; Stammler et al. 2024). The method is particularly
useful for more qualitative and relative analyses, such as comparing the relative effects of
different controllers, turbine locations, or inflow conditions on loads and rating life (Gebel et al.
2025). It involves four major steps: calculating the dynamic axial load rating, dynamic
equivalent axial load, combined basic rating life, and the modified rating and estimated service
life. For clarity, example calculations like those in the appendices of the updated design guide
are provided.

More advanced finite-element-based methods are also described in the “based on ISO TS 16281”
section of the updated design guide and are used by industry but require higher-fidelity models
and significant computational effort (Becker et al. 2024; He et al. 2024; Rezai et al. 2024;
Stammler and Schleich 2025). These models include not only the pitch bearing but also the bolt,
blade root, and hub stiffnesses and can account for the influences of loads from adjacent blades.
Most importantly, the finite element models can account for structural deflection of the bearing
and the stiffness of structures that are attached to the bearing, which significantly influence the
internal load distribution of the bearing. However, a comparison of the modified rating life
between the “approximate calculation” and ISO 16281 methods for a 7.5-MW reference wind
turbine showed very similar results (Menck, Stammler, and Schleich 2020; Stammler et al.
2024).

2.2.1 Basic Dynamic Axial Load Rating
As described in the updated design guide, pitch bearings are best modeled as thrust-type bearings
because the principal load is an eccentrically applied thrust resulting in an axial load and an
overturning moment load. The radial load affects the load distribution, but not significantly.
Therefore, the rating life equations are based on the appropriate set of equations for an axially
loaded bearing.

the operating time as evidenced in Table 3. Even considering the difference in recommended
design life, the updated design guide estimates a modified rating life much closer to the typical

Cage deformation and raceway spalling damage typical for this population of bearings is shown
in Figure 5. Cage damage commonly results from abrasive contact and wear between the cages
and bearing rings. The flexibility of the bearing rings and cages as well as the dynamic and
kinematic behavior of the rolling body set with varying contact angles are the most likely reasons
for this damage mode. There is, however, no conclusive calculation method currently available
to assess this damage mode, with recent attempts limited to examining the forces caused by
different trajectories of rolling bodies (Jauregui et al. 2025). Spalling in the outer race soft spots
also introduced debris in the grease. The spalling observed in the bearings appeared to be most
severe at the ball fill plug locations on the outer race and on unhardened locations of the inner
race, so-called “soft zones” with lower raceway hardnesses (Dvorak 2016; Shapiro 2017). It is
possible that the spalling originated in these locations due to local weaknesses or raceway profile
differences rather than the hardened parts of the raceway for which the life calculation is
intended. This would imply that the life of a comparable bearing with improved hardening
properties would be longer and the updated design guide would still underestimate the life of a
comparable, properly hardened and designed bearing. Alternatively, the spalling may have
originated on the heat-treated parts of the raceway or from debris resulting from ball and cage
interactions and then spread to the unhardened locations, which are typically located outside of
the expected load zones and protected by locally increased clearance that may not suffice once
spalling debris is in the grease. Further, a combination of these factors may be possible; the
unhardened parts of the bearing may have accelerated the damage, but other factors like less
operational time than assumed by DLC1.1/1.2 may have prolonged it in turn.

The effect of the soft zones at the fill plug location could be represented in two ways in the rating
life analysis. The first is through the rating factor, bm . The updated design guide specifies a
value of 1.3, based on the value in ISO 281 for thrust ball bearings, as the principal load on a
pitch bearing is eccentrically applied thrust resulting in an axial load and an overturning moment
load. This assumption is borne out by the much larger values for axial loads and moments
compared to the much smaller radial loads in Table 3. However, for radial ball bearings with a
filling slot, ISO 281 specifies a value of 1.1 resulting in a 15% reduction in the basic dynamic
load rating and an even larger reduction in the basic rating life. The second is the rating factor,
 aHRC . The effect of lower hardness at the filling slot could be represented by a value less than 1.
If either were to be applied, the service life modification factor would have to be increased even
further to yield an estimated service life like the observed service life.

2.3 Bearing Static Strength
The updated design guide uses methods related to ISO 76 and ISO 17956 to determine a static
safety factor for pitch and yaw bearings, determined by directly comparing the maximum
allowable contact stress to a maximum predicted contact stress. If the maximum allowable
contact stress at the center of the most heavily loaded rolling element/raceway contact is reached,
it is likely that a permanent deformation of 0.01% of the rolling element diameter, Dw , will
occur.

The maximum predicted contact stress is based on defining an extreme load case. All operational
conditions—be they in normal power production or extreme load conditions—and the resulting
maximum loads and stresses are considered “static” in this context and can result in extreme
loads, as the loads occur for a long enough period with relatively little rotational speed compared
to a typical rolling element bearing. Extreme loads that can occur during faults like DLC 2.4 and
other situations like emergency stops are normally determined through many simulations of these
individual conditions and can be strongly influenced by the controller strategy. Rather than
conducting hundreds or even thousands of simulations to examine all these situations, the
updated design guide recommends an abbreviated approach for the simple analysis described
herein. In this approach, the extreme loads Fr,ex , Fa,ex , and M ex are determined by applying a

each of the DLC 1.1 turbulent-seed time series (Stammler et al. 2024), similar to what is

2.4 Miscellaneous Design Considerations
The updated design guide also considers the effect of subsurface shear stress in the core of the
bearing ring with respect to the fatigue and yield strengths related to the hardness of the bearing
core material. Although described in the updated design guide, an assessment of truncation is not
considered in this report, as it requires a finite element model of the bearing.

2.4.1 Subsurface Shear Stress
Pitch bearing rings are surface hardened, typically to produce a minimum raceway hardness of
58 HRC by induction-hardening the bearing steel. The hardening depth depends on the hardening
parameters. The hardness is approximately constant within the hardened layer, and then it
decreases rapidly to the core hardness at the interface. The core hardness depends on the tensile
strength of the raw material. The depth from the surface to the start of the decrease in hardness is
called the effective case depth, he .

As described in the updated design guide, a concentrated load applied by the rolling elements on
the raceway results in the highest subsurface shear stress just below the raceway surface within
the hardened layer, but it also results in appreciable subsurface shear stress that reaches well
down into the core material of the bearing ring. The critical location is typically at the depth
where the core hardness is first reached. The core material can deteriorate if the subsurface shear
stress exceeds its yield strength in shear for an extreme load condition or its fatigue strength in
shear in normal operating conditions over time. Such deterioration eliminates support for the

  physically pressing the hardened case material into the core, or the lack of support for the core
  can cause the case to crack and disintegrate. Each type of failure is assessed in the following
  sections.

3 Conclusions
NLR has a research program related to pitch bearing reliability, recognizing its growing
importance as land-based wind turbines continue to age and larger wind turbines are developed
and installed. In this paper, a rating and service life analysis of a reference pitch bearing for a
1.5-MW wind turbine was conducted in accordance with the updated yaw and pitch bearing
design guide based on loads determined from an OpenFAST model of the turbine. Within the
accuracy of the OpenFAST model, including the controller and resultant pitching motions, and
“approximate calculation” method described in the updated design guide, the key conclusions
from the rating and service life analysis are:

 • The static safety factor, calculated by estimation of extreme loads, is slightly over 2. This
 is greater than the recommendation of 1 described by the updated design guide.
 Additionally, the risk of core crushing is low because the subsurface shear stress at the
 extreme load is well below the allowable levels described in the updated design guide.
 • The combined basic rating life of the reference bearing is estimated to be only 4.68 years.
 The estimated service life is 12.1 years primarily because of the recommended service
 life modification factor of 3 in the updated design guide, which is intended to account for
 the difference in time between the first evidence of rolling contact fatigue and
 replacement of the bearing for inoperability.
 • When accounting for all three pitch bearings in the wind turbine, the estimated service
 life is only 5.8 years. Recent evidence indicates that the corresponding service life for
 wind turbines between 1.5 and 1.85 MW is approximately 17 to 20 years, primarily as a
 result of cage damage and raceway spalling. This provides evidence that the
 recommended value of 3 for the service life modification factor in the updated design
 guide is a conservative estimate, with a value of approximately 9 required to match the
 replacement statistics for this population.
Future work should consider the following:

 • The updated design guide does recommend an assessment of bearing ring structural
 fatigue using other available, general structural analysis guidelines. However, the updated
 design guide gives only general guidelines on modeling requirements. The bearing ring
 strain and relative axial displacement measurements acquired on the subject turbine
 (Keller et al. 2024) could be used for validation of such an assessment. This aspect is
 important, as recent evidence indicates that bearing ring cracking has occurred to a
 greater extent on larger wind turbines (Haus, Sheng, and Pulikollu 2025; Pulikollu et al.
 2025).
 • Safety factors could be compared for pitch bearing populations that experience higher
 rates of replacement due to ring cracking than spalling and wear with a focus on whether
 the static safety factor and subsurface shear stress are closer to their corresponding limits.
 If operational histories for such a population can also be obtained, the relationship
 between these design characteristics and the probability of ring cracking could also be
 examined with physics-based or data-driven models.
 • The updated design guide describes more detailed formulations of the distribution of the
 load among the balls based on detailed bearing design parameters and a method based on

 24

 ISO 16281. Future work should compare the results of this ISO 16281-based method and
 the combined basic rating life to the “approximate calculation” method described in this
 report. However, a similar comparison on a 7.5-MW reference wind turbine showed very
 similar results between the two methods (Menck, Stammler, and Schleich 2020;
 Stammler et al. 2024).
  • Because of its outsized effect on estimated service life, additional validation of the
 service life modification factor for a variety of bearings should be conducted. The
 validation should be against a sufficient population of bearings replaced for damage
 typically associated with rolling contact fatigue.
  • A similar exercise could be done on a population of three-row roller bearings to examine
 any differences particularly in the service life modification factor.
