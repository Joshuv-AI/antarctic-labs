 ABSTRACT
Online synthesis of the power distribution is critical in the operation and control of nuclear power reac-
tors to ensure that the core is operating within safety margins, and to provide essential knowledge asso-
ciated with the burnup of the fuel. In light water reactors (LWRs), power synthesis is achieved by using
some a priori knowledge of the state of the reactor core and updating based on the signals coming from
in-core sensors—namely, self-powered neutron detectors (SPNDs). This report aims to study the effects
of fuel burnup and sensor degradation on the ability to accurately synthesize the power distribution in a
LWR. Several modeling tools were used to simulate power synthesis based on the responses of SPNDs,
with emitters made out of Rh or V. A representative pressurized water reactor low-enriched uranium (LEU)
core was modeled using the Polaris/Purdue Advanced Reactor Core Simulator (PARCS) approach. The
Monte Carlo N-Particle Transport 6 (MCNP6) code was used, as well, to calculate response functions be-
tween different segments of fuel to individual SPNDs; this is a crucial parameter for power synthesis. The
Oak Ridge Isotope GENeration (ORIGEN) package in the Standardized Computer Analyses for Licens-
ing Evaluation (SCALE) code was used to model the time-dependent isotopic transmutation in the SPND
emitters. All these data were fed into a custom code that enacted the point-based iterative (PBI) method
to simulate power synthesis. Developmental work was also performed on high-fidelity SPND models in
the GEometry ANd Tracking 4 (Geant4) code, which enables higher-accuracy modeling of the current re-
sponses from SPNDs.
In this work, five sets of time-dependent power synthesis test cases were conducted. In these test cases,
systematic changes in the input conditions enabled an analysis of the effect of (1) slightly inaccurate a pri-
ori power distribution assumptions with respect to fuel burnup, (2) highly inaccurate a priori assumptions
with respect to fuel burnup (such that burnup is not included in the a priori assumed distribution), and (3)
differences between Rh and V SPNDs in terms of downstream consequences of the transmutation in the
emitters. The authors discovered that one may permissibly have slightly inaccurate a priori assumptions
of the fuel burnup (such that the level of burnup may be slightly under- or over-approximated by the accu-
mulated burnup in approximately 9.3 full power days), but to not account for burnup at all in the a priori
assumption leads to severe levels of error, approaching 25% at maximum. The authors also discovered that
V SPNDs are extraordinarily robust in the low-enriched uranium fuel cycle considered in this modeling
work, whereas Rh SPNDs undergo significant transmutation that can result in large errors in the synthe-
sized power distribution.

 1. INTRODUCTION
Traditionally, nuclear power reactors such as light water reactors (LWRs) have an in-core neutron flux
monitoring system, which typically includes either self-powered neutron detectors (SPNDs) or minia-
ture fission chambers. The data collected from these sensors are used to synthesize the 3D power distri-
bution in the core. A variety of power synthesis methods exist, which have been described in a variety of
patents (Nishizawa 1982; Impink 1987; Impink and Grobmyer 1988; Impink 1988; Impink and Grobmyer
1989a, 1989b; Heibel 1996; Fushimi et al. 2011), and studied in peer-reviewed publications and reports
(In et al. 1991; Park and Shin 2014; Fu, Zhengpei, and Yongming 1997; Kai et al. 2019; Ramezani and
Ghofrani 2021; Shim et al. 2015; Urase, Endo, and Yamamoto 2024; Makai and Végh 2017; Saeed and
Rashid 2020; Dias and Silva 2016; Li et al. 2017; Wang et al. 2024). Furthermore, to some extent, these
methods have been adopted by industry and used in core-monitoring codes to perform this power recon-
struction (Boyd 2002; Boyd, Book, and Morita 2004; Boyd et al. 2009; Boyd and Krieg 2010; DiGiovine
and Noël 2003; Molnar and Vocka 2014; Beard and Morita 1994; Morita 2000; General Electric Company
1999; Global Nuclear Fuel 2016). The power synthesis process, which enables accurately determining the

 1

power distribution, is crucial for several reasons, including the following: ensuring that the reactor power
distribution complies with safety limits, such as the heat flux hot channel factor; calculating burnup lev-
els in the various fuel assemblies within the reactor core; and calibrating ex-core sensors by using in-core
sensors to determine the axial neutron flux distribution.
In the power synthesis field, it can be challenging to develop a full understanding of the efficacy of a par-
ticular synthesis method in a given reactor design. Efficacy, in this case, can be understood by various fac-
tors, including the following: how optimal the choices of sensor placement are in or surrounding the core;
whether the fidelity of the synthesized power distribution is sufficient, without resulting in a significant
computational demand to perform the synthesis in real time; whether the method used for synthesis for a
given reactor design is appropriate, or whether a different method would perform better in terms of error
reduction or calculation time; and whether certain reactor physics aspects, such as fuel burnup and sensor
degradation, are appropriately considered. Unfortunately, methodological details, and supporting studies
performed for development, of codes that are implemented commercially are typically limited because of
their proprietary nature. Therefore, research conducted academically or at research institutions and pub-
licly disseminated is the key to elucidating a qualitative and quantitative understanding of reactor power
synthesis. Specifically, this research must consider the appropriateness of different methods in different
contexts, the effect of sensor placement and sensor uncertainty on synthesis accuracy, and the effect of dif-
ferent physics-based perturbations in the reactor power distribution. The authors of this report have con-
ducted several studies to gain a better understanding of such variables and how they affect the ability to
synthesize the power distribution in advanced LWRs (Birri and Blue 2020; Birri, Sweeney, and Ezell 2022;
Birri et al. 2023; Birri, Gates, et al. 2024; Birri, Goetz, Sweeney, et al. 2024). Additionally, various peer-
reviewed studies have been conducted with a similar intent (Wenfeng et al. 2001; Xia, Jiang, and Luxat
2014; Pomerantz, Calabrese, and Grant 2002; Terman, Kojouri, and Khalafi 2018; Bahuguna, Mukhopad-
hyay, and Tiwari 2021; Peng et al. 2019; Dall’Osso, Martinez, and Rio 2005; Jang et al. 2004; Cao, Cao,
and Gan 2021).
In the context of reactor power synthesis, the landscape is changing regarding the design, development,
and operation of nuclear reactors in the near and distant future. One aspect of the current landscape to con-
sider is the push for lifetime extensions of both Generation II and Generation III reactor designs (Fernández-
Arias, Vergara, and Orosa 2020). These extensions have implications regarding the degradation and possi-
ble replacement of sensors that are used both in-core and ex-core to support reactor power monitoring.
Furthermore, the use of higher-burnup and accident-tolerant fuels is increasingly promoted for LWRs (Hall
et al. 2021; Cumberland et al. 2021; Hu, Mertyurek, and Wieselquist 2022), which would result in longer
fuel cycles and thus more significant in-core and potentially ex-core sensor drift over the course of these
fuel cycles. Finally, next-generation reactors (i.e., Generation IV reactors), which are under development,
will have higher temperatures and higher in-core fluxes (Chen et al. 2021). Also, the flux spectra for cer-
tain Generation IV reactors will be significantly different (e.g. significantly more weighted toward fast
neutrons) in comparison with traditional LWRs. Thus, for these reactors, sensor degradation, sensor place-
ment limitations, and implications of fuel burnup must be considered.
Given the future direction of the nuclear industry, the factors that impact the performance of the sensors,
which are used in the in-core and ex-core neutron flux monitoring systems, must be considered, including
the variations of power distributions due to fuel burnups and the burnups of the sensor emitters themselves,
especially in reactors with longer fuel cycles. In this report, particular focus is given to the in-core sensors,
which are exposed to particularly high levels of neturon flux; they are perhaps most relevant for inferring
more localized perturbations to the power distribution that can arise due to burnup on the assembly-to-
assembly and pin-by-pin level. For in-core neutron flux monitoring, the two most prominent sensors used
for direct measurements are SPNDs and fission chambers. In general, SPNDs are used in pressurized wa-
ter reactors (PWRs) (Todt 1996), and fission chambers are used in boiling water reactors (BWRs), and

 2

are called local power range monitors (LPRMs) (Prasad et al. 1998). Both types of detectors are subject
to a loss of sensitivity over time in the reactor because of burnup of the material in question, which is re-
sponsible for driving the current signal. In the case of fission chambers, the fissioning of U-235 results in
the detector sensitivity reduction as a function of time; in the case of SPNDs, detector sensitivity reduces
because of transmutation of the primary emitter isotope(s) responsible for ultimately generating high en-
ergy electrons via neutron/beta, neutron/gamma/electron, or gamma/electron nuclear reactions. This loss
of sensitivity is important to consider because it will result in error in both the inferred thermal power out-
put magnitude and the synthesized power distribution if not appropriately accounted for via calibration or
model-informed corrections.
Clearly, interplay occurs between the burnup of the fuel and the loss of sensitivity in the sensors; both
are dependent on the energy-dependent neutron fluence in the location of that fuel segment or sensor.
Therefore, the impact of both effects, both independently and simultaneously, must be studied. This report
presents results regarding the effects of fuel burnup and isotopic inventory changes in the emitters of SP-
NDs on the ability to accurately synthesize the 3D power distribution in a representative LWR core. Both
Rh and V are considered as materials for SPND emitters for comparison; Rh is more sensitive but exhibits
sensitivity loss at a faster rate, and these are the two predominant emitter materials used in SPNDs. Differ-
ent levels of fuel burnup are assessed, with different a priori assumptions for burnup used as the initial esti-
mates for the power synthesis method used herein, which is the point–based iterative (PBI) method. Thus,
the consequence associated with different levels of error in the assumed level of burnup can be functionally
understood. SPNDs have been primarily modeled with simple steady-state analytical models in this work.
However, this work also includes enhancements of SPNDs models using the GEometry ANd Tracking 4
(Geant4) code, so that the implications of carefully treating electron transport in realistic sensor geometries
can also be understood in the context fuel burnup and sensor degradation.

2.1 LWR CORE MODEL IN POLARIS/PARCS
This work leverages the existing LWR core models generated in a previous project (Hu, Mertyurek, and
Wieselquist 2022). The core models were generated using the Polaris/Purdue Advanced Reactor Core Sim-

and simulations for regulatory reviews. This work focused on the low-enriched uranium (LEU) (with <5%
enrichment) core models generated in that project; work on the LEU+ (with enrichments varying between
5% to 8%) core models is planned for the next phase of this project.

2.2 MCNP6 INCREMENTAL FLUXES
Monte Carlo N-Particle Transport 6 (MCNP6) was used to determine incremental fluxes (i.e., flux con-
tributions from individual segments of fuel to individual SPNDs) by using a homogenized model of the
representative LWR core, which was heterogeneously modeled in PARCS. This approach was taken to sig-
nificantly reduce computational demand associated with calculating incremental fluxes because the total
number of incremental fluxes is equal to the number of SPNDs in the core, multiplied by the number of
fuel segments, for each reactor state (note that, for the models described herein, "fuel segments" are as-
sumed to be axial discretizations of each individual fuel assembly). These incremental fluxes are used to
calculate response functions, which are the ratio of the electrical current contribution, which comes from
one fuel assembly segment to an SPND, and the power generated in that fuel assembly segment. The con-
sequence of using homogeneous response functions, as opposed to heterogeneous response functions, was
explored in a previous conference paper (Birri, Goetz, and Ezell 2025); in short, homogeneous response
functions will result in appropriate trends in perturbation synthesis error but may underestimate error mag-
nitude because homogeneous response functions do not account for SPND "blind spots."
The particle transport simulations were conducted in a geometry of a homogenized sphere, which was seg-
mented such that it contained a 0.5-cm-radius core and 199 0.5-cm-thick shells, as shown in Figure 3(a).
This homogenized geometry for response function calculations was developed based on another study
(Blue and Jones 2023). The central core of the homogeneous sphere had fissions enabled, whereas fissions
were not enabled in any of the shells (where secondary fissions were ignored in this approach, given that
the focus is to account for neutron flux from the central core to the sensor); this design allows the core of
the sphere to act as a respective fuel segment, whereas the shells act as potential regions in which a sen-
sor may exist, and come in contact with neutrons being emitted from the central core. This geometry thus
effectively enables determining incremental flux as a function of distance between a fuel assembly seg-
ment and an SPND. In terms of MCNP6 functionality, the fissions were disabled in all shells by using the
NONU card, and F4 tallies were applied to each shell with log-scaled energy binning to determine the per-
source particle flux distributions. A point source was placed in the core of the sphere using the Maxwell
fission spectrum and the default Maxwellian temperature in MCNP6.
The homogeneous sphere material included the UO2 fuel, Zircaloy cladding and fuel assembly structures,
and water. The ZrB2 in the IFBA rods, as well as the B4 C in the WABA and control rods, were ignored

2.4 GEANT4
Geant4 simulations, especially those involving neutron reactions, are very computationally expensive be-
cause they require a large number of events to achieve statistical significance. An added complication for
power inferencing specifically is that numerous runs are required to account for the contributions of all
the different fuel elements. Therefore, significant efforts were made in this work to streamline the Geant4
simulation while simultaneously adding features relevant to the task at hand.
Fortunately, power inferencing does not require the same level of detail as detector design. The original
branch of the SPND simulation included tracking to identify the source of each electron generated in the

 9

simulation. This tracking is useful during the design phase to tune a detector’s response to, for example,
neutrons over gamma rays. This feature is also useful in designing for detector response time through iden-
tifying which isotope is producing electrons if the chosen element in the emitter has many stable isotopes
or if there is a strongly produced daughter isotope with a non-ideal halflife. This tracking is highly compu-
tationally expensive and was removed, yielding a 2.5× increase in speed on 4 cores.
One feature that has not previously been included in SPND simulation is the space charge effect. Space
charge is the build-up of an electromagnetic field in the insulator of an SPND operating at steady state.
This electromagnetic field will direct an electron leaving the emitter back into the emitter or, if it is ener-
getic enough to pass over the potential barrier, eject it straight to the collector, where it then counts as sig-
nal produced in the detector. Geant4 has a native module for electromagnetic fields, but it is very computa-
tionally expensive, as observed during previous efforts on fission chambers. Therefore, the authors decided
that the best way to handle the electromagnetic field generation was through an empirical model. Sang et
al. demonstrated that this can be achieved by providing an energy cutoff, emin , to the electrons leaving the
emitter, where it is assumed that all electrons with energy greater than emin will pass the potential barrier to
the collector and produce a signal (Sang et al. 2021). To find emin , one must first solve for electric potential
in the insulator using Poisson’s equation, as shown in Eq. 1, where ρ (r) is the space charge density in the

This work considered and compared Rh and V SPNDs. The benefit of using Rh as the emitter material is
that the neutron sensitivity is quite high, resulting in a relatively high electrical current response, which is
easier to amplify and maintain a high signal-to-noise ratio. The trade-off is that the primary stable isotope
that drives the current response following neutron absorption, Rh-103, will burn up at a relatively fast rate,
resulting in a drop-off of signal as a function of time throughout the fuel cycle. Meanwhile, a V emitter
will burn up at a far slower rate (owing to the much lower neutron absorption cross section of the stable
isotope V-51), although its neutron sensitivity is far lower than that of Rh, resulting in a more challenging
signal amplification problem and a lower signal-to-noise ratio. Implications of signal processing were not
considered in this work, which must be considered when comparing the synthesis results that are informed
from Rh or V SPNDs.

3.4 DESCRIPTION OF SYNTHESIS TEST CASES
In total, five sets of power synthesis test cases were considered in this study (with each set containing a
case for V and a case for Rh emitters), such that each test case involved simulating power synthesis based
on an array of SPNDs (as per the configuration in Figure 7) over the course of the 513.5 day fuel cycle at
a time step of approximately 9.17 days, to study the impacts of the a priori power distribution and burnup
effects of the emitter materials. A summary of the different test cases is provided in Table 2. For the V and
Rh emitters, the test cases are designated accordingly with a "-V" or "-Rh" at the end of the test designa-

 16

tion.
In each test case, a different a priori distribution is used; it serves as an estimated distribution that initial-
izes the iterative process, which is inherent to synthesis via the PBI method. This a priori distribution rep-
resents the modeled power distribution that would serve as the initial estimate in an actual power distri-
bution synthesis, with real sensors and instrumentation, before conducting the PBI method based on data
coming from the in-core sensors. In such a case, the true power distribution can never be known but only
approximated based on the converged solution of the PBI method. In this simulated framework, the true
power distribution is known, so the authors could see how close the synthesized power distribution is to
the true distribution. For a simplistic notation, Pt represents the true power distribution, P s represents the
synthesized power distribution based on simulated sensor responses to Pt , and Pa represents the a priori
distribution.
Since this is a time-dependent synthesis study, the variable time (t) was used to assess the consequences of
inaccuracies associated with the a priori distribution being used. In a perfect time-dependent model of the
reactor, the a priori distribution will perfectly match the true distribution (i.e., Pa (t) = Pt (t)) (the power
distributions calculated by PARCS are used as "true" distributions in this work, in absense of actual plant
operation data). This extreme was considered for test cases 1a-V and 1a-Rh. However, a perfect model of
the reactor cannot be acquired, and the extent to which burnup will occur in the reactor core can be under-
or over-predicted. Therefore, test cases 1b-V and 1b-Rh addressed an overprediction case (where Pa (t) =
Pt (t + ∆T )), and test cases 1c-V and 1c-Rh addressed an underprediction case (where Pa (t) = Pt (t − ∆T )).
Finally, the complete non-consideration of burnup in the a priori distribution (Pa (t) = Pt (t = 0)) was used
in test cases 1b-V and 1b-Rh.
The goal of all test cases with a "1" at the beginning of their designation was to isolate the effect of differ-
ent a priori distribution assumptions on the power synthesis accuracy. These cases assumed that there was
no degradation of the emitters. Conversely, the goal of all test cases with a "2" at the beginning of their
designation was to isolate the effects of sensor degradation. In these cases, the signal loss in a V SPND
was considered in test case 2-V, as per Eq. 5, whereas the signal loss in a Rh SPND was considered in test
case 2-Rh, as per Eq. 6.

Test cases 1b-V and 1b-Rh involved a slight over-approximation of the burnup in the a priori distribution
(i.e., Pa (t) = Pt (t + ∆T )), whereas test cases 1c-V and 1c-Rh involved a slight under-approximation of the

used. The emitter material is indifferent because the sensor degradation effects are not considered in these
test cases, and because the PBI method operates based on the normalized distribution of sensor signals
(i.e., the magnitude of the signals is not a factor).
For test cases 1d-V and 1d-Rh, where the burnup was not accounted for in the a priori distribution (and
thus the t = 0 distribution was used), the errmax and erravg rapidly increased as a function of time (as Pa
and Pt depart from one another significantly), thus leading to large errors in P s . In fact, errmax values be-
came unacceptably high, exceeding 20%. Therefore, in the context of this particular reactor fuel loading

densities in comparison to once- or twice-burned assemblies (Figure 8). These variations in the power dis-
tribution occur on a spatial scale that is smaller than the spacing associated with the SPNDs in the radial
direction. Previous systematic studies of power perturbations have demonstrated that spatially small per-

 5. CONCLUSION
This work demonstrates that time-dependent physics associated with the reactor core and in-core SNPD
sensors can significantly influence the ability to accurately perform power synthesis in an online control
algorithm for a representative LEU PWR core. The a priori assumptions regarding the power distribution,
which come from neutronic model estimates, must be sufficiently accurate relative to the true state of the
reactor core at any given time. The need for accurate a priori power distribution assumptions specifically
relates to the manner by which the burnup of fuel causes local perturbations in the radial power distribu-
tion, due to the fuel loading pattern of the fresh, once-burned, and twice-burned fuel assemblies. Not ac-
counting for burnup at all in the a priori assumed distribution would result in significant errors in the syn-
thesized power distribution, which would likely prevent ensuring that safety margins are being maintained
throughout the fuel cycle. Furthermore, V SPNDs are significantly more robust than Rh SPNDs through-
out the fuel cycle because V has significantly less emitter material burnup than Rh; V SPNDs therefore
enable highly accurate power synthesis. This conclusion, of course, ignores the challenges associated with
amplification of the signal from a V SPND. The results from this work provide useful quantitative data to
support the design and optimization of in-core sensor arrays, and to establish the degree to which one must
accurately predict the level of burnup in the core to perform power distribution synthesis with sufficiently
low error. These data may be useful to industry stakeholders who are looking to undergo power uprates
for their plants, are interested in improving their sensing systems and associated control systems, or are
seeking to adapt well-characterized synthesis methods for advanced reactor designs. The next phase of this
project will focus a representative LEU+ PWR core.
