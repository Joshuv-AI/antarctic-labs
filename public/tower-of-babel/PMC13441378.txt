Familial Risk and Resilience Moderate the Association Between Intolerance of Uncertainty and Youth Suicidal Ideation
Authors: Alexa House, Shiane Toleson, Kathryn C Jenkins, Kayla Kreutzer, Nicholas Allan, K Luan Phan, Stephanie Gorka
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundIntolerance of uncertainty (IU), defined as a trait‐like tendency to respond to uncertain situations with negative cognitive, behavioral, and/or physiological reactions, is associated with increased risk for suicidal ideation (SI). Theory and research suggest that family‐level risk and resilience factors may shape how vulnerability traits such as IU relate to SI; however, few studies have tested these associations. The current study therefore examined the unique and interactive effects of IU and familial risk and resilience factors on SI severity in youth.MethodsA total of 184 individuals, ages 16–19, at elevated risk for escalation in alcohol use, completed self‐report measures of IU and current SI severity. The Family Risk and Protective Factors questionnaire was used to create two composites: familial risk and familial resilience. A series of hierarchical linear regressions were used to test the study hypotheses. Biological sex, trauma exposure, and legal minor status were all included in the models as covariates.ResultsThere were main effects of IU, familial risk, and resilience on SI. At high levels of familial risk, but not low levels, greater IU was associated with greater SI severity. Meanwhile at low levels of familial resilience, but not high levels, greater IU was associated with greater SI severity.ConclusionsResults revealed that the link between IU and SI is particularly robust in the context of low familial resilience and high familial risk. Targeting family‐level factors may be one pathway for altering the negative mental health consequences associated with high IU.Keywords: familial risk, intolerance of uncertainty, resilience, suicide risk

Introduction

Suicide is a pressing public health issue in the United States. In 2022, nearly 50,000 Americans died by suicide and another 2.5 million attempted suicide (Substance Abuse and Mental Health Services Administration 2023). Although suicide represents a serious health concern across the lifespan, rates of suicide in youth have shown particularly concerning trends in recent years. Youth suicide deaths increased 62% between 2007 and 2021 (Curtin and Garnett 2023), an even more dramatic surge than the increase in rates across all ages. Suicide is now the second leading cause of death in youth (Garnett and Curtin 2023), with studies showing that 10% of high school students have made a suicide attempt and 22% have considered suicide (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention 2023). These alarming trends have prompted the declaration of a youth mental health crisis by the U.S. Surgeon General (Office of the Surgeon General 2021) and several pediatric organizations (American Academy of Pediatrics 2021).

To address this crisis, there is a need to better understand who is at risk. Suicidal ideation (SI) is a common risk factor for suicide behavior that often arises during adolescence (Nock et al. 2013). Previous literature suggests that the greatest risk for making a suicide attempt occurs within the first year of SI onset (Nock et al. 2008; Nock et al. 2013), indicating that intervention during this critical developmental window is crucial. Not all young people experiencing SI will go on to attempt suicide (May and Klonsky 2016; Centers for Disease Control and Prevention 2023), but severity of SI has been associated with future suicide attempts (Nock et al. 2013; Park et al. 2016). Studying the factors that relate to the severity of SI in youth can help us better understand who is at risk and develop more effective prevention and intervention strategies.

Emerging research suggests that individual differences in intolerance of uncertainty (IU) are related to SI severity across the developmental spectrum (Allan et al. 2021). IU is the trait‐like tendency to respond to uncertain events and situations with negative cognitive, behavioral, and/or physiological reactions (Carleton 2016). Individuals high in IU react negatively to uncertainty and have difficulty coping with unpredictable or ambiguous stressors. Maladaptive responses to uncertainty exacerbate negative affect and arousal states (Greco and Roger 2003), which is theorized to contribute to thoughts of death or suicide as a means of reliable escape or affective relief (Allan et al. 2023). An early model by Shneidman (1993) posited that suicidal behavior is a means of escaping intolerable psychological/emotional distress and that individual differences in thresholds for tolerating this distress reflect thresholds for suicidal behavior. Indeed, escape/avoidance has been shown to underlie the motivational drive for suicidal behavior (Bryan et al. 2013; Bryan and Rudd 2016). (Figure 1).

Although high IU may increase the propensity for SI, not all individuals with high IU experience suicidality and it is important to understand the additional factors that impact suicide‐related outcomes. Broadly, a variety family‐level factors have been linked to mental and physical health outcomes, including suicide (Repetti et al. 2002; Petruccelli et al. 2019). Decades of research indicates that parent and family factors such as conflict, neglect, abuse, and maladaptive attitudes increase the risk of adolescent and young adult psychopathology (Ackard et al. 2006; Consoli et al. 2013) and directly and indirectly increases SI severity and risk of suicide behavior (Gould et al. 2003; Randell et al. 2006). These family stressors contribute to elevated emotional distress, impaired emotion regulation, and diminished perceptions of safety and support, all of which are established correlates of suicide risk (Miller et al. 2013). In addition to fostering internalizing symptoms such as depression and anxiety, chronic family dysfunction may undermine youths’ ability to tolerate stress and cope adaptively with adversity. Meanwhile, prosocial or adaptive family variables such as parental attachment and positive parenting styles can have a buffering and/or positive impact on youth wellbeing (Orbuch et al. 2022). Secure attachment relationships and supportive parenting are associated with lower levels of emotional distress and more adaptive emotion regulation capacities across adolescence. For instance, adolescents who report feeling connected to and supported by their caregivers are at significantly lower risk for suicide and mental health challenges (King et al. 2017; Orbuch et al. 2022). Perceived family support may foster a sense of safety, belonging, and emotional validation that mitigates feelings of hopelessness and reduces vulnerability to suicidal thoughts. This literature highlights that family‐level factors have the potential to broadly amplify risk and strengthen resilience (Tetzner et al. 2022) and exert independent influences on suicide‐related outcomes.

Regarding IU, research and theory suggest that individual differences in IU emerge early in life and are shaped by both temperamental vulnerabilities and environmental experiences

(McEvoy et al. 2019). Temperamental characteristics such as heightened behavioral inhibition and negative emotionality may predispose some children to experience ambiguity as particularly distressing. Over time, these predispositions interact with caregiving environments to influence how uncertainty is interpreted and managed (Lapidow et al. 2022). In home environments characterized by responsiveness and safety, children may learn to tolerate and manage uncertainty and develop adaptive emotion regulation capacities. In contrast, exposure to unpredictable or conflictual home environments may reinforce beliefs that uncertain situations are dangerous or uncontrollable, thereby consolidating maladaptive beliefs about uncertainty and promoting chronic distress, poor affective coping, and the motivational drive for escape via suicide (Allan et al. 2023). Within this developmental framework, family‐level factors may either exacerbate or ameliorate the downstream impact of high IU. However, no prior study to our knowledge has tested this hypothesis, particularly in youth.

The aim of the current study was to address this gap in the existing literature and test the unique and interactive effects of IU and family‐level factors on severity of current and lifetime SI. The sample was comprised of youth, ages 16–19, oversampled for a variety of childhood adversities and a range of early life experiences. All participants completed a well‐validated battery of questionnaires including assessments of IU, family risk and resilience, and SI severity. Consistent with the existing literature, we hypothesized that IU and family factors would have independent, main effects on youth SI severity. We also anticipated significant IU by family factor interactions such that the impact of IU on SI would be amplified in the context of familial risk and buffered in the context of familial resilience.

Methods

Participants

Participants were recruited as part of a larger study examining neurobiological mechanisms underpinning the association between psychiatric symptoms and alcohol use. Individuals were recruited by flyers posted in the Columbus, Ohio area, social media advertisements, and community engagement events at nearby high school and college campuses. Participants were required to be between the ages of 16 and 19 and be able to provide written informed consent or assent with parental consent. Participants were enrolled into one of two groups: 1) lifetime history of interpersonal trauma exposure (i.e., physical assault, sexual assault, or immediate family violence) or 2) no lifetime history of interpersonal trauma exposure. Given that the aims of the larger study included prediction of initial onset of alcohol use disorder, participants were required to have had minimal alcohol exposure at baseline enrollment (i.e., self‐reported consuming >1 but <100 standard alcoholic drinks in their lifetime) but be at risk for the development of alcohol problems by virtue of self‐reporting affiliation with risky peers and having access to alcohol. Exclusionary criteria for the larger study included any major active medical or neurological illness, lifetime history of manic/psychotic symptoms, deafness, traumatic brain injury, current psychotropic medication use, lifetime history of alcohol or substance use disorder, and pregnancy. A total of 181 participants met these inclusion and exclusion criteria inclusionary criteria and were enrolled in the study. Participants completed a virtual screening session that included informed consent, diagnostic interviews, and a battery of self‐report questionnaires. Participants were monetarily compensated for their time. All study procedures were approved by the university Institutional Review Board.

Intolerance of Uncertainty

In youth ages 18 and 19, the Intolerance of Uncertainty Scale (IUS‐12; Carleton et al. 2007) was used to capture individual differences in IU. The IUS‐12 is a 12 item self‐report questionnaire and items are rated on a five‐point Likert scale with responses ranging from 1 (not at all characteristic of me) to 5 (entirely characteristic of me). Item responses were summed to create a total score, with higher scores indicating higher levers of IU. The IUS was adapted to the Intolerance of Uncertainty for Children (IUS‐C)‐Child Report (Comer et al. 2009). Thus, youth ages 16 and 17 completed the IUS‐C to assess individual differences in IU. The IUS‐C is a 27 item self‐report form measured on a five‐point Likert scale ranging from 1 (not at all characteristic of me) to 5 (entirely characteristic of me). Scores can range from 27 to 135, with higher scores reflecting higher intolerance of uncertainty. To standardize and combine these scales, we first converted all total scores to a Z‐score and then merged the variables to create a single set of values.

Suicidal Ideation Severity

Suicidal ideation was assessed using the Beck Scale for Suicidal Ideation (SSI; Beck et al. 1979). The Beck SSI is a 19‐item self‐report instrument that is used to assess intensity of suicidal ideation in the week before evaluation (Beck et al. 1979). Each item is scored on a 0–2 scale and then summed to create a total score ranging from 0 to 38, with higher scores reflecting greater current suicidal ideation severity.

Family Risk and Resilience

Family risk and resilience was assessed utilizing the Family Risk and Protective Factors questionnaire from the Communities That Care Youth Survey (Arthur et al. 2007). The measure included 38‐items designed to capture a variety of risk and protective factors across multiple ecological domains. In the present study, composite family risk and resilience scores were created. The family risk variable was created by averaging the poor family management (8‐items), family conflict (3‐items), and parental attitudes favorable to antisocial behavior (3‐items) subscales. Together, these subscales capture family environments characterized by inconsistent or ineffective supervision, elevated interpersonal hostility, and parental norms that reinforce maladaptive or antisocial behaviors. The family resilience variable was created by averaging the attachment (4‐items), opportunities for prosocial involvement (3‐items), and rewards for prosocial involvement (4‐items) subscales. These subscales capture the extent to which youth perceive strong emotional bonds with caregivers, access to structured opportunities for positive engagement within the family, and reinforcement for prosocial behaviors. The two composite scales were negatively correlated with one another (r = −0.68, p < 0.001).

Depression and Anxiety Symptoms

Current anxiety and depression symptoms were assessed using the Beck Anxiety Inventory (BAI; Beck et al. 1988) and the Beck Depression Inventory‐II (BDI‐II; Beck et al. 1996). The BAI is comprised of 21 self‐report scored on a four‐point scale from 0 (‘not at all’) to 3 (‘severely’). Participants are asked about the past week with total scores ranging from 0 to 63. The BDI‐II is also comprised of 21 self‐report items scored on a four‐point scale from 0 to 3. Participants are asked about the past 2 weeks with total scores ranging from 0 to 63.

Data Analysis Plan

To test our hypotheses, we performed a series of hierarchical linear regression analyses. Severity of current SI was the primary dependent variable. In all models, biological sex was included as a covariate and entered in Step 1, given prior data showing that female youth are more prone to SI than male youth (Nock et al. 2013). Minor (yes/no) was also included as a covariate in Step 1 to account for the fact that minors (16‐ and 17‐year‐olds) and legal adults completed two separate versions of the IUS scale. Trauma group was further included as a covariate given that the sample was enrolled into one of two pre‐defined groups based on history of trauma exposure. The main effects of the family composite score (risk or resilience) and IU were entered in Step 2. The family composite score by IU interaction term was entered in Step 3. Biological sex, minor status, and trauma group were dummy coded, and all continuous variables were mean‐centered. All analyses were conducted using SPSS v27 (IBM).

Any significant two‐way interactions were followed up using a standard simple slopes approach (Aiken, West, & Reno 1991). Specifically, the moderator (i.e., family factor) was re‐centered at 1 SD above the mean for “high” and 1 SD below the mean for “low.” Two new interaction terms were created and post‐hoc additional follow‐up linear regression models were run at high and low levels of the family‐level factor.

Post‐hoc, we conducted sensitivity analyses to explore whether similar patterns of results were observed for the severity of current depression and anxiety symptoms (dependent variables). SI commonly occurs in the context of other psychiatric symptoms, including depression and anxiety (Kanwar et al. 2013). More broadly, both IU and family‐level factors have been linked to depression and anxiety (Carleton et al. 2012) and thus, it is necessary to explore if the present findings are specific to SI or reflect vulnerability to internalizing psychopathology more broadly.

Follow‐up analyses indicated that that at high levels of familial risk, greater IU was robustly associated with greater SI severity (β = 0.31, t = 3.00, p = 0.003). However, at low levels of familial risk, there was no association between IU and SI severity (β = 0.01, t = 0.12, p = 0.907). Meanwhile, at low levels of familial resilience, greater IU was associated with greater SI severity (β = 0.27, t = 2.82, p = 0.005). At high levels of familial resilience, there was no association between IU and SI severity (β = 0.01, t = 0.04, p = 0.967).

Post‐Hoc Sensitivity Analyses

When depression symptom severity was entered as the outcome, we observed main effects for familial risk (β = 0.17, t = 2.48, p = 0.014), familial resilience (β = −0.23, t = −3.38, p < 0.001), and IU (family risk model: β = 0.44, t = 6.73, p < 0.001; family resilience model: β = 0.43, t = 6.61, p <0.001). There were no significant IU by familial risk (β = 0.12, t = 0.38, p = 0.701) or IU by familial resilience (β = 0.03, t = 0.11, p = 0.914) interactions. When anxiety symptom severity was included as the dependent variable, we found robust main effects for IU (familial risk model: β = 0.47, t = 7.08, p < 0.001; familial resilience model: β = 0.46, t = 7.01, p < 0.001): greater IU was associated with greater anxiety symptom severity. However, there were no main effects of familial risk, familial resilience, or any two‐way interactions (all ps > 0.05).

Discussion

The study explored the unique and interactive effects of IU and family‐level factors on SI severity in youth. As expected, findings indicated that greater IU, greater familial risk, and lower familial resilience were independently associated with SI severity. The findings also revealed two interactions. At high familial risk and low familial resilience, greater IU was associated with greater SI severity. However, at low familial risk and high familial resilience, there was no association between IU and SI severity. Interestingly, post‐hoc analyses suggested main effects of IU on depression and anxiety symptoms, and main effects of familial risk and resilience on depression symptoms (only). IU and family‐level factors did not interact in our depression and anxiety models. Taken together, these findings suggest that while IU represents a transdiagnostic vulnerability for internalizing psychopathology, family‐level factors may play an important role in suicide risk by both directly relating to SI severity and moderating the association between IU and SI.

Suicide risk is considered equifinal, meaning that there are multiple risk factors and pathways to the same result (i.e., suicide). Accordingly, previous research has identified numerous risk factors for SI including IU (Jones et al. 2024; Ye et al. 2023) and family‐level tendencies (Frey and Cerel 2015). In our models, IU and family factors had unique, independent effects given that inclusion of both variables in the model still yielded significant effects. Theory and research suggest that IU may exacerbate SI via increasing distress, arousal, and chronic perceptions of danger/harms related to uncertainty in everyday life (Carleton 2016; Morriss et al. 2023). In turn, chronic distress enhances the motivational drive for escape and fuels thoughts and urges of suicide to gain affective relief (Shneidman 1993). At the same time, family‐level factors influence youth distress levels and broader emotion regulation functioning (Freed et al. 2016). It has been demonstrated that while youth with SI can engage in emotion‐regulation strategies given explicit instruction, there appear to be autonomic emotion‐regulation deficits that have implications for youth with SI during emotional crises that may impact the transition from thinking about suicide to acting on suicidal thoughts (Miller et al. 2018). Additionally, a recent meta‐analysis has strongly suggested that interventions including family‐level factors demonstrate the most efficacious treatment of youth suicidality and dysregulation (Asarnow and Mehlum 2019). These results suggest that family‐level factors can greatly influence youth distress while simultaneously demonstrating the independent pathway from emotion regulation to SI.

Our findings also indicated a significant interaction between these independent factors in relation to SI severity. In the context of high familial risk and low familial resilience, greater IU was associated with greater SI severity. Interestingly, low levels of familial risk and/or high levels of familial resilience buffered the impact of IU such that there was no relationship between individual differences in IU and SI. As previously noted, when faced with uncertainty, individuals with high IU are predisposed to experience greater distress and overestimate the likelihood of negative outcomes (Shihata et al. 2016). In the context of high familial risk and/or low familial resilience, these individuals may be especially vulnerable to difficulties regulating emotions and adaptively coping with IU‐related distress (Repetti et al. 2002; Shihata et al. 2016). In other words, high familial risk and low familial resilience may amplify the negative consequences of IU and strengthen the negative reinforcement processes that link IU to suicide risk. When vulnerable individuals are presented with repeated uncertain stressors without adequate familial supports, they may also perceive a sense of hopelessness and thoughts that suicide may offer a predictable viable solution to their chronic distress (Allan et al. 2023).

On the other hand, the relationship between individual differences in IU and SI severity is absent when familial risk is low and/or familial resilience is high. This suggests that positive family dynamics may act as a buffer against SI in individuals with high IU. This finding expands upon a wealth of existing literature showing that healthy, supportive family environments play a protective role in youth suicide risk (Randell et al. 2006; Diamond et al. 2022). High familial resilience and low familial risk may provide individuals with adaptive coping mechanisms, greater ability to self‐regulate, and access to emotional support in times of adversity (Barber and Olsen 1997; Perry and Pollard 1998), which may protect against the deficits found in those with high IU who lack this buffer.

Although IU and family‐level factors were associated with anxiety and depression symptoms, there were no significant IU by family factor interactions on internalizing symptoms. This suggests that family level factors may not alter the robust association of high IU on broader internalizing psychopathology. Many studies have suggested that IU is a core individual difference factor implicated in the onset and maintenance of depression and anxiety (McEvoy and Mahoney 2011; Robichaud et al. 2019). Because IU has such a strong direct effect on these outcomes, it may not be as susceptible to moderating influences. In this vein, IU is an emerging treatment target for the prevention and treatment of internalizing disorders (Boswell et al. 2013; Talkovsky and Norton 2016), and several empirically supported treatments now include IU‐focused modules and components to directly address anxiety and depression symptoms (e.g., CBT‐IU: van der Heiden et al. 2012).

One possible explanation for the specificity of the moderation effects is that SI reflects a distinct motivational and cognitive process that extends beyond general internalizing symptomatology (Klonsky & May, 2015). While depression and anxiety symptoms capture sustained affective distress and physiological arousal, suicidal ideation often reflects escape‐oriented cognitions and perceived intolerability of distress. IU may operate as a broad transdiagnostic vulnerability for internalizing psychopathology, exerting strong and direct main effects on depression and anxiety regardless of contextual factors. In contrast, the emergence of suicidal thinking may depend more heavily on environmental amplification or buffering processes, such as family environment, that shape how distress is interpreted, contained, or escalated. This distinction underscores the conceptual and clinical importance of examining SI as a transdiagnostic yet mechanistically distinct outcome.

The link between IU and suicide risk appears to be nuanced and addressing it may require intervention beyond that which targets IU directly. The findings from the present study suggest targeting family‐level factors could mitigate the association between IU and suicide risk, particularly in youth. For instance, considering a strengths‐based approach, family treatment packages that aim to improve protective factors such as communication, attachment, and prosocial exchanges, may offset the negative consequences of high IU on SI. Family involvement in interventions has been utilized with promising outcomes for a wide range of mental health challenges (Carr 2019; Pine et al. 2024). Thus, targeting family‐level factors in youth interventions may offer an opportunity to indirectly reduce youth suicide risk by improving familial resilience and consequently tempering the effects of high IU on SI.

The present study had numerous strengths, including a large youth cohort and the use of well‐validated assessments. However, the study also had several limitations. The inclusion and exclusion criteria were determined by the design of the larger parent study and excluded individuals currently taking psychotropic medication. Although participants were non‐treatment‐seeking, the sample was intentionally enriched for interpersonal trauma exposure and elevated risk for escalation of heavy alcohol use, resulting in a high‐risk cohort by design. Additionally, the sample was predominantly White and female, which may limit generalizability. Accordingly, the findings may not extend to more demographically diverse populations, individuals currently receiving psychiatric treatment, or youth with differing levels or profiles of suicide risk.

Future longitudinal studies with a more heterogeneous sample are necessary to account for potential study confounds. Additionally, measurements of SI, IU, and family‐level factors in the current study relied upon self‐report data. Though the assessments employed in the current study were well‐validated, responses to self‐report surveys are nonetheless prone to certain types of bias (Althubaiti 2016). Finally, the study focused on SI as a measure of suicide risk. While SI is a prevalent risk factor for suicide, the majority of individuals with SI do not go on to attempt or complete suicide (Nock et al. 2008) and as such, SI is flawed as a correlate of suicide risk. Suicidal behavior, such as past suicide attempts, is distinct from SI and represents a more direct indicator of suicide risk (Borges et al. 2008; May and Klonsky 2016). Future studies should examine the relationship between IU, family‐level factors, and suicidal behavior to expand upon the findings of the present study.

In sum, the current study revealed that the link between IU and SI is particularly robust in the context of low familial resilience and high familial risk. Targeting family‐level factors may be one pathway for altering the mitigating the risk of suicide, but not internalizing symptoms, in youth with high IU.
