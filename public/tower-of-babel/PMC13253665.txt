Impact of chronic cholecalciferol supplementation and vitamin D status on risk of post-vaccination SARS-CoV-2 breakthrough infection
Authors: Luigi di Filippo, Simona Bolamperti, Marco Campaniolo, Licia Gifuni, Andrea Giustina
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionVitamin D has been implicated in modulating immune responses to COVID-19 and anti-SARS-CoV-2 vaccination. However, whether chronic cholecalciferol supplementation influences the risk of post-vaccination breakthrough infection remains unclear. This retrospective observational study investigated the role of chronic vitamin D supplementation and circulating 25(OH) vitamin D concentrations on SARS-CoV-2 breakthrough infection after mRNA vaccination.MethodsAdult patients vaccinated with two doses of BNT162b2 and without prior SARS-CoV-2 infection were included. Individuals chronically supplemented with cholecalciferol were compared with non-supplemented subjects and subsequently matched 1:1 for age, sex, and comorbidities. Breakthrough infection within 12 months after vaccination was recorded.ResultsA total of 114 patients were evaluated; 70 (61.4%) received chronic cholecalciferol supplementation and 27 (23.7%) developed breakthrough infection. Infection occurred less frequently among supplemented patients (17% vs. 34%, p = 0.044). Circulating 25(OH) vitamin D concentrations were significantly lower in patients with infection (p < 0.001) and showed a significant predictive performance (AUROC 75.6%, p < 0.001), with an optimal cut-off of 18.5 ng/mL. In multivariable analysis, 25(OH) vitamin D concentrations emerged as the only independent predictor of breakthrough infection (OR 0.86, p = 0.003). In the matched cohort (n = 78), breakthrough infection remained numerically lower among supplemented individuals (18% vs. 36%, p = 0.125); lower 25(OH) vitamin D concentrations were independently associated with infection risk (OR 0.88, p = 0.007), with similar ROC performance (AUROC 77%, p < 0.001).ConclusionsAdequate vitamin D status, predominantly achieved through chronic cholecalciferol supplementation, was independently associated with reduced risk of SARS-CoV-2 breakthrough infection after vaccination. Maintaining sufficient 25(OH) vitamin D concentrations may represent a simple and potentially relevant strategy to optimize post-vaccination protection.Keywords: Vitamin D, vaccination, COVID-19, cholecalciferol, SARS-CoV-2, anti-COVID-19 vaccine

Introduction

Vitamin D is a pleiotropic hormone with well-established immunomodulatory properties, acting on both innate and adaptive immunity [1–3]. During the COVID-19 pandemic, accumulating evidence suggested that hypovitaminosis D may represent a modifiable risk factor for SARS-CoV-2 infection and adverse outcomes, and a higher risk of Long COVID syndrome [3–6].

Vitamin D has also been investigated as a potential modulator of vaccine-induced immune responses. Preclinical studies demonstrated that vitamin D may enhance antigen-presenting cell migration and promote both humoral and cellular immune responses [7, 8] and, in clinical settings, evidence from influenza vaccination studies suggested a possible strain-specific influence of vitamin D status on seroprotection [9]. With regard to anti-SARS-CoV-2 vaccines, several observational studies have reported positive associations between higher 25(OH) vitamin D concentrations and vitamin D use with stronger and more sustained humoral responses, including higher anti-Spike or neutralizing antibody titers months after vaccination [10–18]. Some data also suggest improved cellular immune responses promoted by vitamin D [19, 20]. However, not all investigations have confirmed these findings failing to demonstrate a consistent benefit of vitamin D supplementation on short-term antibody responses or immunogenicity endpoints after COVID-19 vaccination [21–23]. Overall, the available literature is characterized by heterogeneity in study design, baseline vitamin D status, supplementation regimens, vaccine types, prior infection status, and timing of immune assessment. Moreover, importantly, although several studies have focused on surrogate immunological endpoints, such as antibody titers, no study has specifically addressed whether vitamin D supplementation influences the risk of SARS-CoV-2 breakthrough infection after vaccination. Therefore, the clinical relevance of vitamin D status in the post-vaccination setting remains unclear.

In this context, the aim of the present study was to evaluate whether chronic cholecalciferol use is associated with a reduced risk of breakthrough SARS-CoV-2 infection in vaccinated individuals, thus exploring the potential clinical impact of vitamin D beyond immunogenicity markers and toward real-world infection outcomes.

Materials and methods

Study design

This was a retrospective observational study performed in a tertiary health care centre, the IRCCS Ospedale San Raffaele, Milan, Italy. The study-protocol complies with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the hospital ethics committee (protocol no. CET 178–2023 ABIO/NC/06 ClinicalTrials.gov ID NCT06452082). Signed informed consent was obtained from all patients participating in this study. We included only adult (age ≥ 18 years) patients immunized with two doses of Comirnaty (BNT162b2 mRNA Pfizer-BioNTech) vaccine from January 2021 to December 2021 and who were previously admitted in years 2019 and 2020 in our Hospital Endocrine Outpatient Department for general endocrine consultations. We included only patients without either a previous history of SARS-CoV-2 infection or COVID-19 symptoms before the vaccination.

An assessment on the post-vaccination SARS-CoV-2 breakthrough infection and COVID-19 occurred within the 12 months after immunization were specifically collected from all patients though telephone interviews conducted by trained and dedicated personnels.

Patients treated with vitamin D supplementation were subsequently matched on a 1:1 ratio for age, sex, concomitant comorbidities with patients not supplemented in order to assess the impact of vitamin D supplementation on post-vaccination SARS-CoV-2 occurrence.

Data collection

Data recorded upon endocrine visits were collected directly by patient interview or from medical chart review and were cross-checked with patients during telephone interviews. The following variables were collected: age, gender, body mass index (BMI), history of arterial hypertension, diabetes mellitus, cardiovascular disease, chronic kidney disease, and history of active neoplasia.

To homogenise the investigation of the vitamin D supplementation role, for this study, we considered only patients with or without parent vitamin D formulations, in particular vitamin D3 cholecalciferol. Thus, the vitamin D supplemented patients’ group only included those who were treated, for at least 24 months before the hospital endocrine visit, with cholecalciferol as for clinical practice and recommended guidelines [1, 24–27]. Data about type of supplementation, dosages and starting time were routinely and systematically collected. Serum 25(OH) vitamin D concentrations were recorded upon hospital endocrine visits or, alternatively, if not available, they were retrospectively and specifically collected for the study purpose from electronic regional health-care information system if performed up to 12 months before the hospital visit. 25(OH) vitamin D concentrations were measured using electrochemiluminescence immunoassays (ng/mL). Vitamin D deficiency was defined with concentrations below 20 ng/mL [1, 28].

Confirmed post-vaccination SARS-CoV-2 breakthrough infection was defined as positive real-time reverse-transcriptase polymerase chain reaction from a nasal and/or throat swab together with signs, symptoms, and/or radiological findings suggestive of COVID-19 pneumonia.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were obtained for all study variables. Categorical variables were summarized as counts and percentages. Kolmogorov–Smirnov normality test was performed (p < 0.05) and continuous variables were expressed as medians and interquartile range (IQR) (25th–75th percentile). Fisher exact test or χ2 test and the Mann–Whitney U test or the Kruskal–Wallis test were used to determine the statistical significance of differences in proportions and medians, respectively. Multiple logistic regression analyses were used to estimate the odds ratio (OR) with 95% confidence interval (CI) of the different assessed variables for the post-vaccination SARS-CoV-2 infection occurrence risk. The performance of 25(OH) vitamin D concentrations in predicting the post-vaccination SARS-CoV-2 infection outcome was estimated using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC) with the 95% CI, and the optimal cut-off value was chosen to maximize the sum of the sensitivity and specificity on the Youden index. All statistical tests were two-sided. P value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Statistical analysis was conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics (IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 23.0. Armonk, NY: IBM Corp.) and GraphPad Prism (GraphPad Software for Windows, version 9.0.0, San Diego, California USA).

Results

Demographics, vitamin D supplementation and outcomes

A total of 114 patients with available information during the 12-months after anti-SARS-CoV-2 vaccination collected by telephone interview and who did fulfil the above-mentioned criteria, were included in this study. Demographic characteristics of patients’ cohort are summarized in Table 1. Median [IQR] age was 57 [45–67] years and 17 (15%) were male. At baseline visit, 70 patients (61.4%) were treated with cholecalciferol chronic supplementation that was subsequently maintained for the entire study-period. Median time of supplementation was 36 [25–75] months. A biochemical assessment of serum circulating 25(OH) vitamin D concentrations was available in 82 patients (50 supplemented receiving cholecalciferol and 32 without) and median values were 25.5 [18–31] ng/mL. Among these 82 patients, a status of vitamin D deficiency was observed in 26 of them (31.7%), and its prevalence was significantly higher in patients without supplementation (24/32 75%) as compared to those supplemented (2/50 4%) (p < 0.001). Serum 25(OH) vitamin D concentrations were significantly higher in patients receiving cholecalciferol as compared to those without supplementation (30 [27–33] vs. 18 [16–19] ng/mL; p < 0.001). SARS-CoV-2 breakthrough infection occurred in 27 patients (23.7%) with a median interval time of 6 [3–7] months after vaccination (Table 1).

Post-vaccination SARS-CoV-2 breakthrough infection and predictive factors

Post-vaccination SARS-CoV-2 breakthrough infection occurred in 27 patients (23.7%). No statistically significant differences were observed between patients with and without infection regarding age, gender, and comorbidities (Table 2). A significant lower occurrence rate of post-vaccination SARS-CoV-2 infection was observed in patients supplemented with cholecalciferol (12/70 17% vs. 15/44 34%, p = 0.044) (Table 1). Circulating 25(OH) vitamin D concentrations were available in 20/27 (74%) patients with infection and in 62/87 (71%) without. Patients presenting with post-vaccination SARS-CoV-2 infection were characterized by statistically significant lower 25(OH) vitamin D concentrations as compared to those without (Table 2), and by a non-significant trend toward higher prevalence of vitamin D deficiency (Table 2). Among patients receiving cholecalciferol supplementation, the majority were treated with weekly regimens (45/70, 65%), followed by daily (18/70, 25%) and monthly schedules (7/70, 10%). The occurrence of breakthrough infection was comparable across regimens, being observed in 7/45 (15%) of weekly, 3/18 (16%) of daily, and 2/7 (28%) of monthly treated patients. With regard to supplementation doses, nearly all treated patients (68/70, 97%) received daily equivalent doses ≥ 750 IU. No significant differences in breakthrough infection occurrence were observed between subjects receiving ≥ 1000 IU/day and those receiving < 1000 IU/day (8/37, 21.6% vs. 4/33, 12.1%; p = 0.36).

Vitamin D supplementation matched analyses and post-vaccination SARS-CoV-2 breakthrough infection

Patients supplemented with cholecalciferol were matched on a 1:1 ratio for age, sex, and concomitant comorbidities with those without vitamin D supplementation. A total of 78 patients were included in the analyses, 39 supplemented and 39 without supplementation. A biochemical assessment of serum circulating 25(OH) vitamin D concentrations was available in 62 patients (32 supplemented with cholecalciferol and 30 without) and median values were 23 [18–31] ng/mL. In this matched cohort as well, prevalence of vitamin D deficient status was significantly higher in patients without supplementation as compared to those supplemented (23/30 76.6% vs. 1/32 3.1% p < 0.001), and serum 25(OH) vitamin D concentrations were significantly higher in patients receiving cholecalciferol as compared to those without supplementation (31 [27.2–33] vs. 18 [16-19.2] ng/mL p < 0.001). SARS-CoV-2 infection occurred in 21 patients (26.8%) with a median interval time of 6 [3.5-7] months after vaccination. In this matched cohort, no statistically significant differences were observed between patients with and without infection regarding age, gender, BMI, and comorbidities. A non-significant lower occurrence rate of post-vaccination SARS-CoV-2 infection was observed in patients supplemented with cholecalciferol (7/39 18% vs. 14/39 36%, p = 0.125). Circulating 25(OH) vitamin D concentrations were available in 17 (17/21 81%) patients with infection and in 45 (45/61 74%) without. Patients presenting with post-vaccination SARS-CoV-2 infection were characterized by statistically significant lower 25(OH) vitamin D concentrations as compared to those without (18 [15.5–23] vs. 28 [19–32] ng/mL p = 0.001), and by a non-significant trend toward higher prevalence of vitamin D deficiency (10/17 59% vs. 14/45 31% p = 0.078).

As a result of the match 1:1, no statistically significant differences were observed between patients with and without cholecalciferol supplementation regarding age, gender, BMI, and comorbidities.

ROC analyses showed that the global performances of 25(OH) vitamin D concentrations to predict post-vaccination SARS-CoV-2 infection were significant with the best youden index of 18.5 ng/mL (78% sensitivity, 59% specificity and AUROC 77%, p < 0.001, CI 0.65–0.89). A multiple regression analysis including demographic characteristics, comorbidities, and 25(OH) vitamin D concentrations was performed to estimate the predictive risk factors for post-vaccination SARS-CoV-2 infection occurrence in this matched cohort. 25(OH) vitamin D concentrations resulted as the only variable significantly and independently associated with the outcome (OR 0.88, CI 0.76–0.95 p = 0.007).

Discussion

In this study, lower circulating 25(OH) vitamin D concentrations were consistently associated with a higher risk of SARS-CoV-2 breakthrough infection during the 12 months following mRNA vaccination. Chronic cholecalciferol supplementation was associated with a lower infection rate in the overall cohort, and this association appeared to be largely mediated by the achieved vitamin D status. Indeed, circulating 25(OH) vitamin D concentrations emerged as the only independent predictor of breakthrough infection in both the global and matched analyses.

Vitamin D is widely recognized as a pleiotropic immunomodulatory hormone acting at multiple levels of the immune response [1–3]. Experimental models have shown that calcitriol enhances antigen-presenting cell migration and modulates dendritic cell trafficking to secondary lymphoid organs, thereby influencing T- and B-cell activation and the quality of adaptive immune responses [7, 8]. These mechanistic data provide biological plausibility for a role of vitamin D status in shaping vaccine-induced immunity. In clinical settings, several observational studies have reported associations between lower 25(OH) vitamin D levels and weaker or less sustained humoral responses after SARS-CoV-2 vaccination [9–18]. In healthcare workers receiving BNT162b2, individuals with serum 25(OH) vitamin D concentrations below deficiency thresholds at the time of vaccination exhibited significantly lower antibody titers at medium- and long-term follow-up, with independent correlations between circulating 25(OH) vitamin D concentrations and antibody responses [10]. Similar findings have been described in selected populations, including patients undergoing hemodialysis [14, 15] and in longitudinal cohorts showing a more rapid antibody decline in vitamin D deficient individuals [17, 18]. Complementary mechanistic evidence from interventional studies using calcifediol supplementation has suggested enhanced T-cell activation, memory formation, and upregulation of innate immune pathways following vaccination, supporting a potential role of vitamin D sufficiency in sustaining cellular immune responses [19, 20].

Nevertheless, results across studies have been heterogeneous. Sub-analyses nested within randomized trials and observational investigations in general or institutionalized populations have failed to demonstrate a consistent impact of vitamin D supplementation or baseline status on short-term antibody responses [21–23]. Such discrepancies likely reflect variability in baseline vitamin D levels, supplementation regimens, vaccine platforms, timing of immune assessment, and definitions of deficiency. Importantly, the vast majority of published studies have evaluated immunogenicity markers rather than clinically meaningful post-vaccination infection risk.

In this context, our findings extend previous observations by suggesting that circulating 25(OH) vitamin D concentrations are associated with the risk of SARS-CoV-2 breakthrough infection after vaccination. While chronic supplementation was linked to lower infection rates in the overall cohort, this association appeared largely mediated by achieved vitamin D status. Circulating 25(OH) vitamin D concentrations consistently emerged as independently associated with infection risk after adjustment for demographic characteristics and comorbidities. These data indicate that the biological effect of vitamin D may depend less on supplementation per se and more on whether sufficient circulating levels are achieved and maintained. In our cohort, no clear differences in breakthrough infection rates were observed according to supplementation regimens (daily, weekly, or monthly) or dosing categories. However, these analyses were exploratory and limited by the relatively small sample size of subgroups, with the vast majority of patients receiving doses ≥ 750 IU/day. In addition, only two supplemented patients presented persistent vitamin D deficiency despite cholecalciferol treatment, precluding meaningful analyses according to achieved adequate vitamin D status within the supplemented subgroup. Therefore, no definitive conclusions can be drawn regarding the optimal dosing strategy and these findings further support the concept that clinical outcomes may be closely related to achievement of adequate circulating 25(OH) vitamin D concentrations rather than the specific supplementation regimen or dose per se [29]. Notably, the risk-thresholds identified in our analysis closely approximated the conventional cut-off used to define vitamin D deficiency (< 20 ng/mL), as proposed by international consensus statements and clinical practice guidelines [1, 24–28]. This observation reinforces the concept that the potential benefit of vitamin D in the post-vaccination setting may be particularly relevant in individuals with deficient status. Correction of overt deficiency, rather than generalized supplementation irrespective of baseline levels, may represent a clinically meaningful strategy, as demonstrated in other skeletal and extra-skeletal settings where supplementation trials that did not specifically select participants according to baseline vitamin D deficiency failed to demonstrate consistent vitamin D positive effects [1–3, 30, 31].

From a clinical perspective, our data suggest that maintaining adequate vitamin D status, especially avoiding deficiency, may contribute to optimizing immune competence in the context of vaccination. This consideration may be particularly relevant in individuals with chronic comorbidities, among whom vitamin D deficiency is common and vaccine responsiveness may be attenuated [1, 14, 15]. In this regard, adherence to established recommendations for vitamin D assessment and correction may have implications extending beyond skeletal health.

Several limitations of our study should be acknowledged. The retrospective design and relatively small sample size limit causal inference. Serum 25(OH) vitamin D concentrations were not available for all participants and although matching and multivariable analyses were performed, residual confoundings cannot be excluded. Furthermore, the study was conducted in a single tertiary endocrine outpatient setting and during a specific phase of the pandemic, potentially limiting generalizability to contemporary clinical settings.

In conclusion, in a cohort of individuals vaccinated with mRNA vaccine and without prior SARS-CoV-2 infection, lower circulating 25(OH) vitamin D concentrations were independently associated with a higher risk of breakthrough infection over 12 months of follow-up. These findings support a potential role of adequate vitamin D status in modulating post-vaccination susceptibility to infection and warrant confirmation in prospective studies specifically designed to assess those clinical endpoints.
