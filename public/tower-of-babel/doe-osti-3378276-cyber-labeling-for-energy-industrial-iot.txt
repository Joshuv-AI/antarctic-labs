Executive Summary
Scope of the Cyber Labeling for Energy IIoT Effort
The U.S. Department of Energy’s (DOE) Office of Cybersecurity, Energy Security and Emergency
Response (CESER), at the request of the Deputy National Security Advisor for Cyber and Emerging
Technologies, Anne Neuberger, initiated research in 2023 to develop a cybersecurity labeling proof-
of-concept for energy products to expand on the Federal Communications Commission’s (FCC)
proposed U.S. Cyber Trust Mark program.

DOE mobilized researchers from six National Laboratories to develop and gather feedback on a
proof-of concept label for solar inverters and smart meters, which serve as representative products
for market-facing energy sector Industrial Internet of Things (IIoT). This report details the research
team’s process across two phases and the resulting findings, which include challenges facing cyber
labeling programs and recommendations to implement an expanded IIoT cyber labeling program in
the U.S.

Summary of Findings
The research team engaged with smart meter and inverter vendors in the U.S. market and collected
input on label disclosures and approaches for a verification process. They also reviewed existing
global labeling programs and verified that a labeling program does not currently exist that adequately
addresses the privacy and security concerns specific to IIoT.

The research team worked with vendor partners to develop an initial label mockup to disclose
information related to 47 privacy and security elements and made recommendations for how this
type of label may appear, be used and be stored.

The research team reviewed guidance from the National Institute of Standards and Technology
(NIST) and found close alignment with its own independently developed set of security elements for
IIoT, except for crucial privacy baseline elements. The research team recommends leveraging the
NIST guidance (instead of the initial label mockup) for the Cyber Trust Mark, but strongly
recommends integrating privacy-based elements and adapting the guidelines beyond a product-
based scope to encompass the entire system.

Further, the research team recommends the development and implementation of a “Cyber Trust
Guide” as a supplemental label that would address concerns from vendors about the applicability of
the proposed Cyber Trust Mark to IIoT, allow for larger participation in the labeling process, and
provide easily accessible information about the security of devices and systems—even in more
complex use cases.

The research team also outlines options to verify the validity of information a vendor discloses on the
label and detail the limitations of such claim verification solutions.

Gaps and Additional Research Needed
The implementing body should consider methods to address challenges associated with claim
verification for cyber labeling implementation and to address risks associated with public disclosure
of IIoT security and privacy information. The research team also recommends additional research be

Introduction
Background and Scope
The U.S. Department of Energy’s (DOE) Office of Cybersecurity, Energy Security, and Emergency
Response (CESER), at the request of the Deputy National Security Advisor for Cyber and Emerging
Technologies, Anne Neuberger, initiated research in 2023 to develop recommendations for energy
product cybersecurity labeling, specifically pertaining to smart meters and power inverters. This
effort mobilized researchers from six National Laboratories to identify cybersecurity labeling
requirements that would encourage vendor innovation and investment in product security and
improve transparent communication about security in the energy Industrial Internet of Things (IIoT)
product marketplace.

This research is intended to build upon and inform the Federal Communications Commission’s (FCC)
proposed U.S. Cyber Trust Mark program, which, according to the establishing executive order ,
would “raise the bar for cybersecurity across common devices, including smart refrigerators, smart
microwaves, smart televisions, smart climate control systems, smart fitness trackers, and more.”

IIoT vs IoT: Properties, Consumers, Stakeholders, and Scale of Consequence
The Industrial Internet of Things (IIoT) is a network of smart devices that has its own computing
capabilities. When these devices are connected, they form systems that collect, monitor, exchange,
and analyze data on an industrial scale. 1 It is necessary to investigate the applicability of cyber
labeling to IIoT because of its unique properties. IIoT products have capabilities, consumers,
stakeholders, and consequences that differ from “traditional” home IoT products and may require
different treatment when considering cybersecurity labeling approaches.

Further, IIoT has consumer properties that are unique from that of IoT. Smart meters, one of this
project’s focus technologies, serve as a clear example of the IIoT and IoT dichotomy. The meter is
physically attached to and associated with a private residence or business. Although the property
owner does not own the meter, they are directly impacted by its operation. Typically, a local utility
would own the meter and may be responsible for initial selection and implementation. Alternatively,
the utility may be a subsidiary of a larger system provided by a third-party integrator who makes
purchasing and integration decisions on behalf of the utility.

As such, the stakeholder base to whom IIoT security labeling is relevant to differs significantly from
that of IoT and has different interests, including:

 • End consumers who have vested interest but limited control.
 • Integrators and installers that make purchasing decisions.
 • Consumers and stakeholders with unique definitions of what it means to be secure,
 dependent on the user’s context or use case.
 • Stakeholders to whom specific security controls may be more or less important depending
 on their needs.
 • Entities with complex architecture with functionality spread across multiple devices that may
 impact how security information is interpreted (i.e., the device labeled may not be what is
 actually impacting security.)

These varying properties may impact how the label is interpreted, interfaced with, and utilized in the
energy IIoT space.

Project Overview
This report details the research team’s process across two phases and the resulting findings, which
include challenges facing cyber labeling programs and recommendations to implement an expanded
IIoT energy labeling program in the U.S.

During Phase 1, the research team investigated domestic and foreign standards applicable to a label
for market-facing energy infrastructure, reviewed existing literature on labeling efforts, and gathered
insights from vendor partners. Using this qualitative and quantitative data, the research team then
developed an initial label concept (“label mockup”) to compare with existing recommendations and
serve as the foundation for the next phase of analysis.

In Phase 2, the research team assessed potential labeling implementation approaches by mapping
the label mockup to and comparing against NIST’s cybersecurity baselines for IoT cyber labeling,
refining a supplemental “Cyber Trust Guide” concept, collecting user feedback, and investigating
claim verification methods.

Key Findings
The research team reached several key recommendations, outlined below. The methods, process,
justification, and additional considerations related to these recommendations are detailed further
throughout the body of this report.

Data Elements
The research team supports the recommendation of the NIST-IR 8259 series of reports on IoT
cybersecurity manufacturers and their third-party vendors 2 as the basis for a cybersecurity labeling
program, with some distinct recommended adjustments. The recommended IoT security activities
documented in NIST-IR 8259A/B were closely aligned with the security elements our research team
derived from an independent evaluation of security standards, labeling programs, and baselines
from around the world – with one notable exception. 3 The NIST security baseline considerations did
not include any baseline elements for privacy.

The research team recommends that any IoT and IIoT Cyber Trust Mark include privacy-based
elements that represent prevailing privacy focused standards and controls. In particular, the
research team strongly recommends that any IIoT labeling standard include privacy-based elements
due to the unique circumstances surrounding equipment like smart meters, where the purchaser,
installer, owner, and user of the system may be different entities, each with their own concerns,
priorities, security models, and access controls.

Aside from the lack of privacy elements included in the NIST IoT baselines, the research team found
their recommended capability-based baselines to be adequate for application to IIoT.

Further, the research team recommends that application of baseline in IIoT be scoped by required
functionality of a whole product rather than by device, as some designs include multiple devices that,
when connected, have different capabilities, security controls, or enforcement parameters. To
consider only individual devices and not the products and services those devices rely on to
implement their security would unnecessarily disqualify several products and manufacturers from
achieving a Cyber Trust Mark.

IIoT Cyber Trust Guide
In addition to the Cyber Trust Mark, the research team recommends the FCC consider
implementation of a Cyber Trust Guide for IIoT. The Guide would be analogous to the EnergyGuide,
which complements the Energy Star label managed by the Environmental Protection Agency (EPA),
and be available for IIoT products that may not qualify for the binary Cyber Trust Mark due to the
unique challenges of IIoT contexts. The Cyber Trust Guide would facilitate broader participation in the

Research and Development Phase 1
Goals of Cybersecurity Labeling for IIoT Products
To understand the applicability of a U.S. cyber labeling program to the energy IIoT space, it is
essential to understand what a cybersecurity label aims to achieve and why. Through stakeholder
interviews, workshops, and review of existing research, the research team identified several goals for
an effective cybersecurity label for IIoT that aligns with the U.S. Trust Mark Program. The FCC
outlined their goals as encouraging vendor innovation and investment in product security and
improving transparent communication about security in the energy product marketplace. This report
investigates if, how, and by what means a cybersecurity label for IIoT could achieve the following
additional goals:

 1. Harmonize security expectations of a device across the consumer base.
 2. Drive development of capabilities to meet harmonized security expectations.
 3. Drive consumer demand and vendor competition.
 4. Increase accessibility of product specifications and simplify procurement processes.
 5. Inform configuration and installation decisions to maintain security.
 6. Increase transparency about cybersecurity practices.
 7. Inform consumer choice.
 8. Caution consumers about potential risks.
 9. Increase consumer awareness of security considerations, whether they are the end decision
 maker.

Preliminary Label Data Elements and Concept
Vendor Engagement
At the start of the research initiative, the research team contacted vendors who were identified as
leaders in smart meter and/or inverter production in U.S. markets. In Phase 1, the research team
received input from Enphase, Tesla, Schneider Electric, Solectria, and SolarEdge to inform the cyber
labeling project.

The team of Lab researchers consulted with policy and technology experts from each of the five
vendors through open-ended interviews and facilitated group workshops. As detailed in the following
sections, vendor partners provided insights and feedback on an early label mockup (see Early Label
Mockup Iteration and Stakeholder Feedback), a Survey of Standards, and multiple approaches to
claim verification. The research team concluded from this phase and early discussions that a label
for IIoT in the energy sector may be more effective and widely accepted if it takes an informational

approach, wherein a label would display disclosures about security and privacy measures rather than
an assessment or certification of security (e.g., rating or seal of approval).

Survey of Standards
The research team assessed over 20 global cyber labeling standards, recognized research reports,
and legislation pertaining to labeling, privacy, and security for IoT and IIoT. Policies and standards
from various countries were assessed to better understand the worldwide stance on IoT and IIoT
security. The research team concluded that no existing standard or labeling scheme, as is, would
adequately address privacy and security concerns applicable to energy sector IIoT technologies such
as smart meters and inverters.

At the time this research was conducted, there were several labeling programs around the world,
including in Germany, Finland, Singapore, and the United Kingdom. Singapore, for example, has
requirements to earn a tiered label, starting at Level 1 which requires compliance with three
provisions, and ending at Level 4 which requires compliance with a complete set of 13 provisions
and an accompanying penetration test. Finland and Germany both have a single requirement to
meet all 13 provisions. Finland requires an additional inspection to verify compliance, while Germany
requires only a self-declaration (see Figure 1.) 4

Early Label Mockup Iteration and Stakeholder Feedback
Initially, the multi-lab research team generated around 100 potential cyber label elements using the
standards identified through the survey of standards as a starting point. The research team first
sought to demonstrate alignment with the 13 provisions in ESTI-EN 303 645, to consider elements
other established labels require, and to identify additional elements that could demonstrate security,
privacy, or adherence to key standards.

Over the course of 11 internal working sessions, then followed by vendor discussions, the research
team selected a draft set of 47 discreet label elements, removing elements those were irrelevant to
the IIoT space, and answering the following operationalizing questions:

 • How do we describe this element?
 • What types of data could fill this field?
 • What function does the inclusion of this element fill?
 • Is it verifiable and/or immutable?
 • How could it be verified? By whom?
 • Is it applicable to smart meters and inverters?
 • Does it map to commonly used standards and best practices?
 • Who does the information provide value to?

Through this iterative process, the research team moved toward developing an information
disclosure approach for product specifications in a label mockup. Through this method, rather than
demonstrate compliance or make objective claims about the security of the product, the
manufacturer would disclose a product’s security and privacy features so that the consumer could
draw upon this disclosure to make informed decisions.

The 47 elements used in the resulting mockup can be mapped to 11 categories of information, as
described in the table below. A full list of these elements is represented in the label mockup in
Appendix A.

Through conversation with industry vendor partners, the research team determined that these fields
were valuable to consumers and stakeholders and would provide customers with additional
awareness of security considerations for these devices. After reviewing all disclosed information,
consumers could make decisions about their devices based on their risk appetite. Companies
purchasing these devices would be aware that customers would be looking for devices that have a
Cyber Trust Mark, so participation would incentivize information disclosure and inclusion of security
features and generate a more competitive market in the space.

To provide this information, a simple identifying sticker with a QR code would be displayed most
effectively on the back of the product, product box, or manual, to ensure recognizability and
accessibility. This label and logo should also be included as a digital thumbnail for online purchases.
This URL or QR code on this sticker could be scanned by purchasers or more advanced consumers to
access a more detailed information sheet that details the security measures and standards met by
the product manufacturer.

Responsibility for the distribution of labels would fall to the FCC or implementing body after the
producer or manufacturer has submitted a form with the necessary security and privacy information.
Once approved, the vendor would receive a notification that the label and accompanying information
sheet are available so the stickers could be downloaded and printed for distribution. The information
sheet would be hosted in a central repository and/or made available to the vendor to host on their
own product site. The redundancy of storing the information sheet associated with these labels in a
central repository managed by the implementing body could increase consistency, trustworthiness,
and accessibility for consumers. The ability to access all label versions from various vendors in one
place would simplify the process for consumers to compare products based on the provided cyber
labels.

Public Comment and Project Website
Vendor Data Input
After developing the draft mockup of label data elements, the research team sought vendor input to
validate the proposed content and highlight potential challenges. This collaboration allowed us to
gather early input validating the content of the original mockup, but a more detailed review is still

needed. Conversations with vendors were limited to hypotheticals in the absence of data sharing
agreements, making it difficult to reach a verifiable technical understanding of the types of data that
may be provided in a label, challenges associated with providing this information in a public
disclosure, considerations associated with claim verification, and mechanisms that could be used to
improve data standardization. The research team was unable to finalize data-sharing agreements
within the allotted time for this project. The research team strongly recommends that the
implementing body conduct additional research with data-sharing agreements in place wherein
vendors will populate real solar inverter and smart meter product data in the mockup data form.

Project Website
On August 1, 2024, Sandia National Laboratory stood up a project website 13 to showcase the early
mockups and research findings from this effort and provide a forum for public comment. Currently,
the website has three primary pages. The landing page contains an overview of Phase 1 and its
purpose, highlights the National Laboratories involved, creates a project artifact and outlines
research processes and high-level early findings. This landing page serves the important function of
providing the public with an official source of information about this high-profile project. The landing
page then directs consumers and vendors to opportunities to get involved by providing feedback.

Consumers reviewing the page are directed to a label mockup demonstrating the types of
information that could be included in an IIoT label and how that information may be displayed. This is
intended as a starting point for feedback and discussion; the mockup does not reflect the final
format or content of any actual label.

Vendors and manufacturers reviewing the website are directed to view and navigate through an
interactive label data entry mockup to engage with a proposed label creation process and
understand the types of information that would be required to produce the mockup referenced
above. Information entered in the sample form is not stored. This form serves only as an example,
intended as a starting point for initiate feedback and discussion; it is not reflective of the final format
or content of any actual label.

Although the project website was intended as a feedback mechanism to inform revision of the label
concept through Phase 2, the research team did not receive any additional substantive input on
these mockups in the project email or through the provided contact form. The website is still live and
available as a Phase 1 project artifact.

Research and Development Phase 2
National Institute of Standards & Technology (NIST)
Alignment
NIST Data Element Mapping
In parallel with the IIoT cyber labeling research effort, NIST was tasked to develop recommendations
for the FCC to implement IoT Cyber Labeling. Through this effort, building on years of prior research,
NIST published NIST-IR 8259A: A Core Device Cybersecurity Capability Baseline and NIST-IR 8259B:
IoT Non-Technical Supporting Capability Core Baseline. 14 These documents define technical and
non-technical cybersecurity baselines related to IoT devices and are recommended as the basis for a
binary label that may be granted upon demonstration of compliance with all baselines.

The research team conducted a mapping exercise comparing the NIST baselines and the security
elements independently developed by the research team during Phase 1 as an output of the Survey
of Standards. This mapping demonstrated strong alignment, with both sets of criteria emphasizing
similar indicators of secure processes. In the initial mapping, researchers identified that of our
proposed 47 draft elements, 29 mapped to a baseline in the NIST-IR 8259 series. This most
significantly excluded privacy considerations, data storage, access, and control elements which were
not seen in the NIST-IR 8259 series. Through bidirectional review and further discussion with NIST
representatives, the research team also identified the following NIST-IR capabilities that were not
covered in the research team’s initial set of proposed security elements:

NIST-IR 8259A
 • Device Identification – (Standard 2)
 • Device Configuration – (Standards 1, 2, 3)
 • Data Protection – (Standard 2)
 • Cybersecurity State Awareness – (Standard 1, 2, 3, 4, 5)
 • Software Update – (Standard 2)

NIST-IR 8259B
 • Documentation – (Standards 1, 2, 3.a, 3.c.)
 • Information and Query Reception – Standards (1, 2.)
 • Information Dissemination – (Standards 1.e, 1.h, 2.a, 2.b.)
 • Education and Awareness – (Standards 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6)

Through additional working sessions, the research team proposed additional elements that would
adequately meet the missing baselines for all provisions except NIST-IR 8259B Documentation
Standard 3.a and Information Dissemination Standard 1.h.

Cyber Trust Guide Concept
Through review of the NIST-IR 8259 baselines and conversations with NIST personnel, the research
team also flagged several critical challenges for a proposed labeling program when applied to the
energy sector IIoT. These challenges arise due to the context-dependent and highly variable nature of
security in industrial technology applications, such as:

 • OT equipment typically has multiple stakeholders, not all of which have the same definition
 of what it means to be secure.
 • OT control centers and products are accessible to operators with varying levels of expertise
 compared to standard IoT consumers.
 • OT security baselines may be challenging or even impossible to demonstrate compliance
 with, especially for legacy equipment, which is predominant in OT architectures.
 • OT noncompliance with security baselines may not indicate a lack of security.

Additionally, compared to IoT, consumer-facing IIoT is significantly more constrained with regard to
the digital environments in which it operates (e.g., smart meters do not typically use consumer Wi-Fi
networks).

These challenges raise the need for an alternative mechanism that IIoT product manufacturers and
suppliers could use to clearly communicate their security features even if they do not meet or cannot
demonstrate compliance with the NIST baselines.

Risk Analysis
To better inform implementation decisions, the research team investigated risks and challenges
related to recommended security elements and their presentation in a Cyber Trust Guide. This work
focused primarily on security risks associated with public disclosure of the data elements for IIoT
devices. The research also explored risks related to the use of QR codes and challenges consumers
and stakeholders may face in understanding the information in a Cyber Trust Guide. The risk analysis
findings and potential mitigations are discussed below.

Security Risks of Public Information Disclosure
The research team convened a group of cybersecurity experts for a tabletop red team exercise to
identify security risks associated with the disclosure of data elements for IIoT devices. A red team is
when researchers play the role of a cyber attacker in a fictional scenario. The participants were
asked to consider an instance in which an adversary or the public at large has gained access to
vendor product data demonstrating the product meets each of the technical capability baselines
contained in NIST IR 8259A. Red team participants were then asked to identify if and how an
adversary could use the data to compromise IIoT devices. The results of the exercise are
summarized in three key findings below.

1. Publicly Disclosed Baseline Information Might Aid an Adversary, or it Might Not
An exposure of accurate product information always creates potential risk, but the number of
variables and hypothetical scenarios make it impossible to draw definitive or universal conclusions.
The type and magnitude of that risk depends on the specific scenario, including characteristics of
both the attacker and target. The same information being exposed for two distinct devices on how
they meet the same cybersecurity baseline could lead to an exploitation of a vulnerability in one
device but not in the other, for example.

The red team did not identify any individual element, or combination of elements, they would
generally expect to significantly and directly risk the security of a device that has met the
requirements for certification via a Cyber Trust program. However, the research team did note many
elements that, if circumstances were right, could theoretically assist an adversary in planning an
attack—typically by narrowing the scope of potential attack vectors or highlighting device
characteristics that could make certain tactics or targets more attractive.

The following is a summary of these elements categorized by NIST baseline:

Device Identification
 • Depending on the exact data disclosed, information about identifiers (e.g., serial numbers)
 may aid an adversary in narrowing the scope of potential targets by providing information on
 the role or category of a device within a larger network. There may be other significant risks if
 the unique identifiers are used as a source for keying cryptographic ciphers. The risk may be
 increased if the physical and logical identifiers are represented by the same value.

Device Configuration
 • If justifications indicate a device can be configured remotely, this highlights a potential
 pathway for adversary access and could make the device a more attractive target compared
 to a device that does not offer remote configuration.
 • If justifications include information about how an authorized user is defined or what their
 user permissions allow (e.g., elevated privileges, ability to manipulate device configuration),
 that could assist adversaries in identifying authorized users to target. However, this type of
 information may be available in a user manual, meaning that the risk of disclosing this
 information is not unique to the Cyber Trust Guide.

Data Protection
 • Generally, information regarding which cryptographic systems a device uses should not pose
 a high risk of adversary exploitation. However, an adversary could potentially use that
 information to narrow their search for the key to a particular type.

Logical Access to Interfaces
 • The first element (“The ability to logically or physically disable any local and network
 interfaces that are not necessary for the core functionality of the device”) does not specify
 that this ability is restricted to authorized entities. Thus, a vendor could meet this
 requirement, while also leaving open potential access points that adversaries could take
 advantage of.

Software Update
 • Data indicating that a device meets the requirements for the third element (“The ability for
 authorized entities to roll back updated software to a previous version”) could alert an

DRAFT - PENDING APPROVAL Page 19 of 53

 adversary to the ability to roll a device back to a firmware version that has a known
 vulnerability.
 • Data indicating a device meets the requirements for the sixth element (“Configuration
 settings for use with the Device Configuration capability including, but not limited to: a. The
 ability to configure any remote update mechanisms to be either automatically or manually
 initiated for update downloads and installations,” and “b. The ability to enable or disable
 notification when an update is available and specify who or what is to be notified”) could
 inform an adversary that notifications could be disabled or switched to manual to cause a
 device to miss an important security update.

Cybersecurity State Awareness
 • As discussed further below, the red team noted that to meet the requirements for the
 “Cybersecurity State Awareness” baseline, the manufacturer of a device would be
 responsible for defining the expected cybersecurity state. If that definition is trivial, the
 fulfilment of the baseline may be meaningless.
 • Depending on the information disclosed, the information associated with this baseline may
 or may not explicitly tell an adversary how a “normal” cybersecurity state is defined. Even if it
 does not, it alerts them to the fact that this is defined and, if the adversary can discover
 those parameters via other means, that information would allow them to know what they are
 able to do in the system with low risk of detection.
 • The red team identified multiple risks associated with element five (“The ability to make the
 state information available to a service on another device, such as an event/state log
 server”):
 o This element does not specify that the communications between the device and the
 other device/server must be sent in a secure manner. An affirmative answer to this
 element could reveal a potential vulnerability; if the communication channels are not
 secure, adversaries could send spoofed information to the server indicating that the
 cybersecurity state is normal when it is not. An affirmative answer could also indicate
 a potential channel for gaining system information. For example, an adversary could
 intentionally induce logging by providing incorrect credentials and then, knowing that
 this information is being sent to the log server, observe the traffic using a packet
 analyzer to obtain information about the characteristics of those communications
 (such as a sequence number) to use in later exploits.
 o An affirmative answer to this element can also alert an adversary that the device is
 communicating out to another device/server. Depending on the configuration of the
 network, this could effectively expand the attack surface and open new
 vulnerabilities for exploitation that might be present in the secondary device/server.

Although the red team focused on the technical baselines contained in NIST IR 8259A, analysts also
noted several risks associated with the non-technical baselines contained in NIST IR 8259B. The
information that a vendor would provide to justify meeting many of these baseline elements would
already be in the public domain, as the baselines focus on documentation, information
dissemination, and education, among other topics. However, several baselines could pose additional
risks, including:

Documentation
 • The justification for Element 3.b (“3) Document device design and support considerations
 related to the IoT device, such as: …b. Protection of software and hardware components of
 the IoT device (e.g., secure boot, hardware root of trust, and secure enclave)”) may reveal

 information about boot mechanisms that could assist an adversary in identifying a vector for
 inserting malicious code into a device.
 • The justification for Element 3.d (“3) Document device design and support considerations
 related to the IoT device, such as: …d. Secure software development and supply chain
 practices used”) may reveal information that could assist an adversary in planning a supply
 chain attack.
 • The justification for Element 1.c (“Document assumptions made during the development

 • Depending on the information provided, the justification for Element 1.b (“The procedures to

To mitigate these element-specific risks, the research team carefully considered how much data to
disclose in the Cyber Trust Guide. While some baseline-related data is already contained in
documents such as product packaging or instruction manuals—which minimizes the additional risk of
including that data in the Cyber Trust Guide—other data could provide adversaries with new
information, which could aid in target selection or attack planning. As discussed above, for some
elements, disclosing detailed information regarding how a device meets a requirement poses a risk.
For other elements, even disclosing whether or not the device meets a requirement could provide an
adversary with useful information. The research team weighed these considerations in the design of
the Cyber Trust Guide—tailoring the presentation of each element to balance information disclosure
with risk mitigation.

2. Failure to Meet a Baseline Could Indicate a Vulnerability, or Not
As discussed above, the information provided by the vendor could prove useful to a potential
adversary. However, the red team also noted that the absence of information, or a negative answer
to a particular element, could similarly prove valuable. The knowledge that a particular device does
not meet a baseline could indicate a vulnerability that an adversary could potentially exploit. For
example, if a Cyber Trust Guide indicates that a device does not have the ability to restrict
configuration changes to authorized entities only (Element 2 under the “Device Cybersecurity
Capability” baseline in NISTIR 8259A), this could suggest a potential attack vector.

However, it could be that a device does not meet the Element 2 baseline capability because it does
not allow for configuration changes at all, regardless of authorization. That would not suggest the
same potential attack vector and provides different overall risk in disclosure.

The red team also observed that some of the elements identified in the NIST baselines may not
apply to all devices, depending on their type, design, and capabilities. For example, Element 1 listed
under the “Logical Access to Interfaces” baseline states that a device must have the “ability to
logically or physically disable any local and network interfaces that are not necessary for the core
functionality of the device.” If all interfaces are necessary for a device’s core functionality, this

DRAFT - PENDING APPROVAL Page 21 of 53

element does not apply. While not a security risk, per se, both an affirmative and negative answer to
this element in a Cyber Trust Guide would be misleading. To mitigate this risk, the research team
recommends allowing a vendor to provide a “Not Applicable” answer (with justification) to an
element, while empowering the verification auditor/review board to determine whether such an
answer is appropriate for a given product. This concept is not represented in the Cyber Trust Guide
mockup in Appendix B because the additional research to address the nuance introduced by this
concept fell outside the timeline and scope for this report.

3. Security Depends on Implementation Even When Baselines are Met
The red team frequently noted that the level of security provided by compliance with the baseline
elements is dependent on how they are implemented within specific devices. Because the baselines
do not provide detailed implementation requirements, the red team identified ways in which many
elements could technically be fulfilled, while still leaving major security gaps. These implementation
issues risk undermining how meaningful the Cyber Trust Mark, or any affirmative answers in a Cyber
Trust Guide, would be—potentially causing consumers to have a false sense of security.

For example, in Element 2 listed under the “Software Update” baseline (“The ability to verify and
authenticate any update before installing it”), the authentication protocol for an update could be
trivial (e.g. the filename starts with a particular word), which would technically meet the element
requirements but would not provide a high level of security. In another example, the red team noted
that to meet the requirements for the “Cybersecurity State Awareness” baseline the manufacturer of
a device would be responsible for defining the expected cybersecurity state. If that definition is
trivial—e.g. if it counts login attempts but does not compare them to realistic information about how
many accounts should have access, how many accounts are active, or how often accounts are
logging in—the fulfilment of the baseline may be meaningless.

Additionally, the red team noted that wording issues could lead to loopholes that could cause
baselines to be successfully, but trivially fulfilled. For example, Element 1 listed under the “Logical
Access to Interfaces” baseline (“The ability to logically or physically disable any local and network
interfaces that are not necessary for the core functionality of the device”) does not specify that this
ability is restricted to authorized entities. While this may be intentional, a vendor could meet this
requirement, while also leaving open potential access points that could be exploited by adversaries.
Ambiguous wording may not only lead to security risks but could also lead to inconsistent
interpretations and answers among various vendors.

There are several actions that could assist in mitigating these risks. First, the implementer of a Cyber
Trust Guide or Cyber Trust Mark should clearly state that fulfillment of any or all baselines does not
guarantee security. This message should be prominently displayed and repeated during any rollout
activities targeted at the public. Second, the wording of any vendor-facing requirement documents or
questionnaires should be carefully considered and vetted not only with vendors but with
cybersecurity experts to identify potential loopholes and limit scenarios in which baselines could be
trivially met. Finally, the role of the third-party auditor should be carefully defined—allowing them
discretion to reject vendor justifications that may meet the letter, but not the spirit of the
requirement.

QR Code Risks
As stated in the Early Label Mockup and Feedback and Cyber Trust Guide Concept sections, the
research team proposed using QR codes on product packaging to assist consumers in accessing
Cyber Trust information. The risk analysis team researched QR code-associated risks to ensure a full
understanding of how an adversary might exploit QR codes and how a Cyber Trust program could
mitigate those risks.

Adversaries could exploit the use of QR codes by:

 • Tampering with or replacing legitimate QR codes on devices or packaging with malicious
 codes
 • Leveraging consumer knowledge that the Cyber Trust Mark/Cyber Trust Guide programs use
 QRs and placing fake Cyber Trust codes in strategic locations
 • Sending fraudulent QR codes to consumers via email
 • Exploiting bugs in a QR code reader or in legitimate URLs

Adversaries could use these tactics to deploy malware on consumers’ phones or conduct phishing
attacks. These activities risk not only undermining consumer trust in the program, but—particularly if
energy sector consumers have used their organization-provided devices to scan the code—could
serve as a foothold on energy sector networks or a source of information to facilitate future attacks.

In addition to security risks, the use of QR codes also pose a utilization risk. According to a 2019
survey on consumer IoT security labeling, “participants are unlikely to scan QR Codes to check
product security features, with 48% reporting they would be unlikely to do this. Just over half (54%)
of consumers reportedly know how to scan QR Codes using their mobile device, with 43% having
done it at least once in the past year. Around 57% of consumers have never scanned a QR code, and
those who have, do infrequently (less than twice per year on average) according to the survey
results.” 18

To mitigate these risks, program implementers should consider the following if they plan to use QR
codes:

 • Use a reputable and secure QR code generator, which offers features such as:
 o Data encryption via HTTPS during the generation process
 o Strong access controls (e.g. multi-factor authentication, a single sign-on login option)
 o Security certifications such as SOC2
 o A privacy policy that complies with regulations such as the European Union’s General
 Data Protection Regulation or the California Consumer Privacy Act
 o Data retention and handling policies that preclude sharing/selling data without
 consent
 o Regular security audits
 • Customize the QR code to create a more complex image and include program-specific
 branding to make it more difficult for an adversary to modify it without detection.
 • Test the QR code before distribution to ensure it does not contain bugs or other errors.

Challenges in Consumer/Stakeholder Understanding
As discussed in the introduction, IoT has consumer properties that are unique from that of IoT.
Typically, utilities, integrators, or installers—not homeowners—are making purchasing decisions on
behalf of their organizations. This reduces the risk of challenges consumers may face in
understanding the information in a Cyber Trust Guide—as the buyers are likely to have more
familiarity with cybersecurity requirements. That said, to meet the goals of the program, an IIoT
cybersecurity label and/or guide should strive to be accessible to the largest possible audience.

A 2023 survey by Pew Research found that Americans’ understanding of digital topics varies notably
depending on the subject. For example, while 87% of U.S adults can correctly identify which
password (out of four choices) is the most secure option, only 48% can correctly identify an example
of two-factor authentication from a series of pictures. 19 In another example, a 2022 survey of 2,000
U.S. adults conducted by OnePoll on behalf of AT&T found that “while 70% of people feel
knowledgeable about cybersecurity,” only 39% know that suspicious sites can spread malware and
viruses in their computer, and more than half (54%) were unaware of the difference between active
and passive cybersecurity threats.” 20

To help ensure that even customers with limited cybersecurity knowledge can find value in the
program’s efforts, the research team recommends pairing the Cyber Trust Guide, with a simpler label
such as the Cyber Trust Mark. This would allow more knowledgeable customers (such as those
typically purchasing inverters and smart meters) to reap the benefit of access to more detailed
information, while providing an easy-to-understand resource (in the Cyber Trust Mark) to customers
with less cybersecurity knowledge. In addition, these guides should be made available at varying
levels of detail, including a sticker demonstrating participation, a condensed view demonstrating
high level compliance metrics, and a detailed view detailing all information about the product’s
security and privacy features. See Appendix B for a mockup of these views.

Claim Verification
Label claim verification is the process of demonstrating that the claims made by a product vendor
(and the data provided to back up these claims) who has or is seeking to obtain a cyber label are
accurate and truthful, which can include verifying that the vendor is following listed security practices
and regulatory standards. Consumers need this assurance that the information provided by these
vendors is accurate in order to make informed choices and increase their understanding of potential
security considerations. Without verification, the claims could all be falsified to make the product
more appealing, leading to potential risks and damage to the consumer. However, it is important to
note that claim verification is not intended to demonstrate the effectiveness or security of any given
implementation nor to ensure compliance of best practice measures or regulatory requirements.
Ultimately, claim validation is necessary for a cybersecurity label or guide to be meaningful.

The research team defines four claim verification options based around three tiers of information,
with the level of expertise required for verification increasing at each tier. This section includes
details about each method—including restraints and considerations for determining applicability—to
assist with decision making on which verification methods would be required for different elements.
Considerations related to costs, vendor collaboration measures, and label changes, are also further
described below.

This process was developed with the original label mockup in mind, and further investigation will be
needed to better assess how these concepts map to a Cyber Trust Mark based on the NIST baselines
and/or a Cyber Trust Guide. Discussion with verifying the information provided by a company would
apply to new label versions as well.

Tiered Verification Levels
Verifying each data field will vary in difficulty and complexity and requires various levels of expertise
for validation. Three testing levels have been defined and can be assigned to each element in the
early label mockup, as listed below. The research team recommends that the implementing body
consider how similar levels could be applied to the final Cyber Trust Mark or Guide baselines for
verification.

 • Level 1: The lowest level of expertise required. All level 1 claims can be verified by a
 layperson and do not require detailed knowledge of the subject. Examples of level 1
 verification elements include “Product Name and Model Number,” “Product Description,”
 and “Defined Support Period.”
 • Level 2: Requires some expertise in security. All level 2 claims can be verified by any industry
 professional. Examples of verification level 2 elements include “Authorization Method,” “Final
 Assembly Period,” and “System Access Methods.”
 • Level 3: The highest, and most challenging verification level. All level 3 claims require a
 subject matter expert or domain specialist. Examples of verification level 3 elements include
 “Protection of Data at Rest,” “Offline Functionality,” and “Secure Boot Mechanisms.”

The research team investigated several questions associated with verification levels, and
recommends further research be conducted by the implementing body along the same lines:

 • Can one verifying entity conduct verification at all levels?
 • What limitations may be associated with information at each level related to the potential
 verification processes described below?
 • Should a tiered verification approach be considered?

Verification Process Options
The research team came up with four potential options to successfully conduct claim verification.
These options could be implemented individually, or in combination, building on each other’s core
ideas.

Verification Prior to Label Distribution
Completing the verification process prior to label approval allows the implementing body to validate
information after the vendor applies for and submits label information for publication. Once the
information attested is proven factual, the label is returned for release. This option would allow the
label to be hosted in a government-maintained central repository. This option would create a
universal standard for U.S. vendors, and a more reliable labeling program, while reducing the
likelihood of false information or manipulated facts to consumers. However, this approach adds
more burden to updating the label in the future, as it contains extra steps in the publication process.
Given the ever-changing nature of security features and frequency of updates in IIoT products, this
would be a significant challenge, especially when verifying more complex and time-consuming level
three data fields. Clear guidance would be needed regarding the types of changes that would require
full label renewal, secondary verification, or information change processes.

Subject to Verification by Government Audit
This method allows a vendor to receive a label immediately upon application for the label and self-
attestation of compliance or accuracy. The vendor is required to attest that the information
submitted is accurate and acknowledge that information submitted may be subject to government
audit at any time. Elements of the label that are dynamic and subject to change should be identified
at the original time of registry, as those may receive more frequent audits. This process allows
companies to make minor changes to the label as needed more easily and is more efficient due to
less reliance on additional parties. However, this process is less reliable than pre-validation, as
vendors would receive a label before their information is audited and may have submitted false or
misleading information. This process lacks a systemic approach to verifying labels. This option could
be modified to reduce risk of falsification by requiring pre-verification of level one information, which
could be verified quickly and at low cost, and allowing level 2 and 3 information to be self-attested
and spot-checked.

Subject to Verification by Community Review
Like verification by government review, this method allows for a vendor to receive a label
immediately following application. The vendor is required to attest that the information submitted is
accurate and acknowledge that the information submitted will be made publicly available and may
be subject to audit at any time. This option could be modified to reduce risk of falsification by
requiring pre-verification of level one information, which could be verified quickly and at low cost --
allowing level 2 and 3 information to be self-attested and spot-checked. Rather than requiring the
government to be the sole adjudicator for spot-checking, claims would be verified by a community
review board consisting of level 2 and level 3 verification subject matter experts. This process would
foster community buy-in and transparency. However, this process has some challenges, including
potential introduction of biased spot checking (i.e., when community participants who have a specific
interest in a given organization participate in or conduct verification of that organization’s product).
Additional definitions and boundary requirements would also be needed to ensure verifiers are using
the same criteria. Vendor reputation would also need to be protected.

Self-Verified Elements for Added Recognition
The self-verification process involves a vendor immediately receiving a label upon application and
taking on the responsibility for sourcing and funding any verification processes for added recognition
and credibility, which would be noted on the label. This could be done on a tiered basis or an all-or-
nothing basis. This process does not require additional government entities or funding, and it allows
greater flexibility for companies to take ownership over the verification process. Vendor participation
in the verification process reduces the likelihood of reputational damage and could result in more
volunteer participation. However, this self-verification method could result in few labels undergoing
verification and is dependent on the trust that vendors choose credible and unbiased verifying
entities. As such, self-verification may require greater guidance and requirements.

Input from Verifier
The research team conducted several feedback sessions with Underwriter Laboratories (UL), a
volunteer representative organization involved in claim verification. These sessions aimed to validate
the approaches listed above, identify which claim verification option was optimal and most feasible,
and identify any elements in the proposed Cyber Trust Guide which may pose significant challenges
to an implementing body attempting to conduct claim verification. Claim verification challenges and
considerations that came up in discussions with UL are outlined below.

Time Needed for Verification
The UL representatives noted that the claim verification process can be time consuming for certain
claim types and that faster alternatives are necessary to avoid vendors becoming discouraged and
seeking out less robust/less accurate verification methods. Among the most challenging claims that
UL handles are claims dealing directly with product security, especially how to verify whether the
product meets or exceeds security related to encryption. In their experience, verifying these claims is
a time- and effort-intensive process that most vendors are averse to. Additionally, manufacturers and
vendors often lack a mature cybersecurity process, which can further delay claim verification.

Measuring Against Standards
UL also emphasized the need to balance elements of the cybersecurity label with those included in
existing cybersecurity standards. Three industry standards that the UL team recommended are:

 • The Common Criteria for Information Technology Security Evaluation (ISO/IEC 15408) 21
 • UL’s 2900 Series of Standards 22
    •   ISA/IEC 62443 23

UL representatives recommended these standards and confirmed that given manufacturing and
vendors adherence to these standards will ultimately achieve many of the desired claims verification
goals. Another method that could be used is claim verification standards that can be tracked through
audit-based questions (e.g., has the IIoT product met a certain standard threshold).

However, because most manufacturers and vendors in the IIoT space do not yet have a mature

the more rigorous criteria in standards. For example, the ENERGY STAR program is a widely

Ultimately, a feasible approach for claim verification will likely strike a balance between active and
constant review by a governing body and conformance determination, based on a voluntary audit-
based approach that provides for some self-verification and governance. Attempting to find definitive
answers to whether IIoT devices are “secure,” however, is not feasible, as security will always be a
moving target.

Out-of-Scope Considerations
One consideration that is outside the scope of the team’s research and recommendations, but still
worth consideration relates to the time elapsed between when a consumer requests access to data
and when the vendor delivers said data. In discussions with vendor partners on the subject, they
stated that there were certain elements which they would not disclose publicly but would provide
upon request, potentially requiring NDAs or other agreements that would take time to put into place.
Currently, some of the research team’s recommended elements are phrased to ask, “Is [data
element] available upon request?” with the understanding that an answer in the affirmative means
the data will be provided to the consumer requesting it. However, the claim verification process
should also consider whether to include a measurable deadline for vendors to provide this data to
consumers who request it. Without being held to a deadline, vendors may never send the data being
requested or may send it past the point which the data would have been used to aid a decision-
making process.

In addition, further investigation is needed to assess what independent testers can do with or
without vendor support. Independent testers would need to understand how to comply with existing
standards and IoT variations and know what constitutes conformance or non-conformance to cyber
labeling standards given the vast differences between vendor products. Given consumer demands, it
will be critical for independent testers to work closely with the vendor in a way where the vendor will
be willing to cooperate. This necessitates a simplified and universally understood claims verification
process conducted by independent testers given clearly defined rules and thresholds.

Verification challenges will also be defined by the final form of the Cyber Trust Mark. If the cyber
label is issued based on compliance criteria (such as by compliance with the NIST 8259 baselines),
verification will be simpler but may be required more frequently. If a cyber label, or Cyber Trust
Guide, is issued in association with a disclosure of information rather than compliance, the process
has more room to be dynamic, but verification would also need to take on a more dynamic form.

The research team also recommends that the implementing body consider how cost, both for the
government and participating parties, will impact implementation of a cybersecurity labeling
program.
