Machine learning–based prediction of paracetamol solubility and CO₂ density in supercritical systems using artificial rabbits optimization
Authors: Hadil Faris Alotaibi, Arwa Omar Al Khatib, Junainah Abd Hamid, Subbulakshmi Ganesan, Aman Shankhyan, Rajashree Panigrahi, Fadhil Faez Sead, Aashna Sinha
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe accurate prediction of solubility and solvent properties in supercritical CO₂ systems remains a critical challenge in pharmaceutical process design due to the nonlinear and coupled effects of temperature and pressure. This study proposes a novel artificial intelligence-based modeling framework for predicting solvent density and paracetamol mole fraction under supercritical conditions using temperature and pressure as input variables. Three regression models—Multilayer Perceptron (MLP), Support Vector Regression (SVR), and Tweedie Regression (TDR)—were developed and systematically optimized using the Artificial Rabbits Optimization (ARO) algorithm. Unlike conventional single-model studies, this study provides a comparative and optimization-driven evaluation of both nonlinear machine learning models and statistically grounded regression methods under identical conditions. The results demonstrate that the ARO-optimized MLP model achieves superior predictive performance for both solvent density (R² = 0.99898) and mole fraction (R² = 0.96555), outperforming SVR and TDR models across all evaluation metrics. The study further reveals clear nonlinear dependencies of solubility and density on pressure and temperature, which are effectively captured through data-driven modeling and visualized via contour-based response surfaces. The main innovation of this work lies in the integration of a metaheuristic optimization strategy (ARO) with multiple regression paradigms to establish a unified and systematic framework for supercritical solubility prediction. This approach provides both high predictive accuracy and interpretable process insights, supporting early-stage optimization of pharmaceutical manufacturing in supercritical CO₂ environments.Keywords: Process modeling, Drug delivery, Tweedie Regression, Drug solubility, Machine learningSubject terms: Chemistry, Drug discovery, Engineering, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

The development of efficient and reliable pharmaceutical manufacturing processes remains a major challenge, particularly for active pharmaceutical ingredients (APIs) with low aqueous solubility1,2. Poor solubility often leads to reduced bioavailability and necessitates higher drug dosages, which may increase the risk of side effects. Consequently, there is a growing need for advanced techniques that can enhance solubility and enable precise control of drug properties during manufacturing3–5.

Supercritical fluid technology, particularly using carbon dioxide (CO₂), has emerged as a promising approach for improving drug solubility and particle size reduction. Supercritical CO₂ offers several advantages, including low toxicity, mild critical conditions, and tunable solvent properties through pressure and temperature adjustments6–8. These characteristics make it highly suitable for pharmaceutical applications, especially in the production of nanosized drug particles. However, accurate estimation of solubility under varying thermodynamic conditions remains a critical requirement for process design and optimization9.

Traditional thermodynamic models have been widely used to estimate solubility in supercritical systems. While these models provide valuable insights, they often rely on simplifying assumptions and may struggle to capture complex nonlinear relationships between process variables10,11. In contrast, machine learning (ML) techniques have demonstrated strong capabilities in modeling nonlinear systems without requiring explicit physical assumptions, making them attractive alternatives for solubility prediction12,13.

Among ML approaches, regression-based models such as Multilayer Perceptron (MLP) and Support Vector Regression (SVR) have been extensively applied in chemical and pharmaceutical engineering due to their flexibility and predictive accuracy. MLP, a class of artificial neural networks, is particularly effective in capturing complex nonlinear mappings, while SVR is known for its robustness in handling small datasets and high-dimensional feature spaces14.

In addition to these models, this study explores the use of Tweedie Regression (TDR), a member of the generalized linear model family. The Tweedie distribution is particularly suitable for modeling continuous, non-negative data with skewed distributions and mean–variance relationships15,16. Such characteristics are often observed in physicochemical properties, including solubility data. Therefore, TDR provides a statistically grounded framework that complements nonlinear ML models by offering interpretability and suitability for specific data distributions17.

Despite the growing application of ML in pharmaceutical process modeling, there remains a need for systematic comparison of different regression approaches under identical conditions. Furthermore, the integration of metaheuristic optimization algorithms can significantly enhance model performance by tuning hyperparameters effectively. In this context, the Artificial Rabbits Optimization (ARO) algorithm is employed in this study to optimize model parameters and improve predictive accuracy.

Accordingly, the main objective of this work is to develop and compare ARO-optimized MLP, SVR, and TDR models for predicting solvent density and drug solubility (mole fraction) of paracetamol in supercritical CO₂ as functions of temperature and pressure. By evaluating the strengths and limitations of these models, this study aims to provide insights into the applicability of data-driven approaches for pharmaceutical process optimization.

The novelty of this study lies in the development of a unified and systematically optimized machine learning framework for predicting both solvent density and paracetamol solubility in supercritical CO₂ systems. Unlike previous studies focusing on single-model evaluations, this work integrates multiple regression paradigms (MLP, SVR, and Tweedie Regression) under a consistent evaluation framework and enhances their performance using Artificial Rabbits Optimization (ARO). Additionally, the study provides interpretable process insights through response surface analysis, linking data-driven predictions to thermodynamic behavior in supercritical systems.

To mitigate the risk of overfitting in this study, the dataset was split into a 90/10 train/test ratio, with 90% of the data used for model training and 10% reserved for testing. This ensures that model performance is evaluated on an unseen portion of the data. While the small dataset size limits the ability to fully assess generalization, the use of a train/test split provides an initial check against overfitting. Additionally, although k-fold cross-validation was not applied due to the small dataset size, it will be considered in future work with larger datasets. Confidence intervals and further validation techniques will also be explored in future research to quantify prediction uncertainty more comprehensively.

Methodology

Artificial Rabbits Optimization (ARO)

ARO is a recently proposed meta-heuristic optimization algorithm that is based on the social behavior and movement patterns of rabbits in the wild22. The ARO algorithm is inspired by the social behavior and movement patterns of rabbits in the wild, where rabbits move around in groups and search for food while avoiding predators. The algorithm consists of a population of artificial rabbits, which move around the solution space and search for the optimal solution to the given optimization problem. The algorithm uses a set of rules that govern the movement and interaction of the rabbits, which are derived from the social behavior of rabbits in the wild.

The movement of the rabbits in the ARO algorithm is governed by two key rules: the attraction rule and the repulsion rule. The attraction rule is used to guide the rabbits towards promising regions of the solution space, while the repulsion rule is used to prevent the rabbits from getting stuck in local optima. Specifically, the attraction rule is used to calculate the direction of movement of each rabbit towards the best solution found so far, while the repulsion rule is used to calculate the direction of movement of each rabbit away from the other rabbits in the population.

The ARO algorithm also includes a set of adaptation rules, which are used to dynamically adjust the parameters of the algorithm during the search process. The adaptation rules are inspired by the social behavior of rabbits in the wild, where rabbits adapt their behavior to changing environmental conditions23. The ARO algorithm has been shown to be effective on a wide range of optimization problems, including benchmark functions, engineering design problems, and data clustering problems. The algorithm has also been compared against other meta-heuristic optimization algorithms, such as GA, PSO, ABC, and DE, and has been shown to perform favorably in terms of convergence speed and solution quality.

In summary, Artificial Rabbits Optimization is a recently proposed meta-heuristic optimization algorithm that is based on the social behavior and movement patterns of rabbits in the wild. The algorithm uses a set of rules that govern the movement and interaction of a population of artificial rabbits and includes adaptation rules that are used to dynamically adjust the parameters of the algorithm during the search process.

Support Vector Machine (SVM)

Support Vector Regression (SVR) is a model that is particularly well-suited for regression tasks involving finite data24,25. The SVR model has the ability to analyze data for regression and classification tasks. To ensure an adequate fit, SVR utilizes a hyperplane, which is a straight line in the support vector regression. Kernel functions are used to transform the input parameters into a higher-dimensional space, allowing for the handling of nonlinear problems through feature mapping in a linear context. Multilayer Perceptron (MLP)

Various types of artificial neural networks (ANN) employ feed-forward connections in their processes, of which the Multilayer Perceptron (MLP) is one such network that uses this design. These perceptrons consist of activation functions and weights relevant to them. The main objective of this model is to achieve optimal precision by estimating the weights from the given data14,27,28.

The procedure of MLP can be broken down into the following 5 stages28–31:

1. The initialization of input data and the utilization of small random weights are recommended.

2. Calculate the result of the neural network.

3. Perform the cost/error calculations.

4. Revise the weights through the implementation of backpropagation to account for the error.

5. Repeat No. 2 and 3 until the error gets negligible.

MLPs are considered effective methods of correlating datasets due to their ability to deal with various types of data32,33.

Model settings and parameters

The MLP model was implemented with a two-layer structure, each containing 50 neurons. The activation function used for the hidden layers was ReLU, and the output layer employed a linear activation function. The SVR model utilized the Radial Basis Function (RBF) kernel for nonlinear regression. The ARO algorithm was applied with a population size of 50 and 100 iterations. The optimization bounds were selected based on prior studies and the specific conditions of the system. The TDR model was configured with a p-value of 1.5, which was based on previous works in the field of solubility modeling. No normalization was applied to the input variables (temperature and pressure) as they were within similar physical ranges, and the models employed (SVR and MLP) were capable of handling this without further preprocessing. However, future work may investigate the impact of normalization on model performance.

Choice of optimization algorithm

In this study, the ARO algorithm was selected due to its ability to efficiently balance exploration and exploitation in high-dimensional search spaces. Unlike Grid Search, which is computationally expensive for high-dimensional problems, ARO provides a more efficient approach by simulating natural animal behaviors. This allows ARO to avoid local optima and converge to a global solution more effectively. Bayesian Optimization and Random Search, while commonly used in optimization tasks, were not selected for this study due to their limitations in handling the complexity of the problem. Bayesian Optimization requires predefined priors and careful parameter tuning, which was not feasible with the limited dataset, while Random Search lacks adaptive exploitation of the search space. An ablation study comparing ARO with these other optimization methods was not conducted in this work due to resource limitations, but we plan to include this in future studies for a more comprehensive evaluation of ARO’s effectiveness relative to other approaches.

Results and discussion

In this study, two separate regression modeling tasks were defined: one for predicting solvent density and another for predicting mole fraction (paracetamol solubility). For each task, independent models were developed and optimized using the same input variables (temperature and pressure). This approach was adopted because the two response variables represent different physical properties with distinct behaviors. Modeling them separately allows each model to better capture the underlying relationships without introducing additional complexity associated with multi-output regression.

As mentioned earlier, the ARO algorithm was used to optimize models for predicting the density of solvent and mole fraction of paracetamol drug based on its temperature and pressure. Three different models were used: MLP, SVR, and TDR. In this section, the accuracy of optimized ML models are assessed and compared using several statistical metrics to judge the performance of each model.

The superior performance of the ARO-MLP model can be attributed to its ability to capture nonlinear relationships between temperature, pressure, and the response variables. In supercritical CO₂ systems, both solvent density and solubility are governed by complex thermodynamic interactions, leading to inherently nonlinear behavior. The flexible architecture of MLP allows it to approximate such nonlinear mappings more effectively than regression-based approaches. Although SVR is capable of modeling nonlinear relationships through kernel functions, its performance is highly dependent on kernel selection and parameter tuning. On the other hand, TDR, as a generalized linear model, has limited flexibility in capturing complex nonlinear patterns. It should be noted, however, that the dataset used in this study is relatively small, which may increase the risk of overfitting, particularly for flexible models such as MLP. Therefore, while ARO-MLP demonstrates superior performance, the results should be interpreted with caution, and further validation using larger datasets is recommended.

Overall, the results indicate that ARO-MLP is the most suitable model for predicting both the density and mole fraction of paracetamol drug based on its temperature and pressure. However, it is important to note that further validation of the models using external datasets is required to confirm their reliability and generalizability. Fitting charts of Figs. 1 and 2 that are comparison of predicted and actual values of density and mole fractions (using ARO-MLP) confirm this fact. As observed, great agreement has been obtained for the measured paracetamol solubility values and the estimated ones using ARO-MLP model. Such great agreement stems from the learning nature of the models as well as the efficiency of optimizer used in this study and is way better than the predictions by thermodynamic models for drug solubility in supercritical CO2 solvent. The previous research using ML models indicated some similar behavior for the same drug compound in SC-CO218–20.

While Figs. 3, 4, 5 and 6 show general trends of density and mole fraction with temperature and pressure, a deeper interpretation can be provided based on the thermodynamic behavior of supercritical CO₂. Near the critical region of CO₂, small variations in temperature and pressure can lead to significant changes in solvent density. This sensitivity explains the strong dependence of density on pressure observed in the results. As pressure increases, the fluid becomes more compressed, leading to higher density and enhanced solvent power. The increase in solubility (mole fraction) with pressure can be attributed to the improved solvent power of supercritical CO₂ at higher densities. In supercritical systems, solvent strength is closely related to density; therefore, higher pressure conditions promote greater solute–solvent interactions and increased solubility. The effect of temperature is more complex and can be explained through the concept of crossover pressure. At lower pressures, increasing temperature tends to reduce density, thereby decreasing solvent power and solubility. However, at higher pressures, the vapor pressure of the solute becomes dominant, and solubility may increase with temperature. This competing behavior is characteristic of retrograde solubility in supercritical fluids. Although the dataset used in this study does not explicitly define the crossover pressure point, the observed trends suggest the presence of such thermodynamic behavior. These findings demonstrate that the machine learning models are capable of capturing not only statistical relationships but also physically meaningful patterns in supercritical systems18,19.

Also, density and mole fraction are three-dimensionally displayed as two-variable functions of temperature and pressure in Figs. 7 and 8. One can find the influence of both parameters simultaneously on the response variables which are captured using the ML model. Song et al. reported similar 3D results for paracetamol solubility data which can confirm our ML results19,20.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study demonstrates the application of artificial intelligence techniques in predicting the solvent density and mole fraction of paracetamol drug at different temperatures and pressures. The results show that Multilayer Perceptron (MLP) outperformed Support Vector Machine (SVM) and Tweedie Regression (TDR) models in predicting both output variables. For predicting the density, MLP achieved an R-squared value of 0.99898 and an MSE of 5.8163E + 01, while for predicting the mole fraction, MLP had the highest R-squared value of 0.96555 and the lowest MSE of 6.8234E-14. These findings can provide valuable insights to the pharmaceutical industry for optimizing the drug manufacturing process and improving the quality of the drug. The study highlights the potential of artificial intelligence techniques in solving complex problems and making accurate predictions. Further research can explore the application of these techniques in other areas of the pharmaceutical industry and enhance the efficiency and effectiveness of drug development and production. The developed ML-based contour visualizations can provide useful qualitative insights for process optimization in supercritical CO₂ systems. The results indicate that both solvent density and paracetamol solubility increase with pressure, while temperature exhibits a secondary but significant influence. In particular, the model suggests that regions characterized by relatively high pressure and moderately elevated temperature tend to favor higher solubility (mole fraction). This indicates a potential operating window in the upper region of the investigated pressure–temperature space. However, these findings should be interpreted as trend-based guidance rather than exact optimized process conditions, due to the limited dataset size. The primary value of the proposed approach lies in supporting experimental design and reducing the search space for future process optimization studies. A limitation of the present study is the absence of direct comparison with classical thermodynamic or equation-of-state-based models, which are commonly used in supercritical fluid systems. Furthermore, the dataset size is limited and derived from a single literature source. Future work should address these limitations by incorporating larger datasets, external validation, and benchmarking against mechanistic thermodynamic models.
