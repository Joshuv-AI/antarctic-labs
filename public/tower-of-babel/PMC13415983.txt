A Comprehensive Meta‐Analysis and Review of Intraoperative Radiotherapy in Colorectal Cancer: Examining Radiation Dosage, Long‐Term Prognosis, and Treatment‐Related Complications
Authors: Chuhan Guo, Shuyang Liu, Liangliang Li, Jiayi Zhao, Hongji Zhou, Hongqiang Liu, Tong Liu, Fujian Ji
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundThe effect of device type and radiation dose in intraoperative radiotherapy (IORT) for colorectal cancer is not well established. To explore this, we reviewed studies reporting postoperative complications, disease‐free survival, local control, and long‐term survival in patients treated with non‐implantable IORT techniques—IOERT, KV‐IORT, and HDR‐IORT. Most of the available evidence relates to IOERT or HDR‐IORT, which is usually delivered at 15 Gy (range 10–20 Gy). The median dose for KV‐IORT is typically 12.5 Gy.MethodsWe conducted a systematic search of literature through November 2023 to identify studies that compared non‐implantable IORT with surgery alone or other treatment approaches (PROSPERO registration: CRD42024508349). Twenty‐five studies involving 2664 patients met the inclusion criteria.ResultsPatients treated with non‐implantable IORT tended to achieve better 5‐year overall survival than those managed without it, with IOERT contributing most clearly to this difference. A similar pattern emerged for long‐term local control, where IORT showed an advantage [OR 2.08, 95% CI 1.19–3.64]; studies of IOERT reported an even stronger effect [OR 2.23, 95% CI 1.07–4.65]. Importantly, the use of IORT did not correspond with higher rates of key postoperative complications, including anastomotic leakage, pelvic collections, wound problems, or bowel obstruction.ConclusionsOur results suggest that IORT may improve survival and local control for patients with advanced or recurrent colorectal cancer, despite the heterogeneity among studies. Device‐related and dose‐related factors may also play a role and merit closer study, thus warranting closer examination in future work.

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) is a major contributor to the incidence of cancer and cancer‐related mortality globally, with approximately 1 million new cases reported annually worldwide [1]. In locally advanced rectal cancer (LARC), despite laparoscopic surgery and adherence to the total mesorectal excision principle, 12% to 28% of patients have positive peripheral margins, the local recurrence rate can reach up to 38%, and the 5‐year overall survival rate is less than 70% [2, 3]. For advanced or recurrent rectal cancer (LRRC), a combined approach of surgical intervention and chemoradiotherapy can improve patient survival rates. However, relying solely on external radiotherapy is ineffective and is associated with an increased risk of recurrence [4]. Thus, there is a need to further explore adjuvant treatment for LARC/LRRC. In rectal cancer treatment, intraoperative radiation therapy (IORT) provides a way to reduce toxicity to critical organs such as the bladder, urethra, prostate, vagina, and uterus while protecting the small bowel at the same time [5]. Consequently, this factor is a primary consideration in reducing the dosage during external beam radiotherapy (EBRT).

IORT entails the precise delivery of high‐dose ionizing radiation directly to the neoplasm or neoplasm bed during surgical procedures [6, 7]. The objective of IORT is to optimize radiation delivery to the tumor bed while minimizing exposure to the surrounding normal tissues [8, 9]. In 2019, the Brachytherapy Society of America issued an agreement on intraoperative radiotherapy, categorizing it as either IOERT, kV‐IORT, HDR‐IORT, and implantable I‐125 [10]. At the same time, it also suggests that IORT may be considered as a treatment option when the surgical margin is positive [10]. In recent years, IORT has been increasingly utilized in oncologic surgery and has been introduced as an adjuvant or monotherapy in multimodal treatment for many tumor multimodal therapy regimens [11]. In 2013, Mirnezami [12] reported that the 5‐year local control rate, 5‐year disease‐free survival rate, and 5‐year overall survival rate were improved without increasing the incidence of complications such as urinary tract issues and intestinal obstructions [12]. In 2021, Bin Liu published a meta‐analysis indicating that IORT improved only local control and did not increase complication rates [13]. In the same year, Matthew R. Fahy [14] published a meta‐analysis presenting similar results. Neither study assessed baseline information, such as receipt of adjuvant therapy, TNM stage, gender, and age [14]. As a result, it was impossible to rule out the influence of this baseline information on outcome indicators. Different IORT doses might have led to variations in survival [15]. Moreover, the aforementioned studies did not conduct subgroup analysis for the different types and doses of non‐implantable IORT. To better understand the effect of IORT on CRC, our study aimed to evaluate the 5‐year local control rate, 5‐year disease‐free survival rate, and 5‐year overall survival rate, as well as the postoperative complication rates of LARC/LRRC with different non‐implantable IORT types and doses.

Methods

Literature Search and Eligibility Criteria

The strategy for our study was to electronically search for relevant publications using publicly available datasets, including PubMed, Embase, Ovid, Cochrane, and Web of Science databases, all of which were published between the date of database creation and November 2023. The subsequent keywords, namely “intraoperative radiotherapy,” “colorectal cancer,” and “dose,” were utilized for the search. All clinical trials underwent initial screening and review via selecting relevant abstracts. Furthermore, we retrieved the full texts of all potentially relevant publications and carried out a formal assessment for inclusion of this study. Simultaneously, a manual search was conducted to examine the reference list of relevant publications to avoid any omissions.

Research Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

Inclusion criteria included: (a) original manuscript pertaining to CRC patients with non‐implantable IORT (IOERT/kV‐IORT/HDR‐IORT) and those with surgery/±EBRT/implantation in the I‐125 clinical control study; (b) all patients diagnosed with CRC, regardless of age, ethnicity, and country; (c) original manuscript containing the data required for the study; (d) subjects of the study, the study design, and statistical methods used for data analysis were similar; (e) extracted data can be used with the same statistical indicators; (f) language of the literature was English. Exclusion criteria included: (a) duplicated published literature; (b) literature whose end indicator data could not be extracted; (c) literature with too small a sample size (less than 20 cases); (d) types of literature such as case studies, reviews, conference abstracts, etc.; (e) non‐human research subjects.

All deaths are defined as events in overall survival (OS). Disease‐free survival (DFS) is defined as the time from the random occurrence of a tumor to its recurrence. Local control (LC) is defined as a local response with no recurrence of the tumor.

Data Extraction and Quality Assessment

In this study, literature screening was independently conducted by two researchers. In case of any disagreement, a third researcher reviewed the manuscripts and made the final decision. Both researchers integrated general information from the literature, such as the first author, year, and country. Regarding treatment plans, both researchers particularly focused on IORT equipment and dosage, surgical methods, preoperative and postoperative radiotherapy, and chemotherapy. Additionally, the researchers integrated postoperative survival outcomes and the occurrence of complications.

The evidence quality was also independently and systematically appraised by both researchers. The Newcastle‐Ottawa Scale (NOS) [16] was used for quality assessment of observational studies. NOS is a commonly employed method for meta‐analyses of cohort studies and can be adapted for specific topics. Each domain within the NOS is rated to determine the overall quality of evidence. Disagreements among researchers were resolved through consensus. This study used the risk bias tools of Cochrane risk [17] to evaluate large‐scale clinical randomized controlled trials (Figures S1 and S2).

Data Analysis

Dichotomous variables were expressed as odds ratios (OR) and their 95% confidence intervals (CI). For continuous data, mean differences and 95% CIs were estimated using the I‐V method. Outcome was presented as mean ± SD or median ± IQR. Sometimes, the formula described by Hozo et al. [18] was used to convert the results, such as converting [mean ± SE] to [mean ± SD]. Among the 25 articles reviewed in this study, one had a three‐group control design. This approach is in line with the methodologies outlined in the Cochrane Handbook [19]. Thus, the searched literature was divided into two, equivalent to 26 papers. In total, there are 19 papers using the IOERT device. Since the literature included different ranges of doses and data types, the IOERT's doses were artificially divided into three main categories: median dose 15 Gy (range 10–20 Gy), median dose 20 Gy (range 15–25 Gy), and ≥ 20 Gy (range up to 30 Gy), with a greater number of median doses 15 Gy (10–20 Gy). There were five papers using HDR‐IORT, with the median dose of 10 and 15 Gy (range 10–20 Gy), and 1 paper using eIORT, with a median dose of 12.5 Gy (Figure S3). One study reported IOERT and HDR‐IORT at doses of 10 Gy [20]. When I 2 was greater than 50%, it was defined as the presence of heterogeneity, therefore a random effects model was chosen for analysis. When I 2 ≤ 50%, it was defined as having low heterogeneity, thus a fixed effects model was chosen for analysis. Sensitivity analysis was also conducted using a row‐by‐row division method. Moreover, publication bias analysis was performed using funnel plots and Egger's test, showing the test level with p = 0.05. All results were expressed as OR values and 95% confidence intervals, with p < 0.05 as a threshold to determine statistical significance. All data processing and statistical analysis were conducted using Review Manager 5.3 software and Stata 14.0 software.

Demographics

A total of 2664 patients were included in the data analysis. Among them, 1129 were assigned to the experimental group and 1499 to the control group. Men accounted for 64.6% of all patients. The percentage of males was higher in the experimental group (66.6%) compared to the control group (61.9%). The results indicated statistically significant differences in age between the two groups [SMD = −0.224, 95% CI (−0.333, −0.116)]. In total, 1884 patients underwent the procedure for LARC. LARC accounted for 71.7% of the participants. Additional baseline information is presented in Table S2.

Techniques and Treatment Modalities

In the two groups, 712 patients in the IORT group received external beam radiotherapy (EBRT), accounting for 63.1% of the IORT group. Meanwhile, in the control group, 778 patients received EBRT, constituting 51.9% of the control group. Abdominoperineal resection (APR) emerged as the predominant surgical procedure, with 991 patients undergoing this operation. After APR, low anterior resection (LAR) was the next most common, with 368 patients having this surgery.

Sensitivity Analysis

Sensitivity analysis was performed on the 3‐year and 5‐year overall survival, local control rate, and disease‐free survival, respectively. The results demonstrated that there were no statistically significant differences in the overall effective rates after excluding each study one by one, compared to those before exclusion (p > 0.05). This indicates that the results obtained in this study are robust. The findings are presented in Figures S8A, S8B, S8C, S8D, S8E, and S8F.

Publication Bias Analysis

Publication bias analysis was conducted with 5‐year overall survival as an indicator. Egger's test resulted in a p‐value of 0.027, indicating the existence of publication bias in this study. The outcomes were observed and then analyzed using the subtractive complement method. Before cropping, p = 0.016 (p < 0.05), and after cropping, p = 0.035 (p < 0.05), which shows the stability of this outcome indicator. Additional results regarding publication bias are presented in Figure S9.

Discussion

Globally, colorectal adenocarcinoma ranks third in cancer incidence [45]. The incidence of rectal cancer is on the rise, in line with the growing prevalence of western lifestyles. Approximately 5%–10% of individuals diagnosed with rectal cancer present with locally advanced stages of the disease [46, 47]. In low rectal cancer, the lower margin of the cancer lesion is less than 5 cm from the anal border. The surgical margin is closely related to local recurrence and disease‐free survival [45]. According to the recommendation of the National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN), neoadjuvant radiation and chemotherapy are the most popular treatment options for intermediate‐stage rectal cancer. For patients with low‐ to middle‐stage progressive rectal cancer, the NCCN suggests that treatment should consist of neoadjuvant radio‐chemotherapy, total mesorectal excision, and postoperative adjuvant chemotherapy [48]. However, preoperative radiation has certain disadvantages. Firstly, preoperative radiotherapy is time‐consuming, and some patients cannot tolerate the side effects of radiotherapy. Secondly, during radiotherapy, some patients are at risk of sudden tumor rupture, bleeding, or perforation. Finally, some patients may not receive sufficient radiotherapy to lower the stage of cancer [49]. Although IORT is not included in the guidelines, there are some clinical observational studies and randomized controlled trials (RCTs) implementing this technique. At present, the dose selection and device of IORT are still in the exploratory stage.

Our research demonstrates that IORT offers benefits in enhancing 3‐year overall survival rates, 3‐year disease‐free survival rates, 3‐year local control rates, 5‐year overall survival rates, and 5‐year local control rates among patients. Through our analysis of baseline demographics and clinical factors, including sex, age, TNM stage, chemotherapy, and use of external beam radiotherapy (EBRT), we found that the population in the experimental group was generally younger than that of the control group. In the included studies, intraoperative electron radiation therapy (IOERT) and high‐dose rate intraoperative radiotherapy (HDR‐IORT) were shown to have a positive effect on tumor prognosis. However, there was no literature to explain why this particular type of device was used instead of other types. In the subgroup analysis for evaluating dosage in patients receiving IOERT, those receiving median doses of 15 Gy and ≥ 20 Gy demonstrated a superior five‐year survival rate in the test group, compared to the control group. However, only two original articles with doses ≥ 20 Gy were reviewed. Suzuki's study (1995) showed that 8.1% of patients had a large residual lesion, and 19% had a microscopic residual lesion [24]. In that study, the median IORT dose was ≥ 20 Gy in the gross residual group and 15 Gy in the microscopic residual group. Yojiro's study (1999) indicated that patients with local recurrence of rectal cancer who had previously undergone surgical procedures such as low anterior resection, abdominal perineal resection, or Hartmann's procedure were identified as appropriate candidates for IORT [25]. As a result, IORT dose and energy were adjusted according to the amount and thickness of tumor tissue remaining after maximal surgical excision. IORT dosages greater than 20 Gy were used exclusively in individuals with severe residual disease [25]. Taken together, these two pieces of literature on the use of high‐dose intraoperative radiotherapy show that the high dose is solely based on the individualized treatment of patients with local recurrence. Individualized doses of IORT are what make this subset of patients clinically meaningful. Meanwhile, we collected data from two groups treated with EBRT for analysis. The analysis results demonstrated no statistically significant differences between these two groups [OR = 2.878, 95% CI (0.593, 13.981), p = 0.190]. Patients who underwent chemotherapy demonstrated minimal differences between the two groups, with an odds ratio of 1.034 and a 95% confidence interval of (0.740, 1.445), and a p‐value of 0.884. This finding is sufficient to draw the conclusion that additional adjuvant therapies had no significant impact on the tumor outcome measures of IORT as a treatment regimen.

The equipment for IORT mainly consists of MV‐grade electronic IORT systems (IOERT), high‐dose‐rate intraoperative brachytherapy systems (HDR‐IORT), and intracavitary kV‐grade X‐ray machines (kV‐IORT) [50]. Among them, only IOERT procedures require the treatment room to be vacated [50]. IOERT offers several advantages. Compared with HDR‐IORT, IOERT has a relatively shorter setup and treatment duration, and provides a uniform radiation dose across the depth of the tissue. Nevertheless, a major drawback of IOERT is that its applicators are not suitable for curved areas or confined spaces [51]. In contrast, although HDR‐IORT takes more time to perform, it features flexible applicators that can adjust to any surface contour. Additionally, HDR‐IORT can irradiate larger areas and also has a steeper dose gradient between the target surface and the reference depth, which leads to a more concentrated dose on the target surface [51].

The kV‐IORT X‐ray source can only be placed in a single applicator at a time, limiting its practical clinical applications to single treatment cavities and small superficial treatment areas [10]. It is mainly used for the treatment of diseases like breast cancer, cervical cancer, and vaginal cancer [50]. This accounts for the scarcity of literature on the use of kV‐IORT in colorectal cancer. Overall, the current development trend of IORT equipment is undoubtedly toward a lighter weight, wireless operation, and personalized dose distribution [50]. There is already a patented proposal for a miniature X‐ray bulb with a “cold cathode” structure that can be freely adjusted according to the shape and location of the tumor [52].

The current analysis has both advantages and disadvantages. There is some heterogeneity observed in the 5‐year disease‐free survival and 5‐year local control rate. Most of the studies are non‐randomized comparisons, characterized by a small sample size. Additionally, there is heterogeneity in tumor site, original or recurrent indications, surgical resection completeness, and evaluated outcomes. The studies are mainly conducted at large cancer institutions with a diverse range of patients, staging, and therapy options. Moreover, we observed that most studies still rely on IOERT, whereas the clinical use of HDR‐IORT and kV‐IORT remains relatively limited, which constrains the breadth and comparability of study results. Given the distinct physical characteristics of IOERT and kV‐IORT, each modality may be selected according to specific clinical indications, and both offer unique advantages.

There is no complete harmonization of preset radiation doses for margin R0/R1/R2 resections. Wiig's et al. [27] is the only study with prescribed radiation protocols for R0/R1/R2 resections, and it is also the only one that specifies the indications for IORT [14]. Only one article included in this meta‐analysis used KV‐IORT, and the median dose was typically 12.5 Gy, while the microscopic residual dose and the gross residual dose was 15 Gy and 15–17.5 Gy, respectively. With ongoing technological advances and improvements in radiotherapy equipment, eIORT has increasingly been introduced into clinical practice. In a recent prospective study by Murphy et al. [53] using the eIORT device at a dose of 12 Gy, the report only tracks short‐term complication outcomes.

In conclusion, this literature review offers substantial evidence of a clinical benefit for IORT. Despite the predominance of observational research and the lack of high‐quality randomized controlled trials (RCTs), this situation is similar to that of radical resection for colorectal liver metastases. Although not initially investigated in RCTs, radical resection is now recognized as a standard treatment approach [12]. While the treatment regimen for patients with IORT devices is not fully established, it is hoped that more clinicians will use proven doses of intraoperative radiotherapy to personalize treatment and track long‐term tumor outcomes. Only then will our treatment options become clear, and it is expected that IORT technology will soon be included in colorectal cancer treatment guidelines.

Conclusion

Intraoperative radiation therapy demonstrates certain clinical benefits in aspects such as 3‐year survival, 3‐year local control, 3‐year disease‐free survival rate, 5‐year survival, and 5‐year local control without increasing the risk of postoperative morbidity. Among the three different radiotherapy devices currently available, the IOERT device seems to be more advantageous. Regarding the dose in IOERT, a median dose of 15 Gy for LARC and a dose of ≥ 20 Gy for LRRC can achieve good oncologic benefits.
