Health literacy profiles of riverine populations in the Brazilian Amazon: a cluster analysis
Authors: Paula Gisely Costa Silva, Raquel Gomes da Silva, Marcio Yrochy Saldanha dos Santos, Lucinéia Ferreira Ferreira, Evelin Lorena Sousa do Espírito Santo, Ivaneide Leal Ataíde Rodrigues, Laura Maria Vidal Nogueira
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHealth literacy is essential for individuals to access, understand, assess, and use health-related information. In riverside communities in the Brazilian Amazon, social vulnerability and difficult access to services make it urgent to understand how these communities deal with health information in their daily lives.ObjectiveTo identify the health literacy profiles of riverine communities in the Amazon region of Pará, northern Brazil.MethodsThis is a descriptive, cross-sectional study with 200 residents of the islands of Cotijuba and Combú in the state of Pará, Brazil, in 2024. A multidimensional questionnaire was used to assess health literacy. Cluster analysis allowed the identification of profiles with similar characteristics. The groups were described based on sociodemographic data, lifestyle, and performance on the nine assessed scales, and were named according to their main characteristics.ResultsSeventeen distinct profiles were identified. The greatest differences lie in the dimensions related to social support, to the search for reliable information, and to the ability to use healthcare services.ConclusionsThe heterogeneity in health literacy on the islands points to the need for targeted interventions, especially regarding navigating the system and understanding health information.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-026-28062-0.

Background

The Brazilian Amazon covers approximately 5.217.423 km², corresponding to about 61% of the national territory. Due to its vast size and demographic dispersion, marked by large hydrographic basins and the presence of the world’s largest tropical forest, the region faces significant challenges in access to healthcare and other social issues, compared to other regions of Brazil [1].

In the state of Pará, approximately 2.024.601 people live in rural areas, including riverine communities inhabiting territories with difficult access and limited infrastructure [2]. These communities face restrictions in access to basic resources, such as sanitation and electricity, and depend on urban areas to obtain consumer goods and healthcare services, which are mainly accessible by river. Travel occurs in small boats, with trips lasting from a few minutes to several days [1].

In addition to these logistical difficulties, riverine communities face a double burden of disease, characterized by the coexistence of infectious diseases, such as malaria, parasitic diseases, and Chagas disease, and chronic noncommunicable diseases (NCDs) which are common in urban centers, such as systemic arterial hypertension (SAH) and diabetes mellitus (DM) [1].

The scenario highlighting the need to promote Health Literacy (HL), defined as a set of individual knowledge and skills acquired through daily activities and social interactions over generations. This collection of personal knowledge and skills is influenced by organizational structures and by the availability of resources that enable people to access, understand, assess, and apply information and services to promote and preserve their personal health and well-being, as well as that of their loved ones [3]. The literature has shown a consistent association between low levels of HL and worse health outcomes, especially in populations in situations of social and geographical vulnerability [4].

In Brazil, studies on HL predominantly focus on the functional dimension, showing a high prevalence of insufficient levels, including in riverine communities, as demonstrated in research conducted in the municipality of Abaetetuba, which revealed that out of a sample of 312 participants, 205 had inadequate HL [5]. However, investigation HL beyond functional abilities allows for a better understanding of other aspects inherent to HL, enabling individuals and communities to take responsibility for their health status [6].

It is acknowledged that the geographical characteristics of the Brazilian Amazon region, associated with the context of occupation, form a set of challenging conditions for the implementation of public policies, which exacerbates social, structural, and health service access inequalities [7]. Research demonstrates that the region of residence influences HL skills, with those residing in urban areas showing twice the HL of those residing in rural areas [8].

Furthermore, the limitations imposed on the riverside population regarding health resources have repercussions on health education actions, tools that promote HL, which cannot be implemented effectively, resulting in unsatisfactory HL outcomes and, consequently, worse health outcomes [9–10].

In this sense, multidimensional instruments have been proposed to broaden the understanding of HL in different contexts. Among them, the Health Literacy Questionnaire (HLQ), developed in Australia, stands out, assessing nine distinct domains of HL. Its main advantage lies in its ability to identify specific health needs profiles, allowing for a more comprehensive analysis of the interactions between individuals and health services. Furthermore, the HLQ has been widely used in different cultural contexts, after adaptation and validation processes [6], proving to be a relevant tool for improving communication between health professionals and users and for supporting more targeted interventions [11].

Despite advances in the field, a significant gap remains in the literature regarding the multidimensional analysis of HL in riverside populations of the Amazon, especially considering their sociocultural, geographic, and healthcare access specificities. Most studies still focus on functional measures, failing to capture the complexity of these populations needs.

In this context, the present study contributes to the literature by investigating, in an unprecedented way, the multidimensionais profiles of HL among riverside communities in the Amazon region of Pará. In particular, it broadens the understanding of HL in vulnerable populations and generates scientific evidence to support the development of more equitable and culturally appropriate health promotion strategies in these territories.

This study aims to identify the health literacy profiles of riverine communities in the Amazon region of Pará, northern Brazil.

Methods

Study design, period, and setting

This is a cross-sectional, descriptive study with a quantitative approach, which followed the recommendations of the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines. It was conducted on the islands of Cotijuba and Combú, located in Belém do Pará, Brazil, and data were collected between May and October 2024.

These islands provide a relevant context for public health research due to their geographical and sociocultural characteristics, which directly influence lifestyles and access to health services. Both are accessible only by river.

Population, sample, inclusion and exclusion criteria

The study population consists of riverside dwellers attended at the primary health care units on the studied islands. The sample was defined using a simple sampling technique through the formula 𝑛=𝑧2×𝛿²×𝑁∕𝑒²×(𝑁−1)+𝑧²×𝛿² [12], considering that n corresponds to the sample size; z to the critical value associated with the desired confidence level (1.96); 𝛿 to the population standard deviation of the variable (0.5); and sampling error of (0.05) and N to the universe of riverside dwellers attended at the units on the islands. Based on the unit’s records, a total of 228 visits were estimated in Cotijuba and 65 in Combú during the data collection period.

The sample size initially calculated was 167 riverside dwellers. However, 200 riverside dwellers participated, of whom 144 were residents of Cotijuba and 56 of Combú.

The inclusion criteria were established as follows: being a resident of the islands for at least one year and being aged 18 years or older. Five people with severe neurological, visual, or hearing impairments that prevented oral or written communication were excluded from the study. These conditions were identified through direct observation and initial interaction between participants and researchers at the time of recruitment. The criterion used was the inability to understand or respond adequately to questions related to the study, evidenced by communication difficulties even after repetition or reformulation of the questions.

Data colection

Prior to data collection, meetings were held with health unit managers to present the objectives of the survey, to ensure the collaboration of local health teams, and to facilitate access to data collection sites. A data collection schedule was then drawn up in line with the routine service.

After the initial explanation about the research project, participants were approached individually in the waiting rooms of the units and invited to participate in the study. To ensure transparency, those interested in participating were invited to go to a private room, previously designated by the local manager, where the Free and Informed Consent Form (FICF) was read and explained in detail. After confirming their full understanding of the survey and the content of the form, they were asked to sign it or provide their fingerprint.

After signing the FICF, participants were asked to individually complete the data collection instruments. Only those who had difficulty reading or writing were assisted in the interviews, during which the researchers asked the questions and recorded the answers. This process was conducted in an empathetic and respectful manner, ensuring the participation of all, regardless of their level of literacy. Moreover, the interviewers were properly trained to minimize application biases, ensuring that all participants understood the questions.

Three questionnaires were used in this study to collect data: a questionnaire to characterize participants was developed by the researchers; the Fantastic Lifestyle Questionnaire [13]; and the Brazilian version of the Health Literacy Questionnaire (HLQ-Br) [6, 14]. The questionnaire used to characterize participants was developed by the researchers, addressing issues related to sociodemographic conditions in order to understand the social context and its characteristics. The questionnaire entitled “Fantastic Lifestyle” was created by in 1984 the Department of Family Medicine at McMaster University, Canada. This instrument was later translated and validated for the Brazilian context in 2008, and was used to categorize lifestyles as Excellent, Very Good, Good, and Fair [13]. The HLQ was developed in Australia [14], with the purpose of collecting information for multidimensional analysis of HL, through the assessment of three levels: functional HL, interactive HL, and critical HL [15]. The Brazilian version (HLQ-Br) used in this study was validated in 2021 [6].

Comprising nine Likert scales, totaling 44 items, the titles of the scales with their respective numbers of items are: (1) understanding and support from healthcare professionals (four items); (2) sufficient information to take care of health (four items); (3) active healthcare (five items); (4) social support for healthcare (five items); (5) assessment of healthcare information (five items); (6) ability to interact actively with healthcare professionals (five items); (7) navigating the healthcare system (six items); (8) ability to find good information on healthcare (five items); (9) understanding healthcare information and knowing what to do (five items).

The HLQ-Br is structured in two parts: in the first part (scales one to five), one to four points are assigned, with the responses “strongly disagree” (one point), “disagree” (two points), ‘agree’ (three points), and “strongly agree” (four points); in the second part (scales six to nine), one to five points are assigned, with the responses “I cannot do it or it is always difficult” (one point), “usually difficult” (two points), “sometimes difficult” (three points), “usually easy” (four points), and “always easy” (five points). Thus, the scale scores range from 1 to 4 for the first five scales and from 1 to 5 for scales 6 to 9.

It is worth noting that the HLQ-Br is owned by the University of Swimborn, Australia, the copyright holder. Therefore, authorization for its use was requested. In addition, permission was requested from the authors responsible for its validation and translation into the Brazilian context (HLQ-Br).

Data analysis

The data were organized in a Microsoft Excel 2021 spreadsheet with double checking and analyzed by using IBM SPSS software version 25. Statistical analyses included descriptive statistics (means, standard deviations, and frequency) of sociodemographic variables, of lifestyle extracts, and of HLQ-Br scale scores.

The average was calculated independently for each of the nine scales of the HLQ-Br by adding the item scores and dividing them by the number of items in the scale, since this instrument does not provide an overall score for the HL. A cut-off score of 2,5 was established for the first part and 3,5 for the second part, in accordance with the guidelines of the author responsible for the HLQ. Scores below the cut-off score mean that the community has weaknesses in that HL dimension.

Cluster analysis was conducted using the hierarchical method based on the nine scales of the HLQ-Br, which had been previously standardized due to the different score ranges. The Ward’s linkage method was used, which minimizes the internal variance within the groups, with the squared Euclidean distance as a measure of dissimilarity [16]. The objective was to identify clusters of participants with similar HL profiles.

To determine the optimal number of clusters, 3 to 16 solutions were explored by analyzing the patterns of variation between clusters as recommended by the Ophelia Manual [17]. Demographic and health data such as age, gender, educational level, income and lifestyle were included to contextualize the groups and support interpretation. Small clusters or those considered outliers were maintained, recognizing that they may represent marginalized and socially excluded groups, possibly underrepresented in surveys.

The cluster narratives were developed as interpretive qualitative descriptions based on the sociodemographic composition, the score on each HLQ-Br scale, and the LS categorization. Symbolic names were assigned to the groups, namely: “the resilient with social support,” “the confident navigators,” “the self-managers,” “the socially supported but vulnerable,” “the participatory critical,” “the health-balanced,” and “the supported but not critical,” with the aim of enabling a better understanding of the results, making them more accessible to users and healthcare professionals, managers, and researchers working with traditional populations. This naming strategy is inspired by similar studies [11] that use interpretive labels to communicate profiles in a functional and applicable way.

Results

The study involved 200 riverine residents living on two islands, with the majority of the sample coming from the island of Cotijuba. The average age of participants was 41,56 years, ranging from 18 to 87. The sample was mostly female (81.5%), with a predominance of self-declared brown-skinned individuals. The average number of children was 2,62, ranging from 0 to 17. It was observed that 34,5% of respondents had not completed elementary school, 53% identified themselves as Evangelical, and 46,3% reported an income below the minimum salary.

With regard to cluster analysis, the riverine communities presented different HL profiles, stratified into 11 distinct groups on the island of Cotijuba and 6 groups on the island of Combú. The findings corresponding to the island of Cotijuba are presented in Tables 1 and 2, and those corresponding to the island of Combú are shown in Tables 3 and 4. Given the differences identified in the findings, they were presented separately according to geographical location. The groups express the sociodemographic characteristics of the riverine communities in terms of education, socioeconomic status, religiosity, and HL profile. The choice to present narratives from four groups from the island of Cotijuba and three from Combú was based on two main criteria: representativeness in the sample (higher and lower frequency) and position at the extremes of the scale scores (groups with higher or lower scores).

Among the groups identified on the island of Cotijuba, group 2, called “the resilient with social support,” represents 23,6% of the participants on the island, consisting mainly of males with an average age of 44,1 years and approximately 2,6 children. Most of them are self-declared white, widowed, without formal education, not following any religion, and living on an income below the minimum salary (Table 1). Despite socioeconomic and educational vulnerabilities, they presented a good overall LS score. The lowest scores were observed on scales 2 (sufficient information to take care of health) and 8 (ability to find good healthcare information). On the other hand, the highest score was recorded on scale 4 (social support for healthcare), indicating that, even in the face of adversity, this group has a relevant support network to deal with healthcare-related issues (Table 2).

Another identified profile was that of group 10, called “the confident navigators,” which corresponds to 2,8% of the participants from the island of Cotijuba. This group is also predominantly male, with an average age of 51,2 years and approximately 7,7 children, the highest average number of children across all clusters. Participants self-identified as white, with incomplete high school education, divorced, followers of the Catholic religion, and with a monthly income above the minimum salary (Table 1). This group had a good LS score. The highest scores were observed on scales 1 (understanding and support from healthcare professionals) and 7 (navigating the healthcare system), indicating familiarity with the services and a good relationship with healthcare professionals. The lowest score occurred on scale 5 (assessment of healthcare information), suggesting limitations in critical thinking skills regarding the information obtained (Table 2).

Group 9, called “self-managers,” represents 8,3% of participants and is also predominantly composed of men, with an average age of 42,4 years and approximately 3,1 children. Members self-identified as being brown-skinned, with a college degree, married, Catholic, and with an income above the minimum salary (Table 1). The members of this group had a very good LS score. It is noteworthy that they did not show weaknesses in any of the HLQ scales, suggesting a robust HL profile, with good skills to seek, understand, assess, and apply information for the benefit of their own health (Table 2).

Finally, Group 8, called “the socially supported but vulnerable,” represents 6,3% of the sample. This group is mainly composed of males, with an average age of 44,9 years and approximately 3,2 children. The participants self-identified as black, without formal education, married, followers of the Evangelical religion, and with an income below the minimum salary (Table 1). Despite having a good LS score, the members of this group showed weaknesses on all HLQ scales, except for scale 4 (social support for healthcare). This indicates significant dependence on the social network to deal with healthcare issues, such as access, understanding, assessment, and application of healthcare information (Table 2).

On the island of Combú, group 3, called “participatory critical” represents 25% of the participants on the island. This group is mainly composed of women, with an average age of 34,86 years and approximately 2,14 children. The participants self-identified as yellow, with a predominance of those who had finished elementary school, were unmarried, belonged to religions other than Catholicism and Evangelicalism, and had a monthly income above the minimum salary (Table 3). They presented a good LS score. The highest scores were observed on scales 5 (assessment of healthcare information) and 6 (ability to interact actively with healthcare professionals), indicating a critical and participatory profile in healthcare communication. However, they showed weaknesses on scales 7 (navigating the healthcare system) and 9 (understanding healthcare information and knowing what to do), revealing practical difficulties in applying knowledge and orienting themselves in the available services (Table 4).

Group 4, entitled “healthy-balanced women,” corresponds to 10,7% of the sample on the island of Combú. This group is composed mainly of females, with an average age of 29,17 years and approximately 1,83 children. The participants in this group self-identified as black, mostly with a complete elementary school education, married, without religious affiliation, and with an income above the minimum salary (Table 3). They presented a good LS score and, notably, showed no weaknesses on any of the HLQ scales. This profile indicates solid competence in various LS dimensions, characterizing a functional balance between knowledge, behavior, and social context, even in the face of modest socioeconomic conditions (Table 4).

Group 6, called “supporters, but not critical,” also represents 10,7% of the sample from the island of Combú. This group is composed mainly of males, with an average age of 42,83 years and approximately 1,67 children. The participants self-identified as white or indigenous, with incomplete elementary education, widowed, followers of the Evangelical religion, and with an income above the minimum salary (Table 3). The group presented a good LS score. The highest score was recorded on scale 4 (social support for healthcare), highlighting the importance of support networks in coping with healthcare demands. The lowest score was observed on scale 5 (assessment of healthcare information), revealing weaknesses in critical thinking skills when faced with the information received, which may limit autonomy in healthcare decision-making (Table 4).

Discussion

This study highlights the variations, in clusters, of health literacy profiles in the riverine population, emphasizing the influences that sociodemographic factors exert on the health literacy of this population and guiding the formulation of personalized strategies to improve health equity in populations with significant specificities. Thus, this study is pioneering in identifying such profiles among riverine people in the Amazon region of Pará, making it possible to understand the common characteristics in a given group and their influence on the development of health literacy skills, highlighted in each domain of the HLQ-Br.

The results of this study demonstrate significant variations in the dimensions of HL between the clusters identified on the islands of Cotijuba and Combú, reflecting differences in socioeconomic, educational, and demographic factors that determine how individuals access, understand and use healthcare information. Overall, the findings reinforce the existing literature on HL which highlight the relationship between HL, quality of life, and social support offered to individuals [5, 16, 18, 19].

The data indicate that clusters with lower educational backgrounds and income (G2 and G8 in Cotijuba) and lower educational backgrounds (G6 in Combú) have greater difficulties related to HL dimensions, especially in critically assessing healthcare information and interacting with healthcare professionals. This finding is consistent with the results who identified similar patterns in the Australian population, where individuals with lower educational levels face significant barriers to interpreting and applying healthcare information [16]. In addition, studies point out that hospitalized patients with lower HL tend to have worse clinical outcomes, possibly due to difficulty in understanding healthcare guidelines, which affects adherence to prescribed treatments [18].

In contrast, clusters with higher levels of education and income (G9 and G10 in Cotijuba and G4 in Combú) demonstrated a greater ability to access, understand, and apply healthcare information. This result is in line with the findings who found that transplant patients with higher levels of HL demonstrated a greater ability to better manage their health, adhering to the recommendations of professionals and identifying early signs of complications [19].

The importance of HL becomes even more evident when observing that, even in clusters with significant difficulties in the “sufficient information to take care of health” and “navigating the healthcare system” dimensions (G2 and G8 in Cotijuba and G6 in Combú), there is an active effort toward self-care, mediated by social support and interaction with healthcare professionals. This phenomenon was also reported by studies who highlight the resilience of riverside populations in the Amazon in maintaining healthcare practices despite difficulties in understanding healthcare information [5].

However, while searches linked low functional literacy to factors such as advanced age and low educational level [5], this study found that even individuals with low educational levels may have adequate levels of HL related to social support and interaction with healthcare professionals, thus favoring the adoption of self-care practices. This suggests that, in traditional communities such as the riverside ones, HL is not distributed homogeneously and that vulnerable socioeconomic groups face more barriers.

In addition, clusters with higher HL scores (G9 Cotijuba and G4 Combú) revealed strong support from healthcare professionals, ease of communication, and well-structured support. These findings corroborate the analysis which indicates that patients with more favorable HL profiles tend to establish stronger relationships with healthcare professionals, improving adherence to treatment and well-informed decision-making [18].

In contrast, clusters with lower HL scores (G8 Cotijuba and G6 Combú) revealed difficulties in communicating with healthcare professionals, an issue already discussed by studies in the Amazonian context [5]. The communication barrier, coupled with low educational background, can result in a lack of understanding of healthcare guidelines, leading to inadequate self-care practices and, consequently, to an increase in vulnerability to morbidity and mortality.

Another relevant aspect is social support. In all clusters, this factor appeared as a positive element, suggesting that the community plays a fundamental role in healthcare, partially compensating for deficiencies in other dimensions of HL. This finding is consistent with those who observed that transplant patients with good social support have a better quality of life and greater adherence to treatment, regardless of their individual HL [19].

The lowest scores identified in domain 7 of the HLQ-Br show significant limitations in the LS of most clusters, which highlights the difficulty in accessing health services for this population, since geographical barriers are limiting factors in guaranteeing effective access to health for this population. This corroborates by the other study [20], which identified weaknesses in access to health for this population.

These results challenge the traditional view that HL depends solely on educational and socioeconomic factors. In fact, the findings suggest that, in riverside communities, social support and the ability to establish trust-based relationships with healthcare professionals play an essential role in accessing and making appropriate use of healthcare information. Thus, self-care in these communities transcends educational and economic limitations. Although the results of the present study are similar to other research, they go further by indicating that building strong interpersonal relationships and support networks may be a key factor in improving HL in riverside areas [5].

Limitations of the survey

Despite the significant contributions, there are limitations that must be considered, such as the scenario being restricted to two islands, which may not fully reflect the reality of other riverine populations, including those in the Amazon region. The small sample size is also noteworthy, as it may limit the generalization of the findings and the robustness of the inferences. The data collection approach, based primarily on self-reports, may introduce social desirability bias, especially in responses about healthcare behaviors and habits. Furthermore, the scarcity of scientific evidence on the subject represents a barrier to in-depth analysis and comparisons with other studies, as this is still an emerging field of research with limited scientific output.

Conclusions

This study highlighted the existence of different profiles of HL among riverside populations in the Amazon region of Pará, revealing significant heterogeneity in the experiences, capabilities, and health needs. The identification of clusters with distinct levels of socio-educational vulnerability allowed for an understanding of the complex relationship between socioeconomic factors, HL, and healthy LS. While some groups face difficulties navigating the healthcare system and understanding information, others, even in adverse socioeconomic conditions, demonstrated a greater capacity to mobilize support networks and adopt more positive healthcare behaviors. This segmentation highlighted the internal heterogeneity of riverside communities, emphasizing the influence of social factors in shaping different healthcare patterns.

The application of the HLQ-Br enabled an in-depth and multidimensional analysis of health literacy, contributing to the advancement of literature by exploring a population that has been little investigated from this perspective. Considering the interpretive parameters of the instrument, it was possible to identify specific weaknesses among the analyzed groups, especially in the dimensions related to the evaluation of health information, access to and understanding of information, and navigation within the health system.

These findings allow us to move from a descriptive analysis to the proposition of more targeted interventions. Weaknesses in the evaluation of health information indicate the need for educational strategies aimed at developing critical thinking, focusing on the identification of reliable sources and the safe use of information. Limitations related to access to and understanding of information reinforce the importance of adopting more accessible communication practices, using simple language and culturally adapted educational materials. Difficulties in navigating the health system point to the need to strengthen primary health care, highlighting the role of health professionals as mediators of access and facilitators of care.

Furthermore, identifying groups with higher levels of HL in certain dimensions, even under adverse socioeconomic conditions, reinforces the role of social support as a protective factor, suggesting that interventions based on strengthening community networks can be effective in mitigating inequalities and expanding autonomy in healthcare.

In this way, the construction of HL profiles highlights the internal diversity of riverside communities and provides input for the development of health interventions, the creation of culturally sensitive educational materials, and the formulation of more equitable and contextualized public policies. Furthermore, this approach can be replicated in other vulnerable contexts, contributing to comparability and a deeper understanding of the multiple dimensions of HL.

Therefore, HL should be recognized as a central determinant of health equity, requiring interdisciplinary approaches that integrate health professionals, educators, and public policy makers. Finally, future studies are recommended to further analyze the dimensions of these profiles in different contexts of the Amazon region.
