Development and evaluation of automated screening algorithms for pre-analytical errors in pharmacokinetic data: a simulation-based study
Authors: Minsub Shim, Jaegu Kang, Kyung-Sang Yu, Ki Young Huh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Pre-analytical errors such as sample mix-ups can compromise the integrity of pharmacokinetic (PK) data. We simulated vancomycin PK data and introduced two error types: intra-individual time point swap (TS) errors and inter-individual concentration swap (CS) errors. This study developed and validated two automated detection approaches—a run-test-based non-parametric method and a Mahalanobis distance-based method with leave-one-out cross-validation (LOOCV)—for identifying pre-analytical errors in PK datasets. Performance was evaluated using 12,500 simulated profiles comparing the two methods. The distance-based method demonstrated significantly higher error detection rate (79.7% vs. 59.6%, p < 0.001) and greater area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (0.856; 95% confidence interval [CI], 0.816–0.889 vs. 0.574; 95% CI, 0.541–0.610) compared to the run-test, while maintaining reasonable specificity (76.4% vs. 84.0%). For TS errors, the distance-based method achieved higher recall (80.4% vs. 71.9%) and modestly higher F1-score (0.530 vs. 0.515). For CS errors, the distance-based method demonstrated superior precision (86.1% vs. 59.8%) with comparable F1-score (0.572 vs. 0.564). Detection was robust across alternative PopPK models, a structurally different drug (theophylline), training-set contamination (with the minimum covariance determinant estimator recommended for non-curated training), missing data (with sub-vector marginal handling), and sampling-time jitter. Overall, the distance-based method with LOOCV outperformed the conventional run-test across key performance metrics and can serve as a practical quality control tool for ensuring PK data integrity in clinical pharmacology research.

INTRODUCTION

Pre-analytical errors occurring during sample collection, handling, labeling, and processing can significantly compromise data integrity in clinical studies. These errors account for approximately 60–70% of all laboratory errors [1], and a previous study confirmed that pre-analytical factors (46–68.2%) substantially outweigh post-analytical factors (18.5–47%) as the predominant source of error [2].

Such errors can be particularly consequential in pharmacokinetic (PK) studies, where early and systematic detection is critical. If left unrecognized, pre-analytical errors may introduce bias into parameter estimates, potentially leading to incorrect dosing recommendations [3]. For example, a recent simulation study demonstrated that when drug administration occurs later than scheduled but is recorded as on time (e.g., a 12-hour delay), clearance can be underestimated by up to 31% [3]. Accordingly, pre-analytical errors should be identified and addressed prior to formal PK analyses [12].

From an operational standpoint, pre-analytical errors in PK studies can be broadly categorized into two types. The first type arises when samples within a subject are exchanged between different time points, referred to as intra-individual time point swap (TS). This error typically occurs during sample collection, such as when collection tubes are mislabeled during serial sampling or when actual draw times are recorded incorrectly [1], resulting in a distorted temporal sequence of concentrations. The second type involves the confusion of samples between different subjects at the same time point, termed inter-individual concentration swap (CS). These errors are more likely to occur during laboratory processing or aliquoting, where samples from multiple subjects are handled simultaneously [14], leading to concentration values inconsistent with a subject's expected PK profile.

Despite the prevalence of such errors, systematic methods for their detection in PK datasets remain limited. Traditional approaches often rely on manual visual inspection of individual concentration–time profiles or simple rule-based statistical criteria. However, these methods are inherently subjective and labor-intensive, depending heavily on the analyst’s interpretation [56]. More critically, simple statistical approaches frequently lack the sensitivity to distinguish true physiological variability from technical errors. Because PK parameters typically follow a log-normal distribution with substantial inter-individual variability (IIV), legitimate data points can readily mimic statistical outliers [7]. Consequently, structural errors such as sample mix-ups or time swaps, which may fall within the plausible physiological range but are positionally incorrect, are often missed, leading to biased analyses [68].

For high-throughput screening of pre-analytical errors, non-parametric approaches offer a viable framework for systematic detection. The run-test, derived from the theory of runs by Wald and Wolfowitz [9], provides a formal statistical basis for analyzing the sequence of concentration deviations. While a valid PK profile follows a predictable temporal trajectory, structural errors such as swapped time points can induce unexpected sign changes in residuals, thereby disrupting the expected sequence of runs. This approach provides an objective measure for identifying anomalous patterns and serves as a straightforward screening method.

A more advanced approach involves distance-based algorithms, which are well suited for detecting multivariate outliers [101112]. Mahalanobis distance is especially appropriate for PK datasets because it accounts for correlations between variables by incorporating the covariance structure of the data [13]. Unlike simpler metrics such as Euclidean distance, Mahalanobis distance recognizes that PK observations at different time points are highly interdependent. This property is essential for detecting complex anomalies that may appear physiologically plausible in isolation but are statistically inconsistent within the multivariate context of an individual’s profile. This metric has been successfully applied in clinical laboratory quality control [10], multivariate outlier detection in scientific data analysis [11], and various other domains including anomaly detection [12].

Despite these advantages, determining appropriate detection thresholds for Mahalanobis distance requires careful consideration. Relying solely on theoretical χ2 distributions can lead to overfitting when population parameters are estimated from the dataset itself [13]. To address this issue, we incorporated leave-one-out cross-validation (LOOCV) to establish robust thresholds for the algorithm [14].

In this study, we developed and assessed the feasibility of two automated algorithms for identifying pre-analytical errors in PK data. Specifically, we evaluated the performance of a run-test-based non-parametric method and a Mahalanobis distance-based method with LOOCV using simulated datasets incorporating TS and CS errors. This simulation-based approach enables evaluation of the real-world applicability of the proposed algorithms.

METHODS

Simulated dataset

To evaluate the proposed error detection method, we simulated a simple clinical scenario of intravenous infusion (e.g., vancomycin). We simulated PK data using a well-known population PK model for vancomycin based on the structural parameters in hospitalized patients: a two-compartment model with first-order elimination (typical values; clearance [CL] = 4.5 L/h, central volume of distribution [Vc] = 58.4 L, peripheral volume of distribution [Vp] = 38.4 L, and inter-compartmental clearance [Q] = 6.5 L/h) [15]. Concentration-time profiles were generated at eight clinically relevant sampling times of pre-dose and 1, 2, 4, 6, 8, 12, and 24 hours post-dose.

Covariate effects were incorporated as CL scaled by creatinine clearance (CrCL/120)0.8 and Vc scaled by body weight (WT/70). We sampled the creatinine clearance and body weight distribution from the ranges in the literature [15], assuming truncated log-normal distribution and independence between the two covariates. We set the IIV at 30% for CL, Vc, and Vp, based on the review by Marsot et al. [16], and retrieved combined error models from the original model [15]. To account for within-subject correlation arising from same-batch assay analysis, we applied an intra-subject residual error correlation coefficient (ρ = 0.9), which was derived from fluorescence polarization immunoassay precision data [17].

A total of 10,000 error-free concentration-time profiles were generated as training data to establish reference distributions. For method evaluation, 50 independent Monte Carlo simulation runs were performed, each containing 100 normal profiles, 50 TS error cases, and 100 CS error cases, yielding 12,500 test scenarios.

Development of detection algorithms

For the non-parametric detection algorithm, a run-test-based approach was developed as follows. For each concentration–time profile, the direction of concentration change between consecutive time points was determined and labeled as positive (+) if increasing or negative (−) if decreasing. A “run” was defined as a maximal consecutive sequence of identical signs, and the total number of runs was counted for each profile. Normal PK profiles typically exhibit a consistent sign pattern, yielding an expected range of run counts, whereas profiles containing errors such as swapped time points produce unexpected sign changes and an anomalous number of runs. Profiles with run counts deviating significantly from the expected range were flagged as potentially erroneous (significance level α = 0.05).

For the distance-based detection algorithm, an approach grounded in Mahalanobis distance was developed. Unlike Euclidean distance, which treats each measurement independently, Mahalanobis distance accounts for both the spread and the correlation structure among variables. This property makes it particularly well suited for PK data, where concentrations at adjacent time points tend to be correlated rather than independent. To apply this metric, a data matrix X was constructed with subjects as rows and sampling time points as columns, where Xij denotes the observed concentration of the ith subject at the jth time point. The Mahalanobis distance for a given subject was then defined as: D = √[(x − μ)T Σ−1 (x − μ)] where x is the vector of concentrations across all time points for that subject, μ is the mean concentration vector across subjects at each time point, and Σ is the covariance matrix that captures both the variability at each time point and the correlations between different time points.

Conceptually, this distance quantifies how far a subject's entire concentration–time profile deviates from the population average, weighted by the natural covariance structure of the data. A subject whose profile deviates in a direction that is uncommon relative to the observed inter-time-point correlations will yield a large Mahalanobis distance, even if each individual concentration appears within a plausible range. The covariance matrix was estimated using the Ledoit–Wolf shrinkage estimator to ensure robust estimation regardless of sample-to-variable ratio [18].

To establish a detection threshold while avoiding overfitting to the training data, LOOCV was applied. In each iteration, one subject was held out and its Mahalanobis distance was calculated using μ and Σ estimated from the remaining subjects. This procedure ensures that the distance for each subject is evaluated against parameters derived independently of that subject. The threshold was then set as the mean of these leave-one-out distances. A concentration–time profile was flagged as potentially erroneous if its Mahalanobis distance exceeded this threshold.

The detection algorithm employed distinct data representations depending on the error type. For TS error detection, normalized profiles were used to attenuate inter-individual variability in absolute concentration and focus on deviations in the temporal shape of the profile. Each profile was normalized by dividing all concentrations by the first observed non-zero concentration. For CS error detection, raw (unnormalized) concentrations were retained to preserve absolute magnitude information, which is essential for detecting concentrations that are inconsistent with a subject’s PK characteristics.

The distance-based algorithm detected CS errors through a two-stage procedure. In the first stage, all profiles with Mahalanobis distances exceeding the threshold were identified as anomaly candidates. In the second stage, a swap-back test was performed for each pair of candidate profiles: the suspected swapped concentrations between the two profiles were exchanged back to their original positions, and the Mahalanobis distances were recalculated for both profiles. If both recalculated distances fell below the threshold, this indicated that reversing the swap restored both profiles to a plausible pattern, and the pair was consequently classified as a CS error. This two-stage approach exploits the inherent bidirectional nature of CS errors; when a sample is misassigned from one subject to another, both the donor and the recipient profiles are simultaneously distorted, and correcting one necessarily corrects the other.

Performance evaluation

Performance was evaluated using 50 independent simulated datasets generated by Monte Carlo simulation. Each dataset comprised 100 normal (error-free) concentration–time profiles, 50 profiles containing TS errors, and 50 CS error pairs (100 profiles in total, reflecting bidirectional swaps), yielding 250 profiles per dataset and 12,500 total evaluations across all simulations. The inclusion of normal profiles enabled the calculation of specificity and false positive rate (FPR), providing a more comprehensive assessment beyond detection sensitivity alone.

The following performance metrics were calculated for each dataset. Error detection rate (EDR) was defined as the proportion of error-containing profiles correctly identified as anomalous. Specificity was defined as the proportion of normal profiles correctly classified as non-anomalous. FPR, defined as the proportion of normal profiles incorrectly flagged as anomalous, was calculated as 1 minus specificity. Precision was computed as the ratio of true positives to the total number of flagged profiles (i.e., the sum of true positives and false positives), representing the reliability of a positive flag. Recall, equivalent to sensitivity, was calculated as the ratio of true positives to the total number of error-containing profiles (i.e., the sum of true positives and false negatives), representing the completeness of error detection. The F1-score was determined as the harmonic mean of precision and recall, providing a single measure that balances both metrics. The area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC) was used to assess overall discriminative performance across all threshold values.

Paired t-tests were used to compare performance metrics between the two methods across Monte Carlo runs, and effect sizes were quantified using Cohen’s d. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. All analyses were performed using Python 3.14.2 with the NumPy 2.3.5, SciPy 1.17.0, and scikit-learn 1.8.0 libraries.

Sensitivity analysis

The detection threshold was defined as a weighted combination of the χ2-based theoretical threshold and the LOOCV-derived empirical threshold: Threshold = (1 - λ) × χ20.95,p + λ × LOO_threshold, where χ20.95,p denotes the 95th percentile of the χ2 distribution with p degrees of freedom (p = number of time points), and λ ranges from 0 (purely χ2-based) to 1 (purely LOOCV-based). Sensitivity analysis was performed across λ values of 0, 0.25, 0.5, 0.75, and 1.0 to evaluate the impact of threshold selection on detection performance.

The optimal λ value was determined by maximizing a combined score that balances multiple performance objectives: Combined Score = 0.30 * Error Detection Rate + 0.20 * Specificity + 0.25 * TS F1 + 0.25 * CS F1. The weights were assigned to prioritize overall error detection (0.30) while maintaining balanced consideration of specificity (0.20) and error-type-specific performance (0.25 each for TS and CS F1-scores).

Robustness across PopPK models

Detector dependence on the specific vancomycin PopPK model was assessed with two additional published models, Thomson 2009 [19] and Revilla 2010 [20]. Parameters were taken from the original publications. Training set size and Monte Carlo protocol were unchanged.

Cross-drug transferability to theophylline

Theophylline, a one-compartment oral drug, served as a structurally different test case. The Boeckmann 1994 real 12-subject dataset was the test side; training was synthesized from the Pinheiro and Bates 2000 nlme fit of the same dataset [21]. Synthetic TS and CS errors were injected into the test profiles to retain ground-truth labels. A control set drawn from the training distribution was evaluated in parallel (Supplementary Data 1).

Training-set contamination sensitivity

The clean-training assumption was tested by injecting mixed TS and CS errors at four levels (0, 2.5, 5, 10%) into the training set. For each contaminated set the covariance matrix was re-estimated with the sample, Ledoit and Wolf [18], and minimum covariance determinant (MCD) [22] estimators (MCD support fraction 0.75; theoretical breakdown point of roughly 25% [23] exceeds the highest contamination tested). Test sets and Monte Carlo protocol were unchanged.

Missing-data handling: imputation versus sub-vector marginal detection

Missing-completely-at-random (MCAR) missingness [24] was generated at 0, 5, and 10% rates on timepoints with t > 0. Two detector variants were compared: the imputation-based detector (Det-Imp) imputes missing values with the column mean and applies the original threshold; the sub-vector marginal detector (Det-Sub) computes a sub-vector marginal Mahalanobis distance [25] on observed timepoints, with the λ-weighted threshold re-derived per missing pattern. Training-side missingness uses an expectation-maximization covariance estimate [26] consistent under missing-at-random [2728]; both detectors used λ = 0.75.

Sampling-time jitter sensitivity

Real TDM sampling rarely matches nominal time exactly [2930]. Jitter was drawn independently for each nominal sampling time from a uniform distribution spanning ± 5 or ± 10%. Concentrations were computed at jittered times, but the detector was trained and evaluated on the nominal grid, matching common practice of recording nominal clock times [31].

Post-hoc operating characteristics and weight sensitivity

Three post-hoc analyses on the main 50-run Monte Carlo outputs were performed: TS detection stratified by phase pair; priority-queue re-framing with cases binned into three Mahalanobis-distance tiers [32]; and sensitivity of λ to the weight scheme under main asymmetric (0.30/0.20/0.25/0.25) and equal weights, with regret evaluated across 1,771 weight combinations on a 0.05-step weight simplex grid. Regret is the relative loss versus the optimal λ for each weight combination. Weighting error detection above specificity follows the asymmetry of screening costs [23334]: undetected pre-analytical errors propagate into biased parameter estimates [3536].

RESULTS

Overall performance

Using the optimal threshold parameters (λ = 0.75), the Mahalanobis distance-based method with LOOCV demonstrated significantly superior error detection performance compared to the run-test (Fig. 1, Table 1, Supplementary Fig. 1). The mean EDR was 79.7% (standard deviation [SD] 3.6%) for the Mahalanobis method versus 59.6% (SD 4.3%) for the run-test (Cohen’s d = +4.05, p < 0.001). Both methods demonstrated consistent performance across the 50 Monte Carlo runs (Supplementary Figs. 2 and 3). The coefficient of variation for the EDR was 4.6% for the Mahalanobis method and 7.2% for the run-test, indicating good reproducibility of both approaches. The consistent separation between the two methods across all runs further supports the robustness of the Mahalanobis approach. However, this improved detection came with a trade−off in specificity: 76.4% (SD 2.7%) for Mahalanobis versus 84.0% (SD 3.6%) for run−test (Cohen’s d = −1.81, p < 0.001).

TS error detection

For TS errors, the distance-based method achieved higher recall (80.4% vs. 71.9%, Cohen’s d = +1.17, p < 0.001) with comparable precision (39.6% vs. 40.2%, Cohen’s d = -0.21, p = 0.143) compared to the run-test (Fig. 3A). The TS F1-score was modestly higher for the Mahalanobis method (0.530 vs. 0.515, Cohen’s d = +0.33, p = 0.025). The Mahalanobis method’s higher recall indicates improved sensitivity for detecting temporal mix-ups, which is critical for quality control applications where missing true errors can have serious consequences.

CS error detection

For CS errors, the Mahalanobis method demonstrated substantially higher precision (86.1% vs. 59.8%, Cohen’s d = +5.00, p < 0.001) with lower recall (42.8% vs. 53.5%, Cohen’s d = −2.13, p < 0.001) compared to the run-test (Fig. 3B). The CS F1-score was numerically higher for the Mahalanobis method but did not reach statistical significance (0.572 vs. 0.564, Cohen’s d = +0.19, p = 0.191). The high precision of the Mahalanobis method for CS detection indicates that when it flags a potential CS error, the prediction is highly reliable (approximately 86% of flagged CS pairs are true errors).

Sensitivity analysis

The sensitivity analysis revealed a clear trade-off between EDR and specificity as λ varied from 0 to 1 (Fig. 4, Supplementary Table 1). Key findings included the following: At λ = 0 (χ2 threshold), EDR was 62.2% with high specificity (90.3%), yielding TS F1-score of 0.490 and CS F1-score of 0.548. At λ = 0.75 (optimal), EDR was 79.7% with moderate specificity (76.4%), providing balanced performance (TS F1 = 0.530, CS F1 = 0.572). At λ = 1 (pure LOOCV threshold), EDR was highest (85.8%) but specificity was lowest (63.4%).

Robustness across PopPK models

Detector behavior was consistent across the three vancomycin PopPK models. EDR was 0.797 (Goti 2018), 0.805 (Thomson 2009 modified), and 0.796 (Revilla 2010); specificity was tightly clustered between 0.733 and 0.781. TS F1 spanned a wider range (0.530 to 0.736) and CS F1 between 0.514 and 0.595 (Supplementary Table 3).

Cross-drug transferability to theophylline

On the real Boeckmann 1994 theophylline test set with synthetically injected swap errors, EDR reached 0.884 (SD 0.066), TS F1 0.715 (SD 0.064), and CS F1 0.577 (SD 0.079); specificity was 0.323 (SD 0.113). Within- and cross-subject error detection therefore matched or exceeded the vancomycin baseline without algorithm modification. The synthetic-synthetic control gave specificity 0.711 and EDR 0.713, close to the vancomycin baseline; the 0.39 specificity gap quantifies distribution shift between literature-parameterized training and the real deployment population (Supplementary Table 4).

Training-set contamination sensitivity

The three estimators diverged sharply as contamination rose (Supplementary Table 5, Supplementary Fig. 4). At 10% contamination, Ledoit-Wolf collapsed (EDR fell from 0.797 to 0.297, a 62.7% relative drop; TS F1 from 0.530 to 0.281, a 47.0% relative drop). Sample covariance was more stable (EDR from 0.841 to 0.607, a 27.8% relative drop). MCD was essentially flat (EDR from 0.871 to 0.779, a 10.6% relative drop), consistent with its theoretical breakdown point of roughly 25%. Specificity moved in the opposite direction for Ledoit-Wolf (from 0.764 to 0.918) because contamination inflated the covariance estimate and raised the distance threshold so high that almost no profiles were flagged, the same mechanism behind the EDR collapse. MCD held specificity between 0.637 and 0.773. CS F1 stayed near 0.61 across estimators.

Missing-data handling: imputation versus sub-vector marginal detection

At 0% missing, both detectors reproduced the Table 1 baseline. At 10% MCAR (Supplementary Table 7), Det-Imp’s specificity fell from 0.764 to 0.546 (a 28.5% relative drop) while EDR spuriously rose to 0.832, the diagnostic pattern of over-flagging from mean imputation, which shifts profiles in the covariance ellipsoid and inflates distances. Det-Sub held specificity at 0.740 (a 3.1% relative drop) with modest EDR decline (from 0.797 to 0.764), TS F1 stable at 0.530, and CS F1 down only to 0.537. The preservation of specificity under Det-Sub follows from marginalizing rather than imputing missing dimensions.

Sampling-time jitter and phase-stratified detection

Sampling-time jitter had little effect on detection (EDR 0.797/0.770/0.790; specificity 0.764/0.774/0.772; TS F1 0.530/0.574/0.548; CS F1 0.572/0.513/0.532 at 0/5/10% jitter; Supplementary Table 10). Phase stratification of TS detection (Supplementary Fig. 5, Supplementary Table 8) showed strong dependence on swap span: cross-phase distribution-terminal swaps were detected at 96.4% versus 37.3% for swaps within the Mid-elimination phase. Swaps with relative concentration difference under 10% were detected at 21.0%, rising above 97% when relative concentration difference exceeded 50% (Supplementary Table 9).

Operating characteristics under tiered triage

Treating the Mahalanobis distance as a priority queue rather than a binary flag (Supplementary Table 6) gives a workable triage. The top 10% of cases per run by Mahalanobis distance had positive predictive value 1.00 and captured 16.7% of true errors; extending to the top 30% raised recall to 47.4% at PPV near 0.92. Skipping the bottom 60% by Mahalanobis distance reduced recall from 0.80 to 0.47 but cut manual review by roughly 60%.

Lambda sensitivity under alternative weightings

λ = 0.75 was robust to the weight scheme. Under the main asymmetric weights (0.30/0.20/0.25/0.25) and equal weights, the combined score peaked at λ = 0.75 (0.6671 and 0.6654 respectively). Across 1,771 simplex weight combinations, λ = 0.75 had the lowest mean regret (2.74%) and the largest fraction within 10% of optimal (96.1%), with worst-case regret 15.47% vs. 29.82% for λ = 1.00 (Supplementary Tables 1 and 2).

AUROC with confidence intervals

Table 1 reports AUROC with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) computed by the percentile method across the 50 Monte Carlo runs. The Mahalanobis detector’s AUROC was 0.856 (95% CI, 0.816–0.889); the run-test was 0.574 (95% CI, 0.541–0.610).

DISCUSSION

Conventional quality control of PK data has largely relied on visual inspection of concentration–time profiles, a process that is inherently subjective and impractical for large-scale studies [56]. To address this limitation, we developed two automated detection approaches: the run-test, a sign-based non-parametric method, and the Mahalanobis distance with LOOCV, a multivariate method that accounts for the correlation structure of longitudinal PK data. The Mahalanobis distance-based method significantly outperformed the run-test in overall detection rate (+20.1 percentage points) and discriminative ability (AUROC: 0.856 vs. 0.574) (Figs. 1 and 2). For TS errors, the method achieved high recall (80.4%), which is particularly important given that temporal mix-ups can severely distort parameter estimates in compartmental PK modeling (Fig. 3A). For CS errors, the method achieved remarkably high precision (86.1%), reducing the burden of manual verification for false alarms, although the moderate recall (42.8%) reflects the inherent difficulty of detecting inter-individual swaps when the substituted value falls within the normal range of variability (Fig. 3B).

The superior performance of the Mahalanobis distance-based method can be attributed to several methodological advantages. First, the Mahalanobis distance accounts for the correlation structure of longitudinal PK data, which is particularly important because consecutive concentration measurements in a PK profile are inherently correlated due to the underlying PK processes [37]. Second, the LOOCV-based threshold provides a data-adaptive approach that reflects the actual variability observed in the reference population, rather than relying on fixed theoretical assumptions, leading to more appropriate detection boundaries. Third, the use of the Ledoit-Wolf shrinkage estimator for covariance matrix estimation improves the robustness of the method, particularly when dealing with the moderate sample sizes typical of clinical PK studies [18]. These features collectively enable the Mahalanobis method to capture the multivariate nature of PK profiles.

In contrast, the run-test evaluates only the sequence of signs (i.e., whether the concentration is increasing or decreasing between consecutive time points) without considering the magnitude or correlation structure of the deviations [9]. This sign-based, non-parametric approach reduces the rich information contained in a multivariate concentration-time profile to a binary sequence, thereby discarding quantitative relationships between time points that carry critical diagnostic information. Consequently, errors that preserve the general directional pattern of the profile but disrupt the quantitative covariance structure, such as subtle TSs between adjacent sampling times, are less likely to be detected. Furthermore, the run-test relies on a fixed significance threshold that does not adapt to the distributional characteristics of the specific dataset, which may further contribute to its lower discriminative ability. These findings are consistent with previous observations that methods accounting for the correlation structure among variables outperform those that do not when applied to quality control of complex, correlated datasets [38].

Sensitivity analysis revealed that λ controls the trade-off between EDR and specificity, with lower values favoring specificity and higher values improving detection at the cost of increased false positives. Based on a combined score balancing multiple performance objectives, λ = 0.75 was identified as the optimal default, achieving an EDR of 79.7%, specificity of 76.4%, and balanced F1-scores for both TS and CS errors (0.530 and 0.572, respectively) (Fig. 4, Supplementary Table 1). However, the optimal λ may vary depending on the analytical context; a lower λ may be preferred when high specificity is paramount due to limited resources for manual verification, whereas a higher λ may be more appropriate when comprehensive error detection is critical, such as in safety-focused clinical trials. Datasets with greater inter-individual variability or residual error may also require adjustment to maintain optimal performance.

In addition to the tunable detection threshold, the Mahalanobis distance-based method offers practical benefits for real-world implementation. It is fully automated and requires no subjective judgment once the reference population is established, provides quantitative and reproducible flagging criteria that can be standardized across studies, and is computationally efficient enough for routine application to datasets containing hundreds or thousands of subjects. These characteristics make the method particularly suited for integration into automated data management pipelines in clinical pharmacology research and therapeutic drug monitoring programs, where timely detection of pre-analytical errors is essential for maintaining data integrity [12].

Robustness analyses across multiple PopPK models and a structurally different drug support the detector’s generalizability. EDR clustered tightly (0.796 to 0.805) across the three vancomycin models despite divergent central volumes and residual structures (Supplementary Table 3). Without algorithm modification, the detector matched or exceeded the vancomycin EDR on real theophylline profiles (Supplementary Table 4).

The contamination analysis (Supplementary Table 5, Supplementary Fig. 4) suggests a practical guideline for detector selection. Below 2.5% contamination, the three estimators were comparable, with Ledoit-Wolf preferred for higher specificity. At higher contamination Ledoit-Wolf silently degraded (EDR fell from 0.797 to 0.297) while MCD held nearly constant [2239]. We recommend Ledoit-Wolf under the curated-training assumption, and MCD when reference-population cleanliness cannot be guaranteed.

Missing observations, common in routine TDM practice, were handled cleanly by the sub-vector marginal Det-Sub variant. At 10% MCAR missing, Det-Sub preserved specificity (0.740 versus baseline 0.764), whereas the mean-imputation Det-Imp lost 29% of specificity by over-flagging imputed profiles (Supplementary Table 7). For assay-driven below-the-quantification-limit values, a fully missing-not-at-random-robust treatment along Beal’s M3 or M6 lines [4041] is flagged as future work.

The tiered-triage reanalysis (Supplementary Table 6) addresses the manual-review burden created by a 23.6% FPR at scale. Used as a priority queue, the top 10% of cases per run had PPV 1.00 and captured 16.7% of true errors; the top 30% captured 47.4% at PPV above 0.9. This formulation reduces effort proportionally while preserving detection on the most confident flags, following established principles of prioritized manual review under resource constraints [323334].

Phase-stratified detection (Supplementary Fig. 5, Supplementary Tables 8 and 9) shows that time-point swaps are detected near-perfectly when spanning phases of different concentration magnitude (distribution-terminal: 96.4%), but poorly within the same kinetic phase (within-Mid: 37.3%). Sampling-time jitter up to 10% changed specificity by at most 0.03 and F1 scores by at most 0.06 (Supplementary Table 10). Larger deviations, common in real trough draws [29], would plausibly require time-aware training and are flagged as future work.

This study has several limitations. The algorithm depends on a well-defined reference covariance, and drugs with strongly nonlinear elimination [4243] or with inter-individual variability markedly larger than the vancomycin range were not evaluated. No public PK dataset with documented pre-analytical errors is available, since errors are rarely labeled when they occur and are typically removed before data release [3536]. As a halfway step, the detector was applied to real Boeckmann 1994 theophylline concentrations with synthetic swap injection (Supplementary Table 4); the 0.39 specificity gap from the synthetic-synthetic control to the real test set quantifies distribution shift between literature-parameterized training and a real deployment population. Prospective audits against verified sample-swap ground truth remain needed [2930]. Only TS and CS errors were evaluated; other modes such as dilution or analytical interference were not. The residual correlation ρ = 0.9 represents one specific scenario. These robustness analyses focused on the proposed Mahalanobis detector; analogous evaluation of the run-test baseline remains a direction for future work, with the partial exception of sampling-time jitter, which preserves time ordering and leaves the run-test’s sign-based statistic essentially unchanged.

In conclusion, the Mahalanobis distance-based method with LOOCV demonstrated superior overall discriminative ability compared to the conventional run-test, with higher recall for TS and higher precision for CS errors. These results support its use as a practical and automated quality control tool for ensuring PK data integrity in clinical pharmacology research.
