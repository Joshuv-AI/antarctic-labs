A case report of a severe firearm-related ballistic injury to the external genitals in an adolescent with a favorable outcome in a resource-limited setting
Authors: P’wèréw Rodolphe Plante, Kossi Edem Gueouguede, Gnozi Emmanuel Kanaza, Gnimdou Ariste Botcho, Komi Hola Sikpa, Tchilabalo Matchona Kpatcha
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Introduction:A ballistic wound to the external genitalia is a penetrating injury to the external genitalia caused by a firearm projectile. Although rare, these wounds can be serious enough to compromise urogenital function. This article reports a case that illustrates this point.Case presentation:A 14-year-old boy was admitted with acute urinary retention secondary to open trauma to the external genitalia caused by a firearm projectile. Injury assessment revealed a devastating wound to the external genitalia, with urethral rupture and partial sectioning of the corpora cavernosa. The patient underwent urethral and cavernous repair with closure and a drain blade. The postoperative course was uneventful, with no urogenital sequelae.Clinical discussion:Ballistic wounds to the external genitalia are rare but increasingly common in areas of armed conflict. Whether isolated or associated with other injuries, they primarily affect young people and can present in various forms. There are general principles for the surgical management of ballistic wounds, but no universal consensus. Intraoperative findings, therefore, guide surgical procedures. The aim is to avoid postoperative complications, including surgical-site infection and functional sequelae, particularly of erectile function, which poses a medico-legal problem.Conclusion:The search for urethral, cavernous, and testicular injuries during surgical exploration must be systematic and performed as early as possible in all cases of firearm-related penetrating wounds to the male external genital organs. It is a surgical emergency, and the surgical procedure must preserve the functional prognosis as much as possible.

Introduction

A ballistic wound to the external genitalia is an open injury caused by the penetration of a projectile (ball, pellet, metal fragment, or explosive device content) into these organs[1]. These injuries are rare because of the location of the external genital organs, but they can be very serious[1,2]. They generally occur in young people[2,3]. Lesions caused by firearm projectiles can have specific characteristics, and the injury most often affects the functional prognosis of the urogenital tract. Treatment is usually conservative[2]. We report a case of a severe ballistic wound to the external genitalia in an adolescent with a favorable outcome, detailing the treatment and progression of the condition. Clinical observation

A 14-year-old boy, an unschooler, presented with no significant medical, drug, allergy, family, or social history. He was admitted for acute urinary retention and a wound to the external genitalia, following a shooting in which a stray bullet struck him. The skin was sutured immediately by the nurse at the nearest peripheral care unit after analgesics were administered. The patient was referred to us for further treatment due to acute urinary retention. On admission, the patient was anxious but in good general condition, conscious, and hemodynamically stable, with a Paediatric Risk of Mortality (PRISM) Score of 0. There was a sutured wound on the scrotum and base of the penis, a distended bladder, and urethral bleeding. Deep second-degree burn injuries were also observed on the inner thighs and in some areas of the scrotum. Ultrasound and retrograde urethrocystography could not be performed, as these procedures were not available on an emergency basis in our under-resourced setting. We concluded that there was a ballistic wound to the external genitalia with rupture of the urethra. The patient was immediately admitted to the operating theater, where, under general anesthesia in the supine position and after removal of the skin sutures, we discovered a devastating wound to the scrotum and base of the penis, approximately 12 cm in length, bleeding profusely, with ragged edges (Fig. 1). The right vaginal membrane was open, exposing the unattached right testicle, slightly blackened in places, and the right spermatic cord, which was free of any injury. The base of the penis was severed at two-thirds of its circumference, with rupture of most of the fibers of the corpora cavernosa and complete rupture of the junction between the penile urethra and the membranous urethra (Fig. 2). This classifies it as a grade IV traumatic injury severity to the male external genitalia. No foreign bodies or projectiles were found during the examination of the wound. The skin was prepared with povidone-iodine surgical scrubs, followed by an aqueous povidone-iodine solution. The wound was thoroughly irrigated with saline, followed by a meticulous and minimally invasive excision of necrotic tissue. A transurethral Foley catheter was used to drain urine by passing it through the membranous urethra via the proximal end of the urethral rupture. The urethral rupture was repaired with end-to-end suturing using interrupted 4/0 absorbable monofilament sutures. The cavernous wounds were repaired end-to-end with separate single stitches using 3/0 absorbable woven thread. We performed a right orchidopexy using a non-absorbable, non-woven 3/0 suture and repaired the wound in layers to the skin after placing the scrotal drainage blade (Fig. 3). The immediate postoperative course was uneventful. The drainage tube was removed on the fifth day after surgery. Post-operative treatment consisted of antibiotic therapy with intravenous Ceftriaxone 2 g every 24 hours and intravenous Metronidazole 500 mg every 12 hours, both for 10 days, and intramuscular Gentamicin 160 mg per day for 3 days. Analgesia was provided with intravenous Paracetamol 1 g combined with intravenous Tramadol 100 mg every 6 hours. The surgical wound dressing was changed every 48 hours. The catheter was removed on the 10th day after switching to injectable antibiotics. The patient attended a follow-up appointment 2 weeks after the operation, and a clinical assessment indicated an uneventful postoperative recovery. The transurethral urinary catheter was left in place for 4 weeks; spontaneous urination resumed immediately after catheter removal. A testicular ultrasound at the 3-month post-operative follow-up showed normal testicles. A retrograde voiding urethrocystogram revealed a slight urethral narrowing, most likely due to scarring, with the urinary stream remaining intact. Functionally, the patient reported no urinary discomfort. His urinary stream was normal, and he retained good erectile function. The aesthetic result after wound healing was also satisfactory. Comments

Ballistic trauma to the external genitalia, described as extremely rare, is becoming increasingly common, particularly among the civilian population, with the advent of armed conflict in our developing countries[5,6]. In Senegal, Bah et al[7] reported six cases over 4 years. Simhan[8] reported 97 cases of scrotal gunshot wounds over 20 years in the USA, while in Yemen, Ghilan[9] described 20 cases of external genital injuries caused by firearms between 2005 and 2008. In Togo, Kpatcha et al reported a single case in 2014[2]. This low frequency is due to the external genital organs being infrequent targets of firearm assaults and to their anatomical location, which does not favor accessibility to injury[1,2]. Victims of ballistic injuries to the external genitalia are mainly young, as in Cerwinka’s series, which reported an average age of 29[10], and in Bagga’s series, which showed a predominance of patients aged 18–28[2,3]. This observation has also been reported by African authors[2,7,11], and the patient in our case is also young. In the case presented, the injuries are limited solely to the external genitalia. This is relatively rare, as such injuries are often accompanied by damage to the perineum or abdomen, with digestive and/or muscular injuries to the lower limbs[2,8,9], which can lead to colostomy and/or cystostomy[1]. These associated injuries can be explained by the anatomical proximity of the aforementioned organs to the external genitalia[2]. Although penetrating scrotal wounds are the most common clinical presentation, they are frequently accompanied by cavernous and urethral injuries[2]. Our patient sustained a urethral rupture at the base of the penis and a rupture involving two-thirds of the corpora cavernosa. These injuries appear to be the result of a bullet travelling transversely, slightly obliquely from top to bottom, as in a tearing phenomenon, with no entry or exit wound. The functional prognosis for the testicle is compromised in only 50% of penetrating scrotal wounds[2,9], a figure significant enough to recommend surgical exploration for any penetrating scrotal wound[2]. The significant injury to the right scrotum in our patient exposed the ipsilateral testicle, which was not attached but appeared viable, given its color and the intact spermatic cord. Orchidopexy was therefore performed to prevent subsequent torsion of the spermatic cord. There is no specific therapeutic consensus for ballistic injuries to the external genitalia[10]. However, treatment of ballistic wounds is based on a few principles: wound debridement and initial non-closure; complementary medical treatment (antibiotics, analgesics, and permanganate baths for anorectal wounds)[5]. The surgical procedures performed depend on intraoperative findings. Treatment may be complicated by infections[12], particularly because debridement is limited to preserve and accurately reconstruct the damaged structures. The sequelae may also pose a medico-legal problem post-operatively, regarding the actual cause of any erectile dysfunction and penile curvature during erection, which compromises the patient’s sexual activity[2], due to scarring of the erectile tissue, without, however, compromising the patient’s long-term fertility. In our study, no complications or sequelae were recorded. The postoperative course was uneventful, with the damaged and repaired organs regaining function, consistent with several African studies conducted in resource-limited settings[1,2,6].

Conclusion

Ballistic wounds to the external genitalia are rare but serious, given the poor prognosis for urogenital function. It is a surgical emergency. This underscores the need to assess for urethral, cavernous, and testicular injuries in all cases of firearm-related penetrating wounds to the male external genitalia. This case report demonstrates a positive outcome, both functionally and cosmetically, despite the severity of the injuries, the limited resources, and the technical facilities.

Strengths and limitations

The strengths of this case report lie in the rarity of isolated ballistic trauma to the external genitalia in a child and in the favorable outcome despite the severity of the injury.

However, the limitations include the lack of preoperative imaging facilities, which remains a problem in our disadvantaged areas, as well as the availability and accessibility of these facilities.
