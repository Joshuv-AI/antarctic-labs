Pulmonary toxicity of micro- and nano polyethylene particles following intratracheal instillation in rats
Authors: Hyoung-Yun Han, Seonae Hwangbo, Eun-Jung Park, Se-Myo Park, Mi-Sun Choi, Jung-Hwa Oh, Mi-Jin Yang, Wonkyun Jung, Minjeong Kwak, Tae Geol Lee, Flemming R Cassee, Quan Lu, Seokjoo Yoon
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMicro- and nano-sized polyethylene plastics (PE-MPs and PE-NPs) are emerging as potential risk factors for pulmonary disease and risk assessment. Currently, their is no direct evidence suggests this appreciable health risk extends to humans. Furthermore, the toxicological evaluation of PE-MPs and PE-NPs is insufficient to support their safe use.MethodsThis study was aimed to characterize the toxicity of PE-MPs and PE-NPs after 13 weeks of intratracheal instillation in Sprague–Dawley (SD) rats and to assess the reversibility of any effects during a 4-week recovery period. Exposure levels for PE-MPs and PE-NPs were set at 0, 40, 80, and 120 μg per rat once a week.ResultsThe results indicated that the treatment administration resulted in observable lung tissue alterations. Inflammatory cells were present in the perivascular and peribronchiolar regions in both sexes at ≥ 40 μg per rat in the PE-MPs and PE-NPs-treated groups, indicating a biological response that can turn into toxicity. Additionally, thickened alveolar ducts and alveolar epithelial hyperplasia were noted in males at 120 μg per rat PE-MPs, in males at ≥ 80 μg per rat PE-NPs, and in females at ≥ 40 μg per rat PE-MPs and PE-NPs-treated groups. Interestingly, when instilled repeatedly with both sizes of polyethylene particles, IL-1β and TNF-α secretion was significantly more enhanced in female rats compared to male rats. At the same time, the pulmonary level of IL-6 increased more clearly in male rats than in female rats. The pulmonary level of C-reactive protein, a marker for acute inflammation, was increased in all treated groups.ConclusionThese findings suggest that the lowest-observed-adverse-effect level (LOAEL) for PE-MPs and PE-NPs based on repeated exposures is below 40 μg per rat for both sexes. However, the toxicological data for PE-MPs and PE-NPs remain insufficient to confirm their safety, and further research is necessary to evaluate their potential risks to human health.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12989-026-00688-3.Keywords: Polyethylene micro/nanoplastics, Pulmonary toxicity, Lowest-observed-adverse-effect level, C-reactive protein

Introduction

Plastic is a material extensively used in various contexts, including packaging, transportation, construction, electronics, industrial machinery, and clothing. Its use relies on numerous advantageous characteristics, such as impressive structural strength, durability, insulation, chemical stability, and elasticity. Furthermore, it is cost-effective and showcases excellent processability [1, 2]. Plastic is a highly diverse material, consisting of a wide range of types, materials, and grades. Some of the most recognized examples include acrylic or polymethyl methacrylate (PMMA), polycarbonate (PC), polyethylene (PE), polyester, polypropylene (PP), polystyrene (PS), polyvinyl chloride (PVC), and others [3, 4]. The degradation of plastics can be attributed to several environmental factors, including mechanical wear, ultraviolet radiation, and microbial degradation. These processes can lead to the breakdown of plastics into smaller particles, including macroplastics, microplastics, and nanoplastics [5–7]. The term microplastics refers to particles smaller than 5 mm, while nanoplastics are even tinier, measuring less than 1000 nm. These particles are so small that they can only be seen using specialized microscopes [8]. Both types of particles have been shown to cause a range of toxicological effects [9, 10].

In recent years, there has been a significant increase in the prevalence of these particles, which is now indisputable. They have been identified in various environments, including fresh snowfall in Antarctica and in the depths of the Mariana Trench. Furthermore, these particles have also been found in human biological samples such as blood, breast milk, urine, and tissue from the placenta, lungs, and liver [11–13]. Data from in vitro studies indicate that specific micro- and nano-plastics can promote oxidative stress, inflammation, and apoptosis in endothelial and other vascular cells. Additionally, animal models suggest that micro- and nano-plastics may alter heart rate, impair cardiac function, cause myocardial fibrosis, and lead to endothelial dysfunction [14–17]. Prolonged exposure to these particles has been shown to result in lasting changes to DNA methyltransferases and demethylases, which could impact epigenetic regulation, aging, inflammation, and even benign transformations [18].

A substantial body of experimental evidence from cell and animal studies indicates that the presence of micro- and nano-plastics is linked to a higher prevalence of disease [19].

Moreover, micro- and nano-plastics can enter the body through the skin due to prolonged contact with plastic particles from sources like dust, cosmetics, sanitary pads, and liquid hand cleansers. Researchers are currently investigating this phenomenon [20, 21]. Humans may be exposed to micro- and nano-plastics through inhalation and ingestion. The inhalation of air pollutants into the human body depends on two factors: the concentration of the pollutants and the size of the particles [22, 23]. Considerable research on micro and nano plastics has revealed their potential to cause toxicity in various animal species [24, 25]. However, the health effects of more commonly used plastics, such as polyethylene—which is employed in a wide range of everyday products—have been the subject of much less research. Polyethylene is frequently used to produce beverage bottles, food containers, and certain types of packaging. It is widely accepted for recycling and can be transformed into numerous products, including new bottles and polyester fibers used in making clothing, carpets, and other materials [26, 27]. Therefore, this factor is more closely associated with exposure related to human health and the environment.

Thus, it is critical to evaluate the safety of micro- and nano-sized polyethylene plastic particles (PE-MPs and PE-NPs) by conducting adequate toxicity studies, given the insufficient toxicological information on PE-MPs and PE-NPs to support their safe use. In this regard, the use of PE-MPs and PE-NPs can provide reference data for future studies with different exposure amounts and dosage administration schedules. The objective of the present study was to evaluate the toxicity of intratracheal instillation of PE-MPs and PE-NPs in male and female Sprague Dawley (SD) rats to secure reference data that may be helpful for relevant future studies. Weekly administration of PE-MPs and PE-NPs was conducted over a 13-week period, followed by a four-week recovery phase. The rats were divided into seven groups and subjected to treatment doses of 0, 40, 80, and 120 μg/rat of PE-MPs and PE-NPs, respectively. The effects of PE-MPs and PE-NPs were analyzed through a series of clinical observations, measurements of body weight, and recordings of food consumption and bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) by the rats. To assess the clinical pathology of PE-MPs and PE-NPs, serum chemistry and hematology examinations were performed. Furthermore, gross observations, organ weight measurements, and histopathology were conducted.

Methods

Preparation and physical aging in PE-MPs and PE-NPs

The polyethylene spheres were obtained from Cospheric LLC (CA, USA; CAS No. 9002-88-4). A stock solution of PE-MPs and PE-NPs (10 mg/mL) was thoroughly homogenized in autoclaved drinking water and subsequently diluted serially prior to administration. Particle morphology was obtained using a scanning electron microscope (GeminiSEM 500, Zeiss, Jena, Germany) after the sonication of polyethylene particles.

Polyethylene particle fragments were prepared by treating the surfaces of fluorescent green polyethylene beads with a focused ultrasound system. This ultrasound system (DEBREX, FUST Lab.) utilizes a cylindrical piezoelectric ceramic transducer to focus ultrasonic energy 360 degrees toward the center [28]. When ultrasound is generated, cavitation and collapse processes occur repeatedly, emitting sonoluminescence, including the ultraviolet spectrum. The fluorescent polyethylene beads passed through a strong ultrasonic sound field created at the center. Due to the intense energy from the ultrasonic field, the beads underwent repeated oxidation, resulting in the detachment of surface fragments and the formation of nano- or micro-sized particles [29]. The particle size distributions of fragmented PE-MPs and PE-NPs produced using focused ultrasound are depicted (Supplementary Fig. 1). PE-MPs and PE-NPs dispersion in distilled water is stable more than 5 days. PE particle fragments was suspended in the distilled water and used at concentrations of 40, 80, and 120 μg/mL. The appropriate quantity of the PE was measured and mixed with distilled water to prepare a 120 μg/mL mixture, which was then diluted to prepare the other concentrations. Fresh mixtures were prepared weekly and stored in a container at approximately 5 °C for use. On the day of dosing, the stored dose formulations were sonicated for approximately 10 min before transferred to the animal room and dose formulations were stirred by a vortex mixer while in the animal room (before drawing the dose formulations into a syringe).

Animal and environmental conditions

Fifty-four male and fifty-four female five-week-old SD rats obtained from Orient Bio Co. Ltd. (Korea) were used in this study. The animal room was maintained at a temperature of 23 ± 3 °C, with a relative humidity of 30 to 70%, air ventilation occurring 10 to 20 times per hour, and a light intensity of 150 to 300 Lux. A 12-h light/dark cycle was also employed. During the quarantine and acclimatization periods, five animals were housed per cage, while during the treatment period, two or three animals were housed per cage. The animals were kept in polycarbonate cages (255W × 473L × 200H mm) throughout the study. The pelleted food for experimental animals was sourced from PMI Nutrition International. Tap water was provided to the rats ad libitum, following ultraviolet irradiation and filtration processes.

Experimental design for intratracheal instillation

A total of 108 rats were randomly divided into seven groups (one control group and three treatment groups for PE-MPs and PE-NPs, respectively) using the Pristima system, employing the animals' most recent body weights for this randomization. A total of 108 rats were divided into seven groups, including a control group (n = 10, each sex), and three groups of intratracheal instillation PE-MPs and PE-NPs (low dose: 40 μg/rat (n = 6, each sex), middle dose: 80 μg/rat (n = 6, each sex), high dose: 120 μg/rat (n = 10, each sex)). Dose formulations were administered by intratracheal instillation once per week for 13 consecutive weeks. A weekly intratracheal instillation of PE-MPs and PE-NPs was administered to SD rats for 13 weeks (91 days), followed by a four weeks (28 days) recovery period. The animals of main group were sacrificed at Day 92 and animals of recorvery group were sacrificed at Day 119.

The body weight ranges noted before dosing began were 237.1–271.0 g for males and 165.5–194.9 g for females. This study was performed in facilities approved by the Association for Assessment and Accreditation of Laboratory Animal Care International. The all experiments were reviewed and approved by the Association for the Assessment and Accreditation of Laboratory Animal Care (AAALAC) International and the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of the Korea Institute of Toxicology [Approval no. IAC-23-01-0232-0152] and all experiments were performed according to the ARRIVE guidelines, the guidelines published by the OECD and the GLP regulations for Nonclinical Laboratory Studies of Korea Food Drug Administration. Animal experiments were designed to use all animals for the analysis, except for those that died or suffered from issues related to animal welfare throughout the experiment.

Animal treatment

A total of 108 rats were randomly assigned to seven groups, which included a control group and three groups for intratracheal instillation exposure to PE-MPs and PE-NPs, respectively (low dose: 40 μg/head, middle dose: 80 μg/head, high dose: 120 μg/head, once a week). The dose formulations were administered via intratracheal instillation weekly for a period of 13 consecutive weeks. The animals received the dose at a volume of 60 μl/head. The control group was administered saline (vehicle control) via intratracheal instillation. The animal was anesthetized using isoflurane delivered via an appropriate anesthesia machine to achieve a sufficient depth of anesthesia, ensuring immobility and minimizing distress during the procedure. Once anaesthetized, the rat was removed from the anesthesia machine, and then placed on its back in a head-up position on adjustable stand whose angle was approximately 45° from the horizontal. For this purpose, the upper incisors of the anaesthetized rat were held by a rubber band, which was fixed by two pins on the slant board. The tongue was gently moved to one side of the oral cavity, and then fully extended with small blunt forceps.

Once weekly intratracheal instillation of PE-MPs and PE-NPs was administered to SD rats for 13 weeks, followed by four weeks recovery period. Rats were divided into seven groups and subjected to treatment doses of 0, 40, 80, and 120 μg/rat PE-MPs and PE-NPs, respectively. The effects of the PE-MPs and PE-NPs were analyzed by clinical observation, body weight measurements, and recording of the rats’ food consumption. Serum chemistry and hematology examinations were performed to assess the clinical pathology of PE-MPs and PE-NPs. In addition, during necropsy, gross observation, organ weight and histopathology were performed.

Mortality and body weight

All measurements and examinations were conducted using the Pristima system. The general condition and behavior of the experimental animals were monitored daily throughout the acclimation period. However, during the treatment period and the day prior to the necropsy, clinical signs were examined and recorded twice daily. The animals were weighed on the day of arrival, before randomization, prior to the administration of the first dose, once weekly thereafter, and on the day of necropsy.

Food consumption and ophthalmic examination

Food consumption in cages was recorded once during the acclimation period and weekly throughout the treatment phase. Individual consumption was calculated and expressed in grams per rat per day. An external eye examination was performed on all animals during the acclimation period. However, at week 13, just before the necropsy, both external and fundus examinations of the animals in the control and high-dose groups (120 μg/rat for PE-MPs and PE-NPs) were carried out using a binocular indirect ophthalmoscope (Vantage Plus Digital, Keeler Ltd., England). Before the examination with binocular indirect ophthalmoscopy, a mydriatic agent (Midrin-P; Santen Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd., Japan) was administered to each eye.

Hematology analysis

The animals of main group were sacrificed at Day 92 and animals of recorvery group were sacrificed at Day 119. Before the necropsy, all rats were fasted for about 12 h, during which blood samples were taken. Blood samples were collected from the vena cava under isoflurane anesthesia during necropsy for hematological and clinical chemistry analyses. Samples for hematological analysis were drawn in tubes containing EDTA-2 K. The following parameters were measured using an ADVIA 2120i hematology analyzer (Siemens, USA): white blood cell count, red blood cell count, hemoglobin concentration, hematocrit, mean corpuscular volume, mean corpuscular hemoglobin, mean corpuscular hemoglobin concentration, platelet count, differential leukocyte count (neutrophils, lymphocytes, monocytes, eosinophils, basophils, and large unstained cells), and reticulocyte count. Additionally, blood samples treated with 3.2% sodium citrate were analyzed for prothrombin and activated partial thromboplastin times using an ACL Elite Pro coagulation analyzer (Instrumentation Laboratory, Italy).

Clinical chemistry analysis

Blood samples were collected into tubes without anticoagulants for clinical chemistry analyses. The samples were then allowed to sit at room temperature for at least 90 min and subsequently centrifuged at 3,000 rpm for 10 min at room temperature to separate the serum. Levels of glucose, blood urea nitrogen, creatinine, total cholesterol, total protein, albumin, albumin/globulin ratio, triglycerides, phospholipids, aspartate aminotransferase, alanine aminotransferase, total bilirubin, alkaline phosphatase, gamma-glutamyl transferase, creatine kinase, calcium, inorganic phosphorus, sodium, potassium, and chloride were assessed using an automated analyzer TBA 120FR NEO (Toshiba Corporation, Japan).

Bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) analysis

A total of three rats from each experimental group underwent bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL). The animals were anesthetized with an excessive dose of isoflurane, and blood was collected from the caudal vena cava. The entire lungs were lavaged three times with 30 mL/kg of chilled saline, and the collected BALF was stored in a 15 mL conical tube. The samples were then centrifuged at 2000 rpm for five minutes at a temperature of 4 °C. The cells obtained from each lavage were re-suspended in 1 mL of saline. The total number of cells in the BALF was determined using a NucleoCounter NC-250TM (Chemometec, Allerod, Denmark) with solution 18 (Chemometec, Allerod, Denmark). Cell differentiation was performed on cytospin preparations (Shandon, USA) stained with Diff-Quick staining (Fisher Scientific, USA). Subsequently, the BALF cells (300/rat) were counted differentially under light microscopy based on their cellular morphology, including macrophages, polymorphonuclear leukocytes (PMN), and lymphocytes.

LDH assay

Following the manufacturer’s instructions, BAL fluids were mixed with the LDH reaction mixture (Abcam, Cambridge, UK), and the absorbance was measured using a microplate reader (490 nm, BioTek, Winooski, VT, USA). The relative level of LDH released into the lungs of the treated mice was calculated based on the absorbance value (mean value, 100%) obtained from the BAL fluids of the control mice.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay

Pulmonary levels of inflammation and tissue repair-related mediators were measured using ready-to-use enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay kits for anti-rat C-reactive protein, interleukin (IL)-1β, tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF)-α, IL-6, transforming growth factor (TGF)-β1 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), and chemokine (C-X-C motif) ligand (CXCL)-1 (R&D Systems, MN, USA). All experimental procedures were performed in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions (Park et al., 2022).

Gross findings and histopathological examination

Following the collection of blood samples, the animals were euthanized via exsanguination through the vena cava and aorta under isoflurane anesthesia. A complete necropsy was conducted on all experimental animals. The absolute weights of the brain, pituitary gland, liver, kidneys, spleen, heart, lungs, thymus, salivary gland, thyroid gland, testes, epididymis, prostate, seminal vesicle, uterus, and ovaries were determined, and relative organ weights were calculated as a percentage of the terminal body weight. Following detailed external and internal examinations, tissue samples (skin, mammary gland, testes, epididymis, prostate, seminal vesicle, urinary bladder, ovaries, uterus, vagina, spleen, pancreas, stomach, duodenum, jejunum, ileum, cecum, colon, rectum, kidneys, heart, lungs, adrenal gland, liver, salivary gland, mesenteric lymph node, mandibular lymph node, thyroid gland, aorta, thymus, trachea, tongue, esophagus, sciatic nerve, skeletal muscle, sternum/marrow, femur/joint/marrow, thoracic spinal cord, harderian gland, brain, pituitary gland, and eyes/optic nerve) were collected from each animal and preserved in 10% neutral-buffered formalin. The eyes and optic nerves were fixed in Davidson’s fixative, whereas the testes were fixed in Bouin’s fixative for approximately 48 h and then transferred to 70% ethanol. Preserved tissues were obtained from animals in the control and high-dose groups (120 μg per rat). The liver, lung with bronchi, mediastinal lymph node, spleen, thymus and thrachea were evaluated microscopically in the low and middle-dose groups (40 and 80 μg per rat).

Statistical analysis

The data underwent statistical analysis using various comparison methods. When Bartlett’s test indicated no significant deviations from variance homogeneity, analysis of variance (ANOVA) was employed to identify differences among group means. A p-value (p < 0.05) was considered statistically significant. Dunnett’s test was applied to assess differences between the control and treatment groups when the data were significant according to ANOVA.

Moreover, when significant deviations from variance homogeneity were noted based on Bartlett’s test, a non-parametric comparison test, the Kruskal–Wallis (H) test, was utilized to ascertain any group mean differences (p < 0.05). When a significant difference emerged in the Kruskal–Wallis (H) test, Dunn’s rank sum test was conducted to quantify the specific pairs of group data that significantly differed from the mean. Fisher’s exact test was employed to compare data pairs (including prevalence and percentage). The probability level was set at 1% or 5%. Statistical analyses were carried out by comparing the data for the different dose groups with those of the control group, using the Pristima system.

Results

Characterization of PE-MPs and PE-NPs

The PE-MPs and PE-NPs are illustrated through an SEM image (GeminiSEM 500, Zeiss, Jena, Germany) of polyethylene particles following sonication. The particle size of PE-NPs ranges from approximately 40 to 200 nm, demonstrating a relatively uniform distribution compared to PE-MPs (Figs. 1 and 2). Conversely, the particle size distribution of PE-MPs spans a wide range from 200 nm to 20 µm. For precise analysis, the particle size and shape were evaluated without any surface coating treatment. Since the particles were not merely synthetic beads but were created by scratching the bead surface, the size distribution was considerable. Consequently, the particles removed from the plastic bead surface through sonication were not spherical but rather irregularly shaped fragments. It was confirmed that the PE-MPs suspension produced by sonication exhibited an irregular distribution of several micrometers in size. Meanwhile, the PE-NPs suspension showed a distribution around 200 nm.

Mortality, clinical observations, and body weight

No deaths were attributed to the treatment, and no adverse clinical findings were observed during the study period. The changes in body weight during the treatment period are shown. No treatment-related alterations in body weight were observed among the treatment groups (Supplementary Table 1).

Food consumption, ophthalmic examination, and BALF analysis

No changes or findings in food consumption were observed in any group (Supplementary Table 2). Abnormal ophthalmological findings were not seen in the treated group compared to the vehicle control group (data not shown). No significant changes in the percentage of cells in BALF (macrophages, PMNs, and lymphocytes) were detected between the control and treated group rats (Supplementary Table 3 and Fig. 3).

Hematology and blood chemistry

There were no treatment-related changes in the hematology and blood chemistry (Tables 1 and 2). Noted changes were minimal, not consistent between the sexes, and had no clear dose-dependency. Thus, the hematologic and blood chemistry values were considered within a normal or acceptable range for the biological variation. The results of urinalysis showed no treatment-related changes at any dose level.

Tissue damage and occurrence of acute inflammation

As shown in Fig. 4, the pulmonary level of LDH, which indicates tissue damage, increased in the lungs of rats instilled with different sizes of PE particles (80 and 120 μg) compared to the control group. The secretion of TGF-β, involved in the repair of damaged tissues, also increased noticeably in the lungs of rats exposed to the maximum dose (120 μg) of PE-MPs and PE-NPs. Meanwhile, the pulmonary level of C-reactive protein, which suggests the presence of acute inflammation, waselevated in all treated groups compared to the control. Interestingly, repeated instillation of both sizes of PE particles enhanced TNF-α secretion in female rats more than in male rats, whereas IL-6 levels in the lungs increased more in male rats than in female rats.

Discussion

Polyethylene is the most prevalent plastic in the world and the most common form of waste in the environment [26, 27, 30]. It is used in a wide range of applications, including the production of plastic bottles, milk jugs, grocery bags, shopping bags, and even children's toys [31]. Given its versatility, it is noteworthy that this material has a relatively simple structure, being the simplest among commercial polymers. Various techniques have been utilized to develop products that incorporate polyethylene [1, 2]. Conversely, plastic debris, resulting from the degradation of polyethylene materials due to external factors such as mechanical stress, ultraviolet radiation, and microbial activity, can lead to a variety of unintended consequences. As a result, the potentially harmful effects of micro- and nano-plastics on human health have become a significant concern among toxicologists and regulatory authorities. The safety of these micro- and nano-plastics is increasingly prominent, with a growing number of reports documenting their adverse effects [32–34]. There has been an increase in the prevalence of respiratory diseases linked to the presence of micro- and nano-plastics in airborne particulate matter. Consequently, these materials are emerging as a potential risk factor for pulmonary disease and risk assessment in preclinical studies [11, 13, 35–37].

In the current study, the total number of pulmonary cells increased in rats treated with PE-MPs and PE-NPs (120 μg/lung) compared to the control. Compared to the control, the proportion of neutrophils and eosinophils in the lungs tended to increase following exposure to both sizes of PE plastics, accompanied by a decrease in the proportion of macrophages. Meanwhile, no changes were observed in the proportion of lymphocytes related to the exposure of PE-MPs and PE-NPs. Additionally, the increased level of neutrophils was more pronounced in male rats than in female rats, whereas the increase in eosinophils was more notable in female rats than in male rats. Furthermore, contrary to our expectations, there were no significant differences between the groups treated with PE-MPs and PE-NPs. Similarly, pulmonary levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α, and IL-6) rose following exposure to PE-MPs and PE-NPs, with the elevation of TNF-α and IL-6 being more distinct in female and male rats than in male and female rats, respectively. A previous study suggested that PE-MPs (30.5 ± 10.5 and 6.2 ± 2.0 μm) inhibited cell viability while increasing the production of ROS and NO in six human-derived cell lines (CaCo-2, A549, HACAT, THP-1, U937, and Jurkat cells). While IL-6 and TNF-α levels increased only in HACAT cells exposed to both sizes of PE-MPs and THP-1 cells treated with PE-MPs, the opposite effect was observed in U937 exposed to both sizes of PE-MPs and PE-NPs and THP-1 cells treated with smaller-sized PE-MPs, suggesting a possible organ-specific response to PE-MPs [38]. Lung tissue is composed of various cells that can influence the lung microenvironment, including goblet, ciliated, alveolar type 1 and type 2 epithelial cells, and alveolar fibroblasts [39], while alveolar macrophages recruit various immune cells (neutrophils, eosinophils, and dendritic cells) that inhabit the lung tissue when foreign substances enter [40].

Herein, we hypothesize that sex may be an important factor affecting the defense mechanism against inhaled PE-MPs and that the size-dependent effect will be diminished by the strong aggregation between the plastic particles. Inhalation can lead to continuous lung tissue damage from foreign substances. Similarly, some researchers have suggested that particle size (or diameter) functions are a key factor in determining particle-induced adverse health effects by affecting the accumulation level, accumulation region, and clearance rate in the lung. Increasing data have also showed that biological activity of PE may depend on size state of the PE-MPs and PE-NPs [41, 42]. Inflammation plays a role in the healing process to repair the damaged tissue, with phagocytic cells being central to this inflammatory response. In this study, we observed that LDH and CRP levels were elevated in the lungs of rats instilled repeatedly for 13 weeks with both sizes of PE-MPs. LDH levels serve as an indicator of non-specific tissue damage [43, 44].

For instance, LDH concentration markedly increased in the lungs of rats that repeatedly inhaled nanosized alumina particles and hydrogen chloride gas mixtures, indicating lung tissue injury, which was accompanied by an increase in both the proportion of pulmonary neutrophils and the levels of TNF-α, and GRO/KC [45]. Additionally, CRP has been used to monitor inflammatory lesions, particularly in cardiovascular diseases, for several decades. Growing evidence has also shown that CRP plays a crucial role in both the clearance of apoptotic and necrotic cell debris by phagocytes (macrophages) and the host response to inflammatory cytokine stimulation, especially IL-6 and TNF-α [46–50].

Moreover, the production of IL-1β and the formation of inflammasomes are linked to its upstream signaling [51, 52]. Herein, we hypothesize that IL-6 and TNF-α may be associated with CRP production in response to IL-1β secretion. In this study, hyperplasia of mucous cells and alveolar epithelial cells was observed in the lung tissue of rats exposed to both sizes of PE-MPs and PE-NPs for 90 days, while it was absent in the lung tissues of rats in the recovery groups. Furthermore, the pulmonary level of TGF-β, which is involved in the development of fibrotic lesions, was significantly elevated only in female rats exposed to the maximum dose of PE-MPs for 90 days, and the increase in pulmonary LDH levels following exposure to PE plastics persisted only in females from the recovery group of PE-MPs. Hyperplasia is a numerical proliferation of cells accompanied by an inflammatory response in the clearance process of foreign substances [53] and can also occur due to growth factor stimuli [54]. A recent study suggested that inhaled detergent could induce epithelial hyperplasia and tissue eosinophilia by stimulating the expression of IL-33 protein [55], contributing to tissue repair [56]. If the stimuli are removed, hyperplastic cells undergo apoptosis with the resolution of inflammation; if the stimuli persist, hyperplastic cells may develop into cancer or fibrosis. Therefore, we propose that the inflammatory response induced by PE plastics may be reversible if exposure ceases, but chronic exposure could lead to chronic lung disease.

The deposited doses used in this study represent the fraction of PE-MPs or PE-NPs that reach and remain on the surface of biological tissues after exposure. This metric is more relevant to human health than the administered dose because, in real-world exposure scenarios, only a small percentage of inhaled or ingested particles adhere to human tissues; the rest are exhaled, cleared, or eliminated [57, 58]. Expressing the experimental exposure as a deposited dose allows us to approximate the amount of particles that human tissues would realistically encounter under typical environmental or occupational conditions [59]. This allows for a better comparison between experimental concentrations and estimated human exposure ranges and helps determine whether the tested doses fall within environmentally relevant levels, exceed them, or represent high-dose conditions intended to reveal toxicity mechanisms. The study showed that females exhibited different responses to males. However, as the same dose was administered to both sexes, females with lower body weights may have effectively received a higher dose per kilogram of body weight, which could have contributed to the observed differences. This factor should therefore be considered and investigated further in future studies.

In conclusion, considering the current experimental conditions involving intratracheal instillation in SD rats, the lowest-observed-adverse-effect level of PE-MPs and PE-NPs is estimated to be below 40 μg/rat for both males and females. We suggest that the IL-6/CRP and TNF-α/CRP pathways may be related to the inflammatory response triggered in the lungs of male and female rats, respectively, exposed to PE plastics for 90 days. Additionally, a significant size effect may be diminished because of the strong aggregation of PE plastic particles.
