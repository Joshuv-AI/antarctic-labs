Effect of autonomous motivation support on obesity prevention in early adolescents: a study protocol of cluster-randomized controlled trial based on the integrated SDT-TPB framework
Authors: Jing-qi Kong, Jing Chen, Yang Yang, Yi-hang Yang, Jing-yao Liu, Run-ze Hu, Jian-ying Li, Xiao-rui Zhang, Zheng Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe prevalence of obesity among early adolescents continues to rise, yet most existing interventions demonstrate only short-term efficacy. Children in early adolescence have increasingly strong autonomy, and enhancing their autonomous motivation holds promise for the long-term maintenance of healthy behavior, and further research is urgently needed.MethodsBased on an integral framework of Self-Determination Theory and Theory of Planned Behavior (SDT-TPB), this project will conduct a cluster-randomized controlled trial. This trial will compare an autonomous motivation support group against a control group, with four follow-up assessments over 2 years. We will examine between-group differences in autonomous motivation, health behaviors, and adiposity outcomes to evaluate the intervention’s immediate and long-term maintenance effects. Unlike previous approaches that focused primarily on behaviors, this study specifically targets the motivational drivers of behavior change.DiscussionConsidering the developmental characteristic of early adolescents and theoretical framework of SDT and TPB, this school-based cluster-randomized controlled trial is expected to elucidate the complete pathway from motivation to behavioral change. We anticipate that the findings will help overcome a key bottleneck in pediatric obesity interventions and advance strategies for chronic disease prevention and control.Trial registrationICH GCP NCT06547567. Registered on 7 August 2024

Introduction

From 1990 to 2022, the worldwide prevalence of obesity among school-aged children and adolescents increased dramatically, rising from 1.7% to 6.9% in girls and from 2.1% to 9.3% in boys [1]. In China, the prevalence of overweight and obesity among children has continued to grow in the past three decades [2, 3], with a particularly high obesity prevalence among those aged 10–14 years (i.e., early adolescents) [4]. Obesity not only adversely affects the physical and mental health in childhood, but also elevates the risk of chronic metabolic diseases in adulthood [5–8]. Effective prevention of childhood obesity is urgently needed [9, 10].

Although existing interventions can be effective for childhood obesity control, their long-term impact is often limited. Most children struggle to maintain healthy behaviors and experience weight rebound, presenting a major bottleneck in intervention efforts [11–15]. A key limitation of many current approaches is their primary focus on proximal behavioral factors with insufficient attention to the underlying motivational drivers of behavioral change [12], leading to short-term effects [16]. However, the interventions that respect adolescents and leverage their heightened sensitivity to status can effectively motivate sustained behavioral change. By aligning long-term healthy choices with the immediate psychological rewards of social respect and elevated status, such approaches have the potential to evoke internal drivers of motivation, thereby fostering lasting positive outcomes [17].

Autonomous motivation support, a concept proposed by the Self-Determination Theory (SDT), is crucial for long-term maintenance of healthy behaviors in early adolescents. SDT, a theory of human behavioral motivation first proposed in the 1980 s, posits that the degree of autonomy behind an action is a key determinant of behavioral persistence. The theory emphasizes the role of self-determination in motivation and considers the influence of the external environment on self-determination [18, 19]. Depending on the degree of self-determination, motivation is categorized as autonomous and controlled motivation. Autonomous motivation means that children’s behavior is driven by their personal will with a high degree of autonomy, while controlled motivation means that children’s behavior is driven by the external environmental pressure with a low degree of autonomy [18, 19]. Autonomous motivation fosters long-term health behaviors, whereas controlled motivation can only affect in the short term [12, 19–21]. The more an individual is in the later maintenance phase of weight control behaviors, such as exercise, the more autonomous and self-determined their motivation is [22]. Within an autonomy-supportive environment, controlled motivations can be internalized, shifting toward more autonomous regulation [23], enabling children to transition from passive compliance to active self-regulation.

Although autonomous motivation is key to behavioral change, it does not always translate directly into action [24]. Behavioral intention, the pathway between autonomous motivation and improved obesity-based behaviors, is a construct of the Theory of Planned Behavior (TPB). The core idea of TPB is that the most direct determinant of whether a behavior occurs or not is behavioral intention, which is under the influence of behavioral attitudes, subjective norms, and perceived behavioral control [25]. In the context of weight control, this means that children are more likely to form strong intentions to adopt healthy behaviors when they have positive attitudes, perceive social support from parents and teachers, and feel confident in their ability to succeed [26, 27]. Children’s behavioral intentions can explain and predict eating behaviors well, such as sugar intake, eating breakfast and food choices, as well as exercise behaviors [28–32].

Behavioral intentions and autonomous motivation are closely related in explaining changes in health behavior [33]. It was found that those with higher autonomous motivation have a stronger behavioral intention to improve vegetable and fruit intake, snack intake and breakfast eating behaviors, and actually improve these behaviors at a higher rate [34]. This pathway, whereby autonomous motivation promotes behavior change by strengthening behavioral intentions, is also well-established in physical activity research [35]. A systematic review indicated that autonomous motivation is difficult to directly predict obesity-related behaviors, while the inclusion of behavioral intention as an intermediate variable better explains the pathway [33].

Motivation serves as the foundational driver to behavioral change [12], while family and school environments act as indirect drivers by shaping this motivation. Early adolescence, characterized by physiological maturation and an emerging need for independence and autonomy, represents a special developmental window [36]. Nonetheless, a notable gap exists in empirical evidence, particularly from intervention studies, regarding the effects of autonomous motivation support on obesity in early adolescents [37, 38]. The integrated SDT-TPB framework, which can elucidate the complete pathway from motivation to behavior, remains underexplored in this specific population and cultural context.

This study aims to evaluate the efficacy of autonomous motivation support for early adolescents. Specifically, we will assess its impact on enhancing autonomous motivation, improving obesity-related behaviors, and tracking the maintenance of these healthy behaviors over a two-year period.

Methods

Theoretical framework

The pathway from autonomous motivation to improved obesity-related behaviors is multifactorial. SDT primarily addresses upstream motivational factors, while TPB focuses on downstream factors that directly influence behavior. Given the complementary nature of SDT and TPB and the theoretical plausibility of their integration [33, 35, 39], this study proposes a motivation-behavior pathway based on the integrated framework of SDT and TPB (Fig. 1). The framework integrates SDT and TPB. Autonomous motivation support from school teachers, family members, and peers is hypothesized to increase children’s autonomous motivation. Increased motivational autonomy, together with TPB constructs, including behavioral attitudes, subjective norms, and perceived behavioral control, may strengthen behavioral intention. Strengthened behavioral intention may promote improvement in obesity-related behaviors, and support the maintenance of healthy behaviors. Study design

This is a cluster-randomized controlled trial designed to evaluate the effects of an autonomous motivation support intervention on enhancing autonomous motivation, improving obesity-related behaviors, and assessing its sustainability over time. The students will be recruited from multiple primary schools in China and will be 10–14 years old. Students will be randomly allocated to an autonomous motivation support group or a control group, receiving a 6-month intervention and 4 follow-up surveys over 2 years. The analysis will include stratified evaluations based on gender, age, and personality characteristics to identify intervention effectiveness on children with different characteristics. This study protocol was prepared in accordance with the SPIRIT guidelines [40]. The participant timeline of this cluster randomized controlled intervention trial is shown in Table 1. Sample size estimation

Sample size was calculated based on an intra-cluster correlation coefficient (ρ = 0.01) and an average cluster size of 45, using a cluster randomized trial formula. With the stage of behavioral change and obesity as primary outcomes, a baseline maintenance rate of 25% and obesity rate of 24% were assumed. A post-intervention difference of 15% in maintenance stage and 12% in obesity rate was expected between groups. Given α = 0.05, 6 schools per group, and 50 students per school, a total sample size of 600 (12 schools) yields statistical power of 88% and 86% for the two outcomes, respectively. This calculation incorporates an anticipated informed consent and response rate of over 90%.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Firstly, we will select schools based on school inclusion and exclusion criteria. Second, we will focus on fourth-grade classes. This grade level was chosen because students possess sufficient literacy to comprehend the health education materials, they will remain within the primary school system for the duration of the two-year follow-up, and their age range typically falls within the early adolescent period (10–14 years) from enrollment until the final survey. Randomization process

After the baseline survey, a stratified cluster randomization method will be used to stratify the schools by geographic district (urban/suburban). In each stratum, the schools will be randomized in a 1:1 ratio into either the autonomous motivation support group or the control group, with six schools assigned to each group. The centralized randomization procedure will be performed by an independent statistician not involved in the project’s implementation.

Blinding

Considering the nature of the school-based behavioral intervention, students, teachers and intervention providers cannot be blinded to group allocation. Therefore, only outcome assessors and data analysts will be blinded to group allocation. Group allocation will be coded before data analysis so that data analysts remain blinded to group assignment.

Intervention components

The intervention primarily consists of group-based motivational intervention, individual motivational intervention, and home-school collaboration. The group-based component is delivered to all children in the intervention group, whereas the individual component is targeted specifically at children who show a poor response in autonomous motivation enhancement.

For the group-based Motivational Intervention, both the autonomous motivation support group and the control group will maintain their routine teaching activities and receive knowledge and skills related to obesity through standard health education classes, and increase physical activity during physical education classes. The critical distinction for the autonomous motivation support group is that their teachers (including headteachers, health education teachers, and physical education teachers) will receive specialized training to employ autonomy-supportive teaching strategies. The training curriculum encompasses three key modules: (1) Theoretical background introduction, including explaining the core ideas of SDT and clarifying the definitions and differences between autonomous and controlled motivation through interactive exercises; (2) specific strategies of autonomy-supportive teaching, and the specific implementation methods demonstrated through case studies; (3) teaching practice, allowing participating teachers to carry out teaching exercises through role plays to practically apply the above strategies.

For the individual motivational intervention, a motivational interviewing team will be convened. This multidisciplinary team will include teachers, health psychology professionals, nutritionists, the child, and their parents. This team will collaboratively develop a personalized interview plan, and conduct child-centered interviews. Each intervention cycle will span approximately 1 month, typically involving 4–5 sessions per week, with each session lasting 20–30 min.

In the home-school collaboration part, parents of children in the autonomous motivation support group will participate in training sessions organized by the school. These sessions will primarily focus on strategies for fostering autonomous motivation regarding dietary behaviors. Parents will be encouraged to offer their children autonomous food choices from healthy menus and to involve them in meal preparation during weekends.

Survey instruments

All participants will undergo a standardized physical examination conducted by uniformly trained investigators, who will measure and record height, weight, waist circumference, hip circumference, systolic blood pressure, diastolic blood pressure, and body fat percentage. With the exception of body fat percentage, all anthropometric and blood pressure measurements will be taken twice, and the average value will be used for analysis. Then we will calculate BMI (kg/m2) = weight (kg)/height (m)2. The World Health Organization (WHO) growth curves for school-age children will be used to calculate gender-corrected and age-corrected BMI z-scores to determine whether a person was overweight, obese, or severely obese. Waist-to-height ratio (waist/height) and waist-to-hip ratio (waist/hip) will be calculated to determine central obesity.

A standardized questionnaire was developed for this study through a process of literature review, expert consultation, and pre-testing, with subsequent refinements based on the target population’s characteristics. This questionnaire will be administered to both children and their primary caregivers. The questionnaires will be collected through self-report. Students will complete the questionnaires independently in the classroom under the supervision of trained staff, who will provide standardized instructions and explain the purpose and requirements of the survey. The instrument comprises four sections: (1) General demographic information (school, grade, age, ethnicity, gender, etc.) and environmental factors associated with obesity at home and school; (2) behavioral assessments including eating in the previous week, and physical activity; (3) TPB-related variables; (4) SDT-related variables; (5) psychological well-being. Qualitative interviews will be conducted both at the intervention formulation stage and at the post-intervention stage. The main purpose of the qualitative interviews at the intervention formulation stage is to uncover the needs of the target population in order to identify specific intervention goals and targets. The qualitative interviews at the post-intervention stage will be conducted to evaluate implementation fidelity and subjects’ compliance and the facilitating and hindering factors of the intervention. The number of people to be interviewed was determined based on the principle of information saturation. Statistical analysis

This study will use the Intention-to-treat (ITT), i.e., all schools that have been randomized into clusters and all eligible students will be included in the analysis. Effectiveness indicators for multiple follow-ups will be analyzed using mixed-effects models with the individual as the analysis unit, taking into account the clustering within the whole cluster (school) and among repeated-measures observations. Baseline survey measurements of children’s age, sex, and study outcome will all be corrected in these models. The missing values are assumed to conform to a random distribution, which will be handled by maximum likelihood estimation. Also, sensitivity analyses will be performed after filling in the missing values by multiple imputation. For continuous variables, this study will report model-corrected differences in group means. For categorical variables, model-corrected OR will be reported; 95% confidence intervals and p-values will be calculated for all effect indicators. All analyses are to be conducted using R statistical software.

A stratified analysis will be performed on the basis of the individual characteristics of the children found to influence the motivation-behavior pathway. The effects of the interventions in different strata will first be described and then compared. Interaction analyses will be adopted to test whether the coefficients of the stratification factors and the Group product term are zero in the regression model, thus analyzing whether these stratification factors moderated the intervention effect or the sustainability of the effect.

Quality control

To ensure a high quality of the intervention implementation, multiple strategies will be conducted in stages of this study. Firstly, in the design stage, the measurement tool will be selected through a comprehensive literature search, and the questionnaire and interview outline will be designed and discussed, revised and pre-tested by experts. Secondly, in the preparatory stage, all staff will join in the unified training, and only after passing the examination can they take up their work. Thirdly, in the implementation phase, the staff should monitor participation rate and length of participation. If low compliance is detected, they should actively communicate with children and parents to improve their compliance. Before each survey, precautions to be taken during the survey process will be emphasized to the staff to ensure that the questionnaires or record sheets are correctly filled out. Fourthly, in the evaluation stage, survey data will be entered by specialists, and the investigators selected 20% of the questionnaires for double-entry review, with an allowable error of no more than 1%, and all the errors found during the review will be corrected. For interview data, expert opinions will be taken and in-depth analyses will be conducted, combining with the results of quantitative analyses.

Discussion

Childhood obesity is a significant public health challenge in China. Based on the integrated framework of SDT and TPB, our study aims to promote autonomous motivation and improve obesity-related behaviors and long-term maintenance of healthy behaviors in early adolescents. The results of the study will provide a scientific basis for the establishment of a set of intervention programs that fit the characteristics of early adolescents in China and can sustainably improve childhood obesity.

Previous studies have indicated that early adolescence is a critical window for intervention targeting obesity-related behaviors. Important evidence for school-based obesity prevention interventions among children and adolescents have shown effectiveness, particularly in improving obesity-related behaviors and weight-related outcomes in the short term [11, 13, 14, 16]. Other studies have also reported beneficial effects on cardiorespiratory fitness, self-efficacy, weight perception, and health behavior change [26, 27, 66]. However, the magnitude of these effects varies across studies, and the long-term sustainability of intervention effects remains limited or uncertain [16]. Furthermore, studies focusing on motivation have shown promise in promoting health behavior change and weight management. These studies suggested that autonomous motivation is associated with more physical activity, health behavior change, and long-term weight control [12, 24, 27, 37, 38]. However, most existing interventions primarily focused on behavior change or environmental support, while less attention has been paid to the motivational pathway underlying long-term behavior maintenance.

In order to break through the bottleneck of childhood obesity interventions and to promote preadolescent children to improve obesity-related behaviors, our research protocol has the following features:Designing an autonomous motivation support intervention according to the psychological characteristics of early adolescents;Mining the complete pathway of action of autonomy motivation leading to the improvement of obesity-related behaviors based on SDT and TPB;Exploring the differentiated pathways of autonomous motivation leading to behavioral change in different environments, behaviors and individuals, respectively, providing ideas for designing refined intervention strategies in the future;Evaluating both the immediate intervention effects after the baseline survey and long-term sustainability on the maintenance of healthy behaviors at 12 months, 18 months and 24 months after the baseline survey.
