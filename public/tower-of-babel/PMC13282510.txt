Commentary on ‟Frozen embryo transfer outcomes in patients with prior cesarean delivery: a focus on endometrial lining parameters”
Authors: Amit Jaiswal
Published: 2026

We commend Clarke et al. (1) delivering one of the largest multicenter analyses to date examining frozen embryo transfer (FET) outcomes after prior cesarean delivery and, at face value, offer reassurance: endometrial thickness at final lining assessment does not differ between cesarean and vaginal cohorts. Yet the true significance of their findings lies not in what was confirmed, but in what was unsettled. The data reveal a persistent reduction in live birth, even in single euploid transfers, despite structurally ‟adequate” linings (1). In doing so, this study exposes a critical vulnerability in our field’s long-standing dependence on endometrial thickness as a surrogate for receptivity.

Across >11,000 FET cycles, mean endometrial thickness exceeded 10 mm in both groups, cancellation rates for thin lining were indistinguishable, and yet live birth remained significantly lower among patients with prior cesarean delivery (1). In the euploid subset, where embryonic aneuploidy is largely controlled, the decrement persisted. This dissociation between structural sufficiency and reproductive efficiency demands a conceptual recalibration. If implantation and live birth can diverge from thickness within the conventional ‟optimal” range, thickness cannot reasonably function as a comprehensive marker of implantation competence.

Large registry analyses have shown that extremely thin linings correlate with poorer outcomes (2), but within the mid-to-high physiologic range, predictive resolution is weak. Clarke et al. (1) reinforce this limitation. A lining that measures 10 mm may be morphologically intact yet biologically compromised. The implication is not that thickness is irrelevant, but that it is permissive rather than determinative. In contemporary assisted reproductive technology (ART) practice, where the vast majority of patients achieve acceptable thickness thresholds, the more clinically relevant question is not whether the lining is thick enough, but whether it is functionally synchronized, immunologically tolerant, and biomechanically receptive.

The elevated rate of FET cancellation because of intracavitary fluid in the cesarean cohort further sharpens this point (1). Although absolute rates were low, the adjusted relative risk exceeded twofold. Intracavitary fluid is frequently linked to cesarean scar defects (isthmocele), and prior work has proposed multiple mechanisms by which niche formation may impair implantation, including altered peristalsis, local inflammation, and abnormal endometrial remodeling (3, 4). However, scar morphology was not phenotyped in the present study (1). ‟Prior cesarean” therefore represents a heterogeneous exposure encompassing diverse surgical techniques, healing responses, and peripartum contexts. Without structural stratification of the scar, the biologic gradient remains obscured.

What emerges instead is the possibility that cesarean delivery constitutes a systems-level uterine perturbation rather than a focal endometrial injury. Hypotheses extend beyond thickness: altered myometrial contractility (3), disrupted uterine peristalsis (4), and persistent microbial or inflammatory shifts within scar tissue (5). These mechanisms would not necessarily manifest as thinning. They would influence embryo apposition, implantation timing, and early placentation—subtle disturbances capable of reducing live birth without increasing overt pregnancy loss, a pattern reflected in the study’s comparable miscarriage rates (1).

The euploid subgroup analysis is particularly instructive. By minimizing embryonic chromosomal confounding, it strengthens the inference that uterine factors contribute to the observed difference (1). Yet even here, interpretation requires caution. The cohort consists exclusively of patients who previously achieved a live birth via ART and subsequently returned for FET. This design appropriately standardizes reproductive history but also introduces a form of survivor selection. Women with more severe cesarean-associated impairment may not represent for ART or may not meet criteria for further treatment. The measured effect, approximately a 3%–5% absolute difference in live birth, may therefore represent a conservative estimate of the true biologic impact.

The clinical magnitude is modest, but modest effects in high-frequency exposures carry population consequence. Cesarean delivery rates exceed 30% in the United States and are rising globally. If cesarean delivery confers even a small decrement in subsequent implantation efficiency, the cumulative implications for ART utilization, cost, and patient counseling are substantial. Prior investigations have reported similar associations between cesarean history and reduced in vitro fertilization success (3, 4), and Clarke et al. (1) consolidate these signals within a large contemporary FET cohort. The consistency across studies strengthens the plausibility of a biologic relationship, even as mechanism remains unresolved.

The risk, however, lies in misframing the contribution of this study as reassurance that ‟thickness is unaffected.” That interpretation narrows its intellectual reach. The more consequential message is that structural adequacy does not guarantee functional competence. The field has long leaned on millimetres as a proxy for implantation readiness. Clarke et al. (1) provide compelling evidence that this proxy is insufficient. Moving forward, investigation must extend beyond static measurement toward dynamic and molecular characterization of the postcesarean uterus, scar phenotyping, contractility mapping, transcriptomic profiling, and microbiome analysis (3, 5).

In this sense, the study’s most enduring impact may be methodological rather than descriptive. It challenges reproductive medicine to reconsider what constitutes meaningful endometrial assessment. If implantation efficiency can decline in the presence of a thick lining, then our clinical algorithms require refinement. Cesarean delivery may not thin the lining, but it may recalibrate the uterine environment in ways our current metrics fail to detect.

Clarke et al. (1) have produced a rigorous and carefully adjusted analysis demonstrating that prior cesarean delivery does not impair endometrial thickness yet is associated with reduced live birth after FET. The editorial question is not whether the effect size is dramatic; it is whether the conceptual implication is durable. By exposing the gap between morphology and function, this work invites a shift from structural reassurance to functional inquiry. That shift, if embraced, has the potential to influence both investigative priorities and clinical counseling in reproductive medicine.
