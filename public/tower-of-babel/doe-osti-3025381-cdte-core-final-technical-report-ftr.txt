3. Executive Summary:
CdTe is presently the cost-leading thin-film PV technology, directly competing with Si
at scale, even when domestically manufactured. While an impressive technology, its
efficiency remains much below the detailed balance limit with the largest cause due to
its low photovoltage and fill factor. To realize gains, the carrier concentration, minority
carrier lifetime, and interface recombination all need to be improved simultaneously
over historic levels. Using a new defect chemistry (group V doping instead of copper)
has been identified as a viable route using single crystal systems. This project
focused on implementing this new defect chemistry in scalable, polycrystalline thin-
film photovoltaic CdTe devices with tasks focusing improvements to the front
interface, absorber, and rear interface as well as capability development &
stakeholder engagement.

The goal of the project was to establish a strategy using devices, test structures,
detailed characterization, and modeling to quantify the sources of losses in state-of-the-
art CdTe photovoltaic devices. Using this strategy and advanced synthesis, losses at
the front interface, absorber, and rear interface were worked on in parallel. The final
objective was to significantly improve the voltage deficit in CdTe devices to enable
improvements in photovoltage and efficiency that can be implemented by industry in the
near-term.

Over the course of the project, the team developed new characterization techniques,
analysis, and modeling which were then applied to state-of-the-art materials generated
internally and collaboratively. In particular to enable rapid progress, NLR worked
closely with First Solar where NLR grew complete devices as well as ones that
interleaved process steps where First Solar had completed different steps such as
absorber growth or absorber growth and activation using their baseline methods. Using
detailed characterization and analysis including photoemission, photoluminescence,
and scanning probe techniques enabled understanding of the loss pathways and area
for improvements in our own and First Solar’s materials. Ultimately, this contributed to
the first series of new world record CdTe efficiencies since 2016, culminating in a 23.1%
certified cell that was P-doped along with As-doped cells of similar performance.
Internally, NLR improved the statistical variation in baseline As-doped devices and
improved average photovoltage by over 100 mV. This was done through an
improvement in absorber quality, changed front interface, and improved back contact.

In addition to materially improving the fabrication processes at NLR, characterization,
analysis, and modeling were developed and disseminated. NLR also played a pivotal
role in community building over the course of this project working closely with the
Cadmium Telluride Accelerator Consortium. NLR worked in a series of collaborations
with academic and industry partners, leveraging knowledge and innovations from this
project, as well as helped organize a series of workshops to ensure rapid progress in
the field. Working closely with the academic community has led to a dissemination of
knowledge; working with First Solar as increased US competitiveness – First Solar
expanded domestic production to ~10 GW and opened new facilities.

5. Background:

Cadmium telluride (CdTe)-based thin-film solar cell technology has emerged as the
leading commercialized thin-film photovoltaic (PV) technology due to its low-cost
production, relatively high efficiencies, and reduced environmental footprint compared to
other PV technologies. Scarpulla et al. (2023), which was produced in collaboration with
this project, reviews the progress and critical developments in CdTe PV technology over
the past decade, identifies current challenges, and highlights future research directions
necessary for achieving higher module efficiencies and broader deployment in the
global energy market.

Recent Advances
Improved Efficiency and Manufacturing Scale:
In the last decade, the efficiency of CdTe solar modules has steadily risen, with record
laboratory cell efficiencies exceeding 22% and commercial module efficiencies reaching
19%. First Solar, the leading manufacturer, has achieved significant milestones, with
projected module production expected to surpass 21 GW per year by 2024. These
advancements have been driven by innovations in material deposition technologies like
vapor transport deposition (VTD) and optimizing manufacturing processes to enhance
consistency and scalability [1], [2]

Alloying with Selenium:
A transformative innovation in CdTe solar cells has been the introduction of selenium
(Se) into the CdTe lattice to create Cd(Se,Te) alloys. The alloying reduces the bandgap,
enhancing light absorption in the longer wavelength region and improving the short-
circuit current density (Jsc). Selenium also passivates grain boundaries, boosting
minority carrier lifetimes to over 100 ns and enabling better voltage and current
collection [3], [4].

Introduction of Group V Dopants:
Historically, copper (Cu) has been the primary dopant used in CdTe devices to enhance
p-type conductivity. However, Cu doping has shown long-term stability issues due to its
high mobility within the absorber and low carrier concentrations (low to mid 1014 cm-3). A
switch to group V dopants like arsenic (As) has led to stable doping concentrations in
the 1016 cm-3 range, higher open-circuit voltages (Voc) exceeding 850 mV, and reduced
degradation in commercial modules [5], [6].

Advanced Passivation Techniques:
The CdCl2 treatment step remains critical to CdTe device production, as it recrystallizes
grains, passivates defects at grain boundaries, and improves carrier lifetimes. Further
improvements have been made by incorporating co-passivants such as Se and by
optimizing annealing conditions. These processes minimize interfacial recombination
and stabilize the device's structure [2], [7]

Key Challenges
Voltage Deficit and the Front Contact:
Although CdTe devices exhibit respectable cell efficiencies, the difference between the

 Page 5 of 50

theoretical and practical open-circuit voltage remains significant (~250 mV at the start of
this project). This gap arises from non-radiative recombination losses associated with
defects at the absorber interfaces, grain boundaries, and bulk materials. As a result,
further work is required to tune band alignment at front and back interfaces and reduce
bandgap fluctuations [8], [9].

Back Contact Challenges:
Historically, establishing a stable, low-resistance, and passivated back contact has been
a persistent issue. Past strategies relied on Cu-doped ZnTe or Te-rich back contacts,
but these often resulted in increased recombination and device instabilities. Emerging
strategies focus on Cu-free back contacts and passivation layers such as Al2O3 [10].

Future Prospects
To reach the next milestone in cell efficiencies (>25%) and enable broader adoption,
several research priorities have emerged:
1. Reduction of Non-Radiative Recombination Losses: Further studies must focus on
 reducing intragrain and grain boundary recombination by introducing deep-level
 defect passivation techniques.
2. Improved Contacts and Interface Engineering: The development of carrier-selective,
 Ohmic, and passivated electrodes at both front and rear contacts is essential for
 achieving higher Voc values.
3. Integration with Other Technologies: The advent of bifacial and semi-transparent
 CdTe modules offers new opportunities for their use in building-integrated PV and
 agrivoltaics systems [12].

Conclusion

CdTe photovoltaics continues to make significant contributions to the energy sector,
with advances in material science, manufacturing, and device designs pushing the limits
of performance and scale. The ability to address remaining challenges such as voltage
losses, back contact stability, and material constraints will determine the role of CdTe
PV in helping address future grid-scale energy generation needs. With continued
research, it is possible to achieve economically viable global energy solutions at a
meaningful scale through CdTe technology.

6. Project Objectives:

The goal of this project is to establish a strategy using devices, test structures, detailed
characterization, and modeling to quantify the sources of losses in state-of-the-art CdTe
photovoltaic devices. Using this strategy and advanced synthesis, losses at the front
interface, absorber, and rear interface will be addressed in parallel. The final objective
is to significantly improve the voltage deficit in CdTe devices to enable improvements in
photovoltage and efficiency that can be implemented by industry in the near-term.

Task 1 - Improving the Front Interface and Contact Properties
 The primary goals of this task are to provide a detailed understanding of front
 interfaces in state-of-the art structures/devices (from NLR and industry), validate

 Page 6 of 50

 existing models of front interface voltage loss, and mitigate front interface
 recombination through passivation strategies and emitter engineering. This task will
 also generate samples for, and utilize analysis in, Task 4 which will distinguish front
 interface recombination losses from other pathways. Some topics this task will
 address are:
 • Test our existing models ability to describe the necessary requirements for a
 good front interface (emitter doping, band alignment, surface recombination
 velocity)
 • Identify and screen materials for optimal band alignment, passivation, charge
 extraction, and stability. Options to be explored include 2D materials, selective
 contacts, and tunable emitters.
 • Test our ability to predict front interface evolution using a thermodynamic model
 to aid in the identification of front interface materials compatible with superstrate
 device process flows
 • Determine if group V accumulation at the front interface is beneficial, benign, or
 deleterious
 • Examine structures/measurements to separate front interface losses from the
 bulk and rear.

Detailed Description:
This task’s first goal is to experimentally test front interface modeling which has shown
VOC > 1 V can be achieved with proper band alignment and emitter carrier density, even
with SRV 105 cm/s.1 For this, characterization of the front interface – band alignment,
carrier density, SRV, chemical makeup, defect density, crystal structure – will be critical.
Techniques that will be explored include XPS, AES, cross-sectional KPFM, Hall, TRPL,
ERE, CL, and XRD/GIXRD. Using the cleave technique developed at NLR, gives direct
access to the front interface (very near to or directly at the emitter/absorber interface).
This allows characterization both before and after device processing to give important
insight into the evolution (stability) of the emitter. By reconstructing the emitter on top of
the cleaved absorber surface, material properties can be carefully controlled and
modeling can be tested more directly. The challenge will be to grow the emitter at
conditions (temperature, ambient) which do not negatively affect the absorber/junction,
e.g., via formation of a thick oxide, sputter damage, or adsorption/desorption of species.
By comparing structures across different institutions, we hope to better correlate interface
properties with voltage loss.

To validate the modeling and minimize voltage loss from improper band alignment and
emitter doping, it will be important to have the ability to tune emitter properties. MZO will
be used as a reference emitter as it offers tunable band offsets, but it also has issues with
stability and doping. GMZO and Sn-Zr-O may address this issue and will also be explored.
Device modeling will be used to guide the choice of material. If it is particularly challenging
to obtain optimal properties in a single layer, multi-layer stacks can also be developed
which have an ultra-thin interface layer with appropriate band alignment and a thicker
buffer layer with high carrier density. As experiments progress, other candidate materials
will be identified (possibly borrowing from the CIGS and perovskite communities) and
their stability will be evaluated separately. This work will require ample growth

 Page 7 of 50

optimization in order to attribute any differences between theoretical and experimental
losses to the correct source. In particular, it will be important to have a clear understanding
of defect density, SRV, and chemical makeup of the interface, as well as to use modeling
to understand how band alignment and defect density are related.

We would like to develop a more nuanced understanding of recombination pathways at
the front interface by systematically engineering the emitter to vary the parameters
theorized to have the greatest effect on voltage loss and submitting these samples to
thorough characterization. This will require the utilization of knowledge and models
developed in Task 4 (characterization and capability development) to understand TRPL
of devices and test structures. This will be particularly useful when paired with our new
high-throughput, user TRPL system with two excitation wavelengths – 670 nm, which can
provide surface and bulk information (absorption depth in CdTe ~ 200 nm), and 405 nm,
which is preferentially absorbed near the front interface (absorption depth in CdTe ~ 30
nm). Because full device structures are not needed for TRPL measurements, changes in
carrier lifetime can be evaluated at each stage in the CdTe growth process and conditions
which increase recombination at the front interface can be quickly screened out.
Additionally, by cleaving devices, the heteropartner (and therefore junction field) is
removed, making the interpretation of TRPL data more straightforward. Important
information about recombination pathways and trapping processes can also be obtained
via temperature- and intensity-dependent PL, CL, and XPS/UPS. A critical portion of this
task will continue to further our understanding of the role and limitations of naturally
occurring species at the front interface including CdCl2, CdTeO3, and As2O3.

Finally, this knowledge will be used to develop passivation strategies for the front interface
(e.g., insertion of a tunneling layer) in both cleaved and superstrate devices. For cleaved
devices, the choice of tunneling layer is limited by its growth conditions (i.e., temperature,
ambient), such that the absorber is not adversely affected. However, cleaving also opens
up possibilities for passivation via surface treatment (e.g., Se anneal, thermal
reconstructions, oxidation/reduction of surface). Tunneling layers inserted into
superstrate devices, on the other hand, can be deposited at high temperatures – such as
is required for hexagonal boron nitride (hBN), boron monochalcogenides (BS, BSe, BTe),
TiO2, etc. – but must also be thermally stable. We will use Ellingham-Richardson
diagrams to provide a thermodynamic framework to predict useful processing windows
and stability. Modeling in Sentaurus or COMSOL will also be used to quantify the effect
on performance of inserting a tunneling layer of varied properties (thickness, band
alignment, defect density, etc.) at the front interface. It is noted that many of the listed
candidate materials are two-dimensional van der Waals compounds which are expected
to passivate both chemically (i.e., reduce defect density) and via field effect (i.e., band
bending).

First Solar has agreed to provide samples for characterization before and after
delamination as well as for interface reconstruction experiments. Additionally, First Solar
has agreed to fabricate samples on promising front interface candidates synthesized by
NLR.

Task 2 - Improved VTD Growth of CdTe and Doping
 The goal of this task is to realize the benefits of extrinsic dopants (e.g. As) in vapor
 transport deposited (VTD) CdTe films by increasing activation, reducing
 compensation, minimizing potential fluctuations, and minimizing bulk and grain
 boundary (GB) recombination. This work will prioritize development that
 understands and subsequently incorporates the complex interactions that link
 processing during VTD film growth with downstream CdCl2 and dopant activation
 steps in a CdTe:As device that will yield higher performance. This task will also
 provide samples for analysis in Task 4 to measure and study Se and As
 contributions to band tails as well as bulk and GB recombination. Structures will
 consist of full and partial double-heterostructures (DHs) for photoluminescence (PL),
 time-resolved PL (TRPL), Hall, micro-cathodoluminescence (µ-CL), and scanning
 capacitance microscopy (SCM, for nm-scale active carrier density mapping)
 measurements. Cleaving techniques will be used to prepare samples for
 compositional and structural analysis at interfaces. Guiding topics for this task
 include:

 • Study the effects of post-deposition cooling rate and Cd/Te vapor stoichiometry
 on dopant activation
 • Study the use of different dopant feedstock delivery systems (i.e., ground
 CdTe:As crystals, un-reacted and reacted mixtures of CdTe, Cd3As2, As2Te3)
 and their physical attributes (powder size, CdTe to dopant ratio)
 • Develop activation processes that achieve activation levels > 10% and/or total
 defect density ≤ 5x1017 cm-3 while maintaining 1016 cm-3 net acceptor density
 to reduce Voc deficits associated with band tails and potential fluctuations.
 • Optimize VTD deposition conditions and activation processing at different
 incorporation levels (1017 – 2x1018 cm-3)
 • Obtain  > 50 ns in epitaxially grown CdTe:As devices using large-grain (CGG)
 Cd(Se,Te) seed layers (100 to 500 µm lateral grain dimensions).
 • Determine if large-grain, epitaxially grown CdTe:As films on CGG seed layers will
 reduce or even eliminate the need for CdCl2 treatments universally required by
 state-of-the art, small-grained devices.
 • Determine if very dense CGG Cd(Se,Te) layers can mitigate negative effects of
 As-accumulation at front interfaces and can therefore reduce band tails resulting
 in higher Voc.
 • Expand existing device physics models to incorporate GB/interface accumulation
 (As, Se), very large grain sizes (i.e., CGG), band tails and defect density
 fluctuations.
 • Use novel activation and device applicable processing and structures to mitigate
 expected compensation mechanisms in films including the use of oxygen
 gettering techniques and high density, low GB, CGG layers as diffusion barriers
 to reduce As-oxidation by the TCO.
Detailed Description:
Vapor transport deposition (VTD) is the process used by First Solar. NLR’s VTD
system is a true inline process where films are deposited instantly on moving substrates

 Page 9 of 50

passed under a cylindrical 3-mm slit injector at speeds of greater than 0.5 in/sec. Unlike
CSS where deposition rates are on the order of um/min., the NLR VTD process
deposits at um/sec. This, combined with the ability to quickly heat and cool before and
after deposition, allows for non-equilibrium, fast-quenched condensation where highly
volatile GrV dopants are more efficiently incorporated relative to slower growth,
equilibrium-based CSS processing. It may also be a way to capture stoichiometries
amenable to GrV doping (e.g., promoting Cd-rich regions where GrVTe defects are
more favored). At the same time, the possibility exists for films to exhibit “non-
equilibrium” aspects that have negative effects. For example, even during equilibrium
bulk crystal growth that requires days-to-weeks of growth there are Te and Cd
inclusions. It is very probable that similar segregation occurs in VTD deposited films.

The primary work of this task will be to closely link and understand how processing
conditions during VTD deposition interact with downstream activation afterwards. For
example, the local environment of Cd, Te, and As in the as-deposited VTD film is likely
to affect how As is positioned during subsequent activation, i.e., as either an acceptor
(AsTe), or compensating defect (A-center or Asi). The local environment in turn will be
impacted by variations in Cd, Te, and As present in the vapor, as well as substrate
temperature and deposition rate. We will study this important and neglected
relationship by introducing non-choked vapor transport in the VTD system where the
large difference in Cd and Te gas phase diffusivities will introduce Cd/Te vapor flux
gradients across a 6” substrate as it passes under the injector. Variations in
stoichiometry will also be studied by varying the sources used to supply extrinsic As for
doping (i.e., As2Te3 vs Cd3As2). The variety of different local environments associated
with the GrV dopant will be included into experimental designs incorporating
downstream activation processing. By reducing potential fluctuations due to stochastic
variations in how dopants are activated and their location in the device, we should be
able to reduce band tails and the associated Voc deficit. Work performed during these
experiments will be informed by building upon existing DFT-based defect chemistry
models (for material optimization primarily using non-device structures) and COMSOL
device physics models (for device optimization) by including the effects of Se, O, Cl, as
well as local environment variations associated with stoichiometry, segregation (Se, As)
and overall compositional gradients primarily found in devices. Inherent to all device
optimization work will be the need to maintain a standard best-known-method (BKM) for
VTD growth and activation to serve as a control for investigating possible process
improvements.

We will explore several possible applications of our recent demonstration of using VTD
to very quickly deposit epitaxial CdTe and As:CdTe films with very large grain sizes
(100 – 1000 um) using a CdSexTe1-x alloy “seed” layer. This work will focus on initially
understanding and reducing intragranular defects in resulting large-grain thin film
structures, understanding the growth phenomenon (explosive crystallization) to increase
x (currently CGG is limited to x ≤ 0.1), and exploring techniques to reduce the
roughness of commercial Tec-series glasses (i.e. Tec12d) from NSG (CGG is limited by
surface roughness). If successful, this work will provide a direct path towards CdTe thin
film modules with grain sizes approaching 1-mm. Most importantly, this work may also

                                                  Page 10 of 50

reduce or completely obviate the need for CdCl2 anneals leading to improved activation
and reduced compensation. In addition, the crystalline density of these films (no GBs;
very high x-ray diffraction signals) will be used as a diffusion barrier between As:CdTe
and segregation at the front interface. This work will be combined with attempts to use
sacrificial elements with high oxidation potential to “getter” oxygen in the bulk of the film
to remove oxygen bound to As to improve its activation, while limiting diffusive
movement to the front such that gettering of beneficial oxide species at the front
interface does not occur.

First Solar has agreed to a sample exchange to facilitate absorber development. This
would include unactivated films from First Solar to allow us to maintain and improve our
activation process and wider applicability as we explore different incorporation
approaches in NLR-grown films as well as First Solar’s activation process on a limited
number of NLR grown absorbers. Additionally, they would provide activated films for
direct comparison and characterization. Furthermore, they have agreed to share
existing DFT modeling results (e.g. information regarding interactions of Se, Cl, O, and
As).

Task 3 - Improving the Rear Interface and Contact Properties
 The primary goals of this task are to establish the limits associated with the rear
 interface and contacts in state-of-the-art Cd(Se,Te) and begin to identify improvement
 strategies. Our models indicate that the rear interface and contact will become
 increasingly important as the front interface and absorber continue to improve. Some
 topics that this task will address are:
 • Establish procedures (test structures and characterization methods) to enable
 rapid identification of critical loss pathways for rear contacts in Cd(Se,Te).
 • Identify the most challenging critical loss pathway for the rear interface/contact
 • Develop materials and strategies to enable passivation at the rear interface and
 address the high work function of CdTe and any Fermi-level pinning at its free
 surface and interface

Detailed Description:
Many back-contacting methods for CdTe have been examined in the past, but further
work remains to obtain a rear interface that is sufficiently passivated with a contact that
is hole-selective. Based on modeling at NLR, reducing rear interface recombination
from current typical values or implementing a back-surface reflector is critical to exceed
25% efficiency. We will consider strategies to decouple thin tunneling passivation
layers from hole-selective contact layers. Passivation by surface treatments such as
etching or ion milling have been investigated in the past. We aim to consider these
treatments in addition to additive techniques, including 2D materials that have received
attention recently. As a part of this work, we will consider how passivated emitter rear
contact (PERC) structures could potentially be implemented in CdTe. We will then aim
to combine this passivation with hole-selective contact layers. Most successful back
contacts contain Cu to lower the barrier height due to CdTe’s high work function. Cu,
however, can create a compensating defect chemistry and stability problems. A new
GrV-compatible back contact, preferably Cu-free to avoid stability issues, may be

                                                  Page 11 of 50

important in realizing advances in the rest of the device. Electron reflector materials
have been investigated previously, but front interface and absorber properties have
been insufficient to show a strong effect and/or had insufficient doping in the electron
reflectors, leading to voltage drops at the rear. We plan to examine additional
prospective back contact materials, aided by device modeling. Potential back contact
materials will be screened according to their film properties such as hole concentration,
carrier mobility, and bandgap. Chemical composition will be verified when warranted.

We will grow test structures that will enable us to characterize key contact properties. If
optical properties of the prospective contact layers allow, we will gauge passivation
improvements through them directly via TRPL measurements on thick absorber
structures without a main junction (with front interface passivation by Al2O3, as
necessary), using two-color or 1PE/2PE techniques. Alternatively, for opaque contact
layers, we will use “mirror” structures (e.g. passivated front interface with Al2O3),
enabling measurement of back contact effects through the front side of the structure.
While transmission line measurements (TLM) to measure contact resistance can be
difficult in standard px thin films, we will explore the use of our CGG films with up to
mm-size grains to address this challenge. Band offset measurements will be made in
the most promising contact layers as appropriate via XPS or cross-sectional KPFM.

with an established back contact baseline with which to compare newly developed
contacts. Using a variety of characterization techniques available for devices, we will
compare established contacts (such as NiP, ZnTe, and established Cu-containing

collaboration with the French National Centre for Scientific Research (CRNS). We will

 This task will be divided into three main subtasks. The first subtask (4.1) will
 develop a characterization and analysis approach informed by modeling that
 leverages carefully considered test structures to quantify the bulk, front interface,
 and rear interface losses in state-of-the-art Cd(Se,Te) devices and provide
 guidance for Tasks 1-3. Subtask 4.2 will focus on expanding capabilities –
 specifically high-throughput detailed characterization methods (ERE, CV, and JVT),
 so they can be performed on all potential structures and devices of interest and

 and dissemination of understanding from the project whole. Topics this task will
 address include:
 • Procedure (test structures, devices, characterization method(s), model/analysis)
 to convincingly separate recombination at the front, bulk, and rear in state-of-the-

 • Limits to band tail losses and attribution to Se-grading versus group V

 • Assignment of loss to the different pathways in state-of-the-art Cd(Se,Te) group

Detailed Description:
Subtask 4.1 – Develop new methods/structures to separate voltage losses
This subtask will develop and validate a methodology in terms of test structures and
characterizations to determine/bound the losses in real Cd(Se,Te) devices from front
interface vs. bulk vs. rear interface recombination; band-tailing due to low As activation
vs. Se-grading; and lack of selectivity of contacts. This will require synthesis of
numerous test structures in Tasks 1-3. Spectrally corrected photoluminescence
intensity, TRPL, cathodoluminescence, XPS, and cross-sectional KPFM are expected to
be useful in evaluating these. Furthermore, readily applicable modeling will be critical to
informing the appropriate structures as well as their measurement conditions.

Using spectrally corrected PL to measure ERE on various structures will be critical to
establishing voltage loss to band-tailing. Using a detailed set of test structures, we will
establish limits and understand the onset of voltage losses due to band-tailing. While
ERE can be measured at any stage of the device making process, some of the initial
structures will likely be DHs to systematically separate the tailing from different aspects
of processing (e.g. undoped evaporated CdTe, undoped VTD CdTe, Cl-treated films,
As-incorporated films). Separately, single composition Cd(Se,Te) can be compared to
graded materials in undoped and doped manners. While we believe ERE can give
immediate quantitative insight into the losses in model structures (e.g. activated
CdTe:As in a DH), it remains to be determined if we will need to use it to guide efforts
only using these model structures or if we will be able to generalize the results in

                                                  Page 13 of 50

contrast to the more complicated device absorber that combines Se-grading with group
V doping.

XPS and cross-sectional KPFM can measure band alignment at the front and back
interfaces to establish the predicted selectivity of the contacts. NLR has developed
novel variants of both XPS and KPFM that take into account systematic errors that can
plague researchers. While band alignment measurements with XPS are involved and
can require numerous attempts to have confidence in the results, our ability to
delaminate at the front interface makes XPS band alignment measurements plausible,
with measurements at the rear interface more “standard”. Similarly, sample preparation
and passivation are critical aspects of KPFM to avoid artifacts, but we have
demonstrated the ability to do this in the past. Furthermore, we are developing the
capacity to couple KPFM with sub-bandgap illumination to target interface defects
states. The information that is obtained from these complementary techniques can then
be compared to optical estimates of the losses from contacts.

First Solar has agreed to provide devices at different levels of completion for
characterization and consider fabricating test structures identified in this task to provide
a parallel set of samples on which quantify losses.

Subtask 4.2 – Capability development
This subtask will focus on implementing three high throughput characterization tools as
well as the necessary standard analysis and data handling. The first of these is to
implement spectrally corrected photoluminescence intensity as a high throughput
standard tool that can be used to as part of the evaluation of every film and activation
experiment. The present system NLR uses a more versatile system requiring an expert
scientist – the goal of this is to obviate the need for specialty laser training or detailed
optics knowledge. In addition to new hardware that can be robust to non-expert user
interaction, we will develop a standard analysis framework. Second a multi-channel
JVT to facilitate VOC(T) analysis will be made to support Task 3. This will upgrade the
multi-channel JV system scheduled to be completed this FY. NLR’s existing JVT
system is cryostat based with a limited throughput (~1 sample/day). The hope of a high
throughput JVT system is to improve the throughput to the point where a batch of 16
small area substrates with 4 cells each could be measured daily. The third tool will
implement a multi-channel capacitance-voltage capability with as similar approach. Our
existing system has a single LCR meter with manual probe setup, requiring constant
attendance such that each measurement of carrier concentration requires several
minutes. We intend to automate this to again reduce the amount of person-hours
required per measurement.

Subtask 4.3 – Stakeholder engagement
It is important that NLR both perform work that is valued by the community and that our
latest results are disseminated. Stakeholder engagement will be maintained in three
general ways. We helped initiate and then have played a strong part in
leading/participating in the CdTe working groups (Front Contacts, Absorber, Rear
Contacts, Characterization, and Blue Sky/Future Focus) which have both academic and

                                                  Page 14 of 50

industrial stakeholders. The format for 2021 is that each working group meets every
quarter with a rotation of one or two groups meeting each month. Over the past several
years we have had a Cooperative Research and Development Agreement (CRADA)
with First Solar with biweekly interactions. We intend to maintain this relationship. We
will also continue to interact with other organizations through funded work and
participating in workshops and conferences.

7. Project Results and Discussion:
Task 1 - Improving the Front Interface and Contact Properties
 This task will develop a better understanding of the front interface through detailed
 analysis of test structures and model systems, and examine new front contact
 materials and passivating architectures

In the first year, the primary objectives of this task were to develop and use Ellingham-
Richardson thermodynamic framework to inform front contact material selection. Then
develop and screen alternate front contact materials ultimately improving our
understanding of losses at front with a year-end deliverable of a front contact
replacement for MZO.

Figure 1 : Ellingham-Richardson diagram for group V doped Cd(Se,Te) absorber
oxides, potential front interface TCOs and buffers, and potential reducing/oxidizing
ambient couples that could be used to tune oxidation states in Cd(Se, Te) thin-films.
The Ellingham-Richardson diagram presented in Figure 1 was developed in this project
to help with front interface contact material selection and potential ambient process
selection for group V doped Cd(Se,Te) absorbers. The two competing mechanisms that
we are trying to balance are: 1) reducing the group V oxides that prevent desired
acceptor defects in the bulk Cd(Se, Te), and 2) not reducing the TCOs, buffers and
other beneficial front interface oxides. This tool was initially developed in the first year of
the project, but was further refined and updated to help us prioritize potential front
interface materials and process conditions. Equipment and safety protocols were

                                                   Page 21 of 50

developed to experimentally investigate modulating CO/CO2 ratios. Three different
systems were set up with increasing levels of control. The first one used a modified box
furnace with manually controlled gas manifold. Ultimately, the high residence time
became prohibitive. A smaller chamber rapid thermal processor was later moved to the
lab and connected to the manifold, which reduced the residence time challenge. After
preliminary results that were promising in these systems, work was conducted to set up
a computer controlled close space sublimation system with vacuum to enable greater
reproducibility and control. This was coming online at the end of the period of
performance with further work to be pursued.

Milestone 1.1.1
Identify at least one front contact material that matches or exceeds MZO’s cell efficiency
averaged across at least 5 Group V doped cells.

MZO has been shown to be a powerful and beneficial front interface buffer material for
CdSeTe devices, but depending on how the material is deposited shows a variety of
instabilities both prior to device processing or through various high temperature
processing steps. A more desirable alternate front contact materials would have the
following characteristics:
 - Commercially available and or deposited in a simple and manufacturable way
 (e.g. sputtering)
 - Stable in air to enable batch pre-processing
 - Stable throughout the device processing (high temperatures, high vacuum,
 chemically-oxygen, chlorine, group V elements)
 - Band-gap and band positions don’t hinder device performance
One of the most commonly available commercial TCOs and buffers is SnO2, often in
the form of a doped and undoped stack. Pilkington TEC12D has long been used in our
group as a high quality, air stable superstrate material. Additional buffers such as MZO
have been added to this and similar SnO2 based products. CdTe alone is not well band
aligned to SnO2, so this has traditionally presented a challenge, but alloying with Se
bends the bands more favorably (though still not ideally) for this application. Never-the-
less optimized processing using just TEC12D enabled us to meet this first milestone by
the end of the first year of this project. Again, throughout the project our group V doped
baseline process has improved, and while these results met the milestone, further
improvements were made by year 3 including the NCTE (see Task 4).

                                                  Page 22 of 50

Figure 2 : Year 1 JV and CV parameters comparing devices on TEC12D superstrate
made with MZO and without. No MZO devices, SnO2 only, meet milestone 1.1.1.
In years two and three, Task 1 goals were to continue to develop and screen front
contact materials including detailed characterization of most promising candidates
(band alignment, emitter doping, Sfront) that can be compared to device models.
We wanted to experimentally validate design rules in terms of front interface properties
and framework for stability of interface eventually improving our of materials and
processes that can reduce the voltage deficit in group V doped superstrate devices.

Over the course of the project, we leveraged relationships with different organizations
including Loughborough, Vitro Glass, and the University of Toledo which have each
spent time developing alternate front contact interface materials. Loughborough and
the University of Toledo both had developed materials in-house that we have examined
(ZnO-based and In-Ga-O). With Vitro we have specifically been helping direct the
choice of materials, with a large focus on ways of ultra-thin layers that change the
termination of SnO2. The CRADA with Vitro Glass ramped up over the course of this
project, many of the early materials were underwhelming and “breaking” the front
interface. At the end of this period of performance, collaborative learning is leading to
materials that are starting to be functional. The CRADA will continue in the follow-on
project.

Initial Vitro CRADA work focused on synthesis and testing of nanometer-scale
zirconium oxide (ZrO2) front contacts. Zirconium was chosen based on literature
suggesting that electron traps in rutile-structure oxides such as SnO2 could be mitigated
by alloying with group IV metals (J. Phys. Chem. C 123 (2019) 22358). Group IV oxides
are also highly stable and likely to survive Cd(Se,Te) deposition and post-growth

                                                  Page 23 of 50

processing. Devices made with thin ZrO2 front buffer layers showed degraded
performance in comparison to the control device on TEC12D (Figure 3a). Visually, films
grown on ZrO2 buffer layers appeared different from control films. We used a home-
built optical pinhole detection scheme to assess pinholes in the film set. Figure 3b
shows the ZrO2 caused a drastic increase in pinholes, which appear orange in color.
Analysis of the ZrO2-Cd(Se,Te) interface with XPS and AES showed a correlation
between arsenic pile-up at the interface and the extent of pinholes (Figure 3c). Auger
electron microscopy (Figure 3d) showed very unusual distribution of CdCl2, where
cadmium is used as a proxy for CdCl2. Typically this interface is characterized by a
thin, uniform layer of CdCl2. Zirconium oxide in this is clearly causing increased front
interface oxidation as well as affecting overall film morphology. Although these
detrimental effects were not the desired outcome, it underscores the strong effects of
nanometer-scale layers on the properties of absorber layers grown upon them, and
provides further motivation for understanding interactions between the front oxide
contact and the Cd(Se,Te):As absorber.

Figure 3. Results obtained from devices made using zirconium oxide front buffer layers.
In (a), J-V results. In (b), optical images of pinholes in two represenative devices. In
(c), pinhole images and XPS-derived arsenic concentrations. In (d), Auger electron
micrographs of tin and cadmium froom the SnO2-ZrO2 side of the front interface.
Chemical reactions occurring during processing make it possible to improve front
interfaces solely with processing changes rather than use of different materials. An
example of this comes in our recent work focused on controlling oxidation potentials
during CdCl2 heat treatment by use of varying mixtures of CO and CO2. Using First
Solar FS100 as a starting material, a series of film stacks were subjected to CdCl2
treatment using N2 and mixtures of CO and CO2, the goal being to control activation of
Group V dopants while also controlling the extent of front interface oxidation. XPS in

                                                  Page 24 of 50

conjunction with thermomechanical cleaving was used to examine the front interface
characteristics. Figure 4a is comprised of XPS results that probed the chemical
speciation of tellurium found on the TCO side of the front interface. Samples are
roughly arranged in order of increasing oxidative conditions (greater CO2/CO) from left
to right. XPS shows that as the percentage of CO2 relative to CO increased, the
amount of oxidized tellurium (Te0 and Te4+) generally increased, an expected result. An
unanticipated result is that the varying oxidative conditions also affected chlorine
accumulation at the interface (Figure 4b). Chlorine has long been implicated in
passivation of grain boundaries and interfaces in CdTe-based solar cells, so knowing
that merely changing the oxygen chemical potential during CdCl2 can affect chlorine
distribution is critical. Although trends are not perfect in this early data set, we believe
these results show we have a new way to exert fine control over the final configuration
of the front interface. Additional device results and electro-optic characterizations of this
effort are available and will be published in a forthcoming paper.

processed under oxygen chemical potentials controlled by varying CO/CO2 (a). In (b),
XPS-derived chlorine concentrations from same sample set.

 This task will realize the benefits of Group V dopants and develop a better

by adding a “densification” anneal to improve the grain structure of the CST absorber

Se alloying has become a ubiquitous part of CdTe-base photovoltaics. Similar to the
CdCl2 treatment, a variety of aspects of device performance can be improved by
including Se near the front interface including band alignment with front buffer layers
uch as SnO2, apparent lifetime, total PL intensity (PLQY), and Jsc (from a reduced
bandgap ~1.4 eV). In aggregate, these benefits have played a significant role in
reducing the Voc deficit in CdTe PV over the last 10 years. Unfortunately, some of these
metrics may be inflated by sub-gap features that are continuing to hold back the
performance of CdTe PV. PLQY for example is greatly improved, but includes all
luminescence including that from subgap defect states. Similarly, the extraordinary
lifetimes measured in double heterostructures may be primarily related to trapping
which we would want to minimize to improve device performance.

Figure 8 : a) TRPL and b) absolute spectrally corrected PL showing the effect of
increasing Se concentration in CdSexTe1-x from x = 0 to 0.4 (referring to the alloyed
source material composition) with baseline conditions; x = 0.3 data copied here for
reference.[15]
Se alloying also can introduce significant additional inhomogeneity. The band gap
bowing that is directly related to the Se alloy composition means that a Se graded
absorber is also a band gap graded absorber which may benefit FF slightly.
Unfortunately, Se does not diffuse uniformly so some grains are Se-rich and other Se-

                                                  Page 27 of 50

poor; some grain boundaries have a lot of Se and some less. This results in additional
inhomogeneous internal fields. Moreover, because Se composition alters defect
chemistry enough to impact doping and lifetime, these two parameters become

Band tails in As doped Cd(Se,Te) absorbers originate from a variety of sources
including Se alloying (band gap disorder), poor As activation (electrostatic potential
fluctuations), and other sub gap defects and defect bands. It can significantly impact
voltage loss. A couple metrics directly associated with band tails are Urbach energy (an
energy associated sub gap absorptance) and the radiative Voc. For this milestone we
were specifically interested in reducing the voltage impact associated with the As
doping. In Figure 11 absorptance, Urbach energies, and radiative Voc for several As
doped CST devices and test structures (with an Al2O3 front interface) are compared to
an undoped CST device. The undoped CST provides a known baseline for what might
be expected in terms of voltage loss from band tails related to Se alloying alone. Here
we find that the Voc,rad for As doped devices are often better than the undoped CST
device, meeting the milestone goal. The implication here, is that band tails and
associated radiative Voc loss from As doping vs Se alloying are not directly additive, but
that a more complex relationship exists. From an Urbach energy and associated Voc
loss perspective, As doping + Se alloying is better than Se alloying alone and thus the
idea of “doping” should not be limited to simply improving carrier density, but may also
contribute to other defect passivation/mitigation.

The primary deliverable for Task 2 in year 2 was to generate absorbers with high carrier
concentration within 10% of radiative limit. This milestone proved to be more
challenging than we had initially anticipated and was not met until the end of year 3. The
goals for year 3 were to compare decoupled activation and grain growth processing and
revisit ex-situ GrV doping ultimately enabling an absorber capable of achieving higher
voltages. Towards these ends and with a focus on achieving a better fundamental
understanding of As doping in CdTe our Veeco MBE tool was revitalized. This platform
enables single crystal CdTe films deposited on 3” silicon wafers with detailed control
dopants and metallic overpressures (e.g., CdTe, Te, Cd, As, etc.). This is achieved at
lower substrate temperatures and growth rates than would be used in typical

                                                  Page 29 of 50

polycrystalline device processing and the lattice mismatch between Si and CdTe results
in a high density of dislocations, but it is still a highly valuable asset for more
fundamental and controlled experiments. Building on results from when the tool was first
commissioned, we were able to achieve much higher As activation ratios than in single
crystals compared to polycrystalline device quality material. Figure 12 shows SIMS data
and crystallinity for two films that resulted in >50% dopant activation after a 10 minute
575C anneal in an RTP furnace. Figure 13 shows the hole concentration As activation
as a function of RTP temperature for these films. Figure 14 shows As diffusion (a proxy
for concentration) as it relates to activation, and carrier concentration vs incorporation
for films with different amounts of initial As. There are a few key findings from this work
that have important implications for polycrystalline absorbers.
  1) Controlling total As incorporation to 1E17 or less will likely be important to achieving
  higher activation. Even in single crystals, carrier concentration in CdTe seems to
  saturate around mid-E16 to ~1E17. Thus, if we want 1E16 doping and high activation,
  the amount of dopant we include needs to be reduced from current 1E18 levels.
  2) High temperature anneals enable high doping activation. Annealing at 400-500C
  will likely be insufficient to effectively activate the As dopants (at least for time scales
  on the order of minutes).
  3) Cd overpressure, while potentially helpful in principle - we want As on Te sites so
  would want to saturate Cd sites with Cd - does not offer a significant activation
  advantage compared to films grown without this overpressure. As incorporation does
  change with overpressure, but if the As concentration is controlled to ~1E17 high
  activation can be achieved.

Utilizing this platform and these findings, more complex processing was also worked on
to better understand potential compensation mechanisms in CdTe absorbers. CdCl2
treatments are ubiquitous for polycrystalline absorbers but performed at temperatures
below 500C. In studying single crystals, chlorine was found to potentially impact doping
in bulk absorbers, but only if it can actually be diffused into the bulk of the absorber –
small amounts of surface chlorine do not reduce activation. Lower temperatures may
impact doping, however. A 60 min anneal at 450C was found to reduce doping by a
factor of 2-4 compared to a 10 min anneal at 575C. As such, typical CdCl2 processes
may be inherently limiting As activation ratios.

Task 3 - Improving the Rear Interface and Contact Properties
 In this task, we will develop back contact passivation strategies and consider, model,
 grow, and characterize known and novel back contact materials for CdTe with the
 goal of achieving a stable, passivating, hole-selective, Cu-free back contact.

There were no milestones directly associated with task 3 but we have made significant
progress on back contacts for CdTe as a part of this project.

                                                  Page 33 of 50

In the first year of work, significant work was done on MXenes, a family of 2D materials
which offer a promising route to forming low-cost, low-barrier contacts due to their
demonstrated high work function, metallic conductivity, and simple solution processing.
Ti3C2Tx MXene films, in particular, processed from an aqueous colloidal dispersion can
perform as a hole contact material for CdTe solar cells. A few parameters are important
to assess when investigating back contacts: back contact barrier height, lifetime
(especially if this can be tied to the back surface recombination), extrapolated 0K
voltage (or activation energy) from Voc-T, series resistance, and ultimately device
performance. When comparing Ti3C2Tx to Au, we found that the MXene performed well
in many of these categories. Figure 17 shows the Arrhenius plot of J0,b/T2 to extract
back barrier height (φB) from JVT data, barrier height statistics using the Mott-Shottky
method and Voc-T with extrapolated VOC,0k for both Au and Ti3C2Tx contacts. The
MXene outperforms Au in all of these metrics. The lifetime is somewhat reduced
compared to Au contacts which results in slightly lower overall performance, but while
Au is useful at lab scale given its high work function, it is still an impractical back contact
from a cost and stability perspective at commercial scale.

The rise in bifacial Si devices is also pushing the CdTe community to establish and
improve transparent back contacts. The many aspects of a good back contact for a
single sided device are still present for transparent back contacts, but in addition to the
transparency requirement, all of the aspects become absolutely critical for back side
illumination performance. If you have significant downward band bending near the back
contact with high back interface recombination, the back side illumination performance
is going to suffer greatly. From this perspective and building on the initial Mxene work,
we worked on and demonstrated hierarchical back contact that was also transparent.

contact – AlGaOx (AGO) was intended as a passivation layer (reducing back surface
recombination, Ti3C2Tx has a high work function (reduces the back interface band
bending), and transparent Au deposited by cracked film lithography is highly conductive.

Milestone 1.4.1
Use newly developed loss analysis methods, identify test structures and methods to

The first milestone in task 4 relates directly to the goal of implementing and utilizing test
structures to better understand and constrain secondary device parameters inherent to
performance. Front, back, and bulk recombination velocities often become convoluted
with each other and device aspects such as the primary front junction in simple
measurements of standard devices. Pulling these metrics apart more directly requires a
more complicated protocol and the use of test structures to eliminate (or minimize) the
influence of other parameters. Figure 20 shows the protocol that was designed to more
effectively extract these parameters. There are a few requirements for this protocol to
work: 1) a priori estimates of doping and mobility are required, 2) data need exhibit
thickness dependance, 3) need to assume TRPL is done in a low injection regime to
avoid the dominance of radiative recombination. Requirement (3) can be mitigated by
doing an injection series while taking the TRPL data. Requirement (2) provides its own
set of challenges, however. One approach to deal with this is to utilize beveled
structures as shown in Figure 21. In this case, device processing is completed with a
particular front interface partner, then the stack is beveled prior to back contact for
passivation. One advantage of this approach is that the absorber and front interface
evolution resulting from processing are consistent with processing that would be done in
a typical device. A single sample can also be used for many thickness measurements
so that a larger thickness series doesn’t need to be produced. Moreover, if the absorber
is significantly thinner or thicker than normal, the rest of the processing (CdCl2

                                                  Page 36 of 50

temperature and time, Se layer thickness, post process annealing steps, etc.) may need
to be re-optimized due to various coupled effects. That said, a beveled surface does not
necessarily represent a “standard” back surface and the back contact processing on this
surface may introduce other convoluting factors. Given that any potential approach
would have its own set of benefits and drawbacks, we like the bevel structure approach
since we believe the “pros” outweigh the “cons”. The combination of beveled structure
together with injection dependance we have termed TRPL-ID where I stands for
injection and D stance for depth.

Milestone 1.4.2
Report quantified improvements in measurement and data management

In working to improve CdTe PV performance, there are a variety of measurements that
are fast to perform, straightforward to interpret, and low cost from an operational
perspective - extracting standard solar cell performance metrics from 1-sun JV
measurements for example. There are other measurements that are much slower,
expensive, and/or difficult to interpret, but are often highly valuable. We have worked to
improve two different measurements that are useful for our program.

The first is related to dopant concentration measurements. To understand As doping in
CdTe materials and devices, we typically rely on SIMS. If calibrated correctly, this
technique can provide quantitative As concertation profiles as a function of depth in
CdTe absorbers. By monitoring this as a function of initial deposition conditions and
post processing steps, we get information about how much dopant we have
incorporated and where that dopant diffuses as a result of processing. By coupling this
with carrier concentration measurements from Hall or CV, we can develop an
understanding of dopant activation ratios that may be limiting Voc (discussed in more
depth in Task 2). Unfortunately SIMS is a relatively slow and expensive measurement,
which makes it impractical for evaluating every VTD deposition and/or mapping dopant
incorporation uniformity of our process. To address these shortcomings, we have spent
time developing an ICPMS process that can provide average dopant concentrations in
CdTe films at a fraction of the cost and time. Careful control of acid digestion is key to
successful ICPMS measurement, and several different processes were tested. Once an
effective digestion process was established, we measured different VTD films using
both SIMS as ICPMS – results shown in Figure 24a. In general, there is very good
agreement with average concentrations from ICPMS and SIMS. This process enabled
us to map As concentration over full 6”x3” VTD depositions (Figure 24b) to better
understand the uniformity of our process.

9. Path Forward:
Building on the work here, another project was funded identifying as series of high level
goals including a focus on front interface material development, evolution, band
alignment, and new materials; addressing back contact barrier and recombination;
improving inherent absorber quality through understanding and addressing
recombination centers as well as electronic disorder that leads to bandtails;
understanding light state/dark state behavior; and improving characterization
