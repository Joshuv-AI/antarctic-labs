Broadband Radiative Heat Transfer Suppression via Dispersion-Engineered Metasurfaces
Authors: Lin Jing, Mingze He, Sander A. Mann, Kevin Plocher, Gregory R. Holdman, Mike Onyszczak, Timothy J. Palinski, Andrea Alù
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Suppressing radiative heat transfer using metallic reflectors fails where electrical conduction or material incompatibility are prohibitive, whereas non-metallic alternatives that rely on conventional thermophotonic approaches face fundamental performance-bandwidth trade-offs, bounded by the Bode-Fano limit. Here, we redefine the strategy for broadband radiative suppression by leveraging complementary dispersion engineering in a dielectric metasurface pair. Using stochastic gradient descent (SGD) optimization, we co-design aperiodic distributed Bragg reflector (DBR) pairs with deliberately misaligned passbands across the broad thermal band of interest, enabling effective broadband thermal decoupling within a small thickness and fabrication budget, beyond the limits of conventional approaches. Experimental validation reveals that our optimized 7-layered (7-L) metasurface pair reduces radiative heat exchange significantly compared to fused silica benchmarks, as confirmed through both angle-resolved emissivity measurements (82% reduction) and direct radiometric power quantification (62.5% reduction). Importantly, our compact system exhibits strong robustness against design and fabrication tolerance and operational temperature drift (320–500 K). This work establishes a generalizable framework for bandwidth-unconstrained thermal radiation engineering, with applications in energy-efficient systems, thermal insulation and thermal management. By circumventing the need for metallic components, our approach opens new possibilities for controlling radiative heat transfer in an all-dielectric ultrathin platform.

Introduction

Thermal radiation is a ubiquitous phenomenon in nature, and it underpins a broad spectrum of modern technologies1,2. The past few decades have witnessed transformative advances in thermophotonics, unveiling unprecedented control over thermal emission through the design of selective thermal emitters3–5. These innovations have spurred breakthroughs in infrared sources or sensors6–8, frequency- or angle- selective radiative cooling9–12 and heating13–16, camouflage17–19, thermophotovoltaics20–22, etc. Central to these advancements is the ability to precisely engineer thermal emission properties—including temporal coherence23,24, spatial coherence25–27 and full wavefront manipulation28—via tailored optical phenomena such as surface plasmon/phonon polaritons26,29,30, Fabry-Pérot cavities31,32, distributed Bragg reflectors (DBRs)33,34, epsilon-near-zero (ENZ) materials35,36 and simultaneous local and nonlocal light-matter interactions28,37,38. These photonic platforms, however, face an inherent tradeoff between operational bandwidth and in-band performance, a limitation rooted in the Bode-Fano criterion39, which governs how effectively broadband energy can be transferred to reactive structures without relying on active components or nonlinear mechanisms. When the device footprint is taken into account, performance is further bounded by the Rozanov limit, which constrains the achievable bandwidth for a passive structure of a given thickness40. Consequently, conventional thermal engineering approaches are ineffective for broadband, ultrathin devices. A key example is the suppression of radiative crosstalk between objects, as in cryogenic insulation41, spacecraft shielding42 and electronic thermal protection43–45. Compared with enhancing radiative exchange through the strategic use of broadband absorptive materials such as carbon black, mitigating radiative heat transfer is more challenging due to the limited availability of broadband low-emissivity materials beyond metals46. While metals, such as aluminium, are ideally suited for this purpose due to their broadband reflectivity and low absorptivity, their applicability is restricted in many practical settings. For instance, in passive radiative thermal management for electronics43 or in thermoelectric systems47–49, metal coatings can induce electrical leakage and interfere with optoelectronic components. These limitations, therefore, have made non-metallic radiative suppression schemes imperative50. Using DBRs, it is possible to realize large reflectivities from transparent materials within defined bandwidths, but this involves a compromise between the level of reflectivity and the bandwidth of operation within a given stopband, as dictated by the Bode-Fano and Rozanov bounds. To date, suppressing radiative heat transfer without being subject to bandwidth limitations using non-metallic materials in a compact footprint remains largely untapped, representing a critical gap in the field.

While achieving low emission (absorption) from a non-metallic ultrathin coating across the full spectral range where thermal emission plays a major role is not feasible using linear, passive time-invariant materials, we can leverage the constraints of Bode-Fano bounds to our advantage to realize broadband heat transfer suppression: passivity imposes a limit on the continuous bandwidth over which absorption can be small, but it allows the emergence of multiple low-absorption frequency windows separated by narrowband large absorption resonances, a by-product of the fact that zeros and poles of the reflection coefficient of a passive structure must alternate with frequency51.

It follows that a viable solution is to deliberately offset these narrowband resonances—regardless of their number—of pairs of coatings throughout the entire bandwidth, making them mutually incompatible for heat exchange. This, in turn, eliminates the bandwidth constraint, effectively circumventing the Bode-Fano bound, which focuses on bandwidth performance for a continuous reflection band. One effective implementation of this strategy is to use metasurfaces made of aperiodic DBRs suitably optimized to be thermally incompatible across the entire thermal spectrum. Unlike conventional periodic DBRs, which feature a single high-reflection stopband fulfilling the perfect phase matching condition, aperiodic DBRs—composed of alternating high refractive index contrast materials (dielectrics or semiconductors) with varying layer thicknesses—offer greater flexibility for multi-wavelength reflection bands52,53. Furthermore, the fabrication of these DBR metasurfaces relies solely on thin-film deposition, enabling low-cost, wafer-scale production with minimal processing steps and a naturally compact thickness, underscoring their significant practical potential. Although standard forward designs can achieve resonance misalignment in discrete frequency bands, systematically decoupling all modes across the full spectrum—where thermal weighting convolves with the blackbody radiation profile—poses extreme design challenges. The increased number of variables associated with aperiodic DBRs further intensifies the complexity. To this aim, inverse design protocols excel in efficiently exploring complex parameter spaces to find globally optimal solutions17,53–55 for any target spectral range. While this method has been widely adopted for optimizing thermal metamaterials across 1D to 3D structures56–58, it has been limited to single-object tasks, and end-to-end design for dispersion engineering multiple interacting structures remains largely unexplored.

In this work, we showcase the potential of inverse design to suppress radiative heat transfer between two planar non-metallic surfaces through complementary dispersion engineering across the entire thermal band. Specifically, we employ a stochastic gradient descent (SGD) approach53 to simultaneously optimize aperiodic DBR-based radiator and absorber pairs, with the objective of minimizing their integrated radiative crosstalk. Each optimized planar surface is composed of alternating thin films of a-Si and SiO2 with varying thickness, grown on a fused silica substrate. Given a constraint on the number of layers, we determined the optimal thickness of each layer and fabricated the DBR pair to experimentally validate our approach by measuring angle-resolved emissivity and calculating radiative heat exchange. This radiative decoupling performance was further confirmed by measuring the net radiative power using a custom-built electrical substitution radiometer setup. Taking the optimized 7-layered metasurface pair as an example, their radiative heat transfer is significantly suppressed by ~82% and ~62.5% compared to a pair of fused silica substrates, as determined by emissivity estimation and direct measurement, respectively. Notably, this high performance—achieved with a small overall thickness—exceeds that of traditional periodic DBR structures or bulk Si blocking. Our work thus fills a critical gap in bandwidth-unconstrained engineering for suppressing radiative heat transfer without relying on metallic materials, addressing existing challenges and advancing the field of thermal energy management.

Results

Inverse design protocol

We employ a stochastic gradient descent (SGD) approach to co-optimize dual metasurfaces with incompatible emission-absorption spectra across the entire spectrum of interest to achieve significant integrated radiative exchange reduction. In contrast to our previous research using the same methodology53, where the emissivity of a single emitter was optimized, the present development moves towards end-to-end optimization. By assigning appropriate temperature-dependent Planck distribution weights to each frequency, the objective function becomes the overall radiative exchange power between the radiator and absorber, which is directly calculated and optimized59. To ground the performance, we compare against a benchmark pair of fused silica substrates. As a highly polar dielectric material, SiO2 manifests strong, high-frequency phonon resonances in the mid-IR region60,61, resulting in broadband absorption that establishes it as a promising reference material. Traditional spectral engineering methods, such as Fabry-Pérot cavities31 or DBRs33, are typically limited to single wavelengths or narrow spectral bands (Fig. 1a). In this work, two aperiodic DBRs, serving as radiator and absorber terminals in the radiative transfer system, are simultaneously optimized. Experimental validation by infrared spectroscopy

Following this inverse design protocol, we numerically designed and experimentally realized 3-layered (3-L) and 7-layered (7-L) metasurface pairs. Figure 2a presents SEM images of the fabricated 7-L DBR pair cross-sections, while Fig. 2b shows optical images of the two wafers (3-L DBR pair images are provided in Supplementary Information). We first validated the design by comparing measured emissive spectra from fabricated samples with numerical calculations, examining both the angular dispersion of each individual device and the complementary spectral dispersion of each pair. The emissivity spectra were characterized at 473 K using a customized angle-resolved thermal emission measurement setup (Fig. 2c; detailed measurement procedures are provided in Methods). In these aperiodic DBR structures, multiple Bragg conditions can be satisfied at different wavelengths via flexibly matching the phase between reflected waves, thereby enabling multiple resonance modes. Figure 2d–g clearly demonstrate multiple resonances and relatively small angular dispersion of each resonance mode over a wide emission angle, as evidenced by both TMM calculations and experimental measurements. A side-by-side comparison of the experimental results (Fig. 2e and f) reveals that the resonances in DBR 1 and DBR 2 are offset, effectively preventing radiative coupling. Fabrication errors introduced a slight discrepancy between the optimal design (thickness values in brackets in Fig. 2a) and the actual device; however, this ±3% deviation has a small impact on the results, as demonstrated by the close agreement between the computed and measured emissivity spectra. This underscores the robustness of the design (detailed robustness analysis against fabrication errors is provided in Supplementary Note 4). The remaining discrepancy between calculation and experiment is attributed to experimental factors, such as temperature mismatch, atmospheric absorption, and partial transparency in certain spectral windows.

Given the limited angular dispersion within the target frequency/angle design space, the normal-incidence emissivity (approximated as diffuse emission) provides a good estimate of the net radiative heat transfer for our demonstration. However, near the SiO2 Reststrahlen band (~1100 cm⁻¹), even with polarization averaging, Fig. 2d–g reveal clear dispersive behavior with enhanced emission at large angles. This behavior stems from the phonon-polaritonic response of SiO2, where LO-TO (longitudinal optical–transverse optical) phonon splitting results in negative permittivity within this spectral region60,63. These modes respond exclusively to p-polarized (TM) fields, as confirmed by polarization-resolved measurements (Supplementary Note 3). In the Si/SiO2 multilayer stack, they couple to radiative channels and become increasingly prominent at large emission angles due to improved momentum matching. Consequently, dispersion becomes non-negligible at small separations, where large-angle contributions play a significant role in heat transfer. Mitigating this effect is therefore necessary and is addressed in the next section.

Figure 3a displays the calculated and measured emissivity spectra of the benchmark: a 500 µm bare fused silica substrate. The silica slab exhibits broadband thermal emission across the wavelength range of interest, driven by bond vibration-induced phonon resonances and structural disorder60, though it is sharply attenuated within the Reststrahlen band. Nevertheless, this broadband emission is dramatically modified by our aperiodic DBR structures, as illustrated in Fig. 3b, c for the optimized 3-L and 7-L DBR pairs, respectively. Both configurations feature misaligned resonances, a key mechanism for inhibiting radiative coupling. In the 7-L DBR pair, the resonances sharpen significantly compared to the 3-L structure due to stricter phase-matching conditions enabled by the additional layers. As a result, radiative coupling is further suppressed, as quantified by the radiative heat transfer spectra in Fig. 3d. Notably, dominated by rich vibrational modes at lower frequencies, the heat transfer reduction is less effective across all configurations. To mitigate this limitation, one route is to increase the layer count, which could further narrow the resonance bandwidth and thereby improve spectral decoupling. Figure 3e shows that the mutual emissivity (calculated by normalizing radiative heat transfer to blackbody exchange) exhibits an exponential decay with respect to the number of DBR layers in the design. However, practical implementation requires balancing this improvement against the increased fabrication complexity and thermal stress at higher layer counts. In this work, we selected a 7-L design as a compact, high-yield proof-of-concept that maintains mechanical robustness while already providing strong suppression—adding 10 more layers (i.e., increasing to 17 layers) yields only a modest 6% improvement. More importantly, the use of lossless materials in the spectral range of interest, such as Ge and ZnSe, may further improve the suppression performance, as shown in Supplementary Note 5. While the inverse design assumed fixed radiator and absorber temperatures (500 K and 300 K, respectively), the radiative heat suppression remains effectively stable at the off-design temperatures—radiator from 321 to 500 K and absorber at 320 K. This thermal robustness occurs because the Planck peak displacement within this temperature window produces only minor redistributions of radiative energy across the relevant spectrum. We conducted a systematic analysis to investigate the discrepancy between direct measurements and indirect estimations. Full details, including the role of the wavelength range, angular dispersion and partial transparency, are reported in Supplementary Note 7. In brief, two primary factors were identified: omission of spectral contributions below 500 cm⁻¹ and the assumption of diffuse emission (i.e., angle-independent emissivity) in the original design. To validate these findings, cross-validation was performed using an FTIR spectrometer covering an extended spectral range of 30–8000 cm⁻¹. Rather than directly measuring emissivity, absorptivity was determined as α=1-R-Tα=1−R−T, with the influence of partial transparency verified to be negligible. Based on the extended-range absorptivity measured at 10° (normal incidence was not achievable due to setup limitations), the calculated mutual emissivity (dash-dot lines) shows significantly improved agreement with the direct radiometer measurements, particularly for the silica substrate and the 3-L DBR pair (Fig. 4d). To further account for large-angle contributions, absorptivity was also characterized at a polar angle of 75°, and a two-angle averaged effective emissivity was constructed using data at 10° and 75°. This approach yields additional improvement, with the mutual emissivity of the paired structure (dotted lines) showing closer agreement with the direct measurements (Fig. 4e). Overall, this analysis resolves the discrepancy between direct and indirect methods and demonstrates that incorporating both far-IR spectral contributions and angular dependence is essential—and sufficient—for accurate performance prediction and reliable suppression design. Despite these corrections, both the 3-L and 7-L metasurface pairs exhibit substantial reductions in radiative crosstalk compared to the fused silica baseline across the operating temperature range, thereby validating the proof of concept.

Discussion

This work demonstrates the potential of complementary dispersion engineering of metasurfaces to inhibit radiative heat transfer between planar non-metallic surfaces, overcoming conventional performance-bandwidth-footprint trade-offs. Minimal radiative crosstalk was achieved through a pair of metasurfaces consisting of SGD-optimized aperiodic DBRs featuring misaligned resonances across a broadband range of frequencies. The effectiveness of the design was validated by indirect estimations of radiative heat exchange based on measured emissivity, showing the 7-layered DBR pair achieving 82% suppression relative to fused silica benchmarks. Independent net radiative power measurements, conducted using a custom electrical substitution radiometer, reveal a 62.5% suppression with potential for further improvement. Crucially, we also validated the robustness of our design against fabrication errors and operating temperature variations. This study bridges a critical gap in non-metallic radiative heat suppression, offering a generalizable framework for bandwidth-unconstrained thermal engineering. The same philosophy can be extended to near-field applications64,65, where heat exchange is dominated by evanescent modes with large in-plane wavevectors. In that regime, the design objective must be reformulated to minimize the mode-resolved transmission function—rather than far-field emissivity alone—with careful consideration of surface-mode coupling. Our approach not only addresses long-standing challenges in thermal management but also opens new avenues for applications in energy-efficient systems, photonic devices, and thermal insulation technologies.

Methods

Fabrication

The DBRs were fabricated using plasma-enhanced chemical vapour deposition (PECVD) on fused silica substrates. Prior to deposition, the substrates underwent piranha cleaning (H2SO4:H2O2 = 2:1) for 20 minutes to activate the surface and promote strong adhesion between the deposited amorphous silicon (a-Si) and the silica substrate. Additionally, a deposition rate calibration was performed before each deposition using a reference substrate, with thickness characterized via ellipsometry (J.A. Woollam V-VASE) to ensure accurate control of each layer’s thickness. Simultaneously, the optical properties of the fabricated a-Si and SiO2 thin films were determined for use in the inverse design algorithm.

Emissivity measurement

The angle-resolved emissivity measurement system comprises a Fourier transform infrared spectrometer (FTIR) coupled with a thermal emission collection setup. The sample was mounted on a temperature-controlled stage with dual-angle controls: one for the polar angle θ (ranging from -70° to 70°) and the other for the azimuth angle ϕϕ (ranging from 0° to 360°). To ensure a sufficient signal-to-noise ratio, the sample was heated to 473 K. Then, thermal emission from the sample was collimated using an off-axis parabolic mirror and directed into the FTIR, which is equipped with a mercury-cadmium-telluride (MCT) detector. The signal collected by the MCT detector includes thermal emission from both the sample and the FTIR's internal optics. Thus, in order to isolate the sample’s contribution, a background measurement was performed by replacing the sample with a gold mirror. In addition, a blackbody reference sample (vertically aligned carbon nanotubes with an emissivity of 0.97) was measured under identical conditions. Accordingly, the sample's emissivity was determined by calculating the ratio of the sample signal to the reference signal after subtracting the background signal from both measurements.
