Strengthening Clinical Nurses' Research Capacity: A Scoping Review of Factors That Influence Nurses' Engagement in Clinical Research
Authors: Lyndsay Jerusha Mackay, Laurie Lee, K Alix Hayden, Suzanna Crawford, Gabrielle Harrison, Una Chang, Jessica Son, Nicole Chen, Joshua Yudkin, Sara Moore
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAimTo identify and map evidence on factors that influence nurses' engagement in clinical research.DesignA scoping review was conducted in accordance with the JBI methodology.MethodsFirst, titles and abstracts of articles, then full‐text articles, were screened by two independent researchers against inclusion and exclusion criteria. Data was extracted into a data extraction table. Findings from articles were synthesized using thematic analysis.Data SourcesSearches were conducted in Medline All (Ovid), Embase (Ovid), APA PsycInfo (Ovid), CINAHL Plus with Full Text (Ebsco) and Scopus on April 28, 2025.ResultsSeventy‐nine articles were included in this review. There were significant barriers that prevented nurses from engaging in research, including: lack of time, inadequate training, negative internal beliefs, insufficient organizational support and an unsupportive research culture. Factors that facilitated nurses' engagement in research included: research infrastructure, training and mentorship, visionary leadership, unit‐level support, improved motivational components and enhanced organizational research culture. A multi‐level framework and strategic recommendations are provided, along with an overview of the 23 interventions reported in the included articles are provided.ConclusionsNurses are constrained from conducting research to generate nursing knowledge due to multiple barriers. Visionary leadership is needed that prioritizes nursing research, establishes infrastructure and creates a culture of research excellence to enhance the quality and safety of nursing care.Implications for the Profession and/or Clinical PracticeHealthcare organizations should support nursing research by providing nurses with paid release time from their clinical duties and building an accessible infrastructure. Infrastructure can include programmes, centres and academic‐practice partnerships that provide nurses with access to research training, experienced mentorship, librarian and statistical support, grant and manuscript writing assistance and funding pathways for small and large studies.Reporting MethodThis scoping review is reported in accordance with the PRISMA‐ScR guidelines.Patient or Public ContributionNo patient or public contribution.Protocol RegistrationRegistered with the Open Science Framework: 10.17605/OSF.IO/5VWE9 (12/03/25).

Introduction

The ability of healthcare organizations to create opportunities and pathways for clinical nurses to conduct research and generate evidence is the foundation of evidence‐based practice (EBP) and a clear marker of nursing excellence (Cato et al. 2019; Cimiotti et al. 2005). A strong research culture within a healthcare organization is essential for nurses to deliver high‐quality and safe nursing care (Harding et al. 2016; Ives Erickson and Pappas 2020). This is because nurses' increased engagement with clinical research leads to high patient and staff satisfaction, improved patient outcomes, decreased patient mortality and improved organizational efficiency (Harding et al. 2016; Shepherd et al. 2022). As well, having a hospital organization with a robust nursing research culture helps to attract and retain qualified nurses (Ives Erickson and Pappas 2020).

Nurses make up the majority of the healthcare workforce and spend the most one‐on‐one time with patients and families, providing direct care and implementing research interventions on the front lines (Powell 2015). Thus, they have a unique perspective to recognise when system policies or nursing care workflows fail patients, families and populations (Powell 2015). This positions them to ask the most important and relevant research questions that can directly impact care delivery and patient outcomes. Therefore, nurses should be encouraged and equipped to ask questions about their practice and supported in conducting research that identifies innovative solutions to clinical problems (Ives Erickson and Pappas 2020).

Nursing research includes the systematic inquiry that generates new knowledge and evidence, which can then be evaluated and disseminated to inform nursing practice and advance the profession (Mulkey 2021). Research can begin with a literature review to highlight gaps, then proceed with data collection and analysis, followed by the reporting of study results and interpretations to the healthcare community (Ives Erickson and Pappas 2020; Polit and Beck 2020). EBP includes searching, retrieving, appraising, synthesizing and incorporating current high‐quality evidence with patient values and preferences, including context, into clinical practice to improve nursing care (Cline et al. 2017). EBP is considered a problem‐solving approach to the delivery of nursing care that incorporates the best evidence from high‐quality research studies into the delivery of everyday nursing care (Camargo et al. 2018). It is well‐established within the profession of nursing that to improve patient outcomes, nursing care must be based on the most current evidence (Twelvetree et al. 2019). For nurses to have this evidence upon which to base their practice, they must be granted the opportunity to readily engage in clinically oriented nursing research to produce and develop clinical evidence that they can use to guide their care decisions (Rivera and Shelley 2024; Twelvetree et al. 2019).

Nurses have a professional responsibility to incorporate quality evidence into their practice when it exists and also to generate evidence to answer clinical questions and fill gaps where evidence does not exist (Alomari et al. 2020; Ives Erickson and Pappas 2020). According to the American Nurses Association (ANA), nurses have an ethical obligation to engage in knowledge development, research and scholarly inquiry. Under Provision 7.1 of the ANA Code of Ethics for Nurses, nurses should engage in knowledge production to inform nursing practice, using a diversity of methodologies and ways of knowing to contribute to nursing knowledge (American Nurses Association 2025).

Even though nurses have a professional and ethical responsibility to conduct research, thereby enabling patients to receive high‐quality, safe care that results in improved outcomes, clinical nurses face significant barriers and are rarely given the opportunity to fully engage in clinical research (Henshall et al. 2023). Nursing is considered to be an academic discipline, but the infrastructure that supports nurses' engagement in research is variable and most often underdeveloped (Shepherd et al. 2022). Clinical nurses rarely perform clinical research (Amicucci et al. 2022) because they face significant barriers that prevent them from conducting nursing‐focused research studies (Arthur et al. 2019; Bench et al. 2019; Duffy et al. 2016). Therefore, there is a significant gap: nurses and organizations recognise the value and professional responsibility of nurses' engagement in clinical research, yet nurses are rarely supported to do so. To address this gap, it is important to identify barriers that prevent nurses from engaging in research, as well as facilitators that could be implemented within programmes and initiatives to increase nurses' successful engagement in research.

Aim

To identify and map evidence on factors that influence nurses' engagement in clinical research.

Methods

Study Design

A comprehensive scoping review was conducted to map and identify factors that influence clinical nurses' engagement in research and/or EBP. This scoping review followed the JBI methodology for scoping reviews (Peters et al. 2024), adhered to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews (PRISMA‐ScR) checklist (see File S1) (Tricco et al. 2018) and has been registered with the Open Science Framework (registration number: osf.io/5vwe9). Given the substantial number of included articles (N = 286) and the important distinction between nursing research and EBP activities, the research team strategically organized the included articles into three categories, articles addressing factors that influence nurses' engagement in: (a) research, (b) EBP and (c) both research and EBP activities simultaneously. This categorisation reflects the fundamental difference between nursing research (the systematic generation of nursing evidence through inquiry) and EBP (the utilisation and implementation of existing evidence into clinical practice). By analysing these articles separately according to their grouping, the team aimed to identify both differences and similarities in the barriers and facilitators affecting nurses' engagement in research versus EBP activities, while also highlighting how they are synergistically integrated. This paper reports findings from articles on factors that influence nurses' engagement in research.

Search Methods

Following Peters et al. (2020) guidelines, an initial search was conducted in Google Scholar to identify seed articles that met all the inclusion criteria and triggered none of the exclusion criteria. Next, the relevant keywords related to ‘engagement’ were identified in the titles and abstracts of the seed articles. These keywords, along with keywords associated with evidence‐based practice or research, were used to develop a search strategy in Medline All (Ovid). In addition, the search strategies from the reviews conducted by Boaz et al. 2015 and Chalmers et al. (2023) were adapted and incorporated into the final search strategy for this review. Prior to database translation, the final Medline search was pilot tested with the seed articles to ensure that all were captured by the search.

The final search utilized proximity searching in order to focus results on nurse engagement with EBP or research. Keywords for barriers or facilitators were not incorporated to ensure that the search was comprehensive and articles would not be missed.

Databases searched included: Medline All (Ovid), Embase (Ovid), APA PsycInfo (Ovid), CINAHL Plus with Full Text (Ebsco) and Scopus. The search was the same for all databases, except for the proximity operator, which was translated to be appropriate for the database platform. The search was not limited by date or language. The final searches for all databases are available in File S2.

Inclusion and/or Exclusion Criteria

Population

The population included in this review article was nurses, including registered nurses, nurse practitioners, licensed practical nurses and advanced practice nurses, who work within the clinical setting. Nursing attendants and nursing aids were excluded. Nursing students enrolled in a baccalaureate programme were excluded; however, nurses enrolled in a graduate degree programme were included. Articles that included physicians and allied healthcare professionals were included if > 50% of the sample size were nurses.

Concept

The concept of this review was factors, including barriers and facilitators, that influenced nurses' ability to engage in research activities. Nursing research was defined as the systematic inquiry that generates new knowledge and evidence, which can then be evaluated and disseminated to inform nursing practice and advance the nursing profession (Mulkey 2021; Polit and Beck 2020). Therefore, articles had to discuss factors that directly enabled or inhibited nurses' engagement in the research process (i.e., attaining research training and mentorship, developing a research proposal, collecting data, analysing data, writing findings and manuscripts and disseminating study findings). Articles that included nurses' engagement in the EBP process were not included in this review paper but will be reported in a future manuscript.

Context

The context was limited to inpatient care units, including: intensive care units, emergency departments, oncology units, mental health units, neonatal intensive care units and outpatient care units. Outpatient care clinics were included as long as they were affiliated with a tertiary care hospital. Community health, public health, physician offices, rural health and academia were excluded. Articles reporting research studies were limited to those conducted in developed countries defined by the United Nations Department of Economic and Social Affairs (United Nations 2025). See File S3 for list of 37 countries. This restriction was used to ensure the included articles reported findings from healthcare systems with resource levels, workforce structures and regulatory expectations similar to those in North America. Review articles conducted by authors living in any country were included.

Types of Sources

Qualitative, quantitative and mixed‐methods studies were included, along with reviews (e.g., systematic review, meta‐analysis, literature review and scoping review), project descriptions, quality improvement studies, discussion papers and case studies published in English in 2010 or after. A date limiter was applied to the publication date during full‐text screening as decided by the research team for the following reasons: (a) to keep the final number of included studies manageable, (b) to ensure the articles reflected contemporary models of healthcare delivery and (c) to ensure the articles captured the current conceptualisation of nursing clinical research. Grey literature, including government reports, dissertations, theses, editorials, opinion pieces and conference abstracts, was not searched or included.

Study Selection

All titles and abstracts from the search were uploaded into Covidence (Veritas Health Innovation 2020) and duplicates were removed. The inclusion and exclusion criteria were pilot tested on 50 randomly selected titles and abstracts until the reviewers reached a minimum inter‐rater reliability of 90%. All titles and abstracts were screened by two independent reviewers (UC, LM, JS) based on the inclusion and exclusion criteria. Articles that were deemed included were retrieved in full text and screened again in their entirety by two independent reviewers and reasons for exclusion were recorded. Any discrepancy at any phase of screening was resolved by discussion. See Figure 1 for the results of the search and screening process reported in a PRISMA‐ScR (Tricco et al. 2018).

Data Abstraction

Data from the included articles were extracted into a table adapted from the JBI data extraction instrument (Peters et al. 2020). The data extraction table was pilot tested on the first 10 articles that appeared in alphabetical order as per the authors' last name. Elements of the data extraction table included: authors, date, country, aim/purpose/research question(s), sample with participant characteristics, methods/study design, intervention (if used), measurement tools/theoretical framework (if used)/outcome measures, barriers, facilitators, overall findings, recommendations and outcomes. Quality appraisals were not conducted because they are not required for the JBI scoping review methodology (Peters et al. 2020).

Data Analysis and Presentation

The articles have been compiled and are presented in Table 1 with an overview of the findings from each one. The extracted data from the findings were analysed using thematic analysis (Braun and Clarke 2006). First, data were coded by placing labels on segments of data. Then, the codes were collated and grouped into categories based on similarities. Categories were then collapsed into themes and a description of each is provided.

Results

A total of 11,051 records were identified through database searching on April 28, 2025. After duplicates were removed, 5544 articles were screened by title and abstract. Of these, 830 full‐text articles were assessed for inclusion, resulting in 286 articles included in the larger scoping review. During data extraction, 79 of the 286 were identified as articles reporting factors specific to nurses' engagement in research and are reported in this paper. Articles reporting nurses' engagement in EBP and in EBP combined with research will be reported in future manuscripts.

Characteristics of Included Articles

A total of 79 articles were included in this review. There were a variety of methods, including: quantitative descriptive (n = 26), qualitative (n = 21), mixed methods (n = 14) and reviews (n = 3). Other methods included: concept analysis (n = 2), case study (n = 5), discussion paper (n = 3), descriptive practice report (n = 1), programme description (n = 2) and descriptive programme evaluation (n = 2). No articles were randomized controlled trials. Dates ranged from 2010 to 2024 and all articles were published in English. Countries where the articles were conducted included: United States (n = 28), United Kingdom (n = 24), Australia (n = 8), Denmark (n = 3), Canada (n = 3), Sweden (n = 3), Norway (n = 2), Italy (n = 2), Spain (n = 2), Korea (n = 1), Ireland (n = 1) and International (n = 2). See Figure 2 for a representation of the distribution of countries. See Table 1 for a summary of included articles.

Barriers

During data analysis, it was evident that factors influencing nurses' engagement in research were either barriers that prevented nurses' engagement or facilitators that enabled nurses' engagement. Barriers to nurses' engagement in clinical research included: (a) insufficient time, (b) inadequate research experience, training and mentorship, (c) lack of motivation, (d) insufficient leadership support and (e) an unsupportive research culture within the organization. Insufficient Protection of Research Time Amid Competing Clinical Demands

The most frequently reported barrier to nurses' ability to engage in research was a lack of time, reported in 47 articles. Reasons for a lack of time included: nurses were not provided with designated and protected time away from clinical duties (Akerjordet et al. 2012a; Arthur et al. 2019; Davis et al. 2019; Higgins et al. 2010; McKee et al. 2017; Mendoza et al. 2023; Montgomery et al. 2021; Pintz et al. 2018; Snelgrove and James 2011; Twelvetree et al. 2019), shift patterns that did not allow nurses to attend meetings or participate in research activities (Bench et al. 2019; Hwang et al. 2024; Matikainen 2017; Mazzella Ebstein et al. 2020), competing clinical demands (Colwill et al. 2024; Evans et al. 2014; Higgins et al. 2010; Lehmkuhl et al. 2020; Lieschke et al. 2022; Mazzella Ebstein et al. 2020; McLaughlin et al. 2013), high patient acuity (Williams et al. 2024) and patient‐care backlogs (Martin 2017). Nurses could experience challenges juggling research activities with their clinical role (Evans et al. 2014; Hwang et al. 2024; Janerka et al. 2024; Landeen et al. 2017; Snelgrove and James 2011) because nurses are often expected to engage in research on their own time, on top of their clinical role and are not compensated for their time or released from their clinical duties (Jeffs et al. 2013; Matikainen 2017; Walsh et al. 2023). While on shift, they often face heavy workload pressures that prevent them from engaging in research, as reported in 18 articles. When nurses do have release time from their clinical duties to engage in research, there is often a lack of staff coverage to backfill the shifts that they were released from due to staff shortages (Chlan et al. 2020; Janerka et al. 2024; Jeffs et al. 2013; Matikainen 2017; Morrison et al. 2022; Scarsini et al. 2022). Unfortunately, for those nurses who do engage in research, time constraints result in smaller‐scale projects (Chlan et al. 2020) and the inability to keep up to date with research protocols (Brown et al. 2018).

Inadequate Research Experience, Training and Mentorship

The second most reported barrier to nurses' ability to engage in research was a lack of knowledge and understanding of research and the research process, reported in 37 articles. This included limited research skills and experience (Colwill et al. 2024; Davis et al. 2019; Fullam et al. 2018; Higgins et al. 2010; Kelly et al. 2016; Latimer and Kimbell 2010; Lehmkuhl et al. 2020; Mazzella Ebstein et al. 2020; McLaughlin et al. 2013; Morrison et al. 2022; Snelgrove and James 2011; Walden et al. 2015) that was most often due to a lack of formal training (Kelly et al. 2016; Morrison et al. 2022; Nowlin et al. 2021; Ramon et al. 2022; Robichaud‐Ekstrand 2016; Williams et al. 2024), which is a leading cause of nurses' low confidence in conducting research. Specific research skills that nurses often lacked included: preparing and submitting ethics applications, writing funding applications, setting up study procedures, writing research protocols and collecting and analysing data (Alomari et al. 2020; Drury et al. 2024; Engh et al. 2022; Evans et al. 2014; Gallagher et al. 2021; Kelly et al. 2016; Khair et al. 2012; Latimer and Kimbell 2010; Martin 2017; Matikainen 2017; McLaughlin et al. 2013; Nowlin et al. 2021). Oftentimes, this stemmed from a lack of prior research education in the nurses' undergraduate curriculum (Arthur et al. 2019; Chlan et al. 2020; Drury et al. 2024; Janerka et al. 2024; Morrison et al. 2022; O'Brien et al. 2022; Siedlecki and Albert 2017; Snelgrove and James 2011; Weitzel and Robinson 2011). Most nurses' higher level research training occurred during graduate training (Bench et al. 2019; Gonzalez‐de la Torre et al. 2023). Unfortunately, when research training opportunities were offered, attendance could be low (Hwang et al. 2024) and competition was high when the training took the form of fellowships (Twelvetree et al. 2019).

There were a variety of areas in which nurses lacked research training and experience. For example, nurses do not realise or know that research is time‐consuming (McKee et al. 2017), nor do they know how to use research technology and platforms (e.g., quantitative and qualitative data analysis software) (Gullatte and Corwin 2018; Pintz et al. 2018; Robichaud‐Ekstrand 2016). Nurses reported that they were unable to read and understand research articles because they were not familiar with the research terminology and language (Bench et al. 2019; Higgins et al. 2010; Hwang et al. 2024; Martin 2017; Morrison et al. 2022; Robichaud‐Ekstrand 2016). Most nurses do not know how to serve as a Principal Investigator on a study and oftentimes to be a Principal Investigator, nurses must have an adjunct faculty appointment at an academic institution to properly hold and manage the research funding (Gullatte and Corwin 2018). The majority of nurses are not familiar with the research process (Colwill et al. 2024; Gettrust et al. 2016) or have experience navigating institutional processes (Lehmkuhl et al. 2020), including applying for Institutional Review Board approval (Scarsini et al. 2022). Because nurses lack confidence and experience in research, they may be hesitant to discuss research with patients and families (Krewulak et al. 2024). It is particularly difficult for new graduate nurses to engage in research because they are at the beginning phases of transitioning to professional practice and adding research to their role can feel overwhelming (Crozier et al. 2012; Matikainen 2017).

A key barrier to nurses engaging in research is insufficient mentoring capacity (Chlan et al. 2020; Gallagher et al. 2021; Kelly et al. 2016; Kress 2018; Morrison et al. 2022; Robichaud‐Ekstrand 2016; Scala et al. 2016; Snelgrove and James 2011; Walsh et al. 2023; Williams et al. 2024) due to a lack of access to experts (Akerjordet et al. 2012b), research supervision and support (Akerjordet et al. 2012a) and experienced mentors (Drury et al. 2024). Specifically, nurses rarely have access to experienced mentors with advanced research training to receive assistance in formulating a research question (Hwang et al. 2024), proposal writing (Kelly et al. 2016; Pintz et al. 2018; Westlake et al. 2024), drafting ethics applications, study development (Kelly et al. 2016), study implementation (Brown et al. 2018), data analysis (Westlake et al. 2024) and publishing (Khair et al. 2012). Mentorship can be in the form of a dedicated nurse scientist (Pintz et al. 2018), PhD‐prepared nurses working in an organization (Gonzalez‐de la Torre et al. 2023; Jamerson and Vermeersch 2012) or partnerships between the academic and clinical setting (Higgins et al. 2010; McKee et al. 2017; Robichaud‐Ekstrand 2016). However, it is important that mentors not only have advanced research training, but also expertise on the research topic (McKee et al. 2017). When there are academic‐clinical partnerships, tension can arise when there are different expectations and supervision models (Back‐Pettersson et al. 2013).

Negative Internal Beliefs Resulting in a Lack of Motivation to Engage in Research

Nurses can hold negative attitudes towards engaging in research (Bohman et al. 2013; Brown et al. 2018; Farquharson 2024; Matikainen 2017) and be sceptical (Higgins et al. 2010), which results in a lack of motivation or desire to do or engage in clinical research (Khair et al. 2012; Pepper et al. 2024; Walsh et al. 2023). Most often, nurses lack confidence as a result of the belief that they do not have the skills, knowledge or abilities required to engage in research (Bench et al. 2019; Berthelsen and Holge‐Hazelton 2015, 2017; Carter et al. 2020; Duffy et al. 2015; Khair et al. 2012; Matikainen 2017; Morrison et al. 2022; O'Brien et al. 2022; Pepper et al. 2024; Robichaud‐Ekstrand 2016; Siedlecki and Albert 2017; Snelgrove and James 2011; Walsh et al. 2023). Some nurses do not perceive themselves as having the ability or authority to use knowledge generated from research to implement practice changes (Weitzel and Robinson 2011), initiate a research project or seek funding (Westlake et al. 2024). Some nurses are fearful, anxious and intimidated about the idea of conducting research (Alomari et al. 2020; Bench et al. 2019; Engh et al. 2022; Turjanica et al. 2013; Walden et al. 2015) because the research process can be viewed as complex and onerous (Snelgrove and James 2011). There is a perspective that only nurses with graduate training or PhDs are qualified and positioned to do research (Siedlecki and Albert 2017), along with a negative perception of research due to previous academic experiences (Alomari et al. 2020) or prior research experiences that were frustrating (Martin 2017).

Some nurses simply do not have an interest or desire to engage in nursing research (Akerjordet et al. 2012a; Brown et al. 2018; Fowler and Brown 2020). This can be especially true for senior nurses who are near retirement and do not want to learn new skills (Martin 2017). While other nurses lack motivation due to burnout and lack the energy to invest in learning new research skills (Bench et al. 2019). ‘Good nursing’ can be viewed as providing hands‐on clinical care (Higgins et al. 2010), where research is not perceived as part of the nursing role (Bench et al. 2019; Brown et al. 2018; Colwill et al. 2024; Evans et al. 2014; Higgins et al. 2010; Hwang et al. 2024; Kress 2018; Krewulak et al. 2024; McKee et al. 2017; Shepherd et al. 2022) or relevant to current daily practice (Bohman et al. 2013; Higgins et al. 2010; Kelly et al. 2016). Rather, nursing is considered to be strictly a practice profession and does not need to include research (Gonzalez‐de la Torre et al. 2023). Engaging in clinical research can be viewed as something done by physicians and the medical community (Crozier et al. 2012) and when nurses do engage in research, it is considered a luxury (Higgins et al. 2010). Surprisingly, some nurses do not believe that the nursing profession requires a research‐based knowledge (Gonzalez‐de la Torre et al. 2023) and have the perception that research is imposed on them, with no choice (Fowler and Brown 2020; Higgins et al. 2010).

Nurses may hold misconceptions about the ethical and legal aspects of research. For example, they can view research as burdensome on families during times of stress (Brown et al. 2018) and believe that children should not be research participants (Brown et al. 2018). Also, nurses can be fearful that something could go wrong with the research study (Brown et al. 2018) and worry about the ethical implications, such as consent and equal treatment (Brown et al. 2018; McKee et al. 2017).

Limited opportunities for nurses to implement their research study in the clinical setting and to disseminate their research findings into practice decrease nurses' motivation to engage in research (Back‐Pettersson et al. 2013; Chlan et al. 2020). This results in nurses having no sense of ownership of the study and the findings (Fowler and Brown 2020; Higgins et al. 2010). The fast‐paced clinical environment can result in nurses lacking ambition and motivation to also take on research projects (Bohman et al. 2013). Nurses can be hesitant to invest time into research because they fear they will do all the work to conduct a research study but be unsuccessful at publishing their results (McKee et al. 2017) due to poor writing skills (Siedlecki and Albert 2017). Some nurses who engage in research have feelings of isolation from colleagues who do not engage in research (Back‐Pettersson et al. 2013). Lastly, nurses can feel undervalued due to a lack of recognition and communication about scholarly research work across academic and clinical systems (Gullatte and Corwin 2018; Montgomery et al. 2021): they do not feel nursing research is valued by the organization and therefore, not worth conducting.

Insufficient Support From Organizations and Leadership

Organizational and institutional leaders make operational decisions that determine how clinical nurses allocate their time. A frequently reported barrier to nurses engaging in research was a lack of institutional support (Loke et al. 2014; Montgomery et al. 2021; Morrison et al. 2022; Nowlin et al. 2021; O'Brien et al. 2022; Ramon et al. 2022; Scala et al. 2016; Scarsini et al. 2022). This was demonstrated by inadequate support from leadership (Bench et al. 2019; Bohman et al. 2013; Janerka et al. 2024; Jeffs et al. 2013; Kelly et al. 2016; Robichaud‐Ekstrand 2016; Scarsini et al. 2022; Walsh et al. 2023), nurses' beliefs that their organization did not value nursing research (Bohman et al. 2013; Farquharson 2024) and scarce institutional strategies and opportunities for nursing research (Evans et al. 2014; Scala et al. 2019; Shepherd et al. 2022; Twelvetree et al. 2019; Williams et al. 2024). It was common for studies to report that organizations did not prioritise nurses engaging in research (Higgins et al. 2010; Jeffs et al. 2013; Morrison et al. 2022; Shepherd et al. 2022), but rather prioritized nursing time on direct patient care (Khair et al. 2012; Weitzel and Robinson 2011). Nurses not only faced a lack of support at the leadership and organization level, but also at the unit level from their managers (Fullam et al. 2018; Higgins et al. 2010; Khair et al. 2012; Kress 2018; Lieschke et al. 2022; Martin 2017; O'Brien et al. 2022), where managers prioritized nursing time on short‐term clinical care goals over the long‐term benefits of empowering nurses to gain research skills (Higgins et al. 2010) and viewed research as a potential for disruptions to clinical workflows (Gettrust et al. 2016).

Organizational leadership often provided limited financial support for nursing research (Akerjordet et al. 2012b; Arthur et al. 2019; Chlan et al. 2020; Montgomery et al. 2021). Rarely do nurses receive funding to conduct nursing‐led research studies (Bench et al. 2019; Davis et al. 2019; Higgins et al. 2010; Hwang et al. 2024; Kelly et al. 2016; McLaughlin et al. 2013; Morrison et al. 2022; Pintz et al. 2018; Robichaud‐Ekstrand 2016; Scala et al. 2016; Scarsini et al. 2022; Siedlecki and Albert 2017; Walsh et al. 2023), including small grant competitions (Scala et al. 2016), nor are funds allocated to support nursing research infrastructure needed for nurses to successfully engage in research (Arthur et al. 2019; Engh et al. 2022; Evans et al. 2014; Higgins et al. 2010; Latimer and Kimbell 2010; Lieschke et al. 2022; Loke et al. 2014; McKee et al. 2017; Morrison et al. 2022; Robichaud‐Ekstrand 2016; Stutzman et al. 2016). Such infrastructure includes: library supports (e.g., health sciences librarian and access to research databases) (Bohman et al. 2013; Kelly et al. 2016; Kress 2018; Scarsini et al. 2022), biostatistical support (e.g., biostatisticians) (Hwang et al. 2024; Kelly et al. 2016; Pintz et al. 2018), training and education (Hwang et al. 2024), secretarial support (Kelly et al. 2016), research assistant/coordinator (Hwang et al. 2024; Kelly et al. 2016), research technology (Kelly et al. 2016; Scarsini et al. 2022), data entry staff (Kelly et al. 2016) and release time for nurses (discussed above). When infrastructure is in place, the organization's lack of communication about resources and infrastructure to support nurses' engagement in research is a barrier. When this happens, nurses are not made aware of the opportunities that they have to engage in training programmes and research opportunities (Lehmkuhl et al. 2020; Mendoza et al. 2023; Robichaud‐Ekstrand 2016; Scala et al. 2019; Shepherd et al. 2022), nor are they aware of the nursing research studies that are occurring within their organization (Krewulak et al. 2024). Insufficient communication at the unit level about nursing lead research projects can prevent nurses from being aware of ongoing projects and able to support and engage in them (Fowler and Brown 2020; Gullatte and Corwin 2018; Hwang et al. 2024).

Lack of leadership support for nursing research translates into a lack of support for nurses' professional development. This includes leadership not integrating the role of a researcher into everyday nursing clinical roles (Ramon et al. 2022) or clarifying nurses' clinical roles and research roles (Alomari et al. 2020; McKee et al. 2017; Williams et al. 2024). When nurses do conduct research, it is seldom included, recognized or valued as part of their performance evaluations (Westlake et al. 2024). Some organizations have restrictions on nurses' ability to serve as Principal Investigator on nursing‐led studies; therefore, they are not able to hold research funding (Carter et al. 2020; Stutzman et al. 2016). Other ways organizational leadership can fail to support nurses' professional development via research include: lack of incentives for nurses to participate and engage in research (Brown et al. 2018; Drury et al. 2024; Morrison et al. 2022; Ramon et al. 2022), no structured clinical‐academic career pathway for PhD nurses (Farquharson 2024) and no clear opportunities for nurses to engage in research (Walsh et al. 2023).

Unsupportive Research Culture

The absence of an organizational culture that supports and encourages clinical nursing research creates significant barriers to nurses' participation (Evans et al. 2014; Fowler and Brown 2020; Janerka et al. 2024; Kelly et al. 2016; Morrison et al. 2022; Scarsini et al. 2022). Various factors contribute to a poor research culture within an organization. One is unsupportive peers and colleagues towards nurses conducting research (Alomari et al. 2020; Gallagher et al. 2021; Kress 2018; Morrison et al. 2022), where nurses feel undervalued by their peers and leaders for the work they commit to nursing research (Dupin et al. 2014). Other factors include limited opportunities for nurses to collaborate in research (Arthur et al. 2019), prioritisation of nursing care and clinical care routines (Higgins et al. 2010; Robichaud‐Ekstrand 2016) and resistance to change by staff and/or the larger organization (Dupin et al. 2014).

Clinical nurses are rarely in a position where they have decision‐making power to advocate for funding allowances to engage in research (Bench et al. 2019). However, other disciplines with more decision‐making power, such as physicians, often do not value, support or prioritise nursing research (Farquharson 2024; Pintz et al. 2018). Nurses and organizational cultures can hold the perspective that only physicians do research and witness clinical research dominated by physician‐led research (Kelly et al. 2016; Martin 2017; O'Brien et al. 2022; Snelgrove and James 2011). This is in part because oftentimes physicians have the opportunities, resources and privileges to do research (Loke et al. 2014). To increase nursing research within an organization, a culture shift is required (Shepherd and Quinn 2024).

Facilitators

While numerous barriers impede nurses' ability to engage in research, there are multiple facilitators that enable and support clinical nurses in successfully participating in and leading nursing research. These facilitators represent opportunities for healthcare organizations, nursing leaders and individual nurses to create workplace cultures and environments that foster research engagement and build nursing research capacity. Facilitators to nurses' engagement in clinical research included: (a) research infrastructure, (b) education, training and mentorship, (c) visionary leadership, (d) unit‐level facilitators, (e) motivating factors and (f) organizational culture. Building Research Infrastructure: Dedicated Nursing Research Programmes and Centres

Visible research programmes and centres within the hospital were effective at creating research‐intensive environments (Farquharson 2024) and increasing nurses' engagement in research (Higgins et al. 2010; Mazzella Ebstein et al. 2020). Examples include: a dedicated director of nursing research (Jeffs et al. 2013), research and development groups (Snelgrove and James 2011), shared governance and research councils (Higgins et al. 2010; Kress 2018; Mazzella Ebstein et al. 2020; Scala et al. 2016; Shepherd et al. 2022; Snelgrove and James 2011; Westlake et al. 2024), a nursing hospital‐based research programme (Kelly et al. 2016), Magnet requirement councils (Pintz et al. 2018), an endowed chair for research funding (Engh et al. 2022), a research office or institute (Scala et al. 2016) and a research unit (Matikainen 2017). It was important to have the presence of these dedicated research staff within the organization (Amicucci et al. 2022; McLaughlin et al. 2013) so that the support services were in close proximity to the clinical nurses (Krewulak et al. 2024). These centres and programmes needed to be flexible and adaptable, able to support the variability in nurses' shift patterns (Jeffs et al. 2013).

Partnerships between the hospital and an academic institution were another way to offer frontline practicing nurses access to research mentorship (Gullatte and Corwin 2018; Higgins et al. 2010; Jeffs et al. 2013; O'Brien et al. 2022; Pintz et al. 2018; Scala et al. 2016, 2019; Stutzman et al. 2016; Walden et al. 2015; Westlake et al. 2024). Within such partnerships, transparency and clear guidelines were required between researchers and institutions (Loke et al. 2014), along with strong leadership to act as a liaison between the academic and clinical sites (Gettrust et al. 2016; Higgins et al. 2010). Organizational policies to support the research process were also required for partnerships to flourish (McLaughlin et al. 2013).

Activities within the organization, oftentimes within the centres and programmes themselves, that helped foster nursing research included: research scholarship programmes (Lieschke et al. 2022; Walden et al. 2015), funded nursing fellowships (Higgins et al. 2010; Scala et al. 2016, 2019; Stutzman et al. 2016), residency programmes (Kelly et al. 2016), a research committee (Carter et al. 2020; Pintz et al. 2018) and monthly fellowship meetings (Carter et al. 2020). Involvement and memberships in societies also helped advance nursing research (Gonzalez‐de la Torre et al. 2023), such as the Theta Tau chapter (Weitzel and Robinson 2011). It was important for nurses to be in a position to be a Principal Investigator (PI) on their own research projects (Scala et al. 2016), which could be accomplished through university adjunct positions or academic‐clinical partnerships. The organization needs to have supportive research infrastructure (Mazzella Ebstein et al. 2020; Scala et al. 2016), including: funding and support for preparation of grant proposals (Akerjordet et al. 2012a; Crozier et al. 2012; Stutzman et al. 2016), library services and resources (access to databases and librarian) (Bohman et al. 2013; Landeen et al. 2017; Pintz et al. 2018; Scala et al. 2016; Siedlecki and Albert 2017), budget support (Kelly et al. 2016; Loke et al. 2014), administrative assistance (Kelly et al. 2016), computer software (Kelly et al. 2016), research material expenses (Kelly et al. 2016), support with data entry, analysis and management (Kelly et al. 2016; Scala et al. 2016), biostatistical support (Carter et al. 2020; Landeen et al. 2017; Lieschke et al. 2022; Scala et al. 2016; Siedlecki and Albert 2017) and manuscript writing (Carter et al. 2020). These services could be offered in the form of consultations (Stutzman et al. 2016).

The most reported infrastructure requirement for nurses to engage in research was protected and dedicated paid research time (Back‐Pettersson et al. 2013; Bench et al. 2019; Brown et al. 2018; Carter et al. 2020; Chlan et al. 2020; Colwill et al. 2024; Crozier et al. 2012; Drury et al. 2024; Engh et al. 2022; Evans et al. 2014; Farquharson 2024; Fullam et al. 2018; Kelly et al. 2016; Khair et al. 2012; Loke et al. 2014; Martin 2017; Mazzella Ebstein et al. 2020; Montgomery et al. 2021; Morrison et al. 2022; Pintz et al. 2018; Scala et al. 2016; Twelvetree et al. 2019; Walsh et al. 2023). This could take the form of adapted work hours (Akerjordet et al. 2012a) and manager‐approved release time (Matikainen 2017), with scheduling support (Williams et al. 2024). For example, release time from a clinical shift 1 day/week to work on research (Aloweni et al. 2017). Since clinical research is costly, nurses need awareness and access to sufficient funding opportunities from internal and philanthropic streams to conduct the research (Chlan et al. 2020; Crozier et al. 2012; Davis et al. 2019; Jeffs et al. 2013).

Cultivating Nurse Research Champions: The Power of Education, Training and Mentorship

When nurses are given opportunities to learn about research, attend educational sessions and gain experience in non‐intimidating and supportive environments (Pintz et al. 2018; Scala et al. 2016; Turjanica et al. 2013), they are more likely to conduct research at the bedside. Research training can be accomplished via structured research education (Alomari et al. 2020; Arthur et al. 2019; Bench et al. 2019; Brown et al. 2018; Colwill et al. 2024; Crozier et al. 2012; Evans et al. 2014; Higgins et al. 2010; Hwang et al. 2024; Kelly et al. 2016; Latimer and Kimbell 2010; Martin 2017; Montgomery et al. 2021; Morrison et al. 2022; O'Brien et al. 2022; Ramon et al. 2022; Scala et al. 2019; Williams et al. 2024). These opportunities need to be provided by experts (Landeen et al. 2017), flexible and offered at central locations with an option for online attendance (Bench et al. 2019; Engh et al. 2022; Hwang et al. 2024). Examples of learning opportunities includ: nursing research fair (Turjanica et al. 2013), education sessions (Higgins et al. 2010), one‐on‐one coaching (Kelly et al. 2016), workshops (Arthur et al. 2019; Bench et al. 2019; Carter et al. 2020; Crozier et al. 2012; Pepper et al. 2024), peer‐to‐peer discussion (Kress 2018) and a research training course (Amicucci et al. 2022; Kress 2018), often in the form of an online module (Kress 2018).

Education should be tailored to clinical nurses' needs (Mazzella Ebstein et al. 2020), practical (Snelgrove and James 2011) and adjusted to meet varying knowledge levels (Weitzel and Robinson 2011). Topics should include: learning about the research process (i.e., research questions, protocol/proposal development, IRB, implementation, monitoring, analysis and evaluation) (Crozier et al. 2012; Fowler and Brown 2020; Kelly et al. 2016; Scala et al. 2016), research methods (Martin 2017), literature searching skills (Akerjordet et al. 2012a), research literacy skills (Evans et al. 2014) and writing and publishing (Carter et al. 2020; Khair et al. 2012; Pepper et al. 2024; Scala et al. 2016). This can be accomplished by implementing journal clubs (Bench et al. 2019; Colwill et al. 2024; Higgins et al. 2010; Kress 2018; Matikainen 2017) where nurses regularly read, engage with and discuss research findings from scientific journals (Amicucci et al. 2022; Scarsini et al. 2022). Nurses appreciated when the learning was fun and hands‐on (Dupin et al. 2014; Loke et al. 2014; Walden et al. 2015), includes open discussion (Robichaud‐Ekstrand 2016), fosters reflection, encourages peer learning (Fowler and Brown 2020), is interactive and participatory in nature (Weitzel and Robinson 2011) and involves peer‐to‐peer support (Back‐Pettersson et al. 2013). Nurses also want opportunities for continuing education (Bohman et al. 2013; Kress 2018; Montgomery et al. 2021) and for nurses who are taking advanced degrees, reimbursement for tuition (Pintz et al. 2018).

Having access to mentorship and support from experienced/senior researchers was a frequently reported facilitator that supported nurses' training and built their confidence, reported in 46 articles. Mentorship was a way nurses could learn about the research process from an experienced nurse researcher who clinical nurses viewed as a role model (Akerjordet et al. 2012a; Jamerson and Vermeersch 2012; Siedlecki and Albert 2017). It was important that there was a clear pathway to receive mentorship from PhD prepared nurse scientists (Arthur et al. 2019; Kelly et al. 2016; Morrison et al. 2022; Scala et al. 2016). Ways this mentorship could be delivered included: structured mentorship meetings (Chlan et al. 2020; Colwill et al. 2024), small group coaching (Gullatte and Corwin 2018) and mentorship programmes (Pintz et al. 2018). The mentors needed to be visible and purposefully make themselves available to assist when needed (Bench et al. 2019; Scala et al. 2016; Shepherd et al. 2022). It was helpful if the mentor was a full‐time employee within the clinical institution (Higgins et al. 2010; Jamerson and Vermeersch 2012), a designated nurse scientist (Pintz et al. 2018), someone with track record of successful funding (Farquharson 2024) and able to identify research areas (Akerjordet et al. 2012a). Mentorship was key to learning because mentors walked alongside clinical nurses as they actively conducted research and assisted them in gaining hands‐on experience (Bohman et al. 2013; Drury et al. 2024; Evans et al. 2014), including supporting them through the IRB process (Scala et al. 2016). Mentors could also allow nurses to shadow them (Bench et al. 2019) or participate in larger nursing‐led studies (Khair et al. 2012).

Visionary Leadership: Empowering Nurses' Research Engagement

Organizational support and endorsement from nurse managers and senior leadership were facilitators of nurses' engagement in research, reported in 40 articles. This included strategic alignment with hospital priorities (Engh et al. 2022; Jeffs et al. 2013; Kelly et al. 2016; Scala et al. 2016; Snelgrove and James 2011), strong stakeholder engagement (Jeffs et al. 2013), Chief Nursing Officer (CNO)/Chief Executive Officer (CEO) support and commitment to research (Jamerson and Vermeersch 2012; Kelly et al. 2016; Scala et al. 2016) and strategic buy‐in from senior managers and academic leaders (Twelvetree et al. 2019). It was important for leadership support, from both hospitals and academic institutions, to be visible and transparent for clinical nurses to see (Bench et al. 2019; Crozier et al. 2012; Davis et al. 2019; Dupin et al. 2014; Engh et al. 2022; Fowler and Brown 2020; Gullatte and Corwin 2018; Higgins et al. 2010; Landeen et al. 2017; Latimer and Kimbell 2010). One way to make leadership support visible was for managers at the unit level to support and encourage nurses to lead and engage in research (Bohman et al. 2013; Gallagher et al. 2021; Higgins et al. 2010; Khair et al. 2012; Loke et al. 2014; Martin 2017; Siedlecki and Albert 2017). This type of leadership environment was often present among hospitals with an emphasis on meeting Magnet designation (Chlan et al. 2020; Kelly et al. 2016; McLaughlin et al. 2013). One way hospital organizations could demonstrate strong leadership in support of nursing research was to provide clear communication about research opportunities (Colwill et al. 2024; Evans et al. 2014; Fowler and Brown 2020) and have physicians on board to support nursing research (Loke et al. 2014).

Unit Level Facilitators

There were unit‐level factors that facilitated nurses' engagement in research. These included implementing nurses who acted as a research champion (Engh et al. 2022; Krewulak et al. 2024; Scala et al. 2016; Shepherd et al. 2022), connecting research projects with ongoing EBP activities (Scala et al. 2016) and integrating research tasks into existing daily workflows (Fowler and Brown 2020; Gettrust et al. 2016; Ramon et al. 2022). It was important to have nurses conducting research that was relevant and applicable to the unit‐level context (Alomari et al. 2020; Aloweni et al. 2017; Brown et al. 2018; Dupin et al. 2014; Evans et al. 2014; Gettrust et al. 2016; Gullatte and Corwin 2018; Hwang et al. 2024; Lieschke et al. 2022; Scala et al. 2016, 2019; Snelgrove and James 2011; Williams et al. 2024), asking research questions and conducting research projects that addressed nurses' clinical concerns (Dupin et al. 2014; Lieschke et al. 2022), problems (Janerka et al. 2024) and topics of interest (Scala et al. 2019), while also being aligned with nurses' clinical roles (Aloweni et al. 2017). Also, it was important that nurses conducting the research were familiar with and understood the unit where the research was conducted, because this helped build the staff nurses' trust in the research team (O'Brien et al. 2022).

Nurses were motivated to engage in research when there were positive outcomes from the research (Fowler and Brown 2020; O'Brien et al. 2022), changes were beneficial for patient care (Gettrust et al. 2016; Morrison et al. 2022) and they received positive feedback from families, validating the care they received (Fowler and Brown 2020). Specifically, research that produced findings that were translatable to practice (Back‐Pettersson et al. 2013; Krewulak et al. 2024; Matikainen 2017; Morrison et al. 2022) and had a clear focus on improving care helped ignite nurses' passion for continuing to engage in research (Lieschke et al. 2022).

Making Research Matter: Motivating Factors

Nurse were motivated to engage in research and found fulfilment doing so when they were given the opportunities to engage in all research processes, including funding support for dissemination (Chlan et al. 2020; Davis et al. 2019; Gonzalez‐de la Torre et al. 2023; Gullatte and Corwin 2018; McLaughlin et al. 2013; Montgomery et al. 2021; Morrison et al. 2022). Dissemination activities included: support to present research at conferences (i.e., internal, national and international conferences) (Back‐Pettersson et al. 2013; Bench et al. 2019; Colwill et al. 2024; Gonzalez‐de la Torre et al. 2023; Martin 2017; Turjanica et al. 2013), invitations to present study findings at Nursing Grand rounds within hospital organizations (Walden et al. 2015), research project displayed in newsletters and the hospital library displays (Weitzel and Robinson 2011), journal publication (Gonzalez‐de la Torre et al. 2023; Krewulak et al. 2024) and communication of findings to those involved (researchers and participants) (Brown et al. 2018; Gonzalez‐de la Torre et al. 2023).

There were various internal motivators that propelled nurses to engage in research, including those who had a desire to develop new skills (Janerka et al. 2024), personal locus of control (Colwill et al. 2024), positive attitudes towards research (Bohman et al. 2013), passion to improve patient care (Morrison et al. 2022; Williams et al. 2024), professional curiosity (Colwill et al. 2024; Lehmkuhl et al. 2020), recognition that research improves patient outcomes (Brown et al. 2018), personal experience of a family member in the hospital (Colwill et al. 2024) and the ability to see a purpose and value in research (Akerjordet et al. 2012b). Other motivating factors included organizational incentive (Drury et al. 2024; Lieschke et al. 2022; Ramon et al. 2022; Scala et al. 2019) and a clinical trigger event that prompted inquiry (Morrison et al. 2022; Siedlecki and Albert 2017). Nurses were propelled to engage in research when they experienced incremental success that built their confidence and gave them a sense of ownership, because this made them feel valued and appreciated for their contributions to research (Alomari et al. 2020; Gettrust et al. 2016; Higgins et al. 2010; Mazzella Ebstein et al. 2020; O'Brien et al. 2022). This could be accomplished by recognizing nurses for their work and research involvement (Brown et al. 2018; Evans et al. 2014; Krewulak et al. 2024; Lieschke et al. 2022; Ramon et al. 2022; Scala et al. 2016; Siedlecki and Albert 2017), especially when improvements were made to clinical care (Krewulak et al. 2024), manuscripts were accepted for publication (O'Brien et al. 2022) or a nurse was highlighted in the media (Turjanica et al. 2013). Nurses could experience personal benefits from engaging in research (Evans et al. 2014), such as increased job satisfaction (Janerka et al. 2024; Scala et al. 2019) and feeling empowered to make a difference in their clinical setting (Morrison et al. 2022; Scala et al. 2019).

One commonly noted factor that motivated nurses to engage in research was whether it could help advance their professional development (Colwill et al. 2024; Dupin et al. 2014; Kelly et al. 2016; Kress 2018; O'Brien et al. 2022; Scala et al. 2019). Ways that nursing research supported nurses' professional development included: continuing education credits (Engh et al. 2022), career and professional advancement (Janerka et al. 2024; Morrison et al. 2022; Scala et al. 2019), job promotion (O'Brien et al. 2022) and enhancing the nurses' role to include research activities (Kelly et al. 2016). Nurses appreciated the professional networking opportunities that accompanied engaging in research because this enabled them to collaborate with other nurses who had similar clinical interests from other organizations (Chlan et al. 2020; Gallagher et al. 2021; Khair et al. 2012; Mendoza et al. 2023; O'Brien et al. 2022; Scala et al. 2019). For some nurses, engaging in research encouraged them to pursue higher education (Pintz et al. 2018), while for others, they believed it increased the credibility of the nursing profession (Scala et al. 2019).

Various individual characteristics of nurses were found to influence their engagement in research. Nurses who held an advanced degree (Amicucci et al. 2022; Arthur et al. 2019; Bohman et al. 2013; Drury et al. 2024; Evans et al. 2014; Hwang et al. 2024; Jamerson and Vermeersch 2012; Kelly et al. 2016), including a master's, graduate studies or doctorate and were in non‐direct care roles (e.g., administration, manager or clinical specialist) (Amicucci et al. 2022; Arthur et al. 2019; Colwill et al. 2024; Drury et al. 2024) were more likely to engage in research. Also, nurses who were male (Amicucci et al. 2022), had a personal commitment to improving patient care (Siedlecki and Albert 2017) and were curious, self‐confident and eager to learn (Morrison et al. 2022) were more likely to engage in research.

Building Organizational Cultures That Inspire and Support Nursing Research

During data analysis it was evidence that it was imperative for nurses to work in a research supportive culture, where nursing research was valued, highlighted and encouraged, at both the administrative and unit level (Bench et al. 2019; Gallagher et al. 2021; Higgins et al. 2010; Kelly et al. 2016; Lieschke et al. 2022; Pintz et al. 2018; Siedlecki and Albert 2017; Snelgrove and James 2011; Westlake et al. 2024). There were multiple factors that fostered a research supportive culture, including collaborative multi‐disciplinary research teams (Alomari et al. 2020; Farquharson 2024; Fullam et al. 2018; Gonzalez‐de la Torre et al. 2023; Higgins et al. 2010; Khair et al. 2012; Latimer and Kimbell 2010; McKee et al. 2017; Morrison et al. 2022; Pintz et al. 2018; Scala et al. 2019; Snelgrove and James 2011; Twelvetree et al. 2019). This helped research teams share expertise (Higgins et al. 2010) and collaborate across departments and professional roles (Landeen et al. 2017; Latimer and Kimbell 2010). Another way to gain multi‐disciplinary involvement was to incorporate research project discussions within multi‐disciplinary rounds (Krewulak et al. 2024). Within the research team itself, having positive dynamics and good communication among team members were important (Hwang et al. 2024). Nurses appreciated having support from their peers when conducting research (Bench et al. 2019; Colwill et al. 2024; Matikainen 2017; Shepherd and Quinn 2024; Williams et al. 2024), which could be in the form of peer involvement (Siedlecki and Albert 2017) and a network of peers who engage in research (Pepper et al. 2024).

Supportive research cultures were described as having the following traits: active learning, a spirit of excellence, inquiry (Kelly et al. 2016; Latimer and Kimbell 2010; Matikainen 2017), critical thinking (Dupin et al. 2014), problem solving (Morrison et al. 2022) and respect (Alomari et al. 2020; Morrison et al. 2022). Organizations that openly recognized the value of research for enhancing patient care (Lehmkuhl et al. 2020; Westlake et al. 2024), regularly communicated about nursing research activities (O'Brien et al. 2022), encouraged nurses to pursue advanced degrees (Scala et al. 2016) and normalized nursing research in everyday practice (Landeen et al. 2017; Robichaud‐Ekstrand 2016) fostered positive research cultures.

Multi‐Level Framework of Barriers and Facilitators

Based on the synthesized findings, we developed a multi‐level framework that depicts how barriers and facilitators at four levels (i.e., individual, unit, organizational and system) influence nurses' engagement in clinical research (see Figure 5). At the individual level, nurses' research skills and level of education interact with their personal motivations and professional commitments to improving care. At the unit level, managers' support, research champions, staffing patterns and the priority placed on research in daily practice can either facilitate or hinder nurses' ability to conduct clinical research. At the organizational level, a formal research infrastructure and strategic priorities that foster a culture of research determine if nursing research excellence is a focus within the organization. Finally, system‐level factors, such as academic partnerships with mentorship and nurses' access to funding and regulatory approval, greatly affect the success of clinical nurses in formally developing and implementing a research project. Arrows in the framework illustrate that the four levels are dynamic and interdependent rather than linear. They influence one another in their ability to either support or hinder nurses' engagement in research. For example, the provision of paid release time at the organizational level can be hindered if not implemented at the unit level, with nurse managers' support and appropriate staffing levels.

Strategic Recommendations for Building Nursing Research Excellence

Some authors of included articles made future recommendations based on their findings. These recommendations included strong visionary leadership (Akerjordet et al. 2012a; Back‐Pettersson et al. 2013; Bench et al. 2019; Engh et al. 2022; Jeffs et al. 2013; Kelly et al. 2016; Scala et al. 2019) and organizational support (Back‐Pettersson et al. 2013; Jeffs et al. 2013) that values and prioritizes nursing research (Higgins et al. 2010; Lehmkuhl et al. 2020; Matikainen 2017) and creates a positive research culture within the institution (Akerjordet et al. 2012a; Bench et al. 2019; Drury et al. 2024; Janerka et al. 2024; Kelly et al. 2016; Walden et al. 2015; Walsh et al. 2023). Leadership that recognizes and acknowledges nurses' scholarly contributions (Engh et al. 2022), with managers who create environments where nurses feel inspired to engage in research (Akerjordet et al. 2012b, 2012a; Amicucci et al. 2022; Brown et al. 2018; Morrison et al. 2022). To do this, leadership should conduct a comprehensive assessment of the research infrastructure (Montgomery et al. 2021) and develop various programmes and opportunities, such as research training and education (Akerjordet et al. 2012b; Bohman et al. 2013; Brown et al. 2018; Drury et al. 2024; Gettrust et al. 2016; Kelly et al. 2016; Kress 2018; Krewulak et al. 2024; Mazzella Ebstein et al. 2020; Ramon et al. 2022; Scala et al. 2019; Williams et al. 2024), mentorship and research supervision (e.g., fellowship programmes, scholarships, internship programmes) (Arthur et al. 2019; Drury et al. 2024; Gallagher et al. 2021; Kelly et al. 2016; Lieschke et al. 2022; Matikainen 2017; Mazzella Ebstein et al. 2020; Robichaud‐Ekstrand 2016; Walsh et al. 2023; Williams et al. 2024) and protected and paid release time for nurses to do research (Akerjordet et al. 2012b; Gonzalez‐de la Torre et al. 2023; Kelly et al. 2016; Landeen et al. 2017; Mazzella Ebstein et al. 2020; Walsh et al. 2023; Williams et al. 2024).

The development of clinical‐academic partnerships should be explored, because they are a beneficial way to increase mentorship and training among clinical nurses (Arthur et al. 2019; Back‐Pettersson et al. 2013; Landeen et al. 2017; Robichaud‐Ekstrand 2016). When research does take place, leadership should communicate this to nurses to increase the visibility of research projects within organizations (Brown et al. 2018) and ensure patients are involved in nursing research to increase the relevance of the project (Robichaud‐Ekstrand 2016). Funding should be established for both small and large scale studies (Higgins et al. 2010; Jeffs et al. 2013) and increased infrastructure developed that includes education, multi‐disciplinary collaboration, networking opportunities, financial support, translation of research findings and research assistant support for nurses (Alomari et al. 2020; Amicucci et al. 2022; Arthur et al. 2019; Back‐Pettersson et al. 2013; Higgins et al. 2010; Krewulak et al. 2024; Lieschke et al. 2022; Robichaud‐Ekstrand 2016). Overall, nurses of today need to work within organizations that are dedicated to the provision of high‐quality and safe care to patients and families by providing nurses with opportunities to gain experience conducting and engaging in clinical research (Alomari et al. 2020).

Interventions Geared to Increase Nurses' Engagement in Clinical Research

Twenty‐three of the 79 included articles involved an innovation or intervention implemented among clinical nurses aimed at increasing their engagement with research. In one article, nurses participated in an action research team for a nurse‐led study (Alomari et al. 2020). Eight articless involved the development of a programme that took a multi‐modal approach, with a combination of activities geared at increasing nurses' engagement in research (e.g., release time from clinical shifts, education and mentorship) (Aloweni et al. 2017; Back‐Pettersson et al. 2013; Chlan et al. 2020; Crozier et al. 2012; Fullam et al. 2018; McKee et al. 2017). Two of the mentioned programmes included a fellowship (Shepherd et al. 2022; Stutzman et al. 2016), while two interventions were solely a fellowships where nurses were educated and supported to learn about the research process and conduct a research project (Carter et al. 2020; Mazzella Ebstein et al. 2020). Two articles included the development of an academic‐practice partnership between a hospital organization and an academic school of nursing, which was accompanied by faculty mentorship (Davis et al. 2019; Shepherd and Quinn 2024). One academic‐practice partnership involved the hiring of a paid clinician–scientist (Gullatte and Corwin 2018). Other innovations included the implementation of various paid positions, including: research chair (Engh et al. 2022), nurse research facilitator (Jamerson and Vermeersch 2012) and director of nursing (Jeffs et al. 2013). Another innovation involved research training for nurses who were pursuing a master's degree (Landeen et al. 2017), another utilized a nursing research council (Turjanica et al. 2013) and another supported post‐doctoral clinical academics within the hospital organization to conduct research (Twelvetree et al. 2019). Finally, some authors included fun activities to increase nurses' participation in learning about and engaging in research, including: a research workshop (Nixon et al. 2013), a research initiative using cookies to teach the research process (Walden et al. 2015) and holding a nurses' research week (Weitzel and Robinson 2011). Discussion

In this article, evidence on factors that influence nurses' engagement in clinical research was mapped and synthesized. A total of 79 articles were included and descriptions of barriers and facilitators were provided. The most prominent barriers to clinical nurses engaging in research were a lack of protected time and inadequate training and mentorship. This resulted in nurses often feeling unprepared and unable to conduct clinical research. Other barriers included a lack of motivation, insufficient support from leadership and an unsupportive organizational research culture. Although numerous barriers impeded nurses' engagement in research, there were prominent facilitators that supported and created research‐intensive environments within healthcare organizations. These included strong visionary leadership that built nursing research infrastructure, intentional development of a culture of nursing research and inquiry and investment in programmes, centres and fellowships that afforded nurses the opportunity to attain research training, experience and mentorship. The facilitators and barriers are not isolated factors. Rather, they interact across individual, unit organizational and system levels, as captured in the developed multi‐level framework (see Figure 5). This explains why one single uncoordinated approach will have limited impact. For example, holding a research workshop without protected time, leadership support, research mentorship and management approval will have a limited impact on fostering an organizational culture of nursing excellence. Facilitators at all four system levels must be activated for success.

Within nursing research, a paradox of recognition versus action exists among healthcare organizations. Nurses are eager and willing to engage in clinical research and organizations recognise its value; however, many barriers remain, with limited opportunities for nurses. Nurses from an academic hospital in the United States (N = 99) reported that they had a high willingness to engage in research‐related tasks (Nowlin et al. 2021). However, they also reported that they had low to moderate knowledge and ability to perform research (Nowlin et al. 2021). In a study in Norway of clinical nurses from a university hospital (N = 364), 40% had positive attitudes towards research; however, few were engaged in research‐based activities (Akerjordet et al. 2012a). In yet another cross‐sectional study in the United States, oncology nurses (N = 623) reported positive attitudes towards nursing research and believed research should guide their practice. However, they reported significant barriers to engaging in research, including time, knowledge and lack of resources (Arthur et al. 2019). While nurses have a clear appreciation and eagerness towards nursing research, it remains a reality that most are not given the opportunity to conduct research. This is detrimental to patient care because nurses' ability to engage in research is directly related to the safety and quality of care they provide to patients and families (Harding et al. 2016; Ives Erickson and Pappas 2020).

In this scoping review, time was a reported barrier to nurses engaging in research in 51 of the 79 included articles. Given that nurses are eager and willing to conduct research, it is most likely a lack of time that prevents them from conducting clinical research. For many nurses, it is expected that they conduct research in their own time and unpaid, which results in fewer nurses conducting research. The second most frequently reported barrier to nurses' engagement in research, reported in 30 of the included articles, was a widespread lack of research knowledge and skills. One reason for this was insufficient mentorship. Most undergraduate nursing programmes offer nurses one course on EBP, where they are taught to be consumers of research (Coyne et al. 2018). However, they are rarely taught how to conduct sound clinical research. Then, after completion of their bachelor's degree in nursing, they enter clinical roles where they do not engage in research or generate evidence; therefore, any research skills they learned during their education wane. As demonstrated in the findings of this review, visionary leadership and the development and implementation of innovative nursing programmes and interventions are significant ways to increase the generation of nursing evidence within a healthcare organization. This is because these solutions directly combat the two main barriers: they afford nurses paid clinical release time and education and training on research.

Globally, nurses are experiencing moderate to high levels of burnout syndrome (Ge et al. 2023), healthcare organizations are faced with nursing shortages (Muir et al. 2024) and high levels of nurses are exiting the profession (Mulisa et al. 2022). However, there is a solution that leaders of healthcare organizations can use to combat burnout and nursing shortages: promoting a culture of nursing research and innovation by increasing the generation of nursing evidence. This is because when healthcare organizations promote and prioritise nursing research, nurses have significantly increased levels of work satisfaction, are more loyal to the organization and are retained (Ives Erickson and Pappas 2020). One hospital implemented a research‐focused academic‐service partnership to increase research capacity and research productivity (Duffy et al. 2015). After 1 year, research productivity within the hospital increased 33%. Therefore, there is a practical solution to improve the quality and safety of nursing care and to address nursing burnout and shortages: increasing nurses' ability to engage in research and generation new updated evidence. Leaders are in a position to shift the organizational culture to normalise and value nursing research. This scoping review highlights facilitators that leaders of healthcare organizations can utilise to do just that.

Strengths and Limitations

Several limitations should be acknowledged in this scoping review. First, articles were included if they were published in English from 2010 onwards; therefore, relevant articles published in other languages before 2010 would have been excluded. Grey literature was excluded, which could have resulted in the omission of relevant information. The geographic context of included articles was limited to developed countries; therefore, the findings cannot be generalized to low‐ and middle‐income countries.

There are strengths worth noting in this scoping review. First, it followed the established JBI methodology. The search strategy was conducted using a comprehensive process, titles and abstracts were screened by two independent reviewers and all data was extracted by one reviewer and checked by another. A wide range of article types was included, which resulted in a robust number of included articles, and a comprehensive overview of the multiple barriers and facilitators to clinical nurses engaging in research.

Conclusion

Findings from this scoping review provided an overview of the factors that influence clinical nurses' engagement in research. Multiple barriers were identified that prevented nurses from engaging in research, including a lack of paid release time, training and mentorship. However, multiple facilitators were identified that enabled nurses to engage in research, including visionary leadership and the development of programmes and centres that foster a culture of research excellence. A multi‐level fromaework that depicts how barriers and facilitators at four levels (i.e., individual, unit, organizational, and system) influence nurses engagement in research. Leaders and decision‐makers within healthcare organizations can use this framework to understand the barriers that prevent nurses from engaging in research and utilise the facilitators to advance nursing research in clinical settings. Developing a culture of nursing research excellence within healthcare organizations that empowers nurses to generate research evidence will promote nurses' professional development, ensure safe, high‐quality nursing care and help sustain the nursing workforce.
