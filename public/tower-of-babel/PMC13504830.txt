Global evidence on risk factors for the progression of gestational diabetes to type 2 diabetes: an umbrella review of systematic reviews and meta-analyses
Authors: Tadesse Tarik Tamir, Alebachew Ferede Zegeye, Berhan Tekeba, Enyew Getaneh Mekonen, Helen Lamesgin Endalew, Astewil Moges Bazezew, Gebreeyesus Abera Zeleke, Mulugeta Wassie, Alemneh Tadesse Kassie

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionGestational Diabetes Mellitus is one of the most common metabolic complications of pregnancy, affecting approximately 15.6% of pregnancies worldwide. Women diagnosed with gestational diabetes face a significantly elevated risk of developing type 2 diabetes mellitus later in life. Despite the availability of systematic reviews exploring individual risk factors, there is a lack of consolidated evidence that provides a global perspective on the determinants of type 2 diabetes mellitus progression following gestational diabetes. Therefore, we aimed to systematically synthesize findings from existing systematic reviews and meta-analyses.MethodsA comprehensive literature search was conducted across several databases, including PubMed, EMBASE, ScienceDirect, Cochrane library and google scholar, using the PECO (Population, Exposure, Comparator, Outcome) framework. Systematic reviews and meta-analyses published between January 1, 2005, and January 1, 2025, were included. Data were extracted by two reviewers independently and a structured synthesis was conducted due to heterogeneity in reported effect measures.ResultTwelve systematic reviews and meta-analyses comprising over 400 primary studies and more than 6 million participants were included. The most consistently reported risk factors for progression from gestational diabetes mellitus to type 2 diabetes mellitus included insulin use during pregnancy, elevated body mass index before or after pregnancy, and a positive family history of diabetes. Glycemic markers such as fasting blood glucose, oral glucose tolerance test values, and glycated hemoglobin were also frequently associated with increased risk. Additional risk factors included advanced maternal age, non-white ethnicity, hypertension, and recurrent gestational diabetes. In contrast, lactation was consistently associated with a reduced risk of type 2 diabetes mellitus.ConclusionThis umbrella review offers a comprehensive understanding of the complex factors that influence the transition from gestational diabetes to type 2 diabetes. It brings together diverse findings to highlight how clinical indicators, lifestyle factors, and social determinants interact to shape long-term health outcomes. Notably, while certain risk factors such as insulin use, elevated BMI, family history of diabetes, abnormal glycemic markers, advanced maternal age, non-white ethnicity, hypertension, and recurrent consistently increase vulnerability, lactation stands out as a protective behavior. These insights reinforce the importance of individualized postpartum care and proactive risk management to support women’s health beyond pregnancy.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13098-026-02205-0.Keywords: Risk factors, Progression, Gestational diabetes, Type 2 diabetes, Umbrella review

Introduction

Gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) is one of the most common metabolic complications of pregnancy [1, 2]. According to the International Diabetes Federation (IDF) Diabetes Atlas 11th edition, approximately 15.6% of pregnancies globally are affected by GDM, corresponding to nearly one in six live births worldwide While GDM is often transient, its implications extend far beyond the perinatal period [3]. Women diagnosed with GDM face a significantly elevated risk of developing Type 2 Diabetes Mellitus (T2DM) later in life, with estimates suggesting that up to 70% may progress to T2DM within 10 years postpartum [4, 5]. This transition represents a critical window for preventive interventions, yet the global burden and determinants of this progression remain inadequately synthesized.

The progression from GDM to T2DM is influenced by a complex interplay of biological, behavioral, sociodemographic, and environmental factors. Systematic reviews and meta-analyses have identified several high-risk predictors, including pre-pregnancy obesity, insulin use during pregnancy, family history of diabetes, and elevated postpartum glucose levels [6]. Moreover, disparities in healthcare access, socioeconomic status, and ethnicity further compound the risk, particularly in low- and middle-income countries where maternal care services may be limited [2].

Globally, the rising prevalence of diabetes, of which over 90% is type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM)—poses a major public health challenge [3]. The IDF Diabetes Atlas 11th edition estimates that approximately 589 million adults aged 20–79 years were living with diabetes worldwide in 2024, with projections indicating an increase to 853 million by 2050 [3]. The burden is especially pronounced in regions undergoing rapid urbanization and lifestyle transitions, where GDM serves as an early marker of metabolic dysfunction [7, 8]. Despite the availability of systematic reviews exploring individual risk factors [6, 9, 10], there is a lack of consolidated evidence that provides a global perspective on the determinants of T2DM progression following GDM. This umbrella review fills that gap by identifying consistent and high-impact risk factors, highlighting variations across populations and settings, and clinical practice, guide preventive strategies, and support policy development focused on maternal health and diabetes prevention. Understanding these risk factors is essential not only for improving maternal health outcomes but also for curbing the long-term burden of diabetes globally.

Objective and review questions

This umbrella review aims to synthesize global evidence from systematic reviews and meta-analyses on the risk factors associated with the progression of GDM to T2DM. Specifically, it seeks to address the following review questions:

What are the most consistently reported risk factors for the progression from GDM to T2DM across different populations and settings?

Methods and materials

Study protocol

The protocol for this umbrella review was developed in compliance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analysis Protocols (PRISMA-P) 2015 guidelines, and the review results were reported in adherence to the PRISMA 2020 statement (Supplementary File 1). Initially, the presence of an identical umbrella review was checked on PROSPERO, which showed that no similar studies had been submitted. Then, the study’s protocol was established and registered (CRD420251171320).

Searching strategy

A comprehensive literature search was conducted to identify systematic reviews and meta-analyses examining the risk factors associated with the progression of GDM to T2DM. The search was performed across multiple databases including PubMed, EMBASE, ScienceDirect, Cochrane Library, and the search engine, Google Scholar, covering publications from January 1, 2005 to September 1, 2025. For each database, a tailored search strategy was developed using relevant keywords and Boolean operators ("AND", "OR") to combine terms across four conceptual categories: condition, outcome, population, and study type.

In PubMed, the search included terms such as “Gestational Diabetes” OR “GDM” AND “Progression to Type 2 Diabetes” OR “Postpartum Diabetes” AND “Risk Factors” OR “Predictors” AND “Women” OR “Pregnant Women” AND “Systematic Review” OR “Meta-analysis”, with MeSH terms and free‑text keywords using Boolean operators. The EMBASE search utilized Emtree terms and included ‘gestational diabetes’, ‘type 2 diabetes’, ‘risk factor’, and ‘systematic review’ with filters for review articles. The ScienceDirect search used keyword combinations such as “Gestational Diabetes”, “Type 2 Diabetes”, “Risk Factors”, and “Systematic Review”, limited to review articles. For Google Scholar, the search was conducted using the phrase “Allintitle: Gestational Diabetes AND Type 2 Diabetes AND Risk Factors AND Systematic Review”, with manual screening of titles and abstracts to ensure relevance.

In addition, the Cochrane Library was searched to identify systematic reviews that include cohort studies relevant to the progression of GDM to T2DM. The advanced search interface was used to combine controlled vocabulary (MeSH terms) and free-text keywords such as “Gestational Diabetes”, “Type 2 Diabetes”, “Risk Factors”, “Cohort Study”, and “Systematic Review”. Boolean operators were applied to refine the search, and filters were used to limit results to systematic reviews. Where applicable, validated filters for observational studies—such as those developed by the InterTASC Information Specialists’ Sub-Group (ISSG)—were adapted to enhance sensitivity. This approach ensured the inclusion of reviews that incorporate longitudinal evidence and observational data relevant to the research question. Additionally, snowball sampling was employed by reviewing the reference lists of included studies to identify further eligible reviews. This multi-database approach ensured a broad and inclusive capture of global evidence on the progression of GDM to T2DM (see Supplementary File 2).

Eligibility criteria

This umbrella review included systematic reviews and meta-analyses that synthesized evidence on risk factors associated with the progression of GDM to T2DM. Eligible studies were those that focused on women diagnosed with GDM during pregnancy and reported on biological, behavioral, sociodemographic, clinical, or environmental factors contributing to the development of T2DM postpartum. Only systematic reviews and meta-analyses of observational studies were considered. Studies were required to be published between January 1, 2005, and September 2025, and to be accessible in full text. Reviews that did not specifically address progression to T2DM, focused on populations other than women with GDM, or lacked clearly defined outcomes were excluded. Additionally, narrative reviews, scoping reviews, editorials, letters, conference abstracts, and duplicate publications were not considered for inclusion.

Study selection process

Based on the inclusion and exclusion criteria, all searched studies were transferred to Endnote × 9, a reference management software, to discard duplicate studies. After duplications were removed, two reviewers independently read the titles and abstracts of the remaining articles to identify both potentially eligible articles and any articles for which a determination could not be made from the title and abstract alone. Then, the selected full text of the remaining articles was examined for eligibility.

Outcomes and ascertainment

The central focus of this umbrella review was to identify and synthesize risk factors contributing to the progression from GDM to T2DM. Specifically, we aimed to determine which biological, behavioral, sociodemographic, clinical, and environmental factors were consistently associated with an increased likelihood of developing T2DM among women with a prior diagnosis of GDM. These risk factors included, but were not limited to, maternal age, body mass index (BMI), family history of diabetes, insulin use during pregnancy, ethnicity, and postpartum lifestyle behaviors.

To ascertain these outcomes, we relied on the definitions and measurements reported in the included systematic reviews and meta‑analyses. Risk factors were considered valid if they were reported with quantitative effect estimates, such as odds ratios, relative risks, or hazard ratios, or if they were discussed in a qualitative synthesis demonstrating clear methodological rigor. Any variation in definitions or measurement approaches across studies was documented and was considered during data synthesis and interpretation.

Data extraction and management

Two authors (TTT and ATK) independently carried out data extraction from the included systematic reviews and meta‑analyses using a standardized data abstraction form developed in a Microsoft Excel spreadsheet. Articles were screened and selected in two stages: first by title and abstract, followed by full‑text review. Any disagreements during the screening or data extraction process were initially resolved through discussion between the two reviewers. If consensus could not be reached, a third reviewer independently assessed the study and made the final decision. Following the comprehensive search, potentially eligible publications were imported into EndNote X9, and duplicate records were removed. Structured data extraction was conducted using a Microsoft Excel 2021 spreadsheet (Supplementary File 3).

Quality assessment

The quality of the included article was evaluated by two separate reviewers using the Assessment of Multiple Systematic Reviews (AMSTAR 2) method. The new quality assessment tool builds on the previous AMSTAR. The specific included article was extracted based on the 16 times of the AMSTAR 2 tool. The system categorizes specific systematic review evidence into four categories based on its quality: high, moderate, low, and critically low [11] (Table 1).

Data synthesis and statistical analysis

Data were extracted using Microsoft Excel 2021 spreadsheet. The extracted data were displayed using texts and tables. From the outset, we aimed to quantitatively pool the evidence from existing SRMAs that investigated risk factors for the progression from GDM to T2DM through the meta-analysis technique. However, the included SRMAs reported effect estimates using different effect measures, primarily odds ratios (ORs) and risk ratios (RRs), and these could not be reliably converted into a common metric due to lack of information for converting one effect measure to another as the nature of study is a review of reviews. Consequently, a further quantitative meta-analysis of the SRMA findings was not feasible. To address this methodological heterogeneity, we opted to systematically and narratively synthesize the findings. The direction, strength, and consistency of associations of risk factors were summarized across SRMAs to provide a comprehensive overview of the current evidence base.

Results

Study selection

The study selection process for this umbrella review adhered to a systematic and rigorous approach. A comprehensive search was conducted across multiple databases, including PubMed (n = 128), Embase (n = 225), ScienceDirect (n = 900), and the Cochrane Library (n = 56), as well as the search engine Google Scholar (n = 8,730), yielding a total of 10,039 records. After removing 1,339 duplicate entries prior to screening, 8,700 records were assessed by title and abstract. Of these, 7,500 were excluded due to irrelevance to the topic, and 904 were excluded because they were not systematic reviews or meta-analyses. An additional 346 records were excluded at the abstract level due to differences in reported outcomes. Fifty full-text reports were retrieved and assessed for eligibility, with no retrieval failures. Among these, 38 reports were excluded, 29 for providing incomplete information on risk factors and 9 for being interventional studies. Ultimately, 12 studies met the inclusion criteria and were incorporated into the final review (Fig. 1).

Characteristics of the included systematic review and meta-analysis studies

This umbrella review included 12 SRMAs published between 2009 and 2024, encompassing a total of 401 primary studies. The number of studies included in each SRMA ranged from 9 to 129. The total population covered across all SRMAs exceeded 6.2 million women with a history of GDM, reflecting a broad and diverse evidence base. The geographical distribution of the primary studies within the SRMAs was global, though uneven. Europe (n = 138) and North America (n = 122) contributed the largest number of studies, followed by Asia (n = 107), Australia (n = 19), Central and South America (n = 11), and Africa (n = 4). One SRMA included studies from multiple regions without disaggregating them by continent.

In terms of methodological quality, as assessed by the authors using AMSTER 2, the majority SRMAs were rated as having moderate quality (n = 9), while the remaining three were rated as low quality. No SRMA was rated as high quality. The largest SRMA by population size was conducted by You et al. [12], including over 2.8 million participants, while the smallest was by Baptiste-Roberts et al. [13], with just over 7,000 participants.

Overall, the included SRMAs varied in scope, regional representation, and methodological rigor, however, collectively provide a comprehensive overview of the global evidence on risk factors for the progression from GDM to T2DM (Table 1).

Insulin use during pregnancy

Insulin therapy during pregnancy was consistently identified as a significant risk factor for the development of T2DM. Three SRMAs [6, 9, 10] reported strong associations, with ORs ranging from 3.23 [9] to 4.35 [6] and an RR = 3.66 [10]. Additionally, one systematic review without meta-analysis [13] reported relative measures of association between 2.8 and 4.7, further supporting the elevated risk among women who required insulin treatment during pregnancy. Despite the use of different effect measures, the direction and magnitude of the associations were consistent, suggesting that women with GDM who used insulin during pregnancy had higher risk of progression to T2DM compared to those who did use (Table 2).

Body mass index

Both pre-pregnancy and postpartum BMI were frequently assessed and consistently associated with increased risk of T2DM [6, 9, 12, 15]. For pre-pregnancy BMI, three SRMAs [6, 9, 15] reported ORs ranging from 1.10 to 2.97. Postpartum BMI demonstrated stronger associations, with effect sizes ranging from OR 1.18 [15] to RR 10.21 [12]. These findings were consistent across studies, indicating that elevated BMI at various time points is a prominent risk factor (Table 2). However, BMI was not uniformly categorized across studies, with different thresholds and classifications used (e.g., overweight, obesity, or continuous BMI measures). While several reviews noted increased risk at higher BMI levels, a specific cut‑off such as BMI ≥ 25 kg/m2 was not consistently reported or synthesized across the included studies, precluding a standardized threshold‑based conclusion.

Family history of diabetes

A positive family history of diabetes was reported as a significant risk factor in multiple SRMAs [6, 9, 10, 12, 15]. Effect sizes ranged from OR 1.18 to RR 5.21, with consistent directionality across studies, indicating a robust association between familial diabetes history and increased risk of T2DM following GDM (Table 2).

Lactation

Five SRMAs [10, 14, 16–18] examined the association between lactation and T2DM risk, consistently reporting a protective effect. RRs ranged from 0.42 to 0.77, suggesting that breastfeeding was associated with a lower likelihood of diabetes progression among women with prior GDM (Table 2). While the included SRMAs reported a protective association between lactation and subsequent T2DM risk among women with prior GDM, they did not consistently report standardized breastfeeding definitions or minimum duration thresholds [10, 14, 16–18]. Where duration was examined, reporting varied considerably across studies, precluding synthesis of a specific dose–response relationship. As a result, the current evidence supports an overall protective association of lactation with T2DM risk but does not allow firm conclusions regarding the minimum duration or intensity of breastfeeding required for protection.

Recurrence of GDM

Recurrent GDM was identified as a significant predictor of T2DM in two SRMAs [6, 9] with ORs of 2.63 and 2.11, respectively. These findings indicate that repeated episodes of GDM are associated with a substantially increased risk of future diabetes (Table 2).

Glycemic markers

Several SRMAs evaluated glycemic indicators, including fasting blood glucose (FBG), 1-h and 2-h oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT) values, and glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c) levels. FBG was associated with ORs ranging from 1.15 to 1.58 [6, 9] and an RR of 3.57 [10]. The OGTT values showed ORs and RRs ranging from 1.16 to 3.46 [6, 9, 10]. HbA1c levels were among the strongest predictors, with ORs up to 6.62 and an RR of 2.56 [6, 9, 10]. These markers were consistently associated with increased risk across SRMA studies (Table 2).

Maternal age

The association between maternal age and T2DM progression was reported with varying results. Some SRMAs [6, 12] identified moderate to strong associations (OR: 1.71; RR: 6.34), while others [15, 19] reported weaker or non-significant findings. This inconsistency reflects variability in the strength of association across studies (Table 2).

Hypertension

Hypertensive disorders during pregnancy were examined in two SRMAs, with one reporting an OR of 5.19 [6] and another reporting an RR of 1.38 [10]. Both studies indicated a positive association between hypertension and increased risk of T2DM (Table 2).

Ethnicity

Three SRMAs [4, 10, 15] reported on ethnicity, identifying non-white ethnicity as a risk factor for T2DM progression. Effect sizes ranged from OR 1.49 to RR 7.45, with consistent directionality across studies (Table 2).

Macrosomia

The evidence regarding macrosomia (birth weight > 4,000 g) was mixed. One SRMA reported a significant association (OR: 3.30) [6], while another found a non-significant RR of 0.91 [10]. These findings show variability in the reported association across studies (Table 2).

Discussion

This umbrella review revealed consistently reported risk factors for progression of GDM to T2DM among women with history of GDM worldwide using twelve SRMAs conducted from total of four hundred and two primary studies.

Women with a history of GDM are at significantly increased risk of developing T2DM, with estimates suggesting a nearly tenfold higher risk compared to those with normoglycemic pregnancies [1, 2]. This elevated risk has been consistently linked to several clinical, biochemical, and sociodemographic factors. In the current umbrella review, multiple SRMAs reported significantly elevated risks of T2DM among women who required insulin treatment during pregnancy. These findings were further supported by previous studies [20–23]. The observed association between insulin use during pregnancy and increased long‑term diabetes risk likely reflects more severe gestational diabetes phenotypes or underlying β‑cell dysfunction rather than a causal effect of insulin therapy itself. A systematic review of observational and clinical studies highlighted that insulin-treated women with GDM often represent a subgroup with more severe hyperglycemia or earlier onset of glucose intolerance, both of which are known to be associated with long-term metabolic deterioration [22]. Additionally, a systematic review of 108 studies found that insulin use in pregnancy was frequently associated with adverse maternal outcomes such as higher rates of cesarean section, preterm delivery, and hypertensive disorders, as well as fetal complications including large-for-gestational-age infants and neonatal hypoglycemia [23]. These outcomes may reflect the underlying metabolic severity in women requiring insulin, which could also explain their increased risk of developing T2DM postpartum [23]. Moreover, the review emphasized that insulin-treated women often had higher FBG and postprandial glucose (PPG) levels compared to those managed with metformin or lifestyle interventions, despite insulin being the most potent pharmacologic agent available for glycemic control during pregnancy [23]. This paradox may be attributed to delayed initiation of insulin therapy, suboptimal adherence, or barriers to access and monitoring, particularly in real-world settings. Taken together, the evidence suggests that insulin use during pregnancy is not merely a treatment modality but may serve as a clinical marker of more severe GDM phenotypes. These women may benefit from more intensive postpartum surveillance and early preventive interventions to mitigate their elevated risk of T2DM.

Elevated BMI, both before and after pregnancy, emerged from our umbrella review as one of the most consistent predictors of progression from GDM to T2DM. This finding is strongly supported by existing literature, which consistently identifies overweight and obesity as major determinants of subsequent diabetes among women with prior GDM [6, 9, 12, 15, 24–26]. In this respect, studies have demonstrated that women who were overweight or obese before pregnancy have substantially higher likelihood of developing T2DM compared with those with normal BMI, with pooled estimates suggesting approximately a two- to four-fold increase in risk [10, 19]. The relationship also appears to be dose-dependent, with risk rising progressively across BMI categories [24, 26]. Importantly, postpartum adiposity and weight retention further amplify this risk, as women who fail to return to their pre-pregnancy weight or gain weight after delivery are more likely to experience persistent glucose intolerance and eventual progression to T2DM [25]. The biological plausibility of this association is well established. Excess adiposity induces chronic insulin resistance, promotes systemic inflammation, and leads to dysregulated adipokine secretion, all of which increases the metabolic burden on pancreatic β-cells [24]. During pregnancy—a state already characterized by heightened insulin resistance—these mechanisms become accentuated, and women with higher BMI enter this metabolic milieu with reduced compensatory capacity. The resulting β-cell stress accelerates the transition from transient gestational hyperglycemia to permanent glucose dysregulation after delivery. Furthermore, chronic low-grade inflammation associated with obesity contributes to impaired insulin signaling and progressive β-cell dysfunction, reinforcing the pathway from GDM to T2DM [27]. Evidence from observational cohorts and meta-analyses underscores that BMI functions not only as a biological risk factor but also as a modifiable determinant. Lifestyle interventions aimed at postpartum weight reduction and prevention of weight regain have demonstrated potential to substantially reduce diabetes risk in women with a history of GDM [19, 25]. Accordingly, identifying women with elevated pre-pregnancy or postpartum BMI provides a practical means for targeted follow-up and preventive interventions. Collectively, these findings highlight the central role of adiposity in the natural history of diabetes after GDM and underscore the need for postpartum weight management as an integral component of diabetes prevention strategies in this high-risk population.

A positive family history of diabetes also emerged as a consistent and significant predictor of progression from GDM to T2DM. Multiple systematic reviews and meta-analyses have demonstrated that women with a first-degree relative affected by diabetes are at considerably higher risk of developing T2DM following a GDM pregnancy [10, 19, 27]. The association is biologically and genetically plausible: a family history of diabetes reflects shared genetic susceptibility influencing β-cell function, insulin sensitivity, and glucose metabolism, as well as shared environmental and lifestyle factors such as dietary patterns and physical inactivity [24]. Women with such a background may therefore have reduced β-cell reserve prior to pregnancy and are less able to compensate for the physiological insulin resistance of gestation, predisposing them both to GDM and to its persistence or recurrence as T2DM in later life. In addition to its genetic underpinnings, a positive family history can act as a marker of cumulative exposure to obesogenic and sedentary environments, further compounding metabolic risk. Some studies suggest that the predictive value of family history remains significant even after adjusting for BMI, age, and other metabolic variables, indicating an independent effect [27]. Given its ease of ascertainment, family history represents a simple yet powerful clinical tool for risk stratification. Women with both a history of GDM and a family history of diabetes should therefore be prioritized for postpartum screening, lifestyle interventions, and long-term metabolic surveillance. Targeted preventive strategies in this subgroup may offer high yield in reducing the incidence of T2DM among women at greatest genetic and familial risk.

Lactation appears to confer a clinically meaningful and consistent protective effect against progression from GDM to T2DM. Five systematic reviews and meta-analyses in our umbrella review reported a 23–58% lower risk among women who breast-feed compared with those who do not [10, 14, 16–18]. This pattern is concordant with multiple cohort studies and pooled analyses showing that greater lactation intensity and longer duration are linked to lower incidence of T2DM after GDM, and with meta-analytic summaries restricted to women with prior GDM that similarly report protective point estimates [16]. Mechanistically, the association is biologically plausible: lactation acutely increases maternal glucose utilization and energy expenditure, improves insulin sensitivity, and produces favorable shifts in lipid and hormonal milieu (including prolactin-related effects on β-cell function), all of which may reduce the metabolic stress on pancreatic β-cells during the vulnerable postpartum period and thereby delay or prevent the transition to chronic hyperglycemia [28]. Recent metabolic and lipidomic studies provide complementary evidence that intensive lactation induces systemic metabolic changes that correlate with lower diabetes risk. However, definitions of “lactation” differ across studies (ever vs never, exclusive vs partial, and varying duration thresholds), follow-up intervals are heterogeneous, and residual confounding by pre-existing metabolic health remains possible; in particular, reverse causation is a concern because women with poorer β-cell reserve or more severe insulin resistance may both be less able to sustain lactation and more likely to progress to T2DM [28]. Several high-quality analyses attempt to adjust for these factors and still find a protective association, which strengthens confidence in an independent effect, but heterogeneity in measurement and adjustment strategies explains some variability in pooled effect sizes [16, 28]. From a clinical and public-health perspective, the evidence supports promoting and supporting breastfeeding among women with GDM as a potentially high-value, low-risk strategy for secondary prevention of T2DM: lactation is a modifiable behavior that can be encouraged through targeted postpartum programs (lactation support, early initiation, and policies enabling continued breastfeeding) [28].

Recurrent GDM emerged in our umbrella review as a clear and clinically important predictor of progression to T2DM. This finding is well supported by previous studies that women who experience GDM in more than one pregnancy face substantially higher cumulative and relative risks of later T2DM than those whose GDM is limited to a single pregnancy or who never have GDM [29, 30]. It has been repeated reported that recurrence is common (often ~ 30–60% depending on population and diagnostic criteria) and that recurrence conveys additional metabolic risk beyond that of a single affected pregnancy [25, 29, 30]. In this regard, it has also been documented that high GDM recurrence rates and identified recurrent GDM as a marker of persistent insulin resistance and reduced β-cell reserve, which plausibly explains the stepwise increase in diabetes risk seen in longitudinal studies [29]. Biologically, recurrent GDM likely identifies women with more severe or long-standing underlying metabolic dysfunction [25]. Each pregnancy imposes a transient insulin-resistant state; when that physiologic stress unmasks impaired β-cell compensation repeatedly, it signals a lower threshold for decompensation and a higher probability of irreversible β-cell failure later in life In other words, recurrence is both a marker of higher baseline adiposity/insulin resistance and an indicator of cumulative β-cell “wear and tear” from repeated gestational metabolic stress—mechanisms consistent with cohort data showing higher postpartum glucose levels, earlier onset of impaired glucose tolerance, and greater progression to T2DM among women with recurrent GDM [25]. Several studies show that women with recurrent GDM who also required insulin in pregnancy or had higher diagnostic OGTT values exhibit particularly large absolute risks of T2DM, indicating multiplicative effects when recurrence co-occurs with markers of glycemic severity [31].

Glycemic indicators consistently identified as strong predictors of T2DM in women with a history of GDM. In the umbrella review, SRMAs consistently reported that higher FBG, elevated 1-h and 2-h OGTT values, and increased HbA1c levels were associated with substantially higher future diabetes risk [6, 9, 10]. Across studies, the directionality of these associations was remarkably consistent, indicating that impaired glycemic regulation during or immediately following pregnancy reflects underlying β-cell dysfunction and insulin resistance, which are central pathophysiologic drivers of progression to T2DM. It is plausible that elevated fasting glucose reflects basal hepatic glucose overproduction and impaired insulin action, whereas abnormalities in 1-h and 2-h OGTT values capture postprandial glycemic excursions and β-cell compensatory capacity [27, 32]. HbA1c, integrating both fasting and postprandial glucose exposure, represents cumulative glycemic burden and is particularly predictive of persistent dysglycemia [33]. These measures serve as early, quantifiable markers of β-cell insufficiency and insulin resistance, identifying women who are metabolically vulnerable even before clinical diabetes develops [33, 34]. Clinically, they offer a pragmatic framework for stratifying postpartum risk and guiding preventive interventions, including lifestyle modification and closer glycemic monitoring [32, 33]. While methodological differences across studies such as variation in cut-offs, timing of measurement, and assay standardization; introduce some heterogeneity in effect sizes, the consistent direction and magnitude of the associations across multiple reviews support their reliability as predictive markers. These findings emphasize that higher glycemic values during or after pregnancy are not only markers of immediate metabolic stress but also powerful prognostic indicators of long-term T2DM risk among women with prior GDM.

The association between maternal age and the risk of developing type 2 diabetes mellitus following gestational diabetes was generally positive but heterogeneous in magnitude and statistical significance across the included SRMAs. Although two SRMAs in our umbrella review reported weaker or statistically non-significant associations [15, 19], moderate to strong associations have been identified from another two SRMAS with effect sizes indicating increased risk among women of advanced maternal age [6, 12]. This inconsistency likely reflects differences in advanced maternal age thresholds (e.g., ≥ 30, ≥ 35, ≥ 38 years), underlying population characteristics, follow-up duration, and adjustment for confounders such as BMI and parity [6, 12, 15, 19]. Advancing maternal age reflects cumulative metabolic decline, including reduced insulin sensitivity and β‑cell reserve, such that the metabolic stress of pregnancy is more likely to reveal persistent β‑cell dysfunction and increase the risk of progression from GDM to chronic dysglycemia [35, 36]. Large recent cohort analyses and syntheses reinforce this view while also showing important nuance, the effect of age is often attenuated after controlling for adiposity and other metabolic risk factors, and some high-quality studies report significant age effects only beyond specific cut-points (commonly ≥ 35 years) [37]. These patterns imply that maternal age frequently acts as a marker of accumulated metabolic exposure rather than a sole causal driver, and that its predictive value is greatest when combined with measures of glycemic severity, obesity, or recurrence of GDM. Maternal age continues to be a valuable factor in assessing the risk of developing type 2 diabetes after gestational diabetes [36]. Older women—especially those who also have obesity or elevated blood sugar levels after pregnancy—may benefit from more frequent screening and targeted preventive care. To improve future research, it’s important to standardize how age groups are defined, report both raw and adjusted risk estimates, and explore how age interacts with other factors like BMI, ethnicity, and the severity of gestational diabetes. This will help clarify when and for whom maternal age plays an independent role in diabetes progression.

Ethnic background consistently also identified as an important predictor of progression from GDM to T2DM, with non-white groups frequently demonstrating higher risk across settings. In our umbrella review three SRMAs reported positive associations, with reported effect sizes spanning modest increases in odds to much larger relative risks in specific populations. A pattern mirrored in population-based cohorts and recent syntheses showing substantially higher cumulative incidence of T2DM among Black, Hispanic, South Asian and some Middle Eastern groups after GDM [19, 38]. These disparities likely reflect a combination of genetic susceptibility (differences in β-cell function and insulin sensitivity), earlier and greater accumulation of cardiometabolic risk factors (higher adiposity, central obesity), and social determinants that limit access to preventive care and healthy postpartum environments [38]. Importantly, heterogeneity in magnitude between studies appears driven by differences in the referent group, the ethnic composition of study populations, length of follow-up, and the degree to which analyses adjusted for mediators such as BMI, socioeconomic status, and postpartum behaviors [38]. Several large reviews and recent cohort studies therefore recommend interpreting ethnicity as a marker of elevated population-level risk that points to groups requiring prioritized screening and culturally adapted interventions rather than as a sole biologic causal variable [19, 38, 39].

Hypertensive disorders during pregnancy (HDP) were reported as risk factors for later T2DM in the SRMAs we reviewed, though effect sizes vary. One meta-analytic synthesis reported markedly elevated odds, while other pooled estimates are more modest but still statistically positive, indicating an increased postpartum diabetes risk among women who experienced pregnancy-related hypertension. Both HDP and GDM share common antecedent pathways: endothelial dysfunction, chronic inflammation, insulin resistance, and abnormalities in placental and systemic metabolic signaling [40, 41]. HDP may therefore identify women with a higher baseline cardiometabolic burden and a trajectory toward cardiometabolic disease, including T2DM [40, 41]. Despite these complexities, the reproducible positive associations across reviews imply that the history of HDP should raise clinical suspicion for subsequent dysglycemia and strengthen the case for intensive postpartum metabolic surveillance.

The association between delivering a macrosomic infant and subsequent maternal T2DM is less consistent than for ethnicity or HDP. Some SRMAs and cohort analyses report a significant positive relationship suggesting that mothers of macrosomic infants are at increased long-term risk of T2DM while other syntheses find no significant effect after multivariable adjustment. These mixed findings likely reflect heterogeneity in the definition and ascertainment of macrosomia (birthweight cutoffs and gestational age adjustment), variation in whether macrosomia is attributed to maternal hyperglycaemia versus other causes (maternal obesity independent of glycaemia), and differences in confounder control (particularly for pre-pregnancy BMI and glycaemic severity) [42, 43]. Conceptually, macrosomia can be a marker of more severe maternal hyperglycaemia in pregnancy and thus a proxy for greater β-cell stress and future risk; however, when maternal adiposity or other shared risk factors are accounted for, the independent predictive contribution of macrosomia appears attenuated in some studies [42–44]. Taken together, macrosomia should be interpreted as a potentially informative clinical marker of elevated maternal metabolic risk in the postpartum period, but one whose prognostic value depends on context and covariate adjustment.

Strength and limitations

A key strength of this umbrella review lies in its comprehensive scope, bringing together findings from a wide range of systematic reviews and meta-analyses to offer a consolidated understanding of the factors influencing the progression from gestational diabetes to type 2 diabetes. By synthesizing diverse sources, the review provides a global perspective and highlights consistent risk and protective factors that can inform clinical practice and public health strategies.

However, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the heterogeneity in study designs, populations, and outcome measures across the included reviews limited the ability to perform a quantitative synthesis. Second, the geographic distribution of primary studies was uneven, with minimal representation from Africa and Latin America, which may affect the generalizability of findings to these regions. Additionally, variations in diagnostic criteria for gestational diabetes and type 2 diabetes across studies may have influenced reported associations. Lastly, publication bias and the reliance on previously published reviews may have excluded emerging evidence not yet captured in systematic reviews.

Implications of the study

This review offers valuable insights for both clinical practice and public health policy. By identifying consistent risk factors for the progression from gestational diabetes to type 2 diabetes, it underscores the importance of early postpartum screening and long-term follow-up for women with a history of gestational diabetes. The findings support the need for personalized care plans that consider individual risk profiles, including metabolic, demographic, and lifestyle factors.

Moreover, the protective role of lactation suggests that promoting and supporting breastfeeding could be a simple yet effective strategy to reduce future diabetes risk. Health systems and providers should consider integrating these findings into postpartum care guidelines, ensuring that women receive the education, resources, and support needed to manage their health beyond pregnancy.

Finally, this synthesis highlights gaps in the current literature and encourages future research to explore underrepresented populations and long-term outcomes, helping to build a more inclusive and globally relevant understanding of diabetes prevention.

Recommendations for future research

The geographic distribution of primary studies reveals a notable imbalance, with African populations being significantly underrepresented. This lack of data limits our understanding of how gestational diabetes progresses to type 2 diabetes in diverse sociocultural and healthcare contexts across the continent. Future research should prioritize studies in African settings to ensure that prevention strategies and clinical guidelines are inclusive, equitable, and responsive to regional needs. Expanding the evidence base in these areas will help build a more globally representative understanding of diabetes risk and support tailored interventions for women in underrepresented regions.

Conclusion

This umbrella review offers a comprehensive understanding of the complex factors that influence the transition from gestational diabetes to type 2 diabetes. It brings together diverse findings to highlight how clinical indicators, lifestyle factors, and social determinants interact to shape long-term health outcomes. Notably, while certain risk factors such as insulin use, elevated BMI, family history of diabetes, abnormal glycemic markers, advanced maternal age, non-white ethnicity, hypertension, and recurrent consistently increase vulnerability, lactation stands out as a protective behavior. These insights reinforce the importance of individualized postpartum care and proactive risk management to support women’s health beyond pregnancy.
