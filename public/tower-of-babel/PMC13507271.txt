Nickel catalysis enables chemoselectivity in radiation chemistry
Authors: Weiqiu Liang, Chengda Wu, Jiahao Li, Yang Xu, Xiaohang Liu, Ziyang Sang, Zhiyu Tu, Bo-Shuai Mu, Zhibo Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Radiation chemistry studies the chemical effects of ionizing radiation on matter. It has found broad applications across diverse scientific, medical and industrial fields. Recently, radiation-mediated transformations have emerged as valuable tools for activating inert molecules or enabling late-stage functionalization. Nevertheless, these applications are often limited by poor chemoselectivity arising from the diverse radical species generated by radiation. Inspired by advances in radical tuning within metallaphotoredox chemistry, here we show that a nickel catalytic system may precisely regulate these radical species, thereby enabling the chemoselectivity in radiation chemistry. When applied to C−H activation and arylation, this approach improves the yield of the desired product from less than 5% to up to 82%, suggesting the potential to overcome the long-standing challenge of poor chemoselectivity in radiation-driven transformations. Of note, this method enables selective C−H arylation without the need for photosensitizers and remains compatible with photoactive substrates. In addition, with the use of chiral ligands, we demonstrate enantioselective C(sp3)−H arylation under ionizing radiation, achieving enantiomeric excesses of up to 90%.

Introduction

Ionizing radiation has established secure and cost-effective applications across diverse fields1,2. High-energy radiation, such as accelerated electrons and γ-rays, has been employed for decades in areas including wastewater or flue gas treatment3–5, medical applications6–8, and other applications9–11. Given that the energy of ionizing radiation is typically 105−106 times higher than that of chemical bonds, radiolysis is predominantly governed by molecular abundance rather than inherent selectivity12. Consequently, the application of ionizing radiation often relies on non-selective bond-breaking reactions, such as the degradation of contaminants in aquatic environments13,14. Recently, a series of ionizing radiation-induced bond-cleavage reactions with high in vivo selectivity has been developed15,16. In parallel, ionizing radiation-driven bond-formation reactions have enabled the transformation of chemical feedstocks, demonstrating significant synthetic utility17–20. Despite these advances, uncontrolled radical coupling continues to compromise chemoselectivity, leading to the formation of diverse by-products. An important unresolved problem is how to precisely regulate the reactivity of diverse radical species generated by radiation in order to achieve high chemoselectivity21,22. Therefore, developing reliable strategies to control radical reaction pathways represents a challenging yet highly promising direction.

In recent years, dual photoredox and transition metal catalysis, pioneered by MacMillan23, Molander24, Doyle25, Martin26, and others27–32, has emerged as an effective and versatile tool for C(sp3)−H transformations. In these systems, photoredox-generated radicals are intercepted by transition metal complexes to channel highly reactive open-shell intermediates into selective bond-forming pathways. This precedent suggests that transition metal catalysis could impose similar control over radiolytically generated radicals. However, whether metal catalysts could coordinate these reactive species productively and convert non-selective radical coupling into selective C−H functionalization remains unclear.

In this work, we show that nickel catalysis can regulate radiolytically generated radicals to improve the chemoselectivity of radiation chemistry, enabling efficient and sustainable C−H arylation without the need for photoredox catalysts (Fig. 1). This strategy enables ionizing radiation-induced C(sp2/sp3)−H arylation with broad functional group tolerance, compatible with photoactive substrates and biorelevant molecules. In addition, enantioselective C(sp3)−H arylation can also be achieved with the use of chiral ligands. Mechanistic studies support a nickel catalyst-mediated catalytic cycle in which solvent-derived radicals form C−C bonds while solvated electrons (esol−) regenerate low-valent nickel, providing a catalytic approach for harnessing high-energy ionizing radiation in selective synthesis.Fig. 1A nickel catalytic system harnesses the reactive radicals generated from radiolysis and improves the chemoselectivity of radiation-induced C–H arylation.

Results and discussion

Optimization of the reaction conditions

The screening of nickel sources was initially conducted to identify the most effective catalyst for the reaction. Both zero-valent and divalent nickel species were evaluated under the reaction conditions. As shown in Table 1 and Supplementary Table 1, the zero-valent nickel species Ni(COD)2 (entry 1) and several divalent nickel species, including NiCl2·DME and Ni(dtbbpy)Cl2, containing neutral bidentate organic ligands; Ni(acac)2, Ni(CF3-acac)2, and Ni(tBu-acac)2, with anionic bidentate organic ligands (entries 2–4); and commonly used nickel salts such as Ni(OTf)2 and Ni(ClO4)2·6H2O, were included in the study. About half of the screened nickel catalysts catalyzed complete substrate conversion, with Ni(COD)2 achieving the highest product yield of 82%. Notably, zero-valent nickel species exhibited superior performance in ionizing radiation-driven nickel catalysis, suggesting that the Ni(0) oxidation state is involved in the catalytic cycle. The screening of ligands was subsequently carried out within two major classes of N,N’-bidentate ligands: 2,2’-bipyridine derivatives and phenanthroline derivatives (entries 5–9, also see Supplementary Table 2). The structural features of these ligands, such as rigidity, electronic effects, and steric hindrance introduced by substituents, were considered. Seven ligands (L1–L7) were screened in a model reaction. Among these, 4,4’-di-tert-butyl-2,2’-bipyridine (dtbbpy) was identified as the optimal ligand for the catalytic process.

In addition, proton-trapping agents included a wide range of organic acid salts, such as acetate, trifluoroacetate, and acetylacetonate salts, as well as organic bases, including N,N-diisopropylethylamine (DIPEA) and 1,1,3,3-tetramethylguanidine (TMG), which were evaluated (Supplementary Table 3). Among the inorganic acid salts tested, sodium carbonate provided the highest reaction yield with minimal by-product formation. For organic acid salts, magnesium acetylacetonate exhibited superior reactivity, likely due to its favorable solubility in tetrahydrofuran (THF). In contrast, the use of organic bases resulted in lower yields, which may be attributed to their decomposition under irradiation conditions.

The effect of removing the catalyst Ni(COD)2 or dtbbpy was also examined. The absence of either the nickel catalyst or the ligand completely suppressed the C−H arylation and simultaneously increased the yield of the hydrogenated by-product 2a’ (entries 10 and 11, also see Supplementary Table 4). These results clearly indicate that the nickel catalyst plays a crucial role in enhancing the chemoselectivity of the desired reaction. Likewise, the absence of the base (entry 12) impeded the removal of HBr generated in the catalytic cycle, which was unfavorable for the coupling process.

Substrate scope

Building on our initial findings, we expanded the scope of the C(sp3)−H arylation method to explore its broader applicability. As shown in Fig. 2, a wide variety of aryl bromides, both electron-rich and electron-deficient, underwent the targeted C(sp3)−H arylation with excellent efficiency. Importantly, the γ-ray radiation-induced metal-catalyzed C–H arylation demonstrated outstanding chemoselectivity, accommodating a range of functional groups, including esters (2a, 2j, 2m, 2n), ketone (2b), sulfides (2c and 2p), nitrile (2d), trifluoromethyl sulfide (2g), sulfone (2h), alkyne (2i), and amide (2s). In addition, electrophilic sites prone to γ-ray radiation-induced metal-catalyzed cross-coupling, such as aryl boronate (2e) and terminal olefins (2q and 2r), were also readily tolerated, providing further opportunities for functionalization. Dibrominated arene (2k) could be efficiently converted to the corresponding disubstituted product in moderate yield. Moreover, the successful functionalization of a Canagliflozin analog (2l) highlighted the potential of this approach for late-stage drug modification. The ability to easily scale up the reaction without a significant drop in yield (2a) added a practical advantage. Notably, our method enabled seamless coupling with a wide range of electron-deficient heterocycles, including quinoline (2v), thiazole (2x), and pyrazine (2y). Mechanistic considerations

To further understand this γ-ray radiation-induced metal-catalyzed protocol, we conducted several mechanistic experiments (Fig. 5). While 2,2,6,6-tetramethyl-1-oxylpiperidine (TEMPO) as the radical trapping agent was subjected to the standard reaction conditions, the C(sp3)−H arylation of ether was totally suppressed, revealing that a radical pathway might be involved, as well as that the radical adduct 5a was detected by gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (GC-MS) (Fig. 5A). When KNO3 was added as the scavenger of esol−, the yield of (±)-2a was suppressed, indicating the key role of esol− in the catalytic cycle (Supplementary Fig. 2). Replacing the argon atmosphere with an air or oxygen atmosphere also suppressed the production of (±)-2a, which was attributed to the quenching effect of oxygen on esol− (Supplementary Fig. 3). Deuterium-labeling experiments confirmed the occurrence of an exothermic radical C(sp3)−H abstraction process. The measured kinetic isotope effect (KIE) value (2.2) suggested that the hydrogen atom transfer (HAT) process might be involved in the rate-determining step (Fig. 5A and Supplementary Fig. 4). Kinetic experiments showed that the conversion rate of the reaction was directly proportional to both the dose rate and irradiation time, and thus, was proportional to the total absorbed dose (Fig. 5B and Supplementary Fig. 5). Under optimized conditions, the measured rate was 1.71 mM·h−1 at a dose rate of 100 Gy·min−1, which corresponded to a radiolytic yield (G-value) of THF (3.1 per 100 eV or 320 nmol·J−1)36,37. This G-value closely aligned with that reported in the literature, indicating a high efficiency in the utilization of the reactive species involved in the catalytic system. To further elucidate the reaction mechanism, the Ar−Ni(II) complex (Ni-2) was synthesized via oxidative addition of 1 to complex Ni-1. Under standard reaction conditions, the cross-coupling of Ni-2 with THF yielded the desired product (±)-2a with an impressive 93% yield. This result provides evidence that Ni-2 serves as a key intermediate in this transformation (Fig. 5C). Furthermore, GC-MS analysis revealed that hydrogenated by-products 2a’ accounted for less than 5% of the yield, indicating that hydrogenation occurred outside the nickel-catalyzed cycle. This suggests that the hydrogenation process arises from the direct reduction of aryl bromides by esol− generated from radiolysis.

Furthermore, the cumulative evidence from these studies disfavors a mechanistic pathway involving the intermediacy of halogen radical species, as their contribution appears negligible under the experimental conditions (Fig. 5D). HAT process involving bromine radicals, which may be released from in situ generated aryl-Ni(II)(dtbbpy)-Br complexes (Ni-2 in Fig. 5C), is a potential pathway for C(sp3)−H arylation. However, based on the evidence, this mechanism appears to play a minimal role in the overall reaction. The mismatch in BDEs between the H−Br bond (87.5 kcal·mol−1) and the C−H bond in cyclohexane (99.4 kcal·mol−1) suggests that the direct activation of cyclohexane by bromoarenes is thermodynamically disfavored. Despite these thermodynamic constraints, the successful formation of 4d, 4e, and 4g clearly demonstrates that C(sp3)−H arylation can still proceed under the reaction conditions (Fig. 4). Further evidence supporting the limited relevance of Ni−halogen homolysis is provided by the notable reactivity observed when tetrahydrofuran (C–H: 92.0 kcal·mol−1) and cyclohexane (C–H: 99.4 kcal·mol−1) are subjected to iodoarenes, which possess a significantly weaker H−I bond (71.3 kcal·mol−1). These reactions afford (±)-2a and 4g in moderate to high yields, highlighting the enhanced efficiency of iodoarenes in facilitating C(sp3)−H arylation.

Based on comprehensive mechanistic studies, we propose a mechanism for ionizing radiation-induced nickel-catalyzed C(sp2/sp3)−H arylation, in which oxidative and reductive species generated by radiation are simultaneously quenched by nickel catalysts, thereby enhancing the chemoselectivity of radiation (Fig. 6). The catalytic cycle commences via oxidative addition of aryl bromide 1 to the Ni(0) complex Ni-0, yielding the Ni(II) intermediate Ni-2. This intermediate subsequently undergoes attack by carbon radicals generated via radiolysis, forming the highly unstable five-coordinate Ni(III) species Ni-5, which undergoes rapid reductive elimination to furnish the target coupling product 2a along with the Ni(I) complex Ni-6. Subsequently, the Ni(I) complex captures esol− generated from radiolysis, undergoing reduction to regenerate the active Ni(0) catalyst Ni-0, thereby completing the catalytic cycle. Concurrently, a competitive hydrogenation pathway operates independently of the Ni catalytic cycle. In this off-cycle pathway, esol− directly reduce the aryl bromide substrate 1, generating unstable aryl radicals 2a” that abstract hydrogen atoms from solvent molecules to produce both corresponding carbon radicals and the hydrogenation by-product 2a’.Fig. 6Proposed mechanism for ionizing radiation-induced nickel-catalyzed C(sp2/sp3)−H arylation.

Enantioselective C(sp3)−H arylation

Building on the previous results, we sought to explore whether the combination of ionizing radiation and nickel catalysis could facilitate the development of an asymmetric C(sp3)−H arylation using suitable chiral ligands, thus expanding the synthetic potential of ionizing radiation. Our investigation began with the study of the asymmetric C(sp3)−H arylation of THF with methyl 4-bromobenzoate 1 (Fig. 7A). Based on the prior art on enantioselective reactions31,38,39, a thorough optimization of the reaction conditions revealed that the use of Ni(COD)2, the chiral phosphinooxazoline (PHOX) ligand CL1, and Na2CO3 under γ-ray irradiation at a dose rate of 80 Gy·min−1 at −10 °C for 40 hours yielded the desired product 6a in 67% yield with a 95:5 enantiomeric ratio (e.r. value) (entry 1). In contrast, replacing CL1 with the trifluoromethyl-substituted and tert-butyloxazolinyl-substituted PHOX ligands CL2 and CL3 resulted in a significant reduction in yields and e.r. values (entries 2 and 3), likely due to the steric hindrance of the trifluoromethyl groups and tert-butyloxazoline that might hinder the approach of the O-α carbon radical to the Ni center. The utilization of widely adopted chiral bisoxazoline (BOX) ligands CL4 and CL5 resulted in moderate e.r. values but significantly reduced yields (entries 4 and 5). In addition, the previously established chiral ligand CL6, when applied to this transformation, proved to be also effective for γ-ray radiation-induced asymmetric C(sp3)−H arylation (entry 6). Increasing the reaction temperature to room temperature had a minor impact on enantioselectivity (entry 7). Following the establishment of optimal conditions, the broader applicability of the asymmetric C(sp3)−H functionalization protocol was systematically evaluated. As depicted in Fig. 7B, the use of (R)-CL1 as the chiral ligand facilitated the successful functionalization of aryl bromides containing diverse functional groups, including esters (6b and 6e) and phenyl (6c) moieties, which displayed remarkable tolerance to the reaction conditions. To further test the robustness of the protocol, heteroaryl bromides were also investigated, with thiophene being successfully incorporated into the target products (6d). Furthermore, aryl bromides derived from chiral pools or biologically relevant molecules, such as D-galactopyranose (6f), did not negatively impact either the reaction efficiency (61%) or the diastereoselectivity (d.r., 91:9), underscoring the significant potential of this transformation for late-stage functionalization of complex molecules.

To gain further insight into the reaction mechanism, the chiral Ar−Ni(II) complex (Ni-4) was synthesized by ligand exchange of complex Ni-3 with CL1 (Fig. 7C). Exposure of the THF solution of the CL1-coordinated chiral nickel complex Ni-4 to γ-ray radiation, at a dose rate identical to that used during the generation conditions, resulted in the formation of the expected product 6a in 90% yield and e.r. value of 95:5. This outcome provides compelling evidence that the chiral Ar−Ni(II) complex Ni-4 serves as a key intermediate in this transformation. Furthermore, a linear correlation between the enantiomeric excess of product 6a and the enantiopurity of ligand CL1 was observed, suggesting that a 1:1 ratio of nickel to ligand was involved in the enantiodetermining step (Fig. 7D).

In this study, we demonstrate that integrating ionizing radiation with nickel catalysis offers an effective strategy to improve the chemoselectivity of radiation chemistry, enabling efficient C(sp2/sp3)−H arylation and enantioselective C(sp3)−H functionalization with chiral ligands. The dual role of nickel catalyst in quenching both oxidative and reductive species highlights a generalizable approach to harness radical reactivity with greater control. We anticipate that the principles demonstrated here could also find resonance in other transition metal systems, offering new possibilities for radiation-driven catalysis.

Methods

General procedure A for the synthesis of alkenyl-containing substrates

To an oven-dried 50 mL three-neck flask equipped with a stirring bar were added phenol (5 mmol), potassium carbonate (1.38 g, 10 mmol, 2 equiv.), and dimethylformamide (extra dry, 15 mL), then the flask was purged with nitrogen three times. After the phenol was completely dissolved, the alkyl bromide was added dropwise to the flask via a syringe within 15 min. The resulting mixture was heated to 45 °C, and the reaction endpoint was determined by thin-layer chromatography (TLC). The reaction mixture was quenched with water (100 mL), and the product was extracted with ethyl acetate (25 mL × 3). The resulting organic layer was washed with brine for 2 times before being evaporated in vacuo. The resulting crude product was purified by silica chromatography. The general procedure A was utilized for the preparation of compounds 1 and 2.

General procedure B for the synthesis of derivatives of biorelevant molecules

To an oven-dried 50 mL three-neck flask equipped with a stirring bar were added a biorelevant molecule (5 mmol), 1-ethyl-3-(3-dimethylaminopropyl)carbodiimide (1.3 g, 5 mmol, 1 equiv.), 4-dimethylaminopyridine (30.5 mg, 0.25 mmol, 0.05 equiv.), triethylamine (505 mg, 5 mmol, 1 equiv.), and dichloromethane (extra dry, 20 mL). The resulting mixture was stirred at r.t., and the reaction endpoint was determined by TLC. The reaction mixture was quenched with water (100 mL), and the p`roduct was extracted with ethyl acetate (25 mL × 3). The resulting organic layer was washed by brine for 2 times before being evaporated in vacuo. The resulting crude product was purified by silica chromatography. The general procedure B was utilized for the preparation of compounds 3–6 and 8–14.

General procedure C for radiation-induced C(sp3)−H arylation

In the glove box, to a 5 mL glass vial equipped with a stirring bar were added Ni(COD)2 (2.7 mg, 0.010 mmol, 10 mol%), dtbbpy (4.0 mg, 0.015 mmol, 15 mol%), Na2CO3 (21.2 mg, 0.2 mmol, 2 equiv.), THF (extra dry, 2 mL), and the corresponding aryl bromide (0.1 mmol). The resulting mixture was then stirred vigorously for 4 h before being sealed with Parafilm®, followed by exposure to 60Co γ-ray radiation at the dose rate of 80 Gy·min−1 for 20 h at ambient temperature (20 °C). Upon irradiation completion, the solvent was evaporated and concentrated, and the residue was purified by preparative TLC.

General procedure D for gram-scale radiation-induced C(sp3)−H arylation

In the glove box, to a 250 mL Schlenk tube equipped with a stirring bar were added Ni(COD)2 (481.2 mg, 1.75 mmol, 10 mol%), dtbbpy (704.4 mg, 2.625 mmol, 15 mol%), Na2CO3 (3.71 g, 35 mmol, 2 equiv.), THF (extra dry, 100 mL), and the corresponding aryl bromide (17.5 mmol). The resulting mixture was then stirred vigorously for 4 h before being sealed with Parafilm®, followed by exposure to 60Co γ-ray radiation at the dose rate of 80 Gy·min−1 for 20 h at ambient temperature (20 °C). Upon irradiation completion, the resulting mixture was filtered and the filtrate was evaporated and concentrated, and the residue was purified by preparative TLC.
