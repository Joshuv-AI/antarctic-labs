 Executive Summary

PyARC is an open-source workflow management tool developed at Argonne National Laboratory
(ANL) to support advanced reactor analysis and code-to-code verification within the Nuclear Energy
Advanced Modeling and Simulation (NEAMS) Workbench framework. Designed to integrate a
suite of high-fidelity codes, known as the “extended suite” of Argonne Reactor Computation (ARC)
codes, PyARC provides a unified interface for model creation, real-time validation, execution, and
results post-processing and visualization. This approach streamlines complex multiphysics workflows,
reduces the learning curve for new users, and promotes best practices across industry, academia,
and national laboratories.

 In Fiscal Year (FY) 2026, PyARC underwent significant upgrades and enhancements to meet
evolving user needs and industry standards. Notable achievements include:

 • Transition to GAMSRC: Replacing the deprecated GAMSOR code with GAMSRC for neutron-
 gamma coupled calculations to stay up to date with the ARC suite.

 • Expanded DASSH Geometry Support: Removal of previous geometry restrictions, enabling
 free-form reactor modeling and greater flexibility for users.

 • Bowing Reactivity Worth Calculations: Integration of PERSENT and NUBOW-3D for core
 bowing analysis.

 • ORIGEN-S Upgrade: Enhanced compatibility with SCALE 6.3 and later, supporting detailed
 depletion calculations and isotopic inventory tracking.

 • MC2 -3 Cell ID Extension: Increased the number of allowable cell IDs, enabling more detailed
 reactor models.

 • OpenMC Depletion Zones: Added support for axial depletion zones, improving the accuracy
 of depletion calculations.

 • Language Server Protocol (LSP) Upgrade Progress: Completed key enabling work for template-
 based autocompletion in the LSP workflow, but full deployment of the LSP grammar in the
 Workbench graphical user interface (GUI) was deferred from v4.0.0 pending additional testing
 and bug fixes.

 • Windows Support and Installation Improvements: Enhanced cross-platform reliability and
 streamlined setup procedures for broader accessibility.

 • Software Quality Assurance (SQA): Established a formal SQA program, including the creation
 of a Software Quality Assurance Plan (SQAP) and gap analysis to ensure compliance with
 DOE and ASME standards.

 These upgrades position PyARC as a robust, user-friendly platform for advanced reactor
modeling and verification.

1 Introduction

PyARC [1] was launched as a part of the Nuclear Energy Advanced Modeling and Simulation

(NEAMS) Workbench [2] initiative in Fiscal Year (FY) 2017 to facilitate the deployment of the

high-fidelity codes developed within the NEAMS program [3]. PyARC was originally developed as

an open source workflow management tool for the Argonne Reactor Computation (ARC) [4, 5] codes

and has since expanded to cover other NEAMS and non-ARC codes. Since its initial development,

PyARC has been used by industry, academia, and national labs to perform complex multiphysics

analysis workflows of fast reactors and perform reliable code-to-code verification. Because of its

wide reach and range of developmental work, funding for PyARC has historically come primarily

from both the Department of Energy (DOE) Advanced Reactor Technologies (ART) Fast Reactor

Program (FRP) and the DOE NEAMS Multiphysics Technical Area. As of FY26, all PyARC work

has been funded by DOE ART FRP.

 PyARC wraps the ARC code suite that includes neutronics, thermal hydraulics, safety, and

fuel behavior analysis codes: MC2 -3 [6] and TWODANT [7] for region-wise multigroup cross section

processing, DIF3D [8] for flux calculation, REBUS [9] for depletion and equilibrium calculations,

PERSENT [10] for perturbation theory calculations (perturbation, sensitivity, uncertainty quan-

tification, and bowing calculations), GAMSOR/GAMSRC [11] for gamma heating calculations,

DASSH [12] for subchannel analysis, and NUBOW-3D [13] for structural analysis and core bowing

calculations. Since initiation, other codes have been integrated into PyARC, forming what is known

as the “extended suite” of ARC codes. This extended suite includes the non-ARC codes: ORIGEN-S

[14, 15, 16] for detailed depletion calculations, the PROTEUS-Nodal [17] transport solver, OpenMC

[18] for Monte Carlo neutronics [19], and Griffin [20] for high-fidelity deterministic neutronics. The

complete set of workflows supported by PyARC for these integrated codes as of the v4.0.0 release in

September 2026 [21] is shown in Fig. 1.1.

 In FY26, the PyARC team focused on two major overarching tasks: code development and the

establishment of the Software Quality Assurance (SQA) program. In the former category, the new

2 The Workbench/PyARC Framework

2.1 The Workbench GUI

Workbench is a GUI and RTE developed at Oak Ridge National Laboratory (ORNL) designed to

be a common user interface for model creation, real-time validation, execution, output processing,

and visualization for integrated codes [2]. PyARC relies on the Workbench modules and RTE for

base-level validation, input processing, and execution. PyARC is deployed within Workbench with

each release, and so users can use the latest capabilities almost immediately within the contained

environment, pending any code-specific setup that may be required. An example of what the GUI

might look like when using PyARC within Workbench is shown in Fig. 2.1.

2.2 The PyARC Module

PyARC is an Argonne National Laboratory (ANL)-developed module that relies on Workbench to

support a “black box” approach to using the extended suite of ARC codes. It requires only one

common user input file (the Standard Object Notation (SON) input) as described in Section 2.2.1

and then automatically performs code-specific input validation, code-specific input generation, code

execution, and post-processing for all specified workflows in the SON input file (see Fig. 1.1 for a

visual of available workflows).

input file navigation panel; (B) SON input file; (C) PyARC summary output file (post-processed

2.2.1 Common SON Input and Input Validation

The Workbench input format adopted by PyARC is described as the “common input” since it uses

a single input file used to generate code-specific inputs for all the tools integrated into PyARC.

The main benefits of the common input strategy is to ensure every native input uses consistent

information and to facilitate code-to-code interfacing. The common input is designed specifically

for lattice-based reactor geometries and is intended to be intuitive and flexible for users. It uses

Workbench’s open source Workbench Analysis Sequence Processor (WASP) SON [25] format that

enables the autocompletion, real-time input validation, and access to templates, through the

Workbench interface. The common input parameters are defined by a single Workbench-supported

input definition file in PyARC. The definitions rely on some level of code-independent inherent input

validation provided by the Hierarchical Input Validation Engine (HIVE) module, the built-in input

validation capability from WASP [25]. It is important to note, that the real-time input validation

 5

performed by HIVE is limited to common generic logical rules, and does not provide the capability

for more complex code-specific input validation, which occurs in the precheck stage of PyARC

execution (Section 2.2.2). The PyARC-specific input definitions also provide users with several input

templates for autocompletion that can be populated directly in the Workbench GUI during SON

input creation. Prior to the LSP upgrade that was initiated in FY25 [24], these input parameters

were defined using a “schema” file for PyARC but are now defined in an extensive Python database

that interacts with PyARC’s dedicated language server. A description of how the LSP works is

provided in Section 2.3. Background on the initial phase of the PyARC LSP upgrade is provided

in [24], while FY26 progress and the remaining work needed for full deployment are described in

Section 3.7.

2.2.2 Workflow Management

As noted, PyARC is a tool that manages input processing, code execution, and post-processing in

an easy-to-use system. This complete workflow is done in four stages:

  1. Precheck: performs additional diagnostics beyond what HIVE can provide and verifies a

 compatible RTE is available.

  2. Prerun: generates any code-specific input required.

  3. Run: executes the integrated code(s).

  4. Postrun: post-processes relevant code output, if the run stage was successful, into user-friendly,

 PyARC-standardized notation.

This sequence is triggered by PyARC execution, not during input creation, which means the precheck

stage is not performed until after an input has been fully created and validated by the first stage of

HIVE validation. If the precheck stage fails, then PyARC will stop and not perform the remaining

stages of execution.

 6

2.3 Language Server Protocol

Microsoft’s LSP [26] was first introduced into Workbench for support of MCNP analysis in 2018

[27] and formally integrated in 2019 as a feature available for all tools that rely on the Workbench

system [28]. The LSP facilitates and simplifies the connection between input processing, user

communication, and code development. Workbench in particular supports the following capabilities

to be implemented via a server class in any tool [29]:

 • input diagnostics via interactive checking

 • input navigation via symbol tree hierarchy

 • input autocompletion via app introspection

 • input definitions via key lookup locations

 • input formatting via standardization rules

The schema implementation provides for some of these capabilities to a degree already. However,

the mechanisms for these features are less robust (some details of these features are not available in

the schema implementation) and do not directly communicate with the PyARC module at runtime,

which requires additional input processing steps. In contrast, an LSP-based approach allows input

processing, diagnostics, and user-interface features to be driven directly by the same Python-

based infrastructure used internally by PyARC. In principle, this enables a richer Workbench user

experience, including more detailed diagnostics, improved document navigation, hover definitions,

goto definition, find references, and template-driven autocompletion.

 The initial phase of PyARC’s transition to this LSP-based infrastructure was carried out in

FY25 [24]. In that phase, the backend input-processing workflow was migrated to use a generated

Python database at runtime, but the user-facing Workbench grammar continued to rely on the

legacy schema because full support for template-based autocompletion in the LSP workflow was not

yet available. FY26 work related to this upgrade is described in Section 3.7.

3 New Developments

In FY26, PyARC underwent several upgrades and saw three version releases (v3.0.1 in March 2026

[22], v3.1.0 in June 2026 [23], and v4.0.0 in September 2026 [21]). The first two releases focused

mainly on bug fixes or minor industry- and user-requested updates that could be implemented

immediately, while v4.0.0 focused on major upgrades and new capabilities. The notable changes are

described in the following sections.

3.1 GAMSRC Upgrade

Previous versions of PyARC have used GAMSOR [11], a modified version of DIF3D [8], to obtain

coupled neutron-gamma flux solutions. The GAMSOR software has been deprecated as of FY26,

replaced by the newer GAMSRC code [11] within the ARC code suite. GAMSRC is a simplified

and more robust workflow (Fig. 3.1a) that produces the same types of output files and identical

results to the former GAMSOR workflow (Fig. 3.1b). Thus its functionality within PyARC has

been updated to use GAMSRC, rather than GAMSOR, instead. Additionally, this transition was

also desirable because GAMSRC falls under required SQA protocols for the ARC code suite, while

GAMSOR is no longer included after its deprecation, allowing PyARC to maintain a higher level of

quality assurance.

 The execution steps of GAMSRC in PyARC are very similar to what is shown in Fig. 3.1a.

First, an instance of DIF3D is run to obtain the neutron flux solution. Second, using the PMATRX

gamma data, GAMSRC is able to produce the fixed source distribution profile corresponding to the

neutronics flux profile. Technically, this fixed source distribution is the only output coming from

GAMSRC itself, so for a third step DIF3D is run using this fixed source distribution to obtain the

neutron-gamma coupled results. Upon completion, the coupled results are post-processed using the

dif3d to vtk [30] utility to produce VTK visualization files as well as formatted tables for inclusion

in the summary files created by PyARC.

3.2 DASSH Enhancements

In FY25 [24], the DASSH integration in PyARC was upgraded to use the Fortran version [12] rather

than the Python version [31]. However, this initial integration upgrade was tied to v12.1.0 of the

ARC codes which contained a DASSH version with a high number of geometry restrictions. These

previous restrictions included:

 • no more than three axial zones per assembly (i.e. restricted to three subassembly definitions);

 • all assemblies must have the same number of axial zones with the same axial heights;

 • each axial layer at the same height across all assemblies must be the same type (e.g., all pin

 lattice or all homogeneous).

 • all pin lattices in a single assembly must have the same dimensions.

For most fast reactor designs, these geometry restrictions are not feasible and restrict the modeling

too much. With the release of ARC v12.2.0 and v12.3.0, all these geometry restrictions were removed,

allowing for what is referred to as “free-form” DASSH geometry. The reactor geometry can now

have any number of varying subassemblies defined in different manners (pin lattice, homogeneous,

etc.) across all assemblies. Additionally, although not necessarily recommended, the pin lattice

dimensions can vary axially in a single assembly, and there is no requirement that every assembly

contain a pin lattice.

 Two additional geometry features were introduced into DASSH in v12.2.0: tube lattice and

grid spacers. Both of these features were added to the PyARC integration in v4.0.0. The tube

lattice is a solid medium with cylindrical channels for coolant. These channels are arranged in a

hexagonal lattice but can vary in size. Grid spacers can also now be used in place of wire wrap in a

DASSH model. It is important to note that grid spacers are a DASSH-only feature, meaning they

are not available as a geometry feature in the standard geometry input and are not considered in

other calculations, such as a DIF3D neutronics calculation.

 10

 DASSH v12.2.0 and v12.3.0 also removed, added, or updated various computational and output

settings, and PyARC was updated accordingly.

3.3 PERSENT Bowing Reactivity Worth

A major capability added this FY is the ability to perform core bowing reactivity worth calculations

using PERSENT. The final step in a 7-step workflow using the ARC code suite was implemented

this FY, with the first 6 steps having already existed in PyARC as of FY25 [24]. A brief overview

of the workflow theory is described in Section 3.3.1 with a description of the PyARC-specific

implementation in Section 3.3.2.

3.3.1    Theory

This workflow is a newer ARC-based workflow whose detailed theory is described in [32] and was

initially demonstrated outside PyARC in [33]. The bowing reactivity calculation is a 7-step workflow

involving REBUS [9], DIF3D [8], MC2 -3 [6], GAMSRC [11], DASSH [12], NUBOW-3D [13], and

PERSENT [10] calculations. A visualization of this workflow can be seen in Fig. 3.2.

 Since PyARC’s initiation, the base neutronics components have been a part of PyARC: cross

section processing using MC2 -3 [6], neutron transport using DIF3D [8], and coupled neutron/gamma

flux profile using GAMSOR/GAMSRC [11]. In FY25 [24], subchannel thermal analysis capabilities

based on coupled neutron/gamma flux profiles were added through the upgrade of the DASSH [12]

integration, which allowed for the production of temperature distributions and fast flux profiles

in PyARC. Additionally, in FY25 [24], structural mechanics capabilities based on subchannel

thermal results from DASSH were added through NUBOW-3D [13], which produced assembly-wise

displacement data representative of bowing deformations. The final step in this workflow was to

connect NUBOW-3D to PERSENT to produce core bowing reactivity worth distributions.

 Bowing reactivity calculations take assembly-wise displacement results (contained in an

NHSTRAIN file) and shift each assembly region by the corresponding amount. Note that the base

(unbowed) DIF3D model uses fully homogenized assemblies, while PERSENT changes the homoge-

nization to be only up to the outer duct boundary instead, with explicitly-modeled coolant in the

3.3.2 Implementation

It is important to note, that as part of the workflow, users can either invoke each of these required

earlier calculation steps or simply link to appropriate output files coming from prior or external

simulations. In application, users can bypass DASSH and NUBOW-3D calculations altogether if

they are able to provide assembly displacement results with externally-produced NHSTRAIN files.

This allows for workflows leveraging third-party thermal hydraulic and structural mechanic solvers,

which specifically was requested by industry users. For the purpose of this report, it will be assumed

that the displacement data provided to PERSENT comes from the integrated NUBOW-3D workflow

in PyARC.

 This new feature can be invoked by using the bowing calc block in the SON input, which is

nested within the PERSENT input block: calculations/dif3d/persent/bowing calc. All input

parameters are optional, with most being able to be automatically derived from other input blocks

if present elsewhere. A visual of the input options is shown in Fig. 3.4, and a detailed list of input

parameters is provided here:

 • nhstrain file(X): the *.nhstrain file used to calculate bowing at the X th depletion time

 step (can be automatically generated with NUBOW-3D during the workflow).

 • lower axial surf (or axial offset): the offset surface name (or numerical value [m]) used

 to align the NUBOW-3D model with the DIF3D model (can be automatically derived from

 the NUBOW-3D input).

 • coolant material: the material name of the coolant (can be automatically derived from the

 DASSH input)

 • outer duct surface: the hexagonal or square surface name to use as the outer duct boundary

 (for hexagonal reactors, this can be automatically derived from the DASSH input1 ).
  1

Cartesian lattice geometries are supported for the PERSENT calculation.

 13

 • control assemblies: locations or names of assemblies that are control and will be omitted

 Figure 3.4: Overview of input block options for workflow.

 The workflow expects an instance of nhstrain file(X) for each depletion time point X that a

bowing calculation is desired. Note that each time point in the REBUS depletion sequence does

not need to be called in a bowing calculation, but each time point called by bowing calc must

correspond to a time point in the depletion sequence. If no files are provided, PyARC will default

to using whichever depletion time steps are specified by the NUBOW-3D input, performing one

PERSENT bowing worth calculation per time step. If a user desires only a subset of those time

steps, the dif3d refcalc depsteps input option in the main persent block can be used to specify

which time steps to use. For each calculation, the base reference DIF3D model used by PERSENT

is the DIF3D model corresponding to that time step.

 Both lower axial surf and axial offset account for the potential axial mismatch between

the neutronics DIF3D model and structural mechanics NUBOW-3D model. NUBOW-3D models

can be taller due to the need to include the assembly nozzle at the bottom of the core and top of

assembly contact points with the restraint ring at the top of the core. Only one of lower axial surf

or axial offset are needed if there is a discrepancy between the models. This value can be derived

from the nubow3d input if provided.

 From the dassh input block, the coolant material and duct dimensions can be automatically

derived. To manually define them, the coolant material can be used to specify the material ID

name to be used to fill the interassembly coolant gap between assemblies when constructing the

duct-homogenized model (as seen in Fig. 3.3), and the dimension used for this duct-homogenization

 14

is specified with outer duct surface.

 In most metal-cooled fast spectrum reactors, control assemblies are double-ducted (control

pins inside a smaller inner duct, which is within the outer duct with a clearance gap). Since

assembly bowing is mostly dictated by the displacement of the outer duct, this disconnect between

the inner and outer ducts of control assemblies means that the bowing behavior of control pins

is different (often less) than that of the outer duct, for which NUBOW-3D (and thus PyARC)

calculates displacements. Further, during operation control pins are usually mostly withdrawn due

to a relatively small reactivity swing over cycle in conventional metal-cooled fast spectrum reactor

designs, leaving essentially an empty outer duct filled with coolant for most of the active core height

(region with high neutronic importance). Combining these two elements together, control assembly

bowing in general both has little reactivity impact and is inadequately captured by NUBOW-3D,

so it is recommended to omit these assembly locations altogether from the core bowing reactivity

worth calculation. This is achieved through the control assemblies option, which allows users to

specify the names and/or locations of control assemblies to be omitted in the model.

 For each depletion step, the total core bowing reactivity worth is reported back to the user

in the PyARC summary output file. Additionally, several VTK files produced by PERSENT are

returned to the user for a visualization of the bowing worth. Example reactivity change output

can be found in Fig. 3.5 and a representative visualization of assembly-wise core bowing impact,

demonstrated on the Advanced Burner Test Reactor (ABTR) model [34], can be seen in Fig. 3.6.

3.3.3 Limitations and Future Work

Currently, the PERSENT bowing reactivity workflow can only be applied to reference time points of

a DIF3D/REBUS calculation. This limitation restricts the feature to only being able to run for the

reference model dimensions and material densities and temperatures. Future work will broaden this

capability to additionally being applicable from PERSENT perturbed states. The value of this is

that users could then model core bowing for different power and flow states through perturbations

to surfaces (dimensions), material densities (thermal expansion and coolant heating), and material

temperatures (heating). This includes approach-to-full power ramping as well as slow transients

(i.e., appropriate to model as quasi-static states).

3.4 ORIGEN-S Upgrade

PyARC supports a coupled integration between REBUS [9] and ORIGEN-S [14, 15, 16], referred to

as the REBORS workflow, which provides detailed isotopic inventory tracking during a depletion

analysis. REBUS depletes on a reduced set of nuclides, so the REBORS workflow condenses the

region-wise multigroup flux and cross sections computed by REBUS, builds a problem-specific

ORIGEN library, executes the resulting ORIGEN depletion cases, and returns the detailed inventories

to the PyARC results. Prior to the release of v4.0.0, this integration supported only the ORIGEN-S

input structure distributed with SCALE 6.1 [14] and 6.2 [15]. In v4.0.0 the REBORS workflow was

upgraded to be compatible with SCALE 6.3 [16] and later, while retaining support for the earlier

releases that much of the user base still uses.

 In the SCALE 6.3 release, library construction and depletion were consolidated into a single

=origen sequence using a build lib block, superseding the separate =couple and =origens decks

that PyARC had been generating, and SCALE 6.2 and earlier are incompatible with the newer

structure. SCALE 6.3 still accepts the older structure. To support users on both versions, PyARC

detects the installed generation rather than asking the user to declare it, falling back to the older

structure if the version cannot be determined.

 The rebus to origens input block is unchanged apart from the origen base lib option,

which specifies the energy group structure used to condense cross sections. With SCALE 6.2 and

earlier this option is one of the COUPLE libraries (44, 49, 200, or 238 groups). On SCALE 6.3

and later it is a reaction resource distributed with SCALE, and the 8, 47, 56, 252, and 302-group

structures were added as input options for this purpose. Availability depends on the installation

(the distributed SCALE 6.3 data provides the 8, 56, 200, 252, and 302-group structures), and only

200-group structure files are distributed with both legacy and modern SCALE, so an input that

requests it runs unmodified on either. A precheck stage was added for REBORS that reports

problems before any input is generated: a missing or unusable executable, a group structure the

detected SCALE version cannot supply, and, on SCALE 6.3 and later, a data directory left over

from an older release, whose reaction resource build lib cannot read.

3.5 MC2 -3 Cell ID Extension

A “cell” in MC2 -3 is a unique spatial region or unit cell (homogeneous or heterogeneous) for which

MC2 -3 will generate multigroup cross sections, based on the specified material composition, geometry,

and temperature [6]. Each cell corresponds to a distinct region in the reactor model and is treated

as a separate computational domain in MC2 -3. In PyARC, users assign unique labels (IDs) to these

cells. The cell IDs are used in string label for compositions and isotopes used in the ARC codes

which as a maximum length of six characters. Due to the other information required to be embedded

into this 6-character string to uniquely identify compositions and isotopes, this cell ID is limited to

one single character. Furthermore, due to the case-insensitivity of the Workbench input processor,

this ID is also case-insensitive within the PyARC system. Therefore, historically, the number of

allowable cell IDs has been limited to 36 total (the alphanumeric characters “a”-“z”, “0”-“9”).

 Extending the number of allowable cell IDs in PyARC has been a long-requested upgrade from

users across industry and the national lab complex who are wanting to model larger reactor cores

with many assembly types, where 36 unique cells is insufficient for desired level of heterogeneity.

Extending this cell ID to even just two characters in length (for a total of 36 × 36 = 1296 allowable

cell IDs), has not been feasible due to the refactoring that would be required within PyARC to

 19

change how the isotope and composition IDs are uniquely defined. The long-term, more feasible

solution to this request is to extend the allowable label length to 8 characters in the ARC ecosystem,

but this is still a non-trivial upgrade to implement within the ARC code suite.

 As an intermediate solution, the allowable single-character cell IDs in PyARC were extended

to include select special characters, bringing the total allowable cell IDs to 53, up from 36. These

special characters are @, %, ^, ~, -, , +, [, ], {, }, <, >, ., :, |, and ‘. While this ended up being

a fairly simple code update, the challenge was in the thorough testing across all the necessary

integrated codes and workflows and across different OSs to ensure the special characters did not

cause unintended downstream issues. Artificial intelligence (AI) tools were employed to help with

the extensive testing of the various workflows to determine which characters were allowable. Due to

the challenging nature associated with fully testing the special characters in text-based input, this

feature is currently listed as a beta-feature in v4.0.0.

3.6 OpenMC Depletion Zones

The option to subdivide subassemblies into different axial zones when performing depletion calcula-

tions with OpenMC [18] was added in v4.0.0. This capability was added to improve the accuracy

of depletion calculations and parallel the axial zone option that already exists when performing

depletion calculations with REBUS and the ARC codes. The successful implementation of this

feature, along with the broader depletion capabilities with OpenMC in PyARC, is demonstrated in

3.7 Language Server Upgrade Progress

As noted in Section 2.2.1, the initial phase of the Language Server Protocol (LSP) upgrade was

completed in FY25 [24], when the PyARC input-processing schema was converted into a Python

database, or collection of nested Python classes, that define the same logic for the input parameters.

This conversion was accomplished using an ORNL-developed tool within the WASP package that

 20

converts a schema file into an equivalent Python database. In FY25, PyARC was updated to interface

directly with this Python database via its dedicated language server, bypassing the schema internally

at runtime. However, users still interacted with the schema-based Workbench grammar in the GUI

because the LSP-based workflow did not yet support PyARC’s template-driven autocompletion.

 Therefore, the principal LSP-related effort in FY26 focused on closing this functionality gap

while preparing for eventual migration of users to the LSP-based grammar. Although important

enabling work was completed, the migration itself was not deployed in the v4.0.0 release, and the

schema-based grammar remained the default user-facing interface.

3.7.1 FY26 Enabling Work

In FY26, ORNL developers updated the WASP tooling used to generate PyARC’s Python database

so that PyARC’s existing template-based autocompletion definitions could be represented in the

generated database rather than only in the legacy schema workflow. This completed the main

technical prerequisite identified in FY25 for fully transitioning the user-facing Workbench grammar

to the LSP-based implementation.

 This enabling work is significant because the lack of template-based autocompletion support

was the primary reason that users still needed to interact with the schema-based grammar in the

Workbench GUI, even though PyARC had already transitioned internally to the LSP-based workflow.

With this capability now available in the generated database, the functionality gap between the

schema-based and LSP-based workflows was substantially reduced.

 Although this FY26 work did not itself complete deployment of the LSP grammar to users in

v4.0.0, it represents the key remaining infrastructure step needed before that deployment can occur.

 21

3.7.2 Deferred Deployment and Future Release Expectations

The original intent for FY26 was to complete the LSP upgrade by moving users from the schema-

based Workbench grammar to the LSP-based grammar. However, despite the completion of the

enabling work described in Section 3.7.1, the PyARC team ultimately decided not to include the

LSP-based grammar in the v4.0.0 release. During testing, the integration between the new grammar

and the Workbench GUI remained too unstable for production deployment, and enabling it by

default was judged likely to degrade the user experience rather than improve it.

 An additional factor was the timing of the Workbench release cycle. Normally, PyARC

development benefits from access to a beta version of the upcoming Workbench release so that

compatibility can be tested before finalizing a PyARC release. Due to delays in the next Workbench

release, that beta-testing window was not available in time to confirm whether the remaining

LSP-related GUI issues had been resolved. As a result, the schema-based grammar was retained for

the v4.0.0 release, while the LSP-based grammar remains an anticipated feature for a future release.

 The expectation is that the LSP-based grammar may become available in a future PyARC

release once the remaining Workbench-side issues are resolved and stable behavior is confirmed

through testing. Because the necessary enabling infrastructure is now in place, the remaining

work is focused primarily on bug resolution, integration testing, and coordination with the ORNL

Workbench team.

3.8 Windows Support

Historically, PyARC has been primarily developed, tested, and used on Unix-based OSs (i.e., Mac

and Linux machines). As such, the support for executing PyARC on Windows was minimal and

not often reliable, even though many of the codes within the “extended suite” of ARC codes are

supported on Windows. At the request of industry users, extensive effort was put in to ensure

PyARC was reliable to run on Windows for the ARC code suite at a minimum. Two main challenges

were associated with this upgrade: (1) ensuring file path handling throughout the PyARC codebase

 22

was OS-independent, and (2) adding Windows-equivalent batch execution scripts of those that

exist for Mac and Linux (as described in Section 3.9). A challenge associated with this update is

the fact that the PyARC continuous integration test suite does not run on any Windows-based

OS automatically with new developments, therefore relying on the manual test execution for new

features.

 Until v4.0.0, PyARC relied on hard-coded, Unix-style file path construction throughout the

codebase. For Mac and Linux-based OSs, this was sufficient and reliable. However, this method

of path resolution caused failures when running on Windows systems. In v4.0.0, this was resolved

by introducing a new utility function, get path(), dynamically constructs a properly normalized

path based on the host OS. This includes correctly handling platform-specific path roots, such as

Windows drive letters (e.g., C:) and POSIX-style absolute paths.

 An additional component of the added Windows support was the need for batch setup and

execution scripts that operate in the same manner as the current Unix-based bash scripts. These

Unix bash scripts exist such that the execution procedure is simpler for users and so that automatic

checks and updates about the environment can be performed. As such, AI tools [36] were used to

help generate the equivalent Windows batch scripts, which were then manually tested on Windows

for equivalent functionality (see Section 3.9).

 It is important to note that the support for running PyARC on Windows is still considered a

beta-feature in that it can currently only be tested with the ARC codes. The execution of Griffin

[20] is untested on Windows, and the use of OpenMC [18] is not supported at all on Windows (a

known OpenMC limitation).

3.9 Setup and Installation Improvements

PyARC relies on the Workbench RTE for execution. This RTE is a self-contained conda environment

[37], complete with the basic Workbench-specific dependencies required for execution. Until the

addition of OpenMC in PyARC [38, 39], this base environment in Workbench was sufficient as-is

 23

for PyARC execution. The OpenMC integration in PyARC relies on the OpenMC Application

Programming Interface (API) for input generation, code execution, and post-processing, which

means that OpenMC must be installed in the conda RTE. As such, the addition of a “setup” script

and conda environment management system was added to PyARC in v2.0.0 [39] such that OpenMC

can automatically be installed and accessed within the necessary RTE. In v3.0.0 that was released

in FY25 [24, 40], PyHIT [41] was added as a dependency in PyARC as a tool to generate the

MOOSE-based Griffin input files [42, 20]. Therefore, PyHIT was added to the setup process as an

automatically installed dependency. These added dependencies are installed directly from conda,

meaning they use online installation. A challenge with creating a custom PyARC-specific conda

environment is that other packages from conda that these packages rely on may unknowingly update,

which can cause installation failures if there are dependency conflicts in the conda environment,

requiring a lot of effort to pinpoint the issue and fix it. As such, the dependencies added to

PyARC, beyond what is available in the base Workbench RTE, was reexamined in FY26 with the

intention to make the setup process more reliable. With this, the decision was made to remove the

PyHIT dependency as the benefit it provided to the PyARC system did not outweigh the challenges

associated with trying to maintain its compatibility in a conda environment. On the other hand, the

OpenMC API was determined to be a necessity to enhance reliability of the OpenMC integration

and thus was kept as an optional dependency. Removal of PyHIT allowed for management of

OpenMC within the conda environment to be much more achievable.

 The aforementioned setup script(s) are extensively tested both on Linux and Mac OSs, but it

was discovered with the PyARC v3.0.0 release [40] within Workbench 5.6.0 [2], that the base conda

environment from Workbench 5.6.0 was no longer compatible with the OpenMC dependency on Mac

OS (a known OpenMC limitation). Therefore, the management of the RTE had to be reworked such

that PyARC could determine automatically whether a separate conda installation that is compatible

with OpenMC was required depending on the OS and then install and manage that new RTE behind

the scenes. This lead to the creation of a much more elaborate and sophisticated environment

management and runtime system in PyARC to ideally shield users of these RTE nuances.

 24

 As mentioned, these custom PyARC setup scripts were designed originally for Unix-based

systems, but they did not exist for Windows OSs, therefore on Windows, users continued to use and

rely on the original as-is Workbench conda RTE. Because OpenMC is not available for Windows,

having Windows-specific batch setup scripts was previously not a concern. Technically, this is still

not a concern, but in the interest of cross-OS consistency, it was deemed important to update

the Windows execution to match that of the Unix-based systems. As such, the relevant pieces of

the automated setup process and execution management were ported to Windows batch scripts in

v4.0.0, further streamlining and improving runtime consistency across different OSs.

 Additional fixes were added to the Unix-based scripts to ensure proper process exiting and

functionality across different shell environments (e.g. bash and zsh) on Mac OSs.

3.10 Improving Long-Term Maintainability

With the rise of AI capabilities to specifically support code development in the last year, the

developers saw this as an opportunity to improve the maintainability of PyARC through refactoring

and adding in-code documentation. With the help of the AI tool Claude Code [36], extensive

refactoring to reduce code duplication with the creation of reusable functions, remove unnecessary

lines of code, and add missing in-code documentation to help future developers was performed

across several PyARC modules. If done by hand, this refactoring of the code would have likely

taken weeks to months to achieve, but was completed over the course of just a few days with the

help of AI. It is important to note that all code produced by AI is human-reviewed extensively,

and due to the test-driven development that PyARC uses, changes could be checked in detail with

the passing of existing tests. This refactoring and added documentation were seen as necessary

precursors to the upcoming SQA work that is outlined in Section 4.2.

 An additional improvement was the implementation of using some Python best practices for

file management and code execution. All error handling now moves through a centralized function

that ensures files are properly closed when the code aborts, leaving no dangling open files which can

 25

cause issues for various operating environments. All code execution was also updated to use the

subprocess package in Python which can capture the error output from a code when it aborts, but

allows the PyARC code to continue. This allows PyARC to exit gracefully when an integrated code

fails to run successfully, allowing processes to fully close and not leave dangling execution processes

running on the machine.

 These changes that follow software development best practices for quality assurance not only

enhance the long-term maintainability of the code for current and future developers, but also

enhance the runtime reliability for users.

4 Software Quality Assurance

PyARC is used by industry and external organizations for production analysis. Those organizations

increasingly need to know how the software they depend on is controlled: how changes are reviewed,

what a release means and what it has been tested against, how defects are reported and resolved, and

what evidence exists that a result can be reproduced. Answering those questions in a documented

and auditable way, rather than through just the accumulated practice of the development team, is

the purpose of the Software Quality Assurance (SQA) activities described in this section.

 The governing framework is DOE Order 414.1D, “Quality Assurance” [43], is implemented

at ANL through the Quality Assurance Program Plan (QAPP) [44]. The technical requirements

applicable to software are drawn from American Society of Mechanical Engineers (ASME) Nuclear

Quality Assurance (NQA)-1-2022, Part II, Subpart 2.7 [45]. PyARC is designated as non-safety

software, which permits a graded approach to those Subpart 2.7 requirements qualified by the phrase

“as appropriate.” Note that this does not relieve the end user of responsibility as a PyARC release is

not qualified for use in a safety or quality application until the using organization has performed its

own acceptance and validation for that application.

 How the requirements apply to PyARC depends on the scope boundary that PyARC is a

wrapper and workflow manager rather than a solver. Its requirements are written at the level of the

 26

functions PyARC itself performs: validating user input, generating code-specific input, sequencing

and executing the integrated codes, managing working files and directories, detecting and reporting

failures raised by those codes, extracting and post-processing results, and capturing the information

needed to reproduce a workflow. Requirements are not written for the physics models or numerical

methods of the integrated codes, which remain the responsibility of their own quality assurance

programs. The ARC codes, for instance, are covered by the ARC Software Quality Assurance Plan

(SQAP) [46].

4.1 The SQA Plan

The principal SQA activity this FY was the development of the PyARC Software Quality Assurance

Plan (SQAP). The work proceeded in two stages: an assessment of the current state of the project

against NQA-1-2022 Subpart 2.7, described in Section 4.2, followed by drafting of the plan itself.

Plans of peer software (ARC SQAP [46], Cardinal SQAP [47], and MOOSE SQAP [48]) were

reviewed so that the document would align with existing practices within the ANL and open-source

ecosystems. All three follow a common structure that maps directly onto the standard, and the

PyARC plan adopts it as well.

 Because PyARC is a workflow driver rather than a solver, the plan makes four choices in

applying the standard.

 • Dependencies are assigned a disposition rather than assured. PyARC executes a

 number of analysis codes, some of them developed externally, and depends on external libraries.

 The plan does not perform quality assurance for software the project does not develop, so

 each dependency is assigned one of three dispositions. Software controlled under its own

 documented program is covered by that program, such as the ARC codes under the ARC

 SQAP [46] and Griffin under the MOOSE SQAP [48]. Software that would have an effect

 on PyARC quality, but doesn’t have its own SQA program, is evaluated to make sure it

 works before it is approved as a dependency in a release. Software whose results are verified

 27

  with the design analysis for each application is excluded [45], which is the basis applied to

  general-purpose numerical and plotting libraries because the regression suite exercises their

  results on every change request. The Software Manager reviews the exclusions at each release

  review.

• Verification is automated, and validation belongs to the user. Verification confirms

  that the intended code is invoked with the intended input, that inputs and outputs are

  translated correctly, and that a given input reproduces a given result. It also confirms that

  failures raised by the integrated codes are detected and reported rather than passed over.

  The automated test suite performs it on every change request, supplemented by independent

  review, and it does not extend to the physics or numerical methods of the integrated codes.

  Validation depends on the operating system, Python interpreter, and integrated code versions

  that the using organization chooses, so the plan leaves it to the end user. To support it, each

  release log records the operating environment in which the release was verified, and users

  can run the distributed test suite in their own environment to confirm that it reproduces the

  release-review results.

• Independent review is enforced by the repository. The plan requires that baseline

  branches be protected, that direct pushes to them be prohibited, and that every change request

  be approved by an independent reviewer through the repository approval system before it is

  merged. Final approval rests solely with the independent reviewer, and the system retains the

  reviewer’s identity, the date, and the comments, so each approval leaves a record.

• Problems are classified by their effect on results. Reported problems are classified as

  a defect, an enhancement, or a user mistake, and defects are graded as critical, normal, or

  minor. A critical defect affects the accuracy of results and obliges the project to tell users

  its impact, how to avoid it, the corrective action, and the timeline for resolution. A normal

  defect affects operation or execution with little chance of significantly affecting results, and a

  minor defect does not affect accuracy.

 At the time of this report, a draft of the plan is completed and is being submitted to the

 28

Argonne quality assurance representative for review.

4.2 Gap Analysis

An assessment was conducted to compare the current state of PyARC against the requirements of

NQA-1-2022 Subpart 2.7, as they are carried into the SQAP. It was carried out jointly by the SQA

lead and the Software Manager to identify the major components PyARC does not yet have and to

recommend the tasks needed in FY27 to bring it into compliance with the plan and, through it, the

standard. Each task was given a priority from 1 (highest) to 3 (lowest), an estimated level of effort,

and a proposed owner, and the results are summarized in Table 4.1. Drafting the SQAP itself,

which carried the highest priority, was completed in FY26, pending official review and approval

from the ANL quality assurance representative.

 Much of what the ASME standards require already exist in some form in PyARC, though

not as a controlled document. The regression tests, the tutorial problems, and the documentation

kept in the repository are the basis for many of the tasks, so most are a matter of formalizing and

completing existing material.

 Controlled documents. The largest group of tasks is the set of controlled documents that

the plan calls for and PyARC does not yet have. The assessment concluded that the Software

Design Description (SDD) should take the form of one document for each class or module, supported

by repository-based documentation and a documentation section in the repository. The Software

Requirements Specification (SRS) was rated a lower priority and a low effort because much of it may

already be present in the repository’s main repository-based documentation. The verification report

and the treatment of validation were both rated Priority 1. The plan lists no separate verification

report, so the working assumption is that it is largely a record of the retained test results accessible

through the Requirements Traceability Matrix (RTM), and the task is to confirm that the RTM and

those results already satisfy it. For validation, no PyARC-specific document is expected, consistent

with the plan’s assignment of validation to the using organization. Whether the Failure Analysis

process described in the plan.

the RTM, the verification report, and the full user manual, and these will determine how much of

the list can be completed in FY27. The plan requires its own review at least every three years, and

the remaining gaps are revisited at each release review, so progress against them is recorded as part

of the normal release cycle rather than tracked separately.

5 Conclusion

PyARC continues to serve as a robust, user-friendly workflow management tool for advanced reactor

analysis, leveraging the NEAMS Workbench framework to integrate a suite of high-fidelity codes

developed at ANL and other national laboratories. The developments and upgrades completed in

FY26 further strengthen PyARC’s role in supporting industry, academia, and national laboratories

with reliable, reproducible, and automated multiphysics workflows for fast reactor modeling and

verification.

 This year’s major code-specific enhancements include the transition to GAMSRC for neutron-

gamma coupled calculations (Section 3.1), expanded DASSH geometry support (Section 3.2), new

bowing reactivity worth calculations with PERSENT and NUBOW-3D (Section 3.3), and improved

integration with SCALE’s ORIGEN-S for detailed depletion analysis (Section 3.4). The additional

code enhancements of the extension of allowable MC2 -3 cell IDs (Section 3.5) and the addition

of OpenMC axial depletion zones (Section 3.6) enable more detailed and flexible reactor models.

Reliable Windows support (Section 3.8) and improved setup and installation procedures (Section 3.9)

further broaden accessibility and ease of use across OSs.

 FY26 also saw continued progress on the LSP upgrade, including completion of the key

enabling work needed to support template-based autocompletion in the LSP workflow. Although

this substantially advanced the migration effort, deployment of the LSP-based grammar in the

Workbench GUI was deferred from v4.0.0 pending additional integration testing, bug fixes, and

confirmation against a future Workbench release.

 In addition to technical upgrades, FY26 marked the establishment of a formal SQA program for

PyARC (Section 4). The creation of the SQAP and a comprehensive gap analysis lay the foundation

for continued improvements in documentation, traceability, and verification, ensuring PyARC meets

DOE and ASME NQA-1 quality standards for software used for nuclear applications.

 Through these ongoing efforts, PyARC remains committed to providing the advanced reactor

community with a controlled, documented, and validated platform for complex analysis, fostering

 32

best practices, collaboration, and reproducibility in nuclear science and engineering.
