Building a consumer health informatics introductory course consensus curriculum: an eDelphi study
Authors: Olivia F Krol, Khaya Clark, Phillip D Jenkins, Vishala Mishra, Sriram Iyengar, Christie Martin, Katherine Blondon, Ruchi Thanawala, Julie Doberne
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo develop consensus-based core competencies for an entry-level graduate Consumer Health Informatics (CHI) course using a rigorous eDelphi methodology.Materials and methodsWe conducted initial fieldwork examining the CHI literature and existing educational approaches, identifying foundational themes in the field. Using inductive analysis, these themes were synthesized into preliminary course objectives. We created an advisory board to review and refine all course objectives and resolve disagreements within the survey results. A modified eDelphi process was then employed, systematically soliciting expert feedback through iterative Qualtrics surveys to refine and achieve consensus on core competencies.ResultsThree Delphi survey rounds were completed, resulting in 11 core competencies deemed essential for an entry-level graduate CHI curriculum.DiscussionThese competencies reflect expert agreement on the foundational knowledge and skills required for graduates entering the rapidly evolving CHI landscape.ConclusionAs digital health technologies become increasingly sophisticated and prevalent, CHI education plays a critical role in preparing informatics professionals to support patient-centered care. Despite this need, there are no standardized competencies for CHI courses. The competencies presented here provide a durable curricular framework that can help guide graduate-level CHI education while remaining adaptable for instructors, institutions, and ongoing technological change.Keywords: consumer health informatics, competency-based education, digital health, Delphi technique, curriculum

Introduction

Background

Today, 96% of U.S. adults own at least 1 computing device.1 Ninety-two percent have a smartphone and internet subscription,1 enabling many patients to use the internet for healthcare-related tasks such as accessing information, viewing test results, or communicating with providers. This shift coincides with a move from paternalistic to patient-centered decision-making. Patients are empowered to make informed health choices while increasing their responsibility to obtain knowledge.

As healthcare becomes more reliant on digital tools for care coordination, telehealth, remote monitoring, patient portals, and e-prescribing, patients, medical professionals, and informaticists must navigate these technologies. Consumer Health Informatics (CHI)2 is 1 of the 5 practice areas supported by the American Medical Informatics Association (AMIA). CHI stands at the intersection of patients, providers, and technology, empowering individuals to manage their health and ensuring that health information is accessible, effective, and understandable.2,3

Current educational models

There are over 340 accredited informatics graduate programs in the United States to date.4 In 2017, AMIA joined other education leaders in adopting competency-based education (CBE) to standardize proficiencies among graduates of accredited programs. Unlike traditional educational frameworks that focus on what learners should “know,” competency-based education emphasizes what learners should be able “to do,”5 centering on the skillsets, knowledge, and proficiencies needed for success in a field.6 Competencies are dynamic, promoting an understanding of concepts and processes applicable across various fields, preparing graduates for ongoing learning. AMIA established 10 foundational competencies essential to informatics, yet broad enough to be applicable across all 5 practice areas (medical informatics, public health informatics, consumer health informatics, translational bioinformatics, and clinical research informatics). While Program Directors must ensure these competencies are met upon graduation, individual programs determine their relevance and integration into coursework, leading to variability across programs and classes.

Significance

Given the importance and growing need for well-trained informaticians proficient in CHI, a collaborative effort initiated by the AMIA Consumer Health Informatics Working Group (CHI-WG) aimed to develop core competencies that learners should demonstrate upon completing an introductory graduate level CHI course. As with the CBE model, competencies should be developed by experts outside of the program, ensuring a diversity of opinions and reducing the emphasis on individual education programs, instructors, and past curricula.5 We utilized an electronic Delphi (eDelphi) methodology to survey a panel of experts through anonymous iterative feedback6–15 to create core competencies. AMIA’s 10 core competencies served as a guiding reference.

Objective

To develop core competencies for an entry-level graduate CHI course using the eDelphi methodology to systematically and iteratively solicit expert feedback.

Methods

Ethical considerations

This study was deemed nonhuman-subjects research by the Oregon Health & Science University (OHSU) Institutional Review Board and therefore did not require informed consent. Study participants were given the option to opt-in to participate in the study and provide their email addresses. If participants did not complete a survey, they were excluded from subsequent survey iterations. Data privacy and confidentiality for all participants adhered to OHSU data handling and security rules. Participant identities were blinded to each other and to the advisory panel.

Conducted field study and fieldwork literature review

To better understand the scope of the CHI curriculum, we solicited syllabi from members of the AMIA CHI-WG and the Healthcare Information and Management Systems Society (HIMSS) professional organizations using standard convenience sampling. Seven syllabi were collected. A general MEDLINE review was conducted along with Google search to gather existing CHI curricula worldwide.

Advisory board

We assembled an international advisory panel of 5 experts in CHI or Delphi methods for the purpose of developing objectives for the eDelphi survey iterations, reviewing responses, and refining of each survey iteration. Members were recruited during CHI-WG meetings and through personal communication.

Initial survey development

Utilizing inductive thematic analysis, team members (OK and JD) derived codes directly from the data rather than relying on a predetermined list of terms. The study team identified overarching themes. Competency-related texts were further deconstructed into sub-themes. Items were then summarized, and objectives were drafted.

These objectives were then reviewed by the advisory board, which was ultimately responsible for deciding which objectives would be incorporated into the first eDelphi survey. Given the geographical spread of all members of the advisory panel, we opted for asynchronous collaboration. Study team member (OK) compiled data from the fieldwork into the web-based survey tool, Qualtrics (Seattle, WA and Provo, UT), in questionnaires for the expert panel rank items and provide open-ended feedback. To help prioritize objectives, the advisory panel selected the AMIA foundational domains they felt would be most important to address. Nonblinded questionnaire results were provided back to the advisory panel and discussed via email. The study team (OK) revised objectives based on advisory panel discussion and feedback, sending back to the advisory board for deliberation.

Competencies underwent a series of edits and additions until consensus amongst the expert panel was reached with 8 course objectives. Qualtrics was used to create surveys, with a 3-point ranking provided for each competency, “disagree,” “agreed as written” or “agree with the following change(s)” with branching logic prompting participants to comment if “afagree with the following change(s)” was selected. eDelphi study design flowchart.

A modified 3-point scale was used as it is straightforward and reduces cognitive workload,13,16–21 and a free text option was chosen to allow participants to provide justification for changes.22,23 During Survey 1, participants were asked to provide demographic information including their degree(s), occupation/position(s), years of CHI experience, teaching experience, and CHI research experience. The survey was piloted with 3 study team members (OK, JD, and KC) and graduate students prior to distribution.

Predefined threshold

Setting a predefined agreement threshold is considered best practice,8,15,24,25 and within this domain, a consensus threshold of 70% or greater is considered rigorous.8,12,16,17,26–29 A 70% threshold was established for agreement and competencies meeting this threshold were set aside and excluded from further voting until the final round. Competencies with less than 30% agreement were removed. Survey iterations proceeded until all course objectives met the 70% threshold, or had less than 30% agreement.

Selecting experts

There is no agreement on the optimal sample size; however, most Delphi studies have ≤25 participants, with anywhere between 5 and 23 considered sufficient.15,22,30 Representativeness is assessed not on quantity, but on the quality of experts, and as such, purposeful or convenience sampling was used.6,17,30 We utilized a purposeful sampling approach as we involved AMIA CHI-WG members, IMIA working group leaders relevant to CHI, and AMIA accredited graduate or doctoral or CHI fellowship program directors. We encouraged that these participants to share the survey with other CHI educators and researchers in hopes of collecting a diversity of opinions. We did not have a response limit set a priori.

Delphi rounds

Three survey rounds were administered, allowing for the incorporation of free-text responses. A minimum of 3 iterations allows for free-text responses from Round 1 to be incorporated as new statements in Round 2 and subsequent rounds.27

Disagreements were reviewed by the advisory panel for revisions and modifications. Course objectives that met the 70% agreement threshold were revised for grammatical errors when supported by the majority of expert comments and at the discretion of the advisory board. All modifications made after voting was completed were presented in Survey 3 for final approval by participants.

Analysis

Quantitative data were analyzed using non-parametric descriptive statistics.

Results

We distributed 101 surveys via email; of the 37 individuals (39%) who agreed to participate, 26 completed the survey, with 9 (35%) being referred participants who were not initially sent the survey. Survey 2 was completed by 22 participants (85%), and 20 participants (91%) completed Survey 3.

Discussion

Competency-based education (CBE) has emerged as the gold standard in health professions education and has been adopted by the American Medical Informatics Association (AMIA), which published the first set of biomedical informatics competencies in 2012.2 These efforts have since expanded to include both graduate and baccalaureate-level competencies for informatics degrees.31 However, there remains a gap in defining competencies at the level of individual courses. This is particularly evident in Consumer Health Informatics (CHI).

One reason this is so important is that CHI is broader than many, even within the field of informatics, often realize.32 CHI encompasses not only the patient and provider but also information retrieval experts, data managers, and health IT systems.32,33 For example, CHI promoting holistic, socio-environmental models have faced criticism for neglecting critical elements like health information exchange and the seamless flow of data in digital ecosystems.34,35 A foundational CHI course must equip students to navigate these multifaceted dynamics (spanning technology, policy, and human behavior) with critical awareness.

Because CHI is so broad, course content may vary depending on the university or instructor. Our review of CHI syllabi confirmed this variability, highlighting the need for standardization. Without clearly defined competencies, trainee exposure remains inconsistent, potentially resulting in gaps in foundational knowledge. Given the rise of online health information and its impact on consumer behavior, it is essential that future informaticians who enroll in CHI courses develop a shared baseline of understanding to support patient-centered care and digital health literacy.

Flexibility within defined competencies is crucial in a dynamic field like CHI, where technology and information are constantly evolving. An entry-level CHI course should serve as a stepping stone, offering foundational knowledge to be applied across diverse career paths in clinical or biomedical informatics. Competencies should act as adaptable guidelines, evolving with emerging technologies and patient needs, rather than prescribing a rigid body of knowledge.

CBE focuses on the specific skills and abilities students must demonstrate before graduation, rather than dictating how material is taught. This allows programs to maintain flexibility while ensuring standardized outcomes. For example, the competency “demonstrate how varying levels of health literacy and numeracy influence consumer health decision-making” ensures that programs integrate these concepts into real-world applications without disrupting existing curricula. CBE guarantees that students not only acquire foundational knowledge but also develop the skills to apply it effectively in their chosen informatics careers.

The collective experience of our expert participants was robust. Those with direct CHI experience averaged over 12 years in CHI-related work, research, teaching, or graduate education. Input from informatics program directors with less CHI experience was also valuable, providing a broader perspective on the needs of informatics graduate programs. Over a third of participants were referred to the eDelphi panel from CHI-WG or HIMSS Consumer Health Group as CHI experts. Despite the predominance of men in informatics program leadership and clinical subspecialty fellowships (75% and 83%, respectively, in 201936) our survey had equal representation of men and women (50/50).

Demonstrating the impact of health literacy and numeracy on decision-making was the top-rated competency in Survey 1, with 96% of participants selecting either “agree” or “agree with changes.” The feedback from those selecting “agree with changes” included comments related to slight grammar or wording changes, rather than its context, exemplifying the unanimous support for this topic. According to the Center for Healthcare Strategies, only an estimated 10% of US adults have “proficient” health literacy costing an estimated $349 billion to the U.S. economy annually.37

Numeracy is another barrier consumers must face. Health information requires individuals to perform multiple step calculations and make interpretations (eg, risk, medication dosing, etc.). Too often, numeracy is overlooked in the field of health informatics.38–41 Low health numeracy has been associated with increased number of hospitalizations in those with asthma,40 poorer anticoagulation control for those on anticoagulants,42 and possibly poorer glycemic control with those with diabetes.43 In addition, physicians, too, face challenges with numeracy, particularly in understanding risks and probabilities, unintentionally misleading patients on risks.44,45 Although our syllabi review relied on convenience sampling rather than a systematic approach, we found that literacy and numeracy were not consistently included in course objectives or topics, highlighting the urgent need for a standardized curriculum.

The effectiveness of CHI depends on its usability, which is influenced not only by literacy and numeracy but also by individual characteristics (eg, physical, cognitive, and macroergonomic factors) as well as barriers (eg, disability, limited numeracy/literacy, and internet access).32,46 Experts recognized this, and defining usability and conducting heuristic usability was an additional competency suggestion by Survey 1 participants in Survey 1, achieving an 86% consensus in Survey 2. Improving usability can be achieved by providing clear pathways for information or adapting communication methods (eg, video, audio, and text) to align with users’ preferences.32

Although many acknowledge that health equity should inform CHI, many health informatics interventions have only exacerbated healthcare disparities.33 Digital health is particularly vulnerable to inequity as current interventions often benefit advantaged groups. Recently, Kim and Backonja consolidated a definition of digital health equity as a “multi-level socio-ecological concept that results from fair and just opportunities for everyone to attain their highest level of health through access to technology-enabled health resources and services.”47 This definition positions digital health interventions as a key avenue for improving health equity, underscoring the need for a standardized curriculum grounded in digital health equity, as reflected in the course objectives agreed upon by experts.

Limitations

Limitations inherent to the study design include expert panel recruitment and the use of the Delphi methodology. Many participants were contacted based on the study team’s personal knowledge of their expertise, which may introduce bias and limit representation. Although international experts were recruited, their participation was lower than that of U.S. based participants, partly due to accessibility and study team familiarity.

Delphi studies are often criticized for the lack of standardized guidelines,22 but considering feasibility challenges, the Delphi method offers a rigorous approach to achieving consensus. Anonymity reduces conformity pressures48 among participants, and structured feedback engages experts.11 We adhered to established guidelines, including rationale for Delphi technique, justifying any modifications, predetermined consensus threshold, planned procedures, and providing transparent (but anonymous) reporting of results to experts.15,24

Additional limitations include varying expertise among panelists, a small sample size, and reduced participation with each iteration.13 While there is no established consensus on the optimal sample size, best practice emphasizes the quality and representativeness of experts over quantity, with diverse backgrounds contributing to higher-quality solutions.15,22,30,49 In smaller fields, such as CHI, reliable outcomes can still be achieved with a smaller, homogeneous sample.49 To balance diversity and expertise, we targeted program directors and CHI leaders, encouraging to share the survey within their networks. Retention rates were 85% for Survey 2 and 91% for Survey 3.

Future work

Additional work for this introductory course includes mapping these competencies to the Commission on Accreditation for Health Informatics & Information Management Education (CAHIIM) competencies as well as building a core CHI literature repository. These competencies will be applied to Oregon Health and Science University’s Spring 2026 into to Consumer Health Informatics course.

Future work is needed to establish the core fundamentals of a consumer informatics curriculum, rather than from a single introductory level course. CHI is a robust and rapidly developing field. One course is simply insufficient to cover its range from consumer heuristics to mobile devices.

Conclusion

As digital health continues to evolve, the demand for informaticians with expertise in consumer health grows. CHI explores the integration of patient experience with provider-curated information that is secure, credible, accessible, and empowering. The solution may vary across health systems, technologies, and infrastructures, and it is important that any informatician working on patient-facing tools understands the numerous stakeholders involved. Establishing core entry-level CHI competencies standardizes training and ensures consistency across accredited programs.
