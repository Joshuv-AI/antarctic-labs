Parents Value Shared Decision‐Making and Sensitive Communication During End‐of‐Life Care in Neonatal Intensive Care: An Exploratory Study
Authors: Ana Morillo Palomo, Ana Riverola de Veciana, Montserrat Esquerda Aresté, Francisco José Cambra Lasaosa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAimTo explore parents' needs during end‐of‐life decision‐making and to understand their experiences in a neonatal intensive care unit (NICU) following the death of their child.MethodsThis observational, retrospective study was conducted in the NICU of a tertiary‐level hospital. Data were collected using a structured questionnaire administered to parents following the death of their neonate. Three main domains were explored: information and communication, the decision‐making process and comfort‐focused care.ResultsDuring the study period, 51 neonates died after treatment was redirected towards comfort‐focused care. Of the 102 eligible parents, 52 participated. Information and communication were highly rated (mean 6.5/7), particularly the sensitive delivery of information. Parents generally felt supported during decision‐making (mean 5.6/7), and most (76.9%) preferred professional guidance. However, only half perceived concordance between their preferences and the final decision. Comfort‐focused care was also positively evaluated (mean 6.13/7), although opportunities to choose the place of death were limited. The questionnaire demonstrated acceptable internal consistency (α = 0.72).ConclusionParents value shared decision‐making that respects their values while providing professional guidance. These findings highlight the importance of sensitive communication, adequate time for reflection, ongoing support throughout the decision‐making process, and meaningful opportunities to say goodbye.

neonatal intensive care unit

Introduction

Most deaths in neonatal intensive care units (NICUs) occur following the withdrawal or non‐initiation of life‐sustaining treatments after a decision has been made to redirect care towards comfort‐focused measures [1, 2]. These situations typically arise in the context of severe prematurity, congenital anomalies, or critical illness, where prognostic uncertainty and the risk of significant morbidity complicate clinical decision‐making.

End‐of‐life decision‐making in this setting represents a major clinical and ethical challenge. It requires careful consideration of the infant's best interests, the proportionality of treatment, and anticipated quality of life. In addition, these decisions are deeply influenced by parental values, beliefs, and expectations, making effective communication between healthcare professionals and families essential.

Until recent years, the available literature mainly described the causes and circumstances of death in NICUs, as well as parental accompaniment and support during bereavement. More recently, shared decision‐making in this setting has received increasing attention. This approach emphasises collaboration between clinicians and parents, integrating medical expertise with family perspectives. However, current evidence is largely derived from narrative reviews or qualitative studies based on interviews with families and healthcare professionals [3, 4].

In 2012, a Swiss study used a questionnaire to quantitatively assess the perspectives of parents of paediatric patients receiving end‐of‐life care, including a subgroup of neonates [5]. Since then, few studies have specifically focused on systematically evaluating parents' experiences and needs in the NICU using quantitative approaches.

To provide appropriate support to parents, it is essential to understand their needs during the decision‐making process and at the end of their children's lives.

Therefore, the objectives of this study were to describe the experiences of parents whose children died in the NICU and to explore how they perceived and experienced the decision‐making process. The study also aimed to improve understanding of parents' needs during end‐of‐life decision‐making in the neonatal intensive care setting and to assess whether these experiences influenced their subsequent grieving process.

Methods

Study Design and Setting

This was a single‐centre, observational, retrospective study conducted in the neonatal unit of Sant Joan de Déu Hospital, Barcelona, Catalonia, Spain. This tertiary‐level university maternal and paediatric referral centre serves a substantial proportion of the population of Barcelona and Catalonia. It manages approximately 3500 deliveries annually. The NICU provides highly specialised neonatal care and has a longstanding commitment to Infant‐and Family‐Centred Developmental Care, including accreditation as a NIDCAP training centre. Data were collected using a questionnaire administered to parents of neonates who died in the unit.

Questionnaire Development

The questionnaire was developed through a three‐step process. First, an extensive review of the literature on decision‐making and comfort‐focused care in neonatology was conducted. Second, two professionals with expertise in neonatal comfort‐focused care developed the initial set of questions. Finally, the questionnaire was reviewed by a third professional with expertise in paediatric intensive care and bioethics to ensure its content validity and relevance.

The final questionnaire comprised four domains (Appendix S1).

Sociodemographic characteristics were assessed using eight items. Information received in the neonatal unit was evaluated using eight items, while the decision‐making process was explored through 19 items. Comfort‐focused care was assessed using six items.

The questionnaire included 33 items in total. Three were open‐ended questions, six were multiple‐choice questions, and 24 used an ordinal response scale. These items were rated on a seven‐point Likert‐type scale ranging from 0 to 6, where 0 indicated never and 6 indicated always. For some items, scores ranged from 0, indicating very unimportant, to 6, indicating very important.

Study Population

The study population consisted of parents of neonates who died in the neonatal unit between January 2017 and October 2020, following a decision to initiate palliative care.

Parents were excluded if they had been followed during pregnancy within the Prenatal Palliative Care Programme or if their child had died < 1 year before the start of the study. Additional exclusion criteria included discharge home after the initiation of palliative care and transfer to the referring centre for end‐of‐life care following a decision regarding treatment appropriateness.

Data Collection

Parents were initially contacted by telephone to inform them about the study and invite them to participate. After providing consent, participants received an email containing a link to the questionnaire, which was administered through the REDCap platform.

Statistical Analysis

Study data were collected and managed using the REDCap (Research Electronic Data Capture; Vanderbilt University, Nashville, Tennessee, US) electronic data capture tools hosted at Sant Joan de Déu Hospital [6, 7].

Categorical variables were analysed by grouping categories as appropriate and are presented as frequencies and percentages. When relevant, the median was also calculated as a measure of central tendency. Continuous variables are presented as means and standard deviations, as well as median and, where appropriate, percentiles. As most variables did not follow a normal distribution, non‐parametric tests were used to assess differences between groups. The Mann–Whitney U test was used for comparisons between two groups and the Kruskal–Wallis test for comparisons involving more than two groups.

Internal consistency was assessed using Cronbach's alpha coefficient. Correlations between domains were analysed to evaluate questionnaire performance. Comparisons between domains were performed using the Friedman test, as not all domains were normally distributed.

A p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All analyses were conducted using the SPSS Statistics version 25.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, New York, USA).

Ethics

The study was approved by the Ethics and Research Committee of the Sant Joan de Déu Foundation (protocol code C.I. PIC‐139‐21). Informed consent was obtained from all participating parents before their inclusion in the study. An electronic informed consent form was incorporated into the REDCap platform, and participants were required to review and accept it before accessing the questionnaire.

This domain comprised eight items. The highest mean score was for parents' perception that information was communicated sensitively (mean 6.76). Comfort‐Focused Care

This domain included five items. The highest mean score was observed for the item assessing whether parents were given sufficient time to say goodbye to their baby (mean 6.65). The lowest mean score was for the item evaluating whether parents were able to choose the place of death (mean 4.96). The overall mean score for the five items related to comfort‐focused care was 6.13 (range 3.60–7; SD 0.91). Figure 6 shows the results of this domain.

Comfort‐focused care domain. Items assessed included parental involvement in end‐of‐life decision‐making, the ability to choose the place of death, opportunities for other family members to say farewell to the child, adequacy of time to say goodbye, and whether the NICU experience helped parents cope with the grieving process.

Parents' final comments highlighted several aspects of their experiences with decision‐making and end‐of‐life care. Many expressed gratitude for the care provided by healthcare professionals. Some appreciated being encouraged to hold and accompany their child during the final stages of life. One mother stated that she would have liked the opportunity to take her child home so that death could occur in a more intimate setting. Another mother suggested providing alternative accommodation for families to avoid sharing wards with less severely ill infants, which can be particularly distressing when a child is seriously ill or dying.

Functioning of the Scale

Cronbach's alpha for the questionnaire was 0.72, indicating acceptable internal consistency. Analysis of correlations between domains revealed statistically significant associations, particularly between information and communication, decision‐making, and comfort‐focused care.

Comparison of Domains

Finally, the mean scores of the different domains, information and communication, decision‐making, and comfort‐focused care, were compared according to parent and patient characteristics, including gender, nationality, age, spiritual beliefs, educational level, and the main pathology of the deceased child. Statistically significant differences (p < 0.05) were observed according to the child's main pathology in the domains of information and communication and comfort‐focused care. Discussion

This study provides valuable insight into parents' experiences of comfort‐focused care and decision‐making in the neonatal setting. By using questionnaires completed directly by parents and a numerical rating scale, it was possible to quantify these experiences.

Parents reported that healthcare professionals generally communicated information sensitively, demonstrating empathy and commitment. High scores for items related to listening and engagement reflect the importance parents place on healthcare professionals acknowledging their fears, doubts, and hopes regarding their child's illness [8]. A compassionate approach, sensitive communication, and emotional support provide comfort and help prevent additional distress at the end of life [9]. Establishing synchrony and trust with families has been described as a bridge to more meaningful conversations and shared understanding [10, 11, 12]. Both verbal and non‐verbal communication have a significant impact and highlight the importance of training professionals to communicate effectively in difficult situations [13, 14]. Without such training, opportunities to demonstrate empathy may be missed, and professionals may focus on providing medical information rather than emotional support [15].

Decision‐making scored lower than other domains, reflecting some gaps in the process. Not all healthcare professionals explicitly clarified the role parents were expected to play in decision‐making, with mean scores below 5 on this item. Although most respondents did not feel pressured, comments from the open‐ended questions suggested that some parents required more time to make decisions. This finding may reflect differences between healthcare professionals and parents in their readiness to discuss treatment options [13]. The neonatal end‐of‐life period is often brief and unexpected, contrasting with chronic paediatric conditions where families may anticipate outcomes [16]. Providing time and evidence to process the situation has been associated with greater parental satisfaction and may influence subsequent grief [17, 18].

When parents were asked whether they felt pressured by healthcare professionals during discussions about withdrawal of life‐sustaining treatment or other interventions, most reported that they never felt pressured. This may suggest that although professionals can experience moral distress during the decision‐making process, once a decision has been reached they are able to provide time and space at the end of life without haste or pressure [19].

Comparing parents' preferences for involvement in decision‐making with their perceptions of what occurred revealed discrepancies. While most decisions were reportedly made jointly (63.5%), nearly one‐third of parents who preferred shared decision‐making felt they made the decision alone. Conversely, parents who wanted to make decisions independently often perceived the decision as joint or primarily made by healthcare professionals. This suggests that well‐intentioned paternalism may persist, even within shared decision‐making frameworks, potentially limiting opportunities for parental involvement [20]. Offering options rather than recommendations may better facilitate alignment and participation. A qualitative study that explored parental narratives 3 years after the death of a child in the NICU found that, although parents valued having their opinions heard, they also needed responsibility for decisions to be explicitly shared. The authors suggested that fully autonomous decision‐making should be avoided in such tragic circumstances, as it may be experienced by parents as abandonment [21].

Most parents reported that their beliefs, spirituality, and values were considered, reflecting healthcare professionals' attention to family values during decision‐making [22, 23]. Acknowledging these values is essential and can support decision‐making even in the presence of disagreement [9, 24].

Interestingly, 55.8% of parents reported that discussions about resuscitation or other treatments were not held, which may reflect a protective approach by healthcare professionals once palliative care had been agreed upon [25]. Factors most influencing decisions included evident clinical deterioration, poor prognosis, and anticipated suffering, consistent with the literature [20].

For parents, the opportunity to say goodbye to their baby is central to recognising their parenthood and parental identity through holding, comforting, and caring for their child. Facilitating these experiences is therefore a key component of comfort‐focused care in the NICU. In line with previous studies [26, 27], most parents in our study reported that being able to accompany their child at the end of life helped them in their subsequent grieving process.

The possibility for other family members to say goodbye also received high scores, although these may have been influenced by visiting restrictions during the COVID‐19 pandemic. In the final months of the study period. The lowest‐rated item concerned parents' ability to choose the place of death, with options limited to remaining in the NICU or being transferred to a private maternity room. A parental comment expressing a wish for home death suggests that the restricted range of options may have contributed to this finding and highlights the importance of exploring and facilitating parental preferences whenever possible [28].

Differences according to primary pathology were observed in the information and communication domain for the infection group, although these results should be interpreted with caution because of the small sample size. The rapid progression of infection may limit parents' ability to adapt to the possibility of death, negatively influencing their perceptions. Finally, parents of premature infants reported lower participation in end‐of‐life decisions and reduced choice regarding the place of death. This may reflect the early timing of death, ongoing maternal hospitalisation, and limited opportunity for in‐depth end‐of‐life discussions.

This study has several strengths. It provides quantitative data on parents' experiences of end‐of‐life decision‐making in the NICU, an area that has been explored predominantly through qualitative research. In addition, the questionnaire addressed several domains of care, allowing a broad assessment of parents' experiences.

The study also has several limitations. Firstly, although the response rate is comparable to that of other studies involving bereaved parents, it remains relatively low [5]. In addition, selection bias may have been introduced, as parents with more positive experiences may have been more likely to participate. Migrant families were underrepresented, and language barriers may have further influenced participation. Secondly, the retrospective design introduces the possibility of recall bias. Finally, the single‐centre setting may limit generalisability of the findings to other NICUs with different organisational or cultural contexts.

Conclusion

This study sheds light on the experiences of parents who have lost a child in the NICU, as well as their preferred level of involvement in decision‐making. Most parents expressed a desire to participate in decisions alongside healthcare professionals, while having their values respected. The findings emphasise the importance of sensitive communication, allowing time for reflection, supporting parental involvement, and creating opportunities to say goodbye.

Importantly, the study also reveals a critical gap between parents' expectations regarding decision‐making and the persistence of paternalistic practices in clinical care. Bridging this gap is essential to fostering genuine shared decision‐making, enhancing parental engagement, and ensuring that end‐of‐life decisions are aligned with both clinical judgement and family values.

Overall, these findings highlight the importance of ensuring that neonatal units integrate this knowledge into their local guidelines and regularly monitor adherence among healthcare professionals. Furthermore, routine follow‐up interviews with all parents approximately 6 months after the loss of their infant would provide timely information on the adherence to the guidelines and parental satisfaction with the shared decision‐making process. Such efforts are essential for improving clinical practice, strengthening the ethical basis of care, and providing better support to parents during one of the most challenging periods of their lives.
