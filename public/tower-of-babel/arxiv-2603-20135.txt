Classifier-Based Nonparametric Sequential Hypothesis Testing
Chia-Yu Hsu, Shubhanshu Shekhar

Abstract
We consider the problem of constructing sequential power-one tests where the null and alternative classes are specified indirectly through historical or offline data. More specifically, given an offline dataset consisting of observations from L + 1 L+1 distributions { P 0 , P 1 , … , P L } \{P_{0},P_{1},\ldots,P_{L}\} , and a new unlabeled data stream { X t : t ≥ 1 } ∼ i . i . d . P θ \{X_{t}:t\geq 1\}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle i.i.d.}}{{\sim}}P_{\theta} , the goal is to decide between the null H 0 : θ = 0 H_{0}:\theta=0 , against the alternative H 1 : θ ∈ [ L ] := { 1 , … , L } H_{1}:\theta\in[L]:=\{1,\ldots,L\} . Our main methodological contribution is a general approach for designing a level- α \alpha power-one test for this problem using a multi-class classifier trained on the given offline dataset. Working under a mild “separability” condition on the distributions and the trained classifier, we obtain an upper bound on the expected stopping time of our proposed level- α \alpha test, and then show that in general this cannot be improved. In addition to rejecting the null, we show that our procedure can also identify the true underlying distribution almost surely. We then establish a sufficient condition to ensure the required separability of the classifier, and provide some converse results to investigate the role of the size of the offline dataset and the family of classifiers among classifier-based tests that satisfy the level- α \alpha power-one criterion. Finally, we present an extension of our analysis for the training-and-testing distribution mismatch and illustrate an application to sequential change detection. Empirical results using both synthetic and real data provide support for our theoretical results.

Introduction

The field of sequential hypothesis testing, initiated by
[
34
]
, involves designing statistical procedures for determining whether the null or the alternative hypothesis is true through sequentially querying a sequence of testing data. An important advantage of such procedures is that they can adaptively determine the number of queried samples needed to achieve a desired testing accuracy, thereby using sampling resources more efficiently than fixed-length tests. Some prior work in this area under the assumption that the null is precisely characterized, either by making strong parametric assumptions on the distributions or through some structural properties (e.g., independence, symmetry, moment constraints, etc.).
In this paper, we consider testing problems in which the null and alternatives are specified indirectly through
offline or historical datasets

(L+1)

\{P_{0},\ldots,P_{L}\}

We assume that under the null hypothesis, the test data are generated from
P_{0}

P_{\theta}
, where
\theta\in[L]:=\{1,\ldots,L\}
.
Our goal is to develop a general machinery for designing level-
\alpha
power-one tests
[
6
]
using multiclass classifiers trained on the offline data, and analyze their statistical properties.

This limited knowledge of the ground-truth distribution is motivated by several practical applications, where one cannot specify reliable probabilistic models for the null and alternative hypotheses, but historical data are available. For example, in LLM detection
[
3

4
]
, it is difficult to characterize the text-generation distributions of either humans or machines, but one typically has extensive offline datasets consisting of paragraphs generated by humans and by machines. In such settings, classical likelihood-based sequential tests developed following
[
34

Moreover, due to the nonparametric consideration, rather than studying strong notions of optimality, e.g., minimizing type-I and type-II errors subject to an expected stopping time constraint, which are typically feasible to be developed under parametric assumptions
[
31

, we focus on a weaker but broadly tractable statistical guarantee: the level-
\alpha
, power-one criterion, initiated by
[
6
]
. Under this criterion, one aims to design a
stopping rule
for a uniform termination error control under the null (the probability of stopping in finite time is constrained by a constant under the null), power-one under any alternative (the probability of finitely stopping is one under the alternative), and sometimes a (minimax/instance-dependent optimal) expected stopping time under the alternative.

To leverage offline data generated without making strong distributional assumptions, motivated by
[
26
]
, we propose a
classifier-based
sequential power-one test to bridge this gap. Utilizing a classifier in our design process is motivated the recent advances in applied machine learning (ML), leading to the existence of powerful ML models designed for complex data. For instance, Fast-DetectGPT
[
1
]
effectively identifies machine-generated text in LLM detection problems, while VGG16
[
21
]
and other CNN-based models demonstrate strong classification capabilities for image data. Therefore, to ensure the testing problem is in general well-posed given a classifier family
\mathcal{G}
, we restrict our attention to distribution tuples
\{P_{0},\ldots,P_{L}\}
belonging to a
distinguishable
set specified by
\mathcal{G}
. Specifically, we require the existence of a
separable
classifier
g\in\mathcal{G}
for the tuple: if a data sample (denote as
X
) is drawn from
P_{\theta}
, then the probability of
g(X)=\theta
is greater than that of
g(X)=m
for any
m\in\mathcal{L}\setminus\{\theta\}
for any
\theta\in\{0,\ldots,L\}
.

Our proposed stopping rule uses an e-process
[
24
, § 4.1]
constructed by passing the test stream through the classifier. We briefly introduce the construction as follows: We first collect the offline data generated from these distributions and train a multi-classifier
g\in\mathcal{G}
that predicts which hypothesis (label) a data sample belongs to.
Then, in the online testing phase, the decision maker sequentially queries one testing sample at each time
t
(denote as
X_{t}
), and it is fed into
g
to produce a one-shot predicted hypothesis (or label)
g(X_{t})\in\{0,\ldots,L\}
. We then construct a
test martingale
by turning the label sequence
\{g(X_{t})\}_{t\geq 1}
into an e-process via the
testing-by-betting
paradigm
[
27
]
. Specifically, at each time
t
, we compare whether the current prediction equals the null label versus a running estimate
\hat{j}_{t}
of the most frequent predicted label among the past predictions
\{g(X_{i})\}_{i=1}^{t-1}
. We use this comparison to define a multiplicative betting update.
The e-process stops and rejects the null when it exceeds an
\alpha
-dependent threshold. Intuitively, due to the separability provided by the distributions and the classifier, if the null hypothesis is true,
\{g(X_{t})\}_{t\geq 1}
concentrate on the null label so that
\hat{j}_{t}
tends to indicate the null, and the resulting e-process remains small; in contrast, if the alternative is true,
\{g(X_{t})\}_{t\geq 1}
concentrate on an alternative label so that
\hat{j}_{t}
tends to indicate the alternative, and the e-process grows quickly and eventually crosses the rejection threshold. With this intuition, we show that our proposed stopping rule satisfies the level-
\alpha
power-one criterion under a
fixed
distinguishable tuple by Ville’s inequality
[
33
]
and martingale concentration bounds. Then, we also provide a non-asymptotic upper bound (for each
\alpha\in(0,1)
) for the expected stopping time that, in general, is asymptotically optimal. Moreover, we demonstrate that the
\hat{j}_{t}
will eventually indicate the true index of the underlying distribution almost surely.

The above guarantees are established under the separability condition and ignore how training components (e.g., the size of the training data and the realization of the learning model) affect the resulting classifier-based sequential test. Therefore, we next study (i) how to obtain a classifier that satisfies the separability condition and (ii) how the learning model (training sample size and the family of classifiers) impacts the classifier-based sequential level-
\alpha
power-one test.

For (i), it is intuitive that if the size of the training samples is larger, then it is more likely to satisfy the separability condition. Specifically, we argue that for any
\{P_{0},\ldots,P_{L}\}
in the distinguishable set, there exists an empirical risk minimization (ERM) algorithm that guarantees that the ERM-resulting classifier is also separable for that distribution tuple, provided
\mathcal{G}
is PAC-learnable and the training sample size satisfies a sufficient lower bound.
Next, we address (ii) by establishing
converse
bounds for all
uniformly
achievable tests, i.e., tests satisfying the level-
\alpha
power-one criterion uniformly across the distinguishable set specified by
\mathcal{G}
. We first provide a lower bound on the training sample size, revealing that closer underlying distributions naturally demand a larger training sample size of training samples for the uniform achievability. Furthermore, we establish a minimax lower bound on the type-II error counterpart (specifically, the tail probability of the stopping time), which explicitly depends on the training size, the threshold
\alpha
, and the classifier family. This bound yields two key insights: increasing the training sample size reduces the lower bound (better learnability), yet even with infinite training data, the bound remains positive due to the inherent worst-case testing difficulty; and enlarging the classifier family can further reduce the lower bound.

We further extend our analysis to address practical challenges/applications. First, to model training–testing mismatch motivated by
[
22
]
, we study a distribution-shift setting where the data distribution during the online testing phase mismatches the data distribution for training the classifier applied for the testing phase. We show that the proposed stopping rule remains valid provided that the strength of the mismatch is controlled well, determined by the separability of the classifier for the training dataset.
Second, we use our sequential test as a building block to design a sequential change-point detection procedure and analyze its performance.

Finally, we provide empirical results with synthetic and real data to support our theoretical results. In both our basic case and extensions, we argue that our expected stopping time correctly describes the trend of the corresponding empirical behavior. Moreover, we demonstrate that if one can apply multiple-classifiers on making the decision, e.g., consider a mixture of some parallel e-processes, one can obtain a better expected stopping time. That indicates an interesting future work.

2
Problem Formulation

We begin this section by introducing statistical assumptions on the historical data and the test stream. After that we present the definition a general classifier-based sequential test, and conclude the section by describing the key performance measures.

For some integer
L\geq 1
, let
\mathbf{P}\coloneqq\{P_{0},P_{1},\ldots,P_{L}\}
denote a tuple of data-generating distributions on a general alphabet
\mathcal{X}
.
Let
\{X_{t}\}_{t\geq 1}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle i.i.d.}}{{\sim}}P_{\theta}
denote a stream of
\mathcal{X}
-valued observations, where
P_{\theta}
is an unknown distribution in
\mathbf{P}
.
We have no further information on
\mathbf{P}
, but only the historical or offline data drawn from this tuple:

\displaystyle T_{\vartheta}^{N}\coloneqq\{T_{\vartheta,1},\ldots,T_{\vartheta,N}\}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle i.i.d.}}{{\sim}}P_{\vartheta},\quad\text{for}\quad\vartheta\in\mathcal{L}\coloneqq\{0,1,\ldots,L\}.

(2)

Here, for simplicity, we have assumed that we have an equal number of offline data samples (i.e.,
N
) from each distribution in
\mathbf{P}
. Furthermore, we also assume that there is an exact match between the offline and the test distributions; that is, the unknown test distribution
P_{\theta}
is exactly one of the
(L+1)
elements of
\mathbf{P}
from which the offline data are drawn. As we discuss in Section
4
, this is not strictly necessary, and our proposed test can still work under certain types of distribution shifts.

Then, our goal is to decide between the null
H_{0}:\theta=0
, against the alternative
H_{1}:\theta\in[L]
. We consider a sequential framework where the decision-maker continuously queries a testing sample at each time slot and dynamically stops the sampling process once sufficient evidence is accumulated. The random time at which sampling terminates is defined as the
stopping time
, denoted by
\tau\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{+\infty\}
. Specifically, the decision to stop is determined by the information available up to that moment, including both the offline dataset and the online testing samples received so far. In words, we denote the natural filtration
\mathcal{F}_{t}
as follows:

\forall\,t\geq 1\quad\mathcal{F}_{t}\coloneqq\sigma\!\Bigl(\{T^{N}_{\vartheta}\}_{\vartheta\in\mathcal{L}},\,\{X_{s}\}_{s\leq t}\Bigr)\quad\text{and}\quad\mathcal{F}_{0}\coloneqq\sigma(\{T^{N}_{\vartheta}\}_{\vartheta\in\mathcal{L}}),

(3)

and the stopping time
\tau
is adapted to
\{\mathcal{F}_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
.

Formally, our goal is to construct stopping times satisfying the
level-
\alpha
power-one criterion
, which mandates that under a given ground-truth tuple
\mathbf{P}
, the probability of stopping in finite time under the null must be less than or equal to
\alpha\in(0,1)
, whereas finite-time stopping under the alternative must occur with probability one. In words, we aim to develop a stopping rule such that the resulting stopping time
\tau
satisfies
\mathbb{P}_{\mathbf{P},0}(\tau<\infty)\leq\alpha
and
\mathbb{P}_{\mathbf{P},\theta}(\tau<\infty)=1
for any
\theta\in[L]
,
where
\mathbb{P}_{\mathbf{P},\theta}(\cdot)
denotes the probability measure defined on the filtration
\mathcal{F}_{\infty}\coloneqq\sigma\left(\cup_{t\geq 0}\mathcal{F}_{t}\right)
when the testing samples are
X_{t}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle i.i.d.}}{{\sim}}P_{\theta}
(the
\theta
-th distribution in
\mathbf{P}
) for all
t\geq 1
, and the offline training samples are
T^{N}_{\vartheta}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle i.i.d.}}{{\sim}}P_{\vartheta}
for all
\vartheta\in\mathcal{L}
. Moreover, we denote
\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{P},\theta}[\cdot]
as the expectation under the probability measure
\mathbb{P}_{\mathbf{P},\theta}
for each
\theta\in[L]
.

In this paper, we are mainly interested in cases where the alphabet
\mathcal{X}
may be complicated (e.g., images, text, etc), and thus accurate empirical distribution estimation may be computationally prohibitive. Motivated by prior works such as
[
26
,
16
]
, we bypass this challenge by introducing a
multi-class classifier
, denoted by
g
trained on the offline dataset. This classifier serves as a dimensionality reduction mechanism, mapping the intricate high-dimensional space
\mathcal{X}
into a significantly more tractable, discrete domain. Specifically, the goal of the classifier in this paper is to learn to distinguish samples from
\mathbf{P}=\{P_{0},\ldots,P_{L}\}
based on the offline/historical datasets. We define the learning policy as a function
\phi
which maps a set of data label pairs
\{T^{N}_{\vartheta},\vartheta\}_{\vartheta\in\mathcal{L}}
to a classifier
g
belonging to a family of classifiers
\mathcal{G}
, i.e.,
\phi(\{T^{N}_{\vartheta},\vartheta\}_{\vartheta\in\mathcal{L}})=g
for a
g\in\mathcal{G}
. We assume that
\mathcal{G}
is a hypothesis class of multi-class classifiers mapping from
\mathcal{X}
to
\mathcal{L}
(a relatively simple set), i.e.,
g(x)\in\mathcal{L}
for any
x\in\mathcal{X}
and
g\in\mathcal{G}
, and it has a bounded complexity (details in Section
3.2
). Moreover, we consider a fixed
\mathcal{G}
throughout this paper.

We now introduce the notion of a
distinguishable
distribution family (denoted as
\mathcal{P}_{\text{sep}}
) specified by
\mathcal{G}
, to which
\mathbf{P}
belongs.
Specifically, we consider distributions that are inherently distinguishable by the given classifier family
\mathcal{G}
. Formally,

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{\text{sep}}:=\left\{\mathbf{P}:\exists\,g\in\mathcal{G}\quad\text{such that}\quad\forall\,\theta\in\mathcal{L},\quad\mathbb{P}_{X\sim P_{\theta}}(g(X)=\theta)>\max_{m\neq\theta}\mathbb{P}_{X\sim P_{\theta}}(g(X)=m)\right\}.

(4)

If a tuple
\mathbf{P}\in\mathcal{P}_{\text{sep}}
, we say
\mathbf{P}
and the corresponding
g\equiv g(\mathbf{P})
satisfy the
separability condition
or the classifier
g
is a
separable classifier
for the
\mathbf{P}
.

With this background, we can now introduce the class of sequential testing procedures that will be studied in this paper.
First, the learning policy
\phi
outputs a classifier
g\in\mathcal{G}
trained on the offline training data
\{T^{N}_{\vartheta}\}_{\vartheta\in\mathcal{L}}
. Then, under this classifier, at each time slot
t
, the decision-maker observes a sample
X_{t}
generated
i.i.d.
from an unknown
P_{\theta}
where
\theta\in\mathcal{L}
and inputs them to
g
and gets
g(X_{t})\in\mathcal{L}
. After obtaining the classifier’s output, the decision-maker either stops and declares
\tau=t
, in which case it rejects the null hypothesis, or continues sampling otherwise. Then, we denote
\pi\coloneqq(\phi,\tau)
as an “overall” classifier-based sequential hypothesis test.

Remark 2.1

(Permutation)
.

Note that the class
\mathcal{P}_{\text{sep}}
is invariant under permutation of the labels; that is, if
\mathbf{P}=\{P_{0},\ldots,P_{L}\}\in\mathcal{P}_{\text{sep}}
, then any tuple
\{P_{\sigma(0)},P_{\sigma(1)},\ldots,P_{\sigma(L)}\}
also belongs to
\mathcal{P}_{\text{sep}}
, where
\sigma
is any permutation of
\{0,\ldots,L\}
. Moreover, their corresponding separable classifiers are the same.

Main Results

In Section
3.1
, we first propose a general stopping rule based on an e-process, constructed using any separable classifier
g
for a fixed ground-truth distribution tuple
\mathbf{P}\in\mathcal{P}_{\text{sep}}
(note that this tuple is unknown). This general scheme satisfies the level-
\alpha
power-one property under
\mathbf{P}
and

; that is,
\mathbb{P}_{\mathbf{P},0}(\tau<\infty\mid\mathcal{F}_{0})\leq\alpha
and
\mathbb{P}_{\mathbf{P},\theta}(\tau<\infty\mid\mathcal{F}_{0})=1
. By conditioning on the initial filtration
\mathcal{F}_{0}
, we treat the classifier
g
as fixed, thereby decoupling the design of the stopping rule from the offline training process. Furthermore, we establish an almost sure identification result: as the testing process proceeds, the proposed test identifies the ground-truth index
\theta\in\mathcal{L}
almost surely. Then, in Section
3.2
, we investigate the role of the offline training process. Specifically, we study the impact of the training sample size
N
and the classifier family
\mathcal{G}
, demonstrating some necessary and sufficient conditions for the size
N
and how

and
\mathcal{G}
affect the stopping time.

3.1
The proposed stopping rule and analysis

Since we fix a tuple
\mathbf{P}\in\mathcal{P}_{\text{sep}}
and assume a corresponding separable classifier
g
is given, to simplify the notation and reflect that we have isolated the sequential testing phase from the randomness of the offline training process, we drop
\mathbf{P}
, and the conditioning on
\mathcal{F}_{0}
from the probability measures and expectations. Specifically, we denote
\mathbb{P}_{\theta}(\tau<\infty)\equiv\mathbb{P}_{\mathbf{P},\theta}(\tau<\infty|\mathcal{F}_{0})
and
\mathbb{E}_{\theta}[\tau]\equiv\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{P},\theta}[\tau|\mathcal{F}_{0}]
for any
\theta\in\mathcal{L}
. The dependency of the training phase will be studied in Section
3.2
. Moreover, in this section, for notational simplicity, we define
\boldsymbol{p}_{\theta}[m]\coloneqq\mathbb{P}_{X\sim P_{\theta}}\left(g(X)=m\right)
and
\boldsymbol{p}_{\theta}\coloneqq[\boldsymbol{p}_{\theta}[0],\boldsymbol{p}_{\theta}[1],\ldots,\boldsymbol{p}_{\theta}[L]]
, for all
m,\theta\in\mathcal{L}
.

3.1.1
Proposed stopping rule

The design of our proposed stopping rule is built upon the principle of testing-by-betting
[
27
]
. This says that the amount of statistical evidence against a null is precisely quantified by the gain in wealth made by a fictitious bettor playing a repeated betting game with fair (or sub-fair) odds under the null. Formally, this involves constructing an
e-process
, defined in
[
25
, Definition 7.3]
, such that under the null, the wealth remains low at any time; however, under the alternative, we hope that the amount of the wealth grows (exponentially) fast against the null. Then, the stopping time
\tau\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{+\infty\}
is defined as the first
1/\alpha
-crossing of the betting-wealth process. In fact, if the betting-wealth process is a non-negative martingale (or supermartingale) under the null, then Ville’s inequality immediately guarantees that the probability of stopping in finite time is upper-bounded by
\alpha
, thereby achieving the level-
\alpha
property.

In words, we construct a betting-wealth process
\{W_{n}:n\geq 0\}
adapted to the natural filtration that accumulates the evidence against the null. Hence, the stopping time (or stopping rule) is as follows:

\tau=\inf\{n\geq 1:W_{n}\geq 1/\alpha\}.

(5)

Then, we turn to the construction of the e-process
\{W_{n}\}_{n\geq 0}
satisfying the properties mentioned earlier.

In practice, constructing an e-process typically involves taking the product of sequential
e
-values, modulated by a predictable betting parameter (denoted as
\lambda
)
[
25
, Chapter 7]
. This formulation ensures the process forms a non-negative supermartingale under the null hypothesis while achieving rapid growth under the alternative. Specifically, to address the nonparametric nature of our underlying data distribution, we process the raw sequence
\{X_{t}\}_{t\geq 1}
using a classifier
g
, projecting the complex observations onto a tractable, discrete label space. We then utilize the resulting sequence of predictions
\{g(X_{t})\}_{t\geq 1}
to build our e-process.

The key idea of our construction is that, based on the separability condition of the classifier, if the testing data
X
is generated by
P_{\vartheta}
, the probability of
g(X)=\vartheta
is the largest; we can use this “edge” to design betting games accordingly.
Then, the e-process
\{W_{n}\}_{n\geq 0}
is defined as follows:
\forall\,n\geq 0
,

W_{0}=1,\quad\text{and}\quad W_{n}=\int_{-1}^{0}\left(\prod_{t=1}^{n}\left(1+\lambda\times\left(\boldsymbol{1}_{g(X_{t})=0}-\boldsymbol{1}_{g(X_{t})=\hat{j}_{t}}\right)\right)\right)d\lambda,

(6)

where
\hat{j}_{t}
for every
t\geq 1
is defined as the index with the largest empirical probability:

\displaystyle\hat{j}_{t}=\argmax_{\theta\in\mathcal{L}}\;\hat{\boldsymbol{p}}_{t-1}[\theta]

(7)

and
\hat{\boldsymbol{p}}_{t}
denotes the empirical estimate of the classifier’s probability vector, i.e., for each
t\geq 1
,
\hat{\boldsymbol{p}}_{t}:=[\hat{\boldsymbol{p}}_{t}[0],\hat{\boldsymbol{p}}_{t}[1],\ldots,\hat{\boldsymbol{p}}_{t}[L]]
, where
\hat{\boldsymbol{p}}_{t}[\theta]:=(1/t)\sum_{s=1}^{t}\mathbf{1}_{g(X_{s})=\theta}
for each
\theta\in\mathcal{L}
, with the initialization
\hat{\boldsymbol{p}}_{0}=[1/(L+1),\ldots,1/(L+1)]
, and
\mathbf{1}_{(\cdot)}
is the indicator function.
We assume that ties in the selection of
\hat{j}_{t}
are broken in a deterministic manner (such as selecting the smallest index in the case of ties).

By the separability condition of the classifier, the process
\{1+\lambda\left(\boldsymbol{1}_{g(X_{t})=0}-\boldsymbol{1}_{g(X_{t})=\hat{j}_{t}}\right)\}_{t\geq 1}
forms a sequence of valid e-values under the null, since
\mathbb{E}_{0}\left[1+\lambda\left(\boldsymbol{1}_{g(X_{t})=0}-\boldsymbol{1}_{g(X_{t})=j}\right)\right]\leq 1
for all
\lambda\in(-1,0]
,
j\in\mathcal{L}
, and
t\geq 1
. Therefore, initialized at
W_{0}=1
, the wealth process
\{W_{n}\}_{n\geq 0}
is inherently a non-negative supermartingale (and thus an e-process) under the null, immediately establishing the required level-
\alpha
error control verified by Ville’s inequality.
When the alternative hypothesis is true, at a high-level, the sequence of indices
\{\hat{j}_{t}\}_{t\geq 1}
effectively tracks the most probable class (it holds true under either the null or the alternative), allowing us to accurately identify that
j=\theta
if
P_{\theta}
,
\theta\in[L]
, is the ground-truth distribution. Then, again by the separability condition of the classifier, we have
\mathbb{E}_{\theta}\left[1+\lambda\left(\boldsymbol{1}_{g(X)=0}-\boldsymbol{1}_{g(X)=j}\right)\right]>1
for any
\lambda\in(-1,0]
. Therefore, the e-process
\{W_{n}\}_{n\geq 0}
grows as
n
goes large. These are exactly the properties expected of a good e-process for this testing problem. Finally, instead of selecting a fixed betting parameter
\lambda\in(-1,0]
, we take the mixture approach, “averaging” over all
\lambda\in(-1,0]
. This design choice is a technical necessity for bounding the expected stopping time, allowing us to leverage regret bounds from online convex optimization
[
12
]
to establish our theoretical guarantees.

To end this section, we provide the following remark on implementing the integration for our proposed e-process.

Remark 3.1

(Practical implementation of our e-process)
.

In practice, the integral over
\lambda\in(-1,0]
in the definition of
W_{n}
can be approximated by a simple discretization on a sufficiently fine grid, which leads to an efficient
iterative
implementation. With
\{\lambda_{i}\}_{i=1}^{K}\subset(-1,0]
denoting a grid of
K
points, define

M_{n,i}\;\coloneqq\;\prod_{t=1}^{n}\Bigl(1+\lambda_{i}\bigl(\boldsymbol{1}_{g(X_{t})=0}-\boldsymbol{1}_{g(X_{t})=\hat{j}_{t}}\bigr)\Bigr),\qquad M_{0,i}\coloneqq 1,\quad\text{for each }i\in[K].

(8)

Since the supermartingale property is preserved under averaging, we can then define the Monte-Carlo process
\bar{W}_{n}\;\coloneqq\;\frac{1}{K}\sum_{i=1}^{K}M_{n,i}
.
This process can be implemented via a recursive update rule, where upon observing
X_{n}
in round
n\geq 1
, we compute
\hat{j}_{n}
and then update each component
M_{n,i}
to obtain
\bar{W}_{n}
as follows:

\displaystyle M_{n,i}=M_{n-1,i}\times\Bigl(1+\lambda_{i}\bigl(\boldsymbol{1}_{g(X_{n})=0}-\boldsymbol{1}_{g(X_{n})=\hat{j}_{n}}\bigr)\Bigr),\qquad\forall\,i\in[K],\qquad\bar{W}_{n}=\frac{1}{K}\sum_{i=1}^{K}M_{n,i}.

(9)

This process can then be used to define the stopping time
\bar{\tau}\;\coloneqq\;\inf\{n\geq 1:\bar{W}_{n}\geq 1/\alpha\}
, which in an approximation of the stopping time
\tau
constructed using the process in (
6
).
The time complexity of each iteration is
O(K)
.

3.1.2
Theoretical analysis of the proposed test

In this section, we formally describe the theoretical results of our proposed stopping rule. Our first result of this section, Theorem
3.2
, establishes that the proposed stopping rule
\tau
defined in Section
3.1.1
satisfies the level-
\alpha
power-one criterion under a given separable classifier
g
for a given
\mathbf{P}\in\mathcal{P}_{\text{sep}}
. Moreover, an upper bound of the expected stopping time is also provided. Our next result considers the question of identification of the true alternative index, and in Proposition
3.3
, we show that
\hat{j}_{t}
equals the true index with probability one after a (random) finite time.

Theorem 3.2
.

Our proposed stopping rule
\tau
satisfies the following: under a given separable classifier
g\in\mathcal{G}
for a tuple
\mathbf{P}\in\mathcal{P}_{\text{sep}}
,

\displaystyle\text{Under }H_{0}:\;\mathbb{P}_{0}\left(\tau<\infty\right)\leq\alpha

(10)

\displaystyle\text{Under }H_{1}:\;\forall\,\theta\in[L],\;\mathbb{P}_{\theta}\left(\tau<\infty\right)=1\quad\text{and}\quad\mathbb{E}_{\theta}\left[\tau\right]=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\log(1/(\alpha\Delta_{\theta}))}{\Delta_{\theta}^{2}}\right),

(11)

where
\Delta_{\theta}:=\max_{m\in\mathcal{L}}\boldsymbol{p}_{\theta}[m]-\boldsymbol{p}_{\theta}[0]=\boldsymbol{p}_{\theta}[\theta]-\boldsymbol{p}_{\theta}[0]
.

The proof of Theorem
3.2
is in Appendix
B
. The level-
\alpha
property follows directly from Ville’s inequality, leveraging the fact that
\{W_{n}\}_{n\geq 0}
is a non-negative supermartingale. Regarding the expected stopping time, while we derive a non-asymptotic upper bound valid for all
\alpha\in(0,1)
, the theorem statement presents only the dominant first-order term.
The lower-order terms, which consist of problem-dependent constants related to
\Delta_{\theta}
but are independent of
\alpha
, are deferred to the detailed proof.

In addition to the classic tradeoff between the
\alpha
value and the expected stopping time, Theorem
3.2
also reveals a fundamental dependence on the classifier’s ability to separate the null from the alternative labels. This notion of separability is rigorously quantified by the gap in the classifier-induced probabilities
\Delta_{\theta}
. Consequently, a classifier with higher discriminative power naturally yields a shorter expected stopping time.
Motivated by this dependence, a natural research direction is leveraging heterogeneous classifiers to improve the expected stopping time. In
Section

5.4
, we show empirically that combining multiple classifiers, e.g., by constructing a mixture of parallel e-processes, can reduce the expected stopping time when compared to test based on a single classifier. This provides an interesting future direction for the design and analysis of aggregation methods that exploit classifiers’ heterogeneity.

Finally, the proposed test not only satisfies the level-
\alpha
, power-one criterion, but also enjoys an identification guarantee that says that for any
\theta\in\mathcal{L}
,
\lim_{t\to\infty}\hat{j}_{t}=\theta
almost surely. In other words, as the number of testing samples grows, the procedure identifies the ground-truth index with probability one.

Proposition 3.3
.

Under a given separable classifier
g
for a given
\mathbf{P}\in\mathcal{P}_{\text{sep}}
, for any
t\geq 1
,

\mathbb{P}_{\theta}\left(\hat{j}_{t}\neq\theta\right)\in O(e^{-t}),

(12)

implying
\mathbb{P}_{\theta}\left(\{\hat{j}_{t}\neq\theta\}\;i.o.\right)=0
and
\mathbb{P}_{\theta}\left(\exists T<\infty\,s.t.\,\forall\,t\geq T,\,\hat{j}_{t}=\theta\right)=1
.

This statement, proved in Appendix
C
using Hoeffding’s inequality and the Borel-Cantelli Lemma, then immediately implies the following corollary.

Corollary 3.4
.

For any
\theta\neq 0
, we have
\lim_{\alpha\rightarrow 0}\mathbb{P}_{\theta}(\hat{j}_{\tau}=\theta)=1
.

The proof is also provided in Appendix
C
.
Intuitively, as
\alpha\to 0
(equivalently, as the stopping threshold
1/\alpha\rightarrow\infty
), the test stops later and the probability of correctly identifying the ground-truth index at stopping approaches one by Proposition
3.3
.

Remark 3.5

(Asymptotic optimality)
.

Generally, showing that our derived expected stopping time is asymptotically optimal, for a fixed classifier
g
and tuple
\mathbf{P}
as
\alpha\to 0
, remains an open question. However, there exists a problem instance such that the expected stopping time bounded in Theorem
3.2
is asymptotically optimal. We defer the formal demonstration of this result to Section
4.2
, where we establish this optimality as a consequence of our framework’s application to sequential change-point detection.

Remark 3.6

(Training-testing mismatch)
.

The statement of Theorem
3.2
relies only on the separability condition, and thus its conclusions remain valid under some types of mismatch between training and testing distributions. Specifically, suppose the testing sample under label
\theta
is generated from a mismatched distribution
\tilde{P}_{\theta}
instead of
P_{\theta}
for all
\theta\in\mathcal{L}
. If
\tilde{\mathbf{P}}:=\{\tilde{P}_{0},\ldots,\tilde{P}_{L}\}\in\mathcal{P}_{\text{sep}}
with a classifier
g
trained by the exact ground-truth tuple
\mathbf{P}
(in words,
g
is also a separable classifier for
\tilde{\mathbf{P}}
), then Theorem
3.2
also continues to hold with an appropriately modified gap parameter. In particular, the quantity
\Delta_{\theta}
in the theorem is replaced by
\tilde{\Delta}_{\theta}\coloneqq\tilde{\boldsymbol{p}}_{\theta}[\theta]-\tilde{\boldsymbol{p}}_{\theta}[0]
for all
\theta\in\mathcal{L}
, where
\tilde{\boldsymbol{p}}_{\theta}[m]:=\mathbb{P}_{X\sim\tilde{P}_{\theta}}(g(X)=m)
. The intuition is that in our analysis, we only consider the randomness of the online testing sequences
\{g(X_{t})\}_{t\geq 1}
where
g
is given.
We illustrate this generalization in detail in Section
4.1
.

Remark 3.7

(Application to the sequential change-point detection problem)
.

Our proposed stopping rule can also be extended to the
sequential change-point detection problem
.
Specifically, we regard
P_{0}
as the
pre-change
distribution and
P_{\theta}
, for
\theta\in[L]
, as the
post-change
distribution.
To accommodate an unknown change time, we make a minor modification to our proposed test by recording a set of candidate e-processes, each initialized at a different time index, and monitoring their evidence over time.
The details are provided in Section
4.2
.

3.2
Role of the offline training phase

In the previous section, we fixed a classifier and studied the proposed stopping rule constructed via the e-process
\{W_{n}\}_{n\geq 0}
. By doing so, we ignore the training phase based on the offline dataset, i.e., decoupling the stopping rule from the training sample size
N
and the classifier family
\mathcal{G}
(throughout this paper, we only focus on these two variables in the training phase). However, it remains unclear how large
N
is needed (or must be) to guarantee achievability, and how the interplay between
N
,
\mathcal{G}
, and the performance of the overall test. Consequently, two fundamental questions remain: (i) For any
\mathbf{P}\in\mathcal{P}_{\text{sep}}
, is it theoretically possible to empirically obtain a corresponding separable classifier, and what is the minimum required training sample size
N
for a classifier-based level-
\alpha
power-one test? (ii) How do
N
and
\mathcal{G}
impact the stopping time? We study Question (i) in Section
3.2.1
and Question (ii) in Section
3.2.2
.

3.2.1
Empirical attainability and training sample requirements

In this section, we first establish a sufficient condition on the training sample size
N
to guarantee that a separable classifier can be empirically obtained via an ERM argument. Subsequently, we derive a necessary condition on
N
for any overall test
\pi
achieving the level-
\alpha
power-one criterion under a given
\mathbf{P}\in\mathcal{P}_{\text{sep}}
. Together, these two results delineate the fundamental limits of
N
, moving beyond the trivial intuition that “more data is always better” to mathematically quantify the strict data requirements for achievability. We make a remark for our above argument.

Remark 3.8
.

We focus on deriving the lower bound of
N
, and leave obtaining tight upper bound on the training sample size
N
to future work, as such bounds will depend heavily on the specifics of the learning policy
\phi
, which is not the focus of this paper. Moreover, our ERM analysis serves mainly as a theoretical proof of concept to establish the
existence
of a valid empirical method. In practice, as demonstrated in our experiments (Section
5
), a separable classifier can be readily obtained using standard training procedures for Feedforward Neural Networks (FNNs) and Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs).

We now proceed to the derivation of a sufficient condition on
N
for obtaining a separable classifier under any given
\mathbf{P}\in\mathcal{P}_{\text{sep}}
through an ERM method.

Empirical attainability

Recall that under any
\mathbf{P}\in\mathcal{P}_{\text{sep}}
, we are given offline training data
\{T_{\vartheta}^{N}\}_{\vartheta\in\mathcal{L}}
, where for each
\vartheta\in\mathcal{L}
,
T_{\vartheta}^{N}\coloneqq(T_{\vartheta,1},\ldots,T_{\vartheta,N})\sim P_{\theta}^{\otimes N}
.
For any classifier
g\in\mathcal{G}
and any
\vartheta,m\in\mathcal{L}
, define the empirical and confusion probabilities

\hat{p}_{\vartheta,m}(g)\coloneqq\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbbm{1}\{g(T_{\vartheta,i})=m\},\qquad p_{\vartheta,m}(g)\coloneqq\mathbb{P}_{X\sim P_{\vartheta}}\bigl(g(X)=m\bigr).

(13)

Note that
p_{\vartheta,m}(g)
coincides with
\boldsymbol{p}_{\vartheta}[m]
used in Section
3.1
, but we keep the dependence on
g
explicit here for clearly constructing the ERM-argument.
Define the empirical gap
\hat{\Delta}(g)
for any
g\in\mathcal{G}
and a data-driven classifier
\hat{g}
:

\hat{\Delta}(g)\coloneqq\min_{\vartheta\in\mathcal{L}}\min_{m\in\mathcal{L}\setminus\{\vartheta\}}\Bigl(\hat{p}_{\vartheta,\vartheta}(g)-\hat{p}_{\vartheta,m}(g)\Bigr),\qquad\hat{g}\in\argmax_{g\in\mathcal{G}}\hat{\Delta}(g).

(14)

With this background, we now state the requirements on
N
to ensure that
\hat{g}
is separable.

Proposition 3.9

(Empirical attainability (
Informal
))
.

Suppose that for any
\mathbf{P}\in\mathcal{P}_{\text{sep}}
, there exists
g^{*}\in\mathcal{G}
such that
\Delta^{*}\coloneqq\min_{\vartheta\in\mathcal{L}}\min_{m\in\mathcal{L}\setminus\{\vartheta\}}\Bigl(p_{\vartheta,\vartheta}(g^{*})-p_{\vartheta,m}(g^{*})\Bigr)>0
by the definition of
\mathcal{P}_{\text{sep}}
.
Let
\gamma\coloneqq\Delta^{*}/8
, and for any
\delta\in(0,1)
,
suppose that
N\gtrsim\frac{1}{\gamma^{2}}\log\left(\frac{L}{\delta}\right)
,
where
d
is a positive constant related to the VC-dimension of the classifier family
\mathcal{G}
and is independent of
\delta
,
L
,
\gamma
, and
N
.
Then, with probability at least
1-\delta
, the ERM solution
\hat{g}
satisfies
p_{\vartheta,\vartheta}(\hat{g})>\max_{m\in\mathcal{L}\setminus\{\vartheta\}}p_{\vartheta,m}(\hat{g})
for any
\vartheta\in\mathcal{L}
.

The proof idea is built on VC theorem
[
35
, Theorem 22.18]
, and the details of the argument are in Appendix
D
. We note that this lower bound fundamentally depends on the underlying separability margin,
\Delta^{*}
, achieved by the (optimal) classifier
g^{*}\in\mathcal{G}
. Specifically, a smaller
\Delta^{*}
indicates that the ground-truth distributions are more difficult for
g^{*}
to classify correctly. This formalizes the intuition that if the distributions within
\mathbf{P}\in\mathcal{P}_{\text{sep}}
are inherently “closer” or “less distinguishable”, more offline training data is required to correctly separate them.

After introducing the sufficient condition of
N
to achieve the empirical attainability, we now investigate a necessary condition on the training sample size: the minimum required number of training samples for all classifier-based sequential tests achieving the level-
\alpha
power-one property.

Necessary training sample requirement

We start with the observation that if a classifier-based sequential test
\pi=(\phi,\tau)
satisfies the level-
\alpha
power-one criterion for a given tuple
\mathbf{P}\in\mathcal{P}_{\text{sep}}
, it inherently satisfies this criterion for any permutation of
\mathbf{P}
, as defined in Remark
2.1
. In other words, merely relabeling the hypothesis and the distribution pairs does not compromise the test’s validity. We regard it as a natural assumption; by the definition of
\mathcal{P}_{\text{sep}}
, both the original and permuted distribution tuples share the same separable classifier
g\in\mathcal{G}
.
Building upon this assumption, we can obtain the following result.

Proposition 3.10

(Lower bound of
N
)
.

For any
\mathbf{P}\in\mathcal{P}_{\text{sep}}
, suppose a policy
\pi=(\phi,\tau)
that achieves level-
\alpha
power-one criterion under
\mathbf{P}
and satisfies the assumption mentioned above, then the number of training samples for
\pi
must satisfy

N\geq\frac{\log(1/\alpha)}{\min_{\theta\neq m}\mathrm{J}(P_{m},P_{\theta})},\quad\text{where}\quad\mathrm{J}(P,Q):=D_{\text{KL}}(P\|Q)+D_{\text{KL}}(Q\|P).

(15)

The proof of Proposition
3.10
is provided in Appendix
E
, and it relies mainly on an application of the data processing inequality
[
23
, Chapter 3]
for relative entropy.
This proposition shows that when two ground-truth distributions are nearly indistinguishable, more offline samples are needed to learn a classifier that reliably separates all classes. We take the minimum over all
P_{m}
and
P_{\theta}
since we assume that the test
\pi
satisfies the level-
\alpha
power-one criterion for all permutation tuples. Hence, the ground-truth distributions of the hypotheses may change (relabeling) while the test’s validity is maintained.

3.2.2
Minimax lower bound

To directly answer how the training sample size
N
and the classifier family
\mathcal{G}
impact the stopping time, we now establish a minimax lower bound on an appropriate notion of “type-II error”.
However, by shifting our focus to the minimax lower bound on the type-II error, we leverage Le Cam’s two-point method
[
23
, Chapter 31]
, an information-theoretic technique for the minimax risk’s lower bound, to derive a bound that explicitly characterizes the joint impact of the training sample size and the classifier family.

To present our minimax lower bound, we need specify the test class and the distribution set over which the infimum and supremum are evaluated. For the distribution set, we consider
\mathcal{P}_{\text{sep}}
introduced in (
4
). For the test class, we define the
uniformly achievable
test class, denoted by
\Pi_{\alpha}(\mathcal{P}_{\text{sep}})
, as all tests
\pi
that satisfy the level-
\alpha
and power-one criteria uniformly over the entire family
\mathcal{P}_{\text{sep}}
. That is,

\displaystyle\Pi_{\alpha}(\mathcal{P}_{\text{sep}})\coloneqq\left\{\pi:\sup_{\mathbf{P}\in\mathcal{P}_{\text{sep}}}\mathbb{P}^{\pi}_{\mathbf{P},0}(\tau<\infty)\leq\alpha\quad\text{and}\quad\inf_{\mathbf{P}\in\mathcal{P}_{\text{sep}}}\mathbb{P}^{\pi}_{\mathbf{P},\theta}(\tau<\infty)=1\ \ \forall\theta\in[L]\right\},

(16)

where
\mathbb{P}^{\pi}_{\mathbf{P},\theta}(\cdot)
denotes the probability measure defined in Section
2
, but we add
\pi
as the superscript to emphasize that the classifier-training (
\phi
) and the stopping rule (
\tau
) are decided by the overall policy
\pi=(\phi,\tau)
.
Moreover, we note that the stopping time inherently depends on the trained classifier, which is derived from training samples drawn from the underlying distribution
\mathbf{P}
. Consequently, evaluating the supremum and infimum in the set
\Pi_{\alpha}(\mathcal{P}_{\text{sep}})
implies that for any
\mathbf{P}
, the learning policy
\phi
trains a classifier
g\in\mathcal{G}
on data samples generated from that specific
\mathbf{P}
. This resulting classifier is then utilized to construct the stopping rule. We remark that if for any
\mathbf{P}\in\mathcal{P}_{\text{sep}}
, one can find the corresponding separable classifier (we theoretically show that it can be found with high probability in Section
3.2.1
), then combining it with our proposed stopping rule, the induced overall test is in
\Pi_{\alpha}(\mathcal{P}_{\text{sep}})
. With these two terms, we can define the minimax error as

\displaystyle\Psi\equiv\Psi(\Pi_{\alpha},\mathcal{P}_{\text{sep}},\mathcal{G})\coloneqq\inf_{\pi\in\Pi_{\alpha}(\mathcal{P}_{\text{sep}})}\sup_{\mathbf{P}\in\mathcal{\mathcal{P}_{\text{sep}}}}\mathbb{P}^{\pi}_{\mathbf{P},\theta}(\tau>n).

(17)

To obtain a lower bound on
\Psi
, we will use Le Cam’s two-point method, by working with two parameterized underlying tuples
\mathbf{P}_{\nu_{0}}:=\{P_{i,\nu_{0}}\}_{i\in\mathcal{L}}
and
\mathbf{P}_{\nu_{1}}:=\{P_{i,\nu_{1}}\}_{i\in\mathcal{L}}
, where
P_{i,\nu_{m}}
is parameterized by
\nu_{m}
for
m\in\{0,1\}
for each
i\in\mathcal{L}
by
\nu_{0}
and
\nu_{1}=\nu_{0}+\delta
(for some
\delta>0
), representing two closely related but distinct environments (we assume that
\mathbf{P}_{\nu_{0}}
and
\mathbf{P}_{\nu_{1}}
both are in
\mathcal{P}_{\text{sep}}
). Furthermore, we capture the capacity limitation of the chosen classifier family
\mathcal{G}
through the following structural assumption.

Assumption 3.11

(Classifier capacity limit (
Informal
))
.

No single classifier
g\in\mathcal{G}
can be simultaneously optimal (in terms of distinguishability) for both environments
\nu_{0}
and
\nu_{1}
. Specifically, the minimum aggregate suboptimality gap (we will define in Appendix
F
) of any
g\in\mathcal{G}
across both environments is strictly lower bounded by a constant
B>0
. The formal statement of this assumption is provided in Assumption
F.1
.

This constant
B
represents the restriction imposed by
\mathcal{G}
. A more restricted family
\mathcal{G}
typically yields a larger
B
, as it struggles to adapt to both environments simultaneously more than a richer family. With this conceptual setup, we present the minimax lower bound, which demonstrates the impact of
N
and the classifier family capacity
B
(representing the family
\mathcal{G}
).

Theorem 3.12

(Minimax lower bound)
.

Suppose
\nu_{0}
and
\nu_{1}
satisfy that
\nu_{1}=\nu_{0}+\delta
for some
\delta>0
and there exists a finite constant
M>0
such that
D(P_{i,\nu_{0}}\|P_{i,\nu_{1}})\leq M\delta^{2}
for any
i\in\mathcal{L}
.
Then, under Assumption
3.11
,

\displaystyle\log\Psi\coloneqq\log\inf_{\pi\in\Pi_{\alpha}(\mathcal{P}_{\text{sep}})}\sup_{\mathbf{P}\in\mathcal{\mathcal{P}_{\text{sep}}}}\mathbb{P}^{\pi}_{\mathbf{P},\theta}(\tau>n)\geq-\frac{1}{1-\alpha}\!\left(n\Big(\max_{\nu\in\{\nu_{0},\nu_{1}\}}D(P_{0,\nu}\|P_{\theta,\nu})-\frac{B}{4}e^{-NM(L+1)\delta^{2}}\Big)+\log 2\right)

(18)

for any alternative index
\theta\in\mathcal{L}\setminus\{0\}
.

The proof is deferred to Appendix
F
. We provide the following interpretation of some parameters in this lower bound:

1) Tradeoff between type-I and type-II error
: As
\alpha
increases, the level-
\alpha
constraint becomes looser and the sequential test is allowed to stop more easily. Therefore, the minimax lower bound decreases. Conversely, a smaller
\alpha
means a stricter level constraint, which increases the lower bound.

2) Problem instance matters:
The KL-divergence in the right-hand side reflects the intrinsic difficulty of distinguishing distributions of the worst-case environment over the two chosen ones.

3) The classifier family matters:
If the classifier
\mathcal{G}
is more restricted (
B
is larger), which in turn increases the minimax lower bound.

4) Training sample size matters:
When
N
is large, the offline data provide a more accurate representation of the underlying distributions, so the classifier can learn better and the lower bound decreases. Notably, even as
N\to\infty
, the bound does not vanish: the stopping time is still dominated by the intrinsic testing difficulty represented by the supremum term.

Remark 3.13

(Minimax lower bound for the testing-training mismatch setting)
.

In Section
4.1
, we study a setting where the testing distribution exhibits a shift from the training distribution. A corresponding minimax lower bound for this mismatched scenario can be readily established by following the proof of Theorem
3.12
. However, the bound is not clean and provides less insight. We leave it as future work.

4
Extensions

In Section
3.1
, we have introduced a general stopping rule that satisfies the level-
\alpha
power-one criterion and analyzed its expected stopping time. Subsequently, we investigated the fundamental roles of the training sample size and the classifier family. In this section, we extend our proposed stopping rule to accommodate one practical scenario: training-testing distribution mismatch, along with one concrete application: sequential change point detection. We note that analysis regarding the training sample size and the classifier family is omitted in these extensions, as such theoretical guarantees are either similar to our previous discussions or out of the scope of this paper. We will focus on the extensions themselves.

4.1
Training-testing mismatch

In many practical applications
[
22
]
, the data distributions between model training and online testing exhibit a
mismatch
. Specifically, in our framework, suppose the classifier is trained on offline samples
T_{\vartheta}^{N}\coloneqq\{T_{\vartheta,1},\ldots,T_{\vartheta,N}\}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle i.i.d.}}{{\sim}}P_{\vartheta}
for
\vartheta\in\mathcal{L}
, while the online testing samples are drawn i.i.d. from a testing tuple
\tilde{\mathbf{P}}=\{\tilde{P}_{0},\ldots,\tilde{P}_{L}\}
, where
\tilde{P}_{\theta}
may not be identical to
P_{\theta}
.

There are many ways to characterize the distribution mismatch, such as the total variation or the KL-divergence between the training and testing distributions. In this section, we first consider the KL-divergence between the classifier-induced probabilities under the training and testing distributions. Specifically, given a separable classifier
g
for the training distribution tuple
\mathbf{P}
, we define a set of
\epsilon
-mismatch distribution tuples as follows:

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{\epsilon}\equiv\mathcal{E}_{\epsilon}(g,\mathbf{P})\coloneqq\left\{\tilde{\mathbf{P}}=\{\tilde{P}_{0},\ldots,\tilde{P}_{L}\}\mid D(\boldsymbol{p}_{\theta}\|\tilde{\boldsymbol{p}}_{\theta})\leq\epsilon\quad\text{for all}\quad\theta\in\mathcal{L}\right\},

(19)

where the definition of each
\tilde{\boldsymbol{p}}_{\theta}
is similar to the
pmf

\boldsymbol{p}_{\theta}
defined in Section
3.1
, but the data sample is generated by
\tilde{P}_{\theta}
, i.e., for any
\theta\in\mathcal{L}
,
\tilde{\boldsymbol{p}}_{\theta}:=[\tilde{\boldsymbol{p}}_{\theta}[0],\ldots,\tilde{\boldsymbol{p}}_{\theta}[L]]
and
\tilde{\boldsymbol{p}}_{\theta}[i]=\mathbb{P}_{X\sim\tilde{P}_{\theta}}(g(X)=i)
for all
i\in\mathcal{L}
. Intuitively, if the mismatch strength
\epsilon
is sufficiently small such that the classifier
g
remains separable under the mismatched tuples, one can immediately utilize Theorem
3.2
. In this case, for
\tilde{\mathbf{P}}\in\mathcal{E}_{\epsilon}
, the level-
\alpha
power-one criterion is achieved, and the expected stopping time is bounded by
\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\log(1/(\alpha\tilde{\Delta}_{\theta}))}{\tilde{\Delta}_{\theta}^{2}}\right)
, where
\tilde{\Delta}_{\theta}\coloneqq\tilde{\boldsymbol{p}}_{\theta}[\theta]-\tilde{\boldsymbol{p}}_{\theta}[0]
for any
\theta\in[L]
. However, for larger values of
\epsilon
, the separability of the classifier
g
can be violated.

Therefore, we show that the largest
\epsilon
such that
g
remains a separable classifier for all
\tilde{\mathbf{P}}\in\mathcal{E}_{\epsilon}
is characterized by the strength of
g
’s separability (the “edge” mentioned in Section
3.1
) on the training tuple
\mathbf{P}
. In other words, when the classifier
g
has stronger separability on
\mathbf{P}
, the set of mismatched but separable tuples can be larger, which makes our proposed stopping time and analysis more robust. Specifically, we provide a sufficient condition for
\epsilon
such that given
\{\Delta_{\theta,m}\}_{\theta\in\mathcal{L},m\in\mathcal{L}\setminus\{\theta\}}
, where each
\Delta_{\theta,m}\coloneqq\boldsymbol{p}_{\theta}[\theta]-\boldsymbol{p}_{\theta}[m]
, the classifier trained on the exact
\mathbf{P}
is also separable for all mismatch tuples in the induced
\mathcal{E}_{\epsilon}
. We formally describe this result in Proposition
4.1
.

Proposition 4.1

(Robustness)
.

Under a fixed separable classifier
g
for the training tuple
\mathbf{P}
, if the mismatch strength
\epsilon<\min_{\theta\neq m\in\mathcal{L}}\frac{1}{2}\Delta_{\theta,m}^{2}
, then the classifier
g
is also separable for any
\tilde{\mathbf{P}}\in\mathcal{E}_{\epsilon}
, i.e., for any
\tilde{\mathbf{P}}\in\mathcal{E}_{\epsilon}
,
\tilde{\boldsymbol{p}}_{\theta}[\theta]>\max_{m\in\mathcal{L}\setminus\{\theta\}}\tilde{\boldsymbol{p}}_{\theta}[m]
, for all
\theta\in\mathcal{L}
. Moreover, with such condition for the
\epsilon
, Theorem
3.2
still holds with the the expected stopping time upper bounded by
\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\log(1/(\alpha\tilde{\Delta}_{\theta}))}{\tilde{\Delta}_{\theta}^{2}}\right)
and
0<\tilde{\Delta}_{\theta}\leq\Delta_{\theta}+\sqrt{2\epsilon}
, where
\tilde{\Delta}_{\theta}\coloneqq\tilde{\boldsymbol{p}}_{\theta}[\theta]-\tilde{\boldsymbol{p}}_{\theta}[0]
, for any
\theta\in[L]

Proof.

The proof of Proposition
4.1
is based on Pinsker’s inequality
[
23
, Theorem 7.10]
. In particular, consider any
\tilde{\mathbf{P}}\in\mathcal{E}_{\epsilon}
, and note that for any
\theta\in\mathcal{L}
, we have
D(\boldsymbol{p}_{\theta}\|\tilde{\boldsymbol{p}}_{\theta})\geq 2\text{TV}^{2}(\boldsymbol{p}_{\theta},\tilde{\boldsymbol{p}}_{\theta})
due to Pinsker’s inequality. Then, since
\boldsymbol{p}_{\theta}
and
\tilde{\boldsymbol{p}}_{\theta}
are discrete
pmf
, by the definition of the total variation distance and the triangle inequality,
\text{TV}(\boldsymbol{p}_{\theta},\tilde{\boldsymbol{p}}_{\theta})
is lower bounded by
\frac{1}{2}|\tilde{\Delta}_{\theta,m}-\Delta_{\theta,m}|
for any
m\in\mathcal{L}\setminus\{\theta\}
, where
\tilde{\Delta}_{\theta,m}\coloneqq\tilde{\boldsymbol{p}}_{\theta}[\theta]-\tilde{\boldsymbol{p}}_{\theta}[m]
. Therefore, by the definition of
\epsilon
-mismatch set
\mathcal{E}_{\epsilon}
, we can further get
|\tilde{\Delta}_{\theta,m}-\Delta_{\theta,m}|\leq\sqrt{2\epsilon}
for any
\theta\in\mathcal{L}
,
m\in\mathcal{L}\setminus\{\theta\}
. As a result, by
\Delta_{\theta,m}-\sqrt{2\epsilon}\leq\tilde{\Delta}_{\theta,m}\leq\Delta_{\theta,m}+\sqrt{2\epsilon}
, if we set
\epsilon<\frac{1}{2}(\min_{\theta\in\mathcal{L}}\min_{m\in\mathcal{L}\setminus\{\theta\}}\Delta_{\theta,m})^{2}
, then the lower bound of
\tilde{\Delta}_{\theta,m}
,
\Delta_{\theta,m}-\sqrt{2\epsilon}
, is positive for any
\theta\in\mathcal{L}
and
m\in\mathcal{L}\setminus\{\theta\}
. Therefore, the classifier
g
is also separable for any
\tilde{\mathbf{P}}\in\mathcal{E}_{\epsilon}
and
\tilde{\Delta}_{\theta}\equiv\tilde{\Delta}_{\theta}[0]>0
for any
\theta\in[L]
, and thus Theorem
3.2
applies.
∎

As the proof of Proposition
4.1
suggests, we can also define the mismatch using total variation; that is, the
\epsilon
-mismatch set is

\mathcal{E}^{\text{TV}}_{\epsilon}\equiv\mathcal{E}^{\text{TV}}_{\epsilon}(g,\mathbf{P})\coloneqq\left\{\tilde{\mathbf{P}}=\{\tilde{P}_{0},\ldots,\tilde{P}_{L}\}\mid\text{TV}(\boldsymbol{p}_{\theta},\tilde{\boldsymbol{p}}_{\theta})\leq\epsilon\quad\text{for all}\quad\theta\in\mathcal{L}\right\},

(20)

where
\text{TV}(P,Q)=\frac{1}{2}\lVert P-Q\rVert_{1}
if
P
and
Q
are probability mass functions.
With this modification, we can state a version of Proposition
4.1
, but with the condition on
\epsilon
replaced by
\epsilon<\frac{1}{2}\min_{\theta\in\mathcal{L}}\min_{m\in\mathcal{L}\setminus\{\theta\}}\Delta_{\theta,m}
, leading to
\tilde{\Delta}_{\theta}\in(0,\Delta_{\theta}+2\epsilon]
for any
\theta\in\mathcal{L}
.

Finally, we end this section with a simple but somewhat counterintuitive observation: distribution shift does not always degrade the expected stopping time. If the shift renders the mismatched tuple
\tilde{\mathbf{P}}
more distinguishable, pushing the underlying distributions further apart in the classifier’s decision space, it is possible that
\tilde{\Delta}_{\theta}>\Delta_{\theta}
for some
\theta\in[L]
, thereby accelerating the stopping time.

4.2
Sequential change detection

In this section, we demonstrate that our proposed stopping rule can be applied to the standard sequential change-point detection (SCD) task
[
18
]
.
The goal in SCD problems is to monitor a stream of observations, and identify any abrupt changes in distributions (at an unknown time
T
called the
change point
) with minimal delay, while controlling false alarms within a tolerable limit.
Specifically, we assume that there are a pre-change distribution
P_{0}
and a post-change distribution
P_{\theta}
, for
\theta\in[L]
. At some unknown change point
T\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{\infty\}
, the underlying distribution of the testing samples shifts abruptly from
P_{0}
to
P_{\theta}
. Furthermore, these true data-generating distributions are totally unknown to the decision-maker, beyond the access to offline data. In words, for a fixed
\theta\in[L]
, the testing samples follow
X_{t}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle i.i.d.}}{{\sim}}P_{0}
when
t<T
, but change to
X_{t}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle i.i.d.}}{{\sim}}P_{\theta}
for
t\geq T
.
We write
\mathbb{E}_{\infty}[\cdot]
for “expectation” under the no-change regime (
T=\infty
), and
\mathbb{E}_{T,\theta}[\cdot]
for “expectation” under a change to
P_{\theta}
at some finite time
T<\infty
.

Our proposed e-process in (
6
) can be adapted to the SCD problem as follows: Under a tuple
\mathbf{P}\in\mathcal{P}_{\text{sep}}
, let
\{X_{n}:n\geq 1\}
denote the testing samples. For any
n\geq 1
, and
1\leq k\leq n
, let
\{W^{(k)}_{n}:n\geq k\}
denote the e-process “starting from
k
”:

W^{(k)}_{n}\coloneqq\int_{-1}^{0}\left(\prod_{t=k}^{n}\left(1+\lambda\times\left(\boldsymbol{1}_{g(X_{t})=0}-\boldsymbol{1}_{g(X_{t})=\hat{j}_{t}}\right)\right)\right)d\lambda.

(21)

Then, we define the
e
-detector
[
29
]
process
\{M_{n}:n\geq 1\}
as
M_{n}=\max_{1\leq k\leq n}W_{n}^{(k)}
for any
n\geq 1
, and build the change detection stopping time:
\tau=\inf\{n\geq 1:M_{n}\geq 1/\alpha\}
.

Thus the SCD procedure initializes a new power-one test in every round, and declares a detection as soon of any of the “active” tests reject the null.
Intuitively, if the change occurs at time
T
and
n\geq T
, then the process started at
k=T
, namely
W^{(T)}_{n}
, (and those started after
T
) evolves entirely on post-change samples and should therefore have the same growth behavior as in the sequential hypothesis testing setting under
H_{1}
. Since the observations are i.i.d. within each regime, the resulting expected detection delay should be the same as that in Section
3
. We formalize this in our next result.

Theorem 4.2
.

For any distribution tuple
\mathbf{P}\in\mathcal{P}_{\text{sep}}
, let
P_{0}
and
P_{\theta}
,
\theta\in[L]
denote the pre- and post-change distributions respectively. Then, for any
\theta\in[L]
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\infty}[\tau]\geq 1/\alpha,\quad\text{and}\quad\sup_{T\in\mathbb{N}}\esssup\mathbb{E}_{T,\theta}[(\tau-T)^{+}|\mathcal{F}^{(0)}_{T}]=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\log(1/\alpha\Delta_{\theta})}{\Delta^{2}_{\theta}}\right),

(22)

where
\mathcal{F}^{(0)}_{T}:=\sigma\{\{g(X_{t})\}_{t\leq T}\}
and
\Delta_{\theta}
has the same definition as in
Theorem

3.2
.

The proof of Theorem
4.2
is in Appendix
G
.
Note that in the SCD setting, the expected detection delay generally depends on the ground-truth post-change distribution
P_{\theta}
. Moreover, when
\alpha
is sufficiently small, Proposition
3.3
implies that, at stopping,
\hat{j}_{\tau}
identifies the true post-change index with high probability. This identification is practically important since knowing the post-change parameter can help localize the cause of the anomaly.

We now argue that there exist problem instances for which the expected stopping time derived in Theorem
4.2
cannot be improved. To do so, let us recall the lower bound of the Lorden’s criterion
[
32
, Section IV-B]
applied to the classifier’s output sequence instead of the raw data: if the post-changed distribution is
P_{\theta}
for
\theta\in[L]
, then

\displaystyle\sup_{T\in\mathbb{N}}\esssup\mathbb{E}_{T,\theta}[(\tau-T)^{+}|\mathcal{F}^{(0)}_{T}]\in\Omega\left(\frac{\log(1/\alpha)}{D(\boldsymbol{p}_{\theta}\lVert\boldsymbol{p}_{0})}\right)\quad\text{as }\alpha\rightarrow 0,

(23)

where
\boldsymbol{p}_{\theta}=[\boldsymbol{p}_{\theta}[0],\ldots,\boldsymbol{p}_{\theta}[L]]
and
\boldsymbol{p}_{\theta}[m]:=\mathbb{P}_{X\sim P_{\theta}}(g(X)=m)
for all
m,\theta\in\mathcal{L}
. We prove that there exists a problem instance and a corresponding separable classifier such that the upper bound of the expected delay in Theorem
4.2
cannot be improved asymptotically as
\alpha\rightarrow\infty
. It is sufficient to show that there exists
\{\boldsymbol{p}_{\nu}\}_{\nu\in\mathcal{L}}
satisfying
\boldsymbol{p}_{\theta}[\theta]>\max_{m\in\mathcal{L}\setminus\{\theta\}}\boldsymbol{p}_{\theta}[m]
for all
\theta\in\mathcal{L}
such that
D(\boldsymbol{p}_{\theta}\lVert\boldsymbol{p}_{0})\leq\Delta_{\theta}^{2}
.

Proposition 4.3

(Optimality in some cases)
.

Let
L=1
and
\boldsymbol{p}_{0}=[1-e^{-1},e^{-1}]
and
\boldsymbol{p}_{1}=[0.1,0.9]
, and let
\mathbb{E}_{T,\theta}[\cdot]
denote the expectation when there is a change from
\boldsymbol{p}_{0}
to
\boldsymbol{p}_{1}
at time
T\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{\infty\}
. Then, we have

\sup_{T\in\mathbb{N}}\esssup\mathbb{E}_{T,\theta}[(\tau-T)^{+}|\mathcal{F}_{T}]\in\Omega\left(\frac{\log 1/\alpha}{\Delta^{2}_{1}}\right)\quad\text{as }\alpha\rightarrow 0.

(24)

This result, which is a direct consequence of the fact that relative entropy is bounded by the chi-squared divergence, implies that there exist problem instances for which the expected stopping time bound for our proposed stopping rule derived in Theorem
3.2
is also asymptotically optimal.

After deriving the theoretical results in Section

However, we usually do not know which classifier has the strongest separability since the ground truth tuple is unknown.
Then, one way to utilize the two classifiers is incorporating an dynamic
weighting
on the e-process constructed based on these two classifiers. Specifically, let
\{W_{n,1}\}_{n\geq 0}
and
\{W_{n,2}\}_{n\geq 0}
be the
e
-process defined as follows: Let
\hat{j}_{t,k}=\argmax_{j\in\mathcal{L}}\;\hat{\boldsymbol{p}}_{t-1,k}[j]
and
\hat{\boldsymbol{p}}_{n,k}=(1/n)\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{g_{k}(X_{i})}
for
k\in\{1,2\}
and

\displaystyle W_{0,1}=W_{0,2}=1,\qquad W_{n,k}:=\int_{-1}^{0}\left(\prod_{t=1}^{n}\left(1+\lambda\times\left(\boldsymbol{1}_{g_{k}(X_{t})=0}-\boldsymbol{1}_{g_{k}(X_{t})=\hat{j}_{t,k}}\right)\right)\right)d\lambda.

(31)

Then, we can define the dynamic weighting sequence
\{w_{n}\}_{n\geq 0}
,
w_{n}=[w_{n,1},w_{n,2}]
, which is (adaptively) determined by the past information, resulting the mixture e-process
\{W^{\text{mix}}_{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
defined as
W^{\text{mix}}_{n}=w_{n,1}W_{n,1}+w_{n,2}W_{n,2}
for each
n\geq 0
. The goal is to make
\{w_{n}\}_{n\geq 0}
concentrate to
w_{\infty}=[0,1]
in this case since the classifier
g_{2}
has a stronger separability.

We first argue the validity of this mixture method. Since the sum of non-negative supermartingales is still a non-negative supermartingale, we can directly apply Ville’s inequality to establish the level-
\alpha
property of
\{W^{\text{mix}}_{n}\}_{n\geq 0}
. Moreover, regarding the power-one property, since both classifiers are separable under the ground truth tuple, it is intuitive that the process
\{W^{\text{mix}}_{n}\}_{n\geq 0}
still satisfies the power-one property under any weight.

However, designing the dynamic weighting sequence
\{w_{n}\}_{n\geq 0}
is a nontrivial task. It connects to the classic problem known as the
exploration-exploitation tradeoff
in the multi-armed bandit literature
[
30
]
. A high-level intuition is that we can regard the term
\left(\boldsymbol{1}_{g_{k}(X_{t})=0}-\boldsymbol{1}_{g_{k}(X_{t})=\hat{j}_{t,k}}\right)
as an instantaneous reward and apply a bandit algorithm since for any fixed
\hat{j}_{t,k}\neq 0
, the expected value of this term under
g_{2}
is greater than that under
g_{1}
, due to the stronger separability that
g_{2}
owns. Nevertheless, we leave the detailed analysis as a future direction.

Here, we demonstrate that the benefit of using multiple classifiers exists. Assume an
oracle
weighting mechanism sets
w_{1}=0.1
and
w_{2}=0.9
. The empirical results are plotted in Figure
6
. We observe that the average stopping time under the mixture method (blue dot curve) is smaller than that achieved by a single classifier (orange rectangular curve). Therefore, a weighting mechanism exists that successfully leverages classifier heterogeneity. As noted, designing a dynamic weighting scheme is not addressed in this paper. However, we have provided a clear direction for future work.

6
Conclusion and Future Work

We propose a general framework for constructing nonparametric sequential power-one tests for problems where the null and alternatives are defined indirectly through offline data. Our approach proceeds by constructing an e-process based on an offline-trained separable classifier that yields an anytime-valid test satisfying the level-
\alpha
and power-one properties. Furthermore, we provide a non-asymptotic analysis of the expected stopping time under the alternative, showing that the separability of the classifier plays an important role. We also investigate how the training sample size and the classifier family affect the attainability of separable classifiers and the achievability. Specifically, we establish sufficient and necessary conditions for the required training sample size and analyze its impact, along with the classifier family, on the tail probability of the stopping time. Finally, we extend our stopping rule and theoretical guarantees to the training-testing mismatch problem, showing robustness when the mismatch is bounded by the classifier’s separability, and demonstrate its applicability to sequential change-point detection.

Empirically, we use synthetic and real data to verify that for the proposed stopping rule and its extensions, the observed average stopping times (or average delay for sequential change-point detection) match their theoretical scaling trends. We also verify the robustness of this classifier setting. Regarding future work, it remains unclear whether our proposed test is optimal in general (assuming a fixed classifier and a fixed distribution tuple with no mismatch), as we only establish asymptotic optimality (as
\alpha\downarrow 0
) for specific problem instances. Moreover, the impact of the classifier on the overall performance is not yet fully understood since we do not directly investigate the complexity of
\mathcal{G}
in the minimax lower bound. Finally, a thorough theoretical and empirical study of the dynamic weighting discussed in Section
5.4
is another interesting direction for future work.

Under

Under the null, the result follows directly from the fact that

. Dividing throughout by
n
, gives us the required inequality

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Delta_{\theta}[\hat{j}_{i}]\geq\Delta_{\theta}-\sqrt{\frac{2\log 2n^{3}}{n}}.

(63)

B.1.3
Proof of Lemma
B.3

This is a direct consequence of the previous lemma, and the definition of the event
F_{n}
. In particular, recall that under the event
F_{n}
, we have

\displaystyle\left\lvert\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}Y_{i}+\Delta_{\theta}[\hat{j}_{i}]\right\rvert\leq c_{n}\coloneqq\sqrt{\frac{2\log 2n^{2}}{n}}.

(64)

This fact, combined with Lemma
B.2
implies that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}Y_{i}\leq-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Delta_{\theta}[\hat{j}_{i}]+\sqrt{\frac{2\log 2n^{2}}{n}}\leq-\Delta_{\theta}+\sqrt{\frac{2\log 2n^{3}}{n}}+\sqrt{\frac{2\log 2n^{2}}{n}}\leq-\Delta_{\theta}+\sqrt{\frac{8\log 2n^{3}}{n}},

(65)

where we get the last inequality by loosely upper bounding
\sqrt{(2\log 2n^{2})/n}
with
\sqrt{(2\log 2n^{3})/n}
. Finally,
N_{0}
is defined as the smallest value of
n
at which the term
\sqrt{(8\log 2n^{3})/n}
goes below
\Delta_{\theta}/2
, and thus, for all
n\geq N_{0}
, we have
\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}Y_{i}\leq-\Delta_{\theta}/2
.

B.1.4
Proof of Lemma
B.4

The starting point of the proof of this statement is the regret bound for the mixture method (see, for example,
[
12
, Chapter 4]
), which tells us that

\displaystyle\log W_{n}

\displaystyle=\log\left(\int_{-1}^{0}\left(\prod_{i=1}^{n}(1+\lambda Y_{i})\right)d\lambda\right)\geq\sup_{\lambda\in[-1/2,0]}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log(1+\lambda Y_{i})-2\log n,

(66)

Note that we have also used the fact that by restricting the domain of
\lambda
from
[-1,0]
to
[-1/2,0]
, we can only reduce the value of the supremum.
Now, we can use the lower bound
\log(1+x)\geq x-x^{2}
for
x\geq-0.5
to conclude that

\displaystyle\frac{\log W_{n}}{n}+\frac{2\log n}{n}\geq\sup_{\lambda\in[-1/2,0]}\lambda\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}Y_{i}\right)-\lambda^{2}\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}Y_{i}^{2}\right)\geq\sup_{\lambda\in[-1/2,0]}\lambda\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}Y_{i}\right)-\lambda^{2}.

(67)

This quadratic lower bound is maximized at
\lambda^{*}=(1/2)\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}Y_{i}
, which for
n\geq N_{0}
lies in the set
[-1/2,-\Delta_{\theta}/4]\subset[-1/2,0]
by Lemma
B.3
. Thus, the optimizing value
\lambda^{*}
lies in the valid domain, and plugging this into the lower bound expression, we get

\displaystyle\frac{\log W_{n}}{n}+\frac{2\log n}{n}\geq\frac{1}{4}\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}Y_{i}\right)^{2}\geq\frac{\Delta^{2}_{\theta}}{16}.

(68)

The last inequality again uses the fact that for
n\geq N_{0}
, and under the event
E_{n}\cap F_{n}
, we know from Lemma
B.3
that
(1/n)\sum_{i=1}^{n}Y_{i}\leq-\Delta_{\theta}/2
, which implies that
\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}Y_{i}\right)^{2}\geq\Delta^{2}_{\theta}/4
. On rearranging this, we get

\displaystyle\frac{\log W_{n}}{n}\geq\frac{\Delta^{2}_{\theta}}{16}-\frac{2\log n}{n},\quad\text{for}\quad n\geq N_{0}.

(69)

Finally, let us define
N_{1}=\inf\{n\geq 1:(2\log n)/n\leq\Delta^{2}_{\theta}/32\}
, and conclude that for
n\geq N_{1}\vee N_{0}
, we have the required

\displaystyle\frac{\log W_{n}}{n}\geq\frac{\Delta^{2}_{\theta}}{32}.

(70)

Appendix C
Proof of Proposition
3.3

In this proof, we show that the indicator
\hat{j}_{t}=\argmax_{j\in\mathcal{L}}\hat{p}_{t-1}[j]
, where
\hat{p}_{t-1}[j]=\frac{1}{t-1}\sum_{i=1}^{t-1}\boldsymbol{1}_{g(X_{i})=j}
, eventually identify the true underlying distribution index. Since we have
\mathbb{E}_{\theta}[\mathbf{1}_{g(X)=j}]=\mathbb{P}_{\theta}(g(X)=j)
for any
j\in\mathcal{L}
and
\theta\in\mathcal{L}
and
\{g(X_{i})\}_{i\geq 1}
is a

random sequence, we can apply Hoeffding’s inequality to show the desired result. For any
\theta\in\mathcal{L}
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\theta}\left(\hat{j}_{t}\neq\theta\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}_{\theta}\left(\exists m\in\mathcal{L}\setminus\{\theta\}\,s.t.\,\frac{1}{t-1}\sum_{i=1}^{t-1}\boldsymbol{1}_{g(X_{i})=m}>\frac{1}{t-1}\sum_{i=1}^{t-1}\boldsymbol{1}_{g(X_{i})=\theta}\right)

(71)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{m\in\mathcal{L}\setminus\{\theta\}}\mathbb{P}_{\theta}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{t-1}\left(\boldsymbol{1}_{g(X_{i})=m}-\boldsymbol{1}_{g(X_{i})=\theta}\right)>0\right),

(72)

where the inequality is from the union bound.
Then, by defining

a_{i}:=\left(\boldsymbol{1}_{g(X_{i})=m}-\boldsymbol{1}_{g(X_{i})=\theta}\right)-\left(\mathbb{P}_{\theta}\left(g(X_{i})=m\right)-\mathbb{P}_{\theta}\left(g(X_{i})=\theta\right)\right)\quad\text{for each}\quad i\geq 1,

(73)

we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\theta}\left(\hat{j}_{t}\neq\theta\right)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{m\in\mathcal{L}\setminus\{\theta\}}\mathbb{P}_{\theta}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{t-1}a_{i}>-\sum_{i=1}^{t-1}\left(\mathbb{P}_{\theta}\left(g(X_{i})=m\right)-\mathbb{P}_{\theta}\left(g(X_{i})=\theta\right)\right)\right)

(74)

\displaystyle=\sum_{m\in\mathcal{L}\setminus\{\theta\}}\mathbb{P}_{\theta}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{t-1}a_{i}>(t-1)\Delta_{\theta,m}\right),

(75)

where
\Delta_{\theta,m}:=\mathbb{P}_{\theta}\left(g(X_{i})=\theta\right)-\mathbb{P}_{\theta}\left(g(X_{i})=m\right)=\mathbb{P}_{\theta}\left(g(X)=\theta\right)-\mathbb{P}_{\theta}\left(g(X)=m\right)=\boldsymbol{p}_{\theta}[\theta]-\boldsymbol{p}_{\theta}[m]>0
, for any
m\in\mathcal{L}\setminus\{\theta\}
by the fact that
g
is a separable classifier for the given
\mathbf{P}\in\mathcal{P}_{\text{sep}}
.

Then, since
-2\leq a_{i}\leq 2
, by Hoeffding’s inequality, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\theta}\left(\hat{j}_{t}\neq\theta\right)\leq\exp\left(-\frac{(\Delta_{\theta,m}(t-1))^{2}}{4t}\right)\in O(e^{-t}).

(76)

Hence, by the Borel–Cantelli lemma, one can obtain the implication described in Proposition
3.3
.

The proof of Corollary
3.4
is provided as follows:
Applying Proposition
3.3
, we can bound the misidentification probability under the alternative at the stopping time. Fix
\theta\in[L]
and any integer
K\geq 1
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\theta}(\hat{j}_{\tau}\neq\theta)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}_{\theta}({\tau}\geq K,\ \hat{j}_{\tau}\neq\theta)+\mathbb{P}_{\theta}(\tau<K)

(77)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}_{\theta}(\exists n\geq K,\,\hat{j}_{n}\neq\theta)+\mathbb{P}_{\theta}(\tau<K)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{n=K}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}_{\theta}(\hat{j}_{n}\neq\theta)\;+\;\sum_{n=1}^{K-1}\mathbb{P}_{\theta}\!\left(W_{n}\geq\frac{1}{\alpha}\right),

(78)

where the last inequality uses a union bound together with the definition
\tau=\inf\{n\geq 1:W_{n}\geq 1/\alpha\}
.

The first summation decays exponentially in
K
by Proposition
3.3
, i.e.,
\sum_{n=K}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}_{\theta}(\hat{j}_{n}\neq\theta)\in O(e^{-K})
. For the second term, by the fact that
W_{n}\geq 0
for any
n\geq 1
, Markov’s inequality yields
\mathbb{P}_{\theta}\left(W_{n}\geq\frac{1}{\alpha}\right)\leq\alpha\,\mathbb{E}_{\theta}[W_{n}]
,
and hence

\sum_{n=1}^{K-1}\mathbb{P}_{\theta}\!\left(W_{n}\geq\frac{1}{\alpha}\right)\;\leq\;\alpha\sum_{n=1}^{K-1}\mathbb{E}_{\theta}[W_{n}].

(79)

Using the fact that
\mathbb{E}_{\theta}[W_{n}]=O(2^{n})
, we obtain

\alpha\sum_{n=1}^{K-1}\mathbb{E}_{\theta}[W_{n}]\;\leq\;\alpha\sum_{n=1}^{K-1}O(2^{n})\;=\;O(\alpha\,K2^{K}).

(80)

Combining the above bounds gives

\mathbb{P}_{\theta}(\hat{j}_{\tau}=\theta)=1-\mathbb{P}_{\theta}(\hat{j}_{\tau}\neq\theta)\;\geq\;1-O(e^{-K})-O(\alpha\,K2^{K}).

(81)

In particular, choosing
K\in\Theta(\log_{2}(1/\sqrt{\alpha}))
implies
\alpha\,K2^{K}\to 0
and
e^{-K}\to 0
as
\alpha\to 0
.

P_{\theta}

\Pi_{\alpha}(\mathcal{P}_{\text{sep}})\coloneqq\left\{\pi:\sup_{\mathbf{P}\in\mathcal{P}_{\text{sep}}}\mathbb{P}^{\pi}_{\mathbf{P},0}(\tau<\infty)\leq\alpha\quad\text{and}\quad\inf_{\mathbf{P}\in\mathcal{P}_{\text{sep}}}\mathbb{P}^{\pi}_{\mathbf{P},\theta}(\tau<\infty)=1\ \ \forall\theta\in[L]\right\}.

(104)

Our goal is to demonstrate how the training sample size
N
, the family of classifiers
\mathcal{G}
, and
n
impact the lower bound of
\Psi\coloneqq\inf_{\pi\in\Pi_{\alpha}(\mathcal{P}_{\text{sep}})}\sup_{\mathbf{P}\in\mathcal{P}_{\text{sep}}}\mathbb{P}^{\pi}_{\mathbf{P},\theta}(\tau>n)
. The main idea is to utilize Le Cam’s two-point method
[
23
, Chapter 31]
, a useful technique for proving minimax lower bounds from an information-theoretic viewpoint. We start by applying a decomposition used in the converse of
[
15
, Theorem 2]
and the data processing inequality (DPI)
[
23
, Chapter 3]
to obtain a general lower bound for
\mathbb{P}^{\pi}_{\mathbf{P},\theta}(\tau>n)
for any
\theta\in[L]
. Then, taking the infimum and supremum of this bound, we apply Le Cam’s two-point method to introduce the impact of
N
and
\mathcal{G}
simultaneously. Finally, using Assumption
3.11
, we conclude the proof. Note that we will describe the formal statement of Assumption
3.11
in the following proof.

Step 1: Decomposition and DPI

Consider any
\mathbf{P}\in\mathcal{P}_{\text{sep}}
and
\pi\in\Pi_{\alpha}(\mathcal{P}_{\text{sep}})
, where the null distribution is
P_{0}
and the alternative distribution is
P_{\theta}
for any
\theta\in[L]
. We then apply a variant decomposition provided in the converse proof of
[
15
, Theorem 2]
. Specifically, let
A=\{\tau>n\}
, then we have

\displaystyle d(\mathbb{P}^{\pi}_{\mathbf{P},0}(A)\|\mathbb{P}^{\pi}_{\mathbf{P},\theta}(A))

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}^{\pi}_{\mathbf{P},0}(A)\log\frac{\mathbb{P}^{\pi}_{\mathbf{P},0}(A)}{\mathbb{P}^{\pi}_{\mathbf{P},\theta}(A)}+\mathbb{P}^{\pi}_{\mathbf{P},0}(A^{c})\log\frac{\mathbb{P}^{\pi}_{\mathbf{P},0}(A^{c})}{\mathbb{P}^{\pi}_{\mathbf{P},\theta}(A^{c})}

(105)

\displaystyle=-h_{b}(\mathbb{P}^{\pi}_{\mathbf{P},0}(A))-\mathbb{P}^{\pi}_{\mathbf{P},0}(A)\log\mathbb{P}^{\pi}_{\mathbf{P},\theta}(A)-\mathbb{P}^{\pi}_{\mathbf{P},0}(A^{c})\log\mathbb{P}^{\pi}_{\mathbf{P},\theta}(A^{c}).

(106)

where
d(p\|q)=p\log\frac{p}{q}+(1-p)\log\frac{1-p}{1-q}
and
h_{b}(p)=p\log 1/p+(1-p)\log(1/(1-p))
. Therefore, by rearrangement, we have

\displaystyle\log\frac{1}{\mathbb{P}^{\pi}_{\mathbf{P},\theta}(A)}=\frac{1}{\mathbb{P}^{\pi}_{\mathbf{P},0}(A)}\left(d(\mathbb{P}^{\pi}_{\mathbf{P},0}(A)\|\mathbb{P}^{\pi}_{\mathbf{P},\theta}(A))+h_{b}(\mathbb{P}^{\pi}_{\mathbf{P},0}(A))+\mathbb{P}^{\pi}_{\mathbf{P},0}(A^{c})\log\mathbb{P}^{\pi}_{\mathbf{P},\theta}(A^{c})\right).

(107)

By the level-
\alpha
property, we have
\mathbb{P}^{\pi}_{\mathbf{P},0}(A)=1-\mathbb{P}^{\pi}_{\mathbf{P},0}(\tau\leq n)\geq 1-\alpha
. Moreover, since
A\in\tilde{\mathcal{F}}_{n}
, by data processing (applied to the map
\omega\mapsto\mathbf{1}\{\tau(\omega)>n\}
),
we have
d(\mathbb{P}^{\pi}_{\mathbf{P},0}(A)\|\mathbb{P}^{\pi}_{\mathbf{P},\theta}(A))\leq D(\mathbb{P}^{\pi}_{\mathbf{P},0}\mid_{\tilde{\mathcal{F}}_{n}}\|\mathbb{P}^{\pi}_{\mathbf{P},\theta}\mid_{\tilde{\mathcal{F}}_{n}})
, where
\mathbb{P}^{\pi}_{\mathbf{P},0}\mid_{\tilde{\mathcal{F}}_{n}}
denotes the joint distribution of the tuple
\{\{T_{\vartheta}^{N}\}_{\vartheta\in\mathcal{L}},\{g(X_{i})\}_{i=1}^{N}\}
. Hence, we get

\displaystyle\log\frac{1}{\mathbb{P}^{\pi}_{\mathbf{P},\theta}(A)}\leq\frac{1}{1-\alpha}\left(D(\mathbb{P}^{\pi}_{\mathbf{P},0}\mid_{\tilde{\mathcal{F}}_{n}}\|\mathbb{P}^{\pi}_{\mathbf{P},\theta}\mid_{\tilde{\mathcal{F}}_{n}}))+\log(2)\right),

(108)

where the inequality uses the fact that
h_{b}(p)\leq\log(2)
and
\log\mathbb{P}^{\pi}_{\mathbf{P},\theta}(A^{c})\leq 0
.

Before proceeding with the proof, let
(\boldsymbol{p}_{m}^{g})^{\otimes n}
denote the joint distribution of
\{g,g(X_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
where
X_{i}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle i.i.d.}}{{\sim}}P_{m}
for any
m\in\mathcal{L}
and
g=\phi(\{T^{N}_{\vartheta},\vartheta\}_{\vartheta\in\mathcal{L}})
. Given
g
, if
m\in\mathcal{L}
is the ground truth distribution, these random variables are drawn i.i.d. from the distribution of
g(X)
where
X\sim P_{m}
(denoted as
\boldsymbol{p}_{m}
defined in Section
3.1
). In this section, to emphasize the role of the classifier, we let
\boldsymbol{p}^{g}_{m}\equiv\boldsymbol{p}_{m}
under a given classifier
g
. Consequently, we have
(\boldsymbol{p}_{m}^{g})^{\otimes n}=\Pi_{i=1}^{n}\boldsymbol{p}^{g}_{m}
if
g
is given. Then, we provide an upper bound for the KL-divergence term. Denote
\mathbf{T}^{N}:=\{T_{\vartheta}^{N}\}_{\vartheta\in\mathcal{L}}
.

\displaystyle D(\mathbb{P}^{\pi}_{\mathbf{P},0}\mid_{\tilde{\mathcal{F}}_{n}}\|\mathbb{P}^{\pi}_{\mathbf{P},\theta}\mid_{\tilde{\mathcal{F}}_{n}})

\displaystyle=D(\Pi_{\vartheta=0}^{L}P_{\vartheta}^{\otimes N}\cdot(\boldsymbol{p}_{0}^{g})^{\otimes n}\|\Pi_{\vartheta=0}^{L}P_{\vartheta}^{\otimes N}\cdot(\boldsymbol{p}_{\theta}^{g})^{\otimes n})

(109)

\displaystyle=D(\Pi_{\vartheta=0}^{L}P_{\vartheta}^{\otimes N}\|\Pi_{\vartheta=0}^{L}P_{\vartheta}^{\otimes N})+D((\boldsymbol{p}_{0}^{g})^{\otimes n}\|(\boldsymbol{p}_{\theta}^{g})^{\otimes n}\,|\,\Pi_{\vartheta=0}^{L}P_{\vartheta}^{\otimes N})

(110)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{T}^{N}}\left[D\left((\boldsymbol{p}_{0}^{g})^{\otimes n|\mathbf{T}^{N}}\lVert(\boldsymbol{p}_{\theta}^{g})^{\otimes n|\mathbf{T}^{N}}\right)\right]

(111)

\displaystyle=n\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{T}^{N}}\left[D\left(\boldsymbol{p}_{0}^{g}\lVert\boldsymbol{p}_{\theta}^{g}\right)\right]

(112)

where (
110
) uses the chain rule, (
111
) is since the first term is zero and the definition of the conditional KL-divergence (note that
(\boldsymbol{p}_{0}^{g})^{\otimes n|\mathbf{T}^{N}}
denotes the conditional distribution of
(\boldsymbol{p}_{0}^{g})^{\otimes n}
conditioning on the training samples), and (
112
) is from our problem formulation that given the training tuple
\mathbf{T}^{N}
, equivalently given
g
, the output of the classifier of
X_{i}
is
g(X_{i})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle i.i.d.}}{{\sim}}\boldsymbol{p}^{g}_{\theta}
for each
i\leq n
.

Therefore, plugging (
112
) back into the type-II error bound, we get

\displaystyle\log\frac{1}{\mathbb{P}^{\pi}_{\mathbf{P},\theta}(A)}\leq\frac{1}{1-\alpha}\left(n\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{T}^{N}}\left[D\left(\boldsymbol{p}_{0}^{g}\lVert\boldsymbol{p}_{\theta}^{g}\right)\right]+\log(2)\right).

(113)

Now, we further upper-bound the expectation term to get a general lower bound of
\mathbb{P}^{\pi}_{\mathbf{P},\theta}(A)
. By
\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{T}^{N}}\left[D\left(\boldsymbol{p}_{0}^{g}\lVert\boldsymbol{p}_{\theta}^{g}\right)\right]\leq\sup_{g\in\mathcal{G}}D(\boldsymbol{p}_{0}^{g}\|\boldsymbol{p}^{g}_{\theta})
, where
\mathcal{G}
is the family of classifier, we have

\displaystyle\log\frac{1}{\mathbb{P}^{\pi}_{\mathbf{P},\theta}(A)}\leq\frac{1}{1-\alpha}\left(n\sup_{g\in\mathcal{G}}D(\boldsymbol{p}_{0}^{g}\|\boldsymbol{p}_{\theta}^{g})+\log 2\right)

(114)

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\mathbb{P}^{\pi}_{\mathbf{P},\theta}(\tau>n)\geq\exp\left(-\frac{1}{1-\alpha}\left(n\sup_{g\in\mathcal{G}}D(\boldsymbol{p}_{0}^{g}\|\boldsymbol{p}_{\theta}^{g})+\log 2\right)\right).

(115)

Consequently, we obtain a general lower bound that is valid for all
\theta\in[L]
,
\pi\in\Pi_{\alpha}(\mathcal{P}_{\text{sep}})
, and
\mathbf{P}\in\mathcal{P}_{\text{sep}}
. Observe that for sufficiently large
n
, the dominant exponent is
\sup_{g\in\mathcal{G}}D(\boldsymbol{p}_{0}^{g}\|\boldsymbol{p}_{\theta}^{g})
. Therefore, if
\mathcal{G}
is sufficiently rich, one can find a classifier with stronger separability for
P_{0}
and
P_{\theta}
, leading to a smaller lower bound on the type-II error. However, we remark that no matter how rich
\mathcal{G}
is, by DPI,
D(\boldsymbol{p}_{0}^{g}\|\boldsymbol{p}_{\theta}^{g})\leq D(P_{0}\|P_{\theta})
, which is an intrinsic problem difficulty.

Step 2: Apply Le Cam’s two-point method

Although we have derived the general lower bound, it does not show the impact of the training sample size
N
. This is because we upper bounded the expectation term
\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{T}^{N}}\left[D\left(\boldsymbol{p}_{0}^{g}\lVert\boldsymbol{p}_{\theta}^{g}\right)\right]
, which encodes the information about the training sample size, by simply taking the supremum over the family of classifiers. We now apply Le Cam’s two-point method to introduce the impact of
N
and
\mathcal{G}
simultaneously in the minimax setting.

By applying minimax operation on (
113
), we have

\displaystyle\inf_{\pi\in\Pi_{\alpha}(\mathcal{P}_{\text{sep}})}\sup_{\mathbf{P}\in\mathcal{P}_{\text{sep}}}\mathbb{P}^{\pi}_{\mathbf{P},\theta}(\tau>n)\geq\exp\left(-\frac{1}{1-\alpha}\left(n\sup_{\pi\in\Pi_{\alpha}(\mathcal{P}_{\text{sep}})}\inf_{\mathbf{P}\in\mathcal{P}_{\text{sep}}}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{T}^{N}}\left[D\left(\boldsymbol{p}_{0}^{g}\lVert\boldsymbol{p}_{\theta}^{g}\right)\right]+\log 2\right)\right),

(116)

Then, let us provide a non-trivial upper bound for the
\sup\inf
expectation term.

Let
D^{*}(\mathbf{P}):=\sup_{g\in\mathcal{G}}D(\boldsymbol{p}_{0}^{g}\|\boldsymbol{p}^{g}_{\theta})
. We can write
\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{T}^{N}}\left[D\left(\boldsymbol{p}_{0}^{g}\lVert\boldsymbol{p}_{\theta}^{g}\right)\right]=D^{*}(\mathbf{P})-\left(D^{*}(\mathbf{P})-\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{T}^{N}}\left[D\left(\boldsymbol{p}_{0}^{g}\lVert\boldsymbol{p}_{\theta}^{g}\right)\right]\right)
.
Following this, we define the
non-negative loss
(or suboptimality gap)
\mathcal{R}(g,\mathbf{P}):=D^{*}(\mathbf{P})-D\left(\boldsymbol{p}_{0}^{g}\lVert\boldsymbol{p}_{\theta}^{g}\right)
(note that
g
depends on the tuple
\mathbf{T}^{N}
), leading to

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{T}^{N}}\left[D\left(\boldsymbol{p}_{0}^{g}\lVert\boldsymbol{p}_{\theta}^{g}\right)\right]=D^{*}(\mathbf{P})-\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{T}^{N}}[\mathcal{R}(g,\mathbf{P})].

(117)

Then, we consider two tuples in
\mathcal{P}_{\text{sep}}
which are parametrized by
\nu_{0}
and
\nu_{1}
, denoted as
\mathbf{P}_{\nu_{0}}
and
\mathbf{P}_{\nu_{1}}
defined in Section
3.2.2
, where
\nu_{0}
,
\nu_{1}
are defined in Theorem
3.12
,
\nu_{1}=\nu_{0}+\delta
for some
\delta>0
. These two tuples serve as the basic elements in Le Cam’s two-point method.

Therefore, from (
117
), we have

\displaystyle\sup_{\pi\in\Pi_{\alpha}(\mathcal{P}_{\text{sep}})}\inf_{\mathbf{P}\in\mathcal{P}_{\text{sep}}}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{T}^{N}}\left[D\left(\boldsymbol{p}_{0}^{g}\lVert\boldsymbol{p}_{\theta}^{g}\right)\right]

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{\pi\in\Pi_{\alpha}(\mathcal{P}_{\text{sep}})}\min_{\mathbf{P}\in\{\mathbf{P}_{\nu_{0}},\mathbf{P}_{\nu_{1}}\}}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{T}^{N}}\left[D\left(\boldsymbol{p}_{0}^{g}\lVert\boldsymbol{p}_{\theta}^{g}\right)\right]

(118)

\displaystyle\equiv\sup_{\pi\in\Pi_{\alpha}(\mathcal{P}_{\text{sep}})}\min_{\nu\in\{\nu_{0},\nu_{1}\}}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{T}^{N}}\left[D(\boldsymbol{p}^{g}_{0,\nu}\|\boldsymbol{p}^{g}_{\theta,\nu})\right]

(119)

\displaystyle\leq\max_{\nu\in\{\nu_{0},\nu_{1}\}}D^{*}(\mathbf{P}_{\nu})-\inf_{\pi\in\Pi_{\alpha}(\mathcal{P}_{\text{sep}})}\max_{\nu\in\{\nu_{0},\nu_{1}\}}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{T}^{N}}[\mathcal{R}(g,\mathbf{P}_{\nu})],

(120)

where
\boldsymbol{p}^{g}_{m,\nu}\coloneqq\mathbb{P}_{X\sim P_{m,\nu}}(g(X)=\cdot)
is the probability mass function of
g(X)
where
X\sim P_{m,\nu}
(the
m
-th distribution in the tuple
\mathbf{P}_{\nu}
) for any
m\in\mathcal{L}
and
\nu\in\{\nu_{0},\nu_{1}\}
, and the first inequality uses the fact that taking the infimum over a smaller feasible set is larger.

Then, we continue to lower-bound the second term in (
120
). Following the standard argument of Le Cam’s two-point method
[
23
, Chapter 31]
, we get

\displaystyle\inf_{\pi\in\Pi_{\alpha}(\mathcal{P}_{\text{sep}})}\max_{\nu\in\{\nu_{0},\nu_{1}\}}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{T}^{N}}[\mathcal{R}(g,\mathbf{P}_{\nu})]

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\mathcal{R}_{\nu_{0},\nu_{1}}}{2}\left(1-\text{TV}\left(\Pi_{i=0}^{L}P^{\otimes N}_{i,\nu_{0}},\Pi_{i=0}^{L}P^{\otimes N}_{i,\nu_{1}}\right)\right)

(121)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\mathcal{R}_{\nu_{0},\nu_{1}}}{4}\exp\left(-N\sum_{i=0}^{L}D(P_{i,\nu_{0}}\|P_{i,\nu_{1}})\right)

(122)

where
\mathcal{R}_{\nu_{0},\nu_{1}}:=\inf_{g\in\mathcal{G}}\left(\mathcal{R}(g,\mathbf{P}_{\nu_{0}})+\mathcal{R}(g,\mathbf{P}_{\nu_{1}})\right)
, the first inequality is from Le Cam’s two-point method, and the second inequality is due to

\displaystyle\text{TV}\left(\Pi_{i=0}^{L}P^{\otimes N}_{i,\nu_{0}},\Pi_{i=0}^{L}P^{\otimes N}_{i,\nu_{1}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq 1-\frac{1}{2}\exp\left(-D\left(\Pi_{\vartheta=0}^{L}P^{\otimes N}_{\vartheta,\nu_{0}}\|\Pi_{\vartheta=0}^{L}P^{\otimes N}_{\vartheta,\nu_{1}}\right)\right)

(123)

\displaystyle=1-\frac{1}{2}\exp\left(-N\sum_{i=0}^{L}D(P_{i,\nu_{0}}\|P_{i,\nu_{1}})\right),

(124)

in which the first inequality is due to
[
7
, Lemma 7]
and the second equality is due to the independence of training samples.

Therefore, we get the minimax lower bound: For any
\theta\in[L]
,

\displaystyle\log\inf_{\pi\in\Pi_{\alpha}(\mathcal{P}_{\text{sep}})}\sup_{\mathbf{P}\in\mathcal{P}_{\text{sep}}}\mathbb{P}^{\pi}_{\mathbf{P},\theta}(\tau>n)

(125)

\displaystyle\geq-\frac{1}{1-\alpha}\left(n\left(\max_{\nu\in\{\nu_{0},\nu_{1}\}}D^{*}(\mathbf{P}_{\nu})-\frac{\mathcal{R}_{\nu_{0},\nu_{1}}}{4}\exp\left(-N\sum_{i=0}^{L}D(P_{i,\nu_{0}}\|P_{i,\nu_{1}})\right)\right)+\log 2\right).

(126)

Now, by the assumption described in Theorem
3.12
,
D(P_{i,\nu_{0}}\|P_{i,\nu_{1}})\leq M\delta^{2}
,
\forall\,i\in\mathcal{L}
, we have

\displaystyle\log\inf_{\pi\in\Pi_{\alpha}(\mathcal{P}_{\text{sep}})}\sup_{\mathbf{P}\in\mathcal{P}_{\text{sep}}}\mathbb{P}^{\pi}_{\mathbf{P},\theta}(\tau>n)

(127)

\displaystyle\geq-\frac{1}{1-\alpha}\left(n\left(\max_{\nu\in\{\nu_{0},\nu_{1}\}}D^{*}(\mathbf{P}_{\nu})-\frac{\mathcal{R}_{\nu_{0},\nu_{1}}}{4}\exp\left(-NM(L+1)\delta^{2}\right)\right)+\log 2\right)

(128)

\displaystyle\geq-\frac{1}{1-\alpha}\left(n\left(\max_{\nu\in\{\nu_{0},\nu_{1}\}}D(P_{0,\nu}\|P_{\theta,\nu})-\frac{\mathcal{R}_{\nu_{0},\nu_{1}}}{4}\exp\left(-NM(L+1)\delta^{2}\right)\right)+\log 2\right),

(129)

where the last inequality is due to DPI.

Finally, by Assumption
3.11
(we provide its formal version below), we can get the result shown in Theorem
3.12
.

Assumption F.1

(Classifier capacity limit (
Formal
))
.

For the considered
\mathbf{P}_{\nu_{0}}
and
\mathbf{P}_{\nu_{1}}
, there exists a bounded constant
B>0
such that
\mathcal{R}_{\nu_{0},\nu_{1}}:=\inf_{g\in\mathcal{G}}\left(\mathcal{R}(g,\mathbf{P}_{\nu_{0}})+\mathcal{R}(g,\mathbf{P}_{\nu_{1}})\right)>B
.

Remark F.2
.

The bound,
D(P_{i,\nu_{0}}\|P_{i,\nu_{1}})\leq M\delta^{2}
, is a classical result in information geometry
[
5
, Exercise 11.7]
, which shows that
D(P_{\theta}\|P_{\theta}^{\prime})=\frac{1}{2}I_{\theta}(\theta-\theta^{\prime})^{2}+o((\theta-\theta^{\prime})^{2})
(abusing notation, we assume
\theta\in\mathbb{R}
here, rather than the distribution index), where
I_{\theta}:=\mathbb{E}_{X\sim P_{\theta}}\left[\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}\ln P_{\theta}(X)\right]^{2}
is the Fisher information. If
|\theta^{\prime}-\theta|=\delta
is sufficiently small, we obtain the bound. However, we do not specify the design of
\nu_{0}
and
\nu_{1}
in this paper, leaving it as future work.

Appendix G
Proof of Theorem
4.2

ARL control.

Observe that the process
\{M_{n}:n\geq 1\}
is a Shirayev-Roberts type e-process as defined by
[
29
]
, since each
\{W_{n}^{(k)}:n\geq k\}
is an e-process for
P_{0}
. Hence, as a consequence of
[
29
, Theorem 2.4]
, we have

\mathbb{E}_{\infty}[\tau]\geq\frac{1}{\alpha}
,
when there is no change.

Detection Delay Bound.

Now suppose that there is a change in the distribution at some time
T
. Let us introduce the stopping time
\tau^{(k)}=\inf\{n-k+1:W^{(k)}_{n}\geq 1/\alpha,\;n\geq k\}
. Then, a simple consequence of the definition of
\tau
is that

\displaystyle\tau\leq\inf_{k\geq 1}\left(\tau^{(k)}+k-1\right)\leq\inf_{k\geq T+1}\left(\tau^{(k)}+k-1\right)\leq\tau^{(T+1)}+T,

(130)

almost surely. Hence, we have

\displaystyle(\tau-T)^{+}\leq\tau^{(T+1)}.

(131)

Observe that
\tau^{(T+1)}
is the same as the sequential test we analyzed under the alternative in
Theorem

3.2
. Under the assumption that the post-change observations
\{X_{t}:t\geq T+1\}
are independent of the pre-change observations
\{X_{t}:t\leq T\}
, we then have, assuming
\theta\in\{1,\ldots,L\}
is the ground truth parameter of the post-change distribution,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{T,\theta}\left[(\tau-T)^{+}|\mathcal{F}_{T}\right]\leq\mathbb{E}_{T,\theta}[\tau^{(T+1)}\mid\mathcal{F}_{T}]=\mathbb{E}_{T,\theta}[\tau^{(T+1)}]=\mathbb{E}_{\theta}[\tau^{(1)}].

(132)

The term
E_{\theta}[\tau^{(1)}]
satisfies the same upper bound we derived in
Theorem

3.2
, which by the above chain implies the upper bound required for the detection delay by taking a supremum over
T\in\mathbb{N}
.
