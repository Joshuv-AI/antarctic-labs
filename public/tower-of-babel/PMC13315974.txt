Neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio as a biomarker in erythema nodosum leprosum: A systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Siti Azyyati Nur Shadrina, Starla Gracelie Karlina, Syalikha Arviandra Subhan, Arzetha Kirana, Ari Dwi Putra Sit, Jihan Aliya Najmamahanany, Naila Qanita Putri, Adinda Cleo Putri Panggabean, Aulia Indra Ufaira, Valentina Febri Dahlianingsih, Muhammad Azi Abdillah, Alveron Andreas Tear, Naufal Fakhri Nugraha
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesErythema nodosum leprosum (ENL) is a severe type 2 leprosy reaction causing recurrent episodes, nerve damage, and disability. Diagnosis still relies on clinical assessment and tests often unavailable in resource-limited settings. The neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR), derived from routine complete blood counts, has been proposed as a low-cost inflammatory biomarker. This systematic review and meta-analysis aimed to assess differences in NLR between ENL and non-reactional leprosy.MethodsFollowing PRISMA 2020 and a PECO framework, we searched PubMed, ScienceDirect, Scopus, and Google Scholar up to 20 November 2025 for observational studies reporting NLR in ENL versus non-reactional leprosy. Quality was assessed using the Newcastle–Ottawa Scale. Pooled mean differences were estimated using a fixed-effect model in RevMan 5.4.1, with heterogeneity evaluated by Cochran's Q and the I2 statistic.ResultsOf 677 records, five observational studies (162 ENL and 423 non-reactional leprosy patients) met the inclusion criteria. NLR was higher in ENL, with a pooled mean difference of 5.79 (95% CI 4.58–6.99; I2 = 56%; P < 0.00001).ConclusionsNLR is markedly elevated in ENL compared with non-reactional leprosy, supporting its potential as a simple, low-cost biomarker. Prospective studies are needed to define optimal cut-off values for routine use.

Introduction

Leprosy, caused by Mycobacterium leprae, remains a public health challenge in endemic regions, with 172,717 new cases reported globally in 2024, predominantly in Southeast Asia, Africa, and the Americas [1,2]. Despite the availability of effective multidrug therapy (MDT), the disease burden persists due to delayed diagnosis, social stigma, and the occurrence of immunological complications known as leprosy reactions [1]. Erythema nodosum leprosum (ENL), or type 2 leprosy reaction, is a severe inflammatory complication affecting approximately 50% of lepromatous leprosy (LL) patients and 5–10% of borderline lepromatous (BL) patients [3,4], characterised by painful skin nodules, fever, neuritis, and potential multi-organ involvement including iritis, arthritis, orchitis, and glomerulonephritis [4,5]. Given its acute onset, recurrent nature with 39–77% of patients experiencing multiple episodes, and risk of permanent nerve damage leading to disability [6], early recognition and prompt management of ENL are critical to prevent irreversible sequelae and improve patient outcomes.

The diagnosis and monitoring of ENL remain challenging due to the lack of reliable and accessible biomarkers [7]. Currently, ENL diagnosis relies primarily on clinical assessment, which may be subjective and difficult to standardize, particularly in resource-limited settings where leprosy is most prevalent [8]. While histopathological examination showing neutrophilic infiltration with vasculitis and immunological markers have been investigated [4,8], these methods are often costly, time-consuming, and not readily available in primary healthcare facilities. The neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR), a simple and inexpensive marker derived from routine complete blood count, has emerged as a potential inflammatory biomarker in various conditions [8,9]. Recent studies have explored the utility of NLR in differentiating ENL from non-reactional leprosy states, with varying results reported across different populations [[9], [10], [11], [12]].

Several studies have investigated NLR values in ENL patients compared to non-reactional leprosy cases, with reports indicating significantly elevated NLR levels during ENL episodes [[9], [10], [11], [12]]. Between 2018 and 2024, multiple cross-sectional and retrospective studies across endemic countries examined this association, with sample sizes ranging from 25 to over 246 participants [[9], [10], [11], [12], [13]]. Studies from Indonesia have reported NLR cut-off values of 4.99 (sensitivity 86.4%, specificity 82.5%) for ENL diagnosis [10], while a Brazilian study demonstrated an NLR cut-off of 2.95 (sensitivity 81.0%, specificity 74.0%) for type 2 reactions [9]. Therefore, this systematic review and meta-analysis aims to comprehensively investigate the difference in NLR values between ENL and non-reactional leprosy patients, and to evaluate its potential as a diagnostic and monitoring biomarker in clinical practice.

Methods

Research design

This research was conducted using a secondary data analysis approach through systematic review and meta-analysis methods. The reporting adheres to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) 2020 guidelines. The protocol was prospectively registered in PROSPERO (registration number: CRD420261341619). This review is guided by the PECO framework: Population—patients with leprosy, comparing ENL versus non-reactional leprosy; Exposure—NLR measurements; Comparison—ENL patients versus non-reactional leprosy patients; Outcome—difference in NLR values between ENL and non-ENL groups, evaluating NLR as a potential diagnostic and monitoring biomarker.

Data source and search strategy

The literature search was conducted through four electronic databases—PubMed, ScienceDirect, Scopus, and Google Scholar—to obtain publications up to 20 November 2025. The search strategy used main keywords including “leprosy”, “Hansen disease”, “erythema nodosum leprosum”, “ENL”, “reversal reaction”, “type 1 reaction”, “type 2 reaction”, “neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio”, and “NLR”. The search considered the characteristics of each database, using Medical Subject Headings (MeSH), abstracts, titles, and keyword fields. Within a single concept, terms were combined using the Boolean operator “OR”; distinct concepts were connected using “AND”. The search strategy was modified based on the specific rules of each database. The full search strategy is provided in the Appendix.

Study selection

The study selection process was conducted independently by eleven reviewers who screened the titles and abstracts of all identified publications. Articles with potential relevance were obtained in full text for further evaluation. Assessment of inclusion eligibility was conducted independently by all reviewers based on previously established inclusion and exclusion criteria. In case of disagreement, the final decision was determined through discussion and consensus led by a designated senior reviewer. All stages of the selection process were conducted using Rayyan.

Inclusion criteria were: (1) observational studies, including cohort, case–control, or cross-sectional designs; (2) studies published in English; (3) studies involving patients with confirmed leprosy, specifically comparing multibacillary patients with ENL to non-reactional leprosy patients; (4) studies that measured and reported NLR values, including mean and standard deviation; (5) studies providing complete comparative data between ENL and non-ENL groups; (6) studies conducted up to 20 November 2025; and (7) studies utilising standardised diagnostic methods for ENL, based on clinical diagnosis with or without histopathological confirmation.

Studies were excluded if they met one or more of the following criteria: (1) case reports, case series, letters, reviews, commentaries, or symposium abstracts; (2) in vivo or in silico studies that did not meet other inclusion criteria; (3) studies with incomplete or unavailable data; (4) studies reporting leprosy reactions without clearly differentiating ENL from type 1 reversal reaction; (5) studies not reporting NLR values or reporting only neutrophil or lymphocyte counts separately; (6) studies with overlapping data derived from previously published analyses; (7) studies involving patients receiving corticosteroids or other immunosuppressive medications at the time of blood sampling when such treatments could influence NLR values; and (8) duplicate publications or articles derived from the same dataset.

Data extraction and risk of bias assessment

From the eligible studies, the following variables were collected: (1) first author and year of publication; (2) country; (3) study design; (4) sample size; (5) clinical grouping (ENL vs non-ENL); (6) mean and standard deviation of NLR values; (7) laboratory parameters; and (8) quality of study. Risk of bias was evaluated using the Newcastle–Ottawa Scale (NOS), which assesses observational studies across selection, comparability, and exposure/outcome domains. Each study was awarded stars according to specific criteria, with a maximum possible score of nine stars. All five included studies were classified as having a low risk of bias, with a mean NOS score of 7.2 and individual scores ranging from 7 to 8, indicating consistently strong methodological quality.

Outcome metrics and statistical analysis

The NLR from each included study was analysed using RevMan 5.4.1. A fixed-effect model was applied to pool the mean difference between ENL and non-ENL groups. Heterogeneity across studies was evaluated using the I2 statistic and Cochran's Q test. The primary outcome was the pooled mean difference in NLR, presented with 95% confidence intervals (CI) to reflect the precision of the estimate. Because the number of eligible studies was limited, subgroup analysis and meta-regression were not performed.

Results

Systematic literature search

From the initial search across major databases, a total of 252 records were identified. After removing 24 duplicate entries, 228 studies remained and proceeded to the title and abstract screening phase. During this stage, 178 records were excluded because they did not meet the predefined inclusion criteria. A total of 50 reports were then sought for full-text retrieval; however, 39 could not be accessed due to unavailable full-text or retrieval issues. As a result, 11 full-text articles were successfully assessed for eligibility. Among these, 4 studies were excluded, consisting of 3 articles with ineligible study designs and 1 study with incomplete or insufficient data for analysis. Meta-analysis of NLR between ENL and non-reactional leprosy

The forest plot (Fig. 3) summarises five studies comparing NLR between ENL and non-ENL patients using a fixed-effect model. All studies consistently showed higher NLR in the ENL group, with positive mean differences ranging from 3.72 to 8.70 and confidence intervals that did not cross zero, indicating statistically significant differences. Study weighting varied based on sample size and precision, with Barboza et al. (2024) [15] contributing the largest weight (38.6%). A total of 162 ENL and 423 non-ENL subjects were included, producing a pooled mean difference of 5.79 (95% CI 4.58–6.99). The test for overall effect was highly significant (Z = 9.38, P < 0.00001). Discussion

This systematic review and meta-analysis examined differences in neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR) between patients with erythema nodosum leprosum (ENL) and those with non-reactional leprosy. Understanding systemic inflammatory markers in leprosy reactions is clinically important because leprosy reactions are a major cause of morbidity, disability, and socioeconomic burden, especially in patients with multibacillary forms who are at highest risk for severe reactional episodes such as ENL [16,17]. ENL is an acute-on-chronic inflammatory complication primarily affecting individuals with LL and BL leprosy [3,4,16]. It is characterised by recurrent episodes of painful erythematous nodules accompanied by fever, malaise, and systemic inflammation, often leading to prolonged corticosteroid or thalidomide therapy and associated treatment-related complications [14,16,18].

The immunopathology of ENL provides a strong biological rationale for investigating systemic inflammatory markers such as NLR. ENL represents a complex immunological process involving both immune-complex–mediated and cell-mediated pathways. Histopathological studies consistently demonstrate intense perivascular and interstitial neutrophilic infiltration throughout the dermis and subcutis, frequently accompanied by tissue oedema, panniculitis, and evidence of vasculitis with fibrinoid necrosis [15,16,19]. Direct immunofluorescence studies further show granular deposits of immunoglobulin and complement in ENL lesions, supporting the role of immune-complex deposition in initiating inflammatory cascades [16,19].

At the cellular level, ENL is associated with activation of neutrophils, macrophages, and T lymphocytes, along with increased expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines including TNF-α and IL-12 [5,20]. Circulating neutrophils in BL/LL patients often contain abundant bacilli prior to multidrug therapy, and patients experiencing ENL episodes exhibit marked neutrophilia and alterations in neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio [15,21,22]. Despite neutrophilic leukocytosis being widely recognised as a hallmark of ENL, it is inconsistently reported in clinical studies, partly due to timing of sampling and heterogeneity of disease presentation. Moreover, although neutrophils are a defining histological feature of many ENL lesions, not all clinically confirmed cases demonstrate neutrophilic infiltrates on biopsy, suggesting a dynamic inflammatory process in which tissue timing and sampling significantly influence histopathological findings [19,21].

Given the absence of reliable biomarkers to predict leprosy reactions, simple and accessible inflammatory indicators such as NLR have attracted increasing interest [7,23]. NLR has been used across various inflammatory and infectious diseases as a surrogate marker of systemic immune activation because it simultaneously reflects neutrophil-driven inflammation and lymphocyte suppression during physiological stress [[24], [25], [26]]. Reference values for NLR in healthy populations typically range from 1 to 3 [25]. In COVID-19, elevated NLR on admission has been shown to predict both disease severity and mortality [27], while in sepsis, NLR correlates with mortality outcomes [28]. Beyond these conditions, NLR has demonstrated predictive utility across a broad spectrum of infections. In odontogenic infections, a recent systematic review by Ghasemi et al. reported that elevated NLR correlated positively with disease severity, prolonged hospital stay, postoperative antibiotic requirements, and total antibiotic dose, with an optimal cut-off of 5.19 distinguishing severe from non-severe cases [29]. In Ludwig's angina specifically, NLR predicted both disease severity and mortality (sensitivity 70.8%, specificity 55.5%), and in severe oral and maxillofacial space infections, higher NLR values were associated with greater organ involvement and complications [29]. Parallel evidence from neonatal sepsis further reinforces the diagnostic value of NLR in infectious states: a meta-analysis of 30 studies encompassing 2328 septic neonates by Chen et al. demonstrated significantly elevated NLR in neonates with sepsis compared with healthy controls (pooled SMD = 1.81; 95% CI 1.14–2.48), with a pooled sensitivity of 79% and specificity of 91%, and this elevation persisted when septic neonates were compared with neonates with suspected but culture-negative sepsis or with other ICU-admitted neonates [30]. Collectively, these findings across diverse infectious and inflammatory conditions—including odontogenic infections, sepsis, and now leprosy reactions—support a unifying concept that NLR reflects a common pathophysiological axis of neutrophil-predominant systemic inflammation coupled with stress-induced lymphopenia [29,30]. In the context of ENL, where acute inflammatory flares are driven by neutrophil recruitment, immune-complex deposition, complement activation, and cytokine release, an elevated NLR may capture the underlying immunological dysregulation more sensitively than individual leukocyte parameters [7,24].

Our synthesis of data from five observational studies encompassing 162 patients with ENL and 423 patients with non-reactional leprosy across Brazil, Indonesia, India, and Saudi Arabia [9,11,[13], [14], [15]] demonstrates a consistently elevated NLR in patients experiencing ENL compared to those with stable multibacillary leprosy without reactions. Across all included studies, the mean NLR in ENL patients ranged from 6.56 to 11.71, whereas non-reactional leprosy patients exhibited substantially lower values ranging from 2.00 to 3.68 [9,11,[13], [14], [15]]. This consistent pattern across geographically diverse populations and different study designs supports the biological plausibility that systemic neutrophil-predominant inflammation characterises ENL episodes. The magnitude of difference observed was clinically substantial, with most studies reporting approximately three-to five-fold elevations in mean NLR among ENL patients. For instance, Gomes et al. [9] reported a mean NLR of 9.7 in ENL patients compared to 2.0 in non-reactional patients, while Vaishnani et al. [13] found values of 11.71 versus 3.68 respectively. The consistency of these findings strengthens the evidence that NLR reflects genuine immunological differences between reactional and non-reactional states rather than population-specific or methodological artefacts.

Evidence synthesised in this review originates from geographically diverse regions with varying leprosy endemicity. Brazil and India are among countries with the highest leprosy case detection rates globally and have established national leprosy control programmes, while Indonesia ranks third worldwide in new case detection [1,2]. Despite these geographical differences, the consistent finding of elevated NLR in ENL across all regions suggests that the immunological basis of this reaction transcends population-specific genetic or environmental factors. This geographical consistency supports the potential generalisability of NLR as a biomarker across diverse endemic settings. However, all studies were conducted in hospital-based or referral centre settings, which may introduce selection bias toward more severe or complicated cases.

Methodological approaches across studies varied considerably, ranging from cross-sectional observational designs to retrospective analytical comparisons, with sample sizes varying from 25 to 246 patients. This methodological heterogeneity introduces both strengths and limitations. The variety of study designs enhances external validity by capturing NLR differences across different clinical contexts, but also complicates direct comparisons. Several methodological considerations warrant attention, including variability in timing of blood sampling relative to ENL onset, differences in exclusion criteria regarding concurrent medications (particularly corticosteroids), lack of uniform description of laboratory methods for determining leukocyte counts, and heterogeneity in diagnostic criteria for ENL. Despite these variations, the qualitative consistency of findings universally demonstrating elevated NLR in ENL patients suggests that the observed association is robust across different methodological approaches.

The consistent elevation of NLR in ENL patients suggests several potential clinical applications. NLR could serve as an accessible screening tool to identify patients at higher risk for developing ENL, particularly among newly diagnosed multibacillary leprosy patients initiating multidrug therapy[31]. NLR monitoring during ENL episodes may provide objective assessment of treatment response and guide corticosteroid tapering decisions, complementing clinical assessment by providing quantitative evidence of inflammatory resolution [7]. Additionally, NLR could assist in distinguishing ENL from other conditions presenting with erythematous skin lesions in leprosy patients, including type 1 reversal reactions, particularly in resource-limited settings where skin biopsy may not be readily available [16]. The practical advantages of NLR include its derivation from routine complete blood count testing widely available even in resource-limited endemic settings and its minimal additional cost beyond standard haematological monitoring [10,24].

While this review focused specifically on NLR, contextualising these findings within the broader landscape of inflammatory biomarkers provides important perspective. Previous research has investigated numerous systemic markers in ENL including erythrocyte sedimentation rate, C-reactive protein, serum amyloid A, and pro-inflammatory cytokines [16,19,32]. C-reactive protein and erythrocyte sedimentation rate are non-specifically elevated in many ENL patients but lack specificity to reliably distinguish ENL from other inflammatory conditions [19]. Cytokine profiling provides more mechanistic insights but requires specialised laboratory infrastructure not feasible for routine clinical use in most endemic settings [7,32]. In this context, NLR occupies a middle ground between non-specific acute phase reactants and mechanistically informative but technically demanding cytokine assays, offering a pragmatic balance of clinical utility and feasibility for widespread implementation.

Several important limitations must be acknowledged. The relatively small number of included studies and modest total sample sizes limit statistical power and constrain opportunities for meaningful subgroup analyses. Geographical representation was uneven, with no included studies from several high-burden countries such as Bangladesh, Ethiopia, or the Philippines, limiting confidence in generalisability. All included studies employed observational designs, which limits causal inference, and substantial methodological heterogeneity introduces potential sources of variability. Most included studies did not comprehensively account for potential confounding factors such as concurrent infections, nutritional status, or chronic diseases that can influence peripheral blood leukocyte counts independently of ENL status. Furthermore, no included study prospectively evaluated NLR as a predictor of ENL onset, which limits conclusions regarding its predictive utility. While this review establishes that NLR differs significantly between ENL and non-reactional patients at a group level, determining optimal cut-off values for clinical decision-making requires further validation in independent cohorts.

The findings highlight several priorities for future research. Large-scale prospective cohort studies measuring NLR serially throughout multidrug therapy are needed to determine whether baseline values predict subsequent ENL development. Interventional studies examining NLR dynamics during ENL treatment could clarify whether NLR changes correlate with clinical response and whether NLR-guided therapy algorithms improve outcomes. Comparative studies evaluating NLR alongside other candidate biomarkers would clarify relative performance. Studies exploring NLR in combination with other CBC-derived ratios such as platelet-to-lymphocyte ratio and lymphocyte-to-monocyte ratio may provide improved diagnostic panels [11]. Health economic analyses evaluating cost-effectiveness of NLR-based strategies would inform policy decisions about implementation in resource-limited settings.

Conclusion

This systematic review and meta-analysis demonstrates that NLR is significantly elevated in ENL compared with non-reactional leprosy, with a pooled mean difference of 5.79 (95% CI 4.58–6.99; P < 0.00001). The consistency of this finding across geographically diverse populations—encompassing 162 ENL and 423 non-reactional leprosy patients from Brazil, Indonesia, India, and Saudi Arabia—supports NLR as a robust marker of systemic inflammation in type 2 leprosy reactions. Given that NLR is derived from routine complete blood counts and requires no additional cost or specialised infrastructure, it holds promise as an accessible adjunctive biomarker for ENL screening, diagnosis, and treatment monitoring, particularly in resource-limited endemic settings. Prospective validation studies with standardised methodologies are essential to establish optimal cut-off values, assess predictive performance in diverse populations, and evaluate whether NLR-guided management strategies improve patient outcomes.
