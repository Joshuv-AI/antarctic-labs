Dynamic Grammar-Compressed Self-Index in δ \delta -Optimal Space
Takaaki Nishimoto, Yasuo Tabei

Abstract
A compressed self-index stores a string in compressed form while supporting locate queries without decompression. For highly repetitive strings—arising in web crawls, versioned documents, and genomic collections—static self-indexes can match the δ \delta -optimal lower bound of Ω ⁡ ( δ ​ log ⁡ ( n ​ log ⁡ σ / ( δ ​ log ​ n ) ) ​ log ​ n ) \Omega(\delta\log(n\log\sigma/(\delta\log n))\log n) bits up to constant factors, where n n is the string length, σ \sigma is the alphabet size, and δ \delta is the substring complexity. Their dynamic counterparts, however, remain scarce: every existing dynamic self-index either fails to attain δ \delta -optimal space, pays at least Θ ⁡ ( log ⁡ n ) \Theta(\log n) time per reported occurrence during locate, or exposes the longest common prefix (LCP) of the text inside its update time. We present the dynamic RR-index , a dynamic grammar-compressed self-index built on the restricted recompression run-length straight-line program (RLSLP). To our knowledge, it is the first dynamic self-index to attain δ \delta -optimal space. The index occupies expected 𝒪 ⁡ ( δ ​ log ⁡ ( n ​ log ⁡ σ / ( δ ​ log ​ n ) ) ​ log ​ n ) \mathcal{O}(\delta\log(n\log\sigma/(\delta\log n))\log n) bits, answers locate queries in expected 𝒪 ⁡ ( m + log ⁡ m ​ log 2 ​ n + 𝗈𝖼𝖼 ⁡ ( log ⁡ n / log ⁡ log ⁡ n ) ) \mathcal{O}(m+\log m\log^{2}n+\mathsf{occ}(\log n/\log\log n)) time—where m m is the pattern length and 𝗈𝖼𝖼 \mathsf{occ} is the number of occurrences—and supports insertions and deletions of a length- m ′ m^{\prime} substring in expected amortized 𝒪 ⁡ ( m ′ ​ log 2 ​ n + log 3 ⁡ n ) \mathcal{O}(m^{\prime}\log^{2}n+\log^{3}n) time, with no dependence on the LCP. On eleven highly repetitive corpora, including a 37 37 GB Wikipedia dump and a 59 59 GB human-chromosome collection, the dynamic RR-index is up to 77 × 77\times faster than the dynamic r-index on updates and up to 11 × 11\times faster than other dynamic indexes on locate.

Introduction

Datasets composed of
highly repetitive
strings—those with many repeated substrings—have grown rapidly in recent years.
Typical examples include web pages collected by crawlers
[
14
]
, version-controlled documents such as Wikipedia with its complete edit history
[
46
]
, and, perhaps most notably, biological sequences such as collections of human genomes
[
42
,
45
]
.
These datasets are not static: edits, deletions, and new releases arrive continuously.
Yet the methods practitioners rely on most heavily, such as the r-index
[
15
]
, a compressed index supporting locate queries, are
static
: reflecting even a single edit requires rebuilding the index from scratch.
There is therefore a strong and growing need to develop
dynamic
compressed self-indexes for highly repetitive strings, which can be updated efficiently without rebuilding.

A practical solution must simultaneously (i) use space proportional to the repetitiveness of the string, (ii) answer locate queries quickly, and (iii) support string insertions and deletions without rebuilding.
The third requirement has proven difficult: while compressed
self-indexes
—data structures that represent the string in compressed form while answering locate queries directly—are well understood in the static case, their dynamic counterparts remain challenging to develop.

For highly repetitive strings, compressed self-indexes aim to approach the
\delta
-optimal space
of
\mathcal{O}(\delta\log(n\log\sigma/(\delta\log n))\log n)
bits established by Kociumaka et al.
[
27
]
, where
n
is the string length,
\sigma
is the alphabet size, and
\delta
is the
substring complexity
of the string—a repetitiveness measure satisfying
\delta\ll n
on genomic and versioned corpora.
A line of specialized self-indexes—grammar-based
[
7
,
27
,
26
]
, LZ-based
[
8
]
, block-tree–based
[
2
,
29
,
27
]
, and run-length BWT (RLBWT)–based such as the r-index
[
15
]
and OptBWTR
[
36
,
3
]
—has closed much of this gap, with several attaining
\delta
-optimal space up to constant factors.
None, however, supports updates.

Only a handful of
dynamic
self-indexes have been proposed, and each sacrifices at least one of the three requirements (Table
1
in Appendix
A
gives the full comparison).
The dynamic FM-index
[
44
,
31
]
extends the Burrows–Wheeler Transform (BWT)
[
5
]
but uses
\mathcal{O}(n\log\sigma)
bits, oblivious to repetitiveness, with an
\mathcal{O}(\log^{2+\epsilon}n)
per-occurrence locate factor.
Grammar-based dynamic indexes—the SE-index
[
35
]
, TST-index-d
[
40
]
, and Gawrychowski et al.
[
16
]
—either pay
(\log n\log^{*}n)^{2}
-type factors in locate/update or require
\Omega(n\log n)
bits.
A recent dynamic r-index
[
38
,
39
]
uses
\mathcal{O}(r\log n)
bits (
r
the number of BWT runs), but its update time
\mathcal{O}((m^{\prime}+L_{\max})\log n)
depends on the maximum value
L_{\max}\leq n
of the longest common prefix (LCP) array—the length of the longest repeated substring—which can grow independently of
\delta
and
r
as versioned copies accumulate.
In short, every existing dynamic self-index either fails to compress to
\delta
-optimal space, pays at least
\Theta(\log n)
time per reported occurrence during locate, or exposes the LCP inside update time. We address this gap.

Our contributions.

We present the
dynamic RR-index
, a dynamic grammar-compressed self-index built on the
restricted recompression
RLSLP of Jeż
[
19
]
and Kociumaka et al.
[
28
,
27
]
.
Our starting observation is that the derivation tree of a restricted recompression admits a compact
DAG
representation whose explicit-node count is tied to
\delta
rather than to
r
or
g
.
Moreover, insertions and deletions perturb this DAG only along two short
popped sequences
[
35
,
18
]
,
independently of the maximum LCP value.
Combined with two-dimensional range reporting
[
4
,
33
]
and with a path-cached ancestor field that accelerates locate (
\cref
sec:improvement), this design simultaneously achieves
\delta
-optimal space and a sub-logarithmic per-occurrence locate bound.
To our knowledge, the dynamic RR-index is the first dynamic self-index to attain
\delta
-optimal space.
Concretely, it occupies expected
\delta
-optimal space—
\mathcal{O}(\delta\log(n\log\sigma/(\delta\log n))\log n)
bits—and answers locate queries in expected
\mathcal{O}(m+\log m\log^{2}n+\mathsf{occ}(\log n/\log\log n))
time, where
m
is the pattern length and
\mathsf{occ}
is the number of occurrences.
Insertions and deletions of a length-
m^{\prime}
substring are supported in expected amortized
\mathcal{O}(m^{\prime}\log^{2}n+\log^{3}n)
time.
We implement and evaluate the dynamic RR-index on eleven highly repetitive corpora, including the
37
GB
enwiki
Wikipedia dump and the
59
GB
chr19
genomic collection, and we show that it is up to
77\times
faster than the dynamic r-index on updates and up to
11\times
faster than other dynamic indexes on locate, while using working memory consistent with scaling by
\delta
.
A C++ reference implementation will be released as open-source together with the camera-ready version.

–

Preliminaries

Basic notation.

Let
T
be a string of length
n\geq 2
over a totally ordered alphabet
\Sigma
of size
\sigma=n^{\mathcal{O}(1)}
.
Let
|T|=n
.
We denote the empty string by
\varepsilon
, the
i
-th character of
T
by
T[i]
,
the substring of
T
from position
i
to position
j
by
T[i..j]
,
and the reverse of
T
by
\mathsf{reverse}(T)=T[n],T[n-1],\ldots,T[1]
.
A prefix (resp. suffix) of
T
is a substring beginning at position
1
(resp. ending at position
n
);
for a string
P\in\Sigma^{+}
,

T\cdot P
denotes concatenation. We use the standard lexicographic order on
\Sigma^{*}
.
The set of occurrence positions of
P
in
T
is
\mathit{Occ}(T,P)=\{i\in\{1,\ldots,n-|P|+1\}\mid T[i..i+|P|-1]=P\}
. For a nonempty set
\mathcal{S}
of integers,
\min\mathcal{S}
and
\max\mathcal{S}
denote its minimum and maximum. For a path
\mathbb{P}
in a graph, let
|\mathbb{P}|
denote the number of edges on
\mathbb{P}
.

Model of computation.

We will use base-2 logarithms throughout this paper unless otherwise indicated.
Our computation model is a unit-cost word RAM
[
17
]
with multiplication, randomization, and machine word size
B=\Theta(\log n)
bits.
We measure space in machine words; multiplying by
B
gives the space in bits.
We analyze our randomized algorithms against an
oblivious adversary

[
6
]
: the sequence of updates to
T
is chosen in advance, without access to the random bits used by our algorithms.

Substring complexity.

Substring complexity
[
43
,
7
]
measures the repetitiveness of a string.
For each
d\geq 1
, let
\mathcal{S}_{d}=\{T[i..i+d-1]\mid 1\leq i\leq n-d+1\}
be the set of length-
d
substrings of
T
.
The
substring complexity
of
T
is
\delta=\max_{1\leq d\leq n}|\mathcal{S}_{d}|/d
.

The
\delta
-optimal space
is
\Theta(\delta\log\tfrac{n\log\sigma}{\delta\log n}\log n)
bits: every string can be encoded in the bits, and this bound is tight in
n
,
\sigma
, and
\delta

[
27
]
.

Grammar-based compression.

A grammar-based compression of
T
is a context-free grammar
\mathcal{G}=(\mathcal{V},\Sigma,\mathcal{D},E)
that generates exactly
T
, where
\mathcal{V}=\{X_{1},X_{2},\ldots,X_{g}\}
is a set of
nonterminals
,
\Sigma
is the alphabet of
T
, and
\mathcal{D}
is a set of
production rules

X_{i}\rightarrow\mathsf{expr}_{i}
with
\mathsf{expr}_{i}\in(\mathcal{V}\cup\Sigma)^{*}
.
A rule
X_{i}\rightarrow\mathsf{expr}_{i}
expands
X_{i}
to the sequence
\mathsf{expr}_{i}
.
The
start symbol

E\in\mathcal{V}
does not appear on any right-hand side, while every other nonterminal does appear on at least one.
To avoid cycles, we require
j<i
whenever
X_{j}
appears in
\mathsf{expr}_{i}
.
In addition,
\mathsf{expr}_{i}\neq\mathsf{expr}_{j}
for two distinct integers
i,j\in\{1,2,\ldots,g\}
.

Each nonterminal
X\in\mathcal{V}
derives a substring of
T
; in particular, the start symbol
E
derives
T
itself.
The tree whose root is labeled
E
and whose leaves spell out
T
is the
derivation tree
of
\mathcal{G}
.
Let
\mathsf{val}(X)
denote the substring of
T
derived by
X\in\mathcal{V}
,
and let
\mathsf{val}(X_{1},X_{2},\ldots,X_{d})=\mathsf{val}(X_{1})\cdot\mathsf{val}(X_{2})\cdot\ldots\cdot\mathsf{val}(X_{d})

for nonterminals
X_{1},X_{2},\ldots,X_{d}\in\mathcal{V}
.

A
straight-line program
(SLP)
[
20
]
is a grammar-based compression in which each nonterminal produces either a character or a pair of nonterminals.
A
run-length SLP (RLSLP)

[
34
]
is an SLP whose nonterminals can additionally produce a repetition of a single nonterminal.
Specifically,
\mathcal{D}\subseteq\mathcal{V}\times(\Sigma\cup\mathcal{V}^{+})
, and each production rule in
\mathcal{D}
takes one of the following forms:
(i)
X_{i}\rightarrow c
for
c\in\Sigma
;
(ii)
X_{i}\rightarrow X_{j}X_{k}
(
j,k<i
) for distinct nonterminals
X_{j},X_{k}\in\mathcal{V}
;
(iii)
X_{i}\rightarrow X_{j}
(
j<i
) for
X_{j}\in\mathcal{V}
;
(iv)
X_{i}\rightarrow(X_{j})^{d}
(
j<i
), where
(X_{j})^{d}
denotes
d\geq 2
successive copies of
X_{j}\in\mathcal{V}
.
Several algorithms build an RLSLP for a given string, e.g.,
signature encoding

[
34
]
,
recompression

[
19
]
,
signature grammar

[
8
]
, and
restricted block compression

[
26
]
.

Restricted Recompression

Figure 1:

An illustration of the derivation tree of a restricted recompression RLSLP (left), and the corresponding production rules (right).
Each node is depicted as a rectangle enclosing its corresponding nonterminal
X_{i}
.
The blue integer shown at the upper right of each node is the value of
\mathsf{assign}(X_{i})
.

Restricted recompression
[
27
,
28
]
is a randomized algorithm that constructs an RLSLP
\mathcal{G}^{R}
whose derivation tree is
height-balanced
(i.e., all children of every node have the same height).
Let
H
denote the height of the derivation tree of
\mathcal{G}^{R}
.
For each
h\in\{0,1,\ldots,H\}
, the nonterminals labeling the nodes at height
h
, read left to right, form a sequence
S^{h}
.
In this paper,
\mathcal{G}^{R}
is called
restricted recompression RLSLP
.

Restricted recompression builds the derivation tree bottom-up, processing
S^{0},S^{1},\ldots,S^{H}
in order.
While processing
S^{h}
, each newly introduced nonterminal
X_{i}
is assigned a value
\mathsf{assign}(X_{i})\in\{-1,0,1\}
as follows.
Let
\mu(h)=(8/7)^{\lceil h/2\rceil-1}
.
If
|\mathsf{val}(X_{i})|\leq\mu(h)
, then
\mathsf{assign}(X_{i})
is drawn uniformly at random from
\{0,1\}
; otherwise,
\mathsf{assign}(X_{i})=-1
.
The
\mathsf{assign}
values partition
S^{h}
into segments.
Each segment is one of the following:
(i) a maximal run of the same nonterminal
X_{i}
with
\mathsf{assign}(X_{i})\in\{0,1\}
;
(ii) two distinct nonterminals
X_{i}
and
X_{j}
with
\mathsf{assign}(X_{i})=1
and
\mathsf{assign}(X_{j})=0
; or
(iii) a single nonterminal
X_{i}
with
\mathsf{assign}(X_{i})\in\{-1,0,1\}
.

Figure
1
shows an example of the derivation tree for
T=\mathrm{abaaacbaaa}
.
Since the actual values of
\mu(h)
would make the tree too large to display, we use
\lfloor\mu(0)\rfloor=\lfloor\mu(1)\rfloor=1
and
\lfloor\mu(2)\rfloor=\lfloor\mu(3)\rfloor=\cdots=\lfloor\mu(8)\rfloor=10
in the figure.

The following lemma shows that
H=\mathcal{O}(\log n)
with high probability.

Lemma 1
.

H\leq 2(w+1)\log_{8/7}(4n)+2
holds with probability at least
1-1/n^{w}
for every integer
w\geq 1
.

Proof.

See Appendix
B.1
.
∎

For a user-defined constant
w\geq 2
,
we assume
H\leq 2(w+1)\log_{8/7}(4n)+2
throughout this paper.
By Lemma
1
,
this bound holds with high probability when
w
is sufficiently large.

Let
M
denote the number of nonterminals in
\mathcal{V}
excluding those that produce a single nonterminal.
By Proposition V.19 of
[
27
]
,
\mathbb{E}[M]=\mathcal{O}(\delta\log\tfrac{n\log\sigma}{\delta\log n})
.
Appendix
B.2
gives a detailed description of the construction.
Using this bound on
M
, Section
4
represents the restricted recompression RLSLP as a DAG that achieves expected
\delta
-optimal space.

Dynamic RR-index

Figure 2:
(Left) DAG for the derivation tree of Figure
1
: each rectangle is a node
u_{i}

X_{i}

The
dynamic RR-index
is built on the restricted recompression RLSLP
\mathcal{G}^{R}=(\mathcal{V},\Sigma,\mathcal{D},E)
introduced in the previous section.
It consists of two components: (i) a directed acyclic graph (DAG) representing the derivation tree, and (ii) a set of points in two-dimensional space corresponding to explicit nodes in the DAG. The details of these two components are described in the following subsection.

4.1
DAG Representation of Derivation Tree and Path Decomposition

DAG representation.

The derivation tree of
\mathcal{G}^{R}
is represented as a directed acyclic multigraph
[
34
,
35
]

\mathfrak{D}=(\mathcal{U},\mathcal{E},\mathrm{src},\mathrm{dst},\mathcal{L}_{U},\mathcal{L}_{E})
obtained by merging nodes labeled with the same nonterminal:
\mathcal{L}_{U}:\mathcal{U}\to\mathcal{V}
is a bijection between nodes and nonterminals,
\mathcal{E}
is the set of directed edges; for
e\in\mathcal{E}
,
\mathrm{src}(e)
is its source (corresponding to a parent node in the derivation tree) and
\mathrm{dst}(e)
its destination (a child node), and the outgoing edges of
u_{i}
are in one-to-one correspondence with those of any node labeled
\mathcal{L}_{U}(u_{i})
in the derivation tree. The label
\mathcal{L}_{E}(e)\in\{0,1,\ldots,n-1\}
is
j-i
, where
i
and
j
are the starting positions of the substrings derived from
\mathrm{src}(e)
and
\mathrm{dst}(e)
. Let
d^{+}(u)
denote the outdegree of
u
. Figure
2
(left) illustrates this for Figure
1
.

Path decomposition.

We partition
\mathcal{U}
into

\mathcal{U}_{\mathsf{expl}}=\{u\in\mathcal{U}\mid d^{+}(u)\neq 1\}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\mathcal{U}_{\mathsf{impl}}=\mathcal{U}\setminus\mathcal{U}_{\mathsf{expl}}
.
Nodes in
\mathcal{U}_{\mathsf{expl}}
are called
explicit
, and nodes in
\mathcal{U}_{\mathsf{impl}}
are called
implicit
.
After removing every edge whose source is explicit, the resulting subgraph
\mathfrak{D}^{\prime}
consists of disjoint directed paths, each ending at an explicit node (see Figure
2
, center).

For each explicit node
u\in\mathcal{U}_{\mathsf{expl}}
, let
\mathbb{P}_{u}
denote the directed path in
\mathfrak{D}^{\prime}
that ends at
u
.
Writing
\langle u,k\rangle
for the implicit node
k
edges before
u
on this path,
\mathbb{P}_{u}
has the form

\mathbb{P}_{u}:\langle u,|\mathbb{P}_{u}|\rangle\rightarrow\langle u,|\mathbb{P}_{u}|-1\rangle\rightarrow\cdots\rightarrow\langle u,1\rangle\rightarrow u.

Therefore,
\mathbb{P}_{u}
can be encoded as the pair
(u,|\mathbb{P}_{u}|)
.

The
path decomposition
of
\mathfrak{D}
is the set
\Pi=\{\mathbb{P}_{u}\mid u\in\mathcal{U}_{\mathsf{expl}}\}
, so
|\Pi|=|\mathcal{U}_{\mathsf{expl}}|=M
(see Section
3
).
The directed edges of
\mathfrak{D}
partition into two kinds:
intra-path edges
, which are the edges of
\mathfrak{D}^{\prime}
(equivalently, edges between consecutive nodes on the same path in
\Pi
), and
inter-path edges
, whose source and destination lie on different paths in
\Pi
.

4.2
Point Representation of Explicit Nodes

Figure 3:
Set
\mathcal{P}
: each nonterminal represents the corresponding point in two-dimensional space.

To support efficient locate queries, we map each explicit node
u_{i}\in\mathcal{U}_{\mathsf{expl}}
to a point
p_{i}
in two-dimensional space at
x
-coordinate
L_{i}
and
y
-coordinate
R_{i}
, defined from the production rule of
X_{i}=\mathcal{L}_{U}(u_{i})
as follows: if
X_{i}\rightarrow X_{j}X_{k}
then
L_{i}=\mathsf{reverse}(\mathsf{val}(X_{j}))
and
R_{i}=\mathsf{val}(X_{k})
; if
X_{i}\rightarrow c
for
c\in\Sigma
then
L_{i}=c
and
R_{i}=\varepsilon
; if
X_{i}\rightarrow(X_{j})^{d}
with
d\geq 2
then
L_{i}=\mathsf{reverse}(\mathsf{val}(X_{j}))
and
R_{i}=\mathsf{val}((X_{j})^{d-1})
.
Let
\mathcal{P}
be the resulting point set, and let
\mathcal{X}
and
\mathcal{Y}
be
\mathcal{U}_{\mathsf{expl}}
sorted lexicographically by
L_{i}
and
R_{i}
, with ties broken by
u_{i}
.
This point representation will later allow us to find the relevant explicit nodes
for locate queries by two-dimensional range reporting, i.e., by queries that return all
points in a given axis-aligned rectangle.
Figure
3
illustrates
\mathcal{P}
,
\mathcal{X}
, and
\mathcal{Y}
for the DAG in Figure
2
.

4.3
Dynamic Data Structures for the DAG and Point Set

The DAG
\mathfrak{D}
is indexed by
\mathscr{D}_{\mathsf{DAG}}(\Pi)
and the point set
\mathcal{P}
by
\mathscr{D}_{\mathsf{grid}}(\mathcal{P})
.

Data structure for path decomposition.

\mathscr{D}_{\mathsf{DAG}}(\Pi)
consists of a doubly linked list and two types of hash tables
[
11
]
.
Intra-path edges are implicit in the list structure; inter-path edges are stored in per-path hash tables.
For each explicit node
u\in\mathcal{U}_{\mathsf{expl}}
with
X_{i}:=\mathcal{L}_{U}(u)
, the list stores one element associated with the endpoint
u
that records: a pair
(u,|\mathbb{P}_{u}|)
for recovering
\mathbb{P}_{u}
, the production rule
X_{i}\rightarrow\mathsf{expr}_{i}
, the height of
u
,
|\mathsf{val}(X_{i})|
, and

\mathsf{assign}(\mathcal{L}_{U}(u_{j}))
for each node
u_{j}
on
\mathbb{P}_{u}
(occupying
\mathcal{O}(H)
bits since
|\mathbb{P}_{u}|\leq H+1
and each label is
2
bits).
For each path
\mathbb{P}_{u}\in\Pi
, the set
\mathcal{E}_{\mathsf{expl}}(\mathbb{P}_{u})=\{e\in\mathcal{E}\mid\mathrm{src}(e)\in\mathcal{U}_{\mathsf{expl}},\;\mathrm{dst}(e)\text{ lies on }\mathbb{P}_{u}\}
is indexed by a per-path hash table whose entry for
e
has key
\mathrm{src}(e)
and value
\mathrm{dst}(e)
(only distinct key-value pairs are stored); the keys are also linked in a doubly linked list sorted by the height of the corresponding nodes and indexed by a
list indexing data structure

[
10
]
.
A global hash table indexes the
M
production rules with key
\mathsf{expr}_{i}
(encoded in
\mathcal{O}(B)
bits) and value
X_{i}
.

Data structure for point set.

We index
\mathcal{P}\subseteq\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}
by Blelloch’s dynamic data structure
\mathscr{D}_{\mathsf{grid}}(\mathcal{P})

[
4
]
for range reporting and point insertion/deletion, which requires
\mathcal{O}(1)
-time comparison of elements of
\mathcal{X}
and
\mathcal{Y}
.
We maintain
order maintenance data structures

[
9
]
on
\mathcal{X}
and
\mathcal{Y}
to provide
\mathcal{O}(1)
-time comparison and
\mathcal{O}(\log M)
-time insertion/deletion, and list indexing data structures for
\mathcal{O}(\log M)
-time access; each coordinate is a pointer to the corresponding list element of
\Pi
, so each point uses
\mathcal{O}(B)
bits. Each list element of
\mathscr{D}_{\mathsf{DAG}}(\Pi)
in turn stores pointers into the list indexing and order maintenance structures for
\mathcal{X}
and
\mathcal{Y}
.
Range reporting takes
\mathcal{O}(\log M+\mathit{rocc}(\log M/\log\log M))
time where
\mathit{rocc}
is the number of reported points, and a point insertion or deletion takes amortized
\mathcal{O}(\log M)
time.

4.4
Space Complexity

The doubly linked list contributes
\mathcal{O}(M(H+B))
bits; the global hash table, the per-path hash tables (which hold
\mathcal{O}(M)
distinct key-value pairs in total since every DAG node has at most two children) together with their list indexing structures, and
\mathscr{D}_{\mathsf{grid}}(\mathcal{P})
with its auxiliary structures each contribute
\mathcal{O}(MB)
bits. Summing, the dynamic RR-index occupies
\mathcal{O}(M(H+B))
bits.
Since
B=\Theta(\log n)
and
\mathbb{E}[M]=\mathcal{O}(\delta\log\frac{n\log\sigma}{\delta\log n})
, the total space is
\delta
-optimal in expectation under the assumption that
H=\mathcal{O}(\log n)
.

5
Locate Query Algorithm

This section presents the algorithm answering a locate query for a pattern
P
of length

, establishing the baseline complexity bound (Theorem
5

then improves the per-occurrence factor to
\log n/\log\log n
via ancestor-path caching (Theorem

).
For
m\geq 2
, the algorithm proceeds in four steps, each detailed in a subsection below: from the derivation tree we extract a specific
core

[
32
]
of
P
as the

[
12
,
18
]
of
\rho
runs, build
\rho

\mathcal{P}
, apply range reporting to obtain the
primary occurrences

]

\mathit{Occ}(T,P)
by traversing the DAG to the root. The case

is handled separately in Section

.

5.1
Step (i): Computing the Popped Sequence

This step computes the popped sequence of
P
, a specific core of
P
whose run-length encoding has
\mathcal{O}(\min\{m,H\})
runs.
We first introduce what a core is, and then introduce the popped sequence as a specific construction.

Let
Q=X_{1},X_{2},\ldots,X_{d}
be a sequence of nonterminals (each
X_{i}\in\mathcal{V}
) such that
\mathsf{val}(Q)=P
.
We say that
Q
is
embedded
in the derivation tree of
\mathcal{G}^{R}
at position
s\in\{1,2,\ldots,n\}
if, for every
i\in\{1,2,\ldots,d\}
, some node labeled
X_{i}
in the derivation tree derives the substring
T[s_{i}..s_{i}+|\mathsf{val}(X_{i})|-1]
of
T
, where
s_{i}:=s+\sum_{b=1}^{i-1}|\mathsf{val}(X_{b})|
.
Such an embedding witnesses an occurrence of
P
at position
s
(i.e.,
s\in\mathit{Occ}(T,P)
).
We call
Q
a core of
P
if
Q
is embedded at every position
s\in\mathit{Occ}(T,P)
.

Since a core of
P
is not unique, we use a specific one called the popped sequence of
P
, whose
\rho
runs satisfy
\rho=\mathcal{O}(\min\{m,H\})
and
\mathbb{E}[\rho]=\mathcal{O}(\log m)
.
It is computed from the derivation tree of
\mathcal{G}^{R}
by the algorithm of
[
12
]
, invoked in phase (i) of Section
5.5.2
.
See Appendix
C.1
for the details of the popped sequence.
The next step uses these
\rho

\rho
axis-aligned rectangles; Lemma
2
ensures this suffices for finding all primary occurrences.

5.2
Step (ii): Computing the Rectangles from the Popped Sequence

Primary occurrences and their rectangles.

A primary occurrence
[
35
]
of
P
for a split position
\ell\in\{1,2,\ldots,m-1\}
is a pair
(u_{i},\ell)
with
u_{i}\in\mathcal{U}_{\mathsf{expl}}
such that (i)
\mathsf{reverse}(P[1..\ell])
is a prefix of
L_{i}
and (ii)
P[\ell+1..m]
is a prefix of
R_{i}
; that is,
u_{i}
is an explicit node where an occurrence of
P
crosses the first boundary between substrings derived from
u_{i}
’s production rule.
We write
\mathit{pOcc}(P,\ell)\subseteq\mathcal{U}_{\mathsf{expl}}
for the set of such
u_{i}
at split position
\ell
.
Recall from Section
4.2
that
u_{i}
is mapped to the point
p_{i}
with
x
-coordinate
L_{i}
and
y
-coordinate
R_{i}
. The two prefix conditions constrain
L_{i}
and
R_{i}
, respectively, so
\mathit{pOcc}(P,\ell)
coincides with the set of points of
\mathcal{P}
in the axis-aligned rectangle
\mathsf{rect}_{\ell}=[x,x^{\prime}]\times[y,y^{\prime}]
, where
x,x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}
are the smallest and largest elements whose
x
-coordinate has
\mathsf{reverse}(P[1..\ell])
as a prefix, and
y,y^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Y}
are the analogous bounds for
P[\ell+1..m]
(if any of the four does not exist,
\mathsf{rect}_{\ell}
is empty).

Reducing to
\rho
rectangles.

Because the popped sequence is a core of
P
, only split positions at its run boundaries can yield a nonempty
\mathit{pOcc}(P,\ell)
, as formalized below.

Lemma 2

(Generalization of Lemma 7 in
[
35
]
)
.

Let
Q
be a core of a pattern
P
of length
m\geq 2
, and let
Q[1]
be the first nonterminal of
Q
.
Also, let
\rho
be the number of runs in the run-length encoding of
Q
.
For each
i\in\{1,\ldots,\rho-1\}
, let
\phi(i)
denote the length of the string derived from the first
i
runs.
Then, for any integer
\ell\in\{1,2,\ldots,m-1\}
,
if
\ell\not\in\{|\mathsf{val}(Q[1])|\}\cup\{\phi(i)\mid i=1,2,\ldots,\rho-1\}
,
then
\mathit{pOcc}(P,\ell)=\emptyset
.

Proof.

See Appendix
C.2
.
∎

Applying Lemma
2
to the popped sequence
Q
, this step constructs only the
\rho
rectangles
\mathsf{rect}_{|\mathsf{val}(Q[1])|},\mathsf{rect}_{\phi(1)},\mathsf{rect}_{\phi(2)},\ldots,\mathsf{rect}_{\phi(\rho-1)}
.

5.3
Step (iii): Range Reporting for Primary Occurrences

We apply a range reporting query on Blelloch’s data structure for
\mathcal{P}
(Section
4.3
) to each of the
\rho
rectangles; since
\mathit{pOcc}(P,\ell)
coincides with the points in
\mathsf{rect}_{\ell}
, taking the union yields the complete set
\bigcup_{\ell=1}^{m-1}\mathit{pOcc}(P,\ell)
of primary occurrences.

5.4
Step (iv): Converting Primary Occurrences to
\mathit{Occ}(T,P)

For
u_{i}\in\mathcal{U}
, let
\mathsf{vOcc}(u_{i})\subseteq\{0,1,\ldots,n-1\}
be the starting positions in
T
of the occurrences of
\mathcal{L}_{U}(u_{i})
in the derivation tree (i.e.,
j\in\mathsf{vOcc}(u_{i})
if and only if
\mathcal{L}_{U}(u_{i})
is embedded at position
j
). The following lemma converts primary occurrences into
\mathit{Occ}(T,P)
using
\mathsf{vOcc}
, distinguishing two cases by the production rule of
u_{i}
.

Lemma 3

(
[
35
]
)
.

Recall that
L_{i}
is the
x
-coordinate of
u_{i}
(defined in Section
4.2
).
For each
\ell\in\{1,2,\ldots,m-1\}
and each
u_{i}\in\mathit{pOcc}(P,\ell)
, define
\mathcal{Z}_{\ell}(u_{i})
according to the production rule of
u_{i}
:

•

if the rule has the form
X_{i}\rightarrow X_{j}X_{k}
with two distinct nonterminals,

\mathcal{Z}_{\ell}(u_{i})=\{q+|L_{i}|-\ell\mid q\in\mathsf{vOcc}(u_{i})\};

•

if the rule has the form
X_{i}\rightarrow(X_{k})^{d}
,

\mathcal{Z}_{\ell}(u_{i})=\Bigl\{q+j\cdot|L_{i}|-\ell\;\Big|\;q\in\mathsf{vOcc}(u_{i}),\ j\in\{1,2,\ldots,\lfloor\tfrac{|\mathsf{val}(X_{i})|-(m-\ell)}{|L_{i}|}\rfloor\}\Bigr\}.

Then,

\mathit{Occ}(T,P)=\bigcup_{\ell=1}^{m-1}\bigcup_{u_{i}\in\mathit{pOcc}(P,\ell)}\mathcal{Z}_{\ell}(u_{i}).

Moreover, the sets
\mathcal{Z}_{\ell}(u_{i})
are pairwise disjoint over all pairs
(u_{i},\ell)
with
u_{i}\in\mathit{pOcc}(P,\ell)
.

By Lemma
3
, taking the union
\bigcup_{\ell=1}^{m-1}\bigcup_{u_{i}\in\mathit{pOcc}(P,\ell)}\mathcal{Z}_{\ell}(u_{i})
yields
\mathit{Occ}(T,P)
, completing the locate query.

5.5
Implementation and Complexity Analysis

This subsection gives the full locate algorithm for
m\geq 1
, after first setting up the auxiliary queries it relies on.

5.5.1
Auxiliary queries on the DAG

For the locate algorithm we use two ingredients: Lemma
4
for traversing nodes and edges of the DAG, and random access, LCE, and reversed LCE queries on
T
for computing rectangle coordinates in phase (ii).
Although inter-path edges are stored without labels (Section
4.3
), each label can be reconstructed in
\mathcal{O}(1)
time from the source’s production rule (Observation
9
in Appendix
C.3
); each value in
\mathsf{vOcc}(u_{i})
then equals

plus the sum of edge labels along the corresponding root-to-

path. The following lemmas summarize the resulting queries.

Lemma 4
.

Using the doubly linked list for
\Pi
,
we can support the following four operations on a given node
u_{i}\in\mathcal{U}

lying on a path
\mathbb{P}\in\Pi
:

(i)

return the following information in
\mathcal{O}(1)
time:
(A) the production rule
X_{i}\rightarrow\mathsf{expr}_{i}
corresponding to
u_{i}
,
(B)
|\mathsf{val}(X_{i})|

(C) the height of
u_{i}
,
(D)
\mathsf{assign}(X_{i})
, and
(E) the children of
u_{i}
;

(ii)

return the outgoing edges of
u_{i}
with edge labels in
\mathcal{O}(1)
time per edge;

(iii)

return the directed edges with edge labels in
\mathcal{E}_{\mathsf{expl}}(\mathbb{P})
in
\mathcal{O}(1)
time per edge;

(iv)

return
\mathsf{vOcc}(u_{i})
in
\mathcal{O}(H|\mathsf{vOcc}(u_{i})|)
time if
u_{i}
is explicit.

Proof.

See Appendix
C.3
.
∎

Random access, LCE, and reversed LCE queries.

A random access query returns
T[i]
; an LCE (resp. reversed LCE) query returns the length of the longest common prefix (resp. suffix) of
T[i..n]
and
T[j..n]
(resp.
T[1..i]
and

Kempa and Kociumaka’s algorithms (Theorems 5.24 and 5.25 in
[
24

time; Lemma

enables the same traversal in our representation, and a modification computes a substring of length

starting at position

in
\mathcal{O}(H+\ell)
time.
Phase (ii) uses them to compare, via binary search, a suffix of
P
with
R_{s}
(or the reverse of a prefix of

with

) in
\mathcal{O}(H+\log M)
time per comparison.
See Appendix
C.4
for the details of the three queries.

5.5.2
Full locate algorithm and complexity bound

Combining Steps (i)–(iv) with the auxiliary queries of Section
5.5.1
yields the following.

Theorem 5
.

Given a pattern
P
of length
m\geq 1
, a locate query returning
\mathit{Occ}(T,P)
can be answered in expected

\mathcal{O}\bigl(m+\log m(H+\log M)\log M+\mathsf{occ}(H+\log M/\log\log M)\bigr)

time using the dynamic RR-index.

Proof.

If
m=1
, write
P=c
with
c\in\Sigma
. In
\mathcal{O}(1)
time, the global hash table tells us whether some
X_{i}\in\mathcal{V}
satisfies
X_{i}\rightarrow c
(and returns it if so); otherwise
\mathit{Occ}(T,P)=\emptyset
. The corresponding DAG node
u_{i}
is explicit because
X_{i}\rightarrow c
is not a single nonterminal, so Lemma
4
(iv) enumerates
\mathit{Occ}(T,P)=\mathsf{vOcc}(u_{i})
in
\mathcal{O}(\mathsf{occ}\cdot H)
time, which is absorbed into the stated bound.

For
m\geq 2
, we execute the four phases corresponding to Steps (i)–(iv).

Phase (i).

Compute the popped sequence
Q
of
P
, whose run-length encoding has
\rho
runs, by the algorithm of
[
12
]
(Appendix
C.5
) in expected
\mathcal{O}(m)
time.
If it fails,
\mathit{Occ}(T,P)=\emptyset
and we return
\emptyset
.

Phase (ii).

Compute the
\rho
rectangles
\mathsf{rect}_{|\mathsf{val}(Q[1])|},\mathsf{rect}_{\phi(1)},\ldots,\mathsf{rect}_{\phi(\rho-1)}
by binary search on
\mathcal{X}
and
\mathcal{Y}
for each of the four coordinates. Each of the
\mathcal{O}(\log M)
comparisons takes
\mathcal{O}(H+\log M)
time (Section
5.5.1
), so this phase runs in
\mathcal{O}(\rho(H+\log M)\log M)
time with
\mathbb{E}[\rho]=\mathcal{O}(\log m)
.

Phase (iii).

Issue
\rho
range reporting queries on
\mathcal{P}
to obtain
\bigcup_{\ell=1}^{m-1}\mathit{pOcc}(P,\ell)
in
\mathcal{O}(\rho\log M+\mathsf{occ}_{\mathsf{prim}}(\log M/\log\log M))
time, where
\mathsf{occ}_{\mathsf{prim}}\leq\mathsf{occ}
is the number of primary occurrences.

Phase (iv).

For each
(u_{i},\ell)
with
u_{i}\in\mathit{pOcc}(P,\ell)
, compute
\mathsf{vOcc}(u_{i})
in
\mathcal{O}(H|\mathsf{vOcc}(u_{i})|)
time (Lemma
4
(iv)) and return
\mathcal{Z}_{\ell}(u_{i})
as defined in Lemma
3
.
Since
\sum_{\ell}\sum_{u_{i}\in\mathit{pOcc}(P,\ell)}|\mathcal{Z}_{\ell}(u_{i})|=\mathsf{occ}
, phase (iv) takes
\mathcal{O}(\mathsf{occ}\cdot H)
time.

Summing the four phase bounds and absorbing the
\mathsf{occ}_{\mathsf{prim}}(\log M/\log\log M)
term of phase (iii) into phase (iv) via
\mathsf{occ}_{\mathsf{prim}}\leq\mathsf{occ}
gives the stated complexity.
∎

Faster Locate via Ancestor-Path Caching

Theorem 6

Given a pattern

Additional data structures.

Fix tunable constants
\alpha,\beta=\mathcal{O}(1)
controlling the length of a cached ancestor path and a stored coordinate prefix, respectively.
For each path
\mathbb{P}\in\Pi
with explicit endpoint
u_{i}\in\mathcal{U}_{\mathsf{expl}}

\mathcal{O}(B)
-bit fields:
(i) the pair

encoding the longest path

is the sum of edge labels along the path (if

does not have exactly one parent,
\mathbb{P}_{\mathsf{vOcc}}(u_{i})=(u_{i})
with

and

.
(ii) The first
\lceil\beta B/\log\sigma\rceil

and of

\mathbb{P}_{\mathsf{vOcc}}(u_{i})

5.6.1
Speeding up computation of
\mathsf{vOcc}(u_{i})

The cached paths
\mathbb{P}_{\mathsf{vOcc}}(u_{i})
let us compute
\mathsf{vOcc}(u_{i})
via the following recurrence.

Lemma 7
.

For an explicit node
u_{i}\in\mathcal{U}_{\mathsf{expl}}
, let
(u_{j},W)
be the encoding of
\mathbb{P}_{\mathsf{vOcc}}(u_{i})
and let
\mathbb{P}_{j}\in\Pi
be the path containing

\mathsf{vOcc}(u_{i})=\begin{cases}\bigcup_{e\in\mathcal{E}_{\mathsf{expl}}(\mathbb{P}_{j})}\{W+\mathcal{L}_{E}(e)+q\mid q\in\mathsf{vOcc}(\mathrm{src}(e))\}&\text{if }\mathcal{E}_{\mathsf{expl}}(\mathbb{P}_{j})\neq\emptyset,\\
\{W+1\}&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

Proof.

Lemma

and the property of edge labels stated in Section

.
∎

Guided by Lemma

to the element representing

6.1
Insertion Operation

Given a string
P^{\prime}\in\Sigma^{+}
of length
m^{\prime}=\mathcal{O}(n)
and a position
s\in\{1,2,\ldots,n\}
, the insertion operation produces
T^{\prime}
by inserting
P^{\prime}
into
T
at position
s
and updates the dynamic RR-index so that its RLSLP derives
T^{\prime}
.
For simplicity, we focus on
2\leq s\leq n-1
, so that
T^{\prime}
is the concatenation of
T[1..s-1]
,
P^{\prime}
, and
T[s..n]
; the boundary cases
s=1
and
s=n
are analogous.
Following the standard strategy of dynamic grammar-compressed data structures
[
40
,
35
,
37
,
34
]
, the update proceeds in three phases (see Appendix
D
for details).

Phase 1: constructing the derivation tree of
\mathcal{G}^{R^{\prime}}
.

The first phase constructs the derivation tree of an RLSLP
\mathcal{G}^{R^{\prime}}=(\mathcal{V}^{\prime},\Sigma^{\prime},\mathcal{D}^{\prime},E^{\prime})
deriving
T^{\prime}
by restricted recompression, computing
(H^{\prime}+1)
sequences
S^{\prime 0},S^{\prime 1},\ldots,S^{\prime H^{\prime}}
bottom-up; production rules are shared with
\mathcal{G}^{R}
whenever the right-hand sides agree.
For each nonterminal in
\mathcal{G}^{R^{\prime}}\setminus\mathcal{G}^{R}
, we insert the corresponding node into the DAG and update the dynamic data structures.
A naive construction is
\Omega(n+m^{\prime})
since the tree has
n+m^{\prime}
leaves. We reduce this cost using the popped sequences
Q_{L}
and
Q_{R}
of
T[1..s-1]
and
T[s..n]
: since each popped sequence is a core of its source,
Q_{L}
and
Q_{R}
are embedded in the derivation tree of
\mathcal{G}^{R^{\prime}}
at positions
1
and
s+m^{\prime}
as subtrees that need not be reconstructed.
Concretely, (i) compute
Q_{L}
and
Q_{R}
by
[
12
]
in
\mathcal{O}(H)
time; (ii) build
S^{\prime 0},\ldots,S^{\prime H^{\prime}}
in
\mathcal{O}(|\mathcal{T}_{\mathsf{diff}}|)
time, where
\mathcal{T}_{\mathsf{diff}}
collects (A) the ancestors of the embedded subtree roots, (B) the

leaves on

5
illustrates the embeddings.
The phase runs in expected amortized
\mathcal{O}(|\mathcal{T}_{\mathsf{diff}}|+|\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{expl}}|\max\{H,H^{\prime},\log M\}\log M+|\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{impl}}|)
time, where
\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{expl}}
(resp.
\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{impl}}

D.1
.

Phase 2: removing obsolete nonterminals.

For each nonterminal in
\mathcal{G}^{R}\setminus\mathcal{G}^{R^{\prime}}

This phase takes expected amortized
\mathcal{O}(|\mathcal{R}_{\mathsf{expl}}|\log M+|\mathcal{R}_{\mathsf{impl}}|)

D.2
for details.
Here,
\mathcal{R}_{\mathsf{expl}}
(respectively,
\mathcal{R}_{\mathsf{impl}}

\mathcal{G}^{R^{\prime}}
.

Phase 3: updating ancestor-path caches and coordinate prefixes.

For each explicit node in
\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{expl}}\cup\mathcal{U}_{\mathsf{change}}

\Pi
.
Here,

where

; see Appendix

for details.

Correctness and running time.

Appendix B
Details of Restricted Recompression

B.1
Proof of Lemma
1

Proof.

Fix integers
w\geq 1
and
h\geq 0
, and set
|S^{h}|:=1
for
h>H
; under this extension,
|S^{h}|=1\iff H\leq h
.
By Lemma V.10 of
[
27
]
,
\mathbb{E}[|S^{h}|]<1+\tfrac{4n}{\mu(h+1)}
, so Markov’s inequality applied to the non-negative random variable
|S^{h}|-1
gives

\mathbb{P}[H>h]\;=\;\mathbb{P}[|S^{h}|\geq 2]\;<\;\frac{4n}{\mu(h+1)}.

Setting
h=2(w+1)\log_{8/7}(4n)+2
yields
\mu(h+1)\geq(4n)^{w+1}
, hence
\mathbb{P}[H>h]<1/n^{w}
.
∎

B.2
Construction Algorithm

Restricted recompression proceeds as follows.
At level
0
, for each position
s\in[1,n]
, the algorithm introduces a fresh nonterminal
X_{i_{s}}
with rule
X_{i_{s}}\rightarrow T[s]
and determines
\mathsf{assign}(X_{i_{s}})
.
The sequence
S^{0}
is then
X_{i_{1}},X_{i_{2}},\ldots,X_{i_{n}}
.
Here, for two characters
T[s]
and
T[s^{\prime}]
,
X_{i_{s}}=X_{i_{s^{\prime}}}
if and only if
T[s]=T[s^{\prime}]
.

For each
h\geq 1
,
S^{h}
is built from
S^{h-1}
in two steps. In the first step,
S^{h-1}
is decomposed into segments
F_{1},F_{2},\ldots
left-to-right as follows. Suppose
S^{h-1}[1..s-1]
has been decomposed into
F_{1},\ldots,F_{j-1}
; then the next segment
F_{j}
, starting at position
s
, is:

1.

F_{j}=S^{h-1}[s..s+1]
if
h
is even,
\mathsf{assign}(S^{h-1}[s])=1
, and
\mathsf{assign}(S^{h-1}[s+1])=0
;

2.

F_{j}=S^{h-1}[s..s+\ell-1]
, the longest run of
S^{h-1}[s]
starting at position
s
, if
h
is odd and
\mathsf{assign}(S^{h-1}[s])\neq-1
;

3.

F_{j}=S^{h-1}[s]
otherwise.

In the second step, for each segment
F_{j}
of
S^{h-1}
, the algorithm introduces a fresh nonterminal
X_{i_{j}}
with rule
X_{i_{j}}\rightarrow F_{j}
and determines
\mathsf{assign}(X_{i_{j}})
. Concatenating these nonterminals in order yields
S^{h}
.
Here, for two segments
F_{j}
and
F_{j^{\prime}}
,
X_{i_{j}}=X_{i_{j^{\prime}}}
if and only if
F_{j}=F_{j^{\prime}}
.

These two steps are applied for
h=1,2,\ldots
until the sequence has length one; the final sequence is
S^{H}
, with
H\geq 1
since
|S^{0}|=n\geq 2
.

The algorithm outputs an RLSLP
\mathcal{G}^{R}=(\mathcal{V},\Sigma,\mathcal{D},E)
, where
\mathcal{V}
collects the nonterminals introduced across
S^{0},S^{1},\ldots,S^{H}
;
\Sigma
is the alphabet of
T
;
\mathcal{D}
is the set of production rules created above; and the start symbol
E
is the unique nonterminal in
S^{H}
.

By Corollary V.11 of
[
27
]
, the algorithm terminates with probability
1
, and its expected running time is
\mathcal{O}(n)
.

Appendix C
Details of the Dynamic RR-Index and Locate Algorithm

Representation of nodes and nonterminals.

Each explicit node
u\in\mathcal{U}_{\mathsf{expl}}
is stored as a pointer to the list element of
\Pi
for the path containing
u
;
each implicit node is represented by the explicit node at the end of its path (i.e., the unique explicit endpoint; see Section
4
).
In both cases, the list element is accessible in
\mathcal{O}(1)
time.

Each nonterminal in
\mathcal{V}
is represented by its DAG node.
Equality of two nonterminals is therefore tested by comparing DAG nodes; the dynamic RR-index never stores nonterminal subscripts explicitly.

C.1
Definition and Properties of the Popped Sequence

Definition.

Let
\mathcal{G}_{P}=(\mathcal{V}_{P},\Sigma_{P},\mathcal{D}_{P},E_{P})
be the RLSLP obtained by applying restricted recompression to
P
.
Its production rules are shared with those of
\mathcal{G}^{R}
whenever possible: if

and
\mathcal{D}
contains a rule

with
\mathsf{expr}_{j}=\mathsf{expr}_{i}

.
The derivation tree of
\mathcal{G}_{P}
decomposes into
(\hat{H}+1)
sequences

S_{P}^{0},S_{P}^{1},\ldots,S_{P}^{\hat{H}}
.

The popped sequence of

is defined using
3(\hat{H}+1)
sequences of nonterminals

,

For each

let

be the segments of

obtained by restricted recompression.
Suppose that

is an integer.

L^{h}
:

unless

consists of two distinct nonterminals.
In these two cases,

is the empty sequence.

R^{h}

In these two cases,

is the empty sequence.

C^{h}
:

C^{0}=S_{P}^{0}
.
For

.
Then the popped sequence

.

The popped sequence

has three properties.

, since each

(resp.

C.4
Details of Random Access, LCE, and Reversed LCE Queries

Random access queries.

We compute
T[i..i+\ell-1]
by traversing the derivation tree of
\mathcal{G}^{R}
from the root to
the
\ell
leaves corresponding to the substring.
Using Lemma
4
,
this traversal can be performed in
\mathcal{O}(\kappa)
time,
where
\kappa
is the number of nodes in the derivation tree that satisfy the following two conditions:
(i) the label of the node corresponds to an explicit node in the DAG;
(ii) the node is an ancestor of a leaf corresponding to a character of
T[i..i+\ell-1]
.

\kappa
can be bounded by
\mathcal{O}(H+\ell)
.
Therefore,
T[i..i+\ell-1]
can be computed in
\mathcal{O}(H+\ell)
time.

LCE and reversed LCE queries.

Consider an LCE query on two suffixes
T[i..n]
and
T[j..n]
, and let
\ell\geq 1
denote the length of their longest common prefix.
Let
Q
be the shortest sequence of nonterminals that (i) derives
T[i..i+\ell-1]
and (ii) is embedded at both positions
i
and
j
.
The standard algorithm finds
Q
by traversing the derivation tree and recovers
\ell
as the sum of
|\mathsf{val}(\cdot)|
over the nonterminals of
Q
; reversed LCE is symmetric.

Duyster and Kociumaka
[
12
]
showed that this algorithm runs in
\mathcal{O}(H)
time on the restricted recompression RLSLP, and Lemma
4
lets us simulate it within the same bound.
Given the popped sequence of
P
, the same procedure extends to an LCE between a suffix of
T
and a suffix of
P
(traverse the subtrees rooted at the nodes of the popped sequence), and likewise to a reversed LCE between prefixes.

Comparing a suffix
P_{R}
of
P
with a string
R_{i}\in\mathcal{Y}
.

We compare
P_{R}
and
R_{i}
in the following six steps.

(i)

Access to the node corresponding to
R_{i}
using the list indexing data structure built on
\mathcal{Y}
.

(ii)

Compute the length
\ell
of the longest common prefix of
P_{R}
and
R_{i}
using an LCE query.

(iii)

If
\ell=|P_{R}|
and
|P_{R}|<|R_{i}|
, then
P_{R}
is lexicographically smaller than
R_{i}
.

(iv)

If
\ell=|R_{i}|
and
|P_{R}|>|R_{i}|
, then
R_{i}
is lexicographically smaller than
P_{R}
.

(v)

If
\ell=|P_{R}|=|R_{i}|
, then
P_{R}
and
R_{i}
are equal.

(vi)

Otherwise, comparing
P_{R}
and
R_{i}
reduces to comparing
P_{R}[\ell+1]
and
R_{i}[\ell+1]
.
We access
R_{i}[\ell+1]
by a random access query and return the result of this character comparison.

This algorithm runs in
\mathcal{O}(H+\log M)
time.

Comparing the reverse of a prefix
P_{L}
of
P
with a string
L_{i}\in\mathcal{X}
.

This comparison can be performed in
\mathcal{O}(H+\log M)
time
by modifying the algorithm for comparing
P_{R}
and
R_{i}
.

C.5
Algorithm for Constructing the Popped Sequence

To explain how to construct the popped sequence,
we introduce a
rule query
: given a right-hand side
\mathsf{expr}
, return the nonterminal
X\in\mathcal{V}
such that
X\rightarrow\mathsf{expr}\in\mathcal{D}
, or
\bot
if no such nonterminal exists.
This query is based on the following observation.
If
X
corresponds to an explicit node, then it is stored in the global hash table as the value associated with key
\mathsf{expr}
.
Otherwise,
X
corresponds to an implicit node
u\in\mathcal{U}
; in this case,
\mathsf{expr}
is a single nonterminal
X^{\prime}\in\mathcal{V}
, and the node corresponding to
X^{\prime}
is the child of
u
on a path in
\Pi
.
Using this observation,
a rule query can be answered in
O(1)
time using the data structures maintained by the dynamic RR-index.

The popped sequence of
P
is built bottom up using rule queries, as in restricted recompression.
If the construction would require creating a new nonterminal, the algorithm aborts.
In this case,
\mathit{Occ}(T,P)=\emptyset
follows from the definition of the core.
Otherwise it runs in
\mathcal{O}(\sum_{h=0}^{\hat{H}}|S^{h}_{P}|)
time.
Kociumaka showed
\mathbb{E}[\sum_{h=0}^{\hat{H}}|S^{h}_{P}|]=\mathcal{O}(m)
(Lemma V.10 in
[
27
]
), so the expected running time is
\mathcal{O}(m)
.

Case 2.

(i)

C.7
Speeding up Phase (ii) in Practice

The bottleneck in phase (ii) is comparing the
x
- or
y
-coordinate
s^{\prime}\in\Sigma^{*}
of a point in
\mathcal{P}
with a substring
s
of
P
(either a suffix of
P
or the reverse of a prefix of
P
) using LCE, reversed LCE, and random access queries (see also Section
5.5.1
).
To reduce the number of calls to these three queries, we use the following observation.
Let
s_{\mathsf{short}}\in\Sigma^{*}
and
s^{\prime}_{\mathsf{short}}\in\Sigma^{*}
be the prefixes of length
\lceil\beta B/\log\sigma\rceil
of
s
and
s^{\prime}
, respectively.
If
s_{\mathsf{short}}\neq s^{\prime}_{\mathsf{short}}
, then the lexicographic order between
s
and
s^{\prime}
is the same as that between
s_{\mathsf{short}}
and
s^{\prime}_{\mathsf{short}}
.
Therefore, when the longest common prefix of
s
and
s^{\prime}
is sufficiently short, we can determine the lexicographic order between
s
and
s^{\prime}
without using these queries.
In practice, we expect this case to occur frequently.

We compare
s
and
s^{\prime}
in the following three steps:
(i) compute
s_{\mathsf{short}}
and access
s^{\prime}_{\mathsf{short}}
stored in the doubly linked list for
\Pi
;
(ii) compare
s_{\mathsf{short}}
and
s^{\prime}_{\mathsf{short}}
;
(iii) if
s_{\mathsf{short}}\neq s^{\prime}_{\mathsf{short}}
, return this comparison as the result for
s
and
s^{\prime}
; otherwise, fall back to the algorithm of Section
5.5.1
.

Q_{L}
and
Q_{R}
using the algorithm of
[
12
]
.
This is done by traversing the derivation tree of
\mathcal{G}^{R}
from the root to
the nodes corresponding to the sequence
Q_{L}
embedded at position
1
and the sequence
Q_{R}
embedded at position
s
, respectively.
By Lemma
4
,
these traversals can be performed in
O(H)
time.

(ii)

Compute
\mathcal{T}_{\mathsf{diff}}
in
\mathcal{O}(|\mathcal{T}_{\mathsf{diff}}|)
time using the rule query introduced in Appendix
C.5
.
This computation may create new production rules.
Whenever a new production rule
X_{i}\rightarrow\mathsf{expr}_{i}
is established,
we update the dynamic RR-index accordingly.

Therefore, the expected amortized running time of the first phase is

\mathcal{O}(|\mathcal{T}_{\mathsf{diff}}|+|\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{expl}}|\max\{H,H^{\prime},\log M\}\log M+|\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{impl}}|),

where
\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{expl}}
and
\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{impl}}
are introduced in Section
6
.

D.2
Details of Phase 2

Computation of
\mathcal{R}_{\mathsf{expl}}\cup\mathcal{R}_{\mathsf{impl}}
.

To update the dynamic RR-index,
we need to identify the nodes in
\mathcal{R}_{\mathsf{expl}}\cup\mathcal{R}_{\mathsf{impl}}
.
For this purpose, we use the following observation:
a node
u_{i}
belongs to
\mathcal{R}_{\mathsf{expl}}\cup\mathcal{R}_{\mathsf{impl}}

if and only if all parents of
u_{i}
belong to
\mathcal{R}_{\mathsf{expl}}\cup\mathcal{R}_{\mathsf{impl}}
.

We start from the node
u_{i}
corresponding to the start symbol
E
of
\mathcal{G}^{R}
, letting
\mathbb{P}\in\Pi
denote the path containing
u_{i}
; this
u_{i}
has an incoming edge only if
E
still appears in
\mathcal{G}^{R^{\prime}}
.

\mathcal{R}_{\mathsf{expl}}\cup\mathcal{R}_{\mathsf{impl}}
can be computed recursively as follows:

(i)

Determine whether
u_{i}
has an incoming edge.
This can be verified in
\mathcal{O}(1)
time:

u_{i}
has at least one incoming edge if
(A)
u_{i}
is not the first node of
\mathbb{P}
, or
(B)
u_{i}
is the first node of
\mathbb{P}
and one incoming edge of
u_{i}
is explicitly stored in the list element corresponding to
\mathbb{P}
.

(ii)

If
u_{i}
has an incoming edge, then the recursion stops.
Otherwise,
u_{i}
belongs to
\mathcal{R}_{\mathsf{expl}}\cup\mathcal{R}_{\mathsf{impl}}
;
we output
u_{i}
, remove
u_{i}
and its outgoing edges from the DAG,
and recursively process the target node of each outgoing edge of
u_{i}
that has not yet been removed.

By the observation above, this recursive algorithm correctly computes

\mathcal{R}_{\mathsf{expl}}\cup\mathcal{R}_{\mathsf{impl}}
.
Since it processes
\mathcal{O}(|\mathcal{R}_{\mathsf{expl}}|+|\mathcal{R}_{\mathsf{impl}}|)
nodes,
its running time is
\mathcal{O}(|\mathcal{R}_{\mathsf{expl}}|+|\mathcal{R}_{\mathsf{impl}}|)
.
This bound excludes the update time of the dynamic RR-index.

Update of dynamic RR-index.

Whenever a node is removed from the DAG, the dynamic RR-index is updated accordingly.
As in Phase 1, this update takes expected amortized
\mathcal{O}(\log M)
time if the removed node is explicit, and
\mathcal{O}(1)
time otherwise.
Therefore, Phase 2 takes expected amortized
\mathcal{O}(|\mathcal{R}_{\mathsf{expl}}|\log M+|\mathcal{R}_{\mathsf{impl}}|)
time in total.

D.3
Details of Phase 3

To compute
\mathcal{U}_{\mathsf{change}}
,
we introduce a set
\mathcal{U}_{\mathsf{children}}\subseteq\mathcal{U}
of nodes
u
satisfying the following two conditions:
(i) a parent of
u
is removed from or inserted into the DAG when updating the RLSLP
\mathcal{G}^{R}

(i.e.,
u
is a child of a node in
\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{expl}}\cup\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{impl}}\cup\mathcal{R}_{\mathsf{expl}}\cup\mathcal{R}_{\mathsf{impl}}
); and
(ii)
u
itself is neither inserted into nor removed from the DAG when updating the RLSLP
\mathcal{G}^{R}
.
The set
\mathcal{U}_{\mathsf{children}}
can be computed in

\mathcal{O}(|\mathcal{R}_{\mathsf{expl}}|+|\mathcal{R}_{\mathsf{impl}}|+|\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{expl}}|+|\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{impl}}|)
time
by modifying the algorithm for computing
\mathcal{R}_{\mathsf{expl}}\cup\mathcal{R}_{\mathsf{impl}}
.
Here,
|\mathcal{R}_{\mathsf{expl}}|+|\mathcal{R}_{\mathsf{impl}}|+|\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{expl}}|+|\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{impl}}|=\mathcal{O}(W_{\max})
.

Computation of
\mathcal{U}_{\mathsf{change}}
.

Let
u\in\mathcal{U}_{\mathsf{expl}}
be an explicit node that is neither inserted into nor removed from the DAG when updating the RLSLP
\mathcal{G}^{R}
.
Then,
u\in\mathcal{U}_{\mathsf{change}}
if and only if either
u\in\mathcal{U}_{\mathsf{children}}
or

u
is a descendant of a node in
\mathcal{U}_{\mathsf{children}}
within distance
\lceil\alpha+\log B\rceil-1
.
Hence, analogously to the algorithm for
\mathcal{R}_{\mathsf{expl}}\cup\mathcal{R}_{\mathsf{impl}}
,

\mathcal{U}_{\mathsf{change}}
is computed by traversing the DAG from the nodes in
\mathcal{U}_{\mathsf{children}}
.
This traversal takes
\mathcal{O}(|\mathcal{U}_{\mathsf{change}}|)
time,
where
|\mathcal{U}_{\mathsf{change}}|\leq W_{\max}
.

Recomputation of the first
\lceil\beta B/\log\sigma\rceil
characters in
L_{i}
and
R_{i}
.

For each node
u_{i}
, the first
\lceil\beta B/\log\sigma\rceil
characters of
L_{i}
and of
R_{i}
are each obtained by a random access query in
\mathcal{O}(H^{\prime}+\beta B)
time.

Recomputation of
\mathbb{P}_{\mathsf{vOcc}}(u_{i})
.

We start with a path
\mathbb{Q}
consisting only of
u_{i}
and extend it to
\mathbb{P}_{\mathsf{vOcc}}(u_{i})

by a recursive algorithm.
The algorithm traverses the DAG from
u_{i}
to the root.
Suppose that
\mathbb{Q}
has already been extended to the last node
u_{i_{1}}
of
a path
\mathbb{P}=u_{i_{d}}\rightarrow u_{i_{d-1}}\rightarrow\cdots\rightarrow u_{i_{1}}
in
\Pi
.
Let
\eta
be the number of keys stored in the per-path hash table for
\mathcal{E}_{\mathsf{expl}}(\mathbb{P})
.
For each integer
x\in\{1,2,\ldots,\eta\}
,
let
e_{x}\in\mathcal{E}_{\mathsf{expl}}(\mathbb{P})
be an inter-path edge corresponding to
the
x
-th key
\mathrm{src}(e_{x})
in the doubly linked list for the
\eta
keys,
and let
z_{x}
be the number of directed edges from
\mathrm{src}(e_{x})
to
\mathrm{dst}(e_{x})
.

The recursive algorithm consists of seven steps.

The first step computes
z_{1}
,
z_{\eta-1}
, and
z_{\eta}
.
Each
z_{x}
is computed in
\mathcal{O}(1)
time using Lemma
4
(ii) and the corresponding key-value pair

(\mathrm{src}(e_{x}),\mathrm{dst}(e_{x}))
.
The three keys
\mathrm{src}(e_{1})
,
\mathrm{src}(e_{\eta-1})
, and
\mathrm{src}(e_{\eta})
can be accessed in
\mathcal{O}(1)
time via the doubly linked list, and their values via the per-path hash table.
Therefore, the first step takes
\mathcal{O}(1)
time.

The second step verifies whether
|\mathcal{E}_{\mathsf{expl}}(\mathbb{P})|=0
.
This takes
\mathcal{O}(1)
time, because
|\mathcal{E}_{\mathsf{expl}}(\mathbb{P})|=0
if and only if
\eta=0
.

The third step verifies whether
|\mathcal{E}_{\mathsf{expl}}(\mathbb{P})|=1
.
This also takes
\mathcal{O}(1)
time, because
|\mathcal{E}_{\mathsf{expl}}(\mathbb{P})|=1
if and only if

\eta=1
and
z_{1}=1
.

The fourth step verifies whether
|\mathcal{E}_{\mathsf{expl}}(\mathbb{P})|\geq 2
.
This takes
\mathcal{O}(1)
time, because
|\mathcal{E}_{\mathsf{expl}}(\mathbb{P})|\geq 2
if and only if
either (A)
\eta\geq 2
or (B)
\eta=1
and
z_{1}\geq 2
.

The fifth step verifies whether
u_{i_{d}}
has exactly one incoming edge
e^{\prime}
in the DAG.
This takes
\mathcal{O}(1)
time, because
u_{i_{d}}
has a single incoming edge if and only if

\mathrm{dst}(e_{\eta})=u_{i_{d}}
,
z_{\eta}=1
, and
\mathrm{dst}(e_{\eta-1})\neq\mathrm{dst}(e_{\eta})
.

The sixth step verifies whether
u_{i_{d}}
has at least two incoming edges in the DAG.
This takes
\mathcal{O}(1)
time, because
u_{i_{d}}
has at most one incoming edge if and only if
either (A)
\mathrm{dst}(e_{\eta})\neq u_{i_{d}}
, or (B)
\mathrm{dst}(e_{\eta})=u_{i_{d}}
,
z_{\eta}=1
,
and
\mathrm{dst}(e_{\eta-1})=\mathrm{dst}(e_{\eta})
.

The seventh step extends
\mathbb{Q}
further along
\mathbb{P}
whenever possible.
Let
k\in\{1,2,\ldots,d\}
be the integer satisfying
u_{i_{k}}=\mathrm{dst}(e_{1})
.
Then, one of the following five cases occurs:

|\mathcal{E}_{\mathsf{expl}}(\mathbb{P})|=0
.

In this case,
u_{i}
is the root of the DAG.
Therefore, we return
\mathbb{Q}
as
\mathbb{P}_{\mathsf{vOcc}}(u_{i})
.

|\mathcal{E}_{\mathsf{expl}}(\mathbb{P})|\neq 0
and
|\mathbb{Q}|+k-1\geq\lceil\alpha+\log B\rceil
.

In this case,
\mathbb{Q}
cannot be extended to
u_{i_{s+2}}
,
where
s=\lceil\alpha+\log B\rceil-|\mathbb{Q}|
.
Therefore, we extend
\mathbb{Q}
to
u_{i_{s+1}}
and return it as
\mathbb{P}_{\mathsf{vOcc}}(u_{i})
.

|\mathcal{E}_{\mathsf{expl}}(\mathbb{P})|=1
,
|\mathbb{Q}|+k-1<\lceil\alpha+\log B\rceil
, and
u_{i_{d}}
has exactly one incoming edge.

In this case, we can extend
\mathbb{Q}
to
\mathrm{src}(e^{\prime})
via
\mathbb{P}
.
After extending
\mathbb{Q}
to
\mathrm{src}(e^{\prime})
,
we recursively apply the algorithm to the path in
\Pi
that contains
\mathrm{src}(e^{\prime})
.

|\mathcal{E}_{\mathsf{expl}}(\mathbb{P})|=1
,
|\mathbb{Q}|+k-1<\lceil\alpha+\log B\rceil
, and
u_{i_{d}}
has at least two incoming edges.

In this case, we cannot extend
\mathbb{Q}
beyond
u_{i_{d}}
.
Therefore, we extend
\mathbb{Q}
to
u_{i_{d}}
and return it as
\mathbb{P}_{\mathsf{vOcc}}(u_{i})
.

|\mathcal{E}_{\mathsf{expl}}(\mathbb{P})|\geq 2
and
|\mathbb{Q}|+k-1<\lceil\alpha+\log B\rceil
.

In this case,
u_{i_{k}}
has at least two incoming edges,
and we cannot extend
\mathbb{Q}
beyond
u_{i_{k}}
.
Therefore, we extend
\mathbb{Q}
to
u_{i_{k}}
and return it as
\mathbb{P}_{\mathsf{vOcc}}(u_{i})
.

The recursive algorithm makes
\mathcal{O}(H^{\prime})
recursive calls, each taking
\mathcal{O}(1)
time, so
\mathbb{P}_{\mathsf{vOcc}}(u_{i})
is computed in
\mathcal{O}(H^{\prime})
time.

Running time of Phase 3.

Computing
\mathcal{U}_{\mathsf{change}}
takes
\mathcal{O}(W_{\max})
time,
and updating the additional information takes
\mathcal{O}((|\mathcal{U}_{\mathsf{change}}|+|\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{expl}}|)(H^{\prime}+B))
time.
Therefore, the total running time is
\mathcal{O}(W_{\max}+(|\mathcal{U}_{\mathsf{change}}|+|\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{expl}}|)(H^{\prime}+B))
.

D.4
Proof of Lemma
8

Proof of
|\mathcal{T}_{\mathsf{diff}}|=\mathcal{O}((m^{\prime}+H)H^{\prime})
.

In the derivation tree,
the nodes in
\mathcal{T}_{\mathsf{diff}}
form a tree
\mathcal{T}
of height
H^{\prime}
with
\mathcal{O}(|\mathcal{T}_{\mathsf{leaf}}|+m^{\prime})
leaves.
Here,
\mathcal{T}_{\mathsf{leaf}}
is the set of internal nodes in the derivation tree of
\mathcal{G}^{R^{\prime}}

such that each internal node is a leaf of
\mathcal{T}
.
The nodes in
\mathcal{T}_{\mathsf{leaf}}
correspond to distinct runs in
Q_{L}
and
Q_{R}
,
resulting in
|\mathcal{T}_{\mathsf{leaf}}|=\mathcal{O}(\rho_{L}+\rho_{R})
,
where
\rho_{L}
(respectively,
\rho_{R}
) is the number of runs in
Q_{L}
(respectively,
Q_{R}
).
This is because the nodes corresponding to each run have the same parent (see Appendix
C.1
).
Since
\rho_{L},\rho_{R}=\mathcal{O}(H)
,
the tree has
\mathcal{O}((m^{\prime}+H)H^{\prime})
nodes, resulting in
|\mathcal{T}_{\mathsf{diff}}|=\mathcal{O}((m^{\prime}+H)H^{\prime})
.

Proof of
|\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{impl}}|=\mathcal{O}((m^{\prime}+H)H^{\prime})
.

The nodes in
\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{impl}}
correspond to distinct nodes in
\mathcal{T}_{\mathsf{diff}}
,
resulting in
|\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{impl}}|=\mathcal{O}((m^{\prime}+H)H^{\prime})
.

Proof of
|\mathcal{R}_{\mathsf{impl}}|=\mathcal{O}(H^{2})
.

The popped sequences
Q_{L}
and
Q_{R}
are embedded in the derivation tree of
\mathcal{G}^{R}
at two positions
1
and
s
in
T
, respectively.
Similar to
\mathcal{T}_{\mathsf{diff}}
,
we consider the set
\mathcal{T}^{\prime}_{\mathsf{diff}}
obtained by collecting the ancestors of each root of the subtrees.
Then,

\mathcal{T}^{\prime}_{\mathsf{diff}}
forms a tree
\mathcal{T}^{\prime}
of height
H
with
\mathcal{O}(|\mathcal{T}^{\prime}_{\mathsf{leaf}}|)
leaves.
Here,
\mathcal{T}^{\prime}_{\mathsf{leaf}}
is the set of internal nodes in the derivation tree of
\mathcal{G}^{R}

such that each internal node is a leaf of
\mathcal{T}^{\prime}
.
Similar to
\mathcal{T}_{\mathsf{leaf}}
,
we obtain
|\mathcal{T}^{\prime}_{\mathsf{leaf}}|=\mathcal{O}(H)
.
The tree has
\mathcal{O}(H^{2})
nodes, resulting in
|\mathcal{T}^{\prime}_{\mathsf{diff}}|=\mathcal{O}(H^{2})
.
The nodes in
\mathcal{R}_{\mathsf{impl}}
correspond to distinct nodes in
\mathcal{T}^{\prime}_{\mathsf{diff}}
,
resulting in
|\mathcal{R}_{\mathsf{impl}}|=\mathcal{O}(H^{2})
.

Proof of
|\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{expl}}|=\mathcal{O}(m^{\prime}+H)
and
|\mathcal{R}_{\mathsf{expl}}|=\mathcal{O}(H)
.

The nodes in
\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{expl}}
correspond to the leaves and internal nodes of
\mathcal{T}
with degree at least
2
.
The number of such nodes is
\mathcal{O}(m^{\prime}+H)
,
resulting in
|\mathcal{I}_{\mathsf{expl}}|=\mathcal{O}(m^{\prime}+H)
.
Similarly, we obtain
|\mathcal{R}_{\mathsf{expl}}|=\mathcal{O}(H)
.

Proof of
|\mathcal{U}_{\mathsf{change}}|=\mathcal{O}(HB)
.

For each node
v\in\mathcal{T}_{\mathsf{leaf}}\cup\mathcal{T}^{\prime}_{\mathsf{leaf}}
,
let
f(v)
be the lowest ancestor of
v
such that

f^{\prime}(v)
is inserted into or removed from the DAG when the RLSLP is updated,
where
f^{\prime}(v)
is the DAG-node corresponding to
f(v)
.
Let
\tau=|\{f^{\prime}(v)\mid v\in\mathcal{T}_{\mathsf{leaf}}\cup\mathcal{T}^{\prime}_{\mathsf{leaf}}\}|
.
Since each node in the DAG has at most two children,
any node has at most
2^{\lceil\alpha+\log B\rceil}
descendants within distance
\lceil\alpha+\log B\rceil
.
Therefore,
|\mathcal{U}_{\mathsf{change}}|\leq 2^{\lceil\alpha+\log B\rceil}\tau
,
because each node in
\mathcal{U}_{\mathsf{change}}
is a descendant of some node in

\{f^{\prime}(v)\mid v\in\mathcal{T}_{\mathsf{leaf}}\cup\mathcal{T}^{\prime}_{\mathsf{leaf}}\}

at distance at most
\lceil\alpha+\log B\rceil
.
Moreover,
\tau=\mathcal{O}(H)
follows from

|\mathcal{T}_{\mathsf{leaf}}|,|\mathcal{T}^{\prime}_{\mathsf{leaf}}|=\mathcal{O}(H)
.
Hence,
|\mathcal{U}_{\mathsf{change}}|=\mathcal{O}(2^{\lceil\alpha+\log B\rceil}H)=\mathcal{O}(HB)
.

Appendix E
Details of Deletion Operation

We focus on the case
s\neq 1
and
s+m^{\prime}-1\neq n
, so that

T^{\prime}
is the concatenation of
T[1..s-1]
and
T[s+m^{\prime}..n]
.
Let
Q_{L}
and
Q_{R}
be the popped sequences of the prefix
T[1..s-1]
and suffix
T[s+m^{\prime}..n]
;
they are embedded as subtrees in the derivation tree of
\mathcal{G}^{R^{\prime}}
at positions
1
and
s
in
T^{\prime}
, respectively,
and the computation of these subtrees can be skipped.

The update of the dynamic RR-index proceeds in three phases, paralleling the insertion algorithm.
Phase 1 constructs the derivation tree of
\mathcal{G}^{R^{\prime}}=(\mathcal{V}^{\prime},\Sigma^{\prime},\mathcal{D}^{\prime},E^{\prime})
by restricted recompression, computing the sequences
S^{0},S^{1},\ldots,S^{H^{\prime}}
in a bottom-up manner.
During this construction, the nodes corresponding to the embeddings of
Q_{L}
and
Q_{R}
are skipped.
For each nonterminal in
\mathcal{G}^{R^{\prime}}\setminus\mathcal{G}^{R}
, the corresponding node is inserted into the DAG, and the dynamic data structures are updated.
Phases 2 and 3 are identical to those of the insertion algorithm.

Thus, the expected amortized running time is
\mathcal{O}(m^{\prime}\log^{2}n+\log^{3}n)
.

Results for update operations.

Tables
3
–
4
report the average time and standard deviation for string insertions and substring deletions on all datasets.
For update operations, the dynamic RR-index was the fastest or competitive with
the fastest dynamic index in most settings.
On
enwiki
with
m^{\prime}=1000
, it was approximately
2.1\times
faster than the dynamic SE-index and
16\times
faster than the dynamic r-index. On the Pizza&Chili repetitive corpus with
m^{\prime}=1000
, the best-case speedups were approximately
2.3\times
over the dynamic SE-index (
para
) and
77\times
over the dynamic r-index (
coreutils
).

Results for locate queries.

Table
5
reports the average time and standard deviation for 1,000 locate queries with short, medium, and long patterns on all datasets. On most datasets the r-index was the fastest regardless of pattern length, followed by the dynamic RR-index.

On
enwiki
, the dynamic RR-index matched the r-index for short patterns and was only about
2.6\times
slower for medium patterns; against the other dynamic indexes, it was at least
5\times
faster in the best case.

On most datasets and pattern lengths, the r-index was the fastest, and the dynamic
RR-index was usually the fastest among the dynamic indexes, with a few exceptions:
the dynamic SE-index was faster on
world leaders
for short patterns, and
the dynamic r-index was faster on
influenza
and
world leaders

for medium patterns.
For long patterns, however, it was at most about
2.5\times
slower than the r-index. Against the other dynamic indexes, the dynamic RR-index was at least
3\times
faster in the best case for short patterns,
2\times
for medium patterns, and
2.6\times
for long patterns.

Results for memory consumption.

Table
6
reports the working space used during update operations on all datasets. Among the three dynamic indexes, the dynamic r-index was the most space-efficient, followed by the dynamic RR-index. On
enwiki
, the dynamic RR-index used approximately
5\times
the memory of the dynamic r-index. On the Pizza&Chili repetitive corpus, this ratio ranged from
1.4\times
(
einstein.en.txt
) to
4.6\times
(
influenza
).

Results for construction time.

Table
7
reports the construction times of the dynamic RR-index and the other three methods on all datasets. On
enwiki
, all four methods finished within 11 hours; the dynamic SE-index was the fastest (about 5 hours), followed by the dynamic RR-index (about 8 hours). On the Pizza&Chili repetitive corpus, all methods finished within 7 minutes on every dataset, with no significant difference among the four.
