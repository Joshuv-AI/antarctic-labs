A Mountain-Pass Algorithm for Nonlocal Problems with Super-quadratic Nonlinearities
Loic Cappanera, Gabriela Jaramillo, Joshua M. Siktar

Abstract
. In this paper we consider a nonlinear equation − ℒ ​ u ​ ( x ) = f ⁡ ( x , u ⁡ ( x ) ) \displaystyle-\mathcal{L}u(x)=f(x,u(x)) with a super-quadratic nonlinearity, f \displaystyle f , and a nonlocal operator, ℒ \displaystyle\mathcal{L} , generated by a special class of radially symmetric L 1 \displaystyle L^{1} convolution kernels with finite second moments. The assumptions on this operator are mild and allow for a variety of kernels used in biological and physical applications, including kernels with algebraic decay and sign changing kernels. Using the strong nonlinearities present in the equation, we prove the existence of nontrivial solutions using the classical Mountain Pass Theorem, a central result in minimax theory that equates solutions of our equation to critical points of a corresponding energy functional. This existence result holds with both homogeneous nonlocal Dirichlet and nonlocal Neumann boundary conditions. We supplement these theoretical results with numerical simulations for various nonlinearities with odd maximal degree in the unknown u \displaystyle u . The numerical scheme exploits the resulting energy landscape which allows one to adapt a gradient descent algorithm.



1.
Introduction

In this paper we consider nonlocal equations of the form

(1.1)

\begin{array}[]{c c c c }-\mathcal{L}u(x)&=\ f(x,u)&\quad x\in&\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}\\
\end{array}

where the function
\displaystyle f(x,u)
is nonlinear in
\displaystyle u
and the map
\displaystyle\mathcal{L}
is a nonlocal operator of the form

(1.2)

\mathcal{L}u(x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(u(y)-u(x))\gamma(x,y)\;dy.

We assume that the convolution kernel,
\displaystyle\gamma(x,y)=\gamma(|x-y|)
, is a radially symmetric function living in
\displaystyle L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
and satisfies appropriate conditions, which we make more precise in what follows. In addition, we take
\displaystyle\Omega
to be a bounded domain with smooth boundary,
\displaystyle\partial\Omega
, and consider homogeneous nonlocal Dirichlet and Neumann boundary constraints given, respectively, by

(1.3)

u\ =\ 0\quad x\in\Omega^{c}\ =\ \mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\Omega,

(1.4)

\mathcal{L}u\ =\ 0\quad x\in\Omega^{c}\ =\ \mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\Omega.

Our goals here are two-fold: one is to show that (
1.1
) admits non-trivial solutions using the Mountain-Pass Theorem, and the second is to present an efficient numerical scheme for finding such solutions based on the particular geometry of the energy functional derived from (
1.1
).

Our motivation for considering equations of the form (

) then correspond to time independent solutions, or steady states, for these models. This type of operator also appears in descriptions of excitable systems, including cardiac and brain tissue. In this case, maps like (
1.2
) are used to describe the effects of tissue inhomogeneities
[

, or long-range excitatory and inhibitory interactions between neurons
[

,

. Similarly, models for phase transitions that account for long-range interaction between phases result in Landau-type functionals where the Dirichlet, or gradient, energy is replaced by a convolution

In contrast to other works that model the operator

\displaystyle\gamma

To prove the existence of nontrivial solutions for equation (
1.1
) we adapt the classical Mountain-Pass Theorem to our nonlocal setting. This theorem, originally proven in
[
41
]
, is a central part of minimax theory, as it allows one to prove existence of critical points of an energy functional. These results can then be applied to obtain existence of non-trivial solutions to the corresponding Euler-Lagrange equations. The theorem requires energy functionals to possess a particular geometric structure whereby their graph exhibits a "valley" or saddle structure. In particular, the energy functional must be positive for all points at a fixed distance away from the origin, and be negative at some point farther away. The theorem also requires a compactness condition to hold on sequences of points for which the derivative of the energy functional vanishes in the limit. As we will show, the growth conditions on the nonlinearity
\displaystyle f
play a crucial role in verifying these assumptions.

While the Mountain-Pass Theorem was originally used to prove multiplicity of solutions for elliptic partial differential equations (see for instance

As in the case of fractional operators, we find that the long range interactions inherent to nonlocal models do not significantly affect the verification of the conditions for the Mountain-Pass Theorem; having proper structure on the underlying function spaces, including suitable continuous embeddings, is more important.
Because the nonlocal operators considered here have
\displaystyle L^{2}(\Omega)
as a natural domain, we do not obtain the aforementioned necessary compactness condition. To overcome this difficulty, we reformulate the operators so that they generate bilinear forms with domain
\displaystyle H^{1}(\Omega)\times H^{1}(\Omega)

To accomplish our second goal and obtain an efficient numerical scheme to solve equation (
1.1

[

,
18
,

Recasting the problem as finding critical points of an energy, we exploit a stricter geometric assumption (expressed as a condition on the nonlinearity

) to first find an explicit expression for the maximum of this energy along any radial direction. This allows one to obtain a good first guess for a gradient descent scheme, which then updates the direction of descent (in the “angular" direction) by solving a nonlocal linear equation. Our results show that the proposed scheme performs better than a standard Newton algorithm, which while converging cannot find a true solution to the nonlocal equation.

2.
Background and Preliminaries

In this section we consider operators of the form

(2.1)

\mathcal{L}u\ =\ \int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(u(y)-u(x))\gamma(|x-y|)\;dy,\qquad x\in\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n},

and as stated in the introduction, we assume
\displaystyle\Omega
is a bounded domain with smooth boundary. In addition, we take convolution kernels
\displaystyle\gamma:\mathbb{R}^{n}\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}
that are at minimum symmetric functions in
\displaystyle L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
.
Throughout the paper, we will further assume that these maps can be split into the sum of a diffusive operator,
\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{e}
, and a small perturbation
\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{s}
.
In order to precisely state this assumption, in the next two subsections we first define diffusive operators and then state properties of their corresponding bilinear forms. In Subsection
2.3
we then characterize what we mean when we say that
\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{s}
is a small perturbation. Examples of the type of operators considered here are listed at the end of this subsection. Finally, Subsection
2.5
gives assumptions on the nonlinearities
\displaystyle f(x,u)
considered in this paper.

2.1.
Properties of Nonlocal Diffusive Operators

Throughout the paper, we make the following assumption on the diffusive operator
\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{e}
.

Assumption 2.1
.

There is a constant
\displaystyle\xi_{0}>0
such that the operator
\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{e}
acting on functions
\displaystyle L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
has Fourier symbol
\displaystyle L(\xi)=L(|\xi|)
which is uniformly bounded and analytic on a strip
\displaystyle|\mathrm{Re}(\xi)|<\xi_{0}
of the complex plane. Moreover, the symbol
\displaystyle L(\xi)
has a Taylor expansion

L(\xi)=-|\xi|^{2}+\mathrm{O}(|\xi|^{4})\quad\text{as}\quad|\xi|\to 0.

Assumption
2.1
guarantees that the map
\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{e}
can be written as the composition of an invertible map and the Laplace operator. This result is summarized in the following lemma, which is a consequence of the removable singularity theorem; see
[
32
, Lemma 3.9]
for a proof.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
\displaystyle L(\xi)
denote the multiplication operator satisfying Assumption
2.1
for some
\displaystyle\xi_{0}>0
. Then
\displaystyle L(\xi)
admits the following decomposition:

(2.2)

L(\xi)\ =\ M_{L}(\xi)L_{NF}(\xi)\ =\ L_{NF}(\xi)M_{R}(\xi),

where
\displaystyle L_{NF}(\xi)=-|\xi|^{2}/(1+|\xi|^{2})
, while
\displaystyle M_{L/R}(\xi)
and their inverses are analytic and uniformly bounded on
\displaystyle|\mathrm{Re}(\xi)|<\xi_{1}
for some
\displaystyle\xi_{1}<\xi_{0}
.

We use the above results to make one further assumption on the symbol
\displaystyle L
.

Assumption 2.3
.

Let
\displaystyle L(\xi)
denote the multiplication operator satisfying Assumption
2.1
and consider its decomposition (
2.2
), as stated in Lemma
2.2
. We assume that
\displaystyle M_{R}(\xi)=M_{L}(\xi)
is such that

L(\xi)=-|\xi|^{2}(1+L_{1}(\xi))

with
\displaystyle L_{1}(\xi)
satisfying Assumption
2.1
.

Notice that Assumption
2.1
implies that we can write the operator
\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{e}
as in (
2.1
),
where the convolution kernel
\displaystyle\gamma(|x-y|)=\gamma(z)
is such that:

P1

\displaystyle\gamma:\mathbb{R}_{+}\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}
is exponentially decaying,

P2

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}|\gamma(|x|)|\;dx=1
,

P3

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}x^{2}\gamma(|x|)\;dx\neq 0
.

However, this is not an equivalence relation. The above conditions on
\displaystyle\gamma
are not sufficient to obtain an operator
\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{e}
satisfying assumptions
2.3
.

For illustrative purposes, we give examples of Fourier symbols satisfying the above assumptions. We also give an explicit expression for the associated convolution kernel
\displaystyle\gamma(z)
.

Example 1:

L(\xi)=\frac{1}{b^{2}}\left[-1+\frac{1}{1+b^{2}|\xi|^{2}}\right]=\frac{-|\xi|^{2}}{1+b^{2}|\xi|^{2}}=-|\xi|^{2}\left[1+\underbrace{\left(\frac{b^{2}(1+|\xi|^{2})}{1+b^{2}|\xi|^{2}}\right)\cdot\frac{-|\xi|^{2}}{1+|\xi|^{2}}}_{L_{1}(\xi)}\right].

Tying this symbol back to the definition of the operator
\displaystyle\mathcal{L}
given in (
2.1
) we see that the corresponding convolution kernel
\displaystyle\gamma(z)
, which has Fourier symbol
\displaystyle\hat{\gamma}(\xi)=(1+b^{2}|\xi|^{2})^{-1}
, is the Green’s function for the operator
\displaystyle(\mathrm{Id}-b^{2}\Delta)
, where
\displaystyle b\in\mathbb{R}
. Then,

•

\displaystyle\gamma(z)=\frac{1}{2b}\text{exp}(-|z|/b)
in dimension
\displaystyle n=1
, and

•

\displaystyle\gamma(z)=\frac{1}{b\pi^{2}}K_{0}(|z|/b)
in dimension
\displaystyle n=2
, where
\displaystyle K_{0}
is the modified Bessel function of degree zero.

Example 2:

L(\xi)=-1+\text{exp}(-b^{2}|\xi|^{2}/4)=-\frac{b^{2}}{4}|\xi|^{2}\left[1+\underbrace{\frac{-1+\frac{b^{2}}{4}|\xi|^{2}+\text{exp}(-b^{2}|\xi|^{2}/4)}{-\frac{b^{2}}{4}|\xi|^{2}}}_{L_{1}(\xi)}\right]

In this case, the convolution kernel that gives rise to this Fourier symbol is

•

\displaystyle\gamma(z)=\frac{1}{\pi^{n/2}b^{n}}\text{exp}(-|z|^{2}/b^{2})
, with
\displaystyle b>0
and for all dimensions
\displaystyle n
.

We start by considering the nonlocal Dirichlet problem

\begin{split}-\mathcal{L}_{e}u&=f(x,u)\qquad x\in\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n},\\

\displaystyle e

It is straightforward to check that this space is equivalent to
\displaystyle H^{1}(\Omega)
and so we equip it with the
\displaystyle H^{1}
norm

\|u\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}=\|u\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\|\nabla u\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}.

We then have the following definition.

Definition 2.4
.

Let
\displaystyle\gamma:\mathbb{R}^{n}\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}

(
2.1

It is easy to see that the operator
\displaystyle\mathcal{L}
and the kernel
\displaystyle\gamma

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\mathcal{L}u(x)v(x)dx\ =\ -\frac{1}{2}\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}(u(y)-u(x))\gamma(x,y)(v(y)-v(x))dydx

\displaystyle X^{1}_{D}
and the radial symmetry of the kernel

(2.5)

The bilinear form (which is clearly symmetric) also satisfies the following properties.

Proposition 2.5
.

Let
\displaystyle B_{D}:X^{1}_{D}\times X^{1}_{D}\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}

\displaystyle C_{i}

\displaystyle\beta_{i}

D2

\displaystyle B_{D}[u,u]\geq\beta_{1}\|u\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}

D3

\displaystyle B_{D}[u,\phi]\leq C_{2}\|u\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}\|\phi\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}

D4

\displaystyle B_{D}[u,u]\geq\beta_{2}\|u\|^{2}_{H^{1}(\Omega)}

for all
\displaystyle u,\phi\in X^{1}_{D}
.

The properties
D1
and
D2

To prove the properties

and

to write

(2.7)

\displaystyle B_{D}[u,\phi]=

\displaystyle-(\mathcal{L}u,\phi)_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-(\Delta u-\Delta\mathcal{L}_{1}u,\phi)_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}

2.3.
Properties of our Nonlocal Operators

We are now ready to state our main assumptions for the operator
\displaystyle\mathcal{L}
, defined by the expression (
2.1
) together with Dirichlet (
2.3
) or Neumann (
2.10
) boundary conditions.
We state these in terms of the convolution kernel
\displaystyle\gamma
.

Assumption 2.11
.

We assume that
\displaystyle\gamma(z)
is a radial function in
\displaystyle L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, with average one and finite second moment.
In addition, we suppose that
\displaystyle\gamma
can be written as

\gamma\ =\ \gamma_{e}+\gamma_{s},

with

\Gamma_{e}\ =\ \int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\gamma_{e}(z)\;dz,\qquad\Gamma_{s}\ =\ \int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\gamma_{s}(z)\;dz,

and where

•

\displaystyle\gamma_{e}(z)
generates a nonlocal operator of diffusive type, i.e.,
\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{e}:L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\longrightarrow L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
,

\mathcal{L}_{e}u\ =\ \Gamma_{e}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(u(y)-u(x))\tilde{\gamma}_{e}(|x-y|)\;dy,\qquad\tilde{\gamma}_{e}=\gamma_{e}/\Gamma_{e}

whose Fourier symbol satisfies assumptions
2.1
and
2.3
; as a consequence,
\displaystyle\gamma_{e}
, and so
\displaystyle\gamma_{s}
, is in
\displaystyle L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
.

•

\displaystyle\gamma_{s}(z)
is small in the sense that it is either strictly negative, or

\|\gamma_{s}\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R})}-\Gamma_{s}\ <\ \beta\ =\ \min\{\beta_{0},\beta_{1}\},

where
\displaystyle\beta_{k}>0
is the largest number such that

\beta_{k}\|u\|^{2}_{X^{k}}\ \leq\ (-\mathcal{L}_{e}u,u)_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\ =\ B_{e}[u,u],

for all
\displaystyle u\in X^{k}
. In this case,
\displaystyle B_{e}:X\times X\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}
is associated bilinear form and the space
\displaystyle X=X^{k}_{D/N}
accounts for the corresponding boundary conditions and the necessary regularity
\displaystyle k=0,1
.

•

When considering nonlocal Neumann boundary constraints, we further assume that
\displaystyle\gamma_{e}(z)
is strictly positive.

The above assumptions on
\displaystyle\gamma
guarantee that bilinear forms,
\displaystyle B_{D/N}
, given as in Definitions
2.4
and
2.8
,
are bounded and coercive in a space of functions with sufficient regularity. This allows us to satisfy the compactness conditions,
P1
-
P3
, of the Mountain-Pass Theorem.
Indeed, when
\displaystyle\gamma_{s}=0
, the boundedness and coercivity follow from Propositions
2.5
and
2.9
.
To extend the result to the case when
\displaystyle\gamma_{s}\neq 0
, notice that because
\displaystyle\gamma_{s}\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R})
, we may conclude that these bilinear forms are bounded. If
\displaystyle\gamma_{s}
is not strictly negative, the coercivity is a consequence of first writing

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathcal{L}u\ =

\displaystyle\displaystyle\ \gamma\ast u-u

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathcal{L}u\ =

\displaystyle\displaystyle\ \Gamma_{e}(\tilde{\gamma}_{e}\ast u-u)+\gamma_{s}\ast u-\Gamma_{s}u,

and the next set of inequalities:

\displaystyle\displaystyle\beta\|u\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\ \leq

\displaystyle\displaystyle\ B_{e}[u,u]

\displaystyle\displaystyle\beta\|u\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\ \leq

\displaystyle\displaystyle\ \underbrace{B_{e}[u,u]-(\gamma_{s}\ast u,u)_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\Gamma_{s}(u,u)_{L^{2}(\Omega)}}_{=B_{D/N}[u,u]}

\displaystyle\displaystyle+(\gamma_{s}\ast u,u)_{L^{2}(\Omega)}-\Gamma_{s}(u,u)_{L^{2}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\displaystyle\beta\|u\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\ \leq

\displaystyle\displaystyle\ B_{D/N}[u,u]+(\|\gamma_{s}\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R})}-\Gamma_{s})\|u\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\displaystyle(\beta+\Gamma_{s}-\|\gamma_{s}\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R})})\|u\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\ \leq

\displaystyle\displaystyle\ B_{D/N}[u,u].

The assumptions on
\displaystyle\gamma_{s}
then allows us to conclude that
\displaystyle\beta+\Gamma_{s}-\|\gamma_{s}\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R})}>0
.
A similar argument also gives us

(\beta+\Gamma_{s}-\|\gamma_{s}\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R})})\|u\|^{2}_{H^{1}(\Omega)}\leq B_{D/N}[u,u],

from which we conclude that
\displaystyle B_{D/N}:X_{D/N}\times X_{D/N}\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}
is coercive.

If on the other hand the kernel
\displaystyle\gamma_{s}(x)<0
for all
\displaystyle x
, we then can write

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathcal{L}u\

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\ \gamma\ast u-u

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\Gamma_{e}(\tilde{\gamma}_{e}\ast u-u)-|\Gamma_{s}|(\tilde{\gamma}_{s}\ast u-u)

where as before
\displaystyle\tilde{\gamma}_{e}=\gamma_{e}/\Gamma_{e}
and
\displaystyle\tilde{\gamma}_{s}=\gamma_{s}/\Gamma_{s}
.
Since
\displaystyle\tilde{\gamma}_{s}
is now strictly positive, the results from
[
16
, Lemmas 2.3 and 2.5]
show that the bilinear form,
\displaystyle B_{s}:X^{0}_{D/N}\times X^{0}_{D/N}\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}
defined by

B_{s}[u,u]=|\Gamma_{s}|(\tilde{\gamma}_{s}\ast u-u,u)_{L^{2}(\Omega)}

is coercive. As a result,

\displaystyle\displaystyle\beta_{e}\|u\|^{2}_{X}

\displaystyle\displaystyle\ \leq\ B_{e}[u,u]

\displaystyle\displaystyle\beta_{e}\|u\|^{2}_{X}

\displaystyle\displaystyle\ \leq\ \underbrace{B_{e}[u,u]+B_{s}[u,u]}_{=B_{D/N}[u,u]}-B_{s}[u,u]

\displaystyle\displaystyle\beta_{e}\|u\|^{2}_{X}\ \leq\

\displaystyle\displaystyle B_{D/N}[u,u]-\beta_{s}\|u\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\displaystyle\beta_{e}\|u\|^{2}_{X}\ \leq\

\displaystyle\displaystyle B_{D/N}[u,u]

where
\displaystyle X=L^{2}(\Omega)
or
\displaystyle H^{1}(\Omega)
. We summarize these result in the next proposition.

Proposition 2.12
.

Let
\displaystyle\gamma\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
satisfy Assumption
2.11
and consider the bilinear form
\displaystyle B_{D/N}:X^{1}_{D/N}\times X^{1}_{D/N}\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}
defined by

B_{D/N}[u,\phi]\ =\ \frac{1}{2}\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}}(u(y)-u(x))\gamma(|x-y|)(\phi(y)-\phi(x))\;dy\;dx.

Then there exist positive constants
\displaystyle C_{i}
and
\displaystyle\beta_{i}
,
\displaystyle i=1,2,
such that

T1

\displaystyle B_{D/N}[u,\phi]\leq C_{1}\|u\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\|\phi\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}

T2

\displaystyle B_{D/N}[u,u]\geq\beta_{1}\|u\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}

T3

\displaystyle B_{D/N}[u,\phi]\leq C_{2}\|u\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}\|\phi\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}

T4

\displaystyle B_{D/N}[u,u]\geq\beta_{2}\|u\|^{2}_{H^{1}(\Omega)}

for all
\displaystyle u,\phi\in X^{1}_{D/N}
.

Remark 2.13
.

In general it is difficult to find the value of the coercivity constant,
\displaystyle\beta
, associated with a bilinear form.
In the case of the nonlocal operators studied in Section
2.1
this constant corresponds to the smallest eigenvalue of the operator, which can be approximated as follows. The Fourier Transform,
\displaystyle L_{e}(\xi)
, of the operator corresponds to the curve of the continuous spectrum of
\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{e}:L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\longrightarrow L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
. When we restrict our problem to a bounded domain, the eigenvalues of the operator will land on this curve. The smallest eigenvalue would then be close to the origin,
\displaystyle\xi=0
, where by assumption the Fourier symbol has an expansion

L_{e}(\xi)=-\alpha|\xi|^{2}+\mathrm{O}(|\xi|^{4})

with
\displaystyle\alpha
being the second moment of the kernel
\displaystyle\gamma_{e}
. It then follows that

\beta\sim\alpha\frac{1}{d(\Omega)^{2}},

where
\displaystyle d(\Omega)
represents the diameter of the domain.

3.
Existence of Nontrivial Solutions via Mountain-Pass Theorem

Our goal in this section is to prove existence of non-trivial solutions to

(3.1)

-\mathcal{L}u=f(x,u)\qquad x\in\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n},

with either nonlocal Dirichlet,

u=0\quad x\in\Omega^{c},

or nonlocal Neumann,

\mathcal{L}u=0\quad x\in\Omega^{c},

boundary constraints.
Our strategy is to use the Mountain-Pass Theorem, which requires us to define an energy functional associated to our problem. Here we let
\displaystyle I_{D/N}:X\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}
be given by

(3.2)

I_{D/N}[u]\ =\ \frac{1}{2}B_{D/N}[u,u]-\int_{\Omega}F(x,u)\;dx,

where
\displaystyle F(x,u)=\int_{0}^{u}f(x,v)\;dv
and the space
\displaystyle X
is either
\displaystyle X^{1}_{D}
in the case of Dirichlet boundary constraints, or
\displaystyle X^{1}_{N}
in the case of Neumann boundary constraints.

We now state the Mountain-Pass Theorem (originally proven in
[
41
]
), which gives conditions that guarantee existence of non-trivial critical points of an energy functional
\displaystyle I\in C^{1}(X,\mathbb{R})
. Here
\displaystyle C^{1}
refers to the existence of a
Fréchet derivative
of
\displaystyle I[\cdot]
, and
\displaystyle X^{*}
refers to the dual space of a Banach Space
\displaystyle(X,\|\cdot\|_{X})
.

Theorem 3.1

(Mountain-Pass Theorem)
.

Let
\displaystyle(X,\|\cdot\|_{X})
be a Banach space and take
\displaystyle I\in C^{1}(X;\mathbb{R})
to be a functional. Suppose that

G1

There is a constant
\displaystyle\rho\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
such that

\inf_{\|u\|_{X}=\rho}I[u]\ >\ I[0]=0.

G2

There is another constant
\displaystyle\beta\leq 0
and an element
\displaystyle e\in X
with norm
\displaystyle\|e\|_{X}>\rho
such that
\displaystyle I[e]\leq\beta
.

P-S

Every sequence
\displaystyle\{u_{n}\}
in
\displaystyle X
with
\displaystyle\{I[u_{n}]\}
bounded in
\displaystyle\mathbb{R}
and satisfying
\displaystyle I^{\prime}[u_{n}]\to 0
in
\displaystyle X^{*}
has a convergent sub-sequence.

Then, the value
\displaystyle c
, defined as

(3.3)

c\ :=\ \inf_{\gamma\in\Gamma}\max_{t\in[0,1]}I[\gamma(t)]

where

\Gamma\ =\ \{\gamma\in C([0,1],X):\gamma(0)=0\quad\gamma(1)=e\}

is a critical value of
\displaystyle I:X\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}
. That is, there is an element
\displaystyle w\in X
such that

I[w]=c,\quad\text{and}\quad I^{\prime}[w]=0.

In the forthcoming subsections we prove that the energy
\displaystyle I[\cdot]
satisfies the geometric conditions
G1
and
G2
and the compactness assumption
P-S
stated in Theorem
3.1
. As a result we will obtain our main theorem, which we state here.

Theorem 3.2

(Multiplicity of solutions to state equation)
.

Consider the nonlocal equation (
1.1
) and take
\displaystyle X\subset H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
to be the appropriate Banach space encoding boundary constraints. That is,
\displaystyle X=X^{1}_{D}
in the case of Dirichlet, while
\displaystyle X=X^{1}_{N}
in the case of Neumann boundary constraints.
Then, if
\displaystyle f
satisfies Assumptions
2.14
, the nonlocal problem has at least one non-trivial weak solution
\displaystyle u\in X
.

As a first step, it is straightforward to show that
critical points of these energies correspond to weak solutions of problem (
3.1
).

Lemma 3.3
.

Suppose
\displaystyle u\in X
is a critical point for
\displaystyle I[u]
. Then
\displaystyle u
is a weak solution of (
3.1
).

Proof.

Fix any
\displaystyle v\in X
and write

i(\tau)\ =\ I[u+\tau v]\quad\tau\in\mathbb{R}.

Since
\displaystyle u
is a critical point, we have
\displaystyle\frac{di}{d\tau}(0)=0
.
At the same time, with the notation
\displaystyle u=u(x)
and
\displaystyle u^{\prime}=u(y)
, we have that

\displaystyle i(\tau)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}}\gamma(|x-y|)(u+\tau v-u^{\prime}+\tau v^{\prime})^{2}\;dy\;dx-\int_{\Omega}F(x,u+\tau v)\;dx

\displaystyle\frac{di}{d\tau}(\tau)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}}\gamma(|x-y|)(u+\tau v-u^{\prime}+\tau v^{\prime})(v-v^{\prime})\;dy\;dx-\int_{\Omega}f(x,u+\tau v)v\;dx

\displaystyle\frac{di}{d\tau}(0)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}}\gamma(|x-y|)(u-u^{\prime})(v-v^{\prime})\;dy\;dx-\int_{\Omega}f(x,u)v\;dx

\displaystyle\frac{di}{d\tau}(0)

\displaystyle=(-\mathcal{L}u,v)_{L^{2}(\Omega)}-(f(\cdot,u),v)_{L^{2}(\Omega)}

Since this last condition holds for all

I[u]\ \geq\ \frac{\beta_{2}}{2}\|u\|^{2}_{H^{1}(\Omega)}-\int_{\Omega}F(x,u(x))dx.

Next, fix
\displaystyle\epsilon>0
and use (

) to obtain

(3.7)

I[u]\ \geq\ \frac{\beta_{2}}{2}\|u\|^{2}_{H^{1}(\Omega)}-\int_{\Omega}\epsilon|u(x)|^{2}+\delta(\epsilon)|u(x)|^{\alpha+1}dx.

By Hölder’s inequality,

(3.8)

I[u]\ \geq\ \frac{\beta_{2}}{2}\|u\|^{2}_{H^{1}(\Omega)}-\epsilon|\Omega|^{\frac{2\alpha-2}{\alpha+1}}\|u\|^{2}_{L^{\alpha+1}(\Omega)}-\delta(\epsilon)\|u\|^{\alpha+1}_{L^{\alpha+1}(\Omega)}.

Now due to the embedding
\displaystyle H^{1}(\Omega)\subset L^{2\alpha}(\Omega)\subset L^{\alpha+1}(\Omega)
we have

(3.9)

I[u]\geq\ \frac{\beta_{2}}{2}\|u\|^{2}_{H^{1}(\Omega)}-\epsilon|\Omega|^{\frac{2\alpha-2}{\alpha+1}}M^{2}_{\alpha+1}\|u\|^{2}_{H^{1}(\Omega)}-\delta(\epsilon)M^{\alpha+1}_{\alpha+1}\|u\|^{\alpha+1}_{H^{1}(\Omega)}.

Factor to obtain

(3.10)

I[u]\ \geq\ \|u\|^{2}_{H^{1}(\Omega}\left(\frac{\beta_{2}}{2}-\epsilon|\Omega|^{\frac{2\alpha-2}{\alpha+1}}M^{2}_{\alpha+1}-\delta(\epsilon)M^{\alpha+1}_{\alpha+1}(C_{P}+1)^{\alpha-1}\|\nabla u\|^{\alpha-1}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\right).

Pick
\displaystyle\epsilon>0
sufficiently small so that
\displaystyle\frac{\beta_{2}}{2}-\epsilon|\Omega|^{\frac{2\alpha-2}{\alpha+1}}M^{2}_{\alpha+1}\geq\frac{1}{4}
. Then we may pick
\displaystyle u
so that
\displaystyle\|u\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}=\rho
for
\displaystyle\rho
small enough so that the lower bound in (

) equals some positive value
\displaystyle\beta
(for instance, so the quantity in parentheses exceeds
\displaystyle 1/8
), and that completes the proof.
∎

This next lemma, which verifies

, is a variant of
[

, Proposition 10]
. We cannot use

Lemma 3.6
.

Let
\displaystyle\rho>0
be as defined in Lemma
3.5
.
There exists
\displaystyle e\in X
with norm
\displaystyle\|e\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}>\rho
such that
\displaystyle I[e]<0
.

Proof.

We follow the technique in the proof of
[
35

. Assume without loss of generality that in assumption
A4
, the limit
\displaystyle\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\frac{f(x,t)}{t}=+\infty
; otherwise use a non-positive test function in the steps that follow. As a result of this limit,

(3.11)

\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\frac{F(x,t)}{t^{2}}\ =\ +\infty.

Thus, for any
\displaystyle\epsilon\in(0,1)
there exists
\displaystyle K>0
such that for any
\displaystyle t>K
, we have that
\displaystyle F(x,t)>\frac{t^{2}}{\epsilon}
. To determine a lower bound for
\displaystyle F
on
\displaystyle\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{+}
, we now fix
\displaystyle(x,t)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\times[0,K]
. Using the condition (
2.19
), we get:

(3.12)

F(x,t)\ \geq\ -a_{1}K-\frac{a_{2}}{\alpha+1}K^{\alpha+1}\ \geq\ -a_{1}K-\frac{a_{2}}{\alpha+1}K^{\alpha+1}-K^{2}\ =:\ -\tilde{K}.

It then follows that for all
\displaystyle(x,t)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{+}
we have

(3.13)

F(x,t)\ \geq\ \frac{t^{2}}{\epsilon}-\frac{\tilde{K}}{\epsilon}.

Next, letting
\displaystyle u\in X
with
\displaystyle\|u\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}=1
and
\displaystyle u\geq 0
for a.e.
\displaystyle x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we obtain

(3.14)

\int_{\Omega}\frac{F(x,tu(x))}{t^{2}}dx\ \geq\ \frac{1}{\epsilon}\|u\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}-\frac{\tilde{K}}{\epsilon t^{2}}|\Omega|.

Consequently, we have that

(3.15)

\displaystyle\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\frac{I[tu]}{t^{2}}

\displaystyle=\ \lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\left(\frac{1}{2t^{2}}B[tu,tu]-\int_{\Omega}\frac{F(x,tu(x))}{t^{2}}dx\right)

\displaystyle\leq\ \lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\left(\left(\frac{C_{1}}{2}-\frac{1}{\epsilon}\right)\|u\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\frac{\tilde{K}}{\epsilon t^{2}}|\Omega|\right)

\displaystyle\leq\ -\infty,

where the last line follows from picking
\displaystyle\epsilon
sufficiently small. To complete the proof, we may pick
\displaystyle t>\rho>0
sufficiently large and set
\displaystyle e:=tu
.
∎

Notice that if we further assume that the function
\displaystyle F(x,t)
satisfies

\lim_{|t|\rightarrow\infty}\frac{F(x,t)}{t^{2}}\ =\ +\infty

uniformly for a.e.
\displaystyle x\in\Omega

Lemma 3.7
.

Let
\displaystyle\rho>0

3.2.
Compactness Conditions

Now we start working towards proving the Palais-Smale compactness condition
PS
. To this end we define
\displaystyle\mathscr{F}:X\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
via

(3.17)

\mathscr{F}[u]\ :=\ \frac{1}{\mu}\int_{\Omega}\mathcal{F}(x,u(x))dx\ =\ \int_{\Omega}\frac{1}{\mu}f(x,u(x))u(x)-F(x,u(x))dx.

We need to show this quantity is controlled from below. This comes in three steps, starting with a lower bound on (
3.17
) for arguments of small norm.

Lemma 3.8
.

There exists a
\displaystyle C>0
such that for
\displaystyle u\in X
with
\displaystyle\|u\|_{H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\leq 1
, we have the bound

(3.18)

\mathscr{F}[u]\ \geq\ -C.

Proof.

We bound both terms of (
3.17
) separately. To control the term involving
\displaystyle f
, we use (
2.14
), Hölder’s inequality, the continuous embedding of
\displaystyle H^{1}(\Omega)\subset L^{\alpha+1}(\Omega)
, and Poincaré’s inequality to obtain

(3.19)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\int_{\Omega}f(x,u(x))u(x)dx\ \geq\ \\
&-\int_{\Omega}a_{1}|u(x)|+a_{2}|u(x)|^{\alpha+1}dx\ =\ \\
&-a_{1}\|u\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)}-a_{2}\|u\|^{\alpha+1}_{L^{\alpha+1}(\Omega)}\ \geq\ \\
&-a_{1}|\Omega|^{\frac{1}{2}}\|u\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}-a_{2}\|u\|^{\alpha+1}_{L^{\alpha+1}(\Omega)}\ \geq\ \\
&-a_{1}|\Omega|^{\frac{1}{2}}\|u\|_{H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}-a_{2}M^{\alpha+1}_{\alpha+1}\|u\|^{\alpha+1}_{H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}.\end{aligned}

Then since
\displaystyle\|u\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}\leq 1
we obtain

(3.20)

\int_{\Omega}f(x,u(x))u(x)dx\ \geq\ -a_{1}|\Omega|^{\frac{1}{2}}-a_{2}M^{\alpha+1}_{\alpha+1}.

Similarly, but using (
2.19
) instead of (
2.14
), we obtain

(3.21)

\int_{\Omega}F(x,u(x))dx\ \geq\ -a_{1}|\Omega|^{\frac{1}{2}}-\frac{a_{2}M^{\alpha+1}_{\alpha+1}}{\alpha+1}.

Combining (
3.20
) with (
3.21
), we set

C:=\frac{1}{\mu}(a_{1}|\Omega|^{\frac{1}{2}}+a_{2}M^{\alpha+1}_{\alpha+1})+a_{1}|\Omega|^{\frac{1}{2}}+\frac{a_{2}M_{\alpha+1}}{\alpha+1}

which completes the proof.
∎

The second step is a scaling argument.

Lemma 3.9
.

Let
\displaystyle t\in(1,\infty)
, and let
\displaystyle\theta>1
be the scaling parameter introduced in
A3
. Then we have

(3.22)

\mathscr{F}[tu]\ \geq\ \frac{1}{\theta}\mathscr{F}[u].

Proof.

We use the definition of
\displaystyle\mathcal{F}
from
A3
and its associated scaling inequality:

(3.23)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \mathscr{F}[tu]\ &=\ \frac{1}{\mu}\int_{\Omega}\mathcal{F}(x,tu(x))dx\\
\ &\geq\ \frac{1}{\mu\theta}\int_{\Omega}\mathcal{F}(x,u(x))dx\\
\ &=\ \frac{1}{\theta}\mathscr{F}[u],\end{aligned}

completing the proof.
∎

Here is the final step for getting the bound we need on
\displaystyle\mathscr{F}[\cdot]
.

Lemma 3.10
.

Let
\displaystyle C>0
be the constant appearing in Lemma
3.8
and let
\displaystyle\theta>1
be the scaling parameter introduced in
A3
. Then we have

(3.24)

\mathscr{F}[u]\ \geq\ -\frac{C}{\theta},

for any
\displaystyle u\in X
.

Proof.

If
\displaystyle\|u\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}\leq 1
then the proof is complete by Lemma
3.8
. Otherwise let
\displaystyle w:=\frac{u}{\|u\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}}
and we may apply Lemma
3.9
with
\displaystyle t:=\|u\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}
followed by Lemma

to conclude

(3.25)

\mathscr{F}[u]\ =\ \mathscr{F}[tw]\ \geq\ \frac{1}{\theta}\mathscr{F}[w]\ \geq\ -\frac{C}{\theta},

∎

Since proving

requires a compactness argument, we must show that sequences approximating critical points are bounded.

Lemma 3.11
.

Let
\displaystyle\{u_{j}\}^{\infty}_{j=1}\subset X
be a sequence such that

(3.26)

\lim_{j\rightarrow\infty}I^{\prime}[u_{j}]\ =\ 0

and

(3.27)

\lim_{j\rightarrow\infty}I[u_{j}]\ =\ c

for some
\displaystyle c\in\mathbb{R}
. Then
\displaystyle\{u_{j}\}^{\infty}_{j=1}
is bounded in
\displaystyle X
.

Proof.

Let
\displaystyle v_{j}=\frac{u_{j}}{\|u\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}}
.
Due to our assumptions, there exists
\displaystyle\kappa>0
so that for all
\displaystyle j\in\mathbb{N}^{+}
sufficiently large we have that

(3.28)

\left|\langle I^{\prime}[u_{j}],v_{j}\rangle\right|\ \leq\ \kappa\mu.

and

(3.29)

|I[u_{j}]|\ \leq\ \kappa.

It then follows that, for all large

I[u_{j}]-\frac{1}{\mu}I^{\prime}[u_{j}]u_{j}\ \leq\ \kappa(1+\|u_{j}\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}).

where the constant
\displaystyle\mu
is the same as in Assumption

Now, to find a lower bound for the difference described in (

\displaystyle j\in\mathbb{N}^{+}
(

) and Proposition
3.3
(or Proposition
2.9
) give us

(3.31)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\begin{aligned} I[u_{j}]-\frac{1}{\mu}I^{\prime}[u_{j}]u_{j}\ =\ &\left(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{\mu}\right)B[u_{j},u_{j}]+\int_{\Omega}\frac{1}{\mu}f(u_{j}(x))u_{j}(x)-F(x,u_{j}(x))dx,\\
\ \geq\ &\left(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{\mu}\right)\beta_{2}\|u_{j}\|^{2}_{H^{1}(\Omega)}-\frac{C}{\theta},\end{aligned}

where the inequality follows from Proposition
3.3
(or Proposition
2.9
) and Lemma
3.10
,

Finally, combining (

(3.32)

\left(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{\mu}\right)\beta_{2}\|u_{j}\|^{2}_{H^{1}(\Omega)}-\frac{C}{\theta}\ \leq\ \kappa(1+\|u_{j}\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}),

from which the boundedness of
\displaystyle\{u_{j}\}^{\infty}_{j=1}
in
\displaystyle X

\displaystyle\mu>2

∎

Lemma 3.12

(Palais-Smale Compactness)
.

Let
\displaystyle\{u_{j}\}^{\infty}_{j=1}\subset X
be a bounded sequence in
\displaystyle X
such that
\displaystyle I^{\prime}[u_{j}]\rightarrow 0
as
\displaystyle j\rightarrow\infty
. Then there exists
\displaystyle u\in X
such that, up to a not relabeled sub-sequence,
\displaystyle u_{j}\rightarrow u
strongly in
\displaystyle X
.

Proof.

We proceed similarly to
[
42
, Proposition 12]
. First, by reflexivity, there exists a
\displaystyle u\in X
so that
\displaystyle u_{j}\rightharpoonup u
weakly in
\displaystyle X
(up to a not-relabeled sub-sequence). Then we claim for all
\displaystyle v\in X
,

(3.33)

\lim_{j\to\infty}B[u_{j},v]\ =\ B[u,v].

More specifically, since
\displaystyle u_{j}\rightharpoonup u
weakly in
\displaystyle X
, and
\displaystyle B[\cdot,\cdot]
is symmetric and satisfies the identity (
2.5
) in the Nonlocal Dirichlet case (or (
2.12
) in the Nonlocal Neumann case), we have

(3.34)

\lim_{j\rightarrow\infty}B[u_{j},v]\ =\ \lim_{j\rightarrow\infty}B[v,u_{j}]\ =\ \lim_{j\rightarrow\infty}\langle-\mathcal{L}v,u_{j}\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\ =\ \\
\langle-\mathcal{L}v,u\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\ =\ B[v,u]\ =\ B[u,v].

Due to the weak convergence and compact embeddings, there exists a further, not relabeled sub-sequence so
\displaystyle u_{j}\rightarrow u
strongly in
\displaystyle L^{2\alpha}(\Omega)
and a.e. in
\displaystyle\Omega
. In addition, due to
[
10
, Theorem 9]
, there exists a function
\displaystyle\ell\in L^{2\alpha}(\Omega)
so that
\displaystyle|u_{j}(x)|\leq\ell(x)
a.e.
\displaystyle x\in\Omega
, for all
\displaystyle j\in\mathbb{N}^{+}
. With this in mind, we claim that

(3.35)

\lim_{j\rightarrow\infty}\int_{\Omega}f(x,u_{j}(x))u_{j}(x)\ =\ \int_{\Omega}f(x,u(x))u(x)dx.

First notice that the integrands exhibit pointwise convergence due to the continuity of
\displaystyle f
. Thus to invoke the Dominated Convergence Theorem, we must show that the integrands have a pointwise,
\displaystyle L^{1}(\Omega)
upper bound uniformly in
\displaystyle j
. We use (
2.14
) to choose a candidate for such a bound:

(3.36)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &f(x,u_{j}(x))u_{j}(x)\ \leq\ \ell(x)(a_{1}+a_{2}\ell(x)^{\alpha})\ =\ a_{1}\ell(x)+a_{2}\ell(x)^{\alpha+1}\end{aligned}

The upper bound is finite since
\displaystyle\ell\in L^{2\alpha}(\Omega)
. So, we may apply the Dominated Convergence Theorem to obtain (
3.35
). By the same argument, we have that for any
\displaystyle k\in\mathbb{N}^{+}
,

(3.37)

\lim_{j\rightarrow\infty}\int_{\Omega}f(x,u_{j}(x))u_{k}(x)dx\ =\ \int_{\Omega}f(x,u(x))u_{k}(x)dx.

Our assumption
\displaystyle I^{\prime}[u_{j}]\to 0
, together with the previous limits, shows that

\lim_{j\to\infty}\frac{1}{2}B[u_{j},u_{k}]\ =\ \lim_{j\to\infty}\int_{\Omega}f(x,u_{j}(x))u_{k}(x)dx=\int_{\Omega}f(x,u(x))u_{k}(x)\;dx

We can therefore write

\int_{\Omega}f(x,u(x))u(x)\;dx=\lim_{j\to\infty}\int_{\Omega}f(x,u_{j}(x))u_{j}(x)dx\ =\ \lim_{j\to\infty}\frac{1}{2}B[u_{j},u_{j}]

where the last equality follows from picking a diagonal subsequence, which we do not relabel.
Because the bilinear form
\displaystyle B:X\times X\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}
defines an equivalent norm in the space
\displaystyle X
, (see Proposition
2.9
), it follows that
\displaystyle u_{j}\to u
strongly in
\displaystyle X
.

∎

Having proven
PS
finishes the proof of Theorem
3.2
.

4.
Existence of Nontrivial Solutions for Special Geometries

In this section we provide an alternative proof for the existence of non-trivial weak solutions to our nonlocal problem (
1.1
). Our results are summarized in Theorem
4.1
and form the basis for the numerical scheme presented in Section
5
. The analysis given here extends the results from
[
1
]
to nonlocal equations with nonlinearities satisfying Assumption
2.14
as well as the extra condition (
3.16
). This additional restriction on
\displaystyle f
guarantees that the energy functional attains a negative value for all points on a ball of sufficiently large radius. We will see in Section
5
that the resulting geometric structure makes it possible to find critical points using gradient descent.

As in the previous section, we consider the energy,
\displaystyle I:X\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}
, associated with equation (
3.1
) and defined by

(4.1)

I[u]\ =\ \frac{1}{2}B[u,u]-\int_{\Omega}F(x,u)\;dx.

Again, we take
\displaystyle X=X^{1}_{D}
in the case of Dirichlet and
\displaystyle X=X^{1}_{N}
in the case of Neumann boundary constraints.
We then have that the corresponding bilinear forms
\displaystyle B:X\times X\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}
, defined in Subsection
2.3
, are both bounded and coercive. Recall as well that the nonlinearity
\displaystyle f(x,u)
, appearing in our nonlocal problem (
1.1
), satisfies Assumption
2.14
and defines
\displaystyle F(x,u)=\int_{0}^{u}f(x,v)\;dv
.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
\displaystyle I:X\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}
, given in (
4.1
), be the energy associated with the nonlocal problem (
1.1
). If we further assume that the nonlinear function
\displaystyle F(x,t)
satisfies

(4.2)

\lim_{|t|\to\infty}\frac{F(x,t)}{t^{2}}\ =\ \infty

uniformly for a.e.
\displaystyle x\in\Omega
, then

\bar{c}\ =\ \inf_{w\in X,w\neq 0}\max_{t\geq 0}I[tw]

is a critical value of
\displaystyle I[\cdot]
.

Our goal is to show that there exists an element
\displaystyle\bar{u}\in X
such that
\displaystyle I[\bar{u}]=\bar{c}
and

\bar{c}\ =\ \inf_{w\in X,w\neq 0}\max_{t\geq 0}I[tw]\ =\ c

where
\displaystyle c
denotes the critical value of the energy
\displaystyle I[\cdot]
given in Theorem
3.1
equation (
3.3
).
To do this, we need the next lemma.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
\displaystyle I:X\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}
, satisfy the assumptions of Theorem
4.1
. Then,

\bar{c}\ =\ \inf_{w\in X,w\neq 0}\max_{t\geq 0}I[tw]\ =\ \min_{w\in X,w\neq 0}\max_{t\geq 0}I[tw].

Proof.

Let
\displaystyle w\in X
with
\displaystyle\|w\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}=1
, and define

g_{w}(t)\ =\ I[tw]\ =\ t^{2}B[w,w]-\int_{\Omega}F(x,tw)\;dx.

Notice that
\displaystyle g:\mathbb{R}_{+}\to\mathbb{R}
is a smooth function of
\displaystyle t
.
We want to show that
\displaystyle g
attains its maximum on
\displaystyle\mathbb{R}_{+}
.

First, Proposition
3.4
implies that
\displaystyle g_{w}(0)=0
. Next, Lemma
3.5
shows there are numbers
\displaystyle\rho,\beta>0
such that for
\displaystyle t=\rho
the function
\displaystyle g_{w}(t)>\beta
, independently of
\displaystyle w
.
Since
\displaystyle F(x,t)
satisfies property
4.2
, from Lemma
3.7
we have that there exists a constant
\displaystyle\rho_{2}>\rho
such that for all
\displaystyle t\geq\rho_{2}
the function
\displaystyle g_{w}(t)<0
, independently of
\displaystyle w
. It follows that
\displaystyle g_{w}(t)
attains its maximum for some value
\displaystyle t^{*}\in(0,\rho_{2})
.

Let
\displaystyle G(u)=g_{w}(t^{*})
, with
\displaystyle u:=t^{*}w
and notice that

\bar{c}\ =\ \inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}w\in X,\\
w\neq 0\end{subarray}}\max_{t\geq 0}I(tw)\ =\ \inf_{u\in K}G(u)

where
\displaystyle K
is the closed ball of radius
\displaystyle\rho_{2}
in
\displaystyle X
, i.e.,

K\ =\ \{u\in X:\|u\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}\leq\rho_{2}\}.

From the compact Sobolev embedding
\displaystyle H^{1}(\Omega)\subset\subset L^{p}(\Omega)
, valid for any
\displaystyle p\in(2,2^{*})
with
\displaystyle 2^{*}=2n/(n-2)
, we see in addition that
\displaystyle K
is a compact subset of
\displaystyle L^{p}(\Omega)
. Since
\displaystyle F
satisfies Assumption
2.14
and picking
\displaystyle p>\alpha+1>2
, it follows that
\displaystyle G:L^{p}(\Omega)\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}
is a bounded functional. Indeed, we can write

|G(u)|\ \leq\ |B[u,u]|+\int_{\Omega}\left|F(x,u)\right|\;dx

and bound first

\displaystyle\displaystyle B[u,u]\

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\ (-\mathcal{L}u,u)_{L^{2}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\displaystyle|B[u,u]|

\displaystyle\displaystyle\ \leq\ \|\mathcal{L}u\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\|u\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\displaystyle|B[u,u]|

\displaystyle\displaystyle\ \leq\ \|\mathcal{L}\|\|u\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\displaystyle|B[u,u]|

\displaystyle\displaystyle\ \leq\ C\|\mathcal{L}\|\|u\|^{2}_{L^{p}(\Omega)},

where the last inequality follows from the embedding
\displaystyle L^{p}(\Omega)\subset L^{r}(\Omega)
, valid for bounded domains
\displaystyle\Omega
when
\displaystyle p>r
, and the fact that the operator
\displaystyle\mathcal{L}:L^{2}(\Omega)\longrightarrow L^{2}(\Omega)
is bounded.

Next, from Proposition
3.4
, we have that for any
\displaystyle\epsilon>0
there is a
\displaystyle\delta(\epsilon)>0
such that

\left|F(x,u)\right|\ \leq\ \epsilon|u|^{2}+\delta(\epsilon)|u|^{\alpha+1}.

As a result,

\displaystyle\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\left|F(x,u)\right|\;dx

\displaystyle\displaystyle\ \leq\ \int_{\Omega}\epsilon|u|^{2}+\delta(\epsilon)|u|^{\alpha+1}\;dx

\displaystyle\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\left|F(x,u)\right|\;dx

\displaystyle\displaystyle\ \leq\ \epsilon\|u\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\delta(\epsilon)\|u\|_{L^{\alpha+1}(\Omega)}^{\alpha+1}

\displaystyle\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\left|F(x,u)\right|\;dx

\displaystyle\displaystyle\ \leq\ C(\epsilon\|u\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}^{2}+\delta(\epsilon)\|u\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}^{\alpha+1}),

where again the last inequality follows from selecting
\displaystyle p>\alpha+1
and using the embedding
\displaystyle L^{p}(\Omega)\subset L^{\alpha+1}(\Omega)
.

We therefore conclude that the functional
\displaystyle G:L^{p}(\Omega)\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}
attains its infimum in
\displaystyle K
, and there is a
\displaystyle\bar{u}\in K
such that

\inf_{u\in K}G(u)\ =\ \bar{c}\ =\ G(\bar{u})\ =\ \min_{u\in K}G(u).

∎

Proof Theorem
4.1
.

The results from Lemma
4.2
show there is an element
\displaystyle\bar{u}\in X
such that

I[\bar{u}]\ =\ \bar{c}\ =\ \inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}w\in X,\\
w\neq 0\end{subarray}}\max_{t\geq 0}I[tw].

We now want to show that

(4.4)

I[\bar{u}]\ =\ c\ =\ \inf_{\gamma\in\Gamma}\max_{t\in[0,1]}I[\gamma(t)],

where the set

\Gamma\ =\ \{\gamma\in C([0,1],X):\gamma(0)=0\quad\gamma(1)=e\},

with the element
\displaystyle e\in X
satisfying

\sigma\ :=\ I[e]\ <\ I[0]\ =\ 0.

To prove (
4.4
), we introduce again the trajectories

\displaystyle g_{w}(t)=I[tw]
,
with
\displaystyle w\in X
satisfying
\displaystyle\|w\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}=1
. Let
\displaystyle T_{w}>0

g_{w}(T_{w})\ =\ I[T_{w}w]\ =\ I[e]\ =\ \sigma.

Construct a continuous path,
\displaystyle\tilde{\gamma}(t)
, joining the trajectory
\displaystyle g_{w}(t)
for
\displaystyle t\in(0,T_{w})
and a second curve which stays on the level set
\displaystyle\{I[w]=\sigma\}
and connects
\displaystyle g_{w}(T_{w})
to the element
\displaystyle e
. Rescale the
\displaystyle t
-variable so that
\displaystyle\tilde{\gamma}(t)\in\Gamma
. It then follows that

(4.5)

c\ =\ \inf_{\gamma\in\Gamma}\max_{t\in[0,1]}I[\gamma(t)]\leq\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}w\in X,\\
\|w\|=1\end{subarray}}\max_{t\geq 0}g_{w}(t)=\bar{c}.

To obtain the reverse inequality of (
4.5
), observe that, from Lemma
3.7
, given any
\displaystyle w\in X
with
\displaystyle\|w\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}=1
,
we have that

\max_{t\geq 0}g_{w}(t)\ =\ \max_{t\in[0,T_{w}]}g_{w}(t).

It follows that the set of maximum values

M\ =\ \Big\{\max_{t\in[0,T_{w}]}g_{w}(t):w\in X,\quad\text{with}\quad\|w\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}=1\Big\}

contains all possible local maxima of
\displaystyle I[\cdot]
. In other words,
\displaystyle M
contains the set

m\ =\ \Big\{\max_{t\in[0,1]}I[\gamma(t)]:\gamma\in\Gamma\Big\}.

As a result,

\bar{c}\ =\ \inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}w\in X,\\
w\neq 0\end{subarray}}\max_{t\geq 0}g_{w}(t)\ \leq\ \inf_{\gamma\in\Gamma}\max_{t\in[0,1]}I[\gamma(t)]=c.

and we obtain the conclusion of the theorem.

∎

5.
Numerical scheme and illustrations

In this section, we propose an algorithm to solve the problem (
1.1
) based on the algorithm proposed in
[
1
]
for problems with p-Laplacian and homogeneous Dirichlet boundary conditions.

5.1.
Algorithm

In the following, we describe the various steps of our algorithm for approximating a solution of the problem (
1.1
) supplemented by homogeneous nonlocal Dirichlet or nonlocal Neumann boundary conditions. For clarity, we drop the subscript
\displaystyle D/N
when referring to the space
\displaystyle X_{D/N}^{1}
or the functional
\displaystyle I_{D/N}
.

For any function
\displaystyle u\in X
, we denote by
\displaystyle t^{*}_{u}
a positive real number that satisfies

I[t^{*}_{u}u]=\max_{t\geq 0}I[tu].

The existence of
\displaystyle t^{*}_{u}
is given by Lemma
4.2
. We also denote the operator norm on the energy space of
\displaystyle I
as
\displaystyle\|\cdot\|:=\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal{L}(X,\mathbb{R})}
.

The algorithm for approximating a non-trivial solution
\displaystyle u
of problem (
1.1
) reads as follows.

0.

Let
\displaystyle\epsilon>0
be a given tolerance and
\displaystyle\delta>0
be a given step length.

1.

Let
\displaystyle u_{1}
be an arbitrary nonzero function in
\displaystyle X
that represents an initial guess.

2.

Find
\displaystyle t^{*}_{u_{1}}
and set
\displaystyle w_{1}=t^{*}_{u_{1}}u_{1}
.

3.

If
\displaystyle\|I^{\prime}[w_{1}]\|\leq\epsilon
, return
\displaystyle w_{1}
. Go to step 4 otherwise.

4.

Find
\displaystyle v_{1}\in X
such that

(5.1)

I^{\prime}[w_{1}]v_{1}<0.

5.

Set
\displaystyle\tilde{w_{1}}=w_{1}+\delta v_{1}
.

6.

While
\displaystyle I[t^{*}_{\tilde{w_{1}}}\tilde{w_{1}}]\geq I[w_{1}]
, set
\displaystyle v_{1}=v_{1}/2
and return to Step 5. Go to Step 7 otherwise.

7.

Set
\displaystyle w_{1}=t^{*}_{\tilde{w_{1}}}\tilde{w_{1}}
and return to Step 3.

The following subsection provides more information on how to proceed through Steps 3 and 4 which require us to evaluate
\displaystyle\|I^{\prime}[w_{1}]\|
and find
\displaystyle v_{1}\in X
satisfying (
5.1
).

Remark 5.1
.

In Step 2, the value of
\displaystyle t^{*}_{u}
depends on the choice of nonlinearity
\displaystyle f(x,u)
so no general formula is provided. In Section
5.3
, we consider different functions
\displaystyle f
to perform numerical tests and give an explicit formula for
\displaystyle t^{*}_{u}
in each case.

Remark 5.2
.

In Step 6, we note that the value of
\displaystyle I(t^{*}_{\tilde{w_{1}}}\tilde{w_{1}})
is guaranteed to decrease below
\displaystyle I(w_{1})
as we decrease the factor
\displaystyle\delta
multiplying
\displaystyle v_{1}
. This is a consequence of the definition of
\displaystyle I^{\prime}
. Indeed, we have

\lim_{\delta\rightarrow 0}\frac{I[w_{1}+\delta v_{1}]-I[w_{1}]}{\delta}=I^{\prime}[w_{1}]v_{1}<0.

Therefore, for
\displaystyle\delta>0
small enough we get
\displaystyle I[w_{1}+\delta v_{1}]-I[w_{1}]<0
.

5.2.
Implementation Details

We focus here on the Dirichlet nonlocal problem. The results presented here can be extended to the Neumann problem by replacing
\displaystyle X_{D}^{1}
with
\displaystyle X_{N}^{1}
and
\displaystyle I_{D}
with
\displaystyle I_{N}
.

Let
\displaystyle w_{1}\in X_{D}^{1}
such that
\displaystyle I_{D}^{\prime}[w_{1}]\neq 0
. Consider the problem: find
\displaystyle b\in X_{D}^{1}
such that the following holds for all
\displaystyle v\in X_{D}^{1}

(5.2)

B_{D}[b,v]\ =\ I_{D}^{\prime}[w_{1}]v,

where
\displaystyle I^{\prime}_{D}[w_{1}]\in\mathcal{L}(X_{D}^{1},\mathbb{R})
is defined by

I^{\prime}_{D}[w_{1}]v\ =\ B_{D}[w_{1},v]-\int_{\Omega}f(x,w_{1})v\;dx.

Lemma 5.3
.

Let
\displaystyle b
be the solution of the problem
5.2
with
\displaystyle w_{1}\in X_{D}^{1}
such that
\displaystyle I^{\prime}[w_{1}]\neq 0
.
Then, we have

(5.3)

\beta_{2}\|b\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}\ \leq\ \|I_{D}^{\prime}[w_{1}]\|\ \leq\ C_{2}\|b\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)},

and

I_{D}^{\prime}[w_{1}]\left(\frac{-b}{\|b\|_{X_{D}}}\right)\ <\ 0,

where the constants
\displaystyle C_{2}
and
\displaystyle\beta_{2}
are introduced in Proposition
2.12
.

Proof.

We recall that
\displaystyle X_{D}^{1}
is equipped with the
\displaystyle H^{1}
norm. Therefore, we have

\|I_{D}^{\prime}[w_{1}]\|\ =\ \sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}v\in X_{D}^{1},\\
\|v\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}=1\end{subarray}}|I_{D}^{\prime}[w_{1}]v|.

Let
\displaystyle v\in X_{D}^{1}
be such that
\displaystyle\|v\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}=1
. Using the property
T3
, we get

|I_{D}^{\prime}[w_{1}]v|\ =\ \left|B[b,v]\right|\ \leq\ C_{2}\|b\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}\|v\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}\ =\ C_{2}\|b\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)},

which yields the upper bound of
5.3
.

The lower bound results from applying the property
T4
with
\displaystyle v=\frac{b}{\|b\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}}
. It reads

\|I_{D}^{\prime}[w_{1}]\|\ \geq\ \left|I_{D}^{\prime}[w_{1}]\frac{b}{\|b\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}}\right|\ =\ \left|B\left[b,\frac{b}{\|b\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}}\right]\right|\ =\ \frac{|B[b,b]|}{\|b\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}}\ \geq\ \beta_{2}\|b\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}.

To prove the second inequality, we write

I_{D}^{\prime}[w_{1}]\left(\frac{-b}{\|b\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}}\right)\ =\ -\frac{B[b,b]}{\|b\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}}\leq 0.

We note that the inequality is strict as
\displaystyle B[b,b]
being zero implies that
\displaystyle b
, and so
\displaystyle I_{D}^{\prime}[w_{1}]
, are zero leading to a contradiction.
∎

Note that Lemma
5.3
first allows us to get an upper bound for
\displaystyle\|I_{D}^{\prime}[w_{1}]\|
which we use to check if
\displaystyle\|I_{D}^{\prime}[w_{1}]\|\leq\epsilon
in step 3 of the above algorithm. For numerical validation, see following section, we will use the following stopping criterion in step 3

\|b\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}\leq\epsilon,

where we dropped the constant
\displaystyle C_{2}
from the upper bound
5.3
.
Moreover, the above Lemma also provides us with a definition for
\displaystyle v_{1}
in step 4 of the algorithm by setting

v_{1}=\frac{-b}{\|b\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}},

with
\displaystyle b
being the weak solution of the problem
5.2
.

5.3.
Numerical illustrations

We validate the algorithm described in Section
5.1
by considering various setups in which we impose homogeneous nonlocal Dirichlet or nonlocal Neumann boundary conditions. We consider three types of kernels that are either algebraically or exponentially decaying. The solution
\displaystyle b
of the problem
5.2
is approximated using a second order finite element method where the basis functions are defined as piecewise linear Lagrangian polynomials.

For a given mesh size
\displaystyle h>0
, used to approximate the solution of (
5.2
) with piecewise linear finite elements, we denote by
\displaystyle u_{h}^{*}
the approximation of the solution of problem (
1.1
) generated by our algorithm. As exact solutions are not known, we confirm the convergence of our algorithm by computing the norms
\displaystyle L^{1}
and
\displaystyle L^{2}
of the residual of (
1.1
) defined by:

\begin{array}[]{lll}R_{L^{1}}&\ :=&\int_{\Omega}|-\mathcal{L}u_{h}^{*}(x)-f(x,u_{h}^{*})|dx,\\
R_{L^{2}}&\ :=&\left(\int_{\Omega}(-\mathcal{L}u_{h}^{*}(x)-f(x,u_{h}^{*}))^{2}dx\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}.\end{array}

To further validate our algorithm, we use piecewise linear finite elements to approximate the solution
\displaystyle\bar{u}
of the problem

(5.4)

-\mathcal{L}\bar{u}\ =\ f(x,u^{*})\quad x\in\Omega,

supplemented with the same nonlocal boundary conditions used to obtain
\displaystyle u^{*}
.
For a given mesh size
\displaystyle h
, we can then compute the errors in
\displaystyle L^{1}
and
\displaystyle L^{2}
norms between
\displaystyle u_{h}^{*}
and
\displaystyle\bar{u}_{h}
given by

E_{L^{1}}\ =\ \left(\int_{\Omega}|u_{h}^{*}-\bar{u}_{h}|dx\right),

E_{L^{2}}\ =\ \left(\int_{\Omega}(u_{h}^{*}-\bar{u}_{h})^{2}dx\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

Remark 5.4
.

One could suggest using a classic Newton iterative solver to obtain an approximation of the solution to the nonlocal problem
1.1
. However, applying such a strategy to the examples considered in this section was shown to lead to solutions that do not
correctly approximate the equation. In particular, the residuals
\displaystyle R_{L^{1}}
-
\displaystyle R_{L^{2}}
and errors
\displaystyle E_{L^{1}}
-
\displaystyle E_{L^{2}}
are large and do not decrease with the mesh size. Therefore, developing algorithms like the one proposed here is essential to solve the problem
1.1
and could also be used to provide adequate initial guesses for Newton solvers. We will explore this direction in the future.

6.
Conclusion

This paper proved a multiplicity result for solutions to equations of the form
\displaystyle-\mathcal{L}u=f(x,u)
where
\displaystyle\mathcal{L}
is a convolution operator associated with radially symmetric
\displaystyle L^{1}
kernels with finite second moment,
and with either a nonlocal Dirichlet or a nonlocal Neumann boundary constraint. We also established numerically that a gradient descent-type algorithm tailored to energy functionals with a mountain pass structure allows for convergence even when standard Newton-type algorithms perform poorly. In the future, we plan to study extensively this algorithm by comparing results obtained with our gradient descent algorithm versus the stationary solution obtained from solving equations, like Allen-Cahn, over long time integration.

Numerous continuations to this project are readily available: for one, one can seek similar multiplicity results for equations with the same underlying operator but different geometric behavior, such as Linking-type energies (see
[
46
, Chapter 8]
for an overview of the Linking Theorem); semilinear problems with the same convolution structure also remain open. In addition, we will develop analytical and numerical results for optimal control problems with a constraint equation of type (
2.1
), using
[
45
]
as a guide for the analytic framework.

A.1.
Coercivity-type estimates for positive kernels

Lemma A.1
.

Consider the bilinear form
\displaystyle B_{N}:X^{1}_{N}\times X^{1}_{N}\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}
as given by Definition
2.8
. Assume further that the kernel
\displaystyle\gamma
given in the definition of
\displaystyle B_{N}
is strictly positive. Then, there exists a positive constant
\displaystyle\beta
, such that

(A.1)

\|u\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\ \leq\ \beta B_{N}[u,u].

This result and its proof resemble that of
[
38
, Proposition 1]
in the context of scalar peridynamic models.

Proof.

To reach a contradiction, assume that there exists a sequence
\displaystyle\{u_{k}\}
in
\displaystyle X^{1}_{N}\subset L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
with the property that
\displaystyle\|u_{k}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}=1
and that as
\displaystyle k\rightarrow\infty
,

|B_{N}[u_{k},u_{k}]|=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}\int_{\Omega}(u_{k}(y)-u_{k}(x))^{2}\gamma(x,y)\;dy\;dx\rightarrow 0.

We want to prove that
\displaystyle\|u_{k}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\rightarrow 0
as
\displaystyle k\rightarrow\infty
.

First, since the sequence is bounded in
\displaystyle L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, it has a weakly convergent subsequence, which we again label
as
\displaystyle\{u_{k}\}
. Then,
\displaystyle u_{k}\rightharpoonup\bar{u}
, for some
\displaystyle\bar{u}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
.

In what follows we will use the operator
\displaystyle\mathcal{L}:L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\rightarrow L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
given by

\mathcal{L}\phi\ =\ \int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(\phi(y)-\phi(x))\gamma(x,y)\;dy,

and the functionals
\displaystyle J_{k}:L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, which depend on a fixed
\displaystyle u_{k}\in X^{1}_{N}
, defined via

J_{k}(\phi)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\mathcal{L}(\phi)u_{k}\;dx.

Since the kernel
\displaystyle\gamma
is exponentially localized, the operator
\displaystyle\mathcal{L}:L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\rightarrow L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
is bounded. Therefore,

J_{k}(\phi)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\mathcal{L}(\phi)u_{k}\;dx\longrightarrow\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\mathcal{L}(\phi)\bar{u}\;dx,

At the same time, by the nonlocal form of Green’s identity (
2.4
),

J_{k}(\phi)\ =\ -\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(\phi(y)-\phi(x))\gamma(x,y)(u_{k}(y)-u_{k}(x))\;dy\;dx,

for all
\displaystyle\phi\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, and consequently we have that
\displaystyle J_{k}(\phi)
converges to

-\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(\phi(y)-\phi(x))\gamma(x,y)(\bar{u}(y)-\bar{u}(x))\;dy\;dx,

as
\displaystyle k\rightarrow\infty
.

Our first goal is to show that this last expression is equal to zero for all
\displaystyle\phi\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
.
Using Cauchy-Schwarz, we obtain

|J_{k}(\phi)|\ <\ \frac{1}{2}\left(\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}}(u_{k}(y)-u_{k}(x))^{2}\gamma(x,y)\;dy\;dx\right)^{1/2}\left(\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}}(\phi(y)-\phi(x))^{2}\gamma(x,y)\;dy\;dx\right)^{1/2},

and we notice that the second integral is bounded in
\displaystyle k
since it is equal to
\displaystyle B_{N}[\phi,\phi]
.
On the other hand, the first integral is equal to
\displaystyle B_{N}[u_{k},u_{k}]
, which converges to zero thanks to our
assumption on the sequence
\displaystyle\{u_{k}\}
. Therefore,
\displaystyle J_{k}(\phi)\rightarrow 0
.

Now, letting
\displaystyle\phi=\bar{u}
and keeping in mind that
\displaystyle J_{k}(\bar{u})\rightarrow-\frac{1}{2}B_{N}[\bar{u},\bar{u}]
,
this last result implies that

B_{N}[\bar{u},\bar{u}]=\iint_{\Omega\times\Omega}(\bar{u}(y)-\bar{u}(x))^{2}\gamma(x,y)\;dy\;dx=0

Then, since the kernel
\displaystyle\gamma>0
we also obtain that
\displaystyle\bar{u}
is constant in
\displaystyle\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Since constants are excluded from

\displaystyle L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, we must have that
\displaystyle\overline{u}=0
on
\displaystyle\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

The last thing we need to show is that the sequence
\displaystyle\{u_{k}\}
converges strongly to
\displaystyle\bar{u}
, and thus arrive at our desired contradiction.

Since for fixed
\displaystyle x
,
\displaystyle f(y):=\gamma(x-y)\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
and since the sequence
\displaystyle u_{k}\rightharpoonup 0
in
\displaystyle L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, it follows that
\displaystyle\gamma\ast u_{k}(x)\rightarrow 0
point-wise and then also in
\displaystyle L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
.
As a result,

\displaystyle\displaystyle J_{k}(u_{k})\ =\

\displaystyle\displaystyle\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}}(u_{k}(y)-u_{k}(x))\gamma(x,y)\;dy\;u_{k}(x)\;dx

\displaystyle\displaystyle J_{k}(u_{k})\ =\

\displaystyle\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}u_{k}(\gamma\ast u_{k})\;dx-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}|u_{k}|^{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\gamma(x,y)\;dy\;dx

\displaystyle\displaystyle J_{k}(u_{k})\ =\

\displaystyle\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}u_{k}(\gamma\ast u_{k})\;dx-\Gamma\|u_{k}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}.

Taking the limit as
\displaystyle k\rightarrow\infty

\Gamma\|u_{k}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}=|J_{k}(u_{k})|\longrightarrow 0,

completing the proof.
∎

To prove that the norms
\displaystyle X^{1}_{N}
and
\displaystyle L^{2}(\Omega)
are equivalent one first shows that the following exterior problem has a unique solution. This same result is shown in
[
14
,
16
]
.

Exterior Problem:
Let
\displaystyle g\in L^{2}(\Omega)
and consider

(A.2)

\begin{split}\mathcal{L}u\ &=\ 0\qquad x\in\Omega^{c}\\
u\ &=\ g\qquad x\in\Omega.\end{split}

Define the space

Y_{N}=\{u\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\mid\quad B_{N}[u,u]<\infty,\quad u(x)=0\quad x\in\Omega\}

with norm given by
\displaystyle\|u\|_{Y_{N}}=\|u\|_{L^{2}(\Omega^{c})}+|u|_{B}
where
\displaystyle|u|^{2}_{B}=B_{N}[u,u]
.
The weak formulation of the exterior problem then reads

(A.3)

B_{N}[\hat{u},v]\ =\ (\mathcal{L}\tilde{g},v)_{L^{2}(\Omega^{c})}\quad\forall v\in Y_{N}.

where
\displaystyle\hat{u}=u-\tilde{g}
and
\displaystyle\tilde{g}
is the extension of
\displaystyle g
by zero to
\displaystyle\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

Lemma A.2
.

Let
\displaystyle g\in L^{2}(\Omega)
and define
\displaystyle\tilde{g}
as its extension by zero to
\displaystyle\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Then, the nonlocal Dirichlet problem (
A.2
) has a unique weak solution
\displaystyle u\in L^{2}(\Omega)
, given by
\displaystyle u=\hat{u}+g
, where
\displaystyle\hat{u}\in Y_{N}
satisfies (
A.3
). Moreover, there is a positive constant
\displaystyle C
, such that

\|u\|_{L^{2}(\Omega^{c})}\ \leq\ C\|g\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}.

A proof of this result is given in
[
16
, Lemma 3.1]
and is a consequence of the Lax-Milgram theorem.
The next result is a consequence of the Poincaré inequality (
A.1
) and the above lemma.

Lemma A.3
.

There exist positive constants
\displaystyle c
and
\displaystyle C
such that for any
\displaystyle u\in X^{0}_{N}
,

c\|u\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\ \leq\ \|u\|_{X^{0}_{N}}\ \leq\ C\|u\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}.
