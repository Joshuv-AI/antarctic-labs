3. Executive Summary:
Cadmium telluride (CdTe) photovoltaics are a leading thin-film solar technology with
strong potential for low-cost, large-scale deployment in the United States. However,
device performance has been persistently limited by a plateau in open-circuit voltage
(Voc), typically in the range of 800–900 mV. This limitation is widely attributed to
incomplete understanding of defect activation and passivation during CdCl2 processing,
particularly in relation to dopant activation and carrier recombination. The objective of this
project was to elucidate the fundamental mechanisms governing defect chemistry, dopant
activation, and their interplay with microstructure in next-generation CdTe absorbers.
This project significantly advances the understanding of CdTe materials by establishing
a direct, nanoscale correlation between composition, structure, and electronic
performance. Using a combination of X-ray fluorescence (XRF), X-ray beam induced
current (XBIC), and X-ray absorption spectroscopy (XANES), the research provided
spatially resolved insight into dopant distribution, defect formation, and carrier collection.
These experimental techniques were integrated with drift-diffusion modeling (PyCDTS),
first-principles simulations (DFT and FEFF9), and machine learning-assisted data
analysis, enabling a comprehensive and quantitative interpretation of complex datasets.
This multiscale, multimodal approach represents a substantial methodological
advancement over prior studies that relied on isolated measurements or indirect
inference.
A central outcome of the project is the identification of the chemical origin of limited dopant
activation in CdTe. While dopants such as arsenic are intended to form shallow acceptor
states (e.g., As substituting on Te sites), this work demonstrates that a large fraction of
dopants instead form compensating or electrically inactive species. These include
dopant–chlorine complexes (e.g., As-Cl), oxide phases near interfaces, and secondary
compounds such as Cd2AsCl2. The presence of these species was directly observed and
quantified using XANES, revealing that a substantial portion of dopants do not contribute

 Page 2 of 21

to p-type conductivity. These findings provide a mechanistic explanation for the long-
standing limitation in achievable hole density and Voc in CdTe devices.
The project also demonstrated that carrier collection behavior is governed by the balance
between acceptor dopants and compensating donor defects, rather than electric field
alone. This was established through systematic XBIC measurements and validated with
drift-diffusion modeling, which showed that variations in dopant activation led to shifts in
junction location and, in some cases, buried junction formation. In addition, the role of
CdCl2 processing was clarified: while it enhances grain growth, passivation, and
photoluminescence (with observed increases exceeding 4x), it also introduces chlorine
that can contribute to dopant compensation. This dual role highlights a critical tradeoff in
current manufacturing processes.
From a technical standpoint, the methods developed and demonstrated in this project are
both effective and scalable. The integration of high-resolution X-ray microscopy with
computational modeling enables rapid, quantitative assessment of material properties
relevant to device performance.
The outcomes of this work have clear benefits to the public by supporting the
advancement of domestic solar energy technologies. By identifying the mechanisms
limiting CdTe efficiency and providing pathways to overcome them, this project
contributes to the development of higher-efficiency, lower-cost photovoltaic modules.
This, in turn, supports U.S. energy independence, reduces greenhouse gas emissions,
and strengthens the competitiveness of domestic solar manufacturing. The insights
gained also establish a foundation for future materials and process optimization,
accelerating innovation in thin-film photovoltaics and contributing to national clean energy
goals.

5. Background:

 Page 3 of 21

Cadmium telluride (CdTe) remains one of the most commercially successful thin-film
photovoltaic technologies due to its low manufacturing cost, scalability, and high
efficiency potential. Despite these advantages, further improvements in device
performance have been limited by persistent challenges in achieving high open-circuit
voltage (Voc), largely attributed to incomplete control of defect chemistry and dopant
activation. Recent advances in CdTe research have focused on improving p-type doping
using Group V dopants (e.g., As, P, Sb), optimizing CdCl2 processing, and understanding
defect-mediated recombination mechanisms1,2.
State of the Art in CdTe Doping and Processing
The CdCl2 treatment is widely recognized as a critical step in CdTe device fabrication. It
enhances grain growth, passivates grain boundary defects, and improves carrier lifetime,
thereby enabling high-efficiency devices3,4. However, it also introduces chlorine into the
absorber, which can act as an n-type dopant and potentially compensate acceptor
dopants5. This dual role of CdCl2 has been extensively reported but not fully understood
at the nanoscale.
Group V doping, particularly with arsenic, has emerged as a promising pathway to replace
traditional copper doping due to improved stability and higher achievable carrier lifetimes.
Studies have demonstrated that arsenic-doped CdTe can achieve hole concentrations
above 1016 cm-3 and efficiencies exceeding 20% under optimized conditions5,6. However,
a consistent limitation across polycrystalline CdTe systems is the low dopant activation
efficiency, typically on the order of 1–5%, even when higher incorporation levels are
achieved5,7. This discrepancy between incorporation and activation has been identified
as a key bottleneck for achieving higher Voc and overall device efficiency.
Fundamental studies using single-crystal CdTe have shown that significantly higher
activation efficiencies (>50%) are possible under controlled conditions, suggesting that
limitations in polycrystalline materials arise from additional mechanisms such as grain
boundaries, defect complexes, and processing conditions6. Prior work has also identified
self-compensation mechanisms, including the formation of donor-like defects and
complex defect states (e.g., AX centers), as potential causes of reduced doping
efficiency7. However, the exact chemical and structural origins of these compensating
mechanisms remain unresolved, and no experimental evidence has been offered.
Relation to Prior Work
The present project builds directly on this body of literature by addressing the same
fundamental challenges:
 • Understanding dopant activation and compensation
 • Clarifying the role of CdCl2 processing
 • Linking defect chemistry to device performance
Like prior studies, this work investigates copper- and arsenic- doped CdTe and CdSeTe
absorbers and examines how processing conditions influence carrier concentration,
recombination, and microstructure. It also leverages established experimental techniques
such as X-ray absorption spectroscopy (XANES) and electrical characterization methods
(e.g., capacitance-voltage, photoluminescence) that have been widely used to probe
dopant behavior and defect states3,8.
Distinction from Prior Work

 Page 4 of 21

While previous studies have provided important insights into dopant activation and defect
energetics, they are typically limited in one or more of the following ways:
• Reliance on bulk or averaged measurements rather than spatially resolved analysis
• Use of single-crystal systems that do not fully capture polycrystalline device behavior
• Lack of direct correlation between chemical state, microstructure, and electronic
 performance
• Limited ability to identify specific defect complexes in operating devices
This project addresses these gaps through a multimodal, nanoscale characterization
framework that integrates:
 • X-ray fluorescence (XRF) for compositional mapping
 • X-ray beam induced current (XBIC) for local electrical performance
 • X-ray absorption spectroscopy (XANES) for chemical state identification
These techniques are applied with sub-micron resolution, enabling direct observation of
how dopant distribution and defect chemistry vary across device interfaces and within
grains. In addition, this work incorporates drift-diffusion modeling and first-principles
simulations, allowing experimental observations to be quantitatively interpreted in terms
of defect populations and electronic behavior9,10.
Leveraging Prior Work to Accelerate Progress
The project explicitly leverages prior literature in several ways to accelerate progress and
improve result quality:
• Use of established dopant systems (As, Cu, Cl): Prior studies identifying arsenic
 as a shallow acceptor and chlorine as a compensating donor informed the selection
 of materials and hypotheses tested in this work5.
• Adoption of advanced modeling frameworks: Existing drift-diffusion solvers and
 first-principles methods were extended to interpret nanoscale experimental data,
 reducing development time and enabling rapid validation of physical models9,10.
• Integration of known processing conditions: Industry-standard CdCl2 treatments
 and doping approaches were replicated and systematically varied, allowing direct
 comparison with state-of-the-art devices3.
• Extension of spectroscopy methodologies: Previous work on XANES analysis and
 defect identification was expanded through the development of simulated spectral
 libraries and machine learning-assisted fitting techniques, improving both accuracy
 and throughput8.
By combining these elements, the project moves beyond incremental improvements and
establishes a direct, mechanistic understanding of dopant activation limitations in
polycrystalline CdTe, bridging the gap between controlled model systems and real
devices.

6. Project Objectives:
The objective of this project is to develop a fundamental understanding of the interplay
between defect activation and defect passivation in CdTe absorbers in order to enable
higher-efficiency photovoltaic devices. This work addresses a critical limitation in CdTe

 Page 5 of 21

technology—the persistent stagnation of open-circuit voltage (Voc)—which is closely tied
to incomplete dopant activation and defect-mediated recombination. By resolving the
underlying physical and chemical mechanisms that govern carrier concentration and
recombination, this project directly supports national clean energy goals focused on
improving solar module efficiency while maintaining low manufacturing cost.
Advancements in CdTe performance are particularly important for the United States,
where domestic manufacturing of thin-film photovoltaics represents a strategic
opportunity for energy independence, grid resilience, and economic competitiveness.
The impact of this work lies in its ability to provide actionable insights that can increase
device efficiency and energy yield, thereby reducing the levelized cost of electricity
(LCOE) for solar power. By identifying the mechanisms that limit dopant activation and
carrier collection, this project contributes to the development of more efficient and reliable
photovoltaic modules. These improvements support broader national objectives of
accelerating renewable energy deployment, reducing greenhouse gas emissions, and
strengthening the domestic solar industry. The outcomes of this research are expected
to benefit both industry and the public by enabling more cost-effective solar energy
systems and supporting continued innovation in clean energy technologies.
The project focuses on two central technical challenges in CdTe processing that directly
affect device performance. The first is understanding the mechanisms of CdCl2 treatment,
including its role in grain growth, recrystallization, selenium and tellurium interdiffusion,
and the modification of carrier lifetime and recombination pathways. While CdCl2
processing is essential for achieving high-efficiency CdTe devices, it also introduces
chemical species that can influence dopant behavior in complex ways. The second
challenge is understanding dopant activation, particularly for both traditional copper
dopants and alternative Group V dopants such as arsenic. Although Group V dopants
offer improved stability and the potential for higher carrier lifetimes, their activation
efficiency in polycrystalline CdTe remains low. This project seeks to determine the
chemical, structural, and electronic factors that control dopant activation and
compensation.
The expected outcomes of this work include a quantitative and mechanistic
understanding of how defects, dopants, and processing conditions interact to determine
carrier concentration and device performance. Specifically, the project aims to identify the
dominant defect species and dopant configurations present in CdTe absorbers, determine
how these species evolve during processing, and establish how they influence carrier
recombination and collection. In addition, the work seeks to develop predictive
relationships between processing conditions, material structure, and electronic behavior
that can guide optimization of CdTe devices. These outcomes provide a pathway toward
achieving higher hole densities, reducing recombination losses, and ultimately increasing
Voc.
The significance of this work lies in its integration of advanced characterization techniques
with computational modeling to directly link nanoscale material properties to device-level
performance. The project introduces a multimodal approach that combines X-ray
fluorescence (XRF), X-ray beam induced current (XBIC), and X-ray absorption
spectroscopy (XANES) to map composition, structure, and electrical response with sub-
micron resolution. These experimental capabilities are complemented by drift-diffusion
modeling and first-principles simulations, enabling quantitative interpretation of complex
 Page 6 of 21

datasets and identification of defect populations and electronic behavior. This integrated
approach represents a fundamental advance over prior work, which has typically relied
on either bulk measurements or isolated modeling efforts.
In addition to experimental and modeling innovations, the project advances data analysis
methodologies by incorporating high-throughput and machine learning techniques to
accelerate interpretation of spectroscopic data. These approaches reduce the time and
cost associated with complex measurements while maintaining analytical rigor,
increasing the feasibility of applying such methods in both research and industrial
settings. Collectively, these innovations enable a comprehensive understanding of defect
physics in CdTe that was previously inaccessible.
Through these combined efforts, the project establishes a scientific foundation for
overcoming key limitations in CdTe photovoltaics. By elucidating the mechanisms that
govern dopant activation and defect compensation, this work provides clear direction for
improving device efficiency and advancing the state of the art in thin-film solar technology.
These advances support national clean energy objectives by enabling more efficient,
cost-effective, and domestically produced solar energy solutions.

Dopant Activation and Compensation Mechanisms
A central outcome of this project is the identification of the chemical origin of dopant
activation limitations in CdTe. Results from multiple techniques consistently show that
dopants, while incorporated into the lattice, frequently form compensating or inactive
species. This mechanism suggests competing pathways of dopant incorporation,
activation, and compensation through interactions with chlorine and oxygen.

Task 7 – X-ray Excited Optical Luminescence (XEOL) and Defect-Specific Optical
Response
This task focused on the development and application of X-ray Excited Optical
Luminescence (XEOL) and related spectroscopic techniques to directly correlate local
chemical composition, electronic structure, and radiative recombination at the nanoscale.
The objective of this task was to provide a complementary probe to XBIC and XANES
measurements, enabling separation of defect contributions arising from the absorber
material versus those associated with device architecture and interfaces.
A key accomplishment of this task was the successful development and demonstration
of a multimodal measurement capability that combines X-ray excitation with simultaneous
detection of luminescence, electrical response, and chemical composition. This approach
enables pixel-by-pixel correlation between luminescence intensity, carrier collection
efficiency, and local chemical environment. The measurement concept is illustrated in

Initial XEOL measurements demonstrated the feasibility of collecting spatially resolved
luminescence maps with sub-micron resolution (50–100 nm scale), representing a
significant advance over conventional optical techniques. These measurements enable
direct observation of recombination-active regions and their correlation with
compositional and structural features. As shown in Figure 11, luminescence maps
acquired under X-ray excitation reveal spatial variations that are not fully captured by
electrical measurements alone, highlighting the presence of localized defect states that
influence radiative recombination.

In addition to steady-state luminescence, the project explored frequency-modulated
photoconductivity and synchrotron-based deep-level transient spectroscopy (DLTS) as
tools for probing defect states with energy and temperature selectivity. These
measurements provide insight into the energetic distribution and dynamics of trap states
within the absorber. Results show that temperature- and energy-dependent responses
reveal distinct defect signatures, including shallow and deeper trap states that vary with
excitation energy. These observations demonstrate the potential for site-selective
excitation of defect states, linking specific electronic transitions to local atomic
environments.
The combined XEOL and spectroscopic measurements provide a pathway toward directly
identifying the electronic impact of specific defect species detected through XANES. For
example, preliminary results indicate that defect states associated with oxygen and zinc
near interfaces can be selectively probed, providing additional insight into interface-driven
recombination mechanisms. This capability is particularly important for distinguishing
between bulk and contact-related losses, which cannot be easily separated using
electrical measurements alone.
While the proof-of-concept for XEOL and SDLTS techniques was successfully
demonstrated, several technical challenges were encountered that limited full
implementation during the project. These include low signal levels for certain dopants
(particularly arsenic), beamline-specific limitations in accessible energy ranges, and the
need for further optimization of detection sensitivity and experimental geometry. As a
result, the measurements presented here should be considered an initial demonstration
rather than a fully mature diagnostic tool.
Despite these challenges, this task established the feasibility and value of integrated
optical, electrical, and chemical nanoscale characterization. The ability to directly
                                             Page 15 of 21

correlate luminescence, with local composition, carrier collection and defect levels
represents a significant step toward identifying the specific defects responsible for
performance losses in CdTe devices. With further development, these techniques have
strong potential to become powerful diagnostic tools for both research and industrial
process optimization.

7.5 Comparison to Milestones and Go/No-Go Criteria
The project successfully met or exceeded the majority of its technical milestones. High-
resolution XRM mapping (<120 nm) was achieved, enabling quantitative correlation
between composition and carrier collection. CdCl2 processing was successfully
reproduced, with photoluminescence improvements exceeding the targeted 4x increase.
Computational tools demonstrated significant improvements in analysis speed and
enabled quantitative interpretation of spectroscopic data.
Go/No-Go criteria related to technical performance, analytical capability, and
dissemination were satisfied through successful demonstration of nanoscale
characterization, development of computational frameworks, and publication of results.
Minor deviations from planned activities were primarily associated with experimental
limitations but did not affect the overall conclusions or impact of the project.

8. Significant Accomplishments and Conclusions:
This project achieved a comprehensive, mechanistic understanding of defect activation,
passivation, and evolution in CdTe and CdSeTe absorbers by integrating advanced
nanoscale characterization, in-situ experimentation, and first-principles modeling. The
most significant accomplishment is the identification of the chemical and structural origins
of dopant compensation in CdTe, a long-standing limitation in achieving higher open-
circuit voltage (Voc). Through combined X-ray microscopy (XRF/XBIC), X-ray absorption
spectroscopy (XANES), and modeling, this work demonstrated that dopants such as Cu
and As, while incorporated into the lattice, are frequently rendered electrically inactive
through the formation of complexes with chlorine and oxygen, as well as secondary
phases. This finding provides a unifying explanation for the persistent limitation in
achievable hole density and establishes a clear link between CdCl2 processing and
dopant compensation.
A second major accomplishment is the development and deployment of a multimodal
nanoscale characterization framework capable of simultaneously probing composition,
structure, and carrier collection with sub-micron resolution. This capability enabled direct
correlation between local chemistry and device performance, revealing that carrier
collection is governed by the balance between acceptor dopants and compensating donor
defects rather than electric field alone. The observation of junction relocation and buried
junction behavior in low-activation samples represents a critical advance in understanding
how dopant activation influences device operation.
The project also successfully developed an in-situ X-ray microscopy platform capable of
replicating CdCl2 processing conditions while enabling simultaneous measurement of
compositional and structural changes. Using this platform, along with complementary ex-
situ and aging studies, the work provided direct evidence of selenium diffusion and

                                       Page 16 of 21

redistribution, both during CdCl2 treatment and under long-term thermal and illumination
stress. A key finding is that selenium exhibits both fast and slow diffusion pathways, with
unexpectedly low activation energies, indicating that extended defects such as grain
boundaries and dislocations play a dominant role in transport. Furthermore, XANES
analysis revealed that selenium local environments evolve during aging, including a
reduction in Se–Cl co-passivation and increased substitutional incorporation, which may
reduce defect passivation effectiveness over time.
From a computational perspective, the project generated one of the most extensive defect
energetics libraries for Cd(Se,Te)-based materials, incorporating thousands of
configurations from density functional theory (DFT) calculations. These results were used
to interpret experimental XANES spectra and identify energetically favorable defect
complexes, providing a direct link between atomic-scale structure and measured
spectroscopic signatures. In addition, machine learning approaches were developed to
accelerate spectroscopic analysis, significantly reducing the number of required
measurement energies while maintaining analytical fidelity. This represents an important
step toward scalable, high-throughput characterization of complex materials systems.
In addition to these positive outcomes, the project also identified several challenges and
limitations that provide important guidance for future work. One key challenge was the
difficulty of resolving low-concentration dopants, particularly arsenic, using X-ray
techniques at certain beamlines due to signal-to-noise limitations. This required careful
optimization of experimental conditions and, in some cases, limited the ability to fully
resolve dopant distributions in all regions of the device. Another challenge was the
complexity of interpreting XANES spectra in systems with overlapping spectral features
and multiple coexisting defect species, necessitating the development of improved fitting
methodologies and simulated spectral libraries.
A further important observation is that, despite significant advances in understanding
dopant behavior, the results indicate that dopant activation alone is not sufficient to
guarantee improved device performance. Instead, the interaction between dopants,
chlorine, oxygen, and extended defects must be considered holistically. In some cases,
processing conditions that improve one aspect of device performance (e.g., grain growth
or passivation) may simultaneously introduce compensating mechanisms that limit carrier
concentration. This highlights the need for integrated process optimization rather than
isolated improvements.
No major reductions in scope were required during the project; however, certain aspects
of the work evolved in response to emerging results. In particular, the focus expanded
from initial studies of dopant activation during processing to include long-term defect
evolution and aging mechanisms, driven by the observation that selenium and copper
redistribution continue under operating conditions. This shift provided a more complete
understanding of both performance limitations and device stability, aligning the project
more closely with real-world operating conditions.
Overall, the project delivers a set of fundamental insights and technical capabilities that
significantly advance the state of the art in CdTe photovoltaics. By identifying the
mechanisms that limit dopant activation and linking them to processing conditions and
microstructure, this work establishes a clear pathway toward improving device efficiency

                                       Page 17 of 21

and stability. These findings are directly relevant to U.S. photovoltaic manufacturing and
provide a foundation for future innovations in thin-film solar technologies.

9. Path Forward:
A primary area for future work is the optimization of dopant activation pathways,
particularly for Group V dopants such as arsenic. The results show that a significant
fraction of dopants is rendered inactive through the formation of complexes with chlorine
and oxygen or through segregation into secondary phases. Future efforts should
therefore focus on identifying processing conditions and material chemistries that
promote substitutional incorporation while minimizing compensating defect formation.
This may include controlled modification of CdCl2 treatment conditions (e.g., chlorine
concentration, ambient, and temperature), alternative activation schemes, or post-
processing steps that selectively disrupt compensating complexes. In parallel, further
computational studies and targeted experiments are needed to quantify the
thermodynamic and kinetic pathways governing dopant incorporation and complex
formation.
A second critical direction is the decoupling of the beneficial and detrimental effects of
chlorine. While CdCl2 processing is essential for grain growth and defect passivation, this
work demonstrates that chlorine also contributes to dopant compensation. Future R&D
should aim to retain the structural and passivation benefits of CdCl2 while limiting its
interaction with dopants. Potential strategies include alternative halide treatments,
spatially controlled introduction of chlorine, or process sequences that separate grain
growth from dopant activation. These approaches could enable higher carrier
concentrations without sacrificing material quality.
The project also highlights the importance of extended defects as pathways for mass
transport and defect evolution, particularly for selenium and copper. The observation of
fast diffusion during both processing and aging indicates that grain boundaries,
dislocations, and other extended defects play a dominant role in compositional
redistribution. Future work should therefore focus on controlling the density, structure,
and chemistry of these defects through deposition and annealing conditions. In addition,
further in-situ and operando studies are needed to directly observe defect evolution under
realistic processing and operating environments, including combined thermal, electrical,
and illumination stress.
Another key area for advancement is long-term device stability. The results demonstrate
that both selenium and copper continue to migrate under operating conditions, leading to
changes in local structure, carrier concentration, and contact behavior. Future studies
should aim to mitigate these effects by stabilizing dopant distributions, reducing the
mobility of key species, and improving contact interfaces. This may include the
development of diffusion barriers, alternative contact materials, or modified device
architectures that are less sensitive to compositional changes over time.
From a technology transfer perspective, the methodologies developed in this project-
particularly multimodal X-ray microscopy, XANES-based defect identification, and
integrated modeling frameworks-offer strong potential for adoption in industrial research
and development. These tools enable rapid, spatially resolved diagnosis of performance-
limiting mechanisms and can be used to guide process optimization and quality control.
Continued collaboration with industry partners will be important to translate these

                                       Page 18 of 21

capabilities into manufacturing environments, where throughput, reproducibility, and cost
constraints must be considered. The development of reduced-measurement and machine
learning–assisted analysis workflows represents a key step toward making these
techniques more practical for large-scale deployment.
Despite these opportunities, several technical risks and barriers to commercialization
remain. The primary risk is the complexity of defect chemistry in CdTe systems, where
multiple interacting species (dopants, chlorine, oxygen, and native defects) can lead to
competing and sometimes counteracting effects. Achieving consistent, high levels of
dopant activation across large-area devices will require precise control of processing
conditions and a deeper understanding of defect interactions. In addition, the continued
mobility of key species such as selenium and copper under operating conditions presents
a challenge for long-term stability, particularly for modules expected to operate for
decades in the field.
Another barrier is the translation of advanced characterization techniques to industrial
settings. While synchrotron-based methods provide unparalleled insight into material
behavior, their limited accessibility and throughput pose challenges for routine use.
Bridging this gap will require the development of simplified, high-throughput diagnostic
tools that capture the essential information needed for process control, informed by the
insights gained in this project.
