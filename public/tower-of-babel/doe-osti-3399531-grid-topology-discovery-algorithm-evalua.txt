 Executive Summary of CRADA Work
 This project, conducted by Pacific Northwest National Laboratory (PNNL), in collaboration with
 Survalent and Peninsula Light Company (PenLight), focused on evaluating topology discovery
 algorithms for their suitability in utility deployment. The partnership was instrumental in providing
 real-world feeder models and operational data from PenLight, as well as Advanced Distribution
 Management System (ADMS) software and valuable technical input from Survalent, ensuring
 that the research addressed the practical needs of distribution utilities. Using Milsoft-exported
 planning distribution system models deployed in OpenDSS format, along with advanced
 metering infrastructure (AMI) and SCADA data received from the Survalent ADMS, the team
 developed, validated, and tested a workflow that integrates utility-provided models with
 measurement data to support accurate topology identification.

 The project demonstrated a field-oriented approach by configuring and validating network
 models, integrating AMI and ADMS data, and conducting extensive simulations to evaluate tool
 performance under actual real-world conditions. Results showed that optimized sensor
 placement significantly improves accuracy and robustness of topology discovery, reducing
 misdetection and enhancing operational awareness. Final observations highlight both the
 potential and limitations of current algorithms, particularly under sparse measurement
 scenarios. The report concludes with recommendations for utilities to pursue incremental
 deployment of advanced sensors, prioritize data validation processes, and integrate topology
 discovery tools into operations platforms to improve situational awareness.

 The report is structured as follows:

 Chapter 1.0 introduces the topology identification problem and discusses the motivation behind
 this work. A brief background of the existing approaches, along with their limitations, is also
 given. Chapter 2.0 presents the formulation and details the developmental process of the
 proposed measurement-based topology identification algorithm. The algorithm was developed
 at PNNL, as part of the research on distribution model selection-based state estimation. Chapter
 3.0 and 4.0 discuss the implementation, validation, and demonstration of the topology
 identification algorithm. This work was performed as part of a Cooperative Research and
 Development Agreement (CRADA) agreement between PNNL and Survalent, with the
 overarching objective of demonstrating the topology discovery algorithm in a field test
 environment.

 Key Insights

 Some key insights from the report are summarized below.

 • This work offers actionable insights for integrating utility datasets and workflows into open-
 source simulators and optimization frameworks to enhance grid reliability and visibility.

 • The results in the report highlight that sensor placement is critical to algorithmic accuracy.
 Many existing sensors in the distribution systems that are deployed for protection or
 operational purposes may not always provide the data that is needed for topology
 discovery.

 • A cost-effective sensor deployment strategy that supports multiple objectives is important
 to ensure system observability.

 1.0 Introduction to Topology Identification
 Advanced distribution management applications such as model-based state estimation,
 volt/VAR control, distributed energy resource management, outage management are highly
 contingent on maintaining an accurate grid model in the control center of the distribution system
 operator (Zamani and Nielsen 2020). The challenge of updating the system topology is
 complicated by the highly dynamic nature of electric distribution systems, which typically can
 undergo several reconfigurations in a single day (Li et al. 2018). These reconfigurations are
 driven by multiple operational objectives, including power equipment maintenance and service
 activities, restoring service after faults, minimizing technical losses, balancing feeder loading,
 etc.

 Unlike static planning models, the as-operated distribution network often differs from its
 designed (as-built) configuration due to frequent switching operations, both automatic and
 manual. As a result, maintaining an accurate and up-to-date representation of the network
 topology is essential. Without it, system operators risk making operational decisions based on
 outdated or incorrect models, which can compromise operational safety, reliability, resiliency,
 and efficiency of the distribution grid. As such, the need to identify time-varying operational
 topology is a central motivation for this work, which proposes a graph-theoretic approach to
 model selection.

 1.1 Challenges for Distribution System Model Discovery
 In transmission systems, the topology identification task is relatively straightforward due to
 the widespread deployment of telemetered devices, supervisory control and data acquisition
 (SCADA) systems, and phasor measurement units, which provide rich and high-resolution
 data for processing topology changes (Srivastava et al. 2022). Unlike transmission systems,
 which benefit from extensive redundancy in their metering infrastructure (redundancy ratios of
 1.7–2.2), distribution networks are sparsely equipped with real measurements. Distribution
 networks employ a wide variety of switching devices—such as circuit breakers, disconnector
 switches, load-break switches, tie switches, sectionalizing switches, fuses, and reclosers—to
 enable circuit reconfiguration and isolation. Due to uneven levels of automation and
 communication infrastructure, many of these switching devices cannot transfer their
 open/closed status in real time to the Distribution Management System (Dalavi et al. 2024).

 1.2 Distribution Operations Platform
 Figure 1 illustrates the flow of information and control between the grid, devices, and the
 distribution operations platform, with a particular focus on topology discovery. Sensor and meter
 data are collected from the grid as a function of ongoing operations and capture the effects of
 any actions taken by controllable devices. These measurements serve as a critical input to the
 model enhancement process, where topology discovery—conducted in collaboration with
 Survalent and PenLight—ensures that the system representation reflects the as-operated
 network rather than just the planned configuration. The enhanced model is then maintained
 within the distribution operations platform and supports applications that rely on an accurate,
 up-to-date understanding of the system for monitoring, situational awareness, and control.

 1.3 Approaches for Topology Identification
 Theoretically, topological identification is a nondeterministic polynomial time (NP)-hard
 combinatorial problem that involves searching for a feasible tree within a graph that aligns with
 the measurement data. NP-hard problems that include discrete and continuous measurements
 are not tractable for emerging power distribution systems with many switches and possible
 operating topologies.

 Different categories for solving the topology identification problem are based on 1) generalized
 state estimation (GSE), 2) optimization-based methods, and 3) data-driven approaches. While
 approaches to GSE allow for topology and error identification while simultaneously computing
 the distribution grid state, there are limitations to these approaches; some rely on forecasted or
 pseudo-measurements to work correctly (Korres and Manousakis 2012), and some algorithms
 must solve the GSE for each possible switch topology. A few approaches leverage mixed-
 integer-based optimization to solve the topology identification problem (Tian et al. 2016;
 Gandluru et al. 2020); however, mixed-integer problems are NP-hard and computationally
 complex (Kannan and Monma 1978). Data-driven methods based on regression, expected-
 maximization, and unsupervised machine learning are used to infer the topology of the grid.
 While data-driven approaches for GSE are promising, they pose a few implementation
 challenges. First, they rely on voltage and current measurements at high frequencies from micro
 phasor measurement units or power flow meters at unrealistic deployment levels. Second, most
 of these methods estimate topology within data windows, with window lengths on the scale of
 days or much longer. Similarly, some algorithms assume only one switch change at a time, thus
 restricting the applicability for a real-world distribution system. While many methods have been
 proposed to solve this problem, none have found practical use in the electric utility industry.
 Furthermore, changes in grid structure and function, as well as changes in grid requirements,
 make the problem more complex than past approaches could handle. Therefore, there is a need
 for an algorithm that can detect topology by accounting for practical constraints in the
 distribution grid. To this end, the project team at Pacific Northwest National Laboratory (PNNL)
 hardened existing approaches to ensure the effective evaluation of algorithms for their suitability
 in utility deployment.

 A two-stage approach for topology identification of power distribution systems is demonstrated
 in this work. A graph-theory analysis is implemented in the first stage to reduce the sample
 space of the given combinatorial problem. The system connectivity matrix and system
 measurements identify a set of candidate topologies. The second stage solves an estimation
 problem to infer the grid state of the candidate topologies. The model with the least objective
 value (absolute difference between measurements and grid state) overall is selected as the
 identified topology for the given set of measurements. Unlike mixed-integer-based formulations,

 we adopt a graph-theoretic approach to shrink the sample space of possible switch
 combinations, thus avoiding an exhaustive search among all possible topologies. For solving
 the model selection problem, we leverage sparse measurements from the lines and buses
 where the distribution network is typically instrumented, such as reclosers. Furthermore, the
 algorithm implemented in this work is not restricted to the degree of topology change as
 determined by the number of switch statuses that have changed from the last known topology.

 2.1 Methodology
 The process is divided into two stages: 1) graph-based candidate topology enumeration and
 2) state estimation (SE)-based model selection (SEMS) on the candidate topologies. The first
 stage is carried out in two steps: a) model reduction and b) case reduction. The model reduction
 step is done offline using a static planning model of the distribution network. The case reduction
 step and SEMS stage are implemented in real time using graph-theory analysis and
 optimization, respectively. Note that these processes could be started in parallel to improve
 performance.

 Reducing candidate topologies before invoking the model selection stage can greatly improve
 the computational efficiency of the topology identification process. Since there are several
 possible switching combinations for a traditional SE to calculate a prediction from, it is also more
 likely that an inaccurate topology prediction would occur over the increased sample space

 compared to the reduced sample space. To this end, the proposed graph-theoretic approach
 can improve accuracy and computational performance.

 2.1.1 Model Reduction and Case Reduction

 A distribution planning model comprises multiple feeders and can be operated in numerous
 operational topologies by choosing different switch combinations, as shown in Figure 2. The
 planning model is represented by an undirected graph 𝐺 = 𝑉, 𝐸; the set 𝑉, with cardinality
       |𝑉| = 𝑛 representing buses, and the set 𝐸, with cardinality |𝐸| = 𝑚 representing edges
 associated with the electrical lines, transformers, and switching devices. For the topology
 identification problem, it is the position (ON/OFF) of switching devices that needs to be
 discovered. In this work, we implement a graph-theory-based approach for case reduction to
 eliminate several topologies before invoking the SE algorithm. To implement the case reduction
 technique, only switch-level information is required; thus, the graph-minor technique is
 implemented to consolidate all non-switch edges (Figure 3).

 contract all the edges except the ones with switches, preserving the number of collapsed nodes

 from a graph while simultaneously merging the two vertices it connects. Model reduction using
 graph minor is accomplished using Algorithm 1 as implemented by Python’s “networkx” package
       (Hagberg et al. 2008).

 The reduced model preserves the information necessary for the case reduction procedure.

 output is a new graph object 𝐺𝑐 with endpoints of non-switchable edges (𝑒 ∉ 𝐸𝑠 ) identified in a

 3.5.1 Topology and Parameter Validation

 This function thoroughly checks topology, base voltages, and component parameters to ensure
 the operational accuracy of the network representation.

 Topology validation involves both visual inspection and graph-theory-based analysis. Visual
 inspection helps identify obvious modeling errors such as isolated components, phase
 inconsistencies, or disconnected buses. Graph-theory analysis is used to detect isolated
 networks or unintended partitions within the system systematically. Where missing connectivity
 is identified, discussions with utility partners are initiated to update the model with the
 appropriate components and ensure a fully connected network representation. Additionally, the
 radiality of the system is tested by confirming the network’s as-operated model with utility
 operators, ensuring that the model accurately reflects the operational configuration of the real-
 world distribution system.

 Nominal voltage validation focuses on confirming that the primary and secondary voltages of
 transformers, as well as the nominal voltages assigned to other components—such as

Topology Identification Algorithm – Implementation and Validation 11

 capacitors, loads, distributed energy resources, and voltage regulators—are correctly specified.
 Special attention is given to the control modes of regulators to ensure consistency with field
 operation.

 Component parameter validation includes verifying that critical physical and electrical
 parameters are reasonable and correctly assigned. This includes verifying that line impedance
 parameters, such as insulation values and geometric mean radius, transformer reactance
 values, and line lengths, are non-negative and non-zero where applicable, and that their units
 are consistent and accurate. Cross-verification is performed against engineering standards or
 utility-provided specifications to ensure fidelity.

 This validation process ensures that the distribution network model is configured correctly and is
 ready to conduct a load flow study.

 3.5.2 AMI Data Integration

 Integrating the AMI measurements from individual sites requires three steps. First, the data
 must be mapped to the correct transformer to which the meter would be connected. Second, the
 site power must be applied to one of the potential feeders connected to the secondary side of
 the transformer. Finally, the integrated sensor data must be verified to be a reasonable
 measurement.

 Figure 8 illustrates the process of integrating AMI data from a meter database into an OpenDSS

 individual customer meters. Although this AMI data may not be available in real time, it can
 typically be exported in a structured format, such as a CSV file. To associate this meter data
 with the physical distribution network, ADMS model is leveraged. This allows us to map each
 asset ID (meter ID) to its corresponding service transformer as well as line segment the
 transformer is connected to, enabling us to determine which transformer each customer meter is
 connected to. As multiple meters can be associated with a single service transformer, each
 meter is linked to a specific tripled node within the network model. This creates a direct
 connection between meter readings and precise locations in the network topology.

 3.6 Correcting Model and Component Definitions
 A major objective of the model development and the correction process (Figure 9) is to identify
 the switch elements and the associated switch properties. The process begins by parsing the
 switch data and identifying all the switch types (breakers, reclosers, fuses, reactors). The data
 parsing is performed in Python 3.12, which uses a “py-dss-interface” package to interface with
 the OpenDSS solver engine. The properties of the switch objects are retrieved from the
 OpenDSS models using the Application Programming Interface (API) calls to connect with the
 compiled model of the circuit.

 In the second step of the switch mapping process, the “fuse” objects are identified and removed.
 The switches that are identified are added to the OpenDSS model. With these changes, a new
 OpenDSS switch file (Switch.DSS) is created, and the corresponding “Reactor.DSS” file that
 originally contained all the switch types is modified. Appropriate modifications are made to the
 “SwtControl.DSS” files that contain the control logic of the reactors in the circuit. The process is
 repeated for the second distribution system, and a new switch file (with all the switches except
 fuses) is created, along with new reactor and switch control files.

 5.0 Summary of Research Results
 This project successfully performed a demonstration of a topology discovery algorithm in
 collaboration with Survalent and Peninsula Light Company, leveraging real feeder models and
 sensor data to ensure practical relevance. Through this demonstration, the team showed that
 advanced topology discovery tools can be applied in real-world environments by interfacing
 directly with the ADMS system. The approach relies on actual field data, with all its typical
 deficiencies and characteristics, highlighting the system’s capability to handle real operational
 conditions. As a result, these tools can enhance situational awareness in distribution system
 operations.

 Additionally, results highlighted that sensor placement is critical to algorithm accuracy, yet many
 existing sensors in the distribution grid—such as reclosers—are deployed for protection or
 operational purposes and may not always provide the data needed for topology discovery.
 Therefore, it is essential to conduct sensitivity analyses to determine cost-effective sensor
 deployment strategies that support multiple objectives while ensuring system observability.
 While limitations remain in handling sparse or imperfect data, the research provides a pathway
 for incremental improvements and field deployment. Overall, the study offers actionable insights
 for utilities seeking to modernize operations and lays the groundwork for integrating topology
 discovery tools into commercial platforms to enhance grid reliability and visibility.
