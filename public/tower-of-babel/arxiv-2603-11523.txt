Strict Optimality of Frequency and Distribution Estimation Under Local Differential Privacy
Mingen Pan

Abstract




Abstract

This paper establishes the strict optimality in precision for frequency and distribution estimation under local differential privacy (LDP). We prove that a linear estimator with a symmetric and extremal configuration, and a constant support size equal to an optimized value, is sufficient to achieve the theoretical lower bound of the
\mathcal{L}_{2}
loss for both frequency and distribution estimation. The theoretical
\mathcal{L}_{1}
lower bound is also achieved asymptotically. Furthermore, we derive that the communication cost of such an optimal estimator can be as low as
\log_{2}(\frac{d(d-1)}{2}+1)
bits, where
d
denotes the dictionary size, and propose an algorithm to generate this optimal estimator.

In addition, we introduce a modified Count-Mean Sketch and demonstrate that it is practically indistinguishable from theoretical optimality with a sufficiently large dictionary size (e.g.,
d=100
for a privacy parameter of
\epsilon=1
). We compare existing methods with our proposed optimal estimator to provide selection guidelines for practical deployment. Finally, the performance of these estimators is evaluated experimentally, showing that the empirical results are consistent with our theoretical derivations.

1
Introduction

The frequency of a dataset and the distribution of a population represent the most fundamental statistics. They serve as the essential building blocks for deriving a wide array of statistical properties, ranging from basic mean and variance to more complex higher-order moments.

While computing frequencies or distributions is straightforward when an analyst has unrestricted access to a dataset, typically involving a simple iteration using a hash map, the task becomes significantly more complex when raw individual values are inaccessible. This is often the case when robust data privacy protections are implemented to mask sensitive information.

This paper focuses on a privacy-preserving technique known as Local Differential Privacy (LDP)
[
13
]
, the localized counterpart to Differential Privacy (DP)
[
9
]
, which has become the
de facto
standard for data privacy. Before reporting an aggregate statistic from a dataset, DP injects sufficient noise into the result to ensure that an adversary cannot determine whether a specific data point was included in the original set. However, DP traditionally requires centralized data processing, meaning it collects and stores raw data from clients before adding noise to the final aggregation. This architecture leaves data vulnerable to server breaches and eavesdropping during the transmission of raw values.

LDP was proposed to mitigate these risks by perturbing data locally on the client side before it is sent to the server. Consequently, even if intercepted, an adversary cannot effectively infer the original value from the perturbed output. The degree of this perturbation is quantified by a privacy parameter,
\epsilon
, representing the maximum amount of knowledge an adversary can gain from the perturbed result
[
15
]
.

Since frequency and distribution are such fundamental statistical properties, numerous LDP algorithms have been developed to estimate them effectively. These algorithms are discussed in detail in Section
1.1
. Among them, the Subset Selection mechanism proposed by
[
19
,
22
]
achieved state-of-the-art precision regarding
\mathcal{L}_{1}
and
\mathcal{L}_{2}
losses. These metrics, formally defined in Section
2.2
, measure the overall precision across all estimated frequencies or distributions. Notably, since the introduction of Subset Selection, no subsequent LDP estimator has surpassed its leading precision in these metrics.

While the leading precision has remained unsurpassed for years, it remains unknown whether these algorithms are strictly optimal. The Subset Selection paper
[
22
]
established a lower bound for LDP distribution
estimation, proving that their algorithm is order-optimal when
e^{\epsilon}\ll d
. However, a significant gap remains in the constant term; specifically, the derived lower bound can be as low as
\frac{1}{512}
of the achieved precision.
A follow-up study
[
23
]
suggested that Subset Selection achieves asymptotic strict optimality for distribution estimation, but this does not apply to finite datasets or frequency estimation (see Section
1.1
). It remains unclear whether an algorithm exists to bridge this gap, or if current approaches are already strictly optimal.

This paper directly addresses these questions by establishing a strict lower bound for LDP frequency and distribution estimation precision in terms of
\mathcal{L}_{1}
and
\mathcal{L}_{2}
losses:

Proposition 1
.

Let
d
,
\epsilon
,
n
, and
\hat{f}
denote the dictionary size (the number of all possible inputs), privacy parameter, dataset size, and frequency estimator, respectively. The minimum
\mathcal{L}_{2}
loss is given by

\min_{\hat{f}}\mathcal{L}_{2}(\hat{f})=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\frac{(d-1)[4de^{\epsilon}-(e^{\epsilon}+1)^{2}]}{nd(e^{\epsilon}-1)^{2}}&\text{if }d\geq e^{\epsilon}+1,\\[15.0pt]
\displaystyle\frac{(d-1)(d+2e^{\epsilon}-2)}{n(e^{\epsilon}-1)^{2}}&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

Meanwhile,
\min_{\hat{f}}\mathcal{L}_{1}(\hat{f})\approx\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}\min_{\hat{f}}\mathcal{L}_{2}(\hat{f})}.

Full details are presented in Theorem
3.8
and Corollary
3.3
. Distribution estimation has a similar result:
\min_{\hat{\theta}}\mathcal{L}_{2}(\hat{\theta},n)=\min_{\hat{f}}\mathcal{L}_{2}(\hat{f},n)+\frac{1-1/d}{n}
, and
\min_{\hat{\theta}}\mathcal{L}_{1}(\hat{\theta},n)\approx\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}\min_{\hat{\theta}}\mathcal{L}_{2}(\hat{\theta},n)}
, where
\hat{\theta}
denotes a distribution estimator.

To establish these tight lower bounds, this paper first demonstrates that an estimator constructed from a uniformly random permutation (URP), when paired with an optimized reconstruction matrix, achieves the theoretical
\mathcal{L}_{2}
loss lower bound derived from the Fisher information for any given perturbation matrix. The theoretical
\mathcal{L}_{1}
lower bound is also achieved asymptotically. We then prove that these losses are further minimized when the perturbation matrix maintains a constant support size across all responses. Under these conditions, we express
\mathcal{L}_{1}
and
\mathcal{L}_{2}
as functions of the support size while holding other parameters constant. Finally, we determine the optimal support size to derive the strict lower bounds for both loss functions. The complete proof is provided in Section
3
.

Furthermore, we derive that the communication cost, defined as the number of bits required to transfer a response from a client, for an optimal estimator is upper bounded by
\log_{2}(\frac{d(d-1)}{2}+1)
bits, where
d
denotes the dictionary size. We then develop an optimal estimator, Weighted Subset Selection, which achieves this reduced communication cost despite having high precomputation requirements. Subsequently, we evaluate the optimality of existing algorithms and find that Subset Selection
[
22
]
already achieves strict optimality in terms of precision, while its communication cost is linear in
d
. We also demonstrate that the Count-Mean Sketch (CMS), with minor modifications, can approach strict optimality provided the dictionary size is sufficiently large, such as when
d=100
for
\epsilon=1
. Notably, this modified version, which we refer to as Optimized CMS, requires a significantly lower communication cost of
O(\log_{2}(d))
bits, making it much more efficient than Subset Selection. Consequently, we provide practical guidance for their deployment; the rule of thumb is to employ Optimized CMS when the dictionary size is sufficiently large and to use either Weighted Subset Selection or the original Subset Selection otherwise.

Finally, we conduct two experiments in Section
6
to compare the performance of the optimal algorithms discussed in Section
5
with the theoretical strict lower bounds of
\mathcal{L}_{1}
and
\mathcal{L}_{2}
. Both experiments demonstrate that all three optimal algorithms align perfectly with the strict lower bounds.

1.1
Previous Work

Numerous LDP algorithms have been developed for frequency and distribution estimation. Randomized Response
[
21
,
12
]
, predating the formal LDP definition, was initially designed for the frequency estimation of sensitive data. RAPPOR
[
10
]
later achieved estimation precision for individual values independent of the dictionary size
d
, although its communication cost remains linear in
d
. Subsequently, methods such as Hadamard Encoding
[
4
]
, private Count-Mean Sketch
[
8
]
, Local Hashing and asymmetric RAPPOR
[
20
]
, Subset Selection
[
22
]
, Hadamard Response families
[
1
,
7
]
, Projective Geometry
[
11
]
, Block Design
[
17
]
, Optimized Count-Mean Sketch
[
16
]
, and Modular Subset Selection
[
2
]
were developed to enhance precision, with several reducing the communication cost to
O(\log d)
. Among these, Subset Selection
[
22
]
achieves state-of-the-art precision for
\mathcal{L}_{1}
and
\mathcal{L}_{2}
metrics, while some algorithms
[
11
,
16
]
asymptotically approach this performance.

Several existing studies analyze the optimality of LDP distribution estimation. Kairouz et al. (2014)
[
12
]
demonstrated that any optimal LDP algorithm can be converted into an extremal configuration. While Ye and Barg (2018)
[
22
]
established lower bounds for
\mathcal{L}_{1}
and
\mathcal{L}_{2}
losses when
e^{\epsilon}\ll d
, these bounds are not tight and have constant terms that are significantly smaller than the strict bounds derived in this work. A follow-up study
[
23
]
proved that Subset Selection achieves asymptotic strict optimality for
\mathcal{L}_{1}
and
\mathcal{L}_{2}
in distribution estimation. However, their proof relies on numerous approximations requiring an extremely large dataset size
n
. Furthermore, they do not address how Subset Selection deviates from optimal bounds when
n
is finite. Finally, their proof of optimality for distribution estimation, which involves the probability mass function (PMF) of a multinomial distribution, does not directly translate to frequency estimation; the latter involves estimating a Poisson multinomial distribution, the PMF of which lacks a concise closed-form expression. In contrast, our paper derives the strict
\mathcal{L}_{2}
lower bound for any dataset size in both frequency and distribution estimation, and provides a sufficient condition to achieve this bound.

2
Background

2.1
Local Differential Privacy (LDP)

Local Differential Privacy (LDP) was initially proposed in
[
13
]
to ensure that querying an individual object does not significantly disclose its actual value. Consider an object
X\in[d]=\{1,2,..,d\}
being queried by a mechanism
M
:

Definiton 2.1
.

M
is
\epsilon
-LDP if and only if
\forall x,x^{\prime}\in[d]
, and
\forall o\in[m]
,

\Pr(M(X)=o|X=x)\leq e^{\epsilon}Pr(M(X)=o|X=x^{\prime}),

where
m
represents the output domain size of
M
. In this paper, we may abbreviate
(M(X)|X=x)
as
M(x)
when the specific object is not the primary focus and its value is denoted by
x
.

2.2
Frequency Estimation

Denote
X^{(i)}
to be the
i
-th object of a dataset of size
n
, where each object takes a value from a dictionary
[d]
. Thus,
\forall i\in[n]:X^{(i)}\in[d]
. The true frequency of a value
x
is defined as
f(x)=\frac{\sum_{i\in[n]}\mathbbm{1}(X^{(i)}=x)}{n}
, where
\mathbbm{1}
denotes an indicator function. Let
\mathbf{f}
be the true frequency vector such that its
x
-th element represents
f(x)
.

Under LDP, the privacy of the original dataset is preserved by preventing direct access to its raw values. Instead, each client perturbs their original value using an LDP mechanism
M
and reports the result to the server for frequency estimation. The process is defined as follows:

1.

Each client
X
returns a response
O=M(X)
, represented as a one-hot encoding. If
O
corresponds to a value
o
, its
o
-th element is set to one.

2.

The server collects all responses
[O^{(i)}]_{i\in[n]}
and computes their frequency vector
\mathbf{f_{O}}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}M(X^{(i)})
. The
o
-th element of this vector represents the frequency of the output
o
across all responses.

3.

The estimated frequency vector is computed as
\mathbf{\hat{f}}=\hat{f}(\mathbf{f_{O}})
, where
\hat{f}
is an estimator and the
x
-th element of
\mathbf{\hat{f}}
, denoted
\hat{f}(x)
, serves as an estimate of the true frequency
f(x)
.

To evaluate the overall precision of an estimator
\hat{f}
, we define
L_{1}
and
L_{2}
norms as:

\displaystyle L_{1}(\hat{f},\mathbf{f},n)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{x=1}^{d}|\hat{f}(x)-f(x)|\right],

\displaystyle L_{2}(\hat{f},\mathbf{f},n)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{x=1}^{d}(\hat{f}(x)-f(x))^{2}\right].

Assuming the estimator is unbiased (i.e.,
\forall x:\mathbb{E}[\hat{f}(x)]=f(x)
), we have
\mathbb{E}[(\hat{f}(x)-f(x))^{2}]=\text{Var}(\hat{f}(x))
. Consequently, the
L_{2}
norm can be expressed as:

L_{2}(\hat{f},\mathbf{f},n)=\sum_{x=1}^{d}\text{Var}(\hat{f}(x)).

(1)

Additionally, the worst-case norms (i.e., loss functions) are defined to be

\mathcal{L}_{1|2}(\hat{f},n)=\max_{\mathbf{f}}L_{1|2}(\hat{f},\mathbf{f},n),

where
\mathcal{L}_{1|2}
can be either
\mathcal{L}_{1}
or
\mathcal{L}_{2}
.

Let
P
be the perturbation matrix of
M
, where
P_{o,x}=\Pr(M(x)=o)
. Since
\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{f_{O}}]=P\mathbf{f}
, a linear transformation
Q
can reconstruct
\mathbf{f}
from
\mathbf{f_{O}}
if
QP=I
, where
I
is the identity matrix.

Definiton 2.2
.

\text{LN}(P,Q)=Q\mathbf{f_{O}}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i\in[n]}QM(X^{(i)})
denotes a linear estimator that uses
P
to perturb and
Q
to reconstruct.

Given that
\mathbb{E}[Q\mathbf{f_{O}}]=QP\mathbf{f}=\mathbf{f}
, we obtain the following result:

Theorem 2.1
.

\text{LN}(P,Q)
is an unbiased frequency estimator.

Notably,
P
must be of full column rank; otherwise,
\mathbf{f}
cannot be reconstructed unbiasedly from
\mathbf{f_{O}}
. Since the size of the response space may exceed the dictionary size (i.e.,
m\geq d
), there may exist multiple reconstruction matrices
Q
such that
QP=I
.

We define
\text{Var}(\hat{f})
as the variance vector of
\hat{f}=\text{LN}(P,Q)
, where
x
-th element represents
\text{Var}(\hat{f}(x))
. It can be formulated as follows:

Theorem 2.2
.

\text{Var}(\hat{f})=\frac{1}{n}[(Q\circ Q)P\mathbf{f}-\mathbf{f}],

where
(Q\circ Q)
denotes the point-wise (Hadamard) square, i.e.,
(Q\circ Q)_{i,j}=Q_{i,j}^{2}
.

The proof is provided in Appendix
A.1
.

The relationship between
L_{1}
and
\text{Var}(\hat{f}(x))
is established in the following theorem:

Theorem 2.3
.

The
L_{1}
of
\hat{f}=\text{LN}(P,Q)
is approximated as:

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}L_{1}(\hat{f},\mathbf{f},n)=\sum_{x\in[d]}\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}\text{Var}(\hat{f}(x))}.

(2)

The proof is provided in Appendix
A.3
.

2.3
Distribution Estimation

Assume that each object
X^{(i)}
are independently sampled from an identical category distribution
\mathcal{C}(\bm{\theta})
, where the
x
-th element of
\bm{\theta}
denotes the probability of generating value
x
. If the objects are perturbed by an LDP mechanism following Section
2.2
, the estimator
\text{LN}(P,Q)
also estimates the distribution
\bm{\theta}
. Denote
\hat{\bm{\theta}}
as the estimated distribution, which satisfies

Theorem 2.4
.

If
\hat{\theta}=\text{LN}(P,Q)
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\hat{\theta}]

\displaystyle=\bm{\theta}

\displaystyle\text{Var}(\hat{\theta})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}[(Q\circ Q)P\bm{\theta}-\bm{\theta}^{2}]

The detailed proof is provided in Appendix
A.2
.

2.4
Extremal Configuration

An LDP perturbation mechanism is defined as
extremal
if and only if each specific output has exactly two possible emission probabilities across all input values, and these probabilities maintain a ratio of
e^{\epsilon}
.

Definiton 2.3
.

M
is
\epsilon
-LDP and extremal if and only if
\forall x\in[d]
, and
\forall o\in\mathcal{O}
, the following condition holds:

\Pr(M(x)=o)\in\{p_{o},e^{\epsilon}p_{o}\},

where
p_{o}
is referred to as the base probability associated with response
o
.

Definiton 2.4
.

For a response
o
within an extremal configuration, the support set of
o
is defined as the set of inputs
\{x|\Pr(M(x)=o)=e^{\epsilon}p_{o}\}
.

The support size of
o
, denoted as
k_{o}
, represents the cardinality of this support set.

In the subsequent sections, we define
S_{o,x}
as an indicator function signifying whether
o
supports
x
. Specifically,
S_{o,x}=1
if
x
is an element of the support set of
o
, and
S_{o,x}=0
otherwise. This relationship can be expressed in matrix form as:

P=P_{O}[(e^{\epsilon}-1)S+J],

(3)

where
P_{O}=\text{diag}(p_{1},p_{2},\dots,p_{m})
and
J
is an
m\times d
all-ones matrix.

The extremal configuration satisfies an invariant property that is foundational for the subsequent sections:

Lemma 2.1
.

If
M
is an
\epsilon
-LDP extremal configuration, then

\forall x\in[d]:\sum_{o\in\mathcal{O}}S_{o,x}p_{o}=\frac{1-\sum_{o\in\mathcal{O}}p_{o}}{e^{\epsilon}-1}=\text{const}.

This property is derived directly by summing the probabilities of all possible responses:

\forall x\in[d]:\sum_{o\in\mathcal{O}}S_{o,x}e^{\epsilon}p_{o}+(1-S_{o,x})p_{o}=1\qed

Notably,
p^{*}=e^{\epsilon}\sum_{o\in\mathcal{O}}S_{o,x}p_{o}
is also referred to as the
self-support probability
(Definition
2.5
). The extremal configuration ensures that the self-support probability remains constant across all original values.

Existing research has established the following:

Theorem 2.5
.

Any estimator lacking an extremal configuration can be transformed into one that possesses such a configuration.

One may refer to
[
12
]
for a detailed proof. An informal justification is that any non-extremal response can be decomposed into a collection of extremal responses. For instance, consider a scenario where the dictionary size
d=3
. Suppose the probability of a response
o
satisfies
[Pr(M(x)=o)]_{x\in[d]}=[1,ke^{\epsilon},e^{\epsilon}]\cdot p_{o}
, where
k\in(e^{-\epsilon},1)
. In this case, the response
o
can be replaced by two new responses,
o_{1}
and
o_{2}
, characterized by the following probability sets:

\displaystyle[\Pr(M(x)=o_{1})]_{x\in[d]}=[1,e^{\epsilon},e^{\epsilon}]\cdot\frac{ke^{\epsilon}-1}{e^{\epsilon}-1}p_{o}

\displaystyle[\Pr(M(x)=o_{2})]_{x\in[d]}=[1,1,e^{\epsilon}]\cdot\frac{e^{\epsilon}(1-k)}{e^{\epsilon}-1}p_{o}.

An estimator can simply replace any occurrence of
o_{1}
or
o_{2}
as the response
o
, rendering it identical to the original reconstruction. Consequently, an optimal estimator with an extremal configuration always exists.

2.5
Symmetric Configuration

Wang et al.
[
20
]
reviewed the LDP frequency estimators available at that time and proposed a
symmetric configuration
framework that encompasses all existing estimators. The formal definition is as follows:

Definiton 2.5
.

An
\epsilon
-LDP mechanism
M
has a symmetric configuration, if and only if

\displaystyle\forall x\in[d]:\Pr(M(x))\in\{o|S_{o,x}=1\}=p^{*}.

\displaystyle\forall x,x^{\prime}\in[d]\text{ and }x\neq x^{\prime}:\Pr(M(x^{\prime}))\in\{o|S_{o,x}=1\}=q^{*}.

where
p^{*}
and
q^{*}
are constants independent of
x
and
x^{\prime}
, and
S
is the support matrix of
M
. The values
p^{*}
and
q^{*}
are referred to as the self-support and cross-support probabilities, respectively.

This can be expressed equivalently in matrix form as:

S^{T}P=(p^{*}-q^{*})I+q^{*}J

(4)

where
P
is the perturbation matrix of
M
,
I
is the identity matrix, and
J
is the all-ones matrix.
S^{T}P
is referred to as the
support probability matrix
.

An estimator for symmetric configuration is proposed by
[
20
]
as:

\hat{f}(x)=\hat{\theta}(x)=\frac{(\sum_{i\in[n]}S_{O^{(i)},x})/n-q^{*}}{p^{*}-q^{*}},

(5)

where
S_{O^{(i)},x}
is an indicator function denoting whether the response
O^{(i)}
from the
i
-th object supports the value
x
. The support counts can also be represented in matrix form:

[\sum_{i\in[n]}S_{O^{(i)},x}]_{x\in[d]}=S^{T}\sum_{i\in[n]}M(X^{(i)}).

(6)

The expectation and variance of this estimator are derived as follows:

Theorem 2.6
.

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\hat{f}]=\mathbf{f}

(7)

\displaystyle\text{Var}(\hat{f})=\frac{q^{*}(1-q^{*})}{n(p^{*}-q^{*})^{2}}+\mathbf{f}\frac{1-p^{*}-q^{*}}{n(p^{*}-q^{*})}.

(8)

\displaystyle\text{Var}(\hat{\theta})=\frac{q^{*}(1-q^{*})}{n(p^{*}-q^{*})^{2}}+\bm{\theta}\frac{1-p^{*}-q^{*}}{n(p^{*}-q^{*})}+\frac{\bm{\theta}-\bm{\theta}^{2}}{n}.

(9)

\mathbb{E}[\hat{f}]
and
\text{Var}(\hat{f})
were originally proven in
[
20
]
, while
\text{Var}(\hat{\theta})
is derived similarly. For frequency estimation, the result can be verified by treating the term
\sum_{i\in[n]}S_{o^{(i)},x}
as the sum of two independent binomial random variables:
\text{Bin}(nf(x),p^{*})
and
\text{Bin}(n(1-f(x)),q^{*})
. These variables represent the number of responses from the original value
x
and supporting
x
, and the number of responses from the original value other than
x
and supporting
x
, respectively. For distribution estimation, this term is instead modeled as
\text{Bin}(n,\theta(x)p^{*}+(1-\theta(x))q^{*})
.

2.6
Uniformly Random Permutation

This section presents an approach to transform an arbitrary linear estimator into the one with a symmetric configuration, which will be proven to be an optimal estimator in the following sections.

Let a linear estimator be denoted by
\hat{f}=\hat{\theta}=\text{LN}(P,Q)
, with its underlying perturbation mechanism denoted by
M
. We construct a new perturbation mechanism
U
as follows:

1.

Uniformly sample a permutation
\mathcal{X}_{i}
from the space of all possible permutations of the dictionary
\mathcal{X}=[d]
.

2.

Permute the columns of the perturbation matrix
P
according to
\mathcal{X}_{i}
to derive a new perturbation matrix
P_{i}
, which defines a corresponding new perturbation mechanism
M_{i}
.

3.

Return the output
M_{i}(X)
along with the chosen permutation
\mathcal{X}_{i}
.

There are
D=d!
possible permutations in total. Let
Z_{i}
be a permutation matrix such that the operation
AZ_{i}
reorders the columns of
A
according to the permutation
\mathcal{X}_{i}
. Its transpose satisfies
Z_{i}^{T}Z_{i}=Z_{i}Z_{i}^{T}=I
. These permutation matrices possess the following property:

Lemma 2.2
.

For any
d\times d
matrix
A
,

\frac{1}{D}\sum_{i=1}^{D}Z_{i}^{T}AZ_{i}=(a-b)I+bJ,

where
I
is an identity matrix and
J
is an all-ones matrix. The coefficients are defined as
a=\frac{1}{d}\sum_{i=1}^{d}A_{ii}
and
b=\frac{1}{d(d-1)}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\sum_{j\neq i,j=1}^{d}A_{ij}
.

Proof: The set of all permutations ensures that any index
i\in[d]
is mapped to any
i^{\prime}\in[d]
with probability
\frac{1}{d}
. Furthermore, any pair of distinct indices
(i,j)
with
i\neq j
is mapped to all possible pairs of distinct values with a uniform probability of
\frac{1}{d(d-1)}
.
∎

Subsequently, we establish the following:

Theorem 2.7
.

Perturbation
U
has a symmetric configuration.

Proof: For any permutation
i\in[D]
, the corresponding support probability matrix is given by
S_{i}^{T}P_{i}=Z_{i}^{T}SPZ_{i}
. Applying Lemma
2.2
, we obtain:

\frac{1}{D}\sum_{i\in[D]}S_{i}^{T}P_{i}=(a-b)I+bJ,

where
a
and
b
are constants, aligned with Definition
2.5
.
∎

Meanwhile, an unbiased reconstruction matrix for
U
can be derived from the original reconstruction matrix
Q
:

Theorem 2.8
.

Q_{U}=\begin{pmatrix}Q_{1},Q_{2},Q_{3},...,Q_{D}\end{pmatrix}

is an unbiased reconstruction matrix for
U
, where
\forall i:Q_{i}=Z_{i}^{T}Q
.

Proof: Given
P_{i}=PZ_{i}
, it follows that
Q_{i}P_{i}=I
. Since each permutation is sampled uniformly at random, the perturbation matrix for
U
is given by:

P_{U}=\frac{1}{D}\begin{pmatrix}P_{1},P_{2},P_{3},...,P_{D}\end{pmatrix}^{T}.

Consequently, we have

Q_{U}P_{U}=\frac{1}{D}\sum_{i\in[D]}Q_{i}P_{i}=I.\qed

(10)

As a result, an estimator based on a uniformly random permutation is formally defined as follows:

Definiton 2.6
.

\text{URP}(P,Q)=\text{LN}(P_{U},Q_{U})
, where

\displaystyle P_{U}=\frac{1}{D}(PZ_{1},PZ_{2},...,PZ_{D})^{T}

\displaystyle Q_{U}=(Z_{1}^{T}Q,Z_{2}^{T}Q,...,Z_{D}^{T}Q)

and
QP=I
.

In analyzing its variance, following a derivation similar to Eq. (
10
), we obtain:

(Q_{U}\circ Q_{U})P_{U}=\frac{1}{D}\sum_{i\in[D]}(Q_{i}\circ Q_{i})P_{i}

Applying Theorem
2.2
, we establish the following lemma:

Lemma 2.3
.

\displaystyle\text{Var}(\hat{f}_{U})=(Q_{U}\circ Q_{U})P_{U}\mathbf{f}-\mathbf{f}=\frac{1}{D}\sum_{i\in[D]}\text{Var}(\hat{f}_{i}),

(11)

\displaystyle\text{Var}(\hat{\theta}_{U})=(Q_{U}\circ Q_{U})P_{U}\bm{\theta}-\bm{\theta}^{2}=\frac{1}{D}\sum_{i\in[D]}\text{Var}(\hat{\theta}_{i})

(12)

where
\hat{f}_{U}=\text{URP}(P,Q)
and
\hat{f}_{i}=\hat{\theta}_{i}=\text{LN}(P_{i},Q_{i})
, respectively.

Substituting Lemma
2.3
into Lemma
2.2
, we obtain

\frac{1}{D}\sum_{i\in[D]}(Q_{i}\circ Q_{i})P_{i}=\frac{1}{D}\sum_{i\in[D]}Z_{i}^{T}(Q\circ Q)PZ_{i}=(\alpha-\beta)I+\beta J,

where
\alpha=\frac{1}{d}\sum_{i\in d}[(Q\circ Q)P)]_{ii}
and
\beta=\frac{1}{d(d-1)}\sum_{i\in d}\sum_{j\neq i}[(Q\circ Q)P)]_{ij}
. Consequently, we have proven that:

Theorem 2.9
.

The variance of
\text{URP}(P,Q)
satisfies

\displaystyle\text{Var}(\hat{f}_{U})

\displaystyle=(\alpha-\beta)\mathbf{f}+\beta-\mathbf{f},

\displaystyle\text{Var}(\hat{\theta}_{U})

\displaystyle=(\alpha-\beta)\bm{\theta}+\beta-\bm{\theta}^{2},

where
\alpha=\frac{1}{d}\sum_{i\in[d]}[(Q\circ Q)P)]_{ii}

and
\beta=\frac{1}{d(d-1)}\sum_{i\in[d]}\sum_{j\neq i}[(Q\circ Q)P)]_{ij}
.

Additionally, applying Theorem
2.9
to Theorem
2.3
,
\mathcal{L}_{1}
can be formulated as a function of
\mathcal{L}_{2}
:

Corollary 2.1
.

For a frequency or distribution estimator
\hat{r}
with a uniformly random permutation:

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\mathcal{L}_{1}(\hat{r},n)=\sqrt{\frac{2d}{\pi}\mathcal{L}_{2}(\hat{r},n)}.

The detailed proof is provided in Appendix

A.3
.

3
Strict Optimality in Precision

This section frames the optimality of
\mathcal{L}_{1}
and
\mathcal{L}_{2}
as two distinct minimization problems:

\min_{\hat{r}}\mathcal{L}_{1|2}(\hat{r},n)=\min_{P}\min_{\hat{r}\in\mathcal{R}(P)}\mathcal{L}_{1|2}(\hat{r},n),

(13)

where
\hat{r}
denotes either
\hat{f}
or
\hat{\theta}
, and
\mathcal{R}(P)
represents the set of all estimators that use matrix
P
for perturbation (
\mathcal{R}
stands for reconstruction). Subsequently, we define:

Definiton 3.1
.

An estimator
\hat{r}\in\mathcal{R}(P)
is
P
-optimal if and only if
\forall P:\mathcal{L}_{1|2}(\hat{r},n)=\min_{\hat{r}\in\mathcal{R}(P)}\mathcal{L}_{1|2}(\hat{r},n)
.

Sections
3.1
and
3.2
prove that a URP estimator can achieve
P
-optimality, while Section
3.3
minimizes its
\mathcal{L}_{1}
and
\mathcal{L}_{2}
to establish optimal bounds.

3.1
P
-Optimal Distribution Estimator

We begin by analyzing
P
-optimality for distribution estimation. Since
\mathcal{L}_{1|2}
is the supremum of
L_{1|2}
, it is naturally lower-bounded by any
L_{1|2}
:

\min_{\hat{\theta}\in\mathcal{R}(P)}\mathcal{L}_{1|2}(\hat{\theta},n)\geq\min_{\hat{\theta}\in\mathcal{R}(P)}L_{1|2}(\hat{\theta},\bm{\theta},n).

Subsequently, we prove that

Theorem 3.1
.

\displaystyle\min_{\hat{\theta}\in\mathcal{R}(P)}L_{2}(\hat{\theta},\bm{\theta},n)\geq\frac{1}{n}[\text{Tr}(\mathscr{I}_{C}^{+})]\overset{\mathrm{def}}{=}L_{2}^{\downarrow}(P,\bm{\theta},n).

\displaystyle\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}

\displaystyle\min_{\hat{\theta}\in\mathcal{R}(P)}L_{1}(\hat{\theta},\bm{\theta},n)\geq\sum_{x\in[d]}\sqrt{\frac{2}{n\pi}(\mathscr{I}_{C}^{+})_{x,x}}\overset{\mathrm{def}}{=}L_{1}^{\downarrow}(P,\bm{\theta},n).

where
\mathscr{I}_{C}^{+}=(P^{T}\text{diag}(P\bm{\theta})^{-1}P)^{-1}+\bm{\theta}\bm{\theta}^{T}
and
L_{1|2}^{\downarrow}
represents the theoretical lower bound of
L_{1|2}
.

The proof, provided in Appendix
B.1
, is inspired by the derivation of the Cramér–Rao bound using Fisher information.

Consequently, We will establish the
P
-optimality of
\hat{\theta}_{U}=\text{URP}(P,Q)
by proving:

\min_{Q}\mathcal{L}_{1|2}(\text{URP}(P,Q),n)=L_{1|2}^{\downarrow}(P,\frac{1}{d}\mathbf{1},n)

(14)

where
\mathbf{1}
denotes an all-ones column vector of size
d
. Note that the equality of
\mathcal{L}_{1}
holds asymptotically as
n\to\infty
. Referring to Theorem
2.9
, we derive the
L_{2}
norm of a URP estimator as:

L_{2}(\hat{\theta},\bm{\theta},n)=\sum_{x}(\alpha-\beta)\theta_{x}+\beta-\theta_{x}^{2}=\alpha+(d-1)\beta-\bm{\theta}^{T}\bm{\theta}\\
=\frac{1}{d}\sum_{i}[(Q\circ Q)P]_{ii}+\frac{1}{d}\sum_{i}\sum_{j\neq i}[(Q\circ Q)P]_{ij}-\bm{\theta}^{T}\bm{\theta}\\
=\frac{1}{d}\sum_{i}\sum_{j}[(Q\circ Q)P]_{ij}-\bm{\theta}^{T}\bm{\theta}=\|Q\overline{P}^{\frac{1}{2}}\|_{2}-\bm{\theta}^{T}\bm{\theta}.

(15)

where
\overline{P}=\text{diag}(\frac{1}{d}P\mathbf{1})
;
\|\cdot\|_{2}^{2}
represents the squared
L_{2}
norm of a matrix (i.e., the squared Frobenius norm). It follows that:

L_{2}(\hat{\theta}_{U},\bm{\theta},n)\leq L_{2}(\hat{\theta}_{U},\frac{1}{d}\mathbf{1},n)=\|Q\overline{P}^{\frac{1}{2}}\|_{2}-\frac{1}{d}=\mathcal{L}_{2}(\hat{\theta}_{U},n).

Optimizing
\mathcal{L}_{2}
is thus equivalent to minimizing
\|Q\overline{P}^{\frac{1}{2}}\|_{2}
. For the reconstruction matrix, we have:

Theorem 3.2
.

Let
\hat{\theta}_{U}=\text{URP}(P,Q)
. For a fixed perturbation matrix
P
, the
\mathcal{L}_{2}
loss is minimized when

Q=(P^{T}\overline{P}^{-1}P^{T})^{-1}P^{T}\overline{P}^{-1}\overset{\mathrm{def}}{=}Q^{*},

The resulting minimum
\mathcal{L}_{2}
loss is given by

\min_{Q}\mathcal{L}_{2}(\hat{\theta}_{U},n)=\frac{1}{n}[\text{Tr}((P^{T}\overline{P}^{-1}P^{T})^{-1})-\frac{1}{d}],

where
Tr
denotes the trace of a matrix.

Proof: Based on the Minimal Norm Least Squares Solution (MNLSS)
[
6
]
, for an underdetermined system
Ax=b
, the Moore–Penrose inverse
x=A^{T}(AA^{T})^{-1}b
minimizes
\|x\|_{2}
. Given the constraint
QP=I
(or equivalently
Q\overline{P}^{\frac{1}{2}}\overline{P}^{-\frac{1}{2}}P=I
), minimizing
\|Q\overline{P}^{\frac{1}{2}}\|_{2}
via MNLSS yields

Q=(P^{T}\overline{P}^{-1}P^{T})^{-1}P^{T}\overline{P}^{-1}.

The corresponding optimized
\mathcal{L}_{2}
is derived as

\min_{Q}\mathcal{L}_{2}(\hat{\theta}_{U},n)\\
=\frac{1}{d}\sum_{i}\sum_{j}[(Q^{*}\circ Q^{*})P]_{ij}-\frac{1}{d}=\frac{1}{d}\sum_{i}[(Q^{*}\circ Q^{*})P\mathbf{1}]_{i}-\frac{1}{d}\\
=\text{Tr}(Q^{*}\overline{P}(Q^{*})^{T})-\frac{1}{d}=\text{Tr}((P^{T}\overline{P}^{-1}P)^{-1})-\frac{1}{d}.\qed

This proves the
\mathcal{L}_{2}
component of Eq. (
14
). Following Theorem
2.3
, we obtain:

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\mathcal{L}_{1}(\text{URP}(P,Q^{*}),n)=L_{1}^{\downarrow}(P,\frac{1}{d}\mathbf{1},n).

(16)

Consequently, we conclude:

Theorem 3.3
.

\text{URP}(P,Q^{*})
is a
P
-optimal distribution estimator.

3.2
P
-Optimal Frequency Estimator

We now extend the
P
-optimality established for distribution estimation to frequency estimation. First, consider the following sampling property:

Theorem 3.4
.

Assume a dataset contains
n
objects sampled i.i.d. from a categorical distribution
\mathcal{C}(\bm{\theta})
and subsequently perturbed by an LDP mechanism. If the same unbiased estimator
\hat{f}=\hat{\theta}
is employed for both frequency and distribution estimation, it satisfies:

\sum_{\mathbf{f}}\Pr(\mathbf{f}|\bm{\theta})\text{Var}(\hat{f}|\mathbf{f})=\text{Var}(\hat{\theta})+\frac{\bm{\theta}^{2}-\bm{\theta}}{n}.

The proof is provided in Appendix
B.2
. By relating these variances into
L_{2}
and applying Theorem
3.1
, we obtain:

\sum_{\mathbf{f}}\Pr(\mathbf{f}|\bm{\theta})L_{2}(\hat{f},\mathbf{f},n)=L_{2}(\hat{\theta},\bm{\theta},n)+\frac{\bm{\theta}^{T}\bm{\theta}-1}{n}\\
\geq\frac{1}{n}[\text{Tr}((P^{T}\text{diag}(P\bm{\theta})^{-1}P)^{-1})-1].

Regarding frequency estimation, Eq. (
15
) is adjusted to
\mathcal{L}_{2}(\hat{f}_{U})=\|Q\overline{P}^{\frac{1}{2}}\|_{2}-1
, which modifies Theorem
3.2
as follows:

\mathcal{L}_{2}(\hat{f}_{U}=\text{URP}(P,Q^{*}),n)=\frac{1}{n}[\text{Tr}((P^{T}\overline{P}^{-1}P)^{-1})-1],

where
\overline{P}=\text{diag}(P\frac{1}{d}\mathbf{1})
. Since the worst-case loss
\mathcal{L}_{2}
is lower-bounded by the average
L_{2}
loss, we have:

\min_{\hat{f}\in\mathcal{R}(P)}\mathcal{L}_{2}(\hat{f},n)\geq\min_{\hat{f}\in\mathcal{R}(P)}\sum_{\mathbf{f}}\Pr(\mathbf{f}|\bm{\theta}=\frac{1}{d}\mathbf{1})L_{2}(\hat{f},\mathbf{f},n)\\
\geq\frac{1}{n}[\text{Tr}((P^{T}\overline{P}^{-1}P)^{-1})-1]=\mathcal{L}_{2}(\text{URP}(P,Q^{*}),n),

By definition,
\mathcal{L}_{2}(\text{URP}(P,Q^{*}),n)\geq\min_{\hat{f}\in\mathcal{R}(P)}\mathcal{L}_{2}(\hat{f},n)
, implying that these quantities must be identical. Thus,
\text{URP}(P,Q^{*})
is also
P
-optimal for frequency estimation. Similarly to Eq. (
16
), this
P
-optimality extends to
\mathcal{L}_{1}
. That is,

Theorem 3.5
.

\text{URP}(P,Q^{*})
is a
P
-optimal frequency estimator.

3.3
Strict Lower Bound

Since
\text{URP}(P,Q^{*})
is
P
-optimal for both frequency and distribution estimation, Eq. (
13
) ensures that
\min_{P}\mathcal{L}_{1|2}(\text{URP}(P,Q^{*}),n)
constitutes the strict lower bound for
\mathcal{L}_{1|2}
. This section derives these values as follows:

1.

Among all perturbation matrices sharing the same self-support probability, determine the optimal
P^{*}
that minimizes
\|Q^{*}\overline{P}^{\frac{1}{2}}\|_{2}
.

2.

Prove that
\text{URP}(P^{*},Q^{*})
achieves an Optimal Symmetric Configuration (OSC, as per Definition
3.2
).

3.

Derive the
\mathcal{L}_{2}
loss of the OSC as a function of dictionary size
d
, privacy parameter
\epsilon
, dataset size
n
, and support size
k
.

4.

Minimize the
\mathcal{L}_{2}
loss of the OSC with respect to the support size
k
to establish the strict lower bound for all estimators.

These steps are formally expressed by the following sequence of inequalities:

\forall P:\mathcal{L}_{2}(\text{URP}(P,Q^{*}))\geq\mathcal{L}_{2}(\text{URP}(P^{*},Q^{*}))\\
=\mathcal{L}_{2}(\text{OSC}(d,\epsilon,n,k))\geq\min_{k}\mathcal{L}_{2}(\text{OSC}(d,\epsilon,n,k)).

Notably, this section focuses on
frequency estimation
, but all results are applicable to distribution estimation by adjusting the last term of their variance expressions, i.e., by replacing
\mathbf{f}
with
\bm{\theta}^{2}
. Consequently,
\mathcal{L}_{2}(\hat{\theta},n)=\mathcal{L}_{2}(\hat{f},n)+\frac{1-1/d}{n}
.

We now proceed to find the optimal
P
to minimize
\mathcal{L}_{2}
. Recall that Lemma
2.1
establishes that an extremal configuration is characterized by a constant self-support probability
p^{*}
, such that
\forall x:\sum_{o}e^{\epsilon}S_{o,x}p_{o}=p^{*}
. Building on this, we provide the following result:

Theorem 3.6
.

Among all perturbation matrices with an extremal configuration and a fixed self-support probability
p^{*}
, the one with a constant support size minimizes the
\mathcal{L}_{2}
loss of
\text{URP}(P,Q^{*})
.

The detailed proof is provided in Appendix
B.3
. The core idea involves relating
\mathcal{L}_{2}
to the eigenvalues of
P^{T}\overline{P}^{-1}P
, denoted by
\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\dots,\lambda_{d}
. The proof demonstrates that
\lambda_{1}=d
always holds. Letting
\Lambda=\sum_{i=2}^{d}\lambda_{i}
, we have:

\text{Tr}(P^{T}\overline{P}^{-1}P)=\frac{1}{d}+\sum_{i=2}^{d}\frac{1}{\lambda_{i}}\geq\frac{1}{d}+\frac{(d-1)^{2}}{\Lambda}\geq\frac{1}{d}+\frac{(d-1)^{2}}{\max\Lambda}.

Appendix
B.3
further shows that when the support size for all responses is constant (i.e.,
\forall o:k_{o}=k
), both equalities are achieved simultaneously, thereby minimizing
\mathcal{L}_{2}
.
∎

Theorem 3.7
.

If a perturbation matrix
P^{*}
has an extremal configuration and constant support size, the reconstruction of
\text{URP}(P^{*},Q^{*})
is equivalent to Eq. (
5
).

The detailed proof is provided in Appendix
B.4
. Intuitively, given a constant support size, the reconstruction matrix takes the form
Q=A[(e^{\epsilon}-1)S^{T}+J^{T}]
, where
A
is a
d\times d
matrix. Here,
S^{T}
maps the responses to their supported original values, and
J^{T}
simply adds a constant offset. Consequently, the reconstruction is equivalent to a linear transformation of the frequencies of the values supported by the responses, which matches the operation in Eq. (
5
).
∎

Definiton 3.2
.

An estimator has an Optimal Symmetric Configuration (OSC) if it has an extremal and symmetric configuration with constant support, and employs Eq. (
5
) to reconstruct the frequencies or distributions.

Theorems
3.6
,
3.7
, and
2.7
establish that a URP estimator achieves an OSC. Thus, we have:

\min_{\hat{f}_{\text{OSC}}}\mathcal{L}_{2}(\hat{f}_{\text{OSC}},n)\leq\min_{\hat{f}_{\text{URP}}}\mathcal{L}_{2}(\hat{f}_{\text{URP}},n).

Furthermore, since
\min_{\hat{f}_{\text{URP}}}\mathcal{L}_{2}(\hat{f}_{\text{URP}})
has been established as an optimal bound, it follows that
\min_{\hat{f}_{\text{OSC}}}\mathcal{L}_{2}(\hat{f}_{\text{OSC}})
similarly constitutes an optimal bound.

To derive
\mathcal{L}_{2}
of an OSC estimator, we first formulate the variance of Eq. (
5
) as a function of the dictionary size
d
, privacy parameter
\epsilon
, dataset size
n
, and support size
k
. The extremal configuration ensures that
k
can be directly derived from
p^{*}
, as shown in Lemma
2.1
. Furthermore, the cross-support probability
q^{*}
of OSC is derived as follows:

Lemma 3.1
.

An optimal symmetric configuration satisfies

\displaystyle p^{*}=\frac{e^{\epsilon}k}{k(e^{\epsilon}-1)+d}

\displaystyle q^{*}=\frac{(e^{\epsilon}-1)k(k-1)}{(d-1)[k(e^{\epsilon}-1)+d]}+\frac{k}{k(e^{\epsilon}-1)+d}

This proof is presented in Appendix
B.5
. Subsequently, given Theorem
2.6
, the variance of an OSC frequency estimator is derived as

Lemma 3.2
.

\text{Var}(\hat{f}_{\text{OSC}}(x))=\frac{[k(e^{\epsilon}-1)+d-1][k(e^{\epsilon}-1)+d-e^{\epsilon}]}{nk(d-k)(e^{\epsilon}-1)^{2}}\\
+f(x)\frac{k^{2}(e^{\epsilon}-1)+k(2d-e^{\epsilon}-1)+d-d^{2}}{nk(d-k)(e^{\epsilon}-1)}

(17)

By substituting
\text{Var}(\hat{f}_{\text{OSC}}(x))
into
\mathcal{L}_{1}
and
\mathcal{L}_{2}
, we optimize
k
to minimize these losses:

Theorem 3.8
.

When
k=\frac{d}{e^{\epsilon}+1}\geq 1
,
\mathcal{L}_{1}
and
\mathcal{L}_{2}
achieve their minimum values:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{1}^{*}(n)=\min_{k}\mathcal{L}_{1}(\hat{f},n)\approx\frac{\sqrt{2(d-1)[4de^{\epsilon}-(e^{\epsilon}+1)^{2}}]}{\pi\sqrt{n}(e^{\epsilon}-1)}.

(18)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{2}^{*}(n)=\min_{k}\mathcal{L}_{2}(\hat{f},n)=\frac{(d-1)[4de^{\epsilon}-(e^{\epsilon}+1)^{2}]}{nd(e^{\epsilon}-1)^{2}}

(19)

The detailed proof is provided in Appendix
B.6
. The core methodology involves finding the minima through differentiation with respect to
k
. Consequently, the derivation throughout this section establishes a sufficient condition for an optimal estimator:

Corollary 3.1
.

An estimator is
(\mathcal{L}_{1},\mathcal{L}_{2})
-optimal if it has an optimal symmetric configuration with
k=\frac{d}{e^{\epsilon}+1}
.

The optimality established in Theorem
3.8
assumes
k
to be an integer. When the optimal
k
is non-integral, one may select the nearest integers that provide the higher precision. In such cases, the deviation is bounded as follows:

Corollary 3.2
.

When
k
is a nearby integer of
\frac{d}{e^{\epsilon}+1}
, we have

\displaystyle\min_{\hat{f}_{OSC}}\mathcal{L}_{1}(\hat{f}_{OSC},n)\leq\mathcal{L}_{1}^{*}(n)\sqrt{1+\alpha},

\displaystyle\min_{\hat{f}_{OSC}}\mathcal{L}_{2}(\hat{f}_{OSC},n)\leq\mathcal{L}_{2}^{*}(n)(1+\alpha),

where
\alpha=\frac{(d-1)(e^{\epsilon}+1)^{4}}{(2d+e^{\epsilon}+1)(2de^{\epsilon}-e^{\epsilon}-1)(4de^{\epsilon}-(e^{\epsilon}+1)^{2})}
.

The proof is provided in Appendix
B.7
.

Notably, when
e^{\epsilon}+1\geq d
, the support size
k
cannot be less than one. By setting
k=1
in this regime, the optimized
\mathcal{L}_{1}
and
\mathcal{L}_{2}
losses are given by:

Corollary 3.3
.

When
e^{\epsilon}+1\geq d
, the minimum values for
\mathcal{L}_{1}
and
\mathcal{L}_{2}
are:

\displaystyle\min_{\hat{f}}\mathcal{L}_{1}(\hat{f},n)\approx\frac{\sqrt{2d(d-1)(d+2e^{\epsilon}-2)}}{\pi\sqrt{n}(e^{\epsilon}-1)}.

(20)

\displaystyle\min_{\hat{f}}\mathcal{L}_{2}(\hat{f},n)=\frac{(d-1)(d+2e^{\epsilon}-2)}{n(e^{\epsilon}-1)^{2}}

(21)

This result is established by expressing
\mathcal{L}_{1}
and
\mathcal{L}_{2}
as functions of
k
and substituting
k=1
. Further details are available in Appendix
B.8
.

4
Communication Cost

This section evaluates the upper bound of the communication cost for an optimal frequency or distribution estimator. Let
\mathcal{O}
represent all potential responses from a LDP client, and
p_{o}
denote the base probability of a given response
o\in\mathcal{O}
. To prevent privacy leakage through the encoding process, we require all responses to utilize the same encoding scheme, regardless of the original value that generated them. Therefore, responses are encoded based on their prior distribution. Shannon’s Source Coding Theorem
[
18
]
establishes that if the prior distribution of
\mathcal{O}
is known for any response
o
as
\rho_{o}
, the optimal communication cost for
\mathcal{O}
is
-\sum_{o}\rho_{o}\log_{2}(\rho_{o})
bits. In scenarios where the prior distribution is unknown, the upper bound of the optimal communication cost is
\log_{2}(|\mathcal{O}|)
bits, which occurs when
\forall o:\rho_{o}=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{O}|}
, i.e., the logarithm of the number of possible responses.

Theorem 4.1
.

The communication cost of an optimal estimator can be as low as
\log_{2}(\frac{d(d-1)}{2}+1)
bits.

The detailed proof is provided in Appendix
C
. The core idea is to demonstrate that at most
\frac{d(d-1)}{2}+1
responses are necessary to construct an optimal estimator with an optimal symmetric configuration (Definition
3.2
). To derive this bound, we prove that satisfying a symmetric configuration requires that
\forall i<j:\sum_{o}S_{o,i}S_{o,j}p_{o}=\frac{k(k-1)}{(d-1)[k(e^{\epsilon}-1)+d]}
holds true. This corresponds to
\frac{d(d-1)}{2}
distinct constraints. By leveraging Carathéodory’s theorem
[
3
]
, we establish that only
\frac{d(d-1)}{2}+1
responses are needed; thus, the upper bound of the communication cost is
\log_{2}\left(\frac{d(d-1)}{2}+1\right)
bits.

There are special cases where the communication cost can be further reduced to
\log_{2}d
. For example, when
d=2^{i}
for
i\in\mathbb{N}^{+}
and
k=\frac{d}{2}
(i.e., as
\epsilon\rightarrow 0
), the support matrix
S
can be constructed as a Hadamard matrix with
d
rows, each maintaining a constant support size of
\frac{d}{2}
. This construction is equivalent to the Hadamard Response
[
1
]
. Furthermore, assuming the base probability
p_{o}
is constant across all responses, the construction of
S
is equivalent to a Balanced Incomplete Block Design (BIBD). For more details, refer to
[
5
]
. Depending on the specific values of
(d,k)
, there may exist BIBDs that require only
d
responses
[
17
]
.

Although it is always preferable to identify the optimal communication cost, i.e., a tight lower bound, which this paper does not derive, a communication cost of
O(\log d)
bits remains practically feasible even for a very large dictionary size
d
.

5
Optimal Algorithms

5.1
Subset Selection

Subset Selection, originally proposed by
[
22
]
, returns a random set of
k
distinct elements from the dictionary
\mathcal{X}=[d]
. The output space
\mathcal{O}
consists of all
\binom{d}{k}
possible combinations, where each response
o\in\mathcal{O}
shares identical base probability
p_{o}
(see Definition
2.3
). If a subset contains (supports) the input value
x
, the probability of Subset Selection generating that response is
e^{\epsilon}
times greater than the probability of generating a response that does not contain
x
.

This mechanism naturally follows an optimal symmetric configuration. Consequently, by applying the optimized
k
from Theorem
3.8
, Subset Selection serves as an optimal estimator. Given that it possesses
\binom{d}{k}
possible responses with identical base probabilities, the communication cost is bounded by
k\log_{2}\left(\frac{ed}{k}\right)
bits, following the inequality
\binom{d}{k}\leq\left(\frac{ed}{k}\right)^{k}
.

5.2
Optimized Count Mean Sketch

Count-Mean Sketch (CMS), originally proposed by
[
8
]
, was further refined by
[
16
]
to achieve state-of-the-art precision while maintaining a logarithmic communication cost. The simplified workflow for CMS is as follows:

1.

Determine the hash range
B
(the number of possible hashed values) based on the privacy parameter
\epsilon
.

2.

Uniformly sample a hash function
h
from a hash family
\mathcal{H}
, and map an object’s original value
x
to a hashed value
y=h(x)
.

3.

Apply a randomized response mechanism to perturb
y
within the range
\{1,2,\dots,B\}
, generating a perturbed value
z
. The tuple
(h,z)
is then sent to the server.

4.

Frequencies or distributions are estimated based on the pairs
(h,z)
collected from all objects, using a formula equivalent to Eq. (
5
) as provided by
[
16
]
.

This section demonstrates that CMS is a nearly optimal frequency or distribution estimator, subject to the following minor modifications: (1) expanding the original dictionary size
d
to
d^{\prime}
, where
d^{\prime}
is the smallest prime number such that
d^{\prime}\geq d
; (2) set hash range
m=\text{round}(1+e^{\epsilon})
and (3) constructing the hash family as:

\mathcal{H}=\{h(x)=((ax+b)\mod{d^{\prime}})\mod B|\\
a\in[1,d^{\prime}-1],b\in[0,d^{\prime}-1]\}.

This construction differs from
[
16
]
by explicitly disallowing
a=0
. This modification improves the collision probability from approximately
\frac{1}{B^{2}}
to approximately
\frac{d^{\prime}-B}{B^{2}(d^{\prime}-1)}
(see Appendix
D.1
for details). We refer to this variant as Optimized Count Mean Sketch (OCMS).

First, we demonstrate that OCMS is equivalent to sampling from two optimal symmetric configurations:

Theorem 5.1
.

The output distribution of OCMS with dictionary size
d^{\prime}
and
B
buckets is equivalent to sampling between two optimal symmetric configurations:

•

A configuration with support size
\lfloor\frac{d^{\prime}}{B}\rfloor+1
, sampled with probability
p_{\alpha}=\frac{d^{\prime}\mod B}{B}
, and

•

A configuration with support size
\lfloor\frac{d^{\prime}}{B}\rfloor
, sampled with probability
1-p_{\alpha}
.

The detailed proof is provided in Appendix
D
. The core intuition is that any response
(h,z)
supports all elements mapped to bucket
z
. Each bucket contains either
\lfloor\frac{d^{\prime}}{B}\rfloor+1
or
\lfloor\frac{d^{\prime}}{B}\rfloor
elements. By grouping all possible responses according to their support size, each group constitutes a distinct configuration; thus, OCMS can be reconstructed as a weighted sampling of these two configurations.

This leads to the following result:

Corollary 5.1
.

The variance of OCMS is given by

\text{Var}(\hat{f}_{OCMS}(x))=p_{\alpha}Var(\hat{f}^{*}(x|k=\lfloor\frac{d^{\prime}}{B}\rfloor+1))+\\
(1-p_{\alpha})Var(\hat{f}^{*}(x|k=\lfloor\frac{d^{\prime}}{B}\rfloor)),

where
\hat{f}^{*}(x|k)
represents the estimator with an optimal symmetric configuration and support size
k
.

The full proof is provided in Appendix
D
. Subsequently, we demonstrate that OCMS approaches optimal precision:

Corollary 5.2
.

Define
\alpha=\frac{(d-1)(e^{\epsilon}+1)^{4}}{(4de^{\epsilon}-(e^{\epsilon}+1)^{2})(d-e^{\epsilon}-1)(de^{\epsilon}+e^{\epsilon}+1)}
,
\beta=\frac{(d^{\prime}-d)(e^{\epsilon}+1)^{2}(2dd^{\prime}-d-d^{\prime})}{d^{\prime 2}(d-1)(4de^{\epsilon}-(e^{\epsilon}+1)^{2})}
. If OCMS has an optimized hash range
B=\text{round}(1+e^{\epsilon})
, then

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{1,OCMS}^{*}(n)\leq\mathcal{L}_{1}^{*}(n)\sqrt{(1+\alpha)(1+\beta)}

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{2,OCMS}^{*}(n)\leq\mathcal{L}_{2}^{*}(n)(1+\alpha)(1+\beta)

where
\mathcal{L}_{1}^{*}
and
\mathcal{L}_{2}^{*}
represent the optimized
\mathcal{L}_{1}
and
\mathcal{L}_{2}
given dictionary size
d
and privacy parameter
\epsilon
, respectively.

The proof is detailed in Appendix
D
. Notably, when
B=\text{round}(1+e^{\epsilon})
, OCMS is equivalent to sampling from two optimal symmetric configurations with support sizes of
\lfloor\frac{d^{\prime}}{1+e^{\epsilon}}\rfloor
and
\lceil\frac{d^{\prime}}{1+e^{\epsilon}}\rceil
, respectively. In the regime where
d\gg e^{\epsilon}
, both
\alpha
and
\beta
vanish toward zero. For instance, with
\epsilon=1
,
d=100
, and
d^{\prime}=101
, the
\mathcal{L}_{2}
error of OCMS is only
0.09\%
higher than the theoretical lower bound.

5.3
Weighted Subset Selection

Theorem
4.1
proves that at most
\frac{d(d-1)}{2}+1
responses are required to construct an optimal estimator. These responses constitute a subset of
S^{\star}
, which denotes the set of all possible
\binom{d}{k}
combinations. We can develop an algorithm to identify these
\frac{d(d-1)}{2}+1
responses and construct the corresponding matrices
S
and
P
. The core construction procedure is as follows:

1.

Sample
m
responses from
S^{\star}
either uniformly or via a heuristic, where
m\geq\frac{d(d-1)}{2}+1
.

2.

For each sampled response
o
, compute every
S_{o,i}S_{o,j}
for
i<j
as a vector
v_{o}
.

3.

Construct a matrix
A=[v_{1},\dots,v_{m}]
and a vector
y
of size
\frac{d(d-1)}{2}
, where all elements are set to
\frac{k(k-1)}{(k(e^{\epsilon}-1)+d)(d-1)}
, i.e., the expected value of
\sum_{o}p_{o}S_{o,i}S_{o,j}
.

4.

Find a solution
\mathbf{p}
for the system
A\mathbf{p}=y
, subject to
\mathbf{p}\geq 0
. This can be achieved using Linear Programming (LP) or Non-Negative Least Squares (NNLS). These solvers typically yield sparse solutions, generally resulting in no more than
\frac{d(d-1)}{2}+1
positive values. Refer to the proof of Theorem
4.1
for why solving
\mathbf{p}
for
A\mathbf{p}=y
.

5.

If a solution is successfully found, let
\mathbf{p}=[p_{1},p_{2},\dots,p_{m}]
and construct
S
by stacking the support vectors of all responses where
p_{o}>0
.

6.

If the system is infeasible, return to step (1) and resample.

This algorithm is referred to as Weighted Subset Selection (WSS). The heuristic in Step (1) can be implemented as follows:

1.

Track Support
: Monitor the frequency with which each original value
x
is supported by existing sampled responses using
\sum_{o}S^{\prime}_{o,x}
, where
S^{\prime}
denotes the support matrix of the already sampled responses.

2.

Weighted Sampling
: Randomly select
k
values using weights proportional to
\exp\left(-\sum_{o}S^{\prime}_{o,x}\right)
. This ensures that values with lower current support are assigned a higher probability of being sampled in the next iteration.

In our experiments, this heuristic typically generates a valid
S
when
m=d^{2}
. The constraint
A\mathbf{p}=y
inherently guarantees an optimal symmetric configuration (see the proof of Theorem
4.1
). Consequently, it serves as an optimal estimator provided that the support size
k
is optimized. Since LP or NNLS solvers tend to produce sparse solutions, we observe that
\mathbf{p}
generally contains no more than
\frac{d(d-1)}{2}+1
positive values. Thus, the communication cost is typically bounded by
\log_{2}\left(\frac{d(d-1)}{2}+1\right)
. If a specific solution for
\mathbf{p}
contains more responses than this bound, the process can be restarted to find a sparser alternative.

Notably, LP or NNLS solvers have a computational complexity of
O(H^{2}W)
, where
H
and
W
represent the height and width of
A
, respectively. In this context,
H=\frac{d(d-1)}{2}
and
W=m=O(d^{2})
, resulting in a total computational complexity of
O(d^{6})
. A practical approach to adopting WSS is to precompute valid support matrices
S
for common values of
d
and
k
; clients can then simply download and reuse these matrices for each round of frequency or distribution estimation. Furthermore, as the proof of Corollary
5.2
demonstrates that extending a dictionary to a larger size has a negligible impact on precision, one can simply use the next available precomputed
d
for which a valid
S
exists.

7
Conclusion

This paper establishes the strict optimality of frequency and distribution estimation under LDP by proving that the optimal symmetric configuration achieves the theoretical lower bound of
\mathcal{L}_{2}
loss. The theoretical
\mathcal{L}_{1}
lower bound is also achieved asymptotically. We further demonstrate that the communication cost of an optimal estimator can be reduced to
\log_{2}\left(\frac{d(d-1)}{2}+1\right)
bits, where
d
is the dictionary size, and we develop an algorithm to construct an optimal estimator within this communication bound. Consequently, our results offer a practical deployment strategy: Optimized Count-Mean Sketch is recommended for large dictionaries, while weighted and original Subset Selection are ideal for smaller scales. Experimental evaluations confirm that these optimal algorithms align perfectly with our derived strict lower bounds.

2.3

∎

\displaystyle D(O^{(i)})

The expectation of the score function is:

(22)

The second moment of the unit vectors is given by:

\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{e}_{O^{(i)}}\mathbf{e}_{O^{(i)}}^{T}]=\sum_{o\in[m]}\Pr(o)\mathbf{e}_{o}\mathbf{e}_{o}^{T}=\text{diag}(P\bm{\theta}),

\mathbb{E}[D(O^{(i)})D(O^{(i)})^{T}]\\

Since

\mathbb{E}[(D(O^{(i)})-\mathbf{1}_{d})(D(O^{(j)})-\mathbf{1}_{d})^{T}]=0.

is also constant. Although Lemma

assumes a constant support size, it generalizes to any symmetric configuration where
k=\frac{\sum_{o}p_{o}k_{o}}{\sum_{o}p_{o}}
. For OCMS, since
k=d^{\prime}/B
, the base probability is
p_{o}=\frac{d^{\prime}}{\frac{d^{\prime}}{B}(e^{\epsilon}-1)+d^{\prime}}\cdot\frac{1}{Bd^{\prime}(d^{\prime}-1)}
.

We construct the support matrix of the first configuration,
S^{(1)}
, by stacking all rows of
S
where the response
(h,z)
satisfies
z\leq r=d^{\prime}\mod B
. Since the buckets corresponding to
z\leq r
contain
\lceil\frac{d^{\prime}}{B}\rceil
elements, all rows of
S^{(1)}
have a constant support size of
\lceil d^{\prime}/B\rceil
. Defining the base probability of
S^{(1)}
as a constant, Lemma
B.1
yields
p_{1}=\frac{d^{\prime}}{(\frac{d^{\prime}-r}{B}+1)(e^{\epsilon}-1)+d^{\prime}}\cdot\frac{1}{rd^{\prime}(d^{\prime}-1)}
.

Similarly, the support matrix of the second configuration,
S^{(2)}
, is constructed from rows where
z>r
, resulting in a constant support size of
\lfloor\frac{d^{\prime}}{B}\rfloor
. Its base probability is computed as
p_{2}=\frac{d^{\prime}}{\frac{d^{\prime}-r}{B}(e^{\epsilon}-1)+d^{\prime}}\cdot\frac{1}{(B-r)d^{\prime}(d^{\prime}-1)}
. It can be verified that
p_{\alpha}p_{1}=p_{0}
and
(1-p_{\alpha})p_{2}=p_{0}
, demonstrating that the sampling of these two configurations generates the same support matrix
S
.

These split configurations possess constant support sizes and extremal configurations. We now prove that their support matrix and base probability satisfy the requirements of a symmetric configuration. Taking
S^{(1)}
as an example, this requires
p_{1}\sum_{o}S^{(1)}_{o,i}S^{(1)}_{o,j}
to be constant for all
i\neq j
. For an arbitrary pair
(i,j)
where
i\neq j
, the term
\sum_{o}S^{(1)}_{o,i}S^{(1)}_{o,j}
represents the number of hash functions mapping
(i,j)
into the same bucket of size
\lceil\frac{d^{\prime}}{B}\rceil
. Elements within such a bucket can form
(\lceil\frac{d^{\prime}}{B}\rceil+1)(\lceil\frac{d^{\prime}}{B}\rceil)
distinct pairs, and there are
r
such buckets. Mapping to the same bucket is equivalent to mapping to these distinct pairs. By Lemma
D.1
, there are exactly
r(\lceil\frac{d^{\prime}}{B}\rceil+1)(\lceil\frac{d^{\prime}}{B}\rceil)
hash functions mapping
(i,j)
to these pairs. This holds for every
(i,j)
, ensuring the sum is constant.

The same logic applies to
S^{(2)}
, confirming both configurations are symmetric. Since the sampling between these two configurations generates an equivalent support matrix to OCMS, Theorem
5.1
is proved.
∎

Furthermore, extending this counting method to
B
buckets and ignoring the remainder
r
, one can derive
P(h(x_{1})=h(x_{2}))\approx\frac{d^{\prime}-B}{B^{2}(d^{\prime}-1)}
, verifying the reduction in collision probability discussed in Section
5.2
.

Finally, if a process generates variable
V_{1}
with probability
p
and variable
V_{2}
with probability
1-p
, and
E[V_{1}]=E[V_{2}]
, the variance of the process is
p\text{Var}(V_{1})+(1-p)\text{Var}(V_{2})
. This proves Corollary
5.1
.
∎
