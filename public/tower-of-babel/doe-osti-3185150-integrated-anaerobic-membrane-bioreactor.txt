Executive Summary:

 The overall objective of the project was to demonstrate a successful wastewater resource
 recovery platform comprising an Anaerobic Membrane Bioreactor (AnMBR) to achieve
 >50% Carbon sequestration efficiency as Volatile Fatty Acids (VFAs) or as methane gas
 from agricultural (animal feeding operations) wastewater along with the generation of final
 water exceeding Biological Nutrient Removal (BNR) standards for indirect potable reuse
 by coupling with constructed wetlands has been successfully accomplished. The AnMBR
 achieved stable performance over 350 days, removing 80–90% of COD and BOD₅ and
    recovering methane (0.17±0.07 L CH4/g COD at 66.5±0.03% v/v) at 3-5 LMH flux and 5-
 9 days HRT with 3-5% w/v total solids. Phosphorus recovery via CaO addition in an 80-L
 coagulation-flocculation-sedimentation unit ranged from 40.6% to 99.7%, yielding
 products with 11.4–13.6% P content and citric acid solubility of 32–38.6% P, comparable
 to rock phosphate mineral. Ammonium adsorption achieved ~94.5% recovery with
 exchange capacity of 10 – 16 g NH4-N/kg clinoptilolite. The CW polishing step met Kansas
 discharge standards for BOD5 and TN (<30 and <10 mg /L,) and approached the TP
 standard (~2.5 – 4 mg P/L). These findings have either already resulted in two peer
 reviewed publications, two patent applications, and one publication in conference
 proceedings.
 Parallel research evaluated the feasibility of generating organic acids from arrested
 anaerobic digestion of swine wastewater by itself in anaerobic bioreactor with or without
 integration with an anode of a microbial electrochemical cell. At a 10-day HRT, acetate
 and propionate dominated at pH 5 and 9 (34 mM and 14 mM), later shifting to butyrate
 and iso-caproate (7.8 mM and 7.7 mM), with fermentation efficiencies (fraction of COD
 from all generated VFAs normalized to swine wastewater input COD) of 52% and 57.7%,
 respectively. The highest efficiencies occurred at a 5-day HRT, reaching 69.8% (pH 5) and
 83.2% (pH 9). However, washout at a 2-day HRT led to reduced efficiencies (24.5% and
 36.6%). When a bioanode (-0.3V vs. Ag/AgCl) was incorporated, anode-respiring bacteria
 (ARB) diverted acetate toward high current densities (0.5–2.2 A/m²) at all days, leading to
 selective accumulation of longer-chain VFAs like isovalerate (8.2 mM), valerate (12.6
 mM), and iso-caproate (1.2mM). Microbial analysis revealed that Firmicutes, and
 Clostridia were the dominant phylum and class at both pH 5 and 9, whereas
 Clostridium_sensu_stricto_1 was the predominant genus at pH 5, while it was uncultured
 at pH 9. This study demonstrates the potential of real swine wastewater as a valuable
 resource for VFA synthesis. The integration of microbial electrochemical technologies
 further enhances selective VFAs production and facile separation of the relatively
 immiscible medium to higher chain organic acids. These findings are currently under
 review for peer-reviewed publication.
 Separation of the resultant organic acids from selective fermentation in anaerobic
 membrane bioreactors is crucial for the recovery of the valuable product for valorization.
 This segment of the work developed an adsorptive separation and recovery process for
 anaerobically generated volatile fatty acids (VFAs) from swine wastewater fermentation.
 Batch adsorption studies were conducted using several weak anion exchange resins and
 synthetic adsorbent resins to identify a suitable candidate. Relite RAM2 with a tertiary
 amine functional group showed the highest adsorption (over 98%) of hexanoic acid. Under

 6

the different pH values (1.8–7.5) and extraction temperatures (30–50 °C), the highest
adsorption was observed at a pH of 3.2, which is below the pKa of hexanoic acid, at all the
evaluated temperatures. Under the optimal conditions, 65–72% of VFAs present in
fermentation broth was adsorbed, which was increased to 72–76% by using fresh resins.
The VFAs adsorption and recovery efficiency were maintained for 9 successive cycles
without requiring extensive resin washing and regeneration for both model solution and
fermentation broth. Overall, the work presents a comprehensive study for resin adsorption
to recover VFAs and provides a potential industrial-relevant process to recover VFAs from
fermentation broth.
Liquid–liquid extraction using hydrophobic ionic liquids (ILs) shows great promise for the
extraction and esterification of hydrophilic VFAs. In this study, several ILs were evaluated
to select a water-immiscible and efficient extraction solvent. The selected IL,
trihexyltetradecyl phosphonium dibutylphosphate ([P666,14][DBP]), gave a cumulative
VFA extraction of around 842.8 mg per g IL. The predicted excess enthalpy (HE) and
logarithmic activity coefficients ln(γ) using the COSMO-RS model were validated with the
experimentally obtained VFA recovery from fermentation broth. To understand the
extraction mechanism of VFAs, quantum theory of atoms in molecules (QTAIM) and
noncovalent interaction (NCI) were performed. The results suggest that long chain fatty
acids exhibit strong van der Waals interaction with the DBP anion leading to higher VFA
extraction. The enzymatic esterification of VFAs with ethanol in [P666,14][DBP] was
optimized using the Box–Behnken response surface design of experiment. Under the
optimized conditions, up to 83.7% of hexanoic acid was converted to ethyl esters, while
other shorter chain VFAs have lower conversion efficiency (38.3–63.2%).
A cradle-to-gate life cycle assessment estimated the greenhouse gas emissions from a
simulated full-scale treatment train normalized to 1 kg of nitrogen recovered. For a typical
influent condition, the platform achieved mean net GHG emissions of 0.8 kg CO₂-eq per
kg N recovered. NaCl consumption for clinoptilolite regeneration, fugitive methane,
electricity and Cao addition constituted the principal GHG burdens, while biogas-derived
electricity and P fertilizer production provided environmental credits. One at a time
sensitivity analysis identified biogas production and NaCl regeneration efficiency as the
highest-leverage parameters governing the platform’s life cycle GHG footprint.
The techno-economic and life cycle analyses of the integrated AnMBR platform for swine
wastewater treatment yielded the following key findings: Influent COD concentration
strongly governs both economic and environmental outcomes. At high COD (7,812 mg/L),
the platform achieved near-breakeven levelized net cost and a net GHG benefit of
approximately –1.5 kg CO₂-eq/m³. At low COD (2,604 mg/L), the platform exhibited a net
cost of 0.57 $/m³ and was a net GHG emitter, underscoring that the platform is best suited
for higher-strength wastewaters typical of concentrated animal feeding operations.Plant
scale is a critical determinant of economic feasibility. The levelized net cost decreased
sharply from 0.26 $/m³ at 1 MGD to near zero at 6 MGD, with the platform becoming net
revenue-positive beyond this threshold. Both nitrogen and phosphorus fertilizer MSPs
reached market-competitive levels at approximately 6 MGD, confirming that regional or
centralized facilities are more likely to deliver cost-effective resource recovery

1. Background
Only about 2% of the energy in animal wastes is captured as CH4, since the low price of natural
gas does not warrant the capital costs of Anaerobic Digestion alone.1,2 Complete recovery of
valuable carbon, nutrients, and water contained in concentrated wastes such as municipal sludge,
animal manure, and food waste (including Fats, Oils and Grease, or FOG) have been partially
demonstrated through current technology platforms such as one- or two-stage anaerobic digestion,
incineration and waste heat recovery, pyrolysis, and Hydrothermal Processing (HTP).3-9 Several
Wastewater treatment facilities have been able to achieve energy neutral operation through limited
carbon (mainly methane) and nutrient (struvite alone) sequestration options, the need to enhance
digested biosolids quality while decreasing the quantity, and high capital/operation costs are some
of the current challenges that have limited the widespread adoption of these platforms.
2. Project overview
Anaerobic membrane bioreactors (AnMBRs) are an emerging environmental biotechnology
platform that can address these challenges by enabling efficient anaerobic treatment along with
volatile solids reduction, tailored and separate sequestration of high quality ammonia and
phosphorus, and significantly lower biosolids production.10,11 Anaerobic membrane bioreactors
(AnMBR) have attracted a significant amount of attention due to a unique combination of
advantages from integrating anaerobic biological treatment with membrane filtration. In addition
to the benefits conferred by anaerobic technologies, the use of membranes enables AnMBRs to be
a low-footprint technology that can effectively operate at longer solids retention times (SRT) by
decoupling hydraulic retention time (HRT) and SRT for the treatment of high organic loads, and
produce high quality effluent amenable to reuse.12-15 On the other hand, the introduction of
membranes necessitates fouling control measures that can consume up to 50% of the total energy
demand and increase chemical use.16-21 While the pairing of membranes with anaerobic treatment
represents an advancement, it still falls short of accomplishing holistic treatment.
The lead PI Dr. Parameswaran has operated one of the first pilot scale AnMBRs that combines
both energy and nutrient recovery from municipal wastewater at a rate of 1000 gallons per day,
resulting in a successful long term operation at Ft. Riley, KS. 22-24 The pilot-scale system has now
been relocated to Kansas State University’s animal farm, where it can demonstrate Carbon and
nutrient valorization from swine lagoon wastewater along with valuable water recovery.
Preliminary life cycle analysis/techno-economic analysis for the proposed research has been
performed by co-PI Khanna, and will be further developed in detail in this proposal. Separation
and subsequent extraction of organic acids is a central bottleneck for successful fermentation and
co-PI Dr. Ning Sun (Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory) has the expertise to develop
novel separation, transformation, and extraction techniques for the organic acids, including ionic
liquids, adsorption columns, distillation etc.25-27

3.0 Results and Discussion
3.1 Integrated flat-plate microbial electrochemical cell-assisted AnMBR

3.1.1 AnMBR process performance for swine wastewater treatment

Summary: In this final report, we summarized the findings from the semi-continuous AnMBR
operation treating swine wastewater in a 114L volume system with two membrane modules
(ZeeWeed 2.5) constituting a submerged configuration. We were able to achieve a targeted lower
HRT of around 6 days and experiments are ongoing in this aspect with focus on AnMBR process
performance with respect to solids concentrations and the limiting factors to improve membrane
flux. Also, we have evaluated the performance of a stainless-steel crossflow microfiltration (SS)
membrane module characterized by high fouling resistance and robust operational stability under
high total solids (TS) concentrations. The module, designed to achieve a high net flux (LMH) and
low hydraulic retention time (HRT), was assessed as an alternative to PVDF membranes for
improving fouling management and reducing the energy and chemical footprint during the
treatment of swine wastewater. We also presented and summarized the preliminary results from
the stainless-steel (SS) membrane testing using real swine wastewater. The initial hydraulic
performance evaluation was conducted under a range of operating pressures (15–70 PSI) to
determine the corresponding permeate flow rates (GPM) and assess the membrane’s hydraulic
stability under varying transmembrane pressures. In addition, permeate characterization was
performed to evaluate treatment effectiveness, including measurements of pH, COD, and turbidity
(NTU). These analyses provide important baseline data for assessing the SS membrane’s potential
to achieve stable flux performance, high fouling resistance, and improved effluent quality
compared to conventional polymeric membranes. Building on these results, the next phase of this
study will focus on long-term performance testing, including evaluation of fouling behavior,
optimization of clean-in-place (CIP) procedures, and comparative analysis with PVDF membranes
to fully establish the SS module’s suitability for energy-efficient and low-maintenance swine
wastewater treatment.
Progress – Our study demonstrated the superior removal of BOD5 and COD from swine
wastewater during short period operation (55 days) with average efficiencies greater than 80% and
90%, respectively (Figure 2). The high removal efficiencies were achieved at low Hydraulic
Retention Times (HRTs) at 7-12 days, which was 3-5 X lower than conventional techniques for

swine wastewater treatment. We are targeting for a lower HRT of around 6 days and need more
data for investigating the AnMBR process performance at 6 days HRT.

Figure 3: Impact of Total Solids Concentration on TMP, Flux, and Permeate Production
Rate in AnMBR Operation
 Figure 3 illustrates the operational dynamics of an Anaerobic Membrane Bioreactor (AnMBR)
over a 279-day period, specifically focusing on the relationship between Total Solids (TS)
concentration, Transmembrane Pressure (TMP), permeate Flux, and 10-minute cycle Production
Rate. The blue line indicates TMP (kPa), the orange line represents flux (LMH), the green line
shows short-term permeate production rate (L/hr), and the yellow dots denote TS concentration
(%). Throughout the monitoring period, TMP trends upward initially, peaking around Day 70–
100, before showing fluctuations with smaller peaks and troughs later. This rise in TMP suggests
increased membrane fouling or resistance to flow, likely due to the accumulation of solids or
biofilm development. Conversely, flux remains relatively stable between 2–6 LMH, with transient
dips and spikes, notably aligning with periods of high TMP or production disruptions. The short-
cycle production rate mirrors the flux pattern, demonstrating a drop during periods of high TMP,
reaffirming that membrane resistance significantly affects short-term productivity. TS
concentrations remain between ~2.5–5% and show a moderate correlation with TMP; higher TS
values generally align with increased TMP levels, especially evident between Day 60 and Day
180. This indicates that elevated solids loading contributes to fouling pressure on the membrane.
Spikes in TS concentration also correspond to dips in flux and production, reinforcing the inverse
relationship between solids buildup and membrane performance.

 Figure 4: Methane Production, Methane Percent, OLR, Bioreactor Solids and COD
 As shown in figure 4, Organic Loading Rates (OLRs) as high 320 mg CODww/L-day produced
commensurate improvements to biogas yield (L CH4/kg COD), while dips in yield triggered by

methanogenic yields for swine wastewater from conventional anaerobic digesters and AnMBRs
operated at mesophilic temperatures as well as from AnMBRs performing mainstream municipal
wastewater treatment. It should be noted that the COD removal efficiency and the methanogenic
yield closely tracked the bioreactor OLR and Volatile Solid (VS) content, with the higher removal
efficiencies and commensurate higher methanogenic observed at the higher OLRs and VS
concentrations in the AnMBR, as shown in Figure 4. There was also an observable lag prior to an
increase in methanogenic yield after an increase between 30 – 42 days, while methane yield
increased after 50 days in OLR. This could likely be attributed to anaerobic metabolism adapting
to the increased organic loading, specifically the methanogenesis phase or could also be explained
by a drop in average VS concentration in the AnMBR during the same period.
 Superior process performance from the AnMBR occurred at the expense of membrane fouling,
as can be expected over time from any membrane-based operation. As shown in Figure 5, the total
solid concentration around 3.6-4.0 g/L, which greater higher than standard limit issued by
manufacture (2-3 g/L), could be the main reason reaching 90% fouling (% permeability lost). This
fouling observation may be attributed to the role of inorganic precipitation on membrane surfaces,
which is likely influenced by the Total Solids (TS) concentration in the system. Higher TS
concentrations can contribute to fouling by promoting the accumulation of both inorganic and
organic foulants on membrane surfaces and within pores. The correlation between TS

 13

concentration, fouling, and net flux becomes evident as increased fouling typically reduces the net
flux by limiting the membranes’ permeability. In this context, inorganic precipitation may play a
key role in reversing fouling, potentially creating synergistic effects that help dislodge associated
organic foulants from membrane pores, thereby mitigating the impact of TS concentration on
fouling and improving the net flux. To achieve higher flux, we wasted solids regularly and after
every wasting event a higher flux was observed. We are currently working on to find the

 In this report, we proposed two different fouling control strategies, namely chemically
enhanced backpulse (CEB), and passive batch recovery (PBR) for fouling management in the
pilot-scale swine AnMBR. These were compared to a control group in which permeate was
produced in semi continuous operation (SCO) without cleans or extended (>16 hr) relax between
runs. Two common chemicals used for CEB are bleach (0.63% v/v) (Sodium hypochlorite) and
citric acid (0.54% v/v). Both citric acid and bleach have significant economic and environmental
footprint for their production and delivery. This study aimed to minimize the chemical cleaning
footprint for fouling control while preserving membrane performance and permeate flux. The
volumes of citric acid and bleach used for CIP maintenance were limited to 250 mL per 10-minute
cleaning cycle compared to the manufacturer protocol of 15 L for 5 hours twice a week, and the

 14

cleaning frequency was varied between 0.37 and 2.5 times per week, with an intention to minimize
the chemical footprint for fouling management in the pilot-scale AnMBR as long as process
performance or critical TMP were not reached. A Laboport UN 816 double diaphragm air operated
pump (KNF, NJ, USA) was used for continuous sparging of membranes with biogas pulled from
the headspace at approximately 12-14 L/min, including batch periods. A Wilcoxon Rank Sum test
was chosen to analyze the statistical relevance of the difference in observed treatment between
CEB and PBR due to the differences in sample size and non-normal distribution of data in sample
sizes under n=30. The Wilcoxon test is robust to these conditions.

Summary: The aim of this task is summarized the develop a flat-plate microbial electrochemical

anodes (314 cm² total surface area), while stainless-steel mesh electrodes functioned as cathodes,
separated by anion exchange membranes.

Progress - Since the last annual report, significant efforts have been made to continuously feed
the system with acetate-containing phosphate-buffered saline to support microbial activity, while
the cathode chambers were supplied with alkaline sodium chloride solution. System performance
was evaluated by monitoring acetate removal, gas production, hydrogen generation, and pH during
operation. The following strategies were employed:

 The flat-plate microbial electrochemical cell (MxC) design shown here modifies the traditional
two chamber MxC layout by greatly minimizing the anode and cathode distances, with a common
anode compartment flanked by two cathode chambers on either side, as shown in Figure 13. The

projected to 157 cm2 of available surface for anode respiring bacteria (ARBs) to attach to. The
shared anode chamber and dual cathode chambers were each separated by anion exchange
membranes (AMI, Membranes International, USA) to allow for ion migration. Each of the

three-dimensional rendering of the assembled MxC as well as an exploded diagram are shown in

3.1.3 Swine wastewater fermentation evaluated at pH 5 and pH 9

Summary: The aim of this task is to enhance volatile fatty acid (VFA) production as well as to
quantify and profile the various VFAs generated during pH-controlled fermenter operation fed
with swine wastes (pH 5 and 9). To boost production, strategies such as replacing solids with active
inoculum/solids sourced from the anoxic tank of a local wastewater treatment plant and subjecting
approximately 23% of the solids from the fermenters to heat shock treatment during each feeding
event were implemented.

Progress - Since the last annual report, a significant effort has been made to increase the VFA
production from swine wastes in the fermentation reactors. The following strategies were
employed:

Solid replacement
 Solid replacement procedure was carried out periodically in both fermenters, replacing 1% of
the solids with active inoculum from thickened anoxic sludge. In the pH 5 fermenter, efficiency
increased from 8.23% (Day 451) to 10.42% (Day 460) in the first week, further improved to
12.71% (Day 464) by the second week, and doubled to 24.04% (Day 477) after three weeks, as
shown in Figure 16a. The pH 9 fermenter showed a significant increase in fermentation efficiency,
increasing from 15% (Day 451) to 46% (Day 460) within a week, doubling to 90.81% (Day 464)
by the second week, and then decreasing to 67.74% after three weeks, as seen in Figure 16b. The
observed efficiency improvements may be attributed to the introduction of an active microbial
community or the presence of readily available soluble COD in the anoxic sludge. Additionally,
efficiency trends appear to be directly correlated with solids retention time (SRT), as shown in
Figure 19, highlighting the importance of retaining solids within the system for an extended
period. Implementing the process into the AnMBR could be a promising solution to achieve this
objective. During the experiments, the pH 5 fermenter primarily produced propionate and caproate
(Figure 18), while the pH 9 fermenter predominantly produced acetate and iso caproate. Further

 23

investigation is required to validate these findings and clarify the mechanisms behind these
observations (Figure 18).
 Solid replacement procedure was carried out periodically in both fermenters, replacing 1% of
the solids with active inoculum from thickened anoxic sludge. Figure 19 illustrates the temporal
changes in the proportional distribution of various volatile fatty acids (VFAs) produced during
fermentation at pH 5, over a 62-day operational period (Days 581 to 642). Among the VFAs,
propionate is the predominant product throughout the monitoring period, typically accounting for
over 40% of the total VFA composition. Its dominance becomes even more pronounced after Day
620, where it rises to approximately 60–80%, indicating a potential shift in metabolic activity or
microbial community structure favoring acetogenesis under prolonged operation at low pH.
Acetate is the second most abundant VFA, contributing around 20–40% in the early and middle
phases (Days 585–618), though its share declines significantly toward the end of the monitoring
period as propionate becomes more dominant.
Valerate and iso-valerate appear in moderate proportions, with valerate increasing slightly in the
later phase, suggesting the possible enhancement of chain elongation pathways under certain
conditions. Iso-butyrate and butyrate are consistently present in smaller proportions, indicating
limited but stable production across the operational timeline. Iso-caproate is either absent or
present at negligible levels, suggesting that caproate-type elongation was not favored at this low
pH or under the prevailing substrate and microbial conditions. Notably, a distinct shift in VFA
composition is observed around Day 620, where the propionate fraction increases sharply and
acetate and other minor VFAs decrease, possibly due to operational adjustments, changes in
substrate utilization efficiency, or microbial adaptation. Overall, the profile suggests that
maintaining pH at 5 supports propionate-rich VFA production, with dynamic interactions among
metabolic pathways influencing the relative abundance of other VFAs over time. Maintaining pH
5 effectively directs carbon flow toward VFA production rather than biogas generation, which is
beneficial when targeting VFA-rich effluents for further bioprocessing (e.g., chain elongation,
bioplastic production). The significant accumulation of iso-caproate is particularly promising for
producing medium-chain fatty acids, which are high-value chemicals. Nonetheless, the variability
in fermentation efficiency suggests that further optimization (e.g., feeding strategies, hydraulic
retention time adjustments, or selective bioaugmentation) may be required to stabilize and enhance
VFA yields.

3.2 Volatile Organic acids separation from permeate and its recovery

 Recovery of microbially produced VFAs is challenging due to the high volume/ low VFA
concentrations and the complex nature of the fermentation broth. Several techniques including gas
stripping and absorption using calcium carbonate slurry 9, resin adsorption 10, solvent extraction

 30

11
  , electrodialysis 12, and membrane filtration 13 have been reported for VFA recovery. In this study,
adsorptive recovery and solvent extraction using ionic liquid were evaluated for recovery of
anaerobically produced volatile fatty acids (VFAs).
3.2.1 Adsorptive recovery of VFAs
3.2.1.1 Identification of resin candidate and batch adsorption process conditions
 Several weak base resins including Diaion WA20, Relite RAM2, Diaion WA30, and synthetic
adsorbent resins such as Diaion HP20, Sepabeads SP70 and Sepabeads SP700 were tested with
model hexanoic acid solution to identify the resin with highest VFA adsorption capacity. Using a
10% w/v resin loading and 1% w/v hexanoic acid solution, among the evaluated resins, Relite
RAM2 showed an adsorption capacity of 174.10 ± 8.69 mg of hexanoic acid/ g of resin and was
selected as the resin candidate for this study (Figure 24a). Compared to the literature, Lewatit VC
OP 1065 could adsorb 182.20 ± 25.40 mg/g of acetic acid, 153.37 ± 22.96 mg/g of propionic acid,
207.55 ± 30.44 mg/g of butyric acid and 217.16 ± 31.80 mg/g of valeric acid, at 5 g/L VFA
concentration 14. For a VFA mixture containing 1 g/L of acetic acid, propionic acid, butyric acid,
isobutyric acid, isovaleric acid and valeric acid, Amberlite IRA-67 (50 g/L) and Dowex Optipore
L-493 (75 g/L) had a total equilibrium adsorption capacity of 119.6 mg/g and 54.39 mg/g,
respectively (Eregowda et al., 2020). Using a 1 wt % total VFA concentration (model solution), a
total VFA adsorption capacity of 114 mg/g was observed for nonfunctionalized resin (Reyhanitash
et al., 2017). Thus, adsorption capacity of hexanoic acid onto resin are comparable to reported
values. The hexanoic acid adsorption was highest when the solution pH was ca. 3.2. As weak
adsorption resins have secondary or tertiary amine functional groups, they are proposed to interact
with the carboxylic forms of VFA. As the pKa of VFA is about 4.8, most of the VFA will be in
undissociated form at pH 3.2, thus favoring interactions with the amine groups of resin. The
hexanoic acid adsorption capacity was observed to be independent of reaction temperature (Figure
24b), therefore 30°C was considered optimal in this study, as fermentation process is conducted
close to 30°C. Thereby, minimizing the need of heat exchange and continuous processing of broth.

3.2.1.5 Resin reusability

 After recovery of resin containing adsorbed VFA, the resin was contacted with various
desorption solvents such as water, ethanol, and aqueous NaOH solution (0.5% w/v and 1% w/v)
to identify a suitable desorption solvent that can achieve maximum VFA desorption and recovery.
Among the evaluated desorption solvents, 0.5% w/v NaOH solution achieved nearly complete
desorption of adsorbed VFA with 0.5 h of contact time, and desorption efficiency remained similar
when NaOH concentration was raised to 1% w/v. Only 9 to 14% of the adsorbed VFA can be
desorbed using water. Similarly, ethanol can desorb 6 to 16% of adsorbed acetic acid, propionic
acid, and butyric acid. A recovery of 37% was observed for hexanoic acid using ethanol. Thus,
0.5% w/v NaOH was selected as the desorption solvent for further study.

The resin reusability was evaluated by using the same resin for successive adsorption and
desorption cycles using both model VFA solution and VFA spiked fermentation broth. The resin
recovered after desorption was used for subsequent adsorption cycle without washing. The results
of adsorption and recovery of VFA from model solution and fermentation broth using 0.5% w/v

 37

NaOH are shown in Figure 22. As shown in Figure 22a, above 90% VFA adsorption has been
achieved for 5 successive cycles using the model solution. The adsorption was reduced to 85-90%
in cycle 6-9. Overall, adsorption of over 85% could be maintained for up to 9 cycles. As shown in
Figure 22b, 80% VFA recovery was achieved for 9 successive cycles of the same batch of resin
using 0.5% w/v NaOH as a desorption agent. Similarly, adsorption (Figure 22c) and recovery
(Figure 22d) efficiencies were maintained for 9 successive cycles using broth. Although over 90%
of adsorbed VFA could be recovered, the recovery rate (considering both adsorption and
desorption) was significantly lower for broth than that observed for the model solution. A 1.75 M
NaOH is suggested to regenerate Relite RAM2 (vendor recommended). The use of 0.5% w/v
NaOH (0.125 M) NaOH as a desorption solvent also assisted in the desorption and regeneration
of the resin active group. The applied NaOH concentration is significantly lower, which can have
a positive impact on the economics of the process. It was reported that the adsorption capacity for
acetic acid (85-88%) was maintained over 3 successive cycles using Amberlyst A21 and synthetic
solution (Rebecchi et al., 2016). Similarly, nonfunctionalized resin was stable over four successive
cycles, however a short water-wash stage had to be included between cycles [13]. To our
knowledge, this is the first report showing the reusability of resin for 9 successive adsorption and
desorption cycles using both model solution and broth. The robustness of the process shows great
potential for industrial application of VFA recovery from dilute fermentation broth.

3.2.1.6 Improving VFA adsorption from fermentation broth
 As significantly lower VFA adsorption was achieved using VFA spiked fermentation broth
compared to model solution, various strategies such as a) adjusting pH of fermentation broth to
2.6 similar to model feedstock, b) contacting spent broth with fresh resin, and c) adjusting pH of
spent broth to 2.6 and contacting with fresh resin, were tested to improve the VFA adsorption from
broth. The pH of broth at 0.2% w/v VFA spiking was observed to be ca 4.0 compared to ca 2.6
noted for same VFA concentration model solution. As pH of solution affects the dissociation of
VFAs and thus interaction with weakly basic resins, the pH of VFA spiked broth was adjusted to
ca 2.6. The results of testing different strategies are shown in Figure 23. When the pH of broth
was adjusted to 2.6 using sulfuric acid, the % adsorption increased to about 70% compared to 65%
without pH adjustment. Upon contacting the spent broth containing residual VFAs with fresh resin,
the adsorption was observed to increase from 65% to 72-76%. Whereas, when the pH of spent
broth was adjusted to 2.6 and contacted with fresh resin, the adsorption remained to be 72 - 76%.
Therefore, no significant difference (p<0.05) was observed upon using fresh resin with spent broth
with or without pH adjustment. Acidification of broth to shift the equilibrium towards unionized
form of VFAs did result in improvement of adsorption, however, the maximum adsorption was
obtained by using fresh resin with spent broth. This process is similar to the usage of multiple resin
beds at industrial scale and avoids the need of using additional chemicals.

3.2.2 Solvent assisted VFA recovery

Hydrophobic ionic liquids (ILs) are greener alternatives for VFA recovery with higher extraction
efficiencies compared to organic solvents such as alcohols, ketones, aliphatic hydrocarbons, and
aliphatic amines.11, 16-18. Hydrophobic ILs containing phosphonium or ammonium cations, and
phosphinate or carboxylate anions are considered as potential solvents for VFA extraction.
Additionally, ILs can act as reaction media for esterification of VFAs into their ester derivatives.
In this work, a suitable hydrophobic IL was identified for VFA extraction from fermentation broth
and in-situ enzymatic esterification.
3.2.2.1 Ionic liquid selection
Various ILs including Trihexyltetradecyl phosphonium Dibutylphosphate ([P666,14][DBP]),
Trihexyltetradecyl phosphonium Chloride ([P666,14]Cl)), Trihexyltetradecyl phosphonium
Dimethylphosphate ([P666,14][DMP]), Tributylhexyl phosphonium Chloride ([P444,6]Cl)),
Tributylmethyl phosphonium Bis-2,4,4-trimethylpentyl phosphonium ([P444,1][TMP]) and 1-
dodecyl-3-methylimidazolium Dicyanamide ([C12C1IM][DCA]) were contacted with distilled
water at 1:1 ratio and vortexed for 5 min at 1500 rpm to identify potentially hydrophobic ILs.
Among the evaluated ILs, [P666,14][DMP], [P444,6]Cl), [P444,1][TMP] and [C12C1IM][DCA] were
noted to be miscible in water, whereas, [P666,14][DBP] and [P666,14]Cl) were immiscible in water
and formed two phases upon standing. Hence [P666,14][DBP] and [P666,14]Cl) were selected as IL
candidates to determine their VFAs extraction capacity.

3.2.2.2 VFA extraction capacity

The VFA extraction efficiency in IL was determined using the selected ILs [P666,14][DBP] and
[P666,14]Cl) by contacting 1 ml of model solution containing 0.8% w/v of acetic acid, propionic
acid, butyric acid, and hexanoic acid, each, in water with 100 mg of ILs. The mixture was mixed

 48

at 2000 rpm for 1 h. At the end of 1 h, the mixture was centrifuged for phase separation and an
aliquot from aqueous layer was carefully pipetted and used for VFA quantification. The difference
in VFA concentration in the aqueous phase was used to estimate VFA extraction in ILs. As shown
in Figure 27a, both [P666,14][DBP] and [P666,14]Cl) have similar extraction capacities for hexanoic
acid (68.7 mg/g IL and 68.2 mg/g IL, respectively). However, [P666,14][DBP] extracts more shorter
chain VFAs: acetic acid (21.4 mg/g vs. 15.5 mg/g IL), propionic acid (44.2 mg/g IL vs. 36.3 mg/g
IL) and butyric acid (62.7 mg/g IL vs. 57.3 mg/g IL) compared to [P666,14]Cl). As, [P666,14][DBP]
has relatively higher VFA extraction capacity than [P666,14]Cl), [P666,14][DBP] was selected as the
extraction solvent for further studies.

3.2.2.3 Computational study of VFA-IL interaction

The structural interaction of acetic acid, propionic acid, butyric acid, and hexanoic acid, with ILs
were modeled using density functional theory (DFT) using the TmoleX software package (Version
23.1.0). To generate the data for COSMO-RS calculations, a single point DFT analysis was
conducted using the Becke Perdew 86 (BP86) along with the Triple-Zeta Valence with Polarization
(TZVP) parameters. These COSMO files were then transferred to COSMOThermX (Version
23.0.0). All subsequent COSMO-RS analyses utilized the BP_TZVP_23.ctd parameterization file
for calculations. The generated COSMO files were then used as input to calculate the sigma
profiles, sigma potentials of the isolated molecules, and excess enthalpy (HE) and logarithmic
activity coefficients ln(γ) of VFA solubility in [P666,14][DBP], and [P666,14]Cl). In COSMO-RS
calculations, the molar fraction of VFAs was set as 0.2 and the molar fraction of ionic liquids was
set to 0.8 to mimic the experimental conditions.

the experimental values.

3.2.2.4 Optimization of enzymatic esterification in IL

 Esterification of hexanoic acid was conducted using 0.8% w/w hexanoic acid in [P666,14][DBP]
with 5 wt% enzyme loading and resulted in 85.2 % conversion of hexanoic acid into esters in 4 h
at 60 °C. Increasing the enzyme dose to 10 wt% resulted in a similar conversion (83.4%) yield.
Therefore, to optimize the esterification reactions of VFA with ethanol in in [P666,14][DBP] using
Novozym 435 as the catalyst Box-Behnken response surface design of experiment was used. A
VFA-IL mixture comprising 50 % w/w of VFAs in IL (with equal parts of acetic, propionic,
butyric, and hexanoic acids) was prepared and used for the esterification reaction. The
esterification process was optimized by evaluating the effect of independent variables including
temperature (X1: 30, 45, and 60°C), reaction time (X2: 2, 4, and 6 h), molar ratio of ethanol: acids
molar ratio (X3: 1:1, 3:1, and 5:1) and enzyme dose (X4: 2.5, 5, and 7.5 wt%). The total amount
of ethanol required at each molar ratio was calculated for each VFA. The amount of enzyme
(Novozym 435) required was calculated based on the total reactant weight. For the esterification
reaction, the required amounts of reactants in [P666,14][DBP] were incubated in a shaker (MAXQ
8000, Thermo Scientific, MA, USA) at a specific temperature. Once the temperature was achieved,
the reaction was initiated by adding the enzymes. At the end of the reaction, the mixture was
centrifuged to separate the enzymes. An aliquot of the reaction mixture was diluted and used to
quantify the residual VFAs. The difference in VFA concentration was used to determine the %
conversion of acids into esters.

the highest increase observed in Trial 3. The enhanced capacity is primarily attributed to the
smaller particle size, which increases the specific surface area, shortens internal diffusion
pathways, and improves access to active ion-exchange sites within the clinoptilolite structure.
These results demonstrate that simple mechanical size reduction can significantly improve
ammonium uptake efficiency and may represent a practical pretreatment strategy for optimizing
clinoptilolite performance in ammonium recovery systems.

Twenty CSTRs were operated for this task. Over all HRTs and pH investigated, co-fermentation
mixtures where FW composition was higher than SS showed higher VFA production,

In the 5‑day HRT reactors, VFA production was higher at pH 9.0 than at pH 5.5, especially for

yielded higher VFA production. This is attributed to the higher proportion of readily biodegradable
organic matter in FW, which enhances substrate availability for acidogenic bacteria. Acetic acid
was the predominant VFA produced, particularly at longer HRTs. The highest overall
concentration of acetic acid was 950 mg COD/L under the condition of a 10-day HRT at pH 5.5
with 80% FW:20% SS ratio. However, VFA production decreased significantly at pH 9 with the
extended HRT. This was likely due to methanogenesis and converting the VFAs to methane gas.

3.6 Techno-Economic and Life Cycle Analysis of the Integrated AnMBR Platform
 This
section presents the methodology and results of the techno-economic analysis (TEA) and life cycle
assessment (LCA) conducted for the integrated anaerobic membrane bioreactor (AnMBR)
platform for swine wastewater treatment and resource recovery. The TEA and LCA were
developed to evaluate the economic feasibility and environmental performance of the platform
under varying influent conditions and plant scales.

3.6.1 Process Modeling Framework

The integrated platform was modeled as a sequence of unit operations: anaerobic membrane
bioreactor (AnMBR), vacuum degasser, nitrogen recovery, phosphorus recovery, and constructed
wetland. Each unit was described by established process models, and the full system was solved
at steady state.
3.6.1.1 Anaerobic Membrane Bioreactor
Biological conversion was modeled using the Anaerobic Digestion Model No. 1 (ADM1) [15, 16],
which tracks 35 state variables across acidogenesis, acetogenesis, and methanogenesis pathways.
Gas-phase dynamics, including liquid-to-gas transfer of methane, carbon dioxide, and hydrogen
and headspace pressure, were incorporated from the Benchmark Simulation Model No. 2 (BSM2)
[17]. The BSM2 default parameter set was adopted due to its demonstrated reliability across
anaerobic digestion studies [16] and its suitability in the absence of site-specific calibration data
for swine wastewater.

3.6.3 Life Cycle Assessment

Three influent COD concentrations were evaluated to capture the variability commonly observed
in swine wastewater: a low COD scenario (2,604 mg/L), a base case scenario (5,208 mg/L), and a
high COD scenario (7,812 mg/L). COD governs methane production potential and thus energy
recovery, which strongly influences overall platform economics. The base case COD was used as
the reference condition for subsequent plant capacity and minimum selling price (MSP)
benchmarking analyses. In addition to influent quality, plant treatment capacity was varied across
a representative range (1–8 MGD) to assess economies of scale, with influent COD held constant
at the base case value. The MSP of recovered nitrogen and phosphorus fertilizers was benchmarked
against prevailing market prices to assess competitiveness under different scale conditions. A one-
at-a-time sensitivity analysis varied key techno-economic parameters ±10% from baseline to
identify dominant cost drivers, with results visualized via tornado diagrams [33].

3.6.4 Results and Discussion

A cradle-to-gate life cycle assessment (LCA) was conducted to quantify the environmental impacts
of the integrated AnMBR platform. The functional unit was defined as the treatment of 1 m³ of
swine wastewater. The system boundary encompasses all unit operations from wastewater intake
through final effluent discharge, including chemical and energy inputs, biogas utilization, and
nutrient product recovery. The primary impact category assessed was global warming potential
(GWP).
Life cycle inventory (LCI) data were derived directly from the optimized steady-state mass and
energy balances produced by the TEA framework, ensuring consistency between the economic
and environmental assessments. The LCA was implemented in SimaPro [34] using established
characterization methods. Environmental credits were assigned for recovered products
(bioelectricity, nitrogen fertilizer, phosphorus fertilizer) based on the avoided burden approach,
reflecting the displacement of conventional production pathways.

3.6.4.1 Effect of influent COD concentration on platform economics and environmental
performance

The levelized net cost (LNC) of the integrated platform was evaluated under three influent COD
scenarios at a fixed plant capacity of 1 MGD (Figure 47). At low COD (2,604 mg/L), the platform
exhibited the highest net cost at approximately 0.57 $/m³, reflecting limited methane production
and thus minimal energy revenue to offset treatment costs. At the base case COD (5,208 mg/L),
the LNC decreased to approximately 0.26 $/m³, as increased organic loading improved biogas
yield and energy recovery. At high COD (7,812 mg/L), the platform approached breakeven
operation, with the LNC near zero. These results demonstrate the strong sensitivity of platform

 89

economics to influent wastewater strength, where higher organic loading directly translates to
greater methane recovery and revenue.

Figure 47. Levelized net cost (LNC) of the integrated platform under low, base, and high COD influent scenarios at
1 MGD plant capacity.

The environmental performance followed a similar trend (Figure 48). Net greenhouse gas (GHG)
emissions at low COD were positive (approximately 0.6 kg CO₂-eq/m³), indicating that the
platform was a net emitter under dilute conditions. At base case COD, net GHG emissions became
negative (approximately –0.4 kg CO₂-eq/m³), meaning the platform achieved a net carbon benefit
through avoided emissions from displaced products. At high COD, the net GHG benefit was most
pronounced (approximately –1.5 kg CO₂-eq/m³), driven by substantially greater bioelectricity
generation.

A similar trend was observed for phosphorus fertilizer MSP (Figure 54), which decreased from
approximately 900 $/ton at 1 MGD to approximately 535 $/ton at 8 MGD. The MSP crosses below
the market benchmark (approximately 590 $/ton) at approximately 6 MGD. These results confirm

The one-at-a-time ±10% sensitivity analysis (Figure 55) reveals that ammonia fertilizer price is
by far the dominant driver of platform economics, producing the widest swing in levelized net
cost. Bio-electricity price exerts the second largest influence, followed by plant life and
phosphorus fertilizer price, all of which produce meaningful but smaller shifts in LNC. Discount
rate has a moderate effect, while electricity price, calcium oxide price, Ca:P ratio, clinoptilolite
price, and replacement frequency have comparatively minor effects.

Figure 55. Sensitivity of levelized net cost ($/m³) to ±10% changes in key techno-economic parameters. Red bars
indicate the effect of a +10% increase; blue bars indicate the effect of a –10% decrease. The dashed line represents
the base case LNC.

 The dominance of ammonia fertilizer price is explained by the fact that nitrogen recovery
generates the single largest revenue stream on the platform, constituting the largest share of all
platform revenue and far exceeding the net cost itself. Swine wastewater is exceptionally nitrogen-
rich due to the high protein content of swine feed, resulting in very high TKN concentrations in
the effluent. The clinoptilolite zeolite beds selectively adsorb ammonium at high efficiency, and
the recovered product (anhydrous ammonia) commands a meaningful market price. Because the
nitrogen load is large and the recovery is efficient, a modest percentage change in ammonia
fertilizer price translates to a large absolute shift in net cost. Bio-electricity price ranks second
because the AnMBR digests the organic matter in swine waste anaerobically, and because the
influent COD is high, the biogas yield is substantial. The methane is converted to electricity via
CHP at approximately 30% electrical efficiency and sold to the grid, making bio-electricity the
second largest revenue stream. It ranks below nitrogen recovery because the conversion chain from
COD to methane to electricity dilutes the value compared to the direct nutrient recovery pathway,
but the CHP revenue is nonetheless large enough that the price at which that electricity is sold is
the second most influential parameter. Plant life ranks third because it is the only financial
parameter in the top four — it does not represent a physical resource flow but rather controls how
the capital investment is spread over time. The platform requires significant upfront capital, and

 95

the capital recovery factor is nonlinearly sensitive to plant lifetime: longer asset life spreads the
same capital investment over more years, reducing the annualized payment significantly.
Phosphorus fertilizer price ranks fourth because phosphorus recovery generates the next largest
revenue stream after nitrogen and bio-electricity. However, swine wastewater contains less
phosphorus than nitrogen on a mass basis, and the unit value of phosphate fertilizer per kilogram
of nutrient is lower than that of ammonia fertilizer, so the phosphorus revenue stream, while
substantial, is smaller and a percentage change in its price moves the net cost by a correspondingly
smaller amount. Together, these results underscore that securing stable, competitive pricing for
recovered products — particularly nitrogen fertilizer and bio-electricity — is the most
consequential lever for achieving economic viability. Favorable financing terms and extended
asset life are important secondary factors, while material costs on the phosphorus recovery side
have limited impact under baseline conditions.

3.6.5 Conclusions

 The techno-economic and life cycle analyses of the integrated AnMBR platform for swine
wastewater treatment yielded the following key findings:
Influent COD concentration strongly governs both economic and environmental outcomes. At high
COD (7,812 mg/L), the platform achieved near-breakeven levelized net cost and a net GHG benefit
of approximately –1.5 kg CO₂-eq/m³. At low COD (2,604 mg/L), the platform exhibited a net cost
of 0.57 $/m³ and was a net GHG emitter, underscoring that the platform is best suited for higher-
strength wastewaters typical of concentrated animal feeding operations.
Plant scale is a critical determinant of economic feasibility. The levelized net cost decreased
sharply from 0.26 $/m³ at 1 MGD to near zero at 6 MGD, with the platform becoming net revenue-
positive beyond this threshold. Both nitrogen and phosphorus fertilizer MSPs reached market-
competitive levels at approximately 6 MGD, confirming that regional or centralized facilities are
more likely to deliver cost-effective resource recovery.
 The platform achieves net-negative GHG emissions across all plant capacities evaluated (1–8
MGD) at the base case COD, ranging from –0.60 to –0.45 kg CO₂-eq/m³. The dominant GHG
credits derive from bioelectricity generation and avoided synthetic fertilizer production, while
NaCl consumption for clinoptilolite regeneration represents the largest single emission burden.
Sensitivity analysis identifies the nitrogen fertilizer sales price as the dominant economic driver,
followed by bio-electricity price, plant lifetime, and phosphorus fertilizer price. Discount rate has
a moderate effect, while phosphorus-side material costs and adsorbent parameters show minimal
influence under baseline conditions.
Together, these results demonstrate that the integrated AnMBR platform is a technically and
economically viable pathway for circular swine wastewater management when implemented at
sufficient scale, with stable markets for recovered fertilizers being essential for long-term
economic sustainability.

parameters and determine the effectiveness of the new configuration for long-term application.
