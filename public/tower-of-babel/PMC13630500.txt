Functional Genetic Variation in Ubiquitin D Promoter Influences Susceptibility to Atopic Dermatitis
Authors: Yi Ying Eliza Lim, Yang Yie Sio, Terence Yin Weng Lam, Yee‐How Say, Kavita Reginald, Fook Tim Chew
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Summary A ubiquitin D (UBD) promoter haplotype was identified to confer protection against atopic dermatitis (AD).The rs362513‐G allele suppresses UBD transcription and downstream NF‐κB–regulated cytokine production, independent of the AD‐risk SNP rs995185. Keywords: atopic dermatitis, epistasis, I‐kappa‐B‐alpha (IκBα), nuclear factor kappa B (NF‐κB), single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs), ubiquitin D

To the Editor,

Atopic dermatitis (AD) is a chronic inflammatory skin disorder driven by complex interactions between genetic susceptibility, immune dysregulation and epithelial barrier dysfunction. Central to AD pathogenesis is sustained activation of the nuclear factor kappa B (NF‐κB) pathway, which orchestrates the transcription of pro‐inflammatory cytokines and chemokines in both immune and epithelial compartments [1, 2]. While genome‐wide association studies (GWAS) have identified numerous AD risk loci, the majority reside in non‐coding regions and the functional mechanisms linking regulatory variation to disease remain poorly defined. Ubiquitin D (UBD, also known as FAT10) is a ubiquitin‐like modifier that promotes proteasomal degradation of phosphorylated IκBα, thereby facilitating NF‐κB nuclear translocation and transcriptional activity [3]. Elevated UBD expression has been reported in multiple inflammatory and immune‐mediated contexts, yet its genetic regulation and contribution to AD have not been previously elucidated.

We conducted a GWAS of 5323 Singaporean and Malaysian Chinese individuals, comprising 1261 ad cases and 4062 controls, using five genotyping arrays with imputation against the 1000 Genomes Phase III Beijing Han Chinese reference panel [4, 5]. A genome‐wide significant association was detected at chromosome 6p22.1 within the promoter region of UBD, led by rs995185 (p = 1.7 × 10−15; OR = 1.46, 95% confidence interval, CI = 1.33–1.61). This association was further validated in an independent Precision Health Research, Singapore (PRECISE‐SG100K) cohort comprising 741 ad cases and 14,123 controls of Singaporean Chinese individuals. Fixed‐effect meta‐analysis of both cohorts demonstrated that the minor allele ‘T’ of rs995185 was significantly associated with AD, with a pooled OR of 1.295 (95% CI = 1.206–1.390, p < 0.0001). Although a second promoter SNP of UBD, rs362513, did not independently reach genome‐wide significance and exhibited low linkage disequilibrium with rs995185 (r 2 = 0.13), its regulatory position prompted further haplotype‐level interrogation. In the GWAS cohort (n = 5323), haplotype analysis of these two variants revealed three common promoter haplotypes, among which the rs362513‐G/rs995185‐A haplotype (Haplotype 2) was significantly enriched in controls and conferred protection against AD (Figure 1A).

To characterize these promoter haplotypes, we cloned approximately 2 kb of the UBD promoter encompassing each haplotype into luciferase reporter constructs. In HEK293T cells, Haplotype 2 consistently exhibited markedly reduced promoter activity compared with the other haplotypes at both 24‐ and 48‐h time points (Figure 1B). Notably, this suppression occurred despite the presence of the rs995185‐A allele. These findings suggest a regulatory cascade in which rs362513‐G attenuates the effects of rs995185‐A, demonstrating how different promoter variants may modulate UBD expression.

The biological relevance of this regulatory architecture was supported by complementary ex vivo and in vitro data. In sera from genotyped individuals, Haplotype 2 carriers exhibited significantly reduced levels of NF‐κB‐regulated inflammatory mediators interleukin‐1α (IL‐1α) and programmed death‐ligand 1 (PD‐L1), consistent with attenuated NF‐κB signalling (Figure S1, Online Repository). To validate these effects, we expressed full‐length UBD cDNA under the control of each promoter haplotype in THP‐1 human monocytes. Cells expressing UBD driven by Haplotype 2 secreted significantly lower levels of canonical NF‐κB target cytokines IL‐1β and IL‐8 compared with cells expressing Haplotypes 1 and 3, reinforcing the functional impact of haplotype‐dependent UBD regulation on inflammatory output (Figure S2, Online Repository).

We further interrogated independent transcriptomic datasets derived from human skin biopsies to assess the disease relevance of UBD in AD (Figure S3, Online Repository). Across multiple cohorts, UBD mRNA levels were significantly elevated in lesional AD skin compared with non‐lesional skin and healthy controls [6, 7]. Importantly, UBD expression was consistently reduced following anti‐inflammatory interventions, including topical corticosteroid Betamethasone, calcineurin inhibitor Pimecrolimus and narrow‐band UVB phototherapy [8, 9], supporting a dynamic link between UBD and inflammatory disease activity.

Using HaCaT human keratinocytes, we demonstrated that UBD was significantly upregulated via NF‐κB activation following house dust mite (HDM) stimulation, a major environmental allergen that triggers AD inflammation (Figure S4, Online Repository). Importantly, UBD knockdown attenuated HDM‐induced IL‐1α upregulation, supporting its role in promoting NF‐κB‐associated inflammation in AD (Figure S5, Online Repository). Together, these data integrate genetic association, functional genomics and clinical transcriptomics to implicate UBD as a modulator of NF‐κB‐driven inflammation in AD.

While the associations described here were identified in a Singapore–Malaysia Chinese population and were not detected in large European or Japanese GWAS, this likely reflects population‐specific linkage disequilibrium structures and haplotype frequencies. Such differences underscore the importance of ancestry‐matched analyses and illustrate how regulatory variants with modest individual effects may act epistatically to shape disease risk. Consistent with this, rs362513 has not been prioritized in single‐variant analyses alone despite exerting substantial functional influence in a haplotype context.

To further strengthen and extend the present findings, ongoing and future studies will focus on expanding the sample size and validating these associations in additional cohorts. These efforts will enable finer resolution of haplotype effects, assessment of population generalizability and exploration of how these variants relate to clinically relevant disease features. Mechanistically, future studies will focus on defining allele‐specific transcription factor binding at the UBD promoter and elucidating how rs362513 variants may constrain NF‐κB signaling under inflammatory conditions.

In summary, we identify a promoter haplotype variant in UBD that gates NF‐κB‐associated inflammation, thereby modulating susceptibility to atopic dermatitis. These findings highlight the value of haplotype‐level functional dissection of non‐coding GWAS loci and identify a regulatory variant that influences inflammatory output in a disease‐relevant pathway.
