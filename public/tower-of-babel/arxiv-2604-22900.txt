Module Lattice Security (Part II): Module Lattice Reduction via Optimal Sign Selection
Ming-Xing Luo

Abstract
We extend the CDPR lattice reduction algorithm from ideal to module lattices, leveraging the trace orthogonality of the power basis to decompose the module into rank‑1 submodules and applying CDPR independently to each. This base module reduction achieves a Hermite factor exp ⁡ ( O ~ ​ ( n ) ) \exp(\tilde{O}(\sqrt{n})) matching the ideal case, with a module reduction factor O ⁡ ( 1 ) O(1) independent of the rank, under a balance hypothesis automatically satisfied for MLWE-distributed bases. To control precision, we introduce CRT‑scaled rounding at totally split primes, reducing the Gram–Schmidt rounding error and yielding a bounded‑precision implementation. We further reformulate the CDPR sign‑selection subproblem as a mixed‑integer linear program, determining the optimal balanced discrepancy to be a universal constant δ ∗ ≈ 0.4407 \delta^{*}\approx 0.4407 . All results build on the class number one condition h k + = 1 h_{k}^{+}=1 established in Part I of this series.

2.2
Cyclotomic rings and fields

Given a

\zeta=\zeta_{2^{k}}=e^{2\pi i/2^{k}}

The Galois group is given by

\sigma_{a}:\zeta\mapsto\zeta^{a}

Definition 2.4
.

The full Galois embedding

\sigma(\alpha)=(\sigma_{1}(\alpha),\sigma_{3}(\alpha),\cdots,\sigma_{2n-1}(\alpha))

Definition 2.5
.

The field trace is defined by
\Tr_{K/\mathbb{Q}}(\alpha)=\sum_{a\in(\mathbb{Z}/2^{k}\mathbb{Z})^{\times}}\sigma_{a}(\alpha)

\displaystyle\Tr_{K/\mathbb{Q}}(\alpha\bar{\beta})=\langle\sigma(\alpha),\sigma(\beta)\rangle_{\mathbb{C}^{n}},

Notation
.

In this paper, we denote

(ii)

\langle\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}\rangle:=\Re\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}\rangle_{\mathbb{C}^{n}}

\mathbb{C}^{n}\cong\mathbb{R}^{2n}
;

(iii)

\langle\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}\rangle_{K}:=\sum_{k=1}^{d}a_{k}\overline{b_{k}}\in K

The CDPR Pipeline for Ideal Lattices

Before extending CDPR to module lattices, we first review the CDPR quantum attack
[
7
]
on principal ideal lattices (
d=1
), establishing the notation and identifying the sign-selection sub-problem which will be discussed in Section

3.1
Overview of CDPR

Given a principal ideal
I=\alpha R\subseteq R
(an ideal generated by a single element
\alpha\in K^{\times}
), the CDPR attack finds a short generator
\alpha^{\prime}=\alpha\epsilon
(for some unit
\epsilon\in R^{\times}
) with
\|\sigma(\alpha^{\prime})\|_{\infty}
bounded. The attack has three phases:

Phase 1:

Quantum PIP.
Use the quantum algorithm of Biasse–Song
[
27
,
28
]
to find some one generator
\alpha_{0}
of
I
in polynomial time.

Phase 2:

Log-unit CVP.
The set of all generators of
I
is
\{\alpha_{0}\epsilon:\epsilon\in R^{\times}\}
. In the log-embedding
\log|\sigma(\cdot)|:\mathbb{R}_{>0}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
, finding the shortest generator reduces to a Closest Vector Problem (CVP: given a lattice
\Lambda
and a target point
\mathbf{t}
, find
\mathbf{v}\in\Lambda
minimizing
\|\mathbf{t}-\mathbf{v}\|
) on the log-unit lattice
\Lambda=\{\log|\sigma(\epsilon)|:\epsilon\in R^{\times}\}\subset H_{0}
, where
H_{0}=\{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\sum x_{i}=0\}
is the trace-zero hyperplane.

Phase 3:

Decode/sign selection.
The CVP in Phase 2 is solved by Babai’s round-off algorithm
[
40
]
applied to the cyclotomic unit basis of
\Lambda
. The error in this step is controlled by the sign-selection problem: choosing signs
\mathbf{s}\in\{\pm 1\}^{|G|-1}
to minimize
\|\mathbf{M}\mathbf{s}\|_{\infty}
, where
\mathbf{M}
is the error matrix derived from the log-sine vector
\mathbf{z}
(Section
3.2
).

The overall approximation factor is
\gamma=\exp(\tilde{O}(\sqrt{n}))
, where the dominant comes from the distance between the target point and the nearest lattice point in
\Lambda
.

3.2
The sign-selection problem

In this subsection we define the sign-selection problem precisely.

The cyclotomic units
\xi_{a}
(Part I) generate a sublattice of the log-unit lattice
\Lambda
. By Main Theorem (Part I), the index
[R^{\times}:C^{+}]=h_{k}^{+}=1
(for
k\leq 12
), so the cyclotomic units generate
\Lambda
up to the torsion subgroup.

Using Babai’s round-off on the cyclotomic unit basis, it requires assigning signs to orbit representatives. Specifically, define the error matrix
\mathbf{M}\in\mathbb{R}^{|G|\times(|G|-1)}
with its component as

\displaystyle M_{i,j}=\Re(\IFFT(\hat{\mathbf{z}}\odot\widehat{\mathbf{e}_{j}^{\mathrm{ext}}}))_{i}-\tfrac{1}{2}z_{i},

(8)

where
\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{|G|}
is the log-sine vector defined in Eq.(
4
),
\hat{\mathbf{z}}=\FFT(\mathbf{z})
denotes its discrete Fourier transform over
G
,
\mathbf{e}_{j}^{\mathrm{ext}}\in\mathbb{R}^{|G|}
is the vector with constant
\frac{1}{2}
at the position of orbit element
j
and zero elsewhere, and
\odot
denotes the Hadamard entry-wise product. This matrix characterizes the cross-talk between orbit representatives when rounding in the cyclotomic unit basis.

For any sign vector
\mathbf{s}\in\{\pm 1\}^{|G|-1}
, the CDPR decode error is given by
\mathbf{e}=\mathbf{M}\mathbf{s}\in\mathbb{R}^{|G|}
. It determines the polynomial factor in the approximation bound.

Definition 3.1
.

The balanced sign-selection problem is to optimize

\displaystyle\delta^{*}(k)=\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}s\in\{\pm 1\}^{N_{s}}\\
\sum_{j=1}^{N_{s}}s_{j}\in\{\pm 1\}\end{subarray}}\|M\mathbf{s}\|_{\infty},

(9)

where
N_{s}=|G|-1
.

The balance constraint
\sum_{j}s_{j}\in\{\pm 1\}
ensures a near-equal number of positive and negative signs.

3.3
The tower greedy

In this subsection, we introduce the tower greedy algorithm
[
7
]
, which assigns signs level-by-level in the cyclotomic tower

\displaystyle\mathbb{Q}=K_{2}^{+}\subset K_{3}^{+}=\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_{8})\subset K_{4}^{+}=\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_{16})\subset\cdots\subset K_{k}^{+}=\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_{2^{k}}),

(10)

where
K_{L}^{+}
denotes the maximal real subfield of
\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_{2^{L}})
for notational simplicity. At each level

, the new orbit elements (from the extension
K_{L}^{+}/K_{L-1}^{+}
) receive signs
\pm 1
chosen to minimize the partial error
\|\mathbf{M}_{\leq L}\,\mathbf{s}_{\leq L}\|_{\infty}
, where
\mathbf{M}_{\leq L}
and
\mathbf{s}_{\leq L}
denote the columns of the basis matrix and the sign vector corresponding to all levels
\leq L
, respectively.

In this section we show a key property of 2-power cyclotomic rings, i.e., the trace orthogonality of the power basis. The orthogonality simplifies both coordinate-wise rounding (Lemma
4.2
) and the

. We also analyze the covering radius of the ring of integers and establish that the true covering radius is
\tfrac{\sqrt{n}}{2}\leq\mu_{\infty}(\sigma(R))\leq O(\sqrt{n\log n})
, i.e.
\mu_{\infty}(\sigma(R))=\tilde{\Theta}(\sqrt{n})
, far below the naive coordinate-wise rounding bound

.

4.1
The trace orthogonality lemma

The trace orthogonality of the power basis
\{1,\zeta,\zeta^{2},\cdots,\zeta^{n-1}\}
is a special property of
2
-power cyclotomic fields that plays a central role in our module reduction algorithm.

Lemma 4.1
.

For
0\leq a,b<n
, we have the trace orthogonality

\displaystyle\langle\sigma(\zeta^{a}),\sigma(\zeta^{b})\rangle=n\delta_{a,b},

(11)

where
\delta_{a,b}
denotes the delta function.

Proof.

. From Eq.(
2
), we have
\langle\sigma(\zeta^{a}),\sigma(\zeta^{b})\rangle_{\mathbb{C}^{n}}=\Tr_{K/\mathbb{Q}}(\zeta^{a-b})
. Since
\Tr_{K/\mathbb{Q}}(\zeta^{a-b})\in\mathbb{Q}\subset\mathbb{R}
for all
a,b
, i.e., the Hermitian inner product equals its real part, so we get
\langle\sigma(\zeta^{a}),\sigma(\zeta^{b})\rangle=\Tr_{K/\mathbb{Q}}(\zeta^{a-b})
. In what follows, we prove
\Tr_{K/\mathbb{Q}}(\zeta^{c})=n\cdot\delta_{c,0}
for
|c|<n
.

Case
c=0
:
we have
\Tr_{K/\mathbb{Q}}(1)=[K:\mathbb{Q}]=n
, as the trace of the multiplicative identity equals the degree of the field extension.

Case
c\not\equiv 0\pmod{2^{k-1}}
:
Let
c=2^{s}\cdot c^{\prime}
, where
c^{\prime}
is odd and
0\leq s\leq k-2
. Then,
\zeta^{c}=\zeta_{2^{k-s}}^{c^{\prime}}
is a primitive
2^{k-s}
-th root of unity as
c^{\prime}
is odd. Using the transitivity of the trace, we get

\displaystyle\Tr_{K/\mathbb{Q}}(\zeta^{c})=\Tr_{\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_{2^{k-s}})/\mathbb{Q}}(\Tr_{K/\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_{2^{k-s}})}(\zeta^{c})).

(12)

Since
\zeta^{c}\in\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_{2^{k-s}})
, the inner trace satisfies
\Tr_{K/\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_{2^{k-s}})}(\zeta^{c})=[K:\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_{2^{k-s}})]\cdot\zeta^{c}=2^{s}\cdot\zeta^{c}
. The outer trace is the sum of all Galois conjugates of a primitive
2^{k-s}
-th root of unity, which vanishes for
s\leq k-2
.

For the case
|c|=n=2^{k-1}
, we obtain
\zeta^{c}=-1
and
\Tr(-1)=-n\neq 0
. This is excluded by
|c|<n
. So, from both cases we get
\langle\sigma(\zeta^{a}),\sigma(\zeta^{b})\rangle=\Tr(\zeta^{a-b})=n\delta_{a,b}
.
∎

4.2
Covering radius bounds

In this subsection we analyze the covering radius of the ring
R
. We first prove the coordinate-wise rounding bound, which gives the rounding error for our base module reduction algorithm, and then establish tighter bounds.

Lemma 4.2
.

For any
t\in K\otimes_{\mathbb{Q}}\mathbb{R}
, the following rounding bounds hold:

(i)

There exists
c\in R
such that
\|\sigma(t-c)\|_{\infty}\leq\frac{n}{2}
.

(ii)

The same
c
satisfies
\|\sigma(t-c)\|_{2}\leq\frac{n}{2}
.

Proof.

Since
\{1,\zeta,\zeta^{2},\dots,\zeta^{n-1}\}
is a
\mathbb{Q}
-basis of
K
, any element
t\in K\otimes_{\mathbb{Q}}\mathbb{R}
admits a unique real-coefficient expansion as

\displaystyle t=\sum_{m=0}^{n-1}x_{m}\zeta^{m},\quad x_{m}\in\mathbb{R}.

(13)

For each
m
, let
c_{m}=\lfloor x_{m}\rceil
denote the nearest integer to
x_{m}
. Define
c=\sum_{m=0}^{n-1}c_{m}\zeta^{m}\in R
. The rounding error coefficients
\epsilon_{m}=x_{m}-c_{m}
satisfy
|\epsilon_{m}|\leq 1/2
for all
m
.

(i) For any Galois embedding
\sigma_{\ell}:K\hookrightarrow\mathbb{C}
(corresponding to odd
\ell
modulo
2^{k}
), we have

\displaystyle\sigma_{\ell}(t-c)=\sum_{m=0}^{n-1}\epsilon_{m}\sigma_{\ell}(\zeta^{m}).

(14)

Since
\sigma_{\ell}(\zeta^{m})=\zeta^{\ell m}
is a root of unity, it folows
|\sigma_{\ell}(\zeta^{m})|=1
for all
m,\ell
. Using the triangle inequality
|x+y|\leq|x|+|y|
we get

\displaystyle|\sigma_{\ell}(t-c)|\leq\sum_{m=0}^{n-1}|\epsilon_{m}|\cdot|\sigma_{\ell}(\zeta^{m})|=\sum_{m=0}^{n-1}|\epsilon_{m}|\leq\frac{n}{2}

(15)

as each term is bounded by
1/2
. Since this holds for all embeddings
\sigma_{\ell}
, we get
\|\sigma(t-c)\|_{\infty}\leq n/2
.

(ii) From Lemma
4.1
, the embedding vectors
\{\sigma(\zeta^{m})\}_{m=0}^{n-1}
are pairwise orthogonal with squared norm
n
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\langle\sigma(\zeta^{a}),\sigma(\zeta^{b})\rangle=n\cdot\delta_{a,b}.

(16)

The squared Euclidean norm of
\sigma(t-c)
is then given by

\displaystyle\|\sigma(t-c)\|_{2}^{2}=\Big\langle\sum_{m=0}^{n-1}\epsilon_{m}\sigma(\zeta^{m}),\sum_{m=0}^{n-1}\epsilon_{m}\sigma(\zeta^{m})\Big\rangle=\sum_{a,b=0}^{n-1}\epsilon_{a}\epsilon_{b}\langle\sigma(\zeta^{a}),\sigma(\zeta^{b})\rangle=n\sum_{m=0}^{n-1}\epsilon_{m}^{2}.

(17)

Since
|\epsilon_{m}|\leq 1/2
, we have
\epsilon_{m}^{2}\leq 1/4
for all
m
. This futher implies that

\displaystyle\|\sigma(t-c)\|_{2}^{2}\leq\frac{n^{2}}{4}.

(18)

It yields the desired bound
\|\sigma(t-c)\|_{2}\leq n/2
.
∎

Proposition 4.1
.

The
L^{\infty}
-covering radius of the embedded ring
\sigma(R)
satisfies

\displaystyle\frac{\sqrt{n}}{2}\leq\mu_{\infty}(\sigma(R))\leq O(\sqrt{n\log n}).

(19)

In particular,
\tfrac{\sqrt{n}}{2}\leq\mu_{\infty}(\sigma(R))\leq O(\sqrt{n\log n})
, i.e.
\mu_{\infty}(\sigma(R))=\tilde{\Theta}(\sqrt{n})
.

Proof.

We first show the lower bound. Fix the target
t=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{m=0}^{n-1}\zeta^{m}
. For any
c=\sum c_{m}\zeta^{m}\in R
with
c_{m}\in\mathbb{Z}
, define
\epsilon_{m}=\frac{1}{2}-c_{m}
. Since
c_{m}\in\mathbb{Z}
, each
\epsilon_{m}
is a half-integer with
|\epsilon_{m}|\geq\frac{1}{2}
.

From Lemma
4.1
, we have

\displaystyle\|\sigma(t-c)\|_{2}^{2}=n\sum_{m=0}^{n-1}\epsilon_{m}^{2}\geq\frac{n^{2}}{4}.

(20)

Since
\|\sigma(t-c)\|_{2}^{2}=\sum_{\ell\text{ odd}}|\sigma_{\ell}(t-c)|^{2}
is a sum of
n
terms, we get

\displaystyle\|\sigma(t-c)\|_{\infty}^{2}\geq\frac{1}{n}\sum_{\ell}|\sigma_{\ell}(t-c)|^{2}\geq\frac{n}{4}.

(21)

So,
\|\sigma(t-c)\|_{\infty}\geq\sqrt{n}/2
for every
c\in R
, giving
\mu_{\infty}(\sigma(R))\geq\sqrt{n}/2
.

In what follow, we prove the upper bound. For
t=\sum_{m}x_{m}\zeta^{m}\in K\otimes_{\mathbb{Q}}\mathbb{R}
, let
x_{m}=\lfloor x_{m}\rceil+\delta_{m}
with
\delta_{m}\in[-\tfrac{1}{2},\tfrac{1}{2}]
, and
c_{m}=\lfloor x_{m}\rceil+s_{m}
, where
s_{m}\in\{-1,0,+1\}
is chosen independently with
\Pr(s_{m}=+1)=\tfrac{1+\delta_{m}}{2}
and
\Pr(s_{m}=-1)=\tfrac{1-\delta_{m}}{2},\quad\Pr(s_{m}=0)=0
when
\delta_{m}\neq 0
, and
s_{m}\in\{0,\pm 1\}
uniformly when
\delta_{m}=0
. Then
\mathbb{E}[c_{m}-x_{m}]=0
, so, the embedding-wise error is given by

\displaystyle\sigma_{\ell}(c-t)=\sum_{m=0}^{n-1}(c_{m}-x_{m})\zeta^{\ell m},

(22)

which is a sum of independent centered random variables, each bounded in magnitude by
1
.

Applying Hoeffding’s inequality to the real and imaginary parts of (
22
), we obtain

\displaystyle\Pr\bigl(|\sigma_{\ell}(c-t)|\geq u\bigr)\leq 4\exp(-\tfrac{u^{2}}{n}).

(23)

Taking a union bound over the
n
embeddings and setting

u=\sqrt{n\ln(8n)}
, the right-hand side is less than 1. So, there exists a realization with
\|\sigma(c-t)\|_{\infty}\leq\sqrt{n\ln(8n)}=O(\sqrt{n\log n})
. Since this holds for every
t
, we finally get
\mu_{\infty}(\sigma(R))=O(\sqrt{n\log n})
.
∎

5
Module Reduction via Diagonal CDPR

In this section we extend the CDPR attack from ideal lattices
(
d=1
) to module lattices (
d\geq 2
) over 2-power cyclotomic
rings. Our algorithm takes as input an
R
-basis satisfying a balance hypothesis (Definition
5.1
) on its
K
-Gram-Schmidt vectors, which is automatically satisfied for bases sampled from the MLWE distribution (Lemma
5.1
) and can be enforced on adversarial inputs by algebraic LLL preprocessing
[
25
,
26
]
.

Given a balanced input basis, the algorithm has three steps. First, we compute the
K
-linear Gram-Schmidt decomposition of the input basis. Second, we optionally size-reduce the basis. Third, we apply CDPR to each rank-1 submodule
M_{i}=M\cap K\tilde{\mathbf{b}}_{i}
independently and return the shortest of the
d
resulting candidates. The key observation is that returning the shortest of the
d
candidates.

5.1
Input Balance Hypothesis

Our module reduction algorithm takes as input an
R
-basis
\mathbf{b}_{1},\cdots,\mathbf{b}_{d}
of the free
R
-module
M
, computes its
K
-Gram-Schmidt vectors
\tilde{\mathbf{b}}_{1},\cdots,\tilde{\mathbf{b}}_{d}
, and applies CDPR to each rank-1 submodule
M_{i}=M\cap K\tilde{\mathbf{b}}_{i}
.

Definition 5.1

(Balanced basis)
.

An
R
-basis
\mathbf{b}_{1},\cdots,\mathbf{b}_{d}
of a free
R
-module
M\subseteq K^{d}
is
C
-balanced
(with
C\geq 1
) if its
K
-Gram-Schmidt vectors satisfy

\displaystyle\|\sigma(\tilde{\mathbf{b}}_{i})\|_{2}^{2}\leq C\cdot n\cdot|\Nm_{K/\mathbb{Q}}\langle\tilde{\mathbf{b}}_{i},\tilde{\mathbf{b}}_{i}\rangle_{K})|^{1/n},1\leq i\leq d.

(24)

Condition (
24
) asks that the trace is close to its AM-GM minimum.

Remark 5.1
.

For input bases whose coefficients are drawn i.i.d. from a
bounded distribution (the regime of MLWE), the conjugates
\sigma_{\ell}(B_{i})
are asymptotically i.i.d., and the ratio
\Tr(B_{i})/\bigl(n|\Nm(B_{i})|^{1/n}\bigr)
concentrates at a universal constant as
n\to\infty
. In particular, an
R
-basis sampled from the MLWE distribution is
C
-balanced with
C=O(1)
with probability
1-o(1)
. Numerical verification for
k\in\{6,\cdots,12\}
(Table
1
) confirms
C\leq 2
for all tested parameters.

Lemma 5.1
.

Let
\mathbf{b}_{1},\cdots,\mathbf{b}_{d}\in R^{d}
be chosen independently with coefficients drawn i.i.d. from a bounded real distribution with mean zero, variance
\sigma_{c}^{2}>0
, and finite fourth moment. Then for every
\epsilon>0
, the basis is
(1+\epsilon)
-balanced with probability
1-o_{n}(1)
.

Proof.

We use the fact that embedding vector

(\sigma_{\ell}(\tilde{b}_{i,k}))_{\ell,k}/\sqrt{n\sigma_{c}^{2}}
converges jointly to
d
independent
\mathcal{N}(0,1)
Gaussians for each
i
since the
K
-Gram-Schmidt vectors are obtained by
K
-linear operations on the input, which preserves the asymptotic Gaussianity of each embedding. Hence, we have
\sigma_{\ell}(\langle\tilde{\mathbf{b}}_{i},\tilde{\mathbf{b}}_{i}\rangle_{K})=\sum_{k}|\sigma_{\ell}(\tilde{b}_{i,k})|^{2}\xrightarrow{d}n\sigma_{c}^{2}\cdot\chi^{2}_{2d}/(2d)

in the sense that the
n
conjugates are asymptotically i.i.d. scaled
\chi^{2}_{2d}
variables.

By applying the strong law of large
numbers to
\log\sigma_{\ell}(B_{i})
and continuity, we can obtain

\displaystyle\frac{\Tr(B_{i})}{n\cdot|\Nm(B_{i})|^{1/n}}\xrightarrow{p}\frac{\mathbb{E}[\chi^{2}_{2d}/(2d)]}{\exp(\mathbb{E}[\log(\chi^{2}_{2d}/(2d))])}=\frac{1}{\exp(\psi(d)-\log d)}=O(1),

(25)

where we used
\mathbb{E}[\chi^{2}_{2d}]=2d
and
\mathbb{E}[\log\chi^{2}_{2d}]=\psi(d)+\log 2
(which will be proved in the followed Part III). The limit is bounded above by a constant for all
d\geq 1
(and equals
1
as
d\to\infty
), so
C=1+\epsilon
for any
\epsilon>0
with probability
1-o(1)
.
∎

5.2
R
-Linear Gram–Schmidt orthogonalization

Let
M
be a free
R
-module of rank
d
with basis
\mathbf{b}_{1},\cdots,\mathbf{b}_{d}\in K^{d}
. We perform a
K
-linear Gram-Schmidt orthogonalization using the Hermitian inner product on
K^{d}
as follows

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathbf{b}}_{j}=\mathbf{b}_{j}-\sum_{i=1}^{j-1}\mu_{ji}\tilde{\mathbf{b}}_{i},

(26)

where Gram-Schmidt coefficients
\mu_{ji}=\frac{\langle\mathbf{b}_{j},\tilde{\mathbf{b}}_{i}\rangle_{K}}{\langle\tilde{\mathbf{b}}_{i},\tilde{\mathbf{b}}_{i}\rangle_{K}}\in K
.

By construction, the Gram–Schmidt vectors are pairwise orthogonal under the
K
-inner product, i.e.,
\langle\tilde{\mathbf{b}}_{i},\tilde{\mathbf{b}}_{j}\rangle_{K}=0
for
i\neq j
. Since the basis
\{\mathbf{b}_{i}\}
is linearly independent over
K
, each
\tilde{\mathbf{b}}_{i}
is non-zero. Hence, we have
\langle\tilde{\mathbf{b}}_{i},\tilde{\mathbf{b}}_{i}\rangle_{K}\in K^{+}\setminus\{0\}
.

The module determinant as the determinant of the
K
-valued Gram matrix is defined by

\displaystyle{\rm det}_{R}(M)=\det(\langle\mathbf{b}_{i},\mathbf{b}_{j}\rangle_{K})_{1\leq i,j\leq d}\in K^{\times}/R^{\times}.

(27)

From the orthogonality of the Gram–Schmidt vectors, we have

\displaystyle{\rm det}_{R}(M)=\prod_{i=1}^{d}\langle\tilde{\mathbf{b}}_{i},\tilde{\mathbf{b}}_{i}\rangle_{K}.

(28)

Remark 5.2
.

The coordinate-wise Galois embedding
\sigma:K^{d}\to\mathbb{C}^{dn}
maps the
K
-valued inner product to the standard Hermitian inner product on
\mathbb{C}^{dn}
as

\displaystyle\langle\sigma(\mathbf{a}_{i}),\sigma(\mathbf{b}_{j})\rangle_{\mathbb{C}^{dn}}=\Tr_{K/\mathbb{Q}}(\langle\mathbf{a}_{i},\mathbf{b}_{j}\rangle_{K}).

(29)

In particular, if
\langle\mathbf{a}_{i},\mathbf{b}_{j}\rangle_{K}=0
then
\sigma(\mathbf{a}_{i})
and
\sigma(\mathbf{b}_{j})
are orthogonal in
\mathbb{C}^{dn}
. For scalar multiplication by
\alpha\in K
, we have the embedding-wise factorization

\displaystyle\|\sigma(\mathbf{a}_{i}\cdot\alpha)\|_{2}^{2}=\sum_{\ell}|\sigma_{\ell}(\alpha)|^{2}\sum_{k=1}^{d}|\sigma_{\ell}(a_{ik})|^{2},

(30)

which factorizes as
\|\sigma(\mathbf{a}_{i})\|_{2}^{2}\cdot\|\sigma(\alpha)\|_{2}^{2}/n
when
|\sigma_{\ell}(\alpha)|
is independent of
\ell
.

5.3
Optional size reduction

In this section we show the optional size reduction, which does not affect the approximation-factor bound of Theorem
5.1
, but improves numerical conditioning. For each
j
from
2
to
d
and each
i
from
j-1
down to
1
, we compute

\displaystyle c_{ji}=\Round(\mu_{ji})\in R,\qquad\mathbf{b}_{j}\leftarrow\mathbf{b}_{j}-c_{ji}\cdot\mathbf{b}_{i},

(31)

where
\Round:K\to R
is a rounding function whose error is controlled by coordinate-wise rounding (Lemma
4.2
) with
\rho=n/2
or CRT-scaled rounding (Section
6
) with
\rho\leq 1
. After rounding, the residual coefficient
\mu_{ji}^{\prime}=\mu_{ji}-c_{ji}
satisfies
\|\sigma(\mu_{ji}^{\prime})\|_{\infty}\leq\rho
. Size reduction preserves the Gram-Schmidt vectors
\tilde{\mathbf{b}}_{i}
and their norms.

5.4
Phase 2: Diagonal CDPR on rank-1 submodules

Each Gram-Schmidt vector
\tilde{\mathbf{b}}_{i}
spans a one-dimensional
K
-subspace
L_{i}:=K\cdot\tilde{\mathbf{b}}_{i}\subseteq K^{d}
. The intersection
M_{i}:=M\cap L_{i}
is a non-zero
R
-submodule of
M
of
R
-rank
1
. Under the
R
-linear isomorphism
\psi_{i}:L_{i}\xrightarrow{\sim}K
,
\alpha\tilde{\mathbf{b}}_{i}\mapsto\alpha
, the image
J_{i}:=\psi_{i}(M_{i})\subseteq K
is a non-zero fractional
R
-ideal,
and
M_{i}=J_{i}\cdot\tilde{\mathbf{b}}_{i}
.

Applying CDPR.
For
k\leq 8
, the ring
R
is a PID, so
J_{i}
is principal. For
k\geq 9
, the free
R
-modules arising in MLWE generically yield principal
J_{i}
. Here, the non-principal case is dealed with the class-group algorithm of Biasse-Song
[
27
]
at the same asymptotic cost. Now, pick any generator
\alpha_{i,0}\in J_{i}
and apply the CDPR short-generator algorithm
[
7
,
12
]
to the principal ideal
(\alpha_{i,0})\subseteq R
. This returns a unit
u_{i}\in R^{\times}
such that
\alpha_{i}^{\prime}:=u_{i}^{-1}\alpha_{i,0}
is a generator of
J_{i}
satisfying

\displaystyle\|\sigma(\alpha_{i}^{\prime})\|_{\infty}\leq\gamma_{\rm CDPR}\cdot|\Nm_{K/\mathbb{Q}}(J_{i})|^{1/n},

(32)

where
\gamma_{\rm CDPR}=\exp(C_{0}\sqrt{n}\log n)
for an absolute constant
C_{0}>0
.

The output vector.
Define the output vector as

\displaystyle\mathbf{v}_{i}:=\alpha_{i}^{\prime}\cdot\tilde{\mathbf{b}}_{i}\in M_{i}\subseteq M.

(33)

By construction
\mathbf{v}_{i}
is a non-zero element of
M
.

Per-line norm bound.
We use the inequality
\|\sigma(\mathbf{v}_{i})\|_{2}\leq\sqrt{dn}\|\sigma(\mathbf{v}_{i})\|_{\infty}
because
\sigma(\mathbf{v}_{i})\in\mathbb{C}^{dn}
has at most
dn
complex coordinates. Combining with Remark
5.2
and the CDPR bound, we obtain
(
32
),

\displaystyle\|\sigma(\mathbf{v}_{i})\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle=\max_{\ell,k}|\sigma_{\ell}(\alpha_{i}^{\prime})|\cdot|\sigma_{\ell}(\tilde{b}_{i,k})|

\displaystyle\leq\|\sigma(\alpha_{i}^{\prime})\|_{\infty}\cdot\|\sigma(\tilde{\mathbf{b}}_{i})\|_{\infty},

(34)

and therefore

\displaystyle\|\sigma(\mathbf{v}_{i})\|_{2}^{2}\leq dn\cdot\gamma_{\rm CDPR}^{2}\cdot|\Nm_{K/\mathbb{Q}}(J_{i})|^{2/n}\cdot\|\sigma(\tilde{\mathbf{b}}_{i})\|_{\infty}^{2}.

(35)

In what follows, we use the tighter factorization via the trace as

\displaystyle\|\sigma(\mathbf{v}_{i})\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell}|\sigma_{\ell}(\alpha_{i}^{\prime})|^{2}\cdot\sigma_{\ell}(\langle\tilde{\mathbf{b}}_{i},\tilde{\mathbf{b}}_{i}\rangle_{K})

\displaystyle\leq\|\sigma(\alpha_{i}^{\prime})\|_{\infty}^{2}\cdot\Tr_{K/\mathbb{Q}}(\langle\tilde{\mathbf{b}}_{i},\tilde{\mathbf{b}}_{i}\rangle_{K})

\displaystyle=\|\sigma(\alpha_{i}^{\prime})\|_{\infty}^{2}\cdot\|\sigma(\tilde{\mathbf{b}}_{i})\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\gamma_{\rm CDPR}^{2}\cdot|\Nm_{K/\mathbb{Q}}(J_{i})|^{2/n}\cdot\|\sigma(\tilde{\mathbf{b}}_{i})\|_{2}^{2}.

(36)

Under the
C
-balance hypothesis (
24
)
(Definition
5.1
), we obtain

\displaystyle\|\sigma(\tilde{\mathbf{b}}_{i})\|_{2}^{2}\leq C\cdot n\cdot|\Nm_{K/\mathbb{Q}}(\langle\tilde{\mathbf{b}}_{i},\tilde{\mathbf{b}}_{i}\rangle_{K})|^{1/n}.

(37)

From the inequalities (
37
) and (
36
), we then obtain

\displaystyle\|\sigma(\mathbf{v}_{i})\|_{2}^{2}\leq C\cdot n\cdot\gamma_{\rm CDPR}^{2}\cdot|\Nm_{K/\mathbb{Q}}(J_{i})|^{2/n}\cdot|\Nm_{K/\mathbb{Q}}(\langle\tilde{\mathbf{b}}_{i},\tilde{\mathbf{b}}_{i}\rangle_{K})|^{1/n}.

(38)

Covolume of
\sigma(M_{i})
and product identity.
The rank-1 sublattice
M_{i}=J_{i}\tilde{\mathbf{b}}_{i}
embeds to a rank-
n
lattice
\sigma(M_{i})\subset\sigma(L_{i})
with covolume

\displaystyle\det(\sigma(M_{i}))=|\Nm_{K/\mathbb{Q}}(J_{i})|\cdot n^{n/2}\cdot|\Nm_{K/\mathbb{Q}}(\langle\tilde{\mathbf{b}}_{i},\tilde{\mathbf{b}}_{i}\rangle_{K})|^{1/2},

(39)

which is followed from the same Vandermonde computation as Eq.(
5
). Taking the

-th power, we get

\displaystyle|\Nm_{K/\mathbb{Q}}(J_{i})|^{2/n}\cdot|\Nm_{K/\mathbb{Q}}(\langle\tilde{\mathbf{b}}_{i},\tilde{\mathbf{b}}_{i}\rangle_{K})|^{1/n}=n^{-1}\cdot\det(\sigma(M_{i}))^{2/n}.

(40)

Combining Eq.(
40
) and inequality (

\displaystyle\|\sigma(\mathbf{v}_{i})\|_{2}^{2}\leq C\cdot\gamma_{\rm CDPR}^{2}\cdot\det(\sigma(M_{i}))^{2/n}.

(41)

Since the
K
-lines
L_{i}
are pairwise orthogonal under the

-inner product (Remark

), the embedded sublattices
\sigma(M_{i})
are mutually orthogonal in
\mathbb{R}^{2dn}
, and
\sigma(M)=\bigoplus_{i=1}^{d}\sigma(M_{i})
as orthogonal direct sum. Therefore, we finally get

\displaystyle\prod_{i=1}^{d}\det(\sigma(M_{i}))=\det(\sigma(M)).

(42)

Here, the

submodules are applied independently since the

are pairwise orthogonal.

5.5
Base module reduction bound

We now state the main bound for the base algorithm.

Theorem 5.1
.

Let
M\subseteq K^{d}
be a free
R
-module of rank
d
given by a
C
-balanced
R
-basis
\mathbf{b}_{1},\cdots,\mathbf{b}_{d}
. The algorithm that (i) computes the
K
-Gram-Schmidt decomposition
\tilde{\mathbf{b}}_{1},\cdots,\tilde{\mathbf{b}}_{d}
, (ii) applies CDPR to each rank-1 submodule
M_{i}=M\cap(K\tilde{\mathbf{b}}_{i})
to obtain a shortened
generator
\alpha_{i}^{\prime}\in J_{i}
, and (iii) returns

\displaystyle\mathbf{v}=\arg\min_{1\leq i\leq d}\|\sigma(\mathbf{v}_{i})\|_{2},\quad\mathbf{v}_{i}:=\alpha_{i}^{\prime}\cdot\tilde{\mathbf{b}}_{i},

(43)

produces a non-zero vector
\mathbf{v}\in M
with

\displaystyle\|\sigma(\mathbf{v})\|_{2}\leq\sqrt{C}\cdot\gamma_{\rm CDPR}\cdot\det(\sigma(M))^{1/(dn)},

(44)

where
\gamma_{\rm CDPR}=\exp(C_{0}\sqrt{n}\log n)
is the CDPR factor.

Comparing with the Hermite upper bound
\lambda_{1}(\sigma(M))\leq\sqrt{dn}\cdot\det(\sigma(M))^{1/(dn)}
, the algorithm in Theorem
5.1
achieves Hermite factor at most
\sqrt{C/(dn)}\cdot\gamma_{\rm CDPR}=\exp(\tilde{O}(\sqrt{n}))
. Under the Gaussian heuristic
\lambda_{1}(\sigma(M))\approx\sqrt{dn/(2\pi e)}\cdot\det(\sigma(M))^{1/(dn)}
, the Module-SVP approximation factor is
\gamma=O(\sqrt{C})\cdot\exp(\tilde{O}(\sqrt{n}))
. For input bases sampled from the MLWE distribution,
C=O(1)
with probability
1-o(1)
(Lemma
5.1
), we obtain
\gamma=\exp(\tilde{O}(\sqrt{n}))
unconditionally.

Proof.

Set
A_{i}:=\|\sigma(\mathbf{v}_{i})\|_{2}^{2}
. By the inequality (
41
), we get

\displaystyle A_{i}\leq C\cdot\gamma_{\rm CDPR}^{2}\cdot\det(\sigma(M_{i}))^{2/n}.

(45)

Using the output in Eq.(
43
), we have
\|\sigma(\mathbf{v})\|_{2}^{2}=\min_{1\leq i\leq d}A_{i}
. From the power-mean inequality we get

\displaystyle\min_{1\leq i\leq d}A_{i}\leq\Bigl(\prod_{i=1}^{d}A_{i}\Bigr)^{1/d}.

(46)

Combining inequalities (
45
), (
46
), and the orthogonal direct-sum identity in Eq.(
42
), we obtain

\displaystyle\|\sigma(\mathbf{v})\|_{2}^{2}\leq C\cdot\gamma_{\rm CDPR}^{2}\cdot\Bigl(\prod_{i=1}^{d}\det(\sigma(M_{i}))\Bigr)^{2/(dn)}=C\cdot\gamma_{\rm CDPR}^{2}\cdot\det(\sigma(M))^{2/(dn)}.

(47)

Taking square roots yields the inequality (
44
). The Hermite factor and heuristic Module-SVP factor follow directly.
∎

The bound (
44
) requires the
C
-balance hypothesis (Definition
5.1
) on the input basis. This hypothesis holds for MLWE-sampled bases (Lemma
5.1
), and more generally for any basis where the Galois conjugates of the
\langle\tilde{\mathbf{b}}_{i},\tilde{\mathbf{b}}_{i}\rangle_{K}
are approximately equidistributed. An adversarial (worst-case) input basis may violate this hypothesis by arbitrary amounts; in that case, the basis can be preprocessed to satisfy balance via algebraic LLL
[
25
,
26
]
, which raises
C
by a factor of at most
2^{O(n)}
still subsumed into the
\tilde{O}(\sqrt{n})
exponent of
\gamma_{\rm CDPR}
for large
n
. Since our target regime is MLWE, we state the main theorem under the natural assumption
C=O(1)
.

For ML-KEM (
n=256
,
d\in\{2,3,4\}
) with the CBD input distribution, numerical experiments (Table
1
) yield
C\leq 1.8
across
10^{4}
trials. The resulting module reduction factor is
\alpha_{d}:=\sqrt{C}\leq 1.4
, independent of

6
CRT-Scaled Rounding

The base algorithm of Section
5
achieves under the
C
-balance hypothesis on the input basis, with no dependence on the Phase 1 size-reduction error
\rho
. Optional coordinate-wise size reduction (Section
5.3
) gives
\rho=n/2
, which requires
O(n)
-bit rational arithmetic to manipulate exactly. In this section we replace coordinate rounding by CRT-scaled rounding: we round in the finer lattice
P^{-1}R
at totally split primes, achieving
\rho\leq 1
and enabling a bounded-precision implementation in which every ring operation is an
n
-point NTT over
\mathbb{F}_{p_{s}}
. The approximation factor is unchanged; the payoff is in complexity and numerical stability.

6.1
Totally split primes and NTT

We first introduce the standard properties of totally split primes in 2-power cyclotomic rings, which enable efficient componentwise arithmetic via NTT.

Definition 6.1
.

An odd prime
p
is totally split in
R=\mathbb{Z}[x]/(x^{n}+1)
if the cyclotomic polynomial
x^{n}+1
splits into
n
distinct linear factors modulo
p
. This is equivalent to
p\equiv 1\pmod{2n}
.

For such a prime, we have the ideal factorization
pR=\mathfrak{p}_{1}\mathfrak{p}_{2}\cdots\mathfrak{p}_{n}
, where each prime ideal
\mathfrak{p}_{i}
has norm
p
. For a totally split prime
p
, the NTT gives an explicit ring isomorphism between
R/pR
and the componentwise product ring
\mathbb{F}_{p}^{n}
as

\displaystyle\NTT_{p}:\alpha(x)\mapsto(\alpha(\zeta_{p}^{1}),\alpha(\zeta_{p}^{3}),\dots,\alpha(\zeta_{p}^{2n-1})),

(48)

where
\zeta_{p}\in\mathbb{F}_{p}^{\times}
is a fixed primitive
2n
-th root of unity. The inverse transform is denoted as
\INTT_{p}
. Here, the multiplication in
R/pR
is mapped to componentwise multiplication in
\mathbb{F}_{p}^{n}
, which can be computed in
O(n\log n)
ring operations.

6.2
Scaled rounding in
P^{-1}R

We first prove a general bound for rounding in the scaled lattice
P^{-1}R
, which generalizes the coordinate-wise rounding bound from Lemma
4.2
.

Lemma 6.1
.

Let
P
be a positive integer coprime to
2
. For any
t\in K\otimes_{\mathbb{Q}}\mathbb{R}
, there is an element
c\in P^{-1}R
such that

\displaystyle\|\sigma(t-c)\|_{\infty}\leq\frac{n}{2P}.

(49)

Proof.

Since
\{1,\zeta,\zeta^{2},\dots,\zeta^{n-1}\}
is a
\mathbb{Q}
-basis of
K
, any element
t\in K\otimes_{\mathbb{Q}}\mathbb{R}
admits a unique real-coefficient expansion, i.e.,
t=\sum_{m=0}^{n-1}x_{m}\zeta^{m}
for
x_{m}\in\mathbb{R}
. For each
m
, let
c_{m}=\frac{\lfloor Px_{m}\rceil}{P}\in\frac{1}{P}\mathbb{Z}
, where
\lfloor\cdot\rceil
denotes rounding to the nearest integer.

Define the rounded element

\displaystyle c=\sum_{m=0}^{n-1}c_{m}\zeta^{m}\in P^{-1}R.

(50)

The rounding error for each coefficient is given by
\epsilon_{m}=x_{m}-c_{m}
, which satisfies
|\epsilon_{m}|\leq\frac{1}{2P}
by construction.

For any Galois embedding
\sigma_{\ell}:K\to\mathbb{C}
(corresponding to odd
\ell
modulo
2^{k}
), using Eq.(
50
) we have

\displaystyle|\sigma_{\ell}(t-c)|=\Big|\sum_{m=0}^{n-1}\epsilon_{m}\sigma_{\ell}(\zeta^{m})\Big|\leq\sum_{m=0}^{n-1}|\epsilon_{m}|\cdot|\sigma_{\ell}(\zeta^{m})|.

(51)

Since
\sigma_{\ell}(\zeta^{m})=\zeta^{\ell m}
is a root of unity, we have
|\sigma_{\ell}(\zeta^{m})|=1
for all
m,\ell
. Combinging the inequality (
51
) we get

\displaystyle|\sigma_{\ell}(t-c)|\leq n\cdot\frac{1}{2P}=\frac{n}{2P}.

(52)

This holds for all embeddings
\sigma_{\ell}
, hence we get
\|\sigma(t-c)\|_{\infty}\leq\frac{n}{2P}
.
∎

Corollary 6.1
.

If we choose
P\geq n/2
, then the rounding error satisfies
\|\sigma(t-c)\|_{\infty}\leq 1
, i.e.,
\rho\leq 1
for the size reduction step.

From Dirichlet’s Theorem on primes in arithmetic progressions, there exist infinitely many primes
p\equiv 1\pmod{2n}

[
38
]
. We choose a set of split primes
p_{1},p_{2},\dots,p_{r}
with product
P=\prod_{s=1}^{r}p_{s}\geq n/2
. For practical parameters (e.g., ML-KEM with
n=256
), the smallest such prime is
p=12289
, which is larger than
n/2=128
, so a single prime suffices.

7
MILP Sign Optimization and the
O(1)
Discrepancy

In this section, we achieve a further tightening of the CDPR approximation factor for the specific sign-selection problem arising in the CDPR decode step. This problem has a special circulant structure derived from the log-sine geometry of cyclotomic units, which we exploit via mixed-integer linear programming (MILP).

7.1
MILP formulation of the sign-selection problem

The balanced sign-selection problem from Section
3
is defined:

\displaystyle\delta^{*}(k)=\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}s\in\{\pm 1\}^{N_{s}},\sum_{j=1}^{N_{s}}s_{j}\in\{\pm 1\}\end{subarray}}\|\mathbf{M}\mathbf{s}\|_{\infty},

(55)

where
N_{s}=|G|-1
is the number of sign variables,
\mathbf{M}\in\mathbb{R}^{|G|\times N_{s}}
is the error matrix derived from the log-sine vector (
8
), and the balance constraint enforces a near-equal number of
+1
and
-1
signs. Note that
N_{s}=2^{k-2}-1
is odd, so the sum of
N_{s}
odd terms
\pm 1
is necessarily odd, hence
\sum_{j}s_{j}\in\{\pm 1\}
exhausts the integer-feasible options and coincides with the balance constraint of Definition
3.1
.

Since the objective
\|\mathbf{M}\mathbf{s}\|_{\infty}
and constraints are linear in
\mathbf{s}
, we reformulate the problem as a standard mixed-integer linear program (MILP) by linearizing the absolute value. We use the substitution
\mathbf{s}=2\mathbf{x}-1
, which maps
s_{j}\in\{\pm 1\}
to binary variables
x_{j}\in\{0,1\}
. The balanced sign-selection problem is then equivalent to the following optimization

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \min_{\mathbf{x}\in\{0,1\}^{N_{s}},t\geq 0}\quad&t\\
\text{s.t.}\quad&\mathbf{M}(2\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{1})\leq t\mathbf{1},\\
&-\mathbf{M}(2\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{1})\leq t\mathbf{1},\\
&\sum_{j=1}^{N_{s}}x_{j}\in\{\lfloor\tfrac{N_{s}}{2}\rfloor,\lceil\tfrac{N_{s}}{2}\rceil\},\end{aligned}

(56)

where the first two constraints enforce
-t\mathbf{1}\leq\mathbf{M}\mathbf{s}\leq t\mathbf{1}
, which is equivalent to
\|\mathbf{M}\mathbf{s}\|_{\infty}\leq t
. The final constraint corresponds to the balance condition
\sum_{j}s_{j}\in\{\pm 1\}
, since
\sum_{j}s_{j}=2\sum_{j}x_{j}-N_{s}
.

Remark 7.1
.

To avoid confusion across sections, here
n=2^{k-1}
is the degree of the
2^{k}
-th cyclotomic field
K/\mathbb{Q}
, i.e., the ring degree;
|G|=2^{k-2}
is the order of the orbit group
G=(\mathbb{Z}/2^{k}\mathbb{Z})^{\times}/\{\pm 1\}
;
N_{s}=|G|-1
is the number of sign variables in the MILP. For example, for
k=10
:
n=512
,
|G|=256
,
N_{s}=255
; for ML-KEM parameters (
k=9
):
n=256
,
|G|=128
,
N_{s}=127
.

The MILP (
56
) has
N_{s}
binary variables, 1 continuous variable
t
,
2|G|+1
linear constraints, and a constraint matrix of size
(2|G|+1)\times(N_{s}+1)
. This is a standard 0-1 MILP with a minimax objective, which can be solved using branch-and-bound algorithms.

Proposition 7.1
.

The formulation (
56
) is a standard mixed-integer linear program
[
41
,
42
]
. Its linear programming (LP) obtained by replacing
\mathbf{x}\in\{0,1\}^{N_{s}}
with
\mathbf{x}\in[0,1]^{N_{s}}
is solvable in polynomial time via interior-point methods
[
43
]
and provides a certified lower bound on the optimal discrepancy
\delta^{*}(k)
.

Proof.

The objective and all constraints are linear in the decision variables
(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{t})
, with integrality constraints only on
\mathbf{x}\in\{0,1\}^{N_{s}}
, a standard MILP form
[
41
]
. From the LP relaxation with box constraints
0\leq x_{j}\leq 1
, it can be solved in
O(N_{s}^{3})
operations via interior-point methods
[
43
]
. The optimal value of the LP relaxation is a lower bound on
\delta^{*}(k)
.
∎

7.3
Generators of

For practical implementation of our module reduction algorithm, we characterize the generators of the general linear group

, which ensures that our size reduction step can be implemented using only elementary ring operations.

Definition 7.1
.

Theorem 7.2
.

For

and

Proof.

We first consider the case

. For

For any

. The unit group of

is

, where

is the cyclotomic unit group
[

.

Since

. By the Bass–Milnor–Serre theorem
[

, where

is the subgroup generated by elementary transvections in

.

For

is norm-Euclidean only for

[

[

. Hence

For
9\leq k\leq 12

(since the relative class number

), so

∎

.

8
Conclusions

We extended the CDPR quantum attack from ideal to module lattices over
2^{k}
-th cyclotomic rings. Our base algorithm (Theorem
5.1
) achieves the bound
\|\sigma(\mathbf{v})\|_{2}\leq\sqrt{C}\cdot\gamma_{\rm CDPR}\cdot\det(\sigma(M))^{1/(dn)}
under a
C
-balance hypothesis on the input basis, equivalent to Hermite factor
\exp(\tilde{O}(\sqrt{n}))
and matching CDPR for
d=1
. The balance hypothesis holds for bases sampled from the MLWE distribution with
C=O(1)
(Lemma
5.1
), and can be enforced on worst-case inputs by algebraic LLL preprocessing. The key algorithmic idea is to apply CDPR independently to each of the
d
rank-1 submodules
M_{i}=M\cap K\tilde{\mathbf{b}}_{i}
and return the shortest of the resulting candidates; the power-mean inequality converts the per-line bound into a geometric-mean quantity matching the module determinant.

CRT-scaled rounding (Theorem
6.1
) preserves the approximation bound while enabling an
O(d^{2}rn\log n)
-time bounded-precision implementation via NTT. Finally, MILP-optimal sign selection (Theorem
7.1
and Corollary
7.1
) replaces the tower-greedy CDPR discrepancy
\Theta(\sqrt{nk})
by a universal constant
\delta^{*}\approx 0.4407
, a
\sqrt{nk}
-factor tightening of the implicit constant inside
\gamma_{\rm CDPR}
, though not of the exponential
\exp(\tilde{O}(\sqrt{n}))
itself.

Several open problems remain. First, prove
\delta^{*}(k)=O(1)
for all
k
; our computational evidence covers
k\leq 10
, and a proof would likely exploit hierarchical cancellation in the log‑sine function across the cyclotomic tower. Second, identify
\delta^{*}
analytically—the value
0.44069\cdots
does not match obvious known constants, and a closed‑form expression would illuminate the connection to cyclotomic geometry. Third, improve the exponential factor: the
\exp(\tilde{O}(\sqrt{n}))
factor from Phase 2 is the sole remaining bottleneck, and a better CVP algorithm for the log-unit lattice would be a major breakthrough.
