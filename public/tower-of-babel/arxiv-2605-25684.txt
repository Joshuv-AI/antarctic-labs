Transference of ergodic properties of operators via matrix actions
Daniel Santacreu, Pablo Sevilla-Peris

Abstract
For operators defined on locally convex spaces we define the notions of boundedness and ergodicity associated to an infinite matrix. Given two matrices A A and B B , we study when A A -bounded operators are B B -ergodic. Using this approach we obtain equivalent formulations for the classical notions of power boundedness, Cesàro boundedness and mean ergodicity.

1
Introduction

The ergodic theory of operators deals with the problem of the behaviour of the iteration of the composition of an operator with itself. More precisely, if
E
is a Banach space (or, more generally, a locally convex Hausdorff space) and
T:E\to E
is a (continuous, linear) operator, we consider
T^{0}=\id
, and
T^{n}=T^{n-1}\circ T
for
n\geq 1
. Then, the general question is to analyse the behaviour of
(T^{n})_{n}
as
n
grows, and to find conditions that ensure that the sequence converges in some sense. To this purpose, the Cesàro means of the operator are considered, defined as

T_{[n]}=\frac{1}{n+1}\sum_{k=0}^{n}T^{k}\,.

and say that the operator is
mean ergodic
if the sequence
(T_{[n]})_{n}
converges pointwise to some operator on
E
. Also, the operator is
power bounded
if
\{\|T^{n}\|\}_{n}
is bounded (in the Banach case, if
\{T^{n}\}_{n}
is equicontinuous in the locally convex case). The classical theorems of Dunford-Schwartz
[
8
, Chapter 8]
or Yosida
[
16
]
give conditions so that a power bounded operator is mean ergodic. This seek of connections between ‘boundedness’ and ‘convergence’ properties is a constant along the theory, and there is a number of significant results in this aspect.

Finding alternative notions of convergence of sequences or series (such as Cesàro or Abel convergence) like, in a rather elementary level, for Fourier series, is by now a standard strategy. Toeplitz
[
15
]
was the first one to study systematically these methods of summation, using infinite matrices. This point of view is now somewhat classical (see
[
6
,
9
]
). To our best knowledge, this approach through infinite matrices has not been considered to study ergodic properties of operators. In this note we propose this point of view, defining ‘boundedness’ and ‘convergence’ properties induced by infinite matrices, and finding conditions that allow to relate them, in the spirit of the classical results relating power and Cesàro boundedness with mean ergodicity.

2
Matrix boundedness and ergodicity

We fix now our general framework and establish some basic notation. In this note
A
will always denote an infinite lower triangular matrix That is,
A=(a_{nk})_{n,k=0}^{\infty}
where
a_{nk}\in\mathbb{C}
, with
a_{nk}=0
whenever
i>j
. We say that such a matrix is a
probability matrix
if, moreover, all elements are non-negative and the sum of each row is
1
. That is

1.

a_{nk}\geq 0
for every
n,k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
.

2.

\displaystyle\sum_{i=0}^{n}a_{ni}=1
for each
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
.

Also,
\mathcal{T}
will always denote a sequence sequence of continuous linear operators on some locally convex Hausdorff space
E
. That is,
\mathcal{T}=\{T_{n}\colon n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}\}
, where
T_{n}:E\to E
is linear and continuous for every
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
.
We take a lower triangular matrix
A
, and a sequence
\mathcal{T}
as above and, for each
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
we consider

(A\mathcal{T})_{n}=\sum_{i=0}^{n}a_{ni}T_{i}\,.

(1)

and define the
A
-expected value
of
\mathcal{T}
as

A\mathcal{T}=\{(A\mathcal{T})_{n}\colon n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}\}

With this notation, we say that
\mathcal{T}
is

•

A
-bounded if
A\mathcal{T}
is equicontinuous, that is, for every continuous seminorm
p
there exists a continuous seminorm
q
so that

p\Big(\sum_{i=0}^{n}a_{ni}T_{i}x\Big)\leq q(x)

(2)

for every
x\in E
and all
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
.

•

absolutely
A
-bounded if for every continuous seminorm
p
there exists a continuous seminorm
q
so that

\sum_{i=0}^{n}p\big(a_{ni}T_{i}x\big)\leq q(x)

for every
x\in E
and all
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
.

•

A
-ergodic if there is some operator
P
such that
(A\mathcal{T})_{n}x
converges to
Px
for every
x\in E
.

•

uniformly
A
-ergodic if
A\mathcal{T}
converges uniformly on bounded sets to some operator.

•

A
-null if
(A\mathcal{T})_{n}x\to 0
for every
x\in E
.

Observe that if
\mathcal{T}
is
A
-null then it is
A
-ergodic. Also if
E
is barrelled, then every
A
-ergodic
\mathcal{T}
is
A
-bounded. Let us also point out that, if
E
is a Banach space, then
\mathcal{T}
is
A
-bounded if and only if the sequence
\big(\|(A\mathcal{T})_{n}\|\big)_{n}
is bounded.

Remark 2.1
.

Given a single operator
T
, we consider the sequence of its powers,
(T^{n})_{n}
, and we say that the operator
T
is
A
-bounded,
A
-ergodic or
A
-null if the sequence
(T^{n})_{n}
is so. In this case we will write
(AT)_{n}
in (
1
).

Let us rewrite some classical ergodic properties which, in some sense are the starting point of our research, in these matrix terms.

Remark 2.2
.

Let
I=(\delta_{nk})_{n,k=0}^{\infty}
be the identity matrix. Then, being
I
-bounded is exactly being power bounded. Also, an operator
T
is
I
-ergodic if and only if
(T^{n})_{n}
is pointwise convergent, and uniformly
I
-ergodic if and only if
(T^{n})_{n}
converges uniformly on bounded sets.

Remark 2.3
.

Let us recall (see e.g.
[
5
]
), that the Cesàro mean can also be formulated in terms of matrices. This of course applies also in our setting, and (absolute) Cesàro boundedness and (uniform) mean ergodicity of operators can be described with matrices. To do so, just consider the matrix
M=(m_{nk})_{n,k=0}^{\infty}
with
m_{nk}=\frac{1}{n+1}
when
n\leq k
and
0
elsewhere, that is

M=\begin{pmatrix}1&0&0&0&\ldots\\
\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}&\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}&0&0&\ldots\\
\nicefrac{{1}}{{3}}&\nicefrac{{1}}{{3}}&\nicefrac{{1}}{{3}}&0&\ldots\\
\nicefrac{{1}}{{4}}&\nicefrac{{1}}{{4}}&\nicefrac{{1}}{{4}}&\nicefrac{{1}}{{4}}&\ldots\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots\end{pmatrix}

(3)

This matrix does the job, and being (absolutely) Cesàro bounded and being (absolutely)
M
-bounded are equivalent, and (uniform) mean ergodicity is equivalent to (uniform)
M
-ergodicity.

3
Pairs of matrices

Our first aim is to see under which conditions these properties transfer from one matrix to another. To be more specific, given two lower triangular matrices
A
and
B
, we are interested in seeing what sort of relationship between them gives that every
A
-bounded/ergodic/null sequence is
B
-bounded/ergodic/null. The general idea here is, given two matrices
A
and
B
, try to find some matrix
C
with
CA=B
, and give conditions on
C
so that
A
-properties transfer to
B
-properties.

Remark 3.1
.

Let us in first place note that
A\mathcal{T}=\{(A\mathcal{T})_{n}\colon n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}\}
is again a sequence of continuous linear operators. Then, if
C
is another lower triangular matrix, straightforward computations show that
\big(C(A\mathcal{T})\big)_{n}=\big((CA)\mathcal{T})\big)_{n}
for every
n
, where
CA
is the usual matrix product. Observe also that, if both
A
and
C
are probability matrices, then so also is
CA
.

Suppose that
A
and
B
are two lower triangular matrices for which there is some lower triangular matrix
C=(c_{ij})
such that
CA=B
. Then we say that the pair
(A,B)
satisfies

•

property (
\ast
C) if the sums of the rows of
C
are bounded, that is

K_{C}=\sup_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}|c_{ni}|<\infty

(4)

•

property (
\ast
2C) if it satisfies (
\ast
C) and, moreover, the columns of
C
tend to
0
, that is

\lim_{n}c_{ni}=0\,\text{ for each fixed }i\in\mathbb{N}_{0}

(5)

•

property (
\ast
3C) if the sums of the rows of
C
tend to 0, that is

\lim_{n}\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}|c_{ni}|=0\,.

(6)

These relations between matrices allow to transfer the properties of sequences of operators from one another

Proposition 3.2
.

Let
(A,B)

(
\ast

\ast

We fix a matrix

, a sequence of continuous linear operators

, and

a seminorm on
E
. For
1
we assume that
\mathcal{T}
is
A

satisfies (
4

p\big((B\mathcal{T})_{n}x\big)=p\big((C(A\mathcal{T}))_{n}x\big)\\
=p\Big(\sum_{j=1}^{n}c_{nj}\sum_{i=1}^{j}a_{ij}T_{j}x\Big)\leq\sum_{j=1}^{n}|c_{nj}|\,\,p\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{j}a_{ij}T_{j}x\Big)\leq K_{C}q(x)\,,

for every

To prove

we assume that
\mathcal{T}
is
A
-null, and

satisfies (

) and (

so that

p\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{ni}T_{i}x\Big)<\frac{\varepsilon}{2K_{C}}

for every

. Taking into account that
a_{ji}=0
whenever

, we have

(B\mathcal{T})_{n}x=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}c_{nj}a_{ji}T_{i}x=\sum_{j=1}^{n}c_{nj}\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{ji}T_{i}x=\sum_{j=1}^{n}c_{nj}\sum_{i=1}^{j}a_{ji}T_{i}x\,.

If

then

p\big((B\mathcal{T})_{n}x\big)\leq p\Big(\sum_{j=1}^{N}c_{nj}\sum_{i=1}^{j}a_{ji}T_{i}x\Big)+p\Big(\sum_{j=N+1}^{n}c_{nj}\sum_{i=1}^{j}a_{ji}T_{i}x\Big)\\
\leq\max_{1\leq j\leq N}p\big((A\mathcal{T})_{j}x\big)\sum_{j=1}^{N}|c_{nj}|+\frac{\varepsilon}{2K_{C}}\sum_{j=N+1}^{n}|c_{nj}|\leq K_{2}\sum_{j=1}^{N}|c_{nj}|+\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\,.

Now, for each
j=1,\ldots,N

5
), choose

so that

. Taking
n_{0}=\max\{n_{1},\ldots,n_{N}\}
we have

p\big((B\mathcal{T})_{n}x\big)<\varepsilon\,,

for every
n\geq n_{0}
. This gives the claim.

Finally, to prove that
3

\mathcal{T}
is
A
-bounded, and take

so that

p\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{ni}T_{i}x\Big)\leq q(x)

for every

and all

. Therefore by (
\ast
3C) we have

\lim_{n}p\big((B\mathcal{T})_{n}x\big)=\lim_{n}p\Big(\sum_{j=1}^{n}c_{nj}\sum_{i=1}^{j}a_{ji}T_{i}x\Big)\\
\leq\lim_{n}\Big[\sum_{j=1}^{n}|c_{nj}|\,p\big(\sum_{i=1}^{j}a_{ji}T_{i}x\big)\Big]\leq\,q(x)\lim_{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}|c_{nj}|=0\,,

4
Operators. Eberlein’s theorem and consequences

We focus now on the behaviour of operators (recall Remark
2.1
), trying to find conditions that yield that an
A
-bounded operator is
A
-ergodic, following the guideline of classical results relating power boundedness and mean ergodicity. From now on we will always assume that

is a probability matrix. Our first step is to get an analogue of Eberlein’s mean ergodic theorem (see
[

[

\frac{1}{n}T^{n}x_{0}\to 0
for some

12

(X,\tau)
is a Hausdorff topological space, then a point

contains infinitely many points of

. If

is compact, then every sequence in

has a cluster point.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let

be a probability matrix, and
T
an
A
-bounded operator. Suppose

satisfies

Then, for each fixed

and

belongs to the closed convex hull of the set

y=\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}(AT)_{n}x_{0}
,

3.

is a
\sigma(E,E^{\prime})
-cluster point of
\big\{(AT)_{n}x_{0}\colon n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}\big\}

Proof.

The implications

. Fix a continuous seminorm

Then, for

We deal with the first term using (

To complete the proof let us see that

in such a way that
A

B

4.10
gives conditions on a pair to satisfy this property, namely

•

\lim_{n}b_{n0}=0
,

•

(A,B)
satisfies (

C) and

•

(A,B\Delta)
satisfies (
\ast

Since, as we already noted, our starting point is power and Cesàro boundedness, and mean ergodicity, we are mostly interested in linking the matrices defining these properties (
\id
and
M_{0}
, recall (

) and (
3
)) with other matrices. It is a well known fact (see e.g.
[
4

) that the pair
(\id,M_{0})
satisfies the (BE)-property. This can also be now easily deduced from the fact that
M_{0}\Delta=\left(\frac{1}{n}\delta_{n,i}\right)_{1\leq i\leq n}
and then,

Proposition 6.1
.

Every Cesàro bounded operator on a reflexive space is
M_{-1}
-ergodic.

Proof.

Taking (
18
) and Proposition
5.6
into account, we just have to see that the pair
(M_{0},M_{-1}\Delta)
satisfies (
\ast
3C) ​. To do that note first that, if we denote
M_{-1}\Delta=(d_{ni})
, we have

d_{ni}=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{iS(n,-1)}-\frac{1}{(i+1)S(n,-1)}&\text{ if }i<n\\
\frac{1}{nS(n,-1)}&\text{ if }i=n\\
0&\text{ if }i>n\\
\end{cases}

Then, writing
M_{0}^{-1}=(m_{ni})
, the product
M_{-1}\Delta M_{0}^{-1}=(c_{ni})
is given by

c_{ni}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}d_{nj}m_{ji}=\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}\frac{1}{S(n,-1)}\left(\frac{1}{j}-\frac{1}{j+1}\right)m_{ji}+\frac{m_{ni}}{nS(n,-1)}\\
=\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}\frac{1}{S(n,-1)}\frac{1}{j(j+1)}m_{ji}+\frac{m_{ni}}{nS(n,-1)}\,.

Recall now that
m_{ni}=n\delta_{n,i}-(n-1)\delta_{n-1,i}
and let us consider different cases

•

if
i\leq n-2
then

c_{ni}=\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}\frac{1}{S(n,-1)}\frac{1}{j(j+1)}m_{ji}=\frac{1}{S(n,-1)}\left(\frac{i}{i(i+1)}-\frac{i}{(i+1)(i+2)}\right)\\
=\frac{i}{(i+1)S(n,-1)}\left(\frac{1}{i}-\frac{1}{i+2}\right)=\frac{2}{(i+1)(i+2)S(n,-1)}\,,

•

if
i=n-1
, then

c_{n(n-1)}=\frac{1}{S(n,-1)}\frac{n-1}{(n-1)n}-\frac{n-1}{nS(n,-1)}=\frac{-n+2}{nS(n,-1)}\,,

•

if
i=n
, then
c_{nn}=\frac{n}{nS(n,-1)}=\frac{1}{S(n,-1)}
, and

•

c_{ni}=0
for
i>n
.

Taking all this into account we finally have

\lim_{n\to\infty}\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}|c_{ni}|=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{S(n,-1)}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n-2}\frac{2}{(i+1)(i+2)}+\frac{n-2}{n}+1\right)\\
\leq\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{S(n,-1)}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n-2}\frac{2}{i^{2}}+2\right)\leq\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{S(n,-1)}\left(2+2\frac{\pi^{2}}{6}\right)=0\,,

which gives the conclusion.
∎

We have, then, that the pairs
(\id,M_{0})
and
(M_{0},M_{-1})
have the (BE)-property. We are now interested in finding a probability matrix (say
M_{g}
) so that both
(\id,M_{g})
and
(M_{g},M_{0})
have the (BE)-property, that is, in reflexive spaces every power bounded operator is
M_{g}
-ergodic, and every
M_{g}
-bounded is mean ergodic. To construct such a matrix we consider a continuous strictly positive function
f:[1,+\infty[\to\mathbb{R}^{+}
. Then we define
S(n,f):=\sum_{i=1}^{n}f(i)
, which is increasing in
n
, and the matrix

M_{f}:=\begin{pmatrix}1&0&0&0&\ldots\\
\frac{f(1)}{S(2,f)}&\frac{f(2)}{S(2,f)}&0&0&\ldots\\
\frac{f(1)}{S(3,f)}&\frac{f(2)}{S(3,f)}&\frac{f(3)}{S(3,f)}&0&\ldots\\
\frac{f(1)}{S(4,f)}&\frac{f(2)}{S(4,f)}&\frac{f(3)}{S(4,f)}&\frac{f(4)}{S(4,f)}&\ldots\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots\end{pmatrix}

(21)

This is a probability matrix, and a simple computation shows that its inverse is given by

M_{f}^{-1}=\begin{pmatrix}\frac{S(1,f)}{f(1)}&0&0&0&\ldots\\
-\frac{S(1,f)}{f(2)}&\frac{S(2,f)}{f(2)}&0&0&\ldots\\
0&-\frac{S(2,f)}{f(3)}&\frac{S(3,f)}{f(3)}&0&\ldots\\
0&0&-\frac{S(3,f)}{f(4)}&\frac{S(4,f)}{f(4)}&\ldots\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots\end{pmatrix}

(22)

If the pair
(M_{f},M_{0}\Delta)
satisfies (
\ast
3C) (this is one of the conditions giving that the pair
(M_{f},M_{0})
satisfies the (BE)-property), then the sums of the rows (in absolute value) of the matrix
M_{0}\Delta M_{f}^{-1}
have to tend to
0
(recall (
6
)), that is we need

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n-1}\frac{S(n-1,f)}{f(n)}+\frac{1}{n}\frac{S(n,f)}{f(n)}=0\,.

(23)

A way to achieve this is by adjusting the function
f
in such a way that the element
\frac{S(n,f)}{f(n)}
grows as
\sqrt{n}
(other rates of growth can be considered). This is satisfied by the solutions of the integral equation

\int_{1}^{y}f(x)\dx=\sqrt{y}f(y)+K\,,

which are of the form
f(x)=C\frac{e^{2\sqrt{x}}}{\sqrt{x}}
.
These are natural candidates to be the function defining our matrix.

Remark 6.2
.

Take the function
f(x)=\frac{e^{2\sqrt{x}}}{\sqrt{x}}
, consider the matrix
M_{f}
defined as in (
21
) and let us see that both
(\id,M_{f})
and
(M_{f},M_{0})
have the (BE)-property.

To handle the pair
(\id,M_{f})
we note first that
\lim_{n}\frac{f(1)}{S(n,f)}=0
, and Condition (
\ast
C) is automatic since
M_{f}
is a probability matrix. It is left to see that
(\id,M_{f}\Delta)
satisfies (
\ast
3C) ​. Since
f
is increasing we have that in this case (
6
) is satisfied if and only if

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{f(n)}{S(n,f)}=0\,.

Observe that, for
n>1
, the following inequalities hold

S(n-1,f)\leq\int_{1}^{n}f(x)\dx\leq S(n,f)\,,

(24)

therefore

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{f(n)}{S(n,f))}\leq\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{f(n)}{\int_{1}^{n}f(x)\dx}=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{e^{2\sqrt{n}}}{\sqrt{n}(e^{2\sqrt{n}}-e^{2})}=0\,,

and
(\id,M_{f})
has the (BE)-property.

To see that also de the pair
(M_{f},M_{0})
has the property we have to see first that it satisfies (
\ast
C) ​. We consider the matrix
M_{0}M_{f}^{-1}=C=(c_{in})_{in}
, and note that
c_{ni}=0
for
i>n
. For the remaining cases we recall (
22
) and see that

c_{nn}=\frac{S(n,f)}{nf(n)}\,,

and

c_{ni}=\frac{S(i,f)}{nf(i)}-\frac{S(i,f)}{nf(i+1)}\,,

for
i\leq n-1
. Taking all these into account and the fact that
f
is increasing, we have, for each fixed
n\in\mathbb{N}

\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}|c_{ni}|=\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{ni}=\frac{S(n,f)}{nf(n)}+\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\bigg(\frac{S(i,f)}{nf(i)}-\frac{S(i,f)}{nf(i+1)}\bigg)\\
=\frac{S(n,f)}{nf(n)}+\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\frac{S(i,f)}{nf(i)}-\sum_{i=2}^{n}\frac{S(i-1,f)}{nf(i)}\\
=\frac{1}{n}\bigg(\frac{S(1,f)}{f(1)}+\sum_{i=2}^{n}\frac{f(i)}{f(i)}\bigg)=1\,,

and the claim follows.

It remains to see that
(M_{f},M_{0}\Delta)
satisfies (
\ast
3C) ​. Observe that the limit in (
23
) holds for
f
using (
24
)

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n-1}\frac{S(n-1,f)}{f(n)}+\frac{1}{n}\frac{S(n,f)}{f(n)}\leq\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{2}{n-1}\frac{S(n,f)}{f(n)}\\
\leq\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{2}{n-1}\frac{\int_{1}^{n+1}f(x)\dx}{f(n)}=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{e^{2\sqrt{n+1}}-e^{2}}{n-1}\frac{2\sqrt{n}}{e^{2\sqrt{n}}}\\
\leq\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{2\sqrt{n}}{n-1}e^{2(\sqrt{n+1}-\sqrt{n})}=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{2\sqrt{n}}{n-1}e^{\frac{2}{\sqrt{n+1}+\sqrt{n}}}=0\,.

This gives that
(M_{f},M_{0}\Delta)
satisfies (
\ast
3C) and, then
(M_{f},M_{0})
has the (BE)-property.
