At‐Home Versus in‐Clinic Vital Capacity Measurement: Insights From the HEALEY ALS Platform Trial
Authors: Eric A Macklin, James D Berry, Brittney A Harkey, Lindsay Heyd, Marianne Chase, Hong Yu, Alexander V Sherman, Derek Dagostino, Gale Kittle, Meghan Hall, Mariah R Connolly, Kristin Drake, Elisa Giacomelli, Erica Scirocco, Saloni Sharma, Suma Babu, Michael Benatar, Zachary Simmons, Eufrosina Young, Merit E Cudkowicz, Jeremy Shefner, Sabrina Paganoni, the HEALEY ALS Platform Trial Study Group
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroduction/AimsRespiratory weakness, typically monitored as vital capacity (VC), is a central feature of amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS). VC is increasingly measured remotely in participants' homes, although in‐clinic assessment remains the standard. We tested concordance between at‐home and in‐clinic VC to determine trial eligibility, track progression, and predict survival in a large ALS trial.MethodsAt‐home and in‐clinic VC were assessed at baseline and approximately every 8 weeks for a year in the first four regimens of the HEALEY ALS Platform Trial. At‐home assessments were coached via live videoconference and centrally reviewed. VC measurements, expressed as percent of predicted normal (%PN), were compared cross‐sectionally, longitudinally, and for predicting survival time. Data from 233 participants with 3–8 paired at‐home and in‐clinic VC assessments completed < 14 days apart were analyzed.ResultsAt‐home and in‐clinic VC were well correlated (Lin's r c = 0.82) with no systematic bias. At‐home VC ≥ 60%PN predicted in‐clinic VC ≥ 60%PN with a positive predictive value of 91% and a negative predictive value of 65%. VC slopes were moderately correlated (r c = 0.68). At‐home VC progressed 28% faster than in‐clinic VC with proportionately less variance (at‐home [SE] = −1.882 [0.153] %PN/month, in‐clinic = −1.476 [0.131] %PN/month). Slopes of at‐home and in‐clinic VC explained 15% and 17% of variation in future survival time, respectively.DiscussionAt‐home and in‐clinic VC were well correlated cross‐sectionally. At‐home VC performed well tracking longitudinal change and predicting survival. The reduced participant burden of assessment and concordance with in‐clinic measurement support use of at‐home monitoring of VC. Keywords: amyotrophic lateral sclerosis, remote patient monitoring, spirometry, validation studies as topic

Introduction

Respiratory weakness is a hallmark of amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS). Vital capacity (VC) is the primary measure of respiratory function used for monitoring ALS progression [1]. Baseline VC and change in VC predict survival in ALS [2, 3, 4]. In‐clinic respiratory function testing is the gold standard for measuring VC. Remote measurement of VC has been used since the 1990's and gained importance during the COVID‐19 pandemic. Prior work has reported good correlation between at‐home and in‐clinic VC among people living with ALS [5, 6]. Data on agreement between at‐home and in‐clinic assessments for monitoring change in VC or predicting survival are lacking. This study uses data from the HEALEY ALS Platform Trial to evaluate the accuracy and utility of at‐home VC as a proxy for in‐clinic measurements.

Methods

The HEALEY ALS Platform Trial [7, 8, 9] (NCT04297683) is a perpetual multi‐center, multi‐regimen clinical trial evaluating the safety and efficacy of investigational products for the treatment of ALS. Eligibility criteria included adults with revised El Escorial criteria of possible, laboratory‐supported probable, probable, or definite sporadic or familial ALS; onset of weakness < 36 months before screening; slow vital capacity (SVC) ≥ 50%‐predicted by GLI‐2012 [10]; no use or on a stable dose of riluzole, edaravone, or both; no unstable clinical or psychiatric conditions in investigator's opinion; and no recent exposure to other investigational treatments or prior gene therapy. Participants completed in‐clinic visits at baseline and 4, 8, 16, 24, 28, 32, 40, and 48 or 52 weeks. The master protocol and regimen‐specific appendices were approved by FDA and IRB, and participants provided written informed consent. This study utilizes data from the first four regimens [11, 12, 13, 14].

SVC was assessed at in‐clinic visits (excluding week 4 and visits during the pandemic when aerosol‐generating procedures were restricted) using the EasyOne Air spirometer (ndd Medical Technologies, Andover, MA). Forced vital capacity (FVC) was assessed remotely using the MIR Spirobank Smart spirometer (Medical International Research, Rome, Italy) with data collection and video interface provided by the ZEPHYRx smartphone app (ZEPHYRX LLC, Troy, NY). In each case, certified evaluators coached participants through 3 to 5 maneuvers. A minimum of 2 completed maneuvers was required for analysis (69 of 3996 assessments [1.7%] were excluded). For at‐home assessments, central readers at the Barrow Neurologic Institute Outcomes Center reviewed flow‐loops and adjudicated which assessments were acceptable (235 of 2460 coached assessments [9.6%] were excluded). ATS/ERS 2019 standards [15] were not referenced. The maximum expired volume from accepted assessments was converted to percent of predicted normal (%PN) using GLI‐2012 norms [10]. Pairs of acceptable at‐home and in‐clinic VC measurements were included for analysis if they were assessed fewer than 14 days apart. Participants with paired assessments for at least 3 of 8 planned study visits were included to support comparison of longitudinal changes in VC.

Date of death or date last known alive was recorded by sites at the conclusion of each participant's follow‐up as well as for all participants at the conclusion of the last participant's follow‐up and by a forensic service using publicly available data biannually after the last participant's follow‐up.

All paired at‐home and in‐clinic VC assessments were compared using Bland–Altman plots. Within‐person, cross‐sectional correlation between at‐home and in‐clinic VC was estimated as Lin's concordance correlation coefficient (rc) from a bivariate linear mixed model with unstructured participant‐specific random effects over assessment methods and unstructured participant‐specific repeated measures over visits [16]. Operating characteristics for using at‐home VC ≥ 60%PN as a proxy for in‐clinic VC ≥ 60%PN as a typical inclusion criterion were estimated using mixed model logistic regression with random participant‐specific intercepts on the log‐odds scale. Rates of change of at‐home and in‐clinic VC were estimated in separate linear mixed models with a fixed effect of time and unstructured participant‐specific random intercepts and slopes. Relative precision of slopes was calculated as the estimated slope divided by the slope standard error and multiplied by the square root of the sample size. Prognosis of survival beyond each participant's final follow‐up visit was estimated in separate Cox proportional hazards regressions with and without adjustment for baseline neurofilament light chain protein (NfL) concentration (log‐transformed) and TRICALS score [17]. Explained variation [18] was calculated for each model and as an increment versus a model with NfL and TRICALS only. Analyses were performed using SAS v9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC).

Results

Data were included from 233 participants enrolled from August 2020 to July 2022 out of 439 who completed at least one acceptable at‐home assessment. Others were excluded due to ≥ 14‐day gaps in assessment or < 3 paired assessments. Mean follow‐up was 316 days (SD 71, range 154 to 406 days). Vital status data were collected through January 2026. Participants were generally early in disease course (Table 1). Due to exclusion of participants who died or were lost to follow‐up prior to completing at least 3 paired assessments, the analysis cohort was enriched for slower progressors (Table S1).

Slopes of at‐home and in‐clinic VC were moderately correlated (Figure 1C). At‐home VC progressed 28% faster across the range of VC estimates (Figure 1D) with proportionately less variance, yielding a standardized slope 9% larger than in‐clinic VC (Table S4). Assuming equal proportional effect of an intervention on change in VC by either assessment method, equivalent follow‐up schedules and missed visits, and a comparable patient population to that studied in this analysis, trials using at‐home instead of in‐clinic VC assessments would require sample sizes 17% smaller.

Slopes of at‐home VC estimated over 48–52 weeks explained 90% of the variation explained by slopes of in‐clinic VC (Table 2). Both VC methods explained variation in future survival time beyond that explained by baseline NfL and TRICALS score to a similar degree.

Discussion

At least four features distinguished the at‐home and in‐clinic VC assessment methods used in this study: (1) the setting and ability of the evaluator to facilitate a good seal on the spirometer and to elicit maximal effort, (2) the use of forced versus slow maneuvers, (3) the use of different spirometers, and (4) the use or non‐use of central review for qualifying flow‐loops. Nevertheless, we observed strong cross‐sectional correlation between the two methods with almost no bias on average and limited difference in scale, consistent with previously published studies [5, 6]. Cross‐sectional correlation was strong. Nevertheless, there were wide limits of agreement, and at‐home VC underestimated in‐clinic VC at the low end of the scale. In our population, this underestimation could exclude roughly 35% of otherwise eligible participants from trials based on in‐clinic VC. However, NPV is dependent upon population characteristics and would improve in a population with more individuals with VC substantially below the required cutoff.

At‐home VC progressed 28% faster on average than in‐clinic VC over 48 to 52 weeks in our cohort, but slopes of the two measures were moderately correlated. Although progression rates of at‐home VC were faster, comparable explained variation in long‐term survival suggests that at‐home and in‐clinic VC trends reflected underlying disease progression with similar accuracy. Moreover, comparable precision estimating at‐home and in‐clinic VC slopes suggests that at‐home VC could be an equally powerful endpoint when tracked over a year.

This study had limitations. Our comparison of at‐home and in‐clinic VC was confounded by use of different maneuvers and devices. Prior work has shown good correlation between FVC and SVC in ALS patients cross‐sectionally during in‐clinic assessment [19, 20] and longitudinally during remote assessment [21], although systematically higher values were reported for SVC among patients with bulbar dysfunction [22]. We are not aware of studies comparing the EasyOne and MIR spirometers. Results reported here may not generalize to other ALS populations. The exclusion of participants with fewer than 3 paired at‐home and in‐clinic assessments yielded a sample with slower disease progression, better baseline functional status, and higher vital capacity than the overall HEALEY ALS Platform Trial. There may also be an unmeasured bias toward people who were able to successfully use the at‐home spirometer. Agreement between at‐home and in‐clinic VC would likely decline over shorter intervals where the effect of residual variation is proportionally greater.

The lower burden of at‐home VC assessments for participants and potential for participation of a more representative sample of people living with ALS are attractions. Quantifying the relative performance of at‐home versus in‐clinic VC permits informed decisions about the use of at‐home versus in‐clinic VC for measuring respiratory function at a point in time or for tracking changes in respiratory function to monitor disease progression. Our data, consistent with previous studies, suggest that at‐home VC, when performed with live video coaching and secondary central review and adjudication of all flow‐loops, provides a reasonable approximation of in‐clinic VC, both cross‐sectionally and longitudinally. The reduced burden of assessment for participants, the potential for more frequent assessment, the cross‐sectional and prognostic accuracy, and the longitudinal correlation and precision support the use of at‐home VC measurement both clinically and in ALS trials.
