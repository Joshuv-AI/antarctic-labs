Presentation and diagnosis of female genital and urinary tract malformations
Authors: Pedro Acién, Maribel Acién
Published: 2026

It is well known that the embryology of the genital and urinary tracts is related and, therefore, genital and urinary tract malformations are frequently associated. Clinicians must consider this fact when studying and investigating any anomaly affecting the reproductive system in either sex. For this reason, also, any classification system for female genital anomalies must take genitourinary embryology into account.

Indeed, in Acién et al. [1], we have reviewed the genitourinary embryology and updated the “Embryological-Clinical Classification for Female Genitourinary Malformations” to include in Groups 1 and 6 multiple mesodermal anomalies affecting the genitalia, kidney, and other organs. We have also included in Groups 3B and 3C other anomalies affecting the Müllerian tubercle or both the Müllerian tubercle and ducts. Furthermore, we have analyzed the mesonephric or Wolffian anomalies (MWAs) of Group 2 and also the isolated Müllerian anomalies (without associated mesonephric or extragenital anomalies) of Group 3. We emphasize that congenital absence of a kidney is the most frequently associated malformation of the renal tract in women with obstructive genital anomalies. And it should be remembered that cases with MWAs are always associated with unilateral renal agenesis (URA), although sometimes what is present is renal atresia or renal hypoplasia with an ectopic ureter leading to a blind hemivagina [2], with early and suggestive symptoms in a girl (permanent urinary incontinence between normal voidings). O'Flynn O'Brien et al. [3] and others have also pointed out the need to study the genital tract in patients diagnosed with any renal anomaly to avoid complications from an unrecognized Müllerian anomaly. Indeed, all genital and genitourinary anomalies can produce various clinical symptoms and impact the patient's quality of life, in addition to causing fertility problems. Furthermore, the reproductive outcomes of a given uterine malformation are not the same, whether that malformation is a consequence of a mesonephric anomaly or solely a Müllerian one [4]. Therefore, it is without surprise that Grimbizis et al. [5] have also recently noted that the surgical treatment for patients with complex female genital anomalies depends on the specific type of anomaly, its level of complexity, the symptoms experienced by the patient, and a thorough understanding of the embryological origins of the anomaly.

Therefore, the clinical presentation and appropriate treatment for female genital malformations depend on each specific anomaly, and it is not possible to define a general treatment for all of them. However, the appropriate diagnosis, classification, and categorization of all congenital genitourinary malformations are essential to ensure the best therapeutic approach. To achieve this, we emphasize the importance of obtaining a complete medical history that delves into the symptoms, their onset, age, and personal and family history, followed by a detailed general and gynecological clinical examination, including examination of the external genitalia, a combined vaginal and/or rectal examination, and transvaginal or transrectal ultrasound, also including abdominal and renal fossa ultrasound, always taking into account the sonographic parameters of the normal uterus, the suspicion of a genitourinary malformation, and knowledge of the related embryological aspects. 3D ultrasound, magnetic resonance imaging and/or computed axial tomography will help us to better define the diagnosis and to evaluate the need for and planning of vaginohysteroscopy, vagino/hysterosalpingography, hysterosonography, pyelography, urethrocystoscopy, and diagnostic and/or surgical laparoscopy [6].

Following diagnosis, surgery will sometimes be necessary, but in most patients with the most common malformations, it is generally not. In the remaining cases, it is also usually very simple (although it can be difficult and complicated in complex and combined malformations) [7]. Knowledge, correct diagnosis, and appropriate diagnostic-therapeutic planning (with the possible participation of multidisciplinary teams) are essential to avoid inappropriate and/or unnecessary interventions.

From our studies on female genital malformations analyzed following the embryological-clinical classification, we can summarize that: (1) common uterine or Müllerian anomalies present fewer clinical symptoms but cause most fertility problems, sometimes requiring laparoscopic or hysteroscopic management; (2) mesonephric anomalies and Müllerian anomalies affecting the Müllerian tubercle are usually obstructive, with prominent symptoms that are complex to manage (the same occurs with those of the gubernaculum); and (3) anomalies affecting the urogenital sinus and the cloaca, as well as malformative combinations, can be as simple as an imperforate hymen (although this requires proper diagnosis and urgent intervention), or very complex and therefore require multidisciplinary management, not only for diagnosis but also for planning and definitive intervention.
