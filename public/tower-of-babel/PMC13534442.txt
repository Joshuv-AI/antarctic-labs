Knowledge, attitude, and practice regarding acupuncture among patients with knee osteoarthritis: a path analysis
Authors: Yi Chen, Fangming He, Ai Liu, Yujun Shi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study aimed to investigate the knowledge, attitudes, and practices (KAP) of patients with knee osteoarthritis (KOA) regarding acupuncture. This multicenter cross-sectional study was conducted in multiple hospitals in Beijing between March and April 2025. Patients diagnosed with KOA completed a structured questionnaire. KAP scores were analyzed, and path analysis was used to examine their interrelationships. A total of 532 valid questionnaires were collected. Participants had a mean age of 49.24 ± 10.53 years, with 50.19% male. Most were married (96.62%), urban residents (82.52%), and had at least an associate degree (66.35%). The KAP scores were 6.21 ± 1.45 (possible range: 0–12), 29.05 ± 3.08 (possible range: 7–35), and 27.46 ± 3.06 (possible range: 7–35), respectively. Path analysis revealed that knowledge was directly associated with attitude (β = 0.315, P = 0.026) and practice (β = 0.125, P = 0.009), while attitude was directly associated with practice (β = 0.623, P = 0.009). Knowledge was also indirectly associated with practice through attitude (β = 0.196, P = 0.023), supporting the mediating role of attitude within the model. Patients with KOA exhibited moderate knowledge, generally positive attitudes, and relatively high engagement in acupuncture practices. Introduction

Knee osteoarthritis (KOA) is a prevalent degenerative joint condition characterized by cartilage loss and chronic inflammation, resulting in pain and reduced mobility1,2. In 2020, it affected over 595 million people globally3, with rising prevalence due to aging populations and increasing obesity rates, particularly among women and rural residents in China, where 8.8% of adults over 45 have symptomatic KOA4. Despite advances in pharmacological and surgical treatments, no cure exists, emphasizing the need for therapies that alleviate pain, enhance function, and delay disease progression1,5.

Acupuncture, a key modality in traditional Chinese medicine6,7, involves inserting fine needles into specific anatomical points to modulate pain through mechanisms such as neuroinflammation suppression, synaptic homeostasis restoration, and regulation of neuroimmune signaling pathways8. Reflecting accumulating evidence, acupuncture has been incorporated into major clinical guidelines for KOA management. The 2019 American College of Rheumatology/Arthritis Foundation guideline conditionally recommends acupuncture for knee osteoarthritis, indicating potential benefit with some remaining uncertainty in the strength of evidence9. More recently, an evidence-based clinical practice guideline specifically addressing acupuncture for KOA strongly supports manual acupuncture or electroacupuncture for reducing pain and improving function, thereby reinforcing its role within multimodal KOA treatment strategies10. However, patient engagement with acupuncture varies, potentially due to its cultural context and limited acceptance in some populations. In China, acupuncture is deeply embedded in traditional culture and is widely perceived as a natural, holistic approach to healing. Its long history and integration into everyday health beliefs contribute to higher levels of acceptance, especially among older adults and those residing in rural areas. Conversely, the increasing adoption of Western biomedical paradigms, particularly in urban and highly educated populations, has led to variability in its utilization. Cultural attitudes and generational differences thus play a critical role in shaping patient engagement with acupuncture11–13. In addition, sex-specific factors may also influence symptom perception and healthcare behaviors in KOA. Previous studies have suggested that hormonal fluctuations and menopausal transition may contribute to musculoskeletal pain, joint discomfort, and altered pain sensitivity in women, particularly involving the knee joint. In clinical acupuncture practice, some female patients with KOA retrospectively report experiencing recurrent knee soreness or discomfort during menstruation prior to menopause, suggesting a potential relationship between menstrual-related symptoms and early musculoskeletal manifestations14,15. Therefore, menstrual-related variables were additionally explored in the present study as supplementary observational factors among female participants.

The Knowledge, Attitudes, and Practices (KAP) framework provides a structured approach to assess how awareness, beliefs, and behaviors influence treatment utilization16,17. This model is based on the assumption that increased knowledge leads to positive attitudes, which in turn promote appropriate health practices. While KAP studies have explored conditions like rheumatoid arthritis and low back pain, research on KOA patients is limited18,19. This study aimed to evaluate KAP regarding acupuncture among Chinese KOA patients and to examine the structural relationships among these components using path analysis based on the theoretical KAP framework.

Methods

Study design and participants

This multicenter cross-sectional study was conducted in multiple hospitals in Beijing between March and April 2025. Patients diagnosed with KOA were invited to participate. Ethical approval was obtained from the Research Ethics Committee of the Third Affiliated Hospital (Approval No. BZYSY-2025KYKTPJ-14), with written informed consent secured from all participants.

Questionnaire design

A comprehensive, self-developed questionnaire (Appendix), informed by established clinical guidelines and relevant literature9, was meticulously crafted for this study. Following initial development, the questionnaire underwent refinement based on expert consultations, which led to targeted adjustments in the formulation and structure of baseline questions to enhance clarity and relevance. Before the primary data collection, a small-scale pilot test was conducted using 25 valid responses to assess the instrument’s reliability. The pilot results demonstrated good internal consistency, with a Cronbach’s α coefficient of 0.897, confirming that the questionnaire had high reliability in measuring the intended constructs. Although the questionnaire was developed based on established clinical guidelines and relevant literature, it was not derived from a previously standardized KAP instrument specifically validated for KOA populations. Content validity was evaluated through expert consultation during the development phase. The grouping of items into the three KAP domains was guided by the theoretical KAP framework and expert consensus during questionnaire development. To further evaluate the structural validity of the questionnaire, Kaiser–Meyer–Olkin (KMO) testing and confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) were additionally conducted based on the formal survey sample. The KMO value was 0.611 (P < 0.001), supporting the adequacy of the data for factor analysis. CFA results demonstrated an acceptable overall model fit, with CMIN/DF = 2.806, RMSEA = 0.058, IFI = 0.782, TLI = 0.750, and CFI = 0.778 (Tables S1 and S2 and Fig. S1).

The finalized instrument comprised four sections. The first section collected general demographic data (e.g., age, gender, marital status, and education) and disease-related information, including time since diagnosis, knee joint symptoms, perceived disease severity, and prior treatment history. The second section assessed knowledge through 10 items, including true/false, single-choice, and multiple-choice formats. Scoring criteria were as follows: true/false and single-choice items awarded 1 point for correct responses and 0 for incorrect or uncertain responses; multiple-choice items were scored 2 points for all correct answers, 1 point for partially correct, and 0 for incorrect. The total possible score for this section ranged from 0 to 12. The third section evaluated attitudes using seven items rated on a five-point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree to 5 = strongly agree), with selected items reverse-scored to mitigate response bias. The total attitude score ranged from 7 to 35. The final section measured practice behaviors through seven items, also rated on a five-point Likert scale (1 = not at all consistent to 5 = completely consistent), yielding a total possible score of 7–35.

Data collection and quality control

Data collection was conducted through the Wenjuanxing mini-program (https://www.wjx.cn) on the WeChat platform. Participants accessed the study by scanning a QR code using their WeChat application. To enhance data integrity and prevent duplicate submissions, each IP address was restricted to a single entry, and all questionnaire items were set as mandatory. To ensure data quality, responses were excluded if participants failed embedded attention-check (trap) questions, provided logically inconsistent answers, or submitted identical responses across all items within any of the three KAP domains. In addition, physiologically implausible anthropometric values were screened during data cleaning. Specifically, responses reporting height values of 66 cm, 71 cm, 60 cm, and 68 cm were considered invalid and excluded, as these values were inconsistent with adult anthropometric characteristics and likely reflected input errors.

Sample size

The required sample size was calculated using the following formula for estimating a population proportion:

Assuming a confidence level of 95% (Z1−a/2 = 1.96), an allowable margin of error (δ) of 0.05, and an estimated proportion (p) of 0.5 (used to yield the maximum sample size in the absence of prior estimates), the calculated minimum sample size was 384. To compensate for an anticipated non-response or invalid response rate of approximately 20%, the final target sample size was adjusted to 480.

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was performed using SPSS version 27.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) and Amos version 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Continuous variables were expressed as means ± standard deviations, and categorical variables as frequencies and percentages. Group differences were assessed using the Mann–Whitney U test for two groups and the Kruskal–Wallis H test for three or more groups. Spearman rank correlation analysis was employed to evaluate the associations among the KAP dimensions. In addition, among female participants, the severity of menstrual symptoms was quantified based on the item “Menstrual Impact on Daily Life”, which was scored from 1 to 4 according to increasing severity. These exploratory variables were included based on clinical observations suggesting that some women with KOA reported recurrent knee discomfort during menstruation prior to menopause. Spearman correlation analysis was further conducted to examine the relationships between menstrual symptom severity and KAP scores. Path analysis was subsequently conducted to examine the direct and indirect relationships among the three KAP components. All statistical tests were two-sided, and a P-value less than 0.05 was considered indicative of statistical significance. Previous KAP-based studies have applied structural modeling approaches to examine the relationships among knowledge, attitudes, and practices, providing a theoretical basis for the present analytical framework16,17.

Results

Questionnaire quality

Initially, a total of 555 samples were collected. The following cases were excluded: (1) 4 cases with physiologically implausible height values; (2) 3 cases selected ‘Not sure’ and 2 cases selected “A” for all knowledge items; (3) 2 cases selected “A” for all attitude items; (4) 1 case selected “A”, 1 case selected “C”, and 10 case selected “B” for all practice items. The final number of valid responses was 532, with an effective rate of 95.86%. In the formal study involving 532 participants, the overall internal consistency of the questionnaire remained acceptable, with a Cronbach’s α of 0.786. Subscale reliability coefficients for the knowledge, attitude, and practice domains were also examined and demonstrated acceptable internal consistency, supporting the instrument’s reliability for use in this population.

Demographic information on participants and KAP scores

The study included 532 patients with knee osteoarthritis (mean age 49.24 ± 10.53 years; 50.19% male), predominantly married (96.62%), urban residents (82.52%), and educated at the associate or bachelor’s level (66.35%). The KAP scores were 6.21 ± 1.45 (possible range: 0–12), 29.05 ± 3.08 (possible range: 7–35), and 27.46 ± 3.06 (possible range: 7–35), respectively. Lower educational attainment was associated with improved knowledge scores (P = 0.006). Participants with monthly incomes above 10,000 CNY demonstrated the highest attitude scores (P = 0.011). Urban residents scored higher in knowledge (P = 0.022). Patients with longer disease duration (3–5 years) exhibited higher knowledge (P = 0.012), attitude (P < 0.001), and practice scores (P < 0.001). Severe symptoms impacting daily life correlated with better practice scores (P < 0.001). No significant gender-based differences were observed in KAP outcomes (P > 0.05) (Table 1).

Distribution of responses to knowledge, attitude, and practice

Significant knowledge gaps were observed regarding acupuncture therapy for KOA, particularly concerning treatment limitations and safety. Only 50.56% correctly rejected that acupuncture can replace knee replacement surgery (K3), while 36.47% were uncertain and 12.97% endorsed this incorrect view. Just 53.57% recognized that not all KOA patients are suitable candidates for acupuncture (K4), with 34.96% uncertain and 11.47% incorrectly believing universal applicability. 13.72% erroneously agreed that comorbid conditions like hypertension/diabetes need not be considered during treatment (K6), posing potential safety risks. Among single-choice questions, 27.26% failed to recognize that acupuncture cannot directly repair knee joint damage (K8). For multi-select questions, 29.14% erroneously believed patients should avoid all medications during acupuncture treatment (K11) (Table S3).

Responses to the attitude dimension showed that 2.26% strongly agreed and 6.58% agreed that they are not willing to consider acupuncture, as they believe medication is more effective (A5) and 2.26% strongly agreed and 3.76% agreed that cost of acupuncture treatment for knee osteoarthritis is too high and not worth trying (A7). While 2.82% strongly disagreed and 3.95% disagreed that acupuncture has very few side effects when treating knee osteoarthritis, making it suitable for long-term use (A6) (Table S4).

Responses to the practice dimension showed that only 15.98% always referred to social media or information on the Internet to judge the effectiveness of acupuncture (P2). It is worth noting that when the initial acupuncture treatment was not effective, 8.27% want to stop acupuncture treatment and take other treatments, also 28.20% had this tendency (P6) (Table S5).

Correlations between KAP

In the correlation analysis, significant positive correlations were found between knowledge and attitude (r = 0.262, P < 0.001), knowledge and practice (r = 0.266, P < 0.001), as well as attitude and practice (r = 0.568, P < 0.001), respectively (Table 2). Additional analysis showed that menstrual symptom severity was not significantly correlated with knowledge (r = − 0.103, P = 0.093), attitude (r = 0.061, P = 0.323), or practice (r = 0.105, P = 0.088) scores (Table S6).

Path analysis

Path analysis demonstrated that knowledge was significantly associated with attitude (β = 0.315, P = 0.026) and practice (β = 0.125, P = 0.009). Attitude also showed a strong direct association with practice (β = 0.623, P = 0.009). In addition, knowledge was indirectly associated with practice through attitude (β = 0.196, P = 0.023), further supporting the mediating role of attitude within the model (Table 3 and Fig. 1).

Discussion

This study provides a comprehensive examination of the knowledge, attitudes, and practices surrounding acupuncture among patients with knee osteoarthritis, revealing moderate knowledge levels, predominantly positive attitudes, and considerable engagement in acupuncture practices. The findings highlight the critical interplay among these dimensions, with knowledge being indirectly associated with practice through the mediating role of attitude, offering valuable insights for clinical and educational strategies. From a clinical perspective, acupuncture is increasingly recognized as a nonpharmacologic component of comprehensive KOA management. While earlier guidelines such as the 2019 ACR/AF recommendation provided conditional support, more recent evidence-based guidance has offered stronger endorsement based on accumulating clinical trials. This evolving consensus underscores the importance of understanding patients’ perceptions and engagement with acupuncture within real-world practice contexts.

Significant knowledge deficits were observed, particularly concerning acupuncture’s scope and safety. Many participants harbored misconceptions, such as believing acupuncture could serve as a substitute for knee replacement surgery or that it is universally applicable to all KOA patients. Misunderstandings about the interaction of acupuncture with comorbidities and the appropriateness of concurrent medication use further underscore the need for targeted education. These findings resonate with prior research indicating that patients often lack a nuanced understanding of complementary therapies, which can hinder informed decision-making16. Beyond the behavioral perspective, the underlying mechanisms of acupuncture in KOA management can be interpreted through both biomedical and traditional theoretical lenses. Biomedically, acupuncture modulates peripheral and central sensitization, reduces proinflammatory cytokines, and improves neuromuscular control, thereby alleviating joint pain and stiffness. From a traditional Chinese medicine (TCM) standpoint, KOA is often attributed to patterns such as “Bi syndrome” caused by wind, cold, and dampness obstructing meridians. Acupuncture aims to restore qi and blood circulation, thereby correcting disharmony and relieving symptoms20,21.

The path analysis was consistent with the theoretical KAP framework, showing that knowledge was significantly associated with attitudes, which in turn were strongly associated with practice behaviors. Although knowledge was directly associated with practice, the stronger association observed for attitude indicates that attitudes may play a key mediating role linking knowledge to behavioral engagement, consistent with established behavior change models and KAP studies in chronic conditions such as diabetes and hypertension. This suggests that fostering positive attitudes is essential for translating knowledge into sustained health practices22,23. Comparisons with prior KAP research further support the importance of education and attitude change in promoting sustained health behaviors. KAP-based SEM studies in chronic conditions, including hypertension and diabetes, have similarly reported that knowledge is more likely to influence practice through attitudinal pathways rather than exerting a strong direct effect, highlighting attitude as a critical leverage point for behavior change16,17. Consistent with KAP findings in other musculoskeletal and complementary-therapy contexts, such as knowledge about acupuncture among people with back pain and KAP regarding bee venom acupuncture among patients with rheumatoid arthritis, misconceptions and uncertainty about indications, safety, and realistic expectations can impede informed decision-making and long-term adherence18,19. Taken together, these comparisons suggest that educational efforts should not only deliver factual information but also actively address beliefs, perceived benefits and risks, and confidence in treatment, thereby strengthening attitudinal readiness and supporting sustained engagement.

Demographic characteristics also played an important role in shaping KAP outcomes. Higher income levels was associated with better scores across all dimensions, while longer disease duration corresponded with greater engagement. These findings are in line with existing studies which suggest that individuals with more disease experience or greater access to health resources tend to exhibit more proactive health behaviors24,25. Notably, the findings revealed that patients with lower educational attainment exhibited greater knowledge. This might have been due to highly educated patients’ preference for Western medicine as their primary treatment approach26. However, even among these subgroups, confusion regarding the scope and application of acupuncture was common. This underscores the persistence of informational gaps across different patient populations and suggests that messaging strategies must go beyond generic promotion to address specific points of confusion27,28.

Participants generally expressed positive attitudes toward acupuncture, with most willing to consult acupuncturists, try multiple sessions, or explore combined treatment modalities. However, a minority expressed concerns about cost, long-term efficacy, and side effects, particularly among those with limited insurance coverage29,30. Patients with commercial or comprehensive insurance exhibited more favorable attitudes, highlighting the influence of financial and institutional support on treatment acceptance. These findings align with studies suggesting that structural factors, such as reimbursement policies and healthcare access, significantly shape patient perceptions of complementary therapies31,32.

Although practice levels were relatively high, engagement often appeared reactive and symptom-driven rather than grounded in stable knowledge or belief. Many participants had tried acupuncture or planned to continue, but reliance on online sources for treatment evaluation and early discontinuation due to perceived inefficacy suggest a lack of sustained commitment. Similar patterns have been observed in other musculoskeletal conditions, where complementary therapies are frequently used intermittently19,28.

To address these challenges, educational interventions should focus on dispelling misconceptions, clarifying acupuncture’s role within a multimodal KOA management framework, and setting realistic expectations30,31. Evidence-based, patient-centered materials that address specific knowledge gaps and are delivered through trusted channels, such as physicians or allied health professionals, could enhance credibility and engagement. From a clinical practice perspective, these findings suggest that healthcare providers should proactively assess patients’ baseline understanding of acupuncture and identify specific misconceptions before initiating treatment discussions. Incorporating structured educational conversations into routine consultations may facilitate shared decision-making and align patient expectations with realistic therapeutic goals. Furthermore, recognizing the central role of attitude implies that clinicians should not only provide information but also explore patients’ beliefs, concerns, and prior experiences with complementary therapies. Such an approach may improve adherence, optimize treatment planning within multimodal KOA management, and ultimately enhance long-term outcomes. Strategies should also be culturally tailored to patients’ beliefs, prior exposure to traditional medicine, and local healthcare contexts, while accounting for urban–rural disparities and varying insurance coverage to ensure equitable access to accurate information and appropriate treatment pathways25,32.

The exploratory analysis of menstrual-related variables did not demonstrate significant correlations with KAP scores in the current study. Nevertheless, these variables were included based on clinical observations that some female patients with KOA experienced knee discomfort associated with menstrual periods prior to menopause. Although the present findings did not support a significant association, the results may provide preliminary clinical observations for future studies investigating potential sex-specific or hormonal influences on KOA-related symptoms and healthcare behaviors.

This study has several limitations. First, as a cross-sectional study, it captures associations rather than causal relationships, limiting the ability to infer directionality among KAP components. Second, the sample was drawn from hospitals in Beijing, which may restrict the generalizability of the findings to broader or more diverse populations, particularly rural residents or individuals outside of mainland China, whose cultural beliefs, access to acupuncture, and healthcare systems may differ significantly. In addition, although the questionnaire demonstrated acceptable internal consistency and supplementary confirmatory factor analysis showed an overall acceptable model fit, some fit indices did not fully reach ideal thresholds, indicating that further psychometric validation and optimization of the questionnaire structure may still be warranted in future studies. Moreover, the data collection was conducted over a relatively short period of 2 months, which may have limited seasonal or temporal variability and thereby affected the representativeness and diversity of the sample. Third, the reliance on self-reported data introduces potential bias, including social desirability and recall bias, which may have influenced the accuracy of the responses. Fourth, while the study found statistically significant correlations among KAP components, the relatively low correlation coefficients between knowledge and attitude/practice suggest the presence of potential confounding variables. In addition, variability in acupuncture treatment duration, session frequency, and prior exposure to acupuncture was not quantitatively controlled in the analysis. Moreover, concurrent treatments such as pharmacologic therapy, physical therapy, or other complementary interventions were not systematically adjusted for in the current analysis. Patients’ expectations regarding acupuncture efficacy, which may influence both attitude and reported practice behaviors, were also not directly measured. These factors could have interacted with KAP components and potentially affected the observed relationships. Future studies incorporating comprehensive treatment profiles and expectation-related measures would further clarify acupuncture’s role within integrated KOA management. Differences in treatment intensity or cumulative experience may have influenced patients’ knowledge acquisition, attitude formation, and practice behaviors. Future studies incorporating detailed treatment history and dosage parameters would help clarify these potential effects. Factors such as health literacy, previous treatment experiences, and trust in traditional versus Western medicine may have influenced these relationships but were not explicitly controlled for in the current analysis.

In conclusion, patients with KOA exhibit moderate knowledge, predominantly positive attitudes, and considerable engagement with acupuncture. By situating these findings within the evolving clinical context of KOA management, this study underscores the importance of integrating structured patient education and expectation management into routine care. Addressing specific knowledge gaps, clarifying the role of acupuncture within multimodal treatment strategies, and aligning therapeutic discussions with patients’ beliefs and preferences may support shared decision-making, improve adherence, and enhance real-world clinical outcomes.
