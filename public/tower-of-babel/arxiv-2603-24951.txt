New Characterizations of Nonsmooth Convex Functions via Generalized Derivatives
Vo Thanh Phat

Abstract




Abstract
. This paper studies the convexity properties of nonsmooth extended-real-valued weakly convex functions, a class of functions that is central to modern optimization and its applications. We establish new characterizations of convexity using second-order generalized derivative tools, including subgradient graphical derivatives, second subderivatives, and second-order subdifferentials. These tools allow us to derive necessary and sufficient conditions for convexity in the nonsmooth framework.

Key words
. Convexity, weakly convex functions, second-order subdifferentials, graphical derivatives, second subderivatives

49J52, 49J53, 90C30, 26B25

Introduction

Convexity plays a fundamental and important role in optimization theory and numerical methods. As is well known, the second order derivative test in classical calculus provides a powerful tool for identifying convexity. A twice differentiable function is convex if and only if its Hessian matrix is positive semidefinite. However, in many practical models, the first or second order derivatives of a function may not exist, which makes it impossible to apply this criterion to characterize convexity, especially for nonsmooth extended-real-valued functions.

To address the nonsmooth case, many characterizations utilizing second-order generalized derivatives for convex functions have been investigated. In
[
2
]
, the authors employ the second-order subdifferential introduced by Mordukhovich in
[
26
]
to establish a necessary condition for nonsmooth convex functions, as well as sufficient conditions for convexity in several special cases, including
\mathcal{C}^{1,1}
functions,
\mathcal{C}^{1}
univariate functions, piecewise linear functions, and certain classes of piecewise
\mathcal{C}^{2}
functions. The development of sufficient conditions for convexity of piecewise
\mathcal{C}^{2}
functions was then completed by Chieu and Yao in
[
5
]
. In
[
6
]
, Chieu and Huy provided a characterization of
\mathcal{C}^{1}
-smooth functions via second-order subdifferentials and showed that this result cannot be extended to general locally Lipschitz functions. The characterization of convexity for lower-
\mathcal{C}^{2}
functions was later investigated in
[
7
]
. More recently, Nadi and Zafarani proposed another sufficient condition for the convexity of lower-
\mathcal{C}^{1}
functions in
[
32
]
.

To the best of our knowledge, although many conditions for characterizing the convexity of nonsmooth functions have been discussed above, the characterization of convexity for nonsmooth extended real valued functions via second order subdifferentials has not yet been investigated. Moreover, all previously mentioned conditions require the functions to be locally Lipschitz continuous and real-valued. In addition, while second order subdifferentials have proven to be powerful tools in variational analysis, both in theory and in numerical algorithms as shown in
[
27
,
28
,
29
]
, other generalized derivatives that are also important in theoretical and numerical optimization, including subgradient graphical derivatives and second subderivatives as presented in
[
3
,
4
,
24
,
25
,
31
]
, have not yet been explored for characterizing the convexity of nonsmooth functions.

In this paper, we investigate new characterizations of convexity for extended-real-valued weakly convex functions, a class that plays a fundamental role in optimization and related areas. Our analysis is conducted in the nonsmooth setting and relies on three central second-order generalized derivatives discussed above, namely graphical derivatives, second subderivatives, and second-order subdifferentials. Weak convexity has been extensively studied in variational analysis and optimization due to its rich structure and broad applicability; see, for example,
[
9
,
10
,
37
,
40
]
. In particular, this class supports the convergence analysis of a variety of numerical methods, including proximal-type and first-order algorithms for nonsmooth optimization (cf.
[
10
,
22
]
). Weak convexity also plays an important role in the theory of differential equations, where it typically appears under the name of semiconvexity. In particular, semiconvex functions arise naturally in the analysis of Hamilton-Jacobi equations, viscosity solutions, and optimal control problems, where they provide essential regularity properties such as almost-everywhere second-order differentiability and stability under infimal convolutions; see, for example,
[
1
,
8
]
. These properties are crucial for the well-posedness of solutions, the derivation of comparison principles, and the qualitative analysis of nonsmooth dynamics. This perspective further underscores the broad relevance of weakly convex functions across optimization, variational analysis, and partial differential equations.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. Section
2
presents the necessary definitions and results from variational analysis. In Section
3
, we establish necessary and sufficient conditions for convexity using graphical derivatives of subgradient mappings. Section
4
provides the corresponding characterizations in terms of second subderivatives, while the results based on second order subdifferentials are also discussed in Section
5
. Finally, Section
6
offers concluding remarks and outlines possible directions for future research.

2
Preliminaries from Variational Analysis

This section recalls some notions and notation frequently used in this paper. All our considerations are given in the space
{\rm I\!R}^{n}
with the
Euclidean norm

\|\cdot\|
and
scalar/inner product

\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
defined by

\|x\|:=\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\;\text{and }\langle x,y\rangle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}y_{i}\quad\text{where }x:=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}),\;y:=(y_{1},\ldots,y_{n}).

Throughout the paper,
\overline{{\rm I\!R}}:={\rm I\!R}\cup\{\infty\}
denotes the extended real line, and
{\rm I\!N}:=\{1,2,\ldots\}
denotes the set of natural numbers.

We provide the necessary background on variational analysis and generalized differentiation, drawing on the foundational works of Mordukhovich
[
27
,
28
,
29
]
and Rockafellar and Wets
[
37
]
.
Given a set
\Omega\subset{\rm I\!R}^{s}
with
\bar{z}\in\Omega
, the (Bouligand-Severi)
tangent/contingent cone
to
\Omega
at
\bar{z}
is

T_{\Omega}(\bar{z}):=\big\{w\in{\rm I\!R}^{s}\;\big|\;\exists\,t_{k}\downarrow 0,\;w_{k}\to w\;\mbox{ as }\;k\to\infty\;\mbox{ with }\;\bar{z}+t_{k}w_{k}\in\Omega\big\}.

(2.1)

The (Fréchet)
regular normal cone
to
\Omega

at
\bar{z}\in\Omega
is defined by

\widehat{N}_{\Omega}(\bar{z}):=\Big\{v\in{\rm I\!R}^{s}\;\Big|\;\limsup_{z\overset{\Omega}{\rightarrow}\bar{z}}\frac{\langle v,z-\bar{z}\rangle}{\|z-\bar{z}\|}\leq 0\Big\},

(2.2)

where
the symbol
z\overset{\Omega}{\rightarrow}\bar{z}
indicates that
z\to\bar{z}
with
z\in\Omega
. It can be equivalently described via a duality correspondence with (
2.1
) by

\widehat{N}_{\Omega}(\bar{z})=T^{*}_{\Omega}(\bar{z}):=\big\{v\in{\rm I\!R}^{s}\;\big|\;\langle v,w\rangle\leq 0\;\mbox{ for all }\;w\in T_{\Omega}(\bar{z})\big\}.

(2.3)

The
(Mordukhovich)
limiting normal cones
to
\Omega
at

\bar{z}\in\Omega
is defined by

N_{\Omega}(\bar{z}):=\big\{v\in{\rm I\!R}^{s}\;\big|\;\exists\,z_{k}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\Omega}}{{\to}}\bar{z},\;v_{k}\to v\;\text{ as }\;k\to\infty\;\text{ with
}\;v_{k}\in\widehat{N}_{\Omega}(z_{k})\big\}.

(2.4)

It is important to observe that the regular normal cone defined in (
2.2
) is always convex, whereas the limiting normal cone in (
2.4
) can be nonconvex, as illustrated by the graph of
|\cdot|
at
\bar{z}=(0,0)
. Consequently, it cannot be derived through a duality relation of the form (
2.3
) from any tangential approximation of
\Omega
at
\bar{z}
. Despite this, the limiting normal cone (
2.4
), along with the associated subdifferential constructions for functions, enjoy full calculus rules that are grounded in the variational and extremal principles of variational analysis.

Let us consider an extended-real-valued function
\varphi:{\rm I\!R}^{n}\rightarrow\overline{{\rm I\!R}}:=(-\infty,\infty]
, and its

effective domain
and
epigraph
defined by, respectively,

\mbox{\rm dom}\,\varphi:=\big\{x\in{\rm I\!R}^{n}\;\big|\;\varphi(x)<\infty\big\}\;\mbox{ and
}\;\mbox{\rm epi}\,\varphi:=\big\{(x,\alpha)\in{\rm I\!R}^{n+1}\;\big|\;\alpha\geq\varphi(x)\big\}.

We always assume that
\varphi
is proper and lower semicontinuous (l.s.c.). The
basic/limiting subdifferential
of
\varphi
at
\bar{x}\in\text{dom}\varphi
is defined by

\partial\varphi(\bar{x}):=\big\{v\in{\rm I\!R}^{n}\;\big|\;(v,-1)\in N_{{\rm\text{\rm epi}}\,\varphi}\big(\bar{x},\varphi(\bar{x})\big)\big\}.

(2.5)

Recall that the basic subdifferential

\partial\varphi(\bar{x})
reduces to the gradient
\{\nabla\varphi(\bar{x})\}

if
\varphi
is
{\cal C}^{1}
-smooth around
\bar{x}
(or merely strictly
differentiable at this point), and that
\partial\varphi(\bar{x})
is the
subdifferential of convex analysis if
\varphi
is convex.

Let us now review some classes of extended-real-valued functions
\varphi\colon{\rm I\!R}^{n}\to\overline{{\rm I\!R}}
broadly used in the paper. For
s\in{\rm I\!R}
, the function
\varphi
is called
s
-convex
on
\Omega
if

{\varphi((1-\lambda)x+\lambda y)\leq(1-\lambda)\varphi(x)+\lambda\varphi(y)-\frac{s}{2}\lambda(1-\lambda)\|x-y\|^{2},}

(2.6)

for any
x,y\in\Omega,\;\lambda\in(0,1)
.
Specifically, the function
\varphi
is called convex when
s=0
,
s
-strongly convex
when
s>0
, and
(-s)
-weakly convex
when
s<0
.
An l.s.c. function
\varphi
is called
prox-regular
at a point
\bar{x}\in\mbox{\rm dom}\,\varphi
for a vector
\bar{v}\in\partial\varphi(\bar{x})
if there exist constants
\varepsilon>0
and
r\geq 0
such that

\varphi(x)\geq\varphi(u)+\langle v,x-u\rangle-\frac{r}{2}\|x-u\|^{2}

(2.7)

holds for all
x\in\mathbb{B}_{\varepsilon}(\bar{x})
and
(u,v)\in\mbox{\rm gph}\,\partial\varphi\cap(\mathbb{B}_{\varepsilon}(\bar{x})\times\mathbb{B}_{\varepsilon}(\bar{v}))
with
\varphi(u)<\varphi(\bar{x})+\varepsilon
. If (
2.7
) is satisfied for every
v\in\partial\varphi(\bar{x})
, we say that
\varphi
is prox-regular at
\bar{x}
.
The function
\varphi
is said to be
subdifferentially continuous
at
\bar{x}
for
\bar{v}\in\partial\varphi(\bar{x})
if, for any
\varepsilon>0
, there exists
\delta>0
such that
|\varphi(x)-\varphi(\bar{x})|<\varepsilon
whenever
(x,v)\in\mbox{\rm gph}\,\partial\varphi\cap(\mathbb{B}_{\delta}(\bar{x})\times\mathbb{B}_{\delta}(\bar{v}))
. When this condition holds for all
v\in\partial\varphi(\bar{x})
, we say that
\varphi
is subdifferentially continuous at
\bar{x}
.
An important observation is that if
\varphi
is subdifferentially continuous at
\bar{x}
for
\bar{v}
, the restriction “
\varphi(u)<\varphi(\bar{x})+\varepsilon
” in the prox-regularity definition can be omitted.
Functions that are both prox-regular and subdifferentially continuous are referred to as
continuously prox-regular
. This class encompasses many important functions in second-order variational analysis, including commonly used types in applications such as convex functions,
\mathcal{C}^{2}
-smooth functions, amenable functions; see
[
37
, Chapter 13]
. Moreover, it follows from the subdifferential characterization of weakly convex functions in
[
9
, Theorem 3.1]
that any weakly convex function
\varphi
satisfies condition (
2.7
) and is therefore continuously prox-regular.

The following two well-known constructions play a crucial role in this paper. Given
an l.s.c. function
\varphi:{\rm I\!R}^{n}\to\overline{{\rm I\!R}}
and a parameter value
\lambda>0
, the
Moreau envelope

e_{\lambda}\varphi
is defined by

e_{\lambda}\varphi(x):=\inf\Big\{\varphi(y)+\frac{1}{2\lambda}\|y-x\|^{2}\;\Big|\;y\in{\rm I\!R}^{n}\Big\},\quad x\in{\rm I\!R}^{n},

(2.8)

A function
\varphi
is said to be
prox-bounded
if there exists
\lambda>0
such that
e_{\lambda}\varphi(x)>-\infty
for some
x\in{\rm I\!R}^{n}
. The supremum of the set of all such
\lambda
is the threshold
\lambda_{\varphi}
of the prox-boundedness for
\varphi
. Note that it follows from
[
37
, Exercise 1.24 and Theorem 2.26]
that if
\varphi
is
s
-convex, then
\varphi
is prox-bounded.

Recall that a function
\varphi
is of
class
\mathcal{C}^{1,1}
around
\bar{x}
if it is
{\cal C}^{1}
-smooth and its gradient is Lipschitz continuous around this point. We now recall important properties of the Moreau envelope that follows from
[
10
, Lemma 2.5]
,
[
37
, Proposition 13.37]
and
[
41
, Corollary 3.7]
.

Proposition 2.1

(
Moreau envelopes of prox-regular functions).

Let
\varphi\colon{\rm I\!R}^{n}\to\overline{{\rm I\!R}}
be an l.s.c. and prox-bounded function with threshold
\lambda_{\varphi}>0
. Suppose that
\varphi
is continuously prox-regular at
\bar{x}
for
\bar{v}\in\partial\varphi(\bar{x})
, then for all sufficiently small numbers
\lambda\in(0,\lambda_{\varphi})
there is a convex neighborhood
U_{\lambda}
of
\bar{x}+\lambda\bar{v}
on which the following hold:

(i)

The Moreau envelope
e_{\lambda}\varphi
from (
2.8
) is of class
{\cal C}^{1,1}
on the set
U_{\lambda}
.

(ii)

The gradient of
e_{\lambda}\varphi
is calculated by:

\nabla e_{\lambda}\varphi(x)=\big(\lambda I+(\partial\varphi)^{-1}\big)^{-1}(x)\;\mbox{ for all }\;x\in U_{\lambda}.

(2.9)

In the case where
\varphi
is weakly convex, then
\nabla e_{\lambda}\varphi
is globally Lipschitz continuous on
{\rm I\!R}^{n}
, and all the above conclusions hold with
U_{\lambda}={\rm I\!R}^{n}
.

3
Characterizations via Subgradient Graphical Derivatives

In this section, we develop characterizations for l.s.c., proper, convex functions via the second-order generalized derivative construction called
subgradient graphical derivative
investigated in
[
3
,
4
]
. To proceed, we need to recall some essential concepts in variational analysis. Let
F
be a set-valued mapping between Euclidean spaces
{\rm I\!R}^{n}
and
{\rm I\!R}^{m}
. As usual, the effective domain and the graph of
F
are given, respectively, by

\text{dom}F:=\{x\in{\rm I\!R}^{n}|F(x)\neq\emptyset\}\quad\text{and}\quad\text{gph}F=\{(x,y)\in{\rm I\!R}^{n}\times{\rm I\!R}^{m}|y\in F(x)\}.

The (Painlevé-Kuratowski)
outer limit
of
F
as
x\rightarrow\bar{x}
is given by

\underset{x\rightarrow\bar{x}}{\mathop{{\rm Lim}\,{\rm sup}}}\;F(x):=\big\{y\in{\rm I\!R}^{n}\;\big|\,\exists\mbox{ sequences }x_{k}\to\bar{x},\ y_{k}\rightarrow y\;\mbox{with }\;y_{k}\in F(x_{k}),\;k\in{\rm I\!N}\big\}.

(3.1)

The
graphical derivative
of
F
at

(\bar{x},\bar{y})\in\mbox{\rm gph}\,F
is defined via (
2.1
) by

DF(\bar{x},\bar{y})(u):=\big\{v\in{\rm I\!R}^{m}\;\big|\;(u,v)\in T_{{\rm gph}\,F}(\bar{x},\bar{y})\big\},\quad u\in{\rm I\!R}^{n}.

(3.2)

This concept was introduced in the early 1980s by Jean-Pierre Aubin, who called it the contingent derivative. Here we follow the references
[
12
,
37
]

in using the terminology “the graphical derivative”. If
(\bar{x},\bar{y})\notin\mbox{\rm gph}\,F
, one puts
DF(\bar{x},\bar{y})(w)=\emptyset
for all
w
by convention. In terms of the outer limit given in (
3.1
) and the difference quotient mapping

\Delta_{t}F(x,y)(w)=\frac{1}{t}[F(x+tw)-y]\quad\text{for }y\in F(x),\;t>0,

we can represent the graphical derivative in (
3.2
) as follows:

DF(\bar{x},\bar{y})(u)=\mathop{{\rm Lim}\,{\rm sup}}_{t\downarrow 0,w\to u}\Delta_{t}F(\bar{x},\bar{y})(w),\quad u\in{\rm I\!R}^{n}.

(3.3)

By the definition of graphical derivative, we have the following relationship

v\in DF(\bar{x},\bar{y})(u)\iff u\in DF^{-1}(\bar{y},\bar{x})(v)\quad\text{for all }u\in{\rm I\!R}^{n},v\in{\rm I\!R}^{m}.

(3.4)

In the case where
F(\bar{x})
is the singleton
\{\bar{y}\}
, we omit
\bar{y}
in
the notation of (
3.2
). Note that if the mapping

F\colon{\rm I\!R}^{n}\to{\rm I\!R}^{m}
is a single-valued differentiable mapping at
\bar{x}
, then
DF(\bar{x})=\nabla F(\bar{x})
, where
\nabla F(\bar{x})
is the Jacobian matrix of
F
at
\bar{x}
.

Now we are ready to define the
subgradient graphical derivative
of

\varphi\colon{\rm I\!R}^{n}\to\overline{{\rm I\!R}}
at
\bar{x}\in\mbox{\rm dom}\,\varphi
corresponding to

\bar{v}\in\partial\varphi(\bar{x})
, which is the mapping

\partial^{2}_{D}\varphi(\bar{x},\bar{v})\colon{\rm I\!R}^{n}\rightrightarrows{\rm I\!R}^{n}
defined by

\partial^{2}_{D}\varphi(\bar{x},\bar{v})(u):=\big(D\partial\varphi\big)(\bar{x},\bar{v})(u)\;\mbox{
for all }\;u\in{\rm I\!R}^{n},

(3.5)

i.e., by applying the
graphical derivative (
3.2
) to the first-order subgradient mapping
(
2.5
). The following result is the necessary condition for the convexity of an l.s.c. function.

Theorem 3.1

(
necessary condition of convex functions via subgradient graphical derivatives).

Let
\varphi:{\rm I\!R}^{n}\to\overline{{\rm I\!R}}
be a proper, l.s.c. convex function. Then we have

\langle z,w\rangle\geq 0\quad\text{for all }\;z\in{\partial}^{2}_{D}\varphi(x,y)(w),\;(x,y)\in\mbox{\rm gph}\,\partial\varphi,\;w\in{\rm I\!R}^{n}.

(3.6)

[
Proof.]
Pick any
z\in{\partial}^{2}_{D}\varphi(x,y)(w)
, where
(x,y)\in\mbox{\rm gph}\,\partial\varphi
and
w\in{\rm I\!R}^{n}
. Then we can find sequences
t_{k}\downarrow 0
,
w_{k}\to w
as
k\to\infty
such that

y+t_{k}z_{k}\in\partial\varphi(x+t_{k}w_{k})\quad\text{for all }k\in{\rm I\!N}.

(3.7)

It follows from the convexity of
\varphi
that
\partial\varphi
is monotone. Combining this with (
3.7
) and the fact that
(x,y)\in\mbox{\rm gph}\,\partial\varphi
, we deduce that

\langle x+t_{k}w_{k}-x,y+t_{k}z_{k}-y\rangle\geq 0\quad\text{for all }k\in{\rm I\!N},

which implies that
\langle w_{k},z_{k}\rangle\geq 0
for all
k\in{\rm I\!N}.
Letting
k\to\infty
in this inequality, we have
\langle w,z\rangle\geq 0
. The proof is complete.

Next, we aim to derive necessary and sufficient conditions for convexity of an l.s.c. function under some additional assumptions via subgradient graphical derivatives. We first consider the case where the given function is
\mathcal{C}^{1}
-smooth with Lipschitzian derivatives on an open subset of
{\rm I\!R}^{n}
. To proceed, we need some auxiliary results.
Let
\mathcal{L}^{m}
be the
\sigma
-algebra of the Lebesgue measurable subsets on
{\rm I\!R}^{m}
. We denote the Lebesgue measure on
{\rm I\!R}^{n}
and
{\rm I\!R}
by
\mu
and
\lambda
, respectively.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
\Omega\subset{\rm I\!R}^{n}
be an open, convex set, and
\Omega_{0}\subset\Omega
such that
\Omega_{0}\in\mathcal{L}^{n}
and
\mu(\Omega\setminus\Omega_{0})=0
. Suppose that
a,b\in\Omega
are distinct points. Then there exist sequences of vectors
(a^{k})\subset\Omega
,
(b^{k})\subset\Omega
such that
a^{k}\to a,b^{k}\to b
and

\lambda\left(\{t\in[0,1]:a^{k}+t(b^{k}-a^{k})\in\Omega_{0}\}\right)=1\quad\text{for all }\;k\in{\rm I\!N}.

(3.8)

[
Proof.]
Putting
\Omega_{1}:=({\rm I\!R}^{n}\setminus\Omega)\cup\Omega_{0}
, we have
{\rm I\!R}^{n}\setminus\Omega_{1}=\Omega\setminus\Omega_{0}
, which implies that

\mu({\rm I\!R}^{n}\setminus\Omega_{1})=\mu(\Omega\setminus\Omega_{0})=0.

It follows from the openness of
\Omega
that
\Omega\in\mathcal{L}^{n}
, and thus
\Omega_{1}\in\mathcal{L}^{n}
. Using
[
2
, Lemma 4.1]
, there exist sequences of vectors
a^{k}\to a
and
b^{k}\to b
such that

\lambda\left(\{t\in[0,1]:a^{k}+t(b^{k}-a^{k})\in\Omega_{1}\}\right)=1,\quad\text{for all }\;k\in{\rm I\!N}.

(3.9)

Moreover, due to the openness of
\Omega
,
a^{k},b^{k}\in\Omega
for all sufficiently large
k\in{\rm I\!N}
. Without loss of generality, we can assume that
(a^{k})\subset\Omega
and
(b^{k})\subset\Omega
. By the convexity of
\Omega
, we have that

\{t\in[0,1]:a^{k}+t(b^{k}-a^{k})\in\Omega_{1}\}=\{t\in[0,1]:a^{k}+t(b^{k}-a^{k})\in\Omega_{0}\}\quad\forall k\in{\rm I\!N}.

(3.10)

Combining (
3.9
) and (
3.10
), we obtain (
3.8
).

Here is the aforementioned characterization of differentiable convex functions with Lipschitzian derivatives on an open set.

Theorem 3.3

(
characterizations of differentiable convex function on an open set).

Let
\varphi:{\rm I\!R}^{n}\to\overline{{\rm I\!R}}
be differentiable on a nonempty open convex set
\Omega\subset{\rm I\!R}^{n}
. Suppose that
\nabla\varphi
is Lipschitz continuous on
\Omega
. Then the following assertions are equivalent:

(i)

\varphi
is convex on
\Omega
.

(ii)

\nabla^{2}\varphi(x)
is positive semidefinite for all
x\in\Omega_{0}
, where

\Omega_{0}:=\{x\in\Omega|\;\varphi\;\text{is twice differentiable at }x\}.

(iii)

\partial^{2}_{D}\varphi(x)
is positive semidefinite for all
x\in\Omega
in the sense that

\langle z,w\rangle\geq 0\quad\text{for all }\;z\in\partial^{2}_{D}\varphi(x)(w),\;x\in\Omega,\;w\in{\rm I\!R}^{n}.

(3.11)

[
Proof.]
We firstly clarify the implication [(i)
\Longrightarrow
(iii)]. To proceed, suppose that
\varphi
is convex on
\Omega
,
x\in\Omega
,
w\in{\rm I\!R}^{n}
and
z\in\partial^{2}_{D}\varphi(x)(w)
. Then there exist sequences
t_{k}\downarrow 0
,
w_{k}\to w,z_{k}\to z
such that

(x,\nabla\varphi(x))+t_{k}(w_{k},z_{k})\in\text{\rm gph}\nabla\varphi,\quad\text{for all }\;k\in{\rm I\!N},

which implies that
\nabla\varphi(x+t_{k}w_{k})=\nabla\varphi(x)+t_{k}z_{k}
for all
k\in{\rm I\!N}
. Due to the first-order characterization of differentiable convex functions and the convexity of
\varphi
on
\Omega
, we have

\langle\nabla\varphi(x+t_{k}w_{k})-\nabla\varphi(x),x+t_{k}w_{k}-x\rangle\geq 0,\quad\text{for all sufficiently large }\;k\in{\rm I\!N},

or
t_{k}^{2}\langle z_{k},w_{k}\rangle\geq 0
, which implies that
\langle z_{k},w_{k}\rangle\geq 0
for sufficiently large
k\in{\rm I\!N}
. Letting
k\to\infty
in this inequality, we obtain the inequality
\langle z,w\rangle\geq 0
, which justifies (
3.11
).

We next verify the implication [(iii)
\Longrightarrow
(ii)]. Indeed,
we have
\partial^{2}_{D}\varphi(x)=\nabla^{2}\varphi(x)
for all
x\in\Omega_{0}
. Therefore, if (
3.11
) holds, then (ii) is satisfied.

Next, suppose that (ii) holds, we show that
\varphi
is convex on
\Omega
. It follows from the Lipschitz continuity of
\nabla\varphi
on
\Omega
and the Rademacher theorem that
\Omega_{0}\in\mathcal{L}^{n}
and
\mu(\Omega\setminus\Omega_{0})=0
. Let
x,y\in\Omega
and
x\neq y
. By Lemma
3.2
, there exist sequences
(x^{k})\subset\Omega
,
(y^{k})\subset\Omega
such that
x^{k}\to x
,
y^{k}\to y
and

\lambda\left(\{t\in[0,1]:x^{k}+t(y^{k}-x^{k})\in\Omega_{0}\}\right)=1\quad\text{for all }\;k\in{\rm I\!N}.

Consider the function
f:[0,1]\to{\rm I\!R}
given by
f(t):=\langle\nabla\varphi(x^{k}+t(y^{k}-x^{k})),y^{k}-x^{k}\rangle
for all
t\in[0,1]
. Applying the Newton-Leibniz formula to this function, we obtain

\displaystyle\langle\nabla\varphi(y^{k})-\nabla\varphi(x^{k}),y^{k}-x^{k}\rangle

\displaystyle=f(1)-f(0)=\int_{0}^{1}f^{\prime}(t)dt

\displaystyle=\int_{T^{k}}\langle\nabla^{2}\varphi(x^{k}+t(y^{k}-x^{k}))(y^{k}-x^{k}),y^{k}-x^{k}\rangle dt

(3.12)

where
T^{k}:=\{t\in[0,1]|\;x^{k}+t(y^{k}-x^{k})\in\Omega_{0}\}
. Moreover, for any
t\in T^{k}
, we have
x^{k}+t(y^{k}-x^{k})\in\Omega_{0}
, which implies that

\langle\nabla^{2}\varphi(x^{k}+t(y^{k}-x^{k}))(y^{k}-x^{k}),y^{k}-x^{k}\rangle\geq 0\quad\text{for all }\;t\in T^{k}.

(3.13)

Combining (
3
) and (
3.13
), we have
\langle\nabla\varphi(y^{k})-\nabla\varphi(x^{k}),y^{k}-x^{k}\rangle\geq 0
for all
k\in{\rm I\!N}
. Letting
k\to\infty
, we obtain the inequality
\langle\nabla\varphi(y)-\nabla\varphi(x),y-x\rangle\geq 0
, which justifies the convexity of
\varphi
on
\Omega
.

In the case where the given function
\varphi
is nonsmooth, we can obtain the characterization for the convexity of
\varphi
via subgradient graphical derivatives if we assume further that
\varphi
is weakly convex. To proceed with the proof of this result, we recall the following characterization of convex functions via Moreau envelopes taken from
[
41
, Theorem 3.17]
.

Lemma 3.4

(
characterization of convex functions via Moreau envelopes).

Suppose that
\varphi:{\rm I\!R}^{n}\to\overline{{\rm I\!R}}
is a proper, l.s.c. and prox-bounded function with
threshold
\lambda_{\varphi}>0
. Then
\varphi
is convex if and only if the Moreau envelope
e_{\lambda}\varphi
is convex for some
\lambda\in(0,\lambda_{\varphi})
.

Now we are ready to derive the main result of this section that establishes the characterization of convexity via subgradient graphical derivatives under the additional weak convexity.

Theorem 3.5

(
characterization of nonsmooth convex functions via subgradient graphical derivatives).

Let
\varphi:{\rm I\!R}^{n}\to\overline{{\rm I\!R}}
be a proper, l.s.c function. Then
\varphi
is convex if and only if
\varphi
is weakly convex and condition (
3.6
) is satisfied.

[
Proof.]
By Theorem
3.1
, we only need to show that
\varphi
is convex if it is weakly convex and condition (
3.6
) is satisfied. Using Proposition
2.1
, the weak convexity of
\varphi
tells us that
\varphi
is prox-bounded with threshold
\lambda_{\varphi}>0
, the Moreau envelope
e_{\lambda}\varphi
is continuously differentiable with Lipschitzian derivative on
{\rm I\!R}^{n}
for any sufficiently small
\lambda\in(0,\lambda_{\varphi})
, and (
2.9
) is satisfied with
U_{\lambda}={\rm I\!R}^{n}
. Pick any such
\lambda
, we next show that
e_{\lambda}\varphi
is convex. To proceed, we will verify the convexity of
e_{\lambda}\varphi
on
{\rm I\!R}^{n}
by using Theorem
3.3
. This means that we need to show the following condition holds

\langle z,u\rangle\geq 0\quad\text{for all }\;z\in\partial^{2}_{D}e_{\lambda}\varphi(x)(u),\;x\in{\rm I\!R}^{n},\;u\in{\rm I\!R}^{n}.

(3.14)

Indeed, let
x\in{\rm I\!R}^{n}
,
u\in{\rm I\!R}^{n}
and
z\in\partial^{2}_{D}e_{\lambda}\varphi(x)(u)
. By the definition of the subgradient graphical derivative in (

) and formula (

), we have
u\in D(\nabla e_{\lambda}\varphi)^{-1}(\nabla e_{\lambda}\varphi(x),x)(z)
. Moreover, by using the graphical derivative sum rule from
[
12
, Proposition 4A.2]
, we get

\displaystyle{D}(\nabla e_{\lambda}\varphi)^{-1}(\nabla e_{\lambda}\varphi(x),x)(z)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle D(\lambda I+(\partial\varphi)^{-1})(\nabla e_{\lambda}\varphi(x),x)(z)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lambda z+D(\partial\varphi)^{-1}(\nabla e_{\lambda}\varphi(x),x-\lambda\nabla e_{\lambda}\varphi(x))(z).

It follows that

u-\lambda z\in D(\partial\varphi)^{-1}(\nabla e_{\lambda}\varphi(x),x-\lambda\nabla e_{\lambda}\varphi(x))(z).

In other words,

z\in(D\partial\varphi)(x-\lambda\nabla e_{\lambda}\varphi(x),\nabla e_{\lambda}\varphi(x))(u-\lambda z).

Using (

), we obtain the inequality

\langle z,u-\lambda z\rangle\geq 0
and so
\langle z,u\rangle\geq 0
, which justifies (

is convex by Theorem

. Using Lemma
3.4
, we deduce that
\varphi
is convex, which completes the proof of this theorem.

The following example demonstrates that, in the absence of weak convexity, the condition (
3.6
) does not guarantee that the function is convex.

Example 3.6
.

Consider the function
\varphi:{\rm I\!R}\to{\rm I\!R}
given by

\varphi(x)=\begin{cases}1&\text{if }\;x\neq 0,\\
0&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

By direct computations, it is not hard to get

\partial\varphi(x)=\begin{cases}\{0\}&\text{if }\;x\neq 0,\\
{\rm I\!R}&\text{if }\;x=0,\end{cases}

T_{{\rm\text{\rm gph}}\,\partial\varphi}(x,y)=\begin{cases}\{0\}\times{\rm I\!R}&\text{if }\;x=0,y\neq 0,\\
(\{0\}\times{\rm I\!R})\cup({\rm I\!R}\times\{0\})&\text{if }\;x=0,y=0,\\
{\rm I\!R}\times\{0\}&\text{if }\;x\neq 0,y=0,\end{cases}

which clearly imply that for each
(x,y)\in\mbox{\rm gph}\,\partial\varphi
, we have

\partial^{2}_{D}\varphi(x,y)(v)=\begin{cases}{\rm I\!R}&\text{if }\;v=0,\\
\{0\}&\text{if }\;v\neq 0.\end{cases}

(3.15)

Therefore, (
3.6
) holds. However,
\varphi
is not weakly convex, and thus it is also not convex.

Building on the above characterizations of convex functions, we now present the corresponding consequences for strongly convex functions.

Corollary 3.7

(
strong convexity via subgradient graphical derivatives).

Let
\Omega
be a nonempty open convex set in
{\rm I\!R}^{n}
and
\varphi:\Omega\to\overline{{\rm I\!R}}
be a proper, l.s.c function. Suppose that one of the following conditions holds:

(i)

\varphi
is differentiable, and
\nabla\varphi
is Lipschitz continuous on
\Omega
.

(ii)

\Omega={\rm I\!R}^{n}
, and
\varphi
is weakly convex.

Then
\varphi
is strongly convex with modulus
\kappa>0
if and only if

\langle z,w\rangle\geq\kappa\|w\|^{2}\quad\text{for all }\;z\in{\partial}^{2}_{D}\varphi(x,y)(w),\;x\in\Omega,\;(x,y)\in\mbox{\rm gph}\,\partial\varphi,\;w\in{\rm I\!R}^{n}.

(3.16)

[
Proof.]
Let
\kappa>0
and consider the function
\psi:{\rm I\!R}^{n}\to{\rm I\!R}
given by

\psi(x)=\varphi(x)-\frac{\kappa}{2}\|x\|^{2}\quad\text{for all }x\in{\rm I\!R}^{n}.

Using the sum rule for limiting subdifferentials taken from
[
28
, Proposition 1.30]
, we have

\partial\psi(x)=\partial\varphi(x)-\kappa x\quad\text{for all }x\in\Omega.

Using the sum rule for graphical derivatives
[
12
, Proposition 4A.2]
, we have

\partial^{2}_{D}\varphi(x,y)(w)=\partial^{2}_{D}\psi(x,y-\kappa x)(w)+\kappa w,\;\text{for all }\;x\in\Omega,\;(x,y)\in\mbox{\rm gph}\,\partial\varphi,\;w\in{\rm I\!R}^{n}.

(3.17)

Note that
\varphi
is strongly convex on
\Omega
with modulus
\kappa>0
if and only if
\psi
is convex on
\Omega
. Due to the validity of (i) or (ii), we deduce from Theorem
3.3
and Theorem
3.5
that the strong convexity of
\psi
on
\Omega
is equivalent to the condition

\langle u,w\rangle\geq 0\quad\text{for all }\;u\in{\partial}^{2}_{D}\psi(x,v)(w),\;x\in\Omega,\;(x,v)\in\mbox{\rm gph}\,\partial\psi,\;w\in{\rm I\!R}^{n}.

It follows from (
3.17
) that the above condition is equivalent to (
3.16
), which completes the proof.

4
Characterizations via Second Subderivatives

This section is devoted to employing the
second subderivatives
to characterize the convexity of nonsmooth, l.s.c. functions. The concept of second subderivatives was introduced by Rockafellar (see, e.g.,
[
35
,
36
]
) and later developed for calculus rules under weak constraint qualification conditions in
[
25
]
, as well as for Newton-type methods in
[
31
]
. To proceed, for
\bar{x}\in{\rm I\!R}^{n},\bar{v}\in{\rm I\!R}^{n},
we consider the family of second-order finite differences

\Delta^{2}_{\tau}\varphi(\bar{x},\bar{v})(u):=\frac{\varphi(\bar{x}+\tau u)-\varphi(\bar{x})-\tau\langle\bar{v},u\rangle}{\frac{1}{2}\tau^{2}}

and define the
second subderivative
of
\varphi
at
\bar{x}
for
\bar{v}\in{\rm I\!R}^{n}
and
w\in{\rm I\!R}^{n}
by

d^{2}\varphi(\bar{x},\bar{v})(w):=\liminf_{\tau\downarrow 0\atop u\to w}\Delta^{2}_{\tau}\varphi(\bar{x},\bar{v})(u).

(4.1)

Then
\varphi
is said to be
twice epi-differentiable
at
\bar{x}
for
v
if for every
w\in{\rm I\!R}^{n}
and every choice of
\tau_{k}\downarrow 0
there exists a sequence
w^{k}\to w
such that
\Delta^{2}_{\tau_{k}}\varphi(\bar{x},\bar{v})(w^{k})\to d^{2}\varphi(\bar{x},v)(w)
as
k\to\infty.

Twice epi-differentiability has been recognized as an important concept of second-order variational analysis with numerous applications to optimization; see the aforementioned monograph by Rockafellar and Wets
[
37
]
. In the case where
\varphi
is twice epi-differentiable, and prox-regular at
\bar{x}
for
\bar{v}\in\partial\varphi(\bar{x})
, it is known from
[
37
, Theorem 13.40]
that

\partial^{2}_{D}\varphi(\bar{x},\bar{v})(w)=\partial h\quad\text{with }\;h=\frac{1}{2}d^{2}\varphi(\bar{x},\bar{v}).

(4.2)

We next introduce the notion of
generalized twice differentiability
of functions, originally proposed in
[
38
]
. This concept has been the subject of renewed investigation in recent works
[
11
,
23
,
24
]
. Given a quadratic matrix
A\in{\rm I\!R}^{n\times n}
, denote the
quadratic form
associated with the matrix
A
by

q_{A}(w):=\langle w,Aw\rangle\;\mbox{ for all }\;w\in{\rm I\!R}^{n}.

(4.3)

A function
q:{\rm I\!R}^{n}\to\overline{{\rm I\!R}}
is called a
generalized quadratic form
if it is expressible as
q=q_{A}+\delta_{L}
, where
L
is a linear subspace of
{\rm I\!R}^{n}
, and where
A\in{\rm I\!R}^{n\times n}
is a symmetric matrix. It is said that
\varphi:{\rm I\!R}^{n}\to\overline{{\rm I\!R}}
is
generalized twice differentiable
at
\bar{x}
for
\bar{v}\in\partial\varphi(\bar{x})
if it is twice epi-differentiable at
\bar{x}
for
\bar{v}
and if the second-order subderivative
d^{2}\varphi(\bar{x},\bar{v})
is a generalized quadratic form.
We next present the following new characterizations of convex functions using second subderivatives.

Theorem 4.1

(
convexity of functions via second subderivatives).

Let
\varphi:{\rm I\!R}^{n}\to\overline{{\rm I\!R}}
be a proper, l.s.c and weakly convex function. Then the following assertions are equivalent:

(i)

\varphi
is convex.

(ii)

For all
w\in{\rm I\!R}^{n}
, we have
d^{2}\varphi(x,v)(w)\geq 0
for all
(x,v)\in\mbox{\rm gph}\,\partial\varphi
.

(iii)

For all
w\in{\rm I\!R}^{n}
, we have
d^{2}\varphi(x,v)(w)\geq 0
for all
(x,v)\in\mbox{\rm gph}\,\partial\varphi
such that
\varphi
is twice epi-differentiable at
x
for
v
.

(iv)

For all
w\in{\rm I\!R}^{n}
, we have
d^{2}\varphi(x,v)(w)\geq 0
for all
(x,v)\in\mbox{\rm gph}\,\partial\varphi
such that
\varphi
is generalized twice differentiable at
x
for
v
.

[
Proof.]
The implication [(i)
\Longrightarrow
(ii)] follows from
[
37
, Proposition 13.20]
. It is obvious that (ii)
\Longrightarrow
(iii)
\Longrightarrow
(iv), and thus it remains only to verify the implication [(iv)
\Longrightarrow
(i)]. Indeed, using Proposition
2.1
, the weak convexity of
\varphi
tells us that
\varphi
is prox-bounded with threshold
\lambda_{\varphi}>0
, the Moreau envelope
e_{\lambda}\varphi
is continuously differentiable with Lipschitzian derivative on
{\rm I\!R}^{n}
for any sufficiently small
\lambda\in(0,\lambda_{\varphi})
, and (
2.9
) is satisfied with
U_{\lambda}={\rm I\!R}^{n}
. Pick any such
\lambda
, we next show that
e_{\lambda}\varphi
is convex. To proceed, fix any
u\in{\rm I\!R}^{n}
such that
e_{\lambda}\varphi
is twice differentiable at
u
, we will verify that
\nabla^{2}e_{\lambda}\varphi(u)
is positive semidefinite. Putting

x:=u-\lambda\nabla e_{\lambda}\varphi(u)\quad\text{and }\;v:=\nabla e_{\lambda}\varphi(u),

we deduce from (
2.9
) that
(x,v)\in\mbox{\rm gph}\,\partial\varphi
, and
u=x+\lambda v
. It follows from
[
24
, Theorem 5.8]
that
\varphi
is generalized twice differentiable at
x
for
v
. Since (iv) holds, we have
d^{2}\varphi(x,v)\geq 0
. It follows from
[
24
, Corollary 5.9]
that

e_{\lambda}\left(\frac{1}{2}d^{2}\varphi(x,v)\right)(w)=d^{2}\left(\frac{1}{2}e_{\lambda}\varphi\right)\left(x+\lambda v,\frac{1}{2}v\right)(w)=\frac{1}{2}\langle\nabla^{2}e_{\lambda}\varphi(u)w,w\rangle\quad\text{for all }\;w\in{\rm I\!R}^{n},

which implies that
\nabla^{2}e_{\lambda}\varphi(u)
is positive semidefinite since
d^{2}\varphi(x,v)\geq 0
. Therefore,
e_{\lambda}\varphi
is convex by Theorem
3.3
. Applying Lemma
3.4
, we conclude that
\varphi
is convex, which completes the proof.

Using the above characterizations of convex functions, we derive the corresponding results for strongly convex functions through second subderivatives.

Corollary 4.2

(
strong convexity via second subderivatives).

Let
\varphi:{\rm I\!R}^{n}\to\overline{{\rm I\!R}}
be a proper, l.s.c and weakly convex function. Then the following assertions are equivalent:

(i)

\varphi
is strongly convex with modulus
\kappa>0
.

(ii)

For all
w\in{\rm I\!R}^{n}
, we have
d^{2}\varphi(x,v)(w)\geq\kappa\|w\|^{2}
for all
(x,v)\in\mbox{\rm gph}\,\partial\varphi
.

(iii)

For all
w\in{\rm I\!R}^{n}
, we have
d^{2}\varphi(x,v)(w)\geq\kappa\|w\|^{2}
for all
(x,v)\in\mbox{\rm gph}\,\partial\varphi
such that
\varphi
is twice epi-differentiable at
x
for
v
.

(iv)

For all
w\in{\rm I\!R}^{n}
, we have
d^{2}\varphi(x,v)(w)\geq\kappa\|w\|^{2}
for all
(x,v)\in\mbox{\rm gph}\,\partial\varphi
such that
\varphi
is generalized twice differentiable at
x
for
v
.

[
Proof.]
We first verify the implication [(i)
\Longrightarrow
(ii)]. Indeed, suppose that
\varphi
is strongly convex with modulus
\kappa>0
, and consider the function
\psi:{\rm I\!R}^{n}\to{\rm I\!R}
given by

\psi(x)=\varphi(x)-\frac{\kappa}{2}\|x\|^{2}\quad\text{for all }x\in{\rm I\!R}^{n}.

(4.4)

Using the sum rule for limiting subdifferentials taken from
[
28
, Proposition 1.30]
, we have

\partial\psi(x)=\partial\varphi(x)-\kappa x\quad\text{for all }x\in{\rm I\!R}^{n}.

Using the sum rule for second subderivatives in
[
24
, Proposition 4.8]
, we obtain the equality

d^{2}\varphi(x,v)(w)=d^{2}\psi(x,v-\kappa x)(w)+\kappa\|w\|^{2}\quad\text{for all }\;(x,v)\in\mbox{\rm gph}\,\partial\varphi,\;w\in{\rm I\!R}^{n}.

(4.5)

Since
\varphi
is strongly convex with modulus
\kappa
, it follows that
\psi
is convex, and thus
d^{2}\psi(x,y)(w)\geq 0
for all
(x,y)\in\mbox{\rm gph}\,\partial\psi
and
w\in{\rm I\!R}^{n}
by Theorem
4.1
. Combining the latter with (
4.5
), we obtain (ii).

It is obvious that (ii)
\Longrightarrow
(iii)
\Longrightarrow
(iv), and thus it remains only to verify [(iv)
\Longrightarrow
(i)]. Indeed, suppose that (iv) holds. Consider
\psi
given in (
4.4
), and we show that
\psi
is convex by using Theorem
4.1
. To proceed, fix any
w\in{\rm I\!R}^{n}
,
(x,y)\in\mbox{\rm gph}\,\partial\psi
such that
\psi
is generalized twice differentiable at
x
for
y
.
Using
[
24
, Proposition 4.8]
, we deduce that
\varphi
is generalized twice differentiable at
x
for
y+\kappa x\in\partial\varphi(x)
. By (iv), we deduce that
d^{2}\varphi(x,y+\kappa x)(w)\geq\kappa\|w\|^{2}
. Combining this with (
4.5
), it follows that
d^{2}\psi(x,y)(w)\geq 0
, and thus
\psi
is convex by Theorem
4.1
. This means that
\varphi
is convex, which completes the proof.

5
Characterizations via Second-Order Subdifferentials

In this section, we employ
second-order subdifferentials
to provide further characterizations of convexity for extended-real-valued functions. The concept of second-order subdifferentials was introduced by Mordukhovich in
[
26
]
and has since been extensively developed to establish calculus rules for broad classes of extended-real-valued functions, derive optimality conditions, and characterize convex and generalized convex functions; see, e.g.,
[
2
,
13
,
14
,
15
,
16
,
18
,
30
,
34
,
39
]
and the references therein. The application of second-order subdifferentials to generalized Newton methods and their use in practical areas such as machine learning, statistics, and image processing have also been investigated in
[
17
,
19
,
20
,
21
,
31
]
.

Along with the notions of subdifferentials and normal cones defined in Section
2
, we need some other constructions recalled as follows. First, we recall two notions of coderivatives for a multifunction
F\colon{\rm I\!R}^{n}\rightrightarrows{\rm I\!R}^{m}
. Using the normal cones (
2.2
) and (
2.4
) to the graph of
F
, we define the
regular coderivative
and the
limiting coderivative
of
F
at
(\bar{x},\bar{y})\in\mbox{\rm gph}\,F
, respectively, by

\widehat{D}^{*}F(\bar{x},\bar{y})(v):=\big\{u\in{\rm I\!R}^{n}\;\big|\;(u,-v)\in\widehat{N}_{{\rm gph}\,F}(\bar{x},\bar{y})\big\},\quad v\in{\rm I\!R}^{m},

(5.1)

D^{*}F(\bar{x},\bar{y})(v):=\big\{u\in{\rm I\!R}^{n}\;\big|\;(u,-v)\in N_{{\rm gph}\,F}(\bar{x},\bar{y})\big\},\quad v\in{\rm I\!R}^{m}.

(5.2)

Invoking the coderivatives (
5.1
) and (
5.2
) applied to the limiting subdifferential (
2.5
), we get the second-order subdifferential constructions for
\varphi\colon{\rm I\!R}^{n}\to\overline{{\rm I\!R}}
defined by

\breve{\partial}^{2}\varphi(\bar{x},\bar{v})(u):=(\widehat{D}^{*}\partial\varphi)(\bar{x},\bar{v})(u)\;\mbox{ and }\;{\partial}^{2}\varphi(\bar{x},\bar{v})(u):=({D}^{*}{\partial}\varphi)(\bar{x},\bar{v})(u),\quad u\in{\rm I\!R}^{n},

(5.3)

known as the
combined second-order subdifferential
and the
limiting second-order subdifferential
of
\varphi
at
\bar{x}
relative to
\bar{v}\in\partial\varphi(\bar{x})
, respectively. We next present the following new characterizations of convex functions using second-order subdifferentials.

Theorem 5.1

(
convexity of functions via second-order subdifferentials).

Let
\varphi:{\rm I\!R}^{n}\to\overline{{\rm I\!R}}
be a proper, l.s.c function. Then the following assertions are equivalent:

(i)

\varphi
is convex.

(ii)

\varphi
is weakly convex and

\langle z,w\rangle\geq 0\quad\text{for all }\;z\in\breve{\partial}^{2}\varphi(x,y)(w),\;(x,y)\in\mbox{\rm gph}\,\partial\varphi,\;w\in{\rm I\!R}^{n}.

(iii)

\varphi
is weakly convex and

\langle z,w\rangle\geq 0\quad\text{for all }\;z\in{\partial}^{2}\varphi(x,y)(w),\;(x,y)\in\mbox{\rm gph}\,\partial\varphi,\;w\in{\rm I\!R}^{n}.

[
Proof.]
We show the implications [(i)
\Longrightarrow
(iii)
\Longrightarrow
(ii)
\Longrightarrow
(i)]. The implication [(i)
\Longrightarrow
(iii)] follows immediately
[
2
, Theorem 3.2]
. By the following inclusions

\breve{\partial}^{2}\varphi(x,y)(w)\subset\breve{\partial}^{2}\varphi(x,y)(w)\quad\text{for all }(x,y)\in\mbox{\rm gph}\,\partial\varphi,\;w\in{\rm I\!R}^{n},

we obtain the implication [(iii)
\Longrightarrow
(ii)].
It remains to verify the implication [(ii)
\Longrightarrow
(i)]. Using Proposition
2.1
, the weak convexity of
\varphi
tells us that
\varphi
is prox-bounded with threshold
\lambda_{\varphi}>0
, the Moreau envelope
e_{\lambda}\varphi
is continuously differentiable with Lipschitzian derivative on
{\rm I\!R}^{n}
for any sufficiently small
\lambda\in(0,\lambda_{\varphi})
, and (
2.9
) is satisfied with
U_{\lambda}={\rm I\!R}^{n}
. Pick any such
\lambda
, we next show that
e_{\lambda}\varphi
is convex. To proceed, we will verify the convexity of
e_{\lambda}\varphi
on
{\rm I\!R}^{n}
by
[
6
, Theorem 3.1]
. This means that we need to show the following condition holds

\langle z,u\rangle\geq 0\quad\text{for all }\;z\in\breve{\partial}^{2}e_{\lambda}\varphi(x)(u),\;x\in{\rm I\!R}^{n},\;u\in{\rm I\!R}^{n}.

(5.4)

Indeed, let
x\in{\rm I\!R}^{n}
,
u\in{\rm I\!R}^{n}
and
z\in\breve{\partial}^{2}e_{\lambda}\varphi(x)(u)
, which implies that

-u\in\widehat{D}^{*}(\nabla e_{\lambda}\varphi)^{-1}(\nabla e_{\lambda}\varphi(x),x)(-z).

Moreover, by using the sum rule from
[
27
, Theorem 1.62]
, we get

\displaystyle\widehat{D}^{*}(\nabla e_{\lambda}\varphi)^{-1}(\nabla e_{\lambda}\varphi(x),x)(-z)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\widehat{D}^{*}(\lambda I+(\partial\varphi)^{-1})(\nabla e_{\lambda}\varphi(x),x)(-z)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\lambda z+\widehat{D}^{*}(\partial\varphi)^{-1}(\nabla e_{\lambda}\varphi(x),x-\lambda\nabla e_{\lambda}\varphi(x))(-z).

It follows that

-u+\lambda z\in\widehat{D}^{*}(\partial\varphi)^{-1}(\nabla e_{\lambda}\varphi(x),x-\lambda\nabla e_{\lambda}\varphi(x))(-z).

In other words,

z\in(\widehat{D}^{*}\partial\varphi)(x-\lambda\nabla e_{\lambda}\varphi(x),\nabla e_{\lambda}\varphi(x))(u-\lambda z).

Using (ii), we obtain the inequality

\langle z,u-\lambda z\rangle\geq 0
and so
\langle z,u\rangle\geq 0
, which justifies (
5.4
). Hence,
e_{\lambda}\varphi
is convex. Using Lemma
3.4
, we deduce that
\varphi
is convex, which completes the proof of this theorem.

The preceding characterizations of convex functions allow us to formulate equivalent statements for strongly convex functions in terms of second-order subdifferentials.

Corollary 5.2

(
strong convexity via second-order subdifferentials).

Let
\varphi:{\rm I\!R}^{n}\to\overline{{\rm I\!R}}
be a proper, l.s.c function. Then the following assertions are equivalent:

(i)

\varphi
is strongly convex with modulus
\kappa>0
.

(ii)

\varphi
is weakly convex and

\langle z,w\rangle\geq\kappa\|w\|^{2}\quad\text{for all }\;z\in\breve{\partial}^{2}\varphi(x,y)(w),\;(x,y)\in\mbox{\rm gph}\,\partial\varphi,\;w\in{\rm I\!R}^{n}.

(iii)

\varphi
is weakly convex and

\langle z,w\rangle\geq\kappa\|w\|^{2}\quad\text{for all }\;z\in{\partial}^{2}\varphi(x,y)(w),\;(x,y)\in\mbox{\rm gph}\,\partial\varphi,\;w\in{\rm I\!R}^{n}.

[
Proof.]
The implication [(i)
\Longrightarrow
(iii)] was proved in
[
2
, Theorem 5.1]
, and the implication [(iii)
\Longrightarrow
(ii)] is obvious. We now verify [(ii)
\Longrightarrow
(i)]. Indeed, suppose that (ii) holds, and consider the function
\psi:{\rm I\!R}^{n}\to{\rm I\!R}
given by

\psi(x)=\varphi(x)-\frac{\kappa}{2}\|x\|^{2}\quad\text{for all }x\in{\rm I\!R}^{n}.

(5.5)

Using the sum rule for limiting subdifferentials taken from
[
28
, Proposition 1.30]
, we have

\partial\psi(x)=\partial\varphi(x)-\kappa x\quad\text{for all }x\in{\rm I\!R}^{n}.

Using the sum rule for regular coderivatives in
[
27
, Theorem 1.62]
, we deduce that

\breve{\partial}^{2}\psi(x,v)(w)=\breve{\partial}^{2}\varphi(x,v+\kappa x)(w)-\kappa w,\;\text{for all }\;(x,v)\in\mbox{\rm gph}\,\partial\psi,\;w\in{\rm I\!R}^{n}.

(5.6)

Combining (
5.6
) with (ii), we have

\langle z,w\rangle\geq 0\quad\text{for all }\;z\in\breve{\partial}^{2}\psi(x,v)(w),\;(x,v)\in\mbox{\rm gph}\,\partial\psi,\;w\in{\rm I\!R}^{n}.

Using Theorem
5.1
, we obtain the convexity of
\psi
, which implies that
\varphi
is strongly convex with modulus
\kappa
. The proof is complete.

Remark 5.3

(
discussions on known characterizations of convexity via second-order subdifferentials).

The convexity characterizations obtained in Theorem
5.1
and Corollary
5.2
unify and extend several earlier results that were derived by different approaches and techniques. In particular, they recover the main results of
[
2
]
for
\mathcal{C}^{1,1}
functions and those of
[
7
]
for lower-
\mathcal{C}^{2}
functions. We elaborate as follows:

(i)

A function
\varphi:U\to{\rm I\!R}
, where
U
is an open subset of
{\rm I\!R}^{n}
, is called
lower-
\mathcal{C}^{2}
on
U
if for every
\bar{x}\in U
there exists a neighborhood
V
of
\bar{x}
such that

\varphi(x)=\max_{t\in T}\varphi_{t}(x),

where each function
\varphi_{t}
is of class
\mathcal{C}^{2}
on
V
, the index set
T
is compact, and
\varphi_{t}
together with its first- and second-order partial derivatives depends continuously on
(x,t)\in V\times T
. According to
[
9
, Theorem 3.2 and Remark 3.3]
, if
\varphi
is lower-
\mathcal{C}^{2}
on a nonempty open set
\Omega\subset{\rm I\!R}^{n}
, then
\varphi
is weakly convex on
\Omega
. Consequently, Theorem
5.1
and Corollary
5.2
recover
[
7
, Theorem 4.1 and Corollary 4.3]
.

(ii)

By
[
37
, Proposition 13.34]
, every
\mathcal{C}^{1,1}
-smooth function is lower-
\mathcal{C}^{2}
. Combining this fact with part
(i)
yields that such functions are weakly convex. Therefore, our results also recover
[
2
, Theorem 4.2 and Theorem 5.2(i)]
.

6
Conclusion

In this paper, we have developed new characterizations of convexity for nonsmooth extended real valued functions. By employing three second order generalized derivatives, namely graphical derivatives, second subderivatives, and second order subdifferentials, we established necessary and sufficient conditions for convexity in the nonsmooth setting. These results not only enhance the theoretical understanding of convexity in nonsmooth optimization, but also provide a foundation for the design and convergence analysis of numerical algorithms in optimization and variational analysis. Future research will explore further applications of these characterizations in algorithm development and extend the framework to broader classes of nonsmooth functions.
