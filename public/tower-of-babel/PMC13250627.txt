Disseminated Thoracic Methicillin‐Resistant Staphylococcus aureus Infection Mimicking Tuberculosis in a Patient on Haemodialysis
Authors: Evans Nii Ayitey MacCready, Emmanuel Larbi‐Kwapong, Isaac Anokye‐Manu, Romeo Yeboah Boaheng, Emmanuel Eshun, Papa Kojo Mbroh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAn 18‐year‐old male receiving haemodialysis through a central venous catheter presented with fever, productive cough, night sweats and progressive dyspnoea. Point‐of‐care ultrasound demonstrated bilateral complex pleural effusions, lung consolidation and a complex pericardial effusion. Initial management targeted severe bacterial sepsis, but computed tomography performed after clinical stabilization revealed bilateral consolidations, cavitary lung lesions and cavitating pulmonary nodules, raising concern for disseminated tuberculosis. GeneXpert testing of sputum, pleural fluid and pericardial fluid was negative, while blood, pleural fluid and catheter tip cultures grew methicillin‐resistant Staphylococcus aureus . Despite pleural and pericardial drainage and intravenous vancomycin therapy, the patient deteriorated and died from sepsis. This case highlights how disseminated thoracic MRSA infection may mimic tuberculosis and underscores the value of bedside ultrasound in identifying multi‐compartment thoracic suppuration when advanced imaging is delayed.

Introduction

Methicillin‐resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA) is an important cause of necrotizing pneumonia and cavitary pulmonary disease [1]. Concurrent empyema thoracis and purulent pericarditis are uncommon and associated with high mortality [2]. Cavitary pulmonary disease caused by MRSA may closely mimic pulmonary tuberculosis, posing a diagnostic challenge in tuberculosis‐endemic settings [1].

Point‐of‐care ultrasound (POCUS) is increasingly used in respiratory and critical care practice for bedside evaluation of pleural and pericardial disease [3, 4]. This is particularly useful in resource‐limited settings where access to advanced imaging may be constrained.

We report a case of disseminated thoracic MRSA infection in a patient receiving haemodialysis, in whom the clinical presentation and radiological findings of cavitary lung disease with pleural and pericardial involvement raised concerns for disseminated tuberculosis.

Case Report

An 18‐year‐old male with end‐stage kidney disease secondary to focal segmental glomerulosclerosis presented with a 6‐day history of fever, cough productive of blood‐stained sputum, night sweats and progressive dyspnoea. He had commenced haemodialysis through a central venous catheter 3 weeks before presentation.

On examination, he was febrile at 38.9°C, tachycardic at 124 beats/min, tachypnoeic at 32 breaths/min and hypoxic with oxygen saturation of 88% on room air. Chest examination demonstrated findings consistent with bilateral pleural effusions, with bronchial breath sounds over the right upper and middle lung zones.

Bedside POCUS demonstrated bilateral complex pleural effusions with internal echoes and echogenic debris, bilateral lung consolidation with air bronchograms and a concurrent complex pericardial effusion without tamponade physiology (Figure 1). Cardiac contractility was preserved with no valvular vegetations.

(B) Cardiac ultrasound view demonstrating complex pericardial effusion (arrow). (C) Right lung consolidation with air bronchograms (arrow) and adjacent complex pleural effusion (asterisk). (D) Complex pleural effusion with echogenic debris (arrow), suggestive of empyema.

Ultrasound‐guided thoracocentesis yielded purulent fluid consistent with empyema. Given the presence of a central venous catheter as a potential source of infection, the catheter was removed and sent for culture, and empirical intravenous vancomycin and levofloxacin were commenced, with renal dose adjustment. Bilateral chest tubes drained 3.5 and 2.6 L of purulent fluid from the right and left pleural space, respectively, while approximately 500 mL of purulent pericardial fluid was also drained over 4 days. Full blood count revealed leukocytosis (13.08 × 109/L) with neutrophilia, and severe normocytic anaemia (haemoglobin 5.7 g/dL). HIV testing was negative.

Due to clinical instability requiring intensive care admission and the logistical difficulty of transport to the radiology unit, further chest imaging was deferred. The chest tubes dislodged on Day 4. Contrast‐enhanced chest computed tomography (CT) on Day 5 demonstrated bilateral consolidations, multiple cavitary lung lesions, cavitating pulmonary nodules and bilateral hydropneumothorax, the latter likely related to drain displacement (Figure 2). While the patient was initially managed for severe bacterial sepsis, the CT findings, together with constitutional symptoms and pleuropericardial involvement, raised concern for disseminated tuberculosis with tuberculous empyema and pericarditis.

Contrast‐enhanced chest computed tomography demonstrating bilateral pulmonary consolidations, multiple cavitary lung lesions, pulmonary nodules and bilateral hydropneumothorax, initially raising suspicion for pulmonary tuberculosis.

However, GeneXpert testing of sputum, pleural fluid and pericardial fluid was negative. Peripheral blood, pleural fluid and catheter tip cultures grew MRSA sensitive to vancomycin, gentamicin, amikacin and ciprofloxacin, favouring disseminated MRSA infection as the unifying diagnosis. The cavitating pulmonary nodules may also have represented septic pulmonary emboli. Despite antimicrobial therapy, re‐insertion of chest tubes and continued drainage, the patient deteriorated and died from sepsis on Day 7 of admission.

Discussion

This case demonstrates severe thoracic MRSA infection involving the lungs, bilateral pleural spaces and pericardium in a patient receiving haemodialysis through a central venous catheter. Positive blood, pleural fluid and catheter tip cultures suggested catheter‐related bloodstream infection as the likely source [5], with subsequent spread resulting in cavitary pulmonary infection and pleuropericardial suppuration [1, 2]. The cavitating pulmonary nodules may also have represented septic pulmonary emboli [1].

A major diagnostic challenge arose after CT imaging revealed cavitary lung lesions and pulmonary nodules in a patient with fever, night sweats and pleuropericardial suppuration. In a tuberculosis‐endemic setting, these findings raised concern for disseminated tuberculosis. However, while cavitary lesions and pleuropericardial involvement may occur in both MRSA infection and tuberculosis, the acute and fulminant clinical course was more suggestive of severe bacterial infection than the typically indolent progression of pulmonary tuberculosis over weeks to months. Cavitary pulmonary disease may also occur in fungal infection, septic pulmonary embolic disease and other causes of necrotizing bacterial pneumonia [1]. The overlap in clinical and radiological findings highlights the limitations of using these features alone to reliably distinguish between these conditions. The negative GeneXpert testing from sputum, pleural fluid and pericardial fluid, together with the isolation of MRSA from blood, pleural fluid and catheter tip cultures, favoured disseminated MRSA infection as the more likely unifying diagnosis, underscoring the importance of microbiological confirmation in these scenarios.

POCUS enabled early recognition of complex pleural and pericardial collections. The identification of complex fluid with internal echoes facilitated prompt drainage and escalation of therapy. POCUS is increasingly recognized as a useful tool for the evaluation and management of pleural infection, including characterization of complex effusions and guidance of pleural drainage procedures [3, 4]. Concurrent pleural empyema and purulent pericarditis remain uncommon and are associated with substantial mortality [2].

Despite drainage and targeted antimicrobial therapy, the patient died from progressive sepsis, underscoring the aggressive nature of disseminated MRSA thoracic infection, particularly in patients with end‐stage kidney disease. This case highlights the importance of a broad differential diagnosis in patients presenting with extensive pleuropulmonary and pericardial disease. While tuberculosis remains an important consideration in endemic settings, clinical and radiological findings alone may not reliably distinguish it from severe bacterial infection. Comprehensive microbiological evaluation is therefore essential to establish the correct diagnosis and guide appropriate management. POCUS can facilitate early recognition and drainage of suppurative thoracic collections while definitive microbiological investigations are pursued.
