Use continuance intention of technology: an examination of the Med4All system in selected health facilities in Ghana
Authors: Gordon Abekah-Nkrumah, Maxwell Antwi, Alex Yao Attachey, Tobias F Rinke de Wit, James Duah, Grace Adjei Okai, Emmanuel Anongeba Anaba, Gifty Sunkwa-Mills
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundGhana has over the years implemented interventions to improve on its medicines supply chain. This notwithstanding, significant gaps exist in access to affordable and quality medicines in general, and for non-communicable diseases in particular. The study examined the level and antecedents of use continuance of a digital medicines supply chain system (Med4All) implemented by selected health facilities in Ghana.MethodsData (sample of 124 survey respondents reinforced with 56 respondents for qualitative interviews) on perceived usefulness, perceived ease of use, expectation confirmation, satisfaction, and attitudes toward use and use continuance intention of the Med4All were analysed via descriptive statistics and structural equation modelling for the survey data and thematic analysis for the qualitative interviews.ResultsResults indicate a high level of use continuance intention (3.94 out of 5), perceived usefulness, perceived ease of use, expectation confirmation, satisfaction with, and positive attitudes towards the Med4All system. In addition, perceived usefulness, perceived ease of use, satisfaction, and attitudes toward the use of the Med4All system were key factors associated use continuance intention. More importantly, the speed of transaction turnaround and availability of interest-free loans to health facilities constituted key features that can increase and sustain use continuance. There are however challenges, including delays in deliveries, poor communication between medicines suppliers and health facilities, payment challenges, delays in conducting post-supply surveillance of medicines, and in some cases, the unwillingness of health facility staff to use the Med4All system to procure medicines.ConclusionAlthough the level of satisfaction and use continuance intentions are high, failure to address identified challenges can result in increased dissatisfaction and consequently threaten the sustainability and survival of the Med4All system. Stakeholders are encouraged to address existing challenges as a matter of urgency. Additionally, the fast transaction turnaround and availability of interest-free loans, can be used as a major lever to further improve the Med4All system to speed-up adoption by new users and sustain use continuance.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12911-026-03625-8.Keywords: Adoption, Use continuance, Technology, Health facilities, Med4All

Background

The rising cost of healthcare constitute a major challenge in developed and developing countries [1, 2], with medicines constituting a significant driver of healthcare cost. For example, Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD) countries spend about 17% of their healthcare budget on pharmaceuticals [3, 4], with the situation being much more challenging in Low to Middle-Income Countries (LMIC), where escalating prices of medicines have much more negative implications on public health outcomes [5]. Ghana has over the years improved its medicines supply chain through the implementation of interventions such as the implementation of a pooled procurement system and framework contracting for medicines within the public sector and the National Catholic Health Service (NCHS). However, as in many LMICs [6, 7], Ghana has significant gaps in access to affordable and quality medicines in general, and for non-communicable diseases in particular [8]. There are indeed major challenges with Ghana’s medicines supply chain infrastructure and delivery systems, coupled with a generally weak and ineffective implementation of procurement regulations, which together affect the availability, affordability and accessibility of medicines, especially in public health facilities [8]. Ghana’s medicines availability, affordability and accessibility challenge is more acute in private health facilities where the cost of medicines constitutes between 40% and 80% of the total cost of healthcare, depending on where care is received [9].

To address what seems to be a dysfunctional health facility medicines supply chain system, PharmAccess Foundation (an international NGO), in partnership with the Medical Credit Fund (MCF) (an affiliate of PharmAccess Foundation that provides credit to health facilities), the Christian Health Association of Ghana (CHAG) (an umbrella organisation of health facilities owned by Christian religious bodies), selected medicines suppliers in Ghana and Fidelity Bank (an indigenous Ghanaian Bank), developed a digital online medicines supply chain system referred to as Med4All. Med4All connects medicine manufacturers and importers (referred to as ’suppliers’), healthcare facilities, providers of funds (Fidelity Bank and MCF), medicines regulators and policymakers in a manner that creates a more efficient and quality-controlled medicines supply chain. Activity flow in the Med4All can be categorised into six areas: (1) Request for medicines where the one in charge of medicines (normally the pharmacist), request for medicines. (2) Approval of requested medicines, where the head of the health facility, (normally a medical doctor) approves the requested medicines to be procured. (3) Spending approval, where the accountant of the health facility cross-checks the order with budget information to be sure that there is adequate funds on budget for the proposed request. (4) After approval from the accountant, an order is placed either by the requester or procurement officer depending on the health facility. (5) Suppliers bid on the Med4All platform based on availability of stock, accept and supply the order. (6) After supplying the order, the receiver (normally stores and inventory/procurement officer depending on the health facility) at the health facility confirms supply of the order in the Med4All system, and the supplier is paid by the provider of funds (normally MCF or the Bank). Health facilities later reimburse the provider of funds with the amount paid to suppliers on their behalf. The reimbursement does not happen in the Med4All system.

The Med4All system was deployed on a pilot basis at the beginning of 2020 within 29 CHAG health facilities and was completed before the end of 2020. However, full-scale deployment of the Med4All system began in the last quarter of 2020, first among CHAG health facilities, with the intention that it will be extended to private and subsequently government-owned health facilities. It is however important to point out that CHAG is only an umbrella organisation for the constituent health facilities and its decisions are not necessarily binding on constituent members. Thus, during the scale-up of Med4All, CHAG encouraged member health facilities to use the Med4All system. However, the decision to use the system is solely that of individual health facilities. This means that they could discontinue using the system if they were not satisfied with its performance. In addition, the Med4All system as indicated above, is used by different independent senior officers (e.g. the pharmacist and procurement officer, for requesting, the stores and inventory officer- for receiving, the head of health facility for approving and the accountant for budget availability) of the health facility. Unlike junior officers, senior officers may have the power to discontinue the use of a new system and find an alternative if they believe that the new system is not meeting their expectation. This means that in practice, some of the users of the Med4All system could abandon its use for an alternative considered more efficient in carrying out their functions. Also, given that suppliers on the Med4All system are the same suppliers health facilities were dealing with before the introduction of Med4All, the requester for example could contact such suppliers directly to supply needed medicines if they thought the Med4All was not being efficient.

The above characterisation of the Med4All system is consistent with the existing literature where it is suggested that the initial adoption of an innovation does not necessarily assure use continuance [10]. Thus, anticipated benefits of the Med4All will only become a reality if health facilities that have adopted it continue to use it [11–13]. There are instances where organizations have adopted an innovation but have not continued to use the innovation [14]. Several studies have examined reasons for use continuance of adopted innovation in telecommunications and mobile technology, health, transportation, etc [15–19]. However, most of these studies have relied on quantitative approaches, using structural equation models to examine factors that influence use continuance. The challenge with a quantitative approach in the context of the current study is that it will oversimplify what is a theoretically complex relationship, and therefore mask our understanding of the pathways via which underlying variables embedded in a construct affect another construct. For example, the key drivers of a construct such as satisfaction are likely to be diverse, that they are unlikely to be adequately captured in instruments used for quantitative data collection. However, knowing the multiplicity of variables influencing a construct and the pathways via which that happens (via qualitative interviews) is key for policy intervention, given that in practice, the focus is mostly on variables embedded in the constructs and not necessarily the constructs.

Therefore, the current study uses a survey, augmented by qualitative interviews to examine use continuance of the Med4All system. Specifically, the study examined:

Conceptualizing technology use continuance

The Technology Continuance Theory (TCT), developed by Lio et al. [10], was adapted to address the objectives of the study. The TCT is an integrated theory comprising three models: The Technology Acceptance Model (TAM), Expectation-Confirmation Model (ECM) and Cognitive Model (COGM) [20]. The TCT has five dimensions, namely perceived usefulness (PU), perceived ease of use (PEOU), confirmation (CONf), satisfaction (SATIS) and attitudes (ATT) [10]. The first two dimensions constitute the TAM, which was developed by Davis [21] based on Fishbein and Ajzen’s Theory of Reasoned Action [10]. The proponents of TAM argue that a person’s attitude towards a system is influenced by perceived usefulness of the system and perceived ease of use. Perceived usefulness refers to the prospective user’s subjective probability that using a specific system will increase job performance, while perceived ease of use is the degree to which the prospective user expects the use of the target system to be free of effort.

In addition, the ECM was developed by Bhattacherjee [22], who argued that an individual’s continuance use of a system after the initial adoption is influenced by satisfaction, which in turn is predicted by his/her confirmation of expectations and perceived usefulness of the system. Satisfaction refers to an individual’s post-consumption evaluation of a specific transaction, while confirmation is the extent to which proposed benefits attributed to using the system actualises after using the system. Further, the proponent of the COGM postulates that a person’s use continuance intentions of a system are determined by satisfaction and attitudes towards the system, with attitudes influenced by perceived usefulness, perceived ease of use and satisfaction [23]. Attitude implies the degree of a person’s positive or negative feelings about performing the target behaviour. The relationship discussed above is captured in Fig. 1 below.

It is important to emphasise that TCT has higher explanatory power compared to the individual models and can explain behaviours at different stages of the adoption life-cycle, including initial, short-term and long-term adoption [10]. The theory has been recommended for both qualitative and quantitative studies [10], has been empirically tested, has a solid theoretical underpinning, and has been validated. Besides, the TCT is robust and integrated [24].

TCT and use continuance intentions among health professionals

The TCT has been adopted by researchers globally to explain determinants of use continuance of technology. For instance, Iranmanesh et al. [25] adopted the TCT to explain factors influencing the use continuance of radio frequency identification among health professionals in Malaysia. TCT was also used to assess the use continuance of electronic medical records among medical professionals [25]. The study indicated that user experience played a major role in the relationship between perceived usefulness, attitudes and satisfaction and use continuance intentions. Among all the dimensions predicting use continuance intentions, perceived usefulness emerged as the strongest predictor for short-term and long-term users [25]. In addition, researchers in education and banking have employed TCT to explain determinants of use continuance intentions of innovations [26–29].

Methods

Study design and sampling

A cross-sectional mixed method design relying on a survey and re-enforced with in-depth interviews was adopted for the study. An online survey using Google forms was sent to users (261) of the Med4All system from 58 health facilities (Clinics: 19; Health Centres: 10 and Hospitals: 29) who at the time of the survey had signed on and been trained to use the Med4All system. As already indicated, the users include medical doctors, health administrators, pharmacist, accountants, stores and inventory/procurement officers and others (i.e. other healthcare professional made to act on behalf of those listed if they were not available in the health facility). Out of the 261 users, 124 responded to the survey. A blank copy of both the survey and qualitative interview guide has been attached as a supplementary file (Med4All Protocol.docx), with the survey data also attached as additional supplementary file (Data for study_01.xlsx).

In addition to the survey, 65 respondents in stakeholder organisations were selected (based on their knowledge and experience on the implementation of the Med4All system) for in-depth interviews. The 65 respondents include 3 senior officers from PharmAccess Foundation, 2 senior officers from the CHAG secretariat, 1 sales officer each from 6 medicines suppliers on the Med4All system, the administrator of the MCF, and 53 of the survey participants systematically selected from 20 of the health facilities using the Med4All system to allow for representation across health facility types (Clinics, Health Centres and Hospitals) and geography (rural/urban and regional). Except for CHAG where the 2 participants were interviewed separately due to location constraints, individual participants were interviewed together in each organization. In addition to the 65 respondents, a senior official each from the Ghana Health Service- GHS (operators of the Central Medical Stores/Framework Contracting System of Medicines Procurement) and the National Catholic Health Service- NCHS (operators of the Pooled Procurement Programme) were interviewed to understand the workings of their health facility medicines supply chain system. The last two interviews though not related to the Med4All system, provided some important context information within which the Med4All system could be discussed. It is important to emphasise that both the survey and interviews aimed at collecting organisational level information. Hence respondents did not respond in their private capacity but on behalf of their organisations. In the specific case of the health facilities, individual users were interviewed/surveyed because any decision to continue using the Med4All system or not will first come from them. Most importantly, the users constitute the same people (senior officers) who will make the final decision to either continue or stop using the Med4All system.

Data collection

Questions in the survey and interview guide were based on factors identified in the literature [10, 25] as influencing use continuance of technology. This includes perceived usefulness, perceived ease of use, confirmation, satisfaction and attitudes,. In addition, data on characteristics of respondents (sex, marital status, education, age, role at work, type of health facility, work experience, information technology (IT) experience and location of the health facility) were collected by the survey. In line with prior studies [10, 25], perceived usefulness, perceived ease of use, satisfaction and attitudes were captured using 4-item questions respectively. However, confirmation and use continuance intentions used 3-item questions. Both the 3 and 4-item questions were measured using a 5-point Likert type of scale where Strongly Disagree = 1, Disagree = 2, Neutral = 3, Agree = 4 and Strongly Agree = 5 [30]. Both the questionnaire and interview guide were in English given that all respondents were literate in the English language. The survey tool and interview guide were revised to reflect comments made by leaders from PharmAccess Foundation. The revised survey tool and interview guide were pre-tested using a health facility with similar characteristics as those enrolled in the main study. The pre-testing made it possible to capture important inputs that were not considered at the time of developing the instruments. The survey tool was administered via an online link to all respondents, whilst the interviews were conducted using a combination of face-to-face and a virtual channel (i.e. zoom). Interviewees answered questions on each of the constructs in line with their use of the Med4All system. All interviews were audio-recorded, in addition to taking handwritten notes for the purpose of comparing and triangulating transcripts from the recorded interviews with written notes.

Data analysis

The survey data was analyzed using descriptive statistics and Partial Least Squares-based Structural Equation Modelling (PLS SEM) technique. Descriptive statistics (frequencies, mean and standard deviation) were used to describe the characteristics of respondents and also to identify the level of use continuance intention of the Med4All system. The SEM technique was used to explore relationships among study constructs as outlined in the conceptual model in Fig. 1. To ensure robustness of the results in line with the sample size, the effect of control variables on the main construct (use continuance intentions) were estimated.

To assess the accuracy and consistency of indicators used to measure constructs in the conceptual framework, convergent and discriminant validity assessments were undertaken. Convergent validity was examined using the loadings, composite reliability and the Average Variance Extracted (AVE) scores. The rule of thumb is to ensure that each indicator explains at least 50% of the variance in the construct, hence its loadings should be 0.708, which is the square root of 0.5. As shown in Table AP-1 in the appendix, each of the indicators loaded well on the construct, providing an initial confirmation of convergent validity. Additionally, the composite reliability scores, which measure the level of internal consistency was above the 0.7 threshold. The AVE score describes the average variance that is explained by the indicators. The threshold for the AVE is 0.5 and this implies that indicators used cumulatively explain at least 50% of the total variance. The assessment results as per Table AP-1 indicate AVE scores above the threshold value of 0.5, thus confirming convergent validity of the constructs. Discriminant validity was also assessed by the Fornel Larcker criterion. The rule of thumb is to ensure that the square root of AVE for each construct is greater than its correlation with other constructs. The assessment results in Table AP-2 provides evidence of discriminant validity as the AVE scores for each construct in the diagonal elements of the correlation matrix, are higher than the pairs of correlations among the constructs.

The interviews, which lasted for an average of one hour per interview were recorded, transcribed into text, and read through several times to ensure that the transcripts accurately captured the content of the interviews. Also, the transcripts were compared to notes taken during the interviews to be sure that all relevant information provided during the interview were captured in the transcripts. After validating the transcripts, a data abstraction tool was developed using Microsoft Excel, with columns containing themes that were in line with the conceptual framework, and rows containing organizations/individuals that were interviewed. The validated transcripts were read through again and information in line with pre-defined themes were abstracted and captured into the data abstraction tool. The themes captured in the data abstraction tool formed the basis for reporting the qualitative findings of the study.

Beyond use continuance, additional questions focusing on perceived usefulness, perceived ease of use, confirmation, satisfaction and attitudes were asked to better understand the reason for the levels of use continuance intention as below.

Perceived usefulness

Perceived usefulness had a mean score of 3.84 out of 5. This suggest that respondents perceive the Med4All system to be relatively useful. This was confirmed by interview responses, as respondents from CHAG secretariat indicated that not only were medicines ordered by health facilities delivered to their doorstep irrespective of location, but about 70% of medicines required by health facilities are available on the Med4All platform at prices that are in some cases between 30% and 60% less than open market prices. They also argued that certification of suppliers and their medicines by the FDA, and the fact that suppliers are aware that samples of medicines supplied will be tested (for the presence of stated active ingredient and validated by the FDA) was enough to prevent them from supplying sub-standard medicines. The statement below emphasizes the point made above.

Med4All is a game changer and has the potential to change medicines supply, especially improved access to quality affordable medicines in Ghana. Prices on the Med4All system are much lower than the open market, coupled with access to interest-free loans to pay for medicines means much lower cost of medicines. ……… (IN-1)

Respondents from health facilities equally confirmed easy access to good quality medicines at relatively cheaper prices (compared to open market prices), and the importance of the embedded FDA quality assurance process as per the statement below.

Apart from delays in delivery of medicines ordered, I will say the Med4All system is great. Unlike the open market, you don’t need to roam looking for medication, which in some cases you may not even find. High-quality medicines are delivered to your health facility at much cheaper prices. ……… (R-4)

For medicines suppliers, the Med4All system is associated with reduced operational cost through aggregated orders, reduced cost of marketing and prompt payment of medicines supplied to health facilities as per the quote below.

The unique thing about Med4All is that payment is prompt once the stock is supplied. I don’t need to go to the customer and hustle for orders and payments. Med4All meets up my target, and I don’t need to stress. …………. (SM-1)

Perceived ease of use

The mean score of 3.74, although lower than the mean score for PU, is still high and an indication that respondents perceive the Med4All system as easy to use. The survey findings are supported by the interview results, as respondents from health facilities indicated that it was user-friendly, did not require any special training, can be accessed both online and offline, relies less on the use of computers and associated accessories given that the system can be used via smartphones. This makes the Med4All system cheaper to deploy and can be used in the absence of the internet as per the quotes below.

The system is very easy to use. We did not have to buy new computers or UPS to be able to get onto the system. It can even be used on your phone. So, when the lights are off, you can use your phone and carry out whatever it is that you want to do irrespective of where you are. ……. (R-5)

Confirmation of expectation

Confirmation recorded the lowest mean of 3.56 among all the constructs. This is about 71% of the overall score and can nonetheless be considered as good performance. The survey result is confirmed by the interviews, as respondents indicated that pricing, quality and diversity of medication on the platform met their expectations. This notwithstanding, respondents raised concerns with prolonged delivery time, which may perhaps be responsible for the relatively low scoring on this construct. Nonetheless, respondents believe that the Med4All system is far better than buying medicines from the open market as per the quote below.

Delivery is supposed to be within two weeks of requisition. Unfortunately, in some instances, the delivery went beyond two weeks to a month and sometimes even six weeks due to unavailability of stock. I think the Med4All platform has given us the opportunity to have many medicines that are listed on the essential medicine lists for ordering. ………(R-6)

Satisfaction

The mean score for satisfaction as per Table 2 is 3.6 out of 5. This is about 72% and therefore not different from confirmation of expectation. This suggest that about 28% of respondents were not satisfied with the Med4all system. Reactions from the interviews were also mixed. Some respondents expressed high satisfaction levels, while others expressed average and low satisfaction. For satisfied respondents, the Med4all system had lived up to expectations by ensuring that medicines on the Med4all platform were efficacious, delivered to their doorstep and affordable. For those who were dissatisfied, a major concern was the late delivery of orders.

They [Med4All] told us that they will beat down prices for us and they did, be in the comfort of our office and the medicines will be delivered to us and that’s what they’ve been doing. Whatever they promised us, they have delivered. So, we are satisfied (R-14)

Attitudes

The mean score for attitude is 3.96 out of 5, which is approximately 79%. This suggest that respondents have very good attitudes towards the use of the Med4All system. This is confirmed by the interviews. Positive attitude towards the Med4All is on the basis that it promotes transparency and reduces suspicion of corrupt practices in the procurement of medicines.

This platform is so transparent that there is little contact between the person within the health facility ordering and those supplying. So, there is no room for suspicion that anybody has made any underhand dealings. (R-19)

In addition, suppliers of medicines expressed positive attitudes towards the Med4all system, stating that they have a good relationship with CHAG and PharmAccess. The quote below confirms the above.

Doing business with Med4All, I would say they are 99%. They give you the chance to tender and they give you orders. If you’re not able to supply, they call you back to find out if you won’t be able to supply so they move on. (SM-6)

Clearly, reactions towards the Med4All system have mainly been positive from all respondents (CHAG, PharmAccess, health facilities and medicines suppliers). Also, respondents were unanimous that they have never regretted being part of the platform. They believe that it is innovative, timely, better than existing medicines supply systems (e.g. GHS Central Medical Stores -CMS/Framework Contracting System- FCS, the NCHS Pooled Procurement Programme- PPP and Open market procurement of medicines) in Ghana’s health sector and are therefore willing to recommend it to those who may not know of its existence. According to respondents, the added value of the med4All platform is the relatively low prices, assurance of quality medicines, delivery to the doorstep of the buyer and the fact that about 70% of medicines required by health facilities are available on the platform.

Following the perception that the Med4All system is better than existing medicines supply systems, additional information was collected to understand the workings of the two existing medicines supply chain systems (GHS CMS/FCS and the NCHS PPP) in Ghana’s health sector referred to by respondents. In the case of the GHS CMS, medicines are procured by the CMS using estimated consumption of health facilities and later distributed to Regional Medical Stores (RMS) for onward supply to health facilities. This means that health facilities place their orders with the RMS and make arrangements for the medicines to be delivered to them. Poor and inefficient management of the CMS occasioned the transition to the FCS. The FCS is a pooled form of procurement where independent suppliers bid to supply medicines based on estimated annual consumption of health facilities. The lowest bidder is selected and awarded an annual contract to supply medicines directly to health facilities based on demand and at a pre-determined price to be paid directly to suppliers by health facilities. The NCHS PPP operates in the same way as the GHS FCS. The difference however is that the bidding and contract award for the NCHS PPP is done once a year, but with opportunity to review prices at mid-year to account for changes in the market price of medicines.

A major challenge facing both the GHS FCS and the NCHS PPP is the unwillingness of suppliers to supply medicines to health facilities when needed due to (1) higher market price of medicines at the time of demand by health facilities compared to when the contract was awarded and therefore making it difficult for suppliers to supply. (2) the inability of health facilities to pay for medicines supplied due to erratic reimbursements from the National Health Insurance Authority. This implies that health facilities relying on the two medicines supply chain systems are hugely indebted to suppliers, who in turn have refused to supply medicines when demanded. As a result, the GHS FCS has been put on hold whiles the NCHS PPP is operating but sub-optimally.

Factors associated with use continuance intention of the Med4all system

Two structural equation models were estimated, the first (Fig. 2) without and the second (see Fig. 3) with controls (type of health facility [type]; work experience [work], information technology experience [IT exp] and urban location of the health facility [location]) to examine the relationship between usage continuance intention and their respective sub-constructs. Using a threshold of p < 0.005 and p < 0.10, the results in Fig. 2 indicate that satisfaction (0.369; p = 0.000), perceived usefulness (0.326; 0.001) and attitudes (0.173; p = 0.083) are significantly positively correlated with use continuance intentions respectively. In addition, whiles satisfaction is significantly positively influenced by confirmation of expectation (0.755; p = 0.000) and perceived usefulness (0.212; p = 0.001), attitudes is significantly positively influenced by perceived usefulness (0.390; p = 0.000) and perceived ease of use (0.185; p = 0.044). Finally, perceived usefulness is significantly positively influenced by confirmation (0.431; 0.008) and perceived ease of use (0.309; p = 0.044).

The structural equation was re-estimated to include the control variables, and the results indicate that while’s urban location of a health facility (-0.263; p = 0.026) is significantly negatively correlated with use continuance intentions, IT experience (0.169; p = 0.022) is significantly positively correlated with use continuance intentions. The coefficients on work experience and type of health facility were however not significant, whiles those of the main construct remained stable even with the introduction of the controls.

Challenges associated with the Med4All system

Key challenges facing the implementation of the Med4All system include delays in the delivery of ordered medicines, delivering fewer quantities compared to the order, failure to deliver onsite and late or no feedback from some suppliers regarding their inability to deliver orders assigned to them. Also, there were issues of artificial shortages created by suppliers so that they can use available stock to serve cash-paying customers instead of Med4All customers who will pay on credit. Some orders were also deemed by suppliers as too small to supply, especially when the cost of transporting the medicines ordered was more than the revenue accruing from the transaction. Finally, suppliers found it difficult to supply orders to health facilities located in very remote locations with very poor road networks. The two quotes below support the issues raised above.

Supplier payment is 90 days after supply. However, suppliers have a preference for cash paying customers. So, when you who wants to pay in 90 days, order the same product as the cash customer, the supplier will give the product to the cash customer. So, the medicines may be there but artificially out of stock for a Med4All customer paying on credit. … ( IN-4)

Also, some of the locations are remote and difficult to reach, making it almost impossible to supply to such health facilities. ………… (SM-5)

Other challenges include the inability of innovators to conduct post-supply surveillance of medicines as promised, difficulty in having access to user-support, and difficulty in paying for medications ordered due to cash flow challenges. Further investigation, however, revealed that except for the post-supply surveillance which has not been performed as promised, the innovators have adequate and efficient structures in place for user support, an embedded interest-free loan that can be accessed by health facilities to pay for medicines supplied, except that health facilities were not accessing the loan as per the quote below.

We have available funds from which health facilities can access loans to the tune of GHS 100,000 (USD 17,000) and sometimes even more, to pay for medicines supplied and can be paid back within 6 months ………… ( IN-2)

Transfer and resignation of staff originally trained to run the Med4All also pose a major challenge to some health facilities. There is also the challenge of some health facilities ordering medicines outside of the Med4All system. This seems to be deliberate given that the implementation of the Med4All system has eroded an informal incentive system that benefits some actors within the medicines supply chain in health facilities. For example, an innovator explained that ordering medicines through the Med4All system means that actors within health facilities will forfeit incentives received from pharmaceutical suppliers if orders were placed with them directly. This can be a disincentive to some actors and therefore may decide not to use the Med4All system to order medicines.

Discussion

The study sought to examine use continuance intentions of the Med4All system in selected health facilities in Ghana. As per the survey results and validated by the qualitative interviews, the findings suggest that there is a relatively high level of use continuance intention of the Med4All system.

The findings also suggest that perceived usefulness of the Med4All system is associated with use continuance intentions. Perceived usefulness is seen in improved access to good quality, efficacious medicines at affordable prices, conveniently delivered to the premises of healthcare facilities. This can have a profound positive impact not only on the availability of medicines in healthcare facilities, but also cost of care, patient satisfaction and consequently, the performance of health facilities and pharmaceutical companies through easy access to customers and reduced cost of doing business [31]. There is evidence in the literature to show that there is a positive relationship between perceived usefulness and use continuance intention of an innovation [32–34]. The findings are consistent with evidence from developing countries that suggest that e-procurement systems have been useful [35, 36].

Consistent with the literature [37, 38], the Med4All system was seen as easy to use, a feature that influences usefulness and attitudes and by extension use continuance. The perception that the Med4All system is easy to use can be explained from the viewpoint that it requires little mental effort to use, has an interface that is easy to navigate and is compatible with energy-efficient devices such as laptop computers and smartphones, which can be operated under conditions of restricted electricity supply. There is evidence in the literature to suggest that the above mentioned features make for easy navigation of e-procurement technology and the ability of individuals to carry out tasks swiftly and efficiently [35, 39, 40]. This can make employees effective in the performance of their tasks [41], hence the perception that the Med4All system is easy to use. Consistent with the existing literature [31, 42–44], effective capacity building (training of new users and additional training upon release of updates) and systems support structures (prompt response to users by the Med4All support team) put in place by the innovators as well as the relatively lower cost associated with deploying the system may also be responsible for the positive perception with respect to ease of use of the Med4All system.

The results shows a relatively high level of confirmation which is also significantly correlated with satisfaction and by extension use continuance intention in accordance with the literature [22]. Confirmed expectations of respondents may be due to the fact that medicines on the Med4All system are affordable and also of good quality and efficacious. In Ghana, the cost of medicines constitutes a large proportion of healthcare costs, which in many instances are pushed on to payers (i.e. the patient or insurer), and can contribute to catastrophic healthcare expenditure and low utilization of healthcare services [9].

In line with the literature [45], the level of satisfaction with the Med4All may be explained by perceived quality and affordability of medicines, prompt support services provided by the Med4all staff, and onsite delivery of medicines by suppliers. However, some health facilities indicated that they were not satisfied. Dissatisfaction if not checked, can negatively affect use continuance, especially given the significant correlation between satisfaction and use continuance. Indeed, existing evidence suggest that dissatisfaction is a disabling factor of innovation adoption and can also affect user perceptions about the innovation [46]. For example dissatisfied users of an innovation are less likely to recommend the innovation to their peers, may find ways to circumvent mandated processes [39, 47], and therefore make it difficult to enforce compliance. It will therefore be important for the innovators take steps to address challenges leading to dissatisfaction. This will make it possible for health facilities to take full advantage of the Med4All system [48].

Previous studies have demonstrated that attitude is a significant determinant of use continuance intentions of e-procurement technology [35]. Additionally, individuals have a higher likelihood of forming positive attitudes toward e-procurement systems that prove to be useful to their work [35]. Thus, the positive attitudes towards the Med4All system by users per the results may reflect the overall performance of the system. This includes improved access to quality affordable medicines, transparency in medicines procurement and the potential of the system being a solution to medicines supply chain challenges in Ghana’s health system. Collectively, these positive attributes might have influenced user satisfaction and consequently positive attitudes towards the Med4All system. This may also be a signal that health facilities will use the Med4All system into the foreseeable future given the positive correlation between attitudes and use continuance intention.

Notwithstanding the positives, the study found challenges that needs to be addressed to ensure that the current momentum is sustained and relied upon for scaling up the deployment of the system. There is a need to find lasting solutions to the challenge of delays in the delivery of ordered medicines and lack of timely feedback from suppliers to health facilities, especially when deliveries are delayed.

The issue of post-supply surveillance of medicines seems to be another issue that needs attention. The perception of the quality of medicines supplied is predicated on the post-supply surveillance mechanism incorporated into the Med4All concept. Thus, the thinking that this promise will not be fulfilled can erode the confidence of health facilities in the efficacy of medicines on the platform and consequently the survival and sustainability of the system. Equally important is the issue of credit period and access to liquidity for payment. The fact that health facilities were complaining about credit period and access to liquidity for payment at a time they had not accessed available loans for the same purpose suggests that they may either not be aware of the existence of the loan or may not have a good understanding of how the system works. There may be a need for the innovators to improve on the methods and channels of educating actors on the Med4All system.

Finally, the issue of unwillingness of health facility staff to use the Med4All system to procure medicines for fear of forfeiting incentives associated with existing procurement systems should also be addressed. This finding, though not directly from health facility respondents, is factual, and can constitute a real threat to the sustainability and survival of the Med4All system if not addressed. It is however gratifying to note that the innovators seem to have taken this issue seriously and are at the moment working with suppliers to explore appropriate and adequate solutions to the challenge.

Study limitations

The current study has limitations that were unavoidable and can be taken into consideration in future research. First, the study focused mainly on CHAG health facilities whose unique characteristics may not be applicable to other health facilities in Ghana and other African countries in general. Secondly the relatively small sample size did not make it possible to include a lot more covariate (e.g. sex, marital status, education, age, role at work) believed to influence use continuance intention. Future studies can expand the scope and size of the sample to take care of these limitations especially with the quantitative results.

Conclusion

This study has demonstrated that the Med4All system of medicine procurement is a promising intervention and has the potential to improve access to quality and affordable medicines among health facilities within CHAG and potentially Ghana. Med4All was perceived by respondents as a useful innovation that was easy to use for the required task. Although there are a few people whose expectation were not met or are not satisfied with the Med4All system due to some implementation challenges (delays in deliveries, poor communication between medicines suppliers and health facilities, payment challenges/credit days, delays or failure to conduct post-supply surveillance of medicines and in some cases unwillingness of health facility staff to use the Med4All system to procure medicines), attitudes toward the Med4All system were generally good. Additionally, perceived usefulness, perceived ease of use, satisfaction and attitudes proved to be very important inputs into use continuance of the Med4All system. Most importantly, the implementation challenges identified should be addressed, given that it can lead to improvement in the current levels of satisfaction and consequently use continuance intentions. The inability of managers of the Med4All system to address the challenges can undermine progress made and therefore affect scale-up plans.

What makes the Med4All system different from existing medicines supply chain systems such as the GHS CMS and FCS as well as the NCHS PPP are the speed of transaction turnaround and availability of interest-free loans to enable health facilities to pay suppliers. While the speed of transaction turnaround limits price changes within the transaction cycle, the availability of loans for payment of medicines supplied can profoundly limit issues of debt payment default. Given that the two reasons are at the base of the sub-optimal performance of GHS CMS and FCS as well as the NCHS PPP, the innovators/managers of the Med4All system should ensure that these features are sustained and improved to aid further adoption and continuous use of the Med4All system.

The successful implementation of the Med4All system provides practical lessons for policy makers and mangers in Ghana’s health sector in terms of how appropriate technology, financing and collaborative engagement with private sector operators can make significant difference in implementing a medicines supply chain system. Perhaps the GHS CMS and FCS as well as the NCHS PPP can leverage on the success of the Med4All implementation to address their respective challenges. More importantly, the study emphasizes the importance of post-adoption behaviors (in the current study attitudes and satisfaction with the Med4All) to use continuance.
