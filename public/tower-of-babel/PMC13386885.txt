Background

According to the World Health Organization’s classification, this review defines “older adults” as individuals aged ≥ 60 years [1]. Deep demographic ageing is redrawing the foundations of health-care systems. By 2030, one in five people on the planet—more than 2.1 billion—will be aged sixty or older [1]. This shift has triggered a twin crisis. On the clinical side, about half of all older adults now live with multimorbidity, each juggling two or more chronic conditions that multiply the complexity of care [2]. On the human-resources side, the World Health Organization estimates a global shortfall of 11 million health workers by 2030 [3]. The intersection of these two forces has created critical deficits in long-term care delivery, increasing risks of adverse health outcomes and hospital readmissions among older adults.

The emergence of artificial intelligence has alleviated the current gap to some extent. Literature review indicates that artificial intelligence is extensively applied across various domains [4], including health monitoring and disease management, safety supervision and risk prevention, cognitive and mental health support, social interaction and emotional companionship, as well as daily living assistance and functional compensation. These applications enhance the quality of life for older adults and alleviate medical pressures caused by medical workers’ shortages.

While artificial intelligence demonstrates significant potential to enhance efficiency and accessibility in geriatric healthcare, a closer examination of existing literature reveals several critical challenges that must be addressed. These issues can be broadly categorized into three main dimensions: systemic fractures, such as data interoperability and authentication gaps; user-level resistance, stemming from dehumanization anxiety and fears of job displacement; and broader social divides, including algorithmic bias and cultural barriers. The following section elaborates on these persistent obstacles, which complicate the ethical and practical integration of artificial intelligence in caregiving systems.

Recent reviews have broadly mapped AI applications in aging populations or focused on single domains such as identifying frailty symptom or reducing loneliness [5–7]. However, few have integrated implementation barriers across systemic, user, and societal levels or proposed stakeholder-specific roadmaps. This review bridges these gaps by synthesizing evidence from 352 studies to offer a multi-dimensional barrier analysis and actionable strategies for equitable, human-centered artificial intelligence integration in geriatric healthcare.

In this review, “geriatric healthcare” refers to health-related services and supports for older adults, including preventive care, chronic disease management, functional assistance, and psychosocial support, delivered in community, institutional, or home settings. “artificial intelligence implementation” encompasses the deployment of artificial intelligence systems in real-world or simulated geriatric healthcare contexts, including clinical decision support, monitoring, communication, and physical assistance. The conceptualization of artificial intelligence follows the definition provided in Sect. 2.4, which requires learning or adaptive capabilities and excludes simple rule-based systems.

This study aims to review the current applications of artificial intelligence in the field of geriatric healthcare and comprehensively analyze its practical implementations in daily life. By summarizing existing research outcomes and application cases, it identifies key challenges currently faced by artificial intelligence technologies in geriatric healthcare. On this basis, the study further proposes corresponding optimization strategies and solutions to promote the more efficient and human-integrated adoption of artificial intelligence in geriatric healthcare services, improve the quality of life for older adults, and provide references for technological development and policy-making in related fields.

Methods

This scoping review was conducted in accordance with the Arksey and O’Malley five-stage framework: (1) identifying the research question, (2) identifying relevant studies, (3) study selection, (4) charting the data, and (5) collating, summarizing, and reporting the results [8]. The reporting follows the PRISMA-ScR checklist. A completed PRISMA-ScR checklist is provided as a supplementary file. The final protocol was registered with the Open Science Framework.

Defining the research question

This review explores the literature related to the implementation of artificial intelligence in geriatric healthcare. The research question was deliberately refined to cover the broad scope and nature of the existing literature. The research questions are as follows: In geriatric healthcare, which aspects of diseases do the adopted artificial intelligence technologies primarily concern? How effective are these artificial intelligence products? In which aspects do problems exist?

Identifying relevant studies

A two-step search strategy was employed to retrieve literature published between January 1, 2015, and September 30, 2025. The first step involved a comprehensive literature search across five electronic databases, including PubMed, MEDLINE, the Cochrane Library, EMBASE, and Web of Science, using a combination of subject headings and keywords. The search strategy was customized for each database.

For older adults, a combination of the words ‘elderly’ OR’ senior’ OR ‘geriatric*’ OR ‘old’ OR ‘old people’ OR ‘old person*’ OR’age’OR ‘elder*’ OR ‘older population’ OR ‘older people’ OR ‘older person*’ OR ‘older adult*’ OR ‘aging’ was used.

While the term “senile” was included in the search string to maximize sensitivity to older literature, the authors acknowledge that this term is now considered ageist and inappropriate in contemporary gerontological discourse. Its use was limited to database search syntax only and does not appear in the narrative synthesis.

For artificial intelligence: a combination of the words’ artificial intelligence*’ OR ‘AI’ OR ‘machine learning’ OR ‘deep learning’ OR ‘computer vision*’ OR ‘neural network*’ OR ‘algorithm*’ OR ‘big data’ OR ‘predictive modeling’ OR ‘unsupervised learning’ OR ‘supervised learning’ OR ‘reinforcement learning’ OR ‘random forest’ OR large language model OR Generative AI OR transformer* OR ‘support vector machine’ were used. Study selection

Exclusion criteria

Studies will be excluded if they: (1)report only on user satisfaction or acceptability without substantive outcomes related to effectiveness or implementation barriers. (2)focus solely on artificial intelligence technical development or validation without evaluation in a geriatric healthcare context. (3)involve artificial intelligence for surgical assistance or invasive procedures.(4)are reviews, commentaries, editorials, letters, conference abstracts, theses, or protocols. (5)are not published in English.

Reviews, commentaries, editorials, letters, conference abstracts, theses, or protocols were excluded because they do not present primary data. However, reference lists of relevant reviews were manually screened for additional primary studies (snowballing).

Conceptual boundaries of artificial intelligence in geriatric healthcare

For the purpose of this review, artificial intelligence is defined as systems capable of performing tasks that typically require human intelligence through learning, reasoning, or pattern recognition. Included technologies must involve at least one of the following: machine learning, natural language processing, computer vision, or knowledge representation with adaptive capabilities. Simple rule-based systems without learning or adaptive components were excluded, as they do not meet contemporary definitions of artificial intelligence. This boundary was applied consistently during full-text screening by two authors, with disagreements resolved through discussion.

Quality assessment

Given that the application of artificial intelligence in geriatric healthcare is still in its early stages, with many studies being small-scale and exploratory in nature, a formal quality assessment of the included studies was not performed. Nonetheless, these studies provide valuable insights into the current research landscape and the potential of artificial intelligence in this field.

Data extraction

Two authors independently screened the titles and abstracts to identify potentially eligible studies. Inter-rater agreement was calculated for title/abstract screening (Cohen’s kappa = 0.84) and full-text screening (kappa = 0.79). Disagreements were resolved through discussion.

Results

The detailed study selection process is shown in Fig. 1.

PRISMA-ScR flow chart of the selection process

A total of 46,571 records were initially identified from five databases (PubMed, EMBASE, Cochrane, Web of Science, and MEDLINE). After removing 14,091 duplicates, 32,480 records were screened by title and abstract, which led to the exclusion of 26,334 records. The remaining 6,146 reports were sought for full-text retrieval, but 579 could not be obtained. Subsequently, 5,567 full-text reports were assessed for eligibility, and 5,215 were excluded for various reasons, such as lack of primary data, no geriatric healthcare context, no substantive effectiveness or barrier outcomes, exclusion of simple rule-based systems, and surgical or invasive applications. Ultimately, 352 studies were included in the final review.

Thematic categorization process

The five application domains were derived through an inductive thematic analysis of the 352 included studies. First, two authors independently extracted descriptions of AI functionalities and target outcomes. Second, these descriptions were grouped into preliminary categories through open coding. Third, the research team iteratively discussed and merged categories into higher-order themes. The final five domains—(1) health monitoring and disease management, (2) safety monitoring and risk prevention, (3) cognitive and mental health support, (4) social interaction and emotional companionship, and (5) daily living assistance and functional compensation—emerged from consensus after three rounds of discussion. No a priori framework was imposed.

Artificial intelligence applications in geriatric healthcare

Building on artificial intelligence’s potential to address geriatric healthcare demands amid workforce shortages, this section explores key artificial intelligence applications in geriatric healthcare, categorized into five domains: health monitoring and disease management, safety and risk prevention, cognitive and mental health support, social interaction and emotional companionship, and daily living assistance. This review highlights artificial intelligence’s current role and sets the foundation for evaluating its limitations and future directions in subsequent sections.

Firstly, in the domain of health monitoring and disease management, the focus is on safeguarding older adults’ physical health through continuous and preventive artificial intelligence-driven approaches. Key applications include chronic disease management, remote vital sign monitoring, and guided rehabilitation training. Secondly, safety monitoring and risk prevention aim to mitigate accidents and enhance personal safety for older adults living independently. This is achieved through functionalities such as fall detection and alert systems. Thirdly, in the area of cognitive and mental health support, artificial intelligence technologies are dedicated to maintaining and improving cognitive function and psychological well-being. Furthermore, social interaction and emotional companionship applications seek to alleviate social isolation and loneliness among older adults by facilitating connections with society. Specific implementations include simplified social communication tools and digital companions for emotional interaction. Lastly, daily living assistance and functional compensation leverage artificial intelligence to offset age-related functional decline, supporting older adults in performing everyday activities and maintaining independent living.

Health monitoring and disease management

Firstly, one of the most prominent applications of artificial intelligence is in the realm of health monitoring and chronic disease management. Older patients with chronic diseases often require long-term medication, but issues such as medication errors, missed doses, drug expiration, and improper storage are widespread [9, 10]. These errors in medication use may lead to unnecessary side effects, poor disease control, readmission, and even death [11–13]. In response, existing research has developed smart pillboxes to provide medication reminders [14]. For example, an open-source smart pillbox system (mPillBox) was reported in one study to improve medication safety by integrating drug interaction checking and environmental monitoring [15]. Across the 352 included studies, similar functionalities were described in various forms, including reminder systems, adherence tracking, and remote monitoring platforms [16–18].

Building on this, Naser and his team have developed a system named mPillBox, which not only monitors medication effectiveness—by checking expiration dates and environmental conditions like temperature and humidity to ensure safe storage—but also automatically checks interactions between current and future medications by connecting to public databases such as DrugBank and RxList. For reminders, mPillBox supports both local alerts and remote notifications via Chromecast and other IoT devices for screen casting, as well as email alerts. Compared to current smart pillboxes, mPillBox shows clear advantages in openness, cost-effectiveness, and functional completeness.

The application of artificial intelligence in chronic disease management is gradually changing the traditional medical model. By analyzing massive amounts of health data, artificial intelligence can achieve early disease diagnosis [19–21], personalized treatment [22], and real-time monitoring [23–25], improving management efficiency [26]. For example, an artificial intelligence algorithm can monitor the dynamic glycemia of diabetes patients and optimize the medication scheme [27]; Intelligent devices combined with machine learning can remotely predict hypertensive patients and adjust treatment strategies in a timely manner [28]. In addition, artificial intelligence has also shown outstanding performance in diagnosing chronic obstructive pulmonary disease [29]. Despite facing challenges, the continuous optimization of artificial intelligence is expected to provide more accurate and efficient full process management support for chronic disease patients [30].

Researchers explored the use of artificial intelligence, specifically machine learning and deep learning techniques, to assess frailty in older adults using gait data from inertial measurement unit sensors. They aimed to provide an objective and portable method for early detection of frailty, overcoming limitations of traditional clinical assessments. The study reviews various algorithms and sensor placements, showing promising results in accuracy and reliability. It highlights the potential for developing cost-effective, intelligent healthcare systems to improve geriatric healthcare and reduce the burden on older patients [31].

Artificial intelligence is widely used in post-stroke rehabilitation. A survey indicated that researchers utilize machine learning, deep learning, robotics, and virtual reality and analyze data from imaging, wearable sensors, and clinical assessments to enhance early diagnosis, motor recovery, and cognitive rehabilitation to improve patient outcomes by providing personalized, adaptive, and continuous therapy. Results show significant improvements in motor function and cognitive abilities, with artificial intelligence-driven tools enhancing therapy efficacy and accessibility [32]. Despite these advances, studies in this domain consistently reported fragmented data formats and a lack of interoperability standards as primary implementation barriers.

Safety monitoring and risk prevention

In addition to long-term health management, artificial intelligence is associated with significantly enhanced safety monitoring and accident prevention for older adults living independently.

Falls, which affect more than 25% of older adults annually [33], are among the most common causes of morbidity and the leading cause of injury-related deaths in older adults worldwide [34, 35]. Falls not only cause moderate to severe injuries, hospitalizations, and even mortality in older adults but also impose significant psychological and economic burdens on both older adults and their relatives [34, 36, 37]. The United States spends over 50 billion dollars annually on healthcare related to falls, accounting for 4.4% of Medicare hospital expenditures, 5.7% of payments to physicians and other healthcare professionals, and 11.8% of expenditures on home healthcare services, long-term care facilities, and durable medical equipment. Accompanying the high incidence of fall-related injuries is an annual financial cost of roughly 50 billion dollars in medical care worldwide [38].

Some existing methods achieve fall detection through long-term wearing of wearable devices [39–41]. Researchers have proposed a non-contact fall detection system based on 4D imaging radar and artificial intelligence. This system utilizes a 4D imaging radar to emit high-frequency signals in the 77–81 GHz range and generates point cloud data containing information such as position, velocity, time, and Doppler power. After preprocessing steps including standardization, zero-padding, k-means clustering, and uniform resizing, the point cloud data is input into a Convolutional Neural Network posture classification model, which outputs three postures: standing, sitting, and lying down. Experimental results show that the posture classification model achieved an average accuracy of 98.66% in 5-fold cross-validation, and the rule-based algorithm for fall detection reached an accuracy of 95%, demonstrating overall promising performance. The system excels in technical feasibility, privacy protection, and user experience, providing an effective solution for safety monitoring of older adults [42].

Artificial intelligence is used for intelligent risk assessment and intervention in pressure ulcer prevention and treatment: using electronic medical records to train machine learning models to predict the risk of pressure ulcer occurrence and identify high-risk patients. Combined with intelligent pressure-reducing mattresses, real-time monitoring of pressure distribution, automatic adjustment of position and pressure, and personalized prevention of pressure ulcers [43].

Cognitive and mental health support

Moving beyond physical health, artificial intelligence also offers innovative solutions for supporting cognitive and mental well-being. As global aging accelerates, early detection of mild cognitive impairment has become increasingly critical. Traditional instruments such as the Mini-Mental State Examination and the Montreal Cognitive Assessment ADDIN require trained personnel, take about 10 min to complete, and lack sensitivity for the earliest stages of mild cognitive impairment [44, 45]. Speech, by contrast, offers a non-invasive, low-cost, and readily accessible biomarker that can sensitively track cognitive change. Recently, researchers developed and validated an artificial intelligence model that screens for mild cognitive impairment from only three minutes of free conversation with minimal interviewer intervention. After comparing five algorithms including XGBoost and deep neural networks, the combination of age, sex, and speech features emerged as the best performer, achieving an area under the receiver operating characteristic curve of 0.89 (95% CI: 0.86–0.92). Adding speech features was associated with discrimination (p < 0.0001). This approach introduces a novel, efficient modality for large-scale community screening of mild cognitive impairment and demonstrates that artificial intelligence can markedly enhance the efficiency of detecting mild cognitive impairment in older adults [46].

Artificial intelligence revolutionizes pain assessment by analyzing facial expressions, physiological signals, and clinical data to deliver objective, real-time, and personalized pain evaluation, enhancing accuracy and overcoming limitations of traditional self-report methods in clinical practice [47, 48].

Another study combined genetic data, brain atrophy measures, and cognitive scores into a multimodal hazard score to predict the progression from mild cognitive impairment to Alzheimer’s Disease. This approach demonstrated significant potential for enriching clinical trial populations and reducing trial costs by identifying high-risk individuals more accurately [49]. A study focused on the development of artificial intelligence-based voice biomarker models derived from unstructured conversations [46]. These models showed significant improvements in prediction accuracy, highlighting the potential of artificial intelligence in cognitive and mental health support.

Social interaction and emotional companionship

Addressing loneliness and social isolation, which are detrimental to mental health, artificial intelligence technologies facilitate enhanced social interaction and provide emotional companionship [50–52]. Loneliness is described as the subjective perception of missing social contact or a desired companion, while social isolation is the objective lack of social contact with other persons [53]. Amid rapid population aging in Asia, growing numbers of older adults are moving into nursing homes because of health problems, intensifying feelings of loneliness that can lead to hypertension, heart disease and other physical and mental disorders [54, 55]. Researchers have therefore designed and implemented a socially interactive robot that engages older adults naturally: it recognizes their emotions and responds with emotional support and daily companionship to ease loneliness and enhance mental well-being. Pilot tests in nursing homes and household settings show significant improvements in participants’ mental-health scores and high user acceptance, confirming that the approach is both practical and effective for alleviating social isolation among older adults [56]. Another study shows that artificial intelligence applications have been effective in alleviating loneliness too. A study explored the effectiveness of artificial intelligence companions in reducing loneliness through multiple experimental designs. Results suggest artificial intelligence companions can be effective tools for combating societal loneliness [57].

Assisted daily living and functional compensation

On a practical level, artificial intelligence technologies empower older adults by providing direct assistance in everyday tasks and functional support.

With aging, the function of the ankle plantar flexor muscles during the push-off phase of walking declines, which directly leads to reduced walking speed, shorter step length, increased metabolic energy consumption, and impairs mobility and quality of life [58–62]. Based on this, researchers conducted a study, through an artificial intelligence-driven dual-mode ankle exoskeleton, effectively demonstrates that artificial intelligence has remarkable efficacy in assisting walking, reducing energy consumption, and enhancing muscle strength in older adults [63].

A study proposes the potential of artificial intelligence chatbots in promoting physical activity and a healthy diet through a behavior change model. The model aims to enhance the relational and persuasive capacities of chatbots to drive behavior change. Results show preliminary efficacy in increasing physical activity and improving diet [64].

In summary, artificial intelligence applications in geriatric healthcare span a wide spectrum, from physiological monitoring to psychosocial support and functional assistance. These technologies collectively hold the potential to enhance the quality of life and promote aging in place. However, the implementation and adoption of these innovative solutions are not without challenges. The following section will critically examine the limitations and barriers identified in the current landscape of artificial intelligence for aging populations.

Discussion

Barriers of artificial intelligence in geriatric healthcare

While the potential of artificial intelligence to revolutionize geriatric healthcare is demonstrated by these diverse applications, a critical examination reveals significant challenges that threaten to undermine its successful integration and equitable adoption. The transition from conceptual promise to practical implementation is fraught with complexities, which can be categorized into three critical dimensions: a system-level fracture characterized by data interoperability issues and an authentication vacuum; user-level resistance stemming from fears of dehumanization and job displacement; and a deepening social divide exacerbated by inherent algorithmic biases and cultural barriers.

System-level fracture: data interoperability and authentication vacuum

Data interoperability is the first question. The Healthcare Information and Management Systems Society defines interoperability as the ability of different information systems, devices, and applications to access, exchange, integrate and cooperatively use data in a coordinated manner, within and across organizational, regional and national boundaries, to provide timely and seamless portability of information and optimize the health of individuals and populations globally [65]. In the medical scenario for the older adult, data sources are diverse and fragmented, including hospital information systems, wearable devices, and home health monitoring equipment. These devices and systems often have nonuniform data formats and incompatible interfaces. For example, data from a smart blood pressure monitor may not be able to be directly transmitted to a hospital’s electronic medical record system, making it difficult for medical staff to obtain a complete set of health data, thereby affecting the comprehensive assessment and precise treatment of the older adults’ health status.

Data sharing barriers also exist between different medical institutions. When the older need to visit different hospitals, the artificial intelligence-assisted diagnostic data and treatment records accumulated in the previous hospital cannot be smoothly transferred. As a result, doctors in the new hospital need to recollect and analyze data, which not only wastes time and resources but may also delay disease diagnosis and treatment.

This authentication vacuum means that identical labels may conceal vastly different levels of accuracy, safety, and transparency. Sub-par products risk missed falls, misdiagnosed depression or inappropriate medication prompts, directly endangering frail older adults. Additionally, developers face no mandatory post-market surveillance, allowing performance drift to go unnoticed. The absence of clear certification pathways also deters reputable innovators, who struggle to distinguish themselves from opportunistic vendors.

User-level resistance: dehumanization anxiety and job threats

In the care and companionship of older adults, the application of artificial intelligence may bring the risk of depersonalization [66]. For example, although some intelligent companion robots can provide emotional support and companionship services, they lack the depth of human emotion and empathy [67]. older adults may feel the mechanical and cold nature of communication with robots, leading to a sense of loneliness and neglect. Overreliance on artificial intelligence companionship can also lead to the older adults’ alienation from human society. When robots become the main companions, the interaction between older adults and humans such as family and friends may decrease, thereby affecting their social support network and mental health. In addition, the phenomenon of ‘robots replacing human emotions’ has also sparked ethical controversies, with concerns that it may change human perceptions and values of emotional relationships such as kinship and friendship.

In medical institutions, some medical staff are resistant to the application of artificial intelligence. Taking intelligent scheduling algorithms as an example, some nurses may lack trust in the accuracy and reliability of the algorithms, worrying that the algorithms cannot fully consider the special needs of patients, emergencies, and personal circumstances of medical staff, thereby leading to unreasonable scheduling and affecting the quality and efficiency of care [66, 68, 69]. Medical staff’s resistance to artificial intelligence may also stem from concerns about their own professional status. They worry that artificial intelligence will replace some nursing work, thereby threatening their career development [66, 70]. This psychological barrier can affect the promotion and application of artificial intelligence in medical institutions, hindering its role in the field of geriatric healthcare.

Social divide: algorithm bias and cultural barriers

Algorithmic bias occurs when a model produces skewed or unfair outcomes due to flawed assumptions, unrepresentative training data, or inherent limitations in the design process. For instance, a disease diagnosis model trained predominantly on data from urban populations may perform poorly in rural areas due to differences in demographics, healthcare access, or environmental factors. Similarly, a dermatological artificial intelligence system developed using mostly lighter-skinned individuals may exhibit higher accuracy for that group while failing to generalize to darker skin tones, leading to misdiagnoses or lower sensitivity. Such biases can exacerbate health disparities, particularly for underrepresented groups. Mitigating algorithmic bias requires diverse and inclusive datasets, rigorous fairness testing across subgroups, and ongoing monitoring to ensure equitable performance.

For older adults, language and cultural background are important factors affecting their acceptance of artificial intelligence. Taking dialects as an example, many older people are accustomed to communicating in their local dialects, but current companion chatbots have insufficient dialect compatibility. If chatbots cannot understand and use the dialects of older adults, communication barriers will arise, reducing older adults’ acceptance and willingness to use artificial intelligence. The acceptance of artificial intelligence by older adults also varies across different cultural backgrounds. Some older people with more traditional views may be skeptical of artificial intelligence, believing that machines cannot replace human emotions and care, and thus refuse to use related products and services. In addition, cultural differences may also affect the older adults’ acceptance of the appearance and behavior of artificial intelligence companion robots.

Stakeholder-specific roadmaps

Data interoperability

The establishment of common health information technology research outcome measures would support higher-quality research [71]. Based on findings from 27 of the included studies that reported data integration challenges, we propose that the foundation of effective artificial intelligence in healthcare lies in seamless data exchange. For artificial intelligence systems to function optimally, they require access to comprehensive, high-quality, and interoperable data. Based on 23 of the included studies that explicitly identified data fragmentation as a major systemic barrier, we propose that the integration of FHIR with artificial intelligence offers a promising approach to improve interoperability. This recommendation is further supported by post-hoc literature, which reported 33% lower data integration expenses in FHIR-implementing institutions [72]. According to Tabari et al.’s comprehensive analysis of 127 healthcare institutions implementing Fast Healthcare Interoperability Resources standards, these organizations experienced significant operational improvements, including 47% lower data integration expenses and 56% faster system integration compared to traditional systems [73]. The study further revealed that Fast Healthcare Interoperability Resources adoption led to an 83% enhancement in data precision and cut data retrieval times by 71% across diverse clinical environments. The synergy between Fast Healthcare Interoperability Resources and artificial intelligence proved particularly impactful, boosting real-time data availability by 92% and decreasing manual data input needs by 85% - performance metrics that far surpass those of conventional interoperability approaches [73]. A research indicates that healthcare systems incorporating Fast Healthcare Interoperability Resources technology saw substantial gains in clinical workflow efficiency, with 45% improvement in decision support precision and a remarkable 58% reduction in time previously wasted toggling between disparate patient data systems. These findings collectively demonstrate Fast Healthcare Interoperability Resources’s transformative potential in creating more reliable, efficient, and clinician-friendly healthcare data ecosystems [74].

The use of Fast Healthcare Interoperability Resources represents a paradigm shift in healthcare data interoperability through its innovative use of modern web technologies. Rajhamundry’s evaluation of real-world Fast Healthcare Interoperability Resources deployments revealed that healthcare institutions leveraging this framework experienced dramatic improvements in system integration efficiency - cutting interface development time by 84% and enabling new interface deployment in just over one day [75]. Their findings particularly highlight its exceptional reliability, with successful first-time implementation rates reaching 94% and near-elimination (96% reduction) of post-deployment technical issues that commonly plague conventional healthcare data systems [75].

Universities and research institutions should be funded to develop open-source Fast Healthcare Interoperability Resources validation tools, reducing reliance on expensive proprietary systems. Open-source tools can democratize access to high-quality data validation, ensuring that even small clinics can verify the integrity and compatibility of their data.

Authentication vacuum

Regarding the lack of management requirements, we can develop long-term plans to address this issue. A unified regulatory system at the global or national level must be established. First, legislation should be advanced to classify artificial intelligence in geriatric healthcare under medical device or healthcare service regulations, defining approval processes and continuous monitoring requirements. Second, standardized evaluation tools—including algorithm audits, data quality certification, and ethical review mechanisms—should be developed to ensure fairness, explainability, and safety in artificial intelligence systems.

Dehumanization anxiety

As artificial intelligence becomes more integrated into geriatric healthcare, ethical considerations must take center stage. Companion robots, for example, should prioritize user autonomy by including physical off-buttons and scheduled ‘quiet hours’ to prevent over-reliance. These features can help ensure that the older adults retain control over their environment and are not overwhelmed by constant artificial intelligence interactions.

Auditable interaction logs are another critical feature, enabling caregivers to review artificial intelligence suggestions, such as medication reminders, and intervene when necessary. This transparency allows caregivers to ensure that artificial intelligence recommendations are appropriate and safe for the individual. By using robots to detect signs of loneliness, such as prolonged silence, and alerting caregivers for follow-up, this model balances efficiency with empathy.

To further strengthen human connections, artificial intelligence can play a proactive role in family engagement. For instance, systems could gamify interactions by prompting relatives to send voice notes if an older adult hasn’t received contact in days. However, technology alone isn’t enough, subsidies for internet access in low-income households are essential to ensure these hybrid models are inclusive and accessible to all. Ensuring that all families, regardless of their economic status, can participate in these interactions is crucial for maintaining social bonds and supporting older adults.

Job threats

To alleviate fears about artificial intelligence replacing nurses, emphasize artificial intelligence’s assistive role, hospitals can offer upskilling programs to reposition nurses as artificial intelligence-augmented caregivers, enhancing their irreplaceable human skills. Clear communication from leadership about artificial intelligence as a collaborative tool, not a workforce reduction strategy, is essential.

The successful integration of artificial intelligence into geriatric healthcare hinges on the acceptance and collaboration of healthcare staff. Nurses and caregivers must be involved in the design process from the outset. ‘artificial intelligence shadowing’ programs, where staff observe prototypes for extended periods, can uncover practical issues, such as scheduling tools underestimating the time required for catheter changes. This hands-on involvement can help identify potential pitfalls and ensure that the final artificial intelligence solutions are practical and user-friendly.

Unions should advocate for artificial intelligence integration that aligns with existing workflows, rather than introducing cumbersome new steps. By integrating artificial intelligence in a way that enhances rather than disrupts current processes, staff are more likely to embrace the technology.

Algorithm bias

Artificial intelligence’s potential in geriatric healthcare is undermined if algorithms perpetuate biases or fail to account for diverse populations. Addressing this requires a multi-pronged approach. First, interdisciplinary research consortia should compile comprehensive geriatric datasets that intentionally oversample underrepresented groups, such as rural populations and ethnic minorities. This step is crucial to ensure that artificial intelligence models are trained on diverse data, reducing the risk of bias and improving their generalizability.

Equitable access to artificial intelligence technologies is a pressing challenge, particularly for rural and underfunded facilities. Subsidies should prioritize nursing homes with fewer than 50 beds or those located more than 50 km from urban centers. Funding could be reallocated from hospital IT budgets, with a suggested 2% diversion to support these initiatives. This reallocation can help ensure that smaller and more remote facilities have the resources they need to adopt artificial intelligence technologies.

Mobile artificial intelligence clinics—vans equipped with portable diagnostic tools like handheld ultrasound devices paired with artificial intelligence—can extend care to remote areas, ensuring no community is left behind. These mobile clinics can bring advanced diagnostic capabilities to areas where access to specialized equipment is limited, improving the quality of care for underserved populations.

Public-private partnerships can also play a transformative role. ‘Artificial intelligence libraries’, where rural facilities rent devices like fall sensors through blockchain-managed sharing economies, can reduce upfront costs. China’s experience with artificial intelligence-enabled remote monitoring in rural areas, facilitated by partnerships with tech firms and telecom providers, offers a blueprint for other regions. By working together, governments, private companies, and healthcare providers can create sustainable solutions that ensure equitable access to artificial intelligence-driven care.

Synthetic data generation, using techniques like Generative Adversarial Networks, can fill gaps for rare conditions where real-world data is scarce. Synthetic data can help create more balanced datasets, ensuring that artificial intelligence models are not skewed towards more common conditions or populations. Regulatory bodies must then enforce strict pre-market requirements to ensure models perform equitably across all demographics.

Cultural barriers

To bridge the language-and-culture gap, we propose a participatory, two-layer solution. First, build community-owned voice banks: recruit local high-school and university students to record older adults telling stories in their native dialects, then crowd-source transcriptions and pronunciations to train open-source models that power companion chatbots. Second, embed cultural configurability into the industrial design process: create modular robot skins, colour palettes and gesture libraries that can be swapped at the point of sale or remotely updated via firmware, allowing families or care homes to choose appearances that align with regional aesthetics or religious taboos. By intertwining dialect data, culturally adaptive hardware, and co-creation rituals, we transform artificial intelligence from a foreign artefact into a culturally fluent member of the household.

Future directions

The rapid advancements in artificial intelligence present opportunities to transform geriatric healthcare. However, to fully realize its potential, several key areas require focused attention, innovation, and collaboration. Below, we expand on the future directions for artificial intelligence in geriatric healthcare, detailing emerging trends, technological advancements, and policy considerations that will shape the next decade of innovation.

Integrate generative artificial intelligence for hybrid clinical-psychosocial support

Generative artificial intelligence is poised to bridge the gap between clinical monitoring and psychosocial care, offering a more holistic approach to older adults’ well-being [38]. Future applications should focus on artificial intelligence-powered virtual caregivers that go beyond basic reminders to become proactive health companions. These systems could analyze speech patterns to detect early signs of cognitive decline, such as hesitations or word-finding difficulties, and recommend cognitive exercises or alert healthcare providers. Emotional artificial intelligence, capable of recognizing distress in voice or facial expressions, could initiate supportive conversations or connect older adults with mental health professionals when needed [76].

Personalized behavioral interventions represent another promising avenue. Artificial intelligence could tailor psychosocial support based on an individual’s history, preferences, and real-time emotional state.

In memory care, generative artificial intelligence could play a transformative role. artificial intelligence-driven reminiscence therapy might create personalized digital narratives, such as interactive photo albums or simulated conversations with historical figures, to stimulate long-term memory in dementia patients. Ethically regulated applications of large language models could even simulate conversations with deceased loved ones, providing comfort for grieving older adults while maintaining strict ethical boundaries.

Advocate for global standards on operational artificial intelligence in geriatric healthcare

As artificial intelligence adoption in geriatric healthcare accelerates, the establishment of global standards is essential to ensure ethical, safe, and equitable deployment. Ethical artificial intelligence frameworks must be developed, including the formation of artificial intelligence ethics boards to oversee geriatric healthcare applications. These boards would ensure transparency in algorithmic decision-making, preventing biases in treatment prioritization or resource allocation. Informed consent protocols are equally critical, particularly for older adults with cognitive impairments, to ensure they understand how artificial intelligence interacts with them and their data.

Regulatory and safety standards must keep pace with technological advancements. Standardized testing for artificial intelligence geriatric healthcare devices should be mandatory to verify reliability before deployment. Cybersecurity mandates are also necessary to protect sensitive health data.

Cultural and linguistic adaptation is another vital consideration. Artificial intelligence tools must be designed with cultural sensitivity, accounting for language diversity, social norms, and caregiving traditions across different regions. By addressing these factors, artificial intelligence can be tailored to meet the unique needs of diverse aging populations worldwide.

Promote continuous learning and adaptation

To sustain the benefits of artificial intelligence in geriatric healthcare, continuous learning and system adaptability are paramount. Artificial intelligence literacy programs for caregivers and older adults will ensure effective use of these technologies. Training initiatives should equip healthcare workers with the skills to interpret artificial intelligence-generated insights and troubleshoot issues.

Adaptive artificial intelligence models, trained through federated learning, can improve accuracy across institutions without sharing raw data, preserving patient privacy. Continuous feedback loops, where caregivers correct artificial intelligence recommendations, will further refine algorithms over time, ensuring they remain aligned with real-world care needs.

Public-private-academic collaborations will drive innovation in this space. Governments, tech firms, and universities should co-fund artificial intelligence geriatric healthcare innovation hubs to accelerate research and development.

Comparison with existing reviews

Unlike prior scoping reviews that focused on single artificial intelligence applications, our study synthesizes evidence across five domains and three barrier levels. Compared to Shaheen et al., which mapped artificial intelligence applications but did not propose stakeholder-specific roadmaps, the present review explicitly links barriers to actionable strategies [77]. This integrated barrier–roadmap framework represents a novel contribution to the geriatric artificial intelligence literature.

Limitations

This scoping review has several limitations that should be considered. First, the search was restricted to studies published in English, which may have excluded relevant research published in other languages and limited the cultural breadth of the findings. Second, the field of artificial intelligence in geriatric healthcare is rapidly evolving, and the literature published after our search cutoff date is not included, potentially omitting the most recent technological advances. Third, as a scoping review, this study aimed to map the breadth of the field rather than appraise the quality of individual studies or perform a meta-analysis of efficacy; therefore, the strength of evidence for specific artificial intelligence applications is not assessed. Finally, while we identified key barriers and proposed roadmaps, their practical feasibility and effectiveness require validation through future empirical research and stakeholder engagement in real-world settings. What’s more, as a scoping review, we did not perform a formal quality assessment of included studies. This is a recognized methodological feature of scoping reviews aimed at mapping evidence rather than evaluating internal validity; however, it also means that findings related to artificial intelligence effectiveness should be interpreted with caution and not be overgeneralized.

Conclusions

This review demonstrates that artificial intelligence holds significant promise for addressing the dual crisis of an ageing population and healthcare workforce shortages in geriatric healthcare. Applications span health monitoring, safety, cognitive support, companionship, and daily living assistance, offering pathways to enhance efficiency and accessibility. However, the realization of this potential is impeded by systemic fractures, user-level resistance, and a widening social divide. To navigate these challenges, a coordinated, multi-stakeholder approach is imperative. The proposed roadmaps—emphasizing enforced data interoperability standards, ethical artificial intelligence certification, culturally adaptive design, and workforce upskilling—provide an actionable framework for guiding development and implementation. Future efforts must prioritize equitable, human-centered integration to ensure that artificial intelligence technologies truly enhance the quality of life for older adults globally.
