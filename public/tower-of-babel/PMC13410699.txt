Effect of ultrasound-guided superficial versus deep superior costotransverse ligament on haemodynamics during general anaesthesia induction of thoracoscopic lung resection: protocol for a multicentre, double-blind, randomised controlled trial
Authors: Yajie Xu, Yi Luo, Wenwen Zhang, Zhaojing Fang, Ziqi Qiu, Honggang Yi, Hongwei Shi, Hao Cheng, Xiaoliang Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThoracic paravertebral block (TPVB) is widely used for analgesia in thoracoscopic lung resection, offering effective pain relief and enhanced recovery. However, preoperative TPVB may block both thoracic somatic nerves and cardiac sympathetic nerves, potentially increasing the risk of hypotension during general anaesthesia induction. This study aims to compare the haemodynamic effects of TPVB performed at the superficial vs deep layers of the superior costotransverse ligament (SCTL).Methods and analysisThis randomised controlled trial will enrol 128 patients undergoing thoracoscopic lung resection under general anaesthesia. Patients will be randomised (1:1) to one of two groups: the Deep SCTL Block Group (Group T), receiving ultrasound-guided deep layer SCTL block 30 min before surgery in the pre-anaesthesia room, or the Superficial SCTL Block Group (Group S), receiving ultrasound-guided superficial layer SCTL block at the same time point. In addition, a supplementary single-centre exploratory 3D-CT imaging substudy will enrol 40 patients requiring clinically indicated preoperative CT-guided lesion localisation. These participants will be randomly allocated at a 1:1 ratio to receive either deep or superficial SCTL block before CT-guided localisation. 3D-CT imaging will be performed 30 min after block administration to compare contrast-enhanced local anaesthetic spread patterns and explore their potential associations with peri-induction haemodynamic changes.The primary outcome is the incidence of hypotension during general anaesthesia induction. Secondary outcomes include the incidence of severe hypotension during induction, as well as Visual Analogue Scale scores at rest and during movement at 4 and 24 hours postoperatively.Ethics and disseminationThis trial was approved by the Ethics Committee of Nanjing First Hospital (No. KY20240924-03-KS-01) on 24 September 2024. The results will be peer-reviewed for publication in a scientific journal.Trial registration numberNCT06841822.Keywords: Adult anaesthesia, Pain management, Blood Pressure

Introduction

Thoracoscopic lung resection, a minimally invasive surgical approach associated with enhanced recovery, underscores the need for optimised perioperative pain management strategies. Several regional anaesthesia techniques are commonly used for thoracic surgery, including thoracic epidural analgesia, thoracic paravertebral block (TPVB) and erector spinae plane block (ESPB).1 Thoracic epidural analgesia and TPVB provide reliable analgesia but may produce significant sympathetic blockade, which can lead to hypotension, particularly when the block is performed before induction of general anaesthesia. In contrast, ESPB is generally considered haemodynamically more stable because the spread of local anaesthetic to the thoracic paravertebral space is often indirect and variable, although this may result in less consistent neural blockade.2 Because peri-induction hypotension has been associated with an increased risk of organ hypoperfusion and postoperative complications, strategies that maintain effective analgesia while minimising excessive sympathetic inhibition are of clinical interest. TPVB, as one of the most commonly used regional techniques for thoracic surgery, is therefore of particular concern, since its preoperative administration may induce sympathetic blockade and contribute to haemodynamic instability during anaesthesia induction despite improvements in ultrasound-guided safety techniques.3 4

Anatomically, the thoracic paravertebral space is a wedge-shaped compartment bounded by the vertebral column, costotransverse ligaments and pleura, housing critical neurovascular structures. Recent cadaveric studies by Costache et al and high-resolution micro-CT imaging by Cho et al have elucidated two pivotal anatomical insights: (1) the superior costotransverse ligament (SCTL) does not constitute a complete barrier to local anaesthetic diffusion and (2) the paravertebral space exhibits narrow dimensions with direct continuity to adjacent tissue planes.3 5 These findings suggest that the depth of local anaesthetic injection relative to the SCTL may significantly influence the spread of local anaesthetic into the thoracic paravertebral space and thereby affect the extent of sympathetic blockade. Specifically, superficial SCTL injections—where the local anaesthetic is deposited without penetrating the ligament—may facilitate gradual diffusion into the paravertebral space, potentially reducing sympathetic blockade compared with deep SCTL injection.

This study aims to evaluate whether a superficial SCTL block can maintain haemodynamic stability during anaesthesia induction while providing analgesic efficacy comparable to conventional deep SCTL approaches. To further explore the mechanism underlying potential differences, 3D-CT imaging will be used to visualise the spread of local anaesthetic after superficial and deep SCTL block. By combining clinical outcomes with imaging findings, this trial seeks to determine whether modification of the injection plane relative to the SCTL can improve peri-induction haemodynamic stability without compromising analgesic efficacy.

Randomisation and blinding

This study will use stratified block randomisation to allocate participants into two groups at a 1:1 ratio. Randomisation will be stratified by study centre and performed by an independent statistician using a computer-generated randomisation sequence. Allocation will be concealed using sequentially numbered, sealed, opaque envelopes, which will be opened only by the anaesthesiologist performing the nerve block immediately before the procedure.

Because of the nature of the intervention, the anaesthesiologist performing the SCTL block cannot be blinded to group allocation. However, this anaesthesiologist will not be involved in anaesthesia induction, intraoperative management, postoperative care, data collection or outcome assessment. After completion of the block, this investigator will have no further role in perioperative care.

To minimise performance bias, the block procedure will be performed in the pre-anaesthesia room, and the intraoperative anaesthesia team will not be present during block performance. An independent anaesthesiologist, blinded to group allocation, will be responsible for anaesthesia induction, haemodynamic management and intraoperative anaesthesia according to the predefined protocol. The group assignment will not be recorded in the anaesthesia chart or any clinical document accessible to the intraoperative anaesthesia team.

Sensory blockade assessment will be performed by a separate blinded investigator before anaesthesia induction. The results of sensory assessment will be recorded in the case report form but will not be disclosed to the anaesthesia management team, because the extent of sensory blockade may potentially suggest treatment allocation. Outcome assessors responsible for recording peri-induction haemodynamic data and postoperative outcomes will remain blinded to group allocation throughout the study. Participants will also remain unaware of their allocated intervention.

To prevent inadvertent unblinding, communication regarding treatment allocation will be restricted to the block performer only. Study personnel involved in anaesthesia induction, haemodynamic management, postoperative follow-up, outcome assessment and statistical analysis will be instructed not to discuss allocation information. Any accidental disclosure of group allocation, emergency unblinding or protocol deviation related to blinding will be documented in the case report form and reported to the study coordinating centre. These cases will be considered in the per-protocol and sensitivity analyses when appropriate.

For the supplementary 3D-CT imaging substudy conducted exclusively at Nanjing First Hospital, eligible patients will be allocated into two groups using simple randomisation without stratification factors. Radiologists performing the scans and imaging assessment will remain blinded to group allocation.

Anaesthesia

Preoperative routine follow-up is conducted 1 day prior to surgery for all patients. The patient’s age-adjusted Charlson Comorbidity Index and Clinical Frailty Scale are recorded, along with the patient’s BP in the ward. All patients are required to fast for 8 hours prior to the surgery. After entering the anaesthesia preparation room, peripheral venous access is established, and non-invasive monitoring of BP, ECG and oxygen saturation is performed. All patients undergo invasive BP monitoring with radial artery catheterisation under local anaesthesia and the FloTrac/Vigileo (EV 1000 system, Edwards Lifesciences, Irvine, California, USA) haemodynamic monitoring system is established. Midazolam is administered at 0.01 mg/kg to maintain a calm environment for the patient for at least 5 min, and heart rate (HR) and MAP are measured every minute for three consecutive times, with the average value recorded as the baseline.5 During the nerve block, the anaesthesiologist opens a sealed opaque envelope containing the patient’s group assignment and performs the nerve block accordingly. Group S receives the superficial layer of SCTL, while Group T receives the deep layer.

After 30 min, the patient is transferred to the operating room, and general anaesthesia is administered. No fluids are given prior to anaesthesia induction. During the study period, all patients receive balanced crystalloids at a rate of 10 mL/kg/hour. The anaesthesiologist administers general anaesthesia induction as follows: two target-controlled infusion (TCI) pumps are used, delivering 1% propofol (4.0 µg/mL) for the initial target concentration (effect-site, modified Marsh model, ke0=1.21 /min) and remifentanil (5 ng/mL) at a fixed target concentration (plasma-site, Minto model). Remifentanil’s target concentration (5 ng/mL) is reached 5 min after administration. Propofol is titrated in increments of 0.1 µg/mL to maintain a BIS of 40–50. Following neuromuscular blockade monitoring, rocuronium (0.6 mg/kg) is given after loss of consciousness for muscle relaxation. After confirming that remifentanil has reached the target concentration (5 ng/mL) and Train-of-Four (TOF)=0, tracheal intubation is performed. HR and MAP are measured every minute from the start of induction to 5 min post-induction.

Both groups undergo tracheal intubation 5 min after anaesthesia induction to compare the haemodynamic effects of different nerve blocks. Video laryngoscopy is used for left double-lumen bronchial intubation, and mechanical ventilation is established with fibreoptic bronchoscopy for positioning. The ventilation mode is set to pressure-controlled ventilation–volume guaranteed, with tidal volume (VT) 6–8 mL/kg, FiO2 40%, positive end-expiratory pressure 5 cm H2O, I:E 1:2 and RR adjusted to maintain partial pressure of end-tidal carbon dioxide at 35–45 mm Hg. After induction, sufentanil (0.3 µg/kg) is given, and anaesthesia is maintained with TCI pumps for propofol (1.5–3.5 µg/mL) and remifentanil (4.0–8.0 ng/mL) based on the surgical situation. Rocuronium is intermittently administered to maintain muscle relaxation, and anaesthesia depth is monitored to maintain a bispectral index (BIS) of 40–60. After one-lung ventilation begins, VT is adjusted to 4–6 mL/kg while other respiratory parameters remain unchanged. Throughout surgery, the surgeon assesses for any nerve block-related complications, including bleeding or accidental pleural puncture.

30 min before the end of surgery, a single dose of oxycodone (0.03 mg/kg) is administered. Postoperatively, all patients are transferred to the Post-Anaesthesia Care Unit for recovery. Extubation occurs once the muscle relaxation test (TOF≥0.9) is passed and the patient is awake. Postoperative pain management is provided with patient-controlled intravenous analgesia, consisting of oxycodone (20 mg), tropisetron (6 mg) and saline to 100 mL, with a background dose of 0.5 mL/hour and a bolus dose of 5 mL every 8 min.

Hypotension is categorised as a MAP reduction of 20% or an absolute MAP below 65 mm Hg, while severe hypotension is defined as a MAP reduction of 30% or an absolute MAP below 55 mm Hg. For hypotension management, if the HR exceeds 40 bpm, 50 µg of phenylephrine is administered. If HR is under 40 bpm, 6 mg of ephedrine is given. In cases where systolic arterial pressure rises more than 20% above baseline, 300 µg of nicardipine may be administered. Bradycardia (HR<40 bpm) is treated with 0.5 mg of atropine, and tachycardia (HR>100 bpm) is managed with esmolol at a dose of 0.5 mg/kg.

During the nerve block procedure, the patient is placed in the lateral position on the unaffected side with full exposure of the back. Ultrasound is used to locate and mark the T4 and T6 spinous processes, and sterile technique is applied for disinfection. A portable colour Doppler ultrasound device with a low-frequency probe is used to identify the SCTL, gradually moving the probe along the posterior axillary line of the affected side until the SCTL is visible. The needle is then inserted based on group assignment, either to the superficial (non-penetrating) or deep (penetrating) layer of the SCTL. After confirming no blood, cerebrospinal fluid, or air on aspiration, 0.375% ropivacaine (20 mL) is injected at the T4 and T6 target sites.

Following the block, patients will remain in the supine position. After 30 min, a blinded assessor will evaluate the extent of sensory loss using a cold stimulus at the anterior chest wall (midclavicular line), lateral chest wall (posterior axillary line) and posterior chest wall (paravertebral region). Cold sensitivity testing with a temperature method is performed at the relevant blockade areas along the midaxillary line at 5, 10, 20 and 30 min after the block, and the blockade level is recorded.

In the supplementary 3D-CT imaging substudy, patients who require clinically indicated preoperative CT-guided lesion localisation at Nanjing First Hospital will be screened for eligibility. This substudy will include 40 patients, with 20 patients allocated to the deep SCTL block group and 20 patients allocated to the superficial SCTL block group. 30 min before CT-guided lesion localisation, patients will receive either an ultrasound-guided deep SCTL block or an ultrasound-guided superficial SCTL block on the surgical side at the T4 and T6 levels. A total of 20 mL of injectate will be administered at each level, consisting of 0.375% ropivacaine mixed with iohexol, with a final volume of 20 mL per injection site. Patients will then undergo standard CT-guided lesion localisation, and 3D-CT reconstruction will be performed to assess the spread of contrast-enhanced local anaesthetic.

The 3D-CT imaging substudy is designed as a single-centre exploratory mechanistic component rather than a confirmatory efficacy analysis. It will not contribute to the primary sample size calculation or the primary efficacy analysis of the main multicentre clinical trial. The rationale for this substudy is that the different haemodynamic effects of superficial and deep SCTL block may be related to differences in the anatomical spread of local anaesthetic and the consequent extent of sympathetic blockade. We hypothesise that deep SCTL block may allow more direct or extensive spread of local anaesthetic into the thoracic paravertebral space and adjacent neural structures, potentially resulting in greater sympathetic blockade and a higher risk of BP reduction during anaesthesia induction. In contrast, superficial SCTL block may produce a more limited or gradual spread pattern, thereby attenuating sympathetic inhibition while preserving postoperative analgesic efficacy.

Because sympathetic blockade cannot be directly visualised by CT imaging, the distribution of contrast-enhanced injectate will be used as an anatomical surrogate for the potential extent of neural and sympathetic involvement. Imaging outcomes will include spread to the paravertebral space, neural foramina, epidural space, intercostal space and the craniocaudal extent of distribution. These imaging outcomes will be analysed descriptively and comparatively between the two groups.

Exploratory analyses will be performed, where feasible, to examine whether predefined imaging spread patterns are associated with peri-induction haemodynamic variables, including hypotension occurrence and changes in mean arterial pressure (MAP), stroke volume (SV), cardiac index (CI) and stroke volume index (SVI). However, given the small sample size and single-centre nature of the imaging substudy, these imaging-haemodynamic association analyses will be interpreted as exploratory and hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory evidence.

Data collection

An independent investigator screens patients according to the eligibility criteria and collects the demographics and baseline characteristics. The outcome measurements and perioperative data are documented by independent research investigators via postoperative patient visits and reviewing electronic medical records. All data are recorded in the case report forms and registered in the electronic database. An independent data monitoring committee conducts an ongoing data review throughout this study. Patients’ confidentiality will be ensured and deidentified data will be analysed.

Sample size estimation

Preliminary data from a single-centre pilot study conducted at Nanjing First Hospital showed that the incidence of hypotension during anaesthesia induction was 76.2% (16/21) in the deep-layer SCTL block group and 52.9% (9/17) in the superficial-layer SCTL block group. The pilot study used the same anaesthesia induction protocol, nerve block technique, haemodynamic monitoring method, hypotension definition and outcome assessment procedures as the present trial. Based on these preliminary data, with a two-sided significance level of 0.05 and 80% power, Power Analysis and Sample Size software estimated that 57 participants would be required in each group. After allowing for an anticipated 10% dropout rate, 128 participants will be recruited for the main clinical trial.

Because this is an individually randomised multicentre trial, the original sample size calculation was performed at the individual-patient level. Randomisation will be stratified by study centre, and the anaesthesia protocol, nerve block technique, haemodynamic management strategy, monitoring method and primary outcome definition will be standardised across all participating centres. Therefore, substantial between-centre clustering is not expected. Nevertheless, we acknowledge that centre-level variability may reduce the effective sample size and should be explicitly considered.

To evaluate the potential impact of centre-level clustering, we estimated the design effect using the formula: DE=1+(m−1)×Intraclass Correlation Coefficient (ICC), where m represents the average number of participants per centre and ICC represents the intraclass correlation coefficient. With 128 participants recruited from five centres, the average cluster size is approximately 25.6. Because reliable published ICC estimates are unavailable for this specific endpoint and intervention setting, sensitivity calculations were performed using plausible ICC values of 0.01 and 0.02. The corresponding design effects were 1.246 and 1.492, resulting in effective sample sizes of approximately 103 and 86 participants, respectively. These calculations suggest that the trial may be underpowered if the true between-centre variability is higher than anticipated.

To minimise the influence of imbalanced centre recruitment, enrolment will be monitored throughout the study and distributed across centres as evenly as feasible. No single centre is expected to contribute more than 50% or fewer than 10% of the main trial population. In the primary analysis, study centre will be included as a random effect in a generalised linear mixed model to account for potential between-centre variability. The robustness of the primary findings will also be assessed using ICC-based sensitivity analyses. If the observed centre-level variability is greater than anticipated, this limitation will be acknowledged when interpreting the trial results.

In addition, the supplementary 3D-CT imaging substudy will include 40 patients, with 20 patients allocated to each group. This substudy is designed as a single-centre exploratory mechanistic component to compare local anaesthetic contrast spread patterns between superficial and deep SCTL block. It is not powered for confirmatory efficacy analysis and will not contribute to the primary sample size calculation of the main clinical trial.

Statistical analysis

The primary outcome, defined as the incidence of hypotension during anaesthesia induction, will be analysed according to the intention-to-treat principle. A per-protocol analysis will be performed as a supportive analysis. In case of discrepant findings, the intention-to-treat analysis will be considered the primary inference.

Because this is a multicentre randomised trial, the primary analysis will use a generalised linear mixed model with a logit link. Treatment group will be included as the main fixed effect, and study centre will be included as a random intercept to account for potential clustering by centre. An adjusted GLMM will also be performed by including prespecified baseline covariates, including age, sex, BMI, ASA class and baseline MAP, as fixed effects. These covariates were selected a priori based on clinical relevance rather than data-driven selection.

Sensitivity analyses will be performed to assess the robustness of the primary findings, including per-protocol analysis, missing-data sensitivity analyses and ICC-based sensitivity analyses assuming plausible ICC values of 0.01–0.02. Missing data will be handled using multiple imputation under the missing-at-random assumption, and extreme-case best-case and worst-case scenarios will also be explored.

Continuous secondary outcomes will be compared using independent t-tests or Mann-Whitney U tests, as appropriate. Categorical secondary outcomes will be analysed using χ² tests or Fisher’s exact tests. Time-to-event outcomes, if applicable, will be analysed using Cox proportional hazards models or mixed-effects Cox models. Exploratory analyses of hypotension burden, including the duration of hypotension and area under the MAP threshold of 65 mm Hg during induction, will be performed using continuously recorded arterial pressure data.

No formal adjustment for multiplicity will be applied to secondary or exploratory outcomes; therefore, these analyses will be interpreted cautiously. A two-sided p value<0.05 will be considered statistically significant for the primary outcome. Statistical analyses will be conducted using R software. A detailed statistical analysis plan will be finalised before database lock and before completion of patient enrolment.

Discussion

In this multicentre, randomised double-blind, prospective, parallel controlled trial, the objective of our study is to evaluate the effects of the deep layer SCTL block and the superficial layer SCTL block on hypotension during general anaesthesia induction in patients undergoing thoracoscopic pulmonary resection and to compare their postoperative analgesic efficacy. Our primary hypothesis is that the superficial-layer SCTL block, characterised by reduced initial diffusion of local anaesthetic agents into the thoracic paravertebral space and a slower pharmacokinetic profile, may attenuate sympathetic inhibition, thereby mitigating the risk of hypotension during anaesthesia induction. Furthermore, we hypothesise that, despite differences in diffusion kinetics, gradual permeation of local anaesthetics into the thoracic paravertebral space via the superficial-layer SCTL block is hypothesised to achieve postoperative pain control comparable to that of the deep-layer SCTL block.

Emerging evidence underscores the critical association between intraoperative hypotension and postoperative morbidity. Both the severity and cumulative duration of hypotension exhibit progressive correlations with major complications, including myocardial injury, acute kidney injury and mortality.6–8 Notably, up to 34% of intraoperative hypotension episodes occur during the vulnerable window between anaesthesia induction and surgical incision, a period characterised by abrupt sympathetic inhibition and haemodynamic instability.7 This early-phase hypotension may disproportionately contribute to organ hypoperfusion due to the absence of compensatory surgical stimuli, amplifying its pathophysiological impact.9 Consequently, optimising haemodynamic stability during induction has emerged as a key preventive strategy in enhanced recovery protocols.

We hypothesise that the superficial-layer SCTL block, by modulating local anaesthetic pharmacokinetics to achieve gradual thoracic paravertebral diffusion, may preserve sympathetic tone more effectively than the deep-layer approach. This mechanism-driven design aims to address the clinically urgent need to mitigate induction-phase hypotension while maintaining comparable postoperative analgesia—a dual objective aligned with current recommendations for haemodynamic precision management.9

The supplementary 3D-CT imaging substudy is intended to provide anatomical evidence to support the hypothesised mechanism underlying the different haemodynamic effects of the two SCTL block approaches. We speculate that deep SCTL block may allow more direct and extensive spread of local anaesthetic into the thoracic paravertebral space and adjacent neural structures, which may result in greater sympathetic blockade and more pronounced BP reduction during anaesthesia induction. In contrast, superficial SCTL block may produce a more limited or gradual spread pattern, thereby attenuating sympathetic inhibition while maintaining postoperative analgesic efficacy. Importantly, CT imaging cannot directly demonstrate sympathetic blockade. Therefore, the distribution of contrast-enhanced injectate will be interpreted as an anatomical surrogate for the potential extent of neural and sympathetic involvement. Any associations between imaging spread patterns and peri-induction haemodynamic outcomes, including hypotension occurrence and changes in MAP, SV, CI and SVI, will be considered exploratory and hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory evidence.

This study has several limitations. First, due to concerns regarding radiation exposure and contrast administration, 3D-CT imaging will not be performed in all participants in the main clinical trial. Instead, the imaging substudy will be restricted to patients who already require clinically indicated preoperative CT-guided lesion localisation at Nanjing First Hospital. Therefore, the imaging substudy is single-centre in nature and includes a relatively small sample size. Second, because the 3D-CT substudy is designed as an exploratory mechanistic component, it is not powered to provide confirmatory evidence regarding imaging-haemodynamic associations. Any observed relationship between contrast-enhanced local anaesthetic spread and peri-induction haemodynamic changes should therefore be interpreted cautiously and considered hypothesis-generating. Third, CT imaging can visualise the anatomical distribution of contrast-enhanced injectate, but it cannot directly demonstrate the functional extent of sympathetic blockade. Thus, imaging findings will serve only as an anatomical surrogate for potential neural and sympathetic involvement.
