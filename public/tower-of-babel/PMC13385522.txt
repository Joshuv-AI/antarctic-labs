Reverse cardio-oncology: neuroendocrine axis activation and cardiovascular-disease-derived factors synergistically remodel the tumor microenvironment
Authors: Siyu Ji, Nan Zhang, Xin Hu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCardiovascular disease and cancer are increasingly recognized as biologically interconnected conditions with a bidirectional relationship. In addition to the well-established cardiotoxic effects of cancer therapies, accumulating epidemiological and experimental evidence suggests that cardiovascular disease may influence cancer initiation, progression, and metastasis, a concept referred to as reverse cardio-oncology. However, the mechanisms linking cardiovascular pathology to tumor microenvironment remodeling remain incompletely defined and are likely to involve multiple interacting systemic and local pathways. In this review, we summarize current evidence regarding the potential contribution of cardiovascular stress, neuroendocrine activation, metabolic dysregulation, impaired oxygen delivery, physical inactivity, and cardiovascular disease-derived secretory factors to tumor microenvironment remodeling. Particular attention is given to vascular endothelial growth factor, extracellular vesicles, reactive oxygen species, and inflammatory cytokines, which may act as interconnected mediators of angiogenesis, immune suppression, oxidative stress, and metastatic progression. We further discuss how these pathways may converge within a broader hierarchical and network-based framework, while emphasizing that much of the current mechanistic evidence remains derived from observational studies, animal models, and in vitro systems. Finally, we highlight therapeutic implications, including modulation of neuroendocrine and inflammatory signaling, and propose future directions involving human tissues, clinical samples, and heart-cancer-on-a-chip platforms. Overall, this review aims to provide a balanced synthesis of current knowledge and unresolved questions in reverse cardio-oncology, with the goal of supporting more integrated investigation of cardiovascular disease-cancer interactions.Keywords: Reverse cardio-oncology, Neuroendocrine axis, Cardiovascular disease, Secreted factors, Tumor microenvironment remodeling

Reverse cardio-oncology

Evolution and definition of reverse cardio-oncology

Cardiovascular disease (CVD) and cancer are the leading causes of death worldwide, accounting for nearly 50% of global mortality [1]. According to data from the World Health Organization, CVD causes approximately 17.9 million deaths annually, whereas cancer accounts for 10 million [2]. Moreover, emerging evidence indicates a complex bidirectional relationship between the two conditions [3].

Cardio-oncology has traditionally focused on the cardiovascular effects of cancer and its therapies, primarily addressing the cardiotoxicity of chemotherapy drugs, cardiovascular damage from radiotherapy, and cardiovascular adverse events caused by targeted therapies [4]. However, clinical observations and basic research have revealed that CVD also affects tumor growth and metastasis [5]. Large-scale epidemiological studies have consistently demonstrated significantly increased cancer incidence and mortality among patients with existing CVD, leading to the concept of “reverse cardio-oncology” [6–18] (Fig. 1). The emergence of this field marks an important shift in our understanding of the CVD–cancer relationship from unidirectional tumor effects on the heart to bidirectional interactions.

Stage-dependent CVD-cancer crosstalk

The crosstalk between CVD and cancer is unlikely to be static; rather, it may vary according to the stage, severity, and chronicity of cardiovascular disease. At the population level, patients with pre-existing CVD, particularly those with atherosclerotic CVD and heart failure, have a significantly higher risk of developing cancer compared with those without CVD, even after controlling for conventional risk factors [19]. In early-stage CVD, microvascular endothelial dysfunction is associated with a greater than two-fold increased risk of subsequent solid-tumor cancer, as demonstrated in a prospective clinical cohort study [20]. In contrast, late-stage cardiovascular disease is associated with marked systemic disturbances via innate immune reprogramming. These changes may favor the expansion of immunosuppressive myeloid populations, potentially creating a permissive environment for tumor progression and metastasis [21].

Clinical heterogeneity: disease specificity, prognosis, and demographics

The pro-tumorigenic impact of cardiovascular disease (CVD) on tumor progression is highly heterogeneous across various cardiac pathological states. In terms of association strength, heart failure (HF) and acute myocardial infarction (AMI) are the two cardiac pathologies most closely linked to an increased risk of incident cancer. A meta-analysis of 13 studies demonstrated that HF increases overall cancer risk by 30% (HR = 1.30) [22]. While a nationwide cohort study confirmed that the risk of hematological malignancies rises by 49% following AMI (HR = 1.49) [23]. The magnitude of this risk is closely tied to the severity of cardiac remodeling and reduced ejection fraction [24]. In contrast, the association between isolated cardiovascular risk factors (e.g., hypertension, hyperlipidemia) and cancer tends to be weaker or more dependent on long-term exposure. For instance, evidence indicating that blood pressure reduction is not associated with incident cancer risk (HR = 1.03, 95% CI 0.99–1.06) directly supports this weaker association strength [25]. This essentially forms a risk gradient based on disease severity and pathological type.

Regarding mechanistic drivers and prognostic impacts, different etiologies exhibit distinct temporal characteristics. Acute pathological processes, such as MI, trigger a rapid surge in neuroendocrine stress and the release of specific extracellular vesicles, such as those enriched with miR-22-3p [26]. These factors acutely remodel the tumor microenvironment (TME) by inhibiting ferroptosis and promoting angiogenesis, typically leading to accelerated tumor growth and a rapid deterioration of patient prognosis [21]. Chronic pathological processes, such as atherosclerosis, act as a persistent “incubator” for cancer. Long-term exposure to low-grade systemic inflammatory fluxes (such as IL-1β and IL-6) and oxidative stress from sources like oxidized low-density lipoprotein (ox-LDL) establishes a durable immunosuppressive environment that supports long-term tumor evolution and immune escape [6, 27]. This environment primarily affects patients’ long-term survival outcomes.

Demographically, reverse cardio-oncology is primarily a hallmark of adult and elderly populations, driven by “inflammaging” and accumulated metabolic dysregulation [4]. In pediatric populations, the heart-cancer interaction remains distinct, with research overwhelmingly focused on “classical cardio-oncology”—specifically, myocardial damage induced by therapies such as anthracyclines. Currently, there is insufficient evidence to suggest that acquired cardiovascular remodeling in the developing pediatric population would drive cancer progression [28].

Hierarchical mechanisms: neuroendocrine overactivation and CVD-derived secretory factors

Although the epidemiological association between CVD and an increased risk of cancer has been well established, most existing reviews in reverse cardio-oncology have described individual CVD-derived factors as largely independent, parallel pathways. What remains lacking is a unified framework that defines the regulatory hierarchy among these factors.

This review addresses this gap by proposing a systems-level model in which the neuroendocrine axis serves as a central systemic modulator that orchestrates downstream pro-tumorigenic signals. Specifically, we define a signaling cascade in which the neuroendocrine axis centrally integrates and systemically amplifies local pro-tumorigenic signals that are initially triggered by direct cardiovascular stress. These signals are then propagated through secondary amplifiers, which comprise circulating CVD-derived factors, and ultimately drive the terminal effectors that remodel the TME.

At the origin of this cascade, direct cardiovascular stress acts as the initial trigger. Biomechanical stretch imposed on the failing myocardium, local ischemia and necrosis following myocardial infarction, and hemodynamic shear stress on the atherosclerotic endothelium directly stimulate the release of pro-tumorigenic factors [29–31]. However, the magnitude of these locally released factors alone is insufficient to account for the systemic pro-tumorigenic environment observed in CVD patients. It is the neuroendocrine axis that centrally integrates and massively amplifies these signals, transforming localized pathology into a systemic response.

Various CVD conditions therefore elicit severe stress responses that aberrantly activate three major neuroendocrine pathways: the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system (RAAS), the sympathetic nervous system (SNS), and the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis. RAAS serves as a key pathway for the maintenance of circulatory homeostasis, but it is frequently hyperactivated in HF, hypertension, cardiac hypertrophy, and atherosclerosis [32]. Similarly, acute or chronic stress triggers rapid SNS activation, which not only precipitates acute cardiovascular events but also increases vascular stiffness, further elevating CVD risk [33, 34]. Furthermore, patients with CVD consistently exhibit long-term HPA axis dysfunction, characterized by disrupted circadian rhythms and persistently elevated cortisol levels [35, 36]. The neuroendocrine axis integrates cardiovascular stress signals and may amplify downstream inflammatory, vascular, and metabolic responses.

Subordinate to, yet synergizing with this centrally coordinated neuroendocrine activation, is the massive mobilization of secondary amplifiers. These biological macromolecules are either directly secreted from damaged cardiovascular tissues as a local response to injury, or systemically disseminated under the governance of neuroendocrine signals that amplify their production.

Ultimately, this hierarchically coordinated network systematically remodels the TME. These localized TME changes act as the terminal effectors of the cascade, acting collectively to promote cancer cell proliferation, enhance angiogenesis, suppress anti-tumor immunity, and facilitate metastatic dissemination [27, 37].

Importantly, this CVD-driven hierarchical cascade does not operate in a vacuum. It is profoundly modulated by shared clinical risk factors, including aging, obesity, diabetes, and smoking [38, 39] (Fig. 2). Rather than acting merely as parallel confounders that independently increase the risk of both diseases, these factors serve as critical “effect modifiers” within the reverse cardio-oncology axis [40]. They pre-establish a baseline of systemic inflammation, oxidative stress, and metabolic dysregulation. Consequently, when cardiovascular stress triggers the neuroendocrine and secretory networks described above, this primed pathological “soil” synergistically amplifies the cascade, accelerating TME remodeling and immune evasion far more aggressively than in patients without these underlying conditions.

Potential influence of CVD on selected features of the tumor microenvironment

The TME is a complex dynamic system that forms within and around tumors, primarily composed of tumor cells and various stromal cells [41, 42]. It profoundly influences tumor biological behavior through intricate intercellular interactions, making TME remodeling a central driver of malignant progression [42, 43]. However, in the context of reverse cardio-oncology, this remodeling is not exclusively governed by tumor-intrinsic factors. Instead, it is significantly accelerated by systemic physiological disruptions induced by CVD.

CVD-specific cancer spectrum and tissue-specific TME remodeling

Large-scale primary epidemiological studies have established that HF is most robustly linked to increased incidences of lung, gastrointestinal, and hematological cancers [22], while acute myocardial infarction exhibits high specificity for hematological malignancies, with a 49% increased risk observed in nationwide cohorts [23].

This disease-type specificity is underpinned by distinct, tissue-directed mechanisms, as demonstrated in animal models. In the mammary TME, mouse models of MI have shown that the infarcted heart systemically reprograms innate immunity by triggering the epigenetic expansion of pro-tumorigenic Ly6Chi monocytes in the bone marrow. These cells preferentially home to mammary adipose tissue, remodeling the local TME into an inflammatory environment that facilitates breast cancer metastasis [21]. In mouse models of post-MI heart failure, Caller et al. demonstrated that cMSC-sEVs promote the clonogenic capacity of lung and colon carcinoma cell lines more potently than breast cancer or melanoma cells, indicating that sEV-mediated cardiac-tumor crosstalk exhibits target-tissue selectivity [44]. Consistent with this concept of differential tissue sensitivity, other models of cardiac remodeling have also shown that cardiac-derived factors promote tumor growth in a tissue-dependent manner [24].

While these preclinical models highlight the targeted delivery of CVD-derived signals, the ultimate tissue specificity of CVD-associated cancer risk remains incompletely understood. It is highly likely that this organotropism depends on the inherent receptivity of the target organs, including local vascular density, stromal composition, immune contexture, organ innervation, metabolic state, and pre-existing inflammatory niches.

Impact of CVD-associated metabolic dysregulation on the TME

Tumor cells undergo extensive metabolic reprogramming, including enhanced glycolysis, glutamine addiction, and altered lipid metabolism, to sustain rapid proliferation under nutrient-scarce conditions [42, 45–49]. Beyond these tumor-intrinsic changes, the systemic metabolic milieu is profoundly altered in patients with CVD, creating a permissive environment that complements tumor metabolic adaptation.

Regarding fatty acid metabolism, heart failure induces systemic lipolysis, elevating circulating free fatty acids. Tumor cells exploit this lipid-rich milieu through CD36-mediated fatty acid uptake, which fuels mitochondrial β-oxidation and promotes the accumulation of lipid droplets that buffer oxidative stress [50–52]. With respect to ketone body metabolism, the failing heart shifts from fatty acid oxidation toward ketone body utilization, raising circulating β-hydroxybutyrate levels [53, 54]. While β-hydroxybutyrate may exert anti-tumor effects under certain conditions [55, 56], it can also be co-opted by cancer cells to support metabolic and epigenetic adaptation [57, 58]. In terms of lactate metabolism, CVD-associated hypoperfusion stimulates anaerobic glycolysis, elevating circulating lactate independently of tumor-derived lactate. Within the TME, this exogenous lactate promotes M2 macrophage polarization, suppresses CD8+ T cell responses, and activates regulatory T cells, fostering an immunosuppressive microenvironment that favors tumor immune evasion [59–61].

CVD-driven immunosuppression within the TME

During tumor development, the TME gradually transitions into an immune-suppressive microenvironment. This is typically characterized by the polarization of tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) toward the M2 phenotype, regulatory T cell infiltration, and effector T cell exhaustion, alongside the secretion of inhibitory cytokines (e.g., IL-10, TGF-β) [45, 46, 62].

Crucially, CVD actively accelerates these specific immunosuppressive transitions through both systemic cell mobilization and direct intercellular communication. Systemically, CVD-associated neuroendocrine overactivation triggers the chronic mobilization of myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) from the bone marrow. Upon recruitment to the TME, these MDSCs not only suppress cytotoxic immunity but also act as potent drivers for local Treg infiltration and expansion [63]. Locally, CVD exerts direct immune modulation that exacerbates macrophage reprogramming and subsequent T cell exhaustion. For instance, a landmark study demonstrated that sEVs released from infarcted cardiac fibroblasts travel systemically to the TME and induce macrophages to significantly upregulate PD-L1 expression [44]. This cardiac-driven molecular signal directly triggers effector T cell exhaustion, successfully shielding the tumor from T-cell-mediated attacks. Together, these mechanisms underscore that CVD-induced immune remodeling is a targeted, disease-specific acceleration of the classic tumor immune evasion program, perfectly matching the suppressive phenotypes typically required for tumor progression.

CVD-related oxygen delivery deficits and localized hypoxia

Solid tumors inherently develop local hypoxia due to their abnormal vasculature, which drives pro-metastatic tumor phenotypes [47, 64, 65]. Cardiovascular disease amplifies this hypoxic state through two distinct mechanisms.

First, impaired cardiac function limits systemic oxygen delivery. In heart failure, reduced cardiac output restricts peripheral oxygen supply, creating a chronic systemic hypoxia that may stabilize HIF-1α in distal tissues prior to overt tumor formation [66–69]. Second, CVD-related microvascular pathology, including endothelial dysfunction and capillary rarefaction, generates localized hypoxic niches that favor the selection of aggressive, hypoxia-tolerant malignant clones [70–72]. CVD-associated oxidative stress further reinforces this hypoxic remodeling, as excessive ROS contribute to HIF-1α stabilization and extracellular matrix alterations [73].

Impact of physical inactivity and exercise intolerance on the TME

Physical inactivity and reduced exercise capacity are hallmark features of CVD, particularly heart failure and ischemic heart disease. Rather than being a mere symptom, this decline in activity actively reshapes the TME through metabolic and immunological pathways.

CVD patients frequently exhibit impaired exercise tolerance due to reduced cardiac output, skeletal muscle deconditioning, and chronotropic incompetence [74]. This sedentary state promotes insulin resistance and chronic low-grade inflammation, providing a continuous supply of metabolic substrates for tumor progression [75–77]. In parallel, reduced muscle contraction leads to a decline in the secretion of protective myokines (e.g., SPARC, irisin), compromising the host’s ability to inhibit tumor cell proliferation and triggering deregulated glucose and lipid metabolism that fuels the TME [78–82]. Immunologically, physical inactivity reduces the mobilization of cytotoxic immune cells, particularly NK cells and CD8+ T cells, limiting their recruitment to the TME [83]. Reduced blood flow shear stress further exacerbates endothelial dysfunction and intratumoral hypoxia, collectively fostering an immunosuppressive microenvironment that facilitates tumor immune evasion [84, 85].

Neuroendocrine regulation of CVD-derived secretory factors and TME remodeling

Building upon the hierarchical framework established in Sect. 4, the following sections examine how the neuroendocrine axis orchestrates the production and systemic dissemination of key circulating factors that collectively remodel the TME. These factors include VEGF, extracellular vesicles, reactive oxygen species, and inflammatory cytokines. Each is positioned as a secondary amplifier within the signaling cascade: their secretion is triggered by neuroendocrine signals, as well as by direct cardiovascular stress, and they execute specific pro-tumorigenic functions within the TME.

Angiogenic factors (exemplified by vascular endothelial growth factor, VEGF)

VEGF, a signaling protein for vasculogenesis and angiogenesis, functions as a core angiogenic molecule by participating in proliferation and migration of endothelial and smooth muscle cells [86, 87]. Furthermore, it serves as both a key participant in CVD pathological processes and an important molecular bridge connecting CVD with tumorigenesis [88, 89].

Elevated VEGF in CVD patients

Clinical studies have consistently demonstrated elevated circulating VEGF levels across various forms of CVD [90]. In elderly patients, heart failure is associated with elevated plasma VEGF-D, whereas acute myocardial infarction shows higher VEGF-C [91], suggesting that distinct cardiac pathologies engage different VEGF family members. Mendelian randomization analysis has provided genetic evidence consistent with a directional role of elevated VEGF-D in cardiovascular mortality, although these data do not establish causality [92]. From a reverse cardio-oncology perspective, a prospective multicenter study found that circulating VEGF levels in CVD patients were positively correlated with subsequent cancer risk, with a 1.3-fold increase per standard deviation [93]. However, the available human evidence remains observational, and confounding by shared risk factors, as well as the possibility of reverse causation, limits causal inference.

Upstream drivers and downstream amplifiers: hierarchical regulation of VEGF

VEGF production in the context of CVD is governed by a hierarchical regulatory network, with the neuroendocrine axis serving as the primary systemic driver. This neuroendocrine-VEGF axis operates through multiple converging signals.

A rat model of coronary artery ligation has shown that acute myocardial ischemia elevates corticotropin-releasing hormone and corticosterone levels through the HPA axis, accompanied by sympathetic activation and impaired cardiac function [94]. Elevated serum cortisol in patients with chronic heart failure has been associated with oxidative stress and adverse cardiovascular events [95]. Further experimental evidence indicates that this sympathetic overdrive directly upregulates VEGF in cardiovascular tissues: for instance, ten consecutive days of daily restraint stress significantly elevated VEGF protein levels in the heart and aorta of mice [96]. At the cellular level, catecholamines are the primary effectors of the sympathetic nervous system. They have been widely documented to directly stimulate VEGF transcription in vascular endothelial cells via β-adrenergic receptor activation [97].

Beyond this systemic neuroendocrine drive, VEGF can also be induced directly by local cardiovascular pathology. Myocardial ischemia activates HIF-1α to directly upregulate VEGF gene transcription, providing a neuroendocrine-independent pathway through which the injured heart stimulates angiogenesis [98].

VEGF-mediated TME remodeling in CVD

Persistently elevated VEGF in CVD patients remodels the TME through two principal mechanisms: promoting angiogenesis and modulating anti-tumor immunity.

In the aspect of angiogenesis and vascular remodeling, VEGF-A binds to VEGFR-2 on endothelial cells, activating several downstream signaling cascades [99, 100]. The PI3K/AKT pathway converts PIP₂ to PIP₃, inducing AKT phosphorylation, which in turn activates eNOS to produce nitric oxide and promote endothelial cell proliferation and migration [101, 102]. The PLCγ/PKC pathway increases intracellular Ca2 + levels, enhancing endothelial cell migration [88, 103], while the MAPK pathway further regulates endothelial cell proliferation and survival [101]. The Notch pathway finely controls endothelial cell differentiation and vascular branching [104]. Through these pathways, VEGF drives tumor vascularization once lesions exceed the diffusion-limited size of 1–2 mm [105], increases vascular permeability to promote a tumorigenic molecular environment [106], and produces structurally abnormal vessels with discontinuous basement membranes that can facilitate circulating tumor cell extravasation and distant metastasis [107].

VEGF further contributes to an immunosuppressive TME. VEGF can promote tumor immune escape by inducing PD-L1 expression on endothelial cells [108], and TAMs contribute to vascular network formation in a VEGF-dependent manner [109]. These pro-angiogenic and immunosuppressive effects may partly explain the elevated cancer risk observed in CVD patients.

However, the mechanistic evidence linking CVD-derived VEGF to specific TME remodeling events derives predominantly from animal models and in vitro systems. No human clinical study has yet directly elucidated the molecular mechanisms connecting CVD-associated VEGF signaling to cancer development or TME remodeling. Future studies should therefore employ human-based vascularized TME models, such as patient-derived tumor organoids or tumor-on-a-chip platforms incorporating perfusable vascular structures, to better define the causal role of CVD-derived VEGF signals.

Extracellular vesicles: miRnas and cfDNA as representative cargos

Extracellular vesicles (EVs) are lipid bilayer vesicles that are released from cells through diverse biogenesis pathways. Consistent with the position statement of the International Society for Extracellular Vesicles (MISEV2023), we adopt the broader term “extracellular vesicles” throughout this section, as most studies in cardio-oncology have not yet confirmed the biogenesis of these vesicles specifically via the multivesicular body pathway [110]. Under pathological conditions, the quantity and cargo composition of EVs secreted by cardiovascular cells undergo significant changes, forming disease-specific molecular signatures that include both miRNAs and cell-free DNA (cfDNA) [111, 112]. EVs serve as intercellular information carriers, protecting their cargo from degradation in the bloodstream and enabling stable transport to distant tissues [111, 113].

Hierarchical regulation of EV secretion in CVD—miRnas as a representative cargo

EV-miRNA levels are consistently altered across the CVD spectrum. In a population-based cohort, elevated serum miR-126-3p was prospectively associated with incident heart failure, and Mendelian randomization analysis supported a causal role [114]. In AMI, ischemia–hypoxia and inflammatory responses activate cardiomyocytes to release EVs with significantly elevated miR-22-3p contents [115]. In patients with AMI, serum levels of EV-associated miR-22-3p were 3.2-fold higher than in healthy controls [26].

Sympathetic-neuroendocrine signaling bidirectionally modulates EV-miRNA cargo and biological function under CVD conditions. In chronic heart failure, cardiomyocyte-derived EVs carrying Nrf2-targeting miRNAs traffic to the rostral ventrolateral medulla, suppress central Nrf2 signaling, and induce sympathetic hyperactivity via disrupting redox homeostasis, thereby establishing a pivotal heart–brain EV-miRNA regulatory axis [116]. On the other hand, leptin-sympathetic axis stimulation remodels BAT-EV miR-29c repertoire to confer cardioprotection against ischemic injury [117]. In vitro, endothelial cells exposed to excess catecholamines release EV-miR-126-3p, providing further evidence that neuroendocrine-to-EV-miRNA signaling operates across multiple cell types [118].

Cardiomyocytes are not, however, the sole source of pro-tumorigenic EV-miRNAs in the injured heart. Small EVs released from cardiac mesenchymal stromal cells of the infarcted and failing heart are enriched in miR-21 and directly accelerate the growth of lung and colon cancers in vivo [44]. During atherosclerosis progression, macrophages within the vascular wall can release EVs containing high levels of miR-21, which directly target PTEN in smooth muscle cells to accelerate plaque development [119]. Beyond miR-21, a broader panel of EV-encapsulated miRNAs (including miR-146a, miR-155-5p, miR-221/222, and miR-494) has established roles in cardiovascular disease and is linked to tumor progression, underscoring that miR-21 is unlikely to be the sole functional miRNA driving cardiac-tumor crosstalk [120–123].

EV-mediated TME remodeling—miRnas as an example

CVD-derived EV-miRNAs remodel the TME through several functionally distinct mechanisms. miR-21 functions as a central hub linking multiple pro-tumorigenic pathways. In vitro, miR-21-containing EVs inhibit the tumor suppressor PTEN, thereby releasing the inhibition of the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway and promoting tumor cell proliferation and survival. Concurrently, miR-21 upregulates the matrix metalloproteinases MMP-2 and MMP-9, which degrade extracellular matrix components and enhance tumor cell migration and invasion [124–127]. Crucially, Caller et al. (2024) demonstrated that small EVs enriched in miR-21, isolated directly from the infarcted and failing mouse heart, are taken up by tumor cells and directly accelerate the growth of lung and colon cancers in vivo, establishing a complete functional axis from cardiac injury to EV-mediated tumor promotion [44]. miR-22-3p, once taken up by tumor cells, directly targets ACSL4, a key regulator of lipid peroxidation. This targeted suppression reduces intracellular lipid peroxide accumulation, thereby decreasing cancer cell sensitivity to ferroptosis inducers and promoting tumor cell survival [26]. miR-126-3p remodels the TME primarily through promoting angiogenesis, increasing endothelial cell proliferation and tube formation by approximately 40% via targeted inhibition of EGFR and PIK3R2 in vitro [118].

Evs as carriers of cfDNA and cf-mtDNA

Beyond miRNAs, EVs also serve as vital carriers for circulating cell-free DNA (cfDNA), which is released into the circulation upon cardiovascular injury and reflects the extent of cell death through apoptosis, necrosis, and NETosis. cfDNA levels are significantly elevated across the CVD spectrum and correlate with disease severity, offering prognostic value [128, 129]. These circulating nucleic acids function as damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) capable of acting distantly on the TME [130].

Within the cfDNA pool, cf-mtDNA is particularly relevant to the CVD-cancer axis. Cardiomyocytes possess an exceptionally high mitochondrial density, with mitochondria accounting for approximately 30% of cellular volume [131], making them the primary organelles for DAMP release during myocardial injury and ensuring that mtDNA is preferentially released in quantities far exceeding nuclear DNA [132, 133]. Moreover, owing to its bacterial evolutionary origin and unmethylated CpG motifs, cf-mtDNA acts as a potent innate immune signal, engaging the cGAS-STING and TLR9 pathways to trigger a systemic inflammatory cascade that elevates IL-6 and TNF-α [134–136]. Upon reaching the TME, cf-mtDNA is specifically internalized by polymorphonuclear myeloid-derived suppressor cells (PMN-MDSCs), enhancing their immunosuppressive capacity and shielding tumors from cytotoxic immune attack [137].

Factors associated with oxidative stress: exemplified by ROS

ROS comprise chemically active oxygen-containing compounds that include hydroxyl radicals, superoxide anions, and hydrogen peroxide derivatives [138, 139]. Cardiovascular disease induces ROS production and establishes an oxidative stress milieu, which exerts a complex dual role in tumorigenesis and tumor progression: it promotes tumor development at moderate levels, while high ROS concentrations trigger tumor cell death [140, 141].

Hierarchical regulation of ROS production and antioxidant failure in CVD

At the molecular level, ROS are generated through four core enzymatic systems that are pathologically upregulated across the CVD spectrum. The mitochondrial electron transport chain, specifically Complexes I and III, produces superoxide through respiratory chain uncoupling during ischemia-reperfusion and heart failure [142, 143]. NADPH oxidases, particularly NOX2 and NOX4, function as professional ROS generators: NOX2 is highly expressed in cardiomyocytes, endothelial cells, and inflammatory cells, while NOX4 is constitutively active in cardiac fibroblasts and drives fibrosis. The NOX family is recognized as a major source of ROS in CVD initiation and progression [144]. Xanthine oxidase generates both superoxide and hydrogen peroxide, with its expression markedly increased in ischemia-reperfusion injury and dysfunctional endothelium [145]. Uncoupled endothelial nitric oxide synthase, occurring when its essential cofactor tetrahydrobiopterin is depleted by oxidative stress, shifts from nitric oxide to superoxide production and thereby contributes to vascular oxidative stress [146].

These enzymatic sources are governed by a hierarchical regulatory network. The neuroendocrine axis serves as a primary systemic amplifier of ROS production. Within the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system, aldosterone activates NOX2 in cardiomyocytes via a mineralocorticoid receptor-dependent mechanism [147], while excessive activation of the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis promotes NOX2 activation in the hypothalamus, elevating systemic oxidative stress levels [148]. Importantly, these same enzymatic systems can also be driven independently of neuroendocrine input by direct cardiovascular stress, including ischemia-reperfusion, mechanical stretch, and endothelial dysfunction [142, 146, 149].

This overproduction is compounded by a parallel decline in endogenous antioxidant defenses. Glutathione, the most abundant intracellular antioxidant, is reduced by an average of 27.8% in heart failure patients compared to controls [150], and a meta-analysis of 45 studies confirmed that the activities of superoxide dismutase, glutathione peroxidase, and catalase are inversely correlated with coronary heart disease risk [151]. The resulting collapse of redox buffering capacity is reflected in a significantly decreased ratio of reduced to oxidized glutathione in the failing human myocardium [152]. Together, these data indicate that the endogenous antioxidant barrier in CVD is measurably compromised, amplifying the systemic pro-oxidative milieu established by excessive ROS generation.

ROS as drivers of tumor progression

Cardiovascular disease drives the overproduction of reactive oxygen species, which in turn promotes tumor progression through three primary pathways.

First, ROS activate the NLRP3 inflammasome, triggering vascular inflammation and remodeling that contribute to a tumor-permissive microenvironment [153–155]. Second, ROS directly inflict DNA damage through base oxidation and single- and double-strand breaks, increasing the mutational burden and genomic instability that drive tumor initiation. Iron-driven oxidative stress, in particular, generates hydroxyl radicals, the most destructive ROS species, which directly damage DNA and promote epithelial-mesenchymal transition [156, 157]. Third, ROS remodel the tumor immune microenvironment by promoting the differentiation and function of myeloid-derived suppressor cells, suppressing T cell activity, and enhancing cancer-associated fibroblast differentiation that facilitates extracellular matrix remodeling [158–161]. Together, these three pathways converge to produce a net pro-tumorigenic effect, positioning ROS as a central mediator of the CVD-cancer axis.

Anti-tumor effects of ROS and the antioxidant paradox

Although moderate ROS levels promote tumor development, excessive ROS accumulation exerts potent anti-tumor effects by inducing multiple forms of cell death, including apoptosis, pyroptosis, necroptosis, and ferroptosis, once cellular antioxidant capacity is exceeded [159, 162, 163]. In vitro, exogenous H₂O₂ disrupts tumor vascular endothelial function and causes tumor necrosis [164], while in glioblastoma, artificially elevating ROS to higher levels significantly inhibits tumor growth [165]. This dual role, in which the same molecule promotes or suppresses cancer depending on its concentration and cellular context, creates an inherent therapeutic dilemma.

Intuitively, neutralizing systemic oxidative stress through antioxidant supplementation should confer therapeutic benefits. However, clinical evidence reveals a profound antioxidant paradox. The SU.VI.MAX study demonstrated that non-selective exogenous antioxidants may inadvertently scavenge the pro-apoptotic ROS signals required for cancer cell elimination, thereby potentially protecting malignant cells and accelerating tumor progression [166]. Consequently, managing CVD-associated oxidative stress requires precision targeting of specific ROS sources, such as NOX inhibitors, rather than broad-spectrum antioxidant administration [167, 168].

Inflammatory factors

Chronic inflammation is a common pathological characteristic of multiple CVD. In disease states including myocardial infarction, heart failure, and atherosclerosis, damaged cardiovascular tissues continuously release large quantities of inflammatory factors. As well as participating in pathological remodeling of local tissues, these inflammatory mediators produce systemic inflammatory responses through circulatory systems, thereby profoundly influencing the immune microenvironments of distant organs [169, 170].

Hierarchical regulation of inflammatory factor production

The secretion of inflammatory factors in CVD is governed by a hierarchical regulatory network, with the neuroendocrine axis serving as the primary systemic amplifier. Chronic β-adrenergic stimulation directly induces the expression of TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6 in the myocardium [171], and in patients with coronary artery disease, HPA axis dysfunction is associated with an impaired ability to suppress inflammatory activity [172]. Within the RAAS, angiotensin II activates the NF-κB pathway, a central hub for inflammatory gene expression that upregulates multiple pro-inflammatory cytokines and chemokines, as demonstrated in a mouse model of angiotensin II-induced atherosclerosis and arterial inflammation [173]. Through these converging signals, neuroendocrine overdrive establishes a systemic inflammatory milieu that primes both the cardiovascular system and distant tissues for inflammatory injury.

Subordinate to this neuroendocrine drive, different CVD subtypes exhibit distinct profiles of locally released inflammatory mediators. In atherosclerosis, lipid accumulation and immune cell infiltration in the arterial wall induce macrophages to form foam cells and secrete TNF-α, IFN-γ, and IL-1β, driving plaque instability and thrombosis [174, 175]. After myocardial infarction, cardiomyocytes and cardiac fibroblasts upregulate IL-6, which recruits and polarizes macrophages [176, 177]. Elevated IL-8 in the platelet secretome of AMI patients further correlates with myocardial injury markers [178]. During AMI, IL-1α induces NLRP3 inflammasome activation, exacerbating myocardial damage [179, 180].

Inflammatory factor-mediated remodeling of the tumor immune microenvironment

Accumulating evidence indicates that CVD-associated inflammatory factors create favorable conditions for tumorigenesis and progression through reprogramming of tumor-associated immune cells, suppression of anti-tumor immune surveillance, and promotion of immune escape mechanisms [181, 182]. However, much of the mechanistic evidence linking CVD-derived inflammatory mediators to specific TME remodeling events derives from preclinical models, and dedicated studies involving human clinical samples are warranted.

IL-1 family members have multifaceted key roles in tumor development, mainly participating in tumor initiation, progression, and metastasis via regulation of the TME and inflammatory responses. On the one hand, IL-1 family members induce production of pro-inflammatory mediators and chemokines to form a tumor-promoting inflammatory microenvironment, thereby affecting tumor initiation and progression [183]. On the other hand, they promote tumor growth and metastasis by recruiting and activating immune cells such as neutrophils and myeloid-derived suppressor cells [184]. In pancreatic cancer, the IL-1/IL-1 receptor axis contributes to the formation of an immunosuppressive microenvironment by regulating Th2- and Th17-type inflammatory responses [185].

IL-6 also has a central role in the tumor immune microenvironment, with its expression level directly affecting the differentiation and activation of T cells [186]. IL-6 promotes tumor progression mainly through the JAK-STAT and MAPK signaling pathways [187]. Upon activation, STAT3 upregulates expression of IL-6 receptors on the cell surface, allowing more IL-6 to bind and further activate STAT3, resulting in the formation of a positive feedback loop [188]. MCP-1 secreted by cardiomyocytes post-infarction collaborates with IL-6 through the STAT3 pathway to modulate macrophage polarization [189].

Interconnected amplifiers in CVD-driven TME remodeling

These secondary amplifiers do not operate in isolation but function as an interconnected network that collectively drives TME remodeling. Inflammatory cytokines, including TNF-α and IL-1β, upregulate angiogenic factor expression through NF-κB pathway activation [190], while IL-6 further amplifies this effect by inducing transcription via STAT3 signaling [191]. Reactive oxygen species drive angiogenic factor expression through mitochondrial electron transport and NADPH oxidase [192]. Extracellular vesicles contribute to this network by delivering miRNAs, such as miR-29a, that target VEGFA in recipient endothelial cells [193].

Together, these convergent pathways transform the TME into a pro-angiogenic, immunosuppressive, and growth-permissive environment, underscoring that individual CVD-derived factors function as components of an integrated network rather than as parallel, independent mediators (Fig. 3).

Potential therapeutic implications and future directions

Modulating neuroendocrine signaling

The bidirectional effects of neuroendocrine axis interventions (e.g., β-blockers, RAAS inhibitors, and mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists) on cancer have been comprehensively reviewed recently [194]. Therefore, rather than exhaustively reiterating these established pathways, this section highlights specific emerging mechanistic paradigms. RAAS inhibitors, routinely used for the treatment of CVD, may modulate Ang II/AT1R signaling pathways involved in tumor-associated inflammation, cell proliferation, and angiogenesis. Retrospective clinical analyses have reported associations between RAAS inhibitor use and improved overall survival in selected cancer populations [195, 196].

A recent study provided new mechanistic insight into neuroendocrine axis-directed intervention. Caller et al. (2024) demonstrated in Circulation that in mice with post-myocardial infarction left ventricular dysfunction, spironolactone treatment significantly reduced the number of cardiac mesenchymal stromal cell-derived small extracellular vesicles (cMSC-sEVs) by 28% and suppressed the accelerated tumor growth associated with post-MI cardiac dysfunction. Notably, spironolactone showed no anti-tumor effect in sham-operated mice, indicating that its action is specific to the pathological secretory state induced by myocardial infarction rather than a direct cytotoxic effect on tumor cells [44]. This finding provides direct preclinical evidence that mineralocorticoid receptor antagonism may modulate post-MI cardiac secretory programs relevant to tumor growth, highlighting a candidate pathway for further validation in reverse cardio-oncology.

However, it must be emphasized that these findings remain preliminary. No randomized controlled trial has been specifically designed to determine whether these agents can reduce cancer incidence in CVD patients, and the optimal intervention timing, dosage, and duration remain undefined. Consequently, while the neuroendocrine axis represents a biologically plausible target in reverse cardio-oncology, current clinical evidence does not yet support its routine use for cancer prevention in CVD patients. Dedicated prospective RCTs with cancer incidence as a pre-specified endpoint are urgently needed.

Modulating inflammatory pathway crosstalk

The CANTOS trial provided important proof-of-concept evidence linking inflammatory pathway inhibition with both cardiovascular and cancer-related outcomes. In this randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial enrolling 10,061 patients with prior myocardial infarction and elevated high-sensitivity C-reactive protein, the IL-1β inhibitor canakinumab reduced major adverse cardiovascular events by 15% and was associated with a 67% reduction in lung cancer incidence (HR 0.33) and a 77% reduction in lung cancer mortality (HR 0.23) [27, 197]. However, cancer incidence and mortality were not the primary endpoints of CANTOS; therefore, these findings should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating rather than definitive evidence for cancer prevention in CVD patients.

These findings have motivated exploration of additional inflammatory targets. Given the dual role of IL-6 in both cardiovascular inflammation and tumor immune evasion, IL-6 pathway-directed trials are currently underway. The HERMES trial (NCT05636176) is evaluating the efficacy and safety of ziltivekimab, a humanized monoclonal antibody that neutralizes the IL-6 ligand, in approximately 5,600 patients with HFmrEF/HFpEF and systemic inflammation [198]. The ARTEMIS trial (NCT06118281) is assessing the effects of ziltivekimab on cardiovascular outcomes in patients with acute myocardial infarction. These ongoing studies may provide important insights into the cancer-modulating effects of IL-6 blockade in CVD populations [199].

The double-edged sword of CVD therapeutics: potential cancer risks

While interventions targeting the neuroendocrine axis and inflammatory pathways exhibit promising anti-tumor potential, certain cardiovascular therapeutics have been controversially associated with a modified risk of specific malignancies. For instance, prolonged use of hydrochlorothiazide (HCTZ) has been linked to an increased risk of non-melanoma skin cancer due to its photosensitizing effects [200], whereas historical concerns regarding a putative association between angiotensin II receptor blockers (ARBs) and elevated cancer risk have been largely refuted by subsequent large-scale trials and meta-analyses [201, 202]. Modeling CVD-cancer interactions using heart-cancer-on-a-chip platforms

A critical bottleneck in translating reverse cardio-oncology findings is the lack of human-based models that recapitulate the dynamic interactions between a diseased heart and a developing tumor. Organ-on-a-chip technology holds great promise for bridging this gap. Recent multi-organ platforms have demonstrated the feasibility of co-culturing human cardiac tissues with tumor models within shared microfluidic circuits, as shown by a 2024 cardiovascular organ-chip study in which human iPSC-derived cardiomyocytes and endothelial cells achieved functional and genetic maturation under active fluid flow and rhythmic biomechanical stretch [211]. These platforms can be extended to reverse cardio-oncology, where they may serve as screening tools for pharmacological modulators: by introducing agents such as EV release inhibitors, NOX inhibitors, RAAS blockers, or cytokine-neutralizing antibodies into the microfluidic circulation, researchers could evaluate whether blocking specific heart-derived signals attenuates pro-tumorigenic phenotypes without compromising cardiac function.

Finally, we emphasize that this technology directly addresses a recognized limitation in the field: the over-reliance on murine models for mechanistic studies in reverse cardio-oncology. The transition toward human-based Heart-Cancer-on-a-Chip(HCoC) platforms represents a vital path to generating human-specific mechanistic evidence, thereby supporting the identification of candidate therapeutic targets for further validation. More broadly, an interdisciplinary reverse cardio-oncology approach that breaks down disciplinary barriers could accelerate clinical translation of research findings, ultimately supporting more integrated management of CVD and cancer risks [212].

As a final conceptual consideration, it is crucial to delineate the boundaries of causality within the reverse cardio-oncology axis. Although epidemiological data robustly link cardiovascular disease to increased cancer incidence, the relationship between CVD and de novo human tumor initiation should be considered primarily associative rather than definitively causal. Current evidence indicates that CVD-derived systemic factors, including neuroendocrine hyperactivation, inflammatory cytokines, and extracellular vesicles, are highly unlikely to act as classical mutagens that directly initiate carcinogenesis. Instead, these factors function as potent tumor promoters. By establishing a permissive, immunosuppressive, and pro-angiogenic systemic “soil,” CVD accelerates the survival, proliferation, and clinical manifestation of pre-existing, subclinical malignant cells. Furthermore, in human observational cohorts, distinguishing true de novo tumor initiation from the rapid acceleration of latent micro-tumors following a cardiovascular event remains a profound methodological challenge. Therefore, while preclinical models firmly establish a causal role for CVD in driving tumor progression and TME remodeling, its connection to human tumor initiation remains fundamentally associative and warrants cautious interpretation in future cardio-oncology research.
