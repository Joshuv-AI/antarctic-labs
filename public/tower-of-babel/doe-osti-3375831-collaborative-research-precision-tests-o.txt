 Abstract
 This award supported Berkeley contributions to the ALPHA antihydrogen program from
 the years 2018–2025, emphasizing nonneutral-plasma control, plasma-based magnetic-field di-
 agnostics, patch-field measurements, antihydrogen trap dynamics, and experimental protocols
 and statistical data analysis methods for the ALPHA-g antimatter-gravity measurement.

1 Introduction
The principles of charge-parity-time (CPT) invariance and the weak equivalence principle (WEP)
are central assumptions of modern physics. CPT symmetry is expected to hold in any local, Lorentz-
invariant, unitary relativistic quantum field theory with the usual spin-statistics structure, while the
universality of free fall is central to the geometric interpretation of gravity. Antihydrogen provides
an unusually clean system for testing both principles, because its spectrum can be compared directly
with hydrogen and its gravitational motion can be measured using neutral antimatter. These
tests are experimentally demanding, but they probe foundational assumptions in a way that is
complementary to searches for new particles or interactions.
 The broad objective of the grant was to advance precision tests of CPT symmetry and the
WEP with antihydrogen. The Berkeley effort did this by developing and applying plasma-physics
methods for antihydrogen production and confinement, developing the gravity trap, experimental
protocols, and data analysis, and participating in the ALPHA antihydrogen physics program at
CERN.
 The project made three closely connected contributions to the ALPHA antihydrogen program.
First, it contributed to the design, installation, operation, and data analysis of the ALPHA-g mea-
surements that led to the first direct observation of the effect of gravity on antimatter [1–4]. Second,
it advanced the understanding and control of the charged-particle plasmas used both to synthe-
size antihydrogen and to measure magnetic fields, including the development and application of
plasma-based diagnostics for electron-cyclotron-resonance magnetometry [5], patch-potential mea-
surements [6], rotating-wall theory [7], improved plasma-temperature diagnostics [8, 9], and the
theory of resonant cavity cooling [10]. Third, it investigated antihydrogen motion in magnetic
traps, including the roles of adiabatic invariants, adiabatic cooling, energy mixing, and field asym-
metries [11]. Ongoing analysis suggests that optimized release protocols may provide larger cooling
factors than those reported in Ref. [11].
 Slow mixing between transverse and axial degrees of freedom limits the precision with which
slow-release measurements can infer the gravitational acceleration of antihydrogen. New protocols
are being developed to use controlled release as a phase-space diagnostic, with the goal of separately
measuring T⊥ , T∥ , and the rate of energy exchange between them. These recent results, derived
from work supported by the grant, will be reported separately.
 Finally, a major review paper on plasma traps and antimatter science was co-authored by
Fajans [12].
 The DOE funding supported collaboration on groundbreaking ALPHA studies of antihydro-
gen, including fine-structure [13] and 1S–2S [14] spectroscopy, laser cooling [15] and sympathetic
cooling [16], Be+ assisted confinement of more than 15,000 antihydrogen [17] atoms, 4-ppm accu-
racy ground-state hyperfine spectroscopy [18] and a comprehensive description of the ALPHA-2
apparatus [19] and multi-species beamline [20], and a discussion of plasma physics considerations
in antihydrogen synthesis [21].
 The work also produced reusable scientific tools and methods. These include magnetic-field
characterization procedures, plasma-reservoir techniques for repeated electron-cloud measurements,

patch-field diagnostics, improved temperature-fitting algorithms for low-temperature nonneutral
plasmas, orbit-following methods for antihydrogen trajectories, and Bayesian inference tools for the
ALPHA-g gravity analysis. These products are directly relevant to future antihydrogen experiments
and more broadly to precision experiments using Penning–Malmberg traps and cryogenic nonneutral
plasmas.

Impact and OFES Relevance
The project demonstrates how plasma physics enables precision tests with antimatter. The pro-
duction of antihydrogen is fundamentally a nonneutral-plasma problem: positrons and antiprotons
must be accumulated, cooled, compressed, transported, merged, and diagnosed. The subsequent
neutral-atom experiment remains connected to plasma physics through the same trap infrastruc-
ture, through charged-particle field diagnostics, and through Hamiltonian dynamics in magnetic
minimum traps.
 The work also contributed to the broader physics of phase-space transport. Antihydrogen
trajectories in magnetic-minimum traps exhibit approximate invariants, adiabatic regimes, energy
exchange, and transitions between regular and mixed behavior. These are familiar themes in
plasma confinement and nonlinear dynamics. The antihydrogen system provides a clean, low-
density, collisionless setting in which these effects directly influence measurable quantities such as
trap survival, annihilation position, spectroscopy linewidth, and gravitational escape asymmetry.
Understanding the details of the complex nonlinear dynamics in traps is essential for error analysis,
and a lack of such understanding imposes undesirable systematic uncertainties on the precision of
future experiments. On the other hand, exploiting nonlinear effects may prove a fruitful ground
for developing future phase-space diagnostics.
 The project helped enable several landmark antihydrogen measurements while also producing
plasma-physics and nonlinear-dynamics tools useful for future experiments. The following sec-
tions summarize selected accomplishments, emphasizing both the physics results and the Berkeley
contributions to diagnostics, modeling, and experimental interpretation.

2 Selected Scientific Accomplishments
Berkeley made major contributions to many aspects of the ALPHA antimatter gravity experiment,
including magnet design, ECR magnetometry, co-leading data and error analysis, orbit simulations,
and field-bias experimental protocols.

2.1 The Force of Gravity on Antihydrogen
Observation of the effect of gravity on the motion of antimatter [1]:
The ALPHA-g gravity measurement reported the first direct observation that neutral antimatter,
in the form of antihydrogen, is attracted toward the Earth. Research supported by this award
contributed to the magnet system design, experimental protocols, simulations and data analysis.
 The experiment used the vertically oriented ALPHA-g apparatus, in which antihydrogen atoms
were produced, trapped, accumulated over many mixing cycles, and then slowly released by ramping
down the magnetic mirror fields. Because the trap axis is vertical, gravity changes the relative
probabilities for escape through the upper and lower ends of the magnetic trap. The experiment
therefore converted the sign and magnitude of the gravitational force into a measurable up–down
asymmetry in annihilation positions.

 A crucial feature of the experiment was the use of a controlled magnetic bias between the upper
and lower mirror coils. A small difference in magnetic field between the two ends of the trap
produces a force on the antihydrogen magnetic moment that can either oppose or reinforce gravity.
Bias settings were expressed in units of g, so that a bias near −1g would compensate ordinary
downward gravity in a simplified one-dimensional model. The experiment interleaved multiple bias
settings, measured the magnetic fields in situ using ECR techniques, and compared the observed
annihilation distributions with detailed trajectory simulations and detector-response models.
 The result showed that antihydrogen is gravitationally attracted toward the Earth and ruled
out simple repulsive “antigravity” behavior. The inferred acceleration was

                      aH̄ = (0.75 ± 0.13 (stat. + syst.) ± 0.16 (simulation)) g,

consistent with conventional gravity, aH̄ = g, at the precision of this first-generation measurement.
The result is important as both a fundamental test of antimatter gravity and a demonstration that
antihydrogen can be accumulated, magnetically manipulated, released, and analyzed quantitatively.
This provides the basis for future higher-precision gravitational studies using colder antiatoms,
improved field characterization, and better control of the initial trapped energy distribution.

A Bayesian data analysis method for measuring the gravitational acceleration of anti-
hydrogen [2]:
This paper developed the Bayesian statistical framework used to interpret up–down escape mea-
surements in ALPHA-g. In the experiment, antihydrogen is released from a vertical magnetic
trap while a controlled magnetic bias can compensate or overcompensate gravity. The analysis
is nontrivial because the total number of trapped antiatoms is not known precisely, the detector
efficiencies for upward and downward annihilations may differ, some events can be reconstructed
in the wrong region, and cosmic-ray backgrounds contribute to both channels. The paper models
the upward and downward counts as independent Poisson processes and uses Markov-chain Monte
Carlo methods to infer either the downward escape probability or, when combined with simulations,
the gravitational acceleration parameter (ag ). This work provided the statistical bridge between
raw annihilation counts and the physical inference reported in the ALPHA-g gravity measurement.

2.2 Spectroscopy and Laser Cooling
Berkeley participated in ALPHA’s experimental campaigns on the fundamental spectral properties
of antihydrogen.

Investigation of the fine structure of antihydrogen [13]:
This paper used optical excitation of the 1S − 2P transitions to resolve features of the n = 2 fine
structure of antihydrogen in a 1 T-scale magnetic field. Measurements of the relevant 2P sublevels
allowed the collaboration to infer the zero-field fine-structure splitting and the classic Lamb shift
in antihydrogen. The inferred fine-structure splitting was consistent with QED at the few-percent
level, and this Lamb-shift comparison is an antimatter analogue of one of the historically central
tests of QED. The result was an important step toward using antihydrogen as a precision system
for simultaneous tests of QED and CPT symmetry.

 4

Laser cooling of antihydrogen atoms [15]:
This paper demonstrated laser cooling of antihydrogen for the first time. Pulsed Lyman-α radiation
was used to Doppler cool the trapped antiatoms along one dimension; coupling of the atomic degrees
of freedom in the magnetic trap then redistributed the cooling effect into the full three-dimensional
motion. The experiment reduced the median transverse energy by more than an order of magnitude
and produced substantially narrower 1S −2S spectra. This result changed the experimental outlook
for antihydrogen precision measurements, because colder antiatoms reduce Doppler and transit-
time broadening, improve localization, and directly benefit future spectroscopy and gravitational
measurements.

Four-ppm measurement of the antihydrogen ground-state hyperfine splitting [18]
This measurement improved the antihydrogen ground-state hyperfine splitting precision by about
two orders of magnitude, reaching the 4-ppm level. It demonstrates the ability of the ALPHA
apparatus to perform precision microwave spectroscopy on large samples of trapped antihydrogen.
Using microwave spectroscopy of roughly 24,000 trapped antiatoms in a 1 T-scale magnetic trap,
the experiment determined

                        a1S /h = 1,420,404.8 ± 1.1 (stat.) ± 5.6 (sys.) kHz,

consistent with expectations from hydrogen and thus with CPT. The measurement reaches the
few-ppm scale, where sensitivity to antiproton internal structure becomes relevant and future ex-
periments may address the antiproton radius. The result relied on improved antihydrogen ac-
cumulation, better magnetic-field control and characterization, and refined microwave spin-state
manipulation. It complements the (1S)–(2S) spectroscopy program and greatly strengthens the
broader antihydrogen tests of CPT symmetry.

2.3 Plasma Magnetometry and Nonlinear Dynamics
Berkeley had lead roles in the plasma magnetometry development, modeling and exploiting the
nonlinear dynamics of antihydrogen orbits, and the discovery that patch potentials explain degra-
dation of trapping and antiproton losses after ionization. These results were enabling of ALPHA’s
experimental campaigns.

Electron cyclotron resonance magnetometry [5]:
Studies of CPT and WEP with trapped antihydrogen require accurate in situ knowledge of the
magnetic field. This paper developed an electron-cyclotron-resonance magnetometry technique for
Penning–Malmberg traps using low-density electron plasmas as local magnetic-field probes. The
local field was inferred from microwave heating resonances: an electron plasma was exposed to
a microwave pulse, the microwave frequency was scanned, and the resulting plasma temperature
increase was measured. The cyclotron frequency was then identified from the heating spectrum
and converted to a magnetic field amplitude.
 A central technical advance was the use of a large electron plasma reservoir from which many
small target plasmas could be repeatedly extracted. This allowed destructive temperature measure-
ments on low-density, localized electron clouds while avoiding the time cost and thermal/vacuum
disturbance associated with repeatedly loading from an electron source. The method enabled rapid
automated frequency scans, making it practical to map magnetic fields or monitor drifts under
experimental conditions relevant to antihydrogen spectroscopy and gravity measurements.

 The paper also analyzed the sideband structure of the ECR spectra. Multiple heating peaks
arise from the axial bounce motion of the electrons, and additional substructure is associated with
plasma rotation. By varying the electrostatic confinement and therefore the bounce frequency, the
true cyclotron peak can be identified as the peak that does not shift with the bounce frequency.
The technique achieved sub-ppm absolute accuracy in favorable conditions and relative precision
at the tens-of-ppb level, making it a key enabling diagnostic for the later ALPHA-g gravity work

and for high-precision antihydrogen spectroscopy. A related concept explored in Ref. [10] has not
yet been implemented at CERN, but may be useful in future upgrades.

Measurements of Penning–Malmberg trap patch potentials and associated perfor-
mance degradation[6]:
This paper identified and measured patch-potential effects in the ALPHA Penning–Malmberg trap.
The motivation came from two observations: antiprotons produced by laser ionization of trapped
antihydrogen escaped shallow electrostatic wells much more rapidly than expected, and positron
plasmas heated more rapidly after extended exposure of the trap to ultraviolet laser light. Both
effects can be explained by small, uncontrolled electric fields arising from spatial variations of
the surface potential on cryogenic trap electrodes. Even fields of order 10 mV/cm can produce
significant (E × B) drift of antiprotons in shallow traps.
 The paper developed a simple but physically transparent model in which the leading asymmetric
patch field is represented by a transverse potential proportional to (r cos θ). In a harmonic electro-
static well, such a transverse field displaces the magnetron orbit center by an amount (δ ≃ ϵ/k2 ),
where (ϵ) is the transverse electric field and (k2 ) characterizes the well curvature. When the well is
shallow, the displacement can become comparable to the trap radius, causing antiprotons to drift to
the wall and annihilate. This mechanism explains why antiprotons left behind after laser ionization
of antihydrogen are rapidly lost under conditions where purely axisymmetric electrostatic modeling
would predict confinement.
 The experimental advance was an in situ diagnostic for patch fields using small electron clouds.
Electron clouds were extracted from a reservoir, moved to selected axial locations, placed in shallow
wells, and allowed to acquire magnetron orbits whose radii encode the transverse patch field. After
release to an MCP/phosphor detector, magnetic-field-line expansion amplified the cloud displace-
ment, allowing the patch field to be inferred. The technique was benchmarked using an artificial
patch potential generated with a sectored electrode, and then used to show that laser exposure
modifies patch fields while the trap remains cold. The work is directly relevant to antihydrogen
spectroscopy because laser-induced patch fields can change charged-particle confinement, positron
heating, and the interpretation of annihilation signals following photoionization.

Adiabatic expansion cooling of antihydrogen [11]:
This work demonstrated a proof-of-principle method for cooling antihydrogen by adiabatically
expanding the magnetic trapping volume. Since trapped antihydrogen is extremely dilute, collisions
cannot equilibrate the degrees of freedom; cooling therefore depends on single-particle dynamics
in the time-dependent magnetic trap. In the experiment, antihydrogen released from an expanded
trap escaped at lower average trap depth than antihydrogen released from static control traps.
Simulations indicated a roughly 38% decrease in mean energy after expansion, with partial exchange
between axial and transverse energy. The result is important for future gravitational measurements,
where colder atoms increase sensitivity, and it suggests a cooling path complementary to laser
cooling. Ongoing analysis suggests that substantially larger cooling effects may be obtained with
optimized release protocols; those results will be reported separately.

Shot-noise-induced lower temperature limit of the nonneutral plasma parallel temper-
ature diagnostic [9]:
Electron and positron temperatures at the few-kelvin level must be measured accurately in order
to tune antihydrogen formation in ALPHA. The diagnostic must also be minimally destructive, be-

 7

cause only a small number of particles can be sacrificed in a typical measurement. Existing methods
were too noisy in this regime. We addressed this limitation by developing a maximum-likelihood
estimator for the dynamic evaporative diagnostic, using single-particle escape data and accounting
for the onset of plasma self-potential evolution during the evaporation. Monte Carlo studies showed
that approximately 100 detected particles are sufficient for a ten-percent temperature estimate, and
that the method can extend the measurable lower-temperature bound to 3K for a 1 cm-long plasma
with parameters relevant to ALPHA. This paper extended the detailed results described in Ref. [8].

3 Conclusions
The ALPHA Collaboration has measured the spectral properties of antihydrogen with steadily
increasing precision and has reached the regime in which QED corrections and antiproton structure
become experimentally relevant. Future experiments may use antihydrogen spectroscopy to sharpen
tests of CPT symmetry through measurements sensitive to quantities such as the antiproton charge
radius and the Lamb shift in antihydrogen. Substantially improved tests of antihydrogen charge
neutrality may also become possible.
 Further optimization, together with a better understanding of positron–antiproton synthesis
into antihydrogen, may increase trapping rates and improve control of the initial antiatom dis-
tribution. New techniques are being explored for rapid off-axis magnetometry and for inferring
phase-space information from annihilation data during controlled magnetic-field turn-off protocols.
Systematic uncertainties may be reduced through direct comparison of hydrogen and antihydro-
gen under closely matched conditions. Finally, antihydrogen interferometry could provide a path
toward high-precision measurements of antimatter gravity.
 The research supported by this award strengthened the role of antihydrogen as a precision
platform for testing CPT symmetry and the weak equivalence principle, while also illustrating the
continuing importance of OFES-supported plasma science in experiments beyond traditional fusion
confinement.
