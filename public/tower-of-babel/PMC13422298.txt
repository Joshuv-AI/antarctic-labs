Developing and evaluating an in situ, simulated emergency training course for nurses with low seniority
Authors: Meiyu Shen, Xiaolin Tan, Yunyan Xianyu, Qiuxiang Chen, Yu Fang, Chao Zeng, Guirong Yang, Minghao Pan, Zhongxiang Cai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesThis study aimed to construct and implement an in situ simulated emergency training (SET) course for nurses with low seniority and evaluate its effectiveness.MethodsThis was an experimental study, and a purpose-sampling method was adopted. We selected 82 nurses with less than 5 years of experience working in a grade - III Class A hospital in Hubei province from August to December 2023 as the research objects, and randomly divided them into intervention group (n = 37) and control group (n = 45). The control group underwent routine stratified training, whereas the intervention group underwent in situ SET based on routine stratified training. Before and after training, we compared differences in the scores of the intervention and control groups in terms of critical thinking (CT), clinical communication (CC), clinical reasoning (CR), and first aid (FA).ResultsThe constructed in situ simulated emergency training course consisted of nine courses, each of which covered one theme. The nine themes were acute myocardial infarction, diabetic ketoacidosis, secondary shock due to acute transfusion anaphylaxis, post-operative respiratory depression combined with pulmonary edema, unplanned extubation, impulsive violence, chemotherapeutic drug extravasation, patient suicide, and acute pulmonary embolism. The results of the empirical study showed that before training, there were no differences between the intervention and control groups in the scores for the following abilities: CT, CC, CR, and FA (p > 0.05). After training, the scores for CR and FA in the intervention group were higher than those in the control group (p < 0.05), while the scores for CT and CC did not differ from those in the control group (p > 0.05).ConclusionsThe content of the in situ SET course is comprehensive, which is helpful in improving junior nurses’ CC and FA abilities and can serve as a reference for establishing a training course for them. However, the effects on the CT and CC were not significant. Further empirical studies are required to explore the targeted training courses.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-026-08961-x.Keywords: High fidelity simulation, Junior nurse, Emergencies, Training, Effect

Introduction

With the ongoing growth of the medical industry, the requirements for clinical nurses are increasing and continuous training is needed to enhance their professional skills. Among them, the ability to respond to a crisis is paramount; this encompasses not only first aid (FA) but also emergency disposal, which enable nurses to identify the different conditions and changes in patients in a sensitive and timely manner, exercise reasonable analysis and judgment, and use specific techniques to provide calm and decisive treatment [1, 2]. Nurses’ capacity to provide emergency care is a key factor in patient safety [2]. According to Faraz’s research [3], nurses with fewer working years or less experience are referred to as “novice nurse practitioners” and they have fewer qualifications. According to one survey [2], owing to a lack of clinical experience, newly recruited nurses’ abilities to make judgments and handle emergencies are inadequate and require improvement. In addition to a lack of emergency response skills, novice nurses are deficient in advanced technical abilities such as self-assessment, critical thinking (CT), clinical communication (CC), teamwork, helping roles, and professionalism [4].

To improve new nurses’ emergency response skills and enable them to exercise CT, CC, and a sense of teamwork, hospitals vigorously conduct various kinds of training, including theoretical knowledge, operational skills, and health lectures [5]. However, nurses often lack learning initiatives and have low participation and enthusiasm, and the effects of training are usually poor. To address these issues, hospitals have conducted emergency drills [6] that have, to a certain extent, improved nurses’ emergency response skills within a short period.

To boost the efficiency and quality of training, promote nurses’ active learning, and increase their enthusiasm to participate, increasingly more hospitals have begun to apply the training approach of scenario simulation, which has proven effective [7, 8]. Scenario simulations are evidence-based and have been frequently used in nursing education in recent years. By replicating authentic clinical scenarios, this method provides nurses with opportunities to practice and develop their clinical skills in a safe environment while exercising and improving clinical reasoning (CR), CT, and clinical decision-making [9]. For new nurses with less than 1 year of work experience, regular simulation training and deliberate practice can enhance their knowledge, sense of teamwork, and confidence in handling various situations. In particular, incidents with a low frequency but high severity are more suitable for strengthening the capacity to deal with actual clinical events [10].

In situ simulation (ISS) brings a simulated teaching kit to a real medical unit and uses actual resources (e.g., instruments, equipment, goods, and drugs) from the unit. Participants are also required to engage in simulation training in accordance with their real job roles, skill levels, and responsibilities [11]. As a result, ISS has been shown to play a significant role in advancing skills, procedures, and teamwork and has received positive feedback from participants [12, 13]. In their study of practical nurses, Wang et al. found that ISS can cultivate nurses’ clinical thinking abilities and sense of teamwork in common emergencies, thus improving their comprehensive quality and working capacity, which are of immeasurable value in responding to emergencies [14]. According to Monesi’s research, simulated multi-disciplinary teamwork in a real intensive care unit (ICU) can contribute to effective experiential learning, deepen nurses’ familiarity with equipment and the environment, and enable trainees to improve technical and non-technical skills [15, 16]. Similarly, Lien found that ISS facilitates changes in ICU nurses’ nursing skills and cognitive processes [16].

However, few studies have investigated the in situ simulated emergency training (SET) of nurses with low clinical experience, and the effect of training nurses with clinical experience of 5 years or less remains to be explored. Hence, we studied nurses with less than 5 years of clinical experience in an in situ SET course and analyzed its effect on clinical implementation by comparing the intervention and control groups.

Methods

Developing an in situ SET course for nurses with low seniority

Establishing an ISS training team for junior nurses

The ISS training team for junior nurses consisted of eight members: one associate chief physician, one associate chief nurse, and six supervising nurses. The team, which was engaged in educating nurses on various relevant topics, nursing management, and areas of expertise (e.g., psychiatry, obstetrics, and gynecology), included two doctoral students, one master’s student, and one bachelor’s student. The team members were responsible for consulting the literature; clarifying the investigative content of junior nurses’ training needs; determining the training theme(s), objectives, and evaluation items; constructing complete ISS training cases; acting as mentors to organize and implement the training; and assessing the effects of training. They were guided by two nursing managers who had participated in situational simulation instructions and obtained appropriate certifications from various institutions such as the International Nursing Association for Clinical Simulation and Learning (INACSL). Additionally, they were exposed to the Standards for Simulation developed by INACSL [17, 18], the theoretical framework of ISS training, the design and writing of cases, the organization and implementation process, and the methods and precautions for guided feedback. After the training, all team members passed the assessment, demonstrated proficiency in ISS, and could independently provide feedback on the training of ISS nurses with only a few years of experience.

Determining the training theme and developing the in situ SET course for junior nurses

By consulting the literature, we can understand the research progress of domestic and foreign medical institutions regarding the training of nurses with low seniority and comprehensively access the corresponding training courses and content.

Additionally, we administered a questionnaire to nurses with less than 5 years of experience in all departments of a grade - III Class A hospital in Hubei province to understand their demand for training. In this context, we retrospectively analyzed the adverse safety events reported by hospitals over the past 10 years and combined them with the questionnaire results. The general theme of the course was emergency training. Nine topics were identified: post-operative respiratory depression combined with pulmonary edema, acute myocardial infarction, diabetic ketoacidosis, secondary shock due to acute transfusion anaphylaxis, unplanned extubation, impulsive violence, chemotherapeutic drug extravasation, patient suicide, and acute pulmonary embolism.

The training topics were applicable to nurses in all fields. In the curriculum, we included the knowledge and skills that nurses in all fields need to master. Each topic includes specific training goals, forms, locations, roles, core nursing skills for operations, simulation scenarios, case settings, simulation processes, and precautions. Additionally, we focused on emergency training for nurses with low seniority and used the simulation standard developed by INACSL as a guiding framework [17, 18]. Taking the theme of emergency training on post-operative respiratory depression combined with pulmonary edema as an example, the objective of the scenario cases was to master the following: assessment of vital signs (e.g., consciousness, airway, respiration, and circulation of patients during anesthesia and recovery under emergency conditions); the emergency treatment process of post-operative respiratory depression; the symptoms and nursing measures of acute pulmonary edema; and the execution process of oral medical advice during emergency treatment. The psychomotor skills to be prepared before the scenario simulation included rapid assessment of consciousness; airway, respiration, and circulation; identification of respiratory depression; emergency pressure oxygen with a mandibular mask supported by both hands; assessment of symptoms of pulmonary edema and timely negative pressure suction; slow infusion; elevation of the head of the bed; oxygen inhalation; and accurate oral instructions during emergency treatment. According to the simulation cases of each course, the training team members organized several rehearsals and discussions to improve the scenario, fully consider the occurrence of simulated unexpected situations, and perform well in the setting of contingency scenarios. Evaluating the effectiveness of the in situ SET course for nurses with low seniority

Sample size estimation and selection of the training objects

We used G*Power 3.1 to calculate the required sample size, and independent samples t-test to estimate it [19]. After referring to relevant research data [20], according to our study’s purpose and design, the effect size was 0.75, the α was 0.05, the efficacy power was 0.90, the allocation ratio was 1, and the total sample size was 78.

In order to evaluate the implementation effect of in situ SET course in the group of nurses with low experience, we adopted the purpose sampling method to select nurse in the hospital where the researcher worked as the research objects, and issued a recruitment announcement in August 2023 in a grade - III Class A hospital in Hubei province to recruit clinical nurses of this hospital to participate in in situ SET course. A total of 135 people were recruited. A total of 86 participants were selected by inclusion exclusion criteria. They were randomly divided into a control group (n = 45) and an intervention group (n = 41). However, four participants in the intervention group were absent from the course twice; thus, we finally enrolled 45 and 37 participants in the control and intervention groups, respectively. All participants met the following inclusion criteria: ① had obtained the necessary qualifying certificate to practice as a nurse and be registered as an in-service nurse, ② had been working as a nurse for ≤ 5 years, and ③ had volunteered to participate in the study. We used the following exclusion criteria: ≥ 1 absence from the course due to sick or personal leave.

Scenario setup and preparation

Each training location was set in a real ward or outpatient clinic, and the participants used instruments, equipment, goods, drugs, and other resources for actual clinical work. The equipment in the ward included ventilators, ECG monitors, defibrillators, emergency treatment vehicles, central oxygen inhalation devices, and central negative pressure aspirators. A list of items was created in advance and a standardized patient simulation was applied after training. Items and medicines were prepared according to work routines before each training session, and special props were set up according to the needs of the course such as Bluetooth speakers, simulated blood bags, drainage and intravenous infusion, and prosthetic arms. In addition, projectors and video equipment were procured. The entire training process was recorded and implemented.

The intervention process: a randomized controlled experiment

The control group underwent routine stratified training once a month. The regular training content included specialty business learning (specialty new theory and new technology), case discussions, core systems, emergency plan training and exercises, demonstration of operational skills, and related complication prevention and management. The training method combines theoretical teaching, operational demonstrations, and planning exercises. For example, the training content of respiratory medicine included teaching ventilator use, discussion of a difficult case of a patient with bronchiectasis, an oral medical order system and implementation process in an emergency, a rescue and special incident report handling system, intravenous transfusion allergic reaction emergency plan training and drills, venous blood collection operations, and prevention and treatment of common complications. Based on the training provided to the control group, the intervention group received training in an in situ simulated emergency course. The training sessions were held 2–3 times a month and covered nine topics. All training sessions were completed within 4 months. Based on the actual needs of clinical work, we randomly divided the participants into groups of 4–5 people for a total of nine groups numbered 1–9. All nine groups participated in training sessions, and each session was simulated twice by two groups of nurses. One group of nurses performed the simulation, while the other group of nurses waited in the room without participating and studied in the clinical classroom through monitoring and projection. At the end of the two simulations, all nurses provided guided feedback. We adopted a single-blind design for an in situ SET course for junior nurses. All course content was known only to the team members before the training, including the training theme(s), goals, simulated patient’s upcoming situation, and the corresponding management process. The training team members and all participants signed a confidentiality agreement before the course to ensure that the content would not be disclosed to nurses who had not participated in the simulation before training. Simulation process

The implementation of the in situ SET course for junior nurses can be divided into three parts: pre-simulation introduction, case simulation, and guided feedback.

Briefing

All classes began with a pre-simulation introduction that lasted approximately 10 min. After each group of nurses participating in the simulation was ready, the instructor provided a structured introduction to the arrangement of the course, explained its application to the standardized patients, and informed them of the support that nurses could ask for and receive during the simulation. In order to create the psychological reality of an ISS, it was emphasized that standardized patients should be regarded as real patients in the simulation process, and that different nursing decisions would lead to different patient outcomes. The nurses were given sufficient time to familiarize themselves with the scenarios and training. They were also given the medical histories of the simulated patients, which allowed those participating in the simulation to have a brief discussion and divide their responsibilities. After confirming that the nurses participating in the simulation had no questions regarding the scenarios or cases(s), they participated in the exercises.

The simulation case

The duration of each group of simulated cases was maintained at approximately 20 min. To ensure the authenticity of cases, tutors could use standardized methods to convey information according to the decision-making behaviors of different nurses to help them understand actual situations and promote their operation [21]. During each operation, the two tutors carefully observed and recorded the nurses’ simulation performances. After the first group completed its tasks, the second group repeated the previous simulations. After the simulation in Group 2, all nurses participating in the training gathered in the clinical classroom outside the simulation scene and provided guided feedback.

Debriefing is one of the most important features of in situ SET for nurses with little experience and forms the core of training. Under the instructors’ guidance, nurses can reflect on the behaviors and results of the simulation, analyze the underlying reasons, and adjust their incorrect thinking and psychological frameworks [22, 23]. For each training session, the two tutors co-ordinated the guided feedback. The tutor could flexibly arrange the time according to onsite guidance for approximately 30 min each time. Based on the course goals and the practical performance of the nurses in the simulation, the tutor employed a structured 3-stage model [22] to provide guided feedback, which was divided into periods of response, understanding, and summary. During the response period, the instructor guided the nurses to take the initiative in expressing their feelings about participating in the training. During the understanding period, according to the observed simulation and the nurse’s description, the tutor discussed the simulation process, how the team had handled it, and whether communication and co-operation were reasonable. During the summary period, each nurse was encouraged to review and share the key points learned from the cases and apply them to their clinical work. In addition, the tutor encouraged observing peers to provide feedback on their learning and to actively participate in discussions and reflections.

Evaluation of the course’s effectiveness

Critical Thinking Disposition Inventory – Chinese Version (CT-DI–CV)

The Chinese version of the Critical Thinking Disposition Inventory (CT-DI–CV) was revised by Peng et al. according to the Chinese version developed by Facione et al. [24, 25]. This scale encompasses seven dimensions: seeking truth, having an open mind, analytical ability, systematic ability, confidence in judging one’s thinking, thirst for knowledge, and cognitive maturity. Each dimension contains ten items for a total of 70 items. Indicators at all levels were scored on a 6-point Likert scale, with answers ranging from strongly agree to strongly disagree, and negative items were assigned 1–6 points. We assigned reverse values to the positive items for a total score ranging from 70 to 420 points. The Cronbach’s α coefficient was 0.9 and the content validity (CV) was 0.89. A score > 40 in each dimension or a total score > 280 on the scale indicated positive CT, whereas a dimension score > 50 points or a total scale score > 350 points denoted strongly positive CT. A dimensional score of 30–40 or a total score of 210–280 indicated moderate CT. A dimensional score of < 30 points or a total score of < 210 points suggested a negative CT result.

The nurses’ clinic communication competency scale

We adopted the nurses’ communication competence scale compiled by Zeng Kai [26], which includes six dimensions: affective perception, emotional support, basic nurse–patient verbal communication, basic nurse–patient non-verbal communication, difficult communicative situations, and team communication for a total of 58 items. A 5-point Likert scale was used for scoring, with 1–5 points representing very poor, poor, average, good, and very good, respectively. Higher scores indicate better communication skills. The Cronbach’s α coefficient for the aggregate table was 0.978.

Nurses’ Clinical Reasoning Scale (NCRS)

The NCRS, developed by Liou et al. [27], emphasizes logical problem-solving processes, including nurses’ collection of data from patients, patients’ awareness of problems, and evaluation of nursing interventions. A 5-point Likert scale was used for scoring, with 15 items ranging from strongly disagree to strongly agree on a scale of 1–5, with total scores ranging from 15 to 75. The higher the score, the stronger the CR. The Cronbach’s α coefficient of the scale was 0.9.

Non-emergency department nurses and clinical first aid: the questionnaire

The questionnaire was compiled by Zhao Junzhu [1] with a total of 28 items. Items were scored on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from totally inconsistent to completely consistent, with total scores ranging from 28 to 140 points. Higher scores indicate stronger clinical FA. The Cronbach’s α coefficient of this questionnaire was 0.927.

All evaluation scales were distributed before and after the training sessions and the nurses completed them anonymously. A total of 82 valid questionnaires were returned at both time points for an effective recovery rate of 100%.

Statistical methods

IBM SPSS Statistics version 25 (IBM Inc., Armonk, NY, US) was used for the statistical analysis. We described the measurement data that satisfied normality and variance uniformity using the mean ± the standard deviation and performed inter-group comparisons using t-tests. Paired t-tests were used for intra-group comparisons. Data that did not meet the normality and variance homogeneity criteria were described as medians and quartiles. The Mann–Whitney U and the Wilcoxon signed-rank tests were used for the inter- and intra-group comparisons, respectively. Count data were presented as the number of people and percentages. We used the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test for inter-group comparisons, and the test level was α = 0.05.

Ethical considerations

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of X Hospital (WDRY2023-K126). Prior to participation, the purpose and significance of the study were explained to the participants and written informed consent was obtained. The participants were allowed to withdraw from the study at any time. Personal information was kept confidential, and query data were used for this study only and for no other purposes. In this study, nurses in the control group did not receive ISS emergency course training and instead received routine training organized by hospitals and departments. At the end of the study, we will conduct ISS emergency course training for the control group to achieve unified training.

Discussion

The effectiveness of the in situ SET course on the CT of junior nurses

CT is an essential quality of registered nurses. Nurses with good CT can effectively cope with the diversity and complexity of the clinical environment to ensure patient safety and improve patient prognosis [28]. Research on the simulation teaching of clinical nurses’ CT is still in its preliminary stages [29], and more studies are aimed at exploring the impact of simulation teaching on nursing students’ CT [28, 30]. However, to date, few studies have been conducted on in situ simulated instructions. In the present study, we found that the in situ SET course had no obvious advantage in promoting the development of junior nurses’ CT, which is similar to the results of other studies. For example, Shin et al. [31] provided instructions for a standardized patient-based scenario to undergraduate nursing students from three nursing schools, and the results showed no statistically significant differences in the average post-assessment CT scores of nursing students in the three groups. A systematic evaluation revealed that the existing evidence of the positive impact of SET on CT is insufficient, especially when comparing the results of the intervention groups with those of the control groups; the difference was not significant [32], which is consistent with our findings. The CT scores of the intervention groups differed significantly before and after training; however, after training, there was no significant difference in the CT scores between the intervention and control groups. On the one hand, this can be attributed to the SET curriculum designed by clinical nurses who have been working for less than or equal to 5 years and the main instructional goal of teaching about emergency treatment. Although the operational process of the simulation was set up to require nurses to use CT to solve pertinent problems (e.g., nurses’ need to judge and report a disease according to the patient’s age, illness, history, and symptoms), this study is the first to design an ISS. Given junior nurses’ lack of experience, the case development process may be fairly simple or lack relevance, which may affect the development of nurses’ CT to a certain extent. However, unlike the cultivation of operational skills, improvement in CT does not occur in a short period but rather requires extensive ongoing training and strengthening. Longer intervention durations and more frequent exposure to simulation-based teaching activities can strengthen the impact of simulation teaching on nurses CT [31]. However, owing to time and spatial constraints, each group of nurses participated in the simulation only twice, the other time they only participated as observers. Hence, the time spent practicing and training was far from sufficient, which could have affected CT formation.

The effectiveness of an in situ SET course on junior nurses’ CC

Communication in clinical work is multifaceted and includes nurse–patient communication and communication among nurses. Good nurse–patient communication is important for improving nursing quality and providing high-quality care. Effective communication between nurses and patients is a prerequisite for the orderly development of clinical work, and communication among nurses is related to team cohesion. Therefore, good communication abilities are essential for nurses. Cultivation of communication abilities requires conscious and repeated practice for gradual improvement. Research [33, 34] has shown that scenario simulation can effectively foster nurses’ CC by creating clinical situations and allowing trainees to integrate into them, intuitively feeling the clinical environment through role-play, strengthening the application of theoretical knowledge, exercising practical skills and having them make attempts to communicate in the situation [7, 35–37]. However, in the present study, the in situ SET course had no significant effect on the CC of junior nurses, and there was no difference in the CC scores between the intervention and control groups before and after training. This finding is inconsistent with other results [36]. There are several reasons for this observation. On the one hand, the average career length of the participants of the present study was approximately 2 years. The nurses had mastered certain CC skills and their CC scores were generally high before training. However, owing to the large number of participants and limited time and space, each group only participated in the simulation twice; otherwise, they participated only as observers, resulting in few opportunities for communication. The promotion effect on communication ability is not obvious. Therefore, a longer simulation training period is required to explore its impact on nurses’ CC.

The effects of the in situ SET course on junior nurses’ CR

CR is the “thinking and decision-making process” related to clinical practice and a core component of clinical competence [38]. Educational strategies for CR include exposure to a wide variety of clinical cases, activation of previous knowledge, development of disease scripts, and encouragement to reflect deliberately [39]. Simulations are one of the most commonly used strategies [40]. Relevant studies have shown that simulation instructions can effectively improve CR. The results of a previous randomized controlled trial showed that online patient simulation training is an effective method for teaching CR and significantly reduces diagnostic errors among medical students [41]. Similarly, a systematic review [42] demonstrated that virtual simulation training was effective in boosting nurses’ CR, which is consistent with our results. Compared to virtual simulation, ISS makes use of the real clinical environment to enable students to have a higher degree of psychological imitation and deeper experience so that they can better bring themselves into the role, which is more conducive to training in clinical thinking and promoting clinical reasoning [43]. The results of a study on doctors also showed that, compared with video simulations, doctors who participated in live scenario simulations performed better in clinical reasoning and showed unique characteristics in reflective judgment and cognitive processing [43]. This finding is consistent with the results of the present study. After the training in the ISS emergency course, the clinical reasoning ability score of the intervention group was significantly higher than that of the control group, indicating that the ISS training effectively improved the clinical reasoning ability of the intervention group.

The effects of the in situ SET course on junior nurses’ FA

In clinical practice, nurses are in contact with patients for a long time. In an emergency, nurses are usually the first to arrive, and taking the necessary FA measures in a critical situation is necessary to maintain patient safety [44]. Hospitals often require all medical staff to undergo FA training, including theoretical and operational skills [45]. However, according to relevant surveys [46, 47], the most demanding training for clinical nurses is related to FA and the capacity to handle crises. This finding suggests the need to adopt more effective training methods to improve FA and to manage emergencies. In this study, through nine training sessions in an in situ SET course, we found that the FA scores of clinical nurses with only a few years of experience effectively improved, demonstrating the positive effect of ISS teaching on enhancing the capacity to handle crises. During the training process, nurses felt empowered to make clinical decisions and had opportunities to practice nursing evaluations, interventions (treatments), disease reporting, education, and communication skills. Through real practical experiences combined with timely guided feedback after the simulation, nurses were able to master and apply rescue knowledge and skills to their clinical work. Furthermore, they gained confidence when dealing with emergencies at work. Compared to before the training, nurses’ FA ability significantly improved after all training courses. This is consistent with the findings of other studies [48] in which simulation-based training was found to be an effective strategy for teaching cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR) and teamwork [49, 50]. In one study, a hospital-wide ISS of a cardiac arrest program was implemented to provide effective methods for identifying and mitigating potential safety threats [51]. In another study, nurses trained anesthetized students in basic life support skills through situational and video training and the former was found to be more advantageous [52]. Currently, in situ SET is mostly applied to emergency training in obstetrics, emergency departments [53], neonatology [54], and other areas related to emergency training, as well as to multi-disciplinary and cross-professional team co-operation, and has good application effects. However, few ISS studies have been conducted on nurses’ training in handling emergencies [55, 56]. In this study, we developed and implemented an in situ SET course for nurses with low seniority that effectively improved their FA capacity and provided references for research on in situ SET courses for nurses.

Conclusions

The in situ SET course for junior nurses we provided was comprehensive, practical, and met the training needs of junior nurses. In addition, we verified the effectiveness of the in situ SET course through empirical research and discovered that the in situ SET course for nurses with low seniority can effectively improve their CR and FA abilities and can be further popularized and applied.

Limitations

In the implementation process, owing to site restrictions, time constraints, a large number of participants, and other objective reasons affecting the normal work order, each group of nurses participated in the simulation only twice. The other seven ISS instructors participated as observers, which may have affected the execution of the training program. In the future, training time should be reasonably arranged, the number of ISS teaching sessions should be increased appropriately, and multicenter research should be conducted to explore the impact of ISS teaching on nurses’ knowledge, skills, and other outcome indicators, as well as the impact of transfer to clinical practice.
