Comparison of stress sources and coping strategies in children practicing judo and their non-training peers
Authors: Katarzyna Szczepanik, Paweł Kurowski, Małgorzata Siekańska
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe age of 10 to 15 is a period of intense psychosocial changes, often accompanied by increased emotional tension. To date, there is a lack of in-depth analyses in which stress responses and coping strategies are compared between physically active children and their peers not engaged in regular sports training. The study was grounded in transactional and sport-specific coping frameworks and aimed to compare students practicing judo with those not involved in any sports discipline in terms of stress sources, symptoms and coping strategies. Understanding how stress perception and coping profiles differ between children engaged in regular sport and their non-training peers may help identify targets for preventive and psychoeducational programmes in early adolescence.MethodsA total of 553 students aged 10 to 15 participated in the study, including 276 children who regularly practiced judo and 277 peers not engaged in any sports disciplines. The grouping criterion was a self-reported participation in training at least three times per week or the absence of regular physical activity. Stress coping strategies were assessed using the standardised “How do you cope?” (JSR) questionnaire developed by Juczyński and Ogińska-Bulik, along with an original survey including questions on stress sources, symptoms and responses.ResultsChildren practicing judo reported significantly lower levels of school-related stress than their non-training peers (pHolm = 0.006), with 43.8% indicating they “never” or “rarely” experience stress (vs. 25.3% in the non-training group). Judo students also felt safer at school (“always”: 44.6% vs. 26.7%; pHolm = 0.006). School-related stressors such as grades, teacher criticism, tests, loneliness and other concerns were more frequently reported by the non-training children (all pHolm < 0.038). Psychosomatic symptoms such as lack of concentration (53.8% vs. 32.6%), stomach pain (40.1% vs. 24.3%) and hand tremors were also more common in the non-training group (pHolm = 0.014). Judo practitioners were more likely to cope through physical activity (42.8% vs. 11.3%; pHolm = 0.008), while non-training students more often used social media (46.4% vs. 29.5%) and video games (42.8% vs. 25.0%; pHolm = 0.008 for both). Judo practitioners showed lower emotion-focused coping, whereas evidence for higher situational active coping was weaker and should be interpreted cautiously.ConclusionThe study indicates that judo participation was associated with lower school-related stress and fewer selected psychosomatic symptoms in children, as well as with more frequent use of physical activity as an everyday coping behaviour. Judo practitioners were less likely to use passive stress relief (e.g. social media) and it was more probable for them to engage in physical activity. Findings align with sport-coping taxonomies and suggest applied opportunities for coping-skills training within youth judo. These findings suggest that structured sport participation, such as judo, may be associated with more favourable stress-and-coping profiles in early adolescence, and warrant further investigation in longitudinal designs.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13102-026-01802-w.Keywords: Youth sport psychology, Transactional stress and coping, Coping effectiveness, Emotion-focused vs. task-focused coping, Physical activity and mental health

Introduction

Between the ages of 10 and 15, emotional and cognitive development is particularly dynamic, increasing children's vulnerability to stressors. Stress, defined as a complex physiological and psychological response to demands that exceed an individual's adaptive capacity [16], can disrupt emotional balance during this period and lead to learning difficulties, psychosomatic symptoms and problems with social relationships [37]. In line with contemporary transactional accounts of stress and coping in youth, coping is conceptualised as a dynamic, context-dependent process that unfolds over time and is shaped by appraisals, emotions and available resources, rather than a fixed trait. This perspective is central in developmental work that integrates coping with emotion regulation and adjustment in childhood and adolescence [3]. In research, it is indicated that the most common stressors reported by children and adolescents are school-related situations, such as marks, exams, criticism from teachers and feelings of alienation [11, 36]. Difficult everyday experiences also play an important role, the intensity of which may result in the exacerbation of psychosomatic symptoms or emotional disorders [11, 36, 38, 42].

At the same time, early adolescence is characterised by intense biological changes. Analyses of developmental profiles demonstrate that changes in the hormonal system and the maturation of structures responsible for processing emotions, such as the amygdala and prefrontal cortex, increase susceptibility to stress and may contribute to the occurrence of anxiety symptoms and adaptation difficulties [31].

The ability to cope with stress in childhood is shaped by a number of factors, such as temperament, availability of social support and lifestyle. In research, it is shown that children differ in their responses to difficult situations depending on age, gender, level of perceived tension and previous emotional experiences [11, 22, 36]. In the literature, three basic categories of stress coping strategies are distinguished: adaptive (e.g. active action, planning), maladaptive (e.g. avoidance, withdrawal), and emotional (e.g. crying, isolation) [4]. The use of adaptive strategies promotes more effective coping with tension and is associated with better psychosocial functioning. In turn, the dominance of maladaptive or emotional strategies correlates with severity of anxiety symptoms, concentration difficulties and lowered mood [15, 30, 36].

In research, it has been indicated that regular physical activity is associated with emotional stability and mental health. Engagement in organised forms of training not only has a positive impact on physical fitness but it also has been linked to psychological competencies, such as self-regulation of emotions and coping with situations requiring adaptation [8, 9, 17, 32, 39, 40].

On the other hand, in physically inactive populations, a tendency towards problem avoidance and using passive forms of stress relief (e.g. social media scrolling, isolation) is more frequently observed [39].

Analyses conducted on a sample of 276 children training judo, aged 10–15, provided detailed data on differences in the characteristics of stressors and coping strategies among this population [18]. The predominance of school-related stressors over training-related ones was identified, and the most frequently applied — both dispositionally and situationally — was active coping. Reactions to difficult situations most often assumed the form of lack of concentration, silence and somatisation (e.g. abdominal pain). It is worth emphasizing that as many as 83.7% of the surveyed judokas indicated sports training as an effective method of coping with a demanding, stressful day, noting the potential of physical activity as a natural regulator of emotional tension. At the same time, only 6.5% of the children reported using breathing exercises as a self-regulation technique, which may suggest a low level of knowledge about alternative strategies for coping with stress.

These results constitute a starting point for further comparative analysis, taking a group of children who do not participate in organised sports training into account.

Therefore, it is important to examine whether children engaged in regular organised sport differ from their non-training peers in perceived stress, psychosomatic symptoms, and coping strategies, while recognising that such comparisons cannot determine causal effects of sport participation.

Despite the growing number of studies on stress in youth who participate in sport, comparative evidence including children who do not engage in regular organised physical activity remains limited. Therefore, it is important to examine whether children engaged in regular organised sport differ from their non-training peers in perceived stress, psychosomatic symptoms, and coping strategies, while recognising that such comparisons cannot determine causal effects of sport participation.

The aim of this study was to compare children practicing judo with their non-training peers in terms of school-related stress, reported stressors, psychosomatic symptoms, and coping strategies. Gender-related differences were also considered. The conceptual contribution of the study lies in integrating school stress, psychosomatic responses, everyday coping behaviours, and dispositional and situational JSR coping dimensions within one early-adolescent comparative sample. Thus, the study provides descriptive comparative evidence on stress-related and coping profiles associated with judo participation, without assuming causal effects of training.

Materials and methods

The study was conducted between May and June 2024 among children aged 10–15 years living in the Małopolska and Podkarpackie Voivodeships. The analysis included data from 553 primary school students (grades 4–8), including 276 children regularly practicing judo (at least three times a week) and 277 children not practicing any organised sports. The judo group included 113 girls and 163 boys, while the non-training group included 147 girls and 130 boys. There were no significant differences in age between the groups (M_judo = 11.70; M_ non-training = 12.11; p = 0.581) or between genders (p = 0.347), confirming homogeneity of the sample.

The inclusion criteria for the study were: age 10–15 years, primary school student status (grades 4–8), voluntary consent of the child and their legal guardian, and — in the case of the training group — regular participation in judo training (≥ 3 times per week). The non-training group consisted of children who did not participate in any extracurricular sports activities. Individuals who completed the surveys incorrectly (less than 1%, those who did not complete all fields) were excluded from the study.

In the study, the standardised "How Do You Cope?" (JSR) psychometric tool by Juczyński and Ogińska-Bulik [14] was employed. The JSR assesses dispositional and situational coping indexes and maps onto three higher-order categories: Active Coping (AC), Seeking Social Support (SSS), and Focusing on Emotions and Experiencing Them (FEE). Participants rated the frequency of implementing individual behaviours on a five-point Likert scale (0–4), where 0 meant ‘not at all’ and 4 meant ‘almost always’. Coping strategies (JSR). Coping strategies were assessed using the JSR questionnaire, which distinguishes dispositional and situational coping across three domains: active coping, focusing on emotions, and seeking social support. In the present sample, internal consistency (Cronbach’s α) for the dispositional subscales was 0.70 (active coping), 0.79 (focusing on emotions), and 0.61 (seeking social support). For the situational subscales, Cronbach’s α was 0.66 (active coping), 0.67 (focusing on emotions), and 0.64 (seeking social support). Given that each subscale comprises only three items, α coefficients were expected to be moderate,therefore, results—particularly for the social support subscales—should be interpreted with appropriate caution.

The JSR assesses coping as a set of cognitive and behavioral efforts that children and adolescents use to manage demands appraised as stressful. This construct is directly applicable to youth sport participation, where athletes regularly face evaluative and social stressors and must regulate emotions while maintaining task engagement The three coping domains captured by JSR (active coping, focusing on emotions, and seeking social support), assessed in both dispositional and situational forms, align with core coping processes reported in sport psychology models and empirical work on stress appraisal, emotions, and coping in athletes, including adolescent samples [2, 7, 19, 27, 28].

Although sport-specific instruments exist (e.g., the Coping Inventory for Competitive Sport, CICS/ISCCS), their items are tailored to in-competition contexts and typically validated on older adolescent or adult athletes. Gaudreau and Blondin [10] developed the Inventaire des Stratégies de Coping en Compétition Sportive (ISCCS) to assess coping strategies among athletes in competitive sport settings. Their sequential confirmatory factor analyses supported a ten-factor model, encompassing both task-oriented and emotion-oriented coping dimensions, and demonstrated concurrent validity with measures such as the MCOPE and WOCQ. Importantly, the ISCCS has been primarily validated on older adolescent and adult samples, with coping dimensions such as effort expenditure, relaxation, and venting of unpleasant emotion varying by gender and expertise level. Consequently, while this instrument provides valuable insight into competitive coping, its age- and context-specific focus limits its suitability for younger children assessed in school and everyday contexts. Given our school- and daily-life context and the age range of 10–15 years, the JSR provides developmentally appropriate coverage of core families of coping while preserving comparability with youth-coping literatures in psychology.

The study was conducted in paper-and-pencil form, in group, school and club settings. Participants were guaranteed anonymity, and each stage was carried out in accordance with guidelines for trials involving minors. The researchers provided instructions and supervised completion of the forms. The average completion time was approximately 20 min.

The research project received a positive opinion from the Bioethics Committee of Tarnów University (Resolution No. 12/2024 of April 17, 2024). The study was conducted in accordance with the provisions of the 1964 Declaration of Helsinki [41] and the applicable General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR).

Statistical analysis

Group differences were tested using Pearson’s χ2, Mann–Whitney U, and independent-samples t-tests (JSR scores). Holm correction was applied within each table (pHolm < 0.05). Gender-stratified comparisons and p-values reported in regression models are unadjusted for multiple comparisons and should be interpreted accordingly. Effect sizes (φ, r_rb, Cohen’s d) were reported. Multiple logistic regression with age and gender as covariates was used to complement univariate analyses; odds ratios (95% CI) and Nagelkerke’s R2 were reported (α = 0.05).

After adjustment for age and gender, multivariable logistic regression showed that higher dispositional concentration on emotions was associated with lower odds of belonging to the judo group (OR = 0.75, 95% CI [0.60–0.95], p = 0.016), whereas higher situational active coping was associated with increased odds (OR = 1.30, 95% CI [1.00–1.67], p = 0.047). Gender analysis showed that female judokas had significantly lower scores for focusing on emotions – both dispositional (1.19 ± 1.16 vs. 1.72 ± 1.23; p < 0.001) and situational (1.77 ± 1.05 vs. 2.29 ± 1.10; p < 0.001). Among boys, the greatest differences concerned situational active coping strategies, which were more frequently implemented by male judokas (2.32 ± 0.86 vs. 1.94 ± 1.09; p = 0.001).

This descriptive pattern may be considered in light of sport-specific syntheses that link task-oriented responses to more favourable affective and performance outcomes and emotion-focused/disengagement to less favourable ones [28].

Discussion

This study addresses a gap in the literature on the sources of stress, psychosomatic symptoms and coping strategies among 10- to 15-year-old children practicing judo and their non-training peers. Although the benefits—including mental health—of physical activity are well documented [6], young judokas have not previously been a major focus of research. In the current study, new data are provided on stress-related and coping profiles associated with judo participation in early adolescence. Given the cross-sectional design, all interpretations are framed as associations, and self-selection and unmeasured confounding remain plausible alternative explanations. The most consistent JSR-based finding was lower reliance on emotion-focused coping among judokas. Evidence for higher active or task-oriented coping was less consistent and should therefore be interpreted cautiously. This descriptive pattern may be considered in the context of developmental reviews and the broader meta-analytic literature linking coping profiles to mental health in youth [35], but it cannot be interpreted as evidence that judo participation produced these differences.

A particularly important context for interpreting these results is the need to combine school and sports responsibilities, referred to in the literature as a dual career. This phenomenon is described as one of the key risk factors for mental and physiological overload in training youth athletes [13, 34]. Empirical studies indicate that simultaneously functioning in the roles of student and athlete may contribute to burnout at school and in sport, while limited competence in managing dual-career demands increases the risk of negative psychosomatic consequences [13]. However, in the present study judokas reported significantly lower levels of academic stress and felt safer at school than their non-training peers. Non-training children more frequently reported school stressors and exhibited higher levels of psychosomatic symptoms, including lack of concentration, abdominal pain, and hand tremors—however, these differences may also reflect unmeasured individual, family, school, or socioeconomic characteristics.

Similar observations were made by Nawalana and Baran [25], who studied 160 students aged 13–17. The analysis of their results revealed that school-related factors and peer relationships were the primary sources of stress. Misiuk & Deptuła [23] identified similar categories of difficult situations among sixth- and seventh-grade primary school students, who noted school and peer relationships as the main sources of stress. The authors found that, regardless of the type of stressor, students most frequently used emotion-focused strategies and least often sought social support. In subsequent studies, it was also indicated that the main factors exacerbating anxiety and depression symptoms should be sought in school relationships with peers and teachers, as well as in one’s family environment. As in our study, the authors used the JSR questionnaire, demonstrating that the adolescents in their sample most frequently used the strategy of focusing on emotions. However, in crisis situations, this approach may prove maladaptive in the long run, leading to long-term difficulties.

One of our findings is the difference in everyday coping behaviours and selected JSR coping dimensions between the study groups. Judokas were significantly more likely to report physical activity as a way of dealing with stress. In contrast, the group of non-athletes more often chose passive forms of coping, such as browsing social media or playing video games. Analysis of the JSR questionnaire results showed that young judokas achieved lower scores on the Focus on Emotions scale, both in the dispositional and situational dimensions. Furthermore, higher active coping among judokas was observed mainly in selected analyses and should be interpreted cautiously. Beyond the frequency of specific strategies, current sport-psychology research highlights the concept of coping effectiveness—the functional alignment between chosen strategies, situational demands, and emotional appraisals. Evidence indicates that task-oriented techniques (e.g., effort, relaxation, imagery, thought control) are positively associated with performance satisfaction and emotional stability, whereas distraction and disengagement predict poorer affective outcomes [26, 28]. This literature provides a relevant context for the present findings, particularly the lower emotion-focused coping observed among judokas, because reliance on emotion-focused coping has been associated with less favourable adjustment in adolescent samples [20]. Gender differences observed here (boys: more task-oriented,girls: more emotion-focused) may be considered in relation to prior sport-coping work across youth samples. From an applied perspective, this may help inform future psychoeducational work without pathologising emotion-focused responses [27].

Gender-related differences in stress coping strategies were also observed, and their pattern varied by group. Girls tended to adopt an emotion-based approach, while boys more often chose an action-oriented coping strategy. This profile is consistent with previous observations by Rogowska & Kuśnierz [30] and Litwic-Kamińska [21], who indicated that females more often utilise emotional strategies and social support, while males prefer a task-based approach. In the present study, female judokas showed lower emotion-focused coping than their non-training peers, whereas higher active coping was most evident among boys practicing judo. This pattern is broadly consistent with previous studies reporting associations between judo participation and selected coping-related characteristics, although the present cross-sectional design does not allow causal interpretation [1, 12]. Therefore, these findings should be treated as descriptive associations. They suggest that gender and judo participation were related to different coping profiles in this sample, but they do not show whether judo participation modifies gender-related coping patterns.

The present findings are consistent with broader evidence showing that organised physical activity is associated with psychosocial benefits in children and adolescents, including better self-esteem, social functioning, and mental well-being [5, 6, 24]. However, these studies should be treated as a contextual framework rather than as direct evidence explaining the present results. In the current sample, the most robust findings were lower school-related stress, a higher sense of school safety, fewer selected psychosomatic symptoms, lower emotion-focused coping, and more frequent use of physical activity as a coping strategy among judokas. Thus, the results support a cautious interpretation that judo participation was associated with a more favourable stress-and-coping profile, without allowing conclusions about long-term developmental effects or causal mechanisms.A disturbing observation in our study is the low percentage of children using breathing exercises as a way to reduce stress – both among judokas (6.5%) and their non-training peers (11.2%). This result indicates underutilisation of this emotion regulation technique. Importantly, in the training group, despite the presence of breathing elements in a typical training programme, awareness of the exercises themselves, or even the fact of performing them, is very low. Similar observations were made by Rutkowska [33], who noted that young judokas rarely seek the help of a sports psychologist and, moreover, do not even know what such a specialist does. These data may indicate a potential area for future psychoeducational work, particularly because breathing-based self-regulation has been discussed as one potential pathway of autonomic regulation [29]. However, the present study did not test any breathing-based intervention,therefore, the effectiveness of such techniques in this population should be examined in future intervention studies.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, its cross-sectional design does not allow causal conclusions regarding the relationship between physical activity and coping strategies. It is possible that children with lower baseline stress or more adaptive coping profiles are more likely to engage in organised sport; therefore, a self-selection effect cannot be ruled out, and the observed associations require confirmation in longitudinal designs. Second, stress levels and coping responses were assessed using self-report questionnaires, which may introduce recall or social desirability bias. Third, the categorisation of participants into training and non-training groups relied on self-reported training frequency, which was not independently verified. Fourth, although the JSR questionnaire is developmentally appropriate for children, it is not sport-specific; therefore, some dimensions of athletic coping (e.g. competition-related strategies) could not be evaluated. Internal consistency for some JSR subscales was moderate, which may have attenuated effect sizes and should be considered when interpreting coping profiles. These measurement issues may have reduced the precision of coping assessment, particularly for social support and selected situational coping dimensions; therefore, coping-related group differences should be interpreted cautiouslyFifth, the study did not control for potential confounding variables such as socioeconomic status, parental support or general physical-activity levels beyond organised training. Consequently, the age- and gender-adjusted regression models should not be interpreted as controlling for the broader set of individual, family, school, and socioeconomic factors that may influence both sport participation and coping profiles. Sixth, pubertal timing was not assessed, which limits the interpretation of gender-stratified analyses across the 10–15 age range. Finally, the study was conducted in two regions of Poland, which may limit generalisability to broader populations.

Taken together, these limitations mean that the observed group differences should be interpreted as descriptive associations and not as evidence that judo participation itself directly reduces stress or improves coping.

Practical implications

The obtained results indicate that the school environment remains an important source of stress in the study population, and may therefore represent a relevant target for psychoeducational activities focused on marks, tests and relationships with teachers.. At the same time, the higher sense of safety and lower incidence of psychosomatic symptoms observed in the judo group suggest that regular participation in judo may be associated with more favourable psychosocial functioning in early adolescence; however, causal inferences cannot be drawn from this cross-sectional design. Gender-related differences observed in this study highlight the need to tailor psychoeducational content to diverse developmental needs, with a focus on strengthening a broad, flexible repertoire of adaptive coping strategies (e.g., problem-solving, help-seeking, and effective emotion regulation) in both girls and boys, rather than promoting sex-normative coping patterns. The low use of breathing exercises suggests a potential area for future educational or intervention work, but the present study did not evaluate the effectiveness of such techniques. Therefore, all practical implications should be regarded as preliminary. Longitudinal and intervention studies are needed before firm recommendations for coaching practice, school policy, or mental-health prevention programmes can be formulated.
