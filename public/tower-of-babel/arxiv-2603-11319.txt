On the Robustness of Langevin Dynamics to Score Function Error
Daniel Yiming Cao, August Y. Chen, Karthik Sridharan, Yuchen Wu

Abstract
We consider the robustness of score-based generative modeling to errors in the estimate of the score function. In particular, we show that Langevin dynamics is not robust to the L 2 L^{2} errors (more generally L p L^{p} errors) in the estimate of the score function. It is well-established that with small L 2 L^{2} errors in the estimate of the score function, diffusion models can sample faithfully from the target distribution under fairly mild regularity assumptions in a polynomial time horizon. In contrast, our work shows that even for simple distributions in high dimensions, Langevin dynamics run for any polynomial time horizon will produce a distribution far from the target distribution in Total Variation (TV) distance, even when the L 2 L^{2} error (more generally L p L^{p} ) of the estimate of the score function is arbitrarily small. Considering such an error in the estimate of the score function is unavoidable in practice when learning the score function from data, our results provide further justification for diffusion models over Langevin dynamics and serve to caution against the use of Langevin dynamics with estimated scores.

Introduction

Many sampling algorithms ubiquitous in statistics and Machine Learning (ML) – used from Bayesian inference to modern generative modeling – are score-based sampling algorithms. These sampling algorithms are widely used in a range of scientific and engineering applications, such as image generation (see e.g.
Croitoru et al. 2023
), inverse problems in applied mathematics (e.g.
Sanz-Alonso et al. 2023
), physical sciences (e.g.
Zheng et al. 2025
), protein design and computational biology (e.g.
Guo et al. 2024
), and medical image reconstruction (e.g.
Chung et al. 2022
).
Many such score-based or related sampling algorithms exist. An incomplete list includes Langevin dynamics (see e.g.
Welling and Teh 2011
;
Durmus et al. 2018
;
Chewi 2025
), diffusion models (e.g.
Ho et al. 2020
;
Song and Ermon 2019
;
Song et al. 2021a
;
Song and Ermon 2020
;
Song et al. 2021b
for some foundational early works), flow matching (e.g.
Lipman et al. 2023
), and stochastic interpolants (e.g.
Albergo et al. 2025
).

Among these sampling algorithms, we investigate Langevin dynamics and diffusion models. Langevin dynamics is a classical technique for sampling from target distributions in statistics and ML, while diffusion models are a modern, popular and effective method for generative AI.
At a high level, these algorithms approximately sample from a target distribution
\pi_{\rm tar}
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
by running a stochastic process driven by the
score function

\nabla\log\pi_{\rm tar}
in the case of Langevin dynamics, or a suitable sequence of score functions
\nabla\log\pi_{0},\nabla\log\pi_{1},\ldots,\nabla\log\pi_{k}
(the
annealed score functions
) in the case of diffusion models, where
\pi_{0}\approx\pi_{\rm tar}
and
\pi_{0},\ldots,\pi_{k}
forms a gradually noised version of the target distribution
\pi_{\rm tar}
. The continuous-time idealization of both these processes converges to
\pi_{\rm tar}
under mild assumptions on
\pi_{\rm tar}
, see Section
2.2
.

Our focus:
In generative modeling applications, the score functions
\nabla\log\pi_{\rm tar}
or
\nabla\log\pi_{0}
,
\nabla\log\pi_{1}
,
\ldots
,
\nabla\log\pi_{k}
are not explicitly known and must be estimated from data (from
\pi_{\rm tar}
). This is done in practice by
score matching
: training a suitable parametric model (e.g., neural network) to learn these functions from data, by minimizing a suitable
L^{2}
or similar loss.
See, e.g.,
Ho et al. 2020
;
Song and Ermon 2019
;
Song et al. 2021a
;
Song and Ermon 2020
;
Song et al. 2021b
;
Karras et al. 2022
;
Chen et al. 2023b
for diffusion models, and
Koehler et al. 2023
;
Pabbaraju et al. 2023
;
Koehler and Vuong 2024
;
Koehler et al. 2025
for Langevin dynamics. This yields estimates of the score function(s) that are accurate in, e.g.,
L^{2}
. It is a priori unclear whether running Langevin dynamics or diffusion models with these estimates – as done in practice – leads to faithful samples from the target
\pi_{\rm tar}
as happens with the respective idealized continuous-time processes. This motivates the fundamental question:

Main Question:

Is small
L^{2}
(or more generally
L^{p})
score estimation error with respect to
\pi_{\rm tar}
sufficient for the success of score-based sampling algorithms?

This question has been answered affirmatively in the context of diffusion models (see e.g.
Chen et al. 2023a
;
Benton et al. 2024
;
Li et al. 2024
;
Liang et al. 2025
among many more works) for general target distributions
\pi_{\rm tar}
with bounded second moment. Here we emphasize that this result for diffusion models assumes that a suitable weighted average of the
L^{2}
score estimation errors of
all of

\nabla\log\pi_{0},\nabla\log\pi_{1},\ldots,\nabla\log\pi_{k}
(with respect to
\pi_{0},\pi_{1},\ldots,\pi_{k}
respectively) is small, rather than just small
L^{2}
estimation error of
\nabla\log\pi_{\rm tar}
.
In particular, let
\widehat{\pi}_{\rm tar}
denote the output distribution. With an estimated score
s
and appropriate discretization, it is known that within a number of iterations
polynomial
in
d
, the sampling error scales linearly with the
L^{2}
score estimation error:

\displaystyle\mathrm{TV}(\pi_{\rm tar},\widehat{\pi_{\rm tar}})\lesssim\varepsilon_{\rm score},

where
\mathrm{TV}
is Total Variation (TV) distance and
\varepsilon_{\rm score}^{2}
is a suitable weighted average of the
L^{2}
score estimation errors of
\nabla\log\pi_{0},\nabla\log\pi_{1},\ldots,\nabla\log\pi_{k}
with respect to
\pi_{0},\pi_{1},\ldots,\pi_{k}
respectively.
Such a result justifies the empirical success of diffusion models run with estimated score functions, such as those learned via score matching.

In sharp contrast,
the Main Question has not been answered for Langevin dynamics
.

1.1
Main Results

In this work,
we strongly answer the Main Question in the negative for Langevin dynamics.
We show that the realistic assumption of an
L^{2}
-accurate (and more generally
L^{p}
-accurate) score estimate
\hat{s}
to
\nabla\log\pi_{\rm tar}
with respect to
\pi_{\rm tar}
does not suffice for Langevin dynamics to successfully sample.
As discussed above, to the best of our knowledge, no works have yet answered this question.
As per
Chen et al. 2023a
;
Koehler and Vuong 2024
;
Koehler et al. 2025
, this assumption on
L^{2}
score estimation is most natural in the context of score estimates
\hat{s}
that are learned from data, for example in generative modeling applications.
We establish:

1.

Theorem
1

:
Consider perhaps the most innocuous possible example, when
\pi_{\rm tar}
is an isotropic Gaussian in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and when we initialize Langevin dynamics at a standard Gaussian

. When the estimated score
\hat{s}
satisfies an arbitrarily small global

bound

from this natural initialization remains far from

for any

In particular, the TV distance between

and the law of Langevin dynamics run with

initialization from

. As a corollary, in high dimensions, the mixing time of Langevin dynamics run with

is also larger than any polynomial.

, which is the

Significance of our results:

Theorems
1
,
7
, and
11
demonstrate standard bounds on
L^{2}
error (more generally
L^{p}
) of learning the score is completely insufficient to control sampling error for Langevin dynamics, despite being the objective of choice in practice when estimating the score via score matching.
Note an
L^{\infty}
bound is not possible in score matching where one cannot hope to learn the score in regions not well-covered by
\pi_{\rm tar}
.
As such
our results provide strong evidence against the efficacy of Langevin dynamics with estimated scores (e.g. from data), even in very simple problems
.
This result stands in sharp contrast to diffusion models, where also convergence occurs in
\mathrm{poly}(d)
time; as such our work provides further support for diffusion models from a novel angle.

We emphasize the lower bounds of Theorems
1
and
7
are fundamentally caused by the lack of robustness of Langevin dynamics in high dimensions to
L^{2}
(more generally
L^{p}
) score estimation error; the examples are not degenerate.
The target distribution is as simple as an isotropic Gaussian, the score estimates
\hat{s}
are Lipschitz, and the initializations considered are natural.

We also emphasize the practical prescriptions of our work. Theorem
7
highlights that one should use
fresh
samples when performing data-based initialization, a prescription validated by simulation in Section
4
. However, score functions are often trained on the entire dataset in practice. Our results serve to caution against doing so.

Finally, our work also highlights the importance of
annealing
. By convolving
\pi_{\rm tar}
with a Gaussian and learning the scores along the convolution, an
L^{2}
or
L^{p}
estimate now becomes sufficient for sampling as per the theory of diffusion models.
For Gaussian mixtures, learning the score function
\nabla\log\pi_{\rm tar}
may be the hardest step of learning all the annealed score functions
(
Chen et al. 2025
)
, as intuitively convolution with Gaussians can only ‘soften’ the annealed score functions (though we emphasize that this is not always the case, see
Montanari and Vu 2025
;
Koehler and Vuong 2024
).
Despite this, annealing seems crucial to enable successful sampling from estimated scores.

2.1
Langevin Dynamics

Letting
\pi_{\rm tar}\propto e^{-V(x)}
be the target distribution on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the associated score function is
\nabla\log\pi_{\rm tar}(x)=-\nabla V(x)
.
The overdamped Langevin dynamics is the stochastic differential equation (SDE)

\mathrm{d}X_{t}=\nabla\log\pi_{\rm tar}(X_{t})\,\mathrm{d}t+\sqrt{2}\,\mathrm{d}B_{t},\qquad X_{0}\sim\mu_{0},

(1)

where
\mu_{0}
is arbitrary.
Under mild regularity conditions,
\pi_{\rm tar}
is the stationary distribution for the SDE (
1
), and
X_{t}\overset{d}{\to}\pi_{\rm tar}
as
t\to\infty

(
Chiang and Chow 1989
)
.

Non-Asymptotic Convergence:

The SDE (
1
) converges
efficiently
to
\pi_{\rm tar}
in
\mathrm{TV}
(in polynomial time in
d
) when
\pi_{\rm tar}
satisfies a
Poincaré Inequality
, with stronger guarantees on the convergence when
\pi_{\rm tar}
satisfies a
Log-Sobolev Inequality

(
Villani et al. 2008
;
Bakry et al. 2014
)
. The Poincaré Inequality holds for log-concave
\pi_{\rm tar}
(convex
V(x)
)
(
Bobkov 1999
)
. Meanwhile the Log-Sobolev Inequality holds for strongly-log-concave
\pi_{\rm tar}
(strongly convex
V(x)
)
(
Bakry and Émery 2006
)
. Specifically, if
\nabla^{2}V(x)\succeq\alpha I_{d}
, then the Log-Sobolev constant of
\pi_{\rm tar}
is at most
\frac{1}{\alpha}
.
5
5

5

Or
\frac{2}{\alpha}
, depending on the normalization used.

In particular,
for well-conditioned Gaussians, e.g. isotropic Gaussians, we expect the SDE (
1
) to sample efficiently in
\mathrm{TV}
from
\pi_{\rm tar}
.

Discretization:

One discretizes (
1
) to sample from
\pi_{\rm tar}
with a discrete-time algorithm. There are several discretizations of (
1
) in the literature which enjoy polynomial convergence to
\pi_{\rm tar}
in
\mathrm{TV}
when
\pi_{\rm tar}
is a Gaussian and more generally, when
V
is strongly convex
(
Chewi 2025
)
, and when
\nabla V
is Lipschitz (which is the case for our lower bound constructions in Theorems
1
,
7
). Perhaps the most natural is the
Unadjusted Langevin Algorithm
(ULA):

\displaystyle X_{t+1}=X_{t}+\eta\nabla\log\pi_{\rm tar}(X_{t})+\sqrt{2\eta}\,\epsilon_{t},\qquad X_{0}\sim\mu_{0},

(2)

where
\eta>0
is the step size,
\mu_{0}
is arbitrary, and
\epsilon_{t}\sim N(0,I_{d})
are i.i.d. Gaussians. This algorithm has been widely studied as a natural discretization of (
1
), see e.g.
Vempala and Wibisono 2019
;
Lee et al. 2022
;
Chewi et al. 2025
.
See
Chewi 2025
for many more details. Note
all these discretizations aim to simulate the idealization
(
1
), see
Chewi 2025
for further discussion.

2.2
Diffusion Models

Diffusion models were introduced in several key early works
(
Song and Ermon 2019
;
Ho et al. 2020
;
Song et al. 2021a
;
Song et al. 2021b
)
, and were originally motivated by the idea of
time-reversal
. One considers an SDE or other stochastic process that converges from
\pi_{\rm tar}
to
N(0,I_{d})
, known as the
forward process
. One then applies the theory of time-reversal of SDEs
(
Haussmann and Pardoux 1986
)
to obtain a stochastic process that converges from
N(0,I_{d})
to
\pi_{\rm tar}
, known as the
reverse process
. One discretizes the reverse process, yielding an algorithm that samples from
\pi_{\rm tar}
.

For a high-level overview, one frequently used choice of forward process, proposed in
Song and Ermon 2019
;
Ho et al. 2020
;
Song et al. 2021b
(though certainly not the only one) is the Ornstein–Uhlenbeck (OU) process:

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}\bar{X}_{t}=-\bar{X}_{t}\,\mathrm{d}t+\sqrt{2}\,\mathrm{d}B_{t},\qquad\bar{X}_{0}\sim\pi_{\rm tar}.

(3)

Letting
\pi_{t}
denote the law of
\bar{X}_{t}
, and fixing a terminal time
T\geq 0
, the corresponding reverse process is the SDE

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}X_{t}=(X_{t}+2\nabla\log\pi_{T-t}(X_{t}))\,\mathrm{d}t+\sqrt{2}\,\mathrm{d}B_{t},\qquad X_{0}\sim\pi_{T}\,.

(4)

By the theory of time-reversal of SDEs
(
Haussmann and Pardoux 1986
)
, the above reverse process converges to
\pi_{\rm tar}
; moreover by convergence results for the OU process, for appropriate
T>0
,
\pi_{T}\approx N(0,I_{d})
. This is an example of the Denoising Diffusion Probabilistic Models (DDPM) framework
(
Ho et al. 2020
)
. This is by no means the only type of algorithm subsumed by diffusion models. For example, one can also time-reverse the forward process (
3
) to yield an Ordinary Differential Equation (ODE); this is the Denoising Diffusion Implicit Models (DDIM) framework
(
Song et al. 2021a
)
.

Discretization:

To sample from
\pi_{\rm tar}
with a discrete-time algorithm, one discretizes (
4
) in the case of the DDPMs, and more generally discretizes the suitable reverse process (e.g. the ODE from DDIMs). This leads to a gradually noised sequence of distributions
\pi_{0}\approx\pi_{\rm tar},\ldots,\pi_{k}
. Note
\pi_{0}
is only approximately equal to
\pi_{\rm tar}
due to early stopping often employed in practice, see e.g.
Karras et al. 2022
.
To do so, one must learn estimates of the score functions
\nabla\log\pi_{0},\ldots,\nabla\log\pi_{k}
from i.i.d. data from
\pi_{\rm tar}
via
score matching
, see e.g.
(
Hyvärinen and Dayan 2005
;
Vincent 2011
;
Song and Ermon 2019
;
Karras et al. 2022
)
.

It has been established that with an estimated score
s
and appropriate discretization, the sampling error in
\mathrm{TV}
scales linearly with the
L^{2}
score estimation error, within
\mathrm{poly}(d)
iterations. Specifically, the sampling error in
\mathrm{TV}
scales linearly in a suitable weighted average of the
L^{2}
score estimation errors of
\nabla\log\pi_{0},\nabla\log\pi_{1},\ldots,\nabla\log\pi_{k}
. This result applies for the reverse process of both DDPMs and DDIMs; see e.g.
Chen et al. 2023b
;
Benton et al. 2024
;
Li et al. 2024
;
Jiao et al. 2025
, among many other works.

3.1
Standard normal initialization

Our first Theorem yields a lower bound for Langevin Dynamics when run with standard normal initialization.

Theorem 1

(Standard Normal Initialization Lower Bound)
.

Consider
\pi_{\rm tar}=N(\mu,I_{d})
for any vector
\mu
with
\|\mu\|=7\sqrt{d}
. For a universal constant
\alpha=\alpha_{\ref{lem:OU_hitting_time_general}}>0
given in Lemma
17
, define the score estimate
\hat{s}(x)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}-\alpha x&:\|x\|\leq 4\sqrt{d}\\
-(x-\mu)&:\|x\|\geq 5\sqrt{d}\\
-\psi\big(\frac{x}{\sqrt{d}}\big)\cdot\alpha x-\big(1-\psi\big(\frac{x}{\sqrt{d}}\big)\big)\cdot(x-\mu)&:\text{ else},\end{cases}

where
\psi(\cdot)\in[0,1]
is a suitable interpolating bump function given in Lemma
16
. Then defining
X_{t}
be the SDE

\mathrm{d}X_{t}=\hat{s}(X_{t})\,\mathrm{d}t+\sqrt{2}\,\mathrm{d}B_{t},\qquad X_{0}\sim N(0,I_{d}),

(5)

we have:

1.

Small
L^{p}
error: for any
p\geq 1
and
d\geq d_{0}(p)
large enough in terms of
p
,

\displaystyle\E_{x\sim\pi_{\rm tar}}\big[\|\hat{s}(x)-\nabla\log\pi_{\rm tar}(x)\|^{p}\big]^{1/p}\leq e^{-\Omega(d)}.

(6)

2.

Far from
\pi_{\rm tar}
in Total Variation on sub-exponential time scales: for any

Proof ideas of Theorem
1
:

We show the proof ideas for each of the claimed parts of the Theorem.

Small

error:

The
L^{p}
error under
\pi_{\rm tar}
of

is
exponentially small in high dimensions
by results on Gaussian concentration of the norm (see Lemma

). Under
\pi_{\rm tar}
, the set
\big\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\|x\|\leq 5\sqrt{d}\big\}
has exponentially small mass, while in this region,

is at most polynomial in
d
. Essentially, in high dimensions one can ‘hide’ the bad set
\big\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\|x\|\leq 5\sqrt{d}\big\}
w.r.t.
\pi_{\rm tar}
.

Lipschitz
\hat{s}
:

\hat{s}
is Lipschitz by direct calculation, as
\psi(\cdot)
from Lemma

and is Lipschitz with universal constant bound on its Lipschitz constant.

Far from
\pi_{\rm tar}
:
Suppose the initialization

\|x_{0}\|\leq 1.1\sqrt{d}

If

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}\bar{X}_{t}=-\alpha\bar{X}_{t}\mathrm{d}t+\sqrt{2}\mathrm{d}B_{t},\qquad\bar{X}_{0}=x_{0}.

(8)

The SDE (

.
Since
\alpha

well within

8

Specifically, letting

denote the law of (

Lemma 4
.

Suppose

for a universal constant

1-3\alpha e^{-c_{\ref{lem:OU_hitting_time_general}}d}

.

See Subsection
7.1
for the proof. Now to establish the desired lower bound on

only reaches

puts almost all its mass on

3.2
Data-based initialization

A natural strategy for initializing Langevin dynamics when one has a score estimate
\hat{s}
learned from data drawn from
\pi_{\rm tar}
is
data-based initialization
, studied in
Koehler and Vuong 2024
;
Koehler et al. 2025
.
Here, one initializes Langevin dynamics from i.i.d. samples
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}
drawn from
\pi_{\rm tar}
: we let
X_{0}\sim\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{x_{i}}
, the empirical distribution of these samples.
This is a natural initialization when one has i.i.d. samples from
\pi_{\rm tar}
and aims to produce more of them.
As discussed in
Koehler and Vuong 2024
, p. 4, data-based initialization is also closely related to contrastive divergence
(
Hinton 2002
;
Hinton 2012
;
Xie et al. 2016
;
Gao et al. 2018
)
.

Koehler and Vuong 2024
;
Koehler et al. 2025
established several appealing properties of data-based initialization:

1) It is robust to
L^{2}
-error (with respect to
\pi_{\rm tar}
) in the score estimates
\hat{s}
, a natural assumption when
\hat{s}
is learned from data drawn from
\pi_{\rm tar}
.

2) With
n=\mathrm{poly}(d)
samples, it can alleviate the exponential in
d
mixing time of Langevin dynamics from many mixture distributions
(
Bovier et al. 2004
;
Bovier et al. 2005
;
Menz and Schlichting 2014
)
(though Langevin dynamics mixes rapidly for
\pi_{\rm tar}
in Theorem
7
).

Our result, Theorem
7
, complements the work of
Koehler and Vuong 2024
;
Koehler et al. 2025
, by showing that the robustness of data-based initialization only applies when
fresh
samples different from those used to learn
\hat{s}
are used for the initialization.
We believe this serves as an important warning to use
fresh
samples when applying data-based initialization or similar methods in practice.

To state Theorem
7
, we need the following definition.

Definition 6

(General Position)
.

We say
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
are in general position if
\|x_{i}-x_{j}\|\geq 0.4\sqrt{d}
for all
1\leq i\neq j\leq n
and
0.5\sqrt{d}\leq\|x_{i}\|\leq 2\sqrt{d}
for all
1\leq i\leq n
.

Note by Triangle Inequality that if
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}
are in general position, and if
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is such that
\|x-x_{i}\|\leq 0.16\sqrt{d}
for some
x_{i}
, then this
x_{i}
is unique.

Theorem 7

(Data-Based Initialization Lower Bound)
.

Consider

.
Consider

3.3
Lower bounds for general target distributions

In this section, we consider a broad class of target distributions
\pi_{\rm tar}
beyond the Gaussian case.
For
all
such distributions and
all
initializations, we establish a lower bound on the efficacy of Langevin dynamics in the asymptotic limit
t\rightarrow\infty
.
We consider a target distribution
\pi_{\rm tar}
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
that satisfies the following assumptions.

Assumption 10
.

Assume that (1)
\nabla\log\pi_{\rm tar}
exists almost everywhere and is Lipschitz continuous, (2)
\E_{\pi_{\rm tar}}[\|\nabla\log\pi_{\rm tar}(x)\|^{2}]<\infty
, and (3) the density of
\pi_{\rm tar}
is strictly positive.

We show in this section that there exists a score estimate
\hat{s}
such that
\E_{\pi_{\rm tar}}[\|\hat{s}(x)-\nabla\log\pi_{\rm tar}(x)\|_{2}^{2}]\leq\varepsilon^{2}_{\rm score}
, yet the resulting overdamped Langevin dynamics can be arbitrarily inaccurate.
We define the convex cone
\mbox{Cone}_{\theta,u}
as follows
(
Boyd and Vandenberghe 2004
)
:
for a half–angle
\theta\in(0,\pi/2)
and unit vector
u\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

\displaystyle\mathsf{Cone}_{\theta,u}

\displaystyle:=\Big\{y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\{0\}:\ \big\langle\frac{y}{\|y\|},\,u\big\rangle\geq\cos\theta\Big\}

(13)

\displaystyle=\{y:\ g(y)\geq 0\},\,g(y):=\langle y,u\rangle-\|y\|\cos\theta.

Our Theorem says that for any
\varepsilon_{\rm score},\,\varepsilon_{\rm TV}>0
, we can choose
\theta
and
u
appropriately and in terms of
\theta
and
u
, create a score estimate
\hat{s}
with small
L^{2}
error but large
\mathrm{TV}
sampling error. Specifically, we establish:

Theorem 11
.

For any
\varepsilon_{\rm score},\,\varepsilon_{\rm TV}>0
,
there exist a unit vector
u\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\theta\in(0,\pi/2)
such that the following holds. There exists a score estimate
\hat{s}
that is piecewise Lipschitz such that we have:

1.

Small
L^{2}
error:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{\rm tar}}[\|\hat{s}(x)-\nabla\log\pi_{\rm tar}(x)\|^{2}]\leq\varepsilon^{2}_{\rm score}.

2.

Small cone probability:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\pi_{\rm tar}}(\rm{Cone}_{\theta,u})=\delta_{\theta,u}\in(0,1),\qquad\delta_{\theta,u}\leq\varepsilon_{\rm TV}.

3.

Far from
\pi_{\rm tar}
in Total Variation as
t\rightarrow\infty
: for any initial distribution
\pi_{0}
, define the SDE

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}X_{t}=\hat{s}(X_{t})\mathrm{d}t+\sqrt{2}\mathrm{d}B_{t},\qquad X_{0}\sim\pi_{0},

and let
\pi_{t}
be the distribution of
X_{t}
. Then
\mathbb{P}_{\pi_{t}}(\mathrm{Cone}_{\theta,u})\to 1
as
t\to\infty
. Thus we have by 2) that
\liminf_{t\to\infty}\mathrm{TV}(\pi_{\rm tar},\pi_{t})\geq 1-\varepsilon_{\mathrm{TV}}
.

Note the SDE given above has a unique strong solution by Zvonkin’s Theorem
(
Zvonkin 1974
)
as
\hat{s}
is piecewise Lipschitz. We prove Theorem
11
in Appendix
8
.

Theorem
11
establishes a lower bound against the efficacy of Langevin dynamics run with a score estimate with small
L^{2}
error for a broad class of target distributions
\pi_{\rm tar}
and
any
initialization, that holds in the limit
t\rightarrow\infty
.

4
Simulations

We validate the practical implications of Theorem
7
via simulation.
Specifically, we consider two target distributions
\pi_{\rm tar}
: a single Gaussian
N(\text{1},2I_{d})
with
d=50
and a mixture of Gaussians (GMM)
\frac{1}{2}N(-\text{1},2I_{d})+\frac{1}{2}N(4\text{1},2I_{d})
with
d=25
.
Full details and results are in Section
9
.

Learning the score:
We learn an estimate
\hat{s}
of the score function
\nabla\log\pi_{\rm tar}
with a fully connected neural network with 3 hidden layers trained with Adam
(
Kingma and Ba 2015
)
for 150,000 epochs.
To encourage an overfit or ‘memorized’ score estimate
\hat{s}
, we create a training set of 10,000 samples by drawing 1000 i.i.d. samples from
\pi_{\rm tar}
and duplicating each sample 10 times.
We learn the score by score matching; we learn the denoiser at the 100 lowest noise levels out of a linear noise schedule of 1000 levels (e.g.
Ho et al. 2020
).

Sampling algorithms:
We run fixed-step-size Langevin dynamics for 1000 iterations, evaluating the denoiser at the 10th lowest noise level out of the noise schedule to approximate
\nabla\log\pi_{\rm tar}
at each iteration.
We use 1000 iterations to simulate a
\mathrm{poly}(d)
timescale, and as score-based generative models rarely use more than 1000 denoiser evaluations in practice
(
Karras et al. 2022
)
.
We initialize Langevin dynamics at
n
points to produce
n\in\{1500,7500,13500,16250\}
samples the following three ways, yielding 3 different algorithms. 1) initialize
n
draws from
N(0,I_{d})
, 2) take 30 fresh i.i.d. draws from
\pi_{\rm tar}
each duplicated
n/30
times, and 3) take 30 random draws from the 1000 distinct training samples each duplicated
n/30
times. Here the 30 samples represent a random subset of the training samples or fresh samples; we repeat
n/30
times to produce
n
total samples.

Figure 1
:
KL of produced samples vs Gaussian
\pi_{\rm tar}
. KL is estimated by fitting a Gaussian
\hat{\pi}
to the produced samples and analytically computing
\mathrm{KL}\big(\pi_{\rm tar},\hat{\pi}\big)
.

Figure 2
:
Empirical Wasserstein distance of produced samples vs GMM
\pi_{\rm tar}
. Wasserstein distance was approximated via the Sinkhorn loss
(
Cuturi 2013
)
.

Takeaway:
We plot our results in Figure
1
for Gaussian
\pi_{\rm tar}
and Figure
2
for GMM
\pi_{\rm tar}
.
Algorithms 1, 2, 3 are denoted ‘vanilla’, ’fresh’, ’train’ respectively.
As predicted by Theorem
7
, initializing from samples drawn from the training set (Algorithm 3) generally produces worse results than when the samples are fresh (Algorithm 2).
For Gaussian
\pi_{\rm tar}
, the gap between Algorithms 2 and 3 is significant, and Algorithm 1 performs similarly to Algorithm 2.
For GMM
\pi_{\rm tar}
, the gap between Algorithms 2 and 3 is smaller, although Algorithm 3 still generally does worse than Algorithm 2, and Algorithm 1 performs the worst of the three methods due to the poor spectral gap of the GMM.

5
Conclusion

In this paper, we established that Langevin dynamics is not robust to
L^{p}
errors in the estimate of the score function in high dimensions, even when the
L^{p}
score estimation error is exponentially small.
Our lower bounds apply to natural examples: simple isotropic Gaussian target distributions, Lipschitz score estimates, and natural initializations, namely from
N(0,I_{d})
and from i.i.d. samples used to learn the score.
As such, our work cautions against the use of Langevin dynamics with an estimated score.

Additional Notation:

We let

We first introduce several key technical preliminaries. The first two results we present are on concentration of measure in high dimensions.

Lemma 12

Lemma 13

(Gaussian concentration of Lipschitz functions; see e.g. (5.4.2),
Bakry et al. 2014
)
.

Suppose
f
is a
L
-Lipschitz function. Then there exists an absolute constant
C>0
such that for all
t>0

We will also use the Borell-TIS (Tsirelson–Ibragimov–Sudakov) Inequality to obtain concentration of a relevant supremum.

Theorem 15

\displaystyle\PP\Big(\sup_{t\in T}f_{t}-\E\big[\sup_{t\in T}f_{t}\big]>u\Big)\leq e^{-\frac{u^{2}}{2\sigma_{T}^{2}}}.

\displaystyle\E\big[(\bar{Z}_{t_{1},v_{1}}-\bar{Z}_{t_{2},v_{2}})^{2}\big]

\displaystyle d\big((v_{1},t_{1}),(v_{2},t_{2})\big)\leq\sqrt{8\|v_{1}-v_{2}\|^{2}+8|t_{2}-t_{1}|}\leq 3\big(\|v_{1}-v_{2}\|+|t_{2}-t_{1}|^{1/2}\big)

Simulation details

Learning the score:

We follow the DDPM setup for learning an estimate of the score function (see Algorithm 1 of
Ho et al. 2020
).
In particular, we consider a linear noise schedule at 1000 noise levels, where for each
1\leq t\leq 1000
,
\beta_{t}=\frac{1001-t}{1000}\cdot 10^{-4}+\frac{t-1}{1000}\cdot 10^{-2}
.
We train a NN
\hat{s}_{\theta}(x,t)
that takes as input
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
t\in\{1,\ldots,1000\}
to learn the added noise (the manner in which noise is added will be described next). The NN has three hidden layers, where the hidden layers are of dimension 256 for Gaussian
\pi_{\rm tar}
and are of dimension 512 for GMM
\pi_{\rm tar}
.

We create a training set of 10000 samples by drawing 1000 i.i.d. samples from
\pi_{\rm tar}
and duplicating each sample 10 times.
The NN is trained with the Adam
(
Kingma and Ba 2015
)
optimizer run for 150,000 epochs with learning rate
10^{-3}
.
The batch size is 10,000, the entire dataset.
In training we perform the following procedure.
We draw
t
uniformly from
\{1,\ldots,200\}
.
Let
\bar{\alpha}_{t}=\prod_{t^{\prime}=1}^{t}(1-\beta_{t^{\prime}})
.
Then for all
x_{i}
in the batch (which here is the whole dataset), we let
x_{i}^{\prime}=\sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_{t}}x_{i}+\sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}_{t}}\epsilon_{i}
where
\epsilon_{i}\sim N(0,I_{d})
are drawn i.i.d. for each
x_{i}
.
We aim to minimize the mean square loss between
\hat{s}_{\theta}(x_{i}^{\prime},t)
and
\epsilon_{i}
over the batch (again, which here is the whole dataset).
As we mentioned earlier, these design choices were made to encourage a
\hat{s}
that overfits to the 1000 i.i.d. samples from
\pi_{\rm tar}
.
We focus on
t
drawn uniformly from
\{1,\ldots,200\}
– which are lower noise levels – because we aim to learn an approximation to
\nabla\log\pi_{\rm tar}
, which corresponds to no noise added to the training data.

Sampling algorithms:
We run Langevin dynamics by implementing ULA as written in (
2
) with fixed step-size 0.0025 for 1000 iterations. As mentioned earlier, we use 1000 iterations to simulate a
\mathrm{poly}(d)
timescale (recall here
d=25
or
50
), and as score-based generative models rarely use more than 1000 denoiser evaluations in practice with fewer denoiser evaluations being considered desirable
(
Karras et al. 2022
)
. To use our trained NN
\hat{s}_{\theta}(x,t)
to approximate the score function
\nabla\log\pi_{\rm tar}
, we approximate
\nabla\log\pi_{\rm tar}(x)
by
-\frac{\hat{s}_{\theta}(x,10)}{\sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}_{10}}}
.
We set
t=10
here, a low noise level, as
\nabla\log\pi_{\rm tar}
corresponds to no noise added. We did not set
t
as low as possible and rather chose the noise level
t=10
because it was better represented in training, leading to a
\hat{s}_{\theta}(x,t)
being a more faithful score estimate for such
t
.

Compute:
Our experiments were all run on a single RTX A6000 GPU.

Additional results:
We also plot the empirical Wasserstein distance between the produced samples and
\pi_{\rm tar}
for the case when
\pi_{\rm tar}
is a Gaussian.
The plot is given in Figure
3
.
As with the plot for empirical Wasserstein distance between the produced samples and
\pi_{\rm tar}
for Gaussian
\pi_{\rm tar}
, the Wasserstein distance is computed with the Sinkhorn loss
(
Cuturi 2013
)
.
In particular, for both of these computations, we draw 5000 samples from
\pi_{\rm tar}
. We then run 10 trials, and for each trial we subsample 500 points from the produced sample and the 5000 sampled points from
\pi_{\rm tar}
, computing the Sinkhorn loss between these two sets of 500 points.
The Sinkhorn loss is applied with
p=2
and blur scaling 0.01.
Note we did not plot KL for GMM
\pi_{\rm tar}
since estimating KL from samples is unstable and expensive in high dimensions, and fitting the produced samples to a Gaussian does not make sense when
\pi_{\rm tar}
is not a Gaussian.
