Couple-based preconception care as a gender-responsive strategy for improving health and well-being: a systematic review
Authors: Patience Fakornam Doe, Frederick Acheampong Nimo, Edward Odoom, Mildred Awinpiini Asore, Amidu Alhassan, Godswill Sedinam Lanyo, Justice Enock Kagbo, Bernard Nabe, Mustapha Amoadu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAdverse reproductive outcomes such as infertility, miscarriage, preterm birth, and congenital anomalies remain major public health concerns. Preconception care (PCC) can help address these risks, but most programmes focus mainly on women, leaving men under-involved. Couple-based preconception care (CB-PCC) offers a more inclusive and gender-responsive approach. This review aimed to synthesise global evidence on CB-PCC interventions and their effects on reproductive health outcomes.MethodsA systematic search was conducted in PubMed Central, Scopus, CINAHL, and JSTOR, with additional hand searches. Studies were included if they involved couples or men of reproductive age, delivered PCC interventions before conception, and reported couple-related outcomes. Data were extracted using a structured form and appraised with Joanna Briggs Institute tools. A thematic synthesis was used to summarise findings.ResultsFourteen studies from 10 countries were included, most from high-income settings. CB-PCC interventions covered biomedical, lifestyle, psychosocial, and family planning components. Reported outcomes included improved fertility treatment success, healthier lifestyle habits, reduced psychosocial distress, stronger partner communication, and increased contraceptive uptake. However, studies varied in design, delivery mode, and measurement, limiting direct comparisons.ConclusionCB-PCC shows promise in promoting shared responsibility and improving preconception health behaviours. However, most evidence comes from high-income countries and heterogeneous study designs. More high-quality research is needed in low- and middle-income settings. Future programmes should adapt CB-PCC to local contexts, promote male engagement, and integrate it into existing health systems to strengthen reproductive health outcomes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12978-026-02385-y.Keywords: Couple-based care, Preconception care, Male involvement, Reproductive health, Shared decision-making

Introduction

Adverse reproductive outcomes such as infertility, miscarriage, stillbirth, preterm birth, and congenital anomalies remain major global health challenges [1–3]. Despite remarkable progress in maternal and child health, millions of couples continue to experience preventable reproductive losses each year. The World Health Organization (WHO) estimates that one in six people of reproductive age worldwide experience infertility in their lifetime [4]. Likewise, 13.4 million preterm births and approximately 1.9 million stillbirths occur annually, with the highest burden in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) [1]. Congenital disorders also account for around 240,000 neonatal deaths globally each year [5]. These outcomes impose profound physical, psychological, and socioeconomic consequences on couples and health systems. In LMICs, reproductive risks are further aggravated by inequities in healthcare access, socio-cultural norms, poor health-seeking behaviours, and limited male engagement in reproductive health services. Consequently, global health strategies emphasise the need for preventive and inclusive interventions that address modifiable risks prior to conception where the foundation for successful pregnancies and healthy births is laid.

Preconception care (PCC) has emerged as a vital public health strategy to reduce the burden of adverse reproductive outcomes. It encompasses biomedical, behavioural, and social interventions aimed at improving the health status of women and men before conception occurs [6]. PCC interventions include promoting adequate timing and spacing of pregnancies, avoiding adolescent pregnancy, optimizing nutrition and weight, preventing and managing infections, screening for chronic conditions, and encouraging safer lifestyles [7, 8]. The ultimate goal is to reduce prenatal risks, promote equity, and improve both maternal and newborn outcomes [9]. Importantly, WHO recommends that PCC should be provided to all men and women of reproductive age, regardless of their pregnancy intentions [6]. This inclusive approach recognises that many pregnancies are unplanned and that preconception health optimization benefits both prospective mothers and fathers. Furthermore, engaging men in PCC is critical for preventing modifiable risks such as substance use, poor diet, and infectious diseases, which can affect both fertility and pregnancy outcomes [10, 11].

Despite WHO’s inclusive recommendations, the design and delivery of PCC interventions remain largely female-centered. Studies indicate that most programs target women exclusively, with minimal male participation [12, 13]. This gendered bias mirrors broader patterns in reproductive health services where women bear primary responsibility for fertility planning, antenatal care, and child health. Consequently, the potential biological, behavioral, and psychosocial contributions of men are underexplored. Evidence shows that male partners play critical roles in the preconception period. Men contribute biologically to conception through sperm quality and genetic factors, and behaviorally by influencing health practices, financial readiness, and emotional support [14, 15]. In patriarchal societies, men often make key decisions related to pregnancy, birth planning, and healthcare utilization [16, 17]. Ignoring male involvement in PCC therefore limits the effectiveness of interventions, particularly in contexts where gender norms shape reproductive decision-making.

Couple-based preconception care (CB-PCC) is a collaborative approach that involves both partners in preconception health assessment, counseling, and behaviour change. This model promotes shared responsibility, mutual support, and informed decision-making regarding reproductive health and pregnancy planning. The rationale is grounded in the recognition that reproductive outcomes depend on both partners’ health status and behaviors. Couple-based interventions have shown promising results in enhancing fertility awareness, improving lifestyle behaviours, and promoting emotional well-being [18, 19]. Emerging evidence also suggests that joint participation fosters better communication, strengthens partner support, and enhances adherence to recommended health behaviours [20, 21]. When men and women engage together in PCC, the likelihood of sustained behavioural change and improved pregnancy outcomes increases.

Existing reviews demonstrate the potential benefits of PCC but have not adequately addressed couple-based approaches. For instance, Lassi et al. [8] examined PCC interventions targeting infection prevention and revealed that behavioral interventions can significantly reduce new infection rates and improve vaccination coverage. However, the focus remained on individual risk mitigation. Similarly, reviews by Hussein et al. [22] and Brown et al. [23] noted improvements in maternal knowledge and self-efficacy as a result of PCC but focused primarily on women’s outcomes. More recent syntheses such as Lassi et al. [24] explored the impact of maternal nutrition and family planning in LMICs revealing increased contraceptive uptake and enhanced supplementation intake. However, this review similarly targeted women only [24]. Collectively, these studies underscore the value of PCC but fail to examine the joint participation of couples and its impact on reproductive outcomes. The interventions are predominantly designed for women and implemented at the individual level, with minimal involvement of male partners. As a result, the potential benefits of joint participation by couples in PCC remain largely unexplored. Furthermore, heterogeneity exists in intervention design, delivery platforms (e.g., clinic-based, community, or digital), and outcome measures. Very few studies evaluate clinical reproductive outcomes such as conception rates, miscarriage, preterm birth, or congenital anomalies. The evidence base is therefore fragmented, with limited generalizability and insufficient emphasis on gender-responsive approaches.

Given the evidence gaps, a comprehensive synthesis of couple-based preconception care interventions is both timely and necessary. This systematic review aims to consolidate global evidence on the reproductive outcomes of CB-PCC, providing clarity on its effectiveness, contextual determinants, and potential for integration into existing health systems. It also aligns with global health priorities under the Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) particularly SDG 3, which targets reductions in maternal and neonatal mortality (Targets 3.1 and 3.2), and SDG 5 (Gender Equality), which advocates for shared reproductive decision-making (Target 5.6). By systematically reviewing the available evidence, this study seeks to inform policy and practice toward more equitable and gender-inclusive reproductive health frameworks. The synthesis will guide the design of culturally appropriate, couple-oriented PCC programs capable of reducing preventable reproductive losses and promoting shared responsibility for family health.

The purpose of this systematic review is to synthesize global evidence on the reproductive health outcomes of CB-PCC interventions. Specifically, it aims to: (1) identify and describe existing couple-based PCC interventions across diverse populations and settings; (2) assess their effects on reproductive health outcomes including conception, miscarriage, stillbirth, preterm birth, and congenital anomalies; (3) examine how male involvement influences the success of these interventions; and (4) identify methodological and contextual gaps to inform future research, policy, and practice. This review is therefore positioned to contribute to the global evidence base by framing couple-based preconception care as a timely, equity-driven, and transformative approach for improving reproductive health outcomes and achieving the global targets for maternal and newborn health.

Methods

This review followed the guidelines outlined by the Jonna Briggs Institute (JBI) and the principles outlined by Aromataris & Pearson [25] for conducting systematic reviews. The outlined steps include a clearly defined objective and question, details on inclusion and exclusion criteria, providing a comprehensive search strategy, appraisal of the included studies, analysis of extracted data, presentation and synthesis findings and reporting of the approach taken [25]. Furthermore, the reporting of this review was guided by the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines [26]. The protocol for this review was registered in open science framework with an ID: https://doi.org/10.17605/OSF.IO/VZ84W.

Research question

What are the effects of CB-PCC interventions on reproductive health outcomes?

What are the effects of CB-PCC interventions on reproductive health outcomes?

This research question was structured using the Population-Intervention-Comparator-Outcome (PICO) framework. This approach was adopted to ensure that the review focuses on synthesising evidence on the influence of couple-centred PCC interventions on reproductive health outcomes among couples in different geographical contexts globally. Furthermore, in this review, PCC refers to any healthcare strategy that targets biological, emotional and environmental factors to enhance individual and population health before pregnancy. In line with this, CB-PCC refers to PCC interventions that are provided to couples. The criteria for inclusion and exclusion of papers have been illustrated in Table 1 below.

Search strategy

The search for relevant literature for this systematic review was conducted in four main databases which are PubMed Central, Scopus, JSTOR and CINAHL. A librarian was consulted to provide support in formulating the search terms in PubMed Central and modification of search terms to suit other databases. A search strategy that involves the combination of key terms and Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) terms was developed in consultation with a librarian. The search was first conducted in PubMed Central and later modified for search in the other main databases. Additional hand search was conducted in Google Scholar, ProQuest, Google and institutional repositories. Furthermore, the reference list of all the included studies was manually searched to identify other eligible records, and a librarian was also consulted for articles. All these were done to ensure maximum coverage of a broad spectrum of literatures. The keywords that were used for the search included preconception care, PCC interventions, couple-based care, male involvement, counselling, family planning, shared-decision making, health outcomes, and pregnancy. Selection of relevant studies

Screening for eligible records was conducted in three phases. Firstly, all records that were retrieved from all the databases were imported into Mendeley Referencing Software for removal of duplicates. In the second phase, graduate students from the School of Nursing and Midwifery of University of Cape Coast were trained and supervised to screen the remaining records using the titles and abstract. The reference list of all full-text eligible records were further checked to identify additional relevant records. Finally, the identified full-text eligible records were independently screened by two groups of researchers against the eligibility criteria. Discrepancies that arose were resolved in a meeting facilitated by a third reviewer. The search results and screening process were summarised using the PRISMA flow diagram.

Data extraction

An extraction form was developed using Microsoft Word. Additionally, the extraction form was piloted using five randomly selected papers for reliability of the form and the data that was extracted. The form contained key information including name of the authors and year of publication, country, purpose of the study, study design, study population, sample size, CB-PCC intervention and the outcomes of interventions. Two groups of authors extracted the data independently. Discrepancies that arose from the data chart were resolved by a third reviewer in a meeting of all authors.

Critical quality assessment

The Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) critical appraisal tools for quasi-experimental and randomised controlled trial designs were used in the appraisal of eligible records [27]. The scoring procedures adopted for JBI quasi-experimental study was 1–4 (High risk), 5–6 (Moderate risk) and 7–9 (Low risk). The scoring procedures adopted for JBI randomised controlled trial was 1–6 (High risk), 7–10 (Moderate risk) and 11–13 (Low risk). The use of these tools was very important in ensuring the reliability, quality and credibility of the studies were selected for the systematic review. Additionally, they provide insights into a holistic understanding of the quality and risk of bias of the evidence gathered. Furthermore, the use of these appraisal tools ensured that the review process is methodologically sound thereby enhancing the credibility and validity of the findings presented.

Data analysis and synthesis

A thematic analysis was done to examine and interpret patterns across the studies that were included in this review. This began with a repeated review of the extraction form to ensure familiarity with the data extracted and to ensure thorough understanding of the extracted data. While reviewing the table, codes were assigned to reoccurring themes related to the various types of CB-PCC interventions provided and the reported outcomes. Codes that were similar were further grouped into broader themes, thereby capturing contextual similarities. Discussion on these generated themes were then held among research team to ensure clarity, consistency, and relevance to the review objectives. A synthesis on general study characteristics of the included studies was done taking into consideration geographical setting, methodological characteristics (study design, population characteristics, sample size) and types of interventions provided. Additionally, the influence of CB-PCC interventions on reproductive health were converged into broader domains of PCC themes. The synthesis process of this review maintained analytical rigour and transparency, thereby providing comprehensive understanding of the types of CB-PCC interventions provided globally and the associated impact on reproductive health.

Results

Search results

Relevant studies were retrieved through a comprehensive search in PubMed Central, Scopus, Embase, JSTOR and CINAHL. A total of 7,485 records were retrieved through database searching: 7, 215 from PubMed Central, 57 from JSTOR, 17 from CINAHL and 93 from Scopus while 103 additional hand search records were identified through other sources. 57 duplicates were removed using the Mendeley Reference Manager, leaving a total of 7,428 unique records remaining for title and abstract screening. After thorough screening 7,410 records were removed, remaining 18 eligible full records. Four full text eligible records were retrieved from the reference list making a total of 22 full text eligible records. During the full-text eligibility assessment, eight out of the 22 full text eligible records were excluded from the study. The reasons for exclusions included providing no CB-PCC interventions, providing interventions on pregnant women, focusing only on women and intervention being provided postpartum for managing postpartum depression. A total of 14 records met the inclusion criteria and were included in this systematic review. Fig. 1 presents a PRISMA flow diagram of our search strategy.

Study characteristics

Studies that were included in this review have been reported in Supplementary File 1. The interventions described originated from 10 countries worldwide published between 1994 and 2025. Despite this global interest in CB-PCC interventions, eight of the 14 included studies were conducted in high income countries (HICs) [28–35]. A notable concentration of studies was conducted in The Netherlands (n = 5), and one each in Belgium, South Korea, Australia, Egypt, Malawi, Ethiopia, India, Morocco and Bangladesh. This has been presented in Fig. 2 below. Sample sizes ranged from a very small sample of 8 couples to very large samples, such as the mHealth cohort in The Netherlands [33] with 1878 participants and a birth spacing study in Egypt [36] with 1409 women with their husbands. Majority of the studies (n = 10) employed quasi-experimental designs, while four utilised randomised controlled trials (RCTs) [28, 32, 37, 38].

Quality appraisal

A summary of the quality appraisal has been presented in Supplementary File 2. Seven out of the 10 quasi-experimental studies a moderate risk of bias (n = 7). Only three studies [34, 39, 40] were rated low risk of bias, showing a relatively high methodological rigour. A recurrent methodological setback across all the 10 quasi-experimental studies reviewed was the failure to include multiple measurements of the outcome before and after the intervention. Another concern noted was difference existing between groups involved in the experiment [29, 36, 41]. Despite these limitations, studies demonstrated methodological robustness in other key aspects, particularly in establishing temporality, ensuring consistency in measurement, and applying rigorous statistical techniques. This pattern was observed across all studies.

The appraisal of the RCTs generally indicated a higher quality of evidence, although internal validity threats were still present, particularly related to blinding. Two studies [28, 32] were rated as low risk, thus indicating relatively high methodological quality. On the other hand, the remaining two studies [37, 38] were rated as moderate risk. A crucial weakness observed consistently across all four RCTs was the inadequate clarity on the blinding for both the participants and those delivering the treatment, reflecting the challenge of blinding in complex behavioural interventions. Nevertheless, true randomisation was confirmed in all RCTs and allocation concealment ensured in three studies [28, 32, 37]. Additionally, treatment groups were similar at baseline for all RCTs. This baseline equivalence is crucial for reducing selection bias. Moreover, rigorous statistical methods were consistently employed including the use of Generalized Estimating Equations [37], Fine and Gray sub-distribution hazards regression by Boedt et al. [28] and the use of Intention-to-Treat (ITT) analysis [28, 32]. Finally, the RCT designs were considered appropriate for the research questions, with deviations like premature stopping [28] being accounted for in the analysis. It is worth noting that, critical appraisal rely on statements authors have provided in studies reflecting that questions in the appraisal tools. In some cases, absence of these statements in included studies does not mean authors do not undertake the needed action.

Biomedical interventions

Studies provided CB-PCC interventions that targeted biomedical aspects of PCC. These included providing treatments that target nutritional supplementation [32, 34, 35], screening and treatment for chronic diseases [30, 35] and management of Syphilis [40], thus targeting infectious disease surveillance.

Lifestyle modification interventions

Included studies provided PCC to couple with the aim of improving lifestyle habits. These included targeting modifiable risk factors such as diet quality (fruit, vegetable and grain intake) [28, 30, 32–35], physical activity [28, 30, 33, 35], BMI reduction [28, 30–33], and reducing smoking and intake of alcohol [31–35].

Psychosocial interventions

Some of the interventions some included studies provided interventions focused on key psychosocial domains of PCC. These included interventions that focused on improving couples’ mental and emotional health as well as distress related to subfertility [28, 29, 38]. Additionally, some interventions targeted partner communication [29, 37, 39, 40].

Family planning interventions

A number of the studies primarily in developing nations focused on optmising pregnancy spacing. These included interventions such as birth spacing [36] and enhancing attitudes towards contraceptive uptake [37, 39–41].

Mode of delivery of interventions

The CB-PCC interventions included group- and community-based activities [36, 37, 39] as well as personalised approaches designed to address the unique needs of couples [28–35, 38, 40, 41]. Furthermore, it was revealed that while the community-based group interventions were concentrated in LMICs (n = 3), the CB-PCC interventions delivered through tailored and personalised modes were concentrated in HICs (n = 8).

Out of the 14 studies, six studies relied on digital modes of intervention delivery using mobile and web platforms [28–33]. The remaining studies on the other hand utilised non-digital modes such as face-to-face counseling, group classes, or community agents [34–41]. All the fully digital mode of interventions (n = 6) were conducted in HICs whereas the non-digital modes were spread out between LMICs (n = 6) and HICs (n = 2).

Couple integration and male involvement

The interventions provided in the included studies involved different means of ensuring couple-based PCC as well as male involvement. These were primarily joint training and counselling sessions for couples [28–31, 33–39] and collaborative engagement in activities by the couples [28–30, 36, 37, 40]. On the other hand, male partners were targeted with tailored education and counselling interventions [31, 33, 36, 37, 40, 41].

Male commitment to interventions

The acceptance and involvement rate of male partners in the CB-PCC interventions varied across studies. The study by Nizamani et al. [30] reported 100% acceptance of the True Couples-Based Intervention provided. 84% of husbands participated in the interventions by Caleb-Varkey et al. [40]. Similarly, a 79.3% compliance rate was reported among men participants in another study [32]. However, others reported low acceptance rate among male participants. 38% of men did not actively engage in the intervention by Boedt et al. [28], low turnout was reported in the community study by Abdel-Tawab et al. [36] and 24% prevalence of male participation in the Smarter Pregnancy mHealth intervention [31].

The outcomes of CB-PCC interventions encompassed enhancing the physiological prospects for a healthy conception and pregnancy. In line with this, the interventions were linked to improved success of fertility treatment [33, 34]. Beyond fertility treatment, CB-PCC contributed to general health by achieving a reduction in BMI [30], reduced infection risks and enhanced biomarkers [35].

Outcomes of CB-PCC interventions

Many of outcomes were concentrated on improvement in lifestyle habits. Dietary habit improvement was noted across multiple studies [28, 30, 32, 33, 35]. Alongside, interventions showed success in increasing activity [30, 35]. Additionally, studies reported reduction in smoking [32] and alcohol intake [32, 35].

CB-PCC interventions were found to be highly effective in enhancing psychosocial aspects of PCC. The interventions contributed to a reduction in anxiety [29] and infertility stress [29, 38]. Critically, the couple-based approach enhanced relationship quality among couples [29, 40]. Furthermore, studies reported improved shared responsibility among couples [29, 30, 39] and improved self-efficacy towards fertility care [29, 38].

A significant impact of CB-PCC was on service utilisation. By involving men directly, the interventions achieved increased contraceptive uptake [36, 37, 40, 41]. Furthermore, CB-PCC intervention contributed enhanced family planning service utilisation [36, 39] and increased knowledge on family planning [36].

Summary of findings

The findings from this review revealed that CB-PCC interventions have been applied across several continents worldwide with many of these conducted in HICs. It was also found that these interventions encompass both group-based interventions and personalised interventions tailored towards the needs of the couple and tested out with both quasi-experimental and RCT designs. This review further revealed that the various types of couple-focused interventions including counselling, lifestyle modification and biomedical interventions contribute towards positive health outcomes such as improved fertility, enhanced psychosocial health and positive attitude towards service utilisation.

Discussion

Types of CB-PCC interventions

The interventions provided included biomedical, lifestyle modification, psychosocial and family planning aspects. This means that CB-PCC are multifaceted taking into consideration the various health dimensions of the couple. This is in line with global recommendations for PCC. In order to ensure couples, get the best preparation before conception, all aspects of their health encompassing physical, mental, social, emotional and environmental, should be examined and any concerns promptly addressed. In fact, the WHO recommendations for PCC includes taking parents through 13 domains of preconception risk assessment and care, such as nutritional conditions, vaccine-preventable diseases, genetic disorders, environmental health, and mental health [42]. The inclusion of biomedical components within CB-PCC interventions, such as folic acid supplementation and screening for acute and chronic conditions, reflects their established relevance in improving preconception and pregnancy outcomes. The observed emphasis on these elements across the included studies suggests that interventions are aligning with evidence-based preventive strategies known to reduce risks such as neural tube defects and undiagnosed health conditions prior to conception [43]. Integrating routine screening into couple-based approaches may facilitate early identification and management of underlying conditions, which is particularly important in settings where access to continuous care may be limited. In addition, the joint involvement of partners in chronic disease screening and management may enhance adherence to treatment and promote shared responsibility for health behaviours, as supported by evidence on the effectiveness of couple-based approaches in chronic care [44]. This pattern indicates that the biomedical dimension of CB-PCC is not only clinically relevant but also strengthened when delivered through a couple-oriented framework. The addition of these various health dimensions into CB-PCC implies that adverse perinatal outcomes can be curtailed with a well-designed CB-PCC. In effect, it contributes towards the overarching aim of PCC which seeks to enhance the overall health status of individuals and reduce factors that contribute to poor maternal and child health outcomes [42].

The delivery approaches of the CB-PCC interventions identified in this review indicate that preconception care can be offered to couples through diverse strategies, including community-based, personalized, and digital formats. Moreover, the high concentration of the personalised and digital approaches in HICs depicts the disparities in technological infrastructure, resource availability, and healthcare system capacity across countries. This also reflects the provision of person-centered care, a highly recommended approach to effective client care [45]. However, it is worth noting that in LMICs, some contextual factors may contribute to lack of integration of digital and personalised approaches in the CB-PCC interventions identified in this review. Perhaps, the economic and infrastructural constraints, inadequate access to technology, relatively low digital literacy, and overstretched healthcare systems play significant roles in this gap. Nevertheless, the findings indicate CB-PCC can be provided regardless of these contextual challenges in some jurisdictions since it can be done utilising several adaptable approaches.

Pertaining to structuring PCC to focus on couple and involve male partners, this review indicated it is feasible across different settings. The included studies targeted couples and males using group-based approached and tailored modes. Additionally, the high commitment of males [30, 32, 40] to interventions noticed in some studies indicate that men are willing to participate when interventions are inclusive, culturally appropriate, and relevant to their roles in reproductive health. Evidence suggests that male participation in reproductive health is often deterred by the unwelcoming environment of clinics, such as limited male-focused services and female-oriented environments, as well as the social stigma associated with entering spaces traditionally reserved for women [46]. In view of that, the existing problem of low inclusion of males in PCC could be a result of systemic gaps in program design, limited provider training on male engagement, sociocultural norms that frame reproductive health as a woman’s domain and inadequate policy emphasis on couple-based approaches.

Health outcomes of CB-PCC interventions

This review revealed that CB-PCC interventions are effective in promoting positive health outcomes across four domains, namely reproductive and clinical efficiency, lifestyle improvement, psychosocial wellbeing, and service utilisation. PCC contributes to enhanced reproductive outcomes and improved general health status among individuals. Studies have reported the impact of PCC to include optimised pregnancy and enhanced health and lifestyle behaviours [13]. The CB-PCC interventions in this review contributed to these health outcomes too. Thus, treating couples as a unit and providing them with tailored PCC facilitates joint decision-making, mutual accountability, and coordinated adherence to health recommendations. The consistent improvements in dietary habits including fruit and vegetable intake, folic acid supplementation, physical activity, and reductions in smoking and alcohol intake suggest that health behaviour changes may be sustainable when grounded in relational support. The couple-based approach further affirms that health journeys, particularly on PCC may shaped by shared goals and collective motivation.

Furthermore, the CB-PCC resulted in enhanced psychosocial health of couples. The psychosocial outcomes of CB-PCC interventions reveal the profound emotional and relational dimensions of preparing for parenthood. Individuals preparing for conception include those with subfertility who may be on treatment, with health challenges and couples expecting their first child. These situations may put the couple under significant challenges such as stress, depression and anxiety [47]. Moreover, evidence suggests that the impact of infertility encompass psychological well-being, marital relationships, sexual relationships, and quality of life [48, 49]. The situation could be worse for couples who may find themselves in societies where childbirth is considered an important social expectation [50]. The review findings therefore depict that CB-PCC interventions could help couples navigate the emotional challenges of conception and pregnancy, fostering a supportive environment that promotes both individual and relational well-being.

This review also revealed that CB-PCC contributes towards enhanced utilisation of PCC services particularly the use of contraceptives. Men contribute significantly towards reproductive decision-making and the allocation of resources within households [51]. In view of that, the decisions male partners make during the preconception period could influence the collective attitude of the couple towards PCC service utilisation. The studies in this review that reported on service utilisation were all based in LMICs. Evidence from such societies suggest involving males in family planning have significant impact on service utilisation [52]. Males make key decisions on contraceptive use, timing of pregnancy, and engagement with healthcare services. This review therefore indicates that PCC interventions should be designed to care for couples as a unit while also taking into consideration the needs, perspectives, and influence of men.

Implication for policy, practice and research

Policy

CB-PCC may be considered for integration as a gender-responsive component of reproductive health policy, particularly in settings seeking to strengthen shared decision-making and male involvement. This is because it may help correct the long-standing structural deficit where responsibility for fertility and pregnancy solely left to women. Aligning CB-PCC with existing reproductive health and family planning programmes may enhance service utilisation and support integrated care delivery. Government agencies and stakeholders may consider collaborative approaches to support implementation and evaluation of CB-PCC interventions. Without policy redesign and dedicated funding, services will continue to under-serve men, leaving avoidable risks unaddressed before conception. However, the strength of this recommendation is moderated by the predominance of quasi-experimental designs and the limited number of studies conducted in low- and middle-income settings, which may affect generalisability. The variability in male participation observed across studies further suggests that engagement is context-dependent and may require contextually appropriate strategies to improve uptake among men.

Practice

Furthermore, effective CB-PCC delivery requires a diversified service model that spans clinic-based, community-based, and digital platforms. This multi-platform approach ensures that CB-PCC reaches diverse couple populations across varying levels of healthcare access, thereby maximising intervention coverage and uptake. Community health workers may convene small group dialogues for men and couples, normalising male engagement and addressing barriers such as stigma and work schedules. Providers may require competency-based training on gender norms, motivational interviewing, and couple communication, supported by simple checklists and referral pathways. Facilities may reconfigure waiting areas and records to allow joint counselling without compromising confidentiality. Implementation may benefit from iterative refinement which involve using routine data to identify drop-off points, adapt messaging, and targeting under-represented groups such as young men and low-income couples. The findings also demonstrate that CB-PCC interventions are multidimensional, addressing biomedical, behavioural, psychosocial, and family planning needs simultaneously, which may enhance their overall effectiveness. The feasibility and effectiveness of these strategies are likely to vary across contexts due to differences in health system capacity, cultural norms, and resource availability. Also, the implementation of CB-PCC approaches may face practical constraints, including workforce limitations, competing service priorities, and variability in male participation rates observed across studies.

Research

Given the methodological variability and limited comparability of outcomes across studies, there remains a need for more standardised and high-quality evidence to inform decision-making. Future research should focus on generating more robust and context-specific evidence, particularly in low- and middle-income settings where evidence remains limited. There is a need for multi-site studies and standardised outcome measures to improve comparability across studies. In addition, mixed-methods approaches may help to better understand how and why CB-PCC interventions work across different contexts.

Strengths and limitations

While the findings of this review indicate promising benefits of couple-based preconception care, these implications should be interpreted with caution given the heterogeneity of study designs, intervention approaches, and outcome measures across the included studies.

This review employed a wide and systematic search strategy spanning multiple electronic databases and grey literature sources, reducing the likelihood of missing relevant evidence. The structured review process, including duplicate screening, data extraction by trained reviewers, and adjudicated disagreements, enhances the reliability and rigour of the findings. These methodological safeguards strengthen confidence in the conclusions drawn.

Notwithstanding these strengths, several limitations warrant caution when interpreting the findings and conclusions drawn from this review. Although authors searched multiple databases and grey sources, restricting to English-language publications introduces language bias, and relevant evidence from non-English LMIC settings may have been missed. The 16 included studies were very different in what they did, how they were delivered (community, clinic, or digital), how strong the programmes were, what they were compared with, and how outcomes were measured. Because of this variation, authors could not pool results, and it is hard to draw firm causal conclusions or apply findings everywhere. Also, the evidence base is skewed towards high-income contexts (notably five studies from the Netherlands and all six fully digital interventions), so external validity to resource-constrained health systems is uncertain. The applicability of these findings to low-resource settings remains uncertain, as most included studies were conducted in HICs with stronger health system infrastructure.

Furthermore, most designs were quasi-experimental (10/14) with frequent absence of independent control groups, limited pre/post measurements, and, among RCTs, challenges with blinding. Besides, many outcomes relied on self-report, raising recall and social desirability bias. Meanwhile, incomplete reporting in primary studies impeded appraisal (e.g., intervention dose, adherence, contamination, and equity effects), and our reliance on reported statements risks misclassification in quality ratings. Finally, although screening and extraction used trained teams with adjudication, errors remain possible, and we could not rule out publication bias or duplicate populations across overlapping programme evaluations. Regardless of these challenges, authors did wide and careful search, using many databases and grey sources to find relevant studies. Another strength is the clear and structured review process, with quality checks and more than one reviewer to make the findings more reliable.

Conclusion

This review shows that couple-based preconception care can support better shared decision-making, healthier lifestyles, and stronger partner involvement before pregnancy. These findings highlight its value as a practical and inclusive approach to reproductive health, though results may not apply to all settings. A key limitation is that most studies came from HICs and used different designs and measures, making it hard to generalise. Even so, the evidence suggests that involving both partners is possible and may help improve participation and health behaviours. Future programmes should adapt couple-based care to local cultures, build male-friendly services, and test them more widely in LMICs. More high-quality studies are needed to explore long-term effects, cost, and how best to integrate such care into existing health systems.
