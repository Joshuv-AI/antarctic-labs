EZH2 as regulator of stemness signature and driver of esophageal squamous cell carcinomas
Authors: Fatemeh Nourmohammadi, Maryam M. Matin, Ahmad Reza Bahrami, Bingwei Lu, Vajiheh Zarrinpour, Mohammad Mahdi Forghanifard
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Esophageal squamous cell carcinoma (ESCC) is the sixth leading cause of cancer-related death globally. Current therapies remain inadequate, largely due to the persistence of stem-like residual tumor cells. The role of EZH2 as a cancer stem cell (CSC) regulator in tumor development has not been fully elucidated. We investigated EZH2 expression and its association with CSC markers in ESCC patients using bioinformatics analyses and gene expression assays. Functional analyses were used to assess the role of EZH2 in self-renewal, proliferation, migration, invasion, and chemotherapy resistance. Xenograft models were used to evaluate EZH2 impact on tumor growth, pulmonary metastasis, and angiogenesis in vivo. Additional techniques, including apoptosis assays, qRT-PCR, ICC, and IHC, were used to explore the mechanisms underlying EZH2 effects on stemness properties. EZH2 enhanced stemness and self-renewal in ESCC by upregulating stemness‑associated markers, resulting in increased colony and sphere formation in vitro. It promoted ESCC proliferation by facilitating the G1-to-S transition and upregulating CD markers involved in CSC maintenance. EZH2 also reduced apoptosis and increased cell viability. Additionally, EZH2 increased resistance to 5-fluorouracil and Cisplatin. In vivo studies demonstrated that EZH2 overexpression accelerated tumor growth, metastasis, and angiogenesis. Targeting EZH2 with specific inhibitors significantly suppressed tumor progression and improved survival in ESCC xenograft models. Our molecular docking analysis suggests a PRC2-independent (non-canonical) potential of EZH2 association with stemness regulators (NANOG, OCT4, and SOX2). This findings suggest EZH2 as a potential target for developing novel neoadjuvant therapy strategies in ESCC.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-55157-w.

Introduction

Esophageal squamous cell carcinoma (ESCC) is a highly aggressive malignancy characterized by poor prognosis and a five-year survival rate of approximately 25%. It ranks as the sixth leading cause of cancer-related deaths worldwide and the eighth most common cancer globally1,2. Despite advances in cancer research, the genetic and molecular mechanisms underlying ESCC pathogenesis remain largely unclear3–5. Major clinical challenges in ESCC patients include late diagnosis at advanced stages coupled with metastasis and chemotherapeutic resistance6,7. Notably, 40%−60% of patients with advanced ESCC exhibit limited or no response to chemotherapy, and approximately 15%−30% present with metastases at the time of initial diagnosis8–10.

Although current therapies, including chemotherapy, radiotherapy, and surgery effectively reduce tumor mass, recurrence remains a major challenge, largely due to a subpopulation of residual tumor cells with stem-like properties. Cancer stem cells (CSCs) in ESCC drive tumor initiation, progression, and heterogeneity, exhibiting capacities for self-renewal and multi-lineage differentiation similar to normal stem and progenitor cells. These cells are increasingly recognized as key mediators of treatment resistance and metastatic spread11–13.

EZH2 (Enhancer of Zeste Homolog 2) functions as both a transcriptional activator and repressor and plays a critical role in the regulation of transcriptional, post-transcriptional, and translational modifications14–17. EZH2 acts as the catalytic methyltransferase subunit of Polycomb Repressive Complex 2 (PRC2), targeting H3K27 and H3K9 in SET domain16. Numerous studies have highlighted the pivotal role of EZH2 in chromatin remodeling and epigenetic regulation18,19. The non-canonical role of EZH2 as a transcription factor has recently gained increasing attention. A recent study employing transcriptomics analysis and protein–protein interaction (PPI) network analysis identified EZH2 as one of the key transcription factors significantly upregulated in ESCC, highlighting its potential role in tumor microenvironment remodeling20. Nevertheless, studies investigating the association of EZH2 with stemness and pluripotency in ESCC remain relatively limited.

In this study, we identified EZH2 as a potential master regulator associated with the expression of multiple cancer stem cell markers in ESCC. Our in vitro and in vivo experiments demonstrated that therapeutic targeting of EZH2 suppresses CSC-associated characteristics, potentially reducing tumor recurrence and chemoresistance. These findings suggest a novel therapeutic approach for ESCC through targeting EZH2 as a regulator of esophageal cancer stem cells.

Materials and methods

Data acquisition and bioinformatics

The RNA sequencing data analyzed in this study were obtained from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) database (https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov/). The TCGA-HNSC project was included in the analysis. Tumor transcriptomic data from patients with less than five years of survival were extracted for subsequent analyses. Bioinformatics analyses were performed using RStudio to evaluate the potential impact of EZH2 on esophageal cancer. Molecular docking analyses were conducted to investigate potential interactions between EZH2 and CpG island–rich promoter regions of key stemness‑related genes (NANOG, OCT4, and SOX2). Relevant promoter sequences were identified based on NCBI annotations, and three‑dimensional (3D) DNA models were generated using the 3D‑DART web server. The 3D structure of human EZH2 was obtained from the Protein Data Bank. Docking simulations were performed using HEX 8.0 software, total interaction energies (E_total), and stability metrics were calculated.

Patient samples and ethical approval

Tissue samples were collected from 33 patients diagnosed with ESCC who underwent esophagectomy at two affiliated hospitals of Mashhad University of Medical Sciences (MUMS), Mashhad, Iran. None of the patients had received chemotherapy or radiotherapy prior to surgery, and all tumor samples were confirmed by microscopic examination to contain at least 70% tumor cells. Written informed consent was obtained from all patients for participation in the study and for publication of the results. All procedures involving human samples were conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the relevant institutional ethics committee.

Cell culture and genetic manipulation

The KYSE‑30 cell line was obtained from the Pasteur Institute Cell Bank of Iran (http://en.pasteur.ac.ir), and the YM‑1 cell line was kindly provided by the Department of Molecular Medicine, Faculty of Advanced Medical Technologies, Golestan University of Medical Sciences, Gorgan, Iran. KYSE‑30 cells were cultured in RPMI 1640 medium (Gibco), while YM‑1 cells were cultured in DMEM (Gibco), both supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin. Cells were maintained at 37 °C in a humidified incubator containing 5% CO₂. All cell lines were routinely authenticated and tested for mycoplasma contamination prior to experimental use. To generate EZH2‑silenced ESCC cell lines (EZH2‑si), KYSE‑30 and YM‑1 cells were transfected with EZH2‑specific short hairpin RNA (shRNA) using the RNAi‑Ready pSIREN‑RetroQ vector (kindly provided by Dr. Moein Farshchian, Ferdowsi University of Mashhad, Iran). EZH2 overexpression (EZH2-oe) was induced using a human EZH2 ORF mammalian expression plasmid (C‑HA tagged; Sino Biological Inc., catalog no. HG11337‑CY). Transfected cells were expanded, and the efficiency of EZH2 knockdown or overexpression was confirmed by quantitative Reverse Transcription-PCR (qRT‑PCR).

Molecular and functional assays

Transcript level analysis

Total RNA was extracted from ESCC patient tissues and KYSE‑30 and YM‑1 cells using the Total RNA Extraction Kit (Qiagen, USA; cat. no. 74536). Residual genomic DNA was removed using DNase I (Thermo Fisher Scientific). RNA quality and concentration were evaluated spectrophotometrically prior to cDNA synthesis, performed using the RevertAid First Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific; cat. no. K1622). RT‑qPCR was performed using SYBR Green Master Mix (Ampliqon, Denmark) on a LightCycler® 96 Real‑Time PCR System (Roche, Germany). Relative gene expression levels were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method, with GAPDH as the internal control. Primer sequences are listed in Supplementary Table S9. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism version 8.0.1 (GraphPad Software, La Jolla, CA, USA). Differences between groups were analyzed using one‑way ANOVA or paired Student’s t‑test as appropriate, with p < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Western blot analysis

Proteins were separated by 10% Sodium Dodecyl Sulfate–Polyacrylamide Gel Electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) and transferred to polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes (Millipore). Membranes were blocked with 5% skim milk for 1 h at room temperature. The membranes were incubated with primary antibodies against β‑ACTIN (ab8227, Abcam) and EZH2 (D2C9 XP® Rabbit mAb #5246, Cell Signaling Technology) at the manufacturer‑recommended dilutions.

After washing with Tris-Buffered Saline containing Tween-20 (TBST), membranes were incubated with Horseradish Peroxidase (HRP)‑conjugated secondary antibodies (Thermo Fisher Scientific; catalog no. A27036) for 1 h at room temperature. Protein bands were visualized using enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL; PerkinElmer, Waltham, USA). Band intensities were quantified using ImageJ software.

Sphere formation assay

Sphere formation was assessed by culturing single‑cell suspensions at a density of 1 × 10³ cells/mL in ultra‑low attachment 6‑well plates (Corning) in serum‑free DMEM/F12 medium supplemented with B27 (Gibco), Epidermal Growth Factor (EGF) (20 ng/mL), basic Fibroblast Growth Factor (bFGF) (20 ng/mL), and 1% penicillin–streptomycin. Cells were incubated at 37 °C for 4–7 days. Formed spheres were photographed using an inverted microscope (OPTIKA, Italy). After 7 days, spheres were collected and counted, and both sphere number and diameter were quantified using ImageJ software. All experiments were performed in triplicate.

Immunocytochemistry (ICC)

Immunocytochemistry was performed on cytology samples fixed in an immunocytochemistry fixing solution. Cell smears were prepared using CelltraZone Co., Ltd. (HURO PATH® system), Korea. The ICC reaction was carried out using the Bond‑Max automated immunostainer according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Leica Microsystems, Wetzlar, Germany). Slides were treated with a peroxidase blocking solution and incubated for 30 min at room temperature with the following monoclonal antibodies: CD117 (Master Diagnostica; rabbit anti‑human CD117/c‑Kit monoclonal antibody, clone EP10), SALL4 (Master Diagnostica; mouse anti‑human SALL4 monoclonal antibody, clone EE‑30), CK19 (Medaysis; mouse anti‑cytokeratin 19, clone BA17), and CDX2 (Abcam; ab227201). Chromogenic detection was performed using Novolink™ DAB (Polymer; Leica Biosystems) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. ICC staining intensity was scored as follows: 0 = no staining, 1 = slight staining, 2 = moderate staining, 3 = intense staining, and 4 = strong staining. The percentage of positively stained cancer cells was quantified using ImageJ software.

Colony formation assay

Transfected KYSE‑30 and YM‑1 cells were seeded at a density of 2 × 10³ cells in 60‑mm culture dishes (Merck, Darmstadt, Germany) and cultured for 14 days. Colonies were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 20 min and stained with crystal violet. Plates were photographed, and the number of visible colonies was quantified using ImageJ software.

Flow cytometry analysis

Cells were harvested using trypsin-ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (trypsin–EDTA) (Gibco) and washed with phosphate‑buffered saline (PBS). Approximately 1 × 10⁵ cells were resuspended in 200 µL PBS containing 1% FBS and incubated for 20 min at 4 °C in the dark with fluorochrome‑conjugated antibodies against CD44, CD133, and CD117. Each sample was stained with a single antibody. Following washing steps with PBS, samples were analyzed using a BD Accuri C6 flow cytometer (BD Biosciences, USA). Unstained cells were used as controls to determine baseline autofluorescence. Data were analyzed using FlowJo software version 7.6. The following antibodies were used: APC‑conjugated anti‑CD44 (559942, BD Biosciences), PE‑conjugated anti‑CD133 (Miltenyi Biotec, Cat. no. 130‑113‑670), and PE‑conjugated anti‑CD117 (Miltenyi Biotec, Cat. no. 130‑113‑544).

Cell-cycle assay

Forty‑eight hours after transfection, cells were harvested, washed twice with PBS, and fixed in 70% cold ethanol overnight at 4 °C. Fixed cells were centrifuged, washed, and stained using the Cell-Cycle Analysis Kit (Sigma‑Aldrich, Cat. no. MAK344) for 30 min in the dark at 4 °C. DNA content was analyzed using a FACScan flow cytometer (BD Biosciences, USA).

Transwell assay

Cell migration and invasion were evaluated using Transwell chambers (24‑well plates, Corning, USA). KYSE‑30 and YM‑1 cells were collected 48 h after transfection and resuspended in serum‑free RPMI 1640 or DMEM at a density of 2 × 10⁵ cells/mL. Cells were seeded into the upper chambers. For invasion assays, inserts were pre‑coated with Matrigel (Corning), whereas uncoated inserts were used for migration assay. The lower chambers were filled with 600 µL medium containing 10% FBS as a chemoattractant. After 24 h incubation at 37 °C, non‑migrated or non‑invaded cells on the upper surface were removed with a cotton swab. Cells that migrated to the lower surface were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and stained with 0.5% crystal violet. Stained cells were photographed under a light microscope and quantified using ImageJ software. All experiments were performed in triplicate.

Viability assay

KYSE-30 and YM-1 cell viabilities were assessed using the MTT assay (Sigma-Aldrich, Cat. no. M5655-1 g). Briefly, 48 h post-transfection, cells were seeded at a density of 1 × 10⁴ cells/well in 96-well plates. After 24 h of incubation, cells were treated with 5-fluorouracil (5-FU), Cisplatin, or their combination at the indicated concentrations. Following an additional 24 h incubation, the treatment medium was removed, and 10 µL of MTT solution was added to each well. Plates were incubated at 37 °C for 4 h to allow formazan crystal formation. Subsequently, Dimethyl Sulfoxide (DMSO) was added to dissolve the crystals, and the absorbance was measured at 570 nm using an ELISA microplate reader (ELx808, BioTek, USA). The assay was repeated for 48-hours treatment under the same conditions, and all experiments were performed in triplicate. Dose–response curves were generated from three independent biological replicates, each measured in technical triplicate. IC50 values were calculated using non-linear regression analysis based on a four-parameter logistic (4PL) dose–response model (log [inhibitor] versus normalized response) in GraphPad Prism. The top and bottom constraints were left unconstrained during curve fitting, and IC50 values were estimated with 95% confidence intervals. For statistical comparison, IC50 values were log-transformed to meet assumptions of normality and analyzed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s multiple comparison post-hoc test.

Apoptosis assay

Apoptosis was evaluated using the fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC)-Annexin V/PI Apoptosis Detection Kit (BD Biosciences) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Forty‑eight hours after transfection, 1 × 10⁵ cells were collected, washed, and resuspended in 500 µL binding buffer. Cells were incubated with 5 µL FITC-Annexin V/PI for 10 min at room temperature in the dark. Apoptotic cells were analyzed using a FACScan flow cytometer (BD Biosciences), and the percentage of apoptotic cells was quantified.

EZH2 engineered xenograft model

All xenograft experiments and methods were conducted in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations by Ethics Committee of the Da.C., Islamic Azad University. The study is reported in accordance with the ARRIVE guidelines (https://arriveguidelines.org). Male C57BL/6 nude mice (four weeks old, average weight 18–22 g) were obtained from the Pasteur Institute of Iran and housed under specific pathogen-free conditions, with three to five mice per cage in a double-blind manner (data collection and analysis). Animals had continuous access to food and water, with environmental conditions maintained at 21 ± 0.5 °C, 60 ± 10% relative humidity, and a 12 h light/dark cycle. For xenograft formation, 1 × 10⁶ KYSE-30 or YM-1 cells transfected with EZH2-si, EZH2-oe, or control vectors were resuspended in 100 µL PBS and subcutaneously injected into the dorsal flank of each mouse. Sample size (n = 5) was determined based on a priori power analysis to ensure sufficient statistical power (G Power 3.1 software). General health of the animals was monitored daily. Tumor dimensions were measured twice weekly, and tumor volume was calculated using the formula: volume = length × (width) ² × 0.5. Humane endpoints were predefined before study initiation and included tumor ulceration or necrosis, impaired mobility, and signs of severe pain or distress. In addition, the maximum tumor diameter did not exceed 15 mm, and the minimum body weight of animals did not decrease below 14 g. Exclusion criteria included: unsuccessful cell injection, failure of tumor engraftment, or unexpected illness unrelated to tumor burden. Five weeks post-injection, mice were euthanized using intraperitoneal ketamine (80 mg/kg)/xylazine (10 mg/kg) before tumor cell collection. Tumors were harvested and divided: one portion was fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for immunohistochemistry (IHC) and hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining, while the remaining tissue was preserved in RNAlater solution (Invitrogen) and stored at − 80 °C for Western blot and RT-qPCR analyses. Furthermore, EZH2-oe, EZH2-si and control KYSE-30 and YM-1 cells (2 × 105/100 µL) were injected into the mice via the tail vein (n = 3 per group) for metastasis examination. After five weeks, the mice were sacrificed and lungs were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for H&E staining to analyze lung metastatic lesions. Quantitative data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Tumor volumes and all experimental measurements were analyzed using GraphPad Prism (version 8.0.1). Statistical significance for differences between two groups was assessed using unpaired Student’s t-test. Comparisons among three or more groups were performed by one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post-hoc test. For all analyses, a p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Hematoxylin and eosin staining

Tissue sections were processed using standard histological procedures. Briefly, sections were stained with hematoxylin solution (Gill III, Prosan) for 4 min, differentiated in 1% hydrochloric acid (HCl) in ethanol, and neutralized with lithium carbonate (Li₂CO₃) solution. Slides were then counterstained with eosin for 3 min, dehydrated through graded alcohols, cleared in xylene, and mounted with a distyrene–plasticizer–xylene (DPX) mounting medium. Stained sections were examined and imaged using a light microscope (OPTIKA, Italy).

Immunohistochemistry

Immunohistochemistry was performed on tumor tissues fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde and embedded in paraffin. Tissue sections (4 μm thick) were deparaffinized in xylene and rehydrated through graded ethanol solutions. Endogenous peroxidase activity was blocked using 0.3% hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂) in methanol for 15 min. Sodium citrate buffer (10 mM, pH 6.0) was used for epitope recovery (95 °C, 30 min). Subsequently, the slides were chilled to room temperature, and nonspecific binding was blocked in blocking buffer (2% bovine serum albumin (BSA), 1% skim milk, 0.1% Tween-20, and 10% goat serum) for 1 h and incubated with primary antibodies against SALL4 (mouse anti-human SALL4 monoclonal antibody, clone EE 30; Master Diagnostica) and CK19 (mouse anti cytokeratin 19, clone BA17; Medaysis). After rinsing with PBS, the sections were incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. Visualization was performed using, 3′ diaminobenzidine (DAB) chromogen (Novolink™ DAB Polymer). Slides were counterstained with hematoxylin, dehydrated, cleared, and mounted with DPX. An expert pathologist graded the IHC results of CK19 and SALL4 semi-quantitatively by integrating intensity and distribution. Staining intensity was scored as (negative = 0; mild = 1; moderate = 2; brown = 3; intense brown = 4) and percentage of positive tumor cells (0–100%). Each sample was independently assessed by a pathologist.

Results

EZH2 exhibited increased expression levels in esophageal tumors

To assess EZH2 expression in esophageal cancer patients, we analyzed transcriptomic data from 186 tumor samples in TCGA. EZH2 expression was strongly correlated with tumor grade, clinical stage, and metastasis (Fig. 1a-d). In silico analysis further revealed that EZH2 and a wide range of CSC markers were upregulated in patients diagnosed with esophageal cancer (Fig. 1e). Furthermore, in a separate cohort of 33 patients (male/female: 17/16; age range: 30–80 years), 22 cases exhibited EZH2 overexpression following ESCC diagnosis. However, comprehensive correlation analysis between EZH2 expression and clinicopathological characteristics did not reveal any statistically significant associations (Supplementary Table S1).

EZH2 modulated cancer stemness by regulating key stem cell markers in ESCC

We next investigated the relationship between EZH2 and CSC marker expression in ESCC cell lines through functional approaches involving both EZH2 knockdown and overexpression. Interactive analyses of EZH2 and CSC marker expression were conducted using several open-source bioinformatics platforms (Fig. 1f; Supplementary Fig. S1a, S1b; Tables S2 and S3). GeneMANIA analysis revealed significant protein-protein interaction networks between EZH2 and a panel of CSC markers in esophageal cancer, including SOX2, BMI1, KLF4, SALL4, AKT1, OCT4, c-MYC, and NANOG. Gene Ontology and DAVID enrichment analyses further supported strong cellular and molecular associations linking EZH2 to key CSC-related biological processes (Supplementary Table S2).

To explore potential regulatory interactions, molecular docking analysis was performed between EZH2 and CpG island–rich promoter regions of stemness genes. The analysis suggested a hypothesis-generating binding affinity of EZH2 to CpG island–rich promoter regions of NANOG (–581.55 kcal/mol) and OCT4 (–489.35 kcal/mol) with high interaction energies. Similarly, two CpG clusters within the SOX2 promoter region demonstrated predicted interaction energies of −343.11 and −361.62 kcal/mol (Table S4). These findings hypothesized a potential association between EZH2 and stemness-associated gene promoters. This observation is consistent with patient-derived transcriptomic data indicating concomitant EZH2 upregulation and CSC-related transcriptional modulation (Fig. 1e).

To validate these findings, EZH2 expression was experimentally manipulated in KYSE-30 and YM-1 ESCC cell lines, and CSC markers expression was subsequently assessed. Following EZH2 overexpression in ESCC cells, a notable upregulation of several CSC markers, including OCT4, HES1, CK19, c-MYC, KLF4, NANOG, SALL4, BMI1, ERBB3, ICAM, and CD44, was observed. In contrast, EZH2 downregulation significantly suppressed the expression of these markers (Fig. 2a-c; Supplementary Fig. S1b). Moreover, the expression levels of TIM3, SNAIL, SLUG, TAZ, and HIWI remained largely unaffected by EZH2 modulation.

EZH2 promoted CSC self-renewal and tumorigenesis in ESCC cells

To evaluate the impact of EZH2 on CSC self-renewal and proliferation capacity, sphere and colony formation assays were performed in EZH2-manipulated ESCC cells. EZH2-oe cells exhibited early sphere formation (day 2) and generated larger and more numerous spheres, whereas EZH2-si cells demonstrated markedly reduced sphere size and number, accompanied by decreased expression of CSC-associated markers. Specifically, EZH2-oe YM-1 cells formed larger spheres, while EZH2-oe KYSE-30 cells indicated a higher frequency of sphere formation (Fig. 3a-d). Immunocytochemistry analysis further confirmed increased nuclear and cytoplasmic expression of CSC markers in EZH2-oe spheres, whereas markedly reduced staining intensity was observed in EZH2-si cells (Fig. 3e; Supplementary Fig. S2). These findings were consistent with qRT-PCR results and supported a correlation between EZH2 expression and the expression levels of specific CSC markers, including SALL4, CD117, and CK19 (Fig. 2).

To further evaluate tumorigenic potential, colony-formation assays were performed. EZH2-oe cells formed significantly larger and more numerous colonies in both KYSE-30 and YM-1 cells, whereas EZH2-si cells exhibited a marked reduction in both colony number and size (Fig. 3f, g; Supplementary Fig. S2). Flow cytometric analysis of isolated colonies revealed elevated expression of CSC surface markers, including CD44, CD133, and CD117, in EZH2-oe cells compared with control cells (Fig. 3h; Supplementary Fig. S2). Collectively, these findings support a functional role for EZH2 in promoting CSC self-renewal and tumorigenic potential in KYSE-30 and YM-1 cells.

EZH2 inhibition arrested ESCC cells in G1/S transition

Cell-cycle regulation is another critical role of EZH2 in ESCC proliferation. Analysis of cell-cycle dynamics confirmed previous reports demonstrating that EZH2 overexpression increases the proportion of cells in the S-phase, whereas EZH2 knockdown induces G1/S arrest and reduces S-phase entry, correlating with decreased invasion and proliferative capacity. Integration of cell-cycle assay results with gene expression profiling data further demonstrated that EZH2 silencing impeded S-phase progression and inhibited KYSE-30 and YM-1 cell growth. In contrast, EZH2 overexpression promoted S-phase accumulation and shortened the G1-phase. Importantly, both ESCC cell lines exhibited a consistent pattern of cell-cycle regulation in response to EZH2 modulation (Fig. 4a, b).

EZH2 overexpression enhanced the metastasis of ESCC cells

Metastasis and invasion are primary causes of high mortality rates in ESCC21. We investigated the impact of EZH2 on ESCC cell migration and invasion. EZH2 promoted activation of the epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT) pathway, characterized by decreased expression of epithelial markers and increased expression of mesenchymal markers, which collectively enhance cell motility22. Transwell assay demonstrated that the migration rate of EZH2-oe YM-1 cells toward the lower chamber was approximately 40% higher than that of EZH2-si cells, confirming the enhanced migratory capacity associated with EZH2 overexpression. Conversely, EZH2-si cells exhibited a markedly reduced migration compared to control cells. Consistently, EZH2 silencing significantly suppressed invasion, whereas EZH2 overexpression increased the invasive potential of YM-1 cells by approximately 50% (Fig. 4c-e).

The enhanced migration and invasion were accompanied by upregulation of key metastasis-associated markers, including ALDH1, HES-1, and CD44 (all showing approximately> 5-fold upregulation by qRT-PCR), as well as additional genes associated with the EMT pathway. These findings indicate a positive correlation between EZH2 expression and the migratory and invasive behaviors of KYSE-30 and YM-1 cells, suggesting a potential role for EZH2 in promoting metastatic progression in esophageal cancer.

EZH2 overexpression induced resistance to 5-FU and Cisplatin in ESCC cells

Chemotherapy resistance is a major challenge in the treatment of patients with esophageal cancer23. To investigate the association between EZH2 and cellular responses to chemotherapeutic agents, EZH2-manipulated cells were treated with different concentrations of 5-FU and Cisplatin, both individually and in combination. EZH2 silencing considerably increased cellular susceptibility to the cytotoxic effects of both 5-FU and Cisplatin in KYSE-30 and YM-1 cells compared with control cells. At equitoxic concentrations (determined by IC50 values), Cisplatin exhibited a stronger inhibitory effect than 5-FU in both ESCC cell lines. Notably, combined treatment with 5-FU and Cisplatin produced a synergistic anti-proliferative and anti-self-renewal effect, exceeding the efficacy observed with 5-FU or Cisplatin alone (Fig. 5b and Supplementary Fig. S5). In addition, EZH2 overexpression was associated with elevated proliferative capacity and reduced chemotherapy sensitivity. Monitoring ESCC cell proliferation over a 48 h period further revealed significantly higher proliferation rates in EZH2-oe cells compared with control cells, whereas EZH2-si cells proliferated more slowly (Fig. 5a).

EZH2 induction enhanced CSC maintenance by reducing apoptosis

The precise mechanism underlying EZH2-mediated inhibition of apoptosis remains largely elusive. To clarify this relationship, apoptosis was assessed using the FITC-Annexin V/PI staining assay to determine the impact of EZH2 expression on apoptotic responses. The results demonstrated increased survival of apoptosis-resistant cells following EZH2 upregulation. Conversely, EZH2 downregulation significantly increased the proportion of early apoptotic KYSE-30 and YM-1 cells compared to control groups (Fig. 5c; Supplementary Fig. S4). These findings indicate that EZH2 induction suppressed the apoptotic responses in KYSE-30 and YM-1 cells, consistent with the enhanced apoptosis resistance typically observed in cancer stem-like cells.

EZH2 overexpression enhanced esophageal tumorigenesis in a xenograft tumor model

To investigate the impact of EZH2 in ESCC tumorigenesis, metastasis, and invasion in vivo, we employed an EZH2-engineered xenograft tumor model. Tumor monitoring revealed that EZH2 silencing significantly suppressed ESCC tumor development (Fig. 6b). Conversely, EZH2 overexpression accelerated tumor progression and was associated with notable body-weight loss in tumor-transplanted mice (Fig. 6a-c; Supplementary Table S5). Histopathological analysis further demonstrated higher tumor grade in EZH2-oe tumors, characterized by increased mitotic activity, severe dysplasia, and cellular atypia (Fig. 6d). In contrast, EZH2-silenced tumors exhibited minimal mitotic activity and anaplasia, reflecting a less malignant nature and lower histopathological tumor grade (Fig. 6d; Supplementary Table S6).

Immunohistochemistry (IHC) analyses demonstrated that the malignancy-associated CSC markers SALL4 and CK19 were significantly reduced in EZH2-si tumors, whereas EZH2-overexpressing tumors exhibited elevated protein expression levels (Fig. 6e, f; Supplementary Table S7). The expression patterns of SALL4 and CK19, two key markers associated with tumor proliferation, metastasis, and invasiveness, were further validated by IHC analysis and were consistent with the ICC results. Collectively, these results indicated that EZH2 promoted ESCC tumor growth and enhanced the invasive and migratory capacities of KYSE-30 and YM-1 cells.

EZH2 upregulation was positively associated with distant metastasis in ESCC

Pulmonary metastasis is a common manifestation of advanced esophageal cancer. To further investigate the role of EZH2 in ESCC metastasis, C57BL/6 nude mice were intravenously injected with EZH2-manipulated KYSE-30 and YM-1 cells to induce metastatic nodule formation. Notably, mice in the EZH2-oe group developed a significantly higher number of pulmonary metastatic nodules compared with the control group. In contrast, the injection of EZH2-si ESCC cells effectively attenuated lung metastatic colonization (Fig. 6g; Supplementary Fig S6). Histopathological assessment using H&E staining confirmed the presence of metastatic nodules within lung tissue and revealed a substantially larger metastatic area in the EZH2-oe YM-1 group (Supplementary Fig. S6). Additionally, our subcutaneous and tail-vein xenograft models demonstrated an important role for EZH2 in promoting tumor growth and lung colonization; however, these experimental models may not fully recapitulate the native ESCC tumor microenvironment or the lymphatic- mediated metastatic cascade typically observed during tumor progression in patients.

EZH2 overexpression promoted angiogenesis and reduced survival rate in the ESCC nude mice model

Angiogenesis contributes to tumor growth, invasion, and metastasis across multiple malignancies24–27. Remarkably, following surgical removal of EZH2-overexpressing ESCC tumors, numerous newly formed blood vessels were observed surrounding the subcutaneous tumor sites (Supplementary Fig. S6), particularly in the YM-1 group. Furthermore, mice injected with EZH2-oe cells exhibited a significantly higher mortality rate during weeks 2–4 post-transplantation, which may reflect the development of more aggressive tumor progression. In contrast, nude mice transplanted with EZH2-si cells demonstrated improved survival rates compared with the control groups (Fig. 6h).

Discussion

ESCC is a highly aggressive malignancy characterized by poor prognosis, therapeutic resistance, and high rates of metastasis and recurrence, features largely attributed to the persistence of CSC populations28–31. Despite significant advances in neoadjuvant therapies and immunotherapeutic strategies, effectively targeting CSCs remains a major challenge in cancer treatment. The properties of CSCs, including self-renewal, apoptosis resistance, and enhanced invasive and metastatic potential, contribute significantly to tumor recurrence and poor patient survival32–39. In the present investigation, we identified EZH2 as a master regulator associated with the expression of key CSC markers and stemness-related pathways. Our findings indicate that EZH2 upregulation enhances tumorigenicity, stemness, and self-renewal capacity, and simultaneously reduced apoptosis and chemotherapy response in KYSE-30 and YM-1 ESCC cells.

Although the role of EZH2 as a histone methyltransferase and epigenetic modulator has been extensively documented in various cancers9–12, our study provides integrated experimental and computational evidence suggesting a potential mechanistic contribution of EZH2 to stemness-associated regulatory networks in ESCC. Through an integrative strategy that combined in silico molecular docking analyses, comprehensive transcriptomic profiling, functional in vitro assays, and robust in vivo xenograft models, we identified novel insights into the EZH2 signature in regulating a panel of CSC markers, including NANOG, OCT4, SOX2, CD44, CD133, CD117, ALDH1, BMI1, LGR5, ICAM, SALL4, PYGO, AKT, and ERBB3 (Fig. 7; Supplementary Table S8). Notably, while previous studies in hepatocellular carcinoma have reported EZH2-mediated upregulation of ERBB family members40, our findings extend these observations by suggesting a potential regulatory role for EZH2 in modulating ERBB3 expression specifically in ESCC cells.

Across multiple tumor types, EZH2 has been consistently associated with established CSC populations, highlighting its potential role as a pan-cancer regulator of stemness. Elevated EZH2 expression has been reported in breast CSCs (CD44+/CD24–, ALDH+), pancreatic CSCs (EPCAM+/CD44+/CD24+), ovarian CSCs (CD44+/CD117+), melanoma, skin squamous cell carcinoma, colorectal cancer spheroids (CD133+/CD44+), and chronic myeloid leukemia stem-like cells (CD34+/CD38–). These cross-cancer observations collectively support that EZH2 consistently marks CSC phenotypes across diverse malignancies, supporting our findings in ESCC41. Compared with other cancer types, ESCC cells (KYSE-30 and YM-1) exhibit a unique pattern of EZH2-mediated regulation, particularly involving LGR5, ICAM, and ERBB3, as well as distinct crosstalk with several signaling pathways.

EZH2 regulates the expression of NANOG, SOX2, and c-MYC, ultimately promoting stemness in colorectal cancer42,43. In our study, molecular docking analyses hypothesized a potential structural compatibility between EZH2 and the promoter regions of key stemness-associated transcription factors (NANOG, OCT4, and SOX2). This computational observation led us to hypothesize that EZH2 may associate with stemness-related regulatory elements and contribute to the maintenance of stemness features, potentially independent of its canonical epigenetic role. This non-epigenetic role of EZH2 aligns with recent studies in other cancer types, showing that EZH2 can act as a scaffolding protein or transcriptional co-regulator independent of its catalytic activity, thereby enhancing tumorigenic potential. Importantly, although these in silico predictions highlight possible EZH2-CSC interactions, they represent hypothesis-generating computational models rather than definitive mechanistic evidence. Direct physical binding and functional validation would require experimental approaches such as co‑immunoprecipitation, ChIP‑seq, ChIP‑qPCR, or other related epigenomic assays.

EZH2 has been associated with enhanced cell proliferation and suppression of apoptosis in ESCC cells. In the present investigation, we demonstrated that EZH2 overexpression resulted in a remarkable upregulation of c-MYC and AKT, suggesting that these pathways may contribute to EZH2-mediated survival signaling in KYSE-30 and YM-1 cells. A noticeable decrease in c-MYC expression was observed following EZH2 silencing, concomitant with G1/S cell-cycle arrest, indicating that EZH2 may regulate cell-cycle progression through modulation of c-MYC-dependent mechanisms.

Functionally, EZH2 plays critical roles in tumor initiation, progression, and metastasis. Our previous study demonstrated that EZH2 promotes migratory capacity, EMT, and invasive behavior in ESCC cell culture models22. The current study reveals that EZH2 overexpression results in an approximately 40% increase in cell migration and invasion in YM-1 cells. Furthermore, in EZH2-engineered tumor models, we observed a significant increase in tumor size, aggressiveness, and pulmonary metastasis, whereas EZH2 silencing improved overall survival. Given the exploratory nature of in vivo findings, these results suggest a potential role for EZH2 in the metastatic cascade and indicate that it may contribute to both local invasion and distant metastasis in the YM-1 ESCC model. However, future studies involving a broader panel of ESCC cell lines, replication in larger animal cohorts, and patient-derived xenografts are warranted to confirm the generalizability of these observations.

Our analysis revealed the pro-angiogenic effects of EZH2 overexpression, which promotes ESCC angiogenesis through EGFR regulation, as evidenced by increased xenograft vascularization. Consistently, our in vivo study demonstrated notably enhanced vascularization in EZH2-overexpressing xenografts. These observations support the possibility of EZH2-EGFR crosstalk, highlighting a potential framework for the development of targeted therapeutic strategies aimed at suppressing tumor angiogenesis and progression in ESCC44–46.

The presence of CSC populations within the tumor mass is one of the major contributors to chemotherapeutic resistance in cancers47,48. EZH2 knockdown significantly sensitized cells to chemotherapy, with combined 5-FU and Cisplatin treatment producing a synergistic cytotoxic effect, resulting in an approximately 89% increase in cell death in EZH2-si cells.

Increasing evidence highlights the involvement of EZH2 in crosstalk with multiple oncogenic signaling pathways crucial for CSC maintenance and tumor progression, including the PI3K/AKT/mTOR, Hippo, BMP, and Hedgehog signaling pathways49–51 (Fig. 7). Our bioinformatics analyses and experimental studies revealed a significant association between EZH2 and key oncogenic mediators across multiple signaling pathways. Specifically, EZH2 modulates self-renewal capacity and chemotherapy resistance primarily through activation of the PI3K/AKT signaling axis.

Notably, EZH2 appeared to potentiate the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway, as evidenced by the upregulation of downstream effectors and stemness-associated transcription factors, including NANOG, OCT4, SOX2, and ALDH1. This signaling axis is implicated in regulating cell proliferation, stemness maintenance, tumor invasion, and chemoresistance in ESCC52.

Furthermore, several clinicopathological parameters in patients, such as tumor invasion depth, lymph node metastasis, histological grade, and overall survival, have been correlated with AKT1 overexpression, a key component of the PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling pathway in ESCC52–55. Our results revealed that EZH2 regulates AKT1 expression, providing mechanistic evidence for crosstalk between EZH2 and PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling pathway, resulting in increased proliferation, enhanced self-renewal, and drug resistance in ESCC cells56.

Recent studies have elucidated a critical involvement of EZH2 in the dysregulation of the Hippo signaling pathway in ESCC. EZH2 upregulation has been shown to enhance the expression of key components of the Hippo pathway, promoting tumorigenesis through mechanisms such as EMT and increased cellular migration in ESCC models49. Moreover, EZH2 plays a central role in regulating multiple oncogenic signaling pathways, including PI3K/AKT, Hedgehog, Hippo, and BMP, thereby promoting the expression and maintenance of key CSC markers and supporting stemness features in ESCC cells (Fig. 7)49,56. Ultimately, it is important to note that EZH2 may also exhibit context-dependent or non-oncogenic roles, and under certain physiological or tissue-specific conditions, its function may not be strictly oncogenic.

Acknowledging current limitations, particularly the absence of functional rescue experiments, the need for high-resolution epigenomic profiling within CSC niches, and the development of more representative patient-derived models, future studies employing ChIP-qPCR/ChIP-seq, ATAC-seq, and single-cell transcriptomics with larger sample size will be essential to further refine mechanistic insights. Restoring EZH2 expression in knockdown cells and exploring its non-canonical transcriptional roles could further clarify functional contributions. We also recommend employing CRISPR-Cas9 gene editing to generate precise isogenic or knockout models. Importantly, clinical trials evaluating EZH2 inhibitors, such as the FDA-approved drug Tazemetostat, are warranted to explore potential repurposing as neoadjuvant therapies for ESCC.

Conclusions

In summary, our investigation elucidates the regulatory role of EZH2 in maintaining the stemness state of ESCC. In silico analyses revealed strong correlations between EZH2 expression, stem cell markers, and key clinicopathological parameters in esophageal cancer. Subsequent experimental validation confirmed that EZH2 promotes colony and sphere formation, supporting the potential of EZH2 as a CSC–associated marker in ESCC. Additionally, EZH2 was associated with enhanced cell proliferation, inhibition of apoptosis, and modulation of resistance to 5-FU and Cisplatin in ESCC cells. Moreover, EZH2 exhibited multifaceted oncogenic effects on tumor initiation and progression, both in vitro and in EZH2-engineered nude mice models, contributing to increased tumor aggressiveness. Collectively, these findings identify EZH2 as a key regulator of stemness and malignancy in KYSE-30 and YM-1 ESCC cells, highlighting EZH2 potential as a valuable therapeutic target for eradicating CSC subpopulations and improving therapeutic outcomes in esophageal squamous cell carcinoma.
