The inpatient setting as an opportunity to assess healthcare transition
Authors: Luke Coffman, Ashley McCool, Tanika Middleton, Emily Nations Bufkin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMost health care transition (HCT) interventions target the outpatient setting. However, hospitalization may serve as an additional touchpoint to identify patients who require transition services.Local problemAt a large urban pediatric hospital, inpatient care coordinators (CC) complete an EMR-based general assessment that includes an optional HCT screening item. Baseline data showed low item completion. The project aimed to increase the proportion of eligible admissions among patients aged ≥ 16 years with documented completion of the HCT screening item by 50% from the pre-intervention baseline within 6 months.MethodsUsing the Define, Measure, Analyze, Design, and Verify (DMADV) framework, monthly item completion among eligible admissions aged ≥ 16 years was tracked in a run chart. Training outcomes were assessed with anonymous before and after session surveys.InterventionsStakeholders and process owners co-developed a standardized workflow that defined an age threshold for universal inpatient HCT screening (≥16 years) and delivered an interactive CC training session on HCT concepts and CC roles in HCT.ResultsDocumented completion increased from 44.0% in October 2024–74.4% in May 2025 (p = 0.004). In the training evaluation, after-session survey respondents reported higher self-reported confidence across survey items.ConclusionImplementing standardized inpatient workflow and CC education was associated with improved documented completion of an EMR HCT screening item among eligible admissions for patients 16 years and older. The results support the feasibility of using hospitalization as an additional touchpoint to document HCT status. Future phases will focus on implementing structural EMR changes and developing referral pathways between inpatient and outpatient CC to support patients who need additional HCT support.Keywords: Healthcare transition, Quality Improvement, Care Coordination, Children and Youth, With Special Healthcare Needs, Inpatient Healthcare Transition

Introduction

Problem description

This project was conducted at a large urban safety-net freestanding pediatric hospital licensed for 490 beds and involved coordination between several departments. Since the HCT screening item is administered by care coordinators, efforts were focused on this department. The hospital’s Office of Patient Transition provided support for the project along with a partnership with physicians from the Division of Internal Medicine and Pediatrics.

Care Coordinators (CC) are responsible for coordinating care across the health system to ensure that diverse patient needs are met. Care Coordinators complete a general assessment within 48 h of admission for all hospitalized patients to identify barriers to discharge and post-discharge needs. The assessment includes an optional HCT screening item: “Have you started transitioning care to an adult provider?” with yes/no response options. There is an accompanying free-text field for CC to document additional details. The HCT screening item appears in the assessment for patients of all ages. Out of the 822 patients age 16 and older who completed the general assessment from January 1, 2022, to December 31, 2022, only 48% of (n = 396) patients age 16 and older were screened (Fig. 1). Of those screened, just 4.6% had started transitioning care to an adult provider. For the 141 patients aged 18 and older, just 55% were screened. Of those screened, only 9% reporting they had started transitioning care to an adult provider.

Available knowledge

Health Care Transition (HCT) is best defined as “the purposeful, planned movement of adolescents and young adults with chronic physical and medical conditions from child-centered to adult-oriented health-care systems”.1 The process of HCT was first published in a 2011 clinical report jointly written by the American Academy of Pediatrics, the American Academy of Family Physicians, and the American College of Physicians.2 In 2018, the clinical report was updated to include the Six Core Elements of Transition implementation framework which includes transition policy, transition tracking and monitoring, transition readiness assessments, transition planning, transfer and integration, and transfer completion.3 The Six Core Elements begin as early as age 12 with a discussion of the transition policy. Transition planning can begin as early as age 14.3 According to the 2022 and 2023 National Survey of Children’s Health, only 18.1% of adolescents aged 12–17 had received services to prepare for transition to adult health care. Only 21.8% of youth with special healthcare needs had received transition services.4

HCT is a complex and fragile process because responsibilities are distributed across both pediatric and adult primary care providers, specialists, inpatient care teams, patients, and caregivers. Quality Improvement studies identify communication and care coordination as major needs for transition-age youth.5 The challenging HCT process leads to poor health outcomes, well-documented across many pediatric diseases.6, 7, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12

As adolescents transition into young adulthood, healthcare utilization patterns change. According to one study comparing adolescents to young adults, young adults have a higher percentage of ambulatory encounters in the emergency department, and their medical costs are less likely to be covered by private insurance.13 Although the transition period is associated with increased risk and worse outcomes, it also presents an opportunity for clinicians to foster positive self-management skills and help prevent complications.

Rationale

According to the National Academy for State Health Policy, the National Care Coordination Standards for Children and Youth with Special Healthcare Needs (CYSHCN) include care transition as a key domain for care coordinators with a particular emphasis on transition to adult medical care.14 At the study site, CC complete an assessment of each caregiver-patient dyad, including an optional screening item about the transition to adult care. Because this screening item served as a natural touchpoint for assessing HCT readiness, CC helped characterize the current state of transition preparation and identified opportunities to streamline and standardize their workflow.

It has been well described that CYSHCN utilize hospital services at higher rates than their peers, resulting in increased healthcare utilization and associated costs.15, 16, 17, 18 As medical treatment for chronic conditions of childhood has improved, the prevalence of young adults living with these conditions has increased. During HCT, young adults and caregivers often fear entering a new adult system and hesitate to leave their trusted pediatric institution, making HCT difficult.3 Moreover, self-reported experiences in adult hospitals are less favorable, reinforcing these worries.19

Addressing HCT during inpatient admissions remains largely unexplored in the current literature. Although structured interventions have been successfully implemented, these systems are almost exclusively focused on the outpatient setting. A recently updated meta-analysis of HCT interventions notably excluded inpatient-centered programs.20 Given the healthcare utilization patterns among CYSHCN, the inpatient setting may offer an additional touchpoint to identify unmet HCT needs and reinforce outpatient transition planning. The target age for this intervention was patients age 16 and older, as this age group represents a population that should have initiated HCT planning in the outpatient setting and would benefit from rapid intervention if HCT planning has not begun. A consensus was reached regarding this age cut-off, which is further described in the intervention section. Leveraging the existing inpatient general assessment was a pragmatic approach to screen for HCT readiness among older adolescents and young adults, serve as an operational measure of outpatient HCT preparation, and prompt linkage to outpatient HCT resources following discharge.

Specific aims

This project aimed to increase the proportion of eligible admissions among patients aged 16 and older with documented completion (yes/no response) of the optional HCT screening item in the EMR general assessment by 50% from the pre-intervention baseline within 6 months of implementing a standardized screening workflow and care coordinator education.

Methods

Context

During the design phase, root cause analysis was conducted by the working group, including a fishbone diagram, 5 Whys analysis, and a driver diagram. The working group was built in 2023 and included a nurse with experience in care coordination, a Med-Peds physician, a medical student, and a healthcare administrator. Fishbone diagram domains included EMR documentation, insurance, caregivers, and employee knowledge (Fig. 2). Based on contextual analysis and stakeholder input, the working group implemented the 5 Whys approach. They first hypothesized that a key reason for not asking the HCT screening item to patients was a lack of process standardization and insufficient HCT resources for both patients and CC. Furthermore, the presence of the HCT screening item for all patients, regardless of age, is a key contributor to poor utilization, along with its unclear wording. No educational materials were available to care coordinators on HCT, and there were no guidelines on who should be asked the HCT screening item. From this root cause analysis, key drivers of the problem were identified, including low CC confidence in addressing HCT, a lack of resources for caregivers, frequent omission of the HCT screening item, and an absence of standardized processes for screening for HCT needs during an inpatient stay (Fig. 3).

Interventions

Building on this foundation, stakeholders were recruited including leadership from the care coordination department, care coordinator team leads, and CC employees. A process map of the current state, a shared mental model, and revised workflows were created to guide and standardize the care coordination process on HCT.

The current state was defined by observing CC, recording the current workflow, and confirming observations with CC. At baseline, no standardized process existed for addressing HCT with patients during admissions, with one notable exception. In situations where a discharging physician requested that a patient have an outpatient follow-up appointment scheduled with an adult doctor, the care management assistant would verify the patient’s insurance and identify clinicians who were covered by the insurance plan. For patients not requiring follow-up based in an adult model of care, HCT was not frequently discussed, even for patients over age 18 who lacked formal HCT plans.

After understanding the current state, a new workflow was created, standardizing the age group who must be screened for HCT needs (Fig. 4). Although the current guidelines provide a helpful roadmap for initiating HCT planning, the inpatient setting differs from the outpatient setting. A key component in developing the new workflow was establishing an age threshold for asking the HCT screening item, as the item is present on the assessment for patients of any age. After reviewing baseline data, the multidisciplinary team selected age 16 and older as a target for universal inpatient HCT screening. This threshold aligns with evidence-informed structured process (Six Core Elements framework) timelines, in which transition preparation is initiated earlier in adolescence and is expected to be underway by mid-adolescence for individuals who have received HCT planning in the outpatient setting. Initiating HCT screening at age 16 was also intended to help identify patients at higher risk for delayed or fragmented transition planning, so that the hospitalization could serve as an additional touchpoint to connect patients and caregivers to HCT resources after discharge. Finally, limiting HCT screening to patients aged 16 or older balanced clinical value with CC workload feasibility by avoiding routine screening of younger patients who are further from transfer and less likely to require immediate HCT-related support.

Standardized care coordinator workflow for HCT screening item, developed in partnership with a multidisciplinary team, stakeholders, and process owners. Image Credit: Luke Coffman, MD via Microsoft Visio (Microsoft Corp, USA).

A main barrier to assessing HCT in the inpatient setting was a lack of inpatient staff training on HCT guidelines and workflow expectations. To address this barrier, an HCT training session was developed for inpatient care coordinators using principles of the constructivist experiential learning cycle to promote participant engagement. Key learning objectives of this workshop were to define HCT, examine the role of the CC in HCT, review EMR documentation procedures, and establish a workflow for screening and addressing HCT needs during an inpatient admission. The curriculum was designed to activate participants’ prior knowledge by prompting care coordinators to reflect on their previous experiences conducting HCT screening and counseling. Building on this foundation, the facilitator guided participants through a simulated case delivered in staged “information aliquots,” allowing learners to iteratively apply concepts, problem-solve, and integrate new content with their existing clinical workflow. Core curricular components included standardized definitions of health care transition, an overview of the National Care Coordination Standards for HCT, and instruction on the six core elements of HCT. The session also introduced a standardized workflow—anchored by a recommended universal screening age of 16—and provided patient-facing resources (e.g., handouts, web-based tools) for care coordinators to use in clinical practice.

The inpatient staff training was delivered as a single 60-minute session during the CC department’s regular monthly lunchtime huddle (paid work time). The session was conducted in person at the Children’s Medical Center Dallas campus and simultaneously transmitted virtually to the Plano campus, where care coordinators participated together in person. Attendance was strongly encouraged and included full-time inpatient care CC and CC leadership team; only one session was offered. The session was delivered by the first author and was developed in conjunction with the working group. No pre- or post-session work was required, and no additional follow-up training sessions were conducted during this phase.

This session represented an early phase of intervention with a focus on basic HCT principles and increased screening rates. Further interventions will build upon this with infrastructure for follow-up built into the EHR. To assess changes in learner perceptions, participants completed before and after session surveys measuring self-reported confidence and understanding of HCT.

Study of the interventions

The impact of the intervention was measured by documented completion of the HCT screening item (yes/no response entered) in the EMR-based general assessment. The unit of analysis was eligible admissions; repeat admissions were omitted with the most recent admission being included in the data set. Post intervention data was compared to baseline data. Baseline performance was assessed from January 1, 2022, to December 31, 2022. Post-intervention data was assessed during the 6 months following the CC training session (November 13, 2024). A secondary study of the intervention was self-reported confidence measures from participants in the training session on before and after surveys.

Measures

Measures were chosen to assess processes, outcomes, and balance with undesired outcomes. The main process measure was self-reported confidence metrics from the training session. The main outcome measure was the percent of patients aged 16 and older with the HCT screening item documented in the EMR during the measurement period.

Analysis

Data was analyzed by looking at trends over time in percentage of eligible admissions with a documented yes/no response to the HCT screening item. Screening item completion was plotted monthly to create a run chart with 800 data points, with re-admissions omitted from the data set because only the most recently filled screening item was available for data collection. For a given patient with multiple admissions each year, only the most recent survey results were included. Data was also analyzed by patient age groups. For the training participant survey data, before and after session survey responses were summarized at the group level and compared as unpaired samples. Before and after responses were not matched as the survey did not contain unique identifiers and had fewer participants in the after survey.

Results

Impact on completion rates of the HCT screening item

Following the training session, six months of completion of the HCT screening item in the EMR (yes/no response entered) for all patients aged 16 and older was measured. Data was collected from November 14, 2024, to May 31, 2025, following the November 13, 2024, CC training session. Screening item completion was calculated monthly as the proportion of eligible admissions for patients aged 16 and older with a documented yes/no response to the HCT screening item in the EMR general assessment. The results were displayed in a run chart (Fig. 5) (n = 800 data points, separated by month) with the pre-intervention median calculated from January 1, 2022, to November 13, 2024. The CC training session (November 13, 2024) was indicated with change of color from black to green and standard run-chart rules for nonrandom variation were applied. The HCT screening item completion rate increased from 45.0% (22/49) in October 2024–74.4% (32/43) in April 2025 (6 months post-intervention). The p-value (two-tailed) was 0.004 for the difference in these independent proportions, calculated using the two-proportion z-test. Completion improved across age groups, except for patients 19 years or older (Fig. 5). Among completed items, patients aged < 18 years more frequently reported that transition had not yet begun (“No”) than at baseline (Fig. 6), whereas older adolescents and young adults more frequently reported having started transition (“Yes”) (Fig. 7).

Efficacy of staff training

Training outcomes were assessed using self-reported confidence ratings on a 6-point Likert scale for 11 items derived from the session objectives. Overall confidence (item 12) was assessed on a 1–10 scale. The full survey instrument is provided in Table 1 in the supplementary materials. Anonymous before and after session surveys were collected (pre: n = 18; post: n = 11). Survey findings are presented descriptively and reflect immediate, unpaired self-reported confidence among respondents rather than matched individual change or longer-term retention. Across items, post-session responses indicated higher self-reported confidence (Fig. 7). The mean overall confidence score (item 12) increased from 4.1 to 5.7.

Discussion

Summary

Over the six months following implementation, documented completion of the HCT screening item increased over time and was temporally associated with workflow standardization and CC training. Completion of the HCT screening item increased across age groups, except among patients aged 19 years and older. This subgroup represented a small proportion of the post-intervention analytic sample, limiting the ability to draw definitive conclusions.

As completion improved, the distribution of recorded responses shifted from baseline data. Older adolescents and young adults (18 years and older) more frequently reported having started transition to an adult provider, whereas patients younger than 18 years more frequently reported that transition had not yet begun. These findings may be consistent with delayed HCT planning for many patients and highlight an opportunity to reinforce transition preparation earlier in adolescence, in both the outpatient and inpatient settings.

The CC staff training results were favorable. The session incorporated an interactive, case-based discussion. The training session established a shared understanding of the role of inpatient care coordinators in HCT screening and clarified day-to-day workflows by standardizing the age threshold for HCT screening. Before and after survey data showed improvements in self-reported understanding of HCT principles and confidence in talking to both patients and caregivers about HCT. Respondents indicated improved clarity regarding which patients should be asked the HCT screening item. Post-session responses suggested comparatively lower confidence in more complex tasks, including supporting caregivers in developing HCT plans and assessing the integrity of an existing HCT plan.

Although the CC training session provided key foundational information, the primary value of this intervention was the standardization of the screening process within inpatient care coordination. The strength of this intervention was supported by multidisciplinary buy-in, underscoring the importance of collaboration across all domains of HCT work. The screening age of 16 was supported by care coordination leadership and adopted as part of their departmental approach to addressing HCT.

Interpretation

Interventions that rely solely on education may have limited sustained impact.21, 22 To support lasting change, structural improvements are needed, including improving the clarity of the HCT screening item and establishing a formal follow-up system for patients who require additional HCT support after hospitalization. The core working group developed a proposal to require completion of the HCT screening item for eligible admissions among patients aged 16 years and older.

Future work will focus on developing a direct referral pathway between the inpatient and outpatient care coordination teams for HCT-specific follow-up. To enhance sustainability, the in-person training session was converted to a web-based session for CC employees through a separate project. This work represents a continuous quality improvement effort, with the next phase underway to further develop and evaluate this novel arena for HCT care coordination.

Limitations

Although documented completion of the HCT screening item improved during the post-intervention period, adherence may decrease over time without reinforcement and without EMR-level structural supports. Assessment of the training session was completed by self-reported confidence scores and these responses were not matched, which reduces the ability to make conclusions about the efficacy of the training materials. Data was not collected on the entire population of employees, so a non-response bias analysis was not possible. The study was conducted at a single large urban pediatric hospital, which could limit generalizability to other environments.

Conclusions

This project represents an initial step toward a novel approach to HCT that leverages the inpatient setting as a strategic opportunity to identify patients in need of improved transition to adult care services. A multidisciplinary team and input from process owners were key to the project’s success. Care coordinators were eager and well-placed to serve as the anchor for this approach. Through process standardization and staff training, staff confidence in managing HCT improved, as did increased completion of the HCT screening item. In the future, patients could benefit from an integrated inpatient–outpatient approach to HCT that improves care coordination for the most vulnerable populations, with the inpatient setting serving as a key opportunity to identify those in greatest need of support.
