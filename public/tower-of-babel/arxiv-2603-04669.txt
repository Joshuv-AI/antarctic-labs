Strongly clustered random graphs via triadic closure: Degree correlations and clustering spectrum
Lorenzo Cirigliano, Gareth J. Baxter, Gábor Timár

Abstract
Real-world networks often exhibit strong transitivity with nontrivial local clustering spectra and degree correlations. Such features are not easily modelled in tractable network models, creating an obstacle to the theoretical understanding of such complex network structures. Here, we address this problem using a model for strongly clustered random graphs in which each triad of a random backbone is closed with a certain probability. Despite the intricate loopy local structure of the graphs obtained, we provide exact expressions for the local clustering spectrum and the degree correlations, filling the gap in the theoretical description of this model for random graphs. In particular, we find positive degree assortativity accompanies high transitivity, and non-trivial structure in the clustering spectrum. Exact asymptotic analytical results are complemented with extensive numerical characterization of finite size effects.



I
Introduction

Local features like clustering and neighbor degree correlations are abundant in real-world complex networks, and can have a significant effect on network function. However these features are poorly accounted for in established theoretical models of random graphs.
More specifically, degree-degree correlations are positively associated with transitivity
Newman 2002
;
Vasques Filho and O’Neale 2020
. Local clustering is not typically uniform in nodes of different degrees, as one might expect in a purely random network, but instead the clustering spectrum is usually non-trivial, often decaying with increasing degree
Stegehuis et al. 2017
.

Random networks with strong clustering can be generated in a number of different ways. Analytically tractable models typically rely on some sort of treelike structure of local motifs – such as trees of loops
Newman 2009
, trees of cliques
Gleeson 2009
or trees of partial cliques
Karrer and Newman 2010
– to achieve the desired clustering.
These methods produce constrained ensembles of networks which do not however include overlapping motifs and loops of arbitrary length.
Qualitatively, these models show that there are many ways to produce the same level of global clustering, and a careful analysis of local clustering is necessary to characterize them.

The recently introduced Static Triadic Closure (STC) random graph model
Cirigliano et al. 2024
overcomes these limitations, efficiently producing highly random graphs with strong clustering which contain overlapping loops and motifs with statistics resembling those of real networks.
The key idea behind the construction of these random graphs is simple: take a backbone graph
\mathcal{G}_{0}
, and close each triad with probability
f
, to create a new random graph
\mathcal{G}_{f}
, see Figure
1
.
If the starting backbone
\mathcal{G}_{0}
is locally treelike exact analytical results can be obtained. For instance, site percolation has been exactly solved
Cirigliano 2025
for the case
f=1
, clarifying the role of clustering in determining the universal critical properties of systems defined on networks.
The analysis of the global topological properties, such as the global clustering (transitivity) and small motif densities, of
\mathcal{G}_{f}
obtained from uncorrelated random backbones, was developed in
Cirigliano et al. 2024
.

While the STC model is unbiased, it does produce degree correlations, which have not yet been characterized. Furthermore, the understanding of local clustering properties for this model is still lacking. Here, we complete the analysis of the STC random graphs, providing a detailed numerical and analytical study of degree correlations and clustering spectrum.

We show that, by separating edges into those present in the backbone network and those added via triadic closure, and taking advantage of the uncorrelated random nature of the backbone network, a number of exact results can be obtained.
We give an exact expression for the Pearson correlation coefficient in terms of the moments of the backbone network degree distribution. We find exact formulae for the local clustering spectrum, which in general need to be summed numerically, and derive the asymptotic behavior.

We find that the STC process always creates assortative correlations, evidenced by a positive Pearson coefficient, and that the effect increases with transitivity. This shows that such correlations are a natural consequence of the unbiased triadic closure process, thus explaining at least some of the assortative correlations found in highly clustered real networks.
For scale-free backbones we observe sharp transitions in the Pearson correlation coefficient, mirroring the transition observed in the transitivity
Cirigliano et al. 2024
.
We also characterize the local clustering spectrum, observing a quantitative distinction between homogeneous and heterogeneous structures. We characterize the significant finite size effects present when the backbone network has a powerlaw degree distribution.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. In Sec.
II
, we present some definitions and we briefly recall some results on the STC graphs. In Sec.
III
and Sec.
IV
, we analize the degree correlations and the local clustering spectrum, respectively, in STC graphs with uncorrelated backbones of arbitrary degree distribution.
Finally, in Sec.
V
we summarize our results and discuss their relevance and their implications.

II
The degree distribution of STC graphs

In this section we briefly recall some results for the degree distribution of STC random graphs
\mathcal{G}_{f}

Cirigliano et al. 2024
with an arbitrary uncorrelated random backbone. We also present some novel results for finite-size scale-free STC graphs. We denote by lower case letters quantities related to the backbone network
\mathcal{G}_{0}
, such as the degree
k
and the degree distribution
p(k)
, and with capital letters
K
and
P(K)
the corresponding quantities for to the clustered network
\mathcal{G}_{f}
.

Let us consider a node with original degree
k
surrounded by neighbors with original excess degrees
r_{1},\dots,r_{k}
. After the triadic closure, such a node has degree
K=k+\sum_{i=1}^{k}\nu_{i}
, where
\nu_{i}
are random variables representing the number of new neighbors made along the
i
-th original branch. The excess degrees in
\mathcal{G}_{0}
are independent random variables with distribution
q(r)=(r+1)p(r+1)/\langle k\rangle
, while the
\nu_{i}
are independent binomial random variables distributed, given
r_{i}
, with distribution
\mathcal{B}(\nu_{i}|r_{i},f)={r_{i}\choose\nu_{i}}f^{\nu_{i}}(1-f)^{r_{i}-\nu_{i}}
. The conditional degree distribution is then simply given by
P\left(K|k,\{r_{i}\},\{\nu_{i}\}\right)=\delta\left(K,k+\sum_{i=1}^{k}\nu_{i}\right)
. Averaging over
P(k,\{r_{i}\},\{\nu_{i}\})=p(k)\prod_{i=1}^{k}q(r_{i})\mathcal{B}(\nu_{i}|r_{i},f)
, since
\mathcal{G}_{0}
is uncorrelated, we then get

P(K)=\sum_{k,\{r_{i}\},\{\nu_{i}\}}p(k)\prod_{i=1}^{k}q(r_{i})\mathcal{B}(\nu_{i}|r_{i},f)\delta\left(K,k+\sum_{i=1}^{k}\nu_{i}\right).

(1)

Even if the r.h.s. of Eq. (
1
) cannot be computed explicitly, we can easily find an expression for the generating function
G_{0}(z)=\sum_{K}P(K)z^{K}
of the degree distribution
P(K)
. Multiplying Eq. (
1
) by
z^{K}
and summing over
K
we get

G_{0}(z)=g_{0}\left(zg_{1}(1-f+fz)\right),

(2)

where
g_{0}(z)=\sum_{k}p(k)z^{k}
and
g_{1}(z)=\sum_{r}q(r)z^{r}
. Eq. (
2
) will be fundamental in many calculations, as it allows us to express the moments
\langle K^{n}\rangle
of the degrees in
\mathcal{G}_{f}
in terms of the moments
\langle k^{m}\rangle
, with
m\leq n
, of the degrees in the backbone
\mathcal{G}_{0}

Cirigliano et al. 2024
. In other words, we can characterize the properties of
\mathcal{G}_{f}
via the knowledge of the backbone
\mathcal{G}_{0}
.

II.1
The case of power-law backbones

For power-law (PL) backbones with degree distribution
p(k)\sim k^{-\gamma}
, the generating functions have a singularity at
z=1
given, at leading order in
\varepsilon=1-z
, by
g_{0}(1-\varepsilon)\sim 1-c_{1}\varepsilon^{\gamma-1}
and
g_{1}(1-\varepsilon)\sim 1-c_{2}\varepsilon^{\gamma-2}
,
where
c_{1},c_{2}
are constants depending on
\gamma
and in general on the low-degree part of the
p(k)
. With these expansions from Eq. (
2
) we get at leading order
G_{0}(1-\varepsilon)\sim 1-C\varepsilon^{\gamma-2}=1-C\varepsilon^{\widetilde{\gamma}-1}
, where
\widetilde{\gamma}=\gamma-1
, and
C
is a constant that depends also on
f
. Hence we can conclude, using asymptotic theorems for generating functions
Flajolet and Odlyzko 1990
, that

P(K)\sim K^{-\widetilde{\gamma}}

(3)

for asymptotically large
K
. In other words, the STC mechanism decreases by
1
the exponent of the degree distribution. In Figure
2
we report the results of numerical simulations for the degree distributions
P(K)
of STC networks obtained from power-law backbones with
p(k)\sim k^{-\gamma}
,
\gamma>2
and
k_{\textrm{min}}\leq k\leq k_{\textrm{max}}
, where
k_{\textrm{max}}
grows with the network size as
k_{\textrm{max}}=\min\{N^{1/(\gamma-1)},N^{1/2}\}
. Since we need to reach large sizes to check the theoretical asymptotic predictions, the simulations are performed sampling nodes neighborhoods instead of creating the entire STC graph. Figure
2
clearly shows the scaling as
K^{-\widetilde{\gamma}}
, but, surprisingly, this scaling holds only up to
K^{*}\sim fk_{\textrm{max}}
. For
K^{*}<K<K_{\textrm{max}}
an exponent
\gamma
is observed instead.

To solve this conundrum between theoretical predicions in the infinite-size limit and the double power-law scaling observed for finite-size networks, we identify two distinct mechanisms in the formation of large-degree nodes in
\mathcal{G}_{f}
. The first mechanism affects the degree distribution up to
K\sim fk_{\textrm{max}}
, and it works as follows. Nodes of degree
\mathcal{O}(1)
in
\mathcal{G}_{0}
get a degree
K
which is proportional to their neighbors excess degrees in
\mathcal{G}_{0}
. Since in a finite system these excess degrees are bounded by
k_{\textrm{max}}
, a node of finite degree in
\mathcal{G}_{0}
will have at most degree
K\sim fk_{\textrm{max}}
in
\mathcal{G}_{f}
. This explains the exponent
\widetilde{\gamma}=\gamma-1
up to
K=K^{*}\sim fk_{\textrm{max}}
. However, nodes in
\mathcal{G}_{f}
can have a degree larger than
K^{*}
. Let us now consider hubs in
\mathcal{G}_{0}
, i.e., nodes with degree
k\sim k_{\textrm{max}}
. Since
k_{\textrm{max}}\gg 1
we have, by the law of large numbers,
K\sim k+\sum_{i=1}^{k}fr_{i}\sim k(1+f\langle r\rangle)
, which is a random variable following the original degree distribution
p(k)
, just rescaled by
\theta=1+f\langle r\rangle
. Thus we have
P(K)\sim p(K/\theta)/\theta\sim K^{-\gamma}
, for
k_{\textrm{max}}<K<K_{\textrm{max}}
, where
K_{\textrm{max}}=\theta k_{\textrm{max}}
. Note that
\theta=\mathcal{O}(1)
for
\gamma>3
, implying that
K_{\textrm{max}}\sim k_{\textrm{max}}
, while
\theta\sim k_{\textrm{max}}^{3-\gamma}
for
\gamma<3
, implying that
K_{\textrm{max}}\sim k_{\textrm{max}}^{4-\gamma}
. Such a scaling of
K_{\textrm{max}}
is confirmed in the insets of Figure
2
for various values of
\gamma
and
f
.

In conclusion, the theoretical prediction of the degree exponent
\widetilde{\gamma}
holds in the infinite-size limit, but the degree distributions of finite-size STC networks exhibit a double-power law due to the fact that the degrees and the excess degrees in
\mathcal{G}_{0}
are bounded by
k_{\textrm{max}}
. This double-power law scaling of finite systems is also the reason why STC networks with backbone exponent
\gamma<3
– which are dense scale-free networks – do not violate graphicality constraints for scale-free degree distributions
Del Genio et al. 2011
. In fact, size-dependent cutoffs and crossovers soften the conditions for the graphicality of power-law degree sequences, see
Baek et al. 2012
;
Valigi et al. 2025
.

III
Degree correlations and network assortativity in STC random graphs

A salient feature of real-world networks is the presence of degree correlations. Specifically, if
Q(K_{1},K_{2})
is the probability that a uniformly randomly chosen link connects two nodes of degree
K_{1}
and
K_{2}
, the presence of degree correlations means that
Q(K_{1},K_{2})\neq Q(K_{1})Q(K_{2})
.
Here we discuss how degree correlations naturally arise in the STC model, and we provide a theory to quantify such correlations. Specifically we derive an exact expression for the Pearson correlation coefficient in infinite STC networks and show that its value is positive for any
f>0
, i.e., the STC mechanism always produces assortativity, regardless of the initial degree distribution
p(k)
. In the rest of this section we will often refer to the moments of this distribution, so for simplicity we denote these moments by
\mu_{m}=\langle k^{m}\rangle
.

III.1
The Pearson correlation coefficient

A commonly used global measure of nearest-neighbor degree-degree correlations in networks is the Pearson coefficient
r
(see Ref.
Newman 2018
), which is the covariance of link end-node degrees normalized so that it falls in the range
r\in[-1,1]
:

\displaystyle r=\frac{\mathrm{Cov}(K_{1},K_{2})}{\mathrm{Var}(\rightarrow K)},

(4)

where the covariance is over all links in
\mathcal{G}_{f}
, in both directions, and
\mathrm{Var}(\rightarrow K)
is the variance of link end-node degrees in
\mathcal{G}_{f}
, over all links in both directions. Here we derive
r
in general and show that it is a function of only
f
and the first four moments of the degree distribution
p(k)
of
\mathcal{G}_{0}
.

Computation of
\mathrm{Var}(\rightarrow K)

The variance in the denominator is straightforward to calculate, given that we know the generating function
G_{0}(z)
of the degree distribution of
\mathcal{G}_{f}
, see Eq. (
2
). We can write

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}(\rightarrow K)

\displaystyle=\langle\rightarrow K^{2}\rangle-\langle\rightarrow K\rangle^{2}=\frac{\langle K\rangle\langle K^{3}\rangle-\langle K^{2}\rangle^{2}}{\langle K\rangle^{2}},

(5)

where
\langle\rightarrow K\rangle
and
\langle\rightarrow K^{2}\rangle
are the first two moments of the distribution of link end-node degrees in
\mathcal{G}_{f}
, and
\langle K\rangle
,
\langle K^{2}\rangle
and
\langle K^{3}\rangle
are the first three moments of the degree distribution of
\mathcal{G}_{f}
. We can express these three moments using the first three derivatives of
G_{0}(z)
evaluated at
z=1
, and we find that they can be written in the form

\displaystyle\langle K^{n}\rangle=\sum_{m=0}^{n}A_{n,m}f^{m},

(6)

where the coefficient
A_{n,m}
is an expression (a Laurent polynomial) of only
\mu_{1},\ldots,\mu_{n+1}
. Thus
\mathrm{Var}(\rightarrow K)
is a function of
f,\mu_{1},\mu_{2},\mu_{3},\mu_{4}
and can be easily evaluated once we know the coefficients
A_{n,m}
(see Appendix
A.1
for details).

Computation of
\mathrm{Cov}(K_{1},K_{2})

The covariance in the numerator of Eq. (
4
) may be most transparently expressed as

\displaystyle\mathrm{Cov}(K_{1},K_{2})=\langle K_{1}K_{2}\rangle_{Q(K_{1},K_{2})}-\langle\rightarrow K\rangle^{2},

(7)

where
\langle.\rangle_{Q(K_{1},K_{2})}
is an average over the joint degree-degree distribution
Q(K_{1},K_{2})
of
\mathcal{G}_{f}
. Although this joint distribution cannot be written explicitly, its generating function can be derived and the average in Eq. (
7
) evaluated. This derivation is rather involved, however, therefore we do not present it here. Fortunately there is a considerably simpler approach to express the covariance in Eq. (
4
), by expanding it in terms of conditional covariances.

The law of total covariance states that

\displaystyle\mathrm{Cov}(X,Y)=\langle\mathrm{Cov}_{(X,Y)|Z}(X,Y)\rangle_{Z}+\mathrm{Cov}_{Z}(\langle X\rangle_{(X,Y)|Z},\langle Y\rangle_{(X,Y)|Z}),

where
\langle.\rangle_{Z}
means averaging over the distribution of
Z
,
\langle.\rangle_{(X,Y)|Z}
means averaging over the joint distribution of
(X,Y)
, given
Z
and
\mathrm{Cov}_{(X,Y)|Z}(X,Y)
is the covariance of
X
and
Y
, over the joint distribution of
(X,Y)
, given
Z
. Let
\mathcal{T}
denote the type of link: “old” or “new”. The fraction of new links (see Ref.
Cirigliano et al. 2024
) is given by

\displaystyle\phi=\frac{f\left(\langle k^{2}\rangle-\langle k\rangle\right)}{f\left(\langle k^{2}\rangle-\langle k\rangle\right)+\langle k\rangle}.

Then the covariance we are looking for is

\displaystyle\mathrm{Cov}(K_{1},K_{2})

\displaystyle=\langle\mathrm{Cov}_{(K_{1},K_{2})|\mathcal{T}}(K_{1},K_{2})\rangle_{\mathcal{T}}+\mathrm{Cov}_{\mathcal{T}}(\langle\rightarrow K_{1}\rangle_{(K_{1},K_{2})|\mathcal{T}},\langle\rightarrow K_{2}\rangle_{(K_{1},K_{2})|\mathcal{T}})

\displaystyle=\phi\,\mathrm{Cov}_{(K_{1},K_{2})|\mathrm{new}}(K_{1},K_{2})+(1-\phi)\,\mathrm{Cov}_{(K_{1},K_{2})|\mathrm{old}}(K_{1},K_{2})+\mathrm{Var}_{\mathcal{T}}(\langle\rightarrow K\rangle_{(K_{1},K_{2})|\mathcal{T}}),

(8)

where
\langle\rightarrow K\rangle_{(K_{1},K_{2})|\mathcal{T}}
is the mean end-node degree (over all links, both directions) of links of type
\mathcal{T}
, and
\mathrm{Var}_{\mathcal{T}}(\langle\rightarrow K\rangle_{(K_{1},K_{2})|\mathcal{T}})
is the variance of that number over the two link types. All three terms in Eq. (
III.1
) can be explicitly written without direct knowledge of
Q(K_{1},K_{2})
or its generating function.

Computing the term
\mathrm{Cov}_{(K_{1},K_{2})|\mathrm{old}}(K_{1},K_{2})
relies only on knowledge of the joint distribution of the original degrees
k_{1}
and
k_{2}
of an old link, and the average of the final degree of an end node of an old link of degrees
k_{1}
and
k_{2}
. The end result is a second order polynomial in
f
, whose coefficients are expressions of
\mu_{1},\mu_{2},\mu_{3}
. See Appendix
A.2
for details of the derivation.

Computing the term
\mathrm{Cov}_{(K_{1},K_{2})|\mathrm{new}}(K_{1},K_{2})
is a similar process, and relies only on knowledge of the joint distribution of the original degrees
k_{1}
,
q
and
k_{2}
of an old triad, and the average of the final degree of an end node of a new link made from an old triad of degrees
k_{1}
,
q
and
k_{2}
. The end result is a second order polynomial in
f
, whose coefficients are expressions of
\mu_{1},\mu_{2},\mu_{3},\mu_{4}
. See Appendix
A.3
for details of the derivation.

Finally, computing the “type-mixing” term
\mathrm{Var}_{\mathcal{T}}(\langle\rightarrow K\rangle_{(K_{1},K_{2})|\mathcal{T}})
involves finding the first two moments of the distribution of final degrees of a node of original degree
k
. See Appendix
A.4
for details of the derivation.

Substituting everything into Eq. (
4
), the Pearson coefficient can be written as a rational function,

\displaystyle r=\frac{n_{4}f^{4}+n_{3}f^{3}+n_{2}f^{2}+n_{1}f}{d_{4}f^{4}+d_{3}f^{3}+d_{2}f^{2}+d_{1}f+d_{0}},

(9)

where the
n_{i}
and
d_{i}
are polynomials of the moments
\mu_{1}
,
\mu_{2}
,
\mu_{3}
and
\mu_{4}
(see Appendix
A.5
for the final expressions).

III.1.1
Positivity of the Pearson coefficient

The Pearson coefficient is defined when the denominator in Eq. (
4
) is positive, which is always true apart from the pathological cases where
\mathcal{G}_{0}
is degree-regular and
f=0
or
f=1
.
The sign of the Pearson coefficient is determined by the sign of the covariance in the numerator of Eq. (
4
). In the expansion of this covariance, Eq. (
III.1
), all three terms reduce to variances (see Appendix
A
for details), and are therefore strictly non-negative. The first two terms, the covariances for old and new links, are only zero when
\mathcal{G}_{0}
is degree-regular. But in this case, the third, “type-mixing” variance term is strictly positive, whenever
0<f<1
.

To conclude, the STC mechanism produces strictly assortative degree-degree correlations, whenever the Pearson coefficient is defined.
Qualitatively this may be understood as follows. Degree-degree correlations in the STC mechanism arise from the fact that both final end-node degrees of either an old or new link depend on the same old degrees (of end nodes of an old link or participant nodes of an old triad) in a positively correlated manner. Thus the mechanism that tends to produce a high degree for one end node of a link will also tend to produce a high degree for the other end node. Even in the case of a degree-regular original network, when end-node degrees are uncorrelated for old and new links separately, we still get assortative correlations overall, because the typical degree of nodes connected by old links will be different from that of new links [the third term in Eq. (
III.1
)].

III.1.2
Behavior for small
f

When all four moments
\mu_{1},\mu_{2},\mu_{3},\mu_{4}
are finite, we expand the Pearson coefficient in powers of
f
and find that for small
f
its behavior is given by

\displaystyle r

\displaystyle=\frac{n_{1}}{d_{0}}f+\mathcal{O}(f^{2})=\left[2+\frac{\mu_{1}\mu_{2}-\mu_{1}^{2}}{\mu_{1}\mu_{3}-\mu_{2}^{2}}\right]f+\mathcal{O}(f^{2}),

(10)

where we assumed
d_{0}\neq 0
, which is fulfilled for all but perfectly degree-regular networks.
Since
\mu_{2}\geq\mu_{1}
and
\mu_{1}\mu_{3}\geq\mu_{2}^{2}
for any degree distribution, the slope of
r
at
f=0
must be in the range
[2,\infty]
. Specifically, the slope diverges as the original network approaches a degree-regular network. The other extreme, a slope of
2
, is approached for heavy-tailed degree distributions. Below we deal with these two extreme cases in more detail separately.

III.1.3
Random regular network backbones

Let us consider the special case of random regular networks, with all nodes in
\mathcal{G}_{0}
having degree
c
. As noted earlier, Eq. (
10
) does not apply in this case. However, we now have
\mu_{m}=c^{m}
, so the full expression for the Pearson coefficient simplifies considerably,

\displaystyle r_{\textrm{RR}}(f,c)=\frac{1-f}{(c^{3}-2c^{2}+1)f^{2}+2(c^{2}-c-1)f+(c+1)},

(11)

which is confirmed in numerical simulations, see Figure
3
(a).

As previously noted,
r
is not defined for
f=0

f\to 0
exists,

\displaystyle\lim_{f\to 0}r_{\textrm{RR}}(f,c)=\frac{1}{c+1},

Interestingly, the Pearson coefficient for random regular networks decreases with
f
near
f=0
, contrary to what happens for any other, non-degenerate, degree distribution

[see Eq. (
10
), and the example where
p(k)
is a Poisson distribution, Figure
3
(b)].

The case of
f=1
is more subtle. The limiting behavior is

\displaystyle\lim_{f\to 1}r_{\textrm{RR}}(f,c)=0,

which is verified by simulations in Figure
3
(a). Exactly at
f=1
the Pearson coefficient is undefined if we assume that every node in
\mathcal{G}_{0}
has a perfect regular tree neighborhood up to second nearest neighbors. This is not the case in random regular networks, however: in the infinite size limit the expected number of short loops converges to a finite number. This means that a small disruption to the perfect regularity is expected in infinite random regular networks, meaning that the Pearson coefficient at
f=1
is actually defined but has strong fluctuations. In this case
r_{\textrm{RR}}(f,c)
is a random variable whose distribution converges to a limit distribution in the infinite size limit, depending only on
c
, with a well-defined nonzero variance.

III.1.4
Erdős-Rényi network backbones

When the original network
\mathcal{G}_{0}
is Erdős-Rényi, i.e., has a Poisson degree distribution with mean degree
c
, the expression for the Pearson coefficient of
\mathcal{G}_{f}
reduces to a relatively simple rational function,

\displaystyle r_{\textrm{ER}}(f,c)=\frac{c^{2}f^{4}+(2c^{2}+c)f^{3}+4cf^{2}+3f}{c^{4}f^{4}+(5c^{3}+c^{2})f^{3}+(8c^{2}+2c)f^{2}+(5c+2)f+1}.

(12)

Figure
3
(b)-(c) confirms that this prediction coincides with simulation results. From Eq. (
12
) we see that the Pearson coefficient has well-defined limits when
c\to 0
and
c\to\infty
,

\displaystyle\lim_{c\to 0}r_{\textrm{ER}}(f,c)=\frac{3f}{2f+1},\quad\quad\lim_{c\to\infty}r_{\textrm{ER}}(f,c)=0,

and that
r_{\textrm{ER}}(f,c)
is monotonically decreasing in
c
for any
f
. Interestingly, we find that the behavior for small
f
, according to Eq. (
10
), is universal when the original network is Erdős-Rényi, i.e.,

\displaystyle r_{\textrm{ER}}(f,c)=3f+\mathcal{O}(f^{2}),

independent of the mean degree.

III.1.5
Power-law backbone networks

When the network
\mathcal{G}_{0}
has a power-law degree distribution,
p(k)=Ak^{-\gamma}
for
k_{\textrm{min}}\leq k\leq k_{\textrm{max}}
, we observe two sharp transitions in the Pearson coefficient, in the infinite size limit (when
k_{\textrm{max}}\to\infty
), as a function of
\gamma
. Specifically
r
undergoes a discontinuous transition, jumping from
r=0
to
r=1
at
\gamma=8/3
, and a continuous transition changing from
r=1
to
r<1
at
\gamma=5
. The source of this behavior is the fact that the dominant terms in the numerator and denominator of the expression in Eq. (
9
) change depending on the value of
\gamma
, see Appendix
A.6
for details.
The behavior of
r
in the limit
k_{\textrm{max}}\to\infty
can be summerized as follows,

\displaystyle 2<\gamma<8/3:

\displaystyle r_{\textrm{PL}}(f,\gamma,k_{\textrm{min}})=0,

(13)

\displaystyle\gamma=8/3:

\displaystyle 0<r_{\textrm{PL}}(f,\gamma,k_{\textrm{min}})<1,

(14)

\displaystyle 8/3<\gamma\leq 5:

\displaystyle r_{\textrm{PL}}(f,\gamma,k_{\textrm{min}})=1,

(15)

\displaystyle 5<\gamma:

\displaystyle 0<r_{\textrm{PL}}(f,\gamma,k_{\textrm{min}})<1,

(16)

\displaystyle\gamma\to\infty:

\displaystyle r_{\textrm{PL}}(f<1,\gamma,k_{\textrm{min}})=r_{\textrm{RR}}(f<1,k_{\textrm{min}})

(17)

\displaystyle r_{\textrm{PL}}(f=1,\gamma,k_{\textrm{min}})=\frac{3k_{\textrm{min}}}{k_{\textrm{min}}^{3}+k_{\textrm{min}}^{2}+1}.

(18)

This behavior, presented in Figure
4
(a)-(c), is conceptually similar to that of the transitivity as a function of
\gamma
, in power-law STC networks
Cirigliano et al. 2024
.

The limit
\gamma\to\infty
requires special attention. In this limit a random network of degree distribution
P(k)=Ak^{-\gamma}
converges to a random regular network of degree parameter
k_{\textrm{min}}
, therefore

\displaystyle\lim_{\gamma\to\infty}r_{\textrm{PL}}(f,\gamma,k_{\textrm{min}})=r_{\textrm{RR}}(f,k_{\textrm{min}}),

for any
f<0
. This is true even in the limit
f\to 1
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\lim_{f\to 1}\lim_{\gamma\to\infty}r_{\textrm{PL}}(f,\gamma,k_{\textrm{min}})=\lim_{f\to 1}r_{\textrm{RR}}(f,k_{\textrm{min}})=0.

However, setting
f=1
first and then taking the limit
\gamma\to\infty
results in a positive value, see Figure
4
(c). A simple approach to finding this limit is to substitute the degree distribution
p(k)=Ak^{-\gamma}
with a bimodal degree distribution on two possible degrees,
k_{\textrm{min}}
and
k_{\textrm{min}}+1
. Specifically,

\displaystyle p_{\mathrm{BM}}(k)=\begin{cases}k_{\textrm{min}}&\text{with probability }\rho=k_{\textrm{min}}^{-\gamma}/\left[k_{\textrm{min}}^{-\gamma}+(k_{\textrm{min}}+1)^{-\gamma}\right]\\
k_{\textrm{min}}+1&\text{with probability }1-\rho.\end{cases}

The distribution
p_{\mathrm{BM}}(k)
converges to
p(k)
for
\gamma\to\infty
, because the relative weight that
p(k)
places on degrees
k>k_{\textrm{min}}+1
vanishes as
\gamma\to\infty
. We can evaluate the first four moments of
p_{\mathrm{BM}}(k)
and substitute into Eq. (
9
) with
f=1
. Then taking the limit
\rho\to 1
, which corresponds to the limit
\gamma\to\infty
, we get Eq. (
18
).
This discontinuous behavior of
r_{\textrm{PL}}
, with two different limits, is shown in Figure
4
(d) where the Pearson coefficient is plotted for large values of
\gamma
. The curves for
f=0.1
,
f=0.4
and
f=0.99
approach the limits predicted by Eq. (
17
). The curve for
f=1
appears to almost coincide with the curve for
f=0.99
up to approximately
\gamma\approx 7
, but then converges to the value predicted by Eq. (
18
).

III.2
Average nearest-neighbors degree

To further characterize the mixing patterns in the STC networks, we analyze the average nearest neighbor degree
K_{nn}(K)
, defined as the mean degree of neighbors of nodes with degree
K

Pastor-Satorras et al. 2001
. This quantity can be computed from the conditional probability
P(K^{\prime}|K)
that a node of degree
K
is connected to a node of degree
K^{\prime}

K_{nn}(K)=\sum_{K^{\prime}}K^{\prime}\,P(k^{\prime}|K).

(19)

Note that, by definition, we have

\langle K_{nn}(K)\rangle_{Q(K)}=\langle K\rangle_{Q(K)}=\langle K^{2}\rangle/\langle K\rangle,

(20)

where
\langle\cdot\rangle
denotes the averages with respect to the degree distribution
P(K)
, and
\langle\cdot\rangle_{Q(K)}
denotes the averages with respect to
Q(K)=KP(K)/\langle K\rangle
. For uncorrelated networks,
P(K^{\prime}|K)=Q(K)
is independent of
K
, yielding a constant
K_{nn}(K)=\langle K^{2}\rangle/\langle K\rangle
. Deviations from this behavior signal the presence of degree-degree correlations: an increasing
K_{nn}(K)
with
K
indicates assortative mixing, where high-degree nodes preferentially connect to other high-degree nodes, while a decreasing trend indicates disassortative behavior
Newman 2002
. We can derive an elegant relation between the Pearson coefficient and
K_{nn}(K)
. From Eq. (
4
), writing
Q(K^{\prime},K)=Q(K)P(K^{\prime}|K)
and using Eq. (
19
), we have

r=\frac{\langle K^{\prime}K\rangle_{Q(K^{\prime},K)}-\langle K\rangle_{Q(K)}^{2}}{\langle K^{2}\rangle_{Q(K)}-\langle K\rangle_{Q(K)}^{2}}=\frac{\sum_{K}KQ(K)\sum_{K^{\prime}}K^{\prime}P(K^{\prime}|K)-\langle K\rangle_{Q(K)}^{2}}{\langle K^{2}\rangle_{Q(K)}-\langle K\rangle_{Q(K)}^{2}}=\frac{\langle K_{nn}(K)K\rangle_{Q(K)}-\langle K\rangle_{Q(K)}^{2}}{\langle K^{2}\rangle_{Q(K)}-\langle K\rangle_{Q(K)}^{2}}.

(21)

Thus, the Pearson coefficient
r
is related to a weighted average of
K_{nn}(K)
. This in turn implies that
K_{nn}(K)
contains more information on the mixing patterns in a network than the Pearson coefficient: the price to pay for a larger amount of information is that we have no analytical expressions for
K_{nn}(K)
. However, we can compute
K_{nn}(K)
numerically from simulations of synthetic STC networks. Results for PL backbones with various values of the exponent
\gamma
and
f
are presented in Figure.

K_{nn}(K)
for

, mirroring the double power-law scaling observed in the degree distribution, see Figure

(22)

which grows with the system size as soon as

IV
The clustering spectrum in STC random graphs

The clustering is a measure of how likely nodes form triangles, hence it contains information on the loop structure of the networks. Many possible ways of quantifying the clustering in a network can be defined. Two standard choices are the following. A global measure for the clustering is the ratio between the average number of triangles and the average number of triads, called transitivity, or global clustering coefficient. This is defined as
Latora et al. 2017

T=\frac{3\langle N_{\triangle}\rangle}{\langle N_{\wedge}\rangle}.

(23)

The factor
3
serves to normalize
T
, since each triangle contains three distinct triads. A local measure of the clustering of a given node
i
can be defined as the number of triangles to which
i
belongs divided by the number of triads to which
i
belongs,
C_{i}=n_{\triangle,i}/n_{\wedge,i}

Latora et al. 2017
. From this quantity, the average local clustering coefficient is obtained

\bar{C}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}C_{i}.

(24)

Note that in general
T\neq\bar{C}
, as we can interpret
T
as a weighted average of the local clustering coefficient
C_{i}
with weights given by
n_{\wedge,i}

Latora et al. 2017
. Since a node of degree
K_{i}
belongs to
K_{i}(K_{i}-1)/2
triads, we can condition on the degrees
K
and define the clustering spectrum

C(K)=\frac{2\mathbb{E}\left[n_{\triangle}|K\right]}{K(K-1)},

(25)

where
\mathbb{E}\left[n_{\triangle}|K\right]
is the average number of triangles to which a node of degree
K
belongs. From the clustering spectrum we can compute
\bar{C}=\sum_{K}P(K)C(K)
, in the large network limit. Much more information about the local network structure is encoded in the clustering spectrum. For this reason, it is in general much harder to find an expression for
C(K)
in terms of the network properties.

In
Cirigliano et al. 2024
we provided an exact expression for
T
in STC random graphs with an arbitrary locally treelike backbone, and we compared
T
with
\bar{C}
measured in numerical simulations on synthetic STC networks. Here we fill this conceptual gap. By exploiting the local treelikeness of the backbones
\mathcal{G}_{0}
, we provide exact formulae for the clustering spectra
C(K)
in STC random graphs.

Our task is to compute the conditional expectation
\mathbb{E}\left[n_{\triangle}|K\right]
in Eq. (
25
). To do so, we can condition also on the value of the initial degree
k

\mathbb{E}\left[n_{\triangle}|K\right]=\sum_{k}P(k|K)\mathbb{E}\left[n_{\triangle}|k,K\right]=\frac{1}{P(K)}\sum_{k}p(k)P(K|k)\mathbb{E}\left[n_{\triangle}|k,K\right].

(26)

Let us now work out explicitly the conditional expectation on the r.h.s.. Given
k
and
K
, let us consider all the possible realizations of the excess degrees
\{r_{i}\}_{i=1}^{k}
and of the triadic closures
\{\nu_{i}\}_{i=1}^{k}
, where
\sum_{i=1}^{k}\nu_{i}=K-k
is the number of new neighbors created by the STC process. Note that the
\nu_{i}
are binomial variables distributed according to
\mathcal{B}(\nu_{i}|f,r_{i})
, but we are constraining their sum to
K-k
. A node of original degree
k
and final degree
K
can be part of three types of triangles, depicted in Figure
6
. There are
fk(k-1)/2
triangles of type
\triangle_{1}
,
\sum_{i}\nu_{i}
triangles of type
\triangle_{2}
, and
f\sum_{i}\nu_{i}(\nu_{i}-1)/2
triangles of type
\triangle_{3}
. Thus we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[n_{\triangle}|k,K\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{\{r_{i}\}\{\nu_{i}\}}P(\{r_{i}\},\{\nu_{i}\}|k,K)\left[f\frac{k(k-1)}{2}+\sum_{i=1}^{k}\nu_{i}\,+f\sum_{i=1}^{k}\frac{\nu_{i}(\nu_{i}-1)}{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{fk(k-1)}{2}+K-k+\frac{f}{2}\sum_{\{\nu_{i}\}}P(\{\nu_{i}\}|k,K)\sum_{i=1}^{k}\nu_{i}(\nu_{i}-1),

(27)

where
\delta(x,y)
is the Kronecker’s delta, and using the definition of conditional probability we can write

P(\{\nu_{i}\}|k,K)=\sum_{\{r_{i}\}}P(\{r_{i}\},\{\nu_{i}\}|k,K)=\frac{\sum_{\{r_{i}\}}P(\{r_{i}\},\{\nu_{i}\},K|k)}{P(K|k)},

(28)

where, since the backbone is uncorrelated and locally treelike,

P(\{r_{i}\},\{\nu_{i}\},K|k)=\prod_{i=1}^{k}q(r_{i})\mathcal{B}(\nu_{i}|r_{i},f)\delta\left(K,k+\sum_{j=1}^{k}\nu_{j}\right),

(29)

In general, the last term on the r.h.s. of Eq. (
27
) can be evaluated via Monte Carlo sampling of the variables
k,\{r_{i}\},\{\nu_{i}\}
, as well as for
P(K)
and
P(K|k)
. Using Eq. (
26
) and (
25
) we then get the local clustering spectrum
C(K)
. Remarkably, as shown below, for some specific choices of the backbone degree distributions
p(k)
it is possible to obtain some simpler expressions, and to extract the asymptotic scaling of
C(K)
for large
K
.

IV.1
Random regular backbones

For RR backbones with connectivity
c
, whose degree distribution is
p(k)=\delta(k,c)
, explicit expressions can be obtained for the clustering spectrum
C(K)
.
Let us consider the conditional distribution
P(\{n_{i}\}|k,K)
as in Eq. (
28
). The sum over the excess degrees
\{r_{i}\}
is straightforward since
q(r)=\delta(r,c-1)
. The numerator gives

\displaystyle P(\{\nu_{i}\},K|k)

\displaystyle=\prod_{i=1}^{k}B(\nu_{i}|c-1,f)\delta\left(K,k+\sum_{j=1}^{k}\nu_{j}\right)

\displaystyle=\left[\prod_{i=1}^{k}{c-1\choose\nu_{i}}\right]f^{K-k}(1-f)^{k(c-1)-(K-k)}\delta\left(K,k+\sum_{j=1}^{k}\nu_{j}\right),

(30)

while the denominator is simply given by

P(K|k)={k(c-1)\choose K-k}f^{K-k}(1-f)^{k(c-1)-(K-k)},

(31)

because the variable
K-k
is the sum of
k
independent binomial random variables
\nu_{i}
, thus
S=K-k\sim\mathcal{B}(S|k(c-1),f)
. Putting these two pieces together we have

P(\{\nu_{i}\}|k,K)=\frac{\left[\prod_{i=1}^{k}{c-1\choose\nu_{i}}\right]\delta\left(K,k+\sum_{j=1}^{k}\nu_{j}\right)}{{k(c-1)\choose K-k}},

(32)

which is a multivariate hypergeometric distribution
Feller 1991
. Over this distribution, we have

\mathbb{E}[\nu_{i}(\nu_{i}-1)]=\frac{(K-k)(K-k-1)(c-2)}{k[k(c-1)-1]},

(33)

from which we can finally compute the clustering spectrum, using
P(k|K)=p(k)=\delta(k,c)
,

C(K)=\frac{1}{K(K-1)}\left[fc(c-1)+2(K-c)+\frac{f(K-c)(K-c-1)(c-2)}{c(c-1)-1}\right].

(34)

We recall that for RR backbones with finite
c
the final degree
K
is bounded by
c\leq K\leq c(c-1)
. Furthermore, for
f\to 1
we have
P(K)\to\delta(K,c(c-1))
, thus
C(K)\to C(c(c-1))\delta(K,c(c-1))
. Results of numerical simulations on synthetic RR networks reported in Figure
7
(a) show a perfect agreement with Eq. (
34
). Interestingly,
C(K)
can be monotone or not depending on the value of
f
.

IV.2
Erdős-Rényi backbones

The case of ER backbones, with a Poisson degree distribution
p(k)=e^{-c}c^{k}/k!
, is more complicated then the RR, yet analytical expressions for the clustering spectrum can be obtained. Let us focus on the conditional distribution
P(\{\nu_{i}\}|k,K)
as in Eq. (
28
). Let us start from the numerator

\sum_{\{r_{i}\}}P(\{r_{i}\},\{\nu_{i}\},K|k)=\sum_{\{r_{i}\}}\prod_{i=1}^{k}q(r_{i})\mathcal{B}(\nu_{i}|r_{i},f)\delta\left(K,k+\sum_{j=1}^{k}\nu_{j}\right),

(35)

for the case of Poisson distribution
q(r)=e^{-c}c^{r}/r!
. The sum over the random variables
\{r_{i}\}
gives, for each of the
i
-th terms,

\sum_{r\geq\nu}q(r){r\choose\nu}f^{\nu}(1-f)^{r-\nu}=f^{\nu}\sum_{r\geq\nu}\frac{e^{-c}c^{r}}{r!}\frac{r!(1-f)^{r-\nu}}{\nu!(r-\nu)!}=\frac{(cf)^{\nu}}{\nu!}e^{-c}\sum_{r\geq\nu}\frac{[(1-f)c]^{r-\nu}}{(r-\nu)!}=\frac{(cf)^{\nu}}{\nu!}e^{-fc}.

In other words, the convolution
\sum_{r}q(r)\mathcal{B}(\nu|r,f)
when
q(r)
is Poisson with average
c
, is a Poisson with average
fc
. Thus the variables
\nu_{i}
are independent Poisson variables with average
fc
. The numerator then writes

\sum_{\{r_{i}\}}P(\{r_{i}\},\{\nu_{i}\},K|k)=\prod_{i=1}^{k}\frac{e^{-fc}(fc)^{\nu_{i}}}{\nu_{i}!}\delta\left(K,k+\sum_{i}\nu_{i}\right).

(36)

Coming back to the denominator in Eq. (
28
),
P(K|k)
, the variable
K-k
, given
k
, is the sum of Poisson variables
K-k=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\nu_{i}
, thus it is Poisson with mean
kfc
. We can conclude that
P(K|k)
follows a shifted Poisson distribution with mean
kfc
. Putting these two pieces together we get

P(\{\nu_{i}\}|k,K)=\frac{\prod_{i=1}^{k}\frac{e^{-fc}(fc)^{\nu_{i}}}{\nu_{i}!}\delta\left(K,k+\sum_{i}\nu_{i}\right)}{\frac{e^{kfc}(kfc)^{K-k}}{(K-k)!}}=\frac{(K-k)!}{\prod_{i=1}^{k}\nu_{i}!k^{\nu_{i}}}\delta\left(K,k+\sum_{i}\nu_{i}\right),

(37)

where we recognize a multinomial distribution
Feller 1991
with all the probabilities being
1/k
and the sum constrained to
K-k
. We can thus compute the expectation

\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{k}n_{i}(n_{i}-1)\right]=k\mathbb{E}\left[n(n-1)\right]=k\frac{(K-k)(K-k-1)}{k^{2}}=\frac{(K-k)(K-k-1)}{k}.

(38)

Putting together all these pieces, we have

C(K)=\frac{1}{K(K-1)}\sum_{k}\frac{p(k)p(K|k)}{\sum_{s}p(s)p(K|s)}\left[fk(k-1)+2(K-k)+f\frac{(K-k)(K-k-1)}{k}\right],

(39)

where
p(k)=e^{-c}c^{k}/k!
and
p(K|k)=e^{-kfc}(kfc)^{(K-k)}/(K-k)!
. This average can be easily evaluated numerically. The results are shown in Figure
7

IV.3
Power-law backbones

For STC graphs obtained from a PL backbone, we cannot obtain an analytical expression for

. However, we can numerically compute the sum in Eq. (

C(K)
does not decay to zero for
K\gg 1
. In fact, it approaches a constant value
C(K)\sim f
for
K\to\infty
. This in turn implies that the hubs in
\mathcal{G}_{f}
, i.e., nodes with
K\sim K^{*}\gg 1
, are essentially surrounded by a clique – more precisely, a a partial clique depending on the value of
f
. While we don’t have a theoretical prediction for such a scaling of
C(K)
, one heuristic argument is the following. Consider a node
A
with finite degree in
\mathcal{G}_{0}
which is a first neighbor of a hub in
\mathcal{G}_{0}
, see Figure
9
. Such a node has a degree
K_{A}\sim k+fk_{\textrm{max}}\leq K^{*}
in
\mathcal{G}_{f}
. The number of triads formed by the original neighbors of node
A
and the original hub’s neighbors, which we call triads of type I, is approximately
kfk_{\textrm{max}}
, and these triads are all open. The number of triads formed by node
A
and two of the original hub’s neighbors, which we call triads of type II, is approximately
(fk_{\textrm{max}})^{2}
. By construction, a fraction
f
of these triads is closed. Since the number of triads around node
A
in
\mathcal{G}_{f}
scales as
K_{A}^{2}\sim(fk_{\textrm{max}})^{2}
, in the infinite-size limit triads of type II dominate, which in turn implies
C(K)\to f
. In other words, not only do nodes which are neighbors of a hub in
\mathcal{G}_{0}
become hubs themselves in
\mathcal{G}_{f}
, but they are also surrounded by (partial) cliques. What happens instead if we consider a node
B
which is a hub in
\mathcal{G}_{0}
? Its final degree will be
K_{B}\sim k_{\textrm{max}}(1+f\langle r\rangle)>K^{*}
. The number of triads formed by
B
and its original neighbors is
k_{\textrm{max}}^{2}
, and a fraction
f
of such triads is closed. However, the triads formed by
B
’s first neighbors and
B
’s second neighbors in
\mathcal{G}_{0}
is approximately
f\langle k\rangle k_{\textrm{max}}^{2}
. Similarly, the number of triads formed by two
B
’s second neighbors is approximately
(k_{\textrm{max}}f\langle k\rangle)^{2}
. These triads are always open by construction. Thus we can conclude that the majority of triads in
\mathcal{G}_{f}
around nodes of degree
K>K^{*}
are open. This explains the rapid dropoff above
K^{*}
observed in Figure
8
, and why
C(K)\to f
for
K\gg 1
in the infinite-size limit.

V
Discussion

In this work we have examined the assortativity and local clustering properties of the Static Triadic Closure (STC) random network model
Cirigliano et al. 2024
, in which a clustered network is created via a triadic closure process on a backbone network. We consider the case of a locally treelike backbones, in which triads are then closed with probability
f
to give the final network.
This allows us to obtain analytic expressions for the Pearson correlation coefficient and local clustering spectrum, exact in the infinite-size limit. These results were validated with extensive numerical simulations, which also allowed us to characterize the finite-size effects.

The Pearson coefficient can be written as a function of the first four moments of the backbone degree distribution. Based on this general analysis, we give exact expressions for random regular, Erdős-Rényi, and power-law degree distributed backbone networks.

We found that the triadic closure process introduces assortative degree correlations, such that starting from an uncorrelated random network backbone, the final network (after triadic closure) always has positive correlations. Furthermore, this effect increases with the fraction of triads closed, resulting in a direct positive correlation between degree assortativity and global clustering or transitivity.

A similar correlation can be observed in real-world networks, where larger assortative correlations tend to be associated with higher transitivity, such as typically found in social networks
Newman 2002
;
Vasques Filho and O’Neale 2020
. Of course there are many other factors at play, leading to variability in assortativity, and in some cases negative correlations. Nevertheless, the positive correlations introduced naturally by triadic closure should be considered as a ‘baseline’.

In the case of power-law degree distributions, the divergence of different moments of the backbone degree distribution leads to dramatic transitions in the value of the Pearson coefficient, and in particular a value tending to 1 in the large size limit for any finite value of
f
when the exponent
\gamma
is between
8/3
and
5
. This behavior mirrors similar patterns seen in the transitivity in STC networks with powerlaw backbones
Cirigliano et al. 2024
.
The mixing patterns in the network can also be characterized by the average nearest-neighbor degree (as a function of degree). We show that the Pearson coefficient can be written in terms of a weighted average of the average nearest-neighbor degree. Again, we find, through asymptotically exact expressions non-trivial behavior, particularly with power-law distributed backbones.

We also find asymptotically exact formulae for the clustering spectrum which contain summations over degree distributions. For the three cases studied these can easily be numerically evaluated.
We show that the spectrum has nontrivial properties even for compact backbone degree distributions.
In the case of Erdős-Rényi backbone networks, the resulting clustering spectrum is strikingly power-law like, decaying with an exponent close to
-0.8
, followed by a slower asymptotic decay for extremely large values of the degree.
When starting from a power-law distributed backbone, the clustering spectrum is non-monotonic. There are also strong finite size effects, with a sharp dropoff for degrees beyond the largest degree of the backbone network. For large degrees the spectrum tends to the constant value
f
.

Real world networks often have non-trivial clustering spectra. For instance, in networks with heavy-tailed degree distributions, clustering spectra exhibit heavy tailed decays
Stegehuis et al. 2017
.

The STC model efficiently produces highly random synthetic networks containing complex patterns of overlapping short cycles, local clustering and degree assortativity. Remarkably, we are able to analytically calculate many local structural properties of STC networks. These present varying patterns of these local properties, depending on the backbone network used. This is suggestive that more carefully tuned choices of backbone networks may be able to produce realistic patterns of a variety of global and local network structural properties, while retaining the tractability of STC networks.
This underlines the value of the STC random network model in creating reference network ensembles, allowing analytical treatment of complex structures well beyond classical treelike random network models.

We must compute the first three moments of the degree distribution of

\displaystyle\langle K\rangle

\displaystyle=G_{0}^{\prime}(1)

\displaystyle\langle K^{2}\rangle

\displaystyle=G_{0}^{\prime\prime}(1)+G_{0}^{\prime}(1)

\displaystyle\langle K^{3}\rangle

\displaystyle=G_{0}^{\prime\prime\prime}(1)+3G_{0}^{\prime\prime}(1)+G_{0}^{\prime}(1),

\displaystyle\langle K^{n}\rangle=\sum_{m=0}^{n}A_{n,m}f^{m},

where the coefficients for the first three orders are

\displaystyle A_{1,0}

\displaystyle=\mu_{1}

\displaystyle A_{1,1}

\displaystyle=\mu_{2}-\mu_{1}

\displaystyle A_{2,0}

\displaystyle=\mu_{2}

\displaystyle A_{2,1}

\displaystyle=\frac{2\mu_{2}^{2}-\mu_{1}\mu_{2}-\mu_{1}^{2}}{\mu_{1}}

\displaystyle A_{2,2}

\displaystyle=\frac{\mu_{1}^{3}+\mu_{1}^{2}\mu_{3}-3\mu_{1}\mu_{2}^{2}+\mu_{2}^{3}}{\mu_{1}^{2}}

\displaystyle=\mu_{3}

\displaystyle A_{3,2}

. Then we have

\displaystyle P(k,K)=p(k)P(K|k).

Mean final degree of node arrived at by following a random old link

The probability that a node arrived at by following a random old link has old degree
k
and final degree
K
is

\displaystyle\frac{kP(k,K)}{\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\sum_{K=1}^{\infty}kP(k,K)},

so the probability that a node arrived at by following a random old link has final degree
K
is

\displaystyle\frac{\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}kP(k,K)}{\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\sum_{K=1}^{\infty}kP(k,K)}.

Therefore the mean final degree of a node arrived at by following a random old link is

\displaystyle\langle\rightarrow K\rangle_{(K_{1},K_{2})|\textrm{old}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\sum_{K=1}^{\infty}KkP(k,K)}{\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\sum_{K=1}^{\infty}kP(k,K)}=\frac{\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\sum_{K=1}^{\infty}Kkp(k)P(K|k)}{\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\sum_{K=1}^{\infty}kp(k)P(K|k)}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}kp(k)\sum_{K=1}^{\infty}KP(K|k)}{\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}kp(k)\sum_{K=1}^{\infty}P(K|k)}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}kp(k)\langle K\rangle_{k}}{\langle k\rangle},

(52)

where
\langle K\rangle_{k}
is the mean final degree of nodes of old degree
k
and
\langle k\rangle
is the mean degree of the original network
\mathcal{G}_{0}
. Fortunately, for the STC mechanism this can be easily written as

\displaystyle\langle K\rangle_{k}=k(bf+1),

(53)

where
b=(\langle k^{2}\rangle-\langle k\rangle)/\langle k\rangle
is the mean branching in
\mathcal{G}_{0}
. Using Eq. (
53
) in Eq. (
A.4
) we obtain

\displaystyle\langle\rightarrow K\rangle_{(K_{1},K_{2})|\textrm{old}}=\frac{\mu_{2}}{\mu_{1}}+f\,\frac{\mu_{2}^{2}-\mu_{2}\mu_{1}}{\mu_{1}^{2}}.

(54)

Mean final degree of node arrived at by following a random new link

The probability that a node arrived at by following a random new link has old degree
k
and final degree
K
is

\displaystyle\frac{(K-k)P(k,K)}{\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\sum_{K=1}^{\infty}(K-k)P(k,K)},

so the probability that a node arrived at by following a random new link has final degree
K
is

\displaystyle\frac{\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}(K-k)P(k,K)}{\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\sum_{K=1}^{\infty}(K-k)P(k,K)}.

Therefore the mean final degree of a node arrived at by following a random new link is

\displaystyle\langle\rightarrow K\rangle_{(K_{1},K_{2})|\textrm{new}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\sum_{K=1}^{\infty}K(K-k)P(k,K)}{\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\sum_{K=1}^{\infty}(K-k)P(k,K)}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\sum_{K=1}^{\infty}K^{2}p(k)P(K|k)-\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\sum_{K=1}^{\infty}Kkp(k)P(K|k)}{\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\sum_{K=1}^{\infty}Kp(k)P(K|k)-\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\sum_{K=1}^{\infty}kp(k)P(K|k)}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}p(k)\sum_{K=1}^{\infty}K^{2}P(K|k)-\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}kp(k)\sum_{K=1}^{\infty}KP(K|k)}{\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}p(k)\sum_{K=1}^{\infty}KP(K|k)-\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}kp(k)\sum_{K=1}^{\infty}P(K|k)}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}p(k)\langle K^{2}\rangle_{k}-\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}kp(k)\langle K\rangle_{k}}{\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}p(k)\langle K\rangle_{k}-\langle k\rangle},

(55)

where
\langle K\rangle_{k}
is the mean final degree of nodes of old degree
k
[see Eq. (
53
) ] and
\langle K^{2}\rangle_{k}
is the second moment of the final degree distribution of nodes of old degree
k
.
To find
\langle K^{2}\rangle_{k}
let us write the final degree of a node of original degree
k
as
K=k+\sum_{i=1}^{k}\nu_{i}
, where the
\nu_{i}
are i.i.d. random variables whose distribution is generated by
g_{1}(1-f+fz)
. We can then write

\displaystyle K^{2}=\left(k+\sum_{i=1}^{k}\nu_{i}\right)^{2}=k^{2}+2k\sum_{i=1}^{k}\nu_{i}+\sum_{i=1}^{k}\nu_{i}^{2}+2\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}\sum_{j=i+1}^{k}\nu_{i}\nu_{j},

and so

\displaystyle\langle K^{2}\rangle_{k}=k^{2}+2k^{2}\langle\nu\rangle+k\langle\nu^{2}\rangle+(k^{2}-k)\langle\nu\rangle^{2}.

We have

\displaystyle\langle\nu\rangle

\displaystyle=fg_{1}^{\prime}(1)=f\frac{\langle k^{2}\rangle-\langle k\rangle}{\langle k\rangle}

\displaystyle\langle\nu^{2}\rangle

\displaystyle=f^{2}g_{1}^{\prime\prime}(1)+fg_{1}^{\prime}(1)=f^{2}\frac{\langle k^{3}\rangle-3\langle k^{2}\rangle+2\langle k\rangle}{\langle k\rangle}+f\frac{\langle k^{2}\rangle-\langle k\rangle}{\langle k\rangle},

so we obtain

\displaystyle\langle K^{2}\rangle_{k}

\displaystyle=k^{2}\left[1+2f\frac{\langle k^{2}\rangle-\langle k\rangle}{\langle k\rangle}+f^{2}\left(\frac{\langle k^{2}\rangle-\langle k\rangle}{\langle k\rangle}\right)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\quad+k\left[f^{2}\frac{\langle k^{3}\rangle-3\langle k^{2}\rangle+2\langle k\rangle}{\langle k\rangle}+f\frac{\langle k^{2}\rangle-\langle k\rangle}{\langle k\rangle}-f^{2}\left(\frac{\langle k^{2}\rangle-\langle k\rangle}{\langle k\rangle}\right)^{2}\right],

(56)

and thus

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}p(k)\langle K^{2}\rangle_{k}

\displaystyle=\langle k^{2}\rangle\left[1+2f\frac{\langle k^{2}\rangle-\langle k\rangle}{\langle k\rangle}+f^{2}\left(\frac{\langle k^{2}\rangle-\langle k\rangle}{\langle k\rangle}\right)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\quad+\langle k\rangle\left[f^{2}\frac{\langle k^{3}\rangle-3\langle k^{2}\rangle+2\langle k\rangle}{\langle k\rangle}+f\frac{\langle k^{2}\rangle-\langle k\rangle}{\langle k\rangle}-f^{2}\left(\frac{\langle k^{2}\rangle-\langle k\rangle}{\langle k\rangle}\right)^{2}\right],

(57)

Using Eq. (
A.4
) and Eq. (
53
) in Eq. (
A.4
) we obtain

\displaystyle\langle\rightarrow K\rangle_{(K_{1},K_{2})|\textrm{new}}=\frac{\mu_{2}+\mu_{1}}{\mu_{1}}+f\,\frac{\mu_{3}\mu_{1}^{2}+\mu_{2}^{3}-3\mu_{2}^{2}\mu_{1}+\mu_{1}^{3}}{\mu_{2}\mu_{1}^{2}-\mu_{1}^{3}}.

(58)

Constructing the full variance term

Finally we can write the full variance term that appears in Eq. (
III.1
),

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}_{\mathcal{T}}(\langle\rightarrow K\rangle_{(K_{1},K_{2})|\mathcal{T}})

\displaystyle=\phi\langle\rightarrow K\rangle_{(K_{1},K_{2})|\textrm{new}}^{2}+(1-\phi)\langle\rightarrow K\rangle_{(K_{1},K_{2})|\textrm{old}}^{2}

\displaystyle\quad-\left[\phi\langle\rightarrow K\rangle_{(K_{1},K_{2})|\textrm{new}}+(1-\phi)\langle\rightarrow K\rangle_{(K_{1},K_{2})|\textrm{old}}\right]^{2}

\displaystyle=\phi(1-\phi)\left(\langle\rightarrow K\rangle_{(K_{1},K_{2})|\textrm{new}}-\langle\rightarrow K\rangle_{(K_{1},K_{2})|\textrm{old}}\right)^{2}.

(59)

A.6
Pearson coefficient for power-law backbone networks in the infinite-size limit

We consider each region of
\gamma
separately and determine the dominant terms in the expression for the Pearson coefficient, when
k_{\textrm{max}}\to\infty
.

5<\gamma

In this range neither of the first four moments of the degree distribution diverge with
k_{\textrm{max}}
and the Pearson coefficient assumes a value
0<r<1
.

4<\gamma\leq 5

In this range only
\mu_{4}
diverges with
k_{\textrm{max}}
and the dominant terms in the numerator match the dominant terms in the denominator exactly, therefore, we have
r=1
.

3<\gamma\leq 4

In this range both
\mu_{3}
and
\mu_{4}
diverge with
k_{\textrm{max}}
but again the dominant terms in the numerator match the dominant terms in the denominator exactly, therefore, we still have
r=1
.

2<\gamma\leq 3

In this range
\mu_{2}
,
\mu_{3}
and
\mu_{4}
all diverge with
k_{\textrm{max}}
and we find that the dominant terms change at an intermediate value of
\gamma
. Specifically, for
\gamma>8/3
the terms
\mu_{4}\mu_{2}^{2}
and
\mu_{3}^{2}\mu_{2}
(which scale in the same way with
k_{\textrm{max}}
) dominate over the term
\mu_{3}\mu_{2}^{5}
, but for
\gamma<8/3
it is the latter that dominates. Thus we have
r=1
for
\gamma>8/3
and
r=0
for
\gamma<8/3
.

Appendix B
Asymptotic scaling of the clustering spectrum for ER networks

We want to find the large-
K
behavior
of the function
C(K)
given in Eq. (
39
). Let us write

\displaystyle C(K)=\frac{1}{K(K-1)}\sum_{k}\frac{\Pi_{K}(k)}{\sum_{s}\Pi_{K}(s)}\left[fk(k-1)+2(K-k)+f\frac{(K-k)(K-k-1)}{k}\right],

(61)

where, after some simplifications,

\displaystyle\Pi_{K}(k)=\frac{e^{-kfc}k^{K}}{k!(K-k)!}(kf)^{-k}.

(62)

Intuitively, for large
K
the distribution
Q_{K}(k)
is sharply peaked around it’s average value, because it is a product of very quickly varying functions of
k
. Let us take the logarithm of Eq. (
62
). We have, using Stirling formula for large
K
,

\displaystyle\log\Pi_{K}(k)

\displaystyle=-kfc+K\log(k)-k\log(kf)-\log(k!)-\log[(K-k)!]

\displaystyle\sim-kfc+K\log(k)-k\log(kf)-k\log(k)-(K-k)\log(K-k)+K

\displaystyle=-K\Phi(k/K),

(63)

where

\Phi(x)\equiv-1+x(fc+\log K+\log f)-\log(x)+2x\log x+(1-x)\log(1-x).

(64)

Let
k^{*}(K)=Kx^{*}
denote the maximum of
Q_{K}(k)
. Taking the derivative of
\Phi(x)
we get

\left.\frac{d\Phi(x)}{dx}\right|_{x*}=0\implies fc+\log K+\log f-\frac{1}{x^{*}}+2\log x^{*}+1-\log(1-x^{*})=0.

(65)

Setting
y=1/x^{*}
we get transcendental equation

y+\log(y)+\log(y-1)=1+fc+\log f+\log K\equiv A,

(66)

which can be solved numerically. The solution of this equation gives us the saddle point
k^{*}(K)=K/y
, from which we can compute
C(K)
as

C(K)\sim\frac{1}{K(K-1)}\left[fk^{*}(k^{*}-1)+2(K-k^{*})+f\frac{(K-k^{*})(K-k^{*}-1)}{k^{*}}\right].

(67)

Since we are looking at
K\gg 1
, and
A\sim\log K\gg 1
, the asymptotic behavior of
y
is given by
y\sim A-2\log y
, from which it follows, neglecting terms of order
\log\log K/\log K
,

y\sim A-2\log A.

(68)

With this we can thus compute
C(K)
for large
K
as

\displaystyle C(K)

\displaystyle\sim\frac{f}{y^{2}}\sim\frac{f}{[A-2\log A]^{2}},

(69)

where we recall that
A=1+fc+\log f+\log K
.
