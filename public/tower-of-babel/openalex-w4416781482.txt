Ion Jump Motion as the Background for Muon Diffusion
in Battery Materials Research Using µ SR
Ryosuke Kadono∗

arXiv:2511.21013v2 [cond-mat.mtrl-sci] 5 Feb 2026

Muon Science Laboratory, Institute of Materials Structure Science,
High Energy Accelerator Research Organization (KEK), Tsukuba, Ibaraki 305-0801, Japan
Numerical simulations of muon spin relaxation (µ SR) in ion diffusion were performed using the extended
Kubo-Toyabe (KT) relaxation function GEA
z (t) that incorporates an Edwards-Anderson type autocorrelation
function for the jump motion of ions. The analysis of the generated µ SR spectra using the conventional KT
function GKT
z (t; ∆KT , νKT ) (mimicking the previous analysis procedure) suggest that the anomalous peak in the
ﬂuctuation rate νKT around a speciﬁc temperature T ∗ and associated decrease of the linewidth ∆KT above T ∗ ,
often observed in the previous µ SR studies on ion diffusion, originate from the sharp increase in the ion jump
rate νi against that of the muon νµ with increasing temperature. This indicates that a more detailed reanalysis
of the vintage data using GEA
z (t) is useful for the proper evaluation of νi and νµ . Meanwhile, it also suggests
that the µ SR results showing no such anomaly convey little information on ion diffusion.

Muon spin rotation/relaxation (µ SR) has been used for investigating ion dynamics in various non-magnetic materials
including those for batteries in the past ﬁfteen years, where
nuclear magnetic moments carried by ions served as a marker
of their dynamics. Assuming that the muon itself remains
immobile in the time scale of µ SR measurements (∼101
µ s), the magnitude and ﬂuctuation frequency of random local ﬁelds B(t) from these magnetic moments are deduced
by analyzing µ SR spectra under zero or longitudinal magnetic ﬁeld (ZF/LF-µ SR) using the Kubo-Toyabe (KT) function, GKT
z (t; ∆KT , νKT ) (with ∆KT and νKT being the static
linewidth and its ﬂuctuation rate, repectively) [1], and νKT is
interpreted as the ion jump frequency νi [2–21].
However, a recently developed spin relaxation model based
on the insight that the time correlation of B(t) associated with
ion diffusion retains ﬁnite values even as t → ∞ has revealed
that the relaxation function predicted in such cases exhibits
behavior qualitatively different from that of the KT function
[22]. Speciﬁcally, the situation is described by introducing an
Edwards-Anderson type parameter Q to the autocorrelation
function,

Furthermore, it has been pointed out very recently that the activation energy Ei and prefactor νi0 of the Arrhenius equation,
νi = νi0 exp(−Ei /kB T ), deduced from such analyses deviate
signiﬁcantly from the common values expected for ion diffusion in nearly all cases [23].
Under these circumstances, a detailed reexamination of the
µ SR literature on ion diffusion has revealed striking anomalies: νKT exhibits the maximum at a speciﬁc temperature T ∗ ,
and this peak behavior is often accompanied by a signiﬁcant
reduction in ∆KT at higher temperatures (T & T ∗ ). Despite that
such behaviors are clearly inconsistent with the monotonous
increase of ion jumping frequency with temperature expected
for the thermally activated diffusion, no explicit explanation
for this discrepancy is found in the literature. On the contrary,
it is argued in these studies that the activation energy of νKT
for T < T ∗ corresponds to Ei .
In this paper, we “simulate” the µ SR spectra for various νi and the muon jump frequency νµ using the extended
Kubo-Toyabe function GEA
z (t; ∆, νi , νµ , Q) over the temperature range 10–400 K, assuming the appropriate activation
energies and prefactors for νi and νµ . Subsequently, by
analyzing these simulated spectra via the χ 2 ﬁtting with
GKT
z (t; ∆KT , νKT ) (that mimics the data analysis procedure in
the previous literature), we demonstrate that the anomalous
behavior of ∆KT and νKT can be semi-quantitatively reproduced. This indicates that such behavior stems from the sharp
increase in νi around T ∗ , and that a more detailed reanalysis
using GEA
z (t) is useful to properly evaluate νi together with
νµ . Conversely, it strongly suggests that µ SR results showing
no such anomalies clearly fail to capture ion diffusion.

The ﬁrst example where the model based on Eq. (2) was
applied is µ SR studies on the local ion dynamics of cation
molecules in hybrid perovskites [22]. It should be recalled
that in these materials too, the two aforementioned features in
the temperature dependence of ∆KT and νKT were clearly observed [28–30], suggesting that these anomalies are hallmarks
for the presence of ion dynamics in general. However, while
the lack of such anomalies suggests insensitivity of µ SR to
ion diffusion, whether Q ≈ 0 or νi ≈ 0 should be carefully
distinguished in the interpretation of experimental results. As
shown in Fig. 2(c), the extent of the increase in νKT near T ∗ is
sharply suppressed as Q decreases. Similarly,
√ the decrease of
∆KT with increasing νi is suppressed as 1 − Q approaches
unity. Therefore, when Q is small, even when ion diffusion
is actually occurring (νi > 0), it is expected that µ SR cannot
detect the contribution from νi . Results from µ SR studies on
Nax CoO2 [7, 14] have already demonstrated to be in such situation (Q < 0.25) [23, 31]. The same is likely true for other
examples where no anomalies are observed in νKT and/or ∆KT .
Now, let us discuss the magnitude of Q in the cases where
the nuclear magnetic moments of ions other than those of
Li/Na are negligibly small: this is presumed to be the case
for Li0.98 Ni1.02 , Lix FePO4 , Li4 Ti5 O12 , and Li/NaCx in Table
I. Even in such cases, the probability for a single ﬂuctuation event of B(t) involving multiple ions around the muon
is virtually negligible except at extremely high temperatures.
Therefore, given that the magnitude of ∆ is dominated by nn n
ions, its ﬂuctuation amplitude associated with single ion jump

is approximately evaluated as Q ≈ 1/n. (This is readily understood by considering that ∆2KT is determined by the sum
of ∆2i for each magnetic dipolar ﬁeld exerted from a single
ion to yield ∆2KT ≈ n∆2i , where n = 4–6 in typical materials.)
Moreover, provided that it takes m steps in ion jump motion
to entirely reset B(t) exerted from the n ions (depending the
details of jumping
√ path, so that m ≥ n), the true decay rate of
C(t) for the Q∆ component in Eq. (2) is reduced to νi /m
[23]: this can be regarded as the increase of effective 1 − Q by
e−νi Tµ /m − e−νi Tµ over the µ SR time window Tµ . These two
factors serve to reduce the effective Q for the relatively slow
ion jump (νi /m . ∆KT ), where the n − 1 ions contribute to the
1 − Q component. In fact, the observed value of νKT at T ∗
(which places the upper limit of νi ) is below ∼1 MHz in all
materials listed in Table I.
Finally, we discuss the effect of ion vacancies on muon diffusion. While muons are supposed to occupy both interstitial
sites and vacancies randomly upon their implantation, they often exhibit diffusion-limited trapping (DLT) to vacancies because of a deeper potential well than that at the interstitial
sites. Since ∆KT at the vacancies is considerably smaller than
that at the interstitial site due to the increase in the distance rµ
between the muon and the nn ions (∆KT ∝ 1/rµ3 ), ∆KT is expected to be reduced by the promotion of DLT with increasing
νµ [22]. The gradual decrease of ∆KT below T ∗ observed in
some battery materials [2, 3, 8, 10, 13, 17] can be explained
by this scenario. Note that Eq. (2) is virtually unaffected by
the DLT process, as it only serves to reduce ∆KT and νµ as
mean values.
To conclude, based on simulation data using the extended
KT function GEA
z (t; ∆, νi , νµ , Q) and the analysis of them by
the conventional KT function GKT
z (t; ∆KT , νKT ), it has been
demonstrated that the two anomalous features, the peak of
νKT around T ∗ and associated decrease of ∆KT , observed in
the previous µ SR studies of ion diffusion can be explained by
the sharp increase in νi against νµ . The characteristic temperature T ∗ corresponds
to the boundary where the mode of
√
relaxation for the Q∆ component (describing ion jump motion) exhibits a crossover from the motional broadening to the
narrowing regime with increasing νi . The motional
√ narrowing also leads to the decrease in ∆KT toward 1 − Q∆ for
T > T ∗ . Therefore, when these two features are observed simultaneously in the behavior of ∆KT and νKT , νi may be properly evaluated by the reanalysis using GEA
z (t). It has been also
shown that, as a temporary approach, the activation energy
for ion diffusion may be evaluated from the temperature dependence of ∆KT . On the other hand, it is highly likely that
the previous results showing no such anomalies in νKT and/or
∆KT convey little information on ion diffusion.
The development of the extended KT function has led to
a deeper understanding of how ion dynamics are reﬂected in
µ SR spectra. As a result, it has become strongly suggested
that most µ SR results previously interpreted in terms of ion
diffusion actually pertain to muon self-diffusion. However,
this does not necessarily mean that previous efforts were made
in vain. For example, oxides are important materials not only
for battery applications but also in various ﬁelds such as semiconductors and dielectrics, where it is well known that trace

5
amount of hydrogen signiﬁcantly inﬂuences the physical and
functional properties of these materials [32]. Previous µ SR
studies on battery materials may be valuable as they provide
information on the role of such “hidden hydrogen” via muon
as pseudo-hydrogen. It is strongly hoped that those involved

will revisit their studies from this renewed perspective.
