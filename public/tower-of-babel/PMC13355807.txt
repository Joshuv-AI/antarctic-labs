Quantifying species-specific binding affinities of transthyretin aggregation inhibitors
Authors: Xun Sun, H Jane Dyson, Peter E Wright
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTransthyretin (TTR) amyloidosis is one of the most common forms of systemic amyloidosis, involving deposits of pathogenic TTR aggregates in tissues and organs throughout the body. TTR aggregation is initiated by the dissociation of a native TTR tetramer into a monomeric intermediate, which subsequently misfolds and assembles into insoluble aggregates. Using an efficient 19F-NMR aggregation assay, we previously showed that designed peptide inhibitors can interact with multiple TTR species. However, quantifying species-specific binding affinities has remained difficult because the populations of these TTR species change over time and include a low-abundance monomeric intermediate. Here, we develop a quantitative method to extract species-dependent binding affinities directly from population-resolved 19F-NMR aggregation data. Using diflunisal as a model compound, we determine its binding affinities for both the tetramer and the monomeric intermediate under acidic, aggregating conditions. At acidic pH, tetramer binding becomes approximately 2-fold tighter, and monomer binding becomes 15-fold stronger, compared to neutral pH. We then apply this method to previously collected 19F-NMR aggregation data for designed peptide inhibitors. The results show that concatenating two capping peptides increases their binding affinity to both TTR tetramers and monomeric intermediates by about 2-fold, relative to the same peptides mixed separately at equal concentrations. Our method enables direct, quantitative comparison of species-dependent inhibitor binding, providing mechanistic insights useful for designing and optimizing TTR aggregation inhibitors.

Why it matters

Amyloid disease is caused by aggregated proteins that are deposited in cells, tissues, and organs. Developing targeted small-molecule or peptide-based aggregation inhibitors is an important avenue for potential prevention and therapy. Protein aggregation usually involves multiple intermediates, and inhibitors may interact with more than one aggregated species. Understanding how a single aggregation inhibitor engages with multiple species requires simultaneous determination of species-dependent inhibition efficacies. In this work, we develop a method to quantitatively extract the affinities with which aggregation inhibitors bind to both the tetramer and the monomer of transthyretin, an aggregation-prone protein, using 19F-NMR aggregation data. This approach for quantifying species-dependent binding affinities is expected to provide key insights for designing and optimizing novel aggregation inhibitors for transthyretin and other aggregation-prone proteins.

Introduction

Transthyretin (TTR) amyloidosis is a human protein amyloidosis disease, in which pathogenic TTR aggregates deposit in organs and tissues. To initiate TTR aggregation, a native TTR tetramer (T) must first dissociate into a structured monomer (M), which subsequently misfolds to form a monomeric amyloidogenic aggregation intermediate.1 This aggregation-prone species serves as the building block for the formation of soluble oligomers and, eventually, insoluble aggregates, including fibrils and amorphous aggregates.

Two strategies have been applied to develop aggregation inhibitors for TTR. The first strategy is to stabilize the native tetramer so that formation of the monomeric aggregation building block is suppressed. FDA-approved drugs for treating TTR amyloidosis, including tafamidis2 and acoramidis,3 belong to this class of compounds. In addition, diflunisal, a nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drug that has been repurposed as a tetramer stabilizer,4 also slows the progression of TTR amyloidosis by binding to the central cavity of the TTR tetramer,5 as do tafamidis and acoramidis. By contrast, the second strategy is targeting aggregation-prone β strands in monomeric species using designed blocking peptides with appended charged residues. In this way, the aggregation-prone monomer is stabilized, also suppressing aggregation. Examples of aggregation inhibitors based on this approach include TabF2/H26 and fluorophore-free TAD1 (ffTAD1)7 peptides, where ffTAD1 is a concatenated variant of TabF2 and TabH2, which target the F and H strands, respectively.

To optimize and develop new aggregation inhibitors for TTR, quantitative measurement of binding affinity is needed. However, such measurement is challenging because multiple TTR species along the time-dependent aggregation pathway can bind the same inhibitor, and some aggregation-prone intermediates form transiently at low concentration (<1 μM).8 We previously established a TTR aggregation sensor using a C10S-S85C TTR mutant covalently tagged with a trifluoroacetyl group (denoted as TTRF), which enables simultaneous measurement of the tetramer and low-abundance monomeric aggregation intermediate using their distinct 19F chemical shifts in a real-time 19F-NMR aggregation assay.9 In this work, we develop a quantitative method to extract apparent binding constants of diflunisal, TabF2/H2, and ffTAD1 for both the T and M states directly from 19F-NMR aggregation data, without requiring prior knowledge of their on and off rates. Our results provide direct evidence of diflunisal binding to the misfolded monomeric aggregation intermediate of TTR. This analytical method provides insight into varying binding affinities among small-molecule and peptide TTR aggregation inhibitors.

Materials and methods

Protein expression and purification

The tetrameric TTR construct C10S-S85C was purified and labeled with bromo-1,1,1-trifluoroacetone as previously described.9

NMR experiments

The 19F-NMR aggregation assay was performed for TTRF at 310 K and pH 4.4 in a buffer of 50 mM sodium acetate and 100 mM potassium chloride using a Bruker Avance 700 MHz (1H frequency) spectrometer equipped with a 1H/19F-13C/15N TCI cryoprobe as previously described.9 Interactions between monomeric F87E and diflunisal were studied by titrating F87E from 5 to 60 μM into diflunisal at an initial concentration of 10 μM at 310 K in 50 mM Bis-Tris-MES buffer at pH 6.5. The titrations were monitored by 19F-NMR. At each concentration of titrated F87E, 1D 19F spectra were acquired for 80,000 scans using a recycle delay of 1 s. The peak positions of the 2′ and 4′ fluorine atoms were determined by Lorentzian lineshape fitting. Data were processed using NMRPipe10 and analyzed with NMRFAM-SPARKY11 on NMRbox servers.12

Turbidity assay

Turbidity measurements were performed using 3.6 μM wild-type TTR in the presence of 1 μM diflunisal. Samples were incubated at 310 K and pH 4.4 for 72 h without agitation, following previously described procedures.4 Aggregation of human TTR at physiological pH is immeasurably slow, and weakly acidic conditions are required to accelerate tetramer dissociation and amyloid formation for in vitro assays.13

Modeling

Tetramer:inhibitor binding model (model 1)

This model builds on the previously published 3-state first-order aggregation model for TTRF (T↔M↔A) at pH 4.4 and 310 K.9 The peak areas were normalized using the total peak areas at the first time point such that [T] + [M] + [A] = 1, as previously described.9 To constrain the model, the four rate constants k1, k−1, k2, and k−2 (Figure 1A) were fixed to the values determined for aggregation of TTRF in the absence of inhibitor.9 Model 1 introduces an additional reversible binding step between the tetramer (T) and the inhibitor (Inh), forming the T:Inh complex (Scheme S1), where k3 is the second-order rate constant for T:Inh formation, and k−3 the first-order dissociation rate of the complex. These two parameters are connected by KD,Inh,T = k−3/k3, where KD,Inh,T is the apparent dissociation constant for the T:Inh complex normalized per tetramer, also defined as KD,Inh,T = [T][Inh]/[T:Inh]. To evaluate the performance of the fitting procedure, numerical simulations were performed across a range of KD,Inh,T from 10 nM to 100 μM while keeping k−3 = 100 h−1. Gaussian-distributed noise (2%)—comparable to the experimental noise—was added to the simulated aggregation curves. For each KD,Inh,T value, the ODEs were constrained using ten k−3 values randomly sampled logarithmically from a physiologically relevant range of 100 to 10,000 h−1. The mean fitted KD,Inh,T was then compared with the true input value.

For fitting the 19F-NMR aggregation data, the normalized tetramer peak area was modeled as the sum of [T] and [T:Inh], consistent with their indistinguishable 19F chemical shifts (Figure S1). The normalized monomer peak area reports [M]. Fitting uncertainty for KD,Inh,T was determined using a grid-search analysis and F-statistics at the 95% confidence level.14

Tetramer:inhibitor and monomer:inhibitor binding model (model 2)

Model 2 extends model 1 by introducing additional reversible binding and unbinding steps for the monomer-inhibitor complex (M:Inh) (Scheme S2). In this model, k4 is the second-order rate constant for forming the M:Inh complex, and k−4 is the first-order dissociation rate. These two parameters are connected by KD,Inh,M = k−4/k4, where KD,Inh,M is the apparent dissociation constant for the M:Inh complex normalized per monomer, also defined by KD,Inh,M = [M][Inh]/[M:Inh]. The full set of ODEs corresponding to the extended kinetic scheme is provided in the supplemental information.

We performed similar simulations to test the performance of simultaneously extracting both KD,Inh,T and KD,Inh,M from 10 nM to 100 μM. In the simulations, ten pairs of k−3 and k−4 were varied from a 2D grid with each dimension ranging from 100 to 10,000 h−1. For each pair of KD,Inh,M and KD,Inh,T, the mean of ten fitted values was compared to the true values.

In the data fitting routine, the normalized peak area of the monomer peak was modeled as a sum of [M] and [M:Inh], and the remainder of the fitting routine was identical to that of model 1. Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) and Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC) values for model 1 and model 2 were calculated per Burnham and Anderson.15

Prediction of concentration-dependent diflunisal inhibition

Two models were used to predict the dose dependence of diflunisal-mediated aggregation inhibition of TTR.

The first predictive model, the thermodynamic model, is based on two fitted dissociation constants: KD,Inh,T, and KD,Inh,M. The full binding equation for diflunisal and TTR is provided in the supplemental information and was solved using the MATLAB function roots as previously described.16 The predicted percentage of aggregate formed at each diflunisal concentration was calculated as the mean of the lower and upper bounds, where the lower bound corresponds to the fraction of free tetramer, KD,Inh,T/(KD,Inh,T + [Inh]), and the upper bound is the relative population of free monomer, KD,Inh,M/(KD,Inh,M + [Inh]). This model assumes that the T:diflunisal and M:diflunisal complexes do not aggregate.

The second predictive model, the kinetic model, incorporates the previously reported aggregation rates k1, k−1, k2, and k−2 for TTRF alone at 310 K and pH 4.4,9 together with fitted KD,Inh,T and KD,Inh,M in model 2. These parameters were used to simulate aggregation kinetics at 310 K and pH 4.4 across a range of diflunisal concentrations. The simulated fraction of NMR-invisible aggregates at 72 h (matching experimental conditions) was used as a proxy for large aggregate formation, following the assumption that NMR-invisible species convert to insoluble particles that produce turbidity signals.

Fitting 19F chemical shift of diflunisal to a 1:4 diflunisal:F87E binding model

The model assumes the transient formation of a 1:4 diflunisal:F87E complex, via the transient tetramerization of F87E, that shifts the 19F chemical shifts of both 2′ and 4′ fluorine atoms from their free-state positions. The observed chemical shifts were modeled as population-weighted averages of the free and bound states using a single overall binding constant. The full binding equation is described in the supplemental information and was solved using the MATLAB routine reported previously,16 with the corrected concentrations of diflunisal and F87E due to titration.

To improve numerical stability and avoid overfitting, the peak positions of the free and bound states were constrained using the experimentally observed minimal and maximal peak positions for each fluorine atom. Additional constraints were implemented to enforce a monotonic increase in the bound-state population as F87E concentration increased. The apparent binding constant of diflunisal per F87E monomer was calculated as the fourth root of the fitted overall binding constant, with the unit of μM4 corresponding to the formation of a 1:4 diflunisal:F87E complex at neutral pH.

Results

Diflunisal reduces some, but not all, aggregation rates at acidic pH

Our previous work shows that the combination of kinetic modeling and a high-sensitivity 19F-NMR aggregation assay of TTRF provides kinetic insights into the early steps of the multi-step aggregation pathway.9 At 310 K and pH 4.4, reversible exchange between the tetramer and monomer (k1 and k−1) and between the monomer and NMR-invisible aggregates (k2 and k−2) describes the two coupled equilibria and their population changes over time (Figure 1A). Table 1 compares the four aggregation rate constants measured in the absence and presence of diflunisal. The presence of diflunisal reduces k1, k−1, and k2 but does not change k−2 beyond the fitting uncertainty. Reduction of k1 shows that diflunisal binds to the tetramer but does not account for the reduced k−1 and k2, which can be explained by binding between diflunisal and the monomeric aggregation intermediate, as suggested by our previous numerical simulations.8 In contrast, the absence of a significant change in k−2 shows that diflunisal does not bind to the amorphous aggregates formed under the acidic conditions.17

Binding of diflunisal to both T and M is required to fit the 19F-NMR aggregation data

To quantify the inhibitory efficacy of diflunisal, we first tested a simple model (model 1) in which an aggregation inhibitor only binds to the TTR tetramer (T). As the presence of diflunisal does not alter the 19F chemical shift of the tetramer (T) peak in TTRF (Figure S1), the observed T peak was modeled as the sum of both free and inhibitor-bound tetramers. Numerical simulations showed that, on the timescale of 2–3 days, the observed aggregation kinetics are not sensitive to the dissociation rate constant k−3 of the T:Inh complex within a physically relevant range (100–10,000 h−1; Figure S2A). This insensitivity allows accurate fitting of KD,Inh,T using k−3 as a constrained parameter, without prior knowledge of its true value. Simulations confirmed that KD,Inh,T can be reliably extracted over a wide range (10 nM–100 μM; Figure 1B).

We then applied model 1 to the experimental 19F-NMR aggregation data. While the T and A populations were fitted reasonably well, the fitted M population was consistently underestimated (Figure 1C), suggesting that diflunisal also binds to the monomeric intermediate (M), an interaction not included in model 1.

To address this, we implemented model 2, which includes reversible formation of both T:Inh and M:Inh complexes. As with model 1, the aggregation kinetics on the multi-day timescale were insensitive to the specific values of k−3 and k−4 within the physical range (Figure S2B). Numerical simulations validated that both KD,Inh,T and KD,Inh,M can be simultaneously extracted with high accuracy over the 10 nM–100 μM range (Figures 1D and 1E).

When applied to the experimental 19F-NMR data, model 2 successfully fit the populations of all three species (T, M, and A) simultaneously (Figure 1F). The difference in AIC and BIC of model 1 versus model 2 is 135 and 132, respectively, so the relative likelihood that model 1 is better than model 2 is less than e−66. Consistently, the p-value of the likelihood ratio of 2.3e−15 rejects the null hypothesis that the simpler model 1 is sufficient to describe the diflunisal 19F-NMR aggregation data. All three tests statistically favor model 2 over model 1 in fitting the data. The extracted dissociation constants, KD,Inh,T of 230 nM and KD,inh,M of 410 nM, were constrained by the experimental data (Figures 2A and 2B; Table 2).

The knowledge of both KD,Inh,M and KD,Inh,T allows the deconvolution of bound and free populations of TTR species as a function of diflunisal concentration. As shown in Figure 2C, both a thermodynamic model and a kinetic model (described in the materials and methods) recapitulate the concentration-dependent trend observed in previously reported aggregation-inhibition data for wild-type TTR.4 TTRF and wild-type TTR have very similar aggregation properties and kinetics.9

Diflunisal binds only weakly to the structured monomer at neutral pH

Diflunisal binds to the TTR tetramer with an average affinity of 29018 or 41019 nM at neutral pH, but its interaction with the TTR monomer at neutral pH has not been characterized. To probe this interaction, we used the F87E mutant, which is a stable structured monomer at neutral pH,20 conditions in which the wild-type TTR is predominantly tetrameric at NMR concentrations. The 1H-15N heteronuclear single quantum coherence (HSQC) spectrum of F87E at 298 K and pH 6.5 is essentially unchanged in the presence of 8.3-fold excess diflunisal (Figure S3, no chemical shift changes greater than 0.02 ppm for 1H or 0.15 ppm for 15N), confirming that binding to the fully folded monomer is extremely weak. We monitored the 19F chemical shifts of the two fluorine atoms in 10 μM diflunisal (2′ and 4′ fluorine atoms; see Figure 3A) while titrating the F87E monomer from 5 to 60 μM at pH 6.5 and 310 K, the temperature of the aggregation assays. The observed sigmoid trend of the concentration-dependent 19F chemical shift is fitted with a cooperative 1:4 diflunisal:F87E binding model, yielding an apparent binding constant for diflunisal of 1,300 μM4 (Figures 3B, 3C, and S4). F87E has a tendency to self-associate at 310 K, and the observed stoichiometry suggests that diflunisal binds weakly to transiently formed tetramers. The interaction between diflunisal and F87E is distinct from the diflunisal-wild-type TTR interaction at this temperature, where the 19F peak intensities of the 2′ and 4′ fluorine atoms of diflunisal are substantially reduced without shifting the peak positions (Figure S5).

Discussion

In this work, we describe a method to quantitatively extract apparent binding constants for aggregation inhibitors interacting with the tetramer and monomer of TTR. The method is based on global optimization of aggregation kinetic data, specifically population-resolved 19F-NMR aggregation data. The off rates for inhibitors with binding affinity weaker than 100 nM are generally greater than 200 h−1; for example, the off-rate constant is 216 h−1 for dissociation of tafamidis from its second binding site, with KD = 22 nM, on the TTR tetramer.21 These rates are much faster than the 2–3-day timescale of the aggregation steps and do not affect the aggregation kinetics of TTR (Figure S2). The kinetic decoupling of binding and aggregation processes allows us to model time-dependent aggregation profiles and extract inhibitor dissociation constants without prior knowledge of the kinetic rate constants for the binding of the TTR inhibitor. Based on the TTRF aggregation kinetics, the binding constants that can be accurately determined using this method range from 10 nM to 100 μM (Figures 1B, 1D, and 1E). This approach complements existing NMR methods for extracting binding affinities, such as lineshape fitting22 or chemical shift perturbation analysis.23

For diflunisal and TTR tetramers, the extracted KD,Inh,T is 230 nM at pH 4.4 and 310 K, based on model 2. Using ΔH = −8.38 kcal/mol for diflunisal binding to the TTR tetramer19 and assuming ΔH is constant between 298 and 310 K, the KD,Inh,T at pH 7.0 and 310 K is estimated to be between 500 and 710 nM. These values were extrapolated from the geometric mean (290 nM) of the two dissociation constants reported at pH 7.0 and 298 K18 and the single dissociation constant of 410 nM at pH 7.4 and 298 K,19 both measured by isothermal titration calorimetry. The modest increase in binding affinity at acidic pH could be due to an increase in electrostatic attraction between diflunisal and the TTR tetramer: the net charge of the tetramer shifts from −4.8 at pH 7.0 to +7.1 at pH 4.4, while the carboxyl group of diflunisal remains deprotonated at both pH values (pKa = 2.94).19 The misfolding of tetramer TTR at acidic pH13 could also contribute to the altered binding with diflunisal.

The reduced k2 and k−1 values in the presence of diflunisal (Table 1) suggest binding of diflunisal to monomeric TTR at acidic pH, as indicated by numerical simulations.8 In this work, we show that, for TTR monomers, the binding of diflunisal increases at least 15-fold as the pH is decreased from 6.5 to 4.4. The increasingly positive net charge of TTR monomers (from −3.7 at pH 7.0 to +6.7 at pH 4.4) enhances the electrostatic attraction between the monomer and diflunisal. In addition, TTR monomers are prone to partial unfolding at acidic pH,24 exposing hydrophobic and amyloidogenic strand edges,20,25 which can also contribute to the enhanced binding of diflunisal at pH 4.4 compared to pH 7.0. Such binding is required to reproduce the 19F-NMR aggregation data, and its apparent affinity cannot be determined using standard methods such as isothermal titration calorimetry, which requires protein stability during measurement. Interestingly, we also observed enhanced binding of designed peptides to TTR monomers at acidic pH compared to neutral pH.8 This increase cannot be attributed to electrostatics because, unlike the case with diflunisal, the peptide inhibitors also become positively charged at acidic pH.

The KD,Inh,T and KD,Inh,M values for concatenated ffTAD1 are 2-fold tighter than those from the equal mixture of TabF2 and TabH2 (Table 2). These results are linked to an increased effective concentration of the tethered peptides for the second binding event due to an entropic advantage.

In conclusion, we established a quantitative method to extract two binding affinities for aggregation inhibitors that interact with distinct TTR species using time-dependent aggregation data. This approach enables direct comparison of inhibition efficacies among different inhibitors and provides mechanistic insights useful for designing and optimizing aggregation inhibitors for TTR and other aggregation-prone proteins or peptides.
