Magnitude and determinants of double burden of malnutrition among married women in Africa: a multi-country analysis from 25 countries
Authors: Bedilu Alamirie Ejigu, Fentanesh Nibret Tiruneh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionThe Double Burden of Malnutrition among women in Africa is a pressing public health issue. However, there is a lack of comprehensive evidence on this phenomenon and its determinants. This study aims to bridge this gap by exploring the determinants of the double burden of malnutrition, with a particular focus on women’s decision-making power.MethodsThis study analyzed data come from DHS datasets from 25 African countries. The analysis included 114,101 married non-pregnant women. Nutritional status was assessed using BMI. We conducted chi-squared tests for bivariate analysis and multilevel baseline category logit models for multivariable analysis. Results were presented as relative risk ratio (RRR) with their respective 95% confidence intervals.ResultsThis study found that 11.4% of women were underweight and 17.2% were overweight or obese. Socio-demographic factors such as residence, age, education, wealth status, and decision-making power were significantly associated with malnutrition. Rural residence and younger age groups were associated with higher underweight prevalence, while older age groups and urban residence exhibited increased overweight and obesity risks. Higher education and wealth quantile, health insurance, and economic decision making power were associated with higher overweight and obesity while breastfeeding was found to reduce the risk of obesity and obesity.ConclusionThis study uncovers malnutrition challenges among women residing in 25 African countries. Addressing malnutrition requires diverse strategies, including food security, healthcare enhancement, encouraging breastfeeding with adequate support, implementing culturally sensitive interventions that acknowledge and integrate cultural beliefs and practices concerning nutrition and body image and empowering women.

Introduction

The double burden of malnutrition encompasses the simultaneous presence of under-nutrition as well as overweight or obesity within individuals, households, and populations throughout their lifespan. The double burden of malnutrition is globally a public health problem [1]. Approximately one-third of the global population experiences varying forms of malnutrition, including underweight, overweight, or obesity [2]. In 2022, 2.5 billion adults were overweight, including over 890 million adults who were living with obesity, while more than 600 million adults were underweight in 2016 [3, 4].

The double burden of malnutrition is a complex and concerning phenomenon that affects numerous women in Africa. Married women at reproductive age, as key stakeholders in household nutrition, are particularly vulnerable to the double burden of malnutrition. Preconception maternal under-nutrition results in an increased risk of fetal growth restriction, low birth weight, preterm birth and early childhood stunting [5, 6]. On the other hand, overweight and obesity are associated with an increased risk of adverse antenatal, intrapartum, and postpartum complications, including conditions like gestational diabetes and pre-eclampsia, maternal death, haemorrhage and caesarean delivery, neonatal and infant deaths, neural tube and cardiac defects in new-borns, birth trauma, and macrosomic infants [7–9].

In low-income and middle-income countries (LMICs), the prevalence of under-nutrition remains a significant public health challenge. However, alongside this issue, there is a concerning trend of increasing obesogenic factors that promote excessive body weight gain. Obesogenic factors encompass environmental, societal, and behavioural influences that promote unhealthy eating habits and sedentary lifestyles, resulting in weight gain and obesity. These factors include the accessibility of unhealthy foods, their aggressive marketing, limited access to nutritious options, sedentary behaviour’s due to urbanization and technology, and changing cultural norms [10].

Women are susceptible to experiencing malnutrition due to increased demands for essential nutrients during pregnancy and breastfeeding, as well as the need to compensate for nutrient losses during menstruation [11]. In resource-constrained settings, including many African contexts, women may prioritize feeding their families over themselves, increasing the risk of inadequate dietary intake and undernutrition. Additionally, cultural norms related to household responsibilities and daily routines may limit women’s time, mobility, and opportunities for physical activity, contributing to more sedentary lifestyles. In combination with urbanization and increasing use of technology, this may increase the risk of overweight and obesity [12]. Moreover gender disparities in access to resources, can affect women’s ability to access nutritious food and healthcare. Economic inequalities may result in women having limited control over household resources, making it difficult for them to make decisions regarding food choices and nutrition [13].

Previous studies revealed that maternal under-nutrition is linked to various factors, including occupation, contraceptive use, residence, and wealth. Similarly, women’s and household socioeconomic status, place of residence, age, education, employment, use of hormonal contraceptives, breastfeeding, and exposure to mass media were identified as significant influencers of maternal overweight or obesity [14–16].

Evidence also indicated that to effectively tackle the double burden of malnutrition, it is crucial to implement dual-focused strategies that encompass women’s empowerment and nutrition-sensitive interventions [17]. Women empowerment seeks to address gender-based inequities in accessing and utilizing resources, which serve as barriers limiting women’s ability to reach their maximum potential. Effecting the desired transformation in women’s empowerment necessitates the modification of socio-cultural norms within low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) and a re-evaluation of the role of men as active contributors to the solution [18].

The prevalence of both undernutrition and overweight/obesity within a specific country is high among married women of reproductive age in Africa, reflecting a growing double burden of malnutrition. However, comprehensive evidence on this double challenge of malnutrition and its determinants across the region remains limited. Previous multi-country studies that primarily emphasized sociodemographic and economic factors, often overlooking key determinants such as women’s decision-making power and health service–related factors [19, 20]. Therefore, this study aims to address these gaps by estimating the burden and identifying the determinants of the double burden of malnutrition among married women of reproductive age across 25 African countries, incorporating decision-making and health service variables alongside spatial analytical methods to provide a more comprehensive understanding of the issue. In this study, ‘double burden of malnutrition’ refers to the simultaneous presence of underweight (BMI < 18.5 kg/m²) and overweight/obesity (BMI ≥ 25 kg/m²) among married women in the same national population -recognizing that both forms of malnutrition may occur across different individuals within the same population.

Methods

The most recent Demographic and Health Survey (DHS) conducted between 2010 and 2023 from 25 African countries were utilized for the analysis after obtaining permission from the DHS website (www.dhsprogram.com). These 25 countries represent all countries with available and eligible DHS data on women’s nutritional status within the specified period; no countries were preselected. Only one (the most recent) survey was used for each country. The DHS is a nationally representative survey conducted in multiple countries, providing comprehensive data on health indicators, including nutrition, reproductive health, and socio-economic and demographic characteristics. The Women’s Recode (IR File) datasets from each country were imported into Stata 18.0 for further analysis. These datasets were merged to facilitate comprehensive analysis.

Analytical sample

Among a total of 432,829 women who completed the survey across 25 countries, 114,101 married, non-pregnant women of reproductive age (15–49 years) were included in the final analysis. The sample was restricted to married women because the decision-making variables used in this study are only collected for this group in the DHS dataset, as they are based on spousal or household decision-making dynamics and are not available for unmarried women.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Married, non-pregnant women of reproductive age (15–49 years), including lactating women, were included in the analysis due to the availability of women’s empowerment indicators in the DHS dataset. Pregnant women and those with missing BMI data were excluded due to data availability issues and methodological considerations related to BMI measurement.

Study variables outcome variables

The main outcome variables in this study is the malnutrition status which were measured based on WHO’s standards of body mass index (BMI), calculated as weight in kilograms divided by height squared in meters. Underweight was measured as if women’s BMI less than 18.5 kg/m2; overweight was defined if women’s BMI in between (25–29.99 kg/m2 ) and obesity was defined if women’s BMI greater than 30 kg/m2 [21].

Predictor variables

Explanatory variables included women’s age (15–24, 25–34, and 35–49 years), residence (urban, rural), level of education (no education, primary, secondary and higher), employment status (no/yes), wealth status (poor, middle, and rich), Breastfeeding status ( no/yes), anemia level (severe, moderate, mild, and not anemic), Use of contraceptive (no method used, traditional method and modern method). Having health insurance (no/yes), employment (no/yes), and family size (1–3, 4–6 and > 6), Parity (0, 1–2 and 3+).

Further, three decision-making variables were included in our study analysis: personal health decision-making, economic decision-making, and mobility decision-making.

Personal health decision-making was measured by asking women, “Who makes healthcare decisions for you?” Women who made these decisions either alone or with their husbands were classified as having personal health decision-making autonomy, while other women were classified as not having decision-making autonomy regarding their health.

Economic decision-making autonomy was assessed by asking participants, “Who makes decisions on large household purchases?” Women who made these decisions either alone or with their husbands were classified as having economic decision-making autonomy, while other women were classified as not having economic decision-making autonomy.

In a similar manner, mobility decision-making autonomy was measured by asking participants about their involvement in decisions regarding visits to friends or family. Women who made these decisions either alone or with their husbands were classified as having mobility decision-making autonomy, while other women were classified as not having mobility decision-making autonomy.

Statistical analysis

The complexity of analyzing DHS survey datasets can be attributed to two main factors: (i) the use of stratified multistage cluster sampling and, (ii) the unequal probabilities of selection from target populations for sampled elements, often due to oversampling of key subgroups. To account this complexity survey design, sampling weights were applied when estimating proportions and corresponding 95% confidence intervals. All analyses were performed using STATA Software version 18.0. We conducted a series of bivariate analyses by using chi-squared tests to examine the association of predictor variables with malnutrition.

All analyses accounted for survey sampling weights and clustering using appropriate survey procedures. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Ethical issues

This study solely comprises secondary analyses of existing DHS data. The data collection methods for DHS adhered to pertinent guidelines and regulations. Ethical approval for the survey was obtained from the Institutional Review Boards of the respective countries involved. Prior to interviews, all participants provided written informed consent, with adolescents requiring permission from a parent or guardian to participate in the study. The authors obtained permission from the DHS program to use the data: https://dhsprogram.com/data/available-datasets.cfm.

The chi-square analysis of malnutrition among African women reveals significant differences based on various socio-demographic and individual factors (Table 4). All predictor variables were statistically significant with all type of malnutrition at p-value 0.05 level. Urban women were less likely to be underweight (8.5%) compared to rural women (13.6%). However, urban women had higher prevalence of overweight (24.4%) and obesity (20.6%) than rural counterparts (13.8% and 6.4%, respectively). The prevalence of underweight decreases with age, with the highest percentage among the 15–24 age groups (31.4%). Conversely, overweight and obesity prevalence increase with age, peaking in the 35–49 age groups (61.3% overweight, 49.0% obese).

Significant factors related to underweight compared to normal weight among women were residence, age, educational level, wealth status, breastfeeding status, anemia level, use of contraceptive, health insurance, occupation, parity and personal health decision making power. Women in rural areas had 21% increased risk of being underweight (RRR = 1.21; 95% CI: 1.13–1.29) compared to those to urban areas. Women aged 25–34 years had 11% lower risk of being underweight compared to those aged 15–24 years (RRR = 0.89; 95% CI: 0.83–0.95). Women with higher education had a 21% lower risk of being underweight (RRR = 0.79; 95% CI: 0.66–0.96), and those with higher wealth status had a 31% lower risk (RRR = 0.69; 95% CI: 0.65–0.74), compared to women with no education and poor wealth status, respectively. Breastfeeding women had 6% higher risk of being underweight compared to non-breastfeeding women (RRR = 1.06; 95% CI: 1.01–1.11). Additionally, women without anemia were a 40% lower risk of being underweight than severe anemic status (RR = 0.60; 95% CI: 0.51–0.71). Modern contraceptive use (RRR = 0.79; 95% CI: 0.75–0.84), having health insurance (RRR = 0.76; 95% CI: 0.69–0.83), higher parity (RRR = 0.86; 95% CI: 0.77–0.96), and being employed (RRR = 0.76; 95% CI: 0.72–0.79) were associated with a reduced risk of underweight by 21%, 24%, 14%, and 24%, respectively.

The risk of overweight among African women compared to normal weight women was influenced by various predictor variables including residence, age, education, wealth status, breastfeeding status, anemia level, use of contraceptive method, had health insurance and decision making variables. Women who reside in rural areas were reduced the risk of overweight by 41% (RRR = 0.59; 95% CI: 0.57–0.62) than urban counterparts. Older age groups (25–34 and 35–49 years) had 90% and 55% higher risks of overweight, respectively, compared to younger women (15–24 years) (RRR = 1.90; 95% CI: 1.79–2.01 and RR = 2.55; 95% CI: 2.38–2.73, respectively). A higher education level (RR = 1.88; 95% CI: 1.71–2.07) and wealth status (RR = 1.78; 95% CI: 1.70–1.87)) were associated with 88% and 78% increased risks of overweight. Breastfeeding was associated with a reduced risk of overweight by 24% (RRR = 0.76; 95% CI: 0.70–0.76). Additionally, women without anemia (RRR = 1.61; 95% CI; 1.33–1.93); who used modern contraceptive (RRR = 1.15, 95% CI: 1.10–1.19); had health insurance (RRR = 1.27; 95% CI: 1.20–1.33) and had economic decision making power (RRR = 1.07; 95% CI: 1.02–1.12) were increased the risk of overweight by 61%, 15%, 27% and 7%, respectively. While women who had movement decision making power were reduced the risk of being overweight by 5% (RRR = 0.95; 95%CI: 0.91–0.99).

The risk of obesity among African women compare to normal weight women was significantly affected by similar socio-demographic factors. Rural residence is strongly reduced the risk of obesity by 61% (RRR = 0.39; 95% CI: 0.30–0.42). Older age groups (25–34 and 35–49) had higher risk of obesity by 28% (RRR = 3.28; 95% CI: 2.9–3.60) and 41% (RRR = 6.41; 95% CI: 5.8–7.08) compared to younger women (15–24), respectively. Higher education levels (RRR = 2.74; 95% CI: 2.4–3.04) and wealth status (RRR = 2.44; 95% CI: 2.28–2.60) were associated with 74% and 44% increased risks of obesity, respectively. Breastfeeding was strongly decreases the risk of obesity by 45% (RRR = 0.55; 95% CI: 0.52–0.58). Employed women (RRR = 0.94; 95% CI; 0.89–0.988) had 6% lower risk of obesity than unemployed counterparts. Additionally, women who had no anemic (RRR = 1.77; 95% CI: 1.40–2.23); who had 1–2 and 3 + parity (RRR = 1.26; 95% CI: 1.11–1.42 and RRR = 1.40; 95% CI 1.24–1.59); had health insurance (RRR = 1.39; 95% CI 1.31–1.47) and had economic decision making power (RRR = 1.08 ; 95% CI: 1.01–1.15) were increased the risk of obesity by 77%, 26%, 40%, 39% and 8%, respectively.

Discussion

This study examines the multifaceted issues of malnutrition among married reproductive-aged women across various African countries. The findings provide significant insights into the prevalence of underweight, overweight, and obesity among women and the factors associated with these conditions. The present analysis revealed that the prevalence of malnutrition among African married women highlights critical public health challenges. About, 11.4% of women were underweight, 17.2% were overweight, and 11.4% were obese. These statistics underscore a double burden of malnutrition where both undernutrition and overnutrition coexist in the Africa, potentially exacerbating health disparities [1]. To address these issues, a comprehensive approach that includes improving food security, promoting healthy lifestyles, and empowering women in various aspects of life is required.

Countries like Ethiopia (22.9%), South Africa (22.7%), Chad (18.4%), and Burundi (18.2%) reported the highest magnitude of underweight women. Under-nutrition in women, particularly during the reproductive years, can have far-reaching consequences. It is associated with increased risks of maternal mortality, low birth weight, and impaired child growth and development [22]. This highlights the intergenerational impact of maternal nutrition on child health and development. Proper nutritional interventions and support during reproductive ages are therefore crucial to improving outcomes for both mothers and their children [23]. Poverty, limited access to nutritious foods, poor agricultural productivity, inadequate healthcare, and gender inequalities can all contribute to the high rates of under-nutrition among women in low income countries [24, 25]. By addressing the root causes of under-nutrition, these countries can work towards improving the health and well-being of their population.

On the other hand, countries such as Lesotho (26.5%), Gabon (24.9%), Ghana (25.7%), and Kenya (25.8%) showed a high prevalence of overweight among married women. South Africa, Gabon, and Lesotho also reported alarmingly high obesity rates of 39.4%, 32.3%, and 27.5%, respectively. Obesity is a major risk factor for non-communicable diseases, including cardiovascular diseases, type 2 diabetes, and certain types of cancer [26]. These conditions can further strain already overburdened healthcare systems and perpetuate cycles of poor health and socioeconomic disadvantage. This emerging trend is often linked to the nutrition transition, characterized by the replacement of traditional diets with energy-dense, nutrient-poor foods, alongside urbanization and lifestyle changes [27].

This study identified several socio-demographic, economic, health care and decision making factors associated with malnutrition among married women. Women residing in rural areas had a higher risk of being underweight compared to urban counterparts. Conversely, urban residence increased the risk of overweight and obesity. This urban-rural disparity suggests that while rural areas might suffer from food scarcity and poverty, urban areas face challenges related to sedentary lifestyles and unhealthy diets. This study also found that age, wealth and education were strongly linked with both underweight and overweight. Education and wealth status were a protective against underweight but increased the risks of overweight and obesity. Our findings are consistent with those of previous studies [28, 29]. This paradox highlights the complex interplay between socioeconomic status and nutrition, where better access to resources may lead to higher caloric intake and sedentary lifestyles. Age also played a significant role, with women aged 25–34 and 35–49 showing reduced risks of being underweight but increased risks of overweight and obesity. This trend indicates that older women are more susceptible to overweight, possibly due to a combination of physiological, lifestyle, and socio-demographic factors, including reduced physical activity and metabolic changes over time [30].

In line with other studies findings [31–33], this study indicated that employed women were reducing the risk of both underweight and obesity. Employment provides financial stability, which can improve access to nutritious foods and healthcare. Moreover, the structure and routine associated with employment may support healthier lifestyle choices and physical activity, contributing to balanced nutrition and weight management.

Breastfeeding was associated with an increased risk of underweight and decreased in the risks of overweight and obesity. The observed association between breastfeeding and maternal underweight highlights the importance of maternal dietary diversity and nutritional supplementation during lactation [34]. The protective effect of breastfeeding against overweight and obesity underscores the importance of promoting breastfeeding practices as part of public health strategies [35, 36]. Maternal obesity, combined with a lack of breastfeeding, significantly increases the risk of childhood obesity, highlighting a troubling potential for a trans-generational cycle of obesity and related chronic diseases [37]. Additionally, there is growing evidence that breastfeeding may offer protective benefits for women’s future health, including a reduced risk of developing cardiovascular and metabolic diseases, breast and ovarian cancers [38]. This dual benefit underscores the importance of promoting breastfeeding to improve long-term health outcomes for both mothers and their children.

Unsurprisingly, this study found that women with anemia were at a higher risk of being underweight, but tended to have a reduced risk of being overweight or obese. The possible explanation for these findings is anemia may be associated with poor nutritional intake and absorption issues, which contribute to underweight status. Conversely, anemia reduces the risk of overweight and obesity due to the same nutritional deficiencies [39, 40].

Similar to previous studies finding this study found that women who used modern contraceptive reduce the risk of underweight, while increased the risk of overweight [33, 41–43]. This may be attributed to the better family planning and spacing of births, which allows women to recover nutritionally between pregnancies. Adequate birth spacing may improve maternal nutrition and overall health, reducing the incidence of underweight conditions. On the other hand, modern contraceptives, particularly hormonal methods, have been linked to weight gain in some women. Hormonal contraceptives can alter metabolism and appetite regulation, leading to increased caloric intake and fat accumulation, thereby increasing the risk of overweight [44, 45].

This study indicated that parity significantly reducing the risk of underweight and increasing the risk of obesity. Women who have given birth may experience changes in body composition and metabolism that can lead to increased body weight. Additionally, repeated pregnancies may encourage a higher caloric intake and improved nutrient absorption to support fetal growth and maternal health. However, higher parity is also linked to an increased risk of obesity. Postpartum weight retention and the cumulative effects of multiple pregnancies can lead to significant weight gain. Hormonal changes, lifestyle adjustments, and the demands of childcare can reduce physical activity levels, further contributing to obesity risk, several studies also reported that women who have given birth to multiple children have the highest likelihood of being obese [46–48].

This study also found having health insurance was a protective factor for underweight but risk factor for overweight and obesity. Access to health insurance typically improves healthcare access, allowing for regular medical check-ups, nutritional advice, and treatment for conditions that may lead to under-nutrition. This access helps in early identification and management of under-nutrition and related health issues. Conversely, health insurance can also increase the risk of overweight and obesity. With better access to healthcare services, there might be an increased diagnosis and management of underlying conditions, which sometimes leads to the use of medications that promote weight gain [49].

The current study explored the impact of women’s decision-making power on their nutritional status. Economic decision-making power was associated to a higher risk of overweight and obesity, suggesting that women who have control over financial resources might have better access to high-calorie foods. Financial autonomy may facilitate the purchase of energy-dense, nutrient-poor foods, which are often more accessible and convenient in urban and economically advantaged settings in most African countries. Additional explanation for this result in some African cultures, a larger body size is associated with prosperity, healthy, and beauty. This cultural perception can lead to higher caloric intake as a status symbol [50, 51].

In contrast, decision-making power related to movement was protective against overweight, indicating that autonomy in physical activity could mitigate the risk of overweight. The studies conducted in Ethiopia also reported that women’s decision making autonomy was positively affected with their nutritional status [52, 53]. Women who have movement decision making autonomy are more likely to engage in regular exercise and maintain an active lifestyle, which helps mitigate the risk of gaining excess weight. This autonomy may allow women to participate in physical activities, sports, or even daily walking routines that contribute to maintaining a healthy weight.

This study has some limitations. First, the cross-sectional design prevents establishing causal relationships between the factors and malnutrition. Second, the study relied on self-reported data on explanatory factors, which may be subject to recall and social desirability biases. Third, since it is a secondary analysis, it does not include all variables linked to malnutrition, such as dietary patterns, limiting a comprehensive understanding of nutritional influences. Fourth, the DHS surveys were conducted in different years across the included countries, which may affect comparability due to temporal variations in socioeconomic conditions, health systems, and nutrition transitions. Fifth, the study focused exclusively on married women; therefore, the findings may not be generalizable to unmarried women. Despite these limitations, the study spans multiple African countries, enhancing the generalizability of its findings across diverse regions and socio-economic contexts. Additionally, this study addresses both under-nutrition and over-nutrition, providing a holistic view of malnutrition and its public health implications.

Conclusion

This study examined the nutritional status of married reproductive-aged women across 25 African countries and found that underweight, overweight, and obesity remain important public health concerns, affecting 11.4%, 17.2%, and 11.4% of women, respectively. The prevalence of malnutrition varied considerably across countries, with some countries experiencing a high burden of underweight, while others faced substantial levels of overweight and obesity. South Africa exhibited a notable coexistence of underweight and overweight/obesity among married women.

Residence, age, household wealth, education, employment status, breastfeeding, anemia status, contraceptive use, parity, health insurance coverage, and women’s decision-making autonomy were significantly associated with nutritional status. These findings may help inform context-specific nutrition policies and programs aimed at addressing both undernutrition and overweight/obesity among married women in African countries.

Recommendations

To reduce malnutrition, interventions should be tailored to address socio-demographic and socio-economic inequalities. Targeted nutrition programs should prioritize rural and younger women to reduce undernutrition, while prevention strategies for overweight and obesity should focus on older, urban, and socio-economically advantaged women. Strengthening women’s education, economic empowerment, and household decision-making power, along with expanding access to health insurance, may help reduce malnutrition disparities. Promoting and supporting breastfeeding practices may also contribute to reducing the risk of obesity among women.
