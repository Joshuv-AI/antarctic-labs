Distinguishing between Direct and Parametric Driving in Nanomechanics Using a Vibrating Carbon Nanotube
Sam Dicker, Patrick Steger, Deepanjan Das, Saba M. Khan, Edward A. Laird

Abstract
Parametric driving is a powerful route to amplification and nonlinear control in nanomechanical resonators, but its signatures can be ambiguous because standard dc electrical readout does not directly reveal the frequency of motion. Here we resolve this ambiguity by measuring the motional frequency of a vibrating carbon nanotube independently of the drive frequency. We operate the nanotube as an electromechanical mixer and detect microwave sidebands using a low-noise superconducting amplifier. This frequency-resolved readout distinguishes direct motion of the first overtone from parametric motion of the fundamental, even when the corresponding drive frequencies nearly coincide. The two mechanisms are further separated by their drive-power dependence. Beyond conventional parametric resonance at 2 ​ f 0 2f_{0} , we observe responses to driving at 3 ​ f 0 3f_{0} and 4 ​ f 0 4f_{0} , consistent with high-order parametric excitation associated with nonlinear stiffness terms. Keywords: Nanomechanics, Carbon nanotubes, Parametric resonance

A resonator can be driven directly, by applying a force near the resonant frequency, or parametrically, by modulating a system parameter at a frequency typically near twice the resonant frequency
3
.
In electronics and optics, parametric driving is the basis for ultra-low-noise amplifiers
2
, noise squeezing, and the generation of entangled photons
21
.
In mechanical resonators, parametric coupling has been harnessed for amplification and thermomechanical noise squeezing
24
, for studying synchronization
26
, and for detecting dielectric forces
30
.
Parametric nanomechanical experiments are challenging because the nonlinearity that gives rise to parametric responses is typically weak.
However, carbon nanotube resonators have large and tunable nonlinearity because of the strong dependence of tension on gate voltage, which is further enhanced by Coulomb blockade
27
;
16
.
This makes them ideal for studying parametric amplification
10
and mode coupling
6
;
9
.
Nevertheless, distinguishing directly driven from parametrically driven motion is difficult in the standard detection scheme, which measures the quasi-dc current through the moving nanotube
25
, especially because, for a tension-dominated string, the strongest parametric drive of the fundamental mode occurs near the frequency of the first overtone
22
;
15
.
(We label the fundamental transverse mode as the zeroth mode and its first overtone as the first mode.)

Here we show how to distinguish direct and parametric responses by directly measuring the frequency of motion in a nanomechanical carbon nanotube resonator.
We do this by operating the device as a mixer and detecting the up-converted electromechanical sideband using a microwave amplification chain incorporating a near-quantum-limited superconducting amplifier.
We identify the parametric responses when driving near twice a zeroth-mode frequency, and distinguish them from the directly driven motion in a first mode, for which the frequencies are nearby but distinct.
This method allows us to identify high-order parametric resonances, not previously seen in nanomechanics, when driving at three and four times a zeroth-mode frequency.
These are signatures of high-order nonlinearities of the nanotube stiffness.

To explain these results, we extend the theory of parametric resonance by including high-order nonlinearities of the spring constant.
An analogous electrical high-order parametric driving has previously been predicted
34
and measured in a superconducting resonator at three, four, and five times its zeroth-mode frequency
29

The equation of motion for a nonlinear resonator with one relevant mode at
f_{0}
is

\ddot{u}+\frac{2\pi f_{0}}{Q}\dot{u}+\frac{1}{M}\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}k_{\ell}u^{\ell}=0

(4)

where
u(t)
is displacement as a function of time
t

Q
the quality factor,
M
the mass,
k_{\ell}
the
\ell
th stiffness coefficient of the resonator, and
2\pi f_{0}=\sqrt{k_{1}/M}
.
Parametric driving can be induced by modulating the stiffness coefficients about their average values:

\displaystyle k_{\ell}(t)

\displaystyle=\bar{k}_{\ell}+\delta k_{\ell}(t)

(5)

\displaystyle=\bar{k}_{\ell}\,[1+h_{\ell}\cos(2\pi f_{\text{drive}}t)],

(6)

where
h_{\ell}
are the modulation amplitudes.
Given an initial displacement
u(t)\propto\cos(2\pi f_{0}t)
, driving can occur when
\delta k_{\ell}(t)\,u^{\ell}(t)\propto\cos(2\pi f_{\text{drive}}t)\,\cos^{\ell}(2\pi f_{0}t)
contains a term at the mechanical frequency
f_{0}
.
This condition is satisfied when
f_{\text{drive}}=(\ell+1)f_{0}
for some integer
\ell
.

We have integrated a carbon nanotube resonator with a near-quantum-limited TWPA, thus combining two technologies for high force sensitivity and low-noise electrical amplification.
The resulting sensitivity and bandwidth allow us to distinguish unambiguously between direct and parametric driving by measuring the frequency of the motion directly.
Identifying the underlying mechanical spectrum of a resonator in the presence of parametric resonances, which cannot be cleanly distinguished in dc current alone, enables better characterization of the mode structure and may be useful for nanomechanical mass sensors
11
;
7
, in which knowing the shape of different modes is necessary to infer the added mass.
Our technique also offers a way to detect high-order nonlinearities in the stiffness coefficient and to drive at three or four times the frequency of motion.

In superconducting electronics, parametric coupling has allowed for ultra-sensitive amplifiers and control of single-photon states.
In nanomechanics, this ability to control and characterize parametric effects is a step toward mechanical amplifiers
20
;
1
;
10
capable of high force and mass sensitivity.
Ultimately, it may allow for detection of phase superpositions in flexural modes of the entire nanotube
34
.

C
Details of the Measurement Circuit

Figure

S5
and
table

S1
detail the full circuitry for simultaneous dc and ac measurements.
The nanotube was driven using an rf signal generator at frequency
f_{\text{drive}}
applied to the gate, and probed using a tone at
f_{\text{probe}}=5
GHz applied to the source.
Both signals were applied via attenuators for cooling and bias tees for adding dc voltages.
The current through the device was separated using a bias tee into dc and ac measurement chains.
The dc measurement chain was an I/V converter followed by a digital voltmeter.
The ac chain consisted of a Josephson TWPA (JTWPA) at 12 mK followed by a HEMT amplifier at 4 K and a room-temperature low-noise amplifier (LNA); a series of isolators in the chain protect the device from the amplifier pump tone and thermal noise.
The JTWPA was a pre-commercial AI-TWPA-C from Arctic Instruments, consisting of a periodic array of Josephson junction-based meta-atoms embedded in a thin-film waveguide structure
21
.
The JTWPA supports broadband non-degenerate three-wave mixing when biased with an external dc flux and driven by a microwave pump tone.
It was pumped at
12
GHz and
5.5\times 10^{-4}
W, with a flux bias applied from a battery-powered source.
The flux bias was set using a current of
819~\mu
A, which over a range from
4
to
8
GHz was found to give a gain and noise temperature of
\sim 15
dB and
\sim 500
mK respectively, approximately three times the standard quantum limit.
To stabilize the gain of the LNA, which was affected by fluctuations in the room temperature (despite air-conditioning) a
heat sink and a fan
were used to keep it at a stable temperature.
Following amplification, the output power was measured using Spectrum Analyzer 1, which was set to acquire near
f_{\text{probe}}+f_{\text{sb}}
.

We found that the gain and noise of the TWPA were degraded by leakage of
f_{\text{drive}}
and
f_{\text{probe}}
past the nanotube and into its input.
To prevent this, we added a pair of cancellation lines in order to suppress these signals via destructive interference at the TWPA input.
Each line allowed for tunable amplitude and phase, and successful cancellation was monitored using Spectrum Analyzer 2 to ensure that the leaked power through the ac chain was close to zero.

To set the attenuation and phase in the drive cancellation path, before each sweep,
f_{\text{drive}}
was varied over the entire measurement range, and at each setting, the setpoints for phase and attenuation were adjusted to give near-zero leakage power.
After fitting to functions of a suitable shape, this calibration data can then be used to calculate appropriate parameters for the cancellation path for all setpoints of the experiment.

In the probe cancellation path, even though
f_{\text{probe}}
was not changed, this procedure was found to be inadequate, because the transmission was not stable over the course of a sweep.
We therefore implemented the following dynamic procedure for ensuring the cancellation condition was met.
First, the phase shifter in the path was set to approximately the correct value, determined by minimizing the leakage signal at Spectrum Analyzer 2.
Then, a variable attenuator, in parallel with the phase shifter, was used for fine adjustment of the phase, followed by fine adjustment of the amplitude by adjusting an amplifier gain. (This was achieved by tuning the amplifier supply voltage.)
In order to maintain cancellation, the variable attenuator and the variable amplifier were adjusted up to approximately once per second during the sweep, using a computer-controlled feedback loop to minimize the leakage to Spectrum Analyzer 2.

The largest contribution to the fluctuations in the feedback paths came from the varying temperature of the lab, which affected the phase through the cables and amplifiers.
To suppress cable fluctuations, we used nominally matched pairs of cables, and where that was not possible, phase-stable cables that minimize the effect of Teflon temperature dependence (the “Teflon knee”
5
).
To reduce amplifier fluctuations, the components in the microwave cancellation path were placed in an insulated box.

Origin of Electromechanical Signals

In this section we explain the origin of the electromechanical signals appearing in the dc and ac measurements.
The instantaneous current through the nanotube (approximating linear conductance
G
) is

I(t)=V_{\text{sd}}(t)\,G(u(t))

(S1)

where the source-drain bias is

V_{\text{sd}}(t)=V_{\text{sd,0}}+V_{\text{sd,p},0}\,\cos(\omega_{\text{probe}}t)+V_{\text{sd,d},0}\,\cos\theta_{\text{drive}}

(S2)

This bias includes the applied dc bias
V_{\text{sd,0}}
, the applied probe voltage
V_{\text{sd,p},0}\cos(\omega_{\text{probe}}t)
, and a presumed contribution
V_{\text{sd,d},0}\cos\theta_{\text{drive}}
due to asymmetric parasitic coupling of the drive frequency to the contact electrodes, where

\theta_{\text{drive}}\equiv\omega_{\text{drive}}t+\phi_{\text{drive}}

(S3)

and
\phi_{\text{drive}}
is the phase of the parasitic coupling.
(As usual,
\omega_{\text{probe}}\equiv 2\pi f_{\text{probe}}
, and similarly for other frequencies.)

The conductance is
G(u(t))
, which depends on the displacement because the nanotube is a single-electron transistor
8
, can be expanded as

\displaystyle G(t)

\displaystyle=G_{0}+G^{\prime}u(t)+\frac{1}{2}G^{\prime\prime}u^{2}(t)+\ldots

(S4)

\displaystyle=G_{0}+G^{\prime}A\cos\theta_{\text{m}}+\frac{1}{4}G^{\prime\prime}A^{2}(1+\cos 2\theta_{\text{m}})+\ldots

(S5)

where the displacement is

u(t)=A\cos\theta_{\text{m}}.

(S6)

Here
A
is the displacement amplitude,

\displaystyle\theta_{\text{m}}

\displaystyle\equiv\omega_{\text{m}}t+\phi_{\text{m}}

(S7)

and
\phi_{\text{m}}
is the phase of the motion.

The experiment is sensitive to contributions of
I(t)
at frequencies near dc or near
\omega_{\text{probe}}
.
Substituting
eq.

S2
and
eq.

S5
into
eq.

S1
and keeping only those terms gives

\displaystyle I(t)=

\displaystyle\hbox to0.0pt{\text{(terms independent of displacement)}\hss}

(S8)

\displaystyle+\frac{V_{\text{sd,0}}G^{\prime\prime}A^{2}}{4}

\displaystyle+V_{\text{sd,p},0}G^{\prime}A\cos\omega_{\text{probe}}t\,\cos\theta_{\text{m}}

\displaystyle+\frac{V_{\text{sd,p},0}G^{\prime\prime}A^{2}}{4}\cos\omega_{\text{probe}}t\,(1+\cos 2\theta_{\text{m}})

\displaystyle+V_{\text{sd,d},0}G^{\prime}A\cos\theta_{\text{drive}}\,\cos\theta_{\text{m}}

\displaystyle+\frac{V_{\text{sd,d},0}G^{\prime\prime}A^{2}}{4}\cos\theta_{\text{drive}}\,\cos 2\theta_{\text{m}}

\displaystyle+\cdots

The displacement therefore leads to a dc contribution

I_{\text{dc}}=\frac{V_{\text{sd,0}}G^{\prime\prime}A^{2}}{4}+V_{\text{sd,d},0}G^{\prime}A\langle\cos\theta_{\text{drive}}\cos\theta_{\text{m}}\rangle+\frac{V_{\text{sd,d},0}G^{\prime\prime}A^{2}}{4}\langle\cos\theta_{\text{drive}}\cos 2\theta_{\text{m}}\rangle

(S9)

and ac contributions

\displaystyle I_{\omega_{\text{probe}}\pm\omega_{\text{m}}}(t)

\displaystyle=V_{\text{sd,p},0}G^{\prime}A\cos\omega_{\text{probe}}t\cos\theta_{\text{m}}

(S10)

\displaystyle I_{\omega_{\text{probe}}\pm 2\omega_{\text{m}}}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{V_{\text{sd,p},0}G^{\prime\prime}A^{2}}{4}\cos\omega_{\text{probe}}t\cos 2\theta_{\text{m}}

(S11)

at
\omega_{\text{probe}}\pm\omega_{\text{m}}
,
\omega_{\text{probe}}\pm 2\omega_{\text{m}}
, and so on.
These are the signals amplified by the ac measurement chain and appearing in the measured spectrum as in
fig.

1
.
Higher-order sidebands arise from higher terms in
eq.

S5
not modeled here.

The dc measurement is sensitive to the contribution in
eq.

S9
.
The ac measurement is sensitive to the contributions in
eqs.

S10
to
S11
, which appear as sidebands to the probe tone in the output.

E
High-order parametric excitations

E.1
Derivation of the high-order parametric excitation frequencies

Here we derive the drive frequencies at which parametric resonance is excited by modulating nonlinear spring coefficients.
We model a resonator subject to a restoring force

F(u)=-k(u)\,u

(S12)

where
u(t)
is the displacement as a function of time
t
and
k(u)\equiv\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}k_{\ell}\,u^{\ell-1}
is the nonlinear spring coefficient.
Under parametric driving, the equation of motion, obtained by combining
eqs.

4
to
6
, is

\frac{d^{2}u(t)}{dt^{2}}+\frac{\omega_{0}}{Q}\frac{du(t)}{dt}+\frac{1}{M}\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}\bar{k}_{\ell}\,(1+h_{\ell}\cos\omega_{\text{drive}}t)\,\bigl[u(t)\bigr]^{\ell}=0.

(S13)

As in the main text,
\omega_{0}\equiv\sqrt{\overline{k_{1}}/M}
is the mode frequency,
Q
the quality factor,
M
the mass,
\omega_{\text{drive}}>0
the drive frequency, and
h_{\ell}
a set of parameters that characterize the strength of the drive.

We identify those drive frequencies at which a small initial displacement is amplified.
To do this, we first rewrite
eq.

S13
in terms of two small parameters
g
and
h
, by defining

\displaystyle\frac{\bar{k}_{\ell}}{M}

\displaystyle\equiv g\,\alpha_{\ell},\qquad\ell=2,3,4,\ldots

(S14)

\displaystyle\frac{\bar{k}_{\ell}h_{\ell}}{M}

\displaystyle\equiv h\,\beta_{\ell},\qquad\ell=1,2,3,\ldots.

(S15)

For simplicity, we ignore damping by setting
Q\rightarrow\infty
.

Equation

S13
then becomes

\frac{d^{2}u(t)}{dt^{2}}+\omega_{0}^{2}u(t)+g\sum_{\ell=2}^{\infty}\alpha_{\ell}\,\bigl[u(t)\bigr]^{\ell}+h\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}\beta_{\ell}\,\bigl[u(t)\bigr]^{\ell}\cos\omega_{\text{drive}}t=0.

(S16)

We now do perturbation theory
19
in
h
, by writing

u(t)\approx u_{0}(t)+hu_{h}(t)

(S17)

and requiring
eq.

S16
to be satisfied at zeroth and first order in
h
. This gives:

\displaystyle\frac{d^{2}u_{0}(t)}{dt^{2}}+\omega_{0}^{2}u_{0}(t)+g\sum_{\ell=2}^{\infty}\alpha_{\ell}\,[u_{0}(t)]^{\ell}

\displaystyle=0

(S18)

\displaystyle\frac{d^{2}u_{h}(t)}{dt^{2}}+\omega_{0}^{2}u_{h}(t)

\displaystyle=-g\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}(\ell+1)\,\alpha_{\ell+1}\,[u_{0}(t)]^{\ell}u_{h}(t)-\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}\beta_{\ell}\,[u_{0}(t)]^{\ell}\cos\omega_{\text{drive}}t.

(S19)

Equation

S18
describes unforced motion in an anharmonic potential.
The displacement is assumed to be small, so the anharmonicity can be neglected and the general solution is

u_{0}(t)=A_{0}\cos(\omega_{0}t+\phi_{0})

(S20)

where
A_{0}
and
\phi_{0}
are arbitrary constants.
The left-hand side of
Equation

S19
describes harmonic motion of
u_{h}(t)
, with driving on the right-hand side.
The amplitude will increase if the right-hand side
contains secular terms, i.e., components at the frequency
\omega_{0}
.
These can arise from the anharmonicity alone (via the first sum) or from the driving (via the second sum).
Since we want to know the effect of the drive, we neglect the former (which is also justified in the limit of weak anharmonicity, i.e.,
g\rightarrow 0
).
Substituting
eq.

S20
into
eq.

S19
then gives

\displaystyle\frac{d^{2}u_{h}(t)}{dt^{2}}+\omega_{0}^{2}u_{h}(t)

\displaystyle=-\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}\beta_{\ell}\,A_{0}^{\ell}\,\cos^{\ell}(\omega_{0}t+\phi_{0})\,\cos\omega_{\text{drive}}t

(S21)

\displaystyle=-\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}\beta_{\ell}\,A_{0}^{\ell}\,\sum_{m=0}^{\lfloor\ell/2\rfloor}\frac{2-\delta_{2m,\ell}}{2^{\ell}}\frac{\ell!}{m!(\ell-m)!}\,\cos[(\ell-2m)(\omega_{0}t+\phi_{0})]\,\cos\omega_{\text{drive}}t

(S22)

where the second line follows from a trigonometric identity.

To see when the right-hand side of
eq.

S22
contains terms at
\omega_{0}
, expand

\cos\bigl[(\ell-2m)(\omega_{0}t+\phi_{0})\bigr]\cos\omega_{\text{drive}}t=\frac{\cos\bigl[(\ell-2m)(\omega_{0}t+\phi_{0})+\omega_{\text{drive}}t\bigr]+\cos\bigl[(\ell-2m)(\omega_{0}t+\phi_{0})-\omega_{\text{drive}}t\bigr]}{2}.

(S23)

This contains components at
\omega_{0}
when

\bigl|(\ell-2m)\omega_{0}\pm\omega_{\text{drive}}\bigr|=\omega_{0}.

(S24)

For each value of
\ell
represented in
eq.

S22
, the corresponding parametric driving frequencies are those that satisfy
eq.

S24
for some value of
m
, i.e.,

\frac{\omega_{\text{drive}}}{\omega_{0}}=\bigl|\ell-2m\pm 1\bigr|,\qquad m=0,1,2,\dots,\left\lfloor\frac{\ell}{2}\right\rfloor,

(S25)

or more simply (remembering that
\omega_{\text{drive}}
is restricted to be positive):

\frac{\omega_{\text{drive}}}{\omega_{0}}=\begin{cases}1,3,\,\dots,\ell+1,&\ell\ \text{even},\\[4.0pt]
2,4,\,\dots,\,\ell+1,&\ell\ \text{odd}.\end{cases}

(S26)

As expected, the first-order case
\ell=1
, which corresponds to conventional parametric driving through modulation of the linear spring constant, leads to the well-known primary parametric resonance
14
, visible e.g., in
fig.

2
, at

\omega_{\text{drive}}=2\omega_{0},

(S27)

for which the motion is at frequency
\omega_{\text{drive}}/2
.
(This is the
n=1
case of
eq.

3
.)

High-order parametric driving occurs when the modulation has
\ell\geq 2
.
Parametric resonance at
\omega_{\text{drive}}/\omega_{0}=3
or 4, as in
fig.

5
, arises from the
\ell=2
and
\ell=3
contributions to
eq.

S13
.
The resonances observed in
fig.

5
are the final elements in the sequence
eq.

S26
, i.e.,
\omega_{\text{drive}}=(\ell+1)\omega_{0}
.

The
n>1
cases of
eq.

3
arise when
\ell=1
and the perturbation expansion of
eq.

S17
is expanded to higher order
19
;
14
.
This expansion shows that the parametric drive frequency can be smaller than
eq.

S27
, but never larger as in
eq.

S26
.

Figure

5
is therefore unambiguous evidence of high-order parametric resonance, i.e., modulation of a
k_{\ell}
term with
\ell>1
.

It has been pointed out by Zhang
et al.

for the case
\ell=2
that a variant of this mechanism is for direct driving at
(\ell+1)\omega_{0}
to couple via the

term in
eq.

In this case, the direct drive at
(\ell+1)\omega_{0}

For simplicity (and because our data do not distinguish between the two contributions) we have not included in our model this contribution to the high-order parametric driving, although it is likely to be present.

E.2
Excluding other causes of subharmonics

While our data clearly do not arise from generation of harmonics, there are in principle sources of subharmonics that at first sight resemble a parametric response.
Here we consider these and say why they do not explain our data.

Figure S6:
Spectrum of the rf signal generator when
f_{\text{drive}}=520
MHz, measured as in the inset. No spectral impurity appears at
f_{\text{drive}}/2
; the weak impurity sidebands offset by
\sim 32
kHz do not mimic parametric resonances.

Firstly, a spectrum similar to
fig.

2
would arise if the drive tone at
f_{\text{drive}}
contained a spectral impurity at
f_{\text{drive}}/2
.
To exclude this possibility,
fig.

S6
shows the spectrum of the
f_{\text{drive}}
signal generator set to a similar frequency.
Any impurity at
f_{\text{drive}}/2
is smaller than the main tone by at least 12 orders of magnitude, and therefore cannot explain the
f_{\text{drive}}/2
peak in
fig.

2
, which is smaller by less than 1 order of magnitude.
The probe signal may also contain spectral impurities, but these would be easily identifiable in
fig.

S3
or
fig.

3
as having no dependence on gate voltage.

Secondly, period-doubling or period-multiplication can accompany a stable resonance of a directly driven anharmonic resonator
12
.
In this situation, which is distinct from parametric resonance, the resonator moves primarily at the driving frequency
f_{\text{drive}}
, but also with components at
f_{\text{drive}}/2
or lower fractions.
While superficially this resembles the data of
fig.

2
and
fig.

5

If it did, the peaks in
fig.

3
(c) would track the positions of the first mode pair (i.e. the strong peaks in
fig.

3

Instead, they track the zeroth modes, confirming that these are the modes being excited.

F
Derivation of equations (1) and (2)

Here we derive
eqs.

1
to
2
, used to fit
fig.

4
.
The driving force, arising from the gate voltage, is

\displaystyle F(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}C^{\prime}(V_{\text{G,dc}}+V_{\text{G},0}^{\text{drive}}\cos\omega_{\text{drive}}t)^{2}

(S28)

\displaystyle\propto V_{\text{G,dc}}^{2}+2V_{\text{G,dc}}V_{\text{G},0}^{\text{drive}}\cos\omega_{\text{drive}}t+\frac{1}{2}(V_{\text{G},0}^{\text{drive}})^{2}(1+\cos 2\omega_{\text{drive}}t)

(S29)

\displaystyle\approx F_{0}+F_{\text{drive}}\cos\omega_{\text{drive}}t

(S30)

where
C^{\prime}
is the derivative of the gate capacitance with respect to
u
, and
V_{\text{G,dc}}
and
V_{\text{G},0}^{\text{drive}}
characterize the static and oscillating parts of the gate voltage, respectively.
The amplitude of the driving force at the drive frequency is therefore

F_{\text{drive}}=C^{\prime}V_{\text{G,dc}}V_{\text{G},0}^{\text{drive}}\propto\sqrt{P_{\text{drive}}}.

(S31)

In a directly driven linear resonator, the resulting amplitude is

A=\frac{F_{\text{drive}}}{M\Gamma\omega_{0}}

(S32)

where
\Gamma\equiv\omega_{0}/Q
is the linear damping coefficient.
The output power is measured on the upper sideband and therefore, by
eq.

S10
,

\displaystyle P_{\text{out}}

\displaystyle\propto\langle I^{2}_{\omega_{\text{probe}}+\omega_{\text{m}}}\rangle\propto A^{2}\propto F_{\text{drive}}^{2}

(S33)

\displaystyle\propto P_{\text{drive}}

(S34)

which is
eq.

1
.

In parametric driving, the relationship between driving strength and amplitude is more complicated.
In the simplest case, which presumably applies in
fig.

4
, driving is via modulation at
2\omega_{0}
of the linear
k_{1}
component of the stiffness.
The force component that this contributes at
\omega_{0}
is proportional to both the modulation and the amplitude of the oscillations, i.e., is an effective negative damping.
If the driven resonator were otherwise linear, there would be two possible outcomes: for weak parametric driving the intrinsic damping would overwhelm the drive and the displacement would be zero; for strong driving the displacement would increase without bound.
The existence of stable parametric oscillations, as in
fig.

4
(c,d), shows that either the stiffness or the damping must be nonlinear.

The amplitude in this case is calculated in Ref.
3
.
If the equation of motion is

\frac{d^{2}u(t)}{dt^{2}}+\left(\Gamma+\Gamma^{\text{(nl)}}u^{2}(t)\right)\frac{du(t)}{dt}+\omega_{0}^{2}u(t)+\gamma u^{3}(t)=\frac{h_{1}\bar{k}_{1}}{M}u(t)\cos 2\omega_{0}t

(S35)

where
\Gamma^{\text{(nl)}}
and
\gamma\equiv k_{3}/M
are the nonlinear damping and Duffing coefficients, respectively, then the stable non-zero amplitude
A
satisfies

\left(\Gamma+\frac{\Gamma^{\text{(nl)}}}{4}A^{2}\right)^{2}+\left(\frac{3\gamma}{4\omega_{0}}A^{2}\right)^{2}=\left(\frac{h_{1}\bar{k}_{1}}{2M\omega_{0}}\right)^{2}

(S36)

The solution of
eq.

S36
with real
A
is

A^{2}=\sqrt{\frac{h_{1}^{2}\bar{k}_{1}^{2}}{M^{2}\omega_{0}^{2}K}-\left(\frac{3\Gamma\gamma}{\omega_{0}K}\right)^{2}}-\frac{\Gamma\Gamma^{\text{(nl)}}}{K}

(S37)

where

K\equiv\left(\frac{\Gamma^{\text{(nl)}}}{2}\right)^{2}+\left(\frac{3\gamma}{2\omega_{0}}\right)^{2}.

(S38)

Using

\displaystyle A^{2}

\displaystyle\propto P_{\text{out}}

(S39)

\displaystyle h_{1}^{2}

\displaystyle\propto P_{\text{drive}}

(S40)

and combining the various proportionality constants and parameters in
eq.

S37
gives

P_{\text{out}}=b(P_{\text{drive}}-P_{\gamma})^{1/2}-P_{\Gamma}

(S41)

which is
eq.

2
.
Here

\displaystyle P_{\gamma}

\displaystyle\propto\gamma^{2}/K^{2}

(S42)

\displaystyle P_{\Gamma}

\displaystyle\propto\Gamma^{\text{(nl)}}/K

(S43)

are parameters expressing the relative importance of nonlinear damping and nonlinear restoring force in determining the stable amplitude, and
b
is an overall proportionality constant, parameterizing among other things the overall strength of the parametric drive.

Figure

4
(b, d) was fitted using least-squares regression, with each datapoint weighted as if its uncertainty is proportional to its power, and with the fitting range judged to be where the resonance peak is visible but not distorted.
Panel (d) shows two fits, one with
P_{\gamma}
constrained to be zero,
giving
b=(1.6\pm 0.3)\times 10^{-7}~\text{W}^{1/2}
and
P_{\Gamma}=(1.2\pm 0.3)\times 10^{-10}
W,
and one with
P_{\Gamma}
constrained to be zero,
giving
b=(1.0\pm 0.2)\times 10^{-7}~\text{W}^{1/2}
and
P_{\gamma}=(6.30\pm 0.02)\times 10^{-7}
W.
A fit with both
P_{\gamma}
and
P_{\Gamma}
as free parameters, but constrained to be non-negative, converges to the fit with
P_{\gamma}=0
.
