Construction and validation of a machine learning-based prediction model for in-hospital acute kidney injury in patients with lung cancer complicated with sepsis: clinical and nursing applications
Authors: Huiping Zhang, Jinyan Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAcute kidney injury (AKI) represents a critical complication in patients with lung cancer (LC) complicated by sepsis, exerting a substantial adverse impact on their prognosis. However, to date, no studies have been conducted to predict the risk of AKI in this specific patient population. The primary objective of this study is to develop and rigorously validate seven machine learning (ML) algorithms tailored to predict the probability of in-hospital AKI in patients with LC complicated by sepsis, while simultaneously evaluating their utility in clinical and nursing workflows.MethodsWe retrieved retrospective clinical records pertaining to LC patients presenting with concurrent sepsis directly from the Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care (MIMIC)-IV dataset. All the independent variables were derived from the first measurement records within 24 h after the patient’s admission, the AKI diagnosis period was from 24 h after admission until discharge. Subsequent to a rigorous data preprocessing phase, we employed a randomized splitting protocol to segregate the dataset into distinct training (70%) and testing (30%) cohorts. To authenticate model generalizability, an external validation phase was executed utilizing records from the electronic Intensive Care Unit Collaborative Research Database (eICU-CRD). Feature selection was accomplished through the deployment of both univariate and multivariate logistic regression (LR) methodologies. Seven ML models were developed model performance was evaluated using a comprehensive suite of metrics, prominently including the area under the curve (AUC), sensitivity and accuracy. Additionally, the optimal model was subjected to interpretability analysis utilizing Shapley additive explanations (SHAP).ResultsIn the MIMIC-IV dataset, 523 cases developed AKI, accounting for 79.00%. Among them, 362 cases were male (54.68%), with an average age of (standard deviation) 68.76 (10.89) years, and body mass index (BMI) was 31.34 (141.57) kg/m2, etc. Our analysis identified a distinct profile of independent predictors for in-hospital AKI within this population, specifically: age, body weight, prothrombin time (PT), Acute Physiology Score III (APSIII) score, calcium, glucose, sodium, and potassium. Of the seven ML architectures evaluated, the neural network (NNET) yielded the highest predictive performance, securing AUC values of 0.864 (training), 0.760 (testing), and 0.785 (validation).ConclusionsBy integrating variables such as age, weight, PT, APSIII score, and key electrolytes, the NNET model functions as a potential prognostic instrument for estimating in-hospital AKI risk in patients with LC complicated by sepsis.

Highlight box

Key findings

• We developed a machine learning (ML)-based prediction model for in-hospital acute kidney injury (AKI) in patients with lung cancer (LC) complicated by sepsis, followed by rigorous external validation.

What is known and what is new?

• AKI represents a prevalent complication of sepsis. While prior studies have focused on AKI risk prediction, the AKI risk profile specific to the population of LC patients with concurrent sepsis remains unexplored.

• Our study is the first to identify multiple independent risk factors for in-hospital AKI in this patient cohort and to establish an ML-derived prediction model.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• The independent risk factors identified in this study can inform clinical practice by guiding early monitoring and prompt intervention targeting these indicators, with the aim of mitigating the risk of in-hospital AKI in patients with LC complicated by sepsis.

Introduction

Globally, lung cancer (LC) ranks among the most prevalent malignancies, representing roughly 11.6% of all oncological diagnoses and frequently correlating with adverse clinical outcomes (1-3). Recent technological strides have catalyzed a paradigm shift in LC management, moving away from traditional chemoradiotherapy in favor of targeted agents and immunotherapeutic interventions. In particular, immunotherapy has shown promising outcomes in patients with non-small cell LC (4,5). Nevertheless, the progressive nature of the malignancy, combined with immunotherapy regimens, frequently undermines immune competence in LC patients, thereby heightening their vulnerability to septic complications (6). Sepsis persists as a primary contributor to global mortality statistics, accounting for approximately 19.7% of all recorded deaths (7,8). The concurrent manifestation of LC and sepsis poses a formidable clinical challenge regarding both therapeutic management and prognostication. Among the organs affected during sepsis, the kidneys are especially vulnerable (9). Existing epidemiological evidence highlights that approximately 68% of the septic patient population encounters acute kidney injury (AKI) during the incipient phases of the pathology, with a substantial subset (40%) progressing to severe renal injury stages (10,11). Moreover, LC patients frequently exhibit compromised renal function consequent to the administration of nephrotoxic chemotherapy agents, such as cisplatin, and various targeted therapies. Whether for patients with LC or those with sepsis, the complication of AKI is a major challenge they face. However, previous studies have mostly focused on predicting the individual risk of AKI in patients with sepsis. For the specific patient group with both LC and sepsis, the coexistence of the two diseases makes the pathophysiological mechanism of the patients more complex. The previous single sepsis prediction models are obviously not applicable. Consequently, an imperative exists for profound investigation into AKI specifically within the demographic of patients suffering from LC complicated by sepsis.

As a pivotal domain of artificial intelligence in medicine, machine learning (ML) offers distinct capabilities for processing vast, high-dimensional datasets, enabling the elucidation of intricate non-linear patterns that traditional methodologies might overlook (12,13). In contrast to conventional predictive frameworks, ML algorithms typically demonstrate superior precision and classification efficacy. Previous studies have applied ML to predict AKI in patients with sepsis alone (14,15). The pathophysiological mechanism of LC complicated with sepsis is complex, and there are complex nonlinear relationships and high-dimensional interactions between related features. Only relying on the traditional LR model cannot effectively predict the risk of AKI in patients, so the use of ML algorithm is necessary. Multiple ML models must be used and compared to each other to determine the optimal model. So, we retrieved relevant patient data from the Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care (MIMIC)-IV database and engineered a diverse suite of ML models—ranging from Logistic regression (LR), support vector machine (SVM), gradient boosting machine (GBM), neural network (NNET), extreme gradient boosting (XGBoost), adaptive boosting (AdaBoost), and categorical boosting (CatBoost)—to assess AKI risk in this cohort. We executed external validation utilizing matched records from the electronic Intensive Care Unit Collaborative Research Database (eICU-CRD), with the ultimate goal of establishing a reliable reference framework for clinical decision-making. We present this article in accordance with the TRIPOD reporting checklist (available at https://jtd.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/jtd-2026-0558/rc).

Methods

Data source

Retrospective clinical data were retrieved directly from MIMIC-IV 3.1 and eICU-CRD 2.0, encompassing multi-center patient records. Data access certification was obtained following the completion of required coursework and strict adherence to the regulatory protocols of the databases. The extraction of data was principally executed through the utilization of Navicat Premium 16.0 (Premium Soft Cyber Tech, Hong Kong, China) in conjunction with Structured Query Language (SQL). Due to the complete anonymization of patient records within these repositories, the requirement for ethical board approval was waived for this investigation. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments.

Data collection

Inclusion criteria: the identification of the study cohort relied on specific ICD-9 and ICD-10 diagnostic codes corresponding to sepsis and LC. Exclusion criteria: Exclusion criteria encompassed duplicate entries, ICU admissions lasting under 24 hours, and cases presenting with incomplete essential datasets. Strict adherence to these selection protocols yielded a final analytic sample of 662 patients from the MIMIC-IV database and 230 from the eICU-CRD. All the independent variable characteristics were obtained from the first measurement data within 24 hours after the patient’s admission to the ICU, and the outcome indicator data were obtained from the time period from 24 hours after the patient’s admission to the hospital until discharge. The extracted data primarily included: (I) general information: age, weight, and others; (II) past medical history: chronic kidney disease, myocardial infarction, etc.; (III) vital signs: blood pressure, heart rate, and related parameters; (IV) clinical metrics: indices such as the Acute Physiology Score III (APSIII), sequential organ failure assessment (SOFA) score, and Glasgow coma scale (GCS); (V) laboratory parameters: markers including white blood cell (WBC) count and prothrombin time (PT).

ML model development and evaluation

After standardized data cleaning, the dataset was randomly divided into a training set (70%) and a testing set (30%). The eICU-CRD dataset was utilized as an independent external cohort to validate the generalizability and robustness of the developed models. To isolate potential and independent predictors of in-hospital AKI, we performed both univariate and multivariate LR analyses specifically on the training subset of LC patients with sepsis. In the process of single-factor and multi-factor LR analysis, all tests were two-sided, and P<0.05 indicated a significant difference. Guided by the confirmed independent risk factors, a diverse array of ML architectures was developed, comprising LR, SVM, GBM, NNET, XGBoost, AdaBoost, and CatBoost. Feature importance was evaluated within each model. Evaluation of model robustness involved multiple metrics: receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves, decision curve analysis (DCA) and precision, etc. Finally, Shapley additive explanations (SHAP) visualization is used to further explain the optimal model to address the black box effect of ML models.

Outcome measures

The principal endpoint defined for this study was the incidence of AKI, adjudicated in strict adherence to the diagnostic framework established by the Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes (KDIGO) guidelines (16). All the independent variable characteristics are defined as the first measurement results within 24 hours after the patient’s admission, and the outcome indicator variables are defined as the period from 24 hours after admission to discharge of the patient. We defined AKI using the following parameters: a serum creatinine elevation of ≥0.3 mg/dL (≥26.5 µmol/L) occurring within 48 hours; a reduction in urine output to <0.5 mL/kg/h sustained for 6 hours; or a surge in serum creatinine to ≥1.5 times the baseline level, presumed to have transpired within the preceding 7 days.

Statistical analysis

Patient data were first subjected to data cleaning. For variables exhibiting a missing rate below 20%, the predictive mean matching (PMM) algorithm was employed for imputation, whereas cases exceeding this threshold were systematically removed. First, use the regression model to calculate the predicted mean of the target variable for all samples; for each missing value, find the five candidates whose predicted mean is closest to it among the non-missing samples, with the donor quantity set to 5. Then randomly select one of them, repeat the process and generate five complete datasets, and randomly select one of them as the final interpolation result. Duplicate and abnormal samples were removed. We implemented the SMOTE to effectively mitigate issues related to dataset imbalance. A 10-fold cross-validation strategy was adopted during the training phase to maximize model stability and performance. We reported continuous variables as means ± standard deviations. For comparative analysis, the two-independent-samples t-test was utilized for data exhibiting normal distribution and homogeneity of variance, whereas non-parametric data underwent evaluation via the Mann-Whitney U test. Categorical data were summarized as frequencies and percentages, with statistical significance determined via Chi-squared testing. All tests were two-sided, and P<0.05 indicated a significant difference. Computational analysis and statistical modeling were conducted via the R environment (version 4.3.2, Vienna, Austria) and Python (version 3.10, Austin, TX, USA).

Results

Baseline characteristics

Our screening protocols yielded a final analytic cohort of 662 individuals from the MIMIC-IV database, among whom 523 (79.0%) developed in-hospital AKI. Among them, 362 were male (54.68%), with an average age of (standard deviation) 68.76 (10.89) years, and a BMI of 31.34 (141.57) kg/m2. In the eICU-CRD, 230 patients were obtained, among which 101 were male (43.9%), with an age of 68.07 (10.88) years and a BMI of 25.78 (6.31) kg/m2. After stratifying patients in the training set by AKI status, significant disparities (P<0.05) emerged between the groups regarding myocardial infarction history, age, weight, APSIII score, Charlson comorbidity index, blood urea nitrogen (BUN), glucose, calcium, electrolytes (K, Na), international normalized ratio (INR), and PT. No significant differences were detected in other characteristics. After the division, the training set consisted of 464 patients, among whom 361 cases developed AKI. The modeling features this time were 8 in total, so the value of events per variable was 45.13, which was much higher than the reference value of 10. This indicates that the research results are robust and the risk of overfitting is low. Testing and validation sets

The residual 30% of the MIMIC-IV cohort constituted the testing set for performance verification. The resulting AUC values for each model were: NNET: 0.760 (95% CI: 0.679, 0.841); LR: 0.737 (95% CI: 0.656, 0.818); SVM: 0.737 (95% CI: 0.656, 0.817); GBM: 0.696 (95% CI: 0.612, 0.870); XGBoost: 0.673 (95% CI: 0.579, 0.767); AdaBoost: 0.683 (95% CI: 0.593, 0.773); and CatBoost: 0.727 (95% CI: 0.649, 0.806). The NNET model showed the best performance, while the XGBoost model performed the worst. The ROC curves for the testing set are shown in Figure 6, and the DCA curves are presented in Figure 7. To further validate the model performance, we conducted external validation using data from patients with LC complicated by sepsis in the eICU-CRD. The NNET model attained an AUC of 0.785 (95% CI: 0.712, 0.858). Corresponding ROC and DCA curves for this validation phase are presented in Figures 8,9. Model interpretation

Synthesizing performance data across all cohorts, the NNET model proved to be the most effective predictor of in-hospital AKI within the LC and sepsis patient population. Therefore, SHAP visualization methods were applied to interpret the NNET model. SHAP analysis highlighted the hierarchy of predictive features, with age, APSIII score, calcium, PT, glucose, weight, sodium, and potassium ranked by influence (see Figure 10 for the importance plot and Figure 11 for the summary plot). Furthermore, the integration of single-instance analysis with SHAP visualization revealed that, in sample 52, variables such as weight, calcium, and glucose acted as positive drivers for AKI risk, while other factors exerted a protective effect (Figure 12, SHAP force plot). Additionally, we examined the marginal effects of key variables, specifically APSIII and age, on the predictive output of the NNET model. As shown in Figure 13, no significant interaction effects were observed.

Discussion

Guided by stringent inclusion and exclusion protocols, we isolated a cohort of 662 patients suffering from LC and sepsis from the MIMIC-IV database. Among them, 523 patients developed in-hospital AKI, resulting in an incidence rate of 79.0%, which is consistent with previous studies (17). A random 70% allocation was assigned to the training set, where multivariate LR analysis successfully identified age, APSIII score, calcium, PT, glucose, weight, sodium, and potassium as independent drivers of in-hospital AKI. Using these predictors, we constructed multiple ML models to assess AKI risk. Among all models, the NNET model demonstrated the best performance across the training, testing, and validation sets, indicating robust classification ability and strong generalizability.

Recently, An et al. (18) utilized ML methodologies to forecast in-hospital mortality risks among sepsis and CKD patients, confirming robust model performance through external validation. However, their study did not address complications in patients with LC complicated by sepsis. Similarly, in 2024, Lin et al. (19) predicted AKI complications in sepsis patients but did not specifically focus on the subpopulation with concurrent LC. Moreover, their study only relied on a nomogram for prediction, did not employ ML methods. Previous studies (14,20,21) have not comprehensively investigated the risk of complications in this specific patient group. As no previous studies have comprehensively examined in-hospital AKI in patients with LC complicated by sepsis, we applied multiple ML methods to predict this risk, providing a reference for clinical practice and exploring the relationships between in-hospital AKI and its independent risk factors.

With the increase of the patient’s age, the kidney will appear glomerular sclerosis, renal tubular atrophy and other phenomena, leading to a significant decline in renal reserve and compensatory capacity. Consequently, elderly patients have an increased risk of in-hospital AKI (10), consistent with previous studies (10). Individuals with obesity frequently display elevated concentrations of pro-inflammatory cytokines relative to normal-weight subjects. In addition, endothelial dysfunction, increased oxidative stress, and venous congestion in obese patients can impair renal perfusion (22-26), resulting in pre-renal injury. These findings align with prior research confirming a robust, positive association between BMI and in-hospital AKI risk within the septic patient population (27,28). Higher APSIII scores serve as indicators of critical illness severity and systemic inflammation, factors that can inflict direct damage on renal vascular endothelium and tubules, thereby amplifying the risk of in-hospital AKI. Previous studies have shown that there is a significant correlation between the patient’s coagulation function and AKI complications (29). Extended PT may precipitate increased blood loss, consequently compromising renal perfusion and diminishing kidney function (30). Hyperkalemia can directly damage renal tubular epithelial cells, while hyponatremia and hypocalcemia can disrupt cellular osmotic pressure and membrane stability. Perturbations in electrolyte balance can induce renal injury and escalate the likelihood of developing AKI. We recommend close monitoring of elderly or obese patients, as well as those with rising APSIII score, coagulation abnormalities, or electrolyte imbalances. Prompt clinical interventions aimed at rectifying these parameters may help to mitigate the risk of in-hospital AKI among LC patients complicated by sepsis. The objective limitation of relatively low sensitivity in the external validation of the NNET model may lead to a risk of missed diagnosis when used for independent screening. However, its high specificity, accuracy and precision indicate that the positive prediction results output by the model have high credibility. Given the stable performance of other key evaluation indicators, this model is a potential AKI risk prediction tool in actual clinical work.

Limitations

We acknowledge certain limitations in this study. Since the MIMIC-IV database does not contain pathological data and imaging data, more potential factors may have been omitted. Although we employed the PMM algorithm for imputation, it may still have affected the results. Additionally, data imbalance could have influenced model performance, an issue that the SMOTE algorithm cannot fully eliminate. Due to the different time periods during which patients were obtained from the source of the external validation dataset (eICU-CRD) and the modeling dataset source (MIMIC-IV database), time bias may occur.

Conclusions

Data extraction from the MIMIC-IV database regarding LC patients with sepsis led to the identification of multiple independent risk factors for in-hospital AKI, including age, APSIII score, calcium, PT, glucose, weight, sodium, and potassium. Upon training and validating seven distinct ML models, the NNET architecture yielded the highest performance across all datasets tested.
