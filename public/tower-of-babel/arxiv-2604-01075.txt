Abstract
. We prove that for almost all symmetric spaces X X and for any sequence of compact locally symmetric spaces Y n Y_{n} which is uniformly discrete, has a uniform spectral gap, and converges in the sense of Benjamini–Schramm to X X , the joint eigenfunctions of all invariant differential operators on Y n Y_{n} delocalize on average when their spectral parameters are taken to lie in a fixed spectral window.



1.
Introduction

The celebrated Quantum Ergodicity theorem of Snirelman
[
35
]
, Zelditch
[
37
]
, and Colin de Verdière
[
13
]
states that, on a closed Riemannian manifold
Y
whose geodesic flow is ergodic, the
L^{2}
-mass of almost every Laplacian eigenfunction equidistributes in the limit of high frequency. This is an early example of the transference principle in semiclassical analysis, whereby high-energy Laplacian eigenfunctions inherit the dynamical properties of the underlying Hamiltonian system, in this case given by the geodesic flow.

More precisely, let
\{\psi_{j}\}
be an orthonormal basis of
L^{2}(Y)
consisting of eigenfunctions of the Laplacian. We write
\Delta\psi_{j}=\mu_{j}^{2}\psi_{j}
, where
\mu_{j}\geq 0
is the frequency, and
N(M)=\#\{j:\mu_{j}\leq M\}
. Quantum Ergodicity affirms that, for any
a\in C(Y)
,

\lim_{M\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{N(M)}\sum_{j:\mu_{j}\leq M}\Big|\int_{Y}a|\psi_{j}|^{2}d\textnormal{vol}-\frac{1}{\textnormal{vol}(Y)}\int_{Y}ad\textnormal{vol}\Big|^{2}=0.

In fact, the aforementioned authors proved a stronger version of the above statement in which the multiplication operator
a
is replaced with more general pseudodifferential operators.

An analog of Snirelman’s theorem can be posed in the setting of locally symmetric spaces of non-compact type, which form a subclass of Riemannian manifolds of central interest in automorphic forms and harmonic analysis. To do so effectively, one must take into account the extra symmetries enjoyed by these spaces. Indeed, Snirelman’s theorem does not always directly apply, since the geodesic flow on the cosphere bundle of a locally symmetric space
Y
is ergodic only in rank 1. Nevertheless, the
Weyl chamber flow
can serve as a suitable substitute: if
r
is the rank of
Y
, the Weyl chamber flow is an
\mathbb{R}^{r}
-action on the Weyl chamber bundle, whose orbits descend to immersed maximal flat subspaces of
Y
, the higher rank analog of geodesics. This action is ergodic with respect to the uniform measure, and its quantization yields a rank
r
algebra of commuting differential operators containing the Laplacian. One can then ask for the quantum ergodic properties of the joint eigenfunctions for this algebra as the multispectra goes to infinity, a point of view first advanced in
[
34
]
.

In this paper we shall investigate quantum ergodicity for locally symmetric spaces
Y
under a different limiting procedure to that described above. Following the breakthrough results of
[
3
,
27
]
, rather than fixing a single space
Y
and varying the multispectra, we shall prove a version of quantum ergodicity for a fixed spectral window and a sequence of locally symmetric spaces
Y_{n}=\Gamma_{n}\backslash X
which converge, in the sense of Benjamini–Schramm, to their common universal cover
X
. In so doing we will correct a significant error in the earlier work of the first and third authors
[
11
]
, in which
X
was taken to have isometry group
\text{SL}_{n}(\mathbb{R})
, and greatly expand the scope of that paper. The precise result is stated in Theorem
1.1
below.

The proof of our main theorem requires the introduction of several new techniques and auxiliary estimates, both in harmonic analysis (bounds on spherical functions) and the geometry of higher rank symmetric spaces (bounds on intersection translates), which we believe to be of general interest. These results, and their role in the proof of Theorem
1.1
, are discussed in detail in Section
2
.

1.1.
Review of literature: the rank one case

The framework for studying quantum ergodicity in the Benjamini–Schramm limit originates in the work of Anantharaman–Le Masson
[
3
]
in the context of regular graphs. They considered sequences of
(q+1)
-regular graphs for which the adjacency operator (the discrete analogue of the Laplacian) has a uniform spectral gap and for which the number of short loops is small compared to the number of vertices; the latter condition is equivalent to the Benjamini–Schramm convergence of the sequence of graphs to the
(q+1)
-regular tree. They proved a form of quantum ergodicity for eigenfunctions of the adjacency operator on such graphs, with eigenvalue lying in some fixed interval
I
. More precisely,
I
was taken to be a subinterval of
[-2\sqrt{q},2\sqrt{q}]
, which is the spectrum of the adjacency operator acting on the
(q+1)
-regular tree. Later, Anantharaman–Sabri
[
4
]
extended this result to a wide class of large finite graphs, not necessarily regular, in the presence of a potential.

Analogous results were proven for hyperbolic surfaces by Le Masson–Sahlsten
[
27
]
, building on work of Brooks–Le Masson–Lindenstrauss
[
10
]
, the latter of which gave a new proof of the result of Anantharaman–Le Masson
[
3
]
. Such results were extended to all rank one locally symmetric spaces by Abert–Bergeron–Le Masson
[
2
]
.

1.2.
Our main result

Let us recall some notation related to locally symmetric spaces, which will be necessary to state our main result.

Let
G
be a connected non-compact semisimple real Lie group with finite center and
K<G
a maximal compact subgroup. Let
\mathfrak{g}
and
\mathfrak{k}
be their respective Lie algebras. Then
K
induces a Cartan involution with corresponding eigenspace decomposition
\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{p}\oplus\mathfrak{k}
. We endow
X=G/K
with the
G
-invariant metric coming from the Killing form, making
X
a Riemannian symmetric space. All groups isogenous to
G
yield the same symmetric space. Thus we may assume without loss of generality that
G
is a product of non-compact centerless connected simple real Lie groups.

Let
\Gamma<G
be an irreducible lattice, which we shall assume to be uniform throughout this paper. Then
Y=\Gamma\backslash X
is a compact locally symmetric space. We endow
Y
with the measure
dx
induced from the Riemannian volume form on
X
. Let
D_{G}(X)
be the ring of
G
-invariant differential operators on
X
. The action of
D_{G}(X)
descends to functions on
Y
, and the joint eigenfunctions of
D_{G}(X)
in
L^{2}(Y)
are called
Maass forms
. A Maass form determines an eigencharacter of
D_{G}(X)
, which can then be identified with a spectral parameter
\lambda\in\mathfrak{a}_{\mathbb{C}}^{*}/W
by the Harish-Chandra isomorphism. Here
\mathfrak{a}
is a maximal abelian subspace of
\mathfrak{p}
,
\mathfrak{a}_{\mathbb{C}}
is its complexification, and
W
is the Weyl group of
G
. A Maass form is said to be
tempered
when its spectral parameter lies in
\mathfrak{a}^{*}/W
, the latter being identified with the
D_{G}(X)
-spectrum on
L^{2}(X)
. For example, when
X=\mathbb{H}^{n}
is hyperbolic
n
-space and
Y=\Gamma\backslash\mathbb{H}^{n}
is a compact hyperbolic manifold, a Maass form
\psi
is just a Laplacian eigenfunction
\Delta\psi=\mu^{2}\psi
, and if we write the eigenvalue as
\mu^{2}=\rho^{2}+\lambda^{2}
, with
\rho=(n-1)/2
, then the tempered case
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
corresponds to
\mu^{2}\geq\rho^{2}
.

We shall be interested in the
L^{2}
-mass distribution of Maass forms, when their spectral parameters are confined to a compact subset of
\mathfrak{a}^{*}/W
and the lattice
\Gamma<G
is allowed to vary. A sequence of locally symmetric spaces
Y_{n}=\Gamma_{n}\backslash X
as above is said to
Benjamini–Schramm
converge to
X
if asymptotically almost all points in
Y_{n}
have arbitrarily large injectivity radius. Moreover, if the
\Gamma_{n}
are torsion free, we say that the sequence
\Gamma_{n}<G
is
uniformly discrete
if there is a universal lower bound on the global injectivity radii of
Y_{n}
.

With the above notation, our main theorem is the following.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
G
be a product of non-compact connected centerless simple real Lie groups,
K
be a maximal compact subgroup, and
X=G/K
be the associated symmetric space. Let
\Gamma_{n}<G
be a sequence of torsion free, cocompact, uniformly discrete, irreducible lattices. Suppose
Y_{n}=\Gamma_{n}\backslash X
Benjamini–Schramm converges to
X
as
n\to\infty
. Fix a simple factor
G_{1}
of
G
and suppose that

(1)

its reduced root subsystem is of type
A_{n},B_{n},C_{n},D_{n}
or
E_{7}
,

(2)

the action of
G_{1}
on
L^{2}_{0}(\Gamma_{n}\backslash G)
, the orthocomplement to the constant functions, has a uniform spectral gap.

There exists a finite
W
-stable set of hyperplanes
\{P_{i}\}
in
\mathfrak{a}^{*}
such that, for any compact
W
-invariant subset
\Omega\subset\mathfrak{a}^{*}\smallsetminus\cup_{i}P_{i}
with non-empty interior, the following holds. Let
\{\psi_{j}^{(n)}\}
be an orthonormal basis of
L^{2}(Y_{n})
consisting of Maass forms with associated spectral parameters
\lambda_{j}^{(n)}
, and write
N(\Omega,\Gamma_{n})=\#\{j:\lambda_{j}^{(n)}\in\Omega\}
. Then for any uniformly bounded sequence
a_{n}\in L^{\infty}(Y_{n})
we have

\displaystyle\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{N(\Omega,\Gamma_{n})}\sum_{j:\lambda_{j}^{(n)}\in\Omega}\Big|\int_{Y_{n}}a_{n}(x)|\psi_{j}^{(n)}(x)|^{2}dx-\frac{1}{{\rm vol}(Y_{n})}\int_{Y_{n}}a_{n}(x)dx\Big|^{2}=0.

In particular, the theorem holds for a non-compact simple real Lie group
G
of rank at least 2 satisfying (
1
) and any uniformly discrete sequence of cocompact torsion free lattices
\Gamma_{n}<G
such that
{\rm vol}(Y_{n})\rightarrow\infty
. Indeed, in such a setting the uniform spectral gap assumption (
2
) is automatic by Property (T), and the Benjamini–Schramm convergence of
Y_{n}=\Gamma_{n}\backslash X
to
X
is automatic by
[
1
, Theorem 1.5]
.

The exceptional hyperplanes
\cup_{i}P_{i}
in the statement of Theorem
1.1
can be taken to depend only on the reduced root system of
G_{1}
. In particular, when the root subsystem of reduced roots for
G_{1}
is of type
A_{n},B_{n}
, or
C_{n}
, Theorem
1.1
holds with
\Omega
a compact subset of the regular parameters
\mathfrak{a}^{*}_{\rm reg}
. See Section
4
, and, in particular, Propositions
4.3
and
4.7
, for a more complete description of the
\cup_{i}P_{i}
.

The reader will note that the types
E_{6},E_{8},F_{4}
and
G_{2}
are not considered in Theorem
1.1
. Indeed, we eventually reduce the proof to a combinatorial property of the root system of the simple factor
G_{1}
which fails to hold in these types (see Section
7
). There are precisely eight simple groups/irreducible symmetric spaces excluded from Theorem
1.1
. In the notation of
[
20
, p. 532]
these are the groups

E\ I,\;E\ II,\;E\,III,\;E\ VI,\;E\ VIII,\;E\ IX,\;F\ I,\textrm{ and }G.

Despite the failure of our techniques to treat such spaces, we believe Theorem
1.1
should remain valid for them.

1.3.
Relation with the preceding work of Brumley–Matz

The first investigation of quantum ergodicity in the Benjamini–Schramm limit in higher rank can be found in the work of the first and third authors
[
11
]
who focused on locally symmetric spaces associated to
\textnormal{SL}_{n}(\mathbb{R})
. At a certain step in the proof, which we refer to as the
geometric bound
, they must bound the volume of a certain set in the symmetric space. A mistake in the geometric bound was found by the fourth author as part of his thesis work
[
31
]
. This mistake resulted in a gap in the proof of the main theorem of
[
11
]
.

By modifying the techniques of
[
11
]
, particularly those related to the geometric bound, the fourth author proved quantum ergodicity in the Benjamini–Schramm limit for the group
\textnormal{PGL}_{3}
over a non-archimedean local field, in which case the role of the symmetric space is replaced by that of the Bruhat-Tits building, and the invariant differential operators are replaced by the spherical Hecke algbera
[
32
]
. In Section
2.2
we shall say more about the mistake in the geometric bound of
[
11
]
, the method of “fixing” it for non-archimedean
\textnormal{PGL}_{3}
in
[
32
]
, and how these ideas play a role in the present paper.

2.
Sketch of proof and main technical theorems

The overall strategy of the proof of Theorem
1.1
follows the argument of Le Masson–Sahlsten
[
27
]
, which itself derives from
[
10
]
. The subsequent works of
[
2
]
and
[
11
,
31
]
all followed the same basic structure, with additional difficulties depending on the given context : higher dimensional hyperbolic manifolds, and higher rank real and
p
-adic symmetric spaces, respectively. The main purpose of the present discussion is to recall the various steps of the argument which are common to the above works, point to the specific sections in this paper where they are executed, and describe in detail the geometric and analytic challenges special to our higher rank setting.

Our two main auxiliary results which surmount these difficulties are stated in Sections
2.2
and
2.3
below. Of these, the primary result is stated in Theorem
2.3
; it provides sharp (up to logarithmic powers) upper bounds on intersection volumes of spherical shells in the globally symmetric space
X
, and addresses the issue raised in Section
1.3
. We prove this geometric bound using harmonic analysis. For this, we prove new uniform bounds on spherical functions in Theorem
2.4
, which provides the central ingredient to our analytic approach.

The reader will notice that Theorem
1.1
is stated for
G
semisimple, while Theorem
2.3
takes
G
to be simple. As we describe below, the deduction of the former from the latter result takes place in Section
5
, specifically in Proposition
5.9
. To simplify the exposition, we shall assume throughout Section
2
that
G
is simple.

2.1.
Spectral and geometric reduction steps

The basic strategy to the proof of Theorem
1.1
is to introduce an averaging operator over expanding bi-
K
-invariant sets
S_{tH_{0}}\subset G
to convert the distributional properties of the
L^{2}
-mass of Maass forms into the mean ergodic properties of the
S_{tH_{0}}
(or their intersection translates).

Reduction to mean-zero observables

We begin by observing that it is enough to prove Theorem
1.1
for the class of observables which are orthogonal to constants. Indeed, if
a_{n}
is as in the theorem, then so is
a_{n}-\frac{1}{{\rm vol}(Y_{n})}\int_{Y_{n}}a_{n}
, so we may assume, without loss of generality, that
\int_{Y_{n}}a_{n}=0
. In this case the proof of Theorem
1.1
reduces to showing

(1)

\frac{1}{N(\Omega,\Gamma_{n})}\sum_{\lambda_{j}^{(n)}\in\Omega}\big|\langle a_{n}\psi_{j}^{(n)},\psi_{j}^{(n)}\rangle\big|^{2}\longrightarrow 0

as
n\rightarrow\infty
.

Spectral properties of the propagator (Section
4
)

In Section
4
, we introduce the aforementioned expanding bi-
K
-invariant sets
S_{tH_{0}}
in
G
. More precisely, given a non-zero
H_{0}\in\overline{\mathfrak{a}}_{+}
and real parameters
t,\epsilon_{0}>0
, define the
spherical shell directed by
H_{0}
by

\displaystyle S_{tH_{0}}:=K\textnormal{exp}(B_{\mathfrak{a}}(tH_{0},\epsilon_{0}))K,

where
B_{\mathfrak{a}}(H,r)
denotes the Euclidean ball in
\mathfrak{a}
of radius
r>0
centered at
H\in\mathfrak{a}
. We may associate with
S_{tH_{0}}
its averaging operator
U_{t}
on
L^{2}(\Gamma\backslash G)
, and we denote the corresponding self-adjoint time average by
\mathbf{A}(\tau)
. The latter is an integral operator with kernel given by
\sum_{\gamma\in\Gamma}A(\tau)(g,\gamma h)
, where

(2)

A(\tau)(g,h)=\frac{1}{\tau}\int_{\tau}^{2\tau}e^{-2t\rho(H_{0})}\int_{gS_{tH_{0}}\cap hS_{tH_{0}}}a(x)dxdt,

where
\rho
is the half-sum of the positive roots. The primary goal of Section 4 is then to prove that one can essentially replace the matrix coefficient
\langle a\psi_{j},\psi_{j}\rangle
in the spectral average (
1
) by the matrix coefficient
\langle\mathbf{A}(\tau)\psi_{j},\psi_{j}\rangle
.

This indeed is the content of Theorem
4.1
, which is valid in a wide degree of generality. Since Theorem
4.1
is essentially local in nature, no assumptions on
\Gamma
are necessary. Moreover, the non-zero directing element
H_{0}
can be taken to be arbitrary, provided one restricts to spectral parameters avoiding the exceptional hyperplanes
\{P_{i}\}
figuring in the statement of Theorem
1.1
. To be precise, these exceptional hyperplanes arise in the proof of Proposition
4.6
, and depend on
H_{0}
through the set
\mathfrak{a}_{\rm bad}^{*}
, defined in (
32
). (Eventually
H_{0}
itself will be chosen to depend on the reduced root system of
G_{1}
, implying the claimed dependency of the exceptional hyperplanes
\{P_{i}\}
after Theorem
1.1
.)

Reduction to the intersection volume bound (Section
5
)

The outcome of Section
4
is to reduce Theorem
1.1
to a similar estimate on the Hilbert–Schmidt norm
\|\mathbf{A}(\tau)\|_{{\rm HS}}
. We study the latter by realizing it as the
L^{2}
-norm of its kernel. Theorem
5.1
establishes a bound on
\|\mathbf{A}(\tau)\|_{{\rm HS}}
in terms of various geometric and spectral quantities controlled by the hypotheses in Theorem
1.1
. We then show, using known Limit Multiplicity theorems recalled in Section
3.8
, how Theorem
5.1
suffices to deduce Theorem
1.1
.

As is evident from (
2
), the Hilbert–Schmidt norm of the operator
\mathbf{A}(\tau)
encodes the dynamical properties of the intersection of the spherical shells
S_{tH_{0}}
with their group translates. The most difficult step in the proof of Theorem
5.1
is to bound the volume of these intersections, as stated in Theorem
2.3
below. The bulk of Section
5
is then dedicated to the reduction of Theorem
5.1
to Theorem
2.3
.

This reduction proceeds by bounding
\|\mathbf{A}(\tau)\|_{{\rm HS}}
by means of a thick-thin decomposition of the locally symmetric space
Y=\Gamma\backslash G/K
. This resulting bound produces a main term, denoted as
M(\tau)
and defined in (
45
), and an error term, occuring in Lemma
5.2
. The error term involves the volume of the thin part and the global injectivity radius of
Y
, controlled by the hypotheses of Theorem
1.1
, as well as the support of the kernel function (
2
), determined in Corollary
5.6
.

The analysis of the main term
M(\tau)
is much more delicate. In Proposition
5.9
we use the Minkowski integral inequality and the Nevo ergodic theorem to set up an application of Theorem
2.3
. Once this is inserted, we execute in Section
5.4
one last integral using a degenerate form of Brion’s formula to conclude the proof of Theorem
5.1
.

2.2.
Bounds on intersection volumes (Sections
6
-
9
)

It then remains to bound the volumes of intersection translates of expanding spherical shells. For this, we wish to find an
H_{0}
such that for all
H\in\mathfrak{a}
and
t\gg 1
, the volume of the intersection
e^{H}S_{tH_{0}}\cap S_{tH_{0}}
is “small”, in a suitable sense. We are able to do so for all non-compact simple groups satisfying condition (
1
) of Theorem
1.1
, as we now describe.

In Section
6
, we outline an approach to bounding intersection volumes using
analysis
, rather than geometry. Indeed, we pass to the spectral side using Plancherel inversion and present a strategy which reduces the desired bound to uniform bounds on spherical functions, which we then prove. This reduction strategy only succeeds when the directing element
H_{0}
is highly symmetric.

Let
M
be the centralizer of
H_{0}
inside
G
. In Section
7
, we make this symmetry condition on
H_{0}
precise by examining the way in which the reduced root subsystem of
M
sits inside that of
G
. We first do this abstractly (making no mention of
H_{0}
), by introducing a combinatorial property of reduced root subsystems which encodes its relative fullness. Namely, if
\Phi
is a reduced root system, and
\Phi_{0}\subset\Phi
is a semistandard root subsystem, we say that
\Phi_{0}
is
semi-dense
in
\Phi
if, roughly speaking, for any semistandard root subsystem
\Psi\subset\Phi
,
\Phi_{0}
contains at least half the roots of
\Psi
. The required inequality is stated exactly in Definition
7.1
.

Theorem 2.1
.

An irreducible reduced root system
\Phi
contains a semi-dense root subsystem if, and only if,
\Phi
is of type
A_{n},B_{n},C_{n},D_{n}
or
E_{7}
.

We now return to the setting of Theorem
1.1
and denote by
\Phi
the set of restricted roots for
A
in
G
and by
\Phi_{\rm red}\subset\Phi
the subsystem of reduced (or indivisible) roots. As before, let
M
be the centralizer of
H_{0}
in
G
; then
M
is a standard Levi subgroup of
G
. Let
\Phi_{M}\subset\Phi
be the roots of
A
in
M
, and
\Phi_{M,{\rm red}}\subset\Phi_{\textnormal{red}}
the corresponding subsystem of reduced roots. In the case where
\Phi_{\rm red}
is of type
A_{n},B_{n},C_{n},D_{n}
or
E_{7}
, we define in Section
3.6
a class of elements
H_{0}\in\overline{\mathfrak{a}}_{+}
, which we call
extremal
. Extremal

will turn out to be our primary source of extremal root subsystems, thanks to the following result.

Theorem 2.2
.

Let
G
be such that
\Phi_{\textnormal{red}}
is of type
A_{n},B_{n},C_{n},D_{n}
or
E_{7}
and let
H_{0}
be extremal. Then
\Phi_{M,\textnormal{red}}
is a semi-dense root subsystem of

.

Theorems
2.1
and
2.2
are proved in Section
7
, in the form of Propositions

-

.

We ultimately provide strong bounds on
{\rm vol}(e^{H}S_{tH_{0}}\cap S_{tH_{0}})
for extremal
H_{0}
. To better understand their quality, first remark that the volume of
S_{tH_{0}}
is asymptotically of size
e^{2t\rho(H_{0})}
. Moreover, as we shall see in Section

, the spherical shell
S_{tH_{0}}
and its translate
e^{H}S_{tH_{0}}
, where
H\in\overline{\mathfrak{a}}_{+}
, do not intersect each other as soon as
\rho(H)>2t\rho(H_{0})
. The following bound interpolates between these two extremities (full and empty intersection), losing only a logarithmic factor of
t
.

Theorem 2.3
.

Let
G
be a non-compact simple real Lie group. If
H_{0}\in\overline{\mathfrak{a}}_{+}
is such that
\Phi_{M,\textnormal{red}}
is semi-dense in
\Phi_{\textnormal{red}}
, then there exists a non-negative integer

such that for all

and

:

(3)

\displaystyle\textnormal{vol}(e^{H}S_{tH_{0}}\cap S_{tH_{0}})\ll(\log t)^{k}e^{\rho(2tH_{0}-H)}.

J.-P. Anker has explained to us a straightforward argument which bounds the intersection volume
\textnormal{vol}(e^{H}S_{tH_{0}}\cap S_{tH_{0}})
, for all choices of
H_{0}
, by a similar expression to that in (
3
) but with the
(\log t)^{k}
factor replaced by
t^{n}
for some positive integer

. We review this argument in Section
6.1
. The fact that Theorem
2.3
yields poly-logarithmic factors rather than positive integral powers in

is critical to the overall argument. Indeed, any positive integral power of

would kill the decay in the main term of Theorem
5.1
.

In
[
11
]
, the first and third authors considered a similar quantity to the left-hand side of (

), but for a choice of

which was not extremal. In
[
11
, Lemma 5.8]
, they claimed a bound like the right-hand side but with the

[

]
, the fourth author found a mistake in their argument and showed that the analogous bound for

does not hold. Namely, for the choice of

from
[

]
and specific choices of

\textnormal{vol}(p^{H}S_{tH_{0}}\cap S_{tH_{0}})\gg tp^{\rho(2tH_{0}-H)},

where
S_{tH_{0}}=\textnormal{PGL}_{3}(\mathbb{Z}_{p})p^{tH_{0}}\textnormal{PGL}_{3}(\mathbb{Z}_{p})
. On the other hand, if one takes an extremal

\textnormal{vol}(p^{H}S_{tH_{0}}\cap S_{tH_{0}})\ll p^{\rho(2tH_{0}-H)}
. This suggests that in the archimedean setting we should not expect (

2.3.
Bounds for spherical functions (Section
9
)

The proof of Theorem
2.3
relies crucially on a new bound for the spherical function. As this bound holds on a general semisimple group, and may be of independent interest, we shall state and prove it independently of Theorem
1.1
and Theorem
2.3
.

Let
G
be any non-compact semisimple real Lie group with finite center. We let
\varphi_{\lambda}
denote the Harish-Chandra spherical function on
G
with spectral parameter
\lambda\in\mathfrak{a}^{*}_{\mathbb{C}}
. See Section
3.4
for definitions. To state our bound, we first define

(4)

f_{\alpha}(H,\lambda):=\min(|\alpha(H)|+1,|\langle\lambda,\alpha\rangle|^{-1}+1),

where
\alpha\in\Phi^{+}_{\textnormal{red}}
,
H\in\mathfrak{a}
, and
\lambda\in\mathfrak{a}^{*}_{\mathbb{C}}
. We subsequently define

(5)

\displaystyle\Theta(H,\lambda):=\sum_{w\in W}\prod_{\alpha\in\Phi^{+}_{\textnormal{red}}}f_{\alpha}(H,w\lambda).

Note that
\Theta
is
W
-invariant in both
\lambda
and
H
, and also satisfies
\Theta(H,\lambda)\geq 1
. Let
\mathfrak{a}^{*}(\kappa)
denote those elements in
\mathfrak{a}^{*}_{\mathbb{C}}
whose imaginary part is bounded in size by
\kappa
.

Theorem 2.4
.

There are
a,\kappa>0
such that, for all
H\in\overline{\mathfrak{a}}_{+}
and all
\lambda\in\mathfrak{a}^{*}(\kappa)
, we have

\varphi_{\lambda}(e^{H})\ll(1+\|\lambda\|)^{a}\Theta(H,\lambda)\underset{w\in W}{\max}\,e^{-(\rho+w\Im\lambda)(H)}.

We shall prove Theorem
2.4
in Section
9
. To aid the reader in understanding the statement of the theorem, in Section
9.1
we shall illustrate it in the case of
{\rm SL}_{2}(\mathbb{C})
and
\textnormal{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
, as well as deriving some simpler consequences of it in general. Section
9.2
also contains a discussion of the relation between Theorem
2.4
and previous bounds for the spherical function.

When
G
is a complex group, Theorem
2.4
has an interesting ‘self-improving’ property which allows us to significantly strengthen it, subject to the condition that
\lambda
lie in
\mathfrak{a}^{*}
. In particular, we expect this bound to be sharp in all aspects subject to this tempered condition on
\lambda
. Moreover, this bound may be transferred to the Cartan motion group associated with
G
, whose definition we recall below. This correspondence uses the well-known link between the Cartan motion group and the semisimple group in the complex case. For this reason, we shall state the bound for both groups simultaneously.

The Cartan motion group associated to
G
is the semidirect product
\mathfrak{p}\ltimes K
, which acts on the Euclidean symmetric space
\mathfrak{p}
. For
\lambda\in\mathfrak{a}^{*}_{\mathbb{C}}
, we have the Euclidean spherical function
\varphi_{\lambda}^{E}\in C^{\infty}(\mathfrak{p})
(also known as the generalized Bessel function) defined by

(6)

\varphi_{\lambda}^{E}(Z)=\int_{K}e^{i\lambda(kZ)}dk\quad(Z\in\mathfrak{p}),

where we have extended
\lambda
to an element of
\mathfrak{p}^{*}
by orthogonality. To recall the relationship between
\varphi_{\lambda}^{E}
and
\varphi_{\lambda}
, we let

Q(H)=\prod_{\alpha\in\Phi^{+}}\sinh(\alpha(H))/\alpha(H)\quad(H\in\mathfrak{a}).

As
Q
is Weyl invariant, it extends to a function on
\mathfrak{p}
, and is equal to the Jacobian of the exponential map
\exp:\mathfrak{p}\to G/K
. We then have

\varphi_{\lambda}(e^{Z})=CQ(Z)^{-1/2}\varphi_{\lambda}^{E}(Z)

for some constant
C>0
, see for instance Ch. IV, Theorem 4.7 of
[
19
]
. Our bounds for
\varphi_{\lambda}
and
\varphi_{\lambda}^{E}
are as follows.

Theorem 2.5
.

Suppose that
G
is a complex group. Then for
H\in\overline{\mathfrak{a}}_{+}
and
\lambda\in\mathfrak{a}^{*}
, we have

(7)

\varphi_{\lambda}^{E}(H)\ll\sum_{w\in W}\prod_{\alpha\in\Phi^{+}}(1+|\alpha(H)\langle w\lambda,\alpha\rangle|)^{-1},

and

(8)

\varphi_{\lambda}(e^{H})\ll e^{-\rho(H)}\prod_{\alpha\in\Phi^{+}}(|\alpha(H)|+1)\sum_{w\in W}\prod_{\alpha\in\Phi^{+}}(1+|\alpha(H)\langle w\lambda,\alpha\rangle|)^{-1}.

We note that

Q(H)^{-1/2}\asymp e^{-\rho(H)}\prod_{\alpha\in\Phi^{+}}(|\alpha(H)|+1)

for
H\in\overline{\mathfrak{a}}_{+}
, so that the bounds (
7
) and (
8
) are equivalent. In Section
9.1
we explain why we expect Theorem
2.5
to be sharp based on an analysis of the oscillatory integral (
6
), and in Section
9.2
we discuss the relation between Theorem
2.5
and previous bounds for the spherical function on complex groups. We deduce Theorem
2.5
from Theorem
2.4
in Section
9.7
.

In this section we introduce most of the notation that will be in force throughout the rest of the paper, and recall some of the definitions and foundational results that enter into the statement and proof of Theorem

Throughout this section we shall let

3.1.
Basic notation

Fix a maximal compact subgroup
K
of
G
. Denote by
\mathfrak{k}
and
\mathfrak{g}
their respective Lie algebras. Then
K
induces a Cartan involution
\Theta
on
G
. The differential at the identity of
\Theta
defines an involution on
\mathfrak{g}
, whose
-1
and
+1
eigenspaces determine the Cartan decomposition
\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{p}\oplus\mathfrak{k}
.

Let
\mathfrak{a}\subset\mathfrak{p}
denote a maximal abelian subspace. Let
A=\exp(\mathfrak{a})
be the corresponding analytic subgroup of
G
. Let
\mathfrak{a}^{*}
denote the dual space of
\mathfrak{a}
. Let
\Phi\subset\mathfrak{a}^{*}
be the set of restricted roots. Let
\Phi^{+}\subset\Phi
be a choice of positive roots. We let
\Delta\subset\Phi^{+}
denote the set of simple roots; they form a basis for
\mathfrak{a}^{*}
. Then the fundamental coweights
\widehat{\Delta}^{\vee}=\{\varpi_{\alpha}^{\vee}:\alpha\in\Delta\}\subset\mathfrak{a}
form the dual basis to the simple roots and give rise to decompositions

\lambda=\sum_{\alpha\in\Delta}\lambda(\varpi_{\alpha}^{\vee})\alpha
,
H=\sum_{\alpha\in\Delta}\alpha(H)\varpi_{\alpha}^{\vee}
of elements
\lambda\in\mathfrak{a}^{*},H\in\mathfrak{a}
. Let

\mathfrak{a}_{+}=\{H\in\mathfrak{a}:\alpha(H)>0\;\forall\alpha\in\Delta\}=\sum_{\alpha\in\Delta}\mathbb{R}_{>0}\varpi_{\alpha}^{\vee}\\

be the fundamental Weyl chamber in
\mathfrak{a}
and denote by
\overline{\mathfrak{a}}_{+}=\sum_{\alpha\in\Delta}\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\varpi_{\alpha}^{\vee}
its closure.

We have a restricted root space decomposition

\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{m}\oplus\mathfrak{a}\oplus\sum_{\alpha\in\Phi}\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha},

where
\mathfrak{m}=Z_{\mathfrak{k}}(\mathfrak{a})
is the centralizer of
\mathfrak{a}
in
\mathfrak{k}
and
\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha}
is the root space for
\alpha
. As usual, we let
\rho=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\alpha\in\Phi^{+}}m_{\alpha}\alpha
be the half-sum of the positive roots, where we have put
m_{\alpha}=\dim\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha}
. Let
r=\dim\mathfrak{a}
be the rank of
G
and
W=N_{K}(\mathfrak{a})/Z_{K}(\mathfrak{a})
the Weyl group of
G
. Let
\Phi_{\text{red}}
denote the set of reduced roots of
\Phi
, consisting of those
\alpha\in\Phi
such that
\alpha/2\notin\Phi
. Then
\Phi_{\textnormal{red}}
is a reduced root system, and we refer to it as the reduced root system of
G
. Let
\Phi_{\text{red}}^{+}:=\Phi_{\text{red}}\cap\Phi^{+}
. Note that
\Delta
is also a base for
\Phi_{\textnormal{red}}
.

We let
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
denote the restriction of the Killing form to
\mathfrak{a}
. We equip
\mathfrak{a}^{*}
with the inner product
\langle\alpha,\beta\rangle=\langle H_{\alpha},H_{\beta}\rangle
, where
H_{\alpha}\in\mathfrak{a}
is uniquely determined by
\alpha(Z)=\langle H_{\alpha},Z\rangle
. For any non-zero
\alpha\in\mathfrak{a}^{*}
, we may then define the orthogonal reflection
\lambda\mapsto\lambda-2\frac{\langle\lambda,\alpha\rangle}{\langle\alpha,\alpha\rangle}\alpha
across the root hyperplane
\alpha^{\perp}
. Note that
\lambda\mapsto 2\frac{\langle\lambda,\alpha\rangle}{\langle\alpha,\alpha\rangle}
is an element in
\mathfrak{a}^{**}
, and as such determines an element
\alpha^{\vee}\in\mathfrak{a}
, the
coroot
associated with
\alpha
, such that
2\frac{\langle\lambda,\alpha\rangle}{\langle\alpha,\alpha\rangle}=\lambda(\alpha^{\vee})
. Then the orthogonal reflection can be written
\lambda\mapsto\lambda-\lambda(\alpha^{\vee})\alpha
and hence
\alpha^{\perp}=\{\lambda\in\mathfrak{a}^{*}:\lambda(\alpha^{\vee})=0\}
.

Denote the set of (restricted) coroots and simple coroots by
\Phi^{\vee}=\{\alpha^{\vee}:\alpha\in\Phi\}\subset\mathfrak{a}
and
\Delta^{\vee}=\{\alpha^{\vee}:\alpha\in\Delta\}\subset\mathfrak{a}
, respectively. By definition, the fundamental weights
\widehat{\Delta}=\{\varpi_{\alpha}:\alpha\in\Delta\}\subset\mathfrak{a}^{*}
form the dual basis to the simple coroots
\Delta^{\vee}
. We have corresponding decompositions
\lambda=\sum_{\alpha\in\Delta}\lambda(\alpha^{\vee})\varpi_{\alpha}
,
H=\sum_{\alpha\in\Delta}\varpi_{\alpha}(H)\alpha^{\vee}
of elements
\lambda\in\mathfrak{a}^{*},H\in\mathfrak{a}
. Let

\mathfrak{a}_{+}^{*}=\{\lambda\in\mathfrak{a}^{*}:\lambda(\alpha^{\vee})>0\;\forall\alpha\in\Delta\}=\sum_{\alpha\in\Delta}\mathbb{R}_{>0}\varpi_{\alpha}\\

be the fundamental Weyl chamber in
\mathfrak{a}^{*}
and denote by
\overline{\mathfrak{a}}_{+}^{*}=\sum_{\alpha\in\Delta}\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\varpi_{\alpha}
its closure. We write

(9)

\mathfrak{a}^{*}_{\rm sing}=\bigcup_{\alpha\in\Phi^{+}}\{\lambda\in\mathfrak{a}^{*}:\lambda(\alpha^{\vee})=0\}

for the union of all root hyperplanes and put
\mathfrak{a}^{*}_{\rm reg}=\mathfrak{a}^{*}\smallsetminus\mathfrak{a}^{*}_{\rm sing}
. Then
\mathfrak{a}^{*}_{\rm reg}=W.\mathfrak{a}_{+}^{*}
. Finally, we write
\mathfrak{a}^{*}_{\mathbb{C}}:=\mathfrak{a}^{*}\otimes\mathbb{C}
for the complexification of
\mathfrak{a}^{*}
.

3.2.
Subsets of simple roots and associated structures

Let
\mathfrak{n}=\bigoplus_{\alpha\in\Phi^{+}}\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha}
and write
N=\exp(\mathfrak{n})
for the corresponding analytic subgroup. Let
P=Z_{K}(\mathfrak{a})AN
be the standard minimal parabolic subgroup. The Bruhat decomposition states that

\displaystyle G=\bigsqcup_{w\in W}PwP,

for any choice
w
of coset representatives for
W=N_{K}(\mathfrak{a})/Z_{K}(\mathfrak{a})
.

Any subgroup of
G
containing
P
is called a standard parabolic subgroup. These can be constructed in the following way. Let
I\subseteq\Delta
, and let
W_{I}
be the subgroup of
W
generated by the reflections associated to
\alpha\in I
; we define
W_{\emptyset}=1
. Standard parabolic subgroups are in bijection with subsets
I\subseteq\Delta
, through the map sending
I
to

\displaystyle Q_{I}=\bigsqcup_{w\in W_{I}}PwP;

see
[
21
, §§29.2-3]
. This recovers the Bruhat decomposition for
G
when
I=\Delta
.

Let
\Phi_{I}
be the root subsystem consisting of
\mathbb{Z}
-linear combinations of roots in
I
lying in
\Phi
. Let
\Phi_{I}^{+}=\Phi_{I}\cap\Phi^{+}
. Note that the roots of
Q_{I}
with respect to
A
are
\Phi^{+}\cup(-\Phi_{I}^{+})=(\Phi^{+}\smallsetminus\Phi_{I}^{+})\cup\Phi_{I}

[
21
, §30.1, Theorem (b)]
. The following proposition relates
\Phi_{I}^{+}
with the action of
W_{I}
on
\Phi^{+}
.

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
I\subseteq\Delta
. Then

(10)

W_{I}=\{w\in W\mid w\Phi^{+}\subseteq(\Phi^{+}\smallsetminus\Phi_{I}^{+})\cup\Phi_{I}\}.

In particular,
w(\Phi^{+}\smallsetminus\Phi_{I}^{+})=\Phi^{+}\smallsetminus\Phi_{I}^{+}
if and only if
w\in W_{I}
. Furthermore,

(11)

\displaystyle\Phi_{I}^{+}

\displaystyle=\{\alpha\in\Phi^{+}\mid\exists w\in W_{I}:\,w\alpha<0\},

\displaystyle\Phi^{+}\smallsetminus\Phi_{I}^{+}

\displaystyle=\{\alpha\in\Phi^{+}\mid w\alpha>0\,\forall w\in W_{I}\}.

Proof.

The direct inclusion in (
10
) holds, since
W_{I}
preserves the roots of
Q_{I}
. On the other hand, if
w\in W
is such that
w\Phi^{+}\subseteq\Phi^{+}\cup(-\Phi_{I}^{+})
, then
w
maps
P
into
Q_{I}
and therefore belongs to
Q_{I}
by
[
21
, §29.3, Lemma D]
so that
w\in Q_{I}\cap W=W_{I}
.

To see that
W_{I}
consists precisely of those
w\in W
for which
w(\Phi^{+}\smallsetminus\Phi_{I}^{+})=\Phi^{+}\smallsetminus\Phi_{I}^{+}
we first observe that since
W_{I}
preserves
\Phi_{I}
and
(\Phi^{+}\smallsetminus\Phi_{I}^{+})\cup\Phi_{I}
, it must also preserve
\Phi^{+}\smallsetminus\Phi_{I}^{+}
. Next, taking complements in (
10
) gives

W_{I}=\{w\in W\mid-(\Phi^{+}\smallsetminus\Phi_{I}^{+})\subset w(-\Phi^{+})\}=\{w\in W\mid w^{-1}(\Phi^{+}\smallsetminus\Phi_{I}^{+})\subset\Phi^{+}\},

so that
w(\Phi^{+}\smallsetminus\Phi_{I}^{+})=\Phi^{+}\smallsetminus\Phi_{I}^{+}
implies
w\in W_{I}
as required.

The two statements in (
11
) are clearly equivalent so that it suffices to prove the second. The direct inclusion follows from the fact that
W_{I}
preserves
\Phi^{+}\smallsetminus\Phi_{I}^{+}
. On the other hand, suppose
\alpha\in\Phi^{+}
is such that
w\alpha>0
for all
w\in W_{I}
. It suffices to show that
W_{I}\alpha\subset\Phi^{+}\smallsetminus\Phi_{I}^{+}
, since this implies, by the preceding claim, that
\alpha\in\Phi^{+}\smallsetminus\Phi_{I}^{+}
. Now (
10
) already yields the inclusion
W_{I}\alpha\subset(\Phi^{+}\smallsetminus\Phi_{I}^{+})\cup\Phi_{I}
. But
W_{I}\alpha
has no intersection with
\Phi_{I}
. Indeed, every element in
W_{I}\alpha
is positive while the
W_{I}
-orbit of every element in
\Phi_{I}
meets
-\Phi^{+}
, since
\Phi_{I}
is a root system with Weyl group
W_{I}
.
∎

Let
Q=Q_{I}
be a standard parabolic subgroup. Its unipotent radical
U
has Lie algebra
\bigoplus_{\alpha\in\Phi^{+}\smallsetminus\Phi_{I}^{+}}\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha}
. For example, when
I=\emptyset
, we have
Q=P
and
U=N
. Then
Q
admits a Levi decomposition
Q=L\ltimes U
, where
L=L_{I}=Q\cap\Theta(Q)
. The reductive subgroup
L
is called the Levi component of
Q
; its Lie algebra decomposes as
\mathfrak{a}\oplus\mathfrak{m}\oplus\bigoplus_{\alpha\in\Phi_{I}}\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha}
. By
standard Levi subgroup
we shall mean any Levi component of a standard parabolic subgroup. The Weyl group of
L
, relative to the maximal compact subgroup
K_{L}=K\cap L
, and maximal split torus
A\subset L
, is equal to
W_{I}

[
21
, §27.1, Theorem]
.

The Levi component
L_{I}
itself determines the standard parabolic subgroup
Q_{I}
. Moreover, we may identify
L_{I}
as the centralizer of any element in the open cone
C_{I}=\sum_{\alpha\in\Delta\smallsetminus I}\mathbb{R}_{>0}\varpi^{\vee}_{\alpha}
, and all standard Levi subgroups are obtained in this way
[
21
, §30.2]
. The nodes of the Dynkin diagram of
\Phi
are also naturally labeled by elements in
\Delta
. We thus have one-to-one correspondences between subsets
I\subseteq\Delta
, standard parabolics
Q_{I}
, standard Levi subgroups
L_{I}
, faces
C_{I}
of
\overline{\mathfrak{a}}_{+}
, and subdiagrams of the Dynkin diagram for
\Phi
obtained by deleting the nodes corresponding to
\Delta\setminus I
. In this last correspondence, the type of the resulting Dynkin diagram determines the type of the root system
\Phi_{I}
.

3.3.
Measures and Jacobian factors

Let
X=G/K
be the Riemannian globally symmetric space associated to
G
. Let
B
denote the Killing form on
\mathfrak{g}
. Then
B
is positive definite on
\mathfrak{p}
and hence defines an inner product on
\mathfrak{p}
. Identifying
\mathfrak{p}
with the tangent space of
X
at the origin, we then transport this inner product to a
G
-invariant Riemannian metric on
X
, of non-positive curvature. The restriction of
B
to
\mathfrak{a}
defines a
W
-invariant inner product. Let
dH
denote the corresponding Lebesgue measure on
\mathfrak{a}
. Let
dk
denote the probability Haar measure on
K
. We normalize Haar measure
dg
on
G
in such a way that its quotient by
dk
agrees with the measure
dx
induced by the Riemannian volume form on
X
. If
E
is a subset of
G
or
X=G/K
, we write
{\rm vol}(E)
for
\int_{E}dg
or
\int_{E}dx
, respectively.

The Cartan decomposition states that
G=K\exp(\overline{\mathfrak{a}}_{+})K
. Correspondingly, there is a constant
b_{G}>0
such that, for every
f\in C_{c}(G)
, we have

(12)

\int_{G}f(g)dg=b_{G}\int_{K}\int_{\mathfrak{a}_{+}}\int_{K}f(k_{1}\exp(H)k_{2})J(H)dk_{1}dHdk_{2},

where, for
H\in\mathfrak{a}_{+}
, the radial volume factor
J\in C^{\infty}(\mathfrak{a}_{+})
is given by

(13)

J(H)=\prod_{\alpha\in\Phi^{+}}\big(\sinh\alpha(H)\big)^{m_{\alpha}}.

See
[
17
, §2.4, Prop. 2.4.11]
. By extending to
\mathfrak{a}_{\rm reg}
by
W
-invariance, and then by continuity from
\mathfrak{a}_{\rm reg}
to
\mathfrak{a}
, we may also view
J(H)
as a continuous
W
-invariant function on
\mathfrak{a}
.

More generally, we let
L=L_{I}
be a standard Levi subgroup, where
I
is the corresponding subset of
\Delta
, as in the bijective correspondence of Section
3.2
. Denote by
W_{L}=W_{I}
its Weyl group. Let
\Phi_{L}=\Phi_{I}
, and
\Phi_{L}^{+}=\Phi_{L}\cap\Phi^{+}
. We let

(14)

\mathfrak{a}_{L,+}=\{H\in\mathfrak{a}:\alpha(H)>0\;\forall\alpha\in I\}

be the fundamental Weyl chamber for
L
. We again give
K_{L}
the probability Haar measure
dk
, and we denote by
dl
the Haar measure on
L
, normalized in the same way as for
G
. The Cartan decomposition for
L
states that
L=K_{L}\exp(\overline{\mathfrak{a}}_{L,+})K_{L}
. Once again, there is a constant
b_{L}>0
, depending only on
L
, such that

(15)

\int_{L}f(l)dl=b_{L}\int_{K_{L}}\int_{\mathfrak{a}_{L,+}}\int_{K_{L}}f(k_{1}\exp(H)k_{2})J_{L}(H)dk_{1}dHdk_{2},

where the radial volume factor with respect to
L
is

(16)

J_{L}(H)=\prod_{\alpha\in\Phi_{L}^{+}}\big(\sinh\alpha(H)\big)^{m_{\alpha}}.

We define
J^{L}=J/J_{L}
.

3.4.
Spherical functions, spherical transform, spherical inversion

Let
D_{G}(X)
denote the algebra of
G
-invariant differential operators on
X
. A spherical function on
X
is a
K
-invariant joint eigenfunction of
D_{G}(X)
, normalized to take the value
1
at the identity. Harish-Chandra gave an integral representation for spherical functions, which we now review.

The Iwasawa decomposition for
G
states that the multiplication map
N\times A\times K\rightarrow G
is a diffeomorphism. Let
\mathcal{H}:G\to\mathfrak{a}
be the Iwasawa projection, given by
g=n\exp(\mathcal{H}(g))k
. For
\lambda\in\mathfrak{a}^{*}_{\mathbb{C}}
the map
g\mapsto e^{i\lambda(\mathcal{H}(g))}
descends to a plane wave on
X
. The average over left-
K
-orbits

(17)

\displaystyle\varphi_{\lambda}(g):=\int_{K}e^{(i\lambda+\rho)(\mathcal{H}(kg))}dk

defines a bi-
K
-invariant function on
G
— equivalently, a
K
-invariant function on
X
— such that

\displaystyle D\varphi_{\lambda}=\gamma_{\rm HC}(D)(\lambda)\varphi_{\lambda}\qquad(\forall\;D\in D_{G}(X)),

and
\varphi_{\lambda}(e)=1
. Here,
\gamma_{\rm HC}
denotes the Harish-Chandra isomorphism

\displaystyle\gamma_{\rm HC}:D_{G}(X)\xrightarrow{\;\sim\;}\mathrm{Sym}(\mathfrak{a}_{\mathbb{C}})^{W}

onto the
W
-invariant elements of the symmetric algebra of
\mathfrak{a}_{\mathbb{C}}
, viewed as
W
-invariant polynomials on
\mathfrak{a}^{*}_{\mathbb{C}}
. We call
\varphi_{\lambda}
the
spherical function with spectral parameter
\lambda
.

We have
\varphi_{\lambda}=\varphi_{\lambda^{\prime}}
if and only if
\lambda
and
\lambda^{\prime}
are in the same
W
-orbit. For this reason, we call
\mathfrak{a}^{*}_{\mathbb{C}}/W
the space of
spectral parameters.
The spectral parameters lying in
\mathfrak{a}^{*}/W
are called
tempered spectral parameters
. The tempered subspace
\mathfrak{a}^{*}/W
arises naturally in the decomposition of
L^{2}(X)
, as will be seen in the following subsection.

The group
L
has its own Iwasawa decomposition
L=N_{L}AK_{L}
where
N_{L}
is the analytic subgroup corresponding to
\bigoplus_{\alpha\in\Phi_{L}^{+}}\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha}
. We can thus in turn define the spherical function
\varphi_{\lambda}^{L}
on
L
by the formula (
17
), replacing
\rho
with
\rho_{L}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\alpha\in\Phi_{L}^{+}}m_{\alpha}\alpha
. Then
\varphi_{\lambda}^{L}=\varphi_{\lambda^{\prime}}^{L}
if and only if
\lambda
and
\lambda^{\prime}
are in the same
W_{L}
-orbit.

Let
C_{c}^{\infty}(G/\!\!/K)
denote the space of compactly supported smooth bi-
K
-invariant functions. Given
k\in C_{c}^{\infty}(G/\!\!/K)
let

(18)

\widehat{k}(\lambda)=\int_{G}k(g)\varphi_{-\lambda}(g)dg=b_{G}\int_{\mathfrak{a}^{+}}k(e^{H})\varphi_{-\lambda}(e^{H})J(H)dH

be the Harish-Chandra spherical transform. This transform can be inverted using the Harish-Chandra
c
-function
c(\lambda)
, which is a meromorphic function on
\lambda\in\mathfrak{a}^{*}_{\mathbb{C}}
, given by the product

(19)

c(\lambda)=C\prod_{\alpha\in\Phi_{\textnormal{red}}^{+}}c_{\alpha}\Big(\frac{i\langle\lambda,\alpha\rangle}{\langle\alpha,\alpha\rangle}\Big),\qquad c_{\alpha}(s)=\frac{2^{-s}\Gamma(s)}{\Gamma(\frac{1}{2}(\frac{1}{2}m_{\alpha}+1+s))\Gamma(\frac{1}{2}(\frac{1}{2}m_{\alpha}+m_{2\alpha}+s))},

for a non-zero constant
C
. Then we have the following inversion formula:

\displaystyle k(g)=\int_{\mathfrak{a}_{+}^{*}}\widehat{k}(\lambda)\varphi_{\lambda}(g)|c(\lambda)|^{-2}d\lambda.

More generally, for a standard Levi subgroup
L
, we let
c_{L}
be its
c
-function, given by the product (
19
) but with
\Phi_{\textnormal{red}}^{+}
replaced by
\Phi^{+}_{\textnormal{red},L}
.

3.5.
Bounds on the
c
-function and the spherical function

In this section we recall standard bounds on the
c
-function that will be used throughout the later sections and state the generalized Harish-Chandra asymptotic of the spherical function, a result which undergirds the proof of Theorem
2.4
.

From its definition in (
19
) it is clear that each function
c_{\alpha}(s)
has a simple pole at
s=0

3.7.
Benjamini–Schramm convergence

A lattice
\Gamma<G
is a discrete subgroup of finite covolume. The quotient
Y=\Gamma\backslash X
is a Riemannian locally symmetric space of non-positive curvature. A lattice
\Gamma
is said to be
uniform
if
Y
is compact and
irreducible
if the projection of
\Gamma
onto each simple factor is dense. We will generally assume that
\Gamma
is uniform, irreducible, and torsion-free.

The injectivity radius of a point
y\in Y
, denoted
\textnormal{InjRad}_{Y}(y)
, is the supremum of all
r
such that the ball of radius
r
centered at any lift
\tilde{y}\in X
of
y
maps injectively to
Y
under the canonical projection
X\to Y
. The (global) injectivity radius of
Y
, denoted
\textnormal{InjRad}(Y)
, is the infimum over all
y\in Y
of
\textnormal{InjRad}_{Y}(y)
. We define

\displaystyle Y_{\leq R}:=\{y\in Y:\textnormal{InjRad}_{Y}(y)\leq R\}.

Suppose
Y_{n}
is a sequence of locally symmetric spaces obtained from quotienting
X
by a sequence of torsion-free lattices. We say that the sequence
Y_{n}

Benjamini–Schramm converges
to
X
if, for every
R>0
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{\textnormal{vol}((Y_{n})_{\leq R})}{\textnormal{vol}(Y_{n})}\to 0.

We say that the sequence
Y_{n}
is
uniformly discrete
if there is a universal non-zero lower bound on
\textnormal{InjRad}(Y_{n})
.

3.8.

Let

3.9.
Spectral gap

If
(\pi,V_{\pi})
is a (not necessarily irreducible) separable unitary representation of
G
, then we define its integrability exponent by

\displaystyle q(\pi):=\inf\{q\geq 2:\langle\pi(g)v_{1},v_{2}\rangle\in L^{q}(G)\textnormal{ for $v_{1},v_{2}$ in a dense subspace of $V_{\pi}$}\}.

We say that
(\pi,V_{\pi})
has a spectral gap if
q(\pi)<\infty
, and that a family of unitary representations
\{(\pi_{n},V_{\pi_{n}})\}
has a uniform spectral gap if we can find a uniform upper bound on
q(\pi_{n})
. We call the representation
tempered
if
q(\pi)=2
.

4.
Spectral estimate

In this section, we establish an upper bound on the spectral sums appearing in (

4.1.

We fix a simple factor

Let

where we have used (

in the definition of

is essentially the square-root of the volume of

Some comments are in order on the relation between the sets

and the sets used for similar purposes elsewhere in the literature. The averaging operators used in the work of Le Masson–Sahlsten

Ultimately, the fundamental problem, which we have already emphasized in Section

, is to choose a directing element

Proposition 4.3
.

Lemma 4.5
.

Let

be a non-compact simple real Lie group. Let

Proof.

Since

consists precisely of those elements of

which fix

that vanish on a nonzero element of

\bigcup_{w\notin W_{M}}W.\mathbb{R}(H_{0}-wH_{0})=\bigcup_{w\in W}W\mathbb{R}.(H_{0}-wH_{0}).

Recalling the definition of

in (

\bigcup_{w\in W}W\mathbb{R}.(H_{0}-wH_{0})\supseteq\bigcup_{\alpha\in\Phi}\mathbb{R}.\alpha^{\vee}.

For

denote the orthogonal reflection in

across

H_{0}-s_{\alpha}H_{0}=2\frac{\langle H_{0},\alpha^{\vee}\rangle}{\langle\alpha^{\vee},\alpha^{\vee}\rangle}\alpha^{\vee}.

Note that the subset of all long roots in
\Phi
spans
\mathfrak{a}^{*}
, as does the subset of all short roots. Since

there must therefore be a long root

and a short root

such that

∎

Our goal is to prove Proposition

. From Lemmas

Proposition 4.6
.

Let
G
be a non-compact simple real Lie group. Let

\frac{1}{\tau}\int_{\tau}^{2\tau}\Big|C_{M}\sum_{w\in W/W_{M}}c^{M}(-w\lambda)\widehat{\mathds{1}_{B^{M}}}(w\lambda)e^{-itw\lambda(H_{0})}\Big|^{2}dt>c\epsilon_{0}^{2\dim X_{M}}

Proof.

We expand the square and swap the order of summation and integral to get the sum of the diagonal term

We claim that for any compact

(1)

for all
\lambda\in\Omega
, and

(2)

for all
\lambda\in\Omega
,

with the implied constants depending on
\Omega
. Taking
\tau_{0}
sufficiently large will guarantee that

is negligible with respect to

for
\tau\geq\tau_{0}
.

We begin by proving the stated lower bound on

. It will be sufficient to show that if

is sufficiently small we have

for all
\lambda\in\Omega
. Recall that

is holomorphic and non-vanishing on the open subset containing

described in (
23
). Since

and
\Omega
is compact, we deduce that

is uniformly bounded away from zero on
\Omega
. Furthermore, we have

As in the proof of Lemma
4.2
there exists a neighborhood

of the identity in

such that

for all

and
\lambda\in\Omega
. We can therefore choose
\epsilon_{0}>0
small enough in the definition of

\int_{B^{M}}{\rm Re}\,\varphi_{-\lambda}^{M}(g)dg>\frac{1}{2}{\rm vol}(B^{M})\gg\epsilon_{0}^{\dim X_{M}}.

We deduce that

We now address the off-diagonal term. Firstly, since

, we have that

We thus have

E(\tau,\lambda)\ll\epsilon_{0}^{2\dim X_{M}}\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}w,w^{\prime}\in W\\
wW_{M}\neq w^{\prime}W_{M}\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{\tau}\bigg|\int_{\tau}^{2\tau}e^{-it\lambda(wH_{0}-w^{\prime}H_{0})}dt\bigg|.

5.
Geometric estimate: statement and reduction steps

We deduce from Theorem

The remainder of the paper is organized around the proof of Theorem

5.1.
A general bound on Hilbert–Schmidt norms

Lemma 5.2
.

Let

\|\mathbf{A}\|_{\rm HS}^{2}\leq\int_{\Gamma\backslash G}\int_{G}|A(g,h)|^{2}dgdh+\frac{e^{cT}}{{\rm InjRad}(Y)^{\dim Y}}{\rm vol}\big(Y_{\leq 2T}\big)\|A\|_{\infty}^{2},

for all

Proof.

See Lemma 5.2 of

[

]
to the general setting.
∎

To prove Theorem

Lemma 5.3
.

The integral operator
\mathbf{A}(\tau)
on

, as defined in (

), has kernel
\sum_{\gamma\in\Gamma}A(\tau)(g,\gamma h)
, where

is the function on

given by

A(\tau)(g,h)=\frac{1}{\tau}\int_{\tau}^{2\tau}e^{-2t\rho(H_{0})}\int_{gE_{t}\cap hE_{t}}a(x)dxdt.

Proof.

By definition, for a function

,

(U_{t}aU_{t}^{*})f(g)=e^{-2t\rho(H_{0})}\int_{E_{t}}a(gh_{1})\int_{E_{t}^{-1}}f(gh_{1}h_{2})dh_{2}dh_{1}.

We change variables in the inner sum by setting

and use Fubini’s theorem to get

\displaystyle(U_{t}aU_{t}^{*})f(g)

\displaystyle=e^{-2t\rho(H_{0})}\int_{E_{t}}a(gh_{1})\int_{G}f(h){\mathds{1}}_{E_{t}^{-1}}(h_{1}^{-1}g^{-1}h)dhdh_{1}

\displaystyle=e^{-2t\rho(H_{0})}\int_{G}f(h)\int_{G}a(gh_{1}){\mathds{1}}_{E_{t}^{-1}}(h_{1}^{-1}g^{-1}h){\mathds{1}}_{E_{t}}(h_{1})dh_{1}dh.

We then change variables via
x=gh_{1}
, and use the left-
\Gamma

, to get

\displaystyle(U_{t}aU_{t}^{*})f(g)

\displaystyle=e^{-2t\rho(H_{0})}\int_{G}f(h)\int_{G}a(x){\mathds{1}}_{E_{t}^{-1}}(x^{-1}h){\mathds{1}}_{E_{t}}(g^{-1}x)dxdh

\displaystyle=e^{-2t\rho(H_{0})}\int_{G}f(h)\int_{gE_{t}\cap hE_{t}}a(x)dxdh

\displaystyle=e^{-2t\rho(H_{0})}\int_{\Gamma\backslash G}f(h)\sum_{\gamma\in\Gamma}\int_{gE_{t}\cap\gamma hE_{t}}a(x)dxdh.

Averaging over

yields the result.
∎

We begin by proving the following triangle inequality in the irreducible symmetric space

. Given

\textnormal{Conv}(W.H)
denote the convex hull of the

Lemma 5.4
.

For all
x,y,z\in G/K
, we have

d_{\bar{\mathfrak{a}}_{+}}(x,z)\preceq d_{\bar{\mathfrak{a}}_{+}}(x,y)+d_{\bar{\mathfrak{a}}_{+}}(y,z).

Proof.

We first reduce the proof to the corresponding Lie algebra version, using
[
23
]
. Let
\mathfrak{p}
be the orthogonal complement to
\mathfrak{k}
with respect to the Cartan involution; thus
\mathfrak{p}
is invariant under
\textnormal{Ad}(K)
. We have a Cartan decomposition with respect to adjoint
K
-action on
\mathfrak{p}
, namely
\mathfrak{p}=\bigsqcup_{H\in\overline{\mathfrak{a}}_{+}}K.H
.

Let
\kappa:\mathfrak{p}\to\overline{\mathfrak{a}}_{+}
denote the corresponding Cartan projection, and let
d^{\,\inf}_{\bar{\mathfrak{a}}_{+}}:\mathfrak{p}\times\mathfrak{p}\to\overline{\mathfrak{a}}_{+}
,
d_{\bar{\mathfrak{a}}_{+}}^{\,\inf}(X,Y)=\kappa(Y-X)
, denote the associated Weyl chamber-valued metric; this metric is invariant under the action of the Cartan motion group
K\ltimes\mathfrak{p}
. According to Main Theorem 1.2 of
[
23
]
, given three points

such that
d_{\bar{\mathfrak{a}}_{+}}(x,y)=\lambda_{1}
,
d_{\bar{\mathfrak{a}}_{+}}(y,z)=\lambda_{2}
and
d_{\bar{\mathfrak{a}}_{+}}(x,z)=\lambda_{3}
, there exist three points

such that
d_{\bar{\mathfrak{a}}_{+}}^{\inf}(X,Y)=\lambda_{1}
,
d_{\bar{\mathfrak{a}}_{+}}^{\inf}(Y,Z)=\lambda_{2}
and
d_{\bar{\mathfrak{a}}_{+}}^{\inf}(X,Z)=\lambda_{3}
(and vice versa). Thus, it suffices to show that for any
X,Y,Z\in\mathfrak{p}
we have that

\displaystyle d_{\bar{\mathfrak{a}}_{+}}^{\,\inf}(X,Z)\preceq d_{\bar{\mathfrak{a}}_{+}}^{\,\inf}(X,Y)+d_{\bar{\mathfrak{a}}_{+}}^{\,\inf}(Y,Z).

This is in turn equivalent to showing that

(43)

\displaystyle\kappa(X+Y)\preceq\kappa(X)+\kappa(Y)

for any
X,Y\in\mathfrak{p}
.

Recall that the Killing form on
\mathfrak{p}
is positive definite. Let
\Pi_{\mathfrak{a}}:\mathfrak{p}\to\mathfrak{a}
denote the orthogonal projection with respect to the Killing form. By the Kostant convexity theorem
[

, Theorem 8.2]
, for any

, we have
\Pi_{\mathfrak{a}}(\textnormal{Ad}(K).X)=\textnormal{Conv}(W.X)
. Now, given

, we wish to show (
43
). By applying an element of
K
, we may assume that

, and thus
\kappa(X+Y)=X+Y
. We also have that
X=k_{1}.\kappa(X)
and
Y=k_{2}.\kappa(Y)
for some
k_{i}\in K
. We thus have

\displaystyle\kappa(X+Y)

\displaystyle=X+Y

\displaystyle=\Pi_{\mathfrak{a}}(X+Y)

\displaystyle=\Pi_{\mathfrak{a}}(X)+\Pi_{\mathfrak{a}}(Y)

\displaystyle=\Pi_{\mathfrak{a}}(k_{1}.\kappa(X))+\Pi_{\mathfrak{a}}(k_{2}.\kappa(Y))

\displaystyle\in\textnormal{Conv}(W.\kappa(X))+\textnormal{Conv}(W.\kappa(Y))

\displaystyle\subseteq\textnormal{Conv}(W.(\kappa(X)+\kappa(Y))),

the last equality following from Lemma
5.5
below.
∎

Lemma 5.5
.

If
X,Y\in\bar{\mathfrak{a}}_{+}
, then
\textnormal{Conv}(W.X)+\textnormal{Conv}(W.Y)\subseteq\textnormal{Conv}(W.(X+Y))
.

Proof.

Since both sides of the inclusion are
W
-invariant, it suffices to show

(\textnormal{Conv}(W.X)+\textnormal{Conv}(W.Y))\cap\bar{\mathfrak{a}}_{+}\subset\textnormal{Conv}(W.(X+Y))\cap\bar{\mathfrak{a}}_{+}.

We apply
[
19
, Ch. IV, §8, Lemma 8.3]
to express the right-hand side as
(X+Y-{}_{+}C)\cap\bar{\mathfrak{a}}_{+}
, where

{}_{+}C=\{H\in\mathfrak{a}:\langle H,H^{\prime}\rangle\geq 0\text{ for all }H^{\prime}\in\bar{\mathfrak{a}}_{+}\}.

We are therefore reduced to showing that

(\textnormal{Conv}(W.X)+\textnormal{Conv}(W.Y))\cap\bar{\mathfrak{a}}_{+}\subset\{H\in\bar{\mathfrak{a}}_{+}:\langle H,H^{\prime}\rangle\leq\langle X+Y,H^{\prime}\rangle,\;\forall\;H^{\prime}\in\bar{\mathfrak{a}}_{+}\}.

Let

, where
H_{1}\in\textnormal{Conv}(W.X)
and
H_{2}\in\textnormal{Conv}(W.Y)
. By another application of

Proposition 5.7

(Nevo)
.

Let

\displaystyle\big\|r_{\Sigma}^{0}({\rm vol}(E)^{-1}\mathds{1}_{E})\big\|_{\textnormal{op}}\leq C\textnormal{vol}(E)^{-\theta},

where

denotes the operator norm.

.

Proposition 5.9
.

5.4.
Application of the degenerate Brion’s formula

Recall the polytope

Denote by

6.
A spectral approach to bounding intersection volumes

In this section we reduce the proof of Theorem

under suitable assumptions on

, to one combinatorial result and two spectral theoretic results. One of these spectral theoretic results is Theorem

. The other is stated below as Theorem

and crucially requires the assumptions on

present in Theorem

. These assumptions are assured to hold for groups having classical and

root systems by the combinatorial result, Theorem
2.2
.

After having made this reduction in this section, we then prove Theorem
2.2
in Section
7
, Theorem
6.4
in Section
8
, and Theorem
2.4
in Section

.

Throughout this section, we shall usually assume that

is semisimple, as most of the results hold in this generality, while indicating clearly the places where we need to impose the condition of

being simple. We extend the definition of
S_{t}
from (
28
) to the case of

semisimple in the obvious way.

6.1.
An initial bound due to J.-P. Anker

We first present a bound on
\textnormal{vol}(e^{H}S_{t}\cap S_{t})
communicated to us by J.-P. Anker which gives a weaker bound but with much less work. This will serve as a brief illustration of how harmonic analytic information, such as bounds on the spherical function and the
c
-function, can be used to estimate the geometric intersection volume, and will act as a yardstick for measuring the extent to which a more elementary approach falls short of the bounds in Theorem
2.3
. In this subsection with take
H_{0},H\in\overline{\mathfrak{a}}_{+}
to be arbitrary.

We first recall that, for
\lambda\in\mathfrak{a^{*}}
we have the bound

\displaystyle|\varphi_{\lambda}(e^{H})|\leq\varphi_{0}(e^{H}).

See, for instance,
[
17
, Prop. 4.6.1]
. Inserting this, we find

\displaystyle\textnormal{vol}(e^{H}S_{t}\cap S_{t})

\displaystyle=(\mathds{1}_{S_{t}}*\mathds{1}_{S_{t}}^{\vee})(e^{H})

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathfrak{a}_{+}^{*}}|\widehat{\mathds{1}}_{S_{t}}(\lambda)|^{2}\varphi_{\lambda}(e^{H})|c(\lambda)|^{2}d\lambda

\displaystyle\leq\varphi_{0}(e^{H})\int_{\mathfrak{a}_{+}^{*}}|\widehat{\mathds{1}}_{S_{t}}(\lambda)|^{2}|c(\lambda)|^{2}d\lambda

\displaystyle=\varphi_{0}(e^{H})\|\mathds{1}_{S_{t}}\|^{2}_{L^{2}}=\varphi_{0}(e^{H})\textnormal{vol}(S_{t}).

We have that
\textnormal{vol}(S_{t})\asymp e^{2\rho(tH_{0})}
and by
[
5
]
we have

\displaystyle\varphi_{0}(e^{H})\asymp e^{-\rho(H)}\prod_{\alpha\in\Phi_{\textnormal{red}}^{+}}(1+\alpha(H)).

Letting
L
denote the centralizer of
H
in
G
, we thus get

(48)

\textnormal{vol}(e^{H}S_{t}\cap S_{t})\ll e^{\rho(2tH_{0}-H)}\prod_{\alpha\in\Phi_{\textnormal{red}}^{+}}(1+\alpha(H))\ll e^{\rho(2tH_{0}-H)}\|H\|^{|\Phi_{\textnormal{red}}^{+}\smallsetminus\Phi_{\textnormal{red},L}^{+}|},

as all roots in
\Phi_{\textnormal{red},L}^{+}
vanish on
H
.

Inserting (
48
) (applied to the simple factor
G_{1}
of
G
), instead of Theorem
2.3
, into the analysis leading to (
46
) would yield

\displaystyle M(\tau)\ll\frac{\|a\|^{2}_{2}}{\tau^{2}\theta^{2}}\int_{P_{2\tau}}\|H\|^{2|\Phi_{\textnormal{red}}^{1+}\smallsetminus\Phi_{\textnormal{red},L_{1}}^{+}|(1-\theta)}e^{\theta\rho(H-2\|H\|_{P}H_{0})}dH.

Here,
\Phi_{\textnormal{red}}^{1}
and
\Phi_{\textnormal{red}}^{1+}
are the reduced roots and positive reduced roots of the simple factor
G_{1}
, respectively, and
\Phi_{\textnormal{red},L_{1}}^{+}
denotes the positive reduced roots of
L_{1}
, the centralizer of
H
in
G_{1}
. Now Remark
5.8
shows that
2|\Phi_{\textnormal{red}}^{1+}\smallsetminus\Phi_{\textnormal{red},L_{1}}^{+}|(1-\theta)\geq 1
for all
H\neq 0
, in which case the above integral is at least
\tau^{2}
, cancelling out the factor
\frac{1}{\tau^{2}}
and thus obtaining no decay. We conclude that a bound such as (
48
) would not suffice to complete the proof of Theorem
1.1
.

The point of Theorem
2.3
is to strengthen this bound to replace the polynomial factor in
H
with a log factor, in the case where
H_{0}
is taken to be extremal.

6.2.
The endpoint Kunze–Stein phenomenon

The Kunze–Stein phenomenon
[
26
]
says that if
G
is a semisimple real Lie group, then for any
p\in[1,2)
there is
c_{p}>0
such that, for all
f\in L^{p}(G)
and
g\in L^{2}(G)
, we have

\displaystyle\|f*g\|_{2}\leq c_{p}\|f\|_{p}\|g\|_{2}.

In fact, the Kunze–Stein phenomenon is one of the main ingredients powering the Nevo ergodic theorem
[
30
]
, as stated in Proposition
5.7
.

In case
G
has real rank one, Ionescu
[
22
]
strengthened this to an endpoint estimate

(49)

\|f*g\|_{2,\infty}\leq c\|f\|_{2,1}\|g\|_{2,1},

where
\|\cdot\|_{p,q}
is the
(p,q)
Lorentz norm. It was noticed by the second author that if
G
is a group satisfying the endpoint Kunze–Stein phenomenon for bi-
K
-invariant functions, then

\textnormal{vol}(e^{H}S_{t}\cap S_{t})\ll e^{\rho(2tH_{0}-H)}

for any choice of
H_{0}
and
H
. J.-P. Anker later communicated to us a simplified argument of this implication, which we now present.

For the purposes of this subsection, we shall deviate slightly from our earlier notation. For any
H\in\overline{\mathfrak{a}}_{+}
and
r>0
we define

\displaystyle S_{H,r}:=K\exp(B_{\mathfrak{a}}(H,r))K.

The reason for introducing this more precise notation can be seen in the following elementary statement, where we keep track of this radius.

Lemma 6.1
.

Let
H_{0},H\in\overline{\mathfrak{a}}_{+}
and
r>0
. We have

\textnormal{vol}(e^{H}S_{H_{0},r}\cap S_{H_{0},r})\leq\textnormal{vol}(gS_{H_{0},2r}\cap S_{H_{0},r})

for all
g\in S_{H,r}
.

Proof.

It will be enough to show that, for any
Z\in B_{\mathfrak{a}}(0,r)
,

(50)

\textnormal{vol}(e^{H}S_{H_{0},r}\cap S_{H_{0},r})\leq\textnormal{vol}(e^{H+Z}S_{H_{0},2r}\cap S_{H_{0},r}).

Indeed, for any
g\in S_{H,r}
there is
Z\in B_{\mathfrak{a}}(0,r)
such that
g\in Ke^{H+Z}K
. Since the sets
S_{H_{0},r}
are bi-
K
-invariant, it follows that
\textnormal{vol}(gS_{H_{0},2r}\cap S_{H_{0},r})=\textnormal{vol}(e^{H+Z}S_{H_{0},2r}\cap S_{H_{0},r})
.

The inequality (
50
) in turn follows from

e^{H}S_{H_{0},r}\subset e^{H+Z}S_{H_{0},2r},

valid for any
Z\in B_{\mathfrak{a}}(0,r)
. To see this inclusion, suppose that
x\in e^{H}S_{H_{0},r}
. We deduce from Lemma
5.4
that

\displaystyle d_{\overline{\mathfrak{a}}_{+}}(e^{H+Z},x)\preceq d_{\overline{\mathfrak{a}}_{+}}(e^{H+Z},e^{H})+d_{\overline{\mathfrak{a}}_{+}}(e^{H},x).

The first term on the right-hand side lies in
B_{\mathfrak{a}}(0,r)
, and the second term lies in
B_{\mathfrak{a}}(H_{0},r)
. This implies that the sum lies in
B_{\mathfrak{a}}(H_{0},2r)
, as desired.
∎

We can re-express Lemma
6.1
as stating that

\displaystyle\textnormal{vol}(e^{H}S_{H_{0},r}\cap S_{H_{0},r})\mathds{1}_{S_{H},r}\leq\mathds{1}_{S_{H_{0}},r}*\mathds{1}_{S_{H_{0}},2r}^{\vee}.

We now apply the endpoint Kunze–Stein property (
49
) to obtain

\displaystyle\textnormal{vol}(e^{H}S_{H_{0},r}\cap S_{H_{0},r})\|\mathds{1}_{S_{H,r}}\|_{2,\infty}\leq\|\mathds{1}_{S_{H_{0},r}}\|_{2,1}\|\mathds{1}_{S_{H_{0}},2r}\|_{2,1}.

Finally we have the elementary estimate

\displaystyle\|\mathds{1}_{S_{H_{0}},r}\|_{2,q}\asymp e^{\rho(H_{0})}

for all
1\leq q\leq\infty
. This implies
\textnormal{vol}(e^{H}S_{H_{0},r}\cap S_{H_{0},r})\ll e^{\rho(2H_{0}-H)}
.

Remark 6.2
.

As remarked in
[
22
]
, the bound (
49
) fails when
G
is of rank at least
2
. The fact that Theorem
2.3
nevertheless recovers the same estimate on intersection volumes (up to
\log
factors) for balls directed by extremal
H_{0}
, suggests that a version of the endpoint Kunze–Stein phenomenon, in which one restricts to bi-
K
-invariant functions supported near extremal elements, may extend to the higher rank setting.

6.3.
Reduction to a spectral estimate

We now return to the usual notation for
S_{t}
, as defined in (

We introduce a smooth bump function that dominates

to facilitate the analysis. Let

We have

(k_{t}*k_{t}^{\vee})(e^{H})=\int_{\mathfrak{a}^{*}_{+}}|\widehat{k}_{t}(\lambda)|^{2}\varphi_{\lambda}(e^{H})|c(\lambda)|^{-2}d\lambda.

We shall bound this expression using Theorem
2.4

5

Proposition 6.3
.

Let

be used to define

\widehat{k}_{t}(\lambda)\ll_{N}(1+\|\lambda\|)^{-N}e^{\rho(tH_{0})}\Theta(tH_{0},\lambda),

Assuming Theorem

(k_{t}*k_{t}^{\vee})(e^{H})\ll_{N}e^{\rho(2tH_{0}-H)}\int_{\mathfrak{a}^{*}_{+}}(1+\|\lambda\|)^{-N}\Theta(tH_{0},\lambda)^{2}\Theta(H,\lambda)|c(\lambda)|^{-2}d\lambda.

The right-hand side is now “elementary”, in the sense that, once one replaces

Theorem 6.4
.

Let
G

(52)

\int_{\mathfrak{a}^{*}_{+}}(1+\|\lambda\|)^{-N}\Theta(tH_{0},\lambda)^{2}\Theta(H,\lambda)|c(\lambda)|^{-2}d\lambda\ll(\log t)^{k}

The combination of Theorem

6.4.
Proof of Proposition

We let

Here we have identified the right

\widehat{k}_{t}(\lambda)=b_{G}(\|\rho\|^{2}+\|\lambda\|^{2})^{-N}\int_{\mathfrak{a}_{+}}\Delta^{N}k_{t}(e^{H})\varphi_{-\lambda}(e^{H})J(H)dH.

Proposition

Lemma 6.5
.

Let

To see how Proposition

Recalling the definition (

We recall the expression for

denote the Laplacian on the flat manifold

\Delta_{\rm rad}=\Delta_{\mathfrak{a}}+\sum_{\alpha\in\Phi^{+}}m_{\alpha}\coth(\alpha)X_{\alpha},

where

For

denote the translation operator by

\displaystyle k_{t}(e^{H})=\sum_{w\in W/W_{M}}\psi(wH-tH_{0})=\sum_{w\in W/W_{M}}T_{tH_{0}}\psi(wH).

We define the translated operator
\Delta_{\rm rad}^{t}=T_{-tH_{0}}\Delta_{\rm rad}T_{tH_{0}}

is given by

\Delta_{\rm rad}^{t}=\Delta_{\mathfrak{a}}+\sum_{\alpha\in\Phi^{+}}m_{\alpha}\coth(\alpha(\cdot-tH_{0}))X_{\alpha}.

Therefore,

\displaystyle\Delta^{N}k_{t}(e^{H})=\sum_{w\in W/W_{M}}\big(T_{tH_{0}}(\Delta_{\text{rad}}^{t})^{N}\psi\big)(wH)=\sum_{w\in W/W_{M}}\big((\Delta_{\text{rad}}^{t})^{N}\psi\big)(wH-tH_{0}).

(\Delta_{\rm rad}^{t})^{N}\psi\ll_{N}{\mathds{1}}_{B}

. This follows from the following lemma.

Lemma 6.6
.

Suppose
\mathcal{B}
is a bounded subset of
C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathfrak{a})^{W_{M}}
whose elements are supported in a compact set
\mathcal{K}
. If

are all non-vanishing on

for

and

\bigcup_{t\geq t_{0}}\Delta^{t}_{\textnormal{rad}}\mathcal{B}
is also bounded in
C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathfrak{a})^{W_{M}}
.

Proof.

The operator
\Delta_{\rm rad}^{t}
has singularities along the root hyperplanes corresponding to roots in
\Phi^{+}_{M}
. To deal with these, it will be convenient to also introduce the symmetric space
X_{M}=M/K_{M}
, with Laplacian
\Delta_{M}
. The radial part of
\Delta_{M}
is given by

\Delta_{M,\rm rad}=\Delta_{\mathfrak{a}}+\sum_{\alpha\in\Phi^{+}_{M}}m_{\alpha}\coth(\alpha)X_{\alpha},

so that

\Delta_{\rm rad}^{t}=\Delta_{M,\rm rad}+\sum_{\alpha\in\Phi^{+}\smallsetminus\Phi_{M}^{+}}m_{\alpha}\coth(\alpha(\cdot-tH_{0}))X_{\alpha}.

The advantage of doing this is that we have written
\Delta_{\rm rad}^{t}
as the sum of the radial part of the smooth operator
\Delta_{M}

\coth(\alpha(\cdot-tH_{0}))X_{\alpha}

We shall prove that

We have

are all bounded on

or

, we see that

is also bounded on

\bigcup_{t\geq t_{0}}\Delta_{M,\text{rad}}\mathcal{B}
is a bounded set.

For any choice of
\varepsilon>0
, we have that for every

there exists a

such that
|\frac{d^{n}}{dx^{n}}\coth(x)|<C_{n}
for

. Therefore, by the product rule, we conclude that

\displaystyle\bigcup_{t\geq t_{0}}\Big(\sum_{\alpha\in\Phi^{+}\smallsetminus\Phi^{+}_{M}}m_{\alpha}\coth(\alpha(\cdot-tH_{0}))X_{\alpha}\Big)\mathcal{B}

is also a bounded set.
∎

7.
Some properties of root subsystems

In this section, we introduce the notions of
semi-dense
and
extremal
root subsystems. The former are defined by a combinatorial inequality which appears naturally in the proof of Theorem
2.3
, while the latter, when well-defined, are easy to construct in practice. We then prove Theorems
2.1
and
2.2
, by examining when such root subsystems exist and how they relate to one another.

7.1.
Semi-dense and extremal root subsystems

Throughout this section
\Phi
will denote a reduced root system of rank
n
in a vector space
V
with
\Delta
as a base of simple roots.

We say that a root subsystem of
\Phi
is
standard
if it is the root subsystem generated by a subset of the simple roots, and that it is
semistandard
if it is the image of a standard root subsystem under an element of the Weyl group. Semistandard root subsystems can be characterized as those arising as
\Phi\cap V^{\prime}
where
V^{\prime}
is some subspace of
V
(Chapter VI,§1, Proposition 24 of
[
8
]
).

Definition 7.1
.

Let
\Phi
be a reduced root system. We say that a semistandard root subsystem
\Phi_{0}\subset\Phi
is
semi-dense
if, for every semistandard root subsystem
\Psi\subset\Phi
we have

(54)

|\Psi\cap\Phi_{0}|+\textnormal{rank}(\Psi)\geq\frac{1}{2}|\Psi|.

Remark 7.2
.

Suppose
\Phi=\Phi^{(1)}\sqcup\dots\sqcup\Phi^{(m)}
with each
\Phi^{(j)}
irreducible. Suppose
\Phi^{(1)}
admits a semi-dense root subsystem
\Phi^{(1)}_{0}
. Then the root subsystem
\Phi^{(1)}_{0}\sqcup\Phi^{(2)}\sqcup\dots\sqcup\Phi^{(m)}
is semi-dense in
\Phi
.

As an example of a maximal root subsystem which is
not
semi-dense, let
\Phi
be of type
B_{3}
and take for
\Phi_{0}
the
A_{1}\times A_{1}
root subsystem of
\Phi
which is obtained by removing the middle node from the Dynkin diagram of
B_{3}
. We test the inequality (
54
) with
\Psi=\Phi
. Then
\Phi
has 18 roots, and
\Phi_{0}
has 4 roots. Thus
|\Psi\cap\Phi_{0}|+\text{rank}(\Psi)=|\Phi_{0}|+\text{rank}(\Phi)=4+3
which is smaller than
\frac{1}{2}|\Psi|=\frac{1}{2}|\Phi|=9
.

If
\Phi
is of type
A_{n}
, one can show that any maximal semistandard root subsystem is semi-dense. We will, however, only prove this for a special type of maximal root subsystems, which we call
extremal
, in Proposition
7.4
below. We introduce the notion of extremal root subsystems for root systems of classical type and
E_{7}
. This will be our primary source of examples of semi-dense root subsystems:

Definition 7.3
.

Let
\Phi
be a rank
n
irreducible root system. Let
\Phi_{0}
be a rank
n-1
, irreducible, semistandard, root subsystem of
\Phi
. Then
\Phi_{0}
is said to be
extremal
if

(1)

\Phi
is of classical type, and
\Phi_{0}
in the same family as
\Phi
(with the convention that
B_{1}=A_{1}
,
C_{2}=B_{2}
, and
D_{3}=A_{3}
), or

(2)

\Phi
is of type
E_{7}
and
\Phi_{0}
is of type
E_{6}
.

If
\Phi
is a reducible rank
n
root system,
\Phi=\Phi^{(1)}\sqcup\dots\sqcup\Phi^{(m)}
with all
\Phi^{(j)}
irreducible, we call a rank
n-1
, semistandard root subsystem
\Phi_{0}=\Phi_{0}^{(1)}\sqcup\dots\sqcup\Phi_{0}^{(m)}
of
\Phi
extremal if there exists
1\leq m_{0}\leq m
such that
\Phi_{0}^{(j)}=\Phi^{(j)}
for all
j\neq m_{0}
and
\Phi_{0}^{(m_{0})}
is extremal in
\Phi^{(m_{0})}
in the previous sense.

We include the degenerate case of
\Phi=A_{1}
,
\Phi_{0}=A_{0}=\emptyset
in this definition. Note that in this case
\Phi_{0}
is semi-dense in
\Phi
:
\Psi=\Phi
is the only non-empty root subsystem of
\Phi
, and therefore
|\Psi\cap\Phi_{0}|=0
,
\textnormal{rank}(\Psi)=1
, and
|\Psi|=2
.

The terminology is intentionally reminiscent of the class of extremal fundamental coweights, introduced in Section
3.6
. Indeed, extremal root subsystems, as defined above, are simply root subsystems obtained by applying an element of
W
to the root subsystem
\Phi_{M,\textrm{red}}
of
\Phi_{\textrm{red}}
, with
M
the centralizer of an extremal element. Though not all semi-dense root subsystems are extremal, the extremal ones provide a natural class of semi-dense root subsystems for all types containing a semi-dense root subsystem.

7.2.
Classical root systems

We begin by showing that for classical roots systems, extremal implies semi-dense. This will prove the direct implication of Theorem
2.1
, as well as Theorem
2.2
, for classical groups.

Proposition 7.4
.

Suppose
\Phi
is an irreducible root system of classical type. Then any extremal root subsystem is semi-dense.

Proof.

Let
\Phi_{0}\subset\Phi
be extremal and let
\Psi\subset\Phi
be semistandard. In fact, it will be enough to verify Definition
7.1

, where

|\Psi\cap\Phi_{0}|=|\Psi_{\rm st}\cap w^{-1}\Phi_{0}|

\textnormal{rank}(\Psi)=\textnormal{rank}(\Psi_{\rm st})

\Psi_{\rm st}

We now let
\Psi

\Psi

\Psi

Our strategy is to reduce the verification of (

7.3.
The type

We now complete the proof of the direct implication of Theorem

Proposition 7.5
.

Let
\Phi
be of type

, and let
\Phi_{0}
be an extremal root subsystem. Then
\Phi_{0}
is semi-dense.

Proof.

We have as a model for the

of the form

with

as well as all permutations of

\displaystyle-e_{2}+e_{3},-e_{3}+e_{4},-e_{4}+e_{5},-e_{5}+e_{6},-e_{6}+e_{7},-e_{7}+e_{8},

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}(e_{1}+e_{2}+e_{3}+e_{4}-e_{5}-e_{6}-e_{7}-e_{8})\}.

Notice that all of
\Phi
is orthogonal to

\Phi_{0}

\Phi_{0}

The Weyl group of

2^{10}\cdot 3^{4}\cdot 5\cdot 7
, and that of
E_{6}
has order
2^{7}\cdot 3^{4}\cdot 5
. By the orbit-stabilizer theorem, we get that the
W
-orbit of
e_{1}+e_{8}
is of size
2^{3}\cdot 7
. These are exactly the
2^{2}\cdot 7
vectors of the form
e_{i}+e_{j}
with
i\neq j
, as well as the
2^{2}\cdot 7
vectors of the form
\frac{1}{2}(e_{1}+\dots+e_{8})-(e_{i}+e_{j})
with
i\neq j
.

From here it is now easy to formulate a simple algorithm to verify (
54
). Each standard root subsystem can be found by choosing a subset of the base and then calculating which roots lie in their span. Then for each such standard root subsystem
\Psi
and each vector
v=w.(e_{1}+e_{8})
in the
W
-orbit of
e_{1}+e_{8}
described in the previous paragraph, we calculate the number of roots that are orthogonal to
v
. This determines
|\Psi\cap w\Phi_{0}|
from which (
54
) can be easily checked. We carried out this algorithm in Sage
[
36
]
and found that in all cases (
54
) was satisfied; see Appendix
A
for the Sage code that was used.
∎

7.4.
Exceptional root systems, not of type
E_{7}

Finally, we prove that no root subsystem among the exceptional types
E_{6},E_{8},F_{4},G_{2}
is semi-dense, establishing the reverse implication of Theorem
2.1
. This accounts for the exclusion of such root systems in Theorem
1.1
.

Proposition 7.6
.

Suppose
\Phi
is of type
E_{6},E_{8},F_{4}
or
G_{2}
. Then no root subsystem of
\Phi
is semi-dense.

Proof.

In the case of
G_{2}
this is immediate: the only root subsystem is of type
A_{1}
, and if we take
\Psi=\Phi
we see that the inequality fails (
G_{2}
has 12 roots).

The case of
F_{4}
is also straightforward: one may check that if we take
\Psi=\Phi
(which has 48 roots) and
\Phi_{0}
to be any standard proper root subsystem (which will be of type
C_{3}
,
B_{3}
, or
A_{2}\times A_{1}
if they are maximal), then the desired inequality fails when taking
\Psi=\Phi
.

The type
E
cases are more subtle and the desired inequality “just barely” fails to be true (and in fact it holds for
E_{7}
!). If we take for
\Phi_{0}
the
E_{7}
standard root subsystem in
E_{8}
, or the
D_{5}
standard root subsystem in
E_{6}
, then the desired inequality in fact holds for any
\Psi
standard. Constructing counterexamples to (
54
) therefore requires more work.

We start with the case of
\Phi
being the
E_{8}
root system, which has 240 roots. For every maximal standard subroot system of
E_{8}
other than
E_{7}
, one may compute that the desired inequality fails already simply taking
\Psi=\Phi
. Thus we are just left to analyze the case of
\Phi_{0}
of type
E_{7}
.

We can take as our model for
\Phi
the elements in
\mathbb{R}^{8}
of the form
\pm e_{i}\pm e_{j}
with
i\neq j
as well as
\pm\frac{1}{2}e_{1}\pm\frac{1}{2}e_{2}\pm\dots\pm\frac{1}{2}e_{8}
with an even number of minus signs (sum of coefficients is even). We can take as our basis of simple roots:

\displaystyle\{

\displaystyle e_{1}-e_{2},e_{2}-e_{3},e_{3}-e_{4},e_{4}-e_{5},e_{5}-e_{6},e_{6}-e_{7},e_{6}+e_{7},

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}(e_{1}+e_{2}+e_{3}+e_{4}+e_{5}+e_{6}+e_{7}+e_{8})\}.

Let
\Phi_{0}
be the
E_{7}
standard root subsystem which is obtained from removing
e_{1}-e_{2}
from the base. This is exactly the intersection of
\Phi
with the hyperplane orthogonal to
e_{1}-e_{8}
.

Let
w\in W
be the element switching
e_{3}
and
e_{8}
. Then
w\Phi_{0}
consists of roots orthogonal to
e_{1}-e_{3}
. Consider the standard root subsystem
\Psi
generated by
e_{1}-e_{2}
and
e_{2}-e_{3}
. This generates an
A_{2}
type root system. However, no root in this root subsystem is orthogonal to
e_{1}-e_{3}
. Therefore
w\Phi_{0}\cap\Psi=\emptyset
. Thus
|w\Phi_{0}\cap\Psi|+\textnormal{rank}(\Psi)=2
but
\frac{1}{2}|\Psi|=3
. Thus the desired inequality does not hold for
E_{8}
.

Now suppose
\Phi
is of type
E_{6}
. Recall that
E_{6}
has 72 roots. One may easily check that if we take
\Phi_{0}
to be any maximal subroot system other than type
D_{5}
or
A_{5}
, then the desired inequality fails with
\Psi=\Phi
. Thus we must only analyze further the cases of
A_{5}
and
D_{5}
.

Recall that the
A_{2}
root system has a model as vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
of the form
(1,-1,0)
,
(1,0,-1)
,
(0,1,-1)
or their negatives. We may take as our model of
E_{6}
those vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{9}=\mathbb{R}^{3}\times\mathbb{R}^{3}\times\mathbb{R}^{3}
where we separately take a copy of
A_{2}
inside of each of three copies of
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, together with vectors of the form
(A;B;C)
where
A,B,C\in\{(\frac{2}{3},-\frac{1}{3},-\frac{1}{3}),(-\frac{1}{3},\frac{2}{3},-\frac{1}{3}),(-\frac{1}{3},-\frac{1}{3},\frac{2}{3})\}
, and vectors of the form
(A^{\prime};B^{\prime};C^{\prime})
where
A^{\prime},B^{\prime},C^{\prime}\in\{(-\frac{2}{3},\frac{1}{3},\frac{1}{3}),(\frac{1}{3},-\frac{2}{3},\frac{1}{3}),(\frac{1}{3},\frac{1}{3},-\frac{2}{3})\}
. We can take as our simple roots

\displaystyle\{

\displaystyle e_{8}-e_{9},e_{7}-e_{8},e_{5}-e_{6},e_{4}-e_{5},e_{2}-e_{3},

\displaystyle\frac{1}{3}e_{1}-\frac{2}{3}e_{2}+\frac{1}{3}e_{3}-\frac{2}{3}e_{4}+\frac{1}{3}e_{5}+\frac{1}{3}e_{6}-\frac{2}{3}e_{7}+\frac{1}{3}e_{8}+\frac{1}{3}e_{9}\}.

These roots are all orthogonal to
e_{1}+e_{2}+e_{3},e_{4}+e_{5}+e_{6}
, and
e_{7}+e_{8}+e_{9}
.

First we let
\Phi_{0}
be the standard root system of type
A_{5}
. This is obtained taking the root subsystem generated by the simple roots with
e_{2}-e_{3}
removed; equivalently these are the roots orthogonal to
e_{1}-e_{3}
. Let
w\in W
be the element switching
e_{1}
and
e_{2}
. Then
e_{1}-e_{3}
is moved to
e_{2}-e_{3}
, and
w\Phi_{0}
is those roots orthogonal to
e_{2}-e_{3}
. Let
\Psi
be the root subsystem generated by the last two simple roots in the list above. Then
\Psi
is of type
A_{2}
. However no vector in
\Psi
is orthogonal to
e_{2}-e_{3}
. Therefore
|w\Phi_{0}\cap\Psi|=0
, and we see that (
54
) fails.

Lastly we consider
\Phi_{0}
of type
D_{5}
. We shall use a different model for
E_{6}
than the one above. Specifically, we shall take
\Phi
to be the root subsystem of
E_{8}
obtained from removing the simple roots
e_{1}-e_{2}
and
e_{2}-e_{3}
from the base of
E_{8}
. Then
\Phi
is the root subsystem orthogonal to
e_{1}-e_{2}
and
e_{1}-e_{8}
. Let
\Phi_{0}
be the root subsystem of
\Phi
obtained by further removing the element
e_{3}-e_{4}
from the base. These are the roots which lie in the intersection of the space containing
\Phi
with the hyperplane orthogonal to
e_{1}+e_{2}-3e_{3}+e_{8}
. Let
w\in W
be the element which switches
e_{3}
and
e_{6}
. Then
w\Phi_{0}
is orthogonal to
e_{1}+e_{2}-3e_{6}+e_{8}
. Let
\Psi
be the standard root subsystem generated by
e_{6}+e_{7}
and

; then

is of type

. However, no element in

is orthogonal to

, so

. Thus, like before, (

) fails to hold.
∎

8.
Bounding the elementary spectral integral

In this section we prove Theorem
6.4
. Let
r
be the rank of
G
. We define coordinates on
\mathfrak{a}^{*}_{+}
by the map
x\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}^{r}\mapsto\lambda(x)=x_{1}\varpi_{1}+\ldots+x_{r}\varpi_{r}\in\mathfrak{a}^{*}_{+}
. Applying this change of coordinates, the integral in (
52
) becomes

(57)

\int_{\mathbb{R}_{>0}^{r}}(1+\|x\|)^{-N}\Theta(tH_{0},\lambda(x))^{2}\Theta(H,\lambda(x))|c(\lambda(x))|^{-2}dx.

We shall bound this integral by decomposing
\mathbb{R}_{>0}^{r}
into a barycentric subdivision and a further refinement at the scale
t^{-1}
. Similar decompositions of
\mathfrak{a}_{+}
, rather than
\mathfrak{a}_{+}^{*}
, were considered in
[
28
,
38
]
. Since only reduced root systems occur in this section, we shall simplify notation by dropping the subscript
\Phi_{\textnormal{red}}
everywhere, so that
\Phi
,
\Phi_{M}
,
\Phi_{M}^{+}
, etc. denote sets of reduced roots.

8.1.
Barycentric subdivision and corresponding estimates

The first subdivision we consider is determined by an ordering of the coordinates
x_{i}
. For each permutation
\sigma\in S_{r}
, let

T_{\sigma}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}^{r}:x_{\sigma(1)}>\cdots>x_{\sigma(r)}\}.

Away from the hyperplanes
x_{i}=x_{j}
, the regions
T_{\sigma}
form a disjoint decomposition of
\mathbb{R}_{>0}^{r}
. We refer to this as the
barycentric subdivision
of
\mathbb{R}_{>0}^{r}
, since it induces the usual barycentric subdivision of the affine simplex
\{x\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}^{r}:x_{1}+\cdots+x_{r}=1\}
.

Figure 1.
The barycentric subdivision when
r=3
.

For
1\leq i\leq r
, define
\Phi_{\sigma,i}
to be the set of roots orthogonal to
\varpi_{\sigma(1)},\ldots,\varpi_{\sigma(i)}
. In particular,
\Phi_{\sigma,r}=\emptyset
. We furthermore set
\Phi_{\sigma,0}=\Phi
. We observe that
\Phi_{\sigma,i}
is the standard sub-root system consisting of all roots expressible as linear combinations of simple roots in
\Delta\smallsetminus\{\alpha_{\sigma(1)},\ldots,\alpha_{\sigma(i)}\}
.

Lemma 8.1
.

Let
1\leq i\leq r
. If
\alpha\in\Phi_{\sigma,i-1}\smallsetminus\Phi_{\sigma,i}
, then
|\langle\lambda(x),\alpha\rangle|\asymp x_{\sigma(i)}
on
T_{\sigma}
.

Proof.

We may assume that
\alpha\in\Phi^{+}
. Let
e_{1},\ldots,e_{r}
be the standard basis for
\mathbb{R}^{r}
. If
x\in T_{\sigma}
, then
x=x_{\sigma(1)}e_{\sigma(1)}+\ldots+x_{\sigma(r)}e_{\sigma(r)}
with
x_{\sigma(1)}>\ldots>x_{\sigma(r)}
. We have
\langle\alpha,\varpi_{\sigma(j)}\rangle=0
for
1\leq j\leq i-1
, and
\langle\alpha,\varpi_{\sigma(j)}\rangle>0
for
i\leq j\leq r
, so that

\displaystyle\langle\lambda(x),\alpha\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle x_{\sigma(1)}\varpi_{\sigma(1)}+\cdots+x_{\sigma(r)}\varpi_{\sigma(r)},\alpha\rangle

\displaystyle=x_{\sigma(i)}\langle\varpi_{\sigma(i)},\alpha\rangle+\cdots+x_{\sigma(r)}\langle\varpi_{\sigma(r)},\alpha\rangle\asymp x_{\sigma(i)},

as required.
∎

We now use Lemma
8.1
to estimate the various terms appearing in (
57
) on
T_{\sigma}
.

Lemma 8.2
.

Let
\sigma\in S_{r}
. For
x\in T_{\sigma}
and
\|H\|\ll t
, we have

(58)

\Theta(H,\lambda(x))\ll\prod_{i=1}^{r}\min(t,x_{\sigma(i)}^{-1}+1)^{|\Phi_{\sigma,i-1}^{+}|-|\Phi_{\sigma,i}^{+}|}.

Furthermore we have

(59)

\Theta(tH_{0},\lambda(x))\ll\sum_{w\in W}\prod_{i=1}^{r}\min(t,x_{\sigma(i)}^{-1}+1)^{n_{M}(\sigma,i,w)},

where, for
1\leq i\leq r
, we have put
n_{M}(\sigma,i,w)=|(\Phi^{+}\smallsetminus\Phi^{+}_{M})\cap w(\Phi_{\sigma,i-1}\smallsetminus\Phi_{\sigma,i})|
.
Finally, we have

(60)

|c(\lambda(x))|^{-2}\ll(1+x_{\sigma(1)})^{d}\prod_{i=1}^{r}x_{\sigma(i)}^{2|\Phi_{\sigma,i-1}^{+}|-2|\Phi_{\sigma,i}^{+}|},

where
d:=\sum_{\alpha\in\Phi^{+}}m_{\alpha}+m_{2\alpha}
is the sum of the multiplicities of all relative roots.

Proof.

We begin with
\Theta(H,\lambda(x))
. We first apply the trivial estimate
\alpha(H)\ll t
, which gives

\Theta(H,\lambda(x))\ll\sum_{w\in W}\prod_{\alpha\in\Phi^{+}}\min(t,|\langle w\lambda(x),\alpha\rangle|^{-1}+1).

We next break the product over
\Phi^{+}
into the subsets
\Phi^{+}\cap w(\Phi_{\sigma,i-1}\smallsetminus\Phi_{\sigma,i})
and apply Lemma
8.1
to each subset, which gives

\displaystyle\prod_{\alpha\in\Phi^{+}}\min(t,|\langle w\lambda(x),\alpha\rangle|^{-1}+1)

\displaystyle\asymp\prod_{i=1}^{r}\prod_{\alpha\in\Phi^{+}\cap w(\Phi_{\sigma,i-1}\smallsetminus\Phi_{\sigma,i})}\min(t,x_{\sigma(i)}^{-1}+1)

\displaystyle=\prod_{i=1}^{r}\min(t,x_{\sigma(i)}^{-1}+1)^{|\Phi_{\sigma,i-1}^{+}|-|\Phi_{\sigma,i}^{+}|},

yielding (
58
).

Next, we handle the
c
-function bound. When
|\langle\lambda(x),\alpha\rangle|
is small, by (
20
) we have

\displaystyle|c_{\alpha}(\lambda(x))|^{-2}\ll|\langle\lambda(x),\alpha\rangle|^{2}.

When
|\langle\lambda(x),\alpha\rangle|
is large, by (
21
) we instead have

\displaystyle|c_{\alpha}(\lambda(x))|^{-2}\ll|\langle\lambda(x),\alpha\rangle|^{m_{\alpha}+m_{2\alpha}}\ll(1+x_{\sigma(1)})^{m_{\alpha}+m_{2\alpha}}.

Combining these bounds, we obtain for all
x\in T_{\sigma}

\displaystyle|c(\lambda(x))|^{-2}\ll(1+x_{\sigma(1)})^{d}\prod_{\alpha\in\Phi^{+}}|\langle\lambda(x),\alpha\rangle|^{2}.

Applying the same argument used to prove (
58
) we find (
60
).

Finally, we apply a similar argument to
\Theta(tH_{0},\lambda)
, with the added observation that
\alpha(tH_{0})=0
for
\alpha\in\Phi^{+}_{M}
. These roots therefore make no contribution to the formula for
\Theta(tH_{0},\lambda)
, while for
\alpha\in\Phi^{+}\smallsetminus\Phi^{+}_{M}
we have
\alpha(tH_{0})\ll t
as before. It follows that

\Theta(tH_{0},\lambda(x))\ll\sum_{w\in W}\prod_{\alpha\in\Phi^{+}\smallsetminus\Phi^{+}_{M}}\min(t,|\langle w\lambda(x),\alpha\rangle|^{-1}+1).

As before, we partition
\Phi^{+}\smallsetminus\Phi^{+}_{M}
into the subsets
(\Phi^{+}\smallsetminus\Phi^{+}_{M})\cap w(\Phi_{\sigma,i-1}\smallsetminus\Phi_{\sigma,i})
. This gives

\displaystyle\prod_{\alpha\in\Phi^{+}\smallsetminus\Phi^{+}_{M}}\min(t,|\langle w\lambda(x),\alpha\rangle|^{-1}+1)

\displaystyle\asymp\prod_{i=1}^{r}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}\alpha\in(\Phi^{+}\smallsetminus\Phi^{+}_{M})\\
\cap w(\Phi_{\sigma,i-1}\smallsetminus\Phi_{\sigma,i})\end{subarray}}\min(t,x_{\sigma(i)}^{-1}+1)

\displaystyle=\prod_{i=1}^{r}\min(t,x_{\sigma(i)}^{-1}+1)^{n_{M}(\sigma,i,w)},

yielding (
59
).
∎

8.2.
Singular refinement and corresponding estimates

We now refine the estimates in Lemma
8.2
according to how many of the ordered coordinates
x_{\sigma(i)}
exceed
t^{-1}
. We partition each barycentric chamber
T_{\sigma}
into the following disjoint subregions:

T_{\sigma,l}=\begin{cases}\{x\in T_{\sigma}:t^{-1}>x_{\sigma(1)}\},&l=0;\\
\{x\in T_{\sigma}:x_{\sigma(l)}>t^{-1}>x_{\sigma(l+1)}\},&1\leq l\leq r-1;\\
\{x\in T_{\sigma}:x_{\sigma(r)}>t^{-1}\},&l=r.\end{cases}

Note that
l
records the number of coordinates
x_{\sigma(i)}
that are greater than
t^{-1}
. Similar decompositions of
\mathfrak{a}_{+}
, rather than
\mathfrak{a}_{+}^{*}
, are used in
[
28
,
38
]
;
[
38
]
refers to these as the barycentric–semiclassical subdivision.

Figure 2.
The singular refinement of the barycentric subdivision when
r=3
and
t>3
. The subregion
T_{\sigma,0}
does not appear since it does not meet the affine simplex
\{x\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}^{3}:x_{1}+x_{2}+x_{3}=1\}
.

It will be convenient to introduce the following notation.

Definition 8.3
.

For
\sigma\in S_{r}
,
w\in W
, and
1\leq i\leq r
, let

s_{M}(\sigma,i,w)=|\Phi_{\sigma,i-1}^{+}|-|\Phi_{\sigma,i}^{+}|-2n_{M}(\sigma,i,w),

where
n_{M}(\sigma,i,w)
was defined in Lemma
8.2
. For
1\leq i\leq r+1
put

S_{M}(\sigma,i,w)=|\Phi_{\sigma,i}^{+}|-2|(\Phi^{+}\smallsetminus\Phi^{+}_{M})\cap w\Phi_{\sigma,i}|.

In particular, since
\Phi_{\sigma,r}=\emptyset
, we have
S_{M}(\sigma,r+1,w)=0
, and since
\Phi_{\sigma,0}=\Phi
we have
S_{M}(\sigma,1,w)=|\Phi^{+}|-2|(\Phi^{+}\smallsetminus\Phi^{+}_{M})|=|\Phi^{+}|-2(|\Phi^{+}|-|\Phi^{+}_{M}|)=-|\Phi^{+}|+2|\Phi^{+}_{M}|
.

Lemma 8.4
.

Let
\sigma\in S_{r}
,
w\in W
, and
1\leq l\leq r+1
. There exists an
N^{\prime}
such that for
\lambda(x)\in T_{\sigma,l}
and
\|H\|\ll t
we have

\Theta(tH_{0},\lambda(x))^{2}\Theta(H,\lambda(x))|c(\lambda(x))|^{-2}\ll(1+x_{\sigma(1)})^{N^{\prime}}\Big(\sum_{w\in W}t^{-S_{M}(\sigma,l,w)}\prod_{i=1}^{l}x_{\sigma(i)}^{s_{M}(\sigma,i,w)}\Big),

where here, and throughout, a product from
i=1
to
l
is assigned the value 1 when
l=0
.

Proof.

On
T_{\sigma,l}
we have
x_{\sigma(i)}>t^{-1}
for
i\leq l
and
x_{\sigma(i)}<t^{-1}
for
i\geq l+1
, and so

\displaystyle\min(t,x_{\sigma(i)}^{-1}+1)

\displaystyle\asymp x_{\sigma(i)}^{-1}+1=x_{\sigma(i)}^{-1}(1+x_{\sigma(i)}),\quad i\leq l,

\displaystyle\min(t,x_{\sigma(i)}^{-1}+1)

\displaystyle=t\leq t(1+x_{\sigma(i)}),\quad i\geq l+1.

As a result, we deduce from the bound (
58
) for
\Theta(H,\lambda)
that

\Theta(H,\lambda(x))\ll(1+x_{\sigma(1)})^{|\Phi^{+}|}t^{|\Phi_{\sigma,l}^{+}|}\prod_{i=1}^{l}x_{\sigma(i)}^{-|\Phi_{\sigma,i-1}^{+}|+|\Phi_{\sigma,i}^{+}|}.

Likewise, the bound (
59
) for
\Theta(tH_{0},\lambda)
becomes

\Theta(tH_{0},\lambda(x))\ll(1+x_{\sigma(1)})^{|\Phi^{+}\smallsetminus\Phi^{+}_{M}|}\sum_{w\in W}t^{|(\Phi^{+}\smallsetminus\Phi^{+}_{M})\cap w\Phi_{\sigma,l}|}\prod_{i=1}^{l}x_{\sigma(i)}^{-n_{M}(\sigma,i,w)}.

We simplify the bound (
60
) for
|c(\lambda(x))|^{-2}
to

|c(\lambda(x))|^{-2}\ll(1+x_{\sigma(1)})^{d}t^{-2|\Phi_{\sigma,l}^{+}|}\prod_{i=1}^{l}x_{\sigma(i)}^{2|\Phi_{\sigma,i-1}^{+}|-2|\Phi_{\sigma,i}^{+}|}.

Combining these estimates yields the lemma.∎

For
1\leq l\leq r
, we put

(61)

I_{M,\sigma,l,w}(t)=\int_{x_{\sigma(1)}>\cdots>x_{\sigma(l)}>t^{-1}}(1+x_{\sigma(1)})^{-N}\prod_{i=1}^{l}x_{\sigma(i)}^{s_{M}(\sigma,i,w)}dx_{\sigma(1)}\ldots dx_{\sigma(l)}

and set
I_{M,\sigma,0,w}(t)=1
.

Corollary 8.5
.

In the above notation, and under the conditions of Lemma
8.4
, we have, for
N^{\prime\prime}
much larger than
N^{\prime}
,

\int_{T_{\sigma,l}}(1+x_{\sigma(1)})^{-N^{\prime\prime}}\Theta(tH_{0},\lambda(x))^{2}\Theta(H,\lambda(x))|c(\lambda(x))|^{-2}dx\ll\sum_{w\in W}t^{-S_{M}(\sigma,l,w)+l-r}I_{M,\sigma,l,w}(t).

Proof.

From Lemma
8.4
it follows that the left-hand side is bounded by

\sum_{w\in W}t^{-S_{M}(\sigma,l,w)}\int_{T_{\sigma,l}}(1+x_{\sigma(1)})^{-N}\prod_{i=1}^{l}x_{\sigma(i)}^{s_{M}(\sigma,i,w)}dx_{\sigma(1)}\ldots dx_{\sigma(r)}.

When
l=r
this gives the stated bound. For
0\leq l\leq r-1
, we perform the integrals over the variables which don’t appear in the product, to find

\int_{0}^{t^{-1}}\int_{0}^{x_{\sigma(l+1)}}\cdots\int_{0}^{x_{\sigma(r)}}dx_{\sigma(l+1)}\ldots dx_{\sigma(r)}\leq\int_{0}^{t^{-1}}\cdots\int_{0}^{t^{-1}}dx_{\sigma(l+1)}\ldots dx_{\sigma(r)}=t^{l-r},

as desired.
∎

8.3.
Intersection cardinalities of root systems

Before proceeding to estimate the integrals appearing in Corollary
8.5
, we establish a few important properties of the quantities
s_{M}(\sigma,i,w)
and
S_{M}(\sigma,i,w)
appearing in Definition
8.3
.

Lemma 8.6
.

Let
\sigma\in S_{r}
,
w\in W
, and
\Phi_{M}
a standard sub-root system. Then

(1)

for
1\leq i\leq r
we have
S_{M}(\sigma,i,w)+s_{M}(\sigma,i,w)=S_{M}(\sigma,i-1,w)
;

(2)

for
0\leq i\leq r
we have
S_{M}(\sigma,i,w)=|\Phi_{M}\cap w\Phi_{\sigma,i}|-\frac{1}{2}|\Phi_{\sigma,i}|
.

Proof.

For point (
1
) we have

\displaystyle S_{M}(\sigma,i-1,w)-S_{M}(\sigma,i,w)

\displaystyle=|\Phi_{\sigma,i-1}^{+}|-2|(\Phi^{+}\smallsetminus\Phi^{+}_{M})\cap w\Phi_{\sigma,i-1}|

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\;-(|\Phi_{\sigma,i}^{+}|-2|(\Phi^{+}\smallsetminus\Phi^{+}_{M})\cap w\Phi_{\sigma,i}|)

\displaystyle=|\Phi_{\sigma,i-1}^{+}|-|\Phi_{\sigma,i}^{+}|+2|(\Phi^{+}\smallsetminus\Phi^{+}_{M})\cap w(\Phi_{\sigma,i-1}\smallsetminus\Phi_{\sigma,i})|

\displaystyle=s_{M}(\sigma,i,w).

For point (
2
) we calculate

\displaystyle S_{M}(\sigma,i,w)

\displaystyle=|\Phi_{\sigma,i}^{+}|-2|(\Phi^{+}\smallsetminus\Phi^{+}_{M})\cap w\Phi_{\sigma,i}^{+}|

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}|\Phi_{\sigma,i}|-|(\Phi\smallsetminus\Phi_{M})\cap w\Phi_{\sigma,i}|

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}|\Phi_{\sigma,i}|-(|w\Phi_{\sigma,i}|-|\Phi_{M}\cap w\Phi_{\sigma,i}|),

which simplifies to the stated identity.∎

8.4.
Integrating over the refined subdivision

By contrast with the preceding steps, in which
H_{0}
, and hence
\Phi_{M}
, was allowed to be arbitrary, the remainder of the argument will rely critically on the semi-dense hypothesis on
\Phi_{M}
present in the statement of Theorem
6.4
.

Corollary 8.7
.

Let
\Phi_{M}
be a semi-dense root subsystem. Fix
\sigma\in S_{r}
,
w\in W
, and
0\leq i\leq r
. Then
S_{M}(\sigma,i,w)+r\geq i
.

Proof.

We apply Lemma
8.6
(
2
), and the defining inequality (
54
) for semi-dense root subsystems, with
\Phi_{0}=\Phi_{M}
and
\Psi=w\Phi_{\sigma,i}
.
∎

Recall the integral
I_{M,\sigma,l,w}(t)
defined in (
61
). When combined with Corollary
8.5
, the following lemma completes the proof of Theorem
6.4
.

Lemma 8.8
.

Let
\Phi_{M}
be a semi-dense root subsystem. Fix
\sigma\in S_{r}
,
w\in W
. For
1\leq l\leq r
we let

e_{M}(\sigma,l,w)=\#\{1\leq i\leq l:S_{M}(\sigma,i,w)+r=i\}.

and put
e_{M}(\sigma,0,w)=0
. Then

t^{-S_{M}(\sigma,l,w)+l-r}I_{M,\sigma,l,w}(t)\ll(\log t)^{e_{M}(\sigma,l,w)},

the implied constant depending only on the integer
N
appearing in the definition of
I_{M,\sigma,l,w}(t)
.

Proof.

For notational simplicity, we drop the dependence on
w
,
\sigma
, and
M
in the notation
I_{M,\sigma,l,w}(t)
,
S_{M}(\sigma,l,w)
and
s_{M}(\sigma,i,w)
, writing simply
I_{l}(t)
,
S(l)
and
s(i)
for these quantities. In particular,
S(r)=0
.

For
l=0
, recall from the discussion preceding Corollary
8.5
that, by convention,
I_{0}(t)=1
. We must therefore show that
S(0)+r\geq 0
, which follows from Corollary
8.7
.

For
1\leq l\leq r
we let

J_{l}(t)=\int_{x_{\sigma(1)}>\cdots>x_{\sigma(l)}>t^{-1}}(1+x_{\sigma(1)})^{-N}x_{\sigma(l)}^{S(l)+r-l}\prod_{i=1}^{l}x_{\sigma(i)}^{s(i)}dx_{\sigma(1)}\ldots dx_{\sigma(l-1)}.

As was the case for
I_{M,\sigma,l,w}(t)
, we do not indicate the dependence on
N
in the notation, and we shall allow
N
to be taken sufficiently large. From the inequality
(t^{-1})^{S(l)+r-l}<x_{\sigma(l-1)}^{S(l)+r-l}
, it follows that
t^{-S(l)+l-r}I_{l}(t)<J_{l}(t)
. We shall bound
J_{l}(t)
by induction.

The base case is when
l=1
, which we handle first. Since
N
can be taken sufficiently large, and
S(1)+s(1)=S(0)
from Lemma
8.6
(
1
), we have

J_{2}(t)=\int_{t^{-1}}^{\infty}(1+x_{\sigma(1)})^{-N}x_{\sigma(1)}^{S(1)+s(1)+r-1}dx_{\sigma(1)}\ll\int_{t^{-1}}^{1}x_{\sigma(1)}^{S(1)+s(1)+r-1}dx_{\sigma(1)}=\int_{t^{-1}}^{1}x_{\sigma(1)}^{S(0)+r-1}dx_{\sigma(1)}.

From Corollary
8.7
the exponent satisfies
S(0)+r-1\geq-1
. If this exponent is
\geq 0
then we may extend the lower limit of integration to
0
and obtain a bound of
1
. When the exponent is
-1
, the integral is bounded by
\log t
. This establishes the lemma when
l=1
.

We now assume that
2\leq l\leq r
and aim to show that

(62)

J_{l}(t)\ll(\log t)^{\delta_{l}}J_{l-1}(t),\quad\textrm{where}\quad\delta_{l}=\begin{cases}0,&S(l-1)+r>l-1;\\
1,&S(l-1)+r=l-1.\end{cases}

For this we consider the inner integral over
x_{\sigma(l)}
in
J_{l}(t)
. Using the recursive identity
S(l)+s(l)=S(l-1)
from Lemma
8.6
(
1
) we have

\int_{t^{-1}}^{x_{\sigma(l-1)}}x_{\sigma(l)}^{S(l)+r-l+s(l)}dx_{\sigma(l)}=\int_{t^{-1}}^{x_{\sigma(l-1)}}x_{\sigma(l)}^{S(l-1)+r-l}dx_{\sigma(l-1)}.

From Corollary
8.7
the exponent satisfies
S(l-1)+r-l\geq-1
, with equality occurring precisely when
\delta_{l}=1
. If
\delta_{l}=0
, then this exponent is
\geq 0
and we may extend the lower limit of integration to
0
and obtain a bound of
x_{\sigma(l-1)}^{S(l-1)+r-(l-1)}
, yielding (
62
). If
\delta_{l}=1
, then the inner integration over
x_{\sigma(l-1)}
now gives

\int_{t^{-1}}^{x_{\sigma(l-1)}}x_{\sigma(l)}^{-1}dx_{\sigma(l)}=\log x_{\sigma(l-1)}-\log(t^{-1})\ll(1+x_{\sigma(1)})+\log t\ll(\log t)(1+x_{\sigma(1)}).

The
\log t
term may be brought to the outside of the integral, and the
1+x_{\sigma(1)}
may be absorbed, yielding

J_{l}(t)\ll(\log t)\int_{x_{\sigma(1)}>\cdots>x_{\sigma(l-1)}>t^{-1}}(1+x_{\sigma(1)})^{-N}\prod_{i=1}^{l-1}x_{\sigma(i)}^{s(i)}dx_{\sigma(1)}\ldots dx_{\sigma(l-1)}.

Since, in the definition of
J_{l-1}
, the power of
x_{\sigma(l-1)}
in front of the product
\prod_{i=1}^{l-1}x_{\sigma(i)}^{s(i)}
is
S(l-1)+r-(l-1)=0
when
\delta_{l}=1
, the integral above is
(\log t)J_{l-1}(t)
, establishing (
62
) in this case as well.
∎

9.
Bounds for spherical functions

This section contains the proof of Theorems
2.4
and
2.5
. Section
9.1
provides some illustration of these theorems, and explains why Theorem
2.5
should be sharp, and Section
9.2
discusses the relation between these theorems and previous work. Section
9.3
gives an outline of the proof of Theorem
2.4
, and the proof is carried out in Sections
9.4
to
9.6
. We prove Theorem
2.5
in Section
9.7
.

9.1.
Illustration of Theorems
2.4
and
2.5

In this section, we make some remarks to help the reader understand the significance of Theorems
2.4
and
2.5
. This includes explicating these theorems in the case of
{\rm SL}_{2}(\mathbb{C})
, deriving some simpler consequences of Theorem
2.4
for a general group, and explaining why Theorem
2.5
should be sharp based on stationary phase considerations. We also note that the bounds of Theorems
2.4
and
2.5
are equivalent in the case when
G
is a complex group and
\lambda\in\mathfrak{a}^{*}
is bounded, by Lemma
9.5
below.

9.1.1.
The case of
{\rm SL}_{2}(\mathbb{C})

When
G={\rm SL}_{2}(\mathbb{C})
, we have the following explicit formula for the spherical function. If
\alpha
is the unique element of
\Phi^{+}
, we may identify
\mathfrak{a}^{*}_{\mathbb{C}}
with
\mathbb{C}
and
\mathfrak{a}
with
\mathbb{R}
using the basis elements
\alpha
and
\alpha^{\vee}/2
respectively. We then have

(63)

\varphi_{\lambda}(e^{t})=\frac{\sin(\lambda t)}{\lambda\sinh t},

and we may compare Theorems
2.4
and
2.5
with the bounds that may be deduced from this formula. For simplicity, we shall assume that
\lambda
is real, and we assume without loss of generality that
\lambda
and
t
are non-negative. In this case, Theorem
2.4
states that

(64)

\varphi_{\lambda}(e^{t})\ll(1+\lambda)^{a}\min(t+1,\lambda^{-1}+1)e^{-t},

and Theorem
2.5
states

(65)

\varphi_{\lambda}(e^{t})\ll e^{-t}\frac{t+1}{t\lambda+1}.

We note that these bounds are of the same strength if
\lambda
is bounded, as established in Lemma
9.5
, and it may be checked that (
65
) is stronger than (
64
) as expected.

We shall show that (
65
) is sharp with complete uniformity in
t
and
\lambda
. To do this, we consider the cases
t\lambda<1
and
t\lambda\geq 1
separately. When
t\lambda<1
we have
\sin(t\lambda)\asymp t\lambda
, so that (
63
) implies that
\varphi_{\lambda}(e^{t})\asymp t/\sinh t
. On the other hand, (
65
) gives

\varphi_{\lambda}(e^{t})\ll(t+1)e^{-t}\asymp t/\sinh t,

which shows that (
65
) is optimal in this range.

When
t\lambda\geq 1
the best bound one can give for
\sin(\lambda t)
is
|\sin(\lambda t)|\leq 1
, and so by (
63
) the best bound one can give for
\varphi_{\lambda}(e^{t})
is
\varphi_{\lambda}(e^{t})\ll 1/(\lambda\sinh t)
. In comparison, (
65
) becomes

\varphi_{\lambda}(e^{t})\ll e^{-t}\frac{t+1}{t\lambda},

and this is equivalent to
\varphi_{\lambda}(e^{t})\ll 1/(\lambda\sinh t)
as one sees by applying the asymptotic
1/\sinh t\asymp e^{-t}(t+1)/t
.

The bound (
65
) reflects the fact that the decay of
\varphi_{0}(e^{t})=t/\sinh(t)\asymp te^{-t}
is slower than that of
\varphi_{\lambda}
for
\lambda\neq 0
, and moreover that this transition starts to be observed at the point
e^{t}
when
\lambda\asymp 1/t
. Theorem
2.5
can be viewed as an extension of this bound to a general group, where the function
(t+1)/(t\lambda+1)
is replaced by a product of similar expressions over
\Phi_{\textnormal{red}}^{+}
.

9.1.2.
Consequences on a general group

We next derive some simpler corollaries of Theorem
2.4
, both because of their intrinsic interest, and to facilitate the comparison between Theorem
2.4
and previous bounds for the spherical function in the next section. First, we make the observation that when
\lambda
is at distance
\gg 1
from the singular set, Theorem
2.4
simply gives

(66)

\varphi_{\lambda}(e^{H})\ll(1+\|\lambda\|)^{a}\underset{w\in W}{\max}\,e^{-(\rho+w\Im\lambda)(H)}.

Recall the definition of
f_{\alpha}(H,\lambda)
in (
4
). For general
\lambda
, we may apply the bound
f_{\alpha}(H,\lambda)\leq|\alpha(H)|+1
to deduce

(67)

\varphi_{\lambda}(e^{H})\ll(1+\|\lambda\|)^{a}\underset{w\in W}{\max}\,e^{-(\rho+w\Im\lambda)(H)}\prod_{\alpha\in\Phi_{\textnormal{red}}^{+}}(|\alpha(H)|+1).

Theorem
2.4
may be viewed as giving an interpolation between these two bounds, based on how singular
\lambda
is.

Finally, we have the following.

Corollary 9.1
.

Let
\eta
denote the maximum size of the sets
\Phi^{+}_{\textnormal{red},M}
, where
M
runs over the maximal standard Levi subgroups of
G
. We then have

\varphi_{\lambda}(e^{H})\ll(1+\|\lambda\|)^{a}(1+\|H\|)^{\eta}\min(\|H\|+1,\|\lambda\|^{-1}+1)^{|\Phi_{\textnormal{red}}^{+}|-\eta}\underset{w\in W}{\max}\,e^{-(\rho+w\Im\lambda)(H)}

Proof.

We first apply the bound
\alpha(H)\ll\|H\|
to obtain

f_{\alpha}(H,w\lambda)\ll\min(\|H\|+1,|\langle w\lambda,\alpha\rangle|^{-1}+1),

which gives

\Theta(H,\lambda)\ll\prod_{\alpha\in\Phi_{\textnormal{red}}^{+}}\min(\|H\|+1,|\langle\lambda,\alpha\rangle|^{-1}+1)

and therefore

\varphi_{\lambda}(e^{H})\ll(1+\|\lambda\|)^{a}\underset{w\in W}{\max}\,e^{-(\rho+w\Im\lambda)(H)}\prod_{\alpha\in\Phi_{\textnormal{red}}^{+}}\min(\|H\|+1,|\langle\lambda,\alpha\rangle|^{-1}+1).

It will therefore be enough to show that

\prod_{\alpha\in\Phi_{\textnormal{red}}^{+}}\min(\|H\|+1,|\langle\lambda,\alpha\rangle|^{-1}+1)\ll(1+\|H\|)^{\eta}\min(\|H\|+1,\|\lambda\|^{-1}+1)^{|\Phi_{\textnormal{red}}^{+}|-\eta}

for any
\lambda\in\mathfrak{a}^{*}_{\mathbb{C}}
. We are free to multiply
\lambda
by
i
, and so we may assume that
\|\Re\lambda\|\gg\|\lambda\|
. We may also assume that
\Re\lambda\in\overline{\mathfrak{a}}^{*}_{+}
. It follows that there is some maximal standard Levi subgroup
M
such that
|\langle\lambda,\alpha\rangle|\gg\|\lambda\|
for all
\alpha\in\Phi_{\textnormal{red}}^{+}\smallsetminus\Phi^{+}_{\textnormal{red},M}
, and therefore that

\min(\|H\|+1,|\langle\lambda,\alpha\rangle|^{-1}+1)\ll\min(\|H\|+1,\|\lambda\|^{-1}+1)

for
\alpha\in\Phi_{\textnormal{red}}^{+}\smallsetminus\Phi^{+}_{\textnormal{red},M}
. This implies

\displaystyle\prod_{\alpha\in\Phi_{\textnormal{red}}^{+}}\min(\|H\|+1,|\langle\lambda,\alpha\rangle|^{-1}+1)

\displaystyle\ll\prod_{\alpha\in\Phi^{+}_{\textnormal{red},M}}(\|H\|+1)\prod_{\alpha\in\Phi_{\textnormal{red}}^{+}\smallsetminus\Phi^{+}_{\textnormal{red},M}}\min(\|H\|+1,\|\lambda\|^{-1}+1)

\displaystyle=(1+\|H\|)^{|\Phi^{+}_{\textnormal{red},M}|}\min(\|H\|+1,\|\lambda\|^{-1}+1)^{|\Phi_{\textnormal{red}}^{+}\smallsetminus\Phi^{+}_{\textnormal{red},M}|}

\displaystyle\leq(1+\|H\|)^{\eta}\min(\|H\|+1,\|\lambda\|^{-1}+1)^{|\Phi_{\textnormal{red}}^{+}|-\eta}

as required.
∎

9.1.3.
Theorem
2.5
and stationary phase

We now explain why the bound of Theorem
2.5
is to be expected, and should be sharp, from the viewpoint of stationary phase applied to the integral (
6
). The critical set of the phase function appearing in this integral is understood, see
[
16
, §1]
. In particular, when
Z=H\in\mathfrak{a}
, the integral in (
6
) reduces to one over
K/Z_{K}(\mathfrak{a})
, and
[
16
]
shows that when
H
and
\lambda
are both regular the critical points occur at
W
and are non-degenerate. Moreover, for each
w\in W
, there is a fixed basis for the tangent space
T_{w}(K/Z_{K}(\mathfrak{a}))
in which the Hessian is diagonal with eigenvalues
\alpha(H)\langle\lambda,w\alpha\rangle
, each occurring with multiplicity 2
[
16
, Prop. 1.4]
. (The fact that the multiplicities are 2 is due to
G
being complex.) By stationary phase, the strongest bound one can give for the contribution of the integral near
w
to (
6
) is therefore
(1+|\alpha(H)\langle\lambda,w\alpha\rangle|)^{-1}
, and summing these over
W
gives the bound of Theorem
2.5
.

9.2.
Relation with previous work

We now discuss the relation between Theorem
2.4
and existing bounds for the spherical function. Compared with previous results, Theorem
2.4
has strong dependence on
H
, but weak dependence on
\lambda
, at least when
\lambda
is large. This is convenient for us, on both counts. It is crucial for the applications in this paper that our bounds have good dependence on
H
. On the other hand, the fact that the bound of Theorem
2.4
is polynomially growing in
\lambda
does not matter for us, as in our applications there are always other factors present that decay rapidly in
\lambda
.

On a general group, the problem of giving bounds for the spherical function that are uniform in
H
and
\lambda
is a difficult one, and far from being completely solved. If we restrict our attention to bounds that hold for all
H
and
\lambda
, with
\lambda
varying over
\mathfrak{a}^{*}
or
\mathfrak{a}^{*}_{\mathbb{C}}
, the only previous results we are aware of are obtained by reducing directly to the case of
\lambda=0
, and as a result do not capture most of the interaction between
H
and
\lambda
.

To describe these bounds, it follows from Kostant’s convexity theorem that

|\varphi_{\lambda}(e^{H})|\leq\varphi_{0}(e^{H})\,\underset{w\in W}{\max}\,e^{-w\Im\lambda(H)}

for any
\lambda\in\mathfrak{a}^{*}_{\mathbb{C}}
, see for instance
[
17
, Prop 4.6.1]
. Combined with the Harish-Chandra bound for
\varphi_{0}

[
17
, Theorem 4.6.4]
, which states that

(68)

\varphi_{0}(e^{H})\ll(1+\|H\|)^{|\Phi_{\textnormal{red}}^{+}|}e^{-\rho(H)},

we obtain

(69)

\varphi_{\lambda}(e^{H})\ll(1+\|H\|)^{|\Phi_{\textnormal{red}}^{+}|}\underset{w\in W}{\max}\,e^{-(\rho+w\Im\lambda)(H)}.

The bound (
68
) was strengthened by Anker
[
5
]
to

(70)

\varphi_{0}(e^{H})\asymp\prod_{\alpha\in\Phi_{\textnormal{red}}^{+}}(1+|\alpha(H)|)e^{-\rho(H)},

which gives the corresponding improvement in (
69
) to

(71)

\varphi_{\lambda}(e^{H})\ll\prod_{\alpha\in\Phi_{\textnormal{red}}^{+}}(1+|\alpha(H)|)\underset{w\in W}{\max}\,e^{-(\rho+w\Im\lambda)(H)}.

We note that the upper bound for
\varphi_{0}
provided by Theorem
2.4
is the same as the upper bound in (
70
), so that the theorem is sharp in this case. Moreover, (
71
) is equivalent to the corollary (
67
) of Theorem
2.4
, if we restrict
\lambda
to a bounded set in

(72)

\mathfrak{a}^{*}(\kappa)=\{\lambda\in\mathfrak{a}^{*}_{\mathbb{C}}:\|\Im\lambda\|<\kappa\},

the tube of radius
\kappa>0
about
\mathfrak{a}^{*}\subset\mathfrak{a}_{\mathbb{C}}^{*}
.

The paper
[
29
]
proves an asymptotic for
\varphi_{\lambda}
similar to (
70
) for any
\lambda
. However, the use of the Harish-Chandra expansion in their proof means that their result only holds for
H
in a set of the form
H_{0}+\mathfrak{a}_{+}
for a fixed
H_{0}\in\mathfrak{a}_{+}
. Moreover, their error terms are not uniform in
\lambda
. We have not checked how this asymptotic compares with Theorem
2.4
. We also mention Cor. 4.5.5 of
[
17
]
, which implies a uniform estimate for
\varphi_{\lambda}(e^{H})
when
H\in H_{0}+\mathfrak{a}_{+}
and
\lambda\in\lambda_{0}+\mathfrak{a}^{*}_{+}
for fixed
\lambda_{0}\in\mathfrak{a}^{*}_{+}
.

Finally, we assume that
G
is complex, and discuss the strength of Theorem
2.5
, as well as its relation to existing bounds for the spherical function in this case.

Bounds for
\varphi_{\lambda}
and
\varphi_{\lambda}^{E}
in the complex case were studied by Barlet–Clerc
[
6
]
, Clerc
[
12
]
, and Cowling–Nevo
[
14
]
, and may be deduced from the results of Duistermaat–Kolk–Varadarajan
[
16
]
. Theorem
2.5
strengthens these results, subject to the condition that
\lambda
is real. However, we note that the papers
[
6
,
12
,
14
]
also obtain results for complex
\lambda
, and
[
14
]
also bounds certain derivatives of
\varphi_{\lambda}(e^{H})
in the
H
-variable.

9.3.
Outline of proof of Theorem
2.4

We now give an outline of the proof of Theorem
2.4
. We first discuss the case when
G=\textnormal{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
, before describing the modifications required in the general case. As in the case of
{\rm SL}_{2}(\mathbb{C})
discussed above, we shall identify
\mathfrak{a}
and
\mathfrak{a}^{*}_{\mathbb{C}}
with
\mathbb{R}
and
\mathbb{C}
using the basis elements
\alpha^{\vee}/2
and
\alpha
respectively, where
\alpha\in\Phi^{+}
is the unique positive root. Thus we wish to show that there exist
a,\kappa>0
such that

(73)

\displaystyle|\varphi_{\lambda}(e^{t})|\ll(1+|\lambda|)^{a}e^{-t/2+|\Im\lambda|t}\min(1+t,1+|\lambda|^{-1})

for
t\geq 0
and
|\Im\lambda|<\kappa
.

First, we note that
|\varphi_{\lambda}(e^{t})|\leq e^{|\Im\lambda|t}\varphi_{0}(e^{t})
, which follows by taking absolute values in the Harish-Chandra integral formula and using the fact that
H(ke^{t})\leq t
for all
k\in{\rm SO}(2)
. This implies (
73
) in the range where
t<1
(or
t<R
for any fixed
R
).

Now suppose
t\geq 1
. Let
\theta(t,\lambda)
be defined as

(74)

\displaystyle\theta(t,\lambda):=c(\lambda)e^{i\lambda t}+c(-\lambda)e^{-i\lambda t}.

The asymptotic formula of Gangolli–Varadarajan, recalled in Proposition
3.2
, states that there is
\kappa>0
such that

(75)

|\varphi_{\lambda}(e^{t})-e^{-t/2}\theta(t,\lambda)|\ll(1+t)^{a}(1+|\lambda|)^{a}e^{-(1/2+\varepsilon)t}

for
|\Im\lambda|<\kappa
, where
a,\varepsilon>0
are constants (see
[
17
, §5.1]
for an illustrative discussion of the rank one case). We may take these to be the constants
a
and
\kappa
in our bound (
73
). As the right hand side of (
75
) is less than the right hand side of (
73
), it suffices to show that

(76)

\displaystyle|\theta(t,\lambda)|\ll(1+|\lambda|)^{a}e^{|\Im\lambda|t}\min(1+t,1+|\lambda|^{-1}),

for
t\geq 1
and
|\Im\lambda|<\kappa
. In fact, we will show the stronger bound

(77)

|\theta(t,\lambda)|\ll e^{|\Im\lambda|t}\min(1+t,1+|\lambda|^{-1}).

In the case of
{\rm SL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
, the explicit nature of
\theta(t,\lambda)
means that this can be exhibited quite directly, and we do this first before describing the argument that must be used in the case of a general group. We first suppose that
|\lambda|\geq 1
. If we assume that
\kappa<1
, we have the bound
|c(\lambda)|\ll_{\kappa}1
for
|\Im\lambda|<\kappa
. We thus straight away get

\displaystyle|\theta(t,\lambda)|\ll e^{|\Im\lambda|t}\ll e^{|\Im\lambda|t}\min(1+|\lambda|^{-1},1+t),

as required.

We next suppose that
|\lambda|<1
. The
c
-function is a meromorphic function with a simple pole at
\lambda=0
. Despite this singularity,
\theta(t,\lambda)
can be extended holomorphically to
\lambda=0
. If we write
c(\lambda)=a_{-1}\lambda^{-1}+g(\lambda)
where
g(\lambda)
is an entire function, we have

\displaystyle c(\lambda)e^{i\lambda t}+c(-\lambda)e^{-i\lambda t}=a_{-1}\frac{e^{i\lambda t}-e^{-i\lambda t}}{\lambda}+g(\lambda)e^{i\lambda t}+g(-\lambda)e^{-i\lambda t}.

Because
|\lambda|<1
, the last two terms above can be bounded by
Ce^{|\Im\lambda|t}
for some
C
. The first term can be bounded in two different ways, as

\Big|\frac{e^{i\lambda t}-e^{-i\lambda t}}{\lambda}\Big|\leq 2|\lambda|^{-1}e^{|\Im\lambda|t}\quad\text{and}\quad\Big|\frac{e^{i\lambda t}-e^{-i\lambda t}}{\lambda}\Big|\leq 2te^{|\Im\lambda|t},

where the first bound is elementary and the second follows from applying the mean value theorem to
f(x)=e^{i\lambda x}-e^{-i\lambda x}
on the interval
[0,t]
. We thus get the bound

\displaystyle|\theta(t,\lambda)|\ll e^{|\Im\lambda|t}\min(1+|\lambda|^{-1},1+t),

as required. This completes the proof of Theorem
2.4
in this case.

For later purposes, it will be convenient to use a different method, based on the maximum modulus principle, to establish (
77
). To describe it, we first observe that if
\kappa<1
, we have

c(\lambda)\ll_{\kappa}1+|\lambda|^{-1}

when
|\Im\lambda|<\kappa
, as follows from (
21
). This implies that

(78)

\theta(t,\lambda)\ll(1+|\lambda|^{-1})e^{|\Im\lambda|t},

which establishes (
77
) when
|\lambda|\geq t^{-1}
. We therefore assume that
|\lambda|<t^{-1}
. For these
\lambda
, we shall prove (
77
) by applying the maximum modulus principle, on the disk of radius
2t^{-1}
centered at
\lambda
. This gives

(79)

\theta(t,\lambda)\leq\max_{\alpha}\theta(t,\lambda+2t^{-1}e^{i\alpha}).

As discussed above, we are free to assume that
t\geq R
for any fixed
R
, and we choose
R
large enough that
3R^{-1}<\kappa
. This implies that the contour
\lambda+2t^{-1}e^{i\alpha}
is contained in the strip
|\Im z|<\kappa
, and lies outside the disk
|z|<t^{-1}
. We may therefore combine (
78
) and (
79
) to obtain

\displaystyle\theta(t,\lambda)

\displaystyle\ll\max_{\alpha}(1+|\lambda+2t^{-1}e^{i\alpha}|^{-1})e^{|\Im(\lambda+2t^{-1}e^{i\alpha})|t}

\displaystyle\leq 2e^{|\Im\lambda|t}\max_{\alpha}(1+|\lambda+2t^{-1}e^{i\alpha}|^{-1}),

and moreover, because
|\lambda+2t^{-1}e^{i\alpha}|\geq t^{-1}
, this gives

\theta(t,\lambda)\ll(1+t)e^{|\Im\lambda|t}

as required.

Lemma 9.2
.
