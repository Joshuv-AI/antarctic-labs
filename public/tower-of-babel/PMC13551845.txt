Current-following behaviour during maiden foraging trips predicts survival in naive marine mammals
Authors: Dahlia Foo, Clive R McMahon, Mark A Hindell, Michael Fedak, Martin Biuw, Bernie McConnell, Ben Raymond
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIdentifying the determinants of juvenile survival is crucial for understanding the population ecology of long-lived species, where parental guidance can influence naive juvenile survival rates. In southern elephant seals (Mirounga leonina), offspring receive no foraging site knowledge from their parents, only fat reserves. We focus on how movement patterns during the pups’ maiden foraging trip influence their survival at Macquarie Island. We tracked weaned pups with satellite tags during their post-weaning migration and compared their movements to the post-moult winter migrations of adult females. We found that pups travelled predominantly in an easterly to south-easterly direction, in line with the Antarctic Circumpolar Current. The Antarctic Circumpolar Current may provide a passive survival advantage for naive pups, with current-following predicting survival probability. We propose an “accessibility area” concept in which currents expand the foraging area accessible to energy-limited pups, providing a theoretical framework that may apply broadly to marine species with naive dispersing juveniles. Background

Explaining why young animals die is crucial for studying how populations change over time and the survival of long-lived and slow-reproducing species [1, 2]. One of the key factors influencing juvenile survival is their ability to forage effectively [3, 4]. Young animals, especially in their first year, face numerous challenges in securing food, often due to their physical limitations and inexperience [5, 6]. This vulnerability is evident in the high mortality rates observed among both juvenile birds [3, 7] and mammals [6, 8].

The survival of offspring in many mammal species is often dependent on the guidance and knowledge transfer from adult females or mothers to their young [9, 10]. The extent to which this knowledge is transferred can vary among different species. On one end, in species such as elephants [11], chimpanzees [12], and dolphins [13], social development and the transfer of knowledge from parents to offspring is particularly important. Somewhere in the middle, in seal species such as harbour [14], bearded [15] and Weddell seals [16], there is some maternal supervision of their offspring at-sea, where pups may benefit by observing female foraging locations and preferred prey. At the other extreme, in southern elephant seals (Mirounga leonina), juveniles become independent without maternal knowledge transfer, relying instead on inherited behavioural traits or experience gained in early life.

In southern elephant seals, apart from maternal investment through lactation (during which pups never enter the water), there is no guidance or knowledge transfer from mothers to their pups. Once mothers leave their pups at 24 days of age on average, their pups are completely naive concerning foraging behaviour and areas [17]. Adult and juvenile elephant seals forage in different areas [18, 19], raising questions about the transition from innate to learned behaviours over time. However, the mechanisms that guide these inexperienced pups to their feeding grounds are not well understood. Current theories speculate a mix of innate navigational capabilities, including using environmental cues such as geomagnetic fields and ocean currents [20], and learnt foraging behaviours from conspecifics [21, 22]. This makes elephant seal pups an excellent model for investigating the balance between innate traits and learnt skills in determining an individual’s fitness, especially when there is no direct transfer of knowledge of feeding grounds and foraging behaviour from parent to offspring.

Southern elephant seals breed on sub-Antarctic islands and have a circumpolar distribution [23]. Weaned pups undertake their first foraging trip at sea for several months before returning to their natal island to moult and haul out, when they can be resighted [17, 24]. Southern elephant seals forage across a variety of regions within the Southern Ocean, particularly at inter-frontal zones where their prey—mesopelagic fish, squid, and crustaceans like krill [25–28]—tends to be sparsely and unevenly distributed [29]. These zones, made up of different water masses, are influenced by the Antarctic Circumpolar Current (ACC), which is the dominant eastward-flowing current in the Southern Ocean ecosystem [30, 31]. Previous studies show that weaned pups from Macquarie and the Kerguelen Archipelago predominantly disperse southeast, likely assisted by the easterly ACC [20, 32]. Pups from King George Island, while directed predominantly southwest, may also be influenced by local currents [21, 33]. Conversely, pups from Marion Island exhibit random foraging trip directions [33]. This suggests that large-scale environmental cues, like the ACC, have less impact on their early foraging migrations. Instead, these seals may be responding to smaller-scale, dynamic currents and frontal features near Marion Island [33].

Optimal foraging theory suggests that animals should adopt foraging strategies that maximise their net energy intake –- this means maximising energy gains and minimising energy costs [34]. In the case of naive southern elephant seal pups, their movement within currents may reduce the energetic cost of travel during their vulnerable early weeks. Without knowledge of where prey is located, this passive movement can potentially optimise foraging opportunities [35–37]. Because seals forage in a patchy environment, they often need to travel relatively long distances to find food [31]. Whether intentional or not, being carried by the currents can be beneficial, especially in the early stages of a juvenile’s first trip to sea when they are losing body condition [32]. Building on prior work that described directional patterns or short-term alignment with local currents, we test whether adherence to current-driven trajectories over the initial phase of the first trip predicts survival, using particle traces as a null expectation and linking movement behaviour directly to survival via capture–recapture models.

We used a unique dataset of individually marked weaned elephant seal pups tracked during their first foraging trip and information of their subsequent survival from resighting efforts to: (1) determine where the seals went on their first foraging trip and whether they followed oceanic currents, (2) compare where they went to adult females, and (3) examine the relationship between their first trip movement behaviour and survival probability. The first aim informed our understanding of how innate abilities and environmental cues guide naive pups’ foraging patterns. The second aim provided insights into the developmental process and influence of innate versus learned behaviours. Finally, the third aim correlated movement patterns and survival outcomes, providing a comprehensive view of the challenges and strategies in the early life stages.

Methods

Data collection

Sixty-nine newly weaned pups at Macquarie Island (54°30′ S, 158°57′ E) were tagged with ARGOS satellite relayed data loggers (SRDLs, Sea Mammal Research Unit, St Andrews, UK) during their post-weaning fast in December 1995, 1996, 1999, and 2000. SRDLs provided ARGOS location estimates categorised into different classes based on accuracy (~ 0.5–10 km) [38]. The pups were also flipper-tagged (Jumbo Rototag, Dalton Supplies, Henley-on-Thames, UK) and permanently marked by hot iron branding for future identification as part of a long-term demographic study [2, 39]. Researchers made resights of marked animals every 6 months (summer and winter) at Macquarie Island, with the resights dataset used in this study spanning from 1994 to 2005. Resighting continued through 2005 (24 six-monthly occasions), so survival could be assessed for several years after weaning. During moult and mid-year haulout seals are ashore for about six weeks and can be resighted reliably [2, 24, 40]. Details on how seals were captured, weighed, and handled can be found in Hindell et al. [17].

The weaned pups in this dataset were not selected at random. During the original tagging programme, pups were selected to span a range of body masses (from small to large) to capture variation in early survival linked to body condition (C.R. McMahon, pers. comm.) [40]. This non-random sampling may introduce some selection bias; however, we consider our inference about current-following and survival to be robust to this bias, because seals were not selected on the basis of movement or current-following, and we modelled weaning mass alongside adherence.

We also obtained 67 adult female post-moult winter migration tracks from Macquarie Island which were collected from February to October in 2000–2002, 2004–2005, and 2010 [41]. Of these, 30 were obtained by SRDLs (Sea Mammal Research Unit, St Andrews, UK) and the remaining 37 by archival light-logging geolocation (GLS; Mk6 or Mk9, Wildlife Computers, Redmond, USA). GLS tags are accurate to ~ 70–120 km for southern elephant seals [42, 43]. In the source study [41], at-sea positions for GLS tags were derived using the R package TripEstimation (Bayesian solar-geolocation with a speed prior) [44], and both GLS and ARGOS positions were then used as inputs to state-space models with a 6-hour time-step [45]. We obtained the resulting 6-hour location estimates and interpolated them to 1-day intervals using great circle interpolation (move R package) for consistency with pup tracks. Some tracks that did not start at the colony were removed from further analyses.

Processing pup tracks

We retained weaned pups with sufficient at-sea ARGOS data to fit state-space models. Raw at-sea locations from ARGOS were pre-processed by removing near-duplicate fixes (within 2 min) and excluding locations north of 20° S. We then fitted a correlated random walk state-space model with a maximum velocity of 4 m s− 1 using foieGras [46] (and aniMotum [47] for extraction of predicted locations), yielding estimated locations at 12-hour intervals. For analyses that required daily resolution we used one location per day (first fix per day).

Trips were identified from these state-space locations using distance to Macquarie Island. At-sea locations within 50 km of the colony were considered to be at the colony [17, 20], and consecutive sequences of locations outside this 50 km buffer were classified as individual foraging trips. Pup analyses used only each seal’s first foraging trip. For current-following and survival analyses we restricted the sample to pups whose first trip lasted at least 10 days, so that inference was based on meaningful, sustained foraging trips while balancing data quality and sample size.

Particle trace generation

Particle traces are simulated paths showing where an object would go if it drifted with ocean currents only (no swimming). We use them as a baseline. Comparing real seal tracks to these traces shows how much seals move with the current versus against or across it. To investigate the influence of ocean currents on seal movements, we generated ensemble particle traces corresponding to each seal’s first foraging trip. We obtained subsurface current data at 186 m depth from the Copernicus Global Ocean Physics Reanalysis (GLORYS12V1) for a defined regional and temporal domain (longitude 110°E–234°E, latitude 77°S–30°S, daily fields November 1995–October 2001) covering the study area and tracking period. We developed a custom particle tracking implementation based on the currently R package [48] to simulate Lagrangian particle trajectories from these data. This depth was selected because newly weaned pups at Macquarie Island make most dives in the upper 200 m of the water column [17], making subsurface currents more representative of the oceanographic conditions they experience during foraging trips. When a particle moved outside the downloaded domain or current values were missing, the trace stopped; traces could therefore extend over only part of the outbound phase.

For each seal, we generated six particle traces to capture the inherent variability in ocean currents and provide robust estimates of current-driven movement patterns. Particles were released from each seal’s starting location and date, with the first particle using the exact starting coordinates and subsequent particles using jittered starting positions within a 5 km radius to account for spatial uncertainty. Particle trajectories were simulated using Euler integration with a 24-hour time step, matching the temporal resolution of the current data. The simulation continued until the end of each seal’s outbound foraging phase (defined as the point of maximum distance from the colony) or stopped when the particle left the current-data domain. To ensure consistency and data quality, we retained up to three traces per seal that successfully covered the 10-day early-trip period. Because our adherence analysis was restricted to this initial phase, the total duration of the traces beyond 10 days did not influence the adherence metrics used for survival modelling.

Current-following behaviour analysis

We used only seals with at least one usable particle trace. Particle traces that failed due to missing current data or insufficient duration (< 10 days) were excluded from the ensemble; we retained up to the three longest successful traces per seal to ensure reliable current representation. For each seal, we calculated daily movement bearings for both the seal track and the corresponding particle traces during the outbound phase, and at each time step we averaged across available particle traces to obtain ensemble mean particle bearings. Daily adherence was the mean cosine of the angular difference between seal and particle bearings (− 180° to + 180°), ranging from − 1 (opposition to currents) to + 1 (perfect alignment). Exploratory analyses of daily adherence over trip day indicated that adherence tended to be higher early in the trip (Additional file 1, Fig. S1). Accordingly, we calculated an early-phase weighted adherence index as the time-weighted mean of daily adherence over the first 10 days of each trip (weight = 1/day index, with day 1 = 1.0, day 2 = 0.5, day 3 = 0.33, etc.), summarising how closely seal movement aligned with current-driven trajectories over the initial departure phase.

Statistical analysis

Analyses were conducted in R version 4.5.2 [49]. To test whether mean bearings differed between weaned pups and adult females, we used Watson’s two-sample test of homogeneity [50] implemented in the circular R package [51]. This analysis compared the outbound movement directions of the two groups to assess differences in dispersal patterns.

For survival analysis, we implemented Cormack-Jolly-Seber (CJS) models using the marked R package [52] to properly account for imperfect detection probability. Capture histories were constructed from 6-monthly (summer and winter) resighting occasions at Macquarie Island from 1994 to 2005 (24 occasions). CJS models estimate apparent survival probability (Φ) per 6-month interval and detection probability (p); we interpreted the estimated survival as the probability that a pup survives its first year after weaning, following the same approach as McMahon et al. [39]. Survival was modelled as a function of early-phase weighted adherence, weaning mass, or both covariates. Our candidate set comprised five models: constant survival, survival as a function of weaning mass only, of early-phase weighted adherence only, of both covariates additively, and of their interaction. Detection probability was modelled as constant across time periods; there is no evidence of differences in recapture probabilities that could affect survival estimates in this population [53–55].

McMahon et al. (1999) performed goodness-of-fit testing on this Macquarie Island dataset and found significant departures from CJS assumptions; as in that study, we present survival estimates from the CJS models because such violations do not substantially affect the estimates [39].

Model selection was performed using Akaike Information Criterion corrected for small sample sizes (AICc), where lower AICc values indicate better model fit. We used the difference in AICc (delta AICc) and Akaike weights (wAIC) to compare models, considering those with delta AICc ≤ 2 to have substantial support. Among models with delta AICc ≤ 2, we based inference on the most parsimonious (the one with the fewest parameters). Seals missing weaning mass were excluded from the analysis. We did not include sex as a covariate because it has little effect on first year survival [39] and including it would have reduced the statistical power of our dataset. Results are reported as means ± standard deviation unless stated otherwise; mean bearings are reported with their circular standard error (SE).

Results

Overview of tracking and survival data

Forty-nine pups had state-space model locations. We restricted to pups whose first trip lasted at least 10 days (45 pups); of these, one had no usable particle traces for adherence (all traces failed or were shorter than 10 days) and was excluded. The remaining 44 pups had weaning mass available and were retained for survival analysis. The mean duration of these pups’ first foraging trip was 120.3 ± 68.5 d (Table 1), and the mean outbound phase duration was 75.9 ± 40.1 d. Of these pups, 25 (56.8%) were resighted at least once after their first year. The mean weaning mass was 120.7 ± 37.9 kg. Weaned pups showed a dominant east to southeast trajectory during their first foraging trip, with an overall mean bearing of 115.8° ± 0.1° (east-southeast; SE). Adult females had a mean bearing of 139.1° ± 0.1° (south-southeast; SE) that was different compared to weaned pups (Watson’s two-sample test: test statistic = 0.314, p < 0.001; Fig. 1).

Current adherence and survival analysis

There were three equally supported survival models i.e. the models had ΔAICc ≤ 2 (Table 2). The model with the lowest AICc was the most parsimonious and included only early-phase current adherence. The other two models also included weaning mass (additively or interactively). Across these three supported models, the estimated effects of current adherence and weaning mass on apparent survival were consistently positive, indicating higher survival for pups that more closely followed the currents and those that were heavier at weaning (Fig. 2; Table 3).

Discussion

Juvenile survival is a key determinant of population trajectory, particularly in K-selected species like southern elephant seals [1], which have long lifespans and reproduce slowly with high parental investment in their offspring. We investigated the foraging movement of naive, newly weaned elephant seal pups in relation to ocean currents and their subsequent survival using CJS models that account for imperfect detection probability. We found that travelling with the flow of predominant subsurface currents is a strong predictor of survival probability in weaned pups.

Current-following as a survival advantage

Our analysis revealed that current-following provides a survival advantage for naive southern elephant seal pups. Pups that followed ocean currents during their outbound foraging trip had higher first year survival probabilities. Weaning mass also had substantial support in the model set and is a well-established predictor of juvenile survival in elephant seals [40]; our results do not discount body condition but add current-following as an additional factor associated with survival. At Kerguelen, by contrast, no significant relationship was found between current-following and survival in juveniles [6]. Reported juvenile survival also varies among sub-Antarctic populations. At Macquarie Island, McMahon et al. (1999) estimated first year survival at 66% from the full branded cohort [39]; we used the same resighting dataset but a smaller sample (the subset with tracking data), for which our first-year apparent survival estimate is 50%. Elsewhere, ~ 60% at Marion Island [56], 28–40% yearling survival at Heard Island [57], and 55% first-trip survival at Kerguelen [6] (the latter is more comparable to a single 6-month interval than to first year; our per-interval Φ was 70.4%). Methods and definitions differ (resighting vs. known-fate, first year vs. first trip), so direct comparison is limited. The difference from the Kerguelen result likely reflects both method and site: that study measured short-term deviation of daily swimming direction from local currents, whereas we used particle tracing to measure long-term alignment with subsurface currents over each journey, and ocean conditions differ between Macquarie and Kerguelen.

Weaned pups tended to align with currents more closely in the early stages of their foraging trip, gradually deviating from this pattern as time progresses, indicating that they are not merely drifting with the current. Instead, it appears that while the pups swim without a set destination, they are influenced by the dominant ACC, steering them primarily east-southeast. As they travel, young seals may make subtle directional shifts [58] based on cues such as temperature gradients, currents, topography and olfactory signals produced by dimethyl sulphide from phytoplankton [59–62]. Nonetheless, the prevailing current ensures a consistent south-easterly trajectory. Seals that deviate from this general behaviour might be influenced by specific environmental or behavioural factors that counteract the current’s influence. However, it is interesting to question why these factors would result in poorer survival.

The accessibility area concept: how currents expand foraging opportunities

Travelling with the Antarctic Circumpolar Current may provide naive seals with a survival advantage by increasing their accessible foraging area, i.e. they are able to travel further assisted by the current. For pups with limited energy reserves and no prior knowledge of foraging locations, the area they can search before exhausting their resources becomes critical to survival. Without currents, this searchable area forms a circle around the colony. However, eastward-flowing currents shift this circle downstream, meaning seals can access a much larger potential foraging area for the same energy cost when travelling with the current.

We call this expanded searchable area the “accessibility area.” This mechanism may explain why current-following predicts survival. Newly weaned pups rely entirely on energy stores from their mothers [63] and face high energetic costs of swimming [64, 65]. Moving with ocean currents allows pups to cover greater distances while using less energy [66, 67], effectively making downstream foraging patches “closer than they appear” in terms of time and energy cost. This increases the probability of finding food before running out of energy.

The mechanism does not require seals to actively choose downstream directions. If a pup maintains constant swimming effort and moves randomly, it will naturally cover more ground when moving with the current and less ground when moving against it. The net result over time is a passive downstream drift that increases survival probability. This passive transport also offers additional benefits: reducing predation risk by quickly moving pups away from areas near their colonies where predators are common [6, 68, 69], and allowing exploration of different oceanic regions where they can find and exploit prey patches [19].

Current-driven prey accessibility and evolutionary implications

The natural flow of currents steers seals towards zones where convergent currents concentrate prey [70, 71]. Female southern elephant seals from Kerguelen typically swim downstream, to the east of the island, where they encounter a greater number of eddies [72]. Similarly, Marion Island adult females preferentially swim through areas with high eddy activity [73]. These oceanic features cluster prey into denser aggregations, vital for seals and other apex marine predators during their foraging excursions [72, 74–76]. Conversely, juveniles that forage in areas with weak eddy activity face higher chances of mortality [6]. Over evolutionary time, the initial survival advantage for pups that happen to go downstream could lead to an inherited tendency for eastward dispersal, potentially explaining why even experienced adults show this bias across multiple sub-Antarctic colonies.

From innate drift to learned navigation

Adult female seals display travel patterns that are clearly different from those of weaned pups, traveling more directly southward towards Antarctic water [19, 41]. This distinction reveals the transition from innate to learned foraging strategies. At Marion Island, underyearlings travelled in multiple directions with no repeatability in travel direction within or between individuals and years, and maiden trips took longer than subsequent trips during the first year [33], consistent with a period of exploration and learning. Ocean currents initially guide naive pups to adequate foraging areas through passive transport. However, as seals gain experience, their movements become more refined and directed, enabling them to reach more optimal foraging areas [77, 78].

The contrast between pup and adult movement patterns suggests that current-following provides a crucial survival advantage during the vulnerable juvenile stage, when seals lack the experience to navigate directly to productive foraging grounds. As seals mature, they likely discover superior foraging areas and develop strong site fidelity [79–81], transitioning from passive current-following to active habitat selection.

Limitations

While pup and adult tracking data overlapped in only a single year (2000), our analysis included a robust multi-year dataset for both groups—spanning four years for pups and six years for adults. The high statistical significance of the Watson’s two-sample test suggests that the observed differences in dispersal patterns represent stable, group-level behaviours rather than inter-annual environmental variability. Additionally, while particle traces occasionally failed due to gaps in oceanographic reanalysis data, our adherence analysis was restricted to the initial 10-day phase. Consequently, the selection of trace ensembles did not influence the adherence metrics used for survival modelling, as all retained traces successfully covered this critical early-trip window.

Conclusions

Current-following behaviour is a strong predictor of survival in naive southern elephant seal pups. The “accessibility area” concept provides a mechanistic framework explaining how currents expand foraging opportunities for energy-limited juveniles—a survival strategy potentially applicable to other marine species with naive dispersing offspring.
