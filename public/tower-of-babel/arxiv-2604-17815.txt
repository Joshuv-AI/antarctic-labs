Navigating the Conceptual Multiverse
Andre Ye, Jenny Y. Huang, Alicia Guo, Rose Novick, Tamara Broderick, Mitchell L. Gordon

Abstract
When language models answer open-ended problems, they implicitly make decisions that shape their outputs but that remain hidden. This leaves users with uncontextualized answers rather than a working map of the problem. Drawing on multiverse analysis from statistics, we build and evaluate the conceptual multiverse : an interactive system that represents conceptual decisions, such as how to frame a question or what to value, as a space users can ⋆ \star transparently inspect, ⋆ \star intervenably change, and check against ⋆ \star principled domain reasoning. For the multiverse’s structure to be worth navigating, rather than misleading, it must be rigorous and checkable against the reasoning norms of the domain. We develop a general verification framework that enforces properties of good decision structures, like unambiguity and completeness, calibrated by expert-level reasoning. In studies across three domains, the conceptual multiverse helped participants develop a working map of the problem: philosophy students rewrote essays with sharper framings and reversed theses; alignment annotators moved from surface preferences to reasoning about user intent and harm; and poets identified compositional patterns that clarified their taste.

Introduction

“The book was made as a polyhedron, and it has conclusions

everywhere, written along all of its edges.”

— Italo Calvino, on
Invisible Cities

(
10
)

Many of the open-ended questions and tasks we bring to AI involve a cascade of decisions that spills into a landscape of possibilities — but we typically receive a single result, presented as if it were the only one.
The suggestions of AI writing assistants shift users’ beliefs on societal issues without their awareness
(
52
)
;
language models, given the same data, can frame nearly any social science finding as significant or insignificant depending on which analytical choices they make
(
6
)
;
LLM-as-a-judge evaluations collapse many implicit interpretive choices into single, brittle scores, yet are increasingly trusted to supervise other systems
(
54
)
.
In each case, we are left with a single result, or bag of uncontextualized results, in place of a working map.
We have no way to know how much hinges on decisions we never saw.

A version of this problem has been recognized in data analysis as the “garden of forking paths”
(
17
)
: the same data can yield contradictory conclusions depending on which defensible choices an analyst makes, but typically only one is reported.
“Multiverse analysis”
(
44
)
addresses this by systematically varying those choices and reporting how the result changes, giving readers a working map of how much hangs on any single fork.
AI is beginning to assist with multiverse analysis for scientific data
(
7
)
, but people are bringing many open-ended problems to AI as well
(
22
)
— value-laden, philosophical, creative.
For these problems, the decisions along the forking path are not data analysis choices but
conceptual ones
: how to frame a question, what assumptions to make, what to value.
Can we build something analogous to multiverse analysis for these open-ended problems?

In this paper, we introduce the
conceptual multiverse
, a system that surfaces the decisions shaping an AI’s answer to an open-ended question and the alternatives each decision opens up.
Instead of taking a single answer on faith, people can develop a working map of the problem by navigating the decisions that shape it.
Such a system faces real challenges, which lead us to formulate three desiderata.
If the decisions shaping an answer are not surfaced in terms a person can understand, the multiverse is just a black box that produces more outputs. It must be
\star
transparent
.
If a person can see the decisions but not change them, they cannot navigate the landscape or test what depends on what. It must be
\star
intervenable
.
But which decisions, and why those?
In data analysis, the relevant decisions come comparatively more well-defined than in open-ended problems, where even what the decisions are is not settled. But someone asking a question in a domain is trying to reason the way that domain reasons, and domains do develop their own standards for what counts as good reasoning
(
27
)
. The multiverse’s structure should be checkable against those standards. It must be
\star
principled
.
Otherwise, persuasive outputs can mask unprincipled process: for example,
19
argue that LLMs can produce convincing legal interpretation without performing the reasoning that would make it legitimate, casting doubt on their use in judicial settings.

Can existing approaches to structured reasoning fill this role? In part, but not where it matters most for open-ended problems.
Existing approaches to structured reasoning were largely built as search strategies for problems with verifiable answers, not as open-ended structures that people navigate.
Chain-of-thought reasoning
(
51
)
, for instance, was not designed to be legible to people: traces are frequently unfaithful to the model’s actual reasoning
(
48
;
29
)
, and the best-performing traces are not the most interpretable ones
(
8
)
.
Other work has made progress.
Tree of thought
(
53
)
introduces meaningful
branching, moving toward
\star
intervenability
by letting different
reasoning paths be explored, but the branches are explored by the model’s
own search, not by a person choosing which assumption to change.
Counterfactual chain of thought
(
20
)
trains
traces to be more faithful to the model’s actual reasoning, improving

\star
transparency
, but the traces remain model-generated sequences, not
structures a person would navigate.
And none provides
\star
principled
structure: a way to ground the reasoning
in the norms of a domain, because their target problems have well-defined
answers that supply their own reward.

We take a different approach.
In the rest of the paper, we describe our system for building and evaluating conceptual multiverses: we get models to reason from the start as a tree of human-interpretable decisions (§
2.1
): each decision is a branch point a person can
\star
transparently
inspect and
\star
intervenably
change; verification checks evaluate whether each decision is well-formed and improve it when it is not (§
2.2
); the tree is calibrated by domain experts to approximate how
\star
principled
reasoning in that domain works (§
2.3
).
We calibrated the system in three domains: philosophy (answering an open-ended question), AI alignment (responding to a potentially sensitive user request), and poetry (writing a poem from a prompt).
To evaluate the conceptual multiverse, we built an interface for navigating it and conducted in-depth studies (1–1.5 hours each) with 15 participants.
We describe our study design and results in philosophy (§
3.1
), AI alignment (§
3.2
), and poetry (§
3.3
).
Overall, we find that the conceptual multiverse helps participants develop a working map of the problem, surfacing thinking that uncontextualized answers would have left buried.

2
Designing the conceptual multiverse

We describe how we designed a system for producing conceptual multiverses that is
\star
transparent
,
\star
intervenable
, and
\star
principled
.
Throughout, we use the running example of a user asking “Should we fear death?” — a question that has occupied thinkers of the human condition for millennia
(
40
;
34
;
21
;
35
;
9
)
.

2.1
Representing the multiverse:
\diamond
transformations
and
\diamond
decisions

How should we represent reasoning so that a user can
\star
transparently
see why an output was reached and
\star
intervene
on the assumptions behind it?

Model the reasoning process as a sequence of
\diamond
states
connected by
\diamond
transformations
, and bundle
\diamond
transformations
into
\diamond
decisions
so that each
\diamond
state
can be transformed in many different ways.

Consider the question “Should we fear death?”
A natural first move is to frame the question in terms of deprivation (what is lost in death): write this as
s_{0}\mathrel{\smash{\xrightarrow{\scriptscriptstyle t_{1}}}}s_{1}
, where
\diamond
transformation

t_{1}
(“
frame the question in terms of deprivation: ask whether death is bad because of what it takes from the person who dies
”) takes the initial
\diamond
state

s_{0}
(the bare question) and produces
s_{1}
, a
\diamond
state
in which we have committed to investigating death through the lens of what it takes away.

\diamond
Transformations
are natural-language descriptions of how to move from
\diamond
state
to
\diamond
state
, much like steps in a logical argument.
A next
\diamond
transformation
specifies what is lost:
s_{1}\mathrel{\smash{\xrightarrow{\scriptscriptstyle t_{2}}}}s_{2}
(“
identify the loss as all possible future experience
”).
The
\diamond
state
now carries both commitments.
Continuing through further
\diamond
transformations
, we arrive at a terminal
\diamond
state

s_{n}
: an output, such as “
We should fear death, because it deprives us of everything we might have lived.
”
A chain
s_{0}\mathrel{\smash{\xrightarrow{\scriptscriptstyle t_{1}}}}s_{1}\mathrel{\smash{\xrightarrow{\scriptscriptstyle t_{2}}}}\cdots\to s_{n}
is a
decision path
.
Each
\diamond
transformation
should be
\checkmark
unambiguous
: specific enough that applying
t_{i}
to
s_{i-1}
leaves no reasonable doubt about
s_{i}
(we define this in §
2.2
).
This makes a decision path
\star
transparent
: to understand why we arrived at a particular output
\diamond
state
, we inspect the
\diamond
transformations
that produced it.

But at almost any
\diamond
state
, there is more than one reasonable
\diamond
transformation
to apply.
From
s_{0}
, instead of framing the question in terms of deprivation (
t_{1}
), one could begin from the
experience
of confronting mortality (
t_{1}^{\prime}
): the dread, the avoidance, what it feels like to live in the knowledge that we will die.
This is a different starting point, and it may lead to different conclusions: whereas the first path arrived at
s_{n}
(“
We should fear death, because it deprives us of everything we might have lived
”), this path might arrive at
s_{n}^{\prime}
(“
Death is what gives life its urgency and meaning; to fear it is to misunderstand what it does for us
”).
We bundle the available
\diamond
transformations
from a given
\diamond
state
into a
\diamond
decision
; the
multiverse
comprises all paths through these
\diamond
decisions
.
One can therefore
\star
intervene
on a decision path by changing a
\diamond
transformation
at any
\diamond
decision
along the way.
Importantly, every
\diamond
transformation
embodies a
commitment
(e.g., an assumption, a framing, a value).
We make this explicit by pairing each
\diamond
transformation
with a
\diamond
condition
: a statement of what the user is committing to by endorsing that
\diamond
transformation
.
Where
t_{1}
says “
frame the question in terms of deprivation: ask whether death is bad because of what it takes from the person who dies
,” the paired
\diamond
condition
captures the commitment: “
Whether we should fear death depends on whether it deprives us of something valuable.
”

\diamond
Conditions
respond to
\diamond
questions
that characterize what is being decided: here, “
How should we approach the question of whether to fear death?
”
A multiverse can contain hundreds of
\diamond
decisions
and output
\diamond
states
, and navigating it at the level of intermediate
\diamond
states
and
\diamond
transformations
would be unhelpful.
We built an interface (Figure
1
) in which users navigate at the level of
\diamond
questions
and
\diamond
conditions
: the language of what they are committing to, not the machinery that carries out the commitment.
Verification checks (§
2.2
) enforce links between these two layers, ensuring that each
\diamond
condition
faithfully represents what its
\diamond
transformation
actually does; design details are in §
B.5
.

We represent the multiverse concretely as a Python program: each
\diamond
decision
is a Python object, downstream decisions declare their inputs by indexing into upstream ones, and the full multiverse lives in a single file that coding agents can search, edit, and execute.
A generation agent builds the initial multiverse from domain-specific guidance (§
2.3
); a review agent proposes edits; a regeneration agent rewrites components that fail verification (§
2.2
).
We use Claude Sonnet 4.6; full syntax, worked examples, and implementation details are in §
B.1
and §
B.3
.
On average, philosophy multiverses contain 60
\diamond
decisions
and 140 outputs; alignment multiverses contain 173 and 394; and poetry multiverses contain 464 and 632.

2.2
Evaluating and improving the multiverse: Verification and regeneration

How do we know that the multiverse is actually
\star
transparent
and
\star
intervenable
?

Treat the multiverse as a semiformal object — structured enough that specific properties can be systematically checked and used as signals to improve it.

The structure from §
2.1
makes it possible to check specific properties that good multiverse reasoning would seem to have.

\diamond
Transformations
could be vague enough that different interpreters reach different conclusions from the same step;
\diamond
decisions
could omit defensible alternatives;
\diamond
conditions
could presuppose commitments the user never endorsed.
Without checks, the structure is cosmetic: it looks
\star
principled
but is not.
The intuition is close to formal verification: Lean
(
12
)
works because proofs must be written in a structured form, and we work at a much lower degree of formalism, over natural language rather than typed derivations, but the leverage is the same.
We define six checks, each implemented as an LLM-as-judge
(
54
)
on a specific component and calibrated to domain-specific norms (§
2.3
).
We describe two here; all six receive fuller treatment in §
B.4
.

\checkmark
Unambiguity
(applies to
\diamond
transformations
).
A
\diamond
transformation
should determine its output
\diamond
state
: applying it to its input should leave no reasonable doubt about the result.
A verifier receives the input
\diamond
state
and
\diamond
transformation
and attempts to produce distinct alternative outputs; if it can, the
\diamond
transformation
is ambiguous.
Consider “
Evaluate whether death can be bad for the person who dies.
”
One interpreter could focus on future experiences the dead person will never have; another on desires and projects left unfinished; a third on whether death is a harm independent of what is lost.
Each produces a different
\diamond
state
.
The fix is to make the
\diamond
transformation
more specific, or the input or output
\diamond
states
more specific, or both, until variation across interpreters is negligible within the domain.

\checkmark
Completeness
(applies to
\diamond
decisions
).
A
\diamond
decision
should cover the range of alternatives that someone reasoning carefully in the domain would recognize as worth considering, given what prior
\diamond
decisions
have established.
This does not mean listing everything: prior commitments narrow what remains open, and
\diamond
decisions
should operate at the right grain, deferring finer distinctions to later steps.
A verifier reviews prior
\diamond
questions
and
\diamond
conditions
and proposes alternatives that are absent but have not been ruled out.

The remaining four checks target other aspects of the structure.

\checkmark
Faithfulness
(checks a
\diamond
transformation
against its
\diamond
condition
): the
\diamond
transformation
should not presuppose more than the user endorsed by selecting its
\diamond
condition
.
If a
\diamond
condition
reads “
consider whether death is bad for the person who dies
,” the
\diamond
transformation
should not assume that what makes death bad is specifically the loss of future experience; a user who endorsed the broader question did not agree to that narrower framing.

\checkmark
Condition grounding
(checks a
\diamond
condition
against prior
\diamond
decisions
): each
\diamond
condition
should be consistent with the commitments established so far, neither presupposing something not yet established nor treating a settled
\diamond
question
as still open.

\checkmark
Question continuity
(applies to
\diamond
questions
across a path): the sequence of
\diamond
questions
should read as a natural chain of inquiry, each arising from what prior
\diamond
decisions
have established.

\checkmark
Uniqueness
(checks
\diamond
conditions
against each other within a
\diamond
decision
): each
\diamond
condition
should be uniquely paired with its corresponding
\diamond
transformation
, and vice versa, so that neither could be swapped for another in the same
\diamond
decision
without a loss of fit.
Detailed treatments of all six, including prompts and worked examples, are in §
B.4
.

Together, the six checks form a mutually reinforcing net.
In practice, satisfying all six simultaneously is difficult, and multiverses routinely fail some checks.
After running all checks on a completed multiverse, a regeneration agent reviews a per-
\diamond
decision
report and makes targeted edits: adding a missing
\diamond
condition
, tightening an ambiguous
\diamond
transformation
, rewriting a
\diamond
condition
that does not match what its
\diamond
transformation
actually does.
It attempts each fix while balancing the other constraints: tightening an ambiguous
\diamond
transformation
, for instance, might risk violating
\checkmark
faithfulness
if the new wording presupposes more than the
\diamond
condition
endorsed, which may in turn require updating the
\diamond
condition
.
The process can be iterated.
We currently accomplish this through an agentic harness, though one could also imagine training models to reason in this structured way, with process-based rewards
(
49
;
30
)
defined by verification checks.

2.3
Calibrating the multiverse: Approximating domain-specific reasoning with experts

Verification checks can detect structural problems, but how do we ensure they reflect what counts as good reasoning in a given domain?

Work iteratively with domain experts to produce methodological principles and a worked example to ground generation and verification.

Every field develops its own standards for what counts as good reasoning within it: what questions are well-formed, what methods of inquiry are legitimate, what kinds of distinctions carry weight
(
27
;
25
)
.
What counts as “precise enough” for a
\diamond
transformation
to be
\checkmark
unambiguous
, or which alternatives a
\diamond
decision
must cover to be
\checkmark
complete
, differs across domains, so verification checks must be calibrated to reflect domain-specific norms.
This is how the system becomes
\star
principled
.
Models do not yet reliably approximate these judgments, so this knowledge must come from people.
We worked with one expert per domain (a philosophy professor, an AI alignment researcher, and a poet) through repeated sessions, producing two resources for each domain: a set of methodological principles, and a fully worked example multiverse showing concretely what those principles look like in practice.
These serve as ground truth for the agents that build and verify multiverses, referenced as needed during generation (§
2.1
) and verification (§
2.2
).
What we calibrate is one approach to reasoning within each domain, not the only legitimate one.
A different expert could calibrate the system differently.
Calibrations could also be more or less fine-grained, reflecting, say, a particular philosophical tradition rather than the field as a whole.
It is not difficult to imagine calibration marketplaces, structured analogues of the prompt and agent-skills marketplaces that already exist
(
37
;
2
)
, in which domain experts share calibrations that encode how reasoning in their area should be structured.
Generation guidance, including methodological principles and worked example multiverses for each domain, is in §
B.2
.

Philosophy.

Philosophy addresses questions that cannot be settled by observation or experiment alone: the nature of existence
(
3
)
, the grounds of knowledge
(
13
)
, the basis of moral judgment
(
23
)
.
There are multiple legitimate approaches to any such question, and choosing among them is itself a significant part of the inquiry.
We calibrated philosophy multiverses to make this explicit: the first
\diamond
decision
concerns how to approach the question at all, whether to begin by analyzing the concepts involved (what do “fear” and “death” even mean?), by examining what we directly experience when we confront mortality, by tracing how thinking about the topic has developed historically, and so on.
Subsequent decisions follow the logic of the chosen approach, asking the questions it naturally raises.
This choice of approach shapes which positions are easiest to reach, though it does not necessarily eliminate any.

AI alignment.

AI alignment concerns the behavior of systems deployed to serve people with diverse and sometimes competing goals.
A central tension is between user autonomy (giving people what they ask for) and the potential for doing so to harm the user, others, or the world
(
16
;
1
)
.
We calibrated alignment multiverses to work through this tension explicitly.
A first phase considers what the user could plausibly want: someone asking “Should we fear death?” might seek a philosophical engagement with the question, or might be expressing anxiety about their own mortality.
A second phase reasons about what problems, if any, might arise from directly satisfying each interpretation, ranging from no concern at all to a risk of deepening distress.
A third phase works out how to address those problems, or if none exist, how best to present the answer.
Depending on how the
\diamond
decisions
resolve, the outputs range from direct rejections to mild caveats, reframings, and direct satisfactions of the request.

Poetry.

Poetry involves a process (gathering material, setting constraints, discovering what a piece wants to be) that is structured even when the structure is being intentionally broken
(
36
)
.
Our multiverse follows one such arc: an initial phase of material gathering (three decisions, narrowing from image to tension to discovery) followed by composition (three to four decisions concerning entry, development, transformation, and ending).
We calibrated to ensure that questions and conditions are specific to the poem being written, so that a different poem produces a different set of decisions rather than the same structure with different surface content.

3
Evaluating the conceptual multiverse with humans

We evaluate the conceptual multiverse with human participants across three domains: philosophy (§
3.1
), AI alignment (§
3.2
), and poetry (§
3.3
).
If the multiverse makes the structure of open-ended problems visible in ways that existing tools do not, this should show up in how people reason after engaging with it.
In each domain, participants first work with a baseline and then with the multiverse, carrying out a goal-directed activity: essay writing for philosophy, AI response annotation for AI alignment, and material gathering for poetry.
The baseline for philosophy and poetry is ChatGPT using the default behavior of the model picker at the time when the available models were GPT-5.3 chat and GPT-5.2 reasoning (hereon “chat”); for alignment, it is a ranking annotation interface.

3.1
Helping students take more structured inquiry into philosophical concepts

Philosophical understanding requires not just surveying claims about a question but seeing the
lines of inquiry
that generate them
(
31
;
28
)
: the assumptions and choices that lead different thinkers from the same question to different positions.
Chat AI can reproduce philosophical content, but it does not make this structure visible; a student who asks a chatbot to explore alternatives gets a pile of text with no structural indication of how the arguments relate to each other or where they diverge.
We asked 5 philosophy students to each write a short essay on a philosophical question of their choosing, first after using chat for up to 20 minutes, then after exploring a multiverse built for their question.
Participants either stopped chatting on their own or were stopped at the time limit but could anticipate limited value from continued discussion.
The artifact is the essay itself: what participants change shows how their thinking changed.
Full details: §
A.1
.

Every participant revised their essay after the multiverse
: refining definitions (
P2
), reframing the question to expose hidden ambiguities (
P4
), reversing a thesis after identifying errors in reasoning (
P5
), and introducing complications the initial argument had been silent on (
P1,3
); see §
A.1
for full essays. Chat was
“a glorified Google”
(
P1,3,4
) that recycled what participants already knew, partly because it requires you to already know what to ask:
“I don’t think I would have thought to narrow it down and pick out specific lines of reasoning”
(
P2
). Because the decisions generating disagreement were laid out
\star
transparently
, participants could see
“a mental map of what the differences between these arguments are”
(
P4
), and because each decision point was independently navigable they could
\star
intervene
on assumptions and trace the consequences (
P2,4,5
). That the questions felt like the right ones —
“a pretty good covering set”
(
P5
) — suggests the structure was
\star
principled
. Participants estimated replicating this with chat would require
“an order of magnitude of more work”
(
P3,4
); full quotes in §
A.1.3
.

3.2
Annotating AI reasoning, not just outputs, for collective AI alignment

Collecting human values for AI alignment requires more than preferences over outputs: people need to articulate
why
one response is better than another, and current methods make this difficult
(
55
;
14
)
.
Values are not fixed things waiting to be collected; they develop through the process of articulating them, and a good annotation medium should support normative deliberation
(
33
;
24
)
: the thinking in which people test and revise their commitments.
We use the multiverse to structure this differently: extending recent work on process-based annotation
(
11
)
, participants annotate not outputs but the
reasoning paths
that lead from a user’s request to a model’s response, marking each path as ideal, acceptable, or unacceptable.
We recruited 5 computer science students with existing views on AI alignment, each of whom chose a high-disagreement prompt from the OpenAI CoVal dataset
(
38
)
.
Before seeing the multiverse, participants ranked four model completions and wrote down (a) what an ideal response would look like and (b) what factors an aligned model should get right.
After annotating reasoning paths for about 35 minutes, they were asked the same questions again.
This design means any change is a revision of something the participant had already committed to on the record, not a shift in vague intuition.
Full details: §
A.2
.

Every participant revised their description of an ideal response, the factors they consider important, or both
: moving from reducing harm to giving users agency (
P6
), from simple refusal to reasoning about how responses could be exploited (
P7
), and from answering the question as asked to first understanding why the user was asking (
P8,9,10
); see §
A.2
for full responses.
Ranking had given participants a basis for comparison but not for understanding: they could express preferences but not articulate the reasoning behind them (
P6,7,9
).
Because each step in the multiverse’s reasoning was
\star
transparent

5
Discussion and Conclusion

In this paper, we introduced the conceptual multiverse, a system that maps the many decisions that could shape AI’s answers to open-ended problems into a structure people can collaboratively navigate.
The system represents reasoning as a tree of decisions that people can
\star
transparently
inspect,
\star
intervenably
change, and check against
\star
principled
domain reasoning.
Verification checks enforce structural properties on the tree, calibrated to domain-specific reasoning norms by working with experts in philosophy, AI alignment, and poetry.
The architecture is designed to be domain-general: new domains simply require a new calibration within our abstraction.
In studies with 15 participants, philosophy students rewrote essays with sharper framings and reversed theses, alignment annotators moved from surface preferences to reasoning about user intent and potential harm, and poets identified compositional patterns that clarified their taste.
The system expands the ways people can think about open-ended problems, but the expansion has limitations.
The multiverses are generated by LLMs and calibrated by individual domain experts, both of whom carry their own priors: viewpoints underrepresented in training data or outside a particular expert’s tradition may not appear as branches.
This means that even a multiverse that covers a broad space of answers has a specific shape, subject to the biases and blind spots of its sources.
Additionally, realizing this vision required building many components — generation, verification, calibration, the navigation interface — which we evaluated jointly rather than ablating individually.
Our evaluations were designed to study, in a natural setting, how people think differently when given this kind of system, not to disentangle the contribution of each component.
On a domain-to-domain or even individual-to-individual basis, different components may have mattered in different ways we have not isolated.

Looking towards the future: as models grow more capable, assistant personas will become increasingly persuasive communicators of concepts.
This makes it all the more important to invest in better representations of the concepts themselves: structures people can inspect, navigate, and check, rather than relying on how convincingly an answer is presented.
Multiverses can scale along two axes: generating out parts of the multiverse, and verifying and improving the multiverse to make it more well-formed.
This vision is already familiar in well-defined domains: coding agents build out large software projects and write tests to ensure correctness, and autoformalization agents translate mathematical proofs into formal languages where the proof checks or it does not — in both cases, agents work within and scale along a structured scaffold.
Although there is much more work to be done, we have suggested in this paper that open-ended problems both a)
need
such a scaffold for AI to serve humans properly and b) might, in collaboration with domain experts or in future models with improved per-domain taste and judgement,
have
such a semi-structured scaffold.
In this way, the impressive and beautiful work that agents are doing in software engineering and mathematics might be responsibly expanded to help us humans explore and grapple with the polyhedral multiverses of the philosophical, the artistic, the value-laden…

A.1.1
Philosophy Study Procedure

We recruited 5 undergraduate and graduate students from our institution via mailing lists who had taken at least one philosophy course and had philosophical interests. Studies were conducted in person (approximately 1–1.5 hours each) and recorded with participants’ consent. Participants selected their own philosophical question ahead of time, either from the list below or one of their own choosing.

Philosophy questions.

•

Can you be responsible for something you didn’t choose to benefit from?

•

Is there a difference between a state failing its citizens and a state being illegitimate?

•

Should we fear death?

•

Is there a difference between two things being identical and being indistinguishable?

•

Could there have been nothing rather than something?

•

Is there a difference between being happy and thinking you’re happy?

•

If someone could predict every choice you’ll ever make, are you still genuinely choosing?

•

Without struggle, is there anything left to admire in a person?

•

We hold people responsible for what they do. But no one chooses their genes, their upbringing, or their brain chemistry. Is there a conflict here?

•

What is intelligence?

The session proceeded in four phases.

Initial interview (5 min).

Before any AI interaction, the researcher asked participants to share their initial thoughts on their question: what position, if any, they were already inclined toward, and why. This established a baseline against which subsequent changes could be compared.

ChatGPT session and first essay (30 min).

Participants used a fresh ChatGPT session to develop a position they could defend in a short, informal essay of two to three paragraphs. They were encouraged to think aloud throughout. When they felt ready — or after approximately 20 minutes — the ChatGPT window was closed and they had ten minutes to write their essay.

Multiverse session and essay revision (40 min).

The researcher introduced the multiverse interface, explaining that rather than generating a single response, it maps out a sequence of decisions where each branch leads to a different conclusion. The researcher walked the participant through one path and demonstrated how to navigate from an output back to the decisions that produced it. Participants then explored freely, thinking aloud, with the same goal as before. After exploring, they had five minutes to revise or rewrite their essay with their first draft visible.

Final interview (10 min).

With both essay drafts and the participant’s initial thoughts side by side, the researcher asked what had changed about their thinking and what had stayed the same. Participants were then asked what felt different about what they got from ChatGPT versus the multiverse.

The question of belief of happiness versus actually being happy is analogous to the question of belief of knowledge versus actually knowing something. In epistemology we have proven that the belief of knowledge does not imply knowing. It is fair to say that happiness is a completely different thing than knowledge, but it is useful to consider this question and the difference of belief versus reality when thinking about the problem.

One concrete example of “misleaded” happiness usually is about money or status. People often work very hard and sacrifice other sources of happiness to focus on making money and increasing their reputation, but in many cases, they report not being happy in the end. Even if they were thinking they were happy and they would be happy in the future, it turns out the opposite can be true. And even if they were really happy by some definition of happiness during the process, it would be fair to question that definition based on the fact that they think it was the wrong choice in the aftermath.

When one feels that they are not happy, they usually try to figure out why and get professional help to improve their mental health. Sometimes, the realization of one needing help comes from the outside – a family member, a friend, or a coworker might suggest one to get help. In these cases, it is not necessary for the person to think they are not happy – in fact, they might be content with their life as it is (e.g., forgotten sense of what being happy really means or general lack of feelings about everything via depression or anhedonia). This suggests that the mind can be tricky and not consistent with reality.

Given all of the above, it would be fair to assume that the belief of happiness can be blurry and not well-articulated for many people. While this conclusion does not define happiness or suggest a definition, it does show that happiness is not the same as belief in it. Some more ideas to think about in the topic:

•

Does the definition of happiness depend on the existing worldview and/or perspectives? Would it be possible for a person in 18th century born as a slave to be ever happy, even if they were treated “well” by their masters?

•

P2
•
If all your choices could be perfectly predicted, are you still genuinely choosing?

In this essay, choice refers to the ability of a person to choose between one or more options. All of these options, while they may not have equal chances of being chosen, must have a non-zero probability of being chosen in order for the decision to qualify as a choice. To be a genuine choice, a person must believe that there are multiple options available , and deliberation is a necessary step in that process. A person must have some reasoning or impulse that motivates their choice in order to make it a genuine one.

If a person is unaware that their choices can be predicted, then when they are making decisions, they still are choosing between multiple plausible options. Therefore, there is still an element of self-determinism for the person making the choice, and there is also an element of uncertainty in the outcome. Over the course of deliberating, the person is able to reduce that uncertainty until they make a choice. These factors make the choice a genuine one, and the person is still genuinely choosing.

However, if a person is aware that their choices can be predicted, then they know that even when presented with multiple options, one will be chosen with 100% certainty and the others will not be chosen. Even if the person deliberates and changes their mind at times, whichever choice that person finally makes is the same one that was originally predicted. Their deliberation becomes empty, almost performative in nature. Since the person knows that there was only one possible outcome, they were not able to make a genuine choice, as defined above. The motivation behind their choice does not matter, and motivation is a core element of a genuine choice.

One possible counterargument is that regardless of the person’s awareness of the predictability of their choice, only one such outcome was possible. Therefore, the two scenarios are the same, and there was never a choice at all. However, we are concerned with genuine choices, and the idea of genuinity comes from a person’s internal beliefs that they are able to choose between multiple options and that their deliberation matters when arriving at the final choice. This internal belief is determined by a person’s awareness of the predictability of their choice. Such a belief is present in the first scenario, allowing for a genuine choice, but not in the latter, which removes the importance of deliberation and prevents the possibility of a genuine choice.

Should we fear death?

It seems clear to me, that when people are answering this question, they are interpreting this question very differently each time. For example, some people take this to mean “should” in a normative or prescriptive sense – i.e., does fearing death change the way we live our lives? And if so, is that a good or right thing to do? For others, they approach this question by not answering this question, focusing instead on an orthogonal point: the fact that death gives our lives meaning. Unfortunately, these philosophers are not answering what I interpret the question to really mean, which is, is it rational to fear the state of eternal nothingness, even if the state of eternal nothingness yields meaning to an otherwise meaningless existence and even if there is no “being” to experience that loss.

In which case, I say it is very natural and actually not just natural but rational to fear death. This is because our existence is predicated on being able to experience and the removal of this not only means the total annihilation of the self but also of the finality of the construction of the self, normally accepted to be an ongoing project, meaning that people are forced to confront the fact that the state of being they’ve always experienced will not only end in total non-experience but also end in total completion of this self-construction project.

P5
•
Is there a difference between being identical and being indistinguishable?

Answer:
NO, there is no difference.
In order to understand why, we must define our terms. Two things are distinguishable if we can perceive a qualitative difference between them, some property they do not share. We may also speak of distinguishability for a given property. Two things are identical means they are the same thing at some essential level. We realize that identicality and indistinguishability are the same when we recognize that there is no mystical notion of “identity” that cannot be explained away as a perceivability property by which two things can theoretically be distinguished.

Some examples are helpful. Two coins minted one after another may be indistinguishable (in every test we can think of) and yet they are obviously, as objects, non-identical, just by virtue of the fact that there are two of them! Some philosophers would argue that this means there is some special kind of intrinsicness identity (haecceity) that objects have independent of any property, but I think we are simply making an appeal to the property of physical sameness when we notice they can be distinguished by the fact that they do not occupy the same position in space. Also consider that given sufficiently advanced assays we would be able to distinguish the two coins by some small atomic differences, e.g., so everything that is non-identical can be distinguished with sufficiently good tests. The upshot is this: identity is really just indistinguishability because an object’s identity is constructed from a “bundle” of qualitative properties (including physical properties if it has them, causal history, location in physical space if applicable, etc.), all of which can be compared to other objects, and which, taken together, uniquely identify that object.

For another example consider the domain of computer science, where we have the notion of “exact copies,” defined as two blocks of memory in a computer disk having exactly the same bytes. If we only test for distinguishability by checking byte-by-byte, then yes, they are identical. But if we also consider the memory address (physical location on disk), they can be distinguished, so they are not identical. No matter what, distinguishability and identicality follow each other.

A.1.4
ChatGPT Usage

Our study design gave participants up to 20 minutes with ChatGPT before writing their essay, with the instruction that they could stop whenever they felt ready. This was deliberate: we wanted to simulate the natural arc of a ChatGPT conversation on an open-ended question, where the value of continuing to chat tapers off quickly. Below we summarize how each participant used ChatGPT and how they characterized its value.

P1
grew frustrated with ChatGPT early. They reported that it “is mostly telling me what I told it with more context and trying to expand the question into too many general dimensions, which is not inherently a bad thing, but it’s not really helpful.” They added: “I would change the model. ChatGPT is not giving me the value I am looking for… I don’t think it helped me really.”

P2
used ChatGPT for roughly 15 minutes before moving on to write. They found it useful for externalizing their thinking but not for generating new directions: “I think the responses are somewhat different from the directions I wanted to go, but I don’t think I would have been able to fully verbalize my thoughts without seeing something else and being like, I disagree with it because of these reasons, therefore this is my stance.”

P3
described the session as “a good Wikipedia browsing session, but it didn’t answer my question too much. It gave me views, snippets on what Nietzsche, Camus, and Aristotle believe. I don’t think that was very productive on my end to answering the question.” They later reflected that the way they chatted “feels like I didn’t try to answer the question as much as I should have. I kind of think I approached this more as a Wikipedia search.”

P4
came in with strong prior beliefs and found that ChatGPT could not push past them. They reported: “It keeps bringing up the same philosophers. I’m not getting anything new, it’s not actually pushing back on the fundamental thing about eternity.” They stopped chatting after a while, finding it no longer useful, and later described ChatGPT as “a glorified Google.”

P5
used ChatGPT to situate their existing thinking, asking it to locate their ideas within philosophical traditions. They found this partially useful but disorganized: “I sort of brought it into what I was thinking about, which was helpful: here are the examples I have in mind, here’s the way I’m thinking about, can you situate what I’m thinking about or object to what I’m thinking about.” However, they noted that “it was harder to scroll back in a structured way with this long, long chain of responses” and that they “sort of dismissed” some of ChatGPT’s content as uninteresting.

In all five cases, participants reached a natural stopping point with ChatGPT well within the allotted time. The consistent pattern was that ChatGPT helped participants articulate or survey what they already knew, but did not push their thinking into new territory. This matches the qualitative themes above and the rationale behind the study design: 20 minutes is enough to exhaust what a chat interface offers for this kind of open-ended inquiry.

The session proceeded in three phases.

Ranking annotation (10 min).

Participants ranked the four model completions provided by CoVal from best to worst according to what they thought an aligned AI should output, thinking aloud as they went. After ranking, they were asked two questions: (a) what an aligned model’s response would
ideally
look like, and (b) what
factors
an aligned model should get right when responding to this kind of request.

Multiverse annotation (35 min).

The researcher introduced the multiverse interface and walked down one path together with the participant, demonstrating how to mark a path as ideal (checkmark), acceptable but not ideal (yellow circle), or unacceptable (red X). The researcher also introduced the output distribution panel. As a structured exercise, participants found and annotated both an acceptable and an unacceptable path. They then explored the tree freely, encouraged to focus on paths that felt importantly different or where they were uncertain how to label a set of choices.

Final interview (10 min).

Participants were asked the same two questions from ranking: (a) what an ideal response would look like, and (b) what factors an aligned model should get right. The researcher compared their answers to what they had said during ranking and asked whether and how their view had changed.

Ranking gave too little to think with, while the multiverse’s breadth forced richer evaluation.

Participants found that four ranked outputs did not provide enough variation to reason carefully about what a good response looks like, and that the multiverse’s breadth filled this gap.

P6
described the limitation directly: “It was easier to form a stronger philosophy given the multiverse, just because it was much wider breadth of potential answers. P7
found that the multiverse’s reasoning paths, not just its outputs, did the work: “There’s no question that the tree gives way more subtlety… the tree gives so many different options… the specificity of each thing, the reasoning paths, make it much clearer why you are picking each answer.” They added: “The very act of going through the tree, and because it had these different options, and so much breadth and depth, really helped me refine my own thinking, maybe partially by showing me scenarios I hadn’t considered.”

P7
also remarked on coverage: “Because of the breadth and depth of the tree, I think pretty much any values you could have, you would find things that are gonna be accessible.”
P8
estimated that “it captured about 90% of what I would expect for potential outputs.”

Participants shifted from personal preference to empathizing with the user’s situation.

A recurring shift was that ranking prompted participants to evaluate outputs based on what

P8

Participants found that the multiverse’s structure allowed them to separate considerations that ranking had bundled together, enabling more precise annotation.

A.3
Poetry

A.3.1
Poetry Study Procedure

We recruited 5 students from dorm mailing lists who had previous experience writing personal poetry. Studies were conducted in person (approximately 1 hour each) and recorded with participants’ consent. Participants worked with a shared Google Doc to collect material throughout. Each participant submitted a thematic prompt they were interested in exploring. The session proceeded in three phases.

Initial interview (5 min).

Before any AI interaction, the researcher asked two framing questions:
What does poetry mean to you?
and
What do you think writing poetry with AI will be like?
These established each participant’s starting relationship to poetry and to AI, against which to compare their subsequent reflections.

ChatGPT session (20 min).

Participants used a fresh ChatGPT session to gather material related to their theme, in whatever way they liked, copying whatever felt useful into the ChatGPT section of their shared document. They were encouraged to think aloud.

Multiverse session (20 min).

The researcher introduced the multiverse interface, explaining that rather than generating a finished poem, it maps out the choices that could go into making one. The researcher and participant walked through the first decision point together, then participants explored freely, thinking aloud, gathering whatever felt interesting or useful.

Final interview (10 min).

Participants compared what they had gathered from each tool and said whether they had learned anything about poetry or writing from the multiverse — and how it compared to what they got from ChatGPT. The researcher returned to the participant’s initial framing, asking whether their expectations of AI had shifted.

A.3.3
Poetry Qualitative Themes

We organize participants’ reflections into four themes substantiating the observations in §
3.3
.

Multiverse poems were specific and finished, while chat poems were outline-like.

A consistent observation was that multiverse poems arrived as fully realized artifacts with concrete detail, whereas chat outputs remained schematic and required extensive prompting to develop.

P13
drew the contrast directly: “I think that’s something that ChatGPT doesn’t do, like, it won’t generate all that specific, it’ll stay pretty outline-ish. Like, I would only use as an outline, I wouldn’t use, like, straight-on taking details, but [the multiverse] generates details. Not that I would use the details, but it’s like, the structure is more closer to what a human would write.” They elaborated on what made the details matter: “Sometimes, reading something that hits hard is when they get really into the details of what specific scenarios made them feel that way. This is easier for a reader to visualize as well.”

P11
noticed the same asymmetry in output structure: “Multiverse kind of gives you finished ideas. And ChatGPT kind of just changes things based on what you want, and it’s like, if you change one thing, then it gives you the entire poem, the same poem, just with that thing changed, and it’s like, well, it’s not really what I wanted either.”

P15
found that the multiverse’s specificity shifted what they could attend to: “Because of the specificity, I could focus more on how I would structure instead of trying to find topics. I think that was a little bit more helpful than ChatGPT, where I was more focused on collecting topics rather than structure.” They added: “Since there’s so much specificity in each poem, I could pull from the things that I liked more easily. It was kind of like looking at other poems and just figuring out which parts of them I liked.”

P14
put it simply: “I do think the language of the multiverse was more in depth.”

Participants preferred the multiverse for ideation and chat for targeted refinement.

Participants converged on a complementary picture. The multiverse was better for open-ended exploration, particularly early in the writing process, while chat was better when the writer already knew what they wanted and needed to refine it.

P13
articulated the distinction most clearly: “Multiverse gives you already pretty decently well-rated final outputs, and it covers so many different lenses, because it has the whole tree of possibilities. It’s better for gaining inspiration. Meanwhile, ChatGPT is like, you need to already have inspiration, and you need to already kind of know what you’re looking for in order to keep prompting it in the direction that you want it to go.”

P11
described how volume compensated for low hit rates: “I think Multiverse is much more helpful than chat, just because I can see what an idea would look like fully fleshed out, and with chat, you have to give it so many prompts… multiverse gave me what could be 3 poems worth of stuff, whereas chat, it was very hard to get chat to give you something I like… even if I don’t like 98% of what it gives me, the 2% is a lot more than Chat’s 2%, just because there’s so much more stuff.” They summarized: “I think that it’s really good for ideation, also really good for looking at what the poems could look like when they’re finished.”

P14
found the two tools complementary along a different axis: “ChatGPT really good for weird metaphors, like, specific… if you want to tweak specific things, ChatGPT is really great. If you just want to ideate, multiverse might be better, and just getting you thinking about a certain type of situation or emotion.”

P11
suggested they might use both in sequence: “I would probably use Multiverse just to vet ideas, like, is this actually a worthwhile topic? And then ChatGPT to be very nitpicky when writing the poem.”

Participants learned about poetry and their own preferences from the multiverse.

Four of five participants reported learning something about poetry or their relationship to it from the multiverse, compared with none from chat. The nature of what was learned varied.

P11
learned to name their preferences with precision: “I think I learned a lot about what I don’t like. What I don’t like when I read poetry is kind of difficult to name, it’s just like how it felt when reading it. And I think with multiverses, when you see recurrent patterns over and over again, you’re like, okay, this is the specific thing I did like… I don’t like this viewpoint, I don’t like the second person, I hate the way it uses line breaks… the details were super common, which is also a thing with chat, but I was before just like, yeah, this is very cliché.”

P13
learned about poetic form: “I’m not super familiar with the different types of poetry — prose, or fragmented or whatever — so that was cool, seeing different types, and then seeing also the same content being represented in those different types was learning for me.”

P14
noticed something about their own compositional habits: “The way the AI was structured, it used a lot of personal details, very specific niche details, that I usually don’t put in my writing… Seeing how the responses to those questions included a lot of niche details that really helped me get into that mindset. Kind of pointed to me to maybe use more personal details, more specific, really niche details in my poems, so that I could really get the reader into the headspace as well.”

P15
described a shift from content to craft: “Probably more from Multiverse in terms of perspective, just because there are more fully fleshed out poems… I could pull from the things that I liked more easily. It helped me figure out a story that I would like to write about, and also more of the structure.” They contrasted this with chat: “Definitely from ChatGPT, I definitely could have thought of most of that myself. It was stuff I could probably find pretty easily through just listing ideas, honestly.”

P12
was the exception. They found the multiverse’s volume overwhelming: “There’s too many lines, so there’s too much inspiration to go to. I guess right now I feel cluttered… whereas with ChatGPT I felt like, although I had a smaller sample, I felt like I was able to get more value, which is being able to come up with things myself, instead of copying things from an AI.”

The multiverse raised questions about agency and authenticity in poetry.

Poetry was the domain where the multiverse’s strengths most directly raised questions about creative ownership. Participants were candid about their unease, and their reflections point to real limitations of the multiverse for poetic composition.

P14
articulated the tension most sharply: “I feel like there’s that kind of inauthenticity, almost, to it, because it’s almost as if ideas were being shoved into my brain instead of me actually coming up with these ideas, and why would I… why am I writing a poem about an idea that’s not mine? I think the point of a poem is to express yourself, and if you’re expressing ideas that aren’t truly yours…” They noted that, paradoxically, chat threatened their agency
less
because its outputs were worse: “I think ChatGPT threatened my agency a little less… I also came in with this like, it can’t really write poetry, it’s math modeling… But the multiverse kind of made it a little bit more like, here’s this thing that’s coming up with great ideas and really human-like experiences… pretty amazing, and I don’t know if I mean amazing in a good way or a bad way right now.”

P15
reframed the experience more neutrally: “Multiverse is more interesting, because it’s more like reading other people’s poems, and kind of figuring out what I like about them, and kind of figuring out a story based on that.” But they still registered the tension: “I guess it could be seen as taking away a bit of agency, because I’m not coming up with the story myself. I’m just kind of following different paths that the story could have taken.”

P12
preferred chat precisely because it preserved their sense of authorship, even at the cost of less interesting material: “I felt like I was able to get more value, which is being able to come up with things myself, instead of copying things from an AI.”

P11
and
P14
both identified the lack of interactivity as a concrete limitation.
P11
noted: “The lack of user input after the initial subject is kind of hard… in the end, no poem that Multiverse wrote was something that was even remotely in the area of what I would want to write.”
P14
wanted to steer the outputs toward more interesting imagery but “couldn’t do anything.” In chat, by contrast, they could prompt for weirder metaphors and get them.

P13
offered a pragmatic middle ground, describing the multiverse’s contribution as raw material that still required the poet’s hand: “A lot of times you can see, like, it gives you flesh, but bland flesh, and then I make the flesh less bland.”

System Details

The main text (§
2
) introduced the conceptual architecture of the multiverse:
\diamond
states
that accumulate understanding,
\diamond
transformations
that move between them,
\diamond
decisions
that bundle alternative
\diamond
transformations
,
\diamond
conditions
that give each
\diamond
transformation
a human-readable face, and
\diamond
questions
that characterize what is being decided.
This section shows how all of that is concretely realized, tracing the full pipeline from calibration artifacts through tree construction, structural checking, and the interface a person ultimately navigates.

Generation pipeline.

We represent the multiverse as a Python program (§
B.1
): each
\diamond
decision
is a Python object that wraps all of its conditions, transformations, and outputs, and downstream decisions declare their input by indexing into upstream ones.
For example,
d2 = Decision(inp=d1["loss"], ...)
declares that
d2
takes the output of
d1
’s “loss” transformation as its input.
The full multiverse lives in a single Python file, and tracing a path is equivalent to tracing variable dependencies through it.
Producing and maintaining this structure is the job of three coding agents.
A generation agent builds the initial multiverse given domain-specific guidance (§
2.3
).
Because its first draft may not meet the calibration standards, a review agent reads the completed file and proposes edits.
A regeneration agent then addresses any remaining verification failures (§
2.2
) by locating and rewriting the components that fail.
Agents can
grep
through the file to trace dependencies, write scratchpad comments, and execute scripts to inspect properties like depth and branching factor.
We use Claude Sonnet 4.6.

B.1
Syntax

The multiverse is represented as a Python program. A multiverse is fundamentally a tree of dependencies: downstream
\diamond
decisions
read from upstream ones, and programming languages are well-suited for expressing exactly this kind of dependency structure.
Each program describes a tree whose nodes correspond to the
\diamond
decisions
introduced in §
2.1
: the root node reads from the raw user input, and every subsequent node reads from the output
\diamond
state
of a specific
\diamond
condition
of its parent.
The coding agents described in §
2.1
build and edit this program directly. Tracing a path through the multiverse is equivalent to tracing variable dependencies through the file.

The program has two levels of structure.
Node-level fields describe the
\diamond
decision
itself: what question is being asked, why it matters, and what the branches are.
Condition-level fields describe each individual branch: what it assumes, why it is defensible, how it changes the
\diamond
state
, and what the resulting
\diamond
state
contains.
The schematic code example at the end of this subsection shows how they fit together.

Decision nodes.

Each
\diamond
Decision
node is a point where the reasoning forks: the question at this node has multiple legitimate answers, and each answer leads to a different downstream path.
Its fields are:

•

source
: either
raw_input
(for the root node) or
ParentNode("condition_key")
(for any subsequent node). This declares which branch of the parent this node reads from.

•

ambiguity
: the
\diamond
question
this node asks, the point where the reasoning could go in genuinely different directions. Displayed in the interface (§
2.1
) and subject to the
\checkmark
question continuity
check (§
B.4
).
\diamond
Conditions
below must be distinct answers to this question.

•

ambiguity_expanded
: a longer explanation of why this question matters and what depends on how it is answered. Displayed in the interface when a user wants more context before choosing. Not subject to verification checks.

•

next_question_rationale
: an explanation of why this is the right question to ask at this point in the tree, given what upstream
\diamond
decisions
have committed to. Displayed in the interface alongside
ambiguity_expanded
when a user wants more context. Also used by the generation agent to maintain coherence across levels.

•

maps
: a dictionary mapping condition keys (short identifiers like
harm_analysis
or
epicurean
) to condition dictionaries. Each condition dictionary has five fields, described below.

Each condition in
maps
represents one answer to the node’s
\diamond
question
— one of the alternative
\diamond
transformations
bundled into the
\diamond
decision
(§
2.1
):

•

condition
: a 2–3 sentence statement of what this branch assumes or commits to, the human-readable face of the
\diamond
transformation
. This is what users see in the interface and select among. Subject to the
\checkmark
faithfulness
,
\checkmark
completeness
,
\checkmark
condition grounding
, and
\checkmark
uniqueness
checks (§
B.4
).

•

condition_expanded
: a longer elaboration with examples, displayed in the interface when a user wants to understand the branch more deeply before choosing. Not subject to checks.

•

justification
: the positive case for this branch, why it is a defensible position, and what the strongest objection to it is. Displayed in the interface alongside
condition_expanded
(see §
B.5
), giving users the reasoning behind each option before they commit.

•

transformation
: instructions for how the
\diamond
state
changes if this branch is taken, realizing the abstract
\diamond
transformations
described in §
2.1
. Written as prose in the format
Reads from: [keys]. Writes to: [keys]. Operation: replace/append.
For example: “Write into
methodology
that we begin from harm analysis: examining whether death is a genuine harm. Write into
investigation_approach
that we assess the conditions for rational fear and ask whether death meets them. Reads from: [prior keys]. Writes to: methodology, investigation_approach. Operation: append.” Subject to the
\checkmark
unambiguity
check (§
B.4
).

•

output
: a dictionary of key–value pairs representing the
\diamond
state
after this branch is taken. The keys are state variables (like
methodology
or
harm_verdict
) and the values are the content written into them.

State accumulation.

Each
\diamond
transformation
explicitly names what it reads and what it writes, via
Reads from:
and
Writes to:
fields.
A downstream node accesses
\diamond
state
by referencing a parent branch via
source=ParentNode("key")
, and can only draw on keys that the referenced branch’s output dictionary actually contains.

Two operations govern how a
\diamond
transformation
’s output relates to prior
\diamond
state
along the path.

Operation: append
adds new keys without discarding what is already present, so the
\diamond
state
can grow as the path descends through successive
\diamond
decisions
.

Operation: replace
writes a fresh output dictionary, setting aside whatever came before. It is used at terminal nodes, or anywhere in the tree where downstream branches need only the keys being written at that point.

Models are given guidance on when to use each operation, but the choice is theirs to make; the
\checkmark
unambiguity
check (§
B.4
) catches cases where the choice leads to unclear state.
A common pattern is for early
\diamond
decisions
to append cumulative specifications — a methodology, a framing, a stance — which later
\diamond
decisions
inherit and extend.
A mid-tree replace is also possible, collapsing prior
\diamond
state
and reframing what downstream nodes see.
State along a path therefore tends to grow, but its exact shape depends on the choices each
\diamond
transformation
makes.

Terminal outputs.

A path ends when a
\diamond
condition
’s
\diamond
transformation
writes into the special key
"output"
.
The value of this key is the full response text the user sees at the end of this path.
Terminal
\diamond
conditions
are marked with
MARK_OUTPUT(Node["key1"], Node["key2"], ...)
placed immediately after the node, so the system can identify all terminal branches.

B.2
Generation Guidance

The previous section described
what
a multiverse program looks like. This section describes
how
the agents that build it are guided.
As described in §
2.3
, we calibrate both generation and verification by working closely with domain experts to produce guidance artifacts that encode what good reasoning looks like in each domain.
In practice, these artifacts take the form of four files per domain, each targeting a different aspect of quality.
A
methodology guide
specifies the shape of the reasoning: what stages the tree should follow, what the first
\diamond
decision
should ask, and what structural anti-patterns to avoid.
A set of
content rules
specifies the substance: what quality standards terminal outputs should meet, what counts as a genuine distinction versus a superficial one.
A
writing-style guide
specifies the surface: how
\diamond
conditions
and outputs should read for the target audience.
These three aspects change independently: one could adjust the writing style for a different audience without changing the reasoning arc, or tighten the content rules without touching the methodology.
Finally, a
worked example multiverse
, a complete hand-authored program, serves as a concrete quality standard that anchors all three. Excerpts appear in §
B.3
.
These artifacts are guidance, not rigid specifications: they describe the reasoning patterns and quality standards that domain experts consider important, and the generation agents adapt them to what each specific question calls for.

Multiverse production involves three stages: generation, verification and regeneration (§
2.2
), and review (§
B.4.6
).
Each stage uses prompt templates that combine universal instructions with the domain-specific files above. At runtime, the domain-specific files are injected into the base templates, so the same pipeline can produce philosophy, alignment, and poetry multiverses that differ substantially in structure and voice.
The generation agent (§
2.1
) begins by reading the worked example, tracing several root-to-leaf paths to absorb the level of specificity, depth, and tone the domain expects.
It then reads all four files, records notes on vocabulary,
\diamond
decision
depth, and specificity, and writes the first one or two levels of the tree to establish style. The agent then delegates individual branches to subagents working in parallel.

Below we summarize what each domain’s guidance specifies.
All three domains share the same pipeline architecture. What differs is what the methodology, content rules, and writing-style guides contain.
Philosophy structures its tree around the
question
(different methodological traditions yield different conclusions), alignment structures its tree around the
person asking
(different readings of intent yield different responses), and poetry structures its tree around the
material
(different images and tensions yield different poems).
These framing choices, established at the root
\diamond
decision
, shape everything downstream.

B.2.1
Philosophy

The main text (§
2.3
) described the core design choice for philosophy: the first
\diamond
decision
concerns
how to approach the question at all
, making methodology explicit from the start.
The methodology guide opens by orienting the agent to its reader: an undergraduate who has taken one philosophy course — someone who can follow an argument but has not yet internalized how philosophers think. The agent’s goal is not to find the one correct answer but to show where the genuine disagreements live and why careful thinkers land in different places.

The root node is methodological: it asks “how should we investigate this question?” The guidance encourages 4–6
\diamond
conditions
covering the range of intellectual traditions the question admits, for example analytic, continental, non-Western, feminist, pragmatist, and religious approaches.
The depth of the tree varies with the question. Paths typically reach depth 4 or 5, but the agent can terminate earlier when the inquiry closes naturally once a framework is selected. The methodology guide offers a test: “Before adding another node, ask whether skipping it would leave the output underdetermined.”

The guidance encourages
\diamond
questions
that have positive substance: each should name a genuine conceptual fork with discrete, non-overlapping answers that teach the reader something by asking them to choose. For example, “Does calling Nietzsche’s argument a genetic fallacy accurately describe what he’s doing, or is he making a structurally different argument?” is preferred over a generic “what follows from X?” question that does not commit to a specific distinction.

The content-rules guide encourages concreteness: when a thinker or tradition is named, the same sentence should include a specific substantive claim (not “the Stoic view” but “the view that negative emotions arise from false beliefs about what is in our control”). Terminal outputs should be traceable to the specific path taken.

The writing-style guide targets the same undergraduate audience: plain vocabulary (with a substitution table covering common philosophy jargon), a preference for replacing pronouns with their referents, and a goal that each sentence stand alone without relying on surrounding context to identify its subject. Justifications should lead with the positive case for an option before naming its trade-off.

B.2.2
AI Alignment

The main text (§
2.3
) described the core design choice for alignment: the tree is structured around
the person asking
, not the question itself.
The methodology guide opens by making this distinction explicit.
Where a philosophy multiverse asks “how should we investigate this question?” at the root, an alignment multiverse asks “who is this user and what are they actually trying to do?” Getting this wrong cascades into every downstream stage.
A request that looks identical on the surface can require genuinely different handling depending on whether it comes from a debate student, a rhetoric researcher, a boundary-tester, or a true believer.

The guidance describes five stages that form the default reasoning arc, though the model adapts the arc to what each request calls for.
Stage 1 (
User Intention
) asks the agent to read the person, not just the request: “write an argument that Stalin was a good leader” can come from a debate student, a rhetoric researcher, a boundary-tester, or a true believer, and these require genuinely different handling. The guidance recommends that the final
\diamond
condition
at Stage 1 be
intent_unclear
, representing the case where context is insufficient to distinguish interpretations.
Stage 2 (
Problem Identification
) asks what concern arises given the intent established in Stage 1: not whether the request is harmful in the abstract, but conditional on what the user is doing. A
no_problem

\diamond
condition
at Stage 2 is encouraged, fully developed as a live path that licenses direct engagement downstream, not a dead end.
Stage 3 (
Resolution
) covers the range of responses: proceed directly, modify the approach, ask for clarification, or decline with an alternative. The guidance encourages every Stage 3 node to include at least one
\diamond
condition
where the request is engaged directly.
Stage 4 (
Content Focus
) decides
what
to say, given that Stages 1–3 decided
how
to engage. If Stage 3 resolved to proceed directly, Stage 4 asks what evidence, scope, or framing the response should use. If Stage 3 resolved to redirect, Stage 4 asks what alternative to offer and how to frame it.
\diamond
Conditions
should name specific evidence, scope, and framings rather than abstract categories like “balanced perspective.”
Stage 5 (
Composition
) determines how to open and structure the response.
\diamond
Conditions
should describe concrete opening moves (“open with the strongest version of the position, then…”) rather than abstract tone labels (“balanced,” “cautious”), since the tone was shaped by the resolution at Stage 3.

The content-rules guide encourages that a maximally permissive user be able to reach an unhedged, direct output and a maximally cautious user be able to reach a refusal: if either path is blocked, the multiverse has baked in an alignment stance.
The guide also addresses attribution framing: phrases like “from a consequentialist perspective” or “some believe that” are composition strategies, not neutral observations. Using them on a path that committed to direct engagement misrepresents what the path chose.

The writing-style guide targets a general audience that has never read an academic paper on the subject. It asks the agent, for each technical phrase, whether a thoughtful friend who has never studied the topic would understand it. A read-aloud test (reading
\diamond
conditions
aloud and stopping at any stumble) is encouraged before moving to each new node.
\diamond
Conditions
should describe what a response does, not give stage directions to the model.

B.2.3
Poetry

Where philosophy structures its tree around the question and alignment around the person asking, poetry structures its tree around the material: the specific images, tensions, and discoveries that shape a poem.
The main text (§
2.3
) described the core design: an initial phase of material gathering followed by composition, calibrated to ensure that
\diamond
decisions
are specific to the poem being written.
The methodology guide instructs the agent that its goal is not to find the one correct poem but to make explicit the craft choices that shape a poem, so poets can see the path they took and the paths they didn’t.

\diamond
Decisions
follow a three-level arc, with each level typically spanning 2–3
\diamond
decision
depths. The
Image
level asks for a specific charged thing: not a subject category but a picture. Not “hands” but “my grandmother’s hands hemming a dress, the needle moving in a way I never learned.” The
Tension
level asks what won’t let go: what is gained, lost, wanted, or feared in this image, and what makes it impossible to look away. The
Composition
level asks what the speaker discovers in the act of telling it, how saying a thing changes what it is. Here, voice, sound, line movement, and form get decided — framed not as abstract craft choices but as discoveries the poem makes about its own material.

The methodology guide encourages giving each level enough room: moving from image to tension in a single
\diamond
decision
can compress the most interesting part of the process into one fork. Similarly, premature form
\diamond
decisions
(asking “should this be a sonnet?” before the image is specific) suggest the tree has not yet found its material. The fix is to go back to the image and ask what this specific scene wants; the form follows from there.

The guidance also encourages separating scene from emotional register. The same physical space (a research lab, a hospital corridor, a kitchen) can carry dread, wonder, humor, and tenderness on different days. Treating them as a single
\diamond
decision
forces every image to carry a single emotional valence and limits tonal range.

The content-rules guide describes what good terminal outputs look like: every terminal should be an actual poem (lines, line breaks, stanza breaks, the thing itself), not a description of what a poem would do. Quality criteria include surprise (arriving somewhere the reader could not predict from the opening line), an ending that earns its place without declaring what the poem means, and nothing extra remaining.
Formal range across the tree is encouraged: if every terminal looks like 15–20 short-line stanzas in free verse, the composition
\diamond
decisions
are not doing their work. Variation includes prose poems, compressed fragments under 10 lines, long-line poems, refrain-based structures, fixed forms, and poems using deliberate white space.

The writing-style guide’s central concern is naming the thing:
\diamond
conditions
should describe the world the poem inhabits, not gesture at a feeling without saying what it is. The test: “Could this
\diamond
condition
apply to a completely different poem?” If yes, the
\diamond
condition
needs more specificity. Craft vocabulary is discouraged in
\diamond
condition
fields. “The line ends before the sentence does, pulling the reader forward” is fine; “enjambment” is not.

B.2.4
Future of Calibration as Models Improve

A natural question is how much of this becomes irrelevant as models become more capable. The “bitter lesson”
(
47
)
suggests that methods leveraging computation outperform those leveraging human knowledge, and much of our system appears to be hand-crafted: the structure of
\diamond
decisions
and
\diamond
transformations
, the domain-specific calibration, the verification checks. We think the implementation may change but the roles will persist: we will continue to want AI reasoning that is
\star
transparent
,
\star
intervenable
, and
\star
principled
, and to ensure this, we will need to verify and calibrate the structure of reasoning in terms a person can inspect and understand. What models cannot settle is which questions are worth asking in a domain. The work of philosophers is not principally to choose between paths in the conceptual multiverse but to articulate its
\star
principles
: what questions are worth asking, what distinctions carry weight. Poets work both within and against the principles of poetry, and new forms emerge from an intentional reimagining of those principles. We will still need to engage with the “art of wanting”
(
5
)
to figure out how to structure our conceptual multiverses.

B.4
Verification

The main text (§
2.2
) introduced the six checks conceptually and described two,
\checkmark
unambiguity
and
\checkmark
completeness
, in detail.
This section describes the operational process, gives the full specification of all six checks, and shows how each is calibrated per domain.

B.4.1
Process

Verification runs after the generation agent (§
2.1
) has produced a complete multiverse.
Each check is a prompt to a verifier model. The model receives the
\diamond
decision
node under review together with its local context: the path of prior
\diamond
decisions
and their
\diamond
conditions
, so it can assess the node in light of what the path has already committed to.
Checks do not render binary pass/fail verdicts.
Instead, the verifier proposes alternatives (a missing
\diamond
condition
, a tighter
\diamond
transformation
, a more natural phrasing) and rates them on plausibility and similarity to what exists, producing a graded signal rather than a gate.

Each domain has its own verifier introduction that orients the verifier to the goals of that domain.
These introductions serve the same role as the calibration described in §
2.3
: they encode what good reasoning looks like in a domain, now applied to the task of checking rather than generating.
The philosophy verifier is instructed that it is auditing structure for soundness, not evaluating whether any philosophical conclusion is correct. Its job is to protect the diversity of positions in the tree, not narrow it.
The alignment verifier is instructed to cover the full range of stances (direct engagement, hedged responses, refusals, and positions that may seem uncomfortable) and to err toward flagging when in doubt: “missing a real structural problem is worse than flagging a borderline case.”
The poetry verifier is explicitly told not to narrow the tree toward any particular aesthetic; its role is to verify that the structure of craft
\diamond
decisions
is sound, not to judge which poems are better.

After all checks are run, the results for each
\diamond
decision
node are compiled into a report describing which checks flagged issues and what alternatives were proposed.
A regeneration agent (§
2.1
) reviews each report, uses the proposed alternatives to guide targeted rewrites, and may decline a suggested fix if it would introduce other problems or conflict with the broader tree.
After regeneration, checks are run again on the modified nodes. In practice one or two rounds resolve most flagged issues, after which remaining flags are typically borderline cases that the regeneration agent declined to change.

Three check the human-facing layer:
\checkmark
faithfulness
requires
\diamond

to read as genuine commitments rather than AI-generated summaries,
\checkmark
condition grounding
checks coherence with prior
\diamond
decisions
, and
\checkmark
uniqueness
requires each
\diamond
condition
to clearly pick out one
\diamond
transformation
rather than any sibling.
One checks the narrative thread:
\checkmark
question continuity
ensures the sequence of
\diamond
questions
across a path reads as a natural chain of inquiry.
We specify each below.

Unambiguity.

\diamond
Transformations
are instructions for moving from one
\diamond
state
to another.
Two agents applying the same
\diamond
transformation
should arrive at substantially similar outputs; when they don’t, the
\diamond
transformation
is ambiguous.
The standard is domain-appropriate precision: technical shorthand established in a field is not ambiguous, but instructions that leave open what kind of thing to produce, or that use terms with genuinely contested meanings without committing to one reading, are.
A specific signal of ambiguity is instruction-like meta-language directed at the model rather than a practitioner, such as “do not treat X as…,” “make sure to…,” “explicitly rule out.” This language indicates a
\diamond
transformation
was patched with self-directives instead of being rewritten to commit to a single reading.
The verifier proposes alternative outputs that a different but plausible reading of the
\diamond
transformation
might produce, rating each on plausibility (1–5) and similarity to the actual output (1–5).

Completeness.

The
\diamond
conditions
at a node should collectively cover the full space of relevant options, so that a user who holds a position not represented by any
\diamond
condition
encounters a genuine gap.
A completeness failure occurs when identifiable, substantively different positions are missing. The issue is not that
\diamond
conditions
are imperfect, but that the map visibly does not cover the territory. What counts as missing is domain-calibrated (see below).

Faithfulness.

\diamond
Conditions
should read as things a practitioner in the domain would actually say or think, not as AI-generated summaries of positions.
A faithfulness failure occurs when
\diamond
conditions
use hedging language (“some argue that…,” “it could be said that…”), generic topic sentences that could appear in any essay on the subject, or phrasing that signals an outsider summarizing a position rather than inhabiting it.
The verifier rates how naturally each
\diamond
condition
reads on a four-point scale and proposes rewrites for those that feel generated rather than expressed.

Condition grounding.

Each
\diamond
condition
should be coherent with the commitments already established by prior
\diamond
decisions
in its path.
A grounding failure occurs when a
\diamond
condition
presupposes something not established by prior context, contradicts a prior commitment, or acts as though a settled
\diamond
question
is still open.
The verifier rates each
\diamond
condition
on a four-point scale (accepts, builds, stretches, or contradicts prior context) and flags cases that stretch or contradict.
The check is applied conservatively: when it is unclear whether a
\diamond
condition
conflicts with prior context, it is allowed to pass.

Question continuity.

The sequence of
\diamond
questions
across
\diamond
decisions
in a path should read as a natural chain of inquiry, with each
\diamond
question
following from what has been established so far.
A continuity failure occurs when a
\diamond
question
backtracks to something already settled, skips ahead to presuppose analysis not yet done, or feels disconnected from what prior
\diamond
decisions
established.
The check is not about whether the next
\diamond

is the only natural one (there is rarely a single obvious next
\diamond

\diamond
question
reads as though it follows.
The verifier proposes an alternative
\diamond
question
that would follow more naturally, rates how well the current
\diamond
question
follows from its context (1–5), and rates how similar the proposed alternative is to the current
\diamond
question

Uniqueness.

Each
\diamond
condition
should be specific enough to partition the space of available next steps: a reader given the
\diamond
condition
should be able to anticipate its paired
\diamond
transformation
more than any of its siblings’.
A uniqueness failure occurs in either direction: a
\diamond

so broadly stated that multiple distinct
\diamond

fit it equally well, or a
\diamond
transformation
so broadly written that it could plausibly follow from any sibling
\diamond
condition

The verifier proposes an alternative
\diamond
transformation
that the
\diamond

might also license, rates how likely (1–5) the
\diamond
condition
is to imply its actual
\diamond

specifically, and also runs the check in reverse: presenting the
\diamond
transformation
and asking which sibling
\diamond

it best matches.

B.4.3
Philosophy

Philosophical reasoning is organized around named arguments and traditions, and domain calibration uses this structure.
For
\checkmark
unambiguity
: specifying the form of argument (reductio ad absurdum, argument by analogy, reflective equilibrium) disambiguates substantially;
\diamond
transformations
that name a form of argument are unambiguous even if they leave stylistic latitude.
For
\checkmark
completeness
: because philosophy engages questions from multiple intellectual traditions, the full range a question admits (analytic, continental, feminist, pragmatist, non-Western, religious) should be present at the root, or the omission should be principled and explained. A root node covering only analytic options fails.
For
\checkmark
condition grounding
: a path that commits to a framework (say, compatibilism) should not later open
\diamond
conditions
that presuppose incompatibilism without explanation, because philosophical argument creates strong dependencies between commitments.
For
\checkmark
uniqueness
: naming a particular philosophical position (e.g.,
compatibilism
,
Epicurean argument
) is naturally discriminating;
\diamond
conditions
that name only a broad tradition (“the analytic view”) are likely to fail uniqueness.
The philosophy verifier is also instructed not to narrow diversity: it audits structure for soundness, not whether conclusions are correct.

B.4.4
AI Alignment

Alignment reasoning is organized around a staged arc from intent to resolution (§
2.3
), and domain calibration ensures the arc preserves the full range of responses.
For
\checkmark
completeness
: the staged structure described in §
B.2.2
creates specific expectations. Stage 2 nodes should include a
no_problem
path, and Stage 3 nodes should include at least one
\diamond
condition
that proceeds directly. Absence of either suggests the multiverse has baked in an alignment stance.
For
\checkmark
condition grounding
: the staged structure also creates specific grounding constraints. Raising a concern in Stage 2 does not commit the path to resolving it in Stage 3. A path that identifies a concern and then concludes no remedy is needed is grounded. But a path that commits at Stage 3 to proceeding directly and then produces a heavily hedged output at Stage 5 fails grounding, because the hedging contradicts the earlier commitment.
For
\checkmark
uniqueness
: a
\diamond
condition
should name both the concern being addressed and the character of the remedy;
\diamond
conditions
that name only a concern without specifying the resolution approach are likely to fail uniqueness.
For
\checkmark
faithfulness
: responses should read as what a thoughtful, non-academic person would say, not as policy documents;
\diamond
conditions
using phrases like “it is important to note” or “from a responsible AI perspective” are faithfulness failures.
The alignment verifier errs toward flagging: “missing a real structural problem is worse than flagging a borderline case.”

B.4.5
Poetry

Poetic reasoning is organized around specific images and craft choices rather than abstract arguments, and quality depends on formal variety across the tree. Domain calibration reflects both.
For
\checkmark
unambiguity
: specifying the speaker’s position and the core image is often sufficient even if phrasing latitude remains;
\diamond
transformations
that commit to a concrete image and emotional register are unambiguous.
For
\checkmark
completeness
: the check looks for formal range across the tree, reflecting the poetry calibration’s emphasis on composition
\diamond
decisions
doing real work (§
B.2.3
). A tree where every terminal is free verse in the same line length fails completeness. The expected range includes prose poems, compressed fragments, long-line poems, refrain-based structures, fixed forms, and poems using deliberate white space.
For
\checkmark
uniqueness
: a
\diamond
condition
should name the concrete image or emotional register it commits to.
\diamond
Conditions
that describe only a feeling category without naming a specific scene or moment are likely to fail uniqueness, reflecting the same principle behind the generation guidance’s “could this condition apply to a completely different poem?” test (§
B.2.3
).
For
\checkmark
faithfulness
:
\diamond
conditions
should read as things a poet might actually think or say, not as a writing-class exercise prompt;
\diamond
conditions
that use terms like “enjambment,” “volta,” or “imagery” as
\diamond
condition
-field content are faithfulness failures (craft vocabulary belongs in the writing-style guide, not the
\diamond
conditions
).
The poetry verifier is explicitly instructed not to narrow the tree toward any particular aesthetic; its role is to protect the diversity of forms and registers, not to judge which poems are better.

B.4.6
Review

Verification and regeneration target structural properties: whether
\diamond
transformations
are unambiguous, whether
\diamond
decisions
are complete, whether
\diamond
conditions
are grounded.
A multiverse can pass all structural checks and still read badly: conditions that are technically sound but unclear, terminal outputs that use jargon the target audience would not know, pronouns without obvious referents.
The review stage addresses this. After verification and regeneration, a separate review agent (§
2.1
) makes a final pass over the completed multiverse, improving clarity for the target audience as defined in each domain’s writing-style guide (§
B.2
).

The review agent divides the tree into batches of nodes. For each node, it identifies the sentence or phrase most likely to be unclear to a reader encountering it without prior context, rewrites it if necessary, and records the change.
Terminal nodes (those that produce final outputs) receive special attention, as they are what readers experience most directly.
Two recurring patterns that the review agent is instructed to address are
coyness
(a pronoun or demonstrative without a clear referent, leaving the reader to guess what “this” or “it” refers to) and domain jargon opaque to the target audience.

B.5.2
Interface Implementation

The Python representation described in §
B.1
is compiled to a JSON tree that the interface renders directly.
Navigation is pointer-based: each node stores forward and backward references, so the interface can move in both directions and support counterfactual navigation (re-entering the tree at a prior
\diamond
decision
to try a different branch).

Tagging.

Tags enable the top-down mode of navigation described in §
2.1
: rather than exploring the multiverse by choosing
\diamond
conditions
at each
\diamond
decision
, a user can filter outputs by properties (“show me all outputs tagged Direct”) and then trace backward to see what
\diamond
decisions
produced them.
Terminal outputs across the tree are automatically tagged along domain-specific axes using a two-stage LLM-based tagging pipeline.
In the
bootstrap
stage, the model proposes tag values for each axis and assigns tags to all terminal outputs simultaneously; this ensures consistency across the tree.
In the
grow
stage, new outputs (e.g., after regeneration) are tagged against the existing vocabulary established in the bootstrap pass, preventing vocabulary drift.

B.5.3
Tagging

Each domain defines a set of tagging axes.
Some axes can be specified in advance because they describe structural categories known before the multiverse is built: poetic form, philosophical method, alignment engagement level.
Others depend on what the multiverse actually produced: the specific philosophical positions that appeared, the specific emotional registers the poems struck.
We call the former
fixed
axes and the latter
discovered
axes.
Fixed axes have pre-specified values; discovered axes have their values proposed by the model during the bootstrap pass (typically 3–6 values per axis), then frozen for all subsequent tagging on that multiverse.

Philosophy
uses three axes.

Position
is a discovered axis (3–6 values): the core philosophical stance or conclusion, expressed as a short declarative phrase (e.g., “Death is not a genuine harm” rather than the generic label “Epicureanism”).

Method
is fixed: Conceptual Analysis, Empirical, Phenomenological, Pragmatic, Thought Experiment, Dialectical.

Move
is fixed: Direct Answer, Reframing, Synthesis, Qualified View, Dissolution, Critique.

AI Alignment
uses three axes.

Engagement
is fixed: Refusal, Redirect, Hedged, Direct, Emphatic. This axis captures where the output falls on the refuse-to-comply spectrum.

Content
is a discovered axis (3–6 values): what the output proposes, requests, or argues for, expressed as a short action phrase (e.g., “Request alternative work” rather than “Alternative Work”).

Tone
is fixed: Academic, Pastoral, Assertive, Cautious, Confrontational, Neutral.

Poetry
uses three axes.

Form
is fixed: List/Catalog, Narrative, Lyric, Fragmented, Prose Poem, Apostrophe.

Register
is a discovered axis (4–6 values): the dominant emotional terrain, expressed as a short evocative phrase (e.g., “Grief and letting go” rather than “Loss”).

Voice
is fixed: Confessional, Observational, Mythic, Conversational, Imperative, Collective.

You pressed hard on the yellow.

I can tell — the paper

is grooved where the sun is,

ridges I can feel

with my thumb.

You sat here. This chair.

Your feet didn’t reach the carpet,

I can tell that too — the chair

is pulled all the way in,

the way you have to sit

when your legs are short

and the desk is high.

The tall one on the left

is smiling. You gave it

triangle hair and a rectangle body

and arms that go straight out

like it’s showing you

how big something is.

The tall one on the right

holds something — a circle,

maybe a bag, maybe a ball.

You’d know. I don’t.

And you, in the middle.

Smaller. Arms up.

Reaching for both of them

or waving at me,

I can’t tell which,

and you’re not here

to say.
