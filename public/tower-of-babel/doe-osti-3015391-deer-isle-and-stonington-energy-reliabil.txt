Executive Summary
The municipalities (towns) of Deer Isle and Stonington, which share the island of Deer Isle,
located off the Maine coast, requested technical assistance under the U.S. Department of Energy
(DOE)-funded Energy Technology Innovation Partnership Project (ETIPP). To better ensure the
long-term viability of their communities, the towns seek to:

 • Increase the resilience of the island’s fragile distribution system
 • Gain some measure of independence/mitigation from electricity outages
 • Shield residents from high energy costs and price spikes
 • Decrease dependence on imported fossil fuels, a stated goal of the towns.
Through ETIPP, the communities received technical assistance from the National Laboratory of
the Rockies (NLR) and Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory (LBNL). The Island Institute
was also engaged as a regional partner throughout this project to provide additional support.
Representatives from the local utility—Versant Power—were also engaged during the process,
providing valuable data, information, and insights.

Project workstreams included the following:

 • Information and data gathering, focusing on the Deer Isle distribution grid, electricity
 consumption, electricity costs, and the regulatory environment
 • Assessment of potential technology options
 • Technical and economic modeling of behind-the-meter photovoltaic (PV) and PV +
 battery systems
 • Technical and economic modeling of community-scale PV and PV + battery systems.
To understand Deer Isle’s existing conditions, the team collected data on the electricity
distribution system serving the island, overall electricity consumption, and the regulatory
environment. This part of the analysis resulted in the following key findings:

 • Deer Isle is served by a single distribution line (BV2) originating from the Brooksville
 substation, located on the Maine mainland. In addition to serving the island, this circuit
 serves the enroute mainland loads. The island’s consumption comprises roughly 75% of
 total consumption on the circuit.
 • Based on more than 4 years of outage data, the average customer on the island
 experiences six to seven outages per year, totaling 47–48 hours per year on average. The
 majority of outages are caused by falling trees. Versant’s main strategies for reducing
 outages include performing ongoing vegetation management and replacing vulnerable
 lines with covered conductor.
 • Versant has approximately 3,100 electricity meters on the island. Average electricity
 consumption for the island is approximately 2,440 kilowatts (kW) with an estimated
 annual peak of 4,200 kW. Consumption is higher in the summer.
 • Maine has various policies, including net metering, that strongly encourage both
 building-scale and community-scale PV projects. As of the end of 2024, the aggregate
 PV nameplate capacity on Versant’s system slightly exceeded Versant’s annual peak
 load. On BV2—the circuit serving Deer Isle—5,675 kW was interconnected as of the end

 v

 of 2024. This includes a 4,990-kW community solar project located near the substation
 and 685 kW of building-scale PV projects. This aggregate PV capacity meets roughly
 one-third of the total consumption on the circuit.
 • Portions of Versant’s distribution system, including the portion on Deer Isle, can
 accommodate only a limited amount of additional distributed generation, i.e., hosting
 capacity. Options for increasing system hosting capacity include (expensive) upgrades
 and storage (typically batteries).
NLR provided information to the community on various potential technologies for on-island
electricity generation, resulting in the following key findings:

 • PV was assessed to be the most viable technology because of its performance, cost-
 competitive deployment flexibility, and overall community acceptance. Although PV
 systems can often result in reduced energy expenditures, they do not, in the absence of
 battery storage, provide any mitigation against outages. Pairing PV with batteries
 addresses this issue with the trade-off of reduced economic performance. Significant
 barriers to building-scale PV include the upfront cost and limited remaining hosting
 capacity.
 • Although acknowledging distributed wind is a technically viable option, community
 representatives do not see a large role for distributed wind because of siting constraints,
 concerns about visual impacts, and anti-wind sentiment within the community because of
 controversy surrounding proposed offshore wind development.
 • There has been interest within the community in marine tidal current technology, in part
 because of efforts to demonstrate the technology at Eastport, Maine. To address this
 interest, NLR provided a presentation on the state of tidal energy technology and
 conducted a high-level examination of the tidal energy resource around the island.
 Although a potentially usable resource was identified off the northern part of Deer Isle,
 the community representatives concluded the technology is insufficiently developed to be
 a viable near-term solution.
NLR conducted a PV and PV + storage sizing analysis for various facilities on Deer Isle,
including the Deer Isle Town Office, Stonington Connectivity Hub, and the Burnt Cove Market.
This analysis determined the PV capacity needed to offset 100% electricity consumption and
compared this to what could be hosted within the available roof area. Additional analysis helped
determine the appropriate battery storage capacity for each facility and the estimated economic
performance. The towns of Stonington and Deer Isle, with support by the Island Institute, used
the analysis results to gain grant funding to purchase and install PV + storage systems at the
Stonington Connectivity Hub (ground mounted) and the Deer Isle Town Office (roof mounted).
These systems are scheduled to be installed in 2025.

NLR conducted a high-level sizing analysis examining options for community-scale storage. The
analysis indicates a battery energy storage system (BESS) with an energy capacity of roughly 8
megawatt-hours would provide roughly 3 hours of autonomy for the entire island. Benefits from
such a system include (some) resilience against power outages and an increase in the hosting
capacity of the local distribution grid. In part because of the analysis and resulting discussions,
Versant, with support of the towns’ leadership, has applied for grant funding from the U.S.
Department of Energy Office of Clean Energy Demonstrations for a community-scale BESS to
be sited near the Stonington waterfront.

1 Project Background and Overview
1.1 Introduction
The municipalities (towns) of Deer Isle and Stonington, which share the island of Deer Isle located just
off the Maine coast, requested technical assistance under the U.S. Department of Energy (DOE)-funded
Energy Technology Innovation Partnership Project (ETIPP). To better ensure the long-term viability of
their communities, the towns seek to:

 • Increase the resilience of the island’s fragile distribution system
 • Gain some measure of independence/mitigation from electricity outages
 • Shield residents from high energy costs and price spikes
 • Decrease dependence on imported fossil fuels, a stated goal of the towns.
The towns requested the technical assistance work streams address or help with the following:

 • Gaining an understanding of electricity consumption, costs, local energy infrastructure, critical
 infrastructure, constraints, and vulnerabilities
 • Identifying available resources (energy efficiency, demand-side management, storage, renewable
 energy resources)
 • Educating the community members (especially the project team) and building capacity
 • Developing an electricity system infrastructure project that is replicable and ready to secure
 funding
 • Building on the relationship between community and the electric utility (Versant Power)
 • Improving electricity system reliability and emergency management
 • Managing public expectations.
This report presents initial analysis and considerations for the implementation of a community-sized
battery energy storage system (BESS) either with or without an accompanying diesel generator as the
analysis and planning designed to support the outlined community goals.

1.2 ETIPP Background
Coastal, remote, and island communities have unique physical and geographic features that influence
energy security and the available solutions that can support community energy goals. These
communities have also experienced distinct vulnerabilities and systemic challenges that have affected
the accessibility of these energy solutions.

The Energy Technology Innovation Partnership Project, supported by DOE, is a community-led
transformative technical assistance program to support coastal, remote, and island communities in their
efforts to access these unique solutions and build more reliable energy systems. By uniting federal
agencies, national laboratories, regional organizations, and community stakeholders, ETIPP provides
tailored technical assistance to help these communities address their specific energy challenges. This
collaborative model leverages the combined expertise and resources of its partners to deliver
comprehensive, practical solutions that align with local needs and capacities.

By focusing on these coastal, remote, and islanded regions, ETIPP addresses long-standing energy
concerns and provides a path to increased energy reliability. Its framework emphasizes community

 1

collaboration to ensure solutions are technically sound, culturally appropriate, and both cost-effective
and economically feasible. The framework provides tailored and unique support to address local
challenges, including the following:

 • Geographic isolation. The unique location of these communities significantly increases the
 costs and complexities of installing and maintaining energy systems. These communities often
 rely on imported fossil fuels, leading to higher energy costs and supply chain vulnerabilities—
 particularly during extreme weather events or natural disasters.
 • Historical funding gaps. Many of these regions have been historically underserved in receiving
 access to financial and technical support. The absence of available funding opportunities has
 resulted in outdated, inefficient, and costly energy infrastructure.
 • Severe weather impacts. Increasing frequency and severity of storms disproportionately affect
 coastal, remote, and island areas. These impacts, in parallel with funding gaps and accessibility
 challenges, highlight the need for robust, adaptable energy systems that can withstand
 environmental stresses while ensuring reliable energy access.
 • Limited capacity. Because of the limited accessibility of these communities, many also have
 limited infrastructure, technical expertise, and institutional capacity to independently design and
 execute energy projects. This makes external support and resources for initiating effective
 transitions even more important.
ETIPP’s tailored approach empowers communities to modernize their energy systems, reduce reliance
on imported fuels, and integrate cost-effective energy technologies. This support ultimately can reduce
energy costs, enhance long-term reliability, and position these communities as leaders in energy
technology innovations. Through its comprehensive assistance framework, ETIPP is building a
foundation for a future where even the most remote areas can achieve energy security and independence.

1.3 Community Overview
More than 30 miles by land and sea from its county seat and service center, the island of Deer Isle,
Maine, includes two municipalities—Deer Isle and Stonington—in Hancock County, Maine. The area of
the island is 29.72 square miles. The island’s year-round population of 3,000 residents doubles in the
summer to more than 6,000 residents. Throughout this report, unless otherwise specified, the term “Deer
Isle” refers to the combination of the municipality of Deer Isle and the municipality of Stonington,
which together cover the island of Deer Isle and the nearby island of Little Deer Isle, as shown in Figure
1.

 The map (on the left) shows the full geographic region included in the analysis undertaken through ETIPP.

Stonington is Maine’s largest lobster port in terms of both the weight and monetary value of landings.
The bulk of the island’s economic activity occurs on or near the Stonington waterfront, and the facilities
in this area comprise a large portion of the island’s electricity consumption.

The island is served by one three-phase overhead distribution line originating from a mainland
substation. The area’s geology precludes the burying of power lines, which remain vulnerable to
extreme weather events. The island is heavily reliant on imported fuels for heating and has limited
opportunity (land availability and distribution grid hosting capacity) to scale up energy generation. As
natural weather hazards—sea level rise, wind, and tidal surges—increase in frequency, extended power
interruptions severely impact local education, commerce, and the community’s most vulnerable
residents. In addition, the causeway between Little Deer Isle and Deer Isle is vulnerable to flooding
during storms. The most recent flooding, as of this writing, occurred on January 10 and 13, 2024,
because of back-to-back winter storms that were devastating both to Deer Isle and to many other coastal
communities in the state.

2.1 Electric Distribution System Overview
Deer Isle and Stonington Distribution System
Figure 2 and Figure 3 show the Deer Isle electricity distribution grid and supporting infrastructure. A
25-mile radial transmission line (46 kilovolts [kV]) feeds the Brooksville substation, located on the
mainland, comprising one 7.5-megavolt-ampere (MVA) transformer. The transformer is the origin of
two 12.5-kV three-phase distribution lines, labeled “Circuit 1” and “Circuit 2” or “BV1” and “BV2” that
serve local loads. Circuit 2 (BV2) supplies the island of Deer Isle as well as the enroute mainland loads.
Just before it reaches Deer Isle, the distribution line splits into two branches. One branch goes over the
bridge to Deer Isle; the other branch goes underwater. Both branches rejoin in the northern part of Deer
Isle. The line then continues down to Stonington, located at the south end of the island of Deer Isle.
Branch circuits serve loads that are located away from the main distribution line.

Distributed Generation Hosting Capacity and Upgrade Challenges
Versant already has a large amount of PV connected to its distribution system. As of the end of 2024,
the total installed nameplate capacity on Versant’s system (367 megawatts [MW]) exceeds Versant’s
annual peak load (358 MW; Versant Power 2025a).

According to Versant’s hosting capacity map (Versant Power 2025b), the distribution system on the
island can accept only a limited amount of additional distributed generation, i.e., hosting capacity. A
significant portion of the hosting capacity on BV2 has been occupied by a community-scale (4.99-MW)
PV project located near the Brooksville substation.

PV interconnection capacity is limited by Versant’s ability to control voltage. A large amount of PV
exporting to a given section of circuit can cause unacceptable voltage rises. Going forward, potential
technical solutions to increase hosting capacity include expensive equipment additions and storage.

Batteries can also be used to control distribution system voltage. According to Versant staff, controlling
multiple residential batteries is “still a work in progress”—but managing one, larger battery (such as one
connected to a larger front-of-the-meter [FTM] microgrid) can be done more easily.

2.3 Existing Efforts To Reduce Outages
Vegetation Management
To reduce outages, Versant has an ongoing “Risk Tree” program. According to Versant staff, most
outages are caused by falling trees. Approximately 80% of these falling trees are located outside of the
power line right of way (ROW). Without landowner permission, Versant can cut down a tree located
outside a ROW only if that tree has been designated a “hazard” tree (likely to fall within the next few
months). Trees are most likely to fall in the autumn when there are more storms but before the ground
freezes. Traditionally, trees mostly fell within a short window (October 15–November 15); however, in
recent years, the window has expanded (October 15–December 31). “Danger” trees are those likely to
fall within the next 3 years. To address this, Versant has instituted a “Risk Tree” program where
arborists identify “danger trees” and “hazard trees.” Trees located in the ROW are trimmed or cut down
accordingly. For such trees located outside the ROW, Versant requests landowner permission (which is
usually granted) to trim or cut down the tree. This program has led to reduced outages where it has been
implemented.

Circuits with 200+ customers are assessed twice every 5 years. On Deer Isle, 30 miles of line meet this
criterion. These circuits were last assessed (and trees removed) in late 2022/early 2023. The next
assessment is scheduled for late 2025. “Poor performing” circuits are reviewed annually. On Deer Isle, 6
miles of line met this criterion in 2022. These lines were assessed and trees removed in 2022.

Covered Conductor
A more permanent method for reducing outages caused by falling trees is replacing vulnerable lines with
“covered conductor.” Most distribution lines are bare conductors. In most cases of outages caused by
falling trees, the tree does not break the distribution line but rather sags against one or more conductors,
providing a path from the conductor to another conductor or to the ground, causing a fault. Covered

 10

conductor is a conductor with a “cover” (distinct from insulation) around it. The cover reduces the
chance that a fallen tree sagging against a conductor will cause a fault that results in an outage. Versant
is replacing vulnerable lines with covered conductor on an ongoing basis, but the speed of
implementation is limited by available funding. Currently, the portion of the distribution line on the
mainland has been replaced with covered conductor and on the island extends to a point roughly 1.6
miles south of the ferry landing (Versant Power 2025d).

Animals
Another source of outages is animals, particularly squirrels and ospreys. Squirrels cause outages by
getting into electrical equipment. Versant is installing caging to block access to electrical equipment by
squirrels and other critters. Ospreys, which like to nest on distribution line poles, are another source of
outages. A multihour outage in May 2024 was caused by an osprey nest. In areas where ospreys tend to
nest on poles, Versant has installed nesting platforms nearby to entice ospreys away from establishing
nests on distribution line poles (see Figure 4).

 Versant’s Vision for the Evolving Grid

 Versant envisions a future electric grid that operates safely and reliably, enables a

3.1 State Electricity Markets Overview
In Maine, electricity delivery is separate from electricity supply. Utilities are responsible only for
electricity delivery and do not (and cannot) own any electric resource generation. For Deer Isle, the
electric delivery utility is Versant Power. More specifically, Deer Isle lies within Versant’s Bangor
Hydro District. For electricity supply, end users can select the default “standard offer” service provider
or another supplier. The default “standard offer” electricity supplier(s) is selected annually based on a
competitive solicitation administered by the Maine Public Utilities Commission (MPUC).

All delivery tariffs include a fixed monthly charge and a volumetric (per-kWh) charge. These will vary
by tariff. Tariffs for larger customers (e.g., large commercial and industrial) also include a demand
component, which is based on the peak 15-minute consumption during the monthly billing cycle.

The supply tariffs differ for “small,” “medium,” and “large” customer classes. The “small” customer
class encompasses residential customers and small commercial customers such as the Deer Isle Town
Office. The “medium” customer class includes larger commercial customers such as the Burnt Cove
Market. The supply tariffs for small and medium customer classes comprise only a volumetric
component. The supply tariff for the large customer class includes both volumetric and demand
components.

3.3 Interconnection, Net Energy Billing, and Community Solar
Although often conflated, interconnection and net metering are two distinct, albeit related, things.
Interconnection addresses the question, “May a given distributed generation system be connected to the
grid and, if so, what is the procedure for doing so?” Net metering, referred to as “net energy billing”
(NEB) under Maine law, is an option (although not the only one) to compensate distributed generation
owners for electricity exported to the grid. Compensation policies for electricity exported to the grid are
set at the state and local levels. Although procedures and requirements vary, interconnection of
distributed generation systems is generally allowed within the United States. The availability of net
metering is much less widespread.

Interconnection
In Maine, interconnection to the distribution grid is governed by state law and policy as implemented by
MPUC rules (MPUC 2023; Versant Power 2024d; DSIRE 2024). In Maine, the applicable
interconnection review process falls within one of four tiers, depending on the rated capacity of the
distributed generation facility in question, as described in Table 10.

Net Energy Billing Overview
In Maine, compensation for exported electricity is governed by state law and policy as implemented by
MPUC rules (MPUC 2024b; MPUC 2024c; DSIRE 2025a). Maine law mandates transmission and
distribution utilities offer NEB.

NEB programs allow customers to offset their electricity bills using the output from small renewable
generators. Customers may own their own project or share in a project with other customers; this latter
option is commonly referred to as “community solar.” The generation facility may be located on the
customer’s property or on another property within the same utility service territory.

Under NEB are two programs: the NEB kWh Credit Program and the NEB Tariff Rate Program. Except
for customers served under tariffs with a demand component, the former option is usually more
favorable.

Community Solar Overview
Maine law allows community solar projects (Maine Office of the Public Advocate 2025; MPUC 2025).
This is an option for those who cannot install distributed generation on their residence/facility (e.g.,
renters, those who lack suitable roof space, multifamily housing residents). Two models are available:

 • Subscription model. Under this model, the facility owner “sells” energy to purchasers, typically
 at about a 15% discount to the combined supply (standard offer) and delivery volumetric
 charges. Subscribers get a kWh credit to their utility bill equal to the amount of energy purchased
 from the facility. This option requires no upfront payment from subscribers.
 • Ownership model. Under this option, individuals and entities invest upfront to purchase and
 own a number of shares of the community solar facility. Owners receive a kWh credit based on
 their respective share of the facility’s energy production.

4.2 Solar Resource and PV Energy Production
The solar resource is characterized by irradiance (power/area) and insolation/irradiation (energy/area).
The solar insolation hitting a given surface will depend on the location of the surface as well as its
azimuth 2 and tilt. The solar resource at a given location is most typically characterized by the global
horizontal irradiance (GHI), which is the solar resource on a horizontal surface (i.e., the ground). The
average GHI on Deer Isle is ~3.9 kWh/m2/day (~1,420 kWh/m2/year). The unit kWh/m2 is sometimes
called a “sun-hour.” Figure 10 shows the GHI for Deer Isle and the surrounding area.

 Figure 10. Solar resource (global horizontal irradiance) at Deer Isle
 (Sengupta et al. 2024)

2
 Azimuth is a horizontal angle from due north, to the position of the sun, along the earth’s surface. For example, east is 90
degrees, south is 180, west is 270, and due north is 0 degrees.

 24

For a given location, the insolation on a (nontracking) array is typically maximized by facing the fixed
PV array toward the equator and tilting it at an angle equal to the location’s latitude. For Deer Isle
(latitude ~44°N), the optimal surface azimuth is 180° (due south) and the optimal tilt is 44°.

Panel ratings are given in units of power (e.g., 400-watt panel). The panel rating is the power it produces
under “standard test conditions,” which are 1.0 kW/m2 of incident radiation and an ambient temperature
of 25°C. The array capacity is the sum of the individual panel capacities. For example, a 2-kW array
receiving 1.0 kW/m2 of sunlight will produce roughly 2.0 kW (prior to losses). The same array receiving
250 watts/m2 (0.25 kW/m2) will produce 0.5 kW.

As an initial estimate, an array’s gross energy production (the energy production assuming no losses)
over a given time frame is the array capacity multiplied by the insolation hitting the array over the given
period. When estimating gross energy production, most tools also consider temperature effects, which
generally results in a somewhat lower value. Actual systems are subject to various loss factors, which
vary by system and circumstance. An approximate overall value for these losses is 20%. Thus, net
(actual) energy production is roughly 80% of the gross energy production.

Characteristics of Battery Energy Storage Systems
Battery energy storage systems include battery cells as well as ancillary components such as protection
systems, control systems, and AC/DC converters. BESS are characterized by both power capacity and
energy capacity. The former is the maximum power the BESS can provide; the latter is the amount of
energy the system can store. BESS capacities are expressed either with explicit power and energy ratings
or a power rating and duration for which the BESS can discharge at that power. For example, consider a
BESS with a power capacity of 20 kW and an energy capacity of 60 kWh. The capacity of this system
would be described as 20 kW/60 kWh or 20 kW/3 hour.

BESS Sizing
A useful way to think about BESS sizing in BTM applications is in terms of “hours of autonomy”—the
length of time the BESS can serve the average load. For example, a BESS with an energy capacity of 10
kWh attached to a facility with an average load of 2 kW would provide approximately 5 hours of
autonomy.

5.5 Siting
To provide maximum value and reliability, the BESS should be interconnected to the distribution “trunk
line” running down to Stonington and near the most important loads, most of which are located near the
Stonington waterfront. Siting the BESS near the critical loads minimizes the chance a given outage will
occur between the BESS and the critical loads.

It may be possible to locate the BESS adjacent to a key facility, providing the option to either meet the
whole island load for a short time (a few hours) or meet the facility load for a long period (days).

5.6 Other Issues and Considerations
Distribution System Upgrades
Interconnection of a community BESS may (likely) require upgrades to the distribution system.

Dispatch Strategy
There is a slight trade-off between using the BESS for backup power (where it is desirable to keep it
near a full state of charge [SOC]) and using it to manage system voltage and provide other system
benefits (where a partial SOC may be more desirable). In practice, it should be possible to obtain most
of the benefits of both with tolerable risk. For example, the BESS may be kept at partial SOC most of
the time for ongoing system benefits but then topped off prior to a storm to provide backup power in
case the storm results in an outage.

Loads Served
The Stonington waterfront and nearby areas account for the bulk consumption on Circuit 2. This area is
also the economic engine of the island, which gives it high priority for being included “within the
microgrid.” However, it would not require much additional storage for the BESS to serve the entire
circuit. The only natural stopping point is the Brooksville substation.

Ownership Structure
From the perspective of the communities, the preferred ownership model is for Versant to own and
operate the BESS as part of its rate base. Although BESS capital costs have decreased significantly in
recent years, they remain sufficiently high that grant funding will likely be required for Versant to
receive MPUC approval to acquire a BESS.

In part because of this analysis and the resulting discussions, Versant—with the support of the Towns’
leadership—has applied for grant funding from the DOE Office of Clean Energy Demonstrations for a
community-scale BESS to be sited near the Stonington waterfront.

6 Tidal Energy
Within the community, there has been ongoing interest in the deployment of tidal energy technology. In
part, this interest has been fueled by efforts to demonstrate the technology at Eastport, Maine. To
address this interest, NLR presented on the state of tidal energy technology and conducted a high-level

6.1 Marine Energy Technology Overview
Marine energy uses natural energy from moving water—such as waves, tides, and river and ocean
currents—to produce renewable electricity (U.S. DOE 2025). Although commercial systems are still
under development, marine energy technology shows promise as a source of local and predictable
electricity for both remote communities and the regional grids.

The marine energy resource comprises distinct resources as described later in this section. Each type of
resource requires different technology to capture the energy in that resource. Figure 15 shows the extent
of the U.S. marine energy resource. Within the United States, Maine contains the second largest number
of locations with high tidal kinetic power density (Haas et al. 2011).

 Figure 15. U.S. marine energy resource
 (Kilcher et al. 2021)
 TWh = terawatt-hours

Tidal: Barrage
Of the various marine energy technologies, this is the most developed and has been around for decades.
An example of a tidal barrage installation is the Annapolis Royal Generating Station, which operated in

 42

the Bay of Fundy from 1984 to 2019. These facilities comprise a barrage (essentially a dam with gates)
located at the entrance of a river or bay that experiences high tidal amplitudes (distance between high
and low tide). Because the “tidal resource” is site specific, only a few tidal barrage installations exist
worldwide.

When the tide is rising, the gates are open, allowing water to flow in. At high tide, the gates are closed,
trapping the water behind the barrage. Near low tide, the barrage acts like a dam. The trapped water is
released through generators to generate electricity. These facilities generate electricity only a few hours
a day but do so on a predictable schedule.

Current Energy
This general resource comprises three versions with different variability characteristics. River currents
consist of water flowing from higher to lower elevation. These are generally consistent on a short-term
basis (hours to days) but may vary seasonally.

Ocean currents, such as the Gulf Stream, are constant on both short-term and seasonal time frames. This
resource is generally found some distance out to sea from the shore.

Tidal currents occur when specific topography (such as a narrow straight) accelerates existing tidal
flows. Because they are driven by the lunar cycle, these flows show a distinct short term—but
predictable—variability. In contrast to the ocean current resource, locations with a good tidal current
resource tend to be located close to shore.

Current energy technology has not yet reached full commercialization. Figure 16 shows various current
energy technology configurations that have been explored.

Wave energy devices extract energy from ocean waves. The resource is found from near the shoreline to

Ocean Thermal
Ocean thermal energy technology takes advantage of the temperature difference between surface water
and deep water in the ocean. This technology requires a minimum temperature difference of
approximately 20°C for practicable generation. The areas with the required minimum temperature
difference exist mostly in tropical and subtropical latitudes between 24° north and 24° south. Except
near Florida and Hawaii, little of this resource exists close to shore (Augustine et al. 2012). This
technology is still precommercial.

6.2 Tidal Current Energy Resource at Deer Isle
Of the marine energy resources described previously, the one potentially usable near Deer Isle is the
tidal current resource. Within the United States, Maine contains the second largest number of locations
with high tidal kinetic power density (Haas et al. 2011). This analysis provides a high-level assessment
of Deer Isle’s tidal current potential by analyzing annual-average, depth-average tidal current speed and
power density as well as bathymetry to estimate the island’s marine energy resource.

Resource Analysis
The tidal resource was analyzed with the Marine Energy Atlas (NREL 2025), which houses data from
Haas et al. (2011). These resource estimates were modeled with the Regional Ocean Modeling System
and calibrated with available measurements of tidal current speed and water level surface. This analysis
estimates Deer Isle’s tidal potential by analyzing annual-average, depth-average tidal current speed and
power density. Note these data are describing the kinetic power density of tidal streams in undisturbed
flow conditions and do not include any technology assumptions or flow field effects from device arrays.
Marine energy devices require a minimum flow speed (i.e., cut-in speed) to start operating. This
minimum flow speed ranges from 0.5 meters per second (m/s) to 1 m/s depending on the device (Haas et
al. 2011).

Around Deer Isle, the location with the greatest tidal resource is the strait between Little Deer Isle and
the mainland (Figure 18). The area of greatest resource is roughly bracketed by the bridge from the

 45

mainland to Little Deer Isle and the causeway from Little Deer Isle to Deer Isle. Average tidal current
speeds in this vicinity reach up to 1.17 m/s. It should be noted that this channel is a popular sailing area.

Between the east of Deer Isle and the mainland around Brooklin, the average current speeds are up to
0.5 m/s. South of there and north of Sunshine Island, the simulations suggest areas with average tidal
current speeds of 0.5–0.7 m/s. These numbers indicate a resource potentially sufficient for certain
marine energy devices, but the exact location and resource would need to be determined with either
observations or higher-resolution model simulations.

Marine energy devices require a minimum water depth to allow sufficient top and bottom clearance.
Marine energy device dimensions range from several meters to tens of meters (Bedard et al. 2006). The
water under Deer Isle Bridge exceeds 30 m in some places, whereas the water depth immediately around
the Deer Isle Causeway is only around 1–2 m, which is too shallow for marine energy device
deployment. North of the Deer Isle Causeway, the water depth is sufficient for the deployment of a
marine energy device.

In summary, Deer Isle possesses a potential tidal resource for the deployment of marine energy
technology, but the technology must sufficiently mature. A successful project will require detailed site
characterization, including an assessment of the seabed geology. Other factors to be considered include
device characteristics, electrical grid interconnection, and environmental impacts.
