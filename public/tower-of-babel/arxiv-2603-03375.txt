The Theory behind UMAP?
David Wegmann

Abstract
In 2018, McInnes et al. introduced a dimensionality reduction algorithm called UMAP in [ 5 ] , which enjoys wide popularity among data scientists. The article [ 5 ] introduces a finite variant of a functor called the metric realization from an unpublished draft [ 9 ] by Spivak. This draft [ 9 ] contains many errors, most of which are reproduced by McInnes et al. [ 5 ] and subsequent publications. This thesis aims to repair these errors and provide a self-contained document with the full derivation of Spivak’s functors and McInnes et al.’s finite variant. We contribute an explicit description of the metric realization and related functors. At the end of this thesis, we discuss the UMAP algorithm, as well as claims about properties of the algorithm and the correspondence of McInnes et al.’s finite variant to the UMAP algorithm.

[

The metric realization functor
\text{Real}:\text{sFuzz}\rightarrow\text{EPMet}

Here, the category of simplicial sets

\infty

McInnes et al. build upon Spivak’s result by introducing a finite variant of this metric realization in
[
5
]

[
9
]

[
5
]

[
9
]

The notation for categories in Spivak’s draft
[

]

2.1
Currying of Functors

As mentioned in the introduction, we will require an equivalence referred to as
currying
by computer scientists. In computer science, this usually refers to the isomorphism

C^{A\times B}\cong{C^{B}}^{A},

where
A,B,C
are sets and
Y^{X}
denotes the set of maps from
X
to
Y
.

This isomorphism has a categorical counterpart for categories
\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B},\mathcal{C}
where
\mathcal{Y}^{\mathcal{X}}
denotes the category of functors from
\mathcal{X}
to
\mathcal{Y}
. We can also restrict
\mathcal{C}^{\mathcal{B}}
to a subcategory if we impose an additional condition.
This version is specifically tailored to be used later in
3.3.4
.

Lemma 2.1.1
:

Let
\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B},\mathcal{C}
be categories and let
\mathcal{S}
be a full subcategory of
\mathcal{C}^{\mathcal{B}}
.

Then there is an isomorphism of categories

\mathcal{T}\cong\mathcal{S}^{\mathcal{A}},

where
\mathcal{T}
is the full subcategory of
\mathcal{C}^{\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{B}}
that has as objects all functors
F:\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{B}\rightarrow\mathcal{C}
such that
F(A,-):\mathcal{B}\rightarrow\mathcal{C}
is an object in
\mathcal{S}
for any object
A
in
\mathcal{A}
.

Proof:

We define the functors
\mathit{curry}:\mathcal{T}\rightarrow\mathcal{S}^{\mathcal{A}}

\displaystyle\mathit{curry}(F:\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{B}\rightarrow\mathcal{C})(A)=F(A,-)

\displaystyle\mathit{curry}(\alpha:F\Rightarrow G)_{A}=\alpha_{A,-},

and
\mathit{uncurry}:\mathcal{S}^{\mathcal{A}}\rightarrow\mathcal{T}

\displaystyle\mathit{uncurry}(F:\mathcal{A}\rightarrow\mathcal{C}^{\mathcal{B}})(A,B)=F(A)(B)

\displaystyle\mathit{uncurry}(F:\mathcal{A}\rightarrow\mathcal{C}^{\mathcal{B}})(a:A\rightarrow A^{\prime},b:B\rightarrow B^{\prime})=F(a)_{B^{\prime}}\circ F(A)(b)

\displaystyle\mathit{uncurry}(\alpha:F\Rightarrow G)_{A,B}=(\alpha_{A})_{B}.

We verify that
\mathit{uncurry}\circ\mathit{curry}=\mathit{Id}_{\mathcal{T}}
:

\displaystyle\mathit{uncurry}(\mathit{curry}(F))(A,B)

\displaystyle=\mathit{curry}(F)(A)(B)=F(A,-)(B)=F(A,B)

\displaystyle\mathit{uncurry}(\mathit{curry}(\alpha))_{A,B}

\displaystyle=(\mathit{curry}(\alpha)_{A})_{B}=\alpha_{A,B}.

And that
\mathit{curry}\circ\mathit{uncurry}=\mathit{Id}_{\mathcal{S}^{\mathcal{A}}}
:

\displaystyle\mathit{curry}(\mathit{uncurry}(F))(A)(B)

\displaystyle=\mathit{uncurry}(F)(A,-)(B)=\mathit{uncurry}(F)(A,B)=F(A)(B)

\displaystyle\mathit{curry}(\mathit{uncurry}(F))(A)(b)

\displaystyle=\mathit{uncurry}(F)(A,-)(b)

\displaystyle=\mathit{uncurry}(F)(\mathit{id}_{A},b)=F(\mathit{id}_{A})_{B^{\prime}}\circ F(A)(b)=F(A)(b)

\displaystyle(\mathit{curry}(\mathit{uncurry}(\alpha))_{A})_{B}

\displaystyle=\mathit{uncurry}(\alpha)_{A,B}=(\alpha_{A})_{B}.

\square

Colimits and Full Subcategories

We will need a concrete definition of cocones for the proof of

Definition 2.2.1
:

[
7
, Definitions 3.1.1–3.1.3]
Let
\mathcal{J}
be small and let
\mathcal{C}
be locally small.

1.

•

.

2.

A

with nadir

is a natural transformation
\lambda:F\Rightarrow\Delta(C)
.

3.

Cocones under a diagram

define a functor
\mathit{cocone}\left(F,-\right):\mathcal{C}\rightarrow\mathit{Set}
that sends

•

\mathcal{C}

•

to the cocone
\Delta(f)\circ\lambda:F\Rightarrow\Delta(C^{\prime})

A

is a representing object of this functor:

\mathit{cocone}\left(F,-\right)\cong\mathit{Hom}_{\mathcal{C}}\left(\mathit{colim}\left(F\right),-\right).

Lemma 2.2.2
:

Let
\mathcal{J}
be small and let
\mathcal{S}
be a full subcategory of
\mathcal{C}
.

Let
F:\mathcal{J}\rightarrow\mathcal{C}
be a diagram where
F(J)
is in
\mathcal{S}
for every
J
in
\mathcal{J}
. Then
F
can also be regarded as a functor
F:\mathcal{J}\rightarrow\mathcal{S}
.

If a colimit object
\mathit{colim}\left(F:\mathcal{J}\rightarrow\mathcal{C}\right)
of
F:\mathcal{J}\rightarrow\mathcal{C}
formed in
\mathcal{C}
is in
\mathcal{S}
, then
\mathit{colim}\left(F:\mathcal{J}\rightarrow\mathcal{C}\right)

formed in the full subcategory

.

Let
\mathit{colim}\left(F:\mathcal{J}\rightarrow\mathcal{C}\right)
be a colimit object of

formed in
\mathcal{C}
.

Then
\mathit{colim}\left(F\right)
is a representing object for
\mathit{cocone}\left(F,-\right)
and we have the natural isomorphism

\mathit{cocone}\left(F,-\right)\cong\mathit{Hom}_{\mathcal{C}}\left(\mathit{colim}\left(F\right),-\right):\mathcal{C}\rightarrow\mathit{Set}.

We restrict this natural isomorphism indexed by

\square

2.4
The Simplex Category and (Co)Simplicial Objects

We will use the same definition and mostly the same indexing convention for the simplex category as in Richter’s textbook
[
6
]

[
6
, Chapter 10]
. However, we will explicitly add the index
n
to the face
\delta^{n}_{i}
and degeneracy morphisms
\sigma^{n}_{j}
while Richter simply writes
\delta_{i}
and
\sigma_{i}
. Just like Richter, we use the topologist’s convention, where objects of the simplex category are denoted
[n]=\{0,1,\ldots,n\}

[n]=\{0,1,\ldots,n-1\}
.

Definition 2.4.1
:

[
6
, Lemma 10.1.1]
The
simplex category

\Delta
has

•

as objects the finite non-empty
ordinal numbers

[n]=\{0,1,\ldots,n\}
for
n\in\mathbb{N}
,

•

as morphisms
f:[m]\rightarrow[n]
weakly monotonic maps
f:\{0,1,\ldots,m\}\rightarrow\{0,1,\ldots,n\}
.

All morphisms of this category can be factored into two types of elementary morphisms.

Definition 2.4.2
:

[
6
, Lemma 10.1.2]
The
i
th
face morphism

\delta^{n}_{i}:[n-1]\rightarrow[n]
for
0\leq i\leq n
and the
j
th
degeneracy morphism

\sigma^{n}_{j}:[n+1]\rightarrow[n]
for

are the morphisms

\delta^{n}_{i}(k)=\begin{cases}k&$0\leq k<i$\\
k+1&$i\leq k<n$\end{cases}\qquad\sigma^{n}_{j}(k)=\begin{cases}k&$0\leq k\leq j$\\
k-1&$j<k\leq n+1.$\end{cases}

Lemma 2.4.3
:

[
6
, Lemma 10.1.4]

Every morphism

in

can be expressed uniquely as a composite

with

and
0\leq j_{1}<\cdots<j_{s}<n
, where

.

Having defined the simplex category, we can define simplicial objects over arbitrary categories

as functors from the opposite of the simplex category into
\mathcal{C}
.

[

, Lemma 10.2.1]

2.5

The geometric realization realizes a simplicial set

-simplices.

Definition 2.5.1

[

, the

2.6
Locales

In this thesis, we will need locales, a form of point-free topology. An ordinary topological space
(X,\mathcal{O}_{X})
is defined on a carrier set
X
that is equipped with a topology
\mathcal{O}_{X}
. Instead of demanding the open sets to be subsets of some other set
X
, we could simply have a set
\mathcal{L}
that comes with enough operations and rules such that the elements of
\mathcal{L}
behave like open sets but are not necessarily made up of points.

Open sets can be subsets of each other. To mirror this, we will demand that
\mathcal{L}
comes equipped with a partial order
\leq
. In the resulting poset category
(\mathcal{L},\leq)
, the coproducts and products, called join
(\vee)
and meet
(\wedge)
, are natural candidates to mirror the union
(\cup)
and intersection
(\cap)
of open sets, if they exist. So we shall demand that arbitrary coproducts and finite products exist in
(\mathcal{L},\leq)
. The intersection and union of sets also satisfy the infinite distributivity law, so we will demand it as well. For a textbook reference, see
[
4
]
,
[
4
]

[
4
, Chapter IX]
.

Remark 2.6.1
:

A
poset

(P,\leq)
can be viewed as a category with the elements of
P
as objects. Between objects
p,p^{\prime}
there is a single morphism
*:p\rightarrow p^{\prime}
if
p\leq p^{\prime}
, otherwise the set
\mathit{Hom}_{(P,\leq)}\left(p,p^{\prime}\right)
is empty.

Definition 2.6.2
:

[
4
, Chapter IX,1]
A poset
(\mathcal{L},\leq)
is called a
locale
if:

•

suprema
\bigvee_{i\in I}a_{i}
exist for any family
(a_{i})_{i\in I},

•

infima
\bigwedge_{i\in I}b_{i}
exist for any
finite
family
(b_{i})_{i\in I},

such that the infinite distributivity law is satisfied:

b\wedge(\bigvee_{i\in I}a_{i})=\bigvee_{i\in I}(b\wedge a_{i})\qquad\text{for all families }(a_{i}\in\mathcal{L})_{i\in I}\text{ and }b\in\mathcal{L}.

The suprema and infima are also called
join
(
\bigvee
) and
meet
(
\bigwedge
).

All ordinary topologies
\mathcal{O}_{X}
must include the empty set and the entire space as open sets. These sets can be viewed as least and greatest elements of the poset
(\mathcal{O}_{X},\subseteq)
. Locales again mirror this property.

Notation 2.6.3
:

A poset
(\mathcal{L},\leq)
has a greatest element and a least element, given by the supremum of the entire set and the supremum of the empty set, respectively. We will denote them by

\top:=\bigvee_{a\in\mathcal{L}}a\qquad\text{ and }\qquad\bot:=\bigvee_{a\in\emptyset}a.

The element
\top
is usually called
top
and
\bot
is usually called
bottom
.

The obvious example of a locale is the poset of open subsets of a topological space. The examples we will need in this thesis are subsets of the real numbers
\mathbb{R}
or extended real numbers
\mathbb{R}\cup\{\infty\}
that have all supremums and infimums.

Example 2.6.4
:

•

For any topological space
(X,\mathcal{O}_{X})
, the open sets form the locale
(\mathcal{O}_{X},\subseteq)
.

•

Any closed interval
[a,b]\subseteq\mathbb{R}
yields the locale
([a,b],\leq)
where the join and meet are given by the supremum and the infimum in
[a,b]
.

•

When we reverse the ordering from the previous example, we get the locales
([a,b],\geq)
where the join and meet are given by the infimum and supremum, respectively.

•

If we include infinity with the convention
x\leq\infty
for all
x\in[0,\infty]
, the nonnegative reals with infinity
[0,\infty]
form the locale
([0,\infty],\leq)
.

•

We can again reverse the order from the previous example and obtain the locale
([0,\infty],\geq)
. In this case,
\infty
is the bottom element.

We have defined locales to resemble topological spaces. Similarly, morphisms of locales should resemble continuous maps.

A continuous map
f:X\rightarrow Y
maps points from
X
to points of
Y
, but the condition of continuity is defined backwards. Every open set from the target space
O\subseteq Y
must also be sent to an open set via the inverse image
f^{-1}:\mathcal{P}(Y)\rightarrow\mathcal{P}(X)
. Here
\mathcal{P}(A)
denotes the power set of a set
A
.

Because locales do not contain points and the elements
a\in\mathcal{L}
mirror open sets, we define a morphism from
(\mathcal{L},\leq)
to
(\mathcal{L}^{\prime},\leq^{\prime})
as a map
g:\mathcal{L}^{\prime}\rightarrow\mathcal{L}
in the other direction, to mirror the inverse image.

The inverse image maps subsets
O\subseteq O^{\prime}
to subsets
f^{-1}(O)\subseteq f^{-1}(O^{\prime})
, so we will demand a morphism of locales to be monotone with respect to the underlying poset. To mirror the other usual properties of the inverse image with respect to the intersection and union of sets, we demand that morphisms of locales preserve finite meets and arbitrary joins.

Definition 2.6.5
:

[
4
, Chapter IX,1]
Let
(\mathcal{L},\leq)
and
(\mathcal{L}^{\prime},\leq^{\prime})
be locales. A
morphism of locales
from
(\mathcal{L},\leq)
to
(\mathcal{L}^{\prime},\leq^{\prime})
is a monotone map
f:\mathcal{L}^{\prime}\rightarrow\mathcal{L}
that preserves finite meets and arbitrary joins.

Locales and their morphisms form the
category of locales

\mathit{Loc}
.

Remark 2.6.6
:

Equivalently, a morphism of locales is a functor that preserves finite coproducts and arbitrary products. In that case, the functor axioms correspond to monotonicity.

Example 2.6.7
:

•

The constant map

Remark 2.6.8

Sheaves on Locales

One of the goals of this thesis is to derive the metric realization of fuzzy simplicial sets. In this process, we will encounter sheaves on locales. In this subsection, we present the conventional sheaf condition (
2.7.3

2.7.7

[

. However, because we only need sheaves on locales to summarize results from Barr
[

[

We begin with the definition of presheaves. These are simply contravariant functors into the category
\mathit{Set}
.

Definition 2.7.1
:

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a category. A functor
F:\mathcal{C}^{\mathit{op}}\rightarrow\mathit{Set}
is called a
presheaf
on
\mathcal{C}
. The
category of presheaves on
\mathcal{C}
is the functor category
\mathit{Set}^{\mathcal{C}^{\mathit{op}}}
.

Notation 2.7.2
:

For presheaves
F:(P,\leq)^{\mathit{op}}\rightarrow\mathit{Set}
on a poset category, we will write
F_{a\geq b}:F(a)\rightarrow F(b)
for the restriction maps
F(f:a\rightarrow b):F(a)\rightarrow F(b)
as there is only one morphism
f:a\rightarrow b
that witnesses
a\geq b
. Note that we also flip the poset relation in the index to reduce contravariance confusion:
F_{a\geq b}
maps from
F(a)
to
F(b)
.

Sheaves are presheaves that satisfy the sheaf condition. We require this condition only for locales, where the sheaf condition takes a much simpler form.

Definition 2.7.3
:

[
1
, Section 2]
Let
\mathcal{L}
be a locale and
F:\mathcal{L}^{\mathit{op}}\rightarrow\mathit{Set}
be a presheaf.

•

For
a\in\mathcal{L}
, a family
(a_{i}\in\mathcal{L})_{i\in I}
with
a=\bigvee_{i\in I}a_{i}
is called a
cover
of

•

Given a cover

of

, a family
(x_{i}\in F(a_{i}))_{i\in I}
is called
compatible

F_{a_{i}\geq(a_{i}\,\wedge\,a_{j})}(x_{i})=F_{a_{j}\geq(a_{i}\,\wedge\,a_{j})}(x_{j})\qquad\text{for all }i,j\in I.

•

F
is called a sheaf on
\mathcal{L}
if it satisfies the
sheaf condition
:

For every
a
with cover
(a_{i})_{i\in I}
and every compatible family
(x_{i}\in F(a_{i}))_{i\in I}
, there exists a unique
x\in F(a)
such that

F_{a\geq a_{i}}(x)=x_{i}
for all
i\in I
.

Sheaves on
\mathcal{L}
with natural transformations between them form the category
\mathit{Shv}\left({\mathcal{L}}\right)
. It is a full subcategory of
\mathit{Set}^{\mathcal{L}^{\mathit{op}}}
.

Remark 2.7.4
:

[
1
, Section 2]
Let
F:\mathcal{L}^{\mathit{op}}\rightarrow\mathit{Set}
be a sheaf. Then
F(\bot)\cong\{*\}
.

Proof:

As
\bot=\bigvee\emptyset
, the empty family
\emptyset
is a cover of
\bot
.
Due to the sheaf condition, there must exist a unique element
x\in F(\bot)
such that the restriction conditions
F_{\bot\geq a_{i}}(x)=x_{i}
are satisfied for all
i
in the empty index set. Since there are no elements in the empty family, there are no restriction conditions to check, so any element
x\in F(\bot)
satisfies them vacuously. The sheaf condition states that there is a unique such element in
F(\bot)
, thus
F(\bot)
must be a singleton set.

\square

When defining certain sheaves, it is often necessary to define them by case distinction where
\bot
maps to
\{*\}
and everything else maps to something more interesting. To avoid writing down this case distinction, we can write a single definition to lift a functor
F:\mathcal{L}_{\neq\bot}\rightarrow\mathit{Set}
that is not defined at
\bot
to one that includes
\bot
. If the result is supposed to be a sheaf, this lifting is unique up to unique isomorphism. Note that for a locale
\mathcal{L}
,
\mathcal{L}_{\neq\bot}=\mathcal{L}\setminus\{\bot\}
is merely a poset.

Definition 2.7.5
:

Let
F:\mathcal{L}_{\neq\bot}\rightarrow\mathit{Set}
be a functor.
Then we define the functor
F^{\bot}:\mathcal{L}\rightarrow\mathit{Set}
by

\displaystyle F^{\bot}(a)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}F(a)&a\neq\bot\\
\{*\}&a=\bot\end{cases}

\displaystyle F^{\bot}(*:a\leq b)(x)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}F(*:a\leq b)(x)&a\neq\bot\text{ and }b\neq\bot\\
*&a=\bot\text{ or }b=\bot.\end{cases}

Lemma 2.7.6
:

Let
F:\mathcal{L}_{\neq\bot}\rightarrow\mathit{Set}
be a functor. Then

is a sheaf if and only if

satisfies the

:

For all covers
(a_{i}\in\mathcal{L}_{\neq\bot})_{i\in I}

F(*:a_{i}\leq a)(x)=x_{i}
.

Proof:

1.

Let
F^{\bot}
satisfy the ordinary sheaf condition. We prove that

satisfies the bottomless sheaf condition:

Let
(a_{i}\in\mathcal{L}_{\neq\bot})_{i\in I}
be a cover of
a\in\mathcal{L}_{\neq\bot}
.
As
\mathcal{L}_{\neq\bot}\subseteq\mathcal{L}
and because

and

act identically on non-bottom elements,
we can then use the ordinary sheaf condition of

to derive the unique element for every compatible family.

2.

Let
F
satisfy the bottomless sheaf condition. We prove that
F^{\bot}
satisfies the ordinary sheaf condition:

Let
(a_{i}\in\mathcal{L})_{i\in I}
be a cover of
a\in\mathcal{L}
.

If

If

We can also formulate the procedure of adjoining a bottom element as an equivalence of categories.

Definition 2.7.7
:

The category
\mathit{Shv^{*}}\left({\mathcal{L}}\right)
is the full subcategory of
\mathit{Set}^{\mathcal{L}_{\neq\bot}}

Corollary 2.7.8
:

There is an equivalence of categories between
\mathit{Shv}\left({\mathcal{L}}\right)
and
\mathit{Shv^{*}}\left({\mathcal{L}}\right)

Proof:

We can easily extend

(-)^{\bot}:\mathit{Shv^{*}}\left({\mathcal{L}}\right)\rightarrow\mathit{Shv}\left({\mathcal{L}}\right)
by extending the case distinction to morphisms

\displaystyle(\alpha^{\bot})_{a}=\begin{cases}\alpha_{a}&a\neq\bot\\
*\mapsto*&a=\bot.\end{cases}

It is then just as easy to see that
(-)^{\bot}:\mathit{Shv^{*}}\left({\mathcal{L}}\right)\rightarrow\mathit{Shv}\left({\mathcal{L}}\right)
and the restriction functor

\square

The technical reason we do not get a convenient isomorphism of categories in this case is that the singleton set

is not unique, but only unique up to unique isomorphism. So a sheaf

We conclude this section by investigating how categories of sheaves on different locales are related. More general versions of the following results can be found in

The relationship between different sheaf categories can be explored naturally in the language of category theory: A morphism between locales
\phi:\mathcal{L}\rightarrow\mathcal{L}^{\prime}
induces a functor between the categories
\mathit{Shv}\left({\mathcal{L}}\right)
and
\mathit{Shv}\left({\mathcal{L}^{\prime}}\right)
.

Lemma 2.7.9
:

A morphism of locales
\phi:\mathcal{L}\rightarrow\mathcal{L}^{\prime}
induces a functor
\mathit{Shv}\left({\phi}\right):\mathit{Shv}\left({\mathcal{L}}\right)\rightarrow\mathit{Shv}\left({\mathcal{L}^{\prime}}\right)
defined by

\displaystyle\mathit{Shv}\left({\phi}\right)(F)=F\circ\phi

\displaystyle\mathit{Shv}\left({\phi}\right)(\alpha:F\Rightarrow F^{\prime})_{a}=\alpha_{\phi(a)}.

Proof:

Note that
\phi
is a functor
\phi:\mathcal{L}^{\prime}\rightarrow\mathcal{L}
by
2.6.6
, and thus the restriction maps of
F\circ\phi
are given by

(F\circ\phi)_{a\geq b}=(F\circ\phi)(*:a\geq b)=F(*:\phi(a)\geq\phi(b))=F_{\phi(a)\geq\phi(b)}

for all
a,b\in\mathcal{L}^{\prime}
.
We verify that
F\circ\phi:\mathcal{L}^{\prime}\rightarrow\mathit{Set}
is a sheaf for any sheaf
F:\mathcal{L}\rightarrow\mathit{Set}
.

Let
(a_{i}\in\mathcal{L}^{\prime})_{i\in I}
be a cover of
a\in\mathcal{L}^{\prime}
. As
\phi
preserves joins,
(\phi(a_{i})\in\mathcal{L})_{i\in I}
is a cover of
\phi(a)\in\mathcal{L}
. Any compatible family
(x_{i}\in F\circ\phi(a_{i}))_{i\in I}
in the context of
F\circ\phi
is also compatible in the context of
F
, because
\phi
preserves binary meets.

The sheaf condition of
F
grants us a unique
x\in F(\phi(a))
with
F_{\phi(a)\geq\phi(a_{i})}(x)=x_{i}
. This is also the unique element needed for the sheaf condition of
F\circ\phi
, because
F_{\phi(a)\geq\phi(a_{i})}(x)=(F\circ\phi)_{a\geq a_{i}}(x)
.

\square

\mathit{Shv}\left({\mathcal{L}}\right)
to the locale
\mathcal{L}

Lemma 2.7.10

There is a functor
\mathit{Shv}:\mathit{Loc}\rightarrow\mathit{Cat}
that assigns

•

•

the morphism from

Proof:

We verify the functor laws.

(\mathit{Shv}\left({\psi}\right)\circ\mathit{Shv}\left({\phi}\right))(F)=\mathit{Shv}\left({\psi}\right)(F\circ\phi)=F\circ\phi\circ\psi=\mathit{Shv}\left({\psi\circ\phi}\right)(F)

\displaystyle(\mathit{Shv}\left({\psi}\right)\circ\mathit{Shv}\left({\phi}\right))(\alpha:F\Rightarrow F^{\prime})_{a}

\displaystyle=\mathit{Shv}\left({\psi}\right)(\mathit{Shv}\left({\phi}\right)(\alpha:F\Rightarrow F^{\prime}))_{a}

\displaystyle=\mathit{Shv}\left({\phi}\right)(\alpha:F\Rightarrow F^{\prime})_{\phi(\psi(a))}

\displaystyle=\alpha_{\psi\circ\phi(a)}=(\mathit{Shv}\left({\psi\circ\phi}\right))(\alpha:F\Rightarrow F^{\prime})_{a}

\square

Functors preserve isomorphisms, so we directly obtain an intuitive result: Isomorphic locales have isomorphic sheaf categories.

Corollary 2.7.11
:

An isomorphism of locales
i:\mathcal{L}\rightarrow\mathcal{L}^{\prime}
induces an isomorphism of categories
\mathit{Shv}\left({i}\right):\mathit{Shv}\left({\mathcal{L}}\right)\rightarrow\mathit{Shv}\left({\mathcal{L}^{\prime}}\right)
.

3.1
Classical Valued Sets

An ordinary set
X
is fully defined by the membership predicate
\in
. Membership is binary: we either have
x\in X
or
x\notin X
. A fuzzy set allows for different strengths of membership between these two extremes. A mathematical object
x
could be a full member of strength
1
, a non-member of strength
0
, or anything in between.
We can encode this in regular set theory by equipping a set
X
with a map
\mu_{X}:X\rightarrow(0,1]
that assigns a membership strength to every element, where all elements with strength
0
are simply not included in
X
.

Definition 3.1.1
:

A
classical fuzzy set

(X,\mu_{X})
is a set
X
equipped with a map
\mu_{X}:X\rightarrow(0,1]
, called the
membership map
. For an element
x\in X
, the value
\mu_{X}(x)\in(0,1]
is called the
membership strength
of
x
in
(X,\mu_{X})
.

To organize fuzzy sets into a category, we must define morphisms between fuzzy sets.
For a map
f:X\rightarrow Y
between ordinary sets, whenever there is an element
x\in X
, there is also an element
f(x)\in Y
. An intuitive way to lift this idea to fuzzy sets
(X,\mu_{X}),(Y,\mu_{Y})
is to demand that elements
x\in X
of a membership strength
\mu_{X}(x)
are sent to elements with equal or greater membership strength.

Definition 3.1.2
:

Let
(X,\mu_{X})
and
(Y,\mu_{Y})
be classical fuzzy sets. A morphism of fuzzy sets
f:(X,\mu_{X})\rightarrow(Y,\mu_{Y})
is a map
f:X\rightarrow Y
such that

\mu_{Y}(f(x))\geq\mu_{X}(x)\quad\forall x\in X.

This is a specific type of classical valued set category. Other examples of valued sets are normed sets; these are what we get when we forget the structure of a normed vector space but retain the norm:

Definition 3.1.3
:

A
classical normed set

(X,\left\lVert-\right\rVert_{X})
is a set
X
equipped with a map
\left\lVert-\right\rVert_{X}:X\rightarrow[0,\infty)
, called the
norm
.

Morphisms between normed structures are usually defined to be the morphisms that are non-expansive with respect to the norms, so we also demand this.

Definition 3.1.4
:

A
morphism of classical normed sets
from
(X,\left\lVert-\right\rVert_{X})
to
(Y,\left\lVert-\right\rVert_{Y})
is a map
f:X\rightarrow Y
with
\left\lVert f(x)\right\rVert_{Y}\leq\left\lVert x\right\rVert_{X}
for all
x\in X
.

The reason we define morphisms of normed sets to be non-expansive and morphisms of fuzzy sets to be expansive is only to fit the convention in the literature. For fuzzy sets, the convention is that elements must be sent to elements of greater than or equal strength as explained above. For normed sets we demand that they are compatible with established “normed” categories. For example, the category of normed vector spaces and non-expansive maps can be sent to the category of normed sets with a forgetful functor that only erases the vector space structure but keeps the norm.

In general, we define classical valued sets as sets equipped with a map that takes values in a locale, with morphisms defined as maps compatible with the ordering of the locale.

Definition 3.1.5
:

Let
(\mathcal{L},\leq)
be a locale.
The category
\mathit{CVal}\left({\mathcal{L},\leq}\right)
of classical
(\mathcal{L},\leq)
-valued sets consists of

•

classical
(\mathcal{L},\leq)
-valued sets

(X,\mu_{X})
as objects, where
X
is a set and
\mu_{X}:X\rightarrow\mathcal{L}_{\neq\bot}
,

•

morphisms of
(\mathcal{L},\leq)
-valued sets
from
(X,\mu_{X})
to
(Y,\mu_{Y})
: maps
f:X\rightarrow Y
with
\mu_{X}(x)\leq\mu_{Y}(f(x))
for all
x\in X
.

This definition is equivalent to
\text{Fuz}_{0}(L)
from the paragraph after
[
1
, Theorem 6]
. We exclude the bottom element
\bot
directly by specifying
\mathcal{L}_{\neq\bot}
as the set of possible values, while Barr demands that the inverse image of the bottom element, which he notes as 0, is empty.

We can then recover the specific example categories described above from this definition.

Definition 3.1.6
:

1.

For the locale
([0,1],\leq)
, we obtain the
category of classical fuzzy sets

\mathit{CFuz}

:=\mathit{CVal}\left({[0,1],\leq}\right)
.

2.

For the locale
([0,\infty],\geq)
, we obtain the
category of classical normed sets

\mathit{CNSet}

:=\mathit{CVal}\left({[0,\infty],\geq}\right)
.

The category of classical fuzzy sets in
3.1.6
(1) coincides with the one in Spivak
[
9
, Definition 1.1]
.

We conclude this section by investigating how classical valued sets with values in different locales are related. We would expect that isomorphic locales induce isomorphic categories of classical valued sets. This is the case.
However, not every morphism of locales grants us a functor between the respective classical valued sets, because an arbitrary morphism of locales might map every element to
\bot
, which we have excluded in the definition of the value map. Other morphisms that only send
\bot
to
\bot
can also induce functors between categories that are not an equivalence, but we will only need the claim for isomorphisms.

Lemma 3.1.7
:

An isomorphism of locales
\phi:\mathcal{L}\rightarrow\mathcal{L}^{\prime}
induces a functor that is an isomorphism of categories

\mathit{CVal}\left({\phi}\right):\mathit{CVal}\left({\mathcal{L}^{\prime}}\right)\rightarrow\mathit{CVal}\left({\mathcal{L}}\right)

defined by

\displaystyle\mathit{CVal}\left({\phi}\right)(X,\mu_{X})=(X,\phi\circ\mu_{X})

\displaystyle\mathit{CVal}\left({\phi}\right)(f:(X,\mu_{X})\rightarrow(Y,\mu_{Y}))=f:(X,\phi\circ\mu_{X})\rightarrow(Y,\phi\circ\mu_{Y}).

Proof:

Note that the composite
\phi\circ\mu_{X}:X\rightarrow\mathcal{L}_{\neq\bot}
is well defined, because a morphism of locales
\phi:\mathcal{L}\rightarrow\mathcal{L}^{\prime}
is a map
\phi:\mathcal{L}^{\prime}\rightarrow\mathcal{L}
in the opposite direction and due to
2.6.8
.

The fact that
\phi
is monotone ensures that
\mathit{CVal}\left({\phi}\right)(f)
is a morphism of
\mathcal{L}
-valued sets for every morphism of
\mathcal{L}^{\prime}
-valued sets
f
.

It is easy to see that the induced functor is an isomorphism.

\square

We do not want to claim novelty for this isomorphism of categories. We will see in
Section

3.2
that if
\mathcal{L}
is totally connected, the category
\mathit{CVal}\left({\mathcal{L}}\right)
is equivalent to a subcategory of
\mathit{Shv}\left({\mathcal{L}}\right)
. Sheaf theory tells us that the categories
\mathit{Shv}\left({\mathcal{L}}\right)
and
\mathit{Shv}\left({\mathcal{L}^{\prime}}\right)
are isomorphic for isomorphic locales
\mathcal{L}
and
\mathcal{L}^{\prime}

[
4
, Chapter IX, Section 5, Proposition 5]
.

Corollary 3.1.8
:

The category of classical fuzzy sets and the category of classical normed sets from
3.1.6
are isomorphic.

Proof:

The isomorphism

from
2.6.7
induces the isomorphism of categories
\mathit{CVal}\left({i}\right):\mathit{CVal}\left({([0,1],\geq)}\right)\rightarrow\mathit{CVal}\left({([0,\infty],\leq)}\right)
.

\square

3.2
Valued Sets as Sheaves

For a totally connected locale
\mathcal{L}
, classical
\mathcal{L}
-valued sets can be viewed as sheaves. Given a classical
\mathcal{L}
-valued set
(X,\mu_{X})
, we can describe it as the family of sets
(X^{\geq a})_{a\in\mathcal{L}_{\neq\bot}}
where

X^{\geq a}=\{\,x\in X\,\mid\,\mu_{X}(x)\geq a\,\}

only contains the elements with a value of at least
a
. We just have to add the inclusion maps as restrictions and we have a sheaf.

Theorem 3.2.1
:

[
1
, Theorem 3]
Let
\mathcal{L}
be a totally connected locale. There is a functor
\mathit{M}:\mathit{CVal}\left({\mathcal{L}}\right)\rightarrow\mathit{Shv}\left({\mathcal{L}}\right)
defined by

\displaystyle\mathit{M}

\displaystyle(X,\mu_{X})(a)=\begin{cases}\{*\}&a=\bot\\
X^{\geq a}&a\neq\bot\end{cases}

\displaystyle\mathit{M}

\displaystyle(X,\mu_{X})_{a\geq b}(x)=\begin{cases}*&b=\bot\\
x&b\neq\bot\end{cases}

\displaystyle\mathit{M}

\displaystyle(f:(X,\mu_{X})\rightarrow(Y,\mu_{Y}))_{a}=\begin{cases}*\mapsto*&a=\bot\\
f|_{\mathit{M}(X,\mu_{X})(a)}^{\mathit{M}(Y,\mu_{Y})(a)}&a\neq\bot\end{cases}

Proof:

•

\mathit{M}(X,\mu_{X})_{a\geq b}:\mathit{M}(X,\mu_{X})(a)\rightarrow\mathit{M}(X,\mu_{X})(b)
is well defined:

Let
a\geq b\neq\bot
and
x\in\mathit{M}(X,\mu_{X})(a)
. Then
\mu_{X}(x)\geq a\geq b
, thus
x\in\mathit{M}(X,\mu_{X})(b)
.

It is easy to see that these maps satisfy the functor laws.

•

\mathit{M}(X,\mu_{X})
satisfies the sheaf condition:

Let
(a_{i}\in\mathcal{L})_{i\in I}
be a cover of
a\in\mathcal{L}
.

–

For
a=\bot
, the sheaf condition is satisfied by
2.7.4
.

–

For
a\neq\bot
, let
(x_{i})_{i\in I}
be compatible. The compatibility condition

\mathit{M}(X,\mu_{X})_{a_{i}\geq a_{i}\,\wedge\,a_{j}}(x_{i})=\mathit{M}(X,\mu_{X})_{a_{j}\geq a_{i}\,\wedge\,a_{j}}(x_{j})\quad\forall i,j\in I

reduces to
x_{i}=x_{j}\;\forall i,j\in I
with
a_{i},a_{j}\neq\bot
, because we have
a_{i}\,\wedge\,a_{j}\neq\bot
due to the total connectedness of
\mathcal{L}
.
So all
x_{i}
where
a_{i}\neq\bot
coincide as one
x
.
This
x
is the unique element with
\mathit{M}(X,\mu_{X})_{a\geq a_{i}}(x)=x_{i}
for all
i\in I
with
a_{i}\neq\bot
. For all other
x_{i}=*\in\mathit{M}(X,\mu_{X})(\bot)
we have
\mathit{M}(X,\mu_{X})_{a\geq\bot}(x)=*=x_{i}
as well.

•

\mathit{M}(f)
is a natural transformation:

Let
f:(X,\mu_{X})\rightarrow(Y,\mu_{Y})
and
a\geq\bot
. The valued set morphism condition of
f
ensures that
f|_{\mathit{M}(X,\mu_{X})(a)}^{\mathit{M}(Y,\mu_{Y})(a)}
is well defined.
Naturality trivially follows as the restrictions for
a\geq\bot
are just inclusions. For
a=\bot
this is trivially true as well, as there is just one morphism that maps from the one element set to itself.

\square

By
2.7.6
or
2.7.8
, we could have defined
\mathit{M}
without the case distinctions. We include them in this case, but we will usually omit them later.

When we view a classical fuzzy set
(X,\mu_{X})
as a sheaf, in general we can only ever view a part of it by evaluating
\mathit{M}(X,\mu_{X})(a)
which lets us see all elements of value greater than or equal than
a
. It might be tempting to evaluate
\mathit{M}(X,\mu_{X})(\bot)
and expect to view all elements, but this simply gives us the singleton set
\{*\}
(
2.7.4
).

Example 3.2.2
:

•

For a finite classical fuzzy set
X=\{x,y,z,w\}
with

\displaystyle\mu_{X}

\displaystyle:X\rightarrow(0,1]

\displaystyle\mu_{X}

\displaystyle(x)=\mu_{X}(y)=1/3

\displaystyle\mu_{X}

\displaystyle(z)=1/2

\displaystyle\mu_{X}

\displaystyle(w)=1,

we obtain the fuzzy set

\mathit{M}(X,\mu_{X})(a)=\begin{cases}\{*\}&a=0\\
\{w,z,x,y\}&a\in(0,1/3]\\
\{w,z\}&a\in(1/3,1/2]\\
\{w\}&a\in(1/2,1].\\
\end{cases}

Here we have
\mathit{M}(X,\mu_{X})(a)=X
for any

.

•

For an infinite classical fuzzy set
Y=\mathbb{N}

with

\displaystyle\mu_{Y}

\displaystyle:Y\rightarrow(0,1]

\displaystyle\mu_{Y}

\displaystyle(n)=1/n,

we obtain the fuzzy set

\mathit{M}(Y,\mu_{Y})(a)=\begin{cases}\{*\}&a=0\\
\cdots&\\
\{1,2,3,4\}&a\in(1/5,1/4]\\
\{1,2,3\}&a\in(1/4,1/3]\\
\{1,2\}&a\in(1/3,1/2]\\

In this case we have
\mathit{M}(Y,\mu_{Y})(a)\subsetneq Y
for every

.

Not every sheaf can be viewed as a classical valued set because arbitrary sheaves allow for restriction maps to be non-injective: If

in

which restrict to the same element

for some

are “not equal at membership strength

Definition 3.2.3
:

Let
\mathcal{L}
be a totally connected locale.

•

is called an

-valued set
if the restriction map

.

•

The
category of
\mathcal{L}
-valued sets

\mathit{Val}\left({\mathcal{L}}\right)
is the full subcategory of
\mathit{Shv}\left({\mathcal{L}}\right)
with
\mathcal{L}
-valued sets as objects.

From now on, for an
\mathcal{L}

.

Corollary 3.2.4
:

The functor
\mathit{M}:\mathit{CVal}\left({\mathcal{L}}\right)\rightarrow\mathit{Shv}\left({\mathcal{L}}\right)

\mathit{M}:\mathit{CVal}\left({\mathcal{L}}\right)\rightarrow\mathit{Val}\left({\mathcal{L}}\right)

Proof:

The restriction maps
\mathit{M}(X,\mu_{X})_{a\geq b}

\square

Equivalently, we can view

:

Now we can define the other functor in the equivalence from

The general idea for the object map of the following functor

is sketched out in
[

, Section 1]

Lemma 3.2.10
:

Let
\mathcal{L}
be a totally connected locale. There is a functor
\mathit{C}:\mathit{Val}\left({\mathcal{L}}\right)\rightarrow\mathit{CVal}\left({\mathcal{L}}\right)
defined by

\displaystyle\mathit{C}

4
Extended Pseudo-Metric Spaces and (Finite) Metric Realizations

It is well known that simplicial sets can be used to describe many topological spaces via the geometric realization
\mathit{Geom}:\mathit{Set}^{\Delta^{\mathit{op}}}\rightarrow\mathit{Top}
. To construct the geometric realization, one defines a functor
T:\Delta\rightarrow\mathit{Top}
that interprets objects
[0],[1],[2],[3],\ldots
as points, line segments, triangles, tetrahedrons, …, known as the topological
n
-simplices. Then we form a left Kan extension along the Yoneda embedding
y
.
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\Delta}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathit{Top}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathit{Set}^{\Delta^{\mathit{op}}}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces T}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces y}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\eta}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathit{Geom}=\mathit{Lan}_{y}T}

The existence of this Kan extension is guaranteed because
\mathit{Top}
is cocomplete, which allows us to use the colimit formula for Kan extensions. We can think of this as defining what

should do on basic building blocks for topological spaces, then extending to combinations of those building blocks. For a more explicit derivation, see
Section

2.5
.

We want to define a similar concept for simplicial normed sets. We can use the same basic building blocks, but now we must include the notion of membership strength, which we could interpret as some kind of distance, surface area, or volume. However, topological spaces only have an abstract notion of neighborhood, but there is no notion of distance. Intuitively, we could replace topological spaces with metric spaces and then use points, line segments, triangles, tetrahedrons, …of different scales as basic building blocks.

However, the category of metric spaces is not cocomplete, so the existence of the relevant Kan extension is not guaranteed. This motivates the category of extended pseudo-metric spaces, which includes all ordinary metric spaces but is cocomplete. The associated metric realization was first defined by Spivak
[
9
, Section 3]
.

We will begin with the definition of the category of extended pseudo-metric spaces in
Section

4.1
. Spivak proved that this category is cocomplete
[
9
, Section 2]
. We provide a more explicit proof of this result.

We then construct the metric realization and the respective adjoint in
Section

4.2
. Our construction is similar to
[
9
, Section 3]
, although we use normed sets instead of fuzzy sets to simplify computations in
Section

4.3
. We discuss how Spivak’s construction relates to ours in
Section

4.4
using the results from
Section

4.3
.

Both Spivak’s metric realization
[
9
, Section 3]
and ours from
Section

4.2
are constructed in terms of the sheaf-theoretic variant of fuzzy sets and normed sets. The sheaf-theoretic variants make the construction easier, but have the drawback that the constructed functors are harder to understand. To understand them better, we compute their actions in terms of the equivalent category of classical normed sets

in

Finally, we verify the construction of McInnes et al.’s finite variant of Spivak’s metric realization in

\pi:B\rightarrow B/\mathord{\sim}
.

Proof:

Let
(C,d_{C})
be an extended pseudo-metric space and
\pi^{\prime}:B\rightarrow C
be another non-expansive map with
\pi^{\prime}\circ f=\pi^{\prime}\circ g
. Then
\pi^{\prime}
is constant on the equivalence generated by

. The universal property of the coequalizer then follows from the universal property of the quotient

.

\square

[

This follows from the well known fact that arbitrary colimits may be constructed from arbitrary coproducts and coequalizers. The construction can be found in

4.2
The Metric Realization

We can now define the metric realization similar to the geometric realization from
Section

2.5
as a left Kan extension. For this, we define a functor analogous to the functor
G:\Delta\rightarrow\mathit{Top}
from
2.5.2
. This functor is defined using the metric
n
-simplices.

Definition 4.2.1
:

•

For
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
and
a\in[0,\infty)
, the
metric
n
-simplex of size
a
is the extended pseudo-metric space

\Delta^{n,a}=(\Delta^{n},d^{n,a}_{1})

where
\Delta^{n}
is the same underlying set as for the topological
n
-simplex from
2.5.1
and
d_{1}^{n,a}
is the
\ell_{1}
metric

\displaystyle d_{1}^{n,a}

\displaystyle:\Delta^{n,a}\times\Delta^{n,a}\rightarrow[0,\infty)

\displaystyle d_{1}^{n,a}

\displaystyle(x,y)=a\cdot\left\lVert x-y\right\rVert_{1}^{n}\qquad\text{where }\left\lVert v\right\rVert_{1}^{n}=\sum_{0\leq i\leq n}|v_{i}|,

also known as
Manhattan metric
, scaled by
a
.

This definition is inspired by Spivak
[
9
, Section 3]
. However, Spivak defines the underlying sets of the metric
n
-simplices as

\{(x_{0},\ldots,x_{n})\subseteq\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{n+1}\,|\,x_{0}+\cdots+x_{n}=-\log(a)\}

While we define them as

\Delta^{n}=\{(x_{0},\ldots,x_{n})\subseteq\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{n+1}\,|\,x_{0}+\cdots+x_{n}=1\}.

Essentially, the underlying sets of Spivak’s metric simplices scale with the parameter
a
, while we use the same underlying sets and instead scale the
\ell_{1}
-metric.

This implies that our metric
n
-simplex of size 0 contains infinitely many points, all with distance 0 from each other, while Spivak’s metric
n
-simplex of size 0 only contains one point. Our definition avoids the need for rescaling the points when we later define the functor in
4.2.4
. This will make computations easier in
Section

4.3
and resolves the division by 0 error that results from Spivak’s scaling factor
\frac{\log(b)}{\log(a)}
, where
a
is allowed to be 1
[
9
, Section 3]
.

We will discuss Spivak’s use of
-\log(a)
later after
4.4.1
.

Spivak also incorrectly uses the
\ell_{2}
, or Euclidean metric. However, we are forced to choose the
\ell_{1}
metric to make the degeneracy maps non-expansive. The following counterexample shows that this property breaks down for all other
\ell_{p}
metrics with
p\in(1,\infty)
.

Remark 4.2.2
:

For every
\ell_{p}
metric

\displaystyle d_{p}^{n}

\displaystyle:\Delta^{n}\times\Delta^{n}\rightarrow[0,\infty)

\displaystyle d_{p}^{n}

\displaystyle(x,y)=\left\lVert x-y\right\rVert_{p}^{n}\qquad\text{where }\left\lVert v\right\rVert_{p}^{n}=(\sum_{0\leq i\leq n}(v_{i})^{p})^{1/p}

with
p\in(1,\infty)
, the degeneracy maps
s^{n}_{i}
are not non-expansive for
n\geq 1
.

Proof:

Consider
x:=(0,\ldots,\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2},\ldots,0)\in\Delta^{n+1}
where
\frac{1}{2}
is at the
i
th and
(i+1)
th entry. Then the norm of
x
is

\left\lVert x\right\rVert_{p}^{n+1}=\left(\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{p}+\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{p}\right)^{1/p}=2^{(1/p)-1}.

The norm of
s^{n,a}_{i}(x)
is

\left\lVert s^{n,a}_{i}(x)\right\rVert_{p}^{n}=\left(\left(\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{2}\right)^{p}\right)^{1/p}=1.

We have
2^{(1/p)-1}>1
for
p\in(1,\infty)
, thus
s^{n,a}_{i}
is not non-expansive.

\square

Lemma 4.2.3
:

The face
f^{n}_{i}
and degeneracy maps
s^{n}_{i}
from
2.5.1
define non-expansive maps

\displaystyle f^{n,a}:\Delta^{n-1,a}\rightarrow\Delta^{n,a},\quad x\mapsto f^{n}(x)

\displaystyle s^{n,a}:\Delta^{n+1,a}\rightarrow\Delta^{n,a},\quad x\mapsto s^{n}(x)

for
n\in\mathbb{N},\,a\in[0,\infty),\,i\in\{0,\ldots,n\}
.

Proof:

The metric
d_{1}^{n,a}
from
4.2.1
is induced by the norm
a\cdot\left\lVert-\right\rVert_{1}^{n}
, so it suffices to check non-expansiveness with respect to this norm. Since this norm is obtained by scaling
\left\lVert-\right\rVert_{1}^{n}
with the constant factor
a
, it suffices to check non-expansiveness with respect to
\left\lVert-\right\rVert_{1}^{n}
.

1.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N},\,i\in\{0,\ldots,n\}
. The face maps are distance preserving:

\displaystyle\left\lVert f^{n}_{i}(x)\right\rVert_{1}^{n}=

\displaystyle\left\lVert(x_{0},\ldots,0,\ldots,x_{n-1})\right\rVert_{1}^{n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle|x_{0}|+\cdots+|0|+\cdots+|x_{n-1}|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle|x_{0}|+\cdots+|x_{n-1}|=\left\lVert x\right\rVert^{n-1}_{1}

2.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N},\,i\in\{0,\ldots,n\}
. The degeneracy maps are distance preserving:

\displaystyle\left\lVert s^{n}_{i}(x)\right\rVert_{1}^{n+1}=

\displaystyle\left\lVert(x_{0},\ldots,x_{i}+x_{i+1},\ldots,x_{n})\right\rVert_{1}^{n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle|x_{0}|+\cdots+|x_{i}+x_{i+1}|+\cdots+|x_{n-1}|

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(*)}}{{=}}

\displaystyle|x_{0}|+\cdots+|x_{i}|+|x_{i+1}|+\cdots+|x_{n}|=\left\lVert x\right\rVert^{n}_{1}

where

is only an equality because all coordinates are greater than or equal to zero by

, otherwise we would only have
|x_{i}+x_{i+1}|\leq|x_{i}|+|x_{i+1}|
.

\square

With the previous lemmas, we can directly assemble the metric

.

For

\square

4.3
The Classical Metric Realization

Unfortunately, the metric realization from
4.2.6
gives little insight into the space constructed. Even when it is expressed as the coequalizer from
4.2.7
, simplices of every size participate in the formula. The reason for this is that it is constructed in terms of the sheaf-theoretic variant of valued sets.

We can obtain the classical perspective if we compose with the equivalence from
3.3.4
that translates between the classical and the sheaf-theoretic perspective:

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\Delta\times([0,\infty),\geq)}

\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathit{Sing}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathit{CSing}}

\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\simeq\,}

by composing the metric realization
\mathit{MetRe}
from
4.2.6
with the functor
\mathit{SM}
from
3.3.4
that translates simplicial classical valued sets into sheaf-theoretic uncurried simplicial valued sets. We also obtain the classical singular nerve
\mathit{CSing}
and the classical Yoneda embedding

by composing the functor
\mathit{Sing}
from
4.2.6
and the Yoneda embedding

\mathit{SM}

We can then compute the actions of the functors
\mathit{CMetRe}

\mathit{CSing}

\mathit{MetRe}
,
\mathit{Sing}
,
\mathit{y}

We begin by computing the actions of the classical metric realization. The key insight to understand the following proof is that the equivalence relation
\sim
from
2.5.4
fulfills two distinct roles:

1.

When we allow
b\geq a
but fix the morphism
f=\mathit{id}_{[m]}
, the equivalence relation
\sim
reduces to

\iota_{m,b,S(\mathit{id}_{[m]},*)(s)}(x)\sim\iota_{m,a,s}(T(f,*:b\geq a)(x))=\iota_{m,a,s}(G(\mathit{id}_{[m]})(x))=\iota_{m,a,s}(x).

In that case, a simplex
\Delta^{m,b}
in the component indexed by
S(\mathit{id}_{[m]},*)(s)
is identified with a smaller simplex
\Delta^{m,a}
indexed by
s
.

2.

When we fix
a=b
but allow any morphism
f:[m]\rightarrow[n]
, the equivalence relation
\sim
reduces to

\iota_{m,a,S(\mathit{id}_{[m]},*)(s)}(x)\sim\iota_{m,a,s}(T(f,*:b\geq a)(x))=\iota_{m,a,s}(G(f)(x)),

which resembles the equivalence relation for the geometric realization from
2.5.4
and is responsible for gluing simplices along their faces and eliminating degeneracies.

Whenever two simplices of different sizes are glued as described in 1., the resulting space is equivalent to a space constructed using a coproduct with the same components but missing the larger simplex. So we could remove all simplices
\Delta^{n,b}
from the coproduct in
2.5.4
for which there is a metric
n
-simplex
\Delta^{n,a}
of smaller size
a\leq b
but equal dimension that is glued to it as in 1.

If the uncurried simplicial normed set
S
in
4.2.7
has been constructed by the functor
\mathit{M}
from
3.2.1
from a simplicial classical normed set, then
S(\mathit{id}_{[m]},*:b\geq a)
is the identity map. Then 1. will always identify a component indexed by
s
and
b
with a component that is also indexed by
s
and a smaller size
a
.

Only the smallest size
a
available for a given element
s
will remain. This smallest size is exactly the norm of the element
s
in the original classical normed set, because by
3.2.1
,
s
will only be a member of all sets
S([m],b)
where
b
is greater than or equal to the norm of
s
in the original classical normed set.

We formally capture this intuition in the following proposition. In the proof, we will construct an isomorphism
\phi
from the quotient in
4.2.7
to a simpler quotient that only contains the smallest available simplices as components. The map
\phi
sends every representative of the same point from simplices of different sizes to the smallest simplex available. The inverse map
\psi
injects the remaining smallest representative back into the formula from
4.2.7
.

are mutually inverse, we compute:

\widetilde{\phi}\circ\widetilde{\psi}([\iota_{s}(x)])\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{(def $\widetilde{\psi}$)}}}{{=}}\widetilde{\phi}([\iota_{n,\left\lVert s\right\rVert,s}(x)])\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{(def $\widetilde{\phi}$)}}}{{=}}[\iota_{s}(x)].

\displaystyle\widetilde{\psi}\circ\widetilde{\phi}([\iota_{n,a,s}(x)])

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{(def $\widetilde{\phi}$)}}}{{=}}\widetilde{\psi}([\iota_{s}(x)])

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{(def $\widetilde{\psi}$)}}}{{=}}[\iota_{n,\left\lVert s\right\rVert,s}(x)]

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\sim)}}{{=}}[\iota_{n,\left\lVert s\right\rVert,s}(T(\mathit{id}_{[n]},*:a\geq\left\lVert s\right\rVert)(x))]

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{(def $T$)}}}{{=}}[\iota_{n,a,s}(G(f)(x))]\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{(def $G$)}}}{{=}}[\iota_{n,a,s}(G(f)(x))],

where

is due to the fact that

.

For the action on morphisms, we must precompose with
\widetilde{\psi}
to translate an equivalence class back into the original representation, send it through the original action on a morphism

and then postcompose with

to translate back into the new representation. This gives

\displaystyle\widetilde{\phi}\circ(\mathit{MetRe}\circ\mathit{SM}(\alpha))\circ\widetilde{\psi}([\iota_{s}(x)])=

\displaystyle\widetilde{\phi}\circ(\mathit{MetRe}\circ\mathit{SM}(\alpha))([\iota_{n,\left\lVert s\right\rVert,s}(x)])

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\widetilde{\phi}([\iota_{n,\left\lVert s\right\rVert,\mathit{SM}(\alpha)_{n,\left\lVert s\right\rVert}(s)}(x)])

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\widetilde{\phi}([\iota_{n,\left\lVert s\right\rVert,\mathit{M}(\alpha_{n})_{\left\lVert s\right\rVert}(s)}(x)])

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\widetilde{\phi}([\iota_{n,\left\lVert s\right\rVert,\alpha_{n}(s)}(x)])=[\iota_{\alpha_{n}(s)}(x)].

\square

We now compute the classical singular nerve
\mathit{CSing}:\mathit{EPMet}\rightarrow\mathit{CNSet}^{\Delta^{\mathit{op}}}
that is left adjoint to the classical metric realization. We can directly simplify the action of this functor on an extended pseudo-metric space
E
. Then
\mathit{CSing}
constructs a simplicial classical normed set
S
, where the individual sets
S_{n}
contain equivalence classes of non-expansive maps

that map metric

. This direct simplification is still difficult to work with. However, we can identify the quotient set
S_{n}
with the set that contains all Lipschitz continuous maps
f:\Delta^{n,1}\rightarrow E
without the need for equivalence classes. This also allows us to elegantly describe the norm
\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{S_{n}}
as the best Lipschitz constant of
f
.

Before we prove this, we recall some terminology on Lipschitz continuity.

Definition 4.3.3
:

Let
M
and
N
be extended pseudo-metric spaces and let
f:M\rightarrow N
be a map.

1.

A constant
c\in[0,\infty)
is called a
Lipschitz constant
for
f
if

d_{N}(f(x),f(y))\leq cd_{M}(x,y)\qquad\text{ for all }x,y\in M.

2.

The map
f
is called
Lipschitz continuous
if it has a Lipschitz constant.

3.

The smallest Lipschitz constant for a Lipschitz continuous map
f
is called the
best Lipschitz constant
.

4.

The
set of Lipschitz continuous maps

\mathit{Lip}(M,N):=\{\,f:M\rightarrow N\,|\,f\text{ is Lipschitz continuous}\,\}

becomes a normed set with the norm that assigns the best Lipschitz constant

\left\lVert f\right\rVert:=\inf\{\,a\,|\,a\text{ is a Lipschitz constant for }f\,\}.

The intuition here is that Lipschitz maps are essentially non-expansive maps where we are first allowed to stretch
M
by some factor
c\in(0,\infty]
. Note that even though the extended pseudo-metric might assign
\infty
, we do not allow
\infty
as a Lipschitz constant here. Otherwise all maps would be Lipschitz continuous. Also note that every Lipschitz continuous map has a best Lipschitz constant given by the infimum over all Lipschitz constants, which always exists in
[0,\infty)
and is a Lipschitz constant itself.

Proposition 4.3.4
:

The right adjoint to the functor
\mathit{CMetRe}
is the classical singular nerve

\mathit{CSing}:=\mathit{SC}\circ\mathit{Hom}_{\mathit{EPMet}}\left(T(-,-),-\right):\mathit{EPMet}\rightarrow\mathit{CNSet}^{\Delta^{\mathit{op}}}.

It assigns

•

•

to a non-expansive map

We begin by computing (

Ultimately, the difference between Spivak’s and our metric realization is only how we interpret the result but mathematically immaterial.

When we realize a normed set using our metric realization from
4.2.6
, the norm of each element dictates the size of the corresponding simplex in the metric realization.

When we realize a fuzzy set using Spivak’s metric realization from
4.4.1
, the membership strength dictates “closeness”. Two points connected by a 1-simplex will be closer if the membership strength is larger. At membership strength 1, both points will be identified. If we reduce the membership strength, then the two points will be increasingly farther apart the closer we approach 0.

The choice of the negative logarithm or the interval
(0,1]
is not canonical. Any other isomorphism of locales
\phi:([0,\infty],\geq)\rightarrow(\mathcal{L},\preceq)
would grant us yet another parametrization
\mathit{MetRe}_{\phi}(S)
of our metric realization from
4.2.6
with

\mathit{CMetRe}_{\phi}(S)=\mathit{CMetRe}(\mathit{CVal}\left({\phi}\right)(S)).

All computations from
Section

4.3
could be done with every other parametrization as well. However, this would lead to more complicated formulas, because the reparametrization by
\phi
causes
\phi
to appear in the coequalizer. Our parametrization in terms of normed sets is the only one which avoids this, so it could be argued to be the most canonical choice, or at least the simplest choice.

4.5
The Finite Metric Realization

McInnes et al.
[
5
, Theorem 2]
construct a finite variant of Spivak’s metric realization from
4.4.1
. We will attempt to reproduce this result. The finite metric realization we construct in this section could equivalently be defined in terms of normed sets or any other parametrization obtained through another isomorphic locale as explained after
4.4.3
. We intend to discuss the claimed relationship between this functor and the UMAP algorithm in
Section

5.4
, so we will use the same parametrization as McInnes et al.

For the finite metric realization, McInnes et al. introduce finite variants of the relevant categories.

The first finite variant is the category of finite extended pseudo-metric spaces, introduced in the paragraph just after
[
5
, Definition 6]
. Their definition does not precisely state that finiteness in this case refers to finiteness of the underlying set, but this definition seems to be compatible with the other results from McInnes et al.
[
5
]
, so we will use this definition.

Definition 4.5.1
:

The
category of finite extended pseudo-metric spaces

\mathit{Fin-}{\mathit{EPMet}}
is the full subcategory of
\mathit{EPMet}
where the underlying set is finite.

When we restrict ourselves to
\mathit{Fin-}{\mathit{EPMet}}
, we lose infinite cocompleteness, which was required to show the existence of Spivak’s (
4.4.1
) and our (
4.2.6
) metric realization.

Remark 4.5.2
:

\mathit{Fin-}{\mathit{EPMet}}
is finitely cocomplete, but not cocomplete.

It is easy to see that coequalizers can still be constructed in
\mathit{Fin-}{\mathit{EPMet}}
, because an equivalence relation can only decrease the number of points but not increase it. However, coproducts can only be constructed in
\mathit{Fin-}{\mathit{EPMet}}
if the index set for the components is finite. An obvious counterexample is the coproduct
\coprod_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\{*\}
where
\{*\}
is the finite extended pseudo-metric space that contains a single point with distance of 0 to itself.

Some colimits over infinite diagrams might still exist if the coequalizer identifies enough points such that only finitely many equivalence classes remain in the colimit object. However, the standard construction from
[
7
, Theorem 3.4.12]
of colimits from coequalizers is only guaranteed to work if the diagram category is finite.

McInnes et al. also introduce a finite variant of the category
\mathit{USFuz}
from
3.3.5
(1) in the paragraph just before
[
5
, Definition 7]
. Here McInnes et al. only state that this category is defined as the subcategory of objects in
\mathit{USFuz}
which are “bounded”, but do not define what makes an object in
\mathit{USFuz}
“bounded”. The following is our interpretation of this definition, again, with the goal of obtaining similar results to
[
5
]
.

Definition 4.5.3
:

•

A fuzzy set
X:((0,1],\leq)^{\mathit{op}}\rightarrow\mathit{Set}
is called finite, if there exists an upper bound
B_{X}
such that all sets
X^{\geq a}
for

have a cardinality less than
B_{X}
.

The
category of finite fuzzy sets

\mathit{Fin-}{\mathit{Fuz}}
is the full subcategory of
\mathit{Fuz}
that has finite fuzzy sets as objects.

•

An uncurried simplicial fuzzy set
S:(\Delta\times((0,1],\leq))^{\mathit{op}}\rightarrow\mathit{Set}
is called finite if
S([n],-):(0,1],\leq)^{\mathit{op}}\rightarrow\mathit{Set}
is finite for every

.

The
category of finite uncurried simplicial fuzzy sets

\mathit{Fin-}{\mathit{USFuz}}
is the full subcategory of
\mathit{USFuz}
that has finite uncurried simplicial fuzzy sets as objects.

We will later need a key property of finite uncurried simplicial fuzzy sets to verify the existence of McInnes et al’s finite metric realization in

: For some fuzzy sets

, we cannot see all elements in

by looking at a set

for sufficiently small

, as shown in

. However, for finite classical fuzzy sets, this is always the case.

We will formally capture this in

. For this, we first introduce definitions for the smallest admissible upper bound

of the cardinalities of the sets

and the largest value

:

, because all sets

for

:

Let

Thus, by

,

The Theory Behind UMAP?

In this section we will comment on the relationship between UMAP and the finite metric realization as claimed by

]

We begin by describing the general idea of dimensionality reduction algorithms in

As the UMAP algorithm uses undirected weighted graphs as a data structure in the intermediate computational steps, we introduce them in

5.1
Dimensionality Reduction Algorithms

The UMAP algorithm is a dimensionality reduction algorithm. An overview of topological dimensionality reduction algorithms can be found in
[
10
]
. In general, a dimensionality reduction algorithm accepts an input dataset
X=\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{k}\}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{D}
and possibly additional parameters. It outputs a dataset
Y=\{y_{1},\ldots,y_{k}\}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
where
d<D
, while attempting to preserve properties of interest. What it means to preserve properties of interest is not always formally stated and depends on the application.

For example, one could demand that clusters of points in
X
should result in clusters of the corresponding points in
Y
.

One could also demand that topological features are preserved. For example, if the input points in
X\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{100}
are distributed roughly along a circle
S^{1}
embedded in
\mathbb{R}^{100}
, one could demand that the algorithm produces a set of output points
Y\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{2}
also distributed along a circle
S^{1}
, embedded in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
.

Additionally, we could demand the preservation of geometric features. For example, if the input points are distributed along an ellipse in
\mathbb{R}^{100}
, one might demand that the output points are also distributed along an ellipse while preserving the eccentricity of the original ellipse.

Most algorithms only preserve these conditions if we already know that the input dataset
X
satisfies specific properties necessary for the specific algorithm.

5.2
Weighted Graphs and Fuzzy Unions

A graph consists of a set of vertices, where each pair of vertices may or may not be connected by an edge. In our case these edges are not directed. The edges of a graph can be equipped with additional information, which is usually referred to as weighting the edges.

Definition 5.2.1
:

An
undirected
W
-weighted graph
(V,E,w_{E})
consists of

•

a set
V
called vertices,

•

a subset
E\subseteq\{\{u,v\}\,|\,u,v\in V,u\neq v\}
,

•

and a weight map
w_{E}:E\rightarrow W
with values in a set
W
.

Weighted graphs are commonly used in many algorithms. For example, a route finding algorithm may use graphs to represent a network of roads where the vertices are intersections, the edges are roads, and the weights correspond to the lengths of the roads. Weighted graphs can also relate to non-geometric data. In a social network, a graph could use the vertices to represent users and the edges to model interactions between them, where the weights correspond to the number of interactions.

The UMAP algorithm uses
(0,1]
-weighted graphs in intermediate computational steps. These graphs relate to classical fuzzy sets.

Remark 5.2.2
:

Any undirected
(0,1]
-weighted graph
(V,E,w_{E})
defines a classical fuzzy set
(E,w_{E})
.

One step of the UMAP algorithm requires forming the union of such graphs. This union operation is inspired by the unions of classical fuzzy sets. To define a union of classical fuzzy sets
(A,\mu_{1})
and
(A,\mu_{2})
with the same underlying set, we must decide a way to compute the membership strength of an element
x
from the possibly different membership strengths in
\mu_{1}(x)
and
\mu_{2}(x)
. We also want the union of fuzzy sets to satisfy properties similar to the union of ordinary sets such as symmetry or associativity. One way to define such unions is via
T
-conorms.

Definition 5.2.3
:

[
3
, Section 3.4]
A
T
-conorm
, also called
S
-norm
, is a map
c:[0,1]\times[0,1]\rightarrow[0,1]
satisfying the following axioms for
x,y,z\in[0,1]
:

•

c(x,y)=c(y,x)
(symmetry).

•

c(x,y)\leq c(z,y)\text{ for }x\leq z
(monotonicity).

•

c(x,c(y,z))=c(c(x,y),z)
(associativity).

•

c(x,0)=x
(boundary condition).

Example 5.2.4
:

[
3
, Section 3.4]

•

The maximum map
\max:[0,1]\times[0,1]\rightarrow[0,1]
is a T-conorm.

•

The algebraic sum
P:[0,1]\times[0,1]\rightarrow[0,1]
is given by

P(x,y)=x+y-xy.

This conorm is referred to as the probabilistic
T
-conorm by McInnes et al.
[
5
]
.

A union of fuzzy sets
(A,\mu_{1})
and
(A,\mu_{2})
with the same underlying set
A
can then be defined by assigning each element
x\in A
the membership strength
c(\mu_{1}(x),\mu_{2}(x))
, where
c:[0,1]\times[0,1]\rightarrow[0,1]
is any conorm.

Definition 5.2.5
:

[
3
, Section 3.4]
The
binary union of classical fuzzy sets

(A,\mu_{1})
and
(A,\mu_{2})
with identical underlying sets
A
, with respect to the conorm
c:[0,1]\times[0,1]\rightarrow[0,1]
is defined by

(A,\mu_{1})\cup_{c}(A,\mu_{2}):=(A,\mu_{c}),

where the membership strength map
\mu_{c}:A\rightarrow(0,1]
is given by

\mu_{c}(x)=c(\mu_{1}(x),\mu_{2}(x)).

The reason why
T
-conorms are defined as in
5.2.3
is that the properties of T-conorms result in the following properties of the union of fuzzy sets.

Remark 5.2.6
:

Let
A,B,C
be fuzzy sets with identical underlying sets and let
c
be a
T
-conorm. Then we have

\displaystyle A\cup_{c}B

\displaystyle=B\cup_{c}A

(symmetry)

\displaystyle A\cup_{c}(B\cup_{c}C)

\displaystyle=(A\cup_{c}B)\cup_{c}C

(associativity)

\displaystyle A\cup_{c}\emptyset

\displaystyle=A

(identity element)

The monotonicity condition ensures that this union is compatible with classical fuzzy subsets. However, we will not need classical fuzzy subsets in this thesis.

We can define a similar union operation for undirected
(0,1]
-weighted graphs.

Definition 5.2.7
:

The
binary union of two undirected
(0,1]
-weighted graphs
with identical vertices and edges
G=(V,E,w_{1})
and
G^{\prime}=(V,E,w_{2})
with respect to the conorm
c:[0,1]\times[0,1]\rightarrow[0,1]
is defined by

(V,E,w_{1})\cup_{c}(V,E,w_{2}):=(V,E,w_{c}),

where the weight map
w_{c}:E\rightarrow(0,1]
is given by

w_{c}(e)=c(w_{1}(e),w_{2}(e)).

This union of undirected
(0,1]
-weighted graphs coincides with the union of the classical fuzzy sets induced by graphs from
5.2.2
.

5.3

The algorithm UMAP is motivated and described in
[

with respect to the weights. This yields a set of coordinates
Y_{0}
for the vertices of
G
.

Spectral embedding is a well-known algorithm that can be used to embed the vertices of a weighted graph into any
\mathbb{R}^{n}
for an
n\in\mathbb{N}
of our choice such that the weights of the edges translate roughly to the distance of the points in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
for some metric. The vertices are not required to have coordinates to run the spectral embedding algorithm, because the algorithm treats the vertices purely as labels and generates coordinates.

In the case of UMAP, this means that the spectral embedding step disregards the original coordinates of the vertices
X
in
G
and generates entirely new coordinates while attempting to place vertices connected with an edge of weight close to 1 near each other and vertices connected with an edge of weight closer to 0 or not connected at all further apart.

4.

[
5
, Algorithm 5]
The coordinates in
Y_{0}
are then optimized further by stochastic gradient descent for
n\mathit{-epochs}
iterations.

For this, a new graph
G_{Y_{0}}
is constructed. This graph has the vertex set
Y_{0}
and the edges have weights that depend on the Euclidean distance of the endpoints in the embedding
Y_{0}
.
We then compute one gradient descent step, which attempts to minimize dissimilarity between the weights of
G
and
G_{Y_{0}}
by varying randomly chosen coordinates of
Y_{0}
. This yields new coordinates
Y_{1}
and a new graph
G_{Y_{1}}
.
We then compute the next step, which attempts to minimize cross entropy between
G
and
G_{Y_{1}}
and so on.
We stop this procedure after
n\mathit{-epochs}
iterations. The final output of the algorithm is the dataset
Y:=Y_{n\mathit{-epochs}}
.

In theory, the spectral embedding in step 3 could be omitted by initializing step 4 with random coordinates
Y_{0}
. In essence, step 3 serves the purpose of generating good initial values for faster convergence and better numerical stability of the iterative approximation in step 4
[
5
, Section 3.2]
.

5.4
Discussion

As mentioned in
Section

5.3
, UMAP seeks to preserve the topological structure of a Riemannian manifold
\mathcal{M}
from which we assume the input is sampled. There are no formal theorems to justify this claim in the article
[
5
]
as stated in
[
5
, Section 7]
. However, McInnes et al. informally justify the algorithm with the following claims about intermediate steps of the algorithm. We comment on these claims and relate to our work:

1.

McInnes et al. claim that when the input dataset
X=\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{N}\}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{D}
is assumed to be sampled from a Riemannian manifold
\mathcal{M}
embedded in
\mathbb{R}^{D}
, the metric can be locally rescaled such that the data, even if not sampled uniformly from
\mathcal{M}
, appears uniformly sampled with respect to the locally rescaled metric
[
5
, Section 2.1]
.

Lemma 1 in
[
5
, Section 2.1]
and the related
[
5
, Appendix A]
do not make a formal argument in terms of probability distributions over
\mathcal{M}
. Further work is necessary to verify this claim.

2.

Definition 9 in
[
5
]
defines the fuzzy topological representation of a dataset
X
as
\bigcup_{0\leq i\leq N}\text{FinSing}(X,d_{i})
, where
d_{i}
corresponds to the local metric around
X_{i}
from claim
1
.

In
[
5
, Section 3.1]
, McInnes et al. claim that, when considered as a fuzzy set, the local graphs
G_{i}
constructed in
Section

5.3
, step 1 correspond to the 1-skeletons of
\text{FinSing}(X,d_{i})
from
[
5
, Definition 9]
and that the union of the local graphs
G
corresponds to the 1-skeleton of

\bigcup_{0\leq i\leq N}\text{FinSing}(X,d_{i}).

Here the extended pseudo metrics
d_{i}:X\times X\rightarrow[0,\infty]
are given by

d_{i}(x_{j},x_{k})=\begin{cases}d_{\mathcal{M}}(x_{j},x_{k})-\rho&\text{if }$i = j$\text{ or }$i = k$\\
\infty&\text{else}\end{cases},

where
\rho
is the distance of
x_{i}
to the nearest point and
d_{\mathcal{M}}
is approximated as described in
[
5
, Section 2.1]
.

We have verified the construction of the finite singular nerve in
4.5.9
. By expressing this functor in terms of classical fuzzy sets in
4.5.14
, we established that the classical fuzzy set
\mathit{Fin-}{\mathit{CSing}}(X,d_{i})_{1}
contains an element for each pair of points
x,y
of finite distance with membership strength
\exp(-d_{i}(x,y))
, see
4.5.15
. Thus, by
5.2.2
, the local graphs
G_{i}
correspond to the classical fuzzy set
\mathit{Fin-}{\mathit{CSing}}(X,d_{i})_{1}
, so this claim appears to be correct.

Further work is necessary to extend the union of classical fuzzy sets to simplicial classical fuzzy sets and verify the correspondence between
G
and
\bigcup_{0\leq i\leq N}\mathit{Fin-}{\mathit{CSing}}(X,d_{i}).

3.

In
[
5
, Section 3.1]
, McInnes et al. claim “Intuitively one can think of the weight of an edge as akin to the probability that the given edge exists. Section 2 demonstrates why this construction faithfully captures the topology of the data.”

This claim is later used to theoretically justify the choice for the probabilistic
T
-conorm for the union in step
2

[
5
, Section 4.1]
. It is also used to justify the choice of cross entropy in step
4

[
5
, Section 3.2]
, as cross entropy is a measure of divergence of probability distributions.

The article
[
5
]
does not provide a probability-theoretic argument for this claim. The article
[
5
]
also does not define a probability theoretic event that corresponds to an edge existing. We are thus not yet convinced that the weights of edges of the graph
G
should be thought of as probabilities.

Further work is necessary to formally capture this claim and verify it. If a justified interpretation as probabilities can be found, it should also be verified that this interpretation is compatible with claim
2
.

4.

McInnes et al. claim that if one accepts the claim that
G
meaningfully captures the topological structure of
\mathcal{M}
, the iterative procedure from step
4
produces lower-dimensional output coordinates
Y
that match the topology of
\mathcal{M}
as close as possible. Further work is necessary to verify this claim.

In this thesis, we verified the metric realizations of several variants of fuzzy sets and showed how they can be realized as left Kan extensions along Yoneda embeddings. Together with the explicit descriptions of these Kan extensions, this gives a precise description of the mathematical framework that describes UMAP according to
[
5
]
. However, this thesis does not and cannot establish if this framework explains the efficiency of UMAP or gives any additional insights into this algorithm.
