Longitudinal characterization of gait and balance recovery over 1 year using instrumented analysis in a school-aged child with ischemic stroke: a case report
Authors: Yuta Tsubouchi, Haruka Migita, Shinsuke Nakashima, Kohei Inoue, Yuhi Takeo, Marino Iwakiri, Takashi Kataoka, Taiki Harada, Masashi Kataoka, Nobuhiro Kaku
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundYoung-onset ischemic stroke may lead to persistent motor impairment, despite favorable survival outcomes. However, a detailed longitudinal characterization of gait and balance recovery in young patients remains limited. Instrumented assessments using inertial measurement units and force platforms may provide objective insights into the temporal patterns of functional recovery.Case presentationWe report the case of a previously healthy 11-year-old Japanese girl who developed left hemiparesis due to an ischemic stroke associated with reversible cerebral vasoconstriction syndrome. Gait and balance were assessed longitudinally at 1, 2, 6, 9, and 12 months after onset, using instrumented analysis. Gait stability was quantified using the root mean square (RMS) of trunk acceleration, symmetry using the Lissajous index (LI), and rhythmicity using stride-to-stride time variability (STV). Balance was evaluated using the index of postural stability (IPS). The RMS and IPS improved markedly within the first 6 months, followed by more gradual changes. Gait symmetry continued to improve for up to 9 months. The STV showed a transient increase during early recovery and decreased after 12 months. Lower limb motor function improved from Brunnstrom stage II to stage V during the subacute phase, with corresponding gains in the Fugl–Meyer lower extremity scores.ConclusionsThis case suggests that the recovery of gait stability, symmetry, and rhythmicity after childhood ischemic stroke may follow partially distinct temporal patterns during the first year. Longitudinal instrumented assessments may support stage-specific rehabilitation planning. Further studies are warranted to determine whether similar recovery patterns are consistently observed in larger cohorts.Keywords: Case report, Childhood ischemic stroke, Gait analysis, Postural balance, Inertial measurement unit, Longitudinal assessment, Rehabilitation

Background

Reversible cerebral vasoconstriction syndrome (RCVS) typically resolves within weeks to months and is often considered to have a favorable long-term prognosis. However, when complicated by ischemic stroke, intracerebral hemorrhage, or posterior reversible encephalopathy syndrome, functional impairments may remain [1]. Ischemic lesions associated with RCVS frequently involve the cortical, subcortical, or watershed regions, leading to hemispheric symptoms such as hemiparesis, sensory deficits, aphasia, or apraxia. Even after the acute phase has resolved, impairments that directly affect the activities of daily living, including gait, balance control, and upper limb dexterity, may persist for several months. These residual motor deficits can substantially limit mobility and functional independence despite apparent radiological improvements [2, 3]. Therefore, although RCVS is often regarded as a reversible vascular disorder, early and longitudinal evaluation of motor function, particularly gait and postural control, is essential to guide rehabilitation and support the long-term recovery of physical activity and participation, especially in younger patients.

Young-onset stroke accounts for approximately 10–15% of all stroke cases, with ischemic stroke representing the majority [4]. Although long-term survival is generally expected, motor impairments associated with hemiparesis can profoundly affect life stage-specific roles, including education, employment, parenting, and sports. In younger individuals with hemiparesis, greater neural plasticity often facilitates substantial improvements in muscle strength, gait speed, and balance during the acute and subacute phases [5–7]. However, in the chronic phase, excessive reliance on the nonparetic limb, gait asymmetry, and impaired postural control frequently persist [8–10]. Compared to age-matched healthy individuals, young adults with stroke walk more slowly and less efficiently, with shorter stride lengths and, in some age groups, increased stride widths [11]. These delayed or residual motor impairments are closely associated with patients’ subjective perceptions of recovery and health-related quality of life [12, 13]. Several years after stroke onset, young survivors may continue to experience subtle weaknesses, impaired dexterity, fatigability, and a fear of falling, leading to restrictions in activity and participation. However, previous studies have shown that structured rehabilitation programs, including task-specific gait training, balance exercises, and strengthening exercises targeting the lower limbs and trunk, can improve gait speed, walking endurance, and postural stability, even several months after stroke onset [14, 15]. Therefore, to provide appropriate and individualized rehabilitation for young stroke survivors, a detailed characterization of movement and gait impairments through objective motion and gait analysis is essential.

In recent years, inertial measurement units (IMUs) have been increasingly adopted in clinical physiotherapy for both the quantitative and qualitative assessments of motor function. IMUs impose minimal environmental constraints, are easy to use, and place negligible physical or psychological burden on patients, making them particularly suitable for repeated assessments. IMU-based gait analysis enables objective quantification of key gait characteristics, including trunk sway, gait variability, left–right asymmetry, and gait rhythm, allowing the detection of subtle motor impairments that may not be apparent on routine clinical examination [16–21]. Although IMUs have limitations in capturing detailed gait phase parameters, their low cost, portability, and straightforward data analysis facilitate their implementation in diverse clinical settings, without the need for specialized facilities or personnel. These features make IMUs well-suited for the detailed characterization of gait patterns and longitudinal evaluation of functional recovery with minimal patient burden.

In addition to gait analysis, quantitative assessment of balance function using force platforms provides complementary information on postural control. Although such assessments require dedicated equipment, they allow the objective and sensitive quantification of postural sway within a short measurement time. Compared with commonly used clinical scales, such as the Berg Balance Scale, force platform-based measures can detect subtle changes in balance performance and small improvements that may not be reflected in ordinal scale scores [22]. Although the Berg Balance Scale shows good validity and reliability, it is limited by ceiling effects and ordinal scoring. Force platform assessments provide objective and continuous measures of postural control and may detect subtle changes not captured by clinical scales [23, 24]. Therefore, combining IMU-based gait analysis with instrumented balance assessment enables a more detailed and time-efficient evaluation of motor recovery, supporting the longitudinal monitoring of functional changes during rehabilitation.

This case report aimed to longitudinally characterize gait and balance recovery over the first year after stroke onset in a young patient with ischemic stroke using instrumented analysis and describe the temporal features of motor recovery.

Case presentation

2.1. Case presentation and study design

An 11-year-old Japanese girl with no significant medical history presented with sudden unsteadiness and a fall while participating in endurance running at school (Table 1). She had no history of prior neurological disorders or related medical interventions. She remained conscious and communicative but exhibited weakness of the left upper and lower extremities. Emergency medical services were contacted, and the patient was transported to our hospital. No relevant family history of cerebrovascular or neurological disorders were noted, except that her paternal grandfather had died of stroke. She was a primary school student with no history of smoking, alcohol, or drug use.

Patient characteristics

On admission, a neurological examination revealed left-sided hemiparesis without impaired consciousness. Magnetic resonance imaging revealed acute ischemic lesions in the right middle cerebral artery area. Magnetic resonance angiography revealed moderate stenosis of the right M2 anterior trunk branch (Fig. 1A). Diffusion-weighted imaging with apparent diffusion coefficient mapping revealed reduced signal intensity in the insular cortex, putamen extending to the corona radiata, and the head of the caudate nucleus, consistent with acute ischemic infarction (Fig. 1B). The patient was diagnosed with young-onset ischemic stroke associated with RCVS. Other potential causes of pediatric ischemic stroke were considered but were deemed less likely based on clinical presentation and imaging findings.

Medical management included edaravone administration and calcium channel blockade (Table 2). Rehabilitation therapy was initiated on hospital day 6 and was provided 5 days per week, consisting of 60 minutes of physical therapy and 60 minutes of occupational therapy per session. The rehabilitation program included standing training, balance exercises, gait training, upper limb functional training, and neuromuscular facilitation combined with electrical stimulation. Gait training was progressively advanced according to the recovery stage, incorporating the use of an ankle–foot orthosis during the early phase and body-weight-supported treadmill training in the later phase. Follow-up imaging revealed a gradual reduction in lesion size and improvement in arterial stenosis. The patient was discharged 62 days after the onset of symptoms. After discharge, she returned to elementary school and continued outpatient rehabilitation two to three times per week at another facility. Twelve months after onset, the patient was independent in all activities of daily living and returned to her prestroke level of daily life, although high-intensity physical activity remained restricted. No adverse events related to medical treatment or rehabilitation were observed during the follow-up period.

To characterize the longitudinal changes in gait and balance beyond the early recovery stage, instrumented assessments were conducted at 1, 2, 6, 9, and 12 months after stroke onset. The results of these evaluations are described below.

2.2. Gait analysis using IMU

An IMU (MVP-RF8-GC-2000, MicroStone, Nagano, Japan) was positioned at the third lumbar spinous process and right calcaneal tuberosity. The participants walked barefoot during the measurements. They walked at a self-selected comfortable speed along a 20-m walkway, including 5 m acceleration and deceleration phases. Data were recorded at a 200-Hz sampling frequency and transmitted in real-time to a computer via Bluetooth. The functionality of the IMU was verified visually. Two trials were completed, and data from the second trial were analyzed. The trunk and right foot acceleration data were extracted across 10 gait cycles.

The following gait parameters were computed using Microsoft Excel: stride-to-stride time variability (STV) from right calcaneal tuberosity acceleration to assess gait cycle variability, root mean square (RMS) from trunk acceleration to evaluate gait stability, and Lissajous index (LI) to quantify gait symmetry [16, 17, 19, 20]. The RMS was computed separately for the mediolateral (RMSx), anteroposterior (RMSy), and vertical (RMSz) components. Overall gait instability was quantified using the composite RMS (RMSt). Because RMS is proportional to the square of walking speed, RMS values were normalized by dividing them by the square of walking speed, according to previous studies [18, 19]. In addition, symmetry indices were computed for both the coronal (LIcor) and transverse (LItra) planes. For each plane, Lissajous figures were constructed from the trunk acceleration data. From the posterior view, the peak absolute mediolateral acceleration values were extracted from the four quadrants of the Lissajous figure (first, right upper; second, left upper; third, left lower; fourth, right lower). These peak values were used to calculate symmetry indices for the coronal and transverse planes (Fig. 2).

2.3. Balance function assessment

Balance function was evaluated using the index of postural stability (IPS), which has been employed in several previous studies assessing postural control in young individuals [25, 26]. A force platform (BW-6000; ANIMA, Tokyo, Japan) was used for all measurements. The participant stood barefoot with the medial borders of the feet parallel and separated by 15 cm, maintaining a mildly open stance. During the assessment, the participant faced forward, with both arms hanging naturally on the sides.

Postural sway was first recorded for 10 seconds during quiet standing at the center of the support base. Subsequently, within a range that allowed stable standing, the participant voluntarily shifted her center of pressure (COP) forward, backward, right, and left. Postural sway was recorded for 10 seconds at each position.

For each condition, the sway area was calculated as a rectangular area, obtained by multiplying the maximal anteroposterior and mediolateral COP excursions. The representative sway area was defined as the mean of the rectangular areas obtained under five conditions (center, forward, backward, right, and left). The stability limit area was calculated by multiplying the distance between the mean COP positions during the rightward and leftward shifts by the distance between the mean COP positions during the forward and backward shifts. 2.4. Data analysis

This study involved a single patient; therefore, inferential statistical analyses were not performed. All outcome measures were analyzed descriptively to examine longitudinal changes over time.

Discussion

The main finding of this case report is that gait and balance recovery after young-onset ischemic stroke followed distinct temporal patterns over the first year after stroke onset, as revealed by longitudinal instrumented assessments. To our knowledge, few reports have longitudinally characterized gait and balance recovery over 1 year using combined IMU-based gait analysis and force platform assessment in a school-aged child with ischemic stroke. Regarding gait stability, trunk acceleration variability quantified by RMS showed marked improvement up to 6 months after onset, followed by only minor changes thereafter. In contrast, gait symmetry, quantified using Lissajous indices, continued to improve beyond the early phase, with substantial reductions in asymmetry observed up to 9 months after onset. STV, which reflects gait rhythmicity, showed a transient increase in the early phase and subsequently improved 12 months after onset. Balance function assessed using the IPS also demonstrated a clear time-dependent recovery pattern. Overall, the IPS improved markedly up to 6 months after onset. Analysis of its component parameters indicated that reductions in postural sway occurred predominantly within the first 6 months, whereas expansion of the stability limit area continued for up to 9 months after onset. Taken together, these findings suggest that different components of gait and balance function recover at different rates during the post-stroke course in young individuals.

The improvements observed in RMS and IPS, particularly in the sway area component of IPS, appeared to follow a temporal pattern similar to the general course of motor recovery reported in individuals with post-stroke hemiparesis [27–30]. This pattern has been described across different ages, sexes, and rehabilitation approaches, with early neurobiological processes after stroke considered as key drivers of recovery. Clinically, the first 6 months after onset are regarded as a critical window for motor recovery, beyond which functional gains tend to plateau [30]. Previous studies have suggested that greater motor impairment is associated with increased trunk instability during gait [31]. In the present case, the early reduction in trunk acceleration variability may be consistent with improvements in motor function on the paretic side. Visual inspection of the Lissajous figures 1 month after onset demonstrated reduced trunk acceleration toward the paretic side, accompanied by an apparent compensatory movement toward the nonparetic side. Six months after the onset, this asymmetry in trunk acceleration diminished. Consistent with previous reports indicating that reduced weight shifting toward the paretic side may lead to decreased trunk acceleration and increased gait instability and asymmetry [32], these findings suggest a potential association between motor recovery and changes in mediolateral trunk control (RMSx). However, detailed longitudinal motor impairment assessments beyond 2 months were unavailable. Therefore, the relationship between motor recovery and changes in gait parameters could not be definitively established, and the present observations should be interpreted as descriptive rather than causal.

In contrast to the relatively early improvements in gait variability, gait symmetry quantified by LI continued to improve up to 9 months after onset. This temporal difference suggests that reductions in overall gait instability may not occur simultaneously with the restoration of left–right symmetry. Notably, the stability limit area, a component of the IPS, continued to increase for up to 9 months. This gradual expansion may reflect an increased capacity for weight-shifting within the base of support, potentially including the paretic side. The corresponding increase in trunk acceleration toward the paretic side observed in the Lissajous figures after 6 months may be consistent with this interpretation. These observations may be interpreted in light of previous reports suggesting a relationship between balance control of the paretic limb and post-stroke gait asymmetry [7, 10, 31, 33]. However, given the descriptive nature of this case report, these relationships should be interpreted with caution.

STV exhibited a distinct temporal pattern, increasing during the early phase of recovery and subsequently decreasing by 12 months after onset. Increased stride-to-stride variability has been reported in older adults at a risk of falls and in individuals with neuromuscular disorders. Therefore, we initially anticipated that STV would also be elevated immediately after stroke onset in this case. From the perspective of motor control, previous theoretical frameworks quantitatively demonstrated that the motor system, characterized by redundant degrees of freedom, stabilizes task-relevant variables while allowing variability in other dimensions [34]. In addition, studies on motor learning in rhythmic tasks such as drum tapping have shown that as learning progresses, muscle activation patterns and movement rhythms become more consistent [35]. Considering the present case from the perspectives of motor recovery and motor learning, the transient increase in STV may reflect an intermediate stage of motor reorganization, during which variability is temporarily augmented as the system explores and restructures movement strategies. Over time, this may have been followed by gradual stabilization of the gait rhythm. However, as these observations were derived from a single case, alternative explanations cannot be excluded. Overall, the present findings suggest that the recovery of gait stability, symmetry, and rhythmicity may occur in partially overlapping yet temporally distinct phases, accompanied by gradual changes in balance capacity. Further studies involving larger cohorts are required to clarify these temporal relationships and determine whether similar patterns are consistently observed in young individuals recovering from stroke.

The findings of this case should be interpreted cautiously; however, the temporal patterns observed in gait and balance recovery may provide clinically relevant insights into rehabilitation planning in young stroke survivors. In the present case, improvements in gait stability (RMS) and balance function (IPS) were most prominent within the first 6 months after onset. Previous studies have reported that increased trunk sway in patients with stroke is associated with impaired postural stability and an elevated risk of falls [36]. Therefore, the marked changes observed in RMS and IPS during the early phase may suggest that particular attention to fall prevention is warranted during the first 6 months after onset. Even in young patients, increased physical activity during recovery may lead to residual instability, underscoring the importance of careful monitoring using objective measures. In contrast, gait symmetry (LI) and expansion of the stability limit area continued to improve beyond the early phase. This temporal dissociation indicates that the restoration of the dynamic weight-shifting capacity and left–right symmetry may occur after initial reductions in overall instability. These observations suggest that rehabilitation strategies may need to evolve over time, with an early emphasis on trunk stability and basic gait control, followed by targeted interventions aimed at improving symmetry and paretic-side loading capacity in later stages. Furthermore, the transient increase in STV observed during recovery highlights the importance of interpreting gait variability in a broader clinical context. Temporary increases in variability may reflect adaptive changes, rather than deterioration, in motor control during recovery. Accordingly, longitudinal assessments using multiple quantitative indices may facilitate a more nuanced understanding of functional recovery than reliance on a single clinical measure. Overall, this case illustrates that longitudinal instrumented assessments of gait and balance may support stage-specific rehabilitation planning and contribute to individualized clinical decision-making in young stroke survivors. Given the patient’s age and early motor recovery, a favorable long-term functional prognosis was anticipated.

Several limitations of this case report should be acknowledged. First, it described a single patient; therefore, the findings cannot be generalized to all young patients with stroke. The observed temporal patterns of gait and balance recovery should be interpreted as descriptive rather than definitive. Second, detailed longitudinal assessments of motor impairment, such as the Fugl–Meyer Assessment or Brunnstrom recovery stage scores beyond 3 months after onset, were unavailable. Consequently, the relationship between changes in motor impairment and the observed improvements in gait and balance parameters cannot be definitively established. Third, although IMUs provide a practical and quantitative assessment of trunk acceleration, they do not allow the direct evaluation of joint kinematics or muscle activity. Therefore, the underlying biomechanical mechanisms contributing to the observed changes could not be fully elucidated. In addition, although RMSt and IPS have been associated with fall risk in previous studies, the actual fall incidence was not systematically recorded in this case. Therefore, any inference regarding the fall risk is indirect. Finally, this was an observational report without a control condition, and the rehabilitation content was not experimentally standardized. Accordingly, causal relationships between specific interventions and functional improvements could not be determined. Future studies involving larger cohorts and comprehensive longitudinal motor assessments are required to elucidate the mechanisms and clinical implications of instrumented gait and balance recovery in young survivors of stroke. Therefore, the present findings should be interpreted as exploratory and hypothesis-generating.

Conclusion

This case report demonstrates that the recovery of gait and balance functions after young-onset ischemic stroke may follow partially distinct temporal patterns over the first year after onset. Improvements in gait stability and postural control were observed predominantly within the first 6 months, whereas gait symmetry and dynamic weight-shifting capacity continued to improve beyond this early phase. A transient increase in the gait variability was also observed during the recovery period. These findings highlight the potential value of longitudinal instrumented assessments for capturing stage-specific changes in motor function and may support individualized rehabilitation planning in young stroke survivors. Further studies with larger cohorts are required to confirm these findings.
