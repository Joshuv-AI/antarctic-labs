Introduction

Fixed drug eruptions (FDE) are delayed-type hypersensitivity reactions presenting as well-demarcated, violaceous plaques recurring at the same site following medication re-exposure.1 Lesions may present as erosions, ulcers, or blisters and mimic contact dermatitis, erosive lichen planus, infection, or autoimmune blistering processes.2–4 Although FDE can affect the lips, genital skin, and extremities, vulvar involvement is uncommon.2–4 Histopathology typically shows subepidermal blistering with vacuolar interface change and necrotic keratinocytes, but may be nonspecific in chronic lesions.1,4 Diagnosis, therefore, relies on clinicopathologic correlation. We report a woman with a recurrent fixed vulvar lesion in whom medication withdrawal supported FDE diagnosis despite nondiagnostic biopsy findings.

Case

A 42-year-old woman with well-controlled atopic dermatitis and remote vulvar lichen planus presented with a 2-year history of a recurrent painful erosion on the right posterior labium minus flaring every 2 to 3 days. Medications included combined oral contraceptives, cetirizine, hydroxyzine, pseudoephedrine, omeprazole, and intermittent nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs). Empiric topical corticosteroids were unhelpful.

Examination showed a flaccid bulla with superficial erosion (Fig. 1). Oral mucosa, nails, hair, and nonvulvar skin were unremarkable. Histopathology demonstrated superficial epidermal necrosis and mixed neutrophilic infiltrate without acantholysis (Fig. 2). Features characteristic of lichen planus (LP), including hypergranulosis, saw-toothed acanthosis, and a dense band-like lymphocytic infiltrate, were absent. Direct immunofluorescence was negative for immunoglobulin (Ig)G, IgA, IgM, and C3, a pattern supportive of FDE and atypical for erosive LP, which frequently demonstrates fibrinogen deposition along the basement membrane zone.

Flaccid blister with erosion on the right posterior labium minus, minimal surrounding erythema, with background architectural changes attributable to prior vulvar lichen planus.

Histopathology of the active lesion at 10× magnification demonstrates nonspecific ulceration with necrosis and a mixed inflammatory infiltrate, without acantholysis, subepidermal clefting, or other features suggestive of lichen planus, lichen sclerosus, or autoimmune blistering disease.

A 3-month medication elimination trial discontinuing pseudoephedrine, cetirizine, hydroxyzine, and omeprazole reduced flare frequency but did not eliminate lesions. Subsequent NSAID discontinuation coincided with resolution, although rechallenge was not performed. At follow-up, the patient tolerated acetaminophen, ibuprofen, naproxen, omeprazole, and pseudoephedrine re-exposure without recurrence, making these agents unlikely triggers and suggesting the NSAID association was coincidental. Cetirizine has not been restarted and remains the most likely culprit. Management consists of cetirizine avoidance and supportive care with petrolatum and topical lidocaine.

Discussion

Diagnosing vulvar dermatoses is challenging due to overlapping clinical features and the low diagnostic yield of genital biopsies, which provide definitive diagnoses in only 29% of cases.5 Vulvar FDE is uncommon and frequently underrecognized, particularly with nonspecific histopathology.2,3 Key differentials include erosive lichen planus, autoimmune blistering disease, contact dermatitis, and infectious ulceration. Direct immunofluorescence is typically negative in FDE, helping exclude autoimmune blistering disease.1 Although oral provocation testing is the gold standard, it is often avoided in mucosal disease due to ethical considerations.1 Sulfonamides are most often implicated; other triggers include antibiotics, anticonvulsants, statins, and NSAIDs.1–3

In this case, negative direct immunofluorescence decreased suspicion for erosive LP, an important diagnostic pitfall with chronic vulvar erosions. The patient’s fixed-site recurrence, lack of response to topical corticosteroids, and improvement with medication withdrawal strongly support FDE. Cetirizine is the most likely culprit, as it has not been reintroduced following the elimination trial, while other suspected agents were tolerated upon re-exposure without recurrence. Recognition of this pattern can prevent unnecessary procedures and expedite symptom resolution. Elimination trials may be valuable when multiple potential triggers are present.
