Analogue modelling the influence of topographical slope and viscosity on the landslides in Western Guizhou Province
Authors: Shenghong Li, Wengang He, Peng Luo, Yuanwen Zhao, Bangxiang Wu, Daxiong Yuan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractLandslides are a common geological disaster phenomenon in Guizhou Province. Yearly, different landslides occur and interfere with people’s lives and daily activities; this has attracted the government’s attention and prompted relevant research. However, further research is still needed on the genetic mechanism of landslides and their deformation evolution characteristics. Therefore, five experiments were designed based on field landslide geological parameters, with the topographical slope and the viscosity of the silicone layer as variables, to simulate typical landslides in Western Guizhou Province. Results show that the landslides frequently result from high dip angles and low discontinuity strength. At the same time, the deformation and expansion modes of the landslide change with the geomorphic dip angle and structural strength. Three typical deformation modes are formed: back-edge, middle, and fore-edge; back-edge, fore-edge, and middle; and back-fore edge synchronous, middle, and back-fore edge synchronous. The results are similar to the typical landslides along the Qianxi China-Myanmar oil and gas pipeline, which may effectively direct the work of preventing and reducing landslide disasters, and have important guiding significance for the deformation evolution and early warning of landslides.Keywords: Landslide, Topographical slope, Viscosity of detachment layers, Deformation evolution, Analog modellingSubject terms: Environmental sciences, Natural hazards, Solid Earth sciences

Introduction

Landslides are the most typical geological disasters in the world. Its occurrence will cause huge losses to the population, infrastructure, property, and environment. It has a wide range of distribution, a high frequency of occurrence, a great impact, and a strong rupture force, and it is highly valued by governments at all levels and relevant researchers. Previous studies have shown that the evolution of landslide deformation and influencing factors are closely related to the landform, geological conditions, earthquakes, human activities, and climate change during the slope formation1–6, and many important research results and understandings have been obtained. For example, when the previous applied remote sensing and geographic information systems to study geological disasters, it showed that the formation of landslides can be triggered by natural factors such as earthquakes and rainfall or caused by human production activities7. Early rainfall and brief periods of rainfall can significantly aid in the formation of landslide disasters, according to research by the previous on the effect of Colombia’s climate on landslide deformations8,9. Previous studies analyzed the role of geological disasters induced by the Lushan earthquake and showed that the active nature of the regional fault zone, geomorphic characteristics, topographic gradient, slope stratum inclination, and seismic intensity have important impacts on landslide deformations10. Previous studied that rainfall season, duration, and unique climate change have obvious control effects on landslide deformations11.

In a nutshell, many complex factors influence the deformation evolution of landslides, which are often triggered by multi-factor linkage. In addition to the obvious triggering factors of climate, rainfall, earthquake, and tsunami, as well as glaciers and rivers, its internal control factors are also very obvious. Previous studies have shown that the deformation evolution of landslides can be classified into five formation modes: “slip-pull fracture shear,” “retaining-wall collapse,” “horizontal push,” “anti-dip rock strata toppling deformation,” and “slip-shear of nearly horizontal strata”12–16. Simultaneously, the study shows that the stratum’s ductile shear zone plays a key role in controlling the entire landslide evolution process17–19. However, there is still a scarcity of simulation analysis and discussion on the mechanism of landslides. The characteristics and deformation mechanism of landslides are quite different due to the complexity and variation of the geological conditions and environment in different regions, particularly the complexity of geological factors, and the physical simulation research on the formation mechanism and evolution process of landslides is still very scarce. Moreover, Guizhou Province, located in the middle and upper Yangtze structural belt in the south of China, has extremely complex geological landforms and structural deformation, and the stratum dip angle of the landslide is significantly large. Slope stratum shear zones come in various shapes and strengths, including faults, coal seams, mudstones, and shales. This area has long presented a challenge for the study of structural deformation characteristics in southern China20–23.

The complex geological landforms, large surface fluctuations, and the occurrence of extreme climate have resulted in frequent landslides, rockfalls, debris flows, and other disasters in this area. Furthermore, more than 90% of Guizhou’s land area is mountainous, and most of the population lives in mountainous areas with different landforms (Fig. 1). In 2018, two major geological disasters occurred along the Myanmar-China oil and gas pipelines due to landslide disasters, resulting in heavy casualties and property losses, which attracted considerable attention from the local government. As a result, studies on landslide disasters, especially the simulation studies on the genetic mechanism of landslide disasters along the China-Myanmar oil and gas pipeline, are extremely important for understanding geological disasters and landslide deformation mechanisms in Guizhou and adjacent areas.

Therefore, this study employs physical simulation modelling to investigate the influence of stratum dip angle and the strength of basal weak layers on the deformation mechanisms and evolutionary processes of landslides. The experimental design, grounded in an analysis of the regional geology and geomorphology of the study area, incorporates two distinct series comprising five physical models. These models systematically vary the critical parameters of stratum dip angle and the configuration of silicone (ductile) layers at the base and within the stratigraphic sequence. The primary objective of this research is to elucidate the genetic mechanisms and spatial distribution patterns of landslides endangering oil and gas pipelines in Guizhou, China. The findings are intended to provide a physical-mechanical basis for developing early warning systems for geohazards along pipeline corridors.

Materials and methods

Modelling strategy

Landslides along the Myanmar-China oil and gas pipeline have different common dip angles and shear zones. Manual excavation and detritus accumulation can be seen in a low-angle landslide in this area. The dip angle of the soil stratum is generally within 6°−10°. For example, the slope stratum’s dip angle is 8° for the relocation project in Shazi Town, Qinglong County. The stratum dip angle of the natural slope is often distributed around 15°. Of course, creep landslides with low angles exist. As a result, the first series adopts three groups of 5°, 8°, and 15° dip angle models to simulate the dip angle difference. Furthermore, the shear zone of the landslide mass along the China-Myanmar line is composed of mudstone, shale, and coal seam. Their strengths are quite different viscosities. The geological survey shows that they have an obvious influence on landslides, but their influence on the evolution process of landslides is unclear. For this reason, two series of experiments were designed, and five models with varying viscosities were used for experimentation.

Modelling structure

Modelling experiments were conducted on the landslide set-ups in the Analogue Modelling Laboratory of Zunyi Normal University. The model structure is composed of a silicone layer and quartz sand. The thickness of the basal and middle silicone layers was 5 mm, that of the middle layer of quartz sand was 15 mm, and that of the covering quartz sand was 20 mm (Fig. 2; Table 1). The formation dip angle of Model 1 is 5°. The dip angle of Models 2, 4, and 5 is 8°. The dip angle of Model 3 is 15° (Fig. 2a ~ Fig. 2c; Table 1). Moreover, the viscosity of the silicone layer is 8300 Pa·s in Models 1, 2, and 3. Models 4 and 5 are based on Model 2, and the viscosity of the silicone layer in Model 4 is 11,000 Pa·s. The viscosity of the silicone layer in Model 5 is 20,000 Pa·s (Fig. 2d; Table 1).

Materials and scaling

Loose quartz sand is an ideal material for simulating crustal deformation and has been widely used in the study of tectonic deformation24. Although it is most commonly used to study deformation in basins and orogenic belts, it has also received considerable attention in soil mechanics. For example, the early mechanical experiments of Terzaghi soil were simulated using corresponding sand and clay25–27. Quartz sand is widely favoured because its mechanical properties are more similar28,29. A silicone layer with specific viscosity is an ideal material for simulating ductile deformation and has been widely used in the simulation of weak-layer deformation30–32. At room temperature, a silicone layer with different viscosities can be used to simulate different shear band strengths.

Results

Analog results of different dip angles

Model 1 results

Model 1 has a 5° dip angle, and the simulation results in the low viscosity (8300 Pa·s), as well as the strength of the basement and the intermediate shear zone. After 30 min, the overall forward slip phenomenon was presented. One normal fault was formed at the front edge and three small faults at the rear edge (Fig. 3a, i, b and j). After 60 min, two normal faults were formed at the front edge, three were formed at the rear edge, and two appeared at the lateral profile (Fig. 3c and k). After 90 min of deformation, the front and rear edge fractures increased, the fracture amplitude increased, and the deformation extended to the middle front, forming four faults (Fig. 3d and l). After 120 min, the front, middle, and rear edges’ normal fault amplitudes increased, and the normal faults at the rear edge became more obvious (Fig. 3e and m). After 150 min, three large arc normal faults were generated in the middle front, the amplitude of the rear edge normal faults increased, and three normal faults were visible in the profile (Fig. 3f and n). After 180 min, the amplitude of normal faults at the middle front increased, and three large-amplitude normal faults were generated at the rear edge, which extended and deformed toward the middle (Fig. 3g and o). After 210 min, the fault amplitude of the front, middle, and rear edges gradually increased, and seven obvious normal faults appeared in the profile (Fig. 3h and p). The landslide’s deformation and expansion modes are synchronous front edge and rear edge deformation, slow creep deformation of the middle front edge, and synchronous front edge and rear edge deformation.

Discussion

Summary of results and comparison

Model results with dip angles of 5°, 8°, and 15° show that although the slope mass of each group of models has landslide deformation and formed normal fault cutting slope mass, there are significant differences in the normal fault style and extension mode of slope mass. In Model 1 (Low-dip model), after 210 min of deformation of all models, six normal faults were formed on the plane, and the brittle layer of the basement remained unaltered. In Model 2 (Middle-dip model), the equal dip model formed eight large-scale normal faults on the plane and three normal faults on the trailing edge of the basement’s brittle layer. In Model 3 (High-dip model), ten large-scale normal faults were formed on the sloping plane. The basement brittle layer contained nine normal faults. The slope body’s expansion mode is as follows: In Model 1, the front edge and the rear edge are synchronized - the middle front edge creeps - the middle front edge and the rear edge are synchronized; In Model 2: trailing edge, leading edge, middle slow creep deformation; and in Model 3: the trailing edge-middle-leading edge is gradually expanded and deformed.

Shear band strength difference models, which include the low strength (8300 Pa·s) in Model 2, medium strength (11000 Pa·s) in Model 4, and high strength (20000 Pa·s) in Model 5. After 210 min, Model 2 was still as described above. In Model 4, seven large-scale normal faults formed on the plane, and one normal fault was generated in the base brittle layer at the middle and rear edge. In Model 5, six large-scale normal faults formed on the plane. Two normal faults formed in the basement brittle layer at the rear edge. The expansion mode of Model 4 is slow creep deformation from the trailing edge to the leading edge to the middle part. The expansion mode of Model 5 is slow creep deformation from the leading edge to the trailing edge to the middle part. The expansion modes of Models 2 and 4 are identical, indicating that the deformation expansion mode of slope mass is the same under the strength of medium and low shear zones. At high shear band strength, the deformation propagation mode changes. The simulation results show that the slope inclination and the strength difference in the shear zone have a significant influence on the deformation pattern and evolution process of the landslide. The greater the strength of the shear zone, the smaller the slope angle and the more conducive to slope stability. The smaller the strength of the shear zone, the greater the slope angle and the greater the likelihood of landslides.

The comparative analysis of PIV velocity fields for the models (Model 2, Model 4, and Model 5) indicates that varying viscosities lead to differences in landslide propagation. Under the low viscosity of weak layers (viscosity = 8300 Pa·s) in Model 2, the slope body extends uniformly toward the front, with high-speed fields primarily concentrated in the mid-frontal deformation zone (Fig. 8a to e). In Model 4 under medium-viscosity of weak layers (viscosity = 11000 Pa·s), high-speed fields remain predominantly distributed in the mid-frontal area but exhibit a more limited spatial extent (Fig. 10a to e). In Model 5 under high-viscosity of weak layers (viscosity = 20000 Pa·s), high-speed fields are localized and display a discrete distribution pattern within the slope body (Fig. 10f to j). In summary, the lower the viscosity of the weak layer, the more pronounced the overall landslide, while a higher viscosity of the weak layer may lead to localized landslides.

Stress patterns of the landslide

Analog modelling results from five groups of models show that Model 3 produces the most normal faults and has high deformation strength of the basement’s brittle layer. The deformation strength is closely related to the initial stress characteristics of each set of Models (Fig. 11). When the slope angle is 15°, the shear stress of the slope body is the largest, and the greater the strength of deformation. Simultaneously, as the slope angle increases, the deformation mode of the landslide shifts from the rear to the middle and then to the front edge, with a high deformation rate. The smaller the slope angle, the less the shear stress produced by the slope and, thus, the weaker the strength of the landslide. The initial stress state of the model determines the deformation pattern and evolution process of the landslide.

Comparison between analog results and typical landslides of China-Myanmar oil and gas

In western Guizhou along the Myanmar-China oil and gas pipeline, such as Shasha Town and Qinglong County, the surface is made of loose quaternary sand and conglomerate with a thickness of about 100 to 500 m (Figs. 13, 14 and 15). Below the sand is a coal seam and shale, and below it is hard limestone. The regional geomorphic dip angle is 25°~55°, and the stratum dip angle is 0°–26°. The dip angle of the fault is 55°~80°. The complex deformation in this region makes the slope’s structural plane weak. The shear zones in the western Guizhou structural belt are composed of faults, coal seams, shale, and gypsum33,34–35. The upper and lower sets of weak-layer structural planes combine to form the slope body (Fig. 15).

The simulated structure is similar to the genetic model of typical landslides in western Guizhou. Normal faults are formed at the rear, middle, and front edges of the slope (Figs. 14 and 15). The creep of the slope leads to the deformation. However, the findings of analog modelling results of different slope structures provide us with a new insight. The slope angle and structural plane strength have a direct impact on how the slope deforms during a landslide. As a result, there are obvious differences between the various types of slope structures, different patterns of output, the position and style of deformation extension, and the evolution process. This means that different parts of the slope deform over different amounts of time, which makes it easier to take effective avoidance measures before a landslide occurs. This understanding has extremely important guiding significance for the study of landslide genesis mechanisms and early warning systems.

Conclusion

Combined with the regional geological and structural characteristics, this paper applies the slope analog models of a double-layer weak structural plane to simulate the typical landslide characteristics in Guizhou and obtains the following understandings:

The topography in Western Guizhou Province has a significant drop and changes, and the slope angles are generally large, with an angle distribution of almost greater than 8°~15°. Therefore, landslides are more developed.

The topography in Western Guizhou Province has a significant drop and changes, and the slope angles are generally large, with an angle distribution of almost greater than 8°~15°. Therefore, landslides are more developed.

Analog modelling shows that a structural plane with a low slope angle (5°) and high viscosity (20000 Pa.s) of the detachment layer is conducive to the stability of the slope, while a structural plane with a high slope angle(15°) and low viscosity(8300 Pa.s) of the detachment layer often induces landslides.

Analog modelling shows that a structural plane with a low slope angle (5°) and high viscosity (20000 Pa.s) of the detachment layer is conducive to the stability of the slope, while a structural plane with a high slope angle(15°) and low viscosity(8300 Pa.s) of the detachment layer often induces landslides.

Analogue modelling results and PIV data indicated that the high slope angle of the slope body forms the shear propagation deformation mode from the back edge to the middle edge, which has the fastest deformation rate and the greatest impact and destruction. Under the same dip angle, the ductile shear zone with low strength forms a shear slip mode from the trailing edge to the leading edge and then to the middle of the slope.
