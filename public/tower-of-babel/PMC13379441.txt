Epinephrine Underuse for Anaphylaxis in Infants and Toddlers: A Practical Review for Pediatricians
Authors: Stephanie Leeds, Aikaterini Anagnostou, Michael Pistiner, Jay Lieberman, Nicole B Ramsey, Rachael Griffiths, Julie Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractA recent consensus report defined anaphylaxis as a serious allergic reaction that may involve the skin/mucosa, respiratory, cardiovascular, and/or gastrointestinal systems that can progress rapidly and may cause death. Epinephrine treatment is recommended in suspected anaphylaxis cases; however, numerous barriers exist to consistent, widespread epinephrine use, including difficulty identifying anaphylaxis signs and symptoms; challenges recognizing when to use epinephrine; low propensity of prescribing and filling prescriptions; low epinephrine carrying rates; knowledge gaps in epinephrine administration techniques; fears of contacting emergency medical services; use of antihistamines, inhaled bronchodilators, and other medications as first-line agents over epinephrine; and limited epinephrine device availability in community settings (e.g., schools, daycares, and restaurants). To address these challenges, healthcare providers should provide training and educational resources to caregivers on signs and symptoms of anaphylaxis that warrant epinephrine use, how to use epinephrine devices, and the importance of having epinephrine readily available at all times. System-level strategies, including measures to improve access, legislative efforts to stock epinephrine in childcare centers, and scalable digital education approaches, are also needed. Furthermore, healthcare providers should implement shared decision-making with caregivers to prepare and empower caregivers to make informed decisions when managing anaphylactic events. Understanding barriers to consistent, widespread epinephrine use when infants and toddlers experience anaphylaxis and providing actionable educational tools to caregivers alongside system-level strategies can improve outcomes in this vulnerable population.

Key Points

Prompt use of epinephrine is the standard first-line treatment in cases of suspected anaphylaxis.This review explores numerous barriers to consistent, widespread use of epinephrine when infants and toddlers experience anaphylaxis.Healthcare providers should provide training and educational resources to support caregivers in recognizing the signs and symptoms of anaphylaxis and how to administer epinephrine when needed.

Introduction: Overview of Anaphylaxis

A consensus report from the GA2LEN project defined anaphylaxis as a serious allergic reaction that can progress rapidly and may cause death [1]. Anaphylaxis can present with a wide range of clinical manifestations, including skin/mucosal, respiratory, cardiovascular, and/or gastrointestinal symptoms, with some signs and symptoms that are specific to infants and toddlers (Fig. 1) [1–3]. The prevalence of severe anaphylaxis episodes among pediatric patients has been increasing in the USA for over a decade; a large retrospective cohort study found a 4.2% per year increase in anaphylaxis-related pediatric emergency department (ED) visits from 2016 to 2022 [4, 5].

When anaphylaxis is recognized, prompt use of epinephrine is the first-line treatment for anaphylaxis and may reduce risk of hospitalization and mortality [2]. Despite the efficacy and safety of epinephrine, it is often underutilized in the treatment of anaphylaxis [6, 7]. In the event of an anaphylaxis episode, studies have shown that administration of epinephrine before arrival to the ED (prehospital) resulted in a higher likelihood of patients arriving with vital signs within normal ranges [8, 9]. Currently, there are several epinephrine autoinjectors and an intranasal epinephrine device on the market. The only US Food and Drug Administration-approved community-use epinephrine devices for infants and toddlers weighing 7.5 kg to less than 15 kg is an autoinjector administered intramuscularly or subcutaneously [10]. Intramuscular epinephrine and intranasal epinephrine are both well tolerated; the majority of adverse events related to epinephrine have been mild and resolved without intervention, and the intramuscular route has a well-documented, real-world safety profile, supported by decades of clinical use [2, 11–13].

For this narrative review, a PubMed search was conducted using two search strings: (anaphylaxis) AND (epinephrine); and ((allergic reaction) OR (allergy)) AND (epinephrine). Results were limited to English-language publications and were filtered by study type, including clinical studies, clinical trials, comparative studies, controlled clinical trials, evaluation studies, meta-analyses, multicenter studies, observational studies, randomized controlled trials, emergency department survey studies, and qualitative/mixed-methods studies, with a date range of 2015–2025. Case reports were limited to a date range of 2020–2025. Although the literature search was not conducted according to formal systematic review methodology, studies and recommendations were selected based on their clinical relevance, with an emphasis on evidence pertaining to pediatric populations.

In this review, we highlight the barriers to epinephrine use and recommend strategies for healthcare providers to address these barriers with caregivers (defined as parents, legal guardians, or other family members responsible for the day-to-day care of the patient), with particular focus on the infant and toddler age group.

Barriers to Epinephrine Use

Recognition of Signs and Symptoms

Recognition of anaphylaxis events that warrant the use of epinephrine can be challenging in people of all ages, especially in infants and toddlers in whom signs and symptoms are often difficult to interpret and can vary widely between individual cases [2, 14]. A recent national survey examined primary caregivers’ observation of symptoms during their infant or toddler’s most severe food-induced allergic reaction. The most common symptoms reported were skin reactions (90%), facial and extremity swelling (59%), gastrointestinal issues (51%), and coughing/wheezing (45%). Infants more frequently exhibited mottling, ear pulling, and ear-related behaviors, while toddlers more often experienced throat itching, coughing, and wheezing. Almost half of caregivers identified ≥ 1 symptom retrospectively, with sudden behavioral changes, skin reactions, gastrointestinal issues, and respiratory symptoms being the most commonly overlooked symptoms [15]. In addition, infants and toddlers are often nonverbal or unable to communicate symptoms, and symptoms of anaphylaxis can be mistaken for age-appropriate behaviors (e.g., drooling and spitting up) [2, 14]. Even when symptoms are correctly identified, caregivers may not perceive symptoms to be severe enough to require epinephrine. A survey of caregivers reported that epinephrine was not used in 48% of pediatric cases in which it was warranted because the caregiver did not believe the symptoms were severe enough to require epinephrine [16].

Given the difficulty in identifying anaphylaxis, use of epinephrine outside of the hospital remains low [17]. A meta-analysis assessing international studies of epinephrine use across pediatric patients of all ages in the prehospital setting (including use by emergency medical services) reported a mean epinephrine use rate of 20.98%, whereas the rate of epinephrine use in the pre-emergency medical services setting (self-administration or administration by a caregiver or bystander) was 8.78% [17]. Notable predictors of nontreatment with epinephrine in the pediatric population included the reaction occurring at home and lack of respiratory involvement, based on a retrospective cohort study [18]. A survey of caregivers found that epinephrine use in infants and toddlers during probable anaphylaxis events is more likely in those with a prior diagnosis of food allergy and for whom an anaphylaxis action plan has been provided [19].

In the clinical setting, underdiagnosis of anaphylaxis is a challenge in infants and toddlers as well [2]. The lack of validated clinical criteria identifying anaphylaxis specific to infants and toddlers may contribute to the under-recognition of anaphylaxis by healthcare providers [2, 14]. Anaphylaxis criteria and definitions have been recently published that specifically account for symptoms in infants and toddlers [1, 14]. These modifications to existing anaphylaxis criteria introduce infant-specific enhancements to address developmental limitations in communication, reclassification of mucocutaneous symptoms (e.g., tongue swelling) as respiratory, and the inclusion of physiologically relevant markers of cardiovascular compromise preceding decompensated shock. The modified criteria demonstrated superior sensitivity, capturing 98% of cases compared with 85% when using the standard National Institute of Allergy and Infectious Disease and Food Allergy and Anaphylaxis Network criteria, with notable improvements in detection among infants (23% increase, P < 0.001) and toddlers (10% increase, P = 0.04) [14]. Prescribing and Filling of Epinephrine Prescriptions

A retrospective cross-sectional study in a Medicaid population (ages 0–21 years) reported that 64% of patients in pediatric EDs with a diagnosis of anaphylaxis received a prescription for an epinephrine autoinjector [20]. This study demonstrated poor adherence to anaphylaxis management guidelines among some healthcare providers, including a low propensity of prescribing epinephrine, which may be explained by a lack of confidence around anaphylaxis definitions and limited exposure to clinical guidelines [20]. Despite being first-line treatment for anaphylaxis [2], a meta-analysis of international studies found that the majority of studies reported epinephrine prescription rates of less than 50% in all patients with anaphylaxis. Among those studies, the prescription rate ranged between 24% and 46% in North America [17].

Among those prescribed epinephrine, prescriptions may not be consistently filled, or patients may not replace expired epinephrine. Studies report that epinephrine prescription fill rates range from 55% to 85% in pediatric patients with anaphylaxis; the most common reasons for leaving prescriptions unfilled were “no history of reactions” and “cost” [21, 22]. In a phone survey of caregivers of children prescribed epinephrine in the pediatric ED, 84% of caregivers reported filling their initial prescription; however, only 46% of all caregivers had accessible and non-expired epinephrine [23]. Similarly, a meta-analysis of international studies reported an epinephrine refill rate of 46% among patients of all ages, suggesting that more than half of patients had not refilled their prescription and may have had either expired epinephrine or no epinephrine [17]. Data indicate that expired epinephrine autoinjectors may still contain some biologically active epinephrine, depending on the storage conditions to which each epinephrine autoinjector was subjected [24]. Although it is important to note the reduced concentrations of epinephrine within expired autoinjectors and the possible effects on efficacy, administering epinephrine from an expired autoinjector is generally considered preferable to not administering epinephrine [2, 24].

Immediate Availability of Epinephrine

Cross-sectional survey data indicate that one of the reasons most cited by adults for not using epinephrine during an anaphylactic event is lack of immediate, physical access to an epinephrine device. Survey-based studies estimate that epinephrine is carried 44–57% of the time [21, 25, 26], and the most common reason for not carrying epinephrine was that patients “did not think they would be exposed to the allergen.” Other concerns included size of some epinephrine autoinjectors, never having needed epinephrine before, forgetting, and believing their symptoms are not severe enough to need to carry epinephrine all the time.

Training on Epinephrine Administration

Inadequate training on the proper use of epinephrine devices has been identified as a major gap for caregivers and daycares. One survey-based study found that only 21% of pediatric and adult patients who had been prescribed epinephrine autoinjectors were able to demonstrate all the correct administration steps and that errors occurred 35–43% of the time. However, success with epinephrine autoinjectors may vary between devices. One prospective survey showed that 87.9% of caregivers of pediatric patients who used a US commercially available epinephrine autoinjector that includes audible and visual cues were able to demonstrate correct use, which is higher than the reported 25–47% of caregivers who correctly used other epinephrine autoinjectors [27, 28]. The survey also found that in-person demonstrations by a healthcare provider with a training device were also a significant predictor of correct epinephrine autoinjector use, highlighting the importance of healthcare providers modeling correct epinephrine autoinjector use during clinical visits [27].

Caregiver Fear and Hesitation

Epinephrine is underused, in part, because of caregivers’ fear. A caregiver survey found that 29% of caregivers did not administer epinephrine because they were scared or nervous about the anaphylactic reaction itself, even after reporting that they feel confident following epinephrine autoinjector education or training, thus highlighting the challenge of taking action in stressful situations. A qualitative caregiver survey reported that a common theme described by caregivers was the fear they may hurt their child by administering epinephrine; other caregivers reported fear that they might administer epinephrine incorrectly. A parental survey identified several predictors of fear in caregivers, including having shorter disease duration, having a child who has not experienced a severe reaction, being less satisfied with their epinephrine autoinjector training, and not finding their epinephrine device easy to use [29].

Caregivers may also have safety concerns with the use of epinephrine and its potential side effects, including transient tremors, restlessness, increased anxiety, headache, and palpitations, which have been reported in approximately one in five patients [16, 30, 31]. One survey found that 7% of caregivers have expressed concerns regarding the safety of epinephrine, so they did not use their epinephrine device when warranted [16]. However, studies have shown that adverse reactions to epinephrine for anaphylaxis are usually mild and transient and should not restrict use of epinephrine [32, 33]. The majority of epinephrine autoinjector administration can be done correctly with minimal complications after proper training [34]. Rare instances of unintentional injection to digits, as well as lacerations due to pediatric patient movement during the injection, have been reported with some autoinjector devices [35, 36]. There is a potential concern regarding the ability to accurately inject into muscle in infants and toddlers who are much smaller than older pediatric patients. Although an ultrasound study estimated that, with a 10-lb compression, a needle length of 12.7 mm (the standard length for 0.15 mg epinephrine autoinjectors) could theoretically strike bone in 43% of infants and toddlers, this has not been validated in real-world studies, and true intraosseous injections have been reported only anecdotally [37, 38]. Nonetheless, an epinephrine autoinjector with a shorter needle length (7.4 mm) for infants and toddlers weighing 16.5 to under 33 pounds is commercially available in the USA and may reduce this theoretical risk [10, 28]. Some caregiver concerns exist regarding epinephrine autoinjector needles. For example, a survey of 73 caregivers who did not use an epinephrine autoinjector when warranted found that 3 (4.1%) did not administer epinephrine because their child was scared or nervous about the needle. However, studies suggest that other factors are far more commonly reported (e.g., “symptoms did not seem severe enough,” “first reaction,” and “used other medication instead”) and may present bigger hurdles to appropriate treatment of anaphylaxis [16, 39].

Activation of Emergency Medical Services

There is also a perception among some individuals that the use of epinephrine signifies an escalation in the severity of the situation, in terms of the medical care needed and the prognosis of the overall disease state. There is often a reluctance among caregivers to use epinephrine and activate emergency medical services or visit the ED [5, 16, 39]. The link between emergency medical services and epinephrine administration has created misconceptions that epinephrine is potentially dangerous or that use of epinephrine requires a trip to the ED. However, caregivers should be informed that the use of epinephrine does not necessarily indicate a diagnosis of anaphylaxis nor increase the severity classification of underlying food allergy [2]. In addition, updated expert consensus allows for use of epinephrine without reflex activation of emergency services under certain conditions [2]. A North American group has recently developed validated, updated anaphylaxis action plans incorporating changes in the anaphylaxis management landscape, including activation of emergency medical services as a preference-sensitive option [40].

Use of Second-Line Medications for Anaphylaxis

Against recommendations, other medications such as antihistamines are sometimes given in place of epinephrine for anaphylaxis. Antihistamines are considered second-line treatment for anaphylaxis as they have slow onset of action and do not effectively treat cardiovascular or respiratory symptoms [3]. However, one survey of caregivers reported that one-third of children with anaphylactic events were treated with other medications rather than epinephrine, primarily oral antihistamines [16]. The reason for use of medications other than epinephrine may include greater comfort with other medications or uncertainty around the appropriate use of epinephrine [16].

Recommended Changes to Reduce Barriers to Epinephrine Use

Addressing the barriers to epinephrine use requires a multilevel interventional approach encompassing healthcare provider training, caregiver education, community-based preparedness, and system-level changes. The sections that follow highlight the recommended strategies across these areas.

Education and Resources to Assist Healthcare Providers in Educating Caregivers

Healthcare providers are crucial resources for caregivers; thus, they need to be well informed and proactive. Studies reveal significant provider discrepancies in anaphylaxis diagnosis and epinephrine administration, particularly with infants [41]. This underscores the critical need for continued education for healthcare providers on infant anaphylaxis and epinephrine use. Professional organizations (i.e., American College of Allergy, Asthma & Immunology [ACAAI], American Academy of Allergy, Asthma & Immunology [AAAAI], and American Academy of Pediatrics [AAP]) can support these educational gaps for healthcare providers by providing tools, resources, and educational programming to support counseling of caregivers (Table 2) [42]. This approach may help address barriers related to under-recognition of anaphylaxis.

Education of Caregivers by Healthcare Providers

Education of caregivers by healthcare providers is critical to overcoming barriers to epinephrine use (Fig. 2). Comprehensive training by healthcare providers for people caring for infants/toddlers at risk of anaphylactic reactions has been recommended to improve recognition of signs and symptoms of anaphylaxis and increase the correct use of epinephrine devices [43, 44]. Recommendations include training on allergen avoidance, recognition of symptoms of anaphylactic reactions, when and how to use epinephrine devices, and explanation of device carriage, storage, and expiration [43]. Healthcare providers should also encourage caregivers to regularly practice administering epinephrine using a training device that simulates their specific device and to discuss strategies for managing barriers to adherence specific to each caregiver [2]. This training should be reinforced at regular intervals, and retraining should occur when a different epinephrine product is prescribed [43]. However, an important consideration of healthcare provider-led education efforts is the time investment needed from healthcare providers, which is a challenge for many healthcare providers given the time constraints they already face. When educating caregivers, it is important to emphasize that the administration of epinephrine should not be dependent on meeting a clinical definition of anaphylaxis. If there is any concern or doubt, epinephrine should be administered without concern for premature administration, as symptoms can progress rapidly and studies have shown that early use of epinephrine in cases of food-induced anaphylaxis decreases the likelihood of patients requiring hospitalization and reduces the risk of biphasic reactions [45–47]. Put colloquially, “if symptoms cause you to worry, then administer epinephrine right away.”

Hands-on training with the prescribed epinephrine product, (i.e., autoinjectors or nasal spray) is important to ensure caregiver understanding and confidence in administering epinephrine. With the necessary tools and training, caregivers are more likely to have confidence when facing emergency situations in the future [39]. Given the availability of multiple epinephrine products and variability in product usability across patient populations, product selection should be individualized through shared decision-making between healthcare providers and caregivers by considering patient characteristics and guidelines. Caregivers who participated in a study of infant and toddler oral food challenges in which they administered epinephrine via autoinjector to their child during a reaction reported that they found the experience to be helpful and positive, even though their child had a reaction necessitating epinephrine [48].

A key educational approach for caregivers should include a written action plan completed by the healthcare provider for each patient. Written action plans, such as the AAP Allergy and Anaphylaxis Emergency Plan (aap.org/aaep and aap.org/aaep.spanish) or a recently validated Anaphylaxis Action Plan [40], can serve as a guide for caregivers and daycare/preschool personnel on what signs and symptoms to look for, when to administer epinephrine for anaphylaxis, what specific epinephrine product has been prescribed, and any additional instructions that should be considered [49, 50].

Beyond a written action plan, healthcare providers should consider several avenues for improved education of caregivers such as face-to-face training and both print and digital educational materials (such as the information presented in link 1 and link 2). When comparing face-to-face training and video training, there is evidence indicating that face-to-face training achieves better and faster recognition of anaphylaxis [51]. Education materials should be concise, actionable, and tailored to specific audiences (e.g., caregivers and daycare staff). When developing education materials, caregivers have communicated that they appreciate real-world examples of anaphylactic reactions and hearing from those who have had experiences with anaphylaxis or using an epinephrine device [39]. There are also many supportive resources available online that provide educational content for caregivers to support and reinforce the training received from healthcare providers (Fig. 3). Education of Community-Based Caregivers

Another area in need of educational outreach is community-based caregivers (which include daycare and preschool staff, school nurses, camp staff, and other nonparental individuals who provide care in a community-based setting), as many are not adequately trained to prevent, recognize, or manage anaphylactic events. Knowledge of anaphylaxis among these community-based caregivers can be improved through educational tools such as an Allergy and Anaphylaxis Emergency Plan and epinephrine device training with trainer devices [52]. In a study of school personnel, a workshop-based training of school nurses and personnel involved in the care of children prescribed an epinephrine autoinjector resulted in a dramatic improvement in self-efficacy, defined as the belief in their own ability to organize and execute an appropriate action in a prospective situation [53]. Furthermore, in-person school nurse training sessions on food allergy and anaphylaxis management enhanced knowledge and confidence for up to 24 months [54]. In addition, nurses reported an improved ability to educate school staff, thereby strengthening food allergy prevention strategies and anaphylaxis preparedness [54]. One study reported a higher rate of epinephrine administration for pediatric patients when the anaphylactic episode occurred at school/daycare (66.7%) versus at home (36.7%), noting the difference could be attributed to policies and training on anaphylaxis, showing the positive impacts from robust school policies and well-supported training programs [55].

Allergists and other healthcare providers should support caregivers in communicating with daycares, preschools, and schools by advising caregivers to provide the child’s current allergy action plan and emergency medication to the school/preschool/childcare setting, and by supporting caregivers in educating school staff on food allergies and ways to mitigate risks [56]. These approaches can assist in addressing the barriers related to epinephrine administration in community settings.

Communication of Guidance on Activation of Emergency Medical Services

As outlined above, surveys have found that one barrier to epinephrine use is the desire to avoid an ED visit [16]. A recent update to the anaphylaxis practice parameters included that “activation of emergency medical services may not be required if the patient experiences prompt, complete, and durable response to treatment with epinephrine, provided that additional epinephrine doses and medical care are readily available, if needed” [2]. The “one-size-fits-all” approach was reconsidered for a risk-stratified approach wherein emergency medical services are activated for symptoms that do not resolve or worsen after two doses of epinephrine. The updated recommendations were based on studies showing that 90% of anaphylaxis events resolved after one dose of epinephrine [57]. It is important for healthcare providers to be educated on guideline updates so that they are prepared to share this information with caregivers. Healthcare providers should implement shared decision-making in their discussions with caregivers on when to seek further care, including when it might be necessary to activate emergency medical services or to visit the ED [2]. Healthcare providers should also emphasize the long-standing recommendation of carrying two epinephrine devices because, in cases of anaphylaxis, if the caregiver administers epinephrine but does not activate emergency medical services, it is vital to have access to a second dose of epinephrine if symptoms do not resolve or recur. These strategies can help with determining when activation of emergency medical services would be necessary.

Prioritizing Epinephrine in Suspected Anaphylaxis Cases

A review of prehospital protocols for pediatric respiratory distress in the USA found that protocols typically recommend epinephrine for anaphylaxis and inhaled bronchodilators for wheezing [58]. However, in pediatric patients with severe allergies with comorbid asthma, distinguishing between anaphylaxis and an asthma flare can be difficult. Inhaled medications should not be used as first-line treatment in cases of suspected anaphylaxis; as epinephrine is also a bronchodilator, it can provide benefit for both anaphylaxis and asthma exacerbations. In the setting of suspected food reactions involving the respiratory tract, inhaled bronchodilators can be used for symptom management after first-line use of epinephrine in pediatric patients [59]. This approach is particularly relevant to the barrier related to the underuse of epinephrine as a second-line medication for anaphylaxis.

System-Level Considerations

While education is essential for reducing barriers to epinephrine use, expanding and implementing system-level solutions may also play a critical role in improving access and adherence. Indeed, evidence from reviews, surveys, and economic analyses suggests that prescribing requirements, coverage variability, and patient-level financial considerations can limit access to epinephrine and that addressing these barriers may improve access and support more appropriate use [2, 17, 21]. Healthcare providers should consider affordability and access as part of shared decision-making to help ensure patients and caregivers are able to obtain and maintain access to epinephrine [2].

At a policy level, strategies that emphasize making epinephrine available in a variety of public places, such as daycares, restaurants, and shopping malls, rather than placing the burden of epinephrine availability solely on patients or their caregivers, are necessary to increase the safety of infants or toddlers at higher risk for anaphylaxis or in cases of a patient’s first allergic reaction, which is particularly relevant in the childcare setting. A workgroup appointed by the Joint Task Force on Practice Parameters (on behalf of AAAAI and ACAAI) proposed strategies to improve anaphylaxis preparedness in childcare centers, schools, and community settings (e.g., implementation of staff training for allergy and anaphylaxis management and stocking of undesignated epinephrine devices), aligning with others who have advocated for broader epinephrine availability [2, 17, 60, 61]. Moreover, legislation is critically needed for early childcare centers, as only 19 states in the USA explicitly permit stocking epinephrine in these facilities [62].

Digital education approaches may also complement traditional in-person training [2]. An evaluation of an e-learning platform for school personnel demonstrated that online anaphylaxis training may be effective and scalable, and participants had high self-reported confidence in anaphylaxis management [63].

Limitations

This narrative review has some inherent limitations. Currently, data specific to infants and toddlers remain limited. The evidence base for many of the barriers discussed relies heavily on observational and survey-based studies, and caregiver-reported data may be subject to recall and response bias. Furthermore, many of the cited studies are rooted in the US healthcare system, prescribing environment, school/community infrastructure, and emergency response framework. As such, the barriers and recommendations described may not be fully generalizable to international settings.

Future Priorities

Several important areas warrant further investigation. Prospective studies evaluating the effectiveness of caregiver education programs on epinephrine use during anaphylaxis events in infants and toddlers are needed. In addition, the role of scalable digital training tools, including e-learning platforms and mobile health applications, in improving caregiver and community-based caregiver preparedness warrants further examination.

Conclusions

Anaphylaxis can develop rapidly and cause life-threatening symptoms. Epinephrine remains the first-line treatment for anaphylaxis, and studies have consistently shown that it is well tolerated and effective at reducing the risk of hospitalization and/or potentially death. Despite the efficacy and safety of this lifesaving therapeutic, barriers to more widespread use remain, including identification of the symptoms of anaphylaxis, recognition of when and how to administer epinephrine, fear of causing harm with epinephrine, fear of escalating the situation in such a way to then require emergency medical service activation or a trip to the ED, and availability of epinephrine, either carried by the caregiver or stocked in a public place.

To address these challenges, healthcare providers should provide training and educational resources for caregivers, leveraging creative education options that maximize educational impact while minimizing the time required. Addressing the challenges posed by anaphylaxis in infant and toddler populations requires preparation, knowledge, and action from caregivers, including community-based caregivers, and healthcare providers; this is a challenge we can collectively rise to meet in order to achieve the best possible outcomes for this vulnerable population.
