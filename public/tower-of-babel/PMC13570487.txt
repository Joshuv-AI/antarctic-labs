Dermatologists' Perspectives on Referral Systems and Legal Risk: Insights From a National Survey
Authors: Nicholas M. Muller, Samuel X. Tan, Kathleen Hogan, Veronica Ho, Mahesh Ravutha Gounden, Danielle Gavanescu, Jeremy Gambrill, Yung‐Ching Kao, James Muir, John Devereux, Katherine Darch, Owen Bradfield, Lisa Abbott, H. Peter Soyer, Brian De'Ambrosis
Published: 2026

Patient referral is a crucial step of information exchange with ethical and medicolegal considerations for dermatological practice [1]. While dermatologists regularly receive and make referrals, formal education on this process is sparse in both medical school and specialist training [2]. Variation in referral quality can have consequent implications for patient care, including misdiagnosis and loss to follow‐up. Procedurally, the ability to precisely convey suspicious lesions is important in preventing wrong‐site surgery [3, 4]. We conducted a nationwide survey of Australian dermatologist to evaluate patterns in referral and follow‐up management to inform legally and ethically defensible patient care.

Participant recruitment was conducted from January to April 2025 through the Australasian College of Dermatologists Newsletter. A self‐administered questionnaire collected data on demographics, referral and documentation practices and medicolegal training and experience. Overall, 154 participants responded (mean age 53.4 ± 12.2 years; 58% female), representing 22% of Australian dermatologists. There was representation from dermatologists across the spectrum of medical and surgical practice, with 59% reporting ≥ 15 years in clinical practice (Table 1). Notably, 54% reported receiving lesion referrals without images, and 47% reported receiving referrals without any accompanying information. When sending referrals, dermatologists usually combined multiple modalities: written descriptions (90%) were used alongside images (69%) and occasionally diagrams (14%). However, 57% of respondents lacked systems to ensure patients attended referred appointments.

Spearman's correlation analysis, with statistical significance defined as Benjamini‐Hochberg false discovery rate adjusted p‐value (FDR) < 0.05, revealed a few interesting associations detailed in Figure 1. Male respondents in this cohort were older (ρ: −0.43, FDR < 0.05), reported more formal medicolegal education (ρ: −0.11, non‐significant), and engaged in a higher proportion of surgical dermatology (ρ: −0.15, non‐significant) relative to female dermatologists. Older dermatologists used fewer images in their skin cancer referrals (ρ: −0.34, FDR < 0.05) and reported greater familiarity with medicolegal requirements (ρ: 0.24, FDR < 0.05). Unsurprisingly, a higher proportion of surgical practice was associated with fewer onward referrals (ρ: −0.27, FDR < 0.05).

Our results indicate that dermatologist referral behaviours and legal considerations are influenced by age, gender, proportion of surgical practice and medicolegal training, and we conclude with two actionable takeaways. Firstly, dermatologists frequently reported receiving referrals for skin cancer without clinical images, and occasionally with no documented reason for referral, yet are still expected to diagnose and manage lesions. This exposes the patient, referrer and dermatologist to the risks of wrong‐site treatment or misdiagnosis. Secondly, many dermatologists lacked practice management systems to ensure that referred patients attended their follow‐up appointments, highlighting a potential source of medicolegal vulnerability.

We emphasise the importance of enacting follow‐up/referral assurance mechanisms given that missed diagnosis or delayed management of a high‐risk lesion could prove catastrophic [5, 6]. Even for low‐risk lesions, the potential for infection or clinical deterioration may still constitute significant medicolegal liability [7]. In Rubino v Ziaee [7] ACTSC 331, a general practitioner referred a patient with a persistent plantar clavus to a general surgery service for excision. The patient was not offered an appointment, and 2 years following initial presentation developed an infection requiring admission for incision and drainage. Notably, the general practitioner was initially found negligent for insufficiently following up on the referral, resulting in prolonged pain and functional impairment. In Young v Central Australian Aboriginal Congress Inc. & Ors [8] NTSC 47 the clinic as a whole was found to have neglected to ensure a patient had followed up with investigations and specialist referral.

Medical defence organisations recommend that clinicians prioritise follow‐up and referral as a continuation of the duty‐of‐care rather than as an administrative afterthought [2]. In practice, we must clearly explain to the patient why tests or referrals are required, outline the risks of non‐adherence, document that discussion—including refusal or non‐compliance—and revisit the issue at subsequent reviews. This process does not completely extinguish the doctor's duty of care but does demonstrate exercise of reasonable care in defending a potential complaint or claim. In shared‐care situations, such as a GP referring to a dermatologist or a dermatologist referring to a surgeon, the referring practitioner retains a coordinating role, but each clinician is responsible for acting reasonably within their own sphere of practice. Nonetheless, we suggest a dialogue between the referring and receiving physicians to establish standard information for patient handover.

Conclusions from this study are limited by the self‐administered and cross‐sectional nature of the survey. However, these findings support the need for standardisation in referrals, promoting imagery for specific lesions of concern, as well as systems‐level follow‐up assurance mechanisms to mitigate legal risk and support safer patient care.
