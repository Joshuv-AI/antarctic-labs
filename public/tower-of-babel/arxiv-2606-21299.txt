Characterization of GaN:Si and ZnO:Ga for position-resolved fast timing applications
Julius Meyer, Joshua W. Cates, Woon-Seng Choong, Juan Cristhian Luque Gutierrez, Federico Moretti, Ryan Pavlovsky, Mauricio Ayllon Unzueta, Weronika W. Wolszczak, Markus Roth, Arun Persaud

Abstract
We present the characterization of two fast, crystalline inorganic scintillators, silicon-doped gallium nitride (GaN:Si) and gallium-doped zinc oxide (ZnO:Ga) , and compare their performance with cerium-doped yttrium aluminium perovskite (YAP:Ce) for in-vacuum alpha-detection applications that require high-performance timing, position, and energy resolution, such as 3D elemental mapping, medical imaging, and homeland security applications. In this paper, we propose ZnO:Ga and GaN:Si as high-performance drop-in replacements for the alpha detector in Associated Particle Imaging (API) systems. However, the results reported here also have wide applicability. Prior work has reported on polycrystalline forms of ZnO:Ga , which suffer from self-absorption or non-spectroscopic alpha energy spectra, and, to our knowledge, GaN:Si has not been proposed to be used in API systems. We present room-temperature scintillation time constants obtained via X-ray-induced time-correlated single-photon counting for both proposed materials. They both exhibit exceedingly fast rise times of ≤ 15 ps \leq 15\text{\,}\mathrm{ps} , and high brightness ≥ 1000 ph / MeV \geq 1000\text{\,}\mathrm{p}\mathrm{h}\mathrm{/}\mathrm{M}\mathrm{eV} α with resolved alpha-peaks. Single-crystal ZnO:Ga and single-crystal GaN:Si yield single-component decays of 805 ps 805\text{\,}\mathrm{ps} and 32 ps 32\text{\,}\mathrm{ps} respectively. Using a plastic scintillator reference setup, coincidence timing resolution (CTR) and detector timing resolution (DTR) measurements demonstrate a ≥ \geq 3 × 3\times improvement in timing resolution compared to traditional YAP:Ce . GaN:Si and ZnO:Ga exhibit 35 ± ± 9 ps 35\pm\pm 9\text{\,}\mathrm{ps} and 49 ± ± 5 ps 49\pm\pm 5\text{\,}\mathrm{ps} DTR respectively, compared to 144 ± ± 2 ps 144\pm\pm 2\text{\,}\mathrm{ps} for conventional, single-crystal YAP:Ce . Finally, we evaluate their position resolution in an experimental setup designed for API and measure better than 0.2 mm 0.2\text{\,}\mathrm{mm} for YAP:Ce and approximately 1 mm 1\text{\,}\mathrm{mm} for GaN:Si . We obtain a position resolution of 0.3 mm 0.3\text{\,}\mathrm{mm} for ZnO:Ga from simulations. We also present alpha-induced ionoluminescence emission spectra that reveal direct, red-shifted near-bandgap emission.

Introduction and Background

High-performance radiation detection systems, in particular those achieving fast timing, are of interest to many application domains, from 3D elemental mapping in soils or for planetary exploration to medical imaging, and the detection and imaging of landmines. In medical imaging,
Positron-emission tomography (PET)
and
Computed Tomography (CT)
have aspirational timing-resolution targets of
\leq 10\text{\,}\mathrm{ps}

coincidence timing resolution (CTR)

full width at half maximum (FWHM)

[

PET

Enhancing the scintillator performance would enable, for example, high-precision tumor detection with lower radiation dose for the patient. For 3D elemental mapping, techniques such as
Associated Particle Imaging (API)
can be applied. For this technique, fast scintillators with good energy and position resolution for alpha particle detection are needed to provide trajectory information of the associated particles (the neutrons). At the same time, the scintillator needs to have low sensitivity to gamma-rays and X-rays. Strong candidate materials exhibit fast
detector timing resolution (DTR)

API

1.3
Statistical Timing Performance Limits

5
demonstrated sub-
100\text{\,}\mathrm{ps}
timing at modest detected-photon counts for a
ZnO:Ga
powder sample and benchmarked results against a straight-response model and the statistical
Cramèr-Rao lower bound (CRLB)
.

A useful way to organize timing analysis for scintillators is to separate (i) the scintillation emission kinetics from (ii) the photodetector time response and the time pickoff. Following
20
, we use a straight-response model in which the stochastic emission process is convolved with the Gaussian sensor
transit-time spread (TTS)
, and is then connected to a leading-edge pickoff on the earliest part of the rising waveform. Full derivations and additional background are given in
[
8
, pp. 514–520]
as well as in later lower-bound analyses
[
5
]
.

The emission
probability density function (p.d.f.)
, conditioned on photon detection, is denoted
\phi(t)
. For many materials, the early light is well described by a linear combination of bi-exponential cascades. Writing
p_{k}
for the weight of the
k
-th cascade with rise and decay constants
\tau_{r,k}
and
\tau_{d,k}
, a convenient normalized, causal form is

\displaystyle\phi(t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k}p_{k}\,\frac{e^{-t/\tau_{d,k}}-e^{-t/\tau_{r,k}}}{\tau_{d,k}-\tau_{r,k}},\ \ \ t\geq 0

(1)

\displaystyle\text{with }p_{k}\geq 0,\quad\sum_{k}p_{k}=1\,.

Convolving
\phi(t)
with a normal
p.d.f.

g_{\sigma}(t)=\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{\mathrm{TTS}})
that describes the photodetector’s carrier
TTS
produces the
p.d.f.
for the primary trigger times (conditioned on detection), shifted by the interaction time
\theta
,

f(t\mid\theta)\;=\;(\phi*g_{\sigma})(t-\theta)\;=\;\int_{0}^{\infty}\!\phi(\tau)\,g_{\sigma}\!\bigl(t-\theta-\tau\bigr)\,d\tau\,,

(2)

which we use as the likelihood for estimating
\theta
(the interaction time). In the straight-response picture, the expected analog waveform at the sensor input is obtained by filtering
f(t\mid\theta)
with the (normalized) single-photon response
h(t)
, and its local slope at a chosen threshold predicts the leading-edge timing jitter. See
5
for details of Eqs. (1)–(5).

If
N
photons are detected and their trigger times are treated as independent and identically distributed samples from
f(t\mid\theta)
, the Fisher information about
\theta
is

\mathcal{I}(\theta)\;=\;N\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\!\frac{\bigl(\partial_{\theta}f(t\mid\theta)\bigr)^{2}}{f(t\mid\theta)}\,dt.

(3)

The
Cramèr-Rao lower bound (CRLB)
on any unbiased time estimator
\hat{\theta}
is

\operatorname{Var}(\hat{\theta})\;\geq\;\frac{1}{\mathcal{I}(\theta)}.

(4)

In timing applications, one usually reports a
FWHM
, so the corresponding resolution is

\mathrm{FWHM}_{\text{CRLB}}\approx 2\sqrt{2\ln 2}\,\sqrt{\operatorname{Var}(\hat{\theta})}\,.

(5)

To expose the dominant scaling with scintillation kinetics, consider the single–component limit of Eq. (
1
) with negligible rise time (
\tau_{r}\ll\tau_{d}
) and negligible sensor
\mathrm{TTS}
, such that
f(t\mid\theta)\approx\frac{1}{\tau_{d}}\exp\!\left(-\frac{t-\theta}{\tau_{d}}\right)\Theta(t-\theta)
. In this regime,
\partial_{\theta}f=\frac{1}{\tau_{d}}f
, and inserting into Eq. (
3
) yields

\mathcal{I}(\theta)=N\int\frac{(\partial_{\theta}f)^{2}}{f}\,dt=N\int\frac{1}{\tau_{d}^{2}}f(t\mid\theta)\,dt=\frac{N}{\tau_{d}^{2}}.

(6)

Using Eq. (
4
), the variance therefore satisfies

\operatorname{Var}(\hat{\theta})\geq\frac{\tau_{d}^{2}}{N},

(7)

and consequently

\mathrm{FWHM}_{\text{CRLB}}\propto\frac{\tau_{d}}{\sqrt{N}}.

(8)

Thus, once the rise time and detector jitter no longer dominate, the achievable timing resolution scales linearly with the decay constant and inversely with the square root of the detected-photon statistics.

5
show that for well–conditioned signals (low noise, high gain) and with an optimized leading–edge pickoff on the earliest rise, measured resolutions can closely approach this statistical limit.

2.1
Scintillation Properties

The luminescence of the wide-bandgap semiconductor
GaN:Si
is governed by a competition between near-band-edge recombination and defect-related emission bands. At room temperature, the direct bandgap of GaN is near
3.4\text{\,}\mathrm{eV}
, corresponding to ultraviolet emission around
365\text{\,}\mathrm{nm}
(see, for example,
17
). For heavily Si-doped material, the radiative efficiency increases due to enhanced band-to-band recombination rates
[
19
]
. In addition to band-edge emission, a broad yellow luminescence band centered around
540\text{\,}\mathrm{nm}
is typically observed. This band is commonly attributed to deep defect complexes such as Ga vacancies combined with oxygen or carbon impurities, which act as deep acceptors.

ZnO:Ga
is another wide-bandgap semiconductor with a band edge around
3.37\text{\,}\mathrm{eV}
. The dominant radiative mechanism involves recombination of electrons from Ga
3+
donor levels with ionized holes trapped at shallow acceptors, most effectively hydrogen ions bound to zinc vacancies.
3
have shown that processing ZnO with Ga
2
O
3
followed by hydrogen annealing minimizes defects, resulting in high luminosity and stable sub-nanosecond decay. This donor–acceptor channel produces bright emission centered near
389\text{\,}\mathrm{nm}
, slightly below the band edge by about
0.18\text{\,}\mathrm{eV}
, and explains the ultra-fast, spectrally pure scintillation observed in an optimally prepared sample. In this study, the emission peak of the
ZnO:Ga
sample is at
401\text{\,}\mathrm{nm}
, which suggests strong self-absorption in our relatively thick sample for the
389\text{\,}\mathrm{nm}
emission peak reported by
3
. Therefore, unlike
GaN:Si
, high-quality
ZnO:Ga
shows little contribution from deep-level luminescence, and its performance is set by the balance between Ga donor electrons and shallow acceptors created during processing.

2.2
Self-Absorption in
GaN:Si
and
ZnO:Ga

Both
GaN:Si
and
ZnO:Ga
exhibit strong self-absorption of their emitted light because the emission lies close to the band gap — only about
0.07\text{\,}\mathrm{eV}
below the band edge in
GaN:Si
and
0.18\text{\,}\mathrm{eV}
below the band edge in
ZnO:Ga
. The Urbach tail describes absorption near the band edge as a function of the energy difference between the emission and the gap,
E_{\text{em}}-E_{g}

[
6
]
:

\alpha(E_{\text{em}})=\alpha_{0}\,\exp\!\left(\frac{E_{\text{em}}-E_{g}}{E_{U}}\right),

(9)

where
\alpha(E_{\text{em}})
is the absorption coefficient at the emission energy,
\alpha_{0}
is the band-edge absorption coefficient, and
E_{U}
is the Urbach energy, a measure of the tail width. In heavily doped
GaN:Si
, for example, a doping level of about
8\text{\times}{10}^{18}\text{\,}\mathrm{cm}
may yield
E_{U\text{, \lx@glossaries@gls@link{acronym}{gan}{{{}}GaN:Si} }}\approx$50\text{\,}\mathrm{meV}$

[
6
]
and
E_{U\text{, \lx@glossaries@gls@link{acronym}{zno}{{{}}ZnO:Ga} }}\approx$90\text{\,}\mathrm{meV}$
for
ZnO:Ga

[
23
]
. Because the emission energy lies only slightly below the band gap in both materials, the absorption coefficient remains high in the emission region: evaluating
\alpha
at
E=E_{\text{em}}
gives
\alpha(E_{\text{em}})/\alpha_{0}\approx 14\%
for
ZnO:Ga
and
\approx 25\%
for
GaN:Si
. At the band-edge energy, the absorption coefficients are
\alpha_{0}=$1\text{\times}{10}^{4}\text{\,}\mathrm{cm}$
for
GaN:Si
and
\alpha_{0}=$1.6\text{\times}{10}^{4}\text{\,}\mathrm{cm}$
for
ZnO:Ga

[
18
]
, which yield
\alpha(E_{\text{em}})\approx$2470\text{\,}\mathrm{cm}$
for
GaN:Si
and
\approx$2160\text{\,}\mathrm{cm}$
for
ZnO:Ga
. In contrast, in a host such as
YAP:Ce
with a much larger band gap of
8.1\text{\,}\mathrm{eV}

[
24
]
, the dopant emission at
370\text{\,}\mathrm{nm}
lies far below the absorption edge and is transmitted with negligible reabsorption.

4.2.1
Single-Photon Response Results

To calculate the absolute light yield of the samples used in this work, both the single-photon response and the
quantum efficiency (QE)
of the
PMT
must be known. We used Poisson statistics to extract the single-photon response from the pulsed LED setup discussed in
Section 3.2.1
, as shown in
Figure 10
. All recorded waveforms, triggered on the signal from the pulse generator, were integrated over a fixed time window, independent of whether a photon signal was present. As a result, the distribution includes events with zero detected photoelectrons, forming a pedestal peak centered around zero integrated signal, which corresponds to the electronic noise floor. The full distribution was modeled as a sum of Gaussian contributions corresponding to 0, 1, 2, and higher photoelectron events. The separation between adjacent peaks defines the single-photoelectron response, while the width of each Gaussian reflects the combined effects of electronic noise and gain fluctuations. While the width of the distribution for 0 p.e. is goverend by noise only, the width of the events with 1 or more p.e. also includes the photon response function of the
PMT
. We verified this method to obtain the single photon light yield using an independent approach: by identifying single-photon pulses in individual traces across different LED intensities, we constructed a median pulse shape where the integral agrees well with the single-photoelectron response extracted from the multi-Gaussian fit.

The same measurements were repeated for the other two R9800-100
PMTs
that are used later in the timing experiments. While the single-photon light yield uncertainty of about
32\text{\,}\mathrm{\%}
(
1\sigma
) drives the overall uncertainty of the light-yield calculation, the variation of the mean single-photon light yield among the three
PMTs
was measured to be less than
4\text{\,}\mathrm{\%}
.

5
Conclusions

We characterized single-crystal
ZnO:Ga
and near-single-crystal
GaN:Si
for in-vacuum alpha-particle detection, demonstrating fast timing, energy-resolved alpha peaks, and high position resolution relevant to
API
applications. To our knowledge, the use of
GaN:Si
in
API
systems is novel. We show that these crystals achieve a
\geq
5\times

From a systems perspective, faster decay and reduced pile-up open a path to higher operational rates and finer depth resolution in an integrated
API
system. While accidental coincidences remain a limiting factor at elevated neutron rates, improved
CTR
can narrow the coincidence window for certain applications where it is limited by the
CTR
and not neutron time of flight within the region of interest, and can therefore lower the random-coincidence rate.

Practical limitations are self-absorption and total internal reflection at sample-vacuum interfaces, which reduce the effective light yield (especially for
ZnO:Ga
/
GaN:Si
near the band edge). At the same time, optical design (thinning, reflective layers, index matching) measurably improves both collection efficiency and position resolution.
