Communication practices and experience of connection in telehealth serious illness conversations with rural cancer patients: a qualitative study
Authors: Elise C Tarbi, Stasha Medeiros, Amelia Haque, Serena Verma, Abhishek Ambati, Robert Gramling, Maija Reblin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTelehealth can overcome functional, cost, and geographic barriers to attending in-person visits for people living with cancer in rural areas. Yet this medium presents clinicians, patients, and families with a different sensory and relational environment within which to engage in serious illness conversations. Within this different context, there is a need to better understand how clinicians preserve or adapt their communication strategies to connect interpersonally with patients and ensure high-quality care.AimWe explored the characteristics of telehealth communication that patients reported fostered human connection in serious illness conversations among rural-dwelling patients with cancer.MethodsGrounded in a Basic Science Framework for Serious Illness Communication Research, we conducted semi-structured interviews with adult rural-dwelling patients with cancer. Eligible participants were either currently receiving or had recently completed treatment for their cancer and received some part of their cancer care via teleconferencing. Using directed content analysis, we qualitatively described participants’ experiences with telehealth encounters with their providers (i.e., oncology or palliative care clinicians). We followed the consolidated criteria for reporting qualitative research (COREQ) for rigorous data collection, analysis, and reporting.ResultsWe interviewed 20 participants (average duration = 29 min; range: 14–56 min). Mean age was 66 years and 60% were women. All participants identified as white and 85% identified as financially secure. Over half reported high digital health care literacy, including 65% (13/20) reporting ability to use electronic device software applications (e.g. Zoom) independently. We identified three major themes in interviews: (1) the accessibility, comfort, and convenience of telehealth contributes to patient experience of connection; (2) communication actions that enable connection in this setting are those that foster a sense of patients feeling seen as a person, and allow them to see their clinicians as humans as well; (3) patients’ experience of connection is impacted by relational, situational, and technological factors.ConclusionsAccessibility, clinician communication actions, and contextual factors of telehealth affect the experience of human connection in their cancer care among people with cancer who live in rural areas. This study provides a foundation to drive future research uncovering the optimal telehealth communication strategies to promote connection during virtual serious illness conversations.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12904-026-02192-z.Keywords: Serious illness communication, Telehealth, Telepalliative care, Rural health, Human connection, Qualitative analysis

Introduction

Advances in technology and social distancing policies of the COVID-19 pandemic catalyzed rapid adoption of telehealth1 for cancer care in recent years [1–4]. Bringing cancer care to the patient’s home through synchronous videoconferencing visits has the potential to make care more accessible and improve patient experiences [5–7]. For people living with cancer in rural areas, who have poorer access to health care and a higher risk of cancer mortality [8, 9], telehealth can overcome the substantial time, cost and often physical discomfort to attend more geographically distant in-person clinical visits [4, 5, 10]. Further, access to specialty palliative care is a cornerstone of high-quality comprehensive cancer care [11]; yet, it is unevenly distributed, often limited or absent in rural areas [12–14], with rurality being a predictor of poorer end-of-life cancer care [15]. As observed in telehealth oncology care [16], telehealth-delivered palliative care demonstrates similar quality of life benefits to in-person visits for people with advanced cancer [17, 18]. Thus, telehealth may provide a potential avenue for improving access to evidence-based, comprehensive cancer care for rural residents.

Telehealth offers a different sensory and relational environment for patients, caregivers, and clinicians to engage in conversation [19, 20]. Establishing human connection – a sense of “being known” and feeling deeply heard and understood – is foundational to serious illness conversation [21–24], a core element of state of the art cancer care [25]. However, early evidence is unclear about the barriers and facilitators to doing so in this medium. Oncology and palliative care clinicians cite concerns that picking up on nonverbal cues may be more challenging over telehealth and present a barrier to interpreting patient emotional responses [7, 26, 27]. Some studies observe that patients with cancer are less satisfied with communication through videoconferencing compared to in-person visits [28], explaining they found virtual appointments disruptive to establishing rapport with their clinicians [29, 30].

Therefore, while telehealth may bridge cancer service delivery gaps, especially in rural areas, there is an urgent scientific need to better understand how patients with cancer, their families, and their clinicians connect meaningfully with one another in this different communication environment. Here, we examined perceptions of rural-dwelling patients with cancer regarding whether and how specific characteristics of telehealth communication fostered their experiences of human connection in serious illness conversations. This research provides a foundation for understanding the optimal telehealth communication strategies to enable the delivery of high-quality, comprehensive and geographically accessible cancer care.

Methods

Design

We conducted a single site, qualitative descriptive study between June 2024 and March 2025 [31, 32]. All participants provided informed consent and were offered a digital gift card following participation. The study was approved by the University of Vermont Institutional Review Board (IRB# STUDY00002941). We followed the consolidated criteria for reporting qualitative research guidelines (COREQ) [33] (see Supplementary Material).

Participant eligibility

Patients had to be an adult (≥ 18 years old), currently receiving/or having recently completed treatment for their cancer (i.e., infusion/oral chemotherapy, radiation, cancer surgery, or combination for any cancer diagnosis and stage), live in a rural area (U.S. Census’ Rural-Urban Commuting Area Codes [RUCA] ≥ 4 [34]), have recently received any part of their comprehensive cancer care via telehealth (i.e., synchronous videoconferencing visits within the last 6 months with an oncology or palliative care clinician), be fluent in English, and be able to provide informed consent. Our rationale for recruiting participants who were either actively receiving or who had recently completed cancer treatment was based on the premise that any conversation regarding starting or discontinuing cancer-directed treatment involves discussion of serious news [35, 36].

Data collection

We recruited participants through clinic lists of a community cancer hospital in a rural catchment area in the Northeastern United States. For those who expressed interest, the study team contacted potential participants via phone to discuss the study and confirm inclusion and exclusion screening. We conducted semi-structured interviews with participants in a location of the participants’ choosing (i.e., via Zoom, phone or in-person). Participant selection was based on convenience sampling.

A study team comprised of communication researchers and palliative care clinicians (ET, BG, MR) wrote the interview guide (see Supplementary Material). Interviews were conducted by study investigators (ET, SM, SV) with varied experience in qualitative research and health care (ET is a PhD level, qualitatively trained, nurse researcher with a clinical background in palliative care; SM is a PhD candidate with significant experience in qualitative and communication research; SV is a medical student with previous clinical research experiences). No participant received clinical care from study investigators and only consented participants participated in study interviews.

Interviewers asked about participants’ experiences talking about their cancer via videoconferencing with their clinician (i.e., oncologist or palliative care clinician). Specifically, we asked participants to reflect on conversations that covered serious news, which we defined broadly to participants during interviews as any discussion about their cancer that may include: cancer status, what to expect in the future, and what matters most (such as goals, fears, and worries) [37] (see Supplementary Material). We explored challenges and benefits regarding developing a sense of human connection during these types of conversations [21–23]. Grounded in a Basic Science Framework for Serious Illness Communication Research [38, 39], interview questions were specifically designed to probe communication features (e.g., the words people say and how they say them) and how those elements shaped their experience. Interviews were audio-recorded and automatically transcribed using Zoom. All transcripts were reviewed for accuracy and fully deidentified.

Participants also provided information about their personal (e.g., age, gender, race) and illness (e.g., type of cancer) characteristics. We included three questions adapted from the digital health care literacy scale [40] which asked participants to rate their abilities using telehealth applications on their devices, initiating video chats, and resolving technical issues without assistance (from 0 = “strongly disagree” to 5 = “strongly agree”; higher scores indicate higher digital health literacy). We stored participant data in a REDCap Database, which allowed only authorized users and is a HIPAA best practice, secure web-based application. Interview recordings and transcriptions were saved in an institutional secure drive, in a restricted folder only accessible by the PI, key personnel, and necessary institutional administrators.

Data analysis

We analyzed interviews using directed content analysis [41, 42]. This approach involves drawing comparisons among previously established theoretical constructs and emergent findings from the data. We developed a preliminary coding scheme rooted in a Basic Science Framework for Serious Illness Communication Research, identifying discrete lexical (i.e., verbal), non-lexical (i.e., paraverbal, e.g., tone of voice and cadence, and nonverbal, e.g., body language and eye contact), and contextual (i.e., cultural, spatial, and temporal) communication features that patients reported fostered human connection in telehealth serious illness conversations [38, 39]. Using this coding scheme, to ensure the trustworthiness of study findings [43, 44], three investigators (ET, SM, AH) individually coded and subsequently reviewed all interview conversations. Investigators met bi-weekly to iteratively refine the codebook, resolving any disagreements by reviewing code definitions and group deliberation; we discussed our shared insights related to interrelationships between codes and cross-cutting patterns across participants, which helped us to develop key themes.

The study team had varied experiences caring for persons living with cancer across research, clinical, and personal contexts. Team members included clinicians who provided telehealth care to seriously ill patients, family caregivers who participated in telehealth visits with loved ones, and family members who learned about telehealth encounters through their loved ones’ reports afterward. Throughout data analysis, we engaged in ongoing peer debriefing to reflect on how our positionalities and experiences could influence our interpretation of the data. Reflexivity was encouraged through keeping interview field notes during data collection and analytic memos during the coding process [45]. In these memos, investigators wrote brief notes on thoughts and questions that arose during data collection and analysis, as well as summaries of their impressions of the dataset in response to the research question [46] (see Supplementary Material). These memos created an audit trail for our research team to review, fostering critical engagement and helping propel analysis [47]. The study sample and richness of interview dialogue provided adequate information power to saturate concepts and address the study aims [48]. Qualitative data were managed using Excel.

We described the frequency and distribution of demographic and digital literacy characteristics. We categorized digital health care literacy into three levels according to individual item and average scores across 3 items on the digital health care literacy scale [40] (Low: less than 2; Moderate: 2 to less than 4; High: 4–5). This allowed us to interrogate qualitative descriptions of digital health care literacy and whether this corresponded with participant reported quantitative ratings.

Results

Participant characteristics

Of 64 potentially eligible rural-dwelling patients with cancer, 44 were contacted because doing so met our sampling needs and we then stopped enrolling. Of those contacted, 11 were unable to be reached for interviews (25%), 12 declined to participate (27%), and 21 consented to interviews (48%), with one individual declining to participate after consenting due to worsening health. Participants were interviewed via Zoom (n = 1) or phone (n = 19). Table 1 presents characteristics of the interview participants. Mean duration of the interviews was 29 (range: 14–56) minutes. The mean age of the participants was 66 (range: 48–86) years. Over half of patient participants identified as female (12/20; 60%). All participants identified as white (20/20; 100%) and the majority reported financial security (17/20; 85%). Participants had a wide variety of cancer diagnoses including: lung (4/20; 20%), non-colorectal gastrointestinal (5/20; 25%), colorectal, breast, and prostate (7/20, 35%), and other more rare types (4/20, 20%). The range in travel time to the Cancer Center varied from 28 to 398 min (median = 54).

Qualitative findings

Participants described their experiences of ‘serious news’ conversations to include discussions of their cancer diagnoses, treatment expectations and goals, cancer interval progression, overall prognosis and, occasionally, end-of-life wishes. Participants described their ability to develop connection with their clinicians via telehealth cancer care encounters and emphasized three major themes relevant to this process: (1) the accessibility, comfort, and convenience of telehealth contributes to patient experience of connection; (2) communication actions that enable connection in this setting are those that foster a sense of patients feeling seen as a person, and allow them to see their clinicians as humans as well; (3) patients’ experience of connection is impacted by relational, situational, and technological factors. The accessibility, comfort, and convenience of telehealth contributes to patient experience of connection

Participants spoke to the benefits of telehealth to be able to connect with their clinicians from anywhere. This often meant being able to talk to their clinician from their own home – in their own living room, in their own recliner. Participants expressed how this enabled them to feel more comfortable talking to their care team.

I’m more comfortable here at home than sitting in the hospital waiting after the hour drive, getting stressed out on that interstate, because I hate the interstate. Just- I don’t- just the comforts of home, to me is, there’s no place like home. And I hate […] when I gotta go for treatment, you know, it’s like I go drive an hour, then there’s things that- the way they operate in the hospital that don’t excite me much [laughs] irritates me actually, and I get a little worked up. (Participant 10, 61-year-old cis-female, high digital health care literacy)

Some participants expressed that they noticed their clinicians also conducted televisits from their homes, and participants appreciated this added layer of familiarity saying it felt “more human.”

He does ‘em at home for the most part and you know, I’m at home, he’s at home- that’s good, you know? […] It’s kind of like you’re talking more one on one […] You know I’m at my home he’s at his, and we’re just having a discussion. (Participant 3, 70 -year-old cis-male, high digital health care literacy)

The ability to experience the telehealth visit from the comfort of their own home also helped participants to save time and reduce stress associated with travel to the hospital. Many participants described having to drive over an hour to receive their cancer care; the commute was especially long for patients who were traveling from out of state to receive care.

You’re talking about 2 and a half to 3 h out of the day by the time I leave my, and I get to the ferry right, and you get there, you might, sometimes you hit it just as they’re unloading, you can get on the ferry. Other times, you have to wait for the ferry so that can be up to 20 min, 25 min. Then you got a 20-minute crossing, and you get over there. You gotta wait, you know, depending where you are on the ferry before you can get off, and that can be 10–15 min. Then it’s another 20-minute drive to get to to [Medical Establishment]. Then you get to your appointment. Then you turn around. You’ve got that […] same time frame in reverse. (Participant 20, 60-year-old cis-male, low/mod digital health care literacy)

Participants reflected how telehealth visits enabled them to save time and reduce stress associated with being in the hospital setting as well.

There’s no stress at home. For the most part, I can’t say for everybody. M-most part there’s no stress. You don’t feel [sigh] I don’t know the institution. It’s-it’s you don’t, it’s not like you’re going in, sitting in a-in a chair, waiting for a doctor to take- to look at you, or a nurse to, you know, do your blood pressure. It’s at the same regimented thing that when you’re sitting, it’s like, I really don’t want to be here, you know. At least at home, you can still talk that, you know. You can go over what you gotta go over and still get the same kind of information, and you don’t have to be, you know like waiting or - nothing against the doctors […] they’re not always on time. (Participant 14, 48-year-old cis-male, high digital health care literacy)

Given the added symptom burden of living with cancer and challenges associated with traveling from rural areas, this reduction in traveling and waiting was especially significant.

It’s very beneficial to have a televisit. As I said, I’m not able to get around so I can still have my visits with them but not be at the office. (Participant 9, 63-year-old cis-female, low/mod digital health care literacy)

This convenience could extend to caregivers. While some participants described that their caregivers were present with them regardless of visit type, other participants emphasized that including their caregivers in telehealth visits was easier than in-person visits.

More convenience, you know, my wife can join me without dragging us both, you know to town and find babysitters, you know, that’re just like teenagers. But you know what I’m saying, you know. [laugh] (Participant 7, 52-year-old cis-male, high digital health care literacy).

Participants described how the accessibility, comfort, and convenience of telehealth could enable greater connection with their clinicians. The convenience of telehealth helped participants feel like they had more time with their clinicians, that they weren’t being rushed, and that they were the focus.

Even though you’re in the office, you know you’re in a room they can like pop in and out of the room. When you’re on a telehealth, you’re on the telehealth for the duration of the visit there’s nobody knocking on a door. […] Umm there’s no, you’re not kind of rushed out of the room because you have the allotted time on there. […] It’s like you feel I have their undivided attention, no matter what. And that’s how I feel when I’m on a telehealth. (Participant 16, 56-year-old cis-female, high digital health care literacy)

The availability of telehealth led some participants – even those with low digital literacy – to feel that their clinicians were “at their fingertips,” which could foster security and connectedness between patient and provider.

The, umm telehealth is always at our fingertips, so to speak. So, we’re grateful that we have that, you know. It’s better than a phone call [but] it’s like phone call. It’s like […] Yeah, we just know that we’re always gonna, hopefully, not always, but whether it’s a phone call or telehealth, safe to say, we’re going to reach [somebody], and that caring, we’re very grateful for that. Makes me feel very secure. (Participant 12, 67-year-old cis-female, low/mod digital health care literacy)

At the same time, some participants expressed that they perceived the benefits of telehealth as a tradeoff, balancing a sense of convenience with perhaps some challenges to their experience of connection.

Umm I think it’s just because you’re seeing somebody in 2 dimensions rather than seeing them in person. Uhh, I tend to be fairly empathetic in the way I interact with people, and so, it’s harder to pick up on those details when you’ve got the screen between you. It’s convenient, I mean, it’s handy for me not to have to drive an hour to go talk to somebody, but I want to make sure that they’re actually hearing me, and that they have time to listen to all of my concerns, and that they actually get addressed. (Participant 5, 54-year-old cis-female, high digital health care literacy)

Communication actions that enable connection in this setting are those that foster a sense of patients feeling seen as a person, and allow them to see their clinicians as humans as well

At times, participants struggled to recall specific communication actions that helped them to feel connected in the telehealth setting. Through stories of connection with clinicians in the telehealth environment, participants emphasized that they felt a sense of connection when their clinicians made them feel prioritized, understood, and part of the healthcare team.

Making sure that they, understanding that they see you as a person, you see them as a person, that you’re on the same team, that you’re working together collaboratively to make this thing work, umm yeah, yeah, just reading body language and feeling like you can communicate, and you have a baseline understanding of sort of, who the other one is. (Participant 5, 54-year-old cis-female, high digital health care literacy)

Some participants shared that through thoughtful and empathetic communication, connection was always possible, regardless of the location of the visit.

It’s really the kind of bedside manner, doesn’t matter if it’s coming through the computer or in person, you know, it’s you know, it’s the exchange, and that-that comes through, that comes through either way. It doesn’t matter what the medium is, right? Umm, or the location is, like you feel connected. You feel validated and listened to. That’s, you know, right, it’s almost independent of, you know, of the technology. (Participant 7, 52-year-old cis-male, high digital health care literacy)

Participants expressed they felt known when the clinician showed interest in their personal life – “getting to know me, who I am” – outside of just the cancer.

When I-he comes on, he asks ya, hey, [Participant’s name], how you doing? You know how you been? How’s your daughter? ‘Cause my daughter was with me the first when we had that first interaction […] You know, and so [we’re talking about] things […] hey? How’s the family doing? (Participant 20, 60-year-old cis-male, low/mod digital health care literacy)

Specific communication actions participants emphasized as helping foster this feeling of being seen and heard in telehealth included those which allowed patients to know they were talking with a human through the screen. Actions included making eye contact, maximizing the camera angle to be able to convey and read facial expressions, and using body language to emphasize listening.

Just seeing the face and knowing that you’re talking to a human […] I think seeing them, like, eye-to-eye contact. I think it makes a difference. With me, anyways. (Participant 10, 61-year-old cis-female, high digital health care literacy)

One participant went so far as to describe that it could be helpful to create a “checklist” to help patients with camera placement, to enable a more human interaction.

And so umm, so those-those are all things that I think, for patients if they had, you know, like a little- why would, in my personal opinion, I think you could make a very uh noninvasive, you know an easy little checklist of you know, e-even if they put a little cartoon picture of the like, you know, can-can you see this far, or you know, where do you see a view? Where’s your light? […] You know, uh is your phone, or whatever you’re using in a place that it’s not gonna fall, it’s not gonna [drop] somewhere so you’re gonna be messing with- ha if you start messing with what you have in front of you in front of the camera then that that just like derails the whole concentration of the visit. (Participant 16, 56-year-old cis-female, high digital health care literacy)

Some participants talked about how they felt telehealth enabled greater connection than in-person visits because they perceived their provider was more focused on them and less distracted by documentation than they were during in-person care.

I think I feel more connected over telehealth, actually. […] You know, when I’m sitting, when I- when I’ve been in office visits, my provider is often on a computer, typing in the notes, while we chat. […] In telehealth, I don’t- that doesn’t seem to be as big- I don’t know if they’re doing it, like- like I said, I’ve only had one visit with [Clinician 1], and I didn’t- she wasn’t distracted by- I didn’t see her being distracted by documenting what we were saying. (Participant 11, 65-year-old cis-female, low/mod digital health care literacy)

Patients’ experience of connection in telehealth is impacted by relational, situational, and technological factors

Participants emphasized various factors that impacted their ability to develop connection in the telehealth environment.

Relational

Participants emphasized the importance of a pre-existing relationship with the clinician and how this influenced their ability to connect with their clinician via telehealth. Often, when they knew their clinician already outside of the telehealth environment, this led to them feeling that there wasn’t much of a difference between connecting with their clinician in either setting.

I’ve met with him also personally. So […] I don’t really notice too much of a difference. […] I think that’s just the only way I can describe it-it. It-I don’t think it makes too much difference whether he is, you’re in his office, which is his turf- or actually it makes no difference. (Participant 3, 70-year-old cis-male, high digital health care literacy)

When they didn’t know their clinician in-person, some participants expressed concern around developing a new relationship via telehealth.

Well, what worked so well with-when I was talking to [Clinician1] is that I knew her already. I haven’t met my new oncologist, and I’m concerned about that because you can’t pick up on all the same cues in on telehealth that you could when you’re in person. (Participant 5, 54-year-old cis-female, high digital health care literacy)

Often participants described that a mix of in-person and telehealth visits was important to establish a relationship over time, with several participants describing appreciating meeting their clinician first in-person and then in telehealth for subsequent visits.

I think at least an occasional face-to-face is valuable. I think you get a little better feel for the person […] being able to sit across from them, talk to them. (Participant 2, 77-year-old cis-male, high digital health care literacy)

Participants also described how their telehealth visits could help build connection with their clinicians that then carried into the in-person environment.

and umm when I saw her come over, I immediately knew who she was, you know, ‘cause we hadn’t been to things in person before, […] but w- we knew who each other was, and so there was a good recognition. Umm and she was familiar enough with my case overall that she was able to give me guidance on the spot […] She was, she was able to treat on the spot, so I do feel that there’s definitely a level of personalized care. (Participant 16, 56-year-old cis-female, high digital health care literacy)

Situational

Many participants expressed a preference for certain types of information to be discussed in person as opposed to via telehealth, this included “physical issues” as well as “really big news.” Related to more physical aspects of care, participants emphasized that they would want to be in-person to have someone be able to examine, and treat, their complaints.

The only thing is, I don’t feel comfortable like if something’s bothering me health wise. I don’t feel comfortable just saying, “Hey, this is bothering me”, and you know, how are you gonna do anything when you’re sitting at home on your computer? [laugh] […] And they always tell me there’s somebody in the office that can come see me but […] I mean, if it was something- if it was something serious that pops up, I would definitely say […] it just doesn’t make much sense to me to sit there and tell them about a complaint when they’re not there. And you’re not there. (Participant 17, 73-year-old cis-female, low/mod digital health care literacy)

The emotional weight of the clinical content also influenced some participants’ desire to be face-to-face for discussing “bad news.”

If somebody’s like, I got really bad fucking news, “You’re gonna die.” Of course, tell me that in person, you know? Uhh you know that kind of huge thing, or like “we found something huge,” you know, “something’s different,” you know. […] I’m thinking that would be the only one I could think of. Yeah-Yeah, “you got 3-you got 3 months to live,” you know? Like, yeah, bring me in for that. Bring me in for that shit, you know. I-I don’t want to hear that one over telehealth. (Participant 7, 52-year-old cis-male, high digital health care literacy)

Participants described various reasons for wanting to be in-person for those serious or life-changing visits, for instance, to be able to review scans with their clinician on the computer.

I think the real serious, serious like uh you know, your first initial finding out that you got like cancer or something. Yeah, that should probably be in person, cause I want to see the scans or the X-rays or the CT whatever, I want to see that in-definitely in person. (Participant 14, 48-year-old cis-male, high digital health care literacy)

Other participants described how they wouldn’t want to be at home alone, hearing “something bad.” Some referenced communication norms from outside of their life with cancer, that “you don’t break up with somebody” over the phone. Still, many participants did express feeling that they weren’t limited by telehealth, and that they could discuss any topic in this environment.

No, I don’t- I don’t think there’s any difference. I feel I can say what I need to say on telehealth as well as being in-person. I don’t find that I’m limited. (Participant 9, 63-year-old cis-female, low/mod digital health care literacy)

And one participant did express that they actually preferred having “big news” conversations via telehealth as it allowed them to be in a more “relaxed mental state” and better understand the information being discussed.

Actually, it was reversed for me […] umm the-the oncologist that I have, he is an older gentleman and I-I have I had reservations about him to begin with because the first in person meeting when I ever met him, he went very fast. I didn’t feel like I caught on to everything, that I didn’t necessarily understand the whats and the whys of it, because he just kind of went through it really fast umm including in the office. There’s a much different feel than sitting back in my kitchen being able to talk about these things. […] So I-I think, being able to have the telehealth- I mean, yes you, you’re correct. There are some conversations that have to happen in person umm but it seems for me at least being in a more relaxed environment, not in the stress of the doctor’s office, which you know, get really worked up about umm I think it was helpful for me, because it was able to keep my head more calm, my blood pressure down. You know, take notes more calmly for the things that I needed to follow up if it’s to me it was more relaxed umm mental state that I can help myself with. (Participant 16, 56-year-old cis-female, high digital health care literacy)

Technology

Familiarity with technology could also influence a person’s perceptions of telehealth both globally and specifically about their ability to connect with their clinician in this medium. Some participants described “I’m an engineer” or “I’m a nerd” and didn’t notice a difference in the ability to develop connection with their clinicians via telehealth. In fact, some participants described feeling more connected via telehealth, often citing their familiarity with videoconferencing.

Uh sometimes it’s a little bit better than being in person. […] Uh I- I’m used to this because uh I- […] I’m a store manager. We have meetings once a week where we have to be on like this. (Participant 14, 48-year-old cis-male, high digital health care literacy)

Still, others described preferring face-to-face visits due to not being “too savvy” with technology, or simply preferring in-person contact due to past experiences.

I’m an on-hands person […] I would rather be sitting in the office talking to you face-to-face than on a phone, or what do they call that “pinch time,” or whatever? […] I’m not a computer person. (Participant 1, 79-year-old cis-male, low/mod digital health care literacy)

Participants also described how setting up the telehealth technology – internet connectivity, access to a computer, using Zoom – could require assistance from their family members or clinical team. One participant described how using telehealth changed their traditional approach to communication during visits, because they took the visits on their phone and this disrupted their ability to use their phone to take notes.

I mean, in-person is nice to be able to take notes, like, on my phone […] where I don’t know how to access that feature while I’m having a telehealth video, but, you know, I just write things down pen and paper. (Participant 13, 50-year-old cis-female, high digital health care literacy)

Difficulties with telehealth influencing relationship-building could also be due to provider issues with technology.

One time with my surgeon, he couldn’t even get it going […] and I was sitting there and then, umm, he called me on the phone, thank God, but I was sitting there with you know, wondering what would- what was coming next or whatever. So, I remember some of those created anxiety […] because they weren’t, um you know, prompt enough. (Participant 15, 75-year-old cis-male, high digital health care literacy)

Even participants who didn’t have difficulty with technology worried about how others’ difficulty with technology, specifically surrounding internet access, could affect their ability to access telehealth and develop connection with their clinician.

Either might not have Internet at their house, you know, umm and really again I-I think about those-those folks that could benefit from that-that sense of connection and that- were, but might not be as savvy or have ac- you know, or have the access.[…] I’m fortunate, you know? I have high speed Internet and new computers. You know all that shit, you know. And uhh, and I think, maybe there’s people that don’t have it that wouldn’t be more comfortable. (Participant 7, 52-year-old cis-male, high digital health care literacy)

Still, many participants shared that through technology orientation or training, most barriers to telehealth could be addressed. Additionally, accommodations could be made to meet patient technological needs by the clinical team.

It- it- it worries me, but what they’ve done, they’ve accommodated me. We come early and they put us in a room, and they do their app- iPad. They get it all set up and it’s so much easier than- uhh, I’m already there, I’m already in [Town], but we just come a little earlier for the meeting, and that works out because, umm, I- I just don’t trust myself. (Participant 19, 79-year-old cis-female, low/mod digital health care literacy)

Of note, some people talked about telehealth as one aspect of the digital ecosystem they were using to connect with their cancer care – from MyChart to online support groups. Participants explained how telehealth could enhance their connection, to both their care team and the cancer community more broadly.

And it has made a very isolating, lonely cancer seem just so much less frightening and scary […] the technology is just, it just blows my mind how, how- the impact it’s having on cancer care. And so, you know, telehealth is-is, uhh, an important aspect of that. (Participant 11, 65-year-old cis-female, low/mod digital health care literacy)

Discussion

Our findings highlight key insights into how rural-dwelling patients with cancer experience communication and connection in telehealth-delivered cancer care. Overall, we found that telehealth offers a meaningful way to build connections with clinicians as part of comprehensive cancer care that is accessible, comfortable, and convenient, and that the communication characteristics and processes of telehealth shape this experience for patients.

We identified that the accessibility, comfort, and convenience of telehealth are conditions that can promote connection. This finding adds a new dimension to existing literature that telehealth-delivered comprehensive cancer care can improve access to care, as well as reducing patient travel time and costs, for rural populations [27, 29, 49]. Patient-participants described how taking appointments from their own home, and seeing clinicians in their homes, enhanced a sense of intimacy and familiarity. Additionally, participants spoke to the time-saving nature of telehealth in ways that added to our understanding; participants described that telehealth granted them a sense of having more time, both within and outside the visit. This notion of telehealth offering more time sits in stark contrast to the typical discussion of “time toxicity” involved in cancer care [50] and takes on heightened existential significance when discussing living on a potentially limited timeline due to cancer [51]. Telehealth may therefore offer an important avenue for patients to feel like they have unhurried time to talk, and connect, with their care team in a more familiar environment [21, 52]. These findings have potential policy implications. At the time of this writing, the extension of pandemic-era telehealth flexibilities have lapsed for Medicare beneficiaries, which includes waivers for geographic and originating site restrictions – meaning the ability for individuals to take visits from their home is threatened [53]. Changing these flexibilities not only limits the accessibility of telehealth-delivered comprehensive cancer care for rural populations but may have implications for the development of relationships between patients and clinicians in this setting as well.

Communication behaviors described as important to build connection in our study mirror many recommendations for building connection in the in-person care environment, such as making eye-contact and maintaining an open body position [21, 54–56]. Yet, in virtual settings, these behaviors may require adaptation, such as angling the camera to be able to convey and interpret facial expressions. This is in line with prior observational studies of telehealth encounters which found that nonverbal, paraverbal, and contextual communication practices appeared to reflect adaptations to the technology-mediated environment, including accentuating typical body language and attentiveness to body positioning on camera [19, 20, 27]. Participants described how these behaviors helped them to know they were talking to a human through a screen.

At times, however, participants struggled to recall specific communication actions that helped them to feel connected in the telehealth setting. This finding aligns with theoretical and empirical communication literature that attitudes about others – such as feelings of connection – arise from multiple, complex channels of information, much of which is processed without conscious awareness [57–59]. While patient-participants could identify situations where they felt connected, explaining they felt most seen in the telehealth setting when clinicians sought to know them as a person, outside of their identity as a patient, they were less able to identify specific communication behaviors that fostered that feeling; based on stories of connection with clinicians, we gained an understanding of communication behaviors that contributed to patient experience. These findings carry clinical and research implications. Clinically, our findings support that communication practices such as remembering patients’ names and personal details, as well as exploring their emotional needs and sources of joy, offer ways to attend to elements of personhood and foster connection in virtual settings [21, 52, 60, 61]. Our findings also highlight a need for innovative experimental research designs to be able to more granularly examine which communication features of a serious illness conversation, and in which combinations, influence critical patient outcomes in the virtual setting, notably the experience of connection. This understanding can fuel the creation of empirically derived communication guidelines and training for the telehealth serious illness care context.

Lastly, our findings shed light on the influence of relational, situational, and technological factors on the development of connection in telehealth. Participants described how their experience of virtual cancer care encounters was colored by their technological familiarity as well as the conversational content. Over one third of participants in our study reported needing help setting up applications like Zoom on the Digital Health Literacy Scale [40]; this corresponded with our qualitative findings where participants described how they often required assistance from their family or a member of their clinical team to set-up the telehealth visit (e.g., the internet, computer, and videoconferencing application). This result is not entirely surprising as technological literacy is a commonly cited barrier for telehealth adoption [6]. Yet, despite this barrier, most participants in our study still found telehealth to be a convenient and comfortable care environment. Some participants expressed concern that other people might find technology and internet access barriers to adversely affect their ability to develop connection and experience the benefits of telehealth. This finding echoes prior observations that breakdowns in internet connectivity may challenge the development of human connection during palliative care telehealth encounters [19]. As internet connectivity presents a prominent technical barrier to widespread adoption of telehealth in rural areas given limited or lacking reliable broadband [62], our findings underscore the need for policies that support infrastructure development to feasibly provide equitable access to high-quality telehealth cancer care for rural communities.

Of note, regarding relational factors, participants emphasized how their underlying relationship with their clinician shaped their experience with telehealth communication and connection. Patients described preferring a mix of in-person and telehealth visits, and many expressed a desire to have their first visit in-person. This finding is consistent with prior literature indicating that patients, caregivers, and health care professionals prefer an initial in-person visit before subsequent video consultations to establish rapport [17, 29, 49]. It further supports recommendations for telehealth to be integrated into comprehensive cancer care in a manner that prioritizes patient comfort and choice, supplementing but not replacing in-person care [52]. As telehealth continues to expand, research is necessary to further clarify the optimal timing, frequency, and types of telehealth visits within blended models of care delivery to effectively meet the needs of rural patients with cancer, with attention to these technological, situational, and relational elements.

Limitations and future directions

Our findings should be interpreted in light of several limitations. First, the generalizability of our findings may be limited by the demographic homogeneity of our sample (i.e., predominantly white, older adults, with high socioeconomic status and digital health care literacy). Future work should prioritize exploring the experiences of telehealth connection in more diverse samples, especially given the disparities experienced by rural racial/ethnic minority populations [63] and influence of age on tech literacy [62]. Additionally, because we recruited individuals through the clinic who had telehealth experience, our sample may have represented those who hold more favorable opinions toward telehealth; future studies should prioritize community recruitment to increase the possibility of gathering a wider range of opinions regarding telehealth. Lastly, we only captured one perspective in this study, that of the patient’s, even though communication is often triadic, including patients, caregivers, and clinicians [64, 65]; thus, future research is warranted to explore how caregivers and clinicians experience connection during telehealth conversations.

Conclusions

For individuals with cancer living in rural areas, experiences of human connection in telehealth are shaped by telehealth’s accessibility, clinician communication actions, and contextual factors. Patients valued the comfort, convenience, and accessibility of telehealth which could empower them to have more time to connect. They felt heard and understood when communication enabled them to be known as a person and for them to reciprocally know their clinician was human as well, despite physical separation. Their experience of developing connection in telehealth was influenced by relational, situational, and technological factors, underscoring the need to individually tailor the integration of telehealth into blended models of comprehensive cancer care according to patient comfort and choice. Our study provides a foundation to drive future research uncovering the optimal telehealth communication strategies to promote connection during virtual serious illness conversations, to ultimately improve the quality of life and outcomes for rural-dwelling cancer patients.
