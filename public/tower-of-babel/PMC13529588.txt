Super enhancer-driven transcriptional reprogramming promotes abiraterone resistance via neuroendocrine transition and ferroptosis evasion in castration-resistant prostate cancer
Authors: Yingyin Gao, Yangzhou Liu, Xiaolan Ye, Aochu Liu, Xinyue Li, Shiwen Hu, Xin Chen, Qiaomin Ma, Ladi Mo, Liling Jiang, Lei Tan, Yongle Du, Yueyuan Zheng, Jiankun Yang, Guoxian Luo, Zhigang Zhao, Xianping Shi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAbiraterone resistance represents a major clinical challenge in the management of castration-resistant prostate cancer (CRPC), yet the epigenetic mechanisms that sustain this resistance remain poorly understood. In particular, how super enhancers (SEs) reprogram transcriptional networks to promote this therapy resistance has not been fully elucidated. Here, by integrating chromatin immunoprecipitation sequencing and transcriptome profiling, we identified aberrantly activated oncogenic SEs that drive the transcriptional upregulation of the transcription factors ELF3 and JUNB in abiraterone-resistance CRPC cells. Importantly, SE-driven activation of the ELF3/JUNB axis promotes abiraterone resistance by inducing WNT11-mediated neuroendocrine transition. In parallel, this transdifferentiated state is closely associated with ferroptosis resistance, as evidenced by the upregulation of key ferroptosis-protective genes, including FTH1 and GPX4. In contrast, disruption of the ELF3/JUNB-WNT11 axis markedly restored abiraterone sensitivity and triggered ferroptotic cell death in CRPC cells both in vitro and in vivo. Collectively, our findings highlight targeting SE-driven transcriptional programs as a promising strategy for overcoming abiraterone resistance in CRPC.Subject terms: Cancer genomics, Tumour biomarkers

Introduction

Prostate cancer progression to castration-resistant (CRPC) states represents a major therapeutic challenge, with androgen deprivation therapy (ADT) resistance emerging as a critical clinical barrier [1, 2]. While androgen receptor (AR)-centric resistance mechanisms (e.g., AR-V7 variants) have been well characterized, the role of epigenetic regulation in treatment resistance remains incompletely understood. Despite well-characterized androgen receptor (AR)-centric resistance mechanisms, epigenetic regulation and novel resistance pathways remain incompletely understood, highlighting the need for elucidating actionable targets.

Super enhancers (SEs) are clusters of cis-regulatory elements that drive robust transcriptional activation of key genes through high-density binding of transcription factors, cofactors (e.g., mediator complexes), and epigenetic marks such as H3K27ac [3–7]. Studies have shown that SEs play a key role in cell identity maintenance, developmental regulation, and diseases such as cancer and autoimmune diseases [8]. For example, aberrant SE activity promotes tumorigenesis by activating oncogenic transcriptional networks, including proliferation drivers (MYC, IRF4 in multiple myeloma), and lineage-specific regulators (ASCL1, NEUROD1 in small cell lung cancer) that induce aggressive phenotypes like neuroendocrine differentiation [9, 10]. Critically, SEs also mediate therapy resistance by upregulating drug efflux pumps (ABCB1), anti-apoptotic genes (BCL2), and immune checkpoint molecules (PD-L1), while modulating cytokine signaling (IL-6, TGF-β) to shape the tumor microenvironment [11–13]. Notably, SEs drive tumorigenesis through coordinated transcriptional networks, exemplified by the ELF3/EHF/TGIF1 loop in lung adenocarcinoma progression [14], as we demonstrated, the KLF15/TCF4/NKX2-2 cooperativity is essential for Ewing sarcoma malignancy [15]. In CRPC, SEs are enriched at androgen receptor loci and its target genes (e.g., KLK3, TMPRSS2), sustaining continuous activation of AR signaling pathway [16]. These findings underscore SEs as master regulators of oncogenic programs and potential therapeutic targets. However, whether and how SEs-driven transcriptional reprogramming contribute to abiraterone resistance in prostate cancer cells remains unclear.

Ferroptosis is an iron-dependent form of regulated cell death characterized by the accumulation of lipid peroxidation. Iron mediates this process through Fenton reactions, where ROS promote lipid peroxidation. Excess iron can be sequestered in ferritin, a major iron storage protein, thereby limiting iron-mediated ROS production and reducing ferroptotic susceptibility. Glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4) acts as a gatekeeper of ferroptosis by reducing lipid hydroperoxides (PLOOH) to their corresponding alcohols (PLOH) and its inhibition or loss triggers ferroptotic cell death [17]. Importantly, ferroptosis acts as a tumor-suppressive mechanism in certain cancers [18, 19]. In addition, inducing ferroptosis represents a potential therapeutic strategy to selectively kill cancer cells and inhibit tumor progression [20]. Yet, whether ferroptosis contributes to the development of abiraterone resistance in prostate cancer remains unexplored.

Here, through integrated epigenomic and transcriptomic analysis, we identify ELF3 and JUNB as key SE-activated transcription factors in abiraterone resistance CRPC. Crucially, the ELF3 and JUNB form a mutually reinforcing transcriptional hub maintaining resistance by coordinately reprogramming the CRPC transcriptome, with validation in both abiraterone resistance CRPC cells and patient specimens. Mechanistically, the ELF3/JUNB-WNT11 axis orchestrates resistance through driving neuroendocrine transdifferentiation and suppressing ferroptosis-mediated cell death. Our findings not only reveal a novel SE-mediated resistance network but also identify actionable targets for combating abiraterone resistance CRPC.

Materials and methods

Human sample collection

The specimens were obtained from the laboratory specimen Bank of the Department of Urology, the First Affiliated Hospital of Guangzhou Medical University. The samples included the tumor tissues and their corresponding adjacent normal tissues from patients who progressed to castration-resistant prostate cancer after radical prostatectomy (n = 4), and the tumor tissues from patients with primary prostate cancer (n = 4). Clinical data of the patients involved in the study were verified through the hospital electronic medical record system. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of The Third Affiliated Hospital of Guangzhou Medical University (approval No. 2024-008), and all patients provided written informed consent.

Animal experiments

All animal experiments were approved by the Experimental Animal Ethics Committee of Guangzhou Medical University (approval No. GY2022179). Male C57BL/6 nude mice (3 weeks old) were obtained from Guangdong Yao-Kang Biotechnology Co., Ltd. and maintained under specific pathogen-free conditions. A total of 36 mice underwent bilateral orchiectomy. Two weeks later, castrated mice were randomly divided into six groups (n = 6 per group) and subcutaneously inoculated with corresponding tumor cells. Abiraterone or saline was administered intraperitoneally every 4 days after tumor implantation. Tumor size and body weight were measured every 3 days, and tumor volume was calculated as V = L × W² / 2. On day 31, mice were euthanized; blood was collected via the eye for hormone analysis, and tumors were dissected and weighed.

Plasmid construction and lentivirus production

Overexpression constructs were generated by cloning the full-length coding sequences of human ELF3, JUNB, and WNT11 into the pCDH lentiviral vector. For knockdown, short hairpin RNA (shRNA) oligonucleotides targeting the coding sequences were inserted into the pLKO.1-TRC vector. All oligonucleotide and insert sequences are listed in Supplementary Table 1. Lentiviral particles were produced in HEK293T cells by co-transfecting the transfer plasmid (pCDH or pLKO.1) with the packaging plasmids psPAX2 and pMD2.G using polyethylenimine (PEI). Viral supernatants were collected at 48 and 72 h post-transfection, pooled, concentrated by ultracentrifugation, aliquoted, and stored at –80 °C until use.

siRNA knockdown

Gene-specific siRNAs targeting the sequences of interest and corresponding non-targeting control siRNAs were designed and synthesized by Shanghai GeneChem Co., Ltd. Two independent siRNA oligonucleotides were used per target gene. Transient transfection was performed using Lipofectamine™ RNAiMAX (Invitrogen) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, cells were transfected with 100 nM siRNA diluted in Opti-MEM® I Reduced Serum Medium (Gibco). siRNA-lipid complexes were incubated with cells for 48 h before subsequent functional analyses or RNA/protein extraction.

Cell culture and treatments

All cell lines were cultured at 37 °C in a humidified 5% CO₂ atmosphere and authenticated by short tandem repeat (STR) profiling. The abiraterone resistance C42B-Abi cell line used in our study was generated using a well-established protocol. In brief, parental C42B cells were continuously cultured in medium containing stepwise increasing concentrations of abiraterone acetate, starting at 5 μM and gradually escalating to 50 μM, to select for stable resistant clones. Resistance was routinely maintained in the medium supplemented with 10 μM of abiraterone (Selleckchem). C42, C42B, and the abiraterone resistance C42B-Abi cells were cultured in RPMI 1640 (Gibco) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Excell Bio). NCI-H660 cells were grown in RPMI 1640 with 10% FBS, 10 nM hydrocortisone, 10 nM β-estradiol, 2 mM L-glutamine, and 0.5% insulin-transferrin-selenium (ITS-G; Gibco). HEK293T cells were cultured in DMEM (Gibco) with 10% FBS. C42, C42B, NCI-H660, and HEK293T cells were obtained from ATCC.

Cell proliferation assay

Cell proliferation was assessed using the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8; MeilunBio). Briefly, cells were seeded at 1 × 10³ cells per well in 96-well plates with at least three replicate wells per condition. After treatment, 10 µL of CCK-8 reagent was added to each well and incubated for 1–4 h at 37 °C. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm using an ELx808 microplate reader (BioTek Instruments).

Colony formation assay

Colony formation ability was assessed by plating 1 × 10³ cells per well in 6-well plates (Corning). After 7 days of culture, cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and stained with 0.1% crystal violet solution. The stained colonies were imaged using an inverted microscope (Leica Microsystems). Colony numbers and areas were quantified using ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health).

Migration experiment

Cell migration was evaluated using 24-well Transwell chambers (Corning). Cells were resuspended in serum-free RPMI 1640, and 5 × 10⁴ cells in 300 μL were seeded into the upper chamber. The lower chamber contained 700 μL of RPMI 1640 with 20% FBS as a chemoattractant. After 24 h, non-migrated cells were removed from the upper surface, and migrated cells on the lower membrane were fixed with 100% methanol, stained with 0.1% crystal violet, and imaged using an inverted microscope (Leica). The number of migrated cells was quantified using ImageJ software.

Detection of lipid peroxidation and reactive oxygen species by flow cytometry

Intracellular lipid peroxidation levels were measured using C11-BODIPY⁵⁸¹/⁵⁹¹ (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat: D3861). Cellular reactive oxygen species (ROS) were detected using a commercial ROS Assay Kit (Beyotime Biotechnology, Cat: C1190S). Briefly, cells were incubated with the corresponding fluorescent probes at 37 °C in the dark for 30 min according to the manufacturers’ protocols. After staining, cells were washed, resuspended in PBS, and analyzed immediately on a CytoFLEX flow cytometer (Beckman Coulter) with excitation at 488 nm. Data analysis was performed using FlowJo software (version 10; Tree Star).

Immunofluorescence

Cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 30 min at room temperature and permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 for 15 min. After washing with PBS, cells were blocked with 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA) for 30 min to prevent nonspecific binding. Subsequently, cells were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against MAP2 (Abcam, Cat: ab183830) or NSE (Proteintech, Cat: 10149-1-AP). The following day, cells were washed and incubated for 1 h at room temperature with species-appropriate secondary antibodies conjugated to Alexa Fluor® 488 (for mouse primary antibodies) or Alexa Fluor® 647 (for rabbit primary antibodies) (both from Abcam). Nuclei were counterstained with 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI). Fluorescence images were acquired using a confocal microscope (Carl Zeiss).

RNA extraction and quantitative real-time PCR

Total RNA was extracted from cultured cells or tissue samples using the RNApure Tissue & Cell Kit (NCM Biotech, Cat: M5102). Complementary DNA (cDNA) was synthesized from 1 μg of total RNA per sample using the Hifair® cDNA Synthesis SuperMix (Yeasen Biotechnology, Cat: 12958ES96) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) was performed using TB Green® Premix Ex Taq™ (Yeasen Biotechnology, Cat: 11184ES03) on a QuantStudio™ Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems). The thermocycling conditions were set according to the manufacturer’s protocol. All reactions were run in triplicate. The mRNA expression levels were normalized to GAPDH as an internal control, and relative quantification was calculated using the 2–ΔΔCt method. The sequences of all primers used are listed in Supplementary Table 2.

Western blot analysis and co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP)

Whole cell lysates were prepared in RIPA buffer, and protein concentrations were determined using a BCA Protein Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Equal amounts of protein were separated by 10% SDS-PAGE and transferred onto PVDF membranes. Membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat milk for 1 h at room temperature and incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against ELF3 (Signalway Antibody, 38856), JUNB (Proteintech, 10486-1-AP), GPX4 (Signalway Antibody, 32506), FTH1 (Signalway Antibody, 32180), NSE (Proteintech, 10149-1-AP), SYN (Proteintech, 17785-1-AP), and AR (Abcam, ab088758). After washing, membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated goat anti-rabbit secondary antibody (Proteintech). GAPDH (Proteintech, 10494-1-AP) was used as a loading control. Signals were visualized using enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) reagent (NCM Biotech).

For Co-IP, cells were lysed in IP lysis buffer. Lysates (100 µL) were incubated with 2 µg of specific antibody for 4 h at 4 °C, followed by incubation with Protein A/G magnetic beads (MedChemExpress) overnight at 4 °C. Beads were washed three times with lysis buffer, and bound proteins were eluted by heating in 1× SDS loading buffer at 100 °C for 10 min, then analyzed by immunoblotting.

Luciferase reporter assay

Candidate genomic regions, including the ELF3 promoter (ELF3-P) and two enhancer regions (ELF3-CE1 and ELF3-CE2), were amplified by PCR. ELF3-P was cloned into the pGL3-Basic firefly luciferase reporter vector (Promega), and each enhancer fragment was inserted into the pGL3-Promoter vector (IGEbio). C42B-Abi cells were transfected using BioT Transfection Reagent (Bioland Scientific, B01-00) with a Renilla luciferase control vector (pRL-TK or pRL-SV40, Promega) for normalization. Empty vectors (pGL3-Promoter and pGL3-Basic) were used as controls. Forty-eight hours post-transfection, luciferase activity was measured using the Dual-Luciferase® Reporter Assay System (YEASEN, 11402ES) on a GloMax® Discover microplate reader (Promega).

ChIP-Seq and CUT&Tag analysis

ChIP uses the method described before [15, 21]. However, tumor tissue needs to be extracted for some special processing before standardized ChIP. The tumor tissue was the size of a soybean, and it was completely crushed by using a homogenizer under 2– 3 mL 1% paraformaldehyde (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat: 28908USA) immersion, and then centrifugally filtered under a 25 μM filter. The obtained filtrate can continue to be carried out according to the ChIP step. CUT&Tag was performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions (TD903, Vazyme).

Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) and immunohistochemistry (IHC) staining

Immunohistochemistry was carried out as previously described [22]. Formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded tissue sections from prostate cancer patients were stained with anti-ELF3 and anti-JUNB antibodies. Tumor xenografts were stained with anti-ELF3, anti-JUNB, anti-GPX4, anti-FTH1, anti-NSE, and anti-WNT11 antibodies. Images were acquired and analyzed using the NanoZoomer platform (Hamamatsu Photonics).

RNA-seq analysis

The GRCh37 reference genome was used for HISAT2 (v2.1.0) and FPKM was calculated to normalize the raw reads. Deseq2 (v1.46.0) or limma (v3.62.2) were performed to identify differentially expressed genes that were indicated if p < 0.05 and absolute log2 Fold-Change >1.

Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA)

For the knockdown of JUNB and ELF3, the up-regulated and down-regulated genes were treated as a gene set, log2 Fold-Change, and the corresponding genes were treated as a rank list, then analyzed using the GSEA Prerank (v-4.4.0).

Survival analysis

To check the association between TFs and clinical outcome of patients by the Kaplan-Meier analysis. The cutoff was determined by respective mean expression of TFs, then we performed Kaplan-Meier analysis using survival (v3.8-3) and survminer (v0.5.0).

Statistical analysis

All the statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 22.0 software (SPSS, Inc., USA). The experimental results were statistically evaluated using Student’s t test for comparisons between two groups or one-way ANOVA for comparisons between more than two groups. Differences were considered significant if p < 0.05.

Results

Identification of SE-driven transcription factors in abiraterone resistance CRPC

Through comprehensive characterization of our established abiraterone resistance C42B-Abi cell line, which demonstrates significantly reduced drug sensitivity compared to parental C42B cells in both in vitro and in vivo assays, with resistance confirmed by RNA-seq and whole exome sequencing, we identified substantial epigenetic reprogramming associated with therapeutic resistance [23]. To clarify the role of SE in abiraterone resistance CRPC, we previously performed H3K27ac ChIP-Seq in C42B and C42B-Abi cell lines (Fig. 1A) [23], identifying 642 specific SEs in C42B-Abi and 258 specific SEs in C42B, suggesting that substantial epigenetic reprogramming is associated with drug resistance (Fig. 1B). KEGG analysis revealed C42B-specific SEs were enriched in metabolic processes including sphingolipid de novo biosynthesis, lipogenesis regulation, and aldosterone synthesis/secretion, while C42B-Abi-specific SEs were associated with resistance-related pathways such as axon guidance, lysosome and mitophagy (Fig. S1A, B). These findings demonstrate abiraterone resistance in C42B-Abi cells is mediated through SE-driven transcriptional activation of survival pathways, highlighting the critical role of epigenetic remodeling in therapeutic resistance.

To elucidate the transcriptional regulatory mechanisms driving abiraterone resistance in CRPC, we performed an integrated multi-omics analysis combining SE profiling, transcriptomic data, and clinical validation. Initial characterization of the 642 C42B-Abi-specific SEs (Fig. 1B) identified 44 candidate SE-associated transcription factors (SE-TFs). RNA-seq revealed 11 transcription factors significantly upregulated in resistant C42B-Abi cells compared to parental C42B cells (Fig. 1C), among which six exhibited high expression. Further Pearson correlation analysis of these six TFs using single-cell RNA-seq data from CRPC patients (GSE274229) [24] suggested a potential transcriptional regulatory loop involving JUNB, ELF3, and ATF3 (Fig. 1D). Epigenetic validation using H3K27ac ChIP-Seq across multiple clinical cohorts, including four CRPC tumors with matched adjacent tissues and castration-sensitive prostate cancer (CSPC) tumors [23], as well as integrated data from 15 CRPC patient-derived xenograft (PDX) models and 24 CSPC tumors from the GEO130408 dataset, consistently demonstrated elevated H3K27ac signals at the enhancer regions of these TFs in CRPC compared to CSPC or benign tissues (Fig. 1E) [25]. To verify this regulatory circuit, we performed siRNA-mediated knockdown of each of the three transcription factors in C42B-Abi cells and assessed reciprocal expression changes among the others. A specific regulatory interaction was observed only between ELF3 and JUNB, indicating a bidirectional regulatory relationship (Fig. 1F). Clinical validation confirmed that both ELF3 and JUNB protein levels were significantly elevated in CRPC specimens compared to primary prostate tumors and adjacent benign tissues (Fig. 1G, Fig. S1C). Building upon the experimental identification of the ELF3-JUNB regulatory interaction in resistant cells, we next evaluated its clinical specificity and prognostic relevance through multi-dataset integration. As shown in Figure 1D, there was a significant positive correlation between ELF3 and JUNB expression, which was restricted to CRPC tumors (not in CSPC or other malignancies; Fig. 1H). This disease-specific correlation suggests that the ELF3-JUNB axis represents a distinctive transcriptional feature of CRPC. Kaplan-Meier survival analysis demonstrated CRPC patients exhibiting concurrent high expression of both ELF3 and JUNB had poorer overall survival (Fig. 1I). The adverse prognostic effect was most pronounced in the dual-high expression subgroup, indicative of synergistic oncogenic cooperation between these transcription factors. Collectively, the ELF3-JUNB axis is a CRPC-specific transcriptional program promoting tumor progression and therapeutic resistance.

SE-driven transcription factors ELF3 and JUNB regulate each other in abiraterone resistance CRPC

Based on the high correlation between ELF3 and JUNB, we investigated their mutual regulation and functional cooperation. siRNA-mediated knockdown demonstrated reciprocal regulation between ELF3 and JUNB at both mRNA and protein levels in C42B-Abi cells (Fig. 2A, B), confirming that this transcriptional network mediates CRPC resistance to abiraterone.

To elucidate their co-regulatory mechanism, we performed CUT&Tag analysis using antibodies specific to each transcription factor. The results revealed that ELF3 and JUNB not only bind to each other’s enhancers and promoters but also occupy their own regulatory regions, forming a functionally coherent transcriptional axis in CRPC. Based on these binding profiles, we identified one promoter region (PR1) and two candidate enhancer regions (CE1, CE2) within the ELF3 locus (Fig. 2C). HOMER motif analysis revealed that ELF3 and JUNB binding motifs were separated by 141 bp at ELF3-CE1 and 109 bp at ELF3-CE2, confirming co-binding within compact genomic intervals. Similarly, a 140 bp co-bound region was identified within the JUNB locus (Fig. 2D). Luciferase reporter assays in C42B-Abi cells showed robust activity of PR1, CE1, and CE2, which was markedly reduced upon knockdown of each factor (Fig. 2E). These results support direct regulation of ELF3 promoter and super-enhancer by ELF3 itself and its counterpart JUNB. To further validate the transcriptional functionality of CE1 and CE2, we recruited a transcriptional repressor complex to this non-coding region using a catalytically-dead CRISPR-Cas9 system [26]. The sgRNAs against CE1/CE2 separately reduced the mRNA and protein expression of ELF3/JUNB (Fig. 2F, G). Furthermore, blocking CE1/CE2 also impaired cell proliferation and increased sensitivity to abiraterone (Fig. 2H, I), phenocopying ELF3 knockdown. Additionally, the immunoprecipitation results confirmed protein interaction between ELF3 and JUNB (Fig. 2J). Together, these findings indicate that ELF3 and JUNB function as an interdependent transcriptional complex sustaining oncogenic signaling and therapy resistance in abiraterone resistance CRPC.

ELF3 and JUNB orchestrate the transcriptional regulatory network in abiraterone resistance CRPC

To investigate how ELF3 and JUNB orchestrate the transcriptional network in abiraterone resistance CRPC, we characterized their genome-wide binding patterns and epigenetic features in C42B-Abi cells. Promoters (H3K27ac⁺/H3K4me3⁺/H3K4me1⁻) and distal enhancers (H3K27ac⁺/H3K4me3⁻/H3K4me1⁺) were annotated based on histone modification profiles. Genome-wide binding analysis showed that 73.4% of promoters (4,920/6,706) and 36.8% of enhancers (15,720/42,746) were bound by at least one factor, with co-occupancy occurring more frequently than solo-factor binding (Fig. 3A, B). Dual-binding regions exhibited higher H3K27ac intensity than solo-factor-bound regions at both promoters and enhancers (Fig. 3C) and genes linked to dual-occupied enhancers demonstrated significantly higher expression (Fig. 3D), suggesting that co-binding enhances transcriptional output. Furthermore, dual-occupancy regions showed greater overlap with super-enhancers compared to solo-occupancy regions (Fig. 3E). These findings indicate that ELF3 and JUNB function synergistically to remodel the epigenome of abiraterone resistance CRPC cells, amplifying oncogenic transcriptional activity through coordinated binding. To validate their cooperative transcriptomic regulation, we conducted RNA-seq in C42B-Abi cells following individual knockdown of each transcription factor using shRNA (Fig. 3F). GSEA revealed significant overlap between genes altered upon ELF3 silencing and those altered upon JUNB silencing (Fig. 3G, H). Furthermore, genes repressed by ELF3 or JUNB alone were enriched among transcripts highly expressed in C42B-Abi cells (Fig. 3I), supporting their essential role in maintaining the resistant transcriptional landscape.

To investigate how these TFs contribute to therapy resistance, we conducted KEGG pathway enrichment analysis on genes highly expressed in C42B-Abi cells. The results highlighted significant involvement of the axon guidance signaling pathway and the ferroptosis signaling pathway (Fig. 3J). Knockdown of ELF3 or JUNB also enriched neuroendocrine-related processes, including axon guidance and neuroactive ligand-receptor interaction (Fig. 3K). These findings imply that ELF3 and JUNB collaboratively contribute to abiraterone resistance in CRPC, at least in part through the dysregulation of neuroendocrine signaling pathways.

ELF3-JUNB axis promote the malignant biological behavior of abiraterone resistance CRPC

To validate whether abiraterone resistance CRPC depends on the ELF3-JUNB axis, we performed functional assays in C42B-Abi cells. Knockdown of either ELF3 or JUNB in C42B-Abi cells significantly suppressed cellular proliferation (Fig. 4A), reduced migratory ability and colony formation (Fig. 4B, Fig. S1D), increased sensitivity to abiraterone (Fig. 4C). Conversely, ectopic co-expression of ELF3 and JUNB in abiraterone-sensitive C42B and C42 cells induced resistance-like phenotypes, including enhanced proliferation (Fig. 4D–F), increased clonogenic and migratory capacities (Fig. 4G, Fig. S1E), and reduced drug sensitivity (Fig. 4H).

ELF3 and JUNB regulate neuroendocrine signaling pathways in abiraterone resistance CRPC

We confirmed the role of transcriptional regulatory networks in driving abiraterone resistance in C42B-Abi cells, but the specific mechanism of action remains elusive. Comparative analysis revealed that C42B-Abi cells exhibit increased cellular protrusions and morphological changes relative to parental C42B, consistent with a neuroendocrine phenotype [23]. GSEA revealed that genes co-downregulated upon ELF3 or JUNB knockdown significantly overlapped with neuroendocrine signatures (Fig. 5A, B). Consistent with this, knockdown of each factor reduced the abundance of NSE⁺ and MAP2⁺ synaptic structures and decreased protein levels of NSE and SYN, while their overexpression in C42B cells elevated these markers (Fig. 5C–E, Fig. S2A).

To further validate the role of ELF3/JUNB axis in neuroendocrine signaling pathways, we analyzed the RNA-seq data of C42B, C42B-Abi, and neuroendocrine prostate cancer cell line NCI-H660 (GSE289308). Transcriptomic profiling showed high similarity between C42B-Abi and NCI-H660 cells, defining a distinct “Abi-NE-like” molecular signature (Fig. S2B). Chromatin accessibility analysis identified 6,047 common peaks between C42B-Abi and NCI-H660, far exceeding the 240 shared with parental C42B (Fig. S2C). Among these, we detected 209 SE regions and six upregulated transcription factors, including ELF3 and JUNB, which were also elevated in NEPC patients (Fig. S2D–F). Functional validation in NCI-H660 cells confirmed that knockdown of ELF3 or JUNB reduced NSE and SYN expression and restored drug sensitivity (Fig. S2G, H). These results demonstrate that ELF3 and JUNB drive a neuroendocrine differentiation program that underlies abiraterone resistance in CRPC.

To elucidate how SE-driven TFs regulate neuroendocrine transformation, we integrated CUT&Tag and RNA-seq data for ELF3 and JUNB. This analysis identified 11 common target genes, including WNT11 and SYT13 that were bound by both TFs and exhibited altered expression (Fig. 5F, Fig. S2I). Among these, WNT11, a key ligand in the non-canonical Wnt pathway, has been increasingly implicated in NEPC progression and therapy resistance. Prior studies indicate that WNT11 overexpression can activate CAMKII/NF-κB or JNK signaling, promoting epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT), neuroendocrine differentiation (NED), and even immune evasion in metastatic contexts [27–30]. Based on these findings, we hypothesized that WNT11 acts as a downstream effector of the ELF3/JUNB axis. Knockdown of ELF3 or JUNB significantly reduced WNT11 protein levels (Fig. 5G), and CUT&Tag confirmed direct binding of both TFs to the WNT11 promoter (Fig. S2J). These findings support a model in which ELF3 and JUNB directly activate WNT11 expression to promote neuroendocrine differentiation during abiraterone resistance in CRPC. Knockdown of WNT11 in C42B-Abi cells suppressed proliferation (Fig. 5H), decreased sensitivity to Wnt-C59 (Fig. 5I), reduced synapse formation (Fig. 5J), lowered expression of NSE and SYN (Fig. S3A), decreased migration (Fig. S3B). Conversely, WNT11 overexpression in C42B cells enhanced proliferation (Fig. 5K, Fig. S3C), increased sensitivity to Wnt-C59 (Fig. 5L), increased migration (Fig. S3D), elevated synaptic structures, and upregulated NSE and SYN expression (Fig. 5M, Fig. S3E). Importantly, restoring WNT11 expression in ELF3- or JUNB-knockdown cells rescued the expression of neuroendocrine markers, proliferative capacity and drug sensitivity profiles (Fig. 5N–P). Consistent with these observations, knockdown of WNT11 in the NCI-H660 also reduced NSE and SYN expression and increased drug sensitivity (Fig. S3F, G). The concordance of phenotypes across resistant sublines, parental cells, and neuroendocrine models underscores the robustness and translational relevance of this mechanism. Building upon these mechanistic insights, we evaluated the therapeutic potential of targeting this pathway in CRPC. Pharmacological inhibition using Wnt-C59 significantly suppressed C42B-Abi cell proliferation (Fig. S3I) and reduced WNT11, NSE, and SYN expression (Fig. S3I). Notably, combined treatment with Wnt-C59 and abiraterone synergistically enhanced growth inhibition compared to monotherapy (Fig. S3J). These findings establish WNT11 as a functional downstream effector of the ELF3/JUNB axis that drives neuroendocrine differentiation and abiraterone resistance in CRPC.

Reciprocal antagonism between the ELF3-JUNB axis and AR underlies NEPC-like transformation in abiraterone resistance CRPC

NEPC is an aggressive variant characterized by therapy resistance, loss of AR expression, and elevated neuroendocrine markers such as SYN and NSE, which features increasingly observed following abiraterone treatment [31–34]. In line with this, our resistant C42B-Abi cells exhibited a pronounced NEPC-like phenotype. Integrated analysis of single-cell datasets (GSM4203181, GSE137829, GSM4008646) confirmed that ELF3 and JUNB are upregulated while AR is downregulated during the progression from CRPC to NEPC (Fig. S4A–C), prompting an investigation into the regulatory interplay between the ELF3-JUNB axis and AR. Consistent with NEPC features, C42B-Abi cells showed significantly reduced AR protein levels alongside elevated ELF3 and JUNB expression (Fig. S4D). This is consistent with the consensus that androgen receptor signaling inhibitor treatment leads to dedifferentiation of CRPC into NEPC [35]. Silencing ELF3 or JUNB in C42B-Abi increased AR expression (Fig. S4E), indicating a negative regulatory relationship between AR and this transcriptional regulatory axis. Conversely, AR knockdown in C42B-Abi cells upregulated both mRNA and protein levels of ELF3 and JUNB (Fig. S4F-G), and AR overexpression suppressed their expression (Fig. S4H). Notably, this reciprocal repression was absent in parental C42B cells (Fig. S4I–K), highlighting a context-specific regulatory circuit exclusive to the resistant, NEPC-like state. These findings demonstrate a mutually antagonistic relationship between AR and the ELF3-JUNB axis in abiraterone resistance CRPC, and suggest that targeting this transcriptional network may restore AR expression and partially reverse abiraterone resistance.

ELF3 and JUNB confer ferroptosis resistance in CRPC via transcriptional activation of FTH1 and WNT11

Integrated transcriptomes and KEGG pathway enrichment analysis identified ferroptosis signaling was the key pathway regulated by ELF3 and JUNB in abiraterone resistance CRPC (Figs. 3J, 6A). Compared with C42B cells, C42B-Abi cells exhibited resistance to the ferroptosis inhibitor Ferrostatin-1 (Fig. 6B). Knockdown of ELF3 or JUNB in C42B-Abi cells increased intracellular reactive oxygen species, lipid peroxidation, and ferrous ion levels (Fig. 6C–G), indicating that both ELF3 and JUNB inhibit ferroptosis. To delineate the molecular mechanism, integration of CUT&Tag and transcriptomic profiles identified 15 ferroptosis-related genes downregulated upon TF silencing (Fig. 6H). The ferroptosis negative regulator FTH1 was highly expressed in C42B-Abi cells at both transcript and protein levels (Fig. 6I). Knockdown of ELF3 or JUNB reduced the expression of FTH1 and GPX4 in C42B-Abi cells (Fig. 6J), and CUT&Tag confirmed ELF3 and JUNB directly bind to the enhancer region of the FTH1 gene (Fig. 6K). Consistently, silencing ELF3/JUNB significantly downregulated the protein levels of FTH1 and GPX4 (Fig. 6L), whereas overexpression in C42B cells upregulated the protein expression levels of FTH1 and GPX4 and reduced sensitivity to Ferrostatin-1 (Fig. S5A-B). Subsequent Ferrostatin-1 functional rescue experiments showed the inhibitor restored the proliferation ability and abiraterone resistance of ELF3/ JUNB-deficient cells (Fig. 6M, N), suggesting that ferroptosis inhibition is the core link of ELF3/JUNB axis mediating abiraterone resistance phenotype.

Emerging evidence highlights a critical interplay between neuroendocrine pathways and ferroptosis regulation in cancer progression. For instance, sorafenib has been shown to induce ferroptosis in pancreatic neuroendocrine tumors by suppressing HMOX1-mediated WNT/β-catenin signaling [36], while ASCL1 overexpression drives NEPC progression through ferroptosis resistance [37]. In our study, the ELF3-JUNB axis regulates both neuroendocrine differentiation and ferroptosis, prompting investigation into their potential interplay in CRPC. Single-cell RNA-seq of clinical NEPC specimens revealed elevated expression of ferroptosis inhibitors, particularly FTH1 and GPX4, in NEPC compared to CRPC (Fig. S5C). Functionally, WNT11 knockdown in Abi-NE-like cells increased lipid peroxidation (Fig. S5D) and reduced FTH1 and GPX4 protein expression (Fig. S5E), whereas WNT11 overexpression in C42B cells upregulated both proteins and decreased Ferrostatin-1 sensitivity (Fig. S5F-G). Critically, WNT11 reconstitution in ELF3/JUNB-deficient cells rescued GPX4 and FTH1 expression (Fig. S5H) and suppressed lipid peroxidation (Fig. S5I). These findings establish WNT11 as a key mediator linking the SE-driven ELF3/JUNB axis to ferroptosis resistance and neuroendocrine differentiation, revealing a unified transcriptional mechanism driving abiraterone resistance in CRPC.

Knockdown of ELF3 or JUNB significantly inhibited tumor growth (Fig. 7A–C). Consistent with our in vitro observations, immunohistochemical staining (Fig. 7F, G) revealed markedly reduced protein expression of the neuroendocrine markers WNT11 and NSE, as well as the ferroptosis-related proteins FTH1 and GPX4, in tumors from the shELF3 and shJUNB groups compared with the PLKO control group. These findings indicate that the ELF3/JUNB axis mediates abiraterone response by regulating neuroendocrine differentiation and ferroptosis. Collectively, these in vivo experiments establish the ELF3/JUNB axis as a central driver of malignant progression and treatment resistance in CRPC. Further, ELF3/JUNB knockdown decreased WNT11, neuroendocrine marker NSE, and ferroptosis markers FTH1/GPX4 in xenografts (Fig. 7F–H), confirming the axis mediates abiraterone response via regulating neuroendocrine differentiation and ferroptosis. These in vivo data establish ELF3/JUNB as a central driver of CRPC malignancy and treatment resistance.

Discussion

The progression of prostate cancer to CRPC and NEPC represents a major clinical challenge. Under sustained pressure from anti-androgen therapies such as abiraterone, a subset of CRPC tumors undergoes neuroendocrine differentiation via mechanisms involving lineage plasticity and epigenetic reprogramming, leading to therapeutic resistance and poor outcomes. In this study, we identified the super-enhancer-driven TFs ELF3 and JUNB as key regulators of abiraterone resistance in C42B-Abi cells through integrated multi-omics analyses, including super-enhancer profiling and transcriptome sequencing. Although both ELF3 and JUNB have been implicated in the progression, therapy resistance, and metastasis of various malignancies, their cooperative role in CRPC had not previously been reported.

Recent advances have highlighted SEs as critical epigenetic drivers of PC progression and therapeutic resistance. In enzalutamide-resistant CRPC, SEs near the AR locus and its downstream targets (e.g., FKBP5 and NKX3-1) maintain AR signaling by recruiting coactivators (e.g., MED1 and BRD4) to stabilize chromatin loops and enhance transcriptional output [38, 39]. Notably, SE-mediated mechanisms extend beyond canonical AR pathways: (1) SE-driven AR splice variants (e.g., AR-V7) promote endocrine therapy resistance, and (2) SE-activated SOX2 enhances stemness and drug resistance via Wnt/β-catenin signaling [40]. These findings underscore SEs as master regulators of CRPC plasticity and survival. Importantly, CRISPR/Cas9-based studies now reveal the therapeutic potential of targeting SE hubs to disrupt oncogenic networks in PC [41].

Our study establishes three novel aspects of ELF3 and JUNB function in CRPC. First, the ELF3/JUNB axis is specifically activated in abiraterone resistance CRPC cells and correlates with clinical CRPC specimens, indicating a context-specific role. Second, ELF3 and JUNB form a stable protein complex that co-occupies nucleosomal units with precisely spaced binding motifs, enabling coordinated transcriptional regulation. Third, high co-expression of ELF3 and JUNB predicts significantly poorer patient survival compared to single-high or low expressors, underscoring their synergistic oncogenic effect.

Mechanistically, our findings demonstrate that the ELF3/JUNB axis drives abiraterone resistance through two coordinated pathways: induction of neuroendocrine differentiation and suppression of ferroptosis. WNT11 emerged as a pivotal downstream effector, whose overexpression under AR inhibition induced neuroendocrine markers (e.g., NSE, MAP2, and SYN) and loss of AR signaling, consistent with prior reports linking WNT11 to NEPC [28, 36, 42]. Importantly, we establish that ELF3/JUNB directly activates WNT11 transcription, providing a novel upstream regulatory mechanism underlying WNT11-mediated lineage plasticity. This finding is consistent with emerging evidence for a role for WNT11 in NEPC development but extends its role by demonstrating that ELF3/JUNB is an upstream regulator. In parallel, we uncovered a previously unknown role for ELF3/JUNB in ferroptosis evasion. Although ferroptosis induction has shown promise as an anticancer strategy, e.g., through drug combinations such as flubendazole plus 5-fluorouracil to induce the death of CRPC cells by promoting ferroptosis [37], the transcriptional regulation of ferroptosis in CRPC remained poorly defined. Here, we demonstrate that ELF3/JUNB directly transactivates key ferroptosis defense genes FTH1 and GPX4, thereby enhancing antioxidant capacity and promoting survival under oxidative stress. Crucially, WNT11 serves as a multifunctional node that not only promotes neuroendocrine differentiation but also enhances GPX4 and FTH1 expression, thereby synchronizing lineage plasticity with ferroptosis evasion. This crosstalk underscores a cohesive transcriptional program that concurrently drives both adaptive phenotypes in abiraterone resistance prostate cancer. Thus, the ELF3/JUNB–WNT11 axis synchronously drives two hallmarks of advanced prostate cancer: lineage plasticity and adaptive redox homeostasis. Therapeutically, the efficacy of the WNT11 inhibitor Wnt-C59 in restoring drug sensitivity confirms WNT11 as a key therapeutic downstream effector of this pathway, and combining Wnt-C59 with abiraterone yielded synergistic antitumor effects, underscoring the translational potential of co-targeting these pathways in abiraterone resistance CRPC. Notably, AR expression was significantly downregulated in abiraterone resistance C42B-Abi cells compared to parental C42B cells, reflecting a phenotype consistent with AR-independent transdifferentiation or neuroendocrine dedifferentiation, as previously described [43]. Our results further reveal that the ELF3/JUNB axis plays a critical role in this neuroendocrine transformation. Integration of public RNA-seq and ChIP-Seq data from the NCI-H660 neuroendocrine prostate cancer model reinforced the similarity between C42B-Abi cells and clinical NEPC, supporting the relevance of this mechanism. Although AR expression is typically suppressed during neuroendocrine differentiation, we identified a negative regulatory relationship between AR and the ELF3/JUNB complex specifically in resistant cells, an interaction not observed in treatment-naive counterparts. This finding indicates the activation of an AR-independent survival pathway during resistance development, wherein ELF3/JUNB sustains oncogenic functions despite attenuated AR signaling. These insights help elucidate the failure of AR-directed therapies and illustrate how resistant clones propagate under continuous AR suppression. Despite these advances, important questions remain to be addressed in future studies. First, the upstream signals initiating super-enhancer activation of ELF3/JUNB in resistant cells remain unknown and merit further investigation. Second, the relative contributions of neuroendocrine transition versus ferroptosis evasion to clinical treatment failure require clarification through larger cohort studies. Last, clinical translation and utility are critical. Accumulating evidence highlights that the non-canonical WNT ligand WNT11 represents a promising therapeutic target in advanced prostate cancer, where it promotes immune exclusion, therapy resistance, and neuroendocrine differentiation[27, 29]. Targeting WNT11 signaling cascade could effectively reverse drug resistance and restrict tumor malignant phenotypes in preclinical models [44]. Nevertheless, several limitations restrict its clinical translation. First, WNT11 exhibits widespread physiological functions in normal tissue homeostasis and organ development, which may cause unavoidable off-target toxicity upon systemic blockade [45]. Second, compensatory activation of other Wnt ligands and crosstalk with canonical Wnt/β-catenin signaling may weaken the therapeutic efficacy of single-agent WNT11 inhibition [46]. Moreover, highly specific WNT11-neutralizing antibodies or targeted small-molecule drugs are still lacking at present. Therefore, combined therapeutic strategies and precise patient stratification are required to improve the clinical applicability of WNT11-targeted intervention in future research.

In summary, we unveil an SE-driven transcriptional program (ELF3/JUNB–WNT11 axis) (Fig. 7I) that promotes abiraterone resistance via coordinated neuroendocrine differentiation and ferroptosis suppression. These findings deepen mechanistic understanding of prostate cancer progression and provide a translational framework for targeting treatment-resistant malignancies.
