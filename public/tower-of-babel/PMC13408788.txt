Capnofrasera abieticola, a new species of dothideomycetous sooty moulds on Abies veitchii and its molecular phylogenetic position
Authors: Junta Sugiyama, Akira Hashimoto, Kyung-Ok Nam, Yosuke Degawa, Tsuyoshi Hosoya
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
A new species of dothideomycetous sooty mould, Capnofrasera abieticola, is illustrated and described based on fresh specimens collected in Sugadaira, central Japan. It occurred mixed with the subicula of another dominant sooty mould, Metacapnodium neesii (Metacapnodiaceae), on the living twigs and leaves of Abies veitchii (Pinaceae, Pinales). It is morphologically distinguishable from the closest species Capnofrasera flatbergii on branches of Ulmus sp. (Ulmaceae, Rosales) in Sweden by its shorter and narrower phragmoconidia and its host preference. A single phragmoconidium was successfully isolated from the holotype (TNS F-108821) and yield a sterile culture (i.e., no fructifications). Molecular phylogeny based on the concatenated SSU, LSU, and RPB2 dataset revealed that C. abieticola could be assigned to Neoantennariellaceae, not Antennulariellaceae within Capnodiales s. str. (Dothideomycetes).

1. Introduction

Sooty mould communities in host plants are complex. Subicula on plant hosts are frequently mixed with the mycelia of other sooty moulds (Bose et al., 2014; Chomnunti et al., 2014; Hughes, 1976, 2003; Hughes & Seifert, 2012; Hughes et al., 2020; Sugiyama & Amano, 1987; Sugiyama & Hosoya, 2022; Sugiyama et al., 2020). In the addendum of our previous paper (Sugiyama et al., 2020), based on two specimens (TNS F-91312 and F-91313) collected in Oct 2020, we briefly noted the occurrence of a species of another sooty mould genus Capnofrasera S. Hughes, mixed with the dominant sooty mould Metacapnodium neesii (S. Hughes) Sugiy. & Hosoya on living twigs and leaves of the conifer Abies veitchii in Sugadaira, Nagano Prefecture, central Japan (alt. ca. 1,360 m). Subsequently, additional Capnofrasera specimens (TNS F-108900, F-108821, and F-108901) were collected from the same host at the same locality in Jun and Oct 2024 (Supplementary Fig. S1).

The generic name Capnofrasera was published by Hughes (2003; cf. Seifert et al., 2011) to accommodate a single species C. dendryphioides S. Hughes, based on 12 herbarium specimens collected by Hughes from seven different host plants, including Nothofagus fusca, Weinmannia sp., Baccharis discolor, Arbutus menziesii, and Vaccinium reticulatum, from New Zealand, Chile, Venezuela, Brazil, Canada, and Hawaii, USA. The species was typified by the holotype specimen PDD 76423 (DAOM 96120(b), isotype) occurring on living leaves and twigs of Leptospermum scoparium in New Zealand. Hughes (2003) defined the genus in the Latin diagnosis―here translated from Latin into English―as “Colonies variable, black, thinly velvety. Mycelium superficial, composed of branched, roughly cylindrical hyphae, brown to dark brown, anastomosed. Conidiophores macronematous, scattered or gathered into loose fascicles, tapering, opposite or unilateral branches, branchlets toward apex. Conidiogenous cells ovoid, obpyriform, or somewhat conical, mostly monoblastic, solitary discrete or successively integrated into conidiophores. Scars split crescent-shaped due to proliferation partly through the wall below the scar or lateral, rounded, subdentate. Conidia solitary, obclavate or subcylindrical, brown, thin-walled, smooth or verrucose, sometimes longitudinally striated, (1-) 2-septate to multiseptate, with or a denticulate scar.” Hughes (2003) suggested that C. dendryphioides may have antennulariellaceous affinities and mentioned that several congeneric but extralimital species remained to be described.

Hughes et al. (2020) examined herbarium specimens assigned to Capnofrasera from various host plants and localities, and revised the generic and species concepts. They revealed that the conidium ontogeny (conidiogenesis) of Capnofrasera can be divided into mostly percurrent (pattern 1), sympodial with a sessile scar (pattern 2a), conicotruncate denticles (pattern 2b), and a new conidiogenous cell (pattern 2c). They redescribed C. dendryphioides based on additional specimens and added three new species, namely C. flatbergii S. Hughes, T. J. Atk. & Seifert, C. hedgeri S. Hughes, T. J. Atk. & Seifert, and C. pacifica S. Hughes, T. J. Atk. & Seifert, and one new combination Capnofrasera aucupariae (Jørst.) S. Hughes, T. J. Atk. & Seifert (≡ Coccosporium aucupariae Jørst.). Among these species, only C. aucupariae produced mature perithecia containing bitunicate and eight-spored asci, whereas “dictyoseptate elements” (presumed perithecial initials) were observed in C. dendryphioides, C. aucupariae, and C. hedgeri.

To characterize the newly collected Capnofrasera sp., mixed with the dominant sooty mould M. neesii on the evergreen conifer A. veitchii, we examined the phenotypic and genotypic characters of the species using an integrated approach to determine its taxonomic and phylogenetic placements (Sugiyama, 1998). Detailed illustrations and descriptions, along with a comparison to related taxa, are provided below.

2. Materials and methods

2.1. Sample collection

Five specimens were collected from the conifer A. veitchii (Japanese name: Shirabiso or Shirabe), which was planted in 1956 under the name “Dr. Shunsuke Kusano Memorial Tree” on the grounds of the Sugadaira Research Station, Mountain Science Center (formerly known as Sugadaira Montane Research Center), University of Tsukuba (alt. ca. 1,360 m; N 36°31′25″, E 138°20′50″), Sugadaira-Kogen, Ueda, Nagano Prefecture, Japan, in Oct 2019, Jun 2024, and Oct 2024 (Supplementary Fig. S1).

2.2. Microscopic observations and analyses

For microscopic observations and measurements, small portions of synnematous sooty mould colonies were mounted in Shear's mounting fluid (Gams et al., 1987) and examined using a Leitz Orthoplan phase contrast microscope (Wetzlar, Germany), and photographed using a differential interference contrast microscope (Olympus BX-51, Olympus, Tokyo, Japan) and a Nikon HFX photo system (Nikon, Tokyo, Japan). The measurements were taken primarily on microscope slide preparations in Shear's mounting fluid.

2.3. Isolation

Using a Skerman's micromanipulator (Toyorikoki Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan; Skerman, 1968), a single spore (phragmoconidium) formed on a synnema of a representative collection (voucher specimen: TNS F-108821) was isolated and cultivated on a potato dextrose agar (PDA) plate. Ten identical isolates were obtained, and among these two isolates FC-7674 and FC-9510 were selected as representative isolates for further study and deposited at the Japan Collection of Microorganisms (JCM), RIKEN BioResource Research Center as JCM 39660 and JCM 39661, both derived from each phragmoconidum of the specimen TNS F-108821) were selected as representative isolates and used for further study. Specimens have been maintained in the mycological herbarium of the National Museum of Nature and Science (TNS).

2.4.Molecular phylogeny

2.4.1. DNA extraction, polymerase chain reaction (PCR), and sequencing

To obtain the barcoding sequence and for DNA extraction, PCR, and sequencing, we followed the methods described by Hosaka and Castellano (2008). The PCR was performed as described by Itagaki and Hosoya (2022). The primer sets used were ITS5 and ITS4 for the internal transcribed spacer, including the 5.8S gene (ITS-5.8S; White et al., 1990), LR0R and LR5 for the large ribosomal subunit (LSU; Vilgalys & Hester, 1990), RPB2-6F and RPB2-R7 for the second largest subunit of RNA polymerase II (RPB2; Liu et al., 1999), and NS1 (White et al., 1990) and NS41 for the small ribosomal subunit (SSU; Hibbett, 1996). The resulting DNA voucher was deposited at the Center for Molecular Biodiversity Research, National Museum of Nature and Science (Tsukuba, Ibaraki, Japan) and is available for molecular phylogenetic research. The sequence was deposited in the International Nucleotide Sequence Database Collaboration (INSDC; https://www.insdc.org/) via the DNA Data Bank of Japan (DDBJ; https://www.ddbj.nig.ac.jp/index-e.html).

2.4.2. Molecular phylogenetic analyses

First, the LSU sequences from the two isolates were subjected to BLAST searches in GenBank (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/genbank/); the result indicated affinities with Capnodiales and Mycosphaerellales. Next, based on concatenated SSU-LSU-RPB2 genes, we conducted a preliminary phylogenetic analysis to estimate their approximate placement within Dothideomycetes. Referring to the broader taxon sampling by Hyde et al. (2013) and a recent study by Piątek et al. (2024), we selected and downloaded individual sequences from GenBank. Arthonia dispersa (UPSC 2583) and Dendrographa leucophaea f. minor (Sparrius 7999) were selected as outgroups (Supplementary Table S1). As the isolates were suggested to fall within the Capnodiales s. l. sensu Abdollahzadeh et al. (2020) (Supplementary Fig. S2), we further conducted molecular phylogenetic analyses of the SSU-LSU-RPB2 dataset (Supplementary Table S2), focusing on Capnodiales and Mycosphaerellales using taxon sampling described by Piątek et al. (2024) and Abdollahzadeh et al. (2020). However, Abdollahzadeh et al. (2020) had only authentic taxon samples for Capnodiaceae and used taxa of Dothideomycetes as outgroups.

Default settings were used to align the sequences for each dataset using MAFFT version 7.526 (Katoh et al., 2017). Portions that were ambiguously aligned were manually removed using MEGA7 (Kumar et al., 2016). Phylogenetic analyses were conducted using non-parametric standard bootstrap (BS) with RAxML-NG (Kozlov et al., 2019) for inferring maximum likelihood (ML) and Bayesian Inference (BI) using MrBayes version 3.2.7a (Ronquist et al., 2012) for reconstructing phylogenies. The optimum substitution models for the ML analysis were estimated using Modeltest-NG (Darriba et al., 2019) based on the corrected Akaike information criterion (Sugiura, 1978) for RAxML-NG and using Kakusan4 (Tanabe, 2011) based on the Bayesian information criterion (BIC; Schwarz, 1978) for MrBayes. ML analysis was performed using the following substitution models: TrNef+I+G4 for SSU, TIM3+I+G4 for LSU, GTR+I+G4 for the first codon position of RPB2, TVM+G4 for the second codon position of RPB2, and VTR+I+G4 for the third codon position of RPB2. Bootstrap probabilities were obtained using 1,000 BS replicates. Bayesian analysis with Bayesian posterior probabilities was performed using the following substitution models: K80+G4 for SSU and GTR+I+G4 for LSU, and the first and third codon position of RPB2, and SYM+G4 for the second codon position of RPB2. Two simultaneous and independent Metropolis-coupled Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) runs were performed for two million generations, with the tree sampled every 1,000 generation of the analyses. The convergence of the MCMC procedure was assessed based on the average standard deviation of the split frequencies (< 0.01) and effective sample size scores (all > 100) using MrBayes and Tracer version 1.7 (Rambaut et al., 2018), respectively. The first 25% of the trees were discarded as burn-in and the remaining trees were used to calculate the 50% majority-rule trees and determine the posterior probabilities for individual branches.

3. Results

3.1. Molecular phylogenetic placement of Capnofrasera isolates

The two isolates were identical in every corresponding sequence and were confirmed to be the same fungus. Therefore, only one sequence from JCM 39661 (FC-9510) was deposited for each genetic region (LC879954 for ITS-5.8S, LC881967 for LSU, LC881968 for SSU, and LC881969 for RPB2). ML and Bayesian phylogenetic analyses were conducted using an aligned sequence dataset of 994 nucleotides from SSU, 850 nucleotides from LSU, and 1163 nucleotides from RPB2. The alignment contained 72 taxa, including 31 (43%) SSU, 72 (100%) LSU, and 47 (65.2%) RPB2 (Supplementary Table S2). The RPB2 sequence of Neohortaea acidophila (CBS 113389; KT216521) was excluded from the analyses because the deposited sequence appears to be misidentified and is instead attributable to Cladosporium. No conflicts were observed among individual gene phylogenies. The combined dataset provided higher confidence values at the familial level than individual gene trees (data not shown). In the dataset (SSU-LSU-RPB2) comprising 2,957 aligned characters, concatenated using MEGA7 Sequence Data Explorer, 1,252 characters were variable while 1,616 were conserved. The ML tree with the highest log likelihood (−36,363.8597) generated using RAxML-NG is shown in Fig. 1. The topology did not show any significant conflicts with the previous study (Piątek et al., 2024), except that our analysis supported the monophyly of Readerielliopsidaceae, in agreement with the results of Abdollahzadeh et al. (2020). As shown in Fig. 1 and Supplementary Fig. S2, two isolates of Capnofrasera (FC-7674 and FC-9510), together with Neoantennariella phylicae (CBS 146163), Neoasbolisia phylicae (CBS 146168), and Fumiglobous pieridicola (UBC F23788), were placed in Neoantennariellaceae Abdollahz. & Crous (Abdollahzadeh et al., 2020) but not in Antennulariellaceae Woron. (Woronichin, 1925).

3.2. Taxonomy

The Capnofrasera from Sugadaira-Kogen in Nagano Prefecture was distinguishable from the known Capnofrasera species by the pattern of conidium ontogeny (conidiogenesis), the shape, dimensions, and septation of the phragmoconidia. This is the first Capnofrasera species to be characterized by morphology, culture, and DNA sequences. Therefore, a new species, the sixth species of Capnofrasera, is proposed.

Synnemata and plumose fascicles, occasionally scattered or solidified on subicula surface, blackish brown to black, height ≤ 1.5 mm, base thickness 150-200 μm (in dry conditions).

Conidiophores macronematous, usually lateral from hyphae on upper part of synnemata or terminal, straight main stalks, length ≤ 300 (-600) μm, width 7-11 μm, unbranched or irregularly branched, rarely opposite branches.

Conidiogenous cells, size mostly 10-20 × 5-7 μm, ampuliform to subconical or obyriform, terminating in flattened circular scar, width 3-4 μm. In terms of ecology, C. dendryphioides is associated with hyphae of metacapnodiaceaus or euantennariaceous sooty moulds on its host plants (Hughes, 2003), whereas C. aucupariae and C. pacifica are associated with synanamorphs of euantennariaceous and metacapnodiaceous sooty moulds, respectively (Hughes et al., 2020). A conspicuous sooty mould, C. abieticola proposed in this study as a new species, is frequently mixed with the dominant sooty mould M. neesii on the conifer A. veitchii in Sugadaira-Kogen, Central Japan. Abies abieticola on A. veitchii endemic to central and northern Honshu, Japan (Christian, 2021) is newly added to a map, highlighting the geographic distribution of Capnofrasera in the Northern Hemisphere.

Referring to the records to date, the biogeographic distribution patterns of Capnofrasera spp. are similar to those of euantennariaceous and metacapnodiaceous sooty molds mapped by Pirozynski and Weresub (1979). Regarding the biogeographic view of highly pleomorphic ascomycetes, they stated, “Most sooty-mould species in the Northern Hemisphere are clearly vicariant, and vicariance argues for a greater antiquity of the two families (Euantennariaceae and Metacapnodiaceae) (Cracraft, 1975).” Capnofrasera species may support this hypothesis.

4. Discussion

4.1. Justification for the identification of Capnofrasera specimens from Sugadaira-Kogen in central Japan

Considering the key to species and the synoptic table of Capnofrasera species provided by Hughes et al. (2020), C. abieticola resembles C. flatbergii on Ulmus sp. (Ulmaceae, Rosales) in Sweden and C. hedgeri on the living branches of Baccharis sp. (Asteraceae, Asterales) in Ecuador, but the two species occur on different host plants. Capnofrasera abieticola can be distinguished by its shorter and narrower phragmoconidia (2-7-septate, mostly 14-53 × 4.5-8 μm) than those of C. flatbergii (mostly 3-6-septate, 21.5-45 × 5.5-9 μm) and C. hedgeri (mostly 5-8-septate, 40-100 × 10-14.5 μm) in addition to the host preference and geographical distribution. Regarding the term “dictyoseptate elements” as perithecial initials (Hughes et al., 2020)―originally described as “multicellulate conidia” by Jørstad (1947) and subsequently as “dictyosporous bodies” by Hughes (1976)―our new species C. abieticola and C. flatbergii lack them, whereas C. hedgeri produces them with a diameter of 23.5-30 μm (Figs. 9 and 10 in Hughes et al., 2020; cf., Figs. 3 and 4 in this paper). In the future, development of new methods to induce the maturation of immature “dictyoseptate elements” would be beneficial.

4.2. Higher-level classification of Capnofrasera based on phenotypic and genotypic characters

As stated in the “Introduction” section in our review of the higher-level classification of Capnofrasera, Hughes (2003) suggested that C. dendryphioides might be an anamorph of Antennulariellaceae, based on the connection between Antennariella and Capnodendron synanamorphs and an Antennulariella teleomorph (Antennulariellaceae). Subsequent taxonomy followed this Hughes' placement, namely, Antennulariellaceae, Capnodiales, and Dothideomycetes (e.g., Chomnunti et al., 2014; Hyde et al., 2013; Kirk et al., 2008; Wijayawardene et al., 2012, 2017). However, Wijayawardene et al. (2020) treated Capnofrasera as a genus incertae sedis in Ascomycota. Hughes et al. (2020) did not address the taxonomy of Wijayawardene et al. (2020).

Using the molecular phylogeny and gene sequences of four nuclear loci, namely LSU, ITS, TEF, and RPB2, Abdollahzadeh et al. (2020) introduced the new family, Neoantennariellaceae Abdollahz. & Crous, in the order Capnodiales s. str., separated from Antennariellaceae. Neoantennariellaceae was typified by the new genus, Neoantennariella. Recently, Thiyagaraja et al. (2025) accepted Neoantennariellaceae as one of the seven families within Capnodiales. This family was defined by “Mycelium superficial or immersed, pale brown to brown, branched, smooth, thin-walled, septate hyphae, constricted at septa, a mucilaginous outer wall layer; pycnidia superficial or immersed, mostly globose or cylindrical, pale to dark brown, intercalary, lateral or terminal on erect hyphal branches. Ostiole absent or not well-developed, conidia released, irregular rupture; conidia hyaline, aseptate, ellipsoid to ovoid, minute guttules, smooth, thin-walled.” The sooty mould family Neoantennariellaceae (Abdollahzadeh et al., 2020) comprises three genera: Fumiglobus D.R. Reynolds & G.S. Gilbert (Reynolds & Gilbert, 2006), typified by Fumiglobus ficinus (Bat. et al.) D.R. Reynolds & G.S. Gilbert (Reynolds & Gilbert, 2006), Neoantennariella Abdollahz. & Crous (Abdollahzadeh et al., 2020), typified by N. phylicae Abdollahz. & Crous, and Neoasbolisia Abdollahz. & Crous (Abdollahzadeh et al., 2020), typified by N. phylicae Abdollahz. & Crous. Subsequently, Crous et al. (2021) added the monotypic genus Cippumomyces, typified by Cippumomyces mortalis Crous, Overton & Ricci within Neoantennariellaceae based on an ITS-LSU-TEF1-RPB2 phylogeny.

In contrast to the previous classifications at the family level, two isolates (JCM 39660 and JCM 39661) of C. abieticola were placed with N. phylicae (CBS 146163, CBS 146168), and F. pieridicola (UBC F23788) within Neoantennariellaceae and not Antennulariellaceae, and the node of Neoantennariellacae was strongly supported with 100% bootstrap (Fig. 1, Supplementary Fig. S2). Morphologically, however, all three genera, Fumiglobus, Neoantennariella, and Neoasbolisia are pycnidial coelomycetous, whereas C. abieticola is hyphomycetous (synnematous). As mentioned, the conidiomata, hyphal morphology, and conidium ontogeny of C. abieticola were consistent with those of other known Capnofrasera species. To further elucidate its higher-level classification, living cultures and DNA sequence data for the five known Capnofrasera species, including the type species of C. dendryphioides and related taxa (e.g., Antennulariellaceae), are needed. As these taxa are rarely encountered in the field, most species are rarely reported after their original descriptions. The scarcity of recent collections severely limits our ability to evaluate intra- and interspecific genetic diversity and possible host specificity patterns across various hosts and geographic regions. Therefore, sustained attempts to rediscover related taxa are required.
