A is for ___ : Narrative Medicine Framework for Mental Health Empowerment and Self-Authorship in Asian American Communities
Authors: Matthew J Yan, Hannah J Lee, Alexander J Yan, Victoria C Yan, Jason E Bahk, Justin A Chen, Nhi-Ha T Trinh, Warren Y K Ng, Francis G Lu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMental health stigma and underutilization of psychiatric care remain significant challenges in Asian American, Native Hawaiian, and Pacific Islander (AANHPI) communities, driven by cultural norms of silence, the model minority myth, and structural barriers to care. Narrative medicine, a clinical approach that utilizes storytelling, reflective writing, and deep listening, offers a promising approach to address these challenges by centering identity, voice, and meaning making in mental health engagement, processes that are foundational to psychological healing. In this Perspective, we describe “A is for ___”, a novel narrative medicine–based empowerment framework developed through A-Team Med, a community-based nonprofit organization. The framework is left intentionally blank to symbolize the tremendous diversity of the AANHPI experience and counter monolithic representations of Asian American identity. Applied in community settings, the framework invites participants to complete the prompt “A is for ___” by selecting a personally meaningful word beginning with the letter “A” that reflects the individual’s AANHPI lived experience. This open-ended structure serves as an accessible entry point for self-authorship, critical reflection, and dialogue about the AANHPI experience and mental health topics traditionally underdiscussed within this community. We situate this intervention within theories of narrative identity, cultural psychiatry, and the social determinants of mental health. We describe the implementation of this framework in a community workshop with over 50 AANHPI adolescents across multiple underserved community centers in Los Angeles. Author observations of participant experiences suggest that the exercise facilitated emotional expression, reduced stigma surrounding mental health conversations, and fostered validation and a sense of belonging. We further discuss practical applications of this framework in clinical encounters, community psychoeducation, and youth programs, as well as its potential for digital and telehealth adaptation. Background

How might we change Asian American, Native Hawaiian, and Pacific Islander (AANHPI) conversations and culture on mental health?

The AANHPI community has long been subjected to stereotypes of quiet perseverance, high achievement, and the “model minority myth [1–3].” While superficially positive, these labels erase individuality and obscure mental health struggles, contributing to stigma, invisibility, and underutilization of psychiatric care [4, 5]. For example, rates of depression, anxiety, eating disorders, and body dysmorphic disorder are rising among AANHPI populations, yet they remain among the least likely to access mental health services [6, 7]. Barriers to care often involve a complex interplay of cultural stigma, concerns about family reputation, and norms of silence around distress [7, 8]. Ameliorating this public health issue within cultural psychiatry requires addressing these invisible struggles through culturally sensitive strategies that invite individuals to amplify their voices toward self-authorship [9].

Narrative medicine, rooted in the recognition that stories are central to illness, healing, and identity, offers a possible path forward for re-authoring the AANHPI experience. When communities and individuals narrate their own lives, they move from being passive subjects to active authors of meaning [10]. This process of re-authoring is especially powerful for AANHPI communities, where collective histories often include the silent cultural history of migration, intergenerational trauma, and resilience [9]. Narrative medicine thus becomes not only a clinical tool but also a deeply sociocultural one: a means of reclaiming voice and visibility in psychiatry.

Introduction of the framework

A-Team Med is a nonprofit organization founded in 2022 in Los Angeles, California, dedicated to critically analyzing the AANHPI experience, advancing youth empowerment, and opening dialogue on traditionally understudied mental health topics. To this end, inspired by Eastern and Western traditions of thought, A-Team launched “A is for ___”, a narrative medicine empowerment device that invites participants to literally and metaphorically fill in the blank (Fig. 1). The deliberate emptiness of the blank is central: challenging monolithic narratives of the AANHPI experience by symbolizing the diverse spectrum of AANHPI identities and stories. By countering stereotypes of one homogenous AANHPI monolithic culture and experience, often reduced to phrases such as “All Asians look the same,” “A is for ___” creates both a symbolic and practical space where participants claim agency by selecting a word beginning with the letter “A” that resonates with their AANHPI identity, emotional state, or lived experience. This structure was deliberately chosen to balance openness with an anchored springboard: the fixed initial letter provides a shared scaffold, while the blank space preserves autonomy and cultural flexibility. For example, participants have chosen: A is for Awake. A is for Accountability. A is for Arise. A is for Audacity. Each word becomes an act of authorship: transforming silence into speech, stigma into solidarity, and marginalization into meaning. Methodologically, this narrative device is designed to be an easily accessible empowerment tool, both clinically and nonclinically, to support reflection and dialogue within the AANHPI community.

“A is for ___” narrative medicine template. Developed by A-Team Med, this visual prompt is used in community and clinical workshops to encourage storytelling and reflection. Participants are invited to fill in the blank with a word beginning with the letter A that resonates with their lived experience. The intentional emptiness of the blank symbolizes the diversity of Asian American, Native Hawaiian, and Pacific Islander (AANHPI) identities and emphasizes empowerment through authorship of one’s own narrative

For example, in practice, one participant reflected: “A is for Atypical. There is a common cultural preconception that Asian Americans are one monolith that often overlooks the diverse histories, cultures, languages, socioeconomic backgrounds, and immigrant stories that shape the Asian diaspora story.”

This user-friendly and accessible exercise provides an outlet to break the silence on the AANHPI experience, affirm identity, and generate dialogue about belonging and mental health. By intentionally leaving the ending blank, its symbolic power lies partly in paradoxically leveraging absence. In much of Western thought, meaning is often pursued through addition: more words, more detail, more certainty [11, 12]. In contrast, Eastern traditions often embrace subtraction, ambiguity, and silence as spaces for possibility [11, 12]. “A is for ___” combines both Western and Eastern traditions to uniquely characterize the AANHPI experience: leaving the blank unfilled is absence and an invitation, allowing individuals to step into authorship on their own terms.

As aptly described in the Buddhist Heart Sutra: “form is emptiness, emptiness is form.”12 Paradoxically, less can be more.

Clinical relevance

Clinically, this framework may have applications related to cultural psychiatry and community-based mental health engagement. Narrative prompts such as “A is for ___” can be incorporated into clinical or psychoeducational contexts as a reflective tool to elicit culturally meaningful expressions of identity, stress, and resilience. Practically, the device can be introduced verbally or in writing, used individually or in group settings, and positioned as an opening reflection, discussion anchor, or closing synthesis exercise depending on context, clinician judgment, and shared decision-making. For example, if a visit will discuss the patient’s attitude and reflections towards their cultural identity, clinicians can use the prompt as a 60-second pre-visit intake item or first-minute check-in, followed by a single optional follow-up question such as, “What makes that word fit today?” to guide agenda-setting and culturally grounded coping or referral discussions.

Importantly, participation does not require verbal disclosure. Individuals may engage privately or symbolically, which helps preserve psychological safety in settings where stigma or fear of judgment may remain high. Because the framework emphasizes self-definition rather than symptom identification, it is adaptable across age groups, linguistic backgrounds, and cultural contexts, including schools, community centers, workshops, and healthcare-adjacent spaces. In this way, “A is for ___” functions less as a prescriptive intervention and more as a portable narrative scaffold that can stand alone or be embedded within existing programs to support dialogue, reflection, and cultural identity-affirming engagement.

Social determinants of mental health

From a social determinants of mental health perspective, this narrative framework may help individuals navigate structural and cultural barriers that shape help-seeking behaviors within AANHPI communities, including language access, intergenerational expectations, and pressures associated with the model minority myth in educational and professional environments. By reframing mental health through the lens of narrative agency rather than pathology, “A is for ___” may contribute to destigmatization by normalizing self-reflection and dialogue in communities where silence, emotional restraint, and concealment are often default responses to distress (e.g., A is for Awareness).

Language access represents a critical structural barrier to mental health engagement for many AANHPI individuals, particularly those from immigrant or multilingual households. Although developed in English, the framework is inherently adaptable across languages because it allows participants to define their experiences using self-selected native words rather than predefined psychiatric terminology. In practice, participants may choose words from their primary languages or culturally meaningful expressions that approximate emotional states without requiring direct translation into Western diagnostic constructs. For example, some participants selected Vietnamese words beginning with “A,” such as ăn (“to eat”), a term that in Vietnamese culture is often used to convey care, love, and relational concern through action rather than verbal emotional expression (e.g., asking “Have you eaten?” as a proxy for well-being). In this context, A is for ăn became a way to communicate emotional care and connection in a culturally sensitive manner.

Similarly, in contexts shaped by achievement-oriented norms and model minority pressures, where expressions of vulnerability may be perceived as weakness or failure, the framework offers an indirect and culturally acceptable mechanism for naming stress, pressure, or emotional burden. For example, a participant from a Chinese cultural background selected “A is for āiyā” (哎呀), an exclamatory term commonly used to convey frustration, worry, fatigue, or emotional overload. This selection allowed the participant to articulate diffuse stress and pressure, often associated with academic demands, family responsibility, expectations of success, or immigrant stress, through a culturally familiar and emotionally resonant expression. In this way, the “A is for ___” framework enables participants to externalize internal states using language that feels natural, socially acceptable, and culturally legible.

Furthermore, at the community level, this narrative approach may function as an upstream intervention that addresses social determinants of mental health by fostering identity affirmation, belonging, and narrative coherence, factors closely linked to psychological well-being and resilience [13]. When embedded within educational settings, youth programs, or community organizations, “A is for ___” may serve as an accessible entry point for dialogue among youth and families who are hesitant to engage with formal mental health systems, thereby strengthening trust, expanding pathways to support, and evolving AANHPI mental health culture.

Mental health stigma reduction

From a mental health stigma perspective, conversations within AANHPI communities are often shaped by cultural norms that prioritize silence, emotional restraint, and maintenance of social harmony. These norms may contribute to the internalization of mental health struggles as personal weakness and discourage open disclosure or help-seeking. For example, the Chinese proverb “Keep your broken arm inside your sleeve” reflects the value of concealing personal difficulties, while the Japanese expression iwanu ga hana (言わぬが花; “not speaking is the flower”) suggests that silence may be preferable to disclosure. Within such contexts, individuals may feel pressure to suppress distress to meet perceived cultural and familial face-saving expectations.

Thus, within family and community settings, “A is for ___” may contribute to stigma reduction through several complementary mechanisms. By enabling individuals to complete the prompt on their own terms and articulate internal experiences using self-defined, culturally resonant language rather than purely clinical labels, the framework may reduce self-stigma by normalizing conversations around mental health and internal struggles (e.g., “A is for Ashamed,” “A is for Alone,” “A is for Afraid,” “A is for Avoidance”) and reduce perceived stigma by reframing mental health as part of a shared human experience rather than a marker of pathology. In group or community settings, the visibility and normalization of these narratives may also help challenge public stigma, including assumptions of incompetence, dangerousness, or inability to recover that are often associated with mental health conditions. While traditional AANHPI cultural contexts may emphasize emotional restraint and face-saving, “A is for ___” provides a culturally congruent entry point for expression, creating space for individuals to articulate and define their internal experiences in ways that feel safe, acceptable, and empowering.

Ultimately, through these processes of narrative expression, normalization, and reframing, “A is for ___” may function as an upstream, culturally sensitive mechanism to shift both individual- and community-level perceptions of mental health and lower barriers to help-seeking.

Community workshop activity reflections

We implemented the “A is for ___” framework in a facilitated workshop with over 50 AANHPI adolescents at the Chinatown Service Center in East Los Angeles, an underserved community organization serving predominantly immigrant and first-generation families. The workshop began with a lesson developed by the authors on Asian American Identity, including an overview of the model minority myth, bamboo ceiling, and leadership development. To help foster further critical reflection on the students’ Asian American experience, facilitators shared their “A” word and described the significance of their word choice for their lived AANHPI experience. Participants were then instructed to “Please fill in ‘A is for ___’ by selecting a word beginning with the letter ‘A’ that symbolizes your AANHPI experience or lived story.” (Supplemental Fig. 1). Following the exercise, students were invited, but not required, to share their reflections in small group discussions. Facilitators observed that the simplicity of the prompt appeared to reduce performance pressure and lower barriers to engagement on conversations about their cultural identity, particularly among participants who expressed discomfort with discussing traditional mental health topics. Students appeared to experience the activity as validating in ways that may be less common in traditional health or educational settings. In some cases, the exercise seemed to open peer conversations around mental health and family pressures. For others, it appeared to create space for expressions of pride in their cultural identity not previously shared. These observations are based on facilitator impressions and recollection rather than formal qualitative data collection or analysis. Illustrative examples of participant expressions included sentiments such as: “A is for Anxiety. I don’t usually say that word at home, but writing it here made it feel less overwhelming.” Another reflected, “A is for Achievement, but also pressure. This activity helped me realize how much stress I’ve normalized.” The activity appeared to provide, for some participants, a safe space and first opportunity to name internal experiences related to academic expectations, family responsibility, or cultural identity.

Digital and hybrid adaptations

Within A-Team Med’s initiatives, the symbolic act of choosing a word and declaring “A is for ___” became, for many participants, a declaration of existence: an assertion that their voices matter [14]. To support broader reach and sustainability, this narrative device is particularly well suited for digital and hybrid adaptation due to its low barrier to entry, brevity, and flexibility for personalized engagement.

As many AANHPI youth engage with identity formation, community building, and mental health dialogue through digital platforms and social media, the framework lends itself naturally to scalable online formats that extend beyond in-person workshops. In digital contexts, “A is for ___” could function as a reflective prompt integrated into social media campaigns, digital ambassador prompting, virtual workshops, brief testimonial series (e.g., an “A-word of the day”), or asynchronous community forums. For example, participants may be invited to share their “A” word through short written reflections, visual posts, mindfulness-oriented exercises, or audio recordings, allowing for participation that is self-paced and privacy-preserving. Importantly, the framework does not require public disclosure. Individuals may choose to engage privately, anonymously, or symbolically, which may reduce stigma and increase participation among youth who are hesitant to share their reflections or speak openly about mental health in face-to-face settings.

Hybrid implementations may also be feasible in telehealth-adjacent or educational settings, where the prompt can be used as a pre-session reflection, intake form exercise, virtual discussion anchor, or post-session synthesis activity. In these formats, “A is for ___” serves as a narrative bridge between the individual’s personal experience and facilitated dialogue, enabling clinicians, educators, or community leaders to center participant voice and gain contextual insight into participants’ lived experiences.

Additionally, digital scalability allows this framework to extend broadly across Asian diasporas globally. Many Asian cultures share traditions of silence, face-saving, and collective identity that complicate psychiatric engagement [15]. Digital media offers an opportunity to invite reflection and connection across geographic boundaries, where leaving a blank space for self-definition allows youth and families to articulate inner experiences without contradicting cultural values [13, 14]. Thus, “A is for ___” may offer a cross-cultural, digitally adaptable narrative framework for listening to, and learning from, Asian narratives worldwide.

Limitations

This Perspective has important limitations to consider. First, as a conceptual and narrative-based framework, the approach has not yet been systematically evaluated using formal qualitative or quantitative methodologies. As such, its impact on mental health outcomes, help-seeking behaviors, and long-term engagement remains to be empirically established. Second, while the framework is designed to be flexible and culturally adaptable across AANHPI communities, its implementation may vary across different clinical, community, and cultural contexts, which may influence its acceptability and effectiveness. Third, the framework was initially developed in an English-language context, and although it is designed to allow for multilingual and culturally specific expressions (e.g., as illustrated by examples using Vietnamese and Chinese terms above), there may be practical challenges in standardizing or adapting the prompt across languages with different linguistic structures or alphabets. Nevertheless, the open-ended and participant-driven nature of the framework is intended to offer a low-barrier approach to initiating conversations around the AANHPI experience, support cultural and linguistic flexibility, and empower individuals to engage using personally meaningful words or expressions within their own language contexts. Future research is needed to formally evaluate the effectiveness, scalability, and cross-cultural applicability of this narrative approach across diverse populations and settings.

Conclusion

Ultimately, “A is for ___” extends beyond literary device and is a call to action: an invitation within cultural psychiatry to make room for diverse narratives that uniquely define the AANHPI lived experience. The blank is an opening for unlimited possibility. By giving AANHPI communities the tools for self-authorship, we can reduce cultural stigmas and practice an equitable psychiatry that listens, honors, and heals.

How will you fill in the blank?
