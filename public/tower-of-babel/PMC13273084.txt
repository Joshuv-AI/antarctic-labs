The synergistic pattern of metabolic-inflammatory changes jointly predicts cardiovascular risk in Chinese middle-aged and older adults: a prospective cohort study
Authors: Xiangliong Zhao, Xiaonan Ning, Zhihui Lu, Bao Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The interplay between metabolic dysregulation and chronic inflammation is pivotal in the development of cardiovascular disease. However, the joint prognostic value of their dynamic trajectories remains unclear. Current risk assessment predominantly relies on single-timepoint, static measurements, which fail to capture their temporal evolution and synergistic interactions. This study aimed to investigate the synergistic pattern derived from the dynamic trajectories of metabolism and inflammation for predicting major adverse cardiovascular events(MACE). This prospective cohort study included 4,968 Chinese middle-aged and older adults free of baseline cardiovascular disease from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS). The triglyceride-glucose-body mass index and high-sensitivity C-reactive protein were used to define metabolic and inflammatory status, respectively. Based on measurements at two time points, participants were classified as being at “high risk” if they exhibited sustained high levels or a transition from low to high in either metabolic or inflammatory status. By cross-classifying metabolic and inflammatory high-risk status, four mutually exclusive synergistic patterns were derived: low-low (reference), high-high, metabolism-dominant (high metabolic risk only), and inflammation-dominant (high inflammatory risk only). The primary outcome was incident MACE (a composite of cardiovascular death and self-reported physician-diagnosed heart attack, coronary heart disease, angina, congestive heart failure, other heart problems, or stroke). Cox proportional hazards models were used to calculate hazard ratios for each pattern compared with the low-low group. The incremental predictive value was assessed using the C-statistic, net reclassification improvement, and integrated discrimination improvement. Subgroup and sensitivity analyses were performed. Over a median follow-up of 5.25 years, 1,087 MACE events were documented. Compared with the low-low group, the metabolism-dominant group exhibited the highest risk of MACE (fully adjusted HR = 1.39, 95% CI: 1.15–1.68, p < 0.001), followed by the high-high group (HR = 1.31, 95% CI: 1.10–1.57, p = 0.002). The inflammation-dominant group was not significantly associated with MACE risk (HR = 1.15, 95% CI: 0.95–1.39, p = 0.146). In subgroup analyses, the association for the high-high pattern appeared stronger in participants aged < 60 years (HR = 1.54, 95% CI:1.16–2.05) compared with those aged ≥ 60 years (HR = 1.23, 95% CI: 0.91–1.66), although no significant interaction was detected (P for interaction = 0.204). Adding the synergistic patterns to a model containing traditional risk factors yielded a modest but significant improvement in risk prediction (ΔC-index = 0.006, P = 0.0465; NRI = 0.079, P = 0.031; IDI = 0.0024, P = 0.045). Sensitivity analyses using a stricter definition of high inflammatory status (≥ 75th percentile per wave) confirmed the robustness of the findings (HR for high-high = 1.33, 95% CI: 1.10–1.61, p = 0.003). This study demonstrates that the joint patterns of metabolic and inflammatory changes—defined using two repeated measurements—provide independent prognostic information for cardiovascular events beyond traditional risk factors. Among the four synergistic patterns identified, the metabolism-dominant group exhibited the highest MACE risk, suggesting that, within this cohort and under our measurement framework, metabolic dysregulation may be a more prominent predictor of cardiovascular risk than inflammation alone. The lack of significant association for the inflammation-dominant pattern alone may indicate that low-grade inflammation confers excess risk primarily when accompanied by adverse metabolic status; however, this interpretation should be made cautiously given potential assay-related heterogeneity and limited power for some contrasts.

Background

Cardiovascular disease (CVD) is the leading cause of death among the elderly population globally and in China, severely compromising quality of life and imposing a substantial societal burden1–3. Atherosclerosis (AS) serves as the primary pathological basis of CVD. The deposition of low -density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), particularly in its oxidized form (ox-LDL), within the arterial wall is a key initiating factor, triggering endothelial injury and inflammatory responses that ultimately lead to major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE)4,5. While lowering LDL-C levels has been proven to significantly reduce cardiovascular events, a residual risk of MACE persists even among patients who achieve lipid-lowering targets6. This underscores the necessity to explore the mechanisms underlying this residual risk to identify novel therapeutic targets. The synergistic interplay between metabolic abnormalities (e.g., insulin resistance, obesity, dyslipidemia) and chronic inflammation is increasingly recognized as a core microenvironmental mechanism driving residual atherosclerotic risk7.

In recent years, the limitations of relying on single biomarkers have prompted the development of composite indices that integrate multiple pathways. The triglyceride -glucose -body mass index (TyG-BMI), a robust metric combining markers of insulin resistance and obesity, has demonstrated superior performance for cardiovascular risk prediction and stratified management8,9. Similarly, high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hs-CRP), a gold-standard systemic inflammation marker, is well-established in its association with CVD10. However, most existing indices are based on static, single-timepoint measurements, which fail to capture the dynamic evolution of metabolic and inflammatory states over time. Current research often examines the trajectories of these factors in isolation or merges them into simple static composites (e.g., the C-reactive protein -triglyceride -glucose index, CTI)11, lacking a systematic investigation into their dynamic synergistic patterns12. For instance, the clinical outcomes may differ substantially between a pattern of “worsening metabolism with improving inflammation” and one of “synchronized worsening of both.” Therefore, systematically constructing synergistic patterns from the dynamic trajectories of metabolism and inflammation is crucial for revealing their combined risk at a systemic level, advancing the understanding of residual CVD risk mechanisms, and enabling more precise and earlier intervention.

To address these gaps, we used the nationwide CHARLS prospective cohort to evaluate the joint predictive value of synergistic metabolic–inflammatory change patterns for cardiovascular risk in Chinese middle-aged and older adults. Conceptually, by cross-integrating changes in metabolic and inflammatory status, we distinguish risk driven by metabolic deterioration, persistent inflammation, or their co-evolution, moving beyond static or parallel assessments to a dynamic, synergistic framework. Methodologically, using MACE as the endpoint, we applied survival analysis to quantify the long-term prognostic impact of these patterns, providing greater clinical relevance than single-timepoint or biomarker-only studies. Based on two time-point measurements of metabolic (TyG-BMI) and inflammatory (hs-CRP) status, we classified participants into four mutually exclusive synergistic patterns according to their high-risk status (defined as sustained high levels or a transition from low to high): low-low (reference), high-high, metabolism-dominant (high metabolic risk only), and inflammation-dominant (high inflammatory risk only). Our specific objectives were to: (1) investigate the associations between these four patterns and the risk of major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE); (2) evaluate the incremental predictive value of these patterns beyond traditional cardiovascular risk factors; and (3) explore potential effect modification by key population characteristics, such as age and sex, to inform targeted prevention strategies. By employing multivariable Cox proportional hazards models, interaction analyses, and model performance assessments (C-statistic, NRI, IDI), this study aims to provide critical epidemiological evidence for precise risk stratification and early intervention in CVD.

Methods

Study design and participants

This study utilized data from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS), an ongoing nationwide cohort study designed to collect high -quality microdata representative of Chinese households and individuals aged 45 years and older. The primary aim of CHARLS is to analyze issues related to population aging and to facilitate interdisciplinary research on aging. The CHARLS questionnaire covers a broad range of domains, including individual demographic information, family structure and economic support, health status, physical measurements, healthcare utilization and insurance, employment, retirement and pensions, income, consumption, assets, and community characteristics. The baseline national survey was conducted in 2011 (Wave 1), using a random sampling strategy that covered over 10,000 households across 150 counties and 450 villages in China. Follow-up surveys were conducted in 2013 (Wave 2), 2015 (Wave 3), 2018 (Wave 4), and 2020 (Wave 5). Blood samples were collected during the baseline (Wave 1) and Wave 3 surveys. Detailed data collection methods for CHARLS have been described previously13. The study protocol was approved by the Biomedical Ethics Review Committee of Peking University (IRB00001052-11015), and all participants provided written informed consent.

Fasting blood glucose, triglycerides, low -density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), total cholesterol, hs-CRP, and CRP levels were obtained from blood samples collected during Waves 1 and 3. Participants were excluded based on the criteria detailed in Fig. 1. The final analytical cohort comprised 4968 participants.

Definition of TyG-BMI changes, inflammatory changes, and metabolic-inflammatory synergy patterns

Definition of metabolic changes

Definition of metabolic changes: Metabolic status was assessed using TyG-BMI at Wave 1 and Wave 3. We used wave-specific percentile thresholds to define metabolic high- vs. low-risk status (primary: P50; sensitivity: P75). Using P50, metabolic high-risk was defined as sustained high (W1 TyG-BMI ≥ P50 and W3 TyG-BMI ≥ P50) or low-to-high (W1 TyG-BMI < P50 and W3 TyG-BMI ≥ P50), and metabolic low-risk as sustained low (W1 TyG-BMI < P50 and W3 TyG-BMI < P50) or high-to-low (W1 TyG-BMI ≥ P50 and W3 TyG-BMI < P50). Absolute cutoffs were: P50—198.47 (W1) and 204.20 (W3); P75—227.17 (W1) and 232.43 (W3) (Table 1).

Definition of inflammatory changes

Definition of inflammatory changes: Inflammatory status was assessed using CRP at Wave 1 and hs-CRP at Wave 3. We used wave-specific percentile thresholds to define inflammatory high- vs. low-risk status (primary: P50; sensitivity: P75). Using P50, inflammatory high-risk was defined as sustained high (W1 CRP ≥ P50 and W3 hs-CRP ≥ P50) or low-to-high (W1 CRP < P50 and W3 hs-CRP ≥ P50), and inflammatory low-risk as sustained low (W1 CRP < P50 and W3 hs-CRP < P50) or high-to-low (W1 CRP ≥ P50 and W3 hs-CRP < P50). Absolute cutoffs were: P50—0.97 mg/L (W1 CRP) and 1.40 mg/L (W3 hs-CRP); P75—2.01 mg/L (W1 CRP) and 2.60 mg/L (W3 hs-CRP) (Table 1).

Definition of metabolic-inflammatory synergy patterns

By cross-classifying metabolic high-risk status and inflammatory high-risk status, we derived four mutually exclusive synergistic patterns: Low-low group: metabolic low-risk and inflammatory low-risk (reference group).High-high group: metabolic high-risk and inflammatory high-risk. Metabolism-dominant group: metabolic high-risk and inflammatory low-risk. Inflammation-dominant group: metabolic low-risk and inflammatory high-risk.

Ascertainment of MACE

Following previous studies13,14, the primary endpoint was defined as the first occurrence of MACE during follow-up or reaching the end of follow-up. Non-fatal MACE was ascertained through standardized questionnaire items: “Has a doctor ever told you that you have been diagnosed with a heart attack, coronary heart disease, angina, congestive heart failure, or other heart problems?” and “Have you ever been diagnosed with a stroke?” For fatal MACE, a questionnaire was administered to family members: “Did the patient die from heart disease?” and “Did the patient die from a stroke?” Survival time was calculated as follows: for participants with non-fatal MACE, the event time was defined as the time from baseline (June 1, 2015) to the date of the first event reported in the survey; for fatal MACE, the specific date of death was used; and for participants without events, survival time was calculated from baseline to the date of death from any cause, loss to follow-up, or the study cutoff date (September 1, 2020). We set baseline at Wave 3 (June 1, 2015) because exposure change was defined using Wave 1 and Wave 3; follow-up started after Wave 3. Survival time was expressed in years. Examples from the study cohort include 0 (event occurred at baseline), 3.25 years, 3.78 years, 4.50 years, and 5.25 years, which correspond to the cutoff dates of the respective follow-up waves. This statistical approach has been employed in previous studies11.

Assessment of covariates

Baseline data were collected by trained interviewers using structured questionnaires and included: (1) Demographic and lifestyle data: sex, age, residence, education level, marital status, smoking status, and alcohol consumption. (2) Physical measurements: height, weight, body mass index (BMI), systolic blood pressure (SBP), and diastolic blood pressure (DBP). (3) Disease and medication history: information on existing conditions such as heart disease, hypertension, diabetes, and dyslipidemia, as well as the use of medication for these conditions. (4) Laboratory data: Fasting plasma glucose (FPG), total cholesterol (TC), triglycerides (TG), HDL-C, LDL-C, and glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c).

Handling of missing variables

Missing data were handled using multiple imputation by chained equations (MICE) under a missing-at-random assumption. Fifty imputed datasets were generated. The imputation model included all exposures, outcomes, and covariates (demographics, lifestyle factors, clinical measurements, disease histories, metabolic-inflammatory change patterns, event status, and time-to-event). Categorical variables were imputed using logistic regression and continuous variables using predictive mean matching. Estimates were pooled using Rubin’s rules.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using R software version 4.5.1. A two-sided P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Primary analyses were performed across 50 imputed datasets generated by MICE and pooled using Rubin’s rules. Although some variables in the CHARLS database had missing values, prior analyses suggested that the missing data were likely missing at random15,16. Among the 4968 participants included in the final analysis, the maximum missing rate for any variable was only 1.85%.

Baseline characteristics were described according to the core exposure variable, the metabolic-inflammatory synergy pattern. Continuous variables conforming to a normal distribution are presented as mean ± standard deviation, non- normally distributed variables as median (interquartile range), and categorical variables as frequency (percentage). Group comparisons were performed using analysis of variance (ANOVA), Kruskal -Wallis test, or chi-square test, as appropriate.

The cumulative incidence of MACE across the four synergy patterns was estimated using the Kaplan-Meier method, and differences in survival curves were assessed with the log-rank test. Multivariable Cox proportional hazards regression models were employed to quantify the association between synergy patterns and MACE risk, with the low-low group serving as the reference. Three models were constructed: Model 1 (demographic model) adjusted for age and sex; Model 2 (behavioral model) additionally adjusted for smoking, alcohol consumption, education level, and residence; Model 3 (fully adjusted model) further adjusted for histories of hypertension, diabetes, and dyslipidemia. The proportional hazards assumption was tested using Schoenfeld residuals and was satisfied.

The incremental predictive value of the synergy patterns was evaluated by comparing a “base model” containing only traditional risk factors (age, sex, smoking, alcohol consumption, education, residence, hypertension, diabetes, dyslipidemia) with an “extended model” that added the four-group pattern. Model discrimination was assessed using Harrell’s C-statistic, and the difference between models was tested using the roorrp package. The continuous Net Reclassification Improvement (NRI) and Integrated Discrimination Improvement (IDI) were calculated to quantify the improvement in risk reclassification provided by the new model.

To assess the consistency of the association and explore potential effect modification, subgroup analyses were performed stratified by age (< 60 years vs. ≥60 years), sex, hypertension, diabetes, BMI (< 25 kg/m² vs. ≥25 kg/m²), and smoking status. Within each subgroup, fully adjusted Cox models were fitted to estimate the hazard ratios for the high-high pattern compared with the low-low pattern. Interaction effects were formally tested by including product terms (high-high pattern × subgroup variable) in the fully adjusted Cox model, and their significance was assessed using likelihood ratio tests.

To verify the robustness of the main findings, the following sensitivity analyses were conducted: (1) Minimal adjustment model (age and sex only); (2) Moderate adjustment model (demographic and behavioral factors); (3) Exclusion of MACE events occurring within the first 2 years of follow-up to assess potential reverse causality; (4) Restricting follow-up to 3 years; (5) Restricting follow-up to 5 years; (6) Excluding participants with diabetes at baseline; (7) Using a more stringent cutoff for defining inflammatory high-risk (75th percentile of both CRP and hs-CRP), as detailed in the sensitivity analysis section.

Results

Characteristics of the study population

A total of 4968 participants were included in the final analysis, with a mean age of 58.5 years (SD 8.7), and 53% were female. According to the metabolic-inflammatory change patterns, participants were classified into four groups: low-low (n = 1495, 30.1%), high-high (n = 1586, 31.9%), metabolism-dominant (n = 898, 18.1%), and inflammation-dominant (n = 989, 19.9%).

Baseline characteristics differed significantly across the four groups (all P < 0.001; Table 2). Participants in the high-high and metabolism-dominant groups exhibited a more adverse cardiometabolic profile, characterized by higher BMI, blood pressure, total cholesterol, and triglycerides, as well as lower HDL cholesterol levels, compared with those in the low-low group. Notably, the inflammation-dominant group had the oldest mean age (61.2 years) and the highest proportion of males (58%), current smokers (41%), and current drinkers (37%), while their metabolic indicators (e.g., BMI, triglycerides, HDL cholesterol) were relatively favorable and comparable to the low-low group. In contrast, the metabolism-dominant group had the youngest mean age (56.1 years) and the highest proportion of females (62%) and never smokers (71%).Regarding disease history, the prevalence of hypertension, diabetes, and dyslipidemia was highest in the high-high group (34%, 8.7%, and 14%, respectively), followed by the metabolism-dominant group, whereas the low-low and inflammation-dominant groups had the lowest prevalences of these conditions. These results suggest that the four metabolic-inflammatory change patterns capture distinct phenotypic profiles with respect to demographic, lifestyle, and clinical characteristics.

Association between metabolic-inflammatory change patterns and MACE risk

During a median follow-up of 5.25 years, a total of 1087 MACE events were documented. Kaplan-Meier survival analysis revealed significant differences in MACE-free survival across the four metabolic-inflammatory change patterns (log-rank P < 0.001; Fig. 2). The low-low group exhibited the highest survival probability (approximately 84–85%), followed by the inflammation-dominant group (approximately 82%), while the metabolism-dominant and high-high groups showed the lowest survival probabilities (approximately 78–79%), with their curves nearly overlapping throughout follow-up. Multivariable Cox proportional hazards regression analyses were performed to quantify these associations (Table 3). In the demographic-adjusted model (Model 1), compared with the low-low reference group, all three patterns were associated with significantly increased MACE risk: high-high (HR = 1.65, 95% CI: 1.41–1.93, P < 0.001), metabolism-dominant (HR = 1.55, 95% CI: 1.29–1.85, P < 0.001), and inflammation-dominant (HR = 1.21, 95% CI: 1.00–1.46, P = 0.047). After additional adjustment for behavioral factors (Model 2), the associations remained similar for high-high (HR = 1.68, 95% CI: 1.43–1.96, P < 0.001) and metabolism-dominant patterns (HR = 1.61, 95% CI: 1.34–1.93, P < 0.001), while the inflammation-dominant pattern was no longer statistically significant (HR = 1.17, 95% CI: 0.97–1.41, P = 0.108).In the fully adjusted model (Model 3), accounting for age, sex, smoking, alcohol consumption, education, residence, hypertension, diabetes, and dyslipidemia, the metabolism-dominant pattern conferred the highest risk (HR = 1.39, 95% CI: 1.15–1.68, P < 0.001), followed closely by the high-high pattern (HR = 1.31, 95% CI: 1.10–1.57, P = 0.002). Notably, the inflammation-dominant pattern was not significantly associated with MACE risk after full adjustment (HR = 1.15, 95% CI: 0.95–1.39, P = 0.146).These findings demonstrate a clear gradient of cardiovascular risk across the four metabolic-inflammatory change patterns. The nearly overlapping survival curves and comparable hazard ratios between the metabolism-dominant and high-high groups suggest that metabolic dysregulation may be the dominant driver of cardiovascular risk17, whereas elevated inflammation alone, in the absence of metabolic abnormalities, confers a more modest and statistically non-significant risk after comprehensive adjustment.

Incremental predictive value of metabolic-inflammatory change patterns

The addition of metabolic-inflammatory change patterns to traditional cardiovascular risk factors significantly improved risk prediction for MACE (Table 4). Compared with the base model (including age, sex, smoking, alcohol consumption, education, residence, hypertension, diabetes, and dyslipidemia), the extended model incorporating the four-group pattern demonstrated better model fit, as indicated by a decrease in the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) from 18,033.56 to 18,021.65 (ΔAIC = – 11.91). The likelihood ratio test confirmed that the improvement was statistically significant (χ² = 17.90, df = 3, P = 0.0005), and the explained variance (Nagelkerke R²) increased marginally from 2.23 to 2.58%.

To evaluate potential reverse causality, we excluded MACE events occurring within the first 2 years of follow-up; the association remained significant (HR = 1.40, 95% CI: 1.11–1.78, P = 0.005). The consistent findings under stricter inflammatory definitions further validate the independent prognostic value of metabolic-inflammatory dysregulation beyond traditional risk factors.

Discussion

This study investigated the association between synergistic patterns derived from the dynamic changes of metabolism (TyG-BMI index) and inflammation (hs-CRP/CRP) and the risk of major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE) in Chinese middle-aged and older adults. Our findings demonstrate that these synergistic patterns are independent risk factors for MACE, with the metabolism-dominant pattern conferring the highest risk, followed closely by the high-high pattern. The results underscore the dominant role of metabolic dysregulation in risk prediction, whereas isolated inflammatory elevation, in the absence of metabolic abnormalities, showed limited prognostic value after comprehensive adjustment. Although subgroup analyses revealed numerically stronger associations in individuals aged < 60 years, females, and current smokers, formal interaction tests did not reach statistical significance, suggesting that the predictive value of metabolic-inflammatory patterns is broadly consistent across different population strata. Incorporating these synergistic patterns into models based on traditional cardiovascular risk factors significantly enhanced the predictive value for MACE, offering a novel perspective for precise risk stratification and early intervention.

This study classified metabolic–inflammatory change into four mutually exclusive patterns (low–low, high–high, metabolism-dominant, and inflammation-dominant). The metabolism-dominant and inflammation-dominant patterns may reflect distinct pathogenic pathways and therefore imply different prevention priorities. A metabolism-dominant pattern suggests that cardiovascular risk is primarily driven by cardiometabolic burden related to insulin resistance and adiposity, as captured by TyG-BMI; thus, even in the absence of persistently elevated inflammation, early identification and intensive metabolic management—such as weight control, physical activity interventions, and optimized glycemic and lipid management—may be warranted. By contrast, an inflammation-dominant pattern indicates persistent or worsening low-grade inflammation despite relatively stable metabolic status; clinically, this may prompt evaluation for upstream inflammatory sources or comorbidities (e.g., chronic infection, periodontal disease, autoimmune conditions, and smoking-related inflammation), alongside optimization of lifestyle and traditional risk-factor control. Importantly, even if the inflammation-dominant pattern shows a weaker or non-significant association with MACE in our analyses, this should not be interpreted as evidence that inflammation is unimportant; rather, it may reflect assay-related measurement differences, a smaller effect size, or a context in which metabolic dysregulation constitutes a more proximal upstream driver of risk in this cohort.

Both inflammation and insulin resistance have been established as independent risk factors for cardiovascular disease(CVD), jointly contributing to its pathogenesis18,19.From a pathophysiological perspective, C-reactive protein promotes atherogenesis through mechanisms including the induction of endothelial dysfunction, facilitation of oxidized lipoprotein accumulation, and enhancement of leukocyte infiltration20.The triglyceride -glucose index, reflecting the degree of insulin resistance, impairs glucose metabolism and vascular endothelial function via oxidative stress and lipotoxicity21. Previous studies have predominantly examined the relationship of inflammation or insulin resistance with CVD in isolation, consistently reporting positive associations22–24. In recent years, growing emphasis has been placed on the importance of a combined assessment of inflammation and metabolic abnormalities for the primary prevention of CVD25,26, with recommendations to integrate the triglyceride-glucose index and inflammatory biomarkers to optimize risk stratification11,27.

The “synergistic patterns of metabolic-inflammatory changes” proposed in our study provide significant incremental information to existing risk prediction models. Compared to recently proposed static composite indices like the C-reactive protein-triglyceride-glucose index (CTI)28, our approach enables the dynamic assessment of biomarker changes over two time points. By defining high-risk status based on either sustained elevation or a transition from low to high, our method captures both chronic exposure and deteriorating trends—information that is entirely missed in single-timepoint assessments. Although our four-group classification combines individuals with sustained high and progressive worsening patterns within the same high-risk category, this design ensures sufficient statistical power while still capturing the essential distinction between metabolically and inflammatory driven risk. Furthermore, by cross-classifying metabolic and inflammatory high-risk status separately, our four-group classification can differentiate whether the primary source of risk stems from metabolic dysregulation or inflammatory activation, thereby offering more precise guidance for clinical intervention than single biomarkers or composite indices alone. Compared to cumulative exposure indices, this change-based model moves beyond the simple “dose-response” framework by incorporating the pattern of change, which aligns with and advances the goals of precision medicine practice.

Our results indicate that metabolic abnormalities may play a more prominent role in cardiovascular risk prediction in this cohort. The metabolism-dominant pattern (high metabolic risk with low inflammatory risk) was associated with the highest MACE risk (HR = 1.39), followed closely by the high-high pattern (HR = 1.31). In contrast, the inflammation-dominant pattern (low metabolic risk with high inflammatory risk) did not show a statistically significant association after full adjustment (HR = 1.15, P = 0.146). Together, these findings suggest that, under our operational definitions and measurement framework, metabolic dysregulation may be a stronger predictor of MACE than inflammation alone, and that low-grade inflammation may confer excess risk primarily when accompanied by adverse metabolic status. However, this interpretation should be made cautiously given potential assay-related heterogeneity across waves (e.g., CRP vs. hs-CRP) and limited power for certain contrasts, which may attenuate inflammation-only associations. From a clinical perspective, the observed pattern supports prioritizing comprehensive cardiometabolic risk management (e.g., improving insulin resistance and weight control) while concurrently addressing potential inflammatory contributors, rather than implying that anti-inflammatory strategies are ineffective.

The numerically stronger associations observed in certain subgroups merit discussion, although they should be interpreted with caution given the non-significant interaction tests. The point estimates suggested potentially enhanced effects in participants aged < 60 years (HR = 1.54 vs. 1.23 in ≥ 60 years), females (HR = 1.48 vs. 1.28 in males), and current smokers (HR = 1.60 vs. 1.23 in non-smokers). Several mechanisms may explain these patterns, though confirmation requires further investigation. First, the age-related interpretation may be as follows: (1) younger individuals typically have fewer non-modifiable risk factors (e.g., advanced age and long-standing target-organ damage), which allows modifiable factors such as metabolic and inflammatory dysregulation to account for a larger proportion of their attributable risk3,18,29; (2) adults who already exhibit an early “double-high” metabolic–inflammatory profile at a younger age may have greater underlying genetic susceptibility and/or prolonged exposure to highly unhealthy lifestyles, which together could substantially increase their subsequent risk of MACE; and (3) older individuals with a sustained double-high profile may have been more likely to experience cardiovascular events or death before Wave 3, or to be excluded due to prevalent disease at baseline, resulting in relatively fewer older double-high participants in the analytic sample. Second, established traditional risk factors in older adults, males, and individuals with hypertension or diabetes may attenuate the relative contribution of metabolic-inflammatory dysregulation. Third, smoking may synergistically interact with metabolic-inflammatory abnormalities to amplify cardiovascular risk. However, the absence of statistically significant interactions (all P > 0.05) indicates that the association between the high-high pattern and MACE risk was generally consistent across subgroups. This consistency supports the generalizability of our main findings, suggesting that the prognostic value of metabolic-inflammatory patterns applies broadly across diverse population strata.

Strengths and limitations

The key strengths of this study include the use of a large-scale, nationally representative cohort, a prospective design, a relatively long follow-up period (median 5.25 years), and comprehensive statistical validation through multiple sensitivity and subgroup analyses. By constructing dynamic changes based on two time-point measurements and cross-classifying metabolic and inflammatory high-risk status, our four-group classification captures the joint effects of metabolic-inflammatory dysregulation while maintaining mutually exclusive, clinically interpretable categories with sufficient sample sizes in each group.

This study also has several limitations. First, due to its observational design, causal inferences regarding the relationships between metabolic-inflammatory changes and cardiovascular events cannot be established, despite rigorous multivariable adjustments. Second, our change-pattern definitions were based on only two time points. Although this captures overall direction, it cannot characterize non-linear trajectories or the timing and rate of change, which may carry additional prognostic information. Thus, our classification, while clinically interpretable, likely represents a conservative approximation of the underlying dynamics. Future studies with repeated serial measurements are needed to validate and extend these findings. Third, the assessment of inflammation was limited to CRP/hs-CRP levels and did not encompass other important inflammatory pathways (e.g., IL-6, TNF-α)30–32, which may provide a more comprehensive picture of inflammatory status. In addition, CRP at Wave 1 and hs-CRP at Wave 3 were measured using different assays; although we mitigated this by within-wave percentile standardization and sensitivity analyses, residual measurement error cannot be fully excluded. Fourth, the study population was restricted to Chinese middle-aged and older adults; therefore, the generalizability of our findings to other ethnicities or younger populations requires further validation. Finally, the relatively modest improvements in C-index, NRI, and IDI suggest that while the metabolic-inflammatory change patterns provide statistically significant incremental predictive value, their clinical utility as a standalone screening tool may be limited, and they should be considered as complementary to traditional risk assessment.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this analysis of the CHARLS database demonstrates that synergistic patterns of metabolic-inflammatory changes—particularly the metabolism-dominant and high-high patterns—serve as independent predictors of MACE in Chinese middle-aged and older adults. The metabolism-dominant pattern conferred the highest risk, underscoring the may play a more prominent role in prediction in this cohort of metabolic dysregulation in cardiovascular risk prediction. Although interaction tests were not statistically significant, the associations appeared more pronounced in younger adults (< 60 years), females, and current smokers, suggesting these populations may particularly benefit from metabolic-inflammatory monitoring. This four-group classification provides modest but significant incremental predictive value beyond traditional risk factors, advocating for a shift from static, single-timepoint assessments toward dynamic evaluation of risk factor changes. These findings offer new evidence for implementing precision prevention strategies for cardiovascular disease.
