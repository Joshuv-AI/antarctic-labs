On the Peril of (Even a Little) Nonstationarity in Satisficing Regret Minimization
Yixuan Zhang, Ruihao Zhu, Qiaomin Xie

Abstract
Motivated by the principle of satisficing in decision-making, we study satisficing regret guarantees for nonstationary K K -armed bandits. We show that in the general realizable, piecewise-stationary setting with L L stationary segments, the optimal regret is Θ ⁡ ( L ​ log ⁡ T ) \Theta(L\log T) as long as L ≥ 2 L\geq 2 . This stands in sharp contrast to the case of L = 1 L=1 (i.e., the stationary setting), where a T T -independent Θ ⁡ ( 1 ) \Theta(1) satisficing regret is achievable under realizability. In other words, the optimal regret has to scale with T T even if just a little nonstationarity presents. A key ingredient in our analysis is a novel Fano-based framework tailored to nonstationary bandits via a post-interaction reference construction. This framework strictly extends the classical Fano method for passive estimation as well as recent interactive Fano techniques for stationary bandits. As a complement, we also discuss a special regime in which constant satisficing regret is again possible.

1
Introduction

The multi-armed bandit (MAB) problem is a classical framework for studying the
exploration–exploitation trade-off in sequential decision-making. In the
standard MAB setting, a learner repeatedly chooses among
K
arms with unknown,
arm-specific stationary reward distributions, with the goal of minimizing
cumulative regret
(
26
,
17
)
. For the
canonical MAB problem and its many variants, a rich literature has established
sharp performance guarantees; see, for example,
18
.

While identifying the optimal action is essential in some high-stakes settings,
many practical applications only require a “good-enough,” or

satisficing
, decision. Introduced by
32
,
satisficing aims for an acceptable solution rather than the absolute best one.
This perspective is common in practice. For instance, recommendation and
advertising systems often deploy a candidate only if its KPI exceeds a threshold

S
with sufficient confidence
(
16
,
35
)
,
and inventory control typically targets a desired service level (e.g., 90–95%)
to mitigate stockout costs
(
11
,
15
)
.

These considerations motivate the study of
satisficing bandits

(
33
,
22
,
27
,
10
,
14
)
,
where the learner aims to pull a
satisficing arm
—an arm whose mean reward
is no less than a prescribed threshold
S
—as often as possible. Performance is
measured by the
satisficing regret
,
defined as the cumulative
shortfall of the selected arm’s mean reward relative to
S
. To the best of our knowledge, existing results on
satisficing regret primarily focus on stationary environments:

33
,
22
,
27
study the

K
-armed bandit setting,
10
extend the framework to more
general bandit optimization problems, and
14
study
the average-reward Markov decision process setting. Under a
realizability

condition—namely, that at least one (initially) unknown arm is strictly satisficing with mean
exceeding
S
—these works show that the satisficing regret can be bounded by a constant
independent of the decision horizon
T
.

In many real-world scenarios, however, stationarity fails: consumer preferences
drift with seasons and trends
(
1
)
, and platform
marketplaces (e.g., ride-hailing) experience demand surges and changing
competitive conditions
(
4
)
. As a result, an arm’s
reward distribution may evolve over time. Despite the growing literature on
satisficing regret in stationary environments, much less is understood in the
nonstationary setting that frequently arises in practice. Motivated by this gap,
we study satisficing regret minimization under nonstationarity, focusing on
piecewise-stationary bandits with
L
stationary segments.
Building on the stationary case (corresponding to
L=1
), it is natural to ask:

Can we similarly achieve a
T
-independent satisficing regret in the piecewise-stationary setting?

At first glance, this appears plausible since if the change points were known
in advance, one could simply restart a stationary satisficing algorithm on each
segment, leading to an
\mathcal{O}(L)
satisficing regret.

1.1
Our Contributions

In this work, however, we provide a negative answer to the above conjecture. Specifically,

•

In Section
4
, we establish an

\Omega\bigl(L\log(T/L)\bigr)
lower bound on the satisficing regret for nonstationary
K
-armed bandits. We then propose Algorithm
1
, which achieves an almost matching upper bound of
\mathcal{O}(L\log T)
. Together, these results show that the additional
\log T
factor is unavoidable in general when the change points are unknown, thereby ruling out the conjectured
\mathcal{O}(L)
satisficing regret guarantee.

•

To establish the lower bounds above, we extend recent
interactive
Fano techniques for bandit regret lower bounds
(
7
,
31
)
from stationary to nonstationary environments. More importantly, we introduce a
post-interaction reference
framework that strictly generalizes the classical Fano approach and is particularly well suited to nonstationary bandits. The key feature is that an auxiliary random variable (the “reference”) may influence the data-generating process and is revealed to the estimator
after
the interaction. This leads to two complementary principles for lower-bounding regret (Section
3.2
): (i) reducing a
portion
of the regret to an identification error under a suitable separation condition, and (ii) interpreting a
portion
of the regret as an
information cost
. We further emphasize that this framework may extend to broader classes of nonstationary bandit problems and may be of independent interest.

•

In Section
5
, we complement the general negative
result by identifying a special case in which constant satisficing regret is
still achievable. Specifically, when there exists an
always-realizable
arm and no satisficing arm ever becomes non-satisficing, the stationary algorithms of
22
can be applied directly in the nonstationary setting, yielding
constant
satisficing regret that is independent of both the horizon
T
and the number of stationary segments
L
.

1.2
Related Work

Nonstationary bandits.

Most work on nonstationary bandits evaluates performance through

dynamic regret
, which compares the learner with the best arm at each
round. For piecewise-stationary environments with at most
L
segments,
sliding-window and change-point-based methods achieve

\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(\sqrt{LT})
dynamic regret

(
13
,
3
)
. Under a variation-budget model
with total drift
D
, the optimal rate is

\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(D^{1/3}T^{2/3})

(
5
,
8
)
, and
34

obtain the unified guarantee

\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(\min\{\sqrt{LT},D^{1/3}T^{2/3}\})
.
These results address a stronger benchmark than ours: dynamic regret competes
with the instantaneous best arm, whereas our objective only requires selecting
an arm whose mean exceeds a fixed threshold.

Stationary satisficing regret.

Our work is most closely related to the literature on threshold-based
satisficing regret in stationary bandits.

22
,
27
, and

10
study cumulative satisficing regret under a fixed
threshold and show that, under realizability, constant regret is achievable in
stationary settings. Our paper shows that this phenomenon changes sharply under
nonstationarity: once the environment may change at unknown times, constant
satisficing regret is no longer achievable in general.

Threshold-based pure exploration and other satisficing notions.

In stationary settings, threshold-based objectives are also studied in pure
exploration under the name
thresholding bandits

(
21
,
24
)
. These works aim to
identify the arms whose means exceed a threshold and are evaluated by
probability of error or sample complexity, rather than cumulative satisficing
regret. Although such methods can identify a satisficing arm with high
probability, a naive explore–then–exploit reduction is not sufficient to
obtain optimal cumulative satisficing regret even in stationary problems

(
22
,
10
)
.

28
introduce a discounted notion of satisficing in
stationary bandits, motivated by time-sensitive learning. Their setting and
benchmark differ from ours: they study discounted regret rather than cumulative
threshold shortfall.

For nonstationary bandits, as highlighted in
20
,
19
, the standard notion of dynamic regret can steer algorithm design toward overly aggressive exploration; it can therefore be more meaningful to compete against a
satisficing
benchmark.
23
study nonstationary bandits in a Bayesian model and introduce a notion of satisficing based on competing with a
satisficing action sequence

A^{\dagger}

(
23
, Sec. 7)
. Their ‘satisficing regret” is defined as regret relative to
A^{\dagger}
. In contrast, we study a
threshold-based
notion of satisficing regret, defined as the cumulative shortfall from a fixed threshold
S
. Our formulation does not require that any arm achieves mean exactly
S
at each time. Even if one sets
A^{\dagger}
to be a realizable arm sequence, the two regret notions are not directly comparable: regret relative to
A^{\dagger}
can be negative at times when the learner selects an arm with strictly larger mean than
A_{t}^{\dagger}
, whereas our satisficing regret is nonnegative by definition. Finally,
23
establishes asymptotic lower bounds for the classical dynamic regret, while we provide minimax-optimal
finite-time
guarantees for threshold-based satisficing regret.

2
Problem Formulation

Nonstationary Bandits

We study a nonstationary
K
-armed bandit over a horizon
T\in\mathbb{N}
,
with
K\lesssim T
. At each round
t\in[T]
, each arm

a\in[K]
has an unknown mean reward
\mu_{t}(a)\in[0,1]
.
For integers
1\leq s\leq t\leq T
, define the number of stationary segments on

[s,t]
as

L[s,t]:=1+\sum_{j=s}^{t-1}\mathds{1}\left\{\exists a\in[K]\text{ such that }\ \mu_{j}(a)\neq\mu_{j+1}(a)\right\}.

Let
L:=L[1,T]
denote the total number of stationary segments, and let

1=T_{0}<T_{1}<\cdots<T_{L}=T+1

be the associated change-points that partition the horizon into
L
stationary
segments, where segment
\ell\in\{0,\dots,L-1\}
is the interval

[T_{\ell},\,T_{\ell+1}-1]
. A (possibly randomized) policy
\pi
selects an
action
a_{t}\in[K]
at time
t
, adapted to the natural filtration

\mathcal{F}_{t-1}:=\sigma(a_{1},r_{1},\dots,a_{t-1},r_{t-1})
. Let
\Pi
denote
the class of all such policies. Conditional on
a_{t}
, the learner observes a
reward
r_{t}=\mu_{t}(a_{t})+\xi_{t},
where
(\xi_{t})_{t\geq 1}
are independent, zero-mean,
1
-sub-Gaussian noises.

Satisficing Regret.

The decision maker specifies a threshold
S\in\mathbb{R}
, and performance is
judged by whether the chosen arm meets this level. An arm
a
is

satisficing
at time
t
if
\mu_{t}(a)\geq S
. The one-step satisficing regret is

(S-\mu_{t}(a_{t}))_{+}:=\max\{0,S-\mu_{t}(a_{t})\}
, and the cumulative

satisficing regret
under policy
\pi
is

R_{T}^{S}(\pi;\nu):=\mathbb{E}_{\pi,\nu}\Big[\sum_{t=1}^{T}(S-\mu_{t}(a_{t}))_{+}\Big],

(2)

where
\nu
denotes the underlying nonstationary environment and
\mathbb{E}_{\pi,\nu}
is over the
policy’s randomness and the observation noise.

Satisficing nonstationary bandits.

For each segment
\ell\in\{0,\dots,L-1\}
, let

\mu_{a,\ell}:=\mu_{T_{\ell}}(a)
denote the mean reward of arm

a\in[K]
on segment
\ell
, and define the segment-wise optimal mean

\mu_{\ell}^{*}:=\max_{a\in[K]}\mu_{a,\ell}
. Throughout, we impose the
following realizability condition.

Assumption 1

(Realizability)
.

For every segment
\ell\in\{0,\dots,L-1\}
, there exists at least one arm with
mean strictly above the threshold, i.e.,
\mu_{\ell}^{*}>S
.

Assumption
1
is standard in studies of
satisficing regret in the stationary setting

(
22
,
10
)
. We refer to the nonstationary bandit
problem under Assumption
1
, together with the
objective (
2
), as the
nonstationary satisficing
bandit
problem.

3
A Fano-Based Framework for Nonstationary Bandit Problems

Fano’s lemma

(
25
, Theorem 2.10)
is a classical tool
for minimax lower bounds in multi-hypothesis testing. Let
\mathcal{V}
be a
finite index set, let
\nu\sim\mathrm{Unif}(\mathcal{V})
be a latent instance index,
and let
Z
denote the data generated by an algorithm interacting with the
instance indexed by
\nu
. Fano’s lemma lower bounds the best achievable
identification error in terms of the mutual information
I(\nu;Z)
:

\inf_{\widehat{\nu}(Z)}\mathbb{P}\bigl(\widehat{\nu}(Z)\neq\nu\bigr)\geq 1-\frac{I(\nu;Z)+\log 2}{\log|\mathcal{V}|}.

(3)

Recently, building on (
3
),
7
,
31

developed general Fano-type frameworks for proving regret lower bounds in

stationary
bandit problems. In Section
3.1
, we review how
these works leverage (
3
) to derive interactive Fano methods for
stationary bandits.

Conditional Fano.

It is often useful to apply Fano’s lemma in a structured setting where the index takes the form

\nu=(u,b)\in\mathcal{U}\times\mathcal{B}
. Here the “reference” variable
b
is
independent of
u
, the observation
Z
may depend on
b
, and
b
is
revealed to the estimator before it outputs
\widehat{u}
.
A conditional form of Fano’s inequality
(
30
, Theorem 3)
then
follows directly from (
3
):

\inf_{\widehat{u}(Z,b)}\mathbb{P}\bigl(\widehat{u}(Z,b)\neq u\bigr)\geq 1-\frac{I(u;Z\mid b)+\log 2}{\log|\mathcal{U}|}.

(4)

In Section
3.2
, building on (
4
), we develop a

post-interaction reference
framework which is particularly convenient for
nonstationary bandits and yields two complementary principles for deriving
regret lower bounds.

3.1
Classical Fano Reduction: Small Regret Implies Correct Identification

Draw an environment index
\nu\sim\mathrm{Unif}(\mathcal{V})
. Fix any policy
\pi
,
and let
Z:=(a_{1},r_{1},\dots,a_{T},r_{T})
denote the full interaction transcript
obtained by running
\pi
for
T
rounds in environment
\nu
.
Let
P_{\nu}
denote the law of
Z
conditional on
\nu
, and let

\bar{P}:=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{V}|}\sum_{\tau\in\mathcal{V}}P_{\tau}
be the
resulting mixture distribution. By the definition of mutual information and the
convexity of
D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P\|\cdot)
in its second argument

(
9
)
, we have

I(\nu;Z)=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{V}|}\sum_{\tau\in\mathcal{V}}D_{\mathrm{KL}}\left(P_{\tau}\middle\|\bar{P}\right)\leq\frac{1}{|\mathcal{V}|^{2}}\sum_{\tau,\tau^{\prime}\in\mathcal{V}}D_{\mathrm{KL}}\left(P_{\tau}\middle\|P_{\tau^{\prime}}\right).

(5)

Combining (
5
) with (
3
) yields

\inf_{\widehat{\nu}(Z)}\mathbb{P}\!\bigl(\widehat{\nu}(Z)\neq\nu\bigr)\geq 1-\frac{\frac{1}{|\mathcal{V}|^{2}}\sum_{\tau,\tau^{\prime}\in\mathcal{V}}D_{\mathrm{KL}}\left(P_{\tau}\middle\|P_{\tau^{\prime}}\right)+\log 2}{\log|\mathcal{V}|}.

(6)

In stationary bandits, a standard route to regret lower bounds is to

reduce small regret to accurate identification
of the underlying
instance. Concretely, one constructs an instance family

\{P_{\nu}\}_{\nu\in\mathcal{V}}
satisfying a
separation condition
at
level
\Delta
(see, e.g.,
31
or the more
general formulation in
7
), which enables the
construction of an estimator
\widehat{\nu}(Z)
from the transcript
Z
such
that

\mathbb{P}\bigl(\widehat{\nu}(Z)\neq\nu\bigr)\leq\mathbb{P}\bigl(\mathrm{Reg}_{T}(\pi,\nu)\geq T\Delta\bigl),

(7)

where
\mathrm{Reg}_{T}
denotes the regret of interest.
By Markov’s inequality and (
6
), the Bayesian regret under the
uniform prior satisfies

\mathbb{E}\!\left[\mathrm{Reg}_{T}(\pi,\nu)\right]\;\geq\;T\Delta\cdot\Bigl(1-\frac{\frac{1}{|\mathcal{V}|^{2}}\sum_{\tau,\tau^{\prime}\in\mathcal{V}}D_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\left(P_{\tau}\middle\|P_{\tau^{\prime}}\right)+\log 2}{\log|\mathcal{V}|}\Bigr),

and one then optimizes over the choice of
\{P_{\nu}\}_{\nu\in\mathcal{V}}
to establish the lower bound.

3.2
Post-Interaction Reference and Two Complementary Approaches

In nonstationary settings, the reduction (
7
)
typically breaks down: unlike the stationary case, there is no

canonical separation condition
that directly links regret to identifying the latent
instance, since the underlying structure may vary over time. Motivated by the conditional bound (
4
), we introduce an
interaction protocol with a
post-interaction reference
.

Protocol.

Draw an environment index
(u,b)\sim\mathrm{Unif}(\mathcal{V})
, where

\mathcal{V}:=\mathcal{U}\times\mathcal{B}
. Under the prior

\mathrm{Unif}(\mathcal{V})
, the random variables
u
and
b
are independent. Under the (unknown) environment
(u,b)
, let

Z:=(a_{1},r_{1},\dots,a_{T},r_{T})
denote the interaction transcript, and let

P_{u,b}
denote the law of
Z
conditional on
(u,b)
.
The key feature is that
b
(the
reference
) is revealed to the
estimator only
after

Z
has been generated. When
b
is
uninformative, this reduces to the classical formulation in
Section
3.1
.

Conditional versions of Fano’s lemma have been widely studied in statistics and
information theory
(
2
,
29
,
30
)
,
but have not, to our knowledge, been systematically developed as a general
lower-bound mechanism for nonstationary bandits. We show that a post-interaction
reference enables two complementary approaches for proving regret lower bounds.

Approach I: coordinate-wise identification error.

Analogous to (
5
), conditioning on
b
yields

I(u;Z\mid b)=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{B}|}\sum_{x\in\mathcal{B}}\frac{1}{|\mathcal{U}|}\sum_{\tau\in\mathcal{U}}D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P_{\tau,x}\|\bar{P}_{x})\leq\frac{1}{|\mathcal{B}||\mathcal{U}|^{2}}\sum_{x\in\mathcal{B}}\sum_{\tau,\tau^{\prime}\in\mathcal{U}}D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P_{\tau,x}\|P_{\tau^{\prime},x}),

where
\bar{P}_{x}:=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{U}|}\sum_{\tau\in\mathcal{U}}P_{\tau,x}
.
Plugging this into (
4
) gives

\inf_{\widehat{u}(Z,b)}\mathbb{P}\bigl(\widehat{u}(Z,b)\neq u\bigr)\geq 1-\frac{\frac{1}{|\mathcal{B}||\mathcal{U}|^{2}}\sum_{x\in\mathcal{B}}\sum_{\tau,\tau^{\prime}\in\mathcal{U}}D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P_{\tau,x}\|P_{\tau^{\prime},x})+\log 2}{\log|\mathcal{U}|}.

(8)

A particularly useful instantiation is when
\mathcal{V}
has a product
structure
\mathcal{V}=\mathcal{V}_{1}\times\cdots\times\mathcal{V}_{m}
. For any
coordinate
i\in[m]
, we take

\mathcal{U}=\mathcal{V}_{i},\qquad\mathcal{B}=\mathcal{V}_{-i}:=\mathcal{V}_{1}\times\cdots\times\mathcal{V}_{i-1}\times\mathcal{V}_{i+1}\times\cdots\times\mathcal{V}_{m}.

(9)

This yields a coordinate-wise identification bound which can be paired with a
lower bound on a
partial
regret component:

\inf_{\widehat{u}(Z,b)}\mathbb{P}\bigl(\widehat{u}(Z,b)\neq u\bigr)\lesssim\mathbb{E}\big[\mathrm{Reg}_{T}^{(i)}(\pi,\nu)\big].

(10)

Throughout Section
3
, the notation
\lesssim
may hide constants that
depend on
T
and
\{P_{\nu}\}_{\nu\in\mathcal{V}}
.
An intuitive interpretation of (
8
) and
(
10
) is as follows: under an appropriate separation
condition for a carefully constructed family of nonstationary instances, once
the reference is revealed, any policy
\pi
must incur a nontrivial
coordinate-wise identification error. This nontrivial lower bound on the
identification error can then be translated into a lower bound on the
corresponding partial regret.
If, moreover, the total regret decomposes as

\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{Reg}_{T}(\pi;\nu)]=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{Reg}_{T}^{(i)}(\pi;\nu)]
,
then summing coordinate-wise bounds and optimizing over

\{P_{\nu}\}_{\nu\in\mathcal{V}}
yields Bayesian regret lower bounds. Since (
10
) is more general than
(
7
)—both in allowing a broader separation
condition and in relating identification to
partial
regret through the
post-interaction reference—Approach I can be viewed as a strict extension of
the interactive Fano methods for stationary bandits outlined in
Section
3.1
.

This coordinate-wise viewpoint is closely related to Assouad’s method for
(passive) statistical estimation
(
36
)
. In the bandit setting,

7
develop a general framework for
stationary
problems
that recovers Assouad-type arguments. We show that the reference-aided
coordinate-wise strategy extends naturally to nonstationary bandits: it
yields the optimal dependence on the number of stationary segments
L
, and we
leverage it to prove Theorem
1
.

Approach II: coordinate-wise information cost
.

We again adopt the product structure (
9
). Conditional on

(\mathcal{F}_{t-1},b)
, the action
a_{t}
is drawn using the policy’s internal
randomness, which is independent of
u
. Hence

I(u;a_{t}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1},b)=0
, and the chain rule yields

I(u;Z\mid b)=\sum_{t=1}^{T}I\bigl(u;a_{t}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1},b\bigr)+\sum_{t=1}^{T}I\bigl(u;r_{t}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1},a_{t},b\bigr)=\sum_{t=1}^{T}I\bigl(u;r_{t}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1},a_{t},b\bigr).

Consequently, (
4
) implies

\inf_{\widehat{u}(Z,b)}\mathbb{P}\!\bigl(\widehat{u}(Z,b)\neq u\bigr)\geq 1-\frac{\sum_{t=1}^{T}I\bigl(u;r_{t}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1},a_{t},b\bigr)+\log 2}{\log|\mathcal{U}|}.

(11)

The strength of (
11
) is that it yields regret lower bounds

without
requiring a direct implication of the form
“(under an appropriate separation condition) small (partial) regret

\Rightarrow
correct (coordinate-wise) identification,”
an implication that is used both in the prior interactive Fano methods
reviewed in Section
3.1
and in our Approach I, but that can fail
in certain nonstationary constructions. Instead, we adopt an

information cost
perspective: we construct
\{P_{\nu}\}_{\nu\in\mathcal{V}}

and an estimator
\widehat{u}(Z,b)
such that for some
\rho\in(0,1)
,

\underbrace{\mathbb{P}\bigl(\widehat{u}(Z,b)\neq u\bigr)\leq\rho}_{\text{Identification given the reference}},\qquad\underbrace{\sum_{t=1}^{T}I\bigl(u;r_{t}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1},a_{t},b\bigr)\lesssim\mathbb{E}\big[\mathrm{Reg}_{T}^{(i)}(\pi,\nu)\big]}_{\text{Coordinate-wise cost of information paid by partial regret}}.

(12)

An intuitive interpretation of (
11
) and (
12
) is as follows: under any policy
\pi
, once the reference is revealed, the coordinate-wise identification error admits a nontrivial
upper
bound. Then, by (
11
), the learner must have acquired a sufficient amount of information about the underlying coordinate; in our construction, acquiring this information necessarily incurs some partial regret. In many
nonstationary constructions, once
b
is revealed, information about
u
is
acquired only through actions that incur regret, which makes
(
12
) easier to verify than a direct reduction (
10
). We use this approach to prove
Theorem
2
.

We emphasize that, although classical KL change-of-measure arguments also link
regret to information acquisition, they differ in an essential way from our
approach. In the stationary setting, such arguments typically compare the laws
of the interaction transcript under a small family of alternative environments
and convert the resulting KL divergences into regret lower bounds
(
6
,
12
)

not by itself provide the coordinate-wise decomposition and

distinction further in the next section, where we explain why such classical
arguments do not directly yield the desired logarithmic lower bounds for nonstationary satisficing
bandits.

Regret Guarantees for Nonstationary Satisficing Bandits

In this section, we establish minimax satisficing regret guarantees for general nonstationary satisficing bandits. Sections

4.1
Lower Bounds

In the stationary setting, the minimax-optimal satisficing regret is constant
under realizability
(
22

10

27

.
It is therefore natural to conjecture that, in a piecewise-stationary
environment with
L

\mathcal{O}(L)
guarantee might be achieved
by restarting a stationary algorithm at each change-point (if the change-points
can be efficiently detected).
The main message of
this subsection is that such an
\mathcal{O}(L)
rate is
not
achievable
in general when change-points are unknown: an additional
\log T
factor is
unavoidable.

A canonical hard class.

Let
\mathcal{E}_{L,\Delta}
denote the class of two-armed,
piecewise-stationary bandit instances with
L
segments, where rewards are
Gaussian with unit variance and the mean vector on each segment
\ell
is
either
(S+\Delta,S-\Delta)
or
(S-\Delta,S+\Delta)
. In particular, the
unique satisficing arm swaps at every change-point.

This class is canonical for our problem. It is the simplest symmetric
realizable family that isolates the effect of nonstationarity: there are only
two arms, the gap to the threshold is fixed at
\Delta
on every segment, and
each segment contains exactly one satisficing arm and one non-satisficing arm.
Thus, the difficulty arises solely from the unknown change-points and the
resulting switches in the identity of the satisficing arm. Furthermore, we
emphasize that classical pairwise KL change-of-measure arguments do not
directly yield the desired lower bound on this class. Indeed, if
two instances in
\mathcal{E}_{L,\Delta}
differ only in the location of one
switch, then on the interval where the switch locations disagree. Hence, under unit-variance Gaussian rewards, each round in this
disagreement interval contributes
2\Delta^{2}
to the conditional KL
divergence, regardless of which arm is pulled. As a result, the pairwise KL is
driven primarily by the length of the disagreement interval, rather than by the
number of regretful pulls. This breaks the usual mechanism behind classical
KL-based lower bounds

(
6
,
12
,
13
)
. We therefore
use our post-interaction-reference framework to derive the sharp satisficing
regret lower bound for

Theorem 1

Suppose

When

Relative to the naive

reflects the fact that the change-points are unknown: minimizing satisficing regret is at least as hard as localizing these change-points, which already
incurs an
\Omega(L\log T)
cost. The proof, deferred to

5
When Is Constant Satisficing Regret Achievable

It remains unclear which structural conditions permit
constant

satisficing regret in nonstationary
K
-armed bandits. In the stationary
setting, constant satisficing regret is possible because the learner can
identify satisficing and non-satisficing arms in finite expected time—independent
of
T
—by comparing empirical means to the threshold
S
; see

(
22
, Appendix A)
.

A common feature of the hard instances behind the
\Omega(\log T)
lower bounds
in Section
4
is the presence of
threshold crossings
: an
arm may be satisficing early on but later
down-cross
below
S
at an
unknown time, and the identity of the realizable arm may
also switch across segments. Such changes force the learner to repeatedly
certify satisficing arms, which incurs a logarithmic cost.

In this section, we identify a simple regime in which this obstacle disappears.
Specifically, if (i) there exists an
always-realizable
arm that remains
strictly satisficing throughout, and (ii) no arm ever transitions from
satisficing to non-satisficing (i.e., no down-crossings), then constant
satisficing regret—independent of both the horizon
T
and the number of
stationary segments
L
—is achievable.

Formally, we
impose the following assumption.

Assumption 2

(Always-Realizability)
.

\exists a^{*}\in[K]
such that
\mu_{a^{*},\ell}>S
for all
\ell\in[0,L-1]
.

Assumption 3

(No Down-Crossing)
.

For each arm
a\in[K]
, the indicator sequence

\bigl\{\mathds{1}\{\mu_{t}(a)\geq S\}\bigr\}_{t\in[T]}
is nondecreasing in
t
.

2
and
3
,

given

(\mathcal{F}_{t-1},a_{t},b,u_{-\ell})
, and therefore

\displaystyle I(\nu_{\ell};r_{t}\mid F_{t-1}=f,a_{t}=a,\nu_{-\ell}=x)=0,\quad\forall t\in[T]\setminus\mathcal{I}_{\ell}.

By convexity of
D_{\mathrm{KL}}
in its second argument,
D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P_{+}\|Q)\leq pD_{\mathrm{KL}}(P_{+}\|P_{-})
and
D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P_{-}\|Q)\leq(1-p)D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P_{-}\|P_{+}).
Thus, for any
t\in\mathcal{I}_{\ell}
,

\displaystyle I(\nu_{\ell};r_{t}\mid F_{t-1}=f,a_{t}=a,\nu_{-\ell}=x)

\displaystyle\leq(1-p)pD_{\mathrm{KL}}(P_{+}\|P_{-})+p(1-p)D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P_{-}\|P_{+})

\displaystyle\leq p(D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P_{+}\|P_{-})+D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P_{-}\|P_{+}))=4\Delta^{2}p.

For any
t\in\mathcal{I}_{\ell}
, taking expectation over
(\mathcal{F}_{t-1},a_{t},\nu_{-\ell})
yields

\displaystyle I(\nu_{\ell};r_{t}\mid F_{t-1},a_{t},\nu_{-\ell})

\displaystyle\leq 4\Delta^{2}\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\mathbb{P}(\widetilde{\nu}_{\ell}\leq t\mid F_{t-1},a_{t},\nu_{-\ell})\mathds{1}\{a_{t}=2\}+\mathbb{P}(\widetilde{\nu}_{\ell}>t\mid F_{t-1},a_{t},\nu_{-\ell})\mathds{1}\{a_{t}=1\}\Bigr]

\displaystyle=4\Delta^{2}\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\mathds{1}\{\widetilde{\nu}_{\ell}\leq t,a_{t}=2\}+\mathds{1}\{\widetilde{\nu}_{\ell}>t,a_{t}=1\}\Bigr].

Finally, for any
\ell\in[L/2]
,

I(u_{\ell};Z\mid B,u_{-\ell})\leq 4\Delta^{2}\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\sum_{t=(\ell-1)m+1}^{\nu_{\ell}-1}\mathds{1}\{a_{t}=1\}+\sum_{t=\nu_{\ell}}^{\ell m}\mathds{1}\{a_{t}=2\}\Bigr].

(17)

Construct
\widehat{\nu}_{\ell}(Z,\nu_{-\ell})
.

Fix
\ell\in[L/2]
. For each candidate change-point
\tau\in\mathcal{T}
, define

B_{\ell,\tau}:=\bigl[(\ell-1)m+\tau,\;(\ell-1)m+\tau+l-1\bigr],\qquad N_{\ell,\tau}(Z):=\sum_{t\in B_{\ell,\tau}}\mathds{1}\{a_{t}=1\}.

Fix an integer
r
with
1\leq r\leq l
. For each
\tau
such that
N_{\ell,\tau}(Z)\geq r
,
let
\overline{X}_{\ell,\tau}(r)
denote the average of the
first
r
rewards
of arm
1
observed within
B_{\ell,\tau}
. For each
\tau
such that
N_{\ell,\tau}(Z)<r
, let
\overline{X}^{\prime}_{\ell,\tau}(l-r)
denote
the average of the
first
l-r
rewards of arm
2
observed within
B_{\ell,\tau}
.
Define the estimators

\displaystyle\widehat{\nu}_{\ell}^{\prime}(Z,\nu_{-\ell})

\displaystyle:=\min\Bigl\{\tau\in\mathcal{T}:\ N_{\ell,\tau}(Z)\geq r\ \text{ and }\ \overline{X}_{\ell,\tau}(r)\geq S\Bigr\}

\displaystyle\widehat{\nu}_{\ell}^{\prime\prime}(Z,\nu_{-\ell})

\displaystyle:=\min\Bigl\{\tau\in\mathcal{T}:\ N_{\ell,\tau}(Z)<r\ \text{ and }\ \overline{X}_{\ell,\tau}^{\prime}(l-r)\leq S\Bigr\}

We next state a lemma that bounds the error probabilities of the two estimators above; its proof is deferred to Section
6.2.1
.

Lemma 2
.

Fix integers

l,r\in\mathbb{N}
with
1\leq r\leq l
, and
\ell\in[L/2]
. Then,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\widehat{\nu}_{\ell}^{\prime}(Z,\nu_{-\ell})\neq\nu_{\ell})

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}(N_{\ell,\nu_{\ell}}(Z)<r)+n\exp(-r\Delta^{2}/2),

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\widehat{\nu}_{\ell}^{\prime\prime}(Z,\nu_{-\ell})\neq\nu_{\ell})

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\big(N_{\ell,u_{\ell}}(Z)\geq r\big)+n\exp(-(l-r)\Delta^{2}/2).

Construct the final estimator by flipping an independent coin

\Xi\sim\mathrm{Unif}\{0,1\}
. If
\Xi=0
, set

\widehat{\nu}_{\ell}(Z,\nu_{-\ell}):=\widehat{\nu}_{\ell}^{\prime}(Z,\nu_{-\ell})
; otherwise, set

\widehat{\nu}_{\ell}(Z,\nu_{-\ell}):=\widehat{\nu}_{\ell}^{\prime\prime}(Z,\nu_{-\ell})
.
Combining Lemma
2
with the choices

r=\lceil l/2\rceil
,
l=\big\lceil 6\log(n)/\Delta^{2}\big\rceil+1
, and
n\geq 2
, we obtain

\mathbb{P}(\widehat{\nu}_{\ell}(Z,\nu_{-\ell})\neq\nu_{\ell})\ \leq 1/2+n\exp(-(l-1)\Delta^{2}/2)\ \leq 1/2+1/n^{2}\leq 3/4,

(18)

Combining (

) and (

),
we obtain

Consequently,

\mathbb{E}[R_{T}^{S}(\pi;\nu)]=\Delta\cdot\sum_{\ell\in[L/2]}\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\sum_{t=(\ell-1)m+1}^{\nu_{\ell}-1}\mathds{1}\{a_{t}=1\}+\sum_{t=\nu_{\ell}}^{\ell m}\mathds{1}\{a_{t}=2\}\Bigr]\geq\frac{L}{8\Delta}\Bigl(\frac{1}{4}\log n-\log 2\Bigr).

Recall the constraints

.
Ignoring the ceiling for simplicity, it suffices to choose

Since

6.2.1
Proof of Lemma
2

In this section, we prove the upper bound on
\mathbb{P}(\widehat{\nu}_{\ell}^{\prime}(Z,\nu_{-\ell})\neq\nu_{\ell})
in Lemma
2
; the second bound follows by an analogous argument. Let
E:=\{N_{\ell,\nu_{\ell}}(Z)<r\}
. Then

\mathbb{P}(\widehat{\nu}_{\ell}^{\prime}(Z,\nu_{-\ell})\neq\nu_{\ell})\leq\mathbb{P}(E)+\mathbb{P}(\widehat{\nu}_{\ell}^{\prime}(Z,\nu_{-\ell})\neq\nu_{\ell},E^{\mathrm{c}}).

On the event
E^{\mathrm{c}}
, the estimator
\widehat{\nu}_{\ell}^{\prime}(Z,\nu_{-\ell})
can make error only if either

•

the block at the true change-point is not declared, i.e.,

\overline{X}_{\ell,\nu_{\ell}}<S
; or

•

some earlier candidate is declared first,
i.e.,
\overline{X}_{\ell,\tau}\geq S
for some
\tau<\nu_{\ell}
with

N_{\ell,\tau}(Z)\geq r
.

For the first event, since
\overline{X}_{\ell,\nu_{\ell}}\sim\mathcal{N}(S+\Delta,1/r)

on
E^{\mathrm{c}}
, the Gaussian tail bound gives

\mathbb{P}(\overline{X}_{\ell,\nu_{\ell}}<S)=\mathbb{P}\left(\overline{X}_{\ell,\nu_{\ell}}-(S+\Delta)<-\Delta\right)\leq\exp(-r\Delta^{2}/2).

Next, fix any
\tau<\nu_{\ell}
. On
E^{\mathrm{c}}\cap\{N_{\ell,\tau}(Z)\geq r\}
,
we have
\overline{X}_{\ell,\tau}\sim\mathcal{N}(S-\Delta,1/r)
, and hence

\mathbb{P}(\overline{X}_{\ell,\tau}\geq S)=\mathbb{P}(\overline{X}_{\ell,\tau}-(S-\Delta)\geq\Delta)\leq\exp(-r\Delta^{2}/2).

Taking a union bound over the at most
n-1
indices
\tau\in\mathcal{T}
with

\tau<\nu_{\ell}
, we obtain

\mathbb{P}(\widehat{\nu}_{\ell}^{\prime}(Z,\nu_{-\ell})\neq\nu_{\ell},E^{\mathrm{c}})\leq(n-1)\exp(-r\Delta^{2}/2)+\exp(-r\Delta^{2}/2)=n\exp(-r\Delta^{2}/2).

6.3
Proof of Theorem
3

By the definition of the satisficing regret in (
2
), we have

R_{T}^{S}(\pi;\nu)=\sum_{\ell=0}^{L-1}\sum_{t=T_{\ell}}^{T_{\ell+1}-1}\sum_{a=1}^{K}\Delta_{a,\ell}\mathbb{P}_{\pi,\nu}(a_{t}=a)=\sum_{\ell=0}^{L-1}\sum_{t=T_{\ell}}^{T_{\ell+1}-1}\sum_{a:\mu_{a,\ell}<S}\Delta_{a,\ell}\mathbb{P}(a_{t}=a),

(19)

where, in the last equality, we simplify the notation by omitting the dependence on the policy
\pi
and the environment
\nu
. We first introduce some useful notation. For
\Delta>0
, define

n_{\Delta}:=\min\Bigl\{n\in[T]:\sqrt{\frac{2(4\log T+\log K)}{n}}\leq\Delta/2\Bigr\}\leq c_{1}\frac{\log TK}{\Delta^{2}},

where
c_{1}>0
is a universal constant. Consequently, we have

\beta(n)\leq\Delta/2
for all
n
such that
n_{\Delta}\leq n\leq T
. Define the event
\mathcal{E}_{\operatorname{win}}
that for every arm
a
, epoch
\kappa
,
time
t\leq T
, and every dyadic window
w\in\mathrm{WinSet}(\mathcal{B}_{a}^{(\kappa)}(t))

whose last
w
samples lie entirely inside a stationary segment
\ell
,
we have
\Big|\mathrm{AvgLast}\big(\mathcal{B}_{a}^{(\kappa)}(t),w\big)-\mu_{a,\ell}\Big|\ \leq\ \beta(w).
By Hoeffding’s inequality for
1
-sub-Gaussian variables, for fixed
(a,\kappa,t,w)

whose last
w
samples lie in some stationary segment
\ell
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\Big|\mathrm{AvgLast}\big(\mathcal{B}_{a}^{(\kappa)}(t),w\big)-\mu_{a,\ell}\Big|\geq\beta(w)\right)

\displaystyle\leq 2\exp\big(-\frac{w}{2}\beta(w)^{2}\big)=2\exp(-4\log T-\log K)=\frac{2}{KT^{4}}.

Taking a union bound over all
a\in[K]
, epochs
\kappa\leq T
, times
t\leq T
,
and windows
w\in\mathrm{WinSet}(\mathcal{B}_{a}^{(\kappa)}(t))
, and using that

|\mathrm{WinSet}(\mathcal{B}_{a}^{(\kappa)}(t))|\leq T
, we obtain
\mathcal{E}_{\operatorname{win}}^{c}\leq\frac{2}{T}.
On the event
\mathcal{E}_{\operatorname{win}}
, the windowed confidence bounds behave as follows:

•

Fast rejection.
If
\mu_{a,\ell}=S-\Delta
and
\mathcal{B}_{a}^{(\kappa)}
contains a suffix window of length
w\geq 2n_{\Delta}
entirely within segment
\ell
, then
\mathrm{UCB}^{\mathrm{win}}(\mathcal{B}_{a}^{(\kappa)})\leq S
. Hence any leader whose mean stays
\leq S-\Delta
in an epoch is demoted after at most
2n_{\Delta}
pulls within a single segment.

•

Fast certification.
Similarly, any arm whose mean stays
\geq S+\Delta
in an epoch is certified as satisficing once it accumulates
2n_{\Delta}
pulls within a single segment.

•

At most one false leader per segment.
Within a fixed segment
\ell
, any non-satisficing arm can be certified as leader at most once: after it is demoted its buffer resets, and while its mean remains
\leq S-\Delta
, it cannot satisfy
\mathrm{LCB}^{\mathrm{win}}(\mathcal{B}_{a}^{(\kappa)})\geq S
again before the segment ends.

•

No demotion of the optimal arm.
Within segment
\ell
, once an optimal (satisficing) arm is certified as leader, it is never demoted before the segment ends.

On the event
\mathcal{E}_{\operatorname{win}}
, the exploration length on each segment
\ell
is
at most
2Kn_{\Delta_{\ell}^{*}}
: during exploration the algorithm runs a
fixed round–robin schedule, and once the optimal arm on segment
\ell
has
been pulled
2n_{\Delta_{\ell}^{*}}
times, it is certified as satisficing
and exploration terminates. Moreover, since every non-satisficing arm

a
can be certified as leader at most once in segment
\ell
and, once

2n_{\Delta_{a,\ell}}
pulls, the
regret incurred on segment

is bounded by

\displaystyle\sum_{\ell=0}^{L-1}\sum_{a:\mu_{a,\ell}<S}(\frac{1}{\Delta_{a,\ell}}+\frac{\Delta_{a,\ell}}{(\Delta_{\ell}^{*})^{2}}\big)\cdot\log KT\lesssim\sum_{\ell=0}^{L-1}\sum_{a:\mu_{a,\ell}<S}(\frac{1}{\Delta_{a,\ell}}+\frac{\Delta_{a,\ell}}{(\Delta_{\ell}^{*})^{2}}\big)\cdot\log T

up to a universal constant. Therefore, we have

\displaystyle R_{T}^{S}(\pi;\nu)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\pi,\nu}\Big[\sum_{t=1}^{T}(S-\mu_{t}(a_{t}))_{+}\mid\mathcal{E}_{\operatorname{win}}\Big]\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{E}_{\operatorname{win}})+\mathbb{E}_{\pi,\nu}\Big[\sum_{t=1}^{T}(S-\mu_{t}(a_{t}))_{+}\mid\mathcal{E}_{\operatorname{win}}^{c}\Big]\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{E}_{\operatorname{win}}^{c})

\displaystyle\lesssim\sum_{\ell=0}^{L-1}\sum_{a:\mu_{a,\ell}<S}(\frac{1}{\Delta_{a,\ell}}+\frac{\Delta_{a,\ell}}{(\Delta_{\ell}^{*})^{2}}\big)\cdot\log T+T\cdot\frac{2}{T}\lesssim\sum_{\ell=0}^{L-1}\sum_{a:\mu_{a,\ell}<S}(\frac{1}{\Delta_{a,\ell}}+\frac{\Delta_{a,\ell}}{(\Delta_{\ell}^{*})^{2}}\big)\cdot\log T,

thereby completing the proof of Theorem

.

7
Conclusion

This paper studies satisficing regret in nonstationary bandits. Our results
reveal a sharp contrast with the stationary realizable setting: once the reward
process is allowed to change at unknown times, constant satisficing regret is
no longer achievable in general. In particular, in the realizable
piecewise-stationary setting with
L
stationary segments, we show that the
optimal regret scales as
\Theta(L\log T)
whenever

L\geq 2
.

Our lower bounds are based on a new post-interaction-reference framework, which
strictly extends interactive Fano methods from stationary bandits to
nonstationary settings.

We also show that constant satisficing regret can still be recovered under
additional structural assumptions. An important direction for future work is to
characterize more precisely which structural conditions are sufficient, and
which are necessary, for constant satisficing regret under nonstationarity.
