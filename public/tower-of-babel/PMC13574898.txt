Harnessing the gut microbiome for improved immune checkpoint inhibition in colorectal cancer immunotherapy: a narrative Review
Authors: Desalegn Abebaw, Adane Adugna, Bantayehu Addis Tegegne, Zigale Hibstu Teffera, Bantegzie Selabat, Yonatan Kindie, Melkamu Tilahun, Habtamu Belew, Temesgen Baylie, Getachew Mengistu, Mohammed Jemal, Aytenew Atnaf
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractColorectal cancer (CRC) remains among the most prevalent and deadliest malignancies worldwide, with limited survival outcomes, particularly in patients with metastatic disease. Despite advances in immunotherapy, immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) have shown efficacy mainly in mismatch repair-deficient (dMMR) CRC, while responses in mismatch repair-proficient (pMMR) microsatellite-stable (MSS) cases remain limited. Emerging evidence highlights the gut microbiome as a critical factor influencing CRC development, progression, and therapeutic response. In particular, the gut microbiota has been shown to affect the efficacy of ICIs, with dysbiosis contributing to treatment resistance and specific microbial taxa enhancing antitumor immune responses. Preclinical and clinical studies have demonstrated that microbiome-based interventions, including probiotics, fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT), dietary modulation, and traditional medicines, can restore immune function by modulating immune cell populations and producing immunoregulatory metabolites. These effects may enhance responsiveness to ICIs and contribute to the suppression of tumor growth. However, we also address key limitations in this field, including inconsistent findings and safety concerns, such as infection risks, to guide future translational efforts. Overall, while microbiome-based interventions represent a promising adjunct to CRC immunotherapy, rigorous clinical trials and mechanistic validation are required before their routine clinical implementation.Keywords: Colorectal cancer, Fecal microbiota transplantation, Gut microbiome, Immunotherapy, Probiotics

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) is the third most common and second-deadliest cancer worldwide in both men and women [1, 2]. Globally, CRC accounts for more than 1.85 million cases and 850,000 deaths each year [3]. It is increasing among people under 50 years of age, with only 15% of patients with metastatic disease surviving for 5 years [4, 5]. Cancer stem cells (CSCs) are thought to play a central role in the initiation, progression, metastasis, and resistance to therapy in CRC [6]. Progressive genetic and epigenetic alterations in tumor suppressor, oncogene, and DNA repair genes contribute to CRC [7]. Its progression, however, is likely driven primarily by the recruitment of immunosuppressive cells in the tumor microenvironment (TME) [8].

Even though its role is limited, immunotherapy is used to treat CRC [9], by inhibiting tumor growth through activation of the immune system, enabling the recognition and destruction of cancer cells [10, 11]. Immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs), such as anti-programmed death protein 1 (anti-PD1) and anti-programmed cell death protein 1 ligand (anti-PDL1), show promise for treating CRC, but most patients see little or no benefit [12, 13]. Despite promising effectiveness in dMMR microsatellite instable (MSI) CRC [14, 15], their efficacy in mismatch repair-proficient (pMMR) microsatellite-stable (MSS) CRC is limited [16–18]. The MSS TME is characterized by reduced immunogenicity alongside elevated levels of regulatory T (Treg) cells and myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) [18].

Evidence shows that the human gut microbiome is associated with the occurrence, progression, and immunotherapy resistance of CRC [14, 19]. Findings from animal models further strengthen this association, demonstrating that alterations in the gut microbiome can actively contribute to the initiation and progression of CRC [20–22]. Consistent with these observations, the microbiota of CRC patients differs markedly from that of healthy controls and is characterized by reduced microbial diversity [23, 24]. Furthermore, patients who respond to immune checkpoint therapy exhibit distinct microbiota richness and composition compared with non-responders [25]. Similarly, the gut microbiome differs significantly in both composition and diversity between dMMR and pMMR CRC, which could underlie their differing responses to immunotherapy [26, 27].

Importantly, these compositional and functional differences extend beyond the gut lumen to the TME, where microbiota is not randomly distributed but instead organized into specialized microniches. Within these niches, microbial communities interact closely with immune and epithelial cells, thereby shaping immune infiltration, promoting tumor progression, and modulating response to immunotherapy [28, 29]. Accordingly, the interplay between the microbiome and the immune microenvironment has become a major focus of current research in CRC [30].

The gut microbiome plays a crucial role in CRC immunotherapy by influencing anti-PD1/anti-PDL1 therapy [31, 32], cytotoxic T lymphocyte-associated protein-4 (CTLA-4) blockade, and T-cell activation [33]. Modulation of immune responses and systemic inflammation can markedly affect the efficacy of ICIs, particularly in pMMR/MSS CRC [34]. In MSS CRC, microbiome modulation could reverse the immunosuppressive TME to more immunogenic state by improving CD8+ T cell infiltration, reducing regulatory T cells and immunosuppressive factors, and promoting antigen presentation [34, 35]. Importantly, a balanced gut microbiota in combination with ICIs synergistically reprogrammed exhausted CD8+ T cells into memory/effector CD8+ T cells in CRC animal models [36]. This review examines current knowledge on the immunomodulatory effect of the gut microbiome in the tumor immune microenvironment and improvement of immune checkpoint inhibition in CRC immunotherapy.

Effect of the gut microbiome on the tumor immune microenvironment and its role in CRC

The gut microbiota influences CRC development and prevention through bacterial metabolites, invasion, immune modulation, and host–microbe interactions [37]. Gut dysbiosis contributes to CRC by inducing DNA damage, triggering the Wnt/β-catenin and inflammatory pathways, and disrupting nutrient supply and cancer cell metabolism [38, 39].

Microbiota-driven metabolic alterations within the TME contribute to the accumulation of metabolic waste and protein fermentation by-products, which are associated with an increased risk of CRC [40–42]. In CRC, an increased level of Actinomyces correlated with cancer-associated fibroblasts, activating the TLR2/NF-κB pathway and reducing CD8⁺ T-cell infiltration in the TME [43]. Conversely, the gut microbiome can exert immunostimulatory effects by inducing chemokine release from tumor cells, thereby recruiting dendritic cells (DCs) and beneficial T cells into the TME to enhance antitumor immunity [44, 45].

The gut microbiome shaping the in vivo immune response in CRC

In vivo studies in tumor-bearing mice show that gut bacteria stimulate CRC cells to produce chemokines, which recruit adoptively transferred T cells into tumors [45]. Analysis of tumor-infiltrating immune cells in Peptostreptococcus anaerobius (P. anaerobius)-treated ApcMin/+ mice revealed a marked increase in MDSC, tumor-associated macrophage (TAM), and granulocytic tumor-associated neutrophil (TAN) populations linked to chronic inflammation and tumor progression (Fig. 1) [46]. In CRC animal models, Porphyromonas gingivalis (P. gingivalis) impairs the cytotoxicity of invariant natural killer T (iNKT) cells and enhances their capacity to recruit TANs [47]. Human studies have also demonstrated that female CRC patients enriched in pathobionts, such as Fusobacterium and Parvimonas, exhibit impaired cytotoxic activity of tumor-infiltrating iNKT cells associated with decreased levels of cytotoxic T lymphocytes [48]. Dual RNA sequencing revealed that Bacteroides fragilis (B. fragilis) and Fusobacterium nucleatum (F. nucleatum) were associated with M2 macrophages and Treg infiltration in human CRC tissue biopsies, indicating their immunosuppressive role in the TME [49]. However, Lee et al. demonstrated the protective role of B. fragilis through the inhibition of C-C chemokine receptor 5 (CCR5) expression in the gut of CRC animal models [50]. Despite such conflicting evidence, several studies have reported elevated levels of B. fragilis in human fecal samples, suggesting its potential association with CRC [51–53](Table 1). Tumor-associated bacteria such as Fusobacterium, Parvimonas, Peptostreptococcus, and Porphyromonas are positively correlated with increased frequencies of antigen-presenting cells (APCs), PD-1-expressing T-cells, and Treg cells in humans with CRC [48]. Gut microbiome-derived lipopolysaccharide (LPS) and peptidoglycans induce a distinct S100A11+ epithelial cell in colonic adenomas. Increased S100A11 levels promote tumor growth in mice, accompanied by an increase in MDSCs and a decrease in CD8+ T cells [54].

Gut microbial metabolites, such as deoxycholic acid, impair CD8+ T-cell function and promote CRC in humans and animal models [55]. The microbial metabolite 4-hydroxybenzeneacetic acid (4-HPA), which is produced by polyketide synthase-positive (pks+) Escherichia coli (E. coli) and F. nucleatum, increases polymorphonuclear MDSC levels in tumors. This weakens CD8+ T-cell responses and promotes CRC growth in animal models [56]. On the other hand, beneficial bacteria such as Bifidobacterium longum sub species longum XZ01 promote M0 to M1 macrophage polarization by upregulating interferon gamma (IFN-γ), increasing tumor immunity, and suppressing tumor growth in animal models [57]. In addition to its immunomodulatory effects, members of the Bifidobacteria genus also suppress ammonia metabolism, a process that influences CRC growth [58]. Similarly, the commensal bacterium Roseburia intestinalis (R. intestinalis) has also been shown to inhibit tumor growth by inducing granzyme B+, IFN-γ+, and tumor necrosis factor (TNF)-α+ cytotoxic CD8+ T cells in mouse models [59].

The gut microbiome and in vitro immune dynamics in CRC

In vitro studies have revealed diverse and contrasting roles of gut microbes in shaping immune dynamics within CRC. Pathogenic species such as F. nucleatum enhanced the proliferation and invasion of CRC cell lines through Toll-like receptor 4 (TLR-4) signaling [60], while P. gingivalis activates iNKT cell lines, leading to diminished neutrophil respiratory burst activity and elevated PD-L1 expression [47]. Moreover, the gut microbiota of female patients impaired iNKT cell antitumor activity by disrupting the granzyme-perforin pathway [48].

In contrast, beneficial commensal bacteria exert immunostimulatory and antitumor effects. Lacticaseibacillus paracasei (L. paracasei) upregulates C-X-C motif chemokine ligand 10 (CXCL10) in mouse tumor tissues, promoting CD8+ T-cell recruitment [61]. Supernatants from Bifidobacterium infantis (B. infantis) DS1685 trigger CRC cell death by activating the SMAD4/TGF-β pathway [62], while lysates from Lactobacillus acidophilus (L. acidophilus) inhibited M2 polarization and IL-10 expression in LPS-activated tumor cell lines [63]. Additionally, Lactobacillus intestinalis (L. intestinalis) stimulates CRC cells to secrete chemokine C-C motif ligand 5 (CCL5), promoting DC chemotaxis and inhibiting tumorigenesis [47].

The Gut Microbiome as an adjunct to CRC immunotherapy

Probiotic administration

Probiotics are beneficial live microorganisms that promote host health when they are consumed in adequate amounts [64]. Certain gut microbes enhance the efficacy of ICIs by strengthening the gut barrier, regulating immune cells, and producing immunomodulatory metabolites, such as SCFAs (Fig. 2) [65, 66]. These active microbes critically shape the tumor immune microenvironment for effective immunotherapy [57]. Consistently, experimental evidence shows that gut microbiota from CRC mice can suppress anti-PD1 efficacy. In contrast, the microbiota from healthy donors enhances treatment responsiveness, suggesting that specific gut bacteria may promote responsiveness to anti-PD1 treatment [61].

Recent advances have highlighted the potential of combining probiotics with nanotechnology to enhance cancer immunotherapy. A novel approach involves engineering E. coli with IL-32 and nanoparticles, which synergistically enhance CD8+ T-cell-mediated immunity and help overcome resistance to immunotherapy in CRC [67]. Similarly, a genetically modified E. coli Nissle 1917 strain expressing surface adenosine deaminase has been shown to convert immunosuppressive adenosine into inosine within the TME, thereby enhancing antitumor immunity and therapeutic efficacy in CRC mice [68]. To further optimize delivery and stability, this engineered strain has been encapsulated in a chitosan–alginate oral microgel, which enhances the antitumor effects of TGF-β inhibitors [69].

Extracellular vesicles from Lactobacillus rhamnosus GG (LGG-EVs) have been shown to potentiate anti-PD1 therapy in CRC models by enhancing intestinal immune responses. They increase the CD8+/CD4+ T cell ratio in lymph nodes and increase MHC II+ DCs, as well as tumor-infiltrating CD4+ and CD8+ T-cells [70]. Similarly, direct supplementation with LGG increases colonic CD8+ T-cell abundance, thereby boosting antitumor immunity and reducing tumor burden in CRC mice [71]. Additionally, metabolites from Lactobacillus and yeast grown in soybean-based media further amplified the antitumor efficacy of anti-PD1 therapy in CRC animal models [72].

The role of certain probiotics, such as Akkermansia muciniphila (A. muciniphila) in CRC remains controversial. Some studies have reported that A. muciniphila supplementation in mice can promote CRC development [73]. In clinical studies, A. muciniphila has been identified as a CRC-associated biomarker that may promote tumor formation by inducing epithelial proliferation and inflammation [74, 75]. In contrast, the abundance of A. muciniphila was found to be lower in CRC patients with severe symptoms, suggesting it may not contribute to CRC development [76]. Likewise, Zhang et al. reported a negative correlation between A. muciniphila levels and colon cancer development in human and animal studies [77]. Despite these conflicting findings, the combination of A. muciniphila and Clostridium butyricum (C. butyricum) synergistically enhances anti-PD1 therapy efficacy in CRC models, compared to either strain alone [78].

Quasi-experimental studies in humans indicated that live Bifidobacterium tablets combined with immune checkpoint inhibitors improved clinical outcomes in patients with metastatic CRC [79]. However, despite these encouraging findings, robust clinical evidence supporting the therapeutic efficacy of probiotics in CRC remains limited, underscoring the need for further well-designed translational and clinical studies before their routine clinical application [80]. A key concern in this field is the possible horizontal transfer of drug-resistance genes from probiotic strains to the resident gut microbiota [81]. Ultimately, elucidating the mechanisms governing probiotic safety and efficacy is essential for their successful integration into standard CRC management. Fecal microbiota transplantation

Fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) has emerged as a promising strategy to restore the intestinal microbial balance and enhance cancer immunotherapy [88, 89]. In CRC, the source of the transplanted microbiota plays a crucial role in therapeutic outcomes. Transplantation of microbiota from CRC patients reduces the responsiveness of CRC to immunotherapy [90]. However, fecal transplants from patients with high F. nucleatum levels paradoxically increased anti-PD1 sensitivity in MSS CRC mice [91]. Consistent with this finding, human studies have also shown that elevated levels of F. nucleatum correlated with improved response to PD-1 blockade therapy in patients with CRC [92]. In a phase II clinical trial, combining FMT with tislelizumab (a PD-1 inhibitor) and fruquintinib demonstrated improved survival and manageable safety in patients with metastatic MSS CRC [93]. Furthermore, updated clinical data revealed that elevated levels of peripheral gamma-delta (γδ2) T-cells correlated with improved response, prolonged survival, and sustained antitumor effects, with a stable safety profile [94].

Building on these findings, combining FMT with anti-PD1 therapy has shown superior therapeutic benefits compared to either treatment alone, leading to significantly improved survival and tumor control in CRC mice [89]. Furthermore, FMT from CRC mice treated with A. muciniphila and C. butyricum similarly reduced tumor load and prolonged survival in recipient mice, reinforcing the therapeutic potential of microbiota modulation in CRC [78]. Supporting clinical evidence includes a case report describing successful treatment of a 57-year-old patient with MSS CRC using a combination of FMT, anti-PD1, and anti-vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) therapies [95].

Despite its therapeutic promise, FMT raises significant safety concerns, especially in immunocompromised individuals [96]. One major challenge is the selection of an optimal and healthy donor, which necessitates rigorous screening procedures [97]. FMT may carry potential risks, including the transmission of drug-resistant bacterial strains and unidentified pathogens [98]. Studies have also reported that FMT from a donor with diabetes, obesity, or other metabolic disorders may transfer these conditions to the recipient [99, 100]. In light of these concerns, the current literature emphasizes the need for standardized protocols and well-designed clinical trials to better define and substantiate the role of FMT in the management of CRC [96]. Gut microbiome-targeted drug therapy in CRC

Targeting gut inflammation and microbiota dysbiosis, the key drivers of CRC, is crucial for effective therapy and preventing metastasis [101]. Preclinical studies have demonstrated that modulation of the gut microbiota can significantly influence immunotherapy responses in CRC [102]. One promising approach involves disrupting bacterial quorum sensing, which alters microbial communication pathways linked to cancer progression and metastasis [103]. Targeting specific pathogenic bacteria, such as F. nucleatum, has shown promising therapeutic potential in CRC mice. Nitroreductase enzymes convert specific compounds into assemblies that agglutinate and eradicate F. nucleatum, effectively suppressing tumor growth [104]. LPS, a component of gram-negative bacteria abundant in the gut microbiome, is linked to CRC tissue and a reduced response to anti-PDL1 therapy. In animal models, eliminating gram-negative bacteria or blocking LPS-mediated toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4) signaling alleviates the immunosuppressive TME and enhances T-cell infiltration into CRC tumors [105].

Fucoxanthin, a xanthophyll carotenoid, beneficially modulates the gut microbiota in CRC mice by reducing the abundance of Bacteroidales and Rikenellaceae while promoting beneficial Lachnospiraceae. These changes are associated with decreased colorectal adenocarcinoma multiplicity and increased expression of apoptotic caspase-3 [106]. Similarly, oral dextran-aspirin nanomedicine (P3C-Asp) restored microbial balance by increasing the abundance of immune-associated bacteria such as Lactobacillus and Akkermansia, while reducing pathogenic Bacteroides in mice [101].

Traditional Chinese medicine (TCM) represents another promising approach for modulating the gut microbiota to prevent CRC (Table 2). In a phase II clinical trial, the TCM formulation Quxie capsule (QX), when combined with standard therapies such as chemotherapy, favorably alters the gut microbiota composition of CRC patients. It reduced carcinogenic bacteria, such as Flavonifractor and Butyricimonas, while increasing the abundance of beneficial anticancer taxa, such as Lachnospiraceae [107–109].

Dietary composition is another major determinant of CRC risk through its influence on gut microbiota modulation and function [120]. High-fiber diets and nutritional supplements are linked to a reduced risk of CRC in humans [121, 122]. Conversely, low-fiber, processed food–rich diets are linked to enrichment of CRC-associated microbes such as F. nucleatum, B. fragilis, and pks + E. coli, in both human and animal models [123, 124]. Certain dietary components, such as beans, have demonstrated the ability to beneficially modulate the gut microbiome and regulate host biomarkers linked to CRC in humans [125]. Recent animal studies further showed that diet-shaped microbiota can causally influence CRC risk, with microbiota linked to a pesco-vegetarian diet offering strong protection, whereas those associated with red and processed meat consumption increase susceptibility [126].

Conclusion and future perspectives

The gut microbiome plays a pivotal role in CRC by influencing tumor initiation, progression, and therapeutic response. Accumulating evidence from preclinical, translational, and clinical studies highlights the pivotal role of microbiota in shaping the efficacy of ICIs, particularly in MSS CRC, where treatment options remain limited. Microbial composition, diversity, and functional activity collectively contribute to inter-individual variability in treatment outcomes, underscoring the microbiome as a therapeutic target. Despite these advances, important gaps remain in translating microbiome research into clinical practice. Current evidence is limited by small sample sizes, study heterogeneity, and a lack of standardized methods, while safety concerns such as antimicrobial resistance transfer require further evaluation. Future research should focus on large, well-designed clinical and multicohort studies to validate microbiome-based CRC therapies. In addition, the development of precision microbiome interventions such as defined microbial consortia, engineered probiotics, and targeted microbial metabolites holds promise for enhancing treatment efficacy while minimizing adverse effects. Dosing standardization, delivery systems, and patient stratification criteria will be crucial for reproducibility and clinical translation. Overall, leveraging microbiome-based intervention in combination with current standards of care holds significant potential to advance personalized immunotherapy and improve outcomes for patients with CRC.

Nevertheless, this study is presented as a narrative review and does not involve a systematic literature search or direct comparative analysis of existing studies. Therefore, the conclusions may not be exhaustive and could reflect the author’s interpretation of the available evidence.
