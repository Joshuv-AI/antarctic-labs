Patient-derived organoids for personalized drug response profiling and multi-omics integration in oral squamous cell carcinoma
Authors: Pengyu Li, Nenghao Jin, Ning Zhang, Chunhui Yu, Lingling E, Wei Song, Lei Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryOral squamous cell carcinoma (OSCC) exhibits marked interpatient variability in chemotherapy response, with no reliable predictive tools, hindering personalized care. We established a biobank of patient-derived organoids (PDOs) from 10 patients with OSCC, which retained key histopathological and genetic features of primary tumors. These PDOs recapitulated clinical chemotherapy response diversity. Multi-omics analyses (genomics, transcriptomics, drug sensitivity) identified molecular signatures linked to chemotherapy resistance. Critically, PDO drug responses closely matched patients’ clinical outcomes, validating their potential to predict treatment efficacy. Additionally, PDOs highlighted targeting the JAK-STAT pathway as a promising strategy to overcome resistance. Our findings support OSCC PDOs as a robust tool to guide personalized treatment decisions and improve OSCC patient outcomes.

Introduction

Oral squamous cell carcinoma (OSCC) accounts for over 90% of oral malignancies and remains a significant clinical challenge, particularly in advanced stages.1,2 Despite standard first-line treatments combining surgical resection with perioperative chemotherapy (using agents such as paclitaxel (PTX), platinum compounds, and fluorouracil), the 5-year overall survival rate remains below 60%.3,4,5 A critical barrier to improving outcomes is marked intratumoral heterogeneity, which leads to highly variable chemotherapy responses among patients.6 The underlying mechanisms driving this variability are incompletely understood, and clinically validated tools to predict drug efficacy or guide individualized sensitivity assessments are lacking, often resulting in ineffective treatments and avoidable toxicities.

Over the past decade, multi-omics studies (genomics, transcriptomics, epigenomics) have advanced our understanding of OSCC biology.6,7,8 Large-scale sequencing has identified recurrent mutations in tumor suppressors such as TP53, FAT1, and CDKN2A, while transcriptomic analyses have uncovered subtype-specific expression profiles and immune signatures.9,10,11 Aberrant activation of key signaling pathways (e.g., PI3K-AKT, Wnt/β-catenin, and JAK-STAT) has also been linked to tumor progression and therapeutic resistance.12,13,14 However, a critical gap remains: These molecular insights have not translated into actionable markers for predicting drug response. Current clinical decisions rely primarily on TNM staging and histopathological grading, which fail to capture the molecular complexity underlying interindividual differences in drug sensitivity.15,16 Even well-characterized mutations (e.g., TP53) lack effective targeted therapeutic strategies, and intrinsic/acquired resistance to first-line agents (e.g., cisplatin (CIS) and 5-fluorouracil (5-FU)) persists, highlighting the need for better tools to bridge molecular findings and clinical practice.17,18

Patient-derived organoids (PDOs) have emerged as a powerful ex vivo model, which has partially addressed this gap.19 By preserving the spatial architecture, genetic features, and phenotypic traits of original tumors, PDOs offer a platform to integrate multi-omics data and directly assess drug responses, with results that correlate with clinical outcomes.20,21 While OSCC PDO models have been established and characterized for morphology, molecular fidelity, and preliminary drug sensitivity, systematic analyses linking PDO-based drug responses to multi-omics profiles for personalized treatment remain limited, with slow clinical translation.22,23,24,25

In this study, we established a PDO biobank derived from a clinically heterogeneous cohort of patients with OSCC. We systematically integrated histological, genomic, transcriptomic, and drug sensitivity data to comprehensively assess the fidelity and functional relevance of these models at both the molecular and functional levels. Importantly, we identified specific transcriptomic signatures closely associated with chemotherapy resistance and demonstrated that the drug sensitivity data from PDO models accurately reflect the clinical treatment outcomes of the corresponding patients. Our findings further underscore the reliability and clinical potential of PDO models in predicting drug treatment responses and uncovering resistance mechanisms.

Results

In vitro, OSCC PDOs showed progressive growth: Time-course imaging revealed the gradual formation of compact spheroidal structures within 15 days (Figure 1C). Quantitative proliferation assays confirmed stable growth kinetics across all PDO lines over a 30-day culture period, with no loss of proliferative capacity (Figure 1D). Additionally, PDOs derived from different anatomical sites (tongue, buccal mucosa, and gingiva) all achieved successful formation and expansion, retaining site-specific morphological features through early to late passages (P2–P3) and supporting long-term in vitro culture (Figure 1E). These findings demonstrate the feasibility and scalability of OSCC PDO models, highlighting their potential as physiologically relevant preclinical platforms for investigating tumor heterogeneity, drug responses, and biomarker discovery.

OSCC PDOs retain histological features of primary tumors but exhibit distinct transcriptomic profiles

To verify whether OSCC PDOs preserve the structural and molecular characteristics of their matched primary tumors, we conducted histological and transcriptomic analyses. Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining showed that organoids maintained epithelial architecture and cytological traits comparable to their corresponding tumor tissues across anatomical sites (buccal mucosa, gingiva, and tongue (Figure 2A). Immunohistochemical staining further confirmed that both tumor tissues and PDOs expressed key epithelial and squamous markers (cytokeratin 6, cytokeratin 5), and tumor protein p63 (TP63) and the proliferation marker MKI67 at comparable levels. To assess the fidelity of tumor-specific gene expression programs in PDOs, we performed bulk RNA sequencing on matched tissue-organoid pairs. Unsupervised hierarchical clustering and differential expression analysis revealed a high degree of molecular consistency between tissues and their corresponding PDOs, particularly in the expression of epithelial lineage markers and core oncogenic pathways (Figure 2B). Both tissue and organoid samples shared strongly expressed genes critical for epithelial identity and tumor proliferation.

Notably, we observed specific transcriptomic differences, primarily reflecting the absence of stromal, vascular, and immune cell components in PDO cultures, consistent with the purified epithelial nature of organoids. Gene Ontology Biological Process (GO-BP) analysis supported this: Tissue-enriched signatures were associated with immune-related processes, while PDOs showed enrichment in tumor cell-intrinsic metabolic activities (Figure 2C). These findings highlight that while OSCC PDOs capture the core transcriptional landscape of their parental tumors, they also exhibit distinct transcriptomic profiles shaped by their simplified, epithelium-focused composition, making them a valuable model for studying tumor-intrinsic biology.

Patient-derived OSCC organoids retain genomic alterations and core tumor-driving pathways

To further evaluate the preservation of tumor-specific molecular features in OSCC-PDOs, we compared the genomic mutational landscapes of OSCC-PDOs and their matched primary tumors using whole-genome sequencing. Somatic mutations were detected in both tissues and organoids, with missense mutations being the most frequent, followed by nonsense, splice site, and frameshift variants (Figure 3A). The distribution of mutation types was highly similar between tumors and PDOs, indicating that organoid culture does not introduce significant artifactual mutagenesis and preserves OSCC’s core mutational signatures. Tumor mutational burden (TMB) did not differ significantly between primary tissues and matched PDOs, indicating that global mutational complexity is largely preserved during in vitro expansion. These results confirmed that PDOs maintain tumor-specific mutational landscapes over in vitro propagation (Figure 3B). Single-base substitution (SBS) profiles were stable between tumors and PDOs. PDOs and the primary tumor also showed similarity with respect to the overall trend in the type of point mutations, supporting faithful capture of the tumor mutational process in organoid cultures (Figure 3C). Next, we analyzed mutations in key cancer-related genes. Recurrently altered drivers, including TP53, FAT1, PIK3CA, and CDKN2A, were consistently detected in both tissues and PDOs, with every case harboring at least one known driver mutation (Figure 3D). This confirms the conservation of canonical oncogenic drivers, validating PDO for studying molecular vulnerabilities and pathway-targeted therapies in OSCC. Variant allele frequency (VAF) analysis revealed strong correlations between individual tumors and their matched PDOs (Figure 3E), with most lines showing Pearson’s correlation coefficients >0.7 (Figure 3F). This high allelic concordance underscores PDOs’ clonal representativeness and potential for tracking subclonal dynamics under therapeutic pressure. Next, we examined the preservation of copy number variations (CNVs). Genome-wide CNV events showed remarkable concordance between matched pairs (Figures S1A–S1D), although some heterogeneity was observed. Key oncogenic amplifications (CCND1, MYC, EGFR) were retained in the majority of PDO-tumor pairs (Figure 3G). To explore pathway-level mutation patterns, we performed KEGG pathway analysis on mutated genes identified in the primary tumors. This descriptive visualization highlighted frequent alterations in multiple signaling pathways linked to tumorigenesis and chemoresistance, including JAK-STAT, MAPK, and TP53 (Figure 3H). Patient-derived organoids predict drug response in oral squamous cell carcinoma

To evaluate OSCC PDOs as a tool for guiding personalized chemotherapy, we performed drug screening with four clinically relevant agents: CIS, PTX, 5-FU, and nimotuzumab (NT). PDOs displayed heterogeneous responses, as shown by diverse dose-response curves and normalized area under the curve (AUC) values (Figures 4A–4D). Based on IC50 values, the 10 PDO lines were classified into sensitive and non-sensitive groups (n = 5 each). Among the monotherapies, 5-FU showed the strongest cytotoxicity across the cohort, with significant differences in AUC values between sensitive and non-sensitive groups (p < 0.01), though the magnitude of this difference was smaller than that of other agents. CIS and PTX demonstrated moderate efficacy, with subsets of PDOs showing clear sensitivity versus marked resistance. In contrast, NT had minimal single-agent cytotoxicity in most PDOs, indicating limited monotherapy potential. Morphological changes aligned with viability data: Time-lapse imaging showed progressive shrinkage and disintegration of PDOs treated with CIS, PTX, and 5-FU, whereas NT-treated and control PDOs retained structural integrity (Figure 4E), confirming the robustness of PDO assays in capturing functional drug responses. Quantitative IC50 analyses revealed distinct sensitivity profiles: For example, PDO line T2 was resistant to all agents, while T8 and T10 showed marked sensitivity to PTX and 5-FU (Figure 4F). These patterns mirror clinical variability in chemotherapy outcomes, reinforcing PDOs’ potential to stratify patients by therapeutic susceptibility. Subsequently, we evaluated three clinically established combination chemotherapeutic regimens: TP (CIS + PTX), TPF (CIS + PTX + 5-FU), and TP + NT. All combinations enhanced growth inhibition compared to monotherapies, with the TP + NT showing consistent efficacy across lines (Figures 4G–4I). AUC analyses confirmed superior potency of combinations, particularly TP + NT and TPF (Figure 4J), supporting PDOs as a platform to optimize synergistic, personalized regimens.

A retrospective case study (patient T4) further validated clinical relevance (Figure 4K). The patient with tongue cancer received neoadjuvant PTX + NT (partial tumor regression, enabling resection), followed by CIS + NT (disease progression with lymph node metastasis), and subsequent TP + NT (treatment failure). Ex vivo screening of T4 PDOs aligned with this course: moderate sensitivity to CIS and PTX monotherapy, but limited benefit from combinations (high AUC values, minimal growth inhibition). This concordance between ex vivo and clinical outcomes underscores PDOs’ potential to anticipate resistance and guide alternative strategies for refractory disease. However, this remains as a single case by far. The potential of PDOs in personalizing OSCC therapeutics will require more robustly tested and systematically explored in larger and more diverse cohorts. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that OSCC PDOs capture inter-patient variability in drug responses, enable functional validation of combination therapies, and align with clinical outcomes, supporting their utility as a predictive tool for personalized OSCC treatment.

Transcriptomic profiling identifies gene signatures and pathways linked to chemotherapy sensitivity and resistance

To uncover the molecular mechanisms driving differential chemotherapy responses in OSCC PDOs, we performed transcriptomic analyses comparing PDOs classified as sensitive or resistant to CIS, 5-FU, and PTX. Unsupervised clustering and differential expression profiling revealed distinct transcriptional signatures between sensitive and resistant groups for each drug.

For CIS, heatmap analysis identified differentially enriched gene clusters in each subgroup (Figure 5A). Sensitive PDOs showed the upregulation of genes including E2F7, SBK3, SYT13, and LCP1, while resistant lines exhibited elevated expression of H19, TRIM58, and EPHA3. Volcano plot analysis confirmed statistically significant transcriptional differences between phenotypes (Figure 5B), indicating distinct gene regulatory programs underpin CIS susceptibility. KEGG pathway enrichment analysis further highlighted functional divergence: CIS-resistant PDOs showed the activation of oncogenic pathways such as JAK-STAT, whereas sensitive PDOs were enriched in PI3K-Akt and NF-κB signaling (Figure 5C). These findings suggest that CIS resistance may be driven by pro-tumor survival signaling, while sensitivity correlates with apoptotic and inflammatory stress pathways, identifying actionable vulnerabilities in resistant tumors via pathway-specific inhibitors. For 5-FU, distinct gene expression patterns also separated sensitive and resistant PDOs (Figure 5D). Resistant PDOs upregulated IGF2BP1, NBPF6, and ANKUB1, while sensitive organoids showed higher expression of DSG1, VIT, and PNMA8A (Figure 5E). Functional enrichment, such as 5-FU resistance to the activation of pro-inflammatory pathways (IL-17, TNF, and cytokine-cytokine receptor interactions), whereas sensitivity correlated with amino acid biosynthesis and metabolic regulation (Figure 5F). These results imply inflammatory reprogramming and immune evasion may be core features of 5-FU resistance, while metabolic dependency underlies sensitivity. For PTX, sensitive and resistant PDOs displayed similar transcriptional divergence. Resistant lines upregulated stress response-related genes (e.g., GSTM1, STS15A2, and NTM), while sensitive PDOs were enriched for genes linked to the cell cycle and metabolic regulation (Figures 5G and 5H). Pathway analysis confirmed the activation of survival pathways (Notch and Wnt) in resistant PDOs, whereas sensitive lines showed enrichment in PPAR signaling and retinol metabolism, pathways critical for cellular differentiation and stress adaptation (Figure 5I). These tumor-selective expression patterns support the functional relevance of these genes and pathways as candidate biomarkers for stratifying drug responses. JAK-STAT pathway inhibition enhances cisplatin sensitivity in drug-resistant organoids

To functionally validate the role of JAK-STAT signaling in cisplatin resistance, we focused on T4 (cisplatin-resistant) and T5 (cisplatin-sensitive) organoid lines. Immunohistochemical staining confirmed elevated levels of phosphorylated JAK (p-JAK) and phosphorylated STAT (p-STAT) in T4 organoids, indicating activation of the JAK-STAT signaling pathway in this resistant subgroup (Figures 6A and 6B), supporting its potential role in driving cisplatin resistance in OSCC. Treatment with the JAK-STAT pathway inhibitor C188-9 reduced viability in T4 organoids markedly, while T5 organoids showed minimal response (Figure 6C). Quantitative analysis confirmed this differential sensitivity: T4 organoids exhibited a significantly lower IC50 (0.17 μM) and normalized AUC compared to T5 (IC50 = 11.25 μM) (Figure 6D), highlighting C188-9's specificity for JAK-STAT-activated, cisplatin-resistant tumors. These findings align with our transcriptomic data (Figure 5), reinforcing the link between JAK-STAT activation and cisplatin resistance. C188-9 and cisplatin combined treatment exhibited enhanced cytotoxicity against both PDO lines (T4 and T5). However, C188-9 specifically re-sensitized T4 to cisplatin, highlighting the potential of inhibiting the JAK-STAT pathway in overcoming cisplatin resistance in chemotherapy (Figures 6E and 6F). Discussion

Compared to traditional cancer cell lines and tumor xenograft models, PDO models better preserve the heterogeneity and complexity of tumors, offering a more accurate representation of the patient’s individual pathological and genetic characteristics.20,21 In this study, we successfully established a biobank of OSCC PDOs, comprising organoid lines derived from tumors of 10 patients with OSCC following surgical resection. These organoid lines were sourced from tumor samples from various anatomical locations. We performed a detailed evaluation of the phenotypic and genetic features of the OSCC PDOs and confirmed their high degree of similarity to the corresponding primary tumor tissues in terms of histological characteristics, protein expression, and genomic consistency. Notably, the PDO model effectively predicted chemotherapy responses in patients with OSCC. Therefore, OSCC organoids are not only suitable for cancer research but also provide a powerful tool for personalized treatment, particularly for drug response evaluation and optimization of clinical treatment strategies.

Genomic integrity was evaluated using whole-genome sequencing. Compared with primary tumors, the somatic mutation profiles, tumor mutation burden (TMB), and SBS signatures in PDOs were largely preserved. Recurrent driver alterations (MAPK12, ZMIZ2, SPAG5)26,27,28 were consistently retained, and VAFs showed good correlation between matched pairs. Copy number profiles exhibited substantial but non-uniform concordance; key oncogenic amplifications (CCND1, MYC, EGFR) were maintained in the majority of pairs, although some heterogeneity was observed. This heterogeneity is likely due to the selective expansion of dominant tumor clones, the absence of the tumor microenvironment (TME), and technical variability in detecting low-abundance subclonal CNVs. Pathway-level analysis of mutated genes highlighted frequent alterations in JAK-STAT, MAPK, and TP53 signaling, consistent with the known molecular features of OSCC.29,30,31 These findings demonstrate that OSCC PDOs capture core epithelial and genomic characteristics of their parental primary tumors while reflecting inter-patient heterogeneity.

Consistent with several previous studies, we validated the effectiveness of using OSCC PDOs (patient-derived organoids from patients with OSCC) for drug sensitivity testing and revealed significant heterogeneity in chemotherapy responses across different patients, thereby establishing a robust PDO-based drug screening system.25,32,33 Previous studies have reported the use of OSCC organoid morphological features to guide clinical classification, predict tumor immune responses, and employ oral mucosal organoids as platforms for personalized treatment.22,23,34,35 These studies underscore the potential of organoid models in understanding the morphological differences in OSCC tumors and their application in personalized therapy. In this study, we extended previous work by integrating multi-omics datasets with PDO-based drug sensitivity profiles, which overcame the limitations of unidimensional studies and enables a deeper understanding of tumor biology and drug resistance mechanisms. Transcriptomic comparison revealed broad conservation of gene expression between PDOs and primary tissues, although immune-related pathways were more prominent in tumors and metabolic pathways in organoids—a pattern in line with previous reports.36,37 Multi-omics integration identified gene signatures associated with CIS sensitivity and resistance. In sensitive organoids, tumour-suppressor genes, including TP53 and LCP1, were upregulated, whereas proliferation- and invasion-related genes (e.g., UNC13C and ALPL) were enriched in resistant lines.38,39,40,41

The JAK-STAT pathway, suggested by our transcriptomic analyses, appeared specifically hyperactivated in the resistant group carrying a higher mutation load of related genes. Thus, aberrant JAK-STAT signaling, involved in tumor development and associated with therapeutic resistance, likely contributes to CIS resistance in OSCC.42,43 A patient (T4), insensitive to CIS, experienced relapse and metastasis as a result of a failed response to the CIS-based adjuvant combined chemotherapy. Our transcriptomic analyses suggested that the JAK-STAT pathway underlies the resistance to CIS, which was validated by the subsequent therapeutic profiling. Importantly, inhibiting the JAK-STAT pathway with C188-9 restored the PDO model’s CIS sensitivity (Figure 6), thus presenting new opportunities in overcoming clinical chemotherapeutic resistance. However, its clinical relevance requires further validation, for example, testing if C188-9 sensitizes PDOs to combined therapies containing CIS in more extended clinical cohorts, which would greatly facilitate the application of PDOs in personalized medicines for OSCC.

Limitations of the study

This study has several limitations. First, the current OSCC PDO biobank includes only 10 PDO lines with a relatively small sample size and limited clinical heterogeneity, which may restrict the generalizability of the identified drug-response signatures and resistance mechanisms. A larger, multi-center, and more diverse patient cohort is required to further validate the predictive value of PDOs and facilitate clinical translation. Second, the PDO culture system represents a pure epithelial tumor model that lacks key components of the TME, including cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs), immune cells (T cells, macrophages, neutrophils), vascular endothelial cells, and extracellular matrix interactions. These factors critically modulate tumor proliferation, invasion, and therapeutic responses in vivo, so the current model cannot fully recapitulate TME-mediated drug resistance or immune-related therapeutic effects. Third, long-term in vitro culture may cause mild clonal selection in PDOs, leading to subtle discrepancies in CNVs and subclonal architecture compared with primary tumors, particularly for low-abundance subclones. Although core driver mutations and major genomic features are well preserved, such drift may slightly compromise the accuracy of drug-response prediction for minor subclones. Finally, the current workflow for organoid establishment and drug testing requires 7–14 days for organoid formation plus additional days for drug screening, which may not meet the tight therapeutic window for neoadjuvant or urgent palliative treatment. Further optimization of culture efficiency and high-throughput screening platforms is needed to shorten the turnaround time for clinical implementation.

Experimental model and study participant details

Tumor specimens were obtained from 14 patients diagnosed with oral squamous cell carcinoma (OSCC) at Chinese PLA General Hospital. Tumor tissues were collected during routine surgical resection procedures with informed consent, and the study was approved by the Chinese PLA General Hospital’s ethics committee (S2024-370-02). The patient cohort consisted of (specific details about age, gender, tumor location, etc.), and clinical data, including TNM stage, tumor differentiation, and lymphatic metastasis status, were recorded for each patient. Detailed clinical characteristics of the patients are summarized in Table S1. Tumor samples were immediately processed for the generation of patient-derived organoid (PDO) cultures.

Method details

PDO culture and biobank establishment

To generate patient-derived organoids (PDOs) from oral squamous cell carcinoma (OSCC), tumor specimens were collected from 14 OSCC patients undergoing surgical resection. tumor tissues were minced and enzymatically digested using collagenase IV (Invitrogen, Cat No. 17104019) and hyaluronidase (Sigma-Aldrich, Cat No. H3506) for 30–60 min at 37°C. The resulting cell suspension was filtered through a 100-μm cell strainer (Corning, Cat No. 431751) and then embedded in a 3D Matrigel matrix (Hsienseng, HS03001-01). Like Matrigel, HS03001-01 is derived from the Engelbreth-Holm-Swarm (EHS) mouse sarcoma and mimics the extracellular matrix and supports 3D cultures. Cultures were maintained in organoid culture media (Hsienseng, HS01013-01). This medium is a commercially optimized, ready-to-use formulation for epithelial tumor organoid culture, consisting of Advanced DMEM/F12 (Thermo Fisher Scientific) as the basal medium, supplemented with 1× B-27 Supplement (Thermo Fisher Scientific), 1× N-2 Supplement (Thermo Fisher Scientific), 10 mM HEPES (Thermo Fisher Scientific), 2 mM GlutaMAX (Thermo Fisher Scientific), 1 mM N-acetylcysteine (Sigma-Aldrich), 50 ng/mL recombinant human EGF (PeproTech or equivalent), 10 ng/mL recombinant human FGF10 (PeproTech or equivalent), 10 ng/mL recombinant human FGF2 (PeproTech or equivalent), 500 ng/mL recombinant human R-spondin1 (PeproTech or equivalent), 100 ng/mL recombinant human Noggin (PeproTech or equivalent), 100 μg/mL Primocin (InvivoGen), 10 μM Y-27632 (Selleck Chemicals), and 100 U/mL penicillin +100 μg/mL streptomycin (Thermo Fisher Scientific). All cultures were maintained at 37°C with 5% CO2, and media were replenished every 2–3 days. PDOs were passaged every 7–14 days once they reached 60% confluence, at which point they were mechanically disrupted and passed into fresh Matrigel at a ratio of 1:3. For long-term expansion, PDOs were maintained in the same growth medium, and fresh Matrigel was added every 3–4 passages. Organoids were cryopreserved in a specialized medium (Hsienseng, HS030012-01) at −80°C overnight before being transferred into liquid nitrogen for long-term storage. To recover frozen PDOs, cryovials were thawed in a 37°C water bath for 2 min, and the organoid suspension was immediately transferred to a 15 mL conical tube containing 10 mL of pre-warmed organoid medium. The suspension was centrifuged at 300 × g for 5 min, and the pellet was resuspended in fresh Matrigel to initiate culture. Approximately 1 × 10ˆ6 primary cells were plated in each well (6-well plate) to initiate organoid culture. Organoid growth was monitored for 7–10 days post-recovery to ensure viability and integrity.

Histological analysis and immunohistochemistry

Tumor tissues were collected from surgical resection and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 24 h. Organoids (up to passage 3) were collected by centrifugation (300 g, 5 min), washed with cold PBS, and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 60 min at room temperature. To facilitate paraffin processing, fixed organoids were pre-embedded in low-melting agarose (3% in PBS). Fixed tissues and pre-embedded organoids were dehydrated through graded ethanol washes (70%, 80%, 95%, 100% ethanol, 30 min each), cleared in xylene (2 × 30 min), and infiltrated with paraffin wax at 60°C (2 × 60 min).

Serial sections were cut at a thickness of 5 μm using a rotary microtome. Paraffin sections were mounted on glass slides, dried, and deparaffinized in xylene followed by rehydration through a reverse ethanol series to water.

Prepared sections were then subjected to hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining for morphology. For immunohistochemistry (IHC), antigen retrieval was performed using citrate buffer (pH 6.0) in a pressure cooker or water bath. Sections were blocked for non-specific binding, and stained for respective markers using following primary antibodies: TP63 (Abcam, ab735), MKI67 (Abcam, ab15580), CK5 (Cell Signaling Technology, 71536S), CK6 (Abcam, ab93279), P-STAT (Cell Signaling Technology, 9145S), and P-JAK (Abcam, ab32101). All the slides were counterstained with hematoxylin, dehydrated, cleared, and coverslipped for imaging.

RNA sequencing and transcriptomic analysis

Total RNA was extracted from PDOs and quantified using NanoDrop 2000. RNA-Seq libraries were constructed following a standard Illumina RNA-Seq protocol. Sequencing was conducted on the Illumina NovaSeq platform, generating paired-end reads with a length of 150 bp. Raw FASTQ format data were filtered and trimmed using Trimmomatic and mapped to the complete human reference genome (hg38) using STAR. Read aligned counts to genes were calculated using featureCounts, which enabled differential gene expression analysis DESeq2R package. Enrichment analysis was performed using clusterProfiler for Gene Ontology (GO), Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG), and Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA).

Genomic profiling

Genomic DNA was isolated from cancer tissues, blood, and cultured organoids using the AllPrep DNA/RNA Mini Kit (Qiagen), following the manufacturer’s protocol. Exonic DNA was enriched by generating WES libraries using the Agilent SureSelect Human All Exon V6 Kit (Agilent Technologies, CA, USA) for each sample, with the addition of unique index codes for identification. Paired-end sequencing (2 × 150 bp) of the exomes was performed on the Illumina NovaSeq platform, with a sequencing depth of 100× (approximately 12 Gb per sample) for tumor and organoid samples. Adapter removal and filtering of low-quality reads were carried out using Fastp (v0.12.6). Single-nucleotide variants (SNVs) were called using GATK (v4.1.9). Sequencing reads were mapped to the human reference genome (hg38) using Burrows-Wheeler Alignment (BWA-MEM) v0.7.12 with maximum exact matching. Post-alignment, duplicate marking, channel merging, and insertion-deletion re-alignment were performed using Picard (v2.23.8). Matched blood samples were used as references for calling somatic SNVs and insertions/deletions in tumor and organoid samples using Mutect2 (default settings). Somatic SNVs with a variant allele frequency (VAF) < 0.05 and supporting fewer than three reads were filtered out. Mutation effect predictions and annotations were performed using VEP (version 101). Copy number variation (CNV) was detected using TitanCNA (1.30.0). The clonal structure and VAF of mutation sites in organoids and corresponding tumors were inferred using sciClone.

Drug sensitivity testing and chemotherapy profiling

For the quantitative proliferation assay of PDOs (Figure 1D), P2-P3 OSCC PDOs were stained with CellTrace Red CMTPX (5 μM) at 37°C for 30 min with gentle shaking, and unbound dye was removed by three washes with pre-warmed complete medium at 300 × g for 5 min each. Labeled PDOs were plated in deep-well cell counting plates, and fluorescent images were acquired under a 10× objective lens using a microscope. Four distinct visual fields were randomly selected for each sample to count PDOs to ensure representativeness. Max-intensity z stack projections of fluorescent images were analyzed using ImageJ (v1.54f), and only dye-labeled PDOs with a diameter of more than 20 μm were included in counting to exclude cell debris and non-valid small cell clusters. Cell numbers at each time point (0, 5, 10, 15, 20, 25, 30 days) were normalized to the Day 0 baseline. All measurements were performed in three biological replicates, and the mean ± standard deviation (SD) was calculated to generate the PDO growth curves.

PDOs at passage 3–5 were tested for sensitivity to cisplatin (CIS, Selleck-S1166; 100 mM stock in sterile water; initial concentration 50 μM; 2-fold serial dilution yielding 50 μM, 25 μM, 12.5 μM, 6.25 μM, 3.125 μM, 1.56 μM, 0.78 μM), paclitaxel (PTX, Selleck-S1150; 10 mM stock in DMSO; initial concentration 10 μM; 10-fold serial dilution yielding 10 μM, 1 μM, 0.1 μM, 0.01 μM, 0.001 μM, 0.0001 μM, 0.00001 μM), 5-fluorouracil (5-FU, Selleck-S1209; 100 mM stock in DMSO; initial concentration 64 μM; 3-fold serial dilution yielding 64 μM, 21.3 μM, 7.1 μM, 2.4 μM, 0.79 μM, 0.26 μM, 0.09 μM), nimotuzumab (NT, Selleck-A2944; 10 mg/mL stock in PBS; initial concentration 400 μg/mL; 2-fold serial dilution yielding 400 μg/mL, 200 μg/mL, 100 μg/mL, 50 μg/mL, 25 μg/mL, 12.5 μg/mL, 6.25 μg/mL), and C188-9 (Selleck-S8605; 10 mM stock in DMSO; initial concentration 200 μM; 3-fold serial dilution yielding 200 μM, 66.7 μM, 22.2 μM, 7.4 μM, 2.5 μM, 0.82 μM, 0.27 μM, 0.09 μM, 0.03 μM). Three clinical combination regimens were also tested: cisplatin + paclitaxel (TP), cisplatin + paclitaxel + 5-FU (TPF), and cisplatin + paclitaxel + nimotuzumab (TP + NT), with the working concentration of each agent in the combination set based on its single-agent IC50 value in the corresponding PDO line at three gradient multiples: N/2 (half the single-agent IC50), N (single-agent IC50), and 2N (twice the single-agent IC50). Dose-response curves were generated by measuring organoid viability using CellTiter-Glo 3D (Promega, G9243). The normalized area under the curve (AUC) and IC50 values were calculated for each PDO line to assess drug sensitivity. PDOs were classified as sensitive (AUC <50% of the cohort mean AUC) or resistant (AUC ≥50% of the cohort mean AUC) for each drug.44

Quantification and statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism v8.0 (GraphPad Software) and R (version 4.0.2). Data are expressed as means ± standard deviation (SD), unless otherwise stated. The normality of data distributions was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. For comparisons between two groups, an unpaired Student’s t test was used for normally distributed data, and the Mann-Whitney U test was used for non-parametric data. For multiple comparisons, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s post-hoc test was used. n represents the number of independent patient-derived organoid lines (n = 10 for most experiments). For drug sensitivity assays, n = 3 technical replicates per PDO line. The significance level was set at ∗p < 0.05; ∗∗p < 0.01; ∗∗∗p < 0.001; ns, not significant.
