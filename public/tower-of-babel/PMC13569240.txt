Synergistic transition from type‐I‐like to type‐II‐like symptomology in a pyrethroid mixture and its impact on knockdown–mortality relationships in Musca domestica L
Authors: Junho Yoon, Jun‐Hyung Tak
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBACKGROUNDKnockdown effect, especially in pyrethroid‐based household and public insecticides, is often considered a practical indicator of insecticidal efficacy. However, whether faster knockdown implies lethality remains uncertain because the relationship between the two effects has not been systematically explored. This study examined whether knockdown speed is associated with mortality across type‐I pyrethroids, type‐II pyrethroids, and organophosphate insecticides in the house fly.RESULTSFor type‐I pyrethroids, faster knockdown did not predict higher mortality. Early knockdown did not necessarily lead to lethality, and no significant differences were observed among LD20 , LD50 , and LD80 treatments. In contrast, type‐II pyrethroid and organophosphate insecticides showed a positive knockdown‐mortality relationship, with most lethal individuals being knocked‐down early. In the mixture study, d‐phenothrin showed an additive interaction with prallethrin but a synergistic interaction with tetramethrin, although all three compounds are type‐I pyrethroids. Computer vision–based analyses using a convolutional neural network (CNN) of symptomology revealed a synergistic shift in intoxication phenotype from type‐I‐like to type‐II‐like symptoms in the synergistic d‐phenothrin and tetramethrin mixture.CONCLUSIONKnockdown speed should not be used as a proxy for lethality for type‐I pyrethroids, highlighting class‐ and mixture‐dependent dynamics between knockdown and mortality. These findings support regulatory and operational decisions that consider knockdown and mortality as distinct performance endpoints, particularly when substantiating claims such as ‘quick kill’ or ‘kills on contact’ for pesticide registration. © 2026 The Author(s). INTRODUCTION

Household and public health insecticides, typically formulated as aerosols or dilutable concentrates for sprays, play a pivotal role to mitigate the risks associated with flying disease vectors including houseflies and mosquitoes. 1 , 2 These products commonly contain synthetic pyrethroids (e.g., d‐phenothrin, prallethrin, tetramethrin, λ‐cyhalothrin, α‐cypermethrin) or organophosphates (e.g., chlorpyrifos, dichlorvos). However, the use of organophosphates is declining due to increasing regulatory restrictions and safety concerns. 3 , 4 Additionally, mixtures of type‐I pyrethroids, such as d‐phenothrin combined with prallethrin or tetramethrin, are frequently used. 5 , 6

The speed at which these insecticides induce knockdown, rapid incapacitation of pests, is particularly important for pest management, minimizing the chances of insects escaping treated areas while providing immediate visual confirmation of effectiveness to users. Recognizing its importance, regulatory agencies such as the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) have developed guidelines to ensure accurate pesticide label claims. Specifically, under 40 CFR 156.10(a) (5), claims such as ‘knockdown,’ ‘quick kill,’ or ‘kills on contact’ can be substantiated based on the knockdown rate measured at specific short intervals (e.g., 30 s). 7 This regulatory approach implies a relationship between rapid knockdown and eventual mortality at the endpoint.

However, rapid knockdown, particularly associated with pyrethroids, does not always lead to mortality. Knocked‐down insects frequently recover, indicating that fast knockdown may not guarantee lethality. 8 , 9 , 10 Previous studies investigated this relationship at the compound level by comparing knockdown potency (e.g., median knockdown dose or time) to toxicity (e.g., median lethal dose or time) across different compounds. Some of these studies suggest the absence of a strict positive correlation between knockdown efficacy and lethality, although their primary focus has typically been on the developmental stages of insecticidal compounds rather than practical implications for end‐use products. 11 , 12 , 13

From regulatory and consumer perspectives, responses in specific active ingredients and their mixture formulations are primarily concerned. Therefore, it is crucial to understand individual‐level variations within a single compound or mixture. Even when exposed to an identical dose of insecticide, individuals within a population may vary considerably in their knockdown speed. Thus, a critical and practical question arises: are insects experiencing faster knockdown indeed more likely to die? Understanding this relationship is essential for establishing appropriate knockdown observation endpoints and ensuring accurate efficacy assessments. It also informs decisions on handling data on individuals not knocked‐down by the end of the exposure period. Determining whether to consider these individuals as alive after exposure or to capture and further observe until the mortality endpoint is important, as regulatory authorities differ in their guidelines regarding this issue. 14 , 15 Furthermore, this understanding is crucial for properly substantiating label claims for fast knockdown, such as ‘quick kill’ and ‘kill on contact’. To our knowledge, no previous studies have addressed this knockdown‐mortality relationship at the individual variation level within compounds.

Knockdown is not a homogeneous phenomenon; rather, it is a dynamic process encompassing a range of symptomatic behaviors. Commonly described symptoms include convulsions, tremors, hyperactivity, hypoactivity, incoordination, prostration, paralysis, and misorientation. 16 , 17 , 18 , 19 , 20 , 21 , 22 , 23 The variability in these symptoms likely arises from differences in modes of action, as distinct neuronal effects are expected to result in distinct behavioral outcomes. 24 , 25 , 26 However, a significant knowledge gap remains due to the lack of quantitative measurement of these behavioral symptoms, as most studies rely on simple observational descriptions without clear definitions or standardized criteria. Therefore, establishing quantitative methods would benefit symptomological studies to be used as supportive evidence in insecticide toxicology.

Conventionally, insect behavior is tracked using physical markers such as stickers, paint, ink, or dust. 27 , 28 However, these marking methods can unintentionally affect insect behavior. 29 Furthermore, traditional marking techniques primarily focus on identifying the location of individuals, limiting behavioral analyses to basic parameters such as movement trajectories. Recent advancements in convolutional neural networks (CNNs) have enabled non‐invasive automated detection and tracking of insect body parts. Using the detected body parts, pose of insect in the frame can be estimated by analyzing their geometric configuration. Then, behavioral changes can be quantitatively assessed through changes in these poses across consecutive frames. Since demonstrating their superior performance in image recognition at the ILSVRC‐2012 competition through AlexNet, CNNs have consistently outperformed other deep learning methods in various image recognition tasks. 30 , 31 Subsequently, numerous CNN architectures have been developed and widely applied across diverse fields including medical imaging, facial recognition, and weather forecasting. 31 , 32 , 33 However, the application of CNNs to quantitatively define symptomologies in insects, as proposed in the present study, has not yet been explored.

The present study aimed to identify the relationship between knockdown speed and endpoint mortality within each insecticide compound using individual‐level bioassays, and to determine whether such relationships differ by dose, compound, and compound class. We further tested commonly used intraclass pyrethroid mixtures to assess whether their patterns followed those of individual compounds. In addition, we applied a CNN‐based quantitative symptomology framework to resolve intoxication phenotypes and detect mixture‐associated behavioral transitions.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Test insects

A susceptible strain of the house fly, Musca domestica, was originally obtained from the Korea Disease Control and Prevention Agency (KDCA) and reared in an insectary at Seoul National University. This strain had not been exposed to any known pesticides for over 10 years. Newly hatched larvae were fed a diet consisting of a rodent diet (Purina 38 057; Purina, St. Louis, MO, USA), oak sawdust, and distilled water at a 9:1:10 ratio by weight. Pupae were collected and transferred into mesh cages (30 × 30 × 30 cm) for adult emergence. Adults were provided with continuous access to a 10% sucrose solution. The colony was maintained under laboratory conditions at 26 ± 2 °C, 20 ± 5% relative humidity (RH), and a photoperiod of 12:12 h (L:D).

Chemicals

Technical‐grade synthetic insecticides were obtained as follows. FarmHannong Co., Ltd. (Seoul, South Korea) supplied permethrin (96.1%), tetramethrin (96.5%), α‐cypermethrin (95.2%), and dichlorvos (95.2%). d‐Phenothrin (> 95%) was obtained from Jiangsu Youjia Plant Protection Co., Ltd. (Jiangsu, China), and chlorpyrifos (96%) from Anhui Fuerpont Chemical Co., Ltd. (Chizhou, China). Prallethrin (94.03%) and λ‐cyhalothrin (95%) were supplied by Sumitomo Chemical Co., Ltd. (Tokyo, Japan). Acetone (99.5%), used as the solvent, was purchased from Daejung Chemicals & Metals Co., Ltd. (Siheung, South Korea).

Topical application

In each replication, groups of 10 adult female flies were anesthetized using medical‐grade CO2 and placed onto a chilling plate (Model 1431, Bioquip, Rancho Dominguez, CA, USA) to induce chill coma for treatment. Test solutions were prepared by dissolving each test compound in acetone. A 1.5 μL aliquot of each solution was applied to the notum of each fly using a Hamilton Microliter Syringe (7636–01, 25 μL; Hamilton Company, Reno, NV, USA) coupled with a repeating dispenser (PB 600; Hamilton Company). After treatment, the flies were transferred to clean cups (Ф 8 × 12 cm) and provided with cotton soaked in 1 mL of a 10% sucrose solution. Treated flies were held under the same conditions described above for 24 h, after which mortality was assessed. A fly was considered dead when it failed to respond to gentle prodding. Each compound was tested at seven to 12 different doses, with five replicates per dose. The dose–response was analyzed using a probit analysis using SPSS 29.0 (IBM Co., Armonk, NY, US).

Knockdown‐mortality relationship

To measure knockdown speed, adult female flies were anesthetized by inducing chill coma with ice and then placed individually in confined Petri dishes (Φ 35 mm × h 10 mm, SPL Life Sciences, Pocheon, South Korea). To minimize potential cold‐shock effects, topical application was conducted after full recovery from anesthesia. Flies were gently restrained with forceps, and topically treated with LD20, LD50, or LD80 doses of each insecticide. After treatment, each fly was subsequently transferred to a separate clean cup (Φ 80 mm × h 115 mm) after treatment and observed to determine knockdown time, defined as the time at which the insect was unable to right itself after turning over. Mortality was assessed at 24 h post‐treatment. The association between knockdown time and mortality, while accounting for insecticide dose and compound class, was analyzed using a generalized linear mixed model with a negative binomial distribution and identity link in SAS 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, North Carolina, USA). A negative binomial model was used to accommodate overdispersed count‐like knockdown time data, and model fit was assessed using the Pearson chi‐square/degree‐of‐freedom ratio; the fitted model showed no evidence of problematic residual overdispersion (Pearson chi‐square/df = 0.95). Between 127 and 169 flies were tested for each dose of insecticides.

Video recording of intoxication symptoms

Prior to video recording, each fly was anesthetized on ice and had its wings clipped. Wing clipping was performed to facilitate stable 2D ventral‐view recording by reducing out‐of‐plane movement during pose estimation. After recovery from chill coma Petri dish, each fly received a topical application of the LD50 dose of the corresponding insecticide. The fly was then carefully placed using forceps into a lid‐less Petri dish (Φ 150 mm × h 20 mm) and covered with the lid of a small Petri dish (Φ 35 mm × h 3.5 mm). The shallow height of the lid prevented the flies from crawling upside down while still permitting them enough space to turn over, enabling clear determination of the knockdown endpoint. The ventral side of each fly was recorded using a 4 K UHD webcam (Model APC1000, ABKO Co., Ltd., Seoul, South Korea) mounted upside down at a distance of 150 mm below the dish (Fig. 1(a)). Video recordings were captured at a frame rate of 20–30 fps, with a resolution of 840 × 750 pixels, and continued until the insect reached knockdown (turned over). To control for potential effects of circadian rhythm, all recordings were performed during the light phase of the photoperiod.

Training of CNNs and quantification of poisoning symptoms

The convolutional neural network (CNN) model ResNet 101 was trained to detect the body parts of house flies using DeepLabCut 3.0 with a PyTorch backend. 29 , 36 This module utilizes transfer learning, initializing training with networks pre‐trained on the ImageNet dataset. In our study, we extracted a total of 2100 frames from 253 videos and manually annotated nine specific body parts: right eye (RE), left eye (LE), prothoracic coxa (PC), right foreleg tarsus (RF), left foreleg tarsus (LF), metathoracic coxa (MC), right hind‐leg tarsus (RH), left hind‐leg tarsus (LH), and anus (AN) (Fig. 1(b)). The labeled data were split into training and test datasets at an 8:2 ratio. The training performance was monitored at each epoch by assessing metrics such as validation loss, root mean squared error (RMSE), mean average precision (mAP), and mean average recall (mAR). These metrics were computed using a probability cutoff (P‐cutoff) of 0.6.

To quantify insecticide‐induced poisoning symptoms in house flies, we first extracted the x‐ and y‐coordinates of key body parts from the video using trained CNN. Based on these coordinates, we computed 10 distinct categories of behavioral features, each reflecting characteristic poisoning symptoms (Supporting Information, Fig. S1): head‐thorax tilt, thorax‐anus curvature, legs, movement, rotation, misorientation, convulsions, tremors, incoordination, and prostration. Because qualitative descriptions of such symptoms can overlap conceptually, this framework was designed to define them as quantitatively distinct behavioral features.

Head‐thorax tilt

To detect abnormal lateral head tilting, the triangular conformation of RE‐LE‐PC was analyzed. For each frame, two angles were calculated, RE‐LE‐PC and LE‐RE‐PC. The difference between these two angles was defined as the tilt, representing the degree of lateral deviation of the axis from the thoracic midline.

Frames where the tilt exceeded ±30° were considered significantly tilted, as tilt within this range was also observed in non‐poisoned individuals and thus not considered symptomatic. The tilt data were then divided into three categories: tilted‐right, tilted‐left, and tilted‐total (sum of right and left). For each category, summary statistics were extracted, including mean, standard deviation, median, interquartile range (IQR), maximum, minimum, and the proportion of tilted frames relative to the total number of frames.

Thorax‐anus curvature

To assess body curvature indicative of postural impairment, we computed the thorax–anus axis based on the coordinates of the PC, MC, and A. For each frame, we calculated the angle formed by the PC–MC–A alignment and measured its signed deviation from 180°, which represents a perfectly straight body axis. The sign of the deviation was determined by the direction of the cross product of the vectors PC → MC and A → MC, allowing us to distinguish rightward and leftward body curvatures.

Frames with curvature greater than +10° or less than −10° were considered symptomatic of abnormal body bending. Deviations were grouped as curved‐right, curved‐left, and curved‐total. For each of these categories, we extracted descriptive statistics including mean, standard deviation, median, IQR, maximum, minimum, and the proportion of affected frames relative to the total.

Legs

To quantify foreleg and hindleg activity, we analyzed the respective pairs of tarsi (RF and LF for the forelegs, RH and LH for the hindlegs) in relation to the thoracic segments (PC for forelegs and MC for hindlegs). For each pair, we calculated the Euclidean distance between the right and left tarsus to detect scrubbing behavior, defined as frames where the distance fell below five pixels. The scrubbing proportion was computed as the number of scrubbing frames divided by the total number of frames. To characterize the temporal structure of scrubbing, we calculated the duration of individual scrubbing bouts and extracted descriptive statistics including the mean, standard deviation, median, IQR, and maximum duration in seconds.

In addition, we calculated angles at the thoracic joints to evaluate limb movement. For the forelegs, the angle formed by RF–PC–MC and LF–PC–MC was calculated, while for the hindlegs, angles were formed by RH–MC–PC and LH–MC–PC. These angles reflect the range of motion during leg extension and retraction. Peak detection was applied to the absolute angle series to identify repeated limb motions. The number of detected peaks per second served as an indicator of repetitive leg movement. For each leg, angle‐based descriptive statistics were calculated, including mean, standard deviation, median, IQR, maximum, and minimum.

In addition to scrubbing behavior, we also analyzed foreleg articulation angles. The angle formed at PC by the triplets RF–PC–MC and LF–PC–MC was computed per frame, representing the range of motion during foreleg lifting or twitching. We then applied peak detection on the absolute angle series using prominence and temporal distance thresholds to identify rapid, pronounced leg movements (or kicking). The number of such peaks per second was used as a proxy for repetitive movement frequency. Descriptive statistics including mean, standard deviation, median, IQR, maximum, and minimum of the angles were also computed.

Movement

To quantify general locomotion, we used the coordinate trajectory of the AN as a proxy for whole‐body movement. The instantaneous velocity of AN was calculated by taking the Euclidean norm of its frame‐to‐frame displacement, divided by the frame interval. Directional movement was defined by projecting the displacement vector onto the normalized vector from MC to PC, which represents the body's forward orientation. Movement states were classified into stationary, forward, and backward frames using a threshold of 2 pixels per frame. The proportions of time spent in each state were calculated and normalized to ensure the total summed to one.

Directional velocities were separately analyzed for forward and backward movement segments. For each direction, we extracted descriptive statistics including mean, standard deviation, median, IQR, and maximum velocity. To assess movement intermittency, we performed peak detection on the directional velocity series and calculated the number of peaks per second as an indicator of burst‐like movement.

Rotation

To quantify body rotation, we computed the angular orientation of the body axis based on the relative coordinates of PC and MC. The rotation angle was defined as the arctangent of the vertical and horizontal displacement between PC and MC. The angular change per frame was then calculated by differencing the angle series, and values were normalized to the range of –π to +π radians to account for circular discontinuities. Angular changes below 1° per frame were considered negligible and classified as non‐rotation. Angular changes were classified into clockwise (CW) or counterclockwise (CCW). The proportions of each rotation type relative to the total duration were computed. To quantify rotation speed, angular velocities were converted from radians per frame to degrees per second. Descriptive statistics were extracted for CW, CCW, and total angular velocities, including mean, standard deviation, median, IQR, and the number of rotational bursts per second based on peak detection in each direction.

Misorientation

Misorientation, defined here as a loss of spatial control or directional stability, was assessed using two geometric descriptors of trajectory spread in AN. First, the centroid of the full trajectory was computed. The Euclidean distance between AN and this centroid was calculated for each frame to measure how dispersed the movement was around the central location. The standard deviation, maximum, and minimum of these distances were extracted as indicators of positional variance.

Additionally, the overall area covered by movement was estimated by computing the convex hull enclosing the AN trajectory. The convex hull area serves as a summary of the spatial footprint of the individual's movement during the observation period, with larger areas indicating more extensive exploration and smaller areas suggesting localized or constrained movement.

Convulsions

Convulsion, defined as a sudden, violent, and irregular movement of a limb or body part, was quantified based on abrupt angular displacements in the limbs during stationary periods. To isolate intrinsic motor spasms from general locomotion, only frames where the displacement of MC was below two pixels per frame were considered. Within these stationary frames, angles formed by RF–PC–MC, LF–PC–MC, RH–MC–PC, and LH–MC–PC were computed. Convulsive events were identified as sharp peaks in these angle series, using a detection threshold of 20 degrees. The frequency of convulsions was calculated as the number of such peaks per second, separately for forelegs and hindlegs.

Tremors

Tremor, classically defined as an involuntary, rhythmic muscular contraction leading to shaking movements in one or more parts of the body, was quantified by detecting high‐frequency, low‐amplitude oscillations in limb and abdominal segments. We calculated the Euclidean distances between each tarsus and its respective coxa (RF–PC, LF–PC, RH–MC, LH–MC) for limb tremors, and between MC and AN for abdominal tremors. Peaks in the framewise changes of these distance series were identified using a 5‐pixel threshold to capture fine‐scale twitching of tremors. The frequency was expressed as the number of detected peaks per second, computed separately for the forelegs, hindlegs, and abdomen.

Incoordination

Incoordination, defined as a lack of harmonious or synchronized movement between body parts, was quantified by measuring asymmetry in limb angles during stationary periods. Angles were computed between the tarsus, coxa, and thoracic segment for each foreleg (RF–PC–MC and LF–PC–MC) and each hindleg (RH–MC–PC and LH–MC–PC). To isolate the feature from locomotive activities, analysis was restricted to frames where MC movement was less than two pixels per frame. For each pair of right and left legs, the absolute difference in their angular trajectories was calculated to obtain frame‐by‐frame asymmetry values. Frames with an angular asymmetry greater than five degrees were considered significantly uncoordinated.

Descriptive statistics were extracted from these asymmetry values, including mean, standard deviation, median, IQR, and maximum asymmetry, as well as the proportion of frames exhibiting significant asymmetry. This was done separately for the forelegs and hindlegs.

Prostration

Prostration, typically defined as the state of being completely stretched out or lying flat and motionless, was quantified based on the full extension of all legs away from the thorax. For each frame, we measured the Euclidean distance between each foreleg tarsus (RF and LF) and PC, and each hindleg tarsus (RH and LH) and MC. A frame was considered prostrated if all four distances exceeded 70% of their respective maximum values across the entire time series, indicating leg extension than normal position.

The proportion of time spent in the prostrated state was calculated relative to the total duration. We calculated analyzed both duration and the intervals between successive prostration bouts. The summary statistics calculated were mean, standard deviation, median, IQR, and maximum for both.

Classification of symptomatologic patterns

Classification algorithms were trained using the Classification Learner module in MATLAB R2024b (MathWorks, Natick, MA, USA) to predict the symptomatological class of poisoned individuals. A total of 148 features were used to train 32 classification models spanning nine algorithmic families based on underlying principles: decision tree, discriminant analysis, naive Bayes, support vector machine, regression, k‐nearest neighbor, neural networks, kernel approximation, and ensemble methods. Because these features were extracted from the same individual, some collinearity among predictors was expected; therefore, machine‐learning classifiers were used because they are more robust to such correlation than conventional parametric models. The dataset was partitioned into 80% for training and 20% for testing, and model performance was evaluated using 10‐fold cross‐validation. Prediction accuracy and macro‐averaged F1 scores were used to assess performance on both the validation and test sets.

The three top‐performing models were selected to evaluate two insecticidal mixtures for their similarity of symptoms compared to the class used for training: type‐I pyrethroids, type‐II pyrethroids, organophosphates, and solvent treated control. Data from 15 to 20 individuals per treatment were analyzed.

Among the type‐I pyrethroid insecticides tested, insecticidal activity ranked in the order of tetramethrin, prallethrin, d‐phenothrin, and permethrin based on their LD50 and LD80 values, although their 95% confidence intervals overlapped. At the LD20 level, permethrin exhibited the highest potency (0.069 μg/insect), while the 95% confidence intervals also overlapped. Within type‐II pyrethroids, λ‐cyhalothrin consistently showed greater toxicity than α‐cypermethrin across all dose levels, but their 95% confidence intervals overlapped, indicating no notable difference. Between the two organophosphates, chlorpyrifos exhibited substantially greater potency than dichlorvos did.

Knockdown‐mortality relationship of individual insecticides

Generalized linear models were constructed for each insecticide class to evaluate the fixed effects of insecticide, dose level (effect level), survivorship, and their interactions on knockdown time (Table 2). Significant main effects of insecticide and dose were observed across all classes. Specifically, insecticide significantly affected knockdown time in type‐I pyrethroids (F = 345.53, P < 0.0001), type‐II pyrethroids (F = 1562.19, P < 0.0001), and organophosphates (F = 4.44, P = 0.0354). Dose level also had a strong effect in all classes (type‐I pyrethroids: F = 391.20, P < 0.0001; type‐II pyrethroids: F = 1466.65, P < 0.0001; organophosphates: F = 963.88, P < 0.0001). Survivorship was not a significant factor within the type‐I pyrethroid class (F = 0.16, P = 0.6903), indicating no systematic difference in knockdown time between surviving and non‐surviving individuals. In contrast, survivorship had a strong and highly significant effect in both type‐II pyrethroids (F = 370.81, P < 0.0001) and organophosphates (F = 680.62, P < 0.0001), demonstrating that variation in knockdown time between surviving and non‐surviving individuals was substantial in these classes.

The interaction between insecticide and dose was significant within all insecticide classes (type‐I pyrethroids: F = 93.25, P < 0.0001; type‐II pyrethroids: F = 469.67, P < 0.0001; organophosphates: F = 36.91, P < 0.0001), indicating that dose–response relationships differed among insecticides. Interactions involving survivorship differed markedly among classes. In the type‐I pyrethroid class, neither dose × survivorship (F = 0.01, P = 0.9877) nor compound × dose × survivorship (F = 0.96, P = 0.4738) was significant. In contrast, both interactions were significant in type‐II pyrethroids (dose × survivorship: F = 75.44, P < 0.0001; compound × dose × survivorship: F = 16.27, P < 0.0001) and organophosphates (dose × survivorship: F = 32.08, P < 0.0001; insecticide × dose × survivorship: F = 20.34, P < 0.0001), indicating that survivorship‐dependent differences in knockdown time were modulated by dose and insecticide identity in these classes.

To further assess differences in knockdown time, multiple pairwise comparisons were conducted using the Tukey–Kramer adjustment (Supporting Information, Table S1). Across all tests, knockdown time was significantly shorter at higher doses (P < 0.0001). Within the type‐I pyrethroid class, knockdown time did not differ significantly between surviving and non‐surviving individuals at any LD level (LD20, LD50, or LD80, Fig. 2(a)–(d)), indicating no direct contribution of knockdown speed to lethality. In the type‐II pyrethroid insecticides, knockdown time differed significantly between surviving and non‐surviving individuals at all dose levels (P < 0.0001, Fig. 2(e), (f)), suggesting that individuals with faster knockdown had a higher likelihood of lethality compared to those with slower knockdown. An exception was observed for α‐cypermethrin at LD80, where survivorship had no significant effect on knockdown time (P = 0.9768). A similar pattern was observed for organophosphates, in which survivorship significantly affected knockdown time across all dose levels (P < 0.0001, Fig. 2(g), (h)), and knockdown time was significantly reduced at higher doses (P < 0.0001).

Two binary mixtures of type‐I pyrethroids were tested for their dose–response relationships (Table 3). The LD50 values of d‐phenothrin + prallethrin (1.00:0.77 w/w; hereafter referred to as mixture 1) and d‐phenothrin + tetramethrin (1.00:1.80 w/w; mixture 2) were 1.276 and 0.414 μg/insect, respectively. To evaluate interactions between the components, both mixtures were analyzed under the Loewe dose‐additivity model (Fig. 3). In mixture 1, the calculated SRs were 0.922, 1.000, and 1.076 at LD20, LD50, and LD80, respectively, indicating additive effects across all dose levels. In contrast, mixture 2 showed strong synergy, with SR values of 2.519, 2.551, and 2.564 at the corresponding dose levels, suggesting that the combination of d‐phenothrin and tetramethrin acts synergistically at a 1.00:1.80 (w/w) ratio.

Knockdown‐mortality relationship of type‐I pyrethroid mixtures

Generalized linear models were constructed to evaluate the effects of insecticide, dose, survivorship, and their interactions on knockdown time in type‐I pyrethroid mixtures (Table 4). All interaction terms were also significant, including mixture combination × dose (F = 40.92, P < 0.0001), dose × survivorship (F = 24.02, P < 0.0001), and the mixture × dose × survivorship interaction (F = 14.37, P < 0.0001). These results indicate that the relationship between knockdown time and survivorship differed between mixtures and was modulated by dose level.

In contrast, mixture 2 (d‐phenothrin and tetramethrin) exhibited a strong association between knockdown speed and mortality (Fig. 4(b)). Across all dose levels, individuals that eventually died were knocked down significantly faster than those that survived, indicating a consistent knockdown–mortality relationship. Mixture 1 retained a type‐I‐like response characterized by a decoupling of knockdown speed and lethality, whereas mixture 2 exhibited a type‐II‐like and organophosphate‐like response, in which rapid knockdown was predictive of eventual mortality.

Training and performance of CNN

To estimate house fly body‐part coordinates, ResNet101 was trained using a hand‐labeled dataset (Supporting Information, Fig. S2). Most body‐part detections showed likelihood values of 0.90–0.99, with some between 0.70 and 0.90; therefore, a threshold of 0.60 was used when calculating the validation metrics to exclude low‐confidence coordinate estimates and avoid bias from artifact‐prone predictions within the recordings. Under this threshold, the RMSE decreased from 4.727 pixels in the first training epoch to 2.788 pixels in the final epoch, indicating improved precision in coordinate prediction. The mAP and mAR also improved substantially, from 14.205% and 15.901% to 90.455% and 92.010%, respectively, indicating high accuracy and consistent recovery of labeled body parts. Training and validation loss steadily declined throughout the training process, reaching final values of 0.00144 and 0.00150, respectively, which supports stable model convergence.

Temporal shift in symptomological classification of mixture 2

To further investigate the temporal dynamics of symptoms induced by mixture 2, we divided each recording into an early and late phase based on the median knockdown time. Although mixture 2 consists solely of type‐I pyrethroids, it consistently exhibited a classification pattern resembling type‐II pyrethroid exposure. However, the knockdown onset occurred faster than that of the reference type‐II pyrethroid. This led to the hypothesis that mixture 2 initially induces symptoms characteristic of type‐I pyrethroids, which then evolve into type‐II‐like manifestations over time. The ratio of median knockdown time across individuals exposed to type‐I pyrethroids to type‐II pyrethroids corresponded to approximately 36%. Based on this, we defined the first 36% of each recording as the early phase, and the remaining portion as the late phase.

Classification results supported this hypothesis across all three top‐performing models. In the early phase, the subspace discriminant, quadratic SVM, and coarse neural network classified the majority of samples as type‐I pyrethroid, with 86.36%, 77.27%, and 81.82%, respectively (Fig. 5(c)). In contrast, during the late phase, the same models predominantly classified samples as type‐II pyrethroid with 86.36%, 90.91%, and 95.45%, respectively, with minimal or no assignment to other classes (Fig. 5(d)).

DISCUSSION

Understanding the relationship between knockdown speed and mortality is crucial not only for elucidating the toxicological profiles of insecticides but also for validating efficacy claims made on commercial insecticidal product labels. Regulatory frameworks, such as those provided by the U.S. EPA (40 CFR 156.10(a) (5)), commonly use rapid knockdown observations (e.g., at 30 s post‐exposure) to substantiate claims like ‘quick kill’ or ‘kills on contact’, presuming a strong correlation between rapid incapacitation and eventual lethality. 7 Previous studies primarily examined this relationship by comparing knockdown and lethal dose parameters (e.g., LD50 and KD50 / KT50) across different compounds to assess correlations at the compound level. 8 , 9 , 10 However, individual‐level variation, which is fundamentally important for validating label claims and underlying assumptions, has not been thoroughly explored.

Our findings challenge this presumption, demonstrating that individuals exhibiting rapid knockdown after exposure to type‐I pyrethroid insecticides did not necessarily show a higher likelihood of mortality compared to those knocked down later. Conversely, we observed a strong knockdown‐mortality correlation for individual type‐II pyrethroid and organophosphate insecticides, and notably with a synergistic mixture of d‐phenothrin and tetramethrin, both of which belong to type‐I pyrethroids. This distinction carries significant regulatory implications. If rapid knockdown observed at the designated time point does not reliably predict ultimate mortality for type‐I pyrethroids, then employing knockdown as an indicator for lethality in product claims may misrepresent the efficacy of these products, unlike for type‐II pyrethroids and organophosphates.

A possible explanation for this decoupling phenomenon is metabolic recovery. In the case of type‐I pyrethroids, rapid knockdown is often caused by acute neuroexcitation due to sodium channel modulation. However, subsequent recovery is feasible if an insect possesses sufficient metabolic capacity to detoxify the compound post‐knockdown. 37 , 38 Thus, individuals with heightened neural sensitivity may rapidly become incapacitated but ultimately survive owing to robust detoxification mechanisms. Previous research highlighted the association between increased activities of esterases, cytochrome P450s, and glutathione‐S‐transferases and enhanced recovery and survival rates following insecticide exposure. 39 , 40 , 41 Thereby, another concern arising from this discordance is its implications for insecticide resistance management. Sublethal exposure resulting in temporary incapacitation, but not death, may apply selective pressure on populations, favoring individuals with high metabolic capacity, which is a resistance mechanism associated with significant cross‐resistance risks. 42 , 43 , 44

Nevertheless, metabolic recovery alone cannot entirely explain the observed data patterns. Although the positive knockdown‐mortality relationship was observed in type‐II pyrethroids and organophosphates, they are metabolized through similar enzymatic pathways to type‐I pyrethroids. 45 , 46 , 47 Compounds causing sustained or irreversible neuronal damage might better ensure lethality following knockdown, though further empirical evidence is necessary to fully validate this hypothesis.

This study further explored commercially relevant insecticide mixtures. Mixture 1 (d‐phenothrin + prallethrin) and mixture 2 (d‐phenothrin + tetramethrin) are both commonly used in commercial household aerosol insecticides. 5 , 6 While both mixtures effectively induced rapid knockdown, only mixture 2 demonstrated a consistent relationship between knockdown and lethality in individual level. In contrast, mixture 1 exhibited merely an additive effect, wherein rapid knockdown did not always result in mortality. This divergence suggests that combining a ‘knockdown agent’ (such as prallethrin or tetramethrin) with a ‘killing agent’ (such as d‐phenothrin) may not guarantee alignment between rapid incapacitation and eventual death. 48 Using complementary advantages of them would yield both fast knockdown and high toxicity, yet at an individual level, the insects knocked down might differ from those that ultimately die, leading to the challenges abovementioned.

Behavioral analyses of poisoning symptoms have often remained qualitative, describing the symptoms as ‘tremor’ or ‘paralysis’ without quantifiable metrics. 16 , 22 , 49 Those behavioral symptoms that are quantified are usually movement trajectory or specific reflex behaviors such as jumping and flying. 19 , 21 , 23 , 25 Our study redefined symptomology through CNN‐based body part detection, transforming such behaviors into quantifiable features. These features not only worked very well when used to train the classification algorithms to predict insecticide classes with high accuracy, but in many cases, even in the classical comparison of behavioral patterns such as in section 3.6, the features other than that could be quantified in traditional method like movement category showed significant distinguishment among the classes. This methodological advancement suggests that behavioral symptomology can be used as a diagnostic tool, both to classify modes of action in insecticides and as in this study, to infer potential synergistic interactions. 50 Compared with conventional researcher‐based visual observation, this framework provides a more objective and quantitative way to distinguish symptoms that can conceptually overlap and become confusing when assessed qualitatively, such as tremor, incoordination, and prostration. However, because this framework relied on 2D ventral‐view recording with wing‐clipped flies, some behavioral information may have been lost compared with a full 3D representation. Future application of 3D pose‐estimation approaches may help capture a broader range of intoxication behaviors and further improve symptom quantification.

The synergistic interactions observed in this study were initially confirmed through dose–response evaluations using the Loewe additivity model. In assessing synergism, it is defined as significant positive deviation in toxicity from expected values derived from null models assuming no interaction between compounds. Several null models were developed, each with distinct perspectives on toxicity. 51 Although there is no consensus on which null model to be used, each model's assumptions regarding the similarity of the modes of action can be important criteria for the choice. 52 The Loewe model employed here presumes similar modes of action between the mixture's components and typically provides a conservative determination of synergy. 53 Thus, the synergism consistently observed across LD20, LD50, and LD80 isoboles for mixture 2 strongly suggests genuine synergistic interaction compared to mixture 1.

To further identify the underlying mechanism of this observed synergy, we analyzed the symptomological profiles of each mixture using the previously described computer vision‐based framework. Interestingly, mixture 2, despite being composed of type‐I pyrethroids, frequently exhibited symptom profiles classified as type‐II‐like symptoms. A detailed temporal analysis of behavioral symptoms revealed that early‐phase poisoning resembled type‐I pyrethroid exposure, yet as poisoning progressed, the symptom profile transitioned distinctly toward type‐II characteristics, as supported by the classification algorithms. This emergent shift in behavioral symptoms suggests that the synergism may not simply reflect enhanced toxicity through increased bioavailability, such as facilitated cuticular penetration or inhibition of detoxification enzymes, but may also involve toxicodynamic interaction. However, the precise toxicodynamic basis of this interaction remains unresolved and requires further investigation, especially given the limited previous reports on within‐class distinctions among type‐I pyrethroids. Because only two type‐I pyrethroid mixtures were examined here, broader testing of additional type‐I combinations and type‐I/type‐II combinations will be necessary to determine how general the observed synergistic pattern is. In addition, because this study was conducted using a single susceptible M. domestica strain and a limited set of insecticides, caution should be taken in generalizing these findings to other strains, species, or modes of action. Likewise, because only a limited set of insecticides was examined, further study is needed to determine whether similarity or difference in symptom profiles reflects a shared or distinct mode of action.

CONCLUSIONS

To our knowledge, this study demonstrates for the first time that the relationship between knockdown speed and mortality at the individual level varies significantly among insecticide classes. Rapid knockdown by type‐I pyrethroids did not consistently predict individual mortality, potentially leading to misconceptions regarding product label claims such as ‘quick kill’ or ‘kills on contact’. Furthermore, synergistic interactions between d‐phenothrin and tetramethrin were observed, characterized by enhanced toxicity and advent of the knockdown‐mortality relationship. The mechanism underlying this synergism was explored using convolutional neural network‐based behavioral analyses coupled with classification algorithms. Results indicated that synergistic effects likely resulted from cooperative modes of action, rather than from metabolic inhibition or enhanced cuticular penetration. This study highlights the importance of evaluating individual‐level variation in assessing insecticide efficacy and proposes computer vision‐based quantitative symptomology as a valuable approach to elucidate synergistic mechanisms in insecticide mixtures. Beyond insecticide toxicology, this framework may also be applicable to other behavior‐relevant research areas, including plant–insect interactions and studies of attractants and repellents.
