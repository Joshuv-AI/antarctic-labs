Submodular Ground-Set Pruning: Monotone Tightness and a Non-Monotone Separation
Alan Kuhnle

Abstract
Large-scale subset selection asks for a small useful set of examples, features, sensors, seed users, or context passages from an enormous ground set. Submodular maximization is a canonical model for such diminishing-returns problems, but rapidly growing datasets make even linear-time algorithms ever costlier. We study containment pruning : first reduce the ground set to a smaller core P P , then require that P P contain a near-optimal feasible solution for every downstream budget up to k k . Prior work has formulated many heuristics, but the theoretical limits of this preprocessing problem are largely unknown. For monotone submodular objectives, we prove that 1 − 1 / e 1-1/e is tight: greedy achieves this containment factor, and no algorithm can beat it even with a larger pruning budget. For non-monotone objectives, we give the first 1 / 2 − ε 1/2-\varepsilon containment algorithms under cardinality constraints and extend the approach to knapsack constraints. This 1 / 2 1/2 factor exceeds the best known algorithmic ratio and the known hardness threshold for non-monotone maximization, showing that pruning can be provably easier than optimization. Empirically, pruning lets an exact IP solver run on the reduced MaxCut instance with a ≈ 620 × {\approx}620\times speedup, and proof-of-concept experiments on LLM context selection demonstrate the utility of non-monotone submodular proxies and our proposed containment algorithms.



1
Introduction

Large-scale subset selection.

Many machine-learning and data-analysis tasks ask for a small useful subset of
a much larger ground set: seed users in a social network, sensors or experiment
locations, predictive features, representative documents, or context passages
for an LLM
[
10
,
11
,
12
,
40
,
21
,
22
]
.
Submodular maximization is a canonical model for these problems because it
captures diminishing returns: once a subset already covers a concept, feature,
region, or piece of evidence, adding another similar item helps less. The
ground sets in these applications keep growing: the NCBI Sequence Read Archive
has sustained exponential growth for over a decade
[
43
,
44
]
,
and machine-learning training corpora have grown at roughly
3.7\times
per
year
[
45
]
. Even theoretically efficient submodular
algorithms cannot avoid this growth: in the value-oracle model they require at
least linear time in the ground-set size
[
9
]
. Larger ground sets
therefore mean more oracle queries, higher latency, and larger downstream solver
instances.

Why pruning can help.

When the target solution size
k
is small compared with the ground-set size

n
, much of the data is often redundant or irrelevant. Documents may be near
duplicates, features may become correlated after a few predictors are selected,
retrieved passages may be distractors or paraphrases, and many graph vertices
may have interchangeable marginal value. This suggests a two-stage workflow:
first reduce the large ground set to a smaller core
P\subseteq N
, then run a
more expensive optimizer on
P
alone. This idea appears in pruned
submodularity graphs, multi-stage selection, candidate reduction, learned
graph pruning, and many heuristics
[
26
,
36
,
35
,
13
,
27
,
28
,
29
,
19
]
.
The key question is not whether pruning can speed up a particular implementation,
but whether the pruned universe still contains high-quality feasible solutions
for a downstream optimizer to find. Moreover, the allowable core size may be
set by memory, latency, parallelism, or the largest instance an exact solver can
handle. Following Nath and Kuhnle
[
19
]
, we study
containment as a function of a pruning budget
p\geq k
, and ask for one core
that supports every downstream target size
k^{\prime}\leq k
.

Problem 1.1

(Budget-Range Submodular Pruning)
.

Given
N
,
f\colon 2^{N}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
(submodular), and

k\in\mathbb{N}
, for a pruning budget
p\geq k
, find
P\subseteq N

with
|P|\leq p
such that

\max_{T\subseteq P,\,|T|\leq k^{\prime}}f(T)\;\geq\;\alpha\cdot\mathrm{OPT}_{k^{\prime}},\qquad\forall\,k^{\prime}\leq k,

where
\mathrm{OPT}_{k^{\prime}}=\max_{|T|\leq k^{\prime}}f(T)
.
We call
\alpha
the
containment factor
.

The pruning algorithm does not need to identify the final solution.
It only has to output a smaller universe in which good solutions still exist.
This distinction is what makes containment different from ordinary
optimization.

The gap.

Existing pruning methods have largely been heuristic:
pruned submodularity graphs
[
26
]
and learned policies
such as GCOMB, LeNSE, and COMBHelper
[
27
,
28
,
29
]

reduce search spaces empirically, but do not give worst-case guarantees that a
good downstream solution remains in the pruned set. The main theoretical
exception is Nath and Kuhnle’s
QuickPrune

[
19
]
, which
achieves
\alpha\geq 1/24-\varepsilon
for monotone weakly submodular objectives
under a knapsack constraint.

Intuitively, pruning may be easier than direct optimization, as we are not
required to find an optimal solution, merely to contain it; but
the complexity of containment pruning is still
unclear even for monotone submodular functions, and essentially open for
non-monotone functions.
We investigate this complexity directly: can larger
pruning budgets beat the classical
1-1/e
monotone barrier, and can
non-monotone objectives admit constant-factor budget-range containment?

2
Preliminaries

This section fixes notation and collects the density-dependent
maximization bounds that serve as reference points throughout the paper.
The two key quantities to keep in mind are the
containment factor

\alpha
(how well a pruned set represents the full ground set) and the

density

c=k/n
(which governs both the best
achievable approximation and the composition bound of §
3
).
Readers familiar with submodular maximization may skim this section and
refer back as needed.

Let
N
be a finite ground set with
|N|=n
.
A function
f\colon 2^{N}\to\mathbb{R}
is
submodular
if for all

A\subseteq B\subseteq N
and
x\notin B
,

f(x\mid A)\geq f(x\mid B)
,
where
f(x\mid A)=f(A\cup\{x\})-f(A)
denotes the marginal gain.
We assume
f
is non-negative; note that
f(\emptyset)=0
is
not
required—our proofs carry
f(\emptyset)
explicitly where it appears.
The function is
monotone
if
f(A)\leq f(B)
whenever
A\subseteq B
.
For
\gamma\in(0,1]
, the function is
\gamma
-weakly DR

(diminishing returns) if

f(x\mid A)\geq\gamma\cdot f(x\mid B)

for all
A\subseteq B
and
x\notin B
;
at
\gamma=1
this is ordinary submodularity.
The
total curvature
of a monotone function is

\kappa=1-\min_{j\in N}f(j\mid N\setminus\{j\})/f(\{j\})
;
at
\kappa=0
the function is modular.

We write
O
for an optimal solution:
O\in\arg\max_{|T|\leq k}f(T)
,
and
\mathrm{OPT}=f(O)
.
The
density
of the constraint is
c=k/n
.

2.1
Known density-dependent results

Monotone.

The Measured Continuous Greedy (MCG)—a continuous-relaxation algorithm
that iteratively pushes a fractional solution toward the multilinear
extension’s gradient—achieves approximation ratio

\alpha^{*}(c)=1-(1-c)^{1/c}
at density
c
, which is tight
(Feldman & Kuhnle
[
2
]
, Theorems 4–5).
Concretely:
\alpha^{*}(c)\to 1-1/e\approx 0.632
as
c\to 0
,

\alpha^{*}(1/2)\approx 0.750
,
\alpha^{*}(0.9)\approx 0.935
,
and
\alpha^{*}(c)\to 1
as
c\to 1
.
This function governs the composition bound in §
3

and tells us how much “easier” maximization becomes as the pruned set
shrinks the effective ground set.

Non-monotone.

Buchbinder, Feldman, Naor, and Schwartz (BFNS)
[
3
]
give the
density-dependent bound

\alpha^{*}_{\mathrm{nm}}(c)=\max\!\left\{\frac{1}{e}+0.004,\;\left(1+\frac{1}{2\sqrt{c(1-c)}}\right)^{\!-1}\right\}-o(1).

Concretely, this function is nearly flat across the density regime
relevant to pruning:

\alpha^{*}_{\mathrm{nm}}(0.01)\approx 0.372
,

\alpha^{*}_{\mathrm{nm}}(0.1)\approx 0.400
,

\alpha^{*}_{\mathrm{nm}}(0.2)\approx 0.444
,
rising to
1/2
at
c=1/2
(tight by the symmetry
gap
[
6
,
7
]
).
Buchbinder and Feldman
[
4
]
subsequently achieved

0.401
for all down-closed constraints (density-independent).
The combined best is
\max(0.401,\alpha^{*}_{\mathrm{nm}}(c))
.
The strongest hardness is
0.478
due to Qi
[
5
]
,
improving the earlier
0.491
of Gharan and Vondrák
[
6
]
.
The small spread
[0.401,0.5]
across all densities is why the composition
bound does essentially no work in the non-monotone setting
(§
4
).

One additional non-monotone baseline—the three-term CDG guarantee of
[
3
]
—is used only in the warm-start analysis and is stated in Appendix
B
.

3
Monotone Pruning

Does extra budget help?

The simplest way to analyze pruning is
composition
: prune to
p
elements, then run the best optimization algorithm on the pruned set.
If the pruned set has containment factor
\alpha
and the optimizer has approximation ratio
\alpha^{*}(k/p)
, the combined guarantee is at most
\alpha\cdot\alpha^{*}(k/p)
.
Since this cannot exceed the information-theoretic limit on the original problem, we obtain
\alpha\leq\alpha^{*}(k/n)/\alpha^{*}(k/p)
.
At
p=k
this recovers
\alpha\leq 1-1/e
, but it weakens as
p
grows.
The following theorem resolves the remaining question directly: extra budget truly cannot help.

Theorem 3.1

(Pruning Hardness for Monotone
\gamma
-Weakly DR Functions)
.

For every
\gamma\in(0,1]
, every
\varepsilon>0
,
and every polynomial
C(\cdot)
,
there exists
k_{0}=k_{0}(\gamma,\varepsilon,C)
such that for all
k\geq k_{0}

and
every

p\geq k
:
no (possibly randomized) algorithm making at most
C(|N|)
value queries
and outputting
P\subseteq N
with
|P|\leq p
can guarantee

\mathbb{E}\!\left[\max_{S\subseteq P,\,|S|\leq k}f(S)\right]>(1-e^{-\gamma}+\varepsilon)\cdot\mathrm{OPT}_{k}

for all monotone
\gamma
-weakly DR
f\colon 2^{N}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}

with
|N|=\lceil 60p/\varepsilon\rceil
.
At
\gamma=1
(full submodularity), this recovers the tight bound

\alpha\leq 1-1/e
.
In particular, extra budget beyond
k
provably does not improve the containment
factor for any constant or growing multiple of
k
.

Proof sketch (full proof in Appendix
E
).

The core idea is a cancellation that neutralizes extra budget.
We set
|N|=\lceil 3p/\varepsilon^{\prime}\rceil
and plant a random optimum

\tilde{T}
of size
k
using the construction of Harshaw et
al.
[
16
]
.
Any algorithm’s output
P
(with
|P|\leq p
) is indistinguishable from the
null instance, so
P
is effectively fixed before
\tilde{T}
is drawn.
The expected overlap is then at most

\mathbb{E}[|P\cap\tilde{T}|]\leq pk/|N|\leq\varepsilon^{\prime}k/3
,
which is
independent of
p
: the pruning-budget factor in the
numerator cancels with the one in the denominator (
|N|\propto p
).
A concentration bound shows that with high probability,
P
has too
little overlap with
\tilde{T}
for any
k
-subset of
P
to beat
the null-instance value
1-e^{-\gamma}+O(\varepsilon)
.
∎

Remark 3.2
.

The proof uses two separate hypergeometric tails. First, along the
null-instance transcript, every query is hidden unless it has unusually large
overlap with the random planted set; a two-regime bound covers both

p=O(k)
and
p\gg k
. Second, after the null transcript fixes the output

P
, the expected overlap

\mathbb{E}[|P\cap\tilde{T}|]\leq\varepsilon^{\prime}k/3
is independent of
p
.
Thus the extra budget cancels in the hiding argument rather than revealing
more of the planted optimum.

Real-world submodular functions—coverage, influence, facility
location—often have total curvature
\kappa<1
, meaning elements
retain marginal value even in the presence of many others.
For maximization, low curvature yields strictly better approximation
(
1-\kappa/e
vs.
1-1/e
).
One might hope that low curvature similarly helps pruning.
The next result shows it does not: greedy at
p=k
is already optimal
for every curvature level.

Corollary 3.3

(Pruning Hardness with Bounded Total Curvature)
.

Let
\kappa\in(0,1]
denote total curvature:

\kappa=1-\min_{j\in N}f(j\mid N\setminus\{j\})/f(j)
.
For every
p\geq k
and every
\varepsilon>0
,
no algorithm using polynomially many value queries can prune to

p
elements with containment

\alpha>1-\kappa/e+\varepsilon

for all monotone submodular functions with total curvature at most
\kappa
.
Since the algorithm of Sviridenko, Vondrák & Ward
[
18
]
achieves
\alpha=1-\kappa/e
,
this bound is tight.

(
At
\kappa=0
the function is modular, so exact containment is trivial.
)

Proof.

See Appendix
F
. ∎

For general monotone submodular functions, greedy achieves
1-1/e
and the hardness matches it at every pruning budget
p\geq k
.
Table
4
(Appendix
F
) records how this compares with the composition benchmark.

Remark 3.4

(Fast budget-range pruning)
.

Standard greedy attains
\alpha=1-1/e
in
O(nk)
queries via its prefix property
[
19
]
.
A small-budget grid plus a geometric sweep of
O((1+\log k)/\varepsilon)
calls to the threshold greedy of
[
38
]
achieves exact-size
(1-1/e-\varepsilon)
-containment for all
k^{\prime}\leq k
simultaneously, with
|P|=O(k/\varepsilon)
and
\tilde{O}(n/\varepsilon^{2})
total queries (Theorem
C.1
, Appendix
C
).
The proof first obtains a slightly oversized witness and then thins it to size
k^{\prime}
; monotone submodularity loses only an
O(\varepsilon)
multiplicative factor.

4
Non-Monotone Pruning

Why composition fails for non-monotone functions.

In the monotone setting, composition at least gives the right answer at
p=k

(even though it fails at larger budgets).
For non-monotone objectives, composition is nearly useless even at
p=k
.
The reason is that non-monotone approximation ratios barely change with density:
the best ratio ranges from
0.401
at low density to
0.5
at high density.
Since the numerator and denominator of the composition bound

\alpha\leq\alpha^{*}_{\mathrm{nm}}(k/n)/\alpha^{*}_{\mathrm{nm}}(k/p)

are nearly equal, the bound stays close to
1
for moderate pruning
budgets and approaches the vacuous bound
1
as
p
grows.
Entirely new techniques are needed.

Can we prune non-monotone functions?

We show that
O(k)
elements suffice for
1/2
-factor containment.
For the top budget
k
, storing the output of the
0.401
-approximation
of
[
4
]
gives only a single-budget optimization
baseline; our guarantee is budget-range containment.

Theorem 4.1

(Universal
1/2
Containment)
.

Let
f
be a non-negative submodular function on ground set
N
, and let

k\leq n
.
For any
\varepsilon>0
, there exists a set
G\subseteq N
with

|G|=O(k/\varepsilon)
such that for every
k^{\prime}\leq k
,
(if
|N|<\lceil 1/\varepsilon\rceil k
, take
G=N
;
containment is exact and
|G|<\lceil 1/\varepsilon\rceil k=O(k/\varepsilon)
)

\max_{T\subseteq G,\,|T|\leq k^{\prime}}f(T)\;\geq\;\left(\tfrac{1}{2}-\varepsilon\right)\cdot\mathrm{OPT}_{k^{\prime}},

where
\mathrm{OPT}_{k^{\prime}}=\max_{|S|\leq k^{\prime}}f(S)
.
Moreover,
G
can be constructed in
O(nk/\varepsilon)
oracle queries
(
\ell=\lceil 1/\varepsilon\rceil
greedy passes, each scanning
O(n)

elements at
k
steps).

Proof sketch (full proof in Appendix
G
).

Construction.

Run
\ell
independent greedy runs
G_{1},\ldots,G_{\ell}
, each of size
k
, on disjoint portions of the ground set.
The pruned set is
G=\bigcup_{i}G_{i}
with
|G|=\ell k
.
Some optimal elements may lie outside
G
; call these
\hat{O}=O\setminus G
(“missed” elements,
b=|\hat{O}|
), and let
A_{i}
be the first
b
elements of each greedy run
i
.

Averaging.

Because the runs are disjoint, their collective “damage” to
f(O)
is bounded:
the total loss
\sum_{i}[f(O)-f(O\cup A_{i})]
is at most
f(O)
.
By averaging, some run
i^{*}
satisfies
f(O\cup A_{i^{*}})\geq(1-1/\ell)f(O)
.

Greedy domination.

The key insight is that greedy run
i^{*}
had access to the missed elements
\hat{O}
as candidates but chose
A_{i^{*}}
instead.
So each greedy pick has marginal gain at least as large as the corresponding missed element.
Telescoping this chain gives
2f(A)-f(\emptyset)\geq f(\hat{O}\cup A)
: the greedy prefix captures at least half the value of the prefix augmented with the missed elements.

Combining.

A submodularity decomposition yields
\max(f(A),f(\dot{O}\cup A))\geq(1-1/\ell)f(O)/2
, where
\dot{O}=O\cap G
is the part of OPT already in
G
.
Both candidates are feasible subsets of
G
with size at most
k^{\prime}
,
giving
\alpha\geq(1/2)(1-1/\ell)
.
The argument applies uniformly to every target budget
k^{\prime}\leq k
.
∎

Remark 4.2

(Where the
1/2
comes from, and why it is robust)
.

The
1/2
factor arises from two tight ingredients:
(a) the greedy domination bound
2f(A)-f(\emptyset)\geq f(\hat{O}\cup A)
,
where the non-monotone marginal chain only gives a factor of
2

(vs. the
(1-1/e)^{-1}
one gets for monotone);
and (b) the
\max\geq\mathrm{sum}/2
split in the final step.
Neither bound depends on
|G|
, so the
1/2
is robust to increasing the
pruning budget—with pruning budget
p=\ell k
, the
containment factor is
\frac{1}{2}(1-1/\ell)
:
\ell=2
gives

\alpha\geq 1/4
, while
\ell\to\infty
recovers
1/2
.
Note this is
not
bicriteria: both candidate solutions
A
and

\dot{O}\cup A
satisfy
|T|\leq k
, improving over the bicriteria result
of Feldman and Kuhnle
[
2
]
(
(1/2-\varepsilon)
with
output size
\sim 1.44k
).
The
1/2
is reminiscent of the unconstrained non-monotone hardness
of
[
8
]
, but the analogy is suggestive rather
than exact, and closing the gap to
1-1/e
remains open
(§
6
).

A natural warm-start approach via the BFNS continuous double greedy
[
3
]
peaks at
4/9\approx 0.444
and cannot beat
1/2
; see Appendix
I
for details.

A second proof via window containment.

Sequential disjoint greedy requires
\ell
independent passes over the ground set.
We now give a structurally distinct proof of
1/2
-containment using a single-pass

window
algorithm (Algorithm
1
, Appendix
J
)
that achieves the same factor with a larger accumulated-window output.
At each step, we maintain a window
W_{t}
of the top-
\omega k
elements by marginal gain
and pick one uniformly at random; the window parameter
\omega\geq 1

controls the width of exploration (larger
\omega
means a wider pool of
candidates per step, at the cost of retaining all windows and hence a
larger pruned set of size at most
k+\omega k^{2}
).
This simultaneously guarantees
domination
(every window element has
marginal gain at least as large as any non-window element, so a random
pick dominates any missed OPT element) and
degradation control

(the OPT value is not destroyed by the elements we commit to:

\mathbb{E}[f(O\cup S_{m})]\geq e^{-1/\omega}f(O)
).

Theorem 4.3

(Window Containment — Unconditional)
.

For any non-negative submodular
f
and window parameter
\omega\geq 1
,
Algorithm
1
returns an accumulated-window set

P_{k}\subseteq N
with
|P_{k}|\leq k+\omega k^{2}
such that, for every

k^{\prime}\leq k
,

\mathbb{E}\!\left[\max_{|T|\leq k^{\prime},\;T\subseteq P_{k}}f(T)\right]\;\geq\;\frac{e^{-1/\omega}}{2}\cdot\mathrm{OPT}_{k^{\prime}}\qquad\text{(asymptotically as $k\to\infty$)}.

The finite-
k
lower bound from the proof is
(1-1/(\omega k))^{k}/2=e^{-1/\omega}\bigl(1-O(1/(\omega^{2}k))\bigr)/2
;
in particular,
\alpha\to 1/2
as
\omega\to\infty
.
Total query complexity:
O(nk)
.
Full proof in Appendix
J
; see Table
5
there for the
guarantee as a function of
\omega
(at
\omega=5
,
\alpha\geq 0.410>0.401
).

Remark 4.4

(Window Pruning)
.

Window containment is structurally distinct from sequential disjoint greedy
(Theorem
4.1
): it makes a single wide-exploration pass
rather than
\ell
independent narrow searches.
Both converge to the same
1/2
barrier, suggesting it may be a fundamental limit.

Knapsack extension.

The SDG argument extends beyond cardinality constraints to knapsack constraints, where each element
u
has a positive cost
p_{u}
and a feasible solution must have total cost at most
B
.
(We assume
p_{u}\leq B
for every element, since items too expensive to use alone can be discarded.)
The key change is replacing greedy—which picks the element with the highest marginal gain—by
density-greedy
, which picks the element with the highest
value-per-unit-cost
: at each step, select
u
maximizing
f(u\mid A)/p_{u}
, and keep it if adding it stays within cost
3B
.
Running
\ell
disjoint density-greedy passes (with dummy zero-value items to fill each run to cost
2B
, following
[
2
]
) produces the pruned set.

Theorem 4.5

(Knapsack budget-range containment)
.

For any
\varepsilon\in(0,1/2)
, sequential disjoint density-greedy with

\ell=\lceil 1/\varepsilon\rceil
runs produces a set
P\subseteq N

with
c(P)\leq 3\ell B=O(B/\varepsilon)
such that for every

B^{\prime}\in(0,B]
there exists
Q\subseteq P
with

c(Q)\;\leq\;B^{\prime},\qquad f(Q)\;\geq\;\left(\tfrac{1}{2}-\varepsilon\right)\mathrm{OPT}_{B^{\prime}}\;-\;\Delta_{B^{\prime}},

where
\Delta_{B^{\prime}}\geq 0
is a boundary-fragment loss that arises when the
density-greedy prefix crosses the budget threshold mid-item. When no item
is split,
\Delta_{B^{\prime}}=0
.
Total query complexity:
O(n^{2}\ell)=O(n^{2}/\varepsilon)

(
\ell
adaptive density-greedy runs, each scanning
O(n)
candidates per step).

The boundary term
\Delta_{B^{\prime}}
arises when the density-greedy prefix “overshoots” the budget—the last item added may cost more than the remaining budget allows.
In that case, only a fraction
\gamma
of the item’s value is captured, and the remaining value is lost.
Formally,
\Delta_{B^{\prime}}=\gamma_{i^{\star}}f(\{a_{j^{\star}}^{(i^{\star})}\})
,
where
a_{j^{\star}}^{(i^{\star})}
is the item that crosses the budget boundary;
see Appendix
D
for precise definitions.
The split item may cost more than
B^{\prime}
itself, so
\Delta_{B^{\prime}}
cannot be bounded simply by the value of the best affordable item.

Corollary 4.6

(Small-item knapsack containment)
.

Fix a budget range
\mathcal{B}\subseteq(0,B]
and let

B_{\min}=\inf\mathcal{B}>0
.
Run sequential disjoint density-greedy with a sufficiently small internal
accuracy parameter. Suppose that the item sizes are small at the density
prefix scale used in the proof: for every
B^{\prime}\in\mathcal{B}
, if the proof for

B^{\prime}
selects a split boundary item
a_{j^{\star}}^{(i^{\star})}
with residual
outside-core cost
\beta=c(O\setminus P)
, then

p_{a_{j^{\star}}^{(i^{\star})}}\leq\eta\beta.

In particular, this is the usual no-large-items regime when the relevant
residual prefixes have cost comparable to the query budget; over a fixed range
it is ensured by
p_{\max}\leq\eta B_{\min}
together with

\beta=\Omega(B_{\min})
for the budgets under consideration.
There is a universal constant
C
such that, if
\eta\leq\varepsilon/C
, then
for every
B^{\prime}\in\mathcal{B}
there exists
Q\subseteq P
with

c(Q)\leq B^{\prime},\qquad f(Q)\geq\left(\tfrac{1}{2}-\varepsilon\right)\mathrm{OPT}_{B^{\prime}}.

The proof adapts the sequential disjoint greedy argument of Theorem
4.1
to density-greedy with item splitting,
using the machinery of
[
2
]
.
The proof sketch, simultaneous budget-range guarantee, knapsack hardness argument, and boundary-term discussion appear in Appendix
D
;
the full proof is in Appendix
H.3
.

5
Experiments

In this section, we ask whether containment cores are useful preprocessing
objects. We show three messages. First, on MaxCut, our pruning algorithms
usually retain an optimal or near-optimal
k
-cut and can shrink an exact IP
solve by orders of magnitude. Second, on HotpotQA, LLM context selection is
genuinely non-monotone: adding passages can hurt answer quality, and pruning a
non-monotone proxy improves downstream F1/EM while using fewer passages.
Third, on MuSiQue, the task has the containment structure pruning is designed
for: keep evidence-bearing passages in a reduced pool while filtering
distractors. We show that relevance-gated submodular diversity improves gold-passage recall
over relevance ranking alone.

studies MaxCut on random and real-world graphs,

5.2
Non-monotone LLM context selection

This subsection separates two roles for LLM context selection. HotpotQA tests
whether context selection is genuinely non-monotone and whether a cheap
submodular proxy can guide pruning toward higher-quality reader inputs.
MuSiQue then tests the containment claim: when supporting evidence is dispersed
across hops, a pruner should keep evidence-bearing passages available for a
downstream selector or reader rather than output the final context itself.

Adding context passages to an LLM prompt initially improves answer quality
but degrades it beyond a threshold—the “lost in the middle”
effect
[
24
,
25
]
.
This makes context selection a natural source of non-monotone objectives:
more candidate passages can improve coverage, but more prompt context can
also dilute attention or introduce distracting evidence.

A non-monotone submodular proxy.

We introduce a
non-monotone submodular proxy

\hat{f}(S)=\mathrm{FL}(S)-\theta(|S|)
, where
\mathrm{FL}
is
facility location on TF-IDF passage similarities and
\theta
is a convex,
non-decreasing penalty fitted via isotonic regression from LLM evaluation
data.
Departing from prior submodular-LLM work

[
21
,
22
,
23
,
40
]
,
which uses monotone facility location exclusively, our proxy captures the
empirically observed quality degradation from context overload.
The function
\hat{f}
is submodular (proof in Appendix
L
).

HotpotQA proxy containment.

We test on HotpotQA (distractor setting) with expanded ground sets
of
n=50
(
k=3
) and
n=500
(
k=5
);
setup details are in Appendix
L
.
On the fitted proxy, SeqDisjoint and WindowMax achieve
\alpha=1.0
at every budget
(Table
7
, Appendix
L
);
a larger
n=500
FL-only stress test in Appendix
L

shows that
50\times
compression preserves FL containment
\alpha=1.000
.

Non-monotonicity prevalence and LLM validation.

Among 200 HotpotQA questions evaluated with a local 120B-parameter LLM,

77/199=38.7\%
have some shorter prefix with higher F1 than the full
10-passage context, confirming that non-monotonicity is common enough that
monotone-only pruning can miss the relevant regime.
To verify that proxy containment translates to real answer quality,
we evaluate the final
k
-subsets with the LLM (Table
3
).
At
\omega=2
(
p=6
), proxy-guided greedy selects only
k=3
passages
yet achieves F1/EM
=0.599/0.42
, surpassing the all-original baseline
(F1/EM
=0.556/0.40
, all 10 passages). This supports the proxy claim:
pruning can remove distractors while preserving enough evidence for the reader.
QuickPrune, which collapses on non-monotone inputs,
scores only F1
=0.262
—no better than random.

Relevance-gated passage selection (MuSiQue).

HotpotQA supports the proxy claim, but its gold passages are highly query-salient
(top-

{\sim}80\%

MuSiQue

On a 50-question pilot, RFL with embedding-based

These experiments suggest that non-monotone submodular pruning is a

6
Conclusion

In this paper, we make substantial progress towards resolving the complexity of pruning for submodular
maximization.
Monotone containment is tight at
1-1/e
: greedy achieves it and no algorithm can beat it, regardless of pruning budget (Theorem
3.1
).
For non-monotone functions, two independent algorithms achieve
1/2-\varepsilon
containment under cardinality constraints (Theorems
4.1
,
4.3
), with a knapsack extension (Theorem
4.5
).
Since
1/2
exceeds the best non-monotone maximization ratio (
0.401
) and the known hardness threshold (
0.478
), pruning is provably easier than optimization.
Several open questions remain: the gap from
[1/2,1-1/e]
in the non-monotone setting, and tight algorithms for more sophisticated constraints in the monotone setting.
Empirically, pruning let an exact IP solver run
{\approx}620\times
faster on a reduced MaxCut instance; LLM context selection exhibited genuinely non-monotone behavior in the HotpotQA study (
38.7\%
of questions), and a relevance-gated submodular proxy improved gold-passage recall by
+9.2
percentage points on a 50-question MuSiQue pilot.

Appendix A
Additional Related Work

Ground-set pruning.

Nath and Kuhnle
[
19
]
introduced containment pruning
and gave the first constant-factor guarantee
(
\alpha\geq 1/24-\varepsilon
) for monotone,
\gamma
-weakly submodular
functions under a knapsack constraint via
QuickPrune
.
Zhou et al.
[
26
]
gave a heuristic
ground-set reduction via “pruned submodularity graphs,” but without
containment guarantees.
Several learned approaches—GCOMB
[
27
]
,
LeNSE
[
28
]
, and
COMBHelper
[
29
]
—train pruning policies from data
but likewise lack approximation guarantees; Nath and
Kuhnle showed that
QuickPrune
empirically dominates them
on the monotone benchmarks studied there.

Bicriteria vs. containment.

The bicriteria framework of Feldman and
Kuhnle
[
2
]
outputs
1.44k
elements containing
a
(1-1/e)
-approximate
solution
.
This differs from containment in three respects:
(i) bicriteria finds a specific approximate solution, whereas
containment guarantees that near-optimal solutions
exist
in the
pruned set for downstream re-optimization;
(ii) bicriteria relaxes the budget (
1.44k
vs. strict
k
), whereas
containment enforces
|T|\leq k^{\prime}
;
(iii) bicriteria targets a single budget, whereas our model requires
containment at all
k^{\prime}\leq k
simultaneously.
The two models are complementary: bicriteria is stronger when one
approximate solution suffices, containment is stronger when the pruned
set must support multiple downstream tasks or budgets.

Coresets and distributed reduction.

Composable coresets
[
33
,
34
]

reduce the ground set to
O(k)
elements for distributed submodular
maximization.
Mirrokni and Zadimoghaddam
[
33
]
achieve

1/3
-approximation for monotone functions by merging local greedy
summaries—this can be viewed as fixed-budget containment.
However, their guarantee holds for a single fixed
k
and monotone
objectives only; our budget-range formulation requires containment at all

k^{\prime}\leq k
simultaneously, our
1/2
containment handles non-monotone
functions, and our monotone bound
1-1/e
is strictly stronger than
1/3
.

Two-stage and streaming approaches.

Balkanski et al.
[
35
]
study a
two-stage model achieving
\approx(1-1/e)
-approximation,
but their output is a
solution
, not a pruned universe with
containment guarantees.
Streaming algorithms similarly produce approximate solutions rather than
pruned universes amenable to re-optimization:
Sieve-Streaming
[
37
]
achieves

(1/2-\varepsilon)
, and the optimal streaming bound was established by
Alaluf et al.
[
15
]
.
Wei et al.
[
36
]
use cheap surrogate submodular functions
for multi-round data selection—the “optimize a proxy, then solve
downstream” paradigm that our LLM experiments instantiate with the
non-monotone proxy
\hat{f}
.

Hardness constructions.

Our monotone lower bound uses the planted-set construction of
Harshaw et al.
[
16
]
,
which hides a planted optimal set in the ground set: until the query set
crosses an overlap threshold, the function is indistinguishable from a
null instance with no hidden structure.
This makes planted-set constructions well-suited to pruning hardness,
because any algorithm’s output looks random with respect to the hidden
optimum.
The key technical insight for our result is that scaling

|N|\propto p
makes the expected overlap between a
p
-element pruned
set and the planted optimum
independent of
p
, so extra budget
provably does not help.
This differs fundamentally from the
symmetry gap

technique
[
7
,
6
,
5
]
—the standard
tool for submodular maximization hardness—which exploits
group-invariance: the hard instance is a symmetrized function whose value
depends only on a coarse orbit statistic (roughly, how the queried set
distributes across a hidden partition of
N
), so
every

orbit-balanced subset of sufficient size is near-optimal.
Any pruning algorithm that retains an orbit-balanced slice of
N
—e.g.,
a uniform random
O(k)
-subset—thus achieves containment close to the
maximization ratio, making symmetry-gap constructions unsuitable for
pruning lower bounds.
The curvature-dependent hardness of
Sviridenko et al.
[
18
]

inspires Corollary
3.3
.

Non-monotone submodular maximization.

Our containment results sit within the non-monotone approximation
landscape anchored by two numbers: the
0.401
-approximation of
Buchbinder and Feldman
[
4
]
(best algorithm)
and the
0.478
-hardness of Qi
[
5
]
(strongest lower bound).
The
1/2
-hardness of Feige et al.
[
8
]

for unconstrained non-monotone maximization is suggestive—our two
independent
1/2
-containment proofs match this threshold—but no
formal reduction to the containment setting is known.

Submodular optimization for NLP and LLMs.

Lin and Bilmes
[
40
]
introduced facility-location
functions for document summarization, establishing the submodular
proxy paradigm.
A growing line of work applies such proxies to LLM pipelines:
Kumari et al.
[
21
]
for in-context example
retrieval, BumbleBee
[
22
]
for KV-cache
management, DELIFT
[
23
]
for instruction
fine-tuning.
All perform
selection
—choosing a single set of items to
include—using monotone proxies, and none address what guarantees the
pruning stage itself provides.
Our work differs in two ways: we study
pruning
(reducing the
ground set so that downstream solvers can find near-optimal solutions
at any budget), and we introduce the first
non-monotone
proxy
for context selection.

Non-monotonicity in LLM context.

Liu et al.
[
24
]
document the “lost in the middle”
phenomenon—LLMs degrade when relevant information is buried in long
contexts—and Du et al.
[
25
]
show that context length
alone hurts performance even with perfect retrieval.
These findings motivate our non-monotone proxy

\hat{f}=\mathrm{FL}-\theta
: the convex penalty
\theta
,
fitted from LLM evaluations via isotonic regression,
models the empirical quality degradation that monotone proxies
cannot capture.

BFNS Continuous Double Greedy with warm start.

For completeness, we state the three-term guarantee from
the Continuous Double Greedy (CDG) of
[
3
]
, which we use in
Appendix
I
to analyze a natural warm-start approach
(and show it cannot beat
1/2
):

Theorem B.1

(BFNS Theorem 3.7, adapted)
.

For a non-negative submodular function
g
on a ground set of size
n
with
cardinality constraint
k
, let
v=n/k-1
and
s=\sqrt{v}
.
CDG outputs a set
D
with
|D|\leq k
satisfying

g(D)\;\geq\;\frac{2s}{(s+1)^{2}}\,g(\mathrm{OPT}_{g})\;+\;\frac{v}{(s+1)^{2}}\,g(\emptyset)\;+\;\frac{1}{(s+1)^{2}}\,g(N).

In particular, at
v=4
(density
c=1/5
), the coefficients on

g(\mathrm{OPT}_{g})
and
g(\emptyset)
are both
4/9
.

The
g(\emptyset)
term is normally zero for normalized functions, but becomes
significant when CDG is applied to
g(S)=f(S\cup A)
for a “warm start”
set
A
: then
g(\emptyset)=f(A)
, which can be large.

Appendix C
Fast Budget-Range Pruning

The monotone hardness shows that
\alpha=1-1/e
is the
information-theoretic ceiling, and standard greedy
[
1
]

attains it at
|P|=k
in
O(nk)
value queries by virtue of its
prefix property
[
19
]
: the size-
k^{\prime}
prefix of greedy’s
output achieves
(1-1/e)\,\mathrm{OPT}_{k^{\prime}}
for every
k^{\prime}\leq k
.
For very large ground sets the
O(nk)
query cost is the bottleneck,
so it is natural to ask whether budget-range containment at the full

(1-1/e-\varepsilon)
ratio is attainable with near-linear queries.

A simple application of existing single-budget machinery answers this
affirmatively.
Let
\textsc{ThresholdGreedy}(N,f,k^{\prime},\eta)
denote Badanidiyuru and
Vondrák’s decreasing-threshold
algorithm
[
38
]
, which for monotone
submodular
f
returns
S
with
|S|\leq k^{\prime}
,

.

Theorem C.1

(Fast budget-range pruning)
.

Let

be monotone submodular,

k\leq|N|
, and
\varepsilon\in(0,1/2)

Set

Then

, the construction uses

\tilde{O}(n/\varepsilon^{2})
value queries, and for every

and

Proof overview.

The proof uses two external tools and is otherwise a direct construction.

Step 1: Threshold greedy (imported).

Run Badanidiyuru & Vondrák’s decreasing-threshold
algorithm
[
38
]
at a geometric grid of budgets.
Its guarantee is stated in Fact

below.

Step 2: Rounding down (imported).

When the witness

overshoots the target budget

, thin it to a

-subset using the standard monotone-submodular

).

Step 3: Union bound (original).

The union of all threshold-greedy outputs has total size
O(k/\varepsilon)

and query cost
\tilde{O}(n/\varepsilon^{2})
.

Fact C.2

(ThresholdGreedy guarantee)
.

The decreasing-threshold greedy algorithm of
Badanidiyuru and Vondrák
[
38
]
has the following
guarantee.
For monotone submodular
f
with cardinality constraint
k^{\prime}

and accuracy parameter
\eta\in(0,1)
,

\textsc{ThresholdGreedy}(N,f,k^{\prime},\eta)
returns

with

|S|\leq k^{\prime}
,
f(S)\geq(1-1/e-\eta)\mathrm{OPT}_{k^{\prime}}
,
using

value queries.

Fact C.3

(Random-subset bound)
.

For monotone submodular functions, the following standard random-subset
bound follows from Nemhauser and Wolsey
[
1
]
.
Let
f
be monotone submodular and let

be a set of size
s
.
If

Proof.

Size.

The exact small-budget part contributes at most

O(\min\{k^{2},\eta^{-2}\})
, which is
O(k/\eta)
whether

k\leq 1/\eta
or
k>1/\eta
.
The geometric part contributes

\sum_{j}O((1+\eta)^{j})=O(k/\eta)
.
Thus
|P|\leq\sum_{q\in\mathcal{K}}q=O(k/\eta)=O(k/\varepsilon)
.

Queries.

There are
O(\eta^{-1}+\log k/\eta)
calls to

, each using
O((n/\eta)\log(n/\eta))

queries, for a total of
\tilde{O}(n/\eta^{2})=\tilde{O}(n/\varepsilon^{2})
.

Containment.

Fix
k^{\prime}\leq k
.
By construction there is a

with

k^{\prime}\leq q\leq(1+2\eta)k^{\prime}
: if
k^{\prime}\leq\lceil 1/\eta\rceil
then take

, and otherwise take the first geometric budget at least
k^{\prime}

(the ceiling contributes at most
\eta k^{\prime}
).
By monotonicity of

, so

If

.
Otherwise, let

-subset of

By Fact

\mathbb{E}[f(Q)]\;\geq\;\frac{k^{\prime}}{|S_{q}|}f(S_{q})\;\geq\;\frac{1}{1+2\eta}f(S_{q})\;\geq\;(1-2\eta)f(S_{q}).

Therefore

\mathbb{E}[f(Q)]\;\geq\;(1-2\eta)(1-1/e-\eta)\,\mathrm{OPT}_{k^{\prime}}\;\geq\;(1-1/e-\varepsilon)\,\mathrm{OPT}_{k^{\prime}},

where the last inequality uses
\eta=\varepsilon/4
and

.
Hence some realization of the thinning gives the claimed exact-size witness.
∎

(Rounding down witnesses)

Knapsack Proof Sketch and Additional Remarks

Proof sketch.

Remark D.1

(Simultaneous guarantee across budgets)
.

The item-splitting machinery is imported from

Remark D.2

(Knapsack hardness inherited from cardinality)
.

The monotone hardness of Theorem

(

Remark D.3

(Why the boundary term is explicit)
.

A tempting shortcut is to drop the boundary fragment and compare the lost

singleton-style guarantee for a

Proof overview.

The proof of Theorem

proceeds in three steps.

Setup.

We import the HFWK planted family and properties (P1)–(P4), but instantiate it
on an

ground set with

instead of

m=\lceil 3k/\varepsilon^{\prime}\rceil

The enlargement is our adaptation: it ensures that

Indistinguishability.

We show that a polynomial-query algorithm cannot distinguish

Overlap bound.

Since the algorithm’s output

\mathbb{E}[|P\cap\tilde{T}|]\leq\frac{pk}{m}\leq\frac{\varepsilon^{\prime}k}{3}.

Concentration shows the overlap stays below the threshold

Fact E.1

(Hypergeometric concentration
[
17
]
)
.

Let
X\sim\mathrm{Hypergeometric}(m,K,n)

counts
marked items when drawing
n
items without replacement from a population
of
m
containing
K
marked items. Write
\mu=nK/m
.

(i)

(Additive tail.)
For every
t>0

\;\Pr[X-\mu\geq t]\leq\exp(-2t^{2}/n)
.

(ii)

(Multiplicative tail.)
For every integer
a>\mu
,

\;\Pr[X\geq a]\leq(e\mu/a)^{a}
.

Both follow from Hoeffding’s Theorem 4, which shows that hypergeometric
tails are dominated by binomial tails; bound (i) is then the
standard sub-Gaussian tail, and (ii) is the Chernoff–Poisson
bound applied to the dominating binomial.

Fix
\gamma\in(0,1]
,
p\geq k
, and
\varepsilon>0
.
We may assume
\varepsilon\leq 1
; larger

only weakens the
target bound. Set
\varepsilon^{\prime}=\varepsilon/20
, so

\varepsilon^{\prime}\in(0,1/6)

k

is absorbed into the theorem’s constant

.
Set

.
We instantiate the adapted HFWK family stated above on a
ground set
\mathcal{N}
of size

, all properties
(P1)–(P4) hold for this instance.
The only quantity that changes relative to HFWK’s original setting is
the overlap threshold

g=\left\lceil\varepsilon^{\prime}k+\frac{3k^{2}}{m}\right\rceil\leq\left\lceil\varepsilon^{\prime}k\left(1+\frac{k}{p}\right)\right\rceil\leq 2\varepsilon^{\prime}k+1,

where the last inequality uses
k/p\leq 1
.
By Yao’s minimax principle, it suffices to show the bound for
deterministic algorithms against the uniform distribution over

\{f_{\tilde{T}}\}
, where
\tilde{T}
is a random

\mathcal{N}
.
Let

be a deterministic algorithm

Step 1: Indistinguishability.

Goal:

(which has no planted optimum) or the

(which does).

When given

with

.

(i) with

\displaystyle\leq\Pr[\mathcal{G}]\cdot(1-e^{-\gamma}+12\varepsilon^{\prime})+\Pr[\neg\mathcal{G}]\cdot 1

Appendix G
Full Proof of Theorem
4.1

Theorem

(Theorem
4.1
, restated)
.

Let
f
be a non-negative submodular function on ground set
N
, and let

k\leq n
.
For any
\varepsilon>0
, there exists a set
G\subseteq N
with

|G|=O(k/\varepsilon)
such that for every
k^{\prime}\leq k
,

\max_{T\subseteq G,\,|T|\leq k^{\prime}}f(T)\;\geq\;\left(\tfrac{1}{2}-\varepsilon\right)\cdot\mathrm{OPT}_{k^{\prime}},

where
\mathrm{OPT}_{k^{\prime}}=\max_{|S|\leq k^{\prime}}f(S)
.
Moreover,
G
can be constructed in
O(nk/\varepsilon)
oracle queries.

Proof overview.

The proof uses the
sequential disjoint greedy
(SDG) scheme and
proceeds in four steps, all original to this paper.
The high-level idea: run greedy many times on disjoint parts of the ground set, find the best run by averaging, then show that this run’s greedy prefix captures at least half the optimal value.

Step 1: Averaging.

Build
\ell
disjoint greedy solutions.
Because the runs use disjoint elements, their total “damage” to the optimal value is bounded by submodularity (Fact
G.1
).
Averaging selects the run that complements OPT best, losing only a
(1-1/\ell)
factor.

Step 2: Partial-greedy domination.

The elements of
\hat{O}=O\setminus G
(the “missed” OPT elements) were available as candidates during the chosen greedy run but were not picked.
Since greedy always picks the highest-marginal element, each greedy pick dominates the corresponding missed element.
Telescoping gives
f(\hat{O}\cup A)\leq 2f(A)-f(\emptyset)
: the greedy prefix captures at least half the value of the prefix augmented with the missed elements.

Step 3: Submodularity decomposition.

The lattice inequality
f(X\cup Y)+f(X\cap Y)\leq f(X)+f(Y)
,
applied to
X=\hat{O}\cup A
and
Y=\dot{O}\cup A
,
relates the value of
O\cup A
to the two feasible candidates
A
and
\dot{O}\cup A
.

Step 4: Feasibility.

Both
A
and
\dot{O}\cup A
have size at most
k^{\prime}
and lie in
G
, so the maximum over them
gives the claimed
1/2-\varepsilon
containment.

The argument uses one standard submodularity fact for disjoint sets,
stated for reference:

Fact G.1

(Submodularity of disjoint-union marginals)
.

Let
f
be non-negative and submodular, and let

A_{1},\ldots,A_{\ell}
be pairwise disjoint.
Then for any set
O
:

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}f(A_{i}\mid O)

\displaystyle\geq f\!\bigl(\textstyle\bigcup_{i}A_{i}\,\big|\,O\bigr),

\displaystyle f\!\bigl(\textstyle\bigcup_{i}A_{i}\,\big|\,O\bigr)

\displaystyle\leq f\!\bigl(\textstyle\bigcup_{i}A_{i}\cup O\bigr).

This follows from repeated application of the diminishing-returns property
of submodularity: adding disjoint sets one at a time, each marginal is at
least as large as the joint marginal.

Proof of Theorem

.

Set

Thus each greedy pick

\displaystyle f(A)-f(\emptyset)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{b}f(a_{j}\mid A_{j-1})\geq\sum_{j=1}^{b}f(\hat{o}_{j}\mid A_{j-1})\geq\sum_{j=1}^{b}f(\hat{o}_{j}\mid A)

(1)

(2)

Appendix H
Item-Splitting Machinery and Proof of Theorem
4.5

This appendix makes the knapsack extension fully self-contained.

Why item splitting is needed.

In the cardinality setting, each greedy prefix of length
b
matches the budget exactly.
With knapsack constraints, a density-greedy prefix may overshoot the target budget:
the last item added may have cost larger than the remaining budget allows.
Item splitting resolves this by conceptually breaking the boundary item into two
fragments—one that fits the budget and one that does not—while preserving
submodularity of the objective function.

The item-splitting construction is due to Feldman and
Kuhnle
[
2
]
(Definitions 7.13 and 7.15 there);
we restate the pieces needed for Theorem
4.5

and defer the submodularity-preservation proof
to
[
2
]
.

H.1
The
\gamma
-split extension

Definition H.1

(Gamma-split)
.

This is the split operation of Feldman and Kuhnle
[
2
]
.
Let
f\colon 2^{N}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
be submodular with positive price
vector
p
. For
s\in N
and
\gamma\in(0,1)
, the
\gamma
-split
of
s
produces a new ground set
N^{*}=(N\setminus\{s\})\cup\{\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2}\}
, prices
p^{*}_{\sigma_{1}}=\gamma p_{s}
,

p^{*}_{\sigma_{2}}=(1-\gamma)p_{s}
,
p^{*}_{u}=p_{u}
otherwise, and
function

f^{*}(S)\;=\;f(\widehat{S})\;+\;\gamma\cdot f(s\mid\widehat{S})\cdot\mathbb{I}[\sigma_{1}\in S]\;+\;(1-\gamma)\cdot f(s\mid\widehat{S})\cdot\mathbb{I}[\sigma_{2}\in S],

where
\widehat{S}=S\setminus\{\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2}\}
. When

S\cap\{\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2}\}=\emptyset
,
f^{*}(S)=f(S)
. When

\{\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2}\}\subseteq S
,
f^{*}(S)=f(\widehat{S}+s)
.

Lemma H.2

(Split preserves submodularity)
.

Feldman and Kuhnle
[
2
, Lemma 7.14]
prove that
the function
f^{*}
of Definition
H.1
is non-negative
and submodular on
N^{*}
. Iterating the split operation finitely many times
preserves both properties.

Observation H.3

(Density preservation)
.

This is the density-preservation property from
Feldman and Kuhnle
[
2
, Obs. 7.16]
.
After a
\gamma
-split of
s
, for any set
S\subseteq N^{*}
with

\sigma_{1}\notin S
resp.
\sigma_{2}\notin S
,

f^{*}(\sigma_{1}\mid S)\;=\;\gamma\cdot f(s\mid\widehat{S}),\qquad f^{*}(\sigma_{2}\mid S)\;=\;(1-\gamma)\cdot f(s\mid\widehat{S}).

Together with
p^{*}_{\sigma_{i}}=\gamma_{i}p_{s}
(with
\gamma_{1}=\gamma
,

\gamma_{2}=1-\gamma
), this gives the
density equality

f^{*}(\sigma_{i}\mid A_{\sigma_{i}})/p^{*}_{\sigma_{i}}=f(s\mid A_{s})/p_{s}
, where
A_{s}
is the greedy prefix up to
s
and

A_{\sigma_{i}}
is its image under the split.
Moreover, the equality is stable under iteration: if
s
itself was
produced by a prior
\gamma^{\prime}
-split of an ancestor
\tilde{s}
, then

f^{*}(s\mid A_{s})/p^{*}_{s}=f^{**}(\tilde{s}\mid A_{\tilde{s}})/p^{**}_{\tilde{s}}

by the same identity applied to that earlier split, and inductively
every fragment
u
in the descendants of
\tilde{s}
satisfies

f^{*}(u\mid A_{u})/p^{*}_{u}=f(\tilde{s}\mid A_{\tilde{s}})/p_{\tilde{s}}
,
i.e. the density class of an element is preserved across any
finite sequence of splits applied to it or to disjoint elements
of the prefix.

In this subsection

We write

.

(after possibly splitting some elements of

(Iterative theta-construction)

This is the iterative construction of

[

Initially

. Otherwise

Termination.
Call

Lemma H.5

(Density-domination lemma)
.

This lemma is adapted from the density-preservation observation of
Feldman and Kuhnle
[
2
, Obs. 7.16]
.
Let
\theta\colon U\to V
be the mapping of

H.4
, where

is a density-greedy-ordered

Then for every

and every

\frac{f^{*}(u\mid A_{a})}{p^{*}_{u}}\;\leq\;\frac{f^{*}(a\mid A_{a})}{p^{*}_{a}}.

Consequently,

\sum_{u\in\theta^{-1}(a)}f^{*}(u\mid A_{a})\;\leq\;\frac{f^{*}(a\mid A_{a})}{p^{*}_{a}}\sum_{u\in\theta^{-1}(a)}p^{*}_{u}\;\leq\;f^{*}(a\mid A_{a}),

be

The original item

was a density-greedy candidate when

, hence its density was no larger than the density

. This gives the individual density bound. Multiplying it by

gives the displayed

(Theorem

For any

This is the most technical proof in the paper, and the reader may wish to

The main complication beyond cardinality is that density-greedy prefixes may

The item-splitting machinery of Feldman &

The proof then proceeds in five steps:

For each density-greedy run, identify the prefix whose total cost equals the
residual budget

The disjoint-collection argument from Fact

f^{*}
, identifies the best run

with

f^{*}(O^{\uparrow}\cup A)\geq(1-1/\ell)f(O)
.

Step 3: Density domination (imported+original).

Apply the

-construction (Definition

) to map

into the greedy prefix

.

.

Step 4: Submodular decomposition (original).

Apply the lattice inequality

f^{*}(X\cup Y)+f^{*}(X\cap Y)\leq f^{*}(X)+f^{*}(Y)
to relate

f^{*}(O^{\uparrow}\cup A)
to the two candidates.

Step 5: Feasible real subset (original).

Convert the split-function guarantee back to a real-set guarantee, paying the
boundary-fragment loss
\Delta_{B^{\prime}}
.

P_{k}

and
S_{m}\subseteq S_{k}\subseteq P_{k}
.

Both
S_{m}
and
\dot{O}\cup S_{m}
are
k^{\prime}
-feasible subsets of
P_{k}
:

|S_{m}|=m\leq k^{\prime}
and

|\dot{O}\cup S_{m}|\leq|\dot{O}|+m\leq|O|\leq k^{\prime}
.

By submodularity with
A=\dot{O}\cup S_{m}
and
B=D\cup S_{m}

(so
A\cap B=S_{m}
and
A\cup B=O\cup S_{m}
):

f(O\cup S_{m})+f(S_{m})\leq f(\dot{O}\cup S_{m})+f(D\cup S_{m}).

Substituting the domination bound
f(D\cup S_{m})\leq 2f(S_{m})-f(\emptyset)
:

f(\dot{O}\cup S_{m})\geq f(O\cup S_{m})-f(S_{m})+f(\emptyset),

so
\max(f(\dot{O}\cup S_{m}),\,f(S_{m}))\geq(f(O\cup S_{m})+f(\emptyset))/2
.
In expectation, Lemma
J.3
gives

\mathbb{E}[f(O\cup S_{m})]\geq e^{-1/\omega}(1-O(1/(\omega^{2}k)))\,\mathrm{OPT}_{k^{\prime}}
,
yielding the claimed bound asymptotically in
k
.
Since
k^{\prime}\leq k
was arbitrary, the same accumulated set
P_{k}

certifies containment at every scale.
∎

Why random selection resolves the tension.

The earlier conditional version required max-marginal selection for
domination and random selection for degradation—conflicting requirements.
Window containment resolves this because the top-
w
construction ensures

every
window element dominates every non-window element, including
the uniformly random pick. No max-marginal selection within
W_{t}
is needed.

Appendix K
Additional Open Problems

The two central open problems (tight non-monotone containment and
extraction beyond the
0.401
maximization barrier) are stated in
§
6
.
We collect here three further structural questions suggested by our
proofs.

Open Question K.1

(Non-monotone lower bounds)
.

Develop pruning lower-bound techniques beyond density composition.
Composition is structurally inadequate for non-monotone due to the flat
approximation landscape.
Information-theoretic arguments about distinguishing “useful” from “poisonous”
elements may be needed.

Open Question K.2

(Density-dependent hardness for non-monotone)
.

Is there a density-dependent inapproximability result for non-monotone
cardinality-constrained maximization?
The current hardness of
0.478
(Qi) is essentially density-independent.
A density-dependent hardness that approaches
1/e
at low density would
restore the composition technique’s power for non-monotone pruning.

Open Question K.3

(Monotonicity of the top-
k
set)
.

If
M_{i}
(the top
k
marginals at step
i
of random greedy) could be
shown to induce a “locally monotone” subproblem—that is, if
f

restricted to subsets of
M_{i}
were monotone—then
(1-1/e)

containment would follow immediately.
OPT “witnesses” this:
f
restricted to subsets of
O
is monotone
(by optimality).
Can this structural property be extended to algorithmically identified
sets without knowledge of
O
?

LLM experiment details.

Reader model and decoding.

All LLM reader evaluations use
openai/gpt-oss-120b
served locally
through the OpenAI-compatible vLLM API. The model is the Apache 2.0
open-weight reader listed in Appendix
N
. We query two local
endpoints in round-robin order,
localhost:8000/v1
and

localhost:8003/v1
. Decoding is deterministic:
temperature
0.0
and
max_tokens
=256
.
For every evaluated passage set, the prompt template is:

Answer the following question based on the provided context. Give a short,
direct answer.

Context:

[title] passage text

Question: question text

Answer:

For multiple passages, the context block concatenates titled passages separated
by blank lines. For
gpt-oss
responses we read the first nonempty field
among
message.content
,
reasoning
, and

reasoning_content
, matching the local vLLM behavior.

Scoring.

HotpotQA answers are scored by normalized token F1 and exact match: lowercase,
remove punctuation and the articles “a”, “an”, and “the”, collapse
whitespace, then compute token overlap F1 or exact normalized-string equality.
MuSiQue LLM validation uses the same normalization, but reports the best F1/EM
over the official answer and all answer aliases.

Submodularity of the proxy.

The function
\hat{f}(S)=\mathrm{FL}(S)-\theta(|S|)
is submodular
because the decreasing marginal gains of
\mathrm{FL}
and the
increasing marginal penalty of convex
\theta
reinforce each other:
for any
A\subseteq B
and element
e\notin B
,

\hat{f}(A\cup\{e\})-\hat{f}(A)\geq\hat{f}(B\cup\{e\})-\hat{f}(B)
,
since the
\mathrm{FL}
marginal shrinks while the
\theta
marginal grows.

HotpotQA ground-set construction.

For each question, we pool its 10 original passages (2 gold, 8 distractors)
with distractor passages from other questions, giving
n=50
(
k=3
)
and
n=500
(
k=5
).
We evaluate on the fitted proxy
\hat{f}(S)=\mathrm{FL}(S)-\theta(|S|)
,
where
\mathrm{FL}
is facility location on TF-IDF similarities and

\theta
is fitted from LLM evaluation data. The fitted-proxy file used in
the paper,
fitted_proxy_200q.json
, is based on

exp2_results_200q.json
: 200 HotpotQA development questions, one
random prefix ordering per question, and 1,992 recorded LLM evaluations over
context sizes 1–10. All but one question have all 10 prefix sizes recorded;
one question has sizes 1 and 2 only. This is why the fitted-proxy file reports

n_results
=1992
, not
200\times 10
.
The proxy-containment entries in Tables
7

and
3
use
exp4_results.json
. The reader
F1/EM entries for the
\omega=2
StdGreedy and SeqDisjoint rows use

exp5_llm_c2_50q.json
; the
\omega=5
, QuickPrune, window,
random, gold-only, and all-original rows use

exp5_llm_validation_50q.json
, which records 451 reader calls
including the initial endpoint sanity check.
The proxy is not the reader score itself; it is a cheap objective used for
pruning, and we separately validate the selected passages with the LLM.
Thus proxy containment only says that the reduced pool preserves high-scoring
candidate contexts under
\hat{f}
; the reader experiment checks whether those
candidate contexts are useful for answering.

MuSiQue pilot setup.

We use the MuSiQue v1.0 answerable development split, keep questions with at
least three decomposition hops, shuffle with seed 42, and take the first
50 questions. Each question supplies its native 20 candidate paragraphs; gold
passages are the paragraphs marked as supporting. We evaluate

k\in\{3,5,7,10\}
and
\tau\in\{0.1,0.2,0.3,0.5\}
. Passage embeddings are
computed with

Restricted facility-location (RFL) function.

We define

f_{\tau}(S)=\sum_{v:\,\mathrm{rel}(v,q)>\tau}\max_{s\in S}\mathrm{sim}(v,s)
,
which restricts FL coverage to passages above a relevance threshold
\tau
,
diversifying
within
the query-relevant neighborhood.
The headline method uses embedding cosine similarity for
\mathrm{sim}
and
embedding query similarity for
\mathrm{rel}
, with
\tau=0.3
. We also report
TF-IDF-gated variants in the result file. Gold-passage recall is the fraction
of supporting passages selected.

Per-
k
breakdown and gating comparison.

At
\tau=0.3
, RFL with embedding-based gating
trails top-
k
embedding at
k=3
, ties at
k=5
, and wins at
k=10
,
consistent with the diversity mechanism becoming effective only when
the number of selected passages exceeds the number of trivially salient ones.
At
k=10
, RFL reaches recall
0.740
, which is
2.0
percentage points
above top-
k
embedding (
0.720
) and
9.2
percentage points above
top-
k
TF-IDF (
0.648
).
TF-IDF-based gating fails—it filters out later-hop gold passages whose
vocabulary diverges from the query—confirming that semantic embedding
similarity is essential for bridging entity gaps in multi-hop reasoning.

Bootstrap confidence intervals.

The headline MuSiQue confidence interval is a paired question-level bootstrap
over the 50 pilot questions. For each of 10,000 resamples with replacement
(seed 42), we resample questions and recompute the mean gold-recall difference
between
rfl_emb_tau=0.3
and the baseline. The reported

[+2.8,+15.5]
percentage-point interval is the 2.5/97.5 percentile interval for

rfl_emb_tau=0.3
minus
topk_tfidf
at
k=10
.

Reader saturation.

Despite the
+9.2
percentage-point gold-recall advantage, LLM answer F1 does not
improve: at
k=10
, top-
k
embedding achieves F1
=0.234
while
RFL achieves F1
=0.186
using the local 120B reader.
This
reader-saturation
effect—the reader model extracts answers
from the most salient passages regardless of additional evidence—is
consistent with the “lost in the middle” findings
of
[
24
]
: providing more gold passages helps recall
but does not help a reader that already attends preferentially to early
or highly salient context.
Improving the reader’s ability to synthesize dispersed evidence is an
important open direction; our contribution here is the evidence-coverage
mechanism itself, which the pruning theory certifies.
