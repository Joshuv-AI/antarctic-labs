CCL20-CCR6 axis in autoimmune disease and cancer progression: clinical and therapeutic implications
Authors: Guanhua Yu, An Zhang, Yixiao Liu, Tao Fan, Xinghao Wen, Chunxiang Li, Zheng Jiang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
C-C motif chemokine ligand 20 (CCL20) is widely expressed in various human tissues and immune cells, playing a critical role in immune cell migration and inflammation. As the sole ligand of CCL20, C-C motif chemokine receptor 6 (CCR6) is a G protein-coupled receptor mainly expressed in immune cells and mediates chemotactic function of CCL20. The CCL20-CCR6 axis actively participates in immune homeostasis and activation. Dysregulation of CCL20-CCR6 axis is regarded as an imperative part of the pathogenic mechanism of autoimmune diseases and cancers, and a multitude of therapeutic strategies have been developed to target CCL20-CCR6 axis. In this review, we comprehensively summarized researches of CCL20-CCR6 axis and introduced the biology and functions of CCL20-CCR6 under normal conditions. We further discussed the pivotal pathogenic roles of CCL20-CCR6 axis in progression of autoimmune diseases as well as cancers from multiple directions. The article also explored recent advancements in the field of CCL20-CCR6 axis inhibition in autoimmune diseases and cancers, shedding light on its potential as a promising therapeutic target. A systematic understanding of CCL20-CCR6 axis in progression of autoimmune diseases and cancers is crucial for realizing the full potential of CCL20-CCR6 axis-based interventions, heralding the promise of improved outcomes for patients.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12964-026-02969-y.

Introduction

Chemokines are a class of chemotactic cytokines produced and secreted by both immune and non-immune cells and play crucial roles in regulating immune cell trafficking, positioning, and tissue recruitment [1]. With a molecular mass of 8–10 kDa, chemokines exert their functions by binding to G protein-coupled receptors (GPCRs) on target cells. As research has progressed, chemokines have been recognized to play diverse roles in leukocyte development, differentiation, and migration, as well as in angiogenesis, wound healing, and tumorigenesis [1–5].

C-C motif chemokine ligand 20 (CCL20) is the sole known ligand for C-C motif chemokine receptor 6 (CCR6), and their interaction is highly specific [6]. CCR6, a member of the GPCR family, is expressed on the surface of dendritic cells (DCs), multiple T-cell subsets, and B cells. Therefore, the CCL20-CCR6 axis is essential for the chemotactic behavior of a wide range of immune cells under both homeostatic and inflammatory conditions [7–11]. At the mechanistic level, the CCL20-CCR6 axis generates a directed recruitment signal in different tissue microenvironments, thereby driving the migration and spatial localization of multiple CCR6⁺ immune cell subsets. For example, epithelial tissues can produce CCL20 in response to stimulation, which rapidly recruits immature DCs and their precursors to epithelial or mucosa-associated tissues in a CCR6-dependent manner. This process provides a foundation for subsequent antigen presentation and local immune responses [12]. Meanwhile, CCR6 is also regarded as an important homing receptor for specific effector T-cell subsets. Studies have shown that Th17 cells tend to migrate to the intestine and associated lymphoid tissues, and CCR6 plays a key role in their trafficking to microenvironments with high CCL20 expression, such as Peyer’s patches [13].

The CCL20-CCR6 axis plays an important role in both immune homeostasis and immune activation. Given the widespread involvement of this ligand-receptor pair in the human body, the CCL20-CCR6 axis is implicated in the pathogenesis and progression of various diseases, particularly autoimmune diseases and cancers [14–22].Further studies have shown that, in autoimmune diseases, the CCL20-CCR6 axis often forms a mutually reinforcing loop with the IL-23/IL-17 immune axis associated with Th17 cells. In arthritis models, inflammatory synovial cells and Th17 cells induce CCL20 production, which in turn selectively recruits pathogenic CCR6⁺ Th17 cells to inflamed joints. Blocking CCR6 significantly slows the progression of arthritis [15]. In psoriasis-like inflammation, CCR6 deficiency markedly attenuates the pathological skin changes induced by IL-23, suggesting that CCL20-CCR6-mediated recruitment of CCR6⁺ cells is an important step in sustaining the local IL-17/IL-22 inflammatory loop in the skin [18].

In tumors, the CCL20-CCR6 axis can contribute to tumor progression by promoting malignant cellular behaviors and activating related signaling pathways. More importantly, it mediates the selective recruitment and functional reprogramming of immune cells within the tumor microenvironment (TME). For example, in colorectal cancer, tumor cell-derived CCL20 recruits regulatory T (Treg) cells through CCR6 and promotes immunosuppression, chemotherapy resistance, and poor prognosis, indicating that this axis plays a central role in tumor-immune interactions [23]. In pancreatic cancer, CCL20 secreted by tumor-associated macrophages promotes tumor cell migration, epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT), and liver metastasis, further supporting its role in driving tumor progression within the TME [24].

After more than two decades of research, the CCL20-CCR6 axis has emerged as a potential therapeutic target for both neoplastic and non-neoplastic diseases, providing new insights into how these diseases may be addressed through direct targeting of protumor pathways and indirect modulation of the immune microenvironment [22, 25, 26]. This review provides a comprehensive summary of the biological properties of the CCL20-CCR6 axis. It focuses on recent advances in understanding its role in disease pathogenesis and on emerging therapeutic strategies, thereby offering a systematic framework to support the development of targeted interventions for autoimmune diseases and cancers.

CCL20-CCR6 axis biology

Structure of CCL20 gene and protein

The CCL20 gene, sitting at chromosome 2q 33–37, is a rather special one in terms of location compared to its first-generation CC counterparts which all locate at chromosome 17q 11.2, alienating CCL20 further from the rest of the group [27]. Generally, the composition of the CC chemokine subfamily is highly conserved, with normally three exons and two introns, while CCL20 gene is consists of four exons and three introns [27]. Another exquisite element relating to CCL20 gene is alternative splicing of its mRNA. CCL20 gene leads to two potential splice-sites at the border of intron1 and exon2, generating two open reading frames with a difference of 3 bases and furtherly two different precursor proteins, with one lacking a residue for Alanine (Ala) at position 27 (precursor of Exodus-1) and the other possessing 2 adjacent Ala at position 26 and 27 (precursor of LARC and MIP 3α) [27–29]. Notably, it is exactly after the first or the only alanine residue that the signal sequence cleavage site of the precursor proteins lies, generating mature proteins consisting of 70 or 69 amino acid residues respectively, subsequently referred to as CCL20 [1–67] and CCL20 [2–67] in the canonical chemokine nomenclature system. There are also a small number of CCL20 isoforms generated by cleavage at other positions, such as Exodus-1 which is also derived from the precursor protein that lacks an Ala, but generated by cutting off the signal sequence after the glycine at position 22. Post-translational modifications at the NH2/COOH end are common in the case of CCL 20, giving rise to more forms of variants, although it was found that such modifications rarely have an effect on the binding capacity and biological function of the isoforms [6, 27, 30, 31]. At the tissue and organ level, CCL20 is shown to be expressed in peripheral tissues, such as the skin, mucosal sites (lung and intestines), liver, and thymus [32, 33]. At the cellular level, CCL20 is secreted by endothelial cells and various immune cells, such as Th17 cells, natural killer cells, macrophages, DCs, and B cells [11, 34, 35].

Structure of CCR6 gene and protein

The genetic locus of the CCR6 gene in humans is on chromosome 6q27, while the majority of genes encoding CC chemokine receptors are located on chromosome 3p [36]. The CCR6 gene consists of a sequence of 139,737 nucleotide bases, which ultimately translates into a protein of 374 amino acids with a low molecular weight of 42 kDa [37]. CCR6 is a type of GPCR. GPCRs represent a highly prevalent class of receptors encoded in the human genome, comprising more than 800 distinct members and playing a pivotal role in transmitting nearly 80% of signal transduction across cellular membranes [38]. Through the activation of heterotrimeric G protein subunits, including Gα, Gβ and Gγ, and β-arrestin protein mediators, GPCRs transmit signals and participate in several fundamental biological processes, such as cellular homoeostasis, cell proliferation, and chemotaxis. Moreover, these receptors have been associated with numerous pathological conditions, including cancer, inflammation, and infection [39–41]. As a group of GPCRs, chemokine receptors are subject to regulation by chemokines and are characterized by their globular core shape stabilized 1 to 2 conserved disulfide bridges [42, 43]. The molecular receptors of chemokines are usually GPCRs with seven transmembrane regions and crosstalk between the ligands and receptors are quite common. However, CCR6 is the only high-affinity chemokine ligand of CCL20 [34, 36]. The CCR6 receptor is a transmembrane protein consisting of seven α-helices that are implanted in the cell membrane and are linked by three extracellular loops and three intracellular loops. The extracellular domain of CCR6, which is accountable for the binding of ligands, engages in interactions with G proteins, arrestins, and several downstream effectors. CCR6 is shown to be significantly upregulated in the intestinal mucosa, lung mucosa, lymph nodes, pancreas, spleen and particularly in epithelial tumors [44–47]. At the cellular level, the expression of CCR6 is upregulated in several kinds of immune cells, such as DCs, neutrophils, B cells, Th17 cells, regulatory T cells (Treg) and CD8+ T cells, which aligns with its proven role in immunity regulation and inflammation [47–52].

Systemic functions of CCL20-CCR6 axis

The CCL20-CCR6 axis plays a crucial role in the immune system and is indispensable for both immune homeostasis and inflammation (Fig. 1). As an inflammatory chemokine, CCL20 plays a key role in innate immunity and is upregulated by inflammatory cytokines, including tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α) and interleukin-1β (IL-1β) [53, 54]. CCL20 is rapidly upregulated in response to inflammatory stimuli, such as lipopolysaccharide (LPS) and microorganisms, leading to a marked influx of immune cells [55, 56]. As discussed previously, the range of leukocytes expressing CCR6 is extensive, among which Th17 and Treg stringently regulates immune tolerance in healthy individuals. CCR6 expression in Th17 and Treg is crucial in the maintenance of immunological homeostasis. By producing inflammatory cytokines, such as IL-17 A, IL-17 F, IL-21 and IL-22, CCR6-expressing Th17 contributes to tissue damage and illness [13, 57]. Moreover, Th17 cells also express CCL20 and can facilitate recruitment of Th17 and Treg cells into CCL20-expressing inflamed tissue through specific combination of CCL20 and CCR6 [11]. Conversely, CCR6-expressing Treg cells play a crucial role in promoting immunological tolerance via the release of anti-inflammatory cytokines including IL-35, IL-10 and transforming growth factor-β (TGF-β), therefore contributing to tissue restoration [58]. Interruption of this intricate alliance will result in recruitment of pro-inflammatory Th17 and regulatory Treg cells to the sites of infection or injury. Overall, CCL20 and CCR6 have been identified as imperative modulators that have a substantial influence on human health and illness. Under normal physiological conditions, CCL20-CCR6 axis serves a crucial function in maintaining immunological tolerance by up-regulating immune suppression. When this normal homeostatic function is disturbed, it can lead to a significant elevation of the T helper 1 (Th1) /Th17 axis, which in turn promotes detrimental immunological activity across neurological, pulmonary, gastrointestinal, excretory, skeletal, and reproductive systems and leads to multiple autoimmune diseases including multiple sclerosis (MS) [59–61], rheumatoid arthritis (RA) [15, 62–65], psoriasis [66–68] and inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) [14, 69–71]. Moreover, CCL20 secreted by tumor cells and tumor infiltrating immune cells further activates protumor signaling pathways and reprogram TME by recruiting CCR6-expressing immune cells to promote cancers progression [46, 72–75].

The dual role of the CCL20-CCR6 axis: immune activation and immunosuppression

Although dysregulated CCL20-CCR6 signaling can lead to immune cell recruitment in both settings, it drives excessive immune activation in autoimmune diseases but often promotes an immunosuppressive microenvironment in cancer. This difference is mainly attributable to the distinct local cytokine milieus in different lesions, which shape the fate and function of the recruited cells in different ways [76, 77].

Because both proinflammatory Th17 cells and anti-inflammatory Treg cells highly express CCR6, aberrant CCL20 release can recruit both cell types to target tissues in a nonselective manner [78]. Subsequent changes in the Th17/Treg ratio then lead to distinct outcomes. In autoimmune diseases, local tissues are enriched in potent proinflammatory cytokines, such as interleukin-6 (IL-6) and IL-23. This inflammatory microenvironment strongly promotes the survival and expansion of pathogenic Th17 cells while impairing the suppressive function of Treg cells, leading to a marked increase in the Th17/Treg ratio. This pronounced shift toward Th17 dominance disrupts peripheral immune tolerance and triggers excessive immune responses against self-tissues [79].

In contrast, in cancer, the TME is enriched in TGF-β and IL-10 but contains relatively low levels of IL-6. When CCR6⁺ cells enter the TME, high levels of TGF-β strongly stabilize Foxp3 expression, thereby preferentially promoting the local expansion of Treg cells while suppressing the effector functions of Th17 cells [80]. As a result, the Th17/Treg ratio decreases at the tumor site, ultimately allowing cancer cells to evade immune surveillance [81].

Role of CCL20-CCR6 axis in autoimmune diseases

Under non-pathological conditions, CCR6 shows low tissue-level expression in most non-lymphoid organs. This is largely because CCR6 expression is restricted to specific cell types and is predominantly found on particular leukocyte subsets, such as memory T cells and B cells, rather than being broadly expressed by parenchymal cells. In addition, many organs contain relatively few CCR6⁺ immune cells at steady state. Accordingly, lymphoid organs and certain mucosal tissues display comparatively higher CCR6 expression because of the enrichment of resident immune cells [82]. CCR6 has been found to be significantly overexpressed in IBD, RA, and other autoimmune diseases [26]. Accumulating clinical and animal studies indicate that disruption of immune homeostasis, caused by upregulation of the CCL20-CCR6 axis and an imbalance between Th17 and Treg cells, contributes to various inflammatory disease states. Rheumatoid arthritis

RA is a common inflammatory disease that causes joint destruction and clinically affects small symmetrical joints such as hands and feet. CCL20 levels in RA patients and affected joints were present in the high levels, and CCL20-CCR6 axis has been proved by genome-wide association cohort studies to play an important role in the pathogenesis of the disease [26]. Multiple genome-wide association studies have linked the CCR6 gene to increased risk of RA [16, 83]. CCL20 was secreted by activated synoviocytes and Th17 cells from the joints, suggesting that the CCR6-CCL20 axis was involved in recruiting CCR6+ Th17 cells [15]. This recruitment implied that the paracrine and autocrine signaling mechanism was at work, creating room for long-term inflammation. Deficiency of CCR6 in mice validated the reduced development of collagen-induced arthritis [84]. In peripheral blood and synovial fluid, the CCL20 level was increased in RA patients. In peripheral blood of RA patient were found to have significantly increased CD4+ CD45RO+ CCR6+ memory T cells with an increased level of CCL20 in joints, which helps the migration of the memory CD4+ T cells. CCL20/CCR6 axis was also found to be associated with the recruitment of monocytes at joints of RA patients. It was also evidenced by immature dendritic cells where CD1a+CCR6+ immature DCs migrated toward inflamed joints and can be visualized by co-localization with CCR6 and CCL20 staining. This finding suggested the chemotaxis of immature dendritic cells by CCL20 in the synovial joints of RA patients [85].

Psoriasis dermatitis

Psoriasis can be caused by skin injury, smoking, and genetic factors. Psoriasis is a common, inflammatory, and proliferative T cell-mediated inflammatory skin disease in which Th17 cells are believed to play a critical role [66]. CCL20/CCR6 is crucial in attracting inflammatory cells to the psoriasis lesions [66]. CCL20 is constitutively expressed at low levels in normal human skin by epidermal keratinocytes and endothelial cells and can be strongly enhanced by pro-inflammatory cytokines like TNF-α. This allows for the possibility of a cascade amplification of minor inflammation caused by either etiology through the previously mentioned positive feedback mechanism concerning Th17 cells and CCL20-CCR6 axis. The pathogenic Th17 cells showed a positive association with disease severity among psoriasis patients [86, 87]. In vivo study confirmed the upregulation of the CCL20/CCR6 axis in mouse models of psoriasis [88, 89]. Intradermal or subcutaneous injections of IL-23 reported a significant increase in TNF-α and IL-17 A, which was known to drive increased CCL20 expression and recruited CCR6+CD4+ T cells in the inflamed skin, while deficiency of CCR6 failed to show dermal inflammation [18].

Inflammatory bowel disease

IBD is a chronic inflammatory disorder of the gastrointestinal tract that comprises two main subtypes: Crohn’s disease (CD) and ulcerative colitis (UC). In recent years, Helene et al. have made important contributions to the study of the CCL20-CCR6 axis in IBD. They confirmed that CCL20 is increased in the colonic mucosa during active IBD, particularly in UC, and is present in both epithelial cells and immune cells within the lamina propria [70, 90]. he group further investigated CCL20 and CCR6 in peripheral blood mononuclear cells from patients with IBD and revealed that CCL20 acts as a first-line responder to danger-associated molecular patterns. They also reported that CCL20 release was more than twofold higher in UC than in CD. During active UC, a significant decrease in circulating CCR6⁺ cells was observed, indicating that these cells are chemoattracted from the circulation to intestinal lesions. Further in vitro experiments demonstrated that costimulation with CCL20 and TNF-α increased IL-1β release and recruited additional immune cells, thereby forming a local positive feedback loop and contributing to the chronicity of inflammation [90].

Multiple sclerosis

MS is characterized by neurodegeneration, demyelination, and axonal damage. Its typical pathological features and underlying pathogenesis involve the migration of immune cells from the blood into the central nervous system, accompanied by disruption of the blood-brain barrier and damage to neural tissues [91]. Interactions between CCL20 and CCR6⁺ Th17 cells are critical during the early stage of the disease. In the experimental autoimmune encephalomyelitis (EAE) mouse model, an established animal model of MS, CCR6 and CCL20 expression is upregulated in the spinal cord during disease development. CCR6 deficiency leads to a milder EAE phenotype in mice, and neutralization of either CCR6 or CCL20 reduces Th17 cell infiltration into the central nervous system and attenuates encephalitis, further demonstrating the important role of the CCL20-CCR6 axis in MS [92, 93].

Role of CCL20-CCR6 axis in cancer progression

Growing evidence has demonstrated a strong association between the CCL20-CCR6 axis and multiple cancers, including hepatocellular carcinoma, breast cancer, colorectal cancer, pancreatic cancer, gastric cancer, lung cancer, and kidney cancer. Numerous cancer types have been shown to highly express CCL20 and CCR6, and the expression levels of CCL20 and CCR6 are associated with prognosis, making the CCL20-CCR6 axis a potential biomarker and therapeutic target [21–96]. Notably, beyond its role in cancer progression, whether the CCL20-CCR6 axis also contributes to cancer initiation remains an important question [101]. Nandi et al. found in the APC^Min/+ spontaneous intestinal adenoma model that CCR6 deficiency significantly reduced spontaneous intestinal tumorigenesis and decreased macrophage infiltration in both adenomatous and non-tumorous epithelium. Meanwhile, CCL20 directly promoted colorectal tumor cell proliferation and induced positive-feedback secretion of CCL20, suggesting that this axis may jointly drive adenoma formation, an early event in tumorigenesis, through two complementary mechanisms: immune cell recruitment and epithelial proliferation [34]. Furthermore, population-based evidence showed that elevated circulating CCL20 levels were associated with an increased risk of colorectal adenoma, a precancerous lesion, as well as incident adenoma, further supporting the involvement of CCL20-related pathways in the early stages of colorectal carcinogenesis [102].

In addition to acting directly on tumor cells, the CCL20-CCR6 axis is also closely involved in shaping the TME. By producing a variety of signaling molecules, including chemokines, tumor cells can remodel a tumor-promoting and immunosuppressive microenvironment [103–108]. Therefore, chemokines released by tumor cells, stromal cells, and immune cells are critical regulators of TME formation and may represent promising therapeutic targets in cancer [109–113]. As an important chemokine axis, the CCL20-CCR6 axis has been shown in numerous studies to promote the infiltration of multiple immune cell populations into the TME and to transmit signals through ligand-receptor interactions, thereby reprogramming the TME in a manner that favors tumor progression [111–113]. Hepatocellular cancer

Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) is the most common primary liver cancer and the third leading cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide [114]. The CCL20-CCR6 axis has emerged as an important contributor to HCC progression and a promising candidate for prognostic assessment [94]. Clinical studies have demonstrated that CCL20 is significantly overexpressed in patients with HCC and bile duct tumor thrombus and is negatively associated with surgical outcomes [115]. Furthermore, elevated CCL20 levels are strongly associated with aggressive clinicopathological features, including larger tumor size, multiple nodules, vascular invasion, and poor histological differentiation [116].

High expression of the CCL20-CCR6 axis serves as an independent predictor of tumor recurrence, recurrence-free survival (RFS), and overall survival (OS) after curative resection [117]. At the cellular and mechanistic levels, the CCL20-CCR6 axis exerts both tumor-intrinsic and microenvironmental effects in HCC. In highly invasive HCC cell lines, CCR6 is preferentially upregulated, and activation of the CCL20-CCR6 axis enhances cell proliferation, adhesion, chemotactic migration, and the expression of extracellular matrix-degrading factors, supporting a direct role in HCC invasion and metastasis [118]. More recently, HOXD3 has been shown to transcriptionally activate CCR6 and enhance CCL20 expression by remodeling chromatin conformation through CREBBP/Med15-dependent mechanisms. In parallel, exosome-mediated transfer of CCR6 from HCC cells to endothelial cells further promotes angiogenesis, collectively facilitating tumor migration and dissemination [119].

Beyond these tumor-cell-intrinsic effects, the CCL20-CCR6 axis is also a key organizer of the immune microenvironment in HCC. Tumor-derived CCL20 selectively recruits CCR6⁺ Treg cells, thereby reinforcing local immunosuppression and predicting poor clinical outcomes [94, 120]. Recent evidence further indicates that lipid droplet-laden macrophages represent an additional source of CCL20 in HCC, promoting the recruitment of CCR6⁺ Treg cells while dampening the antitumor activity of CD8⁺ T cells [121]. In addition, CCL20 recruits CCR6⁺CD19⁺CD5⁺ B cells to the TME, where these cells enhance pathological angiogenesis and support tumor growth and metastasis [122]. Collectively, these findings suggest that the CCL20-CCR6 axis is not merely a chemotactic pathway but a coordinated program linking tumor invasiveness, angiogenesis, and immune evasion in HCC [123].

Prostate cancer

Prostate cancer (PCa) is the second most common cancer in men worldwide and remains a leading cause of cancer-related mortality [124]. In PCa, the CCL20-CCR6 axis has emerged as an important mediator of aggressive disease progression. Clinically, elevated CCR6 expression is significantly associated with a higher Gleason score, advanced T stage, greater tumor burden, and lymph node metastasis, indicating that this receptor is closely linked to tumor aggressiveness in primary PCa [125]. In advanced disease, this axis appears to become even more relevant under therapeutic pressure. Suppression of androgen receptor (AR) signaling enhances CCL20 expression, while metastatic PCa tissues exhibit increased CCL20 secretion, supporting a role for this pathway in metastatic dissemination and castration-resistant progression [126].

Mechanistically, CCL20-CCR6 signaling in PCa is closely linked to both non-coding RNA networks and adaptive responses to endocrine therapy. The CircSMARCC1/miR-1322 pathway promotes CCL20 upregulation and activates PI3K-Akt signaling, thereby enhancing tumor cell proliferation, migration, invasion, and epithelial-mesenchymal plasticity. Notably, this circuit also reinforces tumor-associated macrophage recruitment and M2 polarization through CCL20-CCR6 signaling, linking tumor-intrinsic growth programs to microenvironmental remodeling [127]. In parallel, AR suppression activates a compensatory CCL20-CCR6-AKT-Snail cascade that further promotes cell motility and metastatic behavior, suggesting that this axis functions as a prometastatic adaptive program under endocrine pressure [126].

Beyond tumor-cell-intrinsic effects, the CCL20-CCR6 axis is also a key organizer of the metastatic PCa microenvironment. In bone metastases, myeloid cells markedly overexpress CCL20, whereas T cells upregulate CCR6. Importantly, blockade of this axis restores T-cell responsiveness and significantly prolongs survival in preclinical models, identifying the CCL20-CCR6 axis as an actionable immunosuppressive pathway in metastatic PCa [126]. More recent single-cell and spatial studies further suggest that club-like epithelial programs contribute to immune remodeling in aggressive PCa and are associated with increased PMN-MDSC activity, reduced AR signaling, stromal immune infiltration, and therapy resistance, thereby reinforcing the concept that inflammatory chemokine signaling is a central component of advanced PCa biology [128, 129].

Lung cancer

Lung cancer remains the leading cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide, and cigarette smoking is still the predominant etiologic factor [130, 131]. In this context, the CCL20-CCR6 axis serves as an important molecular link between tobacco-induced airway injury and malignant progression. Long-term exposure to the tobacco carcinogen nicotine-derived nitrosaminoketone (NNK) significantly induces CCL20 expression in human bronchial epithelial cells and promotes lung cancer cell proliferation and migration, indicating that smoking-related inflammation may directly activate this chemokine program [132]. Clinically, elevated CCL20 expression is associated with poorer prognosis in lung cancer [132]. Recurrent lung adenocarcinomas also exhibit significantly higher expression of both CCL20 and CCR6 than non-recurrent tumors, supporting the potential value of this axis as a biomarker of recurrence and aggressive disease behavior [133].

From a mechanistic perspective, the CCL20-CCR6 axis promotes lung cancer progression by driving inflammatory signaling, autocrine activation, and malignant phenotypic plasticity. IL-1β stimulates CCL20 production in lung cancer cells through the MAPK and PI3K signaling pathways [134]. Secreted CCL20 further enhances tumor cell migration and proliferation through activation of ERK and PI3K, thereby forming a self-reinforcing prometastatic loop [134]. In lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD), recurrent tumors with CCL20 and CCR6 overexpression also exhibit increased ERK activation and colony-forming capacity [133]. More recent transcriptomic and functional studies further demonstrate that high CCL20 expression is associated with poor survival, enrichment of EMT programs, and a more aggressive tumor phenotype [135]. Consistently, CCL20 knockdown suppresses EMT and tumor growth, indicating that CCL20 is an active driver of LUAD progression rather than a passive inflammatory marker [135].

Within the TME, the CCL20-CCR6 axis contributes to immune evasion primarily by recruiting CCR6⁺ Treg cells into non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) tissues, where they are associated with increased expression of forkhead box P3 (FOXP3) and IL-10, as well as a more immunosuppressive microenvironment [136]. In LUAD, high CCL20 expression has also been associated with a more immunotherapy-refractory state, particularly in the context of TGF-β signaling [135]. From a translational perspective, this pathway may also represent a therapeutically exploitable target. Recent preclinical studies have shown that co-expression of C-X-C motif chemokine receptor 5 (CXCR5) and CCR6 enhances the trafficking and antitumor efficacy of human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2)-targeted chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) T cells in CCL20-rich NSCLC models, highlighting the potential of chemokine-guided cell engineering to improve immune-cell delivery to lung tumors [137].

Colorectal cancer

Colorectal cancer (CRC) is one of the most common malignancies worldwide and remains a major cause of cancer-related mortality [130]. In CRC, the CCL20-CCR6 axis has emerged as both an important biomarker and a driver of aggressive disease behavior. Clinically, CCL20 and CCR6 are elevated in malignant colorectal tissues and colorectal liver metastases compared with non-malignant colorectal tissues [138]. A combined serum panel of CCL20 and IL-17 A shows good diagnostic performance, including in early-stage CRC and carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA)-low CRC, and both markers independently predict prognosis [139]. In addition, high CCR6 expression is associated with advanced disease stage, lymph node invasion, distant metastasis, and shorter overall survival, indicating that this receptor is closely linked to tumor aggressiveness and metastatic potential [46, 140].

The CCL20-CCR6 axis promotes CRC progression through both epithelial-intrinsic signaling and metastatic niche remodeling. Loss of epithelial SMAD4 increases CCL20 expression and the recruitment of CCR6 + immune cells, thereby enhancing susceptibility to colitis-associated carcinoma [141]. In established CRC cells, CCL20 directly promotes proliferation, migration, and autocrine signaling through HGF-c-Met/ERK1/2 and MSP-MSPR pathways [142]. In liver metastasis, highly metastatic CRC cells release miR-181a-5p-rich extracellular vesicles, which activate hepatic stellate cells and induce CCL20 secretion, establishing a CCL20/CCR6/ERK1/2/Elk-1/miR-181a-5p positive-feedback loop that facilitates metastatic colonization [143].

The most compelling evidence for the CCL20-CCR6 axis in CRC comes from its role in mediating immunosuppressive crosstalk among Treg cells, macrophages, and Th17-associated immune programs.Chemotherapy-resistant CRC cells secrete higher levels of CCL20, which recruit CCR6+ Treg cells through the FOXO1/CEBPB/NF-κB pathway and promote resistance to FOLFOX/5-FU [23]. Tumor-associated macrophages further amplify this process by enhancing CCL20 production and CCR6⁺ Treg-cell accumulation [144], while Treg cell-derived RANKL activates RANK/NF-κB-dependent CCL20 production in CRC cells, creating a feed-forward loop that reinforces stemness, metastasis, and immune escape [145]. By contrast, the role of Th17 cells appears more nuanced, as their infiltration may be associated with both protumor inflammatory signaling and, in specific spatial contexts, improved survival through coordination with CD8⁺ T cells and neutrophils [146]. Collectively, these findings indicate that in CRC, the CCL20-CCR6 axis is best viewed as a context-dependent immune-organizing hub that predominantly supports Treg and myeloid-driven immune escape, while its ultimate clinical impact depends on the composition and spatial distribution of the immune infiltrate.

Breast cancer

Breast cancer remains the most commonly diagnosed malignancy in women worldwide and is a leading cause of cancer-related mortality [147]. In this context, the CCL20-CCR6 axis has emerged as a clinically relevant mediator of tumor progression and metastasis. Elevated CCL20 expression in breast cancer is associated with poorer survival outcomes, particularly in basal-like subtypes, in which CCL20 promotes invasion and matrix remodeling. Notably, anti-CCL20 neutralizing antibodies significantly reduce osteolytic bone metastasis in xenograft models, confirming the importance of this axis in breast cancer dissemination, especially to bone [148]. Moreover, high CCL20 expression is also associated with increased immune infiltration and poor prognosis, supporting its potential as a biomarker of aggressive disease and a therapeutic target [149].

The CCL20-CCR6 axis plays a pivotal role in drug resistance, particularly in triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC). In TNBC, tumors that fail to achieve pathological complete response (pCR) after taxane-based chemotherapy exhibit significantly elevated CCL20 expression during treatment, suggesting that CCL20 may serve as a dynamic marker of chemotherapy failure [150]. CCL20 activates NF-κB signaling, which in turn upregulates ABCB1, a drug efflux transporter that mediates resistance to taxanes by exporting the drug from cancer cells [150]. In addition, CCL20 promotes tumor cell invasion and matrix remodeling, thereby driving osteolytic bone metastasis in aggressive breast cancer subtypes and linking this axis to both metastatic dissemination and therapy-induced resistance [148].

Within the TME, CCL20 plays a critical role in shaping an immunosuppressive landscape. Tumor-derived CCL20 activates neutrophils, leading to upregulation of programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1), which subsequently inhibits T cell-mediated antitumor immunity. High levels of PD-L1⁺ neutrophils in the TME are associated with shorter disease-free survival (DFS) in patients with breast cancer, indicating that these cells contribute to immune evasion [151]. Furthermore, CCL20 recruits CCR6⁺ Treg cells and promotes their accumulation in the TME, where they enhance tumor growth and angiogenesis [144]. Recent studies also suggest that CCL20-driven expansion of polymorphonuclear myeloid-derived suppressor cells (PMN-MDSCs) plays a central role in immune suppression by promoting cancer stemness through the C-X-C motif chemokine ligand 2 (CXCL2)-C-X-C motif chemokine receptor 2 (CXCR2)-NOTCH1/HEY1 signaling axis, thereby linking immune evasion to tumor stem cell enrichment [152]. Together, these findings underscore the multifaceted role of the CCL20-CCR6 axis in modulating immune responses and promoting metastasis, highlighting its therapeutic potential in the management of both primary and metastatic breast cancer.

Renal cell carcinoma

Renal cell carcinoma (RCC) accounts for the majority of kidney cancers and remains clinically challenging because a substantial proportion of patients eventually develop advanced or metastatic disease requiring systemic therapy [153]. From a clinical perspective, the CCL20-CCR6 axis appears to be particularly relevant in immunologically active and aggressive RCC, rather than serving merely as a tumor cell-associated marker. In RCC tissues, CCL20 is detectable within the TME, whereas CCR6 is enriched on tumor-infiltrating T cells, particularly Treg cells, supporting a role for this axis in immune escape [154].

At the level of tumor cell-intrinsic signaling, the strongest direct evidence comes from macrophage-tumor crosstalk. Tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) secrete CCL20, which activates CCR6 on RCC cells, induces Akt phosphorylation, promotes EMT, and enhances migratory capacity. Importantly, these effects are attenuated by CCL20 neutralization or Akt inhibition, indicating that the CCL20-CCR6-Akt cascade is functionally required for TAM-driven RCC cell motility [155]. Earlier studies showed that CCR6 contributes to the selective recruitment of intratumoral Treg cells in RCC [154]. More recent single-cell analyses of clear cell RCC (ccRCC) demonstrated that advanced disease is characterized by enrichment of terminally exhausted CD8⁺ T cells and M2-like TAMs, which form an immune dysfunction circuit associated with poorer prognosis [156]. Extending this concept, a 2024 spatial and single-cell transcriptomic study identified a novel terminal effector Treg-cell subset with strong immunosuppressive activity that interacts with MRC1⁺FOLR2⁺ TAMs, further reinforcing a Treg cell-macrophage axis of immune suppression in ccRCC [157]. Collectively, these findings suggest that in RCC, the CCL20-CCR6 axis functions as an immune-organizing hub linking Treg-cell recruitment, TAM-driven EMT, and myeloid-dominant immune suppression.

Gastric cancer

Gastric cancer (GC) remains a major global health burden and is characterized by frequent recurrence, metastatic spread, and marked biological heterogeneity [158]. In this context, the CCL20-CCR6 axis has emerged as a clinically relevant marker of aggressive disease behavior. CCR6 is significantly upregulated in GC tissues, and high CCR6 expression is associated with extracapsular extension, tumor relapse, and poorer overall survival, supporting its value as a marker of recurrence-prone disease [47]. Consistent with these clinical associations, the CCL20-CCR6 axis directly enhances the migratory and invasive phenotype of GC cells. Mechanistically, knockdown of CRK-like proto-oncogene, adaptor protein (CrkL) attenuates CCL20-induced motility and invasiveness, suggesting that a CCL20-CCR6/CrkL-dependent EMT network may represent a plausible therapeutic target in metastatic GC [159]. More recently, this mechanistic framework has been further expanded. A 2025 study identified basonuclin 1 (BNC1) as a transcriptional suppressor of CCL20 in GC. Loss of BNC1 increased CCL20 expression and activated downstream Janus kinase/signal transducer and activator of transcription (JAK-STAT) signaling, thereby broadening current understanding of the upstream regulatory network governing CCL20 in GC [160]. Taken together, these findings indicate that CCL20 functions not merely as a chemotactic cue but as an active regulator of epithelial plasticity, invasive progression, and metastatic potential in GC.

Importantly, the protumorigenic role of CCL20 extends beyond tumor-intrinsic signaling and is closely linked to remodeling of the TME. From this perspective, the strongest direct evidence suggests that CCL20 contributes to the establishment of an immunosuppressive, myeloid-enriched ecosystem in GC. In a peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor delta (PPARδ)-driven gastric adenocarcinoma model, increased epithelial CCL20 selectively chemoattracts CCR6⁺ immunosuppressive cells, including TAMs, myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs), and Treg cells, while concomitantly reducing the abundance of CD8⁺ T cells in gastric tissues [161]. Therefore, CCL20 appears to act at the interface between tumor cell plasticity and immune microenvironmental reprogramming, linking metastatic behavior to immune evasion in GC.

Pancreatic cancer

Pancreatic cancer is one of the deadliest malignancies and is characterized by marked aggressiveness, early metastasis, and profound therapeutic resistance [162]. In pancreatic cancer tissues, the CCL20-CCR6 axis is aberrantly upregulated, and elevated CCL20 expression is associated with more advanced tumor stage, supporting its relevance to disease progression [101]. Functionally, CCL20 derived from M2 macrophages promotes EMT, migration, invasion, tumor growth, and liver metastasis of pancreatic cancer cells through CCR6-dependent signaling [24]. In addition, CCL20 also contributes to resistance to tumor necrosis factor-related apoptosis-inducing ligand (TRAIL) in pancreatic cancer through a RelA/NF-κB-dependent mechanism, largely by paracrine recruitment of immune cells rather than through a purely tumor cell-intrinsic survival effect [163]. Recent work has shown that senescent pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) cells secrete CCL20, suppress complement-driven M1 macrophage activation and antigen presentation, and drive macrophages toward immunosuppressive M2 polarization, thereby reinforcing an immune-desert-like microenvironment [164]. Consistently, a recent study demonstrated that vitamin D receptor (VDR) directly binds to the CCL20 promoter and upregulates its transcription, thereby promoting M2 macrophage polarization and recruitment in pancreatic adenocarcinoma; notably, blockade of CCL20 attenuated these effects [165]. Collectively, these findings indicate that CCL20 is not only a marker of pancreatic cancer progression but also a central mediator linking macrophage-centered immunosuppression, EMT/metastasis, and therapy resistance, highlighting the CCL20-CCR6 axis as a promising therapeutic target in pancreatic cancer.

CCL20-CCR6 axis targeted treatments against autoimmune diseases and cancers

The CCL20-CCR6 axis contributes to the progression of both autoimmune and neoplastic diseases. Given its important role in the pathogenesis of a wide range of diseases, blockade of the CCL20-CCR6 axis has been considered a promising therapeutic strategy. Current inhibitors targeting the CCL20-CCR6 axis mainly include the following categories: (a) anti-CCL20 antibodies; (b) anti-CCR6 antibodies; (c) small-molecule inhibitors of CCL20; (d) small-molecule inhibitors of CCR6; (e) small interfering RNA (siRNA) targeting CCL20; and (f) siRNA targeting CCR6. CCR6-CCL20 inhibition in autoimmune diseases

CCL20/CCR6 axis plays an important role in the pathologies of several autoimmune diseases, including RA, psoriasis dermatitis, MS and IBD. The extensive involvement of the CCL20/CCR6 axis in autoimmune diseases forms a major viable therapeutic target. The TNF-α antagonist infliximab showed significant treatment effect in psoriasis by directly inhibiting regional CCL20 induction [166]. Similar results were also found in a randomized clinical trial in which psoriasis patients treated with anti-TNF-α antibody adalimumab displayed remarkable downregulation of CCL20 and better therapeutic response [167]. With respect to direct blockage of CCL20-CCR6 axis, several studies provide supporting evidence. A fully humanized monoclonal antibody against hCCR6 was applied in the animal models of psoriasis, leading to abolishment of all signs of skin inflammation and suppression of clinical progression [168]. In the psoriasis dermatitis mice model induced by IL-23, the injection of anti-CCL20 monoclonal antibody inhibited recruitment of CCR6+CD4+T cells to the epidermis and decreased psoriasiform dermatitis [169]. Moreover, CCL20S64C, a synthetic partial agonist for CCR6, prevented recruitment of CCR6+ cells and controlled psoriatic inflammation in an IL-23-dependent psoriasis mouse model [170]. Another humanized antibody against human CCL20 called GSK3050002 exhibited dose-dependent reductions in CCR6+ cell recruitment to skin blisters without safety concerns in a first-in-human study [171]. In animal models of RA, the treatment of anti-CCR6 monoclonal antibody substantially suppressed the onset and severity of arthritis in murine models [15]. Furthermore, the administration of neutralizing anti-CCL20 monoclonal antibody reduced the numbers of CCR6-bearing CD4+ and CD8+ T cells in intestinal lamina propria of 2,4,6-trinitrobenzene sulfonic acid-induced colitis murine models, resulting in alleviation of mucosal ulceration [172]. As for murine models of experimental autoimmune encephalomyelitis, the treatment of neutralizing anti-CCR6 antibody restrained the infiltration of Th17 cells in the central nervous system, which consequently reduced the severity and duration of encephalomyelitis [92, 93, 173]. Together, these studies suggest inhibition of CCL20-CCR6 axis promises a potential therapeutic strategy to control autoimmune diseases.

CCR6-CCL20 inhibition in cancers

The CCL20-CCR6 axis involved in carcinogenesis and development of a multitude of malignant tumors by directly activating tumor-promoting signals in tumor cells and indirectly modulating tumor-immune system. Several studies concerning on the role of CCL20-CCR6 axis in cancers demonstrated CCL20-CCR6 inhibition, such as neutralizing antibodies and siRNA, exerts anti-cancer effect in vivo and in vitro experiments. In RCC, it has been found the neutralizing anti-CCL20 antibodies abrogated EMT and migration ability in human renal cell carcinoma cell lines [155]. Similarly, another study showed a monoclonal antibody against the Mip-3α binding domain of CCR6 successfully inhibited pancreatic cancer cell invasion [174]. Furthermore, in vivo and in vitro experiments demonstrated the introduction of anti-CCL20 antibody was capable of suppressing the migration of M2 macrophages in esophageal squamous cell carcinoma, which shed light on the development of novel therapeutic targets against esophageal squamous cell carcinoma [175]. In addition to solid tumors, it has been investigated that antibodies against CCL20 and CCR6 could constrain tumor progression in multiple myeloma [176]. The presence of anti-CCL20 and anti-CCR6 antibodies exhibited satisfactory inhibitory effect on formation of osteoclast and osteolytic bone lesions in multiple myeloma in vitro experiments [176]. Moreover, the role of CCL20-CCR6 inhibition has been further proved in vivo experiments. The osteolytic breast cancer bone metastases in mice were significantly suppressed after intraperitoneal administration of anti-CCL20 antibodies [148]. Another in vivo study utilizing nude mice inoculated with head and neck squamous tumor cells observed the decrease of tumor growth and less infiltration of Th17 and Treg cells in tumors and draining lymph nodes after treatments of anti-CCL20 antibodies [177]. More importantly, mice receiving the combination of anti-CCL20 treatment and radiotherapy witnessed more significant decrease in tumor volume in comparison to mice treated with radiotherapy alone, indicating anti-CCL20 antibody could sensitize tumor cell to radiation therapy [177]. Also, intraperitoneal injection of CCR6 inhibitor was reported to improve radiation-induced liver fibrosis and enhanced radiosensitivity of hepatocellular carcinoma in mice [178]. Besides, microRNA-150 was proved to inhibit tumor invasion and metastases in advanced cutaneous T-cell lymphoma mice by directly downregulating CCR6 [179]. Recently, the CAR-T cell which could force expression of CCR6 has aroused wide concern. The CCR6-expressing CAR-T cells migrated to CCL20-secreting tumors and contributed to superior tumor clearance and better prognosis of a lung cancer xenograft mouse model, providing a novel CCL20-CCR6 axis targeted treatment strategy for solid tumor immunotherapy [180, 181]. However, clinically available drugs modulating the CCL20-CCR6 axis as anticancer agents are rather limited and the progress of such drugs is urgently expected and needed.

Critical evaluation and current limitations

Although the role of the CCL20-CCR6 axis in disease-related immune remodeling has been widely recognized, a careful review of the current literature still reveals several important limitations that hinder its clinical translation. First, many studies evaluating this axis as a prognostic marker are based on relatively small sample sizes, typically ranging from approximately 30 to 120 cases. For example, in a widely cited study showing that CCL20 expression was positively associated with postoperative recurrence in HCC, the cohort included only 120 patients [162]. In pancreatic cancer, which is even more heterogeneous, some mechanistic conclusions regarding CCL20-CCR6-mediated EMT were drawn from clinical tissue analyses involving fewer than 30 cases [101]. Such sample sizes are insufficient to fully exclude the influence of confounding factors on prognostic value; therefore, larger multicenter cohort studies are still needed for external validation.

Second, there remains a clear gap between experimental models and the actual TME. At present, many mechanistic studies rely heavily on in vitro single-cell culture systems, in which high concentrations of recombinant proteins, usually 50–100 ng/mL, are added to simulate chemotactic effects. However, in the human TME, CCL20 is distributed in a highly spatially restricted manner and is often further processed by matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs), which can alter its receptor-binding affinity and signaling kinetics [182]. As a result, simplified in vitro systems may overestimate the contribution of CCL20 alone within a much more complex chemokine network while overlooking the influence of matrix stiffness, hypoxia, and diverse cellular components on signal transmission.

Finally, the CCL20-CCR6 axis does not operate in isolation within the broader chemokine network, and its biological significance is best understood in comparison with other well-characterized pathways. For example, the CXCL12-CXCR4 axis is more strongly associated with systemic tumor dissemination and metastatic homing in multiple cancers [183], whereas the CCL20-CCR6 axis appears to be more involved in selective immune cell recruitment, particularly at mucosal or tissue-specific sites. Similarly, the CCL2-CCR2 axis is well known for promoting the recruitment of monocytes and TAMs, thereby shaping the immunosuppressive microenvironment more broadly [184]. In contrast, the effects of the CCL20-CCR6 axis appear to be more focused on specific CCR6⁺ cell populations, such as Treg cells, Th17 cells, and certain DC subsets. However, most current interventional studies still focus on blockade of a single axis. There is still limited evidence as to whether inhibition of CCR6 induces compensatory upregulation of other pathways, such as CXCR4 or CCR2, which may continue to support immune suppression or metastasis. This may be one important reason why single-target therapies often show limited benefit in clinical settings.

Future perspectives and conclusion

Despite the therapeutic promise of the CCL20-CCR6 axis in preclinical models of cancer and autoimmunity, its clinical translation remains limited by the paucity of clinical trials and the lack of cell-type-specific inhibitors. Current therapeutic strategies primarily rely on broad blockade, even though the functional impact of this axis is profoundly shaped by the complex cytokine milieu within the TME. Rather than pursuing universal inhibition, future therapeutic success may depend on selective modulation of specific immune subsets, such as Th17 cells or Treg cells, according to the underlying pathological context. This context dependence is particularly evident in the divergent roles of Th17 cells and in the distinct clinical significance of this axis across different tumor types and molecular subtypes.

More specifically, this context dependence is especially apparent in the functional polarity of Th17 cells. In some cancers, Th17 cells promote tumor progression by secreting IL-17, which activates downstream STAT3 and NF-κB signaling, recruits MDSCs, and enhances angiogenesis and immune escape [185]. However, in specific settings such as microsatellite instability-high (MSI-H) colorectal cancer, Th17 infiltration has been associated with stronger antitumor immune responses and better clinical outcomes [186]. These findings highlight the marked plasticity of Th17 cells. Whether they exert protumor or antitumor effects largely depends on local immune-regulatory factors within the TME, such as the balance between TGF-β and IL-23. Therefore, future studies should not simply aim to block CCL20-mediated Th17 infiltration, but should also explore whether the cytokine profile of the TME can be modulated to locally reprogram recruited CCR6⁺ Th17 cells toward a more antitumor effector phenotype.

This cellular complexity is further supported by clinical molecular subtyping. In colorectal cancer, for example, microsatellite status strongly influences the clinical significance of this axis. In microsatellite-stable (MSS) CRC, CCL20 mainly recruits Treg cells and contributes to chemoresistance [23]. In contrast, in microsatellite instability-high (MSI-H) CRC, Th17 infiltration often works together with Th1-like responses triggered by high neoantigen load, and this is generally linked to better prognosis [186, 187]. Therefore, future clinical trials should prioritize stratification of the MSS subgroup with high CCL20 expression and low CD8 infiltration, rather than testing this strategy broadly in unselected populations.

More broadly, the biological effects of the CCL20-CCR6 axis are highly tumor-type- and tissue-specific. Besides its tumor-promoting role in colorectal cancer, CCL20 has been reported to support the formation and maturation of tertiary lymphoid structures (TLS) in HPV-related head and neck squamous cell carcinoma, thereby improving the response to anti-PD-1 therapy [188]. Even for the shared component of CCR6⁺ Treg cells, signaling in different tumors may alter their suppressive function through mechanisms such as metabolic reprogramming, leading to very different clinical outcomes [77]. Therefore, the effects of this axis depend heavily on the tumor type and its microenvironment. For some indications, future strategies may include not only blockade, but also agonist-based or local delivery approaches, combined with immunotherapy when appropriate.

In this review, we summarized the emerging role of the CCL20-CCR6 axis in the pathogenesis of autoimmune and neoplastic diseases. We discussed the molecular mechanisms underlying this axis and highlighted recent advances in therapeutic strategies targeting the CCL20-CCR6 axis. One of the most well-established functions of the CCL20-CCR6 axis is the recruitment of CCR6⁺ Th17 cells and Treg cells to tissues with high CCL20 expression. Activated Th17 cells can further secrete CCL20 through paracrine and autocrine mechanisms, thereby forming a positive feedback loop. Depending on the tissue context and local cytokine milieu, this loop may either reinforce an immunosuppressive TME and promote tumor progression or sustain chronic, dysregulated inflammation. As knowledge across related fields continues to advance, further clarification of the crosstalk between the CCL20-CCR6 axis and other chemokines and signaling pathways will provide deeper insight into disease mechanisms and may inform the development of innovative therapeutic strategies.
