Secondary Staphylococcus capitis Infection of a Long-Standing Non-Coronary Sinus of Valsalva–to–Right Atrial Fistula Initially Misdiagnosed as a Gerbode Defect: A Case Report
Authors: Tomáš Toporcer, Marián Homola, Anton Bereš, Michal Trebišovský, Tomáš Lopuchovský, Štefan Lukačín, Adrián Kolesár
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background: Aorto–atrial fistula is a rare pathological communication associated with diagnostic difficulties, progressive hemodynamic consequences, and potential infective complications. Methods and Results: We present a 53-year-old woman with a long-standing congenital non-coronary sinus of Valsalva–to–right atrial fistula that had been followed for nine years under the working diagnosis of a Gerbode defect. The communication was first detected at the age of 44 years during pregnancy and was managed conservatively while the patient remained largely asymptomatic. At the age of 53 years, recurrent exertional dyspnea prompted definitive reassessment. Right heart catheterization demonstrated a hemodynamically significant left-to-right shunt at the right atrial level, with a pulmonary-to-systemic blood flow (Qp/Qs) ratio of 2.1. Intraoperatively, the communication was shown to originate immediately above the non-coronary cusp and drain into the right atrium, establishing the diagnosis of a congenital aorto–right atrial fistula. A granulomatous, windsock-like endocardial lesion was identified at its right atrial opening, and Staphylococcus capitis was isolated from surgically obtained tissue. Histopathology showed nonspecific regressive changes without specific features of active infective endocarditis. The fistula was closed with an autologous pericardial patch, and intravenous flucloxacillin was administered because of probable localized infective involvement. Conclusions: This case illustrates the diagnostic difficulty of distinguishing an aorto–atrial fistula from a Gerbode defect and supports careful multimodality evaluation of the anatomical origin and flow characteristics of intracardiac shunts. Persistent high-velocity flow may contribute to endocardial injury and create a substrate for subsequent bacterial colonization, although causality cannot be established from a single case. Isolation of a low-virulence skin commensal from pathological cardiac tissue should be interpreted in the complete clinical, operative, microbiological, and histopathological context rather than automatically classified as contamination.

1. Introduction

Aorto–atrial fistula (AAF), also referred to as an aorto–atrial tunnel or sinus of Valsalva–to–atrial fistula, is a rare pathological communication between the aorta and an atrial chamber with an unknown true incidence [1,2,3,4]. The term broadly encompasses any abnormal communication between the aorta and either atrium. AAF has been reported more frequently in women, with a female-to-male ratio of approximately 2:1, and may be diagnosed across a wide age range [1]. Most aortocardiac shunts are acquired, commonly following cardiac surgery or interventional procedures [3,5,6]. Among non-iatrogenic causes, infective endocarditis and aortic aneurysmal disease are the most frequently reported, while other acquired etiologies include rupture of a saphenous vein graft aneurysm and complications following ventricular septal defect closure [1,2,5,7,8,9,10].

AAF of Congenital Origin

Congenital communication between the aorta and the atrial chambers comprises two distinct entities. The first is primary congenital AAF, in which the communication is present from birth. The second is rupture of a sinus of Valsalva aneurysm into an atrial chamber, resulting in secondary formation of an aorto–atrial communication later in life. Primary congenital AAF accounts for approximately 11.8% of reported aorto–atrial communications and most commonly drains into the right atrium [1]. Sinus of Valsalva aneurysm is usually congenital and results from incomplete fusion between the aortic media and the annulus fibrosus [11]. It most frequently involves the right coronary sinus, followed by the non-coronary sinus, whereas left coronary sinus involvement is uncommon [12]. Acquired forms may occur in association with infection, trauma, atherosclerosis, cystic medial degeneration, or connective tissue disorders [11]. Clinical presentation usually follows rupture into a cardiac chamber. In contrast to primary congenital AAF, a ruptured sinus of Valsalva aneurysm is characterized by an identifiable aneurysmal component [13].

This report describes a long-standing, initially misclassified intracardiac shunt associated with progressive cardiac remodeling. It further suggests that persistent high-velocity flow may have caused chronic endocardial injury, potentially creating a substrate for subsequent bacterial colonization.

2. Case Report

A 53-year-old woman with a history of four previous deliveries was referred to our institution for definitive evaluation of a long-standing left-to-right intracardiac shunt that had been followed for nine years under the working diagnosis of a Gerbode defect. Her cardiac history began at the age of 44 years during her fourth pregnancy, when she developed progressive dyspnea while undergoing evaluation for suspected pulmonary embolism and was treated with low-molecular-weight heparin. Transthoracic echocardiography demonstrated preserved left ventricular systolic function (LVEF 63%) and a small high-velocity communication measuring approximately 5 mm, with a maximal flow velocity of 4.0 m/s. The lesion was initially interpreted as a ventricular septal defect and subsequently classified as a Type I Gerbode defect with presumed left ventricular outflow tract–to–right atrial flow. At the age of 46 years, follow-up echocardiography demonstrated persistent left ventricular dilatation, with an LVEDD of 59 mm and an LVESD of 39 mm, preserved systolic function (LVEF 63%), and a persistent approximately 5-mm shunt with a maximal velocity of 4.5 m/s (Figure 1 and Figure 2). Subsequent computed tomography was also interpreted as supporting the working diagnosis of a Type I Gerbode defect, although the precise aortic origin and course of the communication were not recognized at that time (Figure 3A). During subsequent follow-up, the communication continued to be regarded as a Gerbode defect. The LVEDD remained 59 mm, while LVEF ranged from 58% to 65%. The right ventricle was initially non-dilated; approximately four years before surgery, it was described as borderline enlarged with mild volume overload but preserved longitudinal systolic function (TAPSE 30 mm). Tricuspid regurgitation could not be reliably quantified because its Doppler signal could not be clearly distinguished from the high-velocity shunt flow. No quantitative assessment of shunt magnitude was available during the earlier surveillance period. Despite conservative management, the patient subsequently reported progressive exertional dyspnea and worsening functional limitation. At the age of 53 years, she was referred to our center for definitive evaluation and consideration of surgical treatment. At referral and throughout the preoperative assessment, the communication remained interpreted as a left ventricular outflow tract–to–right atrial shunt consistent with a Gerbode defect. Selective coronary angiography demonstrated no significant coronary artery disease or other prospectively identified abnormalities. However, retrospective postoperative review of the preoperative angiographic documentation revealed contrast filling compatible with an aorto–right atrial fistula (Figure 3B). Preoperative transthoracic echocardiography demonstrated a non-dilated right ventricle with an end-diastolic diameter of 25 mm and a normal-sized right atrium with an area of 15 cm2. Tricuspid regurgitation again could not be reliably assessed. The estimated mean pulmonary artery pressure was 25 mmHg. Functional status was classified as NYHA class I at the time of examination, although exertional dyspnea had been reported during the preceding follow-up period. Diagnostic right heart catheterization demonstrated a hemodynamically significant left-to-right shunt at the right atrial level, with a pulmonary-to-systemic blood flow (Qp/Qs) ratio of 2.1. Hemodynamic measurements included a right atrial pressure of 6 mmHg, right ventricular pressure of 27/4 mmHg, pulmonary artery pressure of 27/10 mmHg with a mean pressure of 19 mmHg, and pulmonary artery wedge pressure of 16 mmHg. Cardiac output was 7.3 L/min, cardiac index was 4.3 L/min/m2, and pulmonary vascular resistance was 30 dyn·s·cm−5. There was no evidence of pulmonary hypertension or elevated pulmonary vascular resistance. Thus, despite the small anatomical diameter of the communication, catheterization confirmed a physiologically significant shunt. Preoperatively, the patient was afebrile and had no clinical or laboratory evidence suggestive of systemic infection or active infective endocarditis. The CRP level was 1.3 mg/L and the leukocyte count was 5.1 × 109/L. The only abnormal laboratory findings were mildly elevated NT-proBNP (256 ng/L), fibrinogen (3.91 g/L), and D-dimer (0.59 mg/L) levels. Accordingly, the final preoperative working diagnosis remained a hemodynamically significant Gerbode-type left ventricular outflow tract–to–right atrial communication. Following multidisciplinary Heart Team discussion, surgical repair was recommended.

Cardiopulmonary bypass was established using separate cannulation of the superior and inferior vena cava and the ascending aorta. After aortic cross-clamping, modified Del Nido cardioplegia was initially administered into the aortic root because the communication was presumed to originate from the left ventricular outflow tract. However, adequate perfusion pressure could not be achieved. Aortotomy was therefore performed, followed by selective antegrade cardioplegia administered directly into the coronary ostia without complications. Following right atriotomy, the right atrial opening of the communication was identified above the septal leaflet of the tricuspid valve (Figure 4). At the site of fistula drainage into the right atrium, a granulomatous windsock-like endocardial lesion measuring approximately 20 × 5 mm was identified, and tissue samples were obtained for microbiological and histopathological examination (Figure 5). The opposite opening of the tract was subsequently localized within the aortic root immediately above the non-coronary cusp of the aortic valve. These findings excluded a Gerbode defect and established the definitive anatomical diagnosis of a congenital non-coronary sinus of Valsalva-to-right atrial fistula without an aneurysmal component. The fistula was closed with an autologous pericardial patch secured using five pledgeted sutures placed from the right-sided cardiac chamber (Figure 6). Tricuspid annuloplasty was subsequently performed using an open annuloplasty ring. The decision was based on the inconclusive preoperative assessment of tricuspid regurgitation and concern that repair of the fistula adjacent to the septal leaflet might alter tricuspid valve geometry and leaflet coaptation. The patient was separated from cardiopulmonary bypass without complications. Aortic cross-clamp and cardiopulmonary bypass times were 58 and 96 min, respectively. The postoperative course was uneventful. Follow-up echocardiography demonstrated complete elimination of the left-to-right shunt without residual communication. Histopathological examination of the granulomatous tissue showed nonspecific regressive changes with focal calcific deposits and no specific features of active infective endocarditis. However, Staphylococcus capitis was isolated from tissue obtained intraoperatively under sterile conditions directly from the pathological endocardial lesion. The concordance between the macroscopic operative finding and the positive tissue culture raised a strong suspicion of localized infective involvement within the fistulous tract. Under the 2023 Duke–ISCVID criteria, culture of microorganisms from cardiac tissue may fulfill a pathological criterion for definite infective endocarditis; however, the interpretation in this case remained uncertain because Staphylococcus capitis is a low-virulence skin commensal, histopathology showed no active endocarditis, and secondary contamination could not be completely excluded [14]. Secondary contamination during specimen acquisition or processing was considered unlikely but could not be completely excluded. Intravenous flucloxacillin was therefore initiated according to the institutional infective endocarditis protocol at a dose of 3 g every 6 h, with a planned total treatment duration of six weeks. The patient was subsequently transferred to a regional hospital for continuation of intravenous antibiotic therapy. At the time of manuscript preparation, longer-term clinical and echocardiographic follow-up was not yet available because the planned follow-up interval had not been completed.

3. Discussion

3.1. Differential Diagnosis of Aorto-Atrial Fistulas

Transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) represents one of the fundamental diagnostic modalities in cardiology. However, its ability to visualize complex anatomy and flow patterns associated with small intracardiac shunts may be limited [2]. In such cases, transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) and computed tomography (CT) may provide superior anatomical characterization [2]. In the systematic review by Jainandunsing et al., echocardiography and angiography were the most frequently reported diagnostic modalities for AAF, illustrating the importance of combining complementary imaging techniques in this anatomically heterogeneous condition [1]. The differential diagnosis includes various anatomical variants of intracardiac shunts as well as isolated tricuspid regurgitation [6]. Using color Doppler imaging, TEE enables detailed assessment of pathological shunts, including their anatomical characteristics and the detection of vegetations. Although CT is less invasive, its sensitivity for detecting small vegetations remains inferior to that of TEE [2]. Cardiovascular magnetic resonance may provide complementary information in patients with complex or inconclusive anatomy by enabling three-dimensional anatomical assessment, quantification of ventricular volumes and function, and noninvasive estimation of the pulmonary-to-systemic flow ratio using phase-contrast flow measurements [15,16]. Its value as part of a multimodality imaging strategy for defining complex congenital cardiovascular anatomy and supporting multidisciplinary clinical decision-making has also been illustrated by Vlachakis et al. [17]. Furthermore, TEE represents the preferred imaging modality for evaluating treatment outcomes in aortocardiac and other intracardiac shunts [3]. In the presented case, preoperative TEE was not repeated because the defect had already been documented on previous examinations, although it had been incorrectly interpreted as a Gerbode defect for several years. In addition, right heart catheterization demonstrated hemodynamically significant left-to-right shunting, and the patient was therefore indicated for surgical repair. Because there were no clinical or laboratory signs of infection and no repeat preoperative TEE was performed, the localized infective involvement was not recognized before surgery (Table 1).

Although left ventricular–to–right atrial shunting had already been described in the 1930s and 1940s, Gerbode published the first series of successful surgical repairs in 1958, after which this rare anomaly became known as the Gerbode defect [11]. Gerbode defects may be either congenital or acquired, with acquired forms typically developing secondary to trauma, infection, myocardial infarction, or iatrogenic injury [18]. The congenital form accounts for less than 1% of all congenital cardiac defects [18]. According to the classification proposed by Sakakibara and Konno, Gerbode defects are categorized according to their relationship to the tricuspid valve into supravalvular (Type I), infravalvular (Type II), and intermediate (Type III) variants [19]. More recent studies suggest a predominance of the supravalvular Type I variant, accounting for up to 80% of cases [22]. As with a congenital Gerbode defect, congenital AAF most commonly drains above the septal leaflet of the tricuspid valve [23]. Clinical presentation of both entities is often similar and typically includes dyspnea, palpitations, fatigue, and heart failure symptoms [4,18]. Asymptomatic presentation of congenital Gerbode defect is relatively uncommon in adults and has been reported in only approximately 12% of cases [18]. Consequently, differentiation between these entities may be diagnostically challenging. Several authors have described diagnostic confusion between Gerbode defect and ruptured sinus of Valsalva aneurysm communicating with the right-sided cardiac chambers [20,21]. The timing of shunt flow may provide an important distinguishing feature. Whereas left ventricular–to–right atrial communication produces systolic flow, aorto–right atrial communication typically produces flow during both systole and diastole [18]. However, the flow pattern may vary depending on the pressure gradient and anatomical characteristics of the fistula [2]. In the present case, continuous flow was not clearly documented, which contributed to the preoperative diagnostic uncertainty. In addition, Jainandunsing et al., in a systematic review of 132 case reports comprising 136 patients with aorto–atrial fistulas, reported angiography as a diagnostic modality in approximately 60% of AAF cases, supporting differentiation from a Gerbode defect [1]. Aortography has also been described as a useful diagnostic tool in several additional case reports [3,24]. Rarely, the concomitant occurrence of a Gerbode defect and a ruptured sinus of Valsalva aneurysm draining into the right atrium has been reported in two separate case reports, with both lesions identified and treated simultaneously in each patient (Table 1) [11,25].

3.2. Treatment of Aorto-Atrial Fistulas

Early intervention is indicated in patients with an aorto–atrial fistula or ruptured sinus of Valsalva aneurysm associated with hemodynamically significant shunting [13,20]. Contemporary ACHD guidelines emphasize that the physiological significance of a congenital shunt should be determined from its hemodynamic consequences—including symptoms, shunt fraction, chamber remodeling, ventricular function, pulmonary artery pressure, and pulmonary vascular resistance—rather than from the anatomical diameter of the defect alone [26]. Surgical repair represents the most frequently used therapeutic strategy for AAF, whereas interventional treatment is reported in only approximately 10% of cases. Both direct suture closure and patch repair may be used for fistula correction [1,4]. In a systematic review of 132 case reports comprising 136 patients, direct suture closure was performed in 42% of surgically treated patients, while patch repair was used in 20%. Combined surgical techniques were applied in an additional 20% of patients, whereas the remaining reports did not specify the operative strategy [1]. In selected topoanatomical variants of AAF, particularly when the fistulous ostium is located in close proximity to a coronary ostium, aortotomy may be required to ensure safe surgical repair and avoid inadvertent compromise of coronary perfusion. In such cases, coronary ostial mobilization and reimplantation using the button technique may be necessary [4].

Additional therapeutic strategies include transcatheter closure using occlusion devices or coil embolization [1,3]. Warren et al. reported successful coil embolization of an asymptomatic aorto–left atrial appendage fistula diagnosed 16 years after open-heart surgery before implantation of a Watchman device [3]. Successful coil embolization has also been described in two additional cases included in a published meta-analysis [1]. Among interventional techniques, implantation of an Amplatzer occluder represents the most frequently reported strategy, whereas covered stent implantation and balloon closure have been reported less commonly [1]. Transcatheter closure using an Amplatzer occluder may be particularly advantageous in patients in whom AAF develops as a complication of previous cardiac surgery and repeat surgical intervention is associated with high operative risk [9].

3.3. Infective Endocarditis of Intracardiac Shunts

Conservative management may be considered in hemodynamically insignificant, asymptomatic, and uninfected intracardiac shunts [1,11,18,20]. Although rare, spontaneous closure of Gerbode defects or residual leaks following interventional closure of aorto–atrial fistulas has been reported in several published cases [13,18]. Several case reports have also described conservative management of aorto–atrial shunts for periods exceeding two decades. However, progressive deterioration of cardiac function has been documented, including reduction of LVEF to 30% and development of mild-to-moderate left ventricular systolic dysfunction, likely secondary to chronic volume overload [4]. Persistent intracardiac shunting has been associated with an increased risk of progressive heart failure with biventricular volume overload, infective endocarditis, fistula wall calcification, aortic regurgitation, pulmonary hypertension, and increased surgical mortality [4,18]. Infection represents one of the most common primary etiologies of AAF, with nearly half of these cases associated with prosthetic infective endocarditis [2,13]. AAF associated with infective endocarditis may have an unfavorable prognosis and high mortality [2,7]. More broadly, contemporary evidence regarding infective endocarditis involving structurally abnormal or prosthetic cardiac tissue emphasizes the need for individualized multidisciplinary decision-making, as the relative benefit of invasive versus conservative treatment remains influenced by patient selection, anatomical complexity, infection severity, and operative risk [27]. These considerations also emphasize the importance of an up-to-date echocardiographic assessment shortly before planned intervention, particularly in patients with persistent high-velocity intracardiac shunts. In the present case, the most recent preoperative echocardiographic examination had been performed almost three months before surgery and did not describe the windsock-like structure subsequently identified intraoperatively at the right atrial opening of the fistula. Although it remains uncertain whether the lesion was already present but unrecognized or developed during the interval before surgery, a more recent examination could potentially have detected a newly formed intracardiac mass arising at the site of chronic high-velocity flow, prompting evaluation for jet-related endothelial injury, sterile thrombotic deposition, and possible subsequent infective colonization.

Reports describing secondary infective endocarditis developing in long-standing congenital aorto–atrial communications are exceedingly rare [12,24]. Onorato et al. reported infective endocarditis complicating an initially asymptomatic congenital aorto–right atrial tunnel [24]. Similarly, Ikeda et al. described infective endocarditis developing in a chronic rupture of a sinus of Valsalva aneurysm into the right atrium diagnosed several years earlier [12]. However, in neither report had the shunt been confirmed by imaging before the manifestation of infective endocarditis. Both reports described a long-standing cardiac murmur preceding the diagnosis of infective endocarditis involving the aorto–atrial communication. In contrast to the majority of published reports, in which infective endocarditis represents the primary etiology of fistula formation, these observations raise the possibility that persistent high-velocity flow within persistent congenital aorto–atrial communications may predispose to secondary bacterial colonization and infective complications later in life. A possible mechanistic explanation is based on the established concept of flow-mediated endocardial injury. Repeated impact of a high-velocity shunt jet on the endocardial surface may produce a focal jet lesion characterized by endothelial disruption or erosion [28,29]. Such endothelial damage may expose thrombogenic subendothelial structures and thereby permit direct bacterial adhesion during transient bacteremia [29,30]. Alternatively, platelet and fibrin deposition may form a sterile thrombotic nidus at the injured site, which may subsequently be colonized by circulating microorganisms and develop into an infected vegetation [29,30]. Experimental and pathological observations also indicate that damaged endocardium may occasionally serve as a focus of bacterial infection even before a mature sterile thrombus has developed [29]. Clinical reports of mural infective endocarditis developing at the site of direct impact of high-velocity regurgitant jets provide additional support for this mechanism [31,32]. Nevertheless, these mechanisms represent biologically plausible explanations and cannot be proven causally from a single clinical case.

Staphylococcus capitis is a low-virulence coagulase-negative staphylococcus predominantly associated with infections related to implanted intracardiac prosthetic material and cardiovascular devices [33]. As part of the physiological skin flora, particularly of the scalp and forehead, it is frequently regarded as a possible contaminant when isolated in microbiological cultures [34,35]. Nevertheless, although rare, S. capitis has been reported as a causative pathogen of infective endocarditis involving both prosthetic and native cardiac structures [33,35]. Infective endocarditis caused by S. capitis has also been described following surgical procedures involving the scalp [34,35]. Although the specimen in the present case was obtained intraoperatively from pathological tissue under sterile conditions, contamination during specimen acquisition or processing cannot be completely excluded. The microbiological finding should therefore be interpreted in conjunction with the macroscopic operative appearance, histopathological findings, and overall clinical context.

4. Conclusions

The present case highlights the diagnostic difficulty of differentiating ventriculo–atrial from aorto–atrial communications and supports the use of multimodality imaging, including aortography when the anatomical origin remains uncertain. Persistent high-velocity shunt flow may contribute to endothelial injury and susceptibility to secondary bacterial colonization, although causality cannot be established from a single case. Low-virulence skin commensals isolated from surgically obtained pathological cardiac tissue should be interpreted in the full clinical, microbiological, histopathological, and intraoperative context rather than automatically considered contaminants. To the best of our knowledge, only a very limited number of comparable cases have been reported, and we identified no additional publication in which the defect had been documented by imaging several years before the suspected infection.
