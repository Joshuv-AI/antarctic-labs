Barriers to orthodontic treatment among rural children in Guangxi, China: a mixed-methods study
Authors: Lijuan Xiao, Ling Zhao, Wenjia Mai, Lan Nan, Haoyu He, Xiaojuan Zeng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMalocclusion represents a major global oral health burden, impairing function, aesthetics, and psychosocial well-being. The rates of treatment of rural children are significantly lower than for urban children. The objective of this study is to find core barriers to orthodontic treatment for these children and analyze how these factors vary by malocclusion severity.MethodsIn this mixed-methods study, we recruited 42 pairs of 11–12-year-old children with malocclusion and their parents by purposive sampling in 10 rural primary schools in Guangxi, China. Data came from semi-structured interviews; after coding, network analysis was used to find the key barriers to orthodontic treatment and to assess if these barriers varied by malocclusion severity.ResultsFinancial pressure and low orthodontic health literacy were primary barriers to orthodontic treatment. Financial pressure was consistent across all malocclusion groups, but other factors differed. For the definite group, primary barriers were the difficulty of regular follow-up, limited individual awareness of adverse effects, and personal concerns about daily impacts. For the severe group, primary factors were the parental lack of orthodontic knowledge, poor treatment timing awareness, and doubts about efficacy and safety. For the handicapping group, primary barriers were the poor awareness of the treatment time, parental concerns about children's daily life, and doubts about efficacy and safety. With the malocclusion severity increasing, primary factors shifted from child-centered concerns to parent-centered knowledge and trust issues, as individual-level barriers became less and the family-concerned environment factors became more.ConclusionsFinancial pressure and low orthodontic health literacy were the primary barriers to orthodontic treatment among rural children, with differences across malocclusion severity subgroups. From a public health view, future efforts should be focused on improving orthodontic health literacy with targeted education and awareness campaigns, especially tackling subgroup-specific barriers. Furthermore, training primary care dentists in orthodontics can help spread the related knowledge and handle simpler cases locally. Also, cost-effective treatment strategies should be explored and advanced. These could improve the treatment uptake among the rural children.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12903-026-08966-4.Keywords: Orthodontic treatment, Barriers, Access to care, Malocclusion, Network analysis, Mixed-methods research

Background

Malocclusion is one of the most common oral health issues affecting children, with a reported prevalence ranging from 47.9% to 83.5% across different regions of the world [1–4]. Beyond compromising oral function and facial esthetics while elevating the risk of dental caries and periodontal disease [5], malocclusion also brings serious adverse effects to the children’s psychological well-being and social competence [6, 7]. Orthodontic treatment serves as the primary method for correcting malocclusion. It effectively alleviates clinical symptoms and, by enhancing the dental aesthetics, improves patients’ self-confidence and social acceptance, which in turn improves overall quality of life [8, 9]. Childhood is the optimal time for orthodontic intervention, particularly for certain severe skeletal malformations, for which timely treatment during growth spurts can reduce the need for orthognathic surgery in adulthood [10]. Previous studies on the general child population show that the completion rate of orthodontic treatment for 11-year-old children can reach 26.5%–45.56% [8, 11]. Due to factors such as socioeconomic status, healthcare access, and health literacy, the orthodontic treatment rate among rural children is significantly lower than that among urban children. One study reported that the orthodontic treatment rate for rural children is only 3% [12]. Such a low treatment rate underscores the need to identify barriers and improve treatment adoption among rural children.

Whether children undergo orthodontic treatment depends in part on the severity of malocclusion and the child’s own willingness to receive care. For instance, it has been reported that about 58.6% of children meet diagnostic criteria for treatment [13], whereas 46.8% express a subjective desire for it [14]. However, treatment need does not automatically translate into actual treatment uptake. In addition, the decision to seek orthodontic care is also shaped by factors such as family financial capacity, individual health literacy, and the accessibility of healthcare resources [15, 16]. However, only a few studies have explored these factors. For instance, Tianyi Wang et al. found that beyond the severity of malocclusion, treatment-related pain, treatment duration, and cost also prevent patients from initiating orthodontic treatment [8]. Meanwhile, Oskar Bauss et al. reported that a patient's decision to pursue treatment may also be influenced by experiences of bullying due to their malocclusion [17]. Additionally, some scholars have also noted that children’s uptake of orthodontic treatment is associated with the regional density of orthodontic specialists, the child's satisfaction with dental aesthetics, parental attitudes toward treatment, and whether orthodontics is covered by public healthcare schemes [12]. However, these studies mainly adopted structured questionnaires with predefined items derived from existing literature or the researcher’s experience. This approach inevitably restricts the scope and depth of factors that can be identified, thus hindering a comprehensive understanding of the subjective views held by children and parents. Particularly in rural areas, where orthodontic health literacy among the public is often limited, application of these questionnaires may also reduce the validity of survey findings because of possible comprehension bias [8, 14, 18]. Therefore, some researchers have suggested adopting qualitative approaches to better explore the barriers to children’s orthodontic treatment [19]. This approach is particularly effective for capturing participants' subjective perspectives, which are often shaped by their social, cultural, and environmental contexts.

Semi-structured interviews are a commonly used qualitative research method in medical studies [20]. Interviewers carry out interviews with a rough interview guide and may adjust question content and phrasing as needed. This approach not only avoids the constraints imposed by predefined items in traditional questionnaires but also enables an in-depth exploration of respondents’ perspectives within their specific social and situational contexts [21, 22]. Some researchers have suggested that, given the complexity and social embeddedness of health-seeking behavior, semi-structured interviews can shed light on the factors influencing dental treatment decision-making [23]. However, in traditional qualitative analysis, the analysis mainly focuses on the theme identification and description, so it is hard to show us the relationship among many influencing factors or to find out the core determinant. This leaves a critical gap in understanding how these factors interact and in determining priorities for intervention.

Network analysis, a method that reveals interrelationships between variables and pinpoints core versus peripheral factors through centrality indicators [24–26], offers a new way to fill this gap. This method has seen wide use across disciplines, and scholar already have applied it to semi-structured interview data [27], but not yet to data from orthodontics.

The combination of these two methods uses the strength of the exploratory of the interviews to get the point of view of the participants, and the network analysis turns the obtained qualitative data into quantifiable patterns of relations. It helps to identify the key targets of the intervention, and it also covers some of the weaknesses of the qualitative research alone in the mapping of associations among the variables.

Accordingly, the present study is a mixed-methods study, comprising semi-structured interviews with rural children and their parents, followed by network analysis. Specifically, this study is to identify key barriers to orthodontic treatment among rural children, employ network analysis to examine how these barriers interact, and assess if barriers differ by malocclusion severity. The findings are meant to inform personalized, severity-stratified intervention strategies, aiming ultimately to improve treatment uptake in this population.

Methods

Design

We used the Checklist for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ) checklist in the qualitative phase of this mixed-methods research. The study was approved by the Ethics and Research Committee of Guangxi Medical University (Approval No. 2022045). Informed consent was obtained from all the students and their guardians.

This study was done between March and May 2023 in 10 primary schools in rural Guangxi. The sample was selected as follows: (1) randomly drew 5 counties from 28 national-level key counties for poverty alleviation; (2) from each of the sampled counties, randomly selected two rural primary schools; (3)after that, purposive sampling was used to recruit 42 children and their parents from these 10 schools for face-to-face and telephone interviews, respectively. The interviewers were four orthodontists and one public health researcher, all of whom were trained in the qualitative research method. Face-to-face interviews with children were done individually in primary school libraries or quiet faculty offices, while parental interviews were conducted by telephone.

Before each interview, researchers told participants the study’s aims, promised confidentiality, and got informed consent, including permission for audio-recording. Interviews were done according to an interview outline, and researchers could flexibly change the content and manner of questioning according to participants’ understanding and feedback. After each interview, participants were invited to add further information, and reflective notes were made within two hours. Also, all child participants received a free oral care product, and their parents were given 30 RMB in phone credit.

Participant selection

The inclusion criteria for children were as follows: (1) Sixth-grade students aged 11—12. (2) Have not received orthodontic treatment. (3) According to the inspection standards of the DAI scale, the total Dental Aesthetic Index (DAI) [28] score is ≥ 26 points. (4) Parents are willing to accept telephone interviews. The exclusion criteria were: (1) Have cognitive, language, or intellectual disabilities and cannot communicate normally. (2) Are accompanied by congenital malformations affecting the oral cavity or systemic diseases.

Purposive sampling was employed among children fulfilling the inclusion and exclusion criteria. The main characteristics considered were the severity of malocclusion, which was determined by the DAI score, to ensure the diversity of the sample. Oral examinations were conducted prior to interviews using the DAI. Based on DAI scores, enrolled children were categorized into the definite malocclusion group, severe malocclusion group, and handicapping malocclusion group (Table 1). Sample size was determined based on the principle of information saturation, with data collection continuing until no new themes emerged. Information saturation was attained when 11 children had been interviewed per group. To ensure saturation among parents, an additional 3 child–parent dyads were recruited per group. In total, 42 children and 42 parents were included, achieving information saturation for both child and parent interviews. No participants declined participation or withdrew during the study.

Coding framework and interview outline

Determination of the coding framework

We conducted a directed qualitative content analysis. To construct the coding framework, we compiled factors that might affect the initiation of orthodontic treatment in children through a literature review and developed an initial coding framework comprising two dimensions, individual factors and environmental factors, and four sub‑dimensions, personal cognition, personal behavior, external environment, and family environment, with 14 codes [8, 12, 16, 19, 23, 29–42]. This first version was reviewed by a panel of epidemiologists, pediatric dentists, and orthodontic experts. Based on their feedback, two additional codes, parents’ concerns about orthodontic impacts on their children’s daily lives, and Parents’ question efficacy and safety, were added to the family environment sub‑dimension, resulting in a second version with 16 codes. During subsequent analysis of the interview data, iterative refinement of the framework identified two further codes, lack of attention to oral health issues in parents and negative treatment experiences in parents, which were also incorporated into the family environment sub‑dimension. This process yielded the final coding framework comprising 18 codes (Table 2), which was used for data analysis.

Semi-structured interview coding framework

Development of interview outlines

Based on the second version coding framework, interview outlines for children and parents were designed. The children's outline covered the contents of the personal cognition, personal behavior, and external environment sub-dimensions, while the parents' outline focused on the family environment sub-dimension. The outlines were designed by the researchers and reviewed and revised by pediatric dentists, epidemiologists, and qualitative research experts. After obtaining informed consent, a pre-interview was conducted with 5 children and their parents to evaluate the comprehensibility of the questions. The outlines were revised according to the feedback from the pre-interview to form the final version (Appendix 1). The data from the pre-interview were not included in the final analysis.

Data processing and analysis

This study used an exploratory sequential mixed-methods design with data transformation (quantitizing). Specifically, qualitative interview data were the primary source, while quantitative network analysis was the analytical tool. Using directed content analysis based on a theoretically derived coding framework, the qualitative narratives regarding influencing factors of malocclusion in rural children were turned into structured binary data. We defined co-occurrence as two factors reported from the same participant. Then, based on the absolute co-occurrence frequencies of these coded factors, adjacency matrices were built to extract the core nodes and visualize the networks.

Data transcription and coding

Two researchers used NVivo 11.0 software to preliminarily code the interview data according to the second version of the interview outline. Disagreements were settled by discussion. The coding framework was revised from coding feedback to form the third version framework, and all data were recoded.

Network analysis

Using the bootnet and qgraph packages in the R environment, we built undirected weighted networks to visualize and analyze the complex interplay among influencing factors for the overall sample and each malocclusion subgroup. In these networks, nodes stand for specific factors and edges stand for their co-occurrence; the weight of edges reflects the exact number of people who have both factors. The edges that have a frequency of zero are not involved in the networks to reduce the spurious connections and make the visualization clearer. The Fruchterman-Reingold algorithm puts the strongly connected nodes closer to each other. Three standardized centrality indices (Z-scores) were used to find the most important nodes in the networks, which are Strength, Closeness, and Betweenness. Strength (weighted degree) is the sum of the weight of edges that are connected with the node, it shows how much direct and the overall influence of a node has in the network. Closeness is the inverse of the sum of the shortest path distances from a node to all other nodes, indicating how quickly a factor can interact with or affect the entire network. Betweenness counts how many times a node acts as a bridge along the shortest path between two other nodes, showing how critical a node is in connecting different clusters or the transfer of influence in the whole network.

Similarly, these four nodes showed high closeness centrality (EN12: Z = 1.54; EN5: Z = 1.24; IN3: Z = 1.13; EN4: Z = 0.72). Discussion

To gain a more comprehensive and in-depth understanding of the perspectives and attitudes of both patients and their parents, this study employed a mixed-methods approach to investigate the barriers to orthodontic treatment among rural children. Our findings indicate that financial pressure and low orthodontic health literacy among the public are the primary barriers to orthodontic treatment for rural children. Across different malocclusion-severity groups, while financial pressure consistently emerged as the predominant barrier, we observed that as the severity of malocclusion increased, barriers related to individual factors diminished, whereas those associated with environmental factors, particularly family-related factors within the environmental dimension, became more prominent, emerging as significant impediments to orthodontic treatment among adolescents.

Overall barriers to orthodontic treatment

Overall network analysis shows that financial pressure (EN12) is the core factor preventing rural children's access to orthodontic care. In regions where orthodontic treatment is not publicly insured, high treatment costs often determine whether patients begin therapy. As we can see, according to previous research. A study by Leandro S. Marques et al. showed that financial constraints were the primary barrier that did not allow the children to start the orthodontic treatment in Brazil, where about 69% of parents reported high costs as the reason for discontinuing treatment [43]. Vahid Ravaghi et al. also observed socioeconomic gaps in orthodontic treatment uptake among children in the UK, finding much lower treatment rates among low-income families [44].

Furthermore, this study not only found that financial pressure had high strength centrality within the barrier network, but also that its closeness and betweenness centrality were relatively high, indicating that it was closely related to other barriers and was a key factor connected to multiple other factors. This finding suggests that financial pressure not only blocks treatment initiation directly but also correlates with other elements including parental and child awareness of orthodontic treatment timing and procedures, as well as children’s adherence to scheduled follow‑up visits. On the impact of financial pressure on follow‑up compliance, previous studies have also shown that lower family economic status may reduce parental oral health awareness and acceptance of routine dental examinations, which can lead to undetected malocclusion, delayed referral for orthodontic care, and ultimately lower treatment uptake [33]. Additionally, in a study of dental caries and oral health service use, Chaiana Piovesan et al. noted that financial constraints may hinder access to dental care by shaping household oral health behaviors and families’ ability to obtain and understand health-related information [32]. The present study, however, reveals similar associations in orthodontic care, whereby financial pressure links to restricted treatment access alongside family health perceptions and behaviors. This suggests that the correlations between economic factors and treatment decisions may represent a common pattern across oral health conditions.

In addition, this study found that lack of orthodontic knowledge in parents (EN4), limited awareness of treatment timing (EN5), and individuals' concerns about orthodontic impacts on daily life (IN3) were also important factors affecting orthodontic treatment for rural children. These factors are all related to the level of information cognition. Lack of orthodontic knowledge in parents (EN4) reflected that parents lacked the most basic understanding of orthodontic treatment. Limited awareness of treatment timing in parents (EN5) and individuals' concerns about orthodontic impacts on daily life (IN3) reflected the insufficient understanding of orthodontic treatment details by parents and children, indicating that rural residents had widespread lack of knowledge and misunderstandings about malocclusion and orthodontic treatment. This suggests that we should strengthen health promotion efforts to improve orthodontic health literacy among the public.

Notably, in the overall network of barriers, insufficient awareness of the adverse effects of orthodontic treatment among parents (EN3) and the children themselves (IN1) did not emerge as a primary factor hindering the initiation of treatment. This finding contrasts with previous research suggesting that lower household income is often associated with reduced self-perceived need for dental treatment [45]. In the present study, however, most parents and children demonstrated adequate awareness of the negative impacts associated with malocclusion. This indicates that the decision not to pursue treatment stems not from ignorance or tolerance of these issues, but rather from the multifaceted barriers rural children face in accessing orthodontic care [32, 46, 47].

Barriers to orthodontic treatment in the definite malocclusion group

In the definite malocclusion group, the primary barriers to children’s orthodontic treatment choice were limited awareness of adverse effects in individuals (IN1), individuals' concerns about orthodontic impacts on daily life (IN3), difficulty in regular check-ups (EN10), and financial pressure (EN12).

Due to the mildest symptoms in this group, children have limited awareness of the adverse effects of malocclusion (IN1), which in turn reduces their motivation for treatment [12]. In addition, children with mild malocclusion tend to have a lower level of understanding about orthodontic treatment, making them prone to misconceptions about treatment-related discomfort (IN3). Therefore, concerns about pain during orthodontic treatment often become the main reason patients in this group decline treatment [48]. For example, in this study, a participant mentioned: “I’m afraid of pain and don’t know how to eat with braces” (CL12).

As for the family-level factors, beyond financial pressure (EN12), parents in this group also cited difficulties with follow-up appointments (EN10) as a major obstacle to their children's orthodontic care. This is because orthodontic treatment requires multiple follow-up visits, which not only impose additional time and financial burdens on families but may also conflict with other family activities [31, 39, 49]. In this study, several parents expressed concerns about the time required for treatment. One parent noted, “You have to queue at the hospital, and I also have a job, so I simply don’t have the time to take him”(PL3). Another parent highlighted the scheduling conflict between appointments and family responsibilities: “I have four children to care for; where would I find the extra time to take him for treatment?”(PL11). These burdens related to follow-up visits are particularly prominent when parents do not perceive malocclusion as an urgent issue, and even in regions offering free orthodontic services, the combined costs of travel and income loss from missed work can readily lead to treatment refusal or discontinuation [30, 40].

These findings suggest that increasing treatment rates in this group may benefit from enhancing public orthodontic health literacy, reducing adolescents' fears and misconceptions regarding the impacts of treatment on daily life, and improving access to healthcare services, such as offering holiday clinics and streamlining follow-up procedures.

Barriers to orthodontic treatment in the severe malocclusion group

In the severe malocclusion group, the primary barriers shifted from limited individual awareness of adverse effects (IN1) and concerns about orthodontic impacts on daily life (IN3) to parent-related factors, including a lack of orthodontic knowledge (EN4), limited awareness of treatment timing (EN5), and parental doubts about efficacy and safety (EN7)​

This shift is likely attributable to the fact that as malocclusion severity increases, its adverse impacts on daily living become more salient to patients, and their willingness to seek treatment consequently strengthens. In the treatment decision-making process, positive motivations for treatment gradually outweigh negative reservations [50], thus IN1 and IN3 are no longer the primary factors hindering children from receiving orthodontic treatment.

However, as minors, whether children can receive treatment depends on their parents' attitudes and perceptions [42]. Particularly, parents' level of orthodontic knowledge is a critical determinant of whether their children initiate orthodontic treatment [46]. In rural areas, parents' relatively lower educational levels and limited access to information channels often lead to a lack of orthodontic knowledge, thereby hindering their decision to pursue orthodontic treatment for their children [42]. For instance, in this group of parents, the proportion of those who had only received primary education was as high as 42.9%. These parents generally lacked a basic understanding of orthodontic treatment. For example, some parents mentioned, " don’t know how to solve this protrusion problem; I don’t think it can be solved(PM5)" or "There’s probably no way to improve this kind of tooth arrangement (PM8)". Furthermore, parents expressed doubts about the efficacy and safety of treatment, stating, "I'm worried about the outcome; what if I spend money and it doesn't work well (PM1)" or "I'm afraid of swelling and heavy bleeding during treatment (PM2)." Therefore, despite the more severe malocclusion symptoms in this group of children, parental lack of orthodontic knowledge (EN4, EN5, EN7) still impedes the initiation of orthodontic treatment for their children. This finding also corroborates the results of previous research, which indicates that patients' poor self-perception of tooth alignment is associated with lower maternal education levels [51], and that maternal education level is a significant sociodemographic predictor for children's participation in orthodontic treatment [52].

In addition, it is noteworthy that despite significant disparities in healthcare levels between urban and rural areas in China, rural regions not only suffer from a shortage of dentists but also generally exhibit lower oral health awareness among patients [53, 54]. For example, one parent mentioned, "I just take painkillers when I have a toothache; I have never seen a dentist "(PM8). However, most parents in this study did not consider the lack of medical resources as a barrier. This is because rural parents are often unaware that orthodontics requires specialized orthodontists to implement the treatment, believing that "this isn't high-tech; a general dentist can handle it "(PM14). This result also reflects, from another perspective, the impact of rural parents' low orthodontic health literacy on their children's treatment decisions. This underscores the importance of conducting systematic popularization and education of orthodontic knowledge for parents, and the educational content should include key information such as the harms of malocclusion, treatment methods, and treatment safety, to improve the orthodontic treatment rate in this patient group.​​

Barriers to orthodontic treatment in the handicapping malocclusion group

In the handicapping malocclusion group, the lack of orthodontic knowledge in parents (EN4) was no longer the primary barrier. This might be because, with the increase in the severity of malocclusion, its impact on aesthetics and function was more likely to attract the attention of parents and relatives [17, 55]. Therefore, parents in this group proactively or passively obtain more orthodontic knowledge from relatives and friends. For example, one parent mentioned, "When chatting with others, someone pointed out that my child's teeth were not right and suggested considering orthodontic treatment" (PS10).

Notably, however, limited awareness of optimal treatment timing (EN5) remains an important barrier in this group. This indicates that although parents in this group may have a higher level of orthodontic awareness than those in other symptom groups, they still have knowledge gaps regarding key treatment details. This result is consistent with the study by Min Lin et al., who pointed out that many Chinese parents lack a clear understanding of the optimal timing for orthodontic treatment and generally believe that children can only receive treatment at the age of 12 or after reaching adulthood [29]. From the perspective of clinical practice, this misunderstanding may easily cause patients to miss the best treatment opportunities during pre-adolescence or adolescence [8, 12, 56–58]. It may even cause some patients with severe skeletal malocclusion to miss the opportunity of early intervention during the growth spurt period, thereby increasing the probability of them receiving more risky and expensive orthognathic surgery in adulthood [10].

Concurrently, parents' concerns about orthodontic treatment impacting their children's daily lives (EN6) were also an important barrier in this group. For example, one parent mentioned, "Treatment will definitely be painful; I'm worried he won't be able to eat" (PS6). This indicates that when parents face treatment decisions, these details related to the treatment process also become key factors influencing their choices. Therefore, for the handicapping malocclusion group, future orthodontic health education initiatives should also strengthen the dissemination of knowledge related to treatment timing and the treatment process, eliminating parents' misconceptions and unnecessary concerns about orthodontic treatment. In terms of publicity channels, in addition to educating children and parents, it is also recommended to popularize knowledge among community health doctors responsible for children's routine physical examinations and school teachers. This would facilitate timely referrals by health doctors and promote the dissemination of correct scientific information by teachers, thereby improving the timely treatment rate among rural children.

Additionally, since orthodontic treatment is not yet included in free medical coverage, despite the highest treatment need among children in this group, financial pressure (EN12) remains a major barrier to children initiating orthodontic treatment. For example, one parent mentioned, "I heard the cost is very high, and monthly maintenance also costs money; we cannot afford it" (PS2). Therefore, it is necessary to increase public health investments in orthodontic treatment for rural children and to select and promote cost-effective orthodontic treatment programs. For example, timely intervention using facemask appliances during the growth spurt period, which are simple to administer and relatively inexpensive, can correct skeletal discrepancy in patients with severe skeletal Class III malocclusion, thus reducing the risk of subsequent orthognathic surgery. At the same time, opportunities for primary dentists to acquire orthodontic expertise can be increased to facilitate patients in seeking medical treatment nearby and reduce the additional economic burden caused by follow-up visits.

Innovations and limitations

To our knowledge, this is the first mixed-methods study combining semi-structured interviews and network analysis to investigate the multifactorial barriers to orthodontic care access among rural children, offering a novel analytical perspective for the field. Notably, subgroup analyses further identified key barriers to orthodontic treatment among children with varying degrees of malocclusion severity. These results support the design of targeted intervention strategies for different severity groups.

Nevertheless, there are also some limitations for this study. First, the study participants were only 11–12 years old children from rural Guangxi. and a single region may not be representative of other rural populations. Second, this study used purposive sampling; even though the sample size satisfies the data saturation requirement for the qualitative analysis, the relatively small sample and subgroup sizes could make certain spatial network metrics mathematically unstable, calling for cautious interpretation. All of these factors may limit the generalizability of the findings. Also, we did not collect household income, which would limit a full assessment of the socioeconomic diversity in this rural sample. Third, as is the case for any qualitative study, there may be potential interviewer bias in data collection and interpretation. The results of this study need to be validated by larger-scale quantitative studies. For future studies, we should expand to children from different regions, cultural backgrounds, household income levels, and age groups. Also, use the quantitative approach to increase the sample size for further verification and extension of these findings. and minimize interviewer bias to improve data rigor.

Conclusions

This study used a mixed-methods design to identify barriers to orthodontic treatment among rural children. The findings showed that financial pressure was a consistent barrier across all subgroups, and the composition of other primary barriers varied by malocclusion severity, especially individual concerns about the impacts of treatment, low parental orthodontic health literacy, and inadequate awareness of treatment timing and safety.

These findings give important evidence for developing rural orthodontic service strategies. To ease the financial burdens, cost‑effective treatment should be promoted, and orthodontic training for primary dentists should be enhanced to enable nearby care and reduce additional follow‑up costs. As for raising awareness, systematic health education should be done to inform rural families about orthodontic treatment, covering key information, including optimal treatment time and the safety for orthodontic treatment. Besides, health education channels should be expanded via community health physicians and school teachers, who work closely with children, to support timely referral and accurate information dissemination. These measures may raise treatment uptake and in turn, improve the oral health-related quality of life among the rural children.
