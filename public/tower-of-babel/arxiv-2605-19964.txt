Lee-Yang zeros and edge singularity in a mean-field approach
Tatsuya Wada, Győző Kovács, Masakiyo Kitazawa, Takahiro M. Doi

Abstract
The analytic structure of the partition function in finite-volume systems is investigated at complex chemical potentials in a minimal mean-field effective model of QCD with finite-size effects incorporated. We discuss the temperature dependence of the Lee-Yang zeros and their relation to the edge singularity for various system sizes. Different methods for locating the critical point based on finite-size scaling of Lee-Yang zeros and susceptibility ratios are compared. We demonstrate that these methods can successfully identify the critical point, whereas a careful treatment of corrections from irrelevant operators is crucial for its accurate determination.

II
Description of Finite-size Effects

II.1
Finite-volume mean-field approach

To describe the finite-size effects in a manner consistent with the mean-field approximation, we follow the method discussed in Ref.
46
.

Near a CP, properties of a thermal system are well characterized by an order-parameter field. Assuming that it is a real scalar field denoted by
\phi(x)
and integrating out all degrees of freedom except for
\phi(x)
, the partition function is given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{Z}=\int\mathcal{D}\phi e^{-S_{\rm E}(\phi)}\,,

(1)

with the effective Euclidean action
S_{\rm E}(\phi)
.
In the conventional mean-field approximation, one introduces the effective potential
\mathcal{U}(\bar{\phi})=(T/V)S_{\mathrm{E}}(\bar{\phi})
assuming that
\phi(x)
is temporally and spatially uniform,
\bar{\phi}=\phi(x)
, where
T
and
V
are the temperature and volume of the system.
In the standard treatment for the thermodynamic limit, the value of
\bar{\phi}
is determined so as to minimize
\mathcal{U}(\bar{\phi})
, and the free-energy density is given by
f=\mathcal{U}(\bar{\phi})
at the minimum.
As a result, in this prescription, the value of
\bar{\phi}
can have discontinuities or non-analytic behaviors with the variation of thermodynamic parameters, which result in non-analyticities of the free energy corresponding to phase transitions. However, when this procedure is directly applied to a finite-size system, such non-analyticities contradict the fact that the thermodynamics in a finite system is always analytic.

To realize the analyticity at finite
V
with a minimal modification to the mean-field approximation, we assume that the partition function is given by
46

\displaystyle\mathcal{Z}=\int d\bar{\phi}e^{-V\mathcal{U}(\bar{\phi})/T}\,.

(2)

Equation (
2
) is regarded as a modification of the mean-field approximation that incorporates fluctuations of the spatially uniform mode of
\phi(x)
. The free-energy density is then defined from Eq. (
2
) as

\displaystyle f=-\frac{T}{V}\ln\mathcal{Z}\,,

(3)

which, in contrast to the conventional mean-field approach, does not coincide with
\mathcal{U}(\bar{\phi})
. Thermodynamic quantities are obtained from Eq. (
3
) using standard thermodynamic relations. In the thermodynamic limit
V\to\infty
, the conventional mean-field results are recovered because only the global minimum of
\mathcal{U}(\bar{\phi})
contributes to the integral in Eq. (
2
), while Eq. (
2
) is an analytic function at finite
V
provided the analyticity of
\mathcal{U}(\bar{\phi})
.

II.2
Landau potential

To examine how Eq. (
2
) describes thermodynamics near a CP at finite volume, let us employ the Landau potential

\displaystyle U_{\rm Landau}(\bar{\phi};\tau,\eta)=u_{0}(\tau,\eta)T+\frac{\tau}{2}\bar{\phi}^{2}+\frac{b}{4!}\bar{\phi}^{4}-\eta\bar{\phi}\,,

(4)

where
\tau
and
\eta
correspond to the reduced temperature and external magnetic field in the Ising model, respectively. The CP is located at
(\tau,\eta)=(0,0)
as an endpoint of the first-order phase-transition line at
\eta=0
and
\tau<0
. The
\bar{\phi}
-independent term
u_{0}(\tau,\eta)T
is assumed to be regular. Substituting Eq. (
4
) into Eq. (
2
) yields the partition function

\displaystyle\mathcal{Z}_{\rm Landau}(\tau,\eta,L)=e^{-L^{d}u_{0}}\int d\bar{\phi}\,e^{-L^{d}(\tau\bar{\phi}^{2}/2+b\bar{\phi}^{4}/4!-\eta\bar{\phi})/T}\,,

(5)

with the system size
L
and dimensionality
d
.

Performing the scaling transformation

\displaystyle\tau\to\ell^{y_{\tau}}\tau,\quad\eta\to\ell^{y_{\eta}}\eta,\quad\bar{\phi}\to\ell^{y_{\phi}}\bar{\phi},\quad L\to\ell^{-1}L,

(6)

in Eq. (
5
), one finds that the partition function satisfies the finite-size scaling (FSS) relation

\displaystyle\mathcal{Z}_{\rm Landau}(\tau,\eta,L)

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{-L^{d}u_{0}(\tau,\eta)}\ell^{y_{\phi}}}{e^{-L^{d}u_{0}(\ell^{y_{\tau}}\tau,\ell^{y_{\eta}}\eta)}}\mathcal{Z}_{\rm Landau}(\ell^{y_{\tau}}\tau,\ell^{y_{\eta}}\eta,\ell^{-1}L)

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{-L^{d}u_{0}(\tau,\eta)}L^{y_{\phi}}}{e^{-L^{d}u_{0}(L^{y_{\tau}}\tau,L^{y_{\eta}}\eta)}}\tilde{\mathcal{Z}}(L^{y_{\tau}}\tau,L^{y_{\eta}}\eta)\,,

(7)

with the scaling exponents

\displaystyle y_{\tau}=\frac{d}{2},\quad y_{\eta}=\frac{3d}{4},\quad y_{\phi}=\frac{d}{4},

(8)

where on the second equality of Eq. (
7
) we substituted
\ell=L
and introduced the scaling function

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathcal{Z}}(\tilde{\tau},\tilde{\eta})\equiv\int d\phi e^{-(\tilde{\tau}\bar{\phi}^{2}/2+b\bar{\phi}^{4}/4!-\tilde{\eta}\bar{\phi})/T}\,.

(9)

Equation (
7
) also leads to the FSS relation for the singular part of the free-energy density

\displaystyle f_{\rm s}(\tau,\eta,L)=\tilde{f}_{\rm s}(L^{y_{\tau}}\tau,L^{y_{\eta}}\eta)\,,

(10)

with the scaling function
\tilde{f}_{\rm s}(\tilde{\tau},\tilde{\eta})\equiv-T\ln\mathcal{Z}(\tilde{\tau},\tilde{\eta})
.

When a CP manifests itself in a general system, such as QCD, the variables
\tau
and
\eta
are linearly related to thermodynamic parameters in the general system in its vicinity. For the QCD CP on the temperature (
T
)–baryon chemical potential (
\mu_{\rm B}
) plane, for instance, one has

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}\tau\\
\eta\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}a_{11}&a_{12}\\
a_{21}&a_{22}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}T-T_{\rm CP}\\
\mu_{\mathrm{B}}-\mu_{\rm CP}\end{pmatrix}\equiv A\begin{pmatrix}\delta T\\
\delta\mu_{\mathrm{B}}\end{pmatrix},

(11)

with the location of the critical point
(T,\mu_{\rm B})=(T_{\rm CP},\mu_{\rm CP})
.
In this case, the partition function and the singular part of the free-energy density near the CP satisfy

\displaystyle\mathcal{Z}(T,\mu_{\rm B},L)

\displaystyle=e^{-L^{d}u_{0}}L^{y_{\phi}}\tilde{\mathcal{Z}}\big(L^{y_{\tau}}\tau(T,\mu_{\rm B}),L^{y_{\eta}}\eta(T,\mu_{\rm B})\big)\,,

(12)

\displaystyle f_{\rm s}(T,\mu_{\rm B},L)

\displaystyle=\tilde{f}_{\rm s}\big(L^{y_{\tau}}\tau(T,\mu_{\rm B}),L^{y_{\eta}}\eta(T,\mu_{\rm B})\big)\,.

(13)

The unit vectors along the
\tau
- and
\eta
-axes,
\vec{e}_{\tau}
and
\vec{e}_{\eta}
, are related to those along the
T
- and
\mu_{\rm B}
-axes,
\vec{e}_{T}
and
\vec{e}_{\mu}
, as

\displaystyle\vec{e}_{\tau}\propto a_{22}\vec{e}_{T}-a_{21}\vec{e}_{\mu_{\mathrm{B}}}\,,

\displaystyle\vec{e}_{\eta}\propto-a_{12}\vec{e}_{T}+a_{11}\vec{e}_{\mu_{\mathrm{B}}}\,.

(14)

Several remarks are in order.
First, when the Landau’s potential (
4
) has higher order terms of
\bar{\phi}
, such as
\bar{\phi}^{6}
, the FSS relations Eqs. (
7
) and (
10
) are violated due to these terms. However, as shown in Appendix
A
the scaling exponents corresponding to these terms are negative, and hence they behave as irrelevant operators whose effects are suppressed for

) correspond to the mean-field values obtained for the Landau potential. Note that a similar form to Eq. (

) arises in the mean-field approximation of the Ising model, formulated in terms of a continuous magnetization field in leading order

. When the effects beyond the mean-field approximation are incorporated, it is known that the exponents are modified to the values specific to the universality class. For example, in the three-dimensional Ising model, the values are known as
y_{\tau}\simeq 1.5874
and
y_{\eta}\simeq 2.4819

63
;
18
.
Finally, in the above argument, it is implicitly assumed that
\mathcal{U}(\bar{\phi})
does not have
L
dependence. However, at finite volume, the momenta become discrete owing to boundary conditions, and the momentum integrals are replaced by a sum over discrete modes, introducing

\mathcal{U}(\bar{\phi})

II.3
Lee-Yang zeros and Lee-Yang edge singularity

The partition function of a finite system generally has zero points in the complex-parameter plane. We refer to the zeros of Eq. (
5
) in the complex
\eta

\eta_{\rm LY}^{(n)}(\tau,L)\in\mathbb{C}
, with real

as the Lee-Yang zeros (LYZs)

\displaystyle\mathcal{Z}_{\rm Landau}\big(\tau,\eta_{\rm LY}^{(n)}(\tau,L),L\big)=0\,,

where

\eta=\eta_{\rm LY}^{(n)}(\tau,L)^{*}
on the lower-half plane. Because of Eq. (
7
), by defining the zeros of
\tilde{\mathcal{Z}}
as
\tilde{\mathcal{Z}}\big(\tilde{\tau},\tilde{\eta}_{\rm LY}^{(n)}(\tilde{\tau})\big)=0
, the LYZs satisfy

\displaystyle\eta_{\rm LY}^{(n)}(\tau,L)=L^{-y_{\eta}}\tilde{\eta}_{\rm LY}^{(n)}(L^{y_{\tau}}\tau)\,,

(16)

provided that the LYZs do not belong to
e^{-L^{d}u_{0}}
, which is empirically justified near the CP for small

;
74
. In accordance with the Lee-Yang’s theorem
78
, Eq. (
16
) is pure imaginary.

For
\tau<0
, reflecting the first-order phase transition at
\eta=0
the distribution of LYZs around this point becomes denser and the density diverges for
L\to\infty
. On the other hand, for
\tau>0

, which are called the Lee-Yang edge singularity (LYES).
The LYES are obtained by solving the coupled equations
54
;
79

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathcal{U}(\bar{\phi})}{\partial\bar{\phi}}=0\,,\qquad\frac{\partial^{2}\mathcal{U}(\bar{\phi})}{\partial\bar{\phi}^{2}}=0\,.

(17)

Notice that the solutions of Eq. (
17
) are found for complex values of
\bar{\phi}
and
\eta
for
\tau>0
. Since
\mathcal{U}
is complex in general in this case, Eq. (
17
) is a coupled set of four equations for the respective real and imaginary parts. For
\tau<0
, the solutions are found for real
\bar{\phi}
and
\eta
, which correspond to the spinodal points.

For Eq. (
4
), the solutions of Eq. (
17
) are found to be

\displaystyle\eta_{\rm ES}(\tau)=\pm iz_{\rm ES}\tau^{3/2}\,,

\displaystyle z_{\rm ES}=\frac{2}{3}\sqrt{\frac{2}{b}}\,.

(18)

In the general system described by Eq. (
11
), Eq. (
17
) gives

\displaystyle(a_{21}\delta T+a_{22}\delta\mu_{\rm B})^{2}=-z_{\rm ES}^{2}(a_{11}\delta T+a_{12}\delta\mu_{\rm B})^{3}\,.

(19)

Solving Eq. (
19

\delta T

\delta\mu_{\rm B}=0
at
\delta T=0
are expanded as

\displaystyle\mu_{\rm ES}(T)

\displaystyle=\mu_{\rm CP}-\frac{a_{21}}{a_{22}}\delta T\pm iz_{\rm ES}\frac{(\det A)^{3/2}}{a_{22}^{5/2}}\delta T^{3/2}

\displaystyle\phantom{=}+\mathcal{O}(\delta T^{2})\,.

(20)

From Eq. (
20
), one finds that the real part of
\mu_{\rm ES}(T)
with real
T
passes through
(T_{\rm CP},\mu_{\rm CP})
and extend along the direction parallel to
\vec{e}_{\tau}
in Eq. (
14

\delta T

II.4
Intersection analysis of the critical point

Since the thermodynamics of a finite-size system does not exhibit any singular behavior, locating the CP from the information of finite-size systems is a non-trivial task. In Refs.
73
;
74
, methods to utilize the LYZs for this purpose have been proposed.
Near the CP, one can Taylor expand
\tilde{\eta}_{\rm LY}^{(n)}(\tilde{\tau})
as

\displaystyle\tilde{\eta}_{\rm LY}^{(n)}(\tilde{\tau})=i(X_{n}+Y_{n}\tilde{\tau}+\cdots)\,,

(21)

with
X_{n}
and
Y_{n}
being real numbers. Substituting it into Eq. (
16
) one obtains

\displaystyle L^{y_{\eta}}{\rm Im}\,\eta_{\rm LY}^{(n)}(\tau,L)=X_{n}+Y_{n}L^{y_{\tau}}\tau+\mathcal{O}(\tau^{2})\,.

(22)

Equation (
22
) shows that
L^{y_{\eta}}{\rm Im}\,\eta_{\rm LY}^{(n)}(\tau,L)
for different values of
L
intersect at the CP,
\tau=0
, which in turn means that the CP can be determined as the intersection point of Eq. (
22
) if the value of
y_{\eta}
is known. Similarly, one finds from Eq. (
22
) that the ratios of the LYZs behave as

\displaystyle R_{nm}(\tau,L)

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{\eta_{\rm LY}^{(n)}(\tau,L)}{\eta_{\rm LY}^{(m)}(\tau,L)}

\displaystyle=r_{nm}+c_{nm}L^{y_{\tau}}\tau+\mathcal{O}(\tau^{2})\,,

(23)

with
r_{nm}=X_{n}/X_{m}
,
c_{nm}=r_{nm}\left(Y_{n}/X_{n}-Y_{m}/X_{m}\right)
. Therefore, the intersection point of Eq. (
23
) is also used for determining the location of the CP. Notable characteristics of Eq. (
23
) compared with Eq. (
22
) are, (1) the intersection analysis can be performed without knowing the value of
y_{\eta}
, and (2) the value of
R_{nm}(\tau,L)
at the intersection point,

, is a universal constant specific to the universality class
73
. From the

R_{nm}(\tau,L)
around
\tau=0
, one can also examine the value of the critical exponent

.

Another conventional quantity for the intersection analysis is the so-called (fourth-order) Binder cumulant

,

\displaystyle B_{4}(\tau,L)\equiv\frac{\langle\bar{\phi}^{4}\rangle_{\rm c}}{\langle\bar{\phi}^{2}\rangle_{\rm c}^{2}}\bigg|_{\eta=0}+3=\frac{V}{T}\frac{\partial_{\eta}^{4}f}{(\partial_{\eta}^{2}f)^{2}}\bigg|_{\eta=0}+3\,,

where

\displaystyle B_{4}(\tau,L)=b_{4}+c_{4}L^{y_{t}}\tau+\mathcal{O}(\tau^{2})\,,

(25)

which again indicates that this quantity obtained on various

The values of

First,

are obtained by finding the solutions of

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}d\bar{\phi}\,e^{-(\bar{\phi}^{4}-\eta\bar{\phi})}=0\,,

\displaystyle r_{21}

\displaystyle\simeq 1.9645,

\displaystyle r_{31}

\displaystyle\simeq 2.7902,

\displaystyle b_{4}=\frac{\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}d\bar{\phi}\,\bar{\phi}^{4}e^{-\bar{\phi}^{4}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}d\bar{\phi}\,e^{-\bar{\phi}^{4}}}{(\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}d\bar{\phi}\,\bar{\phi}^{2}e^{-\bar{\phi}^{4}})^{2}}\simeq 2.1884.

In the vicinity of the critical point in general systems, by combining Eq. (

From Eq. (

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{nm}(T,L)

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{{\rm Im}\,\mu_{\rm LY}^{(n)}(T,L)}{{\rm Im}\,\mu_{\rm LY}^{(m)}(T,L)}

\displaystyle=\big(r_{nm}+C_{nm}L^{y_{\tau}}\delta T+\mathcal{O}(\delta T^{2})\big)

III
Model and Phase Diagram

To investigate finite-size effects on the analytic structure of the partition function and the distribution of LYZs in the complex
\mu_{\rm B}
plane in QCD, we employ the single-component three-color (
N_{\rm c}=3
) quark-meson model in three space dimensions (
d=3
)
46
, whose Lagrangian density is given by

\mathcal{L}=\frac{1}{2}\partial_{\mu}\phi\partial^{\mu}\phi-U_{\mathrm{cl}}(\phi)+\bar{\psi}\big(i\gamma^{\mu}\partial_{\mu}-\gamma_{0}\mu_{q}-g\phi\big)\psi\,,

(36)

with
\psi
and
\phi
denoting the quark and mesonic fields, respectively, and the classical potential

U_{\mathrm{cl}}(\phi)=\frac{m^{2}}{2}\phi^{2}+\frac{\lambda}{4}\phi^{4}-h\phi\,.

(37)

Here
\mu_{q}
is the quark chemical potential, related to the baryon chemical potential by
\mu_{\mathrm{B}}=3\mu_{q}
.

Taking the mesonic field constant,
\phi(x)=\bar{\phi}
, and performing the integral over the fermionic field, the fermionic contribution to the effective potential in the thermodynamic limit is given by the sum of the vacuum and matter parts as

\displaystyle U_{\bar{q}q}(\bar{\phi},T,\mu_{\rm B})

\displaystyle=U_{\bar{q}q}^{\mathrm{vac}}(\bar{\phi})+U_{\bar{q}q}^{\mathrm{mat}}(\bar{\phi},T,\mu_{\rm B})\,,

(38)

\displaystyle U_{\bar{q}q}^{\mathrm{vac}}(\bar{\phi})

\displaystyle=-2N_{\rm c}\int\frac{d^{3}p}{(2\pi)^{3}}E(p)\,,

(39)

\displaystyle U_{\bar{q}q}^{\mathrm{mat}}(\bar{\phi};T,\mu_{\rm B})

\displaystyle=-2N_{\rm c}T\int\frac{d^{3}p}{(2\pi)^{3}}\left(\log g^{+}+\log g^{-}\right)\,,

(40)

with
E(p)=\sqrt{p^{2}+g^{2}\bar{\phi}^{2}}
and

\displaystyle g^{\pm}=1+e^{-(E(p)\mp\mu_{q})/T}\,.

(41)

In the present study, we keep only the classical potential and fermionic matter part, so that the effective potential reduces to
46

\mathcal{U}(\bar{\phi};T,\mu_{\mathrm{B}})=U_{\mathrm{cl}}(\bar{\phi})+U_{\bar{q}q}^{\mathrm{mat}}(\bar{\phi};T,\mu_{\mathrm{B}})\,.

(42)

It is assumed that vacuum effects are effectively encoded in the parameters of
U_{\rm cl}(\bar{\phi})
. Neglecting the vacuum term removes the
-\bar{\phi}^{4}\log\bar{\phi}^{2}
contribution, which would dominate the large-
\bar{\phi}
behavior and make the potential unbounded from below.

IV
Lee-Yang zeros and edge singularity

In this section, we discuss the behavior of the LYZs and LYES in our model. For the numerical determination of LYZs, we introduce the normalized partition function

\displaystyle\mathcal{Z}_{\rm N}(T,\mu_{\textrm{B}},L)\equiv\frac{\mathcal{Z}(T,\mu_{\textrm{B}},L)}{\mathcal{Z}(T,\mathrm{Re}\,\mu_{\textrm{B}},L)}\,,

(47)

and solve the simultaneous equations

\displaystyle{\rm Re}\,\mathcal{Z}_{\rm N}(T,\mu_{\textrm{B}},L)=0\,,

\displaystyle{\rm Im}\,\mathcal{Z}_{\rm N}(T,\mu_{\textrm{B}},L)=0\,,

(48)

for complex
\mu_{\textrm{B}}
with fixed
T
and
L
. Whereas the normalization in Eq. (
47
) does not alter the final result since the denominator is real and positive, it acts to improve numerical stability. To solve Eq. (
48
) numerically, we set an initial guess for each LYZ and search for the solution around it iteratively. For determining the initial values, we make the contour map of
|\mathcal{Z}_{\rm N}(T,\mu_{\textrm{B}},L)|
in the complex
\mu_{\textrm{B}}
plane and find approximate values of individual LYZs satisfying
|\mathcal{Z}_{\rm N}(T,\mu_{\textrm{B}},L)|=0
. For a search for LYZs at a continuous range of
T
, we vary
T
with a small interval
\Delta T
and use the LYZs obtained at the previous
T
value for the initial guess of the next step. We also found that this procedure can be further improved by employing the initial guess obtained through a linear extrapolation using two previous
T
values.
The LYES are determined by solving Eq. (
17
) for
T>T_{\rm CP}
for complex
\bar{\phi}
and
\mu_{\rm B}
.

VI
Conclusion

We investigated the analytic structure of the partition function in an effective model with integration over the mean-field proposed in Ref.
46
. The integration over the spatially-uniform mode provides a minimal framework for studying both the Lee–Yang zeros (LYZs) at finite volume and the Lee–Yang edge singularity (LYES) in the thermodynamic limit in a simultaneous framework. To analyze the scaling behavior near the critical point (CP), the effective potential is mapped onto the mean-field Landau potential.

We found the
T
dependence of the LYES
\mu_{\rm ES}(T)
in the complex
\mu_{\mathrm{B}}
plane in our model is compatible with previous effective model results
54
;
76
;
79
. The LYES remains close to the real axis for
T\lesssim 150
MeV, beyond which
\imaginary\mu_{\mathrm{ES}}(T)
rapidly increases. While
\real\mu_{\mathrm{ES}}(T)
behaves consistently with the pseudo-critical line
\mu_{\chi_{2}}^{\rm pc}(T)
defined via the peak of
\chi_{2}
over a wide parameter range, a large deviation is observed for
\mu_{\mathrm{B}}\simeq 0
.
The disagreement is modified by employing the pseudo-critical line
\mu_{\rm ES}^{\rm pc}(T)
defiend through
\real\mu_{\mathrm{ES}}(T)^{2}
.

For fixed finite sizes, the location of the first LYZ
\mu_{\rm LY}^{(0)}(T,L)
is significantly deviated from the LYES for
L\lesssim 12
fm. In the thermodynamic limit, they approach the LYES in the crossover and the real axis in the first-order regions, respectively. Nevertheless, for sufficiently large sizes,
L\gtrsim 8
fm,
\real(\mu_{\mathrm{LY}}^{(1)}(T,L))^{2}
is insensitive to
T
. As a result,
\mu_{\rm LY}^{\rm pc}(T,L)
defined in Eq. (
52
) has a good agreement with
\mu_{\chi_{2}}^{\rm pc}(T)
in this
L
range.
We also studied the behavior of the LYZs with fixed
LT
for a comparison with lattice QCD results
6
;
19
;
1
.
In this case, the deviation of the LYZs from LYES increases at high temperatures due to the decreasing linear size. For small sizes
LT=2,4
, even the first LYZ remains far from the LYES. Its trajectory exhibits monotonically decreasing
\real\mu_{\rm LY}^{(1)}(T,L)
with increasing
T
, contrary to lattice results in Ref.
19
.

We also investigated several methods for locating the
L\to\infty
CP at finite sizes based on LYZs and susceptibilities
12
;
73
;
74
. We tested the intersection analyses based on the Lee-Yang zero ratio (LYZR), scaled single LYZ, and Binder cumulant methods. Each quantity gives a good estimate of the CP already at moderate
L
. A detailed analysis of their size dependence leads to two main conclusions. First, scaling corrections from irrelevant operators become significant for large
L
due to the presence of subleading odd-order contributions. This leads to a slower convergence of the intersection analysis for large sizes. Second, at very small sizes, the temperatures of the intersection points exhibit non-monotonic
L
dependence that is not described by finite-size scaling, similarly to those observed in generalized susceptibilities at real parameters
46
. Quantities based on higher LYZs are more sensitive to these effects.

Our approach with the integration over the spatially-uniform mode can be applied to other effective models with a mean-field approach.
Its simplicity also allows for the study of additional aspects of critical behavior. For instance, it can be used for investigating the analytic structure of the partition function in the vicinity of a tricritical point
53
, both at finite and infinite sizes. Such an analysis would be relevant for two opposite limits of QCD: the chiral limit, in connection with the chiral tricritical point, and the heavy-quark limit
70
;
69
, in connection with the tricritical structure of the Roberge–Weiss transition.

Finally, the present model framework lacks several aspects, including the
L
dependence of the potential due to the momentum discretization, deconfinement and the
\mathbb{Z}_{3}
center symmetry, and hence the Roberge–Weiss transition, of QCD, which motivates a systematic improvement of our approach.
In particular, our approach can be implemented with an extended effective action, as also suggested in Ref.
54
, allowing for a more comprehensive discussion of LYZs and the LYES. Restricting such an approach to a constant homogeneous background yields a constraint effective potential
33
;
34
;
55
, providing a numerically tractable theory. The inclusion of nonhomogeneous classical configurations may also be important for a complete qualitative and quantitative description
51
.
