Ebola virus disease
Authors: Maxime J. Billick, William K. Silverstein, Isaac I. Bogoch
Published: 2026

Since the discovery of Ebola virus disease (EVD) in 1976, outbreaks have occurred sporadically in Central and West Africa

The 3 main ebolaviruses associated with human disease are Zaire ebolavirus, Sudan ebolavirus, and Bundibugyo ebolavirus.1 Evidence suggests fruit bats are the reservoir species, and contact with bats or infected intermediate animals cause human infection.1–3 Ebolaviruses transmit person-to-person through bodily fluid contact (e.g., blood, vomit, diarrhea, semen) or contaminated fomites.1,3 The 2026 Democratic Republic of Congo EVD outbreak involves Bundibugyo ebolavirus, which has a case fatality rate of 30% to 50%.2,3

Although EVD has been dubbed a “hemorrhagic fever,” fewer than 50% of patients have hemorrhagic symptoms3

Typical symptoms include fever (≥ 38°C), fatigue, myalgias, and gastrointestinal symptoms (e.g., nausea, vomiting, diarrhea, abdominal pain).1–3 Disease progression may include a maculopapular rash, hepatopathy, coagulopathy, and occasionally hemorrhage.1–3 The incubation period of ebolaviruses is 2 to 21 days.2 Diagnosis is confirmed with blood polymerase chain reaction testing.3

Individuals with compatible symptoms and exposure risk should be tested for EVD

These include travel to EVD-affected areas within the disease’s incubation period; close contact with suspected infected people (alive or deceased) from EVD-affected areas; or close contact with bats, primates, or game from affected areas.4,5 Local public health units should be contacted if someone is symptom free but has relevant exposures.4

People with suspected infection should instigate stringent infection prevention and control (IPAC) practices4,5

A fit-tested N95 respirator, face shield, gloves, and fluid-impermeable gear (gown, apron, body coverings) are recommended.4,5 Local hospital IPAC should be notified immediately and an infectious diseases referral made.5 Health Canada outlines a detailed algorithm for screening, assessment, and IPAC precautions.4

Important advances in the prevention and management of Ebola caused by Zaire ebolavirus3 have been made

Vaccines against Zaire ebolavirus are available: Ervebo has demonstrated high effectiveness in outbreak settings, and Zabdeno–Mvabea is licensed based on safety, immunogenicity, and animal data.3 Monoclonal antibodies (Inmazeb, ansuvimab) reduce mortality from approximately 50% to 35%.3 These therapies are not tailored to Bundibugyo ebolavirus, for which supportive care remains the mainstay of treatment.3
