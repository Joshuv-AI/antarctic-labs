1 EXECUTIVE SUMMARY
1.1 Objectives
The University of North Dakota (UND), in collaboration with a comprehensive team of technical, business
and host-site partners, built on prior technology development to complete a front-end engineering and
design (FEED) and business planning study to recover and refine rare earth elements (REE) and critical
minerals (CM) from North Dakota (ND) lignite mine wastes. The end of project goal was to have an
investment quality project and a committed team ready to commercialize the proposed technologies in a
future construction and operations phase. To achieve this goal, the objectives of the project were as follows:
 1) Quantify the proposed project’s job benefits and evaluate how we will attract, train, and retain a
 qualified workforce.
 2) Identify specific diversity, equity, inclusion, and accessibility (DEIA) goals, targeted outcomes, and
 implementation strategies.
 3) Ensure that the project will provide meaningful benefits to disadvantaged communities and will not
 result in an increased burden to disadvantaged communities.
 4) Identify and implement methods for project stakeholder engagement.
 5) Develop all required permit applications and other regulatory approvals, including the
 Environmental Assessment information for NEPA review, by the end of Phase I.
 6) Develop an Association for the Advancement of Cost Engineering (AACE) Class 3 FEED study
 for the REE Demonstration Facility and satellite sites to advance the project to investment quality.
 7) Perform limited research and development (R&D) to de-risk certain technology subsystems and
 ensure the overall process functions smoothly in the integrated circuits.
 8) Develop the Phase II business and financing plans: Formalize the overall commercial structure
 (owners, operators, investors) and identify construction financing sources.
1.2 FEED Study & Cost Estimate
The project developed the FEED and cost estimate for three facilities located at three sites: 1) mixed rare
earth concentrate (MREC) production at Falkirk Mine (Underwood, ND), 2) MREC production at Center
Mine (Center, ND), and 3) REE separation and metallization facility located at the Rare Earth Salts (RES)
facility in Beatrice, NE. Figure 1 provides a simple summary of the technologies that were integrated in
the Phase I design and the major products.

Lignite mine wastes produced during the normal mining operations at each ND site would be transported

1.3 Technical and Economic Risk Mitigation
This Phase I project identified that the constraints of the legislative language for this program (BIL Sec.
40205) and requirements of DE-FOA-0002618 resulted in a project that is unlikely to be commercially
viable. The project team, therefore, elected not to pursue the second phase of funding under this program,
which would have been construction and operation of the facilities designed in Phase I. While Phase II was
not pursued, the Phase I project proved immensely valuable in identifying and mitigating technical and
economic risks associated with the team’s technologies and their integration.
R&D in Phase I involved successfully de-risking: 1) wastewater disposal via Underground Injection Control
Class I, 2) MREC process improvements, including leach solution recirculation and chemical use reduction
approaches, 3) commercial-scale ore analysis and sorting meant to separate the feedstock into fractions
based on the REE and critical minerals content, and 4) a novel REM production technique.
The Phase I work and FEED design also helped identify key economic pinch points and areas where further
R&D / piloting may be needed or may prove valuable, including: 1) ore processing and leached coal
dewatering equipment, 2) methods to significantly reduce equipment size of leach solution purification
steps, 3) control systems approach and tuning needs, and 4) Ge and Ga separations and purification.
1.4 Path to Commercialization
Leveraging learnings from this project, the project team’s plan going forward is to seek a “right-sized” first-
of-a-kind (FOAK) commercial project designed around a particular resource characterized by: 1) highest
ore grade, 2) most tonnage, and 3) lowest cost mining.
Based upon the FEED study and separate efforts, the current projection is an optimized FOAK project of
30,000-100,000 tons/year of lignite feed (20-50 times smaller than MREC facilities in this project). Costs
of such a plant are currently in development in separate efforts, but preliminary estimates show the potential
to provide positive annual cash flow. A project of this nature would involve the following key features:
 • Co-located with an existing, operational lignite mine and selecting only the highest-grade resources
 that can be produced within the mine’s existing operations (lowest-cost, essentially “free” mining).
 • Large enough to produce material market quantities of multiple critical mineral products, providing
 sufficient revenue to justify ongoing operating expenses.
 • Small enough and designed flexibly enough to serve as a continued R&D and product development
 platform, allowing the team to continue improving technology performance, reduce operating costs
 and add new or increase revenue streams.
 • Synching up the scale of the MREC processing plant with the downstream needs of a partially or
 fully integrated carbon products manufacturing process to valorize the upgraded lignite and support
 process economics at this smaller-scale. Near-term (built at the same time as the MREC plant)
 options include humic acid fertilizers and coal tar pitch.
The project team is ready now to immediately progress to this phase, pending the availability of federal and
state funding to support the capital costs of the facility.

refine REE and critical minerals (CM) from North Dakota (ND) lignite mine wastes. The end of project
goal was to have an investment quality project and a committed team ready to commercialize the proposed
technologies in a future construction and operations phase.
To achieve this goal, the objectives of the Phase I project were as follows:
 1) Quantify the proposed project’s job benefits and evaluate how we will attract, train, and retain a
 qualified workforce.
 2) Identify specific diversity, equity, inclusion, and accessibility (DEIA) goals, targeted outcomes, and
 implementation strategies.
 3) Ensure that the project will provide meaningful benefits to disadvantaged communities and will not
 result in an increased burden to disadvantaged communities.
 4) Identify and implement methods for project stakeholder engagement.
 5) Develop all required permit applications and other regulatory approvals, including the
 Environmental Assessment information for NEPA review, by the end of Phase I.
 6) Develop an Association for the Advancement of Cost Engineering (AACE) Class 3 FEED study
 for the REE Demonstration Facility and satellite sites to advance the project to investment quality.
 7) Perform limited research and development (R&D) to de-risk certain technology subsystems and
 ensure the overall process functions smoothly in the integrated circuits.
 8) Develop the Phase II business and financing plans: Formalize the overall commercial structure
 (owners, operators, investors) and identify construction financing sources.

3 FRONT-END ENGINEERING & DESIGN – AACE CLASS 3 COST ESTIMATE
The FEED study evaluated the development of a first-of-its-kind demonstration facility for the extraction
and concentration of rare earth elements (REEs) and critical minerals (CMs) from lignite mine wastes in
North Dakota, with downstream refining and metallization located in Nebraska.
The process begins with lignite mining at Underwood and Center, North Dakota, where REE-bearing lignite
mine wastes are recovered from existing coal mines. This waste will then be processed at the MREC
facilities in Underwood and Center. The Underwood facility will process approximately 2.5 million metric
tons of lignite waste per year at ~135 ppm REE, while the Center facility will process about 1.7 million
metric tons per year at ~114 ppm REE. Together, these plants will produce roughly 1 metric ton per day of
RE oxalates and carbonates in mixed-REE concentrate products at ≥75% purity. The overall average
recovery rate will be 53% of the REEs contained in the waste. Importantly, the mining and concentration
facilities will use standard, commercial-scale equipment and processes, improving reliability and
operational flexibility.
The mixed REE concentrate products will be shipped to the Rare Earth Salts refining, purification, and
metallization plant in Beatrice, Nebraska. There, final processing will convert the MREC into high-purity
REO (>99%) and selected REM, including scandium (Sc), didymium (mixture of neodymium (Nd) and
praseodymium (Pr)), terbium (Tb), and dysprosium (Dy). Overall recovery efficiencies are projected at
~53% from lignite feedstock to concentrates, followed by 90–95% efficiency during refining and
metallization.
Both the North Dakota and Nebraska facilities are designed to minimize environmental impacts. Air
emissions will be minimal, consisting primarily of dust and low-level combustion gases, and will be
controlled with baghouses and enclosed systems. For wastewater management, the MREC facilities will
utilize underground injection wells, while the RES facility will treat wastewater for onsite disposal. Solid
waste management will involve returning non-hazardous mineral tailings to existing mine disposal sites,
with other limited solid wastes sent off-site for disposal. All emissions and discharges at both facilities are
projected to remain below state permitting thresholds.

3.1.2 Feedstock Type, Composition Quantity and Availability
The feedstock for the Underwood and Center facilities will consist of lignite mine waste containing
extractable REEs. During active mining, the upper portion of coal seams are selectively removed to strip
away thin coal layers mixed with clays, exposing the clean, thicker seam below. These “cleanings” of lignite
are high in ash and not economical for use as thermal coal, and are therefore discarded back into the mining
pit.
At the Falkirk mine site, approximately 2.51 million metric tons per year of lignite mine waste will be
processed, with an average REE ore grade of 135 ppm. At the Center mine site, about 1.70 million metric
tons per year will be processed, with an average ore grade of 114 ppm. The composition of the various
REEs at each site is available in Appendix 1.

 9

The output of the concentrate plants will serve as feedstock for the downstream REE refining and
metallization facility. This feedstock will consist of REE carbonates with a purity of no less than 75wt%,
which will then be converted into REO and REM for sale.
3.1.3 Flexible Plant Performance Targets
The MREC concentrate facilities are designed with built-in flexibility to accommodate variations in REE
concentrations within the waste feedstock. Using analysis technologies developed by MTI, along with
commercially available sorting equipment, the facilities will analyze and separate incoming waste into three
categories: reject material, low grade, and high grade.
The MTI sorting algorithm, tested across a range of REE concentrations, has demonstrated accuracy within
tens of ppm of REEs. To ensure stable operations, facility operators will blend material from high- and low-
grade stockpiles as necessary, maintaining a consistent average REE throughput at both sites to prevent
process upsets.
3.1.4 Chemical Requirements
A range of chemicals will be required throughout the processing of lignite waste into REO and REM:
 • Mineral acids – primarily used in the leaching stage and during purification of rare earth carbonates
 before refining
 • Oxalic acid – employed as the precipitation agent to remove REE from the waste leachate
 • Sodium carbonate – used to precipitate contaminants and to regulate pH within process streams
The concentrate facilities will be equipped with extensive on-site storage to ensure a reliable chemical
supply. At the RES plant, an acid regeneration system will be implemented to minimize operating costs.
Further details on the chemical consumption rates will be provided in Appendix 1.

 3.1.5.1 Underwood, ND Site
The Underwood, ND MREC facility is located on host-site-partner–owned lands, situated north of existing
coal stockpiles used by the adjacent power facility. The primary feedstock consists of seam cleanings (coal
mixed with overburden at the tops of seams) and seam bottoms (coal with higher ash content than is
typically combusted), collectively referred to as lignite mine waste. Material is sourced from multiple mined
seams to ensure a consistent feed supply.
        3.1.5.2     Center, ND Site
The Center, ND MREC facility is also located on host-site-partner–owned lands, positioned northeast of
existing coal transfer points associated with the on-site power facility. Similar to the Underwood site, the
feedstock consists of lignite mine waste consisting of seam cleanings and seam bottoms taken from multiple
mined seams. Equipment and site characteristics are consistent with the Underwood facility, with two key
differences:
 • The Center facility has smaller stockpile capacities, with a live capacity of 4,700 tonnes for sorted
 feedstock and 13,350 tonnes for washed coal.

 12

 • Tailings management at Center is handled by slurry pumping, rather than by stockpiling and
 trucking to mine pits as done at Underwood.
 3.1.6 Rare Earth Refining, Purification, and Metallization
The process design for the Rare Earth Salts (RES) plant is based upon information provided by RES. The
facility will operate in a semi-batch mode, with solid dissolution and precipitation occurring in sequential
stages until the required concentrations and product specifications are achieved.
The major process steps include:
 • Pre-processing and preliminary separation
 • Primary separation and purification
 • Neodymium/Praseodymium (Nd/Pr) separation
 • Electrochemical separation
 • Metallization

3.2 Environmental Targets and Permitting
The project team prepared Environmental Assessment EA-2247 to evaluate the potential impacts of the
proposed REE demonstration facilities. In parallel, a permitting memorandum was developed to outline the
regulatory requirements for each site. Key findings from these studies are summarized below.
3.2.1 Lignite Mining
The lignite mine waste used for the concentrate facilities will be drawn from existing lignite operations that
already supply coal for power generation. No additional coal mining will be required. Because mine
operations will not change, no modifications to existing North Dakota Public Service Commission permits
or Mine Plans are expected.
3.2.2 Mixed Rare Earth Concentrate Production
 3.2.2.1 Air Emissions
Air emissions from the proposed facilities are expected to be minimal. The primary source will be
particulate matter, originating from coal dust and from granular solid chemicals used in plant operations.
High-efficiency fabric filter baghouses will be installed in material handling areas to control particulates,
and all conveying systems will be enclosed to minimize dust generation.
Airborne emissions from liquid processes are not anticipated, since all liquid handling will occur in sealed
tanks. Any vapor loss will consist primarily of water; other process chemicals have vapor pressures equal
to or lower than that of water (<103 Pa) at operational temperatures.
Emissions from calcination of the MREC concentrate have not yet been fully characterized. Based on the
targeted operating temperature (~500°C), the main emissions are expected to consist of carbon dioxide
(CO₂) and water vapor (H₂O). Analytical testing is planned in future project phases to confirm this
expectation. Projected levels are below North Dakota Department of Environmental Quality (NDDEQ)
thresholds; therefore, no air permitting is currently expected to be required.
 3.2.2.2 Water Emissions
The MREC facilities at Falkirk and Center will generate a barren waste solution at a rate of approximately
815 gallons per minute (gpm) and 530 gpm, respectively. Wastewater from both facilities will be disposed
of through Class I injection wells under the Underground Injection Control (UIC) program. Initial well
designs have been completed by WSP, with details available in Appendix 1, Appendix 9 and Appendix
10.

 13

 3.2.2.3 Solids Emissions
Both the Underwood and Center facilities will generate mineral soil tailings. In the process, lignite mine
waste will first be slurried, then processed through spiral concentrators to separate tailings from coal. A
thickened tailings slurry (~50 percent solids by weight) will be further dewatered to ~80 percent solids at
Underwood.
 • At Underwood, dewatered tailings may be hauled to either the Riverdale mine pit (7.5 miles south)
 or the Falkirk mine pit (6.6 miles north) for final disposal in the corresponding pit.
 • At Center, thickened tailings will be transported via pipeline along existing haul roads to existing
 BNI mine pits for impoundment.
Additional solid wastes will include water treatment precipitates and an iron-rich precipitate. The latter is
being evaluated for potential beneficial reuse. If no reuse pathway is identified, both iron products and
wastewater precipitates will be disposed of at licensed off-site facilities.
3.2.3 Rare Earth Refining, Purification, and Metallization
Wastewater containing ammonium and sodium chlorides, along with acid rinse solutions, will be treated
onsite before discharge. Treated effluent will be directed either to the local publicly owned treatment works
(POTW) under the Nebraska Pretreatment Program, or to surface waters under the National Pollutant
Discharge Elimination System (NPDES) program.
Air emissions from refining and purification are expected to be low due to the nature of the processes used.
Most of the process is aqueous, with dry material only handled at the front and back ends:
 • A dust collector will control emissions from a bulk bin unloader at the front end.
 • Electric furnaces at the back end will be equipped with baghouses for particulate control.
For metallization, airborne emissions will be treated using a gas-phase scrubber, potentially equipped to
recover hydrofluoric acid (HF) as a reusable byproduct. Wastewater will contain HF, fluoride ions, and trace
particulates/metals, and will be neutralized prior to discharge under either NPDES or pretreatment permits.
Solid wastes will include spent electrodes (graphite), waste molten salts, electrolytic slag, and possible
metal oxides and fluorides.

3.3.1 Plant Performance Targets and Requirements
The design meets the specific design criteria given in the FOA:
 • Production of one metric ton per day (MTPD) of 75% purity Mixed Rare Earth Oxalate and
 Carbonate (MREC) concentrate, including the elements Sc, Y, and the Lanthanide series within the
 75% purity. Ga and Ge were not considered part of the final purity target.

 • Total recovery of 53% of the above elements from the blended lignite mine waste feedstock into
 the MREC concentrate by acidic leaching of the lignite mine waste feedstock, removal of

 during roasting, and final concentration using a selective ion exchange process.
 • The facility will have a minimal environmental impact, by utilizing deep-well injection wastewater

 those in conventional REE processing.
 • Generation of an upgraded lignite product with reduced ash content relative to the lignite mine

 volatile REE/CM market pricing.
 • Extracting REE from lignite mine waste and producing REO and REM will occur across 5 separate
 facilities:
 o Lignite mining operations by Falkirk Mining Company (Underwood, ND) and BNI Coal
 Ltd. (Center, ND): source of REE-containing lignite mine waste feedstock
 o MREC production (located at each mining site): convert coal feedstock to rare earth oxalate
 and carbonate concentrate
 o Rare Earth Refining, Purification, and Metallization (operated by Rare Earth Salts): convert

3.3.3 Reliability and Flexibility
The MREC facilities emphasize operational simplicity, using straightforward equipment and uncomplicated
process controls. Primary control parameters include solution chemistry and slurry density. This design
approach enhances reliability and allows rapid adjustment to variations in REE feed concentration.
Two ongoing efforts are improving feedstock reliability:
 • North American Coal is developing a real-time truck-loading analysis system for surface mining.
 • MTI is advancing ore analysis and sorting technologies for continuous grade monitoring.
These systems will provide real-time tracking of REE and CM concentrations, improving recovery and
reducing dilution.
The RES process can handle feed variability of up to 20% and could be adapted into a closed-loop system
for greater process control.
3.3.4 Process Model for Combined Process
Mass and energy balances were developed from bench- and pilot-scale data on lignite feedstocks (projects
DE-FE0027006, DE-FE0031835).
 • Lignite coal handling modeled with METSIM™.
 • Concentration process modeled with ASPEN Plus.
 • Data supplemented with vendor quotes for major equipment.
Balances include water, REEs, and other plant materials, with energy estimates for kilns, coal handling, and
pumping/mixing. Water and REE balances account for leaching/precipitation losses. Wastewater is
managed by deep-well injection.
Chemical, water, and electric load balances may be found in Appendix 1.

3.5 Technology Gap Analysis
3.5.1 Current State-of-the-Art Technology Assessment
 3.5.1.1 Technologies for Mining of Lignite Mine Waste Coal-Based REE Resource
To this date no specific mining projects have been completed targeting lignite mine waste for REE
resources. The main cause for this lack of projects is missing information about the resource availability,
which has been rapidly increasing over the past few years as resource mapping has begun.
The technologies to mine the REE rich coal resources utilize the stand state-of-art mining techniques for
lignite surface mining along with sensors to ensure accurate mining of the resources.
 3.5.1.2 Technologies for Producing Rare Earth Concentrates
Current state-of-the-art rare earth production begins with the mining and extraction of rare earth ores, such
as monazite and bastnäsite, which contain REEs like lanthanum, cerium, neodymium, and praseodymium.
These extracted ores are then treated with acids (e.g., sulfuric or hydrochloric acid) to dissolve the REE,
producing a pregnant leach solution containing a mixture of REEs.
These traditional rare earth extraction methods often involve complex and environmentally damaging
processes such as solvent extraction, which has raised concerns about pollution and waste especially with
the management of the radioactive and toxic by-products.
Another drawback is susceptibility to process upsets caused by variations in the feedstock. Slight
differences in the concentration of REE and impurities can cause significant impacts to the extraction
performance, resulting in lower recovery efficiencies and the production of unaccounted wastes.
Furthermore, the ore resources identified in the United States have low concentrations of heavy REEs, such

 38

as Dy and Tb, which results in a need to fill those elemental requirements by overseas resources, such as
ion-adsorbed clays from China. Additionally, these materials are generally not enriched with other critical
elements such as Sc, Ge, and Ga, meaning they produce minimal or no byproduct revenues, increasing risk
exposure to REE pricing volatility.
 3.5.1.3 Technologies for Individually Separating Rare Earth Oxides
The separation of MREC into individual REO is a challenging process due to the chemical similarly of the
REE to each other. While solvent extraction remains the dominant method for REO separation, new
advancements such as ion exchange, electrochemical separation, and membrane filtration hold promise for
more sustainable, efficient, and environmentally friendly processes. However, challenges like chemical
handling, environmental impact, and scalability still need to be addressed for these methods to become
mainstream in large-scale production.
 3.5.1.4 Technologies for Producing Rare Earth Metals
State-of-the-art technologies and methodologies for producing REM from individually separated REO are
metallothermic reduction or molten salt electrolysis. The most common metallothermic reduction method
involves the use of high temperatures (2,600°F) HF gas and calcium metal. Molten salt electrolysis dissolves
the REE in a molten salt (LiCl or KCl) and then an electric current reduces REE ions to metal at the cathode.
3.5.2 Commercial Availability of Equipment
All the mining equipment is standard and commercially available. It is anticipated some additional loaders,
haul trucks and Wirtgen 4200 surface miners would need to be purchased.
The equipment for the concentrate facilities at Underwood and Center are all commercially available. For
all major equipment types, vendor data and quotes have been gathered during this study and may be found
in Appendix 1.
3.5.3 Overcoming the Shortcomings, Limitations, and Challenges
The proposed MREC facility is designed to process a resource uniquely enriched in elements typically
deficient in conventional mineral-based rare earth deposits—specifically heavy rare earth elements
(HREEs), scandium (Sc), germanium (Ge), and gallium (Ga). These elements represent underdeveloped
domestic markets, even when considering current and anticipated rare earth mining initiatives. As such, the
proposed facility would help reduce dependence on limited-supply, high-impact critical materials.
The MREC facilities use lignite mine waste with REE primarily associated in a non-mineralized form.
These REE are bound as ions within organic complexes. This organic association enables an efficient,
ambient-temperature leaching process using dilute acids, which achieves greater than 50% REE recovery
while minimizing co-extraction of impurities (less than 10% extraction of iron, aluminum, and actinides).
The resulting high selectivity significantly mitigates environmental risks—particularly those related to
radioactive elements, which remain in dilute, non-mobile forms within the coal matrix.
Given the expected variability in feedstock composition (both REE and impurity content), the plant design
incorporates semi-batch ion-exchange resin systems for final separation, as opposed to solvent extraction
methods, which are less tolerant of fluctuating concentrations. These resins are applied after initial impurity
removal and rare earth precipitation steps, ensuring stable operation despite feed variability and enabling
production of intermediate products with REE/CM purity levels exceeding 75%.
Furthermore, the facility will employ an electrochemical separation process to achieve individual rare earth
oxide (REO) production. This approach offers significantly higher separation factors compared to
traditional solvent extraction using organic extractants and demonstrates strong resilience to feedstock
variability—capable of accommodating up to 20% fluctuations in input composition with no adverse impact
on process performance.

3.6 Produced Materials Disposition Plan
3.6.1 Critical Mineral Offtakes
Products from the proposed commercial facility will be sold to domestic refineries, including dedicated
REE, Ge, and Ga refineries. The two MREC products will be shipped separately as carbonates to the REE
refinery, in order to preserve the Sc-enriched quality of the material. For the REE refinery (RES), offtake
agreements with domestic REE consumers are already in place, and lignite-derived materials would be used
to supplement the volumes under those contracts.
For Ge and Ga concentrate products, priority will be given to establishing domestic markets through the
refiners. If domestic placement cannot be guaranteed, the MREC facility will retain the ability to stockpile
these resources until suitable domestic offtake options are secured.
3.6.2 Coal Offtakes
For the leached coal materials, the current plan is to blend them into existing power station feedstocks. At
full utilization of the REE plant, the expected blend ratio would be approximately 10-15% for each facility
– a level considered feasible based on additional combustion testing, which has demonstrated favorable
results for power plant use.
In addition, some coal materials may be considered for carbon product applications, such as through
AmeriCarbon. However, these potential offtakes will require more detailed testing before commitments can
be finalized.
4 BUSINESS AND FINANCIAL PLANNING
4.1 Phase II Decision
In September 2024, the project team made the decision not to pursue the Phase II opportunity, which had
an application deadline of October 31, 2024. At the highest level, the project team was unwilling to risk
the relationship and political capital that would have been needed to raise the requisite cost share for Phase
II, given what had been learned up to that date in the project and the team’s view that requirements of the
BIL language and the original FOA forced design and commercial decisions that were not economically
optimal or practical for the team’s commercialization objectives.
This section provides a high-level discussion of the reasoning behind that decision, grouped into categories
of economics, schedule, and resource.

 42

4.1.1 Economic Risks
Based on requirements of the FOA, the Phase II project needed to produce 1-3 metric tonnes per day (mtpd)
of rare earth products with at least 75wt% purity, using only certain types of feedstocks (e.g. mines wastes
or other environmentally deleterious materials); purpose-mined feedstocks were not eligible. With the
technologies and economic/market conditions in their current state and considering what the team had
learned to date during Phase I and in ongoing separate efforts, these restrictions were deemed to result in a
very low possibility of a profitable facility.
Lignite mine waste resources have been discovered with substantially elevated REE/CM concentrations
(e.g., >300 ppm REE) from a number of lignite mines, but many other lignite mine wastes are far lower
(e.g. <100 ppm REE). To be able to supply sufficient feedstock to meet the 1-3 mtpd rare earth product
requirement, these lower quality feedstocks would have been needed, significantly reducing the overall
grade of the resource feeding a project, increasing capital costs, and reducing the project’s revenue potential
per ton of feed without a corresponding decrease in operating costs.
There are two methods to rectify this economic challenge, neither of which would have been allowable
under the BIL or FOA.
 1) Source more of the high-grade material: This would mean exploiting seams above or below the
 current mining horizon (e.g. seams that aren’t currently mined for thermal coal use) and as such
 would not be eligible feedstocks (they would be purpose-mined).
 2) Reducing the size of the facility to correspond only to the quantity of the highest-grade lignite
 mines wastes and optimizing for economic performance at less than the 1-3 mtpd requirement.
In addition to the above two methods, additional time and testing would be valuable to improve technology
efficiency / reduce costs (sorting, physical separations, MREC production, refining and metallization) and
additional time to mature and more closely integrate carbon products manufacturing technologies with
REE/CM production would substantially reduce economic risks. The Phase I / Phase II schedule did not
allow for this additional time.
4.1.2 Schedule Risks
The project team had significant doubt about the practicality of DOE’s planned Phase II schedule for this
project – 2 years of construction and 2 years of commissioning/operation. This aggressive schedule yields
important risks that we felt could not be mitigated sufficiently.
Program Income as Cost Share: The FOA did allow for program income (e.g., REE/CM commercial sales)
to be used as cost share, which would substantially derisk the nonfederal cost share requirement. However,
as the project team has come to learn during discussions with potential offtake partners, there are certain
steps that must be achieved prior to being able to execute guaranteed offtake agreements. Essentially, the
team does not expect to be able to generate program income until after the facilities have been fully
commissioned and able to generate products with consistent quality for some period of time thereafter. This
is also exacerbated by the fact that the sequence of operations (MREC, refining, metallization) means that
each upstream facility must be fully operational before the downstream facility can become fully
operational and generate acceptable product quality demonstrations. The team did not expect that this could
be achieved within the 4-year Phase II timeframe.
Detailed Engineering and Permitting: A FEED study (Phase I) only takes the engineering up to a level of
about 10-25% of construction readiness; detailed engineering is an essential component of building a large
industrial facility and would need to be factored into the Phase II schedule after long-lead equipment
procurement has begun. Additionally, there are certain permits (e.g. wastewater injection and EPA aquifer
exemption) that could take up to 18 months for agency approval. This means that the capital cost of the
facility would be nearly entirely spent and the facilities nearing construction completion prior to key
operating permits being ready. This is on top of persistent supply chain challenges and uncertainty. All told,

 43

the project team believed a 2-year construction period being was unlikely, and being forced into a
constricted schedule would have placed a high-degree of risk burden on private sector capital.
Commissioning: First-of-a-kind facility commissioning of this type should not be underestimated and the
project team expects this to take a considerable amount of time. As noted previously, the need to establish
off-take agreements likely after commissioning and early operation (to establish production rates and
qualities), creates high private sector partner risk of building a plant without the specific sale prices/volumes
known until operation has begun. As such, the project would have needed to assume zero program income
as cost share – as the income from the facility would be unlikely to be present within the 4-year DOE period
of performance (unless significant extensions were granted by DOE).
4.1.3 Resource Risks
While the project team performed considerable sampling, including drilling campaigns, and
characterization of the mine waste resources sufficient to be confident that these materials are enriched in
REE/CM, at the time of the Phase II decision, the team did not feel the resource was certain enough to
attract significant private capital. Additional resource characterization, which would not have been allowed
under Phase I or Phase II funding under this program, would have been needed to confidently move forward.
4.1.4 Conclusions
The project team’s primary goal is to successfully commercialize the team’s technologies and position them
for many commercial replications. As such, it’s important to be pragmatic with how the team approaches
Project #1 to make sure it aligns with this primary goal. The combination of the above risks and
uncertainties were deemed unacceptable, despite the upside of significant federal investment potential in a
Phase II project under this program. Rather than try to shoehorn a project into the constraints of the BIL
and FOA, the project team is aiming to optimize for economics of a first-of-a-kind commercial
demonstration by “right-sizing” the facilities, providing additional time to work through more de-risking,
and refining resource estimates/characterization. Even though the team did not pursue Phase II, the Phase I
project proved immensely valuable in better understanding the economic, technical and environmental
pinch points and in continuing to develop/scale the team’s respective technologies. The final technology
maturation plan (TMP), provided later in Section 8, describes the team’s current plans for the commercial
path forward.

4.4.1 REE Offtake Roadmap
RES is the obvious and planned customer for the MREC products derived from the ND concentrating
plant(s). Based on discussions with RES, all REE products (including Sc) generated from a 1-3 mtpd REE
project could be immediately sold into their existing sales network. Current products include cerium oxide,
lanthanum oxide, neodymium/praseodymium mixed metal oxide/salts and terbium oxide. Regarding the
Ce, La and NdPr oxides, RES' expected capacity is expected to be in the hundreds of tons per annum in the
future based on current projects under development. The products specified in this FEED study represent
approximately 10% of RES' total annual production into the future.
RES is currently scaling its lamp phosphor recycling operation, targeting 12 tons/year of terbium oxide
production. The current lignite project is expected to produce 500 kg terbium oxide per year. This quantity
would represent around 5% of RES’ annual production and would also be easily incorporated into current
offtake agreements.
Finally, regarding the dysprosium and scandium oxides, RES' vendors for the terbium production are also
interested in these two products with requested volumes far exceeding the production volumes from this
project. These elements are expected to be easily absorbed by RES' current vendor network as both elements
represent significant demand requirements far exceeding RES's internal terbium production volumes of 12
tons per annum.
RES has proposed an offtake agreement structure based on a revenue sharing model between the MREC
production plant owner(s) and RES. Under this model, the MREC plant would receive a percentage of the
total separated value of the RES oxide/metal/alloy products. The specific percentage negotiated would be
based on attempting to find the sweet spot where both entities can operate profitably and share in the value
equitably.
RES has indicated the actual timing of getting to an executed offtake agreement for the MREC is not likely
until after the MREC commercial facility has been commissioned and has been operational and
demonstrating in-spec product quality for some period of time. Prior to that, it is possible to use UND pilot
data, with subsequent MREC processing at RES’ facilities, to demonstrate enough technical product quality
information to get to a letter of intent stage. However, this would require additional piloting at UND, with
the specific commercial resource, producing tens of kilograms of high-quality MREC products from the
specific feedstock of interest for evaluation by RES.
4.4.2 Critical Minerals (Ge and Ga) Offtake Roadmap
While the Ge and Ga offtake customers are less certain than RES for the MREC and RES’ existing
customers for the REO/REM, the project team has already identified and had preliminary discussions with
multiple potential customers of both Ge and Ga products. The level of purity/refining needed on the Ge and
Ga products depends on the customer and their in-house refining capabilities. It is assumed that at least 5N
(99.999%) purity will be needed to attract downstream customers (e.g., semiconductor wafer
manufacturers, infrared lens manufactures). It is possible that commercial refining methods could be

 48

developed and integrated into the Ge and Ga separation process. This would result in the sale of high purity
Ge and Ga directly to downstream customers. It may also possible to market lower purity Ge/Ga products
to midstream refiners, should they be identified, but currently that is not anticipated.
In the more likely scenario that the project sells directly to downstream customers, a negotiated long-term
(multi-year) offtake agreement is the most likely approach. As with the MREC and REO/REM products, it
is expected that an executed long-term offtake will not be possible until after the plant is operational and

upon pilot data from UND and Microbeam. Microbeam is currently executing on a separate DOE-funded
project that is demonstrating their technology to separate high purity Ge and Ga products from UND’s
MREC. That project has letters of support from two downstream customers that are interested in purchasing
Ge and Ga once the technology has demonstrated the ability to produce high purity Ge and Ga products.
4.4.3 Upgraded Lignite Offtake Roadmap
This analysis considers two categories of offtake customers, the first being the co-located lignite fired power
plants who would purchase the upgraded lignite as a premium low-alkali blending fuel, and the second
being co-located carbon products manufacturers, such as AmeriCarbon Products, who is interested in
purchasing this product to manufacture EcoPitch™ and derivative products such as synthetic graphite.
Depending on the scale of the REE processing plant (and the lignite mass processed), it is possible or even
likely that multiple customers of the upgraded lignite will be incorporated. For example, carbon products
such as EcoPitch™ are not likely to be large enough to require the full volume of upgraded lignite, meaning
that the power plant or other customers would need to purchase the remaining mass.
 4.4.3.1 Power Plant Customers
One of the potential hang-ups identified early in the Phase I project was the existing coal supply agreements
between the team’s mining partners and utility partners. However, based on discussions with the parties,
there does not appear to be any major hurdles to working within or around these existing agreements.
The primary efforts needed to get to executed offtake agreements are additional piloting and
combustion/emissions testing:
Additional Piloting: While the Phase I project did process several tons of each of the lignite mine wastes
through UND’s MREC pilot plant, one key limitation of the effort was that the coal spirals system was not
operational and that the MREC plant’s coal water washing system (downstream of the coal leaching system)
is not fully optimized. This resulted in a higher ash (dilution from overburden and incompletely flushed
pore water) upgraded lignite product than is expected from a fully operational pilot plant. With these
systems operational and optimized, the ash yield of the upgraded lignite produced from the lignite mine
wastes would be expected to be 10-20wt%, while the actual products produced in Phase I piloting were
more than double that amount. This means the ash produced from combustion is higher, but importantly it
also means the ash composition is substantially different than would be anticipated; UND’s MREC process
targets the organically associated ash and does little to remove mineral phases that are rich in the
overburden/clays contained in the lignite mines wastes.
Additional Combustion/Emissions Testing: Phase I did include pilot combustion testing using the processed
upgraded lignites from each of the two ND mine sites. However, as noted above, the composition of the
upgraded lignites was not what would be expected of an operating commercial system. While the findings
in Phase I generally confirmed the improved combustion/fouling performance of the upgraded lignite over
the baseline run-of-mine coals, the team believes that additional combustion testing is needed to secure
offtake agreements. This would involve operations of UND’s MREC pilot plant with site specific
lignite/lignite mine wastes, with an operational spirals and with a fully optimized coal washing system
implemented. Additional testing at EERC’s pilot combustion systems is expected to raise the confidence
level in the fuel performance and emissions profile to a level sufficient for a letter of intent with utility
partners. However, based upon discussions with utility partners, we expect to need a commercial blending

 49

run (e.g., several weeks at the power plant with a specified blending ratio) after the commercial plant is up
and operational prior to being able to reach a fully executed agreement or incorporate the upgraded lignite
as an in-spec fuel within the existing coal supply agreements.
 4.4.3.2 Carbon Products Manufacturing Customers
UND has long-held the belief that the highest-value and best application of the unique upgraded lignite is
for carbon products applications that can take advantage of it’s unique near-zero organic ash (<0.5wt%
organically associated ash). AmeriCarbon’s coal tar pitch manufacturing process, EcoPitch™, is one such
application, especially when targeting high purity markets such as synthetic graphite for lithium-ion battery
(LIB) anodes. AmeriCarbon is on a similar commercialization timeline as UND’s MREC technology, and
therefore, this analysis provides a roadmap for reaching an executed offtake agreement with AmeriCarbon.
This is based on input from and discussions with AmeriCarbon, who was a subrecipient on this Phase I
project. The following provides a summary of key features/milestones of a roadmap to getting to an
executed offtake agreement with AmeriCarbon.
Introduction: The following sections outline AmeriCarbon’s requirements and planning considerations
related to the long-term purchase of carbon-rich material produced after the extraction of REEs and ash.
The material would serve as a feedstock for AmeriCarbon’s Liquid Carbon Pitch (LCP) process, enabling
the production of Eco-Pitch™ and other carbon-based products. The information provided below is
intended as a planning framework to guide future negotiations and is not intended to constitute a binding
contract.
Material Specifications: AmeriCarbon requires that the carbon-rich material provided following REE and
ash extraction meet certain baseline quality standards to ensure suitability for processing through the LCP
system and the production of Eco-Pitch™ and other carbon-based products. The following preliminary
specifications are necessary for AmeriCarbon to validate and ultimately enter into a long-term supply
agreement:
 • Fixed Carbon Content. The material should have a fixed carbon content of a minimum acceptable
 level. Higher carbon content improves yield and quality in the LCP process.
 • Ash Content. Residual ash content must be kept to a maximum acceptable level to prevent
 contamination during pitch production and to maintain process efficiency. Some exception may be
 possible depending on the nature of the ash.
 • Moisture Content. Material should be delivered with a moisture content below 10% (weight
 basis). If higher, drying processes may need to be incorporated upstream prior to delivery.
 • Sulfur Content. Sulfur must be below 1.0% (dry basis) to meet end-use product specifications for
 battery-grade and specialty carbon materials.
 • Other Impurities. Heavy metals and other non-carbon contaminants must be minimized to
 acceptable levels, particularly those that could impact carbon purity or downstream customer
 applications.
 • Particle Size. While AmeriCarbon’s pilot facility can accommodate a range of feed sizes, an initial
 nominal particle size of 1-inch minus is preferred down to 250 mesh. Further size optimization may
 be determined during pilot validation.
 • Physical Form. The material must be free-flowing bulk solid material, without excessive fines or
 slurry conditions, to facilitate efficient handling, transportation, and feed into the LCP process.
These specifications are preliminary and may be refined during the pilot testing phase. The ability to
consistently meet or exceed these specifications will be a fundamental requirement before AmeriCarbon
can commit to a long-term supply arrangement.
Supply Tonnage: AmeriCarbon anticipates an initial baseline requirement of approximately 30,000 tons per

 50

year of carbon-rich material at full commercial operation. This volume reflects the feedstock input
necessary to support a commercial-scale LCP plant for the production of Eco-Pitch™ and other carbon-
based products. It is also possible to accommodate smaller volumes of the REE by-product carbon if co-
fed lignite coal is available.
Key considerations for supply volumes include:
 • Ramp-Up Phase. During early project stages, AmeriCarbon expects to require smaller quantities
 of material for pilot testing and validation purposes. This would likely involve shipments in the
 range of 1 to 100 tons per batch during pilot trials.
 • Commercial Operations. Upon successful validation and commissioning of AmeriCarbon’s full-
 scale facility, annual supply volumes would ramp up to approximately 30,000 tons per year. Firm
 minimum purchase commitments would be determined as part of the final offtake agreement.
 • Flexibility. AmeriCarbon anticipates needing some flexibility in supply volumes, particularly
 during the initial ramp-up period. Commercial agreements may incorporate minimum purchase
 obligations with provisions for volume adjustments based on plant performance and customer
 demand.
 • Delivery Rates. While specific delivery schedules will be determined based on plant design and
 logistics planning, AmeriCarbon generally expects a steady flow of material, with monthly or bi-
 monthly shipments sized to match continuous plant operations and inventory management needs.
Meeting these volume expectations, along with consistent quality, will be essential to the technical and
economic success of the LCP-based Eco-Pitch™ production.
Testing and Validation Requirements: Before AmeriCarbon can enter into a long-term offtake agreement, it
will be necessary to complete a structured program of testing and validation using the carbon-rich material
provided after REE and ash extraction. This will ensure that the material meets performance, processability,
and economic criteria essential for commercial operations.
The testing and validation process will involve the following steps:
 • Sample Provision: AmeriCarbon will require multiple representative bulk samples of the carbon-
 rich material, preferably in quantities of 1 to 100 tons per sample, to conduct meaningful pilot-scale
 conversion trials.
 • Pilot Plant Processing: AmeriCarbon will process the supplied material through its 10-ton/day
 LCP pilot plant to evaluate:
 o Conversion yields (percentage of feedstock converted into pitch)
 o Processing characteristics (e.g., handling, flowability, reaction behavior)
 o Impact of feedstock quality variations on product output
 • Product Testing: Eco-Pitch™ produced from the carbon-rich material will be analyzed for key
 properties, including:
 o Softening point
 o Carbon purity
 o Volatile content
 o Rheological (flow) properties
 o Suitability for downstream conversion into needle coke, synthetic graphite, and other
 advanced carbon products
 • Customer Validation: Eco-Pitch™ samples will be provided to AmeriCarbon’s existing and
 prospective customers for independent testing and validation against their material specifications
 and application needs.
 • Performance Criteria: Successful validation will require that the carbon-rich material consistently

 51

 produces pitch that meets AmeriCarbon’s internal product specifications and is acceptable to third-
 party customers for their intended uses.
 • Feedback and Iteration: If necessary, AmeriCarbon and project partners may conduct iterative
 adjustments (e.g., feedstock processing adjustments or blending) to optimize material suitability
 before finalizing commercial terms.
Completion of this testing and validation phase with positive results is a critical prerequisite to
AmeriCarbon committing to a long-term supply agreement.
Commercial Conditions Required Before Entering into Long-Term Agreement: In addition to successful
technical validation, AmeriCarbon must satisfy a number of commercial prerequisites before it can enter
into a long-term offtake agreement for the carbon-rich material. These conditions are essential to ensure the
overall viability and sustainability of the project.
Key conditions include:
 • Customer Offtake Agreements: AmeriCarbon must secure sufficient customer commitments or
 offtake agreements for the Eco-Pitch™ and related carbon products derived from the carbon-rich
 feedstock. Long-term customer demand is critical to underpin plant financing and operational
 sustainability.
 • Positive Economic Feasibility: The processing and conversion of the carbon-rich material must
 demonstrate favorable economic metrics through internal modeling and techno-economic analysis.
 This includes achieving acceptable margins on production costs versus product sales pricing.
 • Confirmation of Feedstock Quality and Consistency: AmeriCarbon must receive assurances,
 validated through testing and pilot-scale operations, that the quality and consistency of the carbon-
 rich material can be reliably maintained over time. Variations in feedstock quality could
 significantly impact product quality, plant performance, and customer acceptance.
 • Transportation and Logistics Plan: A cost-effective and reliable transportation and logistics plan
 must be in place for the delivery of material to AmeriCarbon’s processing facility. Delivery terms
 (e.g., FOB mine, FOB AmeriCarbon site) must be negotiated as part of the final offtake agreement.
 • Pricing and Adjustment Mechanisms:
 o Baseline Pricing: The final offtake agreement must include an agreed-upon pricing
 structure that reflects the value and processing cost of the material.
 o Adjustments: Mechanisms should be established to adjust pricing based on factors such
 as changes in feedstock quality (e.g., ash content) or external factors like inflation or
 changes in transportation costs.
 • Supply Commitment and Flexibility: AmeriCarbon will require minimum volume guarantees to

5 TECHNICAL AND ECONOMIC DE-RISKING
Five major technical and economic de-risking focus areas were pursued to strengthen the fidelity of the
FEED study, advance project-critical technologies to higher TRL levels, and identify opportunities to
improve the overall project economics by reducing risks and uncertainties. As described in Section 2.3.6,
these focus areas encompass upstream ore sorting, midstream MREC data collection and economic
optimization, and downstream metallization efforts
5.1 On-Belt Ore Analysis and Sorting
Previous work 6 demonstrated that the MREC process is relatively flexible with respect to variable feed
concentrations of REEs and impurities. However, a notable exception arises in the two-step REE
precipitation process, where instability in the first precipitation zone may occur if REE, Al, and Fe
concentrations fluctuate significantly. To address this, UND contracted MTI to accelerate development of
its on-belt ore analysis technology. The objective was to validate whether REE/CM concentrations could
be reliably measured in real time and to assess the feasibility of a sorting system capable of ensuring feed
concentrations remain within ±20% of the expected average.
This effort included a demonstration at the Milton R. Young Power Station (MRY). A proprietary algorithm
was employed to estimate REE concentrations from spectral analysis, which was then validated under
varying REE feed conditions using lignite and REE-enriched lignite mine wastes. The scope of work
involved:
 • Standardizing the MRY full-spectrum elemental analyzer (FSEA).
 • Installing and integrating the sorting algorithm.
 • Conducting stop-belt sampling and sample analysis.
 • Validating the ability to detect changes in REE content.
 • Assessing the capability of the FSEA to provide sorting information.
The FSEA system comprised two commercial analysis tools: a prompt gamma neutron activation analyzer
(PGNAA) with dual gamma attenuation (DGA). Further details on their operating principles are provided
in Appendix 4.
The developed sorting approach utilizes a software-based application that predicts REE-rich coal in real
time and sends sorting signals to potential belt-sorting technologies (e.g., flop-gates). The REE predictor
leverages PGNAA spectra and a trained neural network to estimate REE and CM contents. The intended
application would divide incoming feedstock into 3–4 categories:
 1. High-REE lignite mine waste feedstocks
 2. High-value, low-REE lignite mine waste feedstocks
 3. Low-REE, low-value lignite mine wastes for rejection to power plant
 4. High-clay materials, to be rejected to mine for disposal (optional depending on mining horizon and
 tolerance of power facility for clay-rich materials)
From these categories, high-REE and high-value feedstocks would be blended to produce a stable feedstock
with consistent REE and valuable element concentrations for the processing facility. Target thresholds were
defined as:
 • High-REE lignite: >200 ppm total REE (TREE) content.
 • High-value lignite: <200 ppm REE but >$60/tonne combined separated oxide value.
 • Low-REE, low-value lignite: <200 ppm REE and <$60/tonne combined separated oxide value.

5.2 Metallization De-Risking
To reduce costs and permitting risks associated with conventional fluoride-based REE electrolysis, RES
endeavored to develop a novel electrochemical reduction platform capable of recycling any vapors and slag
produced during the electrolysis process. This prototype testing was delayed due to extended equipment
delivery times, and experimentation begun in February, 2024. The furnace allowed for production of
anhydrous REE salts, produced from RES’s existing process, and was confirmed to yield over 99.9% purity
of separated anhydrous salts.
Metallization testing focused on utilizing an anhydrous didymium material produced by RES (separation
and conversion to reactive, anhydrous salt). Metal production in both the pure didymium and master alloy
materials were confirmed, although all tests showed significant impurity forms in both the form of

 58

unconverted REE salts, as well as infiltration of crucible materials. Two paths were then undertaken to
improve purities of metals produced – improving the reaction conversion of the salts to metals, and reducing
the possible introduction of crucible elements into the metal matrix. Various crucible materials were
evaluated for reducing potential infiltration into the metal melt, which was partially successful, while the
reaction extent was never fully completed due to unreacted material present in the mixture. The final purities
of didymium metal achieved during this project were >90% metal, but unable to reach the 99.9% necessary
per FOA requirements for the commercial facility.

5.3.2 Leached Coal Combustion Testing and Comparison with Run-of-Mine Material Blending
 5.3.2.1 Pilot Testing Systems Description
The Combustion Test Facility (CTF) and Particulate Test Combustor (PTC) at UND’s Energy &
Environmental Research Center (EERC) were used to evaluate the combustion performance of a 10%
washed coal blend under suspension firing and cyclone firing conditions, respectively. Tests were
performed on September 24, 2024 (CTF) and October 22, 2024 (PTC). The washed coal used in the study
was derived from the REE extraction process.
The CTF is used extensively to investigate SOx and NOx emissions and the transformation of toxic trace
metals (Hg, As, and Pb) during the combustion of coal and other fuels or waste materials. The CTF is
capable of producing gas and particulate samples that are representative of those produced in industrial-
and full-scale pulverized coal (pc)-fired boilers. The test facility has a number of pollution control devices
that may be used to reduce emissions, including either an electrostatic precipitator (ESP) or a fabric filter
baghouse for particulate control, a selective catalytic reduction column for NOx control, and a wet scrubber
(WS) for control of sulfur emissions. The CTF was designed to fire pc at a rate between 550,000 and
750,000 Btu/hr, depending upon desired operating conditions. Because the CTF furnace is refractory-lined,
the firing rate set point is based on the furnace exit gas temperature (FEGT) simulating a specific boiler of
a size and type commonly used in commercial operations at a coal-fired, steam-producing power plant. For
lignite coals and coal blends, the firing rate is usually adjusted to produce a FEGT in the 1900°–2000°F
range. A schematic of the CTF is shown in Figure 15. The ESP and WS with a lime slurry were used for
the CTF testing. The WS was not operated to simulate full-scale generation plant conditions. Instead this
was used to reduce sulfur emissions for EERC’s North Dakota state-issued permit compliance. Sulfur
capture in the WS is achieved by a combination of lime slurry concentration, packing used, slurry
recirculation rate and slurry replacement rate.
The PTC is a 550,000-Btu/hr pc-fired unit designed to generate fly ash representative of that produced in a
full-scale utility boiler. The PTC is capable of producing gas and particulate samples that are representative
of those produced in industrial- and full-scale pc-fired boilers. The test facility has a number of pollution
control devices that may be used to reduce emissions, including either an ESP or a fabric filter baghouse
for particulate control. The PTC was designed to fire pc at a rate between 550,000 and 750,000 Btu/hr,
depending upon desired operating conditions. The unit also has a cyclone-fired burner that can be used for
testing. Because the PTC furnace is refractory-lined, the firing rate set point is based on the FEGT
simulating a specific boiler of a size and type commonly used in commercial operations at a coal-fired,
steam-producing power plant. For lignite coals and coal blends, the firing rate is typically adjusted to
produce a FEGT near 1900°F. A schematic of the PTC is shown in Figure 16. The same sulfur control
devices utilized on the CTF were used for the PTC.

Combustion air in the PTC and CTF is provided by a forced-draft fan in these balanced-draft systems. The
induced-draft fan at the back of the system is used to maintain a slight vacuum in the combustion zone and
exhaust the combustion flue gases to a stack. Combustion air is typically preheated using an electric air
heater and is split between primary, secondary, and over-fire air (OFA). The OFA is introduced through a
port near the furnace exit typically at 15%–20% of the total combustion air.
Combustion gas analysis is provided by continuous emission monitors (CEMs) at the furnace exit, which
is used to monitor and maintain a specified excess air level for all test periods, and the outlet of the
particulate control device, which is used to assess any air in-leakage that may have occurred so that
emissions of interest sampled at the back end of the system can be corrected for the dilution caused by the
in-leakage. For this test series, flue gas analyses at the back end were obtained from the duct at the outlet
of the WS. Each CEM rack contains five modules, one each for the determination of O2, CO2, CO, SO2,
and NOx. Each of the analyzers uses a flue gas conditioner to remove moisture from the gas stream prior to
analysis.
The as-received North Dakota lignite samples (baseline coal and 10% washed coal blend) were inspected
for surface moisture and floor-dried as necessary prior to pulverization. The air-dried samples were crushed
to ¼-inch top size and fed to a hammer mill pulverizer, creating a size distribution of approximately 70

content, owing to the lack of physical separation conducted. Fuel samples were gathered from the feed
hopper to best approximate as-fired characteristics.

5.4 Wastewater Treatment De-Risking
During prior pre-FEED studies (89243320CFE000057-001), wastewater treatment was identified as a
major cost driver for the MREC facility. Evaporation and off-site solids disposal were projected to account
for more than 30% of total operating costs and over 20% of capital expenditures. Identifying a viable,
economic, and environmentally sound wastewater management strategy was therefore critical—not only to
reduce costs, but also to mitigate technical and regulatory risks associated with permitting.
To address this, UND contracted WSP to evaluate the feasibility of Class I injection of wastewater.
Operating Class I wells exist near the proposed host sites (Underwood and Center, ND) and significant
subsurface characterization data was available from the team’s MREC host site partners through their
ongoing investigations and development of CO2 geologic storage projects. Class I injection was considered
a strong candidate due to the expected process water quality (pending verification) and the favorable
geochemical conditions of the subsurface saline aquifer targeted for disposal.
The evaluation focused on four primary tasks:
 • Confirm non-hazardous classification: Evaluate wastewater from multiple discharge options
 against RCRA limits to ensure eligibility for Class I injection.
 • Geochemical modeling: Assess the long-term stability of the injectate within the host aquifer,
 specifically avoiding pore closure from precipitation or containment concerns.
 • Conceptual study of Class I wells: If technically feasible, develop a preliminary design for the
 required wells to support host site acceptance.
 • Integration into FEED: If the above tasks proved successful, develop an AACE Class 3 FEED
 study for the Class I injection wells and integrate with the Class 3 FEED studies for the MREC
 facilities at both sites.
Detailed analyses were performed on three wastewater samples: two from site-specific piloting and one
from an additional feedstock for broader evaluation. Each was analyzed for parameters relevant to
hazardous classification and for properties necessary to evaluate downhole performance (Table 37). All
three samples met the criteria for non-hazardous classification, allowing Class I injection to remain under
consideration. Data for these samples is provided in Appendix 1 and Appendix 9. The tests were conducted

anticipated host-site feedwaters. While significant differences are not expected, there remains a low-

5.5 Process Economic De-Risking
In addition to the technical de-risking measures described above, two major efforts were undertaken to
improve process economics and reduce financial risks for a potential commercial project. Both were aimed
at lowering chemical consumption costs, specifically by increasing effective REE concentrations in solution
and reducing reliance on the most expensive consumable – oxalic acid – through two distinct approaches.
5.5.1 MREC Leaching Circuit Recirculation
Leachate recirculation tests were carried out using UND’s pilot facility (see Section 5.3.1) with the objective
of achieving a 30% recirculation rate. In this approach, 30% of filtered leachate is reused as part of the
leaching fluid introduced to fresh coal, with steady-state conditions assumed after four days of operation.
Preliminary process modeling indicated that recirculation would:
 • Reduce reagent consumption (acid, base, oxalic acid, water, etc.) due to higher REE concentrations
 in solution.
 • Decrease REE recoveries slightly, as more REEs could be trapped within coal pore structures.
Although the pilot facility lacked a fully operational multi-stage coal washing system, limiting potential
benefits and increasing potential losses, the tests still demonstrated a positive impact on process economics.

 67

Key findings included:
 • REE concentrations in the leachate increased by 20–28% after one week of operation, reflecting
 the 30% recirculation target minus observed losses (Figure 20).
 • Process reagent usage decreased by 12–30% (Figure 21).
 • The reduction in reagent costs outweighed the minor REE recovery losses.
 • Additional cost savings are anticipated from smaller required liquid flowrates (reducing tank/pump
 sizing and maintenance).
 • Recovered REE product purities improved slightly due to higher relative REE concentrations
 compared to diluents.
Future testing will be required to identify the optimal recirculation ratio for economic performance. Larger-
scale piloting will be necessary, with at least one week per test condition to achieve stable concentrations.
This work would be best conducted alongside the planned multi-stage washing circuit at the pilot facility.
Additional analysis will also be required to track the fate of hazardous materials (U, Th, RCRA metals) in
recirculated streams.

5.5.2 Oxalate Usage Reduction Pathways
Oxalic acid was determined to be the highest per-unit-cost consumable at the facility. Two methods were
investigated to reduce oxalate usage:
 • Quasi-replacement – employing an alternative precipitation pathway.
 • Recycling – recovering oxalate from processing steps for reuse.
 5.5.2.1 Double-Sulfate Primary Precipitation
Following DOE project peer review discussions, UND evaluated the feasibility of an alternative REE
precipitation route based on the NaREE(SO₄)₂ double-sulfate complex. The concept involved introducing
this step immediately after leaching, potentially replacing the primary impurity removal stage.
Testing was performed on leachates from host site materials (average ~130 ppm REE, resulting in ~30–50
mg/L TREE in solution). Experiments evaluated recovery efficiency as a function of added Na₂SO₄
concentration at 60°C and pH 1.8 (Figure 22).
Three key challenges emerged:
 1. Very low REE concentrations in solution (mg/L range).
 2. High sodium sulfate concentrations required for effective precipitation.
 3. Poor recoveries for Ge, Ga, and Sc – the three most valuable non-REE products.
A preliminary economic analysis concluded that, under the tested conditions, this approach was infeasible
compared to the existing process. However, simulated tests with higher REE concentrations (from higher-
grade ores or extended recirculation per Section 5.5.1) achieved greater REE recoveries. While Ge recovery
remained poor, this pathway could be viable for ores with low Ge content but elevated REE concentrations.

Preliminary tests were also conducted to recover oxalate consumed during precipitation, focusing on the
lower-purity REE second precipitate produced in the UND MREC process. This second MREC precipitate

 • It contains approximately 84% of recyclable oxalate.

 potential.

achieved. In temperature-controlled slurry tests (1:10 solid/liquid ratio, 1M K₂CO₃ solution), reaction
completion occurred in ~2.5 hours at 80°C (Figure 23).

 Figure 23. pH analysis of metathesis of REE/Ca oxalate with K2CO3 solution.
Implementing this process is projected to:
 • Recycle up to 28% of the utilized oxalic acid into a basic, liquid solution (capable of then reducing
 associated base usage).
 • Enable a liquid concentration of oxalate ions up to ~1-2M. This solution, while not recovering the
 complete amount of oxalic acid needed for processing, could result in an economic benefit from
 the difference in pricing of reagents.
 • Enable a significant controllability improvement by allowing a liquid oxalate stream to be utilized
 for rapid control of critical process conditions (with solid being used either before or after during
 less concentration-critical actions). This could be envisioned in a tanks-in-series scenario, where
 solid and liquid oxalate feeds may be separated to aid in controllability based on total suspended
 solids.
Further testing could improve overall recovery and enable Ca separation (ideally in a form that can then be
separable from the liquid at a later point), enhancing REE purity and reducing costs.
5.5.3 Comparative Conclusions on Economic De-risking Approaches
The three economic de-risking pathways investigated – leachate recirculation, double-sulfate precipitation,
and metathesis processing – offer varying levels of technical maturity, cost benefit, and scalability:
 • Leachate Recirculation: Demonstrated the clearest near-term economic benefit, with 12–30%
 reductions in reagent usage and 20–28% higher REE concentrations in solution. Despite minor
 recovery losses, the net effect was positive, particularly when considering potential reductions in
 equipment sizing and operating costs. This pathway is the most ready for scale-up, pending
 integration with the multi-stage washing circuit and larger-scale piloting.
 • Double-Sulfate Precipitation: Technically feasible but economically uncompetitive under current
 conditions. Challenges included low REE concentrations, high sodium sulfate requirements, and
 poor Ge, Ga, and Sc recoveries. While niche applicability may exist for higher-grade feedstocks
 with elevated REE levels and low Ge, this pathway is unlikely to serve as a core strategy for MREC
 economics in the near term.
 • Metathesis Processing: Showed potential to recycle up to 28% of oxalic acid, while also offering

 70

 process control advantages through the generation of a liquid oxalate stream. Although not
 sufficient to fully offset oxalic acid demand, the method represents a moderately promising
 complementary pathway. Further testing to optimize Ca separation and validate long-term stability
 could enhance both cost savings and concentrate quality.
Leachate recirculation currently represents the most practical and impactful economic de-risking strategy,
with clear near-term benefits and relatively low implementation risk. Metathesis processing provides a
secondary, longer-term opportunity to further reduce oxalic acid costs and improve operational flexibility.
Double-sulfate precipitation, while of academic interest, does not presently offer a viable economic benefit
and should be considered only for select ore scenarios or as part of future process intensification efforts.

8 TECHNOLOGY MATURATION PLAN
8.1 Introduction
The project team provided an updated version of the original TMP (submitted with the Phase I application)
to the DOE in October 2023. The following sections provide an update to that version of the TMP and
incorporate findings and lessons-learned from the FEED and business planning studies completed in Phase
I, with a focus on the path towards the FOAK commercial facility and subsequent scaling/replication.
8.1.1 Technology Readiness Assessment System
The Phase I project studied the technology readiness assessment (TRA) system consisting of: 1) ore
processing, 2) MREC production, 3) REO separation, and 4) REM production. Section 3 contains complete
details of each of these four subsystems and how they are integrated, so this information is not repeated
here.
8.1.2 Commercial Application
The targeted commercial application of the TRA system is REE/CM recovery, refining and metallization.
Multiple products will be generated and sold to multiple customers. At the MREC plant locations, products
will include: 1) upgraded lignite material, which will be sold as blending fuel to the adjacent lignite-fired
power plants and/or as feedstock to carbon-based products manufacturing such as coal tar pitch; 2) MREC
products, which will be sold/transferred to the Nebraska-based REO separation facility; and 3) Ge/Ga
concentrates, which can either be sold as is or can be further processed using existing commercial
technologies to generate high purity Ge and Ga. The REO separation facility will generate individually
separated high purity REO or mixtures of select REO. The REE metallization facility will take select REO
and generate high purity REE metals or alloys to be sold for REE product manufacturing (e.g., REE
magnets).
8.2 Maturation of the TRA System
The goal of the limited de-risking R&D performed in Phase I (see Section 5) was, in part, to bring the
technology readiness level (TRL) of each of the technology subsystems to TRL 6. The following sections
provide a discussion of the current TRL of each of the major subsystems, incorporating the advancements
achieved in Phase I.
8.2.1 Ore Processing
Microbeam’s technology for ore sorting, based on novel algorithms integrated with commercial coal
analyzers, was considered TRL 5 at the beginning of Phase I. The work in Phase I involved a series of tests
on an existing full scale elemental analyzer located at project partner Minnkota’s Milton R. Young Station
power plant. This testing was considered a prototype application and demonstrated the real-time prediction
of REE-content in coals and a sorting method for separating coals based on their predicted REE/CM

 81

content. Based on the work completed in Phase I, TRL 6 has been achieved.
Other aspects of ore processing include standard crushing, milling, slurrying, and physical/density
separation. While the Phase I project included work with specific vendors to establish the most appropriate
equipment and configurations, each of these equipment/systems is fully commercial at TRL 9 today.
Overall, the TRL 6 goal for this TRA subsystem was achieved.
8.2.2 MREC Production
UND’s MREC production technology was considered TRL 6 prior to the Phase I project starting, based
upon prior pilot scale testing. Phase I work did not change the TRL of the MREC production technology.
The technologies/methods employed in the Phase I design for Ge and Ga separations were based on a
combination of Microbeam’s pyrometallurgical approach and known, commercial methods to achieving
high purity (readily salable) individual Ge and Ga products. The current state of Microbeam’s
pyrometallurgical Ge/Ga separations technology is TRL 4. Experiments conducted to date have utilized an
integrated, multi-component system with a relevant testing feedstock (produced rare earth oxalate
concentrate) at a low fidelity (i.e. not in a prototype form). Commercial pyrometallurgical processing of Ge
and Ga from various other ore streams is commercially deployed, although no known version utilizing a
MREC feedstock exists. The impacts of the pyrometallurgical process steps on the subsequent
processability of the residual MREC is as-of-yet untested, although it is currently planned for Q4 2025/Q1
2026 in ongoing work (DE-FE0032522). Phase changes have occurred with the MREC at elevated
temperatures (~850°C) under oxidative conditions that rendered a portion of the REEs challenging to
reprocess. 23 For financial purposes at commercial scale, all materials of this feedstock (both the volatile
Ge/Ga and non-volatile REEs) need to be recoverable in valuable forms (processible at higher purity). At
the completion of the ongoing separate DOE project (August 2027), the technology will have validated the
ability to separate Ge and Ga from the MREC feedstock at a bench-scale/small pilot-scale will be considered
TRL 5.
8.2.3 REO Separation
The RES electrochemical separation technology has been demonstrated in a near-commercial /
demonstration facility and is considered TRL 7-8.
8.2.4 REE Metallization
Phase I metallization de-risking efforts focused on evaluating non-fluoride pathways for rare earth
metallization. This focus stemmed from the significant cost, environmental, and safety risks associated with
conventional fluoride-based processes.
As part of this effort, RES developed a prototype electrochemical metallization cell designed to produce
rare earth metals without the use of fluoride salts. While the system demonstrated the capability to metallize
anhydrous REEs using RES-prepared pure salt materials, it was not able to consistently achieve salable-
grade purity. Two primary issues were identified:
 • Incomplete reactions in one type of electrochemical cell.
 • Infiltration of crucible/cell materials into the product in another design.
As a result, the technology was unable to produce metals at the required 99.9% purity threshold for
commercial applications.
In the near term, any commercial deployment would therefore continue to rely on the current state-of-the-
art fluoride-based electrolytic systems, which are commercially available and demonstrated at TRL 9. By
contrast, RES’s non-fluoride metallization technology is assessed at TRL 3-4, with further development

8.3 Post-Project Maturation and Commercialization
8.3.1 Phase I Learnings – Additional R&D and Technical/Economic De-risking
 8.3.1.1 Ore Processing
 8.3.1.1.1 Ore Analysis and Sorting
Phase I did provide a successful prototype demonstration of Microbeam’s ore sorting and blending
technology, confirming the ability to predict REE/CM concentration into high vs. low fractions, and
establishing a preliminary sorting/blending approach. However, to date there has still been insufficient
industrial-scale testing to fully validate the commercial viability of implementing this strategy into a future
commercial project. Additional, long-term data collection is needed to rigorously test and tune the
proprietary algorithms and refine sorting and blending methodologies, and would plan to be achieved during
construction/commissioning of a commercial MREC facility.
 8.3.1.1.2 Ore Physical Separation
While UND’s pilot facility does have a coal spirals system installed, it has yet to be commissioned and
operated. To date, the only available spiraling performance information has been derived from a 3rd party
testing company (Hazen Labs) on a bench-scale (batch) coal spirals, which showed the ability to effectively
separate the lignite into two or more clean density fractions (coal-rich, clay-rich, middlings). Phase I design
included a blade mill and spirals to affect the wanted density separation, based upon discussion with and
limited testing by commercial vendors. The engineering team is confident that this configuration will work
well at commercial scale, but additional testing is needed to establish more robust performance predictions.
 8.3.1.2 MREC Production
 8.3.1.2.1 Coal Dewatering
The filter presses installed at UND’s pilot have proven effective in dewatering the leached lignite from the
pregnant leach solution. However, filter presses represent a major cost factor for a commercial plant. In an
effort to mitigate this while still maintaining dewatering efficiency, the Phase I FEED design incorporated
two stages of dewatering (multi-stage water washing and water recycle), with the first stage using a less
expensive, but less efficient horizontal vacuum belt filter (HVBF), and the second stage using filter presses
which provide a final moisture content in the 30-35% range preferred/needed by the thermal power plant
customers.
For a future commercial project, the dewatering strategy should be designed around the final use of the
upgraded lignite product and what moisture requirements are needed. As much as 30% of the total CAPEX
is from physical separation and coal dewatering systems, offering considerable opportunities for cost
savings if those systems can be simplified or eliminated. For example, one market being considered by the
project team is producing humic acids, which is an aqueous process, and therefore would not require the
more stringent moisture target, allowing the less expensive HVBF systems to be used.
However, Phase I made assumptions around HVBF performance based on discussions with vendors, so
piloting and/or vendor testing is required to establish the HVBF performance, in terms of particle slippage
and final moisture.
 8.3.1.2.2 Iron Precipitation
UND’s pilot facility employs an ambient temperature iron precipitation process, but lab testing and
discussions with the engineering team have revealed that providing a higher temperature (less than boiling,
but higher than ambient temperature) results in dramatically faster particle settling and lower moisture
retention in the precipitate. This results in the ability to reduce the size of the iron particle clarifier and
reduce REE losses (to the retained moisture). UND’s pilot facility needs to be upgraded to incorporate a
heated iron precipitation tank and clarifier to validate this performance improvement at a larger scale.

 83

 8.3.1.2.3 Oxalate Precipitation Process
UND’s patented multi-stage oxalate precipitation process has proven effective during pilot testing in
generating two concentrates, one with high REE concentration, and one where Sc is concentrated into a
separate concentrate. However, pilot testing has also proved challenging from a controls perspective in
tuning this process. Additional piloting, combined with further controls methodology development (perhaps
incorporating AI/ML) would prove valuable in confirming the controls system design/costs.
 8.3.1.2.4 Ge/Ga Recovery from MREC during Roasting
Microbeam’s pyrometallurgical process for recovering and separating Ge and Ga from UND’s MREC is
still under development in separate efforts. While technical feasibility has been established and TRL 4 has
been achieved to date, further experimental testing and economic evaluations are needed to raise the TRL
to 6 and be ready for full commercialization. These efforts are continuing in ongoing separate efforts under
DE-FE0032522.
 8.3.1.2.5 Ion Exchange Purifications
Laboratory testing has identified specific resin compositions/types that can effectively upgrade UND’s
concentrate 1 or concentrate 2 to higher purities, as needed or desired by downstream refiners. However,
piloting is needed to verify performance at a larger scale.
 8.3.1.3 REO/REM Production
RES has performed limited testing to date on UND’s MREC within their electrochemical REO separation
process, but with successful results. However, to achieve executed agreements with offtakers, RES will
need to perform additional validation testing to demonstrate consistent quality of their REO products. This
will require additional piloting at UND to provide the necessary quantity of MREC feedstock.
As noted in Section 8.2.4, RES was able to make progress in demonstrating their novel REM production
approach, but there were some technical challenges that arose in Phase I that prevented them from achieving
the performance they were targeting. If this technology is to be incorporated into the FOAK lignite project,
then additional prototype work will be needed to overcome these technical challenges and achieve TRL 6.
If this cannot be done, then the fallback option would be to work with another REM technology provider
or deploy the commercial standard of fluoride electrolysis.
8.3.2 Commercialization Plan
As discussed in Section 4.1, the project team elected not to pursue the Phase II commercial opportunity
directly due to economic, schedule and resource risks. Perhaps the most impactful of these was the DOE’s
requirement of 1-3 mtpd of REE products as a minimum production quota. This resulted in a lignite mine
waste input that was 10 to 100 times higher than now appears to be economically optimal. Therefore, the
project team’s plan going forward is to seek a “right-sized” FOAK commercial project that will be designed
around a particular resource characterized by: 1) highest ore grade, 2) most tonnage, and 3) lowest cost
mining.
Based upon the FEED study and separate efforts, the current projection is an optimized FOAK project in
the range of 30,000-100,000 tons/year of lignite feed. Costs of such a plant are currently in development in
separate efforts, leveraging information developed as a part of this Phase I project, but preliminary estimates
show the potential to provide positive annual cash flow. A project of this nature would involve the following
key features:
 • Co-located with an existing, operational lignite mine and selecting only the highest-grade resources
 that can be produced within the mine’s existing operations (lowest-cost, essentially “free” mining).
 • Large enough to produce material market quantities of multiple critical mineral products, providing
 sufficient revenue to justify ongoing operating expenses.
 • Small enough and designed flexibly enough to serve as a continued R&D and product development

 84

 platform, allowing the team to continue improving technology performance, reduce operating costs,
 and add new or increased revenue streams:
 o Provide test beds to test new circuits, sensors, or controls methods leveraging AI/ML
 o Offer an ideal basis for digital twin development facilitating future scale-up, replication
 and process improvements
 o Provide intermediate streams for continued product/technology development related to
 Ge/Ga, synthetic graphite/graphene, and carbon building materials (among many other
 possibilities)
 o Other opportunities not yet identified.
 • Synching up the scale of the MREC processing plant with the downstream needs of a partially or
 fully integrated carbon products manufacturing process to valorize the upgraded lignite and support
 process economics at this smaller-scale. Near-term (built at the same time as the MREC plant)
 options include humic acid fertilizers and coal tar pitch.
 o New commercial circuits could be added later to incorporate advanced derivative products
 from either humic acids (or the residual humins) or coal tar pitch. For example, project
 team members are developing technologies to produce graphene materials from humic
 acids that are currently in the TRL 4-5 range and could be ready for commercialization
 within the next few years. Synthetic graphite from coal tar pitch is an even nearer-term
 option that could be ready for immediate commercialization with the base plant.
The project team is ready now to immediately progress to this phase, pending the availability of federal and
state funding to support the capital costs of the facility.
