A scalable plate reader-based protocol to measure mitochondrial respiration in embryonic hearts
Authors: Chiemela Ohanele, Jennifer Q Kwong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryMitochondrial function is central to embryonic development, yet its assessment in the embryonic heart is hampered by limited tissue availability. Here, we present a protocol to measure mitochondrial oxygen consumption rates directly from embryonic mouse hearts using a fluorescence-based plate reader assay. We describe steps for heart isolation, plate setup, and acquisition of signal profiles. We then detail procedures for normalization and rate conversion. This protocol enables real-time readings with a low input requirement of one heart.Subject areas: Cell Biology, Developmental biology, Metabolism, Model Organisms

Before you begin

This protocol was adapted from Cayman’s Oxygen Consumption assay to measure mitochondrial respiration from mouse embryonic hearts. While studies in mouse models of congenital heart disease have emphasized the supportive role of mitochondrial function and maturation in structural and functional cardiac development, emerging evidence suggests that mitochondrial defects may also play a causal role in congenital heart disease (CHD).1,2,3,4,5 Therefore, the ability to measure mitochondrial respiratory chain function by way of oxygen consumption during cardiac development is critical for assessing underlying disease physiology. Current methods to assess mitochondrial respiration in mouse heart tissue often utilize specialized respirometry equipment like the Seahorse Bioanalyzer, Oroboros-O2k, and Clark electrode systems.4,6,7 When applied to embryonic mouse hearts, these approaches require mitochondrial isolation, which demands greater tissue input than a single embryonic heart can provide. Consequently, embryos must be pooled, necessitating same-day genotyping prior to pooling, adding both time and logistical complexity to the workflow. Additionally, accessibility to specialized respirometry equipment may be limited. Therefore, scalable and more accessible approaches are required.

Innovation

The innovation presented in this protocol is that we have adapted a fluorescence-based oxygen consumption assay to enable measurements of mitochondrial respiration from a single mouse embryonic heart. In contrast to other methods, this plate reader-based approach offers a more practical and commonly available alternative for measuring mitochondrial respiration. The measurement of oxygen consumption is obtained through sequential reading of fluorescent lifetime, based on the oxygen-dependent quenching of the fluorescent probe. In the presence of oxygen, the excited state of the probe is quenched, reducing its fluorescent lifetime. As oxygen is consumed in the system, the probe remains in its excited state longer, thereby increasing fluorescent lifetime. This underlying chemistry makes such measurements of fluorescent lifetime specific to oxygen consumption and ultimately mitochondrial respiration. As this assay is designed for a 96-well plate format, mitochondrial respiration rates can be obtained from multiple hearts at a time, thus reducing procedure times. While this protocol presents a step-by-step guide on how to obtain respiration rates from wild-type embryonic day (E) 14.5 (E14.5) hearts, it can be further adapted to assess respiration rates from genetically modified models, embryonic hearts of different ages, or alternative systems with similar tissue requirements.

Step-by-step method details

Embryonic heart isolation

Timing: 25 min

To conduct the oxygen consumption assay on embryonic heart tissue, the embryonic heart must first be harvested. Here, embryonic heart isolation and preparation for fluorescent measurements is described. All animal experiments were conducted under protocols approved by Emory University’s Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee.

Oxygen consumption signal profile tracing

Timing: 15 min

This assay assesses oxygen consumption as a measure of relative fluorescent lifetime. In this step, the rate of oxygen consumption in embryonic hearts will be measured.

Expected outcomes

This protocol describes the steps required to measure mitochondrial respiration in the embryonic heart using a fluorescence-based plate reader. As previous studies have relied on assessing respiration at later stages in development or pooling embryonic hearts, limited by tissue quantity, here respiration is assessed from one heart as early as E14.5.4,6,7,10 The basal signal profile for E14.5 hearts in pyruvate supplemented stress media is shown below (Figure 5). Maximal oxygen consumption rates are achieved with the addition of FCCP, which serves as a mitochondrial uncoupler.11,12 The oxygen consumption rates under this condition should be elevated when compared to basal rates, as we have shown here (Figure 6). Mitochondrial respiration is inhibited by rotenone which blocks electron transfer of complex I, and potassium cyanide, a complex IV inhibitor that prevents oxygen utilization.13,14 If oxygen consumption is due to mitochondrial respiration, this rate should be equal to zero. Here, we demonstrate that signal profiles flatten and become negative after KCN exposure (Figure 7). Sample replicates reported in Figure 8 represent biological replicates obtained from individual embryonic hearts derived from both shared and independent litters. While respiration was assessed in healthy, wild type hearts, it can be assumed that basal and maximal respiration rates would be reduced in embryonic hearts with defects in mitochondrial function and these differences could be readily appreciated through the utilization of this plate reader assay.

Limitations

This fluorescence-based protocol can be generally adapted to most tissue types. However, the size and thickness of the tissue may limit diffusion of the phosphorescent oxygen probe solution throughout the sample. Diffusion limitations may become more pronounced when comparing hearts across developmental stages or disease models with substantial differences in cardiac size or morphology. To minimize this variability, the protocol should be performed using comparable amounts of starting tissue across experimental groups, allowing tissues to be flattened consistently as described in the procedure. While this approach helps improve uniformity in oxygen and compound diffusion, differences in tissue architecture may still influence OCR measurements and should be considered during data interpretation. Additionally, tissues analyzed by this assay must be fresh with intact mitochondria in order to obtain accurate signal profiles. After, obtaining signal profiles, OCR values are then normalized to protein concentrations. If protein cannot be isolated after sample analysis due to sample degradation or rapid protein degradation this would also lead to inaccurate results. Also, if using a plate reader that does not have dual-read TRF settings, OCR calculations will not be as accurate due to artifact interference and gain settings. Lastly, while we have assessed respiration rates on embryonic hearts with genetic variation, our protocol has yet to be validated to established platforms to improve cross-study comparability.8

The Signal to Blank ratio is not greater than or equal to 3.

Potential solution

•A Signal to Blank ratio less than 3 would suggest that the signal is not being detected. This can be circumvented by increasing the gain setting, adjusting the TR-F focal height, increasing the amount of phosphorescent oxygen probe added to each well (15 μl/well), or measuring fluorescence from the bottom of the well.

Problem 2

Lifetime signal profile is negative (Figure 9).

Representative lifetime signal trace with a negative slope

(N = 1 embryonic heart).

(Related to Oxygen consumption signal profile tracing step 3E).

Potential solution

•One cause of a negative lifetime signal profile could be an insufficient oil seal. The mineral oil helps create an oxygen-tight seal, ensuring that any observed decline in oxygen during the reaction reflects consumption within the system rather than external losses. When adding the mineral oil to each well, make sure to line the inner walls of the well circumferentially with mineral oil until it meets at the center of the well.

Problem 3

Relative fluorescence does not increase within the first two minutes of the fluorescent reading (Figure 10).

Representative lifetime signal trace with a delayed upward trend

(N = 1 embryonic heart).

(Related to Oxygen consumption signal profile tracing step 3E).

Potential solution

•The time to a positive fluorescence signal is dependent on the diffusion properties of the tissue. In thicker or larger tissues, this time may be longer as the fluorescent probe equilibrating over a larger volume when compared to thinner and smaller tissues. When working with tissues that are diffusion limited, work with smaller sections and gently flatten the tissue with the tip of the dissecting tissue forceps. Alternatively, the tissue can be minced to reduce the diffusion area, while maintaining tissue volume remains.
