The effect of short-term descent to low altitude in healthy residents at moderate altitude — a randomized crossover trial
Authors: Nadja Deflorin, Michael Furian, Jan Vontobel, David Niederseer, Tsogyal Daniela Latshang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractStudy objectivesWe examined the effect of a short-term descent to low altitude on sleep-disordered breathing (SDB) in healthy moderate altitude residents.MethodsForty-four healthy participants (50% females), median age 47 years, living > 1000 m (median (IQR) altitude of 1230 m (1130; 1560)), were included. Polysomnographies were carried out for two consecutive nights at the participants’ home elevations and for two nights at the sleep laboratory in Chur (590 m), Switzerland, after a 2-week washout period, in accordance with a randomized crossover design. The primary outcome was time spent under 90% oxygen saturation (T90). Important secondary outcomes were nocturnal oxygen saturation (SpO2), apnea–hypopnea index (AHI), and oxygen desaturation index (ODI). Additional outcomes were sleep stage-related parameters. The trial has been registered at clinicaltrials.gov (NCT05826808).ResultsMean ± SE T90 was 1 ± 2 min at 590 m and 5 ± 2 min at > 1000 m (mean difference of 4 min, 95% CI of 1 to 7, p = 0.005). At 590 m, the total AHI was 9.2 ± 1.6/h (2.3% with AHI > 30/h), ODI 6.0 ± 1.4/h, and SpO2 95.2 ± 0.2%. At > 1000 m, the total AHI was 14.2 ± 1.6/h (13.6% with AHI > 30/h, p = 0.025 vs. 590 m), ODI 10.0 ± 1.4/h, and SpO2 93.8 ± 0.2% (p < 0.001 all comparisons versus 590 m). Mean slow wave sleep on the second night at 590 m was 10.0 ± 1% and 8.0 ± 1% at > 1000 m (p = 0.001). In males, descent to low altitude showed a more significant effect on T90 (mean difference of 7 min, 95% CI of 1 to 12, p = 0.024) and REM sleep stage (mean difference of 4%TST, 95% CI of 1 to 7, p = 0.011) compared to females.ConclusionA short-term, low-altitude stay of healthy moderate altitude residents leads to a reduction in nocturnal hypoxemia and SDB, especially in males. These findings suggest that sleep-disordered breathing assessments at lower-than-living altitude might underestimate the severity of SDB in patients permanently living at moderate altitudes.Brief summaryStudy rationale: Several million people worldwide permanently live at moderate altitude. Due to the vast variety in heights worldwide, the altitude of the closest sleep laboratory might vary and be at a lower altitude, which may lead to underestimated results. Study impact: To our knowledge, the current study is the first to investigate the effect of a short-term descent to low altitude on sleep-disordered breathing (SDB) in healthy moderate altitude residents. In this paper, we show that a short-term, low-altitude stay leads to a reduction in nocturnal hypoxemia and less sleep-associated breathing disorders, especially in males. Moreover, the proportion of severe SDB is underestimated at lower-than-living sleep laboratory assessments.

Introduction

Several million people worldwide permanently live at moderate or high altitudes [1]. Highlanders are prone to obstructive events and high-altitude living duration is a positive predictor of AHI and duration of obstructive events [2, 3]. Similarly to low-altitude populations, it can be assumed that a certain number of people living above 1000 m also have to go to a sleep laboratory. However, the altitude of the closest sleep laboratory might vary and might be at a lower altitude. A sleep examination in a lower-situated sleep laboratory rather than at the patient’s higher home elevations may lead to underestimated results, for example, of the severity of sleep apnea.

Therefore, the purpose of the current study was to perform a randomized trial evaluating the hypothesis that a short-term, low-altitude stay of healthy residents at moderate altitude leads to a reduction in nocturnal hypoxemia, sleep-associated breathing disturbances, and sleep disturbances compared to moderate altitude.

Methods

Study design and setting

A randomized, crossover trial was performed to evaluate the effect of short-term descent to 590 m in healthy residents at moderate altitude (median (IQR) altitude of 1230 m (1130; 1560)). The study was carried out from July 2023 to March 2024. Evaluations at lowland took place during two consecutive nights at the Cantonal Hospital of Grisons (590 m), Switzerland. Moderate altitude assessments were conducted at the participants’ homes, located > 1000 m. Assessments between low and moderate altitudes were separated by a 2-week washout phase at > 1000 m. The trial has been registered at clinicaltrials.gov (NCT05826808).

Participants

Participants were recruited via flyer and social media but also included a few colleagues and family members. Healthy males and females living > 1000 m (27 born at this altitude, 17 living for at least a year at this altitude), aged between 18 and 70, with a body mass index between 18.5 and 30 kg/m2 were included in the study. People with active diseases or health-related conditions that require treatment or were on regular medication, current smokers, regular alcohol consumers, or drug users were excluded. The study has been approved by the Cantonal Ethics Committee of Zurich (05–2023), and participants gave their written informed consent.

Randomization and interventions

Participants were randomized to groups A and B. Randomization was achieved with a computer-generated randomization sequence, using the python3 library random. Participants assigned to group A were first examined at 590 m, followed by examinations at their home elevations (> 1000 m). Participants assigned to group B were first examined at > 1000 m and then at 590 m. Participants immediately descended to 590 m before undergoing measurements at the sleep laboratory and spent the day between the two consecutive nights in the sleep laboratory at 590 m. They were instructed not to spend nights at < 1000 m in the previous 4 weeks before their study participation.

Measurements

Sleep studies

All sleep examinations were performed using portable polysomnography (SOMNOmedics GmbH, Randersacker, Germany) for 8 h each night. The polysomnographic recordings included electroencephalography (C3A2, C4A1, F1A2, F2A1, O1A2, O2A1), electrooculography, electromyography (submental and anterior tibialis), electrocardiography, pulse oximetry, nasal thermal airflow and pressure, thoraco-abdominal movements, and transcutaneous capnography (Sentec AG, Therwil, Switzerland). Sleep studies were analyzed in 30-s epochs by two evaluators in joint review according to the American Academy of Sleep Medicine criteria [4]. Briefly, apneas were defined as a reduction in airflow of more than 90% for at least 10 s. Obstructive apneas were differentiated from central apneas by rib cage and abdominal asynchrony. Mixed apneas were scored as obstructive events. Hypopneas were defined as an airflow reduction of 30% for at least 10 s and an oxygen desaturation of 3% or an arousal [5–8].

Clinical examination and questionnaires

Evening and morning evaluations included measurement of blood pressure, heart rate, and lung auscultation. Sleepiness was evaluated by the Karolinska Sleepiness Scale [9], Epworth Sleepiness Scale [10], and Stanford Sleepiness Scale [11]. Subjective sleep quality was rated on a visual analogue scale, marked with “very bad” at 0 mm and “excellent” at 100 mm. Subjective insomnia was evaluated by three questions (“How long did it take you to fall asleep?”, “How often did you wake up in the night?”, and “How long were you awake during the night?”).

Outcomes

The primary outcome was time spent under 90% oxygen saturation (T90). T90 has been shown to be a relevant parameter because it provides information about the duration and degree of hypoxia during the whole sleep. A previous study showed that a cut-off value of 12 min of T90 is an independent predictor of cardiovascular mortality in older males [12]. Important secondary outcomes were nocturnal oxygen saturation (SpO2), apnea–hypopnea index (AHI), and oxygen desaturation index (ODI). Additional outcomes were slow wave sleep (SWS), arousal index (AI), transcutaneous pCO2 (TcCO2), and wakefulness after sleep onset (WASO).

Sample size estimation

By assuming a clinically relevant difference in T90 of 12 min, a standard deviation of 24 min, a power of 80%, and a two-sided alpha level of 0.05, 34 participants are required. To account for dropouts, the recruiting aim was at least 40 participants (20 females, 20 males), who were then randomized into the two aforementioned groups.

Data analysis and statistics

An investigator blinded to the altitude allocation performed all statistical analyses. The data are summarized as means ± SD. The primary outcome was analyzed by intention-to-treat, with missing data replaced by multiple imputations (n = 20) using chained regression equations. Secondary outcomes were analyzed in the per-protocol population, which is defined as all participants completing all four nights. Mean values, SEs, mean differences, and 95% CIs were computed using mixed linear regression models with outcomes as dependent variables, altitude and sex fixed effects, and participants as random effects. To investigate predictors for T90 and ODI, exploratory mixed linear regression analyses were performed, using randomization sequence, age, BMI, sex, and altitude as predictors. All other analyses were performed unadjusted for baseline confounders. Statistical significance was assumed when the 95% CI of mean differences did not overlap with the value zero. Statistical analysis was performed by STATA version 15.1 (StataCorp).

Results

In total, 58 healthy participants were screened; of those 44, 22 (50%) males and 22 (50%) females met the inclusion criteria and were randomized. The participant study flow is illustrated in Fig. 1. The randomized participants had a mean ± SD age of 47.0 ± 13.0 years, body mass index of 23.5 ± 2.8 kg/m2, and were living at a median (IQR) altitude of 1230 m (1130; 1560) (Table 1). No serious adverse events requiring medical treatment or hospitalization were observed during the study.

The findings from the polysomnographies are summarized in Table 2. A total of 174 of 176 (98.9%) recordings were completed successfully, without data loss. In two cases, the recordings were lost due to technical failure, and the primary outcome was imputed accordingly. Compared to T90 measured at > 1000 m (night 1: 1 ± 0%, resp. 5 ± 2 min), T90 significantly decreased at 590 m (night 1: 0 ± 0%, resp. 1 ± 2 min, p = 0.003, resp. p = 0.005) during both nights. The mean altitude effect in T90 was 4 min (95% CI, 1 to 7 min). The nocturnal SpO2 was significantly increased at lower altitude during both nights (night 1 at 590 m: 95.2 ± 0.2%, night 1 at > 1000 m: 93.8 ± 0.2%, p < 0.001). During both nights at 590 m, the total AHI (night 1: 9.2 ± 1.6/h) and ODI (night 1: 6.0 ± 1.4/h) were significantly lower compared with the values at > 1000 m (AHI night 1: 14.2 ± 1.6/h, ODI night 1: 10.0 ± 1.4/h, p < 0.001 all instances). This was mainly because of the decreasing proportion of obstructive AHI at 590 m (night 1: 8.6 ± 1.4/h) compared to > 1000 m (night 1: 13.4 ± 1.4/h, p < 0.001), since there was no significant difference in central AHI at the two altitudes. TcCO2 was significantly increased during the first night at 590 m, mainly during NREM (night 1 at 590 m: 41.1 ± 0.5 mmHg, night 1 at > 1000 m 39.6 ± 0.5 mmHg, p = 0.013). SWS was significantly higher on the second night at 590 m (night 2: 10 ± 1% TST) compared to > 1000 m (night 2: 8 ± 1% TST, p = 0.001). Discussion

We performed a randomized, crossover trial to evaluate the effects of a short-term descent to low altitude in healthy males and females residing at moderate altitude staying two nights at the sleep laboratory at 590 m and two nights at their home elevations at > 1000 m. To our knowledge, the current study is the first to investigate the effect of short-term descent in healthy residents.

We demonstrated with our crossover trial that already a short-term stay at low altitude affects the diagnostic values of polysomnographies in healthy residents at moderate altitude.

A reduction in nocturnal hypoxemia and in AHI could be seen. The observed reduction in total AHI with lower altitude was mainly because of the decreasing proportion of obstructive AHI. Those findings are consistent with the findings in previous studies, which showed that highlanders are prone to obstructive events rather than periodic breathing since highlanders have a better tolerance to hypoxemia [2, 3]. It was seen that high-altitude living duration is a positive predictor of AHI and duration of obstructive events. An explanation for the higher obstructive AHI in highlanders was the larger neck circumference, tongue hypertrophy, and higher waist-hip ratio related to the high salt, meat, and fat intake. The longer apnea duration was explained by the better tolerance to hypoxemia by highlanders. The time needed to reach the arterial oxygen saturation threshold that triggers an arousal during an obstructive apnea is prolonged in highlanders. Second, an association between larger neck circumference and longer apnea duration has been seen. Furthermore, the larger lung volume (forced vital capacity (FVC) and forced expiratory volume in 1 s (FEV1)), seen in highlanders, results in a slower fall in SpO2 [2]. The obstructive events mainly occurred during REM sleep, where the ventilatory response to hypoxia and hypercapnia and the respiratory effort after upper airway occlusion decrease [13–15]. Similar to those findings, the neck circumference of the males from our study population was in the upper normal range and may be related to the high intake in salt, meat, and fat of the Swiss population, in particular in the Swiss Alps like the participants from our study population [16, 17].

Analysis on the severity of OSA at 590 m compared to > 1000 m showed a significant difference in AHI > 30/h in total participants and in males, indicating a potential clinical relevance. Therefore, when interpreting the results from polysomnographies, the height of the sleep laboratory and the height of the participants’ home elevations should be taken into account.

Short-term descent to lower altitude had minor consequences on the sleep architecture. SWS increased significantly at 590 m on the second night compared to moderate altitude, probably because of the reduction in AHI at 590 m, leading to less sleep disruption. Further, previous studies suggested that hypoxia is a reason for this disruption of sleep due to an induction of arousals by carotid chemoreceptor afferents and by directly altering the neuronal function and the control of sleep [6, 18–22]. SWS probably significantly increased only on the second night because of the first-night effect. The first-night effect is a reduction in sleep quality and quantity during the first night of PSG recordings due to discomfort caused by cables and electrodes necessary for the recordings and the unfamiliar surroundings of the sleep laboratory, as well as due to acute stress and anxiety, which are associated with a reduction in REM [23].

Exploratory analysis of sex differences in sleep-disordered breathing and sleep stages showed a significant difference in T90 and REM, which were both significantly higher in males. This indicates that females have better breathing regulation at moderate altitude than males.

A previous study examined acute exposure of healthy lowlanders to 3270 m, and showed that sex is an independent predictor for AHI at 3270 m [7]. An explanation may be the sex-related differences in the hypoxic ventilatory response, baseline AHI, and differences in upper airway anatomy. Males had a higher neck circumference, indicating more soft tissue. Males also had a lower CO2 reserve and higher apnea threshold, which may lead to more nocturnal breathing instabilities than females. They further described that an acute exposure to 3270 m induced higher blood pressure but lower acute mountain sickness and less severe cognitive impairments in males than females [7]. Another study showed different loop gain responses in males and females lowlanders at acute ascent to 3800 m, where healthy males acclimatized with an increase in loop gain instability, which manifested as an increase in CSA severity compared to females [24]. As previously described also, males in our study showed a neck circumference in the upper normal range as possible explanation for the more pronounced findings in males than females.

In conclusion, we demonstrated that a short-term descent to low altitude leads to a reduction in nocturnal hypoxemia, of sleep-associated breathing disorder, and to minor changes in the sleep architecture in healthy residents at moderate altitude. Due to its potential clinical relevance in patients with breathing disorders, it is recommended to carry out polysomnographies in patients living > 1000 m at their individual home elevations, especially in males, where the altitude impact on T90, REM, and on the severity of OSA was more pronounced than in females.

Some limitations of our study should be considered in its interpretation. It is uncertain whether the current trial’s results are also valid in patients with severe comorbidities or residing at higher altitudes. Further investigations are desirable to evaluate the consequences of descent to low altitude in patients with obstructive sleep apnea or other breathing disorders. Moreover, more studies investigating sex differences in sleep-disordered breathing and sleep stages at a descent to low altitude in residents at moderate altitude are desirable.
