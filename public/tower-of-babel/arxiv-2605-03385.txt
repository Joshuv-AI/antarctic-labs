Quantum Loewner evolution in quantum natural time: phases and Markov properties
Morris Ang Deven Mithal

Abstract
Quantum Loewner evolution (QLE) ( γ 2 , η ) (\gamma^{2},\eta) is a family of growth processes in random environments, introduced by Miller and Sheffield as candidate scaling limits of growth processes (such as diffusion-limited aggregation) on random planar maps. The random environments are Liouville quantum gravity (LQG) surfaces with parameter γ \gamma , and the parameter η \eta plays a role analogous to that in dielectric breakdown models. Their construction applies to pairs ( γ 2 , η ) (\gamma^{2},\eta) lying on a curve in parameter space, and the associated time parametrization is independent of the underlying LQG surface. In later work, they defined a quantum natural time variant of QLE ( 8 / 3 , 0 ) (8/3,0) whose time parametrization encodes a notion of distance in the LQG geometry, leading to the identification of 8 / 3 \sqrt{8/3} -LQG with the Brownian map. In this paper we construct quantum natural time QLE ( γ 2 , η ) (\gamma^{2},\eta) for a different but overlapping subset of the same parameter curve. Its time parametrization conjecturally corresponds to the scaling limit of time parametrizations of discrete growth processes on random planar maps. We prove that it exhibits three phases, mirroring those of Schramm–Loewner evolution (SLE); this answers a question of Miller and Sheffield for quantum natural time QLE. Moreover, we establish stationarity of the unexplored surface and, in the relevant phases, identify the random surfaces cut out or swallowed by the process as quantum disks. Our construction builds on recent radial LQG–SLE coupling results of Ang and Yu.

Introduction

A growth model is a random increasing sequence of clusters on an underlying graph
G
, which is frequently taken to be a lattice such as
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
. The study of growth models is a central topic in mathematical physics, as they model a wide variety of rich physical phenomena. For example, diffusion-limited aggregation (DLA)
45
;
46
models the growth of clusters formed by particles undergoing diffusion and attaching upon first contact. Such processes arise, for instance, in electrodeposition and the formation of coagulated aerosols. Another classical example is the Eden model
16
, in which particles are added uniformly at random to sites adjacent to the existing cluster, and which serves as a model for biological growth.
In this paper we are primarily concerned with the dielectric breakdown model (DBM)
33
, in which growth occurs at boundary sites with probability proportional to a power
\eta
of harmonic measure. The special cases
\eta=1
and
\eta=0
correspond to DLA and the Eden model respectively.

It is also natural to study growth processes in random environments, where the underlying graph
G
is itself random. In particular, we will take
G
to be a random planar map. This provides an ideal setting to study the fractal geometry of growth processes, since many kinds of random planar maps enjoy Markov properties when explored by a compatible growth process, endowing the coupling with an exact self-similar structure. For instance, a distinguished unexplored region may satisfy a stationarity property, and if there are multiple unexplored regions, they may be conditionally independent given their boundary lengths. This is the case for the Eden model on uniform random planar maps, and DLA on uniform-spanning-tree-weighted random planar maps
28
.

The breakthrough work of Miller and Sheffield
28
proposed a scaling limit of the
\eta
-DBM growth model on random planar maps. Random planar maps are expected (and in some cases proven) to converge to Liouville quantum gravity (LQG), a family of random continuum geometries indexed by a parameter
\gamma\in(0,2]
, each of which carries intrinsic notions of area and length.
28

(\gamma^{2},\eta)
on
\gamma
-LQG, and conjectured this to be the scaling limit of
\eta
-DBM on random planar maps in the
\gamma
-LQG universality class. Their construction only applies to some pairs
(\gamma^{2},\eta)
which lie on a curve in parameter space, see Figure
2
(right); this is due to a continuum analog of requiring the growth process to be compatible with the random environment. The random environment is a
\gamma
-LQG disk with an interior target point. For each
\delta>0
, they define an approximation of QLE
(\gamma^{2},\eta)
by repeatedly cutting the
\gamma
-LQG surface with a Schramm-Loewner evolution (SLE) curve grown from a random boundary point for time
\delta

\gamma

In this paper we construct a growth process called
quantum natural time QLE
(\gamma^{2},\eta)

(\gamma^{2},\eta)

This construction is novel for all parameters except

\eta

In Theorem

(

Finally, we show in Theorems

1.4

\gamma

We expect that quantum natural time QLE has substantial advantages over capacity time QLE in the study of scaling limits of growth processes on random planar maps. Indeed, its time parametrization should be the scaling limit of the discrete time parametrization, and its Markov properties mirror those of the Eden model on uniform random planar maps and DLA on uniform-spanning-tree-weighted random planar maps; see Section

1.1
Construction of quantum natural time QLE

Liouville quantum gravity (LQG) is a model of random surfaces originating from Polyakov’s seminal work on bosonic string theory
35

e^{\gamma\phi}(dx^{2}+dy^{2})

\gamma
-LQG area measure
\mathcal{A}^{\gamma}_{\phi}
on
\mathbb{D}
and the
\gamma
-LQG boundary length measure
\mathcal{L}^{\gamma}_{\phi}
on
\partial\mathbb{D}
; see Section
2.3
for details.
It is widely believed that
\gamma
-LQG describes the scaling limits of various kinds of random planar maps, and this has been proved in several cases for different notions of convergence. For instance, uniform-spanning-tree-decorated random planar maps converge in the peanosphere topology to SLE
κ
-loop-decorated
\gamma
-LQG where
(\gamma,\kappa)=(\sqrt{2},8)

42

(1.1)

1.3
Phases

In Theorem
1.3

(\gamma^{2},\eta)

We first recall the phases of

38

0<\kappa\leq 4
, SLE

is a simple curve. For the swallowing phase
4<\kappa<8

is a curve that intersects itself but has zero Lebesgue measure. For the space-filling phase
\kappa\geq 8

\mathrm{SLE}_{\kappa}
is a space-filling curve.
These three properties are formulated in terms of curves; we now state a variant better adapted to the setting of growth processes. For a radial SLE

\mathbb{D}
from 1 to 0, its hull at time
t
is the union of the curve at time
t
and all its complementary connected components not containing 0. Denote the hull process by
(K^{\mathrm{SLE}_{\kappa}}_{\cdot})_{[0,\infty)}
, and denote the two-dimensional Lebesgue measure by
\lambda
.

•

(Simple phase) For
0<\kappa\leq 4
, we have
\lambda(K^{\mathrm{SLE}_{\kappa}}_{t})=0
for all
t
a.s.

•

(Swallowing phase) For
4<\kappa<8
, a.s. the function
t\mapsto\lambda(K_{t})
is strictly increasing, has derivative zero almost everywhere, and

increases to
\mathbb{D}\backslash\{0\}
as

•

(Space-filling phase) For

1.5
Quantum zippers

The key result underpinning the construction of capacity time QLE in
28
is a radial variant of the
capacity quantum zipper
of
(
41
, Corollary 1.5)
, which goes as follows. Let
\gamma\in(0,2]
and
\kappa\in\{\gamma^{2},16/\gamma^{2}\}
. Let
\phi
be a certain variant of the free boundary GFF on
\mathbb{D}
and let
\eta
be an independent radial
\mathrm{SLE}_{\kappa}
curve from
1
to
0
. Assume
\eta
is parametrized according to capacity: if
g_{t}
is the conformal map sending the origin-containing connected component of
\mathbb{D}\backslash\eta([0,t])
to
\mathbb{D}
such that
g_{t}(0)=0
and
g_{t}(\eta(t))=1
, then
|g_{t}^{\prime}(0)|=e^{t}
. The stationarity property of the capacity quantum zipper is that, defining
\phi_{t}=g_{t}\bullet_{\gamma}\phi
, we have
\phi_{t}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\phi
when viewed as distributions modulo additive constant.

In order to construct quantum natural time QLE, we instead need a radial variant of the
length quantum zipper
of
(
41
, Theorem 1.8)
, in which one cuts along the SLE curve for
t
units of quantum natural time instead of capacity time, and obtains a stationarity property for the field
\phi_{t}
where, conditioned on the
\gamma
-LQG boundary length
L=\mathcal{L}^{\gamma}_{\phi_{t}}(\partial\mathbb{D})
, the conditional law of
\phi_{t}
is explicit and does not depend on
t
. We discuss these results in Section
3
. The zipper for
\kappa>8
was shown in
6
, and we prove the zippers for
\kappa
in
(0,4)
and
(4,8)
using symmetries of special
\gamma
-LQG surfaces called
quantum disks
and
generalized quantum disks
together with inputs from
5
which identify the conformal weldings of such objects to themselves. We note that the arguments are substantially different from those of
(
41
, Theorem 1.8)
. Indeed,
41
“zooms in” on a boundary point of a
\gamma
-LQG surface to produce an infinite-volume
\gamma
-LQG surface; the
\gamma
-LQG surfaces we study have finite volume and so cannot be obtained by this kind of limiting argument.

The stationarity property of the capacity quantum zipper only identifies the law of the field up to additive constant. By contrast, our quantum natural time quantum zipper views the field as a distribution (not just as a distribution modulo additive constant), so we can study the evolution of geometric notions such as
\gamma

.

1.6
Comparison with discrete models

A main novelty of the present work in comparison to
28
is the derivation of continuum Markov properties which should correspond to scaling limits of Markov properties of the discrete models. In this section, the random planar maps we consider are Boltzmann-weighted.

First, when a uniform-spanning-tree (UST) weighted random planar map has a DLA (
1
-DBM) process on it grown until it reaches its target point, cutting the edges comprising the DLA gives a random planar map with the disk topology such that, conditioned on its boundary length, it has the law of a UST-weighted random planar map
(
28
, Proposition 2.3 (iii))
. Since the scaling limit of UST-weighted random planar maps with the sphere and disk topologies is the quantum sphere and quantum disk with the LQG parameter
\gamma=\sqrt{2}

32
;
42
;
14
, the scaling limit of DLA on UST-weighted planar maps should yield a quantum sphere with a QLE
(2,1)
process, such that the complement of the DLA process should be a quantum disk given its boundary length.
This continuum Markov property is precisely what we show in Theorem
1.5
for QLE
(2,1)
, and more generally the analogous statement holds for all parameters in the dilute phase.

Second, when a uniform triangulation has an Eden model (
0
-DBM) grown on it for finitely many steps, with positive probability some regions are swallowed (separated from the target point) by the growth process; the joint law of these regions conditioned on their boundary lengths is that of conditionally independent uniform triangulations with the disk topology with specified boundary lengths. Results of this type are now standard for uniform random planar maps explored by peeling processes
7
;
13
, but see

(
28
, Proposition 2.2 (iii) and Figure 2.2)
for a justification for this particular setup. Arguing similarly as before, in the scaling limit this Markov property should correspond to Theorem
1.6
for QLE
(8/3,0)
. We note that for
(\gamma^{2},\eta)=(8/3,0)
Theorem
1.6
was already shown in
29
, but it is novel for other parameters.

In both settings of DLA on UST-weighted random planar maps and the Eden model on uniform triangulations, as explained in
(
28
, Proposition 2.3 (ii) and Proposition 2.2 (i))

1.4
.

In the rest of this section, we use the discrete models to motivate our construction of
\delta
-QLE via SLE, following the discussion of
(
28
, Section 2)
.
For the values
\eta=1
and
\eta=0
, the DBM model (in a compatible random environment) enjoys a simple relationship with a random curve. For example, the
0
-DBM process defined on a variant of random triangulation of the sphere can be viewed as a “reshuffling” of the so-called
percolation interface
curve. Both a curve and growth process on a graph define an increasing sequence of vertices (or dual vertices)
C_{t}
such that
|C_{t+1}\setminus C_{t}|=1
. In the
0
-DBM model, one lets
C_{t+1}\setminus C_{t}
be a dual vertex sampled according to the uniform measure on
\partial C_{t}
. For the percolation interface, an initial percolation configuration is chosen on the map, and
C_{t+1}\setminus C_{t}
is chosen on
\partial C_{t}
according to the interface dictated by that configuration. Remarkably, forgetting the location of the tip of
C_{t}
, the law of the unexplored region is identical for the
0
-DBM model and percolation interface. Stronger yet, the laws of the full processes of complementary connected components coincide, so the Markov properties are identical.

A key insight of
28
is that one can also define a growth process in the continuum by reshuffling a curve. Recall that SLE is a family of random fractal curves which arise as the scaling limit of discrete random curves arising in critical statistical mechanical systems; see
22
for an overview. LQG with independent SLE is believed (and in some cases proved) to be the scaling limit for various discrete random curves in discrete random environments. The quantum zipper (Section
1.5
) gives the continuum analog of the stationarity property discussed earlier. Thus, the continuum analogue of an
\eta
-DBM model on a planar map should heuristically be an object defined by a random curve (SLE) on a random environment (LQG) through a
continuum
reshuffling procedure, and this object should share the Markov properties of the associated curve. The reshuffling relationship between an
\eta
-DBM model and a random curve is currently limited to
\eta=0,1
; see
(
28
, Question 9.13)
.

2.2
Schramm-Loewner evolution and measure-driven Loewner evolution

Schramm-Loewner evolution is a family of random fractal curves that arise as scaling limits of interfaces in critical lattice models
39

43

21

44

\kappa>0

In this paper we will be exclusively concerned with the radial variant of SLE
κ

\eta:[0,\infty)\to\overline{\mathbb{D}}

\eta(0)=1

\lim_{t\to\infty}\eta(t)=0

K_{t}

\overline{\mathbb{D}}

\overline{\mathbb{D}}\backslash K_{t}

\overline{\mathbb{D}}\backslash\eta([0,t])

be the conformal map with
g_{t}(0)=0
and
g_{t}^{\prime}(0)>0
. The curve
\eta
is parametrized by capacity, meaning that
g_{t}^{\prime}(0)=e^{t}
for all
t\geq 0
.

Let

\Phi(u,z)=z\frac{u+z}{u-z}.

By the construction of radial SLE
κ
, the family of conformal maps
(g_{t})_{t\geq 0}
solves the radial Loewner equation

\dot{g}_{t}(z)=\Phi(U_{t},g_{t}(z))\quad\text{ for all }t>0\text{ and }z\in\mathbb{D}\backslash K_{t},

where
\dot{g}_{t}(z)
indicates the time-derivative at time
t
for fixed
z

U_{t}
is such that
U_{t}=\exp(i\sqrt{\kappa}B_{t})
with
B_{t}
a standard Brownian motion.
The function

taking values in
\partial\mathbb{D}
is called the driving function. It is the tip of the curve under the map
g_{t}
, and describes the point from which the process will grow.
For more details on radial SLE and other variants, see
22
.

The Loewner equation describes growth processes which, roughly speaking, grow from a single point prescribed by the driving function. We next discuss how to generalize this to the setting where the growth process may grow from more than one point at a time. The growth process will consist of a sequence of (random) sets
(K_{t})_{t\geq 0}
such that

is a compact subset of
\overline{\mathbb{D}}
and
\mathbb{D}\setminus K_{t}
is simply connected, for all
t\geq 0
. Such sets are called

, but we refer to them interchangeably as

in this paper.
For
T>0
, let
\mathcal{N}_{T}
be the collection of measures
\nu

Letting

In Section

Recall that SLE

\mathbb{D}

Definition 2.11
.

Let

and
\gamma=2

Next, we address the swallowing phase. Consider a radial SLE

Let

Remark 2.13
.

(
14
, Theorem 6.5.7)
shows that for a specific variant of the GFF

and for chordal SLE, the countable collection of lengths of swallowed regions, ordered by the time each region is swallowed, agrees in law with the ordered collection of jumps of a totally asymmetric stable Lévy process with index
\frac{4}{\gamma^{2}}

2.6
Topologies and metric spaces

In our argument, we will take subsequential limits of random variables taking values in the following metric spaces.

1.

(
\mathcal{D}
)
A function space on
\mathbb{D}
:

Let
a>0
, and consider the Hilbert space
(-\Delta)^{a}L^{2}(\mathbb{D})
which is the completion of
C_{0}^{\infty}(\mathbb{D})
with respect to the inner product
(f,g)_{a}:=((-\Delta)^{-a}f,(-\Delta)^{-a}g)
where
(\cdot,\cdot)
is the
L^{2}
inner product and
(-\Delta)^{a}f:=\sum_{n}(f,f_{n})(-\lambda_{n})^{a}f_{n}
where
\lambda_{n}
and
f_{n}
are eigenvalues and eigenvectors of the Laplacian on
\mathbb{D}
with Dirichlet boundary conditions. The Gaussian free field with Dirichlet boundary conditions is an element of
(-\Delta)^{a}L^{2}(\mathbb{D})
for any
a>0
; see
40
for details.

In this paper we consider the free boundary GFF, which we denote here by
\widetilde{h}
. By the domain Markov property,
\widetilde{h}
on
\mathbb{D}
restricted to
(1-\varepsilon)\mathbb{D}
can be written as
\widetilde{h}|_{(1-\varepsilon)\mathbb{D}}=h+\mathfrak{h}
where
h
is a Dirichlet GFF on
(1-\varepsilon)\mathbb{D}
and
\mathfrak{h}
is a function harmonic on
(1-\varepsilon)\mathbb{D}
. The distribution
\widetilde{h}|_{(1-\varepsilon)\mathbb{D}}
is therefore an element of
(-\Delta)^{a}L^{2}((1-\varepsilon)\mathbb{D})
which is defined in analogy to the preceding paragraph, and which we denote by
\mathcal{D}_{a}^{\varepsilon}
. The product space
\prod_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\mathcal{D}_{a}^{1/n}
equipped with the metric
d_{a}(\cdot,\cdot):=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}2^{-n}\min(d_{a,1/n}(\cdot,\cdot),1)
is separable and complete, and the subset
\mathcal{D}_{a}:=\{(h_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\in\prod_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\mathcal{D}_{a}^{1/n}:\,h_{m}|_{(1-1/n)\mathbb{D}}=h_{n},\,\forall m>n\}
is closed, and is therefore separable and complete in the subspace metric.

The free boundary GFF takes values in
\mathcal{D}_{a}
almost surely. The constant
a>0
is inconsequential for our arguments, and so we fix some arbitrary
a>0
and write
\mathcal{D}_{a}
as simply
\mathcal{D}
.

2.

(
\mathcal{N}
)
Infinite-time driving measures on
\partial\mathbb{D}
:
Recall that for each finite time
T>0
, we let
\mathcal{N}_{T}
be the space of measures on
[0,T]\times\partial\mathbb{D}
with Lebesgue marginal in the first coordinate, equipped with the Lévy-Prokhorov metric. Each
\mathcal{N}_{T}
thus defined is a compact metric space with metric denoted by
d_{T}
. Let
\mathcal{N}
be the collection of measures on
[0,\infty)\times\partial\mathbb{D}
having Lebesgue marginal in the first coordinate, and equip
\mathcal{N}
with the metric
d(\nu,\nu^{\prime})=\sum_{T\in\mathbb{N}}2^{-T}d_{T}(\nu|_{[0,T]\times\partial\mathbb{D}},\nu^{\prime}|_{[0,T]\times\partial\mathbb{D}})
. Then
(\mathcal{N},d)
is a compact metric space, and convergence in
d
is equivalent to convergence in the Lévy-Prokhorov metric on
[0,T]\times\partial\mathbb{D}
for every
T>0
.

3.

(
\mathcal{B}
)
Finite Borel measures on
\overline{\mathbb{D}}
:
We equip the space of finite Borel measures on
\overline{\mathbb{D}}
with the Lévy-Prokhorov metric. This is a complete separable metric space, and we equip it with the Borel
\sigma
-algebra.

4.

(
\mathcal{J}
)
Càdlàg functions from
[0,1]
to
\mathbb{R}
:
Let
\mathcal{J}
be the collection of functions from
[0,1]
to
\mathbb{R}
which are right-continuous and have left limits. For an increasing homeomorphism
\lambda
from
[0,1]
to itself, let
\|\lambda\|^{\circ}=\sup_{s<t}|\log\frac{\lambda(t)-\lambda(s)}{t-s}|
. When the space
\mathcal{J}
is equipped with the metric
d^{\circ}(f,g)=\inf_{\lambda}\max(\|\lambda\|^{\circ},\|f-g\circ\lambda\|_{\infty})
,
it is a complete separable metric space having the Skorokhod J
1
topology; see
(
10
, Section 12)
for details.

5.

(
\mathcal{I}
)
Nondecreasing càdlàg functions from
[0,\infty)\to[0,\infty]
:
Let
(q_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
be an arbitrary ordering of the nonnegative rational numbers. We equip
\mathcal{I}
with the metric
d(f,g)=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}2^{-j}\left|e^{-f(q_{j})}-e^{-g(q_{j})}\right|
.

Recall that

does not make literal sense since

\mathcal{L}^{\gamma}_{\phi}
are mutually singular. In this section, we will explain that, at least at the level of discrete approximations,

\text{``\quad$((\frac{d\nu}{d\mathcal{L}^{\gamma}_{\phi}})^{\eta}\mathcal{L}^{\gamma}_{\phi})^{\#}=(\mathcal{L}^{\beta}_{\phi})^{\#}$\quad''}\qquad\text{ if }\beta\text{ satisfies }\eta=\frac{1}{4}\beta^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\beta Q+1\text{ and }\beta<Q.

-QLE

Remark 4.8
.

Using the formal relation (

-QLE

(\gamma,\eta)
for which there exists

We now give a formal derivation of (

Fix some large integer

and divide

into

-length; let

\mathcal{L}^{\gamma}_{h}(I)\approx|I|^{\gamma Q/2}e^{\frac{\gamma}{2}h_{I}},\quad\mathcal{L}^{\beta}_{h}(I)\approx|I|^{\beta\hat{Q}/2}e^{\frac{\beta}{2}h_{I}},

where

on

Combining these gives

where in the last step we use that

of

Sampling one of these intervals

By contrast, sampling one of these intervals

Lemma 5.6
.

Let
q>0
be rational and let

be a smooth compactly-supported function on

. Then

\lim_{k\to\infty}(\phi_{q}^{\delta_{k}},f)=(\phi_{q},f)\quad\text{a.s.\ on the event }\{q<\mathcal{S}\}.

Note that the above limit makes sense since
q<\mathcal{S}
implies
q<\mathcal{S}^{\delta_{k}}
for all large
k
so
\phi_{q}^{\delta_{k}}
is defined.

Proof.

Let
(g^{\delta_{k}}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
be the family of mapping out functions for
\nu^{\delta_{k}}
and let
(g_{t})_{t\geq 0}
be the family of mapping out functions for
\nu
as in Proposition
2.1
.
To lighten notation, in this argument we write
g^{k}:=g^{\delta_{k}}_{t^{\delta_{k}}(q)}
and
g:=g_{t(q)}
.
Since
\phi_{q}=g\bullet_{\gamma}\phi=\phi\circ g^{-1}+Q\log|(g^{-1})^{\prime}|
, we have

(\phi_{q},f)=(\phi\circ g^{-1}+Q\log|(g^{-1})^{\prime}|,f)=(\phi,|g^{\prime}|^{2}f\circ g)+Q(\log|(g^{-1})^{\prime}|,f).

where
|g^{\prime}|^{2}f\circ g
is extended smoothly from
\mathbb{D}\backslash K_{q}
to
\mathbb{D}
by setting it equal to zero on
K_{q}
. Similarly,

(\phi_{q}^{\delta_{k}},f)=(\phi^{\delta_{k}},|(g^{k})^{\prime}|^{2}f\circ g^{k})+Q(\log|((g^{k})^{-1})^{\prime}|,f).

Thus, to prove the claim, it suffices to show the almost sure limits

\displaystyle\lim_{k\to\infty}(\log|((g^{k})^{-1})^{\prime}|,f)=(\log|(g^{-1})^{\prime}|,f),

(5.1)

\displaystyle\lim_{k\to\infty}(\phi^{\delta_{k}},|(g^{k})^{\prime}|^{2}f\circ g^{k})=(\phi,|g^{\prime}|^{2}f\circ g).

(5.2)

To prove these, we crucially need that
(g^{k})^{-1}-g^{-1}\to 0
uniformly on compact sets as

; this follows from Proposition
2.2
since
\lim_{k\to\infty}\nu^{\delta_{k}}=\nu
and
\lim_{k\to\infty}t^{\delta_{k}}(q)=t(q)
.
The first limit (
5.1
) is then immediate since the Cauchy integral formula gives
((g^{k})^{-1})^{\prime}-(g^{-1})^{\prime}\to 0
uniformly on compact sets.

We now turn to showing (
5.2
). Fix some
\varepsilon
such that the support of
f\circ g
lies in
(1-2\varepsilon)\mathbb{D}
, then the support of
f\circ g^{k}
lies in
(1-\varepsilon)\mathbb{D}
for all large
k
. Let
\|\cdot\|_{0}
denote the
L^{2}
-norm on
(1-\varepsilon)\mathbb{D}
, and let
\|\cdot\|_{a}=\|(-\Delta)^{-a}\cdot\|_{0}
where
a>0
is the arbitrary constant in the definition of the function space
\mathcal{D}
in Section
2.6
. Since
(-\Delta)
is self-adjoint, the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality yields
(f_{1},f_{2})=(((-\Delta)^{-a}f_{1}),((-\Delta)^{a}f_{2}))\leq\|f_{1}\|_{a}\cdot\|(-\Delta)^{a}f_{2}\|_{0}
. We thus have

\begin{gathered}|(\phi^{\delta_{k}},|(g^{k})^{\prime}|^{2}f\circ g^{k})-(\phi,|g^{\prime}|^{2}f\circ g)|\leq|(\phi^{\delta_{k}},|(g^{k})^{\prime}|^{2}f\circ g^{k}-|g^{\prime}|^{2}f\circ g)|+|(\phi^{\delta_{k}}-\phi,|g^{\prime}|^{2}f\circ g)|\\
\leq\|\phi^{\delta_{k}}\|_{a}\cdot\|(-\Delta)^{a}(|(g^{k})^{\prime}|^{2}f\circ g^{k}-|g^{\prime}|^{2}f\circ g)\|_{0}+\|\phi^{\delta_{k}}-\phi\|_{a}\cdot\|(-\Delta)^{a}(|g^{\prime}|^{2}f\circ g)\|_{0}.\end{gathered}

Since
\lim_{k\to\infty}\phi^{\delta_{k}}=\phi
in
\mathcal{D}
, we have
\lim_{k\to\infty}\|\phi^{\delta_{k}}\|_{a}=\|\phi\|_{a}<\infty
. Moreover, since the

are holomorphic functions which converge uniformly on a neighborhood of

to

, their derivatives converge uniformly on

to the derivatives of

. As

converge uniformly on

to those of

. Thus
\lim_{k\to\infty}\|(-\Delta)^{a}(|(g^{k})^{\prime}|^{2}f\circ g^{k}-|g^{\prime}|^{2}f\circ g)\|_{0}=0
. This implies the first term converges to zero. For the second term,
\lim_{k\to\infty}\phi^{\delta_{k}}=\phi
in

implies
\lim_{k\to\infty}\|\phi^{\delta_{k}}-\phi\|_{a}=0
. Thus (

We will prove that

We first prove that for rational

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}^{\gamma}_{\phi}(K_{s})+\mathcal{A}^{\gamma}_{\phi}(\mathbb{D}\setminus K_{s})

\displaystyle=\mathcal{A}^{\gamma}_{\phi}(\mathbb{D})\quad\text{ on the event }\{s<\mathcal{S}\}

\displaystyle s+\mathcal{A}^{\gamma}_{\phi^{\delta_{k}}}(\mathbb{D}\setminus K_{s}^{\delta_{k}})

\displaystyle=\mathcal{A}^{\gamma}_{\phi^{\delta_{k}}}(\mathbb{D})\quad\text{ on the event }\{s<\mathcal{S}^{\delta_{k}}\}

Passing to a subsequence and recoupling the laws via the Skorokhod representation theorem, we can ensure the limit
