Rings with Clean-Like Properties: Endomorphism, Matrix and Structural Theorems
Adel N. Abyzov, Andrey R. Chekhlov, Peter V. Danchev, Danil T. Tapkin

Abstract
. We investigate three clean-like properties for arbitrary rings, for endomorphism rings of abelian groups and for matrix rings over finite fields. Specifically, we study and establish when a ring is weakly strongly k k -nil-clean for some fixed natural number k ≥ 2 k\geq 2 , when the matrix ring is either quasi 2 2 -nil-clean or quasi 3 3 -nil-clean, and when the endomorphism ring is weakly clean. Our theorems improve substantially on some results due to Goldsmith-Vámos in Rend. Sem. Mat. Univ. Padova (2007), Breaz et al . in Linear Algebra & Appl. (2013), Koşan-Zhou in Front. Math. China (2016), Su et al . in J. Algebra & Appl. (2027), and some other existing results in this current topic.



Key words
: quasi clean rings, quasi nil-clean rings, weakly strongly
k
-nil-clean rings, weakly clean rings, endomorphism rings, matrix rings, abelian groups, fields.

2020 Mathematics Subject Classification
: 16S34, 16U60, 20K21.

of

stands for the full

over

.

Our main motivating tools in writing up the present article are the following four papers, namely
[

]
,
[

]

[

]

[

[

). Later on, in
[

[

).

In the other vein, in
[

]
the authors introduced the concept of a

in a ring

as being an element

such that

, and after that the notions of a

[

the authors looked at the ”nil case” of quasi-cleanness by studying the so-called

Very recently, in

]

Thus, inspired by all of the presented above, we are managing here to explore in-depth when an arbitrary ring is weakly strongly
k
-nil-clean for any

, as well as when endomorphism and matrix rings are either weakly clean and quasi nil-clean by trying to characterize the corresponding rings of these three directions comprehensively and significantly. In this way, our work is organized into three basic sections, and the major results are arranged as follows: In the second section, we try to discover the structure of weakly clean abelian groups adapting some ring-theoretic techniques (see Theorem

2.
Weakly Clean Endomorphism Rings of Abelian Groups

Imitating the terminology from
[
14
]
, we shall say that an abelian group
G
is
weakly clean
if its corresponding endomorphism ring
\E(G)
is weakly clean in the sense that each element is a sum or a difference of a unit and an idempotent (compare with
[
7
]
and
[
11
]
for the corresponding purely ring-theoretic definition of weak cleanness as the firstly cited article is devoted to the commutative case only), that is, each endomorphism of
G
is a sum or a difference of an invertible endomorphism (= automorphism) of
G
and an idempotent endomorphism (= projection) of
G
.

The following technicality extends the corresponding
[
14
, Lemma 1]
.

Lemma 2.1
.

If
A
is a weakly clean group and
B
is a clean group, then
A\oplus B
is a weakly clean group.

Proof.

It is well known that the endomorphisms of the direct sum
A\oplus B
may be regarded as matrices of the form
m=\begin{pmatrix}x&y\\
z&w\end{pmatrix}
, where
x\in\mathrm{End}(A)
,
w\in\mathrm{End}(B)
,
y\in\mathrm{Hom}(B,A)
and
z\in\mathrm{Hom}(A,B)
(cf.
[
13
]
).

Now, as
A
is weakly clean, we can write
x=u\pm c
, where
u
is a unit and
c
is an idempotent. So, one checks that
w-zu^{-1}y\in\mathrm{End}(B)
. But, since
B
is clean, we can also write
w-zu^{-1}y=v\pm d
with
v
a unit,

d
an idempotent, and the sign on
\pm d
is aligned with the sign on
\pm c
(this can be done, because in a clean ring every of its elements can simultaneously be written as
u+e
and as
u^{\prime}-e^{\prime}
, where
u,u^{\prime}
are units and
e,e^{\prime}
are idempotents).

Now, one writes that

m=\begin{pmatrix}u&y\\
z&v+zu^{-1}y\end{pmatrix}\pm\begin{pmatrix}c&0\\
0&d\end{pmatrix},

where the second term is obviously an idempotent. We note that the first term is a unit: in fact, pre-multiplying it by
p=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
-zu^{-1}&1\end{pmatrix}
and post-multiplying it by

q=\begin{pmatrix}u^{-1}&-u^{-1}yv^{-1}\\
0&v^{-1}\end{pmatrix}
leads to the identity matrix and, therefore, both
p
,
q
are invertible, as required.
∎

However, the direct sum of two weakly clean groups need
not
be weakly clean too. In this aspect, the next consequence is worthy of documentation.

Corollary 2.2
.

If
G=A\oplus B
, where
B
is fully invariant in
G
and is clean, then
G
is weakly clean if, and only if,
A
is weakly clean.

Proof.

It easily follows that
E(A)
and
E(B)
both are endomorphic images of
E(G)
, giving the assertion.
∎

Since all divisible groups are always clean in virtue of
[
14
, Lemma 3]
, then we obtain from Lemma
2.1
the following.

Corollary 2.3
.

A group is weakly clean if, and only if, its reduced part is weakly clean.

That is why, we henceforth will consider only
reduced
weakly clean groups.

Let
G
be a
p
-group. Recall that the two-sided ideal

H(G)=\{\varphi\in E(G)\,|\,{\rm height}(x)<\infty\ \Rightarrow\ {\rm height}(\varphi(x))>{\rm height}(x)\ \forall\ x\in G[p]\}

is often referred to as the
Pierce radical
of
G
. For a reduced
p
-group
G
, it is always true that
J(E(G))\leq H(G)
(cf.
[
20
]
,
[
17
]
), where
J(R)
stands for the Jacobson radical of a ring
R
.

Now, we set

J_{r}^{\prime}(R):=\{a\in R\,|\,\mathrm{for\,each}\ r\in R\ \mathrm{the\,element}\ 1-ar\ \mathrm{or}\ 1+ar\ \mathrm{is\,a\,unit\,of\,ring}\ R\}.

and, similarly,

J_{l}^{\prime}(R):=\{a\in R\,|\,\mathrm{for\,each}\ r\in R\ \mathrm{the\,element}\ 1-ra\ \mathrm{or}\ 1+ra\ \mathrm{is\,a\,unit\,of\,ring}\ R\}.

It is quite clear that
J_{r}^{\prime}(R)R\subseteq J_{r}^{\prime}(R)
,
RJ_{l}^{\prime}(R)\subseteq J_{l}^{\prime}(R)
and
J(R)\subseteq J^{\prime}(R):=J_{r}^{\prime}(R)\cap J^{\prime}_{l}(R)
, as well as that
f(J^{\prime}(R))\subseteq J^{\prime}(S)
for any homomorphism
f:R\to S
of rings
R,S
.

The following technicalities are critical keys for our further presentation.

Lemma 2.4
.

If
G
is a reduced weakly clean
p
-group, then
H(G)\subseteq J^{\prime}(E(G))
.

Proof.

Choose
\varphi\in H(G)
and write
\varphi=u\pm\pi
, where
\pi^{2}=\pi
. Same as
[
14
, Lemma 5]
, we must have
\pi=1
, so that
1\pm\varphi\in U(E(G))
, and since
H(G)
is ideal, it follows that
\varphi\in J^{\prime}(E(G))
, as requested.
∎

Lemma 2.5
.

(1) Let
G=A\oplus B
. If
\alpha^{\prime}\in J^{\prime}(E(A))
, then
\alpha\in J^{\prime}(E(G))
, where
\alpha\!\upharpoonright\!A=\alpha^{\prime}
and
\alpha(B)=\{0\}
. If
\alpha\in J^{\prime}(E(G))
, then
\pi\alpha i\in J^{\prime}(E(A))
, where
\pi:G\to A
is a projection and
i:A\to G
is a natural embedding.

(2) If
G=\bigoplus_{n\geq 1}\langle a_{n}\rangle
, where
{\rm order}(a_{n})=p^{r_{n}}
,
r_{1}<r_{2}<\dots
, then

H(G)\nsubseteq J^{\prime}(E(G))
.

(3) If a reduced
p
-group
G
is weakly clean, then
G
does not have a direct summand which is an unbounded direct sum of cyclic groups.

Proof.

(1) We know that the ring
E(G)
is isomorphic to the ring
R
of all matrices of size 2, say

(\varphi_{ij})
, where
\varphi_{11}\in E(A)
. We have to prove that
(\lambda_{ij})\in J^{\prime}(R)
, where

\lambda_{11}\in J^{\prime}(E(A))
and
\lambda_{12}=\lambda_{21}=\lambda_{22}=0
. To that end, suppose
(g_{ij})\in R

and
1\pm\lambda_{11}g_{11}=u_{11}\in U(E(A))
; then,
1\pm(\lambda_{ij})(g_{ij})=(v_{ij})
, where

v_{11}=u_{11}
,
v_{12}=\pm\lambda_{11}g_{12}
,
v_{21}=0
,
v_{22}=1
noticing that
(v_{ij})\in U(R)
.

Similarly, it can be shown that
1\pm(g_{ij})(\lambda_{ij})\in U(R)
for every
(g_{ij})\in R
. So, what remains to establish is that, if
(\lambda_{ij})\in J^{\prime}(R)
, then
\lambda_{11}\in J^{\prime}(E(A))
. Choose
g_{11}\in E(A)
and take

(g_{ij})\in R
such that
g_{12}=g_{21}=g_{22}=0
. But, by assumption,
1\pm(\lambda_{ij})(g_{ij})\in U(R)
and

1\pm(g_{ij})(\lambda_{ij})\in U(R)
and, consequently,
1\pm\lambda_{11}g_{11}\in U(E(A))
and
1\pm g_{11}\lambda_{11}\in U(E(A))
.

(2) Choosing
\alpha\in E(G)
such that
\alpha(a_{n})=p^{r_{n+1}-r_{n}}a_{n+1}
, it is rather evident that
\alpha\in H(G)
. But,
1\pm\alpha\notin U(E(G))
since
a_{1}\notin\im(1\pm\alpha)
, and thus
\alpha\notin J^{\prime}(E(G))
.

(3) It follows immediately from (1) and (2).
∎

In
[
14
, Lemma 8]
is shown that all bounded
p
-groups are clean. Since each reduced unbounded countable
p
-group must have a direct summand which is a direct sum of cyclic groups whose orders are also unbounded, then we quickly derive the following consequence.

Corollary 2.6
.

A countable weakly clean
p
-group
G
is bounded.

Likewise, in
[
14
, Theorem 10]
is proven that an unbounded totally projective
p
-group must have a direct summand which is an unbounded direct sum of cyclic groups, and so we automatically extract the following more general consequence.

Corollary 2.7
.

A totally projective weakly clean
p
-group is bounded.

Besides, it was established in
[
14
, Lemma 8]
that even torsion-complete
p
-groups are always clean. So, with Lemma
2.5
at hand, we detect the following consequence.

Corollary 2.8
.

A direct sum of torsion-complete
p
-groups is weakly clean if, and only if, it is torsion-complete (and hence clean).

We now focus on the torsion-free case that has
not
been considered in
[
14
]
.

If
G
is a torsion-free group, then let
\Pi(G):=\{p\in\mathbb{P}\,|\,pG\neq G\}
. Such a group
G
is said to be
quasi-homogeneous
, provided that
\Pi(G)=\Pi(H)
for each non-zero pure subgroup
H\leq G
.

We also recall that

H(G)=\left\{\varphi\in E(G)\,\Bigg|\begin{array}[]{l}\,x\in D\Rightarrow\varphi(x)=0,\\
x\in G\setminus D,{\rm height}_{p}(x)<\infty\Rightarrow{\rm height}_{p}(x)<{\rm height}_{p}(\varphi(x))\,\mathrm{for\,each}\,p\in\mathbb{P}\end{array}\right\}.

Thus, if
G
is a torsion-free group of finite rank, then
H(G)\leq J(E(G))
(see
[
17
, Lemma 21.1]
and
[
16
]
).

We, thereby, come to the following.

Lemma 2.9
.

If
G
is a torsion-free weakly clean group, then either
1+f\in U(R)
or
1-f\in U(R)
for every
f\in H(G)
.

Proof.

Take
f\in H(G)
and write
f=u\pm e
, where
u
is a unit and
e
is an idempotent. For
x\in G

and
p\in\Pi(G)
with
{\rm height}_{p}(x)<\infty
, as
{\rm height}_{p}(x)<{\rm height}_{p}(f(x))
, we get
{\rm height}_{p}((u-f)(x))={\rm height}_{p}(x)
. So, the difference
u-f
preserves the
p
-height of each element in

G
for all primes
p
. We also claim that
\ker(u-f)=\{0\}
. Indeed, if
0\neq x\in D
, then
(u-f)(x)=u(x)\neq 0
,
and if
x\not\in D
, then
{\rm height}_{p}(x)<\infty
for some
p
. Thus,
{\rm height}_{p}((u-f)(x))={\rm height}_{p}(x)
and so
(u-f)(x)\neq 0
. Since
u-f=\mp e
, it follows that
\ker(e)=\{0\}
, and since
e
is an idempotent, we infer that
e=1
, as wanted.
∎

We proceed by recalling the following.

Lemma 2.10
.

[
19
, Lemma 2.1]

Let
R
be a commutative indecomposable ring. Then:

(1)
R
is clean if, and only if,
R
is local.

(2)
R
is weakly clean but not clean if, and only if,
R
has exactly two maximal ideals and
2\in U(R)
.

(3) If
2\not\in U(R)
,
R
is weakly clean if, and only if,
R
is clean.

The next comments are worthwhile.

Remark 2.11
.

From previous statement, it follows that:

(1) If
G
is a reduced torsion-free clean group of rank 1, then
E(G)
is local, i.e.,
E(G)\cong\mathbb{Z}_{p}

for some prime
p
, where
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
is the ring of all rational numbers with denominators that are mutually prime with
p
.

(2) If
G
is a torsion-free weakly clean non-clean group of rank 1, then
E(G)\cong\mathbb{Z}_{p,q}
for some odd primes
p
,
q
, where
\mathbb{Z}_{p,q}
is the ring of all rational numbers with denominators that are mutually prime with
p
,
q
, and
2G=G
.

(3) (see
[
21
]
) A reduced completely decomposable torsion-free group
G
is clean if, and only if,
G=\bigoplus_{p}G_{p}
, where
G_{p}
are of finite rank the homogeneous components of
G
, and
qG_{p}=G_{p}
for any prime
q\neq p
.

Note that if for a ring
R
we have
a\not\in J^{\prime}(R)

\Rightarrow

a\in U(R)
, then
R
is weakly clean.

Lemma 2.12
.

Let
R
be a weakly clean ring. Then:

(1) Each right ideal
I\nsubseteq J_{r}^{\prime}(R)
contains a non-zero idempotent.

(2) Each left ideal
I\nsubseteq J_{l}^{\prime}(R)
contains a non-zero idempotent.

(3) If the idempotent
e
is primitive, the corner ring
eRe
is weakly clean.

Proof.

(1) Let
a\in R
, and suppose that
aR
contains only the zero idempotent. For
r\in R
, if
ar=u+e
, where

u
is a unit in
R
and
e
is an idempotent in
R
, then

u(1-e)u^{-1}=(ar-e)(1-e)u^{-1}=ar(1-e)u^{-1}\in aR.

Thus,
e=1
and
1-ar=-u
. From equality
ar=u-e
, we analogously conclude
1+ar=u
. So,
a\in J_{r}^{\prime}(R)
.

(2) Same as in (1), it must be that
ra=u\pm 1
, and thus
a\in J_{l}^{\prime}(R)
.

(3) Let
f
be a primitive idempotent, and choose
a\in fRf
with
a\not\in J^{\prime}(fRf)
. Thanks to (1), there exists

0\neq g=g^{2}\in aR\leq fR
, and since
f
is chosen primitive, we find that
gR=fR
. Hence,
aR=fR
, so that
f=ab
for some
b\in R
and
a
has right inverse
fbf
in
fRf
. Similarly,

possesses left inverse, whence

.

Now, taking into account the fact that the elements from
J^{\prime}_{r}(R)
or
J^{\prime}_{l}(R)
are weakly clean, it follows by what we have proved so far that
fRf
is weakly clean ring, as desired.
∎

As a direct consequence, we deduce the following.

Corollary 2.13
.

An indecomposable direct summand of a weakly clean group too is weakly clean.

The following statement is a bit curious.

Lemma 2.14
.

Let
G=\bigoplus_{n\geq 1}G_{n}
, where
G_{n}
are reduced torsion-free groups of rank 1 with types
t(G_{n})\leq t(G_{n+1})
, and
pG_{n}\neq G_{n}
for some prime
p
. Then,
G
is not a weakly clean group.

Proof.

Choose
0\neq g_{n}\in G_{n}\setminus pG_{n}
with characteristics
\chi(g_{n})\leq\chi(g_{n+1})
. Define a mapping
f:G\to G
via
f(g_{n})=pg_{n}+p^{2}g_{n+1}
. By a way of contradiction, we write that
f=u\pm e
, where

u
is a unit in
\E(G)
and
e
is an idempotent in
\E(G)
. If
e\neq 1
, then one inspects that
e(x)=0
for some

x\in G\setminus pG
. Therefore,
px=u(x)
; however,
u(x)\in G\setminus pG
since
u
is an automorphism. So,
e=1
.
Thus,
f\mp 1=u
. But, it is readily verified that
g_{1}\notin\im(f\mp 1)
, a contradiction, as suspected.
∎

The following construction confirms our observation alluded to above that the direct sum of two weakly clean groups is
not
necessarily again weakly clean.

Example 2.15
.

If
A
,
B
are two non-isomorphic weakly clean but not clean torsion-free groups of rank 1, then
A\oplus B
is
not
weakly clean.

Proof.

Utilizing Remark
2.11
, one writes that
R:=E(A)\cong\mathbb{Z}_{p,q}
and
S:=E(B)\cong\mathbb{Z}_{g,t}

for some odd primes
p,q,g,t
, where, for instance,
g\neq p,q
as
A\ncong B
.

Note that, if
\alpha=(2p-q)/(q-p)
, then
\alpha\in U(R)
; but,
\alpha+1=p/(q-p)
,
\alpha+2=q/(q-p)
ensuring
\alpha+1,\alpha+2\not\in U(R)
.

Moreover, if
\beta=(2g-t)/(g-t)
, then
\beta\in U(S)
; but,
\beta-1=g/(g-t)
,
\beta-2=t/(g-t)
assuring
\beta-1,\beta-2\not\in U(S)
.

Consequently,
(\alpha+1)\oplus(\beta-1)
is the asked endomorphism of
A\oplus B
that is
not
weakly clean. In fact, if
(\alpha+1)\oplus(\beta-1)=\delta\pm e
, then we have
\delta=\varphi\oplus\psi
for some
\varphi\in U(R)
,

\psi\in U(S)
and an idempotent
e=e_{1}\oplus e_{2}
. Since

(\alpha+1)=\varphi\pm e_{1}\not\in U(R),(\beta-1)=\psi\pm e_{2}\not\in U(S),

we get
e_{1},e_{2}\neq 0
, so that
e_{1}=1
and
e_{2}=1
. But,
\alpha+2\not\in U(R)
and
\beta-2\not\in U(S)
, thus seeing that the equality
(\alpha+1)\oplus(\beta-1)=\delta\pm(1,1)
is imposable, which really means that the direct sum
A\oplus B
is
not
weakly clean, as pursued.
∎

Let
G
be a reduced completely decomposable torsion-free group such that each its direct summand of rank 1 is weakly clean (see Corollary

). Then,

can be written as
G=G_{1}\oplus G_{2}
, where
G_{1}
is fully invariant in

. Indeed, assume that
G_{1}
is the direct sum of all clean direct summands of rank 1, and
G_{2}
is the direct sum of all weakly clean (but non-clean) direct summands of rank 1. It follows from Remark

that

is fully invariant in

. Also, each homogeneous component of

is fully invariant in
G_{2}
.

The following statement is crucial for characterizing when a weakly clean group is reduced completely decomposable torsion-free.

Proposition 2.16
.

Let
G=A^{n}
for some reduced torsion-free weakly clean non-clean group
A
of rank 1 and some
n\geq 1
. If
G
is weakly clean, then
n=1
.

Proof.

Since by assumption
A
is reduced non-clean, one has that
pA\neq A
and
qA\neq A
for some different odd prime
p,q
. Write
pm-qn=1
for some integers
m,n
, where
n>1
, and suppose
\alpha
is the multiplication of
A
on
pm
,
\beta
is the multiplication of
A^{n-1}
on
-pm
by setting
f:=\alpha\oplus\beta
.

Assume now that
f=u\pm e
, where
u
is a unit and
e
is an idempotent. If
e(x)=0
for some
x\in G\setminus pG
, then
f(x)=u(x)
which is an absurd, because

{\rm height}_{p}(f(x))>{\rm height}_{p}(u(x))={\rm height}_{p}(x),

so that
\ker e=\{0\}
implying
e=1
as

is invertible in view of finite rank restriction on

.

Write now that
f=u+1
. Since
f(A)\leq A
, one sees that
u(A)\leq A
, i.e.,
u\!\upharpoonright\!A
is an automorphism of
A
. Thus,

pma-a=(pm-1)a=qna=u(a)

for some
0\neq a\in A
that is wrong, because

{\rm height}_{q}(qna)>{\rm height}_{q}(u(a))={\rm height}_{q}(a).

The other equality
f=u-1
is also impossible, because

-pmx+x=(-pm+1)x=-qnx

for some
x\in A^{n-1}
yielding

{\rm height}_{q}((-pm+1)x)={\rm height}_{q}(qnx)>{\rm height}_{q}(u(x))={\rm height}_{q}(x).

Therefore, it must be that
n=1
, as expected.
∎

We now accumulated all the information necessary to establish the following criterion.

Theorem 2.17
.

Let
G
be a reduced completely decomposable torsion-free group. Then,
G
is weakly clean if, and only if,
G=G_{1}\oplus G_{2}
, where
G_{1}
is a completely decomposable clean group and
G_{2}
is a weakly clean group of
rank
\leq 1
.

Proof.

Necessity.
Suppose that
G_{1}
is the direct sum of all clean direct summands of rank
1
of
G
, and
G_{2}
is the direct sum of all weakly clean but non-clean direct summands of rank
1
of
G
. It follows from Remark
2.11
that
G_{1}
is fully invariant in
G
and, in particular,
G_{1}
is clean, as claimed, whereas
G_{2}
is weakly clean (in case that it is non-zero).

Furthermore, one observes that each homogeneous component of
G_{2}
is fully invariant in
G_{2}
, so it must be weakly clean and hence Proposition
2.16
works to deduce that this component is of rank
\leq 1
. Moreover, each direct summand of
G_{2}
is weakly clean as being a fully invariant direct summand of a weakly clean group, whence Example
2.15
guarantees that
{\rm rank}(G_{2})=1
provided that
G_{2}\neq\{0\}
, as asserted.

Sufficiency.
It follows at one from Corollary
2.2
.
∎

Commenting on the last necessary and sufficient condition, a difficult question that remains unsettled yet is to decide whether or not homogeneous completely decomposable weakly clean groups are of finite rank.

As we have seen above, there is a torsion-free weakly clean group which is
not
clean. Nevertheless, the following claim concerning the 2-primary case somewhat sounds surprisingly. Before formulating and proving it, we need the following statement.

Lemma 2.18
.

(
[
17
, Lemma 20.3]
)
Let
G
be a
p
-group, and let
\alpha\in\E(G)
. In order to have
\alpha\in U(\E(G))
, it is necessary and sufficient
\ker\alpha\cap G[p]=\{0\}
and
\alpha((p^{n}G)[p])=(p^{n}G)[p]
for each

.

So, we are ready to check truthfulness of the following surprising assertion.

Proposition 2.19
.

Weakly clean
2
-groups are clean.

Proof.

Assume that
\alpha=u-e
, where
u
is an automorphism and
e
is an idempotent. Then, one may write that
\alpha=(u-2e)+e
, where
u-2e\in U(\E(G))
. To substantiate the stated above relation, one observes that, if

, then
(u-2e)(x)=u(x)\neq 0
, i.e.,
\ker(u-2e)=\{0\}
, and thus

(u-2e)((2^{n}G)[2])=u((2^{n}G)[2])=(2^{n}G)[2].

It now just remains to refer to Lemma
2.18
to get the promised implication.
∎

So, a difficult question which logically arises is what happens for weakly clean

-groups in the case of

; we conjecture that there is a weakly clean

-group that is
not
clean, but the formal building of such a construction still eludes us, however. Perhaps the endomorphism ring of such a group, if certainly it exists, should
not
be additively generated by automorphisms of this group.

3.
Variations of Quasi Nil-Clean Matrix Rings over a Finite Field

Standardly, an element
r
of a ring

is said to be

-potent
if there is an integer
m>1
such that
r^{m}=r
. In particular, if
m=2
, the element
r
is termed
idempotent
, and if
m=3
, the element
r
is termed
tripotent
.

More generally, mimicking
[
23
]
, an element

of a ring

is called
quasi idempotent
if
e^{2}=\lambda e
for some
{\lambda\in U(R)\cap C(R)}
. By analogy, an element
f
of a ring

is called
quasi
q
-potent
if
f^{q}=\lambda f
for some
{\lambda\in U(R)\cap C(R)}
. If
e^{2}=\lambda e
with
{\lambda\in U(R)\cap C(R)}
, then one simply checks that
(\lambda^{-1}e)^{2}=\lambda^{-1}e
. Therefore, the set of quasi idempotents coincides with the set of elements

with
{k\in U(R)\cap C(R)}
and

. In particular, every element of a field is necessarily a quasi idempotent. Moreover, if

is a
q
-potent and
{\lambda\in U(R)\cap C(R)}
, then one plainly verifies that
(\lambda f)^{q}=\lambda^{q}f=\lambda^{q-1}(\lambda f)
and
\lambda f
is a quasi
q
-potent. However, for
q>2
, not every quasi
q
-potent can be represented in this form.

The following illustrates some more elementary properties of this sort.

Example 3.1
.

(1) For any
q>1
, every quasi
q
-potent of an integral domain
R
(and, therefore, every element of a direct product of integral domains) has the form
\lambda f
for some
\lambda\in U(R)
and a
q
-potent

. Indeed, assume that
g\in R
and
g^{q}=\mu g
for some
\mu\in U(R)
. Thus, if
g=0
, then
g=1\cdot 0
; otherwise,

is a unit and

.

(2) Consider the matrix

A:=\begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\
1&0\end{pmatrix}\in M_{2}(\mathbb{F}_{3})
. Since one calculates that
A^{3}=-A
, so
A
is a quasi
3
-potent. It is also clear that, if
(\lambda A)^{3}=\lambda A
for some
\lambda\in U(\mathbb{F}_{3})
, then

, a contradiction. Therefore,

cannot be represented as
\lambda f
, where
{\lambda\in U(M_{2}(\mathbb{F}_{3}))\cap C(M_{2}(\mathbb{F}_{3}))}
and

is a tripotent.

We now come to the following notion, which was introduced and explored in
[
23
]
as a non-trivial generalization of clean rings (that are those rings whose elements are a sum of a unit and an idempotent).

Definition 3.2
.

An element

of a ring

is called
(strongly) quasi clean
if

is a sum of a unit and a quasi idempotent (that commute one another). So, a ring is called
(strongly) quasi clean
if each of its elements is (strongly) quasi clean.

For example, it was shown there that if the matrix ring

over a commutative ring

is quasi clean. Likewise, it was proven that for a prime

.

Also, we recall that a ring

is called
(strongly)

-nil-clean
if each of its elements is a sum of a
q
-potent and a nilpotent (that commute). We now introduce two generalizations of this concept.

(1)

A ring

is called
(strongly)
\lambda
-

-nil-clean
if each of its elements is the sum
\lambda f+N
, where

,
{\lambda\in U(R)\cap C(R)}
, and

is a nilpotent (that commute).

(2)

A ring

is called

-nil-clean

-potent and a nilpotent (that commute).

The following chain of implications is pretty obvious:

\text{$R$ is $q$-nil-clean}\;\Rightarrow\;\text{$R$ is $\lambda$-$q$-nil-clean}\;\Rightarrow\;\text{$R$ is quasi $q$-nil-clean}.

These three classes are pairwise distinct as our next arguments show: every infinite field is
\lambda
-

-nil-clean, but
not

-nil-clean. Since

is isomorphic to a direct product of fields, every element of

-nil-clean. However,

is manifestly

-

-nil-clean, because there are no nonzero nilpotent elements in

and

cannot be represented as

, where
\lambda\in U(R)
and

is an idempotent.

Our next commentaries are somewhat worthy of recording.

Remark 3.3
.

Quasi

-nil-clean rings were studied in

under the name

Let

•

•

Let

3.1.
Matrix rings of order
2

We will start our research with the special case of matrix rings of order
2
by proving the following.

Proposition 3.4
.

Let
q\geq 3
be an odd integer. Then, for any field
F
, the ring
M_{2}(F)
is
\lambda
-
q
-nil-clean.

Proof.

Take
A\in M_{2}(F)
. Without loss of generality, we may assume that
A
is in a rational canonical form. There are three different cases:

(a)
A=\lambda I_{2}
for some
\lambda\in F
. Thus,
A=\lambda\cdot I_{2}+0
is a decomposition as a linear combination of a
q
-potent and a nilpotent.

(b)
A=C(p)
with
p=x^{2}-ax-b\in F[x]
and
a\not=0
. So,

A=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}0&b\\
1&a\end{array}\right)=a\cdot\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}0&ba^{-1}\\
0&1\end{array}\right)+\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}0&0\\
1&0\end{array}\right)

is a decomposition as a linear combination of a
q
-potent and a nilpotent.

(c)
A=C(p)
with
p=x^{2}-b\in F[x]
. Considering decomposition

1&0\end{array}\right)=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}-1&b+1\\

one verifies that the last term is obviously nilpotent, whereas

\begin{pmatrix}-1&b+1\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}

is a
q
-potent in any field

, as needed.
∎

Contrary to Proposition

, the case of even

presents strong restriction on a field

.

Proposition 3.5
.

Let
q\geq 2
be an even integer. For a field
F
, the following four statements are equivalent:

(1)

M_{2}(F)
is quasi
q
-nil-clean;

(2)

M_{2}(F)
is
\lambda
-
q
-nil-clean;

(3)

F
is a perfect field of characteristic
2
.

Proof.

It is quite obvious that (2) guarantees (1), so we skip details.

(1)\Rightarrow(3)
. Assume that
\Char F\not=2
and
M_{2}(F)
is quasi
q
-nil-clean. Put

A=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}1&0\\
0&-1\end{array}\right)\in M_{2}(F).

By assumption, there exist
f\in M_{2}(F)
,
\lambda\in F^{*}
and a nilpotent
N\in M_{n}(F)
such that

A=f+N
and
f^{q}=\lambda f
. We thus have

0=\tr A=\tr(f+N)=\tr f.

Since
\Char F\not=2
, we discover that either

, or a matrix

has two distinct nonzero eigenvalues

. In the former case,

is a nilpotent, a contradiction. In the latter case, we must have

a contradiction.

Assume now that
\Char F=2
, but
F
is not a perfect field. Fix

that is not a square.
Set

A=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}0&b\\
1&0\end{array}\right)\in M_{2}(F).

If there exist
f\in M_{2}(F)
,
\lambda\in F^{*}
and a nilpotent
N\in M_{n}(F)
such that

and

, then

Since

is not a nilpotent, we extract

. But
\Char F=2
and evenness of

imply diagonalizability of

, because

is similar to

in

for some

. Hence,

and

However,

ensures

, a contradiction.

(3)\Rightarrow(1)
. Let
A\in M_{2}(F)
. Without loss of generality, we may assume that
A
is in a rational canonical form. Consider four basic cases:

(a)
A=\lambda I_{2}
for some
\lambda\in F
. Then,
A=\lambda\cdot I_{2}+0
is a decomposition as a linear combination of a
q
-potent and a nilpotent.

(b)
A=C(p)
with
p=x^{2}-ax-b\in F[x]
and
a\not=0
. Thus,

A=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}0&b\\
1&a\end{array}\right)=a\cdot\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}0&ba^{-1}\\
0&1\end{array}\right)+\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}0&0\\
1&0\end{array}\right)

is a decomposition as a linear combination of a
q
-potent and a nilpotent.

(c)

with

and

. Since

is a perfect field, there exists

such that

. So,

-potent and a nilpotent.

(d)

∎

3.2.
Quasi

-nil-clean rings

As it was mentioned earlier, a ring

is quasi

-nil-clean if, and only if, it

is
\lambda
-

-nil-clean.

Let

be a field. Exploiting Proposition

, the ring

is quasi

-nil-clean if, and only if,

is a perfect field of characteristic

. For matrix rings of order

Theorem 3.6
.

For a field
F
and an integer

, the following three points are equivalent:

(1)

M_{n}(F)
is quasi
2
-nil-clean;

(2)

M_{n}(F)
is nil-clean;

(3)

.

Proof.

The equivalence

was proved in
[

]
. Since every nil-clean ring is quasi
2
-nil-clean, it is enough to establish only that (1) forces (3).

(1)\Rightarrow(2)
. Assume that
M_{n}(F)
is quasi
2
-nil-clean. Choose an arbitrary element

and take the matrix

A:=\left(\begin{array}[]{c|c}I_{n-1}&0\\
\hline\cr 0&b\end{array}\right)\in M_{n}(F).

By hypothesis, there exist an idempotent

,
\lambda\in F
and a nilpotent
N\in M_{n}(F)
such that

. Since
\dim V_{1}(A)=n-1>n/2
, the matrices

and

have common eigenvector

:

and

. Hence,

is a eigenvector for

. Computing

Consequently,

. Therefore,

Assume that

∎

We present here some common generalizations to some of the results from

. Our chief result here is the following criterion.

Theorem 3.7

For a field

-nil-clean;

(2)

The equivalence

(1)\Rightarrow(4)

\left(\begin{array}[]{cc|c}\mu&1&0\\
0&\mu&0\\
\hline\cr 0&0&0\end{array}\right),\quad\left(\begin{array}[]{cc|c}0&1&0\\
0&0&0\\
\hline\cr 0&0&\mu\end{array}\right).

If
f
is similar to the first matrix, then
\tr(A)=\tr(f)=0
and
b
is in a prime subfield of
F
. If
f
is similar to the second matrix, then
b=\tr(A)=\mu
. So, all elements of the field
F
, except perhaps one, lie in a prime subfield. Consequently, the field
F
is prime indeed.

Claim 2.
The field
F
is isomorphic either to
\mathbb{F}_{2}
or
\mathbb{F}_{3}
.

Since
F
is a prime field, it is enough to show only that
\Char F\not\in\{2,3\}
. To that end, assume the contrary. Consider two major cases:

(a)
n>3
. As we showed earlier,
f
is a tripotent. Since
\dim V_{1}(A)=n-1
, both
\dim V_{-1}(f)
and
\dim V_{0}(f)
are at most one, as for otherwise matrices
A
and
f
would have common eigenvector
v
with
Av=v
and
fv\not=v
. Therefore, the tripotent
f
is similar to the one of the following four matrices

\left(\begin{array}[]{c|c|c}I_{n-2}&0&0\\
\hline\cr 0&-1&0\\
\hline\cr 0&0&0\end{array}\right),\quad\left(\begin{array}[]{c|c}I_{n-1}&0\\
\hline\cr 0&-1\end{array}\right),\quad\left(\begin{array}[]{c|c}I_{n-1}&0\\
\hline\cr 0&0\end{array}\right),\quad I_{n}.

Thus,
\tr f\in\{(n-3)\cdot 1_{F},(n-2)\cdot 1_{F},(n-1)\cdot 1_{F},n\cdot 1_{F}\}
.

Next, put
b=-3\cdot 1_{F}
. We now derive that
\tr f=(n-4)\cdot 1_{F}
and
n-4\equiv n-k\mod\Char F
, where
k\in\{0,1,2,3\}
. It follows at once that
\Char F\mid 4-k
, a contradiction.

(b)
n=3
. As it was proved earlier, if
\tr(A)\not=0
, then
A=f+N
is a decomposition as a sum of a tripotent and a nilpotent. If
\tr(A)=0
, then
A
is a tripotent itself and we can put
f=A
,
N=0
. Similarly to the case (a), we detect
\Char F\mid 4-k
for
b\not=-3\cdot 1_{F}
, a contradiction which concludes the entire argumentation.
∎

It was shown earlier that, for
q\in\{2,3\}
and a field
F
, the ring
M_{n}(F)
is
\lambda
-
q
-nil-clean if, and only if,
M_{n}(F)
is quasi
q
-nil-clean. That is why, the following question is sensible.

Problem 3.8
.

Let
F
be a field and let
q\geq 4
be an integer. Is it true that, for every
n\geq 2
, the ring
M_{n}(F)
is
\lambda
-
q
-nil-clean if, and only if,
M_{n}(F)
is quasi
q
-nil-clean?

3.4.
Some remarks on the general case

It is quite hard to say anything specific about general case. In fact, take integers
q>3
and large enough
n
. Assume, moreover, that
M_{n}(F)
is quasi
q
-nil-clean and
\overline{F}
is an algebraic closure of
F
.

Besides, take arbitrary
b\in F
and put

A=\left(\begin{array}[]{c|c}I_{n-1}&0\\
\hline\cr 0&b\end{array}\right)\in M_{n}(F).

By hypothesis, there exist
f\in M_{n}(F)
,
\lambda\in F^{*}
and a nilpotent
N\in M_{n}(F)
such that

A=f+N
and
f^{q}=\lambda f
. Note that
f
has at most
q
pairwise distinct eigenvalues in
\overline{F}
and
\dim V_{1}(A)=n-1
. Furthermore, consider two possible cases as follows:

(a)
\Char F=0
.

In this case, quasi
q
-potent
f
is diagonalizable if
M_{n}(\overline{F})
. Thus, for
n>q
, matrices
A
and
f
have common eigenvector
v\in\overline{F}^{n}
with
Av=v
. It therefore follows that
f
is a
q
-potent and

A=f+N

is a decomposition as a sum of a
q
-potent and a nilpotent. However, trace of a
q
-potent matrix
f\in M_{n}(\overline{F})
can can only take a finite number of values. Hence,
\tr A=\tr f
tells us that
|F|<\infty
, a contradiction.

(b)
\Char F=p>0
.

The matrix
f
has Jordan normal form over
\overline{F}
. Take an arbitrary eigenvalue
\mu\in\overline{F}
of
f
and consider one of the corresponding Jordan blocks

J_{m}(\mu)=\mu I_{m}+J_{m}(0)\in M_{m}(\overline{F}).

Put
p^{k}
as the greatest nonnegative power of
p
such that
p^{k}\mid q-1
. Since
J_{m}(\mu)^{q}=\lambda J_{m}(\mu)
, we have
1\leq m\leq p^{k}
. Likewise, since there are
1+\frac{q-1}{p^{k}}
different
q
-potents in
\overline{F}
, for any
n>(1+\frac{q-1}{p^{k}})\cdot p^{k}=(q-1)+p^{k}
, the matrices
A
and
f
have common eigenvector
v\in\overline{F}^{n}
with
Av=v
. It thus follows that
f
is a
q
-potent and

A=f+N

is a decomposition as a sum of a
q
-potent and a nilpotent. In particular,
\tr A=\tr f
. If, for some eigenvalue
1\not=\mu\in\overline{F}
of
f
, we have
\dim V_{\mu}(f)>1
, then
1-\mu
is an eigenvalue of
N
, a contradiction. Consequently,
\dim V_{\mu}(f)\leq 1
.

If now
p\nmid q-1
, then every Jordan block of
f\in M_{n}(\overline{F})
is scalar and so
\tr f
cannot take more than
2^{q-1}
different values. If, however,
p\mid q-1
, then there are
1+\frac{q-1}{p^{k}}
different
q
-potents in
\overline{F}
. For an eigenvalue
0,1\not=\mu\in\overline{F}
it is pretty clear that
\tr J_{m}(\mu)\in\mu\mathbb{F}_{p}
. Since there is no Jordan block
J_{m}(0)
for
m>1
in any
q
-potent,
\tr f
cannot take more than
2\cdot p^{-1+(q-1)/p^{k}}
different values. At the same time,
\tr f
as a sum of
q
-potents lies in a splitting field of
x^{q}-x
over
\mathbb{F}_{p}
.

Thus, summarizing the conclusions presented above, we have shown the following general claim.

Proposition 3.9
.

Let
F
be a field, and let
q>3
be an integer.

(1)

If for some
n>q
the ring
M_{n}(F)
is quasi
q
-nil-clean, then
\Char F
is finite.

(2)

Let
\Char F=p<\infty
and
p^{k}
is the greatest non-negative power of
p
such that
p^{k}\mid q-1
. If, for some
n>(q-1)+p^{k}
, the ring
M_{n}(F)
is quasi
q
-nil-clean, then
F
is a finite field and
F
is a subfield of splitting field of
x^{q}-x
over
\mathbb{F}_{p}
. Moreover,

(a)

if
\mathrm{gcd}(p,q-1)=1
, then
|F|\leq 2^{q-1}
;

(b)

if
p\mid q-1
, then
|F|\leq 2\cdot p^{-1+(q-1)/p^{k}}
.

In other words, for any
q>3
, there exists a finite set
A_{q}
consisting of finite fields such that, for any large enough
n
, the quasi
q
-nil-cleanness of
M_{n}(F)
implies
F\in A_{q}
up to an isomorphism. However, it is still unclear whether every element of
F
has to be a
q
-potent or not. Nevertheless, Proposition
3.9
allows us to answer this problem for
q=4
.

Corollary 3.10
.

For a field
F
and an integer
n\geq 7
, the following four issues are equivalent:

(1)

M_{n}(F)
is quasi
4
-nil-clean;

(2)

M_{n}(F)
is
\lambda
-
4
-nil-clean;

(3)

M_{n}(F)
is
4
-nil-clean;

(4)

F\cong\mathbb{F}_{2}
or
F\cong\mathbb{F}_{4}
.

Proof.

(1)\Rightarrow(4)
. Let
p=\Char F
and
q=4
. Since
n>(q-1)+3
, we can apply Proposition
3.9
(2) to get the assertion.

(a) If
\mathrm{gcd}(p,3)
= 1, then
|F|\leq 2^{3}=8
. Thus,
p\in\{2,5,7\}
. Let
f\in M_{n}(F)
be a
q
-potent as in case (b) before Proposition
3.9
; thereby, for any eigenvalue
1\not=\mu
of
f
, we know that
\dim V_{\mu}(f)\leq 1
.

If
p=2
, then a field
F
is a subfield of a splitting field of
x^{4}-x
over
\mathbb{F}_{2}
, i.e.,
F\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{4}
.

If
p=5
, then
F=\mathbb{F}_{5}
. So, the polynomial
x^{4}-x\in\mathbb{F}_{5}[x]
decomposes as
x(x-1)(x^{2}+x+1)
. Set
\alpha,\beta\in\overline{F}
to be the roots of
x^{2}+x+1
. Since the polynomial
x^{2}+x+1\in\mathbb{F}_{5}[x]
is irreducible,
\tr f
can only take one of the following values:

\displaystyle((n-3)+(0+\alpha+\beta))\cdot 1_{\mathbb{F}_{5}},

\displaystyle((n-2)+(\alpha+\beta))\cdot 1_{\mathbb{F}_{5}},

\displaystyle((n-1)+(0))\cdot 1_{\mathbb{F}_{5}},

\displaystyle n\cdot 1_{\mathbb{F}_{5}},

Therefore,
\tr f
can take only
3<|\mathbb{F}_{5}|
different values – a contradiction.

If
p=7
, then
F=\mathbb{F}_{7}
. There are already four
4
-potents in
\mathbb{F}_{7}
, namely
0,1,2,4
. Hence,
\tr f
can only take one of the following values:

\displaystyle((n-3)+(0+2+4))\cdot 1_{\mathbb{F}_{7}},

\displaystyle((n-2)+(0+2))\cdot 1_{\mathbb{F}_{7}},\quad((n-2)+(0+4))\cdot 1_{\mathbb{F}_{7}},\quad((n-2)+(2+4))\cdot 1_{\mathbb{F}_{7}},

\displaystyle((n-1)+(0))\cdot 1_{\mathbb{F}_{7}},\quad((n-1)+(2))\cdot 1_{\mathbb{F}_{7}},\quad((n-1)+(4))\cdot 1_{\mathbb{F}_{7}},

\displaystyle n\cdot 1_{\mathbb{F}_{7}}.

Consequently,
\tr f
can take only
6<|\mathbb{F}_{7}|
different values – a contradiction.

(b) If
p\mid q-1
, then
p=3
and
|F|\leq 2\cdot 3^{-1+3/3}=2
. Thus, there is obviously no such a field

.

(4)\Rightarrow(3)
is precisely
[

, Theorem 2]
. Also, implications

and

are quite trivial, so their verification is voluntarily dropped off.
∎

Similarly to the case of
\mathbb{F}_{5}
in the Corollary

, one can specifically restate Proposition

(2b) as follows: if
\mathrm{gcd}(\Char F,q-1)=1
and

, then
|F|<2^{t-1}
, where

is the number or irreducible factors of
x^{q}-x\in F[x]
.

4.
Weakly Strongly k-Nil-Clean Rings

Let
k>1
be a natural number. Generalizing the basic notion introduced in
[
12
]
, we say that a ring
R
is
weakly strongly
k
-nil-clean
if, for every element
r\in R
, there are
e_{1},\ldots,e_{k}\in Id(R)
and
n\in nil(R)
that all commute such that
r=\pm e_{1}\pm\ldots\pm e_{k}+n
.

The following technical claim is a necessary starting point of view.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
k>1
be a natural number and let
R
be a weakly strongly
k
-nil-clean ring. If
p={\rm char}(R)
is a prime number, then there exists a natural number
t
such that
r^{p^{t}}=r^{p^{t+1}}
for any
r\in R
.

Proof.

Choose
r\in R
. According to the stated conditions, the equality

r=\varepsilon_{1}+\ldots+\varepsilon_{k}+j

holds, where
\varepsilon_{i}\in\{\pm e_{i}\}
for every
1\leq i\leq k
,
e_{1},\ldots,e_{k}\in Id(R)
,
j\in nil(R)
, and the elements
e_{1},\ldots,e_{k},j
commute with each other. Moreover, for some natural number
t
, the equalities
j^{p^{t}}=j^{p^{t+1}}=0
also hold. Since
\varepsilon_{i}^{p^{t}}=\varepsilon_{i}^{p^{t+1}}
, one can write that

r^{p^{t}}=e_{1}^{p^{t}}+\ldots+e_{k}^{p^{t}}=e_{1}^{p^{t+1}}+\ldots+e_{k}^{p^{t+1}}=r^{p^{t+1}},

as needed.
∎

As it is well-known, a ring
R
is called
strongly
\pi
-regular
if, for every element
r\in R
, there is an element
s\in R
such that
r^{n}=sr^{n+1}
for some
n\in\N
.

Our main achievement is this section is the following structural result.

Theorem 4.2
.

Let
k>1
be a fixed natural number. If
R
is a weakly strongly
k
-nil-clean ring, then
R
is a strongly
\pi
-regular ring which is isomorphic to a finite direct product of rings
S
, each of which satisfies the following conditions:

(a)

J(S)
is a nil-ideal;

(b)

Every right primitive ideal
I
of
S
is maximal, and the quotient ring
S/I\cong\F_{p}
, where
p\leq 2k+1
;

(c)

For some prime
p
, the quotient ring
S/J(S)
is a subdirect product of the field
\F_{p}
and
p\leq 2k+1
;

(d)

If for some natural number
m
the ring
(\F_{p})^{m}
is not a weakly strongly
k
-nil-clean ring, then
S/J(S)\cong(\F_{p})^{k}
, where
k<m
.

Proof.

We intend to show that there will exist smallest natural number
N
for which the equality
N\cdot 1_{R}=0
is true. To that purpose, for some commuting idempotents
e_{1},\ldots,e_{k}
and a nilpotent
j
from the ring
R
, the equality

(k+1)\cdot 1_{R}=\varepsilon_{1}+\ldots+\varepsilon_{k}+j

is fulfilled, where
\varepsilon_{i}\in\{\pm e_{i}\}
for each
1\leq i\leq k
. Furthermore, for an arbitrary subset
A
of the set
I=\{1,\ldots,k\},
we put

e_{A}:=\prod_{i\in A}e_{i}\prod_{i\in I\setminus A}(1-e_{i}).

Since

1_{R}=\prod_{i\in I}(e_{i}+(1-e_{i}))=\sum_{A\in 2^{I}}e_{A},

one inspects that

(k+1)\cdot 1_{R}=(\varepsilon_{1}+\ldots+\varepsilon_{k}+j)\sum_{A\in 2^{I}}e_{A}=\sum_{A\in 2^{I}}N_{A}e_{A}+j,

where
N_{A}\in\Z
and
\mid N_{A}\mid\leq k
for every
A\in 2^{I}.
Then,

j=\sum_{A\in 2^{I}}(k+1-N_{A})e_{A}.

Since
(e_{A})_{A\in 2^{I}}
is an orthogonal system of idempotents, for some natural number
n
, the following equalities are valid:

0=j^{n}=\sum_{A\in 2^{I}}(k+1-N_{A})^{n}e_{A}.

Therefore,
(k+1-N_{A})^{n}e_{A}=0
for any
A\in 2^{I}
and so

[1,\ldots,2k+1]^{n}\cdot 1_{R}=\sum_{A\in 2^{I}}[1,\ldots,2k+1]^{n}e_{A}=0.

Thus, indeed, there exists smallest natural number
N
such that the equality
N\cdot 1_{R}=0
is realizable, as claimed.

Writing now
N=p_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}\ldots p_{m}^{\alpha_{m}}
, where
p_{1},\ldots,p_{m}
are pairwise distinct primes, the classical Chinese Remainder Theorem reaches the existence of a ring isomorphism

R\cong R_{1}\times\ldots\times R_{m},

where
p_{i}^{\alpha_{i}}\cdot 1_{R_{i}}=0
for every
1\leq i\leq m
. Next, for an arbitrary
1\leq i\leq m
, we manage to prove that each of the rings
R_{i}
is strongly
\pi
-regular. To substantiate this, choose
r\in R_{i}
. So, Lemma
4.1
teaches us that, for some natural numbers
n_{1},n_{2}
with
n_{1}>n_{2}
, the equality
\overline{r}^{n_{1}}=\overline{r}^{n_{2}}
holds, where
\overline{r}:=r+p_{i}R_{i}\in R_{i}/p_{i}R_{i}
. That is why, the equality
(r^{n_{1}}-r^{n_{2}})^{\alpha_{i}}=0
holds as well and, consequently, for some polynomial
f(x)\in\Z[x]
, we deduce

r^{n_{2}\alpha_{i}}=r^{n_{2}\alpha_{i}+1}f(r).

Now, since a finite direct product of strongly
\pi
-regular rings remains a strongly
\pi
-regular ring, it must be that
R
is a strongly
\pi
-regular ring too. Also, since the Jacobson radical of an arbitrary
\pi
-regular ring is known to be a nil-ideal,
J(R)
is too a nil-ideal.

Furthermore, we fix an arbitrary index
i\in\{1,\ldots,m\}
. We show that every right primitive factor of the ring
R_{i}
is isomorphic to the field
\F_{p_{i}}
. In fact, let
I
be a right primitive ideal of the ring
R_{i}
, and suppose
a
is an arbitrary element of the ring
R_{i}/I
. Then, for some commuting idempotents
f_{1},\ldots,f_{k}\in R_{i}/I
, and a nilpotent
j_{0}\in R_{i}/I
, the equality

a=f_{1}^{\prime}+\ldots+f_{k}^{\prime}+j_{0},

where
f_{i}^{\prime}\in\{\pm f_{i}\}
, will hold for each
1\leq i\leq k
. But, since the ring
R_{i}/I
has characteristic
p_{i}
, the equality
a^{p_{i}^{n}}=a^{p_{i}^{n+1}}
is obviously satisfied for some natural number
n
, as required.

Suppose now that
R_{i}/I
is
not
a division ring. Since the field
\mathbb{F}_{p^{2}_{i}}
can be embedded in the matrix ring
M_{2}(\mathbb{F}_{p_{i}})
, there is an invertible matrix
A
from
M_{2}(\mathbb{F}_{p_{i}})
whose order is exactly
p_{i}+1
. On the other hand, from the reasoning given above and looking at
[
18
, 11.19]
, it follows for some
n\in\mathbb{M}
that the equality
A^{p_{i}^{n+1}-p_{i}^{n}}=E
is fulfilled, where
E
is the standard identity matrix. If, for a moment,
p_{i}
is an odd prime, then
(p_{i}+1,p_{i}^{n+1}-p_{i}^{n})=2
and, thereby,
A^{2}=E
, which contradicts the choice of matrix
A
. If, however,
p_{i}=2
, then
(p_{i}+1,p_{i}^{n+1}-p_{i}^{n})=1
, which is manifestly also impossible. So,
R_{i}/I
is a division ring, as suspected. Since
R_{i}/I
is a weakly strongly
k
-nil-clean ring and
0,1
are the only idempotents of
R_{i}/I
, one concludes that

R_{i}/I\cong\F_{p}

and
p\leq 2k+1
, as formulated above.

Suppose next that, for some natural number
m
, the ring
(\F_{p})^{m}
is
not
a weakly strongly
k
-nil-clean ring. Since, same as above, every right primitive ideal is maximal, it again follows from the Chinese Remainder Theorem that the set of all right primitive ideals of the ring
R
consists of at most
m-1
elements. In particular, we deduce

R_{i}/J(R_{i})\cong(\F_{p})^{k},

where
k<m
, as asserted.
∎

A logical question of important interest, in order to obtain an eventual necessary and sufficient condition, is whether or not the statement in the last theorem can be reversed? At this stage, we are unable to conjecture the way of a probable validity.

Further, as a series of subsequent corollaries, we extract the following ones.

Corollary 4.3
.

If
R
is a weakly strongly
k
-nil-clean ring for some
k\in\N
, then
J(R)=nil(R)
.

The next consequence also appeared in
[
9
]
in a slightly more weak form concerning only
abelian
rings.

Corollary 4.4
.

[
15
, Theorem 1]

The following conditions are equivalent for a ring
R
:

(1)

R
is strongly weakly nil-clean;

(2)

R
is isomorphic to either
R_{1}
,
R_{2}
, or
R_{1}\times R_{2}
, where

(a)

R_{1}
is strongly nil-clean, that is,
J(R_{1})
is nil and
R_{1}/J(R_{1})
is Boolean;

(b)

R_{2}/J(R_{2})\cong\F_{3}
and
J(R_{2})
is nil.

Proof.

(1)\Rightarrow(2)
. Since the element
(1,-1)\in\F_{3}\times\F_{3}
manifestly does not have the form
\pm e
, where
e
is an idempotent in the ring
\F_{3}\times\F_{3}
, the ring
\F_{3}\times\F_{3}
is not strongly weakly nil-clean. The desired implication then follows directly from Theorem
4.2
.

(2)\Rightarrow(1)
. Write
R=R_{1}\times R_{2}
, where the rings
R_{1}
and
R_{2}
satisfy conditions (a) and (b) of point (2), respectively. We now menage to establish that
R
is a strongly weakly nil-clean ring. To that target, assume
a\in R
and set
\overline{a}:=a+J(R)
.

Knowing that
R/J(R)
is isomorphic to the direct product of the field
\F_{3}
and a ring which is a subdirect product of the field
\F_{2}
, it thus follows from the proof of Theorem
4.2
that the ring
\Z[\overline{a}]
is finite and, therefore, is contained in a subring
S
of the ring
R/J(R)
which is isomorphic to the ring
S=\F_{2}^{n}\times\F_{3}
, where
n\in\N
. Furthermore, a direct inspection verifies that
S
is a strongly weakly nil-clean ring. Moreover, since
J(R)\cap\Z[\overline{a}]
is a nilpotent ideal of the ring
\Z[\overline{a}]
, the idempotents of the ring
\Z[\overline{a}]
lift modulo the ideal
J(R)\cap\Z[\overline{a}]
and, thus,
a
is the sum or the difference of a
nilpotent and an idempotent that commute with each other, as required.
∎

Corollary 4.5
.

[
12
, Theorem 2.26]

The following conditions are equivalent for a ring
R
:

(1)

R
is weakly strongly 2-nil-clean;

(2)

R
is isomorphic to either
R_{1}
,
R_{2}
, or
R_{1}\times R_{2}
, where

(i)

R_{1}
is strongly 2-nil-clean, that is,
J(R_{1})
is nil and
R_{1}/J(R_{1})
is 3-potent ring;

(ii)

R_{2}/J(R_{2})\cong\F_{5}
and
J(R_{2})
is nil.

Proof.

(1)\Rightarrow(2)
. Since the element
(1,3)\in\F_{5}\times\F_{5}
manifestly does not have the form
\pm e\pm f
, where
e,f
are idempotents in the ring
\F_{5}\times\F_{5}
, then the ring
\F_{5}\times\F_{5}
is not weakly strongly 2-nil-clean. Then, the implication follows immediately from Theorem
4.2
.

(2)\Rightarrow(1)
. Let the rings
R_{1}
and
R_{2}
satisfy conditions (i) and (ii) of point (2), respectively. It is straightforward to verify that the ring
(\F_{2})^{n}\times(\F_{3})^{m}\times\F_{5}
is a weakly strongly 2-nil-clean ring for arbitrary
n,m\in\N
. Then, using arguments similar to those given in proving implication
(2)\Rightarrow(1)
of Corollary
4.4
, we can straightforwardly demonstrate that the direct product
R_{1}\times R_{2}
is a weakly strongly 2-nil-clean ring, as asked for.
∎

Our final consequence is the following one.

Corollary 4.6
.

The next conditions are equivalent for a ring
R
:

(1)

R
is weakly strongly 3-nil-clean;

(2)

R
is isomorphic to either
R_{1}
,
R_{2}
,
R_{3}
, or
R_{1}\times R_{2}
, or
R_{1}\times R_{3}
, where

(i)

R_{1}
is strongly 2-nil-clean;

(ii)

R_{2}/J(R_{2})\cong(\F_{5})^{m}
, where
m\leq 3
and
J(R_{2})
is nil;

(iii)

R_{3}/J(R_{3})\cong\F_{7}
and
J(R_{3})
is nil.

Proof.

(1)\Rightarrow(2)
. Since the element

(1,-3)\in\F_{5}\times\F_{7}

manifestly does not have the form
\pm e\pm f\pm g
, where
e,f,g
are idempotents in the ring
\F_{5}\times\F_{7}
, it follows that the ring
\F_{5}\times\F_{7}
is not weakly strongly 3-nil-clean. It is, likewise, similarly shown that the ring
\F_{7}\times\F_{7}
is not weakly strongly 3-nil-clean.

Besides, it is directly verified that the element

(1,-1,2,-2)\in(\F_{5})^{4}

does not have the form
\pm e\pm f\pm g
, where
e,f,g
are idempotents in the ring
(\F_{5})^{4}
, whence the ring
(\F_{5})^{4}
is not weakly strongly 3-nil-clean. Then, the implication follows automatically from Theorem
4.2
.

(2)\Rightarrow(1)
. Let us assume that the rings
R_{1}

R_{2}

5.
Concluding Discussion and Further Work

In this final section, in regard to the posed above concrete questions and problems, we now comment some further perspectives of the results we had obtained so far, hoping eventually that their generative expansions could be true, thereby hopefully stimulating an intensive research study of the presented subject in the very near future. Explicitly, the more attractive things that we have achieved above suggest to describe those rings for which each element is the sum of a unit and a linear combination of idempotents with coefficients in
\mathbb{Z}
commuting all together, so the leitmotif here is to ask where or not such rings could be classified in terms of the Jacobson radical and (sub)direct products of already well-characterized classes of rings. So, as a culmination of our recent considerations, we conjecture that this can occur for at least endomorphism and matrix rings happening that their complete description is achievable.
