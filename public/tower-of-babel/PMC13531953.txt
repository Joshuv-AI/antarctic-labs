A case report of nitrous oxide-induced subacute combined degeneration complicated with neurosyphilis in a 23-year-old female
Authors: Wei Li, Lirong Wu, Jing Chen, Jun Tang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundRecreational nitrous oxide (N₂O) abuse has become a common health issue in young adults, which can induce subacute combined degeneration (SCD) of the spinal cord. Neurosyphilis has highly similar clinical and imaging manifestations with SCD. The coexistence of N₂O-induced SCD and asymptomatic neurosyphilis is extremely rare and easily overlooked. We herein report such a case to improve clinical diagnosis.Case presentationA 23-year-old female presented with 3-month progressive limb numbness and gait instability, with a 6-month history of N₂O inhalation. Spinal MRI showed typical “inverted V-sign” of SCD. Further examinations confirmed asymptomatic neurosyphilis, severe vitamin B12 deficiency and hyperhomocysteinemia. After standard combined treatment, her neurological function was significantly improved with no recurrence during follow-up.ConclusionFor young patients with spinal cord lesions, screening for N₂O abuse, vitamin B12 and syphilis should be performed simultaneously. Early diagnosis and combined intervention can effectively avoid permanent neurological damage.Keywords: Nitrous oxide abuse, Subacute combined degeneration, Neurosyphilis, Inverted V-sign, Vitamin B12 deficiency, Young female

Introduction

Subacute combined degeneration of the spinal cord is a demyelinating disease affecting the posterior and lateral columns of the spinal cord caused by impaired absorption or metabolism of vitamin B12. In recent years, with the increasing recreational abuse of N₂O, the incidence of non-nutritional SCD in young adults has risen year by year [1, 2]. N₂O irreversibly oxidizes the cobalt ion in vitamin B12 molecules, rendering it biologically inactive, and can induce neurological damage even with normal serum vitamin B12 levels [3, 4].

Neurosyphilis is caused by Treponema pallidum invading the central nervous system, and the meningomyelitis type can involve the posterior and lateral columns of the spinal cord. Its clinical manifestations are highly similar to those of SCD, and single etiology diagnosis is prone to missing comorbidities and delaying treatment [5]. Cases of N₂O-induced subacute combined degeneration (SCD) complicated with neurosyphilis remain extremely rare, with only sporadic reports published worldwide, leading to very low clinical awareness [6, 7]. In accordance with the SCARE 2023 case report guidelines [8], this paper reports a 23-year-old female case of N₂O abuse-related SCD complicated with neurosyphilis, and analyzes the history, auxiliary examinations, diagnosis, treatment and follow-up results to provide a reference for the differential diagnosis and treatment of similar clinical cases.

Case presentation

A 23-year-old female, a clothing salesperson, was admitted due to “progressive numbness of limbs with unsteady gait for 3 months, aggravated for 2 weeks”. The patient had inhaled N₂O (whipped cream chargers) for 6 months before onset, 3–4 times a week, 10–15 cartridges each time, with no history of gastrointestinal diseases or blood transfusion.

Three months before admission, she developed numbness and tingling sensation in both feet, which gradually ascended upwards to involve proximal lower limbs and both hands, accompanied by weakness of lower limbs and clumsiness of fine movements (e.g., difficulty in arranging clothes and buttoning). Two weeks before admission, she developed unsteady gait and felt like walking on cotton, requiring assistance to walk, without bladder and bowel involvement, headache, fever or cognitive abnormality.

Upon further inquiry, she reported engaging in unprotected sexual intercourse with multiple partners from 11 months to 5 months before admission (approximately 8 to 2 months before symptom onset), denying vulvar ulcers, rashes, or other syphilis-related symptoms. Vital signs were stable, no rash or ulcer on the skin and mucous membranes, and no superficial lymphadenopathy. Neurological examination: cranial nerves were normal; pupils were equal, round, and reactive to light and accommodation. Argyll Robertson pupil was not observed. Fundoscopic examination revealed no optic disc edema, pallor, or other abnormalities. Muscle tone of both lower limbs was decreased, proximal muscle strength was grade 4, distal muscle strength was grade 4-, and upper limb muscle strength was grade 5 bilaterally; decreased vibration sense and joint position sense below the bilateral hip joints, and glove-stocking hypoesthesia; knee and ankle jerks were absent, upper limb tendon reflexes were diminished; romberg sign was positive, heel-knee-tibia test was unstable, and meningeal irritation signs were negative.

Imaging

Cervicothoracic spinal MRI (T2-weighted imaging) demonstrated symmetric hyperintensity involving the posterior and lateral columns of the spinal cord from C2 to C7, with a characteristic “inverted V-sign” on axial sections, suggestive of demyelinating changes. Long-segment linear hyperintensity was also seen in the sagittal T2WI. Brain MRI showed no intracranial lesions or abnormal meningeal signals, with no imaging evidence indicating intracranial syphilitic involvement (Fig. 1).

Supplementary laboratory tests

These laboratory findings (Table 1) provide key evidence for the final diagnosis. Complete blood count indicated mild macrocytic anemia (hemoglobin 112 g/L, mean corpuscular volume 105.2 fL). Serum vitamin B12 was 89 pg/mL, below the normal reference range, indicating severe deficiency. Homocysteine was 48.6 µmol/L, notably above the normal range, representing significant elevation. CSF glucose was 3.2 mmol/L (normal reference range: 2.5–4.4 mmol/L), within normal limits. Screening for HIV, hepatitis B virus (HBV), and hepatitis C virus (HCV) was all negative. These biochemical changes are typical of nitrous oxide–induced subacute combined degeneration (SCD). Positive syphilis serology in both serum and CSF, together with mild lymphocytic pleocytosis and elevated protein in CSF, confirms asymptomatic neurosyphilis. Electromyography showed denervation potentials and chronic neurogenic changes without myopathy; nerve conduction studies revealed sensory-dominant mixed axonal and demyelinating injury with absent H-reflex and F-wave.

Final diagnosis

1. Subacute combined degeneration of the spinal cord caused by nitrous oxide abuse (severe vitamin B12 deficiency); 2. Asymptomatic neurosyphilis.

Differential diagnosis: Multiple sclerosis, neuromyelitis optica spectrum disorder (NMOSD), and MOG antibody-associated disease (MOGAD) were excluded by negative serum aquaporin-4 (AQP4) antibody and myelin oligodendrocyte glycoprotein (MOG) antibody, as well as absent cerebrospinal fluid oligoclonal bands.Spinal cord compression was excluded by MRI.Other nutritional myelopathies were systematically excluded: serum folate and copper levels were normal; anti-intrinsic factor antibody and anti-parietal cell antibody were negative, excluding pernicious anemia.The clinical, imaging, and laboratory findings were fully explained by nitrous oxide-induced subacute combined degeneration combined with asymptomatic neurosyphilis.

The patient received multidisciplinary combined treatment: (1) Anti-syphilitic treatment: Aqueous crystalline penicillin G 4 million U intravenous infusion, once every 4 h for 14 consecutive days; (2) Vitamin B12 supplementation: Cyanocobalamin 1000 µg intramuscular injection, once a day for 2 weeks, followed by maintenance once a week; (3) Addiction intervention: Cognitive behavioral therapy, strict abstinence from N₂O; (4) Rehabilitation training: Limb muscle strength and balance function training.

Reexamination after 8 weeks of treatment: The patient’s limb numbness was significantly relieved, she could walk independently with basically normal gait; serum vitamin B12 increased to 356 pg/mL, homocysteine decreased to 16.2 µmol/L, TRUST titer decreased to 1:8; spinal MRI showed marked reduction of the abnormal T2 hyperintensity in the posterior spinal cord at C2-7, with the characteristic “inverted V-sign” almost completely resolved (Fig. 2). At 6-month follow-up, the patient persisted in abstaining from N₂O, no neurological symptoms recurred, and neurological function continued to recover.

Discussion

N₂O abuse-related subacute combined degeneration (SCD) complicated with asymptomatic neurosyphilis represents an extremely rare dual-etiology myelopathy with considerable diagnostic ambiguity. The overlapping anatomical predilection of both disorders for the spinal posterior and lateral columns, together with the completely silent clinical presentation of neurosyphilis, creates a high risk of missed diagnosis or delayed etiological identification [9, 10]. Unlike typical concurrent neurological disorders, the infectious component in this case caused no overt clinical symptoms despite radiological involvement, which adds a unique layer of difficulty to early etiological judgment and distinguishes this case from previously reported cases with symptomatic neurosyphilis.

The fundamental pathogenesis of N₂O-induced SCD is the functional inactivation of vitamin B12 rather than dietary deficiency. N₂O oxidizes the cobalt ion in methylcobalamin, thereby inhibiting methionine synthase and disturbing myelin synthesis and maintenance, which explains why typical SCD can develop even with adequate oral intake and relatively preserved serum vitamin B12 levels [11]. The “inverted V-sign” on axial MRI is widely recognized as a hallmark of SCD, yet it is not disease-specific. Similar symmetric dorsal column hyperintensity can be observed in patients with syphilitic myelitis, which necessitates confirmatory serological and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) tests for precise differentiation [4, 12, 13]. Radiological features further help distinguish the two conditions: SCD usually presents as symmetric long-segment T2 hyperintensity without cord expansion or meningeal enhancement, whereas syphilitic myelitis often shows patchy lesions, cord swelling, or leptomeningeal enhancement [14]. In this patient, imaging findings were entirely consistent with SCD, and asymptomatic neurosyphilis was detected only through CSF screening, indicating that exclusive reliance on radiological features may overlook occult central nervous system infection.

Treatment of this rare combination requires targeted and combined intervention instead of empirical dual therapy. High-dose parenteral vitamin B12 supplementation and complete N₂O abstinence constitute the core strategy to reverse metabolic demyelination caused by SCD, while anti-syphilitic penicillin therapy is administered to eradicate latent treponemal infection and prevent future neurological complications [15, 16]. The satisfactory neurological and radiological recovery in this patient confirms that early etiological clarification and standardized treatment can effectively prevent irreversible axonal damage, which is consistent with previous evidence in the literature [6–9].

This case provides important clinical lessons for young patients presenting with acute myelopathy. Detailed history taking regarding N₂O abuse and high-risk sexual behavior, combined with routine CSF examination, is essential for detecting asymptomatic comorbidities. Our report underscores that the “inverted V-sign” indicates dorsal column vulnerability rather than a specific diagnosis, and comprehensive laboratory screening remains indispensable in accurate etiological classification.

Conclusion

N₂O-induced SCD complicated with neurosyphilis is a rare association of dual etiology affecting spinal cord with highly overlapping clinical and imaging features, which is prone to misdiagnosis and delayed treatment. For young patients with spinal cord dysfunction, routine screening of N₂O exposure history, vitamin B12 metabolic indicators, syphilis serology and CSF indicators should be performed. Early diagnosis and combined treatment of anti-syphilis, vitamin supplementation and abstinence can effectively improve neurological function and prevent long-term disability.

Limitation

This study has several limitations. First, contrast-enhanced MRI of the brain and spinal cord was not performed, which restricted the definitive evaluation of meningeal enhancement associated with neurosyphilis. Second, this was a single case report with a relatively short follow-up period; therefore, the long-term neurological prognosis and radiological recovery pattern require further observation. Future clinical practice should routinely perform contrast-enhanced MRI in young patients with suspected spinal cord lesions combined with syphilis infection to improve diagnostic accuracy.
