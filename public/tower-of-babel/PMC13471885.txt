The Use of the Four Square Step Test and the 10‐m Walk/Run Test to Determine Fall Risk in Children With Duchenne Muscular Dystrophy
Authors: Numan Bulut, Esra Aldırmaz, İpek Alemdaroğlu‐Gürbüz, Öznur Tunca
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroduction/AimsThe identification of the risk of falling in Duchenne muscular dystrophy (DMD) is essential for the implementation of timely preventive approaches. This study aimed to examine the ability of the four square step test (FSST) and the 10‐m walk/run test (10MWRT) to discriminate between fallers and non‐fallers in children with DMD.MethodsNinety‐five children with DMD were included in this study. Functional level was determined with the Vignos Scale. Children who had fallen at least once in the previous month were categorized as “fallers” (n = 50), while those who had not fallen were categorized as “non‐fallers” (n = 45). The FSST and 10MWRT were performed. The Mann–Whitney U test was performed to examine group differences in the FSST and 10MWRT scores. The predictive values of the FSST and 10MWRT were determined with receiver operating characteristic curves.ResultsThe demographic characteristics of fallers (median age: 9.50 years; median body mass index [BMI]: 19.02 kg/m2) and non‐fallers (median age: 9.00 years; median BMI: 17.95) were similar (p > 0.05). Fallers demonstrated lower functional levels compared with non‐fallers (p < 0.001). The FSST for fallers was 20.04 ± 12.57 s while that for non‐fallers was 11.44 ± 2.94 s with a cut‐off point of 13.10 s (p < 0.05). The 10MWRT for fallers and non‐fallers were 9.42 ± 4.77 and 5.36 ± 2.10 s, respectively, with a cut‐off point of 5.60 s (p < 0.05).DiscussionThe two outcome measures were shown to be sensitive and specific in determining the risk of falls in children with DMD. The cut‐off points may guide professionals in planning a proactive rehabilitation program to reduce fall risk or minimize subsequent injuries.Keywords: fall, gait, muscular dystrophy, physical therapy, timed functional test

Introduction

Duchenne muscular dystrophy (DMD) is a pediatric neuromuscular disease characterized by early symptoms such as delayed gross motor development, frequent falls, gait abnormalities, and difficulty in arising from the floor [1, 2].

Falls are one of the most common causes of injury, occurring in approximately one‐third of children, with a higher risk in children with physical disabilities [3, 4]. Fractures due to falls related to muscle weakness are observed in 27%–43% of cases in DMD [5, 6]. It has been reported that falls reduce the quality of life, make daily living activities difficult, and reduce the level of physical activity [7]. Falls, fall‐related fractures, and the fear of falling worsen the prognosis of the disease by limiting activity in children with DMD [7, 8]. In this respect, predicting falls and taking precautions can significantly reduce the incidence of subsequent complications.

Timed performance tests are used for various purposes in neuromuscular diseases, such as treatment planning, monitoring, and tracking the prognosis of the disease under different circumstances [1, 9, 10]. In recent years, they have also been used to estimate the age at which the patient loses independent ambulation and to predict falls [11, 12]. In a study that aimed to investigate the cut‐off point of the timed up and go test for identifying the risk of falling in adults with neuromuscular disorders, this value was reported to be 8.9 s [11]. It has been reported that children with Down syndrome who performed the four square step test (FSST) in more than 15 s were at high risk of falling [13]. In DMD, practical clinical methods such as the 10‐m walk/run test (10MWRT) and the FSST are valid and reliable measures for assessing both function and balance effectively [9, 14]. However, no outcome measure is reported to be used to predict the risk of falls in children with DMD.

In this study, we aimed to evaluate the ability of the FSST and the 10MWRT to discriminate between fallers and non‐fallers with DMD. It was hypothesized that timed performance tests such as the FSST and 10MWRT would identify fall risk with high sensitivity and specificity.

Methods

Design

This cross‐sectional study was carried out in the pediatric neuromuscular unit at the Faculty of Physical Therapy and Rehabilitation, and was approved by the Health Sciences Research Ethics Committee (Ethical Approval Number: SBA 24/320). Written informed consent was obtained from both the children and their parents.

Participants

The study included children with genetically confirmed DMD who were (a) between 5 and 12 years of age, and (b) ambulant (Vignos scale < Grade 6) [15]. Children with (a) an additional chronic disease (attention‐deficit disorders or attention deficit/hyperactivity disorders, cerebral palsy, and autism spectrum disorders), (b) severe lower extremity contractures (ankle contracture > 30° etc.), (c) the inability to perform the FSST within three attempts, (d) a history of serious injury (fracture, etc.), and/or surgery in the lower extremities in the last 6 months, and (e) poor cooperation were not included in the study.

Outcomes

The assessments of the children were performed in one session, with their demographic and descriptive characteristics, such as age, height, weight, dominant hand, and lower extremity range of motion being recorded. The use of night splints and steroids was also noted. For the participants' safety, all tests were conducted by two physiotherapists who took all necessary precautions, and sufficient rest intervals were provided between tests to prevent fatigue. The evaluations were performed in the following order:

Functional Level

The Vignos Scale evaluates the functional level of children with DMD in 10 grades. Grade 1 indicates that children can walk and climb stairs without support, while Grade 10 suggests that children are confined to bed [15].

Fall Characteristics

The past month's fall history of the children was established. Children who had fallen at least once in the previous month were categorized as “fallers,” while those who had not fallen were categorized as “non‐fallers” [11]. In the fallers group, fall characteristics such as activity during the fall, cause and location of the fall were reported by children or parents. The characteristics of the most serious fall were investigated in children with a history of multiple falls within last month.

Four Square Step Test (FSST)

The FSST has been developed for the assessment of fall risk in older adults through the measurement of dynamic balance [16]. The FSST has recently been shown to be a valid and reliable measure with a high ICC value for assessing dynamic balance in children with DMD [14]. To implement the test, 4 × 90‐cm‐long sticks are placed on the floor at 90° to each other, forming a “+” symbol. An area of four squares is created around the “+” symbol and numbered 1 to 4. The children were given a trial run to make sure they understood the task. They were allowed a maximum of three attempts, and the fastest of their two valid attempts was recorded. During the test, each child was asked to stand on Square 1 and to look at Square 2. The child was then instructed to step forward, right, back and finally left into each square, clockwise “2,3,4,1” and then counter‐clockwise “4,3,2,1.” Timing started with the first foot movement in Square 1. The child was asked to look straight ahead throughout the movement, if possible, and to allow both feet to touch in each square before moving on to the next square as quickly as possible. The key principle of the test was that the child always looked forward—in other words, the child never changed direction during the test. A red circle was attached to the wall as a cue to maintain forward gaze throughout the test [17]. If the child could not complete the sequence correctly without errors, lost balance, touched a stick, or failed to touch the ground with both feet in each frame, the trial was stopped and restarted.

10‐m Walk/Run Test (10MWRT)

Each child was asked to walk/run the 10‐m distance as quickly as possible. Tape was used to mark the starting and finishing lines on the floor. To prevent deceleration before the end of the test, each child was instructed to keep walking or running after crossing the finish line. Timing began when the child's leading foot crossed the start line and ended when the same foot crossed the finish line. Only one trial was performed, and the completion time was recorded in seconds. The 10MWRT has been shown to have high test–retest reliability (ICC: 0.85) and concurrent validity (r = 0.69–0.86) in DMD [9].

Statistical Analysis

SPSS Statistics 26 (IBM, Armonk, New York, USA) was used for statistical analysis. The distribution of the analyzed was evaluated for normality, using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. Numerical data were expressed as “mean ± standard deviation (X ± SD)” in parametric condition, while data were expressed as “median and interquartile range” in nonparametric condition. For nominal and ordinal data, the number (n) and percentage (%) values were stated. The comparison of numerical data of fallers and non‐fallers deployed the Independent Sample(s) t‐test or Mann–Whitney U Test. The chi‐squared Test or Fisher's Exact Test was used for comparing nominal/ordinal data, and p < 0.05 was accepted as the level of statistical significance.

The predictive value of the 10MWRT and FSST was calculated with the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve and the area under the ROC curve (AUC). An AUC value of 0.50 states nonsensitivity, while a value close to 1.00 represents excellent sensitivity and specificity [18]. To demonstrate the adequate validity of a scale, an AUC value greater than 0.70 is recommended [19]. The suitable cut‐off point for the FSST and 10MWRT has been defined by taking the value that is close to 1 in the Youden index (J) method (J = sensitivity + specificity −1) [20].

To determine the exact power of the study, a post hoc analysis was performed using R version 4.4.0 (package “pROC,” R Core Team, 2024). For the ROC analyzes, the post hoc power was 89.65% for the FSST (n fallers = 50, n non‐fallers = 45, AUC = 0.789, significance level = 0.0001) and 99.77% for 10MWRT (n fallers = 50, n non‐fallers = 45, AUC = 0.852, significance level = 0.0001). Overall, the power achieved was sufficient to draw conclusions based on the data collected.

Discussion

This study demonstrated that both the FSST and the 10MWRT could discriminate between fallers and non‐fallers in children with DMD, with the cut‐off points of 13.10 s (FSST) and 5.60 s (10MWRT). We also found that falls were most common outside while walking and were usually caused by weakness.

Children with DMD experience progressive involvement of the proximal muscles, which reduces their ability to control movements against gravity, leading to muscle weakness, frequent falls with possible injury, and increased insecurity [21]. The high rate of multiple falls also supports this view. In addition, the fact that children/parents with DMD in this study reported muscle weakness as the most common reason for falling supported these findings and showed that individuals/families were aware of the severe effects of muscle weakness. Fall‐related fractures have been reported to cause premature loss of ambulation in individuals with DMD [22]. Weakness in the hip and proximal thigh muscles leads to decreased hip abduction and flexion during the swing phase of walking, which reduces the height of the foot from the ground, thereby increasing the risk of tripping [23]. Using simple and short tests to assess fall risk may enable timely implementation of precautions to prevent potential injuries and inform the design of appropriate rehabilitation programs.

The FSST is not only recognized as a reliable and valid outcome measure in a range of adult and pediatric populations [13, 16, 17, 24, 25, 26], but a recent study also indicated its reliability in measuring functional mobility and balance in DMD patients, and highlighted its suitability for this purpose in clinical settings and studies [14]. For dynamic balance assessment, the FSST has been used to evaluate the fall risk in other conditions, such as Parkinson's disease and Down syndrome. Duncan et al. [27] reported in their Parkinson's study that the FSST had a cut‐off point of 9.68 s to discriminate between fallers and non‐fallers. In the present study, a lower value was identified for children with DMD compared to the cut‐off point reported for fall risk in children with Down syndrome [13]. The diversity of cut‐off points of the FSST which was used to determine fall risk in different populations might be explained by variations in disease pathology, age of the populations, and functional levels.

The easy‐to‐use 10MWRT test, which is widely applied in routine outpatient settings for people with DMD, is often preferred by professionals for showing functional status and assisting in prognosis assessment [10, 12]. It was found that the 10MWRT was associated with the functional decline in a 6‐min walk test distance and the loss of ambulation [1, 10]. For 10MWRT, the cut‐off points to make a distinction between ambulatory and nonambulatory DMD children are expressed as a 10% increase compared to the previous evaluation [12]. A 10MWRT time exceeding 5.60 s was determined as a cut‐off point in children with DMD, reflecting both functional deterioration and increased fall risk, which underscores the necessity of reassessing rehabilitation programs and fall prevention strategies in this population.

Higher scores on the Vignos Scale, reflecting lower functional levels, have a negative effect on balance in children with DMD. It has been reported that the dynamic balance performance of children with DMD who are at Grade 3 on the Vignos Scale demonstrates approximately one‐third lower dynamic balance performance than their non‐DMD peers [28]. The current study found, as expected, that the functional level of the fallers was lower than the non‐fallers, as expected. Horlings et al. [29] reported that children with DMD experienced loss of function and balance problems due to progressive muscle weakness. As the level of the Vignos Scale increases, dynamic balance is adversely affected, the risk of falling increases, and the completion of timed performance tests takes approximately twice as long as that of non‐fallers. It was found that the time taken to complete the test increased as the functional level deteriorated in children with DMD [14]. Similarly, it has been reported that children with cerebral palsy and higher functional levels complete the FSST more quickly [17]. These findings are consistent with the results of the current study. However, the similar age distribution observed between fallers and non‐fallers may suggest that even younger ambulatory children with DMD are at risk of falling. DMD fall risk may therefore be attributed to multiple factors beyond age, such as functional level, genotype, cognitive abilities, level of physical activity, and environmental conditions. With the exception of functional level, these factors were not assessed in the present study, which may be considered a limitation. Another limitation of the study is that typically developing children were not included, which prevents normative comparisons and limits the interpretation of the established cut‐off points. A further limitation concerns the implementation of the FSST as two participants were unable to complete the test. This suggests that test requirements (single‐leg balance etc.) should be considered when defining inclusion criteria in future studies.

Conclusion

In this study, the cut‐off points of the FSST and 10MWRT represent the thresholds above which children with DMD showed an increased risk of falling. These cut‐off points demonstrated high sensitivity and specificity in distinguishing between fallers and non‐fallers. Awareness of these cut‐off points may help identify fall risk at an early stage, guide the planning of tailored rehabilitation programs, and facilitate appropriate decisions regarding further assessment and adjustments, ultimately helping to minimize the potential negative outcomes of falls in children with DMD. There is a need for studies to determine cut‐off points in different pediatric neuromuscular diseases across a broader age range.
