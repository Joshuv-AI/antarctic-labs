The Path Less Traveled: Embracing the Arts in Scientific Communications
Authors: Laura Banks
Published: 2026

The Canadian Journal of Cardiology (CJC) Open is a “vehicle for the international dissemination of new knowledge in cardiology and cardiovascular science, particularly serving as the major open-access publication venue for Canadian cardiovascular medicine.”1 CJC Open routinely publishes academically rigorous, peer-reviewed communications in the form of original research articles, case reports, and reviews; however, we also embrace the possibilities of diverse knowledge translation activities that can be equally rigorous. According to the Canadian Institutes of Health Research (2014), knowledge translation can be broadly defined as a “dynamic and iterative process that includes synthesis, dissemination, exchange, and ethically sound application of knowledge to improve the health of [a population] to provide more effective health services/products and strengthen our healthcare system.”2

Embracing Authentic Research Communications with Storytelling

In this issue, Zhao and colleagues3 embrace the academic path less traveled, presenting their novel research titled “Heart Health Begins with Community:

An Arts-Based Report on the Experiences of Heart Health in the Moosonee Community.”

Researchers from the University Health Network (Toronto, Ontario) worked in partnership with the community in Moosonee, Ontario, to move toward a path of reconciliation that empowers Indigenous well-being by exploring community perspectives on a culturally informed cardiovascular health strategy. Collectively, they also embarked on a divergent and intentional path in pursuing excellence in knowledge translation by using a traditional storytelling approach that is most authentic and deeply rooted in the community the research serves. Storytelling remains an invaluable form of knowledge for Indigenous people, being used to share traditions, including cultural beliefs, histories, values, relationships, and practices.4 This approach arguably can improve the possibility of both healing and well-being, given the complex grief and history with which too many Indigenous people continue to live in today’s world.4 This article, in the form of an arts-based graphic report, clearly illustrates the depth of Indigenous storytelling and holistic worldviews on health and healing while strengthening community voices. Numerous themes emerge from the Moosonee community to explain how heart health is shaped by emotional, spiritual, social, and systemic factors.

Strengths and Challenges of the Storytelling Approach in Academic Communications

This arts-based graphic report is academically rigorous, as are other CJC Open publications. The challenge in interpreting this academic contribution at “first glance” is merely because it follows the path less traveled. We often uphold and value the familiarity of original research articles that follow strict reporting guidelines and highly structured, conventional text-based formats, including the introduction, methods, results, and discussion (IMRAD). However, if we can take a moment to pause and appreciate authentic storytelling approaches, the fundamental components of the research are communicated with fluency, while simply captivating the audience with vibrant attention to detail and creativity.

Conclusions

Our academic world will continually evolve with technology and an increasing number of opportunities for knowledge translation (including research communications in the form of podcasts, infographics, and arts-based representations, to name a few). As we move forward, we should embrace research collaborators and communications in all forms, including those who have chosen to take the path less traveled. Zhao and colleagues3 should be commended on their arts-based graphic report, as it upholds the authenticity of Indigenous storytelling, embraces holistic worldviews on health and healing, and strengthens Indigenous community voices.
