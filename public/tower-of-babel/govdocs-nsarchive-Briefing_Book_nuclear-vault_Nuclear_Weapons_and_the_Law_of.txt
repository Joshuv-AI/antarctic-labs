<div class="node__content clearfix"><div class="clearfix text-formatted field field--name-body field--type-text-with-summary field--label-hidden field__item"><p><strong>Washington, D.C., June 6, 2023</strong>&nbsp;- U.S.

officials and NATO allies feared that international talks meant to strengthen the protection of civilians during conflicts could lead to a ban on the use of nuclear weapons, according to a September 1975 memorandum by the Pentagon’s Joint Staff posted today by the National Security Archive.

Published as part of a new Electronic Briefing Book on the negotiations that produced Additional Protocol I to the 1949 Geneva Conventions, the Defense Department memo reveals some of the concerns that underlay the initial U.S.

position that Protocol I would not apply to nuclear weapons use.</p><p>Negotiated during the mid-1970s, in the shadow of the Vietnam War and anti-colonial rebellions, a major purpose of “International Humanitarian Law” was to create rules of war that shielded civilians from intentional attack.

Seeking better arrangements for the protection of prisoners of war and wanting to improve the U.S.

reputation after the Vietnam War, U.S.

negotiators participated in the talks that produced Protocol I, but the results troubled U.S.

officials and NATO allies because of their implications for nuclear weapons use.

It was not “feasible” to apply the provisions for the protection of civilians “in the event of general nuclear war,” according to the Pentagon.</p><p>Today’s posting focuses on U.S.

government and inter-allied discussions of nuclear weapons during the 1974-1977 negotiations.

The story of the negotiations themselves are too complex to take on here, but documents on the nuclear aspects shed light on some of the innovative features of Protocol I and their broader implications.

A West German aide-memoire reveals Bonn’s concern that, if Protocol I prohibitions went into effect, they could call into question the “first use of nuclear weapons [which] is an element contained solely in NATO’s concept of deterrence.” Other declassified documents included in the posting disclose U.S.

efforts to placate West German officials, whose concerns about the nuclear use issue persisted.</p><p>Also included in the posting are major reviews of Protocol I by NATO’s Military Committee and by a Defense Department working group, both of which assumed that the rules did not apply to nuclear weapons use.

The Pentagon report expressed concern about prohibitions of “attacks against the civilian population or civilians by way of reprisals.” Defense officials saw the threat of reprisals “as an essential means for deterring violations of the law of war” and wanted “to preserve the right of reprisal in some types of widespread conventional warfare.” The State Department rejected the Defense proposal, but the problem of reprisals would be a continuing issue in U.S.

government discussions of Protocol I.</p><p>From the onset of the Protocol I negotiations, U.S.

officials did not believe that the limitations and prohibitions would apply to nuclear weapons, and they had a tacit understanding with the Soviet Union on that issue.<a href="#_edn1" name="_ednref1">[1]</a> To make that point absolutely clear, State Department lawyer and U.S.

delegation chief George H.

Aldrich proposed that when the U.S.delegation chief George H.

Aldrich proposed that when the U.S.

signed Protocol I it should disclose an “understanding … to the effect that the rules established by the protocol are not intended to have any effect on and do not apply to the use of nuclear weapons.” Aldrich crafted the understanding when he was working for Secretary of State Henry Kissinger, and the Jimmy Carter administration adopted the same language when it signed the Protocol in December 1977.</p><p>An important element of the discussions was the continuing West German concern about nuclear weapons.

A front-line state in the Cold War housing nuclear weapons delivery systems and U.S.

and British nuclear stockpiles, Bonn was wary about any rules that could conceivably weaken the threats that underlay NATO’s deterrence strategy.

Thus, West German defense officials wanted to avoid any interpretations of the Protocol that could cast doubt on nuclear deterrence.

To alleviate such concerns, U.S.

negotiators assiduously tried to assure Bonn about the need for, and the U.S.

commitment to, interpretive language on Protocol I.

Although the State Department was wary that German defense officials were trying to scuttle Protocol I for domestic legal reasons, they were confident that the Foreign Ministry would support the new rules on political and diplomatic grounds, which it did.</p><p>The reasons for the West German and U.S.

worries were clear enough given the far-reaching implications of the new rules for military operations detailed in the 1977 <a href="https://treaties.un.org/doc/publication/unts/volume%201125/volume-1125-i-17512-english.pdf">Additional Protocol</a>.Although the State Department was wary that German defense officials were trying to scuttle Protocol I for domestic legal reasons, they were confident that the Foreign Ministry would support the new rules on political and diplomatic grounds, which it did.</p><p>The reasons for the West German and U.S.

During the early 1980s, the Joint Chiefs of Staff conducted a major policy review that opposed ratification of the Protocol.

The nuclear weapons issue was one consideration, but also important was the question of reprisals and the rules concerning guerilla movements.

Pentagon official Douglas Feith publicly and successfully <a href="https://www.jstor.org/stable/42894374">attacked</a> the Protocol’s rules on wars of national liberation, saying that they provided legal protection for terrorists.

The Reagan administration decided against ratification, although it favored working with other countries to develop a “common understanding” of the “positive features” of Additional Protocol I.</p><p>The extent to which the Reagan administration pursued a “common understanding” needs to be elucidated.

So does the extent to which nuclear planners during the 1970s and the following years explored the implications of Additional Protocol I to military operations.

While top U.S.

policymakers had been asking defense planners for nuclear targeting options that had less than catastrophic implications for human survival and would minimize “collateral damage,” those plans took years to develop.

Even if defense officials of this period were not ready to apply new rules of war to nuclear operations, over time pressures to do so would emerge, especially with military lawyers (Judge Advocates General) becoming embedded in all of the services, including the U.S.

Strategic Command.

A key development was in 2013, when the Obama administration established a <a href="https://uploads.fas.org/2013/06/NukeEmploymentGuidance_DODbrief061213.pdf#page=6">requirement</a> for strategic planners to begin applying provisions of Protocol I to nuclear operations.

Someday, the lead-up to this decision and the longer story of the impact of Protocol I on nuclear weapons policy may become possible to tell when relevant documents become available.</p><p>&nbsp;</p><h3>Background to Protocol I</h3><p>For decades during the twentieth century, the rules of war only minimally protected civilian populations from attack.

By the time of the atomic bombings of Japan, no internationally accepted rules governed aerial attacks on cities, whether from conventional bombs or weapons of greater destructiveness.

After the bombing of Dresden, U.S.

Army Air Force General George McDonald characterized Allied bombing policy as one of “homicide and devastation.” During the following years, when U.S.

officials developed nuclear weapons target plans they assumed that industrial and military facilities were legitimate targets.Army Air Force General George McDonald characterized Allied bombing policy as one of “homicide and devastation.” During the following years, when U.S.

officials developed nuclear weapons target plans they assumed that industrial and military facilities were legitimate targets.

While the 1949 Geneva Conventions and their antecedents forbade intentional attacks against civilian populations as such, civilians were at risk as long as <a href="https://nsarchive2.gwu.edu/nukevault/ebb390/docs/7-25-80%20PD%2059.pdf">targets</a> included “political control” and “industrial facilities which provide immediate support to military operations during wartime,” any of which would have substantial populations nearby.<a href="#_edn2" name="_ednref2">[2]</a></p><p>Driving the negotiation of new rules during the 1970s was the convergence of several trends.

One was the long-standing efforts of Jean Pictet, a senior official with the International Committee of the Red Cross in Geneva, who believed that if war could not be ended, at least fighting could be constrained to protect civilians.

Especially motivating him was the lack of protection for civilians exposed to aerial bombardment.

Since World War II, Pictet and his colleagues had pressed for international rules that would forbid indiscriminate attacks where collateral damage outweighed military advantage.

Also influencing Pictet’s quest were the developments of the 1960s—the Vietnam war and the violence against anti-colonial national liberation movements.

Generalized public concern about these issues helped Pictet and the ICRC win worldwide support for a proposal to supplement the 1949 Geneva Conventions with prohibitions on the “direct targeting of civilians” and “excessive collateral damage.”<a href="#_edn3" name="_ednref3">[3]</a></p><p>Pictet and the ICRC initiated the project by hosting two conferences of experts, with hundreds of representatives from many nations, including the U.S., which met at Geneva in 1971 and 1972.Also influencing Pictet’s quest were the developments of the 1960s—the Vietnam war and the violence against anti-colonial national liberation movements.

Generalized public concern about these issues helped Pictet and the ICRC win worldwide support for a proposal to supplement the 1949 Geneva Conventions with prohibitions on the “direct targeting of civilians” and “excessive collateral damage.”<a href="#_edn3" name="_ednref3">[3]</a></p><p>Pictet and the ICRC initiated the project by hosting two conferences of experts, with hundreds of representatives from many nations, including the U.S., which met at Geneva in 1971 and 1972.

Those discussions crystallized thinking about major agenda items, with the ICRC developing a concrete proposal for supplements to the Geneva Conventions: Additional Protocol I on laws of war for international conflicts, including wars of national liberation; and Additional Protocol II on rules for internal conflicts.

The widely accepted ICRC concept was that Protocol I would stipulate methods and means of combat, including provisions for greater protection of civilians in combat situations.

Those proposals became the basis for the formal negotiations that began when the Diplomatic Conference met in Geneva in early 1974.<a href="#_edn4" name="_ednref4">[4]</a></p><p>State Department lawyer George Aldrich led the U.S.

delegation at Geneva.

Aldrich had been Henry Kissinger’s chief legal adviser during the negotiation of the 1973 peace agreement that ended U.S.

fighting in Vietnam.

The tough conditions that U.S.

prisoners of war experienced in North Vietnam put protections for POWs high on the agenda of Aldrich and his military advisers.

They also believed that the negative impact of the Vietnam War on the U.S.

image around the world made it necessary for Washington to play a role in developing new rules for international conflict.They also believed that the negative impact of the Vietnam War on the U.S.

image around the world made it necessary for Washington to play a role in developing new rules for international conflict.

As Aldrich wrote some years later, accusations of “indiscriminate bombardment, attacks on civilians, attacks on dikes and the environment” contributed to “[a] pervading sense of defensiveness, if not guilt, about the suffering caused by the war, and by aerial warfare.” Those concerns “resulted … in an increased [U.S.] willingness to participate in the … improvement of that part of international law that deals with the conduct of hostilities.”<a href="#_edn5" name="_ednref5">[5]</a></p><p>What Henry Kissinger thought of the Diplomatic Conference has not surfaced, at least as far as the editor can tell.

Given Kissinger’s “realist” predilections, he was probably skeptical and critical but likely approved U.S.

participation so that Washington could influence the outcome.

That was the gist of a message that Kissinger received from his executive assistant, Lawrence Eagleburger, early in the conference’s proceedings [See Document 1].

Aldrich was probably Kissinger’s choice for chief of delegation to ensure that that the U.S.

negotiating role was in reliable hands.</p><p>When Jimmy Carter became president in early 1977, substantial progress had been made toward reaching agreement on the text of Protocol I, but there was more fine tuning to be done.

<div class="clearfix text-formatted field field--name-field-description field--type-text-with-summary field--label-hidden field__item"><p>A message to Secretary of State Kissinger (then in the Middle East) from his Executive Assistant Lawrence Eagleburger, and transmitted by Brent Scowcroft, reported on a problem at the law of war conference. A key issue was whether to allow the Provisional Revolutionary Government (<a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Provisional_Revolutionary_Government_of_the_Republic_of_South_Vietnam">PRG</a>) of Vietnam and the Royal Government of the National Union of Kampuchea [Cambodia] (<a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/GRUNK">GRUNK</a>) to participate. The question posed by Eagleburger was that, if the two “puppet governments” were seated, should the U.S. walk out or “protest and stay.” Eagleburger explained why the U.S. was participating: to have a voice in decisions on the treatment of prisoners of war and “to refute the many criticisms that will be made of our tactic in Vietnam and elsewhere.” If the U.S. lost the vote and walked out, it would mean “forsaking our objectives” at the conference and “irritating Switzerland and a lot of other countries,” among other problems. As it turned out, the PRG and GRUNK failed in their bid to participate in the conference, so Kissinger did not have to decide about continued U.S. participation.</p>

<div class="clearfix text-formatted field field--name-field-description field--type-text-with-summary field--label-hidden field__item"><p>This report by the U.S. delegation gives perspective on how the “Diplomatic Conference” did its work in the opening months. Aldrich’s confidential discussion&nbsp;explained how the U.S. prevailed on the PRG issue, in part by not objecting to the seating of other “national liberation movements,” which conciliated African and Arab delegations enough to not support the PRG. While Aldrich saw a “politicization” of the conference, with developing countries voting as a bloc to force through a text “applying Protocol 1 to wars of national liberation,” he believed that some delegations, such as Egypt’s, could prove “helpful in getting other states in the same camp off their more extreme positions.” Not sure whether “many states are really serious about drawing up new humanitarian law and in becoming parties to the resulting treaties,” Aldrich believed that it would be “necessary for the United States to consider what advantages and disadvantages may lie in participation in the second session of the Conference and whether it would wish to become a party to the Protocols.”</p>

<div class="clearfix text-formatted field field--name-field-description field--type-text-with-summary field--label-hidden field__item"><p>The ongoing negotiations at the “Diplomatic Conference” led to a Pentagon review of the issues, ranging from prisoners of war to treatment of the “wounded, sick, and shipwrecked.” On the treatment of civilian populations, the Pentagon had no objection to rules that provided “reasonable protection … against the effects of hostilities,” but questioned language forbidding the targeting of civilians in reprisal attacks. On that point, Deputy Secretary of Defense William Clements had written to Deputy Secretary of State Robert Ingersoll that the Joint Chiefs believed that threat of reprisals was an “essential means for deterring serious violations of the laws of war.” The Chiefs recommended the inclusion of language permitting “reprisals against the enemy’s civilian population, but only in response to grave unlawful attacks on the other party’s civilian population.”</p>

<p>Aldrich informed Ingersoll that recent discussions had led to the reworking of the text so that the right of reprisal was “carefully restricted … for egregious cases.” It should be “quite acceptable to Defense.” Aldrich further emphasized the need to “preserve the decision-making authority of the chief of delegation.” With support from Secretary of State Kissinger, Aldrich was in a strong position to ward off such Pentagon moves.<a href="#_edn8" name="_ednref8">[8]</a></p>

<p>Instead of early review by the NATO MC, the Department favored consultations to begin with the “inner core” countries and then commence with NATO political advisers, adding that an MC study should not begin until the conclusion of the diplomatic conference. Reviewing the history of discussions with the “inner core” NATO member governments, the Department saw a good record of consultations. Of the NATO members, West Germany had posed the most problems. The U.S. recognized the West German concern about its domestic law and was “doing utmost to accommodate concern.” The Department was also concerned that “certain FRG officials may be trying to derail [the] draft protocol by using NATO as mechanism.” According to Aldrich, it was in the U.S. “interest to see [the protocols] interpreted reasonably, whether or not we become parties to them,” which was a consideration that the FRG seemed “thus far unable to understand, but we must keep trying to explain it.” The Department believed it was better to initiate consultations of the “inner core” group during the conference and begin formal NATO consultations once the conference has concluded.</p>

From the German perspective, those and other articles would have “negative” implications for the alliance’s “concept of deterrence and defense, including its nuclear component.” There could be calls for a “ban on the use of nuclear weapons, especially in the densely-populated region of Central Europe” and “a drastic reduction of possibilities of conventional warfare.”</p>

<p>The speaking notes also included a point about West German law: that a soldier can lodge complaints with the courts “against the service and training regulations of the federal armed forces.” That would put the “compatibility of NATO’s defence concept with the new rules of international law” as a subject before a supreme court.”</p>

<div class="field field--name-field-title field--type-string field--label-hidden field__item"><a href="/document/30370-document-21-memorandum-major-general-r-torp-acting-director-general-international" hreflang="en"><strong class=" wrapped-title raw-title">Memorandum from Major General R. Torp, Acting Director General, International Military Staff, to Secretary General, North Atlantic Treaty Organization, “Humanitarian Law—Review of Articles 35-60 of Protocol 1,” MCM-76 -77, 31 October 1977, Confidential</strong></a></div>

<div class="field field--name-field-date field--type-datetime field--label-hidden field__item"><time datetime="1977-10-31T12:00:00Z" class="datetime">Oct 31, 1977</time>

<div class="clearfix text-formatted field field--name-field-source field--type-text-long field--label-inline">

<div class="clearfix text-formatted field field--name-field-description field--type-text-with-summary field--label-hidden field__item"><p>The Military Committee’s study, completed past the August 31 deadline, spelled out the implications of Protocol I for alliance military operations. The MC found much of it “militarily acceptable,” as long as the rules did not preclude NATO’s “options to use nuclear weapons.” The literal meaning of Protocol articles 35, 51 and 55 “would inhibit essential uses of nuclear weapons, if they were held to apply to” them. Thus, the detailed review of the Protocol was “based on the understanding that the rules introduced by the Protocol do not affect the use of nuclear weapons, and that member states of the Alliance will coordinate and consult to ensure that their national positions are in harmony and that this understanding shall be legally effective throughout the Alliance.”</p>

<p>The detailed analysis of Articles 35, 51, 55 and 66 made it clear enough why NATO members found them problematic because of their implications for nuclear weapons use. Thus, Article 35 constrained signatories from using “weapons, projectiles and material and methods of warfare of a nature to cause superfluous injury or unnecessary suffering.”</p>

<p>Annexes to the Military Committee report included statements made by various NATO country delegates—such as the previously cited statement by George Aldrich—explaining their votes on specific articles and general statements about the work of the conference.</p>

<div class="clearfix text-formatted field field--name-field-description field--type-text-with-summary field--label-hidden field__item"><p>In the weeks before the signing of Protocol I in Geneva, a Department of Defense working group led by Deputy Assistant Secretary of Defense Walter Slocombe&nbsp;prepared a comprehensive analysis of the Protocol and its negotiating history, the position on each section taken by the U.S., other Western countries, and “Third World” nations, beginning with the preamble and proceeding article by article. &nbsp;The report found much that was beneficial and positive in Protocol I but, like the NATO analysts, the working group was concerned about articles 35 and 55 because of their implications for nuclear weapons use. Such concerns supported the conclusion on the need for an “express understanding that the Rules of the Protocol do not affect the use of nuclear weapons.”</p>

<p>Like the NATO analysts, the working group saw Articles 51 (paragraph 5B) and 57 as affirming the rule of proportionality. Thus, incidental civilian casualties were not prohibited, as long as it was evident that they would not be “excessive in relation to the concrete and direct military advantage anticipated.” Also prohibited was the “indiscriminate” use of weapons that could cause casualties that were “excessive in relation to the concrete and direct military advantage anticipated.” Besides codifying the rule of proportionality, the Article “gives to military commanders uniformly recognized guidance on this responsibility to civilians and the civilian population in carrying out attacks against military objectives.”</p>ality, the Article “gives to military commanders uniformly recognized guidance on this responsibility to civilians and the civilian population in carrying out attacks against military objectives.”</p>

<p>While Defense officials saw the articles as “a major step in the reaffirmation and development of humanitarian law applicable in armed conflict,” they also recognized the implications for nuclear weapons use. Also worrisome was language about weapons causing “long term major health problems.” The Defense group did not believe that nuclear weapons would necessarily cause “excessive casualties,” but they concluded that “in a situation of general nuclear warfare it would be impracticable to apply the rules of the Protocol relating to protections of civilians.” That further supported the view that the Protocol did not apply to nuclear weapons.</p>

<p>As before, the prohibitions against reprisals concerned Pentagon officials, and the report discussed the possibility of lodging a formal reservation on this point when the U.S. signed Protocol I. While the report found it “unlikely that the U.S. would have to resort to reprisals against civilian populations, as such, except in situations involving nuclear weapons,” it expressed interest in a reservation if it could be made without weakening the U.S. understanding that excluded the Protocol's application to nuclear weapons. &nbsp;None of this, however, would be the Pentagon’s last word on reprisals and other issues raised by Protocol I because the Joint Chiefs of Staff had not yet given it a full review, which would have great bearing on any Defense Department recommendations on ratification.</p>

<div class="clearfix text-formatted field field--name-field-description field--type-text-with-summary field--label-hidden field__item"><p>This request for authorization to sign Protocols I and II reviewed their history and included two understandings, one concerning nuclear weapons, which would be declared at the time of the signing. The Defense Department wanted to include a reservation on reprisals, but both the Arms Control and Disarmament Agency and the State Department believed that would be “unworkable in the event of massive and continuing violations of the Convention.” (The Pentagon would continue to treat that as a sticking point). As regards the attitude of the U.S. Congress, the State Department anticipated “no significant” objection.</p>
