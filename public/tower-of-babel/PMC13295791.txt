Effects of biologic and targeted synthetic disease modifying antirheumatic drugs (b/tsDMARDs) on patient-reported outcome domains in rheumatoid arthritis: a systematic review and network meta-analyses
Authors: Luuk van Esveld, Marijke van den Dikkenberg, Nathalie Luurssen-Masurel, Pascal de Jong, Marc Redwart Kok, Tessa Bosch, Mariska Quirina Nikita Hackert, Angelique Weel-Koenders, Deirisa Lopes Barreto, Tjallingius Martijn Kuijper
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionEvidence from head-to-head comparisons of biologic/targeted synthetic disease-modifying antirheumatic drugs (b/tsDMARDs) on patient-reported outcomes (PROs) in patients with rheumatoid arthritis (RA) is lacking. This systematic review and network meta-analysis (NMA) aims to evaluate the relative effects of b/tsDMARDs on the International Consortium for Health Outcomes Measurement (ICHOM) RA-relevant domains of pain, fatigue, activity limitation, and physical and mental health.MethodsA systematic literature search was conducted using the machine learning tool ASReview. Randomised controlled trials were included that compared DMARDs with placebo or other DMARDs and reported the effects on PROs from the ICHOM standard set for inflammatory arthritis. Random effects NMA were performed on PROs.ResultsIn total, 99 studies were included, with heterogeneity within comparisons ranging from low to very high (I²=0%–96%). Janus kinase inhibitors (mean difference (MD) Health Assessment Questionnaire: −0.13; 95% CI−0.23 to −0.04) and IL-6 inhibitors (MD: −0.13; 95% CI −0.27 to −0.00) were significantly better at reducing activity limitation than tumour necrosis factor inhibitors. Other significant differences between b/tsDMARD classes were not observed. The surface under the cumulative ranking curve demonstrated that rituximab was overall ranked best among b/tsDMARDs. IL-6 inhibitors ranked highest in the mental health domain (82%). Early RA (<3 years diagnosis) and worse baseline PRO scores were associated with larger improvements of PROs (p<0.05).ConclusionsDespite heterogeneity, the results of this study may help rheumatologists guide selection of b/tsDMARDs, taking into account PROs that are important to the patient or reflect their primary symptoms. Future research should investigate within-class differences between specific b/tsDMARDs and emphasise individualised, PRO-based augmentation within treat-to-target strategies.PROSPERO registration numberCRD42020147514Keywords: Arthritis, Rheumatoid; Patient Reported Outcome Measures; Biological Therapy; DMARD

Introduction

Rheumatoid arthritis (RA) is a chronic inflammatory disease, negatively affecting day-to-day life. More than 50% of patients with RA experience health problems like pain, fatigue and impaired functioning despite being treated.1 2 Currently, treatment is primarily pharmacological with the aim to reduce inflammation, relieve symptoms and prevent articular damage.3 4 In particular, early initiation of disease-modifying antirheumatic drugs (DMARDs) combined with a treat-to-target strategy has contributed to an improved prognosis in terms of clinical and radiological outcomes.4 Despite improvements in treatment and disease activity, many patients with low disease activity continue to experience clinically relevant residual symptoms and disease burden such as pain, fatigue and mental health challenges.5 6 These symptoms can be captured through patient-reported outcomes (PROs).7,10

Today, there is increasing emphasis on the patient’s perspective, reflected in the shift of healthcare systems towards more person-centred, value-based healthcare (VBHC). VBHC aims to optimise patient value by maximising patient-relevant care outcomes while controlling costs. PROs are key to this shift, as they capture outcomes that are meaningful and relevant to patients. Failure to address these problems contributes to high individual and societal costs.11,13 Hence, the International Consortium for Health Outcomes Measurement (ICHOM) has established a core set containing the most relevant PROs for patients with inflammatory arthritis (IA), with corresponding patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs) to quantify each domain. These most relevant PRO domains for RA were pain, fatigue, activity limitation, physical and mental health impact, and work productivity.14

Nevertheless, the effect of DMARDs on PROs remains unclear, since most RA research on the efficacy of DMARDs focuses on clinical disease activity as the primary outcome. Although PROs are often designated as secondary endpoints in randomised controlled trials (RCTs), they are occasionally not reported at all.10 The limited number of PROs that have been included is within the domains of pain and activity limitation, as recommended by the Outcome Measures in Rheumatology for clinical trials and American College of Rheumatology core sets.15,18

Notwithstanding these limitations, our previous systematic review and meta-analyses comparing DMARDs to placebo and methotrexate (MTX) demonstrated that all DMARDs improve PRO domains of pain, fatigue and activity limitation.10 However, solid evidence to support the use of one DMARD class over another is still missing. This highlights the need for head-to-head comparisons of DMARD classes on PROs.10 Moreover, additional evidence on Janus kinase (JAK) inhibitors is now available. Network meta-analysis (NMA) allows comparison of DMARDs on all PRO domains, including those not directly compared in RCTs, by combining all available indirect and direct evidence.19 20

Heterogeneity due to different populations and endpoints in RA RCTs is well recognised and was anticipated in this analysis.21,23 Established factors driving this heterogeneity are disease duration (early or chronic disease), baseline disease activity, MTX or biologic naivety, and follow-up duration.24,26 However, it remains unclear whether these factors also affect DMARD response on PROs.

Hence, this systematic review and NMA aims to provide an in-depth overview of the relative effects of DMARDs on PROs. The main focus was to identify significant differences between biologic/targeted synthetic (b/ts)DMARD classes within each specific PRO domain. The secondary aim was to explore potential subgroups and study characteristics associated with the effect of DMARD classes on PROs.

Methods

A systematic review was conducted using the novel machine learning tool ASReview, followed by an NMA. This review builds on a prior systematic review that included meta-analyses summarising direct comparisons of DMARDs on several PROs.10 This systematic review is in accordance with the Cochrane Handbook and registered in PROSPERO CRD42020147514.27 Minor changes were added to the initial PROSPERO registration (online supplemental appendix 1). Furthermore, the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses NMA extension was used as a reporting guideline.28

Search strategy

The updated systematic review added the period from September 2019 to January 2025 that has been set up in consultation with two experienced medical librarians (WB and EK).10 Databases searched were Embase, Medline, Web of Science and Google Scholar (search strings in online supplemental appendix 2). Results were automatically checked for duplicates and entered into RefWorks (V.2.0, PROQuest).

Eligibility criteria

RCTs were included if they met the following inclusion criteria: (1) Patients with RA >18 years; (2) Use of DMARDs registered for RA; (3) Reporting the effects on >1 ICHOM-relevant PROs; and (4) Follow-up duration was >8 weeks, with the availability of rescue medication or the start of an open-label extension defining the maximum follow-up.14 In DMARD tapering or switch studies, the moment of first tapering or switching of DMARD was determined as the maximum time to follow-up. Conference reports and studies not published in English language were excluded. Triple therapy studies were also excluded, as the effects could not be traced back to a specific DMARD.

Study selection

The machine learning tool ASReview for systematic reviews was used for title and abstract screening with the SAFE procedure as a stopping rule for screening.29,34 For input articles to train ASReview, agreement between two independent researchers (LvE/MvdD) was 78.3% (n=60). An article was considered relevant for input in ASReview if >1 researcher considered it to be relevant. Title and abstract screening was performed by the same two independent researchers. The ASReview screening process is summarised in online online supplemental figure 1. Full-text screening was manually performed by one researcher (LvE) and checked by the other researcher (MvdD). Disagreements were resolved in consultation with a third researcher (AW-K). Additionally, all reference lists of included studies were screened for eligible missed articles.

Data collection

Data from all eligible studies were extracted by two researchers (LvE/NL-M and MvdD) using a predefined extraction form. In case multiple articles reported the same study, data extraction was combined. Authors were contacted when information was missing or full texts were unavailable.

DMARDs were grouped according to class: tumour necrosis factor (TNF) inhibitors (adalimumab, certolizumab pegol, etanercept, golimumab, infliximab), IL-6 inhibitors (sarilumab, tocilizumab), anakinra (IL-1-inhibitor), rituximab (anti-CD20), abatacept (anti-CD80/86), JAK inhibitors (baricitinib, filgotinib, tofacitinib, upadacitinib), MTX and other (non-MTX) conventional synthetic DMARDs (csDMARDs) (hydroxychloroquine, leflunomide, sulfasalazine).

Outcomes extracted per PRO domain were the Visual Analogue Scale (VAS) or Numerical Rating Scale (NRS) for pain, the Functional Assessment of Chronic Illness Therapy – Fatigue (FACIT-F) for fatigue, the Health Assessment Questionnaire (-Disability Index) (HAQ(-DI)) for activity limitation, the 36-Item Short Form Health Survey Physical Component Score or Mental Component Score (SF-36 PCS/MCS) for physical or mental health impact and the Work Productivity and Activity Impairment: Rheumatoid Arthritis (WPAI:RA) Questionnaire for work productivity.14 In case the HAQ(-DI) or NRS/VAS pain score ranged from 0 to 10 or 0 to 100 instead of 0 to 3 or 0 to 10, means and SDs were multiplied by 0.3 or 0.1, respectively. The FACIT-F and SF-36 scores were multiplied by −1 for interpretability, so higher scores represent worse health outcomes in all of the domains. For all PROs, the mean difference (MD) with 95% CI was used as the main measure of association of relative treatment effectiveness. Values below zero indicated a favourable effect of a DMARD class over the comparator. If not directly provided, mean change scores were calculated by transforming medians, IQRs, SEs and 95% CIs to means and SDs.35 36 When the change SD was not reported, it was estimated from the baseline and follow-up SDs assuming a between measurements correlation (ρ) of 0.5. In case only a baseline SD was reported, the follow-up SDs were assumed to be equal to the baseline SD.37 Reported effects focused on comparisons between b/tsDMARDs, although MTX and other (non-MTX) csDMARDs were included for the purpose of completing the network.

Additional study characteristics extracted were sample size, baseline disease activity, follow-up duration, baseline PRO Score, publication year, early RA (<3 years after RA diagnosis), bDMARD naivety and MTX naivety.

The Cochrane Collaboration’s tool for assessing the risk of bias was used to assess bias in all eligible studies by two researchers independently (LvE or NL-M and MvdD).38 Differences were discussed and consensus was reached in consultation with a third researcher (AW-K). For each of the items, studies were classified as ‘low’, ‘unclear’ or ‘high’ risk of bias.

Evaluation of network geometry

For each PRO domain a network plot was created with DMARD classes and placebo as nodes and their geometry was visually assessed. Node thickness indicated the number of participants per DMARD class and edge (comparison) thickness indicated the number of comparisons between classes. Network characteristics including all nodes and comparisons were reported. Network integrity was checked for the presence of auto loops, indicating the presence of multiple identical treatment arms within the same trial. Auto loops were permitted if a single trial included two arms within the same DMARD class, or included two arms of the same DMARD with and without concomitant MTX.

Statistical analysis

Convergence of the models was verified by inspection of trace plots and Gelman-Rubin and Geweke diagnostics. Model fit was assessed by inspection of leverage plots and comparing deviance statistics (Deviance Information Criteria).

Heterogeneity was evaluated by excluding studies that contributed disproportionately to extreme heterogeneity, arbitrarily defined as |w|>2.5. This |w| represents the absolute value of each study’s standardised residual, indicating its deviation from the model-predicted effect in SE units.39 Moreover, leave-one-out meta-analyses were conducted to evaluate the impact of individual studies on the overall pooled estimate for each comparison.40 Inconsistency of the models was checked by node-splitting.40 41 Comparison-adjusted funnel plots per domain were created to assess publication bias within each PRO domain.40

All models were optimised using the following settings in the nma.run function: adaptation iterations were set to 5000 to stabilise the model, Markov Chain Monte Carlo iterations were set to 100 000 to estimate model parameters, and thinning was set to 10 to reduce autocorrelation.42 Models were adjusted for concomitant MTX therapy within the model.

Forest plots were created showing the relative effectiveness of DMARD classes with TNF inhibitors as the comparator. A surface under the cumulative ranking curve (SUCRA) table was generated (range 0%–100%) to determine the relative ranking of DMARD classes for each PRO domain. Additionally, triangular league tables with heat plots were constructed to display treatment comparisons between DMARD groups, with NMA estimates presented in the upper diagonal and direct evidence shown in the lower diagonal.43 Blue indicated a favourable (or negative PRO Score) effect of a DMARD over the comparator, whereas orange indicated the opposite.

Subgroup analyses excluding studies with patients with early RA, patients with prior bDMARD use or comparison between follow-ups of 12–16 weeks versus 24–30 weeks were performed to assess potential influence of these study characteristics on PROs. Additional robustness analyses were conducted by re-including studies that had previously been excluded due to heterogeneity and comparing results with and without background MTX.

Exploratory variance-weighted clustered linear regression was performed on the effects of study characteristics (early RA, bDMARD naivety, follow-up duration, baseline PRO Score) on mean PRO change scores (p<0.05 was considered statistically significant). The results of these additional analyses are presented in the online supplemental files. The BUGSnet (V.1.1.0) and rjags (V.4-17) packages were used to run BUGS code in JAGS from within R and allow for multiarm trials to create the networks, heat plots and SUCRA table.42 44 The Gemtc package (V.1.0-2) was used to check for consistency by node splitting.45 The Netmeta package (V.3.2-0) was used for leave-one-out meta-analysis.46 RStudio V.4.4.1 was used for statistical analysis.

Results

Study selection and characteristics

As shown in figure 1, a total of 3029 studies were identified after duplicate removal through the updated literature search. 2719 articles were excluded based on title and abstract screening: 1737 through ASReview and 982 manually by the reviewers. Thereafter, 319 articles were assessed in full text, of which 285 were excluded. The majority were protocols, conference abstracts, or studies in which treatments or outcomes did not meet the inclusion criteria. A total of 100 authors were contacted for missing information, of whom 1 provided the requested data. In total, 99 articles were included, combining the articles (n=65) from the previous search with the newly identified ones (n=34; online supplemental table 1). Heterogeneity and inconsistency

Six studies were removed due to heterogeneity, ranging from 0% for fatigue to 6.3% for activity limitation.61 77 89 99 132 139 However, leave-one-out meta-analyses identified substantial heterogeneity between DMARD comparisons (0%–96.5%) within PRO domains (online supplemental figures 4A–E). Node-splitting demonstrated some inconsistency between direct and indirect evidence of TNF inhibitors versus placebo within the domain of activity limitation. This inconsistency was resolved after removing two additional studies from the network.53 146

Estimated effects of DMARDs for each PRO domain

Figure 3 provides a visual overview of the relative effects of DMARDs versus TNF inhibitors. Although uncertainty was considerable, the largest improvements of PRO scores versus placebo were obtained with rituximab for pain (MD VAS: −20.6; 95% CI −34.3 to −8.2; Supplementary Figure 5A), for fatigue (MD FACIT-F: −10.5; 95% CI −17.7 to −4.0; Supplementary Figure 5B) and activity limitation (MD HAQ(-DI): −0.40; 95% CI −0.64 to −0.18; Figure 4). The largest improvement versus placebo of physical health was obtained with other (non-MTX) csDMARDs (SF-36 PCS: −6.1; 95% CI −9.9 to −3.3; Supplementary Figure 5C) and for mental health with IL-6 inhibitors (MD SF-36 MCS: −3.0; 95% CI −4.9 to −0.9; Supplementary Figure 5D). For activity limitation, the triangular table with heat plot (figure 4) was included in the main manuscript, as this was the only PRO domain showing significant differences between b/tsDMARDs. The triangular tables with heat plots for the other PRO domains were therefore provided in the online supplemental material (online supplemental figure 5A–D).

For pain (VAS), all DMARD classes, except for anakinra, improved pain more than MTX or placebo (online supplemental figure 5A). In general, JAK inhibitors showed no additional effect on pain reduction compared with bDMARDs. Although statistical significance was not reached, a trend towards larger effects of rituximab and abatacept versus other b/tsDMARDs was observed (figure 3A; online supplemental figure 5A).

For fatigue (FACIT-F), all DMARD classes were better than placebo (online supplemental figure 5B). Rituximab and IL-6 inhibitors appeared to be more effective in improving fatigue compared with most other b/tsDMARDs (figure 3B; online supplemental figure 5B); however, statistical significance was not reached.

For activity limitation (HAQ(-DI)), all DMARD classes, except for anakinra, were better at reducing activity limitation than placebo (figure 4). JAK inhibitors (MD:−0.13; 95% CI −0.23 to −0.04) and IL-6 inhibitors (MD:−0.13; 95% CI −0.27 to −0.00) were significantly better at reducing activity limitation than TNF inhibitors (figures3 4). Moreover, a significant effect of other (non-MTX) csDMARDs (MD:−0.15; 95% CI −0.27 to −0.02) over TNF inhibitors was observed (figure 4).

For physical health (SF-36 PCS), most DMARD classes were significantly better than placebo and MTX, although very wide CIs for abatacept and rituximab versus MTX prevented statistical significance (figure 3D; online supplemental figure 5C). No other significant differences were observed.

For mental health (SF-36 MCS), improvement compared with placebo was limited in all DMARD classes. Only TNF inhibitors (MD: −1.9; 95% CI −3.7 to −0.3), IL-6 inhibitors (MD: −3.0; 95% CI −4.9 to −0.9) and JAK inhibitors (MD: −2.2; 95% CI −3.3 to −1.2) reached a statistically significant difference versus placebo (online supplemental figure 5D). No other significant differences were observed (figure 3E; online supplemental figure 5D).

Relative DMARD performance scores across PRO domains

In general, SUCRA was high for multiple DMARD classes, with only MTX, anakinra and placebo showing a clearly lower probability of being ranked highest. SUCRA demonstrated that rituximab was ranked highest in improving the PRO domains of pain (91%), fatigue (97%) and activity limitation (85%; table 2). Other (non-MTX) csDMARDs were ranked highest in improving physical health (81%). IL-6 inhibitors ranked highest in improving mental health (82%).

Sensitivity and subgroup analyses

In general, visual inspection of forest plots revealed no major changes after excluding studies with patients with early RA, re-including previously excluded studies due to heterogeneity, and comparing effects with and without background MTX (online supplemental figure 6). However, some observations are noteworthy. First, when including only studies with bDMARD-naive patients (n=30), the effect of JAK inhibitors (MD: −0.22; 95% CI −0.39 to −0.05) and IL-6 inhibitors (MD: −0.15; 95% CI −0.35 to 0.05; online supplemental figure 7) over TNF inhibitors in reducing activity limitation persisted. However, statistical significance remained only for JAK inhibitors. Second, comparing studies with a follow-up of 12–16 weeks to 24–30 weeks demonstrated stability of the effect sizes for DMARD classes, though CIs in both groups were wider. Effect sizes for DMARDs versus placebo in activity limitation remained relatively stable with longer follow-up (online supplemental figure 8).

Clustered linear regression analysis exploring potential associations between study characteristics and PRO outcomes revealed that early RA (except in the mental health domain) and worse baseline PRO scores (except in the physical health domain) were significantly associated with greater improvements in four out of five PRO domains (p<0.05; online supplemental table 4). Moreover, bDMARD naivety was associated with greater improvement in the domain of fatigue. Lastly, longer follow-up was associated with a larger improvement of mental health.

Discussion

This NMA provides an overview of the relative effects of DMARDs on the most relevant PROs for patients with RA. In general, all DMARD classes appeared to improve PROs compared with placebo and MTX at 12–54 weeks of follow-up. JAK inhibitors and IL-6 inhibitors improved activity limitation significantly better than TNF inhibitors. However, no other significant differences within PRO domains were observed between b/tsDMARD classes. Rituximab was most often ranked best by SUCRA, specifically within the domains of pain, activity limitation and mainly fatigue. IL-6 inhibitors may be particularly beneficial in patients presenting with both fatigue and mental health symptoms. Early RA (defined as <3 years after diagnosis) and worse baseline PRO scores were generally associated with a larger improvement of PRO scores.

Although SUCRA ranking provides an overview of which DMARD class is ranked best within a specific PRO domain, results should be interpreted cautiously. SUCRA results should be interpreted in conjunction with significant or clinically relevant differences and uncertainty in the evidence.147 148 Significant differences between DMARD classes were scarce due to wide 95% CIs. This was mainly caused by substantial heterogeneity between studies, which is a common problem when performing meta-analyses in RA populations.21,23 Significant differences were observed only in the domain of activity limitation and may be explained by the relatively large number of studies reporting on this domain (n=64). Significant differences between b/tsDMARDs in other domains could therefore have been undetected.

The effect of DMARDs on mental health appeared to be minimal, which is in line with previous literature.149,151 This confirms that DMARD treatment alone is insufficient for mental health support, where a holistic role for the rheumatologist lies in identifying patients’ needs, providing education and referring patients to mental health professionals.152 153 Previously published minimal clinically important differences (MCIDs) of 2.5–5 points on the SF-36 MCS Scale were exceeded by IL-6 inhibitors versus placebo.152 154 155 IL-6 has been linked to depression and other mental health outcomes.156 157 IL-6 inhibitors were also ranked high within the domain of fatigue in our analysis. Fatigue is considered among the most important symptoms by patients with RA and is closely tied to mental health.156 158 159 IL-6 contributes to fatigue symptoms through overstimulation of the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis and sleep regulation in patients with RA.160 161 Thus, IL-6 inhibitors may be especially beneficial for patients with comorbid fatigue problems.

Patients whose main symptom is fatigue may benefit most from rituximab. This appears to contrast with previous literature where no difference in fatigue reduction between TNF inhibitors and other biologics was described.162 163 On the one hand, rituximab was also ranked best in the domains of pain and activity limitation. Improvements within these PRO domains are considered established drivers of fatigue improvement.164,166 On the other hand, studies that might have provided nuance on this effect were excluded from the analysis due to missing data on SDs of the effect sizes.167 168

Conversely, TNF inhibitors appeared less effective than other b/tsDMARDs, particularly regarding activity limitation. This may be partly due to most included studies (n=70) including patients with prior bDMARD exposure, often reflecting previous TNF inhibitor use. However, a subgroup analysis of studies without prior bDMARD exposure (n=30) showed that this effect largely persisted, suggesting that TNF inhibitors may indeed have a lower impact on activity limitation. Moreover, the relatively strong impact of other (non-MTX) csDMARDs on activity limitation and physical health was primarily driven by a limited number of studies. These studies either included only patients with early RA or contained a substantial proportion of participants who were csDMARD-naive (39%–53%) or classified as having early RA (diagnosis <2 years; 38%–42%).50,52110 In addition, the CareRA Trial included high-dose glucocorticoids alongside csDMARDs in the treatment arms.109 These pronounced effects observed versus placebo likely influenced the indirect comparisons of other (non-MTX) csDMARDs with b/tsDMARDs in these domains. While differences in PRO response between drug classes were observed, the indirect comparisons from the NMA cannot determine whether these differences reflect biological effects beyond inflammatory joints or whether they arise from contextual factors, residual confounding or variation in symptom perception and/or reporting. As such, future research should aim to clarify these pathways, enabling a more comprehensive interpretation of the findings in this study.

Consistent with previous literature, patients with early RA and worse baseline PRO scores exhibited greater improvements.169 170 However, these associations should be interpreted with caution, as patients’ perceptions may change over time due to response shift. When response shift occurs, the change in observed PRO scores will not accurately reflect the construct the PRO intends to measure. This effect is more pronounced in subjective domains (eg, pain, fatigue, mental health) than in more objective domains (eg, activity limitation). In addition, the included studies span more than two decades and originate from different parts of the world. This results in differences in treatment options available to study populations. In settings with fewer therapeutic alternatives, patients may have higher expectations of the study medication, which can inflate the placebo response and thereby reduce the observed differences between treatment arms. On the other hand, the inclusion of studies from various regions across the globe enhances the generalisability of this systematic review.

Our study has several strengths. First, to our knowledge, this is the first study comparing the effects of DMARD classes on PROs in such a wide spectrum of PRO outcomes. Second, we integrated direct and indirect evidence using NMA, nearly doubling the comparisons between DMARD groups. To expand the network and increase the number of comparisons, we included DMARDs evaluated in only one or two studies. Consequently, findings for these DMARDs should be interpreted with caution. Nevertheless, their inclusion enabled a more comprehensive and connected network while avoiding the exclusion of potentially relevant evidence. Third, a wide range of subgroup and sensitivity analyses were performed to assess the influence of specific studies, study characteristics, covariates and overall robustness of the results. Fourth, the novel machine learning tool ASReview was used to update the systematic review.

This study also has several limitations that warrant consideration. First, only one outcome measure per PRO domain was included. This decision was made to enhance the interpretability of results. In addition, selected PROMs are included in the ICHOM IA Standard Set and represent the most commonly reported instruments in RA RCTs.16 171 172 Second, the placebo effect and concomitant MTX background therapy may not have been fully accounted for in this analysis. Estimation of the placebo effect may vary due to background MTX.173 Moreover, the influence of background MTX may vary across studies due to differences in MTX history, dosage and route of administration.174 175 Third, follow-up duration ranged from 12 weeks to 54 weeks, which may limit comparability across studies. Nonetheless, subgroup analyses for activity limitation by follow-up duration showed no major differences in effect. Moreover, even when PROs are reported at the same follow-up time, comparability may still be limited. Differences in onset of action between DMARDs for specific PRO domains are present and often still unknown.176 177 Lastly, substantial heterogeneity within pairwise comparisons was observed, as demonstrated by leave-one-out meta-analysis. This well-established heterogeneity between RA trials is largely explained by methodological differences between studies and included patient populations with different DMARD histories.21,23 However, a wide variety of sensitivity and subgroup analyses confirmed robustness of the results.

Despite the substantial uncertainty reflected in the results, the findings of this study may still provide guidance to rheumatologists when considering specific PRO domains in the selection of a b/tsDMARD. When a particular PRO domain is predominant or especially important to a patient, the forest plots and SUCRA table can serve as useful tools to support shared decision-making. Recent research indicates a link between individualised PRO-based augmentation and clinical outcomes.178 More studies on integrating these PRO domains in the treat-to-target approach are needed.

To identify more significant differences between b/tsDMARDs, future RCTs should include a broader range of PROs as primary endpoints in head-to-head comparisons of b/tsDMARDs.21 Expanding existing core outcome sets with PRO domains may also support this, though reporting of individual core domains remains limited.179 Furthermore, this analysis was limited to detecting differences at the b/tsDMARD class level. Potential within-class differences between individual agents were not addressed, including the potential role of individual JAK inhibitors on pain.180 181 b/tsDMARDs may alleviate symptoms or improve PRO domains only to a limited extent. Altogether, these steps can promote greater use and attention to PROs in clinical practice and support the shift towards more patient-centred VBHC.

Conclusion

Despite substantial heterogeneity and uncertainty in the results, this study offers insights that may support clinical decision making when selecting a b/tsDMARD. Ultimately, the use of one specific b/tsDMARD class over another should depend on patient characteristics, outcome goals and shared decision making. Future research should explore within-class differences between specific b/tsDMARDs, and focus on individualised, PRO-based augmentation within treat-to-target approaches.
