Scoping review of risk factors, cognitive and mental health effects, and interventions for chronic pain among military veterans
Authors: Daniel Miezah, Atta Aboagye Frimpong, Damien Kaba Kwowuje, William Akoto-Buabeng, Jethro Zutah, Susanna Aba Abraham, Isaac Tetteh Commey, Jacob Owusu Sarfo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundChronic pain presents a significant health challenge for military veterans, with a clear association with risk factors and a range of physical, emotional, cognitive, and mental health consequences. This scoping review sought to systematically synthesise studies on risk factors, cognitive and mental health effects, and interventions for chronic pain among veterans.MethodFour major databases (PubMed, PsycINFO, HINARI, and JSTOR) were used for the search. Records from other search engines (Google and Google Scholar) were also included. This review followed the guidelines established by Arksey and O’Malley and PRISMA-ScR.ResultsThe study established various risk elements related to chronic pain, such as age, gender (with a more significant occurrence among females), injury, particular medical or illness conditions, accidents and injuries, and strenuous physical activities from military service. The list of identified consequences of cognitive and mental health includes PTSD, depression, anxiety and anxiety disorders, suicide and suicide ideations, poor attention and executive function, poor learning and memory, catastrophic pain, mood disorders, poor concentration, and poor coping mechanisms. Interventions adopted within chronic pain were effective when implemented early and included multidisciplinary teams.ConclusionTo provide effective and prompt holistic care for veterans with chronic pain, multidisciplinary interventions should be employed, with interdisciplinary healthcare professionals, including mental health and clinical health psychologists, at the centre of treatment. Their function shall be to evaluate pain and its implications for cognitive and mental health, ensuring that all facets of attention are covered.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s44192-026-00491-8.

Introduction

The term “veteran” refers to a person who served in the active military, naval, or air service and was discharged or released from post for reasons other than dishonourable [1]. Veterans often experience wars, disasters, or other life-threatening events, which can increase their risk for long-term health issues. Pain is characterised as an uncomfortable sensory and emotional experience associated with, or resembling, actual or potential tissue damage [2]. Chronic pain, defined as pain persisting or recurring for more than three months, typically beyond normal tissue healing time [3], is a multifaceted condition affecting individuals across different ages, occupations, and backgrounds [4].

Chronic pain is particularly prevalent among military veterans. A 2016 report showed that veterans experience a significantly higher age-related prevalence of chronic pain than non-veterans [5], with approximately 41% of soldiers suffering from long-term pain [6]. It can increase the likelihood that service members will develop long-term health problems [7]. Common causes include musculoskeletal conditions such as back pain and joint disorders [8], as well as war-related injuries like blast wounds and complex polytrauma, which often coincide with traumatic brain injury [9]. The work conditions and exposures during deployment can exacerbate these risks [10]. Chronic pain can restrict physical activity, limit participation in daily life, and reduce overall physical functioning [11, 12]. Chronic pain is also associated with substantial cognitive and mental health effects. It can impair learning, memory, and other cognitive processes [13], and is strongly linked to mental health problems, including anxiety, depression, posttraumatic stress disorder (PTSD), and substance use disorders [14, 15]. When chronic pain co-occurs with these conditions, there may be an increased risk of suicidal ideation [16]. Long-term pain is frequently a key factor in disability among veterans [8], compounding the challenges of managing mental and cognitive health [7, 17].

Chronic pain causes anguish in those who experience it because of its abnormal and insufficient manifestation [18]. PTSD, major depression disorders, and drug abuse are some mental health problems that make it hard for Veterans to deal with their pain [19]. When these illnesses and severe pain happen together, there may be more thoughts of suicide [16]. Traumatic Brain Injury (TBI), major depressive disorder, anxiety, drug use disorder, and sleep disorders often happen together with chronic pain in the military.

The purpose of this review is to map scientific evidence on the risk factors, cognitive and mental health effects, and interventions associated with chronic pain among military veterans. The findings may guide psychologists, nurses, and medical practitioners in supporting veterans presenting with chronic pain. This review aligns with the Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs), particularly Goals 3 (Good Health and Well-being), 8 (Decent Work and Economic Growth), and 10 (Reduced Inequalities) [20].

Methods and materials

This scoping review followed the framework set out by Arksey and O’Malley [21] and used the PRISMA-ScR checklist. The work proceeded through the following stages: (1) identifying and stating the research questions; (2) identifying relevant studies; (3) selection of studies; (4) data collection; (5) data summary and synthesis of results; and (6) consultation. Experts (discipline experts and library staff) were also consulted, and the reference lists were checked for additional relevant studies.

Results

Search results

A total of 3,881 records were retrieved from the four primary databases (PubMed = 23, Hinari = 40, PsycINFO = 3,801, and Journal Storage (JSTOR) = 17). Also, 34 records were retrieved from other sources such as Google and Google Scholar. Of the 3881 records, 32 were duplicates and were removed. In all, 3, 883 records were screened. 3,779 records were removed during screening because their titles or abstracts indicated that they did not address chronic pain among veterans, did not examine cognitive or mental health outcomes, or did not present primary research. Findings

Characteristics of the included studies

This study reviewed 22 articles. Of the 22 articles, 19 were cross-sectional surveys, while 3 were randomised controlled trials. Most studies were conducted in the United States of America (20), while the remaining two were conducted in Canada. All the studies used a sample of 591,186 military veterans. Risk factors of chronic pain among veterans

The study established various risk elements related to chronic pain, such as age, gender, injury, particular medical or illness conditions, accidents and injuries, and strenuous physical activities from military service. Trauma to soft tissues has been implicated in the course of this review as a risk factor for chronic pain among military veterans. Polytrauma to the soft tissues is one of the factors under trauma that causes chronic pain [22, 23]. Microtrauma sustained during field operations and everyday activities also causes chronic pain [23]. Mere trauma also causes chronic pain among military veterans [24, 25]. Chronic pain among military veterans has been found more in women than in men [26]. Studies reported that the age of the veteran is a critical risk factor for chronic pain.

Older age has been implicated as a critical risk factor for chronic pain among military veterans [25, 27]. On the contrary, one study also suggested that younger veterans experience chronic pain [28]. Further, studies found that some medical conditions are significant risk factors for chronic pain among military veterans. Some of these medical conditions include degenerative joint disease and peripheral diabetic neuropathy [25], obesity [26], and worsening health conditions in addition to one or more painful medical conditions [28]. Accidents and injuries have also been identified as influential factors of chronic pain among military veterans. For example, a history of injuries, musculoskeletal and physical injuries sustained during military service can all cause chronic pain among military veterans [26, 27, 29, 30]. Other studies have also shown that physical activity is a significant risk factor for chronic pain among military veterans. For example, work demands, carrying heavy loads, strenuous physical activities, and low aerobic fitness can cause chronic pain among veterans [26], and activity limitations as well as participation restrictions are risk factors for chronic pain among military veterans [31].

Other associated risk factors

There were other risk factors associated with chronic pain among military veterans identified by the review. These included the number of years after the last deployment (1–7 years) and increased symptom awareness [26]. Combat exposures among veterans can also cause chronic pain [32]. Cognitive and mental health effects of chronic pain among military veterans

Cognitive effects of chronic pain among military veterans

The review found that military veterans exhibit poor attention and poor executive functions following the experience of chronic pain [32]. In another study, it was found that due to chronic pain, military veterans suffer from poor concentration [22].

Furthermore, the review identified problems of learning and memory impairment among veterans with chronic pain [13] along with post-concussive symptoms [22]. Poor coping mechanisms were also recognised as a cognitive effect experienced by military veterans as they go through the problems presented by chronic pain [25].

Mental health effects of chronic pain among military veterans

Some studies reported PTSD and PTSD symptoms as mental health effects of chronic pain among military veterans [22, 29–38]. Other studies also indicated depression and depressive symptoms as a common and vital mental health outcome of chronic pain among military veterans [25–31, 35, 38–41] along with other mood disturbances [28]. Anxiety disorders were also identified as a cognitive outcome of chronic pain [26–28, 30, 31, 38, 41, 42]. Some types of stress/distress were identified as serious mental health outcomes of chronic pain among military veterans by this review. These stresses/distresses include psychological stress/distress [24, 32] and emotional and affective distress [28]. Suicide and suicidal thoughts or ideas have been labelled as mental health effects of chronic pain, such that some military veterans are likely to have suicidal thoughts or ideas, and some may even commit suicide [31, 42]. Other military veterans who experience pain may be preoccupied with the view that pain is a central part of their lives and always have catastrophic thinking about the pain. This experience could also lead to a loss of confidence in managing and coping with pain [30]. Additionally, sleep disorders, sleep disturbances, and insomnia were identified as mental health effects of chronic pain among military veterans [31, 32, 35, 38]. Interventions for chronic pain among military veterans

Military veterans with chronic pain need some interventions that will alleviate the pain they are experiencing and reduce other related cognitive and mental health consequences.

For example, studies have reported that pain should be identified early in primary healthcare settings [23, 24].

It has also been suggested that a multidisciplinary pain management program involving primary healthcare providers, behavioural medicine experts, physical medicine and rehabilitation services, and orthopedists should be implemented to manage veterans’ pain [29]. Veterans receiving treatment for chronic pain should also be assessed for depression [27].

Military veterans with chronic pain should be treated entirely in their persons (body and mind) rather than treating a particular disorder [22]. Quality interventions that are needed include improved screening and assessment for early identification of cognitive disorders among veterans with chronic pain [41]. Comprehensive assessment of veterans with chronic pain should include functional status, social determinants of health, exercises, and psychotherapy with drugs in addition [31]. There is a need to treat sleep disorders among veterans with pain and traumatic brain injury [35].

Self-treatment of pain is encouraged among veterans with less pain intensity. However, professional management is needed among veterans with high pain intensity [38].

Veterans with chronic pain and PTSD should be treated with cognitive behavioural therapy by qualified psychologists [30]. Cognitive restructuring and relaxation techniques should be introduced to healthcare providers and should be part of the treatment of chronic pain and PTSD among veterans [37].

There should be explicit written recommendations and frequent follow-ups to ensure veterans complete their treatment, and the use of technology and other devices to support memory should be taught [13]. Discussion

The current review aimed to identify the risk factors, the cognitive and mental health effects, and the management of chronic pain among veterans. The study established various risk elements related to chronic pain, such as age, gender, injury, particular medical or illness conditions, accidents and injuries, and strenuous physical activities from military service. The identified mental health consequences include PTSD, depression, anxiety disorders, suicide suicidal ideations, catastrophic pain, mood disorders, and cognitive impacts such as poor attention and executive function, poor learning and memory, poor concentration, and poor coping mechanisms.

Risk factors of chronic pain among veterans

The findings of the review indicate that the risk factors of chronic pain among veterans include age, trauma, gender, certain medical conditions, accidents and injuries, number of years after the last deployments, and combat exposures.

Older veterans are more likely to experience chronic pain [22, 23]. Nevertheless, Kerns et al. [28] reported that younger veterans are at increased risk of developing chronic pain. Evidence suggests that this pattern is linked to military-specific factors, including intense physical training, musculoskeletal strain, and injuries sustained during service or combat exposure. Older veterans, by contrast, often live with long-standing pain conditions that have persisted over time and may be less likely to report these symptoms in primary care settings [30]. Hence, older adults are less likely to report chronic pain at primary health care centres.

Polytrauma, microtrauma, and trauma to tissues and several organs of the body are also risk factors for chronic pain [22, 23]. Damage or injuries to the musculoskeletal system can leave long-lasting and excruciating pain for veterans [29]. This type of injury may span from blasts as a result of combat exposures [32], work demands and carrying of heavy loads [26], physical injuries from military service [27, 30], and activity limitations and participation restrictions from lack of movement [31].

Some medical conditions have also been identified as risk factors for chronic pain among veterans. Degenerative joint disease and peripheral diabetic neuropathy have been implicated [25]. These conditions cause numbness and tingling, especially in the peripheral joints, such as the legs and knee joints. If this occurs over time, it will result in a painful experience for the veteran, culminating in chronic pain. Obesity among veterans is also a risk factor for chronic pain [26]. The increase in body weight and body mass puts significant pressure on the joints, especially the waist, knees, and ankles. This weight also causes limitations in movement and activity and participation restriction [31], hence resulting in chronic pain.

Interestingly, being a female veteran has been identified as one of the risk factors for chronic pain among veterans [26]. This sex-related risk may be due to calcium depletion in bone mass as women age, leaving bones brittle and fragile. This calcium depletion can also cause osteoporosis, which can cause pain and fractures. Another factor may also be the phenomenon of women being more expressive of their sufferings than men. Thus, females are likely to complain about pain due to this risk.

Cognitive and mental health effects of chronic pain in veterans

The experience of chronic pain among military veterans has left them with serious cognitive and mental health outcomes. These outcomes must be discussed and put in perspective as they bring the neuropsychological issues of chronic pain to bear, not just seen as physical chronic pain.

PTSD, depression, anxiety disorders, psychological distress, affective and emotional distress, suicidal behaviours, poor coping mechanisms, poor attention levels, and executive dysfunctions are the major cognitive and mental health outcomes of chronic pain. Other outcomes include poor concentration, catastrophic thinking about pain, less confidence in the ability to manage pain, viewing pain as more central to their lives, impaired learning and memory, social withdrawal, mood disorders, post-concussive symptoms, insomnia, and other sleep disturbances. Veterans experience all these cognitive and mental health outcomes as a result of the chronic pain they have been experiencing.

Chronic pain among veterans is known to cause poor attention and poor executive functions [32]. The poor executive function makes it difficult for the veteran to make proper judgments and decisions. Hence, they may find it difficult to make everyday decisions about themselves, leading to a high degree of dependency. This dependency may also result in a feeling of inadequacy, which can be stressful for caretakers as well. When executive functions are impaired, there is a resultant effect on perception, attention, expectations, and emotional status [43]. It is expected that high levels of pain may interfere with an individual’s attention span, so that the victim may focus on the pain at the expense of other vital aspects of daily living. As a result, pain can lead to high levels of emotional distress. Poor attention levels also have significant effects on learning and memory formation. Severe episodes of chronic pain affect memory and learning [44], which are essential aspects of the veteran’s executive functions. Thus, learning and memory, which are critical aspects of executive functions, can be severely affected among veterans who are suffering from chronic pain.

Pain is highly related to anxiety disorders such as panic attacks, social phobias, and obsessive–compulsive disorders [43]. Hence, veterans with pain conditions are also likely to have high levels of anxiety. Military veterans who experience chronic pain with PTSD outcomes may also have a lower quality of life. The high intensity of pain is linked to psychological distress and disability. Higher PTSD results in a higher disability rate [45].

Veterans suffering from chronic pain have also been known to experience depression and depressive symptoms [25–30, 35, 38–41, 46]. It is expected that people with chronic pain may be depressed or may have depressive symptoms, and this may be due to the thinking and feeling of being alone or being helpless in life. Major depression can increase one’s number of days experienced in disability up to about 50% [45]. Reports of pain increase in veterans with depression [43]. Hence, veterans reporting to primary healthcare with pains, especially military veterans, need to be screened and treated for depression, which can also enhance the quality of their lives.

Another aspect of the mental health outcomes of chronic pain is mood disorders [28]. The severity of chronic pain can have an impact on the mood of the veteran, which can have a resultant effect on their interpersonal relationships, such as social withdrawal [27]. Consequently, chronic pain may cause psychological distress among the victims of chronic pain, including veterans [24, 32]. Intense pain can cause psychological distress among erstwhile strong veterans. Since the pains can lead to disability and sometimes cause redundancy, it becomes difficult for the victim to develop the proper coping mechanisms needed for adapting and adjusting to everyday life stressors [25]. Veterans are unable to cope with the simple demands of daily living, let alone those of significant others. A serious mental health and cognitive outcome of chronic pain is suicide and the development of suicidal thoughts or ideation [31, 42].

There is a risk of suicidal death among people with chronic pain, which is precipitated by the characteristics of the pain, including the severity and duration of the pain. Sometimes, people with chronic pain feel helpless and miserable, which is a serious predictor of suicide and its related behaviour. Therefore, means that it is vital to screen veterans who report to the primary healthcare services for suicidal thoughts and ideations [43].

Chronic pain among military veterans can also cause insomnia and other sleep disturbances [31, 32, 35, 38]. Severe chronic pain can interfere with the nature and quality of sleep of its victims, including veterans. Headaches, waist pains, backaches, and other body pains cause anxiety, which is likely to interfere with the sleeping patterns of victims. Sleep disturbances appear to contribute to chronic pain among veterans. Hence, pain can lead to poor sleep [47]. The interaction between sleep disorders and pain is that as one increases, the other increases correspondingly. Therefore, this suggests that as chronic pain intensity increases, sleep disturbances also increase. The cycle may be quite apparent and severe among veterans who suffer from polytrauma [47].

Interventions for chronic pain and its cognitive and mental health outcomes among military veterans

Military veterans experiencing chronic pain and their cognitive and mental health effects need prompt and appropriate treatment and interventions to bring them back to the optimum level of healthy living. This early care will reduce the disability and dependence they experience in their daily lives and also result in some level of confidence and hope in their lives. Early identification of pain in primary healthcare settings should be a priority, especially among military veterans [23, 24]. Studies further suggest that pain specialists should be integrated into the interdisciplinary teams that take care of veterans recovering from trauma [23]. Reports of pain in primary healthcare settings should be identified through proper and meticulous screening of veterans’ complaints, including those who are not reporting to the facility because of pain [24]. This approach to screening will enable healthcare officials to know the level of pain experienced by veterans and suggest the appropriate therapy.

Treatment of pain among veterans should not be limited to just the pain or the use of pain medications. The scope of the care should expand to include the services and expertise of behavioural medicine experts, pain specialists, and physical medicine and rehabilitation [30]. These professionals will ensure that the veteran experiencing chronic pain receives holistic treatment. Clinical psychologists can be employed to assess cognitive disorders and mental health needs among veterans with pain early enough [41] for early identification of problems and their corresponding prompt treatment. Healthcare professionals with mental health expertise are needed to take care of veterans with chronic pain [31]. Specifically, multidisciplinary management of pain [27, 29] and the need for cognitive restructuring and therapy for veterans experiencing chronic pain and PTSD co-morbidity [30]. Veterans with memory impairment and chronic pain will benefit from clear and concise written instructions about treatment plans and regular follow-ups [13]. This intervention will help reduce the memory and attention deficit exhibited by some veterans with chronic pain. Healthcare providers of veteran affairs should be made aware of the several treatment methods for PTSD and chronic pain among veterans, which include relaxation and cognitive restructuring techniques [37].

Conclusion

This scoping review provides a comprehensive synthesis of the risk, cognitive and mental health, and interventions for chronic pain among military veterans. Despite the use of cross-sectional studies, it was found that risk factors of chronic pain among veterans include the age of the veteran, gender (higher in females than in males), certain medical conditions, trauma (polytrauma, microtrauma, and trauma), physical injuries, physical activities during military service (workload, carrying of heavy loads, strenuous physical training), number of years after the last deployment, increased symptom awareness and exposure to combats. This review revealed complex cognitive and mental health-related outcomes experienced by veterans suffering from chronic pain and the risk factors of chronic pain among veterans. PTSD and PTSD symptoms, depression and depressive symptoms, anxiety and anxiety disorders, psychological distress, affective and emotional distress, suicide and suicidal ideation, poor coping mechanisms, poor attention and executive function, and poor concentration are some of the cognitive and mental health outcomes of chronic pain among veterans. Other outcomes include catastrophising of the pain, loss of confidence in the ability to manage the pain, the belief that pain is at the central part of life, impaired learning and memory, social withdrawal, mood disorders, and sleeping disturbances. Programmes to enhance the care of veterans with chronic pain should employ multidisciplinary measures and interdisciplinary teams with mental health professionals at the core to ensure the veterans get holistic care and not merely the treatment of pain.

Implication for practice and intervention

The clinical implications of this review cannot be overstated. It is vital to assess PTSD, suicide and suicide ideation, depression, anxiety and anxiety disorders, sleep disturbances, memory and attention deficits, poor concentration and coping mechanisms, and catastrophic thinking about the pains being experienced among veterans with chronic pain. Veterans’ Association (VA) primary health care clinics should routinely screen for symptoms of the cognitive and mental health disorders listed above. Veterans may also greatly benefit from the adoption of this policy by non-VA health facilities. When positive screening or results are identified for any of the above cognitive or mental health outcomes, primary healthcare providers must ensure that clinical services are provided and that health psychologists are employed in the veteran’s overall health and treatment plan.

Furthermore, since these cognitive or mental health outcomes have substantial effects on the severity of pain, veterans experiencing chronic pain with cognitive or mental health co-morbidities must be given intensive therapy. Clinical health psychologists must be employed as a matter of policy to lead the screening and diagnosis of chronic pain among veterans. Ministries and departments of health service, in collaboration with psychological councils and associations, should engage physical therapists and other healthcare providers and introduce them to the use and need of psychological therapies in treating veterans with chronic pain, including veterans. Then, together, they institute some psychological therapies like relaxation techniques and cognitive behavioural therapy to take control of maladaptive self-talk regarding pain management. Exposure therapy to take control of fears, panic, PTSD, and other cognitive outcomes should be explored and used. Maladaptive pain beliefs and coping mechanisms, such as pain catastrophising and pain-related fear of movement, can be dealt with by the use of mindfulness with the help of clinical psychologists. Acceptance of pain and active participation in valued activities must be fostered. Collaboration with families to promote healthy behaviours and self-management is needed to ensure that veterans are accepted, respected, and understood.

Limitations and recommendations for future studies

Most included studies were cross-sectional and conducted in English. Cross-sectional studies have limitations, as causal claims cannot be made, and we may have missed evidence from non-English papers. Additionally, most of the reviewed articles were conducted in the United States of America, with only two conducted in Canada. The proximity of Canada and the United States of America makes the studies lack cultural diversity, which can also influence the expression of pain and its related cognitive and mental health outcomes.
