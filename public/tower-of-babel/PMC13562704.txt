Association of surgical extent with survival in locally advanced gallbladder cancer based on propensity score matching
Authors: Jin Gu, Jia Han, Yan Zhang, Yang Tian
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe treatment for locally advanced gallbladder cancer (GBC) remains controversial, particularly regarding the extent of surgery. This study aimed to evaluate the association between surgical extent and survival outcomes in patients with locally advanced GBC. Patients with locally advanced GBC were identified from the Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER) database. Overall survival (OS) and cancer-specific survival (CSS) were evaluated using Kaplan–Meier curves, Cox proportional hazards models, competing-risk models, and landmark analyses. Propensity score matching (PSM) was performed to minimize confounding, and subgroup analyses were conducted across key clinicopathological variables. Patients in the surgery group had better OS and CSS than those in the non-surgery group (median OS, 16.0 versus 9.0 months; median CSS, 17.0 versus 10.0 months; both P < 0.001), particularly when combined with multimodal therapy. Among surgically treated patients, 388 (19.8%) received radical resection (RR), while 1567 (80.2%) underwent simple resection (SR). RR was associated with improved OS and CSS compared with SR (median OS, 19.0 versus 15.0 months; median CSS, 21.0 versus 16.0 months; both P < 0.05). On multivariate analysis, RR remained independently associated with improved OS and CSS. The survival benefit of RR remained robust in 1- and 3-year landmark analyses. These results remained consistent after PSM and in competing-risk models. Subgroup analyses demonstrated consistent survival benefits from RR across various clinicopathological variables. These findings suggest that surgical intervention is associated with a survival advantage and that RR could be considered an important component of curative-intent treatment for selected patients with locally advanced GBC.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-58974-1.Keywords: Locally advanced gallbladder cancer, Surgery, Radical resection, Multimodal therapy, SEER, SurvivalSubject terms: Cancer, Gastroenterology, Oncology

Introduction

Gallbladder cancer (GBC) is a highly aggressive malignancy arising from the biliary epithelium. It ranks sixth among the most common gastrointestinal malignancies, and is the most prevalent neoplasm of the biliary tract1,2. Based on the global cancer statistics from 2020, there will be a substantial increase in the incidence and mortality due to GBC over the next 20 years, particularly in Asia3. Due to its insidious onset and rapid progression, the diagnosis of GBC is usually made when the disease has already reached an advanced stage, leading to poor treatment outcomes4–6.

The efficacy of systemic treatments, including chemotherapy, radiotherapy, and targeted therapy, for GBC remains unsatisfactory despite recent advances in diagnostic modalities and therapeutic strategies, and surgical resection remains the first choice of curative management for GBC7–10. Patients with American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) stage I and some stage II GBC could achieve favorable postoperative 5-year survival outcomes. However, the majority of patients present with advanced disease, and stage III or higher GBC patients rarely experience long-term survival11–13. Therefore, the management of GBC, a highly lethal cancer, remains a significant challenge in the world, and there is still considerable debate regarding the optimal treatment strategy for patients with advanced GBC.

In this study, we leveraged the latest data from the Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER) database to evaluate the association between surgical treatment and survival outcomes in patients with locally advanced GBC and to identify key prognostic factors. Furthermore, we investigated the impact of extent of surgery on long-term outcomes with rigorous statistical methodologies, including propensity score matching (PSM), subgroup analyses, and landmark analyses.

Materials and methods

Data source and patient population

The patient data were retrieved from the SEER database, a cancer database based on the US population. We obtained the characteristic information of patients from the SEER*Stat Database: Incidence-SEER Research Data, 17 Registries, Nov 2024 Sub (2000–2022), using SEER*Stat version 9.0.4. Histological type was limited to adenocarcinoma (8140/3) according to ICD-O-3. This retrospective cohort study was exempted from the Institutional Review Board of Second Affiliated Hospital of Zhejiang University because SEER data are publicly available and de-identified. The implementation of this study fully adhered to the 1964 Helsinki Declaration.

The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) patients with GBC were clearly diagnosed; (2) patients with locally advanced GBC, defined as AJCC stage III-IV disease without distant metastasis (T1-T4, N0-N2, and M0), and all data had detailed TNM stage information classified according to the 8th Edition of the AJCC cancer staging manual. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) age < 18 years; (2) patients diagnosed with more than 1 malignant primary tumor; (3) patients with a survival time of less than 1 month; (4) absence of causes of death and survival months; (5) unknown treatment. In addition, patients who did not undergo surgery while records failed to indicate whether or not they had received chemotherapy and/or radiotherapy were excluded.

In addition, patients were excluded if they received only local tumor destruction, local tumor excision, electrocautery, laser excision, excisional biopsy, surgery stated to be “debulking”, and surgery not otherwise specified or unknown type. Patients were then divided into two groups based on type of surgery received: simple resection (SR) and radical resection (RR). The SEER coding manual definitions “simple/partial surgical removal of primary site” and “total surgical removal of primary site” were categorized in this study as simple resection (SR) (NAACCR item 1290, codes 30 and 40). The use of radical resection (RR) refers to the SEER designation “radical surgery” (NAACCR item 1290, code 60)14.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 27.0 statistical software (SPSS, Inc., Chicago, IL, USA) and R version 4.4.1 for Windows. Categorical variables are reported as numbers (n) and proportions (%) and were compared using the chi-squared test with Yates’ correction or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. The following variables were included in the PSM analyses: age, gender, marital status, year of diagnosis, race, grade, AJCC T stage, AJCC N stage, chemotherapy status, and radiotherapy status. A 1:1 nearest-neighbor matching technique was used, with a caliper value of 0.115. Covariate balance before and after PSM was assessed using standardized mean differences (SMDs).

Overall survival (OS) was defined as the time from diagnosis until death or last follow-up, whereas cancer-specific survival (CSS) referred to the time from diagnosis until death specifically caused by GBC or last follow-up. Survival analyses of OS and CSS rates were performed using the Kaplan–Meier method and compared using the log-rank test. Survival curves were generated utilizing the “survival” and “survminer” packages in R. The Cox proportional hazards model was used to identify independent risk factors associated with OS and CSS by multivariate analysis. To address competing risks in cancer-specific outcomes, both cause-specific hazard model and Fine-Gray sub-distribution hazard function model were implemented. Landmark analyses at 1-, 3-, and 5-year intervals were conducted to assess the long-term and time-dependent impact of extent of surgery. Furthermore, we analyzed the interactions between the extent of surgery and each prognostic variable using the Cox proportional hazard model. The forest plot was created using the “forestploter” package in R. A two-sided P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Patient and demographics details

Based on our predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria, a total of 2121 patients were finally included in this study (Fig. 1). Table 1 presents a summary of the baseline characteristics for all participants. Among the total cohort, 1955 individuals (92.2%) underwent surgery, while 166 (7.8%) were classified as non-surgery group. Notably, significant statistical differences were observed in gender (P = 0.010), marital status (P = 0.018), race (P = 0.004), and chemotherapy status (P < 0.001) between the two groups. Additionally, treatment type correlated with AJCC T stage, AJCC N stage, and tumor grade (All P < 0.001). Although most patients in the non-surgery group were classified as N0, the majority had advanced AJCC T stage (T3/T4, 95.8%), supporting their classification as locally advanced GBC.

To reduce baseline differences between the two groups, patients with and without surgery were matched using PSM. After 1:1 PSM, 74 patients were matched in each group. Baseline differences between the two groups were reduced (all P > 0.05; Table 1). Covariate balance before and after PSM was further assessed using SMDs, as shown in Supplementary Table 1 and Supplementary Fig. 1.

Survival analysis before and after PSM

The median OS and CSS of the entire cohort were 15.0 months and 16.0 months, respectively. Further investigation revealed that the median OS of the surgery group was significantly greater than that of the non-surgery group (16.0 versus 9.0 months; P < 0.001; Fig. 2A). The OS rates at 1-, 3-, and 5-year in the surgery group were 58.6%, 25.0%, and 15.4%, respectively, while in the non-surgery group, they were 32.8%, 3.8%, and 2.8%, respectively. With regard to CSS, the median CSS of the surgery group was also significantly longer (17.0 versus 10.0 months; P < 0.001; Fig. 2B). The cumulative CSS rates in the surgery group at 1-, 3-, and 5-year were 61.9%, 28.5%, and 19.5%, respectively, whereas they were 35.2%, 4.2%, and 3.2% in the non-surgery group. As indicated in Table 2, the multivariate analysis demonstrated that surgery was associated with a better survival compared to non-surgery (HR = 0.476; 95% CI 0.376–0.602; P < 0.001 for OS; HR = 0.487; 95% CI 0.381–0.623; P < 0.001 for CSS). Similarly, patients who received chemotherapy (HR = 0.706; 95% CI 0.629–0.793; P < 0.001 for OS; HR = 0.718; 95% CI 0.635–0.813; P < 0.001 for CSS) or radiotherapy (HR = 0.819; 95% CI 0.725–0.924; P = 0.001 for OS; HR = 0.860; 95% CI 0.758–0.977; P = 0.020 for CSS) experienced a significant reduction in mortality. Moreover, high mortality for both OS and CSS was independently associated with age over 65, marital status ‘others’, poor/undifferentiated tumor, and AJCC T3/T4 stage while the diagnostic period of both 2010–2015 and 2016–2022 showed a strong relationship to lower mortality.

In the PSM cohort, the median OS of the surgery group was significantly greater than that of the non-surgery group (22.0 versus 11.0 months; P < 0.001; Fig. 2C). The OS rates at 1-, 3-, and 5-year in the surgery group were 66.2%, 35.3%, and 21.6%, respectively, while in the non-surgery group, they were 39.2%, 3.6%, and 1.8%, respectively. As for CSS, there was also a significant difference in median CSS between the surgery and non-surgery cohorts (22.0 versus 11.0 months; P < 0.001; Fig. 2D). The cumulative CSS rates in the surgery group at 1-, 3-, and 5-year were 67.2%, 38.7%, and 28.3%, respectively, whereas they were 43.6%, 4.3%, and 2.1% in the non-surgery group. Multivariate analysis showed that surgery was associated with a better survival compared to non-surgery (HR = 0.436; 95% CI 0.293–0.650; P < 0.001 for OS; HR = 0.443; 95% CI 0.291–0.673; P < 0.001 for CSS) after PSM (Supplementary Table 2).

Considering that multivariate analysis identified surgery, chemotherapy, and radiotherapy as protective factors for patients with locally advanced GBC, the OS and CSS were further compared among the 3 groups: non-surgery, surgery alone, and surgery plus multimodal therapy (combining surgery with chemotherapy, radiotherapy, or both). As shown in Supplementary Fig. 2, compared to the non-surgery group and surgery alone group, the surgery plus multimodal therapy group demonstrated significantly greater improvements in patient prognosis, including both OS and CSS (all P < 0.001).

Prognostic factors from Cox regression

In fully adjusted Cox models, surgery conferred an independent prognostic factor for improved OS (HR = 0.46; 95% CI 0.37–0.59; P < 0.001; Supplementary Table 3), a result substantiated by both cause-specific and subdistribution hazard models. These findings demonstrate the robustness of surgical benefit, independent of other confounders.

Simple resection (SR) versus radical resection (RR)

Among the 1955 patients who underwent surgery, 388 (19.8%) were classified into the RR group and 1567 (80.2%) into the SR group. Patient characteristics are listed in Table 3. The correlation analysis indicated that variables such as age (P < 0.001), gender (P = 0.018), marital status (P = 0.004), year of diagnosis (P = 0.028), race (P = 0.036), grade (P < 0.001), AJCC T stage (P < 0.001), AJCC N stage (P = 0.005), chemotherapy status (P < 0.001), and radiotherapy status (P < 0.001) were significantly associated with RR. After conducting 1:1 PSM, the distribution of all included variables was effectively balanced (Table 3).

Subgroup analyses

Subgroup analyses were conducted to evaluate the association between surgical extent and survival outcomes across different patient subgroups (Figs. 4 and 5). Notably, in analyses stratified by AJCC T stage, RR was associated with better OS and CSS in the T1/T2 subgroup (OS, P = 0.013; CSS, P = 0.002) and in the T3/T4 subgroup (both P < 0.001). Similar associations were also observed across several clinically relevant subgroups, including age, tumor grade, and AJCC N stage.

Overall survival stratified by the extent of surgery across different subgroups.

Cancer-specific survival stratified by the extent of surgery across different subgroups.

The forest plots of Cox subgroup interaction analyses are shown in Fig. 6. For OS, the association between RR and improved survival was observed in the T1/T2 subgroup (HR = 0.61; 95% CI 0.41–0.90; P = 0.014) and the T3/T4 subgroup (HR = 0.73; 95% CI 0.61–0.87; P < 0.001), with no significant interaction by AJCC T stage (P for interaction = 0.448). Similar findings were observed for CSS in the T1/T2 subgroup (HR = 0.51; 95% CI 0.33–0.79; P = 0.002) and the T3/T4 subgroup (HR = 0.71; 95% CI 0.59–0.85; P < 0.001), with no significant interaction by AJCC T stage (P for interaction = 0.198). In the remaining subgroup analyses, the association between RR and improved OS and CSS was generally consistent across age, AJCC N stage, and chemotherapy status. Furthermore, a significant interaction was observed between gender and surgical extent for both OS and CSS (both P for interaction < 0.05), suggesting that the association between RR and survival may differ by gender.

Temporal dynamics of surgical benefit

In terms of overall survival, the RR group exhibited a significant survival advantage over the SR group at 1 and 3 years (all P < 0.05; Fig. 7A, B), but this benefit diminished by the 5-year mark (P = 0.200) (Fig. 7C). Consistent results were observed for cancer-specific survival (Fig. 7D–F).

Discussion

Globally, the incidence and mortality of GBC have been increasing annually, with the highest disease burden observed in Eastern Asia, South America, and Melanesia16. Surgery is considered the main treatment modality for GBC. However, the prognosis of GBC remains dismal, largely attributable to its insidious nature and consequent delayed diagnosis at advanced stages. Currently, it remains controversial whether locally advanced GBC patients can gain survival advantages from more active and radical surgical treatment. Therefore, it is challenging to find better therapeutic strategy for patients with locally advanced GBC.

In our present study, we explored the survival benefit in advanced GBC patients who received surgery using the latest data from the SEER database. Then, to clarify the true impact of treatment modality on long-term survival, we performed PSM analysis by adjusting for potential confounders. We found that patients who underwent surgical procedures had a higher survival rate than the non-surgery group before and after PSM. Multivariate analysis further identified surgery as an independent prognostic factor (P < 0.001). These findings reveal that surgery enhances long-term survival for patients with locally advanced GBC without distant metastases. This is consistent with the results of previous studies. Mao et al.17 studied the impact of surgery on survival in patients with advanced GBC from the SEER database between 2004 and 2015. They included patients with stage III and IV disease according to the 2004 AJCC staging system and reported the improved survival for surgery in advanced GBC patients (P < 0.001). Similarly, Li et al.18 employed the Cox regression model to compare the prognosis among various treatment modalities for patients with advanced GBC. They divided patients into 12 groups according to surgical status, chemotherapy status, and radiotherapy status and reported that “no surgery plus no chemotherapy plus no radiotherapy” model had the lowest CSS. However, neither study further evaluated the prognostic influence of the extent of surgical resection on long-term survival for patients with advanced GBC.

Current literature regarding radical surgical resection for advanced GBC is heterogeneous and conflicting19–21. The scope of radical surgery for advanced GBC generally includes cholecystectomy, the removal of adjacent liver tissue, lymph node dissection, and the resection of influenced organ (s)10. Several studies suggested that aggressive surgery was beneficial for patient survival. Kuipers et al.22 performed a multicenter cohort study to analyze the outcomes of patients who underwent extended resections for locally advanced GBC and confirmed the role of RR resulting in some patients achieving a survival time of more than 2 years. Kang reported that radical surgery can prolong survival time even in stage IV GBC patients23. In addition, studies from Japan indicated that if the tumor was relatively limited and carefully screened, even if the lesion was large and had adjacent organ invasion, patients with stage IV GBC could also achieve long-term survival after RR24,25. However, some authors questioned the therapeutic value of aggressive resection, considering that extended surgery failed to have significant survival benefit, and non-radical surgery was more recommended26,27. Given these mixed findings, we conducted a larger cohort study to evaluate the role of radical surgery in the treatment of locally advanced GBC. Although our study was retrospective in nature, we applied PSM analysis to balance baseline characteristics. Before adjusting for baseline variables by PSM, our study found that both long-term OS and CSS were better in the RR group than in the SR group. At baseline, we found significant imbalances in age, gender, marital status, year of diagnosis, grade, AJCC T stage, AJCC N stage, chemotherapy status, and radiotherapy status between the RR and SR groups, which may affect the final outcome. After adjusting clinical factors with PSM and Cox regression models, long-term OS and CSS remained significantly different in patients receiving RR and SR. The survival advantage was confirmed by competing-risk models, which revealed that the benefit is attributable to a specific reduction in cancer mortality rather than being confounded by competing risks. We further conducted landmark analyses to address potential selection bias from early death. RR demonstrated a robust and sustained OS and CSS benefit in patients surviving beyond 1 and 3 years (all P < 0.05). While the difference did not reach statistical significance at the 5-year landmark, this may reflect limited statistical power due to the reduced sample size among long-term survivors. Furthermore, subgroup analyses suggested that the association between RR and improved survival was generally consistent across key prognostic variables. Notably, this association was evident in both the T1/T2 and T3/T4 subgroups, and no significant interaction was observed by AJCC T stage, suggesting that the main findings were unlikely to be explained simply by differences in tumor invasion depth. Taken together, these findings indicate that advanced tumor stage should not preclude selected patients from consideration for radical surgery.

However, surgery alone is not enough, as the 3-year recurrence rate after curative-intent resection for biliary tract cancer is estimated to be as high as 80%28,29. Surgery, chemotherapy, and radiotherapy were shown to be independent factors influencing survival and prognosis in the present study. Systemic chemotherapy remains an important part of the multimodal approach30. Although GBC is characterized by an aggressive nature and relatively short progression-free survival, our data substantiate that chemotherapy provides a definitive survival advantage. Currently, the gemcitabine and cisplatin (GC) regimen remains the established treatment option for GBC31–33. Moreover, the advent of neoadjuvant chemotherapy has created opportunities for downstaging tumors, therefore enhancing the rate of radical resection and prolonging survival34–36. Recently, with the implementation of novel drugs and more clinical trials, more chemotherapy regimens have emerged, such as FOLFOX, modified FOLFIRINOX, and GEMOX regimens32,37. Furthermore, the value of radiotherapy should not be underestimated. Although less commonly used than chemotherapy, radiotherapy still has a positive role38. Some scholars have pointed out that after surgery for advanced GBC, not using radiotherapy or chemoradiotherapy can increase the risk of local recurrence39,40. In patients with advanced GBC, radiotherapy is usually combined with chemotherapy to enhance therapeutic efficacy, either as adjuvant chemoradiotherapy or neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy41,42. Notably, our stratified analysis revealed that patients receiving surgery plus multimodal therapy achieved significantly superior survival outcomes compared with those in the surgery alone or non-surgery groups. Given the high complexity and mortality associated with GBC, defining the optimal sequencing and selection of these multimodal strategies remains a critical imperative for future research.

The present study has several limitations. First, this retrospective analysis using the SEER database may introduce selection bias. Although a PSM method was used to reduce the confounding factors, selection bias could not be avoided. In the surgery versus non-surgery comparison, only 74 patients in each group were retained after PSM, suggesting limited overlap between the two groups. Therefore, residual imbalance and limited exchangeability may still remain despite matching. Second, the SEER database lacks information on several clinically relevant factors, including comorbidities, performance status, liver function, jaundice severity, perioperative complications, and 90-day postoperative mortality. These factors may have influenced treatment selection and prognosis, particularly the decision not to perform surgery. In addition, the high proportion of unknown tumor grade in the non-surgery group may reflect incomplete diagnostic evaluation, poor clinical condition, rapidly progressive disease, or diagnosis near the end of life, which may have biased the surgery versus non-surgery comparison in favor of surgery. Although tumor grade was included in the PSM and the multivariate Cox regression analyses, residual unmeasured bias could not be completely excluded. Third, the SEER database does not provide sufficiently detailed operative information, which may have led to heterogeneity within the SR and RR categories. Therefore, we were unable to determine R0/R1/R2 resection status, distinguish wedge liver resection from anatomical liver resection, determine whether bile duct resection or hepatopancreaticoduodenectomy was performed, assess the extent of lymphadenectomy, or identify the proportion of incidental GBC cases. Fourth, obtaining specific chemotherapy regimens for GBC proved challenging due to limitations of the SEER database which could potentially influence and lead to bias on results. Fifth, information on postoperative tumor recurrence was unavailable, which may have affected survival outcomes and subsequent treatment. Therefore, further multicenter and high-quality prospective studies are needed to validate the results of the current study.

Conclusion

In this large population-based study of patients with locally advanced GBC, surgical intervention was significantly associated with improved OS and CSS, particularly when integrated with multimodal therapy. Regarding the extent of resection for locally advanced disease, RR was associated with improved long-term survival compared with SR. The survival benefit of RR remained consistent after PSM and across multiple sensitivity and subgroup analyses, including landmark timepoints. Taken together, our findings suggest that RR could be considered an important component of curative-intent treatment for selected patients with locally advanced GBC, and emphasize that a multidisciplinary approach remains a valuable strategy in curative treatment.
