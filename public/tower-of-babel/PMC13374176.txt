Application of Q-methodology in chronic disease research: a scoping review
Authors: Li Zhang, Xue Yang, Guirong Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAs the global population ages, the burden of chronic disease management continues to intensify. Guided by a patient-centered medical philosophy, there is an urgent need to develop research methods capable of capturing in depth the complex subjective experiences of patients, caregivers, and healthcare providers. This approach aims to address the limitations of current homogeneous intervention strategies. Q-methodology, a research method for exploring the complexities of human subjectivity, demonstrates significant potential across multiple fields, including the management of chronic diseases. This study aims to examine the characteristics of existing Q-methodology research on chronic diseases, including research questions, methodological variations, and implications for research in this field.MethodsThis study followed the updated scope review methodology of the Joanna Briggs Institute, as well as the PRISMA-ScR guidelines (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Scoping Reviews). Literature searches were conducted from inception to November 1, 2025, across four databases: PubMed, Web of Science Core Collection, Embase, and Cochrane Library. The search was limited to English-language publications. Data extraction primarily utilized the Q-methodology evaluation and review tool.ResultsFifteen studies from six countries were included. Participants were individuals with chronic diseases, caregivers, and healthcare providers. Sample sizes ranged from 23 to 60 in the Q Sample and 13 to 136 in the P Sample. Research questions fell into three areas: identifying needs and preferences for support and interventions (N = 9), comparing differences in viewpoints (N = 4), and understanding patients' intrinsic cognition and experiences (N = 2). Each study reported 3 to 8 unique perspectives. Q-methodology use is increasing in chronic disease research; however, methodological variations persist. Notable differences appear in determining the Q sample, selecting P-samples, conducting Q-sorting, and data analysis. Its application in chronic disease studies facilitates the exploration of group perspectives and the comparative analysis of differences in viewpoints, enabling a deeper capture of complex subjective experiences within populations.ConclusionsPreliminary evidence suggests that Q-methodology has clinical utility and scientific rigor in chronic disease research. However, given the limited number of studies and methodological heterogeneity, more research is needed to confirm these findings. Future efforts should focus on developing more scientifically comprehensive reporting checklists to standardize their use.Trial registrationAs the research did not directly involve animals or humans, no ethical review was conducted.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12874-026-02898-x.

Background

Rationale

The global trend of population aging and the prevalence of multimorbidity are continuously increasing the burden of chronic disease management [1, 2]. Chronic disease management is essentially a long-term process, with its core lying in patient self-management [3]. This process is not solely driven by objective biomedical indicators, but is profoundly influenced by the patient’s subjective world, including their beliefs about the illness, treatment preferences, and needs [4]. Understanding the subjective world is crucial for predicting and improving patient self-management behaviors and ultimately their health outcomes.

Researchers often opt for familiar qualitative research methods such as semi-structured interviews and focus groups due to the methodological limitations of quantitative research in revealing individuals’subjective worlds [5]. However, qualitative research findings have methodological limitations, such as being difficult to test or verify, and research conclusions are easily influenced by the researcher’s personal biases and characteristics [6].

When a single method is not applicable, mixed research methods may be a better choice, as only from a broader perspective can the complexity of the studied phenomenon be treated fairly [6, 7]. In recent years, one mixed research method, namely the Q methodology, has been considered helpful in identifying and comparing the views of patients, doctors, nurses, and educators in relevant medical research [8, 9], and has shown unique application value in chronic disease management, health behavior intervention and patients' psychological experience research [10, 11]. The Q methodology, proposed by William Stephenson, is a psychological research method for studying human “subjectivity,” combining qualitative and quantitative approaches to reveal a group's attitudes or opinions toward a particular phenomenon [8, 9, 12]. Like qualitative research, the Q methodology uses small sample sizes and is suitable for exploring emerging issues. It does not require a large number of participants to provide highly reliable experimental results, while also possessing the quantitative research advantages of using statistical software packages to analyze data [13–16].

Although the Q methodology has been widely applied in research fields such as sociology and education and is gradually being adopted in medical research, it remains relatively novel. A review of the literature reveals that obstacles such as a lack of understanding of the method’s application and design, obscure technical terminology, unfamiliarity with the data collection process, and the absence of standardized recommendations for reporting Q methodology studies can affect its use by chronic disease researchers [8, 12]. Reading low-quality studies with incorrect procedures may also lead readers to question the scientific validity and rigor of the method. The scoping review systematically retrieves, screens, and integrates existing knowledge to explore an exploratory research question and provide guidance for future research priorities [17]. Its findings can promote the updating of knowledge in a given field [18]. Therefore, this study aims to systematically integrate and analyze relevant literature by reviewing the application of Q methodology in chronic disease research, providing a reference for other researchers to apply Q methodology in the field of chronic diseases, and offering a basis for more targeted and personalized interventions for patients with chronic diseases.

Objectives

Research Questions: 1) What research purposes use Q-methodology in chronic disease studies? 2) What methodological differences exist in the research design and implementation of Q-methodology in chronic diseases? 3) What major insights has Q-methodology provided in chronic disease research?

Methods

Protocol and registration

This study followed the updated scope review methodology of the Joanna Briggs Institute [19] and was conducted according to PRISMA-ScR [20] (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses—Scope Review; Appendix A). We have registered on the Open Science Framework (removed for review).

Eligibility criteria

The inclusion criteria used in our literature screening process were developed based on the research question and detailed using the Joanna Briggs Institute's recommended conceptual framework for defining the scope of a study—namely 'Population, Concept, and Context' [21] (Appendices B.Table S1). To capture subjective perspectives related to chronic diseases, the study subjects are not limited to patients with chronic conditions; their caregivers and healthcare providers are also included. To ensure methodological comparability and rigor in the conclusions of this review, studies that merely mention the Q methodology without actual application or without conducting factor analysis will be excluded. Only peer-reviewed original research published in English will be included, while reviews, books, and master's or doctoral theses will be excluded, focusing on empirical evidence that has undergone peer review and can directly evaluate methodological application. No starting year restriction was set, aiming to systematically trace and capture the full development and application of Q methodology in chronic disease research.

Search strategy

This study follows the three-step method recommended by the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) to develop a systematic and comprehensive search strategy for the following four databases: PubMed, Web of Science Core Collection, Embase, and the Cochrane Library. First, through an initial scoping search in PubMed, we deconstructed the review question into two core concepts: (1) research methods, namely studies applying the Q methodology; and (2) research field, namely studies related to chronic diseases. The search strategy was developed based on the PCC framework, as detailed in (Appendices B. Table S1). Specifically, the two core concepts—Q methodology (corresponding to the Concept, C) and chronic disease (corresponding to the Context, C)—were derived from this framework. For each concept, we extracted all relevant terms from the titles, abstracts, and subject headings of the literature obtained in the preliminary search. Under the guidance of an information specialist, we combined two sets of terms using the Boolean operator “AND” to construct search strategies tailored to each database, and applied the corresponding search filters to optimize the results. The complete search strategies are listed in Appendices B.Table S2. The final search strategy was subjected to sensitivity testing and peer-reviewed by a second information specialist using the Peer Review of Electronic Search Strategies (PRESS) checklist [22]. All database searches were conducted from inception to November 1, 2025. The search was conducted independently by two researchers (Z and Y). After the search, the records retrieved by both researchers were compared to ensure consistency. Any discrepancies in the search results were discussed and resolved, and a consensus was reached on the final set of records to be exported for screening. In addition, we performed backward citation searching of the reference lists of all included studies to supplement any potentially overlooked relevant literature.

Source of evidence screening and selection

All retrieved literature records were imported into EndNote X9.3.3 software for unified management. Before formal screening, the two reviewers (Z and Y) independently piloted 20 randomly selected records to calibrate their understanding of the inclusion criteria. Discrepancies were discussed and resolved before proceeding with the full screening. Literature screening followed a dual independent review process: first, researchers Z and Y independently conducted an initial screening of titles and abstracts based on the inclusion criteria; subsequently, the full texts of the preliminarily included literature were reviewed to determine final eligibility. Literature screening followed a dual independent review process. Inter-rater agreement was not formally assessed. Instead, at each stage, any disagreements between the two reviewers were resolved through discussion; if no agreement could be reached, a third researcher (L) was invited to arbitrate until a final consensus was achieved.

Data extraction

The primary method used was the Q-methodology assessment and review tool (ARIQ) developed by Dziopa and Ahern in 2011 [12] to extract data, covering general study information, research objectives, Q-sort development and design, population, statistical analysis, and study results. Zabala and Kenward et al. added five additional aspects based on this [23, 24]: 1) country, 2) human subject protection, 3) concourse description, 4) variance explanation, and 5) data presentation. During the review process, we referred to the key definitions of Q methodology research summarized by Hensel et al. for reference [8] (Appendices C). Two reviewers (Z and Y) independently extracted data from all included studies using the standardized Excel form. Discrepancies between the two reviewers were resolved through discussion; if no agreement could be reached, a third reviewer (L) was consulted.

Synthesis of results

The synthesis and analysis of this study’s results followed a systematic process in three stages. The first stage presented the basic characteristics of the included studies in table form through descriptive statistics and narrative summaries, including author, year, country, title, journal, research objectives, and study subjects, to outline the overall profile of the field. The second stage focused on methodological comparison, systematically extracting and analyzing each study’s practices in key aspects of the Q methodology, including concourse development, composition of the Q sample, characteristics of the P sample, sorting process, and data analysis methods, using a combination of narrative synthesis and comparative tables. The third stage was narrative integration, aiming to summarize and interpret the substantive findings of each study, identify the core viewpoints or attitude types recognized in the field of chronic disease, and distill their implications for clinical practice, policy-making, and future research.

Results

Selection of sources of evidence

Through the systematic search strategy, we retrieved a total of 2,780 relevant articles from four databases. After removing 351 duplicates, 2,429 articles were screened, and 2,410 articles that did not meet the inclusion and exclusion criteria were excluded. Full-text review was conducted on the remaining 19 articles, of which 15 met the inclusion criteria for this scoping review. We also manually reviewed the reference lists of the included studies, but no additional studies meeting the inclusion and exclusion criteria were identified. Article selection was conducted according to the PRISMA guidelines [25], as shown in Appendices B Figure S1.

The International Journal of Nursing Studies published the most articles (N = 3). These articles were published at irregular intervals between 2003 and 2025. Study characteristics

Research purposes

The research objectives of the 15 studies broadly fall into three categories. Nine studies aimed to identify the needs and preferences for personalized support and interventions among chronic disease populations [27, 29, 31–33, 36–39]. Four studies aimed to reveal comparative perspectives on chronic disease care and management from diverse viewpoints [26, 28, 34, 35]. The first two types predominated, while other research focused on chronic disease patients' intrinsic cognition regarding the complex health concepts and experiences associated with their conditions [30, 40].

Seven studies explored subjective perceptions and preferences in chronic disease management from the patient perspective, focusing on topics such as self-management, medical decision-making, and service transitions [27, 29, 31, 32, 36, 38, 39]. One study examined the unique views of nurses on self-management support in chronic care from the healthcare provider perspective [33]. In comparing differing perspectives across multiple viewpoints, the focus primarily centers on chronic disease patients and the healthcare providers [28, 34, 35]. San et al. adopted a triadic approach to identify priority needs among children with chronic illnesses, their parents, and the nurses caring for them [26]. Chon et al. and Risdon et al., respectively, examined dialysis patients' perceptions of resilience and chronic pain sufferers' experiences of pain [30, 40].

Methodological variations

Determine the Q sample

In most of the 15 studies, the specific number of concourses was clearly reported, ranging from 52 to 372 (mean = 186) (see Table 2). Only two studies did not specify this [35, 36], and one study estimated the minimum value [38]. The determination and generation of the Q-sample were mainly conducted through literature review, collection of publicly available materials, or qualitative interviews [9]. Nine studies adopted a combination of literature review, qualitative interviews, and expert discussions [26, 27, 29–34, 37], two studies combined literature review and expert discussions [28, 35], two studies used only literature review and qualitative interviews [36, 40], and two studies used only qualitative interviews [38, 39].The range of Q samples varied from 23 to 60 (mean = 40.13, standard deviation = 9.13). Extraction/rotation

Select P sample

Among the included studies, ten explicitly reported their P sample selection strategies [26, 27, 29, 31–33, 36, 37, 39, 40], primarily based on purposeful sampling and the principle that Q methodology requires small, information-rich samples to capture diverse perspectives rather than population representativeness. The remaining five studies [28, 30, 34, 35, 38] did not clearly describe their P sample selection process. The average P sample size included in the study exceeded the average Q sample size, with a ratio of approximately 1:0.85.

Q sort

In the Q distribution, only one study did not specify details [38], with distribution ranges including −3/+ 3, −4/+ 4, −5/+ 5, −6/+ 6, and a scale of 9 points (see Table 2). The most common range was −3/+ 3 (see Table 3). Professor Brown believes that the choice of Q-method distribution depends on the number of Q samples and the expected viewpoints, and there is no single correct forced distribution [41]. When the sample size is small and the expected viewpoints vary greatly, a steeper distribution (−3/+ 3) is appropriate; conversely, a flatter distribution (−4/+ 4 or −5/+ 5) can be selected [41]. Regarding sorting instructions, most studies provided clear explanations, explicit sorting instructions (N = 9), brief descriptions (N = 2), unclear or no instructions (N = 2). In terms of comments collected after sorting, except for two studies [38, 40], all others obtained participants’comments. All studies underwent relevant ethical review. Data analysis

There are significant methodological differences and commonalities in the data analysis phase of the studies included. In terms of programs, 15 studies used three main statistical software or programs for data analysis: PQ Method (N = 11), PC QUANL (N = 2), and other programs (N = 2) (see Table 2). PQ Method is the most commonly used software package, with various versions including 2.09, 2.11, 2.33 and 2.35. It demonstrates the long-term influence and continuous updates of this software in the field of Q methodology research. Studies using this program are widely distributed in the Netherlands, the United Kingdom, Turkey, and Sweden. PC QUANL comes second, but it is worth noting that all these studies were completed by Korean authors [30, 39], indicating a preference for this program among Korean academic researchers. One study used the program Ken-Q software (version 2.0.1) [27], and one study used the statistical software SPSS [34]. It should be noted that SPSS cannot generate the descriptive tables necessary for testing consensus views and may not support specific Q-method outputs such as consensus tables [14]. In factor extraction, 10 studies used PCA, and 5 studies used CFA. The Ramlo study mentioned that Brown and Stephenson preferred using CFA over PCA [14]. This is because CFA focuses on common variance, and the results are indeterminate, meaning the process does not yield a unique correct mathematical outcome [42]. Except for two studies that did not specify the rotation method, all other studies used Varimax. Unlike the study by Hensel et al. [8], one study in this review performed By-hand Rotation in addition to Varimax [36].

Data reporting methods appeared to differ by region, which is consistent with the findings of Hensel et al. [8]. In Asian regions represented by Turkey and Korea [26, 27, 30, 39], researchers tend to present original, standardized Z-scores in their final reports, whether using PQ Method or PC QUANL software. In contrast, European regions represented by the Netherlands and the UK [29, 31, 33–38] tend to present statistical test results based on Q-scores. Ten studies explicitly reported the existence of consensus statements. Regarding distinctive statements, eight studies used p < 0.05 or p < 0.01 as the standard for assessment, six studies used an absolute Z-score greater than 1 (|Z|> 1) as the standard, and one study did not specify. In terms of data presentation completeness (Table 3), nine studies fully presented scores for all factors and statements through tables or a combination of text descriptions, while the remaining six studies only displayed the rankings of distinctive statements, highest/lowest scoring statements, or key statements.

Implications for chronic disease research

In the factor results section, 15 studies extracted an average of 4 viewpoints or factors, with an average Variance Explained of 53.64% (Table 3). Most study participants were chronic disease patients, while some studies included both chronic disease populations and healthcare providers, with only one study involving nurses.These studies revealed similarities and differences in the perspectives of patients, family members, and healthcare providers regarding chronic disease care and management. The included studies provided recommendations on how to meet the personalized needs of chronic disease patients. They also explored chronic disease patients’ cognition and understanding of concepts related to their conditions. Additionally, new requirements were proposed for clinical practice and professional development in the field of chronic diseases, such as role cognition.

In chronic disease management, there are generally differences in perspectives among patients, healthcare providers, and family members. Güneş et al. [26] found divergences in care priorities among children, families, and nurses in pediatric chronic disease care. Shahed et al. [28] pointed out that doctors and patients hold differing views on obesity. Stenner et al. [34] emphasized the importance of paying attention to differences in perspectives between chronic low back pain patients and healthcare providers regarding self-management. All of the above studies highlight both the existence of these differences and the importance of acknowledging them to promote the formation of consensus views.

Providing recommendations that meet the personalized needs of chronic disease patients is targeted and directed. Jeong et al. [27] advocate, based on their research findings, the development of personalized smart systems suitable for elderly use and promoting their emotional well-being. Buljac-Samardzic et al. [29] suggest that when formulating strategies to enhance the active participation of chronic disease patients, the diversity of patient perspectives should be taken into account. Hislop et al. [31] point out that, to achieve an effective and efficient transition of adolescent chronic disease patients to adult medical care, their transition preferences and viewpoints should be understood, consistent with Jedeloo et al. [37].

Unlike the focus of the previous studies, the other two studies examined the perceptions and experiences of chronic disease patients regarding their own conditions. Chon et al. [30] pointed out that future research should assess these patients’subjective cognition of resilience. Risdon et al. [40] suggested that future studies should focus on the relationship between changes in identity and chronic pain.

Discussion

The value of Q-methodology in chronic disease research

In the field of chronic diseases, the Q method has unique advantages for identifying different perspectives on a subject. In healthcare, Churruca suggests that the Q method should be considered when an overall understanding of differing views on a topic is needed to achieve convergence [9]. In nursing education, Hensel believes that using the Q method to study group attitudes is a systematic, efficient, and comprehensive approach [8]. Similarly, this review of 15 relevant studies found that the number of distinct perspectives reported per study ranged from 3 to 8, with an average of 53 participants per study. Ramlo also pointed out that the Q method is not limited to Q sorting; it is applicable in any situation where researchers are interested in perspectives, needs, opinions, judgments, decisions, etc., based on subjectivity [25].

In addition, we found that the Q-method is relatively friendly to children, adolescents, and elderly groups with chronic diseases. The interesting operation process of the Q-method offers flexibility in revealing the complex subjective world of chronic disease patients, providing direct insights into the potential structure of participants’perceptions of images [43], and is worth promoting among a wider range of chronic disease populations. More practically, since the Q-method usually requires only a small number of respondents, it is also suitable for in-depth case studies, offering new ideas to researchers who lack sufficient research budgets or the equipment needed to handle large datasets.

Q-methodology research design and reporting transparency require optimization

Through the review of 15 studies, we found that some research designs and reports still need improvement. Specific details are lacking in determining the Q sample, selecting the P sample, conducting Q sorting, and performing data analysis, which urgently calls for a comprehensive and scientific best practice guideline to standardize these processes. Three studies only provided a general description based on literature reviews or qualitative interviews [26, 27, 32], without detailing the specific sources and processes of the concourse, indicating that methodological transparency in this section still needs improvement. There are still considerable differences in the methods used to determine Q samples in the included studies. There is no single or so-called correct approach, but the scientific rigor of the generation process will affect the reliability and validity of the entire study [44]. Specifically, different Q sample generation methods may affect content validity and representativeness and introduce researcher bias, making it difficult to distinguish whether differences in findings reflect true variations in perspectives or methodological artifacts [45]. Therefore, we recommend combining literature reviews, qualitative interviews, and expert discussions. A few studies did not report the structuring of Q samples. In the absence of a theoretical framework, researchers should report the structure or themes, which can help guide the development of Q samples [8].

The included studies showed considerable variations in the transparency of reporting on P sample selection criteria, P sample size, and the ratio of P sample to Q sample. These differences may affect the reproducibility of study findings, the diversity of perspectives captured, and the cross-study comparability of results. Some studies did not follow the usual convention that the P sample should be smaller than the Q sample [29, 33, 38]. Watts and Stenner mentioned in their classic Q methodology work that P sample size is usually smaller than Q sample size [46]. Stephen Brown, a prominent scholar of Q methodology, also suggested that the sample size of P should not exceed 2/3 of Q [41]. Q methodology holds that the different viewpoints within a group are limited, and selecting a larger sample size does not necessarily yield more viewpoints [8]. Therefore, the key to choosing the P sample lies not in the quantity but in its representativeness. Meanwhile, Watts and Stenner pointed out that in Q methodology research, when the size of the P-sample exceeds that of the Q-sample, it violates the methodological principles and may be regarded as a flaw that could lead to a manuscript being rejected for publication [46]. Although the P-sample size is relatively large, we believe these studies still adhered to the core principles of Q methodology, ensuring the adequacy of viewpoint sampling.

In terms of data analysis, the included studies varied in statistical software choice, factor extraction/rotation methods, and data reporting and presentation. These differences may affect the accuracy of analytical results, the cross-study comparability of factor structures, and the reproducibility and transparency of study conclusions. Following data analysis, the included studies also showed significant differences in the depth and rigor of factor interpretation. Factor interpretation is a key component in Q methodology research, used to reveal similarities and differences in collective cognition [47] and is mainly presented through factor arrays, distinguishing statements, consensus statements, participant comments, and contact demographics [8]. Several representative studies, based on factor arrays, integrate distinguishing and consensus statements to outline similarities and differences in viewpoints, citing participants’qualitative comments and linking demographic characteristics [27, 30–32, 35, 36, 39]. A few studies did not specify or lacked consensus analysis, and some studies reported the absence of demographic characteristics as a research limitation. We recommend that future research adopt a multidimensional presentation framework to more comprehensively reveal the subjective world of patients with chronic diseases.

Q-methodology research checklist requires further refinement

This review uses the Q-Methodology Research Checklist, ARIQ, which provides novice researchers, reviewers, and readers with a systematic and clear framework [12]. To enhance the scientific rigor of the review, and with reference to the study by Hensel et al. [8], additional elements were included: concourse development, data display, variance explained, and ethics review. Although ARIQ is effective for guiding research related to the Q methodology, this tool is still unfamiliar to researchers [8]. Using a scientifically comprehensive research checklist can help improve research quality [48]. we recommend it to researchers intending to conduct studies involving the Q methodology. In addition, the 12-item reporting tool developed by Churruca et al. can be used for preliminary rapid screening of Q methodology studies, but this tool lacks sufficient details for beginners in terms of interpretation and guidance [8, 9]. Based on this, we suggest that future studies refer to ARIQ developed by Dziopa et al. [12], the research by Hensel et al. [8],and the work of Churruca et al. [9] to develop a more scientifically comprehensive reporting checklist for Q methodology research.

Limitations

To ensure the scientific rigor of this review, although data extraction was conducted using ARIQ, certain limitations must be acknowledged. First, the search was limited to four databases, excluding other relevant databases. This may have omitted relevant studies, particularly in nursing and allied health fields. Second, we only included peer-reviewed original research articles, excluding grey literature. Although this ensured methodological consistency and reporting quality, it may have introduced publication bias and omitted relevant empirical applications of Q methodology. Future scoping reviews on this topic should consider including grey literature to more comprehensively map the use of Q methodology in chronic disease research. Studies without accessible full texts or published in languages other than English were excluded, which may have led to missing relevant results. Future research could broaden the scope to collect more related studies for improvement. Furthermore, only patients with chronic diseases, caregivers, and healthcare providers were included as research participants, which may have overlooked studies involving other stakeholders in chronic disease contexts.

Conclusion

This review, through examining studies on the use of the Q methodology in the field of chronic diseases, identifies its applications, research designs, implementation processes, and related insights to inform future methodological research. Research suggests that applying the Q methodology in the chronic disease field helps explore group perspectives and compare differences in viewpoints. Although the Q methodology has been widely applied in other disciplines, it remains relatively novel in medicine. In the future, a more scientific and comprehensive reporting checklist for Q methodology research will need to be developed.
