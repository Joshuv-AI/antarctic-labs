Managing Aortic Regurgitation and Dynamic Aortic Thrombus in a Patient With Left Ventricular Assist Device
Authors: Anna Stegmann, Anika Nagel, Pia Lanmüller, Gaik Nersesian, Daniel Lewin, Christoph Falkensteiner, Stephan Dreysse, Axel Unbehaun, Christoph Starck, Evgenij V. Potapov
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPatients with end-stage heart failure undergoing durable left ventricular assist device (dLVAD) implantation are at risk of developing de novo or progressive aortic regurgitation (AR) during long-term support.Case SummaryWe report a dLVAD patient with significant AR, in whom transcatheter aortic valve replacement was unsuitable because of an enlarged aortic annulus and aortic thrombus. A complex hybrid approach included open thrombus removal from the ascending aorta under short circulatory arrest, temporary interventional occlusion of a not-accessible dLVAD outflow-graft, and surgical aortic valve replacement (SAVR).DiscussionAortic thrombus in dLVAD patients carries the risk of coronary, cerebral or peripheral embolism. In addition to this contraindication, SAVR was favored over transcatheter aortic valve replacement because of an aortic annulus diameter >28 mm, according to the institutional treatment algorithm.Take-Home MessageIn a dLVAD patient with AR and aortic thrombus, an individualized hybrid strategy enabled effective outflow-graft occlusion facilitating safe thrombus evacuation and SAVR.

History of Presentation

A 67-year-old man with ischemic cardiomyopathy and a previous durable left ventricular assist device (dLVAD) (HeartMate III, Abbott) implantation 1 year earlier presented with recurrent cardiac decompensation (N-terminal pro–B-type natriuretic peptide 2,793 ng/L), including fatigue, dyspnea, and peripheral edema. The dLVAD speed was set at 5,800 rpm with a flow of 5.6 L/min.

Past Medical History

The patient's medical history included multiple coronary interventions, coronary artery bypass grafting, mitral valve clipping, cardiac resynchronization therapy with defibrillator implantation for ventricular tachycardia, chronic kidney disease, and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease. The dLVAD had been implanted for ischemic cardiomyopathy (left ventricular ejection fraction 15%) via left lateral thoracotomy (LLT) with the outflow-graft (OG) anastomosed to the descending thoracic aorta. Right ventricular function was mildly reduced but remained stable after implantation (right ventricular ejection fraction 45%).

Differential Diagnosis

Computed tomography (CT) excluded relevant OG stenosis, which could have comprised dLVAD flow. Increasing dLVAD speed to 6,000 rpm did not improve symptoms.

Investigations

Echocardiography showed moderate aortic regurgitation (AR) (vena contracta 3 mm) under dLVAD support without systolic valve motion, left ventricular dilatation (LVEDD 70 mm), mildly reduced right ventricular function, and moderate tricuspid regurgitation. Right heart catheterization confirmed pulmonary hypertension due to left heart disease (pulmonary hypertension associated with left heart disease; mean pulmonary arterial pressure 29 mm Hg, pulmonary capillary wedge pressure 25 mm Hg, pulmonary vascular resistance 59 dyn × s × cm−5). Management

AR was identified as the primary cause of ineffective dLVAD support, resulting in congestion and recurrent decompensation. Transcatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVR) was ruled out by the heart team because of severe dilatation of the aortic annulus (perimeter 92.5 mm, average diameter 28.7 mm [24.7-32.7 mm]) and left ventricular outflow tract (LVOT) (perimeter 104.1 mm, average diameter 31.9 mm). In addition, as a contraindication the landing zone was compromised by thrombotic material (Figure 2). Redo surgical aortic valve replacement (SAVR) was inevitable, but considered complex, because of previous coronary artery bypass graft and the OG anastomosed to the descending aorta, inaccessible for direct clamping. Surgery was performed in a hybrid operating theater to enable a hybrid approach. Following general anesthesia, transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) showed moderate AR with continuous retrograde flow across the aortic valve (AV). The jet diameter was about 50% of the LVOT. After median re-sternotomy, careful dissection and full heparinization, cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB) was established via the right axillary artery and the right femoral vein, with left ventricular venting established via the right upper pulmonary vein. dLVAD flow was stopped on CPB support and a 14-mm balloon catheter (Atlas Gold PTA Dilatation Catheter, Becton Dickinson GmbH) was introduced via the right femoral artery and descending aorta into the OG to prevent retrograde left ventricular filling (Figure 3). During CPB, norepinephrine was administered at a dose of 0.05 to 0.12 μg/kg/min to maintain a mean arterial pressure of 65 mm Hg.Figure 3Balloon Occlusion of the Outflow-Graft That Is Anastomosed to the Descending Thoracic Aorta

After cooling to 32 °C, CPB flow was reduced and the brachiocephalic trunk was clamped to initiate circulatory arrest with unilateral antegrade cerebral perfusion. Retrograde crystalloid cardioplegia (1 L Bretschneider solution, Histidine-Tryptophan-Ketoglutarate, Custodiol, Dr Franz Köhler Chemie GmbH) was administered via the coronary sinus. This setting enabled aortotomy and removal of wall-adherent thrombotic material from the coronary sinus and ascending aorta (Figures 4 and 5). A second liter of cardioplegia was subsequently delivered via the cleared coronary ostia. Circulatory arrest was terminated after 3 minutes by retrograde de-airing and clamping of the cleared ascending aorta. Standard SAVR with implantation of a 25-mm bioprothesis (Avalus, Medtronic GmbH) was performed. Total cardiac arrest time was 90 minutes. During circulatory arrest with unilateral antegrade cerebral perfusion under mild hypothermia, there was a one-minute drop in cerebral saturation (R51%/L41%) in the near-infrared spectroscopy, with values rising to baseline (R69%/L70%) after termination of circulatory arrest.Figure 4Removed Thrombotic MaterialFigure 5Intraoperative Situs After Interventional Outflow-Graft Occlusion, Thrombus Removal, Aortic Cross-Clamping, and Excision of the Native Aortic Valve

Following SAVR and rewarming, weaning from CPB was performed under epinephrine 0.1 μg/kg/min and norepinephrine 0.1 μg/kg/min. The OG balloon was deflated. The dLVAD was reactivated at 3,000 rpm and gradually increased to 5,700 rpm under TEE control, resulting in a flow of 4.7 L/min (cardiac index 2.2 L/min/m2). Nitric oxide inhalation as a pulmonary vasodilator was administered. There was no evidence of paravalvular leakage on TEE.

Outcome and Follow-Up

With differentiated fluid and catecholamine management, the cardiac index was increased to 2.5 L/min/m2 before discharge to the intensive care unit. In early postoperative course, transient ventricular arrhythmia occurred and was successfully controlled with amiodarone. Echocardiography demonstrated no paravalvular leakage but revealed early valve thrombosis 1 week after surgery. The patient did not experience any neurological adverse events and was discharged to a rehabilitation facility 3 weeks after surgery.

Discussion

In patients undergoing dLVAD implantation, de novo or progressive AR may occur due to an increased afterload and altered flow pattern in the ascending aorta.1, 2, 3 In the presented case, mild preexisting AR progressed to moderate severity during the first postoperative year. AR can lead to blood recirculation and reduce the effectiveness of dLVAD support.4 Current guidelines recommend biological AV replacement when AR exceeds mild severity.1,4 In selected dLVAD patients, TAVR has emerged as an alternative.5,6 In this case, even in the absence of thrombus, TAVR was ruled out due to enlargement of the aortic annulus and LVOT, raising concerns regarding device migration and paravalvular leakage.5 In dLVAD patients, implantation of an AR-dedicated TAVR device (JenaValve Trilogy), even after landing zone prestenting, is preferable to off-label use of last-generation standard TAVR devices.5 Moreover, implantation of last-generation standard TAVR devices beyond annular dimensions specified by manufacturers has been associated with higher rates and greater severity of paravalvular leakage.7 The JenaValve Trilogy is recommended for annular perimeters up to 90 mm. Based on institutional experience, a treatment algorithm for patients with dLVAD and clinically significant AR has been established: patients with a mean annular diameter up to 28.0 mm are considered for JenaValve implantation, whereas those with diameters exceeding 28.0 mm should be evaluated for SAVR, heart transplantation, interventional AV closure, or conservative medical therapy.5 Interventional valve closure and high-urgency transplantation were deemed unsuitable in this patient because of increased embolic risk, limited transplant candidacy, and possibly prolonged waitlist times.

Thrombus formation at the aortic root and along the ascending aorta has been reported following dLVAD implantation and is closely related to reduced or absent AV opening. Thrombus in the ascending aorta hinders interventional valve procedures and incorporates the risk of embolization to coronary arteries and cerebral and peripheral vessels.8 In the present case, absent valve motion with sludge formation had already been documented in the first month after dLVAD implantation and was initially managed by flow reduction to promote AV opening at least every third heartbeat.

At our institution, dLVAD implantation after previous sternotomy is routinely performed via LLT with OG anastomosis to the descending thoracic aorta to avoid re-stereotomy. With reduced antegrade transaortic flow and a reverse pressure gradient across the AV caused by continuous unloading, predominantly retrograde blood flow from the OG anastomosis within the aortic arch and ascending aorta may be assumed.2 As reported for varying OG orientations in virtual dLVAD models, these altered aortic flow patterns may affect wall shear stress, platelet activation, and thrombogenicity along the aortic wall and root.9 In our case, recurrent bioprosthetic valve thrombosis despite adequate anticoagulation may have resulted from persistent altered flow pattern and reduced AV opening, making conservative management a reasonable strategy in the absence of recurrent AR or coronary compromise.

Serial CT imaging demonstrated dynamic, wall-adherent thrombus formation (Figure 1) despite optimal anticoagulation with a vitamin K antagonist (target INR 2.5-3.0). Fluctuations in thrombus burden may reflect changes in aortic flow patterns over time. As a contraindication, TAVR in a thrombotic landing zone would have led to thrombus fragmentation and embolism.

To minimize the risk of coronary, cerebral, or peripheral embolization in SAVR during aortic clamping and root cardioplegia, circulatory arrest was initiated and retrograde crystalloid cardioplegia was used via the coronary sinus. This strategy allowed careful inspection and clearance of thrombus from the coronary ostia, followed by antegrade cardioplegia and removal of thrombotic material from the ascending aorta before aorta cross-clamping.

The dLVAD implantation via LLT with OG anastomosis to the descending aorta has been associated with improvement in postoperative bleeding and infection rates and is a feasible surgery approach in redo cases.10 However, if redo sternotomy and cardioplegic heart arrest become necessary, OG clamping to avoid retrograde flow during pump stoppage can be technically challenging. The issue was addressed by OG balloon occlusion via femoral arterial access after pump stoppage. This simple implemented technique effectively prevented retrograde pump flow during SAVR.

Overall, the presented surgical strategy addressed hybrid management aspects in this complex clinical setting, particularly the prevention of thromboembolic complication.

Conclusions

Redo SAVR in patients supported by dLVAD requires individualized planning and tailored surgical approach.
