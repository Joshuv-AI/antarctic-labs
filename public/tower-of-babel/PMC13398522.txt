Hdac3 suppresses apoptosis through deacetylating and stabilizing the antiapoptotic protein Diap1
Authors: Dafa Zhou, Bin Liu, Yan Ding, Yujiao Wang, Yuanfei Sun, Simin Xu, Hui Li, Xiaopei Wang, Yunhe Zhao, Qingxin Liu, Zizhang Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Apoptosis is a highly conserved process that eliminates unwanted or damaged cells in both physiological and pathological conditions. Dysregulation of apoptosis leads to developmental abnormalities and various diseases, such as neurodegeneration and cancer. Drosophila inhibitor of apoptosis 1 (Diap1) plays a crucial role in cell survival by inhibiting caspases and preventing apoptosis. However, under stress conditions, the prodeath proteins Rpr, Hid, and Grim (RHG) induce apoptosis by antagonizing Diap1. Despite being a key component of the apoptotic pathway, the mechanism that controls the stability of Diap1 remains unknown. Here, we find that loss of hdac3 results in the activation of apoptosis, which is completely blocked by expressing Diap1. Although Hdac3 localizes in both the cell cytoplasm and nucleus, only the cytoplasmic Hdac3 is able to suppress apoptosis induced by hdac3 deficiency, RHG overexpression, or x-ray irradiation. This finding indicates that Hdac3 exerts an antiapoptotic role independent of its canonical epigenetic functions. Loss of hdac3 decreases Diap1 protein, which is rescued by introducing cytoplasmic Hdac3. The deacetylase activity is necessary for Hdac3 to suppress apoptosis. Mechanistically, Hdac3 interacts with Diap1 to remove the acetyl group from K315 on Diap1, thereby increasing its stability. Compared with the wild-type Diap1, the acetyl-deficient mutant Diap1-K315R exhibits stronger stability and antiapoptotic activity. Last, RHG proteins compete with Hdac3 for Diap1 interaction, directing Diap1 toward degradation and triggering apoptosis. Together, these findings not only reveal the involvement of Diap1 acetylation modification in apoptosis regulation but also clarify the role of Hdac3 in apoptosis.

INTRODUCTION

During development, apoptosis is a crucial process that removes unnecessary cells and tissues, such as larval organs during metamorphosis, to establish organs with proper function and size (1). Dysregulation of apoptosis is implicated in various diseases, including cancer and neurodegenerative disorders (2). A key feature of apoptosis is the activation of the evolutionarily conserved caspase cascade (3). When exposed to death signals, the initiator caspase Dronc in Drosophila undergoes autocleavage in a dArk-dependent manner to acquire enzymatic activity (4, 5). Subsequently, the activated Dronc cleaves effector caspases such as Drice and Dcp-1, granting proteolytic activity to these enzymes (6, 7). Drice and Dcp-1 function in a partially redundant manner to cleave specific substrates, ultimately leading to cellular disintegration (8). Accordingly, cells have evolved antiapoptotic mechanisms to prevent premature cell death (9). In Drosophila, Diap1 serves as a powerful antiapoptotic factor that inactivates initiator and effector caspases, ensuring cell survival under nonlethal conditions (10, 11). Loss-of-function mutations in the gene thread (th), which encodes Diap1, lead to Drosophila embryonic lethality due to excessive apoptosis (12). Structurally, Diap1 has two N-terminal BIR domains for caspase binding and a C-terminal RING domain that confers E3 ligase activity (13, 14). Diap1 is able to recruit caspases including Dronc, Drice, and Dcp-1 through BIR domains to facilitate their ubiquitination and eventual degradation (14–16). Upon receiving death cues, the proapoptotic proteins Rpr, Hid, and Grim (RHG) bind to the BIR domains in Diap1 via their IBM motifs, thereby liberating caspases (17–19).

Because of its critical role in apoptosis, the protein levels of Diap1 are subject to tight regulation to avoid inappropriate cell death. Diap1 undergoes caspase-mediated cleavage at position 20 in response to death signals, exposing its N-terminal degron for N-end rule degradation, ultimately leading to Diap1 proteolysis (20, 21). Moreover, RHG proteins interact with Diap1, leading to Diap1 autoubiquitination and subsequent self-destruction (17, 22). The E3 ligases Morgue and UbcD1 are able to collaborate with Rpr to initiate proteasome-dependent degradation of Diap1 (23, 24). Conversely, a cell-based screening has identified that the deubiquitinase Dubai interacts with Diap1 to suppress Diap1 ubiquitination and degradation (25). While Dubai stabilizes Diap1 in S2 cells, its overexpression results in a marked reduction in eye size (25), raising doubts about the Dubai’s role in physiologically stabilizing Diap1. It is speculated that there must be a mechanism in place to maintain Diap1 stability to prevent the occurrence of unnecessary apoptosis.

Protein acetylation is an enzymatic modification catalyzed by lysine acetyltransferases, which transfer an acetyl group from acetyl–coenzyme A to the ε-amino group of specific lysine residues (26). This modification is dynamically reversible through the action of deacetylases. Among these, the histone deacetylase (Hdac) family represents the largest and most well-characterized class (27). In contrast to other Hdac family members which are predominantly nuclear-localized, Hdac3 exhibits an even distribution between the cytoplasm and nucleus (28), suggesting its involvement in the cytoplasmic processes. Hdac3 is primarily recognized for catalyzing the deacetylation of histones, a fundamental biochemical event that directly contributes to the establishment of repressive chromatin states, thereby enforcing its role as a key epigenetic silencer (29). To function as an epigenetic silencer, Hdac3 must form a silencing complex with the corepressors NCoR and SMRT, which is required for association with chromatin and execution of transcriptional repression programs (30, 31). Unlike the embryonic lethality observed upon global Hdac3 knockout in mice (32), animals carrying mutations in NCoR or SMRT that specifically disrupt their interaction with Hdac3 are born at expected Mendelian ratios (33). This genetic difference implies that Hdac3 has crucial functions that operate independently of its well-characterized role in the NCoR/SMRT corepressor complexes. Although an increasing number of nonhistones have been reported as substrates of Hdac3, most are nuclear-localized proteins, such as the transcription factors NICD1, P53, and GATA2 (34–36). Identifying Hdac3’s cytoplasmic substrates is helpful for comprehending its functions apart from its role as an epigenetic silencer.

The role of Hdac3 in regulating organ size in Drosophila has been somewhat explored, but the findings are controversial. Loss of hdac3 in wing discs leads to an increase in hid expression and triggers apoptosis, without affecting cell proliferation, resulting in a size reduction (37). In contrast, another study demonstrates that depletion of hdac3 antagonizes phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase (PI3K)–mediated cell proliferation in wings and eyes through H4K16 deacetylation, without activating apoptosis (38). A recent study reveals that Hdac3 deacetylates H3K27ac at the E93 promoter, resulting in the suppression of E93 transcription and the inhibition of apoptosis (39). All these studies have concentrated on the Hdac3’s role as a transcriptional suppressor in the nucleus, neglecting the function of cytoplasmic Hdac3. The primary reason for the variation in outcomes is the absence of rigorous genetic rescue experiments in all of these studies.

To elucidate the role of Hdac3 in organ size determination, we used RNA interference (RNAi) or mutant clones to silence hdac3 expression and supplemented different Hdac3 variants through Mosaic Analysis with a Repressible Cell Marker (MARCM) technology. Knockdown of hdac3 did not affect cell proliferation but triggered apoptosis without activating the expression of RHG. Apoptosis caused by hdac3 depletion was completely blocked by introducing cytoplasmic Hdac3, instead of nuclear Hdac3. Moreover, cytoplasmic Hdac3, rather than nuclear Hdac3, was able to inhibit apoptosis induced by RHG overexpression, x-ray irradiation, or etoposide (Eto) treatment, highlighting the crucial role of cytoplasmic Hdac3 in the control of apoptosis. Mechanistically, Hdac3 interacts with Diap1 to deacetylate it at lysine-315 (K315), thereby increasing Diap1 protein stability. Compared with the wild-type Diap1, the acetyl-deficient mutant Diap1-K315R exhibits enhanced stability and antiapoptotic activity. RHG proteins compete with Hdac3 for Diap1 interaction, promoting Diap1 degradation and triggering apoptosis. Last, human HDAC3 suppresses apoptosis in cell lines by stabilizing x-linked inhibitor of apoptosis protein (XIAP), highlighting the conserved role of the Hdac3-Diap1/XIAP axis in regulating apoptosis across species. Together, these findings not only reveal the involvement of Diap1 acetylation modification in apoptosis regulation but also clarify the role of Hdac3 in apoptosis.

RESULTS

Knockdown of hdac3 results in undergrowth through activating apoptosis

To investigate the role of Hdac3 in regulating organ size, we silenced it using three independent RNAi lines (V20814, 2128R-1, and BL-31633), which target distinct regions of hdac3 (40). Efficiencies of these RNAi lines were confirmed by Western blot analysis (fig. S1A) and quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR; fig. S1B). Expressing these RNAi lines using nub-Gal4 resulted in a reduction in wing sizes (Fig. 1, A to D). Compared with the control disc (Fig. 1, E and Ea), knockdown of hdac3 triggered apoptosis, as evidenced by cleaved caspase-3 (cC3) staining (Fig. 1, F to Ha). This result was further validated by terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase–mediated deoxyuridine triphosphate nick end labeling (TUNEL) staining (Fig. 1, I to Ja). The observed apoptosis was attributed to the decrease in hdac3 expression, as it was completely reversed by introducing wild-type Hdac3 (Fig. 1, K and Ka). Furthermore, overexpression of P35, a pan-caspase inhibitor, successfully rescued hdac3 RNAi-induced apoptosis (Fig. 1, L and La), suggesting that knockdown of hdac3 activates canonical caspase-dependent apoptosis. Since RHG are important proapoptotic proteins in Drosophila, we sought to examine whether hdac3 depletion promotes RHG expression. To this end, we used the hdac3N allele, which contains a premature stop codon at position Q57, leading to the production of a truncated and nonfunctional peptide (37). Homozygous hdac3N alleles are lethal at the third-instar larval stage but can survive until the first instar. The RT-qPCR assays using hdac3N homozygous first-instar larvae revealed that the mRNA levels of RHG were not up-regulated compared to controls (fig. S1C). In addition, knockdown of hdac3 in wing discs by en-Gal4 did not alter the expression of hid-LacZ (fig. S1, D to Ea) or rpr-LacZ (fig. S1, F to Ga), questioning the previous observation that Hdac3 depletion induces apoptosis by activating hid (37).

Another study has shown that loss of Hdac3 antagonizes PI3K-mediated cell proliferation in wings through H4K16 deacetylation (38). We analyzed cell proliferation using 5-ethynyl-2′-deoxyuridine (EdU) incorporation, a method that labels cells undergoing DNA replication. In comparison to the control wing disc (fig. S1, H to Hb), knockdown of hdac3 using V20814 (fig. S1, I to Ib) or 2128R-1 (fig. S1, J to Jb) did not result in any change in EdU incorporation. These findings suggest that hdac3 knockdown primarily induces apoptosis rather than inhibiting proliferation, and this apoptotic response takes place without relying on hid up-regulation.

Cytoplasmic Hdac3 is responsible for suppressing apoptosis

Since Hdac3 localizes in both the cytoplasm and nucleus (28), we sought to determine the specific cellular compartment where Hdac3 exerts its antiapoptotic functions. Accordingly, we engineered a membrane-tethered form of Hdac3 (Myr-Hdac3-HA) by introducing a myristoylation signal at its N terminus. In parallel, a constitutively nuclear Hdac3 variant (Myc-Hdac3-NLS) was generated by fusing a nuclear localization sequence (NLS) to its C terminus (40). Compared to wild-type Hdac3, which was present in both the cytoplasm and nucleus (fig. S2, A to Ab), Myr-Hdac3-HA was exclusively localized in the cytoplasm (fig. S2, B to Bb), while Myc-Hdac3-NLS was only observed in the nucleus (fig. S2, C to Cb), demonstrating the effectiveness of these variants.

To better visualize caspase activity in vivo, we used a caspase activity sensor, GC3AI, which produces green fluorescent protein (GFP) signals specifically following caspase-mediated cleavage (41). In line with the cC3 staining, knockdown of hdac3 led to a robust activation of the GC3AI sensor (Fig. 2, A and B), indicating the induction of apoptosis. hdac3 RNAi-induced activation of GC3AI sensor was successfully blocked by introducing wild-type Hdac3 (Fig. 2C) or Myr-Hdac3 (Fig. 2D), rather than Hdac3-NLS (Fig. 2E). We confirmed the result by detecting cleaved Dcp-1 (cDcp-1) in hdac3N homozygous clones generated via the MARCM technique (42). As anticipated, loss of hdac3 resulted in a marked increase in cDcp-1 (Fig. 2, F to Fb), which was rescued by coexpressing wild-type hdac3 (Fig. 2, G to Gb). The elevation of cDcp-1 by hdac3 knockout could be counteracted by Myr-Hdac3-HA (Fig. 2, H to Hb), rather than Myc-Hdac3-NLS (Fig. 2, I to Ib), underscoring the crucial role of cytoplasmic Hdac3 in repressing apoptosis. The same results were observed in Drosophila eye discs (fig. S2, D to Gb). Clones homozygous for the hdac3N allele in both wing and eye discs showed a noticeable reduction in size. This reduction could be reversed by introducing wild-type Hdac3 or Myr-Hdac3, but not Hdac3-NLS. To verify the deacetylase activity of Hdac3-NLS, we evaluated the acetylation status of H4K8 (a well-known substrate of Hdac3) and the expression level of hsp70 (a known transcriptional target of Hdac3) (43, 44). Both Hdac3 and Hdac3-NLS comparably reduced H4K8ac (fig. S2H) and activated hsp70 expression (fig. S2J) in Drosophila. The elevated H4K8ac level and suppressed hsp70 mRNA resulting from hdac3 knockdown were rescued upon introduction of either Hdac3 or Hdac3-NLS (fig. S2, I and K). In our recent study, we observed that the importin inhibitor importazole (IPZ) effectively blocked the nuclear import of Myc-Hdac3-NLS (40). Compared with the control (fig. S2, N and Na), administration of IPZ to wing discs restored the ability of Hdac3-NLS to suppress apoptosis (fig. S2, L to Oa). On the basis of these findings, we infer that the inability of Hdac3-NLS to regulate apoptosis arises from its nuclear localization rather than from a loss of deacetylase activity.

After showing that cytoplasmic Hdac3 is sufficient to suppress apoptosis upon hdac3 depletion, we next explored whether it also mediates protection under stress conditions. Compared with the unstressed wing disc (Fig. 2A), x-ray irradiation led to a robust activation of the GC3AI sensor (Fig. 2J), demonstrating the onset of apoptosis. Wing discs expressing either wild-type Hdac3 or Myr-Hdac3 were insensitive to x-ray–induced apoptosis (Fig. 2, K and L). Nevertheless, overexpression of Hdac3-NLS in the wing disc did not influence the susceptibility to x-ray (Fig. 2M). In contrast to the induction of rpr following x-ray irradiation (45), the expression of hdac3 was down-regulated (fig. S3A), suggesting that x-ray exposure promotes apoptosis, at least in part, through repression of Hdac3. To examine the broad antiapoptotic activity of Hdac3, we treated Drosophila wing discs with Eto, a classic DNA damage–induced apoptosis inducer (46), and tested whether Hdac3 suppresses Eto-triggered apoptosis. The GC3AI reporter was activated upon Eto treatment (fig. S3, B to Cb), and this activation was reversed by introducing Hdac3 or Myr-Hdac3, but not Hdac3-NLS (fig. S3, D to Fb). Collectively, our data establish that cytoplasmic Hdac3 plays a predominant role compared to its nuclear counterpart in suppressing apoptosis.

Hdac3 inhibits RHG-mediated apoptosis

Since RHG proteins are critical endogenous initiators of apoptosis in Drosophila (47), we sought to investigate whether Hdac3 inhibits RHG-mediated apoptosis. Ectopic expression of Hid in eyes under the control of glass multiple reporter (GMR) promoter resulted in a marked decrease in eye size (Fig. 3A). The small eyes of GMR-Hid were aggravated by knocking down hdac3 while partially rescued by expressing Hdac3 or Myr-Hdac3 (Fig. 3, A and B). However, Hdac3-NLS had lost the capacity to restore GMR-Hid eyes (Fig. 3, A and B). Losing one copy of endogenous hdac3 (heterozygous for hdac3N) also exacerbated the small eyes of GMR-Hid (Fig. 3, A and B). Similarly, loss of hdac3 worsened GMR-Rpr–induced small eyes, but overexpression of Hdac3 or Myr-Hdac3, not Hdac3-NLS, restored them (Fig. 3, C and D). To determine whether the rescue effect of Hdac3 is attributed to the inhibition of apoptosis, we examined the cC3 signals in wing discs. Compared with the control disc (Fig. 3E), overexpression of Hid (Fig. 3F), Grim (Fig. 3H), or Rpr (Fig. 3J) robustly up-regulated cC3 signals, reflecting the activation of apoptosis. Notably, RHG-induced apoptosis was effectively attenuated by coexpressing Hdac3 (Fig. 3, G, I, and K).

The inhibition of apoptosis by Hdac3 requires its deacetylase activity

To investigate whether the antiapoptotic function of Hdac3 is dependent on its enzymatic activity, we used a catalytically inactive point mutant in which histidine 140 was substituted with alanine (Hdac3-H140A). Overexpression of Hdac3-H140A under the control of nub-Gal4 elicited a small-wing phenotype similar to hdac3 RNAi (Fig. 4, A and B), suggesting its dominant negative role. Coexpression of wild-type Hdac3 effectively restored the small-wing phenotype caused by Hdac3-H140A (Fig. 4C), further supporting its dominant negative function. Overexpression of Hdac3-H140A led to activation of the GC3AI sensor (Fig. 4D), which was inhibited by introducing wild-type Hdac3 (Fig. 4E). Consistently, the TUNEL signals caused by Hdac3-H140A were also restored by coexpressing Hdac3 (Fig. 4, F and G). These observations show that Hdac3-H140A plays a proapoptotic role as opposed to the antiapoptotic function of the wild-type protein.

We next analyzed whether Hdac3-H140A loses its ability to suppress RHG-induced apoptosis. As expected, the small eye of GMR-Hid was aggravated by expressing Hdac3-H140A, and this effect was abolished by Hdac3 (Fig. 4, H to J). The same result was observed under the GMR-Rpr background as well (Fig. 4, K to M). Overall, the antiapoptotic role of Hdac3 requires its deacetylase activity.

Loss of hdac3 decreases Diap1 protein

Since the cytoplasmic Hdac3 can suppress RHG-induced apoptosis, we speculated that Hdac3 regulates a general apoptotic modulator. Diap1 has drawn our attention mainly due to its cytoplasmic localization and extensive antiapoptotic activity. To test this possibility, we generated homozygous hdac3N clones and examined endogenous Diap1 protein. Notably, Diap1 exhibited a sharp reduction in hdac3N clones (Fig. 5, A to Ab). Furthermore, depletion of hdac3 also resulted in a decrease in tub-Myc-Diap1 (Fig. 5, B to Bb), in which the Myc-tagged Diap1 is expressed under the control of tubulin promoter (48). Consistent with the induction of apoptosis following hdac3 knockdown, the expression of three distinct RNAi lines also led to a decrease in tub-Myc-Diap1 (fig. S4, A to Da). The decrease in tub-Myc-Diap1 due to hdac3 knockdown was rescued by coexpressing Hdac3 (fig. S4, E and Ea). In line with its dominant negative role, overexpression of Hdac3-H140A lowered tub-Myc-Diap1 (fig. S4, F and Fa). To verify whether cytoplasmic or nuclear Hdac3 is responsible for Diap1 regulation, MARCM technology was used to express different Hdac3 variants in hdac3N clones. In comparison to the decreased Diap1 in hdac3N clones (Fig. 5, C to Cb), expressing wild-type Hdac3 (Fig. 5, D to Db) or Myr-Hdac3 (Fig. 5, E to Eb) restored Diap1 protein. However, Hdac3-NLS failed to rescue the reduction of Diap1 in hdac3N clones (Fig. 5, F to Fb). In addition, the reduction of tub-Myc-Diap1 in hdac3N clones was reverted by introducing wild-type Hdac3, rather than Hdac3-H140A (fig. S4, G to Ia). Furthermore, treatment with RGFP966, a well-established Hdac3 inhibitor (49), resulted in a time-dependent reduction of Myc-Diap1 levels without altering Hdac3 abundance, underscoring the requirement of Hdac3 deacetylase activity for Diap1 stabilization (fig. S4J). In Drosophila, Hdac3 functions mainly as a transcriptional repressor through its association with Smrter (Smr) (50). The failure of smr knockdown to affect tub-Myc-Diap1 expression (fig. S4, K to La) suggests that Hdac3 stabilizes Diap1 via a mechanism independent of the Hdac3-Smr complex.

Diap1 undergoes self-ubiquitination and autodegradation, which rely on its E3 ubiquitin ligase activity (17). To determine whether hdac3 depletion triggers Diap1 autodegradation, we generated transgenic flies carrying tub-Myc-Diap1-C406Y and tub-Myc-Diap1-C412Y, both of which are defective in E3 ligase function (51, 52). Depletion of hdac3 in wing and eye imaginal discs robustly decreased the levels of both tub-Myc-Diap1-C406Y and tub-Myc-Diap1-C412Y (fig. S5, A to Db), confirming that Hdac3-mediated regulation occurs independently of Diap1’s autodegradation.

To determine whether the antiapoptotic effect of Hdac3 is mediated by Diap1, we performed genetic rescue experiments. Activation of the GC3AI sensor, resulting from nub-Gal4–driven hdac3 knockdown in the wing disc (fig. S6, A and Aa), was effectively suppressed by Diap1 overexpression (fig. S6, B and Ba). Moreover, the elevation of cDcp-1 in hdac3N clones could be counteracted by expressing Diap1 (fig. S6, C and Ca). Upon exposure to x-ray irradiation, Hdac3-mediated suppression of apoptosis was neutralized by diap1 knockdown (fig. S6, D to Fa). Apoptosis induced by RGFP966 was also blocked by overexpressing Diap1 (fig. S6, G to Ha). Overexpression of Diap1 ameliorated the reduction in eye size induced by overexpression of Hid or Rpr (fig. S6I and J). The rescue effect of Diap1 was further strengthened by co-overexpression of Hdac3 or Myr-Hdac3, but not by Hdac3-NLS (fig. S6, I and J). These findings indicate that Hdac3 exerts an antiapoptotic function primarily by stabilizing Diap1.

Hdac3 interacts with Diap1 to promote its deacetylation

Having demonstrated that Hdac3 controls the protein level of Diap1 via its deacetylase activity, we next sought to investigate their interaction. Coimmunoprecipitation (Co-IP) and glutathione S-transferase (GST) pull-down assays showed a direct physical interaction between Diap1 and Hdac3 (Fig. 6, B and C). Cyclin A (CycA), a known partner of Hdac3, was used as a positive control in the Co-IP assay (53). To map domains responsible for this interaction, we constructed a series of truncated plasmids for Co-IP experiments (Fig. 6A). Both Co-IP and GST pull-down results revealed that Diap1 binds to Hdac3 via its BIR2 domain (Fig. 6, D and E). Deletion of the BIR2 domain abrogated Diap1’s interaction with Hdac3 (Fig. 6F), demonstrating that the BIR2 domain of Diap1 is both necessary and sufficient for mediating the binding. On the other hand, Hdac3 bound to Diap1 through its C terminus (Fig. 6G).

The interaction between Hdac3 and Diap1 prompts us to consider Diap1 as a substrate of Hdac3. To test this hypothesis, we assessed the acetylation status of Diap1 using a pan-acetyl-lysine antibody in the presence or absence of Hdac3. As expected, Hdac3 suppressed Myc-Diap1 acetylation, whereas Hdac3-H140A failed to do so (Fig. 6H). Using an online web server (GPS-PAIL) (54, 55), we identified K315 in Diap1 as a potential acetylation site. To verify this, we generated an acetyl-deficient mutant of Diap1, Diap1-KR, by substituting K315 with R. The K315R mutation abolished the acetylation of Diap1 (Fig. 6I), highlighting the importance of K315 in Diap1 acetylation. However, K315R mutation in Diap1 did not interfere with its affinity for Hdac3 (Fig. 6J). In contrast to the sharp decrease in tub-Myc-Diap1 observed upon hdac3 knockdown (Fig. 6, K to La), tub-Fg-Diap1-KR displayed insensitivity to hdac3 RNAi (Fig. 6, M to Na).

To explore how Hdac3 controls the level of Diap1 protein, we conducted a cycloheximide (CHX) pulse-chase assay in S2 cells and found that hdac3 knockdown shortened the half-life of Diap1 protein (fig. S7A). However, the stability of Diap1-KR remained unchanged despite hdac3 knockdown (fig. S7B). The negative correlation between Diap1 acetylation and its stability infers that Diap1-KR has a stronger antiapoptotic ability than wild-type Diap1. As anticipated, activation of the GC3AI apoptotic sensor triggered by x-ray irradiation was more effectively inhibited by tub-Diap1-KR than by tub-Diap1 (fig. S7, C to Ea). In addition, tub-Diap1-KR conferred greater protection than tub-Diap1 against apoptosis induced by either GMR-Hid (fig. S7, F to H) or GMR-Rpr (fig. S7, I to K). We further compared the effect of Hdac3 on GMR-Hid eye sizes under either tub-Diap1 or tub-Diap1-KR background. Hdac3 effectively enlarged GMR-Hid eyes under tub-Diap1 background, whereas this ability was lost in the presence of tub-Diap1-KR (fig. S7L). Conversely, knockdown of hdac3 reduced GMR-Hid eye sizes under the context of tub-Diap1, rather than tub-Diap1-KR (fig. S7M). These findings strongly suggest that the inhibitory effect of Hdac3 on Hid-induced apoptosis depends on Diap1 deacetylation.

RHG proteins disrupt the Hdac3-Diap1 interaction to activate apoptosis

RHG proteins are endogenous triggers of apoptosis. Upon apoptotic signaling, they are transcriptionally up-regulated and then bind to the BIR domains of Diap1, thereby facilitating Diap1 autodegradation (17, 18). Our data establish Hdac3 as a physiological stabilizer of Diap1, as evidenced by the reduction in Diap1 protein levels following hdac3 depletion. This implies that a mechanism must exist whereby RHG proteins override Hdac3-mediated stabilization of Diap1 to execute apoptosis upon receiving death signals. The Co-IP assays revealed that RHG proteins disrupted the interaction between Hdac3 and Diap1 (Fig. 7A). Compared with the control eye disc (Fig. 7B), overexpression of Hid led to a decrease in tub-Myc-Diap1 (Fig. 7C). This decrease was not an indirect consequence of apoptosis, as coexpression of the potent caspase inhibitor P35 prevented cell death. Expression of Hdac3 only partially restored tub-Myc-Diap1 protein level in the presence of Hid overexpression (Fig. 7D). Consistent with Hid, overexpression of either Grim or Rpr also elicited down-regulation of tub-Myc-Diap1, an effect that was partially suppressed by the introduction of Hdac3 (Fig. 7, E to H). Although RHG proteins exhibited similar binding affinities for both Diap1 and Diap1-KR (fig. S8, A to C), they only weakly decreased the levels of tub-Fg-Diap1-KR (fig. S8, D to G). These findings indicate that the acetylation-deficient Diap1-KR mutant is refractory to RHG-induced degradation. Therefore, Hdac3-mediated deacetylation of Diap1 at K315 represents a critical mechanism for its stabilization.

Human HDAC3 stabilizes XIAP to suppress apoptosis

To determine whether the mechanism by which Hdac3 regulates Diap1 is conserved from Drosophila to humans, we overexpressed human HDAC3 (hHDAC3) in the hdac3 knockout clones and examined the levels of cDcp-1 and tub-Myc-Diap1. As expected, hHDAC3 suppressed cDcp-1 activation (fig. S9, A to Ab) and rescued the down-regulation of Myc-Diap1 induced by hdac3 knockout (fig. S9, B to Bb). We further investigated whether hHDAC3 plays an antiapoptotic role in human cells. Knockdown of hHDAC3 using two distinct small interfering RNAs (siRNAs) or treatment with RGFP966 induced apoptosis in both A549 and H1299 cells, as assessed by TUNEL assays (fig. S9, C to F). The interaction between hHDAC3 and XIAP, BIRC2, or BIRC3, three orthologs of Diap1 in humans (56), was also examined. The Co-IP analysis showed that hHDAC3 exclusively interacted with XIAP (fig. S9G). Furthermore, knockdown of hHDAC3 or treatment with RGFP966 decreased XIAP levels in 293T, A549, and H1299 cells (fig. S9, H to M). Collectively, these results demonstrate the conserved antiapoptotic role of hHDAC3 in human cells.

DISCUSSION

The process of apoptosis is subject to tight regulation, which ensures the precise spatiotemporal elimination of superfluous or dangerous cells. Dysregulation of apoptosis has been implicated in developmental defects and various diseases, such as neurodegeneration and cancer. In Drosophila, Diap1 functions as a master regulatory hub within the apoptotic cascade, serving to suppress inappropriate caspase activation and prevent aberrant cell death. Nevertheless, the mechanism governing the maintenance of Diap1 protein abundance under physiological conditions remains incompletely defined. In this study, we provided a model in which Hdac3 functions as a critical regulator of apoptosis by controlling Diap1 stability (Fig. 7I). Under physiological conditions, cytoplasmic Hdac3 binds to and deacetylates Diap1, thereby stabilizing Diap1 and supporting cell survival. Depletion of hdac3 leads to increased acetylation and accelerated degradation of Diap1, leading to caspase activation and apoptosis. Upon death signals, up-regulated RHG proteins compete with Hdac3 for Diap1 binding, disrupting the protective complex and directing Diap1 toward degradation. Thus, Hdac3 establishes a safeguard mechanism that buffers basal RHG protein activity, preventing premature cell death and ensuring apoptosis only occurs under appropriate apoptotic stimuli.

Diap1 serves as the primary brake of the apoptotic cascade, and its loss-of-function mutations result in Drosophila embryonic lethality due to excessive apoptosis (12). Despite the instability of Diap1 protein, which has a half-life of ∼1 hour (21), there must be a mechanism in place to sustain the basal abundance of Diap1 to prevent unwarranted apoptosis. This study shows that Hdac3 is likely an important stabilizer of Diap1, since its depletion by RNAi or knockout leads to a sharp reduction in endogenous Diap1 protein regardless of apoptotic signals. The level of Diap1 protein is primarily controlled via two degradation routes: the N-end rule pathway and its intrinsic E3 ligase–mediated self-ubiquitination (57). Hdac3-mediated Diap1 stabilization does not result from the inhibition of its N-end rule degradation, as this degradation is dependent on apoptotic signals (20). Furthermore, depletion of hdac3 also leads to a decrease in protein levels of Diap1-C406Y and Diap1-C412Y, both of which lack E3 ligase activity (52). Therefore, Hdac3-mediated Diap1 stabilization is independent of the known degradative mechanisms. There must be an alternative mechanism that promotes Diap1 degradation, which can be counteracted by Hdac3. K315 on Diap1 is a critical recipient of the acetyl group, as its mutation eliminates Diap1 acetylation. In contrast to Diap1, Diap1-KR exhibits enhanced stability and antiapoptotic activity, highlighting the critical nature of K315 acetylation in apoptosis regulation. Hence, it will be fruitful to identify the acetyltransferase responsible for Diap1 acetylation. To our knowledge, this study establishes that acetylation is a critical posttranslational modification governing Diap1 protein stability.

Two previous studies have shown that knockdown of hdac3 lowers histone acetylation, which, in turn, promotes the transcription of proapoptotic genes (37, 39). Both studies focus solely on the Hdac3’s canonical role as a transcriptional suppressor in the nucleus, overlooking the function of cytoplasmic Hdac3. In this study, we carried out rigorous in vivo genetic rescue experiments and determined that cytoplasmic Hdac3, rather than nuclear Hdac3, is accountable for regulating apoptosis. Although an increasing number of nonhistones have been reported as substrates of Hdac3, most are nuclear-localized proteins, such as the transcription factors NICD1, P53, and GATA2 (34–36). This study reveals that the cytoplasmic protein Diap1 is a genuine substrate for Hdac3, deepening the understanding of the function of Hdac3.

Immunostaining and confocal

The protocol for immunostaining imaginal eye or wing discs has been described in our previous study (60). Briefly, third-instar larvae were dissected in phosphate-buffered solution (PBS), fixed in freshly prepared 4% formaldehyde in PBS at room temperature for 20 min, and then washed three times with PBT (PBS with 0.1% Triton X-100). After incubating the larvae overnight with the required primary antibodies in PBT at 4°C, they were washed three times with PBT and then incubated with the appropriate fluorophore-conjugated secondary antibodies for 2 hours at room temperature. After being washed three times in PBT, the discs were separated and mounted in 40% glycerol. For chemical treatment, whole animals or wing discs dissected from the third-instar larvae were incubated in SFX-Insect (HyClone) medium, supplemented with 200 μM RGFP966 (MedChemExpress), 200 μM Eto (MedChemExpress), or 20 μM IPZ (MedChemExpress) for 2 hours. An equal amount of dimethyl sulfoxide was used as a control. TUNEL staining was performed using the TUNEL BrightRed Apoptosis Detection Kit (Vazyme) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. For EdU incorporation assay, the imaginal discs were treated with the EdU Cell Proliferation Kit (CellorLab) following the manufacturer’s guidelines. Images were captured with the Zeiss confocal microscope. Antibodies used in this study were as follows: rat anti-Ci (1:10; DSHB); rabbit anti–β-galactosidase (1:500; MBL); mouse anti-Fg (1:500; Sigma-Aldrich); mouse anti-Myc (1:200; Santa Cruz); mouse anti–hemagglutinin (HA) (1:200; Santa Cruz); rabbit anti-cC3 (1:200; CST); rabbit anti-cleaved Dcp-1 (1:200; CST); mouse anti-Diap1 (1:100; gifted from S. Wu, Nankai University, China); 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI; 1:8000; Coolaber); and Hoechst (CellorLab). All secondary antibodies used in this study were bought from Jackson ImmunoResearch and were diluted at 1:500 for use.

X-ray irradiation

The third-instar larvae of indicated genotypes were placed in an X-Rad225 OptiMAX biological irradiator (PXi) and subjected to x-ray irradiation at a total dose of 15 Gy. After 6 hours of incubation, wing discs were dissected for immunostaining and subsequent imaging as described above.

Cell culture, transfection, immunoprecipitation, and immunoblotting

S2 cells were maintained in SFX-Insect (HyClone) medium supplemented with 1% penicillin/streptomycin (Sangon Biotech) at 25°C. 293T, A549, or H1299 cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (Gibco) or RPMI 1640 (Gibco) medium containing 10% fetal bovine serum (Transgene) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin at 37°C with 5% CO2. Cells were transfected using Lipo2000 (Vazyme) or PEI (Sigma-Aldrich) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Forty-eight hours after transfection, cells were harvested for IP and immunoblotting (IB) analysis with standard protocols. To knockdown hdac3 in S2 cells, the double-stranded RNA (dsRNA) was synthesized by the T7 RNAi Transcription Kit (Vazyme) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. For inhibitor treatment, 293T, A549, or H1299 cells were exposed to 50 μM RGFP966 for 4 hours before harvesting. DNA templates targeting hdac3 (amino acids 1 to 173) and gfp (amino acids 1 to 177) were generated by PCR and used for producing dsRNAs. The ds-gfp was used as a negative control. The dsRNA was transfected into S2 cells using Lipo2000 (Vazyme) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The sequences of siRNAs were as follows: siHDAC3-1, 5′-AAU CAG AAC UCA CGC CAG UAU dTdT-3′; siHDAC3-2, 5′-AAU AUC CCU CUA CUC GUG CUG AdTdT-3′; siNC, 5′-UUC UCC GAA CGU GUC ACG UdTdT-3′. Protein stability assay

Protein pulse-chase experiments were carried out as previously described (58). Briefly, S2 cells were plated in 10-cm dishes and transfected with the indicated plasmids. After 24 hours, the cells were transferred into 12-well cell culture plates with equivalent densities. Cells were treated with CHX (20 μg/ml; Calbiochem) for the indicated durations before harvesting. IB experiments were conducted to examine the levels of indicated proteins.

GST pull-down assay

GST fusion proteins and His fusion proteins were expressed in Escherichia coli BL21 upon isopropyl-β-d-thiogalactopyranoside induction and then purified by corresponding affinity beads (Beyotime). Whole-cell lysates from 293T cells expressing Fg-Hdac3 were mixed with GST fusion proteins and incubated at 4°C for 3 hours, followed by pull-down using Beaver beads GSH (BeaverBio) at 4°C for an additional 2 hours. The bound proteins were detected by IB assay.

RNA extraction and RT-qPCR

The total RNA from the flies or S2 cells was isolated using TRIzol (Invitrogen) following the standard protocol. Statistical analysis
