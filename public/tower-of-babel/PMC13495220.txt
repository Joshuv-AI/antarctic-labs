Impact of a leadership training programme on nursing leadership, self-efficacy and competency among ICU clinical nurses: a quasi-experimental study in China
Authors: Congcong Liu, Fangyan Yue, Min Ding, Xue Chen, Heng Cao, Yu Han
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundRecent trends emphasise the importance of the nursing leadership in China and globally. However, research pertaining to nursing leadership interventions targeted at clinical nurses remains sparse in China.AimThis study evaluated the impact of a leadership training programme on the nursing leadership, self-efficacy, and competency of clinical nurses working in intensive care units (ICU).MethodsA prospective, parallel-group quasi-experimental design was used. Thirty-five clinical nurses with the average age of 33.8 years from the ICUs of two tertiary comprehensive teaching hospitals were enrolled with 17 in the intervention group and 18 in the control group. The intervention group participants underwent an eight-session training programme tailored to clinical nurses. Each session lasted 60–90 min and focused on teambuilding, leadership and management skills, missions and goals, organisational culture and climate, emotional intelligence, communication skills, and implementing changes. The Nursing Leadership Scale, ICU Competency Self-assessment Questionnaire and General Self-efficacy Scale were used for pre-test and post-test evaluations. Semi-structured interviews were conducted with nine participants to complement quantitative measures. Data analyses were performed using thematic analyses.ResultsSignificant positive changes were observed in the dimensions of nursing leadership: organisational, planning, interpersonal, and communication skills (P < 0.05). Intervention groups participants also scored higher on self-efficacy and ICU competencies than did controls (P < 0.05). Semi-structured interviews identified three themes: a broader understanding of nursing leadership, changes in personal thoughts and behaviour, and existing obstacles and expectations for future training.ConclusionThe targeted training programme effectively improved the leadership, self-efficacy, and competency of ICU clinical nurses.Implications for nursing managementThis study contributes to the existing literature by focusing on the development of clinical leadership among ICU nurses and by providing valuable support for nurse administrators to implement effective leadership education on a larger scale.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12912-026-04890-8.Keywords: Intensive care unit, Charge nurse, Nursing leadership, Training programme, Self-efficacy, Competency

Introduction

The global healthcare system is undergoing profound transformation due to population aging, changing disease patterns, and rapid medical advancements, all of which place increasing demands on nursing practice to deliver high-quality, safe, and efficient care. In this context, the nursing profession is expanding beyond traditional task-based duties toward more autonomous and decision-oriented roles. Nurses are now expected not only to excel in clinical tasks but also to coordinate complex care, exercise clinical judgment, and lead quality improvement efforts [1]. This expanded role makes developing Nurse Clinical Leadership both essential and urgent.

Clinical leadership refers to the capacity demonstrated through professional competence in patient care, enabling nurses to inspire teams and continuously improve the quality of nursing practice [2]. It is not synonymous with administrative position or managerial authority; rather, it emphasizes influence, accountability, and the ability to drive change. Nurses with clinical leadership skills excel in complex clinical situations by demonstrating sound clinical judgment, effective teamwork and communication, and a strong commitment to advocating for patient-centered best practices. Previous evidences have shown that effective nursing leadership is closely associated with increased nurse retention, improved health and well-being [3, 4], enhanced work engagement [5], and optimized quality of care [6, 7]. Consequently, a growing number of organizations recognize the necessity of fostering clinical leadership among nurses. For example, the Magnet Recognition Program® by the American Nurses Credentialing Center (ANCC) identifies transformational leadership as one of the five core components required for Magnet® designation [8]. Initiatives such as the “Leadership for Change” program, launched in China through a collaboration between the International Council of Nurses and the Chinese Nursing Association, also reflect this priority [9, 10]. Although the importance of strengthening clinical leadership is globally acknowledged, not all nurses have equal access to leadership education [11, 12]. Many leadership development programs are designed for nurse managers or leaders, resulting in a potential lack of clinical leadership skills among frontline nurses directly involved in patient care [12, 13, 14, 15, 16, 17].

In the ICU setting, clinical nurses are often required to assume leadership roles beyond routine bedside care. They must coordinate with physicians, respiratory therapists, junior nurses, patients’ families, and other healthcare professionals while ensuring timely and accurate implementation of complex treatment plans [18]. ICU nurses also frequently encounter ethical dilemmas, high workload, end-of-life care situations, and rapidly changing clinical conditions. These challenges require strong leadership competencies, including effective communication, clinical decision-making, emotional regulation, problem-solving, and the ability to influence team performance. ICU nurses frequently act as crucial informal leaders in daily clinical practice; however, they often lack training programmes specifically designed for frontline clinical leadership. Most existing leadership training is adapted from courses for nurse managers and lacks contextual customization [19, 20, 21]. This gap underscores the necessity of tailored research and educational interventions focused on ICU nurses’ distinct leadership challenges and needs.

In summary, although increasing attention has been paid to the development of nursing leadership competencies, previous studies have primarily focused on nurse managers, and evidence regarding leadership training for ICU clinical nurses remains limited. Moreover, few interventions have been specifically designed to address leadership challenges in the ICU context. Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate the effectiveness of an eight-week leadership training programme tailored for ICU clinical nurses. We then assessed its impact on nurses’ leadership abilities, self-efficacy, and overall competency.

Materials and methods

Setting and participants

This study employed a prospective, parallel-group quasi-experimental design. The study was conducted in Jinan, Shandong Province, a major urban center in eastern China. Participants were recruited from two care units at two tertiary comprehensive teaching hospitals (commonly classified as “Grade A, Level III” in the Chinese healthcare system). In China, tertiary Grade A hospitals represent the highest level of medical institutions, providing comprehensive and specialized health services, acting as regional healthcare centers, and bearing significant responsibilities in medical education and scientific research. The two selected ICUs were comparable in terms of bed–nurse ratios, management styles, and case-mix distributions, based on hospital records and administrative data, supporting a balanced basis for comparison between the intervention and control groups. The ICU nurses were organised into 5–6 groups based on their work experience and clinical ladder, working collaboratively in teams of 8–9 members per shift. Each care team was led by three nursing team leaders who were responsible for patient safety and operational efficiency during their shifts. They not only cared for their patients but also supported junior nurses, liaised with doctors for bed or staff allocation, and prevented adverse nursing events. Inclusion criteria were (1) nurse team leaders; (2) nurses who agreed to participate in the study and provide informed written consent. Exclusion criteria were nurses who went out for further study or took sick or maternity leave during the study period. For logistical feasibility and to minimize contamination between groups, allocation was conducted at the ICU site level rather than at the individual nurse level. The two ICUs were randomly assigned to either the intervention group or the control group. Accordingly, eligible nurses from the ICU allocated to the intervention group received the eight-week leadership training programme, whereas eligible nurses from the ICU allocated to the control group received routine nursing management. Baseline demographic and professional characteristics were compared between the two groups before the intervention to assess group comparability. A total of 35 clinical senior nurses were enrolled in this study, with 17 in the intervention group and 18 in the control group. None of the participants had received formal leadership training prior to the study. The sample size for this study (N = 35) was appropriate for its quasi-experimental, pilot nature. The primary aim was to assess the feasibility and potential effect of the intervention prior to a larger-scale trial. Therefore, a convenience sample comprising all eligible senior nurses from the two partner hospitals was utilized.

Interventions

ICU clinical nurses in the control group received routine nursing management from a head nurse, including regular shift scheduling, daily work supervision, quality control, and routine meetings, without any additional leadership training, whereas ICU clinical nurses in the intervention group underwent an eight-week leadership training programme. The leadership training programme, developed and implemented by a researcher who had received certification as a training tutor from the International Council of Nurses Leadership For Change Programme (ICN LFC) training programme, was based on the Best Practice Guideline ‘Developing and Sustaining Nursing Leadership (2nd edition)’ by the Registered Nurses’ Association of Ontario in conjunction with a transformational leadership framework [22] and the five defining clinical leadership attributes outlined by Patrick et al. [2]. The programme design was also informed by consultations with a psychoeducation professor and the chief nurse manager. Drawing on the benefits of discussion-based learning and the situated learning model, various interactive learning strategies were employed. This leadership training program followed a standardized intervention manual and consisted of eight sessions, each lasting 60–90 min, held once a week on Wednesday afternoons from June to July 2021 (Table 1). Following the sessions, participants were divided into five subgroups to complete group homework, focusing on identifying the main issues affecting patient safety or quality of care and developing quality improvement plans.

Instruments and data collection

Data were collected between May and October 2021. Online surveys were used for the quantitative analysis. An online survey was created by the researcher at www.wjx.com and shared with participants. The researcher monitored survey completion through the platform’s backend system by checking submission status, response completeness, and completion time. Questionnaires with incomplete responses or unusually short completion times were excluded before data analysis. Pre-test data were collected before the intervention, and post-test data were collected one month after the intervention. Socio-demographic characteristics, such as age, sex, length of work experience, highest education level, professional rank, marital status, and ICU nurse specialist certification were gathered using a personal information form.

Nursing leadership was evaluated using the Nursing Leadership Scale developed by Jia in 2013 [23], which is widely utilised to assess leadership among clinical nurses in China owing to its established reliability and validity. This 46-item scale comprises seven subscales: problem-solving skills, professional nursing competence, interpersonal skills, organisation and planning skills, self-care competence, communication skills, and critical thinking skills. Participants rated their responses on a five-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (totally disagree) to 5 (totally agree), with higher scores indicating stronger nursing leadership. In our research, the Cronbach’s α coefficient for the overall scale was 0.97, with subscales ranging from 0.85 to 0.92, indicating excellent internal consistency.

Self-efficacy was measured using the General Self-efficacy Scale originally developed by Schwarzer and translated by Wang [24]. The scale has been validated among ICU nurses in China and demonstrated good reliability and validity. It is a 10-item scale with responses scored on a four-point frequency scale from 1 (disagree) to 4 (agree), with higher scores reflecting greater self-efficacy. The internal consistency of the total scale was 0.92, indicating excellent internal consistency.

The Competency Self-assessment Questionnaire developed by Qiao [25] was used to assess nurses’ post competence. The questionnaire, validated for its psychological properties, comprised 58 items categorised into four dimensions: professional knowledge, professional skills, professional abilities, and psychological traits. Each item is rated on a five-point Likert scale ranging from 0 (incompetent) to 4 (very competent). Cronbach’s alpha was 0.98 for the total scale, with the subscales ranging from 0.91 to 0.97, indicating excellent internal consistency.

Semi-structured interviews, guided by an interview guide, were conducted within one week of training to gather participants’ subjective perceptions of the intervention. The semi-structured interview guide was developed specifically for this study based on the research objectives, relevant literature on nursing leadership, and the content of the leadership training programme. The research team discussed, refined, and revised the draft guide to ensure that the questions were clear, relevant, and aligned with the study objectives. The semi-structured interview guide is provided in Supplementary Material 1. A purposive sample from the intervention group comprising clinical nurses representing various levels of nursing leadership was selected for process evaluation. Data saturation was achieved by the ninth nurse. The interviews included questions such as follows: (1) How did you perceive the leadership training programme? (2) Did your understanding of leadership change after the training? (3) Did your daily behaviour change post-training? and (4) Do you have any suggestions for improvement? Individual face-to-face interviews, lasting 20–30 min, each were audio-recorded with participants’ consent. The qualitative interview data were transcribed within 24 h post-interview.

Data analysis

All quantitative data were analysed using SPSS (version 23.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). The normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Normally distributed variables were summarized as mean ± standard deviation (M ± SD), while non-normally distributed variables were expressed as median and interquartile range. Categorical variables were described as frequencies and percentages.

Group comparisons of socio-demographic characteristics between the intervention and control groups were performed using two-sided Student’s t-tests for normally distributed continuous variables and two-sided Fisher’s exact tests for categorical variables. Between-group differences in key study outcomes—including nursing leadership, self-efficacy, and competency—were analysed using two-sided Student’s t-tests for normally distributed data and two-sided Mann–Whitney U tests for non-normally distributed data. A two-sided p-value < 0.05 was set a priori as the threshold for statistical significance. The Colaizzi seven-step method for phenomenological studies was employed for qualitative data analysis. Initially, the transcribed text was imported into the NVivo 12.0 qualitative research software. Two interviewers thoroughly reviewed all transcribed interview content, extracted significant statements, formulated meanings, and organised them into clusters of themes. Subsequently, researchers integrated these clusters into a comprehensive description and summarised them to identify the fundamental structure of the phenomenon. Finally, the interview findings were validated by returning them to participants to confirm their accuracy.

Ethical considerations

Ethical approval was obtained from the Institutional Review Board of Shandong Provincial Hospital (no. 2021 − 272). The study was conducted in compliance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Participants were informed that their participation was voluntary and that they could withdraw from the study at any time without any negative consequences. To protect confidentiality, each participant was assigned a unique study code, and identifiable personal information was not included in the dataset used for analysis.

Self-efficacy and post competency

No significant differences in general self-efficacy or post competency were found between the two groups at the beginning of the training programme (Table 4). By the end of the programme, clinical nurses showed statistically significant differences in general self-efficacy and psychological traits (Table 5). Compared to pre-intervention levels, the intervention group showed statistically significant increases in scores for general self-efficacy and psychological traits (Table 6). In contrast, no statistically significant changes were observed in the control group across all measurement outcomes.

Findings from the qualitative review

Eighty-two formulated meanings were created using the Colaizzi method and grouped into clusters of three main themes and eight subthemes.

Theme 1: A broader understanding of nursing leadership

The clinical nurses interviewed reported an increased understanding of nursing leadership, with two emerging subthemes: (a) nursing leadership was seen as a holistic representation of an individual’s capabilities; and (b) nursing leadership was recognised as extending beyond nurse managers, as fellow nurses also demonstrated leadership qualities.

Sub-theme 1: A multidimensional and wide-ranging concept

Clinical nurses stated that after training, they developed a deeper understanding of nursing leadership, viewing it as a multidimensional concept that encompassed various abilities. They believe that nursing leadership is a powerful force that can influence others to achieve organisational goals.

I think nursing leadership is approximately equal to a person’s charisma… which consists of many traits such as his attitude or his ability… Nursing leadership is a comprehensive judgement of a person. (P1)

When I first got to know the concept of leadership, I felt that it might be far away from myself, and it was only involved in nurse managers… Now I think that being a good leader should not only make rules and regulations that employees have to follow but also establish common goals, vision and mission within an organisation and lead the nurses realise these goals. A nurse manager is capable of taking control of any unexpected situations that arise during this process. (P3)

Sub-theme 2: The ability not limited to nurse managers or hospital settings

Participants reported a shift in their perspective following the eight-week training programme, recognising that leadership extends beyond nurse managers to include clinical nurses. Nurse leadership is defined as the capacity to influence a group towards a shared objective, applicable not only in the workplace but also in personal life. Further, participants noted an improved understanding of the distinctions between leaders, leadership, managers, and management skills.

Prior to attending this training programme, I held the belief that leaders and managers were synonymous. However, my perspective has since evolved. It is now evident that the roles of leaders and managers should be integrated. Specifically, the head nurse must excel in both management and leadership capacities. In her managerial role, she is tasked with maintaining control over all aspects. Conversely, in her leadership role, she is expected to inspire, articulate a vision, and motivate others towards the collective objectives of the organisation. Head nurses who possess adept management skills are commonly recognised as effective leaders. (P4)

After the lecture, I have come to realise that leadership is evident in all aspects of our lives, from managing departments and nursing teams to even extending to arranging our family life… The presence of leadership can be observed everywhere. (P3)

Management attaches great importance to the present, emphasises decision-making, command, governance and control, and functions through levels and systems, with a certain degree of coercion… leadership faces the future, emphasises providing direction and guidance to subordinates, and functions through individuals and culture. (P8)

Theme 2: Changes in personal thoughts and behaviour

Sub-theme 1: Imperceptible changes in personal thinking

Participants reported experiencing positive outcomes from the training, noting improvements in both intellectual and emotional well-being. Consequently, they expressed feelings of valuation, encouragement, and empowerment.

I think there is definitely a long-term, subtle effect. Every time the leader speaks, it reminds me of nursing leadership. (P1)

I sense that my perspective or vision has expanded, no longer as limited as before (P5).

Sub-theme 2: Improvement in communication skills

Participants noted that the training was instrumental in helping them recognise the significance of their communication skills. They displayed enhanced empathy, active listening, and effective expression.

The most notable change for me has been my improved ability to communicate with medical personnel, a situation that previously led me to become easily frustrated. Following the training, I am now more aware of my own communication limitations and make a conscious effort to remain calm and consider things from their perspective. I have noticed a significant improvement in my communication abilities, with medical personnel more frequently agreeing with me as a result of my active listening and sincere expressions. (P5)

I used to overlook the importance of empathy and emotional intelligence. I would speak bluntly without regard for others’ feelings, potentially causing harm to relationships with friends and family. However, I have now learned to be more empathetic. (P7)

Learning to express myself in a more gentle manner has made my advice more well-received and has strengthened my collaborations with others. (P9)

Sub-theme 3: Improvement in planning and organisation ability

Clinical nurses play a crucial role in coordinating, managing, and delegating tasks to team members during shifts, which necessitates strong organisational and coordination skills. Following structured education, participants reported significant improvements in their planning, coordination, and organisational abilities.

I learn to assign tasks and duties in accordance with the team members’ competence. Instead of taking on by myself, I learned to delegate. I will take the initiative to think who can complete it, what kind of difficulties they may face and what I can do to help them. This might be empowerment. It provides my team members more chances to undertake responsibility and develop new skills. (P8)

When something unexpected happens, I am not so nervous as before. I become more assertive. I try to think like a leader, keep calm and consider what is needed and what to do first. (P2)

Sub-theme 4: Improvement in regulation and management of self-emotions

Participants demonstrated significant improvements in regulating and managing their emotions, as evidenced by their ability to control their emotions and think rationally.

Before attending this training, I often lost my temper or felt upset when things did not go well. Now I am conscious of the negative effects of emotional fluctuations and are working on enhancing self-discipline… I feel a little more open-minded. (P1)

When I may lose my temper, I will try to calm down for three seconds and take a deep breath first. It seems that the way I speak becomes different and I can control my emotions better. (P7)

It seems that I become much more tolerant. As a nurse team leader and teaching nurse, I should inspire and guide others in working. Sometimes I cannot help blaming them but now I become more inclusive of their shortcomings or weaknesses, which is conducive to creating a positive learning environment and driving them forward. (P9)

Theme 3: Existing obstacles and expectations for future training

Sub-theme 1: Obstacles or confusion

In the realm of obstacles and expectations, while most interviewees reported significant improvements in their communication skills and empathy, obstacles and confusion remained when applying theoretical knowledge to clinical practice. These obstacles manifest as limited applicable scenarios or gaps between theoretical knowledge and practical skills.

In terms of communication skills, I might take it more seriously now. But sometimes I feel that I could not get the point. Maybe more experience needs to be accumulated. (P7)

Leadership of the nursing team leader may be different from that of the head nurse because they deal with different problem. (P8)

Sub-theme 2: Expectations and suggestions

Participants demonstrated high levels of enthusiasm for the training programme and displayed keen interest in both the style and content of the intervention. Clinical nurses anticipated increased opportunities to enhance their leadership skills.

I think leadership development is an ongoing process, which requires sufficient time for practice. I hope that a second cycle of training would be provided to assess the effectiveness of the curriculum. (P9)

We have never received this kind of training. We hope that more classes related to self-growth or humanistic care instead of professional knowledge would be given to promote not only our professional performance but also our personal development… A short break may be beneficial because longer classes can be tiring. (P5)

We were all engaged because it gave us great sense of belonging. Round-table layout may be better in facilitating communication. (P6)

Discussion

This study established a systematic and constructive training programme tailored to the needs of clinical nurses and validated its effects on improving participants’ nursing leadership, self-efficacy, and competency. These findings are consistent with those of previous systematic reviews, indicating that educational interventions are effective in fostering leadership skills among clinical nurses [26]. Given that all participants were nurse team leaders, their extensive clinical experience and expertise were at a high level compared with other nurses. Therefore, the targeted interventions focused on other attributes of the five characteristics, that is communication, collaboration, coordination and interpersonal understanding [2]. Further, we considered the complex clinical challenges faced by ICU nurses and made tailored adjustments in line with the recommendation that interventions should align with the organisational context for optimal implementation [11, 27].

The results showed that the intervention was associated with improved scores on leadership dimensions such as interpersonal skills, organizational skills, planning skills, and communication skills. Qualitative findings also supported this observation. The nurses interviewed reported that, following the training, they had gained a deeper and more comprehensive understanding of leadership. They described nursing leadership as a multidimensional and broad concept involving communication, coordination, influence, planning, emotional regulation, and the ability to mobilize others to achieve organizational goals. Furthermore, participants recognized that leadership is not limited to nurse managers; frontline nurses can also demonstrate leadership in their daily clinical work. This qualitative finding helps explain the improvement in leadership scores at the quantitative level. The project appears to have reshaped participants’ identification with leadership roles, enabling them to view themselves not merely as task executors, but as potential clinical leaders capable of influencing the functioning of the intensive care unit (ICU) team and the quality of care. These results are consistent with those of a prior study that highlighted improvements in seven leadership dimensions, including communication skills, task implementation, and team leadership, among 43 head nurses following a four-month action learning programme [15]. Similarly, Fiset et al. [28] revealed that conflict resolution, communication skills, and organisational management were the preferred skills for clinical staff to learn during leadership training in long-term care settings. Our training curriculum emphasized the teambuilding, communication, interpersonal relationships, organizational context and personal traits that were considered as core components of leadership [14, 29, 30, 31].With regard to delivery format, this study combined both didactic and interactive learning through face-to-face interventions. Various training approaches including case scenarios, role-playing, group discussions and teamwork activities, which have been proved effective in education programme [32, 33, 34] were employed to ensure that trainees applied and integrated what they have learned into actual clinical leadership practice.

A significant increase was observed in the intervention group’s self-efficacy scores after training, which was consistent with prior findings [7]. Self-efficacy refers to beliefs about one’s ability to complete a task successfully. The four sources of information that contribute to self-efficacy are direct experience, vicarious experience, social persuasion, and both somatic and emotional arousal. The interview findings indicated that participants recognised an improvement in their emotional management ability, consistent with previous research [35, 36], suggesting that leadership training enhances nurses’ emotional intelligence, which were beneficial in facing challenging situations and conflicts solving. Emotional management ability refers to the regulation of emotions, it influences how nurses handle various situations, constitutes a key aspect of emotional intelligence, and serves as a crucial foundation for enhancing self-efficacy [37]. Additionally, participants independently undertook small improvement projects, which boosted their confidence in systematically completing projects. Further, participants gained insight into effective leadership practices by observing how other team leaders interacted with their team members and shared their experiences. Vicarious learning helped enhance learners’ confidence in handling challenging situations and stressful events in their daily teamwork.

The training programme is also associated with improvements in psychological traits of participants, which are crucial elements of ICU nurses’ competency. Psychological traits include conscientiousness, emotional stability, a spirit of self-supervision, stress-coping abilities, and service awareness. ICU nurses face various stressors daily, with management and interpersonal relationships being highlighted as significant stressors that lead to emotional exhaustion and affect the quality of care provided. The training programme enabled participants to enhance their ability to express opinions, manage emotions, communicate effectively, and navigate interpersonal relationships, aiding conflict resolution, stress management, and emotional stability, ultimately benefiting the quality of care.

Limitations

This study had some limitations. First, it was limited to two ICUs in China, which means that caution is needed when interpreting the findings because of the small sample size. Future researchers should include a multicentre intervention programme with a larger sample size to validate the current results. Second, the face-to-face delivery format was constrained by time limitations and work commitment, leading to underdeveloped discussions and role-playing activities that may have affected the effectiveness of the intervention. To enhance participation levels, a combination of face-to-face and online education programmes is suggested. Finally, participants were recruited from two ICU units, with one unit randomly allocated to the intervention group and the other to the control group. Although this design was used to minimise contamination between groups, it may have introduced selection bias and institutional confounding. Differences in the clinical environment, team culture, staffing patterns, and routine workflows between the two ICUs may have affected the findings. Although we matched the two ICUs on nurse-to-patient ratio, disease profiles, and workload and assessed baseline comparability, residual confounding cannot be fully excluded. Therefore, future randomised or cluster-randomised multicentre studies are required to confirm these findings.

Conclusion

This study developed and validated a customised leadership training programme for ICU clinical nurses to enhance their interpersonal communication, organisation, and planning skills, which are all dimensions of clinical leadership. The training programme was associated with improvements in ICU nurses’ leadership, self-efficacy, and competency. These findings provide preliminary evidence of its potential effectiveness, although further randomized studies with larger samples and objective outcome measures are needed. Qualitative interviews supported our survey results by revealing shifts in subjective perceptions of nursing leadership post-intervention, transforming it from an exclusive trait to nurse managers to a skill that all nurses should possess.

Implications for nursing management

This study contributes to the existing literature by focusing on the development of clinical leadership among ICU nurses and by providing valuable support for nurse administrators to implement effective leadership education on a larger scale. These findings raise awareness among clinical nurses regarding nursing leadership and highlight the need to enhance staff nurses’ leadership skills. Qualitative methodologies can complement quantitative measures by sensitively reflecting changes in nursing leadership. Introducing a leadership education programme that combines face-to-face and e-learning into daily management practices can enhance participation and improve nursing practices, ultimately leading to better health outcomes.
