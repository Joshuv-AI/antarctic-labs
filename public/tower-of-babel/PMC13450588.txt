Intralesional sodium thiosulfate-induced necrotic ulceration in calcinosis cutis
Authors: JennaKay Colquitt, Taylor Viggiano, David L Swanson, Yul W Yang
Published: 2026

Introduction

Calcinosis cutis (CC) is characterized by the pathologic deposition of insoluble calcium salts within the skin, which can cause substantial morbidity, including chronic pain, recurrent inflammation and infections, functional limitation, and ulceration.1 Sodium thiosulfate (STS) has increasingly been used off-label for calcific disorders.2,3 The precise mechanism by which STS reduces calcium deposits remains unclear, but proposed mechanisms include chelation through formation of calcium thiosulfate complexes, antioxidant properties, and vasodilatory effects that may improve local perfusion.4 Published reports largely describe intralesional STS (ILSTS) as safe and beneficial for reducing lesion burden and improving wound healing.3,5 Here, we describe 2 patients with dystrophic CC who developed significant ulceration and necrosis from ILSTS therapy.

Patient 1

A 68-year-old woman presented with a 7-year history of a progressively enlarging plaque on the right lateral thigh and hip. The lesion appeared abruptly and slowly enlarged over time, with increasing firmness and pain. Prior biopsy demonstrated dystrophic calcification deep within the subcutaneous tissue. Additional workup was negative including SCL 70, SSB, rheumatoid factor, and calcium. ANA and SSA were positive in the setting of known Sjogren syndrome, likely unrelated to her calcinosis cutis. She had no other known rheumatologic conditions. Prior therapies included hydroxychloroquine and topical STS cream without improvement. She had previously undergone ILSTS injections at an outside institution (unknown concentration), with partial symptomatic benefit.

On examination, there was a 10 × 24 cm indurated, skin-colored subcutaneous plaque extending from the lateral thigh to the hip (Fig 1, A). ILSTS therapy was initiated starting focally with 2 mL (0.5 g) of 250 mg/mL STS. At 4 months, 6 mL (1.5 g) of 250 mg/mL ILSTS was administered following anesthesia with lidocaine-epinephrine (1%-1:100,000). At 6 months, she received 4 mL (1.0 g), and at 7 months, the dose was increased to 10 mL (2.5 g). Notably, injections were reported as painful.

One week after the final injection, the patient developed sudden ulceration at the injection site with an 8-cm necrotic eschar with surrounding erythema (Fig 1, B). With wound care, the ulcer gradually improved.

Patient 2

A 58-year-old female presented with a 20-year history of a chronic painful erythematous plaque on the left shin, with a negative workup including calcium and parathyroid hormone, and no history of connective tissue disease. The lesion remained stable until progressively enlarging with increasing pain and pruritus over the last year, prompting further evaluation. Dystrophic CC was confirmed by X-ray demonstrating dystrophic sheet-like calcifications and later by point-of-care ultrasound. Prior treatments included triamcinolone cream and topical 25% STS in zinc oxide without improvement. On examination, there was a 10-cm firm subcutaneous plaque with a 2-cm central ulceration on the left shin (Fig 2, A). Following anesthesia with lidocaine with epinephrine, 5 mL (1.25 g) of 25% STS (250 mg/mL) was injected, resulting in immediate severe pain. Subsequently, she developed a chronic nonhealing wound with full-thickness tissue loss and exposed calcium deposits (Fig 2, B). Plastic surgery performed debridement of nonviable tissue and has been continuing wound management.

Discussion

CC remains difficult to treat, with limited therapeutic treatment options; however, STS has shown promising efficacy with reports of calcium dissolution improving non-healing lesions. ILSTS is generally described as safe and well-tolerated, and there is limited reporting of potential side effects.3 Both of our patients had large, chronic dystrophic CC lesions that were unresponsive to topical STS therapy. After transitioning to ILSTS, each patient reported immediate or early pain at injection sites, followed by progressive ulceration of the overlying skin.

We postulate excessive and/or rapid calcium dissolution contributed to the worsening ulceration. ILSTS injections can cause direct local irritation and inflammation, destabilizing already fragile subcutaneous tissue and vasculature, potentially compounded by epinephrine and lidocaine related microvascular flow effects. Osmotic injury may further contribute, as STS is hypertonic and can cause localized cellular dehydration and cell death.6

In a prior report, concentrations up to 250 mg/mL were administered in volumes of 2-6 mL (maximum 1.5 g) without ulceration or other adverse effects.5 In our series, both patients received 250 mg/mL STS; however, 1 patient received a larger injection volume of 10 mL (2.5 g) and subsequently developed a necrotic ulcer. The second patient developed severe ulceration despite receiving a lower total dose (1.25 g). These findings suggest that ulceration may not be determined solely by concentration or total dose but may instead be influenced by factors such as local tissue perfusion or underlying tissue integrity.

In summary, while STS remains an important therapeutic option for refractory CC, we report the first 2 cases of necrosis and ulceration after intralesional administration.
