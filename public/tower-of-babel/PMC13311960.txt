Parent–infant communication should include vocal and physical interaction from mothers and fathers in the neonatal setting

Context

Infants born preterm have a higher risk of developing speech, language and social behaviour problems and conditions.1 Parental presence, interaction and voice have been linked with improved outcomes.2 3 For this reason, parental involvement must be integrated into the care provided to preterm infants in the neonatal setting. Parental involvement contributes to a greater perceived parent–infant closeness, which is fundamental to parents attaining the skills to communicate with their infant.3 Given that learning to communicate with their preterm infant can be challenging for parents in the neonatal setting, consistent and inclusive support from healthcare providers is essential for parents to gain the confidence and skills required to optimally interact with their infant.

Methods

The purpose of this qualitative study was to investigate parents’ experiences and understanding of factors that enhance and hinder interactions and early communication with their preterm infants in the neonatal unit setting.4 The researchers conducted interviews with eight parents, who were all mothers of preterm infants in a level 1 and level 2 neonatal unit setting in the UK. A narrative approach was used, and thematic analysis was completed to create themes that described the parents’ experiences and perceptions from the interview transcripts.

Findings

Four main themes were revealed in the interview transcripts data analysis including (1) the impact of being in the neonatal unit, (2) different communication strategies, (3) communication barriers and (4) an ongoing need for support at home.4 In the interviews, parents shared feeling overwhelmed and experiencing difficulties communicating with their infants as a result.4 For all parents in this study, touch was noted to be a significant aspect to communication with their infants.4

Commentary

This study aimed to develop an understanding of current practices, perspectives and experiences with communicating and interacting with preterm infants in the neonatal unit setting and produced results that align with current research findings and available literature. It highlighted the importance of parental involvement in their infant’s care and the significant role healthcare providers have in facilitating and supporting parent–infant communication. Parents communicated with their infants using a variety of strategies such as singing, talking and skin-to-skin. Some barriers to communication noted by parents were related to the COVID-19 pandemic, masking requirements, visitor restrictions and the unit environment. During admission, parents experienced feeling overwhelmed, as though their infant was the hospital’s property, and that receiving help to communicate with their infant was underprioritised.

While the aim of the study findings was to improve the understanding of families’ specific learning needs to better communicate with their infants, much of the reported themes reflect barriers and needs of families related to the impact of being in the NICU, effective communication between parents and care providers as well as ongoing support required postdischarge. These findings highlight the importance of building effective relationships with families prior to providing specific parent-targeted learning. The results appear to highlight the complexity between parent–infant physical closeness and vocal interaction. Given that exposure to the parents’ speech during neonatal hospital care has been demonstrated as a potential early marker for later social development in preterm infants, there appears to be an ongoing lack of emphasis on adding this component during the neonatal period.5 Strategies to enhance parent-led interaction during skin-to-skin contact should be incorporated across neonatal care settings. It is also important that the interventions target both mothers and fathers as infants appear to differentiate mother–infant and father–infant interactions, with both contributing to language and non-language development.6
