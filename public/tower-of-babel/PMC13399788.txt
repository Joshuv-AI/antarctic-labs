Tips for Successful Arthroscopic Single‐Bundle Posterior Cruciate Ligament Reconstruction
Authors: Neil Patel, Alexander P Decilveo, Nils Calderón Tejerina, Joseph N Liu, George F Rick Hatch, Bruce A Levy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractInjuries to the posterior cruciate ligament (PCL) are relatively common knee injuries often seen in the setting of multiligamentous knee injuries. Operative treatment for PCL injuries has been described using both single and double bundles with both open and arthroscopic techniques. Proper surgical technique is imperative to prevent residual instability and reduce the risks for graft failure. Our technical note outlines tips to successfully perform an all‐inside arthroscopic single‐bundle PCL reconstruction. We provide a reproducible technique that simplifies graft preparation, improves arthroscopic visualization, and optimizes tunnel positioning while adequately protecting neurovascular structures.

High grade posterior cruciate ligament (PCL) injuries are often associated with other ligamentous knee injuries, most commonly posterolateral corner injuries, in which operative management is generally indicated. 1 The complex anatomy of the PCL and proximity to neurovascular structures makes PCL reconstruction (PCL‐R) a technically demanding procedure. Both open and arthroscopic as well as single‐ and double‐bundle reconstruction techniques have been described. 2 , 3 , 4 , 5 Double‐bundle PCL‐R is more technically challenging and biomechanically superior to single‐bundle techniques, but clinical studies have shown no differences in functional outcomes or graft failure rates. 2 , 3 , 4 , 5 , 6

Implementation of a posteromedial portal for improved arthroscopic visualization has been highlighted as a strategy to safely perform an all inside arthroscopic PCL‐R. 7 , 8 Appropriate graft selection, proper patient positioning, and postoperative rehabilitation protocols limiting active hamstring firing for 4 to 6 months postoperatively play an integral role in improving functional outcomes, reducing rates of revision surgery, and allowing for quicker return to play.

SURGICAL TECHNIQUE

Patient Positioning and Examination Under Anesthesia

The patient is placed supine on the operative table (Video 1). Preoperative examination under anesthesia to assess for concomitant ligamentous injuries and posterior drawer test is performed. An arterial Doppler assessment is performed bedside of the posterior tibial and dorsalis pedis arteries. The operative extremity is positioned in resting 90° of knee flexion with a foot bump and lateral hip post (Figure 1). An upper thigh tourniquet is applied. A full‐size fluoroscopic unit should be available and enter in from the ipsilateral side. If a posterolateral corner reconstruction is also being performed, the fluoroscopic unit should enter from the contralateral side.

Graft Preparation

A fresh, frozen, nonirradiated peroneus longus allograft is quadrupled over 10 to 12 mm in diameter by 90 mm in length. The graft can be prepared with a TightRope RT (Arthrex, Naples, FL) or TightRope ABS (Arthrex, Naples, FL) on the femoral side and a TightRope ABS (Arthrex, Naples, FL) on the tibial side. Each end of the graft is sutured 2 cm from its corresponding loop (Figure 2). Dual suspensory fixation on both the femoral and tibial side is imperative as it allows for sequential tensioning of the graft. The femoral button can be prepared with an InternalBrace (Arthrex, Naples, FL) if desired.

Accessing the Posterior Knee

Standard anterolateral (ALP) and anteromedial (AMP) portals are made. Use an arthroscopic electrocautery and shaver to clear out the footprint of the native PCL on the femur. Remnant tissue can be used to identify the femoral origin of the PCL for subsequent tunnel drilling. Using a modified Gillquist maneuver, the arthroscope can be placed in the posterior knee from the ALP.

Establishing the Posteromedial Viewing Portal (PMP)

With the eyes of the 30° arthroscope facing medially, an 18‐gauge spinal needle can be inserted just proximal to the posteromedial capsular fold aiming towards the tibial PCL remnant (Figure 3). Keeping this portal proximal and high ensures the PMP is not too close to the femoral condyle, which would inhibit instrument freedom of movement.

Tibial PCL Debridement and Placement of PCL Guide

With the arthroscope in the ALP, an arthroscopic electrocautery is used clear a soft tissue plane between the posterior midline septum and the posterior horn of the medial meniscus. This allows the neurovascular structures to relax posteriorly for better visualization of the PCL tibial footprint. Take care to not resect or violate the shiny white fibers of the posterior horn of the medial meniscus.

At this point switching to a 70° arthroscope and viewing through the AMP can improve visualization; however, continued use of a 30° arthroscope is effective. Use a shaver and electrocautery through the PMP to clear out the PCL tibial footprint until it drops off in the back of the knee. It is imperative to visualize the popliteus muscle distally and the mammillary bodies, which lie on either side of the tibial PCL.

Once you delineate the PCL tibial footprint, switch the arthroscope to the ALP and place the anatomic contoured tibial PCL guide through the AMP (Figure 4). Hook the base of the PCL facet and drop your hand. Once the guide is positioned, move the 30° or 70° scope into the PMP for drilling under direct visualization.

Drilling the Tibial Tunnel

Fluoroscopy confirms the correct sagittal position and the arthroscope confirms the correct coronal position of the PCL guide (Figure 5). Placement of the fluoroscopic monitor adjacent to the ipsilateral shoulder of the patient allows the surgeon to see both the fluoroscopic and arthroscopic view simultaneously. A 2.4 mm guide pin is drilled under fluoroscopic guidance with the 70° scope in the PMP, followed by a FlipCutter (Arthrex, Naples, FL).

After visualizing the FlipCutter (Arthrex, Naples, FL) in the posterior knee, detach the tibial guide from the bullet and use the guide itself to retract the posterior capsule and protect the neurovascular structures (Figure 6). The tibial socket is reamed to a depth of 35 mm. If concerned about graft‐tunnel mismatch, drill a longer or full‐length tunnel. Shuttling sutures are passed with a FiberStick (Arthrex, Naples, FL) and retrieved.

Drilling the Femoral Tunnel

The arthroscope is changed to a 30° scope and placed in the AMP. An inside out femoral drill guide enters through the ALP and is placed at anatomic origin of the anterolateral bundle of the PCL, abutting the cartilage margin. A 2.4 mm guide pin is drilled bicortically. An appropriate diameter tunnel is reamed 25 mm in length with an acorn reamer (Figure 7). If a TightRope ABS (Arthrex, Naples, FL) is used on the femoral side, a medial sided incision over the guide pin can be made for later cortical button placement. If a TightRope RT (Arthrex, Naples, FL) is used, no additional medial sided incisions are needed.

(ALP, anterolateral portal; AMP, anteromedial portal; MFC, medial femoral condyle.)

Shuttle sutures are passed through the femoral tunnel. A PassPort cannula (Arthrex, Naples, FL) is placed in the ALP. Both tibial and femoral shuttling sutures are retrieved out of the ALP through the PassPort cannula (Arthrex, Naples, FL) to avoid a suture bridge.

Graft Passage

With the arthroscope in the AMP, the graft is passed into the tibial tunnel first, followed by the femoral tunnel; however, this can be done per surgeon preference. Next, the TightRope II (Arthrex, Naples, FL) button is flipped, or a cortical button is attached on the femoral side depending on graft preparation. The graft is docked in the femur and any remaining graft is pulled into the tibial tunnel. Position of the graft on the tibial side is confirmed while viewing through the PMP.

Graft Fixation and Tensioning

The arthroscope is removed. The knee is cycled. On the tibial side, tension the graft over a cortical button in 90° of knee flexion with an anterior drawer on the tibia. Observe tibial reduction in relationship to the femur under fluoroscopic guidance prior to final tensioning (Figure 8). A SwiveLock (Arthrex, Naples, FL) anchor can be used as backup fixation in the tibia and for the InternalBrace (Arthrex, Naples, FL). Final range of motion is evaluated prior to final tensioning and closure. Arterial Doppler studies are performed in sterile fashion to rule out arterial injury.

On the femoral side, the button (red arrow) is flipped and graft docked in the femoral socket. On the tibial side, tension the graft over a cortical button (blue arrow) in 90° of knee flexion with an anterior drawer on the tibia. Postoperative Rehabilitation

The operative extremity is placed in a hinged knee brace locked in extension. The patient is made partial weight bearing for 6 weeks postoperatively. Prone passive knee flexion from 0° to 90° begins immediately. At 2 weeks, if swelling permits, the patient can be transitioned to a custom dynamic PCL unloader brace. At 6 weeks, progressive weight bearing with crutches begins and the PCL brace can be unlocked. Closed kinetic chain exercises begin at week 6 postoperatively. We limit active hamstring firing until 6 months postoperatively. The PCL unloader brace is worn for a 9 to 12 months postoperatively.

DISCUSSION

Isolated grades I‐II PCL injuries managed conservatively with appropriate rehabilitation protocols and dynamic PCL unloader bracing have excellent outcomes with high rates of return to play. 9 , 10 Shelbourne et al. showed that patients with PCL injuries treated nonoperatively had good subjective outcomes scores, high activity levels, and low rates of osteoarthritis. 10 However, for patients with grade III PCL injuries with residual instability or those associated with multiligamentous knee injuries, PCL‐R or repair is the gold standard for operative management. Numerous arthroscopic techniques for PCL‐R have been described including both single and double bundles and tibial inlay versus transtibial approaches, as well as variations in portal placement and exposure.

Appropriate graft selection and preparation are critical factors for successful PCL‐R. Our technique utilizes a fresh, frozen, nonirradiated single‐bundle peroneus longus allograft augmented with an InternalBrace to maximize graft strength. Although studies have shown double‐bundle PCL‐R may offer greater biomechanical stability, there is limited literature showing differences in clinical outcomes or rates of graft failure. 3 , 4 , 5 , 6 , 11 A prospective randomized controlled trial comparing single‐ and double‐bundle PCL‐R conducted by Yoon et al. found that double‐bundle PCL‐R had increased posterior knee stability but no differences in subjective outcome scores. 4 Similarly, a meta‐analysis performed by Chahla et al. showed that double‐bundle PCL‐R provided improved biomechanical stability but showed no differences in patient reported outcomes in comparison to single‐bundle PCL‐R. 5 Additionally, a double‐bundle technique requires the drilling of 2 separate femoral tunnels which increases the risk of femoral tunnel convergence and medial femoral condyle cortical wall blowout. Given these findings, our technique utilizes a single‐bundle allograft providing excellent clinical outcomes while mitigating the risks associated with a double‐bundle technique.

The anterolateral and posteromedial bundles of the PCL are located within close proximity to the medial meniscal root and popliteal vessels making surgical exposure and transtibial drilling challenging. The difficulty in safely accessing the PCL insertion site may explain the significantly higher rates of complications associated with PCL‐R compared with other commonly performed arthroscopic knee procedures, especially during transtibial drilling. 12 Our technique utilized a modified Gillquist maneuver and 70° scope to safely gain access to and navigate the posterior knee. Additionally, a PMP is established to allow for improved tibial footprint visualization and more accurate tibial tunnel placement thereby limiting risk of injury to adjacent structures. 13 Local anatomy, including the popliteus muscle and mammillary bodies on either side of the PCL remnant, should be used as landmarks to confirm proper positioning of the PCL guide along the tibial insertion of the PCL. Fluoroscopy and arthroscopy used simultaneously during tibial tunnel drilling allows for confirmation of appropriate positioning in both the sagittal and coronal planes, respectively. Additionally, we recommend using the tibial guide itself to retract the posterior capsule for added protection to the neurovascular structures during tunnel reaming.

The inlay PCL‐R technique has been described in recent literature to minimize the theorized mechanical wear and risk of graft failure that may result from abrasion associated with the “killer turn” and transtibial approach. 14 However, studies comparing transtibial and inlay PCL‐R techniques have shown no significant differences in residual laxity, graft failure or functional outcomes. 15 , 16 , 17 Our technique involves the utilization of a PMP during outside‐in transtibial drilling to confirm the anatomic tibial attachment of the PCL and potentially limit the abrasive effects of the “killer turn.”

Strict adherence to a specific PCL‐R postoperative rehabilitation protocol is critical to allow for early return to play while minimizing risk of complications. We allow for partial weight‐bearing for the first 6 weeks while limiting hamstring firing for 6 months postoperatively. Early emphasis on quadriceps muscle activation and simultaneous use of a custom dynamic PCL unloader brace allows for earlier range of motion by maintaining the tibia in a reduced position. 18 Table 1 outlines the pearls and pitfalls associated with our technique in further detail.

Our technical note shows numerous tips for successful PCL‐R but is not devoid of limitations and disadvantages (Table 2). Certain studies have shown that the utilization of transtibial drilling and a single‐bundle technique results in more rapid graft degeneration and residual graft laxity. 19 , 20 Implementation of a PMP may improve visualization but is technically challenging and may increase operative times and risk of injury to nearby neurovascular structures. Certain devices incorporated in our technical note, including a full‐size fluoroscopic unit and 70° arthroscope, may not be readily available or technically challenging to use. Lastly, a custom dynamic PCL unloader brace and dual suspensory fixation may lead to higher implant and patient costs.
