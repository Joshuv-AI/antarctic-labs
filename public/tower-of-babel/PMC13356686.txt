Prime editing enables isogenic modeling of PNPLA3-I148M-associated lipotoxicity in hepatocytes
Authors: Haruna Noguchi, Takuro Uchida, Naoki Wakita, Mitsumasa Takenaga, Tetsushi Sakuma, Serami Murakami, Mitsuki Inomata, Atsushi Ono, Hiroko Hagiwara, Yuka Hirashita, Sotaro Ozaka, Kei Mori, Tomoko Tokumaru, Tomoko Saito, Masao Iwao, Mie Arakawa, Mizuki Endo, Masaaki Kodama, Kazuhiro Mizukami
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe PNPLA3-I148M (rs738409 C>G) variant is a well-established genetic determinant of hepatic steatosis, fibrosis, and hepatocellular carcinoma. However, its precise functional impact on hepatocyte physiology remains incompletely understood. Here, we employed a GFP-linked all-in-one prime editing system to generate isogenic HepG2 clones representing all PNPLA3 genotypes in an identical genetic background. Following palmitic acid (PA) treatment, we evaluated genotype-specific responses to lipotoxic stress. Lipid droplet accumulation and cell viability assays showed that clones harboring the G allele (GG, GC) exhibited enhanced lipid storage and reduced sensitivity to PA-induced toxicity compared to wild-type (CC) clones. Transcriptomic profiling revealed distinct expression patterns among genotypes, consistent with differential regulation of lipid handling and stress adaptation programs. Heterozygous clones displayed partially intermediate phenotypes, suggesting allele-associated effects of PNPLA3-I148M on hepatocellular lipotoxic responses. This study demonstrates that prime editing enables a precise approach for modeling disease-associated genetic variants in human hepatocytes and provides a platform to investigate genotype-associated cellular responses. By combining precise genome editing with human cell modeling, this study establishes a useful system for dissecting PNPLA3-I148M-related genotype-phenotype relationships and for future mechanistic studies of genetically defined liver disease phenotypes.Keywords: MT: Clinical Applications, PNPLA3-I148M, prime editing, isogenic hepatocyte model, lipid droplet accumulation, lipotoxic stress, palmitic acid, transcriptomic profiling, MASLD

Introduction

Non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) now redefined as metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD),1 represents the most common chronic liver disorder worldwide and can progress to cirrhosis and hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC).2 Among the genetic variants associated with disease risk, the I148M substitution (rs738409 C>G) in the patatin-like phospholipase domain-containing 3 (PNPLA3) gene has been defined as the strongest and most consistently validated risk allele across diverse ethnicities.3,4,5 Subsequent studies have further confirmed that PNPLA3-I148M is associated not only with MASLD/NAFLD but also with alcoholic liver disease and viral hepatitis-related fibrosis, underscoring its broad relevance across chronic liver diseases.6,7,8 PNPLA3 exhibits both lipase and acyltransferase activities. It is predominantly localized to the endoplasmic reticulum and lipid droplets, where it plays key roles in regulating lipid droplet (LD) size and abundance.9 Although extensive studies have been performed to elucidate molecular mechanisms underlying these clinically relevant associations, the role of the PNPLA3-I148M variant in lipid metabolism has been incompletely understood. Functional studies using overexpression, knockdown, or mouse knock-in models have suggested that I148M promotes LD accumulation and metabolic dysregulation.10,11 Nevertheless, these approaches are limited by genetic background differences, artificial gene expression levels, or species-specific responses.11 Importantly, widely used hepatic cell lines such as HepG2 and Huh7 are both homozygous for the I148M variant, restricting their utility for direct genotype-dependent comparisons in a human context.12

In MASLD/MASH, hepatocellular injury is thought to reflect lipotoxic stress caused by excessive fatty acid influx and dysregulated lipid handling, rather than neutral triglyceride accumulation itself.13 Importantly, triglyceride accumulation and LD formation may function as adaptive mechanisms that buffer lipotoxic fatty acids.14 Thus, palmitic acid (PA), a major saturated fatty acid, provides a defined in vitro stimulus to examine hepatocyte responses to saturated fatty acid-induced lipotoxic stress.15

Recent advances in precision genome editing, particularly prime editing, enable the generation of scarless, programmable single-nucleotide substitutions without requiring double-strand breaks or donor templates.16 This technology offers a powerful strategy to generate isogenic models for evaluating disease-associated variants in a controlled genetic background. While recent studies have demonstrated the utility of prime editing in stem cells and liver organoids,17 systematic modeling of PNPLA3-I148M in human hepatocytes remains limited.

In this study, we applied a GFP-linked all-in-one prime editing system to generate isogenic hepatoma cells representing all PNPLA3-I148M genotypes (CC [wild type], GC [heterozygous], and GG [homozygous mutant]) and examined their genotype-specific responses to PA-induced lipotoxic stress.

Results

Prime editing of PNPLA3-I148M in hepatoma cell lines

We first confirmed the target sequence of PNPLA3-I148M in HepG2 and Huh7 cells and designed prime-editing gRNA (pegRNA) and nicking gRNA (ngRNA) for PNPLA3-I148M. The I148M variant is positioned at the third exon of the PNPLA3 gene. We designed the engineered pegRNA (epegRNA)18 at the corresponding exon and ngRNA at the second intron. The detailed design of prime editing is shown in Figure S1.

Then, we constructed an all-in-one prime editing vector expressing epegRNA, ngRNA, and modified prime editor harboring nCas9, RT without RNaseH domain.19 The EGFP gene was fused to the prime editor with an intervening autocleavage site and served as a convenient means to measure prime editor expression (Figure 1A).

All-in-one prime editing system was transfected into parental hepatoma cells, and GFP-positive cells were sorted using a fluorescence-activated cell sorter 2 days after transfection and expanded for a week (Figure 1B). Sanger sequencing revealed prime editing on the target I148M, the location of the rs738409 SNP, in the PNPLA3 gene in expanded GFP-positive hepatoma cell lines. Using the EditR,20 we observed 39.3% of target G-to-C conversion in HepG2 (Figure 1C).

For the selection of PNPLA3-I148M variant, the cells were seeded at low density and then selected single clones from the GFP-sorted cells. Selected clones were expanded and genotyped for the PNPLA3-I148M variant; clones with both homozygous and heterozygous variants were identified (Figure 1D). To assess whether prime editing markedly altered PNPLA3 expression, we evaluated PNPLA3 mRNA expression in each independently established clone. Although some clone-to-clone variation was observed, PNPLA3 mRNA expression was broadly maintained across the edited clones without consistent genotype-dependent suppression or overexpression (Figure 1E). PNPLA3 protein expression in the edited clones was also assessed by immunoblotting, and densitometric quantification normalized to GAPDH confirmed that PNPLA3 protein expression was broadly maintained after prime editing (Figure 1F).

Prime editing targeting the PNPLA3-I148M variant was also successfully achieved in Huh7 cells (Figure S2). Huh7 cells harbor a naturally occurring protective variant in HSD17B13 (rs72613567:TA), which has been shown to attenuate liver injury and modulate lipid metabolism.21 Consistent with this protective background, we observed relatively attenuated stress responses to PA treatment in Huh7 cells compared to HepG2 cells (Figure S3). We hypothesize that the protective effect of the HSD17B13 variant may contribute to masking the lipotoxic impact of PNPLA3-I148M in this cell line. Therefore, to evaluate the impact of the PNPLA3 variant with fewer confounding factors, we focused our subsequent functional analyses on the HepG2 isogenic model.

Transcriptomic impact of PNPLA3-I148M in HepG2 isogenic clones

To characterize the transcriptional impact of PNPLA3-I148M, we performed RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) analysis of isogenic HepG2 clones harboring the GG, GC, and CC genotypes. The volcano plot revealed a broad distribution of differentially expressed genes (DEGs), with a notable asymmetry favoring upregulated transcripts in GG cells compared to CC (Figure 2A). Functional enrichment analysis of DEGs (Figure 2B) identified pathways related to xenobiotic metabolism, including drug metabolism-cytochrome P450, metabolism of xenobiotics by cytochrome P450, and chemical carcinogenesis-DNA adducts. Pathways such as PPAR signaling, retinol metabolism, steroid hormone biosynthesis, and ascorbate/aldarate metabolism were also enriched, suggesting genotype-associated transcriptional differences related to lipid sensing and oxidative/redox metabolism. Additional enrichment in the PI3K-Akt pathway and porphyrin metabolism further suggested broader pathway-level differences in GG clones.

To evaluate whether global transcriptional profiles segregate by genotype, we performed unsupervised principal-component analysis (PCA) on 817 genes that were significantly altered in at least one of the three pairwise contrasts (CC vs. GC, CC vs. GG, GC vs. GG; nominal p < 0.05 and |log2FC| > 1). The PCA revealed a separation of CC from both GG and GC, indicating genotype-dependent differences in global transcriptomic profiles. Notably, GC clones formed a cluster separable from CC and shifted from GG along the leading PCs, suggesting that heterozygosity confers detectable transcriptomic deviations from GG, albeit of smaller magnitude than in CC (Figure 2C). Together, these results suggest that the PNPLA3-I148M genotype is associated with genotype-dependent transcriptional profiles, including genes and pathways related to detoxification, lipid sensing, and stress adaptation in hepatocytes.

To validate representative RNA-seq findings from the GG versus CC comparison, we performed qPCR analysis of selected DEGs, including ALDH1A1, AKR1C3, and CDO1. The qPCR results confirmed significant genotype-associated differences in the expression of these genes, supporting the transcriptomic changes identified by RNA-seq (Figure S4).

In summary, the PNPLA3-I148M isogenic HepG2 model revealed genotype-dependent transcriptomic differences under basal conditions.

The I148M variant is associated with lipid droplet accumulation and reduced sensitivity to acute PA-induced stress in HepG2 cells

MASLD is a complex metabolic disorder that is characterized, in part, by an increased flux of free fatty acids into the liver.22 To assess the impact of the PNPLA3-I148M polymorphism on LD accumulation under lipotoxic conditions, we exposed PNPLA3-I148M isogenic HepG2 cells harboring the GG, GC, and CC clones to PA for 48 h. BODIPY staining revealed lower LD accumulation in CC cells compared with GG cells, whereas GC cells exhibited a similar phenotype to GG cells in terms of LD accumulation (Figure 3A).

To quantitatively evaluate LD accumulation, we performed ImageJ-based analysis of BODIPY-stained lipid droplets in PNPLA3-I148M isogenic HepG2 cells. Both LD number and total LD area per cell were significantly increased in the GG and GC clones compared with the CC clone after PA exposure (Figures 3B and 3C). These quantitative image-based analyses provided statistical support for the morphological observations and demonstrated a LD-retention phenotype in PNPLA3 risk allele-harboring clones under PA-induced metabolic stress.

Free fatty acids are either stored as triglycerides in lipid droplets or metabolized via β-oxidation. Overactivity of the β-oxidation pathway can lead to lipotoxicity.23 We then assessed cell viability following PA treatment in PNPLA3-I148M isogenic HepG2 clones (Figure 3D). The clones harboring the G allele (GG and GC) were less sensitive to PA-induced lipotoxicity and maintained high viability close to 80% when compared with untreated cells. In contrast, PA treatment resulted in significant loss of viability in the CC clone compared with the GG and GC clones, indicating genotype-associated differences in the cellular response to PA-induced lipotoxic stress.

Pathway enrichment analysis reveals genotype-specific transcriptional responses to PA exposure

To characterize the PNPLA3-I148M genotype-specific transcriptional responses to lipotoxic stress, we performed RNA-seq analysis using the I148M isogenic clones after PA treatment. Pathway enrichment analysis of DEGs in HepG2 cells carrying the GG, GC, and CC genotypes after PA treatment revealed distinct pathway enrichment patterns among the genotypes.

In the GG clones, the number of significantly enriched pathways was relatively small. Enriched pathways were mainly limited to complement and coagulation cascades, with only modest involvement of PPAR and PI3K-Akt signaling, along with less pronounced metabolic adaptation pathways. These findings suggest a relatively limited transcriptional response related to lipid metabolism under lipotoxic conditions (Figure 4A).

The most extensive transcriptomic changes were observed in the GC heterozygous clones. In addition to enrichment of complement and coagulation cascades, pathways related to fatty acid degradation, central carbon metabolism, PPAR signaling, and cytoskeletal regulation were enriched. Furthermore, genes associated with inflammatory and stress-related pathways, including NF-κB signaling, TNF signaling, Toll-like receptor signaling, and ECM-receptor interaction, were also enriched (Figure 4B). This transcriptomic profile suggests that GC clones exhibit a robust transcriptional response to PA-induced lipotoxic stress, involving simultaneous enrichment of metabolic and inflammatory pathway-related genes, which may reflect an adaptive stress response.

In contrast, the CC clones showed a distinct transcriptional response characterized by enrichment of detoxification- and lipid-handling-related pathways. PA exposure led to the enrichment of genes related to PPAR signaling, xenobiotic metabolism by cytochrome P450, and TGF-β signaling. The complement cascade was also affected but to a lesser extent than in risk-allele clones. This pattern suggests that CC clones retain detoxification- and lipid-handling-related transcriptional responses under PA-induced stress (Figure 4C).

To further validate the RNA-seq findings, qPCR analyses were performed for selected pathway-related genes associated with lipid metabolism and stress responses, including PLIN2, CPT1A, CDO1, TRIB3, and WFS1 (Figure 4D; Table S2). These analyses were performed using representative single clones for each genotype. PLIN2 expression was significantly increased following PA treatment in G allele-harboring clones, consistent with enhanced LD-associated transcriptional responses under lipotoxic conditions. CPT1A expression was more strongly induced in the GC and CC clones than in the GG clone, suggesting attenuated fatty acid oxidation-related transcriptional adaptation in the GG clone. CDO1 and TRIB3 were significantly upregulated in the GC clone, whereas WFS1 showed opposite regulation between the GG and CC clones, suggesting genotype-associated differences in oxidative/redox and ER stress-related gene expression. Overall, these qPCR findings provided targeted transcriptional validation of selected RNA-seq findings and supported genotype-associated transcriptional responses to PA-induced lipotoxic stress.

Discussion

Despite the robust clinical association between PNPLA3-I148M and MASLD progression,3,4,5 the mechanistic basis by which PNPLA3-I148M contributes to disease progression remains not fully elucidated. The PNPLA3-I148M variant has been reported to impair triglyceride hydrolysis,12,24 and human studies have linked this variant to altered hepatic lipid retention.12 Consistent with these prior observations, our PNPLA3-edited HepG2 clones harboring the G allele showed increased LD number and total LD area after PA exposure. However, these findings should be interpreted as quantitative phenotypic evidence of genotype-dependent LD retention, rather than as direct biochemical evidence of impaired PNPLA3-mediated triglyceride hydrolysis. Interestingly, our results showed that the GG clones, despite accumulating higher levels of lipid droplets, exhibited greater viability compared to the CC clones under acute PA stress. This apparent paradox can be explained by the triglyceride buffering capacity of hepatocytes.14 The conversion of toxic free fatty acids into inert neutral lipids within lipid droplets serves as a protective mechanism against lipotoxicity. Given prior reports that the I148M variant impairs the hydrolysis of lipid droplets, risk allele-harboring cells may preferentially sequester fatty acids into lipid droplets, thereby protecting the cells from acute cell death in the short term.24 In this context, the increased LD accumulation observed in GG and GC clones may reflect preferential lipid sequestration under acute PA exposure. However, this enhanced survival may represent a maladaptive response; while it prevents immediate cytotoxicity, the chronic LD retention and subsequent inflammatory pathway-related transcriptional changes, as suggested by our transcriptomic data, may contribute to disease progression and fibrosis over time.

Population-based genetic studies are confounded by environmental factors, polygenic backgrounds, and inter-individual variability. One of the challenges in dissecting PNPLA3 function is the lack of isogenic models that allow direct assessment of the variant’s effects in human hepatocytes. Several studies have established PNPLA3-I148M as a key driver of hepatic steatosis using isogenic models. CRISPR-Cas9-edited hiPSC-derived hepatocytes revealed that I148M induces lipid accumulation and transcriptomic shifts resembling PNPLA3 knockout.11 Another study employed CRISPR-based gene editing in Hep3B cells, showing increased lipid droplets and Golgi remodeling.25 Recently, prime editing was employed for introduction of the I148M into liver organoids, supporting genotype-dependent steatosis.17

Our approach applies prime editing to create isogenic hepatoma cells under endogenous promoter control, allowing precise allele-dose comparisons under lipotoxic stress conditions. While limitations include the absence of multicellular architecture and potential editing mosaicism, our method enables efficient and precise modeling of clinically relevant alleles and provides a robust platform for dissecting PNPLA3 function in hepatocytes. The isogenic analysis indicates that correcting the endogenous locus from G to C attenuated LD accumulation and PA-associated phenotypic differences within the same genetic background. Moreover, heterozygous clones displayed partially intermediate phenotypes. These findings are consistent with an allele-dose-related effect rather than a simple dominant-negative model and mirror clinical observations where heterozygous carriers show a graded risk of steatosis and fibrosis.26,27

Despite these advances, our model also has inherent limitations. First, we did not directly measure PNPLA3-mediated triglyceride hydrolysis in the edited clones. Instead, we used quantitative image-based analyses of LD accumulation as a phenotypic readout of LD retention. Although these assays demonstrated reproducible genotype-associated differences in LD number and total LD area, they do not directly establish the enzymatic mechanism by which PNPLA3-I148M alters LD turnover. Future studies will be required to define how the I148M variant modifies LD remodeling and fatty acid handling in this isogenic system.

Second, we did not perform protein-level validation of downstream pathway-related genes in the present study. Future studies using complementary protein-level or functional pathway assays will be required to determine whether these transcriptional changes lead to corresponding protein-level or functional alterations.

Third, HepG2 cells, while widely used for lipid metabolism studies, are hepatoma-derived and do not fully replicate the primary hepatocyte phenotype. More importantly, fibrosis is a multicellular process, involving the activation of hepatic stellate cells, deposition of extracellular matrix, and complex intercellular signaling networks.28 Our hepatocyte-only system cannot replicate these events. Future work should extend prime editing-based isogenic modeling to non-parenchymal liver cells. For example, introducing PNPLA3-I148M into hepatic stellate cells, such as LX-2, could provide insight into the variant’s role in stellate cell activation, fibrogenesis, and crosstalk with hepatocytes. This approach would facilitate mechanistic studies of how PNPLA3 variants may influence both hepatocyte-driven lipid storage and stellate cell-driven fibrotic remodeling.

Moreover, the scalability of prime editing allows for modeling additional NAFLD-associated variants, such as TM6SF2, MBOAT7, and HSD17B13 alleles, in the same genetic background. Generating combinatorial polygenic models will be crucial to understand gene-gene interactions that underlie interindividual variability in liver disease progression. The development of 3D co-culture systems, organoids,29 and even humanized liver models incorporating these isogenic edits could provide a comprehensive platform for studying steatosis, fibrosis, and hepatocarcinogenesis in a genotype-specific manner.

In conclusion, this study establishes a precise isogenic hepatocyte model of PNPLA3-I148M and demonstrates genotype-associated differences in LD accumulation, PA-induced stress responses, and transcriptomic profiles. This model provides a useful platform for dissecting PNPLA3-I148M-related genotype-phenotype relationships and for future mechanistic studies of genetically defined liver disease subgroups.

Materials and methods

Plasmid design

An all-in-one prime editing plasmid co-expressing modified PEmax, EGFP, epegRNA, and ngRNA from a single vector was constructed by standard molecular cloning methods, such as DNA synthesis, PCR, restriction digestion and ligation, and In-Fusion cloning (Clontech, Mountain View, CA). The full-plasmid sequences and the detailed sequence information of the epegRNA expression cassette are shown in supplemental information.

Cell culture and treatment

HepG2 and Huh7 cells were maintained in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM, #4987481455993, FUJIFILM Wako, Osaka, Japan) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, #172012, Sigma-Aldrich, Taufkirchen, Germany)/1% Penicillin-Streptomycin (PS, #15140122, Gibco, MA), at 37°C and 5% CO2.

0.5M of cells were transfected with 3 μg of the all-in-one prime editing construct for PNPLA3-I148M G-to-C conversion on 6-well plates using Lipofectamine LTX transfection reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA).

A 100 mM Palmitate (PA, #JQ0506018-O-8756; FUJIFILM Wako) stock solution in 99.5% ethanol was prepared. PA was conjugated to 17% bovine serum albumin (FFA-free BSA) to facilitate uptake into the cells at a final concentration of 250 μM. Cells were treated with 250 μM PA for 48 h to induce lipid accumulation and lipotoxic stress. Cells treated with ethanol were prepared as a control.

Cell viability was assessed by CellTiter 96 AQueous One Solution Cell Proliferation Assay (Promega Corporation, Madison, WI) according to the manufacturer’s protocol.

Sequencing analysis and genotyping of PNPLA3-I148M

DNA was extracted using a DNAeasy blood and tissue kit (QIAGEN, Hilden, Germany), and target DNA was amplified using PrimeSTAR Max (TakaraBio, Shiga, Japan) according to the protocol provided by the manufacturer. Sanger sequencing was performed after gel purification using NucleoSpin gel and a PCR Clean-up kit (Macherey-Nagel, Dueren, Germany). The editing efficacy was estimated using EditR (http://baseeditr.com/), an algorithm for predicting potential editing in a guide RNA region from a single Sanger sequencing run.20 For the genotyping assay, the I148M variant (rs738409) was genotyped using a commercial TaqMan SNP Genotyping Assay (Assay ID: C__7241_10; Thermo Fisher Scientific) with TaqMan Genotyping Master Mix (Thermo Fisher Scientific) on the LightCycler 96 system (Roche Diagnostics, Basel, Switzerland) according to the manufacturer’s protocol.

Quantitative real-time PCR

For mRNA quantification, RNA was extracted using NucleoSpin RNA II (Macherey-Nagel). Reverse transcription was performed using SuperScript VILO cDNA synthesis kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Quantitative real-time PCR (real-time qPCR) was performed using LightCycler 480 SYBR Green I Master (Roche Diagnostics) on LightCycler 96 system (Roche Diagnostics) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Quantitative PCR was performed using gene-specific primers listed in Table S1.

Western blotting

Cells were lysed on ice for 20 min in SDS-modified RIPA buffer containing protease inhibitor cocktails (cOmplete Mini; Roche Diagnostics), and then centrifuged at 15,000 rpm for 20 min at 4°C. The resulting cell lysates (20 μg each) were boiled with Laemmli sample buffer and subjected to SDS-PAGE. The samples were transferred to a polyvinylidene difluoride membrane (Merck Millipore, Burlington, MA), which was blocked for 1 h in Block Ace (DS Pharma, Osaka, Japan) at room temperature, then incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies against PNPLA3 (1:2000, Catalog #: AF5208, R&D systems, Minneapolis, MN) and GAPDH (1:1000, Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Dallas, TX). After washing with 1xTBS containing 0.1% Tween 20, the membranes were incubated for 1 h at RT with appropriate secondary antibodies, followed by rewashing. Finally, the signals were detected using an ECL Western blotting analysis system (GE Healthcare, Piscataway, NJ) in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions.

Immunofluorescence microscopy

To visualize the lipid droplets, cells were cultivated on poly-D-lysine-coated coverslips under standard conditions. Prior to cell seeding, cover glasses were coated with 1.4 × 10−2 mg/mL of poly-D-lysine hydrobromide (#A38904-01 0.1 mg/mL; Gibco) to promote cell adhesion. The cells were stained with BODIPY (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Cells maintained on poly-D-lysine-coated cover glasses were fixed for 20 min at room temperature in a 10% formalin neutral buffer solution. Then, the cells were incubated in a 1 μg/mL solution of BODIPY for 30 min at room temperature. Nuclei were stained using DAPI (5 μg/mL) for 30 min at room temperature. Images were captured with FV3000-L4H2-BD-3 (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan).

Analysis of the lipid droplet accumulation

LD accumulation was quantified using ImageJ software (NIH, Bethesda, MD). BODIPY-positive lipid droplets were identified by applying a consistent fluorescence intensity threshold across images acquired under identical imaging conditions. LD number per cell and the total LD area per cell were calculated based on BODIPY-positive signals normalized to the number of DAPI-positive nuclei in each image. Quantitative analyses were performed using five randomly selected fields from each experimental condition.

RNA-seq analysis

HepG2-I148M isogenic clones were treated with or without PA for 48 h (GG: n = 3, GC: n = 3, CC: n = 2). Parental HepG2 cells were prepared as a control. Total RNA was extracted using the NucleoSpin RNA II (Macherey-Nagel). Library preparation was performed using MGIEasy Fast RNA Library Prep Set (MGI Tech Co., Ltd., Shenzhen, China) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Sequencing was performed on the DNBSEQ-G400RS FAST (MGI Tech Co., Ltd).

Low-quality bases and adapter sequences were trimmed from sequencing reads using Trimmomatic (v.0.38). Trimmed reads were mapped to the GRCh38 reference transcriptome using Bowtie2 within RSEM. Gene-level expected counts were generated using RSEM. DEGs were identified using edgeR. Normalized counts per million (CPM) values, log fold-changes (logFC) and p values were obtained from the gene-level raw counts. DEGs were defined as genes with |log2FC| ≥ 1 and p < 0.05.

Statistical analyses

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism version 10 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA). Comparisons between two groups were analyzed using unpaired Student’s t test. Multiple group comparisons were analyzed using two-way ANOVA to evaluate the effects of genotype, PA treatment, and their interaction. A p value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.
