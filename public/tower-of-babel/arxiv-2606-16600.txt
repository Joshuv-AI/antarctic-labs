WaveSync: Constrained Wavefront Optimization for Synchronized Co-Speech Gestures in Humanoid Robots
† Thang Tran Viet 1, † Thanh Nguyen Canh 1,2, Gia Huy Uong 1, Phuc Van Dinh 1, Tan Viet Tuyen Nguyen 3, Xiem HoangVan 1,*, and Nak Young Chong 2,4, *

Abstract
Expressive co-speech gestures are crucial for natural human–robot interaction, but generating them on physical humanoid robots is difficult because gesture strokes must align with speech emphasis while satisfying strict kinematic and dynamic constraints. Unlike virtual avatars, humanoid robots cannot freely execute rapid or overlapping motions, making word-level synchronization and hardware-safe motion planning a coupled problem. We present WaveSync , a hybrid framework in which a Large Language Model decomposes dialogue responses into structured semantic schemas and assigns per-word importance weights, constructing a continuous Semantic Importance Wave. Gesture trajectories are shaped through Dynamic Movement Primitives, enforcing kinematic feasibility while enhancing expressiveness. A Wavefront Optimization stage aligns peak-to-peak gesture–speech synchronization and resolves residual kinematic violations through gesture-duration compression and forward propagation. Experimental evaluation based on five dialogue scenarios shows that our method achieves high synchronization accuracy and outperforms three baselines in both objective and subjective evaluations. Each component in WaveSync plays a necessary role in producing gestures that are expressive, semantically grounded, and kinematically compliant. Introduction

In Human–Robot Interaction (HRI), expressive body language is essential to make robotic communication feel natural and engaging
[
19
,
3
]
. Many studies show that human-like gestures and kinematic cues clearly increase social presence and user trust
[
2
]
. Successful applications include humanoid robots
[
21
,
20
]
, robotic arms
[
9
]
, zoomorphic platforms
[
15
]
, and unmanned aerial vehicles
[
1
]
. On these platforms, nonverbal signals such as posture, gesture, and movement timing help users understand a robot’s intent and build trust-based interactions
[
11
]
.

Generating expressive motion usually involves three stages: defining the gesture, selecting actions from the conversational context, and synchronizing those actions with speech. Researchers have proposed three main approaches to handle this pipeline. Rule-based systems
[
6
,
16
]
are stable but lack flexibility. They compute gestures offline from fixed templates and cannot adapt to live prosody. Data-driven models
[
2
,
21
]
increase gesture diversity. However, they often lack enough emphatic gestures in their training data and can produce trajectories that violate physical hardware limits. Finally, Large Language Model (LLM)-based planners
[
10
,
7
]
provide strong semantic grounding. However, they can hallucinate joint configurations and create latency that undermines real-time responsiveness
[
4
]
.

The third stage, synchronization, is critical for naturalness
[
17
]
but remains the least addressed. Existing state-of-the-art co-speech gesture generation methods mainly focus on virtual avatars
[
13
,
22
]
. However, physical robots are critically different from avatars. Although avatars have unconstrained kinematics, physical robots operate under strict structural and motor velocity limits. They cannot execute the rapid, high-frequency motions that avatars easily perform. Meanwhile, the few studies deployed on physical platforms rely on simple duration matching: stretching the audio or changing the keyframe speed so both streams just end at the same time
[
21
]
. This method ignores sub-utterance structure. As a result, the stress peaks in speech fail to align with gesture stroke maxima, which weakens the synchronization.

To address this gap, this paper introduces
WaveSync
. This framework leverages an LLM to assign importance weights to each word, and these are then combined with Whisper-aligned
[
14
]
timestamps to construct a Semantic Importance Wave (SIW). Gesture trajectories are then shaped by Dynamic Movement Primitives (DMPs)
[
8
]
, which automatically adapt timing and adjust motion style while preserving the original movement. Finally, a Wavefront Optimization module places these gestures onto the SIW. It uses a crossover rule to prevent consecutive gestures from overlapping at their peaks. Additionally, a Forward Propagation mechanism fixes remaining conflicts by adding natural pauses instead of distorting the speech signal. This approach achieves precise peak-to-peak alignment between vocal stress and gesture strokes within the robot’s physical constraints.
The core contributions of this work are as follows:

•

A hybrid architecture that converts LLM-driven semantic intent into robot motions via DMPs to circumvent the risks associated with generating raw joint angles.

•

A co-speech gesture synchronization based on a wavefront optimization module that achieves precise, word-level peak alignment while strictly satisfying the robot’s real-time kinematic and dynamic constraints.

•

Evaluation experiments through high-fidelity simulations demonstrate that our method holds strong promise for future deployment of social robot applications.

Methodology

We present the
WaveSync
pipeline in three stages, illustrated in Fig.
1
. First, an LLM assigns per-word importance weights that are fused with Whisper-aligned timestamps into an SIW. Second, retrieved gestures are adapted via DMPs for time and amplitude scaling. Finally, a wavefront optimizer aligns each stroke peak to its corresponding SIW peak within the robot’s kinematic limits.

:

(1) LLM-Based Semantic Scripting and SIW Synthesis:

(2) Action Modeling via Parabolic Primitives and DMPs:

(3) Co-Speech Gesture Synchronization:

2.1
LLM-Based Semantic Scripting and SIW Synthesis

First, we convert raw conversational text into a continuous energy signal that serves as the optimization target for subsequent modules, proceeding in three sequential steps. Given user input
I
, the LLM generates a response
A
and decomposes it into prosodic segments
S=\{s_{1},\dots,s_{n}\}
. For each word
w_{j,k}
in segment
s_{j}
, the LLM assigns a semantic importance weight
\alpha_{j,k}\in[0,1]
, where values near 1 indicate stressed keywords and values near 0 represent fillers. Each segment is returned as a structured descriptor:

S_{j}=\left\{\;T_{j},\;\alpha_{j,k},\;E_{j}\right\},

(1)

where
T_{j}
denotes the emphasized word within the segment, and
E_{j}
encodes stylistic parameters, such as gesture speed and amplitude, which are passed to the DMPs module.

Since importance weights are defined at the word level rather than in the time domain, a Whisper-based forced-alignment pipeline maps each word
w_{j,k}
to a time interval
[t_{j,k}^{\text{start}},t_{j,k}^{\text{end}}]
in the response audio. The temporal center is defined as:

t_{j,k}^{c}=\frac{t_{j,k}^{\text{start}}+t_{j,k}^{\text{end}}}{2},

(2)

which represent the target timestamp at which the corresponding gesture stroke peak should ideally occur. After that, the discrete pairs
\{(\alpha_{j,k},t_{j,k}^{c})\}
are transformed into a continuous signal
s(t)\in[0,1]
by superimposing Gaussian kernels, which encodes communicative energy as a continuous waveform, serving as the optimization target for the wavefront schedule:

s(t)=\sum_{k=1}^{N}\alpha_{j,k}\cdot\exp\!\left(-\frac{(t-t_{j,k}^{c})^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\right).

(3)

where
\sigma
controls the temporal spread of each peak and
N
is the total word count.

2.2
Kinematic Action Modeling via Parabolic Primitives and DMPs

In parallel, semantic descriptors
S_{j}
is translated into expressive, kinematically constrained trajectories through two steps: parabolic gesture modeling and DMP-based modulation.
Human gestures naturally follow a three-phase structure—preparation, stroke, and retraction
[
18
]
. Accordingly, we model the kinematic energy profile of the
j
gesture as an inverted parabola:

P_{j}(t)=-a\,(t-t^{\text{peak}}_{j})^{2}+h,

(4)

where
t^{\text{peak}}_{j}
is the stroke peak timestamp,
h
is the peak amplitude, and
a
controls the pulse width. This formulation enables gesture peaks to be aligned directly with SIW peaks, forming the mathematical basis for the wavefront optimization in Section 2.3.

After that, gestures are selected from a motion library via SMPL-based
[
12
]
kinematic retargeting from human demonstrations to the robot, and each is encoded as a DMP to achieve adaptive timing and adjust motion style.

\tau^{2}\ddot{x}=\alpha_{x}\bigl(\beta_{x}(g-x)-\tau\dot{x}\bigr)+f(s).

(5)

Two parameters are modulated prior to the wavefront optimization stage. The spatial goal position
g
is dynamically scaled based on the pre-assigned semantic importance weight
\alpha_{j,k}
and the emotional state
E_{j}
, such as an excited state induces wider and faster motion. Stochastic noise is additionally injected into the forcing function
f(s)
to introduce stylistic variation across executions, preventing repetitive, template-like behavior.
Once motion shape and style are fixed, the time-scaling parameter
\tau
serves as the sole free variable for temporal adaptation. Adjusting
\tau
stretches to expand or compress gesture duration
D(\tau)
without altering its underlying kinematic signature, providing the wavefront optimizer with a principled, hardware-safe scheduling handle.

2.3
Speech-Gesture Synchronization

To address challenges of overlaps that arise when gestures with long durations are assigned to closely spaced semantic peaks in the SIW, we present the wavefront optimization algorithm. This resolves temporal conflicts between consecutive gestures while maintaining motion fluency by a three-stage hierarchical policy: peak alignment, execution adjustment, and forward propagation (as shown in Fig.
2
).

Figure 2
:
The three-stage Wavefront Optimization process. (a) Initial temporal conflict where consecutive gestures significantly overlap. (b) DMP duration compression. (c) Safe temporal translation (Forward Propagation) based on the crossover rule to preserve gesture profiles and enable smooth blending.

Firstly, peak alignment mechanism positions each parabolic pulse
P_{j}
such that its stroke peak
t_{\mathrm{peak},j}
coincides with the corresponding SIW peak
t_{k}^{c}
by maximizing the overlap integral:

\max_{\{t^{\mathrm{start}}_{j},\,\tau_{j}\}}\sum_{j=1}^{M}\int_{t^{\mathrm{start}}_{j}}^{t^{\mathrm{end}}_{j}}P_{j}(t)\cdot s(t)\,dt,

(6)

where
t^{\mathrm{end}},j=t^{\mathrm{start}},j+D(\tau_{j})
. Since
s(t)
concentrates higher values around emphasized words, maximizing this objective naturally shifts each gesture pulse toward its nearest semantic peak.

However, peak alignment can cause
P_{j}
to overlap with
P_{j+1}
due to varying action durations. Forcing gestures to execute too rapidly introduces extreme joint velocities that risk hardware damage. Therefore, we adjust the execution duration
D(\tau_{j+1})
, subject to a hard lower bound:

D(\tau_{j+1})\;\geq\;\tfrac{2}{3}\,D^{0}.

(7)

The threshold
D^{0}
is empirically determined to ensure the compressed duration stays at least two-thirds of the nominal length. Compressing beyond this limit causes the forcing function to produce excessively fast motions, which compromises the kinematic integrity of the gesture.

Since execution adjustment alone cannot resolve the conflict, the crossover rule is applied. The descending tail of
P_{j}
must not intrude beyond two-thirds of the ascending phase of
P_{j+1}
, preserving the stroke where the gesture intent is expressed most:

t^{\mathrm{end}}_{j}\;\leq\;t^{\mathrm{start}}_{j+1}+\tfrac{2}{3}\bigl(t^{\mathrm{peak}}_{j+1}-t^{\mathrm{start}}_{j+1}\bigr).

(8)

If (
8
) remains violated at the adjustment limit,
Forward Propagation
shifts all subsequent gestures by the residual overlap:

\Delta_{j}=t^{\mathrm{end}}_{j}-\Bigl[t^{\mathrm{start}}_{j+1}+\tfrac{2}{3}\bigl(t^{\mathrm{peak}}_{j+1}-t^{\mathrm{start}}_{j+1}\bigr)\Bigr].

(9)

Then the start time will be:

t^{\mathrm{start}}_{k}\leftarrow t^{\mathrm{start}}_{k}+\Delta_{j},\qquad\forall\,k>j.

(10)

The accumulated offset
\Delta_{\mathrm{total}}=\sum_{j}\Delta_{j}
is injected into the Text-to-Speech (TTS) pipeline as natural inter-segment pauses, preserving speech prosody while providing the robot sufficient time to complete each kinematically valid stroke. At the intersection boundaries of clipped gestures, motion interpolation ensures smooth transitions from the end of
P_{j}
to the onset of
P_{j+1}
, eliminating joint jerking and producing fluid, natural robotic movement.

Experimental Results

3.1
Experimental Setup

All experiments are conducted in PyBullet with a humanoid upper-body model. Speech is synthesized offline via a neural TTS engine and streamed to the LLM pipeline. The gesture library contains clips covering beat, deictic, and iconic gesture categories, captured from human demonstrations and retargeted to the robot via SMPL-based kinematic mapping. Clips are labeled with semantic tags that the LLM uses to retrieve the appropriate motion for each prosodic segment.

Ablation Baselines.

Three ablated variants isolate the contribution of each WaveSync component:

•

w/o SIW
: Gestures trigger uniformly at word boundaries. This variant disables peak-to-peak alignment with the important words.

•

w/o DMP
: Pre-recorded keyframes replace the DMP execution. This removes kinematic shape modulation and time-scaling (
\tau
).

•

w/o Wavefront
: Removes the hierarchical scheduler; conflicting gestures are hard-clipped or reset to rest pose, with no actuator-limit awareness or state-space blending.

3.2
Objective Evaluation

3.2.1
Metrics

Human-based Sync (HbS).

For each gesture event
i
, the Phase Alignment Metric (PAM) measures the absolute temporal deviation between the predicted gesture-peak timestamp
\hat{T}_{i}^{(m)}
and the human consensus reference
\bar{T}^{*}_{i}
:

\mathrm{PAM}_{i}^{(m)}=\bigl|\hat{T}_{i}^{(m)}-\bar{T}^{*}_{i}\bigr|.

(11)

The event-level HbS score normalises PAM against a 1 s perceptual tolerance threshold
\tau

[
5
]
:

\mathrm{HbS}_{i}^{(m)}=1-\frac{\min\!\bigl(\mathrm{PAM}_{i}^{(m)},\,\tau\bigr)}{\tau},

(12)

and the overall score averages across all events in
\mathcal{S}
:

\mathrm{HbS}^{(m)}=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{S}|}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{S}}\mathrm{HbS}_{i}^{(m)}.

(13)

A score closer to 1 indicates stronger alignment with natural human multimodal interaction. Errors exceeding
\tau
are clamped to 0, reflecting the psycholinguistic finding that asynchronies approaching 1000 ms cause a complete breakdown in perceived naturalness
[
5
]
.

Jerk Magnitude.

Joint jerk magnitude quantifies trajectory fluidity and hardware safety as the
\ell_{2}
-norm of the third derivative of joint positions:

J(t)=\left\|\frac{d^{3}\mathbf{q}(t)}{dt^{3}}\right\|_{2}=\left\|\dot{\mathbf{a}}(t)\right\|_{2},

(14)

where
\mathbf{q}(t)
denotes the joint-angle vector commanded to the robot controllers.

4
Conclusion

We presented WaveSync, a system that synchronizes expressive robot gestures with speech under kinematic and semantic constraints. By combining Semantic Importance Weights, DMP-based trajectory editing, and wavefront optimization, the system achieved accurate peak-to-peak alignment between speech emphasis and gesture strokes. This alignment was done without changing the speech rhythm or violating robot physical limits.
Experiments across five dialogue scenarios showed that WaveSync outperformed all ablated variants across all perceptual dimensions. The ablation study confirmed that each component is necessary.
However, the system depends on a fixed action library, limiting coverage of unconstrained dialogue. Future work will address this through automatic gesture synthesis, and testing WaveSync on real robot hardware remains our next critical step.
