Abstract
Digital twin (DT) is envisioned as a key enabler of sixth-generation (6G) communication systems, evolving from offline descriptive replicas for monitoring and analysis to in-the-loop agents within digital twin networks (DTNs) that couple physical and digital worlds. Recent advances in integrated sensing and communication (ISAC) -driven electromagnetic (EM) scattering methods enable environment twinning by linking channel behaviors to EM properties of the scatterers, supporting interpretable DT states and EM -grounded optimization. However, existing studies primarily focus on DT construction and lack mechanisms for closed-loop control in wireless systems. Moreover, array-geometry mismatch can bias DT reconstruction and degrade control performance, while prior works assume known arrays. To address these gaps, we propose an EM - ISAC -based closed-loop DTN framework with a hierarchical design integrating environment twinning, prior injection, and control decision into an end-to-end loop. Leveraging ISAC measurements, the proposed framework jointly reconstructs scatterer information and array-dependent forward operator and employs a low-complexity Bayesian message-passing algorithm to perform contrast inference and array calibration. The reconstructed DT guides codebook preselection to reduce training overhead and narrow candidate beams. Subsequently, downlink beam-forming (BF) is performed based on DT -predicted channels, enabling latency-bounded closed-loop control. Simulation results demonstrate improved robustness and control performance under array mismatch.

Introduction

Digital twin (DT)
, as a key vision for future
sixth-generation (6G)
communication systems, is driving the evolution toward intelligent design and optimization in wireless networks
[
1
,
13
]
.
Traditionally,
DT
provides a digital replica of the wireless environment and network dynamics, enabling interpretable channel-aware representations and informative priors for system optimization
[
29
]
.
More recently, as wireless networks become reconfigurable and adaptive,
DT
evolves from an offline, monitoring tool into an in-the-loop entity that bridges the physical and digital worlds, enabling closed-loop decision-making.
This transition leads to the concept of
digital twin networks (DTNs)
, which aims to achieve physically–digitally consistent and scalable system optimization
[
2
,
12
]
.

Against this backdrop, existing
DT
-enabled wireless solutions can be broadly categorized as strategies that couple
DT
construction with closed-loop control, depending on what is twinned and how the twin is updated and exploited.
Specifically,
[
3
]
constructs a network
DT
for reliable edge caching, where the physical network is mirrored in the digital domain via vertical and horizontal twinning to support reliability-aware decision-making.
In
[
4
]
, a machine-learning-based
DT
is developed for predictive modeling in wind turbines and integrated with a
next-generation radio access network (NG-RAN)
-assisted cloud platform, enabling virtual monitoring and closed-loop feedback.
In
[
5
]
, a large-model-based channel
DT
is proposed for real-world environment intelligence, where multimodal data and learned representations are used to predict channel behaviors and facilitate downstream wireless optimization.
In
[
6
]
,
DTs
of
electromagnetic (EM)
propagation environments are constructed for live
fifth generation (5G)
networks by passively acquiring multipath parameters from in-network signals and combining
EM
simulation to reproduce site-specific propagation effects in the electrical domain.
Furthermore, in
[
7
]
, an
EM
-scattering-based material reconstruction approach is proposed to reconstruct environmental scatterers using endogenous data from an
integrated sensing and communication (ISAC)
system.

Among these paradigms, the
ISAC
-driven

-scattering perspective has recently attracted increasing attention for
DT
construction, as it provides a physics-consistent view of wireless propagation rooted in
EM
field interactions
[
11
,
12
,
30
]
.
This paradigm is particularly appealing because it enables the construction of a physical

In contrast, conventional approaches to physical-world twinning often rely on extra on-site sensors, such as

and cameras, increasing deployment costs and system complexity.
By linking channel behaviors to the geometry and material properties of environmental scatterers, this approach enables reconstruction of a physically interpretable environment
DT

EM
-grounded optimization.
For wireless systems, such a physics-consistent paradigm turns environment awareness into actionable priors for beam management and resource adaptation, bridging propagation modeling with communication and sensing design
[
21
,
29
]
.
However, most existing studies focus primarily on
DT

[
8
]
, channel reconstruction
[
9
]

A key challenge in closing the loop in practice is how to translate a reconstructed, potentially imperfect
DT

15
,
16
]

DT
-enabled propagation studies assume perfectly known array geometry, under which the propagation channel and array geometry are fully determined once the environment

Motivated by the above gaps, we develop an electromagnetic digital-twin-based closed-loop

Specifically, we move beyond environment twinning and focus on making the reconstructed

To this end, we explicitly account for array calibration by jointly inferring the environment state and the array-dependent forward operator, enabling reliable downstream beam management and

To operationalize this vision, we develop a unified inference-and-control framework that closes the loop from environment twinning to executable beam management.
The proposed framework consists of two tightly coupled stages:

In the first stage,

To address the large problem size, we adopt

[
17
,
18
,
19
]

In the second stage, closed-loop beam management is built upon the reconstructed

DT
-guided codebook preselection reduces training overhead, while uncertainty-aware downlink

based on the
DT
-predicted channel enables latency-bounded control with feedback. From a system-level perspective, the proposed closed-loop

framework trades offline
DT
construction and digital-domain inference for reduced over-the-air training and beam-search overhead.

The main contributions of this work are summarized as follows:

•

We propose a unified inference framework that jointly performs environment reconstruction and array calibration via a

•

We develop a
DT

DT
-guided codebook preselection with uncertainty-aware

•

Through numerical simulations, we demonstrate that the proposed closed-loop
DTN
architecture can be realized entirely from endogenous communication-system data, enabling physical
DT
reconstruction, array calibration, and downstream beam optimization within a unified in-the-loop framework.

II
System Model

We consider a
two-dimensional (2D)
transverse magnetic (
\mathrm{TM}_{z}
)
EM
sensing scenario with an uplink
multiple-input multiple-output (MIMO)
-
orthogonal frequency-division multiplexing (OFDM)
probing protocol.
The
\mathrm{TM}_{z}
assumption is adopted for tractability, under which the
EM
fields are invariant along the
z
-axis.
Let
\mathcal{D}\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
denote the sensing region, which is discretized into
M
grid points, and let
\Gamma
denote a closed observation curve in the far field of
\mathcal{D}
.
A co-located aperture with
L
elements is deployed on
\Gamma
and operates in
time division duplex (TDD)
mode, thus the same aperture alternates between transmission and reception.
In each
TDD
sensing snapshot, all
L
elements are simultaneously excited using a fully digital pilot vector, and an
L
-dimensional baseband observation is collected during the receive phase.
We assume a fully digital baseband architecture throughout.

We consider
K
equally spaced subcarriers centered at
f_{c}
with spacing
\Delta f
, i.e.,

f_{k}\triangleq f_{c}+\Bigl(k-\frac{K+1}{2}\Bigr)\Delta f,\qquad k=1,\ldots,K.

(1)

At subcarrier
k
, the received baseband signal is expressed as

\mathbf{y}_{k}=\mathbf{H}_{k}\mathbf{x}_{k}+\mathbf{n}_{k},\qquad\mathbf{n}_{k}\sim\mathcal{CN}(\mathbf{0},\sigma^{2}\mathbf{I}_{L}),

(2)

where
\mathbf{y}_{k}\in\mathbb{C}^{L\times 1}
denotes the received signal,

\mathbf{x}_{k}\in\mathbb{C}^{L\times 1}
is pilot vector,

\mathbf{H}_{k}\in\mathbb{C}^{L\times L}
represents the end-to-end scattering channel,

\mathbf{n}_{k}
denotes the additive noise,
\mathbf{0}\in\mathbb{C}^{L\times 1}
is the all-zero vector,
and
\sigma^{2}
denotes the noise variance.
By reciprocity under
TDD
operation, the same array is used for both transmission and reception in
DT
construction stage.

II-A

EM-Consistent Scattering Channel and Forward Operator

We adopt an
EM
-consistent scattering model based on the Lippmann–Schwinger equation to characterize the propagation channel, which relates the total field to the incident field and the material contrast within the sensing region.
Let
\omega_{k}=2\pi f_{k}
.
Denote by
E_{k}^{i}(\mathbf{r})
and
E_{k}^{t}(\mathbf{r})
the incident and total
\mathrm{TM}_{z}
fields at pixel
\mathbf{r}\in\mathcal{D}
, respectively.
The Lippmann–Schwinger equation is given by
[
27
]
:

E_{k}^{t}(\mathbf{r})=E_{k}^{i}(\mathbf{r})+\int_{\mathcal{D}}G_{k}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}^{\prime})\,\chi(\mathbf{r}^{\prime};\omega_{k})\,E_{k}^{t}(\mathbf{r}^{\prime})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{r}^{\prime}.

(3)

Here,
\mathbf{r}^{\prime}
is an arbitrary pixel in
\mathcal{D}
and
\chi(\mathbf{r};\omega_{k})
denotes the complex material contrast, capturing both permittivity and conductivity variations:

\chi(\mathbf{r};\omega_{k})\triangleq\varepsilon_{r}(\mathbf{r})-1+\mathrm{j}\frac{\sigma_{r}(\mathbf{r})}{\varepsilon_{0}\omega_{k}}.

(4)

where
\varepsilon_{r}(\mathbf{r})
denotes the relative permittivity,

\sigma_{r}(\mathbf{r})
denotes the electrical conductivity, and
\varepsilon_{0}
is the vacuum permittivity. We assume
\chi(\mathbf{r};\omega_{k})\approx\chi(\mathbf{r};\omega_{c})
with
\omega_{c}\triangleq 2\pi f_{c}
over the probing band.

Define Green’s function as

G_{k}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}^{\prime})\triangleq\frac{-\mathrm{j}}{4}\kappa^{2}(\omega_{k})\,H_{0}^{(2)}\!\bigl(\kappa(\omega_{k})\|\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}^{\prime}\|\bigr),

(5)

where
H_{0}^{(2)}
is the zeroth-order Hankel function of the second kind,
\kappa(\omega_{k})\triangleq\omega_{k}/C
, and
C
is the speed of light.

Discretizing
\mathcal{D}
into
\mathcal{D^{\prime}}
yields the contrast vector
\bm{\chi}\in\mathbb{C}^{M\times 1}
and the in-domain Green’s matrix
\mathbf{G}_{k}^{\mathcal{D}^{\prime}}\in\mathbb{C}^{M\times M}
.
Let
\mathbf{P}_{k}\in\mathbb{C}^{L\times M}
denote the free-space propagation operator between the aperture points on
\Gamma
and the
M
grid points in
\mathcal{D^{\prime}}
.
Under the co-located assumption, the transmit-to-domain and domain-to-receive operators satisfy

\mathbf{H}_{1,k}=\mathbf{P}_{k}^{\mathsf{T}},\qquad\mathbf{H}_{2,k}=\mathbf{P}_{k}.

(6)

Let
\mathbf{E}_{k}^{t,\mathcal{D}^{\prime}},\mathbf{E}_{k}^{i,\mathcal{D}^{\prime}}\in\mathbb{C}^{M\times 1}
denote the discretized total and incident fields in
\mathcal{D^{\prime}}
, respectively.
Then, the discretized Lippmann–Schwinger equation is given by

\mathbf{E}_{k}^{t,\mathcal{D}^{\prime}}=\mathbf{E}_{k}^{i,\mathcal{D}^{\prime}}+\mathbf{G}_{k}^{\mathcal{D}^{\prime}}\diag(\bm{\chi})\mathbf{E}_{k}^{t,\mathcal{D}^{\prime}}.

(7)

Rearranging yields

\mathbf{E}_{k}^{t,\mathcal{D}^{\prime}}=\Bigl(\mathbf{I}_{M}-\mathbf{G}_{k}^{\mathcal{D}^{\prime}}\diag(\bm{\chi})\Bigr)^{-1}\mathbf{E}_{k}^{i,\mathcal{D}^{\prime}}.

(8)

Define the induced (equivalent) scattering source in
\mathcal{D^{\prime}}
as

\mathbf{s}_{k}^{\mathcal{D}^{\prime}}\triangleq\diag(\bm{\chi})\mathbf{E}_{k}^{t,\mathcal{D}^{\prime}}.

(9)

Substituting the above expression yields

\mathbf{s}_{k}^{\mathcal{D}^{\prime}}=\diag(\bm{\chi})\Bigl(\mathbf{I}_{M}-\mathbf{G}_{k}^{\mathcal{D}^{\prime}}\diag(\bm{\chi})\Bigr)^{-1}\mathbf{E}_{k}^{i,\mathcal{D}^{\prime}}.

(10)

For a given pilot
\mathbf{x}_{k}
, the incident field is given by

\mathbf{E}_{k}^{i,\mathcal{D}^{\prime}}=\mathbf{H}_{1,k}\mathbf{x}_{k}=\mathbf{P}_{k}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{x}_{k}.

(11)

The scattered field observed at the aperture is expressed as

\begin{split}\mathbf{y}_{k}&=\mathbf{H}_{2,k}\mathbf{s}_{k}^{\mathcal{D}^{\prime}}+\mathbf{n}_{k}\\
&=\mathbf{P}_{k}\,\diag(\bm{\chi})\Bigl(\mathbf{I}_{M}-\mathbf{G}_{k}^{\mathcal{D}^{\prime}}\diag(\bm{\chi})\Bigr)^{-1}\mathbf{P}_{k}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{x}_{k}+\mathbf{n}_{k},\end{split}

(12)

which shows the received signal depends nonlinearly on the contrast
\bm{\chi}
due to multiple scattering effects.
To enable tractable inference, we reformulate the model into a linear form with respect to
\bm{\chi}
by introducing an equivalent forward operator.
Accordingly, it can be expressed as:

\mathbf{y}_{k}=\mathbf{A}_{k}\bm{\chi}+\mathbf{n}_{k},\qquad\mathbf{n}_{k}\sim\mathcal{CN}\!\left(\mathbf{0},\,\sigma^{2}\mathbf{I}_{L}\right).

(13)

Let

\mathbf{u}_{k}\triangleq\Bigl(\mathbf{I}_{M}-\mathbf{G}_{k}^{\mathcal{D}^{\prime}}\diag(\bm{\chi})\Bigr)^{-1}\mathbf{P}_{k}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{x}_{k}
and use the identity
\diag(\bm{\chi})\mathbf{u}_{k}=\diag(\mathbf{u}_{k})\bm{\chi}
.
Then the forward operator is given by

\mathbf{A}_{k}\triangleq\Bigl(\mathbf{x}_{k}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{P}_{k}\Bigl(\mathbf{I}_{M}-\mathbf{G}_{k}^{\mathcal{D}^{\prime}}\diag(\bm{\chi})\Bigr)^{-\mathsf{T}}\Bigr)\circ\mathbf{P}_{k},

(14)

where
\mathbf{A}_{k}\in\mathbb{C}^{L\times M}
implicitly depends on
\bm{\chi}
through the multiple-scattering interaction.

II-B

Outer Linearization via Born Iterations

The observation model in (
13
) is nonlinear with respect to the contrast vector due to multiple scattering.
To obtain a tractable inference problem, we adopt an outer Born-iteration scheme
[
28
,
31
]
, which linearizes the forward model around the current estimate.
At the
n
-th outer iteration, the linearized operator is constructed by evaluating the forward operator at the previous estimate
\bm{\chi}^{(n-1)}
:

\mathbf{A}_{k}^{(n)}\triangleq\mathbf{A}_{k}\big|_{\bm{\chi}=\bm{\chi}^{(n-1)}}.

(15)

This yields the uncertainty-aware linearized observation model

\mathbf{y}_{k}\approx\mathbf{A}_{k}^{(n)}\bm{\chi}+\tilde{\mathbf{n}}_{k}^{(n)},

(16)

where
\tilde{\mathbf{n}}_{k}^{(n)}
is an effective noise term accounting for both measurement noise and the mismatch caused by the uncertainty in
\bm{\chi}^{(n-1)}
.
When posterior uncertainty of
\bm{\chi}^{(n-1)}
is available, the induced uncertainty of
\mathbf{A}_{k}^{(n)}
can be further propagated into an equation-dependent effective noise covariance.

After collecting multi-frequency observations, the joint inverse problem is formed by stacking
\{\mathbf{y}_{k}\}
and
\{\mathbf{A}_{k}^{(n)}\}
across frequencies.
The resulting linear subproblem in (
16
) is solved by the proposed
GaBP
inference engine
[
17
,
18
,
19
]
, which is scalable to large-scale linear systems.
The outer iterations are repeated until the prescribed convergence criterion is met.

III-A

Linear-Gaussian Model for Joint
DT
Reconstruction

In conventional
EM
inverse scattering, the antenna geometry is assumed to be known, and the inverse problem reduces to estimating the material contrast
\bm{\chi}
under a fixed forward model.
In practical
ISAC
transceivers, however, the array geometry may be mismatched or unknown.
To explicitly capture the dependency, we introduce the array-geometry state
\bm{\theta}
, which parameterizes the propagation operator at subcarrier
k
.
Specifically,
\bm{\theta}
collects the radial and angular coordinates of all array elements.
Accordingly, the propagation operator is expressed as
\mathbf{P}_{k}(\bm{\theta})\in\mathbb{C}^{L\times M}
.

The forward operator is then written as

\mathbf{A}_{k}(\bm{\theta},\bm{\chi})\triangleq\left(\mathbf{x}_{k}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{P}_{k}(\bm{\theta})\big(\mathbf{I}_{M}-\mathbf{G}_{k}^{D}\diag(\bm{\chi})\big)^{-\mathsf{T}}\right)\circ\mathbf{P}_{k}(\bm{\theta}),

(17)

which depends on
\bm{\theta}
through
\mathbf{P}_{k}(\bm{\theta})
and on
\bm{\chi}
through the multiple-scattering interaction.
Thus, the model follows the dependency chain

\bm{\theta}\rightarrow\mathbf{P}_{k}(\bm{\theta})\rightarrow\mathbf{A}_{k}^{(n)}(\bm{\theta})\rightarrow\mathbf{y}_{k}.

(18)

For notational simplicity, we focus on a single subcarrier and omit the subcarrier index
k
in the following.

The inverse problem therefore shifts from estimating
\bm{\chi}
alone to jointly recovering
\bm{\chi}
and
\bm{\theta}
.
This results in a bilinear inverse problem that couples scene and system uncertainties
[
23
,
24
,
25
]
.

A key consequence of this bilinear coupling is the presence of scaling ambiguity.
For the purpose of ambiguity analysis, let
\mathbf{A}^{(n)}(\bm{\theta})
denote the linearized forward operator induced by
\bm{\theta}
.
Then, in the noiseless case, there exists a scalar
\beta\neq 0
such that

\mathbf{A}^{(n)}(\bm{\theta})\bm{\chi}=\mathbf{A}^{(n)}(\bm{\theta}/\beta)\,(\beta\bm{\chi}),

(19)

indicating that different parameter pairs related by
\beta
yield identical measurements.
This ambiguity renders
\bm{\chi}
non-identifiable along the scaling direction and leads to ill-conditioning if not properly regularized.

After outer linearization, we consider the regularized objective

\min_{\bm{\chi},\bm{\theta}}\left\|\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{A}^{(n)}(\bm{\theta})\bm{\chi}\right\|_{2}^{2}+\lambda_{\chi}\|\bm{\chi}\|_{2}^{2}+\lambda_{\theta}\|\bm{\theta}-\bm{\theta}^{(n)}\|_{2}^{2},

(20)

where
\bm{\theta}^{(n)}
denotes the geometry state at the
n
-th outer iteration, and
\lambda_{\chi}
and
\lambda_{\theta}
are the corresponding regularization parameters.

We reinterpret these quadratic penalties in a Gaussian framework and solve the resulting large-scale linear-Gaussian subproblems using the proposed
GaBP
engine
[
17
,
18
]
.
Although the joint problem in
(\bm{\chi},\bm{\theta})
is nonconvex, it becomes conditionally Gaussian when either variable block is fixed.
We therefore adopt an alternating scheme consisting of a
\bm{\chi}
-step with fixed
\bm{\theta}
, and a
\bm{\theta}
-step with fixed
\bm{\chi}
.

The prior for the contrast within the
region of interest (ROI)
is specified as

\chi_{m}\sim\mathcal{CN}\left(\mu_{m}^{(0)},\left(1/\alpha_{\chi,m}^{(0)}\right)\right),\quad\forall m\in\mathcal{M},

(21)

where
\bm{\mu}^{(0)}\in\mathbb{C}^{M}
collects the prior means,
\alpha_{\chi,m}^{(0)}>0
denotes the prior precision for pixel
m
, and
\mathcal{M}\triangleq\{1,\ldots,M\}
.

The nominal array is initialized as a
uniform circular array (UCA)
of radius
R_{0}
, with

\phi_{\ell}^{(0)}\triangleq\frac{2\pi(\ell-1)}{L},\quad\forall\ell\in\mathcal{L},

(22)

and nominal element radius
r_{\ell}^{(0)}\triangleq R_{0}
, where
\mathcal{L}\triangleq\left\{1,2,\cdots,L\right\}
.

At the
n
-th outer iteration, the geometry increment is modeled as

\delta\bm{\theta}_{\ell}\triangleq[\delta r_{\ell},\ \delta\phi_{\ell}]^{\mathsf{T}}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{\Sigma}_{\theta,\ell}),\quad\mathbf{\Sigma}_{\theta,\ell}\triangleq\diag(\sigma_{r}^{2},\sigma_{\phi}^{2}),

(23)

where
\sigma_{r}^{2},\sigma_{\phi}^{2}
are corresponding variances.

III-B

\bm{\chi}
-step: Complex
GaBP
Inference

At the
n
-th outer iteration, the geometry state
\bm{\theta}^{(n-1)}
is treated as fixed.
Accordingly, we define the linearized scattering operator

\mathbf{A}^{(n)}\triangleq\mathbf{A}\!\big(\bm{\theta}^{(n-1)}\big)

and treat it as a known within the current
\bm{\chi}
-step.
The
\ell
-th element of the measurement vector
\bm{y}
is given by

y_{\ell}=\sum_{m\in\mathcal{N}(\ell)}A_{\ell,m}\,\chi_{m}+n_{\ell},\quad n_{\ell}\sim\mathcal{CN}(0,\sigma^{2}),

(24)

for
\ell\in\mathcal{L}
, where
\ell
indexes the aperture elements and
m\in\mathcal{M}
indexes the pixels.
Here,
\mathcal{N}(\ell)
denotes the set of variables connected to factor node
\ell
, and
\mathcal{N}(m)
denotes the set of factors connected to variable node
m
.

Each contrast variable
\chi_{m}
is assigned an independent proper complex Gaussian prior.
At the
n
-th outer iteration, the prior is given by

p(\chi_{m})=\mathcal{CN}\!\big(\chi_{m};\,u_{m}^{(n)},\,v_{m}^{(n)}\big),

(25)

where
u_{m}^{(n)}=\mu_{m}^{(n)}
and

v_{m}^{(n)}=1/\alpha_{\chi,m}^{(n)}
denote the prior mean and variance, respectively.

Expressed in natural-parameter form, the prior becomes

p(\chi_{m})\propto\exp\!\left(-\tau_{m}^{\rm prior}\,|\chi_{m}|^{2}+2\,\Re\!\big\{\left(\eta_{m}^{\rm prior}\right)^{*}\chi_{m}\big\}\right),

(26)

where

\tau_{m}^{\rm prior}=\alpha_{\chi,m}^{(n)},\qquad\eta_{m}^{\rm prior}=\alpha_{\chi,m}^{(n)}\,u_{m}^{(n)}.

(27)

The resulting factor graph consists of
M
variable nodes and
L
factor nodes, each factor corresponds to a linear mixing constraint.

III-B
1
Factor-to-Variable (F2V) Update

For a factor node
\ell
, assume that incoming
variable-to-factor (V2F)
messages are Gaussian:

\varDelta_{m\to\ell}(\chi_{m})=\mathcal{CN}(\chi_{m};\mu_{m\to\ell},v_{m\to\ell}),\quad\forall m\in\mathcal{N}(\ell),

(28)

where
(\mu_{m\to\ell},v_{m\to\ell})
denote the mean and variance of the incoming messages, whose Gaussian form will be established in the subsequent
V2F
update.

The
factor-to-variable (F2V)
message is obtained by marginalizing over all variables except
\chi_{m}
.
Although the exact marginal is not strictly Gaussian, it can be accurately approximated as Gaussian due to the aggregation of many independent contributions in the linear mixture, which can be justified via a central limit theorem argument in the large-system regime
[
26
]
.

Define the row-wise prediction

\widehat{y}_{\ell}\triangleq\sum_{m\in\mathcal{N}(\ell)}A_{\ell,m}\mu_{m\to\ell},\quad S_{\ell}\triangleq\sum_{m\in\mathcal{N}(\ell)}|A_{\ell,m}|^{2}\,v_{m\to\ell}.

(29)

Excluding variable
m

\widehat{y}_{\ell\setminus m}\triangleq\widehat{y}_{\ell}-A_{\ell,m}\mu_{m\to\ell},\quad S_{\ell\setminus m}\triangleq S_{\ell}-|A_{\ell,m}|^{2}\,v_{m\to\ell}.

(30)

Define the effective residual and variance:

y_{\ell\setminus m}^{e}\triangleq y_{\ell}-\widehat{y}_{\ell\setminus m},\quad\sigma_{\ell\setminus m}^{2}\triangleq\sigma^{2}+S_{\ell\setminus m}.

(31)

The resulting

\varDelta_{\ell\to m}(\chi_{m})=\mathcal{CN}\!\left(\chi_{m};\ \frac{y_{\ell\setminus m}^{e}}{A_{\ell,m}},\ \frac{\sigma_{\ell\setminus m}^{2}}{|A_{\ell,m}|^{2}}\right).

(32)

with natural parameters

\tau_{\ell\to m}\triangleq\frac{|A_{\ell,m}|^{2}}{\sigma_{\ell\setminus m}^{2}},\quad\eta_{\ell\to m}\triangleq\frac{A_{\ell,m}^{*}\,y_{\ell\setminus m}^{e}}{\sigma_{\ell\setminus m}^{2}}.

(33)

III-B
2
Variable-to-Factor (V2F) Update

Given Gaussian
F2V
messages and a Gaussian prior, the
V2F
update is obtained by combining them:

\varDelta_{m\to\ell}(\chi_{m})\propto p(\chi_{m})\prod_{\ell^{\prime}\in\mathcal{N}(m)\setminus\{\ell\}}\varDelta_{\ell^{\prime}\to m}(\chi_{m}).

(34)

Since all terms are Gaussian in natural form, the resulting message remains Gaussian, with additive parameters:

\displaystyle\tau_{m\to\ell}

\displaystyle=\tau_{m}^{\rm prior}+\sum_{\ell^{\prime}\in\mathcal{N}(m)\setminus\{\ell\}}\tau_{\ell^{\prime}\to m},

(35)

\displaystyle\eta_{m\to\ell}

\displaystyle=\eta_{m}^{\rm prior}+\sum_{\ell^{\prime}\in\mathcal{N}(m)\setminus\{\ell\}}\eta_{\ell^{\prime}\to m}.

Equivalently, we may express

\displaystyle\varDelta_{m\to\ell}(\chi_{m})

\displaystyle=\mathcal{CN}(\chi_{m};\mu_{m\to\ell},v_{m\to\ell}),

(36)

\displaystyle v_{m\to\ell}

\displaystyle=\tau_{m\to\ell}^{-1},

\displaystyle\mu_{m\to\ell}

\displaystyle=\eta_{m\to\ell}/\tau_{m\to\ell}.

III-B

After a prescribed number of
F2V
and
V2F

\chi_{m}
is given by

\varDelta(\chi_{m})\ \propto\ p(\chi_{m})\prod_{\ell\in\mathcal{N}(m)}\varDelta_{\ell\to m}(\chi_{m}).

(37)

which remains Gaussian in natural form:

\displaystyle\tau_{m}^{\rm post}

\displaystyle=\tau_{m}^{\rm prior}+\sum_{\ell\in\mathcal{N}(m)}\tau_{\ell\to m},

(38)

\displaystyle\eta_{m}^{\rm post}

\displaystyle=\eta_{m}^{\rm prior}+\sum_{\ell\in\mathcal{N}(m)}\eta_{\ell\to m}.

Hence the posterior mean and variance are

v_{m}^{\rm post}=(\tau_{m}^{\rm post})^{-1},\qquad u_{m}^{\rm post}=\eta_{m}^{\rm post}/\tau_{m}^{\rm post},

(39)

and we take
\chi_{m}^{(n)}=u_{m}^{\rm post}
as the

n

To obtain a tractable solution, we adopt a block-diagonal approximation of the global information, thereby decoupling the geometry increments across antennas and leading to a set of per-antenna subproblems.

For each antenna
\ell

(51)

which characterizes the sensitivity of the

\ell

\ell

(52)

with equation-dependent precision

inherited from the global model.

f_{\ell,l}(\delta\bm{\theta}_{\ell})\propto\exp\!\left(-w_{l}^{(n)}\big|e_{l}-\mathbf{j}_{l,\ell}\delta\bm{\theta}_{\ell}\big|^{2}\right).

(53)

(55)

(57)

(58)

(59)

where

is a damping factor.

\xi_{l}^{(n)}\triangleq\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}\mathbf{j}_{l,\ell}\,\mathbf{\Sigma}_{\delta\theta_{\ell}}^{\rm post}\,\mathbf{j}_{l,\ell}^{\mathsf{H}}.

IV-A

DT-Guided Codebook Preselection

Based on the proposed
GaBP
framework, we jointly reconstruct the
EM
properties of environmental scatterers and the antenna-array geometry, enabling simultaneous scatter reconstruction and array calibration.
The resulting physical
DT
is incorporated into the system design and control loop as a scene-specific prior for the propagation model.

Leveraging this prior, the system supports scenario-adaptive parameter tuning and resource reconfiguration within a closed-loop
sensing–modeling–control–feedback
framework.
Here, we first consider codebook preselection to narrow the candidate beam set before beam optimization.

In conventional decoupled systems, identifying the optimal transmit beam requires exhaustive beam training
[
32
]
, in which all codebook beams are sequentially probed.
In large-scale
MIMO
systems, this incurs substantial overhead in training time, signaling cost, and computational complexity.
By contrast, with
DT
-derived propagation priors, dominant departure directions can be inferred directly from the reconstructed scattering geometry, enabling targeted beam transmission without exhaustive codebook sweeping.
This significantly reduces training overhead and search complexity.

Consider a co-located sensing setup in which a
UCA
performs both transmission and reception of multi-frequency probing signals.
Based on the resulting observations, the array geometry is calibrated via the proposed
GaBP
-based
\bm{\theta}
-step.
After calibration, the
UCA
is used as the receive array.
For the subsequent beam-control design, we introduce a separate transmit
uniform linear array (ULA)
with
N_{\mathrm{TX}}
antenna elements and half-wavelength inter-element spacing.

The
\bm{\chi}
-step of
GaBP
yields a discretized contrast distribution over the sensing region
\mathcal{D}
.
Suppose that the reconstruction in
\mathcal{D}
results in
Q
scatterer clusters
\{\Omega_{q}\}_{q=1}^{Q}
, within each of which the contrast is approximately uniform.
We use only the real part of
\chi
to compute the contrast energy, since
\Re\{\chi\}
captures the permittivity contribution that dominates the stored electric energy and directly characterizes dielectric contrast, whereas
\Im\{\chi\}
is primarily associated with conductive loss and is more sensitive to modeling mismatch and measurement noise.

According to (
4
), the relative permittivity is given by

\varepsilon_{r}(\mathbf{r})=\Re\{\chi(\mathbf{r})\}+1.

We then define the contrast energy density of the
q
-th scatterer cluster as

\mathcal{E}(\mathbf{r})\triangleq\left|\varepsilon_{r}(\mathbf{r})\right|^{2},\qquad\mathbf{r}\in\Omega_{q}.

(61)

To handle multiple reconstructed scatterer clusters, we compute an energy-weighted centroid for each cluster and the corresponding geometric departure angle.
Specifically, the scattering center of the
q
-th cluster is defined as

\mathbf{r}^{\mathrm{scat}}_{q}\triangleq\frac{\displaystyle\sum_{\mathbf{r}_{q}\in\Omega_{q}}\mathcal{E}(\mathbf{r}_{q})\,\mathbf{r}_{q}}{\displaystyle\sum_{\mathbf{r}_{q}\in\Omega_{q}}\mathcal{E}(\mathbf{r}_{q})},\quad\forall q\in\mathcal{Q},

(62)

and the associated cluster energy weight is defined as

w_{q}\triangleq\sum_{\mathbf{r}_{q}\in\Omega_{q}}\mathcal{E}(\mathbf{r}_{q}),\quad\forall q\in\mathcal{Q},

(63)

where
\mathcal{Q}\triangleq\left\{1,2,\cdots,Q\right\}
, and
\mathbf{r}_{q}
is a pixel in cluster
\Omega_{q}
.

Let
\mathbf{o}_{\mathrm{TX}}\triangleq[x_{\mathrm{TX}},\,y_{\mathrm{TX}}]^{\mathsf{T}}
denote the geometric center of the
transmit (TX)
array, and let
\mathbf{r}^{\mathrm{scat}}_{q}\triangleq[x^{\mathrm{scat}}_{q},\,y^{\mathrm{scat}}_{q}]^{\mathsf{T}}

The geometric departure angle associated with the

\varphi_{q}^{\mathrm{TX}}\triangleq\arg\!\left((x^{\mathrm{scat}}_{q}-x_{\mathrm{TX}})+j(y^{\mathrm{scat}}_{q}-y_{\mathrm{TX}})\right),\ q\in\mathcal{Q},

(64)

where
\arg(\cdot)
yields an angle of the complex number in
[0,2\pi)
.

DT
-inferred departure angles by selecting the

\mathcal{Q}_{\mathrm{sel}}\triangleq\operatorname{Top}\!-\!Z\big(\{w_{q}\}_{q=1}^{Q}\big),\qquad\Phi_{\mathrm{DT}}\triangleq\{\varphi_{q}^{\mathrm{TX}}:\,q\in\mathcal{Q}_{\mathrm{sel}}\},

(65)

where
\operatorname{Top}\!-\!Z(\cdot)
returns the indices of the
Z

The
DT
-inferred departure-angle set
\Phi_{\mathrm{DT}}
provides a compact abstraction of the reconstructed propagation environment and serves as a natural interface between sensing and control, as shown in Fig.

.
Based on this propagation-aware representation, the closed-loop controller first performs a preprocessing step that uses
\Phi_{\mathrm{DT}}
to guide codebook-based beam preselection.

Consider a
TX

ULA
with
N_{\mathrm{TX}}
elements and half-wavelength spacing.
The corresponding steering vector is defined as
[
34
]

\mathbf{a}_{\mathrm{TX}}(\varphi)\triangleq\frac{1}{\sqrt{N_{\mathrm{TX}}}}\begin{bmatrix}e^{-j\frac{2\pi}{\lambda_{c}}d\,g_{0}\sin\varphi}\\

(66)

where
\lambda_{c}=C/f_{c}
is central wavelength,

is the inter-element spacing, and

denotes the centered element index given by

g_{v}\triangleq v-\frac{N_{\mathrm{TX}}-1}{2},\qquad v=0,1,\ldots,N_{\mathrm{TX}}-1.

(67)

We define a predefined angular codebook consisting of
U

[0,2\pi)

\Phi_{\mathrm{cb}}\triangleq\left\{\varphi_{u}\;\middle|\;\varphi_{u}=\frac{2\pi(u-1)}{U},\;u=1,2,\ldots,U\right\},

(68)

where these angles represent geometric directions of departure, rather than the intrinsic steering angle domain of the array.

The
TX

BF

\mathbf{F}_{\mathrm{TX}}\triangleq\left[\mathbf{a}_{\mathrm{TX}}(\varphi_{1}),\,\mathbf{a}_{\mathrm{TX}}(\varphi_{2}),\,\ldots,\,\mathbf{a}_{\mathrm{TX}}(\varphi_{U})\right].

(69)

For each codebook beam, we evaluate its matching score with the
DT
-inferred departure-angle set
\Phi_{\mathrm{DT}}

S_{u}\triangleq\max_{\varphi^{\prime}\in\Phi_{\mathrm{DT}}}\left|\mathbf{a}_{\mathrm{TX}}^{\mathsf{H}}(\varphi_{u})\,\mathbf{a}_{\mathrm{TX}}(\varphi^{\prime})\right|^{2},\quad u=1,2,\ldots,U,

(70)

where
\varphi_{u}\in\Phi_{\mathrm{cb}}
denotes the
u

We then select

\mathcal{B}_{\mathrm{sel}}\triangleq\operatorname*{arg\,max}_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathcal{B}\subseteq\{1,\ldots,U\}\\
|\mathcal{B}|=B\end{subarray}}\sum_{u\in\mathcal{B}}S_{u},

(71)

where
\mathcal{B}_{\mathrm{sel}}
denotes the index set of the selected beams.

This completes the first technical step of the proposed closed-loop beam-control framework, namely the identification of candidate beam directions for subsequent robust beam design.

IV-B

Closed-Loop
BF

The second technical step of the proposed closed-loop beam-control framework is to perform robust downlink
BF
based on the candidate beams identified in the preceding codebook-preselection step.
The purpose of this step is not to introduce a new
BF
algorithm, but rather to provide a principled way of exploiting the
DT

TX

With the dominant transmission directions explicitly characterized by the preceding codebook-preselection step,
the

a subset of candidate beams.
Specifically, let
\mathcal{B}_{\mathrm{sel}}
denote the index set of the shortlisted codewords, and collect the corresponding codewords into the matrix

\mathbf{W}_{\mathrm{DT}}\triangleq\left[\mathbf{a}_{\mathrm{TX}}(\varphi_{u})\right]_{u\in\mathcal{B}_{\mathrm{sel}}}.

(72)

The
TX

DT

\mathbf{f}=\mathbf{W}_{\mathrm{DT}}\bm{\eta},\qquad\|\mathbf{f}\|_{2}=1,

(73)

where
\bm{\eta}
is a low-dimensional coefficient vector.

Based on the reconstructed scattering clusters, we construct a frequency-dependent predicted
MIMO
channel as

\widehat{\mathbf{H}}(f)\approx\sum_{q=1}^{Q}\alpha_{q}\,e^{-j2\pi f\tau_{q}}\,\mathbf{a}_{\mathrm{RX}}(\varphi^{\mathrm{RX}}_{q})\,\mathbf{a}_{\mathrm{TX}}^{\mathsf{H}}(\varphi^{\mathrm{TX}}_{q}),

(74)

where
\varphi^{\mathrm{RX}}_{q}
and
\varphi^{\mathrm{TX}}_{q}
denote the azimuth
angle of arrival (AoA)
and
angle of departure (AoD)
extracted from the reconstructed propagation geometry.
Moreover,

\tau_{q}\triangleq\frac{L_{q}}{C}

denotes the geometric delay of the
q
-th cluster, where
L_{q}
is the corresponding path length to the scattering center.
The cluster-wise gain proxy is given by

\alpha_{q}\propto\sum_{m:\mathbf{r}_{m}\in\Omega_{q}}\left|[\widehat{\bm{\chi}}]_{m}\right|,

(75)

where
\widehat{\bm{\chi}}
denotes the final contrast estimate.

For notational simplicity, the frequency argument is omitted below when no confusion arises.

The
receive (RX)

\bm{\theta}
-step.

Let
\mathbf{o}_{\mathrm{RX}}
denote the
RX

\mathbf{r}_{\mathrm{RX},\ell}
be the calibrated coordinate of the
\ell
-th element. Then,

\displaystyle\mathbf{a}_{\mathrm{RX}}(\varphi)

\displaystyle\triangleq\frac{1}{\sqrt{L}}\begin{bmatrix}e^{-j\frac{2\pi f_{c}}{C}\,(\mathbf{r}_{\mathrm{RX},1}-\mathbf{o}_{\mathrm{RX}})^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{u}(\varphi)}\\
\vdots\\
e^{-j\frac{2\pi f_{c}}{C}\,(\mathbf{r}_{\mathrm{RX},L}-\mathbf{o}_{\mathrm{RX}})^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{u}(\varphi)}\end{bmatrix},

(76)

\displaystyle\mathbf{u}(\varphi)

\displaystyle\triangleq[\cos\varphi,\ \sin\varphi]^{\mathsf{T}}.

Assuming maximum-ratio combining (MRC) at the receiver, the predicted received
signal-to-noise ratio (SNR)
at frequency
f
is

\widehat{\gamma}(f;\mathbf{f})\triangleq\frac{\left\|\widehat{\mathbf{H}}(f)\,\mathbf{f}\right\|_{2}^{2}}{\sigma_{\mathrm{c}}^{2}},

(77)

and the corresponding wideband
SNR
surrogate is

\widehat{\Gamma}(\mathbf{f})\triangleq\sum_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\widehat{\gamma}(f;\mathbf{f})=\frac{1}{\sigma_{\mathrm{c}}^{2}}\sum_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\left\|\widehat{\mathbf{H}}(f)\,\mathbf{f}\right\|_{2}^{2},

(78)

where
\mathcal{F}
denotes the set of considered frequency tones, and
\sigma_{\mathrm{c}}^{2}
denotes the communication-noise power at the receiver.

Since
\widehat{\mathbf{H}}(f)
is constructed from
DT
-estimated cluster parameters and calibrated geometry,
\widehat{\Gamma}(\mathbf{f})
inherits
DT
mismatch.
We therefore adopt an outage-aware design
[
35
]
:

\Pr\left\{\widehat{\Gamma}(\mathbf{f})\leq\Gamma_{\mathrm{th}}\right\}\leq\epsilon,

(79)

where
\Gamma_{\mathrm{th}}
denotes the target
SNR
threshold and
\epsilon
is the allowable outage probability.
Using a standard conservative sufficient condition based on one-sided concentration bounds, we obtain

\mu_{\Gamma}(\mathbf{f})-\zeta_{\epsilon}\,\sigma_{\Gamma}(\mathbf{f})\ \geq\ \Gamma_{\mathrm{th}},

(80)

where
\mu_{\Gamma}(\mathbf{f})\triangleq\mathbb{E}[\widehat{\Gamma}(\mathbf{f})]
and

\sigma_{\Gamma}^{2}(\mathbf{f})\triangleq\mathrm{Var}[\widehat{\Gamma}(\mathbf{f})]
are taken with respect to the
DT
-induced uncertainty.
Equivalently, we maximize the risk-sensitive utility

\max_{\|\mathbf{f}\|_{2}=1}\ \mu_{\Gamma}(\mathbf{f})-\zeta_{\epsilon}\,\sigma_{\Gamma}(\mathbf{f}).

(81)

Substituting
\mathbf{f}=\mathbf{W}_{\mathrm{DT}}\bm{\eta}
yields a low-dimensional problem over the
DT
-informed subspace.
Since
\mathbf{W}_{\mathrm{DT}}
is not necessarily column-orthonormal, we approximate the mean and variance terms by quadratic forms of
\bm{\eta}
while retaining a closed-form generalized eigen-solution.
Define

\displaystyle\mathbf{G}_{\mu}

\displaystyle\triangleq\frac{1}{\sigma_{c}^{2}}\sum_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\mathbf{W}_{\mathrm{DT}}^{\mathsf{H}}\mathbf{T}_{\mu}(f)\,\mathbf{W}_{\mathrm{DT}},\ \mathbf{T}_{\mu}(f)\triangleq\mathbb{E}\!\left[\widehat{\mathbf{H}}^{\mathsf{H}}(f)\widehat{\mathbf{H}}(f)\right],

(82)

\displaystyle\mathbf{G}_{\sigma}

\displaystyle\triangleq\frac{1}{\sigma_{c}^{2}}\sum_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\mathbf{W}_{\mathrm{DT}}^{\mathsf{H}}\mathbf{T}_{\sigma}(f)\,\mathbf{W}_{\mathrm{DT}}.

where
\mathbf{T}_{\sigma}(f)\succeq\mathbf{0}
captures the
DT
-induced uncertainty in the Gram domain.
Importantly, the proposed
GaBP
-based
DT
construction primarily provides posterior mean estimates of the cluster parameters appearing
in (
74
).
Let

\bm{\psi}\triangleq\big\{\alpha_{q},\tau_{q},\varphi_{q}^{\mathrm{RX}},\varphi_{q}^{\mathrm{TX}}\big\}_{q=1}^{Q}

collect the DT-estimated parameters of the
Q
dominant clusters, including the complex gain, delay, AoA, and AoD of each cluster.
The covariance matrix
\mathbf{C}_{\psi}
quantifies the corresponding estimation uncertainty.
We then obtain
\mathbf{T}_{\sigma}(f)
via first-order uncertainty propagation from
\mathbf{C}_{\psi}
. Specifically, define

\mathbf{h}_{\psi}(f)\triangleq\mathrm{vec}\!\big(\widehat{\mathbf{H}}(f)\big),\qquad\mathbf{J}_{\psi}(f)\triangleq\frac{\partial\mathbf{h}_{\psi}(f)}{\partial\bm{\psi}^{\mathsf{T}}}.

(83)

Since
\widehat{\mathbf{H}}(f)\in\mathbb{C}^{L\times N_{\mathrm{TX}}}
, we partition

\mathbf{J}_{\psi}(f)
row-wise according to the receive-antenna index as

\mathbf{J}_{\psi}(f)\triangleq\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{J}_{\psi,1}(f)\\
\vdots\\
\mathbf{J}_{\psi,L}(f)\end{bmatrix},\qquad\mathbf{J}_{\psi,\ell}(f)\in\mathbb{C}^{N_{\mathrm{TX}}\times d_{\psi}},

(84)

where
\mathbf{J}_{\psi,\ell}(f)
denotes the Jacobian block associated with the

\ell
-th receive row of
\widehat{\mathbf{H}}(f)
, and

d_{\psi}\triangleq\dim(\bm{\psi})
.
Then,
\mathbf{T}_{\sigma}(f)
is approximated as

\mathbf{T}_{\sigma}(f)\approx\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}\mathbf{J}_{\psi,\ell}(f)\,\mathbf{C}_{\psi}\,\mathbf{J}_{\psi,\ell}^{\mathsf{H}}(f),

(85)

where
\mathbf{C}_{\psi}\in\mathbb{C}^{d_{\psi}\times d_{\psi}}
and

\mathbf{T}_{\sigma}(f)\in\mathbb{C}^{N_{\mathrm{TX}}\times N_{\mathrm{TX}}}
.

Under the subspace parameterization
\mathbf{f}=\mathbf{W}_{\mathrm{DT}}\bm{\eta}
,
both
\mu_{\Gamma}(\mathbf{f})
and
\sigma_{\Gamma}(\mathbf{f})
admit quadratic approximations with respect to
\bm{\eta}
.
Accordingly, the objective can be approximated as

\mu_{\Gamma}(\mathbf{W}_{\mathrm{DT}}\bm{\eta})-\zeta_{\epsilon}\,\sigma_{\Gamma}(\mathbf{W}_{\mathrm{DT}}\bm{\eta})\approx\bm{\eta}^{\mathsf{H}}\mathbf{G}_{\mathrm{rob}}\bm{\eta},

(86)

with
\mathbf{G}_{\mathrm{rob}}\triangleq\mathbf{G}_{\mu}-\zeta_{\epsilon}\mathbf{G}_{\sigma}
, where
\mathbf{G}_{\mu}
and
\mathbf{G}_{\sigma}
denote the effective
subspace matrices induced by
\mathbf{T}_{\mu}
and
\mathbf{T}_{\sigma}
, respectively.

Consequently, the second step of the closed-loop beam-control design reduces to the following robust
BF
problem:

\max_{\bm{\eta}}\;\bm{\eta}^{\mathsf{H}}\mathbf{G}_{\mathrm{rob}}\bm{\eta}\quad\text{s.t.}\quad\bm{\eta}^{\mathsf{H}}\mathbf{E}\,\bm{\eta}=1,

(87)

where

\mathbf{E}\triangleq\mathbf{W}_{\mathrm{DT}}^{\mathsf{H}}\mathbf{W}_{\mathrm{DT}}.

(88)

This yields the generalized eigenvalue problem whose solution is given by the dominant generalized eigenvector. Specifically,

\bm{\eta}^{\star}=\operatorname{eig}_{\max}(\mathbf{G}_{\mathrm{rob}},\mathbf{E}),\qquad\mathbf{f}^{\star}=\frac{\mathbf{W}_{\mathrm{DT}}\bm{\eta}^{\star}}{\|\mathbf{W}_{\mathrm{DT}}\bm{\eta}^{\star}\|_{2}}.

(89)

From a control standpoint, the second step performs continuous beam refinement within the DT-informed subspace
spanned by the shortlisted codewords obtained in the preceding preselection step.
More broadly, the proposed closed-loop beam-control framework can be interpreted as a resource-reallocation mechanism:
by leveraging
DT
priors to restrict the effective beam search space, the required pilot-signaling overhead is significantly reduced, while the complexity of
DT
reconstruction can be handled offline.
In this sense, the framework trades offline computational resources for over-the-air training efficiency.

VI
Conclusion

This paper proposed an electromagnetic
ISAC
-assisted digital twin framework for antenna array calibration and DT-guided
BF
. By reconstructing a physically consistent digital twin that captures both the propagation environment and array geometry, the proposed approach mitigates array-manifold mismatch and supports more reliable model-based
BF
. More importantly, the proposed closed-loop architecture provides a resource-reallocation mechanism: rather than relying mainly on exhaustive over-the-air training, it leverages offline electromagnetic reconstruction and digital-twin inference to narrow the beam-management space and support downstream communication optimization. In this sense, the method effectively trades offline computational resources for air-interface resources, reducing online training overhead while maintaining robust
BF
performance. Overall, the results highlight the value of accurate DT reconstruction as a bridge from electromagnetic sensing to actionable communication control, thereby establishing a closed-loop link among environment perception, digital twin construction, and wireless system optimization.
