Schur rings over cyclic groups having Almost Commutative Terwilliger algebras
Nicholas L. Bastian, Stephen P. Humphries

Abstract
Terwilliger algebras are subalgebras of a matrix algebra constructed from an association scheme. Rie Tanaka defined what it means for a Terwilliger algebra to be almost commutative and gave five equivalent conditions in the case where the association scheme is commutative. A sixth condition for a Terwilliger algebra coming from a commutative Schur ring to be almost commutative has since been discovered. In this paper we first provide a classification of orbit Schur rings that produce an almost commutative Terwilliger algebra for a finite cyclic group. In particular, we show that the subgroup of automorphisms used to form the orbit Schur ring is either trivial, or the whole automorphism group when the cyclic group has prime power order. If the cyclic group has order 2 n 2^{n} , these are the only options. If the cyclic group has order p n p^{n} , for an odd prime p p , then the automorphism subgroup of order p n − 1 p^{n-1} also works. If the group has a non-prime power order then the only orbit Schur ring that produces an almost commutative Terwilliger algebra comes from the trivial subgroup of the automorphism group. We then give a condition for when a wedge product of Schur rings produces an almost commutative Terwilliger algebra. This allows us to determine exactly when a Schur ring over a cyclic group produces an almost commutative Terwilliger algebra.

Terwilliger algebra, Camina group, association scheme, automorphism group, Schur ring, cyclic group, wedge product, wreath product

2
. We claim
O(z)\neq O(z^{m-1})^{*}
. Suppose
O(z)^{*}=O(z^{m-1})
. Then
z^{-1}\in O(z^{m-1})
. Hence, there exists some
\varphi\in H
such that
\varphi(z^{-1})=z^{m-1}
. Thus,
\varphi(z)=z^{m+1}
, and since
m
is odd,
\gcd(m+1,2m)
is even. Then
z\mapsto z^{m+1}
is not an automorphism of
G
in this case, a contradiction. Therefore,
O(z)^{*}\neq O(z^{m-1})
.

Now
|H|>1
and
|O(z)|=|H|
, so there is some
b=z^{k}\in O(z)
such that
b\neq z
. Then

\overline{O(z)}\cdot\overline{O(z^{m-1})}=(z+z^{k}+\cdots)(z^{m-1}+\cdots)=z^{m}+z^{m+k-1}+\cdots=\overline{O(z^{m})}+\overline{O(z^{m+k-1})}+\cdots.

Therefore,
T(G,\mathfrak{S})
cannot be
AC
.
∎

Theorem 4.3
.

Let
G
be a cyclic group of non-prime power even order. Let
1<H\leq\Aut(G)
and
\mathfrak{S}=\mathfrak{S}(G,H)
. Then
T(G,\mathfrak{S})
is not AC.

Proof.

Suppose
|G|=2^{a}r
where
r
is odd. We induct on
a
. For
a=1
, Lemma
4.2
gives the result. Now suppose for any cyclic group
L
,
|L|=2^{m}r
,
m\geq 1
we have the following: for every
1\lneq H\leq\Aut(G)
, if
\mathfrak{S}=\mathfrak{S}(L,H)
, then
T(L,\mathfrak{S})
is not AC. We now assume
|G|=2^{m+1}r
. Let
1<H\leq\Aut(G)
and
\mathfrak{S}=\mathfrak{S}(G,H)
be the orbit Schur ring. Suppose
T(G,\mathfrak{S})
is AC.

Let
K_{2}\text{\ char\ }G
of index
2
. As
T(G,\mathfrak{S})
is AC, by Lemma
3.13
,
T(K_{2},\mathfrak{S}_{K_{2}})
is AC. However, by induction for
1<M\leq\Aut(K_{2})
, the orbit Schur ring
\mathfrak{T}=\mathfrak{S}(K_{2},M)
gives
T(K_{2},\mathfrak{T})
that is not AC. Therefore, as
T(K_{2},\mathfrak{S}_{K_{2}})
is AC, we see that
H|_{K_{2}}
is trivial. That is,
H
fixes every element of
K_{2}
.

Let
G=\langle z\rangle
, so
K_{2}=\langle z^{2}\rangle
. Let
\varphi_{t}\in H
. Then
\varphi_{t}(z^{2})=z^{2}
, so
t\equiv 1\mod 2^{m}r
. Then either
t=1
, or
t=1+2^{m}r
. Therefore,
H=\{id,\varphi_{1+2^{m}r}\}
.

We know
|O(z)|=|H|
and so
O(z)=\{z,z^{1+2^{m}p_{1}^{b_{1}}p_{2}^{b_{2}}\cdots p_{m}^{b_{m}}}\}
. Clearly
O(z)\neq O(z)^{*}
. We have

O(z)O(z)=(z+z^{1+2^{m}r})(z+z^{1+2^{m}r})=z^{2}+z^{2+2^{m}r}+z^{2+2^{m}r}+z^{(1+2^{m}r)(1+2^{m}pr)}

=z^{2}+2z^{2+2^{m}r}+z^{1+2^{m+1}r+2^{2m}p^{2}b}=2z^{2}+2z^{2+2^{m}r}.

This is a sum of at least two distinct classes since
z^{2}\in K_{2}
and
\varphi_{t}
fixes
K_{2}
. Hence,
\{z^{2}\}
is a class and we have a contradiction.
∎

Now for cyclic groups of odd non-prime power order we have:

Proposition 4.4
.

Let
n=p^{a}q
for distinct odd primes
p
and
q
, with
a\geq 1
. Let
G=\mathcal{C}_{n}
and
H\leq\Aut(G)
. Let
\mathfrak{S}=\mathfrak{S}(G,H)
. Then
T(G,\mathfrak{S})
is AC if and only if
H
is trivial.

Proof.

First assume
T(G,\mathfrak{S})
is AC. Then
G=K\times L
where
K=\langle x\rangle
,
|K|=p^{a}
and
L=\langle y\rangle
,
|L|=q
. Both
K
and
L
are characteristic. As
T(G,\mathfrak{S})
is AC, by Lemma
3.13
,
T(K,\mathfrak{S}|_{K})
and
T(L,\mathfrak{S}|_{L})
are AC. Since
T(K,\mathfrak{S}|_{K})
is AC we have, by Theorem
3.22
, one of:

1.

H|_{K}
is trivial;

2.

H|_{K}
is generated by the map
x\mapsto x^{1+p}
;

3.

H|_{K}=\Aut(K)
.

Since
|L|=q
and
T(L,\mathfrak{S}|_{L})
is AC by Theorem
3.22
either
H|_{L}=\{id\}
or
H|_{L}=\Aut(L)
. We consider cases based on these possible restrictions of
H
.

Case 1:

H|_{L}=\{id\}
. As
L\text{ char }G
, for
0\leq i<q
,
O(y^{i})=\{y^{i}\}
. We consider three subcases based on
H|_{K}
.

Subcase 1.1:
H|_{K}=\{id\}
. Then
H=\{id\}
.

Subcase 1.2:
H|_{K}=\langle\varphi_{1+p\rangle}
. If
a=1
, then
\varphi_{1+p}
is just the identity map and we are in subcase 1.1. So assume
a>1
. Notice
x^{-1}\in K
and
K\text{ char }G
, so
O(x),O(x^{-1})\subseteq K
. Also
x,x^{1+p}\in O(x)
and
x^{-1},x^{-1-p}\in O(x^{-1})
. We consider
O(x^{-1})O(xy)
. As
O(x^{-1})\subseteq K
and
xy\not\in K
, we have
O(xy)\neq O(x)
. Since
T(G,\mathfrak{S})
is AC we have
O(x^{-1})O(xy)=O(y)
. However,
x^{-1-p}xy=x^{-p}y\in O(x^{-1})O(xy)
. As
a>1
,
x^{-p}y\neq y
. However,
x^{-p}y\in O(x^{-1})O(xy)=O(y)=\{y\}
, a contradiction.

Subcase 1.3:
H|_{K}=\Aut(K)
. As
H|_{K}=\Aut(K)
and
K\text{ char }G
, we have
O(x)
is the orbit of
x
in
K
under
\Aut(K)
. Thus,
O(x)=\{x^{r}\colon\gcd(r,p)=1\}
. Notice
x,x^{-1}\in O(x)=O(x)^{*}
. We consider
O(x)O(xy)
. Since
O(x)\subseteq K
and
O(xy)\not\subseteq K
,
O(x)^{*}\neq O(xy)
. Since
T(G,\mathfrak{S})
is AC,
O(x)O(xy)=O(x^{2}y)
. However,
x^{-1}xy=y\in O(x)O(xy)
, so
y\in O(x^{2}y)
and
O(x^{2}y)=O(y)=\{y\}
. Hence,
x^{2}=e
. However, the order of
x
is
p^{a}
which is odd. We thus have a contradiction.

Case 2:
H|_{L}=\Aut(L)
. Since
L\text{ char }G
and
H|_{L}=\Aut(L)
, we have
O(y)=\{y,y^{2},\cdots,y^{q-1}\}
. Note
O(y)=O(y)^{*}\subseteq L
. Since
xy\not\in L
we have
O(y)^{*}\neq O(xy)
. Then as
T(G,\mathfrak{S})
is AC,
O(xy)O(y)=O(xy^{2})
. However,
xyy^{q-1}=x\in O(xy)O(y)
as well. Then
O(x)=O(xy)O(y)=O(xy^{2})
. Since
x\in K\text{ char }G
,
O(x)\subseteq K
, so
xy^{2}\in K
since
xy^{2}\in O(xy^{2})=O(x)
. Then
y^{2}\in K
, a contradiction.

From these cases, if
T(G,\mathfrak{S})
is AC, then
H|_{K}=\{id\}
and
H|_{L}=\{id\}
, so
H
is trivial. But then
\mathfrak{S}
is the full group ring and
T(G,\mathfrak{S})\cong M_{n}(\mathbb{C})
is AC.
∎

At this point we are ready to prove the result for non-prime power odd order cyclic groups.

Theorem 4.5
.

Let
G
be a finite cyclic group of non-prime power odd order. Let
1<H\leq\Aut(G)
and
\mathfrak{S}=\mathfrak{S}(G,H)
. Then
T(G,\mathfrak{S})
is not AC.

Proof.

We induct on the number
k
of prime divisors of
|G|
. If
k=2
,
|G|=pq
, for distinct odd primes
p
and
q
and by by Proposition
4.4
,
T(G,\mathfrak{S})
is not AC as
H\neq\{1\}
.

Assume for all odd non-prime power cyclic groups
G
whose order has at most
k
prime factors, and
1<H\leq\Aut(G)
that
T(G,\mathfrak{S})
is not AC. Now suppose
|G|=m
where
m
has
k+1
prime factors and let
1<H\leq\Aut(G)
. Let
U_{m}=\{g\in\mathcal{C}_{m}\colon\gcd(g,m)=1\}
. As
m
is not a prime power it has at least two distinct prime divisors. Then by Lemma
4.1
, there exists an
H-
orbit
C\subseteq G\setminus U_{m}
such that
|C|>1
. As
C
contains no units we have
\langle C\rangle\lneq G
. Let
K
be a maximal subgroup of
G
containing
\langle C\rangle
. Then
K
has index
q
for some odd prime
q
. Then
|K|
has
k
prime factors. Now either
|K|
is a prime power, or it is not.

Case 1:
Suppose
|K|=p^{k}
for some odd prime
p
. Then
|G|=|K|\cdot|G\colon K|=p^{k}q
, and by Proposition
4.4
,
T(G,\mathfrak{S})
is not AC as
H
is nontrivial.

Case 2:
Suppose
|K|
is a non-prime power and is odd. Then
K\text{ char }G
. We suppose
T(G,\mathfrak{S})
is AC. By Lemma
3.13
,
T(K,\mathfrak{S}|_{K})
is AC. Notice though that
C\subseteq K
, is an
H-
orbit in
G
. It is then an
H-
orbit in
K
as well since
K\text{ char }G
. Since
|C|>1
,
H|_{K}
must be nontrivial. Then by the inductive hypothesis,
T(K,\mathfrak{S}|_{K})
is not AC. This contradicts
T(K,\mathfrak{S}|_{K})
being AC.
∎

Theorem 4.6
.

Let
G
be a finite cyclic group of non-prime power order. Let
H\leq\Aut(G)
. Let
\mathfrak{S}=\mathfrak{S}(G,H)
. Then
T(G,\mathfrak{S})
is AC if and only if
H
is trivial.

Proof.

First assume that
H
is nontrivial. Then by Theorems
4.3
and
4.5
,
T(G,\mathfrak{S})
is not AC. If
H
is trivial, then
\mathfrak{S}
is the full group ring and
T(G,\mathfrak{S})\cong M_{|G|}(\mathbb{C})
is AC.
∎

5
AC Terwilliger Algebras over Cyclic Groups

In this section we consider AC Terwilliger algebras for any Schur ring over
\mathcal{C}_{n}
, and prove Theorem
1.1
.

Theorem 5.1

(Leung and Man,
[
7
]
)
.

Every Schur ring over a finite cyclic group is either a trivial Schur ring, orbit Schur ring, direct product Schur ring, or wedge product Schur ring.

The case of a trivial Schur ring is the same as a trivial association scheme and has already been considered in
[
4
,
12
]
. The case of orbit Schur rings is covered in Theorems
3.12
,
3.22
, and
4.6
. We next consider the direct product.

Theorem 5.2

(Theorem 3.1,
[
6
]
)
.

Let
\mathcal{A}=(X,R_{i})
and
\mathcal{B}=(Y,S_{j})
be association schemes. Let
x\in X
and
y\in Y
. Let
T_{x}(\mathcal{A})
be the Terwilliger algebra for
\mathcal{A}
with base point
x
and
T_{y}(\mathcal{B})
be the Terwilliger algebra for
\mathcal{B}
with base point
y
. Then

T_{(x,y)}(\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{B})\cong T_{x}(\mathcal{A})\otimes_{\mathbb{C}}T_{y}(\mathcal{B}).

Proposition 5.3
.

Let
\mathcal{A}=(X,R_{i})
and
\mathcal{B}=(Y,S_{j})
be association schemes. Let
x\in X
and
y\in Y
. Then
T_{(x,y)}(\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{B})
is AC if and only if
T_{x}(\mathcal{A})
and
T_{y}(\mathcal{B})
both only have the primary component.

Proof.

Let
V
be the primary component of
T_{x}(\mathcal{A})
and
U
be the primary component of
T_{y}(\mathcal{B})
.

If
T_{x}(\mathcal{A})=V
and
T_{y}(\mathcal{B})=U
, then by Theorem
5.2
,
T_{(x,y)}(\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{B})\cong T_{x}(\mathcal{A})\otimes T_{y}(\mathcal{B})\cong V\otimes U
, which is the primary component. Then
T_{(x,y)}(\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{B})
is AC.

Now suppose without loss of generality that
T(\mathcal{A})
has a non-primary component
W
. Note
T_{(x,y)}(\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{B})\cong T_{x}(\mathcal{A})\otimes_{\mathbb{C}}T_{y}(\mathcal{B})
and the Wedderburn decomposition of a tensor product of two algebras over
\mathbb{C}
, is the tensor product over
\mathbb{C}
of pairs of components from the two algebras, when using the direct product action. Then
W\otimes U
is a component of
T_{(x,y)}(\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{B})
and is not the primary component. Also
\dim(W\otimes U)=\dim(W)\times\dim(U)\geq\dim(U)>1
. Thus,
T_{(x,y)}(\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{B})
has a non-primary component of dimension greater than
1
, so it is not AC.
∎

The following result of Tanaka can now be used to finish the direct product case.

Lemma 5.4

(Lemma 8,
[
12
]
)
.

Let
\mathcal{A}=(\Omega,\{A_{i}\})
be a commutative association scheme. Let
W_{0}
be the primary
T_{x}-
module. Then

\mathbb{C}^{\Omega}=W_{0}
if and only if
\mathcal{A}
is the group association scheme of a finite abelian group.

Theorem 5.5
.

For Schur rings
\mathfrak{S}
and
\mathfrak{T}
over groups
G
and
H
respectively we have that
T(G\times H,\mathfrak{S}\times\mathfrak{T})
is AC if and only if both
T(G,\mathfrak{S})
and
T(H,\mathfrak{T})
are the group algebra.

Proof.

We note that the direct product of Schur rings corresponds to the direct product of the corresponding association schemes. As such Proposition
5.3
gives that for groups
G
and
H
with Schur rings
\mathfrak{S}
and
\mathfrak{T}
over them respectively, we have
T(G\times H,\mathfrak{S}\times\mathfrak{T})
is AC if and only if both
T(G,\mathfrak{S})
and
T(H,\mathfrak{T})
only have the primary component. Then Lemma
5.4
, implies
T(G,\mathfrak{S})
and
T(H,\mathfrak{T})
are the group algebra.
∎

We now define the wedge product of Schur rings. Let
1<K\leq H\leq G
such that
K\trianglelefteq G
. Let
\mathfrak{S}
be a Schur ring over
H
with
K
as an
\mathfrak{S}
-subgroup. Suppose
\pi:G\to G/K
is the natural quotient map. Then
\pi(\mathfrak{S})
is a Schur ring over
H/K
. Let
\mathfrak{T}
be a Schur ring over
G/K
with
H/K
a
\mathfrak{T}-
subgroup such that
\mathfrak{T}_{H/K}=\pi(\mathfrak{S})
. Then we define the
wedge product

\mathfrak{S}\wedge_{K}\mathfrak{T}
by the partition

\mathcal{D}(\mathfrak{S}\wedge_{K}\mathfrak{T})=\mathcal{D}(\mathfrak{S})\cup\{\pi^{-1}(D)\mid D\in\mathcal{D}(\mathfrak{T})\setminus\mathcal{D}(\mathfrak{T}_{H/K})\}.

Under these conditions,
\mathfrak{S}\wedge_{K}\mathfrak{T}
is a Schur ring over
G
(see
[
8
]
for details). Alternatively, a Schur ring
\mathfrak{S}
over
G
is a wedge product if and only if there exist nontrivial, proper
\mathfrak{S}
-subgroups
H,K\leq G
such that
K\leq H
,
K\trianglelefteq G
, and every
\mathfrak{S}
-class outside of
H
is a union of
K
-cosets.

We first consider when
T(G,\mathfrak{S}\wedge_{K}\mathfrak{T})
is AC. For the next several results we assume
1<K\leq H\leq G
with
K\trianglelefteq G
and that
\pi\colon G\rightarrow G/K
is the natural projection map. We have:

Proposition 5.6
.

Suppose
\mathfrak{S}
is a Schur ring over
H
with
\overline{K}\in\mathfrak{S}
,
\mathfrak{T}
is a Schur ring over
G/K
with
\overline{H/K}\in\mathfrak{T}
, and
\pi(\mathfrak{S})=\mathfrak{T}_{H/K}
. If
T(G,\mathfrak{S}\wedge_{K}\mathfrak{T})
is AC, then
T(H,\mathfrak{S})
and
T(G/K,\mathfrak{T})
are both AC.

Proof.

As
T(G,\mathfrak{S}\wedge_{K}\mathfrak{T})
is AC, we have by Theorem
2.2
for
C,D\in\mathcal{D}(\mathfrak{S}\wedge_{K}\mathfrak{T})
with
C\neq D^{*}
, that
CD=E
for some class
E
. In particular, as
\mathcal{D}(\mathfrak{S})\subseteq\mathcal{D}(\mathfrak{S}\wedge_{K}\mathfrak{T})
we have for
C,D\in\mathcal{D}(\mathfrak{S})
with
C\neq D^{*}
, that
CD=E
for some class
E
and
T(H,\mathfrak{S})
is AC.

Next we let
A,B\in\mathcal{D}(\mathfrak{T})
such that
A\neq B^{*}
. We consider four cases.

Case 1:
A,B\not\in\mathcal{D}(\mathfrak{T}_{H/K})
. Then
\pi^{-1}(A),\pi^{-1}(B)\in\mathcal{D}(\mathfrak{S}\wedge_{K}\mathfrak{T})
. If
\pi^{-1}(A)=\pi^{-1}(B)^{*}
, then given
g\in\pi^{-1}(A)
we have
g^{-1}\in\pi^{-1}(B)
. So
gK\in A
and
g^{-1}K\in B
, which implies
A=B^{*}
. As this is false,
\pi^{-1}(A)\neq\pi^{-1}(B)^{*}
, and
\pi^{-1}(A)\pi^{-1}(B)=C
for some class
C\in\mathcal{D}(\mathfrak{S}\wedge_{K}\mathfrak{T})
. If
C\subseteq H
, then
C\in\mathcal{D}(\mathfrak{S})
, so
\pi(C)\in\mathcal{D}(\mathfrak{T}_{H/K})
. Then
\pi(C)=\pi(\pi^{-1}(A)\pi^{-1}(B))=AB
. If
C\not\subseteq H
, then
C=\pi^{-1}(D)
for some class
D\in\mathcal{D}(\mathfrak{T})\setminus\mathcal{D}(\mathfrak{T}_{H/K})
. Thus, we have
\pi^{-1}(D)=\pi^{-1}(A)\pi^{-1}(B)
. So

D=\pi(\pi^{-1}(D))=\pi(\pi^{-1}(A)\pi^{-1}(B))=AB.

Hence, we see
AB
is a single class in this case.

Case 2:
A\in\mathcal{D}(\mathfrak{T}_{H/K})
and
B\not\in\mathcal{D}(\mathfrak{T}_{H/K})
. Then
A=\pi(C)
for some
C\in\mathcal{D}(\mathfrak{S})
and
\pi^{-1}(B)\in\mathcal{D}(\mathfrak{S}\wedge_{K}\mathfrak{T})
. Since
C^{*}\subseteq H
as
C\subseteq H
we have by Theorem
2.2
,
C\pi^{-1}(B)=D
for some class
D\in\mathcal{D}(\mathfrak{S}\wedge_{K}\mathfrak{T})
. If
D\subseteq H
, then
\pi(D)\in\mathcal{D}(\mathfrak{T}_{H/K})
and
\pi(D)=\pi(C\pi^{-1}(B))=\pi(C)B=AB
. If
D\not\subseteq H
, then
D=\pi^{-1}(E)
for some class
E\in\mathcal{D}(\mathfrak{T})\setminus\mathcal{D}(\mathfrak{T}_{H/K})
. We have

E=\pi(\pi^{-1}(E))=\pi(D)=\pi(C\pi^{-1}(B))=\pi(C)B=AB.

Hence,
AB
is a single class in this case.

Case 3:
A\not\in\mathcal{D}(\mathfrak{T}_{H/K})
and
B\in\mathcal{D}(\mathfrak{T}_{H/K})
. The proof is similar to Case 2.

Case 4:
A,B\in\mathcal{D}(\mathfrak{T}_{H/K})
. Then
A=\pi(C)
and
B=\pi(D)
for
C,D\in\mathcal{D}(\mathfrak{S})
. Suppose
C=D^{*}
. Then for
g\in C
we have
g^{-1}\in D
and
gK\in A
,
g^{-1}K\in B
. This contradicts
A\neq B^{*}
. Hence,
C\neq D^{*}
and by Theorem
2.2
,
CD=E
for some class
E\in\mathcal{D}(\mathfrak{S}\wedge_{K}\mathfrak{T})
. Clearly
E\in\mathcal{D}(\mathfrak{S})
, so
\pi(E)\in\mathcal{D}(\mathfrak{T}_{H/K})
. We see
AB=\pi(C)\pi(D)=\pi(CD)=\pi(E)
. Hence,
AB
is a single class in this case.

Thus, in all cases
AB=C
for a single class
C\in\mathcal{D}(\mathfrak{T})
and by Theorem
2.2
,
T(G/K,\mathfrak{T})
is AC.
∎

In the following example we show that the converse of Proposition
5.6
is false.

Example 5.7
.

Let
G=\mathcal{C}_{12}=\langle z\rangle
,

, and

. We take

to be the group ring on

and

to be the group ring on

we see that

Proposition 5.8
.

Suppose
\mathfrak{S}
is a Schur ring over
H
with
\overline{K}\in\mathfrak{S}
,
\mathfrak{T}
is a Schur ring over

with
\overline{H/K}\in\mathfrak{T}
, and
\pi(\mathfrak{S})=\mathfrak{T}_{H/K}
. If
T(G,\mathfrak{S}\wedge_{K}\mathfrak{T})
is AC, then for all classes
C,D\in\mathcal{D}(\mathfrak{T})\setminus\mathcal{D}(\mathfrak{T}_{H/K})
, with
C\neq D^{*}
such that
CD\in\mathcal{D}(\mathfrak{T}_{H/K})
, we have
\pi^{-1}(CD)\in\mathcal{D}(\mathfrak{S}\wedge_{K}\mathfrak{T})
.

Proof.

As
C,D\in\mathcal{D}(\mathfrak{T})\setminus\mathcal{D}(\mathfrak{T}_{H/K})
we have
\pi^{-1}(C),\pi^{-1}(D)\in\mathcal{D}(\mathfrak{S}\wedge_{K}\mathfrak{T})
, say
A=\pi^{-1}(C)
and
B=\pi^{-1}(D)
. Notice that

\pi(AB)=\pi(A)\pi(B)=CD\subseteq H/K,

so
AB\subseteq H
. Suppose
A=B^{*}
. Then for
a\in A
we have
a^{-1}\in B
. So
\pi(a)\in C
,
\pi(a^{-1})\in D
, and
C=D^{*}
a contradiction. Thus,
A\neq B^{*}
. As
T(G,\mathfrak{S}\wedge_{K}\mathfrak{T})
is AC we have
AB=E
for some
E\in\mathcal{D}(\mathfrak{S})\subseteq\mathcal{D}(\mathfrak{S}\wedge_{K}\mathfrak{T})
.

We know
\pi(\mathfrak{S})=\mathfrak{T}_{H/K}
. If
E\in\mathcal{D}(\mathfrak{S})
is the only class such that
\pi(E)=CD
, then
\pi^{-1}(CD)=E
and we are done. So assume
F\in\mathcal{D}(\mathfrak{S})
with
E\neq F
such that
\pi(F)=CD
. By Proposition
5.6
,
T(H,\mathfrak{S})
and
T(G/K,\mathfrak{T})
are both AC, so as
E\neq F
,
EF^{*}=U
for some class
U\in\mathcal{D}(\mathfrak{S})
. Also
CD=V
for some class
V\in\mathcal{D}(\mathfrak{T})
since
C\neq D^{*}
. Observe that

\pi(U)=\pi(EF^{*})=\pi(E)\pi(F)^{*}=VV^{*}.

We know
U\in\mathcal{D}(\mathfrak{S})
, so
\pi(U)
is a class in
\mathfrak{T}_{H/K}
. Clearly
\{K\}\in VV^{*}
and we know
\{K\}
is a class in
\mathfrak{T}_{H/K}
. Hence,
\pi(U)=\{K\}
and
VV^{*}=\{K\}
, the identity class in
\mathfrak{T}
. This is only possible if
V
is a singleton, say
V=\{hK\}
with
h\in H
. Thus,
CD=\{hK\}
and
C
and
D
must be singletons as well, say
C=\{aK\}
and
D=\{bK\}
. Since
CD=V=\{hK\}
, we can write
D=\{a^{-1}hK\}
.

Observe that
A=\pi^{-1}(C)=aK
and
B=\pi^{-1}(D)=a^{-1}hK
. Then
E=AB=hK
. However,

F\subseteq\pi^{-1}(\pi(F))=\pi^{-1}(CD)=\pi^{-1}(\{hK\})=hK=E.

Thus
F\subseteq E
, which contradicts
E,F\in\mathcal{D}(\mathfrak{S})
with
E\neq F
, so
\pi^{-1}(CD)=E\in\mathcal{D}(\mathfrak{S}\wedge_{K}\mathfrak{T})
.
∎

Now we consider the case when
T(H,\mathfrak{S})
and
T(G/K,\mathfrak{T})
are both AC.

Proposition 5.9
.

Suppose
\mathfrak{S}
is a Schur ring over
H
with
\overline{K}\in\mathfrak{S}
,
\mathfrak{T}
is a Schur ring over
G/K
with
\overline{H/K}\in\mathfrak{T}
, and
\pi(\mathfrak{S})=\mathfrak{T}_{H/K}
. If
T(H,\mathfrak{S})
and
T(G/K,\mathfrak{T})
are AC and for all
C,D\in\mathcal{D}(\mathfrak{T})\setminus\mathcal{D}(\mathfrak{T}_{H/K})
with
C\neq D^{*}
, such that
CD\in\mathcal{D}(\mathfrak{T}_{H/K})
we have
\pi^{-1}(CD)\in\mathcal{D}(\mathfrak{S}\wedge_{K}\mathfrak{T})
, then
T(G,\mathfrak{S}\wedge_{K}T)
is AC.

Proof.

Let
A,B\in\mathcal{D}(\mathfrak{S}\wedge_{K}T)
such that
A\neq B^{*}
. We consider three cases.

Case 1:
A,B\in\mathcal{D}(\mathfrak{S})
. Then
AB=C
for some
C\in\mathcal{D}(\mathfrak{S})\subseteq\mathcal{D}(\mathfrak{S}\wedge_{K}\mathfrak{T})
as
T(H,\mathfrak{S})
is AC.

Case 2:
A\in\mathcal{D}(\mathfrak{S})
and
B=\pi^{-1}(C)
for some
C\in\mathcal{D}(\mathfrak{T})\setminus\mathcal{D}(\mathfrak{T}_{H/K})
. As
\pi(\mathfrak{S})=\mathfrak{T}_{H/K}
, there exists some
D\in\mathcal{D}(\mathfrak{T}_{H/K})
such that
\pi(A)=D
. Since
D\in\mathcal{D}(\mathfrak{T}_{H/K})
,
D^{*}\in\mathcal{D}(\mathfrak{T}_{H/K})
and
D^{*}\neq C
. Then
DC=E
for some class
E\in\mathcal{D}(\mathfrak{T})
as
T(G/K,\mathfrak{T})
is AC. As
C\not\in\mathcal{D}(\mathfrak{T}_{H/K})
, there is some
gK\in C
such that
gK\not\in H/K
. Notice if
E\in\mathcal{D}(\mathfrak{T}_{H/K})
, then for all
hK\in D
we have
hKgK\in H/K
, which implies
gK\in H/K
. This is false, so
E\in\mathcal{D}(\mathfrak{T})\setminus\mathcal{D}(\mathfrak{T}_{H/K})
. Thus,
\pi^{-1}(E)\in\mathcal{D}(\mathfrak{S}\wedge_{K}\mathfrak{T})
. As
A\subseteq\pi^{-1}(\pi(A))=\pi^{-1}(D)
, we have
AB\subseteq\pi^{-1}(D)\pi^{-1}(C)\subseteq\pi^{-1}(DC)=\pi^{-1}(E)
. Thus,
AB\subseteq\pi^{-1}(E)
. However,
AB
is a union of principal classes of
\mathfrak{S}\wedge_{K}\mathfrak{T}
. As it is contained inside the principal class
\pi^{-1}(E)
, we must have
AB=\pi^{-1}(E)
.

Case 3:
A=\pi^{-1}(C)
and
B=\pi^{-1}(D)
for
C,D\in\mathcal{D}(\mathfrak{T})\setminus\mathcal{D}(\mathfrak{T}_{H/K})
. If
C=D^{*}
, then for
gK\in C
we have
g^{-1}K\in D
. Then
g\in A
and
g^{-1}\in B
, so
A=B^{*}
. This is false, so
C\neq D^{*}
. Thus,
CD=E
for some class
E\in\mathcal{D}(\mathfrak{T})\setminus\{K\}
, since
T(G/K,\mathfrak{T})
is AC and
K\not\in CD
. If
E\in\mathcal{D}(\mathfrak{T})\setminus\mathcal{D}(\mathfrak{T}_{H/K})
then
\pi^{-1}(E)\in\mathcal{D}(\mathfrak{S}\wedge_{K}\mathfrak{T})
. If
E\in\mathcal{D}(\mathfrak{T}_{H/K})
then by hypothesis
\pi^{-1}(E)\in\mathcal{D}(\mathfrak{S}\wedge_{K}\mathfrak{T})
. So either way
\pi^{-1}(E)
is a principal class of
\mathfrak{S}\wedge_{K}\mathfrak{T}
. Observe
AB=\pi^{-1}(C)\pi^{-1}(D)\subseteq\pi^{-1}(CD)=\pi^{-1}(E)
.
As
AB
is a union of principal classes of
\mathfrak{S}\wedge_{K}\mathfrak{T}
and is contained inside the principal class
\pi^{-1}(E)
, we must have
AB=\pi^{-1}(E)
.

Thus, for all
A,B\in\mathcal{D}(\mathfrak{S}\wedge_{K}\mathfrak{T})
with
A\neq B^{*}
we have
AB=C
for some
C\in\mathcal{D}(\mathfrak{S}\wedge_{K}\mathfrak{T})
. So
T(G,\mathfrak{S}\wedge_{K}\mathfrak{T})
is AC by Theorem
2.2
.
∎

We now give an equivalent condition to
T(G,\mathfrak{S}\wedge_{K}\mathfrak{T})
being AC. Using it, one can determine when a wedge product of two Schur rings results in an AC Terwilliger algebra.

Theorem 5.10
.

Suppose
\mathfrak{S}
is a Schur ring over
H
with
\overline{K}\in\mathfrak{S}
,
\mathfrak{T}
is a Schur ring over
G/K
with
\overline{H/K}\in\mathfrak{T}
, and
\pi(\mathfrak{S})=\mathfrak{T}_{H/K}
. Then
T(G,\mathfrak{S}\wedge_{K}\mathfrak{T})
is AC if and only if

1.

T(H,\mathfrak{S})
and
T(G/K,\mathfrak{T})
are both AC;

2.

For all classes
C,D\in\mathcal{D}(\mathfrak{T})\setminus\mathcal{D}(\mathfrak{T}_{H/K})
, with
C\neq D^{*}
such that
CD\in\mathcal{D}(\mathfrak{T}_{H/K})
, we have
\pi^{-1}(CD)\in\mathcal{D}(\mathfrak{S}\wedge_{K}\mathfrak{T})
.

Proof.

If
T(G,\mathfrak{S}\wedge_{K}\mathfrak{T})
is AC then by Proposition
5.6
,
T(H,\mathfrak{S})
and
T(G/K,\mathfrak{T})
are AC. By Proposition
5.8
, we have for all classes
C,D\in\mathcal{D}(\mathfrak{T})\setminus\mathcal{D}(\mathfrak{T}_{H/K})
, with
C\neq D^{*}
such that
CD\in\mathcal{D}(\mathfrak{T}_{H/K})
, we have
\pi^{-1}(CD)\in\mathcal{D}(\mathfrak{S}\wedge_{K}\mathfrak{T})
.

Conversely if
T(H,\mathfrak{S})
and
T(G/K,\mathfrak{T})
are AC with the property that for all classes
C,D\in\mathcal{D}(\mathfrak{T})\setminus\mathcal{D}(\mathfrak{T}_{H/K})
, with
C\neq D^{*}
such that
CD\in\mathcal{D}(\mathfrak{T}_{H/K})
, we have
\pi^{-1}(CD)\in\mathcal{D}(\mathfrak{S}\wedge_{K}\mathfrak{T})
, then by Proposition
5.9
,
T(G,\mathfrak{S}\wedge_{K}\mathfrak{T})
is AC.
∎

Theorem
1.1
now follows from Theorems
3.12
,
3.22
,
4.6
,
5.5
,
5.10
.
