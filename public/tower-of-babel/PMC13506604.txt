Trends and projections of high body mass index-associated non-hodgkin lymphoma burden (1990–2040): A global burden of disease 2021 analysis
Authors: Yuhao Wang, Ying Li, Yang Li, Min Liu, Lingting Zhang, Xiaoyue Li, Lili Tang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractElevated body mass index (BMI) has been established as an independent risk factor for non-Hodgkin lymphoma (NHL), yet the global distribution and geographic heterogeneity of the attributable disease burden remain inadequately characterized. Drawing on data from the Global Burden of Disease study, this research examined temporal changes in disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) and deaths due to high-BMI-associated NHL from 1990 to 2021, quantified cross-national inequalities using the slope index of inequality (SII) and the concentration index (CI), and projected the burden through 2040 with an autoregressive integrated moving average model (ARIMA). The global age-standardized DALY rate (ASDR) increased from 3.45 per 100,000 in 1990 to 4.00 in 2021 and is projected to reach 4.63 by 2040; the age-standardized mortality rate (ASMR) rose from 0.13 to 0.15 per 100,000 and is expected to climb to 0.17 by 2040. Absolute inequality widened substantially, with the SII rising from 0.35 to 1.44, while the CI changed from −0.39 to −0.21, indicating a modest reduction in relative inequality but a persistent concentration of the burden in regions with a lower socio-demographic index. Both ASDR and ASMR were positively correlated with the socio-demographic index (SDI) across regions and countries. Introduction

Non-Hodgkin lymphoma (NHL) comprises a heterogeneous group of lymphoid tissue malignancies exhibiting diverse clinical behaviors and treatment responses, imposing substantial burdens on patients and society [1]. In 2020, non-Hodgkin lymphoma accounted for 544,000 incident cases and 260,000 deaths globally [2]; in 2022, it ranked among the top 10 most common cancers globally, with 553,010 new cases and 250,475 deaths, accounting for 2.8% of all new cancer cases and 2.6% of all cancer-related deaths worldwide [3]. Furthermore, NHL represents the sixth leading cause of cancer mortality in the United States [4]. The mean age at diagnosis is 67 years (57% of diagnoses are made in patients aged ≥ 65 years), contrasting with a mean age at death of 76 years (78.5% in this age stratum) [5]. Global mortality exhibits male predominance and significant geographic variation in sex-specific incidence, with greater proportions of men being diagnosed with the condition in resource-limited settings versus high-income countries [6]. Occupational exposures associated with elevated NHL risk include solvents (all types), hydrocarbon-based solvents, pesticides, meat processing, and ultraviolet radiation [7].

Obesity has reached epidemic proportions worldwide, with more than 1.9 billion overweight adults and approximately 650 million obese adults [8]. High body mass index (BMI), calculated from weight and height, provides a population-level estimate of adiposity across age groups and sexes [9]. Globally, an increased BMI is the third predominant modifiable cancer risk factor (after tobacco and infections), and the second predominant modifiable cancer risk factor in Western populations such as the United Kingdom [10]. In pediatric and adolescent NHL cohorts, both obesity and underweight status at diagnosis correlate with increased mortality risk [11]. Moreover, BMI increases and NHL incidence are positively associated [12], with risk escalating progressively beyond normal weight ranges—demonstrating a dose‒response relationship with increasing adiposity [13]. A meta-analysis of 16 studies confirmed that being overweight significantly increases NHL risk and correlates with reduced survival [14, 15]. Furthermore, obesity increases NHL incidence, whereas moderate physical activity demonstrates protective effects [16].

Given the paucity of global studies on the burden of NHL in the context of high BMI risk factors, our investigation utilized the most recent Global Burden of Disease (GBD) data for 2021 to provide a comprehensive assessment of this malignancy. Furthermore, through advanced statistical modeling—including autoregressive integrated moving average (ARIMA) and health inequality analysis—we project future epidemiological trajectories and identify priority areas for public health intervention.

Methods

Data sources

Data for this study were obtained from the estimated Global Burden of Disease (GBD) 2021 dataset, which is an exhaustive compilation of the global and regional burden of 369 diseases, injuries, and 88 risk factors for 204 countries and territories for the period 1990 to 2021. All anonymized data can be accessed and downloaded through the Global Health Data Exchange (GHDx) platform (http://ghdx.healthdata.org/gbd-results-tool) [17]. This dataset comprises global, regional, and national-level data on high BMI-associated NHL from 1990 to 2021.

Burden description

DALYs represent a summary measure of overall health loss, calculated by summing the years of life lost (YLL) and years lived with disability (YLD) for each specific cause. All estimates in the GBD study report 95% uncertainty intervals (UIs), calculated by drawing 1000 samples from the posterior distribution and using the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of the uncertainty distribution. Sociodemographic Index (SDI) data were utilized to quantify socioeconomic influences on disease burden throughout the analyses. Globally, countries and territories were categorized into either 5 SDI quintiles or 21 GBD regions [19–21].

Descriptive analysis

To comprehensively assess the global disease burden of high BMI-associated NHL, a descriptive analysis was conducted across five SDI regions, 21 GBD regions, and 204 countries and territories. Between 1990 and 2021, mortality and DALYs — presented as both case numbers and ASR — were compared globally and stratified by sex, SDI, and GBD region. Data from 204 countries and territories were visualized on global maps. The 2021 age-specific distributions of high BMI-associated NHL are depicted in a two-sided bar chart.

Trend analysis

The 95% confidence intervals (UIs) for the EAPC were also derived from this fitted model. The trends were interpreted based on the 95% UI; a lower limit of the 95% UI greater than 0 indicated an upward trend, whereas an upper limit of the 95% UI below 0 indicated a downward trend. Correlation analysis

Spearman’s rank correlation analysis—a nonparametric method—assesses the strength and directionality of monotonic dependencies between two ordinal or continuous variables. Quantified by the ρ coefficient, this approach characterizes increasing or decreasing relationships without assuming linearity. We employed this technique to examine associations between the SDI and ASDR as well as ASMR for high BMI-associated NHL.

Health inequality analysis

To analyze inequality, total DALYs and ASDR were extracted from the dataset. We employed the Lorenz curve and calculated both the Slope Index of Inequality (SII) and the Concentration Index (CI)—analytical tools used to assess income-related absolute and relative disparities across countries. The SII quantifies the absolute difference in predicted outcomes between the highest and lowest socioeconomic groups defined by the SDI. The CI, which ranges from − 1 to 1, was derived by fitting a Lorenz curve to cumulative DALYs and population data, reflecting variations in the burden of laryngeal cancer across nations. A CI of 0 indicates no health inequality, whereas positive or negative values signify a concentration of the disease burden in countries with high or low levels of financial development, respectively [24–26].

Predictive analysis

To project trends from 2022 to 2040, we used an ARIMA model that decomposed the effects of age, period, and cohort [27–29]. The autoregressive integrated moving average (ARIMA) model—a standard time-series forecasting approach—was applied to nonstationary data through differencing (I), autoregressive (AR), and moving average (MA) components to achieve stationarity. In additional to this, to avoid the well-documented risk of over-differencing inherent in automatic ARIMA selection, we performed a systematic pre-modelling diagnostic(Supplementary Materials 1).

Statistical analyses of mortality estimates

In the GBD 2021 study, the Cause of Death Ensemble Model (CODEm) was used to estimate mortality from high BMI-associated NHL. CODEm employs a set of statistical models and systematically tests combinations of covariates based on out-of-sample predictive validity. These results are then synthesized to estimate cause-specific mortality by region, age, sex, and year. CODEm conducted analyses by sex and separately for countries and regions with and without extensive complete vital registration data to mitigate the potential for uncertainty inflation due to high data heterogeneity. Multiple iterations of out-of-sample predictive validity were performed for each model, and the model with the smallest root-mean-square error was weighted to generate a pooled model for specific causes of death [20].

To account for misclassification, the GBD study applies established redistribution algorithms to reassign deaths from vague or non-specific ‘garbage codes’ to specific underlying causes [20, 30]. All estimates used in this analysis incorporate these corrections.

In this study, the exposure rate (P) is defined as the age-standardized summary exposure value (SEV) for high BMI from the Global Burden of Disease (GBD 2021) study. The SEV ranges from 0% to 100% and combines the proportion of the population exposed with the intensity of exposure; it serves as an equivalent measure of prevalence in PAF calculations. To account for the latency period of cancer, we applied a 10-year lag, using the 2011 SEV to estimate the attributable risk of NHL cases in 2021. Based on GBD 2021, the SEV for the study population was 17.40% (95% UI: 15.87%–18.93%).

The relative risk (RR) is derived from published high-quality meta-analyses: for every 5 kg/m² increase in BMI, the risk of NHL increases by 6% (RR = 1.06, 95% CI: 1.03–1.09) [31].

Data processing and visualization

All analyses were conducted using the World Health Organization’s Health Equity Assessment Toolkit and R statistical computing software (version 4.4.1) and RStudio.

Results

Global and SDI-regional burden trends (1990–2021)

Globally, the burden of high BMI-associated NHL remained relatively stable from 1990 to 2021. DALYs increased from 145,238 (95% UI: 49,395 to 245,905) to 343,580 (95% UI: 114,574 to 593,411). The ASDR increased from 3.45 (95% UI: 1.17 to 5.85) per 100,000 to 4.00 (95% UI: 1.33 to 6.90) per 100,000, with an EAPC of 1.68 (95% UI: -0.29 to 3.69). Simultaneously, deaths increased from 5,083 (95% UI: 1,724 to 8,609) to 12,966 (95% UI: 4,319 to 22,308), and the ASMR increased from 0.13 (95% UI: 0.04 to 0.22) per 100,000 to 0.15 (95% UI: 0.05 to 0.26) per 100,000, exhibiting an EAPC of 1.68 (95% UI: -0.33 to 3.74) (shown in Table 1).

ASR, Age-standardized rate; EAPC, Estimated annual percentage change; GBD, Global burden of disease

From 1990 to 2021, the burden of high BMI-associated NHL increased in four out of the five SDI regions; it did not increase in the high-SDI region. Among all five SDI regions, the high-SDI region consistently reported the greatest number of DALYs, increasing from 71,113 (95% UI: 23,533 to 121,447) in 1990 to 115,240 (95% UI: 38,428 to 200,774) in 2021. However, its ASDR decreased from 6.66 (95% UI: 2.21 to 11.38) per 100,000 to 6.07 (95% UI: 2.02 to 10.53) per 100,000, with an EAPC of 0.45 (95% UI: -0.45 to 1.35). Conversely, the low-SDI region had the lowest DALY count, increasing from 4,747 (95% UI: 1,677 to 8,040) to 17,336 (95% UI: 5,813 to 29,917), corresponding to an EAPC of 2.63 (95% UI: 0.88 to 4.52). Notably, the highest DALY EAPC occurred in the low-middle-SDI region at 3.56 (95% UI: 2.67 to 4.46). Regarding mortality, deaths in the low-SDI region increased from 140 (95% UI: 50 to 237) to 5,257 (95% UI: 1,748 to 9,161) over the study period. Although deaths also increased in the high-SDI region, the ASMR exhibited the largest decrease in this region, from 0.25 (95% UI: 0.08 to 0.43) per 100,000 to 0.15 (95% UI: 0.05 to 0.26) per 100,000, with an EAPC of -0.44 (95% UI: -1.39 to 0.50). The low-middle-SDI region had the highest ASMR EAPC at 3.59 (95% UI: 2.69 to 4.49) (shown in Table 1).

Among the 21 GBD regions, both DALYs and deaths due to high BMI-associated NHL generally increased between 1990 and 2021. Southern Sub-Saharan Africa exhibited the highest ASDR in 2021 at 8.68 (95% UI: 2.76 to 15.43). High-income North America recorded DALYs of 37,377 (95% UI: 12,180 to 64,931) in 1990 and 52,972 (95% UI: 17,395 to 90,864) in 2021; however, the ASDR in this region decreased from 11.22 (95% UI: 3.66 to 19.49) to 8.61 (95% UI: 2.83 to 14.72), with an EAPC of -0.30 (95% UI: -1.06 to 0.47). South Asia had the highest ASDR EAPC at 3.86 (95% UI: 3.23 to 4.50). Regarding mortality, high-income North America reported the highest death count in 2021 (2,393; 95% UI: 784 to 4,137) despite a negative EAPC of -0.29 (95% UI: -1.10 to 0.53). Conversely, South Asia had the lowest death counts in both 1990 and 2021, alongside the lowest ASMR, which increased from 0.03 (95% UI: 0.01 to 0.05) to 0.06 (95% UI: 0.02 to 0.11). Nevertheless, this region exhibited the highest ASMR EAPC at 3.89 (95% UI: 3.26–4.54) (shown in Table 1).

National-level burden heterogeneity

In 2021, substantial variation existed in the ASDR attributable to high BMI-associated NHL across 204 countries. Grenada exhibited the highest ASDR at 17.2 per 100,000 (95% UI: 5.49 to 29.67), followed by Libya (15.51; 95% UI: 4.92 to 29.16) and Tonga (14.47; 95% UI: 4.68 to 25.28). Conversely, Timor-Leste had the lowest ASDR at 0.87 per 100,000 (95% UI: 0.30 to 1.58), followed by Bangladesh (1.05; 95% UI: 0.34 to 1.99) and Mali (1.09; 95% UI: 0.34 to 1.94), reflecting considerable global disparities (shown in Fig. 1A). Significant heterogeneity was also observed in the EAPC. Cabo Verde presented the highest EAPC at 5.60 (95% UI: 5.11 to 6.09), followed by the United Arab Emirates (4.42; 95% UI: 3.80 to 5.05) and Zimbabwe (4.39; 95% UI: 3.65 to 5.12), indicating sustained growth. In contrast, Kazakhstan demonstrated the lowest EAPC at -0.41 (95% UI: -1.02 to 0.21), and Sweden (-0.022; 95% UI: -0.71 to 0.67) and Greenland (0.052; 95% UI: -0.51 to 0.62) also exhibited relatively stable trends (shown in Fig. 1C).

Globally and across 21 regions, high BMI-associated NHL exhibited significant positive correlations between the SDI and both ASDR and ASMR. The results demonstrate an increased burden of high BMI-associated NHL concomitant with an increasing SDI at the global, regional, and national levels (shown in Fig. 2).

Burden distribution by sex and age

The global burden of high BMI-associated NHL increased consistently for both sexes from 1990 to 2021. Among males, DALYs increased from 81,841 (95% UI: 27,936 to 136,531) to 191,427 (95% UI: 62,588 to 327,761), with the ASDR increasing from 4.04 (95% UI: 1.38 to 6.76) to 4.67 (95% UI: 1.53 to 8.01). Concurrently, deaths increased from 2,674 (95% UI: 911 to 4,484) to 6,917 (95% UI: 2,292 to 11,836), and the ASMR increased from 0.15 (95% UI: 0.05 to 0.25) to 0.18 (95% UI: 0.06 to 0.31) (shown in Fig. 3A). Projections to 2040

The ARIMA model demonstrates strong goodness-of-fit and was used to predict trends in the ASDR and ASMR for high BMI-associated NHL up to 2040. Globally and across five SDI regions, the ASDR is projected to continue increasing. Globally, the rate is projected to increase from 4.03 (95% UI: 3.98 to 4.08) in 2022 to 4.63 (95% UI: 2.28 to 6.97) by 2040. In the five SDI regions, the ASDR is projected to increase from 6.10 (95% UI: 5.97 to 6.24) to 6.74 (95% UI: 0.0618 to 13.42) in the high-SDI region; from 4.18 (95% UI: 4.11 to 4.25) to 4.71 (95% UI: 4.22 to 5.19) in the high-middle-SDI region; from 3.26 (95% UI: 3.23 to 3.29) to 4.00 (95% UI: 3.8 to 4.18) in middle-SDI regions; from 2.68 (95% UI: 2.67 to 2.70) to 3.41 (95% UI: 3.31 to 3.51) in low-middle-SDI regions; and from 2.68 (95% UI: 2.67 to 2.70) to 3.63 (95% UI: 2.85 to 4.41) in low-SDI regions (shown in Fig. 5A).

Population-attributable risk of high BMI-associated NHL

In 2021, the attributable fraction of NHL cases in this study population attributable to high BMI was 1.03% (95% UI: 0.47% to 1.68%). This result suggests that if the population’s high BMI levels were reduced to the theoretical lowest level of risk exposure, approximately 1.03% of NHL cases could be prevented.

Discussion

This study provides a comprehensive assessment of the global, regional, and national burden of high BMI-associated NHL from 1990 to 2021. Our findings indicate that the global burden remained relatively stable over the past three decades, with EAPCs of 1.68 (95% UI: -0.29 to 3.69) for DALYs and 1.68 (95% UI: -0.33 to 3.74) for deaths. Disparities were observed across SDI regions and countries, both the ASDR and ASMR demonstrated positive correlations with SDI. Absolute inequality in high BMI-associated NHL burden widened significantly globally, whereas relative inequality improved; however, low-SDI countries continued to bear a disproportionate burden. Projections suggest increases in the burden globally and across SDI regions by 2040, although the high-middle SDI region is expected to exhibit a decreasing ASMR trend. Although high BMI is an established NHL risk factor, research gaps persist in this domain [32, 33], potentially leading to underestimated public health impacts. These results may inform policy development, guide resource allocation and intervention planning, and improve epidemiological understanding of high BMI-associated NHL.

Our findings indicate a moderate positive correlation between both the ASDR and ASMR of high BMI-associated NHL and the SDI across 21 regions and 204 countries, suggesting an increasing burden with higher SDIs. Over the past three decades, although the high-SDI region remained relatively stable, the other four SDI regions exhibited increasing trends. First, a high body mass index itself is positively associated with the SDI [34]—ranking as the sixth leading risk factor at Level 3 [35]—and its attributable burden increases with SDI, potentially driving a corresponding increase in NHL burden. Second, the disproportionate increase in NHL burden in high-SDI countries may stem from socioeconomic development, which amplifies high BMI-associated NHL through lifestyle factors such as sedentary behavior and high-calorie diets [36]. Moreover, elevated BMI risk is more pronounced among affluent populations [37]. Among 204 countries, Monaco has the highest ASMR. This may be due to systematic biases in the GBD estimates of ASMR resulting from Monaco’s unique geographic and socioeconomic characteristics, as the model failed to adequately account for factors such as its exceptional wealth, concentration of healthcare resources, and the composition of its health-seeking migrant population. Furthermore, broader modeling assumptions often prove difficult to validate for regions with smaller populations and unique characteristics. When these assumptions fail, it can lead to the unfair diversion or misallocation of limited resources, thereby compromising the health of residents in other regions [38]. The lower burden in less developed regions may be attributable to a reduced likelihood of overnutrition due to constrained resources. Finally, inequitable distribution of health care resources may also contribute to increased disease burden. This pattern aligns with the dual burden of increasing obesity rates in affluent areas, underscoring high BMI as a universal risk transcending socioeconomic boundaries. Our findings underscore the need to develop comprehensive policies through stage-specific interventions: (1) Regions with high SDI scores must strengthen existing food environment and weight management regulations to prevent a reversal of progress; (2) The decline in ASMR in regions with lower SDI scores suggests that their policy experiences should be systematically summarized and disseminated to other regions; (3) Although regions with medium-high, high, and low SDI currently have a lower absolute burden, they exhibit a clear upward trend and are undergoing a nutritional transition. Proactive planning is required to prioritize obesity prevention and control on par with the management of infection-related lymphomas. This approach will address the threefold risk of infection, metabolic disorders, and aging, thereby achieving a comprehensive reduction in the overall burden.

We observed a progressive increase in the global burden of high BMI-associated NHL among both males and females from 1990 to 2021, with males consistently experiencing a greater burden than females. This disparity may be attributable to differences in behavioral factors and biological mechanisms. Key behavioral factors include the following: (1) Males are at greater risk for hematologic malignancies, potentially because of the persistent role of smoking as a leading risk factor for NHL-related mortality [39, 40]; (2) a high BMI is more prevalent among males than females [41]; (3) males are less likely to engage in routine health screenings, often resulting in diagnosis at advanced stages. For example, greater NHL mortality rates among males in rural versus urban areas of the United States are partially explained by limited health care access [42]. Regarding biological mechanisms a high BMI increases estrogen production in postmenopausal women [43, 44]. Estrogen administration reduced mitotic activity and B-cell precursor populations in the bone marrow in murine studies [45, 46]. We propose that elevated estrogen levels may suppress B-cell production under specific contexts or developmental stages. This mechanistic profile may contribute to the comparatively lower burden of high BMI-associated NHL observed in females versus males [47]. Furthermore, global DALYs and deaths from high BMI-associated NHL peaked in the 65–69 and 70–74 year age groups, respectively in 2021, followed by progressive decreases—a pattern likely associated with population aging dynamics. The absence of high BMI-associated NHL data for individuals under 20 years may reflect limited statistical association during data aggregation. However, the escalating global prevalence of high adolescent BMI—which is projected to increase further—warrants enhanced surveillance [48]. The absence of high BMI-associated NHL data for individuals under 20 years may reflect systematic underestimation of adolescent overweight and obesity prevalence when using BMI as the diagnostic metric, thereby compromising associated data capture [49]. Based on the above, we recommend the following: (1) Men with high BMI should be explicitly identified as a high-risk group for NHL in national cancer prevention and control plans or chronic disease management guidelines, and targeted screening strategies should be developed; (2) Health promotion campaigns tailored to men should be designed, such as leveraging non-traditional settings—including workplace health screenings, sporting events, and community pharmacies—to reach men who do not typically seek medical care; (3) Primary care facilities should be equipped with basic lymphoma screening capabilities.

A significant widening of absolute inequality and a concomitant improvement in relative inequality were observed in the burden of high BMI-associated NHL; however, low-SDI countries continued to bear a disproportionate burden. The improvement in relative inequality may be attributable to technological advances, such as the development and clinical application of effective therapies—including rituximab, a monoclonal antibody—which have improved NHL survival rates. Furthermore, the integration of the Internet of Things (IoT) into medical practice may have helped promote health education campaigns aimed at preventing NHL, thereby reducing the incidence and progression of the disease [50]. Scientific and clinical progress has contributed to a moderate alleviation of the NHL burden [51]. Moreover, risk stratification using the modified B-NHL-BFM-90 protocol has proven effective among Chinese children with anaplastic large cell lymphoma (ALCL) [52]. At the same time, PET-CT can accurately delineate tumor boundaries; therefore, radiation therapy guided by 18 F-FDG PET-CT may play a role in the treatment of non-Hodgkin lymphoma [53]. Previous studies on NHL have frequently reported higher ASDR and ASMR in low-SDI regions, likely due to constrained health care resources [2, 54]. This resource limitation may partly account for the pronounced absolute inequality. The unequal distribution of medical infrastructure has led to geographic disparities in the burden of high BMI-associated NHL. In high-SDI regions, technological and resource advantages have partially mitigated this burden, whereas in low-SDI regions, persistently high or increasing incidence rates—compounded by limited resources—have resulted in a continued increase in disease burden. We recommend implementing context-specific policy and environmental interventions, along with public health education and awareness campaigns. Thus, strengthening health systems in low-SDI countries through a proactive strategy that prioritizes both obesity epidemic prevention and rapid enhancement of cancer response capacity is essential.

Based on ARIMA modeling, our analysis projects persistent increases in the global burden of high BMI-associated NHL through 2040 across all five SDI quintiles except for high-middle SDI regions, which are expected to see a decrease in the ASMR. These differential trajectories underscore how SDI stratification reflects profound regional disparities in socioeconomic development, health care accessibility, and public health intervention efficacy that fundamentally shape NHL outcomes. The following factors may contribute to the decrease in the ASMR: (1) The implementation of sugar-sweetened beverage taxes, front-of-package nutrition labeling regulations, and community health initiatives may attenuate BMI-related cancer risk trajectories [55, 56]; (2) moreover, targeted policy interventions mitigate disease burden [57, 58]. These findings advocate for equity-oriented strategies to address burden disparities. We contend that targeted interventions in low-SDI regions are imperative to curb burden escalation(Examples: (1) Expand cost-effective obesity prevention programs; (2) Strengthen basic cancer diagnostic capabilities and referral pathways to prevent advanced-stage NHL; ), while maintaining public health policies in high-SDI settings may stabilize mortality trajectories.

This study presents the first global analysis of high BMI-associated NHL, characterizing temporal trends in the ASDR and ASMR. Along with elucidating the burden-SDI relationship, we project disease burden trajectories through 2040.

However, this study has several limitations. First, DALYs and mortality rates due to NHL may be underestimated in developing countries because of inadequate health care infrastructure and incomplete cancer reporting systems. Conversely, data from some countries, which are primarily derived from cancer registries in major urban centers, may not be representative of the wider population, and rates may therefore be overestimated. Second, although some countries report incidence data, no publicly available mortality data are available, which limits the ability to explore the relationship between DALYs and mortality.

Furthermore, as with all studies based on GBD data, this analysis is subject to limitations in case ascertainment. The classification of NHL in the GBD framework relies on various data sources and coding systems, which may not always align precisely with clinical diagnostic standards. This inconsistency could lead to potential underestimation or overestimation of NHL figures in certain regions or populations. Additionally, the disability weights on which YLD estimates rely may not be fully transferable across cultures, and due to the scarcity of severity data, they cannot capture cross-border differences in treatment accessibility. Second, DALY estimates for COVID-19 are subject to additional uncertainties: the quality of input data is highly heterogeneous, excess mortality models may yield divergent results, and case definitions for long COVID remain inconsistent. Finally, our calculation of the average age at death is not age-standardized; therefore, it is sensitive to population aging and regional population size, and its accuracy is limited by the granularity of existing GBD results.

Conclusion

This comprehensive analysis of the global, regional, and national burden of high-BMI-associated NHL from 1990 to 2021 highlights persistent burden escalation, evolving geographical patterns, and significant SDI associations. High-BMI-associated NHL remains a substantial public health challenge, necessitating prioritized interventions such as enhanced BMI control, optimized NHL therapeutics, regionally tailored prevention programs, the promotion of early diagnosis, and standardized treatment protocols to improve long-term health outcomes in affected populations worldwide.
