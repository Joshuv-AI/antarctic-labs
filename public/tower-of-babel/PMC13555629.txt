Injectable Zein‐Based Photothermal Composite Hydrogel Enhances Osteoporotic Tendon–Bone Healing
Authors: Kai Ye, Peng Ding, Zhenyou Dong, Wenyan Shi, Longxiang Shen, Xianyou Zheng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTOsteoporotic rotator cuff tears represent a major clinical challenge owing to impaired tendon–bone interface (TBI) healing. To address this issue, we engineered an injectable Zein‐based composite hydrogel (ZDC hydrogel) using electron‐beam irradiation and, incorporated niacin‐loaded mesoporous carbon nanospheres to achieve photothermal regulation and niacin release. The ZDC hydrogel exhibited favorable self‐assembly, mechanical properties, photothermal performance, degradability, and biosafety. In vitro, ZDC hydrogel extracts enhanced the osteogenic differentiation of MC3T3‐E1 cells and the tenogenic differentiation of tendon‐derived stem cells (TDSCs). Mechanistically, ZDC hydrogel extracts increased intracellular nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide levels, suppressed p16 and p21 expression, and reduced cytosolic and mitochondrial reactive oxygen species (ROS) accumulation in MC3T3‐E1 cells. In the osteoporotic rat rotator cuff repair model, ZDC hydrogels combined with near‐infrared irradiation (NIR) enhanced new bone formation within the humeral head bone tunnel, improved fibrocartilaginous TBI reconstruction, and strengthened biomechanical properties of the repaired tendon–bone complex. Furthermore, in a rat skin wound model, ZDC hydrogels combined with NIR increased the expression of vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) and CD31 while reducing tumor necrosis factor‐α levels, indicating enhanced angiogenesis and attenuated inflammatory responses. Collectively, these findings suggest that the injectable ZDC hydrogel is a promising strategy for osteoporotic rotator cuff tear repair.

Introduction

Osteoporotic rotator cuff tears are a leading cause of shoulder pain and functional impairment in the elderly, and their incidence continues to rise with the aging population [1]. Although arthroscopic rotator cuff repair has become the standard treatment, the high prevalence of osteoporosis in older adults substantially increases the risk of postoperative retears [2]. Osteoporosis compromises the mechanical support and biological environment at the tendon–bone insertion site, hindering the formation of a complete and stable tendon–bone interface (TBI) after repair, making osteoporotic tendon–bone healing (OTBH) a major clinical challenge [3, 4]. In clinical practice, anti‐osteoporosis medications such as denosumab and zoledronic acid, are commonly used to improve OTBH [5]. Although these medications can reduce the retear rate in patients with osteoporosis following repair [6, 7], they have not been shown to improve clinical outcomes or reduce the rates of revision surgery [8, 9]. Consequently, researchers have investigated additional therapeutic approaches for OTBH, including glutamine [10], abaloparatide [11] alpha‐ketoglutarate [12], and risedronate [13]. While these agents have demonstrated promising effects in promoting OTBH, their efficacy has thus far been limited to animal studies, highlighting the need for further clinical validation.

In recent years, a range of novel therapeutic strategies has been developed to address the unique physiological characteristics of osteoporotic TBI [14]. For example, poor vascularization and low cellular density at the TBI [15] limit the delivery of reparative cells and therapeutic agents. Xu et al. [16] developed microstructured scaffolds that mimic the native TBI and facilitate targeted drug delivery to promote OTBH. Given that the persistent chronic inflammation at the TBI can impair tendon–bone healing, Guo et al. [17] implemented strategies aimed at reducing excessive oxidative stress and inflammatory cell infiltration. Similarly, to address cellular senescence within the osteoporotic TBI, Song et al. [18] focused on remodeling the senescent microenvironment, which thereby alleviating stem cell senescence and, promoting OTBH. Although these studies offer novel therapeutic paradigms for OTBH, more effective and comprehensive strategies are still needed to address clinical challenges, including achieving controlled and sufficient drug release while simultaneously mitigating chronic inflammation and cellular senescence.

To achieve these goals, we designed a tendon–bone repair material composed of a Zein‐based hydrogel matrix, carbon nanospheres as drug carriers, and niacin as the therapeutic agent. Zein, a natural protein, possesses favorable biocompatibility and safety profiles. However, conventional Zein hydrogels exhibit markedly lower strength and stiffness than native tendons [19, 20]. To overcome this limitation, we employed electron‐beam irradiation to crosslink Zein and graft dopamine (DA), thereby enhancing its physical properties while preserving injectability and enabling in situ self‐assembly. Using this approach, we developed a Zein‐based, DA‐grafted, niacin‐loaded carbon nanosphere (ZDC) composite hydrogel. We hypothesized that the Zein hydrogel would provide mechanical support and controlled degradation [21], whereas carbon nanospheres would enable regulated drug release under near‐infrared radiation (NIR) [22, 23]. Niacin, recognized as one of the most effective and safest precursors of nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NAD+), is expected to attenuate excessive inflammation and cellular senescence while promoting osteogenic and tenogenic differentiation [24, 25, 26, 27, 28, 29].

We first evaluated the in vivo degradability and biocompatibility of the hydrogel, along with its in vitro photothermal properties and drug release behavior. We then assessed the effects of hydrogel extracts on the osteogenic and tenogenic differentiation of MC3T3‐E1 cells and rat tendon‐derived stem cells (TDSCs), as well as their effects on the intracellular NAD+, p16 and p21 expression, and reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels in MC3T3‐E1 cells. Finally, we combined the ZDC hydrogel with NIR irradiation in an osteoporotic rat rotator cuff repair model to investigate its effects on TBI healing. A rat skin wound model was subsequently used to validate the underlying pro‐angiogenic and anti‐inflammatory mechanisms. This study aimed to develop an injectable ZDC hydrogel that integrates mechanical support, photothermal regulation, and drug delivery as a therapeutic strategy for the repair of osteoporotic rotator cuff tears (Figure 1).

Schematic illustration of the Zein‐based, dopamine‐grafted, niacin‐loaded carbon nanosphere (ZDC) composite hydrogel for enhancing healing of osteoporotic rotator cuff tears. This electron beam‐modified composite hydrogel is designed for NIR‐responsive release of niacin, which alleviates inflammation, promotes angiogenesis, and facilitates the differentiation of osteogenic cells and TDSCs, thereby accelerating OTBH following a rotator cuff tear repair.

Methods

Synthesis of Electron Beam‐Irradiated Dopamine‐Grafted Zein (ZD) Hydrogel

Under the optimized preparation conditions, 100.0 mg of dopamine hydrochloride (Titan, China) was dissolved in 10.0 mL of 75% aqueous ethanol solution (Sinopharm, China), followed by the stepwise addition of 5.0 g of Zein (Titan, China) with thorough stirring until complete dissolution. The resulting mixture was sonicated for 10 min, transferred into irradiation sample bags, sealed under a nitrogen atmosphere, and subjected to electron beam irradiation at a dose of 30 kGy to obtain ZD. The irradiated samples were prefrozen at −20°C for 4 h and then lyophilized for further use.

To obtain the above optimized conditions, the electron beam‐induced graft modification process was systematically optimized in the following three aspects:

(1) Irradiation dose: A dose range of 0–60 kGy was set with an interval of 10 kGy; (2) Solvent system and irradiation atmosphere: Four combinations were designed, namely 75% aqueous ethanol under nitrogen (EtOH/N2), 90% aqueous acetic acid (Sinopharm, China) under nitrogen (HOAc/N2), deionized water under nitrogen (H2O/N2), and 75% aqueous ethanol under air (EtOH/Air); 3)Amount of grafting monomer: The added amounts of dopamine hydrochloride were 25.0, 50.0, 100.0, 200.0, and 400.0 mg, corresponding to concentrations of 2.5, 5.0, 10.0, 20.0, and 40.0 mg/mL, respectively. The sample with a concentration of 2.5 mg/mL was designated as DA2.5, and the others were named accordingly.

Characterization of ZD Hydrogel

The lyophilized gels were characterized by Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy using a Nicolet iS20 spectrometer (Thermo Scientific, USA) in the range of 400–4000 cm− 1. The Brunauer–Emmett–Teller (BET) specific surface area of the lyophilized gels was determined using an ASAP 2460 surface area and porosity analyzer (Micromeritics, USA). All measurements were performed in duplicate.

The tissue adhesive strength of the gels prepared under different conditions was evaluated by a shear adhesion test (MTS, China). As shown in Figure S1, two pork liver slices (3.5 × 2.5 × 0.5 cm) were first fixed onto glass slides using cyanoacrylate glue. Then, 500 µL of ZD precursor solution was applied between the two liver slices and allowed to form a hydrogel in situ for 10 min. The separation stress per unit area was used as an index of tissue adhesion strength. All measurements were performed in duplicate.

The swelling behavior of the hydrogels was evaluated as follows. Lyophilized gels with the same volume were weighed and recorded as M1. The samples were then immersed in deionized water for 24 h, removed, and gently blotted with filter paper to remove excess surface water before weighing, and the mass was recorded as M2. Two parallel samples were used for each group. The swelling ratio Sr (%) of the hydrogels was calculated according to the following equation: Sr = (M 2 − M 1)/M 1 × 100%.

Self‐assembly performance was assessed by loading the ZD precursor solution into a syringe and slowly injecting it into a vial to evaluate injectability. Excess phosphate‐buffered saline (PBS, Procell, China) buffer was then added, and the self‐assembly gelation process was observed.

Preparation of ZDC Hydrogel

First, mesoporous carbon nanospheres were prepared. A mixture of 0.1 mL ammonium hydroxide (NH4OH, 25% w/w, Titan, China), 7 mL absolute ethanol, 0.26 g cetyltrimethylammonium chloride (Titan, China), and 20 mL deionized water was stirred for 30 min. Then, 0.2 g resorcinol (Titan, China) was added and the mixture was stirred for another 30 min, followed by the addition of 0.36 mL tetraethyl orthosilicate (Titan, China) and 0.28 mL formaldehyde solution (Sinopharm, China), and the reaction was continued at 30°C for 24 h. The solid product was collected by centrifugation and dried at 60°C overnight. The dried product was then heated to 800°C under a nitrogen atmosphere at a heating rate of 1°C/min and maintained for 5 h. After cooling, the obtained product was mixed with 10% hydrofluoric acid solution (Sinopharm, China) and stirred for 12 h, then washed by centrifugation and collected to obtain mesoporous carbon nanospheres. Subsequently, drug‑loaded carbon spheres were prepared. Mesoporous carbon nanospheres (100 mg) were dispersed in 2 mL deionized water, followed by the addition of 100 mg niacin (Titan, China) and stirring for 24 h. The suspension was then centrifuged, washed, and dried, and the drug loading was determined gravimetrically. Finally, ZDC hydrogels were prepared. Drug‑loaded carbon spheres (100 mg) were mixed with 10 mL ZD precursor solution to obtain the ZDC precursor system. The resulting system was pre‑frozen at −20°C for 4 h and then lyophilized for further use.

Characterization of ZDC Hydrogel

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM, Zeiss Sigma 300, Germany) was used to observe the surface morphology of the hydrogel materials and mesoporous carbon nanospheres. Crystal phase analysis was performed using an x‐ray diffractometer (XRD, SmartLab, Rigaku, Japan) with a scan rate of 5°/min over a range of 10°–80°. FTIR spectra of the carbon nanospheres and niacin were recorded using a Nicolet iS20 spectrometer (Thermo Scientific, USA) in the range of 500–4000 cm−1. The shear stress–shear rate and viscosity–shear rate curves of the Zein, ZD, and ZDC precursor solutions were measured using an MCR92 rotational rheometer (Anton Paar, Austria).

Photothermal Performance Test

Aliquots (1 mL each) of 75% ethanol aqueous solution (ethanol aqueous), Zein solution (Zein), aqueous dispersion of carbon nanospheres (CNs, 1 wt%), and ZDC precursor solution (ZDC) were placed in centrifuge tubes. Each tube was irradiated using a near‐infrared laser (Changchun Optics, China) with a power density of 1 W/cm2 and a wavelength of 1064 nm. During irradiation, the temperature evolution of each sample was recorded every 1 min using a thermal imaging camera (FLIR Systems, USA).

In Vitro Drug Release Study

Niacin was prepared as standard solutions at concentrations of 20, 40, 60, 80, and 100 mg/L, and a calibration curve was established by High Performance Liquid Chromatography (HPLC, Agilent Technologies, China) based on the peak area. Subsequently, 2 mL of ZDC was added to an amber serum bottle containing 50 mL of PBS and incubated at 37°C under shaking. Samples were collected at 1, 3, 6, 12, 24, 48, 72, 96, and 120 h; at each time point, 2 mL of supernatant was withdrawn and replaced with an equal volume of fresh PBS. For the ZDC‐NIR group, samples were irradiated with a 1064 nm NIR laser for 2 min before each sampling. Each group was tested in duplicate. The collected samples were stored at 4°C, filtered, and analyzed by HPLC to determine niacin concentrations based on the calibration curve. The drug release rate Dr was calculated using: Dr = C 1 /C 0 × 100%, where C1 is the niacin concentration at each sampling time, and C0 is the theoretical concentration corresponding to complete release. The HPLC conditions were as follows: mobile phase A (water) and B (acetonitrile) at a ratio of 20:80 (v/v), isocratic elution, flow rate 1 mL/min, detection wavelength 254 nm, and column temperature 30°C.

In Vitro Degradation Study

Lyophilized hydrogels of equal volume (Zein, ZD, and ZDC) were placed in centrifuge tubes and immersed in 15 mL PBS containing alkaline protease (2.0 mg/mL, Beyotime, China), followed by incubation in a shaking incubator at 37°C and 150 rpm. The day of incubation was defined as day 0, and samples were collected on days 1, 2, 3, 4, and 5. At each time point, the samples were centrifuged, washed, lyophilized, and weighed; parallel samples were prepared for each group. Each group was tested in duplicate. The remaining weight Rw(%) was calculated as Rw = Wt /W 0 × 100%, where W0​ and Wt are the initial sample mass and the mass at day t, respectively.

In Vivo Degradation Study

Precursor solutions (300 µL) of Zein, ZD, and ZDC were added to culture dishes and mixed with excess PBS to form hydrogels, which were collected as test samples. Anesthetized SD rats received two ∼1 cm transverse incisions on each side of the dorsal midline, and subcutaneous pockets were bluntly created. Sterilized Zein, ZD, and ZDC hydrogels were implanted into three pockets, with one empty pocket as a blank control, followed by wound closure. Rats were given intramuscular penicillin (40 000 U, twice daily for 3 days, Beyotime, China), and their general condition and wound healing were monitored. The day of implantation was defined as day 0; on days 1–5, three rats were sacrificed daily, the four dorsal pockets were excised, and the implants were photographed and weighed to evaluate in vivo degradation.

Cell Isolation and Culture

MC3T3‐E1 cells were purchased from HyCyte Biotechnology Co. Ltd. (Suzhou, China). L929 cells were purchased from Procell Life Science & Technology Co., Ltd.‌ (Wuhan, China). The isolation of rat TDSCs was approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Shanghai Sixth People's Hospital (Approval No. 2023‐0306). TDSCs were isolated from Achilles tendons of 4‐week‐old Sprague–Dawley rats. Immediately after euthanasia, bilateral Achilles tendons were harvested, surrounding fascia was carefully removed, and the midportion of the tendons was collected. The tissue was immersed in 10% penicillin–streptomycin solution (Hyclone, USA) for 5 min and minced into small fragments, followed by digestion with type I collagenase (Beyotime, China) at a final concentration of 2 mg/mL in a 37°C shaking incubator for 4 h. The cell suspension was filtered through a 70 µm cell strainer (NEST, China) and centrifuged at 400 g for 5 min to remove the supernatant. The cell pellet was washed with PBS, resuspended in complete medium, and seeded into 6 cm culture dishes. Cells were cultured at 37°C in a humidified atmosphere of 5% CO2, washed twice with PBS to remove non‐adherent cells after 48 h, and the medium was changed every 3 days during the experiments. The resulting cell pellet was seeded in Petri dishes and expanded until reaching 80%–90% confluence, followed by passaging. TDSCs at passages 2–4 were used in this study. MC3T3‑E1 cells, L929 cells, and TDSCs were maintained in Minimum Essential Medium α (α‑MEM, Gibco, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco, USA) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin (Hyclone, USA).

Preparation of Hydrogel Extracts

Precursor solutions (500 µL) of Zein, ZD, and ZDC were added to 24‐well plates to form hydrogels, followed by immersion in 1 mL complete culture medium for 24 h. The hydrogels were then removed, and the supernatants were collected as hydrogel extracts.

Cell Proliferation and Cell Viability

The effects of Zein, ZD, and ZDC on cell proliferation were evaluated using CCK‐8 and live/dead staining assays. MC3T3‐E1 cells and TDSCs were seeded in 96‐well plates at 2 × 104 cells/well and cultured at 37°C in 5% CO2 until adherence, then treated with varying concentrations of Zein, ZD, or ZDC extracts for 48 h. Subsequently, 10% CCK‐8 solution (Dojindo, Japan) was added and incubated at 37°C for 2 h, and the absorbance was measured at 450 nm. Five parallel cell samples were used for each group (n = 5). L929 cells were similarly seeded at 2 × 104 cells/well, treated with extracts for 1, 3, and 5 days, and analyzed using the same CCK‐8 protocol. For live/dead staining, L929 cells were seeded in confocal dishes at 4 × 104 cells/well, treated with the extracts for 1, 3, and 5 days, incubated with pre‐diluted calcein/PI (Beyotime, China) at 37°C for 30 min, and then imaged by CLSM. Three parallel cell samples were used for each group at each time point (n = 3).

Osteogenic Induction and Evaluation

MC3T3‐E1 cells were seeded into 96‐well plates at a density of 2 × 104 cells/well and cultured at 37 °C with 5% CO2 until reaching approximately 80% confluence. Cells were then incubated either with commercial osteogenic induction medium (OriCell, China) or osteogenic medium supplemented with Zein, ZD, or ZDC extracts. The medium was refreshed every 3 days. After 7 days of induction, alkaline phosphatase (ALP) staining was performed using the BCIP/NBT Alkaline Phosphatase Color Development Kit (Beyotime, China) according to the manufacturer's instructions. After 14 days, Alizarin Red S (ARS) staining was conducted using Alizarin Red S Solution (OriCell, China). Stained cultures were imaged with a digital camera and an optical microscope (Leica, Germany). For semi‑quantitative analysis, images were converted to 8‑bit format in ImageJ software, and the mean gray value was measured, with higher gray values indicating increased ALP activity or calcium nodule formation. In both ALP and ARS staining, three parallel samples were used for each group (n = 3).

For immunofluorescence, glass coverslips (NEST, China) were placed in 24‑well plates, and MC3T3‑E1 cells were seeded at 5 × 104 cells/well. Osteogenic induction was performed as described above. After 7 days, the expression of osteogenesis‑related proteins (ALP, COL1, OCN) was examined. Cells were washed three times with PBS, fixed with 4% PFA (Servicebio, China) for 15 min, and blocked with 3% BSA (Beyotime, China) for 15 min. After three PBS washes, cells were permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X‑100 (Beyotime, China) for 15 min and washed again. Primary antibodies against ALP, COL1, and OCN were applied and incubated overnight at 4 °C. After washing, cells were incubated with the appropriate secondary antibody (Goat Anti‑Rabbit IgG H&L, Alexa Fluor 555, Abcam, UK). Phalloidin (Beyotime, China) and DAPI (Beyotime, China) were then applied for 30 min and 5 min, respectively. Coverslips were collected, gently dried, mounted with antifade mounting medium (Beyotime, China), and placed on glass slides. Fluorescence images were acquired using a DMI8 microscope (Leica, Germany). Fluorescence intensity was analyzed using ImageJ software. Details of the primary antibodies are listed in Table S1. Three parallel cell samples were used for each group (n = 3).

For RT‑qPCR, MC3T3‑E1 cells were seeded in 24‑well plates at 5×104 cells/well and induced osteogenically as described above. After 14 days of induction, total RNA was isolated using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, USA) and reverse‑transcribed into cDNA with the PrimeScript RT kit containing gDNA Eraser (TaKaRa, China). Quantitative PCR was carried out on a QuantStudio Real‑Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems, USA) according to the manufacturer's instructions. The mRNA expression of Runx2, Alpl, Col1a1, Spp1, and Bglap was analyzed. Relative gene expression was calculated using the 2−ΔΔCT method and normalized to GAPDH. Three parallel cell samples were used for each group (n = 3). Primer sequences are provided in Table S2.

Tenogenic Induction and Evaluation

Glass coverslips (NEST, China) were placed in 24‑well plates, and TDSCs were seeded at a density of 5×104 cells/well and cultured at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere with 5% CO2. Cells were then exposed either to tenogenic induction medium or to tenogenic medium supplemented with Zein, ZD, or ZDC extracts. The medium was replaced every 3 days. The tenogenic induction medium consisted of low‑glucose Dulbecco's modified Eagle's medium (L‐DMEM, Gibco, USA) supplemented with 10% FBS (Gibco, USA), 1% penicillin–streptomycin (Hyclone, USA), 25 µmol/L ascorbic acid (Beyotime, China), and 25 ng/mL connective tissue growth factor (MedChemExpress, China). After 7 days of induction, the expression of tenogenic markers (scleraxis [SCX], COL1, and tenomodulin [TNMD]) was examined by immunofluorescence as described above. Three parallel cell samples were used for each group (n = 3). Details of the primary antibodies are provided in Table S1.

For RT‑qPCR, TDSCs were seeded in 24‑well plates at a density of 5 × 104 cells/well and subjected to tenogenic induction as described above. After 14 days, RT‑qPCR was performed and analyzed as previously detailed. The mRNA levels of Scx, Col1a1, and Tnmd were analyzed. Three parallel cell samples were used for each group (n = 3). Primer sequences are listed in Table S2.

Intracellular NAD+ Detection

MC3T3‐E1 cells were seeded in 6‐well plates and cultured until they reached approximately 70% confluence. The control group was treated with PBS, while the experimental groups were administered Zein, ZD, or ZDC extracts. After 24 h of incubation at 37°C, cell samples were collected, and NAD+ levels were measured using the NAD+/NADH Assay Kit with WST‐8 (Beyotime, China) according to the manufacturer's instructions. The results were presented as the NAD+/NADH ratio. Three parallel cell samples were used for each group (n = 3).

ROS Detection

MC3T3‐E1 cells were seeded in 96‐well plates and cultured until reaching approximately 70% confluence. The control group received PBS, while the experimental groups were treated with Zein, ZD, or ZDC extracts. After 24 h of incubation at 37°C, the cells were washed with PBS. For ROS detection, cells were incubated with DCFH‐DA (Beyotime, China) at a final dilution of 1:1000 for 20 min in the dark. For mitochondrial ROS detection, cells were treated with MitoSOX Red (MedChemExpress, China) at a final concentration of 5 µM for 30 min in the dark. After all incubations, the cells were washed with PBS and stained with Hoechst 33258 (Beyotime, China) for 10 min. Finally, the cells were washed twice with PBS and observed using a fluorescence microscope (Leica, Germany). Three parallel cell samples were used for each group (n = 3).

Quantitative PCR Analysis of Senescence‐Related Genes

MC3T3‐E1 cells were seeded into 24‐well plates at a density of 5 × 104 cells/well and cultured at 37 °C with 5% CO2 until reaching approximately 70% confluence. The control group was treated with PBS, while the experimental groups were administered Zein, ZD, or ZDC extracts. After 24 h of incubation, the cells were collected, and the mRNA levels of p16 and p21 were analyzed. Four parallel cell samples were used for each group (n = 4). Primer sequences are listed in Table S2.

Osteoporotic Rotator Cuff Repair Model in Rats

All animal procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Shanghai Sixth People's Hospital (Approval No. 2023‐0306). Twenty‐eight female Sprague–Dawley rats (250 ± 20 g) were used, and osteoporosis was induced by bilateral ovariectomy. Briefly, rats were anesthetized with 0.5% pentobarbital sodium via intraperitoneal injection, the lateral abdominal hair was shaved, and a ∼1 cm abdominal incision was made to access the peritoneal cavity and remove both ovaries. Rats were maintained for 12 weeks post‐ovariectomy to allow the development of stable osteoporosis. After 12 weeks, the 28 osteoporotic rats were randomly assigned to four groups (n = 7 per group): Control (direct suture), ZD (200 µL, once weekly), ZDC (200 µL, once weekly), and ZDC‐NIR (200 µL, once weekly plus daily 1064 nm NIR of the repair site for 2 min at a power density of 1 W/cm2). An acute rotator cuff repair model was established as previously described. Rats were anesthetized with 0.5% pentobarbital sodium intraperitoneally, and the supraspinatus tendon was transected bilaterally at its insertion on the greater tuberosity of the humerus. A bone tunnel was created in the humerus using a 0.5 mm drill, and the supraspinatus tendon was passed through the tunnel and reattached to its original footprint with 4‐0 sutures (Jinhuan Medical, China). After suturing, the corresponding hydrogels were applied to cover the TBI, followed by closure of the deltoid muscle and skin. Postoperatively, rats received intramuscular penicillin (40 000 U, once daily for 3 days, Beyotime, China). Thereafter, the respective formulations were supplemented by injection into the tendon defect using a syringe at the same dose regimen. At 8 weeks postoperatively, rats were euthanized by intraperitoneal injection of an overdose of pentobarbital sodium, and the repair sites were evaluated by Micro‐CT, histology, immunohistochemistry, and biomechanical testing.

Dorsal Skin Wound Model in Rats

All animal procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Shanghai Sixth People's Hospital (Approval No. 2023‐0306). Thirty‐six female Sprague–Dawley rats (250 ± 20 g) were used to establish a dorsal full‐thickness skin wound model. Briefly, rats were anesthetized with 0.5% pentobarbital sodium by intraperitoneal injection. The dorsal area was shaved, and residual hair was removed using a depilatory cream, followed by disinfection of the surgical area with iodophor. Two full‐thickness circular excisional wounds (8 mm in diameter) were symmetrically created on both sides of the dorsal midline using a sterile biopsy punch. The distance between the two wounds was maintained at approximately 1.5–2 cm to minimize interference between the wounds. After surgery, rats received intramuscular penicillin (40 000 U, once daily for 3 days; Beyotime, China). The rats were then randomly assigned to four groups (n = 9 per group): Control (50 µL PBS), ZD (50 µL ZD), ZDC (50 µL ZDC), and ZDC‐NIR (50 µL ZDC plus daily 1064 nm NIR irradiation of the wound site for 2 min at a power density of 1 W/cm2). Treatment was initiated immediately after wound creation, and both wounds in each rat received the same treatment. At 3, 7, and 14 days after wound creation, three rats from each group were euthanized by intraperitoneal injection of an overdose of pentobarbital sodium, and wound tissues were harvested for subsequent immunohistochemical and immunofluorescence analyses.

Micro‐Computed Tomography (Micro‐CT) Analysis

The supraspinatus–humerus complexes were imaged using a Micro‐CT scanner (SkyScan 1176, Bruker, Germany) with a voxel size of 18 µm, tube voltage of 90 kV, and tube current of 278 µA. The acquired datasets were processed and evaluated with DataViewer and CTAn software (version 1.13, CT Analyser). The region of interest was defined as the area between the supraspinatus tendon footprint on the humeral head and the adjacent growth plate.

Histological, Immunohistochemical, and Immunofluorescence Analyses

The humerus–supraspinatus complexes and were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (Servicebio, China) for 48 h, decalcified, dehydrated through graded ethanol, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned at 5 µm for histological, immunohistochemical analyses. For histology, sections were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) and safranin O‐fast green. For immunohistochemistry, deparaffinized and rehydrated sections underwent antigen retrieval in 0.01 M sodium citrate buffer (pH 6.0), followed by blocking of endogenous peroxidase with 3% H2O2 and nonspecific binding with 5% BSA (Servicebio, China). Sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against Collagen II, then with a biotinylated secondary antibody (Servicebio, China) and HRP‐conjugated streptavidin (Servicebio, China). Signals were developed using DAB (Servicebio, China), nuclei were counterstained with hematoxylin (Servicebio, China), and sections were dehydrated, cleared, and mounted.

The skin wound tissues and were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (Servicebio, China) for 48 h, dehydrated, embedded, and sectioned for immunohistochemical and immunofluorescence analyses. Immunohistochemical staining of skin sections was performed as described above, except that an anti‐vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) antibody was used as the primary antibody. For immunofluorescence, antigen retrieval was performed in citrate buffer by microwave heating, followed by washing in PBS containing 0.1% Tween‐20 (Servicebio, China). After blocking with 5% BSA (Servicebio, China) for 30 min at room temperature, sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against CD31 or tumor necrosis factor‐α (TNF‐α). After PBS washing, sections were incubated with fluorescence‐conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h, counterstained with DAPI (Servicebio, China), washed, and mounted with an anti‐fade medium (Servicebio, China). Images were captured with a digital slide scanner (Pannoramic MIDI; 3DHISTECH Ltd, Hungary). Information on the primary antibodies are provided in Table S1.

Biomechanical Evaluation

Humerus–supraspinatus constructs were preserved at −80 °C and brought to room temperature prior to mechanical assessment. The mid‐substance cross‐sectional area of the supraspinatus tendon was determined with a digital caliper. Uniaxial tensile testing of the tendon–bone unit was conducted using a micro mechanical testing device (HSS‐DX1000, Jinan Hengruijin Testing Machine Co. Ltd., China). Each specimen was initially subjected to a 0.1 N preload and subsequently elongated to structural failure at a displacement rate of 10 mm/min. Stiffness was obtained from the force–displacement relationship, stress was derived by dividing the peak force by the measured cross‐sectional area, and the ultimate load to failure was documented. Seven specimens were used for each group (n = 7).

Statistical Analysis

All experimental data were statistically analyzed using GraphPad Prism 10, and the results are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (mean ± SD). Differences among multiple groups were evaluated by one‐way analysis of variance (one‐way ANOVA). Normality of the data was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test, and data were found to be normally distributed (p > 0.05). Post hoc analyses were performed using Tukey's HSD to compare means between multiple groups, with a significance level adjusted using the Bonferroni correction. In the immunofluorescence experiments, normalization was performed by dividing all experimental data points by the average value of the control group. Statistical significance was indicated as follows: “*” for P < 0.05, “**” for P < 0.01, “***” for P < 0.001, and “****” for P < 0.0001. The experiments were randomized, and during experiments and outcome evaluation, the investigators were blinded to assignment.

Results

Characterization of ZD Hydrogels

We first employed FTIR spectroscopy to investigate the changes in the functional group structures of Zein under different irradiation doses. Native Zein exhibited characteristic N–H and C–H stretching bands at 3297 and 2945 cm− 1, respectively, as well as amide I and II bands at 1636 and 1526 cm− 1, respectively (Figure 2A). As the irradiation dose increased from 0 to 40 kGy, a slight increase in the amide I/II ratio was observed, indicating mild alterations in the protein secondary structure. At 50 and 60 kGy, the N–H and C–H stretching bands became markedly more intense, suggesting protein denaturation and chain scission at higher irradiation doses. At a fixed dose of 30 kGy, Zein samples irradiated in different solvent systems and atmospheres exhibited distinct FTIR profiles (Figure 2B). Zein in EtOH/N2 showed only minor changes, whereas samples in EtOH/Air, HOAc/N2, and H2O/N2 demonstrated enhanced N–H and C–H stretching bands, similar to those observed at irradiation doses ≥50 kGy, indicating superior preservation of the protein structure in EtOH/N2. Under the optimized condition (30 kGy, EtOH/N2), FTIR spectra of Zein modified with increasing DA concentrations were obtained (Figure 2C). At DA concentrations ≤ 10.0 mg/mL, the amide I and II bands were enhanced without obvious changes in the N–H stretching band, and a new phenolic C–O band appeared at 1284 cm− 1, confirming successful grafting of DA onto Zein. When the DA concentration was increased to 20.0 and 40.0 mg/mL, a pronounced increase in the N–H stretching band was observed, indicating the presence of excess unreacted amino groups and suggesting DA overloading.

To further determine the optimal electron beam irradiation conditions, the properties of the modified Zein hydrogels prepared under different conditions were evaluated using tissue adhesion strength, BET surface area, and swelling ratio measurements. Tissue adhesion testing showed that the adhesiveness of Zein hydrogels remained unchanged at irradiation doses below 30 kGy and then gradually decreased with further dose increases. Zein hydrogels prepared in EtOH/N2 exhibited tissue adhesion comparable to that of hydrogels formed in other media. Following DA grafting onto Zein in the EtOH/N2 system, tissue adhesive strength increased with DA concentration in the range of 2.5–10.0 mg/mL but did not further increase at higher concentrations (Figure 2D). BET analysis demonstrated that the specific surface area of Zein hydrogels increased with irradiation doses from 0–60 kGy, with a significant difference observed between 0 and 30 kGy. Zein hydrogels prepared in EtOH/N2 and EtOH/Air exhibited significantly greater specific surface areas than those prepared in other media. Similarly, the BET surface area increased with DA concentrations ranging from 2.5–10.0 mg/mL but did not increase further at higher concentrations (Figure 2E). The swelling ratio of Zein hydrogels also increased with irradiation doses over the range of 0–60 kGy. No significant differences were observed among the different solvent systems or irradiation atmospheres. The swelling ratio increased with DA concentration from 2.5–10.0 mg/mL and subsequently reached a plateau at higher concentrations (Figure 2F).

The injectability and self‐assembly of the ZD hydrogels were confirmed through macroscopic observations. The ZD precursor solution was continuously extruded from 2‐mL syringes into vials while remaining good flowability. Upon the addition of PBS to mimic physiological conditions, the samples underwent self‐assembly and formed hydrogels in situ (Figure 2G). In addition, adhesion and simple tensile tests showed that DA‐modified Zein hydrogels exhibited markedly enhanced tissue adhesiveness and mechanical strength compared with unmodified Zein hydrogels (Figure 2H). ZD hydrogels also displayed strong tissue adhesion in rat skin tissue (Figure S2). Collectively, these results demonstrate that Zein hydrogels prepared under optimized conditions with an appropriate level of the DA grafting process possess good injectability and in situ self‐assembly capability while markedly improving tissue adhesion and mechanical performance.

Characterization of the ZDC Hydrogel

The structural characteristics of ZDC hydrogel were subsequently evaluated. SEM images revealed porous structures in the Zein, ZD, and ZDC hydrogels (Figure 3A). Zein hydrogels exhibited relatively smooth and dense surfaces with fewer and smaller pores, whereas ZD and ZDC hydrogels contained abundant pores with diameters of approximately 2 µm, providing channels that facilitate drug release from mesoporous carbon nanospheres within the ZDC hydrogel. Elemental mapping of the niacin‑loaded carbon nanospheres confirmed their composition and distribution. The presence of N indicated successful niacin loading, whereas O was attributed to the carboxyl groups of niacin and oxygen‑containing functional groups on the surface of the carbon nanosphere surface. XRD patterns revealed a distinct diffraction peak at 2θ = 23.8°, corresponding to the (002) crystal plane of carbon, and a peak at 2θ = 43.74°, assigned to the (101) crystal plane (Figure 3B). The sharper diffraction peaks observed for the niacin‑loaded carbon nanospheres indicated good crystallinity and low impurity content (Figure 3C). FTIR spectra of niacin and the niacin‑loaded carbon nanospheres exhibited similar characteristic peaks, confirming successful drug loading. Furthermore, all three materials displayed an O–H stretching vibration band at 3450 cm− 1, indicating the presence of oxygen‑containing functional groups in the mesoporous carbon nanospheres (Figure 3D). The shear stresses (Figure 3E) and viscosities (Figure 3F) of the Zein, ZD, and ZDC precursor solutions were also evaluated. All samples exhibited typical power‑law fluid behavior, with viscosity decreasing as the shear rate increased, indicating pronounced shear‑thinning properties. Compared with the Zein precursor solution, ZD and ZDC precursor solutions exhibited lower initial shear stress and viscosity values. Collectively, these findings confirm the porous structure, successful niacin loading, and shear‐thinning injectability of the ZDC hydrogel.

Photothermal Properties, Degradation, and Biocompatibility of the ZDC Hydrogel

To further validate the feasibility of the ZDC hydrogel for NIR‐assisted drug delivery and in vivo applications, its photothermal performance, degradation behavior, and biocompatibility were systematically evaluated. The photothermal conversion capabilities of the ZDC hydrogel and its components were first evaluated. After 3 min of NIR laser irradiation, the temperatures of the ethanol aqueous solution and Zein groups remained nearly unchanged, whereas those of the mesoporous carbon nanosphere and ZDC groups increased from 22.2°C and 22.9°C to 41.5°C and 40.6°C, respectively, demonstrating the excellent photothermal conversion capability of the carbon nanospheres (Figure 4A). In vitro drug release profiles showed an initial burst for both ZDC and ZDC+NIR during the first 24 h, followed by a gradual decline in niacin release. At all evaluated time points, niacin release was greater in the ZDC+NIR group than in the ZDC group, indicating that NIR irradiation accelerated and modulated niacin release (Figure 4B).

The degradation behavior of the hydrogels was evaluated using an in vitro enzymatic degradation assay in an alkaline protease solution. All three hydrogels exhibited similar degradation behaviors, with degradation rates reaching approximately 80% on day 5 (Figure 4C). We confirmed the biodegradability and biocompatibility of the gel materials through in vivo studies. Following subcutaneous implantation in the dorsal region of rats, all three gels gradually degraded without eliciting obvious inflammatory responses (Figure 4D). Compared with the Zein hydrogel, the ZD and ZDC hydrogels degraded more rapidly in vivo (Figure 4E).

The cytocompatibility of the hydrogel extracts was assessed using CCK‐8 assays. MC3T3‐E1 cells (Figure 4F) and TDSCs (Figure 4G) exposed to the hydrogel extracts for 48 h, demonstrated good biosafety at a 50% concentration for both the ZD and ZDC groups. Furthermore, L929 cells treated with the 50% hydrogel extracts for 1, 3, and 5 days maintained a relative viability above 80% (Figure 4H), indicating favorable cytocompatibility. Consistent with these findings, live/dead staining showed that the majority of L929 cells remained viable across all groups (Figure 4I), suggesting that Zein, ZD, and ZDC hydrogel extracts did not adversely affect cell growth or proliferation. Taken together, these results demonstrate that the ZDC hydrogel provides effective NIR‐responsive drug release, while maintaining favorable degradability and biocompatibility, supporting its application in subsequent in vitro evaluation and in vivo models.

ZDC Hydrogel Extracts Promote Osteogenic Differentiation of MC3T3‐E1 Cells in Vitro

Based on these findings, we further investigated the effects of ZDC hydrogel extracts on osteogenic differentiation in vitro using MC3T3‐E1 cells. After 1 week of osteogenic induction, ALP staining showed that, compared with the control group, the Zein and ZD groups did not exhibit enhanced ALP activity, whereas the ZDC group demonstrated a significant increase in ALP expression (Figure 5A,B). After 2 weeks of induction, ARS staining revealed a similar trend, indicating that ZDC treatment promoted calcium nodule formation (Figure 5C,D). Immunofluorescence staining further demonstrated that ZDC markedly increased the expression of osteogenesis‐related proteins, including ALP, COL1, and OCN (Figure 5E–J). Consistent with these findings, RT‐qPCR analysis showed that ZDC upregulated the expression of osteogenic genes, including Runx2, Alpl, Col1a1, Spp1, and Bglap (Figure 5K–O). Together, these findings indicate that ZDC hydrogel extracts effectively promote osteogenic differentiation of MC3T3‑E1 cells.

ZDC Hydrogel Extracts Enhance NAD+ Levels and Alleviate Oxidative Stress in MC3T3‐E1 Cells

We investigated the mechanisms underlying the pro‐differentiation effects of ZDC hydrogel extracts. Because niacin serves as a precursor for NAD+ synthesis, we first evaluated intracellular NAD+ levels. After 24 h of intervention, the control, Zein, and ZD groups showed no significant increase in the NAD+/NADH ratio, whereas the ZDC group exhibited a marked elevation in the NAD+/NADH ratio (Figure 7A). To assess whether increased intracellular NAD+ was associated with reduced senescence, we measured p16 and p21 expression. The ZDC group exhibited significantly lower p16 and p21 mRNA levels than the other groups (Figures 7B,C). We further assessed intracellular ROS levels and found that cyto‐ROS levels were comparable among the control, zein, and ZD groups, whereas the ZDC group showed a substantial reduction in ROS accumulation (Figures 7D,F). Similar trends were observed for mitochondrial ROS levels (Figures 7E,G). These findings suggest that ZDC hydrogel extracts enhance intracellular NAD+ levels, reduce p16 and p21 expression, and suppress both intracellular and mitochondrial ROS accumulation, thereby alleviating oxidative stress and cellular senescence.

ZDC Hydrogels Combined With NIR Irradiation Promote TBI Healing in Rats

Based on our in vitro findings, we next evaluated the effects of ZDC hydrogels, with or without NIR irradiation, on TBI healing following rotator cuff repair in rats. Eight weeks after surgery, micro‐CT was performed to assess TBI healing under different treatments. Compared with the control and ZD groups, both the ZDC and ZDC+NIR groups exhibited increased new bone formation within the humeral head bone tunnel (Figure 8A). H&E staining revealed sparse collagen fibers and a disorganized tendon structure in the control and ZD groups. By contrast, the ZDC and ZDC+NIR groups exhibited numerous proliferative and deeply stained tenocyte nuclei and collagen fibers that were aligned along the longitudinal axis of the tendon, with nearly complete integration of the TBI in the ZDC+NIR group (Figure 8B). Safranin O‐Fast Green staining revealed little or no fibrocartilage formation and a clearly demarcated boundary between bone and tendon in the control and ZD groups. Although the ZDC group showed a similar pattern, the bone–tendon boundary was less clearly defined. Notably, the ZDC+NIR group exhibited a prominent metachromatic region at the interface, accompanied by increased chondrocyte aggregation and regenerated cartilage‐like tissue arranged in a fibrous pattern resembling native fibrocartilage (Figure 8C). Consistent with these findings, immunohistochemical staining demonstrated negligible Collagen II expression in the control and ZD groups, whereas Collagen II expression was markedly elevated in the ZDC and ZDC+NIR groups (Figure 8D). Biomechanical testing was subsequently performed to evaluate the mechanical properties of the repaired TBI. The cross‐sectional areas were similar among the groups (Figure 8E). Stiffness measurements showed that treatment with ZD alone did not markedly improve tendon stiffness compared to the control group. By contrast, stiffness was significantly increased in the ZDC and ZDC+NIR groups, with the greatest improvement observed in the ZDC+NIR group (Figure 8F). Similar trends were observed for maximum stress (Figure 8G) and ultimate load to failure (Figure 8H). Collectively, these findings indicate that ZDC hydrogels, particularly when combined with NIR irradiation, significantly enhanced TBI healing in rats.

Mechanistic Study of ZDC Hydrogel‐Mediated Healing Using a Rat Skin Wound Model

Because the TBI is relatively hypocellular and hypovascular, a rat skin wound model was used to further investigate the effects of ZDC hydrogels on angiogenesis and inflammation. VEGF expression in wound tissues was assessed at 3, 7, and 14 days after wound creation. VEGF expression was detected in all groups on day 3, with the highest levels in the ZDC+NIR group, followed by the ZDC group. The VEGF expression decreased in all groups by day 7, although levels remained slightly higher in the ZDC+NIR group than in the other groups. By day 14, VEGF expression was low in all groups as the wounds were mostly healed (Figure 9A). Angiogenesis was further assessed by evaluating CD31 expression on day 7. Compared with the control group, CD31 expression was significantly elevated in both the ZDC and ZDC+NIR groups, with the highest levels observed in the ZDC+NIR group (Figure 9B,D). In addition, TNF‐α expression on day 3 revealed a decreasing trend in both the ZDC and ZDC+NIR groups (Figure 9C,E). Taken together, these results suggest that ZDC hydrogels, particularly when combined with NIR irradiation, promote wound repair by enhancing angiogenesis and suppressing inflammation.

Discussion

In this study, we developed an injectable ZDC hydrogel capable of NIR‐responsive niacin release and demonstrated its therapeutic efficacy in promoting TBI healing in an osteoporotic rat rotator cuff repair model. The beneficial effects of ZDC on TBI healing were further augmented by NIR irradiation. In vitro, ZDC extracts promoted the osteogenic and tenogenic differentiation of the relevant cell types. Furthermore, mechanistic studies investigations using a rat skin wound model revealed that ZDC enhances angiogenesis and alleviates inflammation. Collectively, these findings highlight the potential of ZDC hydrogel as a treatment option for osteoporotic rotator cuff tears.

TBI possesses a unique hierarchical structure characterized by continuous transitions in mechanical properties [14]. However, surgical repair often results in the formation of fibrous scar tissue that compromises the healing process, particularly in cases of osteoporosis. To address this challenge, researchers explored variety of innovative approaches, including the utilization of hydrogels, to promote structural restoration in osteoporotic TBI [16, 17, 18]. An ideal biomaterial for osteoporotic rotator cuff tear repair should exhibit excellent biocompatibility and provide mechanical properties comparable to those of the native tissue [30]. However, most naturally derived polymer hydrogels, including Zein—a natural protein from corn endosperm [31, 32]—fail to meet the mechanical strength and optimized drug‐release requirements necessary for effective TBI repair [20, 22, 33]. Wilker et al. [34] mixed Zein with phenolic compounds to generate Zein–polyphenol precursors that exhibited substantially greater adhesive strength than Zein alone. However, the requirement for solvent evaporation and high‑temperature curing limited their clinical applicability. In the present study, we employed electron‐beam irradiation to induce the crosslinking and grafting of Zein, thereby generating a ZD precursor that retained injectability and self‑assembly gelation capability. This modification markedly enhanced the adhesive and mechanical properties of the resulting ZD hydrogel, thereby improving its potential for TBI healing applications. In addition to these mechanical advantages, BET and SEM analyses demonstrated that the modified ZD hydrogel possessed a larger specific surface area and a more porous microstructure. This phenomenon is likely attributable to free radicals generated during irradiation, which promote network crosslinking [35], ultimately enhancing drug loading and release and improving subsequent in vivo performance.

Poor vascularization and low cellular density in the TBI hinder the efficient delivery of reparative cells and therapeutic agents [15]. Consequently, systemic drug administration may not be the ideal solution, making local drug delivery an attractive alternative [36]. In the present study, we utilized mesoporous carbon nanospheres as drug carriers owing to their high specific surface area, porous architecture, and excellent photothermal conversion capability [23]. In our previous studies, we optimized the nitrogen‐doping level of graphite to red‐shift the absorption of carbon dots into the second NIR window and subsequently loaded these carbon dots onto two‐dimensional nanosheets to achieve synergistically enhanced photothermal effects, resulting in potent photothermal chemotherapy and complete tumor eradication [37, 38]. Previous studies have similarly demonstrated the utility of mesoporous carbon nanospheres as drug carriers and photothermal agents, primarily in tumor‐related applications [23, 39, 40]. Building upon this foundation, niacin was loaded onto mesoporous carbon nanospheres and incorporated into ZD hydrogel to construct ZDC hydrogel. In vitro characterization demonstrated that ZDC exhibited excellent photothermal performance and enabled NIR‐responsive niacin release. The underlying mechanism is likely that the mesoporous carbon nanospheres in the composite gel absorb NIR light and convert it into heat, thereby weakening the noncovalent interactions between the carrier and niacin and thus accelerating its release [23]. Furthermore, in vitro drug release profiles demonstrated an initial burst release from both the ZDC and ZDC+NIR groups within the first 24 h, consistent with findings from previous studies [22, 41]. Future research is needed to explore strategies to achieve more sustained and precisely controlled drug release, including the incorporation of polymer coatings or the development of stimuli‐responsive gating systems.

Excessive localized inflammation and cellular senescence can impair healing process in patients with osteoporotic TBI. Therefore, alleviating inflammation and cellular senescence through localized drug delivery may represent an effective therapeutic strategy. In recent years, considerable attention has been directed toward agents that modulate the metabolism of senescent cells, such as glutamine [10] and alpha‐ketoglutarate [12], both of which have demonstrated therapeutic potential in osteoporotic rotator cuff tear repair. In this study, we focused on niacin, a well‐established precursor of NAD+, because restoration of NAD+ levels has been shown to slow or even reverse a variety of age‐related pathological processes [24, 25]. Previous studies have also demonstrated that niacin exerts anti‐inflammatory and blood vasculoprotective effects by reducing oxidative stress, suppressing the production of inflammatory mediators, and promoting anti‐inflammatory immune responses [26, 27, 28, 29]. Our findings showed that ZDC hydrogel extracts promoted the differentiation of both MC3T3‐E1 cells and TDSCs. Notably, treatment with ZDC hydrogel extracts significantly increased intracellular NAD+ levels, reduced p16 and p21 expression, and alleviated the accumulation of intracellular and mitochondrial ROS in MC3T3‐E1 cells. These effects may be attributable to niacin‐mediated improvement of cellular energy metabolism and attenuation of senescence, thereby enhancing the differentiation capacity of stem and progenitor cells [42, 43, 44]. Furthermore, using an osteoporotic rat rotator cuff repair model, we demonstrated that ZDC promoted TBI healing and improved the mechanical properties of the repaired interface, whereas the combination of ZDC and NIR irradiation further amplified these therapeutic effects. Mechanistic validation using a rat skin wound model additionally demonstrated enhanced angiogenesis and reduced local inflammation following treatment. We speculate that, in addition to the intrinsic pro‐angiogenic and anti‐inflammatory effects of mild NIR‐induced hyperthermia [45], the predominant therapeutic benefit arises from the NIR‐responsive release of niacin from the ZDC hydrogel.

Current hydrogel‐based strategies for promoting osteoporotic TBI healing primarily target cellular senescence and inflammation. For example, Song et al. developed a quercetin‐loaded composite hydrogel to modulate the senescent microenvironment [18], whereas Kong et al. engineered hydrogels containing quercetin‐primed small extracellular vesicles to mitigate inflammation and enhance the resilience of bone marrow‐derived mesenchymal stem cells to senescence [46]. Similarly, macroporous hydrogels incorporating adipose‐derived small extracellular vesicles have been shown to improve tissue integration through immunomodulatory effects [47]. By contrast, the ZDC composite hydrogel developed in the present study utilizes niacin, a compound with well‐characterized biological functions, to alleviate cellular senescence and suppress inflammation. Furthermore, the incorporation of carbon nanospheres enables NIR‐responsive drug release, thereby offering superior therapeutic efficacy. Additionally, electron‐beam irradiation was used to modify the zein hydrogel, resulting in enhanced physical and mechanical properties that may better satisfy the biomechanical requirements of osteoporotic TBI.

Despite these promising findings, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, this study was conducted using an osteoporotic rat rotator cuff repair model, which may not fully recapitulate the complexity of human tendon–bone pathology. Second, the precise molecular mechanisms through which niacin and photothermal stimulation regulate immune responses and lineage‐specific differentiation remain incompletely understood. Third, we did not evaluate how the hydrogel affected the function of vascular endothelial cells at the cellular level, which limited our ability to better support its pro‐angiogenic properties. Fourth, although several parameters of the hydrogel system were adjusted and optimized, ZDC was evaluated under a limited range of compositions and treatment conditions, and its performance in more diverse and clinically relevant settings remains unknown. Fifth, comprehensive mechanical characterization of the ZDC hydrogel remains insufficient and should be further investigated to assess its long‐term suitability for rotator cuff repair. Sixth, the current experimental design did not fully distinguish the contributions of the individual components of the ZDC hydrogel, making it difficult to clarify the individual roles of each component. Finally, a mechanistic study was conducted using a skin wound model in which angiogenesis and inflammatory responses were pronounced. However, this model restricted our ability to fully understand the mechanisms underlying TBI healing. Future studies should confirm its effectiveness in large animal models, explore the signaling pathways involved, systematically study how endothelial cells respond, elucidate the individual contributions of each component, and use models that more closely replicate the conditions and biological processes of TBI healing.

Taken together, this study demonstrated that an injectable ZDC hydrogel with NIR‐responsive niacin release can effectively enhance TBI regeneration following osteoporotic rotator cuff repair while promoting both osteogenesis and tenogenesis. In addition, mechanistic studies using a rat skin wound model indicated that ZDC alleviates inflammation and promotes angiogenesis. These findings support the potential application of ZDC as a promising biomaterial‐based strategy to improve OTBH in clinical settings.

Conclusion

We developed a multifunctional injectable ZDC hydrogel that integrates mechanical support, photothermal responsiveness, and niacin release for the repair of osteoporotic TBI. In an osteoporotic rat rotator cuff repair model, ZDC combined with NIR irradiation promoted new bone formation, enhanced fibrocartilaginous interface reconstruction, and improved the biomechanical performance of the repaired tendon–bone complex. In vitro studies further confirmed its ability to simultaneously promote osteogenic and tenogenic differentiation. Moreover, mechanistic investigations using a rat skin wound model demonstrated that ZDC enhances angiogenesis and mitigates inflammation. Collectively, these findings highlight the therapeutic potential of ZDC as a biomaterial‐based strategy for improving TBI healing in osteoporotic rotator cuff tears.
