Personal and professional use of mobile health applications among Iranian nurses: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Narjes Mirabootalebi, Felix Holl, Walter Swoboda, Zahra Tagharrobi, Meghdad Rahati, Fatemeh Rangraz Jeddi, Hossein Akbari, Shahla Rafiei
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionAdvances in technology have made the use of mobile health applications (MHAs) by nurses increasingly important. Examining the current landscape and identifying the factors influencing this use are essential for developing strategies that promote appropriate MHAs use among nurses. Therefore, this study aimed to investigate the personal and professional utilization of MHAs and associated factors among clinical nurses in hospitals affiliated with Kashan University of Medical Sciences in 2022.Materials and methodsIn this cross-sectional study, 250 nurses at Kashan in 2022 were randomly selected. Data were collected on a four-part scale encompassing personal and professional information, perceived knowledge of MHAs, familiarity with MHAs, and use of MHAs. The frequency of personal and professional use of MHAs was estimated with a 95% confidence interval. The data were analyzed via SPSS version 16 and logistic regression.ResultsThe prevalence rates of MHAs’ utilization for nurses’ personal use and professional practice were estimated to be 29.2% (95% CI: 23.56%–34.84%) and 26% (95% CI: 20.6%–31.4%), respectively. Observation of MHAs use by healthcare professionals or others (OR = 4.234, 95% CI: 1.881–9.530; p < 0.0001), addressing patient inquiries about MHAs (OR = 5.200, 95% CI: 2.095–12.908; p < 0.0001), and having close relatives or friends with an educational or professional background in information technology or related fields (OR = 2.371, 95% CI: 1.119–5.022; p = 0.024) were identified as predictors of nurses’ personal use of MHAs. Observation of MHAs use by healthcare professionals or others (OR = 12.900, 95% CI: 4.802–34.657; p < 0.0001) and addressing patient inquiries about MHAs (OR = 5.622, 95% CI: 1.989–15.893; p = 0.001) were significant predictors of nurses’ professional use of MHAs.ConclusionThe use of MHAs for nurses’ personal and professional performance in Kashan hospitals appears to be limited. However, increasing nurses’ exposure to MHAs could increase their commitment to health programs. It is recommended that nursing management train selected nurses to serve as role models in hospital wards to encourage their colleagues to adopt MHAs practices.Keywords: Mobile health application, Nurses, Iran

Introduction

In digital health, nurses are increasingly recognized as key adopters of mobile health applications (MHAs) designed to enhance clinical workflows and patient monitoring. These applications have the potential to significantly improve professional nursing practices [1]. However, empirical evidence reveals a substantial gap between this theoretical potential and actual proficiency. For example, a study by Orhan and Serin (2019) revealed that 69.5% of nurses do not feel adequately equipped to use health technologies effectively [2]. A 2020 study reported that despite a strong emphasis on using technology to improve patient care and safety, the adoption of modern technology among nurses in developing contexts remains limited. Only approximately 26% of nurses reported using modern technology in patient care, and a notably small percentage demonstrated proficiency in using computers and information technology. Consequently, the use of modern technology in nursing practice is often suboptimal [3].

Failure to use MHAs can lead to undesirable outcomes, including a greater likelihood of errors and significant time spent managing paper-based data. Additionally, healthcare staff may frequently visit nursing stations to gather and document information, increasing the risk of forgetting important details or misplacing data [4]. Although using MHAs may pose new challenges to care, nurses must incorporate them into their practices [5]. In this context, the first step is to assess the prevailing circumstances and the factors that influence them thoroughly [6].

The adoption of MHAs can be understood through a socio-technical lens, where interactions among system features, social dynamics, organizational circumstances, and individual preparedness influence technology use. In this framework, characteristics such as information quality, system reliability, and perceived compatibility significantly affect nurses’ perceptions of MHAs as useful and practical for clinical practice [7–9]. Social and environmental factors, including role models, peer behavior, managerial support, and external support systems, strongly influence behavioral intentions by either normalizing or discouraging technology use [7, 9, 10]. Additionally, individual characteristics such as adaptability, innovativeness, and perceived job relevance play critical roles in determining whether nurses are likely to incorporate MHAs into their personal or professional routines [11]. Together, these factors provide a theoretical framework for exploring not only the use of MHAs among nurses but also the reasons for variations in usage across different personal and professional contexts.

While smartphones provide the necessary hardware platform, MHAs can significantly enhance nurses’ professional practice [12]. However, given the diversity within the nursing workforce, application developers should consider the various factors that influence nurses’ use of MHAs during both the design and development stages. This approach helps ensure that MHAs effectively achieve their intended objectives. Although these design considerations are relevant globally, their implementation should be adapted to the specific healthcare context of each region. For example, a 2020 study in Iran highlighted the urgent need for policymakers and officials to thoroughly examine the factors affecting MHAs adoption when new technologies are introduced. This finding emphasizes the importance of understanding these factors to inform effective policymaking and facilitate the successful integration of technological innovations in healthcare settings [13].

This socio-technical perspective is especially significant because the use of MHAs can differ between personal and professional contexts. Intrinsic motivations, self-management needs, and individual lifestyle choices typically influence personal use. In contrast, professional use is affected by additional factors, including clinical relevance, workflow compatibility, data security requirements, managerial expectations, and considerations related to patient care [9, 10, 14]. Therefore, personal familiarity with mobile technologies does not automatically result in professional adoption in clinical settings. Although there is extensive literature on technology acceptance, empirical evidence on the actual use of mobile health applications among clinical nurses remains fragmented and inconsistent. Some international studies report relatively high levels of professional integration, whereas others highlight significant competence gaps and limited adoption rates among nurses [15, 16]. Previous studies in Iran have examined factors influencing mHealth acceptance among medical sciences students [13], but there is a notable gap in understanding the actual usage behaviors of clinical practitioners. This study makes a unique contribution in two ways: first, it provides a dual-domain analysis contrasting personal and professional utilization; second, it evaluates these patterns within the specific context of clinical nursing in Kashan, Iran. This study aimed to investigate the personal and professional use of MHAs, along with associated demographic and professional factors, among clinical nurses working in hospitals affiliated with Kashan University of Medical Sciences, Iran.

Materials and methods

Sampling method

The present study is a descriptive cross-sectional study. The sample size was determined on the basis of prevalence estimates from a pilot study conducted among 30 nurses, which reported frequencies of 26.7% for personal use and 13.3% for professional use of mobile health applications. The higher prevalence of personal use was selected as the primary parameter to provide a conservative estimate and ensure adequate precision. Cochrane’s formula was applied at a 95% confidence level and a relative precision of 0.2p (p = 0.267), yielding a minimum required sample size of 250 nurses (Formula 1).

The adequacy of the final sample size for subsequent univariable and multivariable logistic regression analyses was also evaluated. Variables with p values < 0.20 in univariable analyses were included in the multivariable model to limit the number of predictors and reduce the risk of overfitting, in accordance with methodological recommendations on the events-per-variable ratio for exploratory analyses [17].

The inclusion criteria were Iranian nationality, the absence of known psychological disorders (as reported by the participant), a university degree in nursing, at least six months of clinical experience, participation in clinical activities in inpatient wards, and consent to participate in the study. Refusal to complete the scales was considered an exclusion criterion.

The participants were selected via stratified random sampling. First, all inpatient clinical wards in hospitals affiliated with Kashan University of Medical Sciences were identified and treated as separate sampling strata. The number of nurses selected from each ward was proportional to the number of eligible nurses employed in that ward. Within each stratum, eligible nurses were randomly selected via simple random sampling from the ward’s nursing staff. If a selected nurse was unavailable during the data collection period, declined to participate, or did not complete the questionnaire, another eligible nurse from the same ward was randomly selected as a substitute. The sampling and data collection process took place over approximately two months, from August 16 to October 17, 2022.

Data collection

After approval was obtained from the Research Council and the Ethics Committee of Kashan University of Medical Sciences, the first author visited the participating hospitals and presented an official letter of introduction to the hospital and nursing managers. After the study’s objectives and procedures were explained, administrative permission was obtained, allowing the compilation of lists of inpatient clinical wards and the nurses assigned to each ward. These lists served as the sampling frame. The required number of nurses from each ward was calculated proportionally to each ward’s nursing staff size, and eligible nurses were randomly selected from each ward’s list. Eligible nurses were approached in their respective wards during their scheduled shifts.

Before data collection, each participant was informed of the study’s purpose and procedures, the voluntary nature of participation, the estimated time needed to complete the questionnaire, and their right to withdraw at any time without consequences. Those who agreed to participate provided written informed consent before receiving the questionnaire. The participants completed the questionnaire via self-reports. No identifying information was collected, ensuring that all the responses remained confidential and were used solely for research purposes. Completed questionnaires were collected at the end of each shift and securely stored by the research team. If a participant was unable to complete the questionnaire during their shift, an alternative submission time was arranged. If a selected nurse was unavailable, declined participation, or did not complete the questionnaire, a replacement was randomly selected from the same ward.

Measurement scales

Data were collected via a researcher-developed, multidomain instrument, the Nurses’ Mobile Health Applications Use Questionnaire. This questionnaire was developed following an extensive literature review of mobile health applications, technology use in healthcare, and digital health adoption among healthcare professionals [18, 19]. It includes four main sections: the Demographic and Professional Characteristics Form, the MHAs Perceived Knowledge Scale, the MHAs Familiarity Checklist, and the MHAs Use and Barriers Questionnaire.

In this study, MHAs specifically refer to traditional mobile-based health applications that were readily recognizable to nurses during data collection in 2022. Tools using artificial intelligence were excluded from the study and were not evaluated, as nurses’ awareness and routine clinical use of such tools were limited at that time. Therefore, advancements in AI-enabled digital health are discussed only as contextual background and future benchmarks, not as direct comparisons to the MHAs usage reported by participants in 2022.

The questionnaire was designed to collect both descriptive and analytical information about the personal and professional use of mobile health applications rather than to assess latent psychological constructs. Therefore, the items were kept independent, and composite or total scores were not calculated. The instrument consisted of four distinct sections:

Demographic and professional characteristics included information on age, gender, marital status, educational background, workplace location, work experience, predominant shift type, presence of young or adolescent children, and managerial role.

The MHAs Use and Barriers Questionnaire assesses various aspects of MHAs use, including the types and purposes of MHAs utilization, satisfaction in personal and professional contexts, smartphone ownership and usage patterns, daily internet engagement, willingness to recommend MHAs to patients, interest in educational programs related to MHAs, and perceived barriers to incorporating MHAs in clinical care. Barriers to MHAs use in patient care were assessed with a predefined list of potential obstacles. The participants self-reported the extent to which each factor hindered the adoption of MHAs in clinical settings, using a 5-point ordinal scale from 0 (not at all) to 4 (very high). The qualitative content validity of the newly developed questionnaire was assessed through an expert review process. A panel of 10 faculty members at Kashan University of Medical Sciences evaluated the items for relevance, clarity, and comprehensiveness, and minor wording revisions were made on the basis of their feedback before data collection. Reliability was assessed via the test–retest method with a one-week interval among 20 nurses. The intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) for quantitative items exceeded 0.95, and the kappa coefficient for categorical items exceeded 0.97, indicating excellent reliability. Given the descriptive and multi-domain nature of the instrument and the independence of its items, psychometric analyses such as exploratory factor analysis were not applicable.

Statistical analysis

The data were analyzed via SPSS version 16. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the data, with quantitative variables presented as measures of central tendency and dispersion and categorical variables presented as absolute and relative frequencies. Skewness and kurtosis were assessed to evaluate the data distribution, with values within ± 2 considered indicative of normality. All continuous variables met this criterion and were included in the logistic regression models as continuous variables. The frequency of personal and occupational MHAs use was reported as a relative frequency and estimated with 95% confidence intervals for the target population.

The impact of various factors on each dependent variable was examined in two stages to identify predictors of MHAs use in relation to personal and professional performance. There were no missing data for any study variables, allowing all collected observations to be included in the logistic regression analyses. In the first stage, univariate logistic regression was conducted to explore the relationship between each predictor of MHAs use. The crude odds ratio was reported for variables deemed significant. Variables with p values less than 0.20 in univariate analyses, as well as those identified in prior studies as potential confounders, were considered for inclusion in the multiple logistic regression model. All selected variables were entered simultaneously via the entry method to account for confounding effects. Adjusted odds ratios (AORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) are reported.

Before performing the multiple logistic regression analysis, we assessed multicollinearity among the independent variables by examining variance inflation factors (VIFs) and tolerance statistics. All variables included in the final model showed acceptable collinearity (VIF less than 5 and tolerance greater than 0.2), indicating no problematic multicollinearity. Because continuous predictors were included in the models, we further evaluated the linearity assumption in the logit by examining the relationship between these continuous predictors and the log odds of the outcome. No significant deviations from linearity were observed. The fit of the multiple logistic regression analyses was assessed via pseudo-R² statistics, specifically Cox & Snell and Nagelkerke R². A significance level of less than 0.05 was maintained for all analyses.

Discussion

The present study aimed to examine the personal and professional use of MHAs among clinical nurses in Iran and the factors influencing their adoption. The findings showed that both personal and professional use of these applications were significantly low among the nurses surveyed. Individual reluctance or lack of awareness alone cannot explain this low adoption rate. Instead, broader challenges play a major role, such as technical readiness, organizational culture, and established clinical routines. In settings with limited digital infrastructure and restricted options for application use, nurses may not incorporate these tools into their daily practice. Therefore, the results indicate that minimal MHAs utilization is shaped by both personal attitudes and institutional factors that affect the feasibility and relevance of technology in nursing [20].

Prevalence of MHAs use: a low-adoption context

The findings of this study align with those of Stergiannis et al. (2017), indicating that although many Greek nurses possess smartphones, they tend to be hesitant to use MHAs due to a lack of awareness [21]. This situation underscores that personal ownership of digital tools does not necessarily lead to their effective integration in clinical settings. Similarly, Gaur et al. (2024) reported limited clinical smartphone usage among Indian nurses [14], suggesting that barriers persist in resource-constrained environments. In contrast, studies with robust institutional support demonstrate higher rates of clinical usage. Consequently, the adoption of MHAs should be regarded as an organization-mediated practice shaped by factors such as infrastructure, policy legitimacy, peer behavior, and perceived clinical utility [22, 23].

Gutiérrez-Puertas et al. (2021) explored nursing students’ perceptions of smartphones as platforms for digital health tools in clinical settings. They identified three main themes: smartphones as personal resources, their role in clinical decision-making, and their impact on patient care [24]. The findings suggest that hospitals should train select nurses to serve as role models, promoting appropriate use of MHAs to normalize their use and reduce staff uncertainty. This training should also prepare nurses to address patient questions about MHAs and direct them to credible digital health resources [25].

Inadequate infrastructure is a significant barrier; however, hospitals must prioritize essential technological investments, such as reliable internet and approved applications. Improving infrastructure alone is not enough; skill acquisition remains a challenge. To address this, hospitals and nursing schools should offer brief training sessions and integrate MHAs competencies into their curricula. Managers should also ensure technical readiness and establish structured training pathways for MHAs within clinical workflows. This comprehensive approach may be more effective than focusing only on technological improvements or curriculum changes [26].

The current study highlights disparities in smartphone and MHAs usage among nurses in different income regions. For example, while 80% of nurses in the UK use smartphones for professional purposes [15], only approximately half of nurses in Catalonia use MHAs [12]. This disparity indicates the need for health organizations to endorse such applications before their clinical implementation. In high-income settings, the focus is on integration and governance, whereas in low-income regions, challenges include inadequate infrastructure and limited access to applications [16]. This gap accounts for the lower adoption rates of MHAs, despite high levels of smartphone ownership. Our findings suggest that health systems should tailor policy priorities to their level of digital maturity, emphasizing foundational investments in areas with low adoption and focusing on governance in more digitized environments. Although the COVID-19 pandemic accelerated healthcare technology adoption, our 2022 dataset shows persistently low MHAs utilization, highlighting ongoing disparities and barriers. In well-digitized settings, nurses can use these applications to enhance patient care, reduce errors, and improve communication [27].

The findings revealed a significant discrepancy between participants’ perceived knowledge (mean score = 2.98) and their high satisfaction with MHAs (90.8%). This satisfaction paradox may be attributed to a “technological halo effect”, where users’ perceptions of potential enhancements increase their satisfaction, even if their actual skills are lacking. Alternatively, it may suggest that satisfaction assessments reflect general feelings rather than specific usability frustrations. This finding indicates that while nurses’ advocate for the integration of these tools, their effective use requires targeted professional development [28].

The present study revealed that internet access was the most influential factor affecting nurses’ use of MHAs. Support from healthcare professionals also significantly influenced MHAs use. Dugstad et al. (2020) noted that social support from both managers and nurses served as crucial facilitators in the acceptance of technology [29]. Furthermore, Zhou et al. (2019) confirmed the importance of positive social influences, especially from managers, in shaping the behavioral intention to use these applications [30]. Huiying Zhang et al. (2010) reported a significant correlation between social influence, perceived usefulness, and perceived ease of use in relation to the intentions of 91 nurses [31]. Similarly, Basak et al. (2015) reported a significant correlation between intention to use and social influence among 339 Turkish physicians [32]. In addition, Alasmari and Zhang (2019) confirmed the findings of the present study regarding the impact of other healthcare professionals on MHAs utilization [33]. Moore and Jayewardene (2014) emphasized that three key issues deserve the attention of nurses and physicians: improving access to information, improving decision-making, and increasing efficiency [34]. Together, these findings suggest that colleagues, peers, and managers may play important roles in nurses’ adoption of MHAs.

Barriers to adoption: a systemic issue

The barriers identified in this study can be categorized into three interrelated levels. First, systemic and infrastructural issues, such as inadequate infrastructure and poor app design, indicate that the adoption of MHAs relies on more than individual motivation. The key prerequisites for the professional use of these applications include reliable internet access, secure hospital networks, and well-designed apps. These findings are consistent with evidence from other developing healthcare systems, where limited resources and fragile digital infrastructure hinder technology integration in care [29, 35]. In contrast, high-income settings may face fewer infrastructure barriers but still face challenges related to policy adherence, data governance, and workflow integration.

Second, individual and educational barriers significantly impede the integration of digital health competencies into nursing education and ongoing training. Challenges in skill acquisition and low perceived knowledge of MHAs suggest that nurses often view their use as an added burden rather than a core skill [36]. Previous studies have shown that inadequate perceived knowledge, limited experience, insufficient supervision, and poor computer skills can undermine nurses’ confidence in the use of digital technologies [29, 37]. To improve nurses’ perceived knowledge of digital health and enhance their self-efficacy, curriculum reform and targeted training programs are essential.

Third, cultural and social barriers significantly affect healthcare delivery. Some patients view face-to-face interactions as more legitimate than digital engagements do, which can make nurses hesitant to use MHAs [24]. To address this reluctance, strategies such as public engagement, patient education, and demonstrations of MHAs’ benefits are essential. Ultimately, fostering the adoption of MHAs requires a coordinated approach that includes organizational, educational, and cultural interventions, not just technical solutions [38].

Patterns of personal and professional MHAs use

In this study, nurses primarily used MHAs in both personal and professional contexts. Personally, they used these applications for lifestyle management, specifically for dietary and nutritional tracking. Professionally, their focus was on patient education rather than clinical decision-making, indicating an early stage of MHAs integration into clinical processes. A 2024 survey of nursing students in Saudi Arabia revealed that they mainly used MHAs to monitor physical activity and caloric intake, which contributed to their overall well-being. The most commonly used MHAs focus on nutrition and lifestyle, with female students showing a greater tendency to use applications related to women’s health and pregnancy [39]. A 2013 study revealed that users often cited motivations such as weight loss, increased physical activity, dietary management, and reduced sugar intake for their use of MHAs [40].

When comparing our findings with those of previous studies, it is important to distinguish between general smartphone use and the adoption of MHAs. For example, McBride et al. (2015) reported that nurses frequently use personal smartphones for non-work-related communication during shifts [23]; however, this does not necessarily indicate the use of health-specific applications. This distinction requires careful consideration, as social desirability bias may lead participants to underreport personal device use to conform to professional standards. A 2017 study emphasized that nurses engage in various non-work-related activities, including texting, online browsing, and social media use, even during emergencies [41]. Nurses also reported using smartphones for personal tasks such as online banking and messaging during emergency shifts [42]. Garner et al. (2018) reported that both physicians and nurses often use texting and email for personal purposes [43]. Conversely, Jembai et al. (2022) demonstrated that medical students are aware of and actively use COVID-19 apps and other health-related applications [44]. Given the widespread use of MHAs by nurses for personal tasks, establishing clear guidelines for their use is essential.

In nursing, educational applications are the most commonly used MHAs, primarily for patient education and treatment support. This trend shows that nurses are more likely to use MHAs when these tools align with their professional roles, such as educating patients and providing preventive counseling. However, the limited adoption of MHAs for treatment support suggests that these tools have not yet been widely accepted as clinical decision-support resources. This may be due to factors such as a lack of approved applications, concerns about accountability and data security, or insufficient training. Future strategies should promote the use of MHAs while clearly demonstrating how these tools can enhance safe, evidence-based decision-making in nursing practice [45, 46]. McBride and LeVasseur (2017) reported that nurses prioritize the use of personal communication devices for general work-related tasks [39]. The limited adoption of MHAs for clinical decision support may be attributed to the scarcity of approved applications, concerns about accountability, and a lack of comprehensive training. Future strategies should promote MHAs as tools for safe, evidence-based decision-making. Although nurses often use personal devices for general tasks, the integration of specialized MHAs into patient care remains limited [16]. Hitti et al. (2021) reported that some healthcare professionals use medical applications, such as formularies and medication references [42]. As MHAs continue to develop, the focus on secure application design becomes increasingly important.

The primacy of social influence

A significant contribution of this study is that exploratory analyses identified social exposure and patient-related interactions as potential predictors of MHAs adoption in both personal and professional settings. Specifically, observing healthcare professionals using MHAs and engaging with patient inquiries about these applications showed the strongest associations with their use. This suggests that MHAs adoption may be influenced not only by formal education and training but also by informal social learning and patient-centered digital interactions. When nurses observe colleagues, supervisors, or managers using MHAs in routine clinical environments, the visibility, legitimacy, and practical relevance of these tools increase. Additionally, addressing patient inquiries can increase nurses’ familiarity with MHAs, as patients’ questions create a direct clinical need for perceived digital health knowledge [47].

This finding has clear practical implications: hospitals should consider establishing role models at the ward level, appointing digital champions, securing supervisor endorsement, and implementing patient-centered training strategies that equip nurses to discuss MHAs with confidence and appropriateness. Bautista et al. (2020) noted that perceived organizational support among nurses indirectly influences their use of smartphones for professional purposes, driven by encouragement from hospital management, nursing supervisors, other nurses, and medical colleagues [48]. A 2016 study reported that colleagues’ smartphone use while on duty normalizes their use in the workplace [15]. Further research supports these findings, showing that others’ use of MHAs directly affects nurses’ acceptance and continued use of these applications.

Strengths, limitations, and future research

Although this study benefits from a relatively large sample size and rigorous regression analyses, several limitations should be noted.

First, the cross-sectional design prevents causal inferences, while associations were identified, it remains uncertain whether factors such as peer observation or patient inquiries directly influence the adoption of MHAs.

Second, the generalizability of the findings may be limited. The study was conducted in hospitals affiliated with a single university, and the replacement of 56 participants who were unavailable or uncooperative may have introduced some volunteer bias. It is possible that those who did not respond were less familiar with or interested in mobile technologies than were the participants, which could lead to a slight overestimation of the prevalence of MHAs use compared with the wider nursing population.

Third, reliance on self-report questionnaires entails inherent risks related to recall and social desirability biases, especially when participants report on their technology use, satisfaction, and perceived barriers. Additionally, the assessment of perceived technological knowledge was based on a single self-reported item that captures perceived self-efficacy rather than objective technical proficiency. This distinction is important, as perceived competence can differ from actual skill, potentially impacting the predictive power of the regression models. Additionally, although the self-developed instrument demonstrated acceptable initial validity and reliability, its structural validity requires further psychometric evaluation.

Fourth, there are statistical limitations regarding model stability. To reduce the risk of overfitting, rigorous pre-modeling steps were implemented, including univariable screening (p < 0.2) and assessments for multicollinearity (VIF < 5 and tolerance > 0.2). However, the events-per-variable (EPV) ratios remain limited. Specifically, the model for professional use included 65 events with 17 predictors (EPV ≈ 3.8), whereas the model for personal use included 73 events with 15 predictors (EPV ≈ 4.9). Given these ratios and the absence of internal validation techniques, such as bootstrap resampling, which is not feasible due to resource constraints during the final preparation stage, the stability and generalizability of the parameter estimates may be limited. These findings should be interpreted with caution, and future studies with larger cohorts are encouraged to use resampling methods to rigorously assess model robustness.

Finally, contextual factors should be considered. The data were collected in 2022, a period marked by significant challenges due to the COVID-19 pandemic; it is advisable to replicate this study under standard clinical conditions [36]. Additionally, because data collection occurred before the widespread clinical integration of artificial intelligence (AI), the findings primarily reflect the use of conventional MHAs rather than current trends in AI-assisted digital health. Future research should use qualitative methods to gain deeper insights into the complex barriers to technology adoption and broaden its scope to assess nurses’ use of emerging AI-enabled tools [11].

Conclusion

The current study revealed that nurses employed in hospitals affiliated with Kashan University of Medical Sciences have low rates of MHAs use for both personal and professional practice. Most nurses who use MHAs personally recognize their significant value. Additionally, the majority of those who integrate MHAs into their clinical practice report high levels of satisfaction with these programs.

The findings indicate potential associations between nurses’ personal use of MHAs and three variables: “observation of MHAs use by healthcare professionals or others”, “addressing patient inquiries about MHAs”, and “having close relatives or friends with an educational or professional background in information technology or related fields”. Furthermore, the analyses suggest that the variables “observation of MHAs use by healthcare professionals or others” and “addressing patient inquiries about MHAs” may be associated with increased odds of MHAs use; however, these identified predictors should be interpreted with caution.

The results suggest that nurses are more likely to adopt MHAs when they are regularly exposed to their use in clinical settings. Nursing management should designate specific nurses as role models to demonstrate effective MHAs use and promote their adoption among colleagues. From a managerial perspective, this initiative should be supported by investments in secure and reliable digital infrastructure, including improved hospital internet access and approved applications, as well as ongoing education programs that address the perceived challenges of skill acquisition.
