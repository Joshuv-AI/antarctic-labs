Aging with dual burdens: cross-national evidence on mortality among older Hispanic adults with diabetes and dementia
Authors: Hyeran Chung, Alfonso Rojas-Alvarez, Jose Eduardo Cabrero Castro, Brian Downer
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesOlder Hispanic adults in the United States and Mexico face a high burden of diabetes and dementia, yet cross-national differences in absolute survival associated with these conditions remain unclear. This study compared 5-year survival among Mexican-origin adults aged 74 and older by condition profile and assessed gender differences.MethodsTwo representative longitudinal surveys were harmonized: the Hispanic Established Populations for Epidemiologic Study of the Elderly—Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services (H-EPESE-CMS) linked data for the United States and the Mexican Health and Aging Study (MHAS) for Mexico. Flexible parametric survival models and g-computation estimated standardized 5-year survival, risk differences, and risk ratios across four groups: no condition, diabetes only, dementia only, and dual conditions.ResultsSurvival declined steadily with condition burden in both countries. In the United States, standardized 5-year survival was 73.3% (no condition), 57.9% (diabetes only), 49.2% (dementia only), and 36.6% (dual conditions). In Mexico, absolute survival was higher across all groups: 77.5%, 67.9%, 56.8%, and 40.9%, respectively. Hazard ratios confirmed elevated mortality risks, particularly when both were present. Women had lower mortality than men in the United States, but higher mortality in Mexico. However, gender did not significantly modify the association between condition burden and survival in either country.DiscussionFive-year survival declines with condition burden in both countries, with lower absolute survival and steeper relative penalties in the U.S. Divergent gender patterns across countries reflect overall mortality differences rather than condition-specific modification, highlighting the need for tailored and integrated approaches to chronic disease and dementia care.

Diabetes mellitus and dementia are two of the most consequential chronic conditions in later life. Both are highly prevalent and progressive, and each is independently associated with increased mortality, disability, and care needs (Gudala et al., 2013). Their coexistence is common and clinically important: diabetes elevates subsequent risk of dementia (Barbiellini Amidei et al., 2021), and among people living with Alzheimer’s disease and related dementias (ADRD), coexisting diabetes worsens outcomes (Rojas-Alvarez et al., 2025), underlining multimorbidity as a key driver of survival losses (Huh et al., 2024; Titcomb et al., 2024). Vascular injury, chronic inflammation, and insulin resistance are proposed pathways by which diabetes may increase dementia risk and accelerate decline; conversely, cognitive impairment complicates diabetes self-management and continuity of care (Cao et al., 2024).

Mexican-origin populations in the United States and Mexico offer a conceptually compelling case for cross-national comparison of survival under multimorbidity. In Mexico, the estimated prevalence of diabetes reached 18.3% in 2022 (Basto-Abreu et al., 2023), with projections approaching 30.7% among adults aged 50 years and older by 2030 (Gonzalez-Gonzalez et al., 2017). Mexican-origin adults in the U.S. face elevated rates: the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) data from 2017 to 2020 show a diagnosed diabetes prevalence of 11.1% among Mexican or Mexican American adults, compared to 8.5% among non-Hispanic White adults (CDC, 2024). Consistent with these patterns, the Hispanic Community Health Study/Study of Latinos (HCHS/SOL) multi-site cohort documented a prevalence of 18.3% among adults of Mexican heritage aged 18–74, among the highest across Hispanic/Latino subgroups (Schneiderman et al., 2014). In both countries, women consistently show higher diabetes prevalence than men among Hispanic adults broadly (Pérez et al., 2024; Schneiderman et al., 2014).

Dementia prevalence is substantial and rising with population aging in both countries. In the United States, age- and sex-standardized prevalence among a national study of Medicare beneficiaries increased from 10.5% to 11.8% between 2015 and 2021, even as incidence declined over the same period (Blass et al., 2025). Hispanic/Latino older adults show elevated dementia risk relative to non-Hispanic Whites (Lin et al., 2021). In Mexico, dementia prevalence and incidence grew by approximately 203% between 1990 and 2019, reaching an age-standardized rate of 512 per 100,000 by 2019, with national burden concentrated at older ages and among women (Mejia-Arango & Gutierrez, 2011).

Mortality associated with diabetes and dementia represents a growing public health concern in both countries. Each condition independently elevates mortality risk among older adults (Blass et al., 2025; Gudala et al., 2013), and their co-occurrence confers a higher risk than either alone (Barbiellini Amidei et al., 2021). Gender differences in this mortality related to diabetes and dementia vary across national contexts, reflecting broader social, biological, and health-system factors. In the United States, women generally retain a survival advantage at older ages, including among those with diabetes and dementia, often attributed to better health care access, longevity, and differences in disease progression (Blass et al., 2025). In contrast, evidence from Mexico suggests weaker or reverse survival advantages for women, particularly in later life, where women face compounded disadvantages in socioeconomic status, pension coverage, and health care access (Serván-Mori et al., 2024). These gendered and cross-national patterns reflect, in part, the markedly different health care and social protection systems within which older adults age: near-universal Medicare coverage in the United States alongside fragmented specialty care (Rice et al., 2020), versus Mexico’s mixed public–private system with heterogeneous access and heavy reliance on family caregiving (Gómez-Dantés et al., 2024).

This study is guided by an integrated conceptual framework combining the Chronic Care Model with a health disparities perspective relevant to dementia and chronic disease management among aging Hispanic populations (Angel et al., 2022; National Institute on Minority Health and Health Disparities [NIMHD], 2023). Building on prior work, survival is conceptualized as the cumulative product of individual-level characteristics, such as chronic conditions, caregiving support, and socioeconomic resources, and structural and policy contexts that shape access to care and continuity of disease management (Rojas-Álvarez & Bokun, 2025). A cross-national comparison between the United States and Mexico therefore provides a theoretically motivated opportunity to examine how differences in health systems, social protection, and caregiving infrastructures are associated with mortality among older adults with diabetes, dementia, and their co-occurrence.

This study leverages two well-established longitudinal surveys in the United States (Hispanic Established Populations for the Epidemiologic Study of the Elderly [H-EPESE]) and in Mexico (Mexican Health and Aging Study [MHAS]). Although designed independently, they include harmonized measures that support standardized cross-national survival estimates among Mexican-origin older adults (Markides et al., 2024; Wong et al., 2017).

Most prior studies in each country examine diabetes or dementia survival in isolation using relative hazards (e.g., from Cox models), which are sensitive to proportional-hazards assumptions and can obscure cross-setting differences in baseline risk, and rarely focus on Mexican-origin older adults. The present study addresses this gap by quantifying and comparing standardized 5-year survival among adults aged ≥74 years with four condition profiles—no condition, diabetes only, dementia only, and dual conditions—using flexible parametric survival models with harmonized data from H-EPESE (United States) and MHAS (Mexico). The study hypothesizes that (1) survival will decline monotonically with condition burden in both countries; (2) absolute survival will be lower in the United States than in Mexico, with gender-specific survival patterns differing by country; and (3) gender will modify the association between condition burden and survival, with the expectation that the magnitude of the survival penalty associated with a higher condition burden may differ between women and men within each country. Drawing on prior evidence, we anticipate that women will exhibit a survival advantage relative to men in the United States but a weaker or reversed advantage in Mexico; however, we do not impose a specific directional hypothesis regarding whether condition-related survival penalties will be larger for one gender or the other within each country. As a secondary aim, the study assesses whether cross-standardizing covariates narrows but does not eliminate between-country differences, suggesting health-system contributions beyond compositional factors.

Method

Data and sample

This study drew on data from two representative longitudinal surveys of older Mexican-origin adults: the H-EPESE in the United States and the MHAS in Mexico. Although both were designed as panel studies, this analysis relied on a single wave from each: Wave 5 (2004–2005) of H-EPESE, linked to Medicare claims from 1999 to 2012 (Downer et al., 2019), and the 2003 wave of MHAS. Given the shared ethnic and cultural background of the target populations but different healthcare systems, these datasets were well-suited for the research purposes of this study. In addition, pre-reform waves were selected because of data availability and cross-national comparability. H-EPESE Wave 5 is the only wave with linkage to Medicare claims and mortality ascertainment suitable for this analysis, which determines the analytic window for both countries.

H-EPESE is a population-based study of Mexican-American adults aged 65 and older in five southwestern U.S. states: Arizona, California, Colorado, New Mexico, and Texas (Markides et al., 2009). The sample includes 2,069 respondents in Wave 5. The Medicare linkage was conducted directly by the Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services (CMS) under a data use agreement with the University of Texas Medical Branch. Individuals who did not consent to linkage (n = 554), lacked continuous Medicare enrollment in the 3 years prior to the interview (n = 501), or were not enrolled for at least one quarter afterward (n = 10) were excluded. After further excluding observations with missing information on key condition variables (n = 9), the final U.S. analytic sample comprises 995 respondents (see Figure 1A).

Flowchart of sample selection process for (A) U.S. sample, (B) Mexico sample. H-EPESE = Hispanic Established Populations for the Epidemiologic Study of the Elderly; MHAS = Mexican Health and Aging Study.

The MHAS is an ongoing, nationally representative panel study of adults aged 50 and older (Wong et al., 2017), with waves in 2001, 2003, 2012, 2015, 2018, and 2021. The MHAS analytic sample was drawn from the 2003 wave, which included surviving members of the original 2001 cohort and their spouses or partners. The full 2003 sample consists of 13,497 interviews, including proxies. The study was approved by the Institutional Review Boards or Ethics Committees of the University of Texas San Antonio in the United States, the Instituto Nacional de Estadística y Geografia (INEGI), and the Instituto Nacional de Salud Publica (INSP) in Mexico. To maximize comparability with the H-EPESE sample, the MHAS analytic sample was restricted to respondents aged 74 and older (n = 2,916). After excluding those with missing key variables (n = 26), the final MHAS sample includes 2,890 respondents (see Figure 1B).

Measures

The outcome variable is mortality, defined as death during follow-up and tracked for up to 5 years after baseline. For U.S. participants, vital status was ascertained from linkages to the Medicare Beneficiary Summary File and the National Death Index within the Medicare administrative data. For Mexican participants, it was obtained from the MHAS follow-up interviews and mortality modules.

The key exposure is the condition group, defined by the presence of diabetes and/or dementia. Diabetes was based on self-reported physician diagnosis in both datasets. In contrast, dementia was operationalized using survey-based measures of likely dementia rather than physician diagnosis. In MHAS, for direct interviews, participants were classified as having dementia based on performance on cognitive tasks from the Cross-Cultural Cognitive Examination (CCCE) together with instrumental activities of daily living (IADL) limitations (Mejia-Arango et al., 2021). Specifically, respondents were classified as having dementia if they scored at or below 1.5 standard deviations on two or more cognitive domains and had one or more IADL limitations (Mejia Arango & Gutierrez, 2011). For proxy interviews, participants were assessed using the Informant Questionnaire on Cognitive Decline in the Elderly (IQCODE) (Jorm, 2004), with a cutoff of 3.4 or higher indicating dementia (Mejia Arango et al., 2021). In H-EPESE, directly interviewed respondents were classified as having likely dementia if they had a Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) score below 18 and two or more IADL limitations (Vásquez et al., 2022). Proxy-interviewed respondents were classified as likely dementia when the informant reported mental incapacitation in carrying out basic activities of daily life, including giving informed consent (Mejia‐arango et al., 2020). For simplicity, this category is referred to as dementia throughout, though it reflects survey-based cognitive and functional criteria. The analyses adjusted for age, years of education, hypertension, heart attack, and stroke, as these covariates are associated with both dementia-related processes and all-cause mortality (Kuźma et al., 2018; Livingston et al., 2024) and may otherwise confound the association between condition group and survival.

Mortality analyses

Flexible parametric survival models (Batyrbekova et al., 2022; Lambert & Royston, 2009; Royston & Parmar, 2002) were fit rather than Cox proportional hazard models, as the primary estimands are standardized absolute survival probabilities at specific time points. Absolute survival is directly interpretable and more comparable across populations with differing baseline risks, whereas hazard ratios can be misleading when the proportional hazards assumption is violated (Stensrud & Hernán, 2020). By parameterizing the log-cumulative hazard using restricted cubic splines, these models produce smooth survival functions stable in sparsely populated tail regions and facilitate extrapolation to 5 years. G-computation was applied to estimate the average population-level survival for each diagnostic status (Wang et al., 2017), averaging individual-level predictions over each country’s covariate distribution to obtain marginal, standardized survival by condition group. Models including an interaction between condition group and gender were additionally fit in each country, and g-computation was applied to estimate gender-stratified marginal survival probabilities. Five-year survival is reported with risk differences (RD) and risk ratios (RR) relative to the no-condition reference group. This approach aligns with the cross-national aim to report absolute, policy-relevant survival levels and avoids the limitations of Cox models, which leave the baseline hazard unspecified (Brown et al., 2019) and yield stepwise survival curves with increased uncertainty in the tail when risk sets thin out. All analyses were conducted using Stata 18 (StataCorp, 2023).

Sensitivity analyses

To assess the suitability of a modeling approach, two standardization diagnostics were implemented. Predictions from both countries were first standardized to a pooled, country-balanced covariate distribution. Second, predictions were cross-standardized by applying each country’s model to the other’s covariate distribution. These approaches follow established methods for decomposing cross-national survival differences into compositional and structural components (Hernán & Robins, 2006). As a robustness check, survival estimates from Cox models were compared to those from the flexible parametric models. To evaluate potential selection bias from the age restriction, the analysis was replicated in MHAS among adults aged 60 and older, and survival gradients were compared to those from the primary sample of adults aged 74 and older (Hernán et al., 2004).

Results

Table 1 presents descriptive statistics for analytic samples of older Hispanic adults in the United States (N = 995) and Mexico (N = 2,890). In both countries, mortality rates were strongly patterned by condition group, rising monotonically with the presence of diabetes, dementia, or both conditions. In the United States, mortality was 25.6% among respondents with neither condition (n = 555), 39.8% among those with diabetes only (n = 256), 57.0% among those with dementia only (n = 121), and 63.5% among those with both conditions (n = 63). In Mexico, the corresponding gradient was slightly attenuated but remained evident: mortality rates were 21.8% among those with no condition (n = 1,820), 29.2% for diabetes only (n = 507), 52.1% for dementia only (n = 411), and 59.2% for dual conditions (n = 152). Group characteristics further highlight cross-national differences. Respondents with dementia were the oldest on average in both countries. Education levels were low in both samples, averaging under 5 years in the United States and 3 years in Mexico. Gender distributions varied by country: in the United States, women made up the majority across condition groups, whereas in Mexico, the proportion of women declined with increasing condition burden, falling to 26% among those with dual conditions.

Using flexible parametric survival models adjusted for age, sex, years of education, hypertension, heart attack, and stroke, a pronounced survival gradient was observed relative to those with no condition in both countries; Table 2 presents country-specific coefficients on the log-hazard scale, hazard ratios, and 95% confidence intervals. In the United States, the hazard of death was 84% higher for diabetes-only (HR = 1.84, 95% CI [1.421, 2.372], p < .001), more than doubled for dementia-only (HR = 2.45, 95% CI [1.787, 3.347], p < .001), and nearly tripled for dual conditions (HR = 2.65, 95% CI [1.781, 3.952], p < .001) relative to the no-condition group. In Mexico, the severity ordering was consistent, though magnitudes were somewhat smaller: diabetes-only faced 54% higher hazards (HR = 1.54, 95% CI [1.272, 1.865], p < .001), dementia-only more than doubled (HR = 2.29, 95% CI [1.893, 2.776], p < .001), and dual conditions were associated with nearly a four-fold increase (HR = 3.74, 95% CI [2.937, 4.763], p < .001). Gender associations differed markedly by country. In the United States, women had significantly lower mortality hazards (HR = 0.73, 95% CI [0.585, 0.901], p < .01), consistent with a survival advantage. In Mexico, however, women faced higher mortality hazards (HR = 1.26, 95% CI [1.093, 1.451], p < .01). Standardized marginal survival curves illustrating these patterns are presented in Supplementary Figure 1. Estimates from the Cox models were consistent in both direction and magnitude (results available upon request).

Two additional sensitivity analyses supported the robustness of the main findings. First, standardization diagnostics, including pooled 50/50 and cross-standardization approaches, show that the rank ordering by condition burden and Mexico’s relatively milder survival penalties persist after accounting for compositional differences between countries (Supplementary Table 1). Second, replicating the Mexico analysis using adults aged 60 and older yielded patterns consistent with those observed in the 74 and older sample (Supplementary Table 2), suggesting that the findings are not an artifact of survivor bias introduced by the age restriction.

Discussion

This study provides cross-national evidence on mortality among older Hispanic adults with varying condition profiles in the United States and Mexico. Drawing on two independent datasets, three findings are highlighted. First, survival declines monotonically with condition burden. This gradient underscores the cumulative survival penalty of multimorbidity regardless of national context. Second, absolute 5-year survival is lower in the United States than in Mexico across condition groups, with the widest gap observed among individuals with dual conditions. Third, gender mortality patterns diverge by country, though gender does not significantly modify the condition burden–survival gradient in either country.

Despite near-universal Medicare coverage, survival outcomes in the United States are consistently poorer compared to Mexico, and relative penalties by diagnosis group are steeper. Insurance coverage alone may not ensure care continuity and accessibility. For Hispanic Medicare beneficiaries, factors such as cost-sharing, plan complexity (Parts B/D and supplemental coverage), language barriers, and fragmented referral networks may raise the shadow price of healthcare and widen management gaps for individuals with multimorbidity (Salinas et al., 2013). In contrast, Mexico’s reliance on multigenerational caregiving and household production of health may partially offset gaps left by limited formal coverage (Aguila et al., 2019).

Compositional differences in the analytic samples also help explain observed patterns. The U.S. sample is older on average and has a higher prevalence of stroke, both of which are associated with elevated mortality. Although models adjust for age and other covariates, persistent cross-national contrasts suggest a potential role of residual confounding from cohort effects or unmeasured risk exposures. More broadly, cross-national differences in exposure to diabetes and dementia likely reflect variation in life-course social conditions and cardiometabolic risk. In both countries, lower educational attainment, hypertension, diabetes, stroke, and other vascular risks are closely linked to later-life cognitive impairment and dementia, while obesity, physical inactivity, and limited access to timely diagnosis and treatment can increase diabetes burden and its downstream consequences (Livingston et al., 2024). These processes may operate differently across the two settings. In Mexico, underdiagnosis likely obscures part of the true diabetes burden, and prior MHAS-based work suggests that failing to distinguish undiagnosed cases can underestimate associations between diabetes and health outcomes (Downer et al., 2016). In this study, it could have attenuated the observed survival penalties associated with diabetes in Mexico by shifting some higher-risk individuals into the no-condition or dementia-only groups, thereby making the Mexico gradients appear less steep than they might be with complete diabetes ascertainment. Among Mexican-origin older adults in the United States, by contrast, longstanding disparities in diabetes prevalence, cardiovascular risk, and disease control may contribute to a higher cumulative metabolic burden by late life (Cheng et al., 2019).

The gender-specific mortality patterns observed in this study warrant further discussion. In the United States, women had lower mortality hazards than men, consistent with the well-documented women’s survival advantage in later life. In Mexico, however, women faced higher mortality hazards than men, reversing the expected pattern. The reversal of the women’s survival advantage in Mexico likely reflects structural conditions confronting older Mexican women, including lower levels of education, weaker attachment to the labor market, inadequate retirement income security, and constrained financial autonomy (García-Hernández et al., 2025), alongside disproportionate caregiving responsibilities (Mendez-Luck & Anthony, 2016) and limited access to transportation or reliable health information (Mendez-Luck et al., 2008). System-level barriers, including under-recognition of cognitive decline, inadequate support for diabetes management amid cognitive impairment, and variation in caregiving capacity, may compound these challenges (Avila et al., 2021). Notably, interaction analyses provided little evidence that the survival gradient across condition groups differs by gender in either country, suggesting that the cross-national divergence in gender patterns reflects broader cross-national differences in overall mortality risk rather than gender-specific modification of the condition burden-survival association. These findings underscore the importance of considering gender, aging, and health system design when interpreting cross-national mortality differences in later life.

This study has several limitations. First, as an observational analysis, the findings reflect associations rather than causal effects. Unmeasured confounders, such as lifelong health behaviors, treatment adherence, and disease severity, may bias estimates. This risk was mitigated by harmonizing covariates ex ante and using flexible parametric models with baseline adjustment. Future research would benefit from richer clinical data, prospective designs, and quasi-experimental approaches. Second, the data analyzed reflect a pre-reform period in both countries, driven in part by data availability, particularly the linkage and mortality follow-up structure of H-EPESE Wave 5 in the United States. Accordingly, the findings should be interpreted as estimates of cross-national survival under earlier policy contexts rather than under current health care environments facing older adults in either country. Future research should examine whether these cross-national survival patterns persist in the post-reform context, as both countries have undergone substantial changes in health care coverage and delivery since the analytic period. Third, the use of self-reported diagnoses for diabetes to ensure cross-national comparability may introduce measurement error. Fourth, the dementia measures rely on dataset-specific algorithms rather than a single harmonized instrument, which limits strict comparability. However, the analyses proceed in parallel, estimating models separately within each country rather than pooling data, which is well suited for assessing whether associations between condition burden and survival are consistent across distinct national contexts (Kobayashi et al., 2024). Fifth, the small number of respondents with dementia in the U.S. sample likely reflects survival selection into the interview wave, affecting the precision of estimates for this group and limiting power to detect gender-by-condition group heterogeneity in interaction models, which may partly explain the non-significant interaction terms.

Despite these limitations, this study identifies consistent and policy-relevant patterns in survival among older Hispanic adults with diabetes, dementia, and dual conditions in the United States and Mexico. The marked survival penalties associated with combined metabolic and cognitive burden highlight the importance of healthcare systems that can provide continuous, coordinated care for older adults with complex needs. The lower absolute survival observed in the United States, together with divergent gender patterns across countries, suggests that broader health care and social-care arrangements may contribute to later-life survival. The absence of significant gender-by-condition interactions suggests that the survival penalties associated with diabetes, dementia, and their co-occurrence are comparably severe for both women and men in each country. The findings support greater attention to care continuity, access to chronic disease management, and integration of cognitive and medical care in both countries.
