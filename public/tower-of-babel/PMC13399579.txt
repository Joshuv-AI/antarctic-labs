Radiotherapy and Immunotherapy In Hepatocellular Carcinoma: Priming the Tumor Immune Microenvironment From Bench To Bedside
Authors: Zhigang Fu, Yang Zhang, Zuomin Wang, Zongjuan Li, Shisuo Du
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractHepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) remains a leading cause of mortality from cancer worldwide. Surgical resection is the main curative strategy for early‐stage HCC; however, many advanced cases require multidisciplinary management, including radiotherapy and immunotherapy. Recent advances in radiation delivery have enabled precise tumor targeting while sparing healthy liver tissue, thereby expanding the role of radiotherapy beyond palliation. Moreover, the intricate effect of radiotherapy on the tumor microenvironment (TME) is being increasingly understood. In this review, we systematically examine the bidirectional immunomodulatory effects of radiotherapy on the TME and the biological rationale for combining radiotherapy with immunotherapy in HCC. We also summarize the preclinical and clinical evidence supporting radiotherapy‐immunotherapy combinations in HCC and discuss future directions. Continued translational and clinical research is necessary to fully realize the therapeutic potential of this strategy in HCC management.Keywords: Hepatocellular carcinoma, radiotherapy, immunotherapy, tumor microenvironment

INTRODUCTION TO HEPATOCELLULAR CARCINOMA (HCC) AND ITS IMMUNOSUPPRESSIVE TUMOR MICROENVIRONMENT (TME)

The liver is an immune‐tolerant organ that is constantly subjected to a significant antigen burden from the gastrointestinal tract. Therefore, mechanisms have been developed to maintain a level of immune tolerance to balance appropriate immune reactions against pathogens while avoiding an inflammatory response to non‐pathological gut commensals. The architecture of the liver, which lacks a basement membrane in the liver sinusoids and features a high density of immune cells, requires complex feedback systems to prevent chronic inflammation.

The tumor microenvironment (TME) of hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) constitutes a complex and dynamic ecosystem comprising tumor, stromal, and immune cells. The highly immunosuppressive milieu promotes tumor progression by reprogramming immune cells, thereby enabling immune evasion and suppressing anti‐tumor immunity. 1 Central to this immunosuppressive landscape are tumor‐associated macrophages (TAMs), which often polarize toward an M2 phenotype that fosters tumor growth, angiogenesis, and immune suppression. These TAMs secrete cytokines such as interleukin‐10 (IL‐10) and transforming growth factor‐beta (TGF‐β), which further suppress the activity of cytotoxic T cells and natural killer (NK) cells, thereby reinforcing the immunosuppressive environment. 2

The immunosuppressive network is further strengthened by regulatory T cells (Tregs) and myeloid‐derived suppressor cells (MDSCs). 3 , 4 As a critical subset of CD4+ T cells, Tregs, defined by expression of the transcription factor FoxP3, are co‐opted by tumors to dampen immune responses. They exert immunosuppressive effects via diverse mechanisms, including secretion of inhibitory cytokines such as IL‐10 and TGF‐β, as well as direct cell‐to‐cell contact mediated by checkpoint molecules such as programmed cell death protein 1 (PD‐1) and cytotoxic T‐lymphocyte‐associated protein 4 (CTLA‐4). Collectively, these mechanisms inhibit the activation and proliferation of effector T cells. 3 Complementing Treg activity, MDSCs expand from immature myeloid precursors under the influence of tumor‐derived factors. This heterogeneous population includes monocytic (M‐MDSCs) and granulocytic (G‐MDSCs) subsets, each utilizing distinct immunosuppressive strategies. M‐MDSCs primarily produce an IL‐1 receptor antagonist (IL‐1RA) to inhibit pro‐inflammatory responses, whereas G‐MDSCs express arginase, which depletes L‐arginine and impairs T cell function. 4 MDSCs also enhance the immunosuppressive microenvironment by producing TGF‐β and other factors that promote Treg expansion, establishing a positive feedback loop. 5 Recruitment of these immunosuppressive cells to the hepatic TME is orchestrated by specific chemokine signals. Notably, the stroma‐derived chemokine CCL2 facilitates this process by binding to CCR4 on Tregs and CCR2 on MDSCs 6 Once recruited, Tregs and MDSCs interact with TAMs and other immune components, cementing a robust and self‐reinforcing immunosuppressive network that ultimately promotes tumor progression and metastasis. This intricate crosstalk between tumor and immune cells is also modulated by immune checkpoint molecules, including PD‐1 and CTLA‐4. These checkpoints are upregulated on both tumor and immune cells, leading to T cell exhaustion and impaired anti‐tumor immunity. Tumor cells also secrete exosomes carrying immunosuppressive molecules such as PD‐L1 and TGF‐β, which can directly inhibit cytotoxic T and NK cells. These exosomes contribute to systemic immunosuppression in patients with HCC, further complicating treatment outcomes. 7

Metabolic reprogramming within the HCC TME also substantially contributes to immune suppression. Tumor cells exhibit high glycolytic activity, resulting in lactate accumulation, which impairs the functions of T and NK cells. Competition for essential nutrients, such as glucose and amino acids, further restricts the capacity of immune cells to mount an effective anti‐tumor response. 8

Understanding these multidimensional interactions is critical for the development of novel therapeutic approaches, such as combining immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) with TAM‐targeting agents or metabolic modulators, to overcome the immunosuppressive environment in HCC. 9

IMPACT OF RADIOTHERAPY (RT) ON THE IMMUNOSUPPRESSIVE MICROENVIRONMENT IN HCC

The anatomical proximity of hepatic malignancies to critical structures, such as the functional liver parenchyma and duodenum, demands exceptional precision in radiation delivery. Historically, the role of RT in HCC has been largely confined to palliation owing to technological constraints. However, technological advances, including the CyberKnife, TomoTherapy, MR‐LINAC, and proton therapy, have expanded its applications to include curative‐intent treatments. A comprehensive analysis of 17 studies (n = 1,889) reported that stereotactic body radiotherapy (SBRT) achieved 5‐year local control and overall survival (OS) rates of 82% and 40%, respectively. 10 Additional studies have also supported SBRT as an effective alternative to radiofrequency ablation for small inoperable HCC. 11 , 12

Radiation exerts anti‐tumor effects primarily by inducing direct DNA damage, particularly double‐strand breaks. In addition to direct cytotoxicity, growing evidence indicates that RT modulates the TME through multiple mechanisms, including innate immune activation via the cGAS–STING pathway, DNA damage response (DDR), immunogenic cell death, reactive oxygen species generation, and release of soluble cytokines. 13

In HCC, preclinical studies have confirmed that RT‐induced accumulation of double‐stranded DNA (dsDNA) in hepatoma cells activates the cGAS–STING signaling pathway, 14 which promotes immune cell infiltration and enhances the cytotoxicity of CD8+ T cells. 15 Consistent with these findings, clinical samples from patients with irradiated HCC show elevated interferon‐β (IFN‐β) expression, along with increased infiltration of CD11c+MHCII+CD141+ antigen‐presenting cells and cytotoxic CD8+CD103+CD39+ tissue‐resident lymphocytes. Notably, enhanced IFN‐β signaling is associated with improved clinical outcomes. 16 Paradoxically, cGAS–STING activation can also promote immunosuppressive effects. For instance, Du et al. demonstrated that radiation‐induced IRF3 activation upregulates PD‐L1 expression in HCC cells, thereby attenuating T cell‐mediated cytotoxicity. 14 Clinical observations further confirmed that RT induces PD‐L1 upregulation, which is linked to poor prognosis in patients with HCC. 17 Moreover, excessive cGAS–STING activation in hepatic non‐parenchymal cells may contribute to radiation‐induced liver injury. 18 , 19

RT also triggers a series of DDRs in tumor cells, activating DNA damage sensors and repair pathways to mitigate radiation‐induced damage. 20 21 By applying machine learning to single‐cell RNA sequencing data, Hong et al. quantified DDR activity in HCC and identified distinct TME patterns stratified by DDR status. 22 Targeting key DDR components such as RecQ‐like helicase 4 (RECQL4) has been shown to enhance radiosensitivity by promoting dendritic cell activation and CD8+ T cell recruitment. 23 Similarly, olaparib, an inhibitor of PARP‐mediated alternative end joining, converts single‐strand breaks into double‐strand breaks during DNA replication. When combined with RT, olaparib remodels the immunosuppressive TME through type I interferon induction and production of T‐cell chemokines (CXCL9/10/11 and CCL5), thereby potentiating neoantigen‐specific CD8+ T cell responses. 24

Radiation fractionation parameters critically influence immunogenic outcomes. Zhao et al. demonstrated that a single high‐dose fraction (e.g., 10 Gy × 1) uniquely reprogrammed the HCC immune microenvironment by initiating a self‐amplifying loop of immunogenic cell death. This regimen preferentially induces tumor cell necroptosis, which is characterized by plasma membrane disintegration and substantial release of intracellular dsDNA. The liberated dsDNA activates the cGAS–STING pathway in TAMs, triggering concurrent production of IFN‐β and TNF‐α. Importantly, iRhom2 upregulation in macrophages enhances both STING signaling stability and TNF‐α secretion. The resulting TNF‐α surge further propagates tumor cell necroptosis, establishing a sustained positive feedback circuit that continuously amplifies dsDNA release and cGAS–STING activation, ultimately enhancing anti‐tumor immunity. 25 In contrast, excessively high single fractions (e.g., 12–18 Gy) can induce the expression of Trex1 exonuclease, which degrades cytosolic DNA and thereby diminishes immunogenicity. 26 Although highly relevant, investigations into how current dose‑fractionation regimens modulate the immune microenvironment in HCC remain largely confined to preclinical studies, with no clinical data yet available. Therefore, this area warrants increased attention and further research.

ICIS IN HCC

Immunotherapy has substantially reshaped the treatment landscape for advanced HCC, with ICIs being integrated across various therapeutic settings. Firstly, in the second‐line setting, a meta‐analysis of the KEYNOTE‐240 and KEYNOTE‐394 trials confirmed that pembrolizumab provides clinically meaningful improvements over placebo in patients with advanced HCC after prior therapy, demonstrating significant benefits in OS (hazard ratio [HR]: 0.79, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 0.67–0.93), progression‐free survival (PFS; HR: 0.76, 95% CI: 0.64–0.89), and objective response rate (15.4% vs. 2.8%). 27 These results firmly support pembrolizumab monotherapy as a standard care option after sorafenib failure. Second, tislelizumab has emerged as an effective first‐line monotherapy. The global, phase III RATIONALE‐301 trial demonstrated that tislelizumab was non‐inferior to sorafenib, with a median OS of 15.9 months versus 14.1 months, respectively, in patients with unresectable HCC. 28

Building on the activity of monotherapy, combination strategies that target complementary pathways have yielded superior outcomes. Overexpression of the vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) is a key immunosuppressive pathway in HCC. 29 Anti‐VEGF agents can counteract this suppression and enhance T‐cell infiltration, thereby potentiating the efficacy of PD‐1/PD‐L1 blockade. 30 This mechanistic synergy was convincingly translated into clinical success in the IMbrave150 trial, which established atezolizumab plus bevacizumab as a superior first‐line regimen over sorafenib, showing a significantly improved 12‐month OS (67.2% vs. 54.6%) and median PFS (6.8 vs. 4.3 months). 31 Subsequent studies have continued to validate the benefit of ICI and targeted therapy combinations. 32 , 33

Dual immune checkpoint inhibition is a powerful alternative for patients who are ineligible for antiangiogenic therapies, such as those with a high bleeding risk. This approach leverages the nonredundant mechanisms of PD‐1 and CTLA‐4 inhibition. Recent phase III trials have confirmed significant survival advantages with both tremelimumab plus durvalumab (HIMALAYA regimen) 34 and nivolumab plus ipilimumab (CheckMate 9DW) 35 compared with sorafenib or lenvatinib, solidifying this modality as another first‐line standard.

RATIONALE AND CLINICAL EVIDENCE FOR RT COMBINED WITH IMMUNOTHERAPY IN HCC

Rationale for combining RT and Immunotherapy

The introduction of ICIs has markedly expanded treatment options for HCC. However, the objective response rates to ICIs remain limited, highlighting the need for more effective combination strategies. In contrast, RT, particularly SBRT for early stage lesions, achieves outstanding local control, with a 5‐year rate of 82%. 10 Nevertheless, the OS remains unsatisfactory owing to progression outside the irradiated field, underscoring the importance of combining RT with effective systemic therapy. 36 This complementary spatial effect provides a strong rationale for combining the two modalities.

At the mechanistic level, as previously discussed, RT enhances tumor immunogenicity and remodels the local TME, which is essential for initiating anti‐tumor immune responses. 13 RT also triggers immunosuppressive changes, most notably the upregulation of PD‐L1 expression. This PD‐L1 induction is mediated through several distinct mechanisms: (I) activation of DDR signaling 37 ; (II) engagement of the cGAS‐STING pathway 14 ; (III) interferon‐gamma (IFN‐γ) signaling 38 ; and (IV) epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) pathway activation. 39 Consistent with these findings, clinical evidence in HCC shows increased PD‐L1 expression in tumor tissues after RT, which correlates inversely with CD8+ T‐cell infiltration. 17 Preclinical studies further demonstrated that combining RT with PD‐1/PD‐L1 blockade reverses this immune exhaustion and yields superior anti‐tumor outcomes. 14

Another intriguing line of evidence comes from studies comparing tumor models in different anatomical sites. Although mice with subcutaneous tumors responded effectively to anti–PD‐L1 therapy, those with hepatic tumors showed complete resistance. Further investigation revealed that liver metastases sequester activated CD8+ T cells from the systemic circulation. In the liver, antigen‐specific Fas+ CD8+ T cells undergo apoptosis upon interacting with FasL+ CD11b+ F4/80+ monocyte‐derived macrophages. 40 This phenomenon suggests that liver metastases can induce systemic immune deficiency in preclinical models. Importantly, liver‐directed RT was shown to eliminate these immunosuppressive hepatic macrophages, improve hepatic T‐cell survival, and counteract the “siphoning” of T cells by the liver. These findings elucidate the immunosuppressive mechanisms unique to the hepatic microenvironment and highlight the critical biological rationale for combining RT with immunotherapy for HCC.

Resectable or convertible HCC

The rationale for combining RT with immunotherapy, along with encouraging preclinical data, has prompted the initiation of clinical trials on radioimmunotherapy combinations for HCC. Key clinical trials investigating the combination of RT and immunotherapy for resectable or convertible HCC are summarized in Table 1. In a prospective single‐center, single‐arm phase 1b trial involving 20 patients with HCC at BCLC 0‐A stage, the efficacy and safety of neoadjuvant sintilimab combined with SBRT were evaluated. 41 The treatment regimen consisted of neoadjuvant, surgery, and adjuvant phases. In the neoadjuvant setting, patients received three fractions of SBRT at a total dose of 24 Gy concurrently with two cycles of tislelizumab. Radical hepatectomy was performed approximately 50 days after the initiation of neoadjuvant therapy. Subsequently, adjuvant monotherapy with tislelizumab was initiated at four weeks postoperatively and continued at three‐week intervals for up to one year. The objective response rate (ORR) was 63.2%, the pathological complete response (pCR) rate was 10.5%, and the disease control rate (DCR) reached 100%. During neoadjuvant treatment, 40% of patients experienced grade three treatment‐related adverse events (TRAEs), primarily myelosuppression (e.g., lymphopenia and neutropenia). No grade 4–5 TRAEs occurred. All adverse events resolved spontaneously without corticosteroid intervention and no surgical delays or cancellations were observed. All patients who underwent surgery achieved R0 resection. At the time of analysis, with a median follow‐up of only four months, one patient experienced recurrence at three months post‐surgery, who was subsequently managed with radiofrequency ablation and achieved disease‐free survival thereafter.

Another prospective, single‐center, single‐arm phase II trial involving 30 patients with locally advanced, resectable HCC accompanied by macrovascular invasion (MVI) investigated a defined protocol: patients received neoadjuvant tislelizumab (three cycles) concurrently with intensity‐modulated radiotherapy (IMRT; 45 Gy in 15 fractions over three weeks), underwent resectability assessment at nine weeks, and, if eligible, proceeded to radical surgery followed by 1 year of adjuvant tislelizumab. Therefore, a conversion rate of 50% was achieved. Among the 15 patients who underwent surgery, 66.7% attained pCR or major pathological response (MPR), and 53.3% remained recurrence‐free at a median postoperative follow‐up of 21.77 months. 42

Conversion therapy offers a potential pathway to resectability for patients initially diagnosed with advanced, unresectable HCC. In a prospective multicenter, single‐arm phase II clinical trial evaluating the START‐FIT regimen (transarterial chemoembolization [TACE] on day 1, followed by SBRT with 27.5–40 Gy in five fractions beginning on day 28, and avelumab at 10 mg/kg every two weeks starting 14 days after RT) as conversion therapy for locally advanced HCC. Finally, 55% (18/33) of the enrolled patients met the criteria for curative treatment and 12% (4/33) underwent curative interventions (two surgical resections and two radiofrequency ablations). The median OS was 30.3 months. Grade 3 or higher TRAEs occurred in 33% of the patients (11/33), including transient transaminase elevation after TACE (15%, 5/33) and immune‐related hepatitis or dermatitis (15%, 5/33). 43

Advanced/Unresectable HCC

Combination of RT and immunotherapy

For patients with advanced HCC lacking conversion potential, therapeutic efforts should prioritize prolonging OS. Clinical studies investigating the combination of RT and immunotherapy for unresectable HCC are summarized in Table 2. A prospective multicenter, single‐arm, phase II trial evaluated the efficacy and safety of RT combined with nivolumab in 50 patients with advanced HCC complicated by MVI. The treatment regimen consisted of nivolumab administered at a dose of 3 mg/kg every 2 weeks concurrently with radiotherapy delivered at a median dose of 50 Gy in 10 fractions. The results showed a median PFS of 5.6 months and a median OS of 15.2 months (90% CI: 10.8–19.6). The ORR was 36.0% (90% CI: 24.7–48.6), and the DCR was 74.0% (90% CI: 61.9–83.9), representing a significant improvement over historical data from the CheckMate‐040 trial of nivolumab monotherapy (median PFS: 4.0 months, ORR: 20%, DCR: 64%). Grade 3/4 TRAEs occurred in 12.0% of patients, with no treatment‐related deaths, confirming the regimen as both effective and safe in this population. 44 Furthermore, a survival advantage was suggested for proton beam radiotherapy over IMRT, with median OS of 16.9 months versus 9.1 months, respectively.

In a phase II trial evaluating palliative SBRT (30–50 Gy in 10 fractions) combined with camrelizumab in 21 patients with unresectable HCC, similar outcomes were observed: the median OS was 14.2 months (95% CI: 7.2–21.2), the median PFS was 5.8 months (95% CI: 4.2–7.4), and the ORR was 52.4%. 45 Although most studies administer RT and immunotherapy concurrently, one study demonstrated the efficacy of a sequential approach. In this regimen, anti‐PD‐1/PD‐L1 therapy was initiated 4 weeks after SBRT (median dose 35 Gy in five fractions, range: 27.5–45 Gy) and was discontinued upon achievement of a complete response (CR), which occurred in 29 of 63 patients (46%) with unresectable HCC. 46 Notably, those achieving CR did not undergo surgical intervention and discontinued immunotherapy, supporting a “watch‐and‐wait” strategy in selected cases.

SBRT has also been utilized as salvage therapy after failure of prior immunotherapy, achieving disease control rates exceeding 70%, indicating its potential as an effective option following systemic treatment failure. 47 Another prospective single‐arm, single‐center trial evaluating SBRT (48–60 Gy in 5–10 fractions) combined with sintilimab (200 mg every 3 weeks for up to 12 months or until disease progression) demonstrated promising outcomes in patients with recurrent or oligometastatic HCC: among 25 patients, both the ORR and DCR reached 96%, with a median PFS of 19.7 months after a median follow‐up of 21.9 months, and a median OS that was not reached. Only three patients (12%) experienced grade 3 adverse events. 48 Subsequently, two retrospective propensity score‐matched studies further established the superiority of RT combined with ICIs over either modality alone. 49 , 50

Combination Of Rt And Dual Immunotherapy

The synergistic combination of anti‐PD‐1/L1 and anti‐CTLA‐4 agents targeting the effector and priming phases of immunity, respectively, enhance systemic immune activation when used with RT. Juloori et al. compared the dual immunotherapy approach, combining nivolumab (240 mg every 2 weeks) with ipilimumab (1 mg/kg every 6 weeks) and SBRT (40 Gy in five fractions), to single‐agent immunotherapy combined with RT, reporting superior efficacy (ORR: 57% vs. 31%; median PFS: 11.6 vs. 2.7 months; median OS: 41.6 vs. 4.7 months; all P < 0.05) at the cost of an increase in grade 3 adverse events (71.4% vs. 50.0%). 51

Combination Of Rt, Immunotherapy, And Anti‐Angiogenic Targeted Therapy

The combination of RT, immunotherapy, and anti‐angiogenic therapy delivers a multifaceted effect on cancer cells. RT acts as a localized primer for in situ vaccination, whereas ICIs alleviate systemic immunosuppression. Concurrently, anti‐angiogenic agents foster a favorable immunomodulatory milieu via vascular normalization and exert their own systemic cytotoxic effects. Early phase prospective studies have provided the initial proof of this concept. A multicenter single‐arm prospective trial evaluated IMRT (52–56 Gy/26–28 fractions) combined with atezolizumab and bevacizumab in 30 patients with unresectable HCC and extrahepatic portal vein tumor thrombus (ePVTT). 52 The regimen yielded a remarkable ORR of 76.6% per RECIST 1.1, with a median OS of 9.8 months and a median PFS of 8.0 months. The toxicity was manageable, with neutropenia (46.7%, mostly grade 1–2) and hypertension (16.7%, grade 3/4) identified as notable adverse events, and no treatment‐related deaths were reported. This study highlights the potential of combining localized RT with systemic therapy to achieve high response rates in this high‐risk population; however, larger confirmatory studies are warranted.

To further strengthen these findings, a multicenter, open‐label, randomized phase II trial directly compared camrelizumab and apatinib, with or without SBRT in 60 treatment‐naïve patients with HCC and PVTT. Patients in the SBRT arm received 36–40 Gy in 6–8 fractions, whereas all patients in both arms received concurrent camrelizumab and apatinib. The results demonstrated a clear survival advantage for the SBRT‐containing arm, with a significantly improved median OS (12.7 vs. 8.6 months) and PFS (4.6 vs. 2.5 months). The ORR more than doubled in the SBRT group (47.5% vs. 20.0%), confirming that the addition of RT synergized with dual‐agent systemic therapy to enhance the anti‐tumor efficacy, with an acceptable safety profile. 53

Retrospective analyses, including propensity score‐matched studies, have consistently corroborated the benefits of this trimodal approach (RT + ICI + anti‐angiogenic therapy) over dual systemic therapy alone. One analysis of patients with PVTT found that those receiving PVTT‐directed RT followed by ICIs and bevacizumab had a significantly longer median PFS (9.6 vs. 5.4 months) and higher ORR (48.94% vs. 27.66%) than those in the non‐RT group. 54 The study also suggested that RT may modulate the immunosuppressive TME, as evidenced by favorable changes in the CD4/CD8 T‐cell ratios and inflammatory markers post‐radiation. Similarly, other single‐center retrospective studies reported superior outcomes with triple therapy, demonstrating significantly improved ORR (75.9% vs. 24.1%), PFS (8.3 vs. 4.2 months), and OS (not reached vs. 9.7 months), with no significant increase in severe adverse events. 55 , 56 A comparative analysis from a Chinese center further showed that the combination of SBRT, lenvatinib, and a PD‐1 inhibitor led to an impressive median OS and PFS of 31.2 and 15.6 months, respectively, significantly outperforming the combination of SBRT and lenvatinib. 57

Combination Of Yttrium‐90(Y‐90) Radioembolization And Immunotherapy

In 2018, early evidence emerged from a case report documenting a dramatic biochemical and radiological response in a patient treated with Y‐90, followed by nivolumab, culminating in a successful curative‐intent hepatectomy with a pCR. 58 This proof‐of‐concept was subsequently explored in formal clinical trials. The phase II trial was the first to systematically evaluate this sequence in 36 patients with unresectable HCC. The results revealed an ORR of 30.6% and a median OS of 16.9 months, with a notably higher efficacy in patients without extrahepatic metastases (ORR: 43.5%). 59 The combination demonstrated a manageable safety profile with no treatment‐related deaths, thus establishing the preliminary clinical viability of this strategy.

These findings were corroborated and expanded upon by a larger multicenter NASIR‐HCC phase II trial. In this study of 42 patients, the combination of SIRT and nivolumab yielded a higher ORR (41.5%) and a median OS of 20.9 months. 60 Importantly, this regimen demonstrated significant potential for converting unresectable disease to resectable, with four patients successfully undergoing curative resection after therapy and remaining recurrence‐free for an extended period. This highlights the critical clinical benefits beyond standard palliation.

Further investigations confirmed that the synergy is not limited to a single ICI. A pilot study combining Y‐90 with pembrolizumab in a cohort with a poor prognosis, including patients with portal vein thrombosis, demonstrated an impressive median OS of 27.3 months and a 6‐month PFS rate of 55.6%. 61 Similarly, a phase I/IIa trial exploring Y‐90 with the PD‐L1 inhibitor durvalumab reported one of the highest ORRs in this setting (83.3%), with a compelling median time to progression of 15.2 months. The favorable toxicity profiles across these studies, with low rates of high‐grade adverse events, further support the tolerability of these combinations. 62

Real‐world evidence and comparative data are also emerging. A retrospective analysis directly compared Y‐90 combined with either nivolumab or the atezolizumab/bevacizumab regimen. The results revealed comparable efficacy and safety between the two combinations, with an ORR of 58% across the cohort and a median OS of 12.9 months, providing clinicians with the flexibility to choose a systemic partner for SIRT. 63 The findings from studies investigating Y‐90 radioembolization combined with immunotherapy are summarized in Table 3.

CHALLENGES AND FUTURE DIRECTIONS

Unmet Need For Clinical Data On Rt‐Immunotherapy Combinations

The most pressing challenge in combining RT with ICIs for HCC is the absence of phase III randomized controlled trial (RCT) data. While phaseI/II trials and retrospective analyses have shown promising response rates and manageable toxicity, these hypothesis‐generating findings cannot establish this combination as a standard care. The true magnitude of the survival benefit remains unquantified owing to potential selection bias in single‐arm studies. Critical questions remain regarding optimal patient selection, RT dosing, sequencing, and toxicity management, necessitating validation in large‐scale RCTs. Future trials should compare RT‐ICI combinations against current standards (e.g., atezolizumab/bevacizumab) with OS as the primary endpoint, stratified by key prognostic factors, including macrovascular invasion and extrahepatic spread.

Toxicity And Safety Concerns

The combination of RT and immunotherapy presents unique safety considerations. Radiation‐induced liver disease (RILD) remains a primary concern and is influenced by dose, fractionation, and liver irradiation volume. Although SBRT minimizes toxicity through precision targeting, the risk of RILD persists. The underlying mechanism involves radiation‐triggered dsDNA release activating cGAS‐STING signaling in non‐parenchymal cells, driving type I interferon production and hepatic inflammation. 18 Concurrently, ICIs may cause immune‐related adverse events (irAEs), including hepatitis, colitis, and pneumonitis. This combination potentially exacerbates hepatotoxicity in patients with cirrhosis, and radiation‐induced PD‐L1 upregulation may amplify immune activation. Optimal sequencing to balance the efficacy and toxicity requires further investigation.

Optimal Dosing Of Rt In Combination With Immunotherapy

Translation of radiobiological insights into clinically optimal dosing regimens for immunotherapy combinations remains a pressing challenge. Despite the growing understanding of the dose‐dependent immunomodulatory effects of RT, 25 a significant gap persists between mechanistic hypotheses and robust clinical validation. The current evidence is primarily derived from retrospective analyses and small‐scale prospective studies that are not specifically designed to address dosing optimization. The scarcity of dedicated clinical research fundamentally limits the establishment of evidence‐based guidelines. Future investigations must prioritize the systematic evaluation of different dose fractionation schedules in conjunction with various immunotherapeutic agents, incorporating careful correlations between immunologic biomarkers and clinical outcomes. Such focused research is essential to determine whether universal optimal parameters exist, or whether dosing must be tailored to specific clinical contexts, tumor types, and combination partners.

Personalized Approaches In Hcc Treatment

Personalized approaches are crucial for treating heterogeneous HCC. Building on the resistance mechanisms in RT and immunotherapy, integrating genomic and immune profiling enables the development of tailored strategies. Next‐generation sequencing allows for comprehensive profiling and identification of driver mutations that can guide targeted therapy selection (e.g., lenvatinib and sunitinib). 64 Specific immune microenvironment (e.g., Tregs and TAMs) are key predictors of immunotherapy response. 65 Advanced immunophenotyping further characterizes immune subsets for personalized immunotherapies 66 Liquid biopsies via ctDNA analysis provide noninvasive monitoring of treatment response and minimal residual disease, complementing traditional biomarkers such as AFP. 67 However, challenges persist due to tumor heterogeneity, evolving resistant subclones, and the need for robust bioinformatics to integrate multiomics data. 16 , 17 , 23 , 67 , 68 , 69 , 70

CONCLUSION

The integration of RT and immunotherapy has emerged as a promising strategy in the management of HCC, offering potential synergies to enhance anti‐tumor immune responses and improve clinical outcomes. It is imperative to consolidate the insights gained from investigations into the biological mechanisms, therapeutic strategies, and clinical evidence supporting this combination and translate them into actionable recommendations for clinical practice. In this review, we report the key advancements, address the practical implications of integrating these modalities, and identify future research priorities to optimize the treatment paradigms for patients with HCC.
