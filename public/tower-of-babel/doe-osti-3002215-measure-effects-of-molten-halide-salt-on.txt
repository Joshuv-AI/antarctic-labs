 ABSTRACT

Ongoing commercialization of different molten salt reactor concepts can considerably benefit from
quantitative information on the impact of molten halide salts on the engineering properties such as creep
and fatigue strength of materials of interest. Limited legacy data from the molten salt reactor experiment
(MSRE) program showed a significant reduction in creep rupture strength of a Ni-base alloy in molten
fluoride salt. This task evaluated the creep behavior of candidate structural materials 316H, 709 and 617
for advanced nuclear reactors under molten halide salt environments at 650, 700 and 750°C respectively.
Creep tests were conducted in air, Ar, fluoride (FLiNaK) and chloride (NaCl-MgCl2) salts. The molten salt
environment caused a 50% reduction in creep rupture lifetime for 316H in FLiNaK and an 80% reduction
in creep rupture lifetime in NaCl-MgCl2 compared to air exposures. The molten fluoride salt environment
resulted in almost a 90% and 50% reduction in creep rupture time for 709 and 617 irrespective of the cover
gas environment (air and Ar). Significant differences in the compositional changes between the salt- and
air-facing surfaces were observed in the alloy subsurface which might have potentially lead to formation of
phases detrimental to the creep behavior.

 vii

 1. INTRODUCTION

Efforts to demonstrate the commercial potential of molten salt reactors (MSRs) (Zhao et al. 2023;
Kelleher, Gagnon, and Mitchell 2022) are driven by their viability as an advanced reactor concept to
achieve the short- and long-term climate goals (Locatelli, Mancini, and Todeschini 2013; Serp et al.
2014). These are relying on a combination of the extensive legacy knowledge from the molten salt reactor
experiment (MSRE) (Macpherson 1972) and relatively recent data on materials compatibility of structural
materials of interest such as 316H in molten salts environments (Raiman et al. 2022). However, there are
no long-term data equivalent to expected reactor operating durations (40 years) on the corrosion behavior
of alloys of interest such as 316H and 709 in molten fluoride (FLiNaK or FLiBe) salts. Furthermore, there
are no experimental data on the short- or long-term impact of coupled extremes such as corrosion, stress
and irradiation on the material degradation of these alloys. The 1958 report by Manly (Manly et al. 1958)
had reported a significant influence of the molten fluoride salt NaF-ZrF4-UF4 (50-46-4 mol.%)
environment on the creep behavior of a Ni-base alloy (Ni-15Cr-7Fe wt.%). The ongoing MSR
commercialization efforts can benefit from relevant data on the engineering properties (e.g., creep and
fatigue strength) of structural materials in molten salt environments.

The present task evaluated the creep behavior of the austenitic steel 316H in molten FLiNaK and NaCl-
MgCl2 and of alloys 709 and 617 in molten FLiNaK using hollow creep specimens. A lower stress than
the one chosen for the previous testing program (Pillai and Pint 2024) was selected for the testing in the
current task. Baseline tests were conducted in air and Ar to assess the impact of the molten salts on the
creep behavior. Preliminary microstructural characterization was conducted to derive correlations to the
observed creep behavior.

 2. EXPERIMENTAL PROCEDURE

The cylindrical hollow creep specimens had a 57.2 mm gage length, 12.7 mm outer and 11.2 mm inner
diameter (ID). A stainless-steel cup made of type 316L stainless steel was attached to the bottom of the
creep specimen to capture the molten salt after rupture. This specimen design was derived from a patent
application (Ren et al. 2016). All specimens were subjected to rigorous geometry checks in order to verify
dimensions and surface finish. Particularly, the inner surface was electrical discharge machined (EDM)
and polished to an Ra of 0.4 µm measured using a profilometer. Specimens of 316H were machined from
a 2.54 cm thick 316H plate provided in a solution annealed state. Alloy 709 specimens were machined
from a 1.8 cm thick plate (solution annealed at min. temperature of 1150°C, followed by precipitation
treatment at 775°C for 10-h). The measured composition of the three alloys is given in Table 1.

The FLiNaK salt used for this work was prepared by a now defunct company Electrochemical Systems.
The composition of the as-received salt was previously reported (Raiman et al. 2022). The NaCl–MgCl2
eutectic salt used in this study was prepared with 58.5 mol % anhydrous NaCl salt and 41.5 mol %
anhydrous MgCl2 salt. The salt was homogenously mixed and purified following an established
purification procedure for chloride salts (Mayes et al. 2018). The trace metallic impurities of the NaCl–

MgCl2 eutectic salt mixture were identified by inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry analysis in
which the most prominent impurity elements and the corresponding concentrations were 1.5 ppm Cr, 10.8
ppm Fe and 1.4 ppm Ni (all by weight).

The hollow creep specimens were filled in an Ar-filled glovebox (O2 and H2O < 0.1 ppm) with ~15 g of
the salt and sealed by welding the cap (same material as the specimen) on the top in the glovebox. Before
beginning the creep test, thermocouples were fixed at about 1/3 of the gauge length before threading on
the catch cup. The completed load train (including the pull rods and specimen) was loaded into the
furnace and threaded into the turn buckle before additional insulation was applied. The heating was 1 h to
temperature followed by a 0.5 h hold to reach a steady state. The system was operated within a 3 °C
variance between the thermocouple on the specimen and the control thermocouple of the furnace. The
linear variable displacement transducer (LVDT) used to measure displacement and derive creep strains
was zeroed at this point and the specimen was loaded. The furnace was programmed to be switched off
immediately upon specimen rupture to minimize corrosion damage to the specimen due to the leaking
molten salt. The experimental program is summarized in Table 2. An additional creep test were conducted
for 316H which included an interrupted creep test in FLiNaK where the test was stopped after reaching a
creep strain of 5%. A 709 creep specimen was filled with salt and exposed in a box furnace for 200h at
700°C without loading to isolate the effect of corrosion.

Although the primary goal of this task was to evaluate the creep behavior at an additional lower stress,
additional creep tests were conducted to evaluate the impact of the external environment, which was air in
the previous experimental program, on the creep behavior of the investigated alloys. Additionally,
employing Ar cover gas as the external environment minimizes the interactions of the leaked salt and the
exposed material surfaces with oxygen or moisture.

Postexposure specimens were cross-sectioned for metallographic analyses. The mounted samples were
ground to 1,200 grit with SiC grinding papers and subsequently polished with diamond pastes to 1 μm
surface finish. The final preparation step involved preparation with colloidal silica. Microstructural
characterization to measure compositional changes and identify phase transformations was performed
using scanning electron microscopy, backscattered electron (BSE) microscopy (ZEISS), and energy
dispersive x-ray spectroscopy (EDS; Octane Elect EDS/EDX with Super Silicon Drift Detector). The

chemical compositions of the salts before and after high temperature exposure were measured with
inductively coupled plasma atomic emission spectrometry (ICP-AES)

 3. RESULTS

Figure 1a compares the measured creep strains of 316H in air, Ar (continuing) and NaCl-MgCl2 at 650
°C/150 MPa. The creep test in NaCl-MgCl2 (358h) has resulted in a fiur-fold reduction in creep rupture
lifetime compared to the test in air (1702h). Additionally, there is about a 50% reduction in creep ductility
for the specimen tested in the chloride salt. At the lower stress of 130 MPa, (Figure 1b), the creep rupture
lifetime was 91h in FLiNaK compared to 116h in NaCl-MgCl2. The baseline test without salt and Ar
cover gas is planned. The expected creep rupture lifetime in air at 650C and 130 MPa is about 3000h
(Whittaker, Evans, and Wilshire 2012). This suggests a considerable impact on creep rupture lifetime in
the molten salt environment. The creep test in FLiNaK under the same condition is being repeated to
verify this result and provide statistical scatter in the creep rupture behavior.

Figure 2 compares the measured creep strains of 316H in air and FLiNaK at 650 °C/150 MPa. The test
interrupted after 5% creep strain is also shown in the figure. Both the tests in FLiNaK show good
reproducibility of the creep behavior. The creep test in FLiNaK has resulted in a two-fold reduction in creep
rupture lifetime (781h) compared to the test in air (1702h). Additionally, there is a 50% reduction in creep
ductility for the specimen tested in FLiNaK.

Figure 3a compares the creep behavior of alloy 709 in FLiNaK with two different cover gas environments
Air and Ar at 700°C and 158 MPa. In both cases, a significant reduction in creep rupture lifetime (150h
with air as cover gas and 90h with Ar as cover gas) is observed compared to the creep test without salt
(1095h). The expected creep rupture life for 709 in air under these conditions is 1000h. Figure 3b
provides a similar comparison for alloy 617 with almost identical creep rupture lifetimes for the tests in
FLiNaK with air (1010h) and Ar (924h) as cover gas environments. The creep test in air failed after
1445h which indicates about a 50% reduction in creep rupture lifetime. The creep strains were relatively
low for both 617 specimens tested in air and FLiNaK compared to the data for 617 in the literature
(Wright 2021). This might be due to the lower number of grains expected across the wall thickness of the
current specimen geometry (0.76 mm) which is below the recommended wall thickness of 3.175 mm

(Wright 2021). The thinner wall in the creep specimen will most likely have lesser grains and hence lesser
grain boundaries which can explain lower creep ductility.

 4. DISCUSSION

Initial investigations on the role of creep-corrosion interactions in reducing creep rupture lifetime of the
investigated alloys have been conducted. Figure 5 compares the BSE images and element distribution maps
for Cr, O and Mo in the 316H creep specimen between the creep rupture test in air and the interrupted
(stopped after 5% creep strain) creep testing in FLiNaK with air as outside environment (Figure 2).

 Figure 5: BSE images and element (Cr, O and Mo) distribution maps of the cross-sections of alloy
 subsurface at the inner diameter of (a) 316H after creep rupture in air (1702h) and (b) 316H after
 interrupted creep testing FLiNaK (500h) at 650°C and 150 MPa.

A Cr-rich oxide has formed on the ID of the specimen exposed without salt (Figure 5a) with a Cr depletion
zone evident from the Cr map beneath the oxide scale. Additionally, the presence of finely distributed

CrMo-rich precipitates could be confirmed in the corrosion affected zone. Detailed characterization is
ongoing to identify these precipitates. In contrast, these precipitates were not observed in the specimen
tested with the salt Figure 5b. Furthermore, the grain boundaries were observed to be enriched with Cr and
Mo in this case. Although a direct comparison between the two cases is not possible due to the distinct test
durations, the preliminary results do suggest differences in the corrosion induced microstructural changes.

The unloaded 709 creep specimen exposed in a box furnace for 200h at 700C to isolate the effect of
corrosion while maintaining a similar salt volume to alloy surface area ratio provided some additional
insights into the differences in creep behavior between the two environments. Figure 6a shows the Cr
depletion profile at the inner diameter (ID) and outer diameter (OD) of the specimen and clearly there is
considerably more depletion at the ID. Minimal Cr depletion is observed at the OD which is expected since
709 is known to form a slow-growing chromia (Cr2O3) scale at 700°C. Consequently, the corresponding
Mo concentration profile (Figure 6b) confirms enrichment of Mo at the ID while the concentration at the
OD is similar to the initial Mo concentrations. These differences can have significant implications on the
thermodynamic phase stabilities in the corroded alloy subsurface of 709.

The measured concentration of Cr in the salt after interrupted creep testing of 316H at 650°C and 150 MPa
after 500h was 95 wppm (Figure 7a) which was much lower than the 165 wppm in the FLiNaK salt of the
unloaded 709 creep specimen after 200h at 700°C (Figure 7b). With an initial Cr concentration of 72 wppm
in the as-received salt, this meant a net loss of 23 and 93 wppm for 316H and 709 respectively. This clearly
suggests an increased depletion of Cr in case of the 709 specimen. Based on the previous dissolution studies
of pure Cr in the same batch of FLiNaK salt, about 230-250 wppm of Cr can be expected to be in the salt
at 650 and 700°C since a weak temperature dependence for Cr dissolution was observed (Pint,
Sulejmanovic, and Pillai 2023) after 500h. There is clearly a tendency for faster depletion of Cr in the salt
for 709 and 316H. This can most likely be attributed to the differences in the thermodynamic (Cr activity)
and kinetic (Cr diffusion) factors in the two alloys. The Cr activity of 0.72 in 709 at 700C is higher than the
value of 0.6 in 316H at 650C. Additionally, Cr is expected to diffuse an order of magnitude faster in 709 at
700°C than in 316H at 650°C (based on calculations performed using commercially available
thermodynamic and kinetic databases TCFE11 (Thermo-Calc 2020) and MOBFE6). A combination of these
two factors will deplete Cr faster in 709 than in 316H. Higher depletion of Mn can also be observed in 709.
The Fe impurities in the as-received salt most likely either existed as or formed Fe fluorides at temperature
and were then reduced by the formation of Cr fluorides. The resulting Fe might have potentially plated on
the inner surface of the creep specimen. Ongoing investigations are aiming to identify the correlation
between this increased Cr loss in 709 and the higher impact on creep rupture lifetimes.

The significantly higher depletion of alloying constituents in the alloy subsurface during exposure in molten
salts compared to in air can be expected to result in phase transformations which can be potentially
detrimental to the creep behavior of the alloys not intentionally designed for operation in molten salts.
Efforts to continue to understand the impact of these molten salt induced compositional changes and phase
transformations on the observed creep behavior of the three alloys are currently ongoing.

 5. CONCLUSIONS

The molten salt environment resulted in a decrease in the creep rupture life of 316H, 709 and 617. The
molten salt environment resulted in a 50% reduction in creep lifetime for 316H and 617 with a similar
drop in creep ductility for 316H while no evident impact on creep ductility for 617. The creep test in the
chloride salt showed a 80% reduction in creep rupture time for 316H. The creep testing of 709 in the
fluoride salt resulted in about an 80% reduction in creep rupture time with no evident impact on creep
ductility. Differences in the corrosion induced microstructural changes could be identified between the air
and salt environments for 316H through preliminary microstructural characterizations. Much higher Cr
depletion into the salt was observed in the case of 709 compared to 316H. Future work will focus on
continuing the detailed characterization of the creep specimens, analyzing the salt chemistry after creep
testing and coupled thermodynamic-kinetic modeling to understand the creep-corrosion interactions.
