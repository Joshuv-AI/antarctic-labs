 Abstract
 Open Radio Access Networks (O-RAN) are emerging, software-driven cellular architectures that promote
 flexibility by enabling components from different vendors to interoperate. Multiple control applications called
 xApps can independently adjust network parameters in near real time, often without awareness of each other’s
 actions. This creates a system highly prone to unintended conflicts and performance degradation due to the inherent
 complexity of such openness. To model such systems and ultimately prevent or mitigate xApp conflicts, it is
 essential to understand the dynamic relationships between xApps (A), the control parameters they adjust (P), and
 the resulting KPI responses (K). While the mappings from A to P and from K to A can often be derived from
 xApp specifications, the relationship from P to K is typically hidden within the system’s dynamics and must be
 inferred from observed data. We propose a novel data-driven Boolean inference framework that uncovers the hidden
 P→K dependencies using machine learning and interpretable rule induction. Continuous parameters and KPIs are
 first binarized using decision tree classifiers, and a binary influence matrix L is then inferred by solving Boolean
 matrix equations over time. This compact representation improves interpretability and enables real-time tracking of
 dynamically evolving parameter-KPI dependencies. We demonstrate the effectiveness of our method in a realistic
 mobile handover scenario, where it accurately recovers the underlying logic and enables proactive conflict detection.

 Index Terms
 O-RAN, Boolean inference, machine learning, conflict detection, conflict graph, sliding window.

 I. I NTRODUCTION
 Open Radio Access Networks (O-RAN) represent a major shift in how cellular systems are built and
managed. Unlike traditional Radio Access Networks that are vertically integrated by a single vendor,
O-RAN disaggregates the system into interoperable components connected through open interfaces. This
disaggregation enables cost reduction and innovation, but also introduces new complexity: multiple control
applications, or xApps, may simultaneously adjust overlapping parameters of the radio network. When these
applications make uncoordinated decisions, the result can be conflicts that degrade system performance.
 Recent research on conflict detection in O-RAN has largely focused on building conflict graphs using
graph neural networks (GNNs) or digital-twin profiling pipelines. These approaches can discover hidden
correlations, but they are often opaque, computationally heavy, and reliant on large-scale training data.
More importantly, they typically assume that the mapping from system parameters to key performance
indicators (KPIs) is either known or can be subsumed within a black-box model. In practice, this assump-
tion rarely holds: the parameter-to-KPI (P→K) relationships are highly scenario-dependent, noisy, and
dynamic, yet they form the core mechanism through which xApps interact. Without making these hidden
dependencies explicit, operators cannot fully explain, anticipate, or resolve conflicts.
 In this work we propose a complementary approach based on Boolean inference. Rather than training
complex neural models, we directly learn compact logical relationships that capture how changes in
parameters influence KPI states. After discretizing continuous data streams into Boolean traces, we infer

an evolving binary influence matrix that links parameters to KPIs. This matrix provides a transparent,
low-complexity abstraction of O-RAN dynamics, suitable for real-time monitoring in the near-RT RIC
(near-real-time RAN Intelligent Controller). Conflicts can then be detected by lightweight logical checks
on overlapping dependencies, while the explicit structure also supports interpretability and root-cause
analysis.
  Our main contributions are as follows:
  1) We introduce a two-phase Boolean inference framework (offline learning plus streaming inference)
 that recovers P→K dependencies and enables real-time/streaming conflict detection via lightweight
 logical checks.
  2) We design a sliding-window algorithm that adaptively updates the Boolean influence matrix in
 response to changing conditions, balancing stability with adaptability.
  3) We evaluate the framework on synthetic datasets with known ground truth and discuss its potential
 for deployment on real O-RAN traces.
 II. BACKGROUND AND P ROBLEM S TATEMENT
 In an O-RAN setting, the system is governed by control parameters: continuous values such as
transmission power, scheduling rate, or modulation choice, and is evaluated through key performance
indicators (KPIs), such as throughput, latency, or interference. We denote the parameters as P1 , . . . , PNP
and the KPIs as K1 , . . . , KNK , with each constrained to application-specific ranges.
 At any time t, xApps (independently developed control applications running on the near-RT RIC) may
adjust one or more parameters, producing changes ∆P (t). These adjustments propagate through the system
to induce KPI responses ∆K(t), which are observed together with measurement noise. Our goal is to
capture these dependencies in a compact, interpretable form.
 We formalize this using a time-varying Boolean influence matrix Lt ∈ {0, 1}NK ×NP . An entry Lt [j, i] =
1 indicates that parameter Pi exerts an operative influence on KPI Kj under the current operating
conditions, while Lt [j, i] = 0 means no such influence is detected. The Boolean abstraction is chosen
not to measure the exact magnitude of influence, but to identify which parameters are relevant for each
KPI.

A. Related Work
 Recent approaches to O-RAN conflict detection have made use of graph-based learning (e.g.,Graph
Convolutional Networks (GCNs), Graph Attention Networks (GATs), and dynamic-graph models, with
surveys of the area) [1]–[4], where the goal is to construct a conflict graph—a standard wireless interference
abstraction in which edges denote mutually interfering entities [5], [6]—that represents dependencies and
potential clashes between xApps, control parameters, and KPIs. In this setting, nodes may represent xApps
or parameters, while edges indicate inferred conflicts or influences.
 Zolghadr et al. [7], for example, propose a data-driven Graph Neural Network (GNN) framework [4]
that reconstructs such conflict graphs from observational data. Their method leverages GraphSAGE [8] to
generalize to previously unseen xApps and captures temporal dependencies in KPI dynamics. This enables
the discovery of indirect or hidden conflicts that are not explicitly observable in raw measurements.
 Other works such as PACIFISTA [9] employ digital twins—virtual replicas of networks used for analysis
and “what-if” evaluation—drawn from recent networking/6G literature [10], [11] to run profiling pipelines
and build conflict graphs. While this provides a controlled environment for experimentation, the realism
of the digital twin limits the accuracy of the resulting conflict graphs when transferred back to live
deployments. Earlier game-theoretic or prioritization-based approaches [12], [13] rely on the assumption
that parameter-to-KPI relationships are already known, an assumption that rarely holds in practice because
these relationships depend strongly on traffic load, interference, and environmental conditions.
 Our work departs from these approaches by focusing on Boolean inference. Rather than training a large
neural model, we explicitly learn compact Boolean rules that map parameter activity to KPI effects. Here,
“Boolean” refers to rule-based, binary logic over discretized states, as in Boolean networks and Boolean
matrix decompositions [14], [15]. This has three advantages: (i) transparency, since the rules are human-
readable; (ii) ease of validation, since individual rules can be checked against domain knowledge; and
(iii) adaptability, since rules can be updated incrementally through sliding-window updates that use only
the most recent data. We view Boolean inference as complementary to GNN-based methods: GNNs excel
at capturing hidden correlations in large graphs, while Boolean rules provide immediate interpretability
and lightweight reasoning suitable for near-RT RIC deployment.

 III. O FFLINE P HASE : L EARNING I NTERPRETABLE B OOLEAN RULES
 As highlighted in Fig. 1, the offline phase establishes a set of interpretable rules that map control
parameter activity to KPI effects. Since real O-RAN traces are limited and noisy, we begin by generating
controlled synthetic data where ground-truth dependencies are known. This allows us to design and validate
algorithms in a fully observable setting before applying them to live data. The offline phase proceeds
through four main steps: synthetic data generation, Booleanization, predictive modeling of ∆K, and rule
induction with validation.
A. Synthetic Data Generation
 To support the development and evaluation of our framework, we generate synthetic time series data for
both control parameters (P ) and key performance indicators (K). Unlike approaches that rely on hand-
specified latent matrices or corrective lock-based policies, our simulator emphasizes producing dynamic,
noisy trajectories that mimic the variability of real O-RAN environments.
 At each timestep t, we generate a batch of parameter settings P (t) and compute the corresponding
KPI responses K(t) according to hidden parameter–KPI dependencies. These dependencies are defined
at initialization and remain latent throughout experiments, serving as the ground truth to be recovered.

 • Controllability. The generator allows specification of the number of parameters, number of KPIs,

B. Booleanization
 To recover interpretable logical dependencies, we transform the continuous-valued trajectories of pa-
rameters and KPIs into binary indicators. This step, which we call Booleanization, ensures that both offline
learning and online inference operate on a consistent discrete representation.
 The procedure is defined by simple thresholding rules:
 1) Parameters. Each parameter Pi (t) is mapped to a binary variable bool_Pi (t) according to whether
 it exceeds a sensitivity threshold ϵ. This prevents spurious activation caused by small fluctuations
 or measurement noise.
 2) KPIs. Each KPI Kj (t) is mapped to bool_Kj (t) by comparing it to a KPI-specific threshold θj
 (e.g., derived from normalized sensitivity or service-level requirements). Values above or below this
 threshold are flagged as performance degradations depending on the KPI’s definition.
 3) Temporal alignment. Booleanization is applied consistently across the entire time series, so that
 bool_P(t) and bool_K(t) are directly comparable at each timestep. This alignment is critical for
 solving Boolean matrix equations over sliding windows.
 The result is a pair of binary traces {bool_P(t), bool_K(t)}Tt=1 that serve as the inputs and outputs
of the Boolean inference process. These traces form the basis for estimating the evolving influence matrix
Lt that links parameters to KPIs.
C. Predicting KPI Changes
  In our framework we do not model KPI dynamics through intermediate regression or classification pre-
dictors. Instead, we directly study how binary KPI states evolve in relation to the Booleanized parameters
within a sliding time window.
  At each timestep t, the Boolean traces bool_P(t) and bool_K(t) are observed. Over a window
Wt = {t − W + 1, . . . , t}, we seek a binary influence matrix Lt such that
 bool_K(Wt ) ≈ Lt ⊙B bool_P(Wt ) ,
where ⊙B denotes Boolean matrix multiplication with AND/OR semantics. This formulation treats KPI
changes implicitly: a KPI is predicted to be active whenever at least one of its influencing parameters is
active according to Lt .
 By shifting the window forward in time, we capture the dynamics of KPI behavior without the need for
continuous ∆Kj modeling. Noise robustness is controlled through the thresholding introduced during
Booleanization, and adaptability is achieved by updating Lt whenever the fit between bool_P and
bool_K begins to degrade.
 Thus, KPI changes are represented directly in terms of logical dependencies in the evolving Boolean
influence matrix, rather than through surrogate predictive models.
D. Rule Induction and Boolean Dependencies
  After Booleanization, the key task is to recover interpretable dependencies between parameters and
KPIs. Rather than probing surrogate models, we infer these dependencies directly by solving Boolean
matrix equations over sliding windows of the binary traces.
  For each window Wt , we compute an influence matrix Lt ∈ {0, 1}NK ×NP such that
 bool_K(Wt ) ≈ Lt ⊙B bool_P(Wt ) ,
where ⊙B denotes Boolean matrix multiplication with AND/OR semantics. The rows of Lt identify which
parameters exert a detectable influence on each KPI during that time window. Because Lt is updated
adaptively as the system evolves, the induced rules remain aligned with current operating conditions.

  These Boolean dependencies can be read directly as human-interpretable rules. For example, if the j-th
row of Lt has ones in the columns corresponding to P2 and P5 , the induced rule for KPI Kj is:
 Kj is active ⇐ (P2 active) ∨ (P5 active).
Thus, each row of Lt provides a compact, auditable description of the conditions under which KPIs are
triggered.
 In this way, the Boolean inference framework produces a library of explicit parameter-to-KPI rules
that can be tracked over time. These rules are simple enough for inspection by domain experts, yet rich
enough to capture the dynamic behavior of an O-RAN system under changing conditions.
E. Offline Validation
 The final step of the offline phase is to validate the inferred Boolean influence matrices against the
known ground truth from the synthetic data generator. This step ensures that the recovered dependencies
are not only interpretable but also quantitatively reliable.
 For each parameter–KPI pair (Pi , Kj ), we compare the entries of the inferred matrix Lt with the
ground-truth adjacency matrix L∗ . Validation is carried out along two dimensions:
 • Accuracy metrics. We compute standard measures such as accuracy, precision, recall, and F1 score
 over all (Pi , Kj ) pairs. These metrics quantify how well the inferred influence matrices identify true
 dependencies while avoiding false positives and false negatives. Confusion matrices provide a detailed
 breakdown of correct and incorrect classifications for every KPI row of Lt .
 • Error analysis. Discrepancies are examined to reveal systematic sources of error. Typical cases
 include threshold boundary effects (when KPI values hover near activation thresholds) and ambiguous
 dynamics in noisy conditions. Identifying such patterns helps refine both the Booleanization thresholds
 and the update rules used in the inference step.
 Through this validation process, we verify that the learned Boolean influence matrices are logically
consistent and closely aligned with the underlying simulator. This provides confidence that the framework
can serve as a foundation for the online phase, where Lt is updated in real time.
 Together, these procedures establish a reproducible pipeline for deriving interpretable Boolean rules from
data. Figure 2 provides a consolidated view of this process, showing how historical data and synthetic
simulation feed into model training, Boolean rule induction, and evaluation.
 IV. O NLINE P HASE : A DAPTIVE I NFLUENCE I NFERENCE
  The online phase applies the Boolean rules from the offline stage to streaming O-RAN measurements. Its
goal is to maintain an evolving dependency matrix Lt that reflects the current parameter-to-KPI influences.
Because O-RAN environments are nonstationary, Lt must be updated adaptively without incurring the cost
of full retraining.
A. Boolean Conversion of Streaming Data
  At each timestep t, continuous-valued parameters P (t) and KPIs K(t) are observed from the system.
These values are converted into Boolean indicators bool_P(t) and bool_K(t) using the offline-trained
functions fP and fK . This ensures a consistent discrete representation across both offline and online
phases, while also providing robustness to noise and facilitating logical reasoning.
B. Initialization of the Influence Matrix
  To begin inference, we estimate an initial dependency matrix Linit from a calibration window of length
W . For each KPI row, candidate binary vectors are evaluated to determine which parameter combinations
best explain bool_K from bool_P. When multiple solutions exist, we select the one with minimal
support, favoring sparse explanations. This produces a baseline Linit that approximates the system’s
dependency structure at startup.

C. Sliding-Window Updates
 As time progresses, Lt must adapt to reflect changing conditions. We maintain a sliding window Wt =
{t − W + 1, . . . , t} of recent samples. At each timestep, the system checks whether the previous matrix
Lt−1 correctly predicts the latest bool_K from bool_P. If the prediction matches, we set Lt = Lt−1 . If a
mismatch occurs, a new candidate matrix L et is inferred from the window. The candidate is accepted only
if it improves prediction accuracy or reduces the Hamming distance to the observed bool_K; otherwise,
the previous matrix is retained. This acceptance rule ensures adaptability without oscillations.
D. Role in Conflict Detection
  The evolving matrix Lt provides the foundation for real-time conflict detection. Conflicts occur when
two or more parameters controlled by different xApps exert incompatible effects on the same KPI. Because
Lt explicitly encodes parameter-to-KPI influences, such conflicts can be detected by lightweight logical
checks. For example, if P1 and P2 both map to K3 in Lt , and one xApp increases P1 while another
decreases P2 , a conflict flag can be raised. This representation makes conflict detection interpretable and
computationally efficient, supporting deployment in near-real-time RIC settings.

 V. R ESULTS
 This section describes how the framework will be evaluated and provides placeholders for the results
to be inserted once experiments are completed. Our evaluation covers both the offline and online phases
of the approach.
A. Evaluation Methodology
 For the offline phase, we benchmark the accuracy of Boolean rules induced from synthetic datasets
under varying noise levels, discretization schemes, and model choices. Metrics include accuracy, precision,
recall, F1 score, and confusion matrices for parameter–KPI pairs. We also compute regression metrics
(Mean Squared Error (MSE), Mean Absolute Error (MAE), and coefficient of determination (R2 ).) from
the intermediate ∆K predictors to quantify the fidelity of the supervised models.
 For the online phase, we assess the adaptability of the sliding-window mechanism. Evaluation dimen-
sions include noise ϵ, window size W , number of experiments per timestep, and total number of trials.
We compare our selective update method against baselines such as a static L, a naı̈ve full refit at each
step, and an oracle with access to the ground-truth L.
B. Planned Experiments and Results
  The outputs to be reported include:
  • Accuracy curves over time showing the stability of Lt .
  • Hamming distance trajectories comparing inferred Lt to ground truth in simulations.
  • Update frequency distributions to analyze the tradeoff between adaptability and stability.
  • Runtime benchmarks to confirm feasibility for Near-RT RIC deployment.

 VI. D ISCUSSION AND F UTURE W ORK
 The proposed framework is designed with a strong emphasis on interpretability, even when the system
conditions change over time. Unlike black-box machine learning models that may be difficult to explain,
our use of Boolean rules provides clear, human-readable decision boundaries. These rules can be directly
inspected and validated by network operators. In addition, the sliding-window update mechanism allows
the framework to adapt to nonstationary environments without requiring frequent and costly retraining.
 Nevertheless, several limitations remain. First, the quality of the rules depends on the thresholds used
during Booleanization, and poor threshold choices can lead to incorrect classifications. Second, when
KPIs operate near their boundary values, the system dynamics may appear ambiguous, which can result
in misclassifications. Possible mitigations include adding uncertainty margins (treating near-threshold
cases cautiously) or introducing an abstain option, where the model explicitly defers judgment when the
evidence is unclear.
 Looking ahead, we plan to extend the framework in three directions:
 1) Graph-based conflict analysis. We will integrate the evolving influence matrix Lt with graph-
 theoretic tools that can detect feedback cycles and identify likely root causes of conflicts.
 2) Causal probing. We aim to incorporate techniques from causal inference to help distinguish genuine
 dependencies between parameters and KPIs from correlations that arise due to shared external
 factors.
 3) Validation on real-world data. Finally, we plan to test the framework on actual O-RAN traces. Real
 deployments introduce additional challenges such as heterogeneous cell types, noisy measurements,
 and delays in xApp responses, all of which must be addressed before practical deployment.
 Ultimately, we view Boolean inference as complementary to more complex neural or graph-based
approaches, such as GNN-based conflict reconstruction. While such methods are powerful at uncovering
hidden correlations, Boolean rules provide lightweight, auditable reasoning that can be deployed in near-
real-time RIC environments where transparency and efficiency are critical.

 VII. C ONCLUSION
 We have presented a two-phase Boolean inference framework for conflict detection in O-RAN systems.
In the offline phase, synthetic data are generated, Boolean rules mapping parameters to KPIs are induced,
and their reliability is validated against known ground truth. In the online phase, these rules are applied
to live data streams, with the dependency matrix Lt updated adaptively using a selective sliding-window
procedure. The resulting Boolean influence matrix provides a transparent and computationally efficient
foundation for conflict detection, root-cause analysis, and policy refinement within the near-real-time RIC.
 Although experimental results will be reported in future work, the framework already demonstrates how
explainable inference and adaptive updates can coexist in practice. Future efforts will focus on integrating
graph-based reasoning, applying causal analysis, and validating the approach on real O-RAN deployments.
By combining interpretability with adaptability, the proposed framework moves toward conflict detection
methods that are both technically effective and practically usable.
