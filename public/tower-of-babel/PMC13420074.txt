Rhabdomyolysis caused by severe Legionella pneumonia combined with influenza A
Authors: Zhang Qi, Zhang Caiping, Wang Wei
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Rhabdomyolysis is a clinical syndrome characterized by the destruction and disintegration of striated muscle due to various causes such as compression, intense exercise, high fever, medications, and inflammation. This leads to the release of intracellular contents like creatine kinase and myoglobin into the extracellular fluid and bloodstream, resulting in metabolic disturbances, acute kidney injury, and damage to other tissues and organs. Influenza virus or Legionella infection, after the pathogen invades the human body, it can cause systemic inflammatory response syndrome, toxin-mediated myocyte damage, severe hypoxia, and metabolic derangement and acidosis in the body. Legionella pneumonia combined with influenza A exacerbates the infection and collectively induces rhabdomyolysis. At present, there are no reports on rhabdomyolysis caused by coinfection of Legionella and influenza A virus. In this case, we focus on introducing a case of influenza A combined with severe Legionella pneumonia, which eventually developed into rhabdomyolysis and multiple organ dysfunction, thereby filling the gap in this field.

Case report

The patient, a 69-year-old male, was admitted to the hospital with the chief complaint of “cough accompanied by fever for 1 week and wheezing for 3 days.”

The patient developed fever without obvious cause 1 week ago, with the highest body temperature reaching 39.5 °C, 1 accompanied by cough and expectoration of white sticky sputum, approximately 10–20 ml/day, which was difficult to expectorate. The patient also reported generalized myalgia predominantly involving the limbs, particularly the bilateral thighs and lower back, without associated chest discomfort or dyspnea. The patient self-administered oseltamivir for 5 days and intermittently took paracetamol for fever reduction, claiming to have followed the dosage instructions on the label, though the exact dose was unclear. The body temperature subsequently decreased.

The patient self-administered oseltamivir for 5 days and intermittently took oral acetaminophen as an antipyretic according to the instructions; however, the specific dosage remains unknown. Following this treatment regimen, his body temperature decreased. Three days ago, he developed wheezing symptoms and noted that shortness of breath became more pronounced during physical activity. Additionally, there was an increase in sputum production compared to previous levels; the sputum had changed to a yellow–green color.

Since the onset of the disease, the patient has presented with generalized pain, including persistent soreness in the bilateral lumbar and back regions as well as the limb muscles, accompanied by limb weakness and inability to move out of bed independently. Progressive worsening of generalized jaundice involving the skin and mucous membranes was observed, accompanied by a gradual darkening of urine from light yellow to dark brown, ultimately becoming tea- or cola-colored. Oliguria was present, with a 24-h urine output of approximately 800 ml. The patient denied abdominal pain or abdominal distension, but complained of poor sleep and decreased appetite. The patient’s smoking history spans 50 years at an average rate of 20 cigarettes/day. He also has a 30-year history of alcohol consumption, with daily intake of approximately 500 ml of 52% vol. baijiu.

The patient was previously in good health, denied having underlying diseases such as hypertension, diabetes, and coronary heart disease, and also denied having liver and kidney function-related underlying diseases such as chronic hepatitis, liver cirrhosis, chronic nephritis, and renal insufficiency. The patient had not taken any hepatotoxic or nephrotoxic drugs before admission.

Physical examination of admission

Body temperature was recorded at 38.2 °C, with a heart rate of 103 beats/min and a respiratory rate of 21 breaths/min. Blood pressure measured at 125/80 mmHg. Pulse oxygen saturation was 88% while receiving supplemental oxygen at 5 l/min via nasal cannula; no oximetry value was documented at room air. The patient appeared well-developed and well-nourished, remaining conscious despite the acute fever upon admission to the ward.

The overall skin elasticity was poor, accompanied by dry skin exhibiting yellowish discoloration. No peripheral edema was detected. Asymmetric, geographic, purplish-red macules are present on the distal extremities, peritrunk area, and lateral aspects of the trunk. The lesions are non-blanching with pressure. No superficial lymphadenopathy is detected. Notably, the patient had dry lips and pharyngeal congestion; however, bilateral tonsil enlargement was absent. Neck examination revealed a supple neck without stiffness or meningeal irritation. Jugular venous distention was absent. The thyroid was not enlarged, and no masses or vascular murmurs were detected. The trachea was midline. Pulmonary examination: Respiratory rate was 30 breaths/min with shallow and rapid breathing. Decreased respiratory excursion was noted in the right hemithorax. Dullness to percussion was present over the right lower chest. Crackles with localized wheezes were auscultated bilaterally, predominantly in the right lower lung. No pleural friction rubs were detected. Cardiac examination: Heart rate 110 beats/min, regular rhythm. No pathological murmurs heard in any valve auscultation areas, no pericardial friction rub. No peripheral edema, pulses were symmetric and normal, and no pathological vascular signs were detected. Abdominal examination: Moderate jaundice of the skin and sclera was noted. The abdomen was flat and soft, with no tenderness or rebound tenderness. The liver was not palpable under the costal margin. The spleen was palpable 1 cm below the costal margin, soft in consistency and non-tender. Hepatic percussion tenderness was elicited. Shifting dullness was negative.

Abdominal palpation revealed a soft abdomen without tenderness or rebound pain; muscle tension was also absent. Neurological evaluation showed no positive signs indicative of neurological deficits.

Clinical management

The patient was diagnosed with severe pneumonia based on chest computed tomography (﻿CT) and infection indicators after admission. Creatine kinase (CK) and myoglobin were continuously monitored after admission, and both were abnormally elevated on the third day of admission. CK was significantly elevated to 6506 U/l, and myoglobin was also markedly increased. Combined with manifestations such as muscle pain with dark-colored urine, the patient was diagnosed with rhabdomyolysis (RM). Immediately after diagnosis, targeted therapies including fluid resuscitation, urine alkalinization, and renal protection were initiated. The Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) score was 8 points, indicating moderate organ dysfunction.

Upon admission, chest CT of the patient demonstrated large-area consolidative pneumonia (Figure 1). Combined with the clinical features of hypoxemia accompanied by central nervous system manifestations, hyponatremia, and elevated enzyme levels, a nucleic acid detection test for six common pathogens of the lower respiratory tract was performed. The nucleic acid test for L. pneumophila was positive, and the patient was diagnosed with Legionella pneumonia. According to the Chinese Guidelines for the Diagnosis and Treatment of Adult Community-Acquired Pneumonia, 2 and considering the patient’s condition of severe Legionella pneumonia with hypoxemia, initial dual anti-Legionella therapy with moxifloxacin (a quinolone) combined with doxycycline (a tetracycline) was administered to enhance antimicrobial coverage and achieve rapid infection control. The patient was treated with moxifloxacin 0.4 g/day intravenously and doxycycline 0.2 g/day orally for anti-Legionella therapy. Subsequently, a respiratory virus panel identified influenza A virus, leading to the administration of oseltamivir for antiviral treatment. The patient tested positive for influenza A nucleic acid on day 5 of admission. Combined with upper respiratory symptoms such as fever and sore throat at the early stage of onset, influenza A was considered as a prodromal infection, which caused synergistic infection with Legionella and jointly aggravated the systemic inflammatory response.

Despite the original treatment regimen, the patient still developed respiratory failure, accompanied by persistent high fever and an elevation of procalcitonin to 42 ng/ml. It was considered that the Legionella infection was not effectively controlled and the infection continued to deteriorate. On the basis of the original dual anti-Legionella therapy, imipenem at 1.5 g/day was added to intensify broad-spectrum antimicrobial treatment.

Blood lactate was 5.0 mmol/l on admission and decreased to 2.0 mmol/l after anti-shock and anti-infective treatments.

Chest CT of the patient showed bilateral pleural thickening, which was considered to be caused by chronic inflammation. The small right-sided pleural effusion was considered a reactive effusion secondary to severe Legionella pneumonia.

The patient remained febrile with persistent myalgia, and neurological deterioration ensued with development of stupor. Meanwhile, serum CK levels were progressively rising. Furthermore, oliguria developed alongside progressive darkening of urine, which became soy-sauce colored, secondary to RM leading to acute kidney injury (Figure 2).

Soy sauce-colored urine.

Hemofiltration was performed on day 2 of admission and day 3 of admission; after hemofiltration treatment, the patient’s 24-h urine output gradually increased from 800 to 1500 ml. Laboratory tests revealed decreased levels of CK and myoglobin, with the serum creatinine dropping to 204 μmol/l. These findings indicate a favorable therapeutic response.

As the patient’s condition progressed to multiple organ failure, the physicians fully informed the patient’s family of the detailed condition. After careful consideration, the family believed that there was no clear clinical benefit from further treatment, and given the patient’s advanced age, they refused further hemofiltration therapy and opted for conservative supportive treatment.

During hospitalization, the patient experienced continuous hypotension. Despite continuous blood pressure monitoring, high-concentration oxygen inhalation, and norepinephrine administration for blood pressure support, the patient’s condition deteriorated. Ultimately, the patient succumbed to respiratory failure and hypovolemic shock on day 7 of admission, despite resuscitation efforts (Supplemental Material).

Discussion and conclusion

At present, case reports regarding RM leading to acute kidney injury secondary to co-infection of influenza A virus and Legionella pneumonia remain scarce worldwide. Most previously published studies mainly focus on RM induced by single pathogen infection. There is still no unified consensus on the synergistic pathogenic effects, clinical characteristics, and management strategies of such dual infections. Thus, the present case fills a notable gap in existing clinical literature.

Dual infection with influenza A virus and Legionella triggers muscle injury not merely through simple superposition of independent pathogenic mechanisms, but via interactive crosstalk between pathogens and host immune responses. This combined pathogenic insult causes more severe systemic damage than single-pathogen infection, which represents the core distinctive feature of our case. 3

Most published reports describe RM caused by either influenza A or Legionella alone. Jacob et al. reported RM and Acute Kidney Injury (AKI) due to dual infection, 3 consistent with our patient’s core presentation. Sutarjono et al. 4 and Prasanna et al. 5 reported Legionella-related RM without viral co-infection. Our case is unique due to extremely high CK elevation, rapid progression to Multiple Organ Dysfunction Syndrome (MODS), late neurological deterioration, and fatal outcome despite early hemofiltration.

Consistent with prior clinical evidence, pulmonary infection caused by Legionella is capable of inducing multiple extrapulmonary complications apart from pulmonary lesions, including muscular injury, hepatic dysfunction, and coagulation disorders. 6 A 2022 retrospective cohort study conducted by Wang et al. involving 218 patients with severe Legionella pneumonia in China demonstrated that RM occurred in 10.1% of severe cases. 7 The muscular involvement manifested in our case is consistent with this finding. Nevertheless, distinct from most previously reported cases, our patient suffered from concomitant influenza A virus co-infection, neurological disturbance, severe RM, and subsequent acute kidney injury synchronously. Such extensive multi-system involvement is rarely reported in existing literature.

Notably, these extrapulmonary complications may present as initial symptoms with mild or inconspicuous respiratory manifestations, leading to a high risk of clinical misdiagnosis and missed diagnosis in early stages. Moreover, long-term heavy alcohol consumption in this patient acted as an independent aggravating factor. Chronic alcohol intake directly impairs muscular cell integrity and systemic antioxidant capacity, amplifying inflammation-induced muscle injury. Meanwhile, persistent alcohol exposure compromises host immune function, increasing susceptibility to combined viral and bacterial respiratory infection.

RM is characterized by rupture of striated muscle fibers and massive release of intracellular contents into systemic circulation, resulting in systemic toxic injury of multiple organs. 1 Without timely intervention, this condition may progress to life-threatening organ dysfunction including renal failure.

In patients with severe pneumonia, clinicians should monitor CK, myoglobin, renal function, and urine color routinely. Early fluid therapy, urine alkalization, and organ support are essential. Prompt and broad-spectrum antimicrobial and antiviral treatment improves survival in dual infection.

Conclusions

Co-infection with influenza A virus and L. pneumophila can lead to severe, life-threatening RM and acute kidney injury. Compared with single infection, dual infection causes more severe systemic inflammation and higher mortality. Early recognition of extrapulmonary complications is critical even when respiratory symptoms are mild. Chronic alcohol use is an important risk factor for severe disease. Clinicians should maintain a high index of suspicion for RM in patients with severe dual infection and initiate early intensive intervention to reduce mortality.
