A Spontaneous Mutation in tfoX Impairs Bioluminescence in Photobacterium kishitanii ANT‐2200
Authors: Capucine Le Cam Ligier, Gwenola Simon, Charlotte Berthelier, Corinne Valette, Elisa Lefeuvre, Marc Garel, Laurie Casalot
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBioluminescence in luminous Vibrionaceae is often regulated by quorum sensing, yet several Photobacterium strains emit light independently of cell density, suggesting alternative regulatory inputs. Here, we characterized DimLum, a spontaneous, stable low‐luminescence mutant of Photobacterium kishitanii ANT‐2200. RNA‐seq showed a pronounced downregulation of the lux–rib operon, while whole‐genome sequencing identified a 20‐nucleotide deletion in tfoX predicted to truncate the C‐terminal region of TfoX. Complementation with the wild‐type tfoX gene restored lux–rib expression and bioluminescence, linking this mutation to the DimLum phenotype. The mutant also displayed a limited transcriptional response, including upregulation of a small set of genes such as a Cro/C1‐type regulator. Together, these results identify TfoX as an important component of the regulatory network supporting bioluminescence in P. kishitanii .Keywords: bacterial bioluminescence, gene regulation, lux‐rib operon, tfoX

Introduction

Bioluminescence is widespread among marine bacteria and plays well‐established ecological functions in symbioses with fishes and cephalopods, including counterillumination camouflage, predator deterrence and prey attraction (Haddock et al. 2010; Widder 2010). In free‐living bacteria, however, its ecological significance remains less clear (Ramesh and Mohanraju 2017; Tanet et al. 2020). Most luminous marine bacteria belong to the Vibrionaceae, particularly the genera Vibrio, Aliivibrio, and Photobacterium (Ast et al. 2007; Fuertes‐Perez et al. 2021; Moi et al. 2017; Urbanczyk et al. 2011). Light production depends on the lux operon: luxAB encodes luciferase, luxCDE encodes enzymes required for synthesis of the fatty aldehyde substrate, and luxG contributes to flavin reduction (Brodl et al. 2018; Meighen 1993; Sung and Lee 2004). In Photobacterium, lux operons are particularly diverse and may include accessory genes such as luxF or a downstream riboflavin biosynthesis module, ribEBHA, which may contribute to variation in operon architecture, light output and metabolic integration (Brodl et al. 2020; Gould et al. 2023; Machado and Gram 2017; Urbanczyk et al. 2008).

In well‐studied luminous Vibrionaceae, such as Aliivibrio fischeri and Vibrio campbellii , bioluminescence is regulated by quorum sensing (QS), which coordinates lux expression with cell density (Baldwin et al. 1989; Bellissimo et al. 2024; Dunlap 2014; Qin et al. 2007; Stevens and Greenberg 1997; Verma and Miyashiro 2013). These systems can include local LuxI/LuxR regulators as well as broader Vibrionaceae pathways involving signal receptors, LuxU/LuxO, Qrr small RNAs, and downstream master regulators (Ball et al. 2017). Therefore, the absence of luxI/luxR genes near the lux operon does not necessarily exclude QS regulation. However, several Photobacterium species display luminescence patterns inconsistent with classical cell‐density‐dependent control, suggesting that other mechanisms may influence lux–rib expression (Dunn et al. 2015; Katznelson and Ulitzur 1977; Tanet et al. 2019). Photobacterium kishitanii ANT‐2200, isolated from Mediterranean waters at 2200 m depth, produces strong bioluminescence. During a fermenter experiment, we isolated a spontaneous, stable low‐luminescence mutant, DimLum. This mutant provided a genetic entry point to investigate the determinants associated with reduced light production in this strain. Unlike typical dark mutants in vibrios, which often arise from disruptive lesions in the lux operon itself (O'Grady and Wimpee 2008), DimLum showed no structural alteration in the light production genes after initial verification of this locus. To our knowledge, this makes DimLum the first low‐luminescent variant in Photobacterium described without disruption of the light production genes. We combined transcriptomic, whole‐genome sequencing and genetic complementation to identify the genetic determinant responsible for this regulatory alteration. This approach identified tfoX, encoding a TfoX/Sxy‐family regulator associated with natural competence in Vibrionaceae (Metzger et al. 2019; Wu et al. 2015), as a genetic determinant of full bioluminescence in P. kishitanii .

Results and Discussion

A Spontaneous Reduction of Luminescence Reveals a Global Shutdown of the lux–rib Operon

The spontaneous low‐luminescence mutant DimLum displayed growth kinetics comparable to those of the wild‐type strain (WT). Both strains reached similar maximal optical densities (ODmax = 1.99 ± 0.16 for DimLum and 1.63 ± 0.02 for WT) and showed indistinguishable specific growth rates (Qx = 0.0109 and 0.0106 min−1, respectively; Wilcoxon test, p > 0.05; Table S1). These results indicate that DimLum did not show impaired growth under the tested conditions. In contrast, light production was strongly reduced in DimLum compared with WT, both for maximal raw bioluminescence (BLmax = 2.4 × 103 ± 1.9 × 103 vs. 1.04 × 105 ± 3.0 × 103 photons s−1) and for OD‐normalized light production (qBLmax = 7.6 ± 8.4 vs. 617.1 ± 53.3 photons s−1 ODU−1). The very low and highly variable qBLmax value observed in DimLum therefore indicates a strong reduction in luminescence but does not support consistent light detection throughout growth.

Across early, mid‐ and late exponential phase, transcriptomic analysis revealed a marked and coordinated downregulation of the lux–rib operon in DimLum (Figure 1). The lux and rib genes (Figure 1A) were the only strongly downregulated loci genome‐wide, with fold changes (FC) ranging from approximately 4‐ to 40‐fold (log2FC from −1.97 to −5.37; adjusted p value < 0.05; Table S2; Figure 1B). No mutation was detected in the lux–rib structural genes or their regulatory region, indicating that the reduced luminescence in DimLum is associated with transcriptional downregulation rather than structural disruption of the light production locus.

A Single Frameshift in tfoX Underlies a Reduced Luminescence

Whole‐genome sequencing of WT and DimLum identified 19 high‐confidence variants differentiating the two genomes, including synonymous, upstream, and missense variants, as well as two variants predicted to have high‐impact (Table S3). One high‐impact variant affected an annotated tRNA‐Lys gene (Bloom‐Ackermann et al. 2014), whereas the other corresponded to a 20‐nucleotide deletion in PPBDW_v2_I20952, annotated as a tfoX homologue (Figure 2). Given the coordinated downregulation of the lux–rib operon and the known regulatory role of TfoX/Sxy proteins in Vibrionaceae (Metzger et al. 2019; Wu et al. 2015; Yamamoto et al. 2011), the tfoX mutation was considered the most plausible candidate underlying the DimLum phenotype.

Sequence alignment of the C‐terminal region of TfoX from the wild‐type strain (WT_TfoX) and the DimLum mutant (DimLum_TfoX), generated using Clustal 2.1. A 20‐nucleotide deletion causes a frameshift at amino acid position 171, resulting in an altered amino acid sequence followed by a premature stop codon.

TfoX/Sxy proteins have been characterized as broad regulators in several Gammaproteobacteria, including Vibrionaceae (Metzger et al. 2016). They contribute to coordinated transcriptional programs, including competence‐related pathways, and can act with cAMP–CRP to activate genes containing CRP‐S‐type promoter elements (Cameron and Redfield 2006; Lo Scrudato and Blokesch 2013; Søndberg et al. 2019; Sun et al. 2013). In Vibrio cholerae , tfoX expression is connected to chitin‐dependent competence induction and is posttranscriptionally enhanced by the small RNA TfoR (Dalia et al. 2014; Søndberg et al. 2019; Sun et al. 2013; Yamamoto et al. 2011). The P. kishitanii ANT‐2200 protein encoded by PPBDW_v2_I20952 fits this family: the 195‐amino acid WT sequence was most similar to TfoX/Sxy proteins from Photobacterium, including a Photobacterium phosphoreum homologue showing 90.77% identity over the full query sequence, and more distant full‐length Vibrio homologues showing approximately 60%–62% identity. P. kishitanii ANT‐2200 also encoded a second TfoX‐like protein, but this paralogue was more divergent, sharing 32.8% identity and 52.4% positives with the DimLum‐associated TfoX/Sxy protein over a 189‐aa aligned region. These comparisons support the annotation of the mutated gene as a TfoX/Sxy homologue and indicate that this strain encodes an additional, more divergent TfoX‐like paralogue.

Despite the broad regulatory roles described for TfoX/Sxy proteins in other Vibrionaceae (Metzger et al. 2019), the transcriptional signature associated with the DimLum gene was highly focused under the tested conditions: 99.25% of the transcriptome remained unchanged, whereas the lux–rib operon was strongly downregulated (Table S2). Competence‐associated genes, such as pilA were only weakly affected or unaffected (Table S4), suggesting that the mutation does not simply abolish all TfoX‐related functions under these conditions. This focused response may reflect the growth conditions used, partial activity of the altered TfoX protein, or a contribution of the second TfoX‐like paralogue. Together, these results link the DimLum tfoX frameshift gene to reduced bioluminescence in P. kishitanii ANT‐2200, while leaving open whether TfoX affects the lux–rib operon directly or through intermediate regulators.

Wild‐Type tfoX Restores Bioluminescence and Selected Transcriptional Changes

Complementation of DimLum with the WT tfoX gene restored bioluminescence to near‐WT levels (Figure 3A). Growth of the complemented strain, DimLum(pCLCL‐tfoX1), remained broadly comparable to WT and DimLum, although its specific growth rate was slightly reduced (Qx = 0.0081 min−1 vs. 0.0106 and 0.0109 min−1 for WT and DimLum; Table S1), suggesting a mild cost associated with tfoX expression from the complementation plasmid. In contrast, bioluminescence was restored to near‐WT levels (Figure 3A). Maximal light output reached 8.4 × 104 ± 5.1 × 103 photons s−1, approaching WT levels, while the maximal specific rate of light production (qBLmax) was not only restored but exceeded that of the WT strain (902.8 ± 133.0 vs. 617.1 ± 53.3 photons s−1 ODU−1). Because OD600 is an indirect proxy for biomass, the apparent over‐restoration of normalized light production was interpreted cautiously.

Consistent with phenotypic recovery, RT‐qPCR showed restoration of lux and rib gene expression in the tfoX‐complemented strain (Figure 3B; Table S4). A similar trend was observed throughout growth (Figure S1). The empty‐vector control did not restore luminescence or lux–rib expression, indicating that recovery depended on WT tfoX. Together, these data show that the WT tfoX gene is sufficient to restore lux–rib transcription and bioluminescence in the DimLum background.

Amongst the genes upregulated in DimLum, the strongest signal corresponded to PPBDW_v2_I30036, encoding a putative Cro/C1‐type helix‐turn‐helix regulator (log2FC ≈ 3.35) (Table S2). Cro/C1‐family regulators are often associated with transcriptional switches (Konovalova et al. 2014; Mahmoud and Chien 2018; Schubert et al. 2007), and related regulators have been implicated in phenotypic switching and luminescence variation in Photorhabdus luminescens (Eckstein et al. 2021). This Cro/C1‐like regulator may therefore represent an intermediate component linking the altered tfoX gene to the low‐luminescence transcriptional state.

RT‐qPCR analysis of selected non‐lux genes supported the RNA‐seq results (Table S2) and showed that the expression of three genes was reproducibly shifted toward WT levels upon tfoX complementation: PPBDW_v2_I30036, encoding the Cro/C1‐like regulator; PPBDW_v2_II0075, encoding a predicted metal‐dependent protease; and PPBDW_v2_II1194, encoding a protein of unknown function (Figure 4). Other genes showing intermediate changes, including respiratory or redox‐associated genes such as fdhA, cyoB, and napA, may reflect downstream physiological consequences of the low‐luminescence state rather than direct TfoX‐dependent regulation. This interpretation is consistent with observations in Photobacterium leiognathi KNH6, where variation in bioluminescence was associated not only with the lux, lum, and rib operons, but also with genes linked to metabolism (sucA, mdh) and transcriptional regulation (sspA) (Dunn et al. 2015).

Overall, these results support the involvement of tfoX in the DimLum phenotype and suggest a regulatory link with the lux–rib bioluminescence operon. Under the tested conditions, the transcriptional response remained limited, with lux–rib expression as the main affected output. This study provides evidence that spontaneous variation in tfoX is associated with altered bioluminescence in P. kishitanii ANT‐2200. Whether this effect is direct or mediated by intermediate regulators, such as the Cro/C1‐like factor identified here, remains to be determined. Future work should test TfoX binding, promoter activity in targeted tfoX mutants, and the contribution of candidate regulators to this pathway.

Experimental Procedures

Bacterial Strains, Culture Conditions and Bioluminescence Measurements

Photobacterium kishitanii ANT‐2200, formerly described as P. phosphoreum ANT‐2200, was used as the wild‐type strain. This strain was isolated from Mediterranean deep‐sea waters at 2200 m depth (al Ali et al. 2010; Bjornsdottir‐Butler et al. 2016; Machado and Gram 2017). The spontaneous low‐bioluminescent mutant DimLum was isolated during a controlled fermenter experiment. All strains and plasmids used in this study are listed in Table S5. Photobacterium strains were grown in modified ZoBell medium (al Ali et al. 2010) at 19°C with shaking, while Escherichia coli strains were grown in LB at 37°C. For cloning and conjugation, E. coli JM109, E. coli CC118 λpir, and E. coli CC118 λpir carrying pEVS104 were used as previously described (Yanisch‐Perron et al. 1985; Herrero et al. 1990; Stabb and Ruby 2002). When required, antibiotics were added at standard concentrations. Solid media contained 15 g L−1 agar, and strains were stored at −80°C in 30% glycerol.

For complementation assays, DimLum was transformed with either pVSV104‐tfoX, designated pCLCL‐tfoX1, or the empty pVSV104 vector. Transformants were selected on modified ZoBell agar supplemented with kanamycin. Growth was monitored by measuring optical density at 600 nm (OD600) using a VWR PV4 UV/Visible spectrophotometer (VWR International). Bioluminescence was measured using an H7155 photomultiplier tube coupled to a C8855 photon counting unit (Hamamatsu), with a 10 ms gate time and expressed as detected photon counts s−1.

Plasmid Construction and Genetic Complementation

The wild‐type tfoX gene, including 294 bp upstream of the start codon, was amplified from P. kishitanii ANT‐2200 genomic DNA using primers listed in Table S6. The PCR product was cloned into pGEM‐T Easy, generating pCLCL‐tfoX, and verified by Sanger sequencing. The NcoI–SacI fragment was then subcloned into the broad‐host‐range vector pVSV104, which carries a kanamycin resistance marker (Dunn et al. 2006), to generate pCLCL‐tfoX1.

Plasmid transfer into DimLum was performed by triparental conjugation using E. coli CC118 λpir carrying pCLCL‐tfoX1 as donor and E. coli CC118 λpir carrying pEVS104 as helper strain, as described by Stabb and Ruby (2002). Donor, helper, and recipient cultures were mixed at a 1:1:1 ratio, spotted onto nonselective ZoBell agar, incubated at 28°C, and then plated on selective ZoBell agar containing kanamycin. Empty‐vector controls were generated in parallel.

Whole‐Genome Sequencing and Variant Analysis

Genomic DNA from WT and DimLum strains was sequenced by MGX Genomics using Illumina MiniSeq paired‐end sequencing. Reads were mapped to the P. kishitanii ANT‐2200 reference genome, GenBank accession GCA_000617925.1, using BWA‐MEM. Low‐quality alignments were removed with SAMtools; variants were called with Octopus, and annotations were performed with SnpEff. Only high‐confidence variants with QUAL > 30 and DP > 10 were retained for analysis.

RNA Extraction, RNA‐Seq, and RT‐qPCR

Total RNA was extracted from independent replicate cultures harvested at defined growth stages, as described by Simon et al. (2025). RNA quality was assessed by NanoDrop and TapeStation, and the absence of genomic DNA was confirmed using no‐RT controls. Growth stages used for RNA‐seq, and RT‐qPCR are detailed in Tables S7 and S8.

For RNA‐seq, stranded libraries were prepared and sequenced by MGX Genomics on an Illumina MiniSeq platform. Reads were trimmed with fastp, aligned to the reference genome with STAR, and assigned to annotated features using featureCounts. Differential expression analysis was performed with DESeq2 using an adjusted p value < 0.05 and an absolute log2FC ≥ 2.

For RT‐qPCR validation, total RNA was reverse‐transcribed using PrimeScript RT (Takara), and quantitative PCR was performed using SYBR Green chemistry (SsoAdvanced Universal SYBR Green Supermix; Bio‐Rad). Gene expression levels were normalized to rpoD and calculated using the ΔΔC t method (Livak and Schmittgen 2001). Each sample was analyzed in three technical replicates, with no‐template controls included. Primer sequences are listed in Table S6.
