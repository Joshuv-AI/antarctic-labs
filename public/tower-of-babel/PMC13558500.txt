Clinical Efficacy of a 12% Pure Vitamin C Serum Across All Skin Phototypes: An Integrated In Vitro–In Vivo Evaluation
Authors: Flávia Alvim Sant’Anna Addor, James Odeimi, Gisele Gargantini Rezze, Erika Fernandes, Mariana Feiges, Diala Haykal, Andrea Belén Rey
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionSkin aging is associated with loss of collagen network qualitative and quantitative integrity, which are crucial for skin structure and function. Topical application of pure vitamin C (l-ascorbic acid) may alleviate signs of skin aging (e.g., wrinkles, lack of firmness). The objectives of this open-label, monocentric study were to determine whether topical treatment with a dermocosmetic serum containing a high concentration (12%) of pure vitamin C (PVC12 serum) could increase epidermal thickness and positively influence collagen fiber synthesis and organization, thus alleviating visible signs of skin aging.MethodsThis study investigated the efficacy and tolerability of PVC12 serum over 3 months compared with baseline. Healthy participants aged ≥18 years with any skin phototype received twice-daily treatment with the PVC12 serum. Evaluations included reflectance confocal microscopy measures and instrumental measures of skin firmness and elasticity, as well as clinical scoring of multiple skin aging signs.ResultsThe study included 49 women (skin phototypes I–VI). After 84 days of continuous use of PVC12 serum, epidermal thickness was significantly increased by 16.4% compared with baseline. More than half of the participants showed improvements in total collagen score and cross-linked collagen fibers. A significant decrease in coarse and curled collagen fibers was also observed. There was significant improvement in skin firmness and elasticity throughout the study. At the end of treatment, there were significant and clinically relevant improvements in various parameters (e.g., radiance, skin oiliness, pores). The treatment was well tolerated, with no occurrence of treatment-related adverse events.ConclusionPVC12 serum may exert beneficial effects on skin of all phototypes, including improved skin thickness, collagen fiber synthesis and quality, skin firmness and elasticity, and other parameters. This evidence supports the effectiveness and tolerability of pure vitamin C in the prevention and correction of skin aging signs over time.

Introduction

Skin aging is a natural yet complex biological process governed by a synergy of intrinsic (genetics, hormonal shifts) and extrinsic (ultraviolet radiation [UV] exposure, pollution, smoking, stress) factors. Collectively, these elements induce cumulative structural and functional alterations across all skin layers, manifesting as visible skin quality deterioration, particularly in photo-exposed areas [1–7]. While extrinsic factors—primarily UV radiation—account for over 80% of visible aging [8], both internal and external factors contribute to structural damage by generating reactive oxygen species (ROS) that accumulate over time, thereby overwhelming the endogenous antioxidant defense systems and leading to chronic cutaneous inflammation [9].

Acute inflammation is a vital physiological response designed to repair tissue, restore homeostasis, and counteract the damaging effects of oxidative stress induced by ROS in the short term; its failure to resolve these effects leads to a persistent, sterile inflammatory state known as “inflamm-aging” [10, 11]. Clinically, this phenomenon is characterized by a compromised skin barrier, reduced self-repair capacity, loss of facial volume and elasticity, and the development of deep-set wrinkles [10].

Chronic inflammation and extracellular matrix (ECM) changes have been described as major hallmarks of skin aging [10–12], with collagen metabolism dysregulation being a key characteristic [13–17]. Collagen is the most abundant protein in the human body and a major component of the ECM, accounting for approximately 80% [13, 14, 18–20]. The preservation and promotion of collagen network quality and quantity are crucial as collagen has a very slow turnover rate, with collagen fibers being replaced endogenously approximately every 30 years.

As we age, the skin undergoes structural and functional changes in collagen amount, type, and structure [13]. In addition, the skin’s natural ability to replenish collagen fibers also decreases by 1.0–1.5% per year, resulting in a progressive loss and fragmentation of dermal collagen fibers [13]. This process results in loss of skin firmness and density, leading to skin thinning and wrinkling. Indeed, total collagen content can be reduced by approximately 25% with age, indicating that collagen loss is a key determinant of age-related deterioration [14]. Thus, promoting collagen synthesis represents a practical strategy for mitigating skin aging [21].

Morphologically, healthy youthful skin is characterized by shiny, interconnected, and cross-linked collagen fibers organized in parallel bundles which likely represent newly synthesized collagen fibers exhibiting strong bonding. In contrast, aged skin is marked by coarse, clumped, and curled collagen [22]. Coarse collagen forms thick, irregular, and stiff bundles; clumped collagen appears as disorganized, hyperreflective amorphous aggregates; and curled collagen consists of fragile, wave-like spiral structures. Consequently, promoting collagen synthesis and protecting existing fibers are essential strategies for mitigating age-related skin deterioration [21, 22].

Pure vitamin C (l-ascorbic acid) is among the most powerful naturally occurring antioxidants, and its concentration is higher in the skin than in any other body tissues [4, 23–25]. It has essential functions in human physiology and health, such as its antioxidant and anti-inflammatory activities, which help protect the skin against the harmful effects of external aggressors and damaging ROS propagation. Beyond its well-documented brightening properties, it also protects and stimulates the synthesis of ECM components [2, 26–33]. Notably, it plays a key role in the synthesis and maturation of collagen fibers [3, 4, 23, 34–37] by serving as a cofactor for prolyl and lysyl hydroxylase that cross-link and stabilize collagen fibers, while inhibiting the activity of metalloproteinases responsible for collagen breakdown [3, 23, 25]. In skin cells, the levels of vitamin C are associated with the integrity of dermal collagen, and a deficiency in vitamin C can lead to skin aging [38, 39]. Owing to its multiple beneficial and proven properties in the skin, pure vitamin C is widely used in topical products, especially anti-aging formulations [23].

Moreover, previous research clearly showed that aging reduces the levels of vitamin C in the human epidermis and dermis [40]. Clinical studies have also demonstrated that continuous topical application of vitamin C can increase collagen content in the skin [41, 42] by activating gene expression and transcription factors involved in collagen synthesis and decreasing collagen degradation [25]. Research has also revealed the mechanisms through which vitamin C promotes the production of functionally and structurally mature collagen fibers [43–46]. Thus, external supplementation of vitamin C is a promising anti-aging strategy [4, 24, 38, 39].

Furthermore, beyond its role in direct gene expression, vitamin C acts as a cofactor for translocation enzymes involved in DNA demethylation. Through this activity, vitamin C may promote the demethylation of genes that drive keratinocyte proliferation and epidermal function, thereby contributing to the epigenetic regulation of skin homeostasis. These mechanisms may help counteract age-associated skin thinning and atrophy [47].

The objectives of this study were to determine whether topical treatment with a pure vitamin C serum could (1) positively influence collagen fiber synthesis and organization, potentially enhancing the ECM structure and quality; (2) increase epidermal thickness, thereby resulting in a denser, more resilient skin structure; and (3) alleviate visible signs of skin aging across all skin types and phototypes.

Methods

Study Design

This open-label study investigated the efficacy and tolerability of a pure vitamin C serum over a treatment period of 3 months compared with baseline. The investigation was conducted in one center in São Paulo, Brazil.

The study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki of 1964 (and its subsequent amendments) and Good Clinical Practice guidelines. Quality control was carried out at each stage of the study, in accordance with ISO 9001:2015. All data were collected and stored following the guidelines of the LGPD (General Data Protection Act). The entire study followed the MEDCIN guidelines for ESG (Environmental, Social and Governance) practices. This study was carried out after ethical approval, issued by the Universidade São Francisco - USF Research Ethics Committee on 5 December 2022, under opinion No.: 5.793.585 and CAAE: 65339922.6.0000.5514, with an amendment approved on 17 March 2023 under No. 5.950.231. The participants provided written informed consent for their participation. The participants also provided consent for the publication of patient images.

Treatment

The clinical efficacy of topical l-ascorbic acid is fundamentally dictated by its formulation chemistry rather than its absolute concentration. As a highly hydrophilic molecule, pure l-ascorbic acid faces significant pharmacokinetic challenges, specifically its inherent oxidative instability and its limited capacity to traverse the lipophilic stratum corneum [25]. Consequently, the delivery vehicle and secondary stabilizing factors are the primary determinants of its biological activity.

The tested dermocosmetic (DC) serum (Pure Vitamin C12/PVC12; La Roche-Posay Laboratoire Dermatologique, Levallois-Perret, France) contains a 12% concentration of pure vitamin C (l-ascorbic acid). In this study, a 12% concentration of pure l-ascorbic acid was selected to align with the known percutaneous absorption curve. Research demonstrates that a minimum concentration of 8% l-ascorbic acid is required to achieve biological significance. While cutaneous saturation increases proportionally with the concentration, it reaches a physiological plateau at 20%. By utilizing a 12% l-ascorbic acid concentration, this formulation occupies an optimized therapeutic window. This chosen strength ensures potent antioxidant delivery and high bioavailability, while mitigating the risks of cutaneous irritation and potential pro-oxidant effects often observed with higher-concentration acidic preparations [25, 48]. To further enhance molecular stability and absorption, the formulation incorporates a synergistic complex of dermatological actives, including barrier-protecting glycerin, antioxidant vitamin E, and salicylic acid for gentle exfoliation. Specifically, vitamin E (tocopherol) neutralizes lipid-phase free radicals and is subsequently regenerated from its oxidized state by l-ascorbic acid, thereby sustaining the antioxidant cycle, while salicylic acid may act as a potential penetration enhancer through gentle peeling. The formulation also features both high and low molecular weight hyaluronic acids for multi-depth hydration and preservation of skin barrier integrity [48, 49], alongside soothing La Roche-Posay thermal spring water, naturally rich in selenium. To address the high susceptibility of l-ascorbic acid to thermal and oxidative degradation, the formulation undergoes a specialized industrial stabilization process. Production is conducted under controlled inert conditions with minimal oxygen exposure and regulated temperatures to prevent premature oxidation. The final product is housed in anti-UV lacquered glass packaging, providing a barrier against photodegradation (data on file).

In this study, the treatment was applied twice daily (morning and evening) to cleansed, dry facial skin. The participants also applied a tinted sunscreen (Anthelios XL Protect SPF30 tinted cream; La Roche-Posay Laboratoire Dermatologique) once daily in the morning.

Evaluation Parameters

Participants were evaluated by a dermatologist to examine clinical efficacy parameters before (D0) and after 7 (D7), 28 (D28), 56 (D56), and 84 (D84) days of continuously using the product.

Reflectance confocal microscopy (RCM) was employed in this study for the evaluation of parameters. This is a noninvasive technique utilized to visualize the skin and investigate signs of skin aging [22]. Images were obtained using a reflectance confocal microscope (VivaScope® 1500; Caliber Imaging and Diagnostics, Rochester, NY, USA), which utilizes a laser light source with a wavelength of 830 nm and an immersion objective with ×30 magnification. This device has the capacity to capture real-time images of 500 × 500 μm parallel to the epidermal surface.

The primary evaluation parameter in this study was the epidermal thickness measurement using RCM at D84. The secondary evaluation parameters were as follows. Firstly, collagen fiber characterization (synthesis and quality) using RCM as well as collagen score at D84.

Secondly, instrumental evaluation of skin firmness (R0) and elasticity (R7) using Cutometer® MPA 580 (Courage+Khazaka electronic GmbH, Cologne, Germany) at D0 (i.e., before first application of the product), D7, D28, D56, and D84. Of note, a lower R0 value indicates a greater skin firming effect, while a higher R7 value corresponds to increased skin elasticity.

Thirdly, the clinical effectiveness of the treatment on numerous parameters (i.e., wrinkles, radiance, pores, skin oiliness, skin tone evenness, and the number, intensity, and size of dark spots) was examined at the aforementioned time points.

Fourthly, before/after photographs at D0 and D84 were captured using VISIA® (Canfield Scientific, Parsippany, NJ, USA).

Finally, the cutaneous tolerability of the PVC12 serum was also assessed.

Statistical Analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using R software. Data normality was first verified using the Shapiro–Wilk test. For normally distributed continuous variables (e.g., epidermal thickness, skin firmness, and elasticity), a paired Student’s t test was used to compare post-treatment time points (D7, D28, D56, D84) against baseline (D0). For non-normally distributed parameters (e.g., collagen fiber characteristics), the Wilcoxon signed-rank test was applied for paired temporal comparisons. For clinical efficacy scores (ordinal scales), delta variations (representing a 1-point change on the scale, equivalent to 25%) were calculated and compared using the Mann–Whitney U test. As this was an exploratory clinical study designed to evaluate pre-specified, distinct physiological and clinical parameters of the skin (e.g., barrier structure, collagen organization, and clinical aging signs), no formal correction for multiplicity was applied. Instead, we present exact p values (e.g., p < 0.05) to allow for a transparent interpretation of each independent endpoint.

Results

This investigation was performed from 17 January 2023 to 30 May 2023.

Participants

A total of 49 women with all skin phototypes (I–VI) were included in the study and 42 of those completed the investigation. Among the seven participants who did not complete the study, three were excluded because of adverse events not related to the treatment (i.e., pregnancy, coronavirus disease-2019, and anal fissure), two were lost to follow-up, one was excluded as a result of non-compliance, and one dropped out. The mean age of the participants in this study was 38.7 years (range 19–58 years). The participants had dry (24.5%), combination (24.5%), oily (24.5%), or normal (26.5%) skin. Most participants had phototypes IV and III (41% and 33%, respectively), followed by phototypes V and VI (12% and 8%, respectively), and phototypes II and I (4% and 2%, respectively).

Cutaneous Tolerability

The treatment was well tolerated and there was no occurrence of treatment-related adverse events in this study.

Discussion

Vitamin C acts as a scavenger of free radicals by electron donation, thereby reducing oxidative stress caused by excessive production of ROS, and restores vitamin E in the skin [23, 24, 46]. It also supports the antioxidant defense systems of cells (i.e., superoxide dismutase, glutathione peroxidase, catalase, glutathione, and vitamin E) [24]. Furthermore, it inhibits nuclear transcription factor kappa B, which is responsible for inducing the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines [2].

Clinical evidence indicates that topical application of vitamin C may assist in alleviating the signs of skin aging [3, 42, 50–52]. Specifically, in individuals with accumulated photodamage, treatment with vitamin C for 12 weeks improved facial wrinkles and skin texture compared with placebo. In addition, prolonged application of vitamin C for 6 months led to significant improvement in skin histology and clinical appearance [2]. Topical application of DC serum results in a reservoir effect, thereby prolonging its availability in the skin and its protection against environmental damage [2].

Analysis through RCM demonstrated that treatment with PVC12 serum for 84 days led to an increase in skin thickness. Reduction in dermal thickness is one of the signs of skin aging, identified and measured by RCM [14, 22]. The increased average dermal thickness represents an improvement in ECM density, which may indicate an enhanced collagen metabolism and remodeling.

Moreover, continuous treatment with PVC12 serum induced a significant increase in cross-linked collagen fibers and a significant decrease in coarse and curled collagen fibers. Changes in collagen metabolism resulting from intrinsic and extrinsic skin aging affect dermal thickness as a result of a reduction in the number, diameter, and network density of collagen fibers [22]. Its characterization before and after treatment demonstrates the action of the product on the structure and organization of fibers. The observed cross-linked/fine fibers indicate the production of new collagen fibers, resulting in improvement in the damage caused by skin aging and less coarse and clumped collagen.

In RCM, cross-linked collagen represents fibrillar, thin, and shiny structures. The presence of this form of collagen indicates youthful skin architecture, and the increased values may suggest an improvement in this parameter. Coarse collagen represents coarse filamentous structures. The presence of this form of collagen denotes aged skin, and the decrease observed in this structure indicates an improvement in the skin dermal matrix. Finally, clumped collagen represents large spots of hyperreflective amorphous material, which is poorly visible and more commonly found in aged skin. The decrease noted in this structure indicates an improvement in the skin matrix quality over time [22].

Regarding skin firmness and elasticity, the analysis revealed significant improvements at all examination time points in this study compared with D0.

Furthermore, treatment with the PVC12 serum led to statistically significant clinical improvements in wrinkles, radiance, pores, skin oiliness, number of dark spots, dark spot intensity, and skin tone evenness at D56 and D84. The effect on wrinkles is consistent with previous clinical evidence indicating significant improvement after treatment with topical regimens including various concentrations of vitamin C [3, 53].

Finally, the PVC12 serum was well tolerated, with no treatment-related adverse events reported throughout the investigation. This is a critical finding with regard to the use of pure vitamin C products for sensitive skin types.

The limitations of this investigation must be acknowledged. Firstly, this study involved an open-label, non-randomized, uncontrolled assessment of a DC serum. Therefore, it is difficult to distinguish treatment effects from natural temporal variation and placebo or behavioral effects. Studies in the future should focus on reducing potential observer bias through blinding, randomization, and additional control via comparison with a product or an agent with antioxidant effects instead of baseline. Secondly, this was a monocentric investigation with a small sample size. Hence, multicenter studies involving larger numbers of participants are required to validate the present evidence. Thirdly, the study population consisted exclusively of women; studies examining the effects of the DC serum in men are needed. Fourthly, although the investigation considered all skin types when representing the diversity of Brazilian skin, there were small proportions of participants with fair skin phototypes (i.e., 2% and 4% for phototypes I and II, respectively). Thus, it is necessary to conduct further studies involving more diverse populations. Finally, the statistical analysis did not include confidence intervals and effect size estimates that can provide additional information regarding the precision and magnitude of observed associations. The inclusion of these metrics will be considered for future investigations.

Additional research is warranted to confirm the present findings. Nevertheless, owing to the high level of evidence and its excellent tolerability profile, pure vitamin C remains the gold standard molecule in this aging skin clinical setting [2, 25].

Topical vitamin C/E can be integrated within a functional medicine framework, recognizing that systemic factors such as nutrition, low-grade inflammation, microbiome balance, and circadian alignment modulate the skin’s response to topical antioxidants. Therefore, synchronizing vitamin C/E application with circadian phases may enhance efficacy, particularly when circadian rhythm disruption is present [54, 55]. In addition, research suggests that the cutaneous microbiome is a key modulator of inflammation, barrier integrity, and topical treatment tolerance; interventions that improve microbiome balance may further enhance the clinical efficacy of topical vitamin C/E, especially by reducing subclinical systemic inflammation. Finally, optimization of the systemic antioxidant status through diet or targeted supplementation may potentiate the topical effects of vitamin C/E on collagen restoration, epidermal thickness, and tone uniformity, consistent with the outcomes observed in this study [56–59].

This investigation provides the first comprehensive and novel evaluation of a pure vitamin C-based topical DC serum. Our integrated methodological approach, encompassing rigorous in vitro characterization of the collagen network and subsequent in vivo clinical validation of visible aging signs, establishes a new benchmark for assessing efficacy and safety. This approach directly responds to the dynamic advancements in dermatological science and the evolving expectations of both practitioners and consumers regarding advanced science-supported treatments of aging.

Conclusion

Previous clinical studies have demonstrated that the antioxidative protection is higher with the combination of vitamins C and E than with vitamin C or E alone, highlighting the need for a comprehensive approach to the treatment of aging skin [1].

The findings of the present investigation indicate that topical application of a comprehensive serum containing pure vitamin C and other established dermatologically active molecules (i.e., salicylic acid, vitamin E, hyaluronic acid, and La Roche-Posay thermal spring water) may exert beneficial effects on the skin, thereby alleviating the signs of aging across all skin phototypes (Fig. 7). This comprehensive approach ensures that the vitamin C remains potent and stable over time, providing optimized efficacy across all skin tones and high clinical tolerance, even in sensitive skin types. Specifically, the results indicated that use of a serum containing a high concentration (12%) of vitamin C improved skin thickness over time. Moreover, after 84 days of continuous use, the PVC12 serum improved collagen fiber synthesis and quality, demonstrating a skin rejuvenating effect. Furthermore, the PVC12 serum improved skin firmness and elasticity over time, resulted in statistically significant and clinically relevant improvement in various visible aging parameters (e.g., radiance, skin tone, and wrinkles), and demonstrated an excellent tolerability profile.Fig. 7Percent improvement in parameters at D84.
