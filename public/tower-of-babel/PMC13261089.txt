Natural Environment, Neighborhood Contexts, and Intra‐Urban Suicidal Behavior
Authors: Yehua Dennis Wei, Yu Wang, Meitong Liu, Yue Zhang, Max Coleman, Ming Wen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractHigh suicide rates remain a critical public health challenge in the United States. Using census block group‐level data, we examine spatial clustering of suicidal behavior in Salt Lake City (SLC), Utah, and identify key environmental and socioeconomic determinants. We find that green space and walkability are associated with lower suicidal behavior, likely by promoting stress relief, physical activity, and social interaction. In contrast, greater access to hotels is linked to higher risk, as these spaces may offer privacy that limits timely intervention. Neighborhoods with higher incomes and larger families showed greater resilience, consistent with reduced financial strain and stronger emotional support. Areas with higher labor force participation face increased risk, possibly reflecting pressures from job competition and wage stagnation. Contextual factors further moderate these effects. The protective effect of green space is weaker in neighborhoods with larger shares of non‐Hispanic African American residents and in higher‐income or larger‐family areas, but stronger where the jobs–housing mix is higher. Spatial heterogeneity analyses reveal that mixed land use near downtown is associated with higher risk, likely because of traffic noise, waste, and crime. Meanwhile, higher proportions of non‐Hispanic African American residents are associated with lower risk in eastern SLC, where these populations tend to live in higher‐income neighborhoods with better living conditions. Overall, this study highlights the interplay of environmental, socioeconomic, and contextual factors underlying suicidal behavior and offers a comprehensive understanding of how physical settings and neighborhood characteristics shape vulnerability. These insights inform the development of more targeted and effective suicide prevention strategies.Keywords: suicidal behavior, natural environment, built environment, spatial heterogeneity, Salt Lake City

Introduction

Suicide is one of the leading causes of death in the United States (U.S.) and remains a persistent and escalating public health concern. Since the late 1990s, national suicide rates have steadily risen, reflecting broader societal and mental health challenges. In 2023, the rate reached 14.7 per 100,000 people (Harmon, 2024). Utah consistently reports suicide rates far above the national average, at 20.93 suicides per 100,000 population (Utah Department of Health and Human Services, 2025). Salt Lake City (SLC), as the capital and largest city of Utah, exhibits even higher suicide rates. These alarming rates highlight not only a severe mental health crisis but also broader risks to individual, family, and community well‐being. The consequences extend beyond the loss of life to reduced economic productivity, strained health care systems, and weakened social cohesion, ultimately threatening long‐term sustainability. However, the complex mechanisms underlying suicidal behavior within U.S. cities in general, and SLC in particular, remain underinvestigated.

Suicidal behavior is multifaceted and deeply influenced by individual and local contextual factors. A wide range of methods, including self‐inflicted injuries, drug overdose, falls from heights, and firearm use, pose significant challenges for timely intervention and rescue efforts (Zarkowski & Avery, 2006). Suicide risk shows a significant hereditary component, with research identifying specific gene variants such as APH1B and SUCLA2 linked to a higher likelihood of suicidal behavior (Näher et al., 2020). Additional risk factors include mental illness, alcohol consumption, and smoking, which tend to exert greater influence on already vulnerable populations (Ivbijaro et al., 2021). Studies also explored how socioeconomic status (SES) and demographic characteristics, such as income, education, and race, shape suicidal behavior. More recently, research has expanded to consider the physical environment (Rehkopf & Buka, 2006). The natural environment, particularly access to green space, is shown to protect mental health by reducing stress, alleviating negative emotions, and fostering psychological restoration (Asri et al., 2022; Helbich et al., 2018; Ryan et al., 2024). Growing evidence also highlights the role of the built environment in influencing suicidal behavior. For instance, walkable neighborhoods are often associated with reduced suicidal behavior, as they encourage physical activity and promote social interaction; both are known to buffer stress and support mental well‐being (Jiang et al., 2021; Wang et al., 2020). At the same time, certain urban settings such as hotels warrant monitoring, as they are sometimes chosen for suicide due to their anonymity and reduced chances of intervention or rescue (Weisman & Worden, 1972; Zarkowski & Avery, 2006).

Although this emerging literature provides valuable insights, several critical research gaps remain, limiting our understanding of how environmental and neighborhood contexts shape suicidal behavior. First, much existing research focuses on individual‐level characteristics or relies on large administrative units, such as counties or census tracts, leaving the fine‐scale neighborhood environments (e.g., census block groups) largely understudied, even though environmental stressors and resource access often vary at this level. Second, although mental‐health benefits of green space and walkability are widely documented, their specific associations with suicidal behavior remain mixed and insufficiently explored, especially in U.S. urban settings. Third, access to hotels, an environmental factor that may influence suicide risk by providing privacy and reducing opportunities for intervention, has rarely been examined. Finally, few studies examine how local socioeconomic contexts moderate these environment–suicide relationships, or whether these associations vary spatially across neighborhoods. These gaps highlight the need for a neighborhood‐scale, context‐sensitive, and spatially explicit framework to advance our understanding of urban suicide behavior.

To address this complexity, this paper provides a comprehensive analysis of how the natural environment, the built environment, and SES are related to suicidal behavior. Special attention is given to green space and hotel accessibility, as well as their interactions with SES. In addition, we examine the spatial heterogeneity of these factors, acknowledging that these associations may vary across neighborhoods. This study makes three key contributions. First, we propose an integrated analytical framework to assess diverse potential risk factors associated with suicidal behavior. Second, we investigate the distinct and joint associations of green space and hotel access with suicidal behavior, emphasizing the moderating role of local SES. Third, we explore spatial heterogeneity in these relationships, underscoring the importance of place‐specific analysis for understanding and addressing suicide risk.

Literature Review and Analytical Framework

Socioeconomic Conditions and Suicidal Behavior

Multiple socioeconomic factors shape suicidal behavior. Economic hardship and financial difficulties substantially increase suicide risk, as individuals under financial strain often experience hopelessness, isolation, and a sense of loss, which can contribute to mental health crises and suicidal thoughts (Devitt, 2020; Zalsman et al., 2020). Unemployment, in particular, exacerbates financial stress and is consistently linked to more suicide attempts (Elbogen et al., 2020; Näher et al., 2020). While stable employment and higher income typically protect against suicide risk, precarious or low‐paid work, especially among women, is associated with elevated psychological strain and increased vulnerability to suicide (Nomura et al., 2021). Moreover, evidence suggests a potential threshold effect: when the unemployment rate reaches very high levels, its marginal impact on suicide may diminish (Pescosolido et al., 2020; Stuckler et al., 2009).

Race and family dynamics also influence neighborhood‐level patterns of suicidal behavior. Although non‐Hispanic African Americans have historically exhibited lower suicide rates than Whites (Rockett et al., 2010), disproportionate exposure to structural disadvantages, such as poverty, residential segregation, and community violence, can heighten risk. Consequently, neighborhoods with a larger proportion of non‐Hispanic African Americans often report elevated suicide rates (Platt et al., 2022). Family size further influences suicide risk. Larger families typically provide stronger emotional support systems, which help buffer stress and mental health challenges. Research links larger family size to lower suicide rates (Denney, 2010; Denney et al., 2009). Additionally, greater public investment in social welfare and targeted support for low‐income populations can reduce suicidal behavior by easing their economic burdens (Stack, 2021).

Natural Environment, Green Space and Suicidal Behavior

Recent research increasingly emphasizes the role of the natural environment in shaping suicidal behavior. Environmental stressors, such as climate conditions, air pollution, and natural disasters, affect mental health and societal stability, thereby influencing suicidal behavior (Helama et al., 2013; Lester, 2021). High temperatures can impair neurotransmitter function, contribute to physical and mental exhaustion (Shoib et al., 2023), and are frequently linked to increased suicide rates. The impact of precipitation remains less certain: while low sunlight exposure on rainy days may reduce serotonin levels and disrupt mood, empirical findings are inconsistent, with some studies reporting weak or null associations between rainfall and suicide rates (Asri et al., 2022; Hernández‐Calle & Bravo‐Ortiz, 2021; Hiltunen et al., 2012). Air pollution is another critical factor, as it can trigger systemic inflammation and exacerbate psychiatric conditions such as anxiety, depression, and mood disorders, ultimately leading to a higher incidence of suicide (Bakian et al., 2015). Similarly, natural disasters, such as floods, hurricanes, heatwaves, and droughts, can inflict psychological trauma and economic hardship, disrupting social networks, displacing populations, and heightening emotional distress, all of which increases vulnerability to suicide (Heanoy & Brown, 2024; Makwana, 2019).

Among natural environments, green space has garnered much attention, especially since the COVID‐19 pandemic. Green space provides opportunities for physical activity, relaxation, and psychological restoration, benefits that became especially critical during periods of social distancing and lockdowns (Li & Wei, 2023). Access to green space is consistently associated with reduced stress and improved mood regulation, both of which help protect against suicidal behavior (Bolanis et al., 2024). Empirical evidence shows that neighborhoods with more green space report lower levels of suicidal behavior (Helbich et al., 2018). At the global scale, green space exposure may reduce suicide rates by as much 31%, primarily through mechanisms such as emotional restoration, increased social contact, and enhanced mental resilience (Asri et al., 2022).

Built Environment: Walkability and Accommodation

Growing evidence suggests that the built environment plays an important role in shaping suicide risk. Urban centers often concentrate a variety of public goods and services, as well as social and employment hubs; thus, compact urban form and proximity to city centers may help reduce suicidal behavior by improving access to resources and fostering social engagement (Jiang et al., 2021). However, large population size and high residential density may also create overcrowding, resource competition, and social stress, all of which can elevate suicide risk (Satherley et al., 2022). Contradictory findings also exist: some studies associate dense urban areas with less suicidal behavior, possibly due to greater social integration and service accessibility (Hirsch & Cukrowicz, 2014; Vichi et al., 2020). At the same time, increased regional centralization may elevate suicide risk when it leads to social isolation, economic challenges, and limited access to resources and mental health services (Ivey‐Stephenson, 2017).

Walkability has emerged as a protective factor. Walkable neighborhoods can mitigate social isolation and reduce depression, both key risk factors for suicidal behavior (Berke et al., 2007; Finucane et al., 2022; India‐Aldana et al., 2021). Similarly, land mixed use can serve a variety of landscape functions, enriching visual diversity, promoting physical activity, and encouraging social interactions (Shen et al., 2022), all of which may contribute to lower suicide risk. Job‐housing mix can provide more job opportunities and decrease the unemployment risk, which can help to mitigate suicidal behavior by decreasing financial stress and increasing self‐worth (Milner et al., 2013; Wei et al., 2024).

Hotel accessibility, though less studied, presents unique risks. Hotels offer private and relatively isolated environments where individuals may act without the possibility of intervention by loved ones or others (Chen et al., 2023). Studies have documented strong associations between hotels and high suicide rates in King County (Washington), Clark County (Nevada), and Wayne County (Michigan) (Gemar et al., 2008; Zarkowski & Avery, 2006). Hotels particularly reduce the chance of timely intervention in suicides such as drug overdose or jumping from heights (Chen et al., 2023; Zarkowski et al., 2019). Hanzlick et al. (1990) found that jumping from high‐rise hotels accounts for 1% of all suicides, with 63% of these cases involving White men (Hanzlick et al., 1990).

Few studies, however, have explored the role of hotel registration in suicide risk. Using medical examiner case files from King County, Washington, Zarkowski and Avery (2006) reported that individuals registered at local hotels had higher suicide rates than the general population, with residents more likely to die by suicide than out‐of‐town hotel guests. These findings were later confirmed by Gemar et al. (2008), based on coroner case files in Clark County, Nevada, produced similar results. Zarkowski et al. (2019) further investigated the impact of hotel registrations on suicide rates, using coroner case files from Cuyahoga County, Ohio; Travis County, Texas; and Orange County, California. Their results echoed those of their earlier study (Zarkowski & Avery, 2006) and also showed that drug overdoses accounted for a significantly higher percentage of suicides in hotel rooms compared to the general population.

Analytical Framework

Although previous studies have identified a range of potential risk factors for suicidal behavior, few have developed a comprehensive research framework that examines how natural environment, built environment, and socioeconomic conditions jointly relate to suicide patterns. In particular, the relationships between green space, hotel accessibility, and suicidal behavior remain underexplored. Most existing research focuses on individual‐level factors, such as SES and demographic characteristics, or, less commonly, on broader census tract or county‐level patterns. These approaches often overlook neighborhood‐scale variations, such as those observable at the census block group level. Moreover, existing studies rarely consider how local context moderates the effects of green space and hotel accessibility on suicide outcomes, and spatial heterogeneity in these relationships remains insufficiently addressed.

To address these gaps, we construct an analytical framework for SLC to investigate potential risk factors for suicidal behavior, with particular focus on green space and hotel accessibility (Figure 1).

Analysis framework of the study.

Our analytical framework encompasses three categories of influencing factors. The first includes natural environmental variables: green space, temperature, and precipitation. The second captures built environment factors, such as hotel accessibility, total population size, residential density, walkability, job‐housing mix and the regional centrality level. The third incorporates socio‐economic and demographic factors, including income level, race composition, labor force participation, and family size.

Within this framework, green space and hotel accessibility serve as key independent variables. Greater access to green space may be associated with lower levels of suicide behavior by offering opportunities for relaxation, relief from environmental stressors, and social interaction (Asri et al., 2022; Helbich et al., 2018). Green space may also indirectly reduce suicide risk by moderating local climate conditions and reducing air pollution (Asri et al., 2022). In contrast, high hotel accessibility may be linked to elevated suicide risk by providing private and low‐intervention settings that reduce opportunities for timely intervention (Zarkowski & Avery, 2006). These contrasting dynamics highlight the importance of examining both protective and risk‐enhancing features of the urban landscape.

Data and Methods

Data

We obtained suicide data from the Salt Lake City Police Department, covering the years 2011–2015. According to the Salt Lake City Police Department (SLCPD, 2023) Procedure Manual, incidents involving suicide deaths, as well as threats or attempts of suicide, are formally classified and documented under the department's Crisis Intervention protocols. These records are typically generated from police calls for service and include information such as the individual's name, gender, race, and address. The data set includes both suicide deaths and attempts, recorded as crime cases. Following Moutier (2025), who defines suicidal behavior as a spectrum ranging from preparatory actions and attempts to completed suicide, we included both deaths and attempts in our sample. After selecting relevant records and geocoding individuals' residential addresses, we identified 1,094 valid cases across the 5‐year period, comprising 56 suicide deaths and 1,038 suicide attempts. Hotel location data were collected from the Expedia website; we assigned hotel counts to each census block based on spatial matching. We derived average annual temperature from the 30‐year PRISM (Parameter‐elevation Regressions on Independent Slopes Model) climate data set (1991–2020), which provides 800 × 800 m spatial resolution for the U.S. All remaining variables were drawn from the 2011 to 2015 American Community Survey 5‐Year Estimates. Methods

To comprehensively assess neighborhood‐level associations between environmental factors and suicidal behavior in SLC, this study employs three analytical methods in a sequential and complementary manner. First, hot spot analysis is used to identify spatial clustering of suicidal behavior, green space, and hotel accessibility, determining whether these variables exhibit statistically significant local concentrations. Second, a generalized linear model (GLM) quantifies the relationships between natural, built, and socioeconomic factors and suicidal behavior, while evaluating how green space and hotel accessibility contribute to suicide risk after controlling for other covariates. Finally, geographically weighted regression (GWR) captures spatial heterogeneity in these relationships, revealing where and how the strength or direction of these associations varies across neighborhoods. Together, these methods offer a robust, multi‐scalar understanding of both global and local drivers of suicidal behavior within the urban environment.

Hot Spot Analysis

Hotspot analysis is a geospatial technique for detecting spatial clustering and examining how events or characteristics relate across space. Hotspots indicate areas where the observed concentration of events exceeds what would be expected under random distribution. To uncover these patterns, researchers often apply spatial autocorrelation and cluster analysis techniques. To capture localized spatial relationships, we use local indicators of spatial association (LISA), particularly the Gettis‐Ord Gi* statistic, which assesses whether neighboring areas exhibit similar values based on the degree of contiguity between these units within a specified radius (Anselin, 1995). We also use the Gettis‐Ord Gi* statistic to determine whether green space, access to hotels, and suicidal behavior exhibit spatial autocorrelation, respectively.

Geographically Weighted Regression

GWR is used to examine the spatial variation of potential risk factors' effect on suicidal behavior. GWR allows us to explore spatial heterogeneity by examining how the relationships between variables vary across census block groups. GWR processes operate within a small spatial scale (u, v) rather than the global scale. Although GWR is usually used for Gaussian distribution, it can also be utilized with Poisson distributions (Krivoruchko, 2011). Therefore, we employed GWR with Poisson regression in our analysis. Spatial Distribution and Clustering of Suicide and Physical Environment

Suicidal behavior in SLC is spatially uneven, with the highest suicide rates concentrated in the downtown area (Figure 2). Clear clustering of suicide cases is observed in downtown and adjacent neighborhoods, whereas the southeastern part of the city reports comparatively fewer incidents (Figures 3 and 4). Downtown is characterized by greater socioeconomic disadvantage, including higher rates of unemployment and homelessness, alongside challenges such as elevated crime and environmental pollution. These stressors likely contribute to the elevated incidence of suicidal behavior in this area.

For natural environmental factors, more greenery is significantly associated with less suicidal behavior, whereas higher precipitation levels are linked to increased suicidal behavior. In terms of built environmental factors, hotel accessibility, large population size, and regional centrality are associated with higher levels of suicidal behavior, while areas with greater walkability show a reduction. Socioeconomic factors also play an important role. Neighborhoods with high income level and large family size are related to less suicidal behavior, whereas those with a high proportion in the labor force tend to experience more suicidal behavior.

To complement the analysis of suicide counts, we also modeled suicide rates as a continuous outcome using multiple linear regression (Model 5). Results align with earlier findings: more green space is associated with a lower suicide rate, while higher hotel accessibility and urban centrality predict greater suicide risk. Larger family size again appears protective.

Heterogeneity in the Effects of Green Space and Hotels on Suicide

Among the socioeconomic moderators, the protective effect of green space on suicidal behavior is weaker in neighborhoods with higher proportions of non‐Hispanic African Americans and in high‐income areas, as shown in models 1 and 2 in Table 5. Family size also moderates both green space‐suicidal behavior and hotel accessibility‐suicidal behavior relationships. A larger family size diminishes the protective effect of green space in reducing suicidal behavior, while amplifying the influence of hotel accessibility in facilitating suicidal behavior.

Built environmental factors also moderate these relationships. In regions with larger populations, the protective association between green space and suicidal behavior weakens, while the positive association between hotel accessibility and suicide risk becomes stronger. Conversely, in regions with a high degree of jobs‐housing mix, the protective effect of green space is amplified, further lowering suicide risk.

Spatial Heterogeneity in the Potential Risk Factors of Suicidal Behavior

To examine spatial heterogeneity in the factors associated with suicidal behavior, we use GWR regression to analyze local variations in coefficient estimates. Table 6 summarizes the results. Most factors demonstrate spatial homogeneity, with over 80% of census block groups showing consistent directional associations with suicidal behavior. However, two factors, specifically job‐housing mix and racial composition show significant spatial heterogeneity in the relationship with suicidal behavior.

Conclusion and Discussion

Using census block group data, we examine the demographic characteristics of individuals exhibiting suicidal behavior to highlight demographic differences within this population. Then, we explore the spatial distribution and clustering of suicidal behavior in SLC, focusing on key environmental and socioeconomic risk factors, particularly green space and hotel accessibility. Furthermore, we investigate how the associations of these factors vary spatially and across different neighborhood contexts. Based on these analyses, we report the following findings.

Suicidal behavior is nearly evenly distributed between males and females, with men accounting for 51.1% and women 48.7%. However, there is a pronounced racial disparity: 87.5% of cases involve non‐Hispanic White Americans. This finding is consistent with Curtin et al. (2022), who reported that non‐Hispanic White Americans have higher suicide rates than other racial groups. Nonetheless, the disproportionately high share of non‐Hispanic White individuals in our sample may partly reflect the demographic composition of SLC, where non‐Hispanic White residents make up 64.7% of the total population (Data USA, 2025), rather than indicating a true risk difference. In terms of age distribution, working‐age adults comprise nearly 90% of the cases, with 18–34‐year‐olds representing 45.0% and those aged 35–64 accounting for 44.5%. This concentration among working‐age populations may be linked to stressors such as employment pressures, family responsibilities, and challenges in social integration.

Suicidal behavior in SLC is spatially uneven, with higher concentrations in downtown areas. These regions are characterized by elevated unemployment, homelessness, air pollution, and environmental stressors such as construction and motor vehicle noise. Green space is disproportionately clustered in the more affluent eastern neighborhoods, whereas hotel accessibility is densest in central areas, often overlapping with hotspots for suicidal behavior. Natural environmental factors significantly shape suicidal behavior. More green space is associated with lower levels of suicidal behavior, supporting its role in alleviating stress and promoting psychological resilience (Li & Wei, 2023). Green space may reduce psychological distress by providing restorative environments, encouraging physical activity, and facilitating informal social interaction, which help buffer stress and strengthen coping resources. By contrast, higher precipitation levels correspond to increased suicidal behavior. SLC's long winters, marked by snow and limited sunlight, may contribute to seasonal affective disorders. Precipitation may also elevate physiological stress and reduce opportunities for outdoor activity and social interaction, both of which increase vulnerability (Florido et al., 2021).

The built environmental plays a central role. Hotel accessibility, large population size and regional centrality are related to higher levels of suicidal behavior, while walkable neighborhoods are linked to lower levels. Hotels in particular present a unique risk, offering private and anonymous spaces where individuals can act without intervention (Chen et al., 2023; Zarkowski et al., 2019). High‐rise hotels also facilitate suicide methods such as jumping from heights or drug overdoses. Population size is positively correlated with suicidal behavior, partly reflecting scale effects. High regional centrality may exacerbate psychological stress by intensifying competition for jobs and resources while limiting access to leisure and healthcare facilities (Casant & Helbich, 2022). In contrast, walkable neighborhoods can promote face to face interactions, encourage physical activities, and improve access to healthcare and community support services, which are associated with lower suicide risk (Grasdalsmoen et al., 2020; Verlenden, 2024).

Socioeconomic factors further shape outcomes. Neighborhoods with high income and large family size are associated with reduced suicidal behavior, while a higher proportion of labor force participation correlates with increased risk. High income alleviates financial stress and increases access to healthcare (Meda et al., 2022). Large families often provide stonger emotional support, which buffers against suicidal behavior (Alvarez‐Subiela et al., 2022). Conversely, neighborhoods with a high share of labor force participation may face intensified job competition and wage pressure, raising stress levels (Autor et al., 2023).

When isolating green space and hotel accessibility, results remain robust: green space is consistently associated with lower levels of suicidal behavior, while hotel accessibility is associated with higher levels. However, their effects vary by local context. The protective effect of green space weakens in neighborhoods with higher proportion of non‐Hispanic African Americans, high‐income households, large families, and larger populations. Prior studies suggest that non‐Hispanic African Americans have historically lower suicide rates (Perez‐Rodriguez et al., 2008), which may reduce the marginal benefits of additional green space in these communities. In high‐income neighborhoods, residents often have access to multiple forms of stress relief and health‐promoting resources, potentially dampening the incremental impact of green space. In large families, stronger within‐household support may partially substitute for the social and emotional benefits gained from public green space, while higher consumption and housing costs can intensify financial strain and blunt green space's protective role (Espenshade et al., 1983). In areas with larger populations, increased pressure on infrastructure, services, and environmental quality can reduce overall quality of life and elevate suicidal behavior, thereby diminishing the protective effects of green space. By contrast, in neighborhoods with high levels of job–housing mix, green space appears to exert stronger protective effects, possibly by offsetting stressors associated with dense activity patterns, traffic congestion, and complex social interactions (Asri et al., 2022). Access to hotels shows the opposite pattern: its positive association with suicidal behavior is stronger in large‐family and high‐population areas, where hotels may provide private and low‐intervention spaces in already stressed environments. Together, these contextual interactions suggest that the same quantity of green space can have different implications for suicidal behavior across neighborhoods, underscoring the need for place‐sensitive green space planning and hotel‐based suicide prevention strategies.

Spatially, most predictors show homogeneity effects across SLC, but job‐housing mix and racial composition vary spatially. In most neighborhoods, a high job‐housing mix is associated with reduced suicidal behavior by increasing job opportunities and reducing commuting burdens (Cervero, 1996). However, in downtown areas, it is associated with higher suicide rates, likely due to environmental stressors such as traffic noise, waste, and crime (Foord, 2010). Similarly, while a higher proportion of non‐Hispanic African Americans is generally associated with more suicidal behavior, this relationship reverses in wealthier eastern neighborhoods, where African American residents tend to have higher socioeconomic status and educational attainment, both of which are protective factors (Kem C. Gardner Policy Institute, 2023).

Our findings highlight the urgent need for targeted prevention strategies. Expanding green space in underserved neighborhoods and developing hotel‐based prevention strategies, such as staff training and mental health outreach, may help reduce suicide risk. However, interventions must be context‐specific, as the protective effect of green space is weaker in neighborhoods with higher proportions of non‐Hispanic African Americans, higher incomes, larger populations, and larger family sizes. Hotels in dense, disadvantaged neighborhoods should be prioritized for suicide prevention partnerships. At the national level, broader strategies remain critical, including promoting financial security, improving housing stability, expanding access to mental health care, and restricting access to lethal means (Iskander & Crosby, 2021). At the community level, training frontline staff, enhancing awareness campaigns, and building supportive environments can foster resilience (Hofstra et al., 2020). At the individual level, family and peer support, regular physical activity, and mindfulness practices can help manage suicidal thoughts (Ivbijaro et al., 2020).

This study has limitations. First, due to data availability, the suicide data span only 2011 to 2015, and risk factors may evolve over time. Second, underreporting of suicide attempts, especially those not recorded by police, may limit the completeness of our analysis. Third, while we identified significant predictors of suicidal behavior, underlying mechanisms may need on‐the‐ground investigation. Future research may empirically investigate these pathways to provide a more robust understanding of causal dynamics.

Availability Statement

All data used in this study are publicly available, except for the confidential suicide case data. Crime event data for 2011–2015 were provided by the Salt Lake City Police Department (Wei et al., 2021), and suicide‐related incidents were selected for analysis in this study. Green space data were extracted from the 2011 National Land Cover Database, available at https://www.mrlc.gov/data. Average annual temperature and precipitation were calculated from 800 m × 800 m raster data from the PRISM Climate Group for the 1991–2020 climate normals, available at https://prism.oregonstate.edu/normals/. Hotel data were collected from Expedia (https://www.expedia.com/) and aggregated to the census block group level. Data on total population, the proportion of Black residents, median household income, the proportion of the labor force population, and average family size were obtained from the 2011 to 2015 American Community Survey (ACS), available through NHGIS at https://data2.nhgis.org/main. Residential density, walkability index, jobs–housing mix index, and regional centrality index data were obtained from the Smart Location Database Version 2, provided by the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency, available at https://www.epa.gov/smartgrowth/smart‐location‐mapping. The analysis was conducted using ArcGIS, RStudio, and Stata. These software packages are available from Esri, Posit, and StataCorp, respectively, at https://www.esri.com/en‐us/home, https://posit.co/download/rstudio‐desktop, and https://www.stata.com/products/.
