Clemastine alleviates cognitive dysfunction after SAH by targeting neuronal CNTN1 to regulate the ADA-adenosine pathway
Authors: Long-xiang Li, Bo-yang Wei, Lei Jin, Yu Wu, Ze-yu Yang, Can Li, Jia-ming Zhou, Yu Song, Wen-zhi Gong, Yuan-hao Ou-Yang, Hao Huang, Xin Feng, Shen-quan Guo, Wen-ping Cheng, Ran Li, Shu-yin Liang, Shi-xing Su, Fa Jin, Xin Zhang, Yan-chao Liu, Chuan-zhi Duan, Wen-chao Liu, Xi-feng Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Demyelination following subarachnoid hemorrhage (SAH) is an important cause of cognitive impairment in patients. However, its underlying mechanisms remain unclear. This study aimed to elucidate the molecular basis of SAH-related demyelination and identify potential treatments. Contactin-1 (CNTN1) levels in the cerebrospinal fluid were significantly decreased in patients following SAH. Higher levels of CNTN1 expression correlated positively with Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) scores. However, whether and in which specific cell types CNTN1 expression is downregulated in the SAH brain parenchyma remains unknown. Our single-cell RNA sequencing revealed that Cntn1 was downregulated specifically in neurons, but not in oligodendrocytes. Then knockdown of Cntn1 in neurons via adeno-associated virus (AAV) significantly exacerbated cognitive impairment in mice with SAH. To investigate the mechanisms by which CNTN1 downregulation contributes to cognitive impairment, we performed transcriptome sequencing. This analysis revealed a significant decline in Ada, Plp1, and Mbp following CNTN1 knockdown in neurons. We therefore hypothesized that CNTN1 regulates neuronal ADA and existence of CNTN1-ADA-ADORA2B signaling axis. Through this axis, downregulation of CNTN1 after SAH might lead to demyelination. To validate our hypothesis, we overexpressed Cntn1 in murine neurons using AAV. Results demonstrated that Cntn1 overexpression effectively improved SAH-induced cognitive dysfunction, which can be blocked by ADA enzyme inhibitor. Furthermore, clemastine administration significantly attenuated cognitive impairment following SAH, by mitigating demyelination. Collectively, our results identify neuronal CNTN1 as a critical regulator. Its downregulation exacerbates demyelination through ADA after SAH. Importantly, clemastine might be potential drug used in SAH patients.

Introduction

Subarachnoid hemorrhage (SAH), a severe form of hemorrhagic stroke, carries a mortality rate of nearly 30% [1, 2]. Among survivors, cognitive dysfunction affects about 73% [3, 4], and over 91% of patients who have poor quality of life and cannot fully return to work [5]. Cognitive decline after SAH results from multiple pathological processes, including white matter demyelination, neuroimmune dysregulation, and impaired synaptic plasticity [6, 7]. Among these, white matter demyelination is a key contributor [8]. Myelin, produced by oligodendrocytes, is vital for axonal insulation and signal transmission [9]. Demyelination arises from intrinsic oligodendrocyte injury—such as mitochondrial dysfunction—and extrinsic immune-mediated damage by inflammatory cytokines and glial activation [9–13]. Neural activity critically regulates oligodendrocyte precursor cell proliferation, differentiation, and myelin formation in an axon subtype–specific manner, underscoring its importance in myelin maintenance [9, 13, 14]. However, its protective mechanisms after SAH remain unclear [15].

CNTN1, a neuronal glycosylphosphatidylinositol-anchored cell adhesion molecule, is crucial for axonal-glial interactions. Its deficiency is linked to demyelinating disorders such as multiple sclerosis and chronic inflammatory demyelinating polyneuropathy (CIDP) [16, 17]. In mice, CNTN1 forms a trimeric complex with Caspr and NF155 at the paranodal junctions to maintain myelin integrity [18, 19]. Our single-cell RNA sequencing revealed significant downregulation of Cntn1 in neurons following SAH. Clemastine, an FDA-approved antihistamine for allergic rhinitis and urticaria, has shown neuroprotective and remyelination-promoting effects in multiple sclerosis and ischemic stroke models [20–22]. Furthermore, recent studies indicate that clemastine upregulates Cntn1 mRNA expression [23]. However, its role in mitigating demyelination after SAH remains unclear. This study investigates clemastine’s therapeutic potential and underlying mechanisms in SAH to explore new strategies for post-SAH demyelination treatment.

Adenosine deaminase (ADA) maintains cerebral homeostasis by converting adenosine to inosine [24, 25]. It is involved in many biological process including reducing autophagy, confering neuroprotection [26], demyelinating, neurological disorders such as seizures, developmental delay and hypotonia [24, 27–29]. In mice, downregulation of ADA expression leads to intracellular accumulation of adenosine in neurons, which is subsequently promoted for extracellular transport. Adenosine A2B receptor (ADORA2B) is preferentially activated under pathophysiological conditions where extracellular adenosine levels are markedly elevated, such as hypoxia, tissue injury, and hemorrhage. In the central nervous system, ADORA2B is expressed on multiple cell types, including microglia, astrocytes, and oligodendrocytes. Notably, emerging evidence suggests its specific involvement in oligodendrocyte precursor cell differentiation and myelination, positioning it as a potential regulator of white matter integrity [30, 31]. However, despite its recognized role as a sensor of pathological adenosine surges, the precise function and upstream signaling of ADORA2B in demyelinating pathologies after SAH, remain poorly understood. The study investigated whether neuronal CNTN1 regulates the expression of ADA and influences ADORA2B’s activity or expression level in oligodendrocytes.

In this study, we found that lower levels of CNTN1 in the cerebrospinal fluid of patients were associated with cognitive impairment after SAH. Complementing this, our single-cell RNA sequencing data revealed a significant reduction of Cntn1 specifically in neurons of the murine brain parenchyma following SAH. Using AAV to achieve neuron-specific Cntn1 knockdown, we elucidated its role in post-SAH demyelination and cognitive dysfunction, and characterized its regulatory interplay with the ADA-adenosine-ADORA2B axis. We also evaluated the therapeutic efficacy of clemastine treatment in alleviating demyelination, highlighting neuronal CNTN1 as a key regulator of oligodendrocyte function and a potential therapeutic target for post-SAH cognitive dysfunction.

Materials and methods

ELISA kits for CNTN1 (Jianglai Biotechnology, Shanghai, China, Cat#JL19524-96T), phosphate buffer saline (Servicebio Technology, Wuhan, China, Cat#G0002-2L), tween 20 (Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China, Cat#ST1726), 24-well plate (Servicebio Technology, Wuhan, China, Cat#CCP-24H), citric acid antigen retrieval solution (Servicebio Technology, Wuhan, China, Cat#G1201-1L), poly-L-lysine coated coverslips (Guangzhou Yongjin Biotechnology, Guangzhou, China, Cat#LY14-PLL), cell counting kit-8 (MedChemExpress, New Jersey, USA, Cat#HY-K0301), 4% paraformaldehyde universal tissue fixative (Guangzhou Yongjin Biotechnology, Guangzhou, China, Cat#YJ0002), clemastine fumarate (MedChemExpress, New Jersey, USA, Cat#HY-B0298A), cladribine (Merck KGaA, Germany, Cat#Y0000639), RIPA lysis buffer (Yeasen Biotechnology, Shanghai, China, Cat#20101ES60), ELISA kits for NfL (Proteintech Group, Wuhan, China, Cat#KE00305), primary & secondary antibody diluent for WB (Yeasen Biotechnology, Shanghai, China, Cat#36206ES76), immunol staining primary antibody dilution buffer (Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China, Cat#P0103), immunol fluorescence staining secondary antibody dilution buffer (Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China, Cat#P0108), glycine (Shanghai Macklin Biochemical Technology, Shanghai, China, Cat#G800880-5kg), Tris (Shanghai Macklin Biochemical Technology, Shanghai, China, Cat#T819512).

Collection of human cerebrospinal fluid samples

Between October 2017 and July 2023, a total of 14 healthy controls and 185 patients diagnosed with subarachnoid hemorrhage were prospectively enrolled at Zhujiang Hospital, Southern Medical University (Ethics Approval No: 2023-KY-188-01). At Zhujiang Hospital, when patients are admitted, they are asked whether they are willing to allow surplus samples from clinical tests to be used for research. For those who consent, after the necessary clinical tests are completed, the leftover samples are sent to a biobank for storage. Once the corresponding study has received ethics approval, researchers may retrieve the required samples from the biobank. As a result, some samples are collected prior to ethics approval. All participants provided written informed consent prior to inclusion in the study, which was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Patients were excluded if they had a history of central nervous system disorders, significant comorbidities predating the subarachnoid hemorrhage, or evidence of organ dysfunction within six months prior to enrollment. Cerebrospinal fluid samples from SAH patients were obtained via lumbar puncture. Control cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) samples were obtained from individuals undergoing spinal anesthesia for non-neurological surgical procedures. Immediately after collection, samples were centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 15 min at 4 °C to remove cellular components, and the supernatants were subsequently stored at −80 °C until analysis [32, 33]. Comprehensive clinical data—including baseline characteristics, presenting symptoms, and outcomes—were systematically extracted and curated from electronic medical records.

Animals

Wild-type (WT) male and female C57BL/6 mice (20–25 g) were obtained from the Guangdong Provincial Animal Center and used to establish the SAH model. All animal procedures were approved by the Ethics Committee of Zhujiang Hospital, Southern Medical University (Approval No: LAEC-2024-297) and were performed in strict accordance with Chinese Regulations on the Management of Experimental Animals. Mice were housed in an accredited facility at Zhujiang Hospital under standardized environmental conditions, including controlled temperature and humidity, a 12-h light/dark cycle, and ad libitum access to food and water.

SAH mouse model

SAH model was induced by intravascular perforation, following established protocols [26, 34]. Mice weighing 25–30 g were anesthetized with 1.0%–1.5% isoflurane in oxygen. A sharpened nylon suture was inserted via the left external carotid artery and advanced to perforate the internal carotid artery (ICA), thereby inducing SAH. For sham-operated controls, the suture was advanced into the internal carotid artery (ICA) without inducing perforation. Throughout the procedure, continuous monitoring was performed for vital physiological metrics, including mean arterial pressure, arterial oxygen tension, and cardiac rate. All surgical procedures were conducted by experienced investigators blinded to group allocation. Both male and female mice were included. To standardize the assessment of subarachnoid hemorrhage severity, a validated scoring system was used to evaluate subarachnoid blood distribution in six regions of the basal cistern, as previously described [27]. The total subarachnoid hemorrhage score was calculated as the sum of regional scores across all six cisternal areas. To guarantee model validity and reproducibility, mice exhibiting total scores under 8 were removed from subsequent analyses.

Single-cell RNA sequencing

Nucleus isolation

Nuclei were isolated using a commercial Nucleus Isolation Kit (SHBIO, Shanghai, China, Cat#52009-10) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Prior to use, all reagents were supplemented with RNase inhibitor (Merck KGaA, Germany, Sigma-Aldrich, Cat#3335399001). Briefly, tissue samples were minced and transferred into 5 mL tubes containing pre-cooled lysis buffer, followed by gentle mixing and incubation on ice for 3 min. The lysate was then filtered through a 40 μm cell strainer (Merck KGaA, Germany, Sigma-Aldrich, Cat#BAH136800040) to remove debris. Nuclear concentration and viability were assessed using the SeekMate Tinitan Fluorescence Cell Counter (SeekGene, Beijing, China, Model#M002C) with AO/PI staining. Additionally, a 0.4% trypan blue (Sangon Biotech, Shanghai, China, Cat#E607320-0001) staining was performed, and nuclear morphology was examined under a 40× microscope (Jiangnan Novel Optics, Nanjing, China, Model#XD-202). Only samples with intact nuclear membranes and minimal cytoplasmic contamination were used for subsequent library preparation and sequencing.

Preparation and sequencing of single-nucleus RNA-seq libraries

Single-nucleus RNA sequencing (snRNA-seq) libraries were constructed using the SeekOne® DD Single Cell 3’ Library Preparation Kit (SeekGene, Beijing, China, Catalog No. K00202) following the manufacturer’s protocol. In brief, a defined number of isolated nuclei were resuspended in reverse transcription master mix and loaded into the sample well of a SeekOne® DD Chip S3. Barcoded Hydrogel Beads (BHBs) and partitioning oil were then dispensed into their respective wells on the same chip. Droplet emulsion generation was performed immediately thereafter. Within the droplets, reverse transcription was carried out at 42 °C for 90 min, followed by enzyme inactivation at 85 °C for 5 min. The emulsion was then broken to recover the cDNA, which was purified and subsequently amplified by PCR. The amplified cDNA product was cleaned, fragmented, end-repaired, A-tailed, and finally ligated to Illumina sequencing adapters. An indexing PCR was performed to enrich for fragments representing the 3’ polyadenylated ends of transcripts, each containing a cell-specific barcode and a unique molecular identifier (UMI). The final indexed libraries were purified using VAHTS DNA Clean Beads (Vazyme, Nanjing, China, Cat#N411-01), quantified with a Qubit fluorometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Massachusetts, USA, Cat#Q33226), and assessed for size distribution using a Bio-Fragment Analyzer (Bioptic, Taiwan, China, Cat#Qsep400). Pooled libraries were sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq X Plus platform with a paired-end 150 bp (PE150) configuration.

Primary neuron culture

Reagents and instruments

Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Massachusetts, USA, Cat#11965092), fetal bovine serum (FBS, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Massachusetts, USA, Cat# 10099141), Neurobasal medium (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Massachusetts, USA, Cat#21103049), and B27 supplement (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Massachusetts, USA, Cat#17504044). Accutase was obtained from Merck (Germany, Cat#A6964). Poly-L-lysine-coated coverslips were purchased from Yongjin Biotechnology Co., Ltd (Guangzhou, China, Cat#LY14-PLL). The microtubule-associated protein 2 (MAP2) antibody was acquired from Proteintech (Wuhan, China, Cat#17490-1-AP), and penicillin-streptomycin solution was sourced from HyClone (State of Utah, USA, Cat#SV30010). A CO2 incubator (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Massachusetts, USA, Cat#i160), laminar flow biosafety cabinet (NUAIRE, Minnesota, USA, Cat#NU-543-400S) and a fluorescence inverted microscope (Nikon, Japan, Cat#Ti2-E & Cat#AX NIS-Elements 5.4) were used for imaging.

Reagent preparation and primary midbrain neuron culture

Reagent preparation: Accutase was aliquoted for single use. Neuron seeding medium: DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin-streptomycin. Neuron culture medium: Neurobasal medium supplemented with 2% B27, 0.5% penicillin-streptomycin, and 1 mM glutamine. Culture substrate preparation: Poly-L-lysine-coated coverslips were washed three times with PBS for 5 min each before use.

Isolation and digestion of neonatal midbrain neurons: pregnant C57BL/6 mice were anesthetized and euthanized, and the fetuses were harvested using sterile ophthalmic scissors and forceps. The neonates were immersed in 75% ethanol for 2 min, then rinsed thoroughly with ice-cold PBS. Pups were euthanized by decapitation, and their heads were transferred into ice-cold DMEM. Under a stereomicroscope, the scalp and skull were carefully opened, and the brains were dissected. The meninges were meticulously removed to isolate the cerebral cortex. The midbrain tissue was rinsed twice with PBS and transferred to a 10-cm dish containing DMEM. The tissue was minced into 1–2 mm fragments using a pipette tip and enzymatically digested with 3 mL Accutase at 37 °C for 20 min, with gentle agitation every 5 min. Digestion was terminated by adding an equal volume of DMEM containing 10% FBS, followed by gentle trituration using a 1 mL pipette tip.

Purification and culture of single neurons: the cell suspension was gently pipetted and allowed to settle for 2 min. The supernatant containing dissociated single cells was collected and transferred to a centrifuge tube. The remaining tissue aggregates were resuspended in fresh DMEM, and the process was repeated three times. Pooled supernatants were passed through a 100-mesh nylon filter and centrifuged at 1000 rpm for 5 min. The supernatant was removed, and the cell pellet was reconstituted in neuron seeding medium (DMEM supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum). Cells were seeded into 24-well plates containing poly-L-lysine-coated coverslips, gently agitated to ensure even distribution, and incubated at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 atmosphere. After 4 h, the medium was replaced with pre-warmed neuron culture medium (Neurobasal supplemented with B27), and cellular debris was removed with a DMEM wash. A complete medium change was performed after 12 h, followed by half-medium changes every 2–3 days. Neurons were cultured for 7 days prior to subsequent experiments. Morphological assessment and imaging were conducted using a fluorescence inverted microscope.

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM)

TEM was performed as previously described. Following euthanasia, mice were transcardially perfused with 4% paraformaldehyde at 37 °C for tissue fixation. White matter areas were carefully dissected and post-fixed in 2% osmium tetroxide dissolved in cacodylate buffer. The specimens were then stained with 2% uranyl acetate, dehydrated through a graded ethanol series, and embedded in epoxy-araldite resin. Ultrathin sections were cut using a Leica Microsystems ultramicrotome, followed by contrasting stains with uranyl acetate and lead citrate. TEM imaging was performed to evaluate ultrastructural features [35].

Antibodies for Western blot and immunofluorescence staining

For Western blot analysis, the following primary antibodies were used: anti-β-Actin (1:1000, Proteintech, Wuhan, China, Cat#81115-1-RR), anti-MBP (1:2000, Solarbio, Beijing, China, Cat#K009767M), anti-NF155 (1:1000, Cell Signaling Technology, Massachusetts, USA, Cat#D7B6O), anti-Caspr (1:1000, Proteintech, Wuhan, China, Cat#55417-1-AP), anti-CNTN1 (1:5000, Proteintech, Wuhan, China, Cat#13843-1-AP), anti-ADA (1:2000, Proteintech, Wuhan, China, Cat#13328-1-AP), anti-ADORA2B (1:1000, Proteintech, Wuhan, China, Cat#21071-1-AP), and anti-PLP1 (1:1000, Immunoway, California, USA, Cat#YN3788). For immunofluorescence staining, primary antibodies included anti-MAP2 (1:200, Proteintech, Wuhan, China, Cat#67015-1-Ig), anti-GFAP (1:1000, Cell Signaling Technology, Massachusetts, USA, Cat#3670), anti-NF200 (1:200, Proteintech, Wuhan, China, Cat#18934-1-AP), anti-MBP (1:250, Solarbio, Beijing, China, Cat#K009767M), and anti-CNTN1 (1:200, Proteintech, Wuhan, China, Cat#13843-1-AP). Corresponding fluorescently labeled secondary antibodies were acquired from Thermo Fisher Scientific (Massachusetts, USA, Cat#A-21428 & Cat#A-11008 & Cat#A-21422 & Cat#A-11001). Data were acquired and analyzed using ImageJ software (NIH, Maryland, USA).

Preparation and injection of adeno-associated virus

The vector rAAV-U6-shRNA1(Cntn1)-hSyn-EGFP-SV40polyA was synthesized by Wuhan BrainVTA. The negative control vector rAAV-U6-shRNA1(scramble)-hSyn-EGFP-SV40polyA was used in parallel. Overexpression constructs, including rAAV-hSyn-Cntn1-3×Flag-WPRE3-SV40polyA and its negative control rAAV-hSyn-Scramble-3×Flag-WPRE3-SV40polyA, were purchased from Jingrui Biotechnology (Guangzhou, China). Mice were anesthetized using 1% isoflurane, and a small craniotomy was performed on the left hemisphere. Viral injections were conducted using stereotactic coordinates: 0 mm posterior to bregma, 0.68 mm lateral, and 2.3 mm ventral from the cranial surface.

Co-culture of neuronal cell line (SH-SY5Y) and oligodendrocyte cell line (MO3.13)

SH-SY5Y and MO3.13 cell lines (third-generation) were obtained from Pricella (China). Cells were seeded into 24-well plates at a density of 10,000 cells per well in DMEM supplemented with 17.5% fetal bovine serum (FBS). For the co-culture experiments, SH-SY5Y and MO3.13 cells were co-cultured for 5 weeks. To assess the establishment of the co-culture system, double immunofluorescence staining was performed using antibodies against neurofilament 200 (NF200) and myelin basic protein (MBP), followed by imaging with a confocal laser scanning microscope.

Oral administration of cladribine and intraperitoneal injection of clemastine

Three weeks after AAV-mediated knockdown of Cntn1, cladribine (10 mg/kg; Merck), a known ADA enzyme inhibitor [36–38], was administered via oral gavage once daily for 7 consecutive days. Control mice received an equivalent volume of 0.5% carboxymethyl cellulose solution by gavage over the same period. In parallel, mice received daily intraperitoneal injections of clemastine (10 mg/kg). An additional control group received intraperitoneal injections of an equal volume of 0.01 mol/L phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) following SAH induction, serving as age-matched controls. Mice were subsequently sacrificed at 3 and 7 days post-treatment for Western blot analysis and immunofluorescence staining of brain tissue sections.

Experimental design

This study comprised both cell-based and animal experiments, as illustrated in Supplementary Figs. S1 and S2.

Morris water maze test

Spatial learning and memory were assessed using the Morris water maze. Experiments were carried out in a circular tank (1.5 m in diameter) placed in a room with prominent distal cues. A round escape platform (14 cm in diameter) was submerged 1.5 cm beneath the water surface and the water temperature was maintained at 23–25 °C. To obscure the platform, the water was made opaque by adding a nontoxic white dye. In the training phase, each animal underwent four trials per day for five consecutive days. The hidden platform remained in the same quadrant for all subjects, whereas the starting positions varied randomly across trials. Each trial lasted a maximum of 60 s. If a mouse did not find the platform within the allotted time, the experimenter gently guided it there by nudging its tail. After reaching the platform, the animal stayed on it for 10 s before being returned to a warmed cage for a 25 s rest interval. Twenty-four hours after the final training session, a probe test was conducted in which the platform was removed and mice were allowed to swim freely for 60 s. All sessions were video-recorded, and behavioral performance was analyzed using EthoVision XT software (Noldus Information Technology).

Statistical analysis

Sample sizes are reported in the figure legends and were consistent with those used in previous studies. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 9, with investigators blinded to subgroup allocation. Data are presented as mean ± SD. Pearson correlation analysis was applied to assess associations between variables. Differences between two groups were evaluated using the Student’s t-test. For multiple groups, one-way or two-way ANOVA was used, followed by Tukey’s post-hoc test. Exact P values are provided in the figures or figure legends, and P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Reduced CSF CNTN1 is associated with patients’ cognitive decline after SAH, and Single-Cell RNA Sequencing of murine brain confirms neuron-specific reduction

The baseline clinical characteristics of the enrolled patients are summarized in Supplementary Table S1. To identify independent predictors of cognitive impairment after SAH, we performed a binary logistic regression analysis (Supplementary Table S2). CNTN1 expression was significantly associated with post-SAH cognitive impairment (P < 0.001), whereas most other clinical variables—including age, sex, WFNS grade, aneurysm characteristics, and treatment modalities—were not significant. Delayed cerebral ischemia (DCI) showed a borderline association (P = 0.096). The model demonstrated an overall predictive accuracy of 65.4% and an acceptable goodness-of-fit (Hosmer–Lemeshow test, P = 0.155).

To elucidate the molecular mediators of white matter injury and demyelination following SAH, we conducted proteomic profiling of cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) samples from three groups: healthy controls, early-stage SAH patients (days 1–3) (Fig. 1a and Supplementary Fig. S3a), and late-stage SAH patients (days 4–7) (Supplementary Fig. S3b). In the early-stage SAH group, 108 proteins were significantly downregulated and 46 were upregulated relative to controls. Notably, these 108 downregulated proteins persisted in the late-stage SAH group, alongside an additional 26 proteins that were significantly reduced. To further prioritize functionally relevant candidates, we applied the Dijkstra algorithm (see Supplementary Methods), which identified NEO1, CNTN1, CLSTN1, DCC, and NEGR1 as central nodes within the protein–protein interaction network. Among these, CNTN1 is a well-characterized mediator of axo-glial interaction and a critical regulator of myelin stability. To assess its clinical relevance, we quantified CNTN1 levels in CSF samples from 14 healthy controls and 185 SAH patients using enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA). Three-month cognitive impairment was evaluated using the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA). Patients with poor outcomes (MoCA < 22, n = 87) exhibited significantly lower CSF CNTN1 concentrations compared with those with favorable outcomes (22 ≤ MoCA scores < 26, n = 98), and a strong positive correlation was observed between CNTN1 levels and MoCA scores (R = 0.9073, P < 0.0001) (Fig. 1c, d).

However, the alteration of CNTN1 in the brain parenchyma following subarachnoid hemorrhage remains unknown. We performed single-cell RNA sequencing to obtain a transcriptome-wide view of cell type-specific responses. Unsupervised clustering and Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) visualization resolved all major neural cell types, establishing a comprehensive reference map of the post-SAH cellular landscape (Fig. 1e). Differential expression analysis across these annotated populations revealed widespread transcriptomic reprogramming in diverse cell types, highlighting candidate genes whose expression changes may underlie subsequent protein abundance alterations (Fig. 1f). Among these, the expression pattern of Cntn1 was of particular interest. Analysis at single-cell resolution showed that Cntn1 was predominantly expressed in neuronal clusters and exhibited a significant, specific downregulation after SAH. A direct comparison of neuronal transcriptomes confirmed a significant decrease in Cntn1 expression in SAH compared to sham controls, pinpointing neurons as the key site of its reduction (Fig. 1g, h and Supplementary Fig. S3c). In contrast, parallel analysis of oligodendrocyte populations revealed no significant difference in Cntn1 expression between SAH and sham groups, as visualized by both heatmap and violin plots (Supplementary Fig. S3d, e), underscoring a neuron-specific regulatory mechanism. Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis of these neuronal DEGs revealed significant alterations in biological processes fundamental to neuronal protein homeostasis and function, including axon ensheathment (Fig. 1i). Collectively, this scRNA-seq analysis delineates the cellular architecture of the post-SAH brain and pinpoints neuron-specific transcriptional changes—exemplified by CNTN1 downregulation—that are strongly predictive of consequential protein-level perturbations and functional pathway disruptions.

Consistent with the clinical findings, expression of the CNTN1-associated axo-glial complex—including CNTN1, Caspr, and NF155—was markedly reduced in the brains of SAH model mice at 24, 48, and 72 h post-injury. Myelin basic protein (MBP) levels were similarly decreased during this time course (Fig. 1j, l and Supplementary Fig. S3f–i). ELISA quantification of CSF CNTN1 (normalized to total protein) from SAH patients at 24 h, 48 h and 72 h post-SAH onset showed no significant intergroup difference (all P > 0.05, Supplementary Fig. S3j), with no time-dependent dynamic alteration observed within this time window. Immunofluorescence staining further validated the downregulation of CNTN1 expression in white matter regions following SAH. As shown in representative images (Fig. 1k, m), CNTN1 signals were markedly diminished in the white matter of SAH model mice compared to sham-operated controls. Quantitative analysis confirmed a significant reduction in CNTN1 fluorescence intensity in the SAH group, reinforcing the observation that SAH induces a robust decline in CNTN1 expression within the white matter.

Knockdown of neuronal CNTN1 exacerbated behavioral changes following SAH

In this study, double-label immunofluorescence was performed to examine the cellular localization of CNTN1 in mouse brain tissue, revealing its prominent expression in neurons (Fig. 2a). Consistently, immunofluorescence analysis of primary cultured neurons confirmed high levels of CNTN1 expression in neuronal cells (Fig. 2b). Following a 3-day intervention with Con-KD and Cntn1-AAV-KD viral vectors in primary neurons, immunofluorescence staining demonstrated a marked reduction in CNTN1 expression (Fig. 2c–e). Western blot analysis was subsequently performed to quantify CNTN1 protein levels in Control, Con-KD, and Cntn1-AAV-KD groups at multiplicities of infection (MOI) of 1000 and 5000. A significant decrease in CNTN1 expression was observed at an MOI of 1000 (Fig. 2f, g), which was therefore selected for all subsequent cell viral interventions. After 4 weeks of stereotactic injection of Con-KD and Cntn1-AAV-KD into the lateral ventricles of mouse brains, Western blot analysis confirmed a significant reduction in CNTN1 protein levels (Fig. 2h, i). KEGG pathway enrichment analysis of the differentially expressed proteins highlighted a significant disruption in pathways crucial for myelin sheath and axon development (Fig. 3b). Guided by the downregulation of Ada (adenosine deaminase), a key enzyme in adenosine metabolism, we analyzed its potential receptor. Single-cell RNA sequencing revealed that the adenosine receptor Adora2b was broadly expressed across multiple neural cell types (Fig. 3c). To verify the regulatory effect of CNTN1 on ADA expression in neurons, we performed AAV-mediated CNTN1 knockdown in primary neurons, and detected the protein levels of CNTN1 and ADA via Western blot. The results showed that CNTN1 knockdown significantly downregulated ADA protein expression in neurons, providing direct cellular-level evidence that CNTN1 positively modulates ADA protein levels in neuronal cells (Supplementary Fig. S4b–d).

To dissect the cell-interaction mechanism, we established a neuron-oligodendrocyte (SH-SY5Y/MO3.13) co-culture system (Fig. 3f). In this model, Cntn1 knockdown in neuronal cells followed by hemoglobin exposure (to mimic SAH) led to a significant decrease in ADA and MBP protein levels, coupled with a concurrent upregulation of ADORA2B in the co-culture lysates (Fig. 3g–k). This in vitro finding was robustly recapitulated in vivo. Western blot analysis of brain tissue from Cntn1-AAV-KD + SAH mice confirmed a significant reduction in CNTN1, ADA, PLP1, and MBP protein levels, alongside a marked increase in ADORA2B, compared to Con-KD + SAH controls (Fig. 3l–q). Collectively, these data demonstrate that CNTN1 deficiency disrupts the expression of myelin-related proteins both in vitro and in vivo, and implicates a dysregulated adenosine signaling axis—characterized by decline of CNTN1, downregulated ADA and increased ADORA2B.

We next explored the direct effect of CNTN1 deficiency on adenosine homeostasis. ELISA assays showed that Cntn1 knockdown in primary neurons resulted in a significant accumulation of adenosine, with markedly elevated intracellular and extracellular adenosine levels compared with control neurons, indicating that CNTN1 downregulation disrupts adenosine metabolism and promotes both intracellular retention and extracellular release of adenosine (Supplementary Fig. S4e, f). Consistently, in Cntn1-knockdown SAH mice, administration of NBMPR (a selective adenosine transporter inhibitor) attenuated the downstream molecular alterations induced by Cntn1 deficiency, as evidenced by the partial restoration of ADA, ADORA2B, PLP1 and MBP expression in the white matter (Supplementary Fig. S5a–e). Collectively, these data demonstrate that neuronal CNTN1 deficiency disrupts the expression of myelin-related proteins both in vitro and in vivo via a dysregulated adenosine signaling axis (characterized by CNTN1 reduction-mediated ADA downregulation, adenosine accumulation and ADORA2B upregulation), and inhibition of adenosine transport can alleviate the myelin impairments triggered by CNTN1 deficiency in SAH.

This structural improvement was associated with substantial recovery of cognitive function. In the Morris water maze test, Cntn1-overexpressing SAH mice displayed significantly better spatial learning and memory than controls. To investigate the molecular mechanism, we performed Western blot analysis of key proteins. Elevated adenosine then activates ADORA2B signaling in oligodendrocytes, which suppresses the expression of myelin-related proteins PLP1 and MBP. Consequently, these events trigger progressive demyelination and disrupt axonal integrity.

Consistent with these molecular findings, immunoblotting of white matter showed that CNTN1 expression remained unchanged across SAH, SAH + Clemastine, and SAH + Clemastine + TAK-071 groups, whereas PLP1 and MBP were significantly reduced after SAH and restored by clemastine; this effect was partially reversed by the M1R agonist TAK-071 (Supplementary Fig. S10a–d), indicating that M1R signaling modulates myelin-associated proteins but not CNTN1 following SAH.

Discussion

White matter demyelination is a key contributor to cognitive dysfunction following subarachnoid hemorrhage. Oligodendrocytes, the chief glial cells responsible for myelination in the central nervous system, are highly susceptible to injury from hemorrhagic stress, oxidative damage, and neuroinflammation [39, 40]. These cells are essential for producing and maintaining the myelin sheath that ensures rapid and efficient axonal conduction. Although increasing attention has been paid to white matter injury after SAH, the precise molecular mechanisms underlying oligodendrocyte dysfunction and demyelination remain incompletely defined.

Previous studies have demonstrated that neuronal–glial interactions are essential for maintaining myelin homeostasis, yet the specific neuronal signals that regulate this process in the context of SAH are unclear. Evidence suggests that CNTN1, a neuronal membrane-anchored cell adhesion molecule, plays a key role in axon-glial interactions and myelin organization by binding to Notch1 and Caspr to form paranodal junctions [1, 18]. In our study, clinical proteomic analysis of cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) from SAH patients revealed a marked reduction in CNTN1 levels compared to healthy controls. Furthermore, correlation analysis demonstrated that lower CSF CNTN1 concentrations were significantly associated with more severe cognitive impairment, as assessed by MoCA scores at follow-up. Single-cell sequencing reveals that Cntn1 only significantly declined in neuron, which was further verified by other experiments. To our knowledge, this is the first report identifying neuronal CNTN1 not only as a mechanistic mediator of white matter demyelination but also as a potential prognostic biomarker of white matter injury after SAH.

Our findings also broaden the understanding of SAH-induced white matter injury beyond the traditionally studied temporal cortex. Although hemorrhages are often localized in this region [16], patients commonly exhibit widespread neurological deficits. We observed significant early demyelination not only in the prefrontal cortex but also in the midbrain striatum and cerebellar white matter, which may account for the diverse cognitive and motor symptoms observed in SAH survivors. These findings are consistent with neuroimaging studies reporting diffuse white matter abnormalities in SAH patient population.

CNTN1 has not previously been directly linked to ADA; however, our data revealed that CNTN1 downregulation significantly suppressed neuronal expression of ADA, an enzyme critical for adenosine clearance [41]. Interestingly, a previous study reported that elevated CNTN1 expression in the hippocampus was associated with inflammatory responses, suggesting that CNTN1 may exert context-dependent and complex functions in the central nervous system [42]. To explore the downstream molecular mechanisms, we focused on the link between CNTN1 and adenosine metabolism in neurons. It is reported that ADA deficiency leads to the accumulation of adenosine, which preferentially activates the low-affinity adenosine receptor ADORA2B under pathological conditions such as hypoxia, inflammation, and ischemia [19]. Our results demonstrate that decreased neuronal CNTN1 reduces ADA levels, thereby enhancing ADORA2B signaling in oligodendrocytes. This cascade was associated with suppressed expression of key myelin proteins, including PLP1 and MBP. These findings are supported by earlier work showing that ADA-deficient mice display increased ADORA2B activation and central demyelination, including in the auditory system, which leads to hearing loss [20]. Moreover, ADORA2B signaling has been reported to impair oligodendrocyte precursor cell (OPC) maturation and reduce potassium channel function, ultimately inhibiting myelin gene expression [21, 22]. Our results are consistent with these observations and further expand upon them by identifying neuronal CNTN1 as an upstream regulator of ADA and, consequently, ADORA2B-mediated oligodendrocyte dysfunction. Together, these findings define a previously unrecognized CNTN1-ADA-ADORA2B axis between neurons and oligodendrocytes that governs myelin homeostasis after SAH-induced white matter injury.

Clemastine, an FDA-approved first-generation H1-antihistamine, has shown promising remyelinating effects in various CNS disease models by enhancing OPC differentiation. Recent studies have also suggested that clemastine could upregulate CNTN1 expression [23]. To assess therapeutic potential, we investigated whether pharmacological intervention could modulate this pathway. In our SAH model, daily clemastine administration for seven days significantly preserved white matter integrity, as evidenced by elevated MBP and PLP1 expression, improved myelin morphology under electron microscopy, and increased myelin coverage in immunofluorescence analyses. Behavioral tests revealed improved cognitive and motor outcomes following clemastine treatment, further underscoring its functional benefits. Notably, shorter clemastine treatment durations (three days) failed to produce significant molecular or histological improvements, highlighting the importance of sustained therapeutic exposure. At the molecular level, clemastine restored neuronal CNTN1 and ADA expression while suppressing ADORA2B expression in oligodendrocytes, effectively rebalancing the neuronal CNTN1/ADA–oligodendrocytic ADORA2B axis.

Interestingly, although clemastine is classically recognized as an antagonist of the M1 muscarinic acetylcholine receptor (M1R). M1R has been confirmed to be a key negative regulator of oligodendrocyte precursor cell differentiation and myelination. To investigate whether clemastine exerts its therapeutic effects in mice with SAH via an M1R-independent pathway, we performed Western blot analysis with the following experimental groups: SAH group; SAH + clemastine group; SAH + clemastine + TAK-071 group (TAK-071 is a highly selective M1R agonist used to counteract the inhibitory effect of clemastine on M1R). Our data demonstrate that co-administration of TAK-071 did not completely abrogate the pro-myelinating effect of clemastine, indicating that clemastine promotes myelination through additional pathways beyond M1R. Combined with our aforementioned data, this pro-myelinating effect is mediated by the CNTN1-ADA-ADORA2B signaling axis.

Beyond the direct regulatory effects on neurons observed in our study, we discuss the potential implications of the CNTN1-ADA-ADORA2B regulatory axis on glial function in the central nervous system following SAH, based on our core findings and previously published evidence. Our study confirmed that neuron-derived CNTN1 directly modulates ADA expression, thereby shaping the local adenosine concentration in the lesion microenvironment after SAH. As the core effector cells driving neuroinflammation in the post-SAH CNS, microglia are well-established key cellular targets of adenosine signaling. Adenosine is a well-recognized master regulator of microglial activation and polarization: prior work has demonstrated that adenosine drives the phenotypic switch of microglia from the pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype to the anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype via ADORA2B [43], a receptor highly enriched on microglia. This adenosine-mediated microglial phenotypic modulation may further suppress the release of key pro-inflammatory cytokines, including tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α) and interleukin-1β (IL-1β), and attenuate the neuroinflammatory cascade, representing a potential indirect mechanism through which the CNTN1-ADA-ADORA2B axis may exert neuroprotective effects after SAH. In parallel, astrocytes are the master regulators of CNS adenosine homeostasis, and thus may act as important modulators of this regulatory network. Previous studies have confirmed that astrocytes abundantly express adenosine metabolic enzymes and nucleoside transporters [44, 45], suggesting that the CNTN1-ADA-mediated adenosine metabolism identified in our study may synergize with the intrinsic adenosine regulatory system of astrocytes to fine-tune the spatiotemporal activation of downstream ADORA2B signaling in the lesion microenvironment. Furthermore, reactive astrocytes have been reported to regulate neuronal CNTN1 expression via the secretion of inflammatory mediators [46], which may in turn participate in the upstream and downstream modulation of the CNTN1-ADA-ADORA2B axis, potentially forming a closed-loop neuro-glial regulatory network in the pathological microenvironment after SAH.

Despite these compelling findings, several limitations must be acknowledged. First, although we focused primarily on neuron–oligodendrocyte interactions, other glial cells such as astrocytes and microglia likely modulate adenosine metabolism and inflammatory signaling, warranting further investigation. Second, our findings suggest that alterations in CNTN1 could influence ADA expression, potentially mediated by FAK/Src–MAPK/ERK [47–49] or PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling [50, 51], or indirectly through immune microenvironmental changes. Further experiments are required to determine the specific mechanism by which CNTN1 influences ADA expression. All in all, this study identifies a novel molecular cascade in which neuronal CNTN1 preserves oligodendrocyte myelin by suppressing oligodendrocytic ADORA2B signaling in an ADA-dependent manner. Our data highlight CNTN1 as both a mechanistic driver and a potential biomarker of white matter damage after SAH. Furthermore, the ability of clemastine to restore this pathway and improve neurological outcomes underscores its promise as a therapeutic strategy for promoting remyelination in the post-hemorrhagic stroke. These findings advance our understanding of neuron–glia communication and open new avenues for targeted intervention in SAH-related cognitive dysfunction.

Conclusion

This study demonstrated that CNTN1 expression is significantly downregulated following subarachnoid hemorrhage. The reduction of neuronal CNTN1 expression leads to a concomitant decrease in adenosine deaminase levels, which in turn results in elevated ADORA2B expression in oligodendrocytes. This aberrant signaling cascade ultimately suppresses the expression of key myelin-associated proteins, PLP1 and MBP, thereby contributing to white matter demyelination. Importantly, treatment with clemastine was found to attenuate demyelination by upregulating the neuronal CNTN1/ADA, downregulating ADORA2B expression in oligodendrocytes, and restoring PLP1 and MBP levels. These molecular changes were accompanied by improvements in both cognitive and motor function in SAH models, highlighting clemastine as a promising therapeutic candidate for mitigating white matter injury after SAH.
