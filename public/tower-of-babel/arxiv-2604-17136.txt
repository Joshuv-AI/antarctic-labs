Abstract
. We study the concatenated Fibonacci constant ℱ : = 0 . F 1 F 2 F 3 ⋯ = 0.11235813 ⋯ \mathcal{F}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=0.F_{1}F_{2}F_{3}\cdots=0.11235813\cdots , obtained by concatenating the Fibonacci numbers in the fractional part, and ask whether it is normal. We show that several classical sufficient conditions for normality by concatenation do not apply to the Fibonacci sequence because of its exponential growth, while a criterion of Pollack and Vandehey implies that the normality of ℱ \mathcal{F} in base 10 10 would follow if almost all Fibonacci numbers were ( ε , k ) (\varepsilon,k) -normal in base 10 10 . The Benford bias of leading digits and the Pisano periodicity of trailing digits are shown to contribute asymptotically negligible fractions of the total digits, isolating the distribution of the deep digits of large Fibonacci numbers as the remaining obstruction. Large-scale numerical experiments on the first 500,000 500{,}000 Fibonacci numbers in bases 10 10 and 2 2 indicate that global single-digit counts and k k -block statistics for k = 2 , 3 , 4 k=2,3,4 are compatible with iid-like fluctuations at the scales tested, and that a positional decomposition concentrates the visible structured deviation at the boundaries between consecutive Fibonacci numbers, while pooled interior blocks remain close to uniform. Our computations suggest that any obstruction to normality lies in the asymptotic behavior of the deep digits of F n F_{n} .

1.
Introduction

An irrational number
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\mathbb{Q}
is normal in the integer base
b\geq 2
if every finite string
s\in\{0,1,\dots,b-1\}^{k}
occurs in the base-
b
expansion of
\alpha
with asymptotic frequency
b^{-k}
. If this condition holds for
k=1
, the number is said to be simply normal in base
b
; a number that is normal in every base is called absolutely normal. Borel proved in 1909 that almost every real number is absolutely normal
[
4
]
, yet proving that a given number is normal remains notoriously difficult. Well-known constructive examples of decimal normal numbers are Champernowne’s constant
0.123456\cdots

[
7
]
, the Besicovitch number
0.149162536\cdots

[
3
]
, and the Copeland–Erdős constant
0.23571113\cdots

[
9
]
, all obtained by concatenating sequences of integers whose counting functions grow sufficiently fast. While similar artificial numbers can be constructed in other bases
[
26
,
17
,
1
]
, numbers like
\pi
and Apéry’s constant
\zeta(3)
have not yet been proved normal in any base.

In this paper we study the concatenated Fibonacci constant, henceforth referred to simply as the Fibonacci constant,

\mathcal{F}=0.F_{1}F_{2}F_{3}\cdots=0.11235813213455\cdots,

where
F_{1}=1
,
F_{2}=1
, and
F_{n}=F_{n-1}+F_{n-2}
for
n\geq 3
, and ask whether
\mathcal{F}
is normal in base
10
. The Fibonacci sequence is the simplest exponentially-growing sequence one can concatenate: it is a linear recurrence with the smallest nontrivial growth rate
\phi=\frac{1}{2}(1+\sqrt{5})
, its arithmetic is unusually rich (through Pisano periods, divisibility properties, and Carmichael’s theorem on primitive prime divisors), and it is familiar enough that results about it can be stated and calibrated without specialised preliminaries. At the same time, its exponential growth places
\mathcal{F}
just beyond the reach of the classical Davenport–Erdős machinery, which requires the
n
-th term of the sequence to have at most
O(n^{1-\delta})
digits for some
\delta>0
. This makes the Fibonacci constant a natural test case beyond the polynomial-growth regime covered by the classical theory.

This study has two complementary goals. On the theoretical side, we identify a precise sufficient route to normality and show that the most visible sources of digit bias, the Benford distribution of leading digits and the Pisano periodicity of trailing digits, are asymptotically negligible. This isolates the deep digits of large Fibonacci numbers as the remaining obstruction. On the computational side, we test this structural picture by means of relatively large-scale numerical experiments in bases
10
and
2
. The resulting data show that the observable deviations are concentrated at the boundaries between consecutive Fibonacci numbers and provide no evidence of a persistent bias in the pooled interior digits.

This paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
, we review the classical sufficient conditions for normality by concatenation and show how each fails for the Fibonacci constant. In Section
3
, we apply a criterion of Pollack and Vandehey to identify the
(\varepsilon,k)
-normality of almost all Fibonacci numbers as a sufficient condition for the normality of
\mathcal{F}
, and explain that this condition is not an equivalence. Section
4
analyzes the structural sources of digit bias in
\mathcal{F}
and shows that both the Benford distribution of leading digits and the Pisano periodicity of trailing digits contribute an asymptotically negligible fraction of the total digits, while the distribution of the deep digits of large Fibonacci numbers (the obstacle to applying the aforementioned sufficient route) is argued to lie beyond current equidistribution techniques. We also discuss in this section the complementary row-versus-column perspective arising from recent work of Benfield and Manes and explain why the argument does not settle the concatenation question. Sections
5
and
6
report numerical experiments in bases
10
and
2
, respectively, on the first
500{,}000
Fibonacci numbers, together with a positional decomposition separating interior from boundary blocks. The statistical tests presented are diagnostic rather than formal; see Remark
7
. Finally, Section
7
contains concluding remarks and directions for further work.

2.
Classical sufficient conditions for normality by concatenation

The following proposition establishes the counting function for the Fibonacci sequence, a simple result that is otherwise difficult to find in the standard literature.

Proposition 1
.

The number of Fibonacci numbers up to
N\in\mathbb{N}
is given by

\Phi_{F}(N)=\bigl\lfloor\log[\sqrt{5}(N+\tfrac{1}{2})]/\log{\phi}\bigr\rfloor,

(1)

where
\phi=\frac{1}{2}(1+\sqrt{5})
is the golden ratio.

Proof.

The Binet formula gives
F_{n}=\lfloor{\phi^{n}/\sqrt{5}+1/2}\rfloor
, so the condition
F_{n}\leq N
is equivalent to
\phi^{n}/\sqrt{5}<N+1/2
, i. e.,
n\leq\lfloor{\log[\sqrt{5}(N+1/2)]/\log\phi}\rfloor
, and we get

\Phi_{F}(N)=\sum_{F_{n}\,\leq\,N}1=\sum_{n=1}^{\lfloor\log[\sqrt{5}(N+\frac{1}{2})]/\log{\phi}\rfloor}1=\bigl\lfloor\log[\sqrt{5}(N+\tfrac{1}{2})]/\log{\phi}\bigr\rfloor.

∎

This simple result shows that the Fibonacci sequence is exponentially sparse (
\Phi_{F}(N)\sim\log N
) among the positive integers.

2.1.
The Copeland–Erdős barrier

The classical theorem of Copeland and Erdős
[
9
]
asserts that the concatenation of an increasing sequence
a_{1}<a_{2}<\cdots
is normal in base
b
provided the counting function
\#\{a_{i}\leq N\}
exceeds
N^{\theta}
for every
\theta<1
and all sufficiently large
N
. Since
\log{N}/N^{\theta}\to 0
for any
\theta>0
, Proposition
1
shows that the Fibonacci sequence fails this condition by a large margin.

The converse of the Copeland–Erdős theorem is clearly false: one can regroup the digits of the Copeland–Erdős constant
0.23571113\cdots
into blocks
a_{1}=2
,
a_{2}=35
,
a_{3}=711
, …with
\left|{a_{n}}\right|=n
digits each. The resulting sequence has counting function
\Phi(N)\sim\log_{10}{N}
, yet its concatenation produces a normal number. The Copeland–Erdős theorem can therefore neither prove nor disprove the normality of
\mathcal{F}
.

2.2.
The Davenport–Erdős approach and extensions

A natural attempt to circumvent the sparsity barrier is to appeal to the stronger theorem of Davenport and Erdős
[
10
]
, which shows that
0.p(1)p(2)p(3)\cdots
is normal in base
10
for any polynomial
p
taking positive integer values at positive integer arguments. This was subsequently generalized by Nakai and Shiokawa
[
21
,
20
]
to functions of the form
f(x)=\alpha_{n}x^{\beta_{n}}+\cdots+\alpha_{1}x^{\beta_{1}}
with
\beta_{n}>\cdots>\beta_{1}\geq 0
and
f(x)>0
for
x>0
.

These results are proved using Weyl’s estimates for exponential sums involving polynomials or generalized polynomials. One might hope to bring the Fibonacci sequence into this framework by finding a polynomial
p
with
p(k)=F_{k}
for all
k\geq 1
. However, no such polynomial exists: any polynomial of degree
d
satisfies
p(k)=O(k^{d})
, while
F_{k}=\Theta(\phi^{k})
grows exponentially. Lagrange interpolation produces a polynomial of degree
n-1
through the points
(1,F_{1}),\ldots,(n,F_{n})
, but the Davenport–Erdős theorem requires a fixed polynomial, and its proof mechanism breaks down completely when the degree grows with the number of terms.

The Nakai–Shiokawa generalization extends the class of admissible functions considerably, but all functions in their framework have at most generalized-polynomial growth. Exponential functions like
F_{n}\sim\phi^{n}/\sqrt{5}
remain out of reach.

More recent work by Clanin and Rayman
[
8
]
studies the Davenport–Erdős and Nakai–Shiokawa theorems through the lens of finite-state dimension, according to which a sequence is normal if and only if its finite-state dimension equals
1
. Their results show that rational linear polynomials preserve finite-state dimension of Copeland–Erdős sequences, while polynomials of degree
\geq 2
can change it. The results are elegant, but cannot resolve the normality of
\mathcal{F}
, since the Fibonacci sequence is not obtained by applying any polynomial to a set of integers.

2.3.
The
\varphi
–
\sigma
–
\lambda
approach

Pollack and Vandehey showed that if
f
is any function formed by composing Euler’s totient function
\varphi
, the sum-of-divisors function
\sigma
, or Carmichael’s lambda-function
\lambda
, then the number
0.f(1)f(2)f(3)\dots
obtained by concatenating the base
b
digits of successive
f
-values is
b
-normal
[
24
]
.

That the approach cannot help can be seen from a simple growth argument. Since
\varphi(n)\leq n
,
\lambda(n)\leq n
, and
\sigma(n)=O(n\log\log{n})
, any finite composition
f=f_{1}\circ f_{2}\circ\cdots\circ f_{k}
of them grows at most like
n
times a polylogarithmic factor, so that
\log f(n)=O(\log n)
, which is precisely the weakly-polynomial-growth hypothesis that underlies the Pollack–Vandehey approach
[
24
, condition (1.3) and Theorem 1.1]
. Since
\log F_{n}\sim n\log{\phi}
, the Fibonacci numbers violate this bound by an exponential margin. Hence
n\mapsto F_{n}
lies entirely outside the class of functions to which
[
24
]
applies, and no composition of
\varphi
,
\sigma
,
\lambda
can agree with it even eventually.

A more direct way to see that the Pollack–Vandehey
\varphi
–
\sigma
–
\lambda
approach cannot apply is the following. For any composition
f_{1}\circ f_{2}\circ\cdots\circ f_{k}
, with each
f_{i}
one of
\varphi
,
\sigma
or
\lambda
, to reproduce the Fibonacci sequence, every Fibonacci number must lie in the range of the outermost
f_{1}
. However, already
F_{5}=5
fails this test for all three choices, because (i)
\varphi(m)
and
\lambda(m)
equal
1
for
m=1,2
and are even for
m\geq 3
, and (ii) since
\sigma(1)=1
and
\sigma(m)\geq m+1
for
m\geq 2
, any preimage of
F_{5}=5
under
\sigma
would satisfy
m\leq 4
, but
\sigma(1)=1
,
\sigma(2)=3
,
\sigma(3)=4
, and
\sigma(4)=7
, so
F_{5}
is not in the range of
\sigma
either. Hence no composition of
\varphi
,
\sigma
, and
\lambda
can output
F_{5}
(nor
F_{11}=89
,
F_{13}=233
,
F_{25}=75025
, and many other small odd Fibonacci values that we checked), let alone the whole Fibonacci sequence.

3.
A sufficient condition via digit normality of Fibonacci numbers

In this section we state a sufficient condition for the normality of
\mathcal{F}
in terms of the digit statistics of the Fibonacci numbers.

Definition 2
.

A natural number
n
is
(\varepsilon,k)
-normal in base
b
if

\left|{\frac{N(w,\sigma_{b}(n))}{\left|{\sigma_{b}(n)}\right|}-b^{-k}}\right|\leq\varepsilon

(2)

for every string
w\in\{0,1,\dots,b-1\}^{k}
, where
\sigma_{b}(n)
denotes the base-
b
representation of
n
and
N(w,s)
counts the number of sliding-block occurrences of
w
in the string
s
.

The notion of
(\varepsilon,k)
-normality goes back to Besicovitch
[
3
]
, and the density-bound that underlies the criterion is due to Copeland and Erdős
[
9
]
. The following reformulation as a three-part sufficient condition for concatenations of arithmetic-function values is due to Pollack and Vandehey
[
23
,
24
]
, with closely related variants appearing in
[
18
,
8
]
.

Theorem 3

(Normality criterion for concatenations)
.

Let
a_{1},a_{2},a_{3},\ldots
be a sequence of positive integers. Suppose that as
m\to\infty
the following conditions hold:

(
i
)

The digit lengths grow on average:
m=o\Bigl(\sum\limits_{n=1}^{m}\left|{\sigma_{b}(a_{n})}\right|\Bigr)
.

(
ii
)

No single length dominates:
m\cdot\!\max\limits_{1\leq n\leq m}\left|{\sigma_{b}(a_{n})}\right|=O\Bigl(\sum\limits_{n=1}^{m}\left|{\sigma_{b}(a_{n})}\right|\Bigr)
.

(
iii
)

For every
\varepsilon>0
and
k\in\mathbb{N}
, the number of
n\leq m
for which
a_{n}
is not
(\varepsilon,k)
-normal is
o(m)
.

Then
0.a_{1}a_{2}a_{3}\cdots
is normal in base
b
.

We prove that the Fibonacci sequence verifies conditions (i) and (ii) above.

Proposition 4
.

The Fibonacci sequence
F_{1},F_{2},F_{3},\ldots
satisfies conditions (i) and (ii) of Theorem
3
in base
b=10
.

Proof.

Since
F_{n}\sim\phi^{n}/\sqrt{5}
, the number of base-
10
digits of
F_{n}
is

\left|{\sigma_{10}(F_{n})}\right|=\lfloor{n\log_{10}{\phi}-\log_{10}{\sqrt{5}}}\rfloor+1\sim n\log_{10}{\phi}

(3)

for large
n
, where
\log_{10}{\phi}\approx 0.20898
.

Condition (i):
\sum\limits_{n=1}^{m}\left|{\sigma_{10}(F_{n})}\right|\sim\frac{1}{2}m^{2}\log_{10}{\phi}
, so
m=o(\frac{1}{2}m^{2}\log_{10}{\phi})
.

Condition (ii):
m\cdot\!\max\limits_{1\leq n\leq m}\left|{\sigma_{10}(F_{n})}\right|=m\left|{\sigma_{10}(F_{m})}\right|\sim m^{2}\log_{10}{\phi}=O(\frac{1}{2}m^{2}\log_{10}{\phi})
.
∎

By Theorem
3
, the normality of
\mathcal{F}
is therefore implied by condition (iii), i. e., that almost all Fibonacci numbers are
(\varepsilon,k)
-normal. We state this explicitly.

Corollary 5
.

The Fibonacci constant
\mathcal{F}
is normal in base
10
if, for every
\varepsilon>0
and
k\in\mathbb{N}
,

\#\{n\leq m\colon F_{n}\text{ is not }(\varepsilon,k)\text{-normal in base }10\}=o(m)\text{ as }m\to\infty.

(4)

Condition (
4
) is a statement about the digit distribution within individual Fibonacci numbers. Since
F_{n}\sim\phi^{n}/\sqrt{5}
, it is essentially equivalent to asking whether the base-
10
digits of
\phi^{n}
are equidistributed for most
n
, a question in the same family as the open problem of whether the base-
b
digits of
2^{n}
, or more generally
\alpha^{n}
for fixed algebraic
\alpha>1
, are asymptotically equidistributed for most
n

[
5
,
15
]
.

4.
Structural analysis of the digit distribution

We show that two possible sources of digit bias in
\mathcal{F}
, the Benford distribution of leading digits and the Pisano periodicity of trailing digits, are both negligible.

Before turning to the specific bases used in our numerical experiments, however, it is worth noting that the two structural mechanisms studied in this section have a general origin valid in every integer base
b\geq 2
. On the one hand, the leading digits of
F_{n}
are governed by the fractional parts of
n\log_{b}{\phi}-\log_{b}{\sqrt{5}}
and hence, by Weyl’s equidistribution theorem, exhibit Benford-type behavior. On the other hand, the trailing
k
digits are determined by
F_{n}\bmod b^{k}
and are therefore controlled by the Pisano periodicity modulo powers of the base. Thus the boundaries between consecutive Fibonacci numbers carry deterministic structure in every base, although the precise form and strength of the resulting bias depend on the base and on the digit position.

4.1.
The leading digits

Since
\log_{10}{\phi}
is irrational, Weyl’s equidistribution theorem guarantees that the fractional parts
\{n\log_{10}{\phi}+c\}
(where
c=-\frac{1}{2}\log_{10}5
) are equidistributed modulo
1
. This implies that the leading digit of
F_{n}
follows Benford’s law, according to which digit
d
appears as the first digit with frequency
\log_{10}(1+1/d)
; digit
1
, for instance, should appear
\sim 30\%
of the time.

However, each Fibonacci number contributes only one leading digit out of
\left|{\sigma_{10}(F_{n})}\right|\sim n\log_{10}{\phi}
total digits. Up to
F_{m}
, the leading digits contribute
m
digits out of a total of
D(m)\sim\frac{1}{2}m^{2}\log_{10}{\phi}
digits. The Benford bias therefore affects a fraction
O(1/m)
of all digits and is asymptotically negligible.

More generally, for any fixed position
j
from the left, the
j
-th digit of
F_{n}
(when
F_{n}
has at least
j
digits) is a function of
\{n\log_{10}{\phi}+c\}
alone, namely
\lfloor{10^{j-1+f}}\rfloor\bmod 10
where
f=\{n\log_{10}{\phi}+c\}
. The bias of this function away from
1/10
for each digit value is
O(10^{-j})
, decaying exponentially with the position
[
11
]
. Summing over all fixed positions, the total structural bias in the digit frequency of
\mathcal{F}
is

\left|{\frac{C_{d}(m)}{D(m)}-\frac{1}{10}}\right|_{\text{structural}}\leq\frac{\sum\limits_{j=1}^{\infty}O(10^{-j})\cdot m}{\frac{1}{2}m^{2}\log_{10}{\phi}}=O(1/m).

(5)

4.2.
The trailing digits

The Fibonacci sequence modulo
10^{k}
is periodic with Pisano period
\pi(10^{k})
. For
k=1
, the period is
60
, and the distribution of last digits over one period is non-uniform: the Fibonacci sequence is uniformly distributed modulo
m
if and only if
m
is a power of
5

[
16
,
6
]
. In particular, the distribution modulo
10
is not uniform.

However, the same counting argument employed in the case of the leading digits applies. The trailing
k
digits of each
F_{n}
contribute
k
digits per Fibonacci number, for a total of
km
out of
D(m)\sim\frac{1}{2}m^{2}\log_{10}{\phi}
. For any fixed
k
, this fraction is
O(k/m)\to 0
.

4.3.
The deep digits

The leading and trailing digits each contribute a vanishing fraction of
\mathcal{F}
. The bulk of the digits come from “deep” positions
j
from the left with
j
proportional to
\left|{\sigma_{10}(F_{n})}\right|
. For these positions, the digit is determined by the value of
\lfloor{10^{j-1+f}}\rfloor\bmod 10
for large
j
, which oscillates with period
\sim 10^{-j}
in the parameter
f=\{n\log_{10}{\phi}+c\}
.

A natural approach to establishing uniform distribution of these deep digits, or, equivalently, to bounding the deviation of their empirical frequencies from the uniform
1/10
, is to apply the Koksma–Hlawka inequality to the equidistributed sequence
\{n\log_{10}{\phi}+c\}
with the digit-extraction indicator as integrand. For a function
g
with bounded variation
V(g)
, the discrepancy of the equidistributed sequence
\{n\alpha\}
yields

\left|{\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}g(\{n\alpha+c\})-\int_{0}^{1}g(f)\,d\mkern-1.0muf}\right|\leq V(g)\cdot D_{N},

(6)

where
D_{N}
is the discrepancy of
\{n\alpha\}
. For a generic irrational
\alpha
one has only
D_{N}=o(1)
from Weyl’s theorem, while sharp polynomial rates of the form
D_{N}=O((\log N)^{2}/N)
hold under Diophantine assumptions on
\alpha
(e.g. for badly approximable or finite-type
\alpha
) and are not known unconditionally for
\alpha=\log_{10}{\phi}
. The point is that the argument fails even under such a favourable assumption: the indicator
\text{1}[\lfloor{10^{j-1+f}}\rfloor\equiv d\!\pmod{10}]
has variation
V(g)=O(10^{j})
, and when
j
is proportional to
n
(deep digits) this variation is exponential in
n
, so the right-hand side of (
6
) grows like
O(\phi^{n}(\log{m})^{2}/m)
and diverges even if the optimistic discrepancy bound is granted. The equidistribution machinery therefore cannot control the deep digits, regardless of the Diophantine status of
\log_{10}{\phi}
.

We emphasise that this is a failure of the Koksma–Hlawka discrepancy route, not an impossibility result. The argument above shows only that bounded-variation bounds combined with equidistribution of
\{n\log_{10}{\phi}\}
cannot establish condition (
4
), and leaves open the possibility that some altogether different technique could control the deep digits. Within the class of bounded-variation discrepancy arguments based on equidistribution, however, the fundamental obstacle is clear. The digit at a deep position within
F_{n}
is a highly oscillatory function of the equidistributed parameter
\{n\log_{10}{\phi}\}
, and existing discrepancy bounds are far too weak to control this oscillation. The problem is closely related to the open question of whether the digits of
\alpha^{n}
for algebraic
\alpha>1
are normally distributed in base
10
, a question that remains unresolved even for
\alpha=2
.

4.4.
The Benfield–Manes place-value approach

A different angle on the same problem was taken by Benfield and Manes
[
2
]
, who proposed to attack the normality of
\mathcal{F}
via the periodicity of the Fibonacci sequence modulo powers of the base. For an integer base
\beta\geq 2
and
k\geq 0
, define

\Phi_{\beta^{k}}(n)\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\lfloor{F_{n}/\beta^{k}}\rfloor\bmod\beta,

(7)

the digit at the
\beta^{k}
-place of
F_{n}
in base
\beta
, with the convention
\Phi_{\beta^{k}}(n)=0
when
F_{n}<\beta^{k}
. By Wall’s theorem
[
27
]
, the Fibonacci sequence is purely periodic modulo every integer
m
, with Pisano period
\pi(m)
. Since the
\beta^{k}
-place digit depends only on
F_{n}\bmod\beta^{k+1}
, the sequence
(\Phi_{\beta^{k}}(n))_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is itself periodic, with period dividing
\pi(\beta^{k+1})
. Combining this with classical residue-distribution results of Niederreiter
[
22
]
for moduli
5^{k}
and Jacobson
[
14
]
for moduli
2^{k}
with
k\geq 5
(and more generally for
5^{x}2^{y}
), Benfield and Manes show that for every base of the form
\beta=5^{x}2^{y}
each digit
d\in\{0,1,\ldots,\beta-1\}
appears in
(\Phi_{\beta^{k}}(n))_{n=1}^{\pi(\beta^{k+1})}
exactly
\pi(\beta^{k+1})/\beta
times. In other words, the place-value sequence at every fixed depth is uniformly distributed over one Pisano period. From this they conclude that
\mathcal{F}
is normal in every base of the form
5^{x}2^{y}
.

We accept the Niederreiter–Jacobson–Wall machinery exactly as stated: for every base
\beta=5^{x}2^{y}
and every depth
k\geq 0
, the place-value sequence
(\Phi_{\beta^{k}}(n))_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is periodic with period dividing
\pi(\beta^{k+1})
and each digit
d\in\{0,1,\ldots,\beta-1\}
appears exactly
\pi(\beta^{k+1})/\beta
times per period. What does not follow, in our view, is the inference from this per-period uniformity of each fixed place-value sequence to normality of the concatenated constant
\mathcal{F}
. The obstruction is best appreciated by viewing the digits of
F_{1},F_{2},F_{3},\ldots
as the entries of an infinite ragged array whose
n
-th column holds the base-
\beta
digits of
F_{n}
. The concatenation
\mathcal{F}
reads this array column by column. There are then two distinct natural senses in which one might ask for “uniform digit distribution”:

–

Row uniformity:
fix a row
k
and let
n
vary. This is what the Niederreiter–Jacobson results, fed through Wall periodicity, deliver in the bases
5^{x}2^{y}
.

–

Column uniformity:
fix a column
n
(large) and let the row index vary within that column. This is the requirement that the digit string of
F_{n}
be
(\varepsilon,k)
-normal, that is, condition (
iii
) of Theorem
3
.

The two senses are dual but logically independent. An infinite digit array can have all rows uniform without any column being uniform, and vice versa. The reading order of
\mathcal{F}
is the column order; consequently, the relevant uniformity statement is column-wise, not row-wise.

To illustrate the gap between the two senses, the following example exhibits a ragged digit array with the strongest possible form of row uniformity, to wit, exact periodic balance of the kind delivered by the Niederreiter–Jacobson–Wall machinery, whose column-wise concatenation is simply normal but fails already at block length
2
.

Example 6

(Row uniformity does not imply column-wise normality)
.

Fix base
\beta=10
. Define a ragged digit array by specifying, for each
n\geq 1
, a column length
\ell_{n}=n
and a type
t_{n}\in\{0,1,\dots,9\}
with
t_{n}\equiv n\!\pmod{10}
, and declare the
n
-th column to be the digit string
t_{n}\,t_{n}\,\cdots\,t_{n}
containing
\ell_{n}
copies of
t_{n}
. In the row/column convention of this subsection, row
k
then consists of the
k
-th place-value digit of each column long enough to have one, namely

\bigl(\Phi_{k}(n)\bigr)_{n:\,\ell_{n}>k}\,=\,(t_{n})_{n>k}\,=\,t_{k+1},t_{k+2},t_{k+3},\dots,

which is a shift of the period-
10
sequence
0,1,2,\dots,9
. In particular, every row is purely periodic, with period
10
, and each digit

d\in\{0,1,\dots,9\}
appears exactly once per period at every depth
k
. This is precisely the kind of exact per-period balance furnished by the Niederreiter–Jacobson–Wall machinery for the Fibonacci digit array in bases

5^{x}2^{y}
.

The concatenation read column by column is

C\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=t_{1}(t_{2}\,t_{2})(t_{3}\,t_{3}\,t_{3})(t_{4}\,t_{4}\,t_{4}\,t_{4})\cdots,

so digit
d
appears in maximal runs of length
\ell_{n}
whenever
t_{n}=d
. Single-digit frequencies converge to
1/10
. If

L_{N}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\sum_{n\leq N}\ell_{n}=\sum_{n\leq N}n,

then for each
d\in\{0,\dots,9\}

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\leq N\\
t_{n}=d\end{subarray}}\ell_{n}=\frac{1}{10}L_{N}+O(N),

because the indices in each residue class modulo
10
contribute one tenth of the sum
\sum\limits_{n\leq N}n
up to an
O(N)
error. Since
L_{N}\sim N^{2}/2
, it follows that

\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\leq N\\
t_{n}=d\end{subarray}}\ell_{n}}{L_{N}}=\frac{1}{10}\quad\text{for every }d\in\{0,\dots,9\}.

Thus the column-wise concatenation is simply normal.

At depth
2
, however, the situation is starkly non-uniform. The
2
-block
dd
occurs
\ell_{n}-1
times inside every column of type
t_{n}=d
, so

\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{\#\{(d,d)\text{-blocks in the first }L_{N}\text{ digits of }C\}}{L_{N}-1}=\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\leq N\\
t_{n}=d\end{subarray}}(\ell_{n}-1)}{L_{N}-1}=\frac{1}{10}.

By contrast, if
d\neq e
, then the block
de
can arise only at a boundary between two consecutive columns, and hence contributes at most one occurrence per boundary. Therefore

\frac{\#\{(d,e)\text{-blocks in the first }L_{N}\text{ digits of }C\}}{L_{N}-1}\leq\frac{N-1}{L_{N}-1}\longrightarrow 0.

The
2
-block distribution of
C
is therefore concentrated on the diagonal
\{(d,d)\colon d\in\{0,\dots,9\}\}
, with mass
1/10
on each diagonal block and mass
0
on every off-diagonal block, instead of the uniform value
1/100
required for normality at block length
2
.

In summary, in the above construction every row of the array is perfectly balanced, with exact periodic uniformity at every depth, yet the column-wise concatenation is simply normal and still fails already at block length
2
. Row uniformity and column-wise normality are logically independent properties of a ragged digit array, and no purely row-based argument can suffice to establish the latter.

Example
6
should be understood only as a counterexample to the inference from fixed-depth row uniformity to column-wise normality in a given base. By itself, it does not address the stronger (Pillai-based) route based on simple normality in all powers of the base.

To see why row uniformity does not imply normality of
\mathcal{F}
, fix a base
\beta=5^{x}2^{y}
and consider the partial concatenation of
F_{1},\ldots,F_{m}
. The contribution of row
k
to this partial concatenation consists of those
n\in\{1,\ldots,m\}
with
F_{n}\geq\beta^{k}
, namely
n_{k}\leq n\leq m
with
n_{k}\sim k\log\beta/\log\phi
. The fact that
(\Phi_{\beta^{k}}(n))
contains each digit equally often over one full Pisano period of length
\pi(\beta^{k+1})\asymp\beta^{k+1}
is informative for the partial sum only when
m-n_{k}\gg\pi(\beta^{k+1})
, i. e., when
k\ll\log_{\beta}m
. The rows where the per-period averaging has effectively occurred therefore contribute only
O(m\log m)
digits to the partial concatenation, out of a total of
D(m)\sim\frac{1}{2}m^{2}\log\phi/\log\beta
. The “averaged” fraction of the digits of
\mathcal{F}
at stage
m
is thus
O((\log m)/m)\to 0
, while the bulk of the digits live in rows
k\in[\log_{\beta}m,\,m\log\phi/\log\beta]
, where the period-averaging argument is silent. These “deep but not deep enough” rows are precisely the digits at large place values within large Fibonacci numbers, which is exactly the obstacle isolated in Section
4.3
above.

The two perspectives can therefore be summarised as follows. The Benfield–Manes approach establishes row uniformity of the digit array of
F_{n}
in the bases
5^{x}2^{y}
; the Pollack–Vandehey criterion of Theorem
3
demands column uniformity in the form of
(\varepsilon,k)
-normality of almost all Fibonacci numbers; and neither implies the other. In particular, the bases
5^{x}2^{y}
that are most favourable for the row-side analysis enjoy no special status from the column side: although
(\varepsilon,k)
-normality is itself a base-
b
notion and condition (
4
) is stated here in base
10
, the corresponding column-side obstruction—namely, asking whether almost all
F_{n}
have
(\varepsilon,k)
-uniform digit strings in the chosen base—arises in every base, and the special bases
5^{x}2^{y}
do not appear to simplify it. Condition (
4
) and its analogues in other bases remain, in our view, the natural route to the normality of
\mathcal{F}
via the sufficient criterion of Theorem
3
, and the principal obstacle to carrying that route through.

Remark 7

(Interpretation of the statistical tests)
.

The chi-squared statistics,
p
-values, and
z
-scores reported below are computed under the null hypothesis that the observed digits are independent draws from the uniform distribution on
\{0,\ldots,b-1\}
. For a deterministic concatenation with overlapping blocks, this i. i. d. model is not formally justified, and the reported
p
-values should be read as heuristic diagnostics rather than as tests of a well-specified null. Likewise, the
D^{-1/2}
benchmark we compare against below is the fluctuation scale of an i. i. d. uniform sequence, not a consequence of normality per se: a normal number need not exhibit discrepancy decaying at that rate. The observed digit statistics are, however, compatible with i. i. d.-like fluctuations at the tested scales. We view this as heuristic evidence, not a proof of normality.

5.6.
Summary of base-
10

The numerical evidence across all four tests—single-digit frequencies, block frequencies, individual
(\varepsilon,1)
-normality, and positional decomposition—is consistent with the conjecture that
\mathcal{F}
is simply normal in base
10
, and is consistent with normality at block lengths

, and

. The observed convergence rate is, within the precision afforded by the data, indistinguishable from the

fluctuation scale of an i. i. d. uniform sequence; see, however, Remark
7
. The positional decomposition shows that the pooled interior blocks, aggregated across all Fibonacci numbers, are statistically consistent with uniformity, and the visible structured deviation is concentrated at the boundary between consecutive Fibonacci numbers, an artifact that becomes negligible as
N\to\infty
. This is an aggregate statement and does not imply that each individual
F_{n}
has uniform interior digits.

6.4.
Summary

The base-
2
and base-
10
experiments are mutually consistent and point to the same conclusion: the concatenated Fibonacci constant
\mathcal{F}
is numerically consistent with the behavior expected of a normal number at the scales tested. The positional decomposition in both bases shows that the Benford-biased leading digits and the Pisano-periodic trailing digits of each
F_{n}
create a deterministic boundary pattern that is massively non-uniform in isolation, but this boundary layer comprises less than
0.006\%
of all blocks and has no discernible effect on the overall digit distribution.

In base
10
, the pooled middle blocks are fully consistent with uniformity at every block length tested. In base
2
, Good’s serial statistic on the pooled middle blocks is formally significant only at
k=2
(and not at
k=3,4
), with absolute deviations comparable to those in base
10
and no larger than
2\times 10^{-6}
, so this residual signal should be interpreted cautiously. Taken together, the pooled interior blocks are very close to uniform and the boundary effects account for essentially all of the visible structured deviation. We emphasise that this is an aggregate statement across all Fibonacci numbers; the experiments do not establish that each individual
F_{n}
has uniform interior digits, which is the content of condition (iii) and remains open. Numerical evidence cannot settle the question, which depends on the asymptotic behavior of
F_{n}
for
n
arbitrarily large. The data, however, provide no hint of the kind of persistent bias that would rule out normality.

7.
Concluding remarks

We have identified the
(\varepsilon,k)
-normality of almost all Fibonacci numbers as a sufficient condition for the normality of the Fibonacci constant
\mathcal{F}
, and shown that this condition lies beyond the reach of current equidistribution techniques due to the exponential oscillation of the digit function at deep positions within
F_{n}
. The problem belongs to a broader class of open questions about the digit distribution of exponential sequences. Even the simplest instance of this problem, namely, whether the digits of
2^{n}
in base
10
are equidistributed for most
n
, remains unresolved, despite considerable attention
[
5
]
. A proof of condition (
4
), even for
k=1
, which would yield simple normality of
\mathcal{F}
, would represent significant progress in the area.

Our numerical experiments, carried out on the first
500{,}000
Fibonacci numbers in both base
10
and base
2
, yielding
2.6\times 10^{10}
decimal digits and
8.7\times 10^{10}
bits, respectively, showed the maximum single-digit deviation decaying at a rate indistinguishable from the
D^{-1/2}
i. i. d. benchmark, and the block-frequency statistics for
k=2,3,4
revealing no significant departure from uniformity in either base, both compatible with i. i. d.-like fluctuations at the tested scales. A positional decomposition of the
k
-block counts further shows that the visible structured deviation is almost entirely concentrated at the junctions between consecutive
F_{n}
, a deterministic artifact driven by Benford-biased leading digits meeting Pisano-periodic trailing digits, while the pooled interior blocks are close to uniform (with deviations at or below the
10^{-6}
level). In line with Remark
7
, we stress that these observations are heuristic diagnostics rather than formal evidence: the statistics are suggestive but not decisive, providing evidence compatible with normality but no proof of it, and, crucially, the aggregate uniformity of pooled interior blocks does not imply the per-
F_{n}
uniformity required by condition (
4
). The experiments therefore reveal no obstruction to normality but do not, and cannot, resolve it.

Two possible directions for future work are the following. First, one could exploit the fact that multi-digit addition with carry propagation is known to have a “mixing” effect on digit distributions when the summands are random
[
12
]
. A possible approach is to investigate whether iterating the Fibonacci recurrence produces a similar effect for the deterministic Fibonacci sequence, gradually driving the digits of
F_{n}
, and thence of
\mathcal{F}
, toward equidistribution, though making this intuition rigorous (likely by devising a higher-order Markov chain of some sort) remains an open challenge. Second, one could seek weaker structural results that fall short of full normality but still provide nontrivial information, for instance bounds on the finite-state dimension of
\mathcal{F}
or proofs that the digit frequencies lie in a prescribed interval. We are particularly motivated by the first possibility, which, even if it does not lead to a solution, may still yield partial or obstruction results.

A third direction, arising naturally from the analysis of §
2.3
but somewhat unrelated to the normality of
\mathcal{F}
, takes the failure of the
\varphi
–
\sigma
–
\lambda
approach as the starting point of a question of independent arithmetic interest: which Fibonacci numbers lie in the range of
\sigma
? A numerical census over the first few hundred indices reveals that beyond a small sporadic initial segment, every hit is a multiple of
6
, with only sparse exceptions of the form
6p
for
p
prime. This admits a simple structural explanation via the identity
F_{6}=8=\sigma(7)
together with the divisibility property
F_{d}\,|\,F_{kd}
. The remaining mystery, namely, which primes
p
have
6p
outside
\sigma(\mathbb{N})
, appears genuinely delicate, with no evident modular obstruction separating the two classes. The Fibonacci numbers in the range of
\sigma
, and the arithmetic of their complement, form a modest but interesting corner of the interplay between the Fibonacci sequence and the multiplicative functions of classical number theory, and we are currently pursuing this direction further
[
19
]
.
