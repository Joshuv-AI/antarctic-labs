Predicting nicotine emissions and plasma nicotine boost in E-cigarette users using machine learning
Authors: Simanta Roy, Sreshtha Chowdhury, Tarana Ferdous, Alan Shihadeh, Rola Salman, Rachel El Hage, Sally Salam, Zoran Bursac, Wasim Maziak
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
As e-cigarette use continues to rise globally, the need for reliable and scalable methods to quantify nicotine exposure in e-cigarette users has become more crucial. This study aims to develop supervised machine learning (ML) models that predict nicotine emission and plasma nicotine boost based on puff topography, personal and device characteristics, and e-liquid consumption. This study analyzed 259 measurements from e-cigarette users aged 21–35 years by combining human puff topography data with laboratory toxicant emission tests generated through validated playback procedures. Puffing behavior was recorded during ad libitum vaping sessions and replicated with a puffing robot (playback) to measure nicotine emissions. Plasma nicotine levels were determined via liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry. Six supervised regression models, including ordinary least squares, lasso, random forest, XGBoost, support vector regression, and neural networks, were trained to predict nicotine emissions and plasma nicotine boost from puffing parameters. The models were evaluated using an 80/20 train, test split with bootstrap resampling over 200 iterations, with performance metrics assessed by R², RMSE, and MAE. Among the ML methods evaluated, XGBoost consistently demonstrated the best performance (highest R², minimal RMSE, and MAE) for predicting both nicotine emissions and plasma nicotine boost. For nicotine emission prediction, XGBoost achieved the highest accuracy using either puff number (R² = 0.778 ± 0.153) or liquid consumption (R² = 0.747 ± 0.149) in combination with average puff duration and device characteristics (nicotine concentration and device). Using these emission estimates together with individual characteristics (age, sex, height, and weight), XGBoost-based plasma nicotine boost models showed the best explanatory performances for both puff-based (R² = 0.613 ± 0.174) and liquid-consumption-based (R² = 0.699 ± 0.168) predictors. This study shows that ML methods can effectively estimate nicotine emissions and plasma nicotine boost exposure directly from puffing behavior and device features. Introduction

Electronic cigarette (e-cigarette) use has grown significantly worldwide, altering nicotine consumption patterns and raising concerns about exposure, dependence, and regulation1. Fourth-generation nicotine-salt pod systems, which are now widely available in the market, efficiently deliver high nicotine doses comparable to or exceeding those produced by combustible cigarettes2,3. As nicotine is the primary psychoactive compound responsible for reinforcement, continued use, and abuse liability, accurately quantifying nicotine exposure is essential for assessing product risk, understanding dependence, and guiding regulatory efforts4. Particularly, e-cigarettes are becoming increasingly common among young and nicotine naïve users, and many of them are unaware of the extent of their exposure to this addictive substance through e-cigarettes5. Therefore, the need for reliable and scalable approaches to quantify nicotine exposure among e-cigarette users has become increasingly important6.

Nicotine exposure from e-cigarettes is traditionally quantified using laboratory-based methods. For example, nicotine emission is measured by reproducing a user’s puffing behavior in a smoking robot and sampling the aerosol for nicotine7. In contrast, plasma nicotine, obtained via blood sampling, provides a direct measure of systemic uptake. Although these approaches offer high precision, they require specialized equipment, trained personnel, and controlled laboratory conditions, making them impractical for large-scale or routine assessments. To overcome these limitations, recent studies have explored regression and machine-learning (ML) based methods for quantifying nicotine exposure8. Using ML approaches, Prasad et al. (2024) used wearable-derived physiological signals (e.g., heart rate, heart rate variability) to estimate nicotine exposure in e-cigarette users9. However, these physiological markers are non-specific and can be confounded by many external influences, such as physical activity or stress, and do not capture user and product-related determinants that drive nicotine delivery. For example, prior research shows that puffing behavior, nicotine levels, and devices can strongly influence nicotine exposure10. However, no existing prediction models integrate all these key determinants to provide valid and scalable predictions of nicotine exposure in e-cigarette users. Such a comprehensive model not only reflects actual exposure but also integrates the effects of behavioral and device factors, which are essential for regulatory assessments and population surveillance.

In this study, we address these gaps by developing supervised ML models that integrate detailed data on puff-topography, device characteristics, e-liquid consumption, personal characteristics and clinical-laboratory measurements of plasma nicotine and nicotine emissions. This framework allows us to model the full nicotine-delivery pathway, from user behavior to nicotine intake (mouth-level exposure) to systemic uptake (blood-level exposure), and to predict exposure without requiring blood draws.

Methods

Lab procedures

A total of 259 measurements were collected from individuals aged 21 to 35 who were current e-cigarette users within the past 30 days. The sample was drawn from two ongoing randomized controlled trials investigating the effects of nicotine and flavor content on e-cigarette user responses11,12. All methods were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations, including the Declaration of Helsinki. The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of Florida International University, and all participants provided written informed consent.

In each study, participants who had abstained from nicotine for 12 h (confirmed by plasma nicotine levels ≤ 5.0 ng/mL) visited the laboratory and used an e-cigarette freely for up to 60 min. Three fourth-generation e-cigarettes brands and two flavors, menthol and tobacco, were employed: the Standardized Research E-Cigarette (SREC) (5% nicotine, 58 mg/mL), NJOY (2.4% nicotine, 24 mg/mL; 5% nicotine, 58 mg/mL), and JUUL (3% nicotine, 35 mg/mL; 5% nicotine, 58 mg/mL), following the original study protocols. The JUUL e-cigarette has a PG: VG (Propylene glycol: Vegetable Glycerine) ratio of ~ 30:70, with a power output of ~ 4.3 watts13,14. In contrast, NJOY and SREC have a PG: VG ratio of ~ 50:50, with a power output of ~ 6 watts14,15.

During each vaping session, a mouthpiece-connected topography-recording device (eTop) was used to monitor participants’ puffing behavior throughout the session16. The software recorded detailed topography metrics, including total session time (minutes), puffing time (minutes), average puff duration (seconds), average flow rate (mL/s), average inter-puff interval (seconds), number of puffs, total inhaled volume (mL; total aerosol volume inhaled across the session), average puff volume (mL; mean volume per puff), and maximum puff volume (mL; largest single-puff volume recorded). This puff topography data, along with a matched e-liquid pod, was provided to the Aerosol Research Laboratory (ARL) in Lebanon. Blood samples (5 ml) were collected before and after each ad libitum session (within 10 min) to assess plasma nicotine boost. After centrifugation, plasma was stored at -80 °C for analysis via liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (see Supplementary file). The nicotine boost for each session was determined by subtracting the pre-session plasma nicotine value from the post-session value.

Playback procedures

A validated vaping machine programmed to playback participants’ puffing behavior recorded during their lab sessions was used to generate and sample the e-cigarette aerosol for analysis17. Using this deidentified puff topography data, the Aerosol Lab Vaping Instrument (ALVIN) was programmed to replicate the puff topography patterns of each individual puffing session. Each sample was analyzed for nicotine, and the results were expressed in milligrams (mg).

Predictors and outcomes

Predictors included puffing topography measures, e-liquid consumption, device characteristics (e-cigarette nicotine concentration, device), and personal characteristics (age, height, body weight, sex). Outcomes included nicotine emission and plasma nicotine boost.

Modeling approaches

We applied six supervised ML regression models to estimate nicotine emissions and plasma nicotine boost based on puffing parameters, and to compare their performances. These models included linear (Ordinary Least Squares, OLS) and penalized regression (Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator, LASSO), ensemble methods (Random Forest - RF, Extreme Gradient Boosting - XGBoost), support vector regression (SVR), and artificial neural networks (ANN). The selection of these six models was motivated by their representation of a broad spectrum of regression techniques, from traditional parametric models (e.g., OLS and LASSO) to flexible nonlinear algorithms (e.g., RF, XGBoost, SVR, and ANN). Similar comparative frameworks have been applied in prior research evaluating the performance of learning methods for predicting continuous physiological or clinical variables, such as systolic blood pressure18.

To assess robustness and practical applicability of predictor selection for nicotine emission, three modeling strategies were evaluated: (1) a statistically selected model (see supplementary file for details) incorporating best puffing parameters (e.g., total inhaled volume, average puff duration) and device variables identified based on predictive relevance; (2) a theory-driven model including readily obtainable predictors selected a priori based on biological plausibility and relevance to nicotine emission and exposure; and (3) a proxy-based model using simplified predictors that approximate statistically selected variables (e.g., puff number or liquid consumption in place of total inhaled volume). Each predictor set was applied consistently across all ML algorithms to ensure equitable comparison.

For nicotine emission prediction, a statistically driven approach was used to identify the most predictive puffing topography variables (e.g., total inhaled volume) from all available puffing parameters, using a multi-method feature selection framework (described in the Supplementary file). Then, easily obtainable proxy measures (e.g., e-liquid consumption instead of total inhaled volume) were evaluated to approximate the statistically selected predictor, prioritizing variables that capture similar exposure information without requiring specialized puffing instrumentation. Additionally, a theory-driven approach incorporated easily obtainable predictors (e.g., puff number) informed by prior evidence of their relevance to aerosol generation and nicotine delivery. Average puff duration was also included as another top-ranked predictor. Together, these predictors could capture complementary aspects of vaping behavior: quantity-based measures, such as total inhaled volume, puff number, or e-liquid consumption, reflect the amount of aerosol or liquid used, whereas pattern-based measures, such as average puff duration, reflect how aerosol was consumed during the session. Key device characteristics, including nicotine concentrations and device type, were included in all models. To ensure equitable comparison, each predictor set was applied consistently across all ML algorithms. For plasma nicotine boost prediction, predicted nicotine emission estimates from all ML models were used as model inputs, along with individual characteristics (age, sex, height, and weight) known to influence nicotine pharmacokinetics.

Model training, validation, and resampling

For each outcome, the dataset was randomly split into a training set (80%) and an independent test set (20%). Models were developed using the training data and assessed on the test data. To evaluate model stability and uncertainty, we performed bootstrap resampling with 200 iterations, retraining and testing on resampled datasets. Results from bootstrap and non-bootstrap methods were comparable, confirming the robustness of the model estimates. The models were trained on the full training dataset with selected predictors and evaluated on the test set for reporting.

Performance metrics and visualization

Predictive performance was evaluated using the coefficient of determination (R²), root mean square error (RMSE), and mean absolute error (MAE). For all models, bootstrap-based mean values split were reported. Results were visualized using scatter plots of observed versus predicted values annotated with performance metrics. This process was applied consistently for both outcomes.

Model implementation

All analyses were conducted using R statistical software (version 4.3.2; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). The dataset was partitioned using an 80/20 train-test split, with model performance evaluated through 200 bootstrap iterations to ensure robust estimates. Data preprocessing, transformation, and partitioning were performed using the tidyverse and caret packages. Continuous variables were centered and scaled using the preProcess() function prior to model training. OLS models were implemented using the base lm() function, while LASSO regression was performed using the cv.glmnet() function from the glmnet package (version 4.1-8), with the optimal penalty parameter (λ) selected via fivefold cross-validation. RF models were developed using the randomForest package (version 4.7–1.1) with 1,000 trees and variable importance calculation enabled. XGBoost models were trained using the xgboost package (version 1.7.6) with 200 rounds, “reg: squarederror” objective function, and RMSE as the evaluation metric. SVR models were implemented using the e1071 package (version 1.7–14) with radial basis function kernel, and ANN were trained using the nnet package (version 7.3–19) with one hidden layer containing five nodes, linear output activation function (linout = TRUE), weight decay of 0.001, and a maximum of 500 iterations. Model performance metrics, including R², RMSE, and MAE, were calculated for each bootstrap iteration, with the final performance reported as the mean ± standard deviation (SD) across all iterations. Comprehensive visualizations, including model comparison plots, bootstrap distribution plots, residual analyses, and variable importance charts, were generated using ggplot2 (version 3.4.4), patchwork, and gridExtra packages.

The trained XGBoost models (using puff number) were deployed as an interactive web application (https://huggingface.co/spaces/sroy023/Nicotine_prediction_from_puff) using the Gradio framework on Google Colaboratory. The application loads pre-trained model objects (the main prediction model and 200 bootstrap models for each outcome) into memory at initialization, enabling real-time predictions without retraining. For each prediction request, the application constructs feature matrices specific to each outcome, generates predictions from the main model, and computes bootstrap-derived standard deviations by aggregating predictions across all 200 bootstrap models.

Results

Nicotine emissions

Across all predictor sets, XGBoost consistently demonstrated the strongest predictive performance for nicotine emission, producing the highest R² and the lowest error (MAE and RMSE) over 200 bootstrap iterations (see Supplementary Table 1 for details).

In Figure B1, the model included e-liquid consumption, average puff duration, device, and nicotine concentration. XGBoost achieved an R² of 0.778 ± 0.153, indicating that the model explains nearly 78% of the total variability in nicotine emission with minimal error (MAE: 0.527 ± 0.182; RMSE: 1.296 ± 0.526) (Fig. 1). For Figure A1, the model used puff number instead of e-liquid consumption, XGBoost still outperformed all other algorithms (R² = 0.747 ± 0.149), and ANN showed moderate accuracy (R² = 0.730 ± 0.167 in Model B1; 0.609 ± 0.173 in Model A1). Meanwhile, SVR, LASSO, and OLS regression produced substantially lower predictive power with relatively higher errors. RF performed reasonably well but was consistently inferior to XGBoost (see Supplementary Table 1 for details).

Plasma nicotine boost

For plasma nicotine boost, XGBoost again achieved the highest predictive accuracy across 200 bootstrap iterations, outperforming all other ML models in both predictor sets (see Supplementary Table 2 for details).

In Model A2, which incorporated predicted nicotine emission (from e-liquid consumption, average puff duration, device, and nicotine concentration) and personal characteristics (age, sex, height, weight), XGBoost achieved an R² of 0.699 ± 168, MAE of 2.251 ± 0.755, and RMSE of 5.087 ± 1.626. An R² of 0.699 indicates that the model explains approximately 70% of the variance in plasma nicotine boost. In Model B2, predicted nicotine emission (from puff number, average puff duration, device, and nicotine concentration), and the same personal characteristics were retained. Using this, the model performed slightly lower, explaining 61% of the variability in plasma nicotine boost with minimal error (RMSE = 5.977 ± 1.674 and MAE = 2.717 ± 0.844). RF provided moderate accuracy (Model A2: R² = 0.563 ± 0.142; Model B2: R² = 0.513 ± 0.142), whereas ANN, SVR, OLS, and LASSO regression consistently performed the poorly, producing low R² values (≤ 0.40 in Model A2 and ≤ 0.30 in Model B2) and higher error across all metrics (see Supplementary Table 2 for details).

Discussion

Using e-cigarette use data (e.g., puff number, e-liquid consumption), device (e.g., e-cigarette power and PG: VG ratio), and personal characteristics (e.g., age, sex), we demonstrate that ML models can accurately estimate both nicotine emissions and users’ exposure to nicotine (plasma nicotine boost). Among the evaluated approaches, XGBoost achieved the highest and most consistent accuracy, explaining approximately 78% of the variability in nicotine emissions and 70% of the variability in plasma nicotine boost, with markedly lower error compared to other models. These results suggest that leveraging ML approaches, easily obtainable puffing parameters that do not require specialized topography equipment, such as puff number and e-liquid consumption, can be used to predict nicotine emission and plasma nicotine boost, thereby informing product regulation, addiction risk assessment, and population-level surveillance.

According to this study, puff number, average puff duration, and e-liquid consumption can accurately predict both nicotine emissions and plasma nicotine boost. These parameters, along with device characteristics and nicotine concentration, demonstrated the highest accuracy in predicting nicotine emission. This accuracy can be attributed to the direct impact of these parameters on both aerosol generation and nicotine delivery. For example, device power and PG: VG ratio define its nicotine aerosolization potential19, whereas puffing parameters reflect the user’s contribution to nicotine generation and exposure20. Additionally, prior studies have established a strong relationship between device characteristics (e.g., power, PG: VG ratio) and puffing behavior (e.g., average puff duration), which can significantly influence nicotine delivery21,22. Therefore, modeling these inputs together, along with personal characteristics related to nicotine pharmacokinetics23,24, provides a more precise assessment of nicotine emission and exposure and allows us to evaluate the role of these predictors as potential regulatory targets to reduce nicotine dependence.

To evaluate the predictive accuracy of different puffing parameters and device characteristics on nicotine emission and plasma nicotine boost, we examined six commonly used linear and non-linear ML models. Among them, non-linear tree-based models, particularly XGBoost models, demonstrated strong and reproducible performance, explaining approximately 78% (R² = 0.778 ± 0.153) of the variability in nicotine emissions and 70% (R² = 0.699 ± 0.168) in plasma nicotine boost, with consistently lower prediction errors than other models. The superior performance of XGBoost underscores the nonlinear, interactive structure of nicotine delivery from the e-cigarette devices. In comparison, linear models performed poorly in explaining the data variability (low R² values) in predicting nicotine emission and plasma nicotine boost from puffing and device data. This finding further validates the non-linear, complex relationship between e-cigarette puffing, device characteristics, and nicotine emission/plasma nicotine boost.

Applying these models to readily attainable parameters (e.g., puff number, liquid consumption) enables us to accurately predict real-world exposure to nicotine from e-cigarettes. This has important implications for both regulatory science and clinical practice. For example, puffing-based predictions can support the assessment of product standards, facilitate pre-market review by identifying products with high abuse liability, and strengthen post-market surveillance by estimating nicotine exposure without the need for costly emissions or pharmacokinetic studies. Clinically, these models provide a practical way to approximate nicotine dose from a patient’s readily available use parameters, helping clinicians assess dependence, guide harm-reduction counseling, and design nicotine tapering strategies25.

Strengths and limitations

The main strength of this study lies in the integration of data that captures the complete nicotine-delivery pathway, from user behavior to aerosol generation to systemic uptake, using objective, laboratory-validated measurements rather than simulated or indirectly inferred inputs. This study also enabled the assessment of a non-linear relationship, providing a more plausible integration of the data that was previously thought to be linear. Having the e-cigarette sessions after 12 h, plasma nicotine validated abstinence homogenized the baseline assessment and ensured that all subsequent measurements were mainly device and user-determined (e.g., not influenced by pre-assessment use of any tobacco product).

Nonetheless, several limitations warrant consideration. The sample was restricted to fourth-generation closed-system devices, three nicotine concentrations (2.4%, 3%, 5%), and two flavors (menthol, tobacco), which may limit the generalizability of our findings. However, when we evaluated the models across devices with different powers (e.g., JUUL ~ 4.3 watts vs. NJOY/SREC ~ 6 watts) and PG: VG ratios (e.g., JUUL ~ 30:70 vs. NJOY/SREC ~ 50:50), they performed consistently across these configurations, supporting the robustness of these models. Finally, the models only estimate acute nicotine delivery after 12 h of abstinence and, therefore, do not capture longitudinal or chronic exposure. Still, the acute model can be very fitting for the initial assessment of nicotine delivery of new products for regulatory purposes. Additionally, plasma nicotine was assessed as a nicotine boost, reflecting nicotine uptake during the vaping session rather than the complete nicotine concentration-time pharmacokinetic profile. The interval between the final puff and blood collection may have varied across participants; however, all post-session blood samples were collected within 10 min of session completion, likely reducing the influence of this variability. Future research can adopt and test these models across larger, more diverse populations, incorporating a broader range of device and e-liquid formulations.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates the feasibility and value of using ML approaches to estimate nicotine emissions and plasma nicotine boost directly from readily available puffing data and device characteristics. By integrating clinical data on user behavior, aerosol generation, and systemic uptake, this framework offers a potentially scalable, policy-relevant alternative to resource-intensive laboratory playback and pharmacokinetic studies. As such, with further validation, these models may support population-level exposure assessment, product safety evaluation, and regulatory analysis of device and design features that influence nicotine delivery. Collectively, the findings underscore the potential of data-driven approaches to enhance clinical assessment, inform regulatory oversight, and support evidence-based decision-making as the e-cigarette market continues to evolve.
