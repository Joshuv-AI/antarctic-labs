Previously unreported translocation (14;15)(q32; q11.2) observed in B-cell acute lymphoblastic leukemia: a case report
Authors: Maliha Naz, Jawad Hassan, Neelum Mansoor
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstractt(14;15) have rarely been seen in hematologic malignancies. Here we have reported a case of a 4-year-old male who presented with low grade fever and body aches and, was diagnosed on immunophenotyping as B-cell Acute Lymphoblastic Leukemia. Cytogenetic analysis demonstrated a previously unreported translocation t(14;15)(q32;q11.2). Fluorescent in situ hybridization (FISH) analysis on metaphases was also carried out to confirm the chromosomal aberration. To our knowledge, such a translocation has not been reported in hematological malignancy specifically in B-cell ALL.Keywords: B-cell ALL, Cytogenetic analysis, FISH

Introduction

In this case report, we present a case of B-cell Acute Lymphoblastic Leukemia (ALL) demonstrating a rare and potentially first reports of this specific translocation found through the cytogenetic analysis of bone marrow and has not been reported previously in ALL.

Case presentation

A 4-yr-old male child resident of Karachi, came to our hospital emergency department with the complaints of low grade fever, lethargy, decreased intake & body pains for 2–3 months. According to patient’s attendant, he had multiple hospital admissions in past, resulting in temporary improvements only. A Complete blood count (CBC) done at a laboratory showed 43% blast, after which the patient came to our hospital for further workup. Baseline laboratory tests were performed for suspected leukemia. CBC revealed hemoglobin 9.3 g/dl (11.5–15.5), platelets 63 × 10^9/L (200–490), and white blood cells (WBC) 44.6 × 10^9/L (5.0-14.5). WBC differentials comprised of 13% neutrophils (37–60), 20% lymphocytes (28–48), and 63% blast cells. Coagulation tests were in the normal range. On palpation, the abdomen was mildly tender with a mild hepatomegaly. Few submandibular nodes were also found.

A bone marrow specimen was obtained for morphology, immunophenotyping and genetic analysis.

Based on morphology and immunophenotypic analysis, the patient was diagnosed as B-cell Acute Lymphoblastic Leukemia (WHO classification 2022).

Cytogenetic analysis was conducted on bone marrow cells utilizing established methodologies. After a 24-hour cell culture period, the cells were processed for cytogenetic evaluation through the standard Colcemid™, hypotonic treatment, and fixation procedures. Subsequent to G-banding performed via a conventional Trypsin-Giemsa technique, the cytogenetic results were documented in accordance with the International System for Human Cytogenetic Nomenclature (ISCN 2020) guidelines. Discussion

B-cell acute lymphoblastic leukemia (B-ALL) is a type of blood cancer marked by the uncontrolled proliferation of immature B-cell precursors. Chromosomal translocations are commonly found in B-ALL and are significant in the processes of leukemogenesis, prognosis, and treatment strategies. This report introduces the inaugural documented case of B-ALL associated with a t(14;15)(q32;q11.2) translocation. As of now, this particular chromosomal alteration has not been previously reported in B-ALL, and its clinical and biological implications are still unclear.

The translocation t(14;15)(q32;q11.2) involves two chromosomal regions that are rarely associated with each other in hematologic malignancies. Chromosome 14q32 is a well-known locus for the immunoglobulin heavy chain (IGH) region, which plays a pivotal role in B-cell development and is commonly involved in translocations in B-cell lymphomas and leukemias including multiple myeloma, chronic lymphocytic leukaemia, diffuse large B-cell lymphoma, and mantle cell lymphoma. BCL11B (B-cell lymphoma/leukemia 11B), another gene found on chromosome 14q32, is essential for T-cell differentiation and proliferation and is frequently linked to T-lymphoid cancers [1].

IGH translocations typically result in the dysregulation of oncogenes placed under the control of strong IGH enhancers, leading to aberrant gene expression and contributing to malignant transformation [2, 3].

Chromosome 15q11.2, on the other hand, has not been well-characterized in hematologic malignancies. This region contains several genes, including UBE3A, CYFIP1, NIPA1, and NIPA2, which have been implicated in neuro-developmental disorders such as Angelman and Prader-Willi syndromes, but their role in leukemogenesis is not well established [4–6]. The potential role of this translocation in B-ALL remains speculative, as the genes located at 15q11.2 have not been shown to play a direct role in B-cell development or function.

It is possible that this translocation disrupts regulatory elements near the breakpoint, leading to dysregulation of gene expression either at 14q32 (affecting the IGH locus) or at 15q11.2, potentially resulting in abnormal B-cell proliferation or impaired differentiation. Given that IGH translocations are commonly associated with the activation of oncogenes, this chromosomal rearrangement could result in the abnormal activation of a gene located at 15q11.2. Alternatively, the translocation may cause a fusion gene that contributes to the pathogenesis of B-ALL [7].

The involvement of the IGH locus in translocations suggests a mechanism of enhancer-mediated activation of oncogenes, which has been well documented in other B-cell neoplasms such as Burkitt lymphoma (MYC translocation) and follicular lymphoma (BCL2 translocation) [8]. However, in this case, the gene or genes located at 15q11.2 that are potentially dysregulated due to the translocation remain unidentified. The region harbors several genes related to neurodevelopment and protein ubiquitination, but their precise role in leukemogenesis has not been studied in depth. The t(14;15)(q32;q11.2) translocation could disrupt normal pathways of B-cell development, particularly those involved in cell cycle regulation, apoptosis, or chromatin remodeling. Since the IGH locus plays a central role in B-cell receptor signaling, any disruption in this region might lead to aberrant B-cell receptor function, contributing to the uncontrolled proliferation of immature B cells, as seen in B-ALL [1].

Further molecular analysis is needed to determine whether the translocation results in the creation of a fusion protein, a mechanism that has been implicated in many leukemias and lymphomas. Whole-genome sequencing or transcriptomic analysis of the leukemic cells could help identify the exact breakpoints of the translocation and elucidate which genes are activated or inactivated as a result.

Cytogenetic abnormalities in B-ALL are critical for determining prognosis and guiding treatment strategies. Certain well-characterized translocations, such as t(9;22) (Philadelphia chromosome) and t(12;21) (ETV6-RUNX1), have well-established prognostic implications, with the former conferring a poor prognosis and the latter associated with a favorable outcome [9]. However, the clinical significance of novel or rare translocations like t(14;15)(q32;q11.2) is unknown.

In our case the complete remission of primary disease was observed after one month of treatment therapy. In the absence of previous reports of this translocation, it is challenging to predict its impact on prognosis. Based on the involvement of the IGH locus, it is possible that this translocation could lead to aggressive disease behavior, as seen with other IGH translocations. Alternatively, it may represent a secondary chromosomal event, potentially arising later in the evolution of the disease.

Prognosis in such rare cytogenetic abnormalities is influenced by multiple clinical (age, white blood cell count etc.) and molecular factors, and a short follow-up period cannot reliably substantiate prognostic claims. The current post-induction follow-up is insufficient to draw meaningful conclusions about disease course or long-term survival. Extended follow-up with sequential response assessments, ideally supported by additional cases, is recommended to strengthen prognostic interpretation.

The rarity of this translocation suggests that it should be classified as an intermediate-risk cytogenetic abnormality until more data becomes available. Given the uncertainty, close monitoring of the patient’s response to standard B-ALL therapy is essential, along with additional molecular studies to identify co-occurring mutations that may influence prognosis and treatment outcomes.

Further studies involving similar cases and long-term follow-up will be crucial to better understand the clinical relevance of t(14;15)(q32;q11.2) in B-ALL and to determine whether it carries specific prognostic or therapeutic implications.

Conclusion

This case represents the first documented instance of B-ALL with a t(14;15)(q32;q11.2) translocation. The biological consequences of this translocation and its role in the pathogenesis of B-ALL are not yet understood. Molecular studies are needed to clarify the genes affected by the translocation and their role in leukemogenesis. Future reports and research on this novel chromosomal abnormality will be necessary to determine its clinical significance and its potential role in guiding treatment decisions.
