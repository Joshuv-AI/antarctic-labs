How community dialogues influence norm change around female genital mutilation: a mediation analysis from a high-burden Kenyan setting
Authors: Dennis Juma Matanda, Martina Mchenga, Stella Muthuri, Francis Obare, Viola Rutto, Herbert Barasa Wanyonyi, David Kawai, Claire Arnott, Chi-Chi Undie
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionFemale genital mutilation (FGM) remains highly prevalent in parts of Kenya, including Narok County, where marriageability expectations and social sanctions continue to sustain the practice. Although community-dialogue interventions are central to strategies to end FGM, evidence on their effects and mechanisms of influence remains limited.MethodsWe conducted a cross-sectional survey of 2658 respondents (1272 women and 1386 men) aged 15–60 years in Narok County in 2024. The primary exposure was participation in mixed-sex community dialogues with participation in couples-only dialogues as secondary exposure. Outcomes were measured using survey items adapted from established FGM and social norms literature, including a composite index of beliefs favourable to cutting and single-item measures of intention to cut a daughter, perceived marriageability expectations and opposition to FGM. Analyses used generalised estimating equations, predicted probabilities and generalised structural equation models to assess mediation pathways.ResultsParticipation in community dialogues was associated with more progressive norms, including lower odds of endorsing pro-cutting beliefs (adjusted OR (aOR) 0.61, 95% CI 0.48 to 0.76), intending to cut a daughter (aOR 0.49, 95% CI 0.37 to 0.64) and perceiving uncut girls as unmarriageable (aOR 0.53, 95% CI 0.43 to 0.66), and higher odds of opposing FGM (aOR 2.39, 95% CI 1.84 to 3.11). Couples-only dialogues had smaller and less consistent associations. Predicted probabilities indicated substantial positive associations among participants exposed to community dialogues alone or combined with couples’ sessions. Mediation analysis showed that 88% of the effect of community dialogues on opposition to FGM indirectly linked through shifts in pro-cutting beliefs, intentions to cut and marriageability expectations, with intentions representing the strongest linkages.ConclusionCommunity dialogues are strongly associated with shifts in the social expectations and intentions that underpin FGM in a high-burden Kenyan context. These findings provide support for dialogue-centred strategies to eliminate FGM. Beliefs, intentions and marriageability norms are key intermediate indicators for monitoring normative change.

Introduction

Female genital mutilation (FGM) remains a major public health, human rights and gender-equity concern, with more than 230 million girls and women worldwide having undergone the practice.1 FGM is defined as all procedures that involve the partial or total removal of the external female genitalia, or other injury to the female genital organs, for non-medical reasons.1 It is internationally recognised as a violation of the human rights of girls and women and is associated with a range of immediate and long-term health risks, including severe pain, infection, obstetrical complications and psychological harm.1 Despite global commitments, including Sustainable Development Goal (SDG) target 5.3 to eliminate harmful practices, progress has slowed in several high-burden regions and population growth threatens to increase the absolute number of girls at risk.1 2

In Kenya, national FGM prevalence has fallen to approximately 15%, based on the most recent nationally representative estimates from the Kenya Demographic and Health Survey 2022,3 yet substantial subnational heterogeneity persists. Narok County remains one of the highest-burden settings, with an estimated prevalence of 51%,3 far exceeding the national level. The practice continues to be deeply embedded in cultural identity, gendered expectations and marriageability norms.4

FGM is widely understood as a socially enforced practice, maintained not merely through individual attitudes but through interdependent expectations about what others do (empirical expectations) and what others believe should be done (normative expectations).4 Foundational work in social norms theory5 6 demonstrates that families often cut daughters due to fear of sanctions, including stigma, exclusion and diminished marriage prospects, rather than intrinsic support for the practice. These dynamics are well documented in pastoralist communities across East Africa, where marriageability beliefs exert strong pressure on households.7 8 As a result, interventions must shift collective expectations, not simply individual knowledge, to produce lasting abandonment.

Community interventions such as community dialogues around FGM norms and attitudes have therefore become central to global strategies to end FGM. Evaluations of programmes such as Tostan’s Community Empowerment Programme and UNICEF-UNFPA’s Global Programme have shown promise in transforming community norms, improving knowledge and in some cases reducing cutting.9 10 Yet, high-quality quantitative evidence demonstrating the effectiveness of community dialogues in shifting norms that sustain the practice of FGM remains limited. Most studies rely on small samples, lack adjustment for complex survey designs or do not assess multiple norm dimensions simultaneously.11 12 Crucially, almost no studies quantify the mechanisms of change, specifically, the exact sequence of change indicators that lead to the eventual abandonment of FGM.

In Kenya, counties with high-burden of FGM have expanded community-dialogue platforms under the national Anti-FGM Strategy (2023–2027)13 and the national policy for the eradication of FGM,14 but empirical assessments of these efforts remain sparse. Evidence from comparable settings suggests that norm changes may precede behavioural change by years, meaning early shifts in beliefs, intentions and marriageability perceptions are critical markers of progress.15 16 However, the extent to which such shifts are observable in real-world programme settings, outside controlled trials, remains poorly understood.

This study addresses these gaps using population-based data from Narok County, Kenya. We examine how community dialogues, a widely scaled platform for collective reflection and norm transformation, are associated with: (1) beliefs favourable to cutting, (2) intentions to cut a daughter, (3) marriageability expectations and (4) opposition to FGM. Using multiple-testing correction, and structural equation modelling, we quantify both direct effects and mediated pathways, offering one of the few empirical analyses to unpack the mechanisms through which dialogue-based interventions shift entrenched social norms. By generating rigorous, real-world evidence from a high-prevalence Kenyan setting, this study provides important insights for governments, donors and practitioners seeking to design and scale effective, norms-focused interventions in the effort to accelerate the elimination of FGM.

Methods

Intervention context and implementation approach in Narok County

We conducted a cross-sectional, community-based survey in Narok County, Kenya, to assess how participation in community-level dialogue activities is associated with social norms and opposition to FGM. These activities were implemented by Amref Health Africa under The Girl Generation: Support to the Africa-Led Movement to End FGM (TGG-ALM) programme, which aims to accelerate positive shifts in attitudes and behaviours around FGM in high-burden settings, including Narok County.17

In Narok, Amref Health Africa implemented a package of girl-centred, community-based interventions aligned with a socio-ecological model. A core component of this package is a series of facilitated community dialogues that bring together different segments of the community to reflect on FGM, its health and human-rights implications and alternative pathways for girls. These dialogues are champion-led, rights-based discussion forums in which trained community champions from the area convene and guide participants, encourage open discussion and document shifts in attitudes to inform programme adaptation. Community champions comprised of respected and influential individuals drawn from within the local context who are recognised for their credibility, communication skills and commitment to social change.

The primary intervention examined in this study is the intergenerational community dialogue: multiage forums in which girls, women, boys, men, elders and other opinion leaders meet monthly in village settings to discuss FGM and related gender and marriageability norms. Amref Health Africa-trained champions use standardised community engagement tools developed by the programme to structure the sessions, prompt debate on prevailing norms and support participants to question and renegotiate expectations around FGM.

As the programme evolved, Amref Health Africa introduced a complementary couples’ dialogue model in Narok. Couples’ dialogues are smaller, more intimate sessions that bring together married couples from the same villages approximately every 2 weeks, usually with around 10 couples per session. Amref Health Africa-trained community-based champions mobilise eligible couples, facilitate focused discussions on FGM, decision-making about daughters and marriageability and create a safer space for partners to explore sensitive issues together. This format was developed in response to evidence that beliefs and decisions about cutting are often held and negotiated at the household level, and that working with both parents simultaneously may be more effective than engaging either men or women alone.

Participants were mobilised through a combination of open community invitations and purposive recruitment, coordinated by trained local facilitators working closely with village leaders and existing administrative structures. Open invitations were disseminated during community meetings, public gatherings and through local communication channels to encourage broad, voluntary participation from all community members. To complement this, facilitators applied purposive strategies to ensure inclusion of specific population groups as well as representation across different villages and, where relevant, households. To address potential imbalances in participation, additional targeted outreach was undertaken to include under-represented groups, particularly adolescents and young women. Facilitators were guided by predefined inclusion criteria and community mapping exercises rather than relying solely on existing networks. This approach was intended to minimise the risk of preferential recruitment of already engaged or more progressive individuals. Ongoing supervision and monitoring of participant composition were conducted to ensure diversity and to maintain a balance of perspectives across social, age and gender groups.

Sample size

Data were collected in June and July 2024 in Narok County, Kenya. The sample sizes were computed separately for women and men based on the proportion of individuals in Narok County who reported support for the continuation of FGM or that FGM is required by religion, culture or society in the 2022 Kenya Demographic and Health Survey taking into account 20% non-response. Study investigators worked with the local administration and programme implementers to generate a list of all villages in Narok East subcounty in Narok County where the TGG-ALM programme was being implemented. Villages were randomly selected from the list generated and a research team conducted listing of all households and their members in the sampled villages to generate the sampling frame. Eligible participants were then randomly sampled from the sampling frame. The analytical dataset included 2658 respondents aged 15–60 years, of whom 1272 (47.9%) were women and 1386 (52.1%) were men. Because some survey modules were not completed by all participants, effective sample sizes vary across outcomes and are reported alongside each analysis.

Measures

Exposure variables

The primary exposure of interest was participation in mixed-sex community dialogues. We constructed a binary indicator coded as one when a respondent reported attending at least one community dialogue session and zero otherwise. Among respondents who were interviewed as couples, we also considered participation in couples-only dialogues as a secondary exposure, again measured as a binary indicator of attendance at least once. For couples, we estimated the separate and combined associations of exposure to community dialogues, couples-only dialogues and simultaneous exposure to both platforms.

Outcome variables

The study examined four outcomes capturing distinct dimensions of FGM-related norms: (1) beliefs favourable to cutting, (2) intention to cut a daughter, (3) perceived marriageability of uncut women and (4) opposition to FGM.

Beliefs favourable to cutting were measured using a composite index constructed from five items assessing perceived attributes associated with FGM (eg, whether cutting is associated with being healthy, clean, pure, modern or socially acceptable/different). Each item was first dichotomised into endorsement versus non-endorsement of pro-FGM views and then summed to create an additive index ranging from 0 to 5. This index was subsequently dichotomised into ‘none to low’ (0–2) versus ‘mixed to high’ (3–5) endorsement, reflecting a distinction between consistent rejection and any degree of positive or ambivalent attitudes towards FGM. Internal consistency of the belief scale was assessed using Cronbach’s alpha (α=0.79), indicating acceptable reliability for group-level comparisons (see online supplemental table S1).

The remaining outcomes were measured using single survey items. Intention to cut a daughter was defined as any stated likelihood of subjecting a daughter to FGM. Perceived marriageability norms captured agreement with the statement that men would not marry uncut women. Opposition to FGM was measured as a self-reported position against the practice. All three variables were coded as binary indicators. These measures are grounded in established constructs in the FGM and social norms literature but were adapted to the study context. Detailed item wording and coding are provided in online supplemental table S2.

Covariates

Covariates were selected a priori based on theoretical considerations and prior literature on FGM and gender norms. These included sex, marital status, education level, ethnicity and age group. Age was categorised into four groups (15–19, 20–24, 25–35 and 36–60 years) to reflect key life-course stages relevant for FGM and marriage. Ethnicity was grouped into Maasai versus Kalenjin/Kikuyu/other ethnicities. Almost all respondents (94%) identified as Christian, resulting in minimal variation in religion; this variable was therefore not included as a covariate in multivariable models.

Statistical analysis

All analyses incorporated the stratified and clustered survey design with village-level clustering and robust SEs. Because all eligible individuals had the same probability of selection, the survey was treated as self-weighting and analyses were conducted using equal weights across respondents. The analysis was conducted in the following steps.

First, we described the sample using proportions and corresponding 95% CIs for socio-demographic characteristics and each outcome. Second, because the four outcomes are binary indicators that may arise from shared underlying propensities, we examined their joint structure using tetrachoric correlations and presented the resulting correlation matrix to characterise the co-movement of pro-FGM beliefs, intentions, perceived marriageability norms and opposition to FGM.

Third, to estimate associations between exposure to dialogue activities and each outcome, we fitted logistic regression models using generalised estimating equations (GEE) with an exchangeable working correlation structure and clustering at the village level, adjusting for socio-demographic characteristics. We adopted a population-averaged (GEE) approach because the primary objective was to estimate average associations between dialogue exposure and normative outcomes at the population level, rather than cluster-specific effects. This approach is appropriate for estimating marginal effects in correlated data and aligns with the study’s focus on community-level norm patterns.

Fourth, given that we analysed multiple correlated outcomes and, in complementary analyses, two related exposures (community dialogues and couples-only dialogues), we addressed multiple testing using a Westfall-Young resampling procedure with 100 permutations within exposure category. Family-wise adjusted p values from this procedure are reported in online supplemental table S3.

Fifth, for couples only models, we used the logistic models to derive predicted probabilities for each outcome under four exposure scenarios: no dialogue participation, participation in community dialogues only, participation in couples-only dialogues only and combined participation in both platforms. From these predictions, we calculated absolute percentage-point differences with 95% CIs, which are presented in online supplemental table S4.

Sixth, to examine potential mechanisms linking community dialogues to opposition to FGM, we estimated a generalised structural equation model with Bernoulli-logit links, specifying beliefs, intentions and marriageability norms as mediators. Associations are reported on the log-odds scale, consistent with the model specification. This mediation analysis is exploratory and rests on strong assumptions, including no unmeasured confounding of the exposure-mediator, mediator-outcome and exposure-outcome relationships, as well as correct model specification. These assumptions are unlikely to be fully satisfied in cross-sectional data, where temporal ordering between exposure, mediators and outcomes cannot be established. Accordingly, the estimated indirect linkages should be interpreted as indicative of plausible mechanisms consistent with theory, rather than as evidence of causal mediation. Because the model is specified using a logit link, meaning that effects are estimated on the log-odds scale. Because log-odds and ORs can be difficult to interpret, the results are more meaningfully understood in terms of predicted probabilities.

Unless otherwise specified, all statistical inferences relied on two-sided tests with a significance level of 0.05, survey-adjusted 95% CIs and complete-case analysis. Effective sample sizes therefore vary across outcomes and are reported alongside the corresponding results.

Patient and public involvement

Patients or the public were not involved in the design, or conduct, or reporting, or dissemination plans of our research.

Results

Sample descriptive statistics

The analytical sample comprised 2658 respondents, with 52.1% men and 47.9% women (table 1). Age was broadly distributed: 14.1% were 15–19 years, 17.8% were 20–24 years, 35.7% were ages between 25–35 years and 32.4% were 36–60 years. The majority of the respondents were married (70.4%), while 29.6% were never married or separated. Regarding educational attainment, 27.9% reported no schooling, 26.1% had primary education, 34.6% had secondary education and 11.5% had attained tertiary education. Most of the respondents were of the Maasai ethnic group (86.7%).

Attitudes and intentions related to FGM showed substantial heterogeneity. About 45.0% of the respondents expressed mixed or positive attitudes towards a cut girl, while intentions to cut a daughter were much lower at 13.1%. Belief that uncut girls are not marriageable was held by 25.6% of the respondents. In contrast, the majority of the respondents (81.2%) reported completely opposing FGM. Dialogues were also associated with higher probability of opposing FGM (aOR=2.39, 95% CI 1.84 to 3.11, p<0.001). Gender differences were modest. Women had higher odds than men of holding pro-FGM beliefs (aOR=1.36, 95% CI 1.12 to 1.65, p=0.002) but slightly lower odds of intending to cut a daughter (aOR=0.79, 95% CI 0.62 to 0.99, p=0.042). Age gradients were limited. Compared with 15–19 years old, older age groups were less likely to agree that uncut girls are not marriageable: aOR=0.51 (95% CI 0.37 to 0.69, p<0.001) for ages 25–35 and 0.59 (95% CI 0.43 to 0.82, p=0.002) for ages 36–60. However, compared with 15–19 years old, participants aged 36–60 had higher odds of pro-FGM beliefs (aOR=1.34, 95% CI 1.00 to 1.79, p=0.049).

Education showed a clear protective pattern. Primary education was associated only with lower intentions to cut (aOR=0.75, 95% CI 0.58 to 0.96; p=0.023). Secondary education was linked to lower pro-FGM beliefs (aOR=0.70, 95% CI 0.52 to 0.95; p=0.020), lower intentions to cut (aOR=0.45, 95% CI 0.32 to 0.63, p<0.001), lower marriageability beliefs (aOR=0.66, 95% CI 0.54 to 0.82, p<0.001) and higher opposition to FGM (aOR=1.88, 95% CI 1.39 to 2.54, p<0.001). Tertiary education was associated with markedly lower intentions to cut (aOR=0.39, 95% CI 0.23 to 0.65, p<0.001) and higher opposition to FGM (aOR=2.77, 95% CI 1.90 to 4.02, p<0.001). Ethnic Maasai respondents did not differ significantly from other ethnic groups on norms or intentions but had lower likelihood of opposing FGM (aOR=0.75, 95% CI 0.59 to 0.95; p=0.017).

For beliefs favourable to cutting, the predicted probability under no intervention was 50.1%. Couples-only dialogues showed little change (55.0%), whereas community dialogues (36.9%) and especially exposure to both interventions (41.6%) were associated with noticeably lower endorsement of pro-cutting beliefs. For intention to cut a daughter, the baseline probability was 16.1%. Couple dialogues again produced almost no change (16.7%), while community dialogues (7.9%) and both interventions (8.3%) reduced intentions to about half of the no-intervention level.

For the norm that ‘uncut girls are not marriageable’, the no-intervention probability was 29.4%, falling to 22.0% with couple dialogues, 18.7% with community dialogues and 13.4% with both, showing a graded decline with greater exposure. The baseline predicted probability for opposition to FGM was 75.7%. Couple dialogues were associated with a small increase (79.7%) while community dialogues (88.3%) and both interventions (91.5%) were linked to substantial gains in increasing the likelihood of opposing FGM.

Multiple-testing adjustments using Westfall-Young and Bonferroni procedures, reported in online supplemental table S3, confirmed the robustness of effects for community dialogues across all four outcomes, but indicated a more selective pattern for couple dialogues, with only belief that ‘uncut girls are not marriageable’ showing a marginal significance after correction.

Because the model is specified on the logit scale, results are reported in log-odds; however, they are most meaningfully interpreted in terms of changes in predicted probabilities. Consistent with earlier findings, exposure to community dialogues is associated with a substantial increase in the likelihood of opposing FGM, and a large share of this difference is linked to changes in intermediate normative constructs.

On the log-odds scale, the direct association between community dialogues and opposition to FGM is 0.92 (≈aOR 2.5, 95% CI ≈1.8 to 3.5; p<0.001). The total indirect effect via the three mediators was much larger (NIE=6.82; p<0.001), such that an estimated 88% of the overall impact of dialogues on opposition was mediated (overall proportion mediated=0.88; 95% CI 0.83 to 0.93). The total effect on the log-odds scale was 7.74 (p<0.001). The decomposition of the indirect effect indicates that the largest single pathway is Dialogue → Intention to cut → Opposition (β=2.39; 35% of the mediated effect), followed by Dialogue → Beliefs → Intention → Opposition (2.06; 30%).

Marriageability norms also play an important role: Dialogue → ‘uncut not marriageable’ → Opposition accounts for 14% of the mediated effect (0.96), with a further 10% through Dialogue → Beliefs → ‘uncut not marriageable’ → Opposition (0.71). Finally, the one-step pathway Dialogue → Beliefs → Opposition contributes another 10% (0.70). In combination, these results suggest that community dialogues increase the likelihood to oppose FGM primarily by shifting intentions, beliefs about cutting and marriageability norms, rather than only through a direct effect on opposition.

Discussion

This study provides robust quantitative evidence that community-level dialogues on FGM are strongly associated with more progressive social norms, reduced intentions to cut daughters and substantially higher opposition to the practice. Among married couples, predicted opposition to FGM rises from around three-quarters among non-participants to over 90% among those exposed to both community and couple dialogues, while pro-cutting beliefs, marriageability concerns and intentions to cut fall sharply. This corresponds closely to what social norms theorists would expect when collective expectations begin to shift.5

These findings align with a well-established body of work that describes FGM as a social norm or social convention, maintained by empirical expectations (‘what others do’) and normative expectations (‘what others think I should do’), and less so due to individual preference or a fixed cultural ‘tradition’.18 Game-theoretic work argues that families cut their daughters to secure marriageability and social status in contexts where they expect other families to do the same, and where deviation can attract serious social sanctions.18 Ethnographic and survey studies in Senegambia, Sudan and other settings also describe FGM as a self-reinforcing institution, closely tied to gendered expectations of purity, respectability and bride-worthiness.19 The high baseline prevalence of perceived marriageability sanctions in our sample, and their very strong association with intentions to cut daughters, are consistent with this interpretation.

Within this theoretical landscape, our findings suggest that structured community dialogues, a core element of many global strategies to end FGM, are linked to changes in the social expectations that sustain the practice.20 Participation in community dialogues was associated with lower odds of holding beliefs favourable to cutting, endorsing the view that uncut girls are not marriageable and intending to cut a daughter. It was also associated with higher odds of opposing FGM outright. These results mirror evidence from the Tostan Community Empowerment Programme in Senegal,21 where participatory education and deliberation have been linked to shifts in knowledge, attitudes and perceived norms and to large-scale public declarations of abandonment. They are also consistent with findings from community-conversation and community-dialogue initiatives in Ethiopia and other countries, which report reduced support for FGM and child marriage when communities engage in structured, facilitated reflection.22–24

By modelling multiple normative constructs simultaneously and using mediation analysis, this study moves beyond the usual ‘Does it work?’ question to interrogate how community dialogues may exert their influence. We find that most of the total effect of dialogues on opposition to FGM operates indirectly through shifts in pro-cutting beliefs, marriageability concerns and intentions, a pathway remarkably consistent with Bicchieri’s account of how interventions alter social expectations and thereby unlock behaviour change.5 Similar mechanisms have been described in qualitative and mixed-methods studies from Senegambia,19 where readiness to abandon FGM is shaped by perceptions that others are also reconsidering the practice and that non-cut daughters will not be excluded from marriage. Important to note is that the findings from Senegambia19 informed the TGG-ALM programme’s theory of change and therefore the study findings provide quantitative confirmation of these pathways in a pastoralist Kenyan setting, demonstrating that shifts in perceived marriageability expectations and intentions to cut are not just correlated with, but also mediate, the relationship between dialogue exposure and expressed opposition to FGM.

The strongest single indirect pathway we observe, Dialogue → Intentions → Opposition, suggests that intervening on perceived behavioural expectations is pivotal. This aligns with broader social norms intervention evidence; when programmes explicitly surface and contest ‘what people like us do’, intentions often move first, followed by behaviour once enough people are perceived to have changed.25 In our data, community dialogues were also associated with a sizeable direct linkage to opposition, even after accounting for these mediators. One plausible interpretation is that facilitated spaces not only transmit new information but also enhance individual agency and moral conviction, consistent with reports from participatory FGM and violence-prevention programmes that emphasise critical reflection, collective efficacy and rights-based framing.26

Study results also speak to an important programme design question: Is it sufficient to focus on couples or households, or must interventions also act at the wider community level in order to be effective? Couples-only dialogues in this study were associated with some weakening of marriageability concerns but had far more limited associations with other normative outcomes and with opposition to FGM, particularly when delivered in the absence of community dialogues. This pattern is consistent with the insight that when sanctions and expectations are enforced at community level, by elders, peers, religious leaders and marriage brokers, interventions confined to the nuclear household are unlikely to fully offset the social risks of non-compliance.27 Importantly, even though couples-only dialogues did not have a strong effect on their own, results showed that when combined with community dialogues, they produced an even greater effect than either kind of approach on its own. Evidence from Tostan,21 UNICEF-UNFPA Joint Programme countries,20 and community-conversation models17 24 likewise points to the importance of multilayered approaches that combine work with families, peer groups and community institutions, including religious and traditional leaders.

Study findings therefore support current guidance that positions community-level deliberation and public dialogue, not one-off information campaigns, as the backbone of norm-shifting strategies on FGM.20 This study also adds to the empirical debate on whether FGM norms are rigid or malleable. Some scholarship portrays FGM as deeply entrenched and resistant to change28; other work, especially from Senegambia, highlights fluidity, experimentation and coordinated shifts in practice.29 The strong associations we observe between dialogue exposure and normative outcomes, in a context where FGM prevalence remains high, support the latter view; norms appear responsive when communities are given structured opportunities to reflect, access alternative narratives and see others signal dissent. This is in line with newer analyses showing that people’s readiness to change FGM depends on perceived norm coordination and the availability of credible alternatives, rather than on immutable ‘culture’.30

From a policy perspective, our findings have several implications. First, they provide quantitative support for Kenya’s and the UNFPA-UNICEF Joint Programme’s emphasis on dialogue-centred, community-driven approaches to FGM, complementing case studies and programme reports that have documented large numbers of public declarations and community-level commitments to abandonment.22 24 Second, the identification of beliefs about cutting, perceived marriageability expectations and behavioural intentions as key mediators suggests that these constructs could be used as intermediate indicators in monitoring and evaluation frameworks, capturing normative shifts that precede measurable declines in incidence.20 Third, the comparatively weaker effects of couples-only dialogues underscore that programmes should avoid fixating on individual attitude change but rather combine such approaches with interventions that target to alter shared expectations and sanctioning structures.

At the same time, the study underscores that norm-shifting dialogues are necessary but not sufficient. Structural and material constraints, including poverty, limited schooling options for girls and the organisation of local marriage markets, continue to interact with norms in ways that may slow or reverse progress.31 Evidence from broader gender-norms and child marriage interventions suggests that the most sustainable gains occur when deliberative norm change efforts are combined with investments in girls’ education, economic opportunities and enforcement of protective laws.32 In the Narok context, this implies aligning community dialogues with initiatives that expand schooling and livelihood options for girls, strengthen implementation of Kenya’s anti-FGM legislation and address cross-border cutting in adjacent counties and countries.33

The generalisability of these findings should be interpreted in light of the specific social and institutional context in which the study was conducted. In Narok County, FGM is closely tied to marriageability norms and reinforced through community-level expectations and sanctioning structures. In settings where FGM is less strongly linked to marriage prospects, is practised in more individualised or medicalised forms, or where social enforcement mechanisms are weaker or differently organised, dialogue-based interventions may not produce comparable shifts in normative outcomes or may operate through different pathways. The magnitude of observed associations is therefore likely to be context-dependent, and caution is warranted in extrapolating effect sizes beyond similar high-prevalence settings.

Study strengths and limitations

This study makes three distinct contributions to the FGM norms literature. First, it offers rare, population-representative, quantitative evidence from a real-world implementation setting in East Africa, using rigorous analytical methods and correction for multiple testing. Second, it empirically tests theoretically specified pathways linking community dialogues to multiple normative constructs and to opposition to FGM, providing mediation evidence that has been largely absent from prior work. Third, it differentiates between community and household-level dialogue strategies, providing actionable insight for programme design.

Despite the stated strengths, this study has limitations that should be acknowledged. Its cross-sectional design precludes definitive causal inference and cannot distinguish selection into dialogues from intervention effects. We also rely on self-reported attitudes and intentions, which may be subject to social desirability bias; however, we employed indirect questioning approaches to help mitigate this limitation. Importantly, the persistence of substantial variation across exposure groups suggests that such bias is limited. Third, we do not directly observe FGM incidence; instead, we use normative beliefs and intentions as proximal indicators of potential behavioural change, consistent with established social norms frameworks and programme evaluation practice.20

An additional limitation relates to potential programme targeting and self-selection into dialogue participation. Individuals who are more engaged, socially connected or already more opposed to FGM may be more likely to attend community or couples’ dialogues. Programme implementation may also have prioritised communities or groups perceived as more receptive to engagement. These dynamics could bias the observed associations, potentially inflating the estimated relationships between dialogue participation and normative outcomes. Although we adjust for key socio-demographic characteristics, including education as a proxy for awareness and engagement, residual confounding due to unobserved factors, such as prior attitudes, broader social participation or exposure to other interventions, cannot be ruled out.

In addition, the mediation analysis is exploratory and subject to important methodological constraints. Estimation of indirect effects requires strong, untestable assumptions, including the absence of unmeasured confounding between exposure, mediators and outcomes, as well as correct temporal ordering. These assumptions are particularly restrictive in cross-sectional data, where temporal sequencing cannot be established and reverse causality remains possible. Accordingly, the mediation findings should be interpreted cautiously as indicative of plausible pathways rather than evidence of causal mechanisms.

Finally, the study is conducted in a specific high-prevalence, predominantly Maasai pastoralist setting and caution is therefore warranted in generalising the magnitude of effects to other contexts where the social meaning and drivers of FGM may differ.

Conclusion

Study findings reinforce and extend social-norms theory by demonstrating that structured community dialogues can shift the social expectations underpinning FGM, particularly beliefs about marriageability and intentions to cut, and that these shifts are strongly associated with increased opposition to the practice. In high-burden settings such as Narok County, scaling and sustaining such dialogue platforms, while simultaneously addressing structural drivers of gender inequality, will be critical to accelerating progress towards the elimination of FGM and achieving SDG 5.3.
