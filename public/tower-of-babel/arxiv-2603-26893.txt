Siddhartha Banerjee, Ramiro N. Deo-Campo Vuong, Robert Kleinberg

Abstract
Allocation of dynamically-arriving (i.e., online) divisible resources among a set of offline agents is a fundamental problem, with applications to online marketplaces, scheduling, portfolio selection, signal processing, and many other areas. The water-filling algorithm, which allocates an incoming resource to maximize the minimum load of compatible agents, is ubiquitous in many of these applications whenever the underlying objectives prefer more balanced solutions; however, the analysis and guarantees differ across settings. We provide a justification for the widespread use of water-filling by showing that it is a universally minimax optimal policy in a strong sense. Formally, our main result implies that water-filling is minimax optimal for a large class of objectives – including both Schur-concave maximization and Schur-convex minimization – under α \alpha -regret and competitive ratio measures. This optimality holds for every fixed tuple of agents and resource counts. Remarkably, water-filling achieves these guarantees as a myopic policy, remaining entirely agnostic to the objective function, agent count, and resource availability. Our techniques notably depart from the popular primal-dual analysis of online algorithms, and instead develop a novel way to apply the theory of majorization in online settings to achieve universality guarantees.

Introduction

Allocation of divisible resources (goods or chores) to agents is a fundamental algorithmic primitive. In many applications, the resources must be assigned dynamically as they arrive, under uncertainty about the future availability of resources and their compatibility with different agents, and in a way that is equitable. For example, a funding organization may allocate grants to eligible recipients over a sequence of time periods, without knowing the amount of funds that will be available for distribution in future periods nor which sets of recipients will be eligible. In any such setting, one may ask which allocation policies are minimax optimal, in the sense that their worst-case outcome is as equitable as possible.
It would appear that this goal is underspecified; surely the answer must depend on the metric used to evaluate equitable allocations. The main message of this paper is that, despite the question’s ambiguity, it has an essentially unambiguous answer: the well-known and widely used
water-filling
algorithm, which greedily allocates arriving resources to maximize the minimum load among eligible recipients.

To this point, the ubiquity of
water-filling
— also known as max-min fairness or resource balancing — has largely been explained through case-by-case analysis of different equity-promoting objectives. In each of the cases considered, the
water-filling
algorithm was shown to attain the best possible competitive ratio guarantee in the limit as the number of agents grows, often via primal-dual analysis. Primal-dual techniques, despite producing celebrated results, have left gaps in our understanding of the
water-filling
principle and equitable dynamic resource allocation. In particular, why is greedily equalizing resources seemingly the only viable strategy for many applications of dynamic allocation in worst-case settings with an arbitrary number of agents?

1.1
Overview of Results and Techniques

One can formulate worst-case analysis of dynamic resource allocation as
a two-player zero-sum game between an algorithm designer and an adversary.
The algorithm designer moves first by selecting a
(deterministic or randomized) policy
\mathcal{A}
for making allocation
decisions given the available information. The adversary
responds by constructing a sequence of requests
E
, where
each request designates a quantity of divisible resources
to be allocated and a set of eligible recipients. Running policy

\mathcal{A}
on request sequence
E
leads to a load
vector,
\mathcal{A}(E)
, specifying the total quantity of
resources allocated to each agent. The game’s payoff
is the
\alpha
-regret: for a given maximization objective

f:\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\geq 0}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
and
comparison
factor

\alpha\geq 0
, this is given by the formula

\text{$\alpha$-Regret}=\alpha\cdot\max_{\ell\in\Delta(E)}f(\ell)-\mathbb{E}[f(\mathcal{A}(E))],

where
\Delta(E)
denotes the set of
feasible load vectors for request sequence
E
.
One can also formulate
\alpha
-regret for a
minimization objective
g:\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\geq 0}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
,
using the formula

\displaystyle\text{$\alpha$-Regret}=\mathbb{E}[g(\mathcal{A}(E))]-\alpha\cdot\min_{\ell\in\Delta(E)}g(\ell)

The adversary seeks to maximize
\alpha
-regret,
while the algorithm designer seeks to minimize it.
Formulating worst-case analysis in terms of
\alpha
-regret is at
least as general as formulating it in terms of competitive analysis: an algorithm is
\alpha
-competitive if and only if the supremum
of its
\alpha
-regret is finite, and it is strictly
\alpha
-competitive
if and only if this supremum is non-positive.

Our main result pertains to objective functions that obey the following minimal requirement to be considered
equity-promoting: when one agent (Alice) receives
weakly less resources than another (Bob), the objective value
cannot be improved by taking resources from Alice and giving
them to Bob. This property is called Schur-concavity (for
maximization objectives) or Schur-convexity (for minimization
objectives); we use the term
Schur-monotonicity

as a catch-all for both properties.

We show that in the

\alpha
-regret minimization game defined by
any

Schur-monotone objective and
any
non-negative comparison
factor
\alpha
, the
water-filling
policy
is the optimal play for an algorithm designer against adaptive adversaries.
When the adversary is restricted to be oblivious,

water-filling
remains optimal
for the (narrower) class of symmetric concave maximization objectives
and symmetric convex minimization objectives.
These results on the minimax optimality of
water-filling
persist even if the adversary is constrained to
generate request sequences with a specified number of
offline nodes, number of requests, and
total amount of resources to be allocated.
In other words, even though the
water-filling
algorithm has
no foreknowledge of these parameters, nor of the objective function, it
is minimax optimal even when compared against algorithms that
have such foreknowledge.

In summary, our results show that the worst-case analysis
of dynamic equitable allocation for divisible resources
produces surprisingly robust guidance for algorithm designers:

water-filling
is optimal against adaptive adversaries for essentially
all equity-promoting objectives, and it is optimal against oblivious
adversaries for a broad sub-class of such objectives.
In particular,
Schur-monotone objective functions include
the most common objective functions for
evaluating fractional allocations when agents
are treated symmetrically. In fractional matching
when all agents have equal capacity,
c>0
, the objective
function is
f(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\min(c,\mathbf{x}(i))
,
which is Schur-concave. The Nash
social welfare objective,
\left(\prod_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{x}(i)\right)^{1/n}
,
is likewise Schur-concave. Common minimization
objectives such as
p
-norms and the Gini index are Schur-convex.
In
Section

2.2
we give several examples of widely-studied objectives that fall in this class.

To derive worst-case optimality results pertaining to
such a broad range of objectives, we abstract
away from objective functions altogether and
directly compare load vectors using

majorization
, a preorder relation that compares the equitability of different allocations.
Schur-convex (resp., Schur-concave) functions are
precisely the order-preserving (resp., order-reversing)
functions from the majorization order to the
real numbers. Underpinning our results stated above
is a more fundamental result,
Theorem

1
, that
expresses the optimality of the
water-filling
policy
directly in terms of the majorization relation.
Since majorization is a preorder and not a total order, a given set of vectors need not have a minimal or a maximal element. However, the worst-case analysis
of
water-filling
is facilitated by the existence
of such majorization-extremal elements in two
important cases.
First, in
2
, we show that the set of feasible load vectors for a given request sequence
E
always
has a unique majorization-minimal element, which we denote
by
\textsc{OPT}(E)
. Second, in
Theorem

3
we prove
that for any specified hindsight-optimal vector

\mathbf{\ell}
, as
E
ranges over all the request sequences
with
\textsc{OPT}(E)=\mathbf{\ell}
, the set of load vectors

\textsc{WF}(E)
produced by
water-filling
has a majorization-maximal
element, and we give an explicit
formula for this element.
The minimax optimality of
water-filling
among deterministic algorithms is established via

Theorem

1
,
which asserts that for any request sequence
E
, if an algorithm designer deviates from
water-filling
to some other deterministic allocation policy
\mathcal{A}
, then the adversary can respond by modifying
E
to a
request sequence
E^{\prime}
that produces a less equitable load vector —
\mathbb{E}\mathcal{A}(E^{\prime})
majorizes
\textsc{WF}(E)
—
but a more equitable hindsight-optimal solution, in that

\textsc{OPT}(E^{\prime})
is majorized by
\textsc{OPT}(E)
.
(If
\mathcal{A}
is randomized and the adversary
is allowed to be adaptive, we show in
Appendix

A

that the same conclusion
holds with probability 1 instead of with expectations.)
Regardless of the choice of Schur-monotone
objective function, the adversary’s response to the algorithm designer’s deviation makes
the objective value worse at
\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{A}(E^{\prime})]

and better at
\textsc{OPT}(E^{\prime})
, implying that worst-case

\alpha
-regret increases when deviating from
WF

to any
\mathcal{A}
.
In the case of randomized
\mathcal{A}
and oblivious adversaries,
an application of Jensen’s inequality leads to the same conclusion
provided the objective function is symmetric
and concave (or convex).

To prove
Theorem

1
we take the perspective of an adversarial environment that designs the availability/compatibility of resources to hinder policies trying to produce majorization minimal allocations.
The first part of the proof (
Section

4
) identifies a class of worst-case request sequences for
water-filling
, which are represented by
nested edge sets
on a bipartite graph.
Our proof uses a series of combinatorial operations (
Algorithm

1
) to transform a given request sequence into one encoded by a nested sequence on which
water-filling
provides a majorizing allocation, and the hindsight optimal solution is minorizing.
The second part of our proof (
Section

5
) considers a principal deviating from
water-filling
to play an arbitrary policy.
We show that under such a deviation, the alternative policy produces a less equitable expected allocation than
water-filling
on request sequences encoded by a nested edge set.
Combining these results, we find that
water-filling
yields a majorization minimal expected allocation when requests are generated by a worst-case adversary.

2.1
Setting and Problem Statement

2.1.1
The Allocation Game

We consider a zero-sum game between an algorithm designer and an adversary on a system with a set of
n
offline nodes (agents) and
m
online nodes (resources) that arrive sequentially.
Each online node
t\in[m]
is characterized by a neighborhood
N_{t}\subseteq[n]
of compatible offline agents and a divisible quantity
q_{t}>0
, which the adversary designs.
A
request sequence
is denoted by
E=((N_{t},q_{t}))_{t\in[m]}
, and we use
\mathcal{E}_{n,m,q}
to represent the set of all sequences with total capacity
q=\sum_{t}q_{t}
.
To consider all values for some characteristic, we replace it with “
:
” – for instance,
\mathcal{E}_{n,:,q}=\bigcup_{m\in\mathbb{N}}\mathcal{E}_{n,m,q}
represents sequences with
n
offline nodes and total capacity
q
, but split between an arbitrary number of online nodes.

Upon the arrival of online node
t
, the algorithm designer must immediately select a fractional allocation
\mathbf{x}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{n}
.
She selects her allocation via a (possibly randomized) policy
\mathcal{A}
, i.e. a function that maps the current node and the history of arrivals and decisions to a feasible allocation.
An allocation is feasible if it satisfies:

•

Compatibility:

\mathbf{x}_{t}(i)=0
for all
i\notin N_{t}
.

•

Full Distribution:

\sum_{i\in[n]}\mathbf{x}_{t}(i)=q_{t}
.

Full distribution is necessary for minimization objectives (e.g. in scheduling/load balancing), and without loss for non-decreasing maximization objectives (i.e., settings with ‘free disposal’).

For any request sequence
E=((N_{t},q_{t}))_{t\in[m]}
, we use
\Delta(N_{t},q_{t})
to denote the set of feasible allocations for online node
t
.
The Minkowski sum of
\Delta(N_{t},q_{t})
is
\Delta(E)
, and this set contains every feasible load vector on
E
.
We also define
\mathcal{A}(E)=\sum_{t=1}^{m}\mathbf{x}_{t}
as the cumulative allocation produced by policy
\mathcal{A}
on
E
, and
\bm{\ell}_{t}=\sum_{s=1}^{t}\mathbf{x}_{s}
as the cumulative intermediate allocation (or load-vector) after
t
arrivals.

Finally, we identify a special subset of request sequences that are critical for our characterization.

Definition 1

(Nested sequences)
.

A request sequence
E=((N_{t},q_{t}))_{t\in[m]}
is said to be nested if
N_{1}\supseteq\dots\supseteq N_{m}
, i.e., online neighborhoods form a chain, with each subsequent neighborhood being nested within the previous neighborhoods.

\mathcal{E}^{\textsc{nest}}_{n,m,q}\subseteq\mathcal{E}_{n,m,q}
is the set of nested sequences with
n
offline nodes,
m
online nodes, and total quantity
q
.

2.1.2
Objectives and Competitive Performance

The goal of the algorithm designer for policy
\mathcal{A}
is to optimize some objective function
\mathbb{E}f(\mathcal{A}(E))
.
Moreover, the performance of the policy
\mathcal{A}
is measured against an optimal-in-hindsight solution which knows the entire sequence
E
in advance.
We formalize this via the notion of
\alpha
-regret, which generalizes both regret and the commonly considered competitive ratio objectives.
In more detail, given a comparison factor
\alpha\in R_{>0}
, we consider two types of objectives:

1.

Fair Welfare Maximization:
For any equity/welfare measure
f:\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
, the principal maximizes
\mathbb{E}[f(\mathcal{A}(E))]
against a discounted optimal hindsight solution:

c_{\alpha,f}^{\max}(\mathcal{A},E)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\Delta}}{{=}}\alpha\cdot\max_{\bm{\ell}\in\Delta(E)}f(\bm{\ell})-\mathbb{E}[f(\mathcal{A}(E))]

2.

Load Balancing:
For any load/inequity measure
g:\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
, the principal minimizes
\mathbb{E}[g(\mathcal{A}(E))]
against a marked-up optimal hindsight solution:

c_{\alpha,g}^{\min}(\mathcal{A},E)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\Delta}}{{=}}\mathbb{E}[g(\mathcal{A}(E))]-\alpha\cdot\min_{\bm{\ell}\in\Delta(E)}g(\bm{\ell})

The game between the principal and the adversary is parameterized by offline node count
n\in\mathbb{N}
, online node count
m\in\mathbb{N}
, total quantity
q\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
, objective
f
(or
g
), and comparison factor
\alpha\in R_{>0}
.
The algorithm designer moves before the adversary and selects a policy
\mathcal{A}
.
The adversary responds with a request sequence
E\in\mathcal{E}_{n,m,q}
, which they design with access to the policy
\mathcal{A}
played by the algorithm designer (but not its random coin flips).
In other words, we model the adversary as oblivious. See

Appendix

A
for an extension of our model and results
to adaptive adversaries.
The goal of the algorithm designer is to minimize her worst-case
\alpha
-regret:

\textsc{Reg}_{(n,m,q),\alpha,f}^{\max}(\mathcal{A})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\Delta}}{{=}}\sup_{E\in\mathcal{E}_{n,m,q}}c_{\alpha,f}^{\max}(\mathcal{A},E)\quad\mbox{or}\quad\textsc{Reg}_{(n,m,q),\alpha,g}^{\min}(\mathcal{A})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\Delta}}{{=}}\sup_{E\in\mathcal{E}_{n,m,q}}c_{\alpha,g}^{\min}(\mathcal{A},E).

Comparison factor
\alpha=1
corresponds to standard regret for policy
\mathcal{A}
.
On the other hand, the competitive ratio of
\mathcal{A}
corresponds to the largest (resp. smallest)
\alpha
such that
\textsc{Reg}_{(n,m,q),\alpha,f}^{\max}(\mathcal{A})\leq 0
(resp.
\textsc{Reg}_{(n,m,q),\alpha,g}^{\min}(\mathcal{A})\leq 0
).
Our results encompass all of these settings (parameters
(m,n,q,\alpha)
, both objective types, and a large class of functions
f
) in a universal manner via a single policy.

2.2
Majorization

Unlike previous work that studies different objective functions applied to fractional allocation vectors, we directly compare and analyze the vectors using majorization.
This preorder relation compares the equity of two given vectors, with vectors lower in the order being more equitable.
The economic justification behind majorization is the so-called Pigou-Dalton (or Robin Hood) transfer principle, which states that transferring resources from richer to poorer agents yields a more equitable distribution.
Below, we define the parts of the theory necessary for our work; for a more detailed exposition, refer to
2
;
29
.

Definition 2

(Majorization)
.

Let
\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\geq 0}
with
\mathbf{x}([n])=\mathbf{y}([n])
.
Then we say
\mathbf{x}
majorizes
\mathbf{y}
(denoted
\mathbf{x}\succeq\mathbf{y}
) iff the cumulative sums in decreasing order for
\mathbf{x}
dominate those for
\mathbf{y}
:

\mathbf{x}^{\downarrow}([j])\geq\mathbf{y}^{\downarrow}([j])\quad\forall\,j\in[n]

Equivalently, for cumulative sums in increasing order, we have
\mathbf{x}^{\uparrow}([j])\leq\mathbf{y}^{\uparrow}([j])
for all
j\in[n]
.

We sometimes find it convenient to say
\mathbf{y}
minorizes
\mathbf{x}
(and write
\mathbf{y}\preceq\mathbf{x}
).
Also, if vectors
\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{n}
majorize each other (i.e.
\mathbf{x}\succeq\mathbf{y}
and
\mathbf{x}\preceq\mathbf{y}
), then we say they are in the same equivalence class, and write
\mathbf{x}\sim\mathbf{y}
.
Vectors
\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}
are equivalent iff there exists a permutation matrix
P
satisfying
\mathbf{x}=P\mathbf{y}
. Below are equivalent conditions for
\mathbf{x}\succeq\mathbf{y}
.

Fact 1

(Equivalent Characterizations of Majorization)
.

Let
\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\geq 0}
with
\mathbf{x}([n])=\mathbf{y}([n])
.
Then
\mathbf{x}\succeq\mathbf{y}
if and only if the following equivalent conditions hold:

•

Hardy, Littlewood, and Polya
21
:
\mathbf{y}=A\mathbf{x}
for some double stochastic
A\in\mathbf{R}_{\geq 0}^{n\times n}
.

•

Karamata’s Inequality
1
1

1

We state a special case of Karamata’s theorem. When
\mathbf{x}([n])=\mathbf{y}([n])
, it suffices for the inequality to hold on thresholding functions, whereas consideration of all convex functions is required in general.
:
\sum_{i\in[n]}\left(\mathbf{x}(i)-\gamma\right)^{+}\geq\sum_{i\in[n]}\left(\mathbf{y}(i)-\gamma\right)^{+}
for every threshold
\gamma\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
.

2.2.1
Schur-Monotone Functions

Majorization is closely related to Schur-monotone functions: Schur-concave/Schur-convex functions reverse/follow the majorization order, respectively.

Definition 3

(Schur-monotone Functions)
.

A function
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
is Schur-concave if for all
\mathbf{x,y}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that
\mathbf{x}\preceq\mathbf{y}
, we have
f(\mathbf{x})\geq f(\mathbf{y})
.
In contrast, a Schur-convex function
g:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
has the property that
\mathbf{x}\preceq\mathbf{y}
implies
g(\mathbf{x})\leq g(\mathbf{y})
.

The Schur-concave and Schur-convex function classes contain most meaningful measures of equity and inequity, respectively.
These classes are expressive; for instance, every symmetric and quasi-convex (resp. quasi-concave) function is Schur-convex (resp. Schur-concave)
(
29
, Proposition C.3)
.
We provide a few notable examples; many more are available in
2
;
29
.
In each of the following, we take
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{n}
and
\bar{\mathbf{x}}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i\in[n]}\mathbf{x}(i)
.

Schur-concave Functions

•

Matching:

\sum_{i\in[n]}\min(c,\mathbf{x}(i))
for
c>0

•

Nash Social Welfare:

\left(\prod_{i\in[n]}\mathbf{x}(i)\right)^{1/n}

•

Egalitarian Welfare:

\min_{i\in[n]}{x[i]}

•

Power Means:

\left(\sum_{i\in[n]}|\mathbf{x}(i)|^{p}\right)^{1/p}
for
p<1

Schur-convex Functions

•

Gini Index:

\frac{1}{2n^{2}\bar{\mathbf{x}}}\left(\sum_{i,j\in[n]}|\mathbf{x}(i)-\mathbf{x}(j)|\right)

•

Variance:

\sum_{i\in[n]}\left(\mathbf{x}(i)-\bar{\mathbf{x}}\right)^{2}

•

Makespan:

\max_{i\in[n]}{x[i]}

•

\ell^{p}
-norms:

\left(\sum_{i\in[n]}|\mathbf{x}(i)|^{p}\right)^{1/p}
for
p\geq 1

2.2.2
Majorization Minimal Offline Allocations

For online algorithms, typical performance measures compare the objective value achieved by an online policy to the optimal offline objective (i.e., that of the best allocation in hindsight).
It turns out that each request sequence
E\in\mathcal{E}_{n,m,q}
admits a unique load vector that is majorization minimal.
This allocation thus serves as a universal optimal hindsight solution, as it simultaneously maximizes all Schur-concave functions and minimizes all Schur-convex functions.

Fact 2

(Existence of a Majorization Minimal Hindsight Solution)
.

Given a request sequence
E=((N_{t},q_{t}))_{t\in[m]}
, there is a unique allocation (load vector)
\bm{\ell}^{*}
that is minimal in the majorization preorder:
\bm{\ell}^{*}\preceq\bm{\ell}
for any feasible allocation
\bm{\ell}\in\Delta(E)
.
Consequently,
\bm{\ell}^{*}\in\arg\max_{\bm{\ell}\in\Delta(E)}f(\bm{\ell})
for all Schur-concave
f
and
\bm{\ell}^{*}\in\arg\min_{\bm{\ell}\in\Delta(E)}g(\bm{\ell})
for all Schur-convex
g
.

Based on this, we use the shorthand
\textsc{OPT}(E)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\Delta}}{{=}}\bm{\ell}^{*}

2.3
Water Filling

We now formally define the
water-filling
algorithm.
Upon arrival of an online node
t
,
water-filling
fractionally allocates the arriving resource
q_{t}
to maximize the minimal cumulative allocation of neighboring offline nodes
N_{t}
.

Definition 4

(
water-filling
)
.

The (deterministic)
water-filling
policy proceeds as follows:
after producing allocations
\mathbf{x}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{x}_{t-1}
on the first
t-1
arrivals to yield intermediate load vector
\bm{\ell}_{t-1}=\sum_{s\geq t-1}\mathbf{x_{s}}
, on the arrival of the
t^{th}
online node
(N_{t},q_{t})
,
water-filling
selects the allocation
\mathbf{x}_{t}
which solves the following convex program:

\displaystyle\max\min_{i\in N_{t}}\left\{\mathbf{x}_{t}(i)+\bm{\ell}_{t-1}(i)\right\}\quad\quad\text{s.t.}\quad\mathbf{x}_{t}\in\Delta(N_{t},q_{t})

In
Figure

2
, we show the
water-filling
allocations on the example sequence from
Figure

1
.
We use
\textsc{WF}(N_{t},q_{t},\bm{\ell}_{t-1})
to denote the allocation of
water-filling
for the
t^{th}
resource, and
\textsc{WF}(E)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\Delta}}{{=}}\bm{\ell}_{m}
as the final allocation produced by
water-filling
on request sequence
E\in\mathcal{E}_{n,m,q}
.

Our results also rely on the following properties of
water-filling
, which are easy to verify:

Observation 1

(Properties of
water-filling
)
.

For any non-empty neighbor set
N\subseteq[n]
, quantity
q>0
, and load-vector
\bm{\ell}\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{n}
, the allocation
\mathbf{x}^{*}
selected by

satisfies:

•

Uniqueness:

\mathbf{x}^{*}+\bm{\ell}
is the unique majorization minimal vector in the feasible set
\{\mathbf{x}+\bm{\ell}\}_{\mathbf{x}\in\Delta(N,q)}
.

•

Equalized Loads:

\forall\,i,j
in support
\widehat{N}\coloneqq\{i\in N\mid\mathbf{x}^{*}(i)>0\}
, we have
\mathbf{x}^{*}(i)+\bm{\ell}(i)=\mathbf{x}^{*}(j)+\bm{\ell}(j)

3
Main Results

We now state our main results, establishing that the adversary’s best response is a nested sequence are a centerpiece of our analysis, as they induce majorization maximal
water-filling
allocations.

3.1
Minimax Optimality of
water-filling

We first prove that
water-filling
minimizes
\alpha
-regret compared to any other policy in a minimax sense for a wide range of settings.
This fact is a corollary of our following main theorem, which states that
water-filling
is “majorization minimal” in worst-case environments.

Theorem 1

(
water-filling
is Majorization Minimal)
.

Fix parameters
n,m\in\mathbb{N}
and
q\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
. Given any (possibly randomized) allocation policy
\mathcal{A}
and any request sequence
E\in\mathcal{E}_{n,m,q}
, there is a nested sequence
E^{\prime}\in\mathcal{E}^{\textsc{nest}}_{n,m,q}
for which
\textsc{WF}(E)\preceq\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{A}(E^{\prime})]
and
\textsc{OPT}(E)\succeq\textsc{OPT}(E^{\prime})
.

The expectation above is with respect to the randomness of
\mathcal{A}
.
This result follows immediately from composing the following two lemmas.
The first lemma provides a constructive procedure (
Algorithm

1
) that takes as input a request sequence
E
, and identifies a nested sequence with the same input parameters on which
water-filling
yields a majorizing allocation. This establishes that the adversary’s best response to the
water-filling
policy is always a nested request sequence.

Lemma 1

(Majorization Maximality of Nested Sequences)
.

Given any sequence
E\in\mathcal{E}_{n,m,q}
,
Algorithm

1
returns a nested sequence
\widetilde{E}\in\mathcal{E}^{\textsc{nest}}_{n,m,q}
satisfying
\textsc{WF}(E)\preceq\textsc{WF}(\widetilde{E})
and
\textsc{OPT}(E)\succeq\textsc{OPT}(\widetilde{E})
.

The second lemma shows that other allocation policies also perform poorly on nested request sequences.
We provide a constructive procedure (
Algorithm

2
) that takes as input an arbitrary policy
\mathcal{A}
and a nested sequence, and identifies a second nested sequence with equivalent hindsight optimal allocation; further, the
water-filling
allocation on the original sequence minorizes the
\mathcal{A}
allocation on the new sequence.
Thus, an algorithm designer that deviates from
water-filling
to an alternative policy performs worse against an adversary restricted to playing nested sequences.

Lemma 2

(Policy Deviation)
.

For any policy
\mathcal{A}
and nested sequence
\widetilde{E}\in\mathcal{E}^{\textsc{nest}}_{n,m,q}
, we can transform it (via
Algorithm

2
) into a nested sequence
E^{\prime}\in\mathcal{E}^{\textsc{nest}}_{n,m,q}
with
\textsc{WF}(\widetilde{E})\preceq\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{A}(E^{\prime})]
and
\textsc{OPT}(\widetilde{E})\sim\textsc{OPT}(E^{\prime})
.

For deterministic allocation policies,
Theorem

1
implies that
water-filling
,
simultaneously for every
\alpha

\alpha
-regret (and hence maximizes the competitive ratio) for the goal of
minimizing/maximizing any Schur-convex/Schur-concave function
.
Formally,
water-filling
is a minimax-optimal in the class of deterministic policies in any instantiation of our online allocation game.

(Deterministic Minimax Optimality of

.

Fix

(

In any nested sequence

At a high level, our transformation proceeds as follows:

Second, online nodes are permuted

Such a permutation of online nodes’ arrival order also turns out to preserve the

2
).
The key observation is that the

\widetilde{E}

4.2
Majorizing
water-filling
Allocations via Nested Sequences

We now formally define our procedure for transforming a given request sequence
E
into a nested sequence
\widetilde{E}
.

Input:

A request sequence
E\in\mathcal{E}_{n,m,q}
.

Output:

A nested sequence
\widetilde{E}\in\mathcal{E}^{\textsc{nest}}_{n,m,q}
with
\textsc{WF}(E)\preceq\textsc{WF}(\widetilde{E})
and
\textsc{WF}(E)\succeq\textsc{WF}(\widetilde{E})
.

/*
Step 1: Pruning Inactive Edges */

Identify inactive edges under the
water-filling
allocation
(\mathbf{x}_{t})_{t\in[m]}
:
I_{t}\leftarrow\{i\in N_{t}\mid\mathbf{x}_{t}(i)=0\}
;

Construct sequence
E^{(1)}\leftarrow((N_{t}^{(1)},q_{t}^{(1)}))_{t\in[m]}\in\mathcal{E}_{n,m,q}
with
N_{t}^{(1)}\leftarrow N_{t}\setminus I_{t}
and
q_{t}^{(1)}\leftarrow q_{t}
;

/*
Step 2: Permuting Online Nodes */

Identify permutation
\sigma
satisfying
h_{t}<h_{s}\Rightarrow\sigma(t)<\sigma(s)
, and
h_{t}=h_{s}
with
t<s\Rightarrow\sigma(s)<\sigma(t)
;

Construct sequence
E^{(2)}\leftarrow((N_{t}^{(2)},q_{t}^{(2)}))_{t\in[m]}\in\mathcal{E}_{n,m,q}
with
N_{t}^{(2)}\leftarrow N_{\sigma^{-1}(t)}^{(1)}
and
q_{t}^{(2)}\leftarrow q_{\sigma^{-1}(t)}^{(1)}
;

/*
Step 3: Nestification */

Construct
E^{(3)}\in\mathcal{E}^{\textsc{nest}}_{n,m,q}
as the nested sequence induced by
E^{(2)}
(see
Definition

5
);

return

\widetilde{E}\leftarrow E^{(3)}
;

ALGORITHM 1

Majorization-Dispersing Nested Sequence Transformation

The claim in
Lemma

1
that
\textsc{WF}(E)\preceq\textsc{WF}(\widetilde{E})
depends on establishing two invariance properties under our transformation, and then using our nestification result (
Lemma

3
). The first states that the
water-filling
allocation is preserved by the pruning step (while simultaneously ensuring the resulting sequence has no inactive edges).

Proposition 1

(Invariance under Inactive Edge Removal)
.

Consider any sequence

In

For any given policy

\forall\,i\in[n]

\forall\,t\in[m]

Appendix A
Adaptive Adversaries

In the main body of our work, we focused on an oblivious adversary that designs the entirety of the request sequence
E\in\mathcal{E}_{n,m,q}
without knowledge of the allocations produced by the algorithm designer’s policy
\mathcal{A}
.
Against such adversaries,
Corollary

1
and
Theorem

2
show there is an unavoidable separation between the classes of objections for which
water-filling
is minimax optimal that depends on whether or not the algorithm designer is allowed randomness.
This separation arises because a randomized policy can, with non-zero probability, guess an equitable allocation on any fixed request sequence.

In this section, we turn our attention to a stronger adaptive adversary who can dynamically design the request sequence depending on the play of the algorithm designer’s policy.
An adaptive adversary, when the algorithm designer deploys a deterministic policy such as
water-filling
, is no stronger than an oblivious one.
However, adaptive adversaries can prevent randomized policies from guessing equitable allocations.
As a result, there is no separation between the deterministic and randomized minimax guarantees of
water-filling
against adaptive adversaries.

A.1
Adaptive Adversary Formalization

We use a similar game setting to the one described in
Section

2.1
.
However, instead of being a static request sequence, the adversary’s policies are maps from a history of past requests (online nodes) and the algorithm designer’s allocations on those nodes to the next online node in the sequence.
Up to time
t
, the adversary generates online nodes
((N_{s},q_{s}))_{s\in[t-1]}
and the algorithm designer plays feasible allocations
(\mathbf{x})_{s\in[t-1]}
on those nodes.
On round
t
, an adaptive adversary, with knowledge of
((N_{s},q_{s}))_{s\in[t-1]}
and
(\mathbf{x})_{s\in[t-1]}
, designs the characteristics of
t
th online node
(N_{t},q_{t})
.
We overload notation and use
E
to indicate both static request sequences and a map designed by an adaptive adversary.
Similarly, the set of adaptive request sequences on which the final trajectory of requests has
n
offline nodes,
m
online nodes, and
q
total quantity overloads
\mathcal{E}_{n,m,q}
.

Similar to the oblivious setting, a game instance is defined by the number of offline nodes
n\in\mathbb{N}
, the number of online nodes
m\in\mathbb{N}
, the total quantity of all online nodes

, and a comparison factor

When the algorithm designer uses policy
\mathcal{A}

\displaystyle c_{\alpha,f}^{\max}(\mathcal{A},E)=\mathbb{E}[\alpha\cdot f(\textsc{OPT}(\mathcal{A},E))-f(\mathcal{A}(E))]

are disjoint sets, as a result of the nestedness of

\displaystyle\sum_{t\in[m]}\mathbf{x}_{t}(j)

\displaystyle\text{Sets }\{\Gamma_{i}(E)\setminus\Gamma_{i}(E)\}_{i\in[n]}\text{ are disjoint}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\in[j]}\left(\sum_{t\in\Gamma_{i}(E)\setminus\Gamma_{i-1}(E)}\frac{q_{t}}{n-i+1}\right)

Nestedness

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\in[j]}\frac{\mathbf{z}(i)}{n-i+1}

\displaystyle\text{Definition of }\mathbf{z}

The above is equivalent to
\textsc{WF}(E)=\sum_{t}\mathbf{x}_{t}H\mathbf{z}

For convenience, we henceforth define

\mathcal{I}_{n,q}=\{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}^{n}:\mathbf{x}([n])=q\text{ and }\mathbf{x}_{1}\leq\cdots\leq\mathbf{x}_{n}\}.

Notice that

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{k}\sum_{j=1}^{n}H(i,j)\mathbf{x}(j)

Competitive Ratio Characterizations

We analyze competitive ratios for
n
offline nodes.
The adversary is allowed to choose any number of online nodes
m
and any quantity
q
.
We characterized that the competitive ratio of policy
\mathcal{A}
for
f
maximization (resp.
g
minimization) is the largest (resp. smallest)
\alpha
such that
\textsc{Reg}_{(n,:,:),\alpha,f}^{\max}(\mathcal{A})\leq 0
(resp.
\textsc{Reg}_{(n,:,:),\alpha,g}^{\min}(\mathcal{A})\leq 0
).
By simple algebraic manipulation, it can be seen that the competitive ratio is equivalent to
\inf_{E\in\mathcal{E}_{n,:,:}}\frac{f(\mathcal{A}(E))}{f(\textsc{OPT}(E))}
(resp.
\sup_{E\in\mathcal{E}_{n,:,:}}\frac{f(\mathcal{A}(E))}{f(\textsc{OPT}(E))}
).
We abbreviate the competitive ratio as
\ratio{}_{n,f}^{\max}(\mathcal{A})
(resp.
\ratio{}_{n,g}^{\min}(\mathcal{A})
).
Further, we use
H_{n}=\sum_{i\in[n]}\frac{1}{i}
to denote the
i

H_{0}=0
.

C.1
Nash Social Welfare

Lemma 5

(
NSW
Competitive Ratio)
.

For any number of offline nodes
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
water-filling
achieves the minimax optimal competitive ratio for Nash Social Welfare
\textsc{NSW}(\bm{\ell})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\Delta}}{{=}}\left(\prod_{i}\bm{\ell}(i)\right)^{1/n}
maximization.
Further, this competitive ratio is given by:

\ratio{}_{n,\textsc{NSW}}^{\max}(\textsc{WF})=\left(\frac{1}{n!}\right)^{1/n}

In the limit, this competitive ratio satisfies
\lim_{n\to\infty}n\cdot\ratio_{n,\textsc{NSW}}^{\max}(\textsc{WF})=e
.

Proof.

Since
NSW
is concave and symmetric,
Corollaries

2
and
4
shows that
WF
achieves the minimax optimal competitive ratio bound for
NSW
maximization in comparison to any (even randomized) policy against an oblivious or adaptive adversary.
It remains to find a closed-form solution for this competitive ratio.
We generated the a proof of the closed-form solution in conversation with
19
;
See
Appendix

D
for more details.
We begin with the lower bound.

Corollary

3
shows:

\ratio{}_{n,\textsc{NSW}}^{\max}(\textsc{WF})

\displaystyle=\inf_{\bm{\ell}\in\mathcal{I}_{n,q}}\frac{\textsc{NSW}(H\bm{\ell})}{\textsc{NSW}(\bm{\ell})}

\displaystyle=\left(\inf_{\bm{\ell}\in\mathcal{I}_{n,q}}\prod_{i\in[n]}\left(\frac{\sum_{j\in[i]}\frac{\bm{\ell}(j)}{n-j+1}}{\bm{\ell}(i)}\right)\right)^{1/n}

\displaystyle\geq\left(\prod_{i\in[n]}\left(\frac{1}{n-i+1}\right)\right)^{1/n}

\displaystyle\left(\sum_{j\in[i]}\frac{\bm{\ell}(j)}{n-j+1}\geq\frac{\bm{\ell}(i)}{n-i+1}\right)

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{1}{n!}\right)^{1/n}

To prove an upper bound on the competitive ratio, we use a limiting argument on load vectors.
To simplify our arguments, we assume
q=1
since the competitive ratio of
NSW
is invariant to scaling of
\bm{\ell}
.
For a sufficiently small
\epsilon>0
, define load vector
\bm{\ell}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{I}_{n,q}
with
\bm{\ell}^{\prime}(i)=\epsilon^{n-i}
for
i\in[n-1]
and
\bm{\ell}^{\prime}(n)=1-\bm{\ell}^{\prime}([n-1])
.
Computing the competitive ratio as
\epsilon\to 0
provides an upper bound:

\ratio{}_{n,\textsc{NSW}}^{\max}(\textsc{WF})

\displaystyle\leq\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\frac{\textsc{NSW}(H\bm{\ell}^{\prime})}{\textsc{NSW}(\bm{\ell}^{\prime})}

\displaystyle=\left(\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\prod_{i\in[n]}\left(\frac{\sum_{j\in[i]}\frac{\bm{\ell}^{\prime}(j)}{n-j+1}}{\bm{\ell}^{\prime}(i)}\right)\right)^{1/n}

We argue that the limit of each term in the product exists, so we can take the product of each limiting term.
For
i\in[n-1]
, we have:

\displaystyle\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\frac{\left(\sum_{j\in[i]}\frac{\bm{\ell}^{\prime}(j)}{n-j+1}\right)}{\bm{\ell}^{\prime}(i)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j\in[i]}\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\frac{\epsilon^{n-j}}{(n-j+1)\epsilon^{n-i}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n-i+1}

The limit for
i=n
is:

\displaystyle\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\frac{\left(\sum_{j\in[n]}\frac{\bm{\ell}^{\prime}(j)}{n-j+1}\right)}{\bm{\ell}(n)}

\displaystyle=1+\sum_{j\in[n-1]}\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\frac{\epsilon^{n-j}}{(n-j+1)(1-\bm{\ell}^{\prime}([n-1]))}

Take out
n^{th}
term

\displaystyle=1

\displaystyle\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\bm{\ell}^{\prime}([n-1])=0

Since the limit of each product term exists, the limit of the product is the product of the limits:

\ratio{}_{n,\textsc{NSW}}^{\max}(\textsc{WF})

\displaystyle=\left(\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\prod_{i\in[n]}\left(\frac{\sum_{j\in[i]}\frac{\bm{\ell}^{\prime}(j)}{n-j+1}}{\bm{\ell}^{\prime}(i)}\right)\right)^{1/n}

\displaystyle=\left(\prod_{i\in[n]}\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\left(\frac{\sum_{j\in[i]}\frac{\bm{\ell}^{\prime}(j)}{n-j+1}}{\bm{\ell}^{\prime}(i)}\right)\right)^{1/n}

\displaystyle=\left(\prod_{i\in[n]}\frac{1}{n-i+1}\right)^{1/n}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{1}{n!}\right)^{1/n}

Stirling’s approximation for
n!
proves that this competitive ratio, scaled by
n
, converges to
e
.
∎

C.4
Fractional Matching

In the below proofs,
H_{n}=\sum_{i\in[n]}1/i
is the
n
th number in the Harmonic sequence.
We use
H_{0}=0
for convenience.

Definition 8

(Fractional Matching Sequence)
.

The fractional matching sequence is denoted as
M_{1},M_{2},\dots\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
with
M_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\Delta}}{{=}}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=0}^{n}\min(1,H_{n}-H_{i})
.

Lemma 8

(Fractional Matching Competitive Ratio)
.

For any number of offline nodes
n\in\mathbb{N}
and capacity
c\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
,
water-filling
achieves the minimax optimal competitive ratio for fractional matching
\textsc{FM}_{c}(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{i\in[n]}\min(c,\mathbf{x}(i))
maximization.
Further, the competitive ratio of
water-filling
for this objective is
\ratio{}_{n,\textsc{FM}_{c}}^{\max}=\min_{k\in[n]}M_{k}

Proof.

Corollary

2
proves that
water-filling
yields the minimax optimal competitive ratio for objective
\textsc{FM}_{c}
over all allocation policies
\mathcal{A}
, as this objective is symmetric and concave.
Without loss of generality, assume that
c=1
, as different capacities are equivalent to scaling inputs:
\textsc{FM}_{c}(\mathbf{x})=\textsc{FM}_{1}(c\cdot\mathbf{x})
.
We omit the subscript
c
for clarity for the remainder of the proof.

For an arbitrary hindsight load vector
\bm{\ell}\in\mathcal{I}_{n,:}
, we assume that
0<\bm{\ell}(1)\leq\dots\leq\bm{\ell}(n)=1
when trying to maximize the competitive ratio.
In the case that
\bm{\ell}(i)\geq 1
for some
i
, the alternate load vector
\bm{\ell}^{\prime}
with
\bm{\ell}^{\prime}(i)\leftarrow\min(1,\bm{\ell}(i))
yields
\textsc{FM}(\bm{\ell})=\textsc{FM}(\bm{\ell}^{\prime})
and
\textsc{FM}(H\bm{\ell})=\textsc{FM}(H\bm{\ell}^{\prime})
by definition; the competitive ratio is unchanged.
On the other hand, if
\bm{\ell}(n)<1
, then
\bm{\ell}^{\prime}\leftarrow\bm{\ell}/(\bm{\ell}(n))
gives
\frac{\textsc{FM}(\bm{\ell})}{\bm{\ell}(n)}=\textsc{FM}(\bm{\ell}^{\prime})
and
\frac{\textsc{FM}(H\bm{\ell})}{\bm{\ell}(n)}\geq\textsc{FM}(H\bm{\ell}^{\prime})
, which only decreases the competitive ratio as desired.

By allowing zero values
\bm{\ell}(i)=0
, we can also assume that
\bm{\ell}
takes the form
\bm{\ell}=(0,\dots,0,\beta,1,\dots,1)
for some
\beta
.
For any
\bm{\ell}
that does not satisfy the above, notice that the vector
\bm{\ell}^{\prime}
of the desired form with
\bm{\ell}([n])=\bm{\ell}^{\prime}([n])
satisfies
\textsc{FM}(\bm{\ell})=\textsc{FM}(\bm{\ell}^{\prime})
and
\bm{\ell}\preceq\bm{\ell}^{\prime}
.
Applying
Proposition

4
shows
H\bm{\ell}\preceq H\bm{\ell}^{\prime}
and, since
FM
is Schur-concave, this implies
\textsc{FM}(H\bm{\ell})\geq\textsc{FM}(H\bm{\ell}^{\prime})
.
Thus, the competitive ratio induced by
\bm{\ell}^{\prime}
is no more than that of
\bm{\ell}
.

Consider a vector of the form
\bm{\ell}=(0,\dots,0,\beta,1,\dots,1)
, where
\bm{\ell}(i)=\beta
and parameterize the competitive ratio of this vector in terms of
\beta\in[0,1]
.
We argue that it suffices to consider cases with
\beta\in\{0,1\}
.
The competitive ratio is:

\displaystyle\frac{\textsc{FM}(H\bm{\ell})}{\sum_{i}\bm{\ell}(i)}=\frac{\min\left(1,\beta/i\right)+\sum_{j>i}\min(1,\beta/i+H_{i-1})}{\beta+(n-i+1)}

If
i=n
, then the competitive ratio is
1
for any
\beta
.
Otherwise, the ratio is the quotient of a concave function in
\beta
and a linear function in
\beta
.
Such functions are always quasi-concave, meaning, the minimum occurs at
\beta\in\{0,1\}
.

Let
\bm{\ell}_{k}
be the vector with
\bm{\ell}_{k}(i)=\mathds{1}\left\{n-k+1\leq i\right\}
.
Via our previous arguments, the competitive ratio is minimized by such a vector.
Now, notice that:

\ratio{}_{n,\textsc{FM}}^{\max}(\textsc{WF})

\displaystyle=\min_{k\in[n]}\frac{\textsc{FM}(H\bm{\ell}_{k})}{\textsc{FM}(\bm{\ell}_{k})}

\displaystyle=\min_{k\in[n]}\frac{\sum_{i}\min\left(1,\sum_{j}H(i,j)\bm{\ell}_{k}(j)\right)}{\sum_{i}\bm{\ell}_{k}(i)}

\displaystyle=\min_{k\in[n]}\frac{1}{k}\sum_{j}\min\left(1,H_{k}-H_{j-1}\right)

\displaystyle=\min_{k\in[n]}M_{k}

∎

C.5
Separable Concave Functions

A separable concave function takes the form
f(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{i}{f}(\mathbf{x}(i))
for concave and non-decreasing
{f}:\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
.
We show that in the class of such functions, the matching objective provides worst-case competitive bounds.

Observation 7

(Piece-Wise Approximation)
.

Fix a finite set of points
X\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
with
r=|X|
and a function
{f}:\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
that is concave and non-decreasing.
There are constants
\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{r-1},\gamma\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
and capacities
c_{1},\dots,c_{r-1}\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
such that:

\displaystyle{f}(x)=\gamma x+\sum_{i\in[r-1]}\beta_{i}\min(c_{i},x),\quad\quad x\in X

Lemma 9

(Separable Concave Competitive Ratios)
.

Fix a separable concave function
f
.

water-filling
yields minimax optimal competitive ratios when maximizing
f
.
Further, the competitive ratio of water filling on
f
satisfies
\ratio{}_{n,f}^{\max}(\textsc{WF})\geq\ratio_{n,\textsc{FM}}^{\max}(\textsc{WF})
.

Proof.

For ease of notation, we write
\rho_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\Delta}}{{=}}\ratio_{n,\textsc{FM}}^{\max}(\textsc{WF})

By
Corollary

2
,
water-filling
is minimax optimal for separable concave maximization.
We bound the competitiveness of separable concave functions by showing that fractional matching objectives yield worst-case competitive ratios in this class.
Fix an arbitrary
\bm{\ell}\in\mathcal{I}_{n,:}
and write
\hat{\bm{\ell}}\leftarrow H\bm{\ell}
for ease of notation.
By definition, we have
\textsc{FM}_{c}(\hat{\bm{\ell}})\geq\rho_{n}\cdot\textsc{FM}_{c}(\bm{\ell})
for any
c>0
.
The set of values on which we evaluate
{f}
is
X\leftarrow\{\bm{\ell}(i),\hat{\bm{\ell}}(i)\}_{i}
.
Define
\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{2n-1},\gamma
and
c_{1},\dots,c_{2n-1}
to be the constants defined in
7
with respect to
{f}
.
We now bound the competitive ratio:

\displaystyle f(\hat{\bm{\ell}})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}{f}(\hat{\bm{\ell}}(i))

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}\left(\gamma\hat{\bm{\ell}}(i)+\sum_{j}\beta_{j}\min(c_{j},\hat{\bm{\ell}}(i))\right)

\displaystyle=\gamma\sum_{i}\hat{\bm{\ell}}(i)+\sum_{j}\beta_{j}\left(\sum_{i}\min(c_{j},\hat{\bm{\ell}}(i))\right)

\displaystyle\geq\gamma\sum_{i}\bm{\ell}(i)+\rho_{n}\sum_{j}\beta_{j}\left(\min(c_{j},\bm{\ell}(i))\right)

\displaystyle\hat{\bm{\ell}}([n])=\bm{\ell}([n]),\,\textsc{FM}_{c_{j}}(\hat{\bm{\ell}})\geq\rho_{n}\cdot\textsc{FM}_{c_{j}}(\bm{\ell})

\displaystyle\geq\rho_{n}\sum_{i}\left(\gamma\bm{\ell}(i)+\sum_{j}\beta_{j}\min(c_{j},\bm{\ell}(i))\right)

\displaystyle=\rho_{n}\sum_{i}{f}(\bm{\ell}(i))

\displaystyle=\rho_{n}f(\bm{\ell})

Rearranging the above inequality and taking the supremum on all
\bm{\ell}\in\mathcal{I}_{n,:}
gives:

\ratio{}_{n,f}(\textsc{WF})=\sup_{\bm{\ell}\in\mathcal{I}_{n,:}}\frac{f(H\bm{\ell})}{f(\bm{\ell})}\geq\rho_{n}

∎
