Abstract
. We consider the first eigenvalue of the magnetic Laplacian with zero magnetic field on simply connected compact surfaces and we establish isoperimetric inequalities and upper bounds in terms of a bound on the gaussian curvature. As a corollary, we prove that among all simply connected spherical domains of fixed area, the first eigenvalue is maximal for a geodesic disk with the pole of the magnetic potential at its center; also, for the sphere punctured at two points, the first eigenvalue is maximal when the punctures are antipodal.

1.
Introduction

In this paper we consider the first eigenvalue, or
ground state energy
, of the magnetic Laplacian on simply connected domains of the sphere and, more generally, on compact, simply connected, Riemannian surfaces.

Let
(M,g)
be a compact Riemannian manifold (of any dimension, with or without boundary). A
magnetic potential
is a smooth real
1
-form
A
on
M
. It defines a
magnetic differential

d^{A}
acting on smooth, complex-valued functions as follows:

d^{A}u=du-iuA,

and a
magnetic codifferential

\delta^{A}
, acting on
1
-forms
\omega
as
\delta^{A}(\omega)=\delta\omega+i\langle A,\omega\rangle_{g}
. Here
\delta
is the usual codifferential associated with the metric
g
, which in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
with the Euclidean metric is
-{\rm div}
. The
magnetic Laplacian
acts on smooth complex-valued functions as follows:

\Delta_{A}u=\delta^{A}d^{A}u.

When
\partial M\neq\emptyset
, we consider magnetic Neumann boundary conditions
d^{A}u(N)=0
, which in terms of the dual vector field
\nabla^{A}u:=\nabla u-iuA^{\sharp}
, read
\langle\nabla^{A}u,N\rangle=0
, where
N
is the unit outer normal to
\partial M
. Here
A^{\sharp}
denotes the vector field dual to the
1
-form
A
; the expression
\nabla^{A}u
denotes the
magnetic gradient
of
u
. Then
\Delta_{A}
defines a compact self-adjoint operator on
L^{2}(M,dv_{g})
, hence it has a non-negative spectrum made of eigenvalues of finite multiplicity diverging to
+\infty
:

0\leq\mu_{1}(M,g,A)\leq\mu_{2}(M,g,A)\leq\cdots\leq\mu_{k}(M,g,A)\leq\cdots\nearrow+\infty.

As usual, the eigenvalues are characterized by the min-max principle:

\mu_{k}(M,g,A)=\min_{{\begin{subarray}{c}U\subset H^{1}_{A}(M)\\
{\rm dim}U=k\end{subarray}}}\max_{0\neq u\in U}\frac{\int_{M}|d^{A}u|_{g}^{2}dv_{g}}{\int_{M}|u|^{2}dv_{g}},

where
H^{1}_{A}(M)
is the
magnetic Sobolev space
, namely, the space of complex-valued functions in
L^{2}(M,dv_{g})
with
d^{A}u
in
L^{2}(M,dv_{g})
. If
A
is smooth, this space coincides with the usual Sobolev space
H^{1}(M,dv_{g})
.
Here and in what follows we drop the dependence of the eigenvalue on the metric when it is clear from the context, and if
(M,g)
is a Riemannian manifold, we write simply

\mu_{k}(M,g,A)\doteq\mu_{k}(M,A).

Analogously, one can define the magnetic Dirichlet eigenvalues:

0<\lambda_{1}(M,g,A)\leq\lambda_{2}(M,g,A)\leq\cdots\leq\lambda_{k}(M,g,A)\leq\cdots\nearrow+\infty

which are characterized by

(1.1)

\lambda_{k}(M,g,A)=\min_{{\begin{subarray}{c}U\subset H^{1}_{0,A}(M)\\
{\rm dim}U=k\end{subarray}}}\max_{0\neq u\in U}\frac{\int_{M}|d^{A}u|_{g}^{2}dv_{g}}{\int_{M}|u|^{2}dv_{g}},

where
H^{1}_{0,A}(M)
is the subspace of
H^{1}_{A}(M)
of functions with zero boundary trace. Also in this case we set
\lambda_{k}(M,g,A)\doteq\lambda_{k}(M,A)
. The magnetic Dirichlet eigenvalues will appear in the proof of the main results.

1.1.
Gauge invariance.

A fundamental feature of the magnetic Laplacian is
gauge invariance
. Recall that the
flux
of a
1
-form
A
around a closed loop
c
in
M
is defined as
\frac{1}{2\pi}\oint_{c}A
. We have the following fundamental theorem which characterizes potentials that can be “
gauged-away
” (see
[
17
]
, see also
[
6
, Appendix 5]
).

Theorem 1.1
.

a)

Assume that
A,A^{\prime}
are such that
A-A^{\prime}
is closed and
\frac{1}{2\pi}\oint_{c}(A-A^{\prime})\in\mathbb{Z}
for any simple closed curve
c
in
M
. Then
\mu_{k}(M,A)=\mu_{k}(M,A^{\prime})
.

b)

One has
\mu_{1}(M,A)=0
if and only if
A
is closed and

\frac{1}{2\pi}\oint_{c}A\in\mathbb{Z}
for any simple closed curve
c
in
M
.

In particular, if the flux of
A
around any closed curve in
M
is an integer (in particular, if
A
is exact) then the magnetic spectrum coincides with the usual Laplacian spectrum for Neumann boundary conditions, which we simply denote
\mu_{k}(M)
: that is

\mu_{k}(M,A)=\mu_{k}(M)\quad\text{for all $k$}.

According to this numbering
\mu_{1}(M)=0
so that the first
positive
Neumann eigenvalue is then
\mu_{2}(M)
.

We remark that, classically, the
2
-form
B\doteq dA
is the
magnetic field
associated to
A
. We then remark that the magnetic field does not determine the spectrum; in particular, the magnetic field could be zero and yet the lowest eigenvalue (ground state energy) is positive; this happens when the flux of
A
around some curve is not an integer.

1.2.
Aharonov-Bohm potentials

The scope of the present paper is precisely to estimate the ground state energy when the magnetic field is zero on the regular part of singular potentials called
Aharonov-Bohm potentials
, which are the object of intensive work, both from the physical and the mathematical point of view.

On a simply connected domain
\Omega
, an Aharonov-Bohm potential with pole at
x_{0}\in\Omega
is a closed smooth
1
-form on
\Omega\setminus\{x_{0}\}
. The circulation of
A
around
\partial\Omega
(and around any loop enclosing
x_{0}
) is a real number
\nu
which is called the
flux
of
A
.

In
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, an example of Aharonov-Bohm potential with pole at the origin and flux
\nu
is

A^{(\nu)}_{0}=\nu\left(-\frac{y}{x^{2}+y^{2}}dx+\frac{x}{x^{2}+y^{2}}dy\right).

We consider magnetic Neumann boundary conditions, so that the spectrum is discrete (see e.g.,
[
8
]
). By what we said above
\mu_{1}(\Omega,A^{(\nu)}_{0})>0
if and only if
\nu\notin\mathbb{Z}
.

We remark that any two Aharonov-Bohm potentials with pole at
x_{0}
and flux
\nu
differ by an exact form, hence give rise to the same spectrum.

In this paper we are interested in shape optimization results for the first Aharonov-Bohm eigenvalue of compact, simply connected surfaces (with or without boundary).

1.3.
State of the art

Punctured domains in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
.
The following isoperimetric inequality has been proved in
[
8
]
: for any bounded, smooth domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
and any flux
\nu
we have

(1.2)

\mu_{1}(\Omega,A^{(\nu)}_{x_{0}})\leq\mu_{1}(D,A^{(\nu)}_{0})

for all
x_{0}\in\Omega
.
Here
D
is the disk centered at
0
with the same area:
\left|\Omega\right|=\left|D\right|
. Equality holds if and only if
\Omega=D
and
x_{0}=0
. In fact, the isoperimetric inequality (
1.2
) holds for any domain (not necessarily simply connected). The proof uses the classical argument of Weinberger for the second eigenvalue of the Neumann Laplacian
[
19
]
. Note that in polar coordinates
(r,\theta)
around
0
,
A^{(\nu)}_{0}=\nu d\theta
. The result holds also for bounded, smooth domains of the hyperbolic plane
\mathbb{H}^{2}
, with the same proof.

Spherical domains.
On the two-dimensional round sphere
\mathbb{S}^{2}
, fix a point
x_{0}
and consider polar coordinates
(r,\theta)
based at
x_{0}
, where
r
is the geodesic distance from
x_{0}
. Then
A_{x_{0}}^{(\nu)}\doteq\nu d\theta
defines a smooth
1
-form on
\mathbb{S}^{2}\setminus\{x_{0},-x_{0}\}
which is singular at the two poles:
x_{0}
and
-x_{0}
, and defines a Aharonov-Bohm potential on
\mathbb{S}^{2}
. We note that the flux around any loop that separates
x_{0}
,
-x_{0}
is equal to
\nu
. The following inequality of isoperimetric type is proved in
[
8
]
.

Theorem 1.2
.

Fix
x_{0}\in\mathbb{S}^{2}
and let
\Omega
be a smooth bounded domain that contains
x_{0}
but not
-x_{0}
. Let
D
be the spherical cap centered at
x_{0}
with the same area:
\left|\Omega\right|=\left|D\right|
. Then, the isoperimetric inequality

(1.3)

\mu_{1}(\Omega,A^{(\nu)}_{x_{0}})\leq\mu_{1}(D,A^{(\nu)}_{x_{0}})

holds for all
\nu\in\mathbb{R}
under any of the following assumptions:

a)

\Omega
is contained in the hemisphere centered at
x_{0}
;

b)

\Omega
is simply connected with area
\left|\Omega\right|\leq\frac{1}{2}\left|\mathbb{S}^{2}\right|=2\pi
.

If
\nu\notin\mathbb{Z}
, equality holds in a) and b) if and only if
\Omega
is the spherical cap
D
centered at
x_{0}
.

We remark that under any of the assumptions a) and b), the isoperimetric inequality is known to hold for the lowest positive eigenvalue of the usual Neumann Laplacian, that is:

(1.4)

\mu_{2}(\Omega)\leq\mu_{2}(D);

precisely, it holds under assumption a) by an argument of Weinberger (see
[
1
]
),
and it holds under assumption b) by conformal transplantation (see Bandle
[
2
]
and Szegö
[
18
]
). The proof of Theorem
1.2
is an adaptation of Weinberger’s and
Szegö’ s arguments.

We point out a recent growing interest in the eigenvalues of the Neumann Laplacian on spherical domains. In particular, we mention
[
12
]
where, in the inequality (
1.4
) for simply connected domains, the area bound is relaxed; precisely from
|\Omega|\leq 0.5|\mathbb{S}^{2}|
to
|\Omega|\leq\frac{16}{17}|\mathbb{S}^{2}|
: in other words, (
1.4
) holds for simply connected domains with area at most
94\%
that of the sphere. In the recent paper
[
4
]
the authors show that if the hypothesis on simply-connectedness is dropped, then spherical caps are not necessarily maximisers of the second Laplace eigenvalue among domains of fixed area.

The approach we propose in this paper for the study of isoperimetric inequalities and eigenvalue upper bounds is different from those of Szegö and Weinberger. In fact, among other results, we prove here that Theorem
1.2
, b) holds with no restrictions on
|\Omega|
. We remark that adapting the method to the case of the second Neumann eigenvalue of the standard Laplacian one can prove (
1.4
) for all simply connected spherical domains, with no restrictions on the area. This is done in
[
16
]
.

1.4.
Aims of the paper

Let us give an analytical description of an Aharonov-Bohm potential on a compact, simply connected Riemannian surface
\Omega
with non-empty boundary
\partial\Omega
and pole
z_{0}
. Consider, on the punctured domain
\Omega\setminus\{z_{0}\}
the smooth
1
-form

A_{z_{0}}^{(\nu)}\doteq-2\pi\nu\star d\psi_{z_{0}}\,,

where
\psi_{z_{0}}
is the Green function with pole at
z_{0}
, which is defined by

(1.5)

\begin{cases}\Delta\psi_{z_{0}}=\delta_{z_{0}}\,,&{\rm in\ }\Omega\\
\psi_{z_{0}}=0\,,&{\rm on\ }\partial\Omega,\end{cases}

and
\star
is the Hodge-star operator associated with the Riemannian metric. Note that
A_{z_{0}}^{(\nu)}
is closed and has flux
\nu
around
\partial\Omega
(and around any loop enclosing
z_{0}
).

On the other hand, if
\Sigma
is a compact, simply connected Riemannian surface with empty boundary, an Aharonov-Bohm potential is determined by the flux
\nu
and two poles
z_{1},z_{2}
. We set, on
\Sigma\setminus\{z_{1},z_{2}\}
:

A_{z_{1},z_{2}}^{(\nu)}\doteq-2\pi\nu\star d\psi_{z_{1},z_{2}}\,,

where
\psi_{z_{1},z_{2}}
is the Green function with poles at
z_{1},z_{2}\in\Sigma
, namely

(1.6)

\Delta\psi_{z_{1},z_{2}}=\delta_{z_{1}}-\delta_{z_{2}}\,,\ \ \ {\rm in\ }\Sigma.

In this case,
\nu
is the flux of
A^{(\nu)}_{z_{1},z_{2}}
around any loop separating the poles and
\delta_{P}
denotes the Dirac delta measure at a point
P
.

As we already mentioned, in the case of a geodesic disk in
\mathbb{S}^{2}
with pole
x_{0}
in its center,
A^{(\nu)}_{x_{0}}
takes the simpler form
A^{(\nu)}_{z_{0}}=\nu d\theta
, where
(r,\theta)
are the standard polar coordinates centered at
x_{0}
(with
r
being the distance from
x_{0}
). This is also the form of the Aharonov-Bohm potential on the standard sphere
\mathbb{S}^{2}
with antipodal poles
x_{0}
,
-x_{0}
.

In both cases the spectrum is well-behaved, and we are interested in isoperimetric inequalities and upper bounds for the first eigenvalue, which is positive as long as
\nu\notin\mathbb{Z}
.

The inequalities are obtained by employing the
method of prescribed level lines
which could be dated back to Rayleigh and Ritz (see e.g.,
[
14
]
), and by applying it to the Green function of
\Omega
or
\Sigma
as above. The same method, using test functions which are constant on the level lines of the
torsion
function, has been used in
[
7
]
to establish a reverse Faber-Krahn inequality for the first eigenvalue of the magnetic Laplacian with constant magnetic field on simply connected planar domains, in the weak field regime.

First, we consider the isoperimetric problem and prove that among all simply connected surfaces with boundary, having gaussian curvature bounded above by
1
and fixed area smaller than
|\mathbb{S}^{2}|=4\pi
, the first eigenvalue is maximal for a geodesic disk in
\mathbb{S}^{2}
with the pole of the potential at its center. In particular, among all simply connected spherical domains of fixed area, the geodesic disk with pole at the center is maximal. Note that we have no restriction on the area, extending in this way the result of
[
8
]
, in particular Theorem
1.2
, b).

Another natural question is the following. Consider all simply connected, closed (i.e. compact with empty boundary) Riemannian surfaces of fixed area punctured at two points:
does there exist a maximiser for the first Aharonov-Bohm eigenvalue?
For the second eigenvalue of the usual Laplacian the answer is positive: it is the round sphere
\mathbb{S}^{2}
, and the result is due to Hersch
[
10
]
, and can be conveniently stated in terms of the lowest normalized eigenvalue, which is invariant by homotheties:

\sup_{\Sigma}\left|\Sigma\right|\mu_{2}(\Sigma)=\left|\mathbb{S}^{2}\right|\mu_{2}(\mathbb{S}^{2})=8\pi.

A bit surprisingly, in the magnetic case there is no maximiser for
\left|\Sigma\right|\mu_{1}(\Sigma,A^{(\nu)}_{z_{1},z_{2}})
; in fact, if
\nu\notin\mathbb{Z}
:

\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}\Sigma\end{subarray},\,{z_{1},z_{2}\in\Sigma}}\left|\Sigma\right|\mu_{1}(\Sigma,A^{(\nu)}_{z_{1},z_{2}})=+\infty,

see Theorem
4.5
. The point is that when the flux is not an integer, the first eigenvalue depends on the positions of the poles. When the flux is an integer, the position of the poles have no influence on the spectrum, by gauge invariance. In this paper we prove an upper bound which depends only on an upper bound on the gaussian curvature
K
, see Theorem
2.4
. The dependence on
K
is sharp.

If the surface is
\mathbb{S}^{2}
punctured at two poles, a natural question is then:
what is the optimal position of the poles, for which the first eigenvalue is maximal?
We show in Corollary
2.5
that the two poles must be antipodal (in the appendix we compute explicitly the spectrum in that case).

Even if the focus of the paper is on simply connected surfaces, with or without boundary, we study here also the case of Riemannian annuli (surfaces diffeomorphic to
\mathbb{S}^{1}\times[0,1]
). Then we are able to prove that the first Aharonov-Bohm eigenvalue is maximal for the unique flat cylinder in a given conformal class.

1.5.
Organization of the paper

The paper is organized as follows: in Section
2
we state the main results, namely Theorem
2.1
(isoperimetric inequality) and
2.4
(sharp upper bound) and their consequences on spherical domains, namely Corollaries
2.2
and
2.5
. In Section
3
we prove Theorem
2.1
. In Section
4
we prove Theorem
2.4
and discuss its sharpness. We have included two appendices. In Appendix
A
we compute explicitly the magnetic spectrum of the sphere with opposite poles and discuss other properties of eigenvalues on spherical domains. In Appendix
B
we discuss the case of Riemannian annuli.

2.
Main results

2.1.
Isoperimetric inequality for simply connected surfaces with boundary

For a constant
K>0
, we let

\mathcal{M}_{K}
denote the family of compact simply connected Riemannian surfaces with smooth boundary and gaussian curvature bounded above by
K
. When
\left|\Omega\right|\leq\left|\mathbb{S}^{2}_{K}\right|
the model domain for our eigenvalue problem will be a spherical cap in
\mathbb{S}^{2}_{K}
with same area (eventually, the whole sphere when
\left|\Omega\right|=\left|\mathbb{S}^{2}_{K}\right|
). We get in that case:

Theorem 2.1
.

Let
\Omega\in\mathcal{M}_{K}
, fix a pole
z_{0}\in\Omega
and let
A_{z_{0}}^{(\nu)}
be an Aharonov-Bohm potential with arbitrary flux
\nu\in\mathbb{R}
and pole
z_{0}
. Assume that

\left|\Omega\right|\leq\left|\mathbb{S}^{2}_{K}\right|
. Then

\mu_{1}(\Omega,A_{z_{0}}^{(\nu)})\leq\mu_{1}(D^{\star},A_{x_{0}}^{(\nu)}),

where
D^{\star}
is the spherical cap in
\mathbb{S}^{2}_{K}
centered at some
x_{0}\in\mathbb{S}^{2}_{K}
with area
\left|\Omega\right|
. Equality holds if and only if
\Omega=D^{\star}
and
z_{0}=x_{0}
.

The following is an immediate consequence:

Corollary 2.2
.

Let
\Omega
be a simply connected domain in
\mathbb{S}^{2}
, and choose any
z_{0}\in\Omega
. Then, for any flux
\nu\in\mathbb{R}
we have:

\mu_{1}(\Omega,A_{z_{0}}^{(\nu)})\leq\mu_{1}(D^{\star},A_{x_{0}}^{(\nu)}),

where
D^{\star}
is the spherical cap in
\mathbb{S}^{2}
centered at some
x_{0}\in\mathbb{S}^{2}
with area
\left|\Omega\right|
. Equality holds if and only if
\Omega=D^{\star}
and
z_{0}=x_{0}
.

This inequality was first proved with the restriction
|\Omega|\leq 2\pi
in
[
8
]
.

When the domain
\Omega\in\mathcal{M}_{K}
has area greater than that of
\mathbb{S}^{2}_{K}
the model domain will be the whole sphere, in the following sense.

Theorem 2.3
.

Let
\Omega\in{\mathcal{M}}_{K}
, let
z_{0}\in\Sigma
and let
A_{z_{0}}^{(\nu)}
be an Aharonov-Bohm potential with flux
\nu\in(0,\frac{1}{2}]
and pole
z_{0}
. Assume that
\left|\Omega\right|\geq\left|\mathbb{S}^{2}_{K}\right|
. Then we have:

(2.1)

\displaystyle\mu_{1}(\Sigma,A_{z_{0}}^{(\nu)})

\displaystyle\leq K\nu(\nu+1)

\displaystyle=\mu_{1}(\mathbb{S}^{2}_{K},A_{x_{0}}^{(\nu)})

where
A_{x_{0}}^{(\nu)}
is the Aharonov-Bohm potential on
{\mathbb{S}}^{2}_{K}
with antipodal poles
x_{0}
,
-x_{0}
. Equality holds if and only if
\Omega=\mathbb{S}^{2}_{K}\setminus\{-x_{0}\}
with pole
x_{0}
. If
\nu\in\mathbb{R}
, the inequality holds replacing
\nu
by
\min_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\left|\nu-n\right|
.

2.2.
Upper bounds for simply connected surfaces

For a constant
K>0
, we let

\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{K}
denote the family of closed simply connected Riemannian surfaces with gaussian curvature bounded above by
K
. The model surface is here

\mathbb{S}^{2}_{K}
with antipodal poles. Here is the relevant result.

Theorem 2.4
.

Let
\Sigma\in\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{K}
, let
z_{1},z_{2}\in\Sigma
and let
A_{z_{1},z_{2}}^{(\nu)}
be an Aharonov-Bohm potential with flux
\nu
and poles
z_{1},z_{2}
. Then, for any flux
\nu\in(0,\frac{1}{2}]
we have:

(2.2)

\displaystyle\mu_{1}(\Sigma,A_{z_{1},z_{2}}^{(\nu)})

\displaystyle\leq K\nu(\nu+1)

\displaystyle=\mu_{1}(\mathbb{S}^{2}_{K},A_{x_{0}}^{(\nu)})

where
A_{x_{0}}^{(\nu)}
is the Aharonov-Bohm potential on
{\mathbb{S}}^{2}_{K}
with antipodal poles
x_{0},-x_{0}
. Equality holds if and only if
\Sigma=\mathbb{S}^{2}_{K}
and
z_{1},z_{2}
are antipodal. If
\nu\in\mathbb{R}
, the inequality holds replacing
\nu
by
\min_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\left|\nu-n\right|
.

As an immediate consequence, we take
\Sigma=\mathbb{S}^{2}
and observe the following corollary on the optimal placement of two poles on a round sphere.

Corollary 2.5
.

The following inequality holds for any pair of poles
x_{0},y\in\mathbb{S}^{2}
and any flux
\nu\in(0,\frac{1}{2}]
:

\displaystyle\mu_{1}(\mathbb{S}^{2},A^{(\nu)}_{x_{0},y})

\displaystyle\leq\nu(\nu+1)

\displaystyle=\mu_{1}(\mathbb{S}^{2},A^{(\nu)}_{x_{0}})

where
A_{x_{0}}^{(\nu)}
is the Aharonov-Bohm potential on
{\mathbb{S}}^{2}_{K}
with antipodal poles
x_{0},-x_{0}
. Equality holds if and only if
y=-x_{0}
.

2.3.
Riemannian annuli

Finally, we briefly consider the case of a Riemannian annulus
\mathcal{C}
, namely, a compact surface diffeomorphic to
\mathbb{S}^{1}\times[0,1]
endowed with a Riemannian metric. Let
A^{(\nu)}
be a smooth closed
1
-form on
\Sigma
with flux
\nu\in\mathbb{R}
around any of the boundary circles. For example, in cylindrical coordinates
(\theta,z)
, one can take
A^{(\nu)}=\nu d\theta
. We consider the magnetic Laplacian
\Delta_{A^{(\nu)}}
. Then the Neumann spectrum is discrete, and the first eigenvalue is positive as long as
\nu\notin\mathbb{Z}
and one has by the min-max principle

\mu_{1}(\mathcal{C},A^{(\nu)})=\min_{0\neq u\in H^{1}(\mathcal{C})}\frac{\int_{\mathcal{C}}|d^{A}u|^{2}}{\int_{\mathcal{C}}|u|^{2}}.

Since any two closed
1
-forms with the same flux on
\mathcal{C}
differ by an exact
1
-form, we see by gauge invariance that the magnetic spectrum depends only on
\nu
. Hence we set

\mu_{1}(\mathcal{C},\nu)\doteq\mu_{1}(\mathcal{C},A^{(\nu)}).

Now, any Riemannian annulus
\mathcal{C}
is conformally equivalent to the flat cylinder
\mathcal{C}_{M}\doteq\mathbb{S}^{1}\times[-M,M]
for a unique
M>0
; the number
M
is the so-called
conformal modulus
of
\mathcal{C}
. We refer e.g., to
[
15
]
where the above study is carried out for the magnetic Steklov problem. Here is the relevant result:

Theorem 2.6
.

|\mathcal{C}|\mu_{1}(\mathcal{C},\nu)\leq 4\pi M\min_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}|\nu-n|^{2}

where
M
it the conformal modulus of
\mathcal{C}
. Equality holds if and only if
\mathcal{C}
is homothetic to
\mathcal{C}_{M}
.

Note that the upper bound depends on the minimum distance of
\nu
from the set of integer numbers. In particular, we can take
\nu\in(0,\frac{1}{2}]
so that the upper bound reads

|\mathcal{C}|\mu_{1}(\mathcal{C},\nu)\leq 4\pi M\nu^{2}.

With the same method as in
[
9
]
, we have that the inequality is sharp asymptotically as
\nu\to 0
, because:

\lim_{\nu\to 0}\frac{|\mathcal{C}|\mu_{1}(\mathcal{C},\nu)}{\nu^{2}}=4\pi M.

In other words, knowing the normalised eigenvalue
|\mathcal{C}|\mu_{1}(\mathcal{C},\frac{1}{n})
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
, determines the conformal class of
\mathcal{C}
. In
[
9
]
this phenomenon is studied for closed, orientable surfaces of arbitrary genus
g
: there it is proved that the knowledge of a suitable countable collection of magnetic ground states determines the volume and the conformal class of the surface.

3.
Proof of Theorem
2.1

The proof relies on the so-called
prescribed level sets method
, which consists in using test functions for the Rayleigh quotient that are constant on the level lines of the Green function of
\Omega
with pole
z_{0}
:
\psi_{z_{0}}
. The problem is then reduced to the study of the monotonicity of the first eigenvalue of a one-dimensional eigenvalue problem. The strategy is similar to that employed in
[
7
]
. We remark that from the scaling properties of the eigenvalues, it is sufficient to prove Theorem
2.1
for
\Omega\in\mathcal{M}_{1}
, that is, when the gaussian curvature is bounded above by
1
. Also, by gauge invariance, it is not restrictive to assume
\nu\in\left(0,\frac{1}{2}\right]
. Through all the proof we will also set

M\doteq|\Omega|=|D^{\star}|.

3.1.
Reduction to a one dimensional problem

Let
\nu\in(0,\frac{1}{2}]
and let
z_{0}\in\Omega
be fixed. Let
\psi_{z_{0}}
be the Green function in
\Omega
with pole
z_{0}
defined in (
1.5
). Recall that

A_{z_{0}}^{(\nu)}=-2\pi\nu\star d\psi_{z_{0}}.

Let
D^{\star}
be a spherical cap in
\mathbb{S}^{2}
with
|D^{\star}|=|\Omega|=M<4\pi
, and let
x_{0}
be its center. By
\psi_{x_{0}}
we denote the Green function of
D^{\star}
with pole
x_{0}
and by
A_{x_{0}}^{(\nu)}
the potential in
D^{\star}
defined by
A_{x_{0}}^{(\nu)}=-2\pi\nu\star d\psi_{x_{0}}
. We have
A^{(\nu)}_{x_{0}}=\nu d\theta
. Since
\nu
,
\Omega,D^{\star}
,
z_{0}
and
x_{0}
are fixed, to simplify the notation we shall write, through all the proof

A\doteq A_{z_{0}}^{(\nu)}\,,\ \ \ A^{\star}\doteq A_{x_{0}}^{(\nu)}\,,\ \ \ \psi\doteq\psi_{z_{0}}\,,\ \ \ \psi^{\star}\doteq\psi_{x_{0}}.

It is well-known that
\psi,\psi^{\star}
have no critical points. Consider now the function space

\mathcal{R}(\Omega)\doteq\{u:u=g\circ\psi\,,\ g\in H^{1}(0,\infty)\}\subset H^{1}_{A}.

The space
\mathcal{R}(\Omega)
consists of all functions in
H^{1}_{A}(\Omega)
that are constant on the level lines of
\psi
. We set

(3.1)

\kappa_{1}(\Omega,A)\doteq\min_{0\neq u\in\mathcal{R}(\Omega)}\frac{\int_{\Omega}|d^{A}u|^{2}}{\int_{\Omega}|u|^{2}}.

From the min-max principle we have

(3.2)

\mu_{1}(\Omega,A)\leq\kappa_{1}(\Omega,A).

Now, if
D^{\star}
is a disk in
\mathbb{S}^{2}
of radius
R
and the pole of the magnetic potential coincides with its center, then the first eigenfunction is real and radial (see e.g.,
[
8
, Appendix B.1]
), hence

(3.3)

\mu_{1}(D^{\star},A^{\star})=\kappa_{1}(D^{\star},A^{\star}).

In order to complete the proof of Theorem
2.1
, it is enough to prove

(3.4)

\kappa_{1}(\Omega,A)\leq\kappa_{1}(D^{\star},A^{\star})

and show that the equality holds only if
\Omega=D^{\star}
and
A=A^{\star}
.

To do so, we characterize
\kappa_{1}(\Omega,A)
as the minimizer of a
1
-dimensional Rayleigh quotient associated with a Sturm-Liouville problem. Define a function
G_{z_{0}}:(0,M)\to(0,\infty)
by

(3.5)

G_{z_{0}}(a)\doteq\int_{\psi_{z_{0}}=\beta_{z_{0}}(a)}\frac{1}{|d\psi_{z_{0}}|},

where
\beta_{z_{0}}(a)
is defined by
|\{\psi_{z_{0}}>\beta_{z_{0}}(a)\}|=a
. Analogously we define the function
G_{x_{0}}(a)
(and
\beta_{x_{0}}(a)
) using the Green function
\psi_{x_{0}}
of
D^{\star}
with
x_{0}
the center of
D^{\star}
.

To keep the same notation as above, we will just write, for the rest of the proof

G\doteq G_{z_{0}}\,,\ \ \ G^{\star}\doteq G_{x_{0}}.

We recall that
G(a)\sim 4\pi a
as
a\to 0
. This follows from the fact that
\psi\sim-\frac{1}{2\pi}\log r
as
r\to 0
, where
r
is the geodesic distance from
z_{0}
, and that
\psi+\frac{1}{2\pi}\log r
is a smooth function near
z_{0}
. The same holds true for
G^{\star}
. However, for
G^{\star}
we have an explicit expression:

G^{\star}(a)=a(4\pi-a),

which follows from the
geometric isoperimetric inequality
(
3.9
), valid for spherical domains (see (
3.10
)).

Lemma 3.1
.

We have that

(3.6)

\kappa_{1}(\Omega,A)=\min_{0\neq f\in\mathcal{F}}\frac{\int_{0}^{M}\left(G(a)f^{\prime}(a)^{2}+\frac{4\pi^{2}\nu^{2}}{G(a)}f(a)^{2}\right)da}{\int_{0}^{M}f(a)^{2}da}

where
\mathcal{F}=\{f\in L^{2}(0,M):\sqrt{G}f^{\prime},f/\sqrt{G}\in L^{2}(0,M)\}
. In particular,
\kappa_{1}(\Omega,A)
is the first eigenvalue of the following Sturm-Liouville problem in
(0,M)
:

(3.7)

\begin{cases}-(Gf^{\prime})^{\prime}+\frac{4\pi^{2}\nu^{2}}{G}f=\kappa f\,,&{\rm in\ }(0,M)\,,\\
\lim_{a\to 0}G(a)f^{\prime}(a)=f^{\prime}(M)=0.\end{cases}

The same statements hold for
\kappa_{1}(D^{\star},A^{\star})
, replacing
G
with
G^{\star}
.

Proof.

Let
u
be real-valued and of the form
u=g\circ\psi
. Then, since
|A|^{2}=4\pi^{2}\nu^{2}|d\psi|^{2}
:

|d^{A}u|^{2}=(g^{\prime}\circ\psi)^{2}|d\psi|^{2}+(g\circ\psi)^{2}|A|^{2}=\left((g^{\prime}\circ\psi)^{2}+4\pi^{2}\nu^{2}(g\circ\psi)^{2}\right)|d\psi|^{2}

hence by the coarea formula we get

\int_{\Omega}|d^{A}u|^{2}=\int_{0}^{\infty}\left(g^{\prime}(t)^{2}+4\pi^{2}\nu^{2}g(t)^{2}\right)\int_{\psi=t}|d\psi|\,dt=\int_{0}^{\infty}\left(g^{\prime}(t)^{2}+4\pi^{2}\nu^{2}g(t)^{2}\right)dt.

The last identity follows since

\int_{\psi=t}|d\psi|=\int_{\psi=t}\partial_{N}\psi=\int_{\{\psi>t\}}\Delta\psi=1,

where
N
is the inward unit normal to the set
\{\psi>t\}
. Now, let

\alpha(t)\doteq|\{\psi>t\}|=\int_{t}^{\infty}\int_{\psi=s}\frac{1}{|d\psi|}\,dt

so that

\alpha^{\prime}(t)=-\int_{\psi=t}\frac{1}{|d\psi|}.

Recall that
\psi
has no critical points in
\overline{\Omega}\setminus\{z_{0}\}
, hence
\alpha:(0,\infty)\to(0,M)
is smooth, strictly decreasing, and admits a smooth inverse
\beta:(0,M)\to(0,\infty)
. We change variable and set

t=\beta(a)\,,\ \ \ f=g\circ\beta,

hence
g(t)=f(a)
. We have that

\beta^{\prime}(a)=-\frac{1}{G(a)}

where we recall that
G(a)=\int_{\psi=\beta(a)}\frac{1}{|d\psi|}
. We conclude that

\int_{\Omega}|d^{A}u|^{2}=\int_{0}^{\infty}\left(g^{\prime}(t)^{2}+4\pi^{2}\nu^{2}g(t)^{2}\right)dt=\int_{0}^{M}\left(G(a)f^{\prime}(a)^{2}+\frac{4\pi^{2}\nu^{2}}{G(a)}f(a)^{2}\right)da.

Analogously, we have that

\int_{\Omega}u^{2}=\int_{0}^{\infty}g(t)^{2}\int_{\psi=t}\frac{1}{|d\psi|^{2}}\,dt=\int_{0}^{M}f(a)^{2}da.

This proves (
3.6
). Now, from (
3.6
) it is standard to check that
\kappa_{1}(\Omega,A)
is the first eigenvalue of the Sturm-Liouville problem (
3.7
).
∎

3.2.
Monotonicity of the function
G

We prove now that
G\geq G^{\star}
and discuss the equality case.

Lemma 3.2
.

One has on
(0,M)
:

G\geq G^{\star}

with equality if and only if
\Omega=D^{\star}
and
A=A^{\star}
.

Proof.

For any
t\in(0,\infty)
we have, by Cauchy-Schwarz inequality

(3.8)

|\{\psi=t\}|=\int_{\psi=t}|d\psi|^{1/2}|d\psi|^{-1/2}\leq\left(\int_{\psi=t}|d\psi|\right)^{1/2}\left(\int_{\psi=t}\frac{1}{|d\psi|}\right)^{1/2}\\
=\left(\int_{\psi=t}\frac{1}{|d\psi|}\right)^{1/2}

with equality if and only if
|d\psi|
is constant on
\{\psi=t\}
. Changing variables, and passing to the variable
a=\alpha(t)=|\{\psi>t\}|
, we have that (
3.8
) reads

|\{\psi=\beta(a)\}|^{2}\leq G(a),

where
\beta
is the inverse of
\alpha
. Now, if
\Omega
is a simply connected Riemannian surface with boundary of area
\mathcal{A}
, boundary length
\ell
, and gaussian curvature bounded above by
1
, we have the following isoperimetric inequality:

(3.9)

\ell^{2}\geq\mathcal{A}(4\pi-\mathcal{A}),

with equality if and only if
\Omega
is a disk of constant curvature
1
. We refer e.g., to
[
5
]
for a proof. Therefore we conclude that

(3.10)

G(a)\geq a(4\pi-a)=G^{\star}(a),

with equality if and only if each level set
\psi=t
is a circle and
|d\psi|
is constant on
\psi=t
. This last condition imposes that
\psi
must be a radial function, hence
\Omega=D^{\star}
and
A=A^{\star}
.
∎

3.3.
Optimization of a 1-d problem

Next we consider the following number

\kappa_{1}(G)\doteq\min_{0\neq f\in\mathcal{F}}\frac{\int_{0}^{M}\left(G(a)f^{\prime}(a)^{2}+\frac{4\pi^{2}\nu^{2}}{G(a)}f(a)^{2}\right)da}{\int_{0}^{M}f^{2}(a)da},

where
\mathcal{F}=\{f\in L^{2}(0,M):\sqrt{G}f^{\prime},f/\sqrt{G}\in L^{2}(0,M)\}
and
G:(0,M)\to(0,\infty)
is any smooth, positive function satisfying

(3.11)

G(a)\sim 4\pi a\,,\ \ \ a\to 0.

Note that
\kappa_{1}(\Omega,A^{(\nu)}_{z_{0}})=\kappa_{1}(G_{z_{0}})
where
G_{z_{0}}
has been defined in (
3.5
). Recall also that
G^{\star}=a(4\pi-a)
. We want to optimize
\kappa_{1}(G)
with respect to the weight
G
. In particular we have the following lemma.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
0<G_{1}\leq G_{2}
on
(0,M)
, both satisfying (
3.11
). Then

\kappa_{1}(G_{2})\leq\kappa_{1}(G_{1}).

If
G_{1}<G_{2}
on a set of positive measure, the inequality is strict.

Proof.

For
t\in[0,1]
consider

G_{t}:=(1-t)G_{1}+tG_{2}.

We compute the derivative of
\kappa_{1}(G_{t})
with respect to
t
. Since the eigenvalue is simple, we can apply Feynman-Hellmann formula
[
11
, VII-§4, p.408, n. 4.56]
to get

\frac{d}{dt}\kappa_{1}(G_{t})=\int_{0}^{M}\left(G_{t}(a)^{2}f_{t}^{\prime}(a)^{2}-4\pi^{2}\nu^{2}f_{t}(a)^{2}\right)\frac{G_{2}(a)-G_{1}(a)}{G_{t}(a)^{2}}\,da

where
f_{t}
is the first positive
L^{2}
-normalized eigenfunction corresponding to the eigenvalue
\kappa_{1}(G_{t})
. In order to conclude we have to prove that

G_{t}^{2}f_{t}^{\prime 2}-4\pi^{2}\nu^{2}f_{t}^{2}<0.

Set

R=\frac{G_{t}f_{t}^{\prime}}{f_{t}}.

We see that
R
is smooth in
(0,M)
, and moreover, from the Neumann condition at
M
, we have that
R(M)=0
. We have to show that

|R(a)|<2\pi\nu\ \ \ \forall a\in(0,M).

First step.
Assume by contradiction that there exists
a_{0}\in(0,M)
such that
R(a_{0})\geq 2\pi\nu
. Differentiating
R
we get

(3.12)

R^{\prime}=\frac{4\pi^{2}\nu^{2}-R^{2}}{G_{t}}-\kappa_{1}(G_{t}).

In particular
R^{\prime}(a_{0})<0
, and this implies, along with (
3.12
), that
R
is decreasing (and positive) in the whole interval
(0,a_{0})
. Hence limit
\lim_{a\rightarrow 0^{+}}R(a)
exists, so we have two cases

Case
1
:
\lim_{a\rightarrow 0^{+}}R(a)
is finite. In particular then,
\lim_{a\rightarrow 0^{+}}R(a)>2\pi\nu
. We integrate (
3.12
) and we obtain

R(a_{0})-R(a)=\int_{a}^{a_{0}}R^{\prime}(s)ds=\int_{a}^{a_{0}}\frac{4\pi^{2}\nu^{2}-R^{2}}{G_{t}}-\kappa_{1}(G_{t})ds.

But now we recall that
G_{t}(a)\sim 4\pi a
when
a\rightarrow 0^{+}
, hence, since
\lim_{a\rightarrow 0^{+}}R(a)>2\pi\nu
, we get that the right-hand side of the above equation is unbounded (tends to
-\infty
) as
a\to 0^{+}
, while the left-hand side remains bounded.

Case
2
:
\lim_{a\rightarrow 0^{+}}R(a)=+\infty
. From (
3.12
), recalling that
G_{t}(a)\sim 4\pi a
as
a\to 0^{+}
, we obtain

\lim_{a\rightarrow 0^{+}}\frac{aR^{\prime}}{R^{2}}=-\frac{1}{4\pi}.

Therefore, there exists
\bar{a}>0
and
\epsilon>0
such that

\frac{1}{a}\left(-\frac{1}{4\pi}-\epsilon\right)\leq\left(-\frac{1}{R}\right)^{\prime}\leq\frac{1}{a}\left(-\frac{1}{4\pi}+\epsilon\right)

on
(0,\bar{a})
. We can integrate both sides in
(a,\bar{a})
, and we obtain

\left(-\frac{1}{4\pi}-\epsilon\right)\ln({\frac{\bar{a}}{a}})\leq\frac{1}{R(a)}-\frac{1}{R(\bar{a})}\leq\left(-\frac{1}{4\pi}+\epsilon\right)\ln({\frac{\bar{a}}{a}})

In particular from the above inequality we can conclude that
\lim_{a\rightarrow 0^{+}}R(a)=0
which is a contradiction.

We conclude that there is no
a_{0}\in(0,M)
such that
R(a_{0})\geq 2\pi\nu
.

Second step.
It remains to consider the case in which there exists
a_{0}\in(0,M)
such that
R(a_{0})\leq-2\pi\nu
. This implies, by (
3.12
), that
R^{\prime}<0
on
(a_{0},M)
, and hence
R(M)<-2\pi\nu
, which is a contradiction with
R(M)=0
.
∎

3.4.
Conclusion of the proof

The proof of Theorem
2.1
now follows from (
3.2
), (
3.3
) and Lemmas
3.2
and
3.3
:

\displaystyle\mu_{1}(\Omega,A)

\displaystyle\leq\kappa_{1}(\Omega,A)\ \ \ {\rm by\ \eqref{ineq1}}

\displaystyle\leq\kappa_{1}(D^{\star},A^{\star})\ \ \ {\rm by\ Lemmas\ \ref{lem:isoper}\ and\ \ref{lem:optimsturmmliouville}}

\displaystyle=\mu_{1}(D^{\star},A^{\star})\ \ \ {\rm by\ \eqref{eq1}}.

If equality holds everywhere, then
\kappa_{1}(\Omega,A)=\kappa_{1}(D^{\star},A^{\star})
which implies from Lemmas
3.2
and
3.3
that
\Omega=D^{\star}
and
A=A^{\star}
.

4.
Proof of Theorems
2.4
and
2.3

The proof is similar to that of Theorem
2.1
. However, note that Theorems
2.4
and
2.3
give an upper bound which is not isoperimetric. In particular, in the case of a closed surface
\Sigma
, the bound on the gaussian curvature implies that
|\Sigma|\geq|\mathbb{S}^{2}_{K}|
, and if
\Sigma\neq\mathbb{S}^{2}_{K}
, the inequality is strict. As in Section
3
, we may suppose that
K=1
. We will prove Theorem
2.4
first.

4.1.
Proof of Theorem
2.4
: reduction to a 1-d problem

By gauge invariance, we may always assume
\nu\in(0,\frac{1}{2}]
. Let
\psi_{z_{1},z_{2}}
be the Green function of
\Sigma
with poles
z_{1},z_{2}
(defined in (
1.6
)) and
A^{(\nu)}_{z_{1},z_{2}}=-2\pi\nu\star d\psi_{z_{1},z_{2}}
. We also set

M\doteq|\Sigma|

and define

(4.1)

G_{z_{1},z_{2}}(a)\doteq\int_{\psi_{z_{1},z_{2}}=\beta_{z_{1},z_{2}}(a)}\frac{1}{|d\psi_{z_{1},z_{2}}|}

where
\beta_{z_{1},z_{2}}(a)
is defined by
|\{\psi_{z_{1},z_{2}}>\beta_{z_{1},z_{2}}(a)\}|=a
. The behavior of
G_{z_{1},z_{2}}
near the poles is as follows:
G_{z_{1},z_{2}}(a)\sim 4\pi a
as
a\to 0^{+}
and
G_{z_{1},z_{2}}(a)\sim 4\pi(M-a)
as
a\to M^{-}
. As in the previous section, to simplify the notation we let

A\doteq A_{z_{1},z_{2}}^{(\nu)}\,,\ \ \ \psi\doteq\psi_{z_{1},z_{2}}\,,\ \ \ G\doteq G_{z_{1},z_{2}}.

We also set

A^{\star}\doteq A_{x_{0}}^{(\nu)}\,,\ \ \ \psi^{\star}\doteq\psi_{x_{0}}\,,\ \ \ G^{\star}\doteq G_{x_{0}}=a(4\pi-a),

where
\psi_{x_{0}}
is the Green function on
\mathbb{S}^{2}
with two antipodal poles
x_{0},-x_{0}
,
A^{(\nu)}_{x_{0},}=-2\pi\nu\star d\psi_{x_{0}}=\nu d\theta
and
G_{x_{0}}
is defined by (
4.1
) with
\psi_{x_{0}}
.
Consider now the function space

\mathcal{R}(\Sigma)\doteq\{u:u=g\circ\psi\,,\ g\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R})\}\subset H^{1}_{A}(\Sigma)

and the number

(4.2)

w_{1}(\Omega,A)\doteq\min_{0\neq u\in\mathcal{R}(\Sigma)}\frac{\int_{\Sigma}|d^{A}u|^{2}}{\int_{\Sigma}|u|^{2}}.

From the min-max principle we have

(4.3)

\mu_{1}(\Sigma,A)\leq w_{1}(\Sigma,A).

We have the following lemma, whose proof is analogous to that of Lemma
3.1

Lemma 4.1
.

We have that

(4.4)

w_{1}(\Sigma,A)=\min_{0\neq f\in\mathcal{F}}\frac{\int_{0}^{M}\left(G(a)f^{\prime}(a)^{2}+\frac{4\pi^{2}\nu^{2}}{G(a)}f(a)^{2}\right)da}{\int_{0}^{M}f(a)^{2}da},

where
\mathcal{F}=\{f\in L^{2}(0,M):\sqrt{G}f^{\prime},f/\sqrt{G}\in L^{2}(0,M)\}
. In particular
w_{1}(\Sigma,A)
is the first eigenvalue of the following Sturm-Liouville problem

(4.5)

\begin{cases}-(Gf^{\prime})^{\prime}+\frac{4\pi^{2}\nu^{2}}{G}f=wf\,,&{\rm in\ }(0,M)\,,\\
\lim_{a\to 0^{+}}G(a)f^{\prime}(a)=\lim_{a\to M^{-}}G(a)f^{\prime}(a)=0.\end{cases}

Remark 4.2
.

Note that in the definition of
\mathcal{R}(\Sigma)
we are considering functions
g\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R})
since now the Green function on
\Sigma
with poles
z_{1},z_{2}
assumes all real values (it goes to
+\infty
and
-\infty
at
z_{1}
,
z_{2}
, respectively).

4.2.
Optimization of the
1
-d problem

Let now
G
be any smooth, positive function in
(0,M)
satisfying

(4.6)

G(a)\sim 4\pi a\ {\rm as}\ a\to 0^{+}\ \ \ {\rm and\ \ \ }G(a)\sim 4\pi(M-a)\ {\rm as}\ a\to M^{-},

and consider

w_{1}(G)\doteq\min_{0\neq f\in\mathcal{F}}\frac{\int_{0}^{M}\left(G(a)f^{\prime}(a)^{2}+\frac{4\pi^{2}\nu^{2}}{G(a)}f(a)^{2}\right)da}{\int_{0}^{M}f^{2}(a)da},

where
\mathcal{F}=\{f\in L^{2}(0,M):\sqrt{G}f^{\prime},f/\sqrt{G}\in L^{2}(0,M)\}
. Note that
w_{1}(\Sigma,A^{(\nu)}_{z_{1},z_{2}})=\kappa_{1}(G_{z_{1},z_{2}})
. We have the following

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
0<G_{1}\leq G_{2}
on
(0,M)
, both satisfying (
4.6
). Then

w_{1}(G_{2})\leq w_{1}(G_{1}).

If
G_{1}<G_{2}
on a set of positive measure, the inequality is strict.

Proof.

The proof is identical to that of Lemma
3.3
.
The only difference from the proof of Lemma
3.3
lies in the second step, namely the treatment of the case
R\leq-2\pi\nu
since now also
a=M
is a singular point.

Second step.
Assume by contradiction that there exists a point
a_{0}\in(0,M)
such that
R(a_{0})\leq-2\pi\nu
. We see from (
3.12
) that
R^{\prime}(a_{0})<0
, and this implies, still using (
3.12
), that
R
is decreasing in the whole interval
(a_{0},M)
. Hence limit
\lim_{a\rightarrow M^{-}}R(a)
exists, so we have two cases

Case
1
:
\lim_{a\rightarrow M^{-}}R(a)
is finite. In particular then,
\lim_{a\rightarrow M^{-}}R(a)<-2\pi\nu
. We integrate (
3.12
) and we obtain

R(a)-R(a_{0})=\int_{a_{0}}^{a}R^{\prime}(s)ds=\int_{a_{0}}^{a}\frac{4\pi^{2}\nu^{2}-R^{2}}{G_{t}}-\kappa_{1}(G_{t})ds.

But now we recall that
G_{t}(a)\sim 4\pi(M-a)
when
a\rightarrow M^{-}
, hence, since
\lim_{a\rightarrow M^{-}}R(a)<-2\pi\nu
, we get that the right-hand side of the above equation is unbounded as
a\to M^{-}
, while the left-hand side remains bounded.

Case
2
:
\lim_{a\rightarrow M^{-}}R(a)=-\infty
. From (
3.12
), recalling that
G_{t}(a)\sim 4\pi(M-a)
as
a\to M^{-}
, we obtain

\lim_{a\rightarrow M^{-}}\frac{(M-a)R^{\prime}}{R^{2}}=-\frac{1}{4\pi}.

Therefore, there exists
\bar{a}>0
and
\epsilon>0
such that

\frac{1}{(M-a)}\left(-\frac{1}{4\pi}-\epsilon\right)\leq\left(-\frac{1}{R}\right)^{\prime}\leq\frac{1}{(M-a)}\left(-\frac{1}{4\pi}+\epsilon\right)

on
(\bar{a},4\pi)
. We can integrate both sides in
(\bar{a},a)
, and we obtain

\left(-\frac{1}{4\pi}-\epsilon\right)\ln\left({\frac{M-\bar{a}}{M-a}}\right)\leq\frac{1}{R(a)}-\frac{1}{R(\bar{a})}\leq\left(-\frac{1}{4\pi}+\epsilon\right)\ln\left({\frac{M-\bar{a}}{M-a}}\right)

In particular from the above inequality we can conclude that
\lim_{a\rightarrow M^{-}}R(a)=0
that is a contradiction. The conclusion of Lemma
4.3
is now the same as in Lemma
3.3
.
∎

4.3.
When
\left|\Sigma\right|>\left|\mathbb{S}^{2}\right|
: a sequence of weight functions

Due to the volume bound
|\Sigma|\geq|\mathbb{S}^{2}|
, we cannot directly compare

A.1.
Spectrum of the punctured sphere

In this appendix we compute explicitly the spectrum of the sphere with Aharonov-Bohm potential with two opposite poles and flux
\nu\notin\mathbb{Z}
.

Let
x_{0}\in\mathbb{S}^{2}
be a fixed point. Consider polar coordinates
(r,\theta)
based at
x_{0}
, where
r
is the geodesic distance from
x_{0}
and
\theta
is the polar angle. Let
A^{(\nu)}_{x_{0}}=\nu d\theta
. It is closed and has flux
\nu
around any loop separating
x_{0}
and
-x_{0}
.

Proposition A.1
.

Let
\nu\in(0,\frac{1}{2}]
. The spectrum of the magnetic Laplacian with potential
A^{(\nu)}_{x_{0}}
on
\mathbb{S}^{2}
is described as follows:

a)

if
\nu\in(0,\frac{1}{2})
then the eigenvalues are given by the union of the following sequences of numbers (counted with multiplicities)

\displaystyle\{(n-\nu)(n-\nu+1)\}_{n\geq 1}

\displaystyle\text{ with multiplicity }n

\displaystyle\{(\left|n\right|+\nu)(\left|n\right|+\nu+1)\}_{n\leq 0}

\displaystyle\text{ with multiplicity }|n|+1

b)

if
\nu=\frac{1}{2}
then the eigenvalues are given by the following sequence of numbers (counted with multiplicity)

\{(n-\nu)(n-\nu+1)\}_{n\geq 1}\text{ with multiplicity }2n

c)

In particular, if
\nu\in(0,\frac{1}{2})
one has

\mu_{1}(\mathbb{S}^{2},A^{(\nu)}_{x_{0}})=\nu(\nu+1)

with eigenspace spanned by
u(r,\theta)=\sin^{\nu}(r)
. If
\nu=\frac{1}{2}
the lowest eigenvalue is
\frac{3}{4}
, it is double, and the eigenspace is spanned by
\sin^{\frac{1}{2}}(r)
,
e^{i\theta}\sin^{\frac{1}{2}}(r)
.

d)

If
\nu\in\mathbb{R}
,
\nu\notin\mathbb{Z}
, formulae a), b) and c) hold with
\nu
replaced by
\min_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}|m-\nu|
.

When
\nu\in(0,\frac{1}{2})
the first few eigenvalues
\mu_{k}\doteq\mu_{k}(\mathbb{S}^{2},A^{(\nu)}_{x_{0}})
are then given by

\displaystyle\mu_{1}=\nu(\nu+1),\quad\mu_{2}=(1-\nu)(2-\nu)

\displaystyle\mu_{3}=\mu_{4}=(1+\nu)(2+\nu),\quad\mu_{5}=\mu_{6}=(2-\nu)(3-\nu)\quad\text{etc.}

Remark A.2
.

Note that at the limit as
\nu\to 0
we recover the spectrum of the Laplace-Beltrami operator with multiplicities: the eigenvalues
(n-\nu)(n-\nu+1)
go to
n(n+1)
with multiplicity
n
(
n\geq 1
) and the eigenvalues
(\nu-n)(\nu-n+1)
go to
|n|(|n|+1)
with multiplicity
|n|+1
(
n\leq 0
). Altogether, the eigenvalues are given by
\ell(\ell+1)
with multiplicity
2\ell+1
,
\ell=0,1,2,\cdots
.

Proof.

Through the proof we shall simply write
A\doteq A^{(\nu)}_{x_{0}}=\nu d\theta
. Writing the magnetic Laplacian in polar coordinates
(r,\theta)
based at
x_{0}
we get that, for any smooth, complex-valued function
u
,

(A.1)

\Delta_{A}u=-\partial^{2}_{rr}u-\cot(r)\partial_{r}u-\frac{1}{\sin^{2}(r)}\partial^{2}_{\theta\theta}u+\frac{\nu^{2}}{\sin^{2}(r)}u+\frac{2i\nu}{\sin^{2}(r)}\partial_{\theta}u.

We have no boundary, and the presence of the two singularities is taken into account in the choice of the energy space for the weak formulation, which is
H^{1}_{A}(\mathbb{S}^{2}):=\{u\in L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{2}):|d^{A}u|\in L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{2})\}
. This imposes certain conditions at the two poles.

As customary, looking for solutions to
\Delta_{A}u=\mu u
of the form
u(r,\theta)=e^{in\theta}v(r)
,
n\in\mathbb{Z}
, we end up with a family of Sturm-Liouville problems indexed by
n\in\mathbb{Z}
:

(A.2)

\begin{cases}v^{\prime\prime}(r)+\cot(r)v^{\prime}(r)+\left(\mu-\frac{(n-\nu)^{2}}{\sin^{2}(r)}\right)v(r)=0\,,&{\rm\ in\ }(0,\pi)\,,\\
\lim_{r\to 0^{+}}\sin(r)v^{\prime}(r)=\lim_{r\to\pi^{-}}\sin(r)v^{\prime}(r)=0.\end{cases}

The boundary conditions at the singular endpoints encode the information on the energy space. Now, as shown e.g., in
[
8
, Lemma 23]
, each problem (
A.2
) admits an increasing sequence of simple eigenvalues

0<\mu_{n0}<\mu_{n1}<\cdots<\mu_{nk}<\cdots\nearrow+\infty

with associated eigenfunctions
v_{nk}
which have
k
zeros in
(0,\pi)
. First we will compute explicitly
\mu_{nk}
. Then we show that these numbers exhaust all the spectrum.

Step 1: compute
\mu_{nk}
.
Recall that
\nu\notin\mathbb{Z}
. First, for all
n\in\mathbb{Z}
, we identify
\mu_{n0}
. In fact, take
v(r)=\sin^{|n-\nu|}(r)
. It does not change sign in
(0,\pi)
and solves (
A.2
) with
\mu=|n-\nu|(|n-\nu|+1)
. Thus we deduce that

\mu_{n0}=|n-\nu|(|n-\nu|+1).

When
\nu\in(0,\frac{1}{2})
, the first eigenvalue is attained at
n=0
, and equals

\mu_{00}=\nu(\nu+1);

it is simple, and the corresponding eigenspace is spanned by
\sin^{\nu}(r)
. When
\nu=\frac{1}{2}
we have that the first eigenvalue is

\mu_{00}=\mu_{10}=\frac{3}{4};

it is double, and the corresponding eigenspace is spanned by
\sin^{\frac{1}{2}}(r)
and
e^{i\theta}\sin^{\frac{1}{2}}(r)
.

Now we need to compute all the other eigenvalues. We first observe that a solution of the differential equation in (
A.2
) is given by

P_{\alpha}^{-|n-\nu|}(\cos(r)),

where
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
is related to the eigenvalue
\mu
and is defined by

\mu=\alpha(\alpha+1).

Here
L^{b}_{a}
denotes the Legendre function of degree
a
and order
b
, see
[
13
, §14]
. Note that
P_{\alpha}^{-\alpha}(\cos(r))=c_{\alpha}\sin^{\alpha}(r)
, where
c_{\alpha}
is a normalization constant.

Note also that there are two linearly independent solutions of the differential equation in (
A.2
) which are possibly singular at the endpoints
0,\pi
.
From
[
13
, §14.8, 14.8.1]
we have

(A.3)

P_{\alpha}^{-|n-1|}(x)\sim\frac{1}{\Gamma(|n-\nu|+1)}\left(\frac{1-x}{2}\right)^{\frac{|n-\nu|}{2}}\,,\ \ \ x\to 1^{-}

hence, with our choice of taking
P_{\alpha}^{-|n-\nu|}
we have

\lim_{r\to 0^{+}}P_{\alpha}^{-|n-\nu|}(\cos(r))=0.

Hence, an eigenvalue
\mu
is determined by imposing

\lim_{r\to\pi^{-}}P_{\alpha}^{-|n-\nu|}(\cos(r))=0.

From
[
13
, §14.9, 14.9.7]
we have

(A.4)

P_{\alpha}^{-|n-\nu|}(-x)=\frac{\sin(\nu\pi)}{\sin(|n-\nu|\pi)}P^{-|n-\nu|}_{\alpha}(x)\\
-\frac{\sin((|n-\nu|-\alpha)\pi)\Gamma(\alpha-|n-\nu|+1)}{\sin(|n-\nu|\pi)\Gamma(\alpha+|n-\nu|+1)}P^{|n-\nu|}_{\alpha}(x).

Note that this formula is valid provided
|n-\nu|\notin\mathbb{Z}
, which is our case, and
\alpha\pm|n-\nu|+1\neq-1,-2,-3,...
.

We check the limit of the right-hand side of (
A.4
) as
x\to 1^{-}
. From
[
13
, §14.8, 14.8.1]
we have

(A.5)

P_{\alpha}^{|n-1|}(x)\sim\frac{1}{\Gamma(1-|n-\nu|)}\left(\frac{2}{1-x}\right)^{\frac{|n-\nu|}{2}}\,,\ \ \ x\to 1^{-},

hence the left-hand side of (
A.4
) is unbounded at
-1^{+}
unless

\alpha=|n-\nu|+k\,,\ \ \ k\geq 0.

It remains to consider the cases
\alpha=\pm|n-\nu|+k-1
,
k\in\mathbb{Z}
,
k\leq-1
. We discard the case
\alpha=-|n-\nu|+k-1
: in fact, a corresponding eigenvalue would be
\mu=\alpha(\alpha+1)=(-|n-\nu|+k-1)(-|n-\nu|+k)=(|n-\nu|+\ell)(|n-\nu|+\ell+1)
with
\ell=-k\in\mathbb{Z}
,
\ell\geq 1
, which we already know to be in the spectrum. Hence we are left with the case
\alpha=|n-\nu|+k-1
,
k\in\mathbb{Z}
,
k\leq-1
. In this case, formula (
A.4
) is replaced by the following

(A.6)

P_{|n-\nu|+k-1}^{-|n-\nu|}(-x)=\frac{\sin(\nu\pi)}{\sin(|n-\nu|\pi)}P^{-|n-\nu|}_{|n-\nu|+k-1}(x)\\
-\frac{\pi}{\sin(|n-\nu|\pi)(-k)!\Gamma(k+2|n-\nu|)}P^{|n-\nu|}_{\alpha}(x),

and by (
A.5
) we see that the right-hand side is unbounded near
1^{-}
, hence the left-hand side is unbounded near
-1^{+}
.

So far, we have found that for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
, the eigenvalues of (
A.2
) are given by the collection

\{(|n-\nu|+k)(|n-\nu|+k+1)\}_{k=0}^{\infty}.

Step 2: compute multiplicities.
To count multiplicities, we assume for simplicity that
\nu\in(0,\frac{1}{2}]
.
We know that the first eigenvalue is given by

\mu_{00}=\nu(\nu+1).

We have to distinguish the case
\nu\in(0,\frac{1}{2})
and
\nu=\frac{1}{2}
.

If
\nu\in(0,\frac{1}{2})
, then we consider the two families of eigenvalues corresponding to
n\geq 1
and
n\leq 0
. In fact, we cannot find
k,j
such that

\mu_{nk}=\mu_{mj}

for
n\geq 1
and
m\leq 0
: we would have
n-\nu+k=-m+\nu+j
, which means
n+k+m-j=2\nu\notin\mathbb{Z}
. Now, we count the multiplicities for
n\geq 1
. The eigenvalue
(2-\nu)(1-\nu)
occurs only for
n=1
and it is the first eigenvalue
\mu_{10}
, hence it is simple. The eigenvalue
(2-\nu)(3-\nu)
occurs only for
n=1,2
: it is the second eigenvalue
\mu_{11}
for
n=1
and the first eigenvale
\mu_{20}
for
n=2
. Hence it has multiplicity
2
. In this way we see that the eigenvalue
(n-\nu)(n-\nu+1)
has multiplicity
n
. Reasoning in the same way for
n\leq 0
, we see that the eigenvalue
(\nu-n)(\nu-n+1)
has multiplicity
-n+1=|n|+1
. In particular, for
n=0
, the eigenvalue
\nu(\nu+1)
has multiplicity
1
, and this is the first eigenvalue and it is simple:

\mu_{1}(\mathbb{S}^{2},A)=\nu(\nu+1).

Next, we consider the case
\nu=\frac{1}{2}
. Clearly, all the discussion above holds, and we have two families of eigenvalues given by
(n-\nu)(n-\nu+1)
,
n\geq 1
and multiplicity
n
, and
(\nu-m)(\nu-m+1)
and multiplicity
-m+1=|m|+1
for
m\leq 0
. However we note that the eigenvalues coincide if
n+m=1
. For example, for
n=1,m=0
we have the eigenvalue
3/4
with multiplicity
2
. For
n=2,m=-1
we have the eigenvalue
15/4
with multiplicity
4
. Altogether, we have that
(n-\nu)(n-\nu+1)
,
n\geq 1
, has multiplicity
2n
.

Completeness of the family of eigenfunctions.
To show that we have exhausted all the spectrum, we need to show that the family of functions

is a complete system of eigenfunctions in

. This is a standard fact, and one can find a proof for more general manifolds of revolution around a point in
[

, Appendix B]
.
∎

A.2.
Spectrum of the punctured hemisphere

As a corollary of the above proposition we have that the spectrum of the hemisphere (with either Dirichlet or Neumann conditions) with pole at the center is equal to the spectrum of the whole sphere with antipodal poles (with different multiplicities).

Let
\mathbb{S}^{2}_{+}
denote a hemisphere, and let
x_{0}
be its center. Let
A^{(\nu)}_{x_{0}}=\nu d\theta
the Aharonov-Bohm potential with pole
x_{0}
and flux
\nu
. Then

\{\mu_{k}(\mathbb{S}^{2},A^{(\nu)}_{x_{0}})\}_{k=1}^{\infty}=\{\lambda_{k}(\mathbb{S}^{2}_{+},A^{(\nu)}_{x_{0}})\}_{k=1}^{\infty}\cup\{\mu_{k}(\mathbb{S}^{2}_{+},A^{(\nu)}_{x_{0}})\}_{k=1}^{\infty}

and the union is disjoint: namely, the spectrum on the whole sphere with opposite poles is the disjoint union of the Dirichlet and Neumann spectra for the potential
A^{(\nu)}_{x_{0}}
on the hemisphere
\mathbb{S}^{2}_{+}
. The proof is standard, see e.g.,
[
3
]
. It is enough to observe that the radial parts of spherical eigenfunctions are either even or odd with respect to the equator. The restrictions of eigenfunction with odd radial part to
\mathbb{S}^{2}_{+}
are Dirichlet eigenfunctions on
\mathbb{S}_{+}
, while the restrictions of the eigenfunctions with even radial part to
\mathbb{S}^{2}_{+}
are Neumann eigenfunctions on
\mathbb{S}^{2}_{+}
. Vice-versa, odd/even reflecting Dirichlet/Neumann eigenfunctions of
\mathbb{S}^{2}_{+}
give eigenfunctions on
\mathbb{S}^{2}
. Counting multiplicities we have:

Dirichlet eigenvalues:
they are given by

a)

If
\nu\in(0,\frac{1}{2})

\displaystyle\{(n-\nu)(n-\nu+1)\}_{n\geq 1}

\displaystyle\text{ with multiplicity }{\frac{n-1}{2}}{\rm\ for\ }n\ {\rm odd\ and\ }{\frac{n}{2}}{\rm\ for\ }n\ {\rm even}

\displaystyle\{(\left|n\right|+\nu)(\left|n\right|+\nu+1)\}_{n\leq 0}

\displaystyle\text{ with multiplicity }{\frac{|n|+1}{2}}{\rm\ for\ }n\ {\rm odd\ and\ }{\frac{|n|}{2}}{\rm\ for\ }n\ {\rm even}

b)

if
\nu=\frac{1}{2}

\{(n-\nu)(n-\nu+1)\}_{n\geq 1}\text{ with multiplicity }{n-1}{\rm\ for\ }n\ {\rm odd\ and\ }{n}{\rm\ for\ }n\ {\rm even}

Neumann eigenvalues:
they are given by

a)

If
\nu\in(0,\frac{1}{2})

\displaystyle\{(n-\nu)(n-\nu+1)\}_{n\geq 1}

\displaystyle\text{ with multiplicity }{\frac{n+1}{2}}{\rm\ for\ }n\ {\rm odd\ and\ }{\frac{n}{2}}{\rm\ for\ }n\ {\rm even}

\displaystyle\{(\left|n\right|+\nu)(\left|n\right|+\nu+1)\}_{n\leq 0}

\displaystyle\text{ with multiplicity }{\frac{|n|+1}{2}}{\rm\ for\ }n\ {\rm odd\ and\ }{\frac{|n|+2}{2}}{\rm\ for\ }n\ {\rm even}

b)

if
\nu=\frac{1}{2}

\{(n-\nu)(n-\nu+1)\}_{n\geq 1}\text{ with multiplicity }{n+1}{\rm\ for\ }n\ {\rm odd\ and\ }{n}{\rm\ for\ }n\ {\rm even}

Note that the multiplicity of any Dirichlet/Neumann eigenvalue with
\nu=\frac{1}{2}
is always an even number. For
\nu=0
we find the multiplicities
n
of the eigenvalue
n(n+1)
,
n\geq 0
, as Dirichlet eigenvalue of the Laplacian on
\mathbb{S}^{2}_{+}
, and the multiplicities
n+1
of the eigenvalue
n(n+1)
,
n\geq 0
, as Neumann eigenvalue of the Laplacian on
\mathbb{S}^{2}_{+}
. By our convention, if the multiplicity is
0
then the corresponding eigenvalue is not present in the spectrum.

A.3.
Limit of Dirichlet eigenvalues

In the next lemma we prove that the first Dirichlet eigenvalue of a spherical cap centered at
x_{0}
with potential
A^{(\nu)}_{x_{0}}
converges to the first eigenvalue of the whole sphere
\mathbb{S}^{2}
with the same potential when the radius of the cap tends to
\pi
.

Lemma A.3
.

Let
D_{R}\subset\mathbb{S}^{2}
be a geodesic disk of radius
R
centered at
x_{0}
, and let
A^{(\nu)}_{x_{0}}=\nu d\theta
. Let
\lambda_{1}(D_{R},A^{(\nu)}_{x_{0}})
be the first eigenvalue of
\Delta_{A^{(\nu)}_{x_{0}}}
with Dirichlet conditions on
\partial D_{R}
. Then

(A.7)

\lim_{R\rightarrow\pi}\lambda_{1}(D_{R},A^{(\nu)}_{x_{0}})=\mu_{1}(\mathbb{S}^{2},A^{(\nu)}_{x_{0}})

Proof.

Through the proof we set
A\doteq A^{(\nu)}_{x_{0}}
. We can always assume
R>\frac{\pi}{2}
and
\nu\in(0,\frac{1}{2}]
.
Consider the function
\varphi
defined by

(A.8)

\varphi(r)=\begin{cases}1\,\,&\text{ in }\,\,r\in[0,2R-\pi]\\
\frac{r-R}{R-\pi}\,\,&\text{ in }\,\,r\in[2R-\pi,R]\\
0\,\,&\text{ in }\,\,r\in[R,\pi]\end{cases}

Let
u
be the first eigenfunction on
\mathbb{S}^{2}
, associated with
\mu_{1}(\mathbb{S}^{2},A)
. From Proposition
A.1
, we know that it is radial and is given (up to constant multiples) by
u(r)=\sin^{\nu}(r)
. Set
C_{R}\doteq D_{R}\setminus D_{2R-\pi}
the spherical annulus obtained by removing from
D_{R}
a the disk of radius
2R-\pi
centered at
x_{0}
. Now, we have that
u\varphi\in H^{1}_{0,A}(D_{R})
and from the min-max principle we have

(A.9)

\lambda_{1}(D_{R},A)\leq\frac{\int_{D_{R}}|d^{A}(u\varphi)|^{2}}{\int_{D_{R}}(u\varphi)^{2}}=\frac{\int_{D_{2R-\pi}}|d^{A}u|^{2}+\int_{C_{R}}|d^{A}(u\varphi)|^{2}}{\int_{D_{2R-\pi}}u^{2}+\int_{C_{R}}u^{2}\varphi^{2}}.

Everything is explicit, in fact
|d^{A}(u\varphi)|^{2}=|d(u\varphi)|^{2}+|A|^{2}u^{2}\varphi^{2}=u^{2}\varphi^{2}+\varphi^{2}|du|^{2}+\frac{\nu^{2}}{\sin^{2}(r)}u^{2}\varphi^{2}
. Standard computations show that

\displaystyle\lim_{R\rightarrow\pi}\int_{C_{R}}|d^{A}(u\varphi)|^{2}=0

\displaystyle\lim_{R\rightarrow\pi}\int_{C_{R}}u^{2}\varphi^{2}=0.

Moreover,
\int_{D_{2R-\pi}}|d^{A}u|^{2}\rightarrow\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}|d^{A}u|^{2}
and
\int_{D_{2R-\pi}}u^{2}\rightarrow\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}u^{2}
when
R\rightarrow\pi
,
and then from (
A.9
) we get that

\lambda_{1}(D_{R},A)\leq\mu_{1}(\mathbb{S}^{2},A)+o(1)\,\,\text{as}\,\,R\to\pi.

On the other hand, let
u_{1}
be an eigenfunction on
D_{R}
associated with
\lambda_{1}(D_{R},A)
. Its extension by zero to
\mathbb{S}^{2}
belongs to
H^{1}_{0,A}(\mathbb{S}^{2})
, hence, from the min-max principle for
\mu_{1}(\mathbb{S}^{2},A)
we deduce that
\mu_{1}(\mathbb{S}^{2},A)\leq\lambda_{1}(D_{R},A)
. This concludes the proof.
∎

Appendix B
The spectrum of flat cylinders

Let
\mathcal{C}_{M}=\mathbb{S}^{1}\times[-M,M]
, with coordinates
(\theta,z)
, endowed with the flat metric
d\theta^{2}+dz^{2}
. Take the
1
-form
A=\nu d\theta
, which is closed, co-closed and has flux
\nu
around any boundary component of
\mathcal{C}_{M}
. If we think of
\mathcal{C}_{M}
as a cylinder in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, the potential
1
-form can be expressed in cartesian coordinates as
\frac{\nu}{x^{2}+y^{2}}(-ydx+xdy)
.

In order to compute
\mu_{1}(\mathcal{C}_{M},A)
, we write the magnetic Laplacian
\Delta_{A}
in cylindrical coordinates. We obtain

\displaystyle\Delta_{A}u

\displaystyle=\Delta u+|A|^{2}u^{2}+2i\langle A,\nabla u\rangle

\displaystyle=-u_{zz}-u_{\theta\theta}+\nu^{2}u+2i\nu u_{\theta}.

By separation of variables, we can write the eigenfunctions in the form
u(\theta,z)=e^{in\theta}v_{n}(z)
,
n\in\mathbb{Z}
(see e.g.,
[
15
]
for more details). Plugging this ansatz in the eigenvalue equation, and denoting by
\mu_{n}
a corresponding eigenvalue, we get

-v_{n}^{\prime\prime}(z)=\left(\mu_{n}-\left|n-\nu\right|^{2}\right)v_{n}(z)\,\,\,\,\,\forall z\in(-M,M),

and the boundary conditions, due to the fact that
\langle A,N\rangle=0
on
\partial\mathcal{C}_{M}
, just read
v_{n}^{\prime}(z)=0
at
z=-M
and
z=M
.
This is just a shifted Neumann problem on the segment
(-M,M)
, hence the spectrum is given by the collection

\left\{|n-\nu|^{2}+\frac{k^{2}\pi^{2}}{4M^{2}}\right\}_{k\in\mathbb{N},n\in\mathbb{Z}}

and the first eigenvalue is given by

\mu_{1}(\mathcal{C}_{M},A)=\min_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\left|n-\nu\right|^{2}.

By gauge invariance, we may always assume
\nu\in(0,\frac{1}{2}]
, so that the minimum is attained at
n=0
and we can conclude that

\mu_{1}(\mathcal{C}_{M},A)=\nu^{2}.

independently on
M
. The corresponding eigenspace is spanned by the constant function
u_{1}=1
.

The fact that the first eigenfunction of any flat annulus has constant modulus will imply an upper bound of isoperimetric type on any Riemannian cylinder, similarly to what happens to the magnetic Steklov problem in cylinders, see
[
15
]
. This fundamental fact is used in the proof of Theorem
2.6
.

Proof of Theorem
2.6
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a Riemannian annulus, namely, a differentiable surface diffeomorphic to
\mathbb{S}^{1}\times[0,1]
endowed with a Riemannian metric. It is known that any Riemanniann annulus
\mathcal{C}
is conformally equivalent to the flat cylinder
\mathcal{C}_{M}
for a unique
M>0
, which is called the
conformal modulus
of
\mathcal{C}
. In other words, there exists a conformal diffeomorphism
\Phi:\mathcal{C}\to\mathcal{C}_{M}
. Consider on
\mathcal{C}_{M}
the potential
1
-form
A=\nu d\theta
, which is closed and has flux
\nu
. Consider on
\mathcal{C}
the potential
1
-form
\Phi^{\star}A
, which is again closed, and with flux
\nu
. It is unique up to exact forms. By gauge invariance, the magnetic spectrum of
\mathcal{C}
depends only on
\nu
, see e.g.,
[
8
, §4]
or
[
15
]
for more details. Let
u_{1}=1
. By the min-max principle we have

(B.1)

\mu_{1}(\mathcal{C},\Phi^{\star}A)\leq\frac{\int_{\mathcal{C}}|d^{\Phi^{\star}A}u_{1}|^{2}}{\int_{\mathcal{C}}|u_{1}|^{2}}=\frac{\int_{\mathcal{C}}|\Phi^{\star}A|^{2}}{|\mathcal{C}|}=\frac{\int_{\mathcal{C}_{M}}|A|^{2}}{|\mathcal{C}|}=\frac{|\mathcal{C}_{M}|}{|\mathcal{C}|}\nu^{2}.

That is

|\mathcal{C}|\mu_{1}(\Sigma,\Phi^{\star}A)\leq|\mathcal{C}_{M}|\mu_{1}(\mathcal{C}_{M},A).

As already mentioned, the Aharonov-Bohm spectrum of a Riemannian cylinder depends only on the flux
\nu
, hence we may write
\mu_{1}(\mathcal{C},\nu)\doteq\mu_{1}(\mathcal{C},\Phi^{\star}A)
and
\mu_{1}(\mathcal{C}_{M},\nu)\doteq\mu_{1}(\mathcal{C}_{M},A)
.
If equality holds, then
u_{1}=1
is an eigenfunction of
\Delta_{\Phi^{\star}A}
on
\mathcal{C}
, which means that
|\Phi^{\star}A|^{2}=\nu^{2}
. This means that
\mathcal{C}
supports a closed
1
-form of constant length. But since since the metric on
\mathcal{C}
is conformal to the flat metric, we have that
|\Phi^{\star}A|^{2}=\rho^{2}|A|^{2}=\rho^{2}
for some conformal factor
\rho
, and this implies that
\rho
is constant. Hence
\mathcal{C}
is homothetic to
\mathcal{C}_{M}
.
∎
