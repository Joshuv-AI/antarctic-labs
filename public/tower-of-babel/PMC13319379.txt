An intelligent point-of-care photoacoustic and ultrasound dual-modality imaging system for Rheumatoid arthritis in fingers: Technical development and initial clinical test☆☆☆
Authors: Xiaorui Peng, Kyoungmo Koo, Jianwei Qiu, Grigorios Karageorgos, Aaron Dentinger, Zhanpeng Xu, Siddarth Ragupathi, Zhaoyuan Yang, Soumya Ghose, Janggun Jo, Nada Abdulaziz, Guan Xu, Wei Zhao, David Chamberland, Mark Draelos, Girish Gandikota, David Mills, Xueding Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAiming at an intelligent point-of-care imaging technology for rheumatology clinics, a fully automatic 3D photoacoustic (PA) and ultrasound (US) dual-modality system driven by a robot and powered by deep learning (DL)-based image processing was developed. Automated scanning of volumetric images from patient joints, plus DL-based tissue segmentation and quantification of imaging biomarkers, ensures that the measurements from this system are objective and reproducible. Clinical validation was conducted via a longitudinal study on 43 finger joints from patients affected by inflammatory arthritis. Using manual segmentations as the gold standard, our DL algorithm utilizing the 3D Deep Attentive Feature (DAF3D) model showed satisfactory performance in automatic segmentation of joint space and synovial region and achieved a Dice score of 0.77±0.03 and an IoU of 0.64±0.03. Based on the tissue segmentations facilitated by the DAF3D model, six volumetric imaging biomarkers reflecting the activity of arthritis and its change in response to the treatment were quantified, including hyperemia, blood oxygenation, US power Doppler, joint space echogenicity, joint space volume, and synovial volume. The imaging biomarkers quantified from DL-based segmentation and manual segmentation showed moderate to strong correlations (R: 0.41−0.86). Hyperemia quantified from PA imaging has the strongest association with the disease activity indicated by Clinical Assessment Questionnaire (CAQ) scores, with R2=0.41. Linear models combining the two biomarkers from PA imaging, the four biomarkers from US imaging, and all six imaging biomarkers rendered moderate to very strong associations with the disease activity scores, with R2 of 0.41, 0.26, and 0.52, respectively.Keywords: Ultrasound, Photoacoustics, Medical robotics, Deep learning, Clinical study

Introduction

Rheumatoid arthritis (RA), as a chronic autoimmune disorder affecting approximately 0.5%–1% of the global population [1], is the leading cause of disability in adults, and is associated with substantial activity limitation, work disability, reduced quality of life, and high health-care costs [2]. Early, rapid, and cost-effective diagnosis is essential to initiate timely intervention and achieve remission before irreversible joint damage occurs. However, current imaging modalities used to detect pathological changes are inherently limited in capturing early disease processes. X-ray-based scoring systems primarily identify structural alterations, such as bone erosion and joint space narrowing, which represent late-stage manifestations of disease. Despite providing high contrast for osseous structures, X-rays lack sensitivity to functional and soft-tissue changes [3], [4], particularly within synovial tissues affected by early inflammation. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) offers superior visualization of soft-tissue inflammation; however, its clinical utility is constrained by high cost, prolonged acquisition times, the requirement for intravenous contrast agents, and limited portability [5].

Rheumatology and radiology clinics would enormously benefit from better imaging technologies that are comparable to or better than MRI in sensitivity to soft-tissue inflammation but significantly reduced in costs, improved in availability and portability, and more patient and operator-friendly. With many advantages as an inexpensive, accessible, non-ionizing, non-invasive imaging modality and the ability to produce real-time images and document both bony erosions and enhanced blood flow associated with soft tissue inflammation, ultrasound (US) Doppler imaging has been employed as the primary modality for clinical diagnosis of inflammatory arthritis [6]. Although Doppler US can detect fast blood flow in larger vessels, it remains insensitive to the slow microvascular flow that predominates in early synovitis [7]. Recent clinical studies have highlighted the potential of photoacoustic (PA) imaging for assessing soft-tissue inflammation [8], [9], [10], [11], [12], [13]. By sensitively capturing hemodynamic and metabolic changes, PA imaging enables the mapping of key inflammatory biomarkers, including hyperemia and hypoxia. These features arise from increased metabolic demand coupled with insufficient oxygen delivery in inflamed tissues.

PA imaging can provide additional valuable diagnostic information contributing to a more robust set of parameters for identification and characteristic quantification of inflammatory arthritis. The encouraging findings from the earlier pioneering clinical studies suggest that integration of PA imaging with established US modalities may enable low-cost, accessible point-of-care diagnostic devices, so that critical disease-modifying treatments can be deployed, positively altering the curve of joint deterioration and thus reducing joint-related pain and disability.

With this goal in mind, we developed an intelligent three-dimensional (3D) PA-US dual-modality system powered by a robot for automatic image scanning. In addition, a deep learning (DL) algorithm was developed for automatic image processing and analysis. The highly automatic features of this system, including automated 3D image scanning and image processing, tissue segmentation, and quantification of biomarkers, removes potential operator dependence and renders imaging findings reliable and reproducible. Unlike current clinical US which needs to be operated by experienced radiologists or sonographers, such an intelligent imaging system can be used by anyone after simple training. In addition, unlike 2D US or PA imaging that may lead to inconsistent and inaccurate diagnoses given its susceptibility to scanning plane deviation, angular misalignment, and skills of the operator [14], 3D volumetric measures facilitated by this automated system could be superior in both accuracy and reproducibility. The feasibility and performance of this robot and DL powered intelligent PA-US dual-modality imaging system was evaluated via a prospective longitudinal clinical study on a total of 43 finger joints from a cohort of 14 arthritis patients recruited from the University of Michigan Rheumatology clinic. Facilitated by the high-quality 3D PA and US images, volumetric assessment of multiple functional and morphological imaging biomarkers in localized articular tissues reflecting the activity of arthritis were quantified, including hyperemia, blood oxygenation, US Doppler (i.e., blood flow), joint space echogenicity, synovial volume, and joint space volume. The imaging biomarkers quantified from DL-based tissue segmentation and processing were compared to those quantified from manual tissue segmentation conducted by board certified radiologists as the gold standard. With the longitudinal imaging measurements both before and after the initiation of the medical treatment, the association between each of these imaging biomarkers and the disease activity as indicated by the joint specific patient Clinical Assessment Questionnaire (CAQ) scores was studied via linear mixed model. This revealed which imaging biomarkers are primarily sensitive to the activity of arthritis and its change in response to the treatment. In addition, to evaluate whether multiple imaging biomarkers, facilitated by this multi-modal imaging system, can better reflect disease condition compared to those from a single imaging modality, we fit three linear mixed models combining different groups of imaging biomarkers, including the biomarkers from PA imaging, from US imaging, and from PA-US dual-modality imaging, and quantified and compared their predictive performance on disease activity as indicated by the joint specific CAQ scores.

Building on prior developments [15], [16], [17], the present work makes the following two contributions:

•An integrated, fully automated end-to-end pipeline. We integrate robotic scanning, automatic deep learning-based segmentation, quantitative biomarker extraction, and the relation of these biomarkers to clinical severity into a single unified system. This integration removes the operator dependence and manual analysis inherent to the separate components and represents a necessary step towards the clinical translation of PA imaging in rheumatology.

Methods

Patient recruitment and study

All imaging procedures were conducted in accordance with a protocol approved by the institutional review board of the University of Michigan Medical School (PI: Xueding Wang; protocol #: HUM00003693). Participants were recruited between June 2022 and December 2023 by certified rheumatologists at the University of Michigan Rheumatology Clinic. Women are more likely to develop arthritis than men [18]. All subjects involved in this study were women over 18 years old, with noticeable swelling, pain, and active synovitis on physical exam in at least one of their finger joints at the initial clinic visit. Joint specific disease activity was assessed by joint specific CAQ scores. The joint-specific CAQ score was modeled after the Patient Global Assessment (PGA), which is part of the Clinical Disease Activity Index (CDAI). PGA is one of the most widely used patient-reported outcomes (PROs) in RA practice and research, and is often assessed by questions with 0–10 or 0–100 responses to reflect patient’s perspective of disease activity [19], [20], [21]. In total, 19 subjects were recruited, and 14 subjects among them (median age, 58 ± 14 years) were able to finish the longitudinal study, including imaging of affected joints both before the initiation of the medical treatment and during each biweekly follow-up visit after the treatment, for assessment of their response to the treatment. Patient study flow diagram.

To recruit each patient, a full explanation of the study was provided, and written informed consent from the patient was received. The patient’s identification information was removed to achieve compliance with HIPAA. After recruitment of each patient, the patient’s condition of RA was evaluated by the board-certified rheumatologists following the American College of Rheumatology (ACR) criteria. After confirming the patient’s condition, including identifying the symptomatic finger joints with obvious inflammation as the targeted joints for imaging assessment, the patient entered the longitudinal study. Prior to the initiation of medication-based treatment, baseline 3D US imaging and PA imaging of specifically targeted inflamed joints, as diagnosed by the rheumatologists, were conducted. After the medication-based treatment started, each patient had biweekly follow-up examinations. We repeated 3D US and PA imaging during each visit for the same identified joints. During each visit, a CAQ was conducted by using the questionnaire. Patients’ joint-specific CAQ scores were used for association with joint-specific biomarkers.

We studied a total of 43 joints from the 14 patients who finished the longitudinal study. Each joint underwent 3 to 4 scans at different time points of biweekly visits starting from pre-initiation of medication. During each visit, 3D US imaging and PA imaging of targeted joints were acquired. During the studies of the first 8 patients, the dual-wavelength PA imaging at 750 nm and 850 nm was not ready for use, so PA imaging was conducted at a single wavelength of 750 nm only. Hence, for each of the 18 joints from the first 8 patients, we longitudinally assessed 5 imaging biomarkers, including blood flow, hyperemia, joint space echogenicity, synovial volume, and joint space volume. For each of the 25 joints from the remaining 6 patients, dual-wavelength PA imaging at 750 nm and 850 nm was conducted during each patient visit, facilitating an additional imaging biomarker of blood oxygenation, increasing the total number of quantified imaging biomarkers to 6.

Three-dimensional (3D) automated PA-US dual-modality imaging system

The schematic of the automated PA-US dual-modality imaging system for human inflammatory arthritis is shown in Fig. 2(a) with the customized probe holder shown in Fig. 2(b). Scanning was performed using a 6-DOF UR3 robotic arm (Universal Robots), with a repeatability of ±0.1 mm and a reach of 500 mm. The US/PA probe was mounted on the end-effector via a custom holder. A Nd:YAG OPO tunable laser (Phocus MOBILE, OPOTEK Inc.) was used as the PA excitation source, coupled with a custom-designed fiber bundle. The laser operated at a repetition rate of 10 Hz with a pulse width of 5 ns. At 750 nm, the laser light fluence on the skin was approximately 20 mJ cm-2, which was below the ANSI maximum permissible exposure limit of 25.2 mJ cm-2 for skin at this wavelength (ANSI Z136.1). US and PA image streaming were performed using a GEHC Vivid™ E95 cardiovascular system equipped with an L8–18i-D high-frequency linear array probe. For PA acquisition, the laser trigger output was used to synchronize image capture. The system interface allowed seamless switching between B-mode US, Doppler US, and PA imaging modes. More technical details of this imaging system are described in our previous publications [12], [15], [22]. A custom-designed hand rest was used to minimize patient hand motion during scanning. Finger joint locations were identified using an RGB-D camera mounted on the system, with detection performed via OpenPose (Carnegie Mellon University) [23], [24]. The robot executed a linear trajectory around each joint, acquiring multiple 2D scans for subsequent 3D reconstruction. The scanning parameters are adjustable, including the span, the probe speed, and the number of 2D images scanned.

The platform operates in two complementary acquisition modes: (i) B-mode US paired with dual-wavelength PA imaging, and (ii) B-mode US paired with Doppler US. PA excitation was performed at two wavelengths, 750 nm and 850 nm, selected to enable spectral discrimination between oxygenated and deoxygenated hemoglobin. The three modalities were continuously streamed at their native frame rates (B-mode US: 23 fps; PA: 65 fps; Doppler US: 12 fps). Tap water was used as the acoustic coupling medium; hot and cold water was mixed to reach near body temperature (34–39 °C), and the water was then degassed to remove air bubbles.

For B-mode US + PA acquisition, the robot moved continuously along a linear trajectory while image pairs were captured throughout the movement. As a representative setting, 135 image pairs were acquired over a 2 cm span in 7.4 s.

For B-mode US + Doppler US acquisition, a move–stop mode was used instead: the robot moved to each predefined position along the linear trajectory, paused for a second (which is adjustable), and acquired an image pair before proceeding to the next location. This approach is essential because Doppler US is highly sensitive to motion, and continuous robotic movement introduces significant flow artifacts. Upon the robot’s arrival at each acquisition position, the most recently streamed image pair (B-mode US + Doppler US) was captured. As a representative setting, 100 image pairs were acquired over a 2 cm span in 180 s. B-mode US and Doppler US imaging were performed at transducer frequencies of 7.0 MHz and 7.1 MHz, respectively.

A Linux-based control computer coordinated robotic motion, image acquisition, user interface, path planning, and joint localization throughout the scan. The acquired images were reconstructed into a 3D volume upon completion of the scan. A photograph of the whole imaging system and a volunteer whose hand is being imaged is shown in Fig. 2(c).

Analysis

Image reconstruction and quantification of imaging biomarkers

Real-time PA imaging was enabled by configuring the VE95’s parallel delay-and-sum beamforming algorithm for one-way US propagation of the PA signal [12]. The PA signals, after being processed with a bandpass filter centered at 12.5 MHz and a bandwidth of 3.8 MHz, were used to create a PA image using the power of the beamformed signals following a single laser pulse illumination. The live streaming of US and PA B-mode combined image, similar to US Doppler imaging, was displayed on the VE 95 US machine and was used for biomarker quantification. With the 3D US B-mode image of each joint, the joint space and the synovial region were segmented both manually and by using DL-based automated segmentation. For manually segmented synovial and joint space masks, our radiologists only sparsely annotated 5 to 7 frames (i.e., 2D slides from the 3D scan) per joint volume due to large dataset size. To extend these sparse annotations to the entire joint volume, we applied the 1st mask (from the 1st annotated frame) to the 1st frame through the frame just before the 2nd annotated frame. This process was repeated for subsequent annotations, applying the nth mask to the remaining frames after the nth annotated frame, which allowed us to have a mask for every frame for the entire joint. For DL-based segmented masks, we applied the dense masks onto the US B-mode, US Doppler, and PA images in one-to-one correspondence.

With the joint space and synovial volume segmented, we then quantified six morphological and functional imaging biomarkers from the acquired 3D US B-mode, US power Doppler, and PA images, including US power Doppler, joint space echogenicity, synovial volume, joint space volume, hyperemia, and blood oxygenation. In this study, the US power Doppler as a functional biomarker of arthritis was quantified by averaging the US power Doppler signal intensities within the joint space-masked 3D US Doppler image. Clinically, the enhanced blood flow within the synovium indicates increased vascularity associated with inflammation which can be visualized by US color or power Doppler US. The joint space echogenicity was quantified by the average gray pixel intensity level in the joint space normalized by the average gray pixel intensity level in the subcutaneous fat in the 3D US B-mode image. A more “hypoechoic” (darker) appearance on US image often indicates increased synovial thickening with or without fluid, which can be a sign of active inflammation in a joint. The synovial volume was quantified by counting the total number of voxels in the segmented synovial mask using the 3D US B-mode image. The enlarged size of a joint’s synovium reflects the synovial fluid buildup and is an indicator of synovitis which can be assessed by US. The joint space volume was quantified by counting the total number of voxels in the segmented joint space mask in the 3D US B-mode image. Joint space thickening in the context of arthritis usually refers to a visible sign on X-ray or US where the tissues around the joint space are swelling and thickening due to inflammation. The above four US-based imaging biomarkers are frequently used in rheumatology and radiology clinics to assess the activity of arthritis [25], [26]. Facilitated by PA imaging, two additional biomarkers associated with soft tissue inflammation were assessed, including hyperemia and blood oxygenation. Hyperemia reflecting the increased blood volume in the joint space due to inflammation was quantified by averaging the PA signal intensity in the voxels within the joint space-masked 3D PA image acquired at 750 nm. Blood oxygenation in the joint space was quantified from the PA images at 750 nm and 850 nm by considering the distinctive optical absorption spectra of oxygenated hemoglobin and deoxygenated hemoglobin [27]. As demonstrated in our previous studies and those by other groups [8], [9], [11], [16], [28], the blood oxygenation levels in inflammatory arthritic tissues are relatively low, which is due to the increased metabolic demand and the relatively inadequate oxygen delivery to inflammatory synovial tissue [29].

Modeling and results

Development and validation of the deep learning model for automated segmentation

In order to achieve automated segmentation of joint space and synovial region for quantitative assessment of the US and PA imaging biomarkers, we have developed a deep-learning (DL)-based segmentation pipeline that utilizes the 3D Deep Attentive Feature (DAF3D)-based model to address the challenges in finger joint tissue segmentation in 3D US images [31]. A total of N = 43 3D US images of patients’ finger joints were acquired, including metacarpophalangeal (MCP) joints, proximal interphalangeal (PIP) joints, and distal interphalangeal (DIP) joints. To train the DL-based segmentation, board-certified radiologists sparsely annotated the joint space region in the 3D US images by manually delineating the dark gray/black (hypoechoic) area above the finger bones across multiple 2D slices using ITK-SNAP software [32]. Similarly, the synovial area was also sparsely annotated. Corresponding 3D dense annotations for both the joint space and synovial region were generated using our previous method, a DL-based automated sparse-to-dense annotation model [17]. To artificially enhance the size and diversity of training dataset, various offline data augmentation techniques from TorchIO [33], a Python library designed for medical imaging augmentation, were applied on the US volumes and the corresponding dense 3-D annotations. These techniques included horizontal/vertical flip, random rotation, random brightness/contrast adjustment, random Gaussian blur, and random noise.

The architecture of the DAF3D network is illustrated in Fig. 5. The DAF3D network architecture employs a 3D ResNeXt backbone [34] enhanced with dilated convolutions to capture multi-scale contextual information, which is crucial for accurately delineating the complex anatomical structures of human finger joints. The key innovation of DAF3D lies in its attention mechanism, which refines multi-layer features by computing attention weights at each layer, thereby enhancing feature maps at different resolutions. For segmenting the joint space and synovial region, the DAF3D model processes the 3D US data to extract detailed features from the input images. The network’s ability to integrate multi-level features through its attention modules allows for the precise identification of boundaries and textures specific to these regions. This capability is particularly beneficial in 3D US, where image quality can be variable, and the structures of interest are often small and complex. The manually segmented masks generated by the radiologists were used directly as the ground-truth labels for both training and validation. The DAF3D model was trained using a hybrid loss function that combines Dice loss and cross-entropy loss. Dice loss is particularly effective for addressing class imbalance by maximizing the overlap between the predicted segmentation and the ground truth. Cross-entropy loss complements this by penalizing misclassifications, thereby improving the overall accuracy of the segmentation. The training process was optimized using the Adam optimizer, with the learning rate set to 10−4. This setup ensures a stable and gradual convergence of the model, leveraging the adaptive learning rate capabilities of the Adam optimizer.

DL-based joint tissue segmentation results

To evaluate the performance of the DAF3D model in terms of its accuracy in segmenting human finger joint tissues including the joint space and the synovial region using the manual segmentation results as the ground truth, we employed a 6-fold cross-validation approach to compare the segmentation results from the DL-based method and the manual method. This approach ensures robust assessment and validation by dividing the dataset into six subsets. In each fold, five subsets are used for training, while the remaining subset is used for validation. This process is repeated six times, with each subset serving as the validation set once.

Fig. 6 illustrates an example of automated DAF3D segmentation for both the joint space and the synovial region. The 2D slice images, along with their corresponding ground truth (GT) labels and segmentation masks, are extracted from the 3D volume. The performance metrics used to evaluate the DAF3D segmentation model include the Dice score and Intersection over Union (IoU), computed across 6-fold cross-validation. The summarized performance metrics are presented in Table 2. Overall, the DAF3D model achieved a Dice score of 0.769 ± 0.026 and an IoU of 0.639 ± 0.031. Both evaluation metrics confirmed the good performance of the automated DAF3D segmentation [35], [36]. Additionally, this level of agreement is comparable to the inter-observer Dice coefficients reported among expert readers performing manual segmentation of soft-tissue lesions on similar imaging modalities. Webb et al. reported pairwise inter-expert Dice values of 0.79–0.81 for breast lesion segmentation in ultrasound among three trained radiologists and sonographers [37], and Covert et al. reported a mean inter-observer Dice of 0.79 among three board-certified radiologists contouring hepatocellular carcinoma lesions on contrast-enhanced MRI [38]. The performance of the proposed model therefore falls within the range of agreement observed among expert readers themselves, supporting its suitability for clinical application.

Comparing imaging biomarkers based on DL automated segmentation vs. Manual segmentation

We studied a total of 43 joints from the 14 patients, each scanned at 3–4 different time points, which led to a total of 155 joint imaging results. For each of the 155 joint imaging results, the joint space and the synovial region were segmented both manually by radiologists and automatically by DL-based segmentation. With the joint space and the synovial region segmented from the 3D US image of the joint, either manually or automatically, we quantified the six imaging biomarkers including hyperemia, US power Doppler, joint space echogenicity, joint space volume, synovial volume, and blood oxygenation. Then we compared the quantified imaging biomarkers based on manual segmentation to those based on DL segmentation. The total data points for hyperemia, US power Doppler, joint space echogenicity, joint space volume, and synovial volume are 155 (i.e., N = 155). The total data points for blood oxygenation are 85 (i.e., N = 85). This is because dual-wavelength PA imaging of blood oxygenation was only conducted on the 25 joints of the 6 patients.

The six panels in Fig. 7 show the six quantified imaging biomarkers, where in each panel the results based on manual segmentation are compared to those based on DL automated segmentation. To assess the agreement between the imaging biomarkers based on manual segmentation performed by radiologists vs. those based on DL segmentation, we calculated the Pearson’s correlation coefficient R for each of the six sets of the quantified imaging biomarkers. The Pearson’s correlation coefficient R ranging from 0.41 to 0.86 exhibits moderate to very strong agreement between the two groups of results [39], indicating that, using the results from the manual segmentations as the gold standard, our DL algorithm utilizing the DAF3D model showed satisfactory performance in assessing the PA and US imaging biomarkers reflecting the activity of arthritis. As illustrated in Fig. 7, functional biomarkers (PA hyperemia, blood oxygenation, and US Doppler flow) show lower correlation compared to structural biomarkers (joint space volume, synovium volume, echogenicity). Expected reason of such results is as follows: The manual ground truth was annotated on every fifth slice, whereas the DL model segmented all slices; this difference in annotation density contributes to the deviation observed for the functional biomarkers. In addition, structural biomarkers represent aggregate measures over the entire segmented region, so segmentation errors have limited impact on the final value. In contrast, functional biomarkers are often concentrated near the tissue boundary and margin regions, precisely where segmentation errors are most likely to occur. This spatial alignment between functional signal concentration and segmentation error regions results in greater sensitivity of functional biomarkers to boundary misalignment. While our segmentation performance (Dice = 0.769) is comparable to expert inter-observer agreement, this level of boundary variation has a more pronounced effect on functional signal quantification.

Studying the associations between imaging biomarkers and patients’ disease activity scores

To evaluate how each imaging biomarker can reflect the arthritis disease activity using the patients’ joint specific CAQ scores as the gold standard, we applied linear mixed models (LMMs) [40] to fit each of the six imaging biomarkers to the patients’ joint specific CAQ scores. The choice of LMM over standard linear regression model was justified because each joint was scanned multiple times at different time points, resulting in non-independent data points. LMMs are well-suited for handling such repeated measures, as they account for the association within the data by including random effects that capture the variability between joints or patients. The patients’ joint specific CAQ scores were obtained for each imaged joint at every visit, and all the values were normalized by the baseline joint specific CAQ scores recorded during the first visit. Each of the imaging biomarkers obtained at every visit was also normalized by the baseline measurement from the first scan during the first visit. For each of the LMM models of the six imaging biomarkers, we reported the regression coefficient (i.e., beta), proportion of variance explained (R2), and p-value, as shown in Table 3. All statistical analyses were performed using MATLAB and R Studio.

The outcomes indicated again that the LMM combining the biomarkers facilitated by the PA-US dual-modality imaging achieved better predictive accuracy of the patients’ disease conditions than the LMM based on the biomarkers from a single imaging modality.

Discussion and conclusion

Several previous clinical studies have demonstrated that, with high sensitivity in identifying the functional changes in soft tissues affected by inflammation, the emerging photoacoustic (PA) imaging combined with established ultrasound (US) imaging may allow early detection of inflammatory arthritis so critical disease modifying anti-rheumatic drugs can be started thus preventing further joint damage and minimizing negative downstream disease consequences. To promote the translation of PA imaging to rheumatology clinics, in this work we developed an intelligent 3D PA-US dual-modality system powered by a robot for automatic scanning of both PA and US images and a DL algorithm for automatic image segmentation and biomarker analysis. This imaging system enables quantitative and volumetric assessment of multiple morphological and functional biomarkers in human peripheral joints reflecting the activity of arthritis. To assess the clinical viability of this intelligent PA-US imaging system as well as its performance in assessing the arthritis condition and responses to medical treatment, we conducted a prospective study involving 43 individual joints from 14 patients. A limitation of the current dataset is that the patient cohort consisted exclusively of female participants (Table 1). This reflects the known epidemiology of rheumatoid arthritis, which predominantly affects women, as well as the composition of the available patient cohort during the study period. Nevertheless, we expect the proposed biomarkers to remain applicable to male patients: the structures imaged by our platform—the synovium, joint space, bone surface, and periarticular vasculature—are anatomically common to both sexes and exhibit comparable appearances in ultrasound, and the PA- and Doppler-derived biomarkers reflect the underlying inflammatory pathophysiology of the joint rather than sex-specific characteristics. Future studies incorporating male participants and a broader demographic range will be conducted to fully establish the generalizability of the proposed platform.

This patient study demonstrated that this imaging system powered by a robotic arm could automatically acquire 3D volumetric B-mode US with either US Doppler image or PA image with high image quality, good safety, and satisfactory repeatability. The DL algorithm utilizing the DAF3D model was able to segment joint space and synovial region in the human finger joint automatically and achieved a Dice score of 0.77±0.03 and an IoU of 0.64±0.03 when comparing to the manual segmentations made by board-certified radiologists as the gold standard. Due to its automatic features and 3D volumetric measurements, this system could robustly repeat imaging scans of the same joints during follow-up patient visits, facilitating longitudinal patient assessment with satisfactory reproducibility.

Although only patients with noticeable symptoms were recruited for the current study, our results suggest the potential for earlier detection. Our previous longitudinal study [15] demonstrated that PA- and Doppler-derived biomarkers tracked the progression of joint inflammation from its early course. Because these biomarkers are sensitive to the vascular activity associated with active inflammation, PA and Doppler US allow earlier detection of arthritis than conventional modalities such as X-ray, which is sensitive only to late-stage structural bone damage such as bone erosion and joint space narrowing.

From a practical standpoint, the imaging workflow is designed to be efficient and to require minimal operator involvement. While the scans in this study used a dense sampling of images for data collection purposes, a routine clinical scan requires far fewer images. Given an azimuthal resolution of approximately 4.1 mm for the US probe, the Nyquist sampling theorem indicates that an inter-image spacing of 2 mm (approximately half the azimuthal resolution) is sufficient to capture the available spatial information with minimal loss; for a 2 cm scan range, this corresponds to only 11 images per joint. Under this setting, the B-mode US + PA mode completes acquisition in under 10 s per joint, and the B-mode US + Doppler US mode, which requires a move–stop acquisition, completes in under 20 s per joint. Operator interaction is limited to initiating the system: finger localization is performed automatically by an RGB-D camera, scanning is carried out by the robotic arm, and biomarker measurement and assessment are subsequently handled by the deep learning model. This high degree of automation reduces dependence on operator skill and supports consistent, reproducible acquisition. The main barrier to routine clinical deployment is regulatory: while the current system is validated as a research platform, formal regulatory approval would be required before adoption in routine clinical practice.

Within the DL-based automatically segmented regions of interest in joint, including joint space and synovial region, we were able to quantify six morphological and physiological imaging biomarkers. The LMMs applied to fit individual imaging biomarkers to the disease activity scores indicated that hyperemia, blood oxygenation, US Doppler, and joint space echogenicity showed statistically significant association with the patients’ joint specific CAQ scores. Among the six imaging biomarkers, hyperemia measured by PA imaging achieved the strongest association (R2=0.41) with the disease activity, followed by blood oxygenation (R2=0.30) and US Doppler (R2=0.05). The substantially stronger association of PA-derived hyperemia compared with US Doppler is consistent with the differing sensitivities of the two modalities: Doppler US primarily detects fast flow in larger vessels, whereas PA imaging is sensitive to the slow-flow microvasculature characteristic of active inflammation. As a result, PA imaging more directly captures the angiogenic microvascular changes that underlie disease activity, while the two modalities provide complementary information.

The outcomes from the LMMs utilizing the biomarkers from PA imaging (R2=0.41), the biomarkers from US imaging (R2=0.26), and all the biomarkers (R2=0.52), respectively, as well as additional ANOVA tests, indicated that the LMM combining all the biomarkers facilitated by the PA-US dual-modality imaging could achieve better predictive accuracy of the patients’ disease condition. In particular, our analysis suggests that hyperemia, US Doppler, joint space echogenicity, and blood oxygenation are the most informative biomarkers and provide complementary information on disease activity. In our previous studies [8], [9], we have demonstrated that hypoxia in synovial tissue is a hallmark of arthritis. The findings from this study confirmed this. As shown in Table 3, blood oxygenation, when analyzed alone, was significantly associated with the patient’s condition. However, when blood oxygenation and hyperemia were fitted simultaneously in the model (Table 4), the contribution from hyperemia to the LMM is significant (p<0.001) while the contribution of blood oxygenation was comparatively weaker (p<0.05). This may be due to the fact that, despite the association between blood oxygenation and the disease activity, the contribution from the blood oxygenation in the LMMs could overlap with other biomarkers in the models, e.g., hyperemia. This lack of improvement may be due to the moderate correlation between blood oxygenation and hyperemia. The calculated correlation coefficient of R=0.62 (N = 85) between them suggested that these two biomarkers share overlapping information.

In conclusion, this initial study on arthritis patients demonstrates the clinical feasibility of the newly developed intelligent PA-US dual-modality automated imaging system, for quantitative assessment of arthritis disease progression and response to medical treatment. This system is powered by a robot and a DL algorithm. It can perform automated scanning of 3D structural and functional PA and US images, and automated tissue segmentation to facilitate quantitative analyses of multiple imaging biomarkers reflecting the activity of arthritis. The outcome from the initial patient study demonstrated strong associations between the imaging biomarkers and the patients’ joint specific CAQ scores, which demonstrated potential value of this system to radiology and rheumatology clinics for timely diagnosis and treatment monitoring of arthritis with improved accuracy and reduced cost.
