Two-dose measles vaccination in a post-elimination era: lessons from Japan's 2026 school outbreak
Authors: Yudai Kaneda, Masahiro Kami
Published: 2026

Dear Editor

Measles is resurging in both Japan and the United States, a trend often attributed to declining vaccination coverage after the COVID-19 pandemic. In the United States, 1893 confirmed measles cases had been reported in 2026 by May 14, and MMR coverage among kindergartners declined from 95.2% in 2019–20 to 92.5% in 2024–25 [1]. Japan has also seen recent declines in measles-rubella vaccine coverage: second-dose coverage fell from 92.4% in fiscal year 2022 to 91.0% in fiscal year 2024 [2].

However, Japan's experience suggests that lower coverage alone may not explain the resurgence. By April 15, 2026, Japan had reported 299 measles cases, exceeding the 265 cases reported during all of 2025; people aged 15–39 years accounted for 71% of infections, and 210 cases were considered domestically acquired [3]. Japan had already eliminated endemic measles transmission and introduced routine two-dose measles-rubella vaccination in 2006, with the second dose given before primary school entry.

The most important signal came from a school outbreak in Tokyo. In April, measles spread in an elementary school in Shinjuku Ward, increasing to 47 cases, including 41 pupils; none had a recent history of overseas travel [4]. Importantly, most cases with known vaccination history had reportedly been vaccinated [4]. This outbreak is therefore not simply a story of unvaccinated children. Rather, it occurred in a school-age cohort in which almost all pupils should have completed the two-dose schedule under Japan's routine immunisation programme.

JIHS seroepidemiological data support this concern. In 2024, EIA titres of 2.0 or higher were present in 96.6% of participants, whereas titres of 4.0 or higher were present in only 86.6%; the proportion with titres of 16.0 or higher was below 50% among those aged 8–47 years [5]. Notably, people aged 50 years or older, cohorts with no or only one routine measles vaccine dose, maintained higher antibody titres than younger two-dose cohorts [5]. These data suggest that the immunity induced by routine two-dose vaccination may not always be sufficient to prevent transmission under intense exposure, such as in classrooms where prolonged and repeated close contact occurs.

These observations suggest that the issue is not whether two-dose vaccination is valuable; it remains the foundation of measles control. Rather, the question is whether a two-dose-centred strategy remains sufficient in post-elimination societies where natural boosting has largely disappeared and imported measles can enter dense settings such as schools. This concern is not limited to schools. Health-care workers with insufficient or waning immunity could also amplify transmission in hospitals, where delayed recognition of measles may expose infants, pregnant people, immunocompromised patients, and other vulnerable groups. Maintaining measles elimination will require high routine coverage, age-specific immunity monitoring, reliable vaccination records, rapid detection of school and health-care clusters, and catch-up or additional vaccination for groups with insufficient immunity or high exposure risk, particularly school staff, health-care workers, and international travellers. The durability of vaccine-induced immunity and the possible role of booster strategies or next-generation measles vaccines should also be re-examined.
