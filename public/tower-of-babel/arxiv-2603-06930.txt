Explicit Formulas and Unimodality Phenomena for General Position Polynomials
Bilal Ahmad Rather, [2mm] School of Mathematics, Statistics, Shandong University of Technology, Zibo 255049, China

Abstract
The general position problem in graphs seeks the largest set of vertices such that no three vertices lie on a common geodesic. Its counting refinement, the general position polynomial ψ ⁡ ( G ) \displaystyle\psi(G) , asks for all such possible sets. In this paper, We describe general position sets for several classes of graphs and provide explicit formulas for the general position polynomials of complete multipartite graphs. We specialize to balanced complete multipartite graphs and show that for part size r ≤ 4 \displaystyle r\leq 4 , the polynomial ψ ⁡ ( K r , … , r ) \displaystyle\psi(K_{r,\dots,r}) is log-concave and unimodal for all numbers of parts, while for larger r \displaystyle r , counterexamples show that these properties fail. Finally, we analyze the corona G ∘ K 1 \displaystyle G\circ K_{1} and prove that unimodality of ψ ⁡ ( G ) \displaystyle\psi(G) is retained for numerous natural classes (paths, edgeless graphs, combs). This contributes to an open problem, but the general case remains unknown. The results verify the analogy between general position polynomials and classical position-type parameters, and establish balanced multipartite graphs and coronas as potential subjects for further investigation.



Keywords: General position set, general position number, general position polynomial, unimodality, log-concave

All graphs considered are finite, simple and undirected. For a graph
\displaystyle G

\displaystyle V(G)

2
Preliminaries

We begin with the basic definitions needed in the sequel. Fro a graph
\displaystyle G
, A subset
\displaystyle S\subseteq V(G)
is a
general position set
if there do not exist distinct vertices
\displaystyle x,y,z\in S
such that

d(x,z)=d(x,y)+d(y,z),

that is, no vertex of
\displaystyle S
lies on a shortest path between two other vertices of
\displaystyle S
. The
general position number
of
\displaystyle G
is

\operatorname{gp}(G)=\max\{|S|:S\subseteq V(G)\text{ is a general position set}\}.

For each
\displaystyle k\geq 0
, let
\displaystyle\alpha_{k}(G)
denote the number of general position sets of size
\displaystyle k
. The
general position polynomial
of
\displaystyle G
is

\psi(G)=\sum_{k\geq 0}\alpha_{k}(G)x^{k}.

We note that
\displaystyle\alpha_{0}(G)=1
,
\displaystyle\alpha_{1}(G)=|V(G)|
, and every pair of vertices forms a general position set, so
\displaystyle\alpha_{2}(G)=\binom{|V(G)|}{2}
. Thus, for a graph of order
\displaystyle n
,

\psi(G)=1+nx+\binom{n}{2}x^{2}+\cdots,

and the degree of
\displaystyle\psi(G)
equals
\displaystyle\operatorname{gp}(G)
.

A graph
\displaystyle G
is
complete multipartite
if its vertex set can be partitioned into independent sets
\displaystyle V_{1},\dots,V_{t}
, called the
partite sets
or simply
parts
, such that two vertices are adjacent if and only if they belong to different parts. If
\displaystyle|V_{i}|=n_{i}
for each
\displaystyle i
, we denote such a graph by
\displaystyle K_{n_{1},\dots,n_{t}}.
Thus, complete graphs, complete bipartite graphs and Turán graphs are all special cases of complete multipartite graphs.

The following basic observations will be used repeatedly.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
\displaystyle G
be a connected graph of order
\displaystyle n
.

1.

Every subset of
\displaystyle V(G)
of size at most
\displaystyle 2
is in general position. In particular,

\displaystyle\alpha_{0}(G)=1,\alpha_{1}(G)=n,
and
\displaystyle\alpha_{2}(G)=\binom{n}{2}.

2.

If
\displaystyle H
is an isometric induced subgraph of
\displaystyle G
and
\displaystyle S\subseteq V(H)
, then
\displaystyle S
is a general position set of
\displaystyle H
if and only if it is a general position set of
\displaystyle G
.

Proof.

Part (1) is immediate, since the definition involves three distinct vertices.

For (2), let
\displaystyle x,y,z\in S
be distinct. Since
\displaystyle H
is isometric in
\displaystyle G
, so

d_{H}(x,y)=d_{G}(x,y),\quad d_{H}(y,z)=d_{G}(y,z),\quad d_{H}(x,z)=d_{G}(x,z).

Therefore, we have

d_{H}(x,z)=d_{H}(x,y)+d_{H}(y,z)\iff d_{G}(x,z)=d_{G}(x,y)+d_{G}(y,z).

Hence no vertex of
\displaystyle S
lies on a shortest path between two others in
\displaystyle H
if and only if the same holds in
\displaystyle G
.
∎

The following result records the distances in complete multipartite graphs.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
\displaystyle G=K_{n_{1},\dots,n_{t}}
with
\displaystyle t\geq 2
.

1.

If
\displaystyle u,v
lie in different parts, then
\displaystyle d(u,v)=1
.

2.

If
\displaystyle u\neq v
lie in the same part, then
\displaystyle d(u,v)=2
.

Proof.

If
\displaystyle u,v
lie in different parts, they are adjacent by definition, so
\displaystyle d(u,v)=1
. If
\displaystyle u,v
lie in the same part, they are non-adjacent, However, since
\displaystyle t\geq 2
there exists a vertex
\displaystyle w
in a different part, and then
\displaystyle u\sim w\sim v
, so
\displaystyle d(u,v)\leq 2
. Hence, we obtain
\displaystyle d(u,v)=2
.
∎

3
General position polynomials of complete multipartite graphs

In this section, we characterize general position sets in complete multipartite graphs. The description is remarkably simple: a general position set is either contained in a single part or contains at most one vertex from each part.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
\displaystyle G=K_{n_{1},\dots,n_{t}}
with
\displaystyle t\geq 2
, and let
\displaystyle V_{1},\dots,V_{t}
be its partite sets. A subset
\displaystyle S\subseteq V(G)
is a general position set if and only if one of the following holds:

(A)

\displaystyle S
is contained in a single part, that is,
\displaystyle S\subseteq V_{i}
for some
\displaystyle i\in[t]
, or

(B)

\displaystyle S
contains at most one vertex from each part, that is,
\displaystyle|S\cap V_{i}|\leq 1
for all
\displaystyle i\in[t]
.

Proof.

First suppose
\displaystyle S
satisfies (A) or (B). If
\displaystyle S\subseteq V_{i}
, let
\displaystyle u,v\in S
with
\displaystyle u\neq v
. By Lemma
2.2
(2), we have
\displaystyle d(u,v)=2
, and any shortest
\displaystyle u\text{-}v
path has the form
\displaystyle u\sim w\sim v
, where
\displaystyle w
lies in some
\displaystyle V_{j}
with
\displaystyle j\neq i
. Since, all vertices of
\displaystyle S
lie in
\displaystyle V_{i}
, no vertex of
\displaystyle S
can be an internal vertex of such a path. Thus, no vertex of
\displaystyle S
lies on a shortest path between two others, and
\displaystyle S
is in general position.

If (B) holds, then any two vertices
\displaystyle u,v\in S
lie in different parts, so by Lemma
2.2
(1), we obtain
\displaystyle d(u,v)=1
. A shortest
\displaystyle u\text{-}v
path is of length
\displaystyle 1
, and contains no internal vertex. In particular no third vertex of
\displaystyle S
lies on such a path. Hence,
\displaystyle S
is in general position.

Conversely, assume that
\displaystyle S
is a general position set, and does not satisfy (A), that is, it meets at least two parts. Suppose that (B) fails, then there exists a part, say
\displaystyle V_{i}
, such that
\displaystyle|S\cap V_{i}|\geq 2
, and another part, say
\displaystyle V_{j}
with
\displaystyle j\neq i
, such that
\displaystyle S\cap V_{j}\neq\emptyset
. Select unique vertices
\displaystyle u,v\in S\cap V_{i}
, and a vertex
\displaystyle w\in S\cap V_{j}
. According to Lemma
2.2
(2),
\displaystyle d(u,v)=2
, and by Lemma
2.2
(1),
\displaystyle d(u,w)=d(v,w)=1
. Consequently, it implies that that
\displaystyle d(u,v)=2=d(u,w)+d(w,v).
Thus,
\displaystyle w
is situated on a minimal
\displaystyle u\text{-}v
path. This contradicts the assumption that
\displaystyle S
constitutes a set in general position. Hence, if
\displaystyle S
is a general position set not contained in a single part, it must satisfy (B).
∎

As an immediate consequence, we obtain the general position number.

Corollary 3.2
.

Let
\displaystyle G=K_{n_{1},\dots,n_{t}}
with
\displaystyle t\geq 2
, and let
\displaystyle N=\sum_{i=1}^{t}n_{i},
and
\displaystyle M=\max_{1\leq i\leq t}n_{i}.
Then

\displaystyle\operatorname{gp}(G)=\max\{M,t\}.

Proof.

By Theorem
3.1
, a general position set is either contained in a single part, in this case its size is at most
\displaystyle M
, or uses at most one vertex from each of the
\displaystyle t
parts, in this case its size is at most
\displaystyle t
. Any largest part
\displaystyle V_{i}
provides a general position set of size
\displaystyle M
, and any choice of one vertex from each part yields a general position set of size
\displaystyle t
. Hence, we have
\displaystyle\operatorname{gp}(G)=\max\{M,t\}
.
∎

Remark 3.3
.

For complete graphs
\displaystyle K_{n}
we may regard the graph as
\displaystyle K_{1,\dots,1}
with
\displaystyle t=n
. Then
\displaystyle M=1
and
\displaystyle t=n
, so Corollary
3.2
yields
\displaystyle\operatorname{gp}(K_{n})=n
, as expected. For complete bipartite graphs
\displaystyle K_{m,n}
we have
\displaystyle t=2
and
\displaystyle M=\max\{m,n\}
, so
\displaystyle\operatorname{gp}(K_{m,n})=\max\{m,n\}
, recovering the known result
[
10
,
22
]
.

We now derive a closed formula for the GPA of
\displaystyle K_{n_{1},\dots,n_{t}}
. Let
\displaystyle G=K_{n_{1},\dots,n_{t}},
with
\displaystyle V_{1},\dots,V_{t}
as before
\displaystyle|V_{i}|=n_{i},
for
\displaystyle 1\leq i\leq t
. Let
\displaystyle N=\sum_{i=1}^{t}n_{i}
be the order of
\displaystyle G
. By Theorem
3.1
, every general position set is of one of the following two types: (A) sets entirely contained in a single part, and (B) sets that use at most one vertex from each part. We count the number of general position sets of each type and size. For
\displaystyle k\geq 0
, let
\displaystyle\alpha_{k}(G)
denote the number of general position sets of size
\displaystyle k
. A general position set of type (B) of size
\displaystyle k
is obtained by choosing a subset
\displaystyle I\subseteq[t]
of parts with
\displaystyle|I|=k
or choosing one vertex from each
\displaystyle V_{i}
with
\displaystyle i\in I
. Hence, for
\displaystyle k\geq 0
, we have

\alpha_{k}^{(B)}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}I\subseteq[t]\\
|I|=k\end{subarray}}\prod_{i\in I}n_{i}.

(3.1)

The right-hand side is the elementary symmetric polynomial

e_{k}(n_{1},\dots,n_{t})=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}I\subseteq[t]\\
|I|=k\end{subarray}}\prod_{i\in I}n_{i},

with the convention that
\displaystyle e_{0}(n_{1},\dots,n_{t})=1
. A general position set of type (A) of size
\displaystyle k
is obtained by choosing a part
\displaystyle V_{i}
and then choosing a
\displaystyle k
-subset of
\displaystyle V_{i}
. Thus, we have

\alpha_{k}^{(A)}=\sum_{i=1}^{t}\binom{n_{i}}{k}.

(3.2)

Note that for
\displaystyle k\geq 2
, the two types are disjoint: a set of size at least 2 contained in a single part uses two vertices from that part and hence cannot satisfy the at most one per part condition. For
\displaystyle k=0
and
\displaystyle k=1
there is overlap, but we can easily separate these cases.

We can now state and the main formula related to GPA of multipartite graph.

Theorem 3.4
.

Let
\displaystyle G=K_{n_{1},\dots,n_{t}}
with
\displaystyle t\geq 2
and total order
\displaystyle N=\sum_{i=1}^{t}n_{i}
. Let
\displaystyle e_{k}(n_{1},\dots,n_{t})
be the elementary symmetric polynomials in the variables
\displaystyle n_{1},\dots,n_{t}
. Then the GPA of
\displaystyle G
is

\psi(G)=1+Nx+\sum_{k=2}^{d}\left(e_{k}(n_{1},\dots,n_{t})+\sum_{i=1}^{t}\binom{n_{i}}{k}\right)x^{k},

(3.3)

where
\displaystyle d=\operatorname{gp}(G)=\max\Bigl\{t,\ \max_{1\leq i\leq t}n_{i}\Bigr\}.
Equivalently,
\displaystyle\alpha_{0}(G)=1,\alpha_{1}(G)=N,
and, for
\displaystyle k\geq 2
,

\alpha_{k}(G)=e_{k}(n_{1},\dots,n_{t})+\sum_{i=1}^{t}\binom{n_{i}}{k},

(3.4)

with the understanding that
\displaystyle e_{k}(n_{1},\dots,n_{t})=0
, if
\displaystyle k>t
, and
\displaystyle\binom{n_{i}}{k}=0
, if
\displaystyle k>n_{i}
.

Proof.

By Lemma
2.1
,
\displaystyle\alpha_{0}(G)=1
and
\displaystyle\alpha_{1}(G)=N
. For each
\displaystyle k\geq 2
, every general position set of size
\displaystyle k
is either of type (A) or type (B), and the two types are disjoint. Thus
\displaystyle\alpha_{k}(G)=\alpha_{k}^{(A)}+\alpha_{k}^{(B)}
for
\displaystyle k\geq 2
. Using (
3.1
) and (
3.2
) we have (
3.4
) as

\alpha_{k}(G)=e_{k}(n_{1},\dots,n_{t})+\sum_{i=1}^{t}\binom{n_{i}}{k},

The degree of
\displaystyle\psi(G)
equals
\displaystyle\operatorname{gp}(G)
, which is given by Corollary
3.2
, hence the sum in (
3.3
) runs up to
\displaystyle d=\operatorname{gp}(G)
.
∎

We illustrate Theorem
3.4
on a few standard examples. A complete graph
\displaystyle K_{n}
is viewed as a complete multipartite graph with
\displaystyle t=n
parts of size
\displaystyle 1
. Thus, for
\displaystyle k\geq 2
,
\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\binom{n_{i}}{k}=0,
while

\displaystyle e_{k}(1,\dots,1)=\binom{n}{k}.
So, we obtain

\psi(K_{n})=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom{n}{k}x^{k}=(1+x)^{n},

and is in accordance with
[
15
, Proposition 2.1(i)]
.

Let
\displaystyle G=K_{m,n}
with
\displaystyle m\geq n
. Then
\displaystyle t=2
,
\displaystyle n_{1}=m
,
\displaystyle n_{2}=n
. For
\displaystyle k\geq 2
,

e_{k}(m,n)=\begin{cases}mn;&k=2,\\
0;&k\geq 3,\end{cases}

and
\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{2}\binom{n_{i}}{k}=\binom{m}{k}+\binom{n}{k}.
Thus, we have
\displaystyle\alpha_{0}(G)=1,\alpha_{1}(G)=m+n,\alpha_{2}(G)=mn+\binom{m}{2}+\binom{n}{2}=\binom{m+n}{2},

and for
\displaystyle k\geq 3
,
\displaystyle\alpha_{k}(G)=\binom{m}{k}+\binom{n}{k}.

Therefore, the GPA of
\displaystyle K_{m,n}
is

\psi(K_{m,n})=1+(m+n)x+\binom{m+n}{2}x^{2}+\sum_{k=3}^{m}\left(\binom{m}{k}+\binom{n}{k}\right)x^{k},

which coincides with the expression for
\displaystyle\psi(K_{m,n})
previously obtained in
[
15
, Proposition 2.1(v)]
.

Example 3.5
.

Let
\displaystyle G=K_{r,\dots,r}
be a complete multipartite graph including
\displaystyle t
partitions, each of equal size
\displaystyle r\geq 1
.Then
\displaystyle n_{i}=r
for all
\displaystyle i\in[t]
, and for each
\displaystyle k
, we have
\displaystyle e_{k}(r,\dots,r)=\binom{t}{k}r^{k}
, and
\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{t}\binom{n_{i}}{k}=t\binom{r}{k}.
Thus for
\displaystyle k\geq 2
,

\alpha_{k}(G)=\binom{t}{k}r^{k}+t\binom{r}{k},

(3.5)

and

\psi(K_{r,\dots,r})=1+trx+\sum_{k=2}^{\max\{r,t\}}\left(\binom{t}{k}r^{k}+t\binom{r}{k}\right)x^{k}.

(3.6)

We will consider these graphs again in next Section
4
.

4
Balanced complete multipartite graphs: unimodality and log-concavity

In this section, we investigate the coefficient sequence of
\displaystyle\psi(K_{r,\dots,r})
when all parts have equal size
\displaystyle r
. For balanced complete multipartite graphs, we have the explicit formula (
3.5
) with
\displaystyle\alpha_{0}=1,\alpha_{1}=tr,
and
\displaystyle\alpha_{k}=\binom{t}{k}r^{k}+t\binom{r}{k}
for
\displaystyle k\geq 2.
We first show that for small part size
\displaystyle r\leq 4
, the GPA of
\displaystyle K_{r,\dots,r}
is log-concave and therefore unimodal, independently of the number of parts.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
\displaystyle t\geq 2
and let
\displaystyle K_{r,\dots,r}
be a balanced complete
\displaystyle t
-partite graph
with part size
\displaystyle r\in\{1,2,3,4\}
. Let
\displaystyle\psi(K_{r,\dots,r})=\sum_{k=0}^{d}\alpha_{k}x^{k},
with
\displaystyle d=\max\{r,t\},
and suppose the coefficients are given by
\displaystyle\alpha_{0}=1,\alpha_{1}=tr,
and
\displaystyle\alpha_{k}=\binom{t}{k}r^{k}+t\binom{r}{k}
for
\displaystyle k\geq 2.
Then the sequence
\displaystyle(\alpha_{k})_{k=0}^{d}
is log-concave and hence unimodal.

Proof.
For
\displaystyle r\in\{1,2,3,4\}
and
\displaystyle t\geq 2
we first note that

\displaystyle\alpha_{k}>0
for
\displaystyle 0\leq k\leq d
(and
\displaystyle\alpha_{k}=0
for
\displaystyle k>d
). Hence there are no internal
zeros in
\displaystyle(\alpha_{k})_{k=0}^{d}
, and it suffices to prove log-concavity
[
27
]
.
We consider each case of
\displaystyle r
separately. We need to recall basic facts. For each fixed
\displaystyle t
, the binomial sequence
\displaystyle\left(\binom{t}{k}\right)_{k\geq 0}

is known to be log-concave in
\displaystyle k
, equivalently,

\binom{t}{k}^{2}\geq\binom{t}{k-1}\binom{t}{k+1},\quad\text{for all }k.

Multiplying term wise by
\displaystyle r^{2k}
, we see that the sequence

\displaystyle\left(r^{k}\binom{t}{k}\right)_{k\geq 0}
is also log-concave

\bigl(r^{k}\binom{t}{k}\bigr)^{2}\geq r^{k-1}\binom{t}{k-1}\cdot r^{k+1}\binom{t}{k+1}.

In particular, whenever
\displaystyle\alpha_{k-1},\alpha_{k},\alpha_{k+1}
are all of the form

\displaystyle r^{j}\binom{t}{j}
, the log-concavity inequalities hold trivially.
For
\displaystyle r=1
,
\displaystyle K_{1,\dots,1}\cong K_{t}
is a complete graph and we have (
[
15
, Proposition 2.1(i)]
), we have
\displaystyle\psi(K_{t})=(1+x)^{t}.
Thus,
\displaystyle\alpha_{k}=\binom{t}{k}
, and the sequence
\displaystyle(\alpha_{k})
is strictly log-concave and unimodal.

For
\displaystyle r=2
, and from (
3.5
) we obtain
\displaystyle\alpha_{0}=1,\alpha_{1}=2t,
and, for
\displaystyle k\geq 2
, we have
\displaystyle\alpha_{k}=\binom{t}{k}2^{k}+t\binom{2}{k}.
In particular,
\displaystyle\alpha_{2}=4\binom{t}{2}+t=2t(t-1)+t=t(2t-1),
while for
\displaystyle k\geq 3
, we simply have
\displaystyle\alpha_{k}=2^{k}\binom{t}{k}
. For
\displaystyle k\geq 4
the triple
\displaystyle(\alpha_{k-1},\alpha_{k},\alpha_{k+1})
lies entirely in the
binomial regime, so log-concavity holds by the basic facts. Thus, it
remains to check the indices
\displaystyle k=1,2,3
. For
\displaystyle k=1
, we have
\displaystyle\alpha_{1}^{2}-\alpha_{0}\alpha_{2}=(2t)^{2}-t(2t-1)=t(2t+1)\geq 0,

which holds for all
\displaystyle t\geq 1
. For
\displaystyle k=2
,
\displaystyle\alpha_{1}=2t
,
\displaystyle\alpha_{2}=t(2t-1)
and

\displaystyle\alpha_{3}=8\binom{t}{3}=\tfrac{4}{3}t(t-1)(t-2)
, we obtain

\displaystyle\displaystyle\alpha_{2}^{2}-\alpha_{1}\alpha_{3}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=t^{2}(2t-1)^{2}-(2t)\cdot\frac{4}{3}t(t-1)(t-2)=\frac{t^{2}}{3}\bigl(4t^{2}+12t-13\bigr).

For
\displaystyle t\geq 2
, it follows that
\displaystyle 4t^{2}+12t-13\geq 4t^{2}+12t-16=4(t+4)(t-1)\geq 0
, and the inequality
\displaystyle\alpha_{2}^{2}\geq\alpha_{1}\alpha_{3}
is satisfied.

For
\displaystyle k=3
,
\displaystyle\alpha_{2}=t(2t-1)
,
\displaystyle\alpha_{3}=\tfrac{4}{3}t(t-1)(t-2)
and
\displaystyle\alpha_{4}=16\binom{t}{4}=\tfrac{2}{3}t(t-1)(t-2)(t-3)
. So, we have

\displaystyle\displaystyle\alpha_{3}^{2}-\alpha_{2}\alpha_{4}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\frac{16}{9}t^{2}(t-1)^{2}(t-2)^{2}-t(2t-1)\cdot\frac{2}{3}t(t-1)(t-2)(t-3)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\frac{2t^{2}(t-1)(t-2)}{9}\Bigl(8(t-1)(t-2)-3(2t-1)(t-3)\Bigr)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\frac{2t^{2}(t-1)(t-2)}{9}\bigl(2t^{2}-3t+7\bigr).

The quadratic
\displaystyle 2t^{2}-3t+7
possesses a negative discriminant (
\displaystyle\Delta=9-56<0
) and a positive leading coefficient; therefore, it is positive for all real
\displaystyle t
. Since, also
\displaystyle t^{2}(t-1)(t-2)\geq 0,
for
\displaystyle t\geq 2
, so we obtain

\displaystyle\alpha_{3}^{2}\geq\alpha_{2}\alpha_{4}
. Thus the sequence
\displaystyle(\alpha_{k})
is log-concave for
\displaystyle r=2
.
For
\displaystyle r=3
, from (
3.5
),
\displaystyle\alpha_{0}=1,\alpha_{1}=3t,
and, for
\displaystyle k\geq 2
,
\displaystyle\alpha_{k}=\binom{t}{k}3^{k}+t\binom{3}{k}.
In particular,
\displaystyle\alpha_{2}=9\binom{t}{2}+3t=\frac{3}{2}t(3t-1),

\displaystyle\alpha_{3}=27\binom{t}{3}+t,\alpha_{4}=81\binom{t}{4},\alpha_{5}=243\binom{t}{5},
and so on.
Hence,
\displaystyle\alpha_{k}=3^{k}\binom{t}{k}
for all
\displaystyle k\geq 4
. For
\displaystyle k\geq 5
the triple
\displaystyle(\alpha_{k-1},\alpha_{k},\alpha_{k+1})
lies entirely in the binomial
regime and is log-concave by the preliminary fact. Thus, we only need to check
\displaystyle k=1,2,3,4
. For
\displaystyle k=1
, we have

\alpha_{1}^{2}-\alpha_{0}\alpha_{2}=(3t)^{2}-\frac{3}{2}t(3t-1)=\frac{3t}{2}(3t+1)\geq 0,

which is true for all
\displaystyle t\geq 1
.

For
\displaystyle k=2
, we have

\displaystyle\displaystyle\alpha_{2}^{2}-\alpha_{1}\alpha_{3}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\Bigl(\frac{3}{2}t(3t-1)\Bigr)^{2}-(3t)\bigl(27\binom{t}{3}+t\bigr)=\frac{3t^{2}}{4}\bigl(9t^{2}+36t-37\bigr).

For
\displaystyle t\geq 2
, we derive
\displaystyle 9t^{2}+36t-37\geq 9t^{2}+36t-45=9(t-1)(t+5)\geq 0
, thus concluding that
\displaystyle\alpha_{2}^{2}\geq\alpha_{1}\alpha_{3}
.

For
\displaystyle k=3
, we have

\alpha_{3}^{2}-\alpha_{2}\alpha_{4}=\frac{t^{2}}{16}\bigl(81t^{4}-405t^{3}+1197t^{2}-1971t+1114\bigr)=\frac{t^{2}}{16}P_{3}(t).

The quartic
\displaystyle P_{3}
has positive leading coefficient, and

\displaystyle P_{3}(2)=1600
,
\displaystyle P_{3}(3)=7198
,
\displaystyle P_{3}(4)=21184
, so
\displaystyle P_{3}(t)>0,
for

\displaystyle t=2,3,4
. For
\displaystyle t\geq 5
, we split

P_{3}(t)=81t^{4}-405t^{3}+(1197t^{2}-1971t+1114)\geq 81t^{4}-405t^{3}.

As the bracket is a quadratic with positive leading coefficient, and the
minimum is attained at
\displaystyle t<2
. Hence, it is increasing on
\displaystyle[2,\infty)
, and already
positive at
\displaystyle t=2
. Thus for
\displaystyle t\geq 5
,

P_{3}(t)\geq 81t^{4}-405t^{3}=81t^{3}(t-5)\geq 0.

Combining these cases we have
\displaystyle P_{3}(t)>0
for all
\displaystyle t\geq 2
, and it shows that

\displaystyle\alpha_{3}^{2}\geq\alpha_{2}\alpha_{4}
.

For
\displaystyle k=4
, we have
\displaystyle\alpha_{4}=81\binom{t}{4},
and
\displaystyle\alpha_{5}=243\binom{t}{5}.

So, we have

\alpha_{4}^{2}-\alpha_{3}\alpha_{5}=\frac{81}{320}t^{2}(t-1)(t-2)(t-3)\bigl(9t^{3}-18t^{2}-17t+50\bigr)=\frac{81}{320}t^{2}(t-1)(t-2)(t-3)Q_{3}(t).

For
\displaystyle t\geq 3
the factors
\displaystyle t^{2}(t-1)(t-2)(t-3)
are nonnegative. For the cubic

\displaystyle Q_{3}
, we observe that, for
\displaystyle t\geq 3
,

Q_{3}(t)\geq 9t^{3}-18t^{2}-17t=t(9t^{2}-18t-17).

The quadratic
\displaystyle 9t^{2}-18t-17
possesses a vertex at
\displaystyle t=1
, is growing for
\displaystyle t\geq 1
, and attains a value of
\displaystyle 10
at
\displaystyle t=3
. Consequently,
\displaystyle Q_{3}(t)>0
for all
\displaystyle t\geq 3
. For
\displaystyle t=2
, we verify that
\displaystyle\alpha_{4}=0
and
\displaystyle\alpha_{5}=0
, so the inequality
\displaystyle\alpha_{4}^{2}\geq\alpha_{3}\alpha_{5}
is trivially satisfied. Thus
\displaystyle(\alpha_{k})
is log-concave for
\displaystyle r=3
.

For
\displaystyle r=4
, (
3.5
), gives
\displaystyle\alpha_{0}=1,\alpha_{1}=4t,
and for
\displaystyle k\geq 2
,

\alpha_{k}=\binom{t}{k}4^{k}+t\binom{4}{k}.

In particular,

\displaystyle\alpha_{2}=16\binom{t}{2}+6t=2t(4t-1),\alpha_{3}=64\binom{t}{3}+4t,\alpha_{4}=256\binom{t}{4}+t,\alpha_{5}=1024\binom{t}{5},\alpha_{6}=4096\binom{t}{6},
and so on.
Thus, we obtain
\displaystyle\alpha_{k}=4^{k}\binom{t}{k}
for all
\displaystyle k\geq 5
. As before, for
\displaystyle k\geq 6
the triple
\displaystyle(\alpha_{k-1},\alpha_{k},\alpha_{k+1})
lies entirely in the binomial regime, hence is log-concave. We therefore only need to check
\displaystyle k=1,2,3,4,5
. For
\displaystyle k=1
, we have

\alpha_{1}^{2}-\alpha_{0}\alpha_{2}=(4t)^{2}-2t(4t-1)=2t(4t+1)\geq 0.

For
\displaystyle k=2
, we have

\displaystyle\displaystyle\alpha_{2}^{2}-\alpha_{1}\alpha_{3}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\bigl(2t(4t-1)\bigr)^{2}-(4t)\bigl(64\binom{t}{3}+4t\bigr)=\frac{4t^{2}}{3}\bigl(16t^{2}+72t-73\bigr).

For
\displaystyle t\geq 2
,

16t^{2}+72t-73\geq 16t^{2}+72t-80=8(2t^{2}+9t-10),

and
\displaystyle 2t^{2}+9t-10\geq 2\cdot 4+18-10=16>0
for
\displaystyle t\geq 2
. Thus,

\displaystyle\alpha_{2}^{2}\geq\alpha_{1}\alpha_{3}
.

For
\displaystyle k=3
, we have

\alpha_{3}^{2}-\alpha_{2}\alpha_{4}=\frac{2t^{2}}{9}\bigl(128t^{4}-672t^{3}+2240t^{2}-3972t+2321\bigr)=\frac{2t^{2}}{9}P_{4}(t).

The quartic polynomial
\displaystyle P_{4}
possesses a positive leading coefficient, with
\displaystyle P_{4}(2)=9
,
\displaystyle P_{4}(3)=2789
, and
\displaystyle P_{4}(4)=12033
. Thus,
\displaystyle P_{4}(t)>0
for
\displaystyle t=2,3,4
. For
\displaystyle t\geq 5
, we partition

P_{4}(t)=128t^{4}-672t^{3}+(2240t^{2}-3972t+2321)\geq 128t^{4}-672t^{3}.

The bracket represents a quadratic function in
\displaystyle t
with a positive leading coefficient, indicating that the minimum occurs for
\displaystyle t<2
. Consequently, it is growing on the interval
\displaystyle[2,\infty)
and is positive at
\displaystyle t=2
. Therefore, for
\displaystyle t\geq 5
,

P_{4}(t)\geq 128t^{4}-672t^{3}=128t^{3}(t-5.25)>0.

Consequently,
\displaystyle P_{4}(t)>0
for all integers
\displaystyle t\geq 2
, which indicates that
\displaystyle\alpha_{3}^{2}\geq\alpha_{2}\alpha_{4}
.

For
\displaystyle k=4
, we have

\alpha_{4}^{2}-\alpha_{3}\alpha_{5}=\frac{t^{2}}{45}\Bigl(1024t^{6}-8192t^{5}+20992t^{4}-4160t^{3}-70784t^{2}+116032t-54867\Bigr)=\frac{t^{2}}{45}R_{4}(t).

We express
\displaystyle R_{4}(t)=45+W(t)
and factor
\displaystyle W(t)
as follows:

W(t)=R_{4}(t)-45=64(t-1)(t-2)(t-3)\bigl(16t^{3}-32t^{2}-40t+143\bigr).

For
\displaystyle t\geq 3
, the factor
\displaystyle(t-1)(t-2)(t-3)\geq 0
, and the cubic
\displaystyle 16t^{3}-32t^{2}-40t+143
is increasing on
\displaystyle[3,\infty)
(its derivative has positive leading coefficient and is already positive at
\displaystyle t=3
), and positive at
\displaystyle t=3
. Thus,
\displaystyle 16t^{3}-32t^{2}-40t+143>0
for
\displaystyle t\geq 3
. For
\displaystyle t=2
, we have
\displaystyle W(2)=0
, so it gives

\displaystyle R_{4}(2)=45>0
. Hence,
\displaystyle R_{4}(t)>0
, for all integers
\displaystyle t\geq 2
, and thus we obtain
\displaystyle\alpha_{4}^{2}\geq\alpha_{3}\alpha_{5}
.

For
\displaystyle k=5
,
\displaystyle\alpha_{5}=1024\binom{t}{5}
and
\displaystyle\alpha_{6}=4096\binom{t}{6}
, we have

\displaystyle\displaystyle\alpha_{5}^{2}-\alpha_{4}\alpha_{6}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\frac{256}{675}t^{2}(t-1)(t-2)(t-3)(t-4)\bigl(32t^{4}-160t^{3}+160t^{2}+145t-117\bigr)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\frac{256}{675}t^{2}(t-1)(t-2)(t-3)(t-4)S_{4}(t).

For the index
\displaystyle k=5
to be relevant in the log-concavity condition we need

\displaystyle 5\leq d-1
, that is,
\displaystyle d\geq 6
. Since
\displaystyle r=4
, this forces
\displaystyle t\geq 6
, and thus
all the factors
\displaystyle t^{2}(t-1)(t-2)(t-3)(t-4)
are positive. The quartic
\displaystyle S_{4}
has
positive leading coefficient and one checks that

\displaystyle S_{4}(2)=975
,
\displaystyle S_{4}(3)=4608
,
\displaystyle S_{4}(4)=13425
, hence
\displaystyle S_{4}(t)>0
for

\displaystyle t\geq 2
, and in particular for
\displaystyle t\geq 6
. Therefore, we have
\displaystyle\alpha_{5}^{2}\geq\alpha_{4}\alpha_{6}
. Combining the four cases
\displaystyle r=1,2,3,4
, we conclude that for each
\displaystyle r\in\{1,2,3,4\}
and every
\displaystyle t\geq 2
the coefficient sequence
\displaystyle(\alpha_{k})_{k=0}^{d}
of
\displaystyle\psi(K_{r,\dots,r})
is log-concave. Since all coefficients
\displaystyle\alpha_{k}
with

\displaystyle 0\leq k\leq d
are nonzero, the sequence has no internal zeros, and therefore
it is unimodal (see,
[
27
]
).
∎

Remark 4.2
.

The proof above uses only that the contribution
\displaystyle t\binom{r}{k}
is supported on a small initial segment of the index set when
\displaystyle r
is small, combined with the fact that the pure term
\displaystyle\binom{t}{k}r^{k}
is log-concave in
\displaystyle k
. For larger
\displaystyle r
, the support of the correction term overlaps significantly with that of
\displaystyle\binom{t}{k}r^{k}
, and the simple argument used above is no longer sufficient. Indeed, counterexamples show that the conclusion fails in general.

In contrast to Theorem
4.1
, the GPA of
\displaystyle K_{r,\dots,r}
is not always unimodal (or log-concave) when
\displaystyle r
is larger. We give explicit examples.

Consider the balanced complete bipartite graph
\displaystyle K_{8,8}
, which can be viewed as
\displaystyle K_{r,\dots,r}
with
\displaystyle r=8
and
\displaystyle t=2
. From (
3.5
) derive that
\displaystyle\alpha_{0}=1,\alpha_{1}=16,
and for
\displaystyle k\geq 2
, we have
\displaystyle\alpha_{k}=\binom{2}{k}8^{k}+2\binom{8}{k}.
The explicit polynomial is

\psi(K_{8,8})=1+16x+120x^{2}+112x^{3}+140x^{4}+112x^{5}+56x^{6}+16x^{7}+2x^{8}.

The coefficient sequence
\displaystyle\{1,16,120,112,140,112,56,16,2\}
is not unimodal, as it ascends from
\displaystyle 1
to
\displaystyle 120
, descends to
\displaystyle 112
, ascends again to
\displaystyle 140
, and thereafter descends. Furthermore,
\displaystyle 112^{2}\ngeq 120\cdot 140
. Therefore, neither unimodality nor log-concavity is applicable to
\displaystyle\psi(K_{8,8})
.

For
\displaystyle r=5
and
\displaystyle t=2
, that is, for the balanced complete bipartite graph
\displaystyle K_{5,5}
, a direct computation using (
3.5
) gives
\displaystyle\psi(K_{5,5})=1+10x+45x^{2}+20x^{3}+10x^{4}+2x^{5},
with coefficient sequence
\displaystyle\{1,10,45,20,10,2.\}
The log-concavity inequality fails at index
\displaystyle k=3
as
\displaystyle\alpha_{3}^{2}=20^{2}=400<45\cdot 10=450=\alpha_{2}\alpha_{4}.
Thus,
\displaystyle\psi(K_{5,5})
is not log-concave, although the sequence is still unimodal in this case.

These examples show that, unlike in Theorem
4.1
, the GPA of balanced complete multipartite graphs need not be unimodal or log-concave in general. Determining precisely for which pairs
\displaystyle(r,t)
the polynomial
\displaystyle\psi(K_{r,\dots,r})
is unimodal or log-concave appears to be a delicate problem, and can be studied in future direction of research.

5
Log-concave and unimodal properties of general position polynomial

A broom
\displaystyle B_{s,r},s\geq 0,r\geq 0
, is a graph with vertices
\displaystyle u_{0},\ldots,u_{s},v_{1},\ldots,v_{r}
, and edges
\displaystyle u_{i}u_{i+1}
for
\displaystyle i\in\{0,\ldots,s-1\}
and
\displaystyle u_{0}v_{j}
for
\displaystyle 1\leq j\leq r
, see
\displaystyle B_{4,6}
Figure
1
.

\displaystyle u_{0}
\displaystyle u_{1}
\displaystyle u_{2}
\displaystyle u_{3}
\displaystyle u_{4}
\displaystyle v_{4}
\displaystyle v_{4}
\displaystyle v_{6}
\displaystyle v_{1}
\displaystyle v_{2}
\displaystyle v_{3}

Figure 1:
The broom graph
\displaystyle B_{4,6}
.

The GPA of broom is (see
[
15
]
)

\psi(B_{s,r})=\sum_{k\geq 0}\beta_{k}x^{k}=1+(s+r+1)x+\binom{s+r+1}{2}x^{2}+\sum_{k\geq 3}\left[s\binom{r}{k-1}+\binom{r}{k}\right]x^{k}.

(5.7)

The following result discuss the unimodal and log-concave property of
\displaystyle\psi(B_{s,r}).

Theorem 5.1
.

Let
\displaystyle B_{s,r}
be the broom graph and let
\displaystyle\psi(B_{s,r})=\sum_{k\geq 0}\beta_{k}x^{k}
be its GPA. With
\displaystyle n=s+r+1
the following assertions hold.

1.

For all integers
\displaystyle r\geq 0
and
\displaystyle s\geq 0
, the sequence
\displaystyle(\beta_{k})_{k\geq 0}
is
log–concave at indices
\displaystyle k=1
and
\displaystyle k=2
.

2.

For all integers
\displaystyle r\geq 0
and
\displaystyle s\geq 0
, the tail subsequence

\displaystyle(\beta_{k})_{k\geq 4}
is log–concave. In particular, all possible failures of
log–concavity can occur only at index
\displaystyle k=3
.

3.

For
\displaystyle r\leq 2
and all
\displaystyle s\geq 0
, the full sequence
\displaystyle(\beta_{k})
is
log–concave and hence unimodal.

4.

For every fixed
\displaystyle r\geq 3
there exists
\displaystyle S(r)
such that for all

\displaystyle s\geq S(r)
the sequence
\displaystyle(\beta_{k})
is not log–concave (log–concavity fails at

\displaystyle k=3
).

5.

For
\displaystyle r\leq 5
and all
\displaystyle s\geq 0
, the sequence
\displaystyle(\beta_{k})
is unimodal.

6.

For every
\displaystyle r\geq 6
there exists
\displaystyle S^{\prime}(r)
such that for all

\displaystyle s\geq S^{\prime}(r)
the sequence
\displaystyle(\beta_{k})
is not unimodal. In particular, the polynomial
\displaystyle\psi(B_{17,6})
is neither log–concave nor unimodal.

Proof.

Throughout the proof, we use (
5.7
), so with
\displaystyle n=s+r+1
, we have

\beta_{0}=1,\quad\beta_{1}=n,\quad\beta_{2}=\binom{n}{2},\quad\beta_{k}=\binom{r}{k}+s\binom{r}{k-1}\ (k\geq 3).

For
\displaystyle k=1
, we have

\beta_{1}^{2}-\beta_{0}\beta_{2}=n^{2}-\binom{n}{2}=n^{2}-\frac{n(n-1)}{2}=\frac{n(n+1)}{2}\geq 0,

which holds for all integers
\displaystyle n\geq 0
. Thus log–concavity holds at
\displaystyle k=1
.
For,
\displaystyle k=2
, using
\displaystyle\beta_{3}=\binom{r}{3}+s\binom{r}{2}
, a straightforward calculation gives

\displaystyle\displaystyle\beta_{2}^{2}-\beta_{1}\beta_{3}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\binom{n}{2}^{2}-n\Bigl(\binom{r}{3}+s\binom{r}{2}\Bigr)=\frac{(r+s+1)P_{2}(r,s)}{12},

where

P_{2}(r,s)=r^{3}+3r^{2}s+9r^{2}+9rs^{2}+12rs-4r+3s^{3}+3s^{2}.

All monomials in
\displaystyle P_{2}(r,s)
have nonnegative coefficients except
\displaystyle-4r
. At
\displaystyle s=0
, we have
\displaystyle P_{2}(r,0)=r(r^{2}+9r-4)>0
, for every integer
\displaystyle r\geq 1
. For
\displaystyle s>0
the extra terms

\displaystyle 3r^{2}s+9rs^{2}+12rs+3s^{3}+3s^{2}
are positive, so
\displaystyle P_{2}(r,s)>0
for all

\displaystyle r\geq 1
,
\displaystyle s\geq 0
. Hence,
\displaystyle\beta_{2}^{2}\geq\beta_{1}\beta_{3}
holds.

(2) For
\displaystyle k\geq 4
, consider the auxiliary polynomial

q(x)=(1+sx)(1+x)^{r}=\sum_{k\geq 0}\gamma_{k}x^{k}.

It is clear that

\gamma_{0}=1,\quad\gamma_{1}=s+r,\quad\gamma_{k}=\binom{r}{k}+s\binom{r}{k-1}\quad(k\geq 1).

Thus
\displaystyle\gamma_{k}=\beta_{k}
, for all
\displaystyle k\geq 3
, and in particular
\displaystyle\gamma_{k-1}=\beta_{k-1},\gamma_{k}=\beta_{k},\gamma_{k+1}=\beta_{k+1}
, for every
\displaystyle k\geq 4
. The roots of
\displaystyle(1+x)^{r}
are all real (
\displaystyle x=-1
with multiplicity
\displaystyle r
), and the root of
\displaystyle(1+sx)
is real (
\displaystyle x=-1/s
for
\displaystyle s>0
). Hence
\displaystyle q(x)
has only real
roots, and it is well known by Newton’s Inequality that its coefficient sequence
\displaystyle(\gamma_{k})
is log–concave
[
27
,
11
]
. Therefore for every
\displaystyle k\geq 4
,

\beta_{k}^{2}-\beta_{k-1}\beta_{k+1}=\gamma_{k}^{2}-\gamma_{k-1}\gamma_{k+1}\geq 0.

This proves (2), as any violation of log–concavity can occur only at index
\displaystyle k=3
.

For
\displaystyle r\leq 2
, we already know that log–concavity holds at
\displaystyle k=1,2

\displaystyle\displaystyle d_{H}(v,w)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=d_{G}(v,w),d_{H}(v^{\prime},v)=1,d_{H}(v^{\prime},w)=d_{G}(v,w)+1,d_{H}(v^{\prime},w^{\prime})=d_{G}(v,w)+2.

Two simple but crucial observations are: If a general position set
\displaystyle S\subseteq V(H)
contains
\displaystyle v
and
\displaystyle v^{\prime}
and
at least one more vertex
\displaystyle x
, then
\displaystyle\{v^{\prime},v,x\}
is not in general position. The unique shortest
\displaystyle v^{\prime},x
–path goes through
\displaystyle v
, so
\displaystyle d_{H}(v^{\prime},x)=d_{H}(v^{\prime},v)+d_{H}(v,x),
and
\displaystyle v
lies on a geodesic between
\displaystyle v^{\prime}
and
\displaystyle x
.
Hence for any general position set
\displaystyle S
of size
\displaystyle\geq 3
, we have
\displaystyle|\{v,v^{\prime}\}\cap S|\leq 1
for all
\displaystyle v\in V(G).
If
\displaystyle u,v,w\in V(G)
and
\displaystyle v
lies on some shortest
\displaystyle u,w
–path in
\displaystyle G
, then in
\displaystyle H
the triple
\displaystyle\{u^{\prime},v,w^{\prime}\}
is not in general position. And, we get

d_{H}(u^{\prime},w^{\prime})=d_{G}(u,w)+2=(d_{G}(u,v)+1)+(d_{G}(v,w)+1)=d_{H}(u^{\prime},v)+d_{H}(v,w^{\prime}).

So,
\displaystyle v
lies on a geodesic between
\displaystyle u^{\prime}
and
\displaystyle w^{\prime}
in
\displaystyle H
. These facts show that general position sets in
\displaystyle H
are tightly controlled by geodesics in
\displaystyle G
, but there is no simple, uniform transformation of
\displaystyle\psi(G)
into
\displaystyle\psi(H)
, and it is not a priori clear how log–concavity behaves under the corona.

Let
\displaystyle P_{n}
be the path of order
\displaystyle n
, and let
\displaystyle G_{n}=P_{n}\circ K_{1}
be the comb. For path
\displaystyle P_{n}
, the polynomial
\displaystyle\psi(P_{n})=1+nx+\binom{n}{2}x^{2},
is log–concave. For the comb
\displaystyle G_{n}=P_{n}\circ K_{1}
, and by Theorem
5.2
,
\displaystyle\psi(G_{n})
is log-concave.

Similarly, if
\displaystyle G\cong\overline{K}_{n}
is edgeless on
\displaystyle n
vertices, then
\displaystyle\psi(G\circ K_{1})
is again log–concave. Thus, for these families log–concavity is preserved. These examples might tempt one to conjecture that log–concavity of
\displaystyle\psi(G)
implies log–concavity of
\displaystyle\psi(G\circ K_{1})
in general. However, this is
false.

Let
\displaystyle G=C_{6}
be the cycle of length
\displaystyle 6
. Then, we have

\psi(C_{6})=1+6x+15x^{2}+2x^{3},

and its coefficient sequence
\displaystyle(1,6,15,2)
is log–concave. Now, consider the corona
\displaystyle H=C_{6}\circ K_{1}
, and its GPA is

\psi(H)=1+12x+66x^{2}+88x^{3}+39x^{4}+6x^{5}+x^{6}.

Clearly,
\displaystyle 6^{2}\ngeq 39\cdot 1
, and the log-concave property ceases for
\displaystyle C_{6}\circ K_{1}.

Thus any general theorem about log–concavity of
\displaystyle\psi(G\circ K_{1})
must impose
additional structural conditions on
\displaystyle G
. Corona graphs remain a fertile source
of both positive results (like combs) and counterexamples in the study of
algebraic properties of GPAs. However, if log-concavity is preserved by
\displaystyle\psi(G\circ K_{1})
, then with positive coefficients and without internal zeros, then the unimodal property of
\displaystyle\psi(G\circ K_{1})
will follows.

8
Conclusion and Future Work

The GPA
\displaystyle\psi(G)
provides a powerful algebraic
framework for studying general position sets in graphs. In this work we
extended the foundational results of
[
15
]
by examining new graph classes,
operations, and extremal behaviors related to unimodality and log-concavity. Our investigation reveals a striking dichotomy: while
\displaystyle\psi(G)
displays clean
unimodal or log-concave behavior for highly structured graphs—such as
edgeless graphs, paths, combs, and Kneser graphs
\displaystyle K(n,2)
—it also exhibits
strong irregularity in broader classes, including brooms, multipartite
structures, and several natural corona constructions. This reflects analogous occurrences noted in independence, domination, and clique polynomials (see,
[
1
,
2
,
3
,
5
,
6
,
8
,
12
,
13
,
14
,
16
,
21
]
).

Multiple intriguing study avenues arise:

1.

Does the unimodality of
\displaystyle\psi(G)
imply the unimodality of
\displaystyle\psi(G\circ K_{1})
?
Our results show this holds for paths, combs, and edgeless graphs, but the
general case remains open. A counterexample, if it exists, would likely
require subtle interactions between geodesics in
\displaystyle G
and the pendant structure
added in the corona.

2.

For no nontrivial family is it currently known whether
\displaystyle\psi(G)
is always
real-rooted. Even partial real-rootedness results for chordal graphs, bipartite
graphs, or claw-free graphs (in analogy with
[
8
,
13
]
) would be significant.

3.

While some multipartite constructions behave regularly (as in
[
29
]
),
balanced multipartite graphs exhibit a delicate interplay between the
multipartite geometry and shortest-path constraints. A full characterization
of unimodality and log-concavity in these families is still open.

4.

The visibility and monophonic variants studied in
[
9
,
28
]
suggest natural
analogues of the GPA. The relationships among these
polynomials remain largely unexplored.

5.

The zeros and their bound in real or complex field remains an open challenge.

The GPA integrates geometric, algebraic, and combinatorial elements of graph theory. The numerous parallels with classical
graph polynomials suggest a rich theory ahead, with many unresolved questions and promising avenues for future exploration.
