Implementing and evaluating a seminar-case learning model in postgraduate orthodontic education: perceived effectiveness, student experience, and influencing factors
Authors: Yuanyuan Yin, Ge Feng, Leilei Zheng, Jinlin Song, Lan Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPostgraduate orthodontic education in China predominantly relies on traditional lecture-based learning and clinical apprenticeships, which often fail to adequately foster clinical reasoning, student engagement, and interactive learning. This study aimed to evaluate the feasibility and perceived value of a novel seminar-case learning (SCL) model, which integrates the interactive dynamics of seminars with the clinical relevance of case-based learning, within postgraduate orthodontic education.MethodsA pilot study was conducted involving 64 orthodontic postgraduates from 27 dental schools across China during a summer camp session. Participants were divided into six small groups, each facilitated by an experienced instructor, to discuss three complex orthodontic cases using the SCL model. Questionnaires were administered before and after the intervention to assess students’ baseline competencies, expectations, perceived improvements in various orthodontic treatment domains (diagnosis, treatment planning, execution, monitoring), and overall feedback on the learning experience.ResultsThe results demonstrated high satisfaction with the SCL model, with over 80% of participants rating it superior to traditional teaching methods. A significant majority reported “improvement” or “great improvement” in core orthodontic competencies, including examination and diagnosis (76.56%), treatment planning (78.13%), clinical reasoning (87.50%), and monitoring and management of common issues (75.00%). Furthermore, 87.50% expressed strong confidence in applying the acquired knowledge in clinical practice. Interestingly, perceived gains followed a U-shaped curve relative to clinical experience: the intermediate group (50–80 cases) reported the lowest satisfaction, while beginners (0–20 cases) and advanced learners (> 80 cases) showed higher and comparable levels. Key factors for successful learning were identified as “instructors’ facilitation skills”, “theory-practice integration”, and “case typicality”.ConclusionsThe SCL model demonstrates promising feasibility and high acceptability among orthodontic postgraduates, with participants reporting substantial perceived gains in core clinical competencies and strong engagement in the learning process. These preliminary findings suggest that SCL represents a potentially valuable innovative approach to enrich postgraduate orthodontic training. Future implementation should consider tailoring case complexity to learners’ experience levels, and controlled studies with longitudinal follow-up are needed to establish the efficacy and sustained impact of this pedagogical model.Keywords: Seminar-case learning, Orthodontics, Postgraduate orthodontic education, Questionnaires

Introduction

As a highly practice-oriented discipline, orthodontics requires strong clinical reasoning and the ability to manage complex cases. Postgraduate orthodontic education plays a central role in the training of future orthodontists [1]. The goal of postgraduate orthodontic training is to cultivate clinicians who possess not only specialized knowledge and technical skills but also the comprehensive competence required for independent practice [2, 3]. In China, orthodontic education for postgraduate students traditionally relies on lecture-based learning to deliver foundational and clinical knowledge, complemented by clinical apprenticeships where students assist supervising orthodontists to develop basic practical skills [4]. However, this dual approach presents some limitations. The former approach is often teacher-centered, focusing on the transmission of facts, which can render students passive recipients of abstract information and potentially dampen their initiative and enthusiasm [5]. The latter, while practical, often emphasizes the specifics of individual cases and procedural techniques, lacking a macroscopic analytical perspective for better understanding of clinical cases. Therefore, teaching strategies that enhance student initiative and strengthen case analysis are pivotal for advancing postgraduate orthodontic education. Within orthodontic education, most applications of novel teaching methods have been targeted at undergraduate students [6–10], with relatively less research focused on the postgraduate level [4, 11], underscoring the urgent need for innovative teaching methods in postgraduate orthodontic education.

The seminar-case learning model (SCL) is an innovative teaching methodology that effectively combines seminar and case-based learning (CBL), which integrates the dynamic mutual communication characteristic of seminar learning with the clinical reasoning in CBL [12]. Seminar learning is conducted through small-group discussions focused on specific topics under the guidance of teachers [13]. This method emphasizes continuous teacher-student interaction that harnesses the academic potential of both parties throughout the discussion process [14, 15]. Moreover, it effectively fosters student initiative and cultivates innovative thinking abilities [16]. Complementing this approach, CBL, which originated in medical education as early as 1912, employs clinical cases to prepare students for practice [17]. In this model, theoretical knowledge is linked to practice by applying it to clinical cases through inquiry-based methods [18]. This approach has been demonstrated to encourage active learning, enhance critical thinking skills, and boost student confidence in problem-solving [19, 20]. While both seminar and CBL teaching strategies have gained widespread application in medical education, recent investigations have begun to document the effectiveness of their integrated SCL approach [12, 21–23]. A newly published meta-analysis examining randomized controlled trials comparing SCL with traditional lecture-based models in medical education revealed that SCL implementation significantly improves theoretical knowledge acquisition, enhances case analysis performance, stimulates learning interest and clinical thinking ability, while also promoting student satisfaction [12]. These research findings have consequently motivated our current investigation into the application and student perceptions of the SCL model in postgraduate orthodontic education.

In this study, we aimed to evaluate the feasibility and perceived educational value of implementing the SCL model within a multi-institutional summer camp setting for orthodontic postgraduates. Specifically, we sought to describe participants’ baseline self-rated competencies and prior learning experiences, assess the extent to which they perceived improvement in core orthodontic domains following the SCL session, and identify the learner characteristics and instructional factors associated with higher perceived gains. By addressing these objectives, this pilot study seeks to provide foundational evidence to guide the design of more rigorously controlled evaluations of the SCL model in postgraduate orthodontic education.

Methods

Participants

This study employed a single-arm, pre-post observational design within the context of a pre-scheduled summer camp activity. It included 66 postgraduate students (master’s and doctoral candidates) in orthodontics from 27 dental schools across China. Participants were recruited voluntarily for the 2025 Orthodontic Postgraduate Summer Camp, an extracurricular event organized by the Chongqing Health Commission for Science and Technology Development and hosted by the Stomatological Hospital of Chongqing Medical University. Enrollment in the camp was based on individual student interest, with no academic performance prerequisites or institutional nomination requirements. All 66 students who attended the summer camp participated in the SCL session and were invited to complete the study questionnaires. Additionally, six orthodontists from the host institution participated in the study as supervising instructors.

Design

The SCL model flowchart was implemented as follows (Fig. 1). Sixty-six orthodontic postgraduates were divided into 6 groups (A through F), each consisting of 11 students and supervised by one instructor with substantial clinical experience. Two groups were assigned to discuss one case: Groups A and B discussed Case 1 (orthodontic-periodontal combined treatment), Groups C and D discussed Case 2 (functional appliance therapy in adolescents), and Groups E and F discussed Case 3 (orthodontic-orthognathic combined treatment). First, 3 instructors provided a background introduction to the 3 respective cases. Subsequently, each group conducted a 75-minute internal discussion. During this session, participants analyzed the case diagnosis, treatment plan, and key points for follow-up monitoring, guided by the instructor who facilitated the discussion and managed the pace. Following this, one representative from each of Groups A and B presented their group’s findings for Case 1. After the presentation, the instructor provided comments and revealed the actual diagnosis and treatment plan for Case 1. This process was repeated for Case 2 (presented by Groups C and D) and Case 3 (presented by Groups E and F). After all 3 case presentations and instructor feedback sessions, each group conducted an internal review, summary, and question-and-answer session.

Evaluation

Students voluntarily completed questionnaires before and after the SCL discussion. Their baseline information and post-discussion perceptions and satisfaction levels were assessed via a combination of Likert scales, single-choice and multiple-choice questions, and open-ended responses. The two questionnaires were developed by orthodontic specialists at the Stomatological Hospital of Chongqing Medical University. The consistency and reliability of the survey items was assessed using Cronbach’s α [24], while the validity of the questionnaire structure was evaluated with the Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin (KMO) measure and Bartlett’s test of sphericity [25]. All reliability and validity analyses were performed using SPSS software version 26 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL). The questionnaires were administered via Wenjuanxing, and students accessed them by scanning QR codes with their cellphones.

The pre-discussion questionnaire covered: (1) Demographic information: gender, university, year of study, and number of patients managed (0–20, 20–50, 50–80, > 80). (2) Self-rated mastery of various orthodontic treatment aspects: diagnosis, treatment planning, treatment execution, and problem monitoring, assessed using Likert scales (Cronbach’s α = 0.866; KMO = 0.809, P < 0.001) (3) Prior participation in similar discussions and the perceived level of educational benefit. (4) Other types of case discussions previously engaged in. (5) Expectations for the current discussion, including specific competencies they hoped to improve and the core content they anticipated most.

The post-discussion questionnaire investigated: (1) The extent to which the discussion enhanced their understanding of the same orthodontic treatment aspects, measured by Likert scales (Cronbach’s α = 0.935; KMO = 0.848, P < 0.001). (2) Whether the discussion improved their understanding of the cases, enhanced clinical thinking, stimulated learning interest, and boosted their communication skills (Cronbach’s α = 0.891; KMO = 0.768, P < 0.001). (3) A comparative evaluation against traditional teaching methods. (4) Whether the intra-group discussion was sufficient. (5) Their willingness to participate in similar discussion formats in the future. (6) Key factors they perceived as influencing the learning effectiveness (Cronbach’s α = 0.893; KMO = 0.852, P < 0.001). (7) Aspects requiring improvement in the SCL discussion (Cronbach’s α = 0.923; KMO = 0.838, P < 0.001). (8) Additional open-ended suggestions.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software version 26 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL). Categorical variables are presented as frequencies and percentages. The Kolmogorov–Smirnov test indicated that participant ratings were non-normally distributed (P < 0.05); therefore, the Mann-Whitney U test was used for between-group comparisons. Effect sizes for Man-Whitney U tests were calculated as , with thresholds of 0.1, 0.3, and 0.5 interpreted as small, medium, and large effects, respectively. To further evaluate whether learner characteristics are independently associated with post-discussion outcomes, an ordinal logistic regression was performed with confidence in clinical application as the dependent variable. Clinical experience, gender, and academic degree were entered as predictors. Model fit was assessed using the likelihood ratio test and Pearson goodness-of-fit; the proportional odds assumption was tested via the parallel lines test. Effect sizes are reported as adjusted odds ratios with 95% confidence intervals. Sensitivity analyses using the same model specification were also conducted for the domain-specific perceived gain items. Statistical significance was defined as P < 0.05.

Results

Demographic characteristics of participants

Questionnaires were distributed before and after the case discussion. Participants who completed both questionnaires were considered valid respondents, resulting in 64 valid responses for each time point and a valid response rate of 96.97%. Among the 64 orthodontic postgraduate students, 19 (29.68%) were male and 45 (70.31%) were female. The cohort consisted of 45 master’s students and 19 doctoral students, with master’s students comprising the majority. Regarding patient management experience, the largest proportion of participants had managed 0–20 cases (n = 21, 32.81%), followed by 20–50 cases (n = 17, 26.56%), and 50–80 cases (n = 16, 25.00%). Only 15.63% (n = 10) had managed more than 80 cases.

Pre-discussion baseline status

Prior to the discussion, students’ self-rated mastery of the orthodontic treatment process was surveyed. Over half of the participants reported “basic mastery” in orthodontic examination and diagnosis (50.00%), treatment planning (60.94%), treatment execution (59.38%), and problem monitoring and management (64.06%). Comparative analysis indicated that proficiency in problem monitoring and management was the weakest among the assessed areas, with 18.75% of respondents reporting “limited mastery” (Table 1). As shown in Fig. 2, overall, students with more clinical experience tended to report higher levels of mastery across all orthodontic treatment aspects. An exception was observed in treatment planning, where the 20–50 cases group scored slightly lower than the 0–20 cases group, though the difference was not statistically significant (P = = 0.772, r = 0.06).

Post-discussion feedback and evaluation

Post-discussion surveys indicated that the majority of participants believed the session “improved” or “greatly improved” their competencies in examination and diagnosis (76.56%), treatment planning (78.13%), treatment execution (75.00%), and monitoring and management of common issues (75.00%). Additionally, 89.07%, 87.50%, 85.94%, and 81.25% of students reported that the discussion “improved” or “greatly improved” their case comprehension, clinical reasoning, learning interest, and communication skills, respectively (Table 2). Interestingly, students in the 50–80 cases group reported the least gains from the session (Fig. 4), though this difference was not statistically significant.

Overall, 82.81% of respondents considered this SCL method more effective than traditional teaching methods, and 87.50% expressed over 75% confidence in applying the knowledge gained to their clinical practice. Regarding the level of communication and collaboration within the groups during the discussion, 81.25% of postgraduates reported that the intra-group exchange was either “sufficient” or “highly sufficient”, while 85.94% expressed they were “willing” or “highly willing” to participate in similar case-based discussions in the future.

Ordinal logistic regression adjusting for gender and academic degree revealed that greater clinical experience was independently associated with higher odds of reporting greater confidence in applying SCL-acquired knowledge to clinical practice (adjusted OR = 1.78 per experience category increase, 95% CI: 1.05–3.01, P = 0.031). Neither gender (P = 0.789) nor academic degree (P = 0.103) emerged as significant predictors in the adjusted model. The model demonstrated adequate fit (Pearson χ² = 25.884, df = 36, P = 0.894) and satisfied the proportional odds assumption (parallel lines test: P = 0.298). Sensitivity analyses using the same predictors for domain-specific perceived gain items (examination and diagnosis, treatment planning, treatment execution, problem monitoring and management, case comprehension, clinical reasoning, learning interest, and communication skills) yielded no significant linear associations with clinical experience (all P > 0.05).

Further investigation into the factors deemed most critical to learning effectiveness showed that the five factors received similarly high importance ratings, with mean scores ranging from 4.45 to 4.61 on a 5-point scale, indicating that all were perceived as highly influential (Fig. 5A). Specifically, “instructors’ facilitation skills”, “theory-practice integration”, and “case typicality” were ranked as the top 3 most influential factors (Fig. 5A; Table 3). A multidimensional evaluation of the discussion content indicated that “case complexity” was a relatively underdeveloped aspect of the session. In contrast, “depth and breadth of knowledge points” and “interdisciplinary integration” received the highest ratings from participants (Fig. 5B; Table 3). Additional suggestions provided by participants included: more comprehensive CBCT data, optimized venue arrangements, enhanced explanations of biomechanical principles, greater variety in case types, extended time for treatment planning discussions, improved case typicality, increased number of cases, inclusion of more complex cases, smaller group sizes, and involvement of instructors from other disciplines to facilitate in-depth multidisciplinary diagnosis and treatment.

Discussion

Orthodontics is widely recognized as one of the most challenging specialties within dentistry, requiring practitioners to command extensive knowledge of occlusion, craniofacial growth and development, and interdisciplinary fields such as periodontics and orthognathics to ensure accurate diagnosis and effective treatment. Consequently, postgraduate education is critical for cultivating the clinical reasoning and comprehensive case assessment skills essential for future orthodontists [1, 3]. This study implemented a combined SCL approach in case-based teaching for orthodontic postgraduates and received predominantly positive feedback. Most participants reported that the discussion enhanced their competencies across various orthodontic treatment domains and improved their case comprehension, clinical thinking, learning motivation, and communication skills. Over 80% of respondents considered the SCL model more effective than traditional lecture-based instruction, corroborating previous findings that SCL fosters communication, interaction, and mutual inspiration in medical education [12, 21, 22]. Unlike traditional methods, which often position students as passive recipients of information, SCL encourages active participation and collaborative problem-solving under instructor guidance, thereby promoting deeper engagement and teamwork [12, 14].

Overall, participants with more clinical experience prior to the SCL session tended to rate their baseline mastery of orthodontic treatment higher, a finding consistent with the educational emphasis on clinical exposure, as reflected in the WFO Guidelines for Postgraduate Orthodontic Education, which recommend managing at least 50 cases during residency [26]. Notably, most orthodontic postgraduates in this study reported prior exposure primarily to traditional teacher-led presentations and student-led case reports, with limited experience in other formats such as multidisciplinary consultations, seminar discussions, or simulated clinical encounters. This disparity may be attributed to the higher organizational demands of the latter formats [4, 5].

Participants expressed the strongest desire to improve their abilities in treatment planning, risk control, and diagnosis—needs that align with their early professional development stage. At this phase, postgraduates typically focus on foundational diagnostic and planning skills, whereas advanced topics such as biomechanical design, interdisciplinary coordination, and patient communication often become priorities later in their careers.

The majority of participants reported improvement across various orthodontic competencies after the SCL discussion. An interesting pattern emerged when analyzing perceived gains by clinical experience: improvement ratings generally decreased with increasing case experience, with the 50–80 cases group reporting the lowest gains across all competency domains, while participants managing over 80 cases demonstrated a recovery in perceived learning outcomes, showing satisfaction levels comparable to the 0–20 cases group. This U-shaped trajectory suggests that the SCL model provided comparatively less perceived value for intermediate learners, who may have found the discussed cases insufficiently challenging relative to their clinical exposure. Multivariable analysis adjusting for gender and academic degree provided additional support for this non-linear interpretation. For the integrative outcome of confidence in clinical application, greater experience was independently associated with higher perceived confidence, a linear trend consistent with the accumulation of clinical self-efficacy. In contrast, none of the domain-specific perceived gain items demonstrated significant linear associations with experience. This pattern of findings aligns with the descriptive U-shaped curve and reinforces the view that the perceived incremental value of a single SCL session does not increase monotonically with experience; rather, it appears to depend on how well the case complexity aligns with the learner’s current level of clinical experience. Collectively, these findings highlight the importance of differentiated instructional design in SCL implementation [27], particularly the need to tailor case difficulty to learners’ experience levels.

Orthodontic postgraduates identified “instructors’ facilitation skills”, “theory-practice integration”, and “case typicality” as the top 3 factors influencing learning effectiveness in the SCL session. This result highlights participants’ high expectations for instructors’ guidance and their desire to acquire theoretically grounded knowledge applicable to clinical practice. The emphasis on case typicality further underscores the importance of clinically representative scenarios in the learning process. In the multi-dimensional evaluation of the discussion content, “case complexity” was rated as a comparatively underdeveloped aspect, suggesting a preference for more clinically challenging cases. This may be attributed to the fact that most participating postgraduates had gained clinical experience with moderate patient caseloads. Conventional or moderately difficult cases appear insufficient to meet their advanced learning expectations, indicating the need for more complex clinical scenarios in future SCL sessions to better engage this experienced learner group.

This study has several limitations that warrant cautious interpretation. First, the single-arm, pre-post design without a control group precludes causal attribution of the observed improvements to the SCL intervention. Second, the intervention consisted of a single, half-day session within an extracurricular summer camp; each participant was exposed to only one episode of SCL. Thus, findings reflect immediate, short-term perceptions rather than sustained educational impact. Third, participants were a self-selected cohort who voluntarily registered for an optional summer camp, likely representing individuals with higher baseline motivation and interest in innovative teaching. The high satisfaction rates and perceived gains observed in this study may overestimate the acceptability of the SCL model among the general postgraduate population or in mandatory curricular settings. Fourth, reliance on self-reported post-intervention questionnaires introduces potential response biases, including social desirability and the Hawthorne effect [28], which may have inflated satisfaction ratings beyond levels typical of routine educational contexts. Finally, the study was conducted within the specific context of Chinese postgraduate orthodontic education; cultural and curricular differences may affect the cross-cultural transferability of our findings. Future research employing controlled, longitudinal, and multi-national designs is needed to establish the efficacy and broader applicability of the SCL model.

Conclusion

The SCL model demonstrates promising potential to enhance clinical competencies and engagement in postgraduate orthodontic education. Perceived learning gains were influenced by instructors’ facilitation skills, theory-practice integration, and case relevance. Tailoring case complexity to learners’ clinical experience is crucial for optimizing educational outcomes, particularly for intermediate trainees. Further controlled, longitudinal research is required to confirm the efficacy of this innovative model and to guide its integration into formal orthodontic curricula.
