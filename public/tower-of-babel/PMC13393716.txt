Recurrent urinary tract infections in primary care: a qualitative study of women’s experiences
Authors: Caroline Skovsbo Clausen, Louise Bidstrup Jørgensen, Lars Bjerrum, Anne Holm, Mette Bech Risør
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundUrinary tract infections (UTIs) are highly prevalent, affecting up to 50% of women during their lifetime. Among these, 20–30% develop recurrent UTIs (rUTIs), defined as ≥ 2 UTIs within 6 months or ≥ 3 UTIs within 12 months. In addition to physical symptoms, existing research describes how rUTIs affect women’s quality of life, mental well-being, and intimate and social relationships. Few studies have investigated how women experience encounters with primary care and how rUTIs shape women’s self-image and illness perceptions. The objective of this study was to explore how premenopausal women experience the impact of living with rUTIs, as well as how they experience the encounters with GPs in a Danish primary care setting.MethodsThis qualitative study was based on semi-structured interviews with 12 premenopausal women. The data was analysed using thematic analysis.ResultsThree themes were identified: (1) clashes between practice routines and women’s perceptions, highlighting how standardised diagnostic and treatment practices were experienced as lacking, and that women expressed a need for clearer management pathways and a more patient-centred approach, (2) lack of validation from the GP, reflecting how women felt dismissed and insufficiently acknowledged in clinical encounters; and (3) gendered illness perceptions, illustrating how these experiences shaped women’s understandings of their condition, leading to self-blame, normalisation, or feelings of injustice.ConclusionrUTIs are highly distressing for women and the encounter with primary care often results in frustrations and unmet needs. Perceiving primary care routines as unclear can leave women feeling dismissed, compromise the patient-doctor relationship and affect the women’s self‑image and illness perceptions. Improving rUTI management in primary care requires greater attention to women’s lived experiences. GPs should recognize the broader impact of recurrent symptoms and ensure clear clinical routines that accommodate the recurrent condition rather than relying on repeated tests and prescriptions. Strengthening shared decision-making and aligning care with women’s needs and understandings of health are essential for better support.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12875-026-03448-6.

Background

Urinary tract infections (UTIs) are highly prevalent and distressing among women of all ages. Up to 50% of women experience an UTI, and 20–30% develop recurrent UTIs (rUTIs) [1, 2], typically defined as more than three UTIs within one year or more than two within six months. Symptoms of UTIs can be highly distressing, and recurrent episodes add further distress and worry that extent beyond the physical symptoms [3–7]. For women with UTI symptoms, the usual pathway is to contact primary care, where general practitioners (GPs) may recommend dipstick testing and urine culture before considering antibiotic prescribing. Women with rUTIs, therefore often have frequent primary healthcare encounters, and they have an increased risk of infection with antibiotic-resistant bacteria compared to women with sporadic infections [8, 9]. Recurrent use of antibiotics may therefore cause concerns about antimicrobial resistance. Despite this, relatively few studies have explored how women experience and perceive living with specifically recurring UTIs [5, 10, 11]. Existing research suggests that women prefer to avoid antibiotics when possible and are open to alternative treatments and strategies that empower them as patients [3, 5, 6, 12, 13]. Over the past decade, however, a growing body of research has found how rUTIs significantly affect women’s lives, leading to considerable morbidity and reduced quality of life, with negative consequences for both physical and mental health [3–6, 11, 14–16].

Women have reported greater reductions in social than physical functioning, including diminished quality in intimate and social relationships, lowered self-esteem, and reduced work capacity [10].

Nevertheless, how women perceive rUTIs in relation to their self-image and how their illness perceptions are shaped by the encounters with the health care system, remain largely unexplored in the present literature.

A study published in BMC Family Medicine, drawing on posts from a publicly accessible online self-help forum in the UK, explored women’s experiences with the management of recurrent UTIs. The study showed that women experience GPs as ‘unsupportive,’ ‘dismissive,’ and, at worst, ‘unsympathetic’ and ‘uncaring’ [17], and the perception of GPs stigmatizing women by linking their causes of rUTIs to hygiene and sexual activity has been described [11]. Lack of validation from GPs of women’s experiences with rUTIs, and of the impact on their lives, has also been reported in several qualitative studies [18, 19]. Whether women with rUTIs feel validated and provided with the help and information needed by GPs within the Danish primary healthcare system remains unexplored.

The women’s experience of the pathways to diagnosis and treatment of rUTIs is largely unknown. A single study describes the different treatment pathways as fragmented, leading to inconsistent UTI testing, receiving varying diagnoses, and consequently leading to the experience of inconsistent clinical symptom management [11]. Nevertheless, knowledge on how women navigate these primary healthcare pathways at a systemic level remains sparse, as are the implications and consequences for those affected. Available evidence indicates that the risk of rUTIs exhibits an age-related distribution, with the highest incidence observed among women aged 18–27 and postmenopausal women [20]. No studies have specifically focused on adult women aged 18 to premenopausal status and existing research often focuses on patients with UTIs in general rather than specifically on those with rUTIs [3].

Understanding how patients experience the primary healthcare system is essential for identifying potential challenges in managing rUTI cases, from diagnosis through treatment. Also, a critical gap remains in understanding how women perceive and live with the illness, as well as how the primary healthcare system accommodates this recurring condition.

The purpose of this study was to explore how premenopausal women experience and perceive the impact of living with rUTIs, as well as how they experience being managed by GPs and the extent to which this management meets their needs.

Methods

This qualitative study is based on 12 individual semi-structured interviews. The COREQ framework was followed for transparent reporting [21]. The study adopts a phenomenological stance to explore the women’s lived experiences and subjective perspectives on rUTI. Moreover, we have been inspired by the hermeneutic approach, as the interpretation of women’s accounts enabled a deeper understanding of our data [22].

Positioning and reflexivity of the researcher

All interviews were conducted by CSC, a female medical doctor with clinical training and experience in general practice. Her professional background provided valuable contextual understanding; however, we acknowledge that her role and prior experience may have influenced data collection and interpretation as she might have introduced a degree of power asymmetry, potentially influencing participants to moderate their responses or present their experiences in ways they perceived as clinically relevant or socially desirable. At the same time, insider perspectives of CSC may have facilitated rapport and a deeper contextual understanding of the data including having a phenomenological stance through the interview guide. CSC engaged in ongoing reflexivity throughout the research process, documenting assumptions and reflections in a research diary to enhance transparency and to raise awareness of biases. Moreover, CSC engaged in iterative discussions with MBR and AH throughout the conduction of the study. CSC aimed to approach the women with an open mind, emphasizing that she wished to be perceived as an interested researcher rather than a medical doctor. However, she acknowledged that this distinction did not necessarily eliminate potential biases or preconceptions among the participants.

The research group’s diverse backgrounds, spanning medical anthropology (MBR), medical doctors (CSC)/GPs (AH, LB) ss, and public health (LBJ), contributed complementary perspectives that enriched analytical discussions throughout the research process, which facilitated critical reflection from both clinical and anthropological perspectives, deepening the interpretations of our data. As a result, the collaboration supported a more nuanced, reflexive, and methodologically robust analysis of the data.

Participant selection

Participants were recruited through a post in a private Facebook group (‘Free from urinary tract infections’) and through a 24-hour Instagram story shared by a social media content creator. These platforms were chosen as they align well with the age group of premenopausal women. The content creator was an individual known to CSC (not personally), who has a substantial following in Denmark, particularly within areas such as gender equality, work–life balance, and family life. As such, this creator did not have specific expertise or a particular public profile related to rUTIs, but considered rUTIs to be a topic relevant to her target groups. The content creator did not receive any financial compensation for sharing the recruitment post.

The posts on Facbeook and Instagram were identical and included brief study information and contact details to the first author (CSC). Interested women contacted CSC directly and subsequently received detailed written information. All participants were, prior to the interview, asked whether they met the clinical definition for rUTIs (≥ three UTIs within one year or ≥ two within six months).

Due to the large number of respondents from Instagram, all of whom fulfilled the inclusion criteria (being a premenopausal woman and having rUTIs), CSC purposively selected participants to ensure diversity in age (23–48 years), rUTI severity, and geography. This purposive selection was undertaken to ensure a diverse group of women [23]. The study included 12 participants. Descriptions of all 12 participants are presented in Table 1. Sample size was guided by the principle of information power, i.e. supported by a narrow and clearly defined aim, a specific and homogeneous sample, and a predefined analytical strategy [23]. CSC secured high quality dialogue through strong subject knowledge, relevant experience, and trust building skills, to facilitate a safe and trusting interview environment. CSC employed active listening, open and non-judgemental questioning, and encouraged participants to elaborate freely on their experiences. Participants were reassured of confidentiality and reminded that there were no right or wrong answers.

Contextual information of the primary health care system

The women included in this study were interviewed about their experiences with the primary health care system and the extent to which it provided the support they needed. They described interactions with secretaries, nurses, biomedical laboratory scientists, out-of-hours doctors, and other health staff, as well as their consultations with physicians in primary care (collectively referred to as GPs in the Results section). In addition, some women reported experiences with out‑of‑hours doctors. Out‑of‑hours doctors provide medical care when general practices are closed. Services are delivered by physicians, and in some regions, nurses, who conduct triage, offer telephone consultations, and arrange acute in‑person consultations or home visits when medically indicated.

Setting

The interviews were conducted by CSC either in person (n = 6) or online (n = 6) via a secure video platform, enabling both CSC and the informant to see and hear each other [24]. Interviews were conducted in participants’ homes, workplaces, or at study institutions, with online participation offered when preferred. Some participants found online interviews easier to schedule and more comfortable, whereas in‑person interviews allowed CSC to better capture nonverbal cues and facilitate trust and a sense of safety. All interviews were conducted in the first quarter of 2025.

Data collection

The interview guide was developed for this study and has not previously been published elsewhere. The interview guide was supported and guided by existing literature within this field. The full interview guide is available in Appendix A.

We pilot tested the interview guide on two women (not part of the 12 interviews), who fulfilled criteria for participation, and revised the interview guide accordingly.

The interviews were audio recorded, stored, transcribed, and saved to the program, GoodTape (GDPR secured). The transcriptions were reviewed and refined by CSC. Interviews had a duration between 45 min and 1 h and 40 min.

Data analysis

Reflexive thematic analysis inspired by Braun and Clarke’s six-phase approach was applied throughout the analysis of the data [25]. Familiarisation with all transcripts was conducted by CSC prior to coding. The transcripts were coded inductively, without applying a predefined theoretical framework, allowing data-driven codes to be generated across the dataset. The first four interviews were double-coded by CSC and MBR. This first phase of the analysis resulted in a detailed set of initial codes grounded in the empirical material. Subsequently, the remaining interviews were coded in collaboration between CSC and MBR, with NVivo used to support data management. Through analytical discussions between AH, MBR, and CSC, the inductively generated codes were iteratively reviewed and compared for similarity and coherence to deepen and nuance the interpretation of the data. Quotes within each code were repeatedly revisited and examined, leading to refinement and rearrangement of both quotes and codes. All codes were categorised into coding categories. Based on these coding categories (visualized and refined using the software MindManager), three overarching themes that captured meaningful and significant patterns within the data were constructed. Ethical considerations

According to the Danish Research Ethics Committee, qualitative interview studies do not require ethical approval, because they are not considered biomedical research. However, we adhered to standard ethical principles as described by Kvale and Brinkmann [22]. The study was registered in the University of Copenhagen’s database, Project Like Mine, ensuring safe GDPR conduct. A data management plan was developed to ensure appropriate handling of data in accordance with the FAIR principles. All participants gave informed consent based on written and verbal information.

Results

Based on the thematic analysis, we developed three overarching themes: (1) Clashes between biomedical routines and women’s perceptions, (2) Lack of validation from the GP, and (3) Gendered illness perceptions.

Theme 1: clashes between practice routines and women’s perceptions

The first theme captured women’s experiences of how the primary healthcare system functioned and met their needs, including access to care, treatment, and information provided by practitioners.

Lack of clear care pathways

Most women expressed frustration about the lack of continuity in care, as they often encountered different GPs when seeking help for a new episode of UTI.

Amelia:

“I really think it goes back to that feeling of ‘taking a number and getting in and out again.’ I don’t feel at all that I have a relationship with my doctor. We haven’t had those conversations about what’s going to happen. And that’s probably also because, when you’re at a medical practice, it varies who you see […] you just don’t feel like going to your own doctor when you don’t really know him, and you get tossed around to different ones in that medical practice.”

Meeting new physicians and new faces was perceived as a constraint and a nuisance, because it gave the women the feeling that a relationship with a doctor was non-existent. Most women expressed this frustration and felt they were left to themselves and not prioritized. Their sense of neglect was further reinforced by the absence of initiative from GPs to schedule follow-up appointments where the condition could be addressed.

Amelia:

“…it’s because things aren’t really being taken care of, because there’s no follow-up, because we don’t have a dialogue about it, because we don’t talk about prevention and all sorts of other things. So you can easily be left with the feeling that it’s just [emphasizing this word] a bladder infection. And it’s just like, yeah yeah, all women have that.”

Another woman highlighted the need for rUTI to be recognized as its own diagnosis and emphasized the need for clear management pathways.

Elisabeth:

“Yes, like when you go to the doctor and they say, ‘well, now you’ve got diabetes,’ here are three brochures, here are three flyers, here’s a group you can join, you can call them, you can meet with them, something like that … That it’s seen as something serious, not just, ‘oh well, it’s the tenth time you’ve come in’, but that you’re seen as a patient who needs help. I’d almost compare it to people with migraines. They can’t go to work or do things at home either.”

The women had the impression that GPs did not consider rUTIs a priority and often trivialized the condition. They felt they had to draw attention to the recurrent nature of their symptoms every time they reached out to their GPs, and that responsibility for treatment decisions was shifted onto themselves. This was exemplified by one woman who found her GP dismissive and unhelpful.

Susan:

“… and then the urinary tract infections kept coming every month or every other month, or at least around that. And then I thought, ‘ugh, I’ve had enough now.’ So I went to the doctor in December. She wasn’t the nicest person in the world, sorry to say, but… she was just so unpleasant. She was like, ‘what do you expect me to do?’… ‘Do you want preventive treatment, or do you want a referral to a urologist?’ Well… you’re the doctor, so I just want a little help with this… I felt like it was up to me to come up with suggestions for what she should do.”

For some, such encounters were connected to perceiving the GP as not capable of providing the necessary information and not possessing adequate knowledge, when informing about preventing rUTIs. This left some with a sense of hopelessness and several women refrained from seeking help, because they were not provided with the needed support.

Women’s experiences with treatment strategies: between fear and reassurance

The interviewed women revealed a range of experiences regarding the specific treatment and prevention of rUTIs. The use of antibiotics, particularly their reluctance to use them, was significant for most women. They reported feeling distressed by the side effects associated with antibiotic use.

Lily:

“… And I just want to say that I haven’t called every time I’ve had the symptoms either, because I really don’t want to take any more penicillin. Because I think it also does a lot of other bad things to the body. You can just feel that everything gets kind of killed off. Even things that, well, you could say maybe aren’t the best to kill. You always have to start completely over, and then you get a yeast infection, and then that has to be treated. And then it starts itching like crazy, so that also has to be treated, and then you get a bladder infection again… and then, well, you just end up in this cycle, so I’ve been like, no thanks, it can’t go on like this.”

Overall, women expressed concern about developing antibiotic-resistant bacteria and worried that future infections might become harder to treat. However, during acute UTI episodes these concerns were less prominent, as antibiotics were seen as the only effective relief. The women also described initiating self‑treatment while awaiting urine culture results, using reserves of antibiotics from previous prescriptions (pills saved from prior treatment courses). This practice provided comfort and reassurance, allowing them to start treatment regardless of test results and sometimes before contacting their GP.

Olivia:

“… there are more pills in the pack than I actually need. And sometimes I’ve already taken one beforehand, because I knew I was getting it now. So I’ve taken one pill to start with and then gone to my doctor and been told that it’s just a bladder infection. And I’ve been like, ‘Yes, I know that.’ And then I’ve gotten more penicillin prescribed. And I know you’re not supposed to take penicillin unless it’s really necessary. But I know what it is, if that makes sense.”

Several women expressed feeling distressed when they were unable to obtain antibiotics after their urine samples returned negative. Similarly, all women reported frustration at being left without support during the interim phase before the urine culture results were available, apart from being advised to contact the GP or out-of-hours doctor if symptoms worsened.

In relation to treatment, women expressed a strong desire for GPs to adopt a more open-minded approach toward alternative strategies, by acknowledging e.g. potential benefits of certain nutritional practices and considering natural remedies.

Theme 2: lack of validation from the GP

This theme encompasses how the collaboration and relationship between the women and their GPs rebounded on the women.

Patronizing advice and the struggle to convince

One of the major findings within our data was the women’s perceptions of being patronized with standard advice from the GPs.

Mary:

“I remember it was a much older male doctor, who was very kind of . ‘Yes, well, it’s important that you drink plenty of water, remember to go to the toilet, wipe from front-to-back, but there’s nothing in your urine, so I can’t do anything for you’. And that was just it. But you could really sense that he didn’t take me seriously at all. That’s kind of what I remember from it.”

The perception of receiving standard advice despite experiencing multiple recurrences and repeated encounters with their GP was discouraging for the women. Some were left believing they lacked knowledge about basic self-care, such as simple hygiene practices. For some, this simple advice created a sense of being ridiculed or mocked, even humiliated, by their GP.

These experiences often led women to approach future consultations defensively, feeling they had to prove their adherence to hygiene and convince their GP of the seriousness of their condition. Consequently, some women avoided seeking care when symptoms emerged, and if they did, they felt obliged to not only defend themselves but also to emphasize the severity and burdensome nature of the condition.

Olivia:

”Because . I think I had this feeling, like I was going to court to defend myself. I had done all this preparation [followed all the standard advice]. I felt like I had to convince him that I really had UTIs or issues with them.”

The need to exaggerate the severity and duration of symptoms often stemmed from a perception that the condition alone was not considered serious enough to capture the GP’s attention. Many women reported feeling distressed by the need to secure access to care by convincing GPs of the severity of their condition.

Perceived dismissal

Several women described feeling dismissed and not taken seriously by their GPs, which contributed to feelings of frustration and hopelessness. This was illustrated by one woman who described being close to tears after multiple courses of antibiotics.

Patricia:

“Honestly, I think one of the worst parts of this is that it’s not taken seriously at all […] They say that after I’ve already had four different courses of treatment, each lasting ten days. So we’re talking about a lot of days without any effect. And still nothing is done, and still nothing is taken seriously… which honestly scares me a little when I’ve sat there in front of a doctor, almost in tears… and then I’m sent away and told, “Call if it gets worse.” And I’m thinking, “It honestly can’t get any worse!”

Being told that rUTIs should simply be endured, and, in some cases, the suggestion that they would resolve with age, intensified women’s sense of abandonment and being dismissed. The feeling of being dismissed by her GP was described by another woman.

Sarah:

“I feel that when I meet so much resistance [from the GP] whenever I have to talk to the doctor about it, I also feel the need to know whether others experience just as much resistance, or whether I’m saying something wrong when I call in.”

Overall, the women expressed unmet needs. Experiences of being dismissed and encountering resistance from their GPs led some to question their own role, wondering whether they might be to blame for not communicating their concerns effectively during consultations. Several women described feeling that their GPs did not take their condition seriously and tended to dismiss them when they sought care.

Theme 3: gendered illness perceptions

This theme captures how women living with rUTIs experience the impact of the diagnosis and how it influences their self-image, in the context of invalidating and dismissive health care encounters. These constraints influenced their overall illness perception, with some even questioning whether they had an illness at all.

Elisabeth:

“Because, well, I think if you asked me […] ‘are you healthy?’, I would always say yes. Even when I was going to have surgery, now I’ve had a caesarean section, when they ask, are you healthy, I always say yes. But then they say, ‘wait a minute, you actually have this condition.’ This diagnosis. And then I say, ‘oh right, that’s true’… so in that sense… I see myself as healthy… and I think it’s because I’ve lived with it my whole life… I go to the doctor at least once a month, I’d say.”

Furthermore, many of the women had lived with rUTIs for years, ultimately normalizing the condition as part of their identity rather than viewing it as an illness. On the contrary, some women expressed a persistent feeling that “something was wrong” with their bodies, accompanied by a sense of injustice.

Lily:

“Yes, because you can really feel like you’re the only person in the whole world who has this. Especially if you don’t know whether there are communities like that out there. I mean, damn, I felt so lonely and wrong and weird for a long time, because it just kept coming back and I just had to put up with it because I was a woman.”

That the condition could be perceived as an inherent aspect of being female, even comparing rUTIs to menstruation, contributed to a sense that they simply had to live with rUTIs. For others, unfairness and self‑blame related to the recurrent condition weighed on them.

Elisabeth:

”Oh, if there’s something I’ve cried a lot about, it’s definitely having all these bladder infections. At times, I’ve also felt it was insanely unfair… I thought, why did I get this illness? Like… what have I ever done, and you think back a lot… could I have done something differently? What happened in my childhood that might have caused me to have all these bladder infections?”

The sense of personal responsibility for the rUTIS was particularly pronounced among women experiencing severe symptoms and a high frequency of rUTIs. For these women, the severity and persistence of the condition often reinforced feelings of personal fault, intensifying self‑blame and emotional strain.

Discussion

Our findings revealed frustrating experiences of navigating a primary healthcare system characterized by a lack of clear clinical routines that accommodate the recurrent nature of the condition, alongside a lack of patient-centredness in medical encounters. Women experienced GPs to be patronizing and dismissing the severity of the condition, which made them feel invalidated and discourage healthcare seeking. The lack of validation made some women doubt their own judgment of their illness, describing rUTIs as simply part of being a woman, despite their profound impact on their lives. In our study, medical routines were perceived as lacking and insufficient for detecting or managing the condition or providing a safety net. Women did not experience treatment pathways that ensured detection of their rUTIs, nor did they feel supported with appropriate care. These findings support existing evidence describing rUTI pathways as fragmented, leading to inconsistent clinical symptom management [11, 26].

The feeling of having to convince and being personally responsible for making the HCPs aware of one’s condition, and then being met by the frustrating feeling that the condition is not taken seriously is supported by prior studies describing the general lack of validation [18]. However, the lack of information or support led women to question GPs competences, fostering a broader distrust to the healthcare system. While few studies have focused on how diagnostic and treatment pathways are experienced, the results of our study contribute with new knowledge on how medical routines are experienced by the patients themselves.

It is shown in other research that rUTIs affect women’s lives beyond the bothersome physical symptoms, imposing limitations to daily activities, both socially, sexually and workwise [7, 15, 17, 27, 28], but our study also showed that the condition and the clinical encounters affect women in other ways. As De Boer et al. argue, women with illnesses framed as “women’s conditions” can be especially vulnerable to epistemic injustice, that is, an example of misrepresentation of knowledge [29]. As shown above, the women in our study experience being dismissed, patronized and stigmatized during encounters with GPs as well as noting a dissonance between own accounts and the medical responses and treatment. The frustrating experiences of the women in regard to the medical routines leave the women question the legitimacy of their condition [29]. This doubt is reflected in their illness perception, and could explain why some women downplay symptoms, blame themselves, or interpret them as normal physiological processes. In other words, women’s reactions and the medical routines interact. Drawing on Fricker’s theory of epistemic injustice [30] the experience of invalidation and dismissal can be understood as testimonial epistemic injustice, where individuals are not acknowledged as credible knowers in their own right, often due to stereotypes or prejudices. In this context, women’s experiential knowledge of their condition may be implicitly devalued, a tendency that can partly be explained by the biomedical tradition in which clinicians are trained, where reliance on test results and guidelines may unintentionally produce a hierarchy of knowledge that assigns less epistemic weight to subjective experiences.

At the same time, the experienced lack of knowledge also points to hermeneutical injustice, where limitations in shared medical understanding constrain women’s ability to articulate and make sense of their experiences. This lack of adequate interpretive resources may hinder both patients and clinicians in fully understanding rUTIs, leaving women struggling to communicate their symptoms in ways that are recognized within exisiting clinical frameworks.

Strengths and limitations

This study has several strengths. High interest allowed CSC to purposively select a diverse sample in terms of age, rUTI severity, and geography. In addition, a structured interview guide was developed, piloted with two eligible women, and refined, enabling CSC to address the same topics consistently across all interviews.

However, the study also has limitations. Because participants were recruited via social media, women without access to or engagement with these platforms may have been excluded. While this approach allowed the inclusion of women not necessarily consulting their GP, it may have favoured participation from those more severely affected by rUTIs and potentially more dissatisfied with existing support. As such, the recruitment process may have resulted in a potentially selective sample of women as followers of such profiles may share particular characteristics or perspectives.

Because recruitment did not occur in general practice, rUTI diagnoses were based on self‑report rather than urine‑culture verification.

We acknowledge that we did not collect data on participants’ gender identity or sexual orientation and did not actively or explicitly seek variation in this regard. In addition, the sample consisted exclusively of women of Caucasian background, meaning that the limited variation in our study reflects constraints in both ethnicity and gender identity.

Furthermore, the recruitment process required participants to speak Danish, which may have indirectly excluded some groups.

Our phenomenological stance enabled an in-depth exploration of women’s lived experiences and the meanings they ascribe to them, capturing nuanced, subjective dimensions of the condition beyond the scope of quantitative approaches. To interpret these experiences as conveyed through the interviews, we drew from a hermeneutic perspective [22]. This interpretive approach aligns with the use of reflexive thematic analysis, which acknowledges the active and subjective role of the researcher in interpreting and constructing meaning from the data [25].

Nevertheless, this approach also entails limitations that should be acknowledged. Women’s responses may be shaped not only by the questions posed but also by the interpersonal dynamics of the interview and their broader social context [22]. Moreover, their experiences may be ambiguous, reflecting a perceived world shaped by contradictions [22].

As we acknowledge perspectives of healthcare professionals as equally important, this is the scope of a yet unpublished article.

Practice implications

This qualitative study suggests a potential gap in the primary healthcare management of rUTIs, where women’s lived experiences may not always be fully centred. The findings indicate that GPs could benefit from remaining attentive to the broader impact of rUTIs, recognising that the recurrent nature of the condition may affect women’s social, emotional, and sexual well-being beyond acute physical symptoms.

Participants described a need for clearer and more coherent management strategies, as repeated consultations focused primarily on urine testing and antibiotic prescribing contributed to a sense of remaining “under the radar”. Similar experiences have also been reported in other recurrent conditions [31]. Clear organisational structures and well-defined clinical routines in primary healthcare may support the identification of rUTIs.

The findings also suggest that counselling practices warrant careful consideration, as well-intentioned advice may be experienced as stigmatising. Greater sensitivity in communication, alongside attention to shared decision-making, may help ensure that consultations align more closely with patients’ needs, concerns, and understandings of their own health.

Conclusion

Our study suggests that rUTIs impact pre-menopausal women in multiple ways, making the condition challenging to manage in both diagnosis and treatment. As a result, the women of this study expressed experiences of unmet needs when seeking help in primary healthcare. Frustrations often stemmed from feeling dismissed or insufficiently validated by their GP, combined with a perceived lack of structured medical routines. Our findings suggest how such factors could influence the patient-doctor relationship and further shape how some women’s self-image and illness are perceived.
