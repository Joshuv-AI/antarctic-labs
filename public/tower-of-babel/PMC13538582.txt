Sex-specific associations between prenatal exposure to environmental pollutants and birth outcomes: a meconium biomonitoring study
Authors: Mohammad Mouner Ahmad Alsayegh, Ekbal Ahmad Fadel, Flora Yahya Mayhoub
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPrenatal exposure to environmental pollutants, particularly heavy metals and pesticides, is a major public health concern because of its potential association with fetal growth and development. These toxicants can cross the placental barrier and accumulate in fetal tissues. Meconium, the first neonatal feces, is a well-established biomarker of cumulative fetal exposure during late pregnancy. However, evidence regarding sex-specific associations between prenatal pollutant exposure and neonatal outcomes remains limited, particularly in low- and middle-income countries. A cross-sectional study was conducted among 123 full-term newborns in Syria between August and September 2025, including 68 from Damascus and 55 from Latakia. Meconium samples were collected within the first hours after birth, prior to feeding, using sterile contamination-free procedures. Samples were stored at − 20 °C and transported under controlled cold-chain conditions to accredited laboratories. Heavy metals (Pb, Hg, Cd, As, Cr, Ni, Mn, Cu, Se, Tl, Sb) were quantified using atomic absorption spectrophotometry following microwave-assisted acid digestion. Pesticides (diazinon, chlorpyrifos, malathion, DDT/DDE, permethrin) were extracted using organic solvents and analyzed using high-performance liquid chromatography. Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 23.0, including sex-stratified multivariate regression models adjusted for maternal and neonatal covariates. All target pollutants were detected in meconium samples, indicating widespread prenatal exposure. Considerable inter-individual variability in pollutant concentrations was observed across all analytes. Sex-specific associations were identified between pollutant levels and neonatal outcomes. Among male newborns, mercury was negatively associated with birth weight, whereas nickel showed a positive association. Diazinon exposure was associated with reduced head circumference. Among female newborns, lead, thallium, and DDT/DDE showed associations with birth weight. In addition, DDT/DDE was associated with head circumference and birth length, whereas permethrin was associated with shorter birth length. Descriptive regional variation in pollutant concentrations was observed between Damascus and Latakia, although these differences were not the primary focus of the present analysis. This study identified widespread prenatal exposure to multiple environmental pollutants and observed sex-specific associations with neonatal outcomes. Meconium biomonitoring represents a useful approach for assessing cumulative fetal exposure during late pregnancy. Given the observational cross-sectional design, these findings should be interpreted cautiously. Some pesticide-related findings require confirmation using more selective analytical methods. Introduction

Environmental pollution has become a major global public health concern, particularly in relation to maternal and child health. Among the most concerning pollutants are heavy metals and pesticides, which are widely distributed in air, water, soil, and food systems. These compounds are of particular concern during pregnancy because they can cross the placental barrier and interfere with fetal development, potentially leading to adverse birth outcomes and long-term health consequences1,2.

Heavy metals such as lead (Pb), mercury (Hg), and cadmium (Cd) are well-established toxicants with known neurotoxic and endocrine-disrupting properties. Prenatal exposure to these metals has been associated with low birth weight, impaired neurodevelopment, and altered growth parameters3,4. Similarly, pesticides—particularly organophosphates such as chlorpyrifos and diazinon, as well as persistent organochlorine compounds such as DDT and its metabolites— have been reported to be associated with developmental toxicity, hormonal disruption, and adverse pregnancy outcomes5,6.

Assessing fetal exposure to environmental contaminants remains a methodological challenge. Traditional biological matrices, such as maternal blood or urine, often reflect short-term exposure and may not adequately capture cumulative exposure during pregnancy. In contrast, meconium, the first fecal material passed by the newborn, has emerged as a valuable biomarker for assessing in utero exposure. Meconium begins to form during the second trimester and reflects cumulative fetal exposure primarily during the second and third trimesters of pregnancy7,8. Previous studies have demonstrated the utility of meconium in detecting both heavy metals and organic pollutants, providing a more integrated measure of fetal exposure than conventional biological matrices9.

In recent years, increasing attention has been directed toward sex-specific vulnerability to environmental exposures. Evidence suggests that male and female fetuses may respond differently to toxic insults because of differences in placental function, hormonal regulation, and gene expression patterns10,11. Several studies have reported sex-dependent associations between prenatal exposure to environmental pollutants and neonatal outcomes, including differences in birth weight, birth length, and head circumference12. However, findings remain inconsistent, and further research is needed to clarify these relationships, particularly in underrepresented populations.

Most of the existing literature on prenatal exposure to environmental pollutants has been generated in high-income countries, with limited data available from low- and middle-income regions. In countries such as Syria, environmental exposure may be influenced by a combination of urban pollution, agricultural practices, and socio-environmental conditions, yet biomonitoring data remain scarce. In addition, few studies in the region have simultaneously assessed multiple pollutants or explored sex-specific associations using robust biological matrices such as meconium.

Therefore, this study aimed to (1) assess prenatal exposure to a range of heavy metals and pesticides using meconium samples, (2) explore associations between these exposures and neonatal outcomes, and (3) examine potential sex-specific differences in these associations among newborns from two Syrian governorates.

Materials and methods

Study design and setting

A cross-sectional study was conducted between August and September 2025 in two major Syrian cities: Damascus (urban setting) and Latakia (coastal setting).

Meconium is considered a valuable biomonitoring matrix that reflects cumulative fetal exposure to environmental contaminants, primarily during the second and third trimesters of pregnancy.

The selection of heavy metals and pesticides analyzed in this study was based on their known toxicity, persistence in the environment, and documented associations with adverse fetal and neonatal outcomes. Particular emphasis was placed on compounds that are commonly reported in environmental and biomonitoring studies, including lead, mercury, cadmium, and organophosphate and organochlorine pesticides.

In addition, the selection was guided by regional relevance, as these pollutants are more likely to be present due to local agricultural practices, environmental conditions, and potential exposure sources in Syria.

Practical considerations, including the availability of analytical standards and laboratory capacity, also influenced the selection of target analytes.

Study population

The study included 123 full-term newborns, comprising 68 from Damascus and 55 from Latakia. Eligible participants were live-born neonates with available meconium samples collected within the first hours after birth. Preterm births and cases with missing essential clinical data were excluded.

Maternal and neonatal information, including demographic characteristics, obstetric history, and birth outcomes (birth weight, length, head circumference, gestational age, and mode of delivery), were obtained from medical records and structured questionnaires.

Meconium sample collection and handling

Meconium samples were collected within the first hours of life, prior to the initiation of feeding, to minimize external contamination. Sterile, contamination-free plastic tools were used to transfer samples into clean, tightly sealed plastic containers.

Approximately 5–10 g of meconium were collected from each neonate. Each sample was labeled with a unique identification code to ensure participant confidentiality. No personal identifiers were recorded.

Immediately after collection, samples were stored at − 20 °C to prevent biological or chemical degradation. All samples were subsequently transported under controlled cold-chain conditions to accredited laboratories in Damascus and Latakia. The date and time of collection and transport were carefully documented for each sample to ensure traceability and quality control.

Sample preparation

Prior to analysis, frozen samples were gradually thawed at room temperature, homogenized to ensure uniform distribution of analytes, and processed according to standard laboratory protocols. Defined subsamples were weighed for subsequent analyses.

Determination of heavy metals

For heavy metal analysis, 0.5 g of dried and homogenized meconium was accurately weighed using an analytical balance (± 0.0001 g). Samples were transferred into digestion vessels, and a mixture of 5 mL concentrated nitric acid (HNO₃, 65%) and 2 mL hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂, 30%) was added.

Microwave-assisted digestion was performed at 180–200 °C for 30–45 min until a clear solution was obtained. After cooling, the digested samples were diluted to 50 mL with deionized distilled water and filtered using metal-free filter paper.

Concentrations of Pb, Hg, Cd, As, Cr, Ni, Mn, Cu, Se, Tl, and Sb were measured using atomic absorption spectrophotometry (AAS) (Varian 220). Cold vapor technique was used for mercury determination, hydride generation was applied for As, Se, and Sb, and graphite furnace AAS was used for trace elements at low concentrations. Argon gas of high purity was used as a carrier gas in non-flame techniques. Results were expressed in µg/g.

Determination of pesticides

For pesticide analysis, 1.0 g of dried and homogenized meconium was placed in a sealed glass extraction tube. Ten milliliters of an organic solvent mixture (hexane: acetone, 1:1 v/v) were added. Samples were mechanically shaken for 30 min and then centrifuged at 4000 rpm for 10 min.

The organic phase was transferred to a cleanup column containing 1–2 g of silica gel. Re-extraction was performed using 5 mL hexane. The extract was evaporated Under a nitrogen stream at 40 °C and reconstituted in 1 mL hexane.

Pesticide residues (diazinon, chlorpyrifos, malathion, DDT/DDE, and permethrin) were analysed using high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) (Agilent 1100) equipped with a C18 column. Isocratic elution was applied, and the mobile phase was degassed by ultrasonic treatment before analysis. Detection wavelengths were selected according to the maximum ultraviolet absorbance of each compound. Calibration and quantification were performed using external standard methods with certified analytical standards at multiple concentration levels, showing good linearity (R² > 0.995). To improve analytical specificity and reduce matrix interference, an extensive clean-up procedure using silica gel columns was applied before chromatographic analysis. Although HPLC with ultraviolet detection is a widely used technique for pesticide analysis, confirmatory mass spectrometric analysis (e.g. LC–MS/MS) was not available in the present study. The absence of mass spectrometric confirmation may have reduced analytical specificity, particularly for compounds such as DDT/DDE that exhibit relatively weak UV absorbance and may be susceptible to matrix interference in complex biological samples such as meconium. Consequently, some degree of exposure misclassification cannot be excluded. Nevertheless, extensive sample clean-up procedures, certified analytical standards, calibration verification, recovery testing, procedural blanks, and duplicate analyses were implemented to improve analytical reliability. Results were expressed in ng/g.

Statistical analysis

Data obtained from laboratory analyses and questionnaires were processed using SPSS Statistics version 23.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Descriptive statistics were used to summarize participant characteristics and pollutant concentrations.

Comparative analyses were performed using appropriate statistical tests, including analysis of variance (ANOVA), according to data distribution. Multivariate linear regression models were used to assess associations between pollutant levels and continuous neonatal outcomes (birth weight, birth length, and head circumference), whereas logistic regression analysis was used for categorical outcomes such as mode of delivery.

Potential confounding variables were selected based on prior literature and biological plausibility. These included maternal age, maternal weight, gestational age, mode of delivery, and neonatal sex. These variables were considered because of their known influence on both environmental exposure levels and neonatal outcomes. All multivariate regression models were adjusted for these covariates.

All analyses were stratified by sex to evaluate sex-specific associations. Birth outcomes were analyzed as continuous variables. Although standardized z-scores adjusted for gestational age and sex may provide a more refined assessment, such standardization was not applied in the present study because of data limitations. Instead, gestational age and neonatal sex were included as covariates in the regression models.

To address the issue of multiple comparisons, the false discovery rate (FDR) correction was applied using the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure. Both raw p-values and FDR-adjusted q-values were calculated and are presented in the regression results. Statistical significance after correction was evaluated primarily on the basis of the FDR-adjusted q-values.

Given the exploratory nature of the study and the limited availability of samples, a formal a priori power calculation was not performed. However, the sample size is comparable to similar biomonitoring studies investigating prenatal exposure using meconium samples.

Quality assurance and method validation

Quality control and method validation procedures were implemented to ensure the accuracy and reliability of the analytical results. Calibration curves were established for each analyte using certified reference standards at multiple concentration levels, demonstrating good linearity (R² > 0.995).

Limits of detection (LOD) and limits of quantification (LOQ) were determined based on signal-to-noise ratios of 3 and 10, respectively.

Recovery experiments were performed by spiking samples with known concentrations of target analytes, with recovery rates falling within acceptable analytical ranges.

Analytical precision was assessed through replicate measurements, and relative standard deviation (RSD) values were below 10%.

Procedural blanks were included in each batch to monitor potential contamination, and selected samples were analyzed in duplicate to ensure reproducibility.

Although confirmatory analysis by mass spectrometry was not available, the combination of certified standards, calibration procedures, recovery testing, procedural blanks, and duplicate analyses supported the analytical reliability of the reported measurements. However, the absence of MS confirmation remains a methodological limitation and may have affected the specificity of pesticide identification in meconium samples.

Ethical considerations

The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee of the University of Latakia, Syria (Approval No. 3364).

The study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Written informed consent was obtained from all participating mothers prior to sample collection and questionnaire administration. All samples were coded using unique identification numbers, and no personal identifiers were included in the analytical dataset in order to ensure participant confidentiality.

Results

Participant characteristics

The study included 123 full-term newborns, including 68 from Damascus and 55 from Latakia. The overall sex distribution was balanced (62 males and 61 females), and the mean gestational age was 39.1 ± 1.1 weeks. Birth weight ranged from 2500 to 4100 g, with male newborns generally showing slightly higher birth weights than females. Birth length and head circumference showed only minor sex-related differences. Maternal characteristics, including age, weight, and pregnancy history, were broadly comparable between the two regions. Sex-specific associations with neonatal outcomes

Multivariate regression models adjusted for maternal age, maternal weight, gestational age, and mode of delivery revealed sex-specific associations between pollutant exposure and neonatal outcomes.

Among male newborns, mercury was inversely associated with birth weight (B = − 1.899, p = 0.023, q = 0.258), whereas nickel showed a positive association with birth weight (B = 0.138, p = 0.033, q = 0.298). In addition, diazinon exposure was inversely associated with head circumference (B = − 0.935, p = 0.031, q = 0.298).

Among female newborns, lead (B = − 0.053, p = 0.038, q = 0.298), thallium (B = − 30.983, p = 0.025, q = 0.258), and DDT/DDE (B = 0.058, p = 0.037, q = 0.298) were associated with birth weight. Furthermore, DDT/DDE (B = 0.179, p = 0.036, q = 0.298) was associated with head circumference, whereas permethrin was inversely associated with birth length (B = − 0.539, p = 0.012, q = 0.192).

However, none of these associations remained statistically significant after false discovery rate (FDR) correction, indicating that the findings should be interpreted as exploratory and hypothesis-generating.

Discussion

The present study provides evidence of widespread prenatal exposure to multiple environmental pollutants, including both heavy metals and pesticides, as detected in meconium samples. The universal detection of these compounds across all samples highlights the pervasive nature of environmental exposure during pregnancy, even in settings with limited biomonitoring data such as Syria. This observation is consistent with growing global evidence indicating that fetal exposure to environmental toxicants represents an important public health concern rather than an isolated phenomenon13.

A notable finding of this study was the substantial inter-individual variability in pollutant concentrations. This heterogeneity suggests that prenatal exposure is influenced by multiple factors, including environmental conditions, maternal lifestyle, dietary habits, and local exposure sources. Descriptive regional variation was observed between Damascus and Latakia; however, these differences were not statistically robust and were not the primary focus of the present analysis. Regional differences may reflect variation in urban pollution sources, agricultural practices, and environmental conditions, although the present study was not specifically designed to evaluate geographic determinants of exposure14.

One of the principal findings of this study was the observation of sex-specific patterns of association between pollutant exposure and neonatal outcomes. Among male newborns, mercury was inversely associated with birth weight, whereas nickel showed a positive association. In addition, diazinon exposure was negatively associated with head circumference. Among female newborns, lead, thallium, and DDT/DDE were associated with birth weight, while DDT/DDE and permethrin were associated with head circumference and birth length, respectively. However, none of these associations remained statistically significant after FDR correction and should therefore be considered exploratory findings requiring confirmation in larger cohorts.

The positive association observed between nickel exposure and birth weight among male newborns was unexpected, given the recognized toxicological profile of nickel. One possible explanation is residual confounding by unmeasured environmental, nutritional, or socioeconomic factors. Alternatively, this finding may reflect a chance observation resulting from multiple statistical testing and should therefore be interpreted cautiously until replicated in larger studies.

Similarly, the positive associations observed between DDT/DDE and certain neonatal growth parameters should be interpreted cautiously. Although DDT and its metabolites are generally considered developmental toxicants, previous epidemiological studies have reported heterogeneous findings depending on exposure level, timing of exposure, and population characteristics. The simultaneous observation of positive associations with birth weight and head circumference may reflect complex biological interactions, residual confounding, exposure misclassification, or statistical variability rather than a direct beneficial effect of exposure.

These observations are consistent with previous studies suggesting that male and female fetuses may differ in their biological susceptibility to environmental stressors. For example, a study conducted in China reported that prenatal cadmium exposure was associated with reduced birth weight in female infants but not in males, supporting the concept of sex-dependent vulnerability15,16.

The observed sex-specific associations are biologically plausible and may reflect established differences in placental function, endocrine regulation, and fetal adaptive responses. The placenta plays a central role in modulating fetal exposure to environmental contaminants. Experimental and epidemiological studies indicate that male placentas are generally more growth-oriented and less adaptable to adverse intrauterine conditions, whereas female placentas may exhibit greater adaptive capacity through sex-specific gene regulation and hormonal responses17–19.

In addition, several pesticides investigated in the present study, including organophosphates and organochlorines such as DDT/DDE, are recognized endocrine-disrupting compounds. These chemicals may interfere with estrogenic and thyroid hormone signaling pathways that are essential for fetal growth and neurodevelopment20,21. Heavy metals such as lead, mercury, and cadmium have also been associated with oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, altered fetal metabolic pathways, and epigenetic modifications during critical windows of development22–24. Consistent with these mechanisms, transcriptomic analyses of human placentas containing detectable concentrations of lead and cadmium have demonstrated altered expression of genes involved in immune regulation and protein synthesis, suggesting that metal-induced placental dysfunction may influence fetal growth trajectories25. These findings may provide biological support for the negative associations observed between Pb and birth weight and the relatively large coefficient observed for Tl in female newborns, although further confirmation is required.

The use of meconium as a biomonitoring matrix represents an important methodological strength of this study. Unlike maternal blood or urine, which mainly reflect short-term exposure, meconium provides a more integrated measure of cumulative fetal exposure, primarily during the second and third trimesters of pregnancy17. This characteristic is particularly valuable when investigating complex environmental mixtures. However, meconium does not provide precise temporal resolution and cannot distinguish between specific exposure windows during gestation.

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, the cross-sectional observational design does not permit causal inference, and the reported associations should therefore be interpreted cautiously. Although analyses were adjusted for key maternal and neonatal covariates, including maternal age, maternal weight, gestational age, and mode of delivery, residual confounding from unmeasured factors such as maternal diet, occupational exposure, and environmental air pollution cannot be excluded.

Second, the sample size was modest, particularly after sex stratification. Although comparable with previous meconium-based biomonitoring studies, limited statistical power may have reduced the ability to detect weaker associations17.

Third, because multiple statistical tests were performed, there is an inherent risk of type I error. To address this issue, false discovery rate correction was applied using the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure. The overall pattern of associations remained similar; however, none of the observed associations retained statistical significance after FDR correction. Consequently, the reported associations should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory findings.

Fourth, pesticide identification was based on HPLC with ultraviolet detection without confirmatory mass spectrometric analysis. Although HPLC-UV is a widely used and validated analytical approach, particularly in resource-limited settings, co-eluting compounds in complex biological matrices such as meconium may affect analytical specificity7,9. This limitation may be particularly relevant for compounds such as DDT/DDE, which exhibit relatively weak ultraviolet absorbance and may therefore be more susceptible to analytical interference in complex biological matrices. Nevertheless, the use of certified analytical standards, calibration procedures, recovery experiments, procedural blanks, and duplicate analyses supports the reliability of the reported measurements.

Finally, birth outcomes were analyzed as raw continuous variables rather than standardized z-scores adjusted for gestational age and sex. Although gestational age and neonatal sex were included as covariates in the regression models, the use of standardized growth references such as INTERGROWTH-21st would provide a more refined assessment and should be considered in future research.

Despite these limitations, this study represents one of the first biomonitoring investigations in Syria to simultaneously assess multiple environmental pollutants in meconium and examine sex-specific associations with neonatal outcomes. These findings support the importance of incorporating sex-specific analysis into environmental health research and underscore the need for larger prospective studies with refined exposure assessment to better understand the biological mechanisms underlying prenatal environmental susceptibility.

Conclusion

Prenatal exposure to multiple environmental pollutants was widely detected in meconium samples collected from newborns in this Syrian cohort, indicating common fetal exposure during pregnancy. Several sex-specific associations between pollutant concentrations and neonatal outcomes were observed in the initial regression analyses. However, none of these associations remained statistically significant after false discovery rate (FDR) correction and should therefore be considered exploratory.

These findings highlight the potential value of meconium biomonitoring for assessing cumulative prenatal exposure to environmental contaminants. Given the observational cross-sectional design, modest sample size, and analytical limitations related to pesticide confirmation, the results should be interpreted cautiously. Larger prospective studies using advanced analytical techniques, including confirmatory mass spectrometry, are needed to validate these findings and clarify the biological mechanisms underlying potential sex-specific susceptibility to environmental exposures.
