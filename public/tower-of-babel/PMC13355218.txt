Anthropogenic disturbance decouples coastal soil organic carbon and bulk density
Authors: Feixue Shen, Lin Yang, Xiuqiang Peng, Dianpeng Li, Chenconghai Yang, Yue Pu, Mao Guo, Yuru Yan, Chenghu Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCoastal blue carbon ecosystems play a pivotal role in mitigating global climate change through rapid sediment burial and efficient carbon preservation. However, accurate carbon accounting is severely hindered by the systematic neglect of soil bulk density (BD) variations and the uncritical reliance on terrestrial-derived pedotransfer functions, masking hidden uncertainties in regional carbon stock assessments. Here we present a high-resolution, multi-depth assessment of soil organic carbon (SOC) content and BD across a 1-m vertical gradient in the intensively managed coastal zone of Jiangsu Province, China. Machine learning frameworks reveal a striking spatial and vertical decoupling between SOC and BD driven by divergent environmental controls: SOC content responds to soil depth and ocean salinity, whereas BD aligns with hydro-geomorphic distance to the coast. Vegetation mediates a tight vertical negative coupling (p < 0.001) in natural salt marshes, whereas anthropogenic activities decouple this relationship in croplands, restricting standard pedotransfer function predictability in all layers (R2 ≤ 0.22). Across all layers, conventional spatial estimation models yield high baseline prediction uncertainty (RMSE = 0.22 g cm−3). These findings demonstrate that independent, high-resolution BD profiling is indispensable for valid blue carbon verification. Our results establish a transferable baseline to optimize depth-specific sampling and refine global coastal carbon accounting models under intensifying human pressures.Keywords: Coastal soil, Vertical sampling, Spatial heterogeneity, Digital soil mapping, Machine learning

Introduction

Coastal zones link terrestrial and marine biogeochemical processes and include both natural wetlands and human-modified lands (e.g., croplands and ponds) [1]. Despite intensive human disturbance, these zones play a pivotal role in the global carbon cycle through blue carbon ecosystems, which store and sequester carbon in coastal and marine environments, including salt marshes, mangroves, seagrass beds, and tidal flats [2]. This high carbon sequestration results from high productivity, rapid sediment burial, and efficient carbon preservation in waterlogged soils [[3], [4], [5], [6]]. Accurate estimation of soil organic carbon stocks in coastal zones depends on two interrelated yet distinct soil properties: soil organic carbon (SOC) content and bulk density (BD) [7]. In coastal environments, the spatial heterogeneity of soil properties is uniquely shaped by oceanographic factors, such as tidal inundation and salinity, in addition to the widely recognized drivers of terrestrial ecosystems [8]. Furthermore, these combined influences foster vertical stratification of coastal soil properties—particularly through the dynamic interplay among tidal cycles, vegetation-driven depth-dependent effects, and climate [[9], [10], [11], [12]]. Consequently, an integrated assessment of SOC content and BD across both spatial and vertical scales is essential to support reliable blue carbon accounting and advance coastal-specific carbon cycle science.

Recent advances in high-resolution studies of SOC spatial distribution and its environmental drivers in coastal environments have facilitated accurate coastal carbon accounting at large spatial scales, from regional to global [8,13,14]. These advances have been greatly empowered by digital soil mapping (DSM), which leverages multisource environmental covariates (e.g., remote sensing indices, climate, topography) to model complex, nonlinear relationships between SOC and its environmental drivers across space using machine learning techniques [[15], [16], [17]]. Beyond prediction, the integration of explainable artificial intelligence methods, such as Shapley additive explanations (SHAP), can enhance the interpretability of machine learning models, thereby identifying the global (i.e., overall dataset-level) and local key drivers in soil property prediction [18,19].

However, the spatial variation of BD has received considerably less attention than that of SOC, even though BD is a key indicator of soil physical structure, which directly regulates SOC accumulation and stabilization processes [20]. Emerging insights reposition BD as an active property rather than a static metric [21,22], implying its inherent spatial variability within complex coastal environments. Nevertheless, field-based BD observations are scarce across coastal landscapes compared to SOC [23,24], largely attributable to more labor-intensive and costly procedures [25]. As an alternative, pedotransfer functions (PTFs)—often relying on the terrestrial ecosystem-derived SOC–BD relationship—have been widely adopted to predict missing BD values [7,24,26]. This approach, however, inevitably introduces uncertainties in accurate SOC stock accounting [7,25,27,28]. Therefore, a more comprehensive understanding of the spatial distribution of BD and its coastal-specific relationship with SOC is urgently needed in coastal zones to improve the accuracy of carbon stock assessments.

A further challenge in accurately accounting for coastal carbon stocks is the inadequate characterization of vertical variation in SOC content and BD at regional scales in coastal environments. Most regional studies have adopted simplified depth stratifications to quantify SOC [29], typically grouping soil profiles into broad categories, such as topsoil and subsoil [30]. Such simplifications overlook the fine-scale vertical heterogeneity of SOC content and BD inherent in dynamic coastal environments. More critically, the vertical patterns of soil properties across various coastal landcover types remain largely unexplored, despite expectations of substantial variation. Given that coastal organic carbon sources are not exclusively plant-derived, distinct vertical patterns are anticipated in non-vegetated land-cover types compared with vegetated land-cover types [22,31,32]. Furthermore, in human-modified land-cover types, these vertical distributions are profoundly influenced by anthropogenic activities, such as cropland reclamation [33]. Therefore, a systematic study of vertical SOC content and BD patterns across diverse coastal land-cover types is crucial for advancing our fundamental understanding of coastal soil carbon distribution. Such a study would also inform and optimize vertical sampling strategies across different land-cover types. Furthermore, few studies have explicitly explored the vertical relationships between SOC and BD beyond the spatial dimension. Fine-scale vertical data can significantly enhance our understanding of the vertical SOC–BD relationship, offering new perspectives for achieving more accurate coastal carbon stock assessments.

To establish a comprehensive, coastal-specific understanding of the distributions of SOC content and BD, and their controlling factors, this study combined systematic field sampling with machine learning and explainable AI techniques from both spatial and vertical perspectives. The specific objectives were to: (1) characterize the vertical and spatial patterns of SOC content and BD across major coastal land-cover types; (2) identify and compare the key environmental drivers governing their variations; and (3) produce high-resolution, multi-depth maps of both properties to support regional carbon accounting and targeted climate mitigation strategies in coastal zones.

Materials and methods

Study area

This study was conducted in the coastal zone of Jiangsu Province, eastern China (119° 00′ E−122°18′ E, 31°37′ N–35°08′ N), an economically developed region characterized by intensive human activities. The study area spans the Yellow Sea coast and covers one-quarter of China's total coastal area [34,35]. The region lies along a climate transition from the warm temperate zone in the north to the subtropical zone in the south, featuring distinct seasonal variations, an average annual precipitation of 1000–1200 mm, and a mean annual temperature of 14–15 °C. Characterized by extensive mudflats and salt marshes, the region supports rich biodiversity and significant blue carbon storage [29,36,37]. The area also represents a typical socioecological system due to its rapid development and intense human–environment interactions [38]. We delineated the study area to capture the full spectrum of land–sea interactions (Fig. 1). The landward boundary extended approximately 10 km inland from the 2022 coastline, whereas the seaward boundary followed the outer limits of salt marshes, tidal flats, and permanent water bodies. This spatial delineation includes a broad range of land-cover types, including natural wetlands, such as salt marshes and tidal flats, and human-modified lands, such as agricultural areas and aquaculture ponds.

Soil sampling

We collected 123 soil profiles at 112 locations across the major land-cover types of the Jiangsu coastal zone during the growing seasons (June–August) of 2022–2024. Each profile extended to at least 1 m and sectioned at 10-cm intervals. The sampling strategy employed a transect-based approach [39,40], complemented by random point allocation to ensure comprehensive spatial coverage. Along established transects, sampling points were strategically positioned to represent the main land-cover types within coastal landscapes (Fig. 1). Due to accessibility constraints in tidal flats, fewer sampling sites could be established. To improve data robustness under these conditions, duplicate samples were collected at selected sites. The values from duplicate cores were averaged to represent each site and were not treated as independent samples in spatial analysis or modeling.

Landward soil cores were collected using portable gasoline-powered drills equipped with an inner polyvinyl chloride (PVC) tube, which allowed intact cores to be retrieved and sectioned in the field at standardized 10-cm intervals. At seaward sites, this drilling method was used only for a limited number of samples where soil conditions permitted. Most seaward cores, particularly those from challenging muddy tidal flats and salt marshes, were collected using manual hammering with PVC tubes. All PVC tubes had the same diameter and material to ensure consistency. Manual-hammered cores were transported intact to the laboratory for processing to preserve sediment structure.

Laboratory analysis and data processing

All soil samples were processed under controlled laboratory conditions to ensure measurement consistency. Due to the sedimentary and geomorphic conditions of the intertidal zones, conventional 100 cm3 rings could not be used for BD sampling. Instead, BD samples were collected at all sites using a calibrated polytetrafluoroethylene syringe sampler with a diameter of 1.5 cm and a volume of 4.416 cm3, which minimizes compaction effects during sampling (Supplementary Fig. S1). BD was calculated as the ratio of oven-dried soil mass, dried at 105 °C for 24 h, to its undisturbed sample volume [41]. SOC content was analyzed using the Walkley–Black potassium dichromate oxidation–reduction titration method, with a correction factor of 1.1 applied to account for incomplete oxidation [42,43], following the Chinese agricultural industry standard NY/T 1121.6-2006 [44]. According to this standard, the detection limit is approximately 0.1% carbon (i.e., 1 g C kg−1). Analytical precision is expressed as the permissible absolute deviation for parallel measurements; SOC content was estimated as 0.58 × SOM: the permissible absolute deviation is ≤ 0.5 g kg−1 for SOM <10 g kg−1, ≤1.0 g kg−1 for SOM of 10–40 g kg−1, and ≤3.0 g kg−1 for SOM >40 g kg−1. All SOC content measurements in this study strictly adhered to these precision criteria.

Depth-dependent data were aligned to common standardized depth intervals (e.g., 0–10 cm, 10–20 cm) to facilitate cross-site comparison. For PVC-tube samples from tidal flats, field constraints sometimes resulted in non-standard depth intervals. Digital soil mapping

Environmental covariates

In DSM, soil properties result from complex interactions among environmental covariates [45]. Selecting appropriate covariates is critical for accurate predictions, especially in coastal zones where both terrestrial and marine processes influence soil properties. We compiled five categories of environmental covariates (Table 1)—meteorological, topographic, hydrological, vegetative, and oceanographic factors—to affect the formation and distribution of SOC and BD.

Meteorological covariates. Albedo, precipitation, and temperature were used to represent climatic controls on soil organic matter dynamics. Temperature regulates microbial decomposition rates, while precipitation affects leaching, plant growth, and dissolved carbon transport. Albedo influences surface energy balance, indirectly modulating soil moisture and thermal conditions.

Topographic covariates. Elevation and slope were included to capture variation in drainage, erosion, and sediment deposition, which directly impact SOC accumulation and BD by altering soil structure and organic matter distribution.

Hydrological covariates. Surface runoff, distance to the coastline, and the water index were used to capture water-mediated processes that shape soil properties. Runoff affects erosion and sediment redistribution, while proximity to coastlines indicates exposure to tidal influences and fluctuating water tables, which can enhance organic matter preservation or alter soil compaction.

Vegetative covariates. Annual maximum normalized difference vegetation index (NDVI) was used as a proxy for vegetation productivity and organic inputs from biomass production and root activity, thereby influencing both SOC and soil structure.

Oceanographic covariates. Tidal range, wave height, and ocean salinity were included to represent marine influences on coastal soils. Tidal inundation and wave action modify soil texture and compaction, while salinity affects microbial activity and plant productivity, thereby regulating SOC dynamics.

To ensure consistency in modeling, all covariates not originally at 30 -m resolution were resampled to 30- m resolution using bilinear interpolation, following Liu et al. [49]. Static covariates (e.g., topography, parent material) were used directly. For time-series environmental covariates, values were uniformly extracted for 2022 to align with the 2022–2024 soil sampling period. Covariates with monthly data were aggregated into annual and seasonal means for spring, summer, autumn, and winter in 2022.

Prediction model construction and evaluation

We developed predictive models separately for SOC content and BD using a three-step workflow that combined environmental covariates with quantile random forest (QRF) modeling to learn soil–environment relationships for each property. In the data preparation phase, we aligned all environmental covariates (model inputs) with the sampling locations while retaining depth as a predictor variable, following Hengl et al. [50]. In the feature selection process, we employed recursive feature elimination [49] with tenfold cross-validation to identify optimal predictor sets for each target variable's unique relationships with environmental factors. In the model development phase, QRF models were fitted to generate both accurate predictions and quantifiable uncertainty estimates, particularly valuable for assessing the reliability of carbon stock assessments [51]. Model performance was rigorously evaluated using tenfold cross-validation with three metrics: root mean square error (RMSE), coefficient of determination (R2), and mean absolute error. R2 quantified the proportion of variance explained by the model, while RMSE (in g cm−1) was used to assess prediction accuracy, with lower values indicating better model fit. The final trained models were applied to generate spatially explicit predictions and uncertainties at 30 -m resolution across the study area for each soil property across the upper 1 m of soil at 10 -cm depth intervals.

Exploration of important drivers of SOC and BD

To comprehensively evaluate the influence of environmental factors on SOC and BD in the study area, we employed two complementary approaches for assessing variable importance within a machine learning framework.

First, we calculated the mean decrease in accuracy (MDA) method within the QRF model. MDA is a permutation-based algorithm that evaluates feature importance by measuring the average decrease in model prediction accuracy after randomly permuting each variable [52]. For each environmental factor, its values were shuffled across samples while keeping other variables unchanged, and the resulting decrease in model performance was calculated. Variables that cause larger decreases in accuracy when permuted are considered more important for model predictions. This approach provides a robust, model-agnostic measure of variable importance that accounts for both linear and nonlinear relationships between environmental factors and soil properties as well as potential interactions among variables.

Second, we used SHAP to provide global variable importance rankings and local, instance-level explanations for individual predictions. SHAP values, based on cooperative game theory [53], quantify the marginal contribution of each feature to individual predictions by computing the average contribution across all possible feature coalitions [54]. For each sample location and depth, SHAP values were calculated using the iml package in R, which implements model-agnostic interpretation methods. This approach was used to understand how environmental factors influence SOC and BD predictions at specific spatial locations and depths, revealing spatial heterogeneity in the importance of factors that global importance metrics cannot capture.

Together, MDA and SHAP provided complementary perspectives on environmental controls of SOC and BD. Both methods can identify the dominant predictors at the model level, and SHAP further resolved how their contributions varied across locations and soil depths. This combined analysis therefore captured both the overall importance of environmental factors and the spatially and depth-dependent direction and magnitude of their effects.

Quantifying SOC–BD relationships

Correlation analysis

To compare BD–SOC coupling across spatial and vertical dimensions, we quantified their associations using a unified correlation framework. Assessment of normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test [55] indicated that SOC content data did not follow a normal distribution in several land-cover types and depth intervals (p < 0.05). Accordingly, correlation analyses were conducted using Kendall's rank correlation coefficient (Kendall's tau), a nonparametric method that does not assume bivariate normality, implemented with the cor.test function in R, with statistical significance determined using the normal approximation [56]. Spatial coupling was evaluated separately for each depth interval by correlating measured BD and SOC content values across all samples and within each land-cover class. Vertical coupling was assessed for each land-cover type by correlating mean BD and SOC content values across depth intervals.

Results

Vertical distribution of SOC content and BD samples across land-cover types

The vertical distributions of SOC content and BD exhibited clear differentiation between vegetated and non-vegetated land-cover types (Fig. 2). In vegetated areas, including croplands, afforested lands, and salt marshes dominated by Spartina alterniflora, SOC content generally decreased with soil depth, yet the depth at which SOC content began to decrease varied among land-cover types. For instance, average SOC content in salt marshes decreased continuously with depth, whereas in croplands and afforested lands, it remained relatively constant below 40 cm. In contrast, non-vegetated or sparsely vegetated areas (e.g., aquaculture ponds, tidal flats, and abandoned farmlands) displayed more complex, nonlinear vertical trends. In tidal flats, SOC content even increased slightly with increasing depth.

Black points indicate means, and black error bars denote ±1 standard deviation. Violin width indicates the probability density of the data.

The vertical distribution of BD also varied significantly across land-cover types. In cropland, BD increased in the upper 30 cm but then decreased slightly below this depth. Aquaculture ponds displayed minor fluctuations in BD, while tidal flats maintained nearly uniform BD values throughout the profile. Conversely, in salt marshes, BD progressively increased with depth. Soils with historical land-use changes (e.g., abandoned farmland and afforested land) exhibited more complex and heterogeneous BD depth profiles, likely reflecting legacy effects of past management practices.

Vertical and spatial relationships between SOC and BD samples across land-cover types

BD and SOC were generally negatively correlated in space, but the strength of this relationship varied with land-cover types and soil depth (Fig. 3). Among all land-cover types, salt marshes exhibited the strongest and most depth-consistent negative spatial relationship between SOC and BD, whereas tidal flats also showed relatively strong correlations across almost all depth intervals. Aquaculture ponds, although human-modified, exhibited strong correlations at certain depth intervals, similar to those observed in salt marshes. In contrast, other human-disturbed land-cover types exhibited varied relationships. Croplands showed a significant negative correlation (p < 0.05) only in the surface soil (0–20 cm), while afforested lands and abandoned farmlands exhibited considerable variation with depth. These results suggest that anthropogenic activities may significantly alter the inherent relationship between SOC and BD in coastal soils, with the extent of alteration varying by land-cover type and depth.

The vertical SOC–BD relationship also varied among land-cover types (Fig. 3). Only salt marshes dominated by Spartina alterniflora exhibited a significant negative correlation between SOC content and BD along the soil profile; this vertical correlation was even stronger than the spatial relationship at each depth (p < 0.001). For other land-cover types, the correlations were not statistically significant (p > 0.05). Cropland even showed a weak positive correlation, deviating from the general expectation of a negative SOC–BD relationship. These results suggest that the vertical relationship between SOC and BD is strongly modulated by land-cover type.

Key drivers of SOC and BD spatial and vertical distribution

At the regional scale, global variable-importance rankings identified soil depth as the dominant predictor of SOC distribution, followed by oceanographic, hydrological, and meteorological factors (Fig. 4a). Both MDA and SHAP analysis based on machine learning models consistently identified winter salinity at 0 m depth (salinity0_winter) as one of the most influential oceanographic variables (Fig. 4), with detailed model performance metrics provided in Supplementary Table S1. Based on the most influential predictors identified by the two model-derived methods, we further stratified all grid cells within the study area according to depth and salinity intervals of salinity0_winter. The results showed that SOC content was generally higher in the top 0–30 cm soil layer, with particularly elevated values observed under both low (1.60–6.11) and high (12.53–13.17) salinity conditions (Supplementary Fig. S2a). Below 30 cm, SOC content was generally lower, except under the highest salinity range (12.86–13.17), where it reached its maximum. These findings indicate a positive correlation between high salinity and SOC content, whereas under low salinity conditions, SOC remained predominantly influenced by soil depth.

For bulk density, the controlling factors were more complex, with distance to the coast being the most important predictor, followed by soil depth, meteorological factors, vegetative factors, and hydrological factors (Fig. 4b). Further analysis of the most important factors revealed that BD in the surface layer (0–10 cm) exhibited limited variation with increasing distance from the coast, with the lowest values consistently observed in the nearest coastal areas. In contrast, BD in layers below 10 cm generally increased with distance from the coast, with the most pronounced increase observed in the middle soil layer (20–60 cm) (Supplementary Fig. S2b).

Local-scale controls on SOC and BD also differed across space and depth, as indicated by the two most influential predictors at each grid cell (Supplementary Fig. S3), where influence is defined as the absolute SHAP value. The dominant factors regulating SOC exhibited pronounced spatial and vertical variations, as visualized in the three-dimensional plots provided in the supplementary materials. In the northernmost areas above 34.8° N, SOC was influenced by multiple factors, whereas across northern transects above 34.5° N, it was predominantly controlled by oceanographic factors at almost all soil depths. In the central region (approximately 32.5° N–34.5° N) and the south, SOC controls were more spatially heterogeneous, with a larger proportion of sampling sites in these regions primarily influenced by vegetative or meteorological factors. However, in southern areas (latitude <32.5° N), the proportion of sites dominated by vegetation decreased. By contrast, BD was primarily governed by oceanographic factors across most sampling locations and soil depths. Nevertheless, as with SOC, vegetation- and climate-related factors exerted controlling effects at specific depths within the central region.

Maps of SOC content and BD at multiple depths in the Jiangsu coastal zone

The Jiangsu coastal zone exhibited distinct depth-dependent spatial patterns in SOC content distribution (Fig. 5a). In the topsoil (0–30 cm), SOC content was generally higher in the northern and central regions than in the southern regions. Concurrently, within the 0–40 cm layers of the southern regions, the landward side of the shoreline showed substantially greater SOC content than the seaward side. Below 40 cm depth, SOC content levels decreased across most of the study area, with two notable exceptions: the northern regions maintained relatively high SOC content, and the seaward areas of the southern region exhibited increasing SOC content in deeper layers.

The spatial patterns of BD differed markedly from those of SOC content (Fig. 5b). While most northern areas consistently showed lower BD values with depth, the landward side displayed an obvious increase in BD in the 20–30 cm layer. However, below 30 cm, BD showed a consistent spatial pattern, with the lowest values in the north, the highest value in the central region, and intermediate values in the south. Furthermore, at these deeper layers, the landward side consistently exhibited higher BD values than the seaward side.

We further quantified the vertical correlation between SOC content and BD at the pixel scale (Supplementary Fig. S4). Negative correlations were not universal across the study area, but were most pronounced in the north-central region. In contrast, this negative relationship was weaker in the landward areas of the northern region, where positive correlations predominated. Positive correlations also occurred in some seaward tidal flats and landward areas in the south. These findings highlight the spatial heterogeneity of the vertical SOC–BD relationship, indicating that its underlying drivers require a context-specific understanding.

Discussion

Different dominant controlling factors for BD and SOC and underlying mechanisms governing their vertical and spatial patterns

Previous studies focused primarily on the environmental drivers of SOC distribution, often treating BD merely as a factor influencing SOC [24,60], overlooking its fundamental role as a core soil property [28]. This limitation is particularly concerning in coastal wetlands, where unique land–sea interactions may alter the mechanisms governing BD formation. In this study, we conducted high-vertical-resolution field sampling and high-resolution (30 m) DSM to simultaneously investigate the distributions of SOC content and BD in the coastal zone of Jiangsu, China. Machine-learning-based importance analyses revealed distinct primary controlling factors governing the vertical and spatial patterns of SOC content and BD at both regional and local scales, demonstrating that BD is influenced by a more diverse set of environmental factors.

At the regional scale, SOC content patterns were mainly influenced by soil depth and ocean salinity, and their vertical and spatial distributions were well explained by these two controls. The general decline of SOC content with depth reflects a typical vertical attenuation pattern driven by decreasing organic inputs from surface vegetation and the cumulative effects of decomposition in deeper layers [61]. This pattern was most pronounced in the central region (Fig. 5), where denser vegetation enhanced surface carbon inputs, thereby strengthening the vertical gradient. This observation is consistent with local-scale analyses showing that SOC in this region is primarily controlled by vegetation and meteorological factors (Supplementary Fig. S3). By contrast, some southern areas exhibited an anomalous increase in SOC content with depth, a pattern consistently observed in field samples from non-vegetated tidal flats (Fig. 2). SHAP analysis indicated that SOC in these areas was primarily influenced by marine factors, such as tidal range, which may facilitate the transport and burial of marine-derived organic carbon into deeper layers. This aligns with previous studies demonstrating that tidal action is a key driver of subsoil organic carbon accumulation in coastal wetlands [62]. In addition, the positive SOC–salinity relationship reflects the inhibitory effect of salinity on microbial decomposition(Supplementary Material Fig. S2a) [63].

Bulk density exhibited more complex controls than SOC (Fig. 4), reflecting the key role of hydro-geomorphic processes (e.g., distance from the sea) and the combined influence of multiple factors (e.g., soil depth, meteorological, and vegetative factors). On a regional scale, BD at each depth generally increased with increasing distance from the coast. This pattern reflects the diminishing influence of tidal energy inland, which allows progressive sediment consolidation at greater depths [11,64]. However, not all regions or depths strictly followed this pattern. BD in the 0–10 cm layer showed limited variation with distance from the coast. This indicates that BD in the topsoil is counteracted by several homogenizing mechanisms, such as the accumulation of organic matter. Meanwhile, our mapping results show that BD below 20 cm in the north-central region remains consistently high across the land–sea gradient. This may be attributed to long-term agricultural cultivation [65], which leads to soil compaction in the subsoil. These depth- and region-dependent BD patterns underscore that BD distribution in coastal zones is shaped by the interplay of regional hydrogeomorphic processes and local environmental controls.

Land cover–dependent vertical relationship between BD and SOC in coastal soils

The relationship between SOC and BD is often assumed to be negative, as organic matter typically reduces soil compaction and enhances porosity [7,21]. However, our site-based analysis revealed that this SOC–BD relationships were generally consistent across spatial dimensions but varied by land-cover type along the vertical dimension.

Cropland was the only land-cover type exhibiting a positive vertical SOC content–BD correlation, although this relationship was not significant (p > 0.05). This pattern probably reflects the contrasting vertical trends of SOC content and BD in surface soil (0–30 cm; Fig. 2), combined with anthropogenic disturbance and historical land-use legacies. In the 0–30 cm layer, SOC content decreased with depth, whereas BD increased, reflecting the combined effects of organic matter attenuation and plow-pan compaction from long-term tillage. Below 30 cm, however, both SOC content and BD decreased slightly, indicating that the subsoil remains largely unaffected by mechanical compaction and thus preserves the original loose structure of coastal sediments.

Vegetation appeared to mediate the strength of vertical SOC–BD coupling in coastal lands. In non-vegetated soils, including tidal flats and aquaculture ponds, SOC content and BD showed minimal variation with depth, resulting in nonsignificant negative relationships. In contrast, vertical SOC–BD coupling was even stronger than the spatial pattern in Jiangsu's salt marshes dominated by Spartina alterniflora, where the root system frequently extends beyond 1 m [6]. These differences are likely due to the absence of biological structuring agents, such as roots, in non-vegetated soils, as roots can enhance macroporosity, promote aggregate formation, and directly contribute organic matter, thereby reducing BD while facilitating SOC accumulation. These findings suggest that effectively leveraging this strong vertical relationship in vegetated areas can provide a mechanistic basis for improving BD estimation in data-scarce coastal zone scenarios. Conversely, the weak vertical SOC content–BD correlation observed in non-vegetated coastal areas necessitates greater caution in depth-specific BD assessments.

Evaluating PTFs for BD estimation in coastal environments

The negative relationship between SOC and BD is well established in terrestrial soils [66,67]. This relationship forms the basis for the empirical PTFs to estimate BD [26,27,57]. In this study, we evaluated three commonly used PTF forms using our field-collected data in coastal zones (Supplementary Fig. S7). The exponential model demonstrated the best overall performance (Fig. 6a), but its accuracy remained limited when applied across all samples at each depth, yielding moderate depth-averaged metrics (R2 = 0.34, RMSE = 0.22 g cm−3).

Points are colored by land-cover types, and curves show exponential fits. b–c, Coefficient of determination (R2, b) and root mean square error (RMSE, g cm−3; c) of the exponential models fitted to all samples and to individual land-cover classes.

Model performance also varied across land-cover types. The models performed poorly in human-disturbed areas, with R2 values typically less than 0.25 at most depths, particularly croplands (Fig. 6b). The limited accuracy in anthropogenic land-cover types reflects the decoupling of the SOC–BD relationship under anthropogenic disturbances, where additional controls on BD, such as mechanical compaction, tillage mixing, and altered sediment supply, disrupt their natural coupling. This is consistent with our finding that BD is influenced by more factors than SOC in our study. In contrast, models for natural land-cover types, such as salt marshes and tidal flats, exhibited higher R2; however, they also showed high predictive uncertainty, as indicated by high RMSE values (Fig. 6c). This uncertainty likely resulted from inherent environmental heterogeneity driven by oceanographic factors, as we found in our study. This finding cautions against overinterpreting model performance in coastal natural wetlands and suggests that incorporating oceanographic drivers into PTF development could be crucial for constructing new coastal-specific PTFs with lower uncertainty.

Furthermore, given that PTFs are widely employed in regional and global carbon accounting, particularly in contexts where field-measured BD data are insufficient or entirely absent, we assessed PTF performance under increasing levels of missing data. This was achieved by performing 100 repeated random training–test partitions at each depth, simulating missing-data proportions ranging from 20% to 80%. Our analysis revealed that as the proportion of missing data increased, RMSE values rose systematically across all soil layers (Fig. 7a), while R2 values showed corresponding decreases, except for the 40–50 cm layer (Fig. 7b). PTF performance was consistently weaker in surface soils, where environmental disturbance was most pronounced, compared with deeper layers.

These results collectively indicate that while SOC content-based PTFs in the spatial dimension offer a practical approach to BD estimation in data-scarce scenarios, their application in coastal environments requires careful consideration of both land-cover type and soil depth. In human-modified land-cover types, elevated uncertainty suggests that region-specific PTF calibration or the incorporation of ancillary environmental variables may be necessary to improve BD predictive accuracy and support reliable blue carbon stock assessments.

Implications for BD sampling strategies and BD estimation in coastal SOC stock assessment

To improve the accuracy of SOC stock assessments in coastal environments, our findings suggest a land cover–based sampling strategy. First, in vegetated coastal zones, the sampling depth for BD should account for the distribution of plant roots. In ecosystems dominated by deep-rooted vegetation, such as Spartina alterniflora, sampling should extend to greater depths. In contrast, in non-vegetated areas, such as tidal flats, where both BD and SOC content exhibit minimal vertical variation, sampling resolution and depth can be reduced appropriately under budget constraints. Second, sampling density should be increased in human-modified areas, such as croplands, where PTFs perform the poorest. These environments exhibit more complex SOC–BD relationships that may be altered by anthropogenic activities.

Where BD data are unavailable, SOC content-derived PTFs should be applied. The divergence in driving mechanisms between SOC and BD indicates that these two properties are relatively independent in coastal environments, and that the tightness of their relationship is context-dependent. However, in settings where the SOC–BD relationship is well established, such as in natural wetlands, the spatial relationship may still perform reasonably well. In vegetated wetlands with deep roots, such as salt marshes, the vertical relationship may also be acceptable for estimation purposes.

Limitations and outlook

This study has several limitations. First, our DSM approach was constrained by the resolution and accuracy of the input environmental covariates. This limitation was particularly pronounced for vertical information, as many environmental factors lack multi-depth data or have low vertical resolution, including key drivers of coastal SOC such as ocean salinity. Our analysis was also based on a single-field survey and therefore could not capture potential temporal changes in soil properties. Future studies should integrate high-resolution hydrodynamic modeling and depth-specific remote sensing products to improve vertical representation and incorporate time-series soil sampling and long-term remote sensing observations to capture temporal variability.

Second, although the overall sample size was relatively large for a coastal soil study, seaward sampling sites were limited by challenging soil conditions (e.g., waterlogging, soft sediment, tidal inundation). This limitation is relevant to the positive correlation observed in southern tidal flats, which could reflect biogeochemical processes or sediment dynamics that differ from the more commonly observed negative correlations. Thus, innovative sampling methodologies should be developed to obtain more field samples along seaward transects.

Furthermore, although we observed a vegetation-mediated, land-cover-dependent vertical relationship between SOC and BD, the underlying mechanisms remain to be elucidated. In the vegetated wetlands of the coastal zone, further investigation is needed to understand how vegetation type, root traits, and hydrology regulate the vertical coupling between SOC and BD. In human-modified lands, future work could focus on how management practices such as tillage and irrigation alter soil structure and decouple the SOC–BD relationship. Clarifying these mechanisms will advance coastal-specific BD PTFs and improve the accuracy of coastal carbon assessment.

Conclusions

This study provides a high-resolution, multi-depth assessment of SOC content and BD across the Jiangsu coastal zone, linking their distributions, environmental controls, and SOC–BD relationships across contrasting land-cover types. Field measurements and machine-learning analyses showed that SOC content and BD had land-cover-dependent vertical patterns and distinct environmental controls. SOC is mainly governed by ocean salinity and soil depth, whereas BD responds to a broader set of environmental controls, with distance to the coastline as the primary factor. High-resolution maps revealed that SOC content exhibited considerable spatial variation across depth layers, while BD maintained relatively stable spatial patterns below 40 cm. Contrary to the conventional expectation of a coupled SOC–BD relationship, SOC and BD were decoupled in some land-cover types and depth intervals, particularly in intensively human-modified lands such as croplands. In the vertical dimension, SOC–BD relationship varied across land-cover types and may be mediated by vegetation. These findings support a land-cover-based sampling strategy: high-resolution vertical sampling should be prioritized in vegetated wetlands and intensively human-modified coastal zones, whereas lower-resolution sampling should be sufficient in non-vegetated areas to enhance cost efficiency. Overall, these insights advance mechanistic understanding of coastal soil properties, highlighting the need to directly measure BD and investigate its formation mechanisms to improve coastal carbon accounting. Our study offers actionable strategies to reduce uncertainty in coastal carbon accounting, thereby supporting informed decision-making for coastal zone management and climate mitigation.
