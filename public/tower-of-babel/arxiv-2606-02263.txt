Exact Sampling of Permutations with a Prescribed Longest Increasing Subsequence Length
Peter Clifford, Raphaël Clifford

Abstract
We study exact uniform sampling of permutations of length n n whose longest increasing subsequence (LIS) has prescribed length k k . Our main result is, to the best of our knowledge, the first polynomial-time exact sampler for this problem, valid for every 1 ≤ k ≤ n 1\leq k\leq n . Via the Robinson–Schensted correspondence the problem reduces to sampling a Young diagram with first row of length k k from the hook-length-squared (conditioned Plancherel) law. We sample this shape one coordinate at a time, where each conditional weight, a sum over exponentially many completions, collapses, through the Cauchy–Binet formula, to a single coefficient of the determinant of a small polynomial matrix. A direct implementation runs in expected O ~ ​ ( n 4 ​ k 5 ) \tilde{O}(n^{4}k^{5}) time in the word-RAM model. Exploiting the Hankel structure of the evaluated matrices reduces this to O ~ ​ ( n 3 ​ k 4 ) \tilde{O}(n^{3}k^{4}) . In the linear regime k ∈ Θ ⁡ ( n ) k\in\Theta(n) we give a direct rejection sampler running in expected O ⁡ ( n ​ log ⁡ log ​ n ) O(n\log\log n) time, matching, up to constants, the cost of computing the LIS of one permutation. For the relaxed constraint LIS ⁡ ( π ) ≤ k \operatorname{LIS}(\pi)\leq k , plain rejection sampling gives expected O ⁡ ( n ​ log ⁡ log ​ n ) O(n\log\log n) time for every k ≥ 4 ​ n k\geq 4\sqrt{n} , and so the worst case over all k k improves to O ~ ​ ( n 5 ) \tilde{O}(n^{5}) .

Introduction

The longest increasing subsequence is one of the most intensively studied
of all permutation statistics. Interest in its behaviour on a uniformly
random permutation goes back to Ulam, and its analysis, through
Hammersley’s interacting particle process, the limit-shape theorems of
Logan–Shepp and Vershik–Kerov, and the Baik–Deift–Johansson fluctuation
theorem, helped to build the modern theory connecting random
permutations to random matrices and determinantal processes

[
17
,
24
,
35
,
20
,
5
,
7
,
26
]
. See the papers of Aldous and
Diaconis
[
2
,
3
]
, Stanley’s
survey
[
33
]
, and Romik’s book
[
29
]

for a comprehensive introduction.

This paper asks a basic algorithmic question about this much-studied
statistic, a question that appears to have gone unaddressed. Can one
efficiently sample a permutation of length
n

uniformly at random
, subject to
the constraint that its LIS has a prescribed length
k
? Formally, for a
permutation
\pi\in S_{n}
, let
\operatorname{LIS}(\pi)
denote the length of its longest
increasing subsequence. Given input parameters
(n,k)
, we want to
sample exactly from the uniform distribution on

\Omega_{n,k}:=\{\pi\in S_{n}:\operatorname{LIS}(\pi)=k\}.

Simple approaches fail.

A uniform permutation has
\operatorname{LIS}
equal to a specific
k
with probability
that can be as small as
1/n!
, so rejection sampling from
S_{n}
is hopeless
in general. The one exception is a narrow window around the typical value

k\approx 2\sqrt{n}
, where plain rejection sampling runs in polynomial time. Elsewhere
\mathbf{P}(\operatorname{LIS}=k)
is superpolynomially small. A potentially viable route is to attempt
Markov chain Monte Carlo on
\Omega_{n,k}
, but no chain with a provable
polynomial mixing time is known and our goal is exact, not approximate,
sampling. The Robinson–Schensted correspondence reduces the problem to
sampling a Young diagram with first row of length
k
from a
hook-length-squared distribution, but there are
\exp(\Theta(\sqrt{n}))

partitions of
n
, so a naive dynamic program over shapes is also
superpolynomial.

Summary of results.

Our main result is, to the best of our knowledge, the first

\Omega_{n,k}

Techniques.

The main sampler rests on the Robinson–Schensted correspondence, which
reduces uniform sampling from
\Omega_{n,k}
to sampling a Young diagram
with first row of length
k
from Plancherel measure conditioned on

\lambda_{1}=k
. We conjugate the shape and apply a coordinate shift,
re-expressing this law as a fixed-sum distribution on strict partitions
whose weight is a squared Vandermonde factor times independent factorial
weights. The sampler then exposes the shifted coordinates one at a time.
At each partial state, it chooses the next coordinate with probability
proportional to the total weight of all valid completions.

The completion sums range over exponentially many suffixes, but they are
not enumerated. By Cauchy–Binet, each such sum is a coefficient of the
determinant of a polynomial moment matrix. At each scalar evaluation this
moment matrix is Hankel, so the needed determinant values can be computed
from
O(k)
moments in near-linear time. The resulting completion scores
are exact integers. They are computed modulo word-size primes and
reconstructed by Chinese remaindering, so no rounding enters the
procedure and the output law is exactly uniform.

Conceptually, this is an extension-counting sampler in the sense of
Jerrum, Valiant and Vazirani
[
19
]
. Exact
counts of completions from each partial state determine the conditional
distribution of the next step. The new ingredient here is the
determinant-based oracle that makes these completion counts efficient.

For the linear regime where
k\in\Theta(n)
a lighter idea suffices. We sample from an expanded
space of pairs
(\pi,I)
in which
I
is a distinguished
k
-term increasing
subsequence of the permutation
\pi
, and accept only when
I
is the
leftmost
LIS of

\pi
. This is a canonical witness computable in
O(n\log\log n)
time. Every
permutation with
\operatorname{LIS}=k
then survives in exactly one accepted pair, so
acceptance yields exact uniformity, and a Plancherel-measure tail bound shows
that the acceptance probability is bounded below by a constant. This same rejection sampling idea gives a polynomial-time sampling algorithm as long as
k\in\Omega(n/\log n)
.

On static inputs, the computation of the LIS admits an

On dynamic inputs, variants of the problem have been studied in

These results concern computing or approximating the LIS of a given

permutation conditioned on having a prescribed LIS length.

The closest exact-sampling results we are aware of are also based on Robinson–Schensted-type correspondences and Young tableaux, but they sample different ensembles. Thomas and Yong use Hecke insertion to give an exact polynomial-time sampler for a Plancherel-type measure, while Betea, Boutillier, Bouttier, Chapuy, Corteel, and Vuletić give perfect samplers for Schur processes
[

6

. These samplers produce Plancherel- or Schur-type random shapes in which the total size, and in particular the first row length, are not fixed in advance. By contrast, uniform sampling from
\Omega_{n,k}
requires the two hard constraints
|\lambda|=n
and
\lambda_{1}=k
. One could try to recover these constraints by rejection, but the probability of simultaneously obtaining the prescribed size and first row length is too small in the regimes considered here. Our sampler instead enforces the condition
\lambda_{1}=k
by construction. To the best of our knowledge, exact uniform sampling from
\Omega_{n,k}
has not previously been studied.

The counting counterpart follows from classical components. Write
|\Omega_{n,\leq k}|
for the number of permutations of
[n]
with
\operatorname{LIS}\leq k
. Gessel’s identity
expresses the generating function of these counts as a
k\times k
Toeplitz determinant of
modified Bessel functions of the first kind
[
14
]
.

\sum_{n\geq 0}\frac{|\Omega_{n,\leq k}|}{(n!)^{2}}\,x^{2n}=\det\bigl(I_{i-j}(2x)\bigr)_{1\leq i,j\leq k},

so that
|\Omega_{n,\leq k}|=(n!)^{2}\,[x^{2n}]\det(I_{i-j}(2x))_{1\leq i,j\leq k}
. The exact constraint count then follows
by subtraction:

|\Omega_{n,k}|=|\Omega_{n,\leq k}|-|\Omega_{n,\leq k-1}|.

Algorithmically, truncating each entry modulo
x^{2n+1}
and taking the

k\times k
determinant over the truncated power-series ring

\mathbb{F}[x]/(x^{2n+1})
, computed modulo word-size primes and
reconstructed by the CRT, yields the exact integer counts in

\tilde{O}(k^{3}n^{3})
word operations. We stress, however, that our sampler is
not a simple extension of this counting method. Gessel’s determinant
returns only the single aggregate
|\Omega_{n,k}|
, whereas exact
sequential sampling requires, at every partial state, the total weight of
all valid completions of that state, a conditional finite-
n
refinement of
the same enumeration that the aggregate identity does not provide.
Supplying this entire family of completion counts is the role of the
determinant oracle of Section
3.3
.
|\Omega_{n,k}|
itself is
recovered, up to the fixed normalisation, as the single completion sum at
the empty prefix.

Overview of the shape sampler.

The challenge is to sample the Robinson–Schensted shape conditioned on

\lambda_{1}=k
with weight
(f^{\lambda})^{2}
, where direct enumeration over the up
to
\binom{n}{k}
feasible shapes is not polynomial. Three ideas remove the obstacles to an efficient algorithm in turn. These are a reparameterisation that separates the weight, a determinantal identity that collapses the completion sum, and the Hankel structure that makes the resulting determinant computation cheap. Conjugating the partition and applying
the shift
x_{i}:=\rho_{i}+k-i
rewrites the hook-length formula as

(f^{\lambda})^{2}\propto\Delta(x)^{2}\prod_{i=1}^{k}x_{i}!^{-2}
, separating the
per-coordinate factorial weights from the squared Vandermonde coupling

\Delta(x)^{2}
. This separated weight admits a one-coordinate-at-a-time sampler
that, given a prefix
p
, draws the next coordinate
t
with probability
proportional to the total weight of all completions. The obstacle is that each
such
completion score
sums over up to exponentially many suffixes. By the
Cauchy–Binet formula that sum collapses to a single coefficient of the
determinant of a polynomial matrix, computable in polynomial time. When the
matrix is evaluated at a scalar
\xi
it is
Hankel
. Its determinant
costs
\tilde{O}(L)
rather than
O(L^{3})
, which drives the fast implementation.

The rest of this section formalises the weight, defines the completion scores,
gives the determinant oracle, and proves correctness of the sequential shape
sampler. Section
3.5
completes the reduction to permutations,
Section
3.6

Section
4

3.1
Reparameterisation

We first recall the hook-length formula. For a partition
\nu
and a cell

u=(i,j)
of its Young diagram, let
h_{\nu}(u)=\nu_{i}-j+\nu^{\prime}_{j}-i+1
denote the
hook length of
u
(the number of cells consisting of
u
, those to its right
in row
i
, and those below it in column
j
), where
\nu^{\prime}
is the conjugate
partition whose parts are the column lengths of
\nu
. Write

H_{\nu}:=\prod_{u\in\nu}h_{\nu}(u)
. The hook-length
formula states that
f^{\nu}=|\nu|!/H_{\nu}
, where
f^{\nu}=|\operatorname{SYT}(\nu)|

[
12
]
.

We work with the conjugate partition
\rho=\lambda^{\prime}
rather than
\lambda

directly. Its parts
\rho_{i}
are the column heights of
\lambda
, and it is
these that appear naturally in the hook-length formula. Since
\lambda_{1}=k
,
the partition
\rho
has exactly
k
positive parts, where
\rho_{i}
counts
the rows of
\lambda
of length at least
i
, satisfying

\rho_{1}\geq\rho_{2}\geq\cdots\geq\rho_{k}\geq 1,\qquad\rho_{1}+\cdots+\rho_{k}=n.

Set
x_{i}:=\rho_{i}+k-i
and
N:=n+\binom{k}{2}
. The shift turns the weakly
decreasing sequence
\rho
into the strictly decreasing sequence

n\geq x_{1}>x_{2}>\cdots>x_{k}\geq 1,\qquad x_{1}+\cdots+x_{k}=N,

and the map
x\mapsto\rho
given by
\rho_{i}=x_{i}-k+i
is its inverse. Thus the
shapes
\lambda\vdash n
with
\lambda_{1}=k
are in bijection with the strict
partitions
x
of
N
into exactly
k
parts, each at most
n
. This is the same shift Johansson used to express Plancherel measure as a squared Vandermonde factor times independent weights
[
21
]
. Here we repurpose it for purely algorithmic purposes and apply it to the conjugate partition. Concretely, for the shape of Figure
1
, where
n=7
and
k=4
, we have
\lambda=(4,3)
,
\rho=(2,2,2,1)
,
x=(5,4,3,1)
, and
N=7+\binom{4}{2}=13
.

For a strict partition
x=(x_{1},\dots,x_{k})
, write

\Delta(x):=\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq k}(x_{i}-x_{j})
.

The following lemma makes the factored form of the target weight explicit.
It is the reason the
x
coordinates are useful. The hook-length formula,
when rewritten in terms of
x
, separates into a Vandermonde factor and
independent factorial weights, yielding a target distribution that admits
an efficient sequential sampler.

Lemma 3.1
.

Under the bijection above,

f^{\rho}=n!\,\frac{\Delta(x)}{\prod_{i=1}^{k}x_{i}!}.

Consequently the target distribution on strict partitions
x
is proportional to

\operatorname{wt}(x):=\Delta(x)^{2}\prod_{i=1}^{k}x_{i}!^{-2}.

Proof.

By the hook-length formula
f^{\rho}=n!/H_{\rho}
. For row
i
of
\rho
, the hook
lengths
h(i,c)=\rho_{i}-c+\rho^{\prime}_{c}-i+1
as
c
ranges from
1
to
\rho_{i}
give
the integers
1,\dots,\rho_{i}+k-i
with the values
\rho_{i}-\rho_{j}+j-i
for

j>i
omitted. Hence

\prod_{c=1}^{\rho_{i}}h(i,c)=\frac{(\rho_{i}+k-i)!}{\prod_{j>i}(\rho_{i}-\rho_{j}+j-i)}.

Multiplying over
i=1,\dots,k
gives

H_{\rho}=\frac{\prod_{i=1}^{k}(\rho_{i}+k-i)!}{\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq k}(\rho_{i}-\rho_{j}+j-i)}.

Substituting
x_{i}=\rho_{i}+k-i
yields
(\rho_{i}+k-i)!=x_{i}!
and

\rho_{i}-\rho_{j}+j-i=x_{i}-x_{j}
, so
H_{\rho}=\prod_{i}x_{i}!/\Delta(x)
and

f^{\rho}=n!\,\Delta(x)/\prod_{i}x_{i}!
.

Since transposition preserves the number of standard Young tableaux,

f^{\lambda}=f^{\rho}
. Therefore

(f^{\lambda})^{2}=(f^{\rho})^{2}=(n!)^{2}\Delta(x)^{2}\prod_{i}x_{i}!^{-2}.

As
(n!)^{2}
is independent of
x
, the induced distribution on
x
is
proportional to
\operatorname{wt}(x)
.

3.2
Completion scores

We sample the strict sequence
x_{1}>\cdots>x_{k}
one coordinate at a time. At each step, having fixed a prefix

p=(p_{1},\dots,p_{r})
with
p_{1}>\cdots>p_{r}\geq 1
, we choose the next coordinate

t
with probability proportional to the total
\operatorname{wt}
-mass of all valid
extensions of
(p_{1},\dots,p_{r},t)
to a full strict sequence. These masses are
the completion scores
S_{p}(t)
, which we define and compute below.

At a prefix
p=(p_{1},\ldots,p_{r})
of length
r<k
, define the upper bound

B(p):=\begin{cases}n,&r=0,\\
p_{r}-1,&r>0.\end{cases}

The admissible candidates for the next coordinate are then

t\in\{1,\ldots,B(p)\}
.

For such an admissible
t
, write

L:=k-r-1,\quad s(t):=N-\textstyle\sum_{i=1}^{r}p_{i}-t,\quad E_{t}:=\{1,\dots,t-1\}.

A valid suffix is an
L
-element subset
A\subseteq E_{t}
with
\sum A=s(t)
.
For a finite set
A
, write

\Delta(A):=\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}a,b\in A\\
a<b\end{subarray}}(b-a),

with the convention
\Delta(\varnothing)=1
.

Since the sampler uses only relative probabilities at a fixed prefix, we may
scale all completion masses by any common positive factor. We clear the
factorial denominators of the unrevealed coordinates by multiplying through
by
(n!)^{2(L+1)}
. For a possible later value
a
, define

G_{p}(a):=\Bigl(\frac{n!}{a!}\Bigr)^{2}\prod_{i=1}^{r}(p_{i}-a)^{2},

recording the scaled contribution of
a
and its pairwise interactions with
the prefix. Fixing the next coordinate to
t
introduces a further pairwise
factor
(t-a)^{2}
for each suffix element
a
, giving

\psi_{p,t}(a):=(t-a)^{2}\,G_{p}(a).

The completion score of a candidate
t
is then the total scaled mass of its
valid suffixes,

S_{p}(t):=G_{p}(t)\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}A\subseteq E_{t}\\
|A|=L,\;\sum A=s(t)\end{subarray}}\Delta(A)^{2}\prod_{a\in A}\psi_{p,t}(a),\qquad 1\leq t\leq B(p).

The sum over
A
is an instance of a general quantity, which we isolate so
that the determinant oracle of Section
3.3
can evaluate it
without enumerating the suffixes. For a ground set
E
and a weight function

\psi
on
E
, define the completion counter

Z_{L,s}(E,\psi):=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}A\subseteq E\\
|A|=L,\;\sum A=s\end{subarray}}\Delta(A)^{2}\prod_{a\in A}\psi(a),

with
Z_{0,s}(E,\psi)=\mathbf{1}_{s=0}
since the only suffix of length zero
is
A=\varnothing
. In this notation,

S_{p}(t)=G_{p}(t)\,Z_{L,s(t)}(E_{t},\psi_{p,t}).

Remark.
The completion scores
S_{p}(t)
are nonnegative integers, since
G_{p}(a)
is
a perfect square of an integer and
Z_{L,s(t)}(E_{t},\psi_{p,t})
is a sum of
products of such integers. Hence the weighted choice in
Algorithm
2
can be implemented exactly using these
integer weights. No non-integer rational probabilities are needed.

Lemma 3.2
.

For a fixed prefix
p
, the completion score
S_{p}(t)
is proportional to the
total
\operatorname{wt}
-mass of all valid extensions of
p
with next coordinate
t
, with
a proportionality constant independent of
t
.

Proof.

Fix an admissible candidate
t\in\{1,\ldots,B(p)\}
. Let

U:=(p_{1},\dots,p_{r},t)
. For any valid suffix

A\subseteq E_{t}
, the full extension is
(p_{1},\dots,p_{r},t,a_{1},\dots,a_{L})
,
where
a_{1}>\cdots>a_{L}
are the elements of
A
. Write
\Delta(U,A)
for the
Vandermonde factor of this concatenated sequence. It separates as

\Delta(U,A)^{2}=\Delta(p)^{2}\prod_{i=1}^{r}(p_{i}-t)^{2}\,\Delta(A)^{2}\prod_{a\in A}(t-a)^{2}\prod_{i=1}^{r}\prod_{a\in A}(p_{i}-a)^{2}.

Thus the full weight of this extension is

C_{p}\,\frac{\prod_{i=1}^{r}(p_{i}-t)^{2}}{t!^{2}}\,\Delta(A)^{2}\prod_{a\in A}\frac{(t-a)^{2}\prod_{i=1}^{r}(p_{i}-a)^{2}}{a!^{2}},

where
C_{p}:=\Delta(p)^{2}\prod_{i=1}^{r}p_{i}!^{-2}
depends only on
p
.
Multiplying the non-prefix part by
(n!)^{2(L+1)}
gives

G_{p}(t)\,\Delta(A)^{2}\prod_{a\in A}\psi_{p,t}(a).

Hence the total
\operatorname{wt}
-mass of all valid extensions with next coordinate
t

is

C_{p}(n!)^{-2(L+1)}G_{p}(t)Z_{L,s(t)}(E_{t},\psi_{p,t})=C_{p}(n!)^{-2(L+1)}S_{p}(t).

The proportionality constant is independent of
t
, so
S_{p}(t)
is
proportional to the required total mass.
∎

3.3
A determinant oracle for completion counts

The completion score
S_{p}(t)
involves the counter

Z_{L,s(t)}(E_{t},\psi_{p,t})
, a sum of
\Delta(A)^{2}
-weighted terms over all

L
-element subsets
A\subseteq E_{t}
with
\sum A=s(t)
. Enumerating these subsets directly is infeasible as there are

\binom{t-1}{L}
such subsets, which can be exponential in
n
. The following
lemma shows that
Z_{L,s}(E,\psi)
is instead recoverable as a single
coefficient of the determinant of an
L\times L
polynomial matrix, and that this
determinant can be computed in polynomial time. This determinant
representation is what makes the general sampler polynomial-time.

Let
E=\{e_{1}<\cdots<e_{m}\}
be a finite set of nonnegative integers,
R
a
commutative ring, and
\psi:E\to R
a weight function. For
L\geq 0
, define the following
L\times L
matrix over
R[q]
.

M_{L,E,\psi}(q):=\sum_{y\in E}\psi(y)q^{y}\,v(y)v(y)^{\top},\quad v(y):=(1,y,\dots,y^{L-1})^{\top}\in R^{L}.

This is the weighted moment matrix of
E
, the sum of the rank-one terms

v(y)v(y)^{\top}
scaled by
\psi(y)q^{y}
. Each anti-diagonal
i+j=d
holds a
single weighted power sum
\sum_{y\in E}\psi(y)q^{y}y^{d}
, so
M_{L,E,\psi}(q)

is determined by only
2L-1
such sums. It is built so that its determinant
generates the completion counts
Z_{L,s}
, as the next lemma shows, via the
factorisation
M_{L,E,\psi}(q)=VD(q)V^{\top}
and the Cauchy–Binet formula.

Lemma 3.3

(Determinant oracle)
.

For every
L\geq 0
and integer
s\geq 0
,

[q^{s}]\det M_{L,E,\psi}(q)=Z_{L,s}(E,\psi).

Proof.

The case
L=0
is immediate. For
L\geq 1
, write
M_{L,E,\psi}(q)=VD(q)V^{\top}

where
V
is the
L\times m
Vandermonde matrix with columns
v(e_{j})
and

D(q)=\operatorname{diag}(\psi(e_{1})q^{e_{1}},\dots,\psi(e_{m})q^{e_{m}})
. By the
Cauchy–Binet formula
[
18
, Section 0.8.7]
,

\det\bigl(VD(q)V^{\top}\bigr)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}J\subseteq[m]\\
|J|=L\end{subarray}}\det(V_{J})\det(D_{J}(q))\det(V_{J}^{\top}),

where
V_{J}
and
D_{J}(q)
are the submatrices indexed by
J
. Setting

A=\{e_{j}:j\in J\}
, we have
\det(V_{J})=\Delta(A)
and

\det(D_{J}(q))=\prod_{a\in A}\psi(a)q^{a}
, so

\det M_{L,E,\psi}(q)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}A\subseteq E\\
|A|=L\end{subarray}}\Delta(A)^{2}\prod_{a\in A}\psi(a)\,q^{\sum A}.

Extracting the coefficient of
q^{s}
gives
Z_{L,s}(E,\psi)
.
∎

The general ground set
E
and weight
\psi
are needed only for
Lemma
3.3
. Everywhere else the stage data are fixed, so, with
the prefix
p
(and hence
L
) understood, we abbreviate the matrix of a
candidate
t
by

M_{t}(q):=M_{L,E_{t},\psi_{p,t}}(q).

By Lemma
3.3
, the completion score is

S_{p}(t)=G_{p}(t)\,[q^{s(t)}]\det M_{t}(q).

Thus, for every prefix
p
and every candidate next coordinate
t
, the total
weight of all completions below
t
can be computed exactly by a single
determinant coefficient. Algorithmically, we extract this coefficient by evaluating the determinant
polynomial at sufficiently many field elements and interpolating.

The sequential shape sampler

3.6

Throughout the running-time analysis (this section and

We work in the model of Section

Lemma 3.6
.

For every prefix

Proof.

Every nonzero summand of

4
Running time of the fast implementation

The direct implementation of Section
3.6
is wasteful. For
every candidate
t
and every interpolation point
\xi
it rebuilds the
evaluated matrix
M_{t}(\xi)
and takes its determinant as a
generic dense matrix. The fast implementation removes both costs by exploiting
the Hankel structure of the evaluated matrix, leaving the output law untouched.
The same integer completion scores are computed, reusing the stage weights
G_{p}

and
\psi_{p,t}
, the prime set
\mathcal{P}
, the bit-length bound
(Lemma

changes, improving the bound of Theorem

Outline of the proof.

The running time bound is built from the bottom up, in three nested steps: a single determinant, then one sampling stage, then all stages. The per-stage speedup over the direct implementation rests on two independent ingredients, both internal to Lemma
4.2
. These are an incremental moment sweep and the superfast determinant primitive. The
next subsection records the structural fact that drives everything. Once the determinant polynomial is evaluated at a scalar, the resulting matrix is Hankel.
Lemma
4.1
exploits this to compute a single Hankel
determinant of order
L
in
\tilde{O}(L)
field operations, the superfast
primitive that replaces the dense
O(L^{3})
elimination of the direct
implementation. Building on it, Lemma
4.2
bounds the cost of one sampling stage. Combining the primitive with an incremental sweep of the Hankel moments across the candidates

, it computes all completion scores at a stage of suffix length
L
in
\tilde{O}(n^{3}kL^{2})
word operations. Lemma
4.3
then sums this cost over the
k

introduces the tableau-sampling

Lemma 4.1

(Superfast Hankel determinant primitive)

One determinant, with vanishing minors allowed.

, the clamp is inactive and the last returned term is
exactly

as required. If instead
L>d
, that is
L>\delta+1
, then the last column of

H
, namely
(h_{L-1},h_{L},\ldots,h_{2L-2})^{\top}
, vanishes, since each of its
indices is at least
L-1>\delta
. Hence
\det H=H_{L}(h)=0
. Either way
\det H

is obtained.

The algorithm of Liu–Xin–Zhang
[
23
]
gives a direct
Hankel-determinant primitive in the form needed here. Classical superfast
Toeplitz methods go back to
[

]
, and singular

[

,

]
.
Those methods could also be used, after converting

to a Toeplitz
matrix, but the singular case proceeds through randomised
generic-rank-profile preconditioning and certification. We use

]
because it applies directly to the Hankel moment sequence and
avoids this extra bookkeeping.
∎

We now describe the stage computation. At this stage we must compute, for
every candidate
t
, the completion score
S_{p}(t)
. By
Lemma
3.3
this equals
G_{p}(t)
times a single coefficient of an

L\times L
determinant that depends polynomially on a formal variable
q
. We
extract that coefficient in the standard way, by evaluating the determinant at
enough values of
q
and interpolating. Two structural features make the
evaluations cheap. First, at each fixed value
\xi
of
q
the matrix is
Hankel, so its determinant is fixed by only
2L-1
moments and is computed in

\tilde{O}(L)
field operations (Lemma
4.1
) rather than by a
general
L\times L
elimination. Second, the factor
(t-a)^{2}
that couples a
candidate
t
to a suffix element
a
expands as
t^{2}-2ta+a^{2}
, separating
t

from
a
. Each Hankel moment is then a fixed combination, with scalar
coefficients
t^{2}
,
-2t
, and
1
, of three power sums over
a<t
that are
cumulative in
t
, growing by a single term as
t
advances. All candidates are
therefore handled by one incremental sweep over
t
rather than recomputed
individually. We now give the details.

By the CRT accounting convention, work
over one prime field
\mathbb{F}_{\mathfrak{p}}
. The determinant polynomial
D_{t}(q):=\det M_{t}(q)
of Lemma
3.3
has
degree at most
Ln
, so we use
d:=Ln+1
evaluation points, and every prime

\mathfrak{p}>d_{\max}\geq d
. For
\xi\in\mathbb{F}_{\mathfrak{p}}
, define the
moments

m^{(\gamma)}_{\ell}(t,\xi):=\sum_{a<t}a^{\gamma+\ell}G_{p}(a)\xi^{a},\qquad\gamma=0,1,2,\quad 0\leq\ell\leq 2L-2.

Using
(t-a)^{2}=t^{2}-2ta+a^{2}
, the Hankel moment sequence of

M_{t}(\xi)
is

h_{\ell}(t,\xi)=t^{2}m^{(0)}_{\ell}(t,\xi)-2t\,m^{(1)}_{\ell}(t,\xi)+m^{(2)}_{\ell}(t,\xi),\qquad 0\leq\ell\leq 2L-2,

and
D_{t}(\xi)=\det(h_{\alpha+\beta}(t,\xi))_{0\leq\alpha,\beta<L}
.

Lemma 4.2

(Fast Hankel completion-score oracle)
.

Fix one sampling stage with current prefix
p
and suffix length
L\geq 1
.
All exact completion scores at that stage can be computed in

\tilde{O}(n^{3}kL^{2})
word operations.

Proof.

All costs below are for a single prime
\mathbb{F}_{\mathfrak{p}}
. The CRT
factor for exact integer recovery is applied only at the end. Assume the array
\{G_{p}(a):1\leq a\leq n\}
is
available (computed from scratch in
O(nk)
field operations, or maintained
between stages in
O(n)
field operations via
G_{(p,u)}(a)=G_{p}(a)(u-a)^{2}
). Since

k\leq n
and
L\geq 1
, this
O(nk)
cost is
dominated by the per-stage bound of
\tilde{O}(n^{2}L^{2})
field operations derived below.

Evaluation.
Fix an evaluation point
\xi
and sweep

t=1,\ldots,n
, maintaining the three families

(m^{(\gamma)}_{\ell}(t,\xi))_{0\leq\ell\leq 2L-2}
,
\gamma=0,1,2
– the powers

a^{\ell}
,
a^{\ell+1}
,
a^{\ell+2}
produced by expanding
(t-a)^{2}a^{\ell}
.
These are cumulative sums over
E_{t}=\{1,\ldots,t-1\}
, so they are never
rebuilt per candidate. On reaching
t
the families already hold the sums over

a<t
. From them we form the
2L-1
Hankel moments
h_{\ell}(t,\xi)
in

O(L)
and, by Lemma
4.1
, the determinant
D_{t}(\xi)

in
\tilde{O}(L)
field operations. We then advance to
t+1
by adding the single new term

a=t
,

m^{(\gamma)}_{\ell}(t+1,\xi)=m^{(\gamma)}_{\ell}(t,\xi)+t^{\gamma+\ell}G_{p}(t)\xi^{t},

which, with the powers
t^{0},\ldots,t^{2L}
and
\xi^{t}
kept incrementally,
costs
O(L)
field operations. One sweep therefore costs
\tilde{O}(nL)
field
operations per point, and over the
d=Ln+1
points the whole table of values

D_{t}(\xi_{u})
costs
\tilde{O}(dnL)=\tilde{O}(n^{2}L^{2})
field operations.

Coefficient extraction.
For candidate
t
only the single
coefficient
[q^{s(t)}]D_{t}(q)
is needed, with
s(t)=N-\sum_{i=1}^{r}p_{i}-t
and

N=n+\binom{k}{2}
the target sum. By Lemma
3.3
this coefficient is
the completion count
Z_{L,s(t)}
, and it is zero unless

s(t)\in\{0,\dots,d-1\}
, since
\deg D_{t}\leq Ln=d-1
. The
d
values

D_{t}(\xi_{0}),\ldots,D_{t}(\xi_{d-1})
determine
D_{t}(q)
uniquely, so each
coefficient is a fixed linear combination of these values:

[q^{s}]D_{t}(q)=\sum_{u=0}^{d-1}c_{s,u}D_{t}(\xi_{u}),

where the constants
c_{s,u}
depend only on
s
and on the evaluation
points, not on
t
. We precompute only the rows actually used, never the full

d\times d
table. Let

A(q):=\prod_{u=0}^{d-1}(q-\xi_{u})=\sum_{i=0}^{d}A_{i}q^{i}.

Its coefficients are computed from the
d
roots by a subproduct tree in

\tilde{O}(d)
field operations, and the values
A^{\prime}(\xi_{u})
are computed by
multipoint evaluation in
\tilde{O}(d)
field operations. Since each
\xi_{u}

is a root of
A
, the quotient
A(q)/(q-\xi_{u})
is a polynomial, and
synthetic division gives

[q^{s}]\frac{A(q)}{q-\xi_{u}}=\sum_{i=s+1}^{d}A_{i}\,\xi_{u}^{\,i-s-1}=P_{s}(\xi_{u}),\qquad P_{s}(x):=\sum_{i=s+1}^{d}A_{i}\,x^{\,i-s-1}.

Thus
c_{s,u}=P_{s}(\xi_{u})/A^{\prime}(\xi_{u})
. For one index
s
, the whole row

(c_{s,u})_{u=0}^{d-1}
is obtained by one multipoint evaluation of
P_{s}
,
in
\tilde{O}(d)
field operations. At most
n
distinct indices
s(t)

are needed, costing
\tilde{O}(nd)=\tilde{O}(n^{2}L)
field operations. Then

[q^{s(t)}]D_{t}(q)=\sum_{u}c_{s(t),u}D_{t}(\xi_{u})
is an inner product, costing

O(nd)
field operations in total over all candidates. Multiplying by

G_{p}(t)
gives all scores.

The stage therefore costs
\tilde{O}(n^{2}L^{2})
field operations over one
prime. By the CRT convention this is
\tilde{O}(n^{2}L^{2})\cdot\tilde{O}(kn)=\tilde{O}(n^{3}kL^{2})
word operations.
∎

Lemma 4.3

(Fast shape-sampling time)
.

The exact shape sampler has expected running time
\tilde{O}(n^{3}k^{4})
.

Proof.

The suffix lengths are
k-1,\ldots,1,0
. The terminal stage is trivial. By
Lemma
4.2
, stage
L\geq 1
costs

\tilde{O}(n^{3}kL^{2})
, so

\sum_{L=1}^{k-1}\tilde{O}(n^{3}kL^{2})=\tilde{O}(n^{3}k^{4})
.
∎

Proof of Theorem
2.2
(fast implementation running time of
\tilde{O}(n^{3}k^{4})
).

The sampling law is the same as in Theorem
2.1
. Only the implementation of the completion-score oracle changes. By
Lemma
4.3
, the shape distribution is sampled in expected

\tilde{O}(n^{3}k^{4})
word operations. The subsequent tableau-sampling choices
have expected
O(n^{2})
running time, and the inverse Robinson–Schensted
step is deterministic and takes
O(n^{2})
word operations. Exactness is
unchanged, since the same integer completion scores are computed and the same
exact weighted choices are made.
∎

A ladder of bounds.
The improvement from
\tilde{O}(n^{4}k^{5})
to
\tilde{O}(n^{3}k^{4})
separates
into two independent gains. These are the incremental moment sweep of
Lemma
4.2
, which replaces the per-candidate matrix
rebuild (
O(nL^{2})
field operations) by an
O(L)
update, and the
superfast Hankel primitive of Lemma
4.1
, which
replaces dense
O(L^{3})
elimination by an
\tilde{O}(L)
time determinant computation.
The sweep alone already gives an intermediate bound. With the explicit

L\times L
Hankel matrix formed from its moments and its determinant taken
by plain Gaussian elimination (
O(L^{3})
), the stage costs
O(n^{2}L^{4})
per
prime, giving
\tilde{O}(n^{3}k^{6})
overall. This is no larger asymptotically
than the direct bound for
k\leq n
, and improves on it for
k=o(n)
, while
using no structured-matrix primitive.

Space usage.
With the coefficient extraction above, the peak space usage of the fast
implementation is
\tilde{O}(kn^{2})
words. For one prime, the sweep stores
the values
D_{t}(\xi_{u})
for all
1\leq t\leq n
and
1\leq u\leq d
, which is

O(nd)=O(n^{2}L)
field elements, plus
O(d)
field elements of bookkeeping. The primes are processed sequentially, so this space is reused. Across a
stage, the residues of the
n
candidate scores accumulate over the

\tilde{O}(kn)
primes, and the reconstructed integer scores occupy

O(kn\log n)
bits each, for
\tilde{O}(kn^{2})
words in total. The direct
implementation satisfies the same bound.

5
A direct sampler when
k\in\Theta(n)

In the linear regime
k\in\Theta(n)
a much simpler and near-optimal sampler is
available. It works directly with permutations and uses rejection sampling on
an expanded space of pairs
(\pi,I)
, where
I
is a
k
-term increasing
subsequence of
\pi
. The acceptance rule is designed so that, for each
permutation with
\operatorname{LIS}=k
, exactly one associated pair is accepted, namely the pair
for which
I
is the leftmost LIS. Plain rejection on the thin event

\{\operatorname{LIS}=k\}
is hopeless away from
2\sqrt{n}
. The expansion to pairs
(\pi,I)

gives each target permutation many proposals, and accepting only its
leftmost-LIS witness keeps exactly one, so acceptance is uniform and, for

k\in\Theta(n)
, of constant probability. We summarise the construction here. The
full analysis is in Section
6
. Throughout let
m:=n-k
. The
construction and its exactness are valid for every
1\leq k\leq n
. The constant
acceptance-probability bound, and hence the time bound, is for
k\in\Theta(n)
.

Define the expanded proposal space

\mathcal{E}_{n,k}:=\{(\pi,I):\pi\in S_{n},\;I=(i_{1}<\cdots<i_{k}),\;\pi(i_{1})<\cdots<\pi(i_{k})\},

so
I
is a list of positions whose entries form an increasing subsequence of

\pi
of length
k
. We sample
(\pi,I)\in\mathcal{E}_{n,k}
as follows.
Choose a
k
-subset
V\subseteq[n]
of values and a
k
-subset
I\subseteq[n]

of positions, both uniformly. Place the values of
V
in increasing order into
the positions
I
and fill the remaining
m
positions with the remaining
m

values in uniformly random order.

Proposition 5.1
.

The construction above is uniform on
\mathcal{E}_{n,k}
. In particular,

|\mathcal{E}_{n,k}|=\binom{n}{k}^{2}m!
.

For a permutation
\pi
with
\operatorname{LIS}(\pi)=k
, let
L^{\star}(\pi)
denote the
leftmost LIS of
\pi
, that is, among all increasing subsequences of length
k
, the one
whose list of positions is lexicographically smallest. This is a canonical
choice of LIS, computable in
O(n\log\log n)
time
(Section
6
).

.

then

.

if

\pi
.

end

if

until
false

Proposition 5.2
.

Conditioned on acceptance in one iteration of Algorithm

, the output permutation is uniform on
\Omega_{n,k}
.

Proof.

The accepted proposals are precisely the pairs

\mathcal{A}_{n,k}:=\{(\pi,I)\in\mathcal{E}_{n,k}:\operatorname{LIS}(\pi)=k\text{ and }I=L^{\star}(\pi)\}
.
The projection
(\pi,I)\mapsto\pi
is a bijection from

\mathcal{A}_{n,k}
to
\Omega_{n,k}
, since for each

\pi\in\Omega_{n,k}
there is exactly one accepted pair, namely

(\pi,L^{\star}(\pi))
. By Proposition

, the proposal

\mathcal{E}_{n,k}

.
∎

The acceptance probability of one iteration equals

|\Omega_{n,k}|/|\mathcal{E}_{n,k}|=A_{n,k}
, where

is the

, defined

. That factor records the loss in

. With the

.

The rejection sampler is not limited to the linear regime. Its expected

(Below the linear regime)
.

The rejection sampler of Theorem

This section proves Proposition

6.1
Uniformity of the proposal

Proof of Proposition
5.1
.

The choices made by the proposal are exactly a
k
-set of values
V
, a

k
-set of positions
I
, and an ordering of the remaining
m=n-k
values.
Given these data, the entries on
I
are forced to be the elements of
V
in
increasing order, and the remaining entries are filled according to the chosen
ordering.

Conversely, given
(\pi,I)\in\mathcal{E}_{n,k}
, the set
V
is uniquely the
set of values
\{\pi(i):i\in I\}
, and the ordering of the remaining values is
read off from
\pi
outside
I
. Thus the proposal data are in bijection with

\mathcal{E}_{n,k}
. Since the proposal data are chosen uniformly, the induced
law on
\mathcal{E}_{n,k}
is uniform. The cardinality formula follows from
the same bijection and the fact that
\binom{n}{k}=\binom{n}{n-k}
.
∎

6.2
Testing one proposal in
O(n\log\log n)
time

For a permutation
\pi\in S_{n}
and an index
i\in[n]
, let
r_{i}
be the length
of the longest increasing subsequence of
\pi
that starts at position
i
.

Fact 6.1
.

Let
a\in S_{n}
. In the word-RAM model, one can compute, for every

j\in[n]
, the length
d_{j}
of the longest increasing subsequence of
a

ending at position
j
in
O(n\log\log n)
time.

This is the standard patience-sorting dynamic program with the ordered set of
current tail values implemented by an integer predecessor structure, such as a
van Emde Boas structure. See, e.g., Crochemore and
Porat
[
10
]
.

Lemma 6.2
.

The values
r_{1},\dots,r_{n}
can be computed in
O(n\log\log n)
time in the
word-RAM.

Proof.

Let
b_{j}=n+1-\pi_{n+1-j}
be the reversed-complement permutation.
An increasing subsequence of
\pi
starting at position
i
corresponds
bijectively to an increasing subsequence of
b
ending at position
n+1-i
.
Hence
r_{i}=d_{n+1-i}
, where
d_{j}
is the length of the longest increasing
subsequence of
b
ending at
j
. By Fact
6.1
, all

are computed in
O(n\log\log n)
time, and reading them in reverse
gives all

in the same time.
∎

Assume now that
\operatorname{LIS}(\pi)=k
. We construct the leftmost LIS greedily.
Starting with the dummy values
i_{0}:=0
and
v_{0}:=0
, suppose that

i_{1},\dots,i_{t-1}
have already been chosen and write

v_{t-1}:=\pi(i_{t-1})
. We choose
i_{t}
to be the smallest index

i>i_{t-1}
such that
\pi(i)>v_{t-1}
and such that an increasing
subsequence of length
k-t+1
can still be completed starting from
i
,
that is,
r_{i}\geq k-t+1
. We then set
v_{t}:=\pi(i_{t})
.

Lemma 6.3
.

If
\operatorname{LIS}(\pi)=k
, the greedy reconstruction above returns

.

Proof.

We argue by induction on
t
. Suppose
i_{1},\dots,i_{t-1}
have already been
chosen and coincide with the first
t-1
positions of the leftmost LIS. Let

F_{t}:=\{i>i_{t-1}:\pi(i)>v_{t-1},\;r_{i}\geq k-t+1\}.

Every index in
F_{t}
can be used as the next position of an increasing subsequence of total length
k
. The already chosen prefix has length
t-1
,
and since
i>i_{t-1}
,
\pi(i)>v_{t-1}
, and
r_{i}\geq k-t+1
, it can be
followed by an increasing subsequence of length
k-t+1
starting at
i
. Conversely, the
t
th position of any
LIS extending
i_{1},\dots,i_{t-1}
must lie in
F_{t}
. The greedy rule picks the
smallest index in
F_{t}
, so no lexicographically smaller LIS can differ for
the first time at position

. Thus the chosen

is exactly the

th
position of
L^{\star}(\pi)
.
∎

Corollary 6.4
.

Given a proposal
(\pi,I)
, one can decide whether it is accepted by one
iteration of Algorithm

in

time on the word-RAM.

Proof.

Compute the values

using Lemma

, in

time. Their maximum is
\operatorname{LIS}(\pi)
. If this maximum is not

using

. The reconstruction is a single left-to-right scan
through the permutation and therefore costs

time. Finally compare the

time. The

O(n\log\log n)
.
∎

6.3
Acceptance probability

For a partition
\nu
recall
h_{\nu}
,
H_{\nu}
, and the conjugate
\nu^{\prime}
from
Section
3.1
. For the empty partition we use the convention

\mu_{1}=0
and interpret empty products as
1
.

Any shape with first row of length
k
can be written as
\lambda=(k,\mu)
,
where
\mu\vdash m
is the sub-diagram below the first row. The factor

a_{k}(\mu)
measures the multiplicative loss in
f^{(k,\mu)}
caused by the elongation of the first-row hooks relative to a bare row of length
k
. In column
j
, the
hook length in an isolated row would be
k-j+1
. Placing
\mu
underneath
elongates it by the column height
\mu^{\prime}_{j}
to
k-j+1+\mu^{\prime}_{j}
. Columns

j>\mu_{1}
are unaffected. For
\mu\vdash m
, define

a_{k}(\mu):=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\prod_{j=1}^{\mu_{1}}\frac{k-j+1}{k-j+1+\mu^{\prime}_{j}},&\mu_{1}\leq k,\\[11.99998pt]
0,&\mu_{1}>k.\end{cases}

Define
A_{n,k}:=\mathbf{E}_{\operatorname{Pl}_{m}}[a_{k}(\mu)^{2}]
, where
\operatorname{Pl}_{m}(\mu)=(f^{\mu})^{2}/m!
is
Plancherel measure on partitions of
m
.

Lemma 6.5
.

For
\lambda=(k,\mu)
with
\mu\vdash m
and
\mu_{1}\leq k
,

f^{\lambda}=\binom{n}{m}f^{\mu}a_{k}(\mu)
.
Consequently,
|\Omega_{n,k}|=\binom{n}{m}^{2}m!\,A_{n,k}
.

Proof.

For
\lambda=(k,\mu)
, the hooks below the first row are those of
\mu
,
contributing
H_{\mu}
. The first-row hooks are
k-j+1+\mu^{\prime}_{j}
for

1\leq j\leq\mu_{1}
and
k-j+1
for
\mu_{1}<j\leq k
, so

H_{\lambda}=H_{\mu}\,\frac{\prod_{j=1}^{\mu_{1}}(k-j+1+\mu^{\prime}_{j})}{\prod_{j=1}^{\mu_{1}}(k-j+1)}\cdot k!=H_{\mu}\,\frac{k!}{a_{k}(\mu)}.

The hook-length formula then gives

f^{\lambda}=\frac{n!}{H_{\lambda}}=\frac{n!}{m!\,k!}\cdot\frac{m!}{H_{\mu}}\cdot a_{k}(\mu)=\binom{n}{m}f^{\mu}a_{k}(\mu).

For the second claim, Robinson–Schensted identifies
\Omega_{n,k}
with
pairs of tableaux of shapes
(k,\mu)
,
\mu\vdash m
,

\mu_{1}\leq k

[
32
]
, so

|\Omega_{n,k}|=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mu\vdash m\\
\mu_{1}\leq k\end{subarray}}(f^{(k,\mu)})^{2}=\binom{n}{m}^{\!2}\sum_{\mu\vdash m}(f^{\mu})^{2}a_{k}(\mu)^{2}=\binom{n}{m}^{\!2}m!\,A_{n,k}.\qed

Corollary 6.6
.

The acceptance probability of one iteration of
Algorithm
3
is
|\Omega_{n,k}|/|\mathcal{E}_{n,k}|=A_{n,k}
.

Proof.

By Proposition
5.2
, every permutation in
\Omega_{n,k}

contributes exactly one accepted pair

, so the acceptance probability
equals
|\Omega_{n,k}|/|\mathcal{E}_{n,k}|
. The result now follows from
Proposition
5.1
and Lemma
6.5
.
∎

Lemma 6.7
.

For every
m\geq 1
, if
\mu
is drawn from Plancherel measure on partitions
of
m
, then
\mathbf{P}_{\operatorname{Pl}_{m}}(\mu_{1}\leq 4\sqrt{m})\geq\tfrac{1}{2}
.

Proof.

By the Robinson–Schensted correspondence,
\mu_{1}
under Plancherel measure has
the same distribution as
\operatorname{LIS}(\sigma)
for a uniform random permutation

\sigma
of
[m]
. Fix
r\geq 1
and let
X_{r}
count the increasing
subsequences of length
r
in
\sigma
. Each
r
-subset of positions contributes
with probability
1/r!
, so
\mathbf{E}X_{r}=\binom{m}{r}/r!
. Since
\operatorname{LIS}(\sigma)\geq r

implies
X_{r}\geq 1
, Markov’s inequality gives

\mathbf{P}_{\operatorname{Pl}_{m}}(\mu_{1}\geq r)=\mathbf{P}(\operatorname{LIS}(\sigma)\geq r)\leq\mathbf{E}X_{r}=\frac{\binom{m}{r}}{r!}.

Using
\binom{m}{r}\leq(em/r)^{r}
and
r!\geq(r/e)^{r}
yields

\mathbf{P}_{\operatorname{Pl}_{m}}(\mu_{1}\geq r)\leq(e^{2}m/r^{2})^{r}
. Taking
r=\lfloor 4\sqrt{m}\rfloor+1>4\sqrt{m}
,

\mathbf{P}_{\operatorname{Pl}_{m}}(\mu_{1}>4\sqrt{m})\leq\mathbf{P}_{\operatorname{Pl}_{m}}(\mu_{1}\geq r)\leq\left(\frac{e^{2}}{16}\right)^{r}\leq\frac{1}{2},

which proves the claim.
∎

Lemma 6.8
.

Fix
\eta\in(0,1]
. If
k\geq\eta n
, then for all sufficiently large

n
,

A_{n,k}\geq\frac{1}{2}\exp\!\left(-\frac{4(1-\eta)}{\eta}\right).

In particular, if
k\in\Theta(n)
, then there are constants
c>0
and

n_{0}
such that
A_{n,k}\geq c
for all
n\geq n_{0}
.

Proof.

If
m=0
, then
k=n
and
A_{n,n}=1
, which is larger than the
displayed lower bound. Hence assume
m=n-k\geq 1
. Since
k\geq\eta n
,
we have
\frac{m}{k}=\frac{n-k}{k}\leq\frac{1-\eta}{\eta}
.

Using
\log(1+x)\leq x
, whenever
\mu_{1}\leq k
we have

-\log a_{k}(\mu)=\sum_{j=1}^{\mu_{1}}\log\!\left(1+\frac{\mu^{\prime}_{j}}{k-j+1}\right)\leq\sum_{j=1}^{\mu_{1}}\frac{\mu^{\prime}_{j}}{k-j+1}\leq\frac{m}{k-\mu_{1}+1},

where the last step uses
\sum_{j=1}^{\mu_{1}}\mu^{\prime}_{j}=m
.

By Lemma
6.7
, the event

\{\mu_{1}\leq 4\sqrt{m}\}
has probability at least
1/2
. Since

m\leq((1-\eta)/\eta)k
, for all sufficiently large
n
this event
implies
\mu_{1}\leq k/2
, and in particular
\mu_{1}\leq k
. Hence

k-\mu_{1}+1\geq k/2
. On this event,

-\log a_{k}(\mu)\leq\frac{2m}{k}\leq\frac{2(1-\eta)}{\eta},

and therefore
a_{k}(\mu)^{2}\geq\exp(-4(1-\eta)/\eta)
.
It follows that

A_{n,k}=\mathbf{E}_{\operatorname{Pl}_{m}}[a_{k}(\mu)^{2}]\geq\frac{1}{2}\exp\!\left(-\frac{4(1-\eta)}{\eta}\right)

for all sufficiently large
n
. The final assertion follows by choosing
any
\eta>0
such that
k\geq\eta n
for all sufficiently large
n
.
∎

Proof of Theorem
2.3
(exact
O(n\log\log n)
sampler for
k\in\Theta(n)
).

Exactness is established by Proposition
5.2
. By
Corollary
6.4
, each proposal is tested in
O(n\log\log n)
time,
and generating it takes
O(n)
time. By Corollary
6.6
and
Lemma
6.8
, the acceptance probability is bounded below by a
positive constant for all sufficiently large
n
, so the expected number of
trials is
O(1)
. The expected total running time is therefore
O(n\log\log n)
. Since the
trials are independent and each succeeds with probability bounded below by
a constant, the number of trials has geometric tails, so the same bound
holds with exponentially decaying tail probabilities.
∎

Proof of Corollary
5.3
(polynomial time for
k\in\Omega(n/\log n)
).

On the event
\mu_{1}\leq 4\sqrt{m}
, which has Plancherel probability at
least
1/2
by Lemma
6.7
, we have

-\log a_{k}(\mu)\leq\sum_{j=1}^{\mu_{1}}\frac{\mu^{\prime}_{j}}{k-j+1}\leq\frac{m}{k-\mu_{1}+1}\leq\frac{m}{k-4\sqrt{m}+1}.

Therefore

a_{k}(\mu)^{2}\geq\exp\!\left(-\frac{2m}{k-4\sqrt{m}+1}\right)

on this event. Hence

A_{n,k}\geq\frac{1}{2}\exp\!\left(-\frac{2m}{k-4\sqrt{m}+1}\right).

Since each trial of the rejection sampler costs
O(n\log\log n)
time, the
expected running time is

O\!\left(n\exp\!\left(\frac{2m}{k-4\sqrt{m}+1}\right)\log\log n\right).

Now fix
\eta>0
and suppose
n
is sufficiently large and

k\geq\eta n/\log n
. Since
m\leq n
, we have

4\sqrt{m}\leq 4\sqrt{n}=o(n/\log n)
, so
k>4\sqrt{m}
for all sufficiently
large
n
. Moreover,

\frac{m}{k-4\sqrt{m}+1}\leq\frac{n}{\eta n/\log n-4\sqrt{n}+1}=\frac{\log n}{\eta}+O(1).

Thus

\exp\!\left(\frac{2m}{k-4\sqrt{m}+1}\right)=O(n^{2/\eta}),

and the expected running time is

O\!\left(n^{1+2/\eta}\log\log n\right).

For
\eta=1
, this is
O(n^{3}\log\log n)
.
∎

7
Sampling permutations with LIS at most
k

The samplers above extend to the relaxed constraint
\operatorname{LIS}(\pi)\leq k
by the
same argument, after padding the conjugate with zero parts and replacing the
ambient coordinate set
\{1,\ldots,n\}
by
\{0,\ldots,n+k-1\}
. We prove
Corollary
2.4
.

Proof of Corollary
2.4
.

We first treat the general sampler. Only one thing really changes. Allowing

\operatorname{LIS}(\pi)\leq k
rather than
\operatorname{LIS}(\pi)=k
lets the sampled shape have first row

shorter
than
k
. In the strict-coordinate reparameterisation of
Section
3
this merely widens the range the coordinates may
occupy, so that they may now be as small as
0
and as large as
n+k-1
instead
of lying in
\{1,\dots,n\}
. The separated weight, the determinant oracle, and
the sequential sampler carry over unchanged. We only need to check that the
reparameterisation and the running-time bounds survive this widened range, which
we do now. By the Robinson–Schensted
correspondence, it suffices to sample
\lambda\vdash n
with probability
proportional to
(f^{\lambda})^{2}\mathbf{1}_{\{\lambda_{1}\leq k\}}
and then
proceed exactly as in Lemma
3.5
, whose proof applies
verbatim with the support
\{\lambda\vdash n:\lambda_{1}\leq k\}
in place of

\{\lambda\vdash n:\lambda_{1}=k\}
.

The condition
\lambda_{1}\leq k
states that the conjugate
\rho=\lambda^{\prime}
has

at most

k
parts. Padding
\rho
with zeros to exactly
k
weakly
decreasing parts
\rho_{1}\geq\cdots\geq\rho_{k}\geq 0
and applying the same shift

x_{i}:=\rho_{i}+k-i
produces a strictly decreasing sequence

n+k-1\ \geq\ x_{1}>x_{2}>\cdots>x_{k}\ \geq\ 0,\qquad x_{1}+\cdots+x_{k}=N=n+\binom{k}{2},

and this is a bijection between
\{\lambda\vdash n:\lambda_{1}\leq k\}
and
strict sequences of
k
values in
\{0,\ldots,n+k-1\}
with sum
N
. The
proof of Lemma
3.1
applies verbatim to padded sequences, where an
empty row
\rho_{i}=0
contributes an empty hook product, and consistently

x_{i}!/\prod_{j>i}(x_{i}-x_{j})=(k-i)!/\prod_{j=i+1}^{k}(j-i)=1
, since every row
below an empty row is empty. Hence
f^{\lambda}=n!\,\Delta(x)/\prod_{i}x_{i}!
,
and the target law on padded sequences is again proportional to

\operatorname{wt}(x)=\Delta(x)^{2}\prod_{i}x_{i}!^{-2}
.

The sequential sampler of Section
3
now runs with three
modifications. First, candidates and suffix values are drawn from

\{0,1,\ldots\}
, where the admissible candidates are
t\in\{0,\ldots,B(p)\}
with

B(\varnothing)=n+k-1
, the suffix ground set is
E_{t}=\{0,\ldots,t-1\}
, and
the feasibility window becomes
\binom{L}{2}\leq s(t)\leq Lt-\binom{L+1}{2}
.
Second, since coordinate values may now exceed
n
(they are at most

n+k-1\leq 2n
), the clearing factor
n!
in the stage weights is replaced by

(n+k-1)!
, that is,

G_{p}(a):=\bigl((n+k-1)!/a!\bigr)^{2}\prod_{i=1}^{r}(p_{i}-a)^{2}
,
which keeps every completion score a nonnegative integer. Third, the degree
of
D_{t}(q)
is now at most
L(n+k-1)\leq 2Ln
, so
d:=L(n+k-1)+1=O(Ln)

evaluation points are used, and the primes of
Section
3.6
are chosen larger than

d_{\max}:=(k-1)(n+k-1)+1\leq 2n^{2}
. The sieve interval

(d_{\max},\,n^{2}\log^{2}n]
still contains
\Theta(n^{2}\log n)
primes. With
these changes, Lemma
3.2
, Theorem
3.4
,
Lemma
3.6
(as
(n+k-1)!\leq(2n)!
still has
O(n\log n)

bits and all differences are at most
2n
), and the implementations of
Section
4
go
through with identical proofs, all bounds changing by constant factors only.
The well-definedness argument of Section
3.4
also
applies, with the column shape
\lambda=(1^{n})
, that is

x=(n+k-1,k-2,k-3,\ldots,0)
, witnessing initial feasibility. This gives
expected time
\tilde{O}(n^{4}k^{5})
for the direct and

\tilde{O}(n^{3}k^{4})
time for the fast implementation.

For the second statement in the corollary with
k\geq 4\sqrt{n}
, repeat the following trial. Draw
\pi\in S_{n}

uniformly in
O(n)
time, compute
\operatorname{LIS}(\pi)
in
O(n\log\log n)
time
(Fact
6.1
), and accept if and only if

\operatorname{LIS}(\pi)\leq k
. Conditioned on acceptance, the output is uniform on

\Omega_{n,\leq k}
. By the first-moment bound in the proof of
Lemma
6.7
, applied with
m=n
, setting

r:=\lfloor 4\sqrt{n}\rfloor+1\leq k+1
gives

\mathbf{P}\bigl(\operatorname{LIS}(\pi)>k\bigr)\leq\mathbf{P}\bigl(\operatorname{LIS}(\pi)\geq r\bigr)\leq\Bigl(\frac{e^{2}n}{r^{2}}\Bigr)^{\!r}\leq\Bigl(\frac{e^{2}}{16}\Bigr)^{\!r}\leq\frac{1}{2},

so the acceptance probability is at least
\tfrac{1}{2}
, the number of trials
is geometric, and the expected total time is
O(n\log\log n)
.

Finally, for
k\leq 4\sqrt{n}
the general sampler costs

\tilde{O}(n^{3}k^{4})\subseteq\tilde{O}(n^{5})
, while for
k>4\sqrt{n}
rejection
costs
O(n\log\log n)
. The combined bound
\tilde{O}(n^{5})
follows. We note
the contrast with the constraint
\operatorname{LIS}(\pi)=k
, for which no analogous
rejection shortcut is available away from the typical values close to
2\sqrt{n}
.
∎
