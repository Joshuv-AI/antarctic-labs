Falling fertility on the left as key driver of US birth decline
Authors: Martin Fieder, Susanne Huber
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Political orientation has been shown to correlate with fertility, raising the possibility that demographic processes contribute to long-term ideological change. Using data from the US General Social Survey, we analyze completed fertility across 17 birth cohorts (1898–1982) to examine how political orientation has contributed to fertility decline in the United States and whether emerging selective forces can be detected. Earlier cohorts show little difference in fertility by political orientation. From the 1943–1947 birth cohort onward, however, a pronounced divergence emerges: individuals with right-wing political orientations maintain fertility at or above replacement level, whereas fertility among left-wing individuals declines sharply to well below replacement. Applying Lande–Arnold selection gradient analyses, we find increasing directional selection that may favor right-wing political orientation over time, while education shows consistent negative associations with fertility and religiosity positive but weaker effects. Separate analysis of Black and White Americans reveals, however, that the increasingly stronger association between political orientation and fertility in more recent cohorts holds only true for whites but not for blacks. Nonetheless, these findings suggest that recent fertility decline in the United States is driven disproportionately by left-leaning individuals and point to contemporary demographic processes that may gradually shift the ideological composition of populations.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-57582-3.

Introduction

Fieder and Huber1 examined whether political leanings relate to fertility, potentially reflecting biological selection of political attitudes. Since political attitudes may have genetic bases2, a fertility link could suggest selection pressures. Using the World Value Survey (WVS), they found a U-shaped pattern: individuals with strong left- or right-wing views had more children than moderates, though conservatives showed a stronger effect, suggesting balancing selection. In the European SHARE and the US GSS, however, only conservatives consistently had more children. At least in the US, this conservative advantage emerged after the 1990s; earlier, liberals also had relatively high fertility. This finding has been confirmed by Vogl and Freese3, showing that larger families and individuals with more children tend to hold conservative views on family issues. As a result, population attitudes gradually shift toward the right.

Fieder and Huber4 extended these findings with European data. Across Europe, conservatives had more children and grandchildren than liberals, echoing US patterns. These results suggest that the demographic transmission of conservative views—both socially and genetically—gradually shifts societies toward conservatism in Europe as well. Arpino and Mogi5 explored mechanisms behind this reproductive pattern, finding that extreme-right individuals were 4–5 percentage points more likely than centrists to intend having a child within three years. This effect persisted after controlling for religiosity, education, and income, and was strongest among younger (20–34), highly educated individuals, particularly in Eastern and Southern Europe. Similarly, Rackin and Gibson-Davis6, analyzing US high school seniors (1989–2019), found Republicans consistently desired more children than Democrats, with the gap widening over time, while Neal and Neal7 showed that childfree adults in Michigan were more likely to be liberal.

These findings intersect with broader demographic trends described by the Second Demographic Transition (SDT) framework8,9, which links below-replacement fertility to cultural shifts such as individualization, secularization, and changing family norms. Subsequent work has emphasized how these shifts contribute to sustained low fertility across developed societies8–11. Research further shows that while short-term economic downturns can depress fertility, the underlying SDT dynamics remains central to explaining long-term patterns of delayed childbearing and very low fertility8,10,11. Together, this literature suggests that political fertility differentials operate within a broader demographic context in which below-replacement fertility has become the norm in much of the developed world.

Prior research has established that political orientation is associated with fertility1,4,12,13 is substantially transferred from parents to children14 and is partly heritable2. It remains unclear, however, whether and how the association between political orientation and fertility may have changed over time. The key empirical puzzle we try to address is therefore why a presumable earlier pattern with a reproductive advantage for “both sides”1, consistent with balancing selection has shifted toward a strongly asymmetric, directional pattern toward right leaning attitudes in recent cohorts. Accordingly, our study pursues two complementary aims: first, to examine the association between political orientation and fertility over time and second, to estimate putative selective forces by applying methods of phenotypic selection detection. By combining a cohort perspective with selection-analytic approaches, we move beyond static correlations and show that the relationship between political orientation and fertility has undergone a substantive temporal transformation, with important implications for both demographic change and longer-term evolutionary dynamics.

Results

Although from birth cohort 1923–1927 onwards, mean fertility rate was continuously lower for left-wing than for middle- and right-wing individuals, for the earlier birth cohorts, this difference was not substantial. However, after the birth cohort of 1943–1947, the pattern changed fundamentally: the mean fertility rate of right-wing individuals remained above the replacement fertility rate of 2.1 almost in all cohorts, whereas it dropped dramatically below the replacement rate for left-wing individuals. Analyzing White and Black Americans separately revealed substantially different patterns in the relationship between political orientation and fertility. Among White Americans, the pattern became more pronounced toward the end of the time series, with differences increasing in later birth cohorts (Figure S1a).

Among Black Americans, fertility rates were generally high across all political orientations in earlier birth cohorts, peaking among individuals born in the 1930s–1940s. During this period, right-of-center individuals tended to have slightly higher fertility than left- or center-oriented groups. From the mid-twentieth century onward, fertility declined steadily across all groups and gradually converged toward replacement level (approximately 2.1 children per woman). Unlike among White Americans, no clear pattern of strongly diverging fertility by political orientation emerged in more recent birth cohorts, suggesting only modest political differences in fertility among Black Americans (Figure S1b).

Also in the Lande–Arnold model analyses, among White Americans, later cohorts showed stronger positive associations between fertility, political conservatism, and religiosity, alongside a persistent negative association with education. Whereas among Black Americans, coefficients remained closer to zero and fluctuated less over time, suggesting weaker differentiation in fertility behavior despite declining mean family size (Figure S2a,b).

Discussion

Our analysis of U.S. General Social Survey (GSS) data across 17 birth cohorts (1898–1982) provides evidence for differential fertility by political orientation, with implications for demography and change of the balance in political systems. Furthermore, it can be viewed as an example for contemporary selective forces in modern societies, that have been found recently15. However, our results should not be interpreted as deterministic, as they do not permit a clear causal inference regarding the mechanisms linking ideology and fertility.

We find that while earlier cohorts showed little to no difference in completed fertility by political orientation, from the 1943–1947 cohort onward, a striking divergence emerged: right-leaning individuals maintained fertility rates at or above replacement level, whereas left-leaning individuals experienced a sharp decline in completed fertility, falling well below replacement. Middle-oriented individuals showed intermediate fertility patterns, consistently closer to replacement but lower than conservatives.

These findings align with previous evidence from both the U.S. and Europe. Fieder and Huber1,4 and Vogl and Freese3 similarly found that conservative individuals, and to a lesser extent those with strong ideological leanings, tend to have more children than moderates, with a particularly consistent advantage for conservatives in contemporary populations. Arpino and Mogi5 further demonstrate that in Europe, far-right individuals are significantly more likely to intend childbearing in the near term, even after accounting for socioeconomic and religious factors. Our results are in line with these cross-national trends, suggesting that the found U.S. pattern is part of a broader pattern in post-industrial societies where conservative orientations are associated with higher fertility.

From a demographic and selection-analytic perspective, this shift is consistent with increasing fertility differentials associated with conservative political orientations, due to different birth rates. Earlier theorizing1 proposed that political orientation may have historically been maintained through balancing selection, with both left- and right-leaning strategies providing benefits. However, in modern societies characterized by below-replacement fertility10,16 our results indicate that left-leaning individuals in recent cohorts exhibit lower completed fertility relative to right-leaning individuals, while right-leaning orientations confer a relative reproductive advantage. This is confirmed by our Lande–Arnold selection gradient model, which shows a significant positive interaction between right-wing orientation and later birth cohorts, reflecting an increasing reproductive advantage for conservatives over time. In contrast, the selection gradients for religious attendance and education remain more stable over time; however, the positive effect of religious attendance rather decreases as the positive effect of right-wing political orientation increases sharply.

The mechanisms underlying these fertility differentials are likely multifaceted. Cultural evolutionary theory suggests that fertility transitions are shaped not only by modernization and education but also by a mix of socioeconomic change, evolutionary trade-offs, and cultural transmission17. Liberal orientations are typically linked to greater individualism, egalitarianism, and acceptance of childfree lifestyles7,18,19, all of which correlate with delayed or foregone childbearing. By contrast, conservative orientations emphasize family values, religiosity, and traditional family roles, which encourage higher fertility6.

However, becoming a parent may also be associated with shifts toward more socially conservative positions. For example, Kerry et al.20 report that parenthood and parental care motivation are associated with increased social conservatism. Similarly, Banducci et al.21 find that parenthood can shape policy preferences and may therefore contribute to attitudinal polarization.

These effects should be interpreted within the context of a broader literature demonstrating substantial stability in political orientations across the life course. “Classic work”22, argues that political attitudes are largely shaped during the “impressionable years” of adolescence and early adulthood and remain relatively stable thereafter. This conclusion is further supported by panel-data analyses23, which find that political attitudes remain remarkably stable over the long term. Likewise22, Alwin and Krosnick report that overall stability predominates across adulthood despite some variation.

In addition, substantial evidence indicates that political orientations are partly rooted in stable predispositions, including genetic influences. Twin studies24 suggest that genetics also plays a role in shaping political attitudes and ideologies. Together, these findings support the interpretation that political orientation is not purely situational but instead reflects relatively enduring individual differences established early in life and maintained over time.

Consistent with the assumption that political attitudes may influence reproduction, prospective evidence suggests that political orientation can precede fertility decisions. For example, Arpino and Mogi5 show that individuals with right-wing orientations are more likely to intend to have children in the near future, even after controlling for socioeconomic and attitudinal factors. Similarly, it has been demonstrated6 that political identity in youth predicts fertility desires long before actual childbearing occurs. More recently, Keskintürk25, using longitudinal survey data from Germany, Switzerland, and the United Kingdom, found that major adult life transitions—such as marriage, parenthood, unemployment, retirement, and entry into the workforce—have little to no lasting effect on political beliefs and preferences.

Taken together, the literature suggests that while parenthood can induce modest shifts in political attitudes, these effects operate on the basis of relatively stable, early-formed, and partly heritable political predispositions. Therefore, the assumption that political orientation can shape fertility outcomes remains well supported even though reverse causality cannot be fully excluded.

Furthermore, the rise of political polarization since the late twentieth century has been accompanied by a growing alignment between political identity and core life domains, including religion, family norms, and gender roles. As a result, political orientation has become more strongly associated with preferences directly relevant to fertility behavior, such as desired family size, attitudes toward marriage, and the value placed on children5,6. In addition, processes of assortative mating and social sorting along political orientation likely reinforce these patterns, amplifying differences between ideological groups. In particular, increasing geographic and social sorting has led Republicans and Democrats to cluster in distinct communities and social networks, thereby strengthening the alignment between political identity and family-related norms and behaviors26. Such sorting processes may further intensify differences in fertility-related preferences and behaviors across ideological groups.

Interestingly patterns differ among White and Black Americans. If both are analyzed separately, differences among whites become even more pronounced in later cohorts. Thus, White Americans show clearer and increasingly divergent fertility patterns by political orientation, while Black Americans display weaker and less consistent differences over time. The different patterns may reflect not only different fertility dynamics between blacks and whites, but also differences in how political orientation is measured and understood across racial groups. However, ideological labels such as “liberal” and “conservative” are less consistently understood among Black Americans27, which may explain why political self-identification among Black Americans is less strongly linked to fertility behavior than among White Americans.

We can only speculate on the mechanism leading to different fertility according to political orientation: although majorities of both Democrats and Republicans support the use of contraceptives, fertility differences may nevertheless partly reflect variation in contraceptive use and its underlying motivations (https://impactresearch.com/wp-content/uploads/2024/02/IMPACT-Analysis-Nationwide-Contraception-Poll-FEB-2024_external.pdf). Also attitudes toward abortion differ not only between parties but also within them, with liberals in both camps showing substantially greater acceptance; overall, however, support remains consistently higher among Democrats28 (Pew Research Center, 2022). More recent evidence points to a growing political divide in reproductive behavior already at young ages: for instance, adolescent females in Democratic-leaning states are substantially more likely to use hormonal contraception than those in Republican-leaning states29. European evidence similarly links political orientation to reproductive behavior. Individuals positioned on the political right—particularly at the extreme end—are more likely to express positive fertility intentions than those in the political center, even after accounting for religiosity, values, and socio-economic characteristics5. More broadly, conservative social attitudes are positively associated with fertility across countries, although the strength of this relationship varies substantially across contexts13. These findings underscore the importance of cultural values in shaping the relationship between fertility and political orientation.

Further at the individual level, ideology may influence how contraceptive choices are framed—either as an expression of personal autonomy and sexual freedom (more typical among liberals) or as potentially conflicting with traditional norms and religious teachings (more typical among conservatives). Research in political psychology shows that left-leaning individuals place greater emphasis on individual autonomy and self-expression, whereas right-leaning individuals are more likely to endorse tradition, conformity, and religious values30–32. These value differences extend to attitudes toward sexuality, family formation, and parental roles, which are key determinants of reproductive behavior. Consequently, liberals may be more likely to delay, limit, or forego childbearing than conservatives, consistent with broader evidence linking post-materialist and individualistic values to lower and postponed fertility. In this perspective, contraceptive use may serve different functions across ideological groups: among liberals, it may more often be used to prevent unintended births or reduce overall fertility, whereas among conservatives it may more often facilitate the timing of intended births. Thus, contraceptive behavior could be understood as one among other proximate mechanism reinforcing deeper ideological differences in fertility outcomes. Taken together, differences in contraceptive use may help explain why political orientation has become an increasingly important determinant of fertility in more recent cohorts, even in periods where aggregate fertility trends appear relatively stable.

Our findings also fit within the Second Demographic Transition (SDT) framework, which links below-replacement fertility to individualization, secularization, and changing family norms8,9,33. However, our study shows that these transitions are not ideologically neutral: left-leaning individuals appear more strongly influenced by SDT-related value shifts, leading to sub-replacement fertility, while right-leaning individuals resist or partially counter these shifts. Accordingly, the decline in fertility rates in the later birth cohorts is mainly caused by the dramatic fertility decline of left-wing individuals. If such fertility differentials persist across generations and are accompanied by intergenerational transmission of political orientations, they could contribute to long-term changes in the ideological composition of populations, echoing predictions made by both demographic and behavioral genetics research2,4.

At the same time, fertility is not determined solely by ideology. The interaction between sex and education reveals a significant negative effect for educated women (models not shown), suggesting that education has a more pronounced negative impact on female fertility. This pattern has been documented in previous research11,34. Nevertheless, education remains a powerful predictor of fertility outcomes overall and is consistently negatively associated with number of children among both men and women in our models, in line with findings across the demographic literature.

Additionally, we observed a significant negative interaction between being female and religious attendance (models not shown), indicating that religious attendance may confer stronger reproductive benefits for men than for women. This pattern is consistent with costly signaling interpretations of religious affiliation35.

Economic uncertainty also shapes fertility decisions, as highlighted in recent work from both Western and non-Western contexts36. Thus, while political ideology exerts a measurable and increasing influence, it interacts with structural, cultural, and economic factors in complex ways.

Limitations and future directions

Several limitations warrant caution. First, our measure of political orientation captures self-placement along a left–right continuum, which may oversimplify the multidimensional nature of ideology. For example, social and economic conservatism may have distinct relationships with fertility.

Future research should include individual-level data on fertility intentions and contraceptive use. Moreover, comparative studies across different sociopolitical regimes would help to assess whether the conservative fertility advantage observed in the U.S. and Europe generalizes globally, or whether it is specific to post-industrial, low-fertility societies.

In addition, the present analyses are observational and therefore cannot establish causal relationships between political orientation and fertility outcomes. Political orientation may influence reproductive behavior, but fertility experiences and parenthood may also shape political attitudes over time. Although prior longitudinal research suggests substantial stability in political orientations across adulthood, reciprocal influences cannot be excluded. Furthermore, the estimated selection gradients should be interpreted as statistical descriptions of fertility differentials rather than direct evidence of genetic evolutionary processes.

Conclusion

Our findings demonstrate that the long-term decline in U.S. fertility is not politically neutral. While earlier birth cohorts exhibited little fertility differentiation by ideology, cohorts born after the mid-1940s show a marked and growing divergence, with left-leaning individuals contributing disproportionately to sub-replacement fertility. Right-leaning individuals, by contrast, largely sustain fertility at or above replacement levels.

By applying phenotypic selection models, we show that this divergence translates into increasingly stronger directional selection favoring right-wing political orientations. Separate analyses of Black and White Americans, however, reveal that this holds true only for whites.

Although the observed associations should not be interpreted as evidence of any deterministic change in political ideology, these demographic differences may have implications for the long-term distribution of political orientations if they persist across generations and interact with social and familial transmission processes. More broadly, our study highlights how fertility decline, political values and evolutionary dynamics intersect in contemporary societies.

Methods

Sample-analysis

We used data from the US General Social Survey, which was collected between 1970 and 2022. The data comprised a total of 10,681 men and 12,294 women born between 1903 and 1982. All participants were over 40 years old and had either completed or almost completed their reproductive years. We divided the dataset into 17 five-year age-cohort intervals, from the birth year of 1903 to the birth year of 1982.

Political attitude was measured using a seven-point Likert scale ranging from very left (1) to very right (7), with intermediate categories representing increasing ideological conservatism (4 indicating the political center). Responses of 1–3 corresponded to the left side of the political spectrum, 4 to the political center, and 5–7 to the right side. For visualization purposes, we recoded the original seven-point scale into a three-category variable (1–3 = 0, left; 4 = 1, center; 5–7 = 2, right). In addition, for regression analyses, we constructed a binary indicator distinguishing left (1–3, coded as 0) from right (5–7, coded as 1).

We additionally included religious attendance (never = 0; less than once a year = 1; about once or twice a year = 2; several times a year = 3; about once a month = 4; 2–3 times a month = 5; nearly every week = 6; every week = 7; several times a week = 8), education (in years of education completed), sex (1 = male, 2 = female), as well as race (white = 1; black = 2; other = 3).

First, we plotted the mean number of children for each of the 17 birth year intervals (cohort fertility) according to political orientation (left, middle, and right). Then, we calculated Lande-Arnold37 models of the number of children, regressing them against political orientation, age, education (highest years of schooling completed) religious attendance, sex, race and consecutive birth intervals.

For the Lande–Arnold selection gradient analysis we estimated standardized selection gradients following Lande and Arnold37 using a Poisson GLM with log link. Fitness was measured as number of children; to model relative fitness, we included an offset of log(\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\overline{\mathrm{w}}}$$\end{document}), with \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\overline{\mathrm{w}}}$$\end{document} representing the sample mean fitness. Binary predictors (political attitude, sex) were coded as 0/1, and continuous predictors (age, religious attendance & education) were standardized (mean 0, SD 1). The model included linear terms (directional selection, β) for all predictors and quadratic terms only for continuous traits (nonlinear selection, γᵢᵢ = 2bᵢᵢ from the squared-term coefficient bᵢᵢ).

For the binary variable political attitude and sex, and the categorial term race, quadratic terms are not defined and were thus not estimated. Wald 95% CIs were derived from the GLM. We assessed overdispersion via Pearson χ2/df. In addition, as the quadratic (nonlinear) estimates for the continuous traits (age, religious attendance & education) were not statistically significant (all 95% CIs overlap zero), indicating no detectable stabilizing or disruptive selection for age, religious attendance and education, we proceeded in all analyses only with the linear estimates. Accordingly, only directional selection gradients are further shown. Lande-Arnold estimates for political orientation, religious attendance and education had been normalized by mean fitness (estimate / mean number of children) for the overall model (Table 1) as well as for the separate models for each of the 17 birth year intervals (Table 2).

In the supplement, we additionally analyzed the data separately for Back and White Americans because it is known that Black Americans who identify as “conservative” may frequently misunderstand ideological labels, making the common liberal–conservative scale unreliable for this group27.
