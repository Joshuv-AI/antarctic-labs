Emergence of Open Chemical Reaction Network Thermodynamics within Closed Systems Preprint: AIP/123-QED
Benedikt Remlein, Massimiliano Esposito, Francesco Avanzini

Abstract
We address a fundamental question: under which conditions do the dynamics and thermodynamics of open chemical reaction networks (CRNs), grounded on the notion of idealized chemostats that exchange selected species, emerge from underlying closed CRNs? While open CRNs provide the standard framework to describe out-of-equilibrium chemical systems, real systems are finite and ultimately relax to equilibrium, leaving the status of this description conceptually unresolved. Here we show that open-CRN behavior arises as an asymptotic regime of closed CRNs when two minimal and physically transparent conditions are met: a time-scale separation, whereby fast reactions effectively act as exchange mechanisms, and an abundance separation, whereby a subset of species behaves as chemostats with diverging chemical capacity. In this regime, both the stochastic dynamics and the thermodynamic structure – including local detailed balance, entropy production, and free-energy balance – emerge to leading order from the underlying closed CRN. Our results apply to arbitrary stoichiometries. They show that chemostats need not be introduced as external idealizations, but instead arise as emergent thermodynamic structures within closed systems, providing a unified and physically grounded foundation for the nonequilibrium thermodynamics of CRNs.

Introduction

When chemical systems are fueled
– namely,
when free energy is extracted from the interconversion
of high-energy species (commonly referred to as fuel)
into low-energy species (commonly referred to as waste) –
thermodynamically unfavorable reactions can be sustained,
thereby giving rise to complex out-of-equilibrium phenomena.
Such phenomena are a defining feature of living systems

.
In particular, metabolic processes transfer energy from nutrients to energetic mediator molecules
– primarily adenosine triphosphate –
which are subsequently consumed to power essential nonequilibrium functions.
These functions include, for instance,
directional transport via molecular motors
33
,
intracellular communication via calcium waves

;
24
,
local chemical organization through biomolecular condensates
12
;
78
,
as well as large-scale mechanical organization of the cytoskeleton
50
.
However, out-of-equilibrium phenomena are no longer exclusive to living systems.
Over the past two decades,
artificial systems have been systematically designed and synthesized to
generate analogous behaviors
5
.
Like living systems, these artificial systems can exhibit
directional motion
46
;
16

information transfer
60
,
local organization
66
;
20
,
as well as large-scale mechanical organization
74
.

Our understanding of the mechanisms underlying these complex out-of-equilibrium phenomena relies
to a large extent on
the nonequilibrium thermodynamics of open chemical reaction networks (CRNs)

;
51
;
63
;
54
;
55
;
11
;
67
.
Chemical systems are described in terms of their constituent species
together with the chemical reactions by which they are interconverted,
thereby defining a CRN
32
.
Fueling is modeled by treating CRNs as open systems,
which exchange specific species with the environment,
represented by chemostats.
A chemostat is an
infinitely
large molecular reservoir
that acts analogously to a thermostat:
the latter controls the temperature of a system while remaining at equilibrium,
just as the former controls the abundance of a species while remaining at equilibrium.
Over the past decades,
this nonequilibrium thermodynamic theory of open CRNs has developed into
a powerful and systematic framework, both conceptually and quantitatively.
It has, for instance,
shown that exchanging species is a necessary, but not sufficient, condition
for maintaining a CRN out of equilibrium
55
:
fueling can emerge only when the open CRN operates
as a catalyst for emergent effective reactions
that solely involve the chemostatted species,
while the internal species are continuously restored to their initial abundances.
Furthermore, this theory has elucidated
phenomena such as oscillations and chaos

;
29
,
the transition to homochirality
35
;
34
,
and nonequilibrium phase transitions

;
30
;
43
;
23
;
44
;
57
.
It has also revealed
thermodynamic constraints on out-of-equilibrium dynamics
–
including the cost of maintaining coherent oscillations
40
;
45
;
58
;
62

and the emergence of thermodynamic speed limits
76
;
77

and clarified the response of open CRNs to perturbations,
both in species abundances
18

and in reaction currents
61
;
1
;
21
;
39
.
Beyond its conceptual formulation,
this theory has yielded quantitative predictions.
These include
partitioning the free-energy cost of maintaining chemical systems out of equilibrium
into energy and information flows
2
;
47
;
36

and quantifying the thermodynamic efficiency of processes
from self-assembly

73
;
72

Yet this theory rests on a crucial idealization:
the environment is represented in terms of chemostats.
In reality, chemical systems are fueled by a
finite
amount of fuel,
which is eventually depleted,
and produce only a
finite
amount of waste,
which eventually accumulates.
In other words, real chemical systems are fundamentally closed
and ultimately relax to equilibrium.
This points to a fundamental conceptual issue:
whether the nonequilibrium thermodynamics of open CRNs
reflects an external modeling idealization
or instead corresponds to an emergent regime of closed out-of-equilibrium systems.
Recent studies have begun to investigate how to move beyond the use of chemostats.
Some works have considered alternative models for exchange mechanisms
15
;
8
;
38
;
37
,
while others have examined how finite molecular reservoirs affect CRNs
25
.
However,
their fundamental setup remains rooted in
the framework of open CRNs.
Hence, a fundamental question remains:
under which precise and general conditions
can the dynamics and thermodynamics of open CRNs
genuinely emerge from an underlying closed CRN?
In a previous contribution
56
,
we showed that, in the limit in which the abundances of certain species become macroscopic,

some
CRNs behave effectively as open ones.
However, this required restrictive stoichiometric constraints.
In this paper,
we show that
open-CRN dynamics and thermodynamics do emerge as a well-defined regime of closed CRNs,
and we identify general conditions under which this emergence occurs.

We do so by uncovering
the fundamental physical properties that underpin chemostats,
which are directly analogous to those underpinning thermostats.
A thermostat controls the temperature of a system
through the combination of two properties:
a fast energy-exchange mechanism and a diverging heat capacity.
The former ensures that
any energy variation in the system is rapidly balanced,
while the latter ensures that
the thermostat remains effectively unaffected by these exchanges
and thus at equilibrium.
Analogously, for a chemostat to control the abundance of a species in a CRN,
any variation in that abundance resulting from chemical reactions
must be rapidly balanced by exchanges with the chemostat,
while these exchanges are negligible from the perspective of the chemostat,
which therefore remains at equilibrium.
We characterize this later property by introducing the notion of
chemical capacity
,
which must diverge in chemostats in analogy with the diverging heat capacity of thermostats.
We show that closed CRNs can realize these properties,
thereby
generating an emergent regime in which
the stochastic dynamics governed by a chemical master equation
and the associated thermodynamic structure
– including local detailed balance, entropy production, and free-energy balance –
coincide to leading order with those of an open CRN.

The paper is organized as follows.
The main text presents both the physical picture and the essential derivations of our theory.
Each section begins with a broad, physics-based discussion,
followed by the key derivations, with emphasis on their physical meaning.
We focus on CRNs at the microscopic scale
(represented using the notation introduced in Sec.
II
),
where reactions are stochastic events
and the numbers of molecules are fluctuating variables,
but our approach also extends to CRNs at the macroscopic scale.
For open-CRN dynamics and thermodynamics (Sec.
III
) to emerge,
the two fundamental properties underpinning chemostats must be realized within closed CRNs
(Sec.
IV
).
These properties arise under specific dynamical conditions
on the reactions and species (Sec.
V
).
First, some reactions must act as the exchange mechanism with the chemostats:
they have to be much faster than the remaining reactions,
so that the latter are rapidly balanced
(Subs.
V.1
).
Mathematically,
this corresponds to a time-scale separation between two sets of reactions.
Second, some species involved in the fast reactions must behave
as if they had a diverging chemical capacity:
they have to be much more abundant than the remaining species,
so that their abundances are effectively unchanged by individual reaction events
(Subs.
V.2
).
Mathematically,
this corresponds to an abundance separation between two sets of species.
As a result,
the leading-order dynamics of a closed CRN
reduces to that of an open CRN
(Sec.
VI
).
Specifically, this open-CRN dynamics
emerges as a distinct dynamical regime
in an intermediate time window
that can span large time scales,
after the fast reactions have equilibrated
(Subs.
VI.1
)
and before the slower reactions significantly affect the abundant
species
(Subs.
VI.2
).
For instance,
the periodic oscillations predicted for the open Brusselator when driven far from equilibrium
emerge in a closed Brusselator over this time window (Sec.
VII
).
Moreover,
the emergent open CRN
inherits a fully consistent thermodynamic structure
(Sec.
VIII
).
The local detailed balance condition for open CRNs holds (Subs.
VIII.1
);
the dissipation associated with the emergent open-CRN dynamics is exactly equal to
the leading-order dissipation of the underlying closed CRN (Subs.
VIII.2
)
within the same intermediate time window,
after the fast reactions have equilibrated
and before the slower reactions significantly affect the abundant species
(Sec.
IX
);
furthermore, the dissipation satisfies a balance equation
identical to the second law for open CRNs (Sec.
VIII.3
).
Crucially, the emergence of open-CRN dynamics and thermodynamics
is independent of the stoichiometry of both the fast and the slower reactions
(Sec.
X
).
For particular fast reactions acting as a buffer solution,
our notion of chemical capacity is equivalent to the well-known notion of buffer capacity.
Conclusions are drawn in Sec.
XI
.

Overall, we establish precise physical conditions under which
open-CRN behavior emerges from closed CRNs,
showing that the nonequilibrium thermodynamics of open CRNs
is not merely an idealization,
but an emergent property of closed out-of-equilibrium systems.
This provides
a unified and thermodynamically consistent foundation
for the effective description of fueled chemical systems.

III
Open CRNs

Setup

The solvent is instead a non-reacting species

The environment is modeled in terms of

\mathcal{S}_{{c}}
species

1

Dynamical Conditions for the Emergence of Open CRNs

We now specify the dynamical conditions

Under these conditions, the
\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{exc}}

\mathcal{S}_{{Y}}

V.1
Time-Scale Separation

We begin by assuming that the
\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{exc}}
and
\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{int}}
reactions occur on distinct time scales,
with the former being much faster than the latter.
This assumption reflects the physical requirement that the chemostatting procedure in open CRNs
occurs on a much faster time scale than the internal reactions.
Specifically,
any variation in the abundances of the chemostatted species induced by the internal reactions
is rapidly compensated by the chemostatting procedure,
ensuring that the concentrations
[\bm{c}]
remain effectively constant.
This assumption can be mathematically formalized
in terms of a small dimensionless parameter
\varepsilon\ll 1
:
the kinetic constants
\{k_{\rho_{\mathrm{e}}}\}
and
\{k_{\rho_{\mathrm{i}}}\}

of the
\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{exc}}
and
\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{int}}
reactions, respectively,
satisfy

k_{\rho_{\mathrm{e}}}=\hat{\kappa}_{\rho_{\mathrm{e}}}\quad\text{ and }\quad k_{\rho_{\mathrm{i}}}=\varepsilon\,\hat{\kappa}_{\rho_{\mathrm{i}}}

(18)

with

\hat{\kappa}_{\rho_{\mathrm{e}}}=\mathcal{O}(1)

and
\hat{\kappa}_{\rho_{\mathrm{i}}}=\mathcal{O}(1)

as
\varepsilon\to 0
.

Because of the time-scale separation,
the chemical master equation (
8
)
describing the evolution of
the probability
p_{t}(\bm{n})
of there being
\bm{n}=(\dots,n_{x},\dots,n_{y},\dots,n_{Y},\dots)\in\mathcal{N}

molecules at time
t

can be systematically re-formulated in terms of a hierarchy of equations
of successive orders in
\varepsilon
.
The
\mathcal{O}(1)
equation of the hierarchy
(in the limit
\varepsilon\to 0
)
describes the fastest time scale
and
can be analytically solved as we show in the following.

To do so,
we first recognize that
the stochastic generator in Eq. (
9
)
can be expressed as the sum of two generators according to

W(\bm{n},\bm{m})=\hat{W}_{\mathrm{exc}}(\bm{n},\bm{m})+\varepsilon\,\hat{W}_{\mathrm{int}}(\bm{n},\bm{m})\,,

(19)

where

\displaystyle\hat{W}_{\mathrm{exc}}(\bm{n},\bm{m})

\displaystyle\equiv\sum_{\rho_{\mathrm{e}}}\hat{\omega}_{\rho_{\mathrm{e}}}(\bm{m})\big(\delta_{\bm{n}-\mathbf{S}_{\rho_{\mathrm{e}}},\bm{m}}-\delta_{\bm{n},\bm{m}}\big)\,,

(20a)

\displaystyle\hat{W}_{\mathrm{int}}(\bm{n},\bm{m})

\displaystyle\equiv\sum_{\rho_{\mathrm{i}}}\hat{\omega}_{\rho_{\mathrm{i}}}(\bm{m})\big(\delta_{\bm{n}-\mathbf{S}_{\rho_{\mathrm{i}}},\bm{m}}-\delta_{\bm{n},\bm{m}}\big)\,,

(20b)

account for
the
\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{exc}}
reactions occurring on the fast time scale
and
the
\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{int}}
reactions occurring on the slow time scale,
respectively,
with

\hat{\omega}_{\rho_{\mathrm{e}}}(\bm{m})\equiv\omega_{\rho_{\mathrm{e}}}(\bm{m})

and

\hat{\omega}_{\rho_{\mathrm{i}}}(\bm{m})\equiv\omega_{\rho_{\mathrm{i}}}(\bm{m})/\varepsilon
.
Second,
we characterize
the evolution of the probability
p_{t}(\bm{n})

in terms of two time variables,
rather than just one.
That is,
we introduce the time variable
\tau=t
, capturing the evolution on the fast time scale,
and the time variable
T=\varepsilon t
, capturing the evolution on the slow time scale,
such that

p_{t}(\bm{n})=p_{\tau,T}(\bm{n})

and
\mathrm{d}_{t}p_{t}(\bm{n})=\mathrm{d}_{\tau}p_{\tau,T}(\bm{n})+\varepsilon\mathrm{d}_{T}p_{\tau,T}(\bm{n})
.
Third,
we express the probability
p_{\tau,T}(\bm{n})

using a multiscale expansion in powers of
\varepsilon
:

p_{\tau,T}(\bm{n})=p_{\tau,T}^{(0)}(\bm{n})+\sum_{q=1}^{\infty}p_{\tau,T}^{(q)}(\bm{n})\varepsilon^{q}\,.

(21)

As shown in App.
A.1
,
the result is that
the
\mathcal{O}(1)
equation of the hierarchy
(in the limit
\varepsilon\to 0
)
reads

\mathrm{d}_{\tau}p_{\tau,T}^{(0)}(\bm{n})=\sum_{\bm{m}}\hat{W}_{\mathrm{exc}}(\bm{n},\bm{m})\,p_{\tau,T}^{(0)}(\bm{m})\,.

(22)

We now examine the main properties of Eq. (
22
)
and derive its analytical solution in the long-time limit
of the fast time-scale,
i.e.,
\tau\to\infty
.

The stochastic generator
\hat{W}_{\mathrm{exc}}(\bm{n},\bm{m})

appearing in Eq. (
22
)
and given in Eq. (
20a
)
encodes the fast dynamics arising from the
\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{exc}}
reactions.
Such dynamics,
according to the stoichiometry given in Eq. (
17
),
interconverts
each species
y
(resp.
Y(y)
) into species
Y(y)
(resp.
y
)
via reaction
\rho_{\mathrm{e}}(y)
(resp.
-\rho_{\mathrm{e}}(y)
)
with a one-to-one stoichiometry:
one molecule of
y
is interconverted into one molecule of
Y(y)
and vice versa.
Hence,
the total numbers of molecules
\bm{L}^{y}(\bm{n})=(\dots,L^{y}(\bm{n}),\dots)
,
with

L^{y}(\bm{n})\equiv n_{y}+n_{Y(y)}\,,

(23)

are conserved quantities.
Furthermore, such dynamics,
according to the stoichiometry given in Eq. (
17
),
does not interconvert the
\mathcal{S}_{{x}}
species.
Hence,
the numbers of molecules
\bm{L}^{x}(\bm{n})=(\dots,L^{x}(\bm{n}),\dots)
,
with

L^{x}(\bm{n})\equiv n_{x}\,,

(24)

are conserved quantities too.
This means that,
on the fast time scale described by Eq. (
22
)
and captured by the variable
\tau
,
the numbers of molecules
\bm{n}

(characterized by the specific values
\bm{L}^{x}
and
\bm{L}^{y}

of the quantities
\bm{L}^{x}(\bm{n})
and
\bm{L}^{y}(\bm{n})
, respectively)
evolve within the following stoichiometric compatibility class

\small\mathcal{N}(\bm{L}^{x},\bm{L}^{y})=\big\{\bm{n}\in\mathcal{N}:\bm{L}^{x}(\bm{n})=\bm{L}^{x}\text{ and }\bm{L}^{y}(\bm{n})=\bm{L}^{y}\big\}\,.

(25)

It is only on the slow time scale captured by the variable
T

that the quantities
\bm{L}^{x}
and
\bm{L}^{y}
evolve.
Consequently,
we can factorize the
zero-order probability
p_{\tau,T}^{(0)}(\bm{n})
(with
\bm{n}\in\mathcal{N}(\bm{L}^{x},\bm{L}^{y})
)
in terms of
a probability distribution for
\bm{n}
conditioned on the quantities
\bm{L}^{x}
and
\bm{L}^{y}

whose evolution is captured by the variable
\tau

and
a probability distribution for the quantities
\bm{L}^{x}
and
\bm{L}^{y}

whose evolution is captured by the variable
T
:

\small p_{\tau,T}^{(0)}(\bm{n})=\pi_{\tau}^{(0)}(\bm{n}|\bm{L}^{x},\bm{L}^{y})\,p_{T}^{(0)}(\bm{L}^{x},\bm{L}^{y})\,.

(26)

Equation (
22
) describes the evolution of
\pi_{\tau}^{(0)}(\bm{n}|\bm{L}^{x},\bm{L}^{y})
only.
As shown in App.
A.1
,
in the long-time limit of the fast time scale, i.e.,
\tau\to\infty
,
the probability

\pi_{\infty}^{(0)}(\bm{n}|\bm{L}^{x},\bm{L}^{y})\equiv\lim_{\tau\to\infty}\pi_{\tau}^{(0)}(\bm{n}|\bm{L}^{x},\bm{L}^{y})
,
representing the steady state distribution of the fast dynamics,
reads

\small\pi_{\infty}^{(0)}(\bm{n}|\bm{L}^{x},\bm{L}^{y})=\prod_{y}\pi_{\infty}^{(y)}(n_{y}|L^{y})\,\delta_{n_{y}+n_{Y(y)},L^{y}}\,\prod_{x}\delta_{n_{x},L^{x}}\,,

(27)

where
\delta_{\bullet,\bullet}
is the Kronecker delta and

\small\pi_{\infty}^{(y)}(n_{y}|L^{y})=\frac{L^{y}!}{n_{y}!(L^{y}-n_{y})!}\big(\mathfrak{p}_{y}\big)^{n_{y}}\big(\mathfrak{p}_{Y(y)}\big)^{L^{y}-n_{y}}\,,

(28)

with

\mathfrak{p}_{Y(y)}+\mathfrak{p}_{y}=1\quad\text{ and }\quad\mathfrak{p}_{Y(y)}/\mathfrak{p}_{y}=\hat{\kappa}_{\rho_{\mathrm{e}}(y)}/\hat{\kappa}_{-\rho_{\mathrm{e}}(y)}\,.

(29)

Note that this implies that
the average numbers of molecules of species
y
and
Y(y)

in the long-time limit of the fast time scale,
i.e.,

\langle n_{y}|L^{y}\rangle_{\infty}^{(0)}\equiv\sum_{n_{y}=0}^{L^{y}}n_{y}\,\pi_{\infty}^{(y)}(n_{y}|L^{y})

and

\langle n_{Y(y)}|L^{y}\rangle_{\infty}^{(0)}\equiv L^{y}-\langle n_{y}|L^{y}\rangle_{\infty}^{(0)}
,
read

\displaystyle\langle n_{y}|L^{y}\rangle_{\infty}^{(0)}

\displaystyle=L^{y}\mathfrak{p}_{y}\,,

(30a)

\displaystyle\langle n_{Y(y)}|L^{y}\rangle_{\infty}^{(0)}

\displaystyle=L^{y}\mathfrak{p}_{Y(y)}\,,

(30b)

respectively.

Remark.
In Subs.
VI.1
,
we consider the long-time limit of the fast time scale, i.e.,
\tau\to\infty
,
to derive a closed master equation (Eq. (
36
)) for the probability

p_{T}^{(0)}(\bm{L}^{x},\bm{L}^{y})
,
describing the evolution of the quantities
\bm{L}^{x}
and
\bm{L}^{y}

on the slow time scale captured by the variable
T
.
Such master equation does not coincide with
the chemical master equation of open CRNs (Eq. (
4
)):
the time-scale separation is a necessary, but not sufficient, condition
for the emergence of open-CRN dynamics.
The additional necessary condition is discussed in Subs.
V.2
.

Notation Remark.
With a slight abuse of notation,
we use
\bm{L}^{x}(\bm{n})
and
\bm{L}^{y}(\bm{n})
to denote the values of the conserved quantities
corresponding to given numbers of molecules
\bm{n}
,
while

\bm{L}^{x}
and
\bm{L}^{y}
denote arbitrary values of these conserved quantities.
According to Eq. (
25
),
for arbitrary
\bm{L}^{x}
and
\bm{L}^{y}
,
we have
\bm{L}^{x}(\bm{n})=\bm{L}^{x}
and
\bm{L}^{y}(\bm{n})=\bm{L}^{y}

for any
\bm{n}\in\mathcal{N}(\bm{L}^{x},\bm{L}^{y})
.
Consequently,
the probability distribution
\pi_{\tau}^{(0)}(\bm{n}|\bm{L}^{x},\bm{L}^{y})
(with
\bm{n}\in\mathcal{N}(\bm{L}^{x},\bm{L}^{y})
)
can equivalently be written as
\pi_{\tau}^{(0)}(\bm{n}|\bm{L}^{x}(\bm{n}),\bm{L}^{y}(\bm{n}))
.

V.2
Abundance Separation

We next assume that the
\mathcal{S}_{{Y}}
species are, on average,
much more abundant than the
\mathcal{S}_{{y}}
species.
This assumption reflects the physical requirement that chemostats remain essentially unaffected
by the chemostatting procedure.
As explained in App.
B.2
, if a chemostat is sufficiently large,
any finite exchange of molecules with the CRN
becomes negligible from the chemostat’s perspective.
We refer to this property as
diverging chemical capacity
,
in analogy with the diverging heat capacity of thermostats
(discussed in App.
B.1
).
This assumption can be mathematically formalized
in terms of a large dimensionless parameter
\Omega\gg 1
as follows.
First,
the total number of molecules
L^{y}
are
\mathcal{O}(\Omega)

as
\Omega\to\infty

for each species
y
.
Second, in the long-time limit of the fast time scale,
the average numbers of molecules

\langle n_{y}|L^{y}\rangle_{\infty}^{(0)}
and
\langle n_{Y(y)}|L^{y}\rangle_{\infty}^{(0)}

given in Eq. (
30
)
satisfy

\langle n_{y}|L^{y}\rangle_{\infty}^{(0)}=\mathcal{O}(1)\quad\text{ and }\quad\langle n_{Y(y)}|L^{y}\rangle_{\infty}^{(0)}=\mathcal{O}(\Omega)\,,

(31)

as
\Omega\to\infty
,
ensuring that
\langle n_{y}|L^{y}\rangle_{\infty}^{(0)}\ll\langle n_{Y(y)}|L^{y}\rangle_{\infty}^{(0)}
.
Note that this implies that

\mathfrak{p}_{y}=\mathcal{O}(\Omega^{-1})\quad\text{ and }\quad\mathfrak{p}_{Y(y)}=\mathcal{O}(1)

(32)

and, therefore,

{\mathfrak{p}_{Y(y)}}/{\mathfrak{p}_{y}}={\hat{\kappa}_{\rho_{\mathrm{e}}(y)}}/{\hat{\kappa}_{-\rho_{\mathrm{e}}(y)}}={\mathcal{O}}(\Omega)\,,

(33)

see Eqs. (
29
) and (
30
).
Physically,
the abundance separation corresponds to a time-scale separation between each pair of
forward and backward reactions
\pm\rho_{\mathrm{e}}(y)
.
This correspondence, however, is not general:
it arises from the unimolecular stoichiometry of the
\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{exc}}
considered here,
and breaks down when
the
\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{exc}}
reactions have multimolecular stoichiometries, as discussed in Sec.
X
.

Remark.
In Subs.
VI.2
,
we apply the abundance separation
to the closed master equation
for the probability
p_{T}^{(0)}(\bm{L}^{x},\bm{L}^{y})
(Eq. (
36
)),
in the long-time limit of the fast time scale, i.e.,
\tau\to\infty
.
As a result, the master equation (
36
) converges to
the chemical master equation of open CRNs (Eq. (
4
)),
with the
\mathcal{S}_{{x}}
and
\mathcal{S}_{{y}}
species
acting as internal and chemostatted species, respectively.

V.3
Summary

The dynamical conditions introduced here specify
how the kinetic constants
\{k_{\rho_{\mathrm{e}}}\}
and
\{k_{\rho_{\mathrm{i}}}\}
scale with
\varepsilon

(Subs.
V.1
)
and how the abundances of the
\mathcal{S}_{{Y}}
species scale with
\Omega

(Subs.
V.2
).
They also imply that the abundances of the
\mathcal{S}_{{x}}\cup\mathcal{S}_{{y}}
species
do not scale with
\varepsilon
or
\Omega
,
and that the kinetic constants
\{k_{\rho_{\mathrm{i}}}\}
do not scale with
\Omega
.
In the following, we first take the time-scale separation limit (
\varepsilon\to 0
)
and then the abundance separation limit (
\Omega\to\infty
).
These limits do not commute.
Specifically,

\varepsilon\to 0
faster than
\Omega\to\infty
.
These dynamical conditions are illustrated in Fig.
1
.

Illustration of the dynamical conditions yielding open-CRN behavior
for a particular closed CRN with species
\mathcal{S}=\{x_{1},x_{2},y_{1},y_{2},Y_{1},Y_{2}\}
.
Time-scale separation:
the kinetic constants of the

\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{int}}
(golden arrows) and
\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{exc}}
(dark blue arrows) reactions
are

\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon)
and
\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{0})=\mathcal{O}(1)
,
respectively.
Abundance separation:
the numbers of molecules of the

\mathcal{S}_{{x}}\cup\mathcal{S}_{{y}}
(purple symbols) and
\mathcal{S}_{{Y}}
(geen symbols) species are

\mathcal{O}(\Omega^{0})=\mathcal{O}(1)
and
\mathcal{O}(\Omega)
,
respectively.

VI
Emergent Open-CRN Dynamics

We now show that open-CRN dynamics emerges naturally from closed CRNs
(with species and reactions partitioned as in Sec.
IV
)
under the
dynamical conditions introduced in Sec.
V

and specified by Eqs. (
18
) and (
31
).
To this end, we derive the master equation for the probability
p_{T}^{(0)}(\bm{L}^{x},\bm{L}^{y})
,
which describes the evolution of the quantities
\bm{L}^{x}
and
\bm{L}^{y}

on the slow time scale captured by the variable
T
.
Crucially,
we show that this master equation converges to the chemical master equation for open CRNs
when both
the time-scale separation
(Eq. (
18
) in Sub.
V.1
)
and the abundance separation
(Eq. (
31
) in Sub.
V.2
)
are satisfied.

VI.1
Time-scale Separation

We begin by examining the implications of the time-scale separation
between the
\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{exc}}
and
\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{int}}
reactions,
introduced to reflect the fast chemostatting procedure in open CRNs.
To do so, we focus on the
O(\varepsilon)
equation of the hierarchy
resulting from collecting terms of order
\varepsilon

of the chemical master equation (
8
) (or, equivalently, Eq. (
82
)).
According to App.
A.1
,
such equation reads

\begin{split}\mathrm{d}_{\tau}p_{\tau,T}^{(1)}(\bm{n})+\mathrm{d}_{T}p_{\tau,T}^{(0)}(\bm{n})=&\sum_{\bm{m}}\hat{W}_{\mathrm{exc}}(\bm{n},\bm{m})p_{\tau,T}^{(1)}(\bm{m})\\
&+\sum_{\bm{m}}\hat{W}_{\mathrm{int}}(\bm{n},\bm{m})p_{\tau,T}^{(0)}(\bm{m})\,,\end{split}

(34)

and couples
the zero-order probability
p_{\tau,T}^{(0)}(\bm{n})

and
the first-order probability
p_{\tau,T}^{(1)}(\bm{n})

of the multiscale expansion in Eq. (
21
).
We now exploit the properties of the fast dynamics discussed in Subs.
V.1

to transform Eq. (
34
) into
a closed equation for the zero-order probability

p_{T}^{(0)}(\bm{L}^{x},\bm{L}^{y})
.

To do so,
we first recall that the fast dynamics arising from the
\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{exc}}
reactions
occurs within the stoichiometric compatibility class
\mathcal{N}(\bm{L}^{x},\bm{L}^{y})

given in Eq. (
25
).
This implies that
the
\mathcal{O}(1)
equation, as
\varepsilon\to 0
,
of the hierarchy in Eq. (
22
)
conserves the probability within each stoichiometric compatibility class
\mathcal{N}(\bm{L}^{x},\bm{L}^{y})

or, equivalently,

\sum_{\bm{n}\in\mathcal{N}(\bm{L}^{x},\bm{L}^{y})}\hat{W}_{\mathrm{exc}}(\bm{n},\bm{m})=0
.
Consequently, by summing over
{\bm{n}\in\mathcal{N}(\bm{L}^{x},\bm{L}^{y})}
,
Eq. (
34
) becomes

\begin{split}&\sum_{\bm{n}\in\mathcal{N}(\bm{L}^{x},\bm{L}^{y})}\bigg(\mathrm{d}_{\tau}p_{\tau,T}^{(1)}(\bm{n})+\mathrm{d}_{T}p_{\tau,T}^{(0)}(\bm{n})\bigg)\\
&=\sum_{\bm{n}\in\mathcal{N}(\bm{L}^{x},\bm{L}^{y})}\sum_{\bm{m}}\hat{W}_{\mathrm{int}}(\bm{n},\bm{m})p_{\tau,T}^{(0)}(\bm{m})\,.\end{split}

(35)

Second,
we consider the long-time limit of the fast time scale, i.e.,
\tau\to\infty
.
This physically means that
we consider a slow time scale such that
the fast dynamics arising from the
\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{exc}}
reactions has already relaxed to steady state.
Furthermore, on such a slow time scale,
any quantity evolving on the fast time scale captured by the variable
\tau

is averaged out.
Mathematically,
i)

\lim_{\tau\to\infty}\,\mathrm{d}_{\tau}p_{\tau,T}^{(1)}(\bm{n})=0
,
ii)

\lim_{\tau\to\infty}\,p_{\tau,T}^{(0)}(\bm{n})=\pi_{\infty}^{(0)}(\bm{n}|\bm{L}^{x},\bm{L}^{y})\,p_{T}^{(0)}(\bm{L}^{x},\bm{L}^{y})

for any
\bm{n}\in\mathcal{N}(\bm{L}^{x},\bm{L}^{y})
,
and iii) any function
f(\bm{n})
is averaged over the fast dynamics
leading to

\langle f|\bm{L}^{x},\bm{L}^{y}\rangle_{\infty}^{(0)}\equiv\sum_{\bm{n}\in\mathcal{N}(\bm{L}^{x},\bm{L}^{y})}f(\bm{n})\,\pi_{\infty}^{(0)}(\bm{n}|\bm{L}^{x},\bm{L}^{y})
.
As shown in App.
A.2
,
Eq. (
35
)
thus becomes

\mathrm{d}_{T}p_{T}^{(0)}(\bm{L}^{x},\bm{L}^{y})=\sum_{\rho_{\mathrm{i}}}\big\{\langle\hat{\omega}_{\rho_{\mathrm{i}}}|\bm{L}^{x}-\mathbf{S}_{x,\rho_{\mathrm{i}}},\bm{L}^{y}-\mathbf{S}_{y,\rho_{\mathrm{i}}}\rangle_{\infty}^{(0)}\,p_{T}^{(0)}(\bm{L}^{x}-\mathbf{S}_{x,\rho_{\mathrm{i}}},\bm{L}^{y}-\mathbf{S}_{y,\rho_{\mathrm{i}}})-\langle\hat{\omega}_{\rho_{\mathrm{i}}}|\bm{L}^{x},\bm{L}^{y}\rangle_{\infty}^{(0)}\,p_{T}^{(0)}(\bm{L}^{x},\bm{L}^{y})\big\}\,,

(36)

where the average reaction rates
\{\langle\hat{\omega}_{\rho_{\mathrm{i}}}|\bm{L}^{x},\bm{L}^{y}\rangle_{\infty}^{(0)}\}
can be analytically determined
and read

\small\begin{split}\langle\hat{\omega}_{\rho_{\mathrm{i}}}|\bm{L}^{x},\bm{L}^{y}\rangle_{\infty}^{(0)}=\hat{\kappa}_{\rho_{\mathrm{i}}}V&\prod_{x}\frac{L^{x}!\,\theta(L^{x}-\nu_{x,\rho_{\mathrm{i}}})}{(L^{x}-\nu_{x,\rho_{\mathrm{i}}})!\,V^{\nu_{x,\rho_{\mathrm{i}}}}}\\
\times&\prod_{y}\frac{L^{y}!\,\theta(L^{y}-\nu_{y,\rho_{\mathrm{i}}})}{(L^{y}-\nu_{y,\rho_{\mathrm{i}}})!\,V^{\nu_{y,\rho_{\mathrm{i}}}}}(\mathfrak{p}_{y})^{\nu_{y,\rho_{\mathrm{i}}}}\,.\end{split}

(37)

Equation (
36
) is a closed master equation
for the probability
p_{T}^{(0)}(\bm{L}^{x},\bm{L}^{y})
.
It has the structure of a chemical master equation (
4
):
it describes the evolution of the probability of there being

\bm{L}^{x}
molecules of
\mathcal{S}_{{x}}
species
and

\bm{L}^{y}
total molecules of
\mathcal{S}_{{y}}
and
\mathcal{S}_{{Y}}
species,
arising from the
\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{int}}
reactions whose rates have been averaged over the fast time scale.
However, these average reaction rates in Eq. (
37
)
do not coincide with the reaction rates in Eq. (
5
)
of the chemical master equation for open CRNs,
and
the quantities
\bm{L}^{y}
remain dynamical variables.
As previously discussed,
the time-scale separation is a necessary, but not sufficient, condition for
the emergence of open-CRN dynamics.
In Subs.
VI.2
,
we show that imposing abundance separation as well
leads to the full emergence of open-CRN dynamics.

VI.2
Abundance Separation

We now examine the implications of the abundance separation
between the
\mathcal{S}_{{y}}
and
\mathcal{S}_{{Y}}
species,
introduced to reflect the diverging chemical capacity of chemostats.
To do so, we focus on the master equation (
36
).

We first recognize that
the abundance separation,
i.e.,
L^{y}=\mathcal{O}(\Omega)
and
\mathfrak{p}_{y}=\mathcal{O}(\Omega^{-1})

(see Eq. (
32
)),
implies that the average reaction rates
given in Eq. (
37
) and featuring Eq. (
36
)
can be split into the sum of terms of successive order in
\Omega^{-1}
.
By taking, as
\Omega\to\infty
,
the
\mathcal{O}(1)
term only
and
neglecting the successive orders,
the average reaction rates can be approximated according to

\displaystyle\langle\hat{\omega}_{\rho_{\mathrm{i}}}|\bm{L}^{x},\bm{L}^{y}\rangle_{\infty}^{(0)}

\displaystyle\approx\hat{R}_{\rho_{\mathrm{i}}}(\bm{L}^{x},\bm{L}^{y})\,,

(38a)

\displaystyle\langle\hat{\omega}_{\rho_{\mathrm{i}}}|\bm{L}^{x}-\mathbf{S}_{x,\rho_{\mathrm{i}}},\bm{L}^{y}-\mathbf{S}_{y,\rho_{\mathrm{i}}}\rangle_{\infty}^{(0)}

\displaystyle\approx\hat{R}_{\rho_{\mathrm{i}}}(\bm{L}^{x}-\mathbf{S}_{x,\rho_{\mathrm{i}}},\bm{L}^{y})\,,

(38b)

where

\small\hat{R}_{\rho_{\mathrm{i}}}(\bm{L}^{x},\bm{L}^{y})\equiv\hat{\kappa}_{\rho_{\mathrm{i}}}V\prod_{x}\frac{L^{x}!\,\theta(L^{x}-\nu_{x,\rho_{\mathrm{i}}})}{(L^{x}-\nu_{x,\rho_{\mathrm{i}}})!\,V^{\nu_{x,\rho_{\mathrm{i}}}}}\prod_{y}\bigg(\frac{L^{y}\,\mathfrak{p}_{y}}{V}\bigg)^{\nu_{y,\rho_{\mathrm{i}}}}

(39)

with
L^{y}\,\mathfrak{p}_{y}=\mathcal{O}(1)
.

According to Eq. (
30a
),
each factor
L^{y}\,\mathfrak{p}_{y}
is
the average number of molecules of species
y
for a given
L^{y}

when the fast dynamics, captured by the variable
\tau
,
has already relaxed to steady state.
Each factor
L^{y}\,\mathfrak{p}_{y}/{V}
is thus the corresponding average concentration
entering Eq. (
39
)
in the same functional way the concentration of each chemostatted species enters
the expression of the reaction rates of open CRNs in Eq. (
5
).
However, at this stage,
the equivalence between the master equation (
36
)
endowed with the rates in Eq. (
39
)
and the chemical master equation (
4
) is not complete:
the missing ingredient is the time independence of the average concentrations of the
\mathcal{S}_{{y}}
species,
which reflects the time independence of the concentrations of the chemostatted species in open CRNs.
Such time independence emerges, to leading order, as another implication of the abundance separation.

Indeed, we recognize that the large abundance
L^{y}=\mathcal{O}(\Omega)
further ensures that
the probability
p_{T}^{(0)}(\bm{L}^{y}|\bm{L}^{x})

of there being
\bm{L}^{y}
total molecules for the
\mathcal{S}_{{y}}
and
\mathcal{S}_{{Y}}
species
conditioned on there being
\bm{L}^{x}
molecules for the
\mathcal{S}_{{x}}
species
satisfies a large deviation principle.
In this context, as proved in App.
C
,
this means that
p_{T}^{(0)}(\bm{L}^{y}|\bm{L}^{x})

is sharply peaked around time-independent and
\bm{L}^{x}
-independent
most probable values

VI.3
Summary

Closed CRNs (with species and reactions partitioned as in Sec.
IV
)
give rise, to leading order, to open-CRN dynamics
when two complementary dynamical conditions are satisfied:
a time-scale separation (Eq. (
18
)),
ensuring that the
\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{exc}}
reactions are much faster than the
\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{int}}
reactions,
and
an abundance separation (Eq. (
31
)),
ensuring that the
\mathcal{S}_{{Y}}
species remain effectively unaffected by the slow dynamics.
Together, these conditions allow the
\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{exc}}
reactions to act as a chemostatting procedure
and the
\mathcal{S}_{{y}}
species to play the role of chemostatted species,
while the
\mathcal{S}_{{x}}
species and
\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{int}}
reactions effectively become
the internal species and reactions of the emergent open CRN.
Under this regime,
the chemical master equation of a closed CRN reduces to
that of an open CRN,
demonstrating that open-CRN dynamics emerges naturally from closed CRNs
purely as a consequence of dynamical conditions
(Sec.
V
).

VIII
Emergent Open-CRN Thermodynamics

We now show that open-CRN thermodynamics emerges naturally from closed CRNs
(with species and reactions partitioned as in Sec.
IV
)
under the dynamical conditions
introduced in Sec.
V

and specified by Eqs. (
18
) and (
31
).
To this end, we demonstrate that the emergent open-CRN dynamics
is thermodynamically consistent.
Specifically, we prove that:
i) the reaction rates in Eq. (
41
) satisfy a local detailed balance condition
identical to that of open CRNs (Subs.
VIII.1
);
ii) the dissipation of the emergent open CRN coincides, at leading order,
with that of the underlying closed CRN (Subs.
VIII.2
);
and
iii) the balance equation for the average Gibbs potential has the same structure and physical meaning
as the second law for open CRNs (Subs.
VIII.3
).

VIII.1
Local Detailed Balance Condition

We show here that
the reaction rates in Eq. (
41
) satisfy a local detailed balance condition
which is formally equivalent to the one in Eq. (
10
) for open CRNs.
To do so,
we specialize Eq. (
10

for the
\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{int}}
reactions in Eq. (

(with species and reactions partitioned as in Sec.

\small\begin{split}\ln\frac{\omega_{\rho_{\mathrm{i}}}(\bm{n})}{\omega_{-\rho_{\mathrm{i}}}(\bm{n}+\mathbf{S}_{i,\rho})}=&-\sum_{x}\bigg\{(\mu_{x}^{\circ}-\ln n_{s})S_{x,\rho_{\mathrm{i}}}+\ln\frac{(n_{x}+S_{x,\rho_{\mathrm{i}}})!}{n_{x}!}\bigg\}\\
&-\sum_{y}\bigg\{(\mu_{y}^{\circ}-\ln n_{s})S_{y,\rho_{\mathrm{i}}}+\ln\frac{(n_{y}+S_{y,\rho_{\mathrm{i}}})!}{n_{y}!}\bigg\}\end{split}

(45)

which, by using mass-action kinetics
(given in Eq. (
5

can be, equivalently, rewritten as

\ln\frac{\hat{\kappa}_{\rho_{\mathrm{i}}}}{\hat{\kappa}_{-\rho_{\mathrm{i}}}}=-\sum_{x}(\mu_{x}^{\circ}-\ln[s])S_{x,\rho_{\mathrm{i}}}-\sum_{y}(\mu_{y}^{\circ}-\ln[s])S_{y,\rho_{\mathrm{i}}}\,.

(46)

Note that Eq. (

the kinetic constants
\hat{\kappa}_{\pm\rho_{\mathrm{i}}}=k_{\pm\rho_{\mathrm{i}}}/\varepsilon

and the standard chemical potentials
\{\mu_{x}^{\circ}\}

\{\mu_{y}^{\circ}\}

Hence, by now using Eq. (

\hat{r}_{\rho_{\mathrm{i}}}(\bm{L}^{x}|[\bm{y}])

\hat{r}_{-\rho_{\mathrm{i}}}(\bm{L}^{x}+\mathbf{S}_{x,\rho_{\mathrm{i}}}|[\bm{y}])

41

we obtain that

\small\ln\frac{\hat{r}_{\rho_{\mathrm{i}}}(\bm{L}^{x}|[\bm{y}])}{\hat{r}_{-\rho_{\mathrm{i}}}(\bm{L}^{x}+\mathbf{S}_{x,\rho_{\mathrm{i}}}|[\bm{y}])}=-\Big\{\Delta_{\rho_{\mathrm{i}}}\,{\hat{g}}(\bm{L}^{x})+\sum_{y}\mu_{y}([y])S_{y,\rho_{\mathrm{i}}}\Big\}\,.

\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{int}}

\braket{\dot{\Sigma}^{(1)}_{\mathrm{i}}}_{\infty,T}={\sum_{\rho_{\mathrm{i}},\bm{L}^{x}}\hat{r}_{\rho_{\mathrm{i}}}(\bm{L}^{x}|[\bm{y}])P_{T}(\bm{L}^{x})\ln\frac{\hat{r}_{\rho_{\mathrm{i}}}(\bm{L}^{x}|[\bm{y}])P_{T}(\bm{L}^{x})}{\hat{r}_{-\rho_{\mathrm{i}}}(\bm{L}^{x}+\mathbf{S}_{x,\rho_{\mathrm{i}}}|[\bm{y}])P_{T}(\bm{L}^{x}+\mathbf{S}_{x,\rho_{\mathrm{i}}})}}\,\equiv\langle\hat{\dot{\Sigma}}\rangle_{T}\geq 0\,.

(55)

with
[\bm{y}]
given in Eq. (
42
).
Equation (
55
), together with Eq. (
53
),
establishes the equivalence between
the leading-order contribution to the average entropy production rate
of closed CRNs (left-most-hand side)
and
the average entropy production rate of the emergent open CRN
resulting from applying Eq. (
13
) to Eq. (

),
denoted
\langle\hat{\dot{\Sigma}}\rangle_{T}

(right-hand side).

Remark.
The emergent open-CRN dynamics arises solely from the
\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{int}}
reactions.
Nevertheless, it captures (to leading order) the entire dissipation of the underlying closed CRN
on the slow time-scale
T

because the
\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{exc}}
reactions rapidly equilibrate.

Remark.
The evolution of the emergent open CRN occurs on the slow time scale captured by the variable
T
.
Accordingly, the average entropy production rate
\langle\hat{\dot{\Sigma}}\rangle_{T}

measures the rate of dissipation on this slow time scale.
It thus differs from the average entropy production rate of the underlying closed CRN
by a factor
\varepsilon

since the latter measures the rate of dissipation on the fast time scale,
captured by
t=T/\varepsilon
.
This different scaling becomes explicit when combining
Eq. (
55

53
).

VIII.3
Second Law of Thermodynamics

We derive
here the balance equation for the average Gibbs potential
associated with the emergent open CRN
by decomposing the corresponding average entropy production rate
given in Eq. (
55
).
Crucially,
we show that such a balance equation has the same structure and physical meaning as
the second law of thermodynamics of open CRNs given in Eq. (
15
).

We start from the average entropy production rate given in Eq. (
55
)
and
use the local detailed balance condition (
47
).
We thus obtain

\begin{split}\langle\hat{\dot{\Sigma}}\rangle_{T}=-\sum_{\rho_{\mathrm{i}},\bm{L}^{x}}&\hat{r}_{\rho_{\mathrm{i}}}(\bm{L}^{x}|[\bm{y}])P_{T}(\bm{L}^{x})\,\Big\{\Delta_{\rho_{\mathrm{i}}}\,{\hat{G}_{T}}(\bm{L}^{x})\\
&+\sum_{y}\mu_{y}([y])S_{y,\rho_{\mathrm{i}}}\Big\}\,.\end{split}

(56)

Here,
\Delta_{\rho_{\mathrm{i}}}{\hat{G}_{T}}(\bm{L}^{x})\equiv{\hat{G}_{T}}(\bm{L}^{x}+\mathbf{S}_{x,\rho_{\mathrm{i}}})-{\hat{G}_{T}}(\bm{L}^{x})

is the change in Gibbs potential
along reaction
\rho_{\mathrm{i}}

due to the
\mathcal{S}_{{x}}
species
and

{\hat{G}_{T}}(\bm{L}^{x})={\hat{g}}(\bm{L}^{x})+\ln P_{T}(\bm{L}^{x})

(57)

is the Gibbs free potential
(in units of temperature and Boltzmann constant)
of an ideal solution with
\bm{L}^{x}
molecules of
\mathcal{S}_{{x}}
species
(with
{\hat{g}}(\bm{L}^{x})
the corresponding Gibbs free energy given in Eq. (
48
)).
Note that the Gibbs potential in Eq. (
58
)
has the same structure of the Gibbs potential introduced in Eq. (
14
)
for open CRNs.
We now recognize that Eq. (
56
) can be written
in terms of a balance equation for the average Gibbs potential

\braket{\hat{G}}_{T}=\sum_{\bm{L}^{x}}P_{T}(\bm{L}^{x})\big\{{\hat{g}}(\bm{L}^{x})+\ln P_{T}(\bm{L}^{x})\big\}

(58)

and reads

\langle\hat{\dot{\Sigma}}\rangle_{T}=-\mathrm{d}_{T}\braket{\hat{G}}_{T}+\braket{\hat{\dot{W}}_{\mathrm{chm}}}_{T}\geq 0\,,

(59)

where

\small\braket{\hat{\dot{W}}_{\mathrm{chm}}}_{T}\equiv\sum_{y}\mu_{y}([y])\sum_{\rho_{\mathrm{i}},\bm{L}^{x}}(-S_{y,\rho_{\mathrm{i}}})\hat{r}_{\rho_{\mathrm{i}}}(\bm{L}^{x}|[\bm{y}])P_{T}(\bm{L}^{x})\,.

(60)

has the same functional form as the average chemical work introduced in Eq. (
15
).
This physically means that
\braket{\hat{\dot{W}}_{\mathrm{chm}}}_{T}
can be interpreted as
the free energy exchanges with the
\mathcal{S}_{{Y}}
species,
which act as chemostats.

VIII.4
Summary

Closed CRNs
(with species and reactions partitioned as in Sec.
IV
)
give rise (to leading order) to open-CRN thermodynamics
when the two complementary dynamical conditions giving rise to open-CRN dynamics
(namely, time-scale separation and abundance separation) are satisfied.
Indeed, under these conditions, the emergent open CRN is thermodynamically consistent:
its reaction rates satisfy a local detailed balance condition identical to that of open CRNs,
its dissipation coincides, at leading order, with that of the underlying closed CRN,
and
the balance equation for its average Gibbs potential has the same structure and physical meaning as the second law for open CRNs.
Accordingly, the thermodynamic structure of the closed CRN reduces to that of an open CRN,
demonstrating that open-CRN thermodynamics emerges naturally from closed CRNs
as a direct consequence of the imposed
dynamical conditions (Sec.
V
).

X.1
Stoichiometry and Conserved Quantities

Let us start by setting the stoichiometry of the
\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{exc}}
reactions.
We assume that every species
y
is interconverted
through a specific pair of forward and backward reactions
\pm\rho_{\mathrm{e}}(y)\in\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{exc}}

involving the unique set of species
\mathcal{Y}(y)\subset\mathcal{S}_{{Y}}

(i.e.,
\mathcal{Y}(y)\bigcap\mathcal{Y}(y^{\prime})=\emptyset
if
y\neq y^{\prime}
)
according to the chemical equation

\nu_{y,\rho_{\mathrm{e}}(y)}{}\mathrm{Z}_{y}+\sum_{Y\in\mathcal{Y}(y)}\nu_{Y,\rho_{\mathrm{e}}(y)}{}\mathrm{Z}_{Y}{}\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{0.94722pt}{$\mathrel{\mathop{\makebox[0.0pt]{\to}}\limits^{\mkern 9.0mu{}\mathrm{\text{${\rho}_{\mathrm{e}}(y)$}}\mkern 9.0mu}_{\makebox{\raisebox{3.76735pt}[0.0pt]{$\scriptstyle\mkern 9.0mu\hphantom{{}\mathrm{\text{$-{\rho}_{\mathrm{e}}(y)$}}}\mkern 5.0mu$}}}}$}\hss}\raisebox{-0.94722pt}{$\mathrel{\mathop{\makebox[0.0pt]{\to}}\limits^{\mkern 5.0mu\hphantom{{}\mathrm{\text{${\rho}_{\mathrm{e}}(y)$}}}\mkern 9.0mu}_{\makebox{\raisebox{3.76735pt}[0.0pt]{$\scriptstyle\mkern 9.0mu{}\mathrm{\text{$-{\rho}_{\mathrm{e}}(y)$}}\mkern 9.0mu$}}}}$}}{}\nu_{y,-\rho_{\mathrm{e}}(y)}{}\mathrm{Z}_{y}+\sum_{Y\in\mathcal{Y}(y)}\nu_{Y,-\rho_{\mathrm{e}}(y)}{}\mathrm{Z}_{Y}\,.

(64)

Accordingly,
the entries of
\mathbf{S}_{\rho_{\mathrm{e}}(y)}

corresponding to any species

and
Y^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Y}(y^{\prime})
(with
y^{\prime}\neq y
) vanish,
i.e.,
S_{x,\rho_{\mathrm{e}}(y)}=0
and
S_{Y^{\prime},\rho_{\mathrm{e}}(y)}=0
.
On the other hand,
the entries
corresponding to species
y
and
Y\in\mathcal{Y}(y)

read

S_{y,\rho_{\mathrm{e}}(y)}=\nu_{y,-\rho_{\mathrm{e}}(y)}-\nu_{y,\rho_{\mathrm{e}}(y)}

,

\bm{L}^{x}(\bm{n})=(\dots,L^{x}(\bm{n}),\dots)

\mathcal{S}_{{x}}

with

{Y\in\mathcal{Y}(y)}

(68)

by introducing

\displaystyle a_{Y}(\bm{L}^{\lambda})

\displaystyle\equiv\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda(y)}\overline{\ell}_{\lambda}^{Y}L^{\lambda}\,,

(69a)

\displaystyle b_{Y,y}

\displaystyle\equiv\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda(y)}\overline{\ell}_{\lambda}^{Y}{\ell}^{\lambda}_{y}\,.

(69b)

XI
Conclusions & perspectives

We have identified precise conditions under which the dynamics and thermodynamics of open CRNs emerge from an underlying closed CRN:
a time-scale separation (between the
\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{exc}}
and
\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{int}}
reactions) and
an abundance separation (between the
\mathcal{S}_{{x}}
and
\mathcal{S}_{{y}}
species, on the one hand,
and the
\mathcal{S}_{{Y}}
species, on the other hand).
In the intermediate time window
after the rapid equilibration of the fast
\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{exc}}
reactions
and before the abundant
\mathcal{S}_{{Y}}
species are significantly affected by the reactions,
closed CRNs enter a well-defined regime in which their
leading-order dynamics and thermodynamics reduce
to those of an open CRN.
This time window becomes infinitely large in the asymptotic regime, where

\varepsilon\to 0
and
\Omega\to\infty
.
Crucially, our results do not rely on specific stoichiometric constraints,
and are instead systematically derived using
a perturbation ansatz (to account for the time-scale separation)
and
a WKB ansatz (to account for the abundance separation).
We have thus shown that chemostats do not need to be postulated
as external idealizations;
rather they arise naturally as emergent thermodynamic structures
within closed CRNs under precise physical conditions.

The emergence of open-CRN behavior can be understood as a form of coarse-graining:
the fast
\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{exc}}
reactions and the abundant
\mathcal{S}_{{Y}}
species are effectively eliminated,
yielding an effective description in terms of
the slow
\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{int}}
reactions and the remaining species,
which is equivalent (to leading order) to that of an open CRN.
In the context of CRNs,
other coarse-graining approaches have been developed,
but they typically rely on either time-scale separation or abundance separation alone.
For instance, time-scale separation between reactions
(partitioned in a similar way as in Sec.
IV
)
has been used to derive a closed description in terms of conserved quantities
65
,
while time-scale separation between chemical species
has been used to derive a closed (and thermodynamically consistent)
description in terms of the slowly evolving species interconverted by effective reactions
9
;
10
;
7
.
Abundance separation was used by us in a previous contribution
56
to show that
closed CRNs can behave like open ones, but only
under restrictive stoichiometric constraints.
Here, we have shown that
the combined action of time-scale and abundance separations
circumvents those constraints,
leading to the general emergence of open-CRN behavior independently of the reactions stoichiometry.

From a theoretical perspective,
our results provide a unified foundation
for the nonequilibrium thermodynamics of open CRNs:
they
clarify under which physical conditions
its predictions are quantitatively valid.
From an experimental perspective,
the two dynamical conditions identified here
translate into measurable requirements on reaction rates
as well as on species abundances,
thereby offering practical criteria for interpreting experiments
in fueled chemical systems.
For instance, such criteria can be directly applied to buffer solutions,
constituting a prototypical example of
\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{exc}}
reactions
as discussed in Sec.
X
.

We focused here on CRNs at the microscopic scale,
but our approach extends to CRNs at the macroscopic scale,
as the latter arise from a large-volume limit,
V\to\infty
, of the former
22
.
Specifically,
for our approach to hold, this limit must preserve the abundance separation,
which requires that
\Omega\to\infty
faster than
V\to\infty
.

Our results open several directions for future investigation,
of which we mention only two.
A first step would be to analyze the next-to-leading-order contributions
to the dynamics and thermodynamics of closed CRNs,
thereby quantifying the corrections arising from the finite abundances
of the species that effectively act as chemostats.
A second direction would be to relax the assumption of independent
\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{exc}}
reactions,
which may lead to emergent, time-dependent chemostatted concentrations.

By revealing how open-CRN behavior arises from closed CRNs,
our work shows that the nonequilibrium thermodynamics of open CRNs
is not merely an idealization,
but an emergent property of closed out-of-equilibrium systems.
It thereby provides a unified and physically grounded bridge between
idealized open-CRN descriptions
and real chemical systems.

Appendix B
Reservoirs

We identify the minimal conditions under which a system acts
as a thermal reservoir, i.e., a thermostat (App.
B.1
),
or as a molecule reservoir, i.e., a chemostat (App.
B.2
).

B.1
Thermostats

Consider an isolated supersystem composed of two (weakly interacting) systems,
labeled
{{A}}
and
{{B}}
,
with constant volumes.
The two systems exchange energy,
while the total energy of the supersystem
E\equiv E_{{A}}+E_{{B}}

remains conserved,
with
E_{{A}}
and
E_{{B}}
being the energies of system
{{A}}
and
{{B}}
,
respectively.
The two systems are closed and do not exchange molecules:
both
n_{{A}}
and
n_{{B}}
are conserved
with
n_{{A}}
and
n_{{B}}
being the numbers of molecules of system
{{A}}
and
{{B}}
,
respectively.
The system
{{B}}
acts as a thermostat if
it is always at equilibrium with a well defined temperature
T_{{B}}

which does not change upon energy exchanges with system
{{A}}
.
In thermodynamic terms, this requires that
the inverse heat capacity of system
{{B}}
vanishes,

\frac{1}{C_{T,{B}}}=\frac{\partial T_{{B}}}{\partial E_{{B}}}=0\,,

(99)

or, equivalently, that the heat capacity of system
{B}
diverges, i.e.,
C_{T,{B}}\to\infty
.

Under these conditions,
the probability
p(E_{{A}})
that
system
{A}
has energy
E_{{A}}
is given by the canonical distribution:

p(E_{{A}})\propto\exp(-E_{{A}}/k_{\mathrm{B}}\,T_{{B}})

(with
k_{\mathrm{B}}
being the Boltzmann constant).
Indeed,
according to the microcanonical distribution,

p(E_{{A}})\propto\exp(S_{{B}}(E-E_{{A}})/k_{\mathrm{B}})

where

S_{{B}}(E-E_{{A}})
is the entropy of system
{B}
when its energy is
E_{{B}}=E-E_{{A}}
,
reading

S_{{B}}(E-E_{{A}})=\int_{0}^{E-E_{{A}}}\frac{\mathrm{d}E_{{B}}}{T_{{B}}}=\frac{E-E_{{A}}}{T_{{B}}}\,.

(100)

Here,
the first equality results from the fundamental thermodynamic relation
for closed systems at constant volume,
while the second equality results from Eq. (
99
),
ensuring that
T_{{B}}
is independent of
E_{{B}}
.

Remark.
If system
{B}
were an ideal gas,
its energy would be given by
E_{{B}}=(f/2)k_{\mathrm{B}}n_{{B}}T_{{B}}

(with
f
being the number of degrees of freedom of a single molecule)
and the condition in Eq. (
99
) would specialize to

\frac{1}{C_{T,{B}}}=\frac{1}{(f/2)k_{\mathrm{B}}n_{{B}}}=0\,.

(101)

This physically means that system
{B}
must have
a diverging number of molecules, i.e.,
n_{{B}}\to\infty
,
for its temperature to be unaffected by the energy exchanges
and thus acts as a thermostat.

B.2
Chemostats

Consider an isolated supersystem composed of a single molecular species,
partitioned into two (weakly interacting) systems,
labeled
{{A}}
and
{{B}}
,
with constant volumes.
The two systems exchange energy,
while the total energy of the supersystem
E\equiv E_{{A}}+E_{{B}}

remains conserved,
with
E_{{A}}
and
E_{{B}}
being the energies of system
{{A}}
and
{{B}}
,
respectively.
The two systems exchange molecules too,
while the total number of molecules of the supersystem
n\equiv n_{{A}}+n_{{B}}

remains conserved,
with
n_{{A}}
and
n_{{B}}
being the numbers of molecules of system
{{A}}
and
{{B}}
,
respectively.
The system
{{B}}
acts as a chemostats
if, in addition to acting as a thermostat (see App.
B.1
),
it is always at equilibrium with a well defined chemical potential
\mu_{{B}}/T_{{B}}

which does not change upon exchanges of molecules with system
{{A}}
.
In thermodynamic terms, this requires that
what we name here inverse chemical capacity of system
{{B}}
vanishes,

\frac{1}{C_{\mu,{B}}}=\frac{\partial(\mu_{{B}}/T_{{B}})}{\partial n_{{B}}}=0\,,

(102)

or, equivalently, that the chemical capacity of system
{B}
diverges, i.e.,
C_{\mu,{B}}\to\infty
.

Under these conditions,
the probability
p(E_{{A}},n_{{A}})
that
system
{A}
has energy
E_{{A}}
and number of molecules
n_{{A}}

is given by the grandcanonical distribution:

p(E_{{A}},n_{{A}})\propto\exp(-(E_{{A}}-\mu_{{B}}n_{{A}})/k_{\mathrm{B}}\,T_{{B}})

(with
k_{\mathrm{B}}
being the Boltzmann constant).
Indeed,
according to the microcanonical distribution,

p(E_{{A}},n_{{A}})\propto\exp(S_{{B}}(E-E_{{A}},n-n_{{A}})/k_{\mathrm{B}})

where

S_{{B}}(E-E_{{A}},n-n_{{A}})
is the entropy of system
{B}
when
its energy is
E_{{B}}=E-E_{{A}}

and its number of molecules is
n_{{B}}=n-n_{{A}}
,
reading

\begin{split}S_{{B}}(E-E_{{A}},n-n_{{A}})&=\int_{0}^{E-E_{{A}}}\frac{\mathrm{d}E_{{B}}}{T_{{B}}}-\int_{0}^{n-n_{{A}}}\frac{\mu_{{B}}}{T_{{B}}}\mathrm{d}n_{{B}}\\
&=\frac{E-E_{{A}}}{T_{{B}}}-\frac{\mu_{{B}}}{T_{{B}}}(n-n_{{A}})\,.\end{split}

(103)

Here,
the first equality results from the fundamental thermodynamic relation
for systems at constant volume,
while the second equality results from Eq. (
99
) and Eq. (
102
),
ensuring that

T_{{B}}
is independent of
E_{{B}}

and
{\mu_{{B}}}/{T_{{B}}}
is independent of
n_{{B}}
,
respectively.

Remark.
If system
{B}
were an ideal system with a constant temperature,
its chemical potential would be given by Eq. (
12
)
and the condition in Eq. (
102
) specializes to

\frac{1}{C_{\mu,{B}}}=\frac{k_{\mathrm{B}}}{n_{{B}}}=0\,.

(104)

This physically means that system
{B}
must have
a diverging number of molecules, i.e.,
n_{{B}}\to\infty
,
for its chemical potential to be unaffected by the molecule exchanges
and thus acts as a chemostat.

Appendix C
Large Deviation Principle

We show here that a large deviation principle emerges from the abundance separation
and that, consequently,
the master equation (
36
) boils down to the master equation (
40
).
Specifically, we consider the limit
\Omega\to\infty

which corresponds to diverging total numbers of molecules of the
\mathcal{S}_{{y}}
and
\mathcal{S}_{{Y}}
species
(i.e.,
\bm{L}^{y}=\mathcal{O}(\Omega)
),
while the average numbers of molecules of the
\mathcal{S}_{{y}}
species remain finite
(i.e.,
\bm{L}^{y}\mathfrak{p}_{y}=\mathcal{O}(1)
).

We start by rewriting the master equation (
36
)
with the rates in Eq. (
38
)
plus contributions
\mathcal{O}(\Omega^{-1})

thus obtaining

\mathrm{d}_{T}p_{T}^{(0)}(\bm{L}^{x},\bm{L}^{y})=\sum_{\rho_{\mathrm{i}}}\big\{\big(\hat{R}_{\rho_{\mathrm{i}}}(\bm{L}^{x}-\mathbf{S}_{x,\rho_{\mathrm{i}}},\bm{L}^{y})+\mathcal{O}(\Omega^{-1})\big)\,p_{T}^{(0)}(\bm{L}^{x}-\mathbf{S}_{x,\rho_{\mathrm{i}}},\bm{L}^{y}-\mathbf{S}_{y,\rho_{\mathrm{i}}})-\big(\hat{R}_{\rho_{\mathrm{i}}}(\bm{L}^{x},\bm{L}^{y})+\mathcal{O}(\Omega^{-1})\big)\,p_{T}^{(0)}(\bm{L}^{x},\bm{L}^{y})\big\}\,.

(105)

We then write the probability
p_{T}^{(0)}(\bm{L}^{x},\bm{L}^{y})
as

(106)

and use the change of variables from
\bm{L}^{y}
to
\bm{\eta}^{y}

(with
\bm{\eta}^{y}=(\dots,\eta^{y},\dots)

and

as
\Omega\to\infty

to define

\small P^{(\Omega)}_{T}(\bm{\eta}^{y}|\bm{L}^{x})P^{(\Omega)}_{T}(\bm{L}^{x})\equiv\Omega^{N_{y}}p_{T}^{(0)}(\Omega\bm{\eta}^{y}|\bm{L}^{x})p_{T}^{(0)}(\bm{L}^{x})\,,

\Omega

\Omega

In the

we use the Wentzel–Kramers–Brillouin (WKB) ansatz
