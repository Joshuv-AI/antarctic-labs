Maintaining versus switching antiviral therapy in chronic hepatitis B patients with low-level viremia
Authors: Yongyong Wang, Lanqing Li, Chengrun Song, Jing Zhou, Yujing Li, Muzammal Sahar, Menglan Wang, Yachao Tao, Taoyou Zhou, Fang He, Jiajie Lu, Xuezhong Lei, Enqiang Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe incidence of low-level viremia (LLV) in chronic hepatitis B (CHB) patients receiving first-line nucleos(t)ide analogue (NAs) therapy with negative results on conventional HBV DNA testing is unknown.MethodsA total of 557 treatment-naïve CHB patients who had received first-line NAs therapy for more than 48 weeks and had negative results on conventional HBV DNA testing were enrolled. According to the results of two high-sensitivity HBV DNA tests, CHB patients were divided into two categories: maintained virological response (MVR) and LLV. Finally, LLV patients were divided into two groups: maintaining NAs therapy and switching NAs therapy.ResultsAmong the patients whose conventional HBV DNA testing was negative, the proportions of MVR and LLV were 64.1% and 35.9% respectively. Age and treatment duration are negatively correlated with the occurrence of LLV, while HBsAg level, HBeAg positivity, and ALT level were positively associated with the occurrence of LLV. At the 48th week of follow-up, the complete virological response (CVR) of the switching NAs group and the maintaining NAs group were 81.63% and 86.36% respectively (P = 0.536). In addition, there were no statistically significant differences between the two groups in the HBeAg-positive rate, HBsAg level, ALT level, APRI, and FIB-4 score over the 48-week follow-up period.ConclusionIn CHB patients who had negative results of conventional HBV DNA testing, the incidence rate of LLV is still high. For LLV patients with negative results in conventional HBV DNA testing, the vast majority of patients can achieve CVR whether maintaining or switching NAs, and the efficacy of the two treatment strategies is similar. However, these findings may not be applicable to patients with higher baseline HBV DNA levels, different distributions of NAs types, or those treated in other clinical settings.Keywords: Chronic hepatitis B, nucleos(t)ide analogues, Low-level viremia, Risk factor, Treatment strategy

Introduction

Chronic hepatitis B (CHB) is an infectious disease caused by hepatitis B virus (HBV) and a global public health issue.1 According to the Polaris Observatory Collaborators, the global prevalence of HBV infection in 2022 is 3.2%, corresponding to 257.5 million HBsAg positive people2. CHB can easily progress to liver fibrosis, cirrhosis, hepatocellular carcinoma, and liver failure without timely and effective treatment. The primary goals of treating CHB are to achieve long-term suppression of HBV replication, reduce hepatic inflammation and fibrosis, decrease the incidence of complications, improve patients' quality of life, and extend their survival time.3 Currently, the first-line antiviral drugs recommended by the guidelines are divided into two categories: nucleos(t)ide analogues (NAs) and interferon alpha (IFN-α). Despite achieving higher rates of HBeAg and HBsAg seroclearance, IFN-α is limited to short-term treatment in some advantageous populations due to its weak inhibitory effect on HBV DNA and significant side effects. NAs, including entecavir (ETV), tenofovir disoproxil fumarate (TDF) and tenofovir alafenamide fumarate (TAF), can strongly inhibit the replication of HBV DNA and has a high barrier of drug resistance, so it is the best choice for the treatment of CHB.4

First-line NAs can achieve maintained virological response (MVR) in most CHB patients, with serum HBV DNA below the lower limit of quantification (LLOQ). However, studies have shown that 20%∼40% of patients who receive long-term and regular first-line NAs treatment still have low-level viremia (LLV), which means that serum HBV DNA is continuously or intermittently greater than the LLOQ but less than 2000 IU/ml.5 In CHB patients treated with ETV monotherapy for 5 years, the LLV rates of HBeAg positive and negative were 6% and 5%, respectively.6 After 96 weeks of monotherapy with TDF and TAF, the HBV DNA positivity rates in HBeAg positive patients were 25% and 27%, respectively; In HBeAg negative patients, the HBV DNA positivity rates are 10% and 9%, respectively (with a LLOQ of 29 IU/ml).7 Long-term LLV may induce drug resistance,8 promote the progress of liver fibrosis9 and increase the risk of decompensated cirrhosis and liver cancer.10

In clinical practice, LLV screening mainly relies on the detection method of HBV DNA. The LLOQ ranges for conventional HBV DNA testing and high-sensitivity HBV DNA testing are 100∼1000 IU/ml and 10∼20 IU/ml, respectively.11 In China, high-sensitivity HBV DNA testing has not yet been carried out in many hospitals, and the evaluation of CHB treatment is still mainly based on conventional HBV DNA testing. In addition, the high cost of high-sensitivity HBV DNA testing also limits its widespread application. How to reasonably apply high-sensitivity HBV DNA testing in patients with long-term conventional HBV DNA testing below the LLOQ, and whether the treatment regimen for this part of LLV patients needs to be adjusted remains to be further studied. This study aimed to determine the prevalence of LLV in CHB patients with conventional HBV DNA testing below the LLOQ. By conducting high-sensitivity HBV DNA testing, we further analyzed the clinical characteristics and independent risk factors associated with LLV. In addition, we explored the differences in therapeutic outcomes between maintaining versus switching NAs therapy for patients with LLV.

Materials and methods

Patient selection

This was a single-center, retrospective cohort study. A total of 557 treatment-naïve CHB patients were enrolled in the hepatitis clinic of West China Hospital of Sichuan University from November 2020 to November 2023. Enrollment criteria were: (1) Receive monotherapy with ETV, TDF, and TAF for at least 48 weeks; (2) Conventional HBV DNA testing below LLOQ (LLOQ: 100 IU/ml); (3) Voluntary participation in high-sensitivity HBV DNA testing (LLOQ: 10 IU/ml). Exclusion criteria were: (1) Treatment with IFN-α or non-first-line antiviral drugs within 12 months before enrollment; (2) Combination therapy with other antiviral drugs such as NAs + NAs and NAs + IFN-α within 12 months before enrollment; (3) Combining drug-induced, hereditary, alcoholic, autoimmune, metabolic and other liver diseases; (4) History of liver tumors or other malignant tumors; (5) Accept immunosuppressive drugs, cytotoxic drugs, and radiotherapy and chemotherapy treatment; (6) Patients with poor compliance or self-withdrawal during follow-up. The design of this study was consistent with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and Good Clinical Practice Guidelines and was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the West China Hospital of Sichuan University.

Study design and definition

The data on patient characteristics and outcomes comes from electronic medical records. This study did not involve the collection of human samples, but mainly the retrospective analysis of clinical data. The results of demographic characteristics, HBsAg, HBeAg, HBV DNA, blood routine and biochemical routine were collected before and after antiviral treatment. From CHB patients who had received first-line NAs monotherapy for over 48 weeks and had undetectable HBV DNA by conventional assays (LLOQ: 100 IU/ml), we further selected those individuals who had completed two high-sensitivity HBV DNA tests (LLOQ: 10 IU/ml) with an interval of at least 3 months. According to the results of two high-sensitivity HBV DNA tests, CHB patients were divided into two categories: MVR and LLV. MVR is defined as two high-sensitivity HBV DNA tests both below LLOQ. LLV is defined as sustained or intermittent detectable HBV DNA (10 ≤ HBV DNA<2000IU/ml) in two high-sensitivity HBV DNA tests. Among them, two positive HBV DNA tests are defined as persistent LLV (PLLV), and a single positive test is defined as non persistent LLV (NPLLV). LLV patients who were not lost to follow-up were divided into two groups according to the treatment regimen: maintaining NAs therapy and switching NAs therapy (ETV switch to TDF/TAF, TDF/TAF switch to ETV).

Laboratory testing

Serum biochemical indexes were measured by standard procedures (Olympus AU5400, Olympus Corporation, Tokyo, Japan). Conventional HBV DNA testing uses Cobas TaqMan assay reagent (Roche Diagnostics, Branchburg, USA) to measure serum HBV DNA, with a LLOQ of 100 IU/ml. High-sensitivity HBV DNA was detected by Abbott m2000 Real Time system, the reagent was from Abbott Trading (Shanghai) Co., Ltd., with a LLOQ of 10 IU/ml. Serum HBeAg status was assessed using electrochemiluminescence immunoassay (Roche Diagnostics, Indianapolis, USA). Serum HBsAg levels were quantitatively measured using an Elecsys R HBsAg II Quant Assay (Roche Diagnostics, Penzberg, Germany) with a LLOQ of 0.05 IU/ml.

Outcomes

The main purpose of this study is to evaluate the incidence of LLV and analyze the risk factors for LLV by conducting high-sensitivity HBV DNA testing on CHB patients with first-line NAs antiviral therapy for more than 48 weeks and conventional HBV DNA testing negative. Finally, LLV patients were divided into two groups: maintaining NAs therapy and switching NAs therapy, and followed up for 48 weeks. The primary outcome is the complete virological response (CVR, HBV DNA <10 IU/ml) rate of LLV patients after 48 weeks of treatment. The secondary outcomes include changes in serum HBsAg levels, HBeAg seroconversion rate, ALT normalization rate, aspartate aminotransferase to platelet ratio index (APRI) and fibrosis-4 index (FIB-4) scores.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were reported as the mean ± SD or median (P25-P75) and categorical variables were reported as absolute and relative frequencies. Continuous variables can be analyzed appropriately using t-tests, analysis of variance, or non-parametric tests. Categorical variables can be compared using chi square test or Fisher's exact test. Logistic regression analysis was used to assess the association between variables and study endpoints. The 95% confidence intervals (CI) were calculated for each predictive test. The receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC) were used to assess the predictive value of different risk factors for LLV. All statistical tests were two-sided and a p-value <0.05 was considered significantly different. Statistical analyses are performed using IBM SPSS version 26.0 and GraphPad Prism 8.0 software.

Results

Incidence of LLV in patients with negative conventional HBV DNA testing

From November 2020 to November 2023, 557 CHB patients who received first-line NAs monotherapy for more than 48 weeks and tested negative for conventional HBV DNA were collected. 103 patients who did not meet the inclusion criteria were excluded through electronic medical records and telephone follow-up (Fig. 1). The proportion of patients with different HBV DNA levels was counted among 454 patients who completed the first high-sensitivity HBV DNA test. The proportion of patients with HBV DNA undetectable, HBV DNA detectable but <10, 10 ≤ HBV DNA <20 and 20 ≤ HBV DNA <2000IU/ml was 55.1% (250/454), 16.7% (76/454), 14.1% (64/454) and 14.1% (64/454), respectively (Fig. 2A). Of the 454 patients, 410 completed the second high-sensitivity HBV DNA test at an interval of more than 3 months. The proportion of patients with HBV DNA undetectable, HBV DNA detectable but <10, 10 ≤ HBV DNA <20 and 20 ≤ HBV DNA <2000IU/ml was 75.4% (309/410), 7.6% (31/410), 9.3% (38/410) and 7.8% (32/410), respectively (Fig. 2B). The results of two high-sensitivity HBV DNA tests in 410 patients were statistically analyzed. The green area shows the number of MVR patients, while the yellow area shows LLV patients (Fig. 2C). Therefore, among the patients whose conventional HBV DNA test was negative after 48 weeks of first-line NAs treatment, the proportions of MVR and LLV were 64.1% (263/410) and 35.9% (147/410) respectively (Fig. 2D). Among LLV patients, PLLV and NPLLV accounted for 29.9% (44/147) and 70.1% (103/147) respectively (Fig. 2E).

Analysis of risk factors of LLV according to its clinical characteristics at diagnosis

The 410 patients who completed two high-sensitivity HBV DNA tests were included in the subsequent statistical analysis. A total of 383 patients had complete clinical data at the time of diagnosis, including 247 in the MVR and 136 in the LLV. Among LLV patients, PLLV and NPLLV had 39 and 97 patients, respectively (Table 1). The 383 patients were predominantly young and middle-aged, with a median age of 44 years, 208 males (54.31%), and a body mass index (BMI) of 22.70 ± 2.96 kg/m. 222 There were 48.30% (185/383), 22.45% (86/383) and 29.24% (112/383) patients treated with ETV, TDF and TAF, respectively, and the median antiviral duration was 37 months. The median level of HBsAg was 2.89 log10 IU/ml, and the proportion of HBeAg positive patients was 25.9%. Subgroup analysis of patient clinical data was performed for MVR, PLLV, and NPLLV. There was no significant difference in gender composition, family history, fatty liver, PLT count and abdominal imaging among the three groups (P > 0.05). There were significant differences in age, antiviral duration, antiviral regimen, HBsAg level, HBeAg positive rate, ALT level, AST level and APRI index among the three groups (P < 0.05).

Analysis of risk factors of LLV according to the clinical characteristics of patients before antiviral treatment

In order to further explore whether the clinical characteristics of patients before antiviral therapy can predict the occurrence of LLV, the clinical data of 410 patients before antiviral therapy were collected, 77 patients with incomplete data were excluded, and the data of 333 patients were finally included for analysis, including 218 patients with MVR and 115 patients with LLV (Table 3). There were statistically significant differences in age, fatty liver, HBsAg, HBV DNA, and HBeAg positivity rates between MVR and LLV patients before antiviral treatment (P < 0.05). Univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses were used to identify risk predictors for LLV. In univariate logistic regression analysis, variables such as age, fatty liver, HBsAg, HBV DNA, and HBeAg positivity rate have predictive value (P < 0.05). In multivariate logistic regression analysis, age was negatively correlated with the occurrence of LLV, while HBsAg level, HBV DNA and HBeAg positivity were positively correlated with the occurrence of LLV (Table 4). To further evaluate the value of age, HBsAg, and HBV DNA in predicting LLV, the area under the AUROC curve for each parameter was calculated (Fig. 6). The AUROC values for age, HBsAg, and HBV DNA were 0.691 (95% CI:0.631-0.750, P < 0.001), 0.748 (95% CI:0.696-0.801, P < 0.001), and 0.669 (95% CI:0.609-0.728, P < 0.001), respectively.

Analyzing the influence of maintaining and switching NAs on LLV

Of the 136 patients with LLV, 43 were subsequently lost to follow-up. Among the 93 LLV patients with complete follow-up data, 44 maintained NAs therapy and 49 switched NAs therapy (Table 5). In the switching NAs group, the median age was 40 years, 65.31% of them were male, the median level of HBsAg was 2.98 ± 0.66 log10 IU/ml, the median level of HBV DNA was 12 IU/ml, and the positive rate of HBeAg was 42.86%. Correspondingly, in the maintaining NAs group, the median age was 38.5 years, the proportion of males was 63.64%, the median level of HBsAg was 3.14 ± 0.71 log10 IU/ml, the median level of HBV DNA was 10.5 IU/ml, and the positive rate of HBeAg was 36.36%. There were no significant differences in clinical characteristics between the two groups (P > 0.05).

Discussion

HBV DNA is the most direct indicator of HBV infection, and undetectable HBV DNA is the standard to measure the efficacy of anti-HBV drugs. With the innovation of HBV DNA detection methods, the problem of LLV in CHB patients has become increasingly prominent. However, there are still many unresolved issues in the management of LLV.12 LLV means that HBV DNA is higher than the LLOQ but less than 2000IU/ml after antiviral treatment for more than 48 weeks. The level of LLOQ is the key factor to determine the detection rate of LLV. In China, regular follow-up examinations for CHB patients mainly rely on conventional HBV DNA testing (LLOQ: 100 IU/ml).13 However, due to the high cost of testing, high-sensitivity HBV DNA testing is not routinely conducted (LLOQ: 10-20 IU/ml). Therefore, there may be a specific subset of LLV patients who are negative for routine HBV DNA testing but positive for high-sensitivity HBV DNA testing. This study reported the incidence, risk factors, and treatment strategies of LLV in CHB patients who received long-term first-line NAs antiviral therapy and were conventional HBV DNA negative.

This study showed that among the patients whose conventional HBV DNA test was negative after 48 weeks of first-line NAs treatment, the proportions of MVR and LLV were 64.1% and 35.9% respectively. In the first high-sensitivity HBV DNA test, the proportion of LLV was 28.2%, and the proportion of patients with 10 ≤ HBV DNA <20 and 20 ≤ HBV DNA <2000 IU/ml was 14.1% and 14.1%, respectively. Accordingly, in the second high-sensitivity HBV DNA test, the proportion of LLV was 17.1%, and the proportion of patients with 10 ≤ HBV DNA <20 and 20 ≤ HBV DNA <2000 IU/ml was 9.3% and 7.8%, respectively. Therefore, among the patients with negative results of conventional HBV DNA test, there are still many LLV patients screened out by high-sensitivity HBV DNA test, and the proportion of LLV patients with 10 ≤ HBV DNA <20 IU/ml is high. Similarly, previous studies reported that very low-level viremia (20 ≤ HBV DNA <50 IU/ml) accounted for 52.6% of LLV patients.14 In a retrospective study, the median HBV DNA level of LLV patients screened by high-sensitivity HBV DNA testing was 67.8 IU/ml.15 In LLV patients, most of them have low levels of HBV DNA, which cannot be detected by conventional HBV DNA testing (LLOQ: 100 IU/ml). For CHB patients, it is necessary to carry out high-sensitivity HBV DNA testing in patients with negative results of conventional HBV DNA testing.

In this study, LLV was defined as continuously or intermittently detectable HBV DNA in two high-sensitivity HBV DNA assays, and LLV was classified into PLLV and NPLLV. Among LLV patients, PLLV and NPLLV accounted for 29.9% and 70.1% respectively. In a retrospective cohort study with a median follow-up of 4.5 years, persistent LLV and intermittent LLV accounted for 6.6% and 93.4% of total LLV patients, respectively.10 In another longitudinal retrospective study, LLV was also defined by persistent or intermittent detectable HBV DNA during follow-up. The proportions of MVR and LLV were 73.2% and 26.8%, respectively.16 Correspondingly, a high proportion of LLV patients have intermittent HBV DNA levels higher than LLOQ, and regular high-sensitivity HBV DNA testing will help screen out these patients.

Early identification of people at high risk for LLV can help clinicians to manage these patients comprehensively and may reduce the incidence of LLV to some extent. In this study, we compared the population and clinical characteristics of LLV and MVR, and analyzed the influencing factors of LLV occurrence. A logistic regression analysis based on the data at the time of diagnosis of LLV showed that age and duration of treatment were negatively correlated with the occurrence of LLV, while HBsAg level, HBeAg positivity, and ALT level were positively correlated with the occurrence of LLV. Analysis of the clinical characteristics of LLV patients before antiviral therapy showed that age was negatively correlated with the occurrence of LLV, while HBsAg level, HBV DNA and HBeAg positive were positively correlated with the occurrence of LLV. Therefore, CHB patients who meet one or more of the following conditions should be regularly screened for high-sensitivity HBV DNA testing, such as high HBV DNA load before antiviral therapy, high HBsAg level, HBeAg positive, and short course of antiviral therapy. Similarly, Li et al. found that HBeAg positivity, HBV DNA ≥6 Log10 IU/m1, and HBsAg ≥9000 IU/ml before antiviral treatment were associated with the occurrence of LLV.17 In a real-world study, the results show that age, baseline HBV DNA level, HBsAg level and HBeAg status are independently related to the occurrence of LLV.18 In addition, another single-center retrospective study also found that HBeAg positivity and high HBsAg levels were risk factors for LLV.19 Han et al. found that long antiviral course is a protective factor, while HBeAg positive and ETV treatment are risk factors for LLV.20 In this study, the clinical characteristics of patients before antiviral treatment and at the time of diagnosis of LLV were analyzed, which can screen out the risk factors of LLV more comprehensively. CHB patients with persistently high HBsAg levels, HBeAg positivity, and fluctuating ALT after receiving antiviral therapy may be a potentially high-risk population for LLV.

Currently, the latest guidelines in China suggest that for CHB patients receiving first-line NAs treatment for 48 weeks, high-sensitivity HBV DNA testing should be used for HBV DNA detection. First of all, the influence of poor compliance and test error is excluded. If LLV is confirmed, the treatment plan can be adjusted, such as switching to TDF or TAF for ETV patients, switching to ETV for TDF or TAF patients, or combining the two drugs, or combining IFN-α. In fact, this study focuses on a special type of LLV patients, who have negative results in conventional HBV DNA testing but positive results in high-sensitivity HBV DNA testing, and most of whom have HBV DNA levels below 100 IU/ml. For this special type of LLV patients, whether NAs needs to be adjusted is also worthy of further study. Interestingly, this study retrospectively analyzed the virologic response to switching NAs and maintaining NAs in patients with LLV and found no significant difference in CVR (HBV DNA<10 IU/ml) rates between the two groups after 48 weeks of treatment. Indeed, most studies support adjusting the NAs regimen for LLV patients.21,22 This difference may be due to the fact that this study focused on a specific type of LLV patients. Therefore, switching NAs regimens is not mandatory for LLV patients receiving first-line NAs therapy with negative results on conventional HBV DNA testing. Notably, the definition of LLV varies across studies, with some studies using an upper limit of 200 IU/ml and others using 2000 IU/ml. This difference may influence the reported prevalence and the strength of associations with clinical outcomes. In our cohort, a substantial proportion of LLV patients had HBV DNA levels below 200 IU/ml, and future studies should consider stratifying LLV into subcategories (e.g., 10-199 IU/ml vs. 200-1999 IU/ml) to better understand the clinical implications of different viremia levels. Therefore, we should be cautious when comparing the results of this study directly with other studies, especially when the definition of LLV is different.

Our observation that CVR rates at week 48 were similar between maintaining and switching NAs therapy is in contrast to several previous real-world studies that reported superior efficacy of switching regimens in LLV patients. We believe this discrepancy may be explained by several key factors. First, our study focused on a highly specific LLV population: patients who had persistently negative results on conventional HBV DNA testing (LLOQ 100 IU/ml) but were found to have detectable HBV DNA only by high-sensitivity testing (LLOQ 10 IU/ml). The majority of these patients had extremely low HBV DNA levels, with 14.1% in the 10-20 IU/ml range and another 14.1% in the 20-2000 IU/ml range at the first high-sensitivity test. In contrast, many previous studies enrolled LLV patients with HBV DNA levels up to 2000 IU/ml without requiring prior conventional negativity, and some included patients with HBV DNA >20 IU/ml or >200 IU/ml as LLV. The very low baseline viremia level in our cohort may have made it easier for both the maintaining and switching groups to achieve CVR, thereby narrowing any potential difference between the two strategies. Second, our follow-up duration was 48 weeks, which may be insufficient to capture differential response rates that could emerge later; some studies reported that switching regimens led to higher cumulative CVR only after 96 weeks or longer. Third, the definition of CVR in our study was HBV DNA <10 IU/ml, a stricter criterion than used in some previous reports (e.g., <20 IU/ml or <200 IU/ml), which could also affect comparability. Finally, we acknowledge that our sample size was relatively small, and the non-significant result could be due to type II error rather than true equivalence. Therefore, our findings suggest that for the specific subgroup of LLV patients with conventional HBV DNA negativity and extremely low viremia levels, maintaining the current NAs may still be a reasonable option, while switching remains an alternative.

This study has some limitations. First, selection bias may have occurred because enrolled patients were those who voluntarily underwent high-sensitivity HBV DNA testing. Second, missing data are inherent to retrospective studies. Although we excluded cases with incomplete data from certain analyses, this may have introduced bias. Third, we did not systematically assess patient adherence using validated quantitative measures; although patients with self-reported poor compliance were excluded, intermittent non-adherence cannot be ruled out. Fourth, genotypic resistance testing was not performed, so we cannot determine whether LLV in some patients was attributable to drug-resistant mutations rather than partial virological response. Fifth, the comparison of CVR between the maintaining and switching groups should be interpreted with caution, as the sample size provides insufficient statistical power to establish equivalence. Larger prospective studies are needed to definitively compare these two management approaches in LLV patients. Sixth, the follow-up duration for treatment outcomes was only 48 weeks, which is relatively short for evaluating long-term efficacy. Longer follow-up studies are therefore needed to determine whether the two strategies differ with respect to sustained viral suppression and reduction in liver-related events. Finally, as a single-center study conducted at a hospital in China, our findings may not be generalizable to other healthcare settings with different patient demographics, NAs distribution, monitoring protocols, or adherence patterns.

Conclusions

In CHB patients who received first-line NAs treatment for more than 48 weeks and had negative results of conventional HBV DNA testing, the incidence rate of LLV is still high. For these LLV patients, most achieved CVR whether maintaining or switching NAs, and the two strategies showed similar efficacy in this specific population. However, given the inherent biases of retrospective data, these results should be interpreted with caution. Future prospective, multicenter studies with rigorous adherence assessment and genotypic resistance monitoring are essential to confirm whether maintaining and switching NAs are truly equivalent in managing LLV.
