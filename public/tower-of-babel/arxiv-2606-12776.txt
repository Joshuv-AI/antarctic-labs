An AI Proof of 18-Variable Undecidability for Diophantine Equations over ℤ ⁡ [ i ] \mathbb{Z}[i]
Yuchen Ding, Junfeng Li

Abstract
This paper presents an AI proof that there is no algorithm deciding whether a polynomial equation over the Gaussian integers in 18 18 unknowns has a solution. The proof improves the 20 20 -unknown theorem of Matiyasevich and Sun. It follows their rationality criterion and integer test, but saves two variables: the clearing-denominator variable is avoided by imposing two integer conditions, and the remaining nonzero condition is absorbed into the relation-combining lemma by the one-variable gadget ( 2 ​ R + 1 ) ​ ( 3 ​ R + 1 ) (2R+1)(3R+1) .

Introduction

Hilbert’s Tenth Problem over a commutative ring asks for an algorithm deciding whether a polynomial equation over that ring has a solution. Over
\mathbb{Z}
the answer is negative, by the theorem of Matiyasevich, Davis, Putnam and Robinson; see
[
1
,
2
,
3
]
. A natural refinement asks how many unknowns are needed for undecidability. We shall use a fixed-variable form over
\mathbb{Z}
due to Sun
[
5
]
.
Let
\mathbb{Z}[i]
denote the ring of Gaussian integers. Matiyasevich and Sun proved that undecidability over
\mathbb{Z}[i]
holds for equations with
20
unknowns
[
4
]
. Their proof starts from Sun’s fixed-variable theorem, uses a Diophantine test for rational integers inside
\mathbb{Z}[i]
, and then applies a rationality criterion over
\mathbb{Q}(i)
.

The AI proof keeps the same framework, but makes two small changes. For variables
Z_{1},\ldots,Z_{10}
put

\mathcal{Y}=2\prod_{k=1}^{10}(3Z_{k}+1).

Instead of adding a new variable to clear denominators, we impose

\mathcal{Y}^{10}\in\mathbb{Z},\qquad\mathcal{Y}^{11}+\sum_{k=1}^{10}Z_{k}\mathcal{Y}^{10-k}\in\mathbb{Z}.

Since
\mathcal{Y}\neq 0
, these conditions give

\mathcal{Y}+\sum_{k=1}^{10}\frac{Z_{k}}{\mathcal{Y}^{k}}\in\mathbb{Q},

and the rationality criterion of
[
4
]
then forces all
Z_{k}
to be rational integers.

The second change concerns the remaining nonzero condition. We use

Q(R)=(2R+1)(3R+1).

This polynomial never vanishes on
\mathbb{Z}[i]
, and for every nonzero rational integer
n
there is an integer
R
with
n\mid Q(R)
. Thus the nonzero condition for
Z_{10}V_{0}V_{1}
can be put into the relation-combining lemma without introducing the extra variable used in the earlier construction.

The variables are therefore

Z_{1},\ldots,Z_{10},\ M,R,\ V_{0},X_{0},Y_{0},\ V_{1},X_{1},Y_{1},

namely
10+1+1+3+3=18
variables. The proof gives the following theorem.

Throughout the paper

\mathbb{N}=\{0,1,2,\ldots\},

and
\mathbb{Z}
and
\mathbb{Q}
are regarded as subrings of
\mathbb{Z}[i]
and
\mathbb{Q}(i)
in the usual way.

Theorem 1
.

There is no algorithm to decide, for an arbitrary polynomial

F(X_{1},\ldots,X_{18})\in\mathbb{Z}[X_{1},\ldots,X_{18}],

whether the equation

F(X_{1},\ldots,X_{18})=0

has a solution in
\mathbb{Z}[i]^{18}
.

2
Auxiliary facts

Let

\Box=\{\xi^{2}:\xi\in\mathbb{Z}[i]\}.

We shall use the following facts from
[
4
]
.

Lemma 1

(conjunction over
\mathbb{Z}[i]
)
.

This is
[
4
, Lemma 3.2]
. For any
X,Y\in\mathbb{Z}[i]
,

X=0\ \wedge\ Y=0\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad X^{2}+2Y^{2}=0.

Consequently, any finite system of polynomial equations over
\mathbb{Z}[i]
can be combined into a single polynomial equation, without introducing new variables. In particular, if the original equations have coefficients in
\mathbb{Z}
, then the combined equation also has coefficients in
\mathbb{Z}
.

Lemma 2

(combining two square conditions and one divisibility condition)
.

This is
[
4
, Lemma 3.1]
. Let
A_{1},A_{2},S,T\in\mathbb{Z}[i]
with
A_{1}\neq A_{2}
and
S\neq 0
. Define

f(A_{1},A_{2},S,T,M)=(T-MS)^{4}-2(A_{1}+A_{2})S^{2}(T-MS)^{2}+(A_{1}-A_{2})^{2}S^{4}.

Then

A_{1}\in\Box\ \wedge\ A_{2}\in\Box\ \wedge\ S\mid T

if and only if there exists
M\in\mathbb{Z}[i]
such that

f(A_{1},A_{2},S,T,M)=0.

Lemma 3

(integer test in
\mathbb{Z}[i]
)
.

This is
[
4
, Lemma 3.3]
. For
U\in\mathbb{Z}[i]
, we have
U\in\mathbb{Z}
if and only if there exist
V,X,Y\in\mathbb{Z}[i]
with
V\neq 0
such that

4\bigl(2V(2(2U+1)^{2}+1)-Y\bigr)^{2}-3Y^{2}-1=0

(1)

and

3Y^{2}(2U+1-XY)^{2}+1\in\Box.

(2)

Moreover, if
U\in\mathbb{Z}
, then
V,X,Y
may be chosen in
\mathbb{Z}
.

Lemma 4

(rationality criterion)
.

This is
[
4
, Theorem 1.2]
in the case
K=\mathbb{Q}(i)
. Let
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\in\mathbb{Z}[i]
and put

y=2\prod_{k=1}^{n}(3x_{k}+1).

Then

y+\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{x_{k}}{y^{k}}\in\mathbb{Q}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\in\mathbb{Z}.

We also use Sun’s fixed-variable form of Matiyasevich’s theorem over
\mathbb{Z}
: there exists a recursively enumerable but nonrecursive set
\mathcal{A}\subseteq\mathbb{N}
and a polynomial

P(Z_{0},Z_{1},\ldots,Z_{10})\in\mathbb{Z}[Z_{0},Z_{1},\ldots,Z_{10}]

such that, for every
a\in\mathbb{N}
,

a\in\mathcal{A}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\exists z_{1},\ldots,z_{10}\in\mathbb{Z}\quad\bigl(P(a,z_{1},\ldots,z_{10})=0\ \wedge\ z_{10}\neq 0\bigr).

(3)

This is the precise fixed-variable input used in
[
4
, proof of Theorem 1.1]
, based on
[
5
, Theorem 1.1(ii)]
.

We need one elementary observation which replaces the two-variable nonzero encoding used in the 19-variable version.

Lemma 5

(one-variable nonzero-divisibility gadget)
.

For
R\in\mathbb{Z}[i]
, put

Q(R)=(2R+1)(3R+1).

Then
Q(R)\neq 0
for every
R\in\mathbb{Z}[i]
. Moreover, for every nonzero integer
n\in\mathbb{Z}
, there exists
R\in\mathbb{Z}
such that

n\mid Q(R)

in
\mathbb{Z}
, and hence also in
\mathbb{Z}[i]
.

Proof.

If
2R+1=0
for some
R\in\mathbb{Z}[i]
, then comparing real parts gives an integer solution of
2a+1=0
, which is impossible. Similarly,
3R+1=0
has no solution in
\mathbb{Z}[i]
. Hence
Q(R)\neq 0
for all
R\in\mathbb{Z}[i]
.

Let
n\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}
and put
m=|n|
. If
m=1
, any
R\in\mathbb{Z}
works. Suppose
m>1
, and write

m=2^{a}3^{b}\prod_{\ell\in\mathcal{P}}\ell^{e_{\ell}},

where every
\ell\in\mathcal{P}
is a prime distinct from
2
and
3
. Choose congruences as follows:

3R+1\equiv 0\pmod{2^{a}}\quad(a>0),

2R+1\equiv 0\pmod{3^{b}}\quad(b>0),

and, for each
\ell\in\mathcal{P}
,

2R+1\equiv 0\pmod{\ell^{e_{\ell}}}.

Each displayed congruence is solvable because the relevant coefficient is invertible modulo the indicated prime power. The moduli with exponent
0
are omitted. The remaining moduli are pairwise coprime, so the Chinese remainder theorem gives an integer
R
satisfying all of them simultaneously. Then every prime-power divisor of
m
divides one of the two factors
2R+1
or
3R+1
, and hence
m\mid Q(R)
. Thus
n\mid Q(R)
.
∎

3
Proof of the main theorem

Proof of Theorem
1
.

Fix the recursively enumerable but nonrecursive set
\mathcal{A}\subseteq\mathbb{N}
and the polynomial
P
in (
3
). We shall show that membership in
\mathcal{A}
can be represented by one polynomial equation over
\mathbb{Z}[i]
in
18
unknowns. This will imply the theorem, since a decision algorithm for solvability of arbitrary
18
-variable equations over
\mathbb{Z}[i]
would decide membership in
\mathcal{A}
.

Fix
a\in\mathbb{N}
. For variables
Z_{1},\ldots,Z_{10}\in\mathbb{Z}[i]
, put

\mathcal{Y}=2\prod_{k=1}^{10}(3Z_{k}+1).

(4)

Since
3Z_{k}+1\neq 0
for every
Z_{k}\in\mathbb{Z}[i]
(writing
Z_{k}=a+bi
would otherwise give
3b=0
and
3a+1=0
), we have
\mathcal{Y}\neq 0
. Define

T=\sum_{k=1}^{10}Z_{k}\mathcal{Y}^{10-k},\qquad W=\mathcal{Y}^{11}+T.

(5)

Then

\frac{W}{\mathcal{Y}^{10}}=\mathcal{Y}+\sum_{k=1}^{10}\frac{Z_{k}}{\mathcal{Y}^{k}}.

(6)

The key point is that we do not introduce an auxiliary integer multiplier. Instead, we force the two quantities

\mathcal{Y}^{10}\quad\text{and}\quad W

to be rational integers. If this happens, then (
6
) belongs to
\mathbb{Q}
, because the denominator
\mathcal{Y}^{10}
is a nonzero rational integer; Lemma
4
then gives

Z_{1},\ldots,Z_{10}\in\mathbb{Z}.

We now express the two integer conditions by Lemma
3
. Let

U_{0}=\mathcal{Y}^{10},\qquad U_{1}=W.

For
j=0,1
, introduce variables
V_{j},X_{j},Y_{j}\in\mathbb{Z}[i]
and set

E_{j}=4\bigl(2V_{j}(2(2U_{j}+1)^{2}+1)-Y_{j}\bigr)^{2}-3Y_{j}^{2}-1,

(7)

and

B_{j}=3Y_{j}^{2}(2U_{j}+1-X_{j}Y_{j})^{2}+1.

(8)

Thus, by Lemma
3
, the condition
U_{j}\in\mathbb{Z}
is equivalent to

\exists V_{j},X_{j},Y_{j}\in\mathbb{Z}[i]\quad\bigl(V_{j}\neq 0\ \wedge\ E_{j}=0\ \wedge\ B_{j}\in\Box\bigr).

The nonzero conditions on
V_{0}
and
V_{1}
will be enforced below through the single product
Z_{10}V_{0}V_{1}
, rather than by separate equations.

We shall combine the two square conditions and the nonzero condition using Lemma
2
. Define

C_{0}=9B_{0},\qquad C_{1}=B_{1}.

(9)

Then, in the quotient
\mathbb{Z}[i]/(3)
,

C_{0}\equiv 0\pmod{3},\qquad C_{1}\equiv 1\pmod{3},

because
B_{1}=3Y_{1}^{2}(2U_{1}+1-X_{1}Y_{1})^{2}+1
. Since the rational prime
3
is a Gaussian prime,
\mathbb{Z}[i]/(3)
is an integral domain and in particular
1\neq 0
there. Hence
C_{0}\neq C_{1}
in
\mathbb{Z}[i]
.
Moreover,

C_{0}\in\Box\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad B_{0}\in\Box.

(10)

Indeed, if
B_{0}=\beta^{2}
, then
C_{0}=(3\beta)^{2}
. Conversely, if
C_{0}=\eta^{2}
for some
\eta\in\mathbb{Z}[i]
, then

\eta^{2}=9B_{0}.

The rational prime
3
is a Gaussian prime, since
3\equiv 3\pmod{4}
. Hence
3\mid\eta
, say
\eta=3\eta^{\prime}
. Substituting gives

9(\eta^{\prime})^{2}=9B_{0}.

Since
9\neq 0
and
\mathbb{Z}[i]
is an integral domain, cancellation gives
B_{0}=(\eta^{\prime})^{2}\in\Box
.

Put

N=Z_{10}V_{0}V_{1},\qquad Q(R)=(2R+1)(3R+1).

The already proved inequality
C_{0}\neq C_{1}
supplies the hypothesis
A_{1}\neq A_{2}
in Lemma
2
. Instead of imposing a separate nonzero equation, we impose the single relation-combining equation

f(C_{0},C_{1},N,Q(R),M)=0.

(11)

This equation will force
N\neq 0
automatically. Indeed, if
N=0
, then by the definition of
f
we get

f(C_{0},C_{1},0,Q(R),M)=Q(R)^{4}.

By Lemma
5
,
Q(R)\neq 0
, and since
\mathbb{Z}[i]
is an integral domain,
Q(R)^{4}\neq 0
. Hence (
11
) cannot hold with
N=0
.

We claim that, for the fixed parameter
a\in\mathbb{N}
, the following finite system of equations in the
18
unknowns

Z_{1},\ldots,Z_{10},\ M,R,\ V_{0},X_{0},Y_{0},\ V_{1},X_{1},Y_{1}

has a solution over
\mathbb{Z}[i]
if and only if
a\in\mathcal{A}
:

\displaystyle P(a,Z_{1},\ldots,Z_{10})

\displaystyle=0,

(12)

\displaystyle E_{0}

\displaystyle=0,

(13)

\displaystyle E_{1}

\displaystyle=0,

(14)

\displaystyle f(C_{0},C_{1},Z_{10}V_{0}V_{1},Q(R),M)

\displaystyle=0.

(15)

Here
\mathcal{Y},T,W,U_{j},E_{j},B_{j},C_{j}
are the polynomials defined in (
4
)–(
9
).

First suppose that
a\in\mathcal{A}
. By (
3
), choose
z_{1},\ldots,z_{10}\in\mathbb{Z}
such that

P(a,z_{1},\ldots,z_{10})=0,\qquad z_{10}\neq 0.

Then
\mathcal{Y}\in\mathbb{Z}
and
W\in\mathbb{Z}
, hence
U_{0}=\mathcal{Y}^{10}\in\mathbb{Z}
and
U_{1}=W\in\mathbb{Z}
. By the last clause of Lemma
3
, for
j=0,1
we may choose
V_{j},X_{j},Y_{j}\in\mathbb{Z}
with
V_{j}\neq 0
such that
E_{j}=0
and
B_{j}\in\Box
. Then
C_{0},C_{1}\in\Box
. Moreover, since
\mathbb{Z}[i]
is an integral domain and
z_{10},V_{0},V_{1}
are all nonzero,

N=z_{10}V_{0}V_{1}\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}.

By Lemma
5
, choose
R\in\mathbb{Z}
such that
N\mid Q(R)
. The hypotheses of Lemma
2
now hold:
C_{0}\neq C_{1}
and
N\neq 0
. Applying the lemma with

A_{1}=C_{0},\quad A_{2}=C_{1},\quad S=N,\quad T=Q(R),

gives some
M\in\mathbb{Z}[i]
satisfying (
15
). Thus the system (
12
)–(
15
) has a solution over
\mathbb{Z}[i]
.

Conversely, suppose that the system (
12
)–(
15
) has a solution over
\mathbb{Z}[i]
. Put again

N=Z_{10}V_{0}V_{1}.

By the observation following (
11
), equation (
15
) implies
N\neq 0
. Since
\mathbb{Z}[i]
is an integral domain and
N=Z_{10}V_{0}V_{1}
, each factor is nonzero; hence

Z_{10}\neq 0,\qquad V_{0}\neq 0,\qquad V_{1}\neq 0.

Since
C_{0}\neq C_{1}
for every assignment and
N\neq 0
, Lemma
2
, applied to (
15
) with
S=N
and
T=Q(R)
, gives

C_{0}\in\Box,\qquad C_{1}\in\Box.

By (
10
),
B_{0}\in\Box
, and since
C_{1}=B_{1}
, also
B_{1}\in\Box
.
Together with
E_{0}=E_{1}=0
and
V_{0},V_{1}\neq 0
, Lemma
3
yields

U_{0}=\mathcal{Y}^{10}\in\mathbb{Z},\qquad U_{1}=W\in\mathbb{Z}.

As observed above,
\mathcal{Y}\neq 0
. Hence
\mathcal{Y}^{10}\neq 0
; since
\mathcal{Y}^{10}\in\mathbb{Z}
and
W\in\mathbb{Z}
, the quotient has nonzero rational-integer denominator. Therefore

\mathcal{Y}+\sum_{k=1}^{10}\frac{Z_{k}}{\mathcal{Y}^{k}}=\frac{W}{\mathcal{Y}^{10}}\in\mathbb{Q}.

By Lemma
4
, applied with
x_{k}=Z_{k}
, we conclude that

Z_{1},\ldots,Z_{10}\in\mathbb{Z}.

Now (
12
) and
Z_{10}\neq 0
show, via (
3
), that
a\in\mathcal{A}
.

Thus
a\in\mathcal{A}
if and only if the system (
12
)–(
15
) has a solution over
\mathbb{Z}[i]
in the displayed
18
unknowns. By Lemma
1
, this finite system can be replaced by a single polynomial equation

F_{a}(X_{1},\ldots,X_{18})=0

with coefficients in
\mathbb{Z}
, without adding variables. The coefficients remain in
\mathbb{Z}
because all displayed equations have coefficients in
\mathbb{Z}
and the operation
X^{2}+2Y^{2}
preserves this coefficient ring. The construction of
F_{a}
from
a
is effective: substitute the fixed parameter
a
into the fixed polynomial
P
, write the four displayed equations as polynomials in the
18
variables, and apply Lemma
1
finitely many times.

If there were an algorithm deciding solvability over
\mathbb{Z}[i]
for arbitrary polynomial equations in
18
unknowns with integer coefficients, then applying it to
F_{a}
would decide whether
a\in\mathcal{A}
. This contradicts the nonrecursiveness of
\mathcal{A}
. Hence no such algorithm exists.
∎

Remark 1
.

The first saving comes from replacing the original auxiliary integer multiplier
t
by the two integer conditions

\mathcal{Y}^{10}\in\mathbb{Z},\qquad\mathcal{Y}^{11}+\sum_{k=1}^{10}Z_{k}\mathcal{Y}^{10-k}\in\mathbb{Z}.

The second saving comes from replacing the two-variable nonzero equation by the one-variable polynomial
Q(R)=(2R+1)(3R+1)
inside the relation-combining lemma. Thus the argument avoids both the old variable
t
and one of the two variables previously used to force nonvanishing. Within this particular framework the variable count is

10+3+3+1+1=18,

coming respectively from Sun’s ten variables, the two three-variable integer tests, the combining variable
M
, and the one-variable gadget
R
. No absolute optimality statement is claimed here; lowering the count to
17
would require an additional improvement, such as a smaller integer test, a shared witness for the two integer conditions, a stronger fixed-variable input, or a different combining mechanism.
