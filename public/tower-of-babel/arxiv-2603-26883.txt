Rough sets by reflexive relations and their algebras Journal: International Journal of Approximate Reasoning
Jouni Järvinen, Sándor Radeleczki

Abstract
We consider various types of algebras defined on the completion DM(RS) of the partially ordered set of rough sets induced by a reflexive relation. We restrict ourselves to the cases in which the completion forms a spatial and completely distributive lattice. We derive the conditions under which DM(RS) is a regular pseudocomplemented Kleene algebra and a completely distributive double Stone algebra. Finally, we describe reflexive relations for which DM(RS) has the same properties as in the case of an equivalence relation: it forms a completely distributive and spatial regular double Stone algebra.

1
Introduction

Rough sets were introduced by Z. Pawlak in
[
22
]
.
In rough set theory our knowledge about the elements of a
universe
U
is given in terms of an equivalence relation
E
.
Two elements
x,y\in U
are
E
-related if they are indistinguishable with respect to the available information.
Each set
X\subseteq U
is approximated by two sets: the
lower approximation
X^{\blacktriangledown}
consists of elements that
certainly belong to
X
in view of the knowledge given by
E
, and the upper approximation
X^{\blacktriangle}
consists of elements
that are possibly in
X
.

The
rough set
of
X\subseteq U
is the pair

(X^{\blacktriangledown},X^{\blacktriangle})
. The set of all rough sets is denoted
by
\mathrm{RS}
. The set
\mathrm{RS}
can be canonically ordered
by the coordinatewise order:

(X^{\blacktriangledown},X^{\blacktriangle})\leq(Y^{\blacktriangledown},Y^{\blacktriangle})\iff X^{\blacktriangledown}\subseteq Y^{\blacktriangledown}\text{ and }X^{\blacktriangle}\subseteq Y^{\blacktriangle}.

J. Pomykala and J.A. Pomykala
[
23
]
proved that

\mathrm{RS}
is a complete lattice forming a Stone algebra.
This result was improved in
[
4
]
by S.D. Comer, who showed
that
\mathrm{RS}
forms a regular double Stone algebra.

The literature contains numerous studies in which information about objects
is given in terms of other types of relations generalising
equivalences. For instance, rough approximations defined by an arbitrary
binary relation were considered as early as
[
29
]
.

Rough sets defined by quasiorders (reflexive and transitive relations)
have been investigated by several authors; see

[
7
,
9
,
18
,
19
,
20
,
24
]

In this case the lattice
\mathrm{RS}

is known to be completely distributive and spatial; moreover, a so-called
Nelson algebra can be defined on it.

The lattice
\mathrm{RS}
is also completely
distributive and spatial whenever
R
is a tolerance (a reflexive and
symmetric relation) induced by an irredundant covering of
U
. Moreover, in
this case
\mathrm{RS}
also forms a regular pseudocomplemented Kleene algebra.
Notably, in both highlighted cases,
\mathrm{RS}
forms a regular double
Stone algebra if and only if
R
is an equivalence relation.

However, for an arbitrary tolerance relation, the ordered set

\mathrm{RS}
is not necessarily a lattice; see
[
16
]
.
This fact inhibited research in this direction for a long time.
In
[
27
]
, D. Umadevi presented the
Dedekind–MacNeille completion of
\mathrm{RS}
for arbitrary binary
relations, denoted here by
\mathrm{DM(RS)}
.
Her work provided a starting point for our study,
in which we have restricted ourselves to reflexive relations. This restriction
was motivated by the fact that reflexivity of
R
is equivalent to a natural
requirement for rough approximations: namely, that

X^{\blacktriangledown}\subseteq X\subseteq X^{\blacktriangle}
holds for each subset

X\subseteq U
.

Studying completions is important because the ordered set

\mathrm{RS}
is embedded
within its completion
\mathrm{DM(RS)}
. Consequently, the properties of the completion also characterize the structure of
\mathrm{RS}
.
In many
instances,
\mathrm{RS}
is already a complete lattice, meaning it coincides with its completion. By focusing on the completion, we simplify the analysis. We no longer need to verify the completeness of

\mathrm{RS}
itself, and
this allows for a broader perspective. We can study
\mathrm{DM(RS)}

under various assumptions such as
R
being reflexive, or the completion being completely distributive and spatial.

On the other hand, reflexivity is an indispensable feature of similarity, as
each object is inherently similar to itself. Reflexive relations can be
viewed as
directional similarity relations
, which are not necessarily symmetric.
For instance, A. Tversky states in
[
26
]
that
similarity should not be treated as a symmetric relation. Statements such as
“
a
is like
b
” are directional, with
a
as
the subject and
b
as the referent. This is not equivalent, in general,
to the converse similarity statement “
b
is like
a
”. Tversky also provides
concrete examples, like
“the portrait resembles the person” rather than “the person resembles the
portrait”, and “the son resembles the father” rather than “the father resembles
the son”. It is also clear that similarity relations are
not necessarily transitive.

This study is a continuation of our paper
[
15
]
, where, by describing the
completely join-irreducible elements of
\mathrm{DM(RS)}
for any reflexive relation
R
,
we characterized the case when
\mathrm{DM(RS)}
is a spatial completely distributive
lattice. There, we pointed out that even for a non-transitive reflexive
relation,
\mathrm{DM(RS)}
can form a Nelson algebra. In the present paper by
describing the pseudocomplements and dual pseudocomplements in the case of a
completely distributive
\mathrm{DM(RS)}
, we characterize those reflexive relations

R
on
U
for which
\mathrm{DM(RS)}
forms a regular pseudocomplemented Kleene algebra
or a double Stone lattice.
Surprisingly, we found a specific class of reflexive relations with
the property that
\mathrm{DM(RS)}
forms a regular double Stone lattice,
which is more general than the class of equivalence relations. For instance,
the relation
R
on
U=\{1,2,3\}
such that
R(1)=U
,
R(2)=\{2\}
,
and
R(3)=\{1,3\}
results in the lattice
\mathrm{DM(RS)}

(which in this case coincides with
\mathrm{RS}
)
being a regular double Stone algebra isomorphic
to
\mathbf{2}\times\mathbf{3}
; see Figure
1
.

Figure 1:

\mathrm{RS}
is isomorphic to
\mathbf{2}\times\mathbf{3}
.

Note that throughout this paper, sets in figures are often
denoted simply by the sequence of their elements
(e.g., 123 for
\{1,2,3\}
).

Our paper is structured as follows.
In the next section, we present the basic algebraic
and lattice-theoretic notions used in this work. In Section
3
,
the basic facts about rough approximation operators, the ordered set of rough sets,
and its completion
\mathrm{DM(RS)}
are recalled.
In Section
4
, we show that the lattice

\mathrm{DM(RS)}
is pseudocomplemented and dually pseudocomplemented
whenever the lattice
\wp(U)^{\blacktriangle}
of upper approximations is pseudocomplemented. We describe these pseudocomplement
operations. We also note that if
\wp(U)^{\blacktriangle}
is completely distributive,
then
\mathrm{DM(RS)}
forms a pseudocomplemented Kleene algebra.
In the following sections,
we restrict ourselves to the cases in which the completion
forms a spatial and completely distributive lattice.
Section
5
establishes the conditions
under which
this pseudocomplemented Kleene algebra is regular.
We also demonstrate connections to our previous work on tolerances
[
12
,
13
]
.
Section
6
is devoted to the case where

\mathrm{DM(RS)}

forms a double Stone algebra.
Finally, in Section
7
,
we describe reflexive relations for which

\mathrm{DM(RS)}
forms a regular double Stone algebra.
We note that such a relation
can be viewed as a new generalisation of the equivalence relation.
Some concluding remarks end the paper.

Preliminaries

In this section, we recall some essential algebraic and lattice-theoretic notions and results from the literature
[
3
,
5
,
8
]
that are used in this work.

Let
L
be a bounded lattice. We say that
b\in L
is a
complement

of
a
is
a\vee b=1
and
a\wedge b=0
. In this case, we say that

a
is
\emph{complemented}
. An element
a\in L
is called
neutral
if

(a\wedge x)\vee(x\wedge y)\vee(y\wedge a)=(a\vee x)\wedge(x\vee y)\wedge(y\vee a)

for all
x,y\in L
. It is known that
a\in L
is neutral if
and only if the sublattice generated by
a
,
x
, and
y
is distributive
for any
x,y\in L
. An element is said to be
central
if
it is complemented and neutral.
We denote by
\mathrm{Cen}(L)
the set of central elements
and call it the
center
of
L
.

The
pseudocomplement
of
a
, denoted by
a^{*}
, is the greatest element
x
in
L
such that
a\wedge x=0
. A lattice in which every element has a pseudocomplement is called a
pseudocomplemented lattice
.
The algebra
(L,\vee,\wedge,^{*},0,1)
is called
p
-algebra
for short.
The following properties hold for every
a,b\in L
.

(a)

a\leq b
implies
b^{*}\leq a^{*}
.

(b)

The map
a\mapsto a^{**}
is a closure operator.

(c)

a^{*}=a^{***}
.

(d)

(a\vee b)^{*}=a^{*}\wedge b^{*}
.

(e)

(a\wedge b)^{*}\geq a^{*}\vee b^{*}
.

Now, the set of complemented elements is

\{a\in L\mid a\vee a^{*}=1\}
. Moreover, if
L
is
distributive, then

\mathrm{Cen}(L)=\{a\in L\mid a\vee a^{*}=1\}
.
The set
S(L):=\{a^{**}\mid a\in L\}
coincides with

\{a^{*}\mid a\in L\}
and is called the

skeleton
of
L
.

An algebra
(L,\vee,\wedge,^{*},^{+},0,1)
is called a
double
p
-algebra
if
(L,\vee,\wedge,^{*},0,1)
is a
p
-algebra and
(L,\vee,\wedge,^{+},0,1)
is a

dual
p
-algebra
(that is,
z\geq x^{+}\iff x\vee z=1
for all
x,y\in L
). In this case,

L
is called a
double pseudocomplemented
lattice.

A
Stone algebra
is a pseudocomplemented distributive lattice
L
such that for every
x\in L
,

x^{*}\vee x^{**}=1.

(2.1)

The following lemma can be found in
[
8
, p. 149]
.

Lemma 2.1
.

For any distributive
p
-algebra
L
, the following conditions are equivalent.

(i)

L
is a Stone algebra.

(ii)

(a\wedge b)^{*}=a^{*}\vee b^{*}
for all
a,b\in L
.

(iii)

a,b\in S(L)
implies
a\vee b\in S(L)
.

(iv)

S(L)
is a subalgebra of
L
.

If
L
is a Stone algebra, then
a\in S(L)
means that
a=b^{**}
for some

b\in L
. This implies that
a^{*}=b^{***}=b^{*}
and

a\vee a^{*}=b^{*}\vee b^{**}=1
. Thus,

a\in\mathrm{Cen}(L)
. On the other hand, if
a\in\mathrm{Cen}(L)
,
then

a^{**}=a^{**}\wedge 1=a^{**}\wedge(a\vee a^{*})=(a^{**}\wedge a)\vee(a^{**}\wedge a^{*})=a\vee 0=a,

meaning that
a\in S(L)
. Hence,
S(L)=\mathrm{Cen}(L)
is a
Boolean algebra which is a sublattice of
L
containing
0
and
1
.

By dualising the above notions, we get the concepts of

dual pseudocomplement
,

dual pseudocomplemented lattice
,
and
dual Stone algebra
.
A
double pseudocomplemented lattice
is a pseudocomplemented lattice which is also a dual pseudocomplemented lattice. Similarly, a
double Stone algebra
is a Stone algebra which is also a dual Stone algebra.
Every double Stone algebra satisfies
x^{*}\leq x^{+}
, where
+
denotes the dual pseudocomplement operation.

For a double Stone algebra
L
, the center can be characterized in
several equivalent ways using the pseudocomplementation and
dual pseudocomplementation:

\textrm{Cen}(L)=\{a\in L\mid a^{*}=a^{+}\}=\{a\in L\mid a^{**}=a\}=\{a\in L\mid a^{++}=a\}.

If a double Stone algebra
L
forms a complete lattice,
then
\mathrm{Cen}(L)
is a complete sublattice
of
L
. This is because the map
x\mapsto x^{**}
is a closure operation on
L
,
and the set
\mathrm{Cen}(L)
of its closed elements is a closed under
arbitrary meets in
L
. Similarly,
x\mapsto x^{++}
is an interior operation and therefore
\mathrm{Cen}(L)
is a closed under arbitrary joins in
L
.

We say that a double
p
-algebra is
regular
if it satisfies the
condition

x^{*}=y^{*}\quad\text{and}\quad x^{+}=y^{+}\quad\text{imply}\quad x=y.

(M)

Here “regularity” refers to “congruence-regularity”.
An algebra is
congruence-regular
if every congruence is determined
by any class of it: two congruences are necessarily equal when they have a
class in common.
J. Varlet has proved in
[
28
]
that double
pseudocomplemented lattices satisfying (
M
) are
exactly the congruence-regular ones.

A
De Morgan algebra

(L,\vee,\wedge,{\sim},0,1)
is
an algebra such that
(L,\vee,\wedge,0,1)

is a bounded distributive lattice and the negation
\sim
satisfies the
double negation law

{\sim}{\sim}x=x,

and the two
De Morgan laws

{\sim}(x\vee y)={\sim}x\wedge{\sim}y\quad\text{and}\quad{\sim}(x\wedge y)={\sim}x\vee{\sim}y.

Note that this means that
\sim
is an order-isomorphism
between
(L,\leq)
and its dual
(L,\geq)
.
In a De Morgan algebra, the laws

{\sim}x\vee x=1
and
{\sim}x\wedge x=0
do not necessarily hold.
In addition,
{\sim}1=0
and
\sim 0=1
.

A
Kleene algebra
is a De Morgan algebra satisfying
the inequality

x\wedge{\sim}x\leq y\vee{\sim}y.

(K)

A
pseudocomplemented De Morgan algebra
is an algebra

(L,\vee,\wedge,\sim,^{*},0,1)
such that
(L,\vee,\wedge,\sim,0,1)
is a De Morgan
algebra and
(L,\vee,\wedge,^{*},0,1)
is a
p
-algebra. In fact, such an
algebra forms a double
p
-algebra, where the pseudocomplement operations
determine each other by

{\sim}x^{*}=({\sim}x)^{+}\quad\text{and}\quad{\sim}x^{+}=({\sim}x)^{*}.

(2.2)

A
pseudocomplemented Kleene algebra
is defined
analogously.
Sankappanavar has proved in
[
25
]
that any
pseudocomplemented De Morgan algebra satisfying (M) is congruence-regular. Therefore, we may call pseudocomplemented
De Morgan and Kleene algebras regular when they satisfy (M).

A complete lattice
L
is
completely distributive
if for any doubly indexed subset
\{a_{i,\,j}\}_{i\in I,\,j\in J}
of
L
, we have:

\bigwedge_{i\in I}\Big(\bigvee_{j\in J}a_{i,\,j}\Big)=\bigvee_{f\colon I\to J}\Big(\bigwedge_{i\in I}a_{i,\,f(i)}\Big),

that is, any meet of joins may be converted into the join of all possible elements obtained by taking the meet over
i\in I
of
elements
a_{i,\,k}
, where
k
depends on
i
.
Also a complete sublattice of a completely distributive lattice is
clearly completely distributive.

A weaker form of complete distributivity is
join-infinite distributivity
:
for any
x\in L
and subset
\{y_{i}\}_{i\in I}
of
L
,

x\wedge\bigvee_{i\in I}y_{i}=\bigvee_{i\in I}x\wedge y_{i}.

(JID)

A lattice
L
satisfying (JID) is pseudocomplemented and

a^{*}=\bigvee\{x\in L\mid a\wedge x=0\}.

(2.3)

Let
L
be a complete lattice and let
k\in L
. The element
k
is said to be

\emph{compact}
if for every subset
S
of
L
,

k\leq\bigvee S\mbox{ \ implies \ }k\leq\bigvee F\mbox{ \ for some finite subset $F$ of $S$}.

The set of compact elements of
L
is denoted
\mathcal{K}(L)
.
A complete lattice
L
is said to be
algebraic
if, for each

a\in L
,

a=\bigvee\{k\in\mathcal{K}(L)\mid k\leq a\}.

A nonzero element
j
of a complete lattice
L
is called

completely join-irreducible
if
j=\bigvee S
implies
j\in S

for every subset
S
of
L
.
Note that the least element
0\in L
is not completely join-irreducible. The set of completely join-irreducible elements of
L
is denoted by

\mathcal{J}(L)
, or simply by
\mathcal{J}
if there is no danger of
confusion.
A complete lattice
L
is
spatial
if for each
a\in L
,

a=\bigvee\{j\in\mathcal{J}\mid j\leq a\}.

An element
p
of a complete lattice
L
is said to be

completely join-prime
if for every
X\subseteq L
,
p\leq\bigvee X
implies
p\leq x
for some
x\in X
.
We denote by
\mathcal{J}_{p}(L)
the set of all completely
join-prime elements of
L
.

In a complete lattice
L
, each completely join-prime element is completely join-irreducible.
The converse does not always hold, but if
L
is a completely
distributive, then the set of completely join-prime and completely join-irreducible elements coincide
[
1
]
.

A
complete lattice of sets

\mathcal{L}\subseteq\wp(U)
is a family of sets such that

\bigcap\mathcal{H}
and
\bigcup\mathcal{H}
belong to
\mathcal{L}
for all

\mathcal{H}\subseteq\mathcal{L}
. Note that
\emptyset=\bigcup\emptyset

and
U=\bigcap\emptyset
always belong to any complete lattice of
sets defined on
U
. The following result can be found in

[
5
, Theorem 10.29]
.

Proposition 2.2
.

Let
L
be a lattice. Then the following are equivalent.

(i)

L
is isomorphic to a complete lattice of sets.

(ii)

L
is distributive, and
L
and its dual
L^{d}
are algebraic.

(iii)

L
is complete,
L
satisfies
(JID)
and
L
is spatial.

(iv)

L
is completely distributive and
L
is algebraic.

Note that if
(L,\vee,\wedge,{\sim},0,1)
is a De Morgan algebra,
then the conditions of Proposition
2.2
are
valid if and only if
L
is algebraic or, equivalently,
L
is spatial and completely distributive.

Let
P
be an ordered set with a least element
0
.
An element
a\in P
is an
atom
if it covers 0,
that is,
0\prec a
. The set of atoms of
P
is denoted by

\mathcal{A}(P)
, or simply by
\mathcal{A}
if there
is no danger of confusion.
The ordered set
P
is
atomic
if every element
b>0
has an atom below it, and
P
is
atomistic
, if every element of
P
is the join of atoms below it.

Proposition 2.3
.

Let
B
be a complete Boolean lattice. The following are equivalent.

(i)

B
is atomic.

(ii)

B
is atomistic.

(iii)

B
is completely distributive.

(iv)

B
is isomorphic to
\wp(X)
for some set
X
.

Proof.

\mathrm{(i)}\Leftrightarrow\mathrm{(ii)}
:
Assume that
B
is atomic.
Let
x\in B
and set
y=\bigvee\{a\in\mathcal{A}\mid a\leq x\}
.
Since
B
is complete,
y
exists and
y\leq x
.
We show that
x\wedge y^{\prime}=0
which is equivalent to
x\leq y
, and hence
will imply
x=y
.
Assume for contradiction that
x\wedge y^{\prime}\neq 0
. Since
B
is atomic,
the non-zero element
x\wedge y^{\prime}
must contain an atom, say
a
.
Thus,
a\leq x\wedge y^{\prime}
, which means
a\leq x
and
a\leq y^{\prime}
.
Since
a\leq x
, also
a\leq y
holds. This gives
a\leq y^{\prime}\wedge y=0
,
which is a contradiction, because
a
is an atom. Thus,
B
is atomistic.
The other direction is immediate from the definitions.

The equivalence of (i), (iii) and (iv) is proved in Theorem 2 in
Section
XII.3
in
[
2
]
.
∎

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
L
be a completely distributive lattice.
The following are equivalent.

(i)

L
is a Boolean algebra.

(ii)

L
is spatial and
\mathcal{A}=\mathcal{J}
.

Proof.

\mathrm{(i)}\Rightarrow\mathrm{(ii)}
:
Let
L
be a completely distributive
Boolean algebra. By Proposition
2.3
,

L
is atomistic and hence spatial.
For
j\in\mathcal{J}
,

j=\bigvee\{a\in\mathcal{A}\mid a\leq j\}
,
implying
j\in\mathcal{A}
.

\mathrm{(ii)}\Rightarrow\mathrm{(i)}
: By assumption,
L
is an
atomistic completely distributive lattice. We need to show that

L
is complemented. Let
a\in L
and set

a^{\prime}=\bigvee\{j\in\mathcal{J}\mid j\nleq a\}\in L
.
Because
L
is spatial,
a\vee a^{\prime}=\bigvee\{j\in\mathcal{J}\mid j\leq a\}\vee\bigvee\{j\in\mathcal{J}\mid j\nleq a\}=\bigvee\mathcal{J}=1
. Complete distributivity implies

a\wedge a^{\prime}=a\wedge\bigvee\{j\in\mathcal{J}\mid j\nleq a\}=\bigvee\{a\wedge j\mid j\in\mathcal{J},\ j\nleq a\}\text{.}

Now, consider the term
a\wedge j
. Since
j
is an atom and
j\nleq a
, the
meet
a\wedge j
must be
0
. This implies
a\wedge a^{\prime}=0
. Therefore,

is complemented.

3
Rough approximations and rough sets

In this section, we recall from
[
17
,
15
]
the basic results related to the rough approximation operators,
the ordered set of rough sets, and its completion.

Let
R
be a binary relation on
U
. For any element

x\in U
, we define
R(x)
as the set of all elements
y
in
U
such that the pair
(x,y)
is in
R
. This is expressed as:

R(x):=\{y\in U\mid(x,y)\in R\}

For any subset
X
of
U
, we define its
lower approximation
, denoted
X^{\blacktriangledown}
, as the set of elements
x
in
U
whose
R(x)
is contained within
X
:

X^{\blacktriangledown}:=\{x\in U\mid R(x)\subseteq X\}

The
upper approximation
of
X
, denoted
X^{\blacktriangle}
, is the set of elements
x
in
U
whose
R(x)
has at least one element in common with
X
:

X^{\blacktriangle}:=\{x\in U\mid R(x)\cap X\neq\emptyset\}

Let
\wp(U)
be the family of all subsets of
U
.
We define the families of approximations as follows:

\displaystyle\wp(U)^{\blacktriangledown}:=\{X^{\blacktriangledown}\mid X\subseteq U\},\ \wp(U)^{\blacktriangle}:=\{X^{\blacktriangle}\mid X\subseteq U\},\ \wp(U)^{\triangledown}:=\{X^{\triangledown}\mid X\subseteq U\},\ \wp(U)^{\vartriangle}:=\{X^{\vartriangle}\mid X\subseteq U\}.

Let
P
and
Q
be ordered sets. A pair

(f,g)
of maps
f\colon P\to Q
and
g\colon Q\to P

is a
Galois connection
or a
residuated pair
between
P
and
Q
if

f(p)\leq q\iff p\leq g(q)
for all
p\in P
and
q\in Q
.
The essential properties and results on Galois connections
can be found in
[
3
,
5
,
6
]
, for example.

The pairs of maps
({{}^{\blacktriangle}},{{}^{\triangledown}})
and
({{}^{\vartriangle}},{{}^{\blacktriangledown}})
are
Galois connections on the complete lattice
(\wp(U),\subseteq)
as noted in
[
17
]
, for instance. This means that if
R
is an arbitrary binary relation on

U
, the following facts hold for all
X,Y\subseteq U
and

\mathcal{H}\subseteq\wp(U)
:

(GC1)

\emptyset^{\blacktriangle}=\emptyset^{\vartriangle}=\emptyset
and

U^{\blacktriangledown}=U^{\triangledown}=U
.

(GC2)

X^{\triangledown\blacktriangle}\subseteq X\subseteq X^{\blacktriangle\triangledown}
and

X^{\blacktriangledown\vartriangle}\subseteq X\subseteq X^{\vartriangle\blacktriangledown}
.

(GC3)

X\subseteq Y
implies
X^{\blacktriangledown}\subseteq Y^{\blacktriangledown}
,

X^{\triangledown}\subseteq Y^{\triangledown}
,
X^{\blacktriangle}\subseteq Y^{\blacktriangle}
,

The operations

(3.1)

of

(\wp(U)^{\blacktriangle},\subseteq)\cong(\wp(U)^{\blacktriangledown},\supseteq)

If

is a

on

(Ref1)

\emptyset^{\blacktriangledown}=\emptyset^{\triangledown}=\emptyset

(Ref2)

Because
({{}^{\vartriangle}},{{}^{\blacktriangledown}})

\wp(U)^{\blacktriangledown}

\bigwedge_{i\in I}X_{i}=\bigcap_{i\in I}X_{i}\text{\quad and \quad}\bigvee_{i\in I}X_{i}=\big(\bigcup_{i\in I}X_{i}\big)^{\vartriangle\blacktriangledown}

The set

(X^{\blacktriangledown},X^{\blacktriangle})\leq(Y^{\blacktriangledown},Y^{\blacktriangle})\overset{\rm def}{\iff}X^{\blacktriangledown}\subseteq Y^{\blacktriangledown}\ \text{and}\ X^{\blacktriangle}\subseteq Y^{\blacktriangle}.

]
.

The

Next, we present an example that shows how reflexive directional similarity relations arise in many-valued information systems.

Example 3.1
.

In
[
30
]
, W. Ziarko introduced the
the relative degree of misclassification of
X
with
respect to
Y
by

c(X,Y):=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}1-|(X\cap Y)|\,/\,|X|,&\text{if $X\neq\emptyset$};\\
0,&\text{if $X=\emptyset$},\end{array}\right.

where
|Z|
stands for the cardinality of the set
Z
,
and
X
and
Y
are some finite sets.
This is interpreted so that if we were to classify all
elements of
X
into set
Y
, then
c(X,Y)\cdot 100

percent of cases would make a classification error.

In standard set theory,
A\subseteq B
only if every
element of
A
is in
B
. In

variable precision rough set theory

(VPRS), this is relaxed using a precision parameter

\beta
. The
\beta
-majority inclusion relation

is defined as
X\subseteq_{\beta}Y\iff c(X,Y)\leq\beta
.

The parameter
\beta
typically ranges from
0
to

0.5
. If
\beta=0
, the relation
is the standard set-inclusion relation. For
\beta>0
,
the relation
\subseteq_{\beta}
allows for “misclassified”
elements, making the model more robust against outliers or data errors in large datasets.

In variable precision rough set theory, the set-inclusion
relation is replaced by
{\subseteq}_{\beta}

when defining the approximations. For an equivalence
E
,
the
\beta
-lower approximation of
X
is the set of
elements
x
such that
[x]_{E}\subseteq_{\beta}X
,
where
[x]_{E}
is the
E
-class of
x
.
Similarly, the
\beta
-negative region of
X
is the set
of elements
x
satisfying
[x]_{E}\subseteq_{\beta}X^{c}
.

We may use the relation
{\subseteq}_{\beta}
in
a slightly different way. Let
(U,A)

be a many-valued information system introduced
by E. Orłowska and Z. Pawlak in
[
21
]
.
Here,
U
is the set of objects and
A
is the set
of attributes. Each attribute
a\in A
is a mapping

a\colon U\to\wp(V_{a})
, where
V_{a}
is
the value set of
a
.

For an attribute
a\in A
,
We define a relation
\mathrm{sim}_{\beta}(a)
on
U

by setting

(x,y)\in\mathrm{sim}_{\beta}(a)\iff a(x)\subseteq_{\beta}a(y)
.
As noted by Ziarko,
\subseteq_{\beta}
is reflexive, but generally neither symmetric nor transitive. Consequently,

\mathrm{sim}_{\beta}(a)
is also reflexive but not necessarily symmetric or transitive. This implies that

\mathrm{sim}_{\beta}(a)
represents

directional similarity
:

(x,y)\in\mathrm{sim}_{\beta}(a)
indicates that the

a
-values of
x
are included in those of
y
, provided
that a certain amount of misclassification is allowed.
Essentially,
x
is
\beta
-similar to
y
because
x
does
not introduce
a
-values outside of
a(y)
, given the

\beta
tolerance.

For instance, if
a
is the attribute “knowledge of
languages”, then
(x,y)\in\mathrm{sim}_{\beta}(a)

means that
x
can be considered similar to
y
with respect to spoken languages. This is because
y
appears to speak all languages that
x
speaks, allowing for some uncertainty provided by
\beta
.

Our following lemma shows that for each
A\in\wp(U)^{\blacktriangledown}
,
there is a pair in
\mathrm{DM(RS)}
containing
A
as the first
element. An analogous claim holds for

.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
R
be a reflexive relation.

(i)

If

, then
(A,A^{\vartriangle\blacktriangle})
belongs to

\mathrm{DM(RS)}
.

(ii)

If

, then
(B^{\triangledown\blacktriangledown},B)
belongs to

\mathrm{DM(RS)}
.

Proof.

(a) Let
A\in\wp(U)^{\blacktriangledown}
. Note that
A^{\vartriangle\blacktriangle}
belongs
to
\wp(U)^{\blacktriangle}
and
A^{\vartriangle\blacktriangle}\subseteq A^{\vartriangle\blacktriangle}

holds trivially. Also the inclusion

A\cap\mathcal{S}\subseteq A^{\vartriangle\blacktriangle}\cap\mathcal{S}

is immediate since
A\subseteq A^{\vartriangle\blacktriangle}
.
For the reverse, suppose
x\in A^{\vartriangle\blacktriangle}\cap\mathcal{S}
.
Because
x\in\mathcal{S}
,
\{x\}=R(x)\subseteq A^{\vartriangle\blacktriangle}
means that

.
Therefore, there is

. Because

,

for some

.
Since

,
R(y)\subseteq C
. Furthermore,
y\in\breve{R}(x)

implies

and

. Given that

, we have

and

. This confirms

. Therefore,

A\cap\mathcal{S}=A^{\vartriangle\blacktriangle}\cap\mathcal{S}
.

(b) Let

. Now

belongs to

\wp(U)^{\blacktriangledown}
and

.
Furthermore, by the properties of the approximation operators,

(B^{\triangledown\blacktriangledown})^{\vartriangle\blacktriangle}=B^{\triangledown(\blacktriangledown\vartriangle)\blacktriangle}\subseteq B^{\triangledown\blacktriangle}\subseteq B.

To prove the intersection equality, note that

B^{\triangledown\blacktriangledown}\cap\mathcal{S}\subseteq B\cap\mathcal{S}

.
Let

. Because

for some

,

.
We have

.
Therefore,
B^{\triangledown\blacktriangledown}\cap\mathcal{S}=B\cap\mathcal{S}
.
∎

for all

.

4
Pseudocomplements

In this section, it is shown that if
\wp(U)^{\blacktriangle}
is
pseudocomplemented, then
\mathrm{DM(RS)}
is
pseudocomplemented. The pseudocomplement operation is
described in terms of the pseudocomplement of
\wp(U)^{\blacktriangle}
.
Consequently, if
\wp(U)^{\blacktriangle}
is
completely distributive, then
\mathrm{DM(RS)}

is a completely distributive pseudocomplemented Kleene algebra.
In the case where
\wp(U)^{\blacktriangle}
is completely
distributive and spatial, we provide a method to construct the
pseudocomplement of any element of
\wp(U)^{\blacktriangle}
utilizing the
notion of the core.

Proposition 4.1
.

Let
R
be a reflexive relation.
If
\wp(U)^{\blacktriangle}
is pseudocomplemented, then also
\mathrm{DM(RS)}

is pseudocomplemented. For
(A,B)\in\mathrm{DM(RS)}
,

(A,B)^{*}=(B^{*\triangledown\blacktriangledown},B^{*}),

where
B^{*}
is the pseudocomplement of
B
in
\wp(U)^{\blacktriangle}
.

Proof.

By Lemma
3.2
,
(B^{*\triangledown\blacktriangledown},B^{*})

belongs to
\mathrm{DM(RS)}
.
We first prove that

(A,B)\wedge(B^{*\triangledown\blacktriangledown},B^{*})=(A\wedge B^{*\triangledown\blacktriangledown},B\wedge B^{*})=(\emptyset,\emptyset),

(4.1)

where the meets are taken component-wise in

\wp(U)^{\blacktriangledown}
and
\wp(U)^{\blacktriangle}
, respectively.
Obviously,
\emptyset=B\wedge B^{*}=(B\cap B^{*})^{\triangledown\blacktriangle}
in
\wp(U)^{\blacktriangle}
.
Because
A^{\vartriangle\blacktriangle}\subseteq B
, we obtain

A^{\vartriangle}\subseteq A^{\vartriangle(\blacktriangle\triangledown)}\subseteq B^{\triangledown}
and

A\subseteq A^{\vartriangle\blacktriangledown}\subseteq B^{\triangledown\blacktriangledown}
.
Thus, in
\wp(U)^{\blacktriangledown}
,

A\wedge B^{*\triangledown\blacktriangledown}=A\cap B^{*\triangledown\blacktriangledown}\subseteq B^{\triangledown\blacktriangledown}\cap B^{*\triangledown\blacktriangledown}=(B\cap B^{*})^{\triangledown\blacktriangledown}\subseteq(B\cap B^{*})^{\triangledown\blacktriangle}=\emptyset.

This proves (
4.1
).

Secondly, let
(X,Y)\in\mathrm{DM(RS)}
be such that

(A,B)\wedge(X,Y)=\emptyset
. The condition

B\wedge Y=\emptyset
in
\wp(U)^{\blacktriangle}
implies
that
Y\subseteq B^{*}
. Since
(X,Y)\in\mathrm{DM(RS)}
, we have

X^{\vartriangle\blacktriangle}\subseteq Y\subseteq B^{*}
and

X^{\vartriangle}\subseteq X^{\vartriangle(\blacktriangle\triangledown)}\subseteq Y^{\triangledown}\subseteq B^{*\triangledown}
. It follows

X\subseteq X^{\vartriangle\blacktriangledown}\subseteq B^{*\triangledown\blacktriangledown}
.
We have proved
(X,Y)\leq(B^{*\triangledown\blacktriangledown},B^{*})
,
completing the proof.
∎

B^{*}
the pseudocomplement defined in

.

Let
(A,B)\in\mathrm{DM(RS)}
.

,

In what follows, we present a description of
B^{*}
for

be a binary relation on

\mathfrak{core}R(x):=\{w\in R(x)\mid w\in R(y)\implies R(x)\subseteq R(y)\}.

(4.2)

Similarly, we define the

\breve{R}(x)
by replacing

\breve{R}
in (

[

We define the following set for any

\mathcal{K}(B):=\bigcup\{\mathfrak{core}\breve{R}(x)\mid\breve{R}(x)\subseteq B\}.

We can now compute:

\displaystyle y\in\mathcal{K}(B)^{\vartriangle}

\displaystyle\iff\breve{R}(y)\cap\mathcal{K}(B)\neq\emptyset

\displaystyle\iff\breve{R}(y)\cap\textstyle{\bigcup}\{\mathfrak{core}\breve{R}(x)\mid\breve{R}(x)\subseteq B\}\neq\emptyset

\displaystyle\iff\textstyle{\bigcup}\{\breve{R}(y)\cap\mathfrak{core}\breve{R}(x)\mid\breve{R}(x)\subseteq B\}\neq\emptyset

Trivially,

.

On the other hand,
\mathfrak{core}\breve{R}(x)\neq\emptyset

Because

\mathrm{(i)}\Rightarrow\mathrm{(ii)}

x\in U
.
As
\{\{x\}^{\blacktriangle}\mid x\in U\}
forms an irredundant
covering of
U
, the inclusion

\{y\}^{\blacktriangle}\subseteq\{x\}^{\blacktriangle}
implies

\{y\}^{\blacktriangle}=\{x\}^{\blacktriangle}
for any
y\in U
.
Let
\emptyset\neq Y^{\blacktriangle}\subseteq\{x\}^{\blacktriangle}
. Then,
Y
is nonempty
and it contains at least one element
y
.
Now
\{y\}^{\blacktriangle}\subseteq Y^{\blacktriangle}\subseteq\{x\}^{\blacktriangle}
and

\{y\}^{\blacktriangle}=\{x\}^{\blacktriangle}
imply
Y^{\blacktriangle}=\{x\}^{\blacktriangle}
. This means that
\{x\}^{\blacktriangle}
is an atom of
\wp(U)^{\blacktriangle}
.

Next we prove that

\mathfrak{core}\breve{R}(x)\neq\emptyset
.
Because the covering

\{\{a\}^{\blacktriangle}\mid a\in U\}
is irredundant,
there exists an element
y
in the difference

\{x\}^{\blacktriangle}\setminus\bigcup\{\{b\}^{\blacktriangle}\mid\{b\}^{\blacktriangle}\neq\{x\}^{\blacktriangle}\}.

Suppose that
y\in\breve{R}(c)=\{c\}^{\blacktriangle}

for some
c\in U
. We must have

\breve{R}(c)=\{c\}^{\blacktriangle}=\{x\}^{\blacktriangle}=\breve{R}(x)
.
Thus,
\breve{R}(x)\subseteq\breve{R}(c)
yields

y\in\mathfrak{core}\breve{R}(x)
.

\mathrm{(ii)}\Rightarrow\mathrm{(i)}
:
Suppose that (
rSt
) holds and
that the covering
\{\{a\}^{\blacktriangle}\mid a\in U\}
is
not irredundant. This means that there is
x\in U

such that

\{x\}^{\blacktriangle}\subseteq\bigcup\{\{y\}^{\blacktriangle}\mid\{y\}^{\blacktriangle}\neq\{x\}^{\blacktriangle}\}
.
Now
\emptyset\neq\mathfrak{core}\breve{R}(x)\subseteq\breve{R}(x)=\{x\}^{\blacktriangle}
. Hence, for any
w\in\mathfrak{core}\breve{R}(x)
,
there is
y\in U
with
\{y\}^{\blacktriangle}\neq\{x\}^{\blacktriangle}

and
w\in\{y\}^{\blacktriangle}=\breve{R}(y)
. Then
\{x\}^{\blacktriangle}=\breve{R}(x)\subseteq\breve{R}(y)=\{y\}^{\blacktriangle}
. Since

\{y\}^{\blacktriangle}
is an atom in
\wp(U)^{\blacktriangle}
, we get

\{y\}^{\blacktriangle}=\{x\}^{\blacktriangle}
, a contradiction. Thus,
we have an irredundant covering.
∎

Definition 7.4
.

Let
R
be a reflexive relation. We say that

R
is an
clinker equivalence
if

\{\{x\}^{\blacktriangle}\mid x\in U\}
is an irredundant covering of
U
.

Remark 7.5
.

The term clinker equivalence was selected because the geometric form of

\{x\}^{\blacktriangle}
resembles the sail of a Viking ship, specifically
referencing the historical clinker-built method of Norse shipbuilding.

By Proposition
7.3
,
R

is a clinker equivalence if and only if condition (
rSt
)
holds. Therefore, by applying Theorem
7.2
,
we obtain our following proposition.

Proposition 7.6
.

Let
R
be a reflexive relation on
U
. Then

\mathrm{DM(RS)}
forms a regular double Stone algebra
defined on a completely
distributive spatial lattice if and only if
R
is a clinker
equivalence.

Our next example demonstrates the use of this proposition.

Example 7.7
.

Let
R
be a relation on
U=\{1,2,3,4\}
such that

R(1)=\{1,2,3,4\},\ R(2)=\{1,2\},\ R(3)=\{3\},\ R(4)=\{4\}.

Then,

\{1\}^{\blacktriangle}=\{2\}^{\blacktriangle}=\{1,2\},\ \{3\}^{\blacktriangle}=\{1,3\},\ \{4\}^{\blacktriangle}=\{1,4\}.

Clearly, they form an irredundant covering of
U
. This means
that
\mathrm{DM(RS)}
is a regular double Stone algebra. The lattice

\mathrm{DM(RS)}
is depicted in Figure
4
.

The Hasse diagram of
\mathrm{RS}
.

The lattice
\mathrm{DM(RS)}
is equal to
\mathrm{RS}
and is
isomorphic to the product
\mathbf{2}\times\mathbf{2}\times\mathbf{3}
, where

\mathbf{2}
and
\mathbf{3}
are chains of
two and three elements, respectively.

Remark 7.8
.

Proposition
7.3
gives a simple method to
generate regular double Stone algebras in terms of reflexive relations.
We first define an irredundant covering
\mathcal{C}
of
U
. Then, we
attach to each element
x\in U
a set of
B
of
\mathcal{C}

such that
x\in B
. Also, each set in
\mathcal{C}
needs to be
attached to some element of
U
.
These sets will be the neighbourhoods of the inverse
relation of
R
, that is,
\breve{R}(x)=\{x\}^{\blacktriangle}
for any
x\in U
.
Finally, construct the relation
R
from
\breve{R}
.

Some concluding remarks

In this paper, we considered various algebras that can be defined on
the completion
\mathrm{DM(RS)}
of rough sets determined by a
reflexive relation. In our previous work, we investigated a new way to obtain a Nelson algebra. Here, we have presented similar results for regular pseudocomplemented Kleene algebras and Stone algebras. The case of regular double Stone algebras is of special interest because it led us to introduce
clinker equivalences
, which can be seen as a novel generalisation of equivalence relations. Our forthcoming paper will be devoted to an in-depth study and construction of clinker equivalences.
