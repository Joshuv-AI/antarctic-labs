Scrotal flap phalloplasty and perineal urethrostomy following total penectomy for stage IIIB penile cancer: a case report
Authors: Víctor Hugo Linares-Serrano, Porfirio Damián López-Alvarado, Félix Pérez-Oliva, Omar Alexander Hernández-Hernández, Carlos Eduardo Vázquez-Flores, David Sánchez-Delgadillo, Eduardo Jair Mota-Ortiz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPenile squamous cell carcinoma is a rare malignancy that may require radical surgery in advanced stages. We report a 66-year-old male with a one-year history of a penile lesion diagnosed as verrucous squamous cell carcinoma, staged as T3N2M0 (clinical stage IIIB) based on magnetic resonance imaging findings. The patient underwent total penectomy with scrotal flap phalloplasty and perineal urethrostomy, followed by robotic inguinal lymphadenectomy, which revealed no metastatic involvement (pN0). Postoperative outcomes were favorable, with good flap viability and satisfactory urinary function. This case highlights a practical reconstructive approach and the importance of histopathological confirmation of nodal disease.

Introduction

Penile cancer is an uncommon malignancy, accounting for less than 1% of male cancers in developed countries, with squamous cell carcinoma representing the predominant histologic subtype.1,2 Advanced disease may require radical surgical treatment to achieve adequate oncologic control, particularly in cases with extensive local invasion.3 Stage IIIB penile cancer may involve regional lymph node metastases and often requires aggressive multimodal management. While oncologic control remains the primary objective, the functional and psychological consequences of total penectomy are substantial.4

Reconstruction after total penectomy remains underreported, especially in resource-limited settings where microsurgical options may not be available.5 Scrotal fasciocutaneous flaps represent a practical reconstructive alternative because of their robust vascularity, elasticity, and similarity to penile skin 6, 7. Additionally, perineal urethrostomy provides a reliable urinary diversion when urethral reconstruction is not feasible.3

Case presentation

A 66-year-old male presented with a one-year history of a progressively enlarging penile lesion associated with ulceration and intermittent bleeding. Physical examination revealed a large exophytic ulcerated mass involving the glans and extending to the penile shaft (Fig. 1), with bilateral palpable inguinal lymphadenopathy.

Preoperative appearance of the penile lesion demonstrating a large exophytic ulcerated mass involving the glans and extending to the penile shaft.

Magnetic resonance imaging demonstrated enlarged bilateral inguinal lymph nodes suspicious for metastatic involvement. No distant metastases were identified. Histopathological analysis confirmed verrucous squamous cell carcinoma. The tumor was classified as cT3N2M0 (clinical stage IIIB). Given the extent of local disease and nodal suspicion, radical surgical management was planned. Preoperative marking of a scrotal fasciocutaneous flap was performed (Fig. 2).

Preoperative surgical planning and marking of the scrotal fasciocutaneous flap prior to total penectomy and reconstruction.

Due to the extent of local disease and involvement of the penile shaft, total penectomy with perineal urethrostomy and immediate cosmetic reconstruction was performed. The procedure began with a perineal approach ( Fig. 3 ). Through the perineal incision, the urethra and corporal bodies were identified, dissected, and detached proximally from their attachments to the ischiopubic rami, allowing complete oncologic resection of the penis while preserving adjacent perineoscrotal tissues. Following specimen removal and confirmation of adequate surgical margins, the same incision was utilized to facilitate mobilization of local tissues for reconstruction.

Creation of the perineal urethrostomy following total penectomy, demonstrating maturation of the urethral stump into a tension-free perineal stoma.

The previously marked scrotal skin was preserved and developed into bilateral advancement flaps while maintaining its vascular supply. These flaps were advanced medially and approximated in the midline to cover the defect created by the penectomy. The lateral edges were sutured longitudinally, creating a cylindrical neophallus-like structure. The distal portion of the reconstruction was then tailored to achieve a rounded contour resembling a glans and to improve the overall cosmetic appearance. Excess tissue was excised as necessary, and layered closure was performed without tension. A definitive perineal urethrostomy was subsequently fashioned in the standard manner, and a transurethral catheter was left in place at the end of the procedure.

The postoperative course was uneventful, with no flap necrosis, infection, or wound complications. At 3-month follow-up, the perineal urethrostomy remained patent, allowing satisfactory seated voiding. The reconstructed phallic structure demonstrated good viability and an acceptable cosmetic outcome, and the patient reported improvement in body image.

Because of suspicious lymph nodes identified on imaging, the patient subsequently underwent robotic-assisted bilateral inguinal lymphadenectomy, which was completed without perioperative complications. Histopathological evaluation revealed no metastatic involvement (pN0). No evidence of early oncologic recurrence was observed. The immediate postoperative result is shown in Fig. 4.

Discussion

Stage IIIB penile cancer represents advanced disease with suspected regional lymph node involvement requiring aggressive surgical management. Total penectomy remains the standard approach in cases with extensive local invasion.3 Although verrucous carcinoma is generally associated with low metastatic potential, delayed presentation may result in locally advanced disease requiring radical treatment.

In this case, magnetic resonance imaging suggested nodal involvement; however, final pathology demonstrated no metastasis. This finding is consistent with the biological behavior of verrucous carcinoma, which is characterized by locally aggressive growth and a relatively low metastatic potential. This case highlights the importance of histopathological confirmation of nodal disease because imaging findings may overestimate lymph node involvement in certain histological subtypes.

Reconstruction following total penectomy is essential to address functional and psychological consequences. Microsurgical free flaps may provide superior functional outcomes but require specialized expertise and resources.5,8 Scrotal flap reconstruction offers several advantages, including reliable vascular supply, tissue elasticity, and similarity to penile skin 6, 7. These characteristics make it a practical and reproducible option, particularly in resource-limited settings. However, limitations include reduced potential for sexual function and variability in cosmetic outcomes.

Perineal urethrostomy provides a safe and effective urinary diversion when urethral reconstruction is not feasible and is associated with satisfactory functional outcomes.3 This case demonstrates that scrotal flap phalloplasty combined with perineal urethrostomy offers a balanced approach between oncologic safety and functional restoration in appropriately selected patients.

Conclusions

Scrotal flap phalloplasty with perineal urethrostomy following total penectomy is a safe, feasible, and reproducible reconstructive strategy for advanced penile cancer. Histopathological confirmation of nodal disease remains essential, particularly when imaging findings are inconclusive. This approach may represent a valuable reconstructive alternative in resource-limited settings.
