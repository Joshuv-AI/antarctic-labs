Body roundness index and a body shape index and their associations with menopausal symptoms: a comparative cross-sectional study
Authors: Murat Polat, Sait Erbey, Mehmet Alican Sapmaz, Çağanay Soysal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMenopause is accompanied by a considerable symptom burden, and although body mass index (BMI) is commonly used to assess its association with menopausal symptoms, BMI does not capture central fat distribution. The Body Roundness Index (BRI) and A Body Shape Index (ABSI) specifically reflect central adiposity. This study aimed to investigate the associations of BRI and ABSI with menopausal symptom severity in perimenopausal and menopausal women, and to compare these indices with BMI.MethodsThis cross-sectional study included 638 women aged 44–56 years (319 perimenopausal, 319 menopausal) recruited from a gynaecology outpatient clinic. Menopausal symptom burden was assessed using the Menopause-Specific Quality of Life Scale (MENQOL), and physical activity was evaluated with the International Physical Activity Questionnaire (IPAQ). BMI, BRI, and ABSI were calculated from anthropometric measurements. Kruskal-Wallis test, Spearman correlation analysis, and crude and adjusted multiple linear regression models were used for statistical analysis.ResultsAll three indices showed significant positive associations with total MENQOL scores. In Spearman correlation analysis, ABSI demonstrated the strongest correlation with total symptom burden (r = 0.677, p < 0.01), followed by BRI (r = 0.494, p < 0.01) and BMI (r = 0.332, p < 0.01). In multiple regression models, all associations remained statistically significant after adjustment for age, smoking status, history of chronic disease, BMI (BMI only in BRI and ABSI models) and physical activity. Obese women had the highest MENQOL scores across all subscales in both groups, while overweight women paradoxically showed lower scores than normal-weight women. Physical activity was negatively correlated with total symptom burden (r = − 0.084, p < 0.05), though its independent effect was limited in adjusted models.ConclusionsBRI and ABSI were independently and significantly associated with menopausal symptom burden, with ABSI showing the strongest correlation. Central adiposity indices may add clinically relevant information beyond BMI in menopausal women, and prospective studies are needed to clarify causality.

Background

Menopause is a physiological process defined by the absence of menstruation for 12 consecutive months in women, associated with the natural cessation of ovarian follicular activity. Biologically, this process is characterised by the depletion of ovarian reserve, loss of reproductive capacity, and endocrine changes following a decrease in oestrogen secretion [1]. Although it usually occurs between the ages of 45 and 55 as a natural consequence of ageing, it can also be induced by surgical intervention (bilateral oophorectomy), chemotherapy or pelvic radiotherapy [2]. During the menopausal transition, the majority of women experience various symptoms due to the decline in oestrogen levels. Vasomotor symptoms (hot flushes, night sweats, palpitations) are among the most common and troublesome symptoms of this period, affecting 40 to 70 per cent of women [3]. Psychosocial (depression, anxiety, irritability), physical (joint pain, fatigue, headache) and sexual dysfunction also constitute important components of the menopausal symptom burden; these symptoms have a significant negative impact on women’s quality of life, work productivity and social functioning [4].

In the literature, two main hypotheses are discussed regarding the relationship between BMI and vasomotor symptoms: The “thermoregulatory model” argues that adipose tissue acts as a powerful insulator, hindering heat dissipation and therefore causing obese women to experience more severe hot flushes; the “thin hypothesis” suggests that adipose tissue is a source of oestrogen and therefore obesity may reduce symptoms [1, 5]. Studies investigating BMI and menopause symptoms have produced conflicting results. While some studies have found a positive correlation between BMI and vasomotor symptoms, others have found no relationship or even an inverse relationship [2, 6–10].

Studies examining the relationship between physical activity and menopause symptoms have yielded mixed results. Some studies have reported that regular exercise is associated with a reduction in the frequency and severity of hot flushes [11]. However, some studies have not found a significant effect, and there are studies suggesting that physical activity reduces the severity of hot flushes but does not change their frequency [8–10, 12]. In addition, it has been reported that exercise can acutely trigger hot flushes [11]. In conclusion, the relationship between exercise and hot flushes is unclear.

The Body Roundness Index (BRI) is a relatively new index that measures central adiposity more specifically by combining waist circumference (WC) and height within a geometric ellipse model [13]. The A Body Shape Index (ABSI) is calculated using a mathematical normalisation that makes WC independent of BMI and height; this allows central fat accumulation to be assessed independently of BMI [14]. Both indices have been shown to exhibit strong correlations with cardiometabolic risk markers and chronic disease indicators such as diabetes mellitus (DM) and hypertension (HT); they have been reported to possess superior predictive validity compared to BMI [15, 16]. Although previous studies have suggested that central obesity is associated with an increased burden of hot flushes, at the time of our study, there were no studies examining the relationship between BRI and ABSI and menopausal symptoms [7, 17].

The biologically plausible mechanism linking central adiposity to menopausal symptom burden operates through several interconnected pathways. Oestrogen deficiency during the menopausal transition promotes the preferential redistribution of body fat from peripheral to central depots, resulting in an increase in visceral adipose tissue [1]. Visceral adipose tissue is metabolically active and releases pro-inflammatory cytokines, particularly interleukin-6 (IL-6) and tumour necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α), which are known to lower the hypothalamic thermoregulatory threshold [18, 19]. This narrowing of the thermoneutral zone renders the hypothalamus more sensitive to minor fluctuations in core body temperature, facilitating the triggering of vasomotor events such as hot flushes and night sweats [1]. In addition, central adiposity is associated with increased leptin levels and adipokine dysregulation, which may further amplify neuroendocrine instability during the menopausal transition [17]. These mechanisms collectively suggest that indices capturing central fat distribution, rather than overall adiposity, may be more closely associated with the severity of menopausal symptoms.

The aim of this study is to investigate the relationship between BRI and ABSI values and menopausal symptoms in women aged 44–56 years during the perimenopausal and menopausal periods; to evaluate this relationship comparatively with BMI; and to reveal the role of physical activity on symptoms.

Methods

Study design and ethical approval

This study was designed as a cross-sectional study. The research was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Ethics Committee of Ankara Etlik City Hospital (Decision No: AEŞH-BADEK-2024-1125 Decision date: 27/11/2024). All participants included in the study were informed about the purpose and scope of the research; written informed consent was obtained.

Study population and inclusion/exclusion criteria

The sample size was calculated with an effect size of 0.22, alpha error of 0.05, and 1-β of 0.95. The total sample size was 638, comprising 319 participants in the perimenopausal group and 319 in the menopausal group. Patients aged 44–56 years who were in perimenopause or menopause according to the STRAW (Stages of Reproductive Aging Workshop) criteria were included in the study (Perimenopause: a gap of more than 7 days in the normal menstrual period or skipping ≥ 2 menstrual cycles or no menstruation for ≥ 60 days. Menopause is defined as no menstruation for at least 12 months) [20]. Exclusion criteria were defined as a history of surgical menopause or premature ovarian failure, use of hormone replacement therapy and history of use. Participants were divided into two main groups: menopause and perimenopause. The groups were further subdivided into three subgroups according to the World Health Organisation (WHO) classification: normal weight (BMI ≤ 24.9 kg/m²), overweight (BMI 25.0–29.9 kg/m²) and obese (BMI ≥ 30.0 kg/m²). Among the 638 recruited participants, no woman had a BMI below 18.5 kg/m²; consequently, the WHO underweight category was not represented in the analyses. As there are no accepted limits for BRI and ABSI in the literature, a separate analysis was performed by dividing the sample into tertiles based on the values found within the sample.

Data collection

Our study was conducted with patients who visited the Gynaecology Outpatient Clinic at Ankara Etlik City Hospital between 9 December 2024 and 15 January 2026. Sociodemographic data (age, educational level, marital status, smoking status, presence of chronic diseases, gravida-parity) were collected using a structured questionnaire.

International Physical Activity Questionnaire (IPAQ) Internat

The IPAQ short form was used to assess physical activity levels. The scale consists of seven questions that evaluate walking, moderate and vigorous physical activity, and sedentary behaviour in individuals aged 15–69. MET-minutes/week were computed using the standard IPAQ scoring protocol by multiplying the duration (minutes) and frequency (days/week) of each activity by its assigned MET intensity value: 3.3 METs for walking, 4.0 METs for moderate-intensity activity, and 8.0 METs for vigorous-intensity activity. The total weekly MET-minutes/week was obtained by summing across the three activity types. Participants were then classified into three categories according to IPAQ criteria: low (< 600 MET-min/week), moderate (600–3000 MET-min/week), and high (> 3000 MET-min/week) activity. The validity and reliability study of the scale in Turkish was conducted by Öztürk et al. [21].

Menopause-Specific Quality of Life Scale (MENQOL)

The Menopause-Specific Quality of Life Scale (MENQOL) was used to assess menopausal symptom burden – that is, the presence of menopausal symptoms and the degree to which each symptom bothered the participant. MENQOL is a 29-item Likert-type scale that assesses menopausal symptoms under four subscales: vasomotor (3 items), psychosocial (7 items), physical (17 items), and sexual (3 items). For each item, participants first indicated whether they experienced the symptom; if they did, they rated how much it bothered them on a scale from 0 (did not bother me at all) to 6 (bothered me extremely). Subscale scores were calculated using the mean values of the relevant items; a higher score indicates greater menopausal symptom burden, reflecting the combined impact of symptom presence and the extent to which the symptom bothered the woman. The Turkish version of the MENQOL, with established validity and reliability, was administered to participants [22].

Anthropometric measurements

Height was measured using a wall-mounted stadiometer with 1mm precision, and body weight was measured using a calibrated digital scale with 0.1 kg precision. WC was measured in standing participants, in a fasting state, at the level of the umbilicus using a non-stretchable tape measure with 1 mm precision.

BMI was calculated using the formula weight (kg) / height² (m²). Statistical analysis

In comparing demographic and clinical characteristics according to BMI groups, the one-way ANOVA test was used for continuous variables. For significant ANOVA results, pairwise comparisons between groups were performed using the Scheffe multiple comparison test. The chi-square test was used to compare the distribution of categorical variables between groups.

When comparing MENQOL item scores and subscale means according to BMI groups (normal weight, overweight, obese), the non-parametric Kruskal-Wallis test was applied due to the data not meeting the assumption of normal distribution. Post hoc pairwise comparisons following significant Kruskal-Wallis results were performed using the Dunn test with Bonferroni correction. The relationship between BMI, BRI and ABSI and menopausal symptoms in perimenopausal and menopausal groups was examined using crude and adjusted multiple linear regression models, taking into account age, smoking status, history of chronic disease, BMI and physical activity. When BMI itself was the exposure of interest, it was not simultaneously entered as a covariate; BMI was only included as a covariate in the adjusted models for BRI and ABSI. In the regression models, physical activity was entered as an ordinal variable coded 1 (low), 2 (moderate), and 3 (high) based on IPAQ categories. Prior to model interpretation, standard regression diagnostics were performed, including examination of residual distributions, identification of influential observations via Cook’s distance, and assessment of multicollinearity using variance inflation factors (VIF). No substantial violations were identified. A significance level of p < 0.05 was accepted in all analyses. All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

Results

This study evaluated a total of 638 women (319 perimenopausal, 319 menopausal). When comparing the demographic and clinical characteristics of normal weight, overweight, and obese women in the perimenopausal and menopausal groups, the age distribution was found to be similar across subgroups in both groups; however, the age of the menopausal group was significantly higher than that of the perimenopausal group. (p < 0.001). BMI and WC values did not show a significant difference between the menopause and perimenopause groups (p values were 0.196 and 0.528, respectively), but a significant difference was observed between subgroups (p < 0.001 for all subgroup comparisons). BRI and ABSI values also showed significant differences according to BMI categories in both the perimenopausal and menopausal groups (p < 0.001). No significant differences were found between the groups in terms of gravidity, parity, education level, employment status, marital status, chronic disease, smoking status, and physical activity level (Table 1).

When examining the MENQOL subscales, vasomotor symptom scores were found to be higher in obese women in the perimenopausal group compared to normal weight and overweight women (hot flushes p = 0.003; night sweats p < 0.001; sweating p < 0.001). Similarly, in the menopause group, vasomotor symptoms were significantly higher in the obese group (hot flushes p = 0.004; night sweats p = 0.036; sweating p = 0.004). The vasomotor subscale total score showed significant differences according to BMI categories in both periods (perimenopause p < 0.001; menopause p < 0.001). In terms of psychosocial symptoms, feeling anxious or nervous (p = 0.008), poor memory (p = 0.001), feeling depressed (p = 0.001) and being impatient with other people (p = 0.015) scores were significantly higher in obese women in the perimenopausal group. In the menopause group, accomplishing less than I used to (p = 0.003) and being impatient with other people (p = 0.037) were found to be higher in the obese group. The total score for the psychosocial subscale was significant in both periods (perimenopause p < 0.001; menopause p < 0.001) (Table 2).

When physical symptoms were examined, the perimenopause group showed aching in muscles and joints (p = 0.014), difficulty sleeping (p < 0.001), decrease in stamina (p = 0.009), feeling a lack of energy (p = 0.048), low backache (p = 0.013) and involuntary urination when laughing or coughing (p < 0.001) were significantly higher in the obese group. In the menopause group, flatulence (wind) or gas pains (p < 0.001), aching in muscles and joints (p = 0.025), difficulty sleeping (p = 0.003), aches in back of neck or head (p = 0.003), decrease in stamina (p < 0.001), dry skin (p < 0.001), feeling bloated (p = 0.001), low backache (p = 0.009) and involuntary urination when laughing or coughing (p = 0.009) a significant difference was found between the groups. The sum of the physical subscale is significant in both periods (perimenopause p = 0.015; menopause p < 0.001). In terms of sexual symptoms, vaginal dryness during intercourse differed significantly across BMI groups in the perimenopausal group (p = 0.017), with overweight women scoring lower than both normal-weight and obese women, whose mean values were comparable, and the sexual subscale total score showed a significant difference (p < 0.001). The menopause group showed significant differences in vaginal dryness during intercourse (p = 0.003) and total sexual subscale score (p = 0.013). The total presence of symptoms was significant in both periods according to BMI categories (p < 0.001 for both groups) (Table 2).

Discussion

In this cross-sectional study, the relationship between BMI, BRI and ABSI and the severity of menopausal symptoms in perimenopausal and menopausal women aged 44–56 was investigated. The main findings of the study can be summarised as follows: all three indices (BMI, BRI, ABSI) showed an independent and significant positive correlation with the severity of menopausal symptoms, with ABSI having the highest correlation coefficient, followed by BRI and BMI. Although significant elevations were detected in all MENQOL subscales among obese women, overweight women paradoxically exhibited lower symptom scores compared to normal-weight women, and physical activity level was found to have a weak but significant negative relationship with symptom burden.

Although our study found a significant positive correlation between BMI and total MENQOL score, it is noteworthy that this correlation was markedly weaker compared to BRI and ABSI. The relationship between BMI and vasomotor symptoms has long been debated in the literature. A meta-analysis has reported that a high BMI increases the risk of hot flushes [2]; nevertheless, the contradiction between the thermoregulation model and the thin hypothesis remains relevant [1, 5]. According to the thermoregulation model, increased adipose tissue impedes heat distribution and exacerbates symptoms. The thin hypothesis, however, suggests that adipose tissue functions as a peripheral source of oestrogen and may therefore alleviate the symptoms of obesity [1]. Our findings that overweight women exhibit lower MENQOL scores compared to normal-weight women appear to partially support the thin hypothesis. This pattern, in which an intermediate degree of adiposity is associated with lower symptom burden than either the lowest or highest weight category, may be considered an expression of what has been termed the “overweight paradox.” In other words, the modest increase in peripheral adipose tissue in overweight women may provide a sufficient source of extra-gonadal oestrogen production — via aromatisation of androgens in adipose tissue — to partially buffer the symptomatic effects of ovarian oestrogen decline, without yet crossing the threshold at which the pro-inflammatory and thermoregulatory consequences of excess visceral fat predominate [1]. This interpretation is consistent with the thermoregulatory framework, in which a moderate degree of adipose-derived oestrogen may widen the thermoneutral zone sufficiently to reduce vasomotor event frequency. The complex interplay between adipose tissue volume, oestrogen production, adipokine secretion, and hypothalamic thermoregulation means that the relationship between BMI and menopausal symptoms is unlikely to be linear. Studies examining lipid profiles in relation to menopausal symptoms have similarly demonstrated non-linear patterns, with intermediate hormonal and metabolic states associated with differential symptom profiles [23, 24]. However, our study observed that the obese group had the highest symptom severity, suggesting that beyond a certain level of adiposity, the inflammatory and thermoregulatory burden of visceral fat outweighs any oestrogenic benefit. One possible interpretation of this pattern is that BMI may have a non-linear relationship with menopausal symptoms, though the cross-sectional design of this study does not allow this to be confirmed. Moreover, BMI reflects total body weight without distinguishing between muscle mass and fat mass; it cannot differentiate between visceral and subcutaneous fat distribution. The redistribution of fat triggered by oestrogen deficiency during the menopausal transition accumulates in the central region, and this process does not always correspond to changes in BMI [1].

BRI normalises the WC using a geometric ellipse model and specifically measures central adiposity [13]. In our study, BRI showed a moderate correlation with the total MENQOL score. In the tertile analysis of BRI, higher scores were observed in all MENQOL subdimensions in the T3 group compared to T1. These findings support the observed association between waist circumference-based indices and menopausal symptoms as a reflection of central adiposity. Increased adipose tissue mass increases the release of inflammatory cytokines interleukin-6 and tumour necrosis factor-α, and we believe that this situation may trigger vasomotor symptoms by lowering the hypothalamic thermoregulatory threshold, explaining the relationship between central adiposity and symptom severity [1, 17–19].

Another finding of our study is that ABSI showed stronger correlations with menopausal symptoms than BRI and BMI in both correlation analysis and regression models. ABSI calculates waist circumference by mathematically adjusting for BMI and height; it thus represents “pure” central adiposity independent of BMI [14]. Krakauer et al. demonstrated in their original study that ABSI is an independent predictor of mortality, independent of BMI [14]. ; subsequent studies have reported superior predictive validity in terms of diabetes, hypertension and cardiometabolic risk [16, 25]. However, to our knowledge, our study is the first to investigate the relationship between ABSI and menopausal symptoms. Findings in the ABSI tertile analysis show that the mean vasomotor symptom score is significantly higher in the T3 group in both perimenopause and menopause. This difference is much more pronounced compared to BMI comparisons. This finding is consistent with the study by Thurston et al., which showed that an increase in abdominal adiposity correlates with an increase in hot flushes [26]. Based on the findings from our study, we believe that an increase in WC and its associated index, ABSI, is significantly associated with menopausal symptoms beyond what is captured by BMI; whether this association reflects a causal or directional relationship requires confirmation from longitudinal studies.

In regression analysis, the beta coefficient for BRI in the perimenopausal group (β=+0.241) was found to be slightly higher than that in the menopausal group (β=+0.222). A similar pattern was observed for ABSI, although a contrary finding was noted specifically for sexual symptoms. The relationship between ABSI and sexual symptoms was stronger in the menopause group compared to the perimenopause group. This finding suggests that with menopausal progression, oestrogen deficiency gains prominence in vaginal atrophy and sexual dysfunction. Ongoing ovarian activity during perimenopause may partially counterbalance this effect.

In all regression analysis models, it was observed that adjustments made for age, smoking status, history of chronic disease, BMI (where applicable) and physical activity did not significantly alter the beta coefficients. This finding indicates that the effect of body composition indices on the burden of menopausal symptoms is largely independent of the aforementioned sociodemographic factors. Indeed, we believe that the fact that no significant differences were found across the BMI subgroups in terms of smoking status, chronic disease history, or physical activity distribution supports our view. Age, although significantly different between the perimenopausal and menopausal groups, did not differ across BMI subgroups within either stage, and its influence in the regression models was further accounted for through statistical adjustment.

In our study, physical activity level showed a negative correlation with the total MENQOL score. The effect of exercise on menopausal symptoms is debated in the literature; while some studies show that regular physical activity reduces the severity of hot flushes [6], , others have been unable to detect a significant effect or have reported that exercise may be a trigger for acute hot flushes [11, 12]. A study involving Turkish women also showed that the relationship between exercise and menopausal symptoms is partially shaped by BMI [9]. In our study, the fact that the effect of physical activity did not significantly alter the coefficients of body composition indices in the adjusted regression models leads us to believe that the independent effect of this variable on menopausal symptoms is limited.

The association between central adiposity and menopausal symptom burden may also be partially mediated by metabolic and biochemical pathways that were not directly measured in this study, but which deserve consideration in interpreting our findings. The menopausal transition is characterised by significant alterations in lipid metabolism, including increases in total cholesterol, low-density lipoprotein (LDL) cholesterol, and triglycerides, alongside reductions in high-density lipoprotein (HDL) cholesterol; these changes are closely linked to the decline in oestrogen and contribute to increased cardiovascular risk in postmenopausal women [23]. Importantly, prior research has demonstrated that lipid levels change significantly during the menopausal transition and may themselves be associated with symptom burden, suggesting that the metabolic milieu of the menopausal period is not independent of its symptomatic presentation [23]. Furthermore, clinical evidence indicates that menopausal symptom severity may differ according to menopause type and is associated with specific lipid parameter patterns, underscoring the importance of metabolic context in symptom assessment [24]. In this framework, central adiposity as captured by BRI and ABSI may exert its effect on menopausal symptoms not only through direct thermoregulatory mechanisms, but also through its well-established association with dyslipidaemia and insulin resistance. Visceral fat accumulation promotes hepatic lipid overproduction, reduces insulin sensitivity, and drives a pro-atherogenic lipid profile; these metabolic disturbances may in turn amplify the neuroendocrine dysregulation underlying vasomotor and psychosocial symptoms. The stronger association observed for ABSI relative to BMI may therefore reflect its ability to capture the metabolically active visceral fat depot that drives both lipid dysregulation and thermoregulatory instability. Future studies incorporating fasting lipid profiles, insulin resistance indices, and anthropometric measures simultaneously would allow mediation analyses to formally disentangle these overlapping pathways.

We believe that increased adiposity during the menopausal period is clinically significant not only in terms of metabolic and cardiovascular diseases but also in terms of the severity of menopausal symptoms. The findings of this study suggest that an increase in BRI and ABSI values, which reflect body fat distribution, may be associated with an increase in menopausal symptoms. Obesity is a well-known risk factor for HT, DM, and cardiovascular diseases and is seen as a factor that may increase symptom burden in women during the menopausal period. Whether weight management strategies can reduce menopausal symptom burden alongside their established benefits for chronic disease risk cannot be determined from the present cross-sectional data. These associations are hypothesis-generating and suggest that lifestyle interventions targeting central adiposity during the menopausal period may warrant evaluation in future longitudinal and interventional studies.

Certain methodological limitations of the study should be considered. The cross-sectional design does not allow for establishing a causal relationship; whether changes in body composition increase symptom burden, or whether high symptom burden (particularly through sleep disturbance and reduced energy) accelerates central adiposity by limiting physical activity can only be answered by longitudinal studies. A further limitation of this study is the absence of hormonal measurements, including serum estradiol, FSH, and LH levels. Although menopausal status was defined using validated STRAW criteria based on menstrual history, the lack of endocrine data prevents direct quantification of the degree of oestrogen deficiency in individual participants. An important additional limitation is the absence of biochemical and metabolic measurements. No data were collected on fasting lipid profiles (total cholesterol, LDL, HDL, triglycerides), fasting glucose, or insulin resistance indices such as HOMA-IR. Given that the menopausal transition is associated with significant dyslipidaemia and insulin resistance, and that these metabolic disturbances may act as intermediary pathways linking central adiposity to menopausal symptom severity, their absence limits the mechanistic interpretation of our findings. Future studies should incorporate biochemical and metabolic markers alongside anthropometric indices to allow mediation analyses examining whether the observed associations between BRI/ABSI and MENQOL scores are partly explained by lipid dysregulation or insulin resistance. Consequently, the proposed mechanistic pathways — whereby oestrogen decline drives central fat redistribution, upregulates pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-6 and TNF-α, and thereby lowers the hypothalamic thermoregulatory threshold — cannot be empirically tested within the present dataset and should be regarded as biologically plausible interpretations rather than demonstrated causal mechanisms. Future studies incorporating hormonal assays alongside anthropometric indices would allow mediation analyses to formally evaluate whether the association between central adiposity and menopausal symptom burden is partially mediated by circulating oestrogen levels.

Finally, cut-off points for BRI and ABSI have not yet been standardised internationally; the tertile boundaries used in this study are sample-specific and have limited generalisability to different populations.

Our findings suggest several directions that may be worth exploring in future work. First, prospective longitudinal cohorts could help clarify the temporal sequence between changes in central adiposity and changes in menopausal symptoms, and indicate whether trajectories in BRI and ABSI are paralleled by changes in symptom burden. Second, interventional studies in which weight-management or exercise programmes are targeted at central adiposity, with menopausal symptoms assessed as an outcome, may help establish whether the associations observed here can be modified. Third, studies that incorporate hormonal (estradiol, FSH, LH), metabolic (HOMA-IR, fasting lipid profile), and inflammatory (IL-6, TNF-α, leptin) markers alongside anthropometric indices could contribute to mediation analyses exploring the biological pathways that may link central adiposity to vasomotor, psychosocial, physical, and sexual symptom domains. Fourth, work in larger and more diverse populations would be valuable for refining BRI and ABSI cut-off values in midlife women, since the tertile boundaries used here are sample-specific. Finally, further pragmatic studies could examine the added clinical value of BRI and ABSI alongside BMI in routine menopause care, including their potential role in identifying women who might benefit from earlier counselling.

Conclusions

In this cross-sectional study, BRI and ABSI showed independent and significant associations with menopausal symptom burden measured by MENQOL in perimenopausal and menopausal women, with ABSI demonstrating stronger correlations than BMI and BRI in this dataset; these associations were preserved after adjustment for age, smoking status, history of chronic disease, BMI (in BRI and ABSI models), and physical activity. These findings suggest that indices reflecting central adiposity more specifically may provide clinically relevant information alongside BMI in the assessment of menopausal women. Prospective longitudinal studies are required to elucidate the underlying physiological mechanisms of the observed associations and to test causal directionality.
