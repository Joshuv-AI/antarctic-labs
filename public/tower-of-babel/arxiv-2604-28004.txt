Minimal Parametric Networks in Hyperspaces and their Properties
Arsen Galstyan

Abstract
1 1 1 The work of Arsen Galstyan was carried out at Harbin Institute of Technology with the support of the Postdoctoral Research Start-up Funds (Funding card number: AUGA5710026125). This work investigates minimal parametric networks in hyperspaces of closed subsets of metric spaces endowed with the Hausdorff distance. It is shown that the problems of finding such networks are nontrivial only within finiteness classes, where all Hausdorff distances between elements are finite. It is demonstrated that when studying the properties of minimal parametric networks, it is convenient to view their interior vertices as solutions of the Fermat–Steiner problem on the adjacent vertices. In this connection, already within the framework of the Fermat–Steiner problem, the structure of solution classes in hyperspaces of closed subsets of metric spaces is described. Results on the existence of d d -far points in the case of convex boundary sets are also generalized. Namely, conditions are shown under which a more general construction for realizing one-sided Hausdorff distances holds, which simplifies the objective functional (the sum of such distances).

Introduction

The Steiner problem, which asks for a network of minimal total length connecting a given finite set of points in a metric space, is a classical optimization problem with numerous applications in science and engineering (see, e.g.,
Hwang
,
Review
,
Cheng
). The concept of a minimal parametric network (see Section
3.1
) generalizes this problem by fixing the topology (the graph) of the network and optimizing the positions of its vertices in the ambient space (see, e.g.,
IT
,
Branching
,
Borodin
,
Tropin
).

From the very first formulation by Pierre de Fermat on the vertices of a triangle up to the works of the end of the last century, the Steiner problem was considered exclusively in finite-dimensional normed spaces (see, e.g.,
Hwang
,
Du
,
Bern
,
Cieslik
). In
IT
, Ivanov and Tuzhilin studied the Steiner problem on a connected complete Riemannian manifold. Moreover, in
IT
they considered the Steiner problem within the framework of searching for minimal parametric networks. In
Borodin
and
Bednov
, Borodin, Bednov and Strelkova studied the existence of minimal networks in Banach spaces. In recent years, Ivanov, Tuzhilin, Tropin and Galstyan wrote a series of papers investigating minimal parametric networks in hyperspaces (see Definition
1
) of compact subsets of finite-dimensional normed spaces (see
Tropin
,
ITT
,
Tropin_2
,
Gals_1
,
Gals_2
). Moreover, some statements in these works were formulated in the context of hyperspaces of compact sets of metric spaces or proper metric spaces.

The theory of hyperspaces, which originated at the beginning of the 20th century thanks to the fundamental works of Hausdorff and Vietoris, has grown into a vast area of knowledge closely related to topology, functional analysis, and geometric measure theory (see, e.g.,
Nadler
,
Blackburn
). The seminal 1942 paper of Kelley
Kelley
generated sustained interest in hyperspaces, and subsequent investigations showed (see, e.g., the book
SetAn
) that these spaces provide a natural framework for studying set-valued analysis and variational problems.

The present paper continues and generalizes the research initiated in
ITT
and
Gals_1
. In Section
3
, the problems of finding a minimal parametric network and a Steiner network are formulated in the context of extended metric spaces. An extended metric space differs from an ordinary metric space in that infinite distances are allowed. The problems are intentionally stated in such a space because the main goal of this work is to extend a number of results from
ITT
and
Gals_1
to hyperspaces of unbounded subsets, where the Hausdorff distance
d_{H}
(see Definitions
4
and
5
) can be equal to
\infty
. However, it is shown in Section
3
that if the initial set of points to be connected by a minimal (parametric) network contains points that are at infinite distance from each other, then the problem of finding a minimal network becomes trivial. Namely, every network connecting such a set has length
\infty
(see Proposition
8
). On the other hand, if all pairwise distances in the initial set are finite, then the infimum of network lengths is also finite (see Proposition
9
), and in this case the problem of finding a minimal network becomes meaningful. Therefore, for the case of hyperspaces of unbounded subsets, the problems of finding minimal networks should be considered within finiteness classes (see Definition
23
) of such hyperspaces. We denote the finiteness class of a hyperspace
\mathcal{P}_{*}(X)
everywhere by
\mathcal{P}_{*}^{f}(X)
.

In Subsection
3.3
, a connection is also established between the particular Fermat–Steiner problem (see Definition
26
) and the problem of finding a minimal (not necessarily parametric) network. Namely, it is shown there that each interior vertex (see Definition
15
) of a minimal network can be regarded as a solution of the Fermat–Steiner problem on its adjacent vertices (see Proposition
12
). Thus, by discovering properties of solutions of the Fermat–Steiner problem in a specific type of metric spaces, we automatically extend these properties to all interior vertices of all minimal parametric networks, including minimal Steiner trees. This is why, even though properties of solutions of the Fermat–Steiner problem are studied in Section
4
, that section is entitled “Properties of Internal Vertices of Minimal Parametric Networks in Hyperspaces”. The main results of the paper are presented in Section
4
.

Each solution
K
of the Fermat–Steiner problem on a family of subsets
\mathcal{M}=\{M_{1},\ldots,M_{n}\}
of a metric space
X
corresponds to its own vector
d=\bigl(d_{1}=d_{H}(K,M_{1}),\ldots,d_{n}=d_{H}(K,M_{n})\bigr)
. Different solutions
K_{1}
and
K_{2}
may correspond to the same vector
d
. Thus, for each vector
d
we obtain a whole class of solutions
\Sigma_{d}(\mathcal{M})
, partially ordered by inclusion.

Subsection
4.1
describes the structure of such classes
\Sigma_{d}(\mathcal{M})
. Proposition
13
on the membership of intermediate subsets in the same solution class
\Sigma_{d}(\mathcal{M})
generalizes Assertion 3.4 from
ITT
to the case of hyperspaces of closed subsets of a metric space. Proposition
14
on the existence of a greatest element
K_{d}
in
\Sigma_{d}(\mathcal{M})
also extends the corresponding result Assertion 3.5 from
ITT
to the case of hyperspaces of closed subsets of a metric space. Proposition
16
on the existence of a minimal element in
\Sigma_{d}(\mathcal{M})
generalizes the result Assertion 3.1 from
ITT
to the case of hyperspaces of closed subsets of a finite-dimensional normed space.

Subsection
4.2
is devoted to a generalization of the result of Theorem 3 from
Gals_1
on the existence of
d
-far points (see Definition
27
) for solutions of the Fermat–Steiner problem. For finite-dimensional normed spaces and compact convex boundary sets, it was proved in
Gals_1
that there exists an index
i
and a point
x\in M_{i}
such that
U_{d_{i}}(x)\cap K_{d}=\emptyset
, i.e.,
M_{i}
has a
d
-far point. This implies
d_{i}=\sup_{x\in M_{i}}|x\,K_{d}|
. In other words, at least one of the summands in
S_{\mathcal{M}}(K_{d})
is one-sided, determined by the distances from the boundary set to the solution.

The main goal of Subsection
4.2
is to extend this result to a much broader setting. We drop the assumptions of finite-dimensionality and compactness and work with arbitrary closed convex subsets of a (possibly infinite-dimensional) normed space. The principal result (Corollary
3
) states that if all
M_{i}
lie in a finiteness class of closed convex sets,
\Sigma_{d}(\mathcal{M})\neq\emptyset
, and a certain technical condition on
K_{d}
is satisfied, then, for any
K\in\Sigma_{d}(\mathcal{M})
, there exists an index
i
for which
d_{i}=\sup_{x\in M_{i}}|x\,K|
.

It turns out that in hyperspaces of closed convex subsets of a two-dimensional normed space, the technical condition on
K_{d}
from Corollary
3
is satisfied automatically. Thus, we obtain Corollary
4
. Also, the technical condition on
K_{d}
from Corollary
3
is satisfied automatically in hyperspaces of closed bounded convex subsets of a normed space. Therefore we also have Corollary
5
.

We also address the additional question of whether all the distances
d_{i}
can be “realized” from only one side:
d_{i}=\sup_{x\in M_{i}}|x\,K_{d}|
. Theorem
4.2
shows that, in any connected metric space, for the greatest element
K_{d}\in\Sigma_{d}(\mathcal{M})
, there exists an index
i
such that
d_{i}=\sup_{x\in K_{d}}|x\,M_{i}|
.

2.3
Hausdorff Distance

Definition 4

Given a metric space
X
, we endow
\mathcal{P}_{0}(X)
with the Hausdorff distance
d_{H}
defined as follows
\colon
for any
A,B\in\mathcal{P}_{0}(X)
, we put

d_{H}(A,B)=\inf\bigl\{r:A\subset B_{r}(B),\,B\subset B_{r}(A)\bigr\}.

(30)

It is also well known that there is an equivalent definition of the Hausdorff distance (see, for example, the work
(
TuzLections
, Proposition 5.1)
).

Definition 5

d_{H}(A,B)=\max\bigl\{\sup\limits_{a\in A}|a\,B|,\,\sup\limits_{b\in B}|b\,A|\bigr\}.

(31)

Notice that
d_{H}
is non-negative, symmetric, and satisfies the triangle inequality, but can be zero for
A\neq B
and can be infinite.

Lemma 8

Let
X
be a metric spaces and
A,B\in\mathcal{P}_{0}(X).
If
|A\,B|=d_{H}(A,B)
then

\sup\limits_{a\in A}|a\,B|=\sup\limits_{b\in B}|b\,A|.

(32)

Proof

We have
\inf\limits_{a\in A}|a\,B|=|A\,B|=d_{H}(A,B)=\max\bigl\{\sup\limits_{a\in A}|a\,B|,\,\sup\limits_{b\in B}|b\,A|\bigr\}.
But

\inf\limits_{a\in A}|a\,B|=\inf\limits_{a\in A}\inf\limits_{b\in B}|a\,b|\leq\sup\limits_{a\in A}\inf\limits_{b\in B}|a\,b|=\sup\limits_{a\in A}|a\,B|

(33)

and

\inf\limits_{a\in A}|a\,B|=\inf\limits_{b\in B}\inf\limits_{a\in A}|a\,b|\leq\sup\limits_{b\in B}\inf\limits_{a\in A}|a\,b|=\sup\limits_{b\in B}|b\,A|.

(34)

Hence,
|A\,B|=\sup\limits_{a\in A}|a\,B|=\sup\limits_{b\in B}|b\,A|.
∎

M\in\mathcal{P}_{0}(X)
is called an existence set if for every
x\in X
we have
P_{M}(x)\neq\emptyset
.

Proposition 6

In a finite-dimensional real normed space, a closed nonempty subset is an existence set.

Proof

Let
X
be a finite-dimensional real normed space,
M\in\mathcal{P}_{\operatorname{Cl}}(X)
and
x\in X
. Consider a sequence
\{y_{i}\}\subset M
such that

|x\,y_{i}|\rightarrow|x\,M|

(38)

as
i\rightarrow\infty
, and the numerical sequence
\bigl\{|x\,y_{i}|\bigr\}=\bigl\{||y_{i}-x||\bigr\}
is decreasing. Since
\bigl\{||y_{i}-x||\bigr\}
is decreasing, we have

||y_{i}||\leq||y_{i}-x||+||x||\leq||y_{1}-x||+||x||,

(39)

that is, sequence
\{y_{i}\}
is bounded. In a finite-dimensional real normed space, every bounded sequence contains a convergent subsequence. Without loss of generality, we may assume that
\{y_{i}\}
converges to an element
y
. Since
M
is closed, we have
y\in M
, and by the continuity of the distance function in a metric space we obtain
|x\,y_{i}|\rightarrow|x\,y|
. Hence, by (
38
), we obtain
|x\,y|=|x\,M|
. Consequently,
y\in P_{M}(x)
.∎

Note that in the infinite-dimensional case, a closed nonempty subset need not be an existence set. Specifically, the following result is known.

Theorem 2.3
(
(
Balag
, Corollary 1)
)

Let
X
be a topological space, and let
C(X)
be the infinite-dimensional real normed space of all bounded continuous functions on
X
. There exists a closed bounded convex body
M
in
C(X)
such that for every
f\in C(X)\setminus M
we have
P_{M}(f)=\emptyset
.

3
Minimal Parametric Network Problem

3.1
Minimal Networks

In this subsection, we recall the necessary concepts from graph theory and fix the corresponding notation. More detailed information on graph theory can be found, for example, in
Graphs
.

Definition 8

A simple graph is a pair
(V,E)
consisting of a finite set
V
and a set
E
of two-element subsets
\{u,v\}\subset V
. The elements of
V
are called vertices of the graph, and the elements of
E
are called edges of the graph. If
e=\{u,v\}\in E
, then the vertices
u
and
v
are said to be neighbors or adjacent and are joined by the edge
e
.

Since we will only consider simple graphs henceforth, the word “simple” will be omitted.

Throughout, we will only consider undirected graphs, so we look at each edge
\{u,v\}\in E
as an unordered pair of vertices, that is, we assume that
\{u,v\}
and
\{v,u\}
are the same edge.

We also emphasize that each edge is precisely a two-element set, that is, if
\{u,v\}
is an edge of a graph, then
u\neq v.

Definition 9

The number of vertices adjacent to a given vertex
u
is called the degree of the vertex
u
and is denoted by
\operatorname{deg}(u)
.

Definition 10

A sequence of edges
\{v_{1},v_{2}\},\{v_{2},v_{3}\},\{v_{3},v_{4}\},\ldots,\{v_{n},v_{n+1}\}
of a graph is called a route connecting the vertices
v_{1}
and
v_{n+1}
, or a
v_{1}
-
v_{n+1}
route.

Definition 11

A graph is called connected if any two distinct vertices can be connected by a route.

Definition 12

Let
G=(V,E)
be a connected graph, and let
\mathcal{A}\subset V
. Then
G
is said to join
\mathcal{A}
. The vertices in
\mathcal{A}
are called boundary vertices, the set
\mathcal{A}
itself is called the boundary of the graph, and the vertices in
V\setminus\mathcal{A}
are called interior vertices.

Thus, the boundary of a graph is some distinguished and fixed subset of its vertices. We will henceforth denote the boundary of the graph
G
by
\partial G
.

Definition 13

Let
X
be an arbitrary set. A function
d\colon X\times X\rightarrow[0,\infty]
is called an extended metric if for any
x,y,z\in X
the following conditions hold:

(1)

Non-negativity and non-degeneracy:
d(x,y)\geq 0,
and
d(x,y)=0
if and only if
x=y.

(2)

Symmetry:
d(x,y)=d(y,x).

(3)

Triangle inequality:
d(x,z)\leq d(x,y)+d(y,z).

In this case, the set
X
equipped with such a function
d
is called an extended metric space.

We emphasize that, in our understanding, any metric space is an extended metric space.

As in the case of a metric space, the distance between two points
a,b
of an extended metric space
X
will be denoted by
|a\,b|
instead of
d(a,b)
.

Definition 14

Let
G=(V,E)
be a connected graph. A network of the type
G
in an extended metric space
X
is an arbitrary mapping
g\colon V\rightarrow X
. The graph
G
is also called the parameterizing graph of
g
.

Note that the mapping
g
induces a corresponding mapping on the edges of the graph
G
by the rule
g_{e}\colon\{v_{i},v_{j}\}\mapsto\bigl\{g(v_{i}),g(v_{j})\bigr\}
.

Definition 15

The elements of
g(V)
are called vertices of the network, and the elements of
g_{e}(E)
are called edges of the network. The elements of
g(\partial G)
are called boundary vertices of the network, and the elements of
g(V\setminus\partial G)
are called interior vertices of the network. If
u,v\in V
were adjacent vertices of the graph
G
then we call
g(u),g(v)
adjacent vertices of the network
g
.

Definition 16

An edge
\bigl\{g(v_{i}),g(v_{j})\bigr\}
of the network
g
is called degenerate if
g(v_{i})=g(v_{j});
otherwise, the edge is called non-degenerate. A network all of whose edges are non-degenerate will also be called non-degenerate.

The length of an edge
\bigl\{g(v_{i}),g(v_{j})\bigr\}
of the network
g
is defined as the distance
\bigl|g(v_{i})\,g(v_{j})\bigr|
, and the length of the network
g
is defined as the sum of the lengths of all its edges:

|g|:=\sum\limits_{\{v_{i},v_{j}\}\in E}\bigl|g(v_{i})\,g(v_{j})\bigr|.

(40)

Note that since
X
is an extended metric space, the length of an edge, and hence the length of a network in
X
, may be equal to
\infty
.

Let a boundary
\partial G
be a subset of an extended metric space
X
. We shall assume that in this case all networks
g
satisfy the following condition:
the restriction of
g
to
\partial G
is the identity mapping
. Thus, each such network
g
is uniquely determined by the images of the interior vertices of the parametrizing graph
G
.

Definition 17

Graphs
G=(V,E)
and
H=(W,F)
are called isomorphic if there exists a bijective map
\phi\colon V\rightarrow W
such that
\{u,v\}\in E
if and only if
\bigl\{\phi(u),\phi(v)\bigr\}\in F
. Such a mapping
\phi
is called an isomorphism of the graphs
G
and
H
.

Definition 18

Let a connected graph
G_{1}=(V_{1},E_{1})
parameterizing a network
g_{1}
and a connected graph
G_{2}=(V_{2},E_{2})
parameterizing a network
g_{2}
be given and
\mathcal{A}=\partial G_{1}=\partial G_{2}
. These networks are said to be of the same type
(
parameter
)
, say
G_{1}
, if there exists an isomorphism
\phi\colon V_{1}\rightarrow V_{2}
between the graphs
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
which is the identity on
\mathcal{A}
.

For a fixed boundary
\mathcal{A}
in an extended metric space
X
, consider a connected graph
G=(V,E)
joining
\mathcal{A}
, that is
\partial G=\mathcal{A}\subset X
. Denote by
[G,\mathcal{A}]
the set of all networks of this type
G
in the extended metric space
X
. By the convention made above, all networks
g\in[G,\mathcal{A}]
satisfy the condition
g\big|_{\partial G=\mathcal{A}}=\text{id}
.

Definition 19

A network from
[G,\mathcal{A}]
that has the smallest possible length among all networks in
[G,\mathcal{A}]
is called a minimal parametric network of type
G
joining
\mathcal{A}
.

A minimal parametric network does not always exist. Nevertheless, the infimum of the quantities
|g|
over all networks
g\in[G,\mathcal{A}]
always exists.

Definition 20

The quantity

\operatorname{mpn}[G,\mathcal{A}]=\inf\limits_{g\in[G,\mathcal{A}]}|g|

(41)

is called the length of the minimal parametric network of type
G
joining
\mathcal{A}
.

Definition 21

The length of the minimal Steiner tree joining a subset
\mathcal{A}
is the value

\operatorname{smt}(\mathcal{A})=\inf\limits_{G\colon\partial G=\mathcal{A}}\operatorname{mpn}[G,\mathcal{A}].

(42)

Proposition 7

Let
X
be an extended metric space and
\mathcal{A}\subset X
. If there exist a graph
G
joining
\mathcal{A}
and a network
g\in[G,\mathcal{A}]
such that
|g|=\operatorname{smt}(\mathcal{A})
, then there also exist a graph
G^{\prime}
joining
\mathcal{A}
and a non-degenerate network
g^{\prime}\in[G^{\prime},\mathcal{A}]
such that
|g^{\prime}|=\operatorname{smt}(\mathcal{A})
.

Proof

The boundary
\mathcal{A}
of a graph
G=(V,E)
is a subset of the space
X
, so all its elements are distinct by definition. By convention,
g|_{\mathcal{A}}=\text{id}
. Therefore, if a network
g
is degenerate, that is, if there exist adjacent vertices
u,v\in V
such that
g(u)=g(v)
, then at least one of the vertices
u
or
v
is an interior vertex of the graph
G
.

Without loss of generality, let
u
be interior. We construct a graph
G^{\prime}
by means of the following algorithm.

(1)

Replace all edges of the form
\{u,x\}
in
E
with
x\neq v
by edges
\{v,x\}
;

(2)

Remove from
E
the edge joining the vertices
u
and
v
, and then remove the vertex
u
from
V
;

(3)

If there still remain
u^{\prime},v^{\prime}\in V
such that
g(u^{\prime})=g(v^{\prime})
, then determine which of these two vertices is interior, and return to step (1) of the algorithm.

The sets
V
and
E
transformed by the above algorithm are denoted by
V^{\prime}
and
E^{\prime}
, respectively. Thus, the result of the algorithm is a connected graph
G^{\prime}=(V^{\prime},E^{\prime})
joining the set
\mathcal{A}\subset X
. Note that the corresponding network
g^{\prime}\in[G^{\prime},\mathcal{A}]
is, by construction, a non-degenerate network, and
|g^{\prime}|=|g|=\operatorname{smt}(\mathcal{A}).
∎

The number of elements in
M
is denoted by
\#M
.

Definition 22

Let
X
be an extended metric space, let a graph
G=(V,E)
join
\mathcal{A}\subset X
, let a network
g\in[G,\mathcal{A}]
be non-degenerate and
|g|=\operatorname{smt}(\mathcal{A})
. If there does not exist a graph
G^{\prime}=(V^{\prime},E^{\prime})
joining
\mathcal{A}
and a non-degenerate network
g^{\prime}\in[G^{\prime},\mathcal{A}]
such that
|g^{\prime}|=\operatorname{smt}(\mathcal{A})
and
\#(V^{\prime}\setminus\mathcal{A})<\#(V\setminus\mathcal{A})
, then
g
is called a minimal Steiner tree joining
\mathcal{A}
.

Remark 3

Note that the parametrizing graph of a minimal Steiner tree
g
joining
\mathcal{A}
is a tree in case of
|g|<\infty
, that is, a connected graph without cycles. Indeed, if the parametrizing graph of a minimal Steiner tree has a cycle then one of the edges of this cycle could be removed, which would reduce the length of the original network. But then
g
would not be a minimal Steiner tree by the definition.

Proposition 8

Let
X
be an extended metric space,
\mathcal{A}
be a finite subset of
X
and
G
be a connected graph joining
\mathcal{A}
. If there exist points
a,b\in\mathcal{A}
such that
|a\,b|=\infty
, then
\operatorname{mpn}[G,\mathcal{A}]=\infty
.

Proof

Let
a,b\in\mathcal{A}
be such that
|a\,b|=\infty
. Since the graph
G
is connected, there exists a path
\{v_{0}=a,v_{1}\},\{v_{1},v_{2}\},\ldots,\{v_{m-1},v_{m}=b\}
connecting the vertices
a
and
b
. Consider an arbitrary network
g\in[G,\mathcal{A}]
. Recall that
g|_{\mathcal{A}}=\text{id}
. Hence, by the triangle inequality applied iteratively, we obtain

\infty=|a\,b|=\bigl|g(a)\,g(b)\bigr|\leq\sum_{i=1}^{m}\bigl|g(v_{i-1})\,g(v_{i})\bigr|.

(43)

Thus, any network
g\in[G,\mathcal{A}]
contains an edge of infinite length, hence
|g|=\infty
. Consequently,
\operatorname{mpn}[G,\mathcal{A}]=\inf\limits_{g\in[G,\mathcal{A}]}|g|=\infty.
∎

Remark 4

Thus, by Proposition
8
, if there exist
a,b\in\partial G=\mathcal{A}\subset X
such that
|a\,b|=\infty
then any network from
[G,\mathcal{A}]
is minimal parametric network of type
G
joining
\mathcal{A}
.

Since a graph
G
is arbitrary in Proposition
8
, we obtain

Corollary 1

Let
X
be an extended metric space and
\mathcal{A}
be a finite subset of
X
. If there exist points
a,b\in\mathcal{A}
such that
|a\,b|=\infty
, then
\operatorname{smt}(\mathcal{A})=\infty
.

Remark 5

Note that, since
g\big|_{\partial G=\mathcal{A}}=\text{id}
, then
\bigl[G=(\mathcal{A},E),\mathcal{A}\bigr]
consists of an unique network
g
, that is
\bigl[(\mathcal{A},E),\mathcal{A}\bigr]=\{g\}
. Thus, by Corollary
1
, if there exist
a,b\in\mathcal{A}\subset X
such that
|a\,b|=\infty
and a graph
G=(\mathcal{A},E)
is connected then, due to Definition
22
, the unique network
g
from
[G,\mathcal{A}]
is minimal Steiner tree joining
\mathcal{A}
. Consequently, henceforth we assume that in this case the graph
G=(\mathcal{A},E)
is also a tree.

Proposition 9

Let
X
be an extended metric space,
\mathcal{A}
be a finite subset of
X
and
G
be a connected graph joining
\mathcal{A}
. If for any points
a,b\in\mathcal{A}
we have
|a\,b|<\infty
, then
\operatorname{mpn}[G,\mathcal{A}]<\infty
.

Proof

Let us fix a vertex
a\in\mathcal{A}
and consider such network
g^{\prime}\in[G,\mathcal{A}]
:

g^{\prime}(v)=\left\{\begin{aligned} &v,&&\text{if }v\in\mathcal{A}\\
&a,&&\text{if }v\in V\setminus\mathcal{A}.\end{aligned}\right.

(44)

Since, by condition, for any points
a,b\in\mathcal{A}
we have
|a\,b|<\infty
, then
|g^{\prime}|<\infty
. So
\operatorname{mpn}[G,\mathcal{A}]=\inf\limits_{g\in[G,\mathcal{A}]}|g|\leq|g^{\prime}|<\infty.
∎

Note that on an extended metric space
X
one can naturally introduce a binary relation
\sim
, which is an equivalence relation:
x\sim y
if and only if
|x\,y|<\infty
.

Definition 23

The equivalence class of the relation
\sim
on
X
is called a finiteness class.

Remark 6

According to Remark
4
, Remark
5
, and Proposition
9
, the problem of finding a minimal parametric network or a minimum Steiner tree can be nontrivial only within a finiteness class. Moreover, each finiteness class is a metric space.
Therefore, henceforth all statements will be formulated only for metric spaces.

The following two propositions, as well as Remark
7
, can be found, for example, in
(
Bednov
, Lemma 1 and Lemma 2)
. However, there they are proved for the case when
X
is a real normed space. In fact, the proofs for the case when
X
is a metric space are identical. We present these proofs for completeness.

Proposition 10

Let
X
be a metric space,
\mathcal{A}
be a finite subset of
X
,
G
be a connected graph joining
\mathcal{A}
and
g\in[G,\mathcal{A}]
be a minimal Steiner tree joining
\mathcal{A}
. Then any interior vertex of
G
has degree at least
3
.

Proof

Suppose that in
G=(V,E)
there exists an interior vertex
v\in V
of degree less than
3
. The graph
G
is connected, so the case
\operatorname{deg}(v)=0
is impossible.

The case
\operatorname{deg}(v)=1
is also impossible. Indeed, if
\operatorname{deg}v=1
, then there exists exactly one vertex
u\in V
to which
v
is connected. Remove the edge
\{u,v\}
from
E
and remove the vertex
v
from
V
. Denote the resulting sets of edges and vertices by
E^{\prime}
and
V^{\prime}
, respectively. But then, due to the non-degeneracy of the network
g
, the new network
g^{\prime}=g|_{V^{\prime}}\in\bigl[G^{\prime}=(V^{\prime},E^{\prime}),\mathcal{A}\bigr]
will have a weight less than
|g|
, which contradicts the minimality of
g
.

Show that the case
\operatorname{deg}(v)=2
is also impossible. The vertex
v
is connected to exactly two vertices of
V
, which we denote by
u
and
w
. By the triangle inequality in the metric space
X
we have:

\bigl|g(u)\,g(w)\bigr|\leq|g(u)\,g(v)|+|g(v)\,g(w)|.

(45)

Remove the edges
\{u,v\}
,
\{v,w\}
from
E
, add the edge
\{u,w\}
to
E
, and remove the vertex
v
from
V
. Denote the resulting sets of edges and vertices by
E^{\prime}
and
V^{\prime}
, respectively. But then the network
g^{\prime}=g|_{V^{\prime}}\in\bigl[G^{\prime}=(V^{\prime},E^{\prime}),\mathcal{A}\bigr]
will have a weight not exceeding
|g|
. Again we arrive at a contradiction with the definition of a minimal Steiner tree joining
\mathcal{A}
. Consequently,
\operatorname{deg}(v)\geq 3
.∎

Proposition 11

Let
X
be a metric space, a connected graph
G=(V,E)
join
\mathcal{A}\subset X
,
\#\mathcal{A}=n<\infty
and
g\in[G,\mathcal{A}]
be a minimal Steiner tree joining
\mathcal{A}
. Then the graph
G
has at most
n-2
interior vertices.

Proof

Let
k
be the number of internal vertices of the graph
G
. Then
\#V=n+k
is the total number of vertices. Since, by Remark
3
,
G
is a tree, the fundamental relation

\#E=\#V-1=n+k-1

(46)

holds. It is also well known that the sum of the degrees of all vertices of any undirected graph equals twice the number of edges:

\sum\limits_{v\in V}\operatorname{deg}(v)=2\cdot\#E=2(n+k-1).

(47)

On the other hand, since the graph
G
is connected, for every vertex
v\in V
we have
\operatorname{deg}(v)\geq 1.
Moreover, if
v\in V\setminus\mathcal{A}
, then by Proposition
10
we have
\operatorname{deg}(v)\geq 3.
Hence

\sum\limits_{v\in V}\operatorname{deg}(v)\geq n+3k.

(48)

Consequently,
2(n+k-1)\geq n+3k.
Finally, we obtain
n-2\geq k.
∎

Remark 7

By Cayley’s formula, for every positive integer
m
, the number of trees on
m
labeled vertices is
m^{m-2}
. Consequently, in view of Proposition
11
, we can assume that in the expression
\inf\limits_{G\colon\partial G=\mathcal{A}}\operatorname{mpn}[G,\mathcal{A}]
the infimum is taken over a finite set of graphs. Therefore, the infimum can be replaced by a minimum, that is, we can henceforth write

\operatorname{smt}(\mathcal{A})=\min\limits_{G\colon\partial G=\mathcal{A}}\operatorname{mpn}[G,\mathcal{A}].

(49)

3.2
Existence Theorems

Definition 24

A proper metric space is a metric space in which every closed bounded subset is compact.

In the book
IT
, a proof is given of the existence of a minimal parametric network, as well as of a minimal Steiner tree, on a connected complete Riemannian manifold, see
(
IT
, Chapter 2, Paragraph 2, Section 2.1, Theorem 2.1 and Corollary 2.1)
. As noted by Tropin in his work
Tropin
, the idea of the proof of Theorem 2.1 (and also Corollary 2.1) from
IT
remains exactly the same if one replaces the Riemannian manifold by a proper metric space (indeed, by the Hopf–Rinow theorem, connected Riemannian manifold is a proper metric space if and only if it is a complete metric space). To be complete, we will provide this proof.

Theorem 3.1

Let
X
be a proper metric space and
\mathcal{A}
be a finite subset of
X
. Then for any connected graph
G=(V,E)
with boundary
\mathcal{A}
there exists a minimal parametric network of type
G
joining
\mathcal{A}
.

Proof

Consider a sequence
\{g_{n}\}\subset[G,\mathcal{A}]
such that
\lim\limits_{n\rightarrow\infty}|g_{n}|\rightarrow\operatorname{mpn}[G,\mathcal{A}]
. Without loss of generality, we may assume that the sequence
|g_{n}|
is decreasing. Hence,
\bigl\{|g_{n}|\bigr\}
is a bounded sequence.

For each vertex
v\in V
, the sequence
g_{n}
generates a corresponding sequence of points
\bigl\{g_{n}(v)\bigr\}\subset X
. In the case
v\in\mathcal{A}
, this sequence is constant, because
g_{n}(v)=v
. Fix a point
a\in\mathcal{A}
. Since the graph
G
is connected, for any
v\in V
there exists a route
\{v_{1}=v,v_{2}\},\{v_{2},v_{3}\},\ldots,\{v_{k-1},v_{k}=a\}
connecting
v
and
a
. Applying the triangle inequality successively, we obtain

\bigl|g_{n}(v)\,a\bigr|\leq\sum\limits_{i=1}^{k-1}\bigl|g(v_{i})\,g(v_{i+1})\bigr|\leq|g_{n}|\leq|g_{1}|.

(50)

Since the vertex
v
is arbitrary, then there exists a number
0<r<\infty
such that

\bigcup\limits_{v\in V}\bigcup\limits_{n=1}^{\infty}\bigl\{g_{n}(v)\bigr\}\subset B_{r}(a).

(51)

As
X
is a proper metric space,
B_{r}(a)
is compact. Therefore, for each
v\in V
, there exists a subsequence
\bigl\{g_{n_{m}}(v)\bigr\}\subset\bigl\{g_{n}(v)\bigr\}
converging to some point
v_{g}\in B_{r}(a)
.

Consider the network
\widetilde{g}\in[G,\mathcal{A}]
defined as follows:

\widetilde{g}\colon v\mapsto v_{g}.

(52)

If
a\in\mathcal{A}
, then we have
\widetilde{g}(a)=a
. We show that
|\widetilde{g}|=\operatorname{mpn}[G,\mathcal{A}]
.

The distance function is jointly continuous in both variables, so for any edge
\{u,v\}\in E
we have

\lim\limits_{m\rightarrow\infty}\bigl|g_{n_{m}}(u)\,g_{n_{m}}(v)\bigr|=\bigl|\widetilde{g}(u)\,\widetilde{g}(v)\bigr|.

(53)

Hence,

|\widetilde{g}|=\sum\limits_{\{u,v\}\in E}\bigl|\widetilde{g}(u)\,\widetilde{g}(v)\bigr|=\sum\limits_{\{u,v\}\in E}\lim\limits_{m\rightarrow\infty}\bigl|g_{n_{m}}(u)\,g_{n_{m}}(v)\bigr|=\\
=\lim\limits_{m\rightarrow\infty}\sum\limits_{\{u,v\}\in E}\bigl|g_{n_{m}}(u)\,g_{n_{m}}(v)\bigr|=\lim\limits_{m\rightarrow\infty}\bigl|g_{n_{m}}\bigr|=\operatorname{mpn}[G,\mathcal{A}].

(54)

Thus,
\widetilde{g}
is a minimal parametric network of type
G
joining
\mathcal{A}
.∎

From Theorem
3.1
and Remark
7
we obtain

Corollary 2

Let
X
be a proper metric space and let
\mathcal{A}
be a finite subset of
X
. Then there exists a minimal Steiner tree joining
\mathcal{A}
.

Theorem 3.2

A space
X
is a proper metric space if and only if
\mathcal{P}_{\operatorname{Cl},\operatorname{B}}(X)
is a proper metric space.

Proof

Let
\mathcal{P}_{\operatorname{Cl},\operatorname{B}}(X)
be a proper metric space. Note that
X\subset\mathcal{P}_{\operatorname{Cl},\operatorname{B}}(X)
. Consider the isometric embedding

\pi\colon X\rightarrow\mathcal{P}_{\operatorname{Cl},\operatorname{B}}(X);

(55)

\pi\colon x\mapsto\{x\};

(56)

d_{H}\bigl(\pi(x),\pi(y)\bigr)=|x\,y|,

(57)

for all
x,y\in X
. Let
A\subset X
be closed and bounded. Then
\pi(A)=\bigl\{\{x\}\colon x\in A\bigr\}
is obviously bounded.

Show that
\pi(A)
is closed. If a sequence
\{\{x_{i}\}\}\subset\pi(A)
converges with respect to the metric

then its limit should be a single-point subset
\{x^{\prime}\}
. Because of (
57
), we obtain that
x^{\prime}
is the limit of the sequence
\{x_{i}\}\subset A
. Since
A
is closed, then
x^{\prime}\in A
and so
\{x^{\prime}\}\in\pi(A)
. Consequently,
\pi(A)
is closed and bounded and so
\pi(A)
is compact. Since an isometry is a homeomorphism onto its image,
A
is also compact. Therefore
X
is a proper metric space.

Conversely, suppose

is a proper metric space. Let
\mathcal{A}\subset\mathcal{P}_{\operatorname{Cl},\operatorname{B}}(X)
be closed and bounded. We show that
\mathcal{A}
is compact. Because
\mathcal{A}
is bounded, its diameter with respect to
d_{H}
equals some number
0\leq r<\infty
. Hence there exists
A_{0}\in\mathcal{A}
such that for every

we have, with respect to the metric of

, that

. Since
X
is proper,
B_{r}(A_{0})
is compact. Then, by
(

, Theorem 7.3.8)
, the space
\mathcal{P}_{\operatorname{Cl},\operatorname{B}}\bigl(B_{r}(A_{0})\bigr)
is also compact.

For every
A_{1}\in\mathcal{A}
we have
A_{1}\subset B_{r}(A_{0})
, so
A_{1}\in\mathcal{P}_{\operatorname{Cl},\operatorname{B}}\bigl(B_{r}(A_{0})\bigr)
. The subset
\mathcal{A}
is closed in
\mathcal{P}_{\operatorname{Cl},\operatorname{B}}(X)
, hence it is closed in
\mathcal{P}_{\operatorname{Cl},\operatorname{B}}\bigl(B_{r}(A_{0})\bigr)
as well. Compactness of
\mathcal{P}_{\operatorname{Cl},\operatorname{B}}\bigl(B_{r}(A_{0})\bigr)
then implies compactness of
\mathcal{A}
.∎

3.3
Connection with Fermat–Steiner Problem

Proposition 12

Let
X
be a metric space,
\mathcal{A}
be a finite subset of
X
and
G=(V,E)
be a connected graph joining
\mathcal{A}
. Let
v\in V\setminus\mathcal{A}
and
\{u_{1},\ldots,u_{n}\}\subset V
be a set of all vertices adjacent to
v
in
G
. If
g\in[G,\mathcal{A}]
is a minimal parametric network, then
g(v)
realizes the minimum of the sum of distances in
X
to the elements
g(u_{1}),\ldots,g(u_{n})
.

Proof

Consider an arbitrary point
y\in X
. Construct a new network
g^{\prime}\in[G,\mathcal{A}]
that coincides with
g
on all vertices except
v
, and at vertex
v
takes the value
g^{\prime}(v)=y
. By minimality of
g
, we have
|g|\leq|g^{\prime}|
. Consequently,

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} 0\leq|g^{\prime}|-|g|=\sum_{u\in\{u_{1},\ldots,u_{n}\}}\bigl(|g^{\prime}(v)\,g(u)|-|g(v)\,g(u)|\bigr)=\\
=\sum_{u\in\{u_{1},\ldots,u_{n}\}}\bigl(|y\,g(u)|-|g(v)\,g(u)|\bigr).\end{aligned}

(58)

Thus, for any
y\in X
, we get
\sum_{u\in\{u_{1},\ldots,u_{n}\}}|y\,g(u)|\geq\sum_{u\in\{u_{1},\ldots,u_{n}\}}|g(v)\,g(u)|
, i.e.,
g(v)
is a global minimum point of the function

S_{\{u_{1},\ldots,u_{n}\}}(y)=\sum_{u\in\{u_{1},\ldots,u_{n}\}}|y\,g(u)|.

(59)

Hence,
g(v)
realizes the minimum of the sum of distances to the images of adjacent vertices.∎

Definition 25

Let
X
be a metric space and
\mathcal{A}
be a finite subset of
X
. If the graph
G
is a star graph,
\partial G=\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{A}
is the set of leaves of
G
, then the problem of finding all minimal parametric networks of type
G
joining
\mathcal{A}
is called the Fermat–Steiner problem on
\mathcal{A}
.

Indeed, the Fermat–Steiner problem can be formulated more simply without using the concept of a network in a metric space.

Definition 26

Let
X
be a metric space and
\mathcal{A}
be a finite subset of
X
. The Fermat–Steiner problem on
\mathcal{A}
is the problem of finding all points of
X
such that the sum of distances from each of them to the points of
\mathcal{A}
is minimal.

Remark 8

Thus, according to Proposition
12
,
when studying the properties of an interior vertex
g(v)
of a minimal parametric network

, we will conveniently consider this vertex as one of the solutions to the Fermat–Steiner problem on the set of all vertices adjacent to

in the network

.

3.4
Fermat–Steiner Problem in Hyperspaces

The purpose of this article is to study minimal parametric networks in hyperspaces
\mathcal{P}_{0}(X)
of all nonempty arbitrary subsets of a metric space
X
endowed with Hausdorff distance
d_{H}
. By virtue of the Proposition
5
, we may restrict ourselves to set of all closed subsets
\mathcal{P}_{\operatorname{Cl}}(X)
. In such case, a hyperspace
\mathcal{P}_{\operatorname{Cl}}(X)
is an extended metric space. Henceforth, by
\mathcal{P}_{\operatorname{Cl}}^{f}(X)
we will denote an arbitrary finiteness class of the hyperspace
\mathcal{P}_{\operatorname{Cl}}(X)
.

More specifically, our further goal is to describe some properties of interior vertices of minimal parametric networks in
\mathcal{P}_{\operatorname{Cl}}^{f}(X)
. Therefore, by virtue of Remark
8
, for convenience we will view such vertices as solutions to the Fermat–Steiner problem on the set of vertices adjacent to them in these networks. Namely, let
g
be a minimal parametric network of type
G
joining a finite set
\mathcal{A}\subset\mathcal{P}_{\operatorname{Cl}}^{f}(X)
. Let
v
be an interior vertex of the graph
G
and let
\{u_{1},\ldots,u_{n}\}
be the set of vertices adjacent to
v
in
G
.

Let
\Sigma\subset\mathcal{P}_{\operatorname{Cl}}^{f}(X)
be the set of all solutions of the Fermat–Steiner problem on
\bigl\{g(u_{1}),\ldots,g(u_{n})\bigr\}
. First, by Proposition
12
, we have
g(v)\in\Sigma
. Second, it is also obvious that for any
K\in\Sigma
the network

g^{\prime}(x)=\left\{\begin{aligned} &g(x),&&\text{if }x\neq v,\\
&K,&&\text{if }x=v\end{aligned}\right.

(60)

is a minimal parametric network of type
G
joining
\mathcal{A}
. We introduce the notation

M_{i}:=g(u_{i});

(61)

\mathcal{M}:=\{M_{1},\ldots,M_{n}\}.

(62)

Based on the above, we will focus further on solving the Fermat–Steiner problem on
\mathcal{M}\subset\mathcal{P}_{\operatorname{Cl}}^{f}(X)
in the formulation of Definition
26
. Thus, our task can be formulated as follows: we need to find all elements from
\mathcal{P}_{\operatorname{Cl}}^{f}(X)
that minimize the function

S_{\mathcal{M}}(Y)=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}d_{H}(Y,M_{i}).

(63)

By analogy, the fixed finite set
\mathcal{M}
will be called the
boundary
, and each of its elements
M_{i}
a
boundary set
. Let
\Sigma(\mathcal{M})
denote the set of all solutions of the Fermat–Steiner problem on the boundary
\mathcal{M}
. We introduce some more notation. Let
K\in\Sigma(\mathcal{M})
, then

d_{i}(K):=d_{H}(K,M_{i});

(64)

d(K):=\bigl(d_{1}(K),\ldots,d_{n}(K)\bigr);

(65)

\Omega(\mathcal{M}):=\bigl\{d(K):K\in\Sigma(\mathcal{M})\}.

(66)

Note that for
\mathcal{M}\subset\mathcal{P}_{\operatorname{Cl}}^{f}(X)
, different elements
K
and
K^{\prime}
from
\Sigma(\mathcal{M})
can define the same vector from
\Omega(\mathcal{M})
, i.e.,
d(K)=d(K^{\prime}).
Moreover, for each element
K\in\Sigma(\mathcal{M})
, the vector
d(K)
is uniquely determined. Therefore, the set of solutions
\Sigma(\mathcal{M})
is divided into disjoint classes
\Sigma_{d}(\mathcal{M})
, each corresponding to a distinct vector
d=(d_{1},\ldots,d_{n})\in\Omega(\mathcal{M})
, where
d_{i}=d_{H}(K,M_{i})
for all
K\in\Sigma_{d}(\mathcal{M})
. Furthermore, each class
\Sigma_{d}(\mathcal{M})\subset\mathcal{P}_{\operatorname{Cl}}^{f}(X)
is a partially ordered set with respect to inclusion. Note also, since
\mathcal{M}\subset\mathcal{P}_{\operatorname{Cl}}^{f}(X)
, then
d_{i}<\infty
because of Proposition
9
.

4.1

Proposition 13

Let
X
be a metric space,
\mathcal{M}=\{M_{1},\ldots,M_{n}\}\subset\mathcal{P}_{\operatorname{Cl}}^{f}(X)
,
\Sigma(\mathcal{M})\neq\emptyset

. Let also

9
we obtain

for all

. But

, so

Hence,

.∎

Let
X
be a metric space,
\mathcal{M}=\{M_{1},\ldots,M_{n}\}\subset\mathcal{P}_{\operatorname{Cl}}^{f}(X)
, and
\widetilde{d}=(\widetilde{d}_{1},\ldots,\widetilde{d}_{n})
be a vector with non-negative components. Let

(67)

Since each set

is closed by Lemma

Proposition 14

Let
X
be a metric space,
\mathcal{M}=\{M_{1},\ldots,M_{n}\}\subset\mathcal{P}_{\operatorname{Cl}}^{f}(X)
,
\Sigma(\mathcal{M})\neq\emptyset
, and
d\in\Omega(\mathcal{M})
. Then

is the greatest element with respect to inclusion from
\Sigma_{d}(\mathcal{M})
.

Proof

Let
K\in\Sigma_{d}(\mathcal{M})
. Then, according to the definition of the Hausdorff distance in Definition

, we have

for all

. Therefore,

. Let us now show that

By definition,

. But since

, we have
d_{H}(K_{d},M_{i})=d_{i}
. Thus,
K_{d}\in\Sigma_{d}(\mathcal{M})
and, for any

.∎

Remark 9

It is obvious that in any partially ordered set, if a greatest element exists, then it is unique. Therefore, by Proposition

, if

, then

Proposition 15

Proposition 16

Let
X
be a finite-dimensional real normed space,
\mathcal{M}=\{M_{1},\ldots,M_{n}\}\subset\mathcal{P}_{\operatorname{Cl}}^{f}(X)
,
\Sigma(\mathcal{M})\neq\emptyset
and
d\in\Omega(\mathcal{M})
. Then each
K\in\Sigma_{d}(\mathcal{M})
contains at least one minimal element with respect to inclusion in
\Sigma_{d}(\mathcal{M})
.

Proof

If
K
is finite, then it is obvious that
K
contains a minimal element with respect to inclusion in
\Sigma_{d}(\mathcal{M})
. Now suppose
K
is infinite.

Suppose that
K
contains no minimal elements of
\Sigma_{d}(\mathcal{M})
. Then there exists a sequence of nonempty strictly nested closed subsets
\{K_{i}\}\subset\Sigma_{d}(\mathcal{M})
such that
K_{i}\subset K\in\Sigma_{d}(\mathcal{M})
. Hence we have
d_{H}(K_{i},M_{j})=d_{j}
for all
i
. Consequently,

M_{j}\subset B_{d_{j}}(K_{i})

(68)

for all
i
and
j
.

Let us show that
K_{\lambda}:=\bigcap\limits_{i=1}^{\infty}K_{i}\neq\emptyset
and
M_{j}\subset B_{d_{j}}(K_{\lambda})
. Choose a point
x
in
M_{j}
. According to Proposition
6
, from the finite-dimensionality of
X
and the closedness of all
K_{i}
it follows that
P_{K_{i}}(x)\neq\emptyset
for all
i
. Let
k_{i}\in P_{K_{i}}(x)
. By (
68
), we have
|x\,k_{i}|=|x\,K_{i}|\leq d_{j}
, and therefore the sequence
\{k_{i}\}
is bounded.

In a finite-dimensional real normed space, from every bounded sequence one can extract a convergent subsequence. Without loss of generality, we assume that
k_{i}\rightarrow k
as
i\rightarrow\infty
. For any
i
we have
\{k_{i^{\prime}}:i^{\prime}\geq i\}\subset K_{i}
. Consequently, by the closedness of
K_{i}
we obtain
k\in K_{i}
for all
i
. Therefore
k\in\bigcap\limits_{i=1}^{\infty}K_{i}=K_{\lambda}
. Moreover, from the continuity of the distance function in a metric space it follows that
|x\,k|\leq d_{j}
.

Thus, since the choice of the point
x\in M_{j}
was arbitrary, we obtain
M_{j}\subset B_{d_{j}}(K_{\lambda})
. At the same time, since
d_{H}(K_{i},M_{j})=d_{j}
and
K_{\lambda}\subset K_{i}
, we have
K_{\lambda}\subset B_{d_{j}}(M_{j})
. Therefore
d_{H}(K_{\lambda},M_{j})\leq d_{j}
. Note that
K_{\lambda}
is closed because all
K_{i}
are closed. Consequently,
K_{\lambda}
belongs to the same finiteness class
\mathcal{P}_{\operatorname{Cl}}^{f}(X)
as all
M_{j}
. But since
\min\limits_{Y\in\mathcal{P}_{\operatorname{Cl}}^{f}(X)}S_{\mathcal{M}}(Y)=\sum\limits_{j=1}^{n}d_{j}
, we have
d_{H}(K_{\lambda},M_{j})=d_{j}
. Hence
K_{\lambda}\in\Sigma_{d}(\mathcal{M})
and
K_{\lambda}
is a minimal element of the chain
\{K_{i}\}\subset\Sigma_{d}(\mathcal{M})
.∎

4.2
Implementation of One-Sided Hausdorff Distances on Interior Vertices

Let us write down the following definition.

Definition 27

Let
X
be a metric space,
\mathcal{M}=\{M_{1},\ldots,M_{n}\}\subset\mathcal{P}_{\operatorname{Cl}}^{f}(X)
and
d=(d_{1},\ldots,d_{n})\in\Omega(\mathcal{M})
. A point
x\in M_{i}
is called
d
-far for
M_{i}
if
U_{d_{i}}(x)\cap K_{d}=\emptyset
.

In the paper
(
Gals_1
, Theorem 3)
it was proved that in the case of a finite-dimensional normed
X
and all nonempty convex compacts
M_{i}\in\mathcal{M}
, there exists an
M_{j}\in\mathcal{M}
that has a
d
-far point. This fact essentially relied on the compactness of the sets
M_{i}
.

Note that from
U_{d_{i}}(x)\cap K_{d}=\emptyset
and
d_{H}(M_{i},K_{d})=d_{i}
it follows that
\sup\limits_{x\in M_{i}}|x\,K_{d}|=d_{i}
. Moreover, by the compactness of
M_{i}
and the continuity of the distance function to a nonempty subset, from
\sup\limits_{x\in M_{i}}|x\,K_{d}|=d_{i}
it follows that there exists an
x\in M_{i}
such that
|x\,K_{d}|=d_{i}
and hence
U_{d_{i}}(x)\cap K_{d}=\emptyset
.

Thus, for a finite-dimensional normed
X
, nonempty set of convex compacts
\mathcal{M}=\{M_{1},\ldots,M_{n}\}
and
d\in\Omega(\mathcal{M})
, there always exists a compact
M_{i}
such that
d_{i}=\sup\limits_{x\in M_{i}}|x\,K_{d}|
. That is, one of the Hausdorff distances in the sum
S_{\mathcal{M}}(K_{d})=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}d_{H}(M_{i},K_{d})
will be one-sided precisely from the side of the boundary set.

We will prove under some additional conditions that if we omit the compactness of the sets
M_{i}
and the finite-dimensionality of the space
X
, then for
d=(d_{1},\ldots,d_{n})\in\Omega(\mathcal{M})
there still exists an
i
such that
d_{i}=\sup\limits_{x\in M_{i}}|x\,K_{d}|
.

Similarly, we will denote an arbitrary finiteness class of the hyperspace
\mathcal{P}_{\operatorname{Cl},\operatorname{Conv}}(X)
by
\mathcal{P}_{\operatorname{Cl},\operatorname{Conv}}^{f}(X)
.

Theorem 4.1

Let
X
be a real normed space,

\mathcal{M}=\{M_{1},\ldots,M_{n}\}\subset\mathcal{P}_{\operatorname{Cl},\operatorname{Conv}}^{f}(X),

(69)

\Sigma(\mathcal{M})\neq\emptyset
and
d\in\Omega(\mathcal{M})
. Suppose also that there exists an index
s
such that all nonempty sets of the form
B_{r}(M_{s})\cap K_{d}
belong to the same finiteness class
\mathcal{P}_{\operatorname{Cl},\operatorname{Conv}}^{f}(X)
to which all
M_{i}
belong. Then there exist
i
such that
d_{i}=\sup\limits_{x\in M_{i}}|x\,K_{d}|
.

Proof

Suppose the contrary, i.e., that for all
M_{i}
, we have

d_{i}>\sup\limits_{x\in M_{i}}|x\,K_{d}|\geq 0.

(70)

This means that there exists
0<\varepsilon_{i}<d_{i}-\sup\limits_{x\in M_{i}}|x\,K_{d}|
such that for all points
x\in M_{i}
we have
U_{d_{i}-\varepsilon_{i}}(x)\cap K_{d}\neq\emptyset
. But then for any
i
,

M_{i}\subset U_{d_{i}-\varepsilon_{i}}(K_{d})\subset B_{d_{i}-\varepsilon_{i}}(K_{d})\subset B_{\varepsilon_{i}}\bigl(B_{d_{i}-\varepsilon_{i}}(K_{d})\bigr).

(71)

By Lemma
7
we have
\Bigl|B_{d_{i}-\varepsilon_{i}}(K_{d})\,\,\partial B_{\varepsilon_{i}}\bigl(B_{d_{i}-\varepsilon_{i}}(K_{d})\bigr)\Bigr|=\varepsilon_{i}>0
. Moreover, by Lemma
6
we have
B_{\varepsilon_{i}}\bigl(B_{d_{i}-\varepsilon_{i}}(K_{d})\bigr)=B_{d_{i}}(K_{d})
. Thus,
\bigl|M_{i}\,\partial B_{d_{i}}(K_{d})\bigr|>0
. Hence, because of (
70
), the following quantity is positive:

\gamma_{i}=\min\Bigl\{d_{i},\bigl|M_{i}\,\partial B_{d_{i}}(K_{d})\bigr|\Bigr\}.

(72)

Consequently, since the number of boundary sets is finite, we obtain

\gamma=\min\limits_{i\in[1,n]}\gamma_{i}>0.

(73)

By condition, all
M_{i}
are convex. Then by Lemma
5
, the sets
B_{d_{i}}(M_{i})
are convex. Hence,
K_{d}
is also convex. So, for any
i
, by Proposition
4
we have

M_{i}\subset B_{d_{i}-\gamma}(K_{d}).

(74)

In particular, expression (
74
) holds for the index
s
from the condition of the theorem. Recall that
d_{H}(M_{s},K_{d})=d_{s}<\infty
. Since
d_{s}>\sup\limits_{x\in M_{s}}|x\,K_{d}|
, then we have
d_{s}\in\bigl(|M_{s}\,K_{d}|,\infty\bigr)
, due to Lemma
8
. By the condition, for all
r\in\bigl(|M_{s}\,K_{d}|,\infty\bigr)
, sets
B_{r}(M_{s})\cap K_{d}
belong to the same finiteness class
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{\operatorname{Cl},\operatorname{Conv}}}^{f}(X)
as all
M_{i}
. Therefore, by Theorem
2.1
, the set
B_{d_{s}}(M_{s})\cap K_{d}
varies continuously in the Hausdorff sense under small perturbations of
d_{s}
.

Thus, for
0<\varepsilon<\gamma
there exists
\delta>0
such that

K_{d}=B_{d_{s}}(M_{s})\cap K_{d}\subset B_{\varepsilon}\bigl(B_{d_{s}-\delta}(M_{s})\cap K_{d}\bigr).

(75)

Denote
B_{d_{s}-\delta}(M_{s})\cap K_{d}
by
K
. Thus,
K_{d}\subset B_{\varepsilon}(K)
. Consequently, for any
i
, by Lemma
6
,

M_{i}\subset B_{d_{i}-\gamma}(K_{d})\subset B_{d_{i}-\gamma}\bigl(B_{\varepsilon}(K)\bigr)=B_{d_{i}-\gamma+\varepsilon}(K).

(76)

Moreover,
K\subset K_{d}\subset B_{d_{i}}(M_{i})
for all
i
and
K\subset B_{d_{s}-\delta}(M_{s})
. Hence
d_{H}(K,M_{i})\leq d_{i}
and
d_{H}(K,M_{s})\leq\max(d_{s}-\delta,d_{s}-\gamma+\varepsilon)<d_{s}
. Consequently,
S_{\mathcal{M}}(K)<S_{\mathcal{M}}(K_{d})
, which is a contradiction. The theorem is proved.∎

Corollary 5

Let

\mathcal{M}=\{M_{1},\ldots,M_{n}\}\subset\mathcal{P}_{\operatorname{Cl},\operatorname{B},\operatorname{Conv}}(X),

(79)

Corollary 6

Let
X

\mathcal{M}=\{M_{1},\ldots,M_{n}\}\subset\mathcal{P}_{{\operatorname{Comp}},\operatorname{Conv}}(X),

(80)

A natural question arises: for

Theorem 4.2

Let
X
be a connected metric space,

\mathcal{M}=\{M_{1},\ldots,M_{n}\}\subset\mathcal{P}_{\operatorname{Cl}}^{f}(X),

(81)

\Sigma(\mathcal{M})\neq\emptyset

d\in\Omega(\mathcal{M})

i

d_{i}=\sup\limits_{x\in K_{d}}|x\,M_{i}|
.

Proof

If
K_{d}=X
, then, since
M_{i}\subset X
, we obtain

d_{i}=d_{H}(M_{i},X)=\max\{\sup_{y\in M_{i}}|y\,X|,\sup_{x\in X}|x\,M_{i}|\}=\sup_{x\in X}|x\,M_{i}|.

(82)

Now suppose
K_{d}\neq X
. Assume that for all
i
the strict inequality
\sup_{x\in K_{d}}|x\,M_{i}|<d_{i}
holds. This means that
K_{d}\subset U_{d_{i}}(M_{i})
for all
i

K_{d}\subset\bigcap_{i=1}^{n}U_{d_{i}}(M_{i})

\bigcap_{i=1}^{n}U_{d_{i}}(M_{i})\subset\bigcap_{i=1}^{n}B_{d_{i}}(M_{i})=K_{d}

K_{d}=\bigcap_{i=1}^{n}U_{d_{i}}(M_{i}).

(83)

But then, by Lemmas
3

4

X

\emptyset

X

K_{d}
is nonempty and by assumption
K_{d}\neq X

i
such that
d_{i}=\sup\limits_{x\in K_{d}}|x\,M_{i}|
