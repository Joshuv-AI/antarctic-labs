The effect of mandatory regulatory policies on unsafe sexual behaviours among female sex workers: an evaluation study
Authors: Wangjin Liang, Jian Chen, Yaqin Pang, Ruchen Zhang, Xujiao Li, Lanfen Tan, Jianying Liao, Xu Liang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSocial policies shape individual behaviours and population health. Human Immunodeficiency Virus (HIV) prevention governance measures targeting female sex workers (FSWs) remain controversial. Existing evidence mainly relies on qualitative or cross-sectional self-reported data, limiting objective population-level assessments. Using longitudinal data from China’s HIV sentinel surveillance system, we evaluated whether mandatory regulatory measures have influenced unsafe sexual behaviours among FSWs.MethodWe treated the 2015 policy implementation in Baise City, Guangxi, China, as a natural experiment. Using data from eleven counties covered by the National HIV Sentinel Surveillance System (2010–2020), we applied the Cochran–Armitage trend test and interrupted time series analyses to evaluate changes in AIDS knowledge awareness rate (AKAR), condom use rate at last commercial sexual encounter (CUR-LCSE), and active syphilis positivity rate (ASPR). Deviation of ASPR from the expected level was estimated using the event study method, and the policy effect was validated by a robustness test.ResultsAmong 19 220 observations, AKAR and CUR-LCSE showed significant upward trends from 2010 to 2020 (both p < 0.01). ASPR remained stable before 2015 (p = 0.53) but declined markedly thereafter (p < 0.01). Event study analyses detected no systematic deviation between observed and expected ASPR; robustness tests indicated that the post-2015 decline was random (t = − 0.956; p = 0.39). Overall, mandatory regulatory policies were not associated with measurable changes in unsafe sexual behaviour.ConclusionMandatory regulatory policies showed no clear evidence of elevated syphilis risk among FSWs. The stable prevalence of active syphilis and consistent behavioural indicators further confirmed that these policies did not inadvertently promote unsafe sexual behaviours, and serological findings indicated a lack of substantial behavioural changes in this population. This highlights an urgent need to clarify how structural and social determinants shape unsafe sexual behaviours to inform the development of more integrated and equitable syphilis prevention strategies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-026-28083-9.Keywords: Female sex workers (FSWs), Mandatory regulatory policy, Unsafe sexual behaviours, Interrupted time series, Event Study Method

Introduction

According to a 2025 World Health Organization report, an estimated 39.9 million people were living with HIV in 2023, with 1.3 million new infections and 630 000 HIV-related deaths recorded that year [1]. Unsafe sexual behaviours substantially increase the risk of HIV and other sexually transmitted infections (STIs), serving both as a major driver of HIV acquisition among female sex workers (FSWs) and an important determinant of onward HIV and STI transmission [2, 3]. In this study, FSWs refer to individuals who were assigned female sex at birth. FSWs and their clients are an important bridge population in HIV epidemics, creating key viral transmission pathways across otherwise disconnected social groups. High mobility and contact with multiple concurrent partners generate complex sexual networks that increase the contribution of FSWs to maintaining HIV transmission chains [4]. From a population health perspective, a primary objective of public health interventions is to reduce the total volume of high-risk sexual encounters. Theoretically, mandatory regulatory measures may lower this volume by increasing the economic and social costs associated with commercial sex transactions, including heightened legal risks, operational barriers, and elevated transaction prices for clients, which can shrink the FSW population and reduce the frequency of high-risk contacts, thereby curbing the broader transmission of STIs [5–7]. Reducing unsafe sexual behaviours and high-risk interactions within this group remains a global priority. Targeted interventions are therefore essential—not only to mitigate infection risks for FSWs themselves but also to break the chain of HIV transmission to the wider community.

Since the introduction of the “health promotion” framework as articulated in the Ottawa Charter for Health Promotion in 1986, there has been a greater global emphasis on the pivotal role of governmental and social governance structures in shaping population health. Substantial evidence shows that legal and policy interventions can influence health outcomes by reshaping the broader social environment [8, 9]. Although commercial sex work is illegal in most countries, the use of mandatory public health control measures targeting FSWs remains highly contested. The central debate revolves around two competing structural pathways. On the one hand, strengthening law enforcement may increase risks by driving sex trade activities into more concealed environments, making it harder for health services to access this population. On the other hand, mandatory regulatory policies might reduce STI prevalence by limiting the operational space for commercial sex and decreasing the overall frequency of transactions [10, 11]. A key issue, however, is whether these measures inadvertently lead to an increase in unsafe sexual behaviours, thereby raising the risk of HIV infection. Although the prevailing public health perspective suggests that these policies may shape FSWs’ risk behaviours, this view is largely informed by qualitative and cross-sectional studies [12–14]. Furthermore, this perspective conflicts with governance principles endorsed by many countries, complicating the alignment of HIV prevention strategies with national policy frameworks and contradicting core health promotion tenets, namely, intersectoral collaborative governance and shared risks and responsibility between society and individuals [15]. Previous research has predominantly used qualitative designs or single-year, cross-sectional data, with limited long-term serological or behavioural monitoring and few cohort studies capable of establishing causality. Consequently, robust evidence on whether mandatory regulation increases HIV risk among FSWs remains insufficient.

Current Chinese law prohibits commercial sex and includes policies aimed at safeguarding population health, preventing infectious disease transmission, and maintaining social stability. From a social governance perspective, these measures partly help to reduce the risk of STI epidemics [16, 17]. To further strengthen social order and sustain the decline in crime, the Chinese government issued the “Opinions on Strengthening the Construction of the Social Security Prevention and Control System” in 2015 (hereafter referred to as the “Opinions”). This policy aimed to influence STI transmission by strengthening legal measures, implementing real-name registration requirements, and expanding collaborative governance. These actions effectively increased transaction costs and limited the operational space of the sex trade. As a result, the policy reduced illegal activities, including commercial sex work [18]. This policy shift created a natural experiment suitable for causal inference in the present study. We utilized China’s HIV/AIDS Case Reporting and Information Management System, which has been operational since 2009, to assess whether structural governance changes have led to a reduction in the overall frequency of unsafe sexual behaviour or inadvertently increased risks. This system systematically collects long-term serological and behavioural data on key populations, including FSWs. This comprehensive and continuous surveillance infrastructure provides a robust foundation for evaluating the population-level effects of policy changes.

We combined a community-level context with long-term systematic FSW sentinel surveillance data from 2010 to 2020 in Baise City, Guangxi. Baise was selected because its eleven contiguous counties each maintain stable, well-established surveillance infrastructure and have sustained collaborative partnerships, which offered operational convenience. We used interrupted time series (ITS) analysis and the event study method (ESM) to assess the effect of regulatory measures implemented from a public affairs management perspective on unsafe sexual behaviours and syphilis prevalence trends among FSWs. Measures included AIDS knowledge awareness rate (AKAR), behavioural indicator, and active syphilis positivity rate (ASPR). Based on the logic of social governance, we hypothesised that mandatory regulatory policy would lead to a decrease or stabilisation in syphilis prevalence at the population level. This is expected because the policy increases the economic and social costs of commercial sex, thereby significantly reducing the overall frequency of high-risk transactions. The study addressed two questions: (1) whether regulatory policies influence trends in AIDS knowledge, behavioural indicators, and serological outcomes before and after implementation; and (2) whether these policies affect the prevalence of unsafe sexual behaviours within the target population.

Methods

Data sources

We analysed HIV sentinel surveillance data from Baise City, Guangxi (2010–2020), collected via China’s voluntary HIV surveillance system in accordance with the “National HIV Sentinel Surveillance Implementation Plan”. Field surveys were conducted at community sites frequented by FSWs across all eleven counties. Data included sociodemographics, AIDS knowledge, condom use rate at last commercial sexual encounter (CUR-LCSE) as a behavioural indicator, and syphilis serological indicators (ASPR). AIDS knowledge was evaluated through standardised questionnaires of the National HIV Surveillance System in China. The questionnaire mainly assessed the basic knowledge related to AIDS, including transmission routes, prevention methods, and common misconceptions. An additional AIDS knowledge item file shows this in more detail [see Supplementary Materials 1]. Serological results were laboratory-confirmed, and behavioural and knowledge data were self-reported. Data were collected annually between April and June. According to national guidelines, active syphilis was defined as positivity on both enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay and toluidine red unheated serum test. To ensure the temporal comparability of this large-scale, long-term surveillance data, this definition has been consistently applied. Given that the indicator is based on cross-sectional, anonymous sentinel surveillance data, it should be interpreted primarily as a population-level epidemiological indicator that reflects overall active syphilis trends, rather than as definitive evidence of recent individual infections.

Study design

We used a combined analytical strategy integrating the Cochran–Armitage test for trend (CAT), ITS analysis, and the ESM. Given the nationwide implementation of the policy without a conventional external control group, this study was designed as a natural experiment.

A key assumption underlying the ITS and event study framework is that pre-intervention trends can approximate the counterfactual trajectory in the absence of the policy. To strengthen the plausibility of this assumption, we assessed potential time-varying confounders. No major new HIV prevention programmes targeting FSWs were introduced during the study period beyond routine services, and no abrupt economic changes coincided with the policy implementation. However, unmeasured factors—such as changes in surveillance practices or population mobility—may still introduce residual confounding.

China issued the “Opinions” in April 2015. Allowing for the necessary period of administrative adjustment and enforcement alignment, the effective implementation year was defined as 2016. Because annual surveillance data, collected from April to June each year, capture syphilis cases identified since the preceding survey, the effect of the “Opinions” on local FSWs was assumed to begin in 2016. Accordingly, 2016 was assigned as the event time point (T = 0), with the event window as 2016–2020 and the estimation window as 2010–2015. This temporal structure was applied consistently across the ITS and CAT analyses.

ITS analysis evaluates the causal effect of an intervention by comparing changes in the trajectory of a continuous outcome before and after the intervention is implemented [19, 20]. We used annual data, defining 2016 as the interruption point and dividing the timeline into the pre-policy period (2010–2015) and the post-policy period (2016–2020). We examined changes in both the level and slope of three indicators—AKAR, CUR-LCSE, and ASPR—to independently assess the immediate and sustained effects of the long-term policy intervention. Given that ITS is limited in its ability to distinguish between true intervention effects and random fluctuations within the short post-intervention period, ESM was adopted as a counterfactual-based validation tool. ESM constructs expected trajectories based on pre-intervention trends and quantifies the deviation between observed and expected outcomes after intervention, thereby assessing whether actual changes deviate significantly from the range of random fluctuations. The central role of the ESM in this study is to assess whether the resulting deviations reflect systematic policy effects or random variation, thereby providing an independent robustness test of ITS results.

ITS and ESM play complementary but distinct roles in the analysis. ITS provides estimates of level and slope changes based on models, while ESM validates the observed deviations within a short intervention window through an alternative framework. The ESM provides a robust framework for sociological causal inference, enabling the identification of policy-induced effects by comparing trajectory changes before and after a policy shock while simultaneously accounting for underlying secular trends and potential confounders [21]. By combining these methods, we harness the advantages of long-term trend monitoring alongside the estimation of immediate effects, which improves control over potential temporal confounding variables. This integrated approach enhances the validity of causal inference, providing a more robust and comprehensive framework for policy effect evaluation.

We used ASPR as a serological outcome indicator. As syphilis is a curable sexually transmitted infection, a positive serological result often reflects a recent infection typically acquired within the previous 4 weeks to 6 months [22]. Because syphilis is mainly transmitted through sexual contact and its acquisition risk is strongly associated with condomless sex, its prevalence may serve as an indirect proxy reflecting potential changes in unsafe sexual behaviours among FSWs [23]. Nevertheless, ASPR does not serve as a direct metric of unsafe sexual behaviour, which necessitates a clear distinction between ASPR and the self-reported behavioural indicator. The CUR-LCSE captures individual behavioural intentions and actual practices during the most recent commercial sexual encounter, whereas ASPR represents a biological outcome shaped by multiple factors beyond individual sexual behaviour. These confounding factors include testing coverage, prior treatment history, and syphilis reinfection, among others. As noted earlier, ASPR is a population-level surveillance indicator and should not be overinterpreted as direct evidence of individual-level behavioural change. In this context, ASPR provides a relatively sensitive marker of short-term, population-level variations in risk exposure. In contrast, HIV antibody positivity cannot reliably distinguish between recent and long-standing infections, limiting its utility for capturing short-term behavioural changes within a population [24].

Statistical analysis

To describe short-term and long-term trends in AIDS knowledge, behavioural indicators, and serological outcomes (Objective 1), we used CAT and ITS analysis. CAT was conducted for each outcome, with survey year as the row variable and the following as column variables: adequate AIDS knowledge, CUR-LCSE, and ASPR. A significant linear-by-linear association indicated the presence of a secular trend.

For ITS analysis, stationarity and autocorrelation were assessed using the KPSS test and the Durbin–Watson statistic, respectively. A KPSS p value > 0.05 indicated a stationary series; non-stationary series were log-transformed to achieve stationarity. When autocorrelation was absent, parameters were estimated using ordinary least squares. To elucidate the causal relationship between the policy intervention and subsequent changes in unsafe sexual behaviour (Objective 2), we implemented an event study design. Abnormal positivity rate (ARt) and cumulative abnormal positivity rate (CARt) were used as primary indicators to quantify the extent to which the intervention affected the target population. To assess whether deviations in syphilis positivity represented random fluctuations or systematic changes, we conducted a robustness test on CARt. The hypotheses were defined as follows: H₀: CARt = 0, indicating that observed changes reflect random variation; H₁: CARt ≠ 0, indicating non-random deviations attributable to the intervention. CAT, AR modelling, and Ljung–Box diagnostics were conducted in SPSS AU (Beijing Qingsi Technology Co., Ltd., Beijing, China). ITS analysis, event study estimations, and robustness tests were performed in R version 4.5.0 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Ethical statement

As this study only used the annual AKAR, CUR-LCSE, and ASPR data from FSWs, which were obtained from the Baise CDC’s anonymous monitoring system in accordance with the Infectious Disease Prevention and Control Law, without involving personal privacy or other information, informed consent was waived for this study. Approval was obtained from the Ethics Committee of Youjiang Medical University for Nationalities (Approval No: 2024071201).

Discussion

This study found no clear evidence that regulatory policies substantially altered unsafe sexual behaviour among FSWs. We used ASPR from 2010 to 2020 HIV sentinel surveillance data in Baise as an evaluative indicator to assess their effect on unsafe sexual behaviours. ITS analysis showed that the ASPR was consistent following the policy adjustment. Additionally, the ESM confirmed that ASPR fluctuations occurred randomly. Although trend analysis suggested a decline in ASPR between 2016 and 2020, such short-term patterns are not sufficient to reflect true behavioural change. Given the event study findings, the ASPR decline is most likely a random phenomenon arising from substantial disruptions to community activity among the target population following policy adjustment, which may have affected sampling stability during surveillance. These findings are in sharp contrast to much of the existing evidence, likely because previous work has mainly used qualitative or cross-sectional designs. Such studies typically depend on self-reported behavioural indicators, which are prone to subjectivity and social desirability bias, and lack objective serological measures, limiting their validity. To address these limitations, we have combined longitudinal sentinel surveillance data and serological outcomes to provide a more reliable and objective assessment of policy effects. These results highlight the potential value of incorporating improved longitudinal survey and surveillance methods to better capture underlying trend changes.

FSWs in the study area have long exhibited a persistent “knowledge–attitude–practice” disconnect, a pattern consistent with findings from other regions both within China and internationally [26, 27]. Prior to policy implementation, HIV-related awareness and self-reported condom use among FSWs in Baise City increased markedly. While HIV-related awareness fluctuated in certain years during the study period, ASPR remained stable. The mechanisms underlying the continued stability of unsafe sexual behaviour within this population remain unclear. From a public health perspective, sustained interventions delivered through the local healthcare system may have helped maintain relatively high levels of prevention knowledge and willingness to use condoms, which may partly explain the stable rate of active syphilis. However, this persistent pattern highlights potential limitations of behaviour-change frameworks that assume rational, autonomous decision-making, as evidence from behavioural economics and communication research indicates that human behaviour is strongly shaped by cognitive biases, heuristics, and social influences rather than purely rational deliberation [28]. External contextual pressures, such as unequal bargaining power, economic incentives, or vulnerability to coercion in certain transactional settings, may also contribute to behavioural choices in specific circumstances. Fluctuations in population size and HIV-related awareness during the study period indirectly suggest that the policy adjustment increased pressures associated with commercial sex work. They also indicate that small social clusters within the FSW community may play a critical role in shaping individual behaviours. Qualitative studies have shown that in environments characterised by strong state regulation and pervasive social stigma toward commercial sex, FSWs tend to form tightly connected and relatively stable subgroups [29]. Evidence from behavioural research in other fields similarly demonstrates that such small groups are vital in maintaining stable behavioural patterns within populations [30]. Therefore, from the public health perspective of reducing unsafe sexual behaviour, future behavioural intervention studies should explore the causes of such behaviour among FSWs, taking into account group interactions.

From a social governance perspective, regulatory policies may reduce syphilis transmission through structural mechanisms rather than direct behavioural change. Specifically, strengthened enforcement and regulatory measures increase the economic and social costs of commercial sex, thereby reducing the frequency of transactions and potentially contracting the size of the FSW population [6, 31]. In this context, even if individual-level behaviours remain unchanged, a reduction in the overall volume of commercial sexual activity may lead to fewer unsafe sexual encounters at the population level, thereby lowering opportunities for syphilis transmission. Previous studies indicate that following the implementation of intensified regulatory measures, public health departments recorded a substantial decline in the number of individuals active in community venues [32–34]. However, field-based investigations became markedly more difficult, possibly because of reductions in the number of FSWs or their displacement to more concealed settings, thereby reducing the visibility of this population [35, 36]. Moreover, regulatory policies create greater barriers to contact and raise transaction costs between clients and service providers. Reduced visibility of the target population and increased cost of community-based activities constrain opportunities for group-level interactions, potentially contracting the size of the population participating in these activities. If this population shrinks, then even in the absence of changes in individual transaction frequency or the probability of unsafe sex, the total volume of commercial sexual activity—and consequently the volume of unsafe sexual encounters—may decrease at the aggregate level. However, this is not the only pathway of influence; in an environment with strengthened regulation, risks may also be redistributed among remaining sex workers, potentially leading to increased structural vulnerability. Nevertheless, within a health promotion framework, regulatory policies may still be a potential strategy for reducing the societal and individual burden of HIV.

This study integrated CAT and ITS to accommodate analytical needs for both short- and long-term surveillance data. CAT can capture immediate shifts in study variables after policy rollout, while ITS quantifies the long-term impacts of such interventions. These methods allow not only the examination of policy impact trajectories but also the quantification of changes over time. By including ITS analysis within a sociological event study framework, this approach evaluates shifts in pre- and post-intervention trends, applies defined temporal windows to mitigate temporal inertia, and addresses potential inferential bias that may arise when relying solely on short-term trend analyses. Moreover, the integration of natural experiment principles and event study designs commonly used in the social sciences strengthens causal inference and offers new methodological insights for public health policy evaluation and behavioural research. This interdisciplinary approach increases the analytical rigour of policy impact assessments and provides a theoretical foundation for improving collaboration between public health agencies and other administrative and social governance sectors. Finally, we used standardised surveillance data that were systematically and continuously collected from 2010 to 2020, permitting an accurate assessment of long-term trends in key variables within the target population.

This study has several limitations. First, this natural experiment lacked an external control group, making its causal inference less robust than that of a randomised controlled trial; thus, the findings should be interpreted cautiously. Moreover, the analysis was limited to surveillance data from 2010 to 2020, restricting assessment of long-term policy sustainability, and unmeasured time-varying confounders may have influenced the results. Future studies should extend the surveillance period beyond 2020 and adopt multi-region or control-group designs to strengthen causal inference. Second, behavioural heterogeneity may exist within the FSW population owing to differences in age, work venues, and other factors, yet both the ITS design and the event study approach assume behavioural homogeneity, which may obscure important within-group variations. Future research should apply other methods to explore the differences in policy effects across various demographic and social characteristics, enabling a better understanding of within-group heterogeneity. Third, the sample size was relatively limited, which may have reduced statistical power and widened confidence intervals, thereby weakening the ability to detect true effects. In particular, the robustness test based on CARt was conducted over a short event window, which may further limit inference reliability. Therefore, non-significant findings should be interpreted with caution. Future studies should incorporate longer observation periods or alternative robustness testing strategies to improve inferential reliability. Fourth, ASPR was the only blood-based measure in this study. It served as an indirect reference for changes in overall unsafe sexual behaviour, but it should be interpreted with caution. This limits our ability to capture multidimensional and dynamic changes in behavioural patterns among FSWs. ASPR is influenced not only by individual behaviour but also by other factors, including testing coverage, access to treatment, and syphilis reinfection. Therefore, ASPR should not be considered a direct measure of recent unsafe sexual behaviour at the individual level. Observed declines or fluctuations may partly reflect these background factors rather than true behavioural change. Future studies should include additional STI indicators and comprehensive behavioural data to distinguish policy effects on sexual behaviour from those on disease transmission. Finally, our analysis focused exclusively on female sex workers. Including male sex workers—an equally high-risk yet substantially understudied population—would allow future studies to more fully examine how regulatory policies may differentially affect diverse subgroups.

Conclusion

Mandatory regulatory policies were not associated with measurable changes in unsafe sexual behaviours among FSWs, nor was there clear evidence that these policies inadvertently increased their sexual risk practices. From a social governance perspective, such policies may reduce the size of the sex worker population and raise the costs of commercial sex, thereby lowering the risk of syphilis transmission to the general public. Embedded in the health promotion framework and complementing existing behavioural public health interventions, regulatory policies delivered by non-health sectors can facilitate the development of a multi-level social prevention and control system. Importantly, as these policies do not drive up unsafe sexual behaviours, they protect both the general population and FSWs by cutting down overall high-risk sexual encounters. As such, regulatory policies constitute a valuable part of syphilis prevention strategies and work alongside behavioural interventions to sustain this multi-level prevention and control system.
