Executive Summary
The levelized cost of heat for concentrating solar thermal (CST) systems can be reduced by
improving performance or reducing initial capital costs of heliostat fields. Caribou Labs is
developing wireless controls for a CST system that improves field optical efficiency and targets
heliostat costs of $50/m2. This CST system relies on a closed loop wireless control architecture.
Closed loop control improves performance by dynamically adjusting heliostat aim points to
maximize flux on the receiver in real time. Wireless communication to the heliostat field reduces
initial capital costs by eliminating wiring and associated installation costs, a nontrivial expense
for heliostat fields that may require wiring to tens of thousands of individual units. By supporting
closed loop control strategies and eliminating wires, bidirectional wireless communication
solutions address levelized cost of heat by simultaneously improving performance and reducing
capital costs. Wireless communication remains underutilized in CST applications as a result of
underdevelopment in four performance areas: reliability, throughput, scalability, and
cybersecurity; all of which this project addresses. Outputs of this work include a working
demonstration of a multinode communication system, clear reporting of system performance,
and technical documentation of system development and architecture for reproducibility.

1 Major Goals and Objectives
The objective of this project is to demonstrate a wireless architecture and hardware at the
National Laboratory of the Rockies (NLR) Flatirons campus that is valuable to the entire
heliostat industry. Note that the Flatirons is an outdoor test space with photovoltaic systems and
other existing equipment, but it is not a heliostat field. The field test is intended to demonstrate
performance that will work for any heliostat (latency of approximately hundreds of
milliseconds), reliability at distances between 10 and 1,500 m, versatility to various heliostat
controls and design needs, and costs in line with U.S. Department of Energy (DOE) heliostat
targets of $50/m2. Additionally, the hardware will be made open source for easy adoption by
others in the concentrating solar thermal (CST) industry.

 1

2 Summary of Technical Achievements
Hardware was engineered, manufactured, and delivered to NLR for a 100-node network at the
Flatirons campus. The 100-node network was both simulated and tested in the field. Through this
process, the following were achieved:

 • An open-source repository for manufacturing low-cost (~$30/heliostat) components for
 wireless communications
 • Full documentation of the architecture, communication layers, and messaging structure
 • Software for both the software defined radio (SDR) and heliostat control unit
 communication controller (HCUCC)
 • Simulation software for the Flatirons system
 • Inclusion of protocols/encryption for making the network cyber secure
 • Introduction of a lightweight, open-source framework for programming SDRs: CLER
 which can be found at https://cariboulabs.github.io/cler/.
 • Validation of the hardware and performance through data collection at Flatirons
 • De-risking of the architecture and hardware for sub-second communication and scale-up
 to tens of thousands of heliostats. (Although thousands of heliostats were not tested, the
 data collected demonstrate the design is sound and there are sufficient frequency bands
 for achieving control of tens of thousands of units.)
 • An understanding of the remaining or future work to be off-the-shelf ready.

 2

3 Impact
The project delivered and validated wireless communication hardware that can be scaled to fields
of tens of thousands of heliostats. The hardware is currently open source and can be
manufactured at volume to achieve the cost of less than $30 per heliostat. Although there are
further costs and development time needed to be off-the-shelf ready, the field validation
demonstrated round-trip tower communication latency of 21 ms under heavy interference at the
Flatirons field. The Flatiron HCUCC field test measured 74 heliostat units at ~910 megahertz
(MHz) with a Gaussian minimum shift keying (GMSK) wireless control network. GMSK
provides exceptional spectral efficiency, extended battery life, and high resilience to noise
(similar techniques are used in Bluetooth and other wireless networks). The maximum heliostat-
to-tower range was ~900 m, while signal strength was sufficiently strong to assume 1.5-km
transmission distances are within range for the architecture. There are currently no off-the-shelf
ready wireless communications systems that have been optimized to support the DOE cost target
of $50/m2 for heliostats.

This work is distinct from other DOE/HelioCon-funded heliostat wireless projects with Arizona
State University and Solar Dynamics in its communication rate of 1 Hz or greater, optimization
specific for heliostat fields, cost per heliostat, or its technology readiness level. For more details,
a 2026 HelioCon report is being published to provide a clear comparison of these three unique
projects. The approach in this work is estimated to need an additional $600,00 to $800,000 and a
1-year well-defined project to become a commercially available, secure, and reliable architecture
for heliostat fields.

4.1.1 System Description and Basic Architecture
The wireless communication hardware associated with this work generally is designed to be
operated as follows:

 • Radio communication at 915 MHz and 2.4 GHz (software controlled)
 • Communication link properties:
 o Telemetry/control refresh rates can be greater than 5 Hz but are dependent on
 field size and communication frequencies chosen.
 o Point-to-point communication exists between tower and heliostats (no hopping).
 o TCU computer fully controls frequency allocation for transmitting and receiving
 and synchronizes the system.
 o All fielded HCUCCs implement listen-before-talk protocol.
 o Intended maximum communication range is approximately 1.5 km.
 o Firmware updates over the air (FOTA) capabilities are planned, but additional
 funds are needed to write and vet robust software (the system must default to a
 stable firmware version in the event of any errors occurring during a software
 update).
 o Modulation: GMSK, signal-to-noise ratio (SNR)> 5 decibels (dB), bandwidth-
 time product = 0.3, modulation Index = 0.5 (these specifications for GMSK
 result in high spectral efficiency)

 7

 o Cybersecurity (encryption and authentication), AES-256-CTR mode cipher
 • Open-source hardware at github.com/cariboulabs/heliostat_controller.git.

An asymmetric communication approach is employed in which the TCU transmits jumbo frames
that contain messages for multiple HCUCCs while each HCUCC responds separately.
Additionally, the TCU transmits higher power than the HCUCCs. The TCU includes a Linux
computer (Raspberry Pi) for computations, an SDR for receiving messages (a CaribouLite for the
field validation, where a USRP B206 mini is planned for tens of thousands of heliostats and can
handle both transmitting and receiving messages), and an HCUCC transmitting messages to the
HCUCCs or heliostats in the field. The combination of the CaribouLite SDR and HCUCC allow
simultaneous receiving and transmitting of messages. Power is provided to the TCU from the
grid over ethernet, where transmitting power is greater than 34 decibel-milliwatts (dBm) and 1
megabits per second (Mbps) (considered the uplink side of the system). HCUCCs are mounted
on each heliostat and can send messages to the TCU at ~19 dBm and 100 kilobits per second
(kbps) (considered downlink side) and are powered by a small battery and solar panel. The
HCUCC can be attached to a heliostat controller or serve as the controller if additional motor or
actuator drivers are incorporated. The HCUCC was specifically designed to hit low-cost targets
per heliostat and to use low-power to avoid heliostat field trenching. Figure 7 provides a graphic
of the asymmetric communication between a TCU and many HCUCCs.

4.1.2 Detailed Medium Access Control Layer Message Structure and Spectral
 Frequency Allocation
The TCU transmits uplink jumbo frames, where a single jumbo frame contains messages for 14–
18 HCUCCs (16 in the Flatirons testing). The header within the jumbo frame identifies which
heliostats the message belongs to, and when the match occurs, each HCUCC returns a short
response with status on its sequential order within the jumbo frame. In the meantime, the TCU
continues to simultaneously transmit and receive messages. Figure 8 provides a schematic of
communication between the TCU and heliostats, in which multiple heliostats are packed into
jumbo frames that go out from the TCU. The TCU loops through transmitting to all N jumbo
frames, in which a 5-Hz update is planned, but communication rate is software controlled and
can be adapted as needed for field size. Because the TCU has antennas for parallel transmitting
and receiving, each heliostat or HCUCC response can be received while still looping through
transmitting of jumbo frames.

 8

Figure 8. Communication between the TCU and heliostats, in which multiple heliostats are packed
 into jumbo frames that go out from the TCU

An example jumbo frame message structure is shown in Figure 9. The jumbo frames contain
optionally encrypted messages using AES-256-CTR mode cipher. This is a symmetric
encryption method using a 256-bit key to turn a block cipher into a stream cipher and is highly
efficient for rapid communication. Within the header, multiple HCUCC IDs are addressed, all at
once. Forward error correction, mitigating both random and burst noise, is used to correct errors
and reconstruct corrupted data, reducing packet error rate. Cyclic redundancy checks (CRC)
provide header and payload validation, to maintain the industry standard 6-bit Hamming distance
error detection assurance. In this example, the payload data contain communal data for all
heliostats (e.g., weather, sun position) and unit-specific control data (e.g., adjustments to azimuth
and elevation tracking). The downlink messages, transmitted by the heliostats (see Figure 10),
contain a similar message header, while the payload is smaller, containing a single heliostat
information. The opcode defines what is in the message structure and up to 256 opcodes can be
easily defined per C++ or Python code as needed for different heliostat fields.

In general, the medium access control (MAC) layer in radio communication is responsible for
managing spectrum, setting transmission times, handling data framing, and managing
retransmissions. The MAC layer network channels are divided into spectrally dependent uplink
and downlink bands, with an example of this shown in Figure 11. In Flatirons testing, the 915-
MHz industrial, scientific, and medical (ISM) band (26 MHz of width) was implemented with
the assumption it would not be congested for small-scale testing. The band can be further
expanded to the 2.4-GHz ISM band, which provides a larger bandwidth of 83.5 MHz. Figure 11
shows an example subdivision into 7 channels (frequency sub-bands) with 14 heliostats each,
while field implementation at the Flatirons includes 16 HCUCCs per band. Considering the
uplink operates at 1 Mbps with GMSK and with guarding between channels, 1.5 MHz of
bandwidth was allocated or designed for a single jumbo frame channel, where 7 such channels
occupy 10.5 MHz of bandwidth. On the downlink side, a single HCUCC or heliostat operates at
100 kbps. A 130-kHz bandwidth is allocated/designed for a single heliostat, taking into account
GMSK and guarding. Considering 16 heliostats per channel, this equates to 2.08 MHz allocation
per downlink channel and 14.6 MHz for seven downlink channels. The TCU computer controls
spectral allocation in each message sent to a HCUCC (i.e., the message from the TCU specifies
at what frequency the individual HCUCC should respond). Frequency allocation is not static but
rather varied by the TCU to optimize performance and respond to congestion or potentially
jamming (note that, in the Flatirons testing, the TCU randomized rather than optimized
frequency assignments).

The MAC layer also includes radio traffic control through listen-before-talk (LBT) protocols.
LBT implies that HCUCC radio must listen on its intended frequency to determine that channel
 10

is not in use before transmitting a message. Generally, two heliostats should not be attempting to
communicate at the same time over the same frequency as an HCUCC sends a downlink only
after receiving an uplink from the tower. In other words, frequencies in use by each heliostat are
fully synchronized by the tower. Additionally, messages are tagged with a time-to-live tracking
to discard messages exceeding their time or hop limit if applicable.

 ISM band.

4.1.3 Design for Heliostat Field Size and Communication Rate
The heliostat field size for the 915-MHz band depends on the desired communication rate. If a 1-
Hz communication rate is targeted (all heliostats communicated with in 1 second), calculations
for the uplink and downlink channels are as follows. For the uplink channel: One jumbo frame
requires 1,200 bits; at 1 Mbps, this results in 1.2 milliseconds per jumbo frame. Using 1.5
milliseconds to be conservative, this means 666 jumbo frames can be sent in 1 second. With 16
HCUCCs per jumbo frame, this means communication with 10,656 HCUCCs are possible per
uplink channel in 1 second. Seven uplink channels equates to 7 * 10,656, or 74,592 heliostats.
Note that in the setup at the Flatirons, one HCUCC is used for transmitting from the TCU, where
7 HCUCCs (or a more advanced SDR) can be used to transmit in parallel to achieve
approximately 75,000 heliostats.

The downlink for a single heliostat/HCUCC requires 32 bytes or 256 bits. At 100 kbps, this is
2.6 milliseconds, or 3 milliseconds to be conservative. This results in 333 heliostats messages
being sent in 1 second, where 16 heliostat messages can be sent in parallel over one downlink
channel. Again, there are 7 downlink channels for a total of 16 * 7 * 333, or 37,296 heliostats per
 11

the downlink side. Note that testing at the Flatirons uses a CaribouLite as the SDR, with the
lower band channel configured as the ~2-MHz band parallel receiver. Alternatively, the system
has been designed at scale for the USRP B206 mini, which has a maximum operational
bandwidth of 56 MHz (planned operation in both the 915-MHz and 2.4-GHz bands),
substantially more than the 14.7 MHz for 7 downlink channels. This SDR will improve
substantially the amount of operational channels.

In summary, in the current design, the system is limited by the downlink side, which can handle
approximately 37,000 heliostats at 1 Hz. If faster communication rates or larger heliostat fields
are desired, then operating at the 2.4-GHz ISM band expands to 22 parallel downlink channels,
opening up a factor-of-3 increase in field size or communication rate.

4.1.4 Other Features of Wireless Architecture

4.1.4.1 Network Node Acquisition
When turning on a TCU or powering up a node that is expected to operate within an existing
TCU network, each node is automatically adapted into the network. This includes (1) being
detected, (2) being configured, and (3) operating and being monitored. A TCU is periodically
sending out beacons over uplink channels. The beacon contains Tower ID, software, hardware,
and protocol versions, tower status, temperature, time, tower geo-location and network frequency
channels (acquisition channel and Rx channels). During a configurable period, an acquisition
channel is banned from usage by other active HCUCCs. When a new node receives a beacon, it
is already programmed with the network parameters and tower properties; it knows its location,
radio frequency (RF) time delay, and current wall clock time. The new node or HCUCC
responds over the acquisition channel with its properties and the request to enter the network.
The acquisition message is transmitted after an LBT sequence. Once the TCU receives the
acquisition message, the HCUCC data are checked. If correct, the HCUCC enters the network
and operation, and monitoring is then ongoing. Figure 12 provides an example of how beacon
signals are periodically sent and heliostat data response channels and acquisition channels are
allocated.

 Figure 12. An example of periodic beacons being sent over an uplink channel to provide for
 network node acquisition

4.1.4.2 Tower Control Unit Transmission Power Adaptation
The TCU has an achievable transmission power variation of about 40 dBm. The goal is for each
HCUCC to receive TCU signals with near the same power levels, but without power adaptation,
HCUCCs that are farther away or near obstacles will have a reduced received signal strength.
The TCU computer knows the location of each heliostat and assesses the expected path loss from
the tower. This information is gained from geographic distance and the received signal strength
of a nondeferred message from the tower. In all downlink messages, heliostats or HCUCCs
include received signal strength from the last transmission received from the tower. The tower
then uses this information to adapt and control uplink signal strength as needed.

4.1.4.3 Firmware Update Algorithm Updates Over the Air
The firmware update algorithm was not implemented in this work because of lack of time for
completion and full testing of the code. Multiple firmware updates were applied to the Flatirons
field, but each of these was achieved by a hardwire connection between a laptop and each
HCUCC. Caribou Labs designed the FOTA algorithm scaffolding as follows. The TCU may
issue a “firmware update start command,” in which all heliostats receive a “FOTA” state
transition command. The “firmware update start command” is followed by acknowledgment
messages from the heliostats. Following this command, all heliostats are waiting for data.

The data structure contains chunks of information that are transmitted in a sequential manner in
256 byte chunks. Chunks are meshed (retransmitted) by some of the HCUCCs to allow for safe

 13

and validated delivery to the geographically furthest HCUCCs. As the transfer is finished, the
tower performs a “firmware update end command,” after which all heliostats calculate the hash
value or digital fingerprint of their received data and return it to the tower. After a verification
sequence, the TCU commands the field to continue operation or reboot with the new firmware
version. Although the data transfer protocol is relatively simple and could have easily been
implemented, more development time and vetting are needed to ensure HCUCCs can default to a
previous version of firmware in the event of an error or bug in a new version of firmware. It is
critical to be able to always default to reliable firmware; otherwise a FOTA command could
result in losing communication with tens of thousands of HCUCCs (requiring costly manual
interactions to restore communications at each physical heliostat).

4.1.4.4 CLER Introduced
Digital signal processing (DSP) block libraries exist for programming SDRs to handle items such
as time synchronization implementation of signal modulation. A significant portion of Caribou
Labs’ work was focused on software development for the TCU software defined radio to achieve
the planned wireless architecture. It was expected that existing DSP libraries would be able to be
used within the SDR code, but it was found that existing code was too heavy for embedded
system use. Caribou Labs developed CLER, a new open DSP library that is lightweight and built
for embedded devices. (CLER has an executable size and memory usage that is approximately
100 kilobytes (KB) instead of megabytes (MB) for existing options.) Figure 13 provides a
comparison between CLER and various nearest options. With the completion of the development
of CLER, Caribou Labs also demonstrated GMSK demodulation per the SDR. GMSK is an
efficient digital technique that uses a continuous-phase frequency shift keying method to transmit
data. Figure 14 shows a signal that was sent via the SDR with 31 embedded messages, where
100% of the bytes were correctly detected.

The appendix provides a complete table of the parts, including testing, and assembly for the

4.2 Simulation of the Flatirons Field
A network stack simulation was generated for RF channel emulation, the MAC layer, and
cipher/cybersecurity algorithms. A block diagram for the RF channel emulation is shown in
Figure 19, where the emulation is a complex structure to emulate RF path losses, bit-error-rates
(BERs), SNRs, and received signal strength indicators (RSSIs).

 19

 Figure 19. Block diagram for RF channel emulation

The network node block in Figure 19 is a basic unit that defines a node, which can be a tower or
a heliostat. Network nodes have an “event-driven” mechanism and can subscribe into a channel
and unsubscribe from it. Nodes can transmit a channel and can get the current RSSI on the
channel. The RF message block in Figure 19 is the basic transmitting/receiving raw message that
occupies the channel. It is defined by the sender and is delivered to the subscribers on the
channel. It contains the modulation properties (bit rate, occupation time and data length) and
contains the payload data bytes. The RF message gives the occupancy time on the channel that is
primarily used by the channel simulator. The RF message object can vary so that different types
of modulation are introduced, each with different properties. Currently GMSK modulation is
emulated, which is what is being used in the Flatirons test field. The RF channel block in Figure
19 is the “message-routing” mechanism within the simulation. It contains subscriber nodes
according to their geographic location and calculates the message delivery timing (according to
path distance and speed of light), and the delivery power (RSSI) according to the path distance. It
also introduces BERs and embeds them within the raw messages to emulate data losses.
Terrestrial thermal noise is used in the SNR calculation where SNRdB = Received Signal Level
(dBm) − (−174 + 10log10B + NF), with B representing the bandwidth in Hz and NF being the
noise floor. The BER is approximated as 1/2e ^ (−SNR/2). The time of propagation uses the geo
to cartesian conversion in the GeographicLib library, while the path-loss calculation is a
weighted average of the “ITU-RP1546,” the “Log-Distance,” and the “Two-Ray” Models (all
industry standards). The CSP-Field block in Figure 19 encapsulates the field-level state. This
block reads node locations and parameters from a CSV file and puts every field element in place.

This object is a “Field-Factory,” and once it finishes the “field construction,” it does not have an
active role in the simulation. The running simulation is organized per the diagram in Figure 20.

The simulation was run to match the field layout provided in Figure 15 with 100 HCUCCs and
one TCU. MAC layer encryption and decryption was incorporated with the RF emulation, as
shown in Figure 21.

 Figure 21. MAC layer encryption as incorporated with RF emulation
The simulation included 105 steps with MAC messages generated with: length = [30, 210] bytes,
random variation of error correction parameters, and random variation of encryption keys.
Channel emulation included Gaussian additive noise, injected random bit errors (up to 20 bit-
switches per message), and injection of burst errors (up to 2 bytes erasure).

 21

All results were logged into a CSV file, which can be loaded into Python for analysis and
visualization.

The use of different numbers of parity bits in Hamming, (7,4), (15,11), and (31,36), were
examined, as shown in Figure 22, for uplink messages and Figure 23 for downlink messages. In
uplink messages at the same packet error rate, the SNR can be up to 4 dB lower, with Hamming
included (up to 4.5 dB lower for downlink messages). For both uplink and downlink messages,
all levels of parity bits are sufficient for maintaining a negligible packet error rate if the SNR is
above 6.

4.3 Benchtop Testing and Initial Field Installation and
 Troubleshooting
Each HCUCC was benchtop tested for power up and node acquisition from the TCU, but the 100
nodes were not simultaneously tested for node acquisition and monitoring in the benchtop
configuration. Although this was originally intended, because of late delivery of the full SDR
software, the decision was made to move forward with Flatirons deployment of the TCU and the
first 10 HCUCC nodes to achieve 60 days of field data collection before the project’s end.
Similarly, the HCUCC battery and solar charging hardware were subjected to an abbreviated
validation test. In this case, a small number of HCUCCs and their charging units were deployed
outdoors at NLR’s main campus and basic functionality were confirmed. Some problems were
found and corrected (e.g., certain LEDs were bypassed on the HCUCC to lower overall power
usage), but field deployment was implemented without a multiweek outdoor test that is typical
for this type of hardware. Additionally, the outdoor power testing did not include communication
with the TCU. The HCUCC’s software is designed to control the hardware so that when not in
the relative high-power transmitting/receiving state, a sleep or low-power mode is enabled. By
skipping testing of the power system without the TCU in the loop, the impacts of the software
and the duty cycle (transmitting/receiving high power to low-power sleep) were based on
calculations rather than validation.

The decision to move forward with the Flatirons deployment with less than the desired benchtop
validation did not prove to be effective in accelerating overall system data collection. Data
collection started, but immediately there was a problem in that not all 10 units were successfully

 24

transmitting back to the TCU. Troubleshooting this problem and others in the field proved to be
inefficient compared to the planned benchtop testing (the Flatirons is remote for the test
engineers and winter weather conditions disrupted work on various occasions). After evaluation,
it was deemed likely that those units not being received by the tower were due to their locations
on the outside edges of the TCU directional transmitting and receiving antennas. Additional
HCUCCs were added to the field, but not all of the new units were heard by the TCU, even
though the new units were well within the directional pattern of the antennas.

Physical hardware examination of each of the units not being heard and data from functioning
units indicated various problems: (1) HCUCC software errors, (2) HCUCC antennas not
performing as expected while inside their enclosures, and (3) in some units low battery charge
state. A cycle of correcting the various issues and retesting took multiple weeks that likely would
have taken days if an outdoor benchtop setup had been instituted at NLR’s main campus. This
was because of remote and weather challenges associated with the Flatirons and the fact that,
without FOTA capability and with HCUCC’s spread over a large field, engineers had to walk to
each unit with a laptop to make multiple software updates.

The simple lesson is that when building a wireless communication network such as this, outdoor
benchtop testing with all nodes in close proximity must demonstrate that all nodes can
successfully communicate for multiple hours with the TCU and that the majority of the nodes
can communicate successfully over at least 2 weeks before expanding to a large field. The 2-
week test should also include taking several units to the low battery charge and demonstrating
recovery of full charge after a designed number of sunny hours. If cloudy weather does not
occur, low charge state can easily be achieved by covering the solar panels on certain units. As a
result of skipping the in-depth power system test, a serious problem was not found on the
charging hardware until after more than 40 HCUCCs were installed in the field. There was no
possibility of correcting the defective board components within the remaining project timeline,
and therefore an off-the-shelf Adafruit solar battery charger board was purchased and installed in
the field.

Correcting software bugs and adding new solar chargers increased the number of units that were
successfully communicating with the TCU, but there were still missing units that were clearly
the result of transmitting and receiving signal issues. Investigations altering the HCUCC antenna
orientation and mounting proved that missing HCUCCs could at least be temporarily brought
online. Simulation results and expectations of signal strength based on calculations for the
antenna hardware and power suggested the tower should be receiving the messages regardless of
field position. Investigations with a portable spectrum analyzer showed unexpected 915-MHz
signals present at the Flatirons campus. The tower was set to work as a spectrum analyzer to
gather all spectral signals heard in the 915-MHz region while communication to HCUCCs was
ongoing. Figure 25 shows one chunk of spectral data collected by the tower SDR. The high
levels of interference suggested the tower could be missing messages from some of the HCUCCs
because other stronger signals were heard in the same frequencies of the missing HCUCCs or
LBT could be preventing the HCUCCs from sending downlinks.

Changing antenna location within some HCUCC enclosures served to boost downlink from the

LEDs indicated the most likely problem was that certain HCUCCs were not receiving their
messages from the tower and then were staying silent (rather than the tower not hearing HCUCC
messages among the site interference). In all observations, once the HCUCC receiving LED
came on, the transmitting also came on, and the given unit was also heard at the tower. Further
testing is needed, but observations point to problems with HCUCC antenna or antenna
configuration (the antenna orientation, connections, or shielding by the solar cell on the lid of the
enclosure) rather than field interference. If the problem HCUCCs antennas were sufficient to
receive the uplink without interference, then it is to be expected that these HCUCCs would be
heard at the TCU some of the time because of frequency randomization.

In practice, these HCUCCs were not heard unless adjustments to their antenna were made. With
this knowledge, the remaining HCUCCs were installed as planned for the 100-node network
while attempting to position each antenna to better hear each unit at the TCU. After antenna
adjustment, more than 70 nodes could typically be heard by the TCU, 5 units were dead due to
unknown hardware issues, and approximately 25 units still did not hear the tower unless the lid
was removed from the enclosure. The following options were considered to bring the remaining
units into regular communication as well as boost how often certain units were heard: (1) find the
primary source of interference and pause it for a short-term test of 95 functioning nodes, (2)
replace internal antennas with external antennas on select HCUCCs, (3) lower the TCU antenna

to potentially increase SNR, or (4) run spectral analysis at 2.5 GHz—and if less interference,
make the software change to operate at that band. At this phase in the project (final quarter),
partner Caribou Labs was not accessible, and therefore items 3 and 4 were considered unfeasible.
Attempts were made to find the primary source of interference, but these were unsuccessful. One
external antenna was tested on an HCUCC, but this test did not show significant impact, and
therefore this option was set aside. The decision was made to collect data over 1 day from the
currently communicating HCUCCs, analyze this data, and report on the findings within the
remaining project timeline.

The field results show RSSI/SNR higher than simulated, where review of the simulation shows it

planned SDR, the USRP B206 mini, has 56 MHz of operation bandwidth, and the system will be
able to easily hop between the 914-MHz and the 2.5-GHz bands to be reliable even in a jamming
attack.

4.4 Key Field Test Outcomes
There are further steps or improvements that will be made to the hardware and software under
test, but the data collected demonstrate the overall architecture performs as expected. The results
of this test field substantially de-risk the technology and support the calculations for scaling the
technology to tens of thousands of heliostats while delivering encrypted sub-second
communication. The hardware was designed to be producible using U.S. components, and at
volume, the bill of materials is less than $30 per heliostat. All hardware is open-sourced and can
therefore be easily adopted by heliostat manufacturers or CST field designers as part of hitting
DOE heliostat cost targets of $50/m2. Design and testing of a control stack framework was
achieved, including software/network algorithms and security/encryption. A major DSP engine,
“CLER,” was open-sourced by Caribou Labs and is already in use by others. Testing the system
in a real field with significant interference provided direct understanding of the risks, experience
with mitigation strategies, and a long list of lessons on how to efficiently approach moving to a
production-level system.

4.5 Lessons Learned

4.5.1 System Power Supply and Consumption
As discussed, the battery and charging system were not sufficiently sized and tested for field
deployment in NLR/Colorado winter irradiance conditions. An updated design will optimize the
hardware and battery for at least 8 days of storage (minimizing power usage when not in
transmitting/receiving mode) and must be validated with a longer-term outdoor operational
period. The system enclosure will be increased in size to accommodate a solar panel that can
recharge the battery over an 8-hour sunny period.

The chosen photovoltaic maximum power point tracking integrated component required the
photovoltaic panel output less than 5 volts, which was overly constraining when considering
 31

photovoltaic supply options. Alternatively, using TI BQ25638YBGR or a similar unified device
opens up photovoltaic supply options while supporting power measurement, maximum power
point tracking, and battery management. This component (or similar alternative) should be
facilitated on the main board to seamlessly handle power management. There are several board
power consumption issues that can be revisited and improved: DC-DC power conversion,
maximum power point tracking methodology, removal of constant power consumers like
indicator LEDs (useful in prototyping but not needed at full scale), removal or power gating the
temperature compensated crystal oscillator (alternatively working with the raw quartz crystal
component). Currently, when powered through USB, the device draws extra energy to power the
USB chip (Cypress). This integrated component does not enter low-power mode by default.
Additionally, the low-voltage power entry path goes through a power-hungry diode. Replacing it
with a power path dedicated integrated component should be considered.

4.5.2 Manufacturing Concerns
The project encountered several avoidable development bottlenecks because of manufacturing
too few units (120 for a 100-node network) and delivering hardware units to NLR too fast. Fifty
percent more units should have been manufactured than the original 120. This would provide
sufficient units for in-house development and gradual delivery to NLR as the software was more
fully vetted. Caribou Labs had 25 units manufactured up front, but these were found to have a
fake USB component and were scrapped. This manufacturing problem caused a significant time
delay, and the next batch was pushed to the full 120 to be able to deliver to NLR per contract
deadlines. As mentioned earlier, there were other board failures and problems with the battery
charging system in the batch of 120 units. These problems further introduced delays and forced
temporary patches to problems. It is recommended, while prototyping and testing, to
manufacture small batches of 5 to 10 units that are subjected to a documented test protocol,
including outdoor operation before producing more units. Scaling to larger quantities should not
occur without confidence all is working per the full test protocol.

4.5.3 Hardware-Level Lessons

4.5.3.1 Main Chip
The Texas Instruments CC1352 is the integrated, multiband, and multiprotocol wireless
microcontroller that allows operating in both the 915-MHz and 2.4-GHz frequencies. The
component is very capable for its price and works above expectations. On the other hand, Texas
Instruments’ development and debugging tools are currently prone to issues and difficult to use.
This issue needs to be considered when working with this chipset. On the other hand, Texas
Instruments’ RTOS is a very responsive and mature operating system.

4.5.3.2 Radio Frequency System
The natively provided 19-dBm RF power amplifier from the device is sufficient for the 1,500-m
use case, but it could be valuable to provide the board with an additional power amplifier, which
is gated and can be bypassed (this provides adaptability for greater distances and areas with
interference). Consider adding a power amplifier for at least 27 dBm. Pros include an additional
8 dB of margins for link resilience; cons include higher power consumption (~2.5–3 times more,
 32

on average), higher board cost, and additional complexity to the software and hardware.
Performance of the antenna on the HCUCC side was evaluated in the design phase, but this
evaluation excluded consideration for being inside a box with a solar panel on top (which
requires being tilted and oriented for optimal irradiance collection). The optimization of the
HCUCC antenna needs reevaluation in this context as well as full performance evaluation as
assembled.

4.5.3.3 More Flexibility in the Development Stage
During the project we drew up (has not been printed and tested) an add-on expansion board for
the HCUCC to directly connect the HCUCC to heliostat control hardware. It makes sense to
support future iterations for this type of evaluation kit for research and experimentation. It also is
practical for the research kit to have a larger battery because higher power consumption items
like indicator LEDs are valuable in diagnostics and troubleshooting. Generally, this kit should
have easier and accessible inputs and outputs on the HCUCC board. The usage of miniature
Molex bottom connectors impeded fast-pace debugging and agility, and it may be valuable to
replace them by a standard 0.1-inch connectors.

4.5.3.4 Radio Frequency Surveying and Tooling
By default, it was assumed that the Flatirons campus would not have problematic interference at
the 915-MHz frequency band. A spectrum analyzer was rented for ~$500/month when problems
occurred. At the beginning of the project, it should have been a requirement to survey both the
915-MHz and 2.4-GHz band in planning for deployment. A directional survey could have been
conducted with the rented equipment or broad frequency analysis with the SDR as demonstrated
in the project.

4.5.4 Software-Level Lessons

4.5.4.1 HCUCC Board Firmware
The benefits include a simple architecture that is C++-based, easy to adapt with configuration
flow, and reusable as a platform. The cons include firmware development that is difficult with
Texas Instruments’ current ecosystem. In conclusion, the software should be adapted to open-
source ecosystems. Multiple firmware updates were made as a result of bugs in the system. Field
updates were time-consuming without FOTA, and therefore priority should be placed on
completing and validating FOTA software.

4.5.4.2 Tower-Listener
The system was late in delivery because existing frameworks such as GNURadio proved to be
problematic. Alternatively, the project provided the bonus of developing the open-source CLER
Library, and the TCU proved to work well with CLER (see Other Features of the Architecture
section). CLER provided a multichannel decoder fast enough to be facilitated even on a
constrained hardware such as a RaspberryPi. Additionally, a wider-bandwidth SDR is feasible on
such devices. Future testing should include the USRP B206 mini as it is intended at scale and its
cost is not prohibitive.

 33

4.5.4.3 Tower Application
The TCU works as expected, though improvements can be made on channel feature
approximations, such as optimal subchannel selection based on spectral interferences. For now,
because of a high level of channel clutter, we chose a random channel allocation algorithm,
providing different HCUCCs equal chance to reside on a quiet channel. This stochastic method
provides a good solution for randomly cluttered channels.

 34

5 Next Steps
Next steps can be divided into two categories: (1) improvement and finalizing the existing
system, which is estimated to require $100,000 to $150,000 to complete, and (2) creating a
production-level system (off-the-shelf ready) that is estimated to require greater than $600,000 to
complete. The left of Figure 31 shows a list of items needed to improve or fix the current 100-
node network. There are software features like the TCU graphical output that currently report
meaningless values that can easily be corrected to provide better system feedback. A more time-
consuming software item is completing the code for FOTA updates and testing for resiliency to
bugs in new firmware versions. Although this is time-consuming, it is critical to being able to
rapidly make improvements to the HCUCC software. Improving the HCUCC power system and
antenna mounting would allow the system to run as intended and capture long-term data in
varying field conditions.

These long-term data are expected to provide fruitful information toward further optimizing the
performance and spectral efficiency of the system. A bit error rate test should also be
implemented in the process to accurately report on system performance. The right of Figure 31
provides an overview of the key items needed to achieve an off-the-shelf system for heliostat
fields. There are obvious RF engineering hardware and software iterations needed to prove the
system is robust and ready for seamless field deployment. These processes are well developed
and require a predictable number of engineering hours to complete. Additionally, the cost
associated with regulatory testing and approval are well known. Alternatively, meeting market
needs is a more uncertain task. Although the wireless architecture in this project has great
promise, it will only be adopted if the features and cost dovetail well with the market needs.
With the exception of China, the market is very limited in terms of heliostat fields being
developed. Lack of an active market to work with is currently a significant barrier to finalizing a
field-ready product.
