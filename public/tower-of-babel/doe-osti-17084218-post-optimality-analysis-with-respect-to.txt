ABSTRACT
This report summarizes results from the Computing and Information Sciences LDRD project
“Model discrepancy sensitivities to support optimal decision-making.” The goal of the project is to
develop algorithms to analyze the effect of model discrepancy in large-scale optimization problems.
This is motivated by scenarios in which a high-fidelity model is too complex and computationally
intensive to be used inside an optimization loop. Such scenarios arise when, for instance, the high-
fidelity model requires hours of wall-clock time per evaluation or when the optimization solution is
needed at a time-scale commensurate with the wall-clock time of a single high-fidelity simulation.
The approach developed in this report augments optimization using a lower-fidelity model with a
model discrepancy representation that is calibrated using limited high-fidelity data. The goal of the
analysis is to estimate the high-fidelity optimization solution, with quantified uncertainties, using
limited high-fidelity simulations (potentially as few as one).

SUMMARY

Optimal decision-making is frequently associated with the solution of an optimization problem.
Trust in such computational analysis hinges on the fidelity of the model. However, the complexity
of high-fidelity simulations limit our ability to utilize them. This project develops mathematical
foundations to emulate high-fidelity optimization solutions by fusing high-fidelity data into lower-
fidelity optimization problems. Our approach only requires access to a small number of high-
fidelity simulations and hence is pragmatic for large-scale and time-sensitive applications. We
embed uncertainty quantification to enable optimal decision-making with trust commensurate with
the availability of high-fidelity data.

1. INTRODUCTION

1.1. Project motivation

Computational science has made enormous strides toward developing high-fidelity simulations
to emulate complex physical systems. Such simulation codes, which run on high-performance
computers, utilize a wide range of algorithms and software. Examples include meshing of irregular
geometries, finite element discretization, linear and nonlinear solvers, and distributed memory
parallel linear algebra. Furthermore, for a given physics, it is frequently necessary to utilize domain-
specific parameterizations and numerical schemes to ensure physics fidelity and/or numerical
performance. Unfortunately, the predictive power of high-fidelity models typically comes at the
expense of a great accumulation of complexity that limit the use of the models. In particular,
embedding high-fidelity models within larger analysis pipelines (such as optimization or uncertainty
quantification) poses considerable challenges in both software interfacing and computational cost.
Consequently, despite great advances, high-fidelity simulation codes have not yet realized their full
potential to support decision-making.

This project seeks to enhance the utility of high-fidelity models by enabling their use in large-
scale optimization. Such optimization problems arise in a variety of contexts such as design and
control of engineering systems, calibration of model parameters, and estimation of unobservable
quantities (inverse problems). However, large-scale optimization algorithms require intrusion into
the simulation code to expose derivatives and many model evaluations to assess the progress of
the optimization algorithm as it iterates. This is typically not possible due to the complexity of
high-fidelity models. Rather, the common scenario is to derive a simpler low-fidelity approximation
that is more amenable to optimization. Such low-fidelity models arise in many ways, for example,
through simplifying assumptions in physics derivations, neglecting forces or couplings in the
system, model-order reduction, and constructing data-driven surrogate models. Optimization of
these low-fidelity models returns a solution, but the quality of the solution depends on how well
the low-fidelity model approximates its high-fidelity counterpart.

This project develops the foundational mathematics and algorithms for hyper-differential sensitivity
analysis with respect to model discrepancy (HDSA-MD). The goal is to use data from high-fidelity
simulations to enhance the solution from low-fidelity optimization and quantify uncertainty due
to the discrepancy between the high- and low-fidelity models. In particular, our approach (i) is
applicable with as little as one high-fidelity simulation, (ii) decouples the optimization iterations
from the high-fidelity simulation by conducting post-optimality analysis that only requires access to
read data output from the high-fidelity simulation, and (iii) does not require access to high-fidelity
derivatives. Hence, HDSA-MD is non-intrusive to the high-fidelity simulator and pragmatic for
users.

 15

The non-intrusive and pragmatic aspects of our analysis come at the cost of mathematical guarantees
of optimality. Using only a small number of high-fidelity simulations, the goal of HDSA-MD is to
(i) realize improvement relative to the low-fidelity solution, and (ii) quantify uncertainty due to the
limitation on the number of high-fidelity simulations. The latter point is crucial, as sub-optimality
is inescapable when the number of high-fidelity simulations is limited, but quantifying uncertainty
in the optimization solution provides the confidence in solution quality (or lack thereof) needed to
support decision-making.

We illustrate the HDSA-MD concepts by considering the mass-spring system depicted in Figure 1-
1, which corresponds to representing a bar (in the center) with three connected masses with spring
constants 𝑘 23 = 𝑘 34 = 10. The bar is attached to another mass below and three smaller masses
above. We assume that a time-varying force is applied, pushing the top masses downward. Our
goal is to determine an optimal time-varying force pushing upward on the bottom mass so that we
minimize its displacement.

The high-fidelity model is a system of 14 first order ordinary differential equations that model the
position and velocity of all 7 masses. We consider a low-fidelity model that consists of lumping
all masses other than the bottom mass together as one large mass. Hence, the low-fidelity model is
a system of 4 first order ordinary differential equations modeling the position and velocity of the

this single high-fidelity evaluation, we apply HDSA-MD to compute an updated control solution

1.4. A methodological overview

The HDSA-MD framework consists of two primary steps: (i) calibration of the model discrepancy
representation, and (ii) post-optimality propagation of the model discrepancy through the optimiza-
tion problem. These steps require careful analysis to ensure proper uncertainty quantification, as
well as mature algorithms to enable efficient computation for large-scale applications.
Model discrepancy calibration: Since the number of high-fidelity model evaluations is lim-
ited, and the model discrepancy representation parameters scale with the optimization variable
dimension, the calibration problem is ill-posed, i.e., there exist many potential model discrepancy
representations that fit the data. To address this challenge, we adopt a Bayesian approach to cal-
ibration, where prior knowledge can be used to constrain the model discrepancy representation
and uncertainty can be rigorously quantified. The Bayesian calibration framework requires careful
consideration to ensure that application-specific knowledge can be incorporated into the prior and
that samples from the posterior may be computed efficiently. High dimensionality of the model
discrepancy representation parameters is challenging, but is addressed through judicious problem
formulations and advanced tools from linear algebra.
Post-optimality propagation: To ensure that uncertainty in the optimization solution can be
characterized efficiently, we require algorithms that efficiently predict how the optimization solution
changes as a function of the model discrepancy representation. This may be accomplished through
linearization of the optimality system. However, if the model discrepancy is large, linearization may
introduce intolerable error in the optimal solution update. Post-optimality continuation algorithms
may be leveraged to trade off accuracy and computational efficiency.

1.5. Position of HDSA-MD in the literature

To position HDSA-MD in the literature, we emphasize our assumptions that: (i) the high-fidelity
model can only be evaluated a small number of times, (ii) the high-fidelity model does not admit
derivatives, and (iii) the optimization variable dimension is large. We review a variety of approaches
below, but note at the outset that the HDSA-MD framework is the only approach that is applicable
under all three assumptions.

 18

Derivative-free optimization algorithms [8] can interface with a high-fidelity model, treating it as a
black-box function evaluator. However, such approaches generally require many model evaluations
and are not scalable for optimization in high-dimensional spaces. When a low-fidelity model is
available, multi-fidelity optimization algorithms [2, 1] combine the low- and high-fidelity models
within the optimization loop. These approaches require evaluating the high-fidelity model within
the optimization loop, as well as access to high-fidelity model derivatives. The multi-fidelity
optimization algorithm from [11] does not require high-fidelity model derivatives; however, it faces
challenges scaling to high-dimensional problems.

Various multi-fidelity methods [12] have been developed in other contexts for a wide range of goals
spanning optimization and uncertainty quantification tasks. The goal in most of these methods is
to compute the high-fidelity solution faster by combining low- and high-fidelity model evaluations.
The HDSA-MD framework is different in that we start with an assumption of limited access to
the high-fidelity model and seek to extract as much information as possible from it and quantify
uncertainty due to data limitations.

Our approach has some conceptual similarities with the model discrepancy analysis of Kennedy
and O’Hagan [9] in that we consider an additive discrepancy function that is calibrated in a
Bayesian framework. However, HDSA-MD differs considerably by focusing on general determin-
istic optimization problems (design, control, and inversion). Furthermore, since we assume that
the high-fidelity model may be evaluated for different inputs, unlike [9], we represent the model
discrepancy as a function of the optimization variable and seek to explicitly capture variation in the
model discrepancy with respect to the optimization variable.

1.6. Outline of the project

This project developed novel theory and algorithms to enable the methodology outlined above. The
advances include:

 • Development of a rigorous and computationally efficient framework for Bayesian calibration
 of the model discrepancy representation.

 • Development of a Bayesian optimal experimental design algorithm to determine how the high-
 fidelity model evaluations should be conducted to minimize uncertainty in the optimization
 solution.

 • Development of an efficient framework for post-optimality propagation based on linearization
 and low-rank approximation.

 • Development of post-optimality continuation algorithms that reduce linearization error while
 preserving computational efficiency.

 • Development of prototyping software in Matlab and scalable high-performance computing
 software in C++ that implements all of the aforementioned algorithmic developments.

 • Interfacing of the C++ software with the Sandia codes MrHyDE and Albany.

2. SUMMARY OF PUBLICATIONS

This project produced four articles [6, 10, 4, 3]. In what follows, we provide a summary of each
article and explain its role in achieving the project objectives. The project builds on a previous
article [5], which we include in the summary below. To highlight the progression of development,
we label these articles as “Article X" with X=0,1,2,3,4, where X=0 is the foundational article
predating the project.

2.1. Summary of Article 0: The foundation of HDSA-MD

This article [5] introduces the core HDSA-MD framework that underpins the broader project by
proposing a mathematically systematic way to use a small number of high-fidelity simulations to
improve a low-fidelity optimal solution without requiring high-fidelity derivatives or embedding
the high-fidelity code inside the optimization loop. A parameterized discrepancy representation is
introduced that augments the low-fidelity state with an additive correction intended to approximate
the difference between the high- and low-fidelity solution operators. A critical feature of the
discrepancy representation is its parameterization that admits a Kronecker-product structure. This
ensures that the discrepancy representation parameters, whose dimension scales as the product of
the state and optimization variable dimensions, can be efficiently represented and computation can
be compressed into a dimension that scales as 𝑛𝑢 + 𝑛 𝑧 ≪ 𝑛𝑢 𝑛 𝑧 , where 𝑛𝑢 and 𝑛 𝑧 denote the state and
optimization variable dimensions, respectively. The discrepancy representation is calibrated using
a limited number of high-fidelity model evaluations. By applying the Implicit Function Theorem
to the first-order optimality system of the low-fidelity optimization problem, a sensitivity operator
is derived that maps perturbations in the discrepancy model to updates in the optimization solution.
By applying the sensitivity operator in the direction of the calibrated discrepancy representation,
an optimal solution update formula is derived that maps limited high-fidelity information to an
improvement in the low-fidelity optimizer.
Numerical examples demonstrate the potential of the approach. In a one-dimensional advection-
diffusion control problem, the method uses only two high-fidelity evaluations to update a low-fidelity
optimum and recover a control much closer to the true high-fidelity solution. In a larger fluid flow
example, a Stokes model is used as a low-fidelity approximation to a Navier-Stokes model. The
HDSA-MD framework achieves a substantial improvement in the control solution using only a
single Navier-Stokes forward solve.
This article establishes the foundation for HDSA-MD by introducing the key idea of correcting
low-fidelity optimization solutions through a calibrated model discrepancy and a post-optimality
update whose computational cost is comparable to that of the low-fidelity PDE-constrained opti-
mization problem. This project extends the framework introduced in Article 0 to include rigorous

 21

uncertainty quantification, continuation-based updates to mitigate post-optimality linearization er-
ror, and optimal experimental design to determine how the high-fidelity model evaluations should
be conducted.

2.2. Summary of Article 1: Enabling uncertainty quantification

Limitations on high-fidelity model evaluations translate into considerable uncertainty in the optimal
solution update; quantifying that uncertainty is essential to making the updated optimization
solution useful for supporting decisions. This article [6] begins from the same premise as Article
0: one solves a low-fidelity optimization problem, uses a small number of high-fidelity model
evaluations to infer the discrepancy between the high- and low-fidelity models, and then propagates
the discrepancy through post-optimality sensitivities to improve the optimization solution. Whereas
Article 0 focused primarily on computing a single updated solution, this article formulates a
complete Bayesian framework for propagating discrepancy uncertainty through the optimization
problem and thereby characterizing uncertainty in the optimization solution itself.
A first contribution is the formulation of the discrepancy prior (Article 0 introduced a prior that
was ineffective). This article replaces the earlier prior construction with a more rigorous and
interpretable one. Specifically, a Gaussian prior is derived based on the specification of a precision
matrix that defines a weighted inner product. Computational efficiency is achieved by decomposing
the high-dimensional precision operator into Kronecker products of state and optimization vari-
able operators. In addition to enabling computational efficiency, the prior formulation facilitates
interpretability of the prior and specification of the prior hyperparameters.
A second contribution is the derivation of closed-form expressions for posterior mean and posterior
samples that avoid computation in the extremely large discrepancy parameter space. By carefully
exploiting Kronecker-product structure in the discrepancy representation, prior precision, and data
operators, the article derives scalable sampling algorithms that involve linear solves in the state
and optimization spaces. The resulting posterior admits a useful decomposition into components
informed by the high-fidelity data and components informed primarily by the prior. This decom-
position provides both computational efficiency and conceptual insight for understanding which
uncertainties are most influential.
A third contribution is the introduction of a Hessian-based projection for posterior optimal solution
updating. Uncertainty in the calibrated discrepancy can produce large variations in the optimization
solution along directions where the objective function has very low curvature, even though such
variations have little effect on the objective value itself. To address this, the posterior optimal
solution is projected onto a subspace spanned by dominant Hessian eigenvectors. This has two
benefits: (i) it improves interpretability and robustness by filtering out spurious uncertainty in
directions that are not important for optimization performance, and (ii) it reduces computational
cost by allowing posterior sampling to be performed in a lower-dimensional subspace.
Numerical examples illustrate the capabilities of the framework. A diffusion-reaction example
demonstrates how posterior samples describe uncertainty in an updated optimal source term. A
mass-spring control example shows that the method can recover a tightly concentrated posterior

 22

around an improved controller when only a few high-fidelity evaluations are available. A nonlinear
advection-diffusion example highlights the role of the Hessian projection, showing that substan-
tial uncertainty may exist in the optimization solution while having relatively little impact on
performance because that uncertainty is in objective-insensitive directions.

2.3. Summary of Article 2: Post-optimality continuation

This article [4] develops the post-optimality continuation ideas to enhance the HDSA-MD frame-
work by reducing linearization error. Article 2 focuses on methodological development for a simpler
(although important) problem: efficiently tracking the solution of a PDE-constrained inverse prob-
lem as uncertain auxiliary model parameters vary. In many inverse problems, one fixes auxiliary
model parameters at a nominal value to simplify the inverse problem that estimates the parameter
of interest. However, the solution of the inverse problem depends on the auxiliary parameters,
and hence understanding how the inverse problem solution varies with respect to the auxiliary
parameters is important. A straightforward approach is to repeatedly re-solve the inverse problem
for each potential auxiliary parameter, but solving many large-scale PDE-constrained optimization
problems is computationally prohibitive. This article proposes a more efficient alternative based
on pseudo-time continuation. Furthermore, an adaptive preconditioning strategy is introduced that
makes the continuation process more efficient so that computation for many auxiliary parameter
samples is computationally tractable.
The core idea is to consider the minimizer of the inverse problem as a function of the auxiliary
parameters and then track that minimizer along a path from a nominal auxiliary parameter value to
a perturbed one. Earlier HDSA work provides a linear approximation of this auxiliary parameter-
to-inverse problem solution map through post-optimality sensitivities, but such linearization can
be inaccurate when the auxiliary parameter perturbation is large. This article addresses that
limitation by formulating an ordinary differential equation (ODE) for the evolution of the optimizer
along the parameter path. Solving this ODE yields a continuation strategy that replaces a single
linear approximation with a sequence of local linear approximations. Varying the ODE time
step size provides a mechanism to trade off accuracy and computational cost, with one time step
corresponding to the linear approximation from earlier HDSA work.
A central component of the article is the use of predictor-corrector continuation algorithms tailored
to the PDE-constrained optimization problem. Explicit time stepping methods, including a forward
Euler and a modified Euler predictor, are combined with Newton-based corrector steps that reimpose
first-order optimality at each continuation step. This is important because it leverages the fact that
the evolving solution should remain a local minimizer of a parameterized optimization problem,
not merely the solution of a generic ODE.
The significant advance in this article is its treatment of Hessian solves, which form the computa-
tional bottleneck in the ODE time stepping process since each step requires applying the inverse
Hessian. In large-scale PDE-constrained optimization problems, the Hessian inversion must be
done iteratively using Krylov methods, with each Hessian-vector product requiring incremental
state and adjoint PDE solves. To reduce this cost, the article introduces adaptive quasi-Newton
preconditioners that evolve along the continuation path. Two update mechanisms are proposed.

 23

The first, called the parametric quasi-Newton update, incorporates the effect of changing auxiliary
parameters to produce a BFGS-type update adapted to the parameterized setting. The second,
the block quasi-Newton update, uses Hessian-vector product information generated in the previous
inverse Hessian computation to enrich the preconditioner. The computational cost of this precon-
ditioner update is negligible, as no additional PDE solves are required. These two preconditioner
updates create a self-improving framework in the sense that if a step requires a significant number of
Hessian-vector products to execute the linear solve, this data is used to enhance the preconditioner
so that subsequent Hessian inversions may be computed more efficiently.
Numerical examples illustrate both the algorithmic behavior and the practical value of the approach.
In a diffusion coefficient estimation problem governed by a Poisson equation, the article compares
forward Euler and modified Euler predictors, fixed and adaptive preconditioners, and different
choices of time step sizes, solver tolerances, and preconditioner ranks. These experiments show that
adaptive preconditioning ensures computational efficiency and reduces sensitivity to algorithmic
parameters. A second, more challenging example, concerns bedrock inversion for the Greenland Ice
Sheet using a nonlinear shallow ice model. In this setting, the inverse problem is high-dimensional,
nonlinear, and computationally intensive. The preconditioned pseudo-time continuation method is
shown to efficiently compute perturbed inverse solutions while avoiding parameter tuning issues
that arise if an optimization algorithm is used to solve the perturbed problem.

2.4. Summary of Article 3: Post-optimality continuation and optimal experimental
 design in the HDSA-MD framework

This article [10] develops the HDSA-MD framework in two directions: (i) it incorporates post-
optimality continuation from Article 2 in the model discrepancy setting, and (ii) it develops a
principled approach for selecting high-fidelity model evaluations.
The first major contribution of this article, the extension of post-optimality continuation to the
HDSA-MD setting, evolves the model discrepancy parameters along a path from zero discrepancy
to the posterior discrepancy parameters. The optimization solution is propagated along this path in
a reduced subspace defined by leading Hessian eigenvectors, thus restricting updates to a subspace
where the objective function has significant curvature. In the projected space, the initial Hessian
preconditioner is exact because it uses the decomposition computed to define the subspace. The
Hessian preconditioner updates ensure that the preconditioner remains effective throughout the
time stepping process. This yields a computationally efficient sequence of linear approximations
that accurately tracks how the optimal solution changes as a function of the model discrepancy.
The second major contribution of this article, the development of an optimal experimental design
(OED) strategy, leverages the Bayesian model discrepancy calibration framework to sequentially
select the inputs used in the high-fidelity model evaluations. This is important since the number
of high-fidelity simulations is limited, so each evaluation must be chosen carefully. The article
formulates a goal-oriented Bayesian design problem: determine new high-fidelity evaluations to
reduce posterior uncertainty in the discrepancy in a neighborhood of the current best estimate of
the updated optimal solution. Rather than minimizing global discrepancy uncertainty, the criterion
is localized around the optimization-relevant region, which better aligns the design objective with

 24

the ultimate goal of improving the optimizer. The resulting design criterion, which is defined in
terms of the posterior covariance of the discrepancy, has a statistical interpretation as minimizing
expected Bayes risk for discrepancy prediction near the optimal solution estimate. This criterion
naturally balances exploration and exploitation: it seeks informative data near the predicted optimal
solution while still searching broadly enough to avoid stagnation. The article also introduces an
adaptive strategy to select the size of the design search neighborhood using the distance between
successive optimal solution updates.

Direct evaluation of design criteria is infeasible because the discrepancy representation parameters
live in an extremely high-dimensional space. The article extends the Kronecker-product structure
from posterior analysis developed in Article 1 to derive efficient expressions for the design criterion
and its gradient. As a result, the optimization over candidate high-fidelity evaluation inputs can be
performed without requiring operations in the extremely high-dimensional discrepancy parameter
space. This preserves the scalability of the overall methodology and makes the high-fidelity data
acquisition strategy practical for large-scale applications.

Numerical results highlight both contributions of the article. In a diffusion-reaction example, the
continuation approach improves the quality of the optimal solution update relative to using post-
optimality linearization. The same example is subsequently used to compare the proposed optimal
experimental design strategy against random data acquisition, demonstrating that the designed
evaluations more effectively reduce uncertainty near the relevant optimization region and lead to
better optimization updates. Batched sequential designs are also studied, showing that although
purely sequential acquisition is best when evaluations are extremely scarce, modest batch sizes can
still perform well and may be preferable in settings where parallel high-fidelity simulations are
possible. A second example, involving contaminant source identification in a coupled fluid-flow
setting, demonstrates efficacy of the post-optimality continuation and OED approach for a fully
coupled nonlinear and multi-physics problem, achieving substantial improvement in the recovered
optimal solution using only a small number of high-fidelity evaluations.

2.5. Summary of Article 4: Demonstration of the HDSA-MD framework for
 real-time optimization

This article [3] demonstrates the HDSA-MD framework in a new application regime: real-time
optimization enabled by non-intrusive reduced-order models (ROMs) and model discrepancy-based
solution correction. These concepts can be instrumental in supporting the deployment of digital
twins for which optimization decisions must be made under strict wall-clock-time constraints. The
central challenge is that high-fidelity PDE models are too expensive to be used inside an optimization
loop in real time. Rather, high-fidelity simulations are run using nominal parameters in an offline
phase to train a ROM. An optimization problem constrained by the ROM is solved in an online phase.
However, the parameter regime (e.g., atmospheric conditions) in the online phase may be outside
the offline ROM training dataset. This article proposes using HDSA-MD as an online correction
mechanism. By computing a limited number of high-fidelity evaluations with the online parameter
values and using HDSA-MD to update the ROM optimal solution, the article demonstrates the
potential for higher-fidelity real-time optimization. This yields a corrected optimization solution

 25

without requiring intrusion into the high-fidelity model or repeated high-fidelity solves within the
online optimization process.
The first numerical example concerns atmospheric contaminant control governed by a nonlinear
advection-diffusion-reaction system. A ROM is trained offline using operator inference with an
inaccurate nominal wind field, then used online to solve a decontaminant injection control problem.
Because the true wind field differs substantially from the training conditions, the ROM-constrained
optimizer produces a control that performs poorly. A single high-fidelity simulation with the
true wind conditions is used to calibrate a model discrepancy representation and compute the
optimization solution update. This optimal controller update significantly improves contaminant
suppression and illustrates the use case: offline preparation under uncertain conditions, followed
by rapid online correction when better environmental information becomes available.
The second example concerns wildfire ignition location estimation using the WRF-SFIRE model.
Here, the forward model consists of coupled nonlinear PDEs governing both fire propagation
and atmospheric dynamics, and is impractical to optimize directly. A neural operator ROM is
trained offline using nominal atmospheric conditions, and then used online to solve an inverse
problem for the ignition point. Because the true wind initialization differs substantially from the
training conditions, the ROM-based ignition estimate is far from the true ignition location. The
article shows that a single coupled wildfire simulation with the true atmospheric conditions is
sufficient for HDSA-MD to substantially reduce the ignition-location error. Moreover, the posterior
uncertainty produced by the HDSA-MD framework provides a confidence region for the corrected
ignition estimate that contains the true ignition point.

3. SUMMARY OF SOFTWARE PRODUCTS

Another outcome of this project was the development of software to prototype, test, and deploy the
hyper-differential sensitivity analysis with respect to model discrepancy (HDSA-MD) methodology.
To this end, the project produced two complementary software capabilities: object-oriented libraries
written in Matlab and C++. The Matlab code, Sola1 , is a rapid prototyping environment for
developing and testing new algorithms. This is valuable in the early stages of method development,
when new formulations are being designed and validated. The C++ library, HdsaLib2 , mirrors
the Matlab design in an object-oriented framework while targeting scalable, high-performance
computing applications. This two-level software strategy was an important part of the project:
it enabled fast algorithmic exploration in Matlab while preserving a direct path to larger-scale
implementations suitable for production scientific computing environments.

This chapter summarizes the mathematical structure and software design of the model discrepancy
capabilities developed in these codes. We provide a self-contained summary of the relevant
mathematical objects in the HDSA-MD framework and then highlight where and how they are
implemented in the software.

3.4. Software architecture

The software architecture for the HDSA-MD capability is organized similarly in Sola and HdsaLib.
In both code bases, the relevant functionality resides in the model_discrepancy module, which
is divided into two main parts:
 • interfaces, which define the user-facing abstractions required to connect a new optimiza-
 tion problem and prior model to the HDSA-MD framework, and
 • analysis, which implements prior sampling, posterior calibration, posterior propagation,
 and related auxiliary algorithms.
This design reflects the project’s emphasis on modularity. Users provide problem-specific in-
formation—such as the objective function and low-fidelity model, discrepancy data, and prior
operators—through the interface classes. The analysis classes query methods from these interface
classes to perform HDSA-MD computations.

3.4.1. Interfaces

The interfaces define the minimal information needed to run the HDSA-MD analysis. At the highest
level, the implementation relies on four principal abstract base classes:
 • MD_u_Prior_Interface

 29

 • MD_z_Prior_Interface

 • MD_Opt_Prob_Interface

 • MD_Data_Interface

The classes MD_Opt_Prob_Interface and MD_Data_Interface define, respectively, the opti-
mization problem quantities needed for post-optimality analysis and the discrepancy data needed for
Bayesian calibration. The classes MD_u_Prior_Interface and MD_z_Prior_Interface define
the prior precision operators on the state and optimization variable spaces, respectively. These in-
terfaces are implemented through derived classes tailored to specific applications, including steady
and transient problems, function-valued decision variables, and different prior covariance models.
The code also includes supporting interface layers for prior hyperparameter selection.

3.4.2. Analysis modules

The analysis portion of the software is divided into three main subdirectories:

 • prior

 • posterior

 • auxiliary

The prior subdirectory contains code that implements algorithms for generating prior discrepancy
samples and, where relevant, propagating those prior samples through the optimization problem.
This is useful both for prior interpretation and for hyperparameter tuning.

The posterior subdirectory contains code that implements posterior mean computation, poste-
rior sampling, and posterior propagation through the optimization problem. In particular, the
class MD_Linearization_Update performs post-optimality propagation using the linearized
sensitivity operator, while the class MD_Continuation_Update extends this capability using
pseudo-time continuation methods. The continuation implementation interfaces with the separate
pseudo_time_continuation module, which is general enough to support continuation for both
model parameters and model discrepancy representation parameters.

The auxiliary subdirectory contains additional analysis tools that support the main algorithms.
These include:

 • Hessian eigendecomposition routines used to define the projector in (3.8),

 • spectral analysis tools for Laplacian-like prior operators used in the prior models, and

 • Bayesian optimal experimental design tools for selecting high-fidelity evaluation inputs.

Note that at the time of writing, this document is consistent with the software. However, the
software is under active development, and the structure may change in the future.

3.5. Implementing a new example

To apply the software to a new problem, the user must implement the optimization problem and
data interfaces in a way that is consistent with the problem’s existing discretization, solvers, and
derivative infrastructure. Specifically, the user must provide implementations of:
 • MD_Opt_Prob_Interface, to expose the low-fidelity optimization quantities required for
 post-optimality analysis, and
 • MD_Data_Interface, to provide the discrepancy data arising from high-fidelity evaluations.
The user must also select appropriate derived classes for the state and optimization variable priors.
This choice depends on the structure of the problem, such as whether the state and decision variables
are steady or transient, spatially distributed, or represented through finite-dimensional parameteri-
zations. The example and test problems in the code serve as templates for these implementations.

4. CONCLUSION

This project matured hyper-differential sensitivity analysis with respect to model discrepancy
(HDSA-MD) from an initial concept to a powerful capability. The collection of articles and
software produced by the project positions HDSA-MD as a viable tool to address challenges in
many applications. The original project motivation focused on problems where a high-fidelity
model is sufficiently complex that it can only be evaluated sparingly in an offline fashion. This is
common with many production codes that require expertise to run and potentially hours or even
days of wall-clock time from computing cluster queue submission to job completion. As was
demonstrated in the project, another impactful application area is real-time optimization needed
to support digital twins. In this context, we consider high-fidelity models that may be evaluated
in real-time, but only a small number of such evaluations is feasible within the time constraints.
For instance, if a solution is needed in 10 minutes of wall-clock time and the high-fidelity solution
requires 4 minutes of wall-clock time to evaluate. We have demonstrated the power of HDSA-MD
in this context to significantly improve the optimization solutions required in both calibration and
control problems relevant for operational digital twins.
The foundation laid by this project has already had an impact in other projects. Examples include
work on automating prior hyperparameter selection [7]. Ongoing work in other projects is exploring
extensions of the HDSA-MD framework for stochastic and risk-averse optimization problems.
Many other methodological extensions may be considered in future projects as well. When using
post-optimality continuation algorithms to compute samples of the optimal solution, we may
explore new approaches to judiciously traverse the parameter space and reuse computation across
the samples. In a different line of research, future work may consider more general discrepancy
parameterizations and priors. Examples include designing the discrepancy parameterizations to
enforce known physics constraints such as conservation properties or using data from similar
problems to learn a more informed discrepancy parameterization and/or prior. An ultimate goal
is a foundation model for the discrepancy representation that leverages knowledge of the physics
from theoretical principles and existing datasets. Such approaches will enable more accurate
optimal solution updates with less uncertainty. Lastly, we highlight the potential of using high-
fidelity derivative information. One of the assumptions motivating this work was to avoid requiring
such information. In some applications, it is accessible and can be used to improve the analysis.
Rigorous incorporation of derivative information through either the discrepancy parameterization
or the likelihood function in calibration presents promising avenues for future research.
