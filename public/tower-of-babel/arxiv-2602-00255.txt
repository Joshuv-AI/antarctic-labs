Lower bounds on non-local computation from controllable correlation
Richard Cleve, Alex May

Abstract
Understanding entanglement cost in non-local quantum computation (NLQC) is relevant to complexity, cryptography, gravity, and other areas. This entanglement cost is largely uncharacterized; previous lower bound techniques apply to narrowly defined cases, and proving lower bounds on most simple unitaries has remained open. Here, we give two new lower bound techniques that can be evaluated for any unitary, based on their controllable correlation and controllable entanglement . For Haar random two qubit unitaries, our techniques typically lead to non-trivial lower bounds. Further, we obtain lower bounds on most of the commonly studied two qubit quantum gates, including CNOT, DCNOT, SWAP \sqrt{\text{SWAP}} , and the XX interaction, none of which previously had known lower bounds. For the CNOT gate, one of our techniques gives a tight lower bound, fully resolving its entanglement cost. The resulting lower bounds have parallel repetition properties, and apply in the noisy setting.

The starting point for the lower bounds is the set-up shown in figure

.
We consider the two input wires to the quantum operation of interest.
Consider a correlated state
P_{QA}
.
We refer to
Q
as the reference system,

B
as the control.
Then, we are interested in the total correlation or entanglement between

If the total correlation or entanglement in this state can be varied by adjusting the input to the control wire

, then we obtain a lower bound.

In more detail, in the controllable correlation technique we are interested in a unitary
U_{AB}
and how much we can vary the total correlation in
Q\!:\!A
by adjusting
B
.
Take any choice of initial correlated state
P_{QA}
.
Then, defining

\displaystyle\lambda_{1}

\displaystyle=\max_{\phi^{1}}I(Q:A)_{U_{AB}(P_{QA}\otimes\phi^{1})U_{AB}^{\dagger}},

\displaystyle\lambda_{2}

\displaystyle=\min_{\phi^{2}}I(Q:A)_{U_{AB}(P_{QA}\otimes\phi^{2})U_{AB}^{\dagger}},

(1)

we find that

\displaystyle\boxed{E_{f}(L:R)_{\Psi}\geq\frac{\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{2}}{2}}

(2)

where
E_{f}(L:R)_{\Psi}
is the entanglement of formation in the distributed resource system
\Psi_{LR}
used in the NLQC protocol.

In the controllable entanglement technique we are again interested in a unitary
U_{AB}
and making the entanglement in
Q\!:\!A
, quantified by the entanglement of formation, large on one input to the control wire, and then the state on
Q\!:\!A
close (in trace distance) to separable on a second input.
More specifically, we always take the reference and input to be maximally entangled,
P_{QA}=\Psi^{+}_{QA}
, and define

\displaystyle\lambda_{1}:=\max_{\phi^{1}}E_{f}(Q:A)_{U_{AB}(\Psi^{+}\otimes\phi^{1}_{B})U_{AB}^{\dagger}},\qquad\lambda_{2}:=\min_{\phi^{2}}\min_{\sigma\in SEP}\|\tr_{B}U_{AB}(\Psi^{+}_{QA}\otimes\phi^{2}_{B})U_{AB}^{\dagger}-\sigma_{QA}\|_{1}.

With these definitions, we obtain the lower bound

\displaystyle\boxed{E_{f}(L:R)_{\Psi}\geq\lambda_{1}-2\lambda_{2}^{1/4}}

(3)

under the assumption that
\lambda_{2}\leq 1
.
Both this and the bound from the controllable correlation can be adapted to bound noisy implementations of the relevant unitary.
We give the full bounds with error terms in the main text.

Both lower bound techniques have convenient parallel repetition properties.
For the controllable correlation, if
G
has lower bound
\Delta\lambda\equiv(\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{2})/2
then we find
G^{\otimes n}
has lower bound at least
n\Delta\lambda
.
For the controllable entanglement, because of the requirement that
\lambda_{2}\leq 1
, parallel repetition only holds when
\lambda_{2}=0
.
In this case though, a lower bound of
\lambda_{1}
on a unitary
G
implies a lower bound of
n\lambda_{1}
on
G^{\otimes n}
.

An important special case is the CNOT gate.
For CNOT, the controllable entanglement technique has values
\lambda_{1}=1
,
\lambda_{2}=0
.
Thus we obtain a tight lower bound of
E_{f}(L:R)_{\Psi}\geq n
to implement
n
instances of the CNOT gate in parallel.

A limitation of our results is that we only bound the entanglement cost of unitary operations; new techniques would be needed to address general quantum channels.
We comment further on this in the discussion.

Quantum information tools

We recall some quantum information theory tools that we make use of, and fix our notation.

2.1
Quantum states and distances

We label the dimension of a Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}_{A}
by
d_{A}
, and the (base 2) log dimension by
n_{A}=\log d_{A}
.
Throughout this work
\log
denotes the base 2 logarithm, while
\ln
denotes the natural logarithm.
When considering entanglement across bipartitions of a quantum state
\ket{\psi}_{AB}
, we refer to entanglement across
A:B
where the colon indicates the partitioning of the systems.
We use the notation

\displaystyle\ket{\Psi^+}_{AB}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(\ket{00}_{AB}+\ket{11}_{AB}\right)

(4)

for this particular maximally entangled state of two qubits, and the notation

\displaystyle\rho_{cc}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\outerproduct{00}{00}+\outerproduct{11}{11}\right)

(5)

for the maximally classically correlated state of two qubits.

We quantify the distance between quantum states with the one-norm distance,

\displaystyle\|\rho-\sigma\|_{1}=\tr|\rho-\sigma|.

(6)

Note that
\|\rho-\sigma\|_{1}/2
is known as the trace distance.

We quantify the distance between quantum channels using the diamond norm distance.

Definition 1

Let
\mathbfcal{N}_{B\rightarrow C},\mathbfcal{M}_{B\rightarrow C}:\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}_{A})\rightarrow\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}_{B})
be quantum channels.
The
diamond norm distance
is defined by

\displaystyle\|\mathbfcal{N}_{B\rightarrow C}-\mathbfcal{M}_{B\rightarrow C}\|_{\diamond}=\sup_{d}\max_{\rho_{A_{d}B}}\|\mathbfcal{N}_{B\rightarrow C}(\Psi_{A_{d}B})-\mathbfcal{M}_{B\rightarrow C}(\Psi_{A_{d}B})\|_{1}

(7)

where
\mathcal{H}_{A_{d}}
is a
d
dimensional Hilbert space.

A related notion of the closeness of quantum states is the operational fidelity,

Definition 2

Let
\mathbfcal{N}_{B\rightarrow C},\mathbfcal{M}_{B\rightarrow C}:\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}_{A})\rightarrow\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}_{B})
be quantum channels. The
operational fidelity
is defined by

\displaystyle F_{op}(\mathcal{E},\mathcal{F})=\underset{\ket{\psi}_{AB}}{\inf}F(\mathcal{E}_{B\rightarrow C}(\outerproduct{\psi}{\psi}_{AB}),\mathcal{F}_{B\rightarrow C}(\outerproduct{\psi}{\psi}_{AB}))

(8)

The following relationship between the operational fidelity and the diamond norm follows immediately from the Fuchs van de Graff inequalities.

Lemma 3

Given quantum channels
\mathcal{E}
,
\mathcal{F}
, closeness in diamond norm distance and closeness in operational fidelity are equivalent.
In particular we have,

\displaystyle 1-\sqrt{F(\mathcal{E},\mathcal{F})}\leq\frac{1}{2}||\mathcal{E}-\mathcal{F}||_{\diamond}\leq\sqrt{1-F(\mathcal{E},\mathcal{F})}

(9)

Next we define the entanglement fidelity
[
31
,
27
]
, which gives a useful notion of how well a quantum channel preserves quantum information.

Definition 4

The
entanglement fidelity
of a density matrix
\rho_{A}
and channel
\mathcal{N}_{A}
to be

\displaystyle F_{e}(\rho_{A},\mathcal{N}_{A})=\bra{\psi^\rho}_{AB}\mathcal{I}_{B}\otimes\mathcal{N}_{A}(\psi^{\rho}_{AB})\ket{\psi^\rho}_{AB}

(10)

where
\ket{\psi^\rho}_{AB}
is any purification of
\rho_{A}
.

Note that
F_{e}
is well defined because all purifications are related by unitaries on the purifying system.

We will make use of the following claim.

Lemma 5

Suppose that
\{\mathcal{N}^{i}\}_{i}
are quantum channels, and let
U
be a unitary.
Then if
\|\sum_{i}p_{i}\mathcal{N}^{i}-U\|_{\diamond}\leq\epsilon
, then we have that
\sum_{i}p_{i}\|\mathcal{N}^{i}-U\|_{\diamond}
.

Proof.
We have by assumption that

\displaystyle\epsilon\geq\left\|\sum_{i}p_{i}\mathcal{N}^{i}-U\right\|_{\diamond}

(11)

We claim that this implies the
\mathcal{N}^{i}
are close to
U
on average.
To see this, first use that

\displaystyle\frac{\epsilon}{2}\geq\frac{1}{2}\left\|\sum_{i}p_{i}\mathcal{N}^{i}-U\right\|_{\diamond}\geq 1-\sqrt{F_{op}\left(\sum_{i}p_{i}\mathcal{N}^{i},U\right)}=1-\sqrt{\sum_{i}p_{i}F_{op}(\mathcal{N}^{i},U)}.

(12)

We used lemma
3
, and the last step relied on one entry in the operational fidelity being a unitary.
Rearranging, we get that

\displaystyle(1-\epsilon/2)^{2}\geq\sum_{i}p_{i}F_{op}(\mathcal{N}^{i},U).

(13)

We’d like to upper bound the average
\sum_{i}p_{i}\|\mathcal{N}^{i}-U\|_{\diamond}
.
To do this,

\displaystyle\sum_{i}p_{i}\|\mathcal{N}^{i}-U\|_{\diamond}

\displaystyle\leq 2\sum_{i}p_{i}\sqrt{1-F_{op}(\mathcal{N}^{i},U)}

\displaystyle\leq 2\sqrt{1-\sum_{i}p_{i}F_{op}(\mathcal{N}^{i},U)}

\displaystyle\leq 2\sqrt{1-(1-\epsilon/2)^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq 2\sqrt{\epsilon}.

(14)

In the first line we used lemma
3
, and in the second we used concavity of the function
f(x)=\sqrt{1-x}
.

For intuition, this statement is saying that if a convex mixture of channels is close to a unitary channel, then the average distance of the element in the mixture must be close to the unitary channel.
This happens because a unitary channel is an extreme point in the convex space of quantum channels, and would be false if we replaced
U
with a general quantum channel.

2.2
Entropy and entanglement

The von Neumann entropy of a state
\rho_{A}
is

\displaystyle S(A)_{\rho}=-\tr\rho_{A}\log\rho_{A}.

(15)

The relative entropy is defined as

\displaystyle D(\rho||\sigma)=\tr(\rho\log\rho-\rho\log\sigma).

(16)

The relative entropy is related to the trace distance by Pinsker’s inequality.

Lemma 6

(Quantum Pinsker inequality)

The relative entropy
D(\rho||\sigma)
and the one-norm
\|\rho-\sigma\|_{1}
are related by

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\ln 2}\|\rho-\sigma\|_{1}^{2}\leq D(\rho||\sigma).

(17)

The mutual information is defined by

\displaystyle I(A:B)_{\rho}=S(A)_{\rho}+S(B)_{\rho}-S(AB)_{\rho}.

(18)

The mutual information satisfies the following continuity property.

Lemma 7

Suppose that
\frac{1}{2}\|\rho-\sigma\|_{1}=\epsilon
.
Then

\displaystyle|I(A:B)_{\rho}-I(A:B)_{\sigma}|\leq 3n_{A}\epsilon+2(1+\epsilon)h\left(\frac{\epsilon}{1+\epsilon}\right).

(19)

We also make use of the conditional quantum mutual information,

\displaystyle I(A:B|C)_{\rho}=S(AC)_{\rho}+S(BC)_{\rho}-S(C)_{\rho}-S(ABC)_{\rho}.

(20)

The mutual and conditional mutual informations are related by the chain rule,

\displaystyle I(A:BC)_{\rho}=I(A:B|C)_{\rho}+I(A:C)_{\rho}

(21)

We have the following statement about the conditional mutual information.

Lemma 8

The quantum conditional mutual information satisfies the data processing inequality,

\displaystyle I(A:B|C)_{\rho}\geq I(A:B|C)_{\mathcal{N}_{B}(\rho)}.

(22)

Proof.
This is more commonly stated for the mutual information (corresponding to
C=\varnothing
) but the statement for the conditional mutual information follows immediately,

\displaystyle I(A:B|C)_{\rho}

\displaystyle=I(A:BC)_{\rho}-I(A:C)_{\rho}

\displaystyle\geq I(A:BC)_{\mathcal{N}_{B}(\rho)}-I(A:C)_{\rho}

\displaystyle=I(A:BC)_{\mathcal{N}_{B}(\rho)}-I(A:C)_{\mathcal{N}_{B}(\rho)}

\displaystyle=I(A:B|C)_{\mathcal{N}_{B}(\rho)}.

(23)

We use the chain rule in the first and last lines, data processing for the mutual information in the second line, the third line is trivial, and the chain rule again in the last line.

We next introduce the entanglement of formation
[
19
,
35
]
as a tool for quantifying entanglement.

Definition 9

The
entanglement of formation
is defined as

\displaystyle E_{f}(A:B)_{\rho}=\min_{\{p_{i},\ket{\psi_i}\}}\sum_{i}p_{i}S(B)_{\psi_{i}},

(24)

where the minimization is over ensembles
\{p_{i},\ket{\psi_i}\}
such that
\rho=\sum_{i}p_{i}\outerproduct{\psi_i}{\psi_i}
.

Note that an equivalent definition would replace
S(A)_{\psi_{i}}
with
S(B)_{\psi_{i}}
above.
The entanglement of formation is a faithful measure of entanglement, meaning that it is zero if and only if
\rho
is separable.
This is easy to see from its definition: if it is separable so that

\displaystyle\rho_{AB}=\sum_{i}p_{i}\rho_{A}^{i}\otimes\rho^{i}_{B}

(25)

then we introduce decompositions
\rho_{A}^{i}=\sum_{k}\lambda_{k}^{i}\outerproduct{\phi_k^i}{\phi_k^i}_{A}
and
\rho_{B}^{i}=\sum_{k}\lambda_{k}^{i}\outerproduct{\varphi_k^i}{\varphi_k^i}_{B}
and we see that
E_{f}(A:B)_{\rho}=0
.
Conversely, if
E_{f}(A:B)_{\rho}=0
then there exists a decomposition into states
\psi_{i}
such that
S(B)_{\psi_{i}}=0
for all
i
, which means all
\psi_{i}
are tensor product, and hence
\rho
is separable.

The entanglement of formation satisfies the following property, which shows that it can’t grow too much as you add subsystems.

Lemma 10

The entanglement of formation satisfies

\displaystyle E_{f}(A:BC)_{\rho}\leq E_{f}(A:B)_{\rho}+\log d_{C}.

(26)

This statement follows from the definition of the entanglement of formation, subadditivity of the von Neumann entropy, and the statement
S(X)\leq\log d_{X}
.

We also have a data processing inequality for the entanglement of formation
[
9
]
.

Lemma 11

The entanglement of formation is non-increasing under the action of a local quantum channel,
2
2

2

In fact the entanglement of formation is also decreasing under LOCC operations, although we will not need that stronger property here.

\displaystyle E_{f}(A:B)_{\rho_{AB}}\geq E_{f}(A:B^{\prime})_{\mathcal{I}_{A}\otimes\mathcal{N}_{B\rightarrow B^{\prime}}(\rho_{AB})}.

(27)

Finally, we recall a Fannes type continuity bound
[
28
,
34
]
for the entanglement of formation.

Lemma 12

Consider two states
\rho_{AB}
,
\sigma_{AB}
with
\|\sigma_{AB}-\rho_{AB}\|\leq\epsilon
, define
\eta_{\epsilon}=2\sqrt{\epsilon(1-\epsilon)}
and

.
Then, the entanglement of formation of
\rho_{AB}
and

cannot be too different:

\displaystyle|E_{f}(A:B)_{\rho}-E_{f}(A:B)_{\sigma}|

\displaystyle\leq\eta_{\epsilon}\log d+H(\eta_{\epsilon})

(28)

where

and

.

We will also make brief use of a second entanglement measure, defined next.

Definition 13

The relative entropy of entanglement is defined as

\displaystyle E_{R}(A:B)_{\rho}=\min_{\sigma_{AB}\in SEP_{AB}}D(\rho_{AB}||\sigma_{AB}),

where

3
Lower bound from controllable correlation

In this section we discuss the first of our two lower bounds, which considers the controllable correlation.

3.1
Proof of the lower bound

In this section we give our first lower bound technique.
The technique involves correlating one of the inputs to the gate of interest with a reference system in a state
P_{QA}
.
This state must be correlated across
Q\!:\!A
, but otherwise we leave our choice of this state free for now and fix it in concrete examples later.
We obtain a lower bound on entanglement in the resource state if it is possible to control whether correlation in
Q\!:\!A
is preserved or destroyed by choosing the state of the input on
B
.
We first give the following definition, which captures this notion of controlling correlation more precisely.

Definition 14

Consider a unitary
U_{AB}
and choose a state on
QA
, which we label
P_{QA}
, with
n_{Q}=n_{A}
.
Define the states

\displaystyle\rho^{1}_{QAB}

\displaystyle=U_{AB}(P_{QA}\otimes\phi^{1}_{B})U_{AB}^{\dagger},

\displaystyle\rho^{2}_{QAB}

\displaystyle=U_{AB}(P_{QA}\otimes\phi^{2})U_{AB}^{\dagger}.

(30)

We say that
U_{AB}
has
(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})
-controllable correlation if there exists states
\phi^{1}_{B},\phi^{2}_{B}
, and
P_{QA}
such that

\displaystyle\lambda_{1}=I(Q:A)_{\rho^{1}},\qquad\lambda_{2}=I(Q:A)_{\rho^{2}}.

(31)

We always consider the case where
\lambda_{1}>\lambda_{2}
. If there is no choice of states
P_{AB}
,
\phi^{1},\phi^{2}
such that
\lambda_{1}>\lambda_{2}
we say
U_{AB}
is not controllably correlated.

As a simple example, the CNOT
B→A
gate is controllably correlated: choose for instance
P_{QA}=(\rho_{cc})_{QA}=\frac{1}{2}(\outerproduct{00}{00}_{QA}+\outerproduct{11}{11}_{QA})
. Taking first the control on
B
to be
\ket{0}
, CNOT
B→A
acts identically on
A
, leaving
QA
in the maximally classically correlated state
P_{QA}
, so
\lambda_{1}
is
1
.
On the other hand choosing the input on
B
to be the maximally mixed state erases the state on
A
and leaves it product with
Q
, so
\lambda_{2}=0
.
In contrast, the SWAP
AB
gate is not controllably correlated — regardless of the input on
B
, the final state on
QA
will be product, so we always have
\lambda_{1}=\lambda_{2}=0
.
Similarly, the identity is not controllably correlated since
P_{QA}
will not be influenced by the input on
B
.

Our main result of this section is the following theorem, which expresses that controllably correlated unitaries require entanglement to be implemented as a NLQC.
System labels used in the proof are shown in figure
3
.

Theorem 15

Suppose that unitary
U_{AB}
has
(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})
-controllable correlation.
If a NLQC protocol using a resource
\Psi
gives an
\epsilon
-correct (in diamond norm distance with
\epsilon<1/4
) implementation of
U_{AB}
, then

\displaystyle E_{f}(L:R)_{\Psi}\geq\frac{\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{2}}{2}-\Delta(2\sqrt{\epsilon},n_{A})

(32)

where

\displaystyle\Delta(x,n_{A})=3n_{A}\sqrt{x}+2(1+\sqrt{x})h\left(\frac{\sqrt{x}}{1+\sqrt{x}}\right).

(33)

Proof.
Recall that we defined the states

\displaystyle\rho^{1}_{QAB}

\displaystyle=U_{AB}(P_{QA}\otimes\phi^{1}_{B})U_{AB}^{\dagger},

\displaystyle\rho^{2}_{QAB}

\displaystyle=U_{AB}(P_{QA}\otimes\phi^{2})U_{AB}^{\dagger}.

(34)

These are the states resulting from the exact implementation of the unitary
U_{AB}
.
When
U_{AB}
is replaced by the
\epsilon
-close implementation, we label the resulting states as
\sigma^{1}
and
\sigma^{2}
, and note that we have

\displaystyle\|\rho^{1}_{QAB}-\sigma^{1}_{QAB}\|_{1}\leq\epsilon,

\displaystyle\|\rho^{2}_{QAB}-\sigma^{2}_{QAB}\|_{1}\leq\epsilon,

(35)

which follows from the definition of the diamond norm distance.
Note further that we write
\sigma_{QM_{1}M_{2}}^{1,2}
for the states produced mid-way through the NLQC protocol (see figure
3
) upon giving input
\phi^{1,2}
.
As well, we will drop the state label when considering the entropy of
Q
, since this is unaffected by the state on
B
.
Thus
S(Q)=S(Q)_{\sigma^{1}}=S(Q)_{\sigma^{2}}
.

We wish to understand how systems
M_{1}
and
M_{2}
are related to system
Q
.
First observe that by the causal structure of the circuit,

\displaystyle\sigma_{M_{2}Q}^{i}=\sigma_{M_{2}}^{i}\otimes\rho_{Q}.

(36)

This holds regardless of the input on
B
, so for both
\sigma^{1}
and
\sigma^{2}
.
We will use this below in the form

\displaystyle\boxed{S(M_{2}Q)_{\sigma^{1}}=S(M_{2})_{\sigma^{1}}+S(Q)_{\sigma^{1}}.}

(37)

Next, consider
M_{1}
.
We have by assumption that

\displaystyle I(Q:A)_{\rho^{2}}=\lambda_{2}.

(38)

We need to undo the last step of the NLQC circuit so as to construct the state on
M_{1}M_{2}
from that on
A
.
To do this, we consider taking a dilation of the channel
\mathcal{W}^{L}
applied on Alice’s side in the second round, and label the resulting unitary by
W^{L}_{M_{1}M_{2}\rightarrow AE}
where
E
is the ancillary system produced by the unitary.
Then, note that the output system of this unitary must be of the form

\displaystyle\rho_{QAE}^{2}=\rho^{2}_{QA}\otimes\kappa_{E}.

(39)

This holds because the overall action of the NLQC circuit is to implement the unitary
U_{AB}
, so the outputs (on
QAB
) must not be entangled with the environment.
The actual output then, produced by the noisy implementation, must be
\epsilon
close to the perfect implementation on
QA
, and hence (by Uhlmann’s theorem)
\sqrt{\epsilon}
-close after the dilation to the
E
system,

\displaystyle\|\rho_{QA}^{2}\otimes\kappa_{E}-\sigma^{2}_{QAE}\|_{1}\leq\sqrt{\epsilon}.

(40)

The state on
QM_{1}M_{2}
then satisfies

\displaystyle\|(W^{R}_{M_{1}M_{2}\rightarrow AE})^{\dagger}(\rho_{QA}^{2}\otimes\kappa_{E})W^{R}_{M_{1}M_{2}\rightarrow AE}-\sigma^{2}_{QM_{1}M_{2}}\|\leq\sqrt{\epsilon}.

(41)

Consider the state as above produced on giving input
\phi^{2}
, and consider the mutual information
I(Q:M_{1})_{\sigma^{2}}
,

\displaystyle I(Q:M_{1})_{\sigma^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq I(Q:M_{1}M_{2})_{\sigma^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq I(Q:M_{1}M_{2})_{\rho^{2}}+\Delta(\sqrt{\epsilon},n_{Q})

\displaystyle=I(Q:A)_{\rho^{2}}+\Delta(\sqrt{\epsilon},n_{Q})

\displaystyle=\lambda_{2}+\Delta(\sqrt{\epsilon},n_{Q}).

(42)

The first inequality uses data processing, the second inequality uses equation
41
and the continuity statement lemma
7
and the third uses that
\rho_{QM_{1}M_{2}}=(W^{R}_{M_{1}M_{2}\rightarrow AE})^{\dagger}(\rho_{QA}^{2}\otimes\kappa_{E})W^{R}_{M_{1}M_{2}\rightarrow AE}
.
The error term
\Delta_{\epsilon}
is

\displaystyle\Delta(\epsilon,n_{Q})=3n_{Q}\sqrt{\epsilon}+2(1+\sqrt{\epsilon})h\left(\frac{\sqrt{\epsilon}}{1+\sqrt{\epsilon}}\right).

(43)

Finally, notice that the state on
M_{1}Q
cannot depend on the input on
B
, so that

\displaystyle I(Q:M_{1})_{\sigma^{1}}=I(Q:M_{1})_{\sigma^{2}}\leq\lambda_{2}+\Delta(\epsilon,n_{Q}).

(44)

We will use this below in the form

\displaystyle\boxed{S(M_{1}Q)_{\sigma^{1}}\geq S(M_{1})_{\sigma^{1}}+S(Q)_{\sigma^{1}}-\lambda_{2}-\Delta(\epsilon,n_{Q})}.

(45)

This statement is key to our proof and worth commenting on.
Notice that one way to have the correlation with
Q
preserved across
Q\!:\!A
would just be to keep the input system
A
(relabelled as
M_{1}
) on the left.
Taking
P_{QA}
to be maximally entangled, we would violate the above inequality.
However, our reasoning above reveals that even when given input
\phi^{1}
, system
M_{1}
cannot carry all of the correlation with
Q
.
This is because the
QM_{1}
marginal is the same regardless of the input on
B
, so
\sigma_{QM_{1}}^{1}
must actually not be very correlated.

Continuing, we make use of the statement

\displaystyle I(Q:A)_{\rho^{1}}=\lambda_{1}.

(46)

To do so, we first use continuity of the mutual information to turn this into a statement about
\sigma^{1}
,

\displaystyle\lambda_{1}-\Delta(\epsilon,n_{Q})\leq I(Q:A)_{\sigma^{1}}.

(47)

Then observe that from data processing,

\displaystyle I(Q:A)_{\sigma^{1}}\leq I(Q:M_{1}M_{2})_{\sigma^{1}}

(48)

so then

\displaystyle\lambda_{1}-\Delta(\epsilon,n_{Q})\leq S(M_{1}M_{2})_{\sigma^{1}}+S(Q)_{\sigma^{1}}-S(M_{1}M_{2}Q)_{\sigma^{1}}

(49)

or, rearranging,

\displaystyle\boxed{S(M_{1}M_{2}Q)_{\sigma^{1}}\leq S(M_{1}M_{2})_{\sigma^{1}}+S(Q)-\lambda_{1}+\Delta(\epsilon,n_{Q}).}

(50)

which we will use below.

Now, consider the conditional mutual information
I(M_{1}:M_{2}|Q)_{\sigma^{1}}
.
This is

\displaystyle I(M_{1}:M_{2}|Q)_{\sigma^{1}}

\displaystyle=S(M_{1}Q)_{\sigma^{1}}+S(M_{2}Q)_{\sigma^{1}}-S(Q)_{\sigma^{1}}-S(M_{1}M_{2}Q)_{\sigma^{1}}\qquad\,\,\,\,\,\quad\qquad\text{definition of CMI}

\displaystyle=S(M_{1}Q)_{\sigma^{1}}+S(M_{2})_{\sigma^{1}}-S(M_{1}M_{2}Q)_{\sigma^{1}}\,\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\,\,\,\,\,\qquad\text{eq. \ref{eq:M2}}

\displaystyle\geq S(M_{1})_{\sigma^{1}}+S(Q)+S(M_{2})_{\sigma^{1}}-\lambda_{2}-S(M_{1}M_{2}Q)_{\sigma^{1}}-\Delta(\epsilon,n_{Q})\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\text{eq. \ref{eq:M1}}

\displaystyle\geq S(M_{1})_{\sigma^{1}}+S(M_{2})_{\sigma^{1}}-S(M_{1}M_{2})_{\sigma^{1}}+\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{2}-2\Delta(\epsilon,n_{Q})\quad\quad\,\,\,\,\text{eq. \ref{eq:fixed}}

\displaystyle=\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{2}-2\Delta(\epsilon,n_{Q})\,\,\,\,\quad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\quad\qquad\,\qquad\text{subadditivity}

so that the conditional mutual information is bounded below.

Next, we would like to translate this to a bound on the mutual information of the resource state.
Using that

\displaystyle\sigma_{QM_{1}M_{2}}^{1}=\mathcal{V}^{L}_{AL\rightarrow M_{1}}\otimes\mathcal{V}^{R}_{RB\rightarrow M_{2}}(\sigma^{1}_{QALRB})

(51)

and data processing for the CMI (lemma
8
) we have that

\displaystyle I(M_{1}:M_{2}|Q)_{\sigma^{1}_{M_{1}M_{2}Q}}\leq I(RA:LB|Q)_{\sigma_{QALRB}^{1}}=I(L:R)

(52)

where in the second equality we used that

\displaystyle\sigma^{1}_{QALRB}=P_{QA}\otimes\Psi_{LR}\otimes\phi^{1}_{B}.

(53)

Combined with our lower bound on the CMI, we have then

\displaystyle\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{2}-2\Delta(\epsilon,n_{Q})\leq I(R:L)_{\Psi}.

(54)

Considering in particular a pure state resource, this leads to

\displaystyle S(R)_{\Psi}\geq\frac{\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{2}}{2}-\Delta(\epsilon,n_{Q}).

(55)

Finally, it remains to translate this to a lower bound on the entanglement of formation of the resource state.
For a mixed state resource, we can decompose the state as

\displaystyle\Psi_{LR}=\sum_{i}p_{i}\outerproduct{\Psi_i}{\Psi_i}_{LR}.

(56)

We will take the above to be the optimizing decomposition when computing the entanglement of formation for state
\Psi
.
Let
\mathcal{N}^{i}
be the channel implemented by the NLQC protocol when given resource
\ket{\Psi_i}
.
Then the channel implemented given resource
\Psi
is
\mathcal{N}=\sum_{i}p_{i}\mathcal{N}_{i}
, and we have by assumption that

\displaystyle\epsilon\geq\|\mathcal{N}-U\|_{\diamond}=\left\|\sum_{i}p_{i}\mathcal{N}^{i}-U\right\|_{\diamond}

(57)

From lemma
5
, we have that

\displaystyle\sum_{i}p_{i}\|\mathcal{N}^{i}-U\|_{\diamond}\leq 2\sqrt{\epsilon}.

(58)

Now consider applying the lower bound on the entropy for each
\mathcal{N}^{i}
separately.
Defining
\gamma^{i}=\|\mathcal{N}^{i}-U\|
we have that

\displaystyle S(R)_{\Psi_{i}}\geq\frac{\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{2}}{2}-\Delta(\gamma_{i},n_{Q}).

(59)

Then the entanglement of formation is lower bounded by

\displaystyle E_{f}(L:R)_{\Psi}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}p_{i}S(R)_{\Psi_{i}}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{2}}{2}-\sum_{i}p_{i}\Delta(\gamma_{i},n_{Q})

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{2}}{2}-\Delta\left(\sum_{i}p_{i}\gamma_{i},n_{Q}\right)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{2}}{2}-\Delta(2\sqrt{\epsilon},n_{Q})

(60)

as claimed.
In the second to last line we used that
\Delta(x,n)
is concave in the first argument.

Next, we comment on parallel repetition of lower bounds proven using the controllable correlation.

Corollary 16

Consider a unitary
G
with
(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})
-controllable correlation.
An exact implementation of
G^{\otimes n}
as a NLQC requires entanglement of formation in the resource state
\Psi
lower bounded by

\displaystyle E_{f}(L:R)\geq n\left(\frac{\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{2}}{2}\right)

(61)

where
\lambda_{1}
,
\lambda_{2}
are the parameters appearing in the controllable correlation for
G
.

Proof.
Suppose that
\phi_{1},\phi_{2},P_{QA}
can be used to show
G
has
(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})
-controllable correlation.
Then considering
G^{\otimes n}
, use the correlated state
P_{QA}^{\otimes n}
, and inputs
\phi_{1}^{\otimes n}
,
\phi_{2}^{\otimes n}
, we obtain
\lambda_{1}^{\prime}=n\lambda_{1}
,
\lambda_{2}^{\prime}=n\lambda_{2}
, which lead to the stated lower bound.

Finally, note that if we have a unitary
U_{AB}
which is
\epsilon
close to
G
and implement it
n
times, our techniques so far do not provide a good lower bound.
This is because the error in the implementation of
G^{\otimes n}
becomes
\tilde{n}\gamma
, which eventually becomes larger than 1.
Thus, we can so far only bound the noisy, parallel repeated, setting in the case of zero error.
However, if we assume that the noisy implementation of
U_{AB}
is itself unitary, then we can simply use that it will have controllable correlation
(\lambda_{1}^{\prime},\lambda_{2}^{\prime})
with
|\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{1}^{\prime}|<\Delta(\epsilon,n_{A})
,
|\lambda_{2}-\lambda_{2}^{\prime}|<\Delta(\epsilon,n_{A})
which follows by continuity of the mutual information.
This leads to a lower bound

\displaystyle E_{f}(L:R)\geq n\left(\frac{\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{2}}{2}-\Delta\right).

(62)

It is an open problem to obtain a similar lower bound in the case where we allow the noisy implementation to be a general quantum channel.

4
Lower bound from the controllable entanglement

In this section we discuss our second lower bound technique, the controllable entanglement.
One motivation for considering this second technique is that the controllable correlation does not seem to produce tight lower bounds.
For instance, it gives a lower bound of
1/2
for the CNOT gate, but the best upper bound known is
1
.

4.1
Proof overview

To state the lower bound, we introduce the
(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})
-
controllable entanglement
.
The definition of the controllable entanglement makes use of the entanglement of formation,
E_{f}
, which is reviewed in section
2.2
.

Definition 17

Consider a unitary
U_{AB}
. We say that
U_{AB}
has
(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})
-
controllable entanglement
if there exists states
(\phi_{1})_{B}
,
(\phi_{2})_{B}
such that

\displaystyle(\Psi_{1})_{QAB}=U_{AB}(\Psi^{+}_{QA}\otimes(\phi_{1})_{B})U^{\dagger}_{AB},\qquad(\Psi_{2})_{QAB}=U_{AB}(\Psi^{+}_{QA}\otimes(\phi_{2})_{B})U^{\dagger}_{AB}.

(65)

with

\displaystyle\lambda_{1}:=E_{f}(Q:A)_{\Psi_{1}}>0,\qquad\lambda_{2}:=\min_{\sigma\in SEP}\|(\Psi_{2})_{QA}-\sigma_{QA}\|_{1}.

(66)

and
\lambda_{1}-2\lambda_{2}^{1/4}>0
,
\lambda_{2}\leq 1
.
If there are no such choices of input state, we say that
U_{AB}
is not controllably entangled.

In words, the controllable entanglement considers the following setting: We entangle one input,
A
, to the channel with a reference,
Q
.
We consider varying the choices of input on
B
to try and either make
Q\!:\!A
very entangled (which fixes
\lambda_{1}
) or close to separable (which fixes
\lambda_{2}
).

When
\lambda_{2}
is small enough and
\lambda_{1}
sufficiently large, the controllable entanglement gives a lower bound on entanglement cost in NLQC.
More precisely, we prove the following.

Theorem 18

Suppose that a NLQC protocol using resource state
\Psi_{LR}
implements a unitary
U_{AB}
to within diamond norm distance
\gamma
, and where
U_{AB}
has
(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})
controllable entanglement.
Then for small enough
\gamma
, we have that

\displaystyle E_{f}(L:R)_{\Psi}\geq\lambda_{1}-2\lambda_{2}^{1/4}-2^{9/8}\gamma^{1/16}

(67)

As an example application, consider the
\text{CNOT}_{AB}
gate.
Taking
\ket{\phi_1}=\ket{+}
, we see that

\displaystyle\ket{\Psi_1}=\text{CNOT}_{AB}\ket{\Psi^+}_{QA}\ket{+}_{B}=\ket{\Psi^+}_{QA}\ket{+}_{B}

(68)

which leads to
\lambda_{1}=1
.
Meanwhile, choosing
\ket{\phi_2}=\ket{0}
, we find

\displaystyle\ket{\Psi_2}=\text{CNOT}_{AB}\ket{\Psi^+}_{QA}\ket{0}_{B}=\ket{GHZ}_{QAB}

(69)

This leaves
Q\!:\!A
in a separable state, so
\lambda_{2}=0
.
Thus the theorem above gives a lower bound of exactly 1 for the CNOT gate.

In the remainder of this section we briefly outline the proof strategy for theorem
18
, and highlight how the subsections below correspond to steps in the proof.
Refer to figure
3
for the system labels we use.
Our high level summary assumes
\lambda_{2}=0
for simplicity.

The first step of the proof is given in section
4.2.1
.
The reasoning begins with the functionality of the NLQC protocol, which implements the unitary
U_{AB}
of interest.
We consider the action of this unitary when
A
is maximally entangled with a reference
Q
, and when we take two different choices of input to
B
, call them
\phi_{1}
and
\phi_{2}
.
We assume that the unitary is controllably entangled, so that the
Q\!:\!A
system should be thought of as highly entangled when given
\phi_{1}
and close to product when given
\phi_{2}
.
This gives statements about entanglement in the outputs, which we then need to translate to statements about entanglement in the resource.
To do so, we use the data processing inequality and the causal structure of the circuit to understand the entanglement pattern in the mid-protocol state (the state after the first set of operations in figure
3
, but before the second).
From this, we find that the
Q:M_{1}
wires must be unentangled, but
Q:M_{1}M_{2}
must be strongly entangled.
This then means that
R
must have been large enough to carry the missing entanglement, and we obtain a lower bound on the
dimension
of
R
, and in particular that
\log d_{R}\gtrsim\lambda_{1}
.

In section
4.2.2
, we then continue by showing that in fact the entropy of
R
must also be large.
To do this, we introduce the parallel repetition of the protocol implementing
U_{AB}
.
Repeating this
m
times, our bound on dimension says that
R
needs to consist of at least
m\lambda_{1}
qubits.
But if the entropy of
R
is smaller than
\lambda_{1}
, we can compress it using Schumacher’s compression scheme and take the compressed state as a resource for the implementing
U^{\otimes m}
.
To avoid violating our dimension lower bound then, the resource system must not be compressible to fewer than
\sim m\lambda_{1}
qubits, so we find that the
entropy
of
R
must be at least
\lambda_{1}
.

The entropy lower bound is a good entanglement lower bound when the resource system is pure.
To address the most general, mixed state, case, we upgrade this to a bound on the entanglement of formation.
This is done in section
4.2.3
.
The key observation is that if the NLQC protocol works well on average, the entropy lower bound must apply to every state in any ensemble decomposition of the mixed state resource, and so every state in the ensemble decomposition must have a large entropy.
But the entanglement of formation is defined in exactly this way: it is the minimal average entropy of pure states in any ensemble decomposition of
\Psi
.
Since the entropy is bounded below by
\lambda_{1}
for every term in the decomposition, we are led to the same lower bound for the entanglement of formation.

4.2
Proof of the lower bound

4.2.1
Dimension lower bound

As our first step towards a proof of theorem
18
, we prove the following lemma which lower bounds the dimension of the resource system.

Lemma 19

Suppose
U_{AB}
has
(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})
-controllable entanglement.
A non-local quantum computation which implements
U_{AB}^{\otimes m}
to within diamond norm
\epsilon
and uses a resource system
\Psi_{LR}
must have

\displaystyle n_{R}\geq m(\lambda_{1}-g_{1}(\epsilon,\lambda_{2}))-g_{2}(\epsilon,\lambda_{2})

(70)

where
g_{1}(\epsilon,\lambda_{2}),g_{2}(\epsilon,\lambda_{2})
are positive functions that go to
0
as
\epsilon,\lambda_{2}\rightarrow 0
. More specifically, they are given by

\displaystyle g_{1}(\epsilon,\lambda_{2})

\displaystyle=n_{Q}(\eta_{\epsilon}+\eta_{\sqrt{\epsilon^{\prime}}}),

\displaystyle g_{2}(\epsilon,\lambda_{2})

\displaystyle=H(\eta_{\epsilon})+H(\eta_{\sqrt{\epsilon^{\prime}}}),

(71)

where
\eta_{x}=2\sqrt{x(1-x)}
,
H(y)=(1+y)h\left(\frac{y}{1+y}\right)
, and
\epsilon^{\prime}=\epsilon+\lambda_{2}
.

Proof.
Suppose we have an NLQC protocol that implements
\tilde{U}
, with
\|\tilde{U}-U^{\otimes m}\|_{\diamond}\leq\epsilon
using a resource state
\Psi_{LR}
.
We take as input on the left one end of the maximally entangled state
\ket{\Psi^+}^{\otimes m}_{QA}
, and label the reference system by
Q
.
The set up is shown in figure
3
; we will use the operation and system labels shown there.

We consider two scenarios.
First, consider inputting the
\ket{\phi_1}^{\otimes m}
state into the remaining input, labelled
B^{\prime}
.
We label the state resulting from a perfect implementation of
U^{\otimes m}
on this input as
\rho^{1}
, and from the imperfect implementation as
\sigma^{1}
.
In this case, a perfect implementation of
U^{\otimes m}
would lead to, by the definition of the controllable entanglement,

\displaystyle E_{f}(Q:A)_{\rho^{1}}=\lambda_{1}m.

(72)

Since the protocol instead implements
\tilde{U}
which is close to
U^{\otimes m}
, we need to use the continuity bound of lemma
12
and we obtain

\displaystyle E_{f}(Q:A)_{\rho^{1}}-E_{f}(Q:A)_{\sigma^{1}}\leq m\,n_{Q}\,\eta_{\epsilon}+H(\eta_{\epsilon}).

(73)

Using
E_{f}(Q:A)_{\rho^{1}}=\lambda_{1}m
, we have then that the
A
output from
U_{AB}
is close to being
\lambda_{1}m
entangled with
R
,

\displaystyle E_{f}(Q:A)_{\sigma^{1}}\geq m(\lambda_{1}-n_{Q}\eta_{\epsilon})-H(\eta_{\epsilon}).

(74)

We can also observe that,

\displaystyle E_{f}(Q:M_{1}M_{2})_{\sigma^{1}}\geq m(\lambda_{1}-n_{Q}\eta_{\epsilon})-H(\eta_{\epsilon})

(75)

which follows from the previous line and the data processing inequality (lemma
11
) for the entanglement of formation.

Second, we input
\ket{\phi_2}^{\otimes m}
into
B
.
We label the state created in this case by
\rho^{2}
in the perfect case, and as
\sigma^{2}
in the imperfect case.
For
\lambda_{2}
small, we obtain from its definition that
\rho^{2}
is close to separable,

\displaystyle\lambda_{2}=\min_{\kappa\in SEP}\|\rho^{2}_{QA}-\kappa\|_{1}=\|\rho^{2}_{QA}-\kappa_{*}\|_{1}

(76)

where
\kappa_{*}
is the minimizing choice of separable density matrix.
Next, consider purifying the operation
\mathcal{W}^{L}_{M_{1}M_{2}\rightarrow A}
to a unitary
W^{L}_{M_{1}M_{2}\rightarrow AE}
.
Since the overall channel mapping the inputs
QAB
to
QAB
is unitary, this must produce a state of the form
\rho^{2}_{QA}\otimes\rho_{E}
.
This must be
\epsilon
close to
\sigma^{2}_{QA}\otimes\rho_{E}
, which combined with the above and using the triangle inequality gives that

\displaystyle\left\|\sigma^{2}_{QA}\otimes\rho_{E}-\kappa_{*}\otimes\rho_{E}\right\|_{1}\leq\lambda_{2}+\epsilon=:\epsilon^{\prime}

(77)

Now apply
(W^{R})^{\dagger}
to both states, which won’t change the trace distance, and choose an explicit decomposition of
\kappa_{*}
,

\displaystyle\epsilon^{\prime}

\displaystyle\geq\left\|\sum_{i}p^{i}\rho^{i}_{Q}\otimes(W^{R})^{\dagger}_{M_{1}M_{2}\rightarrow AE}\rho^{i}_{A}\otimes\rho_{E}W^{R}_{M_{1}M_{2}\rightarrow AE}-(W^{R})^{\dagger}_{M_{1}M_{2}\rightarrow AE}\sigma^{2}_{QA}\otimes\rho_{E}W^{R}_{M_{1}M_{2}\rightarrow AE}\right\|_{1}

\displaystyle=\left\|\sum_{i}p^{i}\rho^{i}_{Q}\otimes\rho^{i}_{M_{1}M_{2}}-\sigma^{2}_{QM_{1}M_{2}}\right\|_{1}

(78)

From this we also obtain that the state on
\sigma^{2}_{QM_{1}}
is close to separable, which using continuity of
E_{f}
gives,

\displaystyle E_{f}(Q:M_{1})_{\sigma^{2}}\leq mn_{Q}\eta_{\sqrt{\epsilon^{\prime}}}+H(\eta_{\sqrt{\epsilon^{\prime}}}).

(79)

Finally, notice that by causality the state on
QM_{1}
must be the same regardless of the input at
B^{\prime}
, so that

\displaystyle E_{f}(Q:M_{1})_{\sigma^{1}}\leq m\,n_{Q}\eta_{\sqrt{\epsilon}}+H(\eta_{\sqrt{\epsilon}}).

(80)

In words, we see that in the state
\sigma^{1}
systems
Q:M_{1}
are close to separable, while
Q:M_{1}M_{2}
is entangled.

Now we combine our statements so far to show that this can only occur when
R
is large enough,

\displaystyle m(\lambda_{1}-n_{Q}\eta_{\epsilon})-H(\eta_{\epsilon})

\displaystyle\leq E_{f}(Q:M_{1}M_{2})_{\sigma^{1}}\qquad\qquad\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\text{From eq. \ref{eq:correct}}

\displaystyle\leq E_{f}(Q:M_{1}RB^{\prime})_{\sigma^{1}}\qquad\qquad\,\,\,\text{From data processing}

\displaystyle=E_{f}(Q:M_{1}R)_{\sigma^{1}}\qquad\qquad\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\text{Because $B^{\prime}$ is tensor product}

\displaystyle\leq E_{f}(Q:M_{1})_{\sigma^{1}}+n_{R}\quad\,\,\,\,\,\,\qquad\text{From eq. \ref{eq:SRcontinuity}}

\displaystyle=m\,n_{Q}\eta_{\sqrt{\epsilon^{\prime}}}+H(\eta_{\sqrt{\epsilon^{\prime}}})+n_{R},\,\quad\,\,\,\,\text{From eq. \ref{eq:smallonQM_1}.}

(81)

so we have that

\displaystyle n_{R}\geq m(\lambda_{1}-n_{Q}\eta_{\epsilon}-n_{Q}\eta_{\sqrt{\epsilon^{\prime}}})-H(\eta_{\epsilon})-H(\eta_{\sqrt{\epsilon^{\prime}}})

(82)

as claimed.

4.2.2
Entropy lower bound for any pure state resource

In section
4.2.1
we gave a lower bound on the number of qubits of resource system needed in an NLQC implementing a unitary
U_{AB}
with the controllable entanglement property.
In this section we translate this into a bound on the entanglement in the resource system, under the assumption that the resource system is pure, which we quantify using the entropy of one side of the resource state.
We treat this first in the case where the implementation of
U
is exact , then in the case where the implementation of
U
is approximate.
Our treatment of the approximate setting contains as a special case the exact one so in principle the exact case could be omitted, but the exact case is significantly simpler than the approximate one and conveys the key elements of the proof, so we retain it.

Exact case:
To upgrade our dimension lower bound to a lower bound on entropy, our approach is to make use of Schumacher compression
[
30
]
, stated in the next theorem.

Theorem 20

(Schumacher compression)

Suppose we have a quantum source which produces
\ket{\psi}_{LR}^{\otimes n}
.
Then, for all
\epsilon,\delta\in(0,1)
, there is a large enough
n
such that there exists a compression map
\mathcal{C}_{R^{n}\rightarrow M}
and decompression map
\mathcal{D}_{M\rightarrow R^{n}}
with

\displaystyle\|\outerproduct{\psi}{\psi}^{\otimes n}-\mathcal{D}_{M\rightarrow R^{n}}\circ\mathcal{C}_{R^{n}\rightarrow M}(\outerproduct{\psi}{\psi}^{\otimes n})\|_{1}\leq\epsilon

(83)

and where
\log d_{M}\leq(S(R)+\delta)n
.

We use this along with our lower bound on dimension, lemma
19
, to obtain a lower bound on entropy.
The basic idea is that the resource system
\ket{\psi}_{LR}
can be compressed to contain
S(R)m
qubits using Schumacher compression, but from our dimension bound we know the number of qubits must be
\lambda_{1}m
, so we must have
S(R)\geq\lambda_{1}
.
We give a more careful proof next.

Lemma 21

Suppose that an NLQC protocol implements
U_{AB}
exactly, using a pure resource state
\ket{\psi}_{LR}
, and where
U_{AB}
has
(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})
-controllable entanglement. Then,
S(R)\geq\lambda_{1}-2\lambda_{2}^{1/4}
.

Proof.
Using Schumacher compression (theorem
20
), for any choice of
\epsilon,\delta>0
there is an
m
large enough, choice of compression channel
\mathcal{E}_{R^{m}\rightarrow M}
, and decompression channel
\mathcal{D}_{M\rightarrow R^{m}}
with
n_{M}\leq(S(R)+\delta)m
, such that if we define

\displaystyle\Psi_{L^{m}M}=\mathcal{E}_{R^{m}\rightarrow M}(\outerproduct{\psi}{\psi})

(84)

then

\displaystyle\left\|\outerproduct{\psi}{\psi}^{\otimes m}_{LR}-\mathcal{D}_{M\rightarrow R^{m}}(\Psi_{L^{m}M})\right\|_{1}\leq\epsilon.

(85)

We define an NLQC protocol to implement
U^{\otimes m}
as follows.
The distributed resource state is taken to be
\Psi_{L^{m}M}
.
In the first set of operations, on the right, Bob applies
\mathcal{E}_{R^{n}\rightarrow M}
, leaving Alice and Bob sharing a state
\epsilon
-close to
\ket{\psi}^{\otimes m}
.
Next, they run
m
copies of the protocol, using the
m
copies of
\ket{\psi}
as resource states.
By the properties of the diamond norm distance, this will be
\epsilon
-close in diamond norm to an implementation of
U^{\otimes m}
.

Now we make use of lemma
19
, which tells us that

\displaystyle n_{M}\geq m(\lambda_{1}-g_{1}(\epsilon_{,}\lambda_{2}))-g_{2}(\epsilon,\lambda_{2})

(86)

But also, at large enough
m
,
(S(R)+\delta)m\geq n_{M}
, so that

\displaystyle S(R)\geq\lambda_{1}-\delta-g_{1}(\epsilon,\lambda_{2})-g_{2}(\epsilon,\lambda_{2})/m

(87)

But we can choose
\epsilon
,
\delta
arbitrarily small while
m
becomes arbitrarily large, so this simplifies to

\displaystyle S(R)

\displaystyle\geq\lambda_{1}-g_{1}(0,\lambda_{2})

\displaystyle=\lambda_{1}-2\lambda_{2}^{1/4}\sqrt{1-\sqrt{\lambda_{2}}}

\displaystyle\geq\lambda_{1}-2\lambda_{2}^{1/4}

(88)

as claimed.
Note that in the last line we used that
\lambda_{2}<1
, which we took as part of the definition of the controllable entanglement.

Approximate case:
In case where the NLQC protocol implements
U
approximately, the asymptotic statement of Schumacher compression doesn’t suffice to obtain a lower bound.
The reason for this can be seen by considering equation
87
.
There, the error
\epsilon
in the implementation of
U^{\otimes m}
comes from the approximation to the resource state appearing when decompressing from Schumacher’s scheme.
If each
U
implementation is approximate, there is a contribution to the error from each
U
, so we would replace
\epsilon\rightarrow\epsilon+\gamma\,m
where
\gamma
is the error in a single
U
implementation.
This leaves a term in the lower bound that goes to
-\infty
as
m\rightarrow\infty
, so we do not obtain a bound in the asymptotic setting considered by Schumacher.
To remedy this, we will need to consider Schumacher compression for a finite number of copies of the input state.
This is addressed in
[
1
]
; we briefly recall one of their results here.

A compression protocol consists of a compression channel
\mathcal{C}_{A^{n}\rightarrow M}
and a decompression channel
\mathcal{D}_{M\rightarrow A^{n}}
.
We say the protocol is
\epsilon
-correct if the entanglement fidelity of the input
\rho^{\otimes n}
is
\epsilon
-close to the entanglement fidelity of the output,

\displaystyle F_{e}(\rho_{A}^{\otimes n},\mathcal{D}\circ\mathcal{C}(\rho_{A}^{\otimes n}))\geq 1-\epsilon.

(89)

We denote the minimal log-dimension of
M
needed to achieve
\epsilon
-correct compression on
n
copies of
\rho_{A}
by
M(n,\epsilon,\rho)
.

The value of
M(n,\epsilon,\rho)
is well understood.
To state the result, we define the function on density matrices

\displaystyle V(A)_{\rho}=\tr(\rho_A\log^2\rho_A)-\left(\tr(\rho_A \log\rho_A)\right)^{2}

(90)

and the function

\displaystyle\Phi^{-1}(x)=\sup\left\{z\in\mathbb{R}:\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{-\infty}^{z}e^{-t^{2}/2}dt\leq x\right\}.

(91)

This is known as the
quantile
of the normal distribution; it expresses how far we need to integrate the normal distribution with variance 1 to reach a given value
x
.
The quantile of the normal distribution is defined on
(0,1)
and diverges as
x\rightarrow 0,1
.

Finally, we can state the following theorem, proven in
[
1
]
as theorem 3.

Theorem 22

(Schumacher compression at finite block length)

The minimal achievable value of
M(n,\epsilon,\rho)
in performing
\epsilon
-correct compression of the state
\rho_{A}
is

\displaystyle M(n,\epsilon,\rho)=nS(A)_{\rho}+\Phi^{-1}(\sqrt{1-\epsilon})\sqrt{nV(A)_{\rho}}+O(\log n)

(92)

We use this along with lemma
19
to obtain a lower bound on the entropy.

Theorem 23

Suppose that an NLQC protocol implements
U_{AB}
to within diamond norm distance
\gamma
, using a pure resource state
\ket{\psi}_{LR}
. Then, for small enough
\gamma
,

\displaystyle S(R)_{\psi}\geq\lambda_{1}-2\lambda_{2}^{1/4}-2\gamma^{1/8}.

(93)

Proof.
We consider an implementation of
U^{\otimes m}
, where we will choose
m
later.
Our implementation uses as a resource state a compressed version of
\ket{\psi}_{LR}^{\otimes m}
, that is we use

\displaystyle\Psi_{L^{m}M}=\mathcal{I}\otimes\mathcal{E}_{R_{n}\rightarrow M}(\outerproduct{\psi}{\psi}^{\otimes m})

(94)

where
\mathcal{E}_{R^{n}\rightarrow M}
is an optimal compression channel.
The protocol proceeds by first having Bob decompress
M
into
R^{n}
, and then running
m
parallel implementations of
U
as before.
We use an
\epsilon
-correct compression protocol where we choose
\epsilon
later.
Since the compression protocol is
\epsilon
-correct, and each
U
implementation is
\gamma
-correct, by the properties of the diamond norm the implementation of
U^{\otimes m}
using the compressed resource state will be
\epsilon+\gamma\,m
correct.

Now we make use of lemma
19
, which tells us that

\displaystyle n_{M}\geq m(\lambda_{1}-g_{1}(\epsilon+\gamma m,\lambda_{2}))-g_{2}(\epsilon+\gamma m,\lambda_{2}).

(95)

Now use theorem
22
as an upper bound on
n_{M}
, and using that
V(R)_{\rho}\leq n_{R}^{2}
, we have

\displaystyle S(R)_{\rho}\geq\lambda_{1}-\Phi^{-1}(\sqrt{1-\epsilon})\frac{n_{R}}{\sqrt{m}}-g_{1}(\epsilon+\gamma m,\lambda_{2})-\frac{g_{2}(\epsilon+\gamma m,\lambda_{2})}{m}-O\left(\frac{\log m}{m}\right)

(96)

For intuition, notice that if we take
\gamma=0
we can maximize the lower bound by sending
\epsilon\rightarrow 0,m\rightarrow\infty
, in which case we recover
S(R)_{\rho}\geq\lambda_{1}-2\lambda_{2}^{1/4}
.
At non-zero
\gamma
however, sending
m\rightarrow\infty
would trivialize the lower bound.
To recover a good lower bound, we need to choose
\epsilon,m
in a way that depends on
\gamma
such that the lower bound approaches
\lambda_{1}-2\lambda_{2}^{1/4}
as
\gamma\rightarrow 0
.
We will achieve this with a simple choice by taking

\displaystyle\epsilon

\displaystyle=\gamma,\qquad m=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\gamma}}.

(97)

Inserting this above leads to the lower bound

\displaystyle S(R)_{\rho}\geq\lambda_{1}-\Phi^{-1}(\sqrt{1-\gamma}){n_{R}}{\sqrt{\gamma}}-g_{1}(\gamma+\sqrt{\gamma},\lambda_{2})-\frac{g_{2}(\gamma+\sqrt{\gamma},\lambda_{2})}{m}-O\left(\sqrt{\gamma}\log\gamma\right)

We can see that as
\gamma\rightarrow 0
this approaches the lower bound obtained in the exact case, so this bound is equal to that one plus terms that go to zero as
\gamma\rightarrow 0
.
To obtain the error terms, we expand in a series around
\gamma=0
, obtaining

\displaystyle S(R)_{\rho}\geq\lambda_{1}-2\lambda_{2}^{1/4}-2\delta_{\lambda_{2},0}\gamma^{1/8}-\tilde{O}(n_{R}\gamma^{1/4})\,\,\,\text{as}\,\,\,\gamma\rightarrow 0

(98)

where the
\tilde{O}
notation hides logarithmic factors, and the leading order
\gamma^{1/8}
term appears only at
\lambda_{2}=0
due to a non-analyticity in the lower bound.
For
\gamma
small enough, concretely
\gamma
such that
\gamma\lesssim 1/n_{R}^{8}
then, we obtain the lower bound

\displaystyle S(R)_{\rho}\geq\lambda_{1}-2\lambda_{2}^{1/4}-2\gamma^{1/8}

(99)

as claimed.

4.2.3
Entanglement lower bound

We’ve given lower bounds on the entropy of one end of the resource system for any resource state that allows a unitary
U_{AB}
with controllable entanglement to be implemented as a NLQC.
In the case where the resource is pure, this provides a lower bound on entanglement in the resource.
In the mixed case however, this is no longer true.
Thus some further work is needed to give an entanglement lower bound in the mixed case.

Theorem
18
Suppose that a NLQC protocol using resource state
\Psi_{LR}
implements a unitary
U_{AB}
to within diamond norm distance
\gamma
, and where
U_{AB}
has
(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})
controllable entanglement.
Then for small enough
\gamma
, we have that

\displaystyle E_{f}(L:R)_{\Psi}\geq\lambda_{1}-2\lambda_{2}^{1/4}-2\gamma^{1/8}

(100)

Proof.
Consider any decomposition of
\Psi
into a convex sum of pure states,

\displaystyle\Psi_{LR}=\sum_{x}p_{x}\outerproduct{\psi_x}{\psi_x}_{LR}.

(101)

Then, the protocol implemented can be seen as a probabilistic mixture of protocols which take in the (pure) resource state
\ket{\psi_x}_{LR}
.
Let
\mathcal{P}
denote the channel implemented by the protocol given resource
\rho_{LR}
, and
\mathcal{P}_{x}
the channel implemented when given resource
\psi_{x}
, so that
\mathcal{P}=\sum_{x}p_{x}\mathcal{P}_{x}
.
Then using
\|P-U\|_{\diamond}\leq\gamma
and lemma
5
, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{x}p_{x}\left\|\mathcal{P}_{x}-U\right\|_{\diamond}\leq 2\sqrt{\gamma}.

(102)

Define
\left\|\mathcal{P}_{x}-U\right\|_{\diamond}=\gamma_{x}
, so that the above reads
\sum_{x}p_{x}\gamma_{x}\leq 2\sqrt{\gamma}
.
Then our lower bound given by theorem
23
applied to each
P_{x}
separately tells us that

\displaystyle S(R)_{\psi_{x}}\geq\lambda_{1}-2\lambda_{2}^{1/4}-2(2\sqrt{\gamma_{x}})^{1/8}.

(103)

But now consider the entanglement of formation for
\Psi_{LR}
,

\displaystyle E_{f}(L:R)_{\Psi}

\displaystyle=\min_{\{p_{i},\ket{\psi_i}\}}\sum_{i}p_{i}S(R)_{\psi_{i}},

\displaystyle=\lambda_{1}-2\lambda_{2}^{1/4}-\min_{\{p_{i},\ket{\psi_i}\}}\sum_{i}p_{i}(2(2\sqrt{\gamma_{i}})^{1/8})

\displaystyle\geq\lambda_{1}-2\lambda_{2}^{1/4}-\min_{\{p_{i},\ket{\psi_i}\}}2^{9/8}\left(\sum_{i}p_{i}\gamma_{i}\right)^{1/16}

\displaystyle=\lambda_{1}-2\lambda_{2}^{1/4}-2^{9/8}\gamma^{1/16}.

(104)

where the last inequality used concavity of
f(x)=x^{1/16}
.

A convenient property of our lower bound technique is that parallel repetition is immediate when
\lambda_{2}=0
.
More concretely, we have the following lemma.

Lemma 24

(Parallel repetition)

Suppose that a unitary
U
has
(\lambda_{1},0)
-controllable entanglement.
Then
U^{\otimes n}
has
(n\lambda_{1},0)
-controllable entanglement.

Proof.
The proof follows because if
\ket{\phi_1}
,
\ket{\phi_2}
are choices of
B
input that lead to the values of
\lambda_{1}
and
\lambda_{2}
for
U
, then
\ket{\phi_1}^{\otimes n}
and
\ket{\phi_2}^{\otimes n}
are suitable choices for
U^{\otimes n}
that lead to
\lambda_{1}^{\prime}=n\lambda_{1}
,
\lambda_{2}^{\prime}=0
, so that the lower bound on
U^{\otimes n}
is at least
n
times the lower bound on
U
.

Note that parallel repetition doesn’t follow, at least using the same argument, in the case where
\lambda_{2}>0
.
This is because then we get
\lambda_{2}^{\prime}\leq\lambda_{2}n
because of subadditivity of the trace distance, but for large enough
n
this will violate the requirement that
\lambda_{2}^{\prime}\leq 1
.

4.3
Evaluating the lower bound in simple cases

Next, we begin exploring the value of the controllable entanglement for some simple gates.
We do so numerically.
In particular, we compute
\lambda_{1}
and
\lambda_{2}
for various explicit choices of gate.
While for
CNOT
and gates related by local unitaries it is straightforward to guess the optimal choices of
\ket{\phi_1}
and
\ket{\phi_2}
, for general gates we perform a numerical optimization procedure to find optimal values.
A program is available online
5
5

5

https://drive.google.com/file/d/1ibYEnM-tu69xH5hLS4IJxRh7R_C0EG8Z/view?usp=drive_link
which the reader can download and run to study lower bounds on any chosen two qubit gate.

We describe our numerical approach briefly here and report our results in table
2
.
To obtain our lower bound, we need to compute
\lambda_{1}
and
\lambda_{2}
.
Recall that
\lambda_{1}
is equal to the entanglement of formation in system
Q\!:\!A
with input
B
chosen to be
\ket{\phi_1}
.
The entanglement of formation has a closed form expression for two qubit systems
[
19
]
which we make use of.
Because
E_{f}
is convex in it’s input state, we are assured that the maximizing choice of input on
B
is a pure state.

To compute
\lambda_{2}
is somewhat more challenging.
Recall that
\lambda_{2}
is defined as the minimal trace distance of a state on
Q\!:\!A
to a separable state, when given input
\ket{\phi_2}
.
To make use of pre-built optimization procedures over the space of separable states, we in fact compute the relative entropy of entanglement,

\displaystyle E_{R}(Q:A)_{\rho}=\min_{\sigma\in SEP}D(\rho_{QA}\|\sigma_{QA}).

(105)

By Pinsker’s inequality this upper bounds the object of interest,

\displaystyle E_{R}(Q:A)_{\rho}\geq\frac{1}{2\ln 2}\min_{\sigma\in SEP}\|\rho_{QA}-\sigma_{QA}\|_{1}^{2}.

(106)

We use the Ket package in Julia
6
6

6

https://juliapackages.com/p/ket
to compute
E_{R}
numerically for our states of interest.
We restrict to two qubit states, where this optimization is easy.
We then search over the space of single qubit input states to find a locally minimal choice of state
\ket{\phi_1}
, which again is pure because of convexity of
E_{R}
.
Note that this procedure along with the use of Pinsker’s inequality may relax our bounds, compared to optimizing the trace distance directly, in cases where
E_{R}
is non-zero.
In all cases where the gates we studied had controllable entanglement, they also happen to have
E_{R}=0
and hence
\lambda_{2}=0
, so this approach does not relax the numerical bounds for these gates.
Using this procedure does mean however that our procedure may return a negative (hence trivial) lower bound even when a direct optimization of the trace distance would return a positive value.

We find non-trivial lower bounds for a number of common gates.
In addition to the CNOT gate, we find lower bounds for the Berkeley B gate and for the
XX
interaction with a rotation by (for instance)
\pi/4
.
See appendix
A
for matrix expressions for these gates.
Notice
RXX
and
CNOT
obtain the same numerical lower bound.
This leaves open the possibility that these two gates are equivalent up to local operations, but we can confirm this is not the case by observing that
\text{CNOT}\ket{\Psi^+}=\ket{0}\ket{+}
is product, while
RXX\ket{\Psi^+}\propto\ket{\Psi^+}
is entangled.

Finally, we comment that our lower bound fails to be non-trivial (to give a positive lower bound) for many choices of gate.
This occurs, for example, for the SWAP gate where we find a lower bound of 0.
In this and related examples the failure of our lower bound is expected, since SWAP can be implemented without entanglement and using only quantum communication.
On the other hand, for many gates including Controlled-
T
, Controlled-
S
, and random two qubit unitaries our technique does not give a lower bound, but from the controllable correlation technique we can see that these bounds do require entanglement, so the technique fails in these cases.

5
Discussion

In this work we introduced the controllable correlation and controllable entanglement, and gave lower bounds on NLQC in terms of these properties.
We showed that many simple two qubits gates can be lower bounded by these techniques.

Computing the controllable correlation numerically allows us to explore the entanglement requirements of many gates.
Doing so, we have made some observations that so far do not have analytical explanations.
For instance, we computed the controllable correlation lower bound for 100,000 samples from the Haar distribution on two qubit unitaries and found a positive lower bound for every sample.
For gates that are known to not require entanglement, like the SWAP gate or product unitaries, the bound of course returns 0 correctly, but the set of such gates seems to have small (perhaps zero) measure.
It would be interesting to extend our numerics to larger numbers of qubits, and, if the observation persists, to look for an analytical proof that unitaries with zero entanglement cost are rare.

Another natural question is whether, when a unitary has non-zero entanglement cost, this always leads to a non-zero controllable correlation for some choice of state
P_{QA}
.
If so, this would make the controllable correlation a “faithful” measure of entanglement cost, analogous to a faithful measure of entanglement in quantum states.
A related question is to begin with the assumption that the controllable correlation is
0
for all states
P_{QA}
, and try to extract structural properties of the unitary.
For instance, does this imply the unitary is locally equivalent to either SWAP or identity?
Or are there other unitaries with this property?

We showed that the CNOT gate requires a resource system with
E_{f}
of 1, the same entanglement of formation as one EPR pair.
Another interesting problem is to ask if whenever the CNOT gate can be implemented exactly, the resource state is of the form
\Psi^{+}_{LR}\otimes\Psi_{L^{\prime}R^{\prime}}
up to local unitaries on
LL^{\prime}
and
RR^{\prime}
.
This would be analogous to the self-testing property of certain non-local games.

Our techniques so far assume that the NLQC implements a unitary operation.
This requirement is necessary, and neither the controllable correlation or controllable entanglement provide lower bounds without placing this requirement.
This is clear, since if it applied to quantum channels we could obtain a lower bound on entanglement the cost of
\mathcal{N}_{AB\rightarrow A}(\cdot)=\tr_{B}(U_{AB}\cdot U^{\dagger}_{AB})
, which has the same values of
\lambda_{1}
and
\lambda_{2}
as
U_{AB}
does.
However this channel can always be implemented with zero entanglement (by sending both inputs to the left), so this is a contradiction.
An important open problem then is to find new quantities, or modifications of the controllable correlation / entanglement, which provide lower bounds on the entanglement cost of quantum channels.
